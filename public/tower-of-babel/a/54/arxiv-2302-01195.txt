Operator splitting based dynamic iteration for linear infinite-dimensional port-Hamiltonian systems
Bálint Farkas, Birgit Jacob, Timo Reis, Merlin Schmitz

Abstract
A dynamic iteration scheme for linear infinite-dimensional port-Hamiltonian systems is proposed. The dynamic iteration is monotone in the sense that the error is decreasing, it does not require any stability condition and is in particular applicable to port-Hamiltonian formulations arising from domain decompositions.

Introduction

Operator splitting methods are widely used to reduce (the numerical) solution of a complex problem to the iterative solution of subproblems, into which the original problem is split.
How this splitting arises can be based on various considerations, coming from the governing physical laws, from the geometry of the domain over which a certain partial differential equation is considered, from the structure of the problem, from mathematical reasons, or from the combination of these. See, for instance,
Marchuk 1990
,
McLachlan and Quispel 2002
,
Geiser 2011
,
(
Hundsdorfer and Verwer 2003
, Ch. IV)
,
Hairer et al. 2010
,
Strang 1968
,
Jahnke and Lubich 2000
,
Hansen and Ostermann 2009
,
Faou et al. 2015
,
Hochbruck and
Ostermann 2010
,
Hansen and Henningsson 2017
,
Hansen et al. 2016
for more information and a general overview of splitting methods in various situations.

Operator splitting
is particularly suitable for problems composed of subsystems which are coupled in a particular way; although such a coupling may not be visible immediately. For example, boundary coupled system or problems with dynamic boundary condition, see, e.g.,
Csomós et al. 2021
,
Csomós et al. 2023
, as well as delay equations have been successfully treated via splitting methods, see, e.g.,
Bellen and Zennaro 2013
,
Bellen et al. 2009
or
Bátkai et al. 2016
for an operator splitting approach in the dissipative situation. It is therefore interesting and important to study operator splitting methods for a class of systems that is closed under certain types of couplings. In this paper we are interested in infinite dimensional port-Hamiltonian systems of a specific structure.

The splitting method studied in this paper is originally due to Peaceman and Rachford, see
Peaceman and
Rachford 1955
, who introduced it in the setting of linear operators. The Peaceman–Rachford splitting was then extended to maximal monotone operators on Banach spaces by Lions and Mercier
Lions and Mercier 1979
. And this framework is indeed more suitable for the purposes of this paper, as the occurring operators here will be only affine linear in general. For error analyis of Peaceman–Rachford type splittings and variants we refer, e.g., to
Hundsdorfer and Verwer 1989
.

Operator splitting based dynamic iteration schemes for finite dimensional port-Hamiltonian systems were studied in
Günther et al. 2021
. Here we make the first steps to extend the study to infinite dimensional port-Hamiltonian systems. The Peaceman–Rachford–Lions–Mercier type splitting algorithm will result in a convergent approximation, under suitable conditions, but most importantly the approximation error will be monotonically increasingly convergent to
0
, a feature that is connected with the port-Hamiltonian structure of the problem.

This paper is structured as followes. In the next section we give an explicit description of the dynamic iteration scheme and present our main theorems. The proof of these theorems can be found in Section
5
. In Section
3
we give some background information on the theory of system nodes that will be used in the proof. Further, Section
4
is devoted to the proof of maximal monotonicity of one of the splitting operators.
We end in Section
6.1
with two examples: A coupled wave-heat system and a domain decomposition for the wave equation.

3
Background on system nodes

Let
X
,
U
and
Y
be Hilbert spaces and denote the canonical projections onto
X
and
Y
in
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
respectively by
P_{X}

and
P_{Y}
. Let

S\colon\dom(S)\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\to{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
Y\end{smallmatrix}\right]}

be a linear operator. Its corresponding
main operator
is given by
A\colon\dom(A)\subset X\to X
with
\dom(A)\coloneqq\left\{x\in X\left|\vphantom{x\in X}\,{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in\dom(S)\right.\right\}
and
Ax\coloneqq P_{X}S{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
for all
x\in\dom(A)
. One often sets

A\&B\coloneqq P_{X}S\qquad\text{and}\qquad C\&D\coloneqq P_{Y}S

so
S
can be written as

S=\begin{bmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{bmatrix}.

The concept of system nodes poses natural assumptions on the operator
S
, in order to guarantee favorable properties and a suitable solution concept to the dynamics specified by the differential equation

\begin{bmatrix}\dot{x}(t)\\
y(t)\end{bmatrix}=S\begin{bmatrix}x(t)\\
u(t)\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

For a comprehensive study of system nodes, we refer to the monograph
Staffans 2005
.

Definition 3.1

(System node)
.

A system node on the triple
(Y,X,U)
of Hilbert spaces is a (possibly unbounded) linear operator
S\colon\dom(S)\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\to{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
Y\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

S
is closed.

(ii)

P_{X}S\colon\dom(S)\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\to X
is closed.

(iii)

For all
u\in U
, there exists some
x\in X
with
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in\dom(S)
.

(iv)

The main operator
A
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
\mathfrak{A}(\cdot)\colon[0,\infty)\to{\mathcal{L}}(X)
on
X
.

Remark 3.2

(System nodes)
.

Let
S={\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
be a system node on
(Y,X,U)
.

(i)

It follows from the above definition that
C\&D\in\mathcal{L}(\dom(A\&B),Y)
, where
\dom(A\&B)
is endowed with the graph norm of
A\&B
. In particular, the operator
C
with
Cx\coloneqq C\&D{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
fulfills
C\in\mathcal{L}(\dom(A),Y)
.

(ii)

Since generators of semigroups are densely defined (see
(
Engel and Nagel 2000
, Chap. 2, Thm. 1.5)
),
\dom(S)
is dense in
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
and for given
u\in U
the affine subspace

\left\{x\in X\left|\vphantom{x\in X}\,{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in\dom(A\&B)\right.\right\}

is dense in
X
.

(iii)

Since
A
is a generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup there is
\alpha\in\rho(A)
(in the resolvent set of
A
). The completion of
X
with respect to the norm
\|x\|_{X_{-1}}\coloneqq\|(\alpha\mathrm{I}-A)^{-1}x\|
is denoted by
X_{-1}
. Note that the topology of
X_{-1}
does not depend on the particular choice of
\alpha\in\rho(A)

(
Tucsnak and Weiss 2009
, Prop. 2.10.2)
. The operator
A
extends continuously as
A_{-1}\colon X\mapsto X_{-1}
;
A
and
A_{-1}
are similar, hence have the same spectrum and
A_{-1}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup
\mathfrak{A}_{-1}(\cdot)\colon[0,\infty)\to{\mathcal{L}}(X_{-1})
on
X_{-1}
, which extends
\mathfrak{A}(\cdot)
(and which is similar to
\mathfrak{A}(\cdot)
), see
(
Engel and Nagel 2000
, Sec. II.5)
.

(iv)

A\&B
extends to a bounded linear operator
[A_{-1}\ B]\colon{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\to X_{-1}
, which in fact has such a block structure. Moreover, the domain of
A\&B
(equally: the domain of
S
) fulfills

\dom(A\&B)=\left\{{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\left|\vphantom{{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}}\,A_{-1}x+Bu\in X\right.\right\},

see
(
Staffans 2005
, pp. 3–4)
.

(v)

For all

the norm

\left\|\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\|_{\alpha}\coloneqq\left(\|x-(\alpha\mathrm{I}-A_{-1})^{-1}Bu\|_{X}^{2}+\|u\|^{2}_{U}\right)^{1/2}

is equivalent to the graph norm of

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathrm{I}&-(\alpha\mathrm{I}-A_{-1})^{-1}B\\
0&\mathrm{I}\end{smallmatrix}\right]

maps

{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\dom(A)\\

(
Staffans 2005

.

in the topology of

If

The following result ensures the existence of unique classical trajectories with suitable control functions and initial values.

Proposition 3.5

(Existence of classical trajectories
(

, Thm. 4.3.9)
)
.

Let

be a system node on
(Y,X,U)
, let

In the case of a well-posed system

We provide some further statements on classical/generalized trajectories.

Remark 3.6

(Classical/generalized trajectories)
.

Let
S={\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\

be a system node on

(i)

(ii)

\forall\,t\in[0,T]:\quad x(t)=\mathfrak{A}(t)x(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{A}_{-1}(t-\tau)Bu(\tau)\,{\rm d}\tau,

where the latter has to be interpreted as an integral in the space

(iii)

If

is a generalized trajectory for (

Definition 3.7

Definition 3.10

(Partial flow inverse)

A system node

[C\&D]_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]

(Partial flow-invertibility,

The main motivation for flow-inverses is that they interchange input and output. This is the subject of the following result, which is a slight reformulation of

Proposition 3.12

(Trajectories and flow-inverse)

.

4
Maximal monotonicity of the operator
M

In this section we discuss some important properties of the operator
M
defined in (
3
).
The most important one is the so-called maximal monotonicity, which is defined as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. A set
Y\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
X\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
is called monotone, if

\Re\langle x-u,y-v\rangle\geq 0,\qquad{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right]},{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}u\\
v\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in Y.

Further,
Y\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
X\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
is called maximal monotone, if it is monotone and not a proper subset of a monotone subset of
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
X\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
.
A (possibly nonlinear) operator
A\colon\dom(A)\subset X\rightarrow X
is called (maximal) monotone, if the graph of
A
, i.e.,
\left\{{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
Ax\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\left|\vphantom{{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
Ax\end{smallmatrix}\right]}}\,x\in\dom(A)\right.\right\}
, is (maximal) monotone.

A set
Y\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
X\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
is called dissipative, if

\Re\langle x,y\rangle\leq 0,\qquad{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
y\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\in Y.

Further,
Y\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
X\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
is called maximal dissipative, if it is dissipative and not a proper subset of a dissipative subset of
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
X\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
.
A (possibly nonlinear) operator
A\colon\dom(A)\subset X\rightarrow X
is called (maximal) dissipative, if the graph of
A
, i.e.,
\left\{{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
Ax\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\left|\vphantom{{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
Ax\end{smallmatrix}\right]}}\,x\in\dom(A)\right.\right\}
, is (maximal) dissipative.

\Box

Here are some simple implications and equivalences of this property.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
A\colon\dom(A)\subset X\rightarrow X
be an operator.

(i)

If
A
is linear then
A
is (maximal) dissipative if, and only if,
-A
is (maximal) monotone.

(ii)

Assume that
A
is monotone.
It follows from the definition of monotonicity that
\mathrm{I}+\lambda A
is injective for all
\lambda>0
.

Moreover, the Minty–Browder theory yields the equivalence of the following three statements, see, e.g.,
(
Barbu 2010
, Theorem 2.2 & p. 34)
:

(i)

A
is maximal monotone.

(ii)

\mathrm{I}+\lambda A
is surjective for some
\lambda>0
.

(iii)

\mathrm{I}+\lambda A
is surjective for all
\lambda>0
.

Consequently, if
A
is maximal monotone, then
\mathrm{I}+\lambda A
is bijective for all
\lambda>0
. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields that

\|(\mathrm{I}+\lambda A)x-(\mathrm{I}+\lambda A)y\|\geq\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in\dom(A),

hence
(\mathrm{I}+\lambda A)^{-1}\colon X\rightarrow X
is contractive.

Furthermore,
(\mathrm{I}-\lambda A)(\mathrm{I}+{\lambda}A)^{-1}
is contractive.
This follows with
\tilde{x}\coloneqq(\mathrm{I}+{\lambda}A)^{-1}x
and
\tilde{y}\coloneqq(\mathrm{I}+{\lambda}A)^{-1}y
from

\displaystyle\|x-y\|^{2}-\|(\mathrm{I}-\lambda A)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{I}+{\lambda}A)^{-1}x-(\mathrm{I}-\lambda A)(\mathrm{I}+{\lambda}A)^{-1}y\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}+{\lambda}A\tilde{x}-{\lambda}A\tilde{y}\|^{2}-\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}-(\lambda A\tilde{x}-\lambda A\tilde{y})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Re\lambda\langle\tilde{x}-\tilde{y},A\tilde{x}-A\tilde{y}\rangle\geq 0.

\Box

(iii)

Consequently, if
A
is linear and maximal dissipative, then
\lambda\mathrm{I}-A
is surjective for all
\lambda>0
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
S={\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\colon\dom(S)\subset{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\to{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X\\
U\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
be an operator with the properties as specified in Assumptions
2.1
.

(i)

Dissipativity of
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phantom{-}A\&B\\
-C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
directly implies that
A
is dissipative. Using Remark
4.2
together with
\rho(A)\cap{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\neq\emptyset
,

is even maximal dissipative. By the Lumer–Phillips theorem
(

, Chap. 2, Thm. 3.15)
, we obtain that

generates a strongly continuous semigroup. Consequently,

(ii)

It follows from
(

that
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phantom{-}A\&B\\

is maximal dissipative.

(iii)

The transfer function

of

is monotone (and thus maximal monotone as it is a bounded operator) for all

(iv)

The generalized (and thus also the classical) trajectories of (

\|x(t)\|_{X}^{2}\leq\|x(0)\|_{X}^{2}+2\int_{0}^{t}\Re\langle u(\tau),y(\tau)\rangle_{U}\,{\rm d}\tau\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

(11)

see
(
Staffans 2002
, Thm. 4.2)
.

Remark 6.2
.

To be precise, the output spaces are not equal to the corresponding input spaces but dual to them (as hinted in the definition of the trace spaces). Hence, we can identify them with each other via isomorphic dual mappings and still use our results.

Dissipativity follows from the definition of the trace operators, whereas the closedness claims follow from closedness of the divergence and gradient operators together with boundedness of the involved trace operators. Further, it can be shown that the main operators
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, resp., fulfill
\dom A_{1}=\dom A_{1}^{*}
,
\dom A_{2}=\dom A_{2}^{*}
and

A_{i}^{*}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}p_{i}\\
q_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-\Div(Tq_{i})-d\rho^{-1}p_{i}\\
-\grad(\rho^{-1}p_{i})\end{smallmatrix}\right],\quad i=1,2.

This shows that they are both maximally dissipative, whence they generate a strongly continuous semigroup on
X_{i}
.
Hence, given initial data and existence of a solution we can apply the presented splitting algorithm with the proven convergence properties. An additional advantage of this technique in this special case is that the decomposition into rectangles allows the usage of, say, spectral type methods to solve the two wave equations over rectangles separately, see, e.g.,
Cohen 2002
,
Kopriva 2009
.
