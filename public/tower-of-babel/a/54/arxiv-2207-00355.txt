Radix form in Hyperbolic and Dual numbers
Sayed Kossentini

Abstract
We investigate number systems for the ring of integers of hyperbolic and dual numbers. We characterize all canonical number systems providing radix form for hyperbolic and dual numbers. Our approach allows us to get suitable bases by means of Banach lattice algebra structure.



Key words
: Radix form, Hyperbolic numbers, Dual numbers, Canonical number system, Riesz algebra, Banach lattice.

1
Introduction

Number system for a natural number is a positional representation in any integral basis
b\geq 2
. The most commonly used are binary and decimal systems. Using a negative basis
b\leq-2
, every integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
has a unique representation of the form

n=a_{0}+\cdots+a_{l}b^{l},\quad a_{i}\in\{0,1,\cdots,|b|-1\}.

A natural extension of these concepts to the ring
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of integers of a number field
K
provides the following definition: let
\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\mathfrak{N}
be a complete residue system modulo
\gamma
(i.e. for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
there exists a unique
r\in\mathfrak{N}
such that
\alpha\equiv r~(\mathrm{mod}~\gamma)
), a pair
(\gamma,\mathfrak{N})
is a number system for
\mathcal{O}_{K}
if every
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
has the form

\alpha=r_{0}+r_{1}\gamma+\cdots+r_{k}\gamma^{k},\quad r_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}.

This representation is unique since
\mathfrak{N}
is a complete residue system modulo
\gamma
. The number
\gamma
is called base and
\mathfrak{N}
set of digits of the number system.
(\gamma,\mathfrak{N})
is called canonical number system if
\mathfrak{N}=\mathfrak{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\cdots|N^{K}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma)|-1\}
, where
N^{K}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)
is the field norm of
\alpha
. The first result in this topic is due to Knut
[
13
]
who discovered that
-1+\mathbf{i}
is a base for complex integers. Kátai and Szabó
[
10
]
proved that the only possibles canonical number systems in Gaussian integers are given by the basis
q=a\pm\mathbf{i}
with
a\leq-1
. They also proved that every complex number
z
can be expressed in the form

z=r_{0}+\cdots+r_{l}q^{l}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{i}}{q^{i}},\quad r_{k},s_{j}\in\mathfrak{N}_{0}

Their work was generalized to a quadratic number field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\rho)
by Kátai and Kovacs
[
11
]
and independently by Gilbert
[
9
]
. Kovacs and Peto
[
14
]
gave a characterization of all integral domains which have number systems. More recently, Peto and Thuswaldner
[
15
]
investigate number systems defined over orders of number fields

The present work deals with number systems for non integral domains. To be more precise, number systems for the ring of integers of dual and hyperbolic numbers ( also called duplex, double, perplex and split-complex numbers). These systems, together with complex numbers, are, up to an algebra isomorphism, the three possible two-dimensional real associative algebras, usually known as two-dimensional systems. During many years a number of researchers have been attracted by algebraic, geometrical properties and applications of the two-dimensional systems (see for instance
[
19
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
3
]
). In another direction, Wickstead
[
18
]
studied all possible Riesz algebra orderings on the two dimensional system using cones in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form

P^{\beta}_{\alpha}=\{(x,y);x\geq 0,\alpha x\leq y\leq\beta x\},~\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\},\alpha<\beta.

(1.1)

He proved that dual and hyperbolic numbers are the only two dimensional systems that can be made into Banach lattice algebras. He also studied their representations in the Riesz algebra of regular operator.

In this paper, we will characterize all canonical number systems providing radix form for hyperbolic and dual numbers. This can be done thanks to their Banach lattice algebra structure.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall basic properties of hyperbolic and dual numbers which we will use throughout this paper (for more details we refer the reader to
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
). For the used lattice concepts we recall some notions about Riesz algebras and
f
-algebras (see for instance
[
20
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
17
]
).

A Riesz space is a real linear space
L
that is also a lattice ordered set (i.e. the supremum
u\vee v
and the infimum
u\wedge v
exist in
L
for all
u,v\in L
), and the partial order satisfies:
u\leq v\Longrightarrow u+w\leq v+w
for all
w\in L
and
\alpha u\leq\alpha v
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The set
L^{+}
of all positive elements of
L
(i.e.
u\geq 0
) is called positive cone of
L
. The absolute value of an element
a
in a Riesz space
L
is defined as
|u|=u\vee(-u)
.

A real associative algebra ( with usual operations) is called Riesz algebra if its underlying linear space is a Riesz space with positive cone closed under multiplication. A Banach lattice algebra is a Riesz algebra equipped by a lattice norm (i.e.
|u|\leq|v|\Rightarrow\|u\|\leq\|v\|
) which is also sub-multiplicative.

An
f
-algebra is a Riesz algebra
A
with the additional property: for any
u,v,w\in A^{+}
,
u\wedge v=0\Rightarrow wu\wedge v=u\wedge wv=0

2.1
Hyperbolic numbers

In this section we summarize results of
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
.
The associative real algebra of hyperbolic numbers is

\mathbb{D}:=\Big\{z=x+\mathbf{j}y:\,x,y\in\mathbb{R},\,\mathbf{j}\notin\mathbb{R};\,\mathbf{j}^{2}=1\Big\}.

The group of units
\mathbb{D}^{\times}
is characterized by all hyperbolic numbers
z
with
N_{\mathbb{D}}(z)\neq 0
, where

N_{\mathbb{D}}(z)=z\bar{z}=(\mathrm{Re}(z))^{2}-(\mathrm{Im}(z))^{2}.

The hyperbolic plane has an important basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
where

\mathbf{e}_{1}=\frac{1+\mathbf{j}}{2},\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}=\frac{1-\mathbf{j}}{2}\Rightarrow\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}=\mathbf{e}_{1};\quad\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{e}_{2};\quad\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2}=1;\quad\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}=0.

(2.1)

In this basis, each hyperbolic number
z
can be written as

z=\pi_{1}(z)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\pi_{2}(z)\mathbf{e}_{2},

(2.2)

where
\pi_{1}(x+\mathbf{j}y)=x+y
and
\pi_{2}(x+\mathbf{j}y)=x-y
. From the representation (
2.2
), algebraic operations correspond to coordinate-wise operations, the square norm
N_{\mathbb{D}}(z)
is the product
\pi_{1}(z)\pi_{2}(z)
and the conjugation
\bar{z}
is given by exchanging
\pi_{1}(z)\leftrightarrow\pi_{2}(z)
. Moreover,
\mathbb{D}
is an Archimedean
f
-algebra
[
5
]
with positive cone
\mathbb{D}^{+}=\{z:\pi_{1}(z),\pi_{2}(z)\geq 0\}
. According to (
1.1
),
\mathbb{D}^{+}=\{z=x+\mathbf{j}y:(x,y)\in P^{1}_{-1}\}
.

The partial order
\leq
on
\mathbb{D}
is then

z,w\in\mathbb{D};\,z\leq w\mbox{~if and only if~}\pi_{k}(z)\leq\pi_{k}(w),\quad k=1,2.

(2.3)

A Banach lattice algebra norm on
\mathbb{D}
is given by

\|z\|_{\mathbb{D}}:=\min\Big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}:\,\alpha\geq|z|\Big\}=|z|\vee\overline{|z|}.

It is the supremum norm in the idempotent basis
(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2})
. In the standard basis
(1,\mathbf{j})
it is given by
\|x+\mathbf{j}y\|_{\mathbb{D}}=\max\{|x+y|,|x-y|\}
.

We now recall some results about the ring of hyperbolic integers
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
(see
[
6
]
). It is the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\mathbf{j})=\{z=x+\mathbf{j}y:\,x,y\in\mathbb{Q}\}
given by

\mathcal{Z}_{h}:=\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

(2.4)

The units of
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
are
\{1,-1,\mathbf{j},-\mathbf{j}\}
, and each hyperbolic integer
\upsilon
satisfies the identity

\upsilon^{2}-2\mathrm{Re}(\upsilon)\upsilon+N_{\mathbb{D}}(\upsilon)=0.

(2.5)

Divisibility in
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
is defined in a natural way. We say that
\upsilon
divides
\omega
, write
\upsilon|\omega
, if
\omega=\mu\upsilon
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}
. The congruence relation
\upsilon\equiv\mu\mbox{~mod~}\omega
is defined as
\omega|\upsilon-\mu
. From (
2.4
), divisibility in
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
is component-wise. The ordering (
2.3
) on
\mathbb{D}
induces the same ordering on
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
.

One can extend usual division algorithm to hyperbolic integers; that is, for every pair
(\upsilon,\omega)\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}\times\mathcal{Z}_{h}
with
N_{\mathbb{D}}(\omega)\neq 0
there exists a unique
(\tau,\phi)\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}\times\mathcal{Z}_{h}
such that

\upsilon=\tau+\phi\omega,\quad 0\leq\tau\leq|\omega|-1.

(2.6)

Thus, the size of the group
\mathcal{Z}_{h}/\omega\mathcal{Z}_{h}
, (
N_{\mathbb{D}}(\omega)\neq 0
) is

|\mathcal{Z}_{h}/\omega\mathcal{Z}_{h}|=\mathrm{card}~\{\nu\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}:\,0\leq\nu\leq|\omega|-1\}=N_{\mathbb{D}}(|\omega|).

(2.7)

Just as complex numbers,
hyperbolic Gaussian integers
are defined by

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}=\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{j}]=\{x+\mathbf{j}y:x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

It is a subring of
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
formed by the hyperbolic integers
\upsilon
such that
\upsilon\equiv 0\mathrm{~mod~}2
or
\upsilon\equiv 1\mathrm{~mod~}2
. It is worth noticing here that
the four binary classes are

\mathcal{Z}_{h}/2\mathcal{Z}_{h}=\{\hat{0},\hat{1},\widehat{\mathbf{e}_{1}},\widehat{\mathbf{e}_{2}}\}.

2.2
Dual numbers

The set of dual numbers
\mathbb{A}
is defined in the same way as hyperbolic and complex numbers but with nilpotent imaginary unit; that is

\mathbb{A}:=\Big\{z=x+\varepsilon y:\,x,y\in\mathbb{R},\,\varepsilon\notin\mathbb{R};\,\varepsilon^{2}=0\Big\}.

The group of units
\mathbb{A}^{\times}
is characterized by all dual numbers
z
with
N_{\mathbb{A}}(z)\neq 0
, where

N_{\mathbb{A}}(z)=z\bar{z}=(\mathrm{Re}(z))^{2}.\\

Thus
z=x+\varepsilon y\in\mathbb{A}^{\times}
if and only if
x\neq 0
and in this case the inverse of
z
is

z^{-1}=\frac{1}{x}-\varepsilon\frac{y}{x^{2}}.

Accoring to
[
18
, Proposition 2.1]
, Riesz algebra orderings on
\mathbb{A}
are given by the cones
P^{\infty}_{\alpha}
,
(\alpha\in\left[0,\infty\right))
and
P^{\beta}_{-\infty}

(\beta\in\left(-\infty,0\right])
. The conjugation operator
\sigma(z)=\bar{z}
is an order and algebra isomorphism between
(\mathbb{A},P^{\infty}_{0})
and
(\mathbb{A},P^{0}_{-\infty})
, where
P^{\infty}_{0}=\{z=x+\varepsilon y:\,x,y\geq 0\}
.

In the sequel,
\mathbb{A}
is equipped by the Riesz algebra structure with positive cone
P^{\infty}_{0}
, and so the absolute value of a dual number
x+\varepsilon y
is given by
|x+\varepsilon y|=|x|+\varepsilon|y|
. Further, if we let
\|.\|
denote the lattice euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
then (by
[
18
, Proposition 6.1]
) a Banach lattice algebra norm in
\mathbb{A}
is given by

\|z\|_{\mathbb{A}}=\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{\|\mathrm{x}\|=1}\|\mathcal{M}(z).\mathrm{x}\|,\quad\mathcal{M}(x+\varepsilon y)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&0\\
y&x\texttimes\end{array}\right).

Therefore, an explicit expression of the norm
\|x+\varepsilon y\|_{\mathbb{A}}
is given by

\|x+\varepsilon y\|_{\mathbb{A}}=\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\left[0,2\pi\right]}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+xy\sin 2\theta}.

(2.8)

Just as Gaussian integers,
dual Gaussian integers
are defined by

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathbb{Z}[\varepsilon]=\{z=x+\varepsilon y:\,x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

It is a ring of quadratic integers in dual numbers, and each
\upsilon\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}
satisfies the identity

\upsilon^{2}-2\mathrm{Re}(\upsilon)\upsilon+N_{\mathbb{A}}(\upsilon)=0.

(2.9)

3
Number systems in hyperbolic integers

This section characterize all number systems in hyperbolic integers by considering the complete residue systems

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{h}=\{\nu\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}:\ 0\leq\nu\leq|q|-1\},

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{D}}_{0}=\{0,1,\cdots N_{\mathbb{D}}(|q|)-1\}.

(3.2)

The number system
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{h})
appears to be the natural generalization of the usual number systems. Indeed, given an integral basis
q
then by uniqueness of the representation, the digits
\nu_{i}(n)\in\mathfrak{N}_{h}
of an ordinary integer
n
must be natural numbers, since
\overline{\nu_{i}(n)}=\nu_{i}(n)
for each
i
, and selected from the set
\{0,1,\cdots,|q|-1\}
.

3.1
Number Systems
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{h})

In view of the hyperbolic algorithm division (
2.6
), the set
\mathfrak{N}_{h}
is a complete residue system modulo
q
if and only if it contains at least two digits, i.e. if and only if
N_{\mathbb{D}}(|q|)\geq 2
. For example, the bases
q
with
|q|=2
provide the four idempotent digits
0
,
1
,
\mathbf{e}_{1}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

A pair
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{h})
is a number system for
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
if and only if
q\leq-2
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{h})
is a number system for
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
and let
\upsilon
be an arbitrary hyperbolic integer. Then

\upsilon=\nu_{0}+\cdots+\nu_{k}q^{k},\quad\nu_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}_{h}.

(3.3)

Assuming that
q\notin\mathbb{D}^{-}
(the set of negative hyperbolic numbers), then there exists
j\in\{1,2\}
such that
b=\pi_{j}(q)>0
. Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and take
\upsilon=n\mathbf{e}_{j}
. By (
3.3
) we have

n=a_{0}+\cdots+a_{k}b^{k},\quad a_{i}=\pi_{j}(\nu_{i})\in\mathbb{N}.

This yields a contradiction, and so
q
must be negative with
q\leq-2
since
N_{\mathbb{D}}(|q|)\geq 2
.

Conversely, let
q
be an hyperbolic integer with
q\leq-2
and
\upsilon
be an arbitrary integer, and let
n_{i}=\pi_{i}(\upsilon)
,
b_{i}=\pi_{i}(q)

(i=1,2)
. The
b_{i}
are integral bases with
b_{i}\leq-2
. It follows from usual number systems that we have

n_{1}=r_{0}+r_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+r_{l}b_{1}^{l},\quad r_{i}\in\{0,1,\cdots,|b_{1}|-1\}

and

n_{2}=s_{0}+s_{1}b_{2}+\cdots+s_{l}b_{2}^{l},\quad s_{i}\in\{0,1,\cdots,|b_{2}|-1\},

where some of the leading digits may be zero. Hence,

\upsilon=\nu_{0}+\nu_{1}q+\cdots+\nu_{l}q^{l},\quad\nu_{i}=r_{i}\mathbf{e}_{1}+s_{i}\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\mathfrak{N}_{h}.

∎

3.2
Canonical Number Systems
(q,\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{D}}_{0})

We will formulate the analogue of canonical number systems for complex integers in the setting of hyperbolic integers by considering
\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{D}}_{0}
(
3.2
) as digits.

Theorem 3.2
.

A pair
(q,\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{D}}_{0})
is a canonical number system for
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
if and only if

q\leq 0,\quad N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)\geq 2\mbox{~and~}2\mathrm{Im}(q)=\pm 1.

(3.4)

Proof.

Suppose that
\left(q,\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}\right)
is a number system for
\mathcal{Z}_{h}
and let
\upsilon
be an arbitrary hyperbolic integer. Then
\upsilon
has the form

\upsilon=r_{0}+\cdots+r_{k}q^{k},\quad r_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}.

(3.5)

A similar reasoning as Theorem
3.1
shows that
q\leq 0
with
N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)\geq 2
. Note that

2\mathrm{Im}(q^{n})=\left(\pi_{1}(q)\right)^{n}-\left(\pi_{2}(q)\right)^{n},\quad(n=1,2,\cdots).

(3.6)

Then (
3.6
) together with (
3.5
) yields that
\mathrm{Im}(2q)|\mathrm{Im}2\upsilon
. Take
2\upsilon=1+\mathbf{j}
we get
2\mathrm{Im}(q)|1
, and so
2\mathrm{Im}(q)=\pm 1
.

Conversely, let
q=a\mathbf{e}_{1}+b\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}
satisfying (
3.4
). Assume that
2\mathrm{Im}(q)=a-b=1
(the other case follows by considering the conjugate
\bar{q}
, i.e. by exchanging
a\leftrightarrow b
). Thus

q=a\mathbf{e}_{1}+(a-1)\mathbf{e}_{2},\quad a\leq 0,\mbox{~and}~a^{2}-a\geq 2.

(3.7)

Let
\upsilon=n_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+n_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}
,
n=n_{1}-n_{2}
and
m=n_{1}-(n_{1}-n_{2})a.
Then

\upsilon=m+nq,\quad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(3.8)

At this step we sketch the main idea and technique of the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 1]
to adapt it in the setting of hyperbolic integers. We first prove that
\upsilon
can be written as

\upsilon=u_{0}+u_{1}q+u_{2}q^{2}+u_{3}q^{3},\quad u_{i}\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.9)

To do this, we will use the identity which holds for each
c\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
:

c=|c|\left(q^{2}-(2a-1)q+a^{2}-a+\frac{c}{|c|}\right).

(3.10)

(Note that (
3.10
) is a direct consequence of (
2.5
)).

One can clearly assume, without loss of generality, that
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Identity (
3.7
) shows that
-(2a-1)
and
a^{2}-a+\frac{c}{|c|}
are positive integers for every
c\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then (
3.9
) holds from (
3.10
) by substituting in (
3.8
)
n
and
m
by

|n|\left(q^{2}-(2a-1)q+a^{2}-a+\frac{n}{|n|}\right)

and

|m|\left(q^{2}-(2a-1)q+a^{2}-a+\frac{m}{|m|}\right).

It follows from (
3.9
), that there exists an integer
k\geq 3
such that

\upsilon=d_{0}+d_{1}q+\cdots+d_{k}q^{k},\quad d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.11)

Let
r_{0}\in\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
d_{0}=r_{0}+t_{0}N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)
. Observing that

N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)=q^{3}-2aq^{2}+(a^{2}+a-1)q=a^{2}-a,

one gets from (
3.11
)

\upsilon=r_{0}+\upsilon_{1}q,\quad\upsilon_{1}=d^{(0)}_{1}+\cdots+d^{(0)}_{k}q^{k-1},

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle d_{1}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{1}+(a^{2}+a-1)t_{0},

\displaystyle d_{2}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{2}-2at_{0},~d_{3}^{(0)}=d_{3}+t_{0},

\displaystyle d_{j}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{j},\quad 4\leq j\leq k.

Summing the
d_{j}^{(0)}
over
\{1,2,3,\cdots,k\}
, one has

d_{1}^{(0)}+d_{2}^{(0)}+d_{3}^{(0)}+\cdots+d^{(0)}_{k}=t_{0}N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)+d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}+\cdots+d_{k}.

(3.13)

Let
\sigma(\upsilon)=d_{0}+\cdots+d_{k}
and
\sigma(\upsilon_{1})=d_{1}^{(0)}+\cdots+d^{(0)}_{k}
.
Since
d_{0}=r_{0}+t_{0}N_{\mathbb{N}}(q)
, then from (
3.13
) we have

\sigma(\upsilon)=r_{0}+\sigma(\upsilon_{1}).

(3.14)

Repeating the algorithms (
3.12
) and (
3.14
), we get

\upsilon_{i}=r_{i}+\upsilon_{i+1}q;\quad\sigma(\upsilon_{i})=r_{i}+\sigma(\upsilon_{i+1}),~~r_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}},

(3.15)

where
\sigma(\upsilon)\geq\sigma(\upsilon_{1})\geq\sigma(\upsilon_{2})\geq\cdots\geq 0
. This means that there exists an integer
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that the sequence
(\sigma(\upsilon_{i}))
is constant for
i\geq l
, which implies from (
3.15
) that
q^{i-l}
divides
\upsilon^{l}
for all
i\geq l
. This holds only if
\upsilon_{l}=0
; that is, the algorithm finishes at the step
l
. Hence

\upsilon=r_{0}+\cdots+r_{l-1}q^{l-1}.

It remains to prove that
\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
is a complete residue system modulo
q
, i.e.
\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
has no two elements congruent modulo
q
. Let
s,r\in\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
be such that
s\equiv r\mbox{~mod~}q
,i.e.
s-r=\kappa q
for some
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}_{h}
. Then

|s-r|=|\kappa q|\leq a^{2}-a-1.

(3.16)

As
|s-r|\in\mathbb{N}
means that
|\kappa||q|=\pi_{1}(|\kappa q|)=\pi_{2}(|\kappa q|)
, we get

|\kappa||q|=-a\pi_{1}(|\kappa|)=(1-a)\pi_{2}(|\kappa|).

(3.17)

Since
(-a,1-a)=1
, then (
3.17
) yields that
\pi_{2}(|\kappa|)=n(-a)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and then
\pi_{1}(|\kappa|)=n(1-a)
. Therefore, from (
3.16
) we obtain

n(a^{2}-a)\leq a^{2}-a-1.

This holds only if
n=0
, since
a^{2}-a\geq 2
, and so
\pi_{1}(|\kappa|)=\pi_{2}(|\kappa|)=0
, which means
\kappa=0
. Hence
s=r
and this completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
3.3
shows that every hyperbolic Gaussian integer has a unique representation of the form (
3.5
). However, the bases
q
are not a hyperbolic Gaussian integers, since
2\mathrm{Im}(q)=\pm 1
. The next theorem characterizes all possible bases
q\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}
for which
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}})
is a canonical number system for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
q
be an hyperbolic Gaussian integer. Then,
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}})
is a canonical number system for hyperbolic Gaussian integers if and only if
q=a\pm\mathbf{j}
with
a\leq-2
.

Proof.

The proof is entirely analogous to that of Theorem
3.1
. A base
q
must be negative with
N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)\geq 2
and ( by
\mathrm{Im}(q)|\mathrm{Im}(q^{n}),~n=1,2,\cdots
)
\mathrm{Im}(q)=\pm 1
. Thus,
q=a\pm\mathbf{j}
with
a\leq 0
and
a^{2}\geq 3
; that is,
a\leq-2
. Conversely, we have
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}=\mathbb{Z}[q]
for each basis
q=a\pm\mathbf{j}
. Therefore, using the identities

c=|c|\left(q^{2}-2aq+a^{2}-1+\frac{c}{|c|}\right),\quad c\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}

and

N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)=q^{3}-(2a+1)q^{2}+(a^{2}+2a-1)q,

a similar reasoning shows that each of the bases
q=a\pm\mathbf{j}

(a\leq-2)
can be used to represent hyperbolic Gaussian integers with
\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}=\{0,1,\cdots,a^{2}-2\}
as digits set.

We prove now that
\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
is a complete residue system modulo
q
. Suppose that
q=a+\mathbf{j}
(the other case follows by considering the conjugate
\bar{q}
), and let
s,r\in\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
be such that
s\equiv r\mbox{~{mod}~}q
, i.e.
s-r=\kappa q
for some
\kappa\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}
. Then

N_{\mathbb{D}}(\kappa)N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)=(s-r)^{2}\leq(a^{2}-2)^{2}.

(3.18)

As
\pi_{1}(\kappa q)=\pi_{2}(\kappa q)
, one obtains

\mathrm{Re}(\kappa)=-a\mathrm{Im}(\kappa).

(3.19)

Thus,
N_{\mathbb{D}}(\kappa)N_{\mathbb{D}}(q)=(a^{2}-1)^{2}\mathrm{Im}(\kappa)^{2}
. Since
a^{2}>2
, one gets by (
3.18
),
(a^{2}-1)|\mathrm{Im}(\kappa)|\leq a^{2}-2
. This holds only if
\mathrm{Im}(\kappa)=0
, i.e. by (
3.19
),
\kappa=0
. Hence
s=r
.
∎

4
Canonical Number systems in dual integers

In this section we characterize all canonical number systems
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{A}})
in dual integers, where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{A}}_{0}=\{0,1,\cdots,N_{\mathbb{A}}(q)-1\}=\{0,1,\cdots,\left(\mathrm{Re}(q)\right)^{2}-1\}.

Theorem 4.1
.

(q,\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{A}}_{0})
is a number system in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}
if and only if
q=a\pm\varepsilon
with
a\leq-2
.

Proof.

Let
q=a+\varepsilon b\in\mathcal{G}_{A}
be such that
(q,\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{A}}_{0})
is a number system in
\mathcal{G}_{A}
. Then each dual Gaussian integer
\upsilon
can be expressed as

\upsilon=r_{0}+r_{1}q+\cdots+r_{k}q^{k},\quad r_{j}\in\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{A}}_{0}.

(4.1)

Similarly, we have
b=\mathrm{Im}(q)=\pm 1
. Suppose that
a\geq 0
. It follows from (
4.1
) and the identity
(a+\varepsilon b)^{n}=a^{n}+\varepsilon na^{n-1}

(n=1,2,\cdots)
, that we have

\mathrm{Re}(\upsilon)=r_{0}+r_{1}a+\cdots+r_{k}a^{n}\geq 0.

This is not possible for every
\upsilon
, and so we must have
a<0
which implies
a\leq-2
, since
N(q)=a^{2}\geq 2
..

The converse is proved using the same method of Theorem
3.1
by considering the corresponding identities derived from (
2.9
); that is,

c=|c|\left(q^{2}-2aq+a^{2}+\frac{c}{|c|}\right),\quad c\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

and

N_{\mathbb{A}}(q)=q^{3}-(2a+1)q^{2}+(a^{2}+2a)q.

To complete the proof we show that
\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{A}}_{0}=\{0,1,\cdots,a^{2}-1\}
is a complete residue system modulo
q
for a basis
q=a+\mu\varepsilon
with
a\leq-2
,
\mu=\pm 1
. Let
r,s\in\mathfrak{N}^{\mathbb{A}}_{0}
be such that
r\equiv s\mbox{~{mod}~}q
, i.e.
s-r=\kappa q
for some
\kappa\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}
. Write
\kappa=k_{1}+\varepsilon k_{2}
, then
k_{1}a=r-s
and
k_{1}\mu+k_{2}a=0
, which implies that
r-s=-\mu k_{2}a^{2}
and then
|k_{2}|a^{2}\leq a^{2}-1
. This holds only is
k_{2}=0
. Which gives
r=s
.
∎

5
Radix representations

In this section we will prove that every hypercomplex number
z\in\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{A}
or
\mathbb{D}
is representable in radix form with respect to number systems
(q,\mathfrak{N})
in the integers
Z_{\mathbb{H}}
of
\mathbb{H}
, where
\mathfrak{N}=\mathfrak{N}_{h},\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{D}}
if
Z_{\mathbb{D}}=\mathcal{Z}_{h},\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}
and
\mathfrak{N}=\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{A}}
if
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{A}}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}
. The corresponding bases are characterized by Theorems
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
and
4.1
. Our approach is based on the structure of
Z_{\mathbb{H}}
as a closed (for usual topology) full lattice of the plane
\mathbb{H}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2}
and on a topological property of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{H}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}
, where

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{H}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}:=\{z\in\mathbb{H}:z=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{i}}{q^{i}},~\mu_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}\}.

(5.1)

The corresponding domain in complex number is an arcwise-connected compact set with a fractal boundary (see
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
1
]
). For our purpose we need only to prove compactness of (
5.1
).

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(q,\mathfrak{N})
be a number system in
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{H}}
(with
q\leq-2
if
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{D}
). Then
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{H}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}
is a compact set for usual topology of
\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

Let
(q,\mathfrak{N})
be a number system in
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Suppose first that
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{D}
. One then has
\|q^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{D}}<1
, since
q\leq-2
. This yields that the series
\sum\mu_{i}q^{-i}
is absolute convergent for every sequence
\mu=(\mu_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of elements of
\mathfrak{N}
. Let

(z_{n})
be a sequence of elements of
\mathcal{F}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}
. Then

z_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{(n)}_{i}}{q^{i}},~\mu^{(n)}_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}.

By compactness of
\mathfrak{N}
we get a subsequence
(\mu^{(n_{k})}_{i})
such that
\mu^{(n_{k})}_{i}
converges to
\mu_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}

(i=1,2,\cdots)
. Put

z=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{i}}{q^{i}}

Therefore,

\|z_{n_{k}}-z\|_{\mathbb{D}}\leq\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 1}\|\mu^{(n_{k})}_{i}-\mu_{i}\|_{\mathbb{D}}\|q^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{D}}\|(1-\|q^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{D}})^{-1}.

This means that there is a subsequence
(z_{n_{k}})
of
(z_{n})
that converges to
z
and this proves the compactness of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{D}}{(q,\mathfrak{N})}
.

In the same manner, one can show the compactness of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{A}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{A}})}
provided that
\|q^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{A}}<1
, where
\|.\|_{\mathbb{A}}
is the Banach lattice algebra norm on
\mathbb{A}
(
2.8
). Indeed, from Theorem
4.1
,
(q,\mathfrak{N}_{0}^{\mathbb{A}})
is a number system in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{A}}
if and only if
q=a\pm\varepsilon
with
a\leq-2
, and then
|q^{-1}|=\frac{1}{|a|}+\frac{\varepsilon}{a^{2}}
with
|a|\geq 2
, that is
|q^{-1}|\leq\frac{1}{|a|}(1+\varepsilon)
. From (
2.8
), one has

\|1+\varepsilon\|_{\mathbb{A}}=\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\left[0,2\pi\right]}\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}\theta+\sin 2\theta}\leq\sqrt{3},

and then

\|q^{-1}\|_{\mathbb{A}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{|a|}<1,

as desired.
∎

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(q,\mathfrak{N})
be a number system in
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{H}}
(with
q\leq-2
if
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{D}
). Then every
z\in\mathbb{H}
can be expressed in the form

z=\eta_{0}+\cdots+\eta_{k}q^{k}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{i}}{q^{i}}\quad\eta_{j},\mu i\in\mathfrak{N}.

(5.2)

Proof.

Let
(q,\mathfrak{N})
be a number system in
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{H}}
, and
z\in\mathbb{H}
. As in
[
10
, Theorem 2]
the key idea of the proof is to use the decomposition
\mathbb{H}=Z_{\mathbb{H}}+P_{Z_{\mathbb{H}}}
, where
P_{Z_{\mathbb{H}}}\subset\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\,0\leq z\leq 1\}
is the fundamental parallelepiped of the full lattice
Z_{\mathbb{H}}
in the plane
\mathbb{H}
. Then for every integer
n\geq 1
we have

q^{n}z=\upsilon_{n}+\delta_{n};\quad\upsilon_{n}\in Z_{\mathbb{H}},~\delta_{n}\in P_{Z_{\mathbb{H}}}.

Since
(q,\mathfrak{N})
is a number system in
\mathbb{Z}_{\mathbb{H}}
,
\upsilon_{n}
can be written as

\upsilon_{n}=\nu_{0}+\cdots+\nu_{d}q^{d},~d=d(n),~\quad\nu_{i}\in\mathfrak{N}.

Let
s(n)=\max\{d(n),n\}
and let
(\nu_{i}^{(n)})
be such that
\nu_{i}^{(n)}=\nu_{n-i}
if
i\leq n
and
\nu_{i}^{(n)}=0
, otherwise. Therefore, for every integer
n\geq 1
one has

z=\vartheta_{n}+\varsigma_{n}+\theta_{n},

(5.3)

where

\vartheta_{n}=\nu_{n}+\cdots\nu_{s(n)}q^{s(n)-n},~~~\varsigma_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\nu^{(n)}_{i}}{q^{i}},~~~\theta_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n}}{q^{n}}.

As
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{H}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}
is a compact set (with
q\leq-2
if
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{D}
), we get a subsequence
\varsigma_{n_{k}}
that converges to
\varsigma\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{H}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}
. Then, from (
5.3
) we have for every
k

z=\vartheta_{n_{k}}+\varsigma_{n_{k}}+\theta_{n_{k}}.

It’s clear that
\theta_{n_{k}}\rightarrow 0
, since
\theta_{n}\rightarrow 0
. And since
Z_{\mathbb{H}}
is a closed set (for usual topology), the sequence
(\vartheta_{n_{k}})
converges to
\vartheta\in Z_{\mathbb{H}}
. Hence

z=\vartheta+\varsigma,\quad\vartheta\in Z_{\mathbb{H}},\;\;\varsigma\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathbb{H}}_{(q,\mathfrak{N})}.

This completes the proof.
∎
