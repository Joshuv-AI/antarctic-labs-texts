Nuclear reactions in artificial traps
Peng Guo, Bingwei Long

Abstract
Coupled-channel two-particle systems bound by a harmonic trap are discussed in the present paper. We derive the formula that relates the energy levels of such trapped systems to phase shifts and inelasticity of coupled-channel reactions. The formula makes it possible to extract amplitudes of inelastic nuclear reactions from ab initio calculations of discrete levels of many-nucleon systems in a harmonic trap.



I
Introduction

Studies of nuclear structure have entered a stage where bound states of many nucleons can be solved for with microscopic nuclear forces and with high precision
Navratil et al. 2007
;
Epelbaum et al. 2009
;
Ekström et al. 2013
;
Barrett et al. 2013
;
Lynn et al. 2014
;
Ekström et al. 2015
;
Piarulli et al. 2018
;
Hammer et al. 2020
. For some of those many-body solvers, the computation typically involves expansion of wave functions on a chosen basis, e.g., the harmonic-oscillator (HO) basis. This poses a challenge for immediate extension of basis-expansion methods to continuum-state problems like resonances and reactions. The need for synergy of bound-state and continuum computational methods has been recognized by the nuclear-theory community
Navrátil et al. 2016
;
Yang 2016
;
Johnson et al. 2019
;
Mazur et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2020
.

One of these ab initio methods for elastic scattering of two clusters, normally stable nuclei, utilizes the energy spectrum of all the constituent nucleons, trapped by an artificial field, to generate directly the phase shifts at certain discrete energies decided by the spectrum of the trapped
Rotureau et al. 2010
;
Rotureau et al. 2012
;
Luu et al. 2010
;
Zhang et al. 2020
. This method is mostly enabled by a set of so-called quantization conditions (QCs) that map energy levels to the phase shifts at momenta related to those energy eigenvalues
Busch et al. 1998
;
Zhang 2020
. The harmonic trap is an attractive choice for the artificial field because the HO wave functions are analytically known, so such mapping formulas can be presented in closed forms to a great extent. Existing vast resource of software for the HO basis is also an important incentive
Navratil et al. 2000
. After the pioneering work in Ref.
Busch et al. 1998
to connect the spectrum of the trapped two-body systems to their elastic scattering amplitude, referred to as the BERW formula in the present paper, more theoretical works to extend the formula and applications to nucleon-nucleon and nucleon-cluster scattering followed
Stetcu et al. 2007
;
Stetcu et al. 2010
;
Rotureau et al. 2010
;
Rotureau et al. 2012
;
Luu et al. 2010
;
Yang 2016
;
Zhang 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
. Here we turn our attention to coupled-channel problems where at least one of the two participating particles changes its species after the reaction. This is relevant to nuclear reactions where the nuclei fuse and then break up to different nucleus than before.

Relating discrete energy spectrum of trapped composite particles to their scattering amplitudes has been a topic of great interests in other fields of strong-interaction physics. In these applications, the form of traps may differ, such as periodic cubic boxes in lattice quantum chromodynamics (QCD)
Lüscher 1991
and spherical hard wall in some of the lattice implementations of chiral effective field theory
Bovermann et al. 2019
;
Elhatisari et al. 2016
;
Rokash et al. 2015
. Because the underlying theory to compute the energy spectrum is model-independent and the computation often consumes much computing resources, it is highly desired that the said connection is constructed without uncontrolled modeling of interactions between the composite particles.
This can be achieved when two scales are well separated; one is the wideness of the trap
b
and the other the interaction range between the composite particles
R
,
b\gg R
.
The separation makes it possible to locate a domain inside the trap where the composite particles do not interact appreciably while the confining force is sufficiently weak so that the trapped particles’ wave function resembles that of the scattering state in infinite volume. One then matches the asymptotic form of the scattering wave function, which has undetermined scattering parameters, say, phase shifts, to the trapped wave function.
Doing so pins down the phase shift at the particular energy associated with the trapped wave function. This yields the QC, a compact relation between the discrete energy eigenvalues and the scattering parameters, which often takes the following schematic form:

\det\left[\cot\delta(E)-g(E;b)\right]_{E=\mathcal{E}_{k}}=0\,,

(1)

where
\mathcal{E}_{k}
is the
k
-th the energy eigenvalue and

\delta(E)
refers to the diagonal matrix with phase shifts as the elements. The analytic matrix function

g(E;b)
depends only on the configuration of the trap, such as the functional form of the confining force and the wideness
b
, but not on detail of the inter-particle interactions.

Matching wave functions was the fundamental methodology used by Lüscher to derive his seminal QC formula for periodic cubic boxes
Lüscher 1991
.
Once we are convinced that the QC is irrespective of the interactions, manipulation of wave functions, however, does not have to be the ubiquitous technique of deriving the QC. Actively researched in the field of lattice QCD, other methods have been developed to tackle the problem (see, e.g., Refs.
Rummukainen and Gottlieb 1995
;
Christ et al. 2005
;
Bernard et al. 2008
;
He et al. 2005
;
Lage et al. 2009
;
Döring et al. 2011
;
Guo et al. 2013
;
Guo 2013
;
Kreuzer and Hammer 2009
;
Polejaeva and Rusetsky 2012
;
Hansen and Sharpe 2014
;
Mai and Döring 2017
;
Mai and Döring 2019
;
Döring et al. 2018
;
Guo 2017
;
Guo and Gasparian 2017
;
Guo and Gasparian 2018
;
Guo and Morris 2019
;
Guo et al. 2018
;
Guo 2020a
;
Guo and Döring 2020
;
Guo 2020b
;
Guo and Long 2020a
;
Guo 2020c
;
Guo and Long 2020b
;
Guo 2020d
;
Guo and Gasparian 2021
). We will follow the variational approach spelled out in Refs.
Guo 2020c
;
Guo 2020d
;
Guo and Gasparian 2021
. In particular, contact interactions are used in describing dynamics in both harmonic trap and infinite volume, and the QC is derived by eliminating the contact coupling.

The paper is organized as follows. To establish our notation, derivation of the BERW formula is discussed in Sec.
II

III

IV
.

II
BERW formula

Using the approach in Refs.
Guo 2020c
;
Guo 2020d
;
Guo and Gasparian 2021
, we re-derive the BERW formula for elastic scattering of two spinless particles in this section. We consider cases where the kinematics is such that the reaction does not takes places near threshold. So the long-range Coulomb repulsion, if any, can be neglected and one is left with only the strong interaction, which is finite-ranged.

II.1
Scattering dynamics

In infinite volume, the Lippmann-Schwinger (LS) equation for two-particle scattering has the following operator form,

\hat{T}_{\infty}(E)=-\hat{V}+\hat{V}\hat{G}_{\infty}(E)\hat{T}_{\infty}(E)\,,

(2)

where
\hat{T}_{\infty}
is the
T
-matrix,
E
the energy, and
\hat{V}
the short-range interaction between the clusters. The infinite-volume Green’s function is given in the operator form by

\hat{G}_{\infty}(E)=\frac{1}{E-\hat{H}_{0}}\,,

(3)

with
\hat{H}_{0}
defined in terms of the reduced mass
\mu
and the Laplacian:

\hat{H}_{0}=-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2\mu}\,.

(4)

The solution to the LS equation can be formally written as

\hat{T}_{\infty}(E)=-\left[\hat{V}^{-1}-\hat{G}_{\infty}(E)\right]^{-1}\,.

(5)

The on-shell elements of partial-wave projected
T
-matrix are usually parameterized in the center-of-mass (CM) frame by the phase shift
\delta_{L}
as

T^{(\infty)}_{L}(q,q)=\frac{(4\pi)^{2}}{2\mu q}\frac{1}{\cot\delta_{L}(q)-i}\,,

(6)

where
q=\sqrt{2\mu E}
is relative momentum of particles.

II.2
Two particles trapped by a HO potential

One may still define the
T
-matrix for the trapped two-particle system for any values of
E
, even though there are no scattering states:

\hat{T}_{\omega}(E)=-\hat{V}+\hat{V}\hat{G}_{\omega}(E)\hat{T}_{\omega}(E)\,,

(7)

where the Green’s function of the HO potential with angular frequency
\omega
is given by

\hat{G}_{\omega}(E)=\frac{1}{E-\hat{H}_{\omega}}=\sum_{n}\frac{|n\rangle\langle n|}{E-\omega(n+\frac{3}{2})}\,.

(8)

Here
\hat{H}_{\omega}
is the HO Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}_{\omega}=-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\mu\omega^{2}\mathbf{r}^{2}\,,

(9)

and
|n\rangle
stands for eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{\omega}
operator:

\hat{H}_{\omega}|n\rangle=\omega(n+\frac{3}{2})|n\rangle,\ \ \ \ n=0,1,2\cdots.

(10)

The wideness of the harmonic trap can be characterized by the so-called oscillator length:

b\equiv\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu\omega\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(11)

The bound state sustained by the harmonic trap can be determined by poles of
T_{\omega}(E)
, and

\hat{T}_{\omega}(E)=-\left[\hat{V}^{-1}-\hat{G}_{\omega}(E)\right]^{-1}\,.

(12)

Therefore, any eigenvalue of the full Hamiltonian
(\hat{H}_{\omega}+\hat{V})

E=\mathcal{E}_{k}
satisfies

\det\left[\hat{V}^{-1}-\hat{G}_{\omega}(E)\right]_{E=\mathcal{E}_{k}}=0\,.

(13)

II.3
Contact potentials and the BERW formula

Combining Eqs. (
5
) and (
13
) by eliminating
\hat{V}
,
one finds symbolically

\det\left[\hat{T}_{\infty}^{-1}(E)+\hat{G}_{\omega}(E)-\hat{G}_{\infty}(E)\right]_{E=\mathcal{E}_{k}}=0.

(14)

Although the above equation is formally correct, it is difficult to use in practical calculations because inverting
T_{\infty}(E)
directly is highly non-trivial.

As long as
R/b
is small, as we argued, a model-independent QC must exist. So one can use whatever form of two-body interaction to deduce the QC. We choose a sum of contact interactions, with one term responsible for each partial wave, to describe the cluster-cluster interaction:

V(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}\,)=\sum_{L}\frac{2L+1}{4\pi}(q^{\prime}q)^{L}V_{L}P_{L}(\hat{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{q}})\,,

(15)

where
P_{L}(x)
is the Legendre polynomial and
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
(
\mathbf{q}
) is the outgoing (incoming) momentum.

In the CM frame for infinite volume, the separable form of the potential in Eq.(
15
) allows for a closed-form partial-wave solution to Eq.(
5
) (see also Refs.
Guo 2020d
;
Guo and Gasparian 2021
):

\displaystyle-\frac{2\mu q^{2L+1}}{(4\pi)^{2}}\left[\cot\delta_{L}(q)-i\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{L}}-\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\,,

(16)

where

G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q)=-i2\mu qj_{L}(qr_{<})h_{L}^{(+)}(qr_{>})

is the free-particle Green’s function in the
L
-th partial wave.

In the harmonic trap, the separable form also facilitates a closed-form solution to Eq.(
13
):

\frac{1}{V_{L}}=\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{G^{(\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)})}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\,,

(17)

where
G^{(\omega)}_{L}
is the partial-wave HO Green’s function and is given in Ref.
Blinder 1984
by

\displaystyle G^{(\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)})=-\frac{1}{\omega(rr^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}}{2\omega})}{\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})}

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{M}_{\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}}{2\omega},\frac{L}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}(\mu\omega r^{2}_{<})\mathcal{W}_{\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}}{2\omega},\frac{L}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}(\mu\omega r^{2}_{>})\,.

\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{W}
are the Whittaker functions as defined in Ref.
Whittaker and Watson 1996
.
\epsilon^{(\omega)}
represents the energy shift relative to the HO levels and is related to relative momentum
q
by

\frac{q^{2}}{2\mu}=\epsilon^{(\omega)}+\omega(n+\frac{3}{2})=E\,.

where
\omega(n+\frac{3}{2})
is the CM energy in the harmonic trap, and the CM frame in infinite volume is assumed.

By eliminating
V_{L}
from Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
), and using asymptotic forms of partial-wave Green’s functions
G^{(\omega)}_{L}
and
G^{(\infty)}_{L}
, one obtains the BERW formula:

\cot\delta_{L}(q)=g_{L}(q),

(18)

where

g_{L}(q)=(-1)^{L+1}\left(\frac{4\mu\omega}{q^{2}}\right)^{L+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{4}+\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}}{2\omega})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}}{2\omega})}.

(19)

III
Two-channel reactions

For nuclear reactions where two nuclei fuse, break up, and rearrange, the BERW formula can not be immediately applied. Extension of the derivation in Sec.
II
is needed to extract inelastic scattering amplitudes. We focus on reactions that involve two channels and assume that each channel is made up of two distinguishable particles
A+B
and
C+D
. Labelling two channels by
1
and
2
, we can write, for instance, reaction
1\to 2
as

A+B\to C+D\,.

(20)

The kinetic energies in both channels are related by

\frac{\mathbf{q}_{1}^{2}}{2\mu_{1}}+\frac{\mathbf{P}^{2}}{2M_{1}}=\frac{\mathbf{q}_{2}^{2}}{2\mu_{2}}+\frac{\mathbf{P}^{2}}{2M_{2}}+\Delta=E\,,

(21)

where
\mathbf{P}
is the total momentum,
\mathbf{q}_{1,2}
relative momenta,
M_{1,2}
the total mass of each channel,

\Delta\equiv M_{2}-M_{1},

and
\mu_{1,2}
the reduced mass. For simplicity, we will assume the CM frame in what follows:
\mathbf{P}=0
.

III.1
Coupled-channel energy levels in a harmonic trap

We start with the coupled Schrödinger equations for two interacting spinless particles bound by the HO potential:

\left[\hat{H}_{\omega}+V(\mathbf{r})\right]\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{(1,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\\
\psi^{(2,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{1}&0\\
0&\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{(1,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\\
\psi^{(2,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix},

(22)

where

\hat{H}_{\omega}
is given by

\hat{H}_{\omega}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}}{2\mu_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\mu_{1}\omega^{2}\mathbf{r}^{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}}{2\mu_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}\omega^{2}\mathbf{r}^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(23)

Here
\mathbf{r}
is the relative coordinate and the potential matrix
V(\mathbf{r})
is short-ranged and describes inter-cluster interactions and transition between the channels:

V(\mathbf{r})=\begin{bmatrix}V^{(1,1)}(\mathbf{r})&V^{(1,2)}(\mathbf{r})\\
V^{(2,1)}(\mathbf{r})&V^{(2,2)}(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}.

(24)

\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{1}
and
\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{2}
are
energy shifts in their respective channel and are related to the full energy
E
by

\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{1}+\omega(n_{1}+\frac{3}{2})=\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{2}+\omega(n_{2}+\frac{3}{2})+\Delta=E\,.

The integral representation of Eq.(
22
) is the coupled LS equation:

\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{(1,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\\
\psi^{(2,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}=\int d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}G^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};E)V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{(1,\omega)}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\\
\psi^{(2,\omega)}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\end{bmatrix},

(25)

where
G^{(\omega)}
denotes the coupled-channel version of the HO Green’s function:

\displaystyle G^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};E)=\begin{bmatrix}G^{(1,\omega)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{1})&0\\
0&G^{(2,\omega)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{2})\end{bmatrix},

(26)

and
G^{(\alpha,\omega)}
satisfies

\left[\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}+\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}}{2\mu_{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{\alpha}\omega^{2}\mathbf{r}^{2}\right]G^{(\alpha,\omega)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha})=\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(27)

The partial-wave projection of the HO Green’s function is similar to the uncoupled-channel case:

G^{(\alpha,\omega)}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha})=\sum_{L}\frac{2L+1}{4\pi}G^{(\alpha,\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha})P_{L}(\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}^{\prime}),

(28)

where

\displaystyle G^{(\alpha,\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha})=-\frac{1}{\omega(rr^{\prime})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega})}{\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})}

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{M}_{\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega},\frac{L}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}(\mu_{\alpha}\omega r^{2}_{<})\mathcal{W}_{\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega},\frac{L}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}(\mu_{\alpha}\omega r^{2}_{>}).

(29)

We are going to take one more step to write the integral equation (
25
) in momentum space:

T^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\int\frac{d\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{d\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k})\widetilde{G}^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q};E)T^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{q}),

(30)

where
T^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
matrix is defined by

T^{(\omega)}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=-\int d\mathbf{r}e^{-i\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}}V(\mathbf{r})\begin{bmatrix}\psi^{(1,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\\
\psi^{(2,\omega)}(\mathbf{r})\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

\widetilde{V}
and
\widetilde{G}^{(\omega)}
are the Fourier transform of
V
and
G^{(\omega)}
, respectively.
The partial wave expansion of Eq.(
30
) yields

T^{(\omega)}_{L}(q^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2}dq}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{2}dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}\widetilde{V}_{L}(q^{\prime},k)\widetilde{G}^{(\omega)}_{L}(k,q;E)T^{(\omega)}_{L}(q).

(32)

With the assumption of separable potential,

\widetilde{V}_{L}(q^{\prime},q)=(q^{\prime}q)^{L}V_{L}\,,

(33)

where
V_{L}
is a constant
2\times 2
matrix, it follows that
T^{(\omega)}_{L}(q^{\prime})
matrix must be of the form

T^{(\omega)}_{L}(q)=q^{L}t^{(\omega)}_{L}\,,

where
t^{(\omega)}_{L}
matrix does not depend on
q
.
Therefore, the quantization condition is given by

\det\left[V_{L}^{-1}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2}dq}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{2}dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}(kq)^{L}\widetilde{G}^{(\omega)}_{L}(k,q;E)\right]=0.

(34)

Using the limiting form of the spherical Bessel functions

q^{L}=\frac{2^{L+1}\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{j_{L}(qr)}{r^{L}}|_{r\rightarrow 0}\,,

(35)

one can show that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2}dq}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{k^{2}dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}(kq)^{L}\widetilde{G}^{(\omega)}_{L}(k,q;E)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{G^{(\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\,.

(36)

Therefore, we can rewrite Eq.(
34
) as

\det\left[V_{L}^{-1}-\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{G^{(\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\right]=0,

(37)

which is just the generalization of Eq.(
17
) in the case of coupled-channel reactions.

III.2
Coupled-channel reaction amplitude

In infinite volume, the coupled-channel reaction is described by the two-channel LS equation:

\displaystyle T^{(\infty)}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{q})=-\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle+\int\frac{d\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\widetilde{V}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k})\widetilde{G}^{(\infty)}(\mathbf{k};E)T^{(\infty)}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})\,,

(38)

where
T^{(\infty)}
is a
2\times 2
matrix,

T^{(\infty)}=\begin{bmatrix}T^{((1,1),\infty)}&T^{((1,2),\infty)}\\
T^{((2,1),\infty)}&T^{((2,2),\infty)}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(39)

and the free Green’s function is also in matrix form,

\widetilde{G}^{(\infty)}(\mathbf{k};E)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\frac{q_{1}^{2}}{2\mu_{1}}-\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2\mu_{1}}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\frac{q_{2}^{2}}{2\mu_{2}}-\frac{\mathbf{k}^{2}}{2\mu_{2}}}\end{bmatrix},

(40)

with

\frac{q_{1}^{2}}{2\mu_{1}}=\frac{q_{2}^{2}}{2\mu_{2}}+\Delta=E\,.

The partial-wave expansion of Eq.(
38
) produces

\displaystyle T_{L}^{(\infty)}(q^{\prime},q)=-\widetilde{V}_{L}(q^{\prime},q)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}\widetilde{V}_{L}(q^{\prime},k)\widetilde{G}^{(\infty)}(k;E)T_{L}^{(\infty)}(k,q).

(41)

The separable form of the potential (
33
) enables semi-closed form solutions to the above integration equation:

T_{L}^{(\infty)}(q^{\prime},q)=(q^{\prime}q)^{L}t_{L}(E),

(42)

where

t_{L}^{-1}(E)=-V_{L}^{-1}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}k^{2L}\widetilde{G}^{(\infty)}(k;E).

(43)

When it is on-shell, the matrix
t_{L}
is usually parameterized by phase shifts,
\delta_{L}^{(1,2)}
, and inelasticity
\eta_{L}
,

\frac{t_{L}(E)}{(4\pi)^{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\ \frac{\frac{1}{2i}\left(\eta_{L}e^{2i\delta_{L}^{(1)}}-1\right)}{2\mu_{1}q_{1}^{2L+1}}&\frac{\frac{\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}_{L}}}{2}e^{i\left(\delta_{L}^{(1)}+\delta_{L}^{(2)}\right)}}{2\mu_{1}q_{1}(q_{1}q_{2})^{L}}\\
\frac{\frac{\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}_{L}}}{2}e^{i\left(\delta_{L}^{(1)}+\delta_{L}^{(2)}\right)}}{2\mu_{2}q_{2}(q_{1}q_{2})^{L}}&\frac{\frac{1}{2i}\left(\eta_{L}e^{2i\delta_{L}^{(2)}}-1\right)}{2\mu_{2}q_{2}^{2L+1}}\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

Using the following identity,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}dk}{(2\pi)^{3}}k^{2L}\widetilde{G}^{(\infty)}(k;E)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0},

(45)

where

\displaystyle G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)=\begin{bmatrix}G^{(1,\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q_{1})&0\\
0&G^{(2,\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q_{2})\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle G^{(\alpha,\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q_{\alpha})=-i2\mu_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}j_{L}(q_{\alpha}r_{<})h_{L}^{(+)}(q_{\alpha}r_{>})\,,

(46)

we find the coupled-channel version of Eq.(
16
):

t_{L}^{-1}(E)=-V_{L}^{-1}+\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}.

(47)

III.3
BERW formula for coupled-channel reactions

Using Eq.(
47
) in Eq.(
37
) and , one finds

\displaystyle\det

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{2^{2L+2}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\pi}\frac{G^{(\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)-G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};E)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle+(4\pi)^{2}t_{L}^{-1}(E)\bigg]=0\,.

(48)

The cancellation of ultraviolet divergences manifested by the Green’s functions is made clear if we use
the limiting forms of both Green’s functions:

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2L+2}\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})^{2}}{\pi}\frac{G^{(\alpha,\omega)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha})}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\mu_{\alpha}\omega)^{L+\frac{3}{2}}}{\omega}2^{2L+2}(-1)^{L+1}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega})}

\displaystyle-\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma(L+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\pi}\frac{2\mu_{\alpha}}{r^{2L+1}}|_{r\rightarrow 0},

(49)

and

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2L+2}\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})^{2}}{\pi}\frac{G_{L}^{(\alpha,\infty)}(r,r^{\prime};q_{\alpha})}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=-i2\mu_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}^{2L+1}-\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma(L+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\pi}\frac{2\mu_{\alpha}}{r^{2L+1}}|_{r\rightarrow 0}\,.

(50)

Equation (
48
) is thus reduced to the more compact form:

\displaystyle\quad\eta_{L}\left(1+g_{L}^{(1)}g_{L}^{(2)}\right)\cos\left(\delta_{L}^{(1)}-\delta_{L}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(1-g_{L}^{(1)}g_{L}^{(2)}\right)\cos\left(\delta_{L}^{(1)}+\delta_{L}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle-\eta_{L}\left(g_{L}^{(1)}-g_{L}^{(2)}\right)\sin\left(\delta_{L}^{(1)}-\delta_{L}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(g_{L}^{(1)}+g_{L}^{(2)}\right)\sin\left(\delta_{L}^{(1)}+\delta_{L}^{(2)}\right)=0,

(51)

where

g_{L}^{(\alpha)}=(-1)^{L+1}\left(\frac{4\mu_{\alpha}\omega}{q_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)^{L+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{L}{2}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega})}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{L}{2}-\frac{\epsilon^{(\omega)}_{\alpha}}{2\omega})}.

(52)

The coupled-channel QC in a harmonic trap expressed by
Eq.(
51
) resembles that in
a periodic cube [see Eq.(25) in
Guo et al. 2013
]. In the harmonic trap,
g_{L}^{(\alpha)}
functions play the same role of Lüscher zeta functions in the periodic cube.

Given one energy eigenvalue
E
, we can not determine all of
\delta_{L}^{(1,2)}
and
\eta_{L}
simultaneously. A possible way out is to gather several pairs of
(E,\omega)
to build more constraints on
\delta_{L}^{(1,2)}
and
\eta_{L}
, provided that energy eigenvalues
E
’s are somewhat close to each other and the ratio
E/\omega
is distinct from one another. Though this approach is model- independent, the requirement may only be met by small portion of the calculated levels.
Another useful approach widely used in the lattice QCD community is to model
\delta_{L}^{(1,2)}
and
\eta_{L}
with a few free parameters,
which can be fitted to more energy levels
by using QC as defined in Eq.(
51
),
(see, e.g., discussions in Refs.
Guo et al. 2013
;
Guo 2013
).

IV
Discussions and Summary

The harmonic trap may be replaced with artificial fields of different configuration or boundary condition.
For instance, hard-sphere boundary condition is used in some Monte Carlo simulations
Bovermann et al. 2019
;
Elhatisari et al. 2016
;
Rokash et al. 2015
.
We can repeat all the discussions in previous sections by substituting the following external potential in place of the HO potential:

V_{h.s.}(r)=\begin{cases}0,&r<b\,,\\
\infty,&r>b\,,\end{cases}

(53)

where
b
is the radius of the sphere. The Green’s function must satisfy the boundary condition:

\displaystyle G_{L}^{(h.s.)}(r,r^{\prime};q)

\displaystyle=-2\mu qj_{L}(qr_{<})j_{L}(qr_{>})\left[\frac{n_{L}(qb)}{j_{L}(qb)}-\frac{n_{L}(qr_{>})}{j_{L}(qr_{>})}\right].

(54)

For instance, in the case of the single-channel elastic scattering,
Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
) are replaced with

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2L+2}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\pi}\frac{G^{(h.s.)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q)-G^{(\infty)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=-2\mu q^{2L+1}[\cot\delta_{L}(q)-i].

(55)

Using the asymptotic form of
G^{(\infty)}_{L}
(
50
) and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\frac{2^{2L+2}\Gamma^{2}(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\pi}\frac{G^{(h.s.)}_{L}(r,r^{\prime};q)}{(rr^{\prime})^{L}}|_{r,r^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=-q^{2L+1}\frac{n_{L}(qb)}{j_{L}(qb)}-\frac{2^{2L+1}\Gamma(L+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(L+\frac{3}{2})}{\pi}\frac{1}{r^{2L+1}}|_{r\rightarrow 0},

(56)

the quantization condition of the single-channel case for the spherical hard wall is thus given by a form analogous to the BERW formula (or Lüscher’s for that matter):

\displaystyle\cot\delta_{L}(q)=\frac{n_{L}(qb)}{j_{L}(qb)}.

(57)

The hard-sphere geometry is encoded in
n_{L}(qb)/j_{L}(qb)
, which plays the same role as
g_{L}(q)
to the harmonic trap.
Similarly, the coupled-channel QC can be worked out for the spherical hard wall by
replacing
g_{L}^{(\alpha)}
in Eq.(
52
) as follows

g_{L}^{(\alpha)}\rightarrow\frac{n_{L}(q_{\alpha}b)}{j_{L}(q_{\alpha}b)}\,.

(58)

In summary, we have derived the quantization condition for coupled-channel two-particle systems trapped by the harmonic potential. The formula by itself relates the coupled-channel reaction amplitudes in infinite volume, parametrized by two phase shifts and one inelasticity, to energy eigenvalues of the corresponding trapped system. Although only the quantization condition for the case of distinguishable spinless particles is shown, with the technical detail spelled out in the paper, the extension to include slight complications due to spin, identical particles and so on should be straightforward. Coulomb repulsion is important, however, if reactions take place near the threshold of charged particles. Quantization conditions may be derived for that circumstance in the harmonic trap, following the methodology developed in Ref.
Guo and Gasparian 2021
.
