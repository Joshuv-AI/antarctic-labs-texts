Mixed Neumann-Fourier expressions for solving integral equations
Raimundas Vidunas

Abstract
While teaching a course on integral equations, I noticed that a straightforward combination of Neumann series and Fourier series for the resolvent (or the solution) of an integral equation has good approximation qualities. This short article presents and investigates this combination of approximating series.



1
The mixed series

Fredhol’ms integral equation of the second kind

y(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_{a}^{b}K(x,t)y(t)dt

(1)

with
K(x,t)\in L_{2}([a,b]^{2})
can be solved
[
Kan97
, Ch. 3]
in the resolvent form

y(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_{a}^{b}R(x,t,\lambda)f(t)dt.

(2)

The resolvent
R(x,t,\lambda)
can be expressed as the Neumann series

R(x,t,\lambda)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}K_{n}(x,t)

(3)

in terms of the iterated kernels:

\displaystyle K_{1}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\;K(x,t),

(4)

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\;\int_{a}^{b}K(x,\xi)K_{n-1}(\xi,t)d\xi.

(5)

The resolvent is a meromorphic function of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
with only
\lambda=\infty
as a possible limiting point.
The poles are the eigenvalues of the corresponding homogeneous equation

\psi(x)=\lambda\int_{a}^{b}K(x,t)\psi(t)dt.

(6)

The Neumann series converges for
|\lambda|<1/||K||
, or up to the nearest pole.
The integral equation (
1
) has a unique solution when
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue
[
Kan97
, Ch. 4.2]
.

If the kernel
K(x,t)\in L_{2}([a,b]^{2})
is symmetric, that is, if
K(x,t)=\overline{K(t,x)}
, then we have the Fourier series expression

R(x,t,\lambda)=K(x,t)+\lambda\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}\,(\lambda_{k}-\lambda)}

(7)

in terms of an orthonormal system
\psi_{1}(x),\psi_{2}(x),\ldots
of eigenfunctions
[
Kan97
, Ch. 7]

with the corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots

of the homogeneous Fredholm equation (
6
).
The Fourier series converges absolutely and almost uniformly
when
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue
[
Tri57
, p. 115]
.
And we have the bilinear series

K(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}}

(8)

that converges in the
L_{2}
-norm generally. Accordingly, we can modify (
7
) to

R(x,t,\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda},

(9)

but the convergence is weaker.

On the other hand, we can improve convergence of (
7
)
by subtracting known Fourier series whose terms behave asymptotically as
\sim 1/\lambda_{k}^{2}
.
Suitable Fourier series are for the iterated kernels:

K_{n}(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}^{n}}.

(10)

For
n\geq 2
, these series converges absolutely and almost uniformly. Employing the series with
n=2
, we obtain

R(x,t,\lambda)=K(x,t)+\lambda K_{2}(x,t)+\lambda^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}\,(\lambda_{k}-\lambda)},

(11)

with the Fourier terms behaving asymptotically as
\sim 1/\lambda_{k}^{3}
.
Next, employing
K_{3}(x,t)
we obtain

R(x,t,\lambda)=K(x,t)+\lambda K_{2}(x,t)+\lambda^{2}K_{3}(x,t)+\lambda^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}^{3}\,(\lambda_{k}-\lambda)}.

Generally, we have

R(x,t,\lambda)=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lambda^{n-1}K_{n}(x,t)+\lambda^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}^{m}\,(\lambda_{k}-\lambda)}.

(12)

The obvious practical advantage is that convergence of the Fourier series improves
with increased
m
. The resolvent can be effectively approximated by a few terms of the Neumann series
plus several terms of Fourier series. Even if
\lambda
is near some large eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
,
the terms with
|\lambda_{k}|>|\lambda_{n}|
diminish quickly.

From the probed literature it appears that the closest topic that may suggest formulas (
11
), (
12
)
is “acceleration of convergence of Fourier series” or “overcoming the Gibbs phenomenon” by means of
polynomial subtraction

[
Adc11
, §5.1]
,
[
Ner19
]
. The Neumann series part in (
12
) is often polynomial,
as the example of the following section illustrates.
The term “Fourier-Neumann series” has been used to refer to Fourier series in terms of Bessel functions
[
CV09
]
.

2
An example

Consider the integral equation

y(x)=x+\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\min(x,t)y(t)dt,

(13)

as in
[
Kle11
, p. 124–127]
.
The eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are found by solving the homogeneous equation

\psi(x)=\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\min(x,t)\psi(t)dt,

(14)

or the equivalent differential equation with boundary conditions:

\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\lambda\psi(x)=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\psi(0)=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\psi^{\prime}(1)=0.

(15)

The eigenvalues and the corresponding normalized eigenfunctions are parametrized by
n=1,2,\ldots
:

\lambda_{n}=\left(n-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\pi^{2},\hskip 20.00003pt\psi_{n}=\sqrt{2}\,\sin\!\left(n-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\right)\!\pi x.

(16)

By (
9
), the resolvent is

R(x,t,\lambda)=2\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\pi x\;\sin\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\pi t}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\pi^{2}-\lambda}.

(17)

By (
2
), we have

y(x)=x+\lambda\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}\sin\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\pi x}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\!\pi^{2}\left(\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\!\pi^{2}-\lambda\right)}.

(18)

To compute the iterated kernels for (
12
),
let
\widetilde{K}_{n}(x,t)
denote the restriction of
K_{n}(x,t)
to
t\leq x
.
The iterated kernels are symmetric just as
K(x,t)
, and

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{n+1}(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\xi\,\widetilde{K}_{n}(t,\xi)d\xi+\int_{t}^{x}\xi\,\widetilde{K}_{n}(\xi,t)d\xi+x\int_{x}^{1}\widetilde{K}_{n}(\xi,t)d\xi

(19)

by (
5
). Starting from
\widetilde{K}_{1}(x,t)=t
, we compute

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{2}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\;\textstyle xt-\frac{1}{2}\,x^{2}t-\frac{1}{6}\,t^{3},

(20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{3}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\;\textstyle\frac{1}{3}\,xt-\frac{1}{6}\,x^{3}t-\frac{1}{6}\,xt^{3}+\frac{1}{24}\,{x}^{4}t+\frac{1}{12}\,{x}^{2}{t}^{3}+\frac{1}{120}\,t^{5}.

(21)

We compute the initial integrals

J_{n}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}K_{n}(x,t)f(t)dt=\int_{0}^{x}\widetilde{K}_{n}(x,t)f(t)dt+\int_{x}^{1}\widetilde{K}_{n}(t,x)f(t)dt

(22)

with
f(x)=x
:

\displaystyle J_{1}(x)=

\displaystyle\;\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\,x-\frac{1}{6}\,x^{3},

(23)

\displaystyle J_{2}(x)=

\displaystyle\;\textstyle\frac{5}{24}\,x-\frac{1}{12}\,x^{3}+\frac{1}{120}\,x^{5},

(24)

\displaystyle J_{3}(x)=

\displaystyle\;\textstyle\frac{61}{720}\,x-\frac{5}{144}\,x^{3}+\frac{1}{240}\,x^{5}-\frac{1}{5040}\,x^{7}.

(25)

We get the solution in subsequently quicker approximations:

\displaystyle y(x)=

\displaystyle\;x+\lambda J_{1}(x)+\lambda^{2}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}\sin\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\pi x}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{4}\!\pi^{4}\left(\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\!\pi^{2}-\lambda\right)},

(26)

\displaystyle y(x)=

\displaystyle\;x+\lambda J_{1}(x)+\lambda^{2}J_{2}(x)+\lambda^{3}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}\sin\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\pi x}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{6}\!\pi^{6}\left(\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\!\pi^{2}-\lambda\right)},

(27)

\displaystyle y(x)=

\displaystyle\;x+\lambda J_{1}(x)+\lambda^{2}J_{2}(x)+\lambda^{3}J_{3}(x)

\displaystyle\,\hskip 95.0pt+\lambda^{4}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}\sin\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\pi x}{\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{8}\!\pi^{8}\left(\!\left(n-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\!\pi^{2}-\lambda\right)}.

(28)

The solution exists only if and only if
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue.
For
\lambda>0
or
\lambda<0
, an elementary expression for the solution is,
respectively,

\displaystyle y(x)=\frac{\sin\sqrt{\lambda}\,x}{\sqrt{\lambda}\,\cos\sqrt{\lambda}}\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{or}\hskip 20.00003pty(x)=\frac{\sinh\sqrt{-\lambda}\,x}{\sqrt{-\lambda}\,\cosh\sqrt{-\lambda}}.

(29)

The poles of these solutions at the eigenvalues
\lambda
are well captured by the respective Fourier terms.
With those Fourier terms, the approximation error can be seen as a continuous function of
\lambda
.

With explicit solutions at hand, we may analyze approximation quality of differently
truncated mixed Neumann-Fourier series (
18
), (
26
)–(
2
).

and Table

\approx 100
. The approximation error is largest at the endpoint
x=1
,
because the non-homogeneous term
f(x)=x
in (
13
) does not satisfy the boundary condition (
15
)
at
x=1
, resulting in a weak version of the Gibbs phenomenon
[
Adc11

.

unless
\lambda
becomes large while the number of Fourier terms lags the number of Neumann terms.
The approximation errors for negative
\lambda
are of the same order as respectively those for
|\lambda|
.

3
More formulas

Let us consider the interplay of Fourier series with Fredholm’s representation

R(x,t,\lambda)=\frac{D(x,t,\lambda)}{\Delta(\lambda)},

(30)

where

\Delta(\lambda)=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{k}}\right)

(31)

is an analytic function with the zeroes exactly at the eigenvalues
[
Kan97
, Ch. 4]
.
The analytic series in
\lambda
of
\Delta(x)
and
D(x,t,\lambda)
converge on the whole
\mathbb{C}

when the kernel
K(x,t)
is
L_{2}
-integrable.
The results are unremarkable for approximation purposes, but there is some representational appeal.

The power series coefficients of

\displaystyle\Delta(\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\lambda^{n},\hskip 20.00003ptD(x,t,\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}(x,t)\lambda^{n},

(32)

can be computed
[
Kan97
, (4.2.9)–(4.2.13a)]
from the recurrence relations

\displaystyle c_{n}=

\displaystyle\;-\frac{1}{n}\int_{a}^{b}C_{n-1}(x,x)dx,

(33)

\displaystyle C_{n}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\;c_{n}K(x,t)+\int_{a}^{b}K(x,\xi)C_{n-1}(\xi,t)d\xi,

(34)

and the initial conditions
c_{0}=1
,
C_{0}(x,t)=K(x,t)
.
Keeping in mind orthonormality of the
\psi_{k}(\xi)
’s, manipulation of the Fourier series gives

\displaystyle c_{1}=

\displaystyle\,-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}},

(35)

\displaystyle C_{1}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\,-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\neq k}\frac{\psi_{j}(x)\overline{\psi_{j}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}\lambda_{j}}

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\mathop{\sum\sum}_{j<k}\frac{\psi_{j}(x)\overline{\psi_{j}(t)}+\psi_{k}(t)\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}},

(37)

\displaystyle c_{2}=

\displaystyle\;\mathop{\sum\sum}_{j<k}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}},

(38)

\displaystyle C_{2}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\;\mathop{\sum\sum}_{j<k}\!\sum_{i\not\in\{j,k\}}\frac{\psi_{i}(x)\overline{\psi_{i}(t)}}{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}\lambda_{i}}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{i<j<k}\frac{\psi_{i}(x)\overline{\psi_{i}(t)}+\psi_{j}(t)\overline{\psi_{j}(t)}+\psi_{k}(t)\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}},

(40)

\displaystyle c_{3}=

\displaystyle\,-\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k}},

(41)

etc. The pattern

\displaystyle c_{n}=

\displaystyle\,(-1)^{n}\!\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{N}\;\&\,|S|=n}\Big(\prod_{j\in S}\,\lambda_{j}\Big)^{-1},

(42)

\displaystyle C_{n}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\,(-1)^{n}\!\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{N}\;\&\,|S|=n}\Big(\prod_{j\in S}\,\lambda_{j}\Big)^{-1}\,\sum_{j\in S}\psi_{j}(x)\overline{\psi_{j}(t)}.

(43)

is apparent from (
31
) and eventually from
D(x,t,\lambda)=\Delta(\lambda)R(x,t,\lambda)
as well.
Towards an analogy to (
12
), we have

D(x,t,\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\lambda^{n}C_{n}(x,t)+\!\!\sum_{S\subset\mathbb{N}\;\&\,|S|\geq m}\frac{(-\lambda)^{|S|}}{\prod_{j\in S}\lambda_{j}}\,\sum_{j\in S}\psi_{j}(x)\overline{\psi_{j}(t)}.

(44)

But convergence is expected to be poor generally.
If the series (
35
) for
c_{1}
does not converge, the series for the other
c_{n}

should not converge (absolutely at least) either.
The series for
C_{n}(x,t)
should then converge only in the
L_{2}
-norm.

As an intermediate alternative to (
12
) and (
44
) one may consider

\displaystyle\left(1-\alpha\lambda\right)R(x,t,\lambda)=

\displaystyle\,K(x,t)+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\lambda^{n}\big(K_{n+1}(x,t)-\alpha K_{n}(x,t)\big)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\alpha\lambda_{k})\,\psi_{k}(x)\,\overline{\psi_{k}(t)}}{\lambda_{k}^{m}\,(\lambda_{k}-\lambda)}.

(45)

This modification could be useful for
\lambda
near some large eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
,
taking
\alpha=1/\lambda_{k}
and annihilating the
k
th term in the Fourier series.
Similar expressions can be obtained for
R(x,t,\lambda)
multiplied by several such factors
(1-\alpha_{j}\lambda)

annihilating several peaking Fourier terms. But the Fourier terms in (
3
) decrease as
O(1/\lambda_{j}^{m})

rather than
O(1/\lambda_{j}^{m+1})
in (
12
). Eliminating a peaking Fourier term comes
at the cost of one added Neumann series term for the purpose of better convergence of the Fourier series.
