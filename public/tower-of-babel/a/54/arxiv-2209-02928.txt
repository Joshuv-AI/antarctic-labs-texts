Neal Bez, Shuji Machihara, Tohru Ozawa

Abstract
. We revisit the Rellich inequality from the viewpoint of isolating the contributions from radial and spherical derivatives. This naturally leads to a comparison of the norms of the radial Laplacian and Laplace–Beltrami operators with the standard Laplacian. In the case of the Laplace–Beltrami operator, the three-dimensional case is the most subtle and here we improve a result of Evans and Lewis by identifying the best constant. Our arguments build on certain identities recently established by Wadade and the second and third authors, along with use of spherical harmonics.



1.
Introduction and main results

We will consider the following Rellich inequality

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n(n-4)}{4}\right)^{2}\left\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\right\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|\Delta f\|^{2}_{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the
L^{2}
-norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with respect to Lebesgue measure, and
\Delta
denotes the standard Laplacian on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This inequality is a ubiquitous object in the theory of partial differential equations and spectral theory.
The reader may refer to
[
1
,
5
,
10
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
and references therein for further details, applications and wider perspectives.

If one wishes to treat general functions
f
in the Sobolev space
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, it is necessary to restrict the dimension of the ambient space to
n\geq 5
, since the norm on the left-hand side of (
1.1
) will be infinite for any continuous function with
f(0)\not=0
when
n\leq 4
. Rellich
[
18
,
19
]
proved that (
1.1
) holds for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
when
n\geq 3
, and when
n=2
, in order for the inequality to hold, we must additionally assume

(1.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\cos\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=0.

In fact, even if we replace the constant on the left-hand side by any positive constant, (
1.1
) fails when
n=2
if we admit all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\{0\})
.

A fresh perspective on the Rellich inequality (
1.1
) based on certain identities was recently brought to light by Wadade and the second and third authors in
[
17
]
. Here we continue to develop this perspective by examining the contribution arising from radial derivatives and spherical derivatives. It is well-known that the Laplacian decomposes as
\Delta=\Delta_{r}+\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
where
\Delta_{r}
denotes
the radial Laplacian and
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami operator. The radial Laplacian is given by

\Delta_{r}f=\partial_{r}^{2}f+\frac{n-1}{|x|}\partial_{r}f,

where
\partial_{r}
is the radial derivative given by
\partial_{r}=\frac{x}{|x|}\cdot\nabla
. Also, we introduce the spherical-type derivative
L_{j}=\partial_{j}-\frac{x_{j}}{|x|}\partial_{r}
for each
j=1,\ldots,n
, in which case
we have the relation

(1.3)

\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}=|x|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}.

The first of our key identities is the following and, roughly speaking, may be viewed as a means of capturing the radial and spherical contributions to both sides of (
1.1
). In the following statement, and throughout the paper, we use the notation

\mathbf{R}_{n}=\frac{n(n-4)}{4}

associated with the Rellich inequality (
1.1
).

Theorem 1
.

For
n\geq 2
and
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
we have

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{n}^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}-\bigg\|\Delta_{r}f+\mathbf{R}_{n}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}-2\mathbf{R}_{n}\bigg\|\frac{f_{*}}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

(1.5)

\displaystyle=\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}-\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{n}\Big)\bigg\|\Delta_{r}f+\mathbf{R}_{n}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbf{R}_{n}\|f_{\#}\|_{2}^{2}

and

(1.6)

\displaystyle\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}=\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}+\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+2\mathbf{R}_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg\|\frac{L_{j}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+2\bigg\langle-\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f_{*},f_{*}\bigg\rangle,

where
f_{*}=\partial_{r}f+\frac{n-4}{2}\frac{f}{|x|}
,
f_{\#}=\partial_{r}^{2}f+(n-3)\frac{\partial_{r}f}{|x|}+\frac{(n-4)^{2}}{4}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}
.

The identity (
1.4
) was reported in
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
for
n\geq 5
. Since
\mathbf{R}_{4}=0
, it is immediate that it extends to
n=4
. Here we shall verify the cases
n=2
and
n=3
; although the argument in
[
17
]
extends verbatim when
n=2
, a certain amount of care is needed to check the case
n=3
. The identity (
1.5
) is new and it has the advantage that for
n=2,3
,
both
coefficients
-(1+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{n})
and
\mathbf{R}_{n}
are negative. An identity similar to (
1.6
) was established in
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
(for
n\geq 5
), the only difference being the final term on the right-hand side of (
1.6
). As we shall see, the form we have presented in Theorem
1
is more convenient for our purposes in the present paper (when handling terms in a decomposition of
f
into spherical harmonics), and we also emphasise that
-\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}
is a non-negative operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see the forthcoming discussion on spherical harmonics).

Regarding the second and third terms on the right-hand side of (
1.6
), we have the identities in the forthcoming Theorem
2
in terms of the decomposition of
f
into spherical harmonics. The statement is written is terms of the orthogonal projection operator
P_{k}
onto the closed subspace
\mathcal{H}_{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
spanned by spherical harmonics of
order
k
multiplied by radial functions. When
k=0
, we will often abbreviate
P_{0}
as
P
, and we note this operator is explicitly given by

Pf(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(|x|\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\omega).

The spaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
give rise the standard spherical harmonic decomposition as

\displaystyle L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

and the spherical harmonics of degree
k
are well
known to be eigenfunctions of the Laplace–Beltrami operator with eigenvalue
-\mu_{k}
, where

\mu_{k}:=k(k+n-2);

that is

(1.7)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}P_{k}=-\mu_{k}P_{k}

holds for each
k\geq 0
.

Theorem 2
.

For
n\geq 2
and
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}^{2}\bigg\|\frac{P_{k}f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg\|\frac{L_{j}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}\bigg\|\frac{P_{k}f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.\end{aligned}

As we shall see, Theorems
1
and
2
lead to a number of interesting consequences, some of which are new, and some of which provide new and simpler perspectives on known results.

Firstly, we consider a refinement of the Rellich inequality (
1.1
) in which the Laplacian is replaced by the radial Laplacian. This naturally leads us to also consider a comparison of the size of the norm of the radial Laplacian and the Laplacian. We summarize matters as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{n}^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}

holds for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
. If
n\geq 3
, then

(1.10)

\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}\leq C\|\Delta f\|_{2}

holds for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
with
C=1
, and fails for any constant
C
when
n=2
. Furthermore, the constants in (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) are best possible.

The inequality (
1.9
) can be found already in work of Evans–Lewis
[
13
, Eq. (2.13)]
. We remark that it is possible to quickly derive (
1.9
) from (
1.4
) (for
n\geq 4
) and (
1.5
) (for
n=2,3
). The inequality (
1.10
) with
C=1
follows immediately from (
1.6
) for
n\geq 4
since
\mathbf{R}_{n}\geq 0
. When
n=2
, (
1.10
) must fail for any
C<\infty
since otherwise we could combine with (
1.9
) and obtain an inequality of the form (
1.1
) with some positive constant on the left-hand side; as we have already pointed out, this is impossible. The case
n=3
is slightly more subtle since
\mathbf{R}_{3}<0
, but we may use Theorem
2
to see that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{3}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+2\mathbf{R}_{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\bigg\|\frac{L_{j}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big(\mu_{k}(\mu_{k}-\tfrac{3}{2})\big)\bigg\|\frac{P_{k}f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0,\end{aligned}

where we have used that
\mu_{k}=k(k+1)\geq 2
for
k\geq 1
.

For completeness we also note that the optimality of the constant in (
1.9
) can be shown by arguing as in
[
9
]
using the family of functions
f_{\varepsilon}(x)=|x|^{\frac{4-n}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\chi(|x|)
. Here
\chi
is infinitely smooth,
\chi(0)=1
and
\chi(r)
vanishes for
r\geq 1
. Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have
\varepsilon\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}\to\mathbf{R}_{n}^{2}
and
\varepsilon\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\|_{2}^{2}\to 1
. Also, the constant
C=1
in (
1.10
) clearly cannot be improved since equality obviously holds for radial
f
.

Next we present a direct analogue of Theorem
3
for spherical derivatives.

Theorem 4
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then

(1.11)

\displaystyle(n-1)^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\leq\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

holds for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
such that
Pf=0
. If
n\geq 3
, then

(1.12)

\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbf{C}_{n}\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}

holds for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, with
\mathbf{C}_{n}=1
when
n\geq 4
, with
\mathbf{C}_{3}=\frac{64}{25}
when
n=3
, and the inequality fails for any
\mathbf{C}_{2}<\infty
when
n=2
. Furthermore, the constants in (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) are best possible.

The inequality (
1.11
) follows immediately from Theorem
2
since we have

\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigg\|\frac{P_{k}f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

whenever
Pf=0
, and
\mu_{k}\geq n-1
for
k\geq 1
. It is also clear from this argument that the constant is best possible since equality holds in (
1.11
) for functions in
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

The inequality in (
1.12
) was considered by Evans–Lewis in
[
13
, Corollary 1]

In fact, an abstract version of (
1.12
) was considered in
[

]
, and we shall identify the best constants in such a setting in Section

.
. An examination of their proof yields the explicit constants
\mathbf{C}_{n}=1
when
n\geq 4
, and
\mathbf{C}_{3}=4
(see Section

for details of the three-dimensional case), but there appears to be no discussion on the best constant in

. Note that the fact that (
1.12
) holds with
\mathbf{C}_{n}=1
when

). The case

) and (

).

Finally, in a slightly different direction, we present a refinement of the Rellich inequality (
1.1
) in which an additional term is added to the left-hand side. For the statement, we introduce the constant

\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}=(n-1)(n-1+2\mathbf{R}_{n})

and write
P^{\perp}=I-P
for the projection operator onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
consisting of radially symmetric functions).

Theorem 5
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then

(1.13)

\mathbf{R}_{n}^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\bigg\|\frac{P^{\perp}f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}

holds for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\geq 0
for
n\geq 3
we immediately recover the classical Rellich inequality (
1.1
) from (
1.13
). If we instead apply (
1.13
) for input functions satisfying
Pf=0
(i.e. those
f
which are orthogonal to the subspace of radially symmetric functions), then we obtain

(1.14)

(\mathbf{R}_{n}+n-1)^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{for}\quad Pf=0,

and thus a quantitative improvement in the size of the constant for
n\geq 3
. The improved form of the Rellich inequality (
1.14
) subject to the constraint
Pf=0
is not new and follows from the abstract result in
[
13
, Theorem 1]
(see also work by Caldiroli–Musina
[
6
]
); here we give a different perspective and obtain (
1.14
) from Theorems
1
and
2
, and use this perspective to give a relatively simple proof that there are no (non-trivial) maximizers for the inequality (see Section
4.4
). The fact that the inequality (
1.14
) extends to the Sobolev space
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
n\geq 3
(with
Pf=0
) requires an additional density argument and since we were unable to find this anywhere, we have included details in Section
4.3
of the present paper. We also remark that, conversely, (
1.13
) can be derived from (
1.1
) and (
1.14
)

Indeed, by orthogonality and applications of (
1.1
) and (
1.14
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{n}^{2}\left\|\frac{f}{|\cdot|^{2}}\right\|^{2}+\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\left\|\frac{P^{\perp}f}{|\cdot|^{2}}\right\|^{2}=\mathbf{R}_{n}^{2}\left\|\frac{Pf}{|\cdot|^{2}}\right\|^{2}+(\mathbf{R}_{n}+n-1)^{2}\left\|\frac{P^{\perp}f}{|\cdot|^{2}}\right\|^{2}\leq\|\Delta Pf\|^{2}+\|\Delta P^{\perp}f\|^{2}=\|\Delta f\|^{2}.

In the last step we used the fact that
P
commutes with
\Delta
. The observation here was pointed out to us on an earlier version of the present paper.
.

2.
Proofs of Theorems
1
and
2

We write

\mathbf{H}_{n}=\frac{n-2}{2}

for the constant associated with the Hardy inequality on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof of (
1.4
).

As stated just after Theorem
1
, the argument in
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
is valid as it stands for
n\geq 2
and
n\neq 3
so we refer the reader to that paper for the details. Here we give details for the case
n=3
; this case requires some clarification since the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
proceeds initially using integration by parts to write

\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{2}{(n-3)(n-4)}\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{\partial_{r}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\frac{f}{|x|^{2}},\partial_{r}^{2}f\bigg\rangle\bigg).

In our current case
n=3
, this is valid in the sense that

(2.1)

\bigg\|\frac{\partial_{r}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=-\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\frac{f}{|x|^{2}},\partial_{r}^{2}f\bigg\rangle

as can be verified by an integration by parts. Thus, we may proceed along the same line of reasoning as in the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
as follows. Firstly, we begin with the equality

(2.2)

\mathbf{H}_{3}^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\|\partial_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}-\bigg\|\partial_{r}f+\mathbf{H}_{3}\frac{f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

associated with the Hardy inequality on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. To see (
2.2
), note that an integration by parts gives

\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=-\frac{1}{\mathbf{H}_{3}}\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\frac{f}{|x|},\partial_{r}f\bigg\rangle

from which we obtain the desired equality by an application of the (easily verified) equivalence

(2.3)

\|u\|^{2}=-c\mathrm{Re}\langle u,v\rangle+b\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{c^{2}}\|u\|^{2}=\|v\|^{2}-\bigg\|v+\frac{1}{c}u\bigg\|^{2}+\frac{2b}{c^{2}},

which holds abstractly for
u,v
in some Hilbert space,
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Next, observe that

\bigg\|\frac{\partial_{r}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\bigg\|\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+2\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|},\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\rangle

and (
2.2
) implies

\bigg\|\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{H}_{3}^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\bigg\|\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|}+\mathbf{H}_{3}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

Also, a further integration by parts gives

\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|},\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\rangle=-\mathbf{H}_{3}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

and so we obtain

\bigg\|\frac{\partial_{r}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=(\mathbf{H}_{3}-1)^{2}\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+\bigg\|\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|}+\mathbf{H}_{3}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

Yet another integration by parts gives

\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\frac{f}{|x|^{2}},\partial_{r}^{2}f\bigg\rangle=\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\frac{f}{|x|^{2}},\Delta_{r}f\bigg\rangle+2(\mathbf{H}_{3}-1)\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore, from (
2.1
), we get

\bigg\|\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=-\frac{1}{\mathbf{R}_{3}}\mathrm{Re}\bigg\langle\frac{f}{|x|^{2}},\Delta_{r}f\bigg\rangle-\frac{1}{\mathbf{R}_{3}}\bigg\|\partial_{r}\frac{f}{|x|}+\mathbf{H}_{3}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

and hence, by (
2.3
), we may conclude (
1.4
) for
n=3
.
∎

Proof of (
1.5
).

For
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we start by observing that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2-a)^{2}}{4}\left\|\frac{g}{|x|^{1+\frac{a}{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\frac{\partial_{r}g}{|x|^{\frac{a}{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\frac{n-2-a}{2}\frac{g}{|x|^{1+\frac{a}{2}}}+\frac{\partial_{r}g}{|x|^{\frac{a}{2}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

holds for all
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
. Indeed, this follows by expanding the second term on the right-hand side, and then using integration by parts to see that

\displaystyle 2{\rm Re}\bigg\langle\frac{g}{|x|^{1+\frac{a}{2}}},\frac{\partial_{r}g}{|x|^{\frac{a}{2}}}\bigg\rangle=-(n-2-a)\left\|\frac{g}{|x|^{1+\frac{a}{2}}}\right\|^{2}.

Now taking
a=n+2
and
g
given by

\displaystyle g(x)=\left(\frac{\partial_{r}f}{|x|}+(\mathbf{H}_{n}-1)\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\right)|x|^{\frac{n+4}{2}}

we see that

\displaystyle\partial_{r}g(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta_{r}f+\mathbf{R}_{n}\frac{f}{|x|^{2}}\right)|x|^{\frac{n+2}{2}},

and therefore (
1.5
) follows from (
1.4
) and
(
2.4
).
∎

Proof of (
1.6
).

It was shown in
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
that

\displaystyle\|\Delta f\|_{2}^{2}=\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{2}^{2}+\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+2\mathbf{R}_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg\|\frac{L_{j}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg\|\partial_{r}L_{j}f+\mathbf{H}_{n}\frac{L_{j}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

holds for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})
and
n\geq 5
, but in fact the proof works just as well for any
n\geq 2
.

Now note that
L_{j}\partial_{r}=(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{|x|})L_{j}
and thus, since we also know that
L_{j}
commutes with multiplication by radial functions, we may write

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg\|\partial_{r}L_{j}f+\mathbf{H}_{n}\frac{L_{j}f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg\|L_{j}\bigg(\partial_{r}f+(\mathbf{H}_{n}-1)\frac{f}{2|x|}\bigg)\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.

By using the integration by parts identity

(2.5)

\langle g,L_{j}h\rangle=-\langle L_{j}g,h\rangle+(n-1)\langle g,\frac{x_{j}}{|x|^{2}}h\rangle,

and the fact that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}L_{j}=0
, we obtain (
1.6
).
∎

Proof of (
1.8
).

Firstly, we have

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}^{2}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f\bigg\|_{2}^{2}

Next, observe that

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}\zeta_{j}(x)|\lesssim\begin{cases}\frac{j}{|x|}|\xi^{\prime}(j|x|)|+j^{2}|\xi^{\prime\prime}(j|x|)|&\text{if}\ |x|\in[\frac{1}{2j},\frac{1}{j}],\\
0&\text{if}\ |x|\in[0,\frac{1}{2j}]\cup[\frac{1}{j},j]\cup[2j,\infty],\\
\frac{1}{j^{2}}&\text{if}\ |x|\in[j,2j].\end{cases}

Next, we define
\rho_{j}(x)=j^{n}\rho(jx)
where
\rho:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
is a

C^{\infty}
function supported in the ball
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:|x|\leq 1/4\}
and
\|\rho\|_{1}=1
.

Take
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and define
f_{j}=\rho_{j}*(\zeta_{j}P^{\perp}f)
. It suffices to prove that
P^{\perp}f_{j}\to P^{\perp}f
in
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as
j\to\infty
. To this end, we decompose

P^{\perp}f_{j}-P^{\perp}f=\rho_{j}*P^{\perp}f-P^{\perp}f-P(\rho_{j}*P^{\perp}f)-P^{\perp}(\rho_{j}*((1-\zeta_{j})P^{\perp}f))

Writing
g:=(1-\Delta)f
, it follows that
P^{\perp}g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and thus

\|\rho_{j}*P^{\perp}f-P^{\perp}f\|_{H^{2}}=\|\rho_{j}*P^{\perp}g-P^{\perp}g\|_{2}\to 0.

Also, by using the Cauchy–Schwarz and Minkowski inequalities, we have

\|P(\rho_{j}*P^{\perp}f)\|_{H^{2}}=\|P(\rho_{j}*P^{\perp}g)\|_{2}\leq\int_{|y|\leq 1/4}\rho(y)\|P^{\perp}g(\cdot-\tfrac{1}{j}y)-P^{\perp}g\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\to 0.

For the remaining term in the decomposition, we first estimate

\|P^{\perp}(\rho_{j}*((1-\zeta_{j})P^{\perp}f))\|_{H^{2}}\leq\|\rho_{j}*((1-\zeta_{j})P^{\perp}f)\|_{H^{2}}

and, since we clearly have
\|(1-\zeta_{j})P^{\perp}f\|_{2}+\|(1-\zeta_{j})\Delta P^{\perp}f\|_{2}\to 0
, it suffices to show

(4.7)

\lim_{j\to\infty}\|\nabla\zeta_{j}\cdot\nabla P^{\perp}f\|_{2}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\|\Delta\zeta_{j}P^{\perp}f\|_{2}=0.

From (
4.10
) we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{-2}|\nabla P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty

so the dominated convergence theorem and the estimate (
4.5
) quickly implies
\|\nabla\zeta_{j}\cdot\nabla P^{\perp}f\|_{2}\to 0
.

For the remaining limit in (
4.7
), we use the bound (
4.6
) to get

\displaystyle\|\Delta\zeta_{j}P^{\perp}f\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{4}}|\xi^{\prime}(j|x|)P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{4}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}(j|x|)P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{j^{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

and so, by the dominated convergence theorem, it suffices to check that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{-4}|P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.
To see this, we write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{4}}|P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(f(r\omega)-f(r\omega^{\prime}))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\omega^{\prime})\bigg|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\omega)\,r^{n-5}\mathrm{d}r.

Therefore it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{4}}|P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}|\nabla f(tx+(1-t)|x|\theta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta)

and hence the Hardy inequality implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{|x|^{4}}|P^{\perp}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla^{2}f(tx+(1-t)|x|\theta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta).

Using the change of variables
x\mapsto tx+(1-t)|x|\theta
, it can be shown that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla^{2}f(tx+(1-t)|x|\theta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta)\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla^{2}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty

as desired.
∎

Remark
.

For
n\geq 3
, the above density considerations allow us to extend the Rellich equality to
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
under the constraint
Pf=0
. The classical inequality (
1.1
) extends to all of
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as long as
n\geq 5
, and therefore, as well as raising the value of the constant, adding the constraint
Pf=0
also allows one to consider a larger range of
n
.

4.4.
Non-existence of maximizers

We show that there are no non-trivial cases of equality for (
1.14
) by re-visiting our proof of this inequality and examining when the dropped terms vanish. In particular, assuming that equality holds in (
1.13
) it follows from (
3.6
) that

\Delta_{r}f+\frac{\mathbf{R}_{n}}{|x|^{2}}f=0

and, via the identity

\Delta_{r}f+\frac{\mathbf{R}_{n}}{|x|^{2}}f=|x|^{1-\frac{n}{2}}\partial_{r}(|x|^{-1}\partial_{r}(|x|^{\frac{n}{2}}f))

we obtain

(4.8)

|x|^{-1}\partial_{r}(|x|^{\frac{n}{2}}f)(x)=\psi\Big(\frac{x}{|x|}\Big)

for some function
\psi:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{C}
. Consequently,

(4.9)

\frac{\partial_{r}f(x)}{|x|}+\frac{n}{2}\frac{f(x)}{|x|^{2}}=\frac{\psi(\frac{x}{|x|})}{|x|^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

The
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm of the function on the left-hand side of (
4.9
) is controlled by
\|\Delta f\|_{2}
, and this quantity is finite for
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The fact that the second term is controlled by
\|\Delta f\|_{2}
follows immediately from (
1.14
). For the first term, we use the so-called Hardy–Rellich type inequality

(4.10)

C_{n}\bigg\|\frac{\nabla f}{|x|}\bigg\|_{2}\leq\|\Delta f\|_{2}

for
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
n\geq 3
and
C_{n}
is a strictly positive constant. This inequality goes back to
[
22
]
for
n\geq 5
, and the extension to
n=3,4
can be found in
[
2
]
and
[
15
]
(see also
[
9
]
).

On the other hand, it is easy to see that the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm of the function on the right-hand side of (
4.9
) is infinite, unless
\psi=0
. Thus, it follows from (
4.8
) that
f
is of the form
f(x)=|x|^{-\frac{n}{2}}\phi(\frac{x}{|x|})
and, since
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, this forces
f
to be zero.
