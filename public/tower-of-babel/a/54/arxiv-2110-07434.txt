Abstract
. In this paper we develop certain aspects of perturbation theory for self-adjoint operators subject to small variations of their domains. We use the abstract theory of boundary triplets to quantify such perturbations and give the second order asymptotic analysis for resolvents, spectral projections, discrete eigenvalues of the corresponding self-adjoint operators. In particular, we derive explicit formulas for the first variation and the Hessian of the eigenvalue curves bifurcating from a discrete eigenvalue of an unperturbed operator. An application is given to a matrix valued Robin Laplacian and more general Robin-type self-adjoint extensions.



1.
Introduction

This paper concerns asymptotic perturbation theory for self-adjoint extensions of symmetric operators. Our main goal is to derive explicit second order Taylor expansions for resolvents, spectral projections, and eigenvalue curves for one-parameter families of self-ajdoint extensions of a given symmetric operator. The type of admissible perturbations covers, in particular, variation of boundary conditions for quantum graph operators and, more generally, variation of domain of self-adjointness for extensions of abstract symmetric operators. Such non-additive perturbations arise, for example, in the geometric spectral theory, in which case ad-hoc methods are used to relate, say, the Hessian of parameter-dependent eigenvalues to the reduced Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, see, for example, recent manuscripts
[

,
[

,
[

]
,
[

. We provide a complete operator theoretic treatment for the second order expansion problem which, in particular, yields an abstract version of the reduced Dirichlet-to-Neumann map and its relation to the Hessian of eigenvalue curves. The first and second order expansion formulas go back to Hadamard, Rayleigh, and Rellich and have been closely studied subsequently, cf. e.g.
[

The first and second order asymptotic expansions discussed in this paper are also central in the Maslov index theory, cf.
[

[

We stress that the perturbation theory developed here covers not merely “additive” perturbations of a given self-adjoint operator but rather a “structural” perturbation of the dense subspace on which the perturbed operator is defined. More specifically, we focus on the second order Taylor expansion for resolvents, spectral projections, and eigenvalue curves of

2.
Resolvent expansions

Throughout,
A
denotes a densely defined, symmetric operator acting in a complex Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
and having equal deficiency indices, i.e.,
\dim\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}-{\bf i})=\dim\operatorname{ker}(A^{*}+{\bf i})\leq\infty
. We equip the vector space
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}=\operatorname{dom}(A^{*})
with the graph scalar product

(2.1)

\langle u,v\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{+}}:=\langle u,v\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}+\langle A^{*}u,A^{*}u\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}},\ u,v\in\operatorname{dom}(A^{*}).

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{-}:=({\mathcal{H}}_{+})^{*}
be the dual to
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}
space consisting of linear bounded functionals. We notice a chain of natural embeddings,

{\mathcal{H}}_{+}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
,
where the first embedding is given by
{\mathcal{H}}_{+}\ni u\mapsto u\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and the second embedding is given by
{\mathcal{H}}\ni v\mapsto\langle\cdot,v\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. Let
\Phi^{-1}:{\mathcal{H}}_{+}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{-}
be the conjugate linear Riesz isomorphism such that

{}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{+}}\langle u,\Phi^{-1}w\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{-}}=\langle u,w\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{+}}=\langle u,w\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}+\langle A^{*}u,A^{*}w\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}},u,w\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+}.

Next, we fix the standard setting of boundary triplets, see
[
2
,
24
]
for more details, the history of the subject and a vast bibliography. Below, we write
[a,b]^{\top}
for a
2\times 1
column vector with entries
a,b

Hypothesis 2.1
.

Assume that
(\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\mathfrak{H})
is a boundary triplet. That is, we assume that we are given an auxiliary (boundary) Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
and a trace operator

\mathrm{T}:=[\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1}]^{\top}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{+},\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}),

which is bounded, surjective and satisfies the following abstract Green identity,

(2.2)

\langle A^{*}u,v\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}-\langle u,A^{*}v\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle\Gamma_{1}u,\Gamma_{0}v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}-\langle\Gamma_{0}u,\Gamma_{1}v\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\text{ for all $u,v\in{\mathcal{H}}_{+}$}.

Let us introduced a bounded symplectic form
\omega
defined by the formulas

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\omega\big((f_{1},f_{2})^{\top},(g_{1},g_{2})^{\top}\big):=\langle f_{2},g_{1}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}-\langle f_{1},g_{2}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\\
&\quad=\left\langle J(f_{1},f_{2})^{\top},(g_{1},g_{2})^{\top}\right\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}},\ J:=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{\mathfrak{H}}\\
-I_{\mathfrak{H}}&0\end{bmatrix},f_{k},g_{k}\in\mathfrak{H},k=1,2,\end{split}

so that the right-hand side of (
2.2
) becomes
\omega(\mathrm{T}u,\mathrm{T}v)
.
We denote the annihilator of a subspace
{\mathcal{F}}\subset\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}
by

(2.4)

{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}:=\{(f_{1},f_{2})^{\top}\in\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}:\omega\big((f_{1},f_{2})^{\top},(g_{1},g_{2})^{\top}\big)=0\text{\ for all}\ (g_{1},g_{2})^{\top}\in{\mathcal{F}}\},

and recall that the subspace
{\mathcal{F}}
is called

{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}
. Further, let
\Lambda(\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H})
denote the metric space of Lagrangian subspaces of

where

is the orthogonal projection onto
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}
acting in
\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}
,

.
Let us also recall the classical, and central for this paper, fact that the Lagrangian planes in

are in one-to-one correspondence with self-adjoint extensions of

, see

]
. That is, for every self-adjoint extension

of

is Lagrangian with respect to

, see

In applications, e.g. such as quantum graphs, spectral theory of differential operators, one often describes a Lagrangian plane

\displaystyle\quad\underset{\eqref{dqjtu}}{=}\langle(1-Q)\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{T}S)^{*}Q\dot{Z}^{*}\phi,\dot{Z}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}}

\displaystyle\quad=\langle\mathrm{T}(Q\mathrm{T}S)^{*}\dot{Z}^{*}\phi,(1-Q)\dot{Z}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}}=\langle\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{T}S)^{*}\dot{Z}^{*}\phi,(1-Q)\dot{Z}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}},

where we used
Q\mathrm{T}S=\mathrm{T}S
which holds since
\text{\rm{ran}}(S)\subset\text{\rm{ran}}(Q)
.

To complete the proof of the theorem we derive (
2.113
). To that end, we have

(2.124)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{T}S)^{*}\dot{Q}J\mathrm{T}u,\dot{Q}J\mathrm{T}u\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}}\underset{\eqref{dqjtu}}{=}\langle\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{T}S)^{*}Q\dot{Z}^{*}\phi,Q\dot{Z}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}}

(2.125)

\displaystyle\quad=\langle\mathrm{T}(Q\mathrm{T}S)^{*}\dot{Z}^{*}\phi,Q\dot{Z}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}}=\langle\mathrm{T}(\mathrm{T}S)^{*}\dot{Z}^{*}\phi,Q\dot{Z}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}\times\mathfrak{H}},

where, as before, we used
Q\mathrm{T}S=\mathrm{T}S
.
∎
