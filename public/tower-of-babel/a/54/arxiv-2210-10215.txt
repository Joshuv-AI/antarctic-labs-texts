Spiral shifting operators from the enumeration of finite-index submodules of 𝔽 q ​ [ [ T ] ] d {{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]^{d}
Yifeng Huang, Ruofan Jiang

Abstract
Fix an integer d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 . Solomon [ 26 ] counts index- n n submodules of the free module R d R^{d} for any Dedekind domain R R that admits a Dedekind zeta function. The proof involves the Hermite normal form for matrices. We propose another normal form in the case where R = 𝔽 q ​ [ [ T ] ] R={{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]] , which unlike the Hermite normal form, recovers the Smith normal form. We use our normal form to give a uniform approach to Solomon’s result and a refinement of it due to Petrogradsky [ 25 ] , which counts finite-index submodules M M of R d R^{d} such that R d / M R^{d}/M has a given module structure. Our normal form and Hermite’s can be interpreted as reduced Gröbner bases with respect to two different monomial orders. Counting with our normal form gives rise to a “total distance” statistics W W and certain “spiral shifting” operators g 1 , … , g d g_{1},\dots,g_{d} on the set X = ℕ d X={\mathbb{N}}^{d} of d d -tuples of nonnegative integers. These combinatorial structures are of independent interest. We show that these operators commute, and collectively act on X X freely and transitively, which in particular gives a nontrivial self-bijection on ℕ d {\mathbb{N}}^{d} . We give a formula on how g j g_{j} interacts with the W W -statistics on X X , leading to a rational formula for the generating function for W W -statistics over any forward orbit in X X under these operators.

(i,n)+1/d=(i+1,n)
if
1\leq i\leq d-1
, and
(d,n)+1/d=(1,n+1)
. Essentially, we are arranging the points of
[d]\times{\mathbb{N}}
on an upright cylinder with a
spiral
tracing the points in the height order, so the operator
+s/d
means shifting upwards following the spiral by
s
steps. Note also that
(i,n)+1=(i,n)+d/d=(i,n+1)
, hence the notation.

Now the operator
g_{1}
can be alternatively understood as shifting every point upwards by one step:

g_{1}(x):=x^{\prime}\text{ such that }\Delta(x^{\prime})=\{\delta+1/d:\delta\in\Delta(x)\}.

(3.8)

To define the operators
g_{2},\dots,g_{d}
, we first list the points of
\Delta(x)
in the height order:

\Delta(x)=\{\delta^{1}(x)\prec\delta^{2}(x)\prec\dots\prec\delta^{d}(x)\}.

(3.9)

As an informal definition of
g_{j}
, the operator
g_{j}
keeps
\delta^{1}(x),\dots,\delta^{j-1}(x)
fixed, and simultaneously shifts
\delta^{j}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\delta^{d}(x)
upwards to the next available seat (namely, a seat not occupied by the fixed points). More formally, define
g_{j}(x)=x^{\prime}
such that
\Delta(x^{\prime})=\{\delta^{k}(x^{\prime}):k\in[d]\}
, where

\delta^{k}(x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\delta^{k}(x),&k<j;\\
\delta^{k}(x)+s_{k}/d,&k\geq j,\end{cases}

(3.10)

where
s_{k}
is the minimal positive integer such that

i(\delta^{k}+s_{k}/d)\in\{i(\delta^{j}(x)),\dots,i(\delta^{d}(x))\}.

(3.11)

Implicit in the definition is an important observation that
g_{j}
preserves the height ranking among the points, i.e., we do have
\delta^{1}(x^{\prime})\prec\dots\prec\delta^{d}(x^{\prime})
if
\delta^{k}(x^{\prime})
is given by definition (
3.10
). Indeed, when going from
x
to
x^{\prime}
, the lowest
j-1
points are fixed, and the remaining points are raised in a way that preserves the relative order within.

Note the subtlety that the definition of
g_{j}
involves an interplay between the height-rankings and the seats of the points.

Example 3.1
.

We use the example with
d=5,x=(n_{1},\dots,n_{5})=(0,2,1,0,1)\in X={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
and
j=3
to illustrate the definition of
g_{j}(x)
.

First, we plot
x
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by collecting the points
(i,n_{i})
:

\Delta(x):=\{(1,0),(2,2),(3,1),(4,0),(5,1)\}.

(3.12)

Sorting the points by the height order, we have

\Delta(x)=\{\delta^{1}(x)\prec\dots\prec\delta^{5}(x)\}=\{(1,0)\prec(4,0)\prec(3,1)\prec(5,1)\prec(2,2)\}.

(3.13)

We now describe
x^{\prime}:=g_{3}(x)
by specifying how each point of
\Delta(x)
is moved. By definition,
g_{3}
leaves the two lowest points intact. In this case, we have

\delta^{1}(x^{\prime})=(1,0),\delta^{2}(x^{\prime})=(4,0).

(3.14)

For the remaining points, we sort them back according to the seat:

\delta^{5}(x)=(2,2),\delta^{3}(x)=(3,1),\delta^{4}(x)=(5,1)

(3.15)

We say the seats
2,3,5
to be “available”. The operator
g_{3}
moves each of the points horizontally to the next available seat to the right, except the rightmost seat among the available seats:

\delta^{5}(x^{\prime})=(3,2),\delta^{3}(x^{\prime})=(5,1).

(3.16)

For the rightmost available seat, we move the point to the leftmost available seat and
raise the level
by
1
.

\delta^{4}(x^{\prime})=(2,1+1)=(2,2).

(3.17)

Collecting the point, we have

\{\delta^{1}(x^{\prime})\prec\dots\prec\delta^{5}(x^{\prime})\}=\{(1,0)\prec(4,0)\prec(5,1)\prec(2,2)\prec(3,2)\},

(3.18)

namely,

x^{\prime}=g_{3}(x)=(0,2,2,0,1).

(3.19)

4
Properties of the operators

The first property of the operators
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
is that they commute.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
x\in X
and
j^{\prime}<j
, we have

g_{j^{\prime}}(g_{j}(x))=g_{j}(g_{j^{\prime}}(x)).

(4.1)

Proof.

Because both
g_{j^{\prime}}
and
g_{j}
leave the lowest
j^{\prime}-1
points
\delta^{1},\dots,\delta^{j^{\prime}-1}
intact, we may remove these points and consider the remaining points only. Effectively, we are assuming
j^{\prime}=1
without loss of generality.

Let
k=j-1
and
l=d-k
. Say the lowest
k
points of
\Delta(x)
are

\{\delta^{1}(x),\dots,\delta^{k}(x)\}=\{(p_{1},m_{1}),\dots,(p_{k},m_{k})\},

(4.2)

where
p_{1}<\dots<p_{k}
. (That is, we sort these points by their seats.) Let us also name the highest
l
points of
x
as

\{\delta^{j}(x),\dots,\delta^{d}(x)\}=\{(q_{1},n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l},n_{l})\},

(4.3)

where
q_{1}<\dots<q_{l}
. We shall prove that
g_{1}(g_{j}(x))=g_{j}(g_{1}(x))
by describing both sides explicitly. As a convenient convention, we define
(d+1,n)
to be the point
(1,n+1)
in
[d]\times{\mathbb{N}}
.

By definition, we have

\Delta(g_{j}(x))=\{(p_{1},m_{1}),\dots,(p_{k},m_{k}),(q_{1},n_{l}+1),(q_{2},n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l},n_{l-1})\}

(4.4)

and

\Delta(g_{1}(x))=\{(p_{1}+1,m_{1}),\dots,(p_{k}+1,m_{k}),(q_{1}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l})\},

(4.5)

where the convention
(d+1,n)=(1,n+1)
applies to exactly one of points in the list for
\Delta(g_{1}(x))
. Applying
g_{1}
to
\Delta(g_{j}(x))
, we have

\Delta(g_{1}(g_{j}(x)))=\{(p_{1}+1,m_{1}),\dots,(p_{k}+1,m_{k}),(q_{1}+1,n_{l}+1),(q_{2}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l-1})\}.

(4.6)

Now we apply
g_{j}
to
\Delta(g_{1}(x))
. Notice that
g_{1}
does not change the height ranking within the points, because it raises the height of every point by exactly
1/d
. Thus
(p_{1}+1,m_{1}),\dots,(p_{k}+1,m_{k})
continue to be the lowest
k
points of
\Delta(g_{1}(x))
, so
g_{j}
will shift
(q_{1}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l})
. We need to consider two cases:

(a)

If
q_{l}\neq d
, then
(q_{1}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l})
are their honest coordinates, so shifting by
g_{j}
results in
(q_{1}+1,n_{l}+1),(q_{2}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l-1})
.

(b)

If
q_{l}=d
, then the honest coordinates of
(q_{1}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l})
are
(1,n_{l}+1),(q_{1}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l-1}+1,n_{l-1})
, so shifting by
g_{j}
results in
(1,n_{l-1}+1),(q_{1}+1,n_{l}+1),\dots,(q_{l-1}+1,n_{l-2})
. But note that
(1,n_{l-1}+1)
can be represented by
(d+1,n_{l-1})=(q_{l}+1,n_{l-1})
by our convention.

We see that in both cases, we have

\Delta(g_{j}(g_{1}(x)))=\{(p_{1}+1,m_{1}),\dots,(p_{k}+1,m_{k}),(q_{1}+1,n_{l}+1),(q_{2}+1,n_{1}),\dots,(q_{l}+1,n_{l-1})\},

(4.7)

mathcing the expression of
\Delta(g_{1}(g_{j}(x)))
. Hence, we have proved the desired equality
g_{1}(g_{j}(x))=g_{j}(g_{1}(x))
.
∎

For a multi-index
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{d})
in
{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
, define

g^{a}(x):=g_{1}^{a_{1}}g_{2}^{a_{2}}\dots g_{d}^{a_{d}}(x).

(4.8)

By commutativity (Theorem
4.1
), we have

g^{a+b}=g^{a}\circ g^{b}\text{ for }a,b\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}.

(4.9)

In other words, the free abelian semigroup
\Gamma:={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
acts on the set
X={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
via

a\cdot x=g^{a}(x).

(4.10)

The second property of the operators
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
is that the semigroup action defined above is free and transitive, in the following sense.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider the configuration
\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)
in
X={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
. Then for any configuration
x\in X
, there exists a unique
a\in\Gamma={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
such that

g^{a}(\mathbf{0})=x.

(4.11)

Equivalently, the map
a\mapsto g^{a}(\mathbf{0})
is a bijection from
\Gamma={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
to
X={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
.

The proof requires the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
2\leq r\leq d
, say a configuration
x\in X
to be
r
-tight
if
\delta^{r}(x)
is the lowest point in

\{i(\delta^{r}(x)),\dots,i(\delta^{d}(x))\}\times{\mathbb{N}}

(4.12)

that is higher than
\delta^{r-1}(x)
. Then for any
1\leq j\leq r-1
, the configuration
g_{j}(x)
is
r
-tight whenever
x
is
r
-tight.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Notice that the lowest
j-1
points of
x
are fixed by
g_{j}
and are not involved in
\{i(\delta^{r}(x)),\dots,i(\delta^{d}(x))\}\times{\mathbb{N}}
. By removing the lowest
j-1
points of
x
and reindexing the remaining seats, we may assume
j=1
. Then
g_{1}
spiral shifts every point up by one step (in the sense of definition (
3.7
) where
s=1
), so the
r
-tightness is preserved.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Because
g_{i}
’s commute with each other, we may choose to set up the equation
g^{a}(\mathbf{0})=x
in the form

g_{d}^{a_{d}}\dots g_{1}^{a_{1}}(\mathbf{0})=x.

(4.13)

Look at the lowest point
\delta^{1}
. Since the only operator that moves
\delta^{1}
is
g_{1}
, we must have

\delta^{1}(g_{1}^{a_{1}}(\mathbf{0}))=\delta^{1}(x),

(4.14)

which determines
a_{1}
uniquely. In fact, we have

a_{1}/d=h(\delta^{1}(x))-h(\delta^{1}(\mathbf{0})),

(4.15)

since
g_{1}
increases the height of every point by
1/d
.

Having determined
a_{1},\dots,a_{r-1}
(
2\leq r\leq d
), we now determine
a_{r}
by looking at the
r
-th lowest point
\delta^{r}
. Let

x^{\prime}=g_{r-1}^{a_{r-1}}\dots g_{1}^{a_{1}}(\mathbf{0}),

(4.16)

and consider the equation

g_{d}^{a_{d}}\dots g_{r}^{a_{r}}(x^{\prime})=x.

(4.17)

The only operator among
g_{d},\dots,g_{r}
that moves
\delta^{r}
is
g_{r}
, so we must have

\delta^{r}(g_{r}^{a_{r}}(x^{\prime}))=\delta^{r}(x).

(4.18)

It remains to show that there is a unique integer
a_{r}\geq 0
satisfying the equation above. The uniqueness is clear because every application of
g_{r}
increases the height of
\delta^{r}
strictly (see (
3.10
)). It suffices to prove the existence. The key observation is that
\delta^{r}(x)\succeq\delta^{r}(x^{\prime})
. We now prove this observation.

Note that
\mathbf{0}
is
r
-tight in the sense of Lemma
4.3
. Since
x^{\prime}=g_{r-1}^{a_{r-1}}\dots g_{1}^{a_{1}}(\mathbf{0})
, by repetitively applying Lemma
4.3
, we have that
x^{\prime}
is also
r
-tight.

By construction,
\delta^{j}(x^{\prime})=\delta^{j}(x)
for all
1\leq j\leq r-1
. Thus, the sets
\{\delta^{r}(x^{\prime}),\dots,\delta^{d}(x^{\prime})\}
and
\{\delta^{r}(x),\dots,\delta^{d}(x)\}
are the same (denoted by
K
), because they are both the complement of
\{\delta^{1}(x),\dots,\delta^{r-1}(x)\}
. Since
x^{\prime}
is
r
-tight, the point
\delta^{r}(x^{\prime})
is the lowest point of
K\times{\mathbb{N}}
that is higher than
\delta^{r-1}(x^{\prime})
. Since
\delta^{r}(x)\succ\delta^{r-1}(x)=\delta^{r-1}(x^{\prime})
and the seat of
\delta^{r}(x)
also lies in
K
, we have
\delta^{r}(x)\succeq\delta^{r}(x^{\prime})
, proving the desired observation.

Now, we recall from the definition (
3.10
) that for any configuration
y
such that
\{\delta^{r}(y),\dots,\allowbreak\delta^{d}(y)\}\allowbreak=K
, the
r
-th lowest point
\delta^{r}(g_{r}(y))
of
g_{r}(y)
is defined to be the successor of
\delta^{r}(y)
in
K\times{\mathbb{N}}
. Applying the above fact to
y=x^{\prime},g_{r}(x^{\prime}),g_{r}^{2}(x^{\prime}),\dots
, we see that
\delta^{r}(g_{r}^{a_{r}}(x^{\prime}))
ranges over all points of
K\times{\mathbb{N}}
that are higher than or equal to
\delta^{r}(x^{\prime})
. Together with the observation
\delta^{r}(x)\succeq\delta^{r}(x^{\prime})
that we have just proved, it follows that there exists
a_{r}\geq 0
such that
\delta^{r}(g_{r}^{a_{r}}(x^{\prime}))=\delta^{r}(x)
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

The group
\Gamma
acts on any configuration
x\in X
freely, namely, if
g^{a}(x)=g^{b}(x)
, then
a=b
.

Proof.

By the existence part of Theorem
4.2
, there is
c\in\Gamma
such that
x=g^{c}(\mathbf{0})
. Hence

g^{a+c}(x)=g^{b+c}(x).

(4.19)

By the uniqueness part of Theorem
4.2
, we have
a+c=b+c
. Since the semigroup
\Gamma={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
is cancellative, we have
a=b
.
∎

5
Some combinatorial statistics

For a configuration
x=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
in
X
, we define its
size
to be

n(x):=n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}.

(5.1)

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
x\in X
and
j\in[d]
, we have

n(g_{j}(x))=n(x)+1.

(5.2)

Proof.

When we apply
g_{j}
to
x
, the rightmost point (i.e., the point with the largest seat) among
\delta^{j}(x),\dots,\delta^{d}(x)
loops around and increases the level by 1, while the levels of other points are unchanged.
∎

We now introduce a more interesting statistics. For two points
\delta_{1}\prec\delta_{2}
in
[d]\times{\mathbb{N}}
, we define their
distance
to be

b(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}):=\lfloor h(\delta_{2})-h(\delta_{1})\rfloor.

(5.3)

For a configuration
x\in X
consisting of points
\Delta(x)=\{\delta^{1}(x)\prec\dots\delta^{d}(x)\}
, we define the
weight
of
x
to be

W(x):=\sum_{j<k}b(\delta^{j}(x),\delta^{k}(x)).

(5.4)

We note that this definition of
W(x)
is equivalent to (
2.15
). The statistics turns out to interact consistently with the operators
g_{j}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
x\in X
and
j\in[d]
, we have

W(g_{j}(x))=W(x)+j-1.

(5.5)

Proof.

We refer to the points in

L:=\{\delta^{1}(x),\dots,\delta^{j-1}(x)\}

(5.6)

as low points, and the points in

H:=\{\delta^{j}(x),\dots,\delta^{d}(x)\}

(5.7)

as high points. The weight of
x
consists of three parts: distances within
L
, distances within
H
, and distances from points of
L
to points of
H
. When applying
g_{j}
, every distance within
L
does not change (because every point of
L
is fixed). We claim that every distance within
H
does not change as well. To prove the claim, note that when defining distances between two points of
H
, we may ignore the seats of
L
and reindex the seats of
H
by
1,\dots,d-j+1
. Thus, we may assume
j=1
without loss of generality. In this case,
g_{1}
increases the height of every point by
1/d
, so the distance is unchanged from definition (
5.3
).

Therefore, the weight difference
W(g_{j}(x))-W(x)
is solely contributed by the distances from low points to high points. Let
\alpha\in L
and
\beta\in H
. Then we note as an important observation that while
\beta
is shifted upward following the spiral, the distance
b(\alpha,\beta)
increases by 1 every time the seat of
\beta
passes through the seat of
\alpha
. More formally, for any
\alpha\prec\beta\prec\beta^{\prime}\in[d]\times{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

b(\alpha,\beta^{\prime})-b(\alpha,\beta)=\#\bigg\lparen(\beta,\beta^{\prime}]\cap(\{i(\alpha)\}\times{\mathbb{N}})\bigg\rparen,

(5.8)

where
(\beta,\beta^{\prime}]\cap(\{i(\alpha)\}\times{\mathbb{N}})
is the set of all points
\delta
with the same seat as
\alpha
such that
\beta\prec\delta\preceq\beta^{\prime}
.

Sort the seats of high points in ascending order:

i(H)=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{d-j+1}\}.

(5.9)

Let
\beta_{k}
denote the point of
H
seated at
i_{k}
. Let
\beta^{\prime}_{k}
denote the result of
\beta_{k}
after a shift by
g_{j}
. Then the seat of
\beta^{\prime}_{k}
is
i_{k+1}
if
k<d-j+1
, and
i_{1}
if
k=d-j+1
.

Partition the set of seats of points in
L
by intervals

I_{1}=(i_{1},i_{2}),\dots,I_{d-j}=(i_{d-j},i_{d-j+1}),I_{d-j+1}=(i_{d-j+1},d]\cup[1,i_{1}).

(5.10)

Denote by
L_{k}
the set of low points seated in
I_{k}
. Note that upon applying
g_{j}
, the point
\beta_{k}
is “shifted across” the interval
I_{k}
. The discussions so far imply that

b(\alpha,\beta^{\prime}_{k})-b(\alpha,\beta_{k})=\begin{cases}1,&\alpha\in I_{k};\\
0,&\alpha\in L\setminus I_{k}.\end{cases}

(5.11)

It follows that

\displaystyle W(g_{j}(x))-W(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in L\\
1\leq k\leq d-j+1\end{subarray}}b(\alpha,\beta^{\prime}_{k})-b(\alpha,\beta_{k})

(5.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq d-j+1}\lvert I_{k}\rvert

(5.13)

\displaystyle=\lvert L\rvert=j-1,

(5.14)

as desired.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
. For
x\in X
, we define the
content
of
x
to be the monomial

\Cont(x):=t^{n(x)}q^{W(x)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[[t,q]]

(5.15)

in formal variables
t
and
q
.

Theorem 5.3
.

We have an identity of power series in
{\mathbb{Z}}[[t,q]]
:

\sum_{x\in X}\Cont(x)=\frac{1}{(1-t)(1-tq)\dots(1-tq^{d-1})}.

(5.16)

In particular, the number of configurations
x
with
n(x)=n
and
W(x)=W
equals the number of size-
W
partitions of length at most
n
with parts at most
d-1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
, we have

\sum_{x\in X}\Cont(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma}\Cont(g^{a}(\mathbf{0})).

(5.17)

By Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
and noting that
\Cont(\mathbf{0})=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\Cont(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\geq 0}g_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots g_{d}^{a_{d}}(\mathbf{0})

(5.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\geq 0}t^{a_{1}+\dots+a_{d}}q^{0a_{1}+1a_{2}+\dots+(d-1)a_{d}}

(5.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)(1-tq)\dots(1-tq^{d-1})}.

(5.20)

The final assertion of the theorem follows from the elementary identity

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\lambda\subseteq[d-1]\times[n]}t^{n}q^{\lvert\lambda\rvert}=\frac{1}{(1-t)(1-tq)\dots(1-tq^{d-1})},

(5.21)

where the inner sum extends over all partitions of length at most
n
with parts of size at most
d-1
, and
\lvert\lambda\rvert
denotes the size of
\lambda
.
∎

We note the following alternative proof of Theorem
5.3
that uses only
g_{1}
.

Alternative proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Since we will vary
d
, we write
X
as
X_{d}
. Observe from (
3.1
) that the map
g_{1}:X_{d}\to X_{d}
is injective with image being the complement of

0\times X_{d-1}:=\{(0,n_{2},\dots,n_{d})\in X\}.

(5.22)

For any
x\in X_{d}
, we note that
n(g_{1}(x))=n(x)+1
and
W(g_{1}(x))=W(x)
, the latter being because
g_{1}
preserves the distance
b(\delta^{j}(x),\delta^{k}(x))
for every
j<k
.

We notice that for
x=(0,x^{\prime})\in 0\times X_{d-1}
, we have
n(x)=n(x^{\prime})
and
W(x)=W(x^{\prime})+n(x^{\prime})
, the latter being because the distance from the point
(1,0)
to
(i,n_{i})
is
n_{i}
.

Let
f_{d}(t,q):=\sum_{x\in X_{d}}\Cont(x)
. Then we have a recurrence

\displaystyle f_{d}(t,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X_{d}}\Cont(g_{1}(x))+\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X_{d-1}}\Cont((0,x^{\prime}))

(5.23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X_{d}}t^{n(x)+1}q^{W(x)}+\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X_{d-1}}t^{n(x^{\prime})}q^{W(x^{\prime})+n(x^{\prime})}

(5.24)

\displaystyle=tf_{d}(t,q)+f_{d-1}(tq,q).

(5.25)

Hence

f_{d}(t,q)=\dfrac{1}{1-t}f_{d-1}(tq,q).

(5.26)

Since it is vacuously true that
f_{0}(t,q)=1
, by applying (
5.26
) repetitvely, we have
f_{1}(t,q)=(1-t)^{-1}
,
f_{2}(t,q)=(1-t)^{-1}f_{1}(tq,q)=(1-t)^{-1}(1-tq)^{-1}
,
\dots
,
f_{d}(t,q)=(1-t)^{-1}(1-tq)^{-1}\dots(1-tq^{d-1})^{-1}
.
∎

Compared to the alternative proof, the
current
proof of Theorem
5.3
has the advantages that it suffices to work on a fixed
d
, and that the product form of the generating function is manifest. Furthermore, a small modification of this proof gives a summation formula over the forward orbit of any element in
X
under any finitely generated subsemigroup of
\Gamma
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
x_{0}
be a configuration in
X
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a subsemigroup of
\Gamma
freely generated by
a^{(1)},\dots,a^{(r)}
. Then

\sum_{x\in\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}}\Cont(x)=\Cont(x_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{1-\Cont(a^{(i)})},

(5.27)

where the content of an element
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
of
\Gamma
is defined as

\Cont(a)=t^{a_{1}+\dots+a_{d}}q^{0a_{1}+1a_{2}+\dots+(d-1)a_{d}}.

(5.28)

Proof.

Corollary
4.4
implies

\sum_{x\in\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}}\Cont(x)=\sum_{a\in\Gamma^{\prime}}\Cont(g^{a}(x_{0})).

(5.29)

Note from Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
that

\Cont(g^{a}(x))=\Cont(a)\Cont(x)

(5.30)

for all
a\in\Gamma
and
x\in X
. Note also that
\Cont(a+b)=\Cont(a)\Cont(b)
for
a,b\in\Gamma
.

Since elements of
\Gamma^{\prime}
can be traversed by the set of integers
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0
by setting

a=k_{1}a^{(1)}+\dots+k_{r}a^{(r)},

(5.31)

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}}\Cont(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}\Cont(g^{k_{1}a^{(1)}+\dots+k_{r}a^{(r)}}x_{0})

(5.32)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}\Cont(k_{1}a^{(1)}+\dots+k_{r}a^{(r)})\Cont(x_{0})

(5.33)

\displaystyle=\Cont(x_{0})\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\geq 0}\Cont(a^{(1)})^{k_{1}}\dots\Cont(a^{(r)})^{k_{r}}

(5.34)

\displaystyle=\Cont(x_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{k_{i}\geq 0}\Cont(a^{(i)})^{k_{i}}

(5.35)

\displaystyle=\Cont(x_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{1-\Cont(a^{(i)})}.

(5.36)

∎

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
x_{0}
be a configuration in
X
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a finitely generated subsemigroup of
\Gamma
. Then
\sum_{x\in\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}}\Cont(x)
is a rational function in
t
and
q
.

Proof.

By a theorem of Ito
[
18
, Theorem 2]
, any finitely generated subsemigroup of
\Gamma={\mathbb{N}}^{d}
is a disjoint union of finitely many cosets of (possibly trivial) free subsemigroups of
\Gamma
. Hence, there are free subsemigroups
\Gamma^{1},\dots,\Gamma^{r}
of
\Gamma
and elements
a^{1},\dots,a^{r}
of
\Gamma
such that

\Gamma^{\prime}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{r}a^{i}\Gamma^{i}.

(5.37)

It follows that

\sum_{x\in\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot x_{0}}\Cont(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{x\in\Gamma^{i}\cdot(a^{i}x_{0})}\Cont(x),

(5.38)

where the right-hand side is a finite sum of rational functions by Theorem
5.4
.
∎

6
Relation to Smith normal form

We may translate the language of hlex Gröbner bases back to a matrix normal form that is different from the Hermite normal form (
1.5
). In particular, the general form of the reduced Gröbner basis described in Section
2
implies that every finite-index submodule
M
of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[t]]^{d}
can be uniquely expressed as the column span of a matrix of the form

M=\im\begin{bmatrix}T^{n_{1}}&a_{12}(T)&\cdots&a_{1d}(T)\\
a_{21}(T)&T^{n_{2}}&\cdots&a_{2d}(T)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{d1}(T)&a_{d2}(T)&\cdots&T^{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix},

(6.1)

where (
2.13
) translates into

a_{ij}(T)\in\begin{cases}\Span\{T^{b}:n_{j}<b<n_{i}\},&\text{if }i<j;\\
\Span\{T^{b}:n_{j}\leq b<n_{i}\},&\text{if }i>j.\end{cases}

(6.2)

A special property of this new normal form is that the diagonal entries, with the order forgotten, recover the Smith normal form, or equivalently, the
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]
-module structure of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]^{d}/M
. This is not the case for the Hermite normal form. For example, consider

A=\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
1&T\end{bmatrix}.

(6.3)

Then the Smith normal form of
A
is
\mathrm{diag}(1,T^{2})
(up to permutation of diagonal entries), not
\mathrm{diag}(T,T)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
M
be of the form (
6.1
). Then as an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]
-module, we have

\frac{{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]^{d}}{M}\cong\frac{{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]}{T^{n_{1}}}\oplus\dots\oplus\frac{{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]}{T^{n_{d}}}.

(6.4)

Proof.

Recall that to obtain the Smith normal form, we are allowed to perform row and column operations. Consider
x=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
and let
i_{1},\dots,i_{d}
be the seats of
\delta^{1}(x),\dots,\delta^{d}(x)
. Look at the
i_{1}
-th row. Because
\delta^{1}(x)=(i_{1},n_{i_{1}})
is the lowest among all
\delta^{j}(x)
, we observe from (
6.2
) that the
i_{1}
-th row only has the diagonal entry. We also observe from (
6.2
) that every entry of a column is divisible by the diagonal entry of this column. Therefore, we may use the
i_{1}
-th row to eliminate the nondiagonal entries of the
i_{1}
-th column through row operations, without causing other changes.

We continue the process inductively. Having cleared the nondiagonal entries of the
i_{1}
-th,
\dots
,
i_{k-1}
-th columns, we look at the
i_{k}
-th row. Because
\delta_{k}(x)
is the lowest among
\delta_{j}(x)
,
j\geq k
, the entries
a_{i_{k},i_{j}}(T)
must be zero for
j>k
for similar reasons. For the entries
a_{i_{k},i_{j}}(T)
for
j<k
, they are already cleared in the previous steps of the process. Hence the
i_{k}
-th row only has the diagonal entry, and we may proceed similarly.

After finishing the process at
k=d
, we obtain the Smith normal form
\mathrm{diag}(T^{n_{1}},\dots,T^{n_{d}})
(or any permutation) and we are done.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Both Proposition
2.1
and Proposition
6.1
hold if we replace
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
by any field. We did not state them in the this generality to avoid distraction.

In
[
25
]
, Petrogradsky computed a refinement of the Solomon zeta function that not only remembers the index of a sublattice
M
of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, but also the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module structure of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}/M
. The essence of Petrogradsky’s theorem is a local result that holds for all discrete valuation ring.

Theorem 6.3

(Petrogradsky
[
25
, Theorem 3.1(5), Theorem 8.1(2)]
)
.

Let
R
be a discrete valuation ring with uniformizer
\varpi
and residue field
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
. For a finite-index submodule
M
of
R^{d}
, we define
\lambda_{1}(M),\dots,\lambda_{d}(M)
by

\frac{R^{d}}{M}\cong\frac{R}{\varpi^{\lambda_{1}(M)}}\oplus\dots\oplus\frac{R}{\varpi^{\lambda_{d}(M)}},\quad\lambda_{1}(M)\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d}(M).

(6.5)

Consider the series

Z(R^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d}):=\sum_{(R^{d}:M)<\infty}t_{1}^{\lambda_{1}(M)}\dots t_{d}^{\lambda_{d}(M)}

(6.6)

in variables
t_{1},\dots,t_{d}
.

Then
Z(R^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
is a rational function in
q,t_{1},\dots,t_{d}
that depends only on
d
, with denominator dividing
\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-z_{j})
, where
z_{j}=q^{j(d-j)}t_{1}t_{2}\dots t_{j}
.

We note that
Z(R^{d};t,t,\dots,t)
recovers the Solomon zeta function for
R^{d}
. The rational formula for
Z(R^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
is also given in
[
25
]
, restated below. For a permutation
\pi\in S_{d}
of
[d]
, define the set of descents as
D(\pi)=\{i\in[d-1]:\pi(i)>\pi(i+1)\}
, and let
\mathrm{inv}(\pi):=\{i<j:\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}
denote the number of inversions of
\pi
. Define

w_{D}(q):=\sum_{\pi\in S_{d},D(\pi)=D}q^{\mathrm{inv}(\pi)}.

(6.7)

Then

Z(R^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d})=\frac{\sum_{D\in[d-1]}\left\lparen w_{D}(q^{-1})\prod_{j\in D}z_{j}\right\rparen}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-z_{j})},

(6.8)

where
z_{j}=q^{j(d-j)}t_{1}t_{2}\dots t_{j}
.

Proposition
6.1
immediately expresses the Petrogradsky zeta function
Z(R^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
as a generating function for the
W
statistics.

Corollary 6.4
.

We have

\sum_{x\in X}q^{W(x)}t_{1}^{n(\delta^{d}(x))}t_{2}^{n(\delta^{d-1}(x))}\dots t_{d}^{n(\delta^{1}(x))}=Z({{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d}).

(6.9)

Note that
n(\delta^{d}(x)),\dots,n(\delta^{1}(x))
is nothing but sorting
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
in descending order, similar to the role of
\lambda_{1}(M),\dots,\lambda_{d}(M)
. When specialized at
t_{1}=\dots=t_{d}=t
, the left-hand side is equal to
\sum_{x\in X}q^{W(x)}t^{n(x)}
, the subject of Theorem
1.1
. Notice that in this special case, the denominator given in Theorem
6.3
is not tight: the denominator is
\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left\lparen 1-\left\lparen q^{d-j}t\right\rparen^{j}\right\rparen
, which is divisible by but not equal to the denominator
\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-q^{d-j}t)
in Theorem
1.1
.

Our next goal is to compute the left-hand side of (
6.9
). This will recover Petrogradsky’s theorem, as another application of the spiral shifting operators. We first give a brief overview of several alternative interpretations of the positive-coefficient polynomial
w_{D}(q)
given in
[
25
, §5]
.

6.1
Descents and multiset permutations

Given
D\subseteq[d-1]
(recall that we shall view it as a set of descents of some permutation), we introduce some notation. Let
D=\{d_{1}<\dots<d_{k}\}
, where
k=\lvert D\rvert
. For
j=0,1,\dots,k
, write
m_{j}=d_{j+1}-d_{j}
, where
d_{0}:=0
and
d_{k+1}:=d
.

Cosider a standard
\mathrm{inv}
-preserving bijection frequently used in Schubert calculus:

\displaystyle\beta_{D}:\big\{\pi\in S_{d}:D(\pi)\subseteq D\big\}

\displaystyle\to\big\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Map}([d],\{0,\dots,k\}):\lvert\sigma^{-1}(i)\rvert=m_{i}\big\}

(6.10)

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle\mapsto(\pi(i)\mapsto\text{unique $j$ such that }d_{j}<i\leq d_{j+1}).

For example, if
d=5
,
D=\{2,4\}
,
\pi=25|13|4
(the verticle bars denote positions where descents are allowed; note how they encode
(m_{0},m_{1},m_{2})=(2,2,1)
), then
\beta_{D}(\pi)
sends
2,5
to
0
, and
1,3
to
1
, and
4
to
2
. The two-line notation

\left\lparen\begin{matrix}25\\
00\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}13\\
11\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}4\\
2\end{matrix}\right\rparen

is instructive. We shall view
\beta(\pi)
as a permutation of the multiset
0^{m_{0}}1^{m_{1}}\dots k^{m_{k}}
; in this example,
\beta_{D}(\pi)
is the multiset permutation
10120
. It is instructive to read
\beta_{D}(\pi)
by permutating the columns of the two-line notation above, such that the first row is sorted:
\displaystyle{12345\choose 10120}
. We always have
\mathrm{inv}(\pi)=\mathrm{inv}(\beta_{D}(\pi))
. In this example, both numbers of inversions are 4.

The map
\beta_{D}
induces a bijection

\big\{\pi\in S_{d}:D(\pi)=D\big\}{\xrightarrow{\cong}}\big\{\sigma:\text{For any $j\in[d]$, there is $i_{1}<i_{2},\sigma(i_{1})=j,\sigma(i_{2})=j-1$}\big\}.

(6.11)

We call the latter the set of
strict
multiset permutations, namely, multiset permutations that has at least a
j
put before a
j-1
for any
1\leq j\leq d
. For example,
10120
is not a strict multiset permutation because every
1
is before every
2
, and this corresponds to the fact that the second vertible bar of
\pi=25|13|4
is not actually a descent.

The bijection (
6.11
) gives the alternative formula
[
25
, Theorem 5.3(1)]
:

w_{D}(q)=\sum_{\sigma\text{ strict}}q^{\mathrm{inv}(\sigma)},

(6.12)

where the sum is over all strict multiset permutations of
0^{m_{0}}1^{m_{1}}\dots k^{m_{k}}
.

Another formula for
w_{D}(q)
can be obtained from a formula of MacMahon
[
1
, §3.4]

\sum_{\pi\in S_{d},D(\pi)\subseteq D}q^{\mathrm{inv}(\pi)}={d\brack m_{0},m_{1},\dots,m_{k}}_{q},

(6.13)

which can be viewed as the Schubert cell decomposition of the flag variety
\mathrm{Fl}(D;d):=\mathrm{Fl}(d_{1},\dots,d_{k};d)
. We denote by
{d\brack D}_{q}
the Gaussian
q
-multinomial polynomial
{d\brack m_{0},m_{1},\dots,m_{k}}_{q}
. Using the Möbius inversion formula and the expression of the Möbius function on the Boolean poset of subsets of
[d-1]
(see for example,
[
24
, Proposition 2.44]
), one obtains

w_{D}(q)=\sum_{D^{\prime}\subseteq D}(-1)^{\lvert D\rvert-\lvert D^{\prime}\rvert}{d\brack D^{\prime}}_{q}.

(6.14)

Petrogradsky
[
25
, §3]
used this instead of (
6.7
) as the original definition of
w_{D}(\lambda)
.

6.2
Alternative proof of Petrogradsky’s theorem for
{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]^{d}

We now compute the left-hand side of (
6.9
). Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the subsemigroup of
\Gamma
(freely) generated by
g_{1}^{d},g_{2}^{d-1},\dots,g_{d}^{1}
. We notice that the effect of
g_{d+1-j}^{j}
on
X
is raising the level of the highest
j
points of
\Delta(x)
by
1
while fixing their seats; this is because
g_{d+1-j}
is spiral shifting within the highest
j
elements, so shifting
j
times completes a full cycle. For
x\in X
, we define the
seat permutation
of
x
to be the permutation
i(\delta^{1}(x)),i(\delta^{2}(x)),\dots,i(\delta^{d}(x))
.

Lemma 6.5
.

There is an orbit decomposition of
X
:

X=\bigsqcup_{\pi\in S_{d}}\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot x_{\pi}

(6.15)

for certain
d!
elements
x_{\pi}\in X
. Moreover, each orbit consists precisely of elements of
X
with seat permutation
\pi
.

Proof.

The index of
\Gamma^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
is
d\cdot(d-1)\cdot\dots\cdot 1=d!
, and
\Gamma
is clearly a disjoint union of
d!
cosets of
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Say
\Gamma=\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{d!}a_{k}\Gamma^{\prime}
for some
a_{k}\in\Gamma
. Then we have a decomposition of
X
into
d!
disjoint
\Gamma^{\prime}
orbits:

X=\Gamma\cdot\mathbf{0}=\bigsqcup_{k=1}^{d!}\Gamma^{\prime}\cdot(a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{0})

(6.16)

By the discussion above this lemma, each orbit has a constant seat permutation. Let
\pi_{k}
be the seat permutation of
a_{k}\cdot\mathbf{0}
(and thus the seat permutation of every element in the
k
-th orbit). Every
\pi
occurs as some
\pi_{k}
, since there exists an element of any given seat permutation (for instance, if
\pi=i_{1}\dots i_{d}
, then consider the element
x
with
\Delta(x)=\{(i_{1},1),\dots,(i_{d},d)\}
). Because there are
d!
permutations and
d!
orbits, all
\pi_{k}
must be distinct. Hence the proof of the lemma is complete by relabeling.
∎

Define

\Cont^{\prime}(x):=q^{W(x)}t_{1}^{n(\delta^{d}(x))}t_{2}^{n(\delta^{d-1}(x))}\dots t_{d}^{n(\delta^{1}(x))},

(6.17)

so that the left-hand side of (
6.9
) is
\sum_{x\in X}\Cont^{\prime}(x)
. We have

\displaystyle\Cont^{\prime}(g_{d+1-j}^{j}(x))

\displaystyle=q^{W(g_{d+1-j}^{j}(x))}t_{1}^{n(\delta^{d}(x))+1}\dots t_{j}^{n(\delta^{d+1-j}(x))+1}t_{j+1}^{n(\delta^{d-j}(x))}\dots t_{d}^{n(\delta^{1}(x))}

(6.18)

\displaystyle=q^{j(d-j)}t_{1}t_{2}\dots t_{j}\Cont^{\prime}(x)

(6.19)

\displaystyle=:z_{j}\Cont^{\prime}(x),

(6.20)

where the first equality results from the discussion at the beginning of this section, and the second equality is by Lemma
5.2
. The orbit decomposition thus imply the following formula.

Theorem 6.6
.

We have

\sum_{x\in X}q^{W(x)}t_{1}^{n(\delta^{d}(x))}t_{2}^{n(\delta^{d-1}(x))}\dots t_{d}^{n(\delta^{1}(x))}=\frac{1}{(1-z_{1})\dots(1-z_{d})}\sum_{\pi\in S_{d}}\Cont^{\prime}(x_{\pi}).

(6.21)

Proof.

Perform the same proof as Theorem
5.4
for each orbit in Lemma
6.5
.
∎

In particular, we have proved that
Z({{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[[T]]^{d};t_{1},\dots,t_{d})
is rational with the desired denominator. Moreover, we obtain yet another formula
\sum_{\pi\in S_{d}}\Cont^{\prime}(x_{\pi})
for the numerator, which implies that the numerator is a positive-coefficient polynomial in
q,t_{1},\dots,t_{d}
with total coefficient
d!
(i.e., evaluates to
d!
at
q=t_{1}=\dots=t_{d}=1
).

We now verify that (
6.21
) does recover Petrogradsky’s formula (
6.8
). This means that we shall prove

\text{Claim: }\sum_{\pi\in S_{d}}\Cont^{\prime}(x_{\pi})=\sum_{D\in[d-1]}\left\lparen w_{D}(q^{-1})\prod_{j\in D}z_{j}\right\rparen.

(6.22)

We first observe that
x_{\pi}
is given by the strict multiset permutation
\beta_{D(\pi)}(\pi)
. For example, if
d=5
and
\pi=25|14|3
(labeling all descents with verticle bars), then
\beta_{D(\pi)}(\pi)=10210
and
x_{\pi}=(1,0,2,1,0)
. Indeed, from the orbit decomposition in Lemma
6.5
, the element
x_{\pi}
must be the “lowest possible” element with seat permutation
\pi
. If we write
\pi=i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{d}
and
\Delta(x_{\pi})=\{(i_{1},n_{i_{1}})\prec(i_{2},n_{i_{2}})\prec\dots\prec(i_{d},n_{i_{d}})\}
, then
x_{\pi}
is obtained from greedily assigning the lowest possible values for
n_{i_{1}},n_{i_{2}},\dots,n_{i_{d}}
such that
(i_{1},n_{i_{1}})\prec(i_{2},n_{i_{2}})\prec\dots\prec(i_{d},n_{i_{d}})
. The resulting
x_{\pi}
is precisely
\beta_{D(\pi)}(\pi)
, because we start at
n_{i_{1}}=0
and the level of the next point must rise by
1
if the seat descends.

We then claim that

t_{1}^{n(\delta^{d}(x_{\pi}))}t_{2}^{n(\delta^{d-1}(x_{\pi}))}\dots t_{d}^{n(\delta^{1}(x_{\pi}))}=\prod_{j\in D}y_{d-j},

(6.23)

where
y_{j}:=t_{1}t_{2}\dots t_{j}
. The proof is clearer if illustrated on an example. Consider
\pi=16|235|48|7
, then
D(\pi)=\{2,5,7\}
and
(m_{0},\dots,m_{3})=(2,3,2,1)
. We have
\Delta(x_{\pi})=\left\lparen\begin{matrix}16\\
00\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}235\\
111\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}48\\
22\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}7\\
3\end{matrix}\right\rparen
, which reads
\{(1,0),(6,0),\dots,(7,3)\}
. Then reading the levels of
\Delta(x_{\pi})
from high to low, we obtain

t_{1}^{n(\delta^{d}(x_{\pi}))}t_{2}^{n(\delta^{d-1}(x_{\pi}))}\dots t_{d}^{n(\delta^{1}(x_{\pi}))}=t_{1}^{3}t_{2}^{2}t_{3}^{2}t_{4}^{1}t_{5}^{1}t_{6}^{1}t_{7}^{0}t_{8}^{0}=y_{1}y_{3}y_{6}=\prod_{j\in D}y_{d-j}.

(6.24)

We now compute
W(x_{\pi})
. Write
\pi=i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{d}
,
D(\pi)=\{d_{1}<\dots<d_{k}\}
, and

\Delta(x_{\pi})=\left\lparen\begin{matrix}i_{1}&\dots&i_{d_{1}}\\
0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}i_{d_{1}+1}&\dots&i_{d_{2}}\\
1&\dots&1\end{matrix}\,\vrule\,\cdots\,\vrule\,\begin{matrix}i_{d_{k}+1}&\dots&i_{d}\\
k&\dots&k\end{matrix}.\right\rparen

(6.25)

Note that the sequence
n(\delta^{1}(x_{\pi})),\dots,n(\delta^{d}(x_{\pi}))
is the second row of (
6.25
), which only depends on
D=D(\pi)
. We denote the sequence by
n_{1}(D),\dots,n_{d}(D)
; it is
m_{0}
copies of
0
’s, followed by
m_{1}
copies of
1
’s, …,
m_{k}
copies of
k
’s. Then for
a<b
, the columns
\delta^{a}(x_{\pi}),\delta^{b}(x_{\pi})
have distance

if

, or the above number minus one if

. Hence

W(x_{\pi})=-\mathrm{inv}(\pi)+\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq d}(n_{b}(D)-n_{a}(D)).

(6.26)

We shall simplify
\sum_{a<b}(n_{b}(D)-n_{a}(D))
. For
0\leq r<s\leq k
, the difference

occurs as the difference
n_{b}(D)-n_{a}(D)
for
m_{r}m_{s}
times. Thus

\sum_{a<b}(n_{b}(D)-n_{a}(D))=\sum_{0\leq r<s\leq k}m_{r}m_{s}(s-r).

(6.27)

One may verify that

\sum_{0\leq r<s\leq k}m_{r}m_{s}(s-r)=m_{0}(m_{1}+\dots+m_{k})+(m_{0}+m_{1})(m_{2}+\dots+m_{k})+\dots+(m_{0}+\dots+m_{k-1})m_{k}

(6.28)

by noting that the term
m_{r}m_{s}
occurs

(6.29)

\Cont^{\prime}(x_{\pi})=q^{-\mathrm{inv}(\pi)}q^{\sum_{j\in D(\pi)}j(d-j)}\prod_{j\in D(\pi)}y_{d-j}=q^{-\mathrm{inv}(\pi)}\prod_{j\in D(\pi)}z_{d-j}.
