Abstract
. We establish the well-posedness of viscosity solutions for a class of semi-linear Hamilton-Jacobi equations set on the space of probability measures on the torus. In particular, we focus on equations with both common and idiosyncratic noise, and with Hamiltonians which are not necessarily convex in the momentum variable. Our main results show (i) existence, (ii) the comparison principle (and hence uniqueness), and (iii) the convergence of finite-dimensional approximations for such equations. Our proof strategy for the comparison principle is to first use a mix of existing techniques (especially a change of variables inspired by [ BEZ ] to deal with the common noise) to prove a “partial comparison” result, i.e. a comparison principle which holds when either the subsolution or the supersolution is Lipschitz with respect to a certain very weak metric. Our main innovation is then to develop a strategy for removing this regularity assumption. In particular, we use some delicate estimates for a sequence of finite-dimensional PDEs to show that under certain conditions there exists a viscosity solution which is Lipschitz with respect to the relevant weak metric. We then use this existence result together with a mollification procedure to establish a full comparison principle, i.e. a comparison principle which holds even when the subsolution under consideration is just upper semi-continuous (with respect to the weak topology) and the supersolution is just lower semi-continuous. We then apply these results to mean-field control problems with common noise and zero-sum games over the Wasserstein space.



1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(
HJB
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}U-(1+a_{0})\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}(D_{x}D_{m}U\big)(t,m,x)m(dx)-a_{0}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(D_{mm}U\big)(t,m,x,y)m(dx)m(dy)\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\big(x,D_{m}U(t,m,x),m\big)m(dx)=0,\quad(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\
U(T,m)=G(m),\quad m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).\end{cases}

The data consists of two functions

\displaystyle H=H(x,p,m):\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R},\qquad G=G(m):\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R},

as well as the parameter
a_{0}\geq 0
which represents the intensity of the common noise. The unknown is a map
U=U(t,m):[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
.

Equations of the form (
HJB
) have received significant attention in recent years, in large part because of their connection to mean field control. In particular, when
H
is convex in
p
, (
HJB
) is the dynamic programming equation of a corresponding mean field control problem. When
H
is non-convex (i.e. not convex in
p
), (
HJB
) arises in the study of zero-sum stochastic differential games of mean field type, as proposed in
[
CP19
]
. We refer also to
[
Mou21
]
for an equation similar to (
HJB
) which arises in the setting of spin glasses, and where the non-convexity of the Hamiltonian creates some interesting challenges.

It turns out that (
HJB
) may not have a classical solution even if the data is smooth. In the convex case this is related to the non-uniqueness of optimal trajectories for the control problem, and an example can be found in
[
BC18
]
. As a consequence, it is necessary to work with weak solutions, and various notions of viscosity solutions have been proposed and studied. The comparison principle for viscosity sub/super-solutions of (
HJB
) is particularly subtle, and has received a huge amount of attention in recent years. We refer to
[
GT19
,
CKT23
,
CGK+21
,
SY22
,
SY23
,
DS23
,
BEZ
,
Sha23
,
TTZ23
,
Ber
]
for recent efforts in this direction. These references employ various notions of viscosity solutions, and work under a variety of assumptions (convex or non-convex
H
, common noise or no common noise, degenerate or non-degenerate idiosyncratic noise, semilinear or fully nonlinear) and so we make no attempt to give a full description of the state of the art. Instead, we describe in the next subsection the approaches which are most relevant to the present paper.

In addition to the comparison principle, we are interested in the connection between (
HJB
) and the sequence of finite-dimensional PDEs

(
\text{HJB}_{N}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V^{N}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{x^{i}}V^{N}-a_{0}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}\big(D_{x^{i}x^{j}}V^{N}\big)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(x^{i},ND_{x^{i}}V^{N},m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N})=0,\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\hskip 227.62204pt(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N},\vskip 2.84544pt\\
V^{N}(T,{\boldsymbol{x}})=G(m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N}),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}.\end{cases}

In particular, it is expected that the viscosity solution
U
of (
HJB
) arises as the limit of the solutions
V^{N}
to (

HJB
N

), in the sense that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}}|V^{N}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})-U(t,m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N})|=0.

When
H
is convex, (
1.1
) is related to the convergence problem in mean field control, and can be verified by control-theoretic compactness arguments (see
[
Lac17
]
and
[
DPT22a
]
). In the convex setting, it is in fact possible to sharpen the asymptotic statement (
1.1
) with a rate of convergence, see
[
CDJS
,
DDJ
]
. When
H
is not convex, the convergence (
1.1
) is not as well studied, but has been established in the case of purely common noise in
[
GMS21
]
.

As indicated in the title, the purpose of this paper is to address well-posedness for (
HJB
) (in particular the comparison principle) and convergence for (

HJB
N

) in the presence of both idiosyncratic and common noise (
a_{0}>0
) and without assuming that
H
is convex in
p
. We briefly summarize our main results here (precise assumptions and results will be state in Section
2
below):

•

We prove a comparison principle (Theorem
2.7
), and hence uniqueness, for viscosity solutions of (
HJB
) for general (nonconvex)
H
and under minimal growth and regularity assumptions the data and the solutions. In particular, Lipschitz regularity is measured with respect to the
\mathbf{d}_{1}
-metric, which generalizes results of
[
SY22
,
BEZ
]
(see the discussion below).

•

Relying purely on PDE arguments, we establish the convergence statement (
1.1
) (Theorem
2.8
). We thus extend the convergence results of
[
Lac17
,
DPT22a
,
GMS21
]
to equations with both common noise and idiosyncratic noise and with possibly non-convex Hamiltonians. In order to prove this convergence result, we verify that every “limit point” of the sequence
(V^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
(given by (

HJB
N

)) is a viscosity solution of (
HJB
). Together with a compactness argument and the comparison principle, this implies that there exists a unique viscosity solution of (
HJB
) (Theorem
2.6
), and it arises as the limit of the solutions of the finite-dimensional equations (

HJB
N

).

•

The equation (
HJB
) is shown in Theorem
2.9
to propagate regularity. Namely, if
H
and
G
are sufficiently smooth, then the unique viscosity solution
U
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with respect to the
C^{-k}
-metric, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
depends on the smoothness of
H
and
G
.

•

Finally, we use the equations (
HJB
) and (

HJB
N

) to study mean field control and mean field zero-sum games (depending on whether or not
H
is convex), as well as the analogous large population models. Among the new results we prove are a) the characterization of the value function from mean field control with idiosyncratic and common noise as the unique viscosity solution of (
HJB
), b) the convergence (
1.1
) for the upper and lower value functions for
N
-player stochastic differential games to the corresponding value functions for the mean field problems, and c) the coincidence of the upper and lower mean-field value functions under the so-called Isaacs condition.

1.1.
Some existing approaches to comparison

In order to better explain the difficulties we face, we now summarize a number of existing approaches to the comparison principle, and explain why they do not seem to apply to equations with non-convex Hamiltonians and/or both common and idiosyncratic noise.

Exploiting the connection to mean field control:

When
H
is convex in
p
, the connection to mean field control makes it relatively easy to establish the existence of a solution - one checks by dynamic programming that the value function of the control problem is a viscosity solution of the equation. This connection can in fact also be helpful in establishing a comparison principle. For example, when
a_{0}=0
and
H
is convex in
p
, the equation (
HJB
) is covered by the results in
[
CGK+21
]
. While in some ways the comparison principle in
[
CGK+21
]
is remarkably general (it covers a class of fully non-linear equations), the approach in
[
CGK+21
]
relies heavily on the connection to mean field control. In particular, in this setting the convergence (
1.1
) has already been established by control-theoretic arguments in
[
Lac17
]
, and the proof of comparison in
[
CGK+21
]
uses this convergence result heavily. Thus while it is possible that the techniques of
[
CGK+21
]
could be adapted to the case
a_{0}>0
, it does not seem that they can be used when the Hamiltonian
H
is not convex in
p
.

The lifting approach:

For equations with no idiosyncratic noise (roughly speaking, this means that the
(1+a_{0})
appearing in (
HJB
) is replaced with
a_{0}
), it is possible to define a notion of viscosity (sub/super)-solutions by “lifting” the equation to a Hilbert space of square-integrable random variables as in
[
GT19
]
. An extensive survey of optimal control problems in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces can be found in the book
[
FGS17
]
. See also
[
GMS21
]
and
[
MS23
]
, where comparison principles obtained through this lifting approach are used to show convergence. The lifting approach does not rely on the control formulation, and so works for non-convex Hamiltonians. On the other hand, there is a major obstacle to adapting this approach to the case of non-zero idiosyncratic noise - roughly speaking, the “common noise operator”

(1.2)

\displaystyle a_{0}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}(D_{x}D_{m}U)(t,m,x)m(dx)+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}(D_{mm}U)(t,m,x,y)m(dx)m(dy)\Big)

appearing in (
HJB
) “lifts” nicely to the Hilbert space, but the “idiosyncratic noise operator”

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}(D_{x}D_{m}U)(t,m,x)m(dx)

results in a singular second-order operator. For a short, self-contained explanation of this difficulty, see the introduction of
[
DS23
]
. Thus the lifting approach does not seem immediately applicable to the equation (
HJB
).

Doubling of variables with entropy penalization:

One of the basic roadblocks when trying to prove the comparison principle for (
HJB
) is that many of the natural metrics on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
(for example the 1-Wasserstein metric
\mathbf{d}_{1}
or the 2-Wasserstein metric
\mathbf{d}_{2}
) do not have good regularity properties, and in particular maps like
m\mapsto\mathbf{d}_{2}^{2}(m,m_{0})
for fixed
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
are not typically smooth. This means that these metrics cannot be used naively as a penalization in a standard doubling of variables argument. The recent work
[
DS23
]
of the first and last named authors circumvents this issue by adding an extra “entropy penalization” in the doubling of variables argument, to force the maximum in the relevant optimization problem to occur at a place where the squared 2-Wasserstein distance is in fact differentiable in an appropriate sense; see also works of Feng et al. where similar techniques were used in a slightly different context
[
FK09
,
FMZ21
,
FS13
,
FN12
]
. This approach ultimately led to a comparison principle for an equation of the form (
HJB
) with nonconvex
H
but with no common noise (
a_{0}=0
), but it seems that there are serious challenges to adapting the approach to the case
a_{0}>0
. Moreover, we mention that while the comparison principle in
[
DS23
]
is established without convexity of
H
, the notion of viscosity solution employed there is more strict than the one we use here, and this makes existence more challenging. In particular, existence is established in
[
DS23
]
only when
H
is convex in
p
, by appealing to the control formulation.

Doubling of variables with weaker metrics:

Another way to get around the regularity issues in the standard doubling of variables argument is to simply work with a metric
\rho
whose square is sufficiently smooth. Heuristically, it seems that
weaker
metrics on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
have better regularity properties, and in particular
[
SY22
]
showed that a comparison result can be obtained by doubling variables and penalizing with the metric inherited from a “sufficiently negative” Sobolev space. This idea was later combined with a clever change of variables in
[
BEZ
]
to cover the case
a_{0}>0
. In particular,
[
BEZ
]
took advantage of the fact that the “common noise operator” appearing in (
HJB
) can be re-cast as a finite-dimensional Laplacian. This allowed
[
BEZ
]
to obtain the first comparison principle for an equation of the form (
HJB
) (in fact, a much more general version of (
HJB
)) with both common and idiosyncratic noise. Their strategy was to double variables and penalize with a weak metric, as in
[
SY22
]
, and then handle the additional challenge of the common noise operator with a finite-dimensional Ishii’s lemma.

The drawback of working with weaker metrics is that, in order to execute the doubling of variables argument, it seems necessary to assume that either the subsolution or the supersolution under consideration is uniformly Lipschitz with respect to this weak metric (see the hypotheses in Theorem 4.1 of
[
SY22
]
and Theorem 3.2 of
[
BEZ
]
). In general it is not a priori clear when there exists a solution satisfying this Lipschitz regularity, which makes these comparison principles difficult to apply. Even in the case of (
HJB
) with
H
convex in
p
, the value function of the associated mean field control problem is not expected to be uniformly Lipschitz with respect to the relevant weak metric unless the data is very smooth and the noise is non-degenerate (see
[
DDJ
]
for some results along these lines in the case
a_{0}=0
). It is therefore not clear whether the comparison principles in
[
BEZ
,
SY22
]
can be applied apart from these special situations.

1.2.
Our results

To explain our main results, we first need to discuss our definition of viscosity (sub/super)solutions. The notion of viscosity solution we adopt is inspired by the proof strategy in
[
BEZ
]
, and in particular the observation that if
U
is smooth and we define a function
\overline{U}=\overline{U}(t,z,m):[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
via the formula

(1.4)

\displaystyle\overline{U}(t,z,m)=U\big(t,(\text{Id}+z)_{\#}m\big),

then we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{z}\overline{U}(t,z,m)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}(D_{x}D_{m}U\big)(t,m^{z},x)m^{z}(dx)+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(D_{mm}U\big)(t,m^{z},x,y)m^{z}(dx)m^{z}(dy),

where here and throughout the paper we use the notation

(1.5)

\displaystyle m^{z}=(\text{Id}+z)_{\#}m

for simplicity. Using this observation, one can check that if
U
is a smooth solution to (
HJB
), then
\overline{U}
satisfies the PDE

(
\text{HJB}_{z}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\overline{U}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}(D_{x}D_{m}\overline{U}\big)(t,z,m,x)m(dx)-a_{0}\Delta_{z}\overline{U}(t,z,m)\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\overline{H}(x,z,D_{m}\overline{U}(t,z,m,x),m\big)m(dx)=0,\quad(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\overline{U}(T,z,m)=\overline{G}(z,m),\quad(z,m)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\end{cases}

where we have set

(1.6)

\displaystyle\overline{H}(x,z,p,m)\coloneqq H(x+z,p,m^{z}),\quad\overline{G}(z,m)\coloneqq G(m^{z}).

The equation (

HJB
z

) is much easier to deal with, because the key “common noise operator,” which contains true second-order derivatives in the measure variable, has been exchanged for a finite-dimensional Laplacian. Thus we define viscosity solutions for the original equation (
HJB
) in terms of viscosity solutions of the transformed equation (

HJB
z

), i.e. we say a function
U
is a viscosity (sub/super)solution of (
HJB
) if the function
\overline{U}
defined by (
1.4
) is a viscosity (sub/super)solution of (

HJB
z

). Viscosity (sub/super)solutions of (

HJB
z

), meanwhile, are defined in a natural way in terms of smooth test functions (see Definition
2.2
below for the precise definition).

Our main results are (i) existence, (ii) the comparison principle, and (iii) convergence of the finite-dimensional approximations for the equation (
HJB
) under the assumption that
G
is Lipschitz with respect to
\mathbf{d}_{1}
, and
H
satisfies a structural condition like

\displaystyle|H(x,p,m)-H(x^{\prime},p^{\prime},m^{\prime})|\leq C(1+|p|+|p^{\prime}|)\big(|x-x^{\prime}|+|p-p^{\prime}|+\mathbf{d}_{1}(m,m^{\prime})\big).

We emphasize two points about our main results. First, our comparison principle allows to compare between a subsolution
U^{1}
which is merely upper semi-continuous and a supersolution
U^{2}
which is merely lower semi-continuous, unlike
[
DS23
]
,
[
SY22
]
,
[
BEZ
]
, which all require some sort of Lipschitz continuity for one or both of the semisolutions. Second, our existence result does not rely on the representation of the solution in terms of a stochastic control problem or game, and instead we construct the solution as a “limit point” of the solutions of the finite-dimensional PDEs (

HJB
N

). While the change of variables we employ in some sense enlarges the set of test functions available (making comparison easier), the fact that we can construct a solution in a straightforward way (without the help of a representation formula) indicates that our set of test functions is not “too large”, i.e. the notion of viscosity solution we employ is not too restrictive to be widely applicable.

In Section (
6
), we treat two applications. In Subsection
6.1
, we use our main results to identify the value function of a mean field control problem with common noise as the unique viscosity solution of the corresponding dynamic programming equation. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first such characterization. In the same setting, our convergence result also gives a new analytical proof of the convergence of the values of the finite particle stochastic control problems towards their mean field limit, although we still rely on the dynamic programming principle in
[
DPT22b
]
to check rigorously that the value function is a viscosity solution of the relevant equation. In Subsection
6.2
, we treat a zero-sum game on the Wasserstein space (without common noise, for technical reasons). This game was introduced in
[
CP19
]
, where dynamic programming principles for the upper and lower value functions were established. Here we obtain for the first time a rigorous proof that (i) the upper and lower value functions
U^{+}
and
U^{-}
arise as the uniform limits of corresponding finite-dimensional upper and lower value functions
(V^{N,+})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(V^{N,-})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and (ii)
U^{+}=U^{-}
when Isaacs’ condition holds.

1.3.
Proof strategy

Our proof of comparison starts by following the strategy of
[
BEZ
]
to prove a
partial
comparison principle, i.e. a comparison result under the additional assumption that one of the semi-solutions has some extra regularity. In particular, we prove in Proposition
5.4
a comparison principle assuming that one of the semisolutions is
H^{-k}
-Lipschitz, i.e. Lipschitz with respect to the metric inherited from the negative Sobolev space
H^{-k}
defined in Section
5
for some large
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Much of the paper is devoted removing this extra regularity assumption by constructing approximate sub and supersolutions of (
HJB
) with
H^{-k}
-Lipschitz regularity. Since we are able to prove that any limit point of the sequence
(V^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is a solution of (
HJB
), we thus reduce the problem to proving corresponding regularity estimates for the finite-dimensional equations (

HJB
N

). Unfortunately, it does not seem directly possible to prove an estimate of the form

(1.7)

\displaystyle|V^{N}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})-V^{N}(t,{\boldsymbol{y}})|\leq C\|m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N}-m_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}^{N}\|_{-s}=C\sup_{\|f\|_{s}\leq 1}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(f(x^{i})-f(y^{i})\big)

uniformly in
N
.
On the other hand, it is simple enough to establish the estimate

\displaystyle|D_{x^{i}}V^{N}|\leq C/N,

and therefore the results of
[
FG15
]
imply that, if
\sup_{(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})}|V^{N_{k}}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})-U(t,m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N_{k}})|\to 0
for some subsequence
(N_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, then it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{V}^{N_{k}}\to U,\text{ uniformly on }[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\text{where }\widehat{V}^{N}(t,m)\coloneqq\int_{(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}}V^{N}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})m^{\otimes N}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}).

Remarkably, understanding the Lipschitz regularity of
\widehat{V}^{N}
with respect to negative Sobolev spaces is much more tractable than trying to prove an estimate like (
1.7
). Indeed, explicit computing the linear derivative
\frac{\delta\widehat{V}^{N}}{\delta m}
reveals that
\widehat{V}^{N}
is
C^{-k}
-Lipschitz (hence
H^{-k}
-Lipschitz), uniformly in
N
, provided we can find a constant
C
such that the estimate

(1.8)

\displaystyle\|D_{x^{i}}^{j}V^{N}\|_{\infty}\leq C/N

holds for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,...,N
, and
1\leq j\leq k
.

The estimate (
1.8
) is proved by differentiating (

HJB
N

) repeatedly. As a consequence, the constant
C
in (
1.8
) depends on higher order derivatives of
H
and
G
in the measure variable. On the other hand, constructing the regular sub/supersolutions of (
HJB
) involves replacing
H
and
G
by smooth sequences
(H_{n})
and
(G_{n})
through a mollification procedure. The resulting constant
C
in (
1.8
) thus blows up in
n
, which provides a complication in showing that the
H^{-s}
-Lipschitz functions we obtain from limits of
(V^{N})
are in fact sub/supersolutions up to small factors that converge to zero as
n\to\infty
. We must then carefully factor the estimate (
1.8
) as

(1.9)

\displaystyle\|D_{x^{i}}^{j}V^{N}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}/N+o(1/N),

where now the constant
C_{0}
depends only on bounds for the
first
derivatives in the measure variable of
G
and
H
.

The most technical part of the paper is Section
3
, where we verify that (
1.9
) holds provided that the
H
and
G
are sufficiently smooth. The strategy for proving (
1.9
) is reminiscent of the nonlinear adjoint method. In particular, we repeatedly differentiate (

HJB
N

), and integrate the resulting equation against an auxiliary Fokker-Planck equation. The
j^{\text{th}}
iteration of this procedure yields pointwise bounds like (
1.9
), but also
L^{2}
bounds on quantities like
DD_{x^{i}}^{j}V^{N}
, when integrated against the auxiliary Fokker-Planck equation. Both the
L^{\infty}
and
L^{2}
bounds obtained on the
j^{\text{th}}
iteration are “plugged in” to the
(j+1)^{\text{th}}
iteration, and in the end this scheme allows to prove (
1.9
) for all relevant
k
through induction.

To complete the proof of the comparison principle, we start with a viscosity subsolution
U^{1}
and a viscosity supersolution
U^{2}
, and assume without loss of generality that both are strict semi-solutions. Then we mollify the data, to produce sequences
(H^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(G^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We use the estimates discussed above to verify that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can produce a solution
W^{n}
to the equation (
HJB
) (with
H
replaced by
H^{n}
and
G
replaced by
G^{n}
) which is
H^{-k}
-Lipschitz and which is
almost
a solution to the original equation (
HJB
) in the viscosity sense. This allows us to conclude that for large
n
,

\displaystyle U^{1}\leq W^{n}\leq U^{2},

and in particular
U^{1}\leq U^{2}
as required.

1.4.
Outlook

There are a number of natural questions about the broader applicability of our approach, which we briefly address here. First, we expect that it should be possible to extend our results to non-compact state space (i.e. replace
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). The key modifications would be to work with weighted Sobolev spaces, and to use a variational principle as in e.g.
[
BEZ23
]
in the doubling of variables argument. Also, there should be no problem with allowing non-constant (but uncontrolled ) idiosyncratic noise, which amounts to replacing the idiosyncratic noise operator (
1.3
) with an operator of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(a(t,x,m)D_{x}D_{m}U(t,m,x)\big)m(dx),

provided that
a
is smooth enough and non-degenerate.

On the other hand, we rely heavily on the non-degeneracy of the idiosyncratic noise, since without this the key estimate (

) is in general false, even if all the data is smooth. Thus, extending our results to allow for degenerate noise seems to require new ideas. For the same reason, it is not clear how to handle fully non-linear equations using our methods. Also, because we use the fact that the common noise operator can be understood as a finite-dimensional derivative, it is important that the common noise have constant coefficients, which is certainly a major restriction (which also appears in
[
BEZ
]
, the only previous paper to treat an equation with both common and idiosyncratic noise). It is the authors’ view that treating an equation like (

) with variable common noise (and non-zero idiosyncratic noise) is an important open question, since it seems to require a genuinely infinite-dimensional version of Ishii’s lemma.

2.1.
Basic notation

We define
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
the flat

the set of Borel probability measures over it. We endow the latter with the first Wasserstein distance

, defined, for all

by

where the supremum is defined over the

\varphi:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
With this metric,
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
becomes a compact space.
Unless specified differently, semicontinuity or Lipschitz continuity for functions defined over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is understood with respect to this distance.

For

we denote by

the space of functions over
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with continuous derivatives up to order

and we endow it with the norm

\left\|f\right\|_{C^{k}}:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\;|j|\leq k}\left\|D^{j}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

where
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
is the usual supremum norm, and, for

, we set
|j|=j_{1}+j_{2}+\cdots+j_{d}
and

D^{j}:=\frac{\partial^{j_{1}}}{\partial x_{1}^{j_{1}}}\dots\frac{\partial^{j_{d}}}{\partial x_{d}^{j_{d}}}.

We denote by
C^{-k}
its dual space, ie the space of bounded linear functionals over
C^{k}
and we denote by
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C^{-k}}
the dual norm.

Following
[
CD18
]
Chapter 5, we say that

belongs to

if there is a jointly continuous map
\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

(2.1)

\Phi(m_{2})-\Phi(m_{1})=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}\bigl((1-h)m_{1}+hm_{2},x)d(m_{2}-m_{1})(x)dh.

The linear derivative
\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}
is defined up to an additive constant and we adopt the normalizing convention (similar to
[
DDJ
]
)

(2.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x)dx=0,\quad\forall m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

When

we define the intrinsic derivative of
\Phi
at

by

D_{m}\Phi(m,x)=D_{x}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x).

Unlike the linear derivative, the intrinsic derivative is uniquely defined if it exists.

For

,

, and

, we frequently use the notation

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{y}}^{-i}=(y^{1},...,y^{i-1},y^{i+1},...,y^{N})\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N-1},\quad({\boldsymbol{y}}^{-i},x)=(y^{1},...,y^{i-1},x,y^{i+1},...,y^{N})\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}.

Proof of Lemma

Observe first that

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V^{N,k}

Let

Remark 3.6

When

4.

We will call a function

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N_{k}}}\big|U(t,m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N_{k}})-V^{N_{k}}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\big|\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0.

Proposition 4.1

Suppose that Assumption

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that

By (

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,z,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}}\big|\overline{U}(t,z,m_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{N})-\overline{V}^{N}(t,z,{\boldsymbol{x}})\big|

5.
Comparison and convergence

The goal of this section is to prove the comparison principle, Theorem
2.7
, and explain how it implies the convergence result, Theorem
2.8
. We fix throughout this section a
k\in\mathbb{N}
with

\displaystyle k>d/2+2.

We start with some preliminary notation and reminders about the Sobolev spaces
H^{k}
and
H^{-k}.
First, we define

H^{k}:=\bigl\{\varphi\in L^{2};\mbox{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\mbox{ with }|j|\leq k,D^{j}\varphi\mbox{ exists in the weak sense and belongs to }L^{2}\bigr\}

It becomes a Hilbert space when endowed with the inner product

\langle\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\rangle_{k}:=\sum_{|j|\leq k}\langle D^{j}\varphi_{1},D^{j}\varphi_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

We denote by
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{k}
the associated norm. We define
H^{-k}
as the dual of
H^{k}
, i.e. the set of bounded linear functionals over
H^{k}
, and we denote

\left\|q\right\|_{-k}:=\sup_{\varphi\in H^{k},\left\|\varphi\right\|_{k}\leq 1}q(\varphi)

the associated norm. For any
q\in H^{-k}
we denote by
q^{*}
its dual element, i.e. the unique element of
H^{k}
satisfying

q(\varphi)=\langle q^{*},\varphi\rangle_{k},\quad\mbox{for all }\varphi\in H^{k}.

The map
q\mapsto q^{*}
is an isometry from
H^{-k}
into
H^{k}
, if the inner product in
H^{-k}
is defined by
\langle\mu,\nu\rangle_{-k}=\langle\mu^{*},\nu^{*}\rangle_{k}
, which also coincides with the definition of the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{-k}
.

Recall that, for
k>d/2+2
,
H^{k}
continuously embeds in
C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. In particular,
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
can be seen as a compact subset of
H^{-k}
via the identification
\mu(\varphi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi d\mu
, for all
\varphi\in H^{k}
and all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Finally from
[
DDJ
]
Lemma 2.11, or a simple computation, (the restriction of ) any function
\Phi\in C^{1}(H^{-k})
(in the classical Hilbertian sense) belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and

\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x)=D_{-k}\Phi(m)(x)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(D_{-k}\Phi(m)\bigr)(y)dy,

where
D_{-k}\Phi\in H^{k}
is the (Frechet) differential of
\Phi.
In particular, by our choice of convention (
2.2
), for any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|_{-k}^{2}
belongs to

with

\frac{\delta}{\delta m}(\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|^{2}_{-k})=(\mu-\nu)^{*}.

Remark 5.1

The Hilbert spaces

and

introduced above can be explicitly defined in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the corresponding elements, as in
[

is a smoothing operator that can be explicitly described by the inverse of a certain differential operator, although this particular characterization is not relevant to the results in this section.

Let us also note that the idea of measuring the distance between

U^{\gamma}(t,z,\mu):=U(t,z,\mu)-\gamma(T-t)-\gamma\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{T}\right).

It is straightforward to check that

(5.7)

\left\{\begin{split}&U^{\gamma}(T,\cdot,\cdot)=U(T,\cdot,\cdot),\quad U^{\gamma}\leq U,\quad\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}U^{\gamma}=-\infty,\quad\text{and}\\
&\text{$U^{\gamma}$ is a subsolution of \eqref{hjbz} with right-hand side $-\gamma$.}\end{split}\right.

By (
5.6
) and compactness of
[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we can choose
\gamma>0
small enough that

(5.8)

(t,z,m)\mapsto U^{\gamma}(t,z,m)-V(t,z,m)

attains a strictly positive maximum value
\omega>0
at some
(t_{*},z_{*},m_{*})
.
In view of the penalizing property (
5.7
) and the fact that
U^{\gamma}(T,\cdot,\cdot)=U(T,\cdot,\cdot)\leq V(T,\cdot,\cdot)
, we have
t_{*}\in(0,T)
. For
\epsilon>0
we define the functional
\Phi^{\epsilon}:[0,T]^{2}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}\times\big(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\big)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Phi^{\epsilon}(\theta)=U^{\gamma}(s,y,\mu)-V(t,z,\nu)-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|_{-k}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|y-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|t-s|^{2}

where
\theta=(s,t,y,z,\mu,\nu)
is a generic element of
[0,T]^{2}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}\times\big(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\big)^{2}.

Moreover, we define

\displaystyle M^{\epsilon}:=\sup_{\theta\in[0,T]^{2}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}\times\bigl(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\bigr)^{2}}\Phi^{\epsilon}(\theta)

and we denote by
\overline{\theta}:=(\overline{s},\overline{t},\overline{y},\overline{z},\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu})
a maximizer of
\Phi^{\epsilon}
. The map
\epsilon\rightarrow M^{\epsilon}
is non-increasing and lower bounded by
\omega
and therefore converges as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. In view of the inequality

M^{\epsilon}\leq M^{\epsilon/2}-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\overline{y}-\overline{z}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\overline{s}-\overline{t}|^{2}

and the fact that
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}M^{\epsilon}-M^{\epsilon/2}=0
we conclude that

(5.9)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\overline{y}-\overline{z}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\overline{s}-\overline{t}|^{2}=0

and every (weak) limit point of
(\overline{s},\overline{y},\overline{\mu})
and
(\overline{t},\overline{z},\overline{\nu})
must coincide and be a maximum of
U^{\gamma}-V
. In particular
\overline{s},\overline{t}
belong to
(0,T)
for
\epsilon
small enough.

Step 2: Estimates from regularity.
Assume without loss of generality that
U
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mu
with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{-k}
with Lipschitz constant
L>0
, uniformly in
(t,z)
. Then, comparing
M^{\epsilon}
with
\Phi^{\epsilon}(\overline{s},\overline{t},\overline{y},\overline{z},\overline{\nu},\overline{\nu})
, we have

\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}^{2}\leq U^{\gamma}(\overline{s},\overline{y},\overline{\mu})-U^{\gamma}(\overline{s},\overline{y},\overline{\nu})\leq L\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}

which leads to

(5.10)

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}\leq 2L.

Since
k\geq d/2
, the classical Sobolev embedding implies also that

(5.11)

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left\|(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}\left\|(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}\right\|_{k}=\frac{C}{\epsilon}\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}\leq 2CL,

for some
C>0
depending on dimension only.

At this stage, we need the following key lemma, which is a sort of “parameterized version” of the classical Ishii’s lemma in finite dimensions.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\overline{\theta}=(\overline{s},\overline{t},\overline{y},\overline{z},\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu})
be a local maximum of
\Phi^{\epsilon}
. For every
\kappa>0
we can find two matrices

satisfying the matrix inequality

(5.12)

-\bigl(\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigr)I_{2d}\leq\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{pmatrix}\leq\frac{1+2\kappa}{2\epsilon}\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}

and such that

(5.13)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{s}-\overline{t}),\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{y}-\overline{z}),X,\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}\Bigr)\in\overline{J}^{2,+}U^{\gamma}\bigl(\overline{s},\overline{y},\overline{\mu}\bigr),\\
\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{s}-\overline{t}),\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{y}-\overline{z}),Y,\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}\Bigr)\in\overline{J}^{2,-}V\bigl(\overline{t},\overline{z},\overline{\nu}\bigr).\end{array}

We continue with the proof of the comparison principle. Using the sub-solution property, as in Lemma
5.4
, for
U^{\gamma}
at
(\overline{s},\overline{y},\overline{\mu})

and we get

(5.14)

-\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{s}-\overline{t})-a_{0}\operatorname{tr}{X}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta_{x}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}(x)d\overline{\mu}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\Bigl(x+\overline{y},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\mu}\Bigr)d\overline{\mu}(x)\leq-\gamma.

Arguing similarly for

we find

(5.15)

-\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{s}-\overline{t})-a_{0}\operatorname{tr}{Y}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta_{x}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}(x)d\overline{\nu}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\Bigl(x+\overline{z},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\nu}\Bigr)d\overline{\nu}(x)\geq 0.

Subtracting the two inequalities and rearranging then leads to

(5.16)

\gamma\leq I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\Bigl(x+\overline{z},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\nu}\Bigr)d\overline{\nu}(x)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\Bigl(x+\overline{y},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\mu}\Bigr)d\overline{\mu}(x),

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=a_{0}\operatorname{tr}(X-Y),

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Delta_{x}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}(x)d(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})(x).

In view respectively of the matrix inequality (
5.12
) in Lemma
5.5
as well as the non-positivity of the Laplacian in
H^{k}
, we know that
I_{2}\leq 0
and
I_{3}\leq 0
. It remains to estimate

We rewrite

\displaystyle I_{1}=I_{1,a}+I_{1,b},\text{ with }

\displaystyle I_{1,a}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\Bigl(x+\overline{z},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\mu}\Bigr)d(\overline{\nu}-\overline{\mu})(x),

\displaystyle I_{1,b}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Big[H\Bigl(x+\overline{z},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\nu}\Bigr)-H\Bigl(x+\overline{y},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*},\overline{\mu}\Bigr)\Bigr]d\overline{\mu}(x).

On the one hand, using Assumption
2.5
as well as (
5.10
) (with

), we see that

\displaystyle x\mapsto H\Big(x+\overline{z},\frac{1}{\epsilon}(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})^{*}(x),\overline{\mu}\Big)

is, uniformly in
\epsilon
, Hölder continuous. Since
\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\|_{-k}\rightarrow 0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we conclude easily that

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}I_{1,a}=0.

On the other hand, using Assumption (
2.5
)
and recalling (

) we find that

|I_{1,b}|\leq C\bigl(|\overline{z}-\overline{y}|+\mathbf{d}_{1}(\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu})\bigr),

for some

independent from
\epsilon
. From (
5.9
), we have
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}(\overline{z}-\overline{y})=0
and
\lim_{\rightarrow 0}\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|_{-k}=0
which leads to

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}I_{1,b}=0.

Coming back to (
5.16
) we find, letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,

\gamma\leq 0,

which is the desired contradiction.
∎

It remains to prove Lemma
5.5
, which we do similarly as in
[
BEZ
]
. We first prove the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space with norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
and the inner product of
\mu,\nu\in H
denoted by
\mu\cdot\nu
. Take
\mu,\nu,\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}
in

. Then

\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|^{2}\leq 2\mu\cdot(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})-2\nu\cdot(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})+2\left\|\mu-\overline{\mu}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\nu-\overline{\nu}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|^{2}

and inequality holds if and only if
\mu-\overline{\mu}=\nu-\overline{\nu}
.

Proof.

Developing the square
\left\|\mu-\overline{\mu}+\overline{\nu}-\nu\right\|^{2}
and rearranging leads to

\left\|\mu-\nu\right\|^{2}+\left\|\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu}\right\|^{2}=2\mu\cdot(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})-2\nu\cdot(\overline{\mu}-\overline{\nu})+\left\|\mu-\overline{\mu}+\overline{\nu}-\nu\right\|^{2}.

It remains to notice that

\left\|\mu-\overline{\mu}+\overline{\nu}-\nu\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|\mu-\overline{\mu}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\nu-\overline{\nu}\right\|^{2}

with equality if and only if
\mu-\overline{\mu}=\nu-\overline{\nu}.

∎

6.
Applications

6.1.
Mean-field control with common noise

The goal of this subsection is to show that

(1)

Theorem
2.7
allows us to identify the value function of a mean field control problem with common noise as the unique viscosity solution (in the sense of Definition
2.4
) of the corresponding HJB equation, and

(2)

Theorem
2.8
yields a new analytical proof that the value functions of certain finite-dimensional control problems converge to their mean field counterparts.

In order to do so, we will need to use the dynamic programming principle from
[
DPT22b
]
, and so we take care to formulate the mean field problem in a way which is compatible with the main results in
[
DPT22b
]
. The data in this setting will consist of

\displaystyle(b,L):\mathbb{T}^{d}\times A\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\quad G:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R},

where
A
is, for simplicity, a compact metric space. We set define
H:\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
by

(6.1)

\displaystyle H(x,p,m)=\sup_{a\in A}\bigl\{-L(x,a,m)-b(x,a,m)\cdot p\bigr\}.

We assume the following on the data:

Assumption 6.1
.

The functions
L
and
b
are continuous, and Lipschitz in
(x,m)
, uniformly in
a
. The function
G
is Lipschitz.

Remark 6.2
.

Under Assumption
6.1
, it is easy to check that
H
and
G
satisfy Assumption
2.5
. It should be possible to relax the assumption that
L
is uniformly Lipschitz in
(x,m)
, but this Assumption is needed to apply the results in
[
DPT22b
]
.

To define the mean field value function, we work on the canonical space

\displaystyle\Omega^{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C},\text{ with }\mathcal{C}=C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}).

For each
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, let
\mathbb{P}^{\mu}
denote the probability measure

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mu}=\mu\otimes\mathbb{P}^{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{0},

where
\mathbb{P}^{0}
is the Wiener measure on
C
. Write
(x,\omega,\omega^{0})
for the general element of
\Omega^{0}
, and denote by
(\xi,W,W^{0})
the canonical random variable and processes on

6.2.
A zero-sum game on the Wasserstein space

Here we consider a zero-sum game, which is formulated as follows. First, the game will be set on a fixed filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
satisfying the usual conditions and hosting a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
W=(W_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
. We furthermore assume that
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is atomless, and
\mathbb{F}
is the (augmentation of) the filtration generated by
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
W
. For simplicity, we fix compact metric spaces
A^{1}
and
A^{2}
. The data of the game consists of functions

\displaystyle(b,L)(x,a^{,}a^{2},m):\mathbb{T}^{d}\times A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\quad G:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}.

We make the following assumptions on the data.

Assumption 6.5
.

The functions
b
and
L
are continuous, and Lipschitz in
(x,m)
uniformly in
a^{1},a^{2}
. The function
G
is Lipschitz.

For each
t_{0}\in[0,T]
,
i=1,2
we denote by
\mathcal{A}^{i}_{t_{0}}
the set of all progressive processes
\alpha^{i}
defined on
[t_{0},T]
and taking values in
A^{i}
. Informally, the game is played as follows. We fix
(t_{0},m_{0})\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and then choose a
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\xi
with
\xi\sim m_{0}
. The dynamics of the state process
X
is given by

(6.7)

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b\big(X_{t},\alpha_{t}^{1},\alpha^{2}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X_{t})\big)dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},\quad t_{0}\leq t\leq T,\quad X_{t_{0}}=\xi,

and the payoff is determined by a functional

(6.8)

\displaystyle J(t_{0},\xi,\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2})=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}L\big(X_{t},\alpha_{t}^{1},\alpha_{t}^{2},\mathcal{L}(X_{t})\big)dt+G(X_{T})\bigg],\quad\text{where $X$ is given by \eqref{xdefgame}.}

Player I chooses
\alpha^{1}\in\mathcal{A}^{1}_{t_{0}}
in order to maximize

, while Player II chooses
\alpha^{2}\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{t_{0}}
in order to minimize

\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}^{1,\text{s}}
the set of strategies for player I, i.e. the set of all maps

\alpha^{1,s}[\alpha^{2}]:\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}^{1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[\alpha^{2}_{r}=\overline{\alpha^{2}}_{r}\text{ for a.e. }r\in[t_{0},t]\Big]=1\implies\mathbb{P}\Big[\alpha^{1,\text{s}}\big[\alpha^{2}\big]_{r}=\alpha^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1,}\text{s}}\big[\overline{\alpha^{2}}\big]_{r}\text{ for a.e. }r\in[t_{0},t]\Big]=1.

The set
\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}^{2,\text{s}}
of strategies for player 2 is defined in a similar way. The lower value function

(6.9)

\displaystyle U^{-}(t_{0},m_{0})=\inf_{\alpha^{\text{2,\text{s}}}\in\mathcal{A}^{2,\text{s}}_{t_{0}}}\sup_{\alpha^{1}\in\mathcal{A}^{1}_{t_{0}}}J\big(t,\xi,\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2,\text{s}}\big[\alpha^{1}\big]\big),\quad\text{ where $\xi$ is $\mathcal{F}_{0}$-measurable and }\xi\sim m_{0}

and the upper value functions is defined similarly by

(6.10)

\displaystyle U^{+}(t_{0},m_{0})=\sup_{\alpha^{1,\text{s}}\in\mathcal{A}^{1,\text{s}}_{t_{0}}}\inf_{\alpha^{2}\in\mathcal{A}^{2}_{t_{0}}}J\big(t,\xi,\alpha^{1,\text{s}}\big[\alpha^{2}\big],\alpha^{2}\big),\quad\text{ where $\xi$ is $\mathcal{F}_{0}$-measurable and }\xi\sim m_{0}.
