A T(L) theorem for the Calderón–Zygmund operators in BMO ⁡ ( ℝ d ) \mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
Andrei V. Vasin

Abstract
. A variant of the global T ⁡ ( 1 ) T(1) criterion to characterize the bounded Calderón–Zygmund operators on BMO ⁡ ( ℝ d ) \mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) is proved. We apply it to the certain Calderón commutators.

Primary 42B20; Secondary 46E30

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

It is well known that Calderón–Zygmund operators bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, also are bounded from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
To extend Calderón–Zygmund operators on homogeneous
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and to have an estimate on seminorms

(1)

\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}},

it is assumed necessarily, that
T1=const
(for convolution operators see
[
7
]
).
One may escape this assumption and consider
normed

\mathrm{BMO}
on bounded domains
[
9
]
. This may be done even in non doubling setting for the Tolsa space
\textrm{RBMO}(\mu)
with finite measure
[
3
]
.
Closely related approach concerns the Hermite–Calderón–Zygmund operators on the Hermite
\mathrm{BMO}_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
space
[
1
]
.

In this paper we consider the classical space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as a normed space, where we define the Calderón–Zygmund operators. A criterion to characterize the bounded Calderón–Zygmund operators is proved. Formally, the result obtained is similar to ones in
[
1
,
9
]
and is related to characterization of pointwise multipliers in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
6
]
. We apply T(L) theorem to research the certain Calderón commutators on

\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

1.2.
The space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Let
Q=Q(c,\ell)
be a cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with sides parallel to the axes, with side length
\ell=\ell(Q)
and the centre
c=c(Q)
, and let
|Q|
be its volume.
The space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of those
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that have the finite seminorm

(2)

\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}=\sup_{Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf_{b_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f-b_{Q}|dx.

One may naturally define a norm in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
f_{Q}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}f(x)dx
be a standard average over a cube
Q
.
Fix a cube
Q_{0}
with the centre in origin and with side length equaled to 1.
Then the space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, equipped with the norm

(3)

\|f\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}=\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}+|f_{Q_{0}}|,

is a Banach space. Replacing
Q_{0}
by any other cube, we obtain a norm equivalent to initial one.

1.3.
Calderón–Zygmund operators

A Calderón–Zygmund kernel is a measurable function

K(x,y)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

satisfying the following conditions:

(4)

\displaystyle|K(x,y|\leq C|x-y|^{-d}

(5)

\displaystyle|K(x_{1},y)-K(x_{2},y)|\leq C\frac{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\delta}}{|x_{1}-y|^{d+\delta}},

where
2|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq|x_{1}-y|
and
\delta
,

0<\delta\leq 1
, is a regularity constant specific to the kernel
K
and
|x-y|
is euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

A singular integral operator defined as

(6)

Tf(x)=\int K(x,y)f(y)dy.

when
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\textrm{supp}f
is called a Calderón-Zygmund operator associated to the kernel
K(x,y)
if it is extended to a bounded operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Even
T
is bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in Section 2 we follow to the modified argument
[
8
, p. 156-157]
to indicate what does it mean that
T
is defined and bounded in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

1.4.
T(L) theorem and applications

To formulate the main result we introduce an auxilliary space. Given a cube
Q=Q(c,\ell)
,
let
\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{Q}(\tilde{c},\tilde{\ell})
be a smallest cube that contains a cube
Q
and the fixed cube
Q_{0}
. Define a function (indeed, a log–distance function between cubes
Q
and
Q_{0}
, similar to ones in
[
4
,
6
]
)

L(Q)=\log\max(\tilde{\ell}/\ell,\tilde{\ell})+1.

The logarithmic mean oscillation space
\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
consists of those
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that have finite seminorm

(7)

\|f\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}=\sup_{Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf_{b_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{L(Q)}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f-b_{Q}|dx.

Let us denote
1=\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and

\log_{s,-s}(x)=\log|x-s|-\log|x+s|
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The main theorem may be interpreted as a T(1) theorem, as well as a testing criterion on the certain family
\log|x|\cup\{\log_{s,-s}(x)\}_{s}
of typical unbounded functions in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
T
be a Calderón–Zygmund operator associated to a standard kernel
K
and bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The following properties are equivalent:

(i)

T
is bounded from the normed space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the homogeneous
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; there is an estimate with a constant independent of
f

(8)

\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\|f\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}};

(ii)

T1\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(iii)

T\log|x|,\,T\log_{s,-s}(x)\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\|T\log_{s,-s}(x)\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\|\log_{s,-s}(x)\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}},

uniformly with respect to
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 1
.

Observe that even seminorms
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
, as well
\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}
are correctly defined, we can not define
\int_{Q_{0}}Tf(x)dx
, in general. Therefore, we can not replace seminorm
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
by norm
\|Tf\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}
in (
8
) (a similar estimate of maximal function in BMO see in
[
8
, p. 179]
,
[
2
]
). We return to this issue in Section 2.3.

To illustrate the T(L) theorem, observe that if
T1=const
, and hence
\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}=0
, we obtain a standard estimate (
1
) on seminorms.

A more interesting application of the T(L) theorem concerns the Calderón commutators
\mathcal{C}_{A}
associated with the kernel

K_{A}(x,y)=\frac{A(x)-A(y)}{(x-y)^{2}},

where
A(x)\in Lip_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. The question in order is to state, for which
A(x)\in Lip_{1}(\mathbb{R})
the Calderón commutator
\mathcal{C_{A}}
is bounded on

\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R})
. We have the following sufficiency condition.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A(x)\in Lip_{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
A^{\prime}\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mathcal{C_{A}}
is bounded on
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 2
.

In fact, the condition
A^{\prime}\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
exactly characterises the pointwise multipliers on
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R})

[
6
]
, where one can find examples of the certain multipliers suitable for Theorem
1.2
.

The assumption
A^{\prime}\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R})
is sharp. We check this on
the Calderón commutator
\mathcal{C}
associated with
A(x)=|x|
. It turns out that
\mathcal{C}
is bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, as well,
from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
to
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R})

[
8
, Section 5.17]
. However,

\mathcal{C}
is unbounded on
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.
Auxiliary results

2.1.
Calderón–Zygmund operators from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

For any cube
Q
with the centre
c
, and for a bounded function
f
the following value is well defined

(9)

T_{Q}f(x)=\int_{2Q}K(x,y)f(y)dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}(K(x,y)-K(c,y))f(y)dy,\quad x\in Q,

where by
\alpha Q
we denote a cube
Q
dilated
\alpha
times with respect to its centre.
The first summand on the right is well defined since
T
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the second one is well defined by property (5) of the kernel
K
. Define the oscilation

\|f\|_{Q}=\inf_{b_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f-b_{Q}|dx.

Then we have

\|T_{Q}f\|_{Q}\leq C\|f\|_{\infty}

uniformly with respect to
Q
. If
Tf
is well defined, for instance for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

(10)

\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}=\sup_{Q}\|T_{Q}f\|_{Q}.

So, even
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not dense in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we may define seminorm
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
by (
10
) in any case. Also, we see that operator
T
so defined, is bounded from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to homogeneous
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

2.2.
Calderón–Zygmund operators on
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

We extend the construction of Section 2.1 on
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
f_{Q}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}f(x)dx
be the standard average over a cube
Q
.
Consider the following decomposition

f=f_{2Q}+(f-f_{2Q}).

Define

T_{Q}f=T_{Q}f_{2Q}+T_{Q}(f-f_{2Q})

by formula (
9
) separately for each summand.

Since
f_{2Q}
is a constant, then by Section 2.1, the first summand
T_{Q}f_{2Q}=f_{2Q}T_{Q}1
is well defined. Even seminorm
\|T_{Q}f_{2Q}\|_{Q}
depends obviously on
Q
, it is not issue for us.

For the second summand, we prove a next lemma that ascends to
[
5
]
;
see also
[
3
]
for non doubling measure and
[
1
]

for the Hermite–Calderón–Zygmund operators. We give the proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists an estimate for seminorms

\|T_{Q}(f-f_{2Q})\|_{Q}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

with a constant independent of
f
and
Q
.

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle T_{Q}(f-f_{2Q})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{2Q}K(x,y)(f(y)-f_{2Q})dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash 2Q}(K(x,y)-K(c,y))(f(y)-f_{2Q})dy.

For the first summand, we have by the Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\left|\int_{2Q}K(x,y)(f(y)-f_{2Q})dy\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\left|\int_{2Q}K(x,y)(f(y)-f_{2Q})dy\right|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}.

Since
T
is bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

I_{1}\leq C\left(\frac{1}{|2Q|}\int_{2Q}|f(y)-f_{2Q}|^{2}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and then by the John–Nirenberg inequality
[
8
, Corollary, p. 144 ]
for
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has

(11)

\leq C\frac{1}{|2Q|}\int_{2Q}|f(y)-f_{2Q}|dy\leq C\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}.

For the second summand, by property (5) of the kernel
K(x,y)
for
x\in Q
, one has

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}(K(x,y)-K(c,y))(f(y)-f_{2Q})dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}\frac{|x-c|^{\delta}}{|x-y|^{d+\delta}}|f(y)-f_{2Q}|dy.

Split the integral in dyadic sum

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell^{\delta}}{(2^{k-1}\ell)^{d+\delta}}\int_{2^{k+1}Q\setminus 2^{k}Q}|f(y)-f_{2Q}|dy

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell^{\delta}}{(2^{k}\ell)^{d+\delta}}\left(\int_{2^{k+1}Q}|f(y)-f_{2^{k+1}Q}|\,dy+|2^{k+1}Q||f_{2Q}-f_{2^{k+1}Q}|\right).

Since
|f_{2Q}-f_{2^{k+1}Q}|\leq Ck\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell^{\delta}}{(2^{k}\ell)^{d+\delta}}|2^{k+2}Q|(1+k)\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{2^{k\delta}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}.

Thus, combining with the estimate for
I_{1}
, we have

\|T_{Q}(f-f_{2Q})\|_{Q}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}},

and we are done.
∎

Lemma
2.1
implies that for each cube
Q
and for each
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
seminorm
\|T_{Q}f\|_{Q}
is well defined. We are ready to define
T
on

\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Put for any
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{Q}\|T_{Q}f\|_{Q}

\displaystyle=\sup_{Q}\|f_{2Q}T_{Q}1+T_{Q}(f-f_{2Q})\|_{Q},

if the right side is finite.
Observe, that for
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the definition of seminorm
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
coincides with (
10
) from Section 2.1.

2.3.
Normed version of Theorem
1.1

Let us return back to the issue left in Remark 1 of Section 1.4. Obtained the correct definition of seminorm
\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
, one can not define
Tf
a.e. for
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. On the other hand,
T_{Q}f
from (
9
) is well defined a.e. for any
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. So far, for the fixed cube
Q_{0}
we write

\widetilde{T}f=T_{Q_{0}}f.

Since

T_{Q}f-T_{Q_{0}}f=C(Q,Q_{0})
is a constant for any cube
Q
, it holds

\|T_{Q}f\|_{Q}=\|T_{Q_{0}}f\|_{Q},

and therefore, one has equality for seminorms

\|\widetilde{T}f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}=\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}.

Also, replacing the fixed cube
Q_{0}
by any other cube, we obtain the same seminorm.
The advantage of the operator
\widetilde{T}
compared to
T
is that, now we can estimate the mean value

\displaystyle|(\widetilde{T}f)_{Q_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{2Q_{0}}|\|T_{Q_{0}}1\|_{Q_{0}}+\|T_{Q_{0}}(f-f_{2Q_{0}})\|_{Q_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C(|f_{2Q_{0}}|+\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}})

for
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This follows

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
T
be a Calderón–Zygmund operator associated to a standard kernel
K
and bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then any of the properties of Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to

\mathrm{(\tilde{i})}

\widetilde{T}
is bounded on the normed space
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and it holds

(12)

\|\widetilde{T}f\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\|f\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}

with a constant independent of
f
.

However, it must be considered that when the both operators are well defined, then
\widetilde{T}
is a one-dimensional perturbation of a Calderón-Zygmund operator
T

Tf(x)-\widetilde{T}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash 2Q_{0}}K(0,y)(f(y)-f_{2Q_{0}})dy.

3.
Proof of T(L) theorem

Implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii) is obvious. Let us prove others.

3.1.
Proof (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)

By Lemma
2.1
, we see that

\displaystyle\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\sup_{Q}|f_{2Q}|\,\|T_{Q}1\|+\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}})

Then,

|f_{2Q}|\leq|f_{2Q}-f_{\tilde{Q}}|+|f_{\tilde{Q}}-f_{Q_{0}}|+|f_{Q_{0}}|,

where
Q_{0}
is a fixed cube and
\tilde{Q}
is the smallest cube containing
2Q
and
Q_{0}
.
Clearly

|f_{2Q}-f_{\tilde{Q}}|\leq C(\log\tilde{\ell}/\ell+1)\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

and

|f_{Q_{0}}-f_{\tilde{Q}}|\leq C(\log\tilde{\ell}+1)\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
,
therefore,

(13)

|f_{2Q}|\leq CL(Q)\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}+|f_{Q_{0}}|,

and hence,

\displaystyle\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\sup_{Q}(L(Q)\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}+|f_{Q_{0}}|)\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}+\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}})

\displaystyle\leq C(\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\sup_{Q}L(Q)\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}+|f_{Q_{0}}|\sup_{Q}\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}+\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}).

Note that
\sup_{Q}L(Q)\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}=\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}
and
\sup_{Q}\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}=\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
, so continue

\displaystyle\|Tf\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}+|f_{Q_{0}}|\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}+\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}})

\displaystyle\leq C(\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}+\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}|f_{Q_{0}}|)

\displaystyle\leq C(\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}+\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}})\|f\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}.

Of course,
\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}<\infty
, since
T
is bounded from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to homogeneous
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This completes the proof (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

3.2.
Proof (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii)

We start with a lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\log_{Q}
be the mean value of the function
\log|x|
over a cube
Q=Q(c,\ell)
. Then, it holds

|\log_{Q}-\log(\max(|c|,\ell))|\leq C

with a constant independent of
Q
.

Proof.

Since
\log|x|\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for an arbitrary cube
Q
there is a constant
b_{Q}

such that

\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\log|x|-b_{Q}|dx\leq C\|\log|x|\,\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}.

It turns out that one may take

b_{Q}=\log|c|
, when
|c|\geq\ell
, and
b_{Q}=\log\ell
, when
|c|\leq\ell
.
This fact is easily derived by scaling argument
[
8
, p. 140–141]
.

With the choice of
b_{Q}
we have

|\log_{Q}-\log\max(|c|,\ell)|\leq C\|\log|x|\,\|_{\mathrm{BMO}},

that is required.
∎

Proceed to the proof of
(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)
.

Step 1.

By assumptions of
(iii)
,

\|T\log|x|\,\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\|\log|x|\,\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}\approx 1,

and hence, by Lemma
2.1
and
by triangle inequality, we have uniformly with respect to
Q

|\log_{2Q}|\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C.

This follows

(|\log_{2Q}|+1)\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}.

By Lemma
3.1
,

|\log(\max(|c|,\ell))|\leq|\log_{2Q}|+C

with a constant independent of
Q
, and therefore,

|\log(\max(|c|,\ell))|\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q},

If
|c|\leq\max(\ell,1)
, i.e. when the euclidean distance
between cubes
Q
and
Q_{0}
is small enough compared to the size of the cubes, then it holds

L(Q)\approx|\log(\max(|c|,\ell))|+1.

So, for cubes of such kind we obtained an estimate

(14)

L(Q)\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C(1+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}),

where a constant is independent of
Q
.

Step 2.
Observe, that if
|c|\geq\max(\ell,1)
, i.e. when euclidean distance
between cubes
Q
and
Q_{0}
is large enough compared to the size of the cubes, then

L(Q)\approx\log\frac{|c|}{\ell}+1

and therefore
L(Q)
is not majorized by
|\log(\max(|c|,\ell))|+1
, in general.
However, we prove the estimate (
14
) for cubes of this kind. Let
\log_{s}|x|=\log|x-s|
be a shift and
\log_{s,Q}
be the mean value of it over a cube
Q
.
For
the family of functions
\log_{s,-s}(x)=\log_{s}|x|-\log_{-s}|x|
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we prove a lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Norms
\|\log_{s,-s}(x)\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}
are bounded uniformly in
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Obviously, seminorms of
\log_{s,-s}(x)
are estimated uniformly in
s
, as follows

\displaystyle\|\log_{s,-s}(x)\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\log|x|\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C.

Also, by Lemma
3.1
, we have

|\log_{s,Q}-\log\max(|c-s|,\,\ell)|\leq C.

Therefore, for the mean value
\log_{s,-s,Q}
over
Q
of
\log_{s,-s}(x)
we get

(15)

|\log_{s,-s,Q}-(\log\max(|c-s|,\,\ell)-\log\max(|c+s|,\,\ell))|\leq C.

Particularly, for the cube
Q_{0}
with
c=0
and
\ell=1

|\log_{s,-s,Q_{0}}|\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
s
.
Combining, we have

\displaystyle\|\log_{s,-s}(x)\|^{*}_{\mathrm{BMO}}

\displaystyle=\|\log_{s,-s}(x)\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}+|\log_{s,-s,Q_{0}}|\leq C,

that is required.
∎

We are ready to finish the proof.
By assumptions of
(iii)
and Lemma
3.2
,

\|T(\log_{s,-s}(x))\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C.

Then, by Lemma
2.1
and by triangle inequality,
it holds

|\log_{s,-s,2Q}|\,\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C

and, so,

(|\log_{s,-s,2Q}|+1)\,\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}.

By (
15
), we have

|\log\max(|c-s|,\,2\ell)-\log\max(|c+s|,\,2\ell)|\,\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}.

Put
s=-c
, we obtain

|\log\max(|c|/\ell,\,1)|\,\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}.

Since
L(Q)\approx|\log(\max(|c|/\ell,\,1))|+1
, when
|c|\geq\max(\ell,1)
, one has

L(Q)\,\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q}\leq C(1+\|T_{Q}1\|_{Q})\\

that gives us (
14
)
for the rest of the cubes.

Now, taking the supremum with respect to
Q
in (
14
), we get

\|T1\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}\leq C(1+\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}).

Since
T
is bounded from
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to homogeneous
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\|T1\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}<\infty
. This completes the proof (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

4.
Calderón commutators

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We will prove
\mathcal{C}_{A}1\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathrm{R})
, and apply Theorem
1.1
.

For arbitrary interval
Q=(c-\ell/2,c+\ell/2)
let
2Q=(c-\ell,c+\ell)
be a dilated interval with the same centre.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}
be the operators we get by (
9
) for
\mathcal{C}_{A}
.
We start with the proof that
\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}

inherits the certain properties of
\mathcal{C}_{A}
, indicated in
[
8
, p. 312]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
x\in Q
one has the equality

\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}1(x)=\mathcal{H}_{Q}A^{\prime}(x)+C(Q),

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hilbert transform with the kernel
\frac{1}{x-y}
and
C(Q)
is a constant.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}1(x)=\int_{2Q}\frac{A(x)-A(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}\left(\frac{A(x)-A(y)}{(x-y)^{2}}-\frac{A(c)-A(y)}{(c-y)^{2}}\right)dy,

where the first term is a PV integral. Integrating by parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}1(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(x)-A(y)}{x-y}\bigg|^{c+\ell}_{y=c-\ell}+\left(\frac{A(x)-A(y)}{x-y}-\frac{A(c)-A(y)}{c-y}\right)\bigg|^{c-\ell}_{y=-\infty}+\bigg|^{\infty}_{y=c+\ell}

\displaystyle+\int_{2Q}\frac{A^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}-\frac{A^{\prime}(c)}{c-y}\right)dy

\displaystyle=N(x)+\mathcal{H}_{Q}A^{\prime}(x).

The nonintegral term
N(x)
is a constant on
Q
. Indeed,

N(x)=\frac{A(c)-A(y)}{c-y}\bigg|^{c+\ell}_{y=c-\ell}+\left(\frac{A(x)-A(y)}{x-y}-\frac{A(c)-A(y)}{c-y}\right)\bigg|^{\infty}_{y=-\infty}.

Since
A^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
implies
A(y)=o(y^{2})
at infinity, the second summand above is zero, while the first one depends on
c
and
\ell
. That is

N(x)=C(Q)
.
∎

Even the classical Hilbert transform is zero on constants, it takes some times in setting of the paper to check
\mathcal{H}_{Q}1(x)=0
for
x\in Q
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{Q}1(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{2Q}\frac{1}{x-y}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}\left(\frac{1}{x-y}-\frac{1}{c-y}\right)dy

\displaystyle=-\log|x-y|\bigg|^{c+\ell}_{c-\ell}-(\log|x-y|-\log|c-y|)\bigg|^{c-\ell}_{-\infty}+\bigg|^{\infty}_{c+\ell}

\displaystyle=\log|x-c|\bigg|^{c-\ell}_{c+\ell}=0.

Hence, for
x\in Q
we may write

\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}1(x)=\mathcal{H}_{Q}(A^{\prime}-A^{\prime}_{2Q})(x)+C(Q),

where
A^{\prime}_{2Q}
denotes the mean value with respect to
2Q
. So,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}1\|_{Q}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{H}_{Q}(A^{\prime}-A^{\prime}_{2Q})\|_{Q}.

In order to estimate
\|\mathcal{H}_{Q}(A^{\prime}-A^{\prime}_{2Q})\|_{Q}
, consider the terms
I^{\prime}_{1}
and
I^{\prime}_{2}
we get from the terms
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
in Lemma
2.1
, replacing
f
by
A^{\prime}
and
T
by
\mathcal{H}
. Repeating argument of Lemma
2.1
, it holds for
I^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\left|\int_{2Q}\frac{A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2Q}}{x-y}dy\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{|2Q|}\int_{2Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2Q}|dy

\displaystyle=C\frac{L(Q)}{L(Q)}\frac{1}{|2Q|}\int_{2Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2Q}|dy

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{L(Q)}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}.

Taking care of the term
I^{\prime}_{2}
is not so easy. Split it in dyadic sum,

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 2Q}(A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2Q})\left(\frac{1}{x-y}-\frac{1}{c-y}dy\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell^{\delta}}{(2^{k}\ell)^{1+\delta}}\left(\int_{2^{k+1}Q\backslash 2^{k}Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|\,dy+|2^{k+1}Q||A^{\prime}_{2Q}-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|\right),

where by
A^{\prime}_{2^{k}Q}
we define the mean values of
A^{\prime}
over
2^{k}Q
, By telescopic summation we have

\displaystyle|A^{\prime}_{2Q}-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{k}|A^{\prime}_{2^{s}Q}-A^{\prime}_{2^{s+1}Q}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1}{|2^{k+1}Q|}\int_{2^{s+1}Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2^{s+1}Q}|dy,

and substituting in
I^{\prime}_{2}
, it holds

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell^{\delta}}{(2^{k}\ell)^{1+\delta}}\bigg(\int_{2^{k+1}Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|dy

\displaystyle+|2^{k+1}Q|\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1}{|2^{s+1}Q|}\int_{2^{s+1}Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|dy\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k\delta}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1}{|2^{s+1}Q|}\int_{2^{s+1}Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|dy

\displaystyle=C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k\delta}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{L(2^{s+1}Q)}{L(2^{s+1}Q)|2^{s+1}Q|}\int_{2^{s+1}Q}|A^{\prime}(y)-A^{\prime}_{2^{k+1}Q}|dy

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k\delta}}\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{\|A^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{LMO}}}{L(2^{s+1}Q)}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}L(2^{s+1}Q)}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{LMO}},

where in the last line we change the summation order.

We claim that

(16)

\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}L(2^{s+1}Q)}\leq C\frac{1}{L(Q)}.

With estimate (
16
) in hand, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{A,Q}1\|_{Q}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{H}_{Q}A^{\prime}\|_{Q}

\displaystyle\leq C(I^{\prime}_{1}+I^{\prime}_{2})

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{L(Q)}\|A^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{LMO}},

which follows
\mathcal{C}_{A}1\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathrm{R})
, and Theorem
1.1
completes the proof.

So, it remains to prove (
16
). Consider
two cases according to whether Euclidean distance between a cube
Q
and the fixed cube
Q_{0}
is less or greater 1.

Case 1.
If the distance
d
between a cube
Q
and the fixed cube
Q_{0}
is less than 1, we compare
\ell_{s}=\ell(2^{s}Q)=2^{s}\ell

with 1.
If
\ell_{s}\leq 1
, that is
s\leq\log 1/\ell
, then
L(2^{s}Q)\approx\log 1/\ell_{s}+1
, while if

\ell_{s}>1
, i.e.
s>\log 1/\ell
, then
L(2^{s}Q)\approx\log\ell_{s}+1
. Also, split the sum (
16
) on level
\ell_{s}=1
, then if the first sum on the
right hand side is non empty, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}L(2^{s+1}Q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{s}\leq 1}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log 1/\ell_{s}+1)}+\sum_{\ell_{s}>1}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log\ell_{s}+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq\log 1/\ell}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log 1/2^{s}\ell+1)}+\sum_{s>\log 1/\ell}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log 2^{s}\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{1}^{1/\ell}\frac{dt}{t^{1+\delta}(\log 1/t\ell+1)}+\int_{1/\ell}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{1+\delta}(\log t\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\log 1/\ell+1}+\ell^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\log 1/\ell+1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{L(Q)}.

Obviously, the same estimate is valid if the first sum is empty (for instance if
\ell>1
). Then the sum (
16
) is estimated by
\ell^{\delta}=o(1/L(Q))
.

Case 2.
If the distance
d
between a cube
Q
and the fixed cube
Q_{0}
is not less than 1, we compare
\ell_{s}
with
d
.
We have
L(2^{s}Q)\approx\log(d/\ell_{s}+1)
, if
\ell_{s}\leq d
, and
L(2^{s}Q)\approx\log(\ell_{s}+1)
, if

\ell_{s}>d
. Also, split the sum
16
on level
\ell_{s}=d
. Like the previous observation, whether the first sum on the right side is empty or not, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}L(2^{s+1}Q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{s}\leq d}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log d/\ell_{s}+1)}+\sum_{\ell_{s}>d}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log\ell_{s}+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq\log d/\ell}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log d/2^{s}\ell+1)}+\sum_{s>\log d/\ell}\frac{1}{2^{s\delta}(\log 2^{s}\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{1}^{d/\ell}\frac{dt}{t^{1+\delta}(\log d/t\ell+1)}+\int_{d/\ell}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{1+\delta}(\log t\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\log d/\ell+1}+(\ell/d)^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{\log d/\ell+1}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{L(Q)},

that is required for the second case and for Theorem
1.2
.

4.2.
Sharpness of condition
A^{\prime}\in\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R})

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the Calderón commutator associated to
A(x)=|x|
. Let
Q=(c/2,3c/2)
be an interval with the centre
c>0
and length
|Q|=c
,
We will prove that
\|\mathcal{C}_{Q}1\|_{Q}
is separated from zero. From (
7
) this follows

\mathcal{C}1\notin\mathrm{LMO}(\mathbb{R})
, and by Theorem
1.1
,
\mathcal{C}
is unbounded in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}
).

Defining
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
by (
9
), we write for
y\in Q

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{Q}1(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{2Q}\frac{|x|-|y|}{(x-y)^{2}}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus 2Q}\left(\frac{|x|-|y|}{(x-y)^{2}}-\frac{|c|-|y|}{(c-y)^{2}}\right)dy

\displaystyle=PV\int_{0<y<2c}\frac{1}{x-y}dy

\displaystyle+\int_{y>2c}\left(\frac{1}{x-y}-\frac{1}{c-y}\right)dy

\displaystyle+\int_{y<0}\left(\frac{x+y}{(x-y)^{2}}-\frac{c+y}{(c-y)^{2}}\right)dy

\displaystyle=2(\log x-\log c).

Then,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{Q}1\|_{Q}

\displaystyle=\inf_{b_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{2}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\log x-\log c-b_{Q}|dy

\displaystyle=2\inf_{b_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{1/2}^{3/2}|\log t-b_{Q}|dt

\displaystyle\geq C>0,

that is required.
