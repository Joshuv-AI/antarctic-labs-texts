Mean value formulas for classical solutions to some degenerate elliptic equations in Carnot groups
Diego Pallara Sergio Polidoro

Abstract
We prove surface and volume mean value formulas for classical solutions to uniformly elliptic equations in divergence form with Hölder continuous coefficients. The kernels appearing in the integrals are supported on the level and superlevel sets of the fundamental solution relative the adjoint differential operator. We then extend the aforementioned formulas to some subelliptic operators on Carnot groups. In this case we rely on the theory of finite perimeter sets on stratified Lie groups.



1
Introduction

The mean value property that characterizes harmonic functions is a fundamental tool in potential theory and provides us with simple proofs of maximum principles, Harnack inequalities, regularity properties of harmonic functions and compactness results.

It is known that the proof of mean value formulas relies on the divergence theorem and on the fact that the Euclidean sphere is a level set of the fundamental solution of the Laplace equation. Based on this idea, this formula has been first extended in 1951 by Pini
[
22
]
to the heat equation in one space variable, then by Watson
[
27
]
in 1973 to the heat equation in several space variables. Mean value formulas for degenerate operators in the form of sum of squares of Hörmander vector fields have been proved in 1993 by Citti, Garofalo and Lanconelli
[
7
]
. Still in the framework of hypoelliptic operators, we recall the article by Garofalo and Lanconelli
[
14
]
, who prove in 1990 mean value formulas for Kolmogorov operators.

The general class of uniformly parabolic operators with smooth coefficents has been considered by Fabes and Garofalo
[
8
]
in 1987, and by Garofalo and Lanconelli in
[
13
]
in 1989. More recently, uniformly parabolic operators with weaker regularity assumptions on the coefficients of the operator have been considered by the authors and Malagoli
[
19
]
. We point out that the main difficulty encountered in
[
19
]
is due to the fact that the fundamental solution of the parabolic equation is not explicitly known and that, as a consequence, it is impossible to check the regularity of its level sets, which are the boundaries of the domain where the divergence theorem needs to be applied. This problem was bypassed in
[
8
,
13
]
by assuming the smoothness of the coefficients, which guarentees the applicability of Sard’s theorem, and has been circumvented in
[
19
]
in two ways: one relies on a refinement of the classical divergence theorem in the classical vein, the other on the theory of
sets with finite perimeter
.

The aim of the present paper is to come back to elliptic operators, with natural regularity assumptions, and to address the problem for their degenerate counterpart on Carnot groups. By
natural regularity
we mean that we consider possibily degenerate elliptic operators with
C^{1+\alpha}
diffusion and drift coefficients, so that both
\mathscr{L}
and its adjoint
\mathscr{L}^{*}
can be written in non-divergence form with Hölder continuous coefficients.

As we will see in the following, the mean value formula for
uniformly elliptic operators
with natural conditions on the coefficients is rather simple. Indeed, several results on the existence of a fundamental solution
\Gamma
of uniformly elliptic equations with Hölder continuous coefficients are avaliable in literature. All of them rely on the Levi’s parametrix method. We mainly refer on the Kalf article
[
15
]
, where some bounds of the gradient of
\Gamma
ensure that, for sufficiently large
C
, the level set
\big\{\Gamma=C\big\}
is a
C^{1}
manifold. We give the proof of the mean value formulas for uniformly elliptic operators for the sake of completness, and also because it describes in the Euclidean setting the procedure that we adopt in the more difficult setting of the Carnot groups.

The generalization of the mean value property to
subelliptic operators
in a Carnot group
{\mathbb{G}}
involves a substantial difficulty. Indeed, the fundamental solution
\Gamma
of a subelliptic equation
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u=0
is not smooth, as only first and second order
horizontal
derivatives are defined as continuous functions. Therefore the level sets of
\Gamma
may have wide subsets of singular points and the same assertion is true for the fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}
of the adjoint equation
\mathscr{L}^{*}_{\mathbb{G}}v=0
. In this setting we cannot rely on any refinement of the classical divergence theorem as we did in
[
19
]
, and we rely on the theory of
sets with finite perimeter
, which has been developed in the framework of stratified groups. We refer to the lecture notes
[
24
]
by Serra Cassano and the wide bibliography therein, which can be applied also to families of
degenerate parabolic subelliptic operators
, see
[
21
]
.

We present our results on uniformly elliptic operators in Section
2
, where we also recall the main results we
need on the existence and the main properties of a fundamental solution. We devote Section
3
to recalling the setting of Carnot groups, the relevant notions on sets of finite perimeter, an existence result on the fundamental solution, and to proving the mean value formulas for a class of subelliptic operators in Carnot groups.

2
Uniformly elliptic operators

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We consider classical solutions
u
to the equation
\mathscr{L}u=f
in
\Omega
, where
\mathscr{L}
is an elliptic operator in divergence form defined for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as follows

\mathscr{L}u(x):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(a_{ij}(x)\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}(x)\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}(x)+c(x)u(x).

(2.1)

In the following we use the notation

A(x):=\left(a_{ij}(x)\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,N},b(x):=\left(b_{1}(x),\dots,b_{N}(x)\right)
and we write
\mathscr{L}u
in the short form

\mathscr{L}u(x):={\text{\rm div}}\left(A(x)\nabla u(x)\right)+\langle b(x),\nabla u(x)\rangle+c(x)u(x).

(2.2)

The adjoint operator is of course

\mathscr{L}^{*}u(x):={\text{\rm div}}\left(A(x)\nabla u(x)\right)-\langle b(x),\nabla u(x)\rangle+(c(x)-{\rm div}\,b(x))u(x).

Here
{\text{\rm div}},\nabla
and
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
denote the divergence, the gradient and the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, respectively. We assume that the matrix
A(x)
is symmetric and that
\mathscr{L}
is uniformly elliptic,
i.e.
, there exist two constants
\lambda,\Lambda
, with
0<\lambda<\Lambda
, such that

\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq\langle A(x)\xi,\xi\rangle\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2},

(2.3)

for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and for
i,j=1,\dots,N
. We finally assume that the coefficients
a_{ij},b_{i},c
and their derivatives
\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial b_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}
are bounded Hölder continuous functions of exponent
\alpha\in]0,1]
, for
i,j=1,\dots,N
. Under the above assumptions, the classical parametrix method provides us with the existence of a fundamental solution
\Gamma
. Let us quote from the article of Kalf
[
15
]
the results we need for our purposes. We denote by
D_{\Omega}
the diagonal
D_{\Omega}=\{(x,x)\in\Omega\times\Omega\}
.

According to the Definition at page 259 of
[
15
]
, we say that a function
\Gamma:(\Omega\times\Omega)\backslash D_{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is a fundamental solution to the equation
\mathscr{L}u=0
if the following conditions hold for every
y\in\Omega
:

i)

\Gamma(\cdot,y)\in C^{2}(\Omega\backslash\{y\})
and
\mathscr{L}\,\Gamma(\cdot,y)=0
;

ii)

\int_{\Omega}|\Gamma(x,y)|\,dx<+\infty;

iii)

For every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
we have

\varphi(y)=-\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(x,y)\mathscr{L}^{*}\varphi(x)\,dx.

In the following we use property
iii)
with
\varphi\in C_{c}^{2}(\Omega)
, by a density argument.

Under our hypotheses, Theorem 5 of
[
15
]
ensures that for every connected open set
\Omega_{1}
such that
\overline{\Omega_{1}}\subset\Omega
there exists a fundamental solution
\Gamma:(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{1})\backslash D_{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
of
\mathscr{L}u=0
. Our assumptions on the coefficients of
\mathscr{L}
ensures that there exists as well a fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}:(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{1})\backslash D_{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
of
\mathscr{L}^{*}u=0
.

The parametrix method used in
[
15
]
also provides us with the following estimates: there exist positive constants
c^{-},c^{+},c_{0},c_{1}
, only depending on the dimension
N
, on the operator
\mathscr{L}
and on the set
\Omega_{1}
, such that the following bounds hold for every
x,y\in\Omega_{1}
:

c^{-}\log\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{|x-y|}\right)-c_{0}|x-y|^{\alpha/2}\leq\Gamma(x,y)\leq c^{+}\log\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{\Lambda}}{|x-y|}\right)+c_{0}|x-y|^{\alpha/2}

(2.4)

in the case
N=2
, while for
N>2
we have

\frac{c^{-}}{|x-y|^{N-2}}-\frac{c_{0}}{|x-y|^{N-2-\alpha}}\leq\Gamma(x,y)\leq\frac{c^{+}}{|x-y|^{N-2}}+\frac{c_{0}}{|x-y|^{N-2-\alpha}}.

(2.5)

Moreover,

\frac{c^{-}}{|x-y|^{N-1}}-\frac{c_{0}}{|x-y|^{N-1-\alpha}}\leq|\nabla_{x}\Gamma(x,y)|\leq\frac{c^{+}}{|x-y|^{N-1}}+\frac{c_{0}}{|x-y|^{N-1-\alpha}}

(2.6)

for every
x,y\in\Omega_{1}
. The above inequalities also hold for
\Gamma^{*}
. We refer to Theorem 3 and to the formulas (2.5), (2.6) and (2.8) of
[
15
]
.

We introduce some notation and we state our main results. For every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and for every
r>0
, we set

\begin{split}\psi_{r}(x_{0})&:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})=\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}\right\},\\
\Omega_{r}(x_{0})&:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})>\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}\right\}.\end{split}

(2.7)

Note that, as observed in Remark 7 of
[
15
]
, the bounds (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) imply
\Gamma(x,y)>0
and
\Gamma^{*}(x,y)>0
whenever
x
is sufficiently close to
y
. Moreover,
\Omega_{r}(x_{0})
is bounded and
\psi_{r}(x_{0})
is a
C^{1}
manifold, for sufficiently small positive
r
.

We finally introduce the following kernels

\begin{split}K(x_{0},x)&:=\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{|\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|},\\
M(x_{0},x)&:=\frac{N}{(N-2)}\cdot\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})^{\frac{2(N-1)}{N-2}}}.\end{split}

(2.8)

Note that
\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x_{0},x)\neq 0
in
\Omega_{r}(x_{0})
for sufficiently small
r
and
\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\neq 0
in
\psi_{r}(x_{0})
, by its very definition. In the following,
\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}
denotes the
(N-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, see e.g.
[
20
]
. The first achievements of this note are the following mean value formulas.

Theorem 2.1

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
N>2
,
f\in C(\Omega)
and let
u
be a classical solution to
\mathscr{L}u=f
in
\Omega
. Then, for every
x_{0}\in\Omega
there is
r_{0}>0
such that for every
0<r<r_{0}
we have

\begin{split}u(x_{0})=\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(x)+&\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}f(x)\left(\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}-\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\right)\ dx\\
+&\frac{1}{r^{N-2}}\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)u(x)\ dx,\end{split}

(2.9)

\displaystyle u(x_{0})=\frac{1}{r^{N}}\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\!\!\!\!\!M(x_{0},x)

\displaystyle u(x)\,dx+\frac{{N}}{r^{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\left({\varrho}^{N-1}\!\!\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\!\!\!\!\!f(x)\left(\tfrac{1}{{\varrho}^{N-2}}-\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\right)dx\right)d{\varrho}

\displaystyle+\frac{{N}}{r^{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\left({\varrho}\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)u(x)\,dx\right)d{\varrho}.

(2.10)

Remark 2.2

The usual proof of the mean value formula relies on the application of the second Green identity on the set
\Omega_{r}(x_{0})\backslash\Omega_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
, with
0<\varepsilon<r\leq r_{0}
, which produces the first integral in the right hand side of (
2.9
) and
\int_{\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(x)
. The conclusion then follows from the fact that
\int_{\psi_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(x)\to u(x_{0})
as
\varepsilon\to 0.
We adopt here an alternative approach which relies on the properties of the fundamental solution. This approach simplifies the proof of the mean value formula in the setting of degenerate subelliptic operators on Carnot groups.

Proof
. Let
r_{1}>0
be such that
\overline{\Omega_{r_{1}}(x_{0})}\subset\Omega
and consider an open set
\Omega_{1}
such that
\overline{\Omega_{r_{1}}(x_{0})}\subset\Omega_{1}
and that
\overline{\Omega_{1}}\subset\Omega
. Then there exists the fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}
of the equation
\mathscr{L}^{*}v=0
in
\Omega_{1}
. As noticed above, we can, and we do, choose
r_{0}\in]0,r_{1}]
such that
|\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})|\neq 0
in
\Omega_{r_{0}}(x_{0})
, hence
\psi_{r}(x_{0})
is a
C^{1}
-manifold for every positive
r<r_{0}
. We choose
r\leq r_{0}
and we introduce a further positive parameter
\varepsilon
small enough to have
B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}):=\{|x-x_{0}|<\varepsilon\}\subset\Omega_{r}(x_{0})
. We let
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a function such that
{\rm supp}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon})\subset B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
and
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x)=1
for
|x-x_{0}|\leq\varepsilon/2
. Since
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u\in C_{c}^{2}(\Omega_{1})
, the very definition of
\Gamma^{*}
yields

{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(y)u(y)=-\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,y)\mathscr{L}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,dx

for every
y\in\Omega_{1}
. In particular,

u(x_{0})=-\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\mathscr{L}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,dx.

(2.11)

We next consider the functions
w(x):=(1-{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x))u(x)
and
v(x):=\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})-\frac{1}{r^{N-2}}
and we note that

\begin{split}w(x)\mathscr{L}^{*}v(x)-v(x)\mathscr{L}w(x)=&{\text{\rm div}}\big(w(x)A(x)\nabla v(x)-v(x)A(x)\nabla w(x)\big)\\
&-{\text{\rm div}}\big(w(x)v(x)b(x)\big)\end{split}

(2.12)

for every
x\in\Omega\backslash\big\{x_{0}\big\}
. Recalling that
\mathscr{L}^{*}v=\frac{1}{r^{N-2}}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b-c\right)
in

\Omega\backslash\big\{x_{0}\big\}
, equation (
2.12
) can be written as follows

\frac{1}{r^{N-2}}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)w(x)-v(x)\mathscr{L}w(x)={\text{\rm div}}\,\Phi(x),\quad\Phi(x):=\big(wA\nabla v-vA\nabla w-wvb\big)(x).

By our choice of
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}
we have that
\Phi
vanishes in

B_{\varepsilon/2}(x_{0})
, then it can be extended to a
C^{1}(\Omega)
function by setting
\Phi(x_{0})=0
. By the divergence theorem we find

\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\left(\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)w(x)-v(x)\mathscr{L}w(x)\right)dx=-\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})}\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle d\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1},

(2.13)

where
\nu(x)=\tfrac{\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})}{\lvert\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rvert}
.
In order to conclude our proof, we rewrite (
2.11
) in its equivalent form

u(x_{0})=-\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\left(\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}+v(x)\right)\mathscr{L}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)dx.

(2.14)

We then recall that
u=w+{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u
, so that
f=\mathscr{L}u=\mathscr{L}w+\mathscr{L}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)
. Than, by subtracting (
2.14
) and (
2.13
), we find

\begin{split}u(x_{0})=&-\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}v(x)f(x)\,dx+\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\left(\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x))\right)(1-{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x))u(x)\,dx\\
&+\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})}\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle d\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}-\tfrac{1}{r^{N-2}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\mathscr{L}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,dx.\end{split}

(2.15)

Now we let
\varepsilon\to 0
. Then,
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x)\to 0
a.e. and moreover

\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\mathscr{L}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,dx=\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}{\rm div}\,(A\nabla({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u))\,dx+\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\langle b,\nabla({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)\rangle\,dx+\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}c{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u\,dx.

The first integral vanishes by the divergence theorem, as
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}=0
on the boundary, the last one tends to
0
because the function
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u
is bounded, whereas for the second one we notice that

\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}\langle b,\nabla({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)\rangle\,dx=\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u\langle b,\nu\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}-\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u{\rm div}\,b\,dx

where the surface integral vanishes and the second one tends to
0
. Finally, we have

\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle(x)=K(x_{0},x)u(x),

since
\nu(x)=\tfrac{\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})}{\lvert\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rvert}
. This concludes the proof of the first statement of Theorem
2.1
.

The proof of the second assertion of Theorem
2.1
is a direct consequence of the first one and of the coarea formula. Indeed, fix a positive
r
as above, multiply (
2.9
) (with
{\varrho}
in place of
r
) by
\frac{N}{r^{N}}{\varrho}^{N-1}
and integrate over
]0,r[
. We find

\begin{split}\frac{N}{r^{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\varrho^{N-1}u(x_{0})d\varrho=&\frac{N}{r^{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\varrho^{N-1}\bigg(\int_{\psi_{\varrho}(x_{0})}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}(x)\bigg)d\varrho\,\\
&+\frac{N}{r^{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\varrho^{N-1}\bigg(\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}f(x)\left(\tfrac{1}{{\varrho}^{N-2}}-\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\right)dx\bigg)d\varrho\\
&+\frac{N}{r^{N}}\int_{0}^{r}\varrho\bigg(\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)u(x)\,dx\bigg)d\varrho.\end{split}

The left hand side of the above equality equals
u(x_{0})
, while the last two terms agree with the last two terms appearing in the statement of Theorem
2.1
. In order to conclude the proof we only need to show that

\frac{1}{N-2}\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}M(x_{0},x)u(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{r}\varrho^{N-1}\bigg(\int_{\left\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=\tfrac{1}{\varrho^{N-2}}\right\}}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}(x)\bigg)d\varrho.

(2.16)

With this aim, we substitute
y=\frac{1}{\varrho^{N-2}}
in the left hand side of (
2.16
) and we recall the definition of the kernel
K
. We find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}\varrho^{N-1}

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\left\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=\tfrac{1}{\varrho^{N-2}}\right\}}\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{|\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|}u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}_{e}(x)\bigg)d\varrho

(2.17)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\frac{1}{N-2}\int_{\frac{1}{r^{N-2}}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{\frac{2(N-1)}{N-2}}}\bigg(\int_{\left\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=y\right\}}\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{|\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|}u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}(x)\bigg)dy

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=\frac{1}{N-2}\int_{\frac{1}{r^{N-2}}}^{\infty}\bigg(\int_{\left\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=y\right\}}\!\!\!\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})^{\frac{2(N-1)}{N-2}}|\nabla\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|}u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}_{e}(x)\bigg)dy.

We conclude the proof of (
2.16
) by applying the coarea formula, see e.g.
[
20
]
.

\square

Remark 2.3

If
N=2
we start of course from (
2.4
). Let us show how the results and the proofs have to be adapted. First, the integration domains are

\begin{split}\psi_{r}(x_{0})&:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})=\log\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\right)\right\},\\
\Omega_{r}(x_{0})&:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})>\log\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\right)\right\},\end{split}

(2.18)

the kernel
K(x_{0},x)
is the same as in the case
N\geq 3
, whereas

M(x_{0},x):=2\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{\exp\{2\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\}}.

(2.19)

Arguing as in the case
N\geq 3
we easily get

\begin{split}u(x_{0})=\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)+&\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}f(x)\left(\log\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\right)-\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\right)\ dx\\
+&\log\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)u(x)\ dx\,d{\varrho}.\end{split}

(2.20)

In order to deduce the mean value formula corresponding to
(
2.10
) we write

\displaystyle u(x_{0})=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}{\varrho}u(x_{0})\,d{\varrho}=\frac{2}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}{\varrho}\int_{\psi_{\varrho}(x_{0})}K(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\,d{\varrho}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}{\varrho}\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}f(x)\left(\log\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\right)-\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\right)\ dx\,d{\varrho}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}{\varrho}\log\left(\frac{1}{{\varrho}}\right)\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}\left({\text{\rm div}}\,b(x)-c(x)\right)u(x)\ dx\,d{\varrho}.

Concerning the first integral, substituting
y=\log(1/{\varrho})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}{\varrho}\int_{\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=\log(1/{\varrho}\}}\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{|\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|}u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\,d{\varrho}

\displaystyle\int_{\log(1/r)}^{\infty}e^{-2y}\int_{\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=y\}}\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{|\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|}u(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\,dy

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{x}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{\exp\{2\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\}}u(x)\,dx,

whence

\displaystyle u(x_{0})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}M(x_{0},x)u(x)\,dx

Remark 2.4

In Theorem

3
Subelliptic operators in Carnot groups

In this section we state and prove the mean value formula for a class of subellitic operators in Carnot groups. We need much more preliminar information with respect to the uniformly elliptic case treated in Section
2
, and accordingly we split this section in various subsections. In the first one we describe the structure of the Carnot groups. In the second one we present the class of operators we are interested in, we recall the properties of fundamental solutions and state the main result, Theorem
3.3
. In the third one we discuss the properties of sets with finite perimeter and, finally, in the last one we prove Theorem
3.3
.

We consider
m
smooth vector fields (
1\leq m\leq N
)

X_{j}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varphi_{k}^{j}(x)\partial_{x_{k}},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

(3.1)

with
\varphi_{k}^{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. We introduce the Lie algebra generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}

{\mathfrak{g}}={\rm Lie}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})

(3.2)

and we assume the following:

[H.1]

The vector fields
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
satisfy the Hörmander’s rank condition

{\rm rank}\,{\mathfrak{g}}(x)=N\qquad\text{for every}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(3.3)

[H.2]

there exists a homogeneous Lie group

\mathbb{G}=\left(\mathbb{R}^{N},\circ,{\delta}_{\lambda}\right)
such that

i)

X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
are left translation invariant on
\mathbb{G}
;

ii)

X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
are
{\delta}_{\lambda}
-homogeneous of degree one.

Moreover,
X_{j}(0)
agrees with the
j
-th element of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, for
j=1,\dots,m
.

3.1
Stratified groups

A Lie group
\mathbb{G}=\left({\mathbb{R}}^{N},\circ\right)
is said
homogeneous
if a family of dilations
\left({\delta}_{\lambda}\right)_{\lambda>0}
exists on
\mathbb{G}
and it is an automorphism of the group:

{\delta}_{\lambda}(x\circ y)=\left({\delta}_{\lambda}x\right)\circ\left({\delta}_{\lambda}y\right),\quad\text{for all}\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ \text{and}\ \lambda>0.

The assumptions [H.1] and [H.2] induce a direct sum decomposition of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}

\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{\nu},

(3.4)

where
V_{1}=\text{span}\big\{X_{1},\dots X_{m}\big\},V_{k+1}=\text{span}\big\{[X,Y],\mid X\in V_{1},Y\in V_{k}\big\},
for
k=1,\dots,\nu-1
and
\big\{[X,Y],\mid X\in V_{1},Y\in V_{\nu}\big\}=\big\{0\big\}.

In the sequel we denote by
n_{j}
the dimension of
V_{j}
, for
j=1,\dots,\nu
. Note that [H.2] yields
m=n_{1}
. The dilation
\delta_{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
will be represented by a diagonal matrix, which necessarily has the following form

\delta_{\lambda}={\text{\rm diag}}(\lambda{\mathbb{I}}_{n_{1}},\lambda^{2}{\mathbb{I}}_{n_{2}},\ldots,\lambda^{\nu}{\mathbb{I}}_{n_{\nu}}),

(3.5)

the integer
Q=n_{1}+\dots+n_{\nu}
will be called
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{G}
and we have

\det\delta_{\lambda}=\lambda^{Q}.

(3.6)

We refer to the monograph
[
4
]
for a more detailed treatment of homogeneous Lie groups and for an exhaustive bibliography on this subject. In particular, from Proposition 1.38 in
[
4
]
it follows that

X_{j}^{*}=-X_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,m.

(3.7)

Let us introduce the distance that we use in this paper. From
[
12
, Theorem 5.1]
we know that there are constants
\varepsilon_{j}\in]0,1],\ j=1,\ldots,\nu
, with
\varepsilon_{1}=1
, such that the function

x\mapsto\|x\|_{\infty}=\max_{j=1,\ldots,\nu}\{\varepsilon_{j}|x_{j}|^{1/j}\},

(3.8)

where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
and
|\cdot|
denotes the usual Euclidean norm, defines a norm and as a consequence the distance

d_{\infty}(x,y)=\|y^{-1}\circ x\|_{\infty}.

(3.9)

We notice that
d_{\infty}
is equivalento to the
Carnot-Carathéodory distance and that for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
there exist two positive constants
c_{K}^{-}
and
c_{K}^{+}
, such that

c_{K}^{-}|x-y|\leq d_{\infty}(x,y)\leq c_{K}^{+}|x-y|^{\frac{1}{\nu}},\qquad\text{for all}\ x,y\in K.

(3.10)

The invariance properties

d_{\infty}(y\circ x,y\circ z)=d_{\infty}(x,z),\quad d_{\infty}(\delta_{\lambda}x,\delta_{\lambda}y)=\lambda d_{\infty}(x,y),

(3.11)

hold for every
x,y,z
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and for every positive
\lambda
, see again
[
12
]
. For every
\nu\in V_{1}
, denote by
\nu^{\bot}
the codimension 1 subspace of
V_{1}
orthogonal to
\nu
and introduce the hyperplane
N=\nu^{\bot}\oplus V_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\nu}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the constant

\theta=\max_{z\in B(0,1)}\{\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}(B(z,1)\cap N)\}

(3.12)

which is independent of
\nu
because
d_{\infty}
is
vertically symmetric
according to Definition 6.1 in
[
17
]
, see Remark 6.2 and Theorem 6.3 in
[
17
]
. The constant
\theta
is introduced in
[
18
]
, is called spherical factor and is denoted
\omega_{{\mathbb{G}},Q-1}
there. Let us set
{\rm diam}_{\mathbb{G}}(E)=\sup_{x,y\in E}d_{\infty}(x,y)
and define the
h
-dimensional spherical Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{S}^{h}_{\mathbb{G}}
of a Borel set
E
,
0\leq h\leq Q
. First set for
r>0

{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathbb{G}},r}^{h}(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\theta}{2^{h}}{\rm diam}_{\mathbb{G}}\,(B_{i})^{h}:B_{i}\ d_{\infty}{\rm-balls,}\ E\subset\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}B_{i},\ {\rm diam}_{\mathbb{G}}\,(B_{i})\leq r\right\}

and then

{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{G}}^{h}(E)=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathbb{G}},r}^{h}(E)=\sup_{r\geq 0}{\mathcal{S}}_{{\mathbb{G}},r}^{h}(E).

(3.13)

The role of the constant
\theta
in the definition of
\mathcal{S}^{h}_{\mathbb{G}}
will be discussed in the Remark
3.11
below.

Finally, we recall the notations of Lie derivatives and Hölder spaces. For any
x_{0}\in{\Omega}
and
j=1,\dots,m
, let
\gamma
be the solution to the Cauchy problem

\gamma^{\prime}(s)=X_{j}(\gamma(s)),\qquad\gamma(0)=x_{0}.

Then the Lie derivative
X_{j}u(x_{0})
of

at
x_{0}
is

X_{j}u(x_{0}):=\frac{d}{ds}u(\gamma(s))_{\mid s=0}.

We say that
u
belongs to
C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}({\Omega})
if
u,X_{j}u
for
j=1,\dots,m
are continuous functions on
\Omega
and that
u\in C^{2}_{\mathbb{G}}({\Omega})
if
u,X_{j}u,X_{i}X_{j}u
are continuous in
\Omega
for
i,j=1,\ldots,m
. For
\alpha\in]0,1]
we say that
u
is
\alpha
–Hölder continuous, and we write
u\in C_{\mathbb{G}}^{\alpha}(\Omega)
, if there exists a positive constant
M
such that

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq Md_{\infty}(x,y)^{\alpha},\quad\text{for every}\ x,y\in\Omega.

(3.14)

Moreover, we say that
u
belongs to
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{1+\alpha}({\Omega})
(resp.
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{1+\alpha}({\Omega})
) if
u
, the derivatives
X_{1}u,\dots,X_{m}u
(resp. and
X_{i}X_{j}u
,
i,j=1,\dots,m
) belong to the space
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{\alpha}({\Omega})
. The function
u
belongs to
C^{\alpha}_{{\mathbb{G}},{\text{\rm loc}}}(\Omega)
(
C^{k+\alpha}_{{\mathbb{G}},{\text{\rm loc}}}({\Omega})
, respectively) if it belongs to
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{\alpha}(K)
(resp.
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{k+\alpha}(K)
) for every compact set

.

3.2
Mean value formulas in Carnot groups

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
be a family of vector fields satisfying the assumptions [H.1] and [H.2]. We consider the class of operators given as follows

\begin{split}\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}u+2\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}X_{j}a_{ij}X_{i}u+\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}X_{i}X_{j}a_{ij}u\\
&={\rm div}_{\mathbb{G}}(A\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u)+\langle b,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\rangle+cu,\end{split}

(3.15)

where we have set

b_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}a_{ij},\qquad c=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}X_{i}X_{j}a_{ij}.

Concerning the horizontal gradient
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
and the divergence
{\rm div}_{\mathbb{G}}
appearing in the above formulas, we agree to identify a
horizontal section

F=\sum_{j=1}^{m}F_{j}X_{j}
with its canonical coodinates
F=\left(F_{1},\dots,F_{m}\right)
. With this agreement, we denote the gradient of
f\in C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and the divergence of
F\in C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{m})
by

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f:=\sum_{j}(X_{j}f)X_{j}\quad{\rm and}\quad{\rm div}_{\mathbb{G}}F:=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}^{*}F_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}F_{j}.

(3.16)

We assume that
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,m}
is a symmetric matrix satisfying the condition (
2.3
) for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and that for every
i,j=1,\dots,m
, the coefficients
a_{ij}
belong to the space
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{2+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\alpha\in]0,1]
. The reason for this particular choice of the coefficients
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
and
c
is that, because of (
3.7
), the
adjoint operator
of
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}
has the following simple form

\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}.

(3.17)

We point out that we rely on a result by Bonfiglioli, Lanconelli and Uguzzoni
[
3
]
, who prove the existence of a fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}
for operators
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}
in the form (
3.17
). Less restrictive assumptions on
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
and
c
would be allowed as soon as more general existence results for
\Gamma^{*}
will be available.

Let
\Omega
denote any open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
u
be a real valued function defined on
\Omega
. We say that
u
is a
classical solution
to the equation
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u=f
in
\Omega
if
u
belongs to
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}({\Omega})
and the equation
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u=f
is satisfied at every point of
{\Omega}
. The analogous meaning is given to
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}v=g
.

Let us collect the relevant results on the fundamental solutions. If
\Omega
denotes any open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we let

D_{\Omega}=\{(x,x)\in\Omega\times\Omega\}
be its diagonal. As we already pointed out in the Introduction, we assume the existence of a local fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}
to the adjoint equation
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}v=0
. With this we mean a function
{\Gamma}^{*}={\Gamma}^{*}(y,x)
defined in
\big(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\big)\backslash D_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
, which satisfies the following conditions:

1.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the function
{\Gamma}^{*}(\,\cdot\,,x)
belongs to
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{x\})
and is a classical solution to
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\,{\Gamma}^{*}(\cdot,x)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\backslash\{x\}
;

2.

for every
{\varphi}\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\Omega})
the function

w(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Gamma^{*}(y,x){\varphi}(x)dx

(3.18)

belongs to
C_{{\mathbb{G}},{\rm loc}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and is a classical solution to
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}w=-\varphi
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

The existence of a fundamental solution for the operator
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}
when the coefficients are constant has been proved by Folland
[
9
]
and by Kogoj and Lanconelli in
[
16
]
with a different approach. The existence of a local fundamental solution for operators
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}
with Hölder continuous coefficients has been established by Bonfiglioli, Lanconelli and Uguzzoni in
[
3
]
for the operator
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}
in non-divergence form (
3.17
). Let us give the complete statement of the result in
[
3
]
, that has been obtained by the Levi’s parametrix method.

Theorem 3.1

(Theorem 1.5 in
[
3
]
)

Let
X_{1},\dots X_{m}
be a family of Hörmander vector fields satisfying the assumptions
[H.1]
and
[H.2]
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N>2
. Consider the differential operator
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}
in (
3.17
), where
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,m}
is a symmetric matrix satisfying the condition (
2.3
) for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and for some constants
0<\lambda<\Lambda
. Suppose that for every
i,j=1,\dots,m
, the coefficients
a_{ij}
belong to the space
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\alpha\in]0,1]
. Then, for every bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
there exists a fundamental solution
{\Gamma}^{*}
of
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}
in
\Omega
. Moreover, for every
x\in{\Omega}
the function
{\Gamma}^{*}(\,\cdot\,,x)
belongs to
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{2+\alpha}({\Omega}\backslash\{x\})
and, for every compact set
K\subset{\Omega}
there exists a positive constant
C
such that

0\leq{\Gamma}^{*}(x,y)\leq C\left(1+d_{\infty}(x,y)^{2-Q}\right),

(3.19)

for every
y\in K
and
x\in{\Omega}
.

Remark 3.2

Notice that the case
N\leq 2
reduces to the Euclidean one, which is not considered in this section devoted to degenerate subelliptic operators. In particular, we always have
Q>N\geq 3
. Moreover, in the proof of our main result we use the following property of
\Gamma^{*}
, which follows from (
3.18
). If
u\in C_{{\mathbb{G}},c}^{2}({\Omega})
then

u(y)=-\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,y)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\,dx.

(3.20)

Indeed, for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
u\in C^{2}_{{\mathbb{G}},c}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi(y)\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,y)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)dx\ dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u(x)\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,y)\varphi(y)dy\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u(x)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,y)\varphi(y)dy\Bigr)\ dx=-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\varphi(x)\,dx,

and we conclude by the arbitrariness of
\varphi
.

We are now in a position to state the main result of this section. We keep the notation
\Omega_{r}(x_{0}),\ \psi_{r}(x_{0})
used in Section
2
and define the kernels

\begin{split}K_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{0},x)&:=\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{|\nabla\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}(x,x_{0})|},\\
M_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{0},x)&:=\frac{Q}{(Q-2)}\cdot\frac{\langle A(x)\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0}),\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rangle}{\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})^{\frac{2(Q-1)}{Q-2}}},\end{split}

(3.21)

where
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
is defined in (
3.16
). We agree to set
K_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{0},x)=0
whenever
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})=0
.

Theorem 3.3

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

and let

be a classical solution to

(3.22)

\begin{split}u(x_{0})=&\frac{1}{r^{Q}}\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}M_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{0},x)u(x)\,dx\\
&+\frac{{Q}}{r^{Q}}\int_{0}^{r}\left({\varrho}^{Q-1}\int_{\Omega_{\varrho}(x_{0})}f(x)\left(\tfrac{1}{{\varrho}^{Q-2}}-\Gamma(x,x_{0})\right)dx\right)d{\varrho}.\end{split}

(3.23)

The second statement holds for
every

such that

Definition 3.8
(
Essential boundary)

Let
E\subset{\mathbb{G}}
be a measurable set. We say that
x\in\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E
if

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\lambda_{N}(E\cap B(x,r))}{\lambda_{N}(B(x,r))}>0,\qquad\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\lambda_{N}(B(x,r)\setminus E)}{\lambda_{N}(B(x,r))}>0

and we call
\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E
the measure-theoretic or essential boundary of
E
.

Observe that two different but equivalent distances on
{\mathbb{G}}
give the same essential boundary.

Let us see that the divergence theorem (
3.27
) can be rewritten in a form much closer to the classical formula, see
[
1
, Theorems 5.3, 5.4]
, where the problem is settled in general metric measure spaces, see also
[
2
, Theorem 1.6]
.

Theorem 3.9

Given a set of finite perimeter
E\subset{\mathbb{G}}
, for
P_{\mathbb{G}}(E,\cdot)
-a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{G}}
there is
\bar{r}(x)>0
such that

\ell_{\mathbb{G}}r^{Q-1}\leq P_{\mathbb{G}}(E,B_{c}(x,r))\leq L_{\mathbb{G}}r^{Q-1}

for every
r<\bar{r}(x)
, where
0<\ell_{\mathbb{G}}\leq L_{\mathbb{G}}<\infty
are two constants depending only on the group. As a consequence,
P_{\mathbb{G}}(E,\cdot)
is concentrated on
\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E
, i.e.,
P_{\mathbb{G}}(E,{\mathbb{G}}\setminus\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E)=0
. Moreover,
there is a Borel function

\beta_{E}:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to[\ell_{\mathbb{G}},L_{\mathbb{G}}]
such that

P_{\mathbb{G}}(E,B)=\int_{B\cap\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E}\beta_{E}(x)\,d{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}(x),\quad\forall B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{G}}).

(3.29)

The above theorem allows us to rewrite formula (
3.27
) as an integral on the essential boundary with respect to the
(Q-1)
-dimensional spherical Hausdorff measure as follows:

\int_{E}\mathrm{div}_{\mathbb{G}}g(x)dx=-\int_{\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{\ast}E}\langle g,\nu_{E}\rangle\,\beta_{E}(x)\,d{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}.

(3.30)

Remark 3.10

We collect here some useful results proved by Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano
[
10
, Theorem 2.3.5]
on functions belonging to
C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega)
, for which much more information is available.

If
\Omega
is bounded, a function
u
in
C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega)
also belongs to
BV_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega)
and by (
3.26
) the equalities

\int_{\Omega}X_{j}^{*}g(x)u(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}g(x)X_{j}u(x)dx,\qquad j=1,\ldots,m,

hold for every
g\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
. Recalling (
3.7
), we find that the measure derivative of
u
is
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\,\lambda_{N}
, where
\lambda_{N}
is the Lebesgue measure. Moreover, we say that
S\subset\Omega
is a
{\mathbb{G}}
-regular surface if for any
p\in S
there are an open neighbourhood
U
of
p
and
f\in C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}(U)
such that

S\cap U=\{x\in U:\ f(x)=0\ \textrm{and}\ \nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f(x)\neq 0\}.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
f\in C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega)
,
E=\{f<0\}
,
S=\{f=0\}
, and let
p\in\Omega
be such that
f(p)=0
and
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f(p)\neq 0
. Then, as proved in
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
p
such that
S\cap U
has finite perimeter and

\nu_{E}(x)=-\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f(x)}{|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f(x)|},\qquad x\in S\cap U.

In such a situation the equality
\partial^{*}_{\mathbb{G}}(E\cap U)=\partial(E\cap U)
holds, see
[
11
, Theorem 3.3]
. Notice also that the topological dimension of a
C^{1}_{\mathbb{G}}
-regular surface is
N-1
, see
[
11
, Proposition 3.1]
, whereas its Hausdorff dimension with respect to the distance
d_{\infty}
(or any other equivalent metric) is
Q-1
, see
[
11
, Corollary 3.7]
.

Remark 3.11

If
E
is a finite perimeter set and
\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E
is
{\mathbb{G}}
-regular, then formulas (
3.29
) and (
3.30
) become simpler. Indeed, in this case for every
x\in\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E
the normal unit vector
\nu(x)
is defined and, denoting by
\nu^{\bot}(x)
the codimension 1 subspace of
V_{1}
orthogonal to
\nu(x)
, we can introduce the hyperplane
N(x)=\nu^{\bot}(x)\oplus V_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\nu}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then,
\beta_{E}
is given by

\beta_{E}(x)=\theta^{-1}\max_{z\in B(0,1)}\{\mathcal{H}_{e}^{N-1}(B(z,1)\cap N(x))\}

and thus
\beta_{E}(x)=1
for every
x\in\partial^{*}E
, by Theorem 4.1 in
[
17
]
and the definition of the constant
\theta
in (
3.12
). This is the reason why we have chosen the distance
d_{\infty}
. These considerations are important in our proof of Theorem
3.3
, where (
3.30
) is applied to sets with finite perimeter such that
a part of
the essential boundary is
{\mathbb{G}}
-regular. Indeed, Theorem 4.1 in
[
17
]
is local, hence if
F\subset\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}E
is
{\mathbb{G}}
-regular and relatively open, then
\beta_{E}=1
in
F
.

We end this subsection with the coarea formula for
BV_{\mathbb{G}}
functions, and refer to
[
10
, Theorem 2.3.5]
for its proof.

Proposition 3.12
(
Coarea formula in
{\mathbb{G}}
)

If
u\in BV_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega)
then for a.e.

\tau\in\mathbb{R}
the set
E_{\tau}=\{x\in\Omega:\ u(x)>\tau\}
has finite
\mathbb{G}
-perimeter and

\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|(\Omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\chi_{E_{\tau}}\|(\Omega)d\tau.

(3.31)

Conversely, if
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\chi_{E_{\tau}}\|(\omega)d\tau<\infty

then
u\in BV_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega)
and equality (
3.31
) holds. Moreover, if
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a Borel function, then

\int_{\Omega}g(x)d\left\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u\right\|(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}g(x)d\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\chi_{E_{\tau}}\|(x)d\tau.

(3.32)

3.4
Proof of Theorem
3.3

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is similar to that of Theorem
2.1
. We sketch it and underline the points where different arguments are needed.

Proof

of Theorem
3.3
.
Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let
u
be a classical solution to
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u=f
in
\Omega
. Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
and let
r_{0}>0
be such that
\overline{\Omega_{r_{0}}(x_{0})}\subset\Omega
. Consider an open set
\Omega_{1}
such that
\overline{\Omega_{r_{0}}(x_{0})}\subset\Omega_{1}
and that
\overline{\Omega_{1}}\subset\Omega
. Then there exists the fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}
of the equation
\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}v=0
in
\Omega_{1}
. According to Remark
3.10
,
\Gamma^{*}\in BV_{\mathbb{G}}(\Omega_{1})
, hence we can apply the coarea formula (
3.32
) to the set
E_{r}:=\Omega_{r}(x_{0})=\{\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})>r^{2-Q}\}
, which has finite perimeter for a.e.
0<r<r_{0}
.

For such a choice of
r
, we choose a positive parameter
\varepsilon>0
small enough to have
B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0}):=\{d_{\infty}(x,x_{0})<\varepsilon\}\subset\Omega_{r}(x_{0})
. We let
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a function such that

{\rm supp}({\varphi})\subset B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
and that
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x)=1
for every
x
belonging to
B_{\varepsilon/2}(x_{0})
. Clearly,
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u\in C_{{\mathbb{G}},c}^{2}(\Omega_{1})
, then the very definition of
\Gamma^{*}
yields

u(x_{0})={\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})u(x_{0})=-\int_{\Omega}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,dx.

(3.33)

We next consider the functions
w(x):=(1-{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(x))u(x)
and
v(x):=\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})-r^{2-Q}
and we note that

\begin{split}w(x)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}v(x)-v(x)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}w(x)=&{\text{\rm div}}_{\mathbb{G}}\big(w(x)A(x)\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v(x)-v(x)A(x)\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w(x)\big)-\\
&{\text{\rm div}}_{\mathbb{G}}\big(w(x)v(x)b(x)\big),\end{split}

(3.34)

which can be written as follows

-v(x)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}w(x)={\text{\rm div}}_{\mathbb{G}}\,\Phi(x),\qquad\Phi(x):=\big(wA\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v-vA\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w-wvb\big)(x).

By our choice of
{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}
we have that
\Phi
vanishes in
B_{\varepsilon/2}(x_{0})
, then it can be extended to a
C_{\mathbb{G}}^{1}(\Omega)
function by setting
\Phi(x_{0})=0
. We then apply (
3.30
) to
\Omega_{r}(x_{0})
and we find

\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}v(x)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}w(x)dx=\int_{\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle\beta_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\,d\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}.

(3.35)

Here
\nu(x)
is the generalized unit normal as in Definition
3.6
, which coincides with

\tfrac{\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})}{\lvert\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\rvert}
whenever
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\neq 0
according to Remark
3.10
.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, from the equality
u=w+{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u
we deduce

u(x_{0})=-\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\left(\tfrac{1}{r^{Q-2}}+v(x)\right)\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)dx

(3.36)

We then recall that
f=\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}u=\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}w+\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)
so that, by adding (
3.36
) and (
3.35
), we find

u(x_{0})=-\int_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}v(x)f(x)+\tfrac{1}{r^{Q-2}}\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,dx+\int_{\psi_{r}(x)}\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle\beta_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\,d\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}.

(3.37)

Finally, notice that
v=0
on
\psi_{r}(x_{0})
and then
\Phi(x)=0
for every
x\in\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\Omega_{r}(x_{0})
such that

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})=0
, hence we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\partial_{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}

\displaystyle\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle\beta_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}\,d\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}=\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})\setminus\{\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=0\}}\langle\nu,\Phi\rangle\,d\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})\setminus\{\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,x_{0})=0\}}\Big\langle\frac{\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})}{|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})|},A\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\Gamma^{*}(x,x_{0})\Big\rangle u(x)\,d\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\psi_{r}(x_{0})}K_{\mathbb{G}}(x_{0},x)u(x)\,d\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q-1},

where we have taken into account that
\beta_{\Omega_{r}(x_{0})}=1
in the
{\mathbb{G}}
-regular part of
\psi_{r}(x_{0})
, see Remark
3.11
. The last step to conclude the proof of (
3.22
) consists in letting
\varepsilon\to 0
as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
to get rid of the integral of
r^{2-Q}\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{G}}({\varphi}_{\varepsilon}u)
in (
3.37
).

To deduce (
3.23
) from (
3.22
) we argue as in Section
2
, replacing
N
by
Q
and using the coarea formula provided by Theorem
3.12
in the last step.

\square

Remark 3.13

If the coefficents
a_{ij}
are
C^{\infty}
then the fundamental solution
\Gamma^{*}
is
C^{\infty}
as well and the level sets
