Inverse scattering transform for continuous and discrete space-time shifted integrable equations
Mark J. Ablowitz, Ziad H. Musslimani, Nicholas J. Ossi

Abstract
Nonlocal integrable partial differential equations possessing a spatial or temporal reflection have constituted an active research area for the past decade. Recently, more general classes of these nonlocal equations have been proposed, wherein the nonlocality appears as a combination of a shift (by a real or a complex parameter) and a reflection. This new shifting parameter manifests itself in the inverse scattering transform (IST) as an additional phase factor in an analogous way to the classical Fourier transform. In this paper, the IST is analyzed in detail for several examples of such systems. Particularly, time, space, and space-time shifted nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) and space-time shifted modified Korteweg-de Vries (mKdV) equations are studied. Additionally, the semi-discrete IST is developed for the time, space and space-time shifted variants of the Ablowitz-Ladik integrable discretization of the NLS. One-soliton solutions are constructed for all continuous and discrete cases.

Complex reductions of physical equations.

It is widely understood that complex reductions of physical problems are very important. For example, self-similar reductions of many evolution equations lead to Painlevé type equations (see e.g.
[
7
,
8
]
). The underlying basis of these equations are their properties in the complex plane, i.e. no moveable critical points. Two other well-known examples are the self-dual Yang Mills equations which are:
i) reductions of the Yang-Mills equations
[
40
]
and ii) the self-dual Einstein equations which are reductions of the Einstein equations
[

]

see also
[

Indeed, a further reduction of the self-dual Einstein equations are the one dimensional self-dual reductions of the Bianchi IX cosmological models
[

[

. An important feature of the Darboux-Halphen-Chazy equations is that they exhibit natural barriers where the solution is analytic inside and outside a moveable circle

1.3
Organization

In this paper, we present a detailed analysis of the inverse scattering transform associated with all the above symmetry reductions associated with the NLS and mKdV systems. This process involves three major steps: (i) the study of a direct scattering problem, which involves identifying certain crucial symmetries; (ii) determining the time evolution of the so-called scattering data; and (iii) solving an inverse scattering problem via a Riemann-Hilbert formulation. Our study of shifted continuous NLS equations is also extended to the discrete domain (continuous in time and discrete in space), where a detailed presentation of the inverse scattering transform associated with the shifted reductions of the Ablowitz-Ladik hierarchy is outlined. One-soliton solutions for the continuous and discrete shifted cases are given and their properties are studied. Additionally, in the study of classical Fourier transforms, an important general result is that a shift in physical space manifests as a phase shift in Fourier space. We find a similar result in the scattering theory associated these shifted nonlinear equations.

The paper is organized as follows:

•

In section 2, we discuss the connection between the shifted nonlocal integrable equations of the present work and their unshifted counterparts. The translational and scaling “pseudo-invariances” of these shifted equations are highlighted.

•

In section 3, we summarize the direct scattering problem for the general AKNS scattering problem associated with the NLS and mKdV coupled
(q,r)
systems. The phase shift formulae are discussed in section 3.3.

•

In section 4, we provide detailed derivations of the symmetries of the scattering data induced by each of the shifted nonlocal reductions presented above.

•

In section 5, we summarize the inverse scattering problem for the general AKNS scattering problem, and discuss the time evolution of the scattering data in both the NLS and mKdV cases.

•

In section 6, we provide detailed derivations of certain explicit representations of the scattering data that are useful in constructing soliton solutions.

•

In section 7, we construct the one-soliton solution for each of the shifted nonlocal NLS and mKdV variants.

•

In sections 8-12, we repeat all of the analyses of sections 3-7 in the context of shifted nonlocal semi-discrete NLS (Ablowitz-Ladik) systems. In section 8.3 the discrete analogue of the phase shift formula derived for the continuous problem is obtained.

•

In section 13, we offer some concluding remarks and suggestions for future exploration.

Connection between shifted and unshifted nonlocal equations

2.1
Continuous systems

2.1.1
A family of nonlocal equations with space-time pseudo-translation symmetries

The nonlocal integrable systems with temporal and/or spatial shifts (like those studied in this paper) provide an important and natural generalization of the nonlocal equations with temporal and/or spatial reflection that have been studied in recent years (precisely the ones without any shifts)
[
12
]
. They provide two important physical and mathematical aspects that the unshifted system lacks: (i) space and/or time translation symmetries and (ii) a nonlinearly induced complex PT symmetric potentials with a non-zero reflection symmetry point (like the case studied in
[
39
]
where a PT symmetric complex optical lattice
with shifted two-fold symmetry points were introduced and studied – see Sec. 1.2 for more details). In this section, we focus our discussion on the crucial role that the shift parameters
x_{0}
and
t_{0}
play in the restoration of a broken space-time translation symmetries.

The classical (local) nonlinear Schrödinger equation

iq_{t}(x,t)=q_{xx}(x,t)-2\sigma q^{2}(x,t)q^{*}(x,t),\;\;\;\sigma=\pm 1,

(26)

admits an important translational invariance symmetry given by the following:
If
q(x,t)
is a solution to Eq. (
26
) so is
q(x-x_{0}/2,t-t_{0}/2)
for any real constants
x_{0},t_{0}.
This statement has two consequences: (i) Space translation invariance allows for the construction of a larger class of solutions with additional free parameters. (ii) Time translation invariance symmetry implies that one does
not
need to specify the initial data at time zero, i.e.,
q(x,0)
. Instead, one can supplement Eq. (
26
) with an initial condition at any suitable “initial time” while preserving its
well-posedness.

In contrast, the PT symmetric, reverse-time only and reverse space-time nonlocal equations
(here
\sigma=\pm 1
)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)-2\sigma q^{2}_{\text{un}}(x,t)q^{*}_{\text{un}}(-x,t)\;,

(27)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)-2\sigma q^{2}_{\text{un}}(x,t)q_{\text{un}}(x,-t),

(28)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)-2\sigma q^{2}_{\text{un}}(x,t)q_{\text{un}}(-x,-t),

(29)

respectively break space, time, and space-time translation symmetry on the real line. That is to say:

\text{If~}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)~\text{solves~}(\ref{unshifted-PT-nonloc-NLS})\text{~then~}q_{\text{un}}(x-x_{0}/2,t)\text{~does~not~satisfy~}(\ref{unshifted-PT-nonloc-NLS}),x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

(30)

This means, despite Eq. (
27
) being a constant coefficient evolution equation, it breaks space translation invariance on the real line. Instead, this symmetry is preserved on the imaginary axis. Indeed, we have:

\text{If~}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)~\text{is~a~solution~to~}(\ref{unshifted-PT-nonloc-NLS})\text{~so~is~}q_{\text{un}}(x-ix_{0}/2,t),\;x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

(31)

Since Eq. (
27
) is an integrable system, it admits infinitely many conservation laws, two of which are the energy (corresponding to time translation invariance)
and momentum (related to space translation symmetry). Questions that immediately arise are:
Why does Eq. (
27
) break translation invariance on the real line and is there a “hidden” symmetry associated with it that would restore that broken symmetry? The answer is connected with the shift parameter
x_{0}
. In fact, we have the following statement (which is further explained in the next subsection):

\text{If~}q_{\text{un}}(x,t)~\text{solves~}(\ref{unshifted-PT-nonloc-NLS})\text{~then~}q_{\text{un}}(x-x_{0}/2,t)~\text{is~a~solution~to~the~shifted~nonlocal~NLS~}(\ref{shifted-PT-nonloc-NLS}),\;x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

(32)

Thus, the statement given in (
32
) implies the following important result:

The shifted NLS Eq. (
6
) represents a one-parameter family of nonlocal integrable equations with
endowed pseudo-translation symmetry in the following sense: if
q(x,t)
is a flow that belongs to the family, so is
q(x-x_{0}/2,t)
.

The situation is even more intricate when dealing with Eq. (
28
), where now time translation symmetry is broken both on the real and the imaginary axis. This critical fact again raises the question as to what causes the loss of such
time-invariance? The resolution to this issue is provided by the introduction of the time shift parameter
t_{0}
which endows
(
7
) with a pseudo-time translation symmetry.

It is also crucial to note that the temporal shifting parameter
t_{0}
restricts the time at which initial data should be provided. Namely, the time-shifted equations (
7
), (
8
), (
13
), and (
14
) are only well-posed if an initial condition is given specifically at time
t=t_{0}/2
. This is in contrast to
time-local integrable systems (including (
6
)), for which the well-posedness of initial value problems does not depend on the choice of the initial instant.

2.1.2
Relations between solution sets of shifted and unshifted nonlocal equations

As we show below, the solution set of a given shifted integrable equation is related to the solution set of its corresponding unshifted counterpart via a simple transformation. For example, suppose that
q_{\text{un}}(x,t)
is a given solution of the
unshifted

PT symmetric nonlocal NLS Eq. (
27
). Then, one can show that
q(x,t)
defined by

q(x,t)\equiv q_{\text{un}}(x-x_{0}/2,t),

(33)

is a solution of Eq. (
6
). Thus, this transformation maps a solution of the unshifted nonlocal NLS to a solution of the shifted nonlocal NLS.
Similar statements can be made regarding Eqns. (
7
) and (
8
). That is to say:

•

Let
q_{\text{un}}(x,t)
be a solution of the
unshifted
reverse-time only nonlocal NLS Eq. (
28
).
Then,

q(x,t)\equiv q_{\text{un}}(x,t-t_{0}/2),

(34)

is a solution to the time only shifted NLS Eq. (
7
).

•

Let
q_{\text{un}}(x,t)
be a solution of the
unshifted
reverse space-time nonlocal NLS Eq. (
29
).
Then,

q(x,t)\equiv q_{\text{un}}(x-x_{0}/2,t-t_{0}/2),

(35)

is a solution to the space-time shifted NLS Eq. (
8
).

At this point we make two important remarks.

1.

The shift relation (
33
) provides a direct connection between solutions of Eqns. (
27
)
and (
6
). As an example, a pure soliton solution
of Eq. (
27
)
yields a soliton solution to Eq. (
6
) via the shift relation (
33
).
On the other hand, as we have found in
[
21
]
,
solving the linear system of equations from IST for a pure soliton solution does not necessarily yield the soliton solution obtained from the shift relation (
33
). The reason is that there are a family of soliton solutions to Eq. (
27
) due to the fact that there is freedom of choice of norming constants which depend on the shift parameter
x_{0}
. There will be a suitable choice of norming constants that satisfy equation (
33
) but they may not come out directly from IST.
For example, the solutions given in Fig. 1 of
[
21
]
obtained from IST for different value of
x_{0}
are all pure soliton solutions but they differ in the time where the solitons interact or can be viewed as distributed differently at, e.g., large negative times. This observation has been verified numerically.

2.

There is also a direct transformation between two shifted equations (with different shifts). Namely if
q(x,t)
satisfies

iq_{t}(x,t)=q_{xx}(x,t)-2\sigma q^{2}(x,t)q^{*}(x_{0}-x,t),

(36)

then
\tilde{q}(x,t)=q(x+\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-\tilde{x}_{0}),t)
satisfies

i\tilde{q}_{t}(x,t)=\tilde{q}_{xx}(x,t)-2\sigma\tilde{q}^{2}(x,t)\tilde{q}^{*}(\tilde{x}_{0}-x,t).

(37)

This property highlights the nontrivial role that the shift parameters play in restoring an otherwise broken space translation
symmetry on the real line.

2.1.3
Nonlocal Painlevé equations

It is interesting to note that the shift properties between solutions of the unshifted and shifted nonlocal NLS equations manifest in their self-similar reductions to Painlevé type equations as well. In fact, we have the following statements:

1.

The space-shifted nonlocal NLS Eq. (
6
)
can be reduced to the nonlocal Painlevé equation

f_{zz}(z)+izf_{z}(z)+(i+\alpha)f(z)-2\sigma f^{2}(z)f^{*}(-z)=0,

q(x,t)=\varphi^{1/2}f(z),\;\;\varphi=\frac{e^{i\alpha\log t}}{2t},\;\;z=\frac{x-x_{0}/2}{(2t)^{1/2}}.

(39)

Note that the self-similar variable
z
includes the shift parameter
x_{0}
.

2.

The time-shifted nonlocal NLS Eq. (
7
)
can be reduced to the nonlocal Painlevé equation

f_{zz}(z)+izf(z)+if(z)-2\sigma\kappa f^{2}(z)f(\kappa z)=0,

(40)

where
\kappa
can be
i
or
-i
depending on the choice of branch, using the change of variables

q(x,t)=\frac{1}{(2t-t_{0})^{1/2}}f(z),\;\;z=\frac{x}{(2t-t_{0})^{1/2}}.

(41)

3.

The space-time-shifted nonlocal NLS Eq. (
8
)
can be reduced to the nonlocal Painlevé equation

f_{zz}(z)+izf(z)+if(z)-2\sigma\kappa f^{2}(z)f(-\kappa z)=0

(42)

using the change of variables

q(x,t)=\frac{1}{(2t-t_{0})^{1/2}}f(z),\;\;z=\frac{x-x_{0}/2}{(2t-t_{0})^{1/2}}.

(43)

2.1.4
Relation between inverse scattering and Fourier transforms

It is a well-known result in Fourier analysis that the Fourier transform of a function with a shifted argument is related to its unshifted counterpart by a phase factor. Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the Fourier transform defined by

\mathcal{F}[f(x)](k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-ikx}dx,

(47)

where
f
is a square-integrable function. Applying this transform to (
33
) we find

\mathcal{F}[q(x,t)](k)=e^{ikx_{0}/2}\mathcal{F}[q_{\text{un}}(x,t)](k).

(48)

As the IST is viewed as a generalization of the classical Fourier transform
[
5
]
, one can in fact derive relationships similar to (
48
) between the scattering data obtained for the unshifted nonlocal NLS equations (see for example
[
12
]
)
and the scattering data obtained in the present work for shifted cases. These relations are computed explicitly in Section 3.3.

2.2
Discrete systems

The above discussions related to functional connections between solution sets of continuous shifted and non-shifted space-time nonlocal NLS equations persist in the discrete domain. Indeed, consider the Ablowitz-Ladik
(Q_{n},R_{n})
system given by

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(t)-2Q_{n}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)-Q_{n}(t)R_{n}(t)(Q_{n+1}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)),

(49)

\displaystyle-i\partial_{t}R_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{n+1}(t)-2R_{n}(t)+R_{n-1}(t)-Q_{n}(t)R_{n}(t)(R_{n+1}(t)+R_{n-1}(t)).

(50)

In analogy to the continuous cases mentioned earlier, the coupled discrete system
(
49
)-(
50
) admits several shifted nonlocal symmetry reductions first found in
[
23
]
. Namely (recall that
\sigma=\pm 1
),

\displaystyle R_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma Q^{*}_{n_{0}-n}(t),\;n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z},

(51)

\displaystyle R_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma Q_{n}(t_{0}-t),\;t_{0}\in\mathbb{C},

(52)

\displaystyle R_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma Q_{n_{0}-n}(t_{0}-t),\;n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[i],\;t_{0}\in\mathbb{C}.

(53)

In (
53
), the notation
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
means that the real and imaginary parts of the complex parameter
n_{0}
are integers.
Customarily, we take
n
to be an integer throughout, though it is worth noting that the system (
49
)-(
50
) can be solved by IST for generic
n
without this restriction to the integers.
The above reductions lead to the integrable discrete space, time, and space-time NLS equations, respectively given by

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(t)-2Q_{n}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)-\sigma Q_{n}(t)Q^{*}_{n_{0}-n}(t)\left(Q_{n+1}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)\right),

(54)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(t)-2Q_{n}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)-\sigma Q_{n}(t)Q_{n}(t_{0}-t)\left(Q_{n+1}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)\right),

(55)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(t)-2Q_{n}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)-\sigma Q_{n}(t)Q_{n_{0}-n}(t_{0}-t)\left(Q_{n+1}(t)+Q_{n-1}(t)\right).

(56)

Now, let
Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)
denote a solution to the discrete (unshifted) spatially nonlocal equation

i\partial_{t}Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)=Q^{\text{un}}_{n+1}(t)-2Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)+Q^{\text{un}}_{n-1}(t)-\sigma Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)Q^{\text{un}*}_{-n}(t)\left(Q^{\text{un}}_{n+1}(t)+Q^{\text{un}}_{n-1}(t)\right).

(57)

Then,

Q_{n}(t)\equiv Q^{\text{un}}_{n-n_{0}/2}(t),

(58)

solves the
shifted
evolution equation (
54
). Similar arguments can be made for the time and space-time shifted cases as well. For example, if now
Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)
represents a solution to the (unshifted) space-time discrete nonlocal NLS

i\partial_{t}Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)=Q^{\text{un}}_{n+1}(t)-2Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)+Q^{\text{un}}_{n-1}(t)-\sigma Q^{\text{un}}_{n}(t)Q^{\text{un}}_{-n}(-t)\left(Q^{\text{un}}_{n+1}(t)+Q^{\text{un}}_{n-1}(t)\right),

(59)

it then follows that,

Q_{n}(t)\equiv Q^{\text{un}}_{n-n_{0}/2}(t-t_{0}/2),

(60)

is a solution to the
shifted
problem (
56
). Finally, if the semi-discrete Fourier transform is defined by

\mathcal{F}[f_{n}](Z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}Z^{n},\;\;|Z|=1,

(61)

then we have the following result

\mathcal{F}[Q_{n}](Z)=\mathcal{F}[Q^{\text{un}}_{n-n_{0}/2}](Z)=Z^{n_{0}/2}\mathcal{F}[Q^{\text{un}}_{n}](Z).

(62)

This is analogous to the continuous result given in (
48
). In section 8.3, we find a similar relationship between the scattering data associated with the shifted and unshifted Ablowitz-Ladik scattering problems.

3.1
Linear pair

We begin with the AKNS spectral problem
[

]

\displaystyle v_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}v,\;\;\mathcal{X}=\begin{pmatrix}-ik&q(x,t)\\
r(x,t)&ik\end{pmatrix},

(63)

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}v.

(64)

where
v(x,t,k)=(v_{1}(x,t,k),v_{2}(x,t,k))^{T}
with
T
denoting matrix transpose, and
q(x,t)
and
r(x,t)

are complex-valued functions of the real variables
x,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Here,
k
is a complex spectral parameter independent of
x,t
. Furthermore,
q
and
r
are referred to as potentials in the AKNS scattering problem and are assumed throughout the rest of the paper to decay rapidly to zero as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
;
for the scattering problem we are studying (
63
), AKNS
[
5
]
established that
q,r\in L^{1}
is sufficient.
If
\mathcal{T}
is chosen to be of the form

\mathcal{T}_{\text{NLS}}=\begin{pmatrix}2ik^{2}+iqr&2kq-iq_{x}\\
2kr+ir_{x}&-2ik^{2}-iqr\end{pmatrix},

(65)

then the compatibility condition between (
63
) and (
64
) (namely,
v_{xt}=v_{tx}
) results in the coupled NLS system given in (
1
)-(
2
). Alternatively, if
\mathcal{T}
is given by

\mathcal{T}_{\text{mKdV}}=\begin{pmatrix}-4ik^{3}-2ikqr+rq_{x}-qr_{x}&4k^{2}q+2ikq_{x}+2q^{2}r-q_{xx}\\
4k^{2}r-2ikr_{x}+2qr^{2}-r_{xx}&4ik^{3}+2ikqr-rq_{x}+qr_{x}\end{pmatrix},

(66)

then the compatibility condition gives rise to the mKdV system
(
9
)-(
10
).

3.2
Eigenfunctions and scattering data

We now summarize the major components needed to carry out the inverse scattering transform for generic potentials. The first step involves solving the direct scattering problem (
63
) at the initial instant. For local in time reductions, the initial instant can be any time (without loss of generality we take it to be
t=0
). However, it is important to note that for the time-shifted reductions,
the initial instant is set to be
t=t_{0}/2
so that the direct problem is well-posed, i.e. we assume an initial condition
q(x,t_{0}/2)
is provided.

Consider the following eigenfunctions of (
63
) defined by the boundary conditions they satisfy:

\displaystyle\phi(x,t,k)\sim e^{-ikx}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}},\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}(x,t,k)\sim e^{ikx}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{as}\;x\rightarrow-\infty,

(67)

\displaystyle\psi(x,t,k)\sim e^{ikx}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}},\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x,t,k)\sim e^{-ikx}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}},\;\;\;\;\text{as}\;x\rightarrow+\infty.

(68)

Here, and throughout the rest of the paper,
\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}}=(1,0)^{T}
and
\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}}=(0,1)^{T}
. Next, since each of the eigenfunction pairs
\{\phi,\bar{\phi}\}
and
\{\psi,\bar{\psi}\}
are separately linearly independent, we may construct an expansion of the form

\displaystyle\phi(x,t,k)=a(t,k)\bar{\psi}(x,t,k)+b(t,k)\psi(x,t,k),

(69)

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}(x,t,k)=\bar{a}(t,k)\psi(x,t,k)+\bar{b}(t,k)\bar{\psi}(x,t,k),

(70)

where
a,b,\bar{a},\bar{b}
are referred to as the scattering data and are given by

\displaystyle a(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(\phi(x,t,k),\psi(x,t,k)),

(71)

\displaystyle\bar{a}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(\bar{\psi}(x,t,k),\bar{\phi}(x,t,k)),

(72)

\displaystyle b(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(\bar{\psi}(x,t,k),\phi(x,t,k)),

(73)

\displaystyle\bar{b}(t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(\bar{\phi}(x,t,k),\psi(x,t,k)),

(74)

with
W
being the Wronksian defined by
W(u,v)=u_{1}v_{2}-v_{1}u_{2}
with
u=(u_{1},u_{2})^{T}
and
v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{T}
.
Furthermore, it can be shown that the scattering data satisfy

a(t,k)\bar{a}(t,k)-b(t,k)\bar{b}(t,k)=1.

(75)

It will also be useful to define the following modified eigenfunctions that satisfy constant boundary conditions:

\displaystyle M(x,t,k)=e^{ikx}\phi(x,t,k),\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{M}(x,t,k)=e^{-ikx}\bar{\phi}(x,t,k),

(76)

\displaystyle N(x,t,k)=e^{-ikx}\psi(x,t,k),\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{N}(x,t,k)=e^{ikx}\bar{\psi}(x,t,k).

(77)

Importantly, it can be shown that
M
and
N
can be analytically continued into the upper half complex
k
-plane, while
\bar{M}
and
\bar{N}
are analytic in the lower half complex
k
-plane. These analyticity properties will be crucial in the formulation of the inverse scattering problem. Particularly, one can rewrite (
69
) and (
70
) as

\displaystyle\mu(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{N}(x,t,k)+\rho(t,k)e^{2ikx}N(x,t,k),

(78)

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(x,t,k)+\bar{\rho}(t,k)e^{-2ikx}\bar{N}(x,t,k),

(79)

where
\mu=M/a
and
\bar{\mu}=\bar{M}/\bar{a}
, and the so-called reflection coefficients are given by
\rho=b/a
and
\bar{\rho}=\bar{b}/\bar{a}
. The system (
78
)-(
79
) will later be viewed as a Riemann-Hilbert problem on the real
k
-axis.

Relation between the scattering data for the spatially shifted and unshifted AKNS systems

Here, we make a note of the scattering-space relationships between spatially shifted nonlocal equations and their unshifted counterparts, as mentioned in Section 2. Since the relations here apply to all reductions with a spatial shift, we suppress the time argument throughout this section. First, recall the physical-space relationship (

)
q(x)=q_{\text{un}}(x-x_{0}/2)\equiv q_{\text{un}}(y)

\displaystyle\Psi(y,k)\sim e^{iky}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}},\;\;\;\;

(81)

Since

, comparing with (

\displaystyle\phi(x,k)=e^{-ikx_{0}/2}\Phi(x-x_{0}/2,k),\;\;\;\;

Furthermore, let

Note the similarity between the property of the Fourier transform given in (

4.3.1
Symmetries between the eigenfunctions

Assume that

\displaystyle-v_{2x}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})

\displaystyle ikv_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})+\sigma q(x,t)v_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*}),

from which we can deduce the following:

{\rm If}\;v(x,t,k)\;{\rm solves}\;(\ref{scat})\;{\rm with}\;r(x,t)=\sigma q^{*}(x_{0}-x,t)\;{\rm so\;does}\;\Lambda_{-}v^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*}).

In a similar fashion to the space-time shifted case, by comparing boundary conditions one can obtain the following symmetries:

\displaystyle\psi(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikx_{0}}\Lambda_{-}\phi^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*}),

(111)

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-ikx_{0}}\Lambda_{-}^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*}),

(112)

which in turn lead to symmetries between the modified eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle N(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{-}M^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*}),

(113)

\displaystyle\bar{N}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{-}^{-1}\bar{M}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*}).

(114)

4.3.2
Symmetries of the scattering data

Starting from (
71
) and using (
111
), we have

a(t,k)=e^{ikx_{0}}[\phi_{1}(x,t,k)\phi_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})+\sigma\phi_{2}(x,t,k)\phi_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})].

(115)

Letting
x\rightarrow x_{0}-x
,
k\rightarrow-k^{*}
, and complex conjugating leads to the result:

a(t,k)=a^{*}(t,-k^{*}).

(116)

Also, the scattering data
\bar{a}
satisfies an analogous property:

\bar{a}(t,k)=\bar{a}^{*}(t,-k^{*}).

(117)

Note that the previous results imply the following fact:

\displaystyle\text{If}~a(k_{j})=0~\text{and}~\bar{a}(\bar{k}_{j})=0,~\text{then}~a(-k_{j}^{*})=0~\text{and}~\bar{a}(-\bar{k}_{j}^{*})=0.

Next, start form (
74
) and apply (
111
) to obtain

\bar{b}(t,k)=e^{ikx_{0}}[\bar{\phi}_{1}(x,t,k)\phi_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})+\sigma\bar{\phi}_{2}(x,t,k)\phi_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})].

(118)

Additionally, from (
73
) using (
112
) we have

b(t,k)=e^{-ikx_{0}}[\bar{\phi}_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})\phi_{2}(x,t,k)+\sigma\bar{\phi}_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t,-k^{*})\phi_{1}(x,t,k)].

(119)

Let
x\rightarrow x_{0}-x
, conjugate, and let
k\rightarrow-k
(noting that
b
is only defined for real
k
), to get

\bar{b}(t,k)=\sigma e^{2ikx_{0}}b^{*}(t,-k)\;,\;\;k\in\mathbb{R}

(120)

4.4
Conjugate space-time shifted reduction (mKdV)

4.4.1
Symmetries between the eigenfunctions

Assume that
v(x,t,k)
is any solution of (
63
) with
r(x,t)=\sigma q^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)
. Letting
x\rightarrow x_{0}-x
,
t\rightarrow t_{0}-t
,
k\rightarrow-k^{*}
; taking the complex conjugate; and rearranging gives

\displaystyle\sigma v_{1x}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ik\sigma v_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})-\sigma q^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)v_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}),

\displaystyle-v_{2x}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ikv_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*})+\sigma q(x,t)v_{1}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}),

(121)

from which the we obtain the following conclusion:

{\rm If}\;v(x,t,k)\;{\rm solves}\;(\ref{scat})\;{\rm with}\;r(x,t)=\sigma q^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)\;{\rm so\;does}\;\Lambda_{-}v^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}).

In a similar fashion to the space-time and space shifted reductions, by comparing boundary conditions one can obtain the following symmetries:

\displaystyle\psi(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikx_{0}}\Lambda_{-}\phi^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}),

(122)

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-ikx_{0}}\Lambda_{-}^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}).

(123)

These in turn lead to symmetries between the modified eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle N(x,t,k)=\Lambda_{-}M^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}),

(124)

\displaystyle\bar{N}(x,t,k)=\Lambda_{-}^{-1}\bar{M}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k^{*}).

(125)

4.4.2
Symmetries of the scattering data

Since the calculations are very similar to the previous two reductions, we only summarize the results:

\displaystyle a(t,k)=a^{*}(t_{0}-t,-k^{*}),\;\;\;\bar{a}(t,k)=\bar{a}^{*}(t_{0}-t,-k^{*}).

(126)

\displaystyle\text{If}~a(k_{j})=0~\text{and}~\bar{a}(\bar{k}_{j})=0,~\text{then}~a(-k_{j}^{*})=0~\text{and}~\bar{a}(-\bar{k}_{j}^{*})=0.

\displaystyle\bar{b}(t,k)=\sigma e^{2ikx_{0}}b^{*}(t_{0}-t,-k)\;,\;\;k\in\mathbb{R}.

(127)

5
Inverse scattering and time dependence – Continuous case

5.1
Inverse scattering: Riemann-Hilbert approach

The next step of the inverse scattering transform is to solve an inverse problem with the goal of constructing an explicit formula for the potentials
q(x,t)
and
r(x,t)
using the known scattering data. This is accomplished by reformulating the AKNS scattering problem as a Riemann-Hilbert problem.
The scattering problem (
63
) can admit discrete eigenvalues. We will assume that these occur at simple zeros of
a(k)
and
\bar{a}(k)
in the upper and lower half planes respectively, and will denote them by
\{k_{j}:\Im k_{j}>0\}_{j=1}^{J}
, and
\{\bar{k}_{j}:\Im\bar{k}_{j}<0\}_{j=1}^{\bar{J}}
, respectively. Furthermore, it is useful to define the so-called norming constants,

C_{j}\equiv\frac{b_{j}}{a_{k}(k_{j})},\;\;\bar{C}_{j}\equiv\frac{\bar{b}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{k}(\bar{k}_{j})},

(128)

where
b_{j},\bar{b}_{j}
are bound state coefficients and
a_{k}
denotes derivative of
a
with respect to
k
. The details of the Riemann-Hilbert problem associated with the AKNS scattering problem are discussed thoroughly in
[
6
]
. Presently, we quote the main result of the inverse problem. That is, from (
78
) and (
79
) it can be shown that the modified eigenfunctions satisfy a system of algebraic-integral equations involving the discrete eigenvalues, norming constants, and reflection coefficients:

\displaystyle\bar{N}(x,\bar{k}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}\frac{C_{\ell}e^{2ik_{\ell}x}N(x,k_{\ell})}{\bar{k}_{j}-k_{\ell}}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\rho(\xi)e^{2i\xi x}N(x,\xi)}{\xi-(\bar{k}_{j}-i0)}d\xi,

(129)

\displaystyle N(x,k_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\bar{J}}\frac{\bar{C}_{\ell}e^{-2i\bar{k}_{\ell}x}\bar{N}(x,\bar{k}_{\ell})}{k_{j}-\bar{k}_{\ell}}-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\bar{\rho}(\xi)e^{-2i\xi x}\bar{N}(x,\xi)}{\xi-(k_{j}+i0)}d\xi.

(130)

Once the eigenfunctions are determined from the above system, the potentials can be recovered using the following formulae derived using the asymptotic expansions of the modified eigenfunctions (see
[
6
]
for details):

\displaystyle r(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2i\sum_{j=1}^{J}e^{2ik_{j}x}C_{j}(t)N_{2}(x,k_{j})+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(\xi)e^{2i\xi x}N_{2}(x,\xi)d\xi,

(131)

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2i\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{J}}e^{-2i\bar{k}_{j}x}\bar{C}_{j}\bar{N}_{1}(x,\bar{k}_{j})+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{\rho}(\xi)e^{-2i\xi x}\bar{N}_{1}(x,\xi)d\xi.

(132)

5.2
Time evolution

In this section, we provide the time evolution of all scattering data and norming constants. The time evolution of the scattering data is the primary difference between the inverse scattering transforms associated with the coupled NLS (
1
)-(
2
) and mKdV (
9
)-(
10
) systems due to the difference in the choice of
\mathcal{T}
in (
64
).

5.2.1
NLS time evolution

If the time-evolution component of the linear pair is chosen to be
\mathcal{T}_{\text{NLS}}
as in (
65
), the scattering data
a,b
evolve in time according to the following results (see for example
[
6
]
):

a_{t}(t,k)=0\;,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;b_{t}(t,k)=-4ik^{2}b(t,k)\;.

(133)

Similarly, the other set of scattering data
\bar{a},\bar{b}
satisfy

\bar{a}_{t}(t,k)=0\;,\;\;\;\;\;\bar{b}_{t}(t,k)=4ik^{2}\bar{b}(t,k)\;.

(134)

For the local in time reductions (in the present work, only the space-shifted case), we have

\displaystyle a(t,k)=a(0,k),\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{a}(t,k)=\bar{a}(0,k),

(135)

\displaystyle b(t,k)=e^{-4ik^{2}t}b(0,k),\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{b}(t,k)=e^{4ik^{2}t}\bar{b}(0,k).

(136)

As noted in section III, for all time-shifted reductions, the initial condition must be provided at
t=t_{0}/2
, in which case the initial scattering data will be obtained at that time. As such, we denote the time evolution of the scattering data by

\displaystyle a(t,k)=a(t_{0}/2,k),\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{a}(t,k)=\bar{a}(t_{0}/2,k),

(137)

\displaystyle b(t,k)=e^{-4ik^{2}(t-t_{0}/2)}b(t_{0}/2,k),\;\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{b}(t,k)=e^{4ik^{2}(t-t_{0}/2)}\bar{b}(t_{0}/2,k).

(138)

The evolution of the norming constants
C_{j}
and
\bar{C}_{j}
defined in equation (
128
)
is given for time-local cases by

C_{j}(t)=C_{j}(0)e^{-4ik_{j}^{2}t},\;\;\;\bar{C}_{j}(t)=\bar{C}_{j}(0)e^{4i\bar{k}_{j}^{2}t},

(139)

and for time-shifted cases by

C_{j}(t)=C_{j}(t_{0}/2)e^{-4ik_{j}^{2}(t-t_{0}/2)},\;\;\;\bar{C}_{j}(t)=\bar{C}_{j}(t_{0}/2)e^{4i\bar{k}_{j}^{2}(t-t_{0}/2)}.

(140)

5.2.2
mKdV time evolution

If the time-evolution component of the linear pair is chosen to be
\mathcal{T}_{\text{mKdV}}
as in (
66
), the scattering data
a
and
\bar{a}
are again independent of time, and
b
and
\bar{b}
evolve according to

b(t,k)=e^{8ik^{3}(t-t_{0}/2)}b(t_{0}/2,k)\;,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\bar{b}(t,k)=e^{-8ik^{3}(t-t_{0}/2)}b(t_{0}/2,k)\;,

(141)

since we are only considering time-shifted reductions to the mKdV system. Following from this, the time evolution of the norming constants is given by

C_{j}(t)=C_{j}(t_{0}/2)e^{8ik_{j}^{3}(t-t_{0}/2)},\;\;\;\bar{C}_{j}(t)=\bar{C}_{j}(t_{0}/2)e^{-8i\bar{k}_{j}^{3}(t-t_{0}/2)}.

(142)

6
Trace formulae and symmetries for
b_{j},\bar{b}_{j}
– Continuous case

For the time-shift reduction case (as well as the classical NLS) the symmetries connect the scattering data and norming
constants in the upper half plane to their corresponding quantities defined in the lower half plane. This implies that the eigenvalues

k_{j}
and norming constants
C_{j}
are counted as free parameters and the values of
\bar{k}_{j}
and
\bar{C}_{j}
are uniquely determined by
the underlying symmetries. However, in the cases involving spatial nonlocality, the symmetries of the scattering data and norming constants do
not
relate their respective values in the upper and lower half planes. In order to understand the underlying symmetries of the norming constants

C_{j}=b_{j}/a_{k}(k_{j})
and
\bar{C}_{j}=\bar{b}_{j}/\bar{a}_{k}(\bar{k}_{j})
we consider their numerators and denominators separately. For the denominators we employ trace formulae which can be used to show that the data
a_{k}(k_{j}),\bar{a}_{k}(\bar{k}_{j})
depend on the eigenvalues
k_{j},\bar{k}_{j}.
For the numerators, it turns out that we can find symmetries relating
b_{j},\bar{b}_{j}
.

6.1
Trace formulae

6.1.1
Space-time shifted reduction

The derivation of the trace formulae follows an identical set of steps as shown in
[
13
]
. As such, the details are omitted. We
assume that
a(k)
and
\bar{a}(k)
have simple zeros
\{k_{j}:\Im k_{j}>0\}_{j=1}^{J}
and
\{\bar{k}_{j}:\Im\bar{k}_{j}<0\}_{j=1}^{J}
respectively.
The following trace formulae can be obtained:

\displaystyle\log a(k)

\displaystyle\underset{\Im k>0}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left(\frac{k-k_{j}}{k-\bar{k}_{j}}\right)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log[1+b(t,\xi)\bar{b}(t,\xi)]}{\xi-k}d\xi,

(143)

\displaystyle\log\bar{a}(k)

\displaystyle\underset{\Im k<0}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left(\frac{k-\bar{k}_{j}}{k-{k}_{j}}\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log[1+b(t,\xi)\bar{b}(t,\xi)]}{\xi-k}d\xi.

(144)

We shall restrict the discussion to reflectionless potentials for which
b=\bar{b}=0.
In this case, the second terms in (
143
) and (
144
) vanish. If we assume we have only a single eigenvalue
k=k_{1},\bar{k}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, we find

a_{k}(k_{1})=\frac{1}{k_{1}-\bar{k}_{1}}\;,\;\;\;\;\;\bar{a}_{k}(\bar{k}_{1})=\frac{1}{\bar{k}_{1}-k_{1}}\;.

(145)

6.1.2
Space shifted reduction and conjugate space-time shifted reduction

In the space shifted case, as well as the conjugate space-time shifted case associated with the mKdV system, we found that the zeros of
a
and
\bar{a}
appear in pairs.
Denote by

\{k_{j},-k_{j}^{*}:\Im k_{j}>0\}_{j=1}^{J}
and
\{\bar{k}_{j},-\bar{k}_{j}^{*}:\Im\bar{k}_{j}<0\}_{j=1}^{J}
, the (simple) zeros of

a(k)
and
\bar{a}(k)
respectively. The general trace formulae are:

\displaystyle\log a(k)

\displaystyle\underset{\Im k>0}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left[\frac{(k-{k}_{j})(k+{k}_{j}^{*})}{(k-\bar{k}_{j})(k+\bar{k}_{j}^{*})}\right]+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log[1+b(t,\xi)\bar{b}(t,\xi)]}{\xi-k}d\xi,

(146)

\displaystyle\log\bar{a}(k)

\displaystyle\underset{\Im k<0}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left[\frac{(k-\bar{k}_{j})(k+\bar{k}_{j}^{*})}{(k-k_{j})(k+k_{j}^{*})}\right]-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log[1+b(t,\xi)\bar{b}(t,\xi)]}{\xi-k}d\xi.

(147)

Again, we consider reflectionless potentials and assume a single eigenvalue pair
k=k_{1},-k_{1}^{*},\bar{k}_{1},-\bar{k}_{1}^{*}
, in which case we find

\displaystyle a_{k}(k_{1})=\frac{k_{1}+k_{1}^{*}}{(k_{1}-\bar{k}_{1})(k_{1}+\bar{k}_{1}^{*})}\;,\;\;\;a_{k}(-k^{*}_{1})=\frac{k_{1}^{*}+k_{1}}{(k_{1}^{*}+\bar{k}_{1})(-k_{1}^{*}+\bar{k}_{1}^{*})}\;,

(148)

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{k}(\bar{k}_{1})=\frac{\bar{k}_{1}+\bar{k}_{1}^{*}}{(\bar{k}_{1}-k_{1})(\bar{k}_{1}-k_{1}^{*})}\;,\;\;\;\bar{a}_{k}(-\bar{k}^{*}_{1})=\frac{\bar{k}_{1}^{*}+\bar{k}_{1}}{(\bar{k}_{1}^{*}+k_{1})(-\bar{k}_{1}^{*}+k_{1}^{*})}\;.

(149)

Later, for simplicity, we will consider the case when
k_{1},\bar{k}_{1}
are on the imaginary axis. With this restriction, the calculations become identical to the space-time shift case, and the formulae for
a_{k}
and
\bar{a}_{k}
given in (
145
) hold.

6.2
Computing symmetries of
b_{j}
and
\bar{b}_{j}

In this section we obtain the symmetries that
b_{j}
and
\bar{b}_{j}
satisfy.
This in turn will be later used to determine the dependence of the norming constants
C_{j}
and
\bar{C}_{j}
on these eigenvalues.

6.2.1
Space-time shifted reduction

At a discrete eigenvalue
k_{j}
, we have the relationship

M(x,t,k_{j})=b_{j}(t)e^{2ik_{j}x}N(x,t,k_{j})\;.

(150)

Using the symmetry relation (
102
), together with the second component of Eq. (
150
), results in

N_{1}(x,t,k_{j})=-\sigma b_{j}(t_{0}-t)e^{2ik_{j}(x_{0}-x)}N_{2}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,k_{j})\;.

(151)

Next, applying the symmetry relation
(
102
) to the first component of
(
150
) gives rise to

N_{2}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,k_{j})=b_{j}(t)e^{2ik_{j}x}N_{1}(x,t,k_{j})\;.

(152)

Substituting (
152
) into (
151
) one
obtains the symmetry

b_{j}(t_{0}-t)b_{j}(t)=-\sigma e^{-2ik_{j}x_{0}}\;\;\;\;\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\;\;\;b^{2}_{j}(t_{0}/2)=-\sigma e^{-2ik_{j}x_{0}}\;,

(153)

where we used the time evolution of
b(t)
given by (
133
). For a one soliton solution we find

b_{1}(t_{0}/2)=\gamma_{1}e^{i(1+\sigma)\pi/4}e^{-ik_{1}x_{0}}\;,\;\;\;\gamma_{1}=\pm 1\;.

(154)

To determine the value of
\bar{b}_{j}(t)
, one follows similar steps, which lead to

\bar{b}_{j}(t_{0}-t)\bar{b}_{j}(t)=-\sigma e^{2i\bar{k}_{j}x_{0}}\;\;\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\;\;\bar{b}^{2}_{j}(t_{0}/2)=-\sigma e^{2i\bar{k}_{j}x_{0}}\;.

(155)

In obtaining the expression for
\bar{b}_{j}(t_{0}/2)
we made use of the evolution of the scattering data
\bar{b}_{j}(t)
given
by (
134
). For the one soliton case we find

\bar{b}_{1}(t_{0}/2)=\bar{\gamma}_{1}e^{i(1+\sigma)\pi/4}e^{ik_{1}x_{0}}\;\;,\;\;\;\bar{\gamma}_{1}=\pm 1\;.

(156)

With the help of (
145
) and (
140
) we are now ready to compute the norming constants
C_{1}
and
\bar{C}_{1}
for all time.
We thus have

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}e^{i(1+\sigma)\pi/4}(k_{1}-\bar{k}_{1})e^{-ik_{1}x_{0}}e^{2ik_{1}^{2}t_{0}}e^{-4ik_{1}^{2}t}\;,

(157)

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{1}e^{i(1+\sigma)\pi/4}(\bar{k}_{1}-{k}_{1})e^{i\bar{k}_{1}x_{0}}e^{-2i\bar{k}_{1}^{2}t_{0}}e^{4i\bar{k}_{1}^{2}t}\;.

(158)

6.2.2
Space shifted reduction

Our starting point is
again Eq. (
150
). Substituting the symmetry condition
(
113
) into the second component one finds

N_{1}(x,k_{j})=-\sigma b_{j}^{*}e^{2ik_{j}(x_{0}-x)}N_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,-k_{j}^{*})\;.

(159)

Next, we use the symmetry (
113
)
to rewrite the first component of Eq. (
150
) in the form

N_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,-k_{j}^{*})=b_{j}e^{2ik_{j}x}N_{1}(x,k_{j})\;.

(160)

Substituting Eq. (
160
) back
into (
159
) and taking the one-soliton case gives

|b_{1}|^{2}=-\sigma e^{-2ik_{1}x_{0}}.

(161)

This is only possible if
\sigma=-1
and
k_{1}
is on the imaginary axis, i.e.
k_{1}\equiv i\eta_{1}
where
\eta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. In this case we have

b_{1}=e^{i\theta_{1}}e^{\eta_{1}x_{0}},\;\;\;\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}.

(162)

A similar expression can be obtained for
\bar{b}_{j}.
Indeed, following an analogous procedure one can find

|\bar{b}_{1}|^{2}=-\sigma e^{2ik_{1}x_{0}}\;.

(163)

Again, we take
\sigma=-1
and
\bar{k}_{1}\equiv-i\bar{\eta}_{1}
with
\bar{\eta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, leading to

\bar{b}_{1}=e^{i\bar{\theta}_{1}}e^{\bar{\eta}_{1}x_{0}}\;,\;\;\;\bar{\theta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\;.

(164)

To write down a closed form expression for the norming constants
C_{1}
and
\bar{C}_{1}
we use the above formulae, the definition and time-evolution of the norming
constants, and Eq. (
145
):

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{i\theta_{1}}(\eta_{1}+\bar{\eta}_{1})e^{\eta_{1}x_{0}}e^{4i\eta_{1}^{2}t}\;,

(165)

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ie^{i\bar{\theta}_{1}}({\eta}_{1}+\bar{\eta}_{1})e^{\bar{\eta}_{1}x_{0}}e^{-4i\bar{\eta}_{1}^{2}t}\;.

(166)

6.2.3
Conjugate space-time shifted reduction

Recall that this symmetry is associated with the conjugate space-time shifted mKdV equation. We start by applying the symmetry (
124
) to the second component of (
150
) gives

N_{1}(x,t,k_{j})=-\sigma b_{j}^{*}(t_{0}-t)e^{2ik_{j}(x_{0}-x)}N_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k_{j}^{*}).

(167)

Now, applying (
124
) to the first component of (
150
) we also have

N_{2}^{*}(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t,-k_{j}^{*})=b_{j}(t)e^{2ik_{j}x}N_{1}(x,t,k_{j}).

(168)

Substituting (
168
) into (
167
) we find

b_{j}(t)b_{j}^{*}(t_{0}-t)=-\sigma e^{-2ik_{j}x_{0}}.

(169)

Unlike the previous reductions, here we apply the mKdV time evolution of
b
given by

b(t,k)=e^{8ik^{3}(t-t_{0}/2)}b(t_{0}/2,k)
, which for the one-soliton case leads to

|b_{1}(t_{0}/2)|^{2}=-\sigma e^{-2ik_{1}x_{0}}.

(170)

This is only possible if
\sigma=-1
and
k_{1}
is on the imaginary axis, i.e.
k_{1}\equiv i\eta_{1}
with
\eta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Under these assumptions, we have

b_{1}(t_{0}/2)=e^{i\theta_{1}}e^{\eta_{1}x_{0}},\;\;\;\;\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}.

(171)

Following an analogous series of steps, one can obtain

|\bar{b}_{1}(t_{0}/2)|^{2}=-\sigma e^{2i\bar{k}_{1}x_{0}}.

(172)

Taking
\sigma=-1
and
\bar{k}_{1}\equiv-i\bar{\eta}_{1}
with
\eta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\bar{b}_{1}(t_{0}/2)=e^{i\bar{\theta}_{1}}e^{\bar{\eta}_{1}x_{0}},\;\;\;\;\bar{\theta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}.

(173)

To write down a closed form expression for the norming constants
C_{1}
and
\bar{C}_{1}
, we use the above formulae, the definition and time-evolution of the norming
constants, and Eq. (
145
):

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ie^{i\theta_{1}}(\eta_{1}+\bar{\eta}_{1})e^{\eta_{1}x_{0}}e^{-4\eta_{1}^{3}t_{0}}e^{8\eta_{1}^{3}t},

(174)

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ie^{i\bar{\theta}_{1}}(\eta_{1}+\bar{\eta}_{1})e^{\bar{\eta}_{1}x_{0}}e^{-4\bar{\eta}_{1}^{3}t_{0}}e^{8\bar{\eta}_{1}^{3}t}.

One-soliton solutions – Continuous case

In this section we compute 1-soliton solutions for the time, space-time, and space shifted NLS equations, as well as the complex space-time shifted mKdV equation. Before considering the relevant nonlocal reductions, one can obtain the general 1-soliton for the coupled
(q,r)

7.1
Time shifted reduction

In this case, we have symmetries connecting the “upper” scattering data to the “lower” scattering data. Particularly, from (

\bar{k}_{1}=-k_{1}

Substituting (

q(x,t)=\frac{-2i\sigma C_{1}(t_{0}/2)e^{-2ik_{1}^{2}t_{0}}e^{4ik_{1}^{2}t}e^{2ik_{1}x}}{1-\sigma\frac{C_{1}(t_{0}/2)^{2}}{4k_{1}^{2}}e^{4ik_{1}x}}.

This soliton solution has two arbitrary parameteres

7.2
Space-time shifted reduction

Recall that unlike the time-shifted case, here the “upper” and “lower” scattering data are not connected. Instead,

and

are free parameters and the norming constants can be expressed explicitly in terms of these two parameters using the formulae given in (

. The parameters
k_{1},\bar{k}_{1}
are free complex constants defined in the upper/lower half plane respectively, and
\gamma_{1},\bar{\gamma}_{1}

\pm 1

7.3
Space shifted reduction

While the previous two reductions allow for 1-soliton solutions for either choice of
\sigma
and complex eigenvalues, in the space shifted case we have the restrictions that

(181)

This soliton has four arbitrary real parameters

7.4
Conjugate space-time shifted reduction (mKdV)

While the previous three cases correspond to soliton solutions of shifted nonlocal NLS type equations, we now construct the 1-soliton for the conjugate space-time shifted mKdV equation (

Direct scattering – Discrete case

8.1
Linear Pair

We start by considering the Ablowiz-Ladik (AL) scattering problem

\displaystyle v_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}v_{n},\;\;\mathcal{N}=\begin{pmatrix}z&Q_{n}(t)\\
R_{n}(t)&z^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(183)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}v_{n},\;\;\mathcal{T}=\begin{pmatrix}iQ_{n}(t)R_{n-1}(t)-\frac{i}{2}(z-z^{-1})^{2}&-i(zQ_{n}(t)-z^{-1}Q_{n-1}(t))\\

(184)

where
v_{n}(t)\equiv(v_{n}^{(1)}(t),v_{n}^{(2)}(t))^{T}
is a complex valued function of

t\in\mathbb{R}

and we take

. Furthermore,
Q_{n}(t)
and
R_{n}(t)
are complex valued potentials that are assumed to rapidly decay to zero as
n\rightarrow\pm\infty.
Here,
z
is a spectral parameter taken to be (in general) complex. The discrete compatibility condition
\partial_{t}v_{n+1}=(\partial_{t}v_{m})_{m=n+1}
yields the system of equations (
49
) and (
50
).
Due to the spatially nonlocal nature of the symmetry reductions (
52
) and (
53
), it proves crucial for obtaining the symmetries in scattering space to supplement the AL system (
183
) with a “backward” scattering problem (obtained by
inverting Eq. (
183
)) defined by

w_{n-1}=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}w_{n},\;\;\tilde{\mathcal{N}}=\begin{pmatrix}z^{-1}&-Q_{n}(t)\\
-R_{n}(t)&z\end{pmatrix}.

(185)

It was shown in
[
20
]
that any solution
v_{n}(z,t)
of (

) can be related to a solution of (

) via the transformation

w_{n}(z,t)=f_{n}(t)v_{n+1}(z,t),\;\;\;f_{n}(t)\equiv\prod_{k=-\infty}^{n}\frac{1}{1-Q_{k}(t)R_{k}(t)}\;.

(186)

8.2
Eigenfunctions and scattering data

In this section, we provide the main ingredients necessary to solve the AL scattering problem for generic potentials.
Since the discrete potentials
Q_{n},R_{n}
vanish rapidly as
n\rightarrow\pm\infty
, we may define the following eigenfunctions by their boundary conditions
[
6
]
:

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(z,t)\sim z^{n}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}},\ \ \

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{n}(z,t)\sim z^{-n}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}},\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\rightarrow-\infty,

(187)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(z,t)\sim z^{-n}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}},\ \ \

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{n}(z,t)\sim z^{n}\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}},\;\,\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\rightarrow+\infty.

(188)

Clearly, the eigenfunction pairs
\{\phi_{n},\bar{\phi}_{n}\}
and
\{\psi_{n},\bar{\psi}_{n}\}
are two linearly independent sets of solutions, so we can write

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(z,t)\bar{\psi}_{n}(z,t)+b(z,t)\psi_{n}(z,t),

(189)

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{n}(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{a}(z,t)\psi_{n}(z,t)+\bar{b}(z,t)\bar{\psi}_{n}(z,t),

(190)

where
a(z,t),\bar{a}(z,t),b(z,t),\bar{b}(z,t)
are the scattering data given by the relations

\displaystyle a(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{n}(t)W(\phi_{n}(z,t),\;\psi_{n}(z,t)),

(191)

\displaystyle\bar{a}(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{n}(t)W(\bar{\psi}_{n}(z,t),\;\bar{\phi}_{n}(z,t)),

(192)

\displaystyle b(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{n}(t)W(\bar{\psi}_{n}(z,t),\;\phi_{n}(z,t)),

(193)

\displaystyle\bar{b}(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{n}(t)W(\bar{\phi}_{n}(z,t),\;\psi_{n}(z,t)).

(194)

Here,
W
is the Wronskian defined by

W(v_{n},w_{n})=v^{(1)}_{n}w^{(2)}_{n}-v^{(2)}_{n}w^{(1)}_{n}

where
v_{n}=(v^{(1)}_{n},\;v^{(2)}_{n})^{T}
and
w_{n}=(w^{(1)}_{n},\;w^{(2)}_{n})^{T}
and

c_{n}(t)=\prod_{k=n}^{+\infty}\left(1-Q_{k}(t)R_{k}(t)\right).

(195)

The above scattering data also satisfies the condition

a(z,t)\bar{a}(z,t)-b(z,t)\bar{b}(z,t)=c_{-\infty}.

(196)

As we shall see later, the scattering data
a,\bar{a}
and the product
b\bar{b}
turn out to be time-independent
(see
[
6
]
for further details). This fact implies that
c_{-\infty}
is time-independent as well, thus making it a constant of motion.
In the following analysis, it is convenient to consider functions with constant
boundary conditions. We define the bounded eigenfunctions as follows:

M_{n}(z,t)=z^{-n}\phi_{n}(z,t),\ \ \bar{M}_{n}(z,t)=z^{n}\bar{\phi}_{n}(z,t),\ \ N_{n}(z,t)=z^{n}\psi_{n}(z,t),\ \ \bar{N}_{n}(z,t)=z^{-n}\bar{\psi}_{n}(z,t).

(197)

Notably, it can be shown that
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
are analytic inside the unit circle
|z|=1
, while
\bar{M}_{n}
and
\bar{N}_{n}
are analytic outside the unit circle (see for example
[
6
]
). Additionally, it proves convenient to modify these eigenfunctions and introduce instead a new set of eigenfunctions

M_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)=\mathcal{A}M_{n}(z,t),\;\;N_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)=\mathcal{A}N_{n}(z,t),\;\;\bar{M}_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)=\mathcal{A}\bar{M}_{n}(z,t),\;\;\bar{N}_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)=\mathcal{A}\bar{N}_{n}(z,t),

(198)

where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the matrix

\mathcal{A}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&c_{n}(t)\end{pmatrix}.

(199)

We will use the set of eigenfunctions (
198
) in the formulation of the inverse problem later on. Similarly to the continuous case, one can write (
189
) and (
190
) as

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)+\rho(z,t)z^{-2n}N_{n}^{\prime}(z,t),

(200)

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{n}^{\prime}(z,t)+\bar{\rho}(z,t)z^{2n}\bar{N}_{n}^{\prime}(z,t),

(201)

where
\mu_{n}^{\prime}=M_{n}^{\prime}/a
,
\bar{\mu}_{n}^{\prime}=\bar{M}_{n}^{\prime}/\bar{a}
,
\rho=b/a
, and
\bar{\rho}=\bar{b}/\bar{a}
. Eventually, (
200
) and (
201
) will be viewed as a Riemann-Hilbert problem on the unit circle in the complex
z
-plane.

We now derive the connection between the shifted and unshifted scattering data alluded to in section 2.2. Denote by

Next, we derive the symmetry relations between the “bar” eigenfunctions. Following similar steps as before, we compare the boundary condition satisfied by the eigenfunction

10
Inverse scattering and time dependence – Discrete case

10.1
Inverse scattering: Riemann-Hilbert approach

The next step of the inverse scattering transform is to solve an inverse problem with the goal of constructing an explicit formula for the potentials
Q_{n}(t)
and
R_{n}(t)
using the known scattering data. This is accomplished
by reformulating the AL scattering problem as a Riemann-Hilbert problem.
The scattering problem (
183
) can possess discrete eigenvalues that occur at the zeros of
a(z)
and
\bar{a}(z)
, which will be denoted
\{z_{j}:|z_{j}|>1\}_{j=1}^{J}
and
\{\bar{z}_{j}:|\bar{z}_{j}|<1\}_{j=1}^{\bar{J}}
, respectively (we assume that these zeros are simple and outside and inside the unit circle respectively). Furthermore, it is useful to define norming constants,

C_{j}\equiv\frac{b_{j}}{a_{z}(z_{j})},\;\;\bar{C}_{j}\equiv\frac{\bar{b}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{z}(\bar{z}_{j})},

(256)

where
b_{j}
and
\bar{b}_{j}
are complex coefficients. The details of the Riemann-Hilbert problem for the AL coupled system are thoroughly discussed in
[
6
]
. Presently, we quote the main result of the inverse problem. That is, from (
200
) and (
201
) it can be derived that the modified eigenfunctions satisfy a system of algebraic-integral equations involving the discrete eigenvalues, norming constants, and reflection coefficients:

\displaystyle\bar{N}^{\prime}_{n}(\bar{z}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}_{1}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{J}C_{\ell}z_{\ell}^{-2n}\bigg[\frac{N_{n}^{\prime}(z_{\ell})}{\bar{z}_{j}-z_{\ell}}+\frac{N_{n}^{\prime}(-z_{\ell})}{\bar{z}_{j}+z_{\ell}}\bigg]-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\frac{\zeta^{-2n}\rho(\zeta)N^{\prime}_{n}(\zeta)}{\zeta-\bar{z}_{j}}d\zeta,

(257)

\displaystyle N^{\prime}_{n}(z_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{e}_{2}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\bar{J}}\bar{C}_{\ell}\bar{z}_{\ell}^{2n}\bigg[\frac{\bar{N}_{n}^{\prime}(\bar{z}_{\ell})}{z_{j}-\bar{z}_{\ell}}+\frac{\bar{N}_{n}^{\prime}(-\bar{z}_{\ell})}{z_{j}+\bar{z}_{\ell}}\bigg]+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\frac{\zeta^{2n}\bar{\rho}(\zeta)\bar{N}^{\prime}_{n}(\zeta)}{\zeta-z_{j}}d\zeta.

(258)

Once the eigenfunctions are determined from the above system, the potentials can be recovered using the following formulae derived using the asymptotic expansions of the modified eigenfunctions (see
[
6
]
for details):

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{J}C_{j}z_{j}^{-2(n+1)}N^{(2)^{\prime}}_{n}(z_{j})+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\zeta^{-2(n+1)}\rho(\zeta)N^{(2)^{\prime}}_{n}(\zeta)d\zeta,

(259)

\displaystyle Q_{n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{J}}\bar{C}_{j}\bar{z}_{j}^{2(n-1)}\bar{N}^{(1)^{\prime}}_{n}(\bar{z}_{j})+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\zeta^{2(n-1)}\bar{\rho}(\zeta)\bar{N}^{(1)^{\prime}}_{n}(\zeta)d\zeta.

(260)

10.2
Time evolution

In this section, we provide the time evolution of all scattering data and norming constants. Following similar lines of derivation as detailed in
[
6
]

one finds the time-dependence of
a
and
b
is given by

a_{z}(z,t)=0\;,\;\;\;b_{z}(z,t)=2i\omega b(z,t).

(261)

Similarly, the other set of scattering data satisfy

\bar{a}_{z}(z,t)=0\;,\;\;\;\bar{b}_{z}(z,t)=-2i\omega\bar{b}(z,t).

(262)

For time-local cases, the explicit time evolution of the scattering data is thus given by

\displaystyle a(z,t)=a(z,0),\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{a}(z,t)=a(z,0),

(263)

\displaystyle b(z,t)=e^{2i\omega t}b(z,0),\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{b}(z,t)=e^{-2i\omega t}\bar{b}(z,0).

(264)

For the time-shifted cases for which the initial scattering data is obtained at time
t=t_{0}/2
, the time evolution is given by

\displaystyle a(z,t)=a(z,t_{0}/2),\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{a}(z,t)=a(z,t_{0}/2)

(265)

\displaystyle b(z,t)=e^{2i\omega(t-t_{0}/2)}b(z,t_{0}/2),\;\;

\displaystyle\bar{b}(z,t)=e^{-2i\omega(t-t_{0}/2)}\bar{b}(z,t_{0}/2),

(266)

The evolution of the norming constants
C_{j}
and
\bar{C}_{j}
defined in equation (
256
)
is given for time-local cases by

C_{j}(t)=C_{j}(0)e^{2i\omega_{j}t},\;\;\;\bar{C}_{j}(t)=\bar{C}_{j}(0)e^{-2i\bar{\omega}_{j}t},

(267)

and for time-shifted cases by

C_{j}(t)=C_{j}(t_{0}/2)e^{2i\omega_{j}(t-t_{0}/2)},\;\;\;\bar{C}_{j}(t)=\bar{C}_{j}(t_{0}/2)e^{-2i\bar{\omega}_{j}(t-t_{0}/2)},

(268)

where

\omega_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(z_{j}-z_{j}^{-1})^{2},\;\;\;\bar{\omega}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{z}_{j}-\bar{z}_{j}^{-1})^{2}\;.

(269)

11
Trace formulae and symmetries for
b_{j},\bar{b}_{j}
– Discrete case

For the time-shift reduction case (as well as the classical Ablowitz-Ladik case) the symmetries connect the scattering data and norming
constants inside the unit circle to their corresponding quantities defined outside the unit circle. This implies that the eigenvalues

z_{j}
and norming constants
C_{j}
are counted as free parameters and the values of
\bar{z}_{j}
and
\bar{C}_{j}
are uniquely determined by
the underlying symmetries. However, in the cases involving spatial nonlocality, the symmetries of the scattering data and norming constants do
not
relate their respective values inside and outside of the unit circle. In order to understand the underlying symmetries of the norming constants

C_{j}=b_{j}/a_{z}(z_{j})
and
\bar{C}_{j}=\bar{b}_{j}/\bar{a}_{z}(\bar{z}_{j})
we need to consider their numerators and denominators separately. For the denominators we employ trace formulae which can be used to show that the data
a_{z}(z_{j}),\bar{a}_{z}(\bar{z}_{j})
depend on the eigenvalues
z_{j},\bar{z}_{j}.
For the numerators, it turns out that we can find symmetries relating
b_{j},\bar{b}_{j}
.

11.1
Trace formulae

11.1.1
Space-time shifted reduction

The derivation of the trace formulae follows an identical set of steps as shown in
[
20
]
. As such, the details are omitted. We
assume that
a(z)
and
\bar{a}(z)
have simple zeros
\{\pm z_{j}:|z_{j}|>1\}_{j=1}^{J}
and
\{\pm\bar{z}_{j}:|\bar{z}_{j}|<1\}_{j=1}^{J}
respectively.
The following trace formulae can be obtained:

\displaystyle\log a(z)

\displaystyle\underset{|z|>1}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\bigg(\frac{z^{2}-z_{j}^{2}}{z^{2}-\bar{z}_{j}^{2}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\frac{\zeta\log[c_{-\infty}+b(\zeta)\bar{b}(\zeta)]}{\zeta^{2}-z^{2}}d\zeta,

(270)

\displaystyle\log\bar{a}(z)

\displaystyle\underset{|z|<1}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\bigg(\frac{z^{2}-\bar{z}_{j}^{2}}{z^{2}-z_{j}^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\frac{\zeta\log[c_{-\infty}+b(\zeta)\bar{b}(\zeta)]}{\zeta^{2}-z^{2}}d\zeta.

(271)

We shall restrict the discussion to reflectionless potentials for which
b=\bar{b}=0.
In this case, the second term in (
270
) vanishes, and the second term in (
271
) becomes
\log c_{-\infty}
. If we assume we have only a single eigenvalue pair
z=\pm z_{1},\pm\bar{z}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, we find

(272)

11.1.2
Space shifted reduction

Again, the derivation follows the same set of steps as in the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric case in
[
20
]
, so the details are omitted. The difference from the space-time shifted case is the existence of more zeros of the scattering data

and
\bar{a}(z)

Denote by

, the (simple) zeros of

and

\displaystyle\log a(z)

\displaystyle\underset{|z|>1}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\bigg[\frac{(z^{2}-z_{j}^{2})(z^{2}-z_{j}^{*2})}{(z^{2}-\bar{z}_{j}^{2})(z^{2}-\bar{z}_{j}^{*2})}\bigg]-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\frac{\zeta\log[c_{-\infty}+b(\zeta)\bar{b}(\zeta)]}{\zeta^{2}-z^{2}}d\zeta,

(273)

\displaystyle\log\bar{a}(z)

\displaystyle\underset{|z|<1}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\bigg[\frac{(z^{2}-\bar{z}_{j}^{2})(z^{2}-\bar{z}_{j}^{*2})}{(z^{2}-z_{j}^{2})(z^{2}-z_{j}^{*2})}\bigg]+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta|=1}\frac{\zeta\log[c_{-\infty}+b(\zeta)\bar{b}(\zeta)]}{\zeta^{2}-z^{2}}d\zeta.

(274)

Again, we consider reflectionless potentials and assume a single complex eigenvalue quartet

, in which case we find

\displaystyle a_{z}(z_{1})=\frac{2z_{1}(z_{1}^{2}-z_{1}^{*2})}{(z_{1}^{2}-\bar{z}_{1}^{2})(z_{1}^{2}-\bar{z}_{1}^{*2})},\;\;\;a_{z}(z^{*}_{1})=\frac{2z^{*}_{1}(z_{1}^{*2}-z_{1}^{2})}{(z_{1}^{*2}-\bar{z}_{1}^{2})(z_{1}^{*2}-\bar{z}_{1}^{*2})},

(276)

Later, for simplicity, we will consider the case when

are

Space shifted reduction

Our starting point is

(286)

{N^{(2)^{\prime}*}_{n_{0}-n}}(z_{j}^{*})=b^{*}_{j}z_{j}^{-2(n+1)}N^{(1){{}^{\prime}}}_{n+1}(z_{j}).

Substituting Eq. (

This is only possible if

\displaystyle C_{1}(t)

1-soliton solutions – Discrete case

In this section we compute 1-soliton solutions for the time, space-time, and space shifted discrete NLS systems. Before considering the relevant nonlocal reductions, one can obtain the general 1-soliton for the coupled
(Q_{n},R_{n})
system by setting
J=\bar{J}=1

\displaystyle R_{n}(t)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{-2\bar{C}_{1}(t)\bar{z}_{1}^{2n}(z^{2}_{1}-\bar{z}^{2}_{1})^{2}}{(z^{2}_{1}-\bar{z}^{2}_{1})^{2}+4\bar{C}_{1}(t)C_{1}(t)\bar{z}_{1}^{2n+2}z_{1}^{-2n}}.

(296)

To determine the 1-soliton solutions for each specific reduction, we apply the relevant symmetries in scattering space to the general form (

12.1
Time shifted reduction

In this case, we have symmetries connecting the “inside” scattering data to the “outside” scattering data. Particularly, from (
221

\bar{z}_{1}=1/z_{1}
with
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}

\bar{C}_{1}(t)=-\sigma z_{1}^{-2}C_{1}(t_{0}-t)=-\sigma z_{1}^{-2}C_{1}(t_{0}/2)e^{i\omega_{1}t_{0}}e^{-2i\omega_{1}t}.

(297)

Substituting (

Q_{n}(t)=\frac{2\sigma C_{1}(t_{0}/2)e^{i\omega_{1}t_{0}}e^{-2i\omega_{1}t}{z}_{1}^{-2(n+1)}}{1-\sigma\frac{4C_{1}(t_{0}/2)^{2}}{(z_{1}^{2}-{z}_{1}^{-2})^{2}}z_{1}^{-4(n+1)}}.

This soliton solution has two arbitrary parameters

12.2
Space-time shifted reduction

Recall that unlike the time-shifted case, here the “inside” and “outside” scattering data are not connected. Instead,

and

(299)

where we have used the fact that

z_{1},\bar{z}_{1}

\pm 1

We remark that while no choice of parameters will cause the denominator to vanish for all time, this soliton can develop singularity in finite time.

12.3
Space shifted reduction

While the previous two reductions allow for 1-soliton solutions for either choice of
\sigma
and complex eigenvalues, in the space shifted case we have the restrictions that
\sigma=-1

This soliton has four arbitrary real parameters

13
Conclusion

In this work we have presented the inverse scattering transform for both continuous and discrete examples of nonlocal integrable systems possessing a shifted spatial and/or temporal argument. The spatial shifting parameter appears as an additional factor in the scattering data, while the primary role of the temporal shifting parameter is a restriction on the time at which the initial condition must be provided. We have shown the details of the IST for each case in an effort to illustrate the effect of the shifting parameters throughout. Additionally, this work serves the purpose of compiling the symmetries and soliton solutions for a wide variety of nonlocal integrable equations into a single reference.

Many directions for future exploration remain in the area of nonlocal integrable systems. The Davey-Stewartson equation
[
45
]
also has integrable space and/or time shifted variants, for example

\begin{cases}iq_{t}(x,y,t)+\frac{1}{2}[\gamma^{2}q_{xx}(x,y,t)+q_{yy}(x,y,t)]=[\varphi(x,y,t)-q(x,y,t)q(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)]q(x,y,t)\;,\\
\varphi_{xx}(x,y,t)-\gamma^{2}\varphi_{yy}(x,y,t)=2[q(x,y,t)q(x_{0}-x,y_{0}-y,t_{0}-t)]_{xx},\;\;\;\gamma^{2}=\pm 1\;.\end{cases}

(301)

The IST for such nonlocal (2+1)D systems has not yet been studied, and will be explored in a future work. Another interesting direction would be to consider NLS-type equations with multiple different types of shifted nonlocality, which can be obtained from matrix-valued versions of the integrable coupled NLS system. The simplest example arises from the 2-component vector version of the system (
1
)-(
2
):

\displaystyle iq^{(1)}_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{(1)}_{xx}(x,t)-2[q^{(1)}(x,t)r^{(1)}(x,t)+q^{(2)}(x,t)r^{(2)}(x,t)]q^{(1)}(x,t)

(302)

\displaystyle iq^{(2)}_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{(2)}_{xx}(x,t)-2[q^{(1)}(x,t)r^{(1)}(x,t)+q^{(2)}(x,t)r^{(2)}(x,t)]q^{(2)}(x,t)

\displaystyle-ir^{(1)}_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{(1)}_{xx}(x,t)-2[q^{(1)}(x,t)r^{(1)}(x,t)+q^{(2)}(x,t)r^{(2)}(x,t)]r^{(1)}(x,t)

\displaystyle-ir^{(2)}_{t}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{(2)}_{xx}(x,t)-2[q^{(1)}(x,t)r^{(1)}(x,t)+q^{(2)}(x,t)r^{(2)}(x,t)]r^{(2)}(x,t)

Following the technique of
[
46
]
used to obtain similar unshifted nonlocal equations, making the set of reductions
r^{(1)}(x,t)=q^{(1)}(x,t_{0}-t)
,
r^{(2)}(x,t)=q^{(2)}(x,t_{0}-t)
, and
q^{(2)}(x,t)=q^{(1)}(x_{0}-x,t)
, one obtains the following integrable evolution equation for the function
q^{(1)}(x,t)\equiv q(x,t)

involving terms with space, time, and space-time shifts:

iq_{t}(x,t)=q_{xx}(x,t)-2[q(x,t)q(x,t_{0}-t)+q(x_{0}-x,t)q(x_{0}-x,t_{0}-t)]q(x,t).

(303)

Understanding the scattering-space symmetries and soliton solutions of equations such as this could be a topic for further study.
