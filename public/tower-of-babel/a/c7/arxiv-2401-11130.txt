Identification and Estimation of Conditional Average Partial Causal Effects via Instrumental Variable
Yuta Kawakami, Manabu Kuroki, Jin Tian

Abstract
There has been considerable recent interest in estimating heterogeneous causal effects. In this paper, we study conditional average partial causal effects (CAPCE) to reveal the heterogeneity of causal effects with continuous treatment. We provide conditions for identifying CAPCE in an instrumental variable setting. Notably, CAPCE is identifiable under a weaker assumption than required by a commonly used measure for estimating heterogeneous causal effects of continuous treatment. We develop three families of CAPCE estimators: sieve, parametric, and reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)-based, and analyze their statistical properties. We illustrate the proposed CAPCE estimators on synthetic and real-world data.

Introduction

Instrumental variable (IV) analysis is a powerful tool used to elucidate causal relationships between treatment (
X
) and outcome (
Y
) when a controlled experiment is not feasible
[
Imbens 2014
,
Angrist and Krueger 2001
]
.
Traditionally, there are a large number of works focusing on binary or categorical treatment variables
[
Imbens and Angrist 1994
,
Balke and Pearl 1997
]
; recently, there has been a growing interest in continuous treatment variables
[
Hirano and Imbens 2005
,
Kennedy et al. 2017
,
Bahadori et al. 2022
]
.
There is also considerable recent interest in estimating heterogeneous causal effects across subsets of the population
[
Athey and Imbens 2016
,
Ding et al. 2016
,
Athey and Imbens 2019
,
Künzel et al. 2019
,
Wager and Athey 2018
,
Zhang et al. 2022
,
Singh et al. 2023
]
, including IV-based methods
[
Angrist 2004
,
Syrgkanis et al. 2019
,
Huntington-Klein 2020
,
Bargagli-Stoffi et al. 2022
]
.
Most of the works focus on
conditional average causal effect (CACE)

\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}-Y_{0}|{\bm{w}}]
, also known as conditional average treatment effect (CATE), for evaluating heterogeneous causal effects of a binary
X
, where
Y_{x}
denotes the potential outcome under treatment
X=x
, and
{\bm{W}}
are covariates (e.g. gender, age, and race).

In this work, we study estimating heterogeneous causal effects of a
continuous
treatment via the IV method. Existing work in this direction has focused on estimating
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
. The most widely used methods include parametric two-stage least squared (PTSLS)
[
Wright 1928
,
Angrist and Pischke 2009
,
Wooldridge 2010
]
, sieve nonparametric two-stage least squared (sieve NTSLS)
[
Newey and Powell 2003
,
Chen and Christensen 2018
]
, and Kernel IV
[
Singh et al. 2019
]
. The line of works in
[
Syrgkanis et al. 2019
,
Dikkala et al. 2020
,
Muandet et al. 2020
,
Bennett et al. 2023
]
focus on the efficiency of estimators assuming simple additive errors. All these methods rely on a
separability
assumption for identifying
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]

[
Newey and Powell 2003
]
.

Another quantity for evaluating the causal effects of a continuous treatment is average partial causal effect (APCE)
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}]

[
Chamberlain 1984
,
Wooldridge 2005
,
Graham and Powell 2012
]
.
Wong 2022
provided a condition for identifying
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}]
and
Kawakami et al. 2023
presented APCE estimators.

In this paper,
we consider
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
, termed
conditional average partial causal effect (CAPCE)
, to capture the heterogeneous causal effects of a continuous treatment. CAPCE extends APCE and is a natural generalization of the CACE of a binary treatment.
The quantity represented by CAPCE has been implicitly studied in the literature (e.g.
[
Galagate 2016
]
). Still existing works have focused on
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
.
One contribution of this work is to show that under the IV model, CAPCE is identifiable under a
weaker
separability assumption than required by the previous work (sieve NTSLS, PTSLS, Kernel IV) for identifying
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
. Thus, computing CAPCE allows scientists to estimate causal effects in a larger class of models.
Granted, given an estimated
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
, one can compute its derivative to obtain CAPCE, but not the other way around. However, in practice, the causal effect from a reference point (e.g., CACE) is often the main interest, and CAPCE is enough to compute causal effects from a reference point:
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{x^{\prime\prime}}-Y_{x^{\prime}}|{\bm{w}}]=\int_{x^{\prime}}^{x^{\prime\prime}}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dx
.

We then develop three families of methods for estimating CAPCE: sieve, parametric, and reproducing
kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)-based, and analyze their statistical properties. Finally, we illustrate the proposed estimators on synthetic data, showing superior performance to existing methods. We also evaluate CAPCE in a real-world dataset.

Related work.

Under the IV model,

Newey and Powell 2003
introduced sieve NTSLS for identifying and estimating

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z]=\int_{\Omega_{\bm{W}}}\int_{\Omega_{X}}\mathfrak{p}(X=x,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}|Z=z)\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dxd{\bm{w}}

\begin{gathered}f_{Y}(X,{\bm{W}},{\bm{H}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})=f_{Y}^{1}(X,{\bm{W}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})+f_{Y}^{2}({\bm{H}},{\bm{u}}_{Y}),\\
\mathbb{E}[f_{Y}^{2}({\bm{H}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})|{\bm{W}}]=0,\end{gathered}

(2)

which says the function

and one over

[
Angrist and Pischke 2009
,
Wooldridge 2010
]
and Kernel IV
[

]

Recently,
Wong 2022
introduced an integral equation for identifying APCE

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z]-\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z_{0}]=-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z)-\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z_{0})\}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}]dx

Kawakami et al. 2023

3
Identification of CAPCE

First, we formally define
conditional average partial causal effect (CAPCE)
to capture the heterogeneous causal effects of a continuous treatment.
Then we present a theorem for identifying CAPCE under the IV model.

Definition 1

(CAPCE)
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{U}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Y_{x}({\bm{U}})\Big|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}\right]
.

CAPCE is a real-valued function from
x\in\Omega_{X}
and
{\bm{w}}\in\Omega_{\bm{W}}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
It is a generalization of CACE for continuous treatment. It is also a generalization of APCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}]

to represent heterogeneous causal effects.
Next, we present conditions for identifying CAPCE under the IV model.

Assumption 3.1
.

Under the SCM
{\cal M}_{IV}
, given
{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}
,

1.

Instrument relevance: IV
Z
has a causal effect on
X
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[X_{z}]
is not a constant function of
z
.

2.

Y_{x}
is differentiable and bounded in
x\in\Omega_{X}
.

3.

\displaystyle\sup_{x,z,{\bm{w}}}\mathfrak{p}(X_{z}=x|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})<\infty
.

4.

The set of distributions
\mathbb{P}(X|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})
induced by varying
z
is a complete set.

The first assumption is standard for the IV setting.
The second assumption means that there exists CAPCE for all subjects for
x\in\Omega_{X}
and
{\bm{w}}\in\Omega_{\bm{W}}
.
The third assumption means the density function of
X_{z,{\bm{w}}}
is bounded.
The fourth assumption implies that

is a zero function if
\mathbb{E}[h(X)|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]
does not depend on

for all
{\bm{w}}\in\Omega_{\bm{W}}
, which is also assumed in
[
Newey and Powell 2003
]
for identifying
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
.

Assumption 3.2

(Separability on
X
)
.

f_{Y}(X,{\bm{W}},{\bm{H}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})
is in the form of a summation of two functions over
X
and
{\bm{H}}
separately, i.e.,

f_{Y}(X,{\bm{W}},{\bm{H}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})=f_{Y}^{1}(X,{\bm{W}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})+f_{Y}^{2}({\bm{W}},{\bm{H}},{\bm{u}}_{Y})

We obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.1

(Identification of CAPCE)
.

Under SCM
{\cal M}_{IV}
and Assumptions

, CAPCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
is identifiable from distributions

\displaystyle\mu(z)=\int_{\Omega_{\bm{W}}}\int_{\Omega_{X}}k(z,x,{\bm{w}})\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dxd{\bm{w}},

(3)

where
\mu(z)=\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z_{0}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z],k(z,x,{\bm{w}})=\mathfrak{p}(X\leq x,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}|Z=z)-\mathfrak{p}(X\leq x,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}|Z=z_{0})

Remark:
Assumption

is weaker than the assumption (
2

[
Newey and Powell 2003
]

[

]

[

Sieve estimators are a class of non-parametric estimators that use progressively more complex models to estimate an unknown function as more data becomes available

\hat{\bf M}^{-}
denotes the generalized inverse that satisfies
\hat{\bf M}\hat{\bf M}^{-}\hat{\bf M}=\hat{\bf M}
.
Let
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
and
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})=(z_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,z_{N_{1}}^{(1)},z_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,z_{N_{2}}^{(2)})
. We will compute predicted values in (

Stage 2.

Estimate parameters
{\bm{\beta}}
based on the linear equation
c={\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{d}}
.
Let
\hat{\bm{c}}=(\hat{c}_{1},\ldots,\hat{c}_{N})^{T}
,

,

\hat{\bf D}=(\hat{\bm{d}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{d}}_{N})^{T}
, and
the empirical risk be

\displaystyle{Q}_{3}({\bm{\beta}}\ ;{\cal D}^{(1)},{\cal D}^{(2)})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{c}_{i}-\hat{\bm{d}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{\beta}})^{2}.

(11)

Under Assumption
4.3
, our estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
is given by the optimization problem below:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\beta}}{Q}_{3}({\bm{\beta}}\ ;{\cal D}^{(1)},{\cal D}^{(2)})\text{ subject to }{\bm{\beta}}^{T}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{\beta}}\leq B_{S},

(12)

where

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\Lambda}_{i,j}=\sum_{|\lambda|\leq l}\int\left\{D^{\lambda}{\varphi}_{i}(x,{\bm{w}})-D^{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[{\varphi}_{i}(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z_{0}]\right\}

(13)

\displaystyle\times\left\{D^{\lambda}{\varphi}_{j}(x,{\bm{w}})-D^{\lambda}\mathbb{E}[{\varphi}_{j}(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z_{0}]\right\}

\displaystyle\times\{1+\|(x,{\bm{w}}^{T})\|^{2}\}^{\kappa}dxd{\bm{w}}

for
i,j=1,\ldots,J
, and
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\{{\Lambda}_{i,j}\}_{i,j=1}^{J}
.

{\bm{\Lambda}}

]

(14)

where
\zeta_{S}

Then, our proposed sieve CAPCE estimator is given by

4.2
Parametric CAPCE estimator

Next, we develop a parametric CAPCE (P-CAPCE) estimator.
We consider the setting that the CAPCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
takes the form of the following parametric model:

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\theta_{k}(x,{\bm{w}}),

(15)

where
\displaystyle\{\theta_{k}(x,{\bm{w}})\}_{k=1}^{K}
are a set of known functions, and
{\bm{\gamma}}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K})^{T}
are unknown model parameters to be estimated from data.

The derivation of the P-CAPCE estimator is very similar to that of the sieve CAPCE estimator, so we skip the details in the following. Denote the anti-derivatives
\displaystyle\vartheta_{k}(x,{\bm{w}})=\int\theta_{k}(x,{\bm{w}})dx
for
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Let

{c}=\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z]-\mathbb{E}[Y|z=z_{0}]
,
and
{\bm{e}}=({e}^{1},\ldots,{e}^{K})^{T}
where

{e}^{k}=\mathbb{E}[\vartheta_{k}(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z]-\mathbb{E}[\vartheta_{k}(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z_{0}]
. Then, equation (
3
) reduces to a linear equation
c={\bm{\gamma}}^{T}{\bm{e}}
.

P-CAPCE estimator.
Given datasets
{\cal D}^{(1)}=\{x^{(1)}_{i},{\bm{w}}^{(1)}_{i},z^{(1)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}}
and
{\cal D}^{(2)}=\{y^{(2)}_{i},z^{(2)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{2}}
, our P-CAPCE estimator consists of two stages.

Stage 1.
Let basis functions be
{\bm{q}}(z)=(q_{1}(z),q_{2}(z),\ldots,q_{P}(z))^{T}
. Denote
\hat{c}_{i}=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z=z_{i}]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z=z_{0}]
and
\hat{e}_{i}^{k}=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\vartheta_{k}(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z_{i}]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\vartheta_{k}(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z_{0}]
.
Let variance-covariance matrices
\hat{\bf M}^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}N_{1}^{-1}{\bm{q}}(z^{(1)}_{i}){\bm{q}}(z^{(1)}_{i})^{T}
and
\hat{\bf M}^{(2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}N_{2}^{-1}{\bm{q}}(z^{(2)}_{i}){\bm{q}}(z^{(2)}_{i})^{T}
.
We obtain the following predication values

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\hat{c}_{i}=({\bm{q}}(z_{i})-{\bm{q}}(z_{0}))^{T}\hat{\bf M}^{(2)-}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{2}}\frac{1}{N_{2}}{\bm{q}}(z^{(2)}_{l})y^{(2)}_{l}\\
\hat{e}_{i}^{k}=({\bm{q}}(z_{i})-{\bm{q}}(z_{0}))^{T}\hat{\bf M}^{(1)-}\\
\hskip 56.9055pt\times\sum_{l=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{1}{N_{1}}{\bm{q}}(z^{(1)}_{l})\vartheta_{k}(x^{(1)}_{l},{\bm{w}}^{(1)}_{l})\\
\end{array}\right.

(16)

for
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Let
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
and
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N})=(z_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,z_{N_{1}}^{(1)},z_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,z_{N_{2}}^{(2)})
. We will compute predicted values in (
16
) for all
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Stage 2.

Estimate parameters
{\bm{\gamma}}
based on the linear equation
c={\bm{\gamma}}^{T}{\bm{e}}
.
Let
\hat{\bm{c}}=(\hat{c}_{1},\ldots,\hat{c}_{N})^{T}
,
\hat{\bm{e}}_{i}=(\hat{e}^{1}_{i},\ldots,\hat{e}^{K}_{i})^{T}
,

\hat{\bf E}=(\hat{\bm{e}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{e}}_{N})^{T}
, and
the empirical risk be

\displaystyle{Q}_{4}({\bm{\gamma}}\ ;{\cal D}^{(1)},{\cal D}^{(2)})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}(\hat{c}_{i}-\hat{\bm{e}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{\gamma}})^{2}.

(17)

We make the following assumption:

Assumption 4.4
.

Given a positive regularization parameter
B_{P}
,
{\bm{\gamma}}
satisfies
{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}{\bm{\gamma}}\leq B_{P}
.

Under Assumption
4.4
, our estimator
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
is given by the optimization problem below:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\gamma}}{Q_{4}}({\bm{\gamma}}\ ;{\cal D}^{(1)},{\cal D}^{(2)})\text{ subject to }{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}{\bm{\gamma}}\leq B_{P}.

(18)

This problem can be solved by the ridge regression method with the following solution
[
Hilt et al.
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\gamma}}=(\hat{\bf E}^{T}\hat{\bf E}+\zeta_{P}{\bf I}_{K})^{-1}\hat{\bf E}^{T}\hat{\bm{c}},

(19)

where
\zeta_{P}
is a regularization parameter, and
{\bf I}_{K}
is a
K\times K
identity matrix. Then, our proposed P-CAPCE estimator is given by
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\partial_{x}{Y}_{x}|{\bm{w}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\gamma}_{k}\theta_{k}(x,{\bm{w}})
.

Model Selection.
We presume the models in Stage 1 have been selected appropriately. We can use the empirical risk in equation (
17
) as a performance metric of the trained model in Stage 2 with parameters
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
if given separate test datasets
{\cal D}^{(1)^{\prime}}=\{x^{(1)^{\prime}}_{i},z^{(1)^{\prime}}_{i},{\bm{w}}^{(1)^{\prime}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}^{\prime}}
and
{\cal D}^{(2)^{\prime}}=\{y^{(2)^{\prime}}_{i},z^{(2)^{\prime}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{2}^{\prime}}
.
Let
N^{\prime}=N_{1}^{\prime}+N_{2}^{\prime}
. Assume
\hat{c}^{\prime}_{i}
and
\hat{\bm{e}}^{\prime}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
are computed using
{\cal D}^{(1)^{\prime}}
and
{\cal D}^{(2)^{\prime}}
.
Then, we can evaluate the trained model by the test error
\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{4}(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\ ;{\cal D}^{(1)^{\prime}},{\cal D}^{(2)^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}}(\hat{c}^{\prime}_{i}-\hat{\bm{e}}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}T}\hat{\bm{\gamma}})^{2}
.
Given a separate dataset, this performance metric can be used for model selection from various candidate basis functions or the number
K
or
P
of basis terms.

4.3
RKHS CAPCE estimator

Finally, we develop a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) CAPCE estimator.
RKHS models are popular and widely used in nonparametric regression
[
Theodoridis and Koutroumbas 2006
,
Schölkopf et al. 2013
]
.

RKHS model.
Let
k_{X,{\bm{W}}}:\Omega_{X,{\bm{W}}}\times\Omega_{X,{\bm{W}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
k_{Z}:\Omega_{Z}\times\Omega_{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be measurable positive definitive kernels corresponding to RKHSs
{\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}}
and
{\cal H}_{Z}
.
A symmetric function
k:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called positive-definite kernel if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}c_{j}k({\bm{a}}_{i},{\bm{a}}_{j})\geq 0

for all
{\bm{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{a}}_{n}\in\Omega
given any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

[
Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini 2004
]
.
Denote the feature map
\eta:\Omega_{X,{\bm{W}}}\rightarrow{\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}}
,
(x,{\bm{w}})\mapsto k^{\prime}_{X,{\bm{W}}}(x,{\bm{w}},\cdot,\cdot)
and
\psi:\Omega_{Z}\rightarrow{\cal H}_{Z}
,
z\mapsto k_{Z}(z,\cdot).

In addition, we denote the antiderivative feature function
\pi:\Omega_{X,{\bm{W}}}\rightarrow{\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}},(x,{\bm{w}})\mapsto k_{X,{\bm{W}}}(x,{\bm{w}},\cdot,\cdot)
with
\displaystyle\pi(x,{\bm{w}})=-\int\eta(x,{\bm{w}})dx
and the antiderivative kernel function
\displaystyle k_{X,{\bm{W}}}(x,{\bm{w}},x^{\prime},{\bm{w}}^{\prime})=\int k^{\prime}_{X,{\bm{W}}}(x,{\bm{w}},x^{\prime},{\bm{w}}^{\prime})dxdx^{\prime}
.
Assume that the CAPCE takes the form

\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]=H(\pi(x,{\bm{w}}))

(20)

for some operator
H\in{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}},\Omega_{Y})
, where
{\cal L}_{2}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})
is the
{\cal L}_{2}
measurable function space from
\Omega_{1}
to
\Omega_{2}
, and
H(\pi(x,{\bm{w}}))
is a composition function
H\circ\pi:\Omega_{X,{\bm{W}}}\rightarrow\Omega_{Y}
.
Our RKHS CAPCE estimator consists of two stages (a detailed derivation is provided in Appendix
A.2
).

Stage 1.
We learn an operator
G_{1}\in{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{Z},{\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}})

that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\pi(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z]=G_{1}(\psi(z))
,
and learn an operator
G_{2}\in{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{Z},\Omega_{Y})
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z]=G_{2}(\psi(z))
.

Stage 2.

We learn an operator
H\in{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}},\Omega_{Y})
that satisfies
\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z=z]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y|Z=z_{0}]=H(\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\pi(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\pi(X,{\bm{W}})|Z=z_{0}])\Leftrightarrow\hat{G}_{1}(\psi(z)-\psi(z_{0}))=H(\hat{G}_{2}(\psi(z)-\psi(z_{0})))
, where
\hat{G}_{1}
and
\hat{G}_{2}
are learned in Stage 1.

We learn
\hat{G}_{1}
,
\hat{G}_{2}
, and
\hat{H}
by the following optimization problems using datasets
{\cal D}^{(1)}
and
{\cal D}^{(2)}
:

\displaystyle\min_{G_{1}}\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\left\|\pi(x_{i}^{(1)},{\bm{w}}_{i}^{(1)})-G_{1}(\psi(z_{i}^{(1)}))\right\|^{2}_{{\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}}}

(21)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\left\|G_{1}\right\|^{2}_{{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{Z},{\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}})},

\displaystyle\min_{G_{2}}\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}\left\|y_{i}^{(2)}-G_{2}(\psi(z_{i}^{(2)}))\right\|^{2}

(22)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{2}\left\|G_{2}\right\|^{2}_{{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{Z},\Omega_{Y})},

\displaystyle\min_{H}\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}\Big\|\hat{G}_{2}(\psi(z_{i}^{(2)})-\psi(z_{0}))

(23)

\displaystyle-H(\hat{G}_{1}(\psi(z_{i}^{(2)})-\psi(z_{0})))\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\xi\left\|H\right\|^{2}_{{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{X,{\bm{W}}},\Omega_{Y})}+\lambda_{3}\left\|H\circ\hat{G}_{1}\right\|^{2}_{{\cal L}_{2}({\cal H}_{Z},\Omega_{Y})},

where
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\xi)
are regularization parameters. From the representer theorem
[
Schölkopf et al. 2001
]
, the optimal
G_{1}
exists in
\text{span}\{\psi(z_{1}^{(1)}),\ldots,\psi(z_{N_{1}}^{(1)})\}
, and the optimal
G_{2}
and
H
exist in
\text{span}\{\psi(z_{1}^{(2)}),\ldots,\psi(z_{N_{2}}^{(2)})\}
.

We denote gram matrices
{\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(1)}}=\{k_{Z}(z_{i}^{(1)},z_{j}^{(1)})\}_{i,j=1}^{N_{1}}
;

{\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}z_{0}}
is
N_{1}\times N_{1}
matrix
\{k_{Z}(z_{i}^{(1)},z_{0})\}_{i,j=1}^{N_{1}}
; and
{\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}(x,{\bm{w}})}
is
N_{1}
-dimension vector
\{k_{X,{\bm{W}}}(x_{i}^{(1)},{\bm{w}}_{i}^{(1)},x,{\bm{w}})\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}}
.
Then, the RKHS CAPCE estimator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\partial_{x}{Y}_{x}|{\bm{w}}]=\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{T}{\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}(x,{\bm{w}})},

(24)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\alpha}}=(\hat{\bf O}\hat{\bf O}^{T}+N_{2}\xi{\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}}+N_{2}\lambda_{3}{\bf I}_{N_{2}})^{-1}

(25)

\displaystyle\times\hat{\bf O}\{{\bm{y}}^{(2)T}({\bf K}_{Z^{(2)}Z^{(2)}}+N_{2}\lambda_{2}{\bf I}_{N_{2}})^{-1}

\displaystyle\times({\bf K}_{Z^{(2)}Z^{(2)}}-{\bf K}_{Z^{(2)}z_{0}})\},

\displaystyle\hat{\bf O}={\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}}({\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(1)}}+N_{1}\lambda_{1}{\bf I}_{N_{1}})^{-1}

(26)

\displaystyle\times({\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(2)}}-{\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}z_{0}}),

and
{\bf I}_{N}
is a
N\times N
identity matrix.

Model Selection.

We presume the models in Stage 1 have been selected appropriately, and introduce a model selection method in Stage 2 following
[
Singh et al. 2019
]
.
Assume we have separate datasets
{\cal D}^{(1)^{\prime}}=\{x^{(1)^{\prime}}_{i},{\bm{w}}^{(1)^{\prime}}_{i},z^{(1)^{\prime}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}_{1}}
and
{\cal D}^{(2)}=\{y^{(2)^{\prime}}_{i},z^{(2)^{\prime}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}_{2}}
.
We determine the optimal
\lambda_{1}^{*}
by minimizing

\displaystyle L_{1}(\lambda_{1})=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}_{1}}\text{Trace}\Big[{\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)^{\prime}}(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)^{\prime}}}

(27)

\displaystyle-2{\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)^{\prime}}(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}}{\bf P}_{1}+{\bf P}_{1}^{T}{\bf K}_{(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}(X,{\bm{W}})^{(1)}}{\bf P}_{1}\Big],

where
{\bf P}_{1}=({\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(1)}}+N^{\prime}_{1}\lambda_{1}{\bf I}_{N_{1}})^{-1}{\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(2)}}
. We determine the optimal
\lambda_{2}^{*}
by minimizing

\displaystyle L_{2}(\lambda_{2})=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}_{2}}\text{Trace}\Big[{\bm{y}}^{(2)^{\prime}}{\bm{y}}^{(2)^{\prime}T}

(28)

\displaystyle-2{\bm{y}}^{(2)^{\prime}}{\bm{y}}^{(2)T}{\bf P}_{2}+{\bf P}_{2}^{T}{\bm{y}}^{(2)}{\bm{y}}^{(2)T}{\bf P}_{2}\Big],

where
{\bf P}_{2}=({\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(1)}}+N_{1}\lambda_{2}{\bf I}_{N_{1}})^{-1}{\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(2)}}
.
Finally, we determine the optimal
\xi^{*}
and
\lambda_{3}^{*}
by minimizing test error

\displaystyle L(\lambda_{3},\xi)=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\prime}_{2}}\|{\bm{y}}^{(2)^{\prime}T}({\bf K}_{Z^{(2)^{\prime}}Z^{(2)^{\prime}}}+N^{\prime}_{2}\lambda_{2}^{*}{\bf I}_{N^{\prime}_{2}})^{-1}({\bf K}_{Z^{(2)^{\prime}}Z^{(2)^{\prime}}}-{\bf K}_{Z^{(2)^{\prime}}z_{0}})-\hat{H}_{\lambda_{3},\xi}(x_{i}^{(1)^{\prime}},{\bm{w}}_{i}^{(1)^{\prime}})\|^{2}

where
\hat{H}_{\lambda_{3},\xi}
is learned with
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}^{*}
and
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{2}^{*}
using
{\cal D}^{(1)}
and
{\cal D}^{(2)}
.

Application in a Real-World Dataset

In this section, we present an application of our CAPCE estimators to a real-world dataset in economics.

Real-world Dataset.

We take up an open dataset “the National Longitudinal Survey of Young Men” in the R package “wooldridge" (
https://cran.r-project.org/package=wooldridge
), which has been analyzed by many works, e.g., in
[
Griliches 1977
,
Blackburn and Neumark 1992
]
.
The sample size is 935 with 857 left after excluding missing values. We evaluate the heterogeneity of the effect of years of education on monthly wages, which is of great interest in economics
[
Angrist and Krueger 1991
,
Card 1999
]
. We followed
Blackburn and Neumark 1992
to use mother’s education as an instrument to uncover the
effect of education on wages. The use of mother’s education as an instrument in this dataset has been subjected to debate in the literature (e.g.,
[
Card 1999
,
Kling 2001
,
Wooldridge 2010
]
).
We take the subject’s years of education as the treatment variable (
X
), their monthly wage as the outcome (
Y
), their mother’s years of education as the IV (
Z
), and their IQ as a covariate (
W
).
The domains of
X
and
Z
are
[9,18]
, ranging from the 1st year of high school to the 2nd year of a master’s degree.
The domain of
W
is
[50,145]
.

Settings.

We applied P-CAPCE and PTSLS. Other estimators are not used due to the small sample size. We use terms
\{1,W,W^{2},X,XW,XW^{2}\}
for P-CAPCE and
\{1,W,W^{2},X,XW,XW^{2},X^{2},X^{2}W,X^{2}W^{2}\}
for PTSLS. Detailed settings are in Appendix
G
.

Results.

The estimated CAPCE values are shown in Appendix
G
. For subjects with IQ 100, the estimated CAPCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|W=100]
of years of education (
X
) on wages (
Y
) is given by
94.905-5.618x
by P-CAPCE and
108.491-5.882x
by PTSLS. Both predict that years of education increase wages, which is consistent with previous works
[
Blackburn and Neumark 1992
,
Wooldridge 2010
]
.
The results also show that education significantly affects wages at the compulsory school level, but the effect gets weaker with more years of education, consistent with the results in
[
Angrist and Krueger 1991
,
Caplan 2018
]
. On the other hand, for subjects with IQ 80, the estimated CAPCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|W=80]
is
60.740-3.598x
by P-CAPCE and
69.465-3.057x
by PTSLS. For subjects with IQ 120, the estimated CAPCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|W=120]
is
136.662-8.086x
by P-CAPCE and
156.181-9.531x
by PTSLS.

While we estimate the heterogeneity of causal effects of education on wages across subjects with different IQs, existing works
[
Blackburn and Neumark 1992
,
Card 1999
,
Kling 2001
,
Wooldridge 2010
,
Kawakami et al. 2023
]
using this dataset have focused on the effects of education on wages over the whole population.
Card 1999
and
Wooldridge 2010
provided a summary of the early works on IV estimates and showed that the estimates of all studies were positive implying education increases wages. On the other hand, our results give two new insights into the effects of education on wages. First, our results suggest that for each sub-population
\text{IQ}=80,100,120
, education significantly affects wages at the compulsory school level; but has little effect at the college level. This result is consistent with the result of APCE estimates for the whole population given in
[
Kawakami et al. 2023
]
. Second, we reveal that the effect of education on wages is more significant for high IQ students, especially at the compulsory school level. To the best of our knowledge, this result has not been revealed in previous studies of this dataset, but it is consistent with the panel data analysis result in
[
Altonji and Dunn 1996
]
.

7
Conclusion

We study conditional average partial causal effect (CAPCE) to represent the heterogeneous causal effects of a continuous treatment. We present a method for identifying CAPCE in the IV model.
Notably, CAPCE
\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
is identifiable under a weaker assumption than required by
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
, showing the merit of studying CAPCE instead of
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]
, which has been the focus of existing work. We develop three families of CAPCE estimators: sieve, parametric, and RKHS, and
analyze their statistical properties.
We empirically demonstrate that the proposed CAPCE estimators are superior to the existing widely used IV methods PTSLS
[
Angrist and Pischke 2009
,
Wooldridge 2010
]
, sieve NTSLS
[
Newey and Powell 2003
]
, and Kernel IV
[
Singh et al. 2019
]
in settings where the standard separability assumption (
2
) is violated.
The work provides scientists with a new tool for analyzing the heterogeneous causal effects of a continuous treatment.
The results can be extended to an IV model with an additional edge
{\bm{W}}\rightarrow Z
. An identification theorem similar to Theorem
3.1
can be derived, which uses
\mathbb{P}(Y|Z,{\bm{W}})
as input instead of
\mathbb{P}(Y|Z)
. We present this result in Appendix
A.2
.

\displaystyle\mu(z)=\int_{\Omega_{\bm{W}}}\int_{\Omega_{X}}k(z,x,{\bm{w}})\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dxd{\bm{w}},

(30)

where

Proof.

First, we show the following integral equation holds under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
following the idea in
[
Wong 2022

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z_{0},{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]

(31)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})-\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z_{0},{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})\}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dx.

From the setting of the IV, the following integral equation holds:

\displaystyle Y_{X_{z}}=\int_{\Omega_{X}}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z}=x}Y_{x}dx,

(32)

given
{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}
for each subject, where
\mathbbm{1}_{\cdot}
is a delta function or indicator function.
This equation means
X_{z}=x\Rightarrow Y_{X_{z}}=Y_{x}
from the definition of delta function.
By substituting the integral equations
\displaystyle Y_{X_{z}}=\int_{\Omega_{X}}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z}=x}Y_{x}dx
and
\displaystyle Y_{X_{z_{0}}}=\int_{\Omega_{X}}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z_{0}}=x}Y_{x}dx

\displaystyle Y_{X_{z}}-Y_{X_{z_{0}}}=\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z}=x}-\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z_{0}}=x}\}Y_{x}dx

(33)

holds. Since the Heaviside step function is the integration of the delta function,

\displaystyle Y_{X_{z}}-Y_{X_{z_{0}}}=\left[\{\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z}=x}-\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z_{0}}=x}\}\partial_{x}Y_{x}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z}\leq x}-\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z_{0}}\leq x}\}\partial_{x}Y_{x}dx.

(34)

Because

\displaystyle\left[\{\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z}=x}-\mathbbm{1}_{X_{z_{0}}=x}\}\partial_{x}Y_{x}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=0

Then, the integral equation becomes

\displaystyle Y_{X_{z}}-Y_{X_{z_{0}}}=-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z}\leq x}-\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z_{0}}\leq x}\}\partial_{x}Y_{x}dx.

(35)

From the separability with covariate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{X_{z}}|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{X_{z_{0}}}|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]

(36)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\mathbb{E}[\{\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z}\leq x}-\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z_{0}}\leq x}\}\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]dx

(37)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z}\leq x}|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{I}_{X_{z_{0}}\leq x}|{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]\}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dx.

(38)

Then, the integral equation becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z_{0},{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]

(39)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{X}}\{\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})-\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z_{0},{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})\}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]dx.

Next, the integral equation can be given by multiplying
\mathfrak{p}({\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}|Z=z)
and marginalizing for
{\bm{W}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{W}}[\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}]]=

(41)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{X}}\int_{\Omega_{\bm{W}}}\mathbb{P}(X\leq x|Z=z,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}})\mathfrak{p}({\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}|Z=z)\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]d{\bm{w}}dx

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z]=\int_{\Omega_{X}}\int_{\Omega_{\bm{W}}}\mathfrak{p}(X\leq x,{\bm{W}}={\bm{w}}|Z=z)\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x}Y_{x}|{\bm{w}}]d{\bm{w}}dx\

(42)

Since

is a nontrivial function, there does not exist a function which satisfies

Taking a contraposition, there does not exist a function which satisfies

for any
z\in\Omega_{Z}
and
{\bm{w}}\in\Omega_{\bm{W}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\{{\bm{y}}^{(2)T}({\bf K}_{Z^{(2)}Z^{(2)}}+N_{2}\lambda_{2}{\bf I}_{N_{2}})^{-1}({\bf K}_{Z^{(2)}Z^{(2)}}-{\bf K}_{Z^{(2)}z_{0}})\}

(45)

(46)

using

\displaystyle\hat{G_{1}}(\cdot)=\left<{\pi_{X^{(1)},{\bm{W}}^{(1)}}}({\bf K}_{Z^{(1)}Z^{(1)}}+N_{1}\lambda_{1}{\bf I})^{-1}\psi_{Z}^{(1)T},\cdot\right>

(51)

We present detailed settings of numerical experiments in the following.

Setting of P-CAPCE’.
We learn the conditional expectations of basis functions

\mathbb{E}[Y|Z=z,W=w]
,
\mathbb{E}[X|Z=z,W=w]
,
\mathbb{E}[WX|Z=z,W=w]
and
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}|Z=z,W=w]

by the nonlinear model,

b_{0}+b_{1}Z+b_{2}Z^{2}
.
We used the basis terms
\{1,X,X^{2}\}
for P-CAPCE’ and
\{1,X\}
for PTSLS, which match setting (A), and let
z_{0}=-1
and
w=1
. Regularize value is determined by test error from
\{1,10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3}\}
.

Setting of S-CAPCE’.
We learn the conditional expectations by the nonlinear model,

b_{0}+b_{1}Z+b_{2}Z^{2}+b_{3}Z^{3}+b_{4}W+b_{5}W^{2}+b_{6}W^{3}
,
We consider the basis terms
h_{p}(X)
for
p=0,1,2
, where
h_{p}
is Hermite polynomial functions (
h_{0}(t)=1
,
h_{1}(t)=t
,
h_{2}(t)=t^{2}-1
and
h_{3}(t)=t^{3}-3t
), and let
z_{0}=-1
and
w=1
.
Let
\kappa=2
and
l=1
, and we calculate
\hat{\Lambda}
by Monte Carlo integration using uniform distribution
x\sim U(-4,4)
, where
\Omega_{X}\subseteq[-4,4]
. Regularize value is determined by test error from
\{1,10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3}\}
.
We estimate CAPCE via differentiating estimated
\mathbb{E}[Y_{x}|{W}={w}]
.

Setting of RKHS CAPCE’ estimator.
We use polynomial kernel function
k_{Z,{W}}((z,{w})(z,{w})^{T}+C_{1})^{C_{2}}
and
k_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=(xx^{\prime}+C_{3})^{C_{4}}
.
We select the kernel parameters
(C_{1},C_{2})
and
(C_{3},C_{4})
