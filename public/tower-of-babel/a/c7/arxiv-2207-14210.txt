On the largest sum-free subset problem in the integers Thanks: The author thanks Ben Green for useful discussions.
George Shakan

Abstract
. Let A ⊂ ℤ > 0 A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0} of size n n . It is conjectured that for any C > 0 C>0 and n n large enough that A A contains a sum-free subset of size at least n / 3 + C n/3+C . We study this problem and find an alternate proof of Bourgain’s result that one make take C = 2 / 3 C=2/3 .



1.
Introduction

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a set of size
n
. We say
A
is
sum-free
if there are no solutions to

x+y=z,

with
x,y,z\in A
. Let
s(A)
be the largest sum-free subset of
A
. Erdős
[
Er85
]
observed that
s(A)\geq n/3
, Alon and Kleitman
[
AK90
]
remarked that his argument proves
s(A)\geq(n+1)/3
. Bourgain later proved the following.

Theorem 1

(
[
B97
]
)

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a set with co-prime elements of size
n
. Then either
A=\{1,2\}
or

s(A)\geq(n+2)/3.

The main question in the area is the following.

Conjecture 1

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
of size
n
and
S
be a positive integer. Then there is a
N_{S}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

s(A)\geq(n+S)/3,

for all
n\geq N_{S}
.

Bourgain
[
B97
]
also showed that there is a
C>0
such that

s(A)\geq n/3+C||\sum_{a\in A}\cos(2\pi a\cdot)||_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})},

(1)

answering Conjecture
1
for a “typical” set
A
. In light of Bourgain’s proof of Theorem
1
, we let

f(x)=1_{[1/3,2/3)}-1/3,

and

f_{A}(x)=\sum_{a\in A}f(ax).

We also set

m_{A}:=\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f_{A}(x).

We have the following stronger conjecture.

Conjecture 2

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
of size
n
and
S
be a positive integer. Then there is a
N_{S}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

m_{A}\geq S/3,

for all
n\geq N_{S}
.

Our first result shows that in order to establish Conjecture
2
, it is enough to establish it for finitely many values of
n
.

Theorem 2

Suppose that Conjecture
2
is true for all
n
with
N_{S}<n\leq 3N_{S}+2S
. Then Conjecture
2
is true for all
n\geq N_{S}
.

Thus it remains to understand what happens for small values of
n
. First we ask, for a fixed
n
, is determining that
m_{A}\geq S/3
a finite computation? We cannot answer this, but by replacing
f
in Conjecture
2
with a suitable minorant, we can. We will use the Selberg polynomials (the exact choice is not important), which we recall in (
6
) below. The important properties of
S_{K}
is that it is a minorant of
f
, its Fourier support is contained in
\{-K,\ldots,K\}
and
\int(f-S_{K})\leq\frac{1}{K+1}
.

Theorem 3

Let
\delta>0
, and
C=C(\delta)
be large enough. Suppose for some
K\geq 1

\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\sum_{a\in A}S_{K}(ax)\geq S/3+\delta,

for all sets
A
of size
n
contained in
\{1,\ldots,T\}
where
T=\exp(O_{\delta}(n\log(nK)))
. Then Conjecture
2
is true for sets of size
n
and the same value of
S
.

The discretized nature of
f_{A}
ensures that
m_{A}\geq S/3
and
m_{A}\geq S/3+\delta
are not different conditions for
\delta<1/3
. However, this is not the case in Theorem
3
.

In light of Theorem
2
, it is natural to ask whether we can understand the behavior of
m_{A}
for small values of
n
. For instance the first open case is whether
m_{A}\geq 1
for all
A
of large enough size. As
m_{A}
is an integer, by Theorem
1
one only has to check for sets that have size
=1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
. In order to illustrate some relevant techniques, we classify sets of size 4 which have
m_{A}\leq 2/3
.

Theorem 4

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
of size 4 with co-prime elements. Then either
m_{A}>2/3
,

A=\{u,2u,v,2v\},

or
A
is one of

\{1,2,3,4\},\{1,2,4,5\},\{1,4,5,8\},\{2,3,5,10\}.

On the other hand, we do find an example with
n=10
such that
m_{A}=2/3
. In fact, this comes from work on the Lonely Runner conjecture
[
BHK01
]
, which we elaborate on in Section
7
.

We prove Theorem
2
in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we provide a computation that computes the Legesgue measure of

\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}:1/3\leq||\theta a||,||\theta b||<2/3\}.

In Section
4
, we give proof of Bourgain’s Theorem
1
, utilizing Theorem
2
. In Section
5
, we characterize when
m_{A}\leq 1
for sets of size 3. We then use this result to provide a similar characterization for sets of size 4 in Section
6
. In Section
7
, we mention a connection the the lonely runner conjecture. In section
8
, we prove Theorem
3
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
2

We let
A=A_{0}\cup A_{1}
where
A_{0}
are the elements of
A
co-prime to
3
and
A_{1}
are the elements that are divisible by 3. We have the following easy lemma, which establishes Theorem
2
if
A_{0}
is large.

Lemma 1

With the notation above,

m_{A}\geq|A_{0}|/6-|A_{1}|/3.

Proof.

By pigeon-hole, either at least half the elements of
A_{0}
or at least
1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
or
2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
. Thus either

f_{A_{0}}(1/3)\geq|A_{0}|/6,

or

f_{A_{0}}(2/3)\geq|A_{0}|/6.

The result follows as

m_{A}\geq\max\{f_{A_{0}}(1/3),f_{A_{0}}(2/3)\}-|A_{1}|/3.

∎

We now provide the inductive step.

Lemma 2

With the notation above,

m_{A}\geq m_{A_{1}}.

Proof.

We let
x\in\mathbb{T}
such that

f_{A_{1}}(x)=m_{A_{1}}.

As the elements of
A
are divisible by 3, we have

f_{A_{1}}(x)=f_{A_{1}}(x+1/3)=f_{A_{1}}(x+2/3).

Summing over
a\in A_{0}
, we find

f_{A}(x)+f_{A}(x+1/3)+f_{A}(x+2/3)=3m_{A_{1}}+\sum_{a\in A_{0}}\left(f(ax)+f(a(x+1/3))+f(a(x+2/3))\right).

On the other hand, for any
a\in A_{0}
, we have

f(ax)+f(a(x+1/3))+f(a(x+2/3))=0,

and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We may assume that the elements of
A
are co-prime, so that
A_{0}\neq\emptyset
. We proceed by strong induction, where
N_{S}<n\leq 3N_{S}+2S
are the base cases. By Lemma
1
, we may assume

|A_{0}|/6-|A_{1}|/3\leq S/3.

Using that
|A_{0}|+|A_{1}|=n
, we simplify this to

\frac{n-2S}{3}\leq|A_{1}|.

(2)

By our assumption in Theorem
2
, we may assume
n>3N_{S}+2S
and combining with (
2
), we find

N_{S}<|A_{1}|.

In particular
N_{S}<|A_{1}|<n
. The result follows from Lemma
2
and the induction hypothesis.

∎

3.
An explicit computation for two element sets

We input the following computation as it will be somewhat useful later. Furthermore, it is worth noting that for two element sets, we have a complete understanding, as in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

Let
a,b
be co-prime positive integers. Then

\mu(\{x\in\mathbb{T}:1/3\leq||ax||,||bx||<2/3\})=\frac{1}{9}(1+\frac{2\chi(ab)}{ab}).

Note that when
a,b=1,2
, the measure is zero and is otherwise positive. This is all we need in what follows in future sections (and there are shorter proofs of this fact).

Proof.

It is enough to show

\int_{0}^{1}(1/3+f(ax))(1/3+f(bx))dx=\frac{1}{9}(1+\frac{2\chi(ab)}{ab}).

Using that
f
has mean zero, it is enough to show

\int_{0}^{1}f(ax)f(bx)dx=\frac{2}{9}\frac{\chi(ab)}{ab}.

We expand
f
in its Fourier series, and use
\int_{0}^{1}\cos(2\pi ux)\cos(2\pi vx)dx=\frac{1}{2}1_{u=\pm v}
, so it is enough to show

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)\chi(m)}{nm}1_{an=bm}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{27}\frac{\chi(ab)}{ab}.

Note that the solutions to
an=bm
are precisely
(n,m)=d(b,a)
for
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and so
it remains to establish

\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(d)^{2}}{d^{2}}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{27}.

But by the Euler product representation,

\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(d)^{2}}{d^{2}}=(1-1/3^{2})\zeta(2),

and so the result follows from
\zeta(2)=\pi^{2}/6
.
∎

4.
Proof of Bourgain’s Theorem

We now show how to prove Theorem
1
, using the ideas from Theorem
2
alongside some of Bourgain’s original ideas. By Proposition
1
, is enough to establish Conjecture
1
for all
n\geq 3
.
As
\int f_{A}=0
, we have
m_{A}>0
. Furthermore, since
m_{A}\in|A|/3+\mathbb{Z}
, it is enough to establish Theorem
1
for
n=2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
. By Theorem
2
, with
N_{S}=S=2
, it is enough to consider the cases
n=5
and
n=8
.

We first recall a lemma of Bourgain that, in general, handles the case where
1\notin A
.

Lemma 3

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
of coprime elements of size
n
. Suppose
1\notin A
. Then
m_{A}>1/3
.

We include his proof for completeness.

Proof.

It is enough to construct a function
\phi\geq 0
such that
\int\phi=1
and
\int\phi f_{A}>1/3
. Let
u
be the smallest element and
v
be the smallest element such that
u\nmid v
. Let
c(x)=\cos(2\pi x)
. We set

\phi(x)=(1-c(ux))(1-c(vx)).

Then
\phi\geq 0
and
\int\phi=1
. By Plancherel, we have for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,

\int\phi(x)c(mx)dx=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}1_{m=na}.

If
m=u
, the
na=u
implies
a<u
and so there are no possible choices of
a
. If
m=v
, then
a<v
and so by our definition of
v
, we must have
u|a
. But this implies
u|v
and so again by our choice of
v
there is no possible choice of
a
. If
m=v-u
we can apply a similar argument to the case
m=v
. Lastly, if
u+v=na
and
n>1
we have
a<v
. Thus
u|a
and so there are no possible choices of
a
.

Putting this altogether, we find

\int\phi f_{A}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}(2-\frac{1}{2}1_{A}(u+v))>1/3.

∎

In what follows we make use of the notation and proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 4

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a set of size
5
. Then
m_{A}>1/3
.

Proof.

By the proof of Theorem
2
, we may suppose
A_{1}=\{v,2v\}
. By Lemma
3
, we may suppose
1\in A
. Let
\phi(x)=(1-(4/3)c(x)+(2/3)c(2x))
. Then
\phi\geq 0
,
\int\phi=1
and

\int\phi f_{A}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{5}{3}-\frac{2}{3}1_{A}(2)\right).

Thus we may suppose
2\in A
. Let
u
be the remaining element. We consider

\phi_{u}(x)=\phi(ux).

Then a computation reveals, using
\chi(n)=0
if
3|n
, that

\int f_{A}\phi=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}(5/3+\frac{\chi(u)}{u}-\frac{8\chi(u)}{3u}1_{u=0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)})>1/3,

as
2u\notin A
.
∎

Theorem
1
then follows from the following.

Lemma 5

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a set of size
8
. Then
m_{A}>1/3
.

Proof.

Following Lemma
4
, we may suppose
A_{1}=\{v,2v\}
and that
1,2\in A
. Letting
u\neq 1,2,v,2v
be the smallest such element, we may also suppose
2u\in A
, again following Lemma
4
. Thus we have described six of the eight elements of
A
.

We now show the remaining two elements are larger than
2v
. Indeed, as in the proof of Theorem
2
,

f_{A_{0}}(x)+f_{A_{0}}(x+1/3)+f_{A_{0}}(x+2/3)=0.

Let
x=1/(6v)
. As
f(2vx)=1/3
, we conclude
m_{A}>1/3
unless

f_{A_{0}}(x)=f_{A_{0}}(x+1/3)=f_{A_{0}}(x+2/3)=0.

As
f(z)=-1/3
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
z<1/3
, we have

f(x)=f(2x)=f(ux)=f(2ux)=-1/3,

and so we may assume
a>2v
for the remaining two elements of
a\in A
.

We now let

\phi(x)=(1-c(x))(1-c(vx)).

We first evaluate

\int f_{A}(x)c(vx)dx=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{a\in A}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}1_{na=v}.

Suppose
na=v
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\in A
. Then
a<v
and so
a\in\{1,2,u,2u,v\}
. As
v=0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
, we have
n=0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
when
a\neq 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)
. Thus in this case
\chi(n)=0
. It follows that the only solution is
a=v
and
n=1
.

Performing similar computations, we find

\displaystyle\int f_{A}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\left(2-\frac{(-1)^{v}\chi(v-1)}{2(v-1)}-\frac{(-1)^{v}\chi(v+1)}{2(v+1)}-\frac{1}{2}E_{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\left(2+\frac{(-1)^{v}}{(v+1)(v-1)}-\frac{1}{2}E_{A})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\left(2-1/8-\frac{1}{2}E_{A})\right),

where

E_{A}.=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}(1_{nju=v+1}+1_{nju=v-1}).

Note that we cannot have both
u|(v+1)
and
u|(v-1)
as
u>3
. Thus
E_{A}\leq 1/2
and we conclude

\int f_{A}\phi>1/3,

as desired.

∎

5.
The case
n=3

When
A
has 3 elements,
m_{A}=1
or
m_{A}=2
. In this section we classify when
m_{A}=1
, which will be useful in the case
n=4
. We first need a useful lemma that handles the case when two elements share a common factor that is not 2.

Lemma 6

Let
A=\{u,v,w\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a set with co-prime elements. Suppose
\gcd(u,v)>2
,
u\neq 2v
and
v\neq 2u
. Then
m_{A}>1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, there is a
x\in\mathbb{T}
such that

f_{\{u,v\}}(x)\geq 4/3.

Letting
d=\gcd(u,v)
, and noting
\gcd(w,d)=1
, we have the points

w(x+j/d),

are
d
points spaced
1/d
apart. As
1/d\leq 1/3
one must lie in
[1/3,2/3)
and the result follows.

∎

We now need a lemma that will eventually help to handle some small cases.

Lemma 7

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Suppose
u,v\in A
with
u<v
and
v\neq 2u
. Let
w\in A
such that
w>2uv
. Then there is a
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that

1/3\leq||ux||,||vx||,||wx||<2/3.

Proof.

By Proposition
1
, we know there is an
x\in\mathbb{T}
such that

1/3\leq||xu||,||xv||<2/3.

But the set of
\theta
such that
1/3\leq u\leq 2/3
consists of intervals of the form

[\frac{3k+1}{3u},\frac{3k+2}{3u}),

where
k=0,\ldots,u-1
. If this intersects an interval of the same form with
v
in the place of
u
, then the length of the intersection must be at least
1/(3uv)
. The gap between consecutive intervals, with
w
in the place of
u
is
2/(3w)
. Thus if
2/(3w)<1/(3uv)
there must be an
x
such that

1/3\leq||xu||,||xv||,||xw||<2/3.

∎

We now move onto the general case of sets of size 3.

Lemma 8

Let
A=\{u<v<w\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with co-prime elements. Then
m_{A}=1
if and only if

A=\{u,v,u+v\},

A\cap 2\cdot A\neq\emptyset,

or

A=\{1,5,8\},\{2,3,10\},

Proof.

In order to establish
m_{A}>1
, it is enough to construct a function
\phi
such that
\phi\geq 0
,
\int\phi=1
and
\int f_{A}\phi>1
. Let
c(x):=\cos(2\pi x)
. First note that

\phi_{1}:=(1+c(x)))\geq 0
. It then follows that

\phi_{2}(x):=\frac{\phi_{1}(x)^{3}}{7}=1+\frac{12}{7}c(x)+\frac{6}{7}c(2x)+\frac{2}{7}c(3x)\geq 0.

Thus

\phi_{3}(x):=\phi_{2}(x+1/2)\geq 0.

We let
\phi_{u}=\phi_{3}(ux)
and
\phi_{v}=\phi_{3}(vx)
and

\phi=\phi_{u}\phi_{v}.

Then
\phi\geq 0
, as
\phi_{3}\geq 0
. Also, as
\int\phi_{3}=1
, we have that either
\int\phi=1
there is a solution to

ju=kv,

(3)

with
j,k\in\{1,2,3\}
. We first suppose there is no such solution. We let
\widehat{\phi}(n)
the the coefficient of
c(nx)
in the Fourier expansion of
\phi
. Then we have

m_{A}\geq\int\phi f_{A}=\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{8\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{3}\sum_{k=0}^{3}\widehat{\phi_{u}}(j)\widehat{\phi_{v}}(k)\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(m)}{m}\sum_{a\in A}1_{\pm ma\pm ju\pm kv=0}.

We note that typically this sum is
>1
and if it is not then we will show this enforces a small linear relations on
u
and
v
. While the sum is at first sight infinite, only finitely many terms can be non-zero by the condition

\pm ma\pm ju\pm kv=0.

(4)

Our next goal is to understand solutions to (
4
).

We first suppose there is a solution to

mu=\pm ju\pm kv.

Then it follows that
(u/k)|\gcd(u,v)
and so by Lemma
8
, we have
u\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
. A symmetric argument works for the equation

mv=\pm ju\pm kv.

Thus we conclude
\min A>6
, or the only solutions to (
4
) with
a\in\{u,v\}
are with
jk=0
.

Suppose

mw=\pm ju\pm kv.

Then

v<w<\frac{(|j|+|k|)}{m}v,

and so
m<j+k
. Thus
m<6
. By a similar argument as above, we may suppose
jk\neq 0
. We also can assume
w\neq u+v
and by size considerations,
w\neq v-u
. Then one can check if there is no non-trivial solution to

\frac{ju+kv}{m}=\frac{j^{\prime}u+k^{\prime}v}{m^{\prime}},

(5)

(where the trivial solutions are from the solutions when
u,v
are linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) with
j,k\in\pm\{1,2,3\}
, then
\int\phi f_{A}>1
.
Thus we have

u=\frac{k^{\prime}m-km^{\prime}}{jm^{\prime}-j^{\prime}m}v.

Thus by Lemma
6
, either
m_{A}>1
or
v=d
or
v=2d
for some
d|(jm^{\prime}-j^{\prime}m)
. Thus
u<v\leq 60
and

for some

. It turns out these cases are easy to check by a computer. Indeed, for each

one can check there is a
j/d
such that
f_{A}(j/d)>1
by iterating over all the choices of
1\leq j\leq d-1
and
5\leq d\leq 50
.

It now remains to handle the case where

. First, suppose there is a solution to (
3
). Then
v|j\gcd(u,v)
and so

. By Lemma
7
, we may suppose

and this is easily checked by computer.

Thus we may suppose there is no solution to (

) and

. Everything outlined above still works in this case, except there may be some contribution from

. In this case

. By the computer search outlined above, we may suppose

and so

, as desired.
∎

6.
The case
n=4

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
A=\{u,v,w,x\}
. It is enough to show there is a
A^{\prime}\subset A
of size 3 such that
m_{A^{\prime}}>1
. First, suppose
1,5,8\in A
. Then by Lemma
7
, either
m_{A}>2/3
unless the remaining element is at most 80. This is easily checked by computer, the only example with
m_{A}=2/3
is
A=\{1,4,5,8\}
. Now suppose
u,5u,8u\in A
with
u>1
. We apply Lemma
8
to
\{5u,8u,x\}
and conclude that either
3u,13u,10u,16u,4u,5u/2\in A
. But the elements of
A
are coprime, so we must have
u=2
and
A=\{2,5,10,16\}
. This case is ruled out as
f_{A}(2/3)=5/3
.

We do a similar argument if
2u,3u,10u\in A
. The only example with
m_{A}=2/3
in this case is
A=\{2,3,5,10\}
.

Thus we may now suppose we are not in these two cases. We now suppose
u,v,u+v\in A
for some choice
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Let
w
be the remaining element. By Lemma
8
, applied to
\{u,v,w\}
, we may suppose
w=2u
,
w=2v
,
v=2u
,
u=2v
,
u=2w
,
v=2w
,
v=u+w
, or
u=v+w
.

We first suppose
w=2u
. Then we apply Lemma
8
to
\{2u,v,u+v\}
. Using that
u,v>0
and
u\neq v
, we conclude that (after possibly swapping
u,v
)
A=\{u,2u,3u,4u\}
or
A=\{u,2u,4u,5u\}
. As the elements of
A

Definition 1

(Frieman isomorphism)

Let
M\geq 1
and
A,B
be subsets of some abelian groups. A Freiman isomorphism of order
M
is a bijective map
\phi:A\to B
with the property that for all
a_{1},a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a_{m},a^{\prime}_{m}\in A

a_{1}+\cdots a_{M}=a^{\prime}_{1}+\cdots+a^{\prime}_{M},

if and only if

\phi(a_{1})+\cdots+\phi(a_{M})=\phi(a^{\prime}_{1})+\cdots+\phi(a^{\prime}_{M}).

We say
\phi
fixes zero if
0\in A
and
\phi(0)=0
.
