On the degrees of irreducible characters fixed by some field automorphism, p p -solvable groups
Nicola Grittini

Abstract
It is known that, if all the real-valued irreducible characters of a finite group have odd degree, then the group has normal Sylow 2 2 -subgroup. We generalize this result for Sylow p p -subgroups, for any prime number p p , while assuming the group to be p p -solvable. In particular, it is proved that a p p -solvable group has a normal Sylow p p -subgroup if p p does not divide the degree of any irreducible character of the group fixed by a field automorphism of order p p .

Introduction

The search for results linking the normal structure of a finite group and the degrees of its irreducible characters is a classical problem in character theory. The best known result linking the two properties is the celebrated Ito-Michler Theorem, and many variants of this theorem have already been found.

In
[
1
]
a variant of Ito-Michler Theorem is studied, involving real-valued characters. In fact,
[
1
, Theorem A]
states that, if the prime 2 does not divide the degree of any real-valued irreducible character of a finite group
G
, then
G
has a normal Sylow 2-subgroup.

If we look at the real-valued characters as the characters fixed by the complex conjugation, it is possible to generalize this theorem for any prime
p
, at least for
p
-solvable groups.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a finite
p
-solvable group, for some prime
p
, and let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}_{\left|G\right|}/\mathbb{Q})
of order
p
. Suppose that
p
does not divide the degree of any
\sigma
-invariant irreducible character of
G
. Then,
G
has a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup.

Theorem
A
generalizes
[
1
, Theorem A]
for
p
-solvable groups, since
[
1
, Theorem A]
follows directly from Theorem
A
if we take the complex conjugation as
\sigma
. In fact, the proof of Theorem
A
is sometimes similar to the proof of
[
1
, Theorem A]
; however, we also use some original techniques.

If we assume the group

-solvable, Theorem

Theorem B
.

Let

be a finite group, for some prime

, let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}_{\left|G\right|}/\mathbb{Q})
of order
p

-subgroup of

-invariant irreducible character of

Despite our restriction to the

Preliminary results

We shall prove some preliminary results first. We begin with some results about
p
-groups acting on vector spaces. We will then apply them to the action on characters of elementary abelian groups.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p
be a prime number and let
P>1
be a
p
-group. Let
V
be a finite vector space which is also an irreducible
P
-module, such that
\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=0
. Let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(V)
of order
p
such that, for every
v\in V
and every
x\in P
,
(v^{x})^{\sigma}=(v^{\sigma})^{x}
. Then, there exists
g\in P
such that
v^{\sigma}=v^{g}
for every
v\in V
.

Proof.

Notice that, since
\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=0
, then
\operatorname{C}_{P}(V)<P
and, thus, if we replace
P
with
\bar{P}=P/\operatorname{C}_{P}(V)
we may assume
\operatorname{C}_{P}(V)=1
. Moreover, since
V
is an irreducible
P
-module, then
V=\langle v^{P}\rangle
for every
0\neq v\in V
.

Let
z\in Z=\operatorname{Z}(P)
and suppose
z\in\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)
for some non-trivial
v\in V
; then, for every
y\in P
,
(v^{y})^{z}=(v^{z})^{y}=v^{y}
. Since by hypothesis
V=\langle v^{P}\rangle
, it follows that
z
centralizes all the generators of
V
and, thus,
z\in\operatorname{C}_{P}(V)=1
. Therefore,
Z\cap\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)=1
for every
0\neq v\in V
.

Let now
H=\langle\sigma\rangle\times Z
and consider the group
\Gamma=H\ltimes V
. Since
H
is an abelian
p
-group and it is not cyclic, it follows from
[
4
, Corollary 6.10]
that
\Gamma
is not a Frobenius group. As a consequence of
[
4
, Theorem 6.4]
, there exists
w\in V\setminus\{0\}
such that
D=\operatorname{C}_{H}(w)\neq 1
. However, we know from the previous paragraph that
Z\cap D=1
; thus, there exists
z\times\sigma^{-m}\in D
for some
m
coprime with
p
and some
z\in Z
. Now, let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
am=bp+1
for some
b\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
(z\times\sigma^{-m})^{a}=z^{a}\times\sigma^{-1}\in D
and, thus,
w^{z^{a}}=w^{\sigma}
. We may take
g=z^{a}\in\operatorname{Z}(P)
.

Let
v\in V
. Since, by hypothesis,
V=\langle w^{P}\rangle
, we can write
v=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}w^{p_{i}}
for some
p_{i}\in P
and some
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Now we have that

v^{\sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i}w^{p_{i}})^{\sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i}w^{\sigma})^{p_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i}w^{g})^{p_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i}w^{p_{i}})^{g}=v^{g}

and the thesis follows.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
p
be a prime number and let
P>1
be a
p
-group. Let
V
be a finite vector space which is also a
P
-module, and suppose that
\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=0
. Let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}{V}
of order
p
such that, for every
v\in V
and every
x\in P
,
v^{\sigma}\in\langle v\rangle
and
(v^{x})^{\sigma}=(v^{\sigma})^{x}
. Then, there exists
g\in P
and
0\neq v\in V
such that
v^{\sigma}=v^{g}
and
\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)\right|=\min\{\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(w)\right|\mid 0\neq w\in V\}
.

Proof.

At first, we recall that, since
V
is a finite vector space, then it is a vector space over a field of finite characteristic
q
. From the fact that
\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=0
, it follows that
q\neq p
.

Thus, by Maschke’s Theorem, we can write
V
as a sum of irreducible
P
-modules
V=W_{1}\oplus...\oplus W_{n}
. Let
v\in V
such that
\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)\right|=\min\{\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(w)\right|\mid 0\neq w\in V\}
, we claim that

can be chosen such that
v\in W_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,...,n\}
. In fact, let
v=w_{1}+...+w_{n}
for some uniquely determined
w_{i}\in W_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,...,n\}
. Then,
\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{C}_{P}(w_{i})
and, by the minimality of
\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)\right|
, it follows that, for each
i\in\{1,...,n\}
, either
w_{i}=0
or
\operatorname{C}_{P}(w_{i})=\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)
. Since
v\neq 0
, then there exists
i\in\{1,...,n\}
such that
w_{i}\neq 0
and, if we replace
v
with
w_{i}
, we prove the claim.

Thus, without loss of generality, we can claim that there exists
v\in W_{1}
such that
\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(v)\right|=\min\{\left|P:\operatorname{C}_{P}(w)\right|\mid 0\neq w\in V\}
. Moreover, notice that
{W_{1}}^{\sigma}\leq W_{1}
and, thus,
\sigma_{\mid_{W_{1}}}\in\operatorname{Aut}(W_{1})
and either
\sigma_{\mid_{W_{1}}}=1
or it has order
p
. Since
\operatorname{C}_{W_{1}}(P)\leq\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=0
and
W_{1}
is an irreducible
P
-module, it follows from Lemma
2.1
that there exists
g\in P
such that
v^{\sigma}=v^{g}
.
∎

We now prove some preliminary results concerning character theory.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let
p
be a prime number and let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}_{\left|G\right|}/\mathbb{Q})
such that
o(\sigma)
is a power of
p
. Suppose
H\leq G
such that
p\nmid\left|G:H\right|
and suppose, for some
\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(H)
, that
\psi(1)
divides the degree of every irreducible constituent of
\psi^{G}
. If
\psi
is fixed by
\sigma
, then
\sigma
also fixes some irreducible constituents of
\psi^{G}
.

Notice that the hypothesis of
\psi(1)
dividing the degree of every irreducible constituent of
\psi^{G}
is automatically verified if
H
is normal in
G
.

Proof.

Since
\psi^{\sigma}=\psi
, then also
(\psi^{G})^{\sigma}=\psi^{G}
and, thus,
\sigma
acts on the irreducible constituents of
\psi^{G}
. Let
C_{1},...,C_{k}
be the orbits of this action, then we have

\left|G:H\right|\psi(1)=\psi^{G}(1)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|C_{i}\right|n_{i}\chi_{i}(1),

where
\chi_{i}
is a representative of
C_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,...,k\}
, and
n_{i}
is the multiplicity of
\chi_{i}
in
\psi^{G}
. Notice that, since
o(\sigma)=p^{a}
for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
, then the size of each orbit
\left|C_{i}\right|
is a power of
p
.

It follows that

\left|G:H\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|C_{i}\right|n_{i}\frac{\chi_{i}(1)}{\psi(1)}

and, since
p\nmid\left|G:H\right|
and the quotients
\frac{\chi_{i}(1)}{\psi(1)}
are all integers, we have that
\left|C_{i}\right|=1
for some
i\in\{1,...,k\}
and, thus, there exists some
\chi=\chi_{i}\in\operatorname{Irr}(G\!\mid\!\psi)
such that
\chi^{\sigma}=\chi
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let

\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)

(\left|I_{G}(\varphi)/N\right|,o(\varphi)\varphi(1))=1

. Moreover,

Let

. Let

, then by
[

, Corollary 6.28]
there exists a unique character
\hat{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Irr}(T\!\mid\!\varphi)
extending

. Moreover,
\hat{\varphi}^{\sigma}
lies over
\varphi^{\sigma}
and, analogously, it is the unique character in

such that

. Since

, then

\chi=(\hat{\varphi}^{g})^{G}=(\hat{\varphi}^{\sigma})^{G}=\chi^{\sigma}.

∎

3
Simple groups

In this section, we will prove some properties of finite simple groups of Lie type, which then will lead to the proof of Theorem
C
. We will rely often to the Deligne-Lusztig theory, which connects the irreducible characters of a finite group of Lie type with the characters of the maximal tori of the group. A complete description of the theory can be found in
[
2
]
. Notice that most presentations of the Deligne-Lusztig theory prefer to consider semisimple elements of a dual group in place of the irreducible characters of the maximal tori. In the context of this paper, however, it is probably better to consider characters of subgroups, since it will make our arguments clearer.

Despite the use of the Deligne-Lusztig theory, however, some steps of our proofs still rely on the Classification of finite simple groups.

We first need to prove a result on characters of abelian groups, which then we will apply to the characters in maximal tori.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(A)

{\chi_{A_{0}}}^{\nu}\neq\chi_{A_{0}}
, and such that either

\left|A_{0}\right|=k^{p}-1

Consider now

. Now, fix

of order

. Consider

and notice that both

and

act on

. Moreover, if

. Suppose now that

3.1
, there exists
\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(\tilde{T})
such that
{\theta_{T}}^{\nu}\neq\theta_{T}
and
\theta
is fixed either by
\sigma
or by
\nu^{m}\times\sigma^{\shortminus 1}
, for some positive integer
m
coprime with
p
.

Let
\rho\in P\times\langle\sigma\rangle
such that
\theta^{\rho}=\theta
and either
\rho=1\times\sigma
or
\rho=\nu^{m}\times\sigma^{\shortminus 1}
, and let
\tilde{\psi}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\tilde{S})
be a semisimple character lying over
\theta
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\tilde{\psi}^{\rho}
is a semisimple character lying over
\theta^{\rho}=\theta
and, since
\mathbf{\tilde{S}}
has connected centre, by
[
2
, 2.6.10]
we have that
\tilde{\psi}^{\rho}=\tilde{\psi}
. Since
p\nmid\left|\tilde{S}:\tilde{S}\cap G\right|
(and since
\tilde{S}/S
is abelian, because
\tilde{S}\leq\operatorname{Aut}(S)
), we have that there exists at least one irreducible constituent
\psi
of
\tilde{\psi}_{\tilde{S}\cap G}
which is fixed by
\rho
. Moreover, it follows from
[
2
, Corollary 2.6.18]
that all the irreducible constituents of
\psi_{S}
are semisimple characters lying over
\theta_{T}\in\operatorname{Irr}(T)
.

Now, notice that
\theta_{T},{\theta_{T}}^{\nu}\in\operatorname{Irr}(T)
are not conjugated in
S
, since otherwise the Frattini argument would imply that
\nu\in I_{G}(\theta_{T})S
and, thus,
\nu\in I_{G}(\theta_{T})
. Since the irreducible constituents of
{\psi^{\nu}}_{S}
are semisimple characters lying over
{\theta_{T}}^{\nu}
, it follows from
[
2
, Theorem 2.6.2]
that
({\psi^{\nu}}_{S},\psi_{S})=0
and, in particular, that
\psi
is not fixes by
\nu
. Since, however,
\nu
is contained in every proper subgroup of
\langle\tau\rangle
, we have that
I_{G}(\psi)=G\cap\tilde{S}
, and it finally follows from Lemma
2.4
that
\chi=\psi^{G}\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
is
\sigma
-invariant and
p\mid\chi(1)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
C
.

It follows from the Classification of finite simple groups that, in all the listed situations,
p
,
S
and
G
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
3.3
. The existence of the group
\tilde{S}
for Theorem
3.3
, on the other hand, is guaranteed by
[
2
, Lemma 1.7.3]
.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
A
and Theorem
B

We are now ready to prove Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

We prove the theorem by induction on
\left|G\right|
. Let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
and suppose
P>1
, since otherwise the theorem follows trivially. We proceed by the following steps.

Step 1.
We can assume
\operatorname{O}_{p}(G)=1
and
\operatorname{O}^{p^{\prime}}(G)=G
, otherwise the thesis follows by induction.

If
\operatorname{O}_{p}(G)>1
, then
G/\operatorname{O}_{p}(G)
has a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup
P/\operatorname{O}_{p}(G)
and, thus,
P
is normal in
G
. Moreover, if
O=\operatorname{O}^{p^{\prime}}(G)
and
\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(O)
is
\sigma
-invariant, then by Proposition
2.3
there exists
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G\!\mid\!\psi)
which is
\sigma
-invariant and, since
p\nmid\chi(1)
by hypothesis, we have that
p\nmid\psi(1)
. It follows that
p
does not divide the degree of any
\sigma
-invariant irreducible character of
O
. If
O<G
, it follows by induction that
P
is a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup of
O
and, therefore,
P
is normal in
G
.

Step 2.
Let
N
be a minimal normal subgroup of
G
. Then,
N
is a
p^{\prime}
-group and, by induction,
PN/N\lhd G/N
. It follows that
PN=\operatorname{O}^{p^{\prime}}(G)=G
.

Moreover, one may notice that
C=\operatorname{C}_{G}(N)\lhd G
and, if
P_{0}\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}\left(C\right)
, then
P_{0}=\operatorname{O}_{p}(C)\leq\operatorname{O}_{p}(G)=1
. It follows that
\operatorname{C}_{P}(N)=P\cap C=1
, and either
C=N
and
N
is abelian, or
C=1
.

Step 3.
If
N
is non-abelian, the thesis follows.

Suppose
N
is non-abelian. Then, it is a direct product of non-abelian simple groups which are transitively permuted by
P
. Write
N=S_{1}\times...\times S_{n}
, where each
S_{i}
is simple non-abelian. Write
S=S_{1}
and let
H=\operatorname{N}_{G}(S)
. Let
1_{s}\neq\delta\in\operatorname{Irr}(S)
be a rational character (see Theorem
2.5
), let
\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
be defined as

\psi=\delta\otimes 1_{S_{2}}\otimes...\otimes 1_{S_{n}}

and notice that
\psi
is
\sigma
-invariant,
I_{G}(\psi)\leq H
and
(\left|H/N\right|,o(\psi)\psi(1))=1
. Thus, it follows from Lemma
2.4
and from our hypothesis that
G=H=I_{G}(\psi)
,
N=S
and, since
\operatorname{C}_{P}(N)=1
, we have that
S<G\leq\operatorname{Aut}(S)
. Since
p\nmid\left|S\right|
, then
p
must be odd and it follows from the Classification of finite simple groups that
S
is a simple group of Lie type. Then, the thesis follows from Theorem
C
.

Step 4.
The thesis follows also if
N
is abelian.

Assume
N
to be abelian, then it is an elementary abelian
q
-group for some
q\neq p
. Let
V=\operatorname{Irr}(N)
, then
V
is a finite vector space over a field of characteristic
q
; since
G=PN
and
N
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
,
V
is also an irreducible
P
-module, with
P
acting on
V
by conjugation. Since
\operatorname{C}_{P}(N)=1
and
N
is minimal, then also
\operatorname{C}_{N}(P)=1
and
\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=1_{N}
. Moreover, the action of
\sigma
on
V
commutes with the action of
P
. Then, by Lemma
2.1
, there exist
g\in P
and
\lambda\in V=\operatorname{Irr}(N)
such that
\lambda^{\sigma}=\lambda^{g}
and, by Lemma
2.4
, there exists
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
such that
\chi^{\sigma}=\chi
and
\chi(1)=\left|G:I_{G}(\lambda)\right|
. Since by hypothesis
p\nmid\chi(1)
, it follows that
G=I_{G}(\lambda)
and, therefore, there exists some
1\neq x\in N
such that
\operatorname{C}_{G}(x)=G
. It follows that
x\in\operatorname{Z}(G)\cap N
and, being
N
a minimal normal subgroup of
G
,
N\leq\operatorname{Z}(G)
and
G=P\times N
. Thus, the thesis follows also in this case.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B
.

We prove the theorem by induction on
\left|G\right|
. Let
N=\operatorname{N}_{G}(P)
and let
M/K
be a chief factor of
G
, by induction we can assume that
K=1
and, thus, that
M
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
. If
p\mid\left|M\right|
, then
1<P\cap M\leq N\cap M
and the theorem is proved in this case. Thus, we can assume
M
to be a
p^{\prime}
-group.

Notice that, in this situation,
N\cap M=\operatorname{C}_{M}(P)
. Therefore, we need to prove that
C=\operatorname{C}_{M}(P)>1
. We prove it both when
M
is a non-abelian minimal normal subgroup and when
M
is abelian.

Case 1.

M
is non-abelian.

Suppose
M
is a non-abelian
p^{\prime}
-subgroup. Then, it is a direct product of non-abelian simple groups which are transitively permuted by
G
. Write
M=S_{1}\times...\times S_{n}
, where each
S_{i}
is simple non-abelian, and write
S=S_{1}
. Let
1_{S}\neq\delta\in\operatorname{Irr}(S)
be a rational character (see Theorem
2.5
). Let

\psi=\delta\otimes 1_{S_{2}}\otimes...\otimes 1_{S_{n}}\in\operatorname{Irr}(M)

and let
T=I_{G}(\psi)
. Let
P_{0}=P\cap T
; by eventually replacing
\psi
with some
G
-conjugate, we can assume that
P_{0}
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup for
T
.

Let
\hat{\psi}\in\operatorname{Irr}(P_{0}M\mid\psi)
be the canonical extension of
\psi
to
P_{0}M
, then
\hat{\psi}
is
\sigma
-invariant and
\hat{\psi}(1)=\psi(1)
divides the degree of every irreducible constituent of
\hat{\psi}^{T}
. Since
p\nmid\left|T:P_{0}M\right|
, it follows from Proposition
2.3
that there exists
\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(T)
lying over
\hat{\psi}
, and thus lying also over
\psi
, such that
\theta^{\sigma}=\theta
. If
\chi=\theta^{G}\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
, then

\chi^{\sigma}=(\theta^{G})^{\sigma}=(\theta^{\sigma})^{G}=\theta^{G}=\chi

and, by hypothesis,
p\nmid\chi(1)=\left|G:T\right|\theta(1)
. It follows that
p\nmid\left|G:T\right|
and, thus,
P\leq T
and
\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}_{P}(M)
, with
\operatorname{Irr}_{P}(M)
being the set of the
P
-invariant irreducible characters of
M
. By Glauberman correspondence, see
[
3
, Theorem 13.1]
,
\left|\operatorname{Irr}(C)\right|=\left|\operatorname{Irr}_{P}(M)\right|>1
and it follows that also
C>1
.

Case 2.

M
is abelian.

Assume
M
to be abelian, then it is an elementary abelian
q
-group for some
q\neq p
. Let
V=\operatorname{Irr}(M)
,
V
has the structure of a finite vector space over a field of characteristic
q
and it is also a
P
-module, with
P
acting on
V
by conjugation. If we suppose
C=\operatorname{C}_{M}(P)=1
, then also
\operatorname{C}_{V}(P)=1_{M}
. Moreover, the action of
\sigma
on
V
commutes with the action of
P
and, for each
\xi\in V
,
\ker(\xi)=\ker(\xi^{\sigma})
and, therefore,
\xi^{\sigma}\in\langle\xi\rangle
. By Lemma
2.2
, there exist
g\in P
and
\lambda\in V
such that
\lambda^{\sigma}=\lambda^{g}
. Moreover, we can choose
\lambda
such that
\left|P:I_{P}(\lambda)\right|=\min\{\left|P:I_{P}(\xi)\right|\mid 1_{M}\neq\xi\in\operatorname{Irr}(M)\}
and, in particular, it follows that
\left|P:I_{P}(\lambda)\right|\mid\theta(1)
for every
\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(PM)
such that
M\nleq\ker(\theta)
.

Now, by Lemma
2.4
, there exists
\eta\in\operatorname{Irr}(PM)
lying over
\lambda
such that
\eta^{\sigma}=\eta
and
\eta(1)=\left|P:I_{P}(\lambda)\right|
. Let
\psi
be an irreducible constituent of
\eta^{G}
, then
\psi_{PM}=n_{1}\theta_{1}+...+n_{r}\theta_{r}
for some
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\theta_{i}\in\operatorname{Irr}(PM)
such that, for each
i\in\{1,...,r\}
,
M\nleq\ker(\theta_{i})
, since
M\nleq\ker(\psi)
and
M
is normal in
G
. It follows from the previous paragraph that
\eta(1)
divides the degree of every
\theta_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,...,r\}
, and, thus,
\eta(1)\mid\psi(1)
.

Thus,
\eta(1)
divides the degree of every irreducible constituent of
\eta^{G}
and, since
p\nmid\left|G:PM\right|
, it follows from Proposition
2.3
that
\sigma
fixes some irreducible constituent
\chi
of
\eta^{G}
. By hypothesis,
p\nmid\chi(1)
and, thus,
p\nmid\eta(1)=\left|P:I_{P}(\lambda)\right|
. It follows that
1_{M}\neq\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}_{P}(M)
and, again by Glauberman correspondence,
\left|\operatorname{Irr}(C)\right|=\left|\operatorname{Irr}_{P}(M)\right|>1
and, thus,
C>1
.
∎
