Abstract
. It was showed by Donner in [ Don82 ] that every order complete vector lattice X X may be embedded into a cone X s X^{s} , called the sup-completion of X X . We show that if one represents the universal completion of X X as C ∞ ​ ( K ) C^{\infty}(K) , then X s X^{s} is the set of all continuous functions from K K to [ − ∞ , ∞ ] [-\infty,\infty] that dominate some element of X X . This provides a functional representation of X s X^{s} , as well as an easy alternative proof of its existence.

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

The concept of a sup-completion of a vector lattice was introduced
in
[
Don82
]
and then further investigated
in
[
Az19
,
AN22
]
; this concept was utilized in a series
of papers by J.Grobler and C.Labuschagne

[
Gro14
,
GL17a
,
GL17b
,
GL17c
,
GL19
]
. The
intuitive idea behind sup-completion is rather simple: one wants to
enlarge a function space by allowing functions that take value

+\infty
on non-negligible sets. In particular, the sup-completion of

\mathbb{R}
is
(-\infty,+\infty]
. However, the formal definition of
sup-completion and the proof of its existence (for order complete
vector lattices) in
[
Don82
]
is quite technical. In this note,
we provide an alternative (and, hopefully, more intuitive) way of
constructing sup-completions.

We refer the reader to
[
AB03
,
AB06
]
for
background on vector lattices. Throughout this note, all vector
lattices are assumed to be Archimedean. If
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
are
two Hausdorff topological spaces, we write
C(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})
for the
set of all continuous functions from
\Omega
to
\Omega^{\prime}
. We write

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
for
[-\infty,\infty]
. For any interval
I

in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we equip
C(\Omega,I)
with the pointwise
partial order. We write
C(\Omega)
for
C(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
; this is
a vector lattice under pointwise operations. If
K
is an extremally
disconnected Hausdorff topological space then
C(K)
is order (or
Dedekind) complete. Suppose that
K
is an extremally disconnected
compact Hausdorff topological space; we then write
C^{\infty}(K)
for
the vector lattice of all continuous functions from
K
to

[-\infty,\infty]
that are finite almost everywhere (a.e.), that is,
except on a nowhere dense set. Scalar multiplication and lattice
operations on
C^{\infty}(K)
are defined pointwise. Addition is defined
a.e.: for
f,g\in C^{\infty}(K)
there exists a unique

h\in C^{\infty}(K)
such that
f(t)
and
g(t)
are finite and

h(t)=f(t)+g(t)
for all
t
in an open dense set; we then define

h=f+g
. For a function
u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
and

\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we write
\{u\leqslant\lambda\}
as a
shorthand for
\bigl\{t\in K\>:\>u(t)\leqslant\lambda\bigr\}
; we define

\{u=\lambda\}
in a similar fashion.

Maeda-Ogasawara Theorem asserts that every vector lattice
X
may be
represented as an order dense sublattice of
C^{\infty}(K)
, where
K

is an extremally disconnected compact Hausdorff topological space. If

X
is order complete then it is an ideal in
C^{\infty}(K)
.

Here is the main theorem of this paper:

Theorem 1
.

Let
X
be an order complete vector lattice represented in
C^{\infty}(K)
as
above. Then the sup-completion of
X
is

\bigl\{u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})\>:\>u\geqslant f\mbox{ for some $f$ in }X\bigr\}.

This theorem may be viewed as an alternative definition of a
sup-completion. Before we prove it, we recall the definition of a
sup-completion from
[
Don82
]
. By a
cone
we mean a
commutative semigroup with zero
(C,+,0)
equipped with a non-negative
scalar multiplication operation

(\lambda,a)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times C\mapsto\lambda a\in C
, which
satisfies the following conditions:

\lambda(a+b)=\lambda a+\lambda b
,
(\lambda+\mu)a=\lambda a+\mu a
,

\lambda(\mu a)=(\lambda\mu)a
,
1a=a
and
0a=0
for every
a,b\in C

and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. It is easy to see that the set
C_{0}
of
all invertible elements in
C
is a group. For example, if

C=(-\infty,\infty]
then
C
is a cone and
C_{0}=\mathbb{R}
(we take

0\cdot\infty=0
). The scalar multiplication on

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times C_{0}
may be extended to
\mathbb{R}\times C_{0}
via

(-r)x=-(rx)
when
r>0
; it is straightforward that
C_{0}
is a vector
space over
\mathbb{R}
.

We now impose several additional conditions that describe an order on

C
and the way
C_{0}
“sits” in
C
:

(i)

C
is equipped with a partial order, such that

a\leqslant b
implies
a+c\leqslant b+c
and
\lambda a\leqslant\lambda b

for all
a,b,c\in C
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
;

(ii)

C
is a lattice under this order;

(iii)

C
has the greatest element;

(iv)

C_{0}
has an ideal property in
C
in the sense that
if
x\in C_{0}
and
a\in C
such that
a\leqslant x
then
a\in C_{0}

(v)

C
is order complete in the sense that every subset
A
of

C
has supremum; if
A
is bounded below then
\inf A
exists;

(vi)

C_{0}
is order dense in
C
in the following
sense:
a=\sup\{x\in C_{0}\>:\>x\leqslant a\}
for every
a\in C
;

(vii)

a+(x\wedge b)=(a+x)\wedge(a+b)
whenever
a,b\in C

and
x\in C_{0}
;

(viii)

for any two non-empty subsets
A
and
B
of
C
,
if
\sup A=\sup B
and
x\in C_{0}
then

\sup\{a\wedge x\>:\>a\in A\}=\sup\{b\wedge x\>:\>b\in B\}

in
C
.

It is easy to see that
C_{0}
is an order complete vector lattice. We
say that
C
is a sup-completion of
C_{0}
. More precisely, if
X
an
order complete vector lattice and
X=C_{0}
for a cone
C
satisfying
the properties listed above, we say that
C
is a

sup-completion
of
X
. It was proven in
[
Don82
]
that
every order complete vector lattice admits a sup-completion;
Theorem
1
provides an alternative proof of this.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1

The proof is tedious but
straightforward. Let
C
be the set in the
theorem:

(1)

C=\bigl\{u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})\>:\>u\geqslant f\mbox{ for some $f$ in }X\bigr\}.

By definition,
X
is a subset of
C
. It is easy to see that
the set
\{u>-\infty\}
is open and dense for every
u\in C
.

We will now define operations on
C
. Non-negative scalar
multiplication on
C
is defined pointwise; it clearly satisfies

(\lambda+\mu)u=\lambda u+\mu u
,
\lambda(\mu u)=(\lambda\mu)u
,

1u=u
, and
0u=0
for every
u\in C
and
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. On
X
, it agrees with the non-negative scalar multiplication of

C^{\infty}(K)
.

Defining addition on
C
requires some care. We do it similarly to

C^{\infty}(K)
. Recall that
K
is extremally disconnected.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that

u\colon U\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a continuous function on an
open dense subset
U
of
K
. Then
u
extends uniquely to a
function in
C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is topologically and order isomorphic
to
[-1,1]
via, say,
\tan\frac{\pi t}{2}
, we may replace

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
in the statement with
[-1,1]
.
So suppose that

u\colon U\to[-1,1]
. Let

G=\Bigl\{v\in C\bigl(K,[-1,1]\bigr)\>:\>\forall t\in U\quad v(t)\geqslant u(t)\Bigr\}.

Since
K
is extremally disconnected,
C(K)
is order complete. It
follows from
G\geqslant-\mathbbold{1}
that
w:=\inf G
exists in

C(K)
. Clearly,
w\in C\bigl(K,[-1,1]\bigr)
.

We will now show that
w
extends
u
. Fix
t\in U
. Since
K
is
totally disconnected, we can find a clopen set
V
such that

t\in V\subseteq U
. Put
v=u\cdot\mathbbold{1}_{V}+\mathbbold{1}_{K\setminus V}
; then

v\in G
and, therefore,
w\leqslant v
; it follows that

w(t)\leqslant u(t)
. On the other hand, for
every
v\in G
we have
v\geqslant u\cdot\mathbbold{1}_{V}-\mathbbold{1}_{K\setminus V}
, so that

w\geqslant u\cdot\mathbbold{1}_{V}-\mathbbold{1}_{K\setminus V}
and, therefore,
w(t)\geqslant u(t)
.

This proves that
w
extends
u
. Since
U
is dense, the extension
is unique.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
and let

U=\{u_{1}>-\infty\}\cap\{u_{2}>-\infty\}
. If
U
is dense than there
exists a unique
u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
such that

u(t)=u_{1}(t)+u_{2}(t)
for all
t\in U
.

Proof.

Define
v\colon U\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
via

v(t)=u_{1}(t)+u_{2}(t)
;
v
is well defined and continuous. By
Lemma
2
,
v
extends to a function

u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. Uniqueness follows from the
density of
U
.
∎

We are now ready to define addition on
C
. Suppose that
u_{1},u_{2}\in C
. Let
U=\{u_{1}>-\infty\}\cap\{u_{2}>-\infty\}
.
There exist
f_{1},f_{2}\in X
such that
f_{1}\leqslant u_{1}
and
f_{2}\leqslant u_{2}
. Let
V
be the set where both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are finite. Then

V
dense. It follows from
V\subseteq U
that
U
is
dense. Let
u
be as in Corollary
3
. For every
t\in V
,
we have

(f_{1}+f_{2})(t)=f_{1}(t)+f_{2}(t)\leqslant u_{1}(t)+u_{2}(t)=u(t).

Since
V
is dense, continuity of
f_{1}+f_{2}
and
u
implies that

f_{1}+f_{2}\leqslant u
on
K
. Therefore,
u\in C
. Naturally, we define
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
.

Clearly, this definition does not depend on the choice of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.
It is straightforward that
u_{1}+u_{2}=u_{2}+u_{1}
and that

\lambda(u_{1}+u_{2})=\lambda u_{1}+\lambda u_{2}
when

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
u_{1},u_{2}\in C
. Furthermore, if

u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\in C
, we have
(u_{1}+u_{2})+u_{3}=u_{1}+(u_{2}+u_{3})
because the
two continuous functions agree with
u_{1}(t)+u_{2}(t)+u_{3}(t)
for every

t
is the dense set where all the three functions are different from

-\infty
. This shows that
C
is a cone.

We claim that
C_{0}=X
. Indeed, if
u\in C_{0}
then
u,-u\in C
, hence,
there exist
f,g\in X
such that
f\leqslant u\leqslant g
. It follows that
u
is
finite on an open dense set, hence
u\in C^{\infty}(K)
. Since
X
is
order complete, it is an ideal in
C^{\infty}(K)
and, therefore,

u\in X
. Conversely, if
f\in X
then, clearly,
f
and
-f
are both
in
C
, hence
f\in C_{0}
. Note that the operations of addition and
non-negative scalar multiplication that we defined on
C
agree with
those of
C^{\infty}(K)
on
C\cap C^{\infty}(K)
. It follows that the
vector space operations induced on
C_{0}
by
C
agree with the
“native” operations on
C^{\infty}(K)
.

We define order on
C
pointwise. It follows from the definition of

C
that if
u\in C
and
v\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
with

u\leqslant v
then
v\in C
. We will now verify conditions
(
i
)–(
viii
).

(
i
), (
ii
), and (
iii
) are
straightforward. It is easy to see that lattice operations on
C
are
pointwise.

(
iv
) Suppose that
v\leqslant h
for some
v\in C
and
h\in X
. There exists
f\in X
such that
f\leqslant v\leqslant h
. It follows that

v\in C^{\infty}(K)
and, furthermore,
v\in X
.

Observe that if
f\leqslant u\leqslant g
for some
f,g\in X
and

u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
then
u\in X
. Indeed, it follows
from
f\leqslant u
that
u\in C
; it now follows from (
iv
) that

u\in X
.

(
v
)
As in the proof of Lemma
2
, we observe that

C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
is order complete. Let
A\subseteq C
with

A\neq\varnothing
. It follows that
v:=\sup A

exists in
C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
. Take any
w\in A
, then
v\geqslant w\in C
implies
v\in C
, hence
v
is the supremum of
A
in
C
.

Now suppose that
u\leqslant A
for some
u\in C
. Then
v:=\inf A
exists in

C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
; it follows from
u\leqslant v
that
v\in C

and, therefore,
v
is the infimum of
A
in
C
.

(
vi
) Suppose that
u\in C
and let

A=\{f\in X\>:\>f\leqslant u\}
; we need to show that
u=\sup A
. By the
definition of
C
,
A
is non-empty; fix some
h\in A
. We clearly
have
A\leqslant u
. Suppose
A\leqslant v
for some
v\in C
; it suffices to show
that
u\leqslant v
. Suppose not. Then there exists
t_{0}\in K
with

v(t_{0})<u(t_{0})
. Find a clopen neighbourhood
U
of
t_{0}
such that

v(t)<u(t)
for all
t\in U
. Since
\mathbbold{1}_{U}
is in
C^{\infty}(K)
and

X
is order dense in
C^{\infty}(K)
, we can find
g\in X
such that

0<g\leqslant\mathbbold{1}_{U}
. Then
g(t_{1})>0
for some
t_{1}\in U
. Let
f
be a
scalar multiple of
g
such that
v(t_{1})<f(t_{1})<u(t_{1})
. It follows
from
f\wedge h\leqslant f\wedge u\leqslant f
that
f\wedge u\in X
and, therefore,

f\wedge u\in A
. However,
(f\wedge u)(t_{1})>v(t_{1})
, which
contradicts
A\leqslant v
.

(
vii
) Let
u,v\in C
and
f\in X
; we need to prove that

u+(f\wedge v)=(u+f)\wedge(u+v)
. Let
U
be the set on which
u
,

v
, and
f
are all different from
-\infty
. Then
U
is open and
dense, and it is straightforward that the functions
u+(f\wedge v)

and
(u+f)\wedge(u+v)
agree on
U
. Since they are continuous, they
are equal on
K
.

(
viii
) Suppose that
\sup A=\sup B
for two non-empty subsets

A
and
B
of
C
, and let
f\in X
. It suffices to show that

\sup(A\wedge f)\leqslant\sup(B\wedge f)
. Suppose not. Then there exists

u\in A
such that
u\wedge f\not\leqslant h
, where
h=\sup(B\wedge f)
. There exists
t\in K
such that
u(t)\wedge f(t)>h(t)
. Fix

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u(t)\wedge f(t)>\lambda>h(t)
. By
continuity, we can find a clopen neighbourhood
U
of
t
such that for all

s\in U
we have

u(s)\wedge f(s)>\lambda>h(s)\geqslant v(s)\wedge f(s)

for
v\in B
. It follows from

u(s)\wedge f(s)>\lambda>v(s)\wedge f(s)

that
u(s)>\lambda>v(s)
for all
s\in U
and
v\in B
.

Consider the function
w\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
that is equal
to
\lambda
on
U
and
+\infty
on
K\setminus U
. Then
w\geqslant v
for
all
v\in B
and, therefore,
w\geqslant b
, where
b=\sup B
. It follows
that
u(s)>w(s)\geqslant b(s)
for all
s\in U
. This contradicts
\sup A=\sup B
.

This completes the proof of the theorem. We now establish a
useful distributive property of
C
. Here
C
is as in (
1
).

Lemma 4
.

For every
u\in C
and
A\subseteq X
,
\sup(u+A)=u+\sup A
.

Proof.

For every
f\in A
we have
f\leqslant\sup A
, so that
u+f\leqslant u+\sup A

and, therefore,
\sup(u+A)\leqslant u+\sup A
.

To prove the converse inequality, let
U=\{u<\infty\}
; observe that

\overline{U}
is clopen. Viewing
u
,
\sup A
, and
\sup(u+A)
as
functions in
C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
, it suffices to prove the
inequality for their band projections onto the bands generated by

\overline{U}
and
\overline{U}^{C}
. On the latter set,
u
is
identically
\infty
, so the inequality is easily satisfied. So
replacing
K
with
\overline{U}
, we may assume that
u
is a.e. finite and, therefore,
u\in C^{\infty}(K)
. Then
-u
is defined in

C^{\infty}(K)
and we have

\sup A=\sup(-u+u+A)\leqslant-u+\sup(u+A),

so that
u+\sup A\leqslant\sup(u+A)
.
∎

3.
Uniqueness

Donner in
[
Don82
]
proved that the sup-completion is
unique. His proof relies on his construction of a sup-completion. We
now present an alternative proof using our Theorem
1
instead
(but our proof is built on the same ideas as that
in
[
Don82
]
). We need the following variant of Riesz
Decomposition Property:

Lemma 5
.

Let
C
be a sup-completion of
X
. Suppose that
x\leqslant u+v
for
some
x\in X
and
u,v\in C
with
v\geqslant 0
. Then there exist

y,z\in X
such that
x=y+z
,
y\leqslant u
, and
z\leqslant v
.

Proof.

Since
x\wedge u\in X
by (
iv
), we can define
y=x\wedge u

and
z=x-x\wedge u
in
X
. We clearly have
x=y+z
and
y\leqslant u
. It
is left to verify that
z\leqslant v
. Since
z\in X
,
v-z=v+(-z)
is
defined. Using (
vii
), we get

v-z=(v-x)+x\wedge u=(v-x+x)\wedge(v-x+u)=v\wedge(u+v-x)\geqslant 0.

∎

Theorem 6
.

Sup-completion of an order complete vector lattice is unique. That
is, if
C
and
D
are two sup-completions of
X
then there exists
a bijection
J\colon D\to C
such that
u\leqslant v
iff
Ju\leqslant Jv
,
J(\alpha u)=\alpha Ju
, and
J(u+v)=Ju+Jv
when

u,v\in D
and
\alpha\geqslant 0
, and
J
agrees with the identity on
X
.

Proof.

WLOG,
C
is the sup-completion that we constructed in
Theorem
1
, i.e.,
C
is as in (
1
). For a
non-empty set
A\subseteq X
, we define

J\bigl(\sup_{D}A)=\sup_{C}A
. Let’s verify that
J
is
well-defined. Suppose that
A
and
B
are two non-empty subsets of

X
with
\sup_{D}A=\sup_{D}B
. For every
a\in A
, (
viii
) yields

\textstyle a=\sup_{D}(A\wedge a)=\sup_{D}(B\wedge a)=\sup_{X}(B\wedge a)\leqslant\sup_{C}B.

It follows that
\sup_{C}A\leqslant\sup_{C}B
. The opposite inequality is
similar. It is left to verify that
J
is defined on all of
D
: if

u\in D
then it follows from (
vi
) that
u=\sup_{D}A
where

A=\{x\in X\>:\>x\leqslant u\}
. Hence,
J(u)=\sup_{C}A
, that is,

(2)

J(u)=\textstyle\sup_{C}\{x\in X\>:\>x\leqslant u\}

Since the definition of
J
is symmetric, it is easy to see that
J

is a bijection, with the inverse given by

J^{-1}\bigl(\sup_{C}A)=\sup_{D}A
for
A\subseteq X
.
It is straightforward that
J(\alpha u)=\alpha J(u)
when

u\in D
and
\alpha\geqslant 0
. It follows from (
2
) that
u\leqslant v

implies
J(u)\leqslant J(v)
. Since the definition of
J
is symmetric,
the converse is also satisfied, hence
u\leqslant v
iff
J(u)\leqslant J(v)
.

It is left to show that
J
is additive. If
u\in D
and
y\in X
,
it follows from (
2
) and Lemma
4
that

J(y+u)=\textstyle\sup_{C}\bigl\{x\in X\>:\>x\leqslant y+u\bigr\}=\sup_{C}\bigl(y+A)=y+\sup_{C}A=y+J(u),

where
A=\{x\in X\>:\>x\leqslant u\}
.

Now fix
u,v\in D
. Put

A=\{x\in X\>:\>x\leqslant u\}
and
B=\{y\in X\>:\>y\leqslant v\}
. Then

u=\sup_{D}A
and
v=\sup_{D}B
by (
vi
), and

J(u)=\sup_{C}A
and
J(v)=\sup_{C}B
by (
2
). Fix
x\in A

and
y\in B
. We have
x+y\leqslant u+v
. It follows from (
2
) that

x+y\leqslant J(u+v)
, so that
x\leqslant J(u+v)-y
. Taking supremum in
C
over
x\in A
, we get
J(u)\leqslant J(u+v)-y
and, therefore,
J(u)+y\leqslant J(u+v)
. By
Lemma
4
, we have

J(u)+J(v)=\textstyle J(u)+\sup_{C}B=\sup_{C}(J(u)+B)=\sup_{C}\{J(u)+y\>:\>y\in B\}\leqslant J(u+v).

To prove the other inequality, we first assume that
v\geqslant 0
. Note
that
J(u+v)=\sup_{C}B
, where
B=\{x\in X\>:\>x\leqslant u+v\}
. For every

x\in B
, find
y
and
z
as in Lemma
5
. It follows that

B\subseteq\{y\in X\>:\>y\leqslant u\}+\{z\in X\>:\>z\leqslant v\}\leqslant J(u)+J(v),

so that
J(u+v)\leqslant J(u)+J(v)
.

Finally, if
u
and
v
are two arbitrary elements of
D
, it
follows from
v^{-}\in X
that

J(u+v)=J(u+v^{+}-v^{-})=J(u+v^{+})-v^{-}\leqslant J(u)+J(v^{+})-v^{-}=J(u)+J(v^{+}-v^{-})=J(u)+J(v).

∎

4.
Applications

We now use our representation of sup-completion to provide simple
proofs of several results of
[
Az19
,
AN22
]
and improve
some of them. Throughout this section,
X
is (again) an order
complete vector lattice. We write
X^{u}
and
X^{s}
for the universal
completion and the sup-completion of
X
, respectively. As before, we
represent
X^{u}
as an order dense sublattice of
C^{\infty}(K)
for some
extremally disconnected compact
K
; we represent
X^{s}
as in
Theorem
1
.

We start by revisiting Corollary 7 in
[
Az19
]
. It follows
immediately from Theorem
1
that every non-negative function
in
C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
belongs to
X^{s}
. In particular, we
have
X^{u}_{+}\subseteq X^{s}
. Suppose now that
Y
is an order dense
order complete sublattice of
X
. Then clearly
Y
is still order
dense in
C^{\infty}(K)
, hence
C^{\infty}(K)=Y^{u}
, and

Y^{s}=\bigl\{u\in C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})\>:\>u\geqslant f\mbox{ for some $f$ in }Y\bigr\}.

It follows that both
(X^{s})_{+}
and
(Y^{s})_{+}
consist of all
non-negative function in
C(K,\overline{\mathbb{R}})
and, therefore,

(X^{s})_{+}=(Y^{s})_{+}
. Note that if
Y^{s}=X^{s}
then
Y=X
because every
negative
f\in X
belongs to
Y
by (
iv
).

Recall that if
X
has a weak unit
e
, one can choose the
representation so that
e=\mathbbold{1}
.

Proposition 7

(
[
Az19
]
)
.

If
e
is a weak unit in
X_{+}
and
0\leqslant u\in X^{s}
then

u=\sup_{n}(ne\wedge u)
.

Proof.

WLOG,
e=\mathbbold{1}
. Let
v=\sup_{n}(n\mathbbold{1}\wedge u)
. Clearly,
v\leqslant u
. Fix
t\in K
. Then
v(t)\geqslant n\wedge u(t)
for all

n
. Considering separately the cases when
u(t)=\infty
and when

u(t)<\infty
, we see that
v(t)\geqslant u(t)
and, therefore,
v\geqslant u
.
∎

By Maeda-Ogasawara theory (see, e.g., Chapter 7
in
[
AB03
]
), there is a one-to-one correspondence between
clopen subsets of
K
and bands in
X
: if
U
be a clopen set in
K

then the set
\{x\in X\>:\>\supp x\subseteq U\}
is a band in
X
, and
every band in
X
is of this form; we denote it by
B_{U}
. The
corresponding band projection
P_{U}
is given by

P_{U}x=x\cdot\mathbbold{1}_{U}
. It is clear that the universal completion of

B_{U}
is
C^{\infty}(U)
and, hence the sup-completion of
B_{U}
can be
computed as in Theorem
1
.

In particular, for
a\in X
, the principal
band projection
P_{a}
is given by the following: for
x\in X
and

t\in K
, we have

(P_{a}x)(t)=\begin{cases}x(t)&\mbox{ if $t\in U$, and}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise },\end{cases}\qquad\mbox{ where }U=\overline{\{a\neq 0\}}.

The preceding formula clearly extends to the case when
a\in X^{s}
,
yielding a band projection on
X
.

In Theorem 15 of
[
AN22
]
, the authors prove that every
element
u
in
X^{s}
can be split into its finite and infinite
parts. Using our representation, this is now easy: let
U
be the
closure of
\{u<\infty\}
, then the finite part
x
of
u
is defined
as
x=P_{U}u
and is the function that agrees with
u
on
U
and
vanishes on
U^{C}
, while the infinite part
w
of
u
is defined as

P_{U^{C}}u
and is the function that vanishes on
U
and is identically

\infty
on
U^{C}
. Clearly,
u=x+w
and
x\perp w
. It follows from

x\in C^{\infty}(K)
that
x\in X^{u}
. It follows from
u\in X^{s}
that

u\geqslant f
for some
f\in X
, hence
P_{U}u\geqslant P_{U}f
and, therefore,

x\in X^{s}
. Clearly,
u\in X^{u}
iff its infinite part
w
equals zero
and
u
equals
x
, its finite part. Note also that
x
may be viewed
as the restriction of
u
to
U
; so we may view
x
as an element of

C^{\infty}(U)
and of
U^{s}
.

We now extend the Riesz Decomposition Theorem to
X^{s}
;
cf. Lemma
5
and
[
Az19
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that
x\leqslant u+v
for
some
x,u,v\in X^{s}
with
v\geqslant 0
. Then there exist

y,z\in X^{s}
such that
x=y+z
,
y\leqslant u
, and
z\leqslant v
.

Proof.

Let
U
be the closure of
\{u<\infty\}
. Then
U
is clopen and

X=B_{U}\oplus B_{U^{C}}
. It suffices to prove the statement on
U

and on
U^{C}
. That is, replacing
K
with
U
or
U^{C}
, we reduce
the problem to two special cases: when
u
is constant infinity and
when
u<\infty
on a dense open set. If
u
is constant infinity
then we take
y=x
and
z=0
; it is clear that all the requirements
are satisfied. Suppose now that
u<\infty
on a dense open set. That
is,
u\in C^{\infty}(K)
.

Put
y=x\wedge u
. Then
y\in X^{s}
by (
ii
), and

y\leqslant u
yields
y\in C^{\infty}(K)
. It follows that

z:=x-x\wedge u=x-y
is defined in
X^{s}
. Clearly,
x=y+z

in
X^{s}
. It is left to show that
z\leqslant v
.

Again, this is trivially satisfied on the band corresponding to the
infinite part of
v
as
v
is constant infinity there. So, passing
to the complementary band corresponding to

\overline{\{v<\infty\}}
, we may assume WLOG that
v\in C^{\infty}(K)
. It follows from
x\leqslant u+v
that
x\in C^{\infty}(K)
,
hence also
y,z\in C^{\infty}(K)
. As in the proof of Lemma
5
, we have

v-z=(v-x)+x\wedge u=(v-x+x)\wedge(v-x+u)=v\wedge(u+v-x)\geqslant 0.

∎

Theorem 9

(
[
Az19
]
)
.

Let
e\in X_{+}
be a weak unit and
u\in X^{s}
. Then

u\notin X^{u}\qquad\mbox{ iff }\qquad\inf_{\lambda\in(0,\infty)}P_{(u-\lambda e)^{+}}e>0.

Proof.

WLOG, we may chose the representation so that
e=\mathbbold{1}
. Let

v=\inf_{\lambda\in(0,\infty)}v_{\lambda}
, where

v_{\lambda}=P_{(u-\lambda\mathbbold{1})^{+}}\mathbbold{1}
. Suppose that
u\notin X^{u}
. Then

\Int\{u=\infty\}
in non-empty; denote this set by
V
. For every

t\in V
and every
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
we have

(u-\lambda\mathbbold{1})^{+}(t)=\infty
, so that
v_{\lambda}(t)=1
. It follows
that
v_{\lambda}\geqslant\mathbbold{1}_{\overline{V}}
and, therefore,

v\geqslant\mathbbold{1}_{\overline{V}}>0
.

Conversely, suppose that
v>0
. Then the set
W:=\{v>0\}
is
open. Fix
t\in W
. For every
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
it follows from

v_{\lambda}\geqslant v
that
v_{\lambda}(t)>0
, so that

(u-\lambda\mathbbold{1})^{+}(t)>0
and, therefore,
u(t)\geqslant\lambda
. It
follows that
u(t)=\infty
for all
t\in W
and therefore,
u\notin C^{\infty}(K)
.
∎

It is proved in Theorem 19 in
[
AN22
]
that if
0\leqslant u\in X^{s}

and
e
is a weak unit in
X
then

P_{w}e=\inf_{\lambda\in(0,\infty)}P_{(u-\lambda e)^{+}}e
, where
w
is
the infinite part of
u
. This fact can now be easily proved
analogously to Theorem
9
. Other properties of
decompositions of
u
into the finite and the infinite part
in
[
AN22
]
can be proved in a similar way.

Fix a weak unit
e
in
X_{+}
. WLOG, we may assume that
e
corresponds
to
\mathbbold{1}
in the
C^{\infty}(K)
representation of
X^{u}
. Similarly to
how we defined addition on
C^{\infty}(K)
, one can define
multiplication, making
X^{u}
into an f-algebra with
e
being a
multiplicative unit. Recall that
(X^{s})_{+}
consists of all continuous
positive functions from
K
to
[0,\infty]
. Similarly to how we
defined addition on
X^{s}
, we can define product on
(X^{s})_{+}
. That
is,
uv=w
iff
u(t)v(t)=w(t)
for all
t
in an open dense set; we
again follow the convention that
0\cdot\infty=0
. It is easy to see
that the resulting product agrees with that defined in Section 3.2
of
[
AN22
]
.
