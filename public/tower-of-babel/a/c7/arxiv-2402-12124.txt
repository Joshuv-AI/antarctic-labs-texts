I
Introduction

Brownian motion in a time dependent
harmonic potential is a stochastic process important from both
theoretical and experimental points of view. It provides an insightful example and testing ground for subtle theoretical results, like fluctuation theorems
PP
.
It is also directly relevant to important experimental techniques
to study mesoscopic systems using optical traps. In
such experiments, the system is driven from thermal equilibrium
by varying the strength of the trap, i.e. the stiffness of the harmonic potential
k(t)
, according to a
certain protocol,
and the work
W
on the system, as well as the system’s response, are recorded
traps1
;
traps2
.

The optical trap setup has been used also
to design Brownian engines with a single optically trapped Brownian particle
as a working substance and with the trap stiffness
k(t)
as a control parameter
Blickle
;
Martinez
;
SM
;
HR
;
Ryabov_review
. Such machines often operate out of equilibrium
using driving protocols of finite duration and thus belong to the realm of finite-time thermodynamics
FT
;
Watanabe
.

The steps of isothermal compression (expansion) of the macroscopic Carnot cycle correspond in Brownian engines to the increase (decrease) of the stiffness
k(t)
at fixed temperature during a finite switching time interval
(0,t_{s})
.
The corresponding work
W
strongly fluctuates and is governed by equations of stochastic dynamics of Langevin or Fokker-Planck types with the time-dependent potential
V(q,t)=k(t)q^{2}/2
.
The microscopic work can be written as
Jar

\displaystyle W=\int_{0}^{t_{s}}\frac{dV(q,t)}{dk}\,\dot{k}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{0}^{t_{s}}q^{2}(t)\,\dot{k}(t)\,dt,

(1)

and the average work is a functional of the mean-square displacement

\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle
,

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{0}^{t_{s}}\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle\,\dot{k}(t)\,dt.

(2)

Since the microscopic work is a nonlinear functional of

q
, the work distribution is not Gaussian in general.
For overdamped Langevin dynamics with white Gaussian noise the asymptotic distribution for large absolute values of work was found to have the form

\displaystyle f(W)=c_{1}\frac{e^{-c_{2}|W|}}{\sqrt{|W|}},

(3)

where
c_{1},c_{2}
depend on the specific form of the protocol
k(t)

Engel
;
NE
. This form, with
an exponential tail and a power-law prefactor,
was found for
f(W)
also for Langevin dynamics beyond the overdamped limit
Kwon
.
On the other hand, the central part of the work distribution was predicted to have
an approximately Gaussian form
in the limit of a slow driving
Seifert
;
Speck
.

The evaluation of the work distribution
in closed form for an arbitrary protocol
and for the whole range of
W

appears to be a rather formidable task.
However, for
a special protocol, often referred to as the instantaneous quench
PP
, the calculations are very simple. In that protocol at
t=0

the stiffness is instantaneously switched from
k_{0}
to a new value
k_{1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle k(t)=k_{0}+(k_{1}-k_{0})\,\theta(t),

(4)

where
\theta(t)
is the step function.
The corresponding microscopic work
on the system

is equal to the difference of the
system’s potential energy

\displaystyle W=\frac{k_{1}-k_{0}}{2}\,q^{2},

(5)

where
q=q(0)
. Because the protocol’s duration is effectively zero,
statistics of
W
does not depend on details of stochastic dynamics (such as statistics of the noise) and is completely determined by
the distribution of the oscillator’s initial coordinate
q
. Suppose at
t<0
the oscillator with the stiffness
k=k_{0}
is in thermal equilibrium and the
coordinate and velocity are distributed according to the canonical distribution

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(q,v)=\frac{1}{Z}\,\exp\left(-\frac{m\,v^{2}}{2T}-\frac{k_{0}\,q^{2}}{2T}\right).

(6)

Here and below temperature is in energy units.
Taking the average of Eq. (
5
) with distribution (
6
) gives the mean work

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=\frac{k_{1}-k_{0}}{k_{0}}\,\frac{T}{2}.

(7)

This result also follows from Eqs. (
2
) and (
4
).
According to Eqs. (
5
)-(
7
),
the normalized work

x=W/\langle W\rangle

is a square of the standard normal random variable
z=\sqrt{k_{0}/T}q
,
x=z^{2}
. Therefore
x
is distributed with the chi-squared
\chi_{1}^{2}
distribution
Simon

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{e^{-x/2}}{\sqrt{x}}\,\theta(x),

(8)

and the distribution for
W=x\,\langle W\rangle
has the form

\displaystyle f(W)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\langle W\rangle\,W}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{W}{2\langle W\rangle}\right)\,\theta\left(\frac{W}{\langle W\rangle}\right).

(9)

This result corroborates Eq. (
3
), but in contrast to the latter it holds for any
W
.
Thus for the instantaneous quench the work distribution is non-Gaussian for any values of
W
. The distribution is exactly zero when
W
has a sign opposite to that of
\langle W\rangle
.

For the instantaneous quench the validity of the Jarzynski equality
Jar

\displaystyle\langle e^{-W/T}\rangle=e^{-\Delta F/T}

(10)

can be directly verified.
For the oscillator with stiffness
k
in thermal equilibrium the free energy is

F(k)=-T\,\ln(2\pi T\sqrt{m/k})
. Then

\Delta F=F(k_{1})-F(k_{0})=-T\,\ln(\sqrt{k_{0}/k_{1}})
,
and the right-hand side of Eq. (
10
) equals

\sqrt{k_{0}/k_{1}}
. The average in the left-hand side
can be taken either over
q,v
with distribution (
6
), or over
W
with distribution (
9
):

\displaystyle\langle e^{-W/T}\rangle=\iint exp\left(-\frac{k_{1}-k_{0}}{2T}\,q^{2}\right)\,\rho_{0}(q,v)\,\,dq\,dv=\int\exp\left(-\frac{W}{T}\right)\,f(W)\,dW=\sqrt{\frac{k_{0}}{k_{1}}}.

(11)

The purpose of this paper is to consider another limiting and idealized case
when
the stiffness is perturbed according to the protocol

\displaystyle k(t)=k_{0}+a\,\delta(t),

(12)

where
\delta(t)
is the Dirac delta function and the intensity of the perturbation
a
, which may be of any sign, has units of mass/time. We shall refer to Eq. (
12
) as the delta protocol.
Of course,
the delta protocol cannot be practically realized per se
and should be viewed as an asymptotic form of spike-like perturbations
with a high amplitude and a short duration.
We shall see that the properties of the
the delta protocol in many respects differ
from that for the instantaneous quench.

II
Work

In the sections to follow we show that for the delta protocol (
12
)
the microscopic work on the system is

\displaystyle W(q,v)=\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\,q^{2}-a\,q\,v,

(13)

where
q=q(0^{-})
and
v=v(0^{-})
are initial coordinate and velocity just before the perturbation is applied.
Similar to the instantaneous quench, see Eq. (
5
), the statistics of
W
is completely determined by that of the initial state,
but for the delta protocol it depends on both initial coordinate
and velocity.
The average work is governed by
the mean square displacement
\langle q^{2}\rangle
and
the position-velocity correlation

\langle q\,v\rangle
in the initial state.

Let us assume that for
t<0
the system is in thermal equilibrium with distribution (
6
). In that case the initial correlation

\langle qv\rangle
is zero, and the average work
is determined by the initial mean square displacement
\langle q^{2}\rangle
only,

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=\iint W(q,v)\,\rho_{0}(q,v)\,dq\,dv=\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\langle q^{2}\rangle=\frac{a^{2}T}{2mk_{0}}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\,T,\qquad\alpha=\frac{a}{m\,\omega_{0}}.

(14)

Here
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{k_{0}/m}
is the oscillator’s frequency, and
\alpha
is the characteristic dimensionless parameter of the protocol.
One observes that
W
and
\langle W\rangle
may be, in contrast to the instantaneous quench, of different signs.
Using Eq. (
13
), one can readily evaluate the moment-generating function
for the dimentionless work
w=W/T
,

\displaystyle M_{w}(t)=\langle e^{tw}\rangle=\iint e^{t\,W(q,v)/T}\,\rho_{0}(q,v)\,dq\,dv=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}(t+t^{2})}}.

(15)

Then the moments of
W
can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\langle W^{n}\rangle=T^{n}\,\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}\,M_{w}(t)\bigg\rvert_{t=0}.

(16)

For the first few moments one obtains

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{2}\,\alpha^{2}\,T,

\displaystyle\mu_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\alpha^{2}+\frac{3}{4}\,\alpha^{4}\right)\,T^{2}=2\,T\,\mu+3\,\mu^{2},

\displaystyle\mu_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{9}{2}\,\alpha^{4}+\frac{15}{8}\,\alpha^{6}\right)\,T^{3}=18\,T\,\mu^{2}+15\,\mu^{3},

\displaystyle\mu_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(9\,\alpha^{4}+\frac{45}{2}\,\alpha^{6}+\frac{105}{16}\,\alpha^{8}\right)T^{4}=36\,T^{2}\,\mu^{2}+180\,T\,\mu^{3}+105\,\mu^{4},

(17)

and the variance
\sigma^{2}=\mu_{2}-\mu^{2}
equals

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\left(\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\alpha^{4}\right)\,T^{2}=2T\mu+2\mu^{2}.

(18)

From the thermodynamics point of view, the delta protocol is a cycle process,
for which the difference of the free energy is zero,
\Delta F=0
, and the Jarzynski equality (
10
) takes the form
\langle e^{-W/T}\rangle=1
. The validity of this result for the delta protocol is immediately obvious from Eq. (
15
),

\displaystyle\langle e^{-W/T}\rangle=M_{w}(-1)=1.

(19)

For the dimensionless work
w=W/T
, the moment-generating function taken at
-t
is a bilateral (two-sided) Laplace transform
\mathcal{B}
of the distribution function (probability density function)
f(w)
,

\displaystyle M_{w}(-t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-tw}f(w)\,dw=\mathcal{B}\{f(w)\}.

(20)

Then from Eqs. (
15
) and (
20
), one finds

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\{f(w)\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}(t-t^{2})}}.

(21)

The inversion of this relation yields (see the Appendix)

\displaystyle f(w)=\frac{1}{\pi\,|\alpha|}\,\exp\left(\frac{w}{2}\right)\,K_{0}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}\,|w|\right),

(22)

where
K_{0}(x)
is the modified Bessel function, the parameter
\Delta
is

\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{\frac{4}{\alpha^{2}}+1},

(23)

and
\alpha
is defined by Eq. (
14
).
The distribution function for the work
W=T\,w
takes the form

\displaystyle f(W)=\frac{1}{\pi\,|\alpha|\,T}\,\exp\left(\frac{W}{2T}\right)\,K_{0}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2T}\,|W|\right),

(24)

which is shown in Fig. 1.
As for the instantaneous quench, the work distribution diverges as
W\to 0
. This divergence does not lead to any singularities of measurable quantities. In particular, using
f(W)
to evaluate
the moments

\mu_{n}=\int f(W)W^{n}dW
, one recovers Eqs. (
17
). Also, the validity of the Jarzynski equality, evaluated with
f(W)
, can be directly verified:

\displaystyle\langle e^{-W/T}\rangle=\int e^{-W/T}\,f(W)\,dW=\int e^{-w}\,f(w)\,dw=1.

(25)

Figure 1:
The work distribution function (
24
) for several values of
|\alpha|=|a|/(m\,\omega_{0})
.

Another remarkable, though quite expected, in view of Eq. (
13
), feature of the distribution
f(W)
is that it is asymmetric.
Taking into account the asymptotic form of the modified Bessel function
K_{0}(x)\approx\sqrt{\pi/(2x)}\,e^{-x}
for large
x
,
one finds that the asymptotic tails of the distribution for
|W|>2T/\Delta
have the forms

\displaystyle f(W)\approx\frac{1}{\alpha\sqrt{\Delta\,\pi\,T\,W}}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta-1}{2T}\,W\right),\quad W>0

(26)

and

\displaystyle f(W)\approx\frac{1}{\alpha\sqrt{\Delta\,\pi\,T\,|W|}}\exp\left(\frac{\Delta+1}{2T}\,W\right),\quad W<0.

(27)

The distribution decays faster for negative values of
W
. If in addition
|\alpha|\gg 1
, then
\Delta\to 1
,
\Delta-1\to 2/\alpha^{2}
, and the above expressions are further simplified:

\displaystyle f(W)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\,\pi\,\langle W\rangle\,W}}\exp\left(-\frac{W}{2\langle W\rangle}\right),\quad W>0,

(28)

and

\displaystyle f(W)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\,\pi\,\langle W\rangle\,|W|}}\exp\left(\frac{W}{T}\right),\quad W<0.

(29)

Note that the asymptotic expression (
28
) coincides with the exact distribution for the instantaneous quench, Eq. (
9
).

Whereas the derivation of the above results
is quite straightforward, there is a subtlety
related to the fact that for the delta protocol the microscopic velocity is
discontinuous at
t=0
, see Eq. (
51
) below.
The next section elaborates on that.

III
Preliminaries

For the delta protocol (
12
)
Eq. (
2
) for the average work takes the form

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=\frac{a}{2}\,\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle\,\dot{\delta}(t)\,dt,

(30)

with
\epsilon>0
.
Integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=-\frac{a}{2}\,\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!\delta(t)\,\frac{d}{dt}\,\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle\,dt.

(31)

As a next step, it might be tempting to apply
the defining property of the delta function

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\delta(t)f(t)dt=f(0)

(32)

to obtain

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=-\frac{a}{2}\,\,\frac{d}{dt}\,\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle\bigg\rvert_{t=0}=-a\,\langle q(0)\,v(0)\rangle.

(33)

Suppose the system is in thermal equilibrium at
t<0
, so that
the position-velocity correlation
\langle q(t)\,v(t)\rangle
is zero at
t=0^{-}
.
Assuming that the correlation is continuous at
t=0
, one would find
from Eq. (
33
) that
the mean work is zero,
\langle W\rangle=0
.

That conclusion is incorrect because, as we shall see below, the function

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\,\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=2\,\langle q(t)\,v(t)\rangle

(34)

is discontinuous at
t=0
. Since
f(0)
is not well-defined,
the property (
32
) is ambiguous
and
the transition
from Eq. (
31
) to Eq. (
33
) is in general erroneous.
The standard way to extend the property (
32
) for the
case of discontinuous
f(t)
is to replace
f(0)
by the average of
f(t)
at the
discontinuity

\displaystyle\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\delta(t)f(t)dt=\frac{1}{2}\left[f(0^{-})+f(0^{+})\right].

(35)

In our case
f(0^{-})=0
(if the system is in equilibrium at
t<0
) and
f(0^{+})
will be shown
below, see Eq. (
60
), to be

\displaystyle f(0^{+})=-\frac{2aT}{mk_{0}}.

(36)

Then from Eqs. (
31
) and (
35
) one gets

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=-\frac{a}{4}\,f(0^{+})=\frac{a^{2}}{m\,k_{0}}\,\frac{T}{2},

(37)

which is the result (
14
).

The ansatz (
35
) is known to be inconsistent in certain cases
and ought to be used with care
GW
.
Below we consider the problem with two methods. The first method is based on using
ansatz (
35
).
The second method does not rely on ansatz (
35
), but involves a specific representation of
\delta(t)
as a limit of a rectangular
impulse.
We find that the two methods lead to the same results.

IV
Method I

According to Eq. (
1
), for the delta protocol the microscopic work is

\displaystyle W=\frac{a}{2}\,\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!q^{2}(t)\,\dot{\delta}(t)\,dt=-a\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\!q(t)\,v(t)\,\delta(t)\,dt.

(38)

Anticipating
q(t)
and
v(t)
to be, respectively, continuous and discontinuous at
t=0
, and applying ansatz (
35
)
one gets

\displaystyle W=-\frac{a}{2}\,q(0)\,[v(0^{+})+v(0^{-})].

(39)

Here
q=q(0)
and
v=v(0^{-})
are drawn from the initial distribution, which may be, but not necessarily, the equilibrium distribution (
6
).
In order to find
v(0^{+})
we need to solve equations of stochastic dynamics, find
v(t)
for
t>0
, and
take the limit
t\to 0^{+}
.

To this end, we assume that for
t>0

the system is described by the generalized Langevin equation

;
Weiss

\displaystyle\!\!m\,\ddot{q}(t)\!=\!-k(t)\,q(t)\!-\!m\int_{0}^{t}\!K(t-\tau)\,\dot{q}(\tau)\,d\tau+\xi(t).

(40)

Here
\xi(t)

is the stationary fluctuating
force (“noise”) which is zero-centered,
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
,
and related to the dissipation kernel
K(t)

via the fluctuation-dissipation relation

\displaystyle\langle\xi(t)\,\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=m\,T\,K(|t-t^{\prime}|).

(41)

We do not assume that
\xi(t)
is Gaussian.
The anticipated results (
13
) and (
14
) do not depend on the form of the dissipation kernel
K(t)
. Therefore, instead of the generalized Langevin equation one can use its more simple and familiar Markovian form with
K(t)=\gamma\,\delta(t)
. Yet we prefer to work with the Langevin equation of the form (
40
) which is more general and,
in contrast to the Markovian
counterpart, does not imply coarse-graining of time. We shall show in Conclusion that the
overdamped approximation (when the acceleration term is put to zero) is inadequate to describe the delta protocol.

The Langevin equation (
40
) for
protocol (
12
) takes the form

\displaystyle\ddot{q}(t)=-\left[\omega_{0}^{2}+\frac{a}{m}\,\delta(t)\right]\,q(t)-\int_{0}^{t}K(t-\tau)\,\dot{q}(\tau)\,d\tau+\frac{1}{m}\xi(t),

(42)

where
\omega_{0}^{2}=k_{0}/m
is the oscillator’s natural frequency.
The equation is linear and can be readily solved using Laplace transforms

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\{f(t)]=\tilde{f}(s)=\int_{0^{-}}^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)\,dt.

(43)

Assuming that
q(t)
(but not

) is continuous at

,
one finds

\displaystyle q(t)=S_{0}(t)\,q(0)+G_{0}(t)\left[v(0^{-})\!-\!\frac{a}{m}q(0)\right]+\frac{1}{m}\{G_{0}*\xi\}.

(44)

Here the asterisk in the last term stands for the convolution

\{G_{0}*\xi\}=\int_{0}^{t}G_{0}(t-\tau)\,\xi(\tau)\,d\tau
,
and the relaxation functions
G_{0}(t)
and
S_{0}(t)
are defined in the Laplace domain as follows,

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{0}(s)=\frac{1}{s^{2}+s\tilde{K}+\omega_{0}^{2}},\quad\tilde{S}_{0}(s)=\frac{s+\tilde{K}}{s^{2}+s\tilde{K}+\omega_{0}^{2}}=\frac{1}{s}\,\left[1-\omega_{0}^{2}\,\tilde{G}_{0}(s)\right].

(45)

It is also convenient to use another relaxation function
R_{0}(t)
, which is connected to
G_{0}(t)
in the Laplace domain as

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{0}(s)=s\,\tilde{G}_{0}(s).

(46)

One can show that the relaxation functions coincide with the normalized
correlations for the oscillator with
k=k_{0}
in thermal equilibrium

;
DV
;
Goychuk
;
Plyukhin

\displaystyle\langle q(t)q(t^{\prime})\rangle_{eq}=\frac{T}{k_{0}}\,S_{0}(t-t^{\prime}),\quad\langle v(t)v(t^{\prime})\rangle_{eq}=\frac{T}{m}\,R_{0}(t-t^{\prime}),\quad\langle q(t)v(t^{\prime})\rangle_{eq}=\frac{T}{m}\,G_{0}(t-t^{\prime}).

(47)

From these relations it is clear that the relaxation functions are continuous at
t=0
, and
their initial values are

\displaystyle S_{0}(0)=R_{0}(0)=1,\quad G_{0}(0)=0,\quad\dot{S}_{0}(0)=\dot{R}_{0}(0)=0,\quad\dot{G}_{0}(0)=1.

(48)

These initial values can also be obtained directly using the definition of the relaxation
functions in the Laplace domain, Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
).

Using the above relations, one finds that in the time domain the relaxation functions are connected as follows

\displaystyle\dot{G}_{0}(t)=R_{0}(t),\quad\dot{S}_{0}(t)=-\omega_{0}^{2}G_{0}(t).

(49)

Then differentiating Eq. (

\displaystyle v(t)=-q(0)\,\left[\omega_{0}^{2}G_{0}(t)+\frac{a}{m}R_{0}(t)\right]+v(0^{-})\,R_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{m}\,\{R_{0}*\xi\}.

Taking in Eqs. (

\displaystyle v(0^{+})=v(0^{-})-q(0)\,\frac{a}{m}.

(51)

This relation can also be obtained directly by integrating the Langevin equation (

V
Energy

Suppose the system at
t<0
is in thermal equilibrium with the distribution
\rho_{0}
, Eq. (
6
).
For the instantaneous quench
k_{0}\to k_{1}
at
t=0

the average kinetic energy
E_{k}=m\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle/2
can be shown to be continuous and the average potential energy
E_{p}=k(t)\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle/2
has the instantaneous gain:

\displaystyle E_{k}(0^{+})=E_{k}(0^{-}),\quad E_{p}(0^{+})=E_{p}(0^{-})+\langle W\rangle.

(53)

Here
E_{k}(0^{-})=E_{p}(0^{-})=T/2
and
\langle W\rangle
is the mean work on the system given by Eq. (
7
). In other words, immediately after the quench the work is adopted by the system entirely in the form of potential energy.

One can show that
for the delta protocol the situation is, in a sense, opposite: At
t=0
the average potential energy is continuous, whereas the kinetic energy receives instantaneously a finite gain,

\displaystyle E_{p}(0^{+})=E_{p}(0^{-}),\quad E_{k}(0^{+})=E_{k}(0^{-})+\langle W\rangle.

(54)

Here again
E_{k}(0^{-})=E_{p}(0^{-})=T/2
and
\langle W\rangle
is the mean work on the system given by Eq. (
14
). Thus, immediately after the delta perturbation the potential energy is unaffected, and the work on the system is utilized as the system’s kinetic energy.

The average potential energy is determined by

\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle
.
Squaring Eq. (
44
) and taking the average with the equilibrium distribution
\rho_{0}
yields

\displaystyle\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=\frac{T}{k_{0}}\left\{S_{0}(t)-\frac{a}{m}G_{0}(t)\right\}^{2}+\frac{T}{m}\,G_{0}^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,\langle(G_{0}*\xi)^{2}\rangle.

(55)

The average square of the convolution in this expression can be,
using the fluctuation-dissipation relation (
41
),
worked out to the form

\displaystyle\langle(G_{0}*\xi)^{2}\rangle=\frac{m\,T}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\,[1-S_{0}^{2}(t)]-m\,T\,G_{0}^{2}(t).

(56)

Then we get

\displaystyle\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=\frac{T}{k_{0}}\left\{1-\frac{2a}{m}\,S_{0}(t)\,G_{0}(t)+\left(\frac{a}{m}\right)^{2}G_{0}^{2}(t)\right\}.

(57)

This expression holds for
t>0

Taking the limit
t\to 0^{+}
and taking into account that
G_{0}(0)=0
, see Eq. (
48
), one finds

\displaystyle\langle q^{2}(0^{+})\rangle=\langle q^{2}(0^{-})\rangle=T/k_{0}.

(58)

This leads to the continuity of the potential energy.

Although the mean square displacement is continuous, its derivative,
which is also the two times
position-velocity correlation,

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\,\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=2\langle q(t)v(t)\rangle,

(59)

is discontinuous at
t=0
. Indeed, from Eq. (
57
) and (
48
) one finds

\displaystyle f(0^{+})=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{d}{dt}\,\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=-\frac{2aT}{mk_{0}}.

(60)

On the other hand, for
t<0
the system is in equilibrium, so that
f(0^{-})=0
, and

\displaystyle f(0^{+})=f(0^{-})-\frac{2aT}{mk_{0}}.

(61)

We referred to the discontinuity of
f(t)
is Sec. III, evaluating the mean work using Eq. (
31
).

Consider now the average kinetic energy.
Squaring and averaging of Eq. (
50
) yields

\displaystyle\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{T}{k_{0}}\,\left\{\omega_{0}^{2}G_{0}(t)+\frac{a}{m}R_{0}(t)\right\}^{2}+\frac{T}{m}\,R_{0}^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,\langle(\xi*R_{0})^{2}\rangle.

(62)

Similar to Eq. (
56
),
the last term can be worked out to the form

\displaystyle\langle(R_{0}*\xi)^{2}\rangle=m\,T\,[1-R_{0}^{2}(t)-\omega_{0}^{2}\,G_{0}^{2}(t)],

(63)

then

\displaystyle\!\!\!\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle=\frac{T}{m}\left\{1\!+\!\frac{2a}{m}\,G_{0}(t)\,R_{0}(t)\!+\!\left(\frac{a}{m\,\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}\!\!R_{0}^{2}(t)\right\}.

(64)

Taking the limit
t\to 0^{+}
we find that the velocity’s second moment is discontinuous,

\displaystyle\langle v^{2}(0^{+})\rangle=\langle v^{2}(0^{-})\rangle+\left(\frac{a}{m\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}\frac{T}{m},

(65)

where
\langle v^{2}(0^{-})\rangle=T/m
.
Written for the average kinetic energy

E_{k}(t)=m\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle/2
, this discontinuity relation takes the form
Eq. (
54
) and leads to an
observation that the mean work
\langle W\rangle
performed on the system
is collected by the system entirely in the form of the kinetic energy.

VI
Method II

One may worry about the validity of the method I exploited in Sec. IV, because it takes for granted the ad hoc ansatz
(
35
) for the discontinuous function
f(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=2\langle q(t)\,v(t)\rangle
. Also, solving the Langevin equation (
40
) using the Laplace transformation (
\mathcal{L}
), we tacitly used the relation
\mathcal{L}\{\delta(t)\,q(t)\}=q(0)
. This implies the continuity of
q(t)
at
t=0
, which is not obvious for the delta protocol.

In this section we apply another method which does not rely
on the ansatz (
35
) and does not assume the continuity of
q(t)
, but instead
adopts a specific representation of the delta function.
Namely, we assume that the stiffness
k(t)
is varied according to the piecewise-constant protocol

\displaystyle k(t)=\begin{cases}k_{0},&t<0,\\
k_{1}=k_{0}+a/\epsilon,&0\leq t<\epsilon,\\
k_{0},&t\geq\epsilon,\end{cases}

(66)

where
\epsilon,a>0
.
The perturbation has the form of a rectangular impulse of duration
\epsilon
and amplitude
a/\epsilon
.
In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, which will be applied later, protocol (
66
)
converges to the delta protocol.

The protocol (
66
) can be viewed as a sequence of two instantaneous quenches:

k_{0}\to k_{1}
at
t=0^{-}
and
k_{1}\to k_{0}
at
t=\epsilon
.
Accordingly,
the microscopic work has two contributions,

\displaystyle W=W_{01}+W_{10},

(67)

where, similar to Eq.(
5
),

\displaystyle W_{01}=\frac{k_{1}-k_{0}}{2}\,q^{2}(0^{-})=\frac{a}{2\epsilon}\,q^{2}(0^{-}),\quad W_{10}=\frac{k_{0}-k_{1}}{2}\,q^{2}(\epsilon)=-\frac{a}{2\epsilon}\,q^{2}(\epsilon).

(68)

In order to find
q^{2}(\epsilon)
, one
needs to solve the Langevin equation (
40
) for
t>0
with
k(t)=k_{1}

\displaystyle\ddot{q}(t)=-\omega_{1}^{2}\,q(t)-\int_{0}^{t}K(t-\tau)\,\dot{q}(\tau)\,d\tau+\frac{1}{m}\xi(t),

(69)

where
\omega_{1}^{2}=k_{1}/m
. Solving the equation with Laplace transforms, one finds

\displaystyle q(t)=q(0^{-})\,S_{1}(t)+v(0^{-})\,G_{1}(t)+L_{1}(t).

(70)

Here
L_{1}(t)
denotes the convolution

\displaystyle L_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\,\{G_{1}*\xi\}=\frac{1}{m}\,\int_{0}^{t}G_{1}(t-t^{\prime})\xi(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime},

(71)

and
the relaxation functions
S_{1}
and
G_{1}
are defined as in the previous section but now they corresponds to the stiffness
k_{1}
and frequency
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{k_{1}/m}
.
Then for the microscopic work

W=W_{01}+W_{10}
we obtain

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2\epsilon}\,[q^{2}-q^{2}(\epsilon)]

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2\epsilon}\left\{[1-S_{1}^{2}(\epsilon)]\,q^{2}-G_{1}^{2}(\epsilon)\,v^{2}-L_{1}^{2}(\epsilon)\right\}-\frac{a}{\epsilon}\left\{S_{1}(\epsilon)G_{1}(\epsilon)\,q\,v-[S_{1}(\epsilon)\,q+G_{1}(\epsilon)\,v]\,L_{1}(\epsilon)\right\}.

Here and below for brevity we use the notations
q=q(0^{-})
,
v=v(0^{-})
.

We are looking for the asymptotic form of the expression (
72
) in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
,
when the protocol (
66
) converges to the delta protocol.
According to Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
) (in which one needs to replace the subscript
0
by
1
), the initial values of the relevant functions are

\displaystyle G_{1}(0)=0,\quad\dot{G}_{1}(0)=1,\quad\ddot{G}_{1}(0)=0

\displaystyle S_{1}(0)=1,\quad\dot{S}_{1}(0)=0,\quad\ddot{S}_{1}(0)=-\omega_{1}^{2}\approx-\frac{a}{m\epsilon}

\displaystyle L_{1}(0)=0,\quad\dot{L}_{1}(0)=0,\quad\ddot{L}_{1}(0)=\frac{\xi(0)}{m}.

(73)

Then at small
t=\epsilon

the relaxation functions have the following asymptotic forms

\displaystyle G_{1}(\epsilon)=\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),\quad S_{1}(\epsilon)=1-\frac{a}{2m}\,\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),\quad S_{1}^{2}(\epsilon)=1-\frac{a}{m}\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),\quad L_{1}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(74)

Substituting into Eq. (
72
) yields to the leading (zero) order in
\epsilon
, i.e. in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, the expression

\displaystyle W=\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\,q^{2}-a\,q\,v.

(75)

This coincides with the result we obtained in Sec. IV with method I.

VII
Response

The system’s response to the delta perturbation, i.e. the dynamics for
t>0
,
is more convenient to consider with method I by solving
Langevin equation (
42
). Let us focus on
the mean square displacement
\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle
, which determines the response of the system’s potential energy.

Let us assume that at
t<0
the system is in thermal equilibrium with the distribution
\rho_{0}
, Eq. (
6
).
In Section V we obtained for
\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle
the result Eq. (
57
).
Let us re-write it as

\displaystyle\langle q^{2}(t)\rangle=\varphi(t)\,\langle q^{2}(0^{-})\rangle,

(76)

where
\langle q^{2}(0^{-})\rangle=T/k_{0}
is the equilibrium mean-square displacement (before the perturbation is applied) and the dimensionless response function
\varphi(t)

has the form

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=1-2\alpha\,S_{0}(t)\,\omega_{0}G_{0}(t)+\alpha^{2}\,[\omega_{0}G_{0}(t)]^{2}.

(77)

Since
G_{0}(0)=0
, the initial value of
\varphi(t)
is
1
for any memory kernel
K(t)
in the Langevin equation. The behavior of
\varphi(t)
for
t>0

depends on a particular form of the dissipation kernel
K(t)
. Below we consider two examples for which the relaxation functions
G_{0}(t)
and
S_{0}(t)
are available in closed form.

The response function
\varphi(t)
, defined by Eq. (

), for the

K(t)=\gamma\,\delta(t)
, for
\beta=\gamma/\omega_{0}=5.0
and
several positive (left) and negative (right) values of the perturbation
intensity
\alpha=a/(m\omega_{0})
.

As the first example consider the kernel

\displaystyle K(t)=\gamma\,\delta(t),\qquad\gamma^{2}-4\omega_{0}^{2}>0,

(78)

which corresponds to the Markovian Langevin equation for the overdamped oscillator. According to Eq. (
45
), the relaxation functions have the forms

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\,G_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4}}\left\{e^{-s_{1}(\omega_{0}t)}-e^{-s_{2}(\omega_{0}t)}\right\},\quad S_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4}}\,\left\{\frac{1}{s_{1}}\,e^{-s_{1}(\omega_{0}t)}-\frac{1}{s_{2}}\,e^{-s_{2}(\omega_{0}t)}\right\},

(79)

where dimensionless parameters
\beta,s_{1},s_{2}
are

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{\gamma}{\omega_{0}}>2,\quad s_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\,(\beta-\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4}),\quad s_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\,(\beta+\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4}).

(80)

The corresponding response function
\varphi(t)
, Eq. (
77
), for
\beta=5.0

is shown in Fig.
2
.
For positive
\alpha
, the behavior of
\varphi(t)
is remarkably different for smaller and larger values of
\alpha
. For
\alpha<\alpha_{0}\approx 9.6
,
\varphi(t)
first decreases, reaches a minimum, and then monotonically increases back to
1
. For
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
, after reaching a minimum,
\varphi(t)
develops a maximum, and then monotonically decreases back to
1
.
For
\alpha<0
,
\varphi(t)
behaves in a similar way for all values of
\alpha
:
It first increases,
reaches a maximum, and then monotonically decreases back to the initial value
1
.

Our second example is the kernel

\displaystyle K(t)=\frac{\omega_{*}}{2}\,\frac{J_{1}(\omega_{*}t)}{t}=\frac{\omega_{*}^{2}}{4}[J_{0}(\omega_{*}t)+J_{2}(\omega_{*}t)],

(81)

where
J_{n}(t)
are the Bessel functions of the first kind.
The kernel has the absolute maximum at
t=0
and for
t>0

it oscillates with an amplitude decaying with time as
t^{-3/2}
.
The generalized Langevin equation with kernel (
81
)
describes
a particle attached to a semi-infinite harmonic chain, which plays the role of the thermal bath
Weiss
, for the case when the mass of the particle and the masses of chain particles
are the same.
The frequency
\omega_{*}
has the meaning of the maximal frequency of the normal modes of the bath.

The oscillator described by the generalized Langevin equation with kernels like Eq. (

)
was studied in Ref.
Plyukhin
. Here
we consider only the special case when
the oscillator frequency
\omega_{0}
and the maximal bath frequency

are related as

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\omega_{*}/\sqrt{2}.

(82)

In that case the relaxation functions can be presented in closed form as follow

\displaystyle G_{0}(t)=\frac{2}{\omega_{*}}\,J_{1}(\omega_{*}t)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\omega_{0}}\,J_{1}(\sqrt{2}\,\omega_{0}\,t),\quad S_{0}(t)=J_{0}(\omega_{*}t)=J_{0}(\sqrt{2}\,\omega_{0}\,t).

(83)

Then the response function (

) takes the form

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=1-2\sqrt{2}\,\alpha\,J_{0}(\sqrt{2}\,\omega_{0}t)\,J_{1}(\sqrt{2}\,\omega_{0}t)+2\,\alpha^{2}J_{1}^{2}(\sqrt{2}\,\omega_{0}t).

remains close to the equilibrium value. For larger values of
\alpha

the minimal value of

Suppose the delta perturbation is followed by

. By choosing the moment of the quench

VIII
Conclusion

A special property of the instantaneous quench and delta protocols is that the microscopic work
in both cases does not depend on parameters describing stochastic dynamics, but is completely
determined by equilibrium statistics of the initial state. Intuitively, that might be expected on the ground that both protocols have effectively zero duration. However, in contrast to the instantaneous quench, in order to evaluate the work for the delta protocol one does need to take stochastic dynamics into account, though only on the asymptotically short time scale.
On that scale the properties of the relevant relaxation (correlation) functions are generic and do not depend on the specific form of the
dissipation kernel
K(t)
and statistics of noise in the generalized Langevin equation.

From that perspective it is clear that
the approximation of overdamped Langevin dynamics, which implies a coarse-graining of time, is inadequate to describe the delta protocol. The solution of the overdamped Langevin equation for the unperturbed oscillator

\displaystyle\gamma\,\dot{q}(t)=-\omega^{2}\,q(t)+\frac{1}{m}\,\xi(t)

(85)

is expressed in term of the exponential
relaxation function
S(t)=\exp(-\omega^{2}t/\gamma)
. The derivative of the latter at
t=0
is finite, whereas
beyond the overdamped approximation the derivative of the corresponding relaxation function is strictly zero,

\dot{S}(0)=0
, see Eq. (
73
) and also Eqs. (
79
) and (
83
) for specific examples.
The difference is essential.
Repeating the arguments of Section VI one finds that for the overdamped case the work diverges as the width
\epsilon
of the perturbation impulse goes to zero, which is, of course, an unphysical result.

It is but natural to ask
to what extent the idealized delta protocol can mimic realistic spike-like perturbations
with finite duration and amplitudes.
Consider, for instance, a Gaussian protocol

\displaystyle k(t)=k_{0}+\frac{a}{\epsilon\,\sqrt{\pi}}\,e^{-(t/\epsilon)^{2}},

(86)

which converges to the delta protocol when
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.
In that case solving the Langevin equation (
40
) analytically
appears to be not feasible. Instead, we can take into account that the Langevin equation
with the kernel (
81
) describes the terminal atom of a semi-infinite harmonic chain and
to study the response of this system with numerical simulation. In that way
we found that Eq. (
84
) describes the response reasonably well
already for
\omega_{*}\epsilon=0.1
(underestimating local maxima of the response function by less than ten per cent).
For

\omega_{*}\epsilon<0.01
we found that the response function
\varphi(t)
and the mean work
\langle W\rangle
for the delta and Gaussian protocols are practically indistinguishable.

Consider the specific setting with

\omega_{0}=\omega_{*}/\sqrt{2}
and
\alpha=a/(m\omega_{0})=\sqrt{2}
. For the delta protocol the first two moment of the dimensionless work

w=W/T
are
\langle w\rangle=1
and
\langle w^{2}\rangle=5
, see Eq. (
17
).
For the Gaussian protocol (
86
) the simulation (of about
10^{4}
runs) gives
the values

\langle w\rangle=\{0.99,\,0.96,\,0.91\}
and

\langle w^{2}\rangle=\{4.94,\,4.68,\,4.3\}

for
\omega_{*}\epsilon=\{0.01,\,0.05,\,0.1\}
,
respectively.

In this paper we assumed that dynamics of the system for
t>0
is governed by the generalized Langevin equation (
40
). Its derivations usually assume that at
t=0^{-}
the bath has equilibrated in the presence of the system. An alternative is that the bath is equilibrated separately, i.e. in the absence of the system. In that case the Langevin equation still can be derived under certain model assumptions
Zwanzig
;
Weiss
, but now it has somewhat different form

\displaystyle m\,\ddot{q}(t)\!=\!-k(t)\,q(t)\!-\!m\int_{0}^{t}\!K(t-\tau)\,\dot{q}(\tau)\,d\tau+\xi(t)-m\,K(t)\,q(0),

(87)

which involves
the additional term in the right-hand side
depending on
q(0)
(often referred to as the initial slip).
Using the same methods as above, one can show that
the Langevin equation (
87
) leads again to the result (
13
) for the work.

(A5)
