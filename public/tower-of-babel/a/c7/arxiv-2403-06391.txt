Towards verifications of Krylov complexity


Abstract
Krylov complexity is considered to provide a measure of the growth of operators evolving under Hamiltonian dynamics. The main strategy is the analysis of the structure of Krylov subspace 𝒦 M ​ ( ℋ , η ) \mathcal{K}_{M}(\mathcal{H},\eta) spanned by the multiple applications of the Liouville operator ℒ \mathcal{L} defined by the commutator in terms of a Hamiltonian ℋ \mathcal{H} , ℒ := [ ℋ , ⋅ ] \mathcal{L}:=[\mathcal{H},\cdot] acting on an operator η \eta , 𝒦 M ​ ( ℋ , η ) = span ​ { η , ℒ ​ η , … , ℒ M − 1 ​ η } \mathcal{K}_{M}(\mathcal{H},\eta)=\text{span}\{\eta,\mathcal{L}\eta,\ldots,\mathcal{L}^{M-1}\eta\} . For a given inner product ( ⋅ , ⋅ ) (\cdot,\cdot) of the operators, the orthonormal basis { 𝒪 n } \{\mathcal{O}_{n}\} is constructed from 𝒪 0 = η / ( η , η ) \mathcal{O}_{0}=\eta/\sqrt{(\eta,\eta)} by Lanczos algorithm. The moments μ m = ( 𝒪 0 , ℒ m ​ 𝒪 0 ) \mu_{m}=(\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{L}^{m}\mathcal{O}_{0}) are closely related to the important data { b n } \{b_{n}\} called Lanczos coefficients. I present the exact and explicit expressions of the moments { μ m } \{\mu_{m}\} for 16 quantum mechanical systems which are exactly solvable both in the Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures . The operator η \eta is the variable of the eigenpolynomials. Among them six systems show a clear sign of ‘non-complexity’ as vanishing higher Lanczos coefficients b m = 0 b_{m}=0 , m ≥ 3 m\geq 3 .

2
Orthonormalisation of operators in the Krylov subspace

Let us start with a brief review of the general setting of the Krylov complexity
along the line of the seminal work
[
1
]
and others
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
in order to introduce
proper notions and appropriate notation.
The orthonormalisation of operators in the Krylov subspace is the basic ingredient for the evaluation of
Krylov complexity.
For simplicity of presentation, the discrete quantum mechanical systems are discussed at the beginning.
That is, the basic vector space is assumed to be
\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}

in this section and up to §
5
.
Later in §
6
, infinite dimensional Hilbert space is introduced for the treatment
of the traditional quantum mechanical systems.

Let us begin with the notation.
The ordinary Krylov subspace
[
14
]
is spanned by a series of vectors generated by multiple
applications of a certain operator
\mathcal{H}
on a vector
v
,

K_{M}(\mathcal{H};v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{span}\{v,\mathcal{H}v,\mathcal{H}^{2}v,\ldots,\mathcal{H}^{M-1}v\}.

For the evaluation of Krylov complexity
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
,
a different type of subspaces is necessary.
It is spanned by a series of operators generated by multiple applications
of adjoint actions of a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}

on an operator, to be named as
\eta
throughout this paper,

\mathcal{K}_{M}(\mathcal{H};\eta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{span}\{\eta,\mathcal{L}\eta,\mathcal{L}^{2}\eta,\ldots,\mathcal{L}^{M-1}\eta\},

(2.1)

in which the Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}
denotes the commutator by the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
;

\mathcal{L}\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}[\mathcal{H},\eta],\quad\mathcal{L}^{2}\eta=[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{L}\eta],\quad\cdots.

(2.2)

The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
,
\eta
and other
operators
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{W}
etc are all
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrices with the complex components,

\mathbb{C}\ni\mathcal{V}_{x\,y},\mathcal{W}_{x\,y},\qquad x,y\in\mathcal{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{0,1,\ldots,N\}.

(2.3)

The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is a
positive semi-definite hermitian
matrix,

\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{H},\quad\mathcal{H}^{*}_{x\,y}=\mathcal{H}_{y\,x},

(2.4)

in which
\dagger
denotes the hermitian conjugation and
*
means the complex conjugation.
Throughout this paper the operator
\eta
is assumed to be a real diagonal matrix,

\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{diag}\{\eta(0),\eta(1),\ldots,\eta(N)\},\quad\eta(x)\in\mathbb{R},\quad x\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.5)

Two types of bra-ket notation are used. The bra
(\!(x|
and ket
|x)\!)
correspond to the
x
-th
unit vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{x}
in
\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
, that is,

(\!(x|y)\!)=\delta_{x\,y},\qquad(\!(x|\mathcal{V}|y)\!)=\mathcal{V}_{x\,y},\quad x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.6)

The orthonormal basis corresponding to the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
the bra
\langle n|
and the ket
|n\rangle
,

\langle m|n\rangle=\delta_{m\,n},\quad\langle m|\mathcal{H}=\langle m|\mathcal{E}(m),\quad\mathcal{H}|n\rangle=\mathcal{E}(n)|n\rangle,\quad\mathcal{E}(m),\mathcal{E}(n)\geq 0,\quad m,n\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.7)

The inner product of operators
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
is defined by the trace,

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{Tr}[\![\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}]\!]=(\mathcal{W}^{\dagger},\mathcal{V}^{\dagger})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}(\!(x|\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}|x)\!)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{X}}\langle n|\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}|n\rangle=(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V})^{*},

(2.8)

which is real if
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
are both hermitian or anti-hermitian.
The norm of an operator
\mathcal{V}
is denoted by,

|\!|\mathcal{V}|\!|=\sqrt{(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V})}=\sqrt{\text{Tr}[\![\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{V}]\!]}\geq 0.

(2.9)

It should be stressed that the Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}
flips from the right to left side and vice versa,
under the present definition of the inner product,

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{W})=\text{Tr}[\![\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{H}\mathcal{W}-\mathcal{W}\mathcal{H})]\!]=\text{Tr}[\![(\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}\mathcal{V}^{\dagger})\mathcal{W}]\!]=\text{Tr}[\![[\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}]^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}]\!]=(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}).

(2.10)

It is also obvious that
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{V})
vanishes if
\mathcal{V}

is hermitian or anti-hermitian,

\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}=\pm\mathcal{V}\Longrightarrow(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{V})=\pm\text{Tr}[\![\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{H}\mathcal{V}-\mathcal{V}\mathcal{H})]\!]=0.

(2.11)

The orthonormalisation of the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{m}(\mathcal{H},\eta)
(
2.1
),

\{\mathcal{O}_{n}\}
,
n=0,1,\ldots

is much simpler than the ordinary Gram-Schmidt orthonormalisation due to the built-in structure of

\mathcal{K}_{M}(\mathcal{H},\eta)
.
The orthonormalisation à la Lanczos
[
15
]
starts with

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\eta/|\!|\eta|\!|,\qquad|\!|\eta|\!|^{2}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\eta(x)^{2},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{0},\quad b_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|\!|\mathcal{W}_{0}|\!|=|\!|\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{0}|\!|,\quad\text{if}\ \ b_{1}\neq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{O}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{b_{1}}\mathcal{W}_{0},

(2.13)

and it goes on until a zero norm operator is produced. By the above property (
2.11
),
\mathcal{O}_{1}

is automatically orthogonal with the previous
\mathcal{O}_{0}
,

(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{O}_{l})=\delta_{j\,l},\quad j,l=0,1.

(2.14)

The next step is the orthogonalisation of
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{1}
with
\mathcal{O}_{0}
,

\mathcal{W}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{1}-b_{1}\mathcal{O}_{0},\quad b_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|\!|\mathcal{W}_{1}|\!|,\quad\text{if}\ \ b_{2}\neq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{O}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{b_{2}}\mathcal{W}_{1}.

(2.15)

The orthogonality
(\mathcal{O}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{1})=0
is obvious by (
2.11
) and (
2.14
).
By construction, the orthogonality
(\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{W}_{1})=0
holds. As

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{W}_{1})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{1})-b_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{O}_{0})=(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{O}_{1})-b_{1}=\frac{1}{b_{1}}(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{0})-b_{1}=0,

the orthonormality up to 2 is established

(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{O}_{l})=\delta_{j\,l},\quad j,l=0,1,2.

(2.16)

The flip property
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{W})=(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
(
2.10
)
plays an important role in the above and further calculations.
The process goes on as

\mathcal{W}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{k}-b_{k}\mathcal{O}_{k-1},\quad b_{k+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|\!|\mathcal{W}_{k}|\!|,\quad\text{if}\ \ b_{k+1}\neq 0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{O}_{k+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{b_{k+1}}\mathcal{W}_{k}.

(2.17)

It is easy to prove that
\mathcal{O}_{k+1}
is orthogonal to all the previous ones

(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{O}_{k+1})=0
,
j=0,1,\ldots,k
, by assuming the previous ones are
orthonormal

(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{O}_{l})=\delta_{j\,l},\quad j,l=0,1,\ldots,k.

(2.18)

It is obvious by construction
(\mathcal{O}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{k})=0=(\mathcal{O}_{k},\mathcal{O}_{k+1})
.
Likewise,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O}_{k-1},\mathcal{W}_{k})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{k-1},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{k}-b_{k}\mathcal{O}_{k}\bigr)=\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{k-1},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{k}\bigr)-b_{k}=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{k-1},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{W}_{k-1}\bigr)-b_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\bigl(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{k-1},\mathcal{W}_{k-1}\bigr)-b_{k}=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\bigl(\mathcal{W}_{k-1}+b_{k-1}\mathcal{O}_{k-2},\mathcal{W}_{k-1}\bigr)-b_{k}=b_{k}-b_{k}=0,

as
(\mathcal{O}_{k-2},\mathcal{W}_{k-1})=b_{k}(\mathcal{O}_{k-2},\mathcal{O}_{k})=0
by assumption.
For
j\leq k-2
, the same logic goes

\displaystyle(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{W}_{k})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{k}-b_{k}\mathcal{O}_{k-1}\bigr)=(\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{k})=\frac{1}{b_{k}}(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{O}_{j},\mathcal{W}_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b_{k}}(\mathcal{W}_{j}+b_{j}\mathcal{O}_{j-1},\mathcal{W}_{k-1})=0,

and the induction is complete.
\square

Remark 2.1

The orthonormalisation is complete when all the Lanczos coefficients
\{b_{n}\}
are determined.
The orthonormalisation stops at
\mathcal{O}_{k}
when
b_{k+1}
vanishes,
b_{k+1}=0
.
Two explicit examples of the stopped orthonormalisation will be shown in §
5.1
.
Since the Hilbert space is
\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
, the totality of the basis
\{\mathcal{O}_{n}\}
is less than
(N+1)^{2}
.

Remark 2.2

The orthonormal basis
\mathcal{O}_{n}
has the following structure

\mathcal{O}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{[\tfrac{n}{2}]}c_{j}^{(n)}\mathcal{L}^{n-2j}\mathcal{O}_{0},\quad\bigl(i^{n}\mathcal{O}_{n}\bigr)^{\dagger}=i^{n}\mathcal{O}_{n},

in which

The squares of the Lanczos coefficients up to

\mu_{2m}

\mu_{2m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\mathcal{O}_{0},\mathcal{L}^{2m}\mathcal{O}_{0}),\quad 0\leq m\leq n,\quad\mu_{0}=1.

(2.20)

For example
(cf
[
19
]
Table 3-2)

b_{1}^{2}=\mu_{2},\quad b_{2}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{4}}{\mu_{2}}-\mu_{2},\quad b_{3}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{2}(\mu_{6}-2\mu_{2}\mu_{4}+\mu_{2}^{3})}{\mu_{2}(\mu_{4}-\mu_{2}^{2})}-\frac{\mu_{4}}{\mu_{2}}+\mu_{2}.

(2.21)

Such formulas can be checked by considering the formal scaling properties,

\mathcal{H}\to\lambda\mathcal{H}\ \Longrightarrow\mu_{2m}\to\lambda^{2m}\mu_{2m},\quad b_{n}\to\lambda b_{n}.

(2.22)

4
Solutions of Heisenberg equation of motion

As shown in (
2.24
), the explicit form of the Heisenberg operator solution is essential
for the determination of the functions
\{\varphi_{n}(t)\}
.
Although the Heisenberg solution of the harmonic oscillator potential (
x^{2}
)
was known in the early days of quantum mechanics,
it was late 1970’s that those for four other potentials in one-dimensional quantum mechanics
were reported by Nieto and Simmons
[
21
]
–
[
24
]
.
They were for the potentials
x^{2}+1/x^{2}
,
1/\sin^{2}x
,
-1/\cosh^{2}x

and the Morse potential.
The term ‘sinusoidal coordinate’ was also coined by them,
meaning that
\eta
undergoes sinusoidal motion with frequencies depending on the energy.
About a quarter century after Nieto and Simmons, the list of exact Heisenberg operator solutions
was enlarged by Odake and myself to include many discrete quantum mechanics
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
25
]

and some multi-particle dynamics
[
26
]
.

The essence is the discovery
[
18
]
(this paper will be cited as I) that the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and the sinusoidal coordinate
\eta

of exactly sovable systems in the Schrödinger picture all satisfy a simple commutation relation

[\mathcal{H},[\mathcal{H},\eta]\,]=\eta\,R_{0}(\mathcal{H})+[\mathcal{H},\eta]\,R_{1}(\mathcal{H})+R_{-1}(\mathcal{H}).

(4.1)

in which
R_{0}(\mathcal{H})
,
R_{1}(\mathcal{H})
and
R_{-1}(\mathcal{H})
are polynomials
in
\mathcal{H}
of maximal degree 2, 1 and 2, respectively,
reflecting the power counting of
\mathcal{H}
on both sides.
Those
R_{i}(\mathcal{H})
’s may contain some system parameters but
not dynamical operators other than
\mathcal{H}
.
In terms of the Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}
, the above commutation relation reads

\mathcal{L}^{2}\eta=\eta\,R_{0}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{L}\eta\,R_{1}(\mathcal{H})+R_{-1}(\mathcal{H}),

(4.2)

which is obviously generalised to

\mathcal{L}^{m}\eta=\eta\,\mathcal{A}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{L}\eta\,\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{C}_{m}(\mathcal{H}),\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

with the obvious initial conditions

\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathcal{H})=1,\quad\mathcal{A}_{1}(\mathcal{H})=0,\quad\mathcal{A}_{2}(\mathcal{H})=R_{0}(\mathcal{H}),\\
&\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{H})=0,\quad\ \mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})=1,\quad\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})=R_{1}(\mathcal{H}),\\
&\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathcal{H})=0,\quad\ \,\mathcal{C}_{1}(\mathcal{H})=0,\quad\ \mathcal{C}_{2}(\mathcal{H})=R_{-1}(\mathcal{H}).\end{split}

(4.3)

By solving the recurrence relations,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m+1}(\mathcal{H})=R_{0}(\mathcal{H})\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H}),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{m+1}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{A}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+R_{1}(\mathcal{H})\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m+1}(\mathcal{H})=R_{-1}(\mathcal{H})\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{R_{-1}(\mathcal{H})}{R_{0}(\mathcal{H})}\mathcal{A}_{m+1}(\mathcal{H}),

one arrives at,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{m}\eta=

\displaystyle\,\eta\,\mathcal{A}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{L}\eta\,\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{C}_{m}(\mathcal{H}),\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}(\mathcal{H})=R_{0}(\mathcal{H})\frac{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})^{m-1}-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})^{m-1}}{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})^{m}-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})^{m}}{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})},

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{m}(\mathcal{H})=R_{-1}(\mathcal{H})\frac{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})^{m-1}-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})^{m-1}}{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})},\quad m\geq 1,

(4.7)

in which two operators
\alpha_{\pm}(\mathcal{H})
are the roots of the quadratic equation,

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{H})^{2}-R_{1}(\mathcal{H})\alpha(\mathcal{H})-R_{0}(\mathcal{H})=0,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{1}(\mathcal{H})\pm\sqrt{R_{1}(\mathcal{H})^{2}+4R_{0}(\mathcal{H})}\right),

(4.9)

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})+\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})=R_{1}(\mathcal{H}),\quad\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})=-R_{0}(\mathcal{H}).

(4.10)

They also satisfy the relations as shown in
[
18
]
(I.2.22) and (I.2.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(n+1)-\mathcal{E}(n)=\alpha_{+}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr),\quad\mathcal{E}(n-1)-\mathcal{E}(n)=\alpha_{-}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr).

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(n)-\mathcal{E}(n-1)=\alpha_{+}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n-1)\bigr),\quad\mathcal{E}(n)-\mathcal{E}(n+1)=\alpha_{-}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n+1)\bigr),

(4.12)

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n-1)\bigr)=-\alpha_{-}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr),\quad\alpha_{-}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n+1)\bigr)=-\alpha_{+}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr).

(4.13)

Summing up
\{\mathcal{L}^{m}\eta\}
leads to the exact Heisenberg operator solution of
\eta

[
17
,
18
]
,

\displaystyle e^{it\mathcal{H}}\eta\,e^{-it\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it)^{m}}{m!}\mathcal{L}^{m}\eta,

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\eta\cdot\frac{e^{i\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})t}-e^{i\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})t}}{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})}-R_{-1}(\mathcal{H})/R_{0}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(\eta+R_{-1}(\mathcal{H})/R_{0}(\mathcal{H})\bigr)\frac{-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})e^{i\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})t}+\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})e^{i\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})t}}{\alpha_{+}(\mathcal{H})-\alpha_{-}(\mathcal{H})}.

(4.14)

Strictly speaking,
\eta
on both sides represents the initial value at

of the time-dependent Heisenberg
operator.
It should be stressed that the derivation from (

) to (
4.4
)–(
4.7

and (

As shown above,
\eta

depending on the total energy.

A simple and useful information is extracted from the above expression (

),
the energy level dependence of the expectation value of the Heisenberg operator

, which is obviously time-independent

\langle n|e^{it\mathcal{H}}\eta\,e^{-it\mathcal{H}}|n\rangle=\langle n|\eta|n\rangle
.
Sandwiching (

) by
\langle n|
and
|n\rangle
and noting

,
one obtains

\displaystyle\left(\langle n|\eta|n\rangle+\frac{R_{-1}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)}{R_{0}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)}\right)\times\left(1-\frac{-\alpha_{-}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)e^{i\alpha_{+}\left(\mathcal{E}(n)\right)t}+\alpha_{+}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)e^{i\alpha_{-}\left(\mathcal{E}(n)\right)t}}{\alpha_{+}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)-\alpha_{-}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\Longrightarrow\langle n|\eta|n\rangle=-\frac{R_{-1}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)}{R_{0}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)},\qquad n\in\mathcal{X},

(4.15)

as the second factor is time-dependent and non-vanishing.

7
Summary and comments

For a group of exactly solvable compact discrete quantum systems, the moments of the
operators in a Krylov subspace spanned by a Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}:=[\mathcal{H},\cdot]

and the sinusoidal coordinate
\eta
are evaluated explicitly.
They provide the essential tool for measuring the growth of operators evolving under Hamiltonian
dynamics
[
1
]
.
Understanding of the complexity of exactly solvable quantum systems
would reveal the nature of quantum chaos by contrast.
The moments of exactly solvable
non-compact
discrete dynamics and ordinary one-dimensional
quantum systems are also evaluated explicitly by adopting Wightman inner product involving the Boltzmann factor.
These exactly solvable discrete quantum mechanical systems
can be regarded as a very special type of matrix models.

There are many exactly solvable ordinary quantum mechanical systems having finitely many discrete energy levels.
Among them, for example, the Morse potential and the soliton potential (
-1/\cosh^{2}x
) are
exactly solvable in the Heisenberg picture, too
[
18
]
and the formula

\mathcal{L}^{m}\eta=\,\eta\,\mathcal{A}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{L}\eta\,\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathcal{H})+\mathcal{C}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
(
4.4
) is
applicable.
It is quite natural to expect that their ‘complexity’ is qualitatively different from that of the systems
having infinitely many energy levels only.
I cannot apply the Lanczos algorithm to such systems as I do not know how to include the contribution of
the continuous energy levels in the operator inner product.

Another type of exactly solvable discrete quantum mechanical systems is also known
[
18
,
25
]

and they are also solvable in the Heisenberg picture.
Their eigenvectors contain the hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme,
the Wilson, Askey-Wilson, continuous (dual) (
q
) Hahn, Meixner-Pollaczek, Al-Salam-Chihara,
continuous (big)
q
-Hermite, continuous
q
-Jacobi (Laguerre) polynomials.
It is expected that the moments of these quantum systems can be evaluated explicitly in a similar manner.

Exact Heisenberg operator solutions are also known for a family of multi-particle dynamics,
the Calogero models based on any root systems
[
26
]
.
It is a good challenge to generalise the present method for multi-particle systems.

Four explicit examples of multivariate discrete orthogonal polynomials, the multivariate Krawtchouk, Meixner
and two types of Rahman polynomials, are constructed recently by myself
[
27
]
–
[
29
]
.
They are eigenvectors of respective Hamiltonians having nearest neighbour and other types of interactions.
Investigation of their complexity through Krylov orthonormalisation
would expose the contrast between integrable and chaotic multi-particle dynamics.

One of the motivations of the present research is to display the main ingredients of Krylov complexity
with the explicit description of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
and the operator
\eta
,
which is lacking in some reports.
