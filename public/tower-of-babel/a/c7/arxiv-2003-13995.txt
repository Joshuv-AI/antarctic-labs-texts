Geometrical framework for picture changing operators in the pure spinor formalism
Andrei Mikhailov, and Dennis Zavaleta

Abstract
It is well known in NSR string theory, that vertex operators can be constructed in various “pictures”. Recently this was discussed in the context of pure spinor formalism. NSR picture changing operators have an elegant super-geometrical interpretation. In this paper we provide a generalization of this super-geometrical construction, which is also applicable to the pure spinor formalism.

Introduction

Pure spinor formalism is very promising for studying strings in
AdS
, since it naturally
includes the Ramond-Ramond fields
Berkovits:2008ga
;
Berkovits:2008qc
.
However, the progress
has been slowed down by the lack of explicit formula
for vertex operators
(see
Berkovits:2000yr
;
Mikhailov:2011si
for definitions and
Bedoya:2010qz
for a simple example).
Recently, a promising new method was suggested in
Berkovits:2019rwq
. The main idea is to
construct the vertex in nonzero picture.

Picture changing operators were previously discussed in the context of pure spinor formalism
in
Grassi:2004tv
;
Berkovits:2006vi
. More generally, the target space picture changing
operators were essential in constructing the Chern-Simons actions in
Cremonini:2019aao
.

This means allowing delta-functions of the pure
spinor ghosts. It was shown in
Berkovits:2019rwq
, that the ansatz for 1/2-BPS vertex is simplified
in the -8 picture. Then, the vertex operator in picture zero can be obtained by applying picture
raising operators, as explained in
Martins:2019wwi
.
However, this picture-raising procedure is not the usual one
Friedan:1985ge
, because the pure spinor variables
are constrained to live on a cone. In particular, it does not immediately fit into the geometrical
framework of
Belopolsky:1997bg
;
Belopolsky:1997jz
.

In this paper we will develop a generalization of the approach of
Belopolsky:1997bg
;
Belopolsky:1997jz
, which does cover the construction of
Berkovits:2019rwq
;
Martins:2019wwi
.

The pure spinor target space can be considered a generalization of the odd tangent bundle
\Pi TX

over the super-space-time
X
(see Section
2.1
).
The generalization consists of imposing some constraints on the
coordinates in the fiber. If
X
has coordinates
x,\theta
, then
\Pi TX
has coordinates

x,\theta,dx,d\theta
. We consider a submanifold

C\subset\Pi TX
defined by some quadratic and linear equations — see Section
4

and
Movshev:2012gc
.
The idea is to construct the action of some “odd loop group”
\Pi TG
on
C
and then average over
its orbits, using the natural measure on
\Pi TG
.
The integration removes the delta-functions of the pure spinor ghosts, and the result is the picture
zero vertex operator.

One lesson from our study: it is useful to consider pure spinor vertex operators as,
roughly speaking, differential forms on space-time:

•

vertex operators are (pseudo) differential forms

The pure spinor variable
\lambda
is, in some sense,

d\theta
— the differential of the fermionic coordinate of the target space, with the pure spinor
constraint imposed
Movshev:2012gc
. The de Rham operator
d
is identified with the BRST operator
Q
.
In this language picture changing operators are geometrical
operations on forms, basically averaging over an orbit of some group. We want this operation
to commute with the action of
Q=d
. To achieve that, we take the group to be of the form
\Pi TG

for some Lie supergroup
G
, and construct its action in such a way that
d
agrees with
the nilpotent vector field of
\Pi TG
, as explained in Section
2.2
.
The operation of averaging is usually non-local, an integral transform.
But it becomes local if all integrations are absorbed by delta-functions.
We do not want to allow delta-functions of the target space
coordinates, because we consider vertex operators corresponding to smooth supergravity solutions.
Instead, we introduce delta-functions of
d\theta
, which means allowing “pseudo-differential forms”.
It seems that our construction requires
G
to be purely odd supergroup (in particular, abelian).
Basically, it is
{\bf R}^{0|8}
. If bosonic directions were present in
G
, then integration over them
would not be absorbed by delta-functions, leading to non-locality.

The main point of our approach is defining a structure of differential
{\bf R}^{0|8}
-module on
the space of vertex operators, Section
3
. The construction is nontrivial,
and “depends on some luck”, Section
5
. We hope that it would generalize to
the case of pure spinor superstring in AdS, but at this time we do not have such generalization.

It is also worth noting that this formalism has also been applied to the superstring in other dimensions (e.g.
D=3
in
Grassi:2004tv
)
where there are no pure spinor constraints and so the delta functions on these variables does not present
any problem.

Geometrical interpretation of picture changing

2.1
Reminder on odd tangent bundle
\Pi TX

When studying a supermanifold
X
, it is often useful to consider, for any
“test” supermanifold
S
, the space of maps:

{\cal F}_{X}[S]=\mbox{Map}(S,X)

(1)

(see J. Bernstein’s lectures in
Deligne:1999qp
, which are also available online at

https://www.math.ias.edu/QFT/fall/
).
This defines a contravariant functor
S\mapsto{\cal F}_{X}[S]
from
supermanifolds to sets.
This is the “functor of points”;
{\cal F}_{X}[S]
is called
S
-points of
X
.

If
X
is a supermanifold, then functor
\Pi T
in the category of supermanifolds is defined
as follows:

\mbox{Map}(S,\Pi TX)=\mbox{Map}(S\times{\bf R}^{0|1},X)

(2)

Functions on
\Pi TX
are called “pseudo-differential forms (PDFs) on
X
”.

In particular, for a Lie supergroup
G
, the odd tangent space
\Pi TG
is also
a Lie supergroup, which might be considered an odd analogue of the loop group of
G
.
Let
{\bf g}=\mbox{Lie}(G)
be the Lie algebra of
G
.
The Lie algebra of
\Pi TG
is usually called “cone of
\bf g
” and denoted
C{\bf g}
:

C{\bf g}=\mbox{Lie}\Pi TG

(3)

2.2
Picture raising operators

Suppose that a Lie supergroup
G
acts on a supermanifold

.
Then
\Pi TG
acts on
\Pi TX
. This means that pseudodifferential forms on

\Gamma\omega=\int_{g\in\Pi TG}\omega\circ g

(4)

We restrict ourselves to those

We observe that:

\displaystyle(\Gamma\omega)\circ g=0\quad\forall\quad g\in\Pi TG

(5)

\displaystyle d\Gamma\omega=\Gamma d\omega

(6)

Eq. (

Eq. (

This construction is usually applied to the case when

is purely odd,

take the simplest example

and
\Pi TG={\bf R}^{1|1}
with the target supermanifold

parametrized by
(Z^{M})

Suppose that

contains enough delta-functions to absorb the integration along
odd variables.
In this case the convergence of the integral in Eq. (
4

and moreover

is actually a local operation. To be explicit,
suppose that the Lie algebra
{\bf g}={\bf R}^{0|1}
is generated by the odd vector fields
\nu

\nu=\nu^{M}(Z)\frac{\partial}{\partial Z^{M}}

(7)

The action of the group
{\bf R}^{0|1}\times X\rightarrow X
is then given by:

(\epsilon,Z^{M})\mapsto Z^{M}+{\epsilon}\nu^{M}

(8)

The corresponding action of
\Pi TG
on
\Pi TX
is:

\displaystyle a\;:\;\Pi TG\times\Pi TX\;\rightarrow\;

\displaystyle\Pi TX

(9)

\displaystyle(\epsilon,d\epsilon,Z^{M},dZ^{M})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\mapsto}}\;

\displaystyle(Z^{M}+{\epsilon}\nu^{M}\,,\,dZ^{M}+d\epsilon\nu^{M}-\epsilon d\nu^{M})

(10)

The integral of Eq. (
4
) is:

(\Gamma_{\nu}\omega)(Z,dZ)=\int D(d{\epsilon})D({\epsilon})\ \omega(Z^{M}+{\epsilon}\nu^{M},dZ^{M}+d{\epsilon}\nu^{M}-{\epsilon}d\nu^{M})

(11)

For any supermanifold
M
, the odd tangent space
\Pi TM
has a canonical vector field
d
.
If we thing of
\Pi TM
as the space of maps
{\bf R}^{0|1}\rightarrow M
, then this

is the
infinitesimal shift in
{\bf R}^{0|1}
. Eq. (
6
) can be derived by observing that for
any
\omega
, a function on
\Pi TX

(d_{\Pi TG}+d_{\Pi TX})(\omega\circ a)=(d_{\Pi TX}\omega)\circ a

(12)

After integrating on the odd parameter
{\epsilon}
, we can arrive at the “usual” (in string theory literature)
expression for the PCO:

(\Gamma_{\nu}\omega)=[d,\Theta({\iota}_{\nu})]

(13)

where
\iota_{\nu}=\nu^{M}\frac{\partial}{\partial dZ^{M}}
.

This can be generalized to
G={\bf R}^{0|n}
and
\Pi TG={\bf R}^{n|n}
where
{\bf g}={\bf R}^{0|n}
is generated by
n
odd vector fields
\nu_{a}
with
a=1,\ldots,n
. Then the integral becomes

(\Gamma_{\nu}\omega)(Z,dZ)=\int\prod_{a}D(d{\epsilon}^{a})\prod_{a}D({\epsilon}^{a})\ \omega(g^{{\epsilon}^{a},d{\epsilon}^{a}}(Z,dZ))

(14)

where
g^{{\epsilon}^{a},d{\epsilon}^{a}}
is the flow corresponding to the infinitesimal transformation

(\epsilon^{a},d\epsilon^{a},Z^{M},dZ^{M})\mapsto(Z^{M}+{\epsilon}^{a}\nu_{a}^{M}\,,\,dZ^{M}+d\epsilon^{a}\nu_{a}^{M}-\epsilon^{a}d\nu_{a}^{M})

(15)

4.3
Solving the pure spinor constraint

The ten-dimensional gamma matrices can be decomposed to give the eight-dimensional ones and also chiral projectors

\displaystyle{}[{\gamma}^{i}_{{\alpha}{\beta}}]

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\sigma}^{i}_{a{\dot{b}}}\\
{\sigma}^{i}_{{\dot{a}}b}&0\end{array}\right)\ ,\quad[{\gamma}^{i{\alpha}{\beta}}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\sigma}^{ia{\dot{b}}}\\

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\

i_{a}={\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}}+\left.{\frac{\partial\lambda_{-}^{b}}{\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}}\right|_{\bar{\lambda}=const}{\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{-}^{b}}}+\left.{\partial dx^{m}\over\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}\right|_{\bar{\lambda}=const}{\partial\over\partial dx^{m}}+\left.{\partial dx^{+}\over\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}\right|_{\bar{\lambda}=const}{\partial\over\partial dx^{+}}

where the derivative
\left.{\frac{\partial\lambda_{-}^{b}}{\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}}\right|_{\bar{\lambda}=const}

), and
{\partial dx\over\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}
includes

on
\lambda_{+}
as well as dependence through
\lambda_{-}(\lambda_{+})

(see Eqs. (

), (

), (

)).
This vector field is tangent to the cone.
This is the same as to consider the vector field of Eq. (
53
) on
C

in coordinates
(\lambda^{a}_{+},\bar{\lambda}^{a}_{+},\bar{\lambda}^{a}_{-})
.

Next, we define:

l_{a}=[i_{a},d]=\Pi T({\nabla}^{+}_{a})+\left.{\frac{\partial\lambda_{-}^{b}}{\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}}\right|_{\bar{\lambda}=const}\Pi T({\nabla}^{-}_{b})

(55)

In deriving Eq. (
55
), the following observation is useful.
If
\upsilon
is an even vertical vector field on
\Pi TX

\mu_{*}\left(\iota_{\Pi T\Pi TX}([\upsilon,d_{\Pi TX}])\right)=\upsilon

(56)

where
\mu

Indeed, the RHS of Eq. (

as in Eq. (

We observe that
\lambda_{-}^{b}
is a linear function of
\lambda_{+}^{a}

{\frac{\partial^{2}\lambda_{-}^{a}}{\partial\lambda_{+}^{b}\partial\lambda_{+}^{c}}}=0

[l_{a},i_{b}]=0

and this implies

5.1
Type IIB supergravity vertex operator at picture
(-8)

In coordinates
y^{+}=x^{+}-2{\theta}_{+}^{a}{\theta}_{-a}
:

\displaystyle{\nabla}_{+a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\theta}_{-}^{a}}+4{\theta}_{+a}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{+}}\ ,\quad{\nabla}_{-a}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\theta}_{+}^{a}}

(59)

\displaystyle q_{+a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\theta}_{-}^{a}}\ ,\quad q_{-a}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\theta}_{+}^{a}}-4{\theta}_{-a}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{+}}

(60)

We have lowered their
+,-
superscripts such that
\nabla_{\pm a}:=\nabla_{a}^{\mp}
and
q_{\pm a}:=q_{a}^{\mp}
. These coordinates simplify the
{\theta}
-expansion of the dilaton superfield
\Phi(y^{+},{\theta}_{\pm})
since it satisfies
{\nabla}_{-}\Phi=0
. We have

\displaystyle\Phi(y^{+},{\theta}_{-})\;=\;{}

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle e^{ik_{+}y^{+}}\left(C+C^{a_{1}}{\theta}_{-a_{1}}+\frac{1}{2!}C^{a_{1}a_{2}}{\theta}_{-a_{1}}{\theta}_{-a_{2}}+\ldots+\frac{1}{8!}C^{a_{1}\ldots a_{8}}{\theta}_{-a_{1}}\ldots{\theta}_{-a_{8}}\right)

(61)

where the constants
C^{a_{1}\ldots a_{k}}
are bosonic (fermionic) when
k
is even (odd).

When working with the vertex operator corresponding to type IIB supergravity is useful to know the following identity

\displaystyle\prod_{b=1}^{8}{\nabla}_{+b}\left(e^{ik_{+}y^{+}}{\theta}_{-}^{a_{1}}\ldots{\theta}_{-}^{a_{k}}C_{a_{1}\ldots a_{k}}\right)\;=\;{}

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle(4\partial_{+})^{8-k}C^{a_{1}\ldots a_{k}}q_{-a_{1}}\ldots q_{-a_{k}}\left(e^{ik_{+}y^{+}}\prod_{b=1}^{8}{\theta}_{+}^{b}\right)

(62)

which in turn implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\partial_{+})^{8}}\prod_{b=1}^{8}{\nabla}_{+}^{b}\Phi(y^{+},{\theta}_{-})\;=\;

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{k=0}^{8}\frac{(4\partial_{+})}{k!}^{-k}C^{a_{1}\ldots a_{k}}q_{-a_{1}}\ldots q_{-a_{k}}\right]\left(e^{ik_{+}y^{+}}\prod_{b=1}^{8}{\theta}^{b}_{+}\right)

(63)

We start by considering the vertex operator in a
(-8)
-picture that corresponds to the type IIB supergravity scalar state

V_{-8}^{scalar}=({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})e^{ik_{+}y^{+}}\prod_{a=1}^{8}{\theta}^{a}_{+}{\delta}({\lambda}^{a}_{+})

(64)

and by applying the
q_{-a}
supersymmetry generators, we can generate the full type IIB supergravity multiplet using (
63
)

V_{-8}=({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})\frac{1}{(4\partial_{+})^{8}}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{8}{\delta}({\lambda}_{+}^{a}){\nabla}_{+}^{a}\right)\Phi(y^{+},{\theta}_{-})

(65)

5.2
Type IIB supergravity vertex operator at picture
(0)

We will now use the vector fields
\rho(l_{a})
,
\rho(i_{a})
constructed in Section
5
to
transform the supergravity vertex from picture -8 to picture 0. As we explained in
Section
2.2
and Section
3
, this amounts to computing the integral:

V_{0}:=\Gamma V_{-8}=\int\prod_{a=1}^{8}D(d{\epsilon}^{a})\frac{\partial}{\partial{\epsilon}^{a}}\exp\left({\epsilon}^{a}\rho(l_{a})+d{\epsilon}^{a}\rho(i_{a})\right)V_{-8}

(66)

The explicit expressions for these vector fields are:

\displaystyle\rho(l_{a})

\displaystyle=\Pi T({\nabla}^{+}_{a})+\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-}^{b}}{\partial{\lambda}_{+}^{a}}\Pi T({\nabla}^{-}_{b})=\left({\nabla}^{+}_{a}-2{\lambda}_{-a}\frac{\partial}{\partial dx^{+}}\right)+\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-}^{b}}{\partial{\lambda}_{+}^{a}}\left({\nabla}^{-}_{b}-2{\lambda}_{+b}\frac{\partial}{\partial dx^{+}}\right)

(67)

\displaystyle\rho(i_{a})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{+}^{a}}+2\theta_{-}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial dx^{+}}\right)+\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-}^{b}}{\partial{\lambda}_{+}^{a}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\lambda}_{-}^{b}}+2\theta_{+}^{b}{\frac{\partial}{\partial dx^{+}}}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle\rho(d)

\displaystyle=d

(69)

After some computation we can verify that these vector fields satisfy

[\rho(l_{a}),\rho(l_{b})]=[\rho(i_{a}),\rho(i_{b})]=[\rho(l_{a}),\rho(i_{b})]=0

(70)

Now we can integrate along the orbits of
\Pi T{\bf R}^{0|8}
, as in Eq. (
14
):

\displaystyle V_{0}:={\Gamma}V_{-8}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{a=1}^{8}D(d{\epsilon}^{a})\frac{\partial}{\partial{\epsilon}^{a}}\exp\left({\epsilon}^{a}\rho(l_{a})+d{\epsilon}^{a}\rho(i_{a})\right)V_{-8}\;=\;{}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{a=1}^{8}D(d{\epsilon}^{a})\rho(l_{1})\ldots\rho(l_{8})\exp\left(d{\epsilon}^{a}\rho(i_{a})\right)V_{-8}\;=\;{}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4\partial_{+})^{8}}({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})\rho(l_{1})\ldots\rho(l_{8})\left(\prod_{a=1}^{8}{\nabla}_{+}^{a}\right)\Phi(x^{+}-2{\theta}^{a}_{+}{\theta}_{-a},{\theta}_{-})

(71)

where in the last line we have used that the only
{\lambda}_{\pm}
-dependence of
V_{-8}
is through
{\delta}({\lambda}_{+}^{a})
which means that the integral on
d{\epsilon}
eliminates all deltas at once. To proceed with the computation first notice that there is no dependence on coordinate
dx^{+}
, so we can drop the
\frac{\partial}{\partial dx^{+}}
-part in
\rho(l_{a})
. We also change to coordinates
y^{+}=x^{+}-2{\theta}_{+}^{a}{\theta}_{-a}
to obtain

\displaystyle{\Gamma}V_{-8}\;=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\partial_{+})^{8}}({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})\prod_{a=1}^{8}\left({\nabla}^{+}_{a}+\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-}^{b}}{\partial{\lambda}_{+}^{a}}{\nabla}^{-}_{b}\right)\left(\prod_{b=1}^{8}{\nabla}_{+}^{b}\right)\Phi(y^{+},{\theta}_{-}){}

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\partial_{+})^{8}}({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R}){\epsilon}^{a_{1}\ldots a_{8}}\left[\frac{1}{8!}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{8}}+\frac{(4\partial_{+})}{6!2!}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{6}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{8}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{7}}_{+}}\right.{}

\displaystyle+\frac{(4\partial_{+})^{2}}{4!(2!)^{3}}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{1}}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{2}}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{3}}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{4}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{6}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{5}}_{+}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{8}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{7}}_{+}}+\frac{(4\partial_{+})^{3}}{3!(2!)^{4}}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{1}}{\nabla}^{+}_{a_{2}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{4}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{3}}_{+}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{6}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{5}}_{+}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{8}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{7}}_{+}}{}

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{(4\partial_{+})^{4}}{4!(2!)^{4}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{2}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{1}}_{+}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{4}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{3}}_{+}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{6}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{5}}_{+}}\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-a_{8}}}{\partial{\lambda}^{a_{7}}_{+}}\right]\left(\prod_{b=1}^{8}{\nabla}_{+}^{b}\right)\Phi(y^{+},{\theta}_{-})

(72)

We want to further transform this expression.
First, we substitute Eq. (
44
) for

\frac{\partial{\lambda}_{-}}{\partial{\lambda}_{+}}
.
Then, we anticommute all
{\nabla}^{+}
’s all the way to right and make use of:

{\nabla}^{+}\Phi=0

(73)

(Also, remember that
\nabla_{a}^{-}=\nabla_{+a}
and
\nabla_{a}^{+}=\nabla_{-a}
).
The computation uses some identities for the commutators of the SUSY-invariant derivatives,
namely Eqs. (
80
), (
81
) and (
82
) in Appendix Section
A
.
The result is the following expression for the type IIB supergravity vertex operator
V_{0}={\Gamma}V_{-8}
:

\displaystyle V_{0}

\displaystyle=({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})\left[\Phi+\frac{1}{(32\partial_{+})}\frac{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\sigma}^{ij}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}_{ij}{\nabla}_{+})\Phi\right.{}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2!(32\partial_{+})^{2}}\prod_{n=1}^{2}\frac{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\sigma}^{i_{n}j_{n}}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}_{i_{n}j_{n}}{\nabla}_{+})\Phi{}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{3!(32\partial_{+})^{3}}\prod_{n=1}^{3}\frac{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\sigma}^{i_{n}j_{n}}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}_{i_{n}j_{n}}{\nabla}_{+})\Phi{}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{1}{4!(32\partial_{+})^{4}}\prod_{n=1}^{4}\frac{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\sigma}^{i_{n}j_{n}}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}{({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})}({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}_{i_{n}j_{n}}{\nabla}_{+})\Phi\right]

(74)

This formula reproduces the result of
Martins:2019wwi
where the computation was performed using the PCOs in their standard form
\Gamma_{a}=[Q_{BRST},\Theta(\omega^{+}_{a})]
.
Our computation is, in some sense, more streamlined. In particular, we do not have any poles in
\lambda_{+}^{a}
. (The computation of
Martins:2019wwi
has such poles at the middle steps; they appear
each time a
\Theta(\omega^{+}_{a})
hit a
\delta(\lambda^{a}_{+})
.)

Still, our computation does suffer from the poles with denominators

(\bar{\lambda}_{L}\bar{\lambda}_{R})
. The only terms which could potentially have such denominators
are the third, fourth and fifth. Notice, however, that the fifth term is
({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})
times
an expression which contains
{\bar{\lambda}}_{L}
and
{\bar{\lambda}}_{R}
only through
the Pfaffian of the matrix
M
defined in Eq. (
45
). Since
M^{2}={\bf 1}
, the Pfaffian
is
\pm 1
. Therefore, this last term only depends on
{\bar{\lambda}}_{L}
and
{\bar{\lambda}}_{R}
through the
factor
({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})
. It is equal to:

\pm({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})\frac{1}{(16\partial_{+})^{4}}\nabla_{+}^{1}\nabla_{+}^{2}\cdots\nabla_{+}^{8}\Phi

(75)

The fourth term (the one containing six
\nabla_{+}
) is also nonsingular. Indeed, it follows from

\mbox{det}(M)=1
that its dependence on
{\bar{\lambda}}_{L}
and
{\bar{\lambda}}_{R}
is linear in
({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})M
.
The only term which could potentially have a pole is the third (middle) term, which contains four
\nabla_{+}
.
It could have a first order pole in
({\bar{\lambda}}_{L}{\bar{\lambda}}_{R})
. The cancellation of this pole is not obvious;
it was proven in
Martins:2019wwi
using certain identities for the
(\sigma^{j})_{a\dot{a}}
matrices.

Thus the zero-picture vertex operator is actually
of the form
V_{0}=\bar{\lambda}_{L}^{\dot{a}}\bar{\lambda}_{R}^{\dot{b}}A_{\dot{a}\dot{b}}(x^{+},\theta_{\pm})
.

6
Open Questions

1.

We constructed
some
action of
C{\bf R}^{0|8}
on the pure spinor target space.
How flexible is this construction? Is it in some sense natural?
Given
V_{-8}
, is there a
canonical
way to construct
V_{0}
?
(This question is very important for the computation of amplitudes.
If there is no
canonical
map
V_{-8}\rightarrow V_{0}
, then
using the -8 picture in computation of amplitudes is not apriori justified.)

2.

It is not immediately clear how to apply our method in AdS.
A naive analogue of our vector fields
i_{a}
and
l_{a}
from Section
5

is not well-defined in AdS (it would not be gauge invariant).

3.

The use of delta-functions on the cone is potentially dangerous.
We would want to understand, when generalized functions of the form of
products of
\delta(\lambda)
are well-defined.

Appendix A
Anticommuting
{\nabla}_{-}
and
{\nabla}_{+}

Here we will prove some formulas of SUSY-invariant derivative
\nabla
which
are needed to derive Eq. (

There are two relations that are useful when anticommuting the
{\nabla}_{-}
and
{\nabla}_{+}
operators. The first one which can be proven by induction is

\displaystyle{\nabla}_{-a_{k}}\ldots{\nabla}_{-a_{1}}{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{k}}

\displaystyle=({\nabla}_{-a_{k}}{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{k}}){\nabla}_{-a_{k-1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{-a_{1}}{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{k-1}}{}

\displaystyle\quad-(k-1)(4\partial_{+}){\nabla}_{-a_{k-1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{-a_{1}}{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{k-1}}

This equation is used to prove the second one

\displaystyle{\nabla}_{-a_{n}}\ldots{\nabla}_{-a_{1}}{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{n}}({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}^{kl}{\nabla}_{+})\;=\;{}

\displaystyle\;=\;

\displaystyle({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}^{kl}{\nabla}_{+})\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}C^{(n)}_{i}(4\partial_{+})^{n-i}{\nabla}_{-a_{i}}\ldots{\nabla}_{-a_{1}}{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{1}}\ldots{\nabla}_{+}^{a_{i}}\right)

(77)

where the numeric coefficients
C^{(n)}_{i}
with
i=0,\ldots,n
are found recursively starting with

C^{(1)}_{0}=-2\ ,\quad C^{(1)}_{1}=1

(78)

and the following ones with

\displaystyle C^{(n+1)}_{0}

\displaystyle=-(n+2)C^{(n)}_{0}

(79)

\displaystyle C^{(n+1)}_{i}

\displaystyle=(i-n-2)C^{(n)}_{i}+C^{(n)}_{i-1}\ ,\quad i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle C^{(n+1)}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=C^{(n)}_{n}

Using equations (
76
) and (

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\;=\;2!(4\partial_{+})^{2}({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}^{ij}{\nabla}_{+})({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}^{kl}{\nabla}_{+})

\displaystyle({\nabla}_{+}{\sigma}^{mn}{\nabla}_{+})\Phi

(80)
