Relative Ideal Classes of Arbitrary Order
David L. Pincus, Lawrence C. Washington

Abstract
. We adapt a known technique for searching for ideal classes of arbitrary order and then apply it to three families of number fields. We show that a family of cyclic sextic number fields has infinitely many fields in it that contain a relative ideal class of order r , r, where r r is a positive integer relatively prime to the degree of the extension. We then show that the same holds true for a family of cyclic quartic number fields. Though the technique is traditionally applied to Galois extensions, we show how it may be adapted to handle a family of non-Galois cubic number fields and prove that this family contains infinitely many fields with an ideal class of arbitrary order relatively prime to three.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathfrak{F}:=\{K_{n}\}
denote an indexed family of number fields and let
r
be a positive integer. Let
\mathcal{I}_{K_{n}}
denote the group
of fractional ideals of
K_{n}\in\mathfrak{F}.

Can we find a field
K_{n}\in\mathfrak{F}
that contains an ideal class of order

r?
We may answer in the affirmative if the following criteria are satisfied.

(1)

We can find an index
n
and a principal ideal
w{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}\in\mathcal{I}_{K_{n}}
that factors
as
w{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}=J^{r}
for some fractional ideal
J\in\mathcal{I}_{K_{n}}.

(2)

We can show that no smaller power of
J
is principal.

This sort of approach has been used by many authors going back at least as far as Nagell
[
7
]
.

A well-known method for meeting Criterion (
1
)
begins by looking for solutions to the equation

(1.1)

Y^{r}=f_{n}(X),

where
f_{n}(X)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
is the minimal polynomial of a primitive element for
K_{n}/{\mathbb{Q}}.

Indeed if
K_{n}/{\mathbb{Q}}
is Galois with primitive element
\rho,
then a solution

(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
to Equation (
1.1
) gives

(1.2)

y^{r}=f_{n}(x)=\prod_{i=0}^{[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]-1}(x-\rho_{i}),

where the
\rho_{i}
run over the Galois conjugates of
\rho:=\rho_{0}.
If one can show that
the exact power of a prime ideal that divides, say,
(x-\rho_{i}){\mathcal{O}}_{K}
is also the exact power of the same
prime ideal that divides
y^{r}{\mathcal{O}}_{K},
then one may conclude that
(x-\rho_{i}){\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}=J^{r}

for some ideal
J\in\mathcal{I}_{K_{n}}.

A well-known method for meeting Criterion (
2
) is then to assume
the contrary and suppose that
(x-\rho_{i}){\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}
is the
p
th power of a principal ideal, where

p
is a prime dividing
r.
Element-wise this implies that there is a
z\in K_{n}^{\times}

and a unit
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}^{\times}
such that

(1.3)

x-\rho_{i}=uz^{p}.

By restricting our consideration to those solutions
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
that have the property that
for each prime divisor
p
of
r
the prime divisors of
y

satisfy certain
p
th power residue conditions, we can ensure that for each prime

p\mid r,
Equation (
1.3
) fails to hold.

In this paper we extend the technique outlined above in the following three ways.

A.
If several of the (ideal) factors
(x-\rho_{i}){\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}
on the right hand side of Equation (
1.2
) factor as

r
th powers of ideals, then so do products and quotients of these ideals. By taking appropriate
products and quotients of such factors, we may obtain a principal ideal
that has trivial norm down to each proper subfield of
K_{n}
and which also
factors as an
r
th power, say as
J^{r}.
The ideal class of
J

then has trivial norm down to each proper subfield of
K_{n}
and is
called a
relative ideal class
. We use this technique in Section 2 (resp. Section 3) below to
search for relative ideal classes of order
r
in a family of sextic (resp. quartic) number fields. The qualifier

relative
is important. Had we simply been interested in searching for ideal classes of order
r,
then
it would have sufficed to look for a principal fractional ideal belonging to any of the proper subfields of
K_{n}
that factors as an
r
th power. The fact that the ideal classes we detect are relative ideal classes guarantees that these classes are
new
and not coming from ideal classes of any proper subfield.

B.
If
K_{n}/{\mathbb{Q}}
is non-Galois with primitive element
\rho\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}
and
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is such that

y^{r}=f_{n}(x),
then Equation (
1.2
) no longer holds in
K_{n}.

It is still true, however, that

y^{r}=f_{n}(x)=(x-\rho)q(x),

where
q(x)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}.
In particular, if for each prime ideal

\mathfrak{P}
dividing
(x-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}},
we have
q(x)\not\equiv 0\pmod{\mathfrak{P}},
then
we still have that
(x-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}=J^{r}
for some ideal
J\in\mathcal{I}_{K_{n}}.
We use
this technique in Section 4 to search for ideal classes of order
r
in a family of non-Galois
cubic number fields.

C.
Congruence conditions are not always sufficient to guarantee that
for each prime
p
dividing
r,

(x-\rho_{i})/u
doesn’t have a
p
th root in
K_{n}.
Likewise, congruence
conditions don’t always suffice to guarantee that for each prime
p\mid r,

there is no
p
th root of
w/u
in
K_{n},

where
w
is a product of powers of the
(x-\rho_{i}).
What the congruences can do, however, is limit the
possibilities for
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}^{\times}.
Eliminating the possibility that there is
a prime
p\mid r
such that
w/u
has a
p
th root in
K_{n}
can then be reduced to
showing that the polynomial
\varphi(X^{r})
is irreducible in
{\mathbb{Q}}[X],
where

\varphi(X)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
is the minimal polynomial of
w/u.
Since
\varphi(X^{r})
depends on both
r
and the parameter
n
indexing our families of number fields, proving irreducibility is not easy. In Sections 2 and 3 we make use of Hilbert’s Irreducibility Theorem
[
4
]
to do so.

Using these techniques we are able to show that each of the families mentioned above has
infinitely many fields that contain an ideal class of order
r
. In the case of
the sextic and quartic families, these are
relative
ideal classes.
In all cases
r
is a positive integer relatively
prime to the degree of the extension, but otherwise arbitrary. Although the methods we employ may be extended to handle the case when
(r,[K:{\mathbb{Q}}])>1,
the result clutters the
presentation and obscures the techniques we are trying to illustrate. Thus our presentation sticks to the
case in which
(r,[K:{\mathbb{Q}}])=1.

2.
A family of sextic number fields.

\begin{gathered}\rho_{0}=\rho\\
\rho_{3}=-(\rho+2)/(2\rho+1)\end{gathered}\qquad\begin{gathered}\rho_{1}=(\rho-1)/(\rho+2)\\
\rho_{4}=-(\rho+1)/\rho\end{gathered}\qquad\begin{gathered}\rho_{2}=-1/(\rho+1)\\

\mathfrak{F}:=\{K_{n}\mid n\neq 0,\pm 6,\pm 26\}.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{K}
denote the group

and for a fractional ideal

let

[J]

denote the unique cubic subfield
of

Then
[J]
is said to be a

k_{2}
and
k_{3}.

Note that
\rho_{i},

(i=0,\ldots,5)
is an algebraic integer if and only if

So it is also worthwhile to note that
K_{n}={\mathbb{Q}}(\rho_{0}/\rho_{3})
and that
\rho_{0}/\rho_{3}
is a zero
of the polynomial

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{n}(X)&:=(X-1)^{6}-(n^{2}+108)(X^{2}+X)^{2}\\
&=X^{6}-6X^{5}-(n^{2}+93)X^{4}-(2n^{2}+236)X^{3}-(n^{2}+93)X^{2}-6X+1.\end{split}

In particular,
\rho_{0}/\rho_{3}
and its Galois conjugates are algebraic integers for any

i.e., regardless of whether or
not
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}.
Since

N_{K\mid k_{2}}(\rho_{0}/\rho_{3})=1
and
N_{K\mid k_{3}}(\rho_{0}/\rho_{3})=1
the Galois conjugates
of
\rho_{0}/\rho_{3}
are, in fact, relative units of
K_{n}.

Our primary result is

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
r>0
be an integer with
(6,r)=1.
There are infinitely many fields

containing a
relative ideal class of order
r.

It is clear that
K_{n}=K_{-n},
since the zeros of
\varphi_{n}(X)
serve as primitive elements
for
K_{n}/{\mathbb{Q}}
and since
\varphi_{n}(X)=\varphi_{-n}(X).

As we make no additional assumptions regarding the injectivity of the parametrization of
\mathfrak{F}
given
by

it would be insufficient for us to show that there are infinitely many
n
for which

K_{n}
contains a relative ideal class of order

Instead we put most of our effort into first proving

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

be an integer with
(6,r)=1
and let

be a prime such that

Assume that

There is a field

(i)

There is an ideal

whose ideal class,

has order

(ii)

(i=2,3)

2.2

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

There are infinitely many primes
q\nmid 210r
for which

\mathcal{P}(X)

Since only finitely many primes can ramify in

with
\rho:=\rho_{0}.

Then

(2-\rho_{1})(2-\rho_{2})=\rho^{2}+(2+n)\rho+(4+3n)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K_{n}}.

Proof.

Since
f_{n}(X)=X^{3}+nX^{2}+nX-1,
we have that
\sum_{i=0}^{2}\rho_{i}=-n
and

\prod_{i=0}^{2}\rho_{i}=1.

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

If a prime
p\mid(6n+7)
and
\mathfrak{P}\mid p{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
is a prime common divisor of
(2-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K}
and

(2-\rho_{1})(2-\rho_{2}){\mathcal{O}}_{K},
then
p=37.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
4.3
, we find that
5n+12=(2-\rho_{1})(2-\rho_{2})+(2-\rho)(\rho+4+n)\in\mathfrak{P}.

We thus have that
5n\equiv-12\pmod{p}
and
6n\equiv-7\pmod{p}.
These hold simultaneously only if

p=37.

∎

Proposition 4.5
.

If
37\nmid(6n+7)
and
6n+7=y^{r}
for some
y\in{\mathbb{Z}},
then there is an ideal
J\in\mathcal{I}_{K}
such that

(2-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K}=J^{r}.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a prime ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
with
\mathfrak{P}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}:=p{\mathbb{Z}}
and with
\mathfrak{P}\mid(2-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K}.

Then
6n+7=f_{n}(2)=\prod_{i=0}^{2}(2-\rho_{i})\in\mathfrak{P}.
Hence
p\mid 6n+7
and since

37\nmid 6n+7,
we find that
\mathfrak{P}\nmid(2-\rho_{1})(2-\rho_{2}){\mathcal{O}}_{K}.
Let

\mathfrak{P}^{\nu_{\mathfrak{P}}}
denote the exact power of
\mathfrak{P}
dividing
(2-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K}.

Then it must also be the exact
power of
\mathfrak{P}
dividing
y^{r}{\mathcal{O}}_{K}=(6n+7){\mathcal{O}}_{K}=\prod_{i=0}^{2}(2-\rho_{i}){\mathcal{O}}_{K}.
Hence
r\mid\nu_{\mathfrak{P}}.

We now set

J:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textmd{prime ideals }\\
\mathfrak{P}\mid(2-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{P}^{\nu_{\mathfrak{P}}/r},

and note that
J^{r}=(2-\rho){\mathcal{O}}_{K}.

∎

In Proposition
4.12
we add sufficient conditions to those of
Proposition
4.5
to ensure that
[J]
has order
r.
We turn first to a sequence of
lemmas that we use in our proof of Proposition
4.12
. We gently remind the reader that

n
is odd.

Lemma 4.6
.

The algebraic integer

\mu:=\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)(1+\rho)+\rho^{2}

is a unit in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
with
N_{K\mid{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mu)=1.

Proof.

Let
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{Z}}.
A straightforward calculation shows that

N_{K\mid{\mathbb{Q}}}(a+b\rho+c\rho^{2})=(a^{2}c+ac^{2}-abc)n^{2}-(2a^{2}c+a^{2}b-2ac^{2}-ab^{2}-bc^{2}+b^{2}c)n+(a^{3}+b^{3}+c^{3}-3abc).
Substituting
a=b=(n+3)/2
and
c=1
into this expression yields

N_{K\mid{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mu)=1.
Since
\mu\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K},
the lemma is established.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

The unit
\mu\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
is totally positive, while the unit
\rho\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
is neither
totally positive nor totally negative.

Proof.

The unit
\rho
is a zero of
f_{n}(X).
As mentioned in
the introduction, two of the zeros of
f_{n}(X)
are
<-1,
while the third is
>0.
In particular,
\rho
is neither totally positive nor totally negative.
Since
\rho_{i}^{3}+n\rho_{i}^{2}+n\rho_{i}-1=0,

(i=0,1,2),
we see that

n=(1-\rho_{i}^{3})/(\rho_{i}^{2}+\rho_{i}).
Substituting this expression for
n
into

\mu_{i}=\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)(1+\rho_{i})+\rho_{i}^{2}
yields

(4.3)

\mu_{i}=(\rho_{i}+1)^{3}/(2\rho_{i})

The location of the
\rho_{i}
now confirms that each
\mu_{i}
is positive.
∎

Let
U:=\langle-1,\mu,\rho\rangle\leq{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}.
We will need to know that
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}:U]<\infty.
It will suffice to prove the following.

Lemma 4.8
.

The subset
S:=\{\mu,\rho\}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}
is multiplicatively independent.

Proof.

When
n\geq 7,
the zeros of
f_{n}(X)
satisfy
\rho_{0}\in(1-n,2-n),

\rho_{1}\in(-1-3/n,-1-1/n)
and
\rho_{2}\in(1/(n+1),1/n).
The regulator,
R,
of
S
is

R=\bigg|\log|\mu_{0}|\log|\rho_{1}|-\log|\rho_{0}|\log|\mu_{1}|\bigg|.

For
n\geq 7,
we have that
1+1/n<|\rho_{1}|<1+3/n
and

1/n<|\rho_{1}+1|<3/n.

Using the relation given in Equation (
4.3
), we conclude that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle>\frac{27}{2n^{2}(n+1)}=(3/n)^{3}/(2(1+1/n))

\displaystyle>|\mu_{1}|=|\rho_{1}+1|^{3}/(2|\rho_{1}|)

\displaystyle>(1/n)^{3}/(2(1+3/n))=\frac{1}{2n^{2}(n+3)}.

Since
1-n<\rho_{0}<2-n,
we have that
n-2<|\rho_{0}|<n-1
and

n-3<|\rho_{0}+1|<n-2.

Using Equation (
4.3
) again, we conclude that

(n-2)^{2}/2=(n-2)^{3}/(2(n-2))>|\mu_{0}|=|\rho_{0}+1|^{3}/(2|\rho_{0}|)>(n-3)^{3}/(2(n-1))>1.

Hence

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\log|\mu_{0}|\log|\rho_{1}|-\log|\rho_{0}|\log|\mu_{1}|

\displaystyle\geq\log\left(\frac{(n-3)^{3}}{2(n-1)}\right)\log(1+1/n)-\log\left(n-2\right)\log\left(\frac{27}{2n^{2}(n+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\log(n-2)\log\left(\frac{2n^{2}(n+1)}{27}\right)

\displaystyle>0,

and we conclude that
S
is a multiplicatively independent subset of
K_{n}
for
n\geq 7.

It may be verified by hand that
R\neq 0
when
n=5,
so that
\{\rho,\mu\}
is
a multiplicatively independent subset of
K_{n}
for all
n
under consideration.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}
be a prime ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
with
\mathfrak{P}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=p{\mathbb{Z}}.
If
\rho\equiv 2\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}

and
p\nmid\disc(f_{n}),
then
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/\mathfrak{P}:{\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}}]=1.

Proof.

Noting that

f_{n}(X)=(X-\rho)(X^{2}+(\rho+n)X+\rho^{2}+n\rho+n)
and making use of the fact that

\rho\equiv 2\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}
along with the monomorphism

\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{p{\mathbb{Z}}}[X]\hookrightarrow\frac{{\mathcal{O}}_{K}}{\mathfrak{P}}[X],
we see that

f_{n}(X)
factors as

(X-2)(X^{2}+(n+2)X+4+3n)
in
({\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}})[X].
Applying Dedekind’s Factorization Theorem,
we conclude that
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/\mathfrak{P}:{\mathbb{Z}}/p{\mathbb{Z}}]=1.

∎

Lemma 4.10
.

If a prime
p
is a common divisor of
6n+7
and
\disc(f_{n}),
then
p=37
or
p=47.

Proof.

The congruences
n^{4}-18n^{2}-27=\disc(f_{n})\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and

hold simultaneously only if

or
p=47.

∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
not divisible by
either
37
or
47
and such that
y^{r}=6n+7,

and let
p
be a prime dividing
r.
If there are primes
\ell_{i}\mid(6n+7),

(i=1,2),
and such that

(i)

(2\,|\,\ell_{1})_{p}=1
and
(3\,|\,\ell_{1})_{p}\neq 1,

(ii)

(2\,|\,\ell_{2})_{p}\neq 1
and
(3\,|\,\ell_{2})_{p}\neq 1,

then
p\nmid[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}:U].

Proof.

Choose prime ideals
\mathcal{L}_{i}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{K},

(i=1,2),
with
\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=\ell_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}

and such that
\rho\equiv 2\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.
Then by
equation
4.3

\mu\equiv 27/4\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.

If
p\mid[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}:U],
then there is a unit
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}\smallsetminus U

and
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with

We remark that

4.10
guarantees that
\ell_{i}\nmid\disc(f_{n})
and so
Lemma
4.9
then guarantees that
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/\mathcal{L}_{i}:{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}]=1.

If

then by making use of the fact that
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/\mathcal{L}_{i}:{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}]=1

along with the congruences for
\rho
and
\mu,
we find that
(2^{a-2b}3^{3b}\,|\,\ell_{i})_{p}=1.

We remind the reader that
p\neq 3,
since
p\mid r
and since

Hence upon applying condition
(i)
we conclude that
p\mid b
and hence that

Setting

we conclude that
(2^{a}\,|\,\ell_{i})_{p}=1.
Applying condition
(ii)
we find that
p\mid a

and so

Hence

Hence

and
u^{2}=\pm\rho^{a}\mu^{b}.
Since
\pm u^{2}\mu^{-b}
is either totally positive or totally

\rho

Furthermore, since both
\mu
and

are totally positive, we are forced to conclude that

If

Therefore

and so contradict condition

∎

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
(37\times 47,y)=1
and such that
y^{r}=6n+7.
If for each
prime
p\mid r
there are primes
\ell_{i}\mid(6n+7)

(i=1,2,3)
satisfying

(i)

(2\,|\,\ell_{1})_{p}=(37\,|\,\ell_{1})_{p}=1,
and
(3\,|\,\ell_{1})_{p}\neq 1,

(ii)

(37\,|\,\ell_{2})_{p}=1,

(2\,|\,\ell_{2})_{p}\neq 1,
and
(3\,|\,\ell_{2})_{p}\neq 1,

(iii)

(37\,|\,\ell_{3})_{p}\neq 1,

then
(2-\rho)=J^{r}
where
[J]
has order
r.

Proof.

Since the hypotheses of Proposition
4.5
hold, we have that

(2-\rho)=J^{r}
for some ideal
J\in\mathcal{I}_{K}
and so the order of
[J]
is

\leq r.
If it is strictly
<r,
then
(2-\rho)
is a prime power of a principal ideal for
some prime
p\mid r.
Supposing this to be the case and writing

(2-\rho)=(\beta)^{p},
for some
\beta\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K},
we have that

2-\rho=u\beta^{p}
for some
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}.
Since the hypotheses of Lemma
4.11
hold
we have that
p\nmid[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}:U].

Writing
1=gp+h[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}:U]
for some

we find that

2-\rho=u\beta^{p}=u^{gp+h[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{\times}:U]}\beta^{p}=(\pm\rho^{a^{\prime}}\mu^{b^{\prime}})^{h}(u^{g}\beta)^{p}=\pm\rho^{a}\mu^{b}(\beta^{\prime})^{p}.

For notational convenience, replace
\beta^{\prime}
with
\beta
and note that
the same relation holds in the other two isomorphic cubic number fields to conclude that

2-\rho_{i}=\pm\rho_{i}^{a}\mu_{i}^{b}\beta_{i}^{p}

(i=0,1,2).

Choose prime ideals
\mathcal{L}_{i}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{K},

(i=1,2,3),
with
\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=\ell_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}

and such that
\rho\equiv 2\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.
Then
\mu\equiv 27/4\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.

On the one hand,

(2-\rho_{1})(2-\rho_{2})=(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})^{a}(\mu_{1}\mu_{2})^{b}(\beta_{1}\beta_{2})^{p}=\rho^{-a}\mu^{-b}(\beta_{1}\beta_{2})^{p}\equiv 2^{2b-a}3^{-3b}(\beta_{1}\beta_{2})^{p}\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.

On the other hand,

(2-\rho_{1})(2-\rho_{2})=\rho^{2}+(2+n)\rho+(4+3n)\equiv 5n+12\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\equiv 37/6\pmod{\mathcal{L}_{i}}.

Hence

Lemma
4.10
guarantees that
\ell_{i}\nmid\disc(f_{n})
and so

4.9
then guarantees that
[{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/\mathcal{L}_{i}:{\mathbb{Z}}/\ell_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}]=1.

Thus
(2^{a-2b-1}3^{3b-1}37\,|\,\ell_{i})_{p}=1.
Applying condition
(i)
, we conclude that

Using this result along with condition
(ii)
, we find that

Since

divides

and

we deduce that

(37\,|\,\ell_{3})_{p}=1
and so contradict condition
(iii)
. We conclude that

(2-\rho)
is not a prime power of a principal ideal for any prime

and
hence that
[J]
has order
r.

∎

Having established sufficient conditions for a field
K\in\mathfrak{F}
to posses an ideal class of arbitrary nontrivial order

we now prove Proposition
4.2
by showing that for each such
r

there is a field

satisfying these conditions.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
p
be a prime dividing
r.
Let
L_{1}={\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{p},2^{1/p},37^{1/p})
and

where
\zeta_{n}
denotes a primitive

th root of unity. A prime

\ell
totally splits in

if and only if

and

(2\,|\,\ell)_{p}=(37\,|\,\ell)_{p}=1.
Similarly a prime
\ell
totally splits in
L_{2}

if and only if
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{2p}
and
(3\,|\,\ell)_{p}=1.
Since

L_{2}\not\subset L_{1},
we apply Bauer’s Theorem to conclude that there are infinitely
many primes
\ell

L_{1}
but not in
L_{2}.
Hence
there are infinitely many primes
\ell

(2\,|\,\ell)_{p}=(37\,|\,\ell)_{p}=1,
and
(3\,|\,\ell)_{p}\neq 1.
In other words,
there are infinitely many primes
\ell
satisfying condition
(i)
of Proposition
4.12
.
Analogous arguments show that there are infinitely many primes satisfying
condition
(ii)
and infinitely many primes satisfying condition
(iii)
of Proposition
4.12
.

For each prime
p\mid r,
choose a triple of primes
\{\ell_{i,p}\}_{i=1}^{3}
satisfying conditions

(i)
-
(iii)
of Proposition
4.12
making sure never to choose

or

Let

with

and such that

If

Since

Reducing this equation

∎
