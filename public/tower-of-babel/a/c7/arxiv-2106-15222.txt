Quasi-3-D Spectral Wavelet Method for a Thermal Quench Simulation
J.B.Jonas Bundschuh, L.A.M.Laura A.M. D’Angelo, H.D.G.Herbert De Gersem

Abstract
The finite element method is widely used in simulations of various fields. However, when considering domains whose extent differs strongly in different spatial directions a finite element simulation becomes computationally very expensive due to the large number of degrees of freedom. An example of such a domain are the cables inside of the magnets of particle accelerators. For translationally invariant domains, this work proposes a quasi-3-D method. Thereby, a 2-D finite element method with a nodal basis in the cross-section is combined with a spectral method with a wavelet basis in the longitudinal direction. Furthermore, a spectral method with a wavelet basis and an adaptive and time-dependent resolution is presented. All methods are verified. As an example the hot-spot propagation due to a quench in Rutherford cables is simulated successfully.

1
Introduction

A standard finite element method allows to discretize and solve differential equations on arbitrary geometries
[
1
]
. Attaining a sufficient resolution with a standard three-dimensional FE approach may be intractable within a limited computation time.
For certain geometries, however, an adapted discretization and simulation method may reduce the computational expense.
This work focuses on multi-scale problems featuring domains with a high ratio between the extend in one spatial direction, referred to as longitudinal direction, and the extend in the other spatial directions, referred to as cross-section, which are, moreover, the translationally invariant with respect to the longitudinal direction.
If the cross-section requires a high resolution, because it is e.g. very detailed, performing a standard finite element method would be computationally very expensive due to a large number of degrees of freedom. Usually, one would remedy this issue by reducing the model to a two-dimensional
[
2
]
or even one-dimensional
[
3
]
model by exploiting the symmetry planes.
Unfortunately, this simplification is no longer possible as soon as the physical behavior breaks the symmetry, e.g. for a longitudinally asymmetrical heat source in a thermal heat conduction problem.

The described configuration occurs in superconducting magnets in particle accelerators. These magnets struggle with the quench phenomenon, which is a sudden and local transition from the superconducting to the normal state
[
4
]
.
Quenches emerge randomly during continuous operation.
The affected regions normally cool down again and return to a superconducting state in doing so.
Sometimes, however, a quench leads to a thermal runaway and the magnet can take irreversible damage if protection measures are not taken in time
[
4
]
.
Sensitive safety margins lead to a high fault time of the accelerator
[
5
]
.
The further improvement of the protection systems necessitates the study of the transient effects inside the magnet by numerical simulations
[
6
]
.

An alternative to the FE approach is based on a quasi-3-D method, that becomes applicable by exploiting the geometrical symmetry of the model
[
7
,
8
]
.
Here, a two-dimensional finite element method in the cross-section is combined with a one-dimensional spectral method
[
9
]
in the longitudinal direction.
While this approach utilizes FE ansatz functions of lowest order, the order of the spectral element ansatz functions is chosen arbitrarily or appropriately for the problem at hand.
Hence, the quasi-3-D method is similar to an anisotropic
hp
-method
[
10
]
.

In earlier works, harmonic functions
[
7
]
and orthogonal polynomials
[
8
]
have been used as basis functions for the spectral method.
In this work, however, wavelets and the corresponding scaling functions are used as an alternative to these. A comparison of orthogonal polynomials and wavelets is made.
Wavelets are functions that are defined by properties within the multiresolution analysis
[
11
]
.
In this work, the Daubechies wavelets
[
12
]
are utilized.

Figure 1
:
Cross-section of cos-theta magnet for Future Circular Collider and High-Energy Large Hadron Collider. A stack of three Rutherford cables is highlighted in red
[
13

.

This paper is structured as follows.
First, an introduction to wavelets is given and a spectral method using wavelets is derived.
Then, the finite element method and the spectral wavelet method are combined into a hybrid quasi-3-D method.
Next, an adaptive resolution strategy is discussed for the spectral method.
Lastly, the methods are compared and employed to compute the heat conduction in a benchmark model representing a superconducting Rutherford cable as used in superconducting accelerator magnets
[
14
,
13
]
, see Fig.
1
.
Here, the asymmetrical heat excitation generated by a quench is simulated by an artificial heat source.
To obtain a realistic quench simulation, a magneto-thermal coupling is required, which would necessitate the use of vectorial edge shape functions
[
15
]
. These are more involved than nodal ones and beyond this work’s scope
[
16
]
.

2
Introduction to wavelets

2.1
General statements

The multiresolution analysis
[
11
]
consists of closed spaces
V_{j}
that are nested subspaces of each other, i.e. it holds that
V_{j}\subset V_{j-1}
. Furthermore, the spaces
W_{j}:=V_{j}^{\perp}\cap V_{j-1}
are defined as the orthogonal complement of
V_{j}
in
V_{j-1}
. Thus, it holds that

V_{j-1}=V_{j}\oplus W_{j}

(1)

and

W_{i}\perp W_{j}

(2)

for
i\neq j
. By applying (
1
) to itself, one gets

V_{j}=V_{J}\oplus\bigoplus_{k=0}^{J-j-1}W_{J-k}\,,

(3)

where
\oplus
denotes the direct sum. There are
scaling functions

\phi_{j,n}
and
wavelets

\psi_{j,n}
. They are equipped with a scale
j
and a translation
n
and are defined as

\displaystyle\phi_{j,n}(x)

\displaystyle:=2^{-\nicefrac{{j}}{{2}}}\phi(2^{-j}x-n)\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\psi_{j,n}(x)

\displaystyle:=2^{-\nicefrac{{j}}{{2}}}\psi(2^{-j}x-n)\,.

(5)

Within these definitions, the
mother scaling function

\phi
and the
mother wavelet

\psi
are used. These coincide with the scaling function and wavelet, respectively, for
j=0
and
n=0
.

The scaling functions and wavelets at fixed scale
j
constitute an orthonormal basis to the space
V_{j}
and
W_{j}
, respectively. To be more precise, it holds that

\displaystyle V_{j}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\spn\left\{\phi_{j,n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}}\,,

(6)

\displaystyle W_{j}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\spn\left\{\psi_{j,n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}}\,.

(7)

2.2
Daubechies-
N
kind wavelets

Figure 2
:
Scaling function
\phi(x)
(black and solid) and wavelet
\psi(x)
(blue and dashed) of Daubechies-
N
kind with
N=6
.

There is a great variety of different kinds of wavelets
[
12
,
17
]
. However, within the spectral methods of this work, scaling functions and wavelets of the Daubechies-
N
kind are used, because they are compactly supported and constructed in a way to have a maximum number of vanishing moments
[
12
]
. To be more precise, the wavelets have a number of
N
vanishing moments, i.e. it holds

\int x^{k}\psi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0

(9)

for
k=0,\dots,N-1
. Moreover, they are constructed such that they have a minimal support width for the given number of vanishing moments
[
18
]
.
The scaling functions used in this work are continuous for
N>1
. In
[
19
]
one can find estimates for the Hölder regularity for the scaling functions with
N=2,\dots,10
. For
N=2,3,4
, these are also determined in
[
12
]
. It follows that the scaling functions are continuously differentiable for
N=3,4,5
and two times continuously differentiable for
N=6,7,8
. The wavelets have the same regularity properties.
For
N=6
, scaling function and wavelet are depicted in Fig.
2
.

The differential equations to be solved in the subsequent sections are formulated on intervals. Consequently, for
N\geq 2
there are always functions that are partially inside and partially outside the interval. But a basis that is defined on the interval is required. The construction of such a basis as proposed in
[
20
]
is used in this work. Let
I=\left[a,b\right]
be an interval. For each boundary
N
boundary scaling functions and boundary wavelets are constructed, such that the number of vanishing moments is preserved. Inside the interval, the ordinary functions are used.

Because the boundary scaling functions and boundary wavelets can be written as a linear combination of scaling functions, they feature the same regularity.
For a sufficient small scale
j
, the scaling functions on the interval
I
are defined by

\hat{\phi}_{j,n}:=\begin{cases}\phi^{a}_{j,n}\,,\quad&n=0,\dots,N-1\,,\\
\phi_{j,n}\,,\quad&n=N,\dots,\mathcal{J}+N-1\,,\\
\phi^{b}_{j,n}\,,\quad&n=\mathcal{J}+N,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1\,.\end{cases}

(10)

Herein,
\mathcal{D}
indicates the number of basis functions on the interval and
\mathcal{J}
indicates the number of interior basis functions. With this definition, the space of scaling functions at scale
j
on the interval
I
can be defined as

V^{I}_{j}:=\left\{\hat{\phi}_{j,n}:n=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1\right\}\,.

(11)

The space of wavelets at scale
j
on the interval
I
, indicated by
W^{I}_{j}
, is defined analogously.

3
Spectral methods with wavelets-bases

As stated above, the quasi-3-D methods is a combination of a two-dimensional finite element method and a one-dimensional spectral method. The latter one, that uses a wavelet-basis, is examined in this section. It is exemplary developed for the one-dimensional heat equation. The partial differential equation together with the boundary conditions and the initial condition reads as

-\partial_{x}\big(\lambda(x)\partial_{x}\theta(x,t)\big)+c_{\mathrm{V}}(x)\partial_{t}\theta(x,t)=q(x,t)\,,

(12)

with
(x,t)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{t}}
,

\displaystyle\theta(x,t)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\mathrm{dir}}(x,t)\,,\quad

\displaystyle(x,t)

\displaystyle\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{dir}}\times(0,\infty)\,,

(13)

\displaystyle-\lambda(x)\partial_{x}\theta(x,t)

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{neum}}(x,t)\,,\quad

\displaystyle(x,t)

\displaystyle\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{neum}}\times(0,\infty)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\theta(x,0)

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}(x)\,,\quad

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in\Omega\,.

(15)

Here,
x
is the spatial coordinate in
\mathrm{m}
,
t
the time in
\mathrm{s}
,
\theta(x,t)
the unknown temperature in
\mathrm{K}
,
\lambda(x)
the thermal conductivity in
\mathrm{W}\text{/}\text{(}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}\text{)}
,
c_{\mathrm{V}}(x)
the volumetric heat capacity in
\mathrm{J}\text{/}\text{(}{\mathrm{m}}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{K}\text{)}
and
q(x,t)
the volumetric heat flux density in
\mathrm{W}\text{/}{\mathrm{m}}^{3}
. Furthermore, the problem is defined on the domain
\Omega_{\mathrm{t}}:=\Omega\times\left(0,\infty\right)
, where
\Omega
is the spatial domain. The boundary
\partial\Omega
is divided in
\Gamma_{\mathrm{dir}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{neum}}
, with
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{\mathrm{dir}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{neum}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{dir}}\cap\Gamma_{\mathrm{neum}}=\emptyset
, on which Dirichlet
\theta_{\mathrm{dir}}(x,t)
in
\mathrm{K}
and Neumann boundary conditions
g_{\mathrm{neum}}(x,t)
in
\mathrm{W}\text{/}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
are defined, respectively. The initial condition uses the initial value
\theta_{0}(x)
in
\mathrm{K}
.

Let for this section be
\Omega=(0,L)
. The temperature
\theta(x,t)
is then approximated by
\tilde{\theta}(x,t)
in
V^{\Omega}_{j}
for each time instance. The ansatz of method of lines leads to

\theta(x,t)\approx\tilde{\theta}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\mathcal{D}-1}u_{n}(t)\hat{\phi}_{j,n}(x)\,.

(16)

The Galerkin approach is applied to (
12
). Therefore, (
12
) is tested with the basis functions of
V^{\Omega}_{j}
. With the inner product
\left(u(x)\,,v(x)\right):=\int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, this leads to

\left(-\partial_{x}\big(\lambda(x)\partial_{x}\theta(x,t)\big)\,,\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\right)+\left(c_{\mathrm{V}}(x)\partial_{t}\theta(x,t)\,,\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\right)=\left(q(x,t)\,,\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\right),

(17)

for
m=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1
. After inserting (
16
) into (
17
), one receives the semi-discrete equation

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\partial_{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{sf}}(t)\,.

(18)

The used components are defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left(A^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}\right)_{m,n=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}

\displaystyle:=\left(M^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}};\,m,n}\right)_{m,n=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}}\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{sf}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(q^{\mathrm{sf}}_{m}\left(t\right)\right)_{m=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(u_{n}\left(t\right)\right)_{n=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}\,,

(22)

where

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\lambda(x)\partial_{x}\hat{\phi}_{j,n}(x)\partial_{x}\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\left[\lambda(x)\partial_{x}\hat{\phi}_{j,n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\right]_{0}^{L}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}};\,m,n}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}c_{\mathrm{V}}(x)\hat{\phi}_{j,n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(24)

\displaystyle q^{\mathrm{sf}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}q(x,t)\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(25)

Integration by parts is used in the calculation of the entries of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda}
. For homogeneous material properties,
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}
is a diagonal matrix due to the orthonormality of the scaling functions. Consequently,
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}
is a regular matrix and (
18
) is a system of ordinary differential equations.

For the boundary conditions to be satisfied, one can find a matrix
\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{sf}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times\mathcal{D}}
and a vector
\mathrm{\mathbf{b}}^{\mathrm{sf}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
such that the equation

\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}^{\mathrm{sf}}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{b}}^{\mathrm{sf}}

(26)

has to hold for every time instance. For discretizing (
18
) in time, an arbitrary time integration method can be used.

4
Quasi-3-D method

4.1
Definition of the domain and spaces

The quasi-3-D method shall be derived by means of the three-dimensional heat equation

-\nabla\cdot\big(\lambda(\mathbf{x})\nabla\theta(\mathbf{x},t)\big)+c_{\mathrm{V}}(\mathbf{x})\partial_{t}\theta(\mathbf{x},t)=q(\mathbf{x},t)\,,

(27)

where
(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{t}}
. With a two-dimensional and open domain
\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
I=(0,L)
, the spatial domain defined as

\Omega:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:(x,y)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{S}},\,z\in I\right\}

(28)

extends in
z
-direction from 0 to
L
. Moreover, it it assumed that the geometry is translationally invariant with respect to the
z
-direction. The boundary
\partial\Omega
is divided in three different parts

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{front}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:(x,y)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,,z=0\right\},\,

(29a)

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{back}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:(x,y)\in\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}\,,z=L\right\},\,

(29b)

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{side}}

\displaystyle:=\partial\Omega\setminus\left\{S_{\mathrm{front}}\cup S_{\mathrm{back}}\right\}\,.

(29c)

Next the related spaces need to be defined. Let

S_{m}:=\left\{v\in C(\bar{\Omega}):v|_{T}\in\Pi_{m},\;\forall T\in\mathcal{T}\right\}

(30)

be the space of polynomial finite elements of order
m
, where
\Pi_{m}
is the set of all polynomials up to order
m
and
\mathcal{T}
is a two-dimensional triangulation (see
[
1
]
). With this,

K^{j}:=\{v\in C(\bar{\Omega}):v(x,y,z)=f(x,y)g(z),\,f\in S_{1},\,g\in V^{I}_{j}\}\,.

(31)

Combining the bases of both spaces,
S_{1}
and
V^{I}_{j}
, a basis for
K^{j}
is given by

\left\{\hat{\kappa}_{j,m,n}=\nu_{m}\hat{\phi}_{j,n-1}:m=1,\dots,\mathcal{I},\,n=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}\right\}\,,

(32)

with
\nu_{m}\in S_{1}
,
\hat{\phi}_{j,n-1}\in V^{I}_{j}
and
\mathcal{I}
being the number of nodes in the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
. To make the following derivation clearer, the notation

\kappa_{j,k}:=\hat{\kappa}_{j,m,n}\,,\qquad k=n+(m-1)\mathcal{I}

(33)

is used to reduce the number of indices from 3 to 2. Thus, the basis for
K^{j}
can be written as

\left\{\kappa_{j,k}:k=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}\right\}\,.

(34)

4.2
Derivation of the quasi-3-D method

The quasi-3-D method is derived for the three-dimensional heat equation (
27
). The temperature is approximated in
K^{j}
. Using the ansatz of method of lines leads to

\theta(\mathbf{x},t)\approx\tilde{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}}u_{n}(t)\kappa_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\,.

(35)

The heat equation is tested with
\kappa_{j,m}
for
m=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}\mathcal{I}
. Together with (
35
), one receives the semi-discrete equation

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}_{\lambda}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\partial_{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}(t)

(36)

similar to (
18
). It is assumed that the material parameters can be written as
\lambda(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{xy}(x,y)\lambda_{z}(z)
and
c_{\mathrm{V}}(\mathbf{x})=c_{\mathrm{V},xy}(x,y)c_{\mathrm{V},z}(z)
. Then, the matrices can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda}+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda}

(37)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\,,

(38)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. The remaining matrices, i.e. the stiffness matrix of the two-dimensional finite element method and mass matrices of the spectral and finite element method, are defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left(A^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}\right)_{m,n=1,\dots,\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}}\,,

(39)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}

\displaystyle:=\left(M^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}};\,m,n}\right)_{m,n=1,\dots,\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left(M^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}\right)_{m,n=1,\dots,\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}}\,,

(41)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\left(M^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}\right)_{m,n=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}}\,,

(42)

with

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}}\lambda_{xy}(x,y)\nabla\nu_{m}(x,y)\cdot\nabla\nu_{n}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}A\,,

(43)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}};\,m,n}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}}c_{\mathrm{V},xy}(x,y)\nu_{m}(x,y)\nu_{n}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}A\,,

(44)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}}\lambda_{xy}(x,y)\nu_{m}(x,y)\nu_{n}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}A\,,

(45)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{sf}}_{\lambda;\,m,n}

\displaystyle:=\int_{I}\lambda_{z}(z)\hat{\phi}_{j,n}(z)\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

(46)

For homogeneous material properties,
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}
is symmetric and positive-definite because of

\bm{\upxi}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\bm{\upxi}=\sum_{m,n=1}^{\mathcal{I}}\xi_{m}\xi_{n}M^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}};\,m,n}=c_{\mathrm{V},xy}\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{\mathcal{I}}\xi_{m}\nu_{m}(x,y)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}A>0\,,

(47)

for
\bm{\upxi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
. Thus,
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}
is a regular matrix. Because
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{sf}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}
is regular as well,
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}
is non-singular due to the properties of the Kronecker product and thus, (
36
) is a system of ordinary differential equations.

Under the assumption that the volumetric heat flux density can be written as
q(\mathbf{x},t)=q_{xy}(x,y,t)q_{z}(z,t)
, the right hand side of (
36
) reads as

\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{Q3D}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{fe}}(t)\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{sf}}(t)\,,

(48)

with

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{fe}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(s^{\mathrm{fe}}_{m}(t)\right)_{m=1,\dots,\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}}\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{sf}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(s^{\mathrm{sf}}_{m}(t)\right)_{m=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}}

(50)

and

\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{fe}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{S}}}q_{xy}(x,y,t)\nu_{m}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}A\,,

(51)

\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{sf}}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{I}q_{z}(z,t)\hat{\phi}_{j,m}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

(52)

For the realization of the boundary conditions, the three different parts of the boundary, defined in (
29
), are considered individually. At the front and back side,
S_{\mathrm{front}}
and
S_{\mathrm{back}}
, this is done with the spectral method, and at the hull
S_{\mathrm{side}}
with the finite element method.

5
Adaptive resolution

In the spectral method in Sec.
3
, scaling functions at the same scale are used for the whole interval as basis functions. Thus, the resolution is constant for the whole interval as well.
When considering a very long domain in
z
-direction, a high resolution becomes computationally very expensive due to the large number of degrees of freedom.
With the adaptive resolution method derived in this section, a resolution depending on the location is chosen utilizing the wavelet transform, such that the overall accuracy is sufficiently high.
This can strongly decrease the required degrees of freedom.

Figure 3
:
Decomposition of
V^{I}_{0}
in spaces of scaling functions and wavelets of greater scale. The arrows denote a decomposition. The addition of approximations of a function in the boxed spaces yields the same result as approximating the function in
V^{I}_{0}
. Here,
j_{\mathrm{max}}=3
and
j_{\mathrm{min}}=1
.

The boundary basis functions (Sec.
2.2
) are chosen such that the main properties of wavelets still hold. In particular, it holds that
V^{I}_{j}\subset V^{I}_{j-1}
,
W^{I}_{j}={V^{I}_{j}}^{\perp}\cap V^{I}_{j-1}
and

V^{I}_{j-1}=V^{I}_{j}\oplus W^{I}_{j}\,.

(53)

This allows to decompose the spaces
V^{I}_{j}
. Fig.
3
shows an example of such a decomposition.
The space
V^{I}_{0}
is decomposed in
V^{I}_{1}
and
W^{I}_{1}
,
V^{I}_{1}
in
V^{I}_{2}
and
W^{I}_{2}
, and so on.
Approximating a function in
V^{I}_{0}
is thus equivalent to approximating the same function in the boxed spaces and adding the solutions.

Let
j_{\mathrm{max}}
and
j_{\mathrm{min}}
denote the maximum and minimum scale used in the approximation, respectively. The approximation is then equivalent to one with scaling functions at scale
j=j_{\mathrm{min}}-1
. The basis for the adaptive resolution is

\displaystyle\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\hat{\phi}_{j_{\mathrm{max}},k}:k=0,\dots,\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}-1\right\}

(54)

\displaystyle\cup\left\{\bigcup_{j=j_{\mathrm{min}}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\left\{\hat{\psi}_{j,\widehat{K}_{j,n}-1}:n=1,\dots,\eta_{j}\right\}\right\}\,.

(55)

It contains all basis functions of
V^{I}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}
(first set) to ensure that every point in the interval
I
is covered by at least one function. Moreover, it contains subsets of the bases of the spaces
W^{I}_{j}
for
j=j_{\mathrm{min}},\dots,j_{\mathrm{max}}
(second set). The information which of the latter are used is stored in the
\eta_{j}
-tuples
\widehat{K}_{j}=\left(\widehat{K}_{j,1},\dots,\widehat{K}_{j,\eta_{j}}\right)
. For these,
\widehat{K}_{j,i}<\widehat{K}_{j,i^{\prime}}
for
i<i^{\prime}
and
1\leq\widehat{K}_{j,i}\leq\mathcal{D}_{j}
for all
i
. How to choose the tuples is discussed later on page
5
.

The tuples are also used to define the truncation matrices

\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}_{j}=\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{\widehat{K}_{j,1}}\;\cdots\;\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{\widehat{K}_{j,\eta_{j}}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\eta_{j}\times\mathcal{D}_{j}}\,,

(56)

where
\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{\widehat{K}_{j,n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}_{j}}
is the
\widehat{K}_{j,n}
-th unit column vector. The basis
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
is used within a spectral method for the one-dimensional heat equation from (
12
). This yields the semi-discrete equation

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{d}}\partial_{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)\,.

(57)

Herein, the used vectors and matrices as well as
\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathrm{d}}
, the matrix used for the boundary conditions, are determined with the help of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)
. The subscript s stands for a static resolution, whereas d stands for a dynamic resolution. One would receive the static matrices and vectors if all wavelet basis functions in
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
were used, i.e. with
\widehat{K}_{j}=\left(1,\dots,\mathcal{D}_{j}\right)
for
j=j_{\mathrm{min}},\dots,j_{\mathrm{max}}
. Because of (
53
), these matrices and vectors can be derived from those from Sec.
3
at scale
j_{\mathrm{min}}-1
. With the transformation matrix

\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\[3.0pt]
&&\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}&\\
&&&\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}_{\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\eta\times\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}-1}}\,,

(58)

where
\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}_{\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\times\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}}
is the identity matrix and
\eta:=\mathcal{D}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}+\sum_{j=j_{\mathrm{min}}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\eta_{j}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,,

(61)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\,.

(63)

When changing the resolution, the matrices
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{d}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{d}}
have to be recalculated. Because
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{s}}
are known beforehand and
\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
is sparse, the computational costs for recalculation are low.

It remains to determine the tuples
\widehat{K}_{j}
, where
\left\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\right\rangle:=\left|\left(\cdot\,,\cdot\right)\right|
is used. Note that
\left\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\right\rangle
is not an inner product but an abbreviation. The initial condition
\theta_{0}(x)
is used at the beginning of the simulation:

k+1\in\widehat{K}_{j}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\left\langle\theta_{0}(x)\,,\hat{\psi}_{j,k}\right\rangle>\tau\,.

(64)

The basis function
\hat{\psi}_{j,k}
is used if the corresponding coefficient is greater than a tolerance
\tau>0
, i.e. if the basis function
\hat{\psi}_{j,k}
has a significant contribution to the solution.

Figure 4
:
Flow chart for adapting the resolution.

During the simulation it may occur that the resolution needs to be adapted. One has to distinguish between decreasing and increasing the resolution (see Fig.
4
). For the former case, after every time step those basis functions whose coefficients are smaller than
\tau
are removed from
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
. For the latter case, two different causes are considered. An increase of the resolution may be necessary due to the source term
q(x,t)
or heat flow. Because the source term is known beforehand, the required resolution can be determined as for the initial condition. The function
\hat{\psi}_{j,k}
is used if
\left\langle q(x,t)\,,\hat{\psi}_{j,k}\right\rangle>\tau
.

Because the solution of the partial differential equation is approximated with the basis
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
, it is challenging to detect where a higher resolution caused by heat flow is needed. One possible approach is presented in the following.

Whenever a coefficient in
\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)
changes from one time step to another by a certain factor
\alpha
, the resolution in the interval of the support of the corresponding basis function
\hat{\psi}_{j,k}
is increased. Therefore, the wavelets at scale
j-1
, whose support is a subset of the support of
\hat{\psi}_{j,k}
, are added to
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
, if
j-1
does not exceed the minimum scale
j_{\mathrm{min}}
, i.e. if
j>j_{\mathrm{min}}
. Assuming that
j>j_{\mathrm{min}}
, the scaling functions at scale
j-1
with the indices

\Lambda:=\left\{-N+1+2k,\dots,N+2k\right\}\cap\left\{0,1,\dots,L2^{-j+1}-1\right\}

(65)

are added to
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
, i.e.
\tilde{k}+1\in\widehat{K}_{j-1}
for all
\tilde{k}\in\Lambda
. The corresponding coefficients to these wavelets are initialized with 0. Hence, the added wavelets must not be removed from
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
for a time interval of length
\beta

because the coefficients may need a certain time to exceed the tolerance
\tau
. This approach has been implemented and is used for the results in Sec.
7.3
.

6
Adaptive Quasi-3-D method

The adaptive resolution method from the previous section is combined with a two-dimensional finite element method into an adaptive Q3D method. The adaptivity only takes place in the longitudinal direction, i.e. the two-dimensional mesh in the cross-section does not change. The method is derived for the three-dimensional heat equation (
27
) and on the domain
\Omega
defined in (
28
). The space for basis and test functions is

K^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}:=\{v\in C(\bar{\Omega}):v(x,y,z)=f(x,y)g(z),\,f\in S_{1},\,g\in\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\}\,.

(66)

In contrast to the quasi-3-D method, here the function
g
is chosen from the space
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
.
With
\left\{\chi_{n}:n=1,\dots,\eta\right\}
being a basis of
\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
, a basis for
K^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
is given by

\left\{\hat{\kappa}_{m,n}=\nu_{m}\chi_{n}:m=1,\dots,\mathcal{I},\,n=1,\dots,\eta\right\}\,,

(67)

with
\nu_{m}\in S_{1}
and
\chi_{n}\in\widehat{\varXi}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
.

Similar to (
36
), the semi-discrete equation is

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}_{\lambda}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\partial_{t}\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}(t)\,.

(68)

Herein, the matrices can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{d},\lambda}+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{d},\lambda}

(69)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{d},c_{\mathrm{V}}}\,.

(70)

With (
59
) and (
60
) and the definition

\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}:=\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{T}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\,,

(71)

where
\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{I}}
is the identity matrix, the matrices can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{s},\lambda}+\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathrm{s},\lambda}\right)\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

(72)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{fe}}_{c_{\mathrm{V}}}\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathrm{s},c_{\mathrm{V}}}\right)\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\,.

(73)

The middle term is independent of the resolution and has to be calculated only once at the beginning of the simulation.

Similar to the matrices, the right-hand side vector is

\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathrm{{AQ3D}}}(t)=\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathrm{fe}}(t)\otimes\mathrm{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)\right)\,.

(74)

The boundary conditions are realized at
S_{\mathrm{front}}
and
S_{\mathrm{back}}
with the adaptive resolution method and at
S_{\mathrm{side}}
with the finite element method.
The process of changing the resolution is very similar to the adaptive resolution method. Here, a separate resolution is calculated for every edge in
z
-direction in the same way as explained in Sec.
5
. The resolutions of all edges and the source term are then merged with each other to get a single resolution for all edges. Consequently, the matrix
\mathrm{\mathbf{Q}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}
can be recalculated and the simulation can proceed.

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}

(75)

compares the vector
\mathrm{\mathbf{u}}
with a vector

that contains the coefficients of the analytical solution.

8
Conclusion

A spectral method using Daubechies scaling functions has been formulated and verified. This has been combined with a two-dimensional finite element method with a nodal basis into a quasi-3-D method. With this method, a stack of three simplified Rutherford cables has been simulated successfully. A third method with an adaptive resolution in space and time has been formulated for the one-dimensional heat equation. This method has been extended to a quasi-3-D method in the same manner as the spectral method.
A comparison between the adaptive and associated non-adaptive methods showed a significant reduction of the number of basis functions, preserving the accuracy.
