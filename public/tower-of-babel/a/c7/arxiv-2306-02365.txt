Restrictions of Pure states to subspaces of C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras Thanks: The author was partially supported by an NSERC Discovery Grant.
Raphaël Clouâtre

Abstract
. Through the lens of noncommutative function theory, we study restrictions of pure states to unital subspaces of C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebras, in the spirit of the Kadison–Singer question. More precisely, given a unital subspace M M of a C ∗ \mathrm{C}^{*} -algebra B B , the fundamental problem is to describe those pure states ω \omega on B B for which E ω = { ω } E_{\omega}=\{\omega\} , where E ω E_{\omega} is the set of states on B B extending ω | M \omega|_{M} . In other words, we aim to understand when ω | M \omega|_{M} admits a unique extension to a state on B B . We find that the obvious necessary condition that ω | M \omega|_{M} also be pure is sufficient in some naturally occurring examples, but not in general. Guided by classical results for spaces of continuous functions, we then turn to noncommutative peaking phenomena, and to the several variations on noncommutative peak points that have previously appeared in the literature. We perform a thorough analysis of these various notions, illustrating that all of them are in fact distinct, addressing their existence and, in some cases, their relative abundance. Notably, we find that none of the pre-existing notions provide a solution to our main problem. We are thus naturally led to introduce a new type of peaking behaviour for ω \omega , namely that the set E ω E_{\omega} be what we call an M M - pinnacle set . Roughly speaking, our main result is that ω | M \omega|_{M} admits a unique extension to B B if and only if E ω E_{\omega} is an M M -pinnacle set.

This can be viewed as a generalized version of a question that has garnered sustained interest in the case where

-algebra

,

The starting point of this discussion is the simple observation that, in order for

When the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra

, then because all pure states on

In Section
2
, we explore the pure extension property for unital subspaces of general
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras. Despite it being a natural property, it does not seem to have been investigated in detail before (although it is alluded to in
[
18

). In Proposition

, we exhibit examples of subspaces with the pure extension property coming from the theory of matrix convexity and free spectrahedra.
We also illustrate in Example

In light of this, we must look elsewhere to answer our main question in general. A contractive net

\lim_{\lambda}\omega(e_{\lambda})=1

Excisions were first introduced and utilized by Anderson in

Subsequently, the idea of excision was fleshed out further in

[

(once again, different terminology was used in this last paper). Related concepts also appear in

In Section

for general unital subspaces, and relate it to Hay’s noncommutative peaking phenomenon for projections
[
43

. There is a unique projection
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}\in B^{**}
such that

\{\xi\in B^{**}:\omega(\xi^{*}\xi)=0\}=B^{**}(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}).

Building on deep work of Blecher, Hay, Neal and Read, we show in Corollary

that an excision for

if and only if

is a so-called

. This latter peak property is characterized in Proposition

whenever

is a peak projection. As we illustrate in Example

In spite of this, by analogy with the commutative case, one expects that alternative peaking phenomena may still be relevant. Let us recount some of the classical details that guide us throughout the paper.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space and let
A\subset\mathrm{C}(X)
be a unital norm-closed subalgebra that separates the points of

(equivalently,
\mathrm{C}^{*}(A)=\mathrm{C}(X)
). Then, the pure state

\omega
is given by evaluation at some point

(a)

E_{\omega}=\{\omega\}

(b)

\omega|_{A}
is pure.

(c)

: there is a function

\{x\}=\{y\in X:|a(y)|=1\}

The equivalence of (a) and (b) was mentioned above, while the implication (c)
\Rightarrow
(a) is straightforward. The crucial implication is (a)
\Rightarrow
(c), which relies on the following.

Bishop’s criterion.

There is

A clever trick of Bishop can be used to show that, for every unital norm-closed subspace

Going back to the general setting where

We are thus led to consider relaxations of this notion, a task that we undertake in Section

-peak sets/states

[

[

We give a characterization in Proposition

of those states

contains a non-empty

-peak set. We emphasize here that such noncommutative peak sets are much more restrictive than their usual classical counterparts, being contained entirely in

In Section

Let

2.
The pure extension property

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace. Recall that we say that
M
has the
pure extension property
in
B
if every pure state on
M
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
. A standard argument using the Krein–Milman theorem reveals that this is the same as requiring that every pure state on
M
admits a unique extension to a
pure
state on
B
.

As explained in the introduction of the paper, when the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
is commutative and
M
generates it, then
M
automatically has this property, since pure states on
B
are multiplicative. Of course, for more general
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, pure states are not always multiplicative, and thus the pure extension property imposes significant restrictions on the pair
M\subset B
. In particular, one might expect that the pure extension property is somewhat rare. It is certainly the case that the property is not automatic: David Sherman has found a
2
-dimensional operator system in
{\mathbb{M}}_{4}
for which this property fails
[
57
]
. (Here and throughout, for a natural number
n\geq 1
we denote by
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
the space of
n\times n
complex matrices.) Another example appears in
[
31
, Example 2.3]
.
Nevertheless, we can exhibit some naturally occurring examples where the property does hold.

2.1.
Spectrahedra and the pure extension property

For our first class of examples, we need to recall some definitions.

Let
S
be an operator system. For each
n\geq 1
, let
{\mathcal{W}}_{n}(S)
denote the set of all unital completely positive maps
\psi:S\to{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
. The sequence
{\mathcal{W}}(S)=({\mathcal{W}}_{n}(S))
is then called the
matrix state space
of
S
. In the language of
[
36
]
, this is a matrix convex set in the dual space of
S
.

Next, fix integers
d\geq 1
and
g\geq 1
along with self-adjoint matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{g}\in{\mathbb{M}}_{d}
. Put
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{g})
. For each natural number
n\geq 1
, we define the set
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}(n)\subset({\mathbb{M}}_{n})^{g}
to consist of those
g
-tuples
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{g})
of self-adjoint matrices satisfying

\sum_{k=1}^{g}A_{k}\otimes X_{k}\leq I.

The sequence
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}=({\mathcal{D}}_{A}(n))
is then called the
free spectrahedron
corresponding to
A
. It is a matrix convex set in
{\mathbb{C}}^{g}
. For more detail on free spectrahedra and their relation to matrix convexity, the reader may consult
[
46
]
and the references therein.

Given
X=[x_{ij}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
, we let
X^{\dagger}=[\overline{x_{ij}}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
. Accordingly, we put

{\mathcal{D}}_{A}(n)^{\dagger}=\{(X_{1}^{\dagger},\ldots,X_{g}^{\dagger}):(X_{1},\ldots,X_{g})\in{\mathcal{D}}_{A}(n)\}.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
S\subset B
be an operator system with
\mathrm{C}^{*}(S)=B
. Assume that the matrix state space of
S
is a free spectrahedron
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}
satisfying
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}(2)^{\dagger}={\mathcal{D}}_{A}(2)
. Then, any pure state on
S
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
, and this extension is a
*
-representation. In particular,
S
has the pure extension property in
B
.

Proof.

Let
\omega
be a pure state on
S
. Equivalently, this means that
\omega
is an extreme point of
{\mathcal{W}}_{S}(1)
.
Next, we may apply
[
39
, Theorem 6.1]
(see also the corrected version
[
38
]
) to conclude that
\omega
is a so-called Arveson extreme point of
{\mathcal{W}}(S)
, which implies the desired conclusion.
∎

Operator systems satisfying the conditions above can easily be constructed as follows. Start with a free spectrahedron
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}
such that
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}(2)^{\dagger}={\mathcal{D}}_{A}(2)
. Then, one can construct the operator system of continuous matrix affine functions on
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}
, which will have
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}
as its matrix state space; see
[
59
, Section 3]
for details.

2.2.
The joint numerical range

For our next class of examples, we need to introduce some notation.
Given finitely many elements
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
in a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
, we denote by
W(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
the convex subset of those points of the form
(\psi(b_{1}),\ldots,\psi(b_{n}))
for some state
\psi
on
B
.
It is easily verified that, given a state
\psi
on
B
, the point
(\psi(b_{1}),\ldots,\psi(b_{n}))\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is an extreme point of
W(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
precisely when
\psi
restricts to a pure state on the unital subspace of
B
generated by
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
.

We now show that certain “maximal” extreme points of the joint numerical range give rise to pure states with unique extensions. The following standard argument is well known to experts, but we record it as we lack an exact reference.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in B
be elements satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}b_{k}^{*}\leq I
. Assume that the extreme points of
W(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
lie on the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Let
M=\operatorname{span}\{I,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
, let
A\subset B
denote the algebra generated by
M
and let
D\subset B
denote the operator system generated by
\{ab^{*}:a,b\in A\}
. If
\omega
is a pure state on
M
, then there is a unique state
\psi
on
D
extending
M
. In fact, the state
\psi
satisfies

\psi(b_{k}d)=\omega(b_{k})\psi(d)\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(b_{k}db_{j}^{*})=\omega(b_{k})\psi(d)\omega(b_{j})^{*}

for each
1\leq j,k\leq n
and
d\in D
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be any state extension of
\omega
to
D
.
Since
\omega
is pure, we see that
(\omega(b_{1}),\ldots,\omega(b_{n}))
is an extreme point of

, and hence it lies on the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. By the Schwarz inequality we find

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\omega(b_{k})|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\psi(b_{k}b_{k}^{*})=\psi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}b_{k}^{*}\right)\leq 1

so that

\psi(b_{k}b_{k}^{*})=|\omega(b_{k})|^{2}=|\psi(b_{k})|^{2},\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

Applying

and
d\in D
we find

\displaystyle\psi(b_{k}d)

\displaystyle=\psi(b_{k})\psi(d)=\omega(b_{k})\psi(d)

and

\psi(b_{k}db_{j}^{*})=\psi(b_{k})\psi(d)\psi(b_{j})^{*}=\omega(b_{k})\psi(d)\omega(b_{j})^{*}

In particular,
\psi
is uniquely determined by
\omega
.
∎

The previous result should be compared with
[
9
, Theorem 3.1.2]
,
[
10
, Corollary 3]
,
[
25
, Theorem 3.4]
. As an application, we show how certain spaces arising naturally in multivariate operator theory have the pure extension property.

Example 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer and let
F^{2}_{d}
denote the full Fock space on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
. Let
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}\in B(F^{2}_{d})
denote the standard left creation operators, which satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{d}L_{k}L_{k}^{*}\leq I
. The closed unital subalgebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{d}\subset B(F^{2}_{d})
generated by
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
is Popescu’s non-commutative disc algebra
[
53
]
,
[
54
]
. It is easily verified that the set
\{ab^{*}:a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{d}\}
spans a dense subset of
\mathrm{C}^{*}({\mathcal{A}}_{d})
.

The symmetric part of the full Fock space
F^{2}_{d}
can be identified with the Drury–Arveson space
H^{2}_{d}
of analytic functions on the open unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}_{d}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}

[
10
]
(see
[
42
]
for a modern survey). If we denote by
{\mathcal{B}}_{d}\subset B(H^{2}_{d})
the norm-closure of the polynomial multipliers on this space, then there is a unital completely contractive homomorphism
\Gamma:{\mathcal{A}}_{d}\to{\mathcal{B}}_{d}
sending each
L_{k}
to the operator
Z_{k}
of multiplication by the
k
-th coordinate function (see for instance
[
28
, Theorem 2.2]
for a proof). In particular, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{d}Z_{k}Z_{k}^{*}\leq I.

By
[
10
, Theorem 5.7]
, we see that the set
\{ab^{*}:a,b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d}\}
spans a dense subset of
\mathrm{C}^{*}({\mathcal{B}}_{d})
. Furthermore,
[
10
, Theorem 5.7]
implies that the closed unit ball is contained in
W(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
, and
hence in
W(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d})
also. Conversely, the inequalities

\sum_{k=1}^{d}L_{k}L_{k}^{*}\leq I\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{d}Z_{k}Z_{k}^{*}\leq I

imply that the joint numerical ranges are contained in
\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}
. Therefore,

W(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d})=W(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})=\overline{{\mathbb{B}}_{d}}

and the extreme points of this set are precisely those on the unit sphere.

Finally, let
{\mathcal{L}}_{d}=\operatorname{span}\{I,L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}\}\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{d}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{d}=\operatorname{span}\{I,Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{d}
. Applying Proposition
2.2
, we conclude that
{\mathcal{L}}_{d}
has the pure extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}({\mathcal{A}}_{d})
, while
{\mathcal{Z}}_{d}
has the pure extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}({\mathcal{B}}_{d})
.
∎

A natural question that we are unable to answer at present is whether the algebras
{\mathcal{A}}_{d}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{d}
above have the pure extension property inside their respective
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras.

2.3.
The case of
2\times 2
matrices

In this section, we investigate the pure extension property for subspaces of
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
. We give a somewhat streamlined version of the argument given in
[
15
]
,
[
47
]
.

We start with a reduction.

Lemma 2.4
.

The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

Every
2
-dimensional unital subspace
M\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
satisfying
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)={\mathbb{M}}_{2}
has the pure extension extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
.

(ii)

Every unital subspace
M\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
has the pure extension extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
.

Proof.

Assume that (i) holds and let
M\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
be a unital subspace. We must show that
M
has the pure extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
.

If
\dim M=1
, then
M={\mathbb{C}}I=\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
and the desired conclusion is trivial. Likewise, if
\dim M=4
then
M=\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
, and once again the conclusion is trivial. It thus only remains to deal with the case where
\dim M=3
.

Let
S=M+M^{*}\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
. It is well known that any state on
M
has a unique extension to a state on
S
. We may therefore assume without loss of generality that
S
is a proper subspace of
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
. In particular,
\dim S<4
and we must have
\dim M=\dim S=3
, so
M=S
and
M
is in fact self-adjoint.

Thus, there are self-adjoint elements
g,h\in M
such that
M=\operatorname{span}\{I,g,h\}
. Put
a=g+ih\in M
. Then,
M=N+N^{*}
, where
N=\operatorname{span}\{I,a\}
. We claim that
N
has the pure extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(N)=\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
. To see this, observe that
N
has dimension
2
. If
\mathrm{C}^{*}(N)\cong{\mathbb{C}}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}
, then the claim holds trivially. The alternative is when
\mathrm{C}^{*}(N)={\mathbb{M}}_{2}
, in which case the claim follows from our assumption.

Finally, observe that a pure state on
M
restricts to a pure state on
N
, so that
M
also has the pure extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
.
∎

We can now proceed to show that the pure extension property is always satisfied in
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
M\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
be a unital subspace. Then,
M
has the pure extension property in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
.

Proof.

By virtue of Lemma
2.4
, we may assume that
M
has dimension
2
and that
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)={\mathbb{M}}_{2}
. Choose an element
g\in M
such that
M=\operatorname{span}\{I,g\}
. Up to unitary equivalence, we may assume that
g
is upper triangular, so that

g=\begin{bmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}\\
\ 0&g_{22}\end{bmatrix}

for some complex numbers
g_{11},g_{12},g_{22}
. Note that
g_{12}\neq 0
, for otherwise
\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
is commutative, contrary to our assumption.
Thus, replacing
g
by

\frac{1}{g_{12}}g-\frac{g_{22}}{g_{12}}I

we may assume that

g=\begin{bmatrix}te^{i\gamma}&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

for some
t\geq 0
and
0\leq\gamma<2\pi
.

To establish the desired result, we fix a pure state
\omega
on
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
whose restriction to
M
is pure, and we aim to show that
\omega
is uniquely determined by the number
\lambda=\omega(g)
.

Consider the numerical range of
g
, namely

W=\{\varphi(g):\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{M}}_{2})\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}.

The pure states on
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
are precisely the vector states, so by the Krein–Milman theorem we see that
W
coincides with the convex hull in
{\mathbb{C}}
of the numbers of the form
\langle g\xi,\xi\rangle
where
\xi=(s,e^{i\alpha}\sqrt{1-s^{2}})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
for some
0\leq s\leq 1
and
0\leq\alpha<2\pi
. In other words,
W
is the convex hull in
{\mathbb{C}}
of

\{te^{i\gamma}s^{2}+se^{i\alpha}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}:0\leq s\leq 1,0\leq\alpha<2\pi\}.

Let
0\leq r\leq 1
and
0\leq\theta<2\pi
be chosen such that
\omega
is the vector state corresponding to
(r,e^{i\theta}\sqrt{1-r^{2}})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Our goal now becomes to show that
r
and
\theta
are uniquely determined by
\lambda
.

Since
\omega
is pure on
M
, the number

\lambda=\omega(g)=te^{i\gamma}r^{2}+re^{i\theta}\sqrt{1-r^{2}}

is an extreme point of
W
. On the other hand, it is known that
W
is an ellipse (see for instance
[
49
]
), so that
\lambda
is an exposed point of
W
, in the sense that there is a supporting line for
W
that intersects
W
only at
\lambda
. In other words, there is a complex number
z
such that

(1)

\operatorname{Re}(z\lambda)>\operatorname{Re}(z\mu)

for every
\mu\in W
distinct from
\lambda
.
Choose
\rho>0
and
0\leq\beta<2\pi
such that
z=\rho e^{i\beta}
.

Define now a function
f:[0,2\pi)\times[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

f(\alpha,s)=\operatorname{Re}\left(z\left\langle g\begin{pmatrix}s\\
e^{i\alpha}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}s\\
e^{i\alpha}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\right)

for each
\alpha\in[0,2\pi),s\in[0,1]
. By virtue of (
1
), to show that
r
and
\theta
are uniquely determined by
\lambda
, it suffices to show that
f
attains its global maximum at a unique point
(\alpha_{*},s_{*})
.

We compute for
0\leq\alpha<2\pi
and
0\leq s\leq 1
that

\displaystyle f(\alpha,s)

\displaystyle=\rho(s^{2}t\cos(\gamma+\beta)+s\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\cos(\alpha+\beta)).

Let now
\alpha_{*}\in[0,2\pi)
be the unique number such that
\cos(\alpha_{*}+\beta)=1
. Then, for each
0\leq\alpha<2\pi
distinct from
\alpha_{*}
we see that

f(\alpha,s)<f(\alpha_{*},s),\quad 0\leq s\leq 1.

Define
F:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

F(s)=f(\alpha_{*},s),\quad 0\leq s\leq 1.

We need to show that
F
attains its maximum at a unique point
s_{*}\in[0,1]
. Differentiating, for
0<s<1
, we find

F^{\prime}(s)=\rho\left(2ts\cos(\gamma+\beta)+\frac{1-2s^{2}}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}\right)

so that
F^{\prime}(s)=0
if and only if

(2)

2ts\cos(\gamma+\beta)=\frac{2s^{2}-1}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}.

This equation implies

Cs^{4}-Cs^{2}+1=0

where
C=4(1+t^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma+\beta))
, so that

s^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C-4}{C}}.

On the other hand, since
0<s<1
, we note from (
2
) that
s^{2}-1/2
has the same sign as
\cos(\gamma+\beta)
.

When
\cos(\gamma+\beta)\geq 0
, we see that

s_{+}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C-4}{C}}}

is the only possible critical point of
F
in
(0,1)
and

F(s_{+})=\frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{C-4}+\sqrt{C})

so

F(0)=0<F(1)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{C-4}<F(s_{+}).

When
\cos(\gamma+\beta)<0
, we see that

s_{-}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C-4}{C}}}

is the only possible critical point of
F
in
(0,1)
and

F(s_{-})=\frac{1}{4}(-\sqrt{C-4}+\sqrt{C})

so

F(1)=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{C-4}<F(0)=0<F(s_{-}).

In either case, we see that the maximum value of
F
is attained at a unique point in
[0,1]
, as desired.
∎

In summary, any unital subspace of
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
has the pure extension property, while there are subspaces of
{\mathbb{M}}_{4}
without this property
[
57
]
. We do not know what happens for subspaces in
{\mathbb{M}}_{3}
.

3.
Excisions

The previous section illustrates that in order to obtain a complete answer to our main question, one must look further than the pure extension property. In this section, we draw inspiration from another source, based on the following notion.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
\omega
be a state on
B
. A contractive net
(e_{\lambda})
in
B
is an
excision
for
\omega
if
\lim_{\lambda}\omega(e_{\lambda})=1
and

\lim_{\lambda}\|e^{*}_{\lambda}(b-\omega(b)I)e_{\lambda}\|=0,\quad b\in B.

Equivalently, the second condition means that

\limsup_{\lambda}\|e_{\lambda}^{*}be_{\lambda}\|\leq|\omega(b)|,\quad b\in B.

Replacing nets with sequences, this notion was studied in
[
29
]
under the name “characteristic sequence”. The argument used to prove
[
29
, Theorem 4.2]
adapts verbatim to show that the state
\omega
is automatically pure if it admits an excision; this fact will be used below.

We record the following basic well-known facts.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
. If
\xi\in B^{**}
is a contraction, then
\omega(\xi)=1
if and only if
\xi{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
.

Proof.

Assume
\xi\in B^{**}
is a contraction such that
\omega(\xi)=1
. Then,
\xi
lies in the multiplicative domain of
\omega

[
51
, Theorem 3.18]
, so
\omega((I-\xi)^{*}(I-\xi))=0
and
(I-\xi){\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}=0
, or
\xi{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
. Conversely, if
\xi{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
, then
\omega((I-\xi)^{*}(I-\xi))=0
which implies
\omega(\xi)=1
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
. Let
(e_{\lambda})
be an excision for
\omega
. Then,
(e_{\lambda})
converges to
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
in the weak-
*
topology of
B^{**}
.

Proof.

By compactness of the closed unit ball of
B^{**}
in the weak-
*
topology, it suffices to show that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is the only cluster point of the net
(e_{\lambda})
. Fix such a cluster point
\xi\in B^{**}
, so that
\|\xi\|\leq 1
. Upon passing to cofinal subnet if necessary, we may thus assume that
(e_{\lambda})
converges to
\xi
in the weak-
*
topology of
B^{**}
.
Because

\omega(\xi)=\lim_{\lambda}\omega(e_{\lambda})=1

we have
\omega(\xi^{*})=1
and we conclude from Lemma
3.1
that
\xi^{*}{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
, or
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}\xi
. It thus only remains to show that
(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})\xi=0
.

Since
\omega
is assumed to be pure, the projection
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is closed
[
27
, Lemma 2.6]
. We may then choose an increasing net
(b_{\mu})
of positive contractions in
B
converging to
I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
in the weak-
*
topology of
B^{**}
. Let
\varphi
be a state on
B
. We find

\displaystyle|\varphi((I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})\xi)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu}\lim_{\lambda}|\varphi(b_{\mu}e_{\lambda})|^{2}\leq\limsup_{\mu}\limsup_{\lambda}\varphi(e_{\lambda}^{*}b_{\mu}^{2}e_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\mu}\omega(b_{\mu}^{2})\leq\limsup_{\mu}\omega(b_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\omega(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=0

by virtue of the Schwarz inequality and the excising property of
(e_{\lambda})
. Since
\varphi
was an arbitrary state on
B
, this implies that
(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})\xi=0
as desired.
∎

We also require the following.

Theorem 3.3

(Anderson)
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra. Let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\omega|_{A}
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
.

(ii)

\omega
admits an excision in
A
.

Proof.

If either (i) or (ii) holds, then
\omega|_{A}
is pure. Hence, the result follows from
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
.
∎

This result makes it clear that excisions are meaningful objects for the purposes of answering our main question on unique state extensions, and so we examine them a bit closer. In particular, we aim to relate their existence to a form of noncommutative peaking, which we describe next.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
{\mathcal{S}}(B)
denote its state space. Let
\omega
be a state on
B
, and let
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}\in B^{**}
denote its left support projection, that is the unique projection in
B^{**}
satisfying

B^{**}(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=\{x\in B^{**}:\omega(x^{*}x)=0\}.

It follows that
\omega({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=1
and that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
lies in the multiplicative domain of
\omega
.

Let
X\subset B
be any subset.
Inspired by
[
43
]
, we say that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is a
X
-peak projection
when there is
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
x{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
and
\|xp\|<1
for every closed projection
p\in B^{**}
orthogonal to
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
. This property may be reformulated entirely in terms of states, as follows.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that
\omega
is a pure state on
B
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is an
X
-peak projection.

(ii)

There is
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
\omega(x)=1
and
\varphi(x^{*}x)<1
if
\varphi
is a state on
B
satisfying
\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=0
.

(iii)

There is
x\in X
such that
\omega(x)=1
and
\varphi(x^{*}x)<1
if
\varphi
is a state on
B
distinct from
\omega
.

If we assume in addition that
X
is a unital subspace, then these statements are further equivalent to the following.

(iv)

There is
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
\omega(x)=1
and
|\varphi(x)|<1
if
\varphi
is a state on
B
satisfying
\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=0
.

(v)

There is
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
\omega(x)=1
and
|\varphi(x)|<1
if
\varphi
is a state on
B
distinct from
\omega
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii): This equivalence is contained in
[
43
, Theorem 5.1]
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii): We need only verify that
\|x\|=1
, which in this setting is equivalent to
\omega(x^{*}x)\leq 1
. This is trivially true if
x
is a scalar multiple of the identity, so we may suppose that
B
has dimension bigger than
1
, in which case there exists a state
\psi
on
B
distinct from
\omega
. Given
0<\varepsilon<1
, we see that
(1-\varepsilon)\omega+\varepsilon\psi
is a state distinct from
\omega
, so by assumption

(1-\varepsilon)\omega(x^{*}x)+\varepsilon\psi(x^{*}x)<1.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we find
\omega(x^{*}x)\leq 1
as desired.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Let
\varphi
be a state on
B
distinct from
\omega
. By
[
29
, Lemma 2.2]
, we see that
\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})<1
. Define
\psi:B\to{\mathbb{C}}
as

\psi=\frac{1}{\varphi(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})}\varphi((I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})\cdot(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})).

Clearly,
\psi
is a state on
B
with
\psi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=0
, so by assumption
\psi(x^{*}x)<1
.
Next, by Lemma
3.1
we have that
x{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
, which forces
x
to commute with
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
since
\|x\|=1
. We conclude that
x^{*}x={\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}+(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})x^{*}x(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})
, so that

\displaystyle\varphi(x^{*}x)=\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})+\varphi(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})\psi(x^{*}x)<\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})+\varphi(I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=1.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(v): The Schwarz inequality implies that
|\varphi(x)|^{2}\leq\varphi(x^{*}x)
for any state
\varphi
on
B
.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(iv): This is trivial.

Assume henceforth that
X
is a unital subspace.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(ii): There is
x\in X
with
\|x\|=1
such that
\omega(x)=1
and
|\varphi(x)|<1
if
\varphi
is a state on
B
satisfying
\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=0
. As in
[
43
, Lemma 3.7]
, we consider
y=(I+x)/2\in X
. Then,
\|y\|\leq 1
and
\omega(y)=1
. We compute

y^{*}y=\frac{1}{4}(I+x+x^{*}+x^{*}x)

so that, given a state
\varphi
on
B
, we have

\varphi(y^{*}y)=\frac{1}{4}(1+2\operatorname{Re}\varphi(x)+\varphi(x^{*}x))\leq\frac{1}{4}(2+2|\varphi(x)|)\leq 1.

Hence,
\varphi(y^{*}y)=1
implies that
|\varphi(x)|=1
, and in turn we find
\varphi({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})\neq 0
.
∎

One consequence of the previous result is that the notion of peak projection considered in
[

]
coincides with that introduced earlier in
[
43
]
, and hence with our current working definition, contrary to the guess formulated by the author at the time of writing
[

]
.

We now arrive to our result connecting excision and peaking. In the proof, we use a highly non-trivial fact about the existence of certain approximate units. It is a combination, due to Blecher, Hay, Neal and Read (and various subsets thereof), of known deep results from
[
43
]
,
[
17
]
and
[

]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital norm-closed subalgebra. Let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

There is a net in
A
forming an excision for
\omega
.

(ii)

The projection
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
lies in
A^{\perp\perp}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): This follows at once from Lemma
3.2
, since
A^{\perp\perp}
coincides with the weak-
*
closure of
A
inside of
B^{**}
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): The projection
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is closed since
\omega
is pure
[
27
, Lemma 2.6]
. By
[
20
, Corollary 2.25]
, there is a contractive approximate right unit
(I-e_{\lambda})
for the left ideal
L_{\omega}=\{b\in B:b{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}=0\}
converging to
I-{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
in the weak-
*
topology of
B^{**}
and with the property that each
e_{\lambda}
is contractive. Note then that
\lim_{\lambda}\omega(e_{\lambda})=\omega({\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega})=1
and

\lim_{\lambda}\|be_{\lambda}\|=0,\quad b\in L_{\omega}.

We can now proceed as in the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 2.3]
. Let
b\in B
. Then,
b-\omega(b)I\in\ker\omega
. The purity of
\omega
implies that
\ker\omega=L_{\omega}^{*}+L_{\omega}

[
34
, Proposition 2.9.1]
, so we may find
s,t\in L_{\omega}
with
b-\omega(b)I=s^{*}+t.
We obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{\lambda}\|e_{\lambda}^{*}(b-\omega(b)I)e_{\lambda}\|

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\lambda}\|e_{\lambda}^{*}(s^{*}+t)e_{\lambda}\|

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\lambda}(\|e_{\lambda}^{*}s^{*}\|+\|te_{\lambda}\|)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\lambda}(\|se_{\lambda}\|+\|te_{\lambda}\|)

\displaystyle=0.

We conclude that
(e_{\lambda})
is an excision for
\omega
.
∎

The following consequence summarizes the relationships between our notions of interest.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital, separable, norm-closed subalgebra. Let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
. Consider the following statements.

(i)

\omega
admits an excision in
A
.

(ii)

{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is an
A
-peak projection.

(iii)

The restriction
\omega|_{A}
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
.

Then, we have that

{\rm(i)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(ii)}\Rightarrow{\rm(iii)}.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii): This is Proposition
3.5
combined with
[
26
, Corollary 3.3]
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): This can be found in
[
27
, Proposition 3.2]
.

∎

When
A
is a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra, then statements (i),(ii) and (iii) are equivalent by virtue of Theorem
3.3
. We show next that the implications (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii) fails in the nonselfadjoint setting.

Example 3.7
.

Let
A\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
be the algebra of upper triangular matrices. Then,
A+A^{*}={\mathbb{M}}_{2}
, which readily implies that every state on
A
has a unique extension to a state on
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
. Denote by
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
the standard orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, and put
\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e_{1}+e_{2})
. Let
\omega:{\mathbb{M}}_{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be the pure state defined as

\omega(t)=\langle t\xi,\xi\rangle,\quad t\in{\mathbb{M}}_{2}.

Then,
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is the rank-one projection onto
{\mathbb{C}}\xi
, which does not lie in
A=A^{\perp\perp}
. We conclude from Proposition
3.5
that
\omega
does not admit an excision in
A
, so in particular
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is not an
A
-peak projection by Corollary
3.6
. ∎

In light of this example, we see that excisions and peak projections do not characterize unique extensions for states, and hence do not provide an answer to our main question. Indeed, a weaker notion of peaking is needed.

4.
Weaker notions of noncommutative peak points

In the previous section, given a state
\omega
on a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
along with a unital subspace
M\subset B
, we saw that the property that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
be an
M
-peak projection is typically strictly stronger than that of
\omega|_{M}
admitting a unique extension to a state on
B
. We are thus lead to relax this notion of peaking, in hopes of precisely characterizing the unique extension property. Two natural such relaxations are examined in this section.

As before, we denote by
{\mathcal{S}}(B)
the state space of
B
. Let
X\subset B
and
E\subset{\mathcal{S}}(B)
be non-empty subsets. We say that
E
is an
X
-peak set
if there is
x\in X
such that
\omega(x)=1
for every
\omega\in E
, while
|\psi(x)|<1
for every
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)\setminus E
. We record a basic property of these sets.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
X\subset B
be a non-empty subset. Then, any
X
-peak set must contain a pure state on
B
.

Proof.

Let
E\subset{\mathcal{S}}(B)
be a non-empty
X
-peak set. Then, there is
x\in X
such that
\omega(x)=1
for every
\omega\in E
, while
|\psi(x)|<1
for every
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)\setminus E
. This means that the weak-
*
continuous convex function

\varphi\mapsto|\varphi(x)|,\quad\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)

must attain its maximum on
E
. By virtue of Bauer’s maximum principle
[
3
, Theorem I.5.3]
, we conclude that
E
contains a pure state.
∎

Next, let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace. Put
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)=\{a^{*}a:a\in M\}
. Let also
\omega
be a state on
B
. Although the above definition of a peak set allows for arbitrary choices of
X
and
E
, for the rest of the paper we will be solely interested in the cases where
X
is either
M
or
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
, and
E
is either
\{\omega\}
or a subset of the collection
E_{\omega}
of states on
B
extending
\omega|_{M}
.
When
E=\{\omega\}
is an
X
-peak set, then we say that
\omega
is an
X
-peak state
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace. Let
\omega
be a state on
B
such that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is an
M
-peak projection. Then,
\omega
is both an
M
-peak state and a
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak state.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the Schwarz inequality along with Proposition
3.4
.
∎

We thus have the following diagram.

(3)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}\text{ is}\\
M\text{-peak projection}\end{subarray}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\text{ has}\\
M\text{-peak support}\end{subarray}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\text{ is}\\
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)\text{-peak state}\end{subarray}}

In Section
6
, we shall see that neither implication can be reversed in general (see (
17
)).

For now, we illustrate how certain special peak sets arise. Note that if
x\in M
has norm
1
, then the set
\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):\varphi(x^{*}x)=1\}
is clearly a
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak set. Something similar is true for
M
-peak sets, as we show next.

Given an element
b\in B
, we define its
numerical radius
as

\operatorname{nr}(b)=\sup_{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)}|\varphi(b)|,

which then satisfies

\|b\|/2\leq\operatorname{nr}(b)\leq\|b\|.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace. Let
x\in M
be an element such that
\operatorname{nr}(x)=1
. Then, the set

\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):\varphi(x)=1\}

is an
M
-peak set.

Proof.

Let
y=\frac{1}{2}(I+x)\in M
, so that
\operatorname{nr}(y)\leq 1
. An elementary calculation reveals that a state
\varphi
on
B
satisfies
|\varphi(y)|=1
precisely when
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)=1
. Hence,
\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):\varphi(x)=1\}
is an
M
-peak set.
∎

We now define four sets for an element
b\in B
:

N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}=\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):\varphi(b^{*}b)=\|b\|^{2}\},\quad R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}=\{\varphi|_{M}:\varphi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}\}

and

N^{\operatorname{nr}}_{b}=\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):|\varphi(b)|=\operatorname{nr}(b)\},\quad R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{b}=\{\varphi|_{M}:\varphi\in N^{\operatorname{nr}}_{b}\}.

Given a state
\omega
on
B
, we define
\Sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\omega}\subset B
to be the subset of elements
b
with
\|b\|=1
for which
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{b}=\{\omega|_{M}\}
. Likewise,
\Sigma^{\operatorname{nr}}_{\omega}\subset B
is the subset of elements
b
with
\operatorname{nr}(b)=1
for which
R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{b}=\{\omega|_{M}\}
. The following will be of use in Section
5
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace.
Let
\omega
be a state on
B
. Consider the following statements.

(i)

\omega
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak state.

(ii)

There is a non-empty
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak set contained in
E_{\omega}
.

(iii)

There is
x\in M\cap\Sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\omega}
such that
\{\omega|_{M}\}=R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
.

(iv)

\omega
is an
M
-peak state.

(v)

There is a non-empty
M
-peak set contained in
E_{\omega}
.

(vi)

There is
x\in M\cap\Sigma^{\operatorname{nr}}_{\omega}
such that
\{\omega|_{M}\}=R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{x}
.

Then, we have that
{\rm(i)}\Rightarrow{\rm(ii)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(iii)}
and
{\rm(iv)}\Rightarrow{\rm(v)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(vi)}
.
When
\omega|_{M}
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
, then
{\rm(i)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(ii)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(iii)}
and
{\rm(iv)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(v)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(vi)}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) and (iv)
\Rightarrow
(v): The peak set can trivially be taken to be
\{\omega\}
in both cases.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): By assumption, there is
x\in M
such that
\varphi(x^{*}x)=1>\psi(x^{*}x)
for every
\varphi\in E
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)\setminus E
. In particular,
\|x\|=1
and we see that
E=N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
. Since
E\subset E_{\omega}
this implies
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}=\{\omega|_{M}\}
and
x\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\omega}
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii): By assumption, there is
x\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{Q}}_{\omega}
such that
\{\omega|_{M}\}=R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
. Then,
N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}\subset E_{\omega}
is the desired non-empty
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak set.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(vi): By assumption, there is
x\in M
such that
\varphi(x)=1>|\psi(x)|
for every
\varphi\in E
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)\setminus E
. In particular,
\operatorname{nr}(x)=1
and we see that
E=N^{\operatorname{nr}}_{x}
. Since
E\subset E_{\omega}
this implies
R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{x}=\{\omega|_{M}\}
and
x\in\Sigma^{\operatorname{nr}}_{\omega}
.

(vi)
\Rightarrow
(v): By assumption, there is
x\in\Sigma^{\operatorname{nr}}_{\omega}
such that
\{\omega|_{M}\}=R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{x}
. Choose
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|\zeta|=1
such that
\omega(\zeta x)=1
, and put
y=\zeta x\in M
. Then,
R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{y}=R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{x}=\{\omega|_{M}\}
. Applying Lemma
4.3
, we see that
\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):\varphi(y)=1\}
is an
M
-peak set contained in
E_{\omega}
, and it is non-empty since it contains
\omega
.

The final sentence is clear, since in this case we have
E_{\omega}=\{\omega\}
.
∎

4.1.
A concrete example on the Dirichlet space

Our next goal is to analyze a concrete operator algebra of analytic functions through the lens of noncommutative peak point theory. This is predicated on the following basic observation.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and let
B\subset B(H)
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra containing the ideal
{\mathfrak{K}}
of compact operators. Let
q:B\to B/{\mathfrak{K}}
denote the quotient map. Let
\xi\in H
be a unit vector, and let
\omega=\langle\cdot\xi,\xi\rangle
be the corresponding pure vector state on
B
. For
b\in B
with
\|b\|=1
, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\omega(b^{*}b)=1>\psi(b^{*}b)
for every state
\psi
on
B
distinct from
\omega
.

(ii)

\|q(b)\|<1
and
\ker(b^{*}b-I)={\mathbb{C}}\xi
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): If
\|q(b)\|=1
, then there is a state
\varphi
on
B/{\mathfrak{K}}
such that
(\varphi\circ q)(b^{*}b)=1
. The assumption then forces
\omega=\varphi\circ q
, which is absurd as
\omega
does not annihilate the rank-one projection onto
{\mathbb{C}}\xi
.
Next, we see that

\langle b^{*}b\xi,\xi\rangle=\omega(b^{*}b)=1

so by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we find
{\mathbb{C}}\xi\subset\ker(b^{*}b-I)
. Given an arbitrary unit vector
\eta\in\ker(b^{*}b-I)
, the corresponding pure vector state
\psi=\langle\cdot\eta,\eta\rangle
on
B
clearly satisfies
\psi(b^{*}b)=1
, so that
\psi=\omega
by assumption, and in turn
\eta\in{\mathbb{C}}\xi
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): By
[
27
, Lemma 2.1]
, it suffices to fix a
pure
state
\psi
on
B
distinct from
\omega
and show that
\psi(b^{*}b)<1
. By basic representation theory of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras, either
\psi=\varphi\circ q
for some state
\varphi
on
B/{\mathfrak{K}}
, or
\psi
is a vector state. In the first case, we find
\psi(b^{*}b)\leq\|q(b)\|^{2}<1
. Alternatively, if there is a unit vector
\eta\in H
such that
\psi=\langle\cdot\eta,\eta\rangle
, then using
\psi\neq\omega
we see that
\eta\notin{\mathbb{C}}\xi=\ker(b^{*}b-I)
, so
\psi(b^{*}b)<1
.
∎

Example 4.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}
denote the Dirichlet space on the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
. This is a classical object of study in complex function theory and harmonic analysis; the reader may consult
[
37
]
for more detail. We only recall here the salient facts the we require for our analysis.

The space
{\mathcal{D}}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
, and it contains the constant functions.
Every polynomial
p
induces a bounded multiplication operator
M_{p}:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
with the property that
\|M_{p}\|\geq\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}}|p(z)|.
There are some polynomials for which this inequality is strict (contrary to the case of the Hardy space on
{\mathbb{D}}
).

Let
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})\subset B({\mathcal{D}})
denote the norm-closed unital subalgebra generated by the polynomial multipliers, and let
{\mathfrak{T}}({\mathcal{D}})=\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}}))\subset B({\mathcal{D}})
. The
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{T}}({\mathcal{D}})
contains the ideal
{\mathfrak{K}}
of compact operators on
{\mathcal{D}}
, and there is a unital surjective
*
-homomorphism
\pi:{\mathfrak{T}}({\mathcal{D}})\to\mathrm{C}({\mathbb{T}})
with
\ker\pi={\mathfrak{K}}
such that
\pi(M_{p})=p
for every polynomial
p
(
[
41
, Example 1 and Theorem 4.6]
). Here,
{\mathbb{T}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
denotes the unit circle. We now examine the various notions of peaking introduced above for the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
{\mathfrak{T}}(D)
relative to
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
.

Let
\omega
be a pure state on
{\mathfrak{T}}({\mathcal{D}})
. As above, there are two possibilities for
\omega
.

If
\omega
annihilates
{\mathfrak{K}}
, then there is a state
\tau
on
\mathrm{C}({\mathbb{T}})
, necessarily pure since
\omega
is, such that
\omega=\tau\circ\pi
. Then,
\tau
is given as evaluation at a point in
{\mathbb{T}}
. Combining
[
31
, Proposition 9.2]
with
[
19
, Corollary 5.8]
, we see that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is an
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
-peak projection.

The remaining case is that where there is a unit vector
\xi\in{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\omega=\langle\cdot\xi,\xi\rangle
. Note that if
a\in\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
satisfies
\omega(a)=1=\|a\|
, then by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we find
a\xi=\xi
. Since
{\mathcal{D}}
consists of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
, this forces
a=I
. In particular, we conclude from Proposition
3.4
that
{\mathfrak{l}}_{\omega}
is not an
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
-peak projection. Moreover,
\omega|_{\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})}
typically admits several extensions to a state on
{\mathfrak{T}}({\mathcal{D}})
. For instance,
\langle\cdot 1,1\rangle
agrees with
\tau_{0}\circ\pi
on
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
, where
\tau_{0}
is the state of integration against arclength measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
. In particular, this shows that
\omega
is typically not an
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
-state. Finally, by Proposition
4.5
, we see that
\omega
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}}))
-peak state if and only if there is
a\in\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
with
\|a\|=1>\|q(a)\|
and
\ker(a^{*}a-I)={\mathbb{C}}\xi
. There are many such elements in
\mathrm{A}({\mathcal{D}})
, as seen in
[
2
, Theorem 10.2]
.∎

Much of the analysis above can be replicated for a large class of spaces, namely the unitarily invariant complete Pick reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces on the open unit ball
[
31
]
. One crucial feature of
{\mathcal{D}}
is that
[
2
, Theorem 10.2]
is available, which was required by Proposition
4.5
. The validity of the corresponding statement in the setting of, say, the ubiquitous Drury–Arveson space, was raised as a question in
[
24
]
, and still does not seem to be known.

5.
Abundance of peak sets

We start this discussion with a simple observation, already noted in
[
43
]
.
Let
A\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}
be the unital subalgebra of upper-triangular matrices with constant diagonal. Then, it is readily verified that there exist simply no
A
-peak projections in this case. If one is to meaningfully consider peak projections as the noncommutative analogues of peak points, then this dearth is a rather serious impediment, as it conflicts with the classical case of continuous functions. Indeed, peak points are always dense in the set of pure states with pure restrictions in the commutative case
[
52
, Corollary 8.4]
.

In this section, we establish a noncommutative analogue of this density fact using the relaxed notions of peaking introduced in Section
4
. To do so, we will rely on an important Banach space geometry result, which we now describe. Let
X
be a normed space, and denote by
U\subset X
the unit sphere. Recall that a point
x\in U
is said to be
smooth
if there is a unique bounded linear functional
\lambda:X\to{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\|\lambda\|=1=\lambda(x)
. A proof of the following may be found in
[
52
, Theorem 8.3]
, for instance.

Theorem 5.1

(Mazur)
.

If
X
is a separable Banach space, then the smooth points of its unit sphere
U
form a dense subset of type
G_{\delta}
in
U
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace.
Our first order of business will be to show that, by applying this result to an appropriate Banach space, we can establish that certain special
M
-peak sets (of the type appearing in Proposition
4.4
) are rather abundant. For this purpose, we recall that the numerical radius
\operatorname{nr}(\cdot)
defines a norm on
M
, equivalent to the original norm coming from the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra structure of
B
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace. Let
U
denote the unit sphere of
(M,\operatorname{nr}(\cdot))
. Let
x
be a smooth point of
U
. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

There is a pure state
\omega
on
B
such that
|\omega(x)|=1
and
\omega|_{M}
is pure.

(ii)

If
\psi
is a state on
B
with
|\psi(x)|=1
, then
\omega|_{M}=\psi|_{M}
.

(iii)

E_{\omega}
contains a non-empty
M
-peak set.

Proof.

(i) By weak-
*
compactness, we see that the set
\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(M):|\varphi(x)|=\operatorname{nr}(x)\}
is non-empty. Further, it is a face of
{\mathcal{S}}(M)
, so by the Krein–Milman theorem there is a pure state
\omega_{0}
on
M
with
|\omega(x)|=\operatorname{nr}(x)=1
. It thus suffices to choose
\omega
to be any pure state extension of
\omega_{0}
.

(ii) Note that both
\omega|_{M}
and
\psi|_{M}
are norm-one linear functionals on
(M,\operatorname{nr}(\cdot))
. Choose
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|\alpha|=|\beta|=1
such that
\alpha\omega(x)=1=\beta\psi(x)
. Since
x
is smooth, we see that
\alpha\omega|_{M}=\beta\psi|_{M}
. Evaluating at the unit
I\in M
, we conclude that
\alpha=\beta
, whence
\omega|_{M}=\psi|_{M}
.

(iii) By (i) and (ii), we have
R^{\operatorname{nr}}_{x}=\{\omega|_{M}\}
. Hence by Proposition
4.4
we see that
E_{\omega}
contains a non-empty
M
-peak set.
∎

We now extract a sort of dual statement to Theorem
5.1
, which shows that certain special
M
-peak sets are always abundant.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital, separable, norm-closed subspace. Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
be the set of pure states
\omega
on
B
for which there is a smooth point
x
of the unit sphere of
(M,\operatorname{nr}(\cdot))
satisfying
|\omega(x)|=1
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}(M)
denote the set of pure states
\omega
on
B
such that
E_{\omega}
contains a non-empty
M
-peak set. Then, the sets
\{\omega|_{M}:\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\}
and
\{\omega|_{M}:\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}(M)\}
are both weak-
*
dense in the pure states on
M
.

Proof.

Note that
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(M)
and
\omega|_{M}
is pure for every
\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
by Lemma
5.2
, so it suffices to show that the weak-
*
closure of
\{\omega|_{M}:\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\}
contains the pure states on
M
.

Let
Y=(M,\operatorname{nr}(\cdot))
, which is a separable Banach space since
\operatorname{nr}(\cdot)
is a norm on
M
equivalent to the original norm coming from the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
. Denote by
K
the closed unit ball of
Y^{*}
.

We first claim the convex hull of
\{\zeta\omega|_{M}:\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}},|\zeta|=1,\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\}
is weak-
*
dense in
K
. For otherwise, there is
y\in U
such that

\sup_{\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}}|\omega(y)|<1.

By virtue of Theorem
5.1
, we may assume that
y
is a smooth point of
U
. In this case, Lemma
5.2
implies that there is
\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
with
|\omega(y)|=1
. This is absurd, and the claim is established. In particular, by Milman’s converse
[
52
, Proposition 1.5]
, we know that the extreme points of
K
are contained in the weak-
*
closure of
\{\zeta\omega|_{M}:\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}},|\zeta|=1,\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\}
.

Next, let
\varphi
be a state on
M
, and assume that
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})
for
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in K
. Then, using that
|\lambda_{i}(I)|\leq\operatorname{nr}(I)=1
and

1=\varphi(I)=\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{1}(I)+\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{2}(I))\leq 1

we see that
\lambda_{1}(I)=\lambda_{2}(I)=1
, so
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are in fact states on
M
. This shows that
{\mathcal{S}}(M)
is a weak-
*
closed face of
K
, so that the pure states on
M
are extreme points of
K
. Hence, the pure states on
M
are contained in the weak-
*
closure of
\{\zeta\omega|_{M}:\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}},|\zeta|=1,\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\}
, by the previous paragraph. Since states are unital maps, the pure states on
M
must in fact be contained in the weak-
*
closure of
\{\omega|_{M}:\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}_{0}\}
.
∎

Under an additional assumption
M
, we can in fact replace
M
-peak sets with
M
-peak states.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital , separable, norm-closed subspace. Assume that
M
has the pure extension property in
B
. Then, the
M
-peak states are weak-
*
dense in the pure states on
B
that restrict to be pure on
M
.

Proof.

Because of the pure extension property, we note that
{\mathcal{P}}(M)
consists of
M
-peak states. Consider the restriction map
\rho:{\mathcal{S}}(B)\to{\mathcal{S}}(M)
, and let
E\subset{\mathcal{S}}(B)
denote the set of pure states on
B
that restrict to be pure on
M
. In other words,
E
is the preimage under
\rho
of the pure states on
M
. Since every pure state on
M
admits a unique extension to a pure state on
B
by assumption, the map
\rho
implements a weak-
*
homeomorphism between
E
and the pure states on
M
. By Theorem
5.3
, we see that
\rho({\mathcal{P}}(M))
is weak-
*
dense in
\rho(E)
, so
{\mathcal{P}}(M)
is in fact weak-
*
dense in
E
.
∎

Note that if
B
above is commutative and
B=\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
, then
M
necessarily has the pure extension property, and the previous result recovers the classical fact that the peak points are dense in the Choquet boundary of
M

[
52
, Corollary 8.4]
.

Our next objective is to obtain an analogous abundance result for
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak sets. For this purpose, we require an adaptation of Theorem
5.1
. The key technical step is the following; our argument is closely modelled on the proof of
[
52
, Proposition 8.3]
. We use the same notation as in Proposition
4.4
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
B
be a unital separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a norm-closed subspace. Fix
u\in M
with
\|u\|=1
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then, the set

D=\{x\in M:\|x\|=1,\operatorname{Re}(\varphi(u^{*}x)-\psi(u^{*}x))<\varepsilon\text{ for every }\varphi,\psi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}\}

is open and dense in the unit sphere of
M
.

Proof.

First, observe that since
B
is assumed to be separable, the weak-
*
topology on the state space
{\mathcal{S}}(B)
is metrizable. This will be used implicitly throughout.

Denote by
U
the unit sphere of
M
.
Let
(y_{n})
be a sequence in
U\setminus D
which converges in norm to some
y\in U
. Thus, for each integer
n\geq 1
there are states
\varphi_{n},\psi_{n}\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{y_{n}}
such that

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_{n}(u^{*}y_{n})-\psi_{n}(u^{*}y_{n}))\geq\varepsilon.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B)
be weak-
*
cluster points of the sequences
(\varphi_{n})
and
(\psi_{n})
respectively. Since
(y_{n})
converges to
y
in norm, it is easily verified that
\varphi,\psi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{y}
and

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi-\psi)(u^{*}y)\geq\varepsilon.

We conclude that
y\in U\setminus D
, whence
D
is open.

Assume next for the sake of contradiction that
D
is not dense in
U
, so that there is
x\in U\setminus D
along with
\delta>0
such that
y\in U\setminus D
whenever
y\in U
satisfies
\|x-y\|<\delta
. We put
x_{1}=x
and choose
\varphi_{1},\psi_{1}\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x_{1}}
such that

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_{1}(u^{*}x_{1})-\psi_{1}(u^{*}x_{1}))\geq\varepsilon.

Suppose that, given
n\geq 1
, we have constructed
x_{n}\in U\setminus D
such that

\|x_{1}-x_{n}\|<\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\delta

along with
\varphi_{n},\psi_{n}\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x_{n}}
such that

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n})-\psi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n}))\geq\varepsilon.

Since both
x_{n}
and
u
have norm
1
, we may choose
\alpha_{n}>0
small enough so that
x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\neq 0
,

(4)

\alpha_{n}(1-\varphi_{n}(u^{*}u))<\varepsilon

and

(5)

\frac{\|(1-\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|)x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|}<\frac{\delta}{2^{n+1}}.

Define

x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|}(x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u)\in U.

Using (
5
), we obtain

(6)

\|x_{n}-x_{n+1}\|=\frac{\|(1-\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|)x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|}<\frac{\delta}{2^{n+1}}

and thus

\displaystyle\|x_{1}-x_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{1}-x_{n}\|+\|x_{n}-x_{n+1}\|<\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\delta+\frac{\delta}{2^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\right)\delta<\delta

so we see that
x_{n+1}\in U\setminus D
by choice of
\delta
. In particular, we may choose
\varphi_{n+1},\psi_{n+1}\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x_{n+1}}
such that

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n+1})-\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n+1}))\geq\varepsilon.

By induction, we obtain a sequence
(x_{n})
in
U\setminus D
, a sequence
(\alpha_{n})
of positive numbers, and sequences
(\varphi_{n}),(\psi_{n})
of states on
B
such that

(7)

x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|}(x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u),

(8)

\varphi_{n}(x_{n}^{*}x_{n})=\psi_{n}(x_{n}^{*}x_{n})=1

and

(9)

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n})-\psi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n}))\geq\varepsilon

for every
n\geq 1
.
Using (
7
) and (
8
), we observe that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{n}(x_{n+1}^{*}x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|^{2}}(\varphi_{n}(x_{n}^{*}x_{n})+2\alpha_{n}\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}^{2}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}u))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|^{2}}(1+2\alpha_{n}\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}^{2}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}u))

and

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n+1}(x_{n+1}^{*}x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|^{2}}(\psi_{n+1}(x_{n}^{*}x_{n})+2\alpha_{n}\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}^{2}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}u))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\|x_{n}+\alpha_{n}u\|^{2}}(1+2\alpha_{n}\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}^{2}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}u)).

Combining these inequalities we infer

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}u)

\displaystyle\leq 2\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}u)

\displaystyle\leq 2\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n})+\alpha_{n}

and therefore

(10)

\operatorname{Re}(\varphi_{n}(u^{*}x_{n})-\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}x_{n}))\leq\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{n}(1-\varphi_{n}(u^{*}u))<\varepsilon/2

by virtue of (
4
).

Note now that (
6
) implies that the sequence
(x_{n})
in Cauchy, so it converges in norm to some
z\in U
. Choose
N
large enough so that
\|x_{n}-z\|<\varepsilon/16
for each
n\geq N
. Then, (
9
) and (
10
) easily imply that

\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}z)\geq\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n}(u^{*}z)+7\varepsilon/8\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Re}\psi_{n+1}(u^{*}z)>\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{n}(u^{*}z)-5\varepsilon/8

for each
n\geq N
. Given any integer
m\geq 1
, we thus find

\displaystyle 1\geq\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{N+m}(u^{*}z)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{Re}\psi_{N+m}(u^{*}z)+7\varepsilon/8>\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{N+m-1}(u^{*}z)+\varepsilon/4

\displaystyle\geq\ldots\geq\operatorname{Re}\varphi_{N}(u^{*}z)+m\varepsilon/4

which is absurd.
∎

We now obtain our desired analogue of Mazur’s theorem.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
B
be a unital separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital norm-closed subalgebra. Let
A_{0}\subset A
be the subset of elements
x
with
\|x\|=1
for which the set
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
is a singleton. Then,
A_{0}
contains a
G_{\delta}
dense subset of the unit sphere of
A
.

Proof.

Denote by
U
the unit sphere of
A
.
Since
A
is separable, so is
U
, and there is a countable subset
\{u_{n}:n\geq 1\}
which is dense in
U
. For each
n,m\geq 1
, we set

D_{nm}=\{x\in U:\operatorname{Re}(\varphi(u_{n}^{*}x)-\psi(u_{n}^{*}x))<1/m\text{ for every }\varphi,\psi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}\}.

Let
x\in\bigcap_{n,m=1}^{\infty}D_{nm}
and let
\varphi,\psi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
. Then,
\operatorname{Re}\varphi(u^{*}_{n}x)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(u^{*}_{n}x)
for every
n\geq 1
. By density, this means that
\operatorname{Re}\varphi(u^{*}x)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(u^{*}x)
for every
u\in U
. For each
u\in U
, using that
A
is a subalgebra we have
xu\in U
so
\operatorname{Re}\varphi(u^{*}x^{*}x)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(u^{*}x^{*}x)
. Observe now that
x^{*}x
belongs to the multiplicative domain of
\varphi
and
\psi
since
\varphi,\psi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
, so
\operatorname{Re}\varphi(u^{*})=\operatorname{Re}\psi(u^{*})
for every
u\in U
. We conclude that
\operatorname{Re}\varphi=\operatorname{Re}\psi
on
A
, and hence
\varphi=\psi
on
A
.
In other words,
x\in A_{0}
. This shows that
A_{0}
contains
\bigcap_{n,m=1}^{\infty}D_{nm}.

Using Lemma
5.5
along with the Baire category theorem, we see that
\bigcap_{n,m=1}^{\infty}D_{nm}
is a
G_{\delta}
dense subset of
U
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Although our argument above requires
A
to be an algebra to conclude that
xu\in U
, it is conceivable that the result itself could hold for unital norm-closed subspaces as well, but we have not been able to adapt the argument to this more general setting.

We now obtain a result illustrating that
{\mathcal{Q}}(A)
-peak sets are always somewhat plentiful.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
B
be a unital separable
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital norm-closed subalgebra. Let
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Q}}(A))
denote the set of pure states
\omega
on
B
such that
E_{\omega}
contains a non-empty
{\mathcal{Q}}(A)
-peak set. Then,

\sup\{\omega(a^{*}a):\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Q}}(A))\}=\|a\|^{2}

for every
a\in A
.

Proof.

Let
A_{0}\subset A
be defined as in Theorem
5.6
. For each
x\in A_{0}
, there always is a pure state
\psi
on
B
lying in
N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
. By definition of
A_{0}
, we find
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}=\{\psi|_{M}\}
. Proposition
4.4
implies that
E_{\psi}
contains a non-empty
{\mathcal{Q}}(A)
-peak set, so
\psi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Q}}(A))
which implies

\sup_{\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Q}}(A))}\omega(x^{*}x)=\|x\|^{2}.

Since
A_{0}
is norm-dense in the unit sphere of
A
by virtue of Theorem
5.6
, we conclude that

\sup_{\omega\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Q}}(A))}\omega(a^{*}a)=\|a\|^{2},\quad a\in A.

∎

5.1.
From peak sets to peak states in matrix algebras

At the time of this writing, we do not know if the statement of Corollary
5.7
can generally be improved by replacing peak sets with peak states. We close this section by exhibiting instances of small matrix algebras where this can be done. The key observation is the following.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
M\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
be a unital subspace.
Let
x\in M
with
\|x\|=1
such that
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
is a singleton while
N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
contains two distinct pure states
\varphi
and
\psi
. Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

There is a two dimensional subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with the property that
P_{V}a|_{V}=\varphi(a)I
for each
a\in M
.

(ii)

If we put
z=P_{V^{\perp}}x|_{V}
, then
z^{*}z=(1-|\varphi(x)|^{2})I
.

Proof.

The pure states
\varphi
and
\psi
are vector states. Thus, there exist two linearly independent unit vectors
\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that

\varphi(b)=\langle b\xi,\xi\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(b)=\langle b\eta,\eta\rangle

for every
b\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
. Let
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be the subspace spanned by
\xi
and
\eta
. The assumption that
\varphi,\psi\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
means that

1=\|x\|^{2}=\varphi(x^{*}x)=\psi(x^{*}x).

When combined with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, this implies that
V\subset\ker(x^{*}x-I)
. In particular, if
v\in V
is a unit vector then the state
\theta
on
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
defined as

\theta(b)=\langle bv,v\rangle,\quad b\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}

satisfies
\theta(x^{*}x)=1
or
\theta\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
. Since
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
is a singleton, this means that
\theta
and
\varphi
agree on
M
, so

\langle av,v\rangle=\varphi(a),\quad a\in M.

Because
v\in V
is arbitrary, we infer that

P_{V}a|_{V}=\varphi(a)I,\quad a\in M

which is (i). Finally, put
z=P_{V^{\perp}}x|_{V}
. We compute using (i) that

\displaystyle I=P_{V}x^{*}x|_{V}

\displaystyle=P_{V}x^{*}P_{V}x|_{V}+P_{V}x^{*}P_{V^{\perp}}x|V

\displaystyle=|\varphi(x)|^{2}I+z^{*}z

so (ii) is established.
∎

In some case, we can now replace
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak sets by
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak states.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
M\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
be a unital subspace. Put
B=\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)
. Assume that either

(a)

n\leq 2
, or

(b)

n=3
and the only normal matrices in
M
are scalar multiples of the identity.

Let
x\in M
with
\|x\|=1
such that
R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
is a singleton and let
\omega\in N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
. Then,
\omega
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak state.

Proof.

If
M={\mathbb{C}}I
, then
B=\mathrm{C}^{*}(M)={\mathbb{C}}I
and the claim is trivial. Hence, we assume throughout that
n\geq 1
and that
{\mathbb{C}}I
is properly contained in
M
.

To show that
\omega
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak state, it suffices to fix another pure state
\psi
on
B
such that
\psi(x^{*}x)=1
, and to establish that
\psi=\omega

[
27
, Lemma 2.1]
.

If
\omega
and
\psi
are distinct, then we may apply Lemma
5.8
to find a two dimensional subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that

P_{V}a|_{V}=\omega(a)I,\quad a\in M

and
z^{*}z=(1-|\omega(x)|^{2})I
for
z=P_{V^{\perp}}x|_{V}
.

If
n=2
, then
V={\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
a=\omega(a)I
for every
a\in M
, contrary to our standing assumption that
{\mathbb{C}}I\neq M
.

Next, assume
n=3
and that the only normal matrices in
M
are scalar multiples of the identity.
In this case,
\dim V^{\perp}=1<\dim V
, so
z^{*}z
cannot be invertible. This forces
|\omega(x)|=1
, so that
P_{V}x|_{V}
is a unimodular scalar multiple of the identity on
V
. Because
x
has norm
1
, we infer that
V
is a reducing subspace for
x
. Therefore,
x
is a diagonal matrix with respect to the decomposition
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}=V\oplus V^{\perp}
, and hence it is normal. By assumption, we must have that
x\in{\mathbb{C}}I
. It follows that
{\mathcal{S}}(B)=N^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}
, so
\{\omega|_{M}\}=R^{\mathcal{Q}}_{x}={\mathcal{S}}(M)
, which forces
M={\mathbb{C}}I
. Once again, this contradicts our standing assumption.

In both cases, we thus conclude that
\psi=\omega
, so that indeed
\omega
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(M)
-peak state.
∎

We now arrive at our desired result.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
A\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
be a unital subalgebra. Assume that either

(a)

n\leq 2
, or

(b)

n=3
and the only normal matrices in
A
are scalar multiples of the identity.

Then,

\sup\{\omega(a^{*}a):\omega\text{ is a }{\mathcal{Q}}(A)\text{-peak state on }B\}=\|a\|^{2}

for every
a\in A
.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.6
, it suffices to prove that for a fixed
a\in A_{0}
, there is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(A)
-peak state
\omega
with
\omega(a^{*}a)=1
. For such an element
a
, there always exists a pure state
\omega
on
B
with
\omega(a^{*}a)=1
. But then Proposition
5.9
implies that
\omega
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}(A)
-peak state, as desired.
∎

Of course, this result is of limited applicability, given how restrictive the assumptions are. One non-trivial case where it is applicable is the subalgebra of upper triangular Toeplitz matrices in
{\mathbb{M}}_{3}
.

Finally, it is natural to wonder if Theorem
5.3
can be similarly improved to replace peak sets by peak points for small matrix algebras. We do not know the answer, but we mention that the results
[
48
]
appear relevant in this regard.

6.
Unique state extensions for peak states

We return to our main question, which we recall for convenience.

Main question.
Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a subspace. Let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
and let
E_{\omega}
denote the set of states on
B
extending
\omega|_{M}
. When does
E_{\omega}
reduce to the singleton
\{\omega\}
?

In this section, we thoroughly examine whether the noncommutative analogues of peak points considered hitherto provide an answer.

First, we note that if
\omega
is an
M
-peak state, then it follows immediately from the definition that
\omega
is the unique state on
B
extending
\omega|_{M}
. Thus, we have the following implication.

(11)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\text{ is}\\
M\text{-peak state}\end{subarray}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{subarray}{c}E_{\omega}=\{\omega\}\end{subarray}}

In some simple situations, we have a converse.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. Let
x\in B
and put
M=\operatorname{span}\{I,x\}\subset B
. Let
\omega
be a state on
B
such that
\omega(x)=\operatorname{nr}(x)
.
Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\omega
is an
M
-peak state.

(ii)

\omega|_{M}
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): This always holds, as mentioned before the proposition.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): When
x=0
, we have
M={\mathbb{C}}I
so the assumption implies that there is a unique state on
B
, which is then automatically an
M
-peak state. Assume henceforth that
x\neq 0
. Upon normalizing
x
if necessary, we may also suppose that
\operatorname{nr}(x)=1
. By assumption, we see that

\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(B):\varphi(x)=1\}=\{\omega\}
. We then conclude from Lemma
4.3
that indeed
\omega
is an
M
-peak state.
∎

Generally speaking however, the converse of (
11
) fails, even if
M
is a unital norm-closed subalgebra of
B
. Our next goal is to construct an example that illustrates this failure.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a subspace. Define
{\mathcal{A}}(M)\subset{\mathbb{M}}_{2}(B)
to be the unital subalgebra consisting of elements of the form
\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&x\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}
for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
x\in M
. Further, given a state
\omega
on
B
, we define a state
\Phi_{\omega}
on
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}(B)
as

\Phi_{\omega}([b_{ij}])=\langle[\omega(b_{ij})]v,v\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}},\quad[b_{ij}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{2}(B)

where
v=\frac{1}{2}(1,1)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. In other words,

\Phi_{\omega}([b_{ij}])=\frac{1}{2}(\omega(b_{11})+\omega(b_{12})+\omega(b_{21})+\omega(b_{22}))

for each
[b_{ij}]\in{\mathbb{M}}_{2}(B)
. This last expression implies in particular that, given another state
\omega^{\prime}
on
B
,
\Phi_{\omega}=\Phi_{\omega^{\prime}}
holds precisely when
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset B
be a unital subspace. Let
\omega
be a state on
B
. Consider the following statements.

(i)

\omega|_{M}
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
.

(ii)

\Phi_{\omega}|_{{\mathcal{A}}(M)}
admits a unique extension to a state on
{\mathbb{M}}_{2}(B)
.

(iii)

\omega
is an
M
-peak state.

(iv)

\Phi_{\omega}
is an
{\mathcal{A}}(M)
-peak state.

Then, we have
{\rm(i)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(ii)}
and
{\rm(iv)}\Rightarrow{\rm(iii)}
.
If
B
is commutative, then
{\rm(iii)}\Leftrightarrow{\rm(iv)}
.

Proof.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
\psi
be a state on
B
extending
\omega|_{M}
. Then, it follows that
\Phi_{\psi}=\Phi_{\omega}

7.
The characterization: pinnacle sets

In this section, we prove one of the main results of the paper, which gives a characterization of the unique extension property for states in terms of weaker form of peaking, thereby answering our main question.

7.1.
Preliminaries

We start our preparation with a computational fact about derivatives of certain exponential functions in non-commutative algebras.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
a\in B
. Let
\psi
be a state on
B
.
Define a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty)
as

f(t)=\psi(e^{ta*}e^{ta}),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then, for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

f^{\prime}(t)=2\psi(e^{ta*}(\operatorname{Re}a)e^{ta})

and

f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\psi(e^{ta*}(4(\operatorname{Re}a)^{2}+(a^{*}a-aa^{*}))e^{ta}).

In particular, if
4(\operatorname{Re}a)^{2}+(a^{*}a-aa^{*})\geq 0
, then
f
is convex.

Proof.

For distinct numbers
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}
we compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t-s}(f(t)-f(s))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t-s}\psi\left(e^{sa*}e^{(t-s)a*}e^{(t-s)a}e^{sa}-e^{sa*}e^{sa}\right)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(e^{sa*}\frac{1}{t-s}\left(e^{(t-s)a*}e^{(t-s)a}-I\right)e^{sa}\right).

Letting
s\to t
, we infer that

f^{\prime}(t)=\psi(e^{ta*}d_{1}e^{ta}),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}

where
d_{1}\in B
is the derivative at
t=0
of the function

t\mapsto e^{ta*}e^{ta}.

Power series expansions yield that
d_{1}=2\operatorname{Re}a
, so we obtain

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)=2\psi\left(e^{ta*}\left(\operatorname{Re}a\right)e^{ta}\right),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Similarly, for
s\neq t
we now find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t-s}(f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s))

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{t-s}\psi\left(e^{sa*}e^{(t-s)a*}(\operatorname{Re}a)e^{(t-s)a}e^{sa}-e^{sa*}(\operatorname{Re}a)e^{sa}\right)

\displaystyle=2\psi\left(e^{sa*}\frac{1}{t-s}\left(e^{(t-s)a*}(\operatorname{Re}a)e^{(t-s)a}-\operatorname{Re}a\right)e^{sa}\right).

Letting
s\to t
, we infer that

f^{\prime\prime}(t)=2\psi(e^{ta*}d_{2}e^{ta}),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}

where
d_{2}\in B
is the derivative at
t=0
of the function

t\mapsto e^{ta*}(\operatorname{Re}a)e^{ta}.

Power series expansions yield that
d_{2}=a^{*}(\operatorname{Re}a)+(\operatorname{Re}a)a
, so we obtain

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(t)=2\psi\left(e^{ta*}\left(a^{*}(\operatorname{Re}a)+(\operatorname{Re}a)a\right)e^{ta}\right),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Finally, put
b=\operatorname{Re}a
and
c=\operatorname{Im}a
. For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we compute

\displaystyle a^{*}(\operatorname{Re}a)+(\operatorname{Re}a)a

\displaystyle=b^{2}-icb+b^{2}+ibc=2b^{2}+i(bc-cb)

and

a^{*}a-aa^{*}=2i(bc-cb)

whence

f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\psi\left(e^{ta*}\left(4(\operatorname{Re}a)^{2}+(a^{*}a-aa^{*})\right)e^{ta}\right).

The final statement follows at once from this identity.
∎

Next, we establish some crucial estimates.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
a\in B
. Let
\psi
be a state on
B
.
Then, the following statements hold.

(i)

For each
t\geq 0
, we have
\|e^{ta}\|\leq\|e^{\operatorname{Re}a}\|^{t}
.

(ii)

Let
\kappa=6\|a\|^{2}\|e^{\operatorname{Re}a}\|^{2}
. Then,

|\psi(e^{ta*}e^{ta})-(1+2(\operatorname{Re}\psi(a))t)|\leq\kappa t^{2}

for every
0\leq t\leq 1
.

Proof.

By a well-known inequality (see for instance
[
55
, Theorem 3.2]
), we have
\|e^{ta}\|\leq\|e^{\operatorname{Re}(ta)}\|
for each
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus, for each
t\geq 0
, we find
\|e^{ta}\|\leq\|e^{\operatorname{Re}a}\|^{t}
, which is (i).

Next, define a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty)
as

f(t)=\psi(e^{ta*}e^{ta}),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

By combining Lemma
7.1
with (i), we see that
f^{\prime}(0)=2\operatorname{Re}\psi(a)
and

|f^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq\kappa,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1.

In turn, by the mean value theorem, given
0<t\leq 1
, there are real numbers
u
and
v
with
0<v<u<t
such that

f(t)=f(0)+f^{\prime}(u)t

and

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=f(0)+(f^{\prime}(0)+f^{\prime\prime}(v)u)t

\displaystyle=1+2(\operatorname{Re}\psi(a))t+f^{\prime\prime}(v)ut.

Hence

|f(t)-(1+2(\operatorname{Re}\psi(a))t)|\leq\kappa t^{2}

for every
0\leq t\leq 1
, which establishes (ii).
∎

Next, we reformulate the property of a state admitting a unique extension in terms of certain real subspaces of self-adjoint elements. The following is routine, but we lack an exact reference so we include the proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
M\subset N\subset B
be unital subspaces. Let
\omega
be a state on
B
. Assume that the restriction
\omega|_{M}
admits a unique state extension to
N
. Consider the following real subspaces of
B
:

R=\operatorname{span}_{{\mathbb{R}}}\{\operatorname{Re}a:a\in M\},\quad S=\operatorname{span}_{{\mathbb{R}}}\{\operatorname{Re}b:b\in N\}.

Then, the restriction
\omega|_{R}
admits a unique
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear contractive extension to
S
.

Proof.

Put
\rho=\omega|_{R}
and let
\rho^{\prime}:S\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear contractive extension of
\rho
. We may then define
\omega^{\prime}:N\to{\mathbb{C}}
as

\omega^{\prime}(b)=\rho^{\prime}(\operatorname{Re}b)-i\rho^{\prime}(\operatorname{Re}(ib)),\quad b\in N.

It is readily verified that the functional
\omega^{\prime}
is complex linear and unital. Next, given
b\in N
there is a unimodular complex number
\zeta
such that
|\omega^{\prime}(b)|=\omega^{\prime}(\zeta b)
. Thus,

|\omega^{\prime}(b)|=\operatorname{Re}\omega^{\prime}(\zeta b)=\rho^{\prime}(\operatorname{Re}(\zeta b))\leq\|\operatorname{Re}(\zeta b)\|\leq\|b\|

so that
\omega^{\prime}
is contractive, and hence is a state on
N
. In addition,
\omega^{\prime}
extends
\omega|_{M}
since
\rho^{\prime}
extends
\rho
. By assumption, we then infer that
\omega^{\prime}=\omega|_{N}
. It follows that

\rho^{\prime}(\operatorname{Re}b)=\omega^{\prime}(\operatorname{Re}b)=\omega(\operatorname{Re}b),\quad b\in N

so indeed
\rho
has a unique
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear contractive extension to
S
.
∎

7.2.
The main result

With these preliminaries out of the way, we can prove a technical result that reformulates the unique extension property for a state in terms of a weak form of peaking.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital norm-closed subalgebra. Let
\omega
be a state on
B
, and let
f\in B
be a positive contraction such that
\omega(f)=1
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\omega|_{A}
has a unique extension to a state on
A+{\mathbb{C}}f
.

(ii)

For every
0<\gamma<1
and
\varepsilon>0
satisfying
\log(\gamma+\varepsilon)<0
, there is
x\in A
with
\|x\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
such that

\Omega(x^{*}x)>1>\psi(x^{*}x)

if
\psi
is a state on
B
with
\psi(f)<\gamma
, and
\Omega
is a state on
B
agreeing with
\omega
on
A
.

(iii)

For every
0<\gamma<1
, there is
x\in A
such that

\Omega(x^{*}x)>1>\psi(x^{*}x)

if
\psi
is a state on
B
with
\psi(f)<\gamma
, and
\Omega
is a state on
B
agreeing with
\omega
on
A
.

Proof.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): This is trivial.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
\psi
be a state on
B
with
\psi(f)\neq\omega(f)
, so that
\psi(f)<1
. Choose
0<\gamma<1
small enough so that
\psi(f)<\gamma
. Then, the assumed inequality immediately implies that
\psi
cannot agree with
\omega
on
A
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Fix
0<\gamma<1
and
\varepsilon>0
satisfying
\log(\gamma+\varepsilon)<0
.
Let
g=\log(f+\varepsilon I)\in B
. Let
\Omega
be a state on
B
agreeing with
\omega
on
A
. By assumption, we find that
\Omega(f)=\omega(f)=1
. Because
f
is a positive contraction, it follows from the Schwarz inequality that
f
lies in the multiplicative domain of
\Omega
, and we see that

\Omega(g)=\log(\Omega(f)+\varepsilon)=\log(1+\varepsilon).

In other words,
\omega|_{A}
has a unique extension to a state on
A+{\mathbb{C}}g
. By virtue of Lemma
7.3
, we know that the restriction of
\omega
to the real subspace
\{\operatorname{Re}a:a\in A\}
admits a unique
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear extension to a contractive functional on the real subspace
\{\operatorname{Re}a+tg:t\in{\mathbb{R}},a\in A\}
, namely the corresponding restriction of
\omega
.
The classical Hahn–Banach argument (see for instance
[
13
, Proposition 6.2]
) then implies that

\log(1+\varepsilon)=\omega(g)=\sup\{\omega(\operatorname{Re}a):a\in A,\operatorname{Re}a\leq g\}.

Hence, there is
a\in A
with
\operatorname{Re}a\leq g
such that

(18)

\omega(\operatorname{Re}a)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\log(1+\varepsilon).

By Lemma
7.2
, for
t\geq 0
we see that

(19)

\|e^{ta}\|\leq\|e^{\operatorname{Re}a}\|^{t}\leq\|e^{g}\|^{t}=\|f+\varepsilon I\|^{t}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{t}.

Given a state
\varphi
on
B
, we define

Lemma 7.5

Proposition 7.9
.

Let

be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let

Theorem 7.10

Let

be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let

be a unital subspace. Let
\omega
be a state on

which is

-detectable. Let

. Then, the following statements hold.

. Then,
\sigma
is a subrepresentation of any Stinespring representation of

.

(ii)

\sigma
is a boundary representation for

Proof.

(i) There is a unit vector

Let

.

8.
Pinnacle representations

The notions of pinnacle set and pinnacle state from the previous section, while inspired by Bishop’s criterion from the introduction, do not provide perfect analogues of it. In this final section, we aim to fix this flaw by replacing states with representations.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra. We let
\operatorname{spec}(B)
denote the
spectrum
of
B
, that is the set of unitary equivalence classes of irreducible
*
-representations of
B
, equipped with its usual topology (see
[
34
, Chapter 3]
for details).

For our purposes, we require a technical assumption. Let
X\subset B
be a subset. We say that a state
\psi
on
B
is
X
-convex
if, for every
a\in X
, the function

t\mapsto\psi(e^{ta*}e^{ta}),\quad t\geq 0

is convex. This condition is easily seen to be satisfied if
\psi
is multiplicative. We will see more examples below.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
A\subset B
be a unital norm-closed subalgebra. Let
\omega
be a pure state on
B
. Assume that
\omega
is
A
-convex, and that
\omega|_{A}
admits a unique extension to a state on
B
. Let
K\subset\operatorname{spec}(B)
be a closed subset not containing the class of the GNS representation of
\omega
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
a\in A
with
\|a\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
such that
\omega(a^{*}a)=1
, while
\|\pi(a)\|<\varepsilon
for every irreducible
*
-representation
\pi
whose class lies in
K
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we denote by
\sigma:B\to B(H)
the GNS representation of
\omega
, with distinguished unit cyclic vector
\xi\in H
satisfying

\omega(b)=\langle\sigma(b)\xi,\xi\rangle,\quad b\in B.

By definition of the topology on
\operatorname{spec}(B)
, we see that
\sigma
does not vanish on the ideal
J=\bigcap_{[\pi]\in K}\ker\pi
. The restriction of
\sigma
to
J
is thus still irreducible
[
34
, Lemma 2.11.13]
. By the Kadison transitivity theorem, there is
0\leq f\leq I
in
J
such that
\sigma(f)\xi=\xi
, that is
\omega(f)=1
. Note in particular that
f
lies in the multiplicative domain of
\omega
.
Letting
g=\log(f+\varepsilon I)\in B
, we find
\omega(g)=\log(1+\varepsilon)
.

By virtue of Lemma
7.3
, we see that the restriction of
\omega
to the real subspace
\{\operatorname{Re}b:b\in A\}
admits a unique
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear extension to a contractive functional on the real subspace of self-adjoint elements in
B
. The classical Hahn–Banach argument (see for instance
[
13
, Proposition 6.2]
) then implies that

\omega(g)=\sup\{\omega(\operatorname{Re}b):b\in A,\operatorname{Re}b\leq g\}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, there is
b\in A
with
\operatorname{Re}b\leq g
such that

\omega(\operatorname{Re}b)\geq\omega(g)-\varepsilon=\log(1+\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\geq-\varepsilon.

Put
a_{0}=e^{b}
. By Lemma
7.2
(i), for any
*
-representation
\rho
of
B
we see that

\|\rho(a_{0})\|\leq\|e^{\operatorname{Re}\rho(b)}\|\leq\|e^{\rho(g)}\|\leq\|\rho(f)+\varepsilon I\|.

This implies that
\|a_{0}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon
and that
\|\pi(a_{0})\|\leq\varepsilon
if
[\pi]\in K
, since
f\in J
.

Next, consider the function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,\infty)
defined as

f(t)=\omega(e^{tb*}e^{tb}),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Using that
\omega
is
A
-convex along with Lemma
7.1
, we see that

f^{\prime}(t)\geq f^{\prime}(0)=2\omega(\operatorname{Re}b),\quad t\geq 0.

The mean value theorem then implies

\omega(a_{0}^{*}a_{0})=f(1)\geq 1+2\omega(\operatorname{Re}b)\geq 1-2\varepsilon.

Finally, put
a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega(a^{*}_{0}a^{*})}}a_{0}
so that
\omega(a^{*}a)=1
. We find

\|a\|\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}}

and

\|\pi(a)\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}},\quad[\pi]\in K.

This readily implies the desired conclusion.
∎

To put this result in proper perspective, we next turn to a discussion of the
X
-convexity condition.
Below, we identify a class of subalgebras where every state has the required convexity property. The basic observation is as follows.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
B
be a unital commutative
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let
X\subset B
be a subset. Let
H
be a Hilbert space. Let
\rho:B\to B(H)
be a unital completely positive map with the property that

\rho(a^{*}a)=\rho(a)^{*}\rho(a),\quad a\in X.

Then, any state on
B(H)
is
\rho(X)
-convex.

Proof.

For
a\in X
, we see that

\rho(a)^{*}\rho(a)=\rho(a^{*}a)=\rho(aa^{*})

since
B
is commutative. Then, the Schwarz inequality implies that

\rho(a)^{*}\rho(a)\geq\rho(a)\rho(a^{*})

whence

4(\operatorname{Re}\rho(a))^{2}+(\rho(a)^{*}\rho(a)-\rho(a)\rho(a)^{*})\geq 0

for
a\in X
. Lemma
7.1
then implies that every state on
B(H)
is
\rho(X)
-convex.
∎

Next, we identify a natural class of algebras arising from such a map
\rho
.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
B
be a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra and let

be commuting elements satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}^{*}b_{k}=I
. Let
A\subset B
denote the unital norm-closed subalgebra generated by
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
. Then, any state on
B
is
A
-convex.

Proof.

We may assume that there is a Hilbert space
F
such that
B\subset B(F)
.
It follows from
[
14
, Proposition 2]
that there is a Hilbert space
H
containing
F
and commuting normal operators
u_{1},\ldots u_{n}\in B(H)
with
\sum_{k=1}^{n}u_{k}^{*}u_{k}=I
such that
F
is invariant for each
u_{k}
and

b_{k}=u_{k}|_{F},\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

Define a unital completely positive map
\rho:\mathrm{C}^{*}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\to B(F)
as

\rho(t)=P_{F}t|_{F},\quad t\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}).

Then,
\rho(u_{k})=b_{k}
for each
1\leq k\leq n
, and the fact that
F
is invariant for
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
readily implies that

\rho(c^{*}c)=\rho(c)^{*}\rho(c)

for every
c
in the unital algebra generated by
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
. Moreover,
\mathrm{C}^{*}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
is commutative by Fuglede’s theorem. An application of Lemma
8.2
completes the proof.
∎

We close the paper with a discussion of Theorem
8.1
. The result appears very promising at first glance, as its conclusion is a rather close analogue of Bishop’s criterion from the introduction. Recall now that it is shown in
[
16
, Theorem 6.5]
that Bishop’s criterion is equivalent to a genuine peaking condition for subalgebras of
\mathrm{C}(X)
, when the topology on
X
is metrizable (or at least not too pathological). It thus stands to reason that one could adapt the argument of
[
16
]
and use Theorem
8.1
to prove, for instance, that the GNS representation of
\omega
is an
A
-peak representation in the sense of
[
13
]
.

There are some difficulties with this approach, stemming from the fact that the topology on the spectrum of a general
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra need not be Hausdorff. In fact, the class of unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras with Hausdorff spectra is rather restrictive: such a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra must be liminal, in the sense that it only admits finite-dimensional
*
-representations
[
23
, Theorem 1]
.
