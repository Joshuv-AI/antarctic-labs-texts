High-Order Mixed Finite Element Variable Eddington Factor Methods
Samuel Olivier a,b,* and Terry S. Haut c

Abstract
We apply high-order mixed finite element discretization techniques and their associated preconditioned iterative solvers to the Variable Eddington Factor (VEF) equations in two spatial dimensions. The mixed finite element VEF discretizations are coupled to a high-order Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) discretization of the Discrete Ordinates transport equation to form effective linear transport algorithms that are compatible with high-order (curved) meshes. This combination of VEF and transport discretizations is motivated by the use of high-order mixed finite element methods in hydrodynamics calculations at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. Due to the mathematical structure of the VEF equations, the standard Raviart Thomas (RT) mixed finite elements cannot be used to approximate the vector variable in the VEF equations. Instead, we investigate three alternatives based on the use of continuous finite elements for each vector component, a non-conforming RT approach where DG-like techniques are used, and a hybridized RT method. We present numerical results that demonstrate high-order accuracy, compatibility with curved meshes, and robust and efficient convergence in iteratively solving the coupled transport-VEF system and in the preconditioned linear solvers used to invert the discretized VEF equations.

Introduction

The Variable Eddington Factor (VEF) method
[
1
]
, also known as Quasidiffusion
[
2
]
, is an efficient iterative method for solving the Boltzmann transport equation, a crucial component in the modeling of nuclear reactors, high energy density physics experiments, astrophysical phenomena, and medical physics.
In VEF, the transport equation is iteratively coupled to the VEF equations, a moment-based, reduced-dimensional model of transport formed through discrete closures. The VEF closures are weak functions of the transport solution allowing the design of rapidly converging and robust iterative schemes.
A key advantage of VEF is that the discretized VEF and transport equations do not need to be algebraically consistent to maintain this rapid convergence, even in the thick diffusion limit
[
3
]
. These so-called independent VEF methods
[
4
]
then have the flexibility to choose the discretization of the VEF equations to meet the requirements of the overall algorithm, such as computational efficiency and multiphysics compatibility.

Mixed finite element methods are a class of discretization techniques for solving the mixed variational form of a partial differential equation. This variational form is characterized by the inclusion of multiple (typically two) physically disparate quantities resulting in a saddle point problem. By contrast, primal formulations operate on a single quantity and produce minimization problems. Mixed methods were invented to 1) allow incorporation of a constraint (e.g. divergence free velocity in fluid flow), 2) provide direct access to an intermediate variable (e.g. the stress in elasticity), and 3) allow a weaker formulation than the corresponding primal formulation. In the context of neutron diffusion, mixed methods are applied to the first-order, or
P_{1}
, form of radiation diffusion and 1) explicitly include the constraint of particle balance, 2) solve for the current in addition to the scalar flux, and 3) allow scalar flux solutions with no continuity requirements at interior element interfaces.
Through a process called mixed finite element hybridization, the resulting block system of equations can be reduced to a positive definite system that can be efficiently solved with Algebraic Multigrid (AMG)
[
5
]
.
The block system can also be directly preconditioned through block diagonal and lower block triangular preconditioners based on applying AMG to an approximate Schur complement
[
6
]
.

In this paper, we investigate the use of mixed finite elements to solve the VEF equations in two dimensions. The research goals are to achieve high-order accuracy, compatibility with high-order (curved) meshes, and scalable preconditioned iterative solvers. The motivation for this research is that high-order mixed finite element methods on curved meshes are used in hydrodynamics calculations at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL)
[
7
,
8
]
.
In particular, we are interested in designing a discretization of the VEF equations that matches as closely as possible to that of
Maginot and Brunner 2018
, the mixed finite element method used for radiation diffusion at LLNL.
Such a method would 1) have element-local particle balance, 2) solve for the current directly potentially leading to high accuracy coupling to the hydrodynamics’ momentum equation, and 3) allow the scalar flux to be approximated in the same finite element space as the hydrodynamics’ thermodynamic variables.
In addition, a mixed finite element VEF discretization could serve as a drop-in replacement for radiation diffusion at LLNL providing a transport algorithm that allows reuse of the linear and nonlinear solvers already in place for diffusion. Mixed finite element discretizations of radiation diffusion have also been used in reactor analysis
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

Olivier and Morel 2017
developed a lowest-order, hybridized mixed finite element discretization of the VEF equations in one spatial dimension for the linear transport problem.

Lou et al. 2019
and
Lou and Morel 2021
used this algorithm to form efficient, VEF-based thermal radiative transfer and radiation-hydrodynamics algorithms, respectively.
Multi-dimensional, high-order Discontinuous Galerkin VEF discretizations with scalable solvers were developed in
[
16
]
. However, such methods do not directly solve for the current, precluding the possibility of high-order coupling to the momentum equation. Furthermore, a mixed finite element VEF discretization has immediate mathematical and implementational compatibility with the mixed methods used in the hydrodynamics framework of
[
7
]
.

Here, we extend the lowest-order mixed finite element discretization from
Olivier and Morel 2017
to high-order accuracy in two spatial dimensions, prescribe efficient preconditioned iterative solvers for the discretized VEF equations, and compare the performance of the hybridized and unhybridized mixed finite elements techniques.
The extension to multiple dimensions is non-trivial due to the elevation of the VEF closure from a scalar in one spatial dimension to a symmetric tensor in multiple dimensions.
This introduces complications in approximating the vector-valued neutron current that prevent a straightforward extension of the one-dimensional discretization.
An early form of this work was presented in
Olivier et al. 2019
.

The paper proceeds as follows. The VEF algorithm is introduced analytically. We present background on high-order meshes and the finite element spaces used to solve the VEF and transport equations numerically. We discuss the algorithmic connections between the VEF discretizations and a high-order DG discretization of the S
N
transport equations (e.g.
[
18
,
19
]
).
We then apply mixed finite element techniques to the VEF equations. We show that, due to the presence of the Eddington tensor in the VEF first moment equation, the standard Raviart Thomas (RT) mixed finite element methods
[
20
,
21
]
are not appropriate for the VEF equations. We present two alternatives: a method where each component of the current is approximated with continuous finite elements and a non-conforming approach where the RT space is used along with DG-like numerical fluxes.
A lower block triangular preconditioner that uses a combination of classical smoothing and AMG is defined for each of the above VEF discretizations.
We then propose a hybridized version of the RT method that increases the computational efficiency of the RT method by reducing the number of globally coupled unknowns.

Next, numerical results are presented. We investigate the accuracy of the methods on a third-order mesh using the method of manufactured solutions.
Convergence of the fixed-point iteration is tested in the thick diffusion limit on both an orthogonal mesh and a severely distorted third-order mesh generated using a Lagrangian hydrodynamics code.
The robustness of the preconditioned iterative solvers to mesh distortion is also investigated.
The efficiencies of the outer fixed-point iteration and inner preconditioned linear iteration are compared on a challenging, multi-material problem.
We then document the degraded solution quality associated with the method that uses continuous finite elements for each component of the current on a simple radiation diffusion eigenvalue problem and provide intuition for AMG’s inability to effectively precondition the corresponding linear system.
A weak scaling study is presented for the RT and hybridized RT methods showing that the discrete VEF systems can be solved with similar efficiency to that of an analogous symmetric radiation diffusion system.
Finally, we give conclusions and recommendations for future work.

2
VEF Algorithm

Here, we describe the VEF method for the steady-state, mono-energetic, fixed-source transport problem with isotropic scattering. VEF methods simultaneously solve the coupled transport and VEF equations given by:

\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\psi+\sigma_{t}\psi=\frac{\sigma_{s}}{4\pi}\varphi+q\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(1a)

\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})=\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\partial\mathcal{D}\ \mathrm{and}\ \boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}<0\,,

(1b)

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}+\sigma_{a}\varphi=Q_{0}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(2a)

\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\right)+\sigma_{t}\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(2b)

\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}=E_{b}\varphi+2J_{\text{in}}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\partial\mathcal{D}\,,

(2c)

where
\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
is the angular flux,
\varphi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
and
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
the VEF scalar flux and current, respectively,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
the direction of particle flow,
\mathcal{D}
the spatial domain with
\partial\mathcal{D}
its boundary,
\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
,
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
, and
\sigma_{a}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})-\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
the total, scattering, and absorption macroscopic cross sections, respectively,
q(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
the fixed source with
Q_{i}=\int\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{i}q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega
its angular moments, and
\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
the boundary inflow function with
J_{\text{in}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}<0}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,\bar{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega
the inflow partial current. The Eddington tensor and boundary factor are defined as:

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{\int\boldsymbol{\Omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Omega}\,\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\int\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega}\,,

(3a)

E_{b}=\frac{\int|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}|\,\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\int\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega}\,,

(3b)

respectively. The
Miften and Larsen 1993
boundary conditions are used for the VEF equations. Observe that the transport equation (Eqs.
1
) is linearly coupled to the VEF equations (Eqs.
2
) through the scattering source and the VEF equations are nonlinearly coupled to the transport equation through the Eddington tensor and boundary factor.
The VEF closures are weak functions of the transport solution allowing the design of efficient iterative solution techniques.

The simplest algorithm to solve the coupled transport-VEF system is fixed-point iteration. The iteration is: 1) invert the transport equation given a scattering source from the previous iteration or an initial guess, 2) compute the Eddington tensor and boundary factor from the angular flux from stage 1), and 3) invert the VEF equations for a new scalar flux and current. The iteration is repeated until the VEF scalar flux converges. A discussion of the derivation of the above system and the application of advanced nonlinear solvers, such as Anderson acceleration, are provided in
[
16
]
.

The following sections present methods for efficiently computing the VEF fixed-point operator numerically. We present the description of high-order meshes and the procedure for integration over arbitrary elements, the finite element spaces used to discretize the transport and VEF equations, the representation of the VEF data using finite element interpolation and angular quadrature, and finally three novel mixed finite element discretizations for the VEF equations.

3
Mesh and Finite Element Preliminaries

This section provides background on the representation of high-order meshes and the transformations used to facilitate numerical integration over arbitrary elements. We pay particular attention to the transformation of vector-valued functions and their gradient and divergence on high-order meshes. These transformations are crucial for the implementation of the approximation techniques used for the current described in §
4
.

3.1
Description of the Mesh

The domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is tesselated into a collection
\mathcal{T}
of quadrilateral elements
K_{e}
such that

\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{K_{e}\in\mathcal{T}}K_{e}\,.

(4)

Each element
K_{e}
is obtained as
K_{e}=\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}(\hat{K})
where
\hat{K}=[0,1]^{2}
is the reference element. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}(\hat{K})
be the space of polynomials of degree less than or equal to
m
and
n
in the first and second variables, respectively, with
\mathcal{Q}_{m}(\hat{K})=\mathcal{Q}_{m,m}(\hat{K})
. The mapping
\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}\in[\mathcal{Q}_{m}(\hat{K})]^{2}
is derived from a set of global control points and an element-local nodal basis. Figure
1(a)
shows an example mesh where the control points labeled 2, 7, and 12 are shared so that the mesh coordinates are continuous across the interior interface between the two elements.

(a)

(b)

:
Depictions of (a) the mesh control points in a quadratic quadrilateral mesh and (b) the reference transformation used to describe the left element of (a).

A nodal basis for
\mathcal{Q}_{m}(\hat{K})
is defined using Lagrange interpolating polynomials. Let
\xi_{i}
denote the
m+1
Gauss-Lobatto points in the interval
[0,1]
. The
(m+1)^{2}
points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
on the unit square
\hat{K}=[0,1]^{2}
are given by the two-fold Cartesian product of the one-dimensional points. Let
\ell_{i}
denote the Lagrange interpolating polynomial that satisfies
\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})=\delta_{ij}
where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta. The set of functions
\{\ell_{i}\}
form a basis for
\mathcal{Q}_{m}(\hat{K})
.
For each element, the mapping is then

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\sum_{i=1}^{(m+1)^{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{e,i}\ell_{i}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in K_{e}
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\hat{K}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{e,i}
are the control points corresponding to element
K_{e}
. Figure
1(b)
depicts the mesh transformation used for the left element in Fig.
1(a)
.

We define
\Gamma
as the set of unique faces in the mesh with
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma\setminus\partial\mathcal{D}
the set of interior faces and
\Gamma_{b}=\Gamma\cap\partial\mathcal{D}
the set of boundary faces so that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{b}
. We denote the outward unit normal to element
K
as
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}
. On an interior face
\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}
between elements
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, we use the convention that
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
is the unit vector perpendicular to the shared face
K_{1}\cap K_{2}
pointing from
K_{1}
to
K_{2}
. On such an interior face, the jump,
\left\llbracket\cdot\right\rrbracket
, and average,
\left\{\!\!\left\{\cdot\right\}\!\!\right\}
, are defined as

\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket=u_{1}-u_{2}\,,\quad\left\{\!\!\left\{u\right\}\!\!\right\}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{1}+u_{2})\,,\quad\mathrm{on}\ \mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\,,

(6)

where
u_{i}=u|_{\partial K_{i}}
with analogous definitions for vectors. Note that a continuous function
u
satisfies
\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket=0
on each interior face.
On boundary faces, the jump and average are set to

\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket=u\,,\quad\left\{\!\!\left\{u\right\}\!\!\right\}=u\,,\quad\mathrm{on}\ \mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{b}\,,

(7)

and likewise for vector-valued functions on the boundary.
The tangent vector is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and we use the convection that
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is a
90^{\circ}
counter-clockwise rotation of the normal vector.

Finally, we define the “broken” gradient, denoted by
\nabla_{h}
, obtained by applying the gradient locally on each element. That is,

(\nabla_{h}u)|_{K}=\nabla(u|_{K})\,,\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}\,.

(8)

This distinction is important for the piecewise polynomial spaces discussed in §
4
.

3.2
Integration Transformations

The mesh transformations
\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}
are used to facilitate numerical integration on arbitrary elements.
Letting
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi&\eta\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\hat{K}
denote the reference coordinates and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}x&y\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathcal{D}
the physical coordinates such that
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
, the Jacobian of the transformation is

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{e}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}&\frac{\partial x}{\partial\eta}\\
\frac{\partial y}{\partial\xi}&\frac{\partial y}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(9)

with
J_{e}=|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{e}|
its determinant. The partial derivatives of the mesh transformation are computed by taking derivatives of the nodal basis functions. In other words,

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{(m+1)^{2}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{e,i}\otimes\hat{\nabla}\ell_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{(m+1)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{e,i}\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial\xi}&x_{e,i}\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial\eta}\\
y_{e,i}\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial\xi}&y_{e,i}\frac{\partial\ell_{i}}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{e,i}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{e,i}&y_{e,i}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and
\hat{\nabla}
denotes the gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

A mesh transformation is called affine when it can be written as

\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}=\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}\boldsymbol{\xi}+b

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are constant with respect to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. In such case, the Jacobian matrix is
\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}=\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}
and the Hessian of the transformation, defined as
\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}^{2}}
, is identically zero. Quadrilateral elements obtained by scaling, stretching along the
\xi
or
\eta
axes, or rotating the reference element are all affine while general quadrilateral elements, such as trapezoidal elements, and curved elements are not affine.

In this document, integration over the domain is implicitly computed in reference space using the following sum:

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\cdot\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\left(\cdot\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{\hat{K}}\left(\cdot\right)\,J\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(12)

This provides a systematic way to integrate over arbitrary domains composed of arbitrarily shaped elements as well as the use of numerical quadrature rules defined on the reference element
\hat{K}
.
We now discuss the transformations used to represent the integrand of Eq.
12
in reference space.
For a scalar function
u:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, denote by
\hat{u}:\hat{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
its representation in reference space. The functions
u
and
\hat{u}
are related by

u(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\hat{u}(\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}))\,.

(13)

Integration over the physical element is then equivalent to

\int_{K}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{\hat{K}}\hat{u}\,J\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(14)

Using the chain rule, the gradient of a scalar function transforms as

\nabla\hat{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\hat{u}}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\hat{u}}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial\hat{u}}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\hat{u}}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-T}\hat{\nabla}\hat{u}\,.

(15)

In this way, the gradient in physical space can be computed using the Jacobian of the mesh transformation and the gradient in reference space.

For vector-valued functions, the basis the vector is defined on must also be considered. The simplest basis is the canonical basis,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}
, corresponding to the
x
and
y
axes. In this case, a vector
\boldsymbol{v}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is

\boldsymbol{v}=v_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}+v_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}

(16)

and each component transforms independently as
v_{i}=\hat{v}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}))
. Writing

\nabla\boldsymbol{v}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial y}\\
\frac{\partial v_{2}}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial v_{2}}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla v_{1})^{T}\\
(\nabla v_{2})^{T}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(17)

the gradient of a vector defined as in Eq.
16
transforms as

\nabla\boldsymbol{v}=\begin{bmatrix}(\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-T}\hat{\nabla}\hat{v}_{1})^{T}\\
(\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-T}\hat{\nabla}\hat{v}_{2})^{T}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(18)

Note that defining a vector in this way does not preserve the normal or tangential components under a rotation. That is,
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}
are linear combinations of the
v_{i}
instead of a single component representing the normal or tangential components, respectively.

Alternatively, the contravariant Piola transform represents vectors on the so-called tangent basis so that the normal component can be preserved
[
23
,
24
]
. Such a transformation is required by the Raviart Thomas space introduced in §
4.3
in order to strongly enforce continuity in the normal component of the current. The contravariant Piola transform is:

\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}\,.

(19)

Here,
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}:\hat{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a vector in reference space.
Writing the columns of the Jacobian matrix as

\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(20)

the contravariant Piola transformation is equivalent to

\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}(\hat{v}_{1}\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1}+\hat{v}_{2}\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2})\,.

(21)

Observe that, on the reference canonical basis
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{i}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\hat{v}_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{1}+\hat{v}_{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{2}
, and thus the contravariant Piola transform maps the canonical reference basis to the tangent space spanned by
\{\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2}\}
and scales by
1/J
.

When the mesh transformation
\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}
is not affine, the tangent basis is not orthogonal. In this case, the usual method of selecting components of a vector through the dot product (e.g.
v_{i}=\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
) is inappropriate since
\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{j}\neq\delta_{ij}
. Instead, a dual basis, referred to as the cotangent basis, is constructed such that

\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{j}=\delta_{ij}\,.

(22)

Vectors the satisfy Eq.
22
are called bi-orthonormal.
Since the
\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{i}
are the columns of the Jacobian matrix, defining the cotangent basis as the rows of the inverse of the Jacobian matrix satisfies the bi-orthonormality condition since
\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
. In other words, the cotangent basis is defined such that

\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}^{T}\\
\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(23)

For a contravariant vector, the usual method of selecting a component is now replaced with
v_{i}=\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
.
The cotangent space is associated with vectors normal to the faces. By representing a vector on the tangent space, the contravariant Piola transform allows selection of the component representing the normal component through
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
.
Note that for non-affine meshes,
\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}
depends on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and thus the tangent and cotangent bases also depend on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

A depiction of the tangent and cotangent bases at the point
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(0,0)
under a non-affine mesh transformation.

Figure
2
depicts an example non-affine mesh transformation and the tangent and cotangent bases evaluated at the point
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(0,0)
. Observe that the pairs
(\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2})
and
(\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1})
are perpendicular. The pairs
(\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2},\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2})
do not point in the same direction but their magnitudes and directions balance so that
\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{i}=1
. Thus, the bi-orthonormality condition
\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
is satisfied. In addition, the tangent vectors and cotangent vectors are tangential and normal, respectively, to one of the faces connecting at the point
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(0,0)
.

For a contravariant vector,

\int_{K}\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{\hat{K}}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-T}\hat{\nabla}\hat{u}\cdot\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{v}\,J\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}=\int_{\hat{K}}\hat{\nabla}\hat{u}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(24)

The gradient transforms as

\nabla\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla\left(\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{v}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}\right)=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\!\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right)\!\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}

(25)

where

\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}=\frac{1}{J}\hat{\nabla}\!\left(J\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\right)\!\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\,.

(26)

This result is derived by direct computation in Appendix
A
along with the details required to implement this transformation using the machinery commonly provided in finite element libraries. It is also shown that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}=0
when the mesh transformation is affine and that
\tr(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}})=0
for any transformation. This last result is known as the Piola identity
[
23
]
. Using the Piola identity, the linearity of the trace, and the invariance of the trace under similarity transformations, the divergence transforms as

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\tr\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\right)=\frac{1}{J}\tr\!\left(\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\!\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right)\!\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\right)=\frac{1}{J}\hat{\nabla}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\,.

(27)

Thus,

\int_{K}u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{\hat{K}}\hat{u}\,\hat{\nabla}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(28)

Combining the results from Eqs.
24
and
28
yields:

\int_{\partial K}u\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\partial\hat{K}}\hat{u}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hat{s}\,,

(29)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}}
is the normal vector in reference space corresponding to the physical space normal
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
.
In other words, the contravariant Piola transformation preserves the normal component.

In this document, integration is implicitly computed using numerical quadrature on the reference element.
Integration over surfaces is performed over the one-dimensional reference element using the transformed element of length.

4
Finite Element Spaces

In this section, we define the finite element spaces used to approximate the VEF equations. These finite element spaces are defined on the mesh
\mathcal{T}
or the interior skeleton of the mesh
\Gamma_{0}
and consist of an element-local function space and a set of inter-element matching conditions.
The inter-element matching conditions enforce various types of continuity of the solution between elements. The combination of a locally smooth function space and suitable matching conditions allows finite element spaces to be discrete subspaces of Sobolev spaces such as
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
,
H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
, and
H(\div;\mathcal{D})
. The following subsections define the element-local function space and matching conditions used for the scalar flux, current, and the interface variable used later in hybridization of the mixed finite element method.

4.1
Discontinuous Galerkin

The Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) space is a discrete subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, the space of square-integrable functions. In other words, if
u
is an element of the DG space,

\int u^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}<\infty\,.

(30)

Since only square integrability is required, functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, and thus DG spaces, do not need to be continuous. DG functions are represented using piecewise-discontinuous polynomials that are defined on the reference element and mapped to the physical element using the inverse mesh transformation
\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}^{-1}:K_{e}\rightarrow\hat{K}
. In other words, on each element, the solution belongs to:

\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K_{e})=\{u=\hat{u}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}^{-1}:\hat{u}\in\mathcal{Q}_{p}(\hat{K})\}\,.

(31)

The distinction between
\mathcal{Q}_{p}(\hat{K})
and
\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K_{e})
is important for non-affine mesh transformations. In such case, the inverse mesh transformation is generally non-polynomial so that the composition
u=\hat{u}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}_{e}^{-1}
is also non-polynomial.

The degree-
p
DG space is

Y_{p}=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}):u|_{K}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K)\,,\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}\}\,.

(32)

An example of the distribution of the degrees of freedom in a linear DG space on a
3\times 3
mesh is shown in Fig.
3
. Note that degrees of freedom are not shared between elements. Since there are no continuity requirements in the DG space, the basis for the local polynomials can use either open or closed points. That is, a nodal basis can be formed with Lagrange interpolating polynomials through the two-fold Cartesian product of either the closed Gauss-Lobatto points or the open Gauss-Legendre points.

Figure 3
:
A depiction of the distribution of degrees of freedom in the linear DG space. The Legendre nodes are used to illustrate that degrees of freedom are not shared between elements.

4.2
[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}

Here, we define a discrete subspace of
H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
, the space of functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
with square-integrable gradient.
Let the degree-
p
, scalar continuous finite element space be

V_{p}=\{u\in C_{0}(\mathcal{D}):u|_{K}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K)\,,\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}\}

(33)

so that each function
u\in V_{p}
is a piecewise-continuous polynomial mapped from the reference element. Since
u\in V_{p}
is locally smooth and
V_{p}\subset C_{0}(\mathcal{D})
, it can be shown that
V_{p}\subset H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
and, in particular, that
u\in V_{p}
satisfies
\nabla u=\nabla_{h}u\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}

[
25
, Prop. 3.2.1]
. The distribution of degrees of freedom for the space
V_{2}
is shown in Fig.
4
. Here, continuity is enforced by sharing degrees of freedom between elements. Due to this, a nodal basis using closed points, such as the Gauss-Lobatto points, must be used.

The vector-valued analog

W_{p}=\{\boldsymbol{v}:v_{1}\in V_{p}\ \mathrm{and}\ v_{2}\in V_{p}\}

(34)

uses the scalar continuous finite element space for each component. In this way,
\boldsymbol{v}\in W_{p}\subset[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
and thus
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2\times 2}
. Since each component is defined independently using the scalar space, vectors
\boldsymbol{v}\in W_{p}
transform according to Eq.
16
.

4.3
Raviart Thomas

The Raviart Thomas (RT) space is a discrete subspace of
H(\div;\mathcal{D})
, the space of vector-valued functions with square-integrable divergence. That is,

H(\div;\mathcal{D})=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}:\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\}\,.

(35)

The requirements of a discrete subspace are codified in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

(Cf.
Quarteroni and Valli 1994
, Proposition 3.2.2)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that

1.

\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[H^{1}(K)]^{2}
for each
K\in\mathcal{T}

2.

\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
for each
\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}

then
\boldsymbol{v}\in H(\div;\mathcal{D})
. Conversely, if
\boldsymbol{v}\in H(\div;\mathcal{D})
and (a) is satisfied, then (b) holds.

Proof.

It must be shown that, given (a) and (b),
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. We proceed by leveraging the fact that
\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
(since
\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[H^{1}(K)]^{2}
) and then show that
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
, proving the claim.
Let
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
be the space of infinitely differentiable functions that are zero on the boundary of the domain.
Using Green’s identity, we have that for each
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
:

\displaystyle\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=-\int\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

(36)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,

where we have used that
u=0
for each
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\partial\mathcal{D}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[H^{1}(K)]^{2}
for each
K
, we can integrate by parts locally on each element to give:

\displaystyle\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\!\left[\int_{K}u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int_{\partial K}u\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=\int u\,\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int_{\Gamma_{0}}u\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int u\,\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})\,,

since
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
satisfies is continuous such that
\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket=0
and
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
from (b). Therefore,
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

On the other hand, if
\boldsymbol{v}\in H(\div;\mathcal{D})
then
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
and, given
\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[H^{1}(K)]^{2}
, we obtain

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}u\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})\,,

(38)

hence, (b) holds.
∎

Thus, a discrete subspace of
H(\div;\mathcal{D})
must (a) have a smooth function space on each element and (b) have suitable matching conditions so that the normal component is continuous across interior mesh interfaces.

The RT space uses the local polynomial space
\mathcal{Q}_{p+1,p}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{p,p+1}(\hat{K})
. This choice can be motivated by the discrete de Rham complex
[
26
]
in that

\mathcal{Q}_{p+1}(\hat{K})\xrightarrow{\hat{\nabla}\times}\mathcal{Q}_{p+1,p}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{p,p+1}(\hat{K})\xrightarrow{\hat{\nabla}\cdot}\mathcal{Q}_{p}(\hat{K})\,.

(39)

As an example, the lowest-order polynomial space is

\mathcal{Q}_{1,0}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{0,1}(\hat{K})=\spn\!\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\,,\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}\right\}\,,

(40)

and thus we have that:

\hat{\nabla}\cdot\mathcal{Q}_{1,0}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{0,1}(\hat{K})=\spn\!\left\{1\right\}=\mathcal{Q}_{0}(\hat{K})\,.

(41)

The nodal basis for
\mathcal{Q}_{p+1,p}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{p,p+1}(\hat{K})
uses the closed Gauss-Lobatto points in the normal direction and the open Gauss-Legendre points in the tangential direction. The interpolating points for the first three orders are shown in Fig.

. The circles denote degrees of freedom corresponding to the
\xi
component while squares denote the
\eta
component.

The contravariant Piola transformation is used to allow sharing the degrees of freedom associated with the normal component with neighboring elements.
Note that this transformation is still required even when continuity in the normal component is relaxed and enforced weakly with Lagrange multipliers as in the hybridization procedure discussed in §

This is due to the use of anisotropic polynomial interpolation (i.e. different degree polynomials are used in each variable) which requires the vector’s basis to be rotated along with the transformation to properly orient the interpolating polynomials in physical space.
Combining the local function space
\mathcal{Q}_{p+1,p}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{p,p+1}(\hat{K})
with the contravariant Piola transform yields:

\mathbb{D}_{p}(K)=\{\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}:\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{p+1,p}(\hat{K})\times\mathcal{Q}_{p,p+1}(\hat{K})\}\,.

(42)

Here, both the inverse mesh transformation and
1/J
are generally non-polynomial when
\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}
is non-affine.

We now define the degree-
p
RT space as:

\mathit{RT}_{p}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}:\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in\mathbb{D}_{p}(K)\,,\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}\ \text{and}\ \left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0\,,\ \forall\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\}\,.

(43)

Note that since the contravariant Piola transform is used, functions in RT transform according to Eqs.

,

, and
27
.

From Proposition
1
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}
satisfies
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
However, the RT space does not have the continuity to allow a square-integrable gradient. In other words,
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\notin[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2\times 2}
and
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\neq\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}
.

\mathcal{P}_{p}

p
and

\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathcal{F})=\{u=\hat{u}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}:\hat{u}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\hat{\mathcal{F}})\}

(44)

the space of univariate polynomials mapped from the reference line,
\hat{\mathcal{F}}=[0,1]
.

\Lambda_{p}=\{\mu\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{0}):\mu|_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{P}_{p}(\mathcal{F})\,,\quad\forall\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\}\,.

5
Transport Discretizations

We assume the transport equation is discretized with the Discrete Ordinates (S
N
) angular model and an arbitrary-order DG spatial discretization compatible with curved meshes (e.g.
[
18
,
19
]
). The transport equation is collocated at discrete angles
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d}
and integration over the unit sphere is numerically approximated with a suitable angular quadrature rule,
\{w_{d},\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d}\}_{d=1}^{N_{\Omega}}
. Let
\psi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\psi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d})
be the angular flux in the discrete direction
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d}
. The DG discretization uses
\psi_{d}\in Y_{p}
for each discrete angle. Through finite element interpolation,
\psi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})
can be evaluated at any point in the mesh. The VEF data are computed with S
N
angular quadrature and finite element interpolation as:

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{\sum_{d=1}^{N_{\Omega}}w_{d}\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d}\,\psi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{\sum_{d=1}^{N_{\Omega}}w_{d}\psi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}\,,

(46a)

E_{b}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{\sum_{d=1}^{N_{\Omega}}w_{d}\,|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}|\,\psi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{\sum_{d=1}^{N_{\Omega}}w_{d}\psi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}\,.

(46b)

Note that we represent the VEF data as ratios of DG grid functions. That is, on each element
K
, each component of the Eddington tensor and the boundary factor can be written as
q/p
where
q,p\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K)
and are thus improper rational polynomials mapped from the reference element.
Note that
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is defined on the canonical basis
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{i}
. Thus, each component of the Eddington tensor transforms independently as a scalar and the Piola transform is not required to map the Eddington tensor between reference and physical space.

Since
\psi_{d}\in Y_{p}
can be discontinuous across interior mesh interfaces, the VEF data can also be discontinuous. Thus, global derivatives of the VEF data are not well defined and, in particular, the Eddington tensor is not single-valued on interior mesh interfaces. The VEF discretizations presented here are designed to avoid the need for derivatives of the Eddington tensor and, when needed, use the average to provide a single-valued approximation of the Eddington tensor on interior mesh faces.
The boundary factor is only needed on the boundary of the domain (i.e.
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\Gamma_{b}
) and is thus always single valued.

We consider problems where
\psi\geq\delta
in the domain for some
\delta>0
. This assumption is reasonable for our applications but may not apply in shielding or deep penetration problems. Where necessary, negative flux fixups are used to ensure that
\psi>0
numerically since positive angular fluxes are crucial for generating physically realistic VEF data.
Note that the Eddington tensor and boundary factor are angular flux-weighted averages of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}|
, respectively. Combined with the positivity assumption, this means that each component of the Eddington tensor and the boundary factor are bounded functions in space such that
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\in[L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})]^{2\times 2}
and
E_{b}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
.

The VEF scalar flux is coupled to the transport equation in the scattering source. A mixed-space scattering mass matrix is used to support the use of differing finite element spaces for the angular flux and VEF scalar flux. That is, the scattering source is built using test functions and trial functions from the spaces corresponding to the angular and VEF scalar fluxes, respectively.

6
Mixed Finite Element Discretizations

We now derive mixed finite element discretizations of the VEF equations with Miften-Larsen boundary conditions. We seek approximations to the scalar flux and current in the finite-dimensional spaces
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{V}
, respectively, and test the zeroth and first moments with functions in the spaces
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, respectively. We consider Galerkin discretizations so that the test and trial spaces for the scalar flux and current are the same. In other words, we restrict ourselves to the case that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}
. We proceed by first informally deriving the weak form assuming the spaces
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{V}
have the requisite regularity to allow the resulting weak form to be well defined. We will see that there is no ambiguity in the choice
\mathcal{E}=Y_{p}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. However, due to the presence of the Eddington tensor, the standard Raviart Thomas methods are inappropriate and so two choices for
\mathcal{V}
are presented: a method with
\mathcal{V}=W_{p+1}\subset[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
and a non-conforming method where
\mathcal{V}=\mathit{RT}_{p}\subset H(\div;\mathcal{D})
.

6.1
Weak Form

Multiplying the zeroth and first moments with sufficiently smooth functions
u
and
\boldsymbol{v}
, respectively, and integrating over the domain yields:

\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,

(47a)

\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,.

(47b)

The differentiability requirements on the Eddington tensor and the VEF scalar flux can be reduced by integrating the first moment equation by parts:

\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,

(48a)

\int_{\partial\mathcal{D}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,\bar{\varphi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,

(48b)

where
\varphi=\bar{\varphi}
on the boundary of the domain. We have used Green’s identity for a tensor multiplied by a vector:

\int\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\oint\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,

(49)

where

\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}:\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}A_{ij}B_{ij}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\rm{A}},\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\,.

(50)

Integrating by parts moves derivatives from the Eddington tensor and VEF scalar flux to the test function
\boldsymbol{v}
allowing weaker requirements for both
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}
and
\varphi
. In addition, we assume
\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathcal{V}
has enough regularity to allow
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
(i.e.
\mathcal{V}\subset H(\div;\mathcal{D})
) so that
\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
is well defined. Thus, we can unambiguously take
u,\varphi\in\mathcal{E}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

However, the test function
\boldsymbol{v}
now has increased regularity requirements. Namely, we must have
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
instead of the typical requirement that
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
In the thick diffusion limit,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
and this requirement reduces to
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{1}{3}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
In this case, RT methods apply directly for both
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{J}
. However, for a general Eddington tensor, the RT space does not have the continuity requirements to allow the term
\int\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}<\infty
. This requirement is investigated in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

For a tensor
\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in[L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})]^{2\times 2}
satisfying
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
, let
\boldsymbol{v}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that

1.

\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[H^{1}(K)]^{2}
for each
K\in\mathcal{T}

2.

\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
for each
\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}

then
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
. Conversely, if
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and (a) is satisfied, then (b) holds.

Proof.

Let
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
denote the space of infinitely differentiable functions that are zero on the boundary of the domain.
Using Green’s identity, the following holds for each
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
:

\displaystyle\int\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=-\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}u\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=-\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{K}\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\cdot\nabla\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}u\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

(51)

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\!\left[\int_{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int_{\partial K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\,u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,

where we have used (b) to cancel the integration over
\Gamma_{0}
.
The above identifies
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}
with
\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}
.
Given
\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in[L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})]^{2\times 2}
and (a),
\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and thus we have that
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}=\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

On the other hand, if
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, then
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}=\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}
and, given
\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in[H^{1}(K)]^{2}
, we obtain

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})\,,

(52)

hence, (b) holds.
∎

Observe that Proposition
2
reduces to Proposition
1
when
\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}=\boldsymbol{\rm{I}}
.
Due to the DG interpolation used to approximate the angular flux, the Eddington tensor does not satisfy
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and thus Proposition
2
does not apply directly.
However, we can consider approximating the Eddington tensor by projecting it onto a space that satisfies this requirement. In such case, Proposition
2
implies that

\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\iff\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0\,,\quad\forall\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\,.

(53)

Figure

depicts an example of the Eddington tensor rotating and scaling the normal vector, altering the continuity requirement of the space. Note that since the Eddington tensor is symmetric positive definite,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}>0
and thus
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. In other words, the Eddington tensor cannot rotate the normal past a direction tangential to the face.
This altered continuity requirement makes standard RT methods an inappropriate choice for the test function
\boldsymbol{v}
.

In light of Eq.
53
, the weak form in Eq.
48
will hold only when the space
\mathcal{V}
is chosen so that both
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
and
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
on all interior faces. These conditions can only be met by using
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathcal{V}\subset[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
so that all components of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{J}
are continuous. A Petrov-Galerkin discretization where the test space satisfies
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
and the trial space satisfies
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
may be possible. In this case, the test space would need to use a more general Piola transform that preserves the
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
component of a vector, making the test space dependent on the angular flux.
Furthermore, Proposition
2
indicates this approach would require the use of an approximate projection of the Eddington tensor that satisfies
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
, which could degrade solution quality on problems with steep solution gradients in parts of the domain.
The Petrov-Galerkin discretization is not considered here due to these complications.
Alternatively, non-conforming, DG-like techniques can be used to allow use of the RT space for both the test and trial spaces. That is, both
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathcal{V}=\mathit{RT}_{p}\subset H(\div;\mathcal{D})
and the discontinuity in
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
is handled with numerical fluxes.

Setting
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathcal{V}\subset[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}

Background on the discrete inf-sup condition is provided in Appendix

in the context of the Poisson equation.

The discretization is complete by supplying boundary conditions. Solving the Miften-Larsen boundary conditions (Eq.

\varphi

\bar{\varphi}=\frac{1}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}-2J_{\text{in}}\right)\,.

(54)

The
[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}\times L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
mixed finite element VEF discretization is then: find
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{J})\in Y_{p}\times W_{p+1}

\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,,

(55a)

-\int\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+2\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,J_{\text{in}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in W_{p+1}\,.

(55b)

Equation
18
is used to compute the gradient and divergence of
\boldsymbol{v}\,,\boldsymbol{J}\in W_{p+1}
in reference space.

Using

is simple to implement in that it relies only on the scalar continuous finite element space and does not require interior face bilinear forms.
However, this choice has been seen to degrade both solution quality and solver performance due to allowing non-physical, spurious modes. These so-called checkerboard modes are a well-known issue with
[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}\times L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
discretizations in the context of fluid flow
[
27
]
and are a consequence of the mismatch between the spaces
\nabla\cdot W_{p+1}
and
Y_{p}

\mathcal{V}\subset[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
is either too small with respect to

or too large, allowing spurious modes for

8.5
in the context of radiation diffusion. Furthermore, Appendix
B.2
investigates these modes analytically on a lowest-order, single-element Poisson problem.

Raviart Thomas

If
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathcal{V}\subset H(\div;\mathcal{D})
, a non-conforming approach must be used for the first moment equation due to the presence of the Eddington tensor. We proceed by locally integrating the first moment equation by parts on each element so that the global gradient of
\boldsymbol{v}\in H(\div;\mathcal{D})
is avoided. This requires the introduction of an auxiliary equation, referred to as the numerical flux, that approximates the product of the Eddington tensor and VEF scaluar flux on interior mesh interfaces.
The local weak form for the first moment corresponding to each element
K
is:

\int_{\partial K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{K}\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{p}(K)\,,

(56)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}
is the aformentioned numerical flux for the Eddington tensor and scalar flux.
Summing over all elements
K\in\mathcal{T}
:

\int_{\Gamma}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\right\rrbracket\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}\,.

(57)

We have used the fact that on a face
\mathcal{F}=K_{1}\cap K_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{1}=-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{2}
and the definitions of the jump and broken gradient in Eqs.
6
and
8
, respectively.
In addition, we assume the use of a so-called conservative numerical flux such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
is single-valued on interior faces. In other words, the numerical flux satisfies

\left\llbracket\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0\,,\quad\left\{\!\!\left\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\}\!\!\right\}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,,\quad\text{on}\ \mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\,,

(58)

where the average is defined in Eq.
6
.
In this form, only the gradient restricted to each element is required of the test function,
\boldsymbol{v}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in\mathbb{D}(K)\subset[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
on each

\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2\times 2}
is well defined.

We now define the numerical flux and boundary conditions. The resulting discretization will provide optimal accuracy if
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
is an optimal approximation of the true value of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
on interior faces.
A conservative numerical flux that satisfies this requirement is:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}=\left\{\!\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\}\!\!\right\}\!\left\{\!\!\left\{\varphi\right\}\!\!\right\}\,,\quad\mathrm{on}\ \mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\,.

(59)

While many choices of the numerical flux are possible, we show below that this particular choice of numerical flux has the benefit of limiting to a standard RT discretization of radiation diffusion in the thick diffusion limit.
The Miften-Larsen boundary conditions are applied with

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}-2J_{\text{in}}\right)\,,\quad\mathrm{on}\ \mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{b}\,.

(60)

This is derived by solving Eq.
2c
for the scalar flux and multiplying by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
. The
Y_{p}\times\mathit{RT}_{p}
discretization is then: find
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{J})\in Y_{p}\times\mathit{RT}_{p}
such that

\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,,

(61a)

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left\{\!\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\}\!\!\right\}\right\rrbracket\left\{\!\!\left\{\varphi\right\}\!\!\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+2\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,J_{\text{in}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}\,.

(61b)

Since RT vectors use the contravariant Piola transform, we substitute
\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
in all terms involving
\boldsymbol{v}
and use Eqs.
25
and
27
to evaluate
\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}
and
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}
, respectively.

In the thick diffusion limit,
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
and

\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left\{\!\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\}\!\!\right\}\right\rrbracket=\frac{1}{3}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0\,,

(62)

since
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}
has a continuous normal component. Furthermore,
\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\frac{1}{3}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
. This discretization with this choice of numerical flux is then equivalent to the standard RT discretization of diffusion in the thick diffusion limit.

The RT space satisfies
\nabla\cdot\mathit{RT}_{p}=Y_{p}
avoiding the spurious modes seen for the
[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}\times L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
discretization. This allows superior solution quality and excellent solver performance. However, the RT method is more complex due to the need for interior face bilinear forms, the contravariant Piola transform, and the comparatively less simple RT space.

where for

\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muJ\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,

Note that the integration transformations described in §

.

We use a lower block triangular preconditioner of the form

\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}\\
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(65)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}
is an approximation to the Schur complement
\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}=\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}_{a}-\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\rm{G}}
. Block preconditioners seek to modify the system such that it has a minimal polynomial with small degree
[
6
]
. Iterative solvers with an optimality condition, such as GMRES, can then converge in a small number of iterations. However, computing the generally dense Schur complement and exactly inverting it are impractical. Instead, we use an approximate Schur complement formed from a sparse approximation to
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{-1}
and sparse matrix multiplication. That is, we use

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}=\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}_{a}-\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\rm{G}}

(66)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}
is the lumped mass matrix and boundary term. On elements with no boundary faces (i.e.
\partial K\cap\Gamma_{b}=\emptyset
), the lumping procedure is to sum the rows of the matrix into the diagonal. This is computed on the element-local matrix as:

\tilde{A}^{e}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\sum_{k}A_{ik}^{e}\,,&i=j\\
0\,,&i\neq j\end{cases}\,,

(67)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}
are the unlumped and lumped matrices associated with the degrees of freedom corresponding to element
K_{e}
, respectively.
On elements with a boundary face, the boundary integral over
\Gamma_{b}
contributes. Due to the Eddington tensor,
\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}
couples degrees of freedom corresponding to the normal and tangential components of
\boldsymbol{v}
. We leverage the block structure of the local matrices to lump the boundary elements. Let

\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}_{11}&\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}_{21}&\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}_{22}\end{bmatrix}

(68)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{e}_{ij}
is the sub-block corresponding to the degrees of freedom of the
i^{th}
and
j^{th}
components of the test and trial functions, respectively. We then lump each of these sub-blocks separately so that:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}_{11}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}_{12}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}_{21}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(69)

The lumped local matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}
is diagonal by vector component. That is, each row has at most two entries corresponding to the two components of a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
This lumping procedure allows approximation of the boundary terms and has an inverse that can be computed efficiently without fill-in.

For both interior and boundary elements, the local matrices
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{e}
are assembled into the global matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}
. For rows corresponding to interior degrees of freedom, the lumped matrix is diagonal and thus the inverse is simply
1/\tilde{A}_{ii}
. For rows corresponding to boundary degrees of freedom,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}
is a diagonal matrix for each vector component. The inverse is computed by gathering the entries corresponding to each vector component into a
2\times 2
matrix, inverting it, and scattering the inverse back to a sparse matrix representing
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{-1}
.
The above lumping procedure results in a sparse
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}}^{-1}
that approximates the true inverse
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{-1}
.
Finally, the lumped Schur complement is formed with sparse matrix multiplication according to Eq.
66
. Note that computing the Schur complement is numerically analogous to eliminating the current in the analytic equations to form a second-order, elliptic partial differential equation. Thus, one Algebraic Multigrid (AMG) V-cycle is expected to be a spectrally equivalent approximation to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}^{-1}
.

The approximate inverse of the block preconditioner in Eq.
65
is applied with forward substitution. In other words, we solve

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}\\
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}\\
r_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(70)

by approximately solving the block problems:

\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}x_{1}=r_{1}\,,

(71a)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}x_{2}=r_{2}-\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}x_{1}\,.

(71b)

We stress that the sub-problems in Eqs.
71
do not need to be solved exactly.
In fact, one iteration of Jacobi smoothing and one AMG V-cycle applied to
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}
, respectively, often lead to a scalable preconditioner.
More accurately solving the sub-problems (e.g. using more than one Jacobi/AMG iteration or nested iteration) generally improves robustness to problem size but typically not to the extent that fewer Jacobi iterations and AMG V-cycles are performed.
This behavior is investigated in §
8.6
where we compare the scaling of solving the RT VEF system using one and three AMG V-cycles per preconditioner application as well as the effect of using one iteration of Gauss-Seidel, a more expensive and thus more robust smoother, to approximate
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}^{-1}
.

7
Hybridization

A hybridized version of the RT mixed method is obtained by relaxing the continuity requirements of the space
\mathit{RT}_{p}
and reimposing them weakly. Removing the continuity requirement from
\mathit{RT}_{p}
yields the broken space

\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}:\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in\mathbb{D}_{k}(K)\,,\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}\}\,.

(72)

This space is equivalent to
\mathit{RT}_{p}
on each element but
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
does not have the matching conditions that strongly enforce continuity in the normal component. Note that
\mathit{RT}_{p}\subset\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
and that
\boldsymbol{v}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
belongs to
\mathit{RT}_{p}
if and only if
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
on all interior mesh interfaces. In other words, the mixed problem can be reformulated to use the space
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
instead of
\mathit{RT}_{p}
by adding the constraint that
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
for each
\mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}
. The methods presented in this section enforce this constraint with a Lagrange multiplier.

Hybridized methods are attractive for three reasons. First, since
\boldsymbol{J}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
and
\varphi\in Y_{p}
are both discontinuous, their degrees of freedom are coupled only locally on each element. It is then possible to locally eliminate the scalar flux and current arriving at a system of equations for just the Lagrange multiplier. This reduced system is much smaller than the original
2\times 2
system. Second, the reduced system for the Lagrange multiplier will be positive definite and AMG can be applied directly, avoiding the need for block preconditioners. Finally, the Lagrange multiplier provides an additional approximation for the scalar flux not provided by the original mixed problem.

Since the VEF equations are not symmetric, the variational principles typically used to derive hybridized mixed finite element methods are not appropriate. We first show the derivation of a hybridized method for the symmetric case of radiation diffusion using variational principles. This method is extended to the VEF equations by emulating the properties of the symmetric case. Finally, we discuss the details of an efficient implementation.

7.1
Derivation for Radiation Diffusion

In this section, we provide background on the dual, mixed, and hybrid variational forms associated with the symmetric radiation diffusion system with Dirichlet boundary conditions:

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}+\sigma_{a}\varphi=Q_{0}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(73a)

\nabla\varphi+3\sigma_{t}\boldsymbol{J}=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(73b)

\varphi=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\partial\mathcal{D}\,,

(73c)

where the source has been assumed to be isotropic. This coupled system can be viewed as the first moment equation (Eq.
73b
) with the constraint of particle balance (Eq.
73a
).
The RT mixed finite element discretization of radiation diffusion is: find
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{J})\in Y_{p}\times\mathit{RT}_{p}
such that

\int 3\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=0\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}\,,

(74a)

\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,.

(74b)

Our goal is to identify the variational problem associated with this mixed finite element discretization, modify it to support the use of the broken RT space,
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
, and derive the hybridized system of equations that solves this modified variational problem.
We follow
Quarteroni and Valli 1994
in the presentation of these topics.

The dual formulation is to minimize the so-called complementary energy functional:

I(\boldsymbol{J})=\frac{1}{2}\int 3\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

(75)

under the constraint of particle balance.
Quarteroni and Valli 1994
shows that this constrained minimization problem and Eqs.
73
are equivalent formulations. Mixed methods enforce the particle balance constraint using a Lagrange multiplier,
\varphi
. Let the Lagrangian functional be
\mathcal{L}:\mathit{RT}_{p}\times Y_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi)=I(\boldsymbol{J})-\left(\int\varphi\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{1}{2}\int\sigma_{a}\,\varphi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\varphi\,Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\right)\,.

(76)

By minimizing over
\boldsymbol{J}
and maximizing over
\varphi
, we can minimize
I(\boldsymbol{J})
while enforcing particle balance. To see this, observe that, for
\boldsymbol{J}
fixed,

-\left(\int\varphi\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{1}{2}\int\sigma_{a}\,\varphi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\varphi\,Q\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\right)

(77)

is a concave quadratic functional with respect to
\varphi
and is maximized when particle balance occurs such that
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}+\sigma_{a}\varphi=Q
. The resulting mixed variational problem is then written:

\inf_{\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}}\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{p}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi)\,.

(78)

Such a problem finds the “saddle point” that balances the convex functional
I(\boldsymbol{J})
with the concave particle balance constraint. Since
I(\boldsymbol{J})
and the particle balance constraint are quadratic functionals, the saddle point occurs at
\nabla\mathcal{L}=0
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{J}}=\int 3\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=0\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathit{RT}_{p}\,,

(79a)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\varphi}=-\int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=0\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,.

(79b)

Observe that this system of equations for the saddle point exactly matches the mixed discretization in Eq.
74
.
Thus, the solution of the mixed discretization is also the saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
.

We now wish to modify
\mathcal{L}
to define a variational problem with an equivalent solution that allows use of the broken RT space
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
. This is accomplished by searching for
\boldsymbol{J}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
and adding an additional constraint that the normal component of the current is continuous such that
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
.
Let
\hat{\mathcal{L}}:\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}\times Y_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi)=I(\boldsymbol{J})-\left(\int\varphi\,\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\frac{1}{2}\int\sigma_{a}\,\varphi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\varphi\,Q_{0}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\right)\,,

(80)

be the broken Lagrangian functional. Since
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
and
Y_{p}
are piecewise discontinuous,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{L}
on each element due to the use of the broken divergence.
The constrained variational problem is then:

\inf_{\boldsymbol{J}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}}\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{p}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi)\,,\quad\text{such that}\ \left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0\,.

(81)

As with particle balance, the normal continuity constraint is imposed with a Lagrange multiplier. Defining
\mathcal{H}:\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}\times Y_{p}\times\Lambda_{p}
as

\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi,\lambda)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi,\lambda)+\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\lambda\,\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,

(82)

the constrained saddle point problem is equivalent to:

\inf_{\boldsymbol{J}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}}\sup_{\varphi\in Y_{p}}\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{p}}\mathcal{H}(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi,\lambda)\,.

(83)

If
(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi,\lambda)
is a solution of Eq.
83
, then
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
, otherwise setting
\lambda=\lambda_{\tau}=\tau\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket
for some
\tau>0
would give

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\lambda_{\tau}\,\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\tau\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(84)

and thus

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi,\lambda_{\tau})=\infty\,.

(85)

In other words, the supremum over
\lambda
drives the solution towards currents with continuous normal components.
The solution of the hybridized variational form is found by setting
\nabla\mathcal{H}=0
:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\boldsymbol{J}}=\int 3\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\lambda\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}\,,

(86a)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\varphi}=-\int u\,\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=0\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,,

(86b)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\lambda}=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\mu\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall\mu\in\Lambda_{p}\,.

(86c)

Since
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
and
Y_{p}
are discontinuous spaces, the hybridized mixed method is equivalent to:

\int_{K}3\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}-\int_{K}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\,\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\partial K\cap\Gamma_{0}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}\lambda\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{p}(K)\,,\ K\in\mathcal{T}\,,

(87a)

\int_{K}u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}|_{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{K}\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{K}u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K)\,,\ K\in\mathcal{T}\,,

(87b)

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\mu\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall\mu\in\Lambda_{p}\,.

(87c)

Here, it can be seen that the degrees of freedom for the scalar flux and current are no longer globally coupled. In fact, if
\lambda
were known, the scalar flux and current could be recovered by solving element-local radiation diffusion problems where
\lambda
plays the role of a weak boundary condition applied on each element. Note that the non-zero boundary condition
\varphi=\bar{\varphi}
for
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\Gamma_{b}
can be applied by subtracting
\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,\bar{\varphi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
from the right hand side of Eq.
86a
or equivalently by subtracting
\int_{\partial K\cap\Gamma_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}\,\bar{\varphi}
from the right hand side of Eq.
87a
.

In hybridization, continuity of the normal component is enforced weakly (e.g. see Eq.
87c
).
However, it is well known that the resulting discrete solution will actually satisfy continuity of the normal component in a strong sense (e.g. see
Dobrev et al. 2019
where hybridization is cast as an algebraic technique similar to static condensation).
To see this, consider the bilinear form
b:\Lambda_{p}\times\Lambda_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

b(\mu,\chi)=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\mu\chi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,.

(88)

Note that
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is an inner product on the space
\Lambda_{p}
and thus

b(\mu,\chi)=0\quad\forall\mu\in\Lambda_{p}\iff\chi=0\,,

(89)

since
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is positive definite.
For
\boldsymbol{J}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
,
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\in\Lambda_{p}
so that Eq.
87c
is equivalent to the statement

b(\mu,\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket)=0\quad\forall\mu\in\Lambda_{p}\,,

(90)

which, by Eq.
89
, holds if and only if the normal component of the current is pointwise continuous such that
\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket=0
.
The strong statement of continuity of the normal component is then implied by the weak statement in Eq.
87c
, meaning the hybridized current will in fact have a continuous normal component.

7.2
Extension to VEF

The above variational process cannot be applied directly to the VEF equations due to their lack of symmetry. Without symmetry, it is unclear which potential the weak VEF equations correspond to or whether it would have a unique, global saddle point found by setting its gradient to zero. However, we can define a hybrid method for the VEF equations by mimicking the properties seen above for the symmetric case. In particular, we use the broken RT space,
\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}
, and a Lagrange multiplier that 1) weakly enforces continuity of the normal component of the current and 2) provides inter-element boundary conditions for element-local VEF problems. As in the symmetric case, this will allow elimination of the scalar flux and current, leading to a smaller system for just the Lagrange multiplier where AMG can be applied directly. However, since the resulting method cannot be derived from a variational principle it is unclear whether the resulting hybrid formulation will be equivalent to the original mixed formulation.

The hybridized diffusion method can be extended to the VEF equations with Miften-Larsen boundary conditions by replacing the diffusion first moment with the VEF first moment equation and using the boundary condition
\bar{\varphi}=\frac{1}{E_{b}}(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}-2J_{\text{in}})
.
This can be accomplished by using the element-local weak form of the first moment equation in Eq.
56
and setting

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}=\left\{\!\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\}\!\!\right\}\lambda\,,\quad\mathrm{on}\ \mathcal{F}\in\Gamma_{0}\,.

(91)

Here, we are using the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\in\Lambda_{p}
as a single-valued approximation for the scalar flux on interior faces.
The numerical flux on the boundary is the same as in Eq.
60
. For each
K
, the element-local VEF problem is then:

\int_{\partial K\cap\Gamma_{0}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left\{\!\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}\right\}\!\!\right\}\lambda\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{K}\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\partial K\cap\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}\right)\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
=\int_{K}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+2\int_{\partial K\cap\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}_{K}\,J_{\text{in}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{D}_{p}(K)\,,

(92a)

\int_{K}u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{J}|_{K}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{K}\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{K}u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}(K)\,.

(92b)

The resulting hybrid VEF method is: find
(\boldsymbol{J},\varphi,\lambda)\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}\times Y_{p}\times\Lambda_{p}
such that

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left\{\!\!\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\}\!\!\right\}\right\rrbracket\lambda\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int\nabla_{h}\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int\sigma_{t}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\!\left(\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+2\int_{\Gamma_{b}}\frac{1}{E_{b}}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\,J_{\text{in}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}\,,

(93a)

\int_{K}u\,\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}+\int_{K}\sigma_{a}\,u\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\int_{K}u\,Q_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,,

(93b)

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\mu\left\llbracket\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathrm{n}}\right\rrbracket\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0\,,\quad\forall\mu\in\Lambda_{p}\,.

(93c)

Observe that this represents element-local VEF problems where the boundary conditions are provided either by the Miften-Larsen boundary conditions on the boundary of the domain or by the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
for interior elements. Thus, if
\lambda
were known, the scalar flux and current could be solved for independently on each element.
In addition, since Eq.
93c
is unmodified from the diffusion case (Eq.
87c
), the normal component of the current will be continuous.

\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{W}}\!\left(\hat{\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mug\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}-\boldsymbol{\rm{C}}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\lambda\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\right)+\boldsymbol{\rm{X}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\\
\boldsymbol{\rm{Y}}\!\left(\hat{\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mug\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}-\boldsymbol{\rm{C}}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5mu\lambda\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\right)+\boldsymbol{\rm{Z}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\end{bmatrix}\,.

(103)

In this way, only
\dim(\Lambda_{p})
globally coupled unknowns must be solved for as opposed to the
\dim(\mathit{RT}_{p})+\dim(Y_{p})
required by the original mixed formulation.

In practice, the blocks of the inverse
\boldsymbol{\rm{W}}
,
\boldsymbol{\rm{X}}
,
\boldsymbol{\rm{Y}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\rm{Z}}
are formed using dense matrix operations applied on the element-local matrices corresponding to the degrees of freedom of a single element. The local matrices are then broadcast to a global sparse matrix in order to perform the sparse matrix multiplication required to form the reduced system for the Lagrange multiplier. The Lagrange multiplier can be scalably solved for by preconditioning
\boldsymbol{\rm{H}}
with AMG. In addition, recovering the scalar flux and current is a post-processing step that is independent on each element and thus scales optimally.

8
Results

The VEF algorithms presented here were implemented using the MFEM
[
28
,
29
]
finite element framework. The stabilized bi-conjugate gradient (BiCGStab) solver from MFEM was used to solve the discretized VEF equations. Lower block triangular preconditioners were built using MFEM’s Jacobi smoother and BoomerAMG from the sparse linear algebra package
hypre

[
30
]
. KINSOL, from the Sundials package
[
31
]
, provided the fixed-point and Anderson-accelerated fixed-point solvers.
As described in
Hindmarsh et al. 2005
, the fixed-point and Anderson-accelerated fixed-point iteration is terminated when the max norm of the difference between successive iterates is below the iterative tolerance.
The parallel implementation of the sparse direct solver SuperLU
[
32
]
is used when preconditioned iterative solvers results are not presented.
The streaming and collision operator is inverted using the transport solver from
[
19
]
.
Unless otherwise noted, the angular flux and VEF scalar flux are approximated using the same degree finite element spaces. However, the positive Bernstein polynomials
[
33
]
are used for the transport discretization’s local polynomial basis whereas the Lagrange basis through the Gauss-Legendre points is used for the VEF scalar flux.
The positive transport basis facilitates the application of the quadratic programming negative flux fixup from
[
34
]
that is used on the crooked pipe problem in §
8.4
.

Since all methods produce a VEF scalar flux in
Y_{p}
, the methods are parameterized by their choice of space for the current. Thus, we refer to the
Y_{p}\times W_{p+1}
,
Y_{p}\times\mathit{RT}_{p}
, and
Y_{p}\times\hat{\mathit{RT}}_{p}\times\Lambda_{p}
methods as H1, RT, and HRT, respectively.

8.1
Method of Manufactured Solutions

The accuracy of the methods are determined with the Method of Manufactured Solutions (MMS). The solution is set to

\psi=\frac{1}{4\pi}\!\left[\alpha(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+\boldsymbol{\Omega}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Omega}:\boldsymbol{\rm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\right]\,,

(104)

where

\alpha(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)+\delta\,,

(105a)

\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(x+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(y+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\\
\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(x+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(y+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\end{bmatrix}\,,

(105b)

\boldsymbol{\rm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi(x+\zeta)}{1+2\zeta}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi(y+\zeta)}{1+2\zeta}\right)&\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(x+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(y+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\\
\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(x+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi(y+\omega)}{1+2\omega}\right)&\frac{1}{4}\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi(x+\zeta)}{1+2\zeta}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi(y+\zeta)}{1+2\zeta}\right)\end{bmatrix}\,.

(105c)

Here,
\delta=1.25
is used to ensure
\psi>0
and
\zeta=0.1
and
\omega=0.05
are used to test spatially dependent, non-isotropic inflow boundary conditions. The domain is
\mathcal{D}=[0,1]^{2}
.
With this definition:

\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\alpha(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+\frac{1}{3}\tr\boldsymbol{\rm{\Theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\,,

(106a)

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\,,

(106b)

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{\alpha(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})}{3}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}+\frac{1}{15}\begin{bmatrix}3\Theta_{11}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+\Theta_{22}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})&\Theta_{12}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\\
\Theta_{21}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})&\Theta_{11}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})+3\Theta_{22}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}})\end{bmatrix}\,.

(106c)

This leads to an exact Eddington tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}/\phi
that is dense and spatially varying. The MMS
\psi
and
\phi
are substituted into the transport equation to solve for the MMS source
q
that forces the solution to Eq.
104
.

The accuracy of the VEF discretizations are investigated in isolation by computing the VEF data from the MMS angular flux and setting the sources
Q_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
to the moments of the MMS source. This is accomplished by projecting the MMS angular flux onto a degree-
p
DG finite element space and using Level Symmetric
S_{4}
angular quadrature to compute the VEF data, the moments of the MMS source, and the inflow boundary function. The VEF equations are then solved as if
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}
,
E_{b}
,
Q_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
, and
J_{\text{in}}
are given data. Errors are calculated with the
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
norm for scalars and the
[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
norm for vectors given by

\|u\|=\sqrt{\int u^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}\,,

(107)

and

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=\sqrt{\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}}\,,

(108)

respectively. We also use the
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
projection operator
\Pi_{p}:L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\rightarrow Y_{p}
such that

\int u(v-\Pi_{p}v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=0\,,\quad\forall u\in Y_{p}\,,

(109)

for some
v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.
In particular,
\Pi_{p}
is used to project the exact MMS scalar flux onto a
Y_{p}
finite element grid function in order to investigate a superconvergence property of mixed finite elements.

8.2
Thick Diffusion Limit

The convergence of the VEF methods are investigated in the thick diffusion limit. The material data are set to

\sigma_{t}=1/\epsilon\,,\quad\sigma_{a}=\epsilon\,,\quad\sigma_{s}=1/\epsilon-\epsilon\,,\quad q=\epsilon\,,

(113)

where
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and the thick diffusion limit corresponds to the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. We use two coarse meshes that do not resolve the mean free path to stress the convergence of the VEF method. The first is an orthogonal
8\times 8
mesh with
\mathcal{D}=[0,1]^{2}
. The second is the triple point mesh shown in Fig.
11
, a third-order mesh generated with a Lagrangian hydrodynamics code where
\mathcal{D}=[0,7]\times[0,3]
.
On the triple point mesh, the streaming and collision operator cannot be reordered to be lower block triangular by element due to the presence of reentrant/concave faces.
A standard transport sweep can be applied by iteratively lagging the strictly upper block triangular components of the streaming and collision operator.
The pseudo-optimal element reordering proposed in
Haut et al. 2019
is used to minimize the amount of information lagged due to reentrant faces.
Since the angular flux is only approximately inverted at each iteration it is expected that iterative efficiency will degrade compared to an analogous problem on a straight-edged mesh.
In addition, highly distorted elements have poor approximation properties. We use Level Symmetric
S_{4}
angular quadrature. The three methods are compared when
p=2
. The coupled transport-VEF system is solved with fixed-point iteration.

8.5
Eigenvalue Problem

It was observed that the H1 discretization exhibited poor solution quality in under resolved problems and could not be scalably solved using block preconditioners.
Here, we investigate the presence of so-called “checkerboard” modes that are allowed by the H1 discretization. These modes are not physical, contaminate solution quality, and degrade the effectiveness of AMG.
To investigate this issue, we consider the following eigenvalue problem:

-\nabla^{2}u=\lambda u\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}\,,

(115a)

u=0\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\in\partial\mathcal{D}\,,

(115b)

with
\mathcal{D}=[0,1]^{2}
.
The exact solutions are

u=\sin(k_{x}\pi x)\sin(k_{y}\pi y)\,,\quad\lambda=\pi^{2}(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})\,.

(116)

The
Y_{1}\times W_{2}
discretization’s lumped Schur complement is used to discretize this problem as: find
u\in Y_{1}
such that

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\lambda\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\,,

(117)

where
\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}
is the
Y_{1}
mass matrix and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rm{S}}}
is the lumped Schur complement defined in Eq.
66
. The Locally Optimal Block Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (LOBPCG) solver from
hypre
was used to solve for the five smallest eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors on this problem. The H1 discretization correctly produced the first four smallest eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors but found the high-frequency, checkerboard mode shown in Fig.
16
for the fifth. This checkerboard mode corresponded to a non-physically degenerate eigenvalue of
8\pi^{2}
.
The presence of this mode indicates the
Y_{p}\times W_{p+1}
discretization allows non-physical, spurious modes that are slowly decaying and high frequency. Such modes are slow to remove with relaxation and also cannot be accurately represented on a coarser grid, meaning AMG will not be an effective preconditioner.
Furthermore, the presence of these oscillatory modes can degrade solution quality in underresolved problems leading to increased negativities in the VEF scalar flux.
We note that the lumped Schur complement associated with the RT discretization does not contain these non-physical modes and can thus be effectively preconditioned by AMG.

Figure 16
:
A depiction of an eigenmode corresponding to an eigenvalue of
8\pi^{2}
of the Poisson eigenvalue problem discretized with the
Y_{1}\times W_{2}
discretization’s lumped Schur complement. For this eigenvalue, the exact solution is
\sin(2\pi x)\sin(2\pi y)
meaning this mode is spurious. The presence of high-frequency spurious modes in the
Y_{p}\times W_{p+1}
discretization’s lumped Schur complement degrades the effectiveness of AMG and thus the performance of the block preconditioners used to solve the full
Y_{p}\times W_{p+1}
discretization.

8.6
Weak Scaling

Finally, we show that the RT and HRT methods weak scale in parallel on the first iteration of the linearized crooked pipe problem from §
8.4
. The inversion of the streaming and collision operator is approximated with one iteration of a parallel block Jacobi sweep where each processor performs a transport sweep on its processor-local domain using angular fluxes on inflow processor boundaries that are iteratively lagged. Due to this, the transport sweep is not exact when more than one processor is used.
Uniform refinements are used in tandem with increasing the processor count by four so that the number of unknowns per processor remains constant.
Since the sizes of the systems corresponding to the RT and HRT methods differ, the data are tabulated in terms of the number of scalar flux degrees of freedom.
We stress that the linear system for the RT method additionally includes the degrees of freedom associated with each component of the current and that the HRT system solves the reduced problem for the Lagrange multiplier unknowns only.
The results were generated on 29 nodes of the
rztopaz
machine at LLNL which has two 18-core Intel Xeon E5-2695 CPUs per node.
Timing data is presented as the minimum time achieved across three repeated runs.
We compare the efficiency and performance of the inner solvers for the RT and HRT discretizations with
p=2
when 1) the parallel block Jacobi transport sweep is used to compute the VEF data and 2) the VEF data are set to their asymptotic, diffusive values of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}=1/3\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
and
E_{b}=1/2
.
The BiCGStab tolerance was
10^{-8}
.

Table

compares the number of iterations to convergence and solve times for solving the RT VEF and RT diffusion linear systems. For the VEF system, a range of preconditioner options are presented. The preconditioners are parameterized by the use of one iteration of Jacobi (J) or Gauss-Seidel (GS) for approximating the inverse of the total interaction mass matrix and the number of AMG V-cycles applied to the lumped Schur complement at each preconditioner application. We consider the permutations of using Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel, one AMG V-cycle, and three AMG V-cycles. The RT diffusion system is preconditioned by one iteration of Jacobi on the total interaction mass matrix and one AMG V-cycle on the lumped Schur complement (i.e. the “J-1” preconditioner).
The J-1 preconditioner led to a scalable solver for the diffusion system with an increase of only 6 iterations when the problem size was increased by a factor of 1024.
However, the J-1 preconditioner did not perform as uniformly when applied to the VEF system showing an increase of 14 iterations over the same range of problem sizes. Increasing the number of AMG V-cycles per preconditioner application to three led to an algorithm that scaled more closely to the diffusion case.
Switching to Gauss-Seidel for approximating the inverse of the total interaction mass matrix did reduce the total number of iterations to convergence but did not alter the scaling of iterations with problem size seen with the J-1 preconditioner. The GS-3 option required the least iterations to converge and iteration counts scaled similarly to that of the J-1 preconditioner applied to the diffusion system.
These results suggest that scalability can be improved by performing more AMG V-cycles on the lumped Schur complement. However, using a more expensive approximation of the inverse of the total mass matrix was less effective.
In terms of solve time, the J-1 preconditioner was the fastest.
In other words, the increased robustness in iteration count to problem size provided by the more expensive preconditioners did not adequately balance their increased cost per iteration.
For the largest problem size, the J-1 preconditioner applied to the VEF system was only 1.23x more expensive than solving the diffusion system with the J-1 preconditioner.

9
Conclusions

We have developed high-order mixed finite element discretizations for the Variable Eddington Factor (VEF) equations that are compatible with curved meshes. The methods were designed to have element-local particle balance and immediate multiphysics compatibility with the mixed finite element techniques used for hydrodynamics calculations in
[
7
]
. Each method produces a scalar flux solution in the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) space to match the approximation space used for the thermodynamic variables in
[
7
]
. We considered three choices for the finite element space that approximates the current: a discrete subspace of
[H^{1}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}
where each component is represented with continuous finite elements, a method that uses the Raviart Thomas (RT) space along with DG-like numerical fluxes to treat the discontinuities arising from the presence of the Eddington tensor in the VEF first moment equation, and a hybrid RT method where continuity of the normal component of the current is enforced weakly with a Lagrange multiplier. These methods are referred to as H1, RT, and HRT, respectively. The VEF discretizations were paired with a high-order DG discretization of the S
N
transport equations to solve problems from linear transport.

On manufactured solutions problems, the H1, RT, and HRT methods all had optimal
\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1})
convergence for the VEF scalar flux on refinements of a curved mesh. However, optimal convergence for the VEF current was observed only on diffusive problems.
This result suggests that the VEF scalar flux and the zeroth angular moment of the discrete angular flux converge at equivalent rates.
However, on transport problems, the VEF current could not be solved to the same accuracy as the first angular moment of the discrete angular flux.
In addition, the mixed finite element superconvergence property for the VEF scalar flux was lost on the standard use case of equal degree interpolation for the VEF scalar and angular fluxes on a quadratically anisotropic transport problem. This suggests that post-processing techniques, such as the method proposed by
Stenberg 1991
, that leverage the mixed finite element superconvergence property to produce more accurate solutions would not be effective on transport problems where equal degree interpolation is used for the VEF scalar and angular fluxes.

All three methods showed rapid and robust convergence on a single-material thick diffusion limit test problem on both a simple orthogonal mesh and a severely distorted third-order mesh generated with a Lagrangian hydrodynamics code. The methods were tested on mesh and polynomial order refinements of a two-material linearized crooked pipe problem that had a 1000x difference in total cross section. Fixed-point convergence was robust for all three methods with RT and HRT converging equivalently. The H1 method converged slower, requiring
\approx
​ 1.75x more iterations than RT and HRT on the largest mesh for each polynomial order.
It was observed that the H1 VEF discretization produced scattering sources more likely to induce negativity in the transport sweep leading to an increased reliance on the negative flux fixup compared to the RT and HRT methods.

We also investigated preconditioned iterative solvers for the H1, RT, and HRT methods. Lower block triangular preconditioners were used for the H1 and RT methods that employ Jacobi smoothing on the total interaction mass matrix and Algebraic Multigrid (AMG) on the lumped Schur complement. The solvers for the HRT method leverage the element-by-element block structure generated by discontinuous approximations to form a reduced system for the Lagrange multiplier only, leading to fewer globally coupled unknowns than in the H1 or RT methods. AMG is applied directly to the reduced problem. The preconditioned iterative solvers were tested on a series of increasingly distorted meshes to test their robustness. The H1 and HRT methods converged for all distortions but the RT method failed to converge once the mesh became too distorted. The RT and HRT methods were shown to have scalable solvers in both
h
and
p
on the linearized crooked pipe problem. However, the solvers for H1 were not scalable. It was found that AMG was struggling to adequately precondition the lumped Schur complement due to the presence of highly oscillatory, slowly decaying modes. These modes are a consequence of the mismatch between the finite element spaces used to approximate the VEF scalar flux and current and were present even on a simple Poisson eigenvalue problem. Finally, a weak scaling study demonstrated that the RT and HRT methods can be scalably solved out to 1024 processors and over 40 million VEF scalar flux unknowns. Compared to solving the symmetric positive definite radiation diffusion system, solving the non-symmetric VEF equations was only 1.23x and 1.19x more expensive for the RT and HRT methods, respectively.

The primary takeaway from this work is that the combination of a DG S
N
discretization and the RT or HRT VEF discretizations form an effective high-order method for linear transport problems.
Both the RT and HRT discretizations of the VEF equations have high-order accuracy, compatibility with curved meshes, and robust and scalable convergence in both outer fixed-point iterations and inner preconditioned linear solver iterations.
The performance of the methods was differentiated only in the presence of severely distorted meshes. In such case, the preconditioned iterative solver for the HRT method was robust to mesh distortion whereas the solver for the RT method was not.
The H1 method is not recommended for use in a production code due to the lack of scalable iterative solvers. In addition, the H1 method had lower fixed-point iteration efficiency and higher reliance on the negative flux fixup on the linearized crooked pipe problem when compared to the RT and HRT methods.

In radiation-hydrodynamics calculations, the scalar flux and current are coupled to the hydrodynamics’ energy balance and momentum equations, respectively.
Due to the sub-optimal accuracy of the VEF current on transport problems, it is unclear whether the mixed finite element methods presented here would yield improvements in physics fidelity commensurate with the increased cost of solving for both the VEF scalar flux and current. Future work includes investigating the quality of the solution provided by the RT and HRT methods on tightly coupled multiphysics problems as compared to the DG VEF methods presented in
[
16
]
.

Appendix A
The Gradient of the Piola Transform

The goal of this section section is to derive a formula for the transformation of the gradient of a vector defined under the contravariant Piola transformation. For the contravariant Piola transform
\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}^{-1}
the inverse transform is:

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=J\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}\circ\boldsymbol{\rm{T}}\,.

(119)

Here, we seek to derive

\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\hat{\nabla}\!\left(J\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}\right)\,,

(120)

so that we can solve for
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}
. The goal is to derive the functional form of the transformation in terms of functionality commonly implemented in finite element codes. That is, we cast the computation in terms of the Jacobian matrix and Hessian of the transformation.

Through their connection to the Jacobian matrix and the inverse of the Jacobian matrix, the tangent and cotangent spaces are related by

\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{3}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{3}\times\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1}\,,

(121)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{3}
points out of the page. In other words,
\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}
is a 90 degree clockwise rotation of
\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}
is a 90 degree counterclockwise rotation of
\boldsymbol{\rm{t}}_{1}
(see Fig.
2
). Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\\
J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix}

(122)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\xi}&J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\eta}\\
J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\xi}&J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}\,.

The second term can be written as

\begin{bmatrix}J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\xi}&J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\eta}\\
J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\xi}&J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}=J\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\hat{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v}=J\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}\,,

(123)

where
\hat{\nabla}\boldsymbol{v}=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}
transforms the reference gradient to the physical gradient. The first term is a third-order tensor contracted with a vector to yield a second-order tensor. By expanding the dot products, we can emulate this contraction as a sum of two second-order tensors:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{n}}_{2})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{11})v_{1}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{12})v_{2}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{11})v_{1}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{12})v_{2}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{21})v_{1}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{22})v_{2}&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{21})v_{1}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{22})v_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(124)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{11})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{11})\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{21})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{21})\end{bmatrix}v_{1}+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{12})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{12})\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}(Jn_{22})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}(Jn_{22})\end{bmatrix}v_{2}

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{1}^{-1})v_{1}+\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{2}^{-1})v_{2}

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{-1}_{i}
are the columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{-1}
. Typically, finite element codes provide the Hessian matrix of the forward map but not the inverse map. Thus, to leverage existing functionality, we must write the above matrices in terms of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}=\hat{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}
instead of
\hat{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}
. Assume that the code computes the Hessian matrix in
flattened
and symmetric form as:

\langle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\xi^{2}}&\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\xi\partial\eta}&\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\eta^{2}}\\
\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\xi^{2}}&\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\xi\partial\eta}&\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\eta^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(125)

Then the above can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{1}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}F_{22}\\
-F_{21}\end{bmatrix}

(126)

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial y}{\partial\eta}\\
-\frac{\partial y}{\partial\xi}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\xi\partial\eta}&\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\eta^{2}}\\
-\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\xi^{2}}&-\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial\xi\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}H_{22}&H_{23}\\
-H_{21}&-H_{22}\end{bmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{2}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}-F_{12}\\
F_{11}\end{bmatrix}

(127)

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial\eta}\\
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\xi\partial\eta}&-\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\eta^{2}}\\
\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\xi^{2}}&\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\xi\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-H_{12}&-H_{13}\\
H_{11}&H_{12}\end{bmatrix}\,.

We can define the matrix

\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}=\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1})\boldsymbol{v}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{22}&H_{23}\\
-H_{21}&-H_{22}\end{bmatrix}v_{1}+\begin{bmatrix}-H_{12}&-H_{13}\\
H_{11}&H_{12}\end{bmatrix}v_{2}\,.

(128)

This is computed in flattened form as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{1}^{-1})\rangle&\langle\hat{\nabla}(J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}_{2}^{-1})\rangle\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}

(129)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}H_{22}&-H_{12}\\
H_{23}&-H_{13}\\
-H_{21}&H_{11}\\
-H_{22}&H_{12}\end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

where
\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
was used. Finally, we have that

\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}+J\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{-1}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}\iff\nabla\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}\!\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right)\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{-1}\,.

(130)

We can then say that

\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}\!\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right)\!\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{-1}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\,J\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

(131)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right):\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}^{-T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

Here, we use the fact that
\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}:\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}=\tr(\boldsymbol{\rm{A}}\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}^{T})
and apply the cyclic property of the trace to permute
\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}
and
\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-1}
.
In this form, we can implement the gradient calculation as a matrix-vector product of the flattened referential gradient and the coefficients of
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
.

When the mesh transformation is affine,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}=0
since the Hessian of an affine transformation is zero. In addition, the Piola identity states that
\tr\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}=0
. This can be most easily seen in Eq.
128
where

\tr\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}=(H_{22}-H_{22})v_{1}+(-H_{12}+H_{12})v_{2}=0\,.

(132)

Using the Piola identity and Eq.
131
, we have that

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}

(133)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right):\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}\boldsymbol{\rm{F}}^{-T}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\hat{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm{B}}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\hat{\nabla}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

Thus, in the thick diffusion limit when
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}\propto\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
,
\nabla\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}
simplifies to the standard transformation for the divergence of a contravariant vector.

Appendix B
Discrete Inf-Sup Condition

Here, we discuss the the inf-sup condition that governs the solvability of the
2\times 2
block systems arising in mixed finite element discretizations. Two excellent references for this topic are
Brezzi 2003
and
Benzi et al. 2005
. We present an analysis for Poisson’s equation since methods not effective for this simpler problem have no hope of being effective for the VEF equations.

B.1
Conditions for Solvability

Consider the linear system:

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}&-\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T}\\
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\\
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\end{bmatrix}\,,

(134)

which corresponds to the mixed finite element discretization of

\boldsymbol{q}+\nabla u=0\,,

(135a)

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{q}=f\,.

(135b)

Note that the above is Poisson’s equation,
-\nabla^{2}u=f
, in mixed form. The matrices are of the form:

\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\int\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muw\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\int w\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,.

(136)

We wish to demonstrate the conditions for when the block system in Eq.
134
is non-singular. To show the solution is unique, it must be verified that
f=0
implies that
u=0
and
\boldsymbol{q}=0
. When
f=0
, we have that

\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\iff\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})\,,

(137)

where
N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})
denotes the nullspace of
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}
such that

N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})=\{\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu:\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\}\,.

(138)

For some
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})
, the first equation reads

\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\,.

(139)

Since
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})
,
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T}=0
. Thus, we have that

\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\,,\quad\forall\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\in N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})\,.

(140)

Since
\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}
is a mass matrix, it is symmetric positive definite and thus,
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{T}\boldsymbol{\rm{M}}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\iff\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0
. In other words, we have shown that
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\Rightarrow\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0
. Setting
\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muq\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0
in the first row of Eq.
134
yields:

\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\,.

(141)

For the block system to be non-singular, we must have that

\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T}\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\iff\mkern 1.5mu\underline{\mkern-1.5muu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\,.

(142)

Equivalently, we require that the nullspace of
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T}
has only the trivial nullspace of zero (i.e.
N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T})=\{0\}
). The discrete inf-sup condition is precisely this condition on the matrix
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}
that
N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T})=\{0\}
.

B.2
Characterization for a Single Element

We now particularize the matrix
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}
for a single element,
K=[0,1]^{2}
, of the

and

We consider the

On the single element

\mathit{RT}_{0}=\spn\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\

0\end{pmatrix}\,,\begin{pmatrix}0\\

(143a)

W_{1}=\spn\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\

while the divergence of the

N(\nabla\cdot\mathit{RT}_{0})=\spn\{\begin{pmatrix}-x\\

(147a)

N(\nabla\cdot W_{1})=\spn\{\begin{pmatrix}-x\\

(147b)

Here, we can already see an issue forming: the nullspace for

D(u,\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{K}u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\,,\quad u\in Y\,,\ \boldsymbol{v}\in X\,,

(148)

where

By contrast, with

X=\mathit{RT}_{0}
,
\nabla\cdot\mathit{RT}_{0}=Y_{0}
meaning
M
is the
L^{2}(K)
inner product of functions in
Y_{0}
. In such case,
M
is symmetric and positive definite and thus has only the trivial nullspace. This means
N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}})=N(\nabla\cdot\mathit{RT}_{0})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}^{T})=\{0\}
. The
\mathit{RT}_{0}\times Y_{0}
discretization is then non-singular and
\boldsymbol{\rm{D}}
has only the physical nullspace associated with the divergence operator.

The takeaway is that for the pairing
W_{1}\times Y_{0}
,
W_{1}
is rich enough to ensure non-singularity but it is too rich with respect to
Y_{0}
such that spurious modes are allowed. The
W_{1}\times Y_{1}
discretization has the opposite problem: while
\nabla\cdot W_{1}
is small enough with respect to
Y_{1}
to avoid spurious modes it is too small to allow the block system to be non-singular. In general,
Y_{p}\subset\nabla\cdot W_{p}\subset Y_{p+1}
and thus one must always compromise between solvability and avoiding spurious modes. On the other hand,
\nabla\cdot\mathit{RT}_{p}=Y_{p}
so that the
\mathit{RT}_{p}\times Y_{p}
discretization is both solvable and does not allow spurious solutions.
