Abstract
. We prove that the disordered monomer-dimer model does not admit infinite volume incongruent ground states in ℤ d \mathds{Z}^{d} which can be obtained as a limit of finite volume ground states. Furthemore, we also prove that these ground states are stable under perturbation of the weights in a precise sense. As an application, we obtain a CLT for the ground state weight for a growing sequence of tori. Our motivation stems from a similar and long standing open question for the short range Edwards-Anderson spin glass model.

1.
Introduction

Understanding ground states for disordered statistical physics models has been one of the most tantalizing research topics in the past few decades. A notable example is the
Edwards Anderson spin glass model
, which is essentially the well-studied Ising model but with the couplings given by i.i.d. Gaussian weights. Despite fantastic progress of rigoruous mathematical understanding of the mean field case
[
22
,
21
,
23
,
20
]
, progress has been slow in the short range setting (i.e. on
\mathds{Z}^{d}
), and in fact has even been a source of considerable controversy in the physics literature (see
[
13
]
and references therein). The goal of this article is to address the question of uniqueness and central limit theorem for the ground state of the disordered
monomer-dimer model
in the short range setup. We also shed light on the question of
disorder chaos
for this particular model.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite, connected, simple graph and let
\Sigma=V\cup E
. A monomer-dimer covering of
G
is defined by a collection of non overlapping edges and vertices of
G
, which we denote by
M\subsetneq\Sigma
, otherwise known as a
matching
. By non overlapping, we mean that no two distinct edges in
M
share an endpoint and no vertex in
M
is an endpoint of an edge in
M
. The vertices in
M
are called
monomers
and the edges
dimers
.

Figure 1.
A monomer-dimer configuration on the
5\times 5
grid

We will also think of
M
as a percolation on
\Sigma
, which allows us to think of it as an element of
\{0,1\}^{\Sigma}
, where a 1 corresponds to an edge or a vertex being occupied and
0
corresponds to them being unoccupoied. Given a collection of weights
J:\Sigma\to\mathds{R}
on a finite graph the weight of a matching is given by

H(M):=\sum_{x\in M}J_{x}.

The ground state of the monomer-dimer model on
G
with weights
J
is any matching of minimal weight. On finite graphs, if the weights are independent random variables with continuous distribution, there is zero probability that any two distinct monomer dimer configurations will have the same weight, thus uniqueness of the ground state holds with probability
1
.

On infinite graphs, while the notion of weight of the matching does not make sense any more, following
[
1
,
19
]
, we can still extend notion of ground state using the energy difference of two configurations that differ at finitely many sites. Namely, we can define a matching
M\subset V\cup E
to be a ground state if we cannot ‘lower the energy’ by modifying it at finitely many edges and vertices, where the lowering of the energy is given precise meaning by considering energy differences (see
Section

3.1
). If the weights come from i.i.d. distribution then the cardinality of the set of ground states
{\sf G}(\boldsymbol{J})
is almost surely a constant via ergodicity. What can we say about this number, in particular, is it finite or infinite? We answer this question for the particular case of ground states which are obtained through an infinite volume limit of finite volume tori.

In this paper, we work on the integer lattice
\mathds{Z}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 2
with nearest neighbor edges. Let
\sf V
be the vertex set and
\sf E
be the edge set of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
, with
\sf\Sigma=\sf V\cup\sf E
. We let
\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
to be the torus
(\mathds{Z}/n\mathds{Z})^{d}
. For ease of proof technique, we let
{\boldsymbol{J}}=(J_{x})_{x\in\sf\Sigma}
be a collection of i.i.d. weights coming from a
good
distribution, see
Definition

3.1
. For now let us mention that this includes a large class of distributions, in particular the cases of
N(0,1)
(standard Gaussian) and Exp
(1)
(standard exponential).
Let
M_{n}
denote the ground state for the monomer-dimer configuration on for the weights restricted to the vertices and edges of
\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
. We think of
(\boldsymbol{J},M_{n})
as a joint probability distribution on
[\beta,\infty)^{\sf\Sigma}\times\{0,1\}^{\sf\Sigma}
where
M_{n}
is extended periodically to the infinite lattice. It is standard that the collection of probability measures induced by
\{(\boldsymbol{J},M_{n})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is tight and hence has subsequential limits. Our main theorem concerns the uniqueness of these limits.

Theorem 1.1
.

The weak limit of
\{(\boldsymbol{J},M_{n})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
exists as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, letting
(\boldsymbol{J},M)
be the limit,
M
is a measurable function of
\boldsymbol{J}
.

Even though each
M_{n}
is clearly a measurable function of
\boldsymbol{J}
, measurability might not hold for weak limits in general. For example, on
\mathds{Z}
, we can define a matching on
\mathds{T}_{2n}
as follows: the matching is a perfect matching (only dimers and no monomers) if the sum of the weights on
\mathds{T}_{2n}
is strictly positive, and otherwise, the matching is all monomers and no dimers. It is easy to see that in the limit, we get all monomers with probability
1/2
and a perfect matching with probability
1/2
, which is in fact completely independent of
{\boldsymbol{J}}
.

The broad strategy to prove
Theorem

1.1
is as in
[
19
]
: superimpose two potential ground states and arrive at a contradiction. To that end let
({\boldsymbol{J}},M)
and
({\boldsymbol{J}},M^{\prime})
be two distinct subsequential limits. Conditioned on
{\boldsymbol{J}}
, sample
M
and
M^{\prime}
independently according to their conditional laws (the spaces are Borel which allows us to consider the a.s. conditional laws). This defines a coupling
({\boldsymbol{J}},M,M^{\prime})
. We prove the following theorem

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
({\boldsymbol{J}},M,M^{\prime})
be as above. Then
M=M^{\prime}
almost surely.

It is easy to see that
Theorem

1.1
follows immediately from
Theorem

1.2
. Indeed, since
M
and
M^{\prime}
can be coupled to be equal, all subsequential limits must be the same. Furthermore, taking
M
and
M^{\prime}
to be limits along the same subsequence, we conclude that
M
is a measurable function of
{\boldsymbol{J}}
.

Our next result concerns the change in the ground state under a translation invariant perturbation. To that end, for any two elements
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\sf\Sigma}
, let
\xi\Delta\xi^{\prime}:=\{x\in\Sigma:\xi_{x}\neq\xi^{\prime}_{x}\}
denote their symmetric difference. Let
{\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}}
be an independent copy of
{\boldsymbol{J}}
. Fix
p\in[0,1]
and now define
{\boldsymbol{J}}(p)
as follows: for each edge toss an independent coin and change
J_{e}
to
J_{e}^{\prime}
with probability
p
. Note that
{\boldsymbol{J}}(p)
has the same law as
{\boldsymbol{J}}
. We get a new ground state
M(p)
using
Theorem

1.1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
p\in[0,1]
and let
M,M(p)
be as above. Then
M\Delta M(p)
has finite components almost surely. Furthermore,
M\Delta M(p)\to\emptyset
as
p\to 0
in law.

The result
M\Delta M(p)\to\emptyset
as
p\to 0
in law is analogous to the absence of disorder chaos (see
[
8
,
13
]
and references therein).

As an application of our results, we obtain a central limit theorem for the partition function.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
M_{n}
be the ground state for the monomer dimer model with
\boldsymbol{J}
now additionally satisfying the condition that
\E(|J_{x}|^{4+\delta})<\infty
for some
\delta>0
. Then as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{H(M_{n})-\E H(M_{n})}{\sqrt{\var{H(M_{n})}}}\to N(0,1)

in distribution.

As a by product of our proof, we also obtain a decay of correlation for the ground state, see
Corollary

6.6
for a precise statemtent.

Remark 1.5
.

For each of our results, it is possible to consider the weight distributions to be different for the vertices and edges. As we will touch upon later, it is also possible to choose the monomer weights to be a fixed constant. See
Section

6.4
for details.

The monomer dimer model in the disordered setting has been studied before in the context of a
Gibbs measure

[
3
,
2
,
14
,
17
]
. The mean field setting with two distinct types of environmental disorder, the first being studying the standard monomer dimer model on the Erdos-Renyi random graph, and the second corresponding to the vertex weights being i.i.d random variables are addressed in
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
respectively. In each case, the limiting free energy and limiting monomer densities are computed explicitly. The approaches used are inherently mean field, in particular using the cavity method to express the limiting free energy in terms of a fixed point relation. In
[
14
,
17
]
, the authors establish the finite temperature analogue of
Theorem

1.4
in the setting of pseudo one dimensional graphs and general bounded degree graphs respectively, and in
[
14
]
, the CLT for the ground state energy is briefly addressed. However, none of these works analyze the ground state in detail, which is far more rigid in structure.

Our proof is more inspired by the technique of Newman and Stein
[
19
]
where they analyze the superimposition of two ground states in dimension 2 and derive a contradiction via ergodic theoretic methods. Although their proof does not yield the uniqueness of the ground state for the Edwards Anderson model, which is a major open question in the area of spin glasses with far reaching consequences, the special structure of the monomer dimer model does allow us to obtain uniqueness in any dimension. In contrast, it is unclear whether the ground state for the Edwards Anderson model is in fact unique in any dimension (see
[
13
]
for a recent account), although it is strongly believed to be the case in dimension 2. Our proof also uses the concept of
metastates
put forth by Aizenmann and Wehr
[
1
]
, which is a crucial concept used to capture the chaotic behaviour of the ground states. From a broader perspective, the ground state of the disordered monomer-dimer model is the solution of a randomized combinatorial optimization problem, in the spirit of
[
11
,
10
,
4
,
15
]
.

1.1.
Outline of the proof

One convenient aspect of using tori to obtain limits is that
({\boldsymbol{J}},M,M^{\prime})
is invariant in law with respect to translations of the lattice, which enables us to use ergodic theoretic methods. Following Newman and Stein
[
19
]
we consider
M\Delta M^{\prime}
. It follows immediately from the fact that
M
and
M^{\prime}
are ground states that
M\Delta M^{\prime}
cannot have any finite component almost surely. Furthermore, any infinite component of
M\Delta M^{\prime}
can be either a (one-ended) infinite path of dimers started from a monomer, or a bi-infinite simple path of dimers.
We first use the celebrated technique of Burton and Keane
[
9
]
to rule out the existence of the one-ended paths (in fact this is a much more straightforward consequence of the amenability of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
, we do not need the full force of the Burton-Keane technique). This in turn rules out the presence of any monomer in
M\Delta M^{\prime}
.

It remains to rule out bi-infinite path. Here we crucially use the fact that we are in the monomer-dimer setup and define a quantity which we call the
optimality
of a vertex, denoted
O(v)
(see
Definition

2.4
). Optimality is a local function of the weights and has the property that
O(v)<0
implies that
v
has to be a monomer in both
M
and
M^{\prime}
. Consequently, none of the monomers in any bi-infinite path of
M\Delta M^{\prime}
can have negative optimality.

Another concept we borrow from Newman and Stein is the notion of an
inaccessible
edge, which is the analogue of a super satisfied edge from
[
19
]
. Namely an edge is inaccessible if its weight is so high compared to the two monomers adjacent to it that no ground state can occupy it.

The next notion we introduce the idea of
flexibility
of a vertex or an edge, which tells us the amount by which we need to change the weight of that vertex or edge in order to change the ground state (the analogue of this idea for Edwards Anderson model was introduced in
[
19
,
6
]
). One needs to be careful with this definition in infinite volume limits which is done by taking appropriate weak limits along subsequences borrowing from the notion of metastates going back to
[
1
]
. A crucial property of flexibility is that if we change the weight of some
x\in\sf\Sigma
by
\varepsilon
, the paths obtained by superimposing the new and the old ground states cannot pass through an edge of flexibility strictly bigger than
\varepsilon
.

Let us restrict to the case where the density is positive on all of
\mathds{R}
for simplicity, the other cases need a slight tweak which we don’t mention here.
The main argument is now as follows; we restrict to the event that a bi-infinite path
P
passes through the origin, and fix
\varepsilon>0
so small so that there are infinitely many edges of flexibilities at least
\varepsilon
along both directions of
P
. Find the two nearest ones to the origin and let
Q
be the portion of
P
between these edges of high flexibility. Next we let
m
be the infimum of the optimality of the vertices along
P
, which is non-negative as argued above. We further restrict to the event that the origin has optimality at most
{\sf m}+\delta
with
\delta<\varepsilon/2
and all the edges adjacent to vertices in
Q
except the origin are inaccessible. We show that this event has positive probability. Now we decrease the weight of the origin by
2\delta
to obtain a new collection of weights
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}
and new ground states
\tilde{M},\tilde{M}^{\prime}
. We prove that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
{\boldsymbol{J}}
and with some more work
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},\tilde{M},\tilde{M}^{\prime})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
({\boldsymbol{J}},M,M^{\prime})
as well.

The perturbation causes one of two things to happen to the ground state; either it changes, or it doesn’t. In the former case, due to topological constraints, a monomer has to appear in
\tilde{M}\Delta\tilde{M}^{\prime}
. This is a contradiction as
M\Delta M^{\prime}
cannot have a monomer with positive probability and the law of
\tilde{M}\Delta\tilde{M}^{\prime}
is absolutely continuous with that of
M\Delta M^{\prime}
. The crucial thing we need to rule out for this step is that lowering the weight of the origin does not wipe out the entire bi-infinite path through it.

In the latter case when there is no change to the ground state, we are on an event where the infimum of optimality over all the vertices in the bi-infinite path of
\tilde{M}\Delta\tilde{M}^{\prime}
passing through the origin is achieved at some vertex, an event which has probability 0 in
M\Delta M^{\prime}
because of translation invariance. The contradiction again follows from absolute continuity.

The proof of
Theorem

1.3
follows similar lines, except we are allowed to have finite components in this setting. The proof of
Theorem

1.4
uses the powerful Normal approximation technique put forth by Chatterjee
[
12
]
, and the crucial input of
Theorem

1.1
which yields a mild form of spatial decorrelation of the ground states.

2.
Perturbation of the Ground State on Finite Graphs

In this section, we will provide certain basic lemmas that primarily concern the change of the ground state when one or more of the weights are perturbed. The difference between two ground states is clearly encoded in the symmetric difference between them. Throughout this section, we assume that
G=(V,E)
is a finite, connected, simple graph, with
\Sigma=V\cup E
. Sometimes we shall write
w_{u,v}
to be the weight of
(u,v)
admitting an abuse of notation. We also denote by
\Omega_{G}
the set of all matchings of
G
. A collection of weights
\{w_{x}\}_{x\in\Sigma}
is said to be generic if for every finite set
S\subset\Sigma
and every collection of integers
\{k_{x}\}_{x\in S}
not all 0,

\sum_{x\in S}k_{x}w_{x}\neq 0.

Throughout this section, we assume that the weight collection
W=\{w_{x}\}_{x\in\Sigma}
are deterministic and
generic
.
Clearly, the weights being generic guarantees the uniqueness of the ground state, and any collection of i.i.d. continuous random variables are generic almost surely.
A
simple, finite path
is a sequence
(u_{1},(u_{1},u_{2}),\ldots,(u_{k-1},u_{k}),u_{k})
in
\Sigma
so that and
u_{i}
s are all distinct vertices. A
simple loop
is a finite sequence of edges
((u_{1},u_{2}),(u_{2},u_{3}),\ldots(u_{k-1},u_{1}))
so that
u_{i}
s are all distinct.

Given a finite, simple path
P:=(u_{1},(u_{1},u_{2}),\ldots,(u_{k-1},u_{k}),u_{k})
, we can
partition it into two subsets
P_{1}:=\{u_{1},(u_{2},u_{3}),(u_{4},u_{5}),\ldots,(u_{k-2},u_{k-1}),u_{k}\}
and
P_{2}:=\{(u_{1},u_{2}),(u_{3},u_{4}),\ldots,(u_{k-1},u_{k})\}
. Call the pair
P_{1},P_{2}
,
complementary matchings of
P
.
Similarly, given a simple finite loop
L:=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}=u_{1})
, we can decompose its edges into
L_{1}:=(u_{1},u_{2}),(u_{3},u_{4}),\ldots
and the remaining
L_{2}:=(u_{2},u_{3}),(u_{4},u_{5}),\ldots
, and call the pair
(L_{1},L_{2})
complementary matchings of
L
. Observe that if one of
L_{i}
( or
P_{i}
) is in a matching, we can remove every element of
L_{i}
(or
P_{i}
) from it, and add back its complementary pair, to get another valid matching.
Let
N(v)
denote the
neighborhood
of
v
which includes
v
and all the edges adjacent to
v
. We start with a few elementary lemmas.

Figure 2.
M_{1}
is depicted on the left in blue,
M_{2}
is depicted on the right in red. The symmetric difference
M_{1}\Delta M_{2}
is depicted in the middle.

Lemma 2.1
.

The symmetric difference of any two matchings
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
is a disjoint collection of simple, finite paths and simple loops.

Furthermore, in each component, the set of edges and vertices in
M_{1}
and those in
M_{2}
form a complementary pair.

Proof.

This is elementary as for any vertex
v
, if
N(v)\cap(M_{1}\Delta M_{2})\neq\emptyset
, then exactly one of the following two cases occur. Either
v
is occupied by one matching and exactly one edge adjacent to
v
is occupied by the other; or
v
is occupied by neither matchings and exactly one edge adjacent to
v
is occupied by
M_{1}
and another different edge is occupied by
M_{2}
. Thus the set of edges of
M_{1}\Delta M_{2}
is a subgraph with degree either
0,1
or
2
. Furthermore if a vertex
v
has degree 0, both matchings occupy
v
, if it has degree 1, exactly one of them occupy
v
, and if it has degree 2, none of them occupy
v
. It is easy to see that these constraints make every component of
M_{1}\Delta M_{2}
either a simple, finite path or a simple loop.

The second assertion is a straightforward from the definition of a complementary pair.

\blacksquare

In light of
Lemma

2.1
, we call each element of the disjoint collection of finite, simple paths and loops a
component
of the symmetric difference between two matchings.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be the ground state and assume
v\in M
. Suppose we change the weight at
v
to
\tilde{w}_{v}
, so that it remains generic and the new ground state is
\tilde{M}
. Then the symmetric difference
M\Delta\tilde{M}
is either empty or is a simple, finite path beginning at the vertex
v
.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

2.1
, another way to state the lemma is to claim that there is no component of
M\Delta\tilde{M}
which do not contain
v
, which is what we shall prove. Take one such component
C
. Since the weight of all the edges and vertices in
C
is the same for
M
and
\tilde{M}
, switching between the complementary matchings of
C
either decreases the weight of
M
or
M^{\prime}
, contradicting the minimality of their weights.

\blacksquare

Prior to completely delving into the issue of perturbations that cause the ground state configuration to change, we state the following notion of
gauge invariance
for the monomer-dimer model.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
M_{G}
denote the ground state associated to a system of weights
\{w_{x}\}_{x\in\Sigma}
. Let
u\in V
be a vertex, and let
E_{u}
denote the collection of edges incident to
u
. For a fixed
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
, consider the new system of weights given by

w^{u,\lambda}_{x}:=\begin{cases}w_{x}+\lambda\text{ if $x\in\{u\}\cup E_{u}$ }\\
w_{x}\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

The ground state
M_{G}
remains the same for the new collection of weights.

Proof.

Note that every matching
M\in\Omega_{G}
must either contain
u
itself or exactly one edge contained in
E_{u}
. Thus,
\forall M\in\Omega_{G}
,
H^{u,\lambda}(M)=H(M)+\lambda
, where
H^{u,\lambda}
is the energy functional defined with respect to the new weights. Thus, although the energies have changed, the energy of every matching has been shifted by
\lambda
, and the energy differences remain the same.
M_{G}
remains to be the ground state.

\blacksquare

The question of stability of certain vertices and edges with regards to modification of the weights is natural and central to our analysis, and we will explore this now. We write
u\sim v
if
u
is adjacent to
v
.

Definition 2.4
.

The
optimality
of
v
, denoted
O(v)
is defined as

(2.1)

O(v):=\max_{u:u\sim v}\left(w_{u}+w_{v}-w_{u,v}\right).

An edge
e=(u,v)
is called
inaccessible
if

(2.2)

\displaystyle w_{e}>w_{u}+w_{v}.

Note that both the notions in
Definition

2.4
are completely local which will be useful later in the infinite volume setup. The next lemma justifies the nomenclature.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
v\in V,e\in E
. If
e=(u,v)
is inaccessible, then
e
is not in the ground state matching.
If
O(v)<0
, then
v
must be in the ground state matching.

Proof.

If
e=(u,v)
is inaccessible and is in a matching, then we can lower the energy by removing it and adding back two monomers at
u
and
v
. Thus
e
cannot be in a ground state. If
O(v)<0
then all the edges adjacent to
v
are inaccessible, and we apply the previous result.

\blacksquare

2.1.
Excited States

For any
S\subset\Sigma
we define the external boundary of
S
, denoted
\partial_{\textrm{ext}}S
as the collection

\partial_{\textrm{ext}}S:=\{x:x\in S^{c},x\sim y\in S\}.

We will define for any set
S
,

\overline{S}:=S\cup\partial_{\textrm{ext}}S.

Next, we consider configurations
\xi:S\to\{0,1\}
, which are
valid
in the sense that they are extendable to yield a matching. That is,
\xi
is said to be valid if
\exists\zeta\in\Omega_{G}
such that
\zeta(x)=\xi(x)
for all
x\in S
. (For example, we are not allowed to have a vertex
v
and all its neighborhood to be in
S
and empty.) Let
\mathcal{V}(S)
be the set of all valid configurations in
\{0,1\}^{S}
. We define
M_{G,S,\xi}
to be the ground state with the constraint
M_{G,S,\xi}(x)=\xi(x)
for all
x\in S
. More precisely,

(2.3)

\displaystyle M_{G,S,\xi}:=\argmin\{H(\zeta):\zeta\in\Omega_{G},\text{ }\zeta(x)=\xi(x)\text{ }\forall x\in S\}.

Note that since the weights are generic, the constrained ground state is still unique. We also define

\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})=H(M_{G,S,\xi^{\prime}})-H(M_{G,S,\xi})

to be the energy difference between two ground states with constraints
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
on
S
. For a given
S
, the energy difference enables us to examine how the ground state changes as
\{w_{x}\}_{x\in S}
are varied, with all other weights remaining fixed . Consider the following two simple observations:

•

For local configurations
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
and
\xi^{\prime\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{S}
,

(2.4)

\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})+\Delta H(G,S,\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime})=\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime\prime}).

•

M_{G,S,\xi}
does not depend on the weights
(w(x))_{x\in S}
.

Using these elementary properties, we can partition
\mathds{R}^{S}
into sets where
M_{G}=M_{G,S,\xi}
as follows. Define

\mathcal{I}(G,S,\xi)=\{w_{S}:\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})>0\text{ for all }\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}(S),\xi^{\prime}\neq\xi\}.

Let
B(n,S)=\{w_{S}\in\mathds{R}^{S}:\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})=0\text{ for some }\xi\neq\xi^{\prime},\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}(S)\}
.
The following lemma is clear now since the weights are chosen to be generic.

Lemma 2.6
.

\{\mathcal{I}(n,S,\xi)\}_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}\cup B(n,S)
is a partition of
\mathds{R}^{S}
and furthermore
w_{S}\not\in B(n,S)
.

The following equality is clear from the definitions:

(2.5)

M_{G}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}M_{G,S,\xi}1_{w_{S}\in\mathcal{I}(G,S,\xi)}.

The following elementary corollary will be useful later when we discuss absolute continuity of limiting measures on matchings.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\{w_{x}\}_{x\in\Sigma}
be a collection of generic weights and
S\subset\Sigma
be a finite subset. Let
f:\mathds{R}^{S}\to\mathds{R}^{S}
be a measurable function such that the new weights defined by
\tilde{w}_{x}=f(w_{x})
for all
x\in S
, all other weights unchanged, remain generic. Then the ground state with respect to the new weights is given by
M_{G,S,\xi}
where
\xi
is such that

f(w_{S})\in\mathcal{I}(G,S,\xi).

Another extremely convenient feature, when it comes to taking the infinite volume limits is the tightness of
\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})
in the size of
G
(which will be applied later to a growing sequence of tori). To that end, we first need the following combinatorial lemma, which essentially says that two different valid configurations in
S
can be extended to be the same outside a small neighborhood of
S
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}(S)
which are extendable to
G
. Given any extension
m_{\xi}
of
\xi
, there exists an extension
m_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}
of
\xi^{\prime}
such that
m_{\xi}\Delta m_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}\subset\partial_{\textrm{ext}}\bar{S}
.

Proof.

Define
m_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}
as follows. We begin with
m_{\xi}
and replace the configuration for all
x\in S
with
\xi^{\prime}
. If at the end of this replacement we are left with a valid matching, we are done. If not, firstly, consider all vertices
v\in V\cap S
such that
\xi^{\prime}(v)=0
and
\xi^{\prime}(e)=0
for all
e\in E\cap S
where
v
is an end point of
e
. For every such
v\in S
, we add an edge
(v,w)
for some
w\notin S
, and
w
uncovered by any dimer adjacent to
S
. Such a
w
will always exist, because otherwise
\xi^{\prime}
would not be extendable. The introduction of these edges, as well as the prior replacement can result in adjacent dimer pairs or an adjacent monomer dimer pair. Such defects may at most extend to
\partial_{\textrm{ext}}\overline{S}
, and we deal with them now. For every covered edge
e
connecting a vertex in
\partial_{\textrm{ext}}S
and
\partial_{\textrm{ext}}\overline{S}
, we perform a regularizing procedure. If both endpoints of
e
are covered by other monomers/dimers, we remove
e
from the configuration. If only one endpoint is otherwise covered, we remove
e
and place a monomer at the now uncovered remaining vertex. Next if
v\in\partial_{\textrm{ext}}{S}\cup\partial_{\textrm{ext}}\overline{S}
, is a vertex that is covered by a monomer as well as an adjacent dimer, we remove the monomer from the configuration.

\blacksquare

Let
M_{G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime}}
be a matching which is defined as in
Lemma

2.8
for
m_{\xi}=M_{G,\xi}
. The following trivial bound now holds:

(2.6)

|\Delta H(G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})|\leqslant\max\{|H(M_{G,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime}})-H(M_{G,S,\xi})|,|H(M_{G,S,\xi^{\prime},\xi})-H(M_{G,S,\xi^{\prime}})|\}\leqslant\sum_{x\in\overline{\overline{S}}}|w_{e}|.

Let us now focus on the case where
S
is a single
x\in\Sigma
. In this case, the partition in
Lemma

2.6
is simply two intervals of the form
(-\infty,K_{G,x})\cup(K_{G,x},\infty)\cup\{K_{G,x}\}
where
K_{G,x}
is called the

at

. Using this notation, (
2.5
) takes the following simple form:

(2.7)

M_{G}=M_{G,x,0}1_{w_{x}>K_{G,x}}+M_{G,x,1}1_{w_{x}\leqslant K_{G,x}}

Definition 2.9
.

For any
x\in\Sigma
, we define
flexibility
of

to be
F_{G}(x):=|K_{G,x}-w_{x}|=|\Delta H(G,x,0,1)|
.

Both nomenclature of the transition point and the flexibility follows the usage of Newman and Stein
[
19
]
. In fact, it is not too hard to see that we can write down the following formula for

:

(2.8)

K_{G,x}=H(M_{G,x,0})-H(M_{G,x,1})+w_{x}.

We note here several elementary properties of
F_{G}(x)
, whose proof is straightforward application of the definition, so we skip it.

Lemma 2.10
.

Fix
x\in\Sigma
.

i.

K_{G,x}
if a function of
\{w_{y}:y\in\Sigma\setminus\{x\}\}
.

ii.

x
is in the ground state if and only if
w_{x}\leqslant K_{G,x}
.

Now let us argue that perturbing the weight of an edge or a vertex by a quantity smaller than the flexibility of
x\in\Sigma
will not change the status of
x
. This lemma justifies the use of the nomenclature ‘flexibility’.

Lemma 2.11
.

Suppose
M
is the ground state and
x\in\Sigma
. Suppose the weight of some
y\in\Sigma
is either increased or decreased by
\varepsilon<F(x)
, to get a possibly new ground state
\tilde{M}
. Then
x\notin M\Delta\tilde{M}
.

Remark 2.12
.

Note the lemma when applied to
x=y
simply yields
M\Delta\tilde{M}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We observe from (
2.8
) that changing the weight of a vertex or an edge by
\varepsilon
changes
K_{G,x}
by at most
\varepsilon
. Indeed, if
y=x
,
K_{G,x}
is unchanged. On the other hand, if
y\neq x
then if
y
is present in both
M_{G,x,1}
or
M_{G,x,0}
, or if it is present in neither,
K_{G,x}
does not change. However, if it is present in one of
M_{G,x,1}
or
M_{G,x,0}
,
K_{G,x}
changes by at most
\varepsilon
and
w_{x}
does not change. Now we can use
Lemma

2.10
, item
ii.
and conclude.

\blacksquare

The next lemma is fairly intuitive, we include it for the sake of exposition. It concerns the robustness of the flexibility with regards to the raising of the weights of edges (or vertices) not selected by the ground state.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
M
be the ground state, let
S\subset\Sigma
such that no element of
S
is in
M
. Consider new weights
w^{\prime}_{y}\geqslant w_{y}
for all
y\in S
. Then the ground state is unchanged and furthermore for any
x\in\Sigma
,
F_{G}(x)
for the new weights cannot be strictly smaller than that for the old weights.

Proof.

If
x\in M
, then
M=M_{G,x,1}
and by
Lemma

2.10
, item
ii.

F_{G}(x)=K_{G,x}-w_{x}
. Using the formula (
2.8
) of
K_{G,x}
, clearly
K_{G,x}
cannot decrease by the given change of weights and hence
w_{x}
remains smaller than
K_{G,x}
for the new weights. Thus the ground state remain unchanged.

Note that (
2.8
) also entails that

F_{G}(x)=H(M_{G,x,0})-H(M_{G,x,1})
if
x\in M
and
F_{G}(x)=H(M_{G,x,1})-H(M_{G,x,0})
otherwise.
It is easy to see from this formula that
F_{G}(x)
cannot decrease.

\blacksquare

We conclude this section with a lemma which perhaps seems contrived right now, but will be a crucial ingredient of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
S:=(u_{1},(u_{1},u_{2}),\ldots,(u_{k-1},u_{k}),u_{k})
be a simple, finite path and let
1<j<k
be an integer such that the following conditions hold

•

The edges of
S
are alternatingly occupied by dimers corresponding to the ground state
M
.

•

(u_{j},u_{j+1})
is occupied by a dimer.

•

F_{G}((u_{k-1},u_{k}))\geqslant\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon
.

Let
E_{S}
be the set of edges adjacent to
(u_{2},u_{3},\ldots,u_{j-1},u_{j+1},\ldots,u_{k-1})
which are not in
S
. Let
\{\tilde{w}_{x}\}_{x\in\Sigma}
denotes a new set of generic weights defined as follows:

•

\tilde{w}_{e}=w_{e}+z_{P}
for all
e\in E_{S}
,
z_{S}>0
chosen to make every edge in
E_{S}
inaccessible

•

\tilde{w}_{(u_{1},v)}={w}_{(u_{1},v)}+\varepsilon/2\text{ }\forall v\sim u_{1}

•

All other weights are unchanged.

Let
\tilde{M}
be the ground state with respect to the new weights. Then
\tilde{O}(u_{1})=O(u_{1})-\varepsilon/2
, and the following dichotomy holds for
M\Delta\tilde{M}
. Either

•

M\Delta\tilde{M}=\emptyset
, or,

•

M\Delta\tilde{M}=(u_{j},(u_{j},u_{j+1}),\ldots,(u_{p-1},u_{p}),u_{p})
for some
p\leqslant k-1
.

Proof.

The order of changing the weights in this context does not matter as the ground state is unique. We first apply a gauge transformation at
u_{1}
, and add
\varepsilon/2
to the weights at
{u_{1}}
as well as all adjacent edges. This of course preserves the ground state by Lemma
2.3
. We next change the weights for all the edges in
E_{S}
. We choose

(2.9)

\displaystyle z_{S}:=\max_{(u_{i},v)\in E_{S}}|w^{u_{1},\varepsilon/2}_{(u_{i},v)}-w^{u_{1},\varepsilon/2}_{u_{i}}-w^{u_{1},\varepsilon/2}_{v}|+V,

where
V
is a uniform
[0,1]
random variable, and define
\tilde{w}_{e}
for this choice of
z_{S}
.
V
is included so that the system of weights
\{\tilde{w}_{x}\}
remains generic. It is a consequence of Lemma
2.13
that the ground state remains unchanged, and it is immediately clear that all
e\in E_{S}
are inaccessible as per (
2.2
). Next, we decrease the weight of
u_{1}
by
\varepsilon/2
. The fact that
\tilde{O}(u_{1})=O(u_{1})-\varepsilon/2
is trivial and follows directly from the definition of optimality (
2.1
). Since we are changing the weight of the vertex
u_{1}
, we know that if
M\Delta\tilde{M}\neq\emptyset
,
u_{1}\in M\Delta\tilde{M}
, and furthermore
M\Delta\tilde{M}
must be a simple, finite path beginning at
u_{1}
by
Lemma

2.2
. We denote this path by
Q
. Since
(u_{1},u_{2})\in M
, we must have that
u_{1}\in\tilde{M}
and thus the path
Q
begins at
u_{1}
and contains the edge
(u_{1},u_{2})
. Now, none of the other edges that are adjacent to
u_{2}
can be in
M\Delta M^{\prime}
, since the adjacent weights are unaffected and they remain inaccessible. Therefore, the path
Q
must proceed along the path
S
. Finally, by Lemma
2.11
,
(u_{k-1},u_{k})\notin Q
since
F_{G}((u_{k-1},u_{k}))\geqslant\varepsilon
. Thus,
Q
must terminate before reaching
u_{k-1}
. This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

3.
Ground State on Infinite Graphs and Modifications

3.1.
Defining the ground state

In this section go back to the Euclidean lattice
\mathds{Z}^{d}
and their approximations by tori
\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
. We will use the notations from
Section

2
, with
G=\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
. For notational simplicity we will replace
\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
by simply
n
. We will prove
Theorem

1.1
for weights with
good
distribution, and will remark later how this can be generalized.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
J
be a random variable where
\{x:p(x)>0\}\supseteq(\beta,\infty)
where
\beta\in\{-\infty\}\cup\mathds{R}
. Let
g_{z}
denote the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the distribution
X+z
with respect to that of
X
for some fixed
z>0
, that is
g_{z}(x)=p(x-z)/p(x)
. We say that the distribution of
J
is good if
p(\cdot)
is continuous and there is some
\alpha>1
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle C(z,\alpha)=\int_{\beta}^{\infty}\bigl(g_{z}(x)\bigr)^{\alpha}p(x)dx

is finite and continuous in
z
.

To see that the goodness condition is rather broad, observe that it applies to Gaussian, Exponential and several families of power-law distributions, covering the spectrum in terms of tail. We do not know if
Theorem

1.1
holds for any nonatomic weight distribution, and we do not pursue such avenues in this article.

A collection of i.i.d. random variables coming from a good distribution is of course, almost surely generic. Given a matching
M
of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
, its energy is now undefined. Nevertheless given two matchings
M
and
M^{\prime}
such that
M\Delta M^{\prime}
is finite, we can define the energy difference between
M
and
M^{\prime}
as
H(M)-H(M^{\prime}):=\sum_{x\in M\setminus M^{\prime}}J_{x}-\sum_{y\in M^{\prime}\setminus M}J_{y}
. We say
M
is a
ground state of
\boldsymbol{J}
if there is no other matching
M^{\prime}
such that
M\Delta M^{\prime}
is finite and
H(M)-H(M^{\prime})>0
.

One way to construct ground states is to consider the a.s. unique ground state
M_{n}
of
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{n}:=(J_{x})_{x\in\Sigma_{n}}
. Let
\tilde{M}_{n}
be the periodic extension of
M_{n}
to all of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
. Then the collection
\{({\boldsymbol{J}}_{n},\tilde{M}_{n})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
form a tight collection of random variables with range
\mathds{R}^{\Sigma}\times\Omega_{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
. For any subsequential weak limit
(\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime},M)
, the following properties hold:

•

\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}
has the same law as
\boldsymbol{J}
,

•

M
is a ground state matching with respect to
\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime}
almost surely,

•

The law of
(\boldsymbol{J}^{\prime},M)
is invariant with respect to translations of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
.

The final item above holds simply because we took limits along tori. See
Section

6.4
about other possibilities.

Recall the definition of the excited states from
Section

2.1
and the notation
(M_{n,S,\xi})
from
Equation

2.5
. Let
\boldsymbol{M}_{n}
denote the vector

(M_{n,S,\xi})_{S\subset\Sigma,|S|<\infty,\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}.

Of course, this includes
M_{n}
as a marginal, corresponding to
S=\emptyset
. Let
\Delta H(n,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})
be as in (
2.6
) (but with
G=\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
). Let
\Delta\boldsymbol{H}
be the vector

\Delta\boldsymbol{H}:=(\Delta H(n,S,\xi,\xi^{\prime}))_{S\subset\Sigma,|S|<\infty,\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}(S)}.

Here
\mathcal{V}(S)
is the set of valid configurations on
S
in
\mathds{Z}^{d}
. We point out here that a configuration is valid in
\mathds{T}_{n}^{d}
for a large enough
n
if and only if it is valid in
\mathds{Z}^{d}
, see
Lemma

2.8
. Since we are only interested in the limit, this slight abuse of notation is unambiguous.

Tightness of
\Delta\boldsymbol{H}
is guaranteed by (
2.6
), and hence we can take a further subsequential limit such that the vector
(\boldsymbol{J}_{n},\boldsymbol{M}_{n},\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n})
weakly converges to
(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{M},\Delta\boldsymbol{H})
. We emphasize that the limit at this point depends on the subsequence chosen, although it is not reflected in the notation. We will use different notations for different subsequential limit in later sections.

For later use, we list the following observations:

•

The vector
(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{M})
includes the limit
M
as a marginal by taking
S=\emptyset
.

•

The flexibility vector
(F_{n}(x))_{x\in\sf\Sigma_{n}}
is a continuous function of
(\boldsymbol{J}_{n},\boldsymbol{M}_{n},\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n})
and hence converges in law, call the limit
(F(x))_{x\in\sf\Sigma}
.

•

For every
S,\xi
as above,
M_{S,\xi}
is independent of
J_{S}
since this is true in finite volume. Using this fact and the continuity of the distribution of
J_{x}
, we conclude that for every
x\in\Sigma
,

(3.2)

\mathds{P}(F(x)>0)=1.

•

By an application of the Skorokhod representation theorem and Fatou’s lemma, the infinite volume version of
Equation

2.4
is also true almost surely.
Now observe that since the weights come from a continuous distribution,

\{{\boldsymbol{J}}:\Delta H(S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})\neq 0\text{ for all $S\subset\Sigma$},|S|<\infty,\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}(S),\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\}

is a set of probability
1
. This allows us to define the following set:

(3.3)

\mathcal{I}(S,\xi)=\{J_{S}:\Delta H(S,\xi,\xi^{\prime})<0\text{ for all }\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}(S)\}.

which partitions a set of probability 1. Consequently,
(1_{J_{S}\in\mathcal{I}(S,\xi)})_{S\subset\Sigma,|S|<\infty,\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}
is an almost surely
continuous function of
(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{M},\Delta\boldsymbol{H})
. Thus, (
2.5
) also translates in the infinite volume to the following equality which holds almost surely:

(3.4)

M=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}M_{S,\xi}1_{J_{S}\in I(S,\xi)}

It is a standard measure theoretic fact that the conditional law of
{\boldsymbol{M}}
given an a.s. sample of
{\boldsymbol{J}}
exists, call it
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{J}}}
(the regular conditional probability exists for random variables on Borel spaces, see
[
16
]
). It is also standard that we can write
{\boldsymbol{M}}=\Phi({\boldsymbol{J}},U)
where
\Phi
is a measurable function and
U
is a Uniform random variable independent
\boldsymbol{J}
(since we are working on Borel spaces). Since
M_{S,\xi}
is independent of
J_{S}
, we can actually write
M_{S}=\phi_{S,\xi}(J_{S^{c}},U)
. Applying (
3.4
), we conclude that

(3.5)

\displaystyle M=\Phi({\boldsymbol{J}},U)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}\phi_{S,\xi}(J_{S^{c}},U)1_{J_{S}\in\mathcal{I}(S,\xi)}.

For a given finite set
S
, by the joint convergence of
(\boldsymbol{J}_{n},\boldsymbol{M}_{n})
, Corollary
2.7
extends in a straightforward manner to the infinite lattice setting.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose
S\subset\sf\Sigma_{n}
is finite.
Let

be a continuous function, and consider a new system of weights
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{n}
such that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{n,S}=f(\boldsymbol{J}_{n,S})
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{n,\Sigma_{n}\setminus S}=\boldsymbol{J}_{n,\Sigma_{n}\setminus S}
. Similarly define

and

. Let

. Define

where

\tilde{M}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}M_{S,\xi}1_{\tilde{J}_{S}\in\mathcal{I}(S,\xi)}.

Then

(\boldsymbol{J}_{n},\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{n},\tilde{M}_{n},\boldsymbol{M}_{n},\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{n})\xrightarrow{}(\boldsymbol{J},\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},\tilde{M},\boldsymbol{M},\Delta\boldsymbol{H})

in law where the limit can be taken along any subsequence along which

converges. Consequently, we can write

\tilde{M}=\Phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},U)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}\phi_{S,\xi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{S^{c}},U)1_{\tilde{J}_{S}\in\mathcal{I}(S,\xi)}.

for some measurable functions

,

and

[0,1]
.

The point of the above corollary is that we can write
\tilde{M}
as the

function of
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},U)
as that in (

) which will allow us to conclude absolute continuity of

with respect to

from absolute continuity of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{J}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}_{n}
is a continuous function of
\boldsymbol{J}_{n}
. Furthermore, by

,

satisfies

\tilde{M_{n}}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{V}(S)}M_{n,S,\xi}1_{\tilde{J}_{n,S}\in\mathcal{I}(n,S,\xi)}.

This ensures that
\tilde{M}_{n}
is an a.s. continuous function of
(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{M},\Delta\boldsymbol{H})
.
Combining these two facts, the corollary is immediate.

\blacksquare

.

Let

It remains to be shown that these sequences of conditional probabilities do in fact converge to

An

(\dots,(v_{-2},v_{-1}),(v_{-1},v_{0}),(v_{0},v_{1}),(v_{2},v_{3}),\dots)
such that

s are all distinct. An analogue of

in the infinite setup is that the symmetric difference of two matchings in

is a disjoint union of simple loops, finite simple paths, simple infinite and bi-infinite paths.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be two ground states in
\mathds{Z}^{d}
for a collection of almost surely generic weights
{\boldsymbol{J}}
. Then

is a disjoint union of simple infinite and bi-infinite paths.

Proof.

There cannot be a finite, simple path or a simple loop in

as we can switch between the complimentary matchings of one such component and lose energy, which is impossible as both

are ground states.

\blacksquare

Definition 4.2

A vertex

If

Lemma 4.3
.

\mathds{P}(\text{There is a starting point of $M\Delta M^{\prime}$})=0
.

Proof.

The proof follows from a version of the Burton-Keane argument. We do not know that the law of
\Xi
is ergodic with respect to translations of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
, nevertheless we can restrict to its ergodic components. Let
\tilde{\Xi}=(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},\tilde{M},\tilde{M}^{\prime})

Let

in

.
Observe that by ergodic theorem, almost surely,

belong to two different such components. Thus

for all large enough

\blacksquare

4.1.
Nonexistence of bi-infinite paths

We know from

4.3
that

consists of bi-infinite paths only.
The strategy now as described in
Section

1.1
is to do an absolutely continuous modification to the weights and arrive at a contradiction.

To achieve this, the strategy is to perform a modification of a sensitive vertex weight on the path that changes each ground state in a controlled manner. By prior edge weight modifications, we will force the changes to occur along the bi-infinite path. We will do this by first finding two edges of high flexibility along the path such that the high sensitivity vertex is sandwiched between them, and then by making any edge neighbouring the path which is not occupied inaccessible. Of course, such a region with a sensitive vertex sandwiched by high flexibility edges has to be shown to exist with positive probability.

Lemma 4.5

\mathds{P}(M\Delta M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P},\exists\text{ unique }v\in P,O(v)={\sf m})=0

6.
Central Limit Theorem

In this section, we will be working under the additional assumption that
\E|J_{x}|^{4+\delta}<\infty
for some
\delta>0
. The Central Limit Theorem for the ground state energy is obtained via an application of Chatterjee’s powerful method for normal approximation
[
12
]
. While the ground state
M_{n}
can be sensitive to perturbation of the weights, the ground state energy
H(M_{n})
is quite robust in the following sense. Let
x\in\Sigma
. If we modify the weight of
x
, that is we consider the ground state with the new weight
J_{x}^{\prime}=J_{x}+\varepsilon
, then we have that
|H(M^{\prime}_{n})-H(M_{n})|<\varepsilon
. In particular, the ground state energy is a
1-
Lipschitz function of the weights
\boldsymbol{J}
. The uniqueness of the ground state enables an adaptation of Lam and Sen’s application of Chatterjee’s method for the central limit theorem of the free energy in the zero temperature setting considered here. The following definitions for independent perturbations of our weights
\boldsymbol{J}
will be useful.

(a)

\boldsymbol{J}^{S}
is the random vector obtained when all the weights corresponding to
S\subset\Sigma
, that is
\{J_{x}\}_{x\in S}
are replaced by independent copies
\{J_{x}^{\prime}\}_{x\in S}
.

\boldsymbol{J}^{x}
is the same random vector defined above for singletons
S=\{x\}
.

(b)

For a measurable function
g:\mathds{R}^{\Sigma_{n}}\to\mathds{R}
,
\partial_{x}g:=g(\boldsymbol{J})-g(\boldsymbol{J}^{x})
.

(c)

For
S\subset V_{n}\sqcup E_{n}
not containing
x
,
\partial_{x}g^{S}(\boldsymbol{J}):=g(\boldsymbol{J}^{S})-g(\boldsymbol{J}^{S\cup\{x\}})
.

Chatterjee’s result, as stated and used in
[
17
]
, bounds the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance
d_{KS}(\cdot,\cdot)
between a scaled and centered version of
g
and a standard Gaussian random variable
Z
as follows:

(6.1)

\displaystyle d_{KS}\left(\frac{g(\boldsymbol{J})-\E g(\boldsymbol{J})}{\sigma_{g}},Z\right)\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sigma_{g}}\left(\sum_{x,y}c(x,y)\right)^{1/4}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{g}^{3/2}}\left(\sum_{x}\E|\partial_{x}g|^{3}\right)^{1/2},

where

(6.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{g}^{2}:=\var(g)\text{ and }c(x,y):=\max_{S,T}\cov(\partial_{x}g\cdot\partial_{x}g^{S},\partial_{y}g\cdot\partial_{y}g^{T}),

for all
S
not containing
x
and all
T
not containing
y
. The function
g
for our purposes is
H(M_{n})
where recall that
M_{n}
is the a.s. unique ground state.

6.1.
Variance Bounds

A variance lower bound is key to the application of (
6.1
), it is a necessary ingredient in showing that the error terms vanish as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 6.1
.

There exist constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

C_{1}n^{d}\leqslant\var(H(M_{n}))\leqslant C_{2}n^{d}.

As is usually expected, the lower bound is less straightforward to obtain as compared to the upper bound. Recall from (
2.8
) the definition of the transition point for
x\in\mathsf{V}\sqcup\mathsf{E}
,
K_{n,x}
. By definition,

\{K_{n,x}>J_{x}\}=\{x\in M_{n}\}\text{ and }\{K_{n,x}<J_{x}\}=\{x\notin M_{n}\}.

The following lemma is obvious from the convergence of
K_{n,x}
and its independence with respect to
J_{x}
. However, the following quantitative version of the proof is instructive.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
x\in\Sigma
. Let
J_{x}
be the weight associated to
x
and
J_{x}^{\prime}
be an independent copy. Then
\mathds{P}(\max\{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}\}<K_{n,x})>c_{1}>0
uniformly in
n
.

Proof.

The probability of the event
\{x\in M_{n}\}
admits entirely local bounds which are independent of
n
. When
x
is a vertex, observe:

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{x}:=\{O(x)<0\}=\{\max_{u:u\sim x}\left(J_{u}+J_{x}-J_{u,x}\right)<0\}\subseteq\{x\in M_{n}\}.

Likewise, if
x=(u,v)
is an edge,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{x}:=\{J_{u,v}<\min_{t\sim u,w\sim v}(J_{t,u}-J_{t}+J_{v,w}-J_{w},J_{v,w}-J_{w}+J_{u},J_{t,u}-J_{t}+J_{v},J_{u}+J_{v})\}\subseteq\{x\in M_{n}\}.

We provide the proof for the vertex case as it is more convenient to write, the same principle applies to the edge case. If
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{\prime}
is the analogous event defined with only
J_{x}
replaced with
J^{\prime}_{x}
, then

\mathds{P}(\mathcal{A}_{x}\cap\mathcal{A}_{x}^{\prime})\leqslant\mathds{P}(J^{\prime}_{x},J_{x}<K_{n,x}).

Since all our weights have continuous distribution with full support, it follows that
\exists c_{1}>0
such that
\mathds{P}(\mathcal{A}_{x}\cap\mathcal{A}_{x}^{\prime})>c_{1}
. Clearly, since the event
\mathcal{A}_{x}
depends only on the immediate neighbourhood of
x
,
c_{1}
is independent of
n
.

\blacksquare

Note that the same method used to prove Lemma
6.2
can be used to show that the event
\{J_{x}<K_{n,x}<J_{x}^{\prime}\}
has strictly positive probability, uniformly bounded away from zero in
n
. Restriction to the event described in Lemma
6.2
enables an easier computation of the variance lower bound.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

The upper bound follows via a straightforward application of the Efron-Stein inequality. Let
x\in{\mathsf{\Sigma}_{n}}
, and consider the ground state that rises from independent replacement of the weight
J_{x}
with an independent copy
J_{x}^{\prime}
. Since the energy of the ground state is a
1-
Lipschitz function of the weights, we know that

|H_{n}(M_{n})-H_{n}(M_{n}^{x})|\leqslant|J_{x}-J^{\prime}_{x}|.

Thus, by the Efron-Setin inequality,

\var(H(M_{n}))\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Sigma_{N}}\E(H_{n}(M_{n})-H_{n}(M_{n}^{x}))^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Sigma_{n}}\E(J_{x}-J^{\prime}_{x})^{2},

which immediately implies that there is a constant
C_{1}
such that

\var{H(M_{n})}\leqslant C_{1}n^{d}.

For the lower bound, we will employ a martingale approach. Let us enumerate the vertices in
\mathsf{V}_{n}
. Consider the sequence of filtrations
\mathcal{F}_{j}:=\sigma(\{J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}\ldots J_{j}\})
. We use this filtration to define a martingale

H_{n,j}:=\E(H(M_{n})|\mathcal{F}_{j}),

and corresponding martingale differences

\Delta H_{n,j}:=H_{n,j+1}-H_{n,j}.

Adding up the martingale differences and using the fact that the variance is always bounded below by the variance of the conditional expectation, we get

\var{H(M_{n})}\geqslant\var{H_{n,n^{d}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n^{d}-1}\var{\Delta H_{n,j}}.

Let
J_{j+1}^{\prime}
denote an independent copy of
J_{j+1}
, let
M_{n}^{(j+1)}
be the ground state obtained after the replacement of
J_{j=1}
. We may express the martingale difference in terms of independent replacement as

{\Delta H_{n,j}}=\E(H(M_{n})|\mathcal{F}_{j+1})-\E(H(M_{n})|\mathcal{F}_{j})=\E(H(M_{n})-H(M_{n}^{(j+1)})|\mathcal{F}_{j+1}).

Thus,

\var(\Delta H_{n,j})=\E(\E(H(M_{n})-H(M_{n}^{(j+1)})|\mathcal{F}_{j+1})^{2})

By a combination of Jensen’s inequality and the tower property,

\displaystyle\E(\E(H(M_{n})-H(M_{n}^{(j+1)})|\mathcal{F}_{j})^{2})\geqslant

\displaystyle\E(\E(H(M_{n})-H(M_{n}^{(j+1))})|J_{j})^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\E(\E(H(M_{n})-H(M_{n}^{(j+1)})|J_{j+1},J_{j+1}^{\prime})^{2})

We may further bound below by restricting to the event
\{J_{j+1}^{\prime}<K_{n,j+1}\}\cap\{J_{j+1}<K_{n,j+1}\}
.

(6.4)

\displaystyle\E(H(M^{(j+1)}_{n})-H(M_{n}))^{2}\geqslant\E(H(M^{(j+1)}_{n})-H(M_{n}))^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{j+1},J_{j+1}^{\prime}<K_{n,j+1}}

On this event,

(6.5)

\displaystyle(H(M_{n}^{(j)})-H(M_{n}))^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{j}^{\prime},J_{j}<K}=(J_{j}^{\prime}-J_{j})^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{j}^{\prime},J_{j}<K_{n,j}}

Note that
J^{\prime}=J
with probability
0
. If
\E(J^{\prime}-J)^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{A}}=0
, then it must be that
\mathds{P}(\mathcal{A})=0
. Thus,
\E(J_{j}^{\prime}-J_{j})^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{j}^{\prime},J_{j}<K(j)}>C_{2}
for some
C_{2}>0
by Lemma
6.2
. In turn, this tells us that
\var(\Delta M_{n,j})>\varepsilon
, and finally on adding up the martingale differences,

\var{M_{n}}>C_{2}n^{d}.

\blacksquare

There are two error terms in (
6.1
) which we need to show vanish in the limit. The variance lower bound is adequate to show that the latter of the two vanishes when
g(\boldsymbol{J})=H(M_{n}(\boldsymbol{J}))
. Indeed, thanks to the finite fourth moment assumption,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\var H(M_{n}))^{3/4}}\left(\sum_{x\in V_{n}\sqcup E_{n}}\E|\partial_{x}H(M_{n})|^{3}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\frac{1}{C_{1}n^{3d/4}}\left(\sum_{x}|J_{x}^{\prime}-J_{x}|^{3}\right)^{1/2}\leqslant\frac{Cd^{1/2}n^{d/2}}{C_{2}n^{3d/4}}.

6.2.
Transition Point Convergence

We now upper bound the first term on the right hand side of (

).

Lemma 6.3
.

There exists a constant

and

We will be defining the sequence
\varepsilon(R,n)
to be the deviation in a certain sense, of a ground state configuration that arises from the restriction to a box

. We define

to be the ground state of the monomer dimer model defined on

with periodic boundary conditions for

. Observe that

(6.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}H(M_{n})=(J_{x}^{\prime}-J_{x})\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{n,x}}

\displaystyle+(K_{n,x}-J_{x})\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x}<K_{n,x}<J_{x}^{\prime}}

(6.8)

\displaystyle+(J_{x}^{\prime}-K_{n,x})\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{n,x}<J_{x}}.

Define

(6.9)

\displaystyle E_{x,n,1}:=\frac{K_{n,x}-J_{x}}{J_{x}^{\prime}-J_{x}}\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x}<K_{n,x}<J^{\prime}_{x}}\text{ and }E_{x,n,2}:=\frac{J_{x}^{\prime}-K_{n,x}}{J_{x}^{\prime}-J_{x}}\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{n,x}<J_{x}}.

We define
E_{x,R,1}
and
E_{x,R,2}
analogously using
K_{R,x}
instead of
K_{n,x}
. Observe that
|E_{x,\cdot,\cdot}|\leqslant 1
almost surely, and further

|E_{x,n,\cdot}-E_{x,R,\cdot}|\leqslant 2.

In extracting a bound for the

, we will be replacing
\partial_{x}H(M_{n})
with its corresponding local version, and then show that the error of replacement is small. In particular, we seek a bound on
\partial_{x}H(M_{n})-\partial_{x}H(M_{n,R})
. We may express (
6.7
) in terms of the random variables defined in (

\displaystyle\partial_{x}H(M_{n})=(J^{\prime}_{x}-J_{x})\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{n,x}}+E_{x,n,1}+E_{x,n,2}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}H(M_{n})-\partial_{x}H(M_{R,x})=(J^{\prime}_{x}-J_{x})\biggl((\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{n,x}}-\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{R,x}})+(E_{x,n,1}-E_{x,R,1})+(E_{x,n,2}-E_{x,R,2})\biggr)

For convenience, we define

(6.10)

\displaystyle A_{1}(n,R,x)

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{n,x}}-\boldsymbol{1}_{J_{x},J_{x}^{\prime}<K_{R,x}}

\displaystyle A_{2}(n,R,x)

\displaystyle:=E_{x,n,1}-E_{x,R,1}

\displaystyle A_{3}(n,R,x)

\displaystyle:=E_{x,n,2}-E_{x,R,2}.

To reiterate,
|A_{1}|,|A_{2}|,|A_{3}|\leqslant 2
almost surely. By Hölder’s inequality, with

and

(6.11)

\displaystyle\E|\partial_{x}H(M_{n})-\partial_{x}H(M_{R})|^{4}\leqslant\left(\E(J_{x}^{\prime}-J_{x})^{4+\delta}\right)^{4/(4+\delta)}\cdot\left(\E|A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}|^{(16+4\delta)/\delta}\right)^{\delta/(4+\delta)}.

The reason for the marginally higher moment requirement as compared to

is now apparent, the finite temperature version is appropriately “smoothed out”, enabling the use of

(6.12)

Proving the CLT comes down to showing that

Lemma 6.4
.

As

\mathds{P}(|K_{n,x}-K_{R,x}|>\delta,\max\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}>\beta)\to 0.

Proof.

By

converges in law to
(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{M})

is a measurable function of
\boldsymbol{J}
. Thus for any

(6.13)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\biggl(J_{x}\in\bigl(\min\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\},\max\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}\bigr)\biggr)=\mathds{P}(x\in M_{n}\Delta M_{R})\to 0

as

, where the symmetric difference is appropriately defined via periodic extension. Define

(6.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n,R,\delta}:=\{J_{x}\in(K_{n,x},K_{R,x}),\text{ }|K_{n,x}-K_{R,x}|>\delta,\text{ }\max\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}>\beta\},

and for

and

, define

\mathcal{A}_{n,R,\delta,M,y}:=\mathcal{A}_{n,R,\delta}\cap\{-M\leqslant K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\leqslant M\}\cap\bigl\{\max\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}>y+\beta\bigr\}.

Now, using the independence of

and

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(\mathcal{A}_{n,R,\delta,M,y})\geqslant\mathds{P}\biggl(|K_{n,x}-K_{R,x}|>\delta,\text{ }\max\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}\in(\beta+y,M),\text{}\min\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}>-M\biggr)\cdot P_{M,y}

and

is the density of

\mathds{P}\biggl(|K_{n,x}-K_{R,x}|>\delta,\text{ }\beta+y<\max\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}\leqslant M,\text{ }\min\{K_{n,x},K_{R,x}\}>-M\biggr)\leqslant\frac{\mathds{P}(\mathcal{A}_{n,R,\delta,M,y})}{P_{M,y}}.

Now, let

Corollary 6.6
.

Using the bilinearity of the covariance, we can expand the covariance as follows:

\displaystyle\cov\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{1}\in M_{n}},\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{2}\in M_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cov\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{1}\in M_{R}},\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{2}\in M_{R}}\right)+\cov(A_{1}(n,R,x_{1}),\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{2}\in M_{R}})

\displaystyle+\cov(\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{1}\in M_{R}},A_{1}(n,R,x_{2}))+\cov(A_{1}(n,R,x_{1}),A_{2}(n,R,x_{2})).

Note, since

and
x_{2}
are disjoint. Therefore, the ground states defined on the respective boxes are independent, since the corresponding weights are independent. This tells us

\cov(\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{1}\in M_{R}},\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{2}\in M_{R}})=0.

The following bound is straightforward since
\boldsymbol{1}_{x\in M_{R}}
is bounded above by
1
;

\displaystyle\cov(A_{1}(n,R,x_{1}),\boldsymbol{1}_{x_{2}\in M_{R}})\leqslant 4\E|A_{1}(n,R)|.

Of course an analogous bound follows when the roles of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are swapped. Next, as a consequence of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we find that

\cov(A_{1}(n,R,x_{1}),A_{2}(n,R,x_{2}))\leqslant 4\E(A_{1}(n,R))^{2}.

The final bound follows from Jensen’s inequality.

\blacksquare

6.3.
Proof of CLT

We emphasize now that
\partial_{x}H(M_{n})
and
\partial_{x}H(M_{n})^{S}
are identically distributed, so any moment calculations directly carry over. We will write, for convenience

(6.15)

\displaystyle G_{R,S,x}:=\partial_{x}H(M_{n})^{S}-\partial_{x}H(M_{R})^{S}.

and

G_{R,x}=G_{R,\emptyset,x}.

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

The primary ingredient in this proof is a double application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Note, for
S
not containing
x
and
T
not containing
y
, we need to evaluate the following covariance:

\cov\biggl(\partial_{x}H(M_{R,x})+G_{R,x})\cdot(\partial_{x}H(M_{R,x})^{S}+G_{R,S,x}),(\partial_{y}H(M_{R,y})+G_{R,y})\cdot(\partial_{y}H(M_{R,y})^{T}+G_{R,T,y})\biggr).

There are
16
terms that arise on expansion of the product. First, observe that for any monomial that contains a
G
term, that is
\cov(XY,ZW)
where atleast one of
X,Y,Z,\text{ or }W
is one of the error terms
G
, we have that

\cov(XY,ZW)\leqslant C\varepsilon(n,R).

We obtain this bound by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality twice and using (
6.11
). We still need a bound on the expectation of the unique monomial containing all non
G
terms;

(6.16)

\displaystyle\cov(\partial_{x}H(M_{R,x})\partial_{x}H(M_{R,x})^{S},\partial_{y}H(M_{R,y})\partial_{y}H(M_{R,y})^{T}).

In this case, note that the expectation vanishes unless
y\in B_{R}(x)
, or equivalently
x\in B_{R}(y)
. This is because if
x
and
y
are separated by a distance greater than
R
, then
M_{R,x}
and
M_{R,y}
are independent. We know that
H
is
1-
Lipschitz, and thus,

|\partial_{x}H(M_{R})|^{4}\leqslant|J_{x}-J_{x}^{\prime}|^{4}

This is bounded in expectation since we know that
\left\|J\right\|_{4+\delta}<\infty
. Thus, (
6.16
) is bounded above by

16C\varepsilon(n,R)+C_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{y\in B_{R}(x)}.

Summing over
x,y
completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Lemma
6.3
was the last piece required to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof.

We have already shown that

\frac{1}{({\var{H(M_{n})}})^{3/4}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathsf{V}_{n}\sqcup\mathsf{E}_{n}}\E|\partial_{x}H(M_{n})|^{3}\right)^{1/2}\to 0.

The only step remaining is to specify the
R
in Lemma
6.3
. The only requirement is that
R\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and
R/n=o(1)
. A valid choice is yielded by
R=\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor
. It is clear then that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\var{H(M_{n})}}}\left(\sum_{x,y}c(x,y)\right)^{1/4}\leqslant C\varepsilon(n,\sqrt{n})\to 0.

\blacksquare

6.4.
Generalizations

We have defined the finite model on the torus and then taken a large
n
limit so that translation invariance is guaranteed from the start. However, the methods described will work for any weight independent boundary condition (with suitable averaging over translates to yield translation invariance in limit). This method can also easily be adapted to the setting of amenable, unimodular, transitive graphs. This is because translation invariance and the Burton Keane argument are the key steps. Our methods should also be generalizable to obtain a ‘
1
or
\infty
’ type result for the cardinality of
\sf G(\boldsymbol{J})
in the spirit of
[
7
]
, however we do not pursue this in this article.

Another direction in which our results can be generalized is related to the support of the distribution of the weights on the monomers. Our methods (directly) cover the important case of the weights on the edges being random variables with good distribution taking values in
[\beta,\infty)
where
\beta<0
, but for the vertices the weights are identically 0. This is because in the perturbation step
Equation

4.4
, we only perturb the edge weights, and hence absolute continuity for this case still holds. Another observation is that this case is equivalent to studying the minimum weight independent set on the line graphs of transitive, unimodular, amenable graphs, which is perhaps of independent interest. Our results directly imply uniqueness and a CLT for ground states of independent sets on such graphs.

8.1.
Mass Transport Principle

In the study of translation invariant processes the mass transport principle (MTP) is a crucial tool. We state it below in the form used here, and refer the reader to
[
18
,
5
]
for more details.

Let us consider a random function
{\sf X}:{\sf\Sigma}\to\Gamma
where
\Gamma
is some complete separable metric space. Let us assume
\sf X
is translation invariant in law. We will apply this for
{\sf X}=\Xi
or
{\sf X}=X
where
\Xi
and
X
are as in (
4.5
). A function
g:{\sf V}\times{\sf V}\times\Gamma^{{\sf\Sigma}}\to[0,\infty]
is said to be invariant under diagonal action of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
if for every
\alpha\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
, and every
\xi:{\sf\Sigma}\to\Gamma
,

g(x,y,\xi)=g(x+\alpha,y+\alpha,\xi+\alpha)

where
\xi+\alpha:{\sf\Sigma}\to\Xi
is defined as
(\xi+\alpha)_{i+\alpha}=\xi_{i}
for all
i\in\Sigma
.
The function
g(x,y,\xi)
can be thought of as the ‘mass sent from
x
to
y
’. The mass transport principle states that in expectation (over the law of
{\sf X}
), the total mass out of the origin is the same as the total mass in.

Lemma 8.1

(Mass transport principle)
.

Let
g
be a function as above which is invariant under the diagonal action of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
. Then

\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{y}g({\boldsymbol{0}},y,{\sf X})\right)=\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{y}g(y,{\boldsymbol{0}},{\sf X})\right)

8.2.
Absolutely Continuous Perturbations

In this section we finish the proof of
Lemma

4.8
. Let
\Sigma
be a countable set. Recall the definition of
g_{z}
from
Definition

3.1
, as well as
C(z,\alpha)
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Consider
(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in[\beta,\infty)^{\Sigma}\times[0,1]^{2}
, where
(U,U^{\prime})
are independent uniform random variables, which are independent of
\boldsymbol{J}
. Let
S\subset\Sigma
be a fixed subset. Let
\boldsymbol{Z}\in[0,\infty)^{\Sigma}
depend measurably on
(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})
and assume that
\boldsymbol{Z}
is identically 0 outside
S
, that is
Z_{x}=0
for all
x\notin S
. Define
\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}}
by setting
\tilde{J}_{x}=J_{x}+Z_{x}
for all
x\in\Sigma
. The distribution of
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},U,U^{\prime})
is absolutely continuous with respect to the distribution of
(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})
.

We state the following measure theoretic fact before proving our lemma. Let
\boldsymbol{z}\in[0,\infty)^{\Sigma}
be such that it is
0
except at a finite fixed collection of indices
S\subset\Sigma
. The following equality is a standard computation for any Borel set
\mathcal{A}\subset[\beta,\infty)^{\Sigma}
and measurable
F
such that the expectations are finite on both sides:

(8.1)

\displaystyle\E\bigl(F(\boldsymbol{J}+\boldsymbol{z})\boldsymbol{1}_{\boldsymbol{J}+\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigr)=\E\biggl(F(\boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{1}_{\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathcal{A}}\prod_{x\in\Sigma}g_{z_{x}}(J_{x})\biggr)

Note that
g_{z_{x}}(J_{x})=1
if
x\not\in S
.

Proof of Lemma
8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Borel subset of
[\beta,\infty)^{\Sigma}
, and
\mathcal{B}
be a Borel subset of
[0,1]^{2}
. For fixed
\boldsymbol{z}\in[0,\infty)^{S}
and
\varepsilon>0
, let
B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{z})
denote the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
\boldsymbol{z}
, appropriately truncated at the boundary. We know that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\left((\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},U,U^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B},\boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left((\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in(\mathcal{A}-\boldsymbol{Z})\times\mathcal{B},\boldsymbol{Z}(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\E\biggl(\boldsymbol{1}_{(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in(\mathcal{A}-\boldsymbol{Z})\times\mathcal{B}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\boldsymbol{Z}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{z})}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\E\biggl(\boldsymbol{1}_{(\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\boldsymbol{Z}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{z})}\cdot\prod_{x\in S}g_{Z_{x}}(J_{x})\biggr).

By Hölder’s inequality, we obtain an upper bound given by

\displaystyle\max_{y\in B_{\varepsilon}(z)}\E\biggl(\prod_{x\in S}g^{\alpha}_{y_{i}}(J_{x})\biggr)^{1/\alpha}\cdot

\displaystyle\mathds{P}((\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{\boldsymbol{y}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{z})}C(y_{i},\alpha)^{|S|/\alpha}\cdot\mathds{P}((\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}.

This is sufficient to conclude that if
\mathds{P}((\boldsymbol{J},U,U^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})=0
, then
\mathds{P}((\tilde{\boldsymbol{J}},U,U^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})=0
by a standard limiting argument involving
\boldsymbol{z}
.

\blacksquare
