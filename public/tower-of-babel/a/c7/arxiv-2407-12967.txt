Rényi-infinity constrained sampling with d 3 d^{3} membership queries
Yunbum Kook Matthew S. Zhang

Abstract
Uniform sampling over a convex body is a fundamental algorithmic problem, yet the convergence in KL or Rényi divergence of most samplers remains poorly understood. In this work, we propose a constrained proximal sampler, a principled and simple algorithm that possesses elegant convergence guarantees. Leveraging the uniform ergodicity of this sampler, we show that it converges in the Rényi-infinity divergence ( ℛ ∞ \mathcal{R}_{\infty} ) with no query complexity overhead when starting from a warm start. This is the strongest of commonly considered performance metrics, implying rates in { ℛ q , 𝖪𝖫 } \{\mathcal{R}_{q},\mathsf{KL}\} convergence as special cases. By applying this sampler within an annealing scheme, we propose an algorithm which can approximately sample ε \varepsilon -close to the uniform distribution on convex bodies in ℛ ∞ \mathcal{R}_{\infty} -divergence with 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d 3 ​ polylog ​ 1 ε ) \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\,\text{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}) query complexity. This improves on all prior results in { ℛ q , 𝖪𝖫 } \{\mathcal{R}_{q},\mathsf{KL}\} -divergences, without resorting to any algorithmic modifications or post-processing of the sample. It also matches the prior best known complexity in total variation distance.

Introduction

Uniform sampling from convex bodies is a fundamental question in computer science.
Its applications have spanned from differential privacy
[
MT07
,
HT10
,
Mir17
]
to scientific computing
[
CV16
,
HCT+17
,
KLSV22
]
and machine learning
[
BCJ+19
,
Sta20
]
.

The standard computational model assumes that, given a convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfies
B_{1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{K}\subseteq B_{D}(0)
, the algorithm can access a
membership oracle

[
GLS88
]
which, given a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, answers
Yes
or
No
to the query “Is
x\in\mathcal{K}
?”.
This framework possesses two advantages: (i) it is the most general framework under which one can conduct analysis, subsuming other computational models as particular cases,
(ii) it has been thoroughly studied both in optimization and sampling.

Under this computational model, what is the complexity in terms of membership oracle queries to generate a random sample from a convex body
\mathcal{K}
(i.e., from
\pi=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
)?
As many of the desired applications require the dimension
d
to be large, it is typically impractical to generate samples exactly from the uniform distribution. Among other difficulties, this is due to the intractability of deterministically computing the normalizing factor
\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})
. Instead, one resorts to sampling from an approximate distribution which is
\varepsilon
-close to the uniform in an appropriate sense. Subsequent work has aimed to develop algorithms and analysis with optimal asymptotic dependence both on the dimension
d
and error tolerance
\varepsilon
.

The seminal work of
[
DFK91
]
proposed a randomized polynomial time algorithm with oracle complexity
\text{poly}(d,\log\frac{D}{\varepsilon})
.
Since then, a proliferation of improvements to both the algorithm and analysis
[
LS90
,
Lov91
,
AK91
,
LS93
,
KLS97
,
LV06b
,
CV18
]
have led to a refined understanding of the complexity of this problem. Such algorithms are typically instances of a random walk, which first samples a point
x_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
for a tractable initial measure
\mu_{0}
, and then produces
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k_{*}}
following a well-specified, possibly random procedure for some number of iterations
k_{*}
. Under mild assumptions, it is possible to make
\mu_{k_{*}}
, the distribution of
x_{k_{*}}
,
\varepsilon
-close to
\pi
in some desired sense. These iterative schemes are called often Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.

The notion of closeness can take many different forms. Natural choices of metric

\mathsf{TV}
) fit into the following hierarchy (see §

\mathsf{TV}^{2}\leq\mathsf{KL}\text{ divergence}\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}\text{ divergence}\leq\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\text{ divergence}.

While the complexity of uniformly sampling convex bodies was known to be
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
for
\varepsilon
-
\mathsf{TV}

[

, the best known rates in any other metric had much worse dependence on key parameters. Additionally, they relied on algorithmic modifications which deviated from practical implementations
[
BDMP17
,
GHZ22
,
MV22
]
.
On the other hand, the literature on unconstrained sampling has provided stronger guarantees in
\mathsf{KL}
or

overhead in complexity, through refined analyses of Langevin-based algorithms
[
VW19
,
CEL+22
,
CCSW22
]
.
Adapting those guarantees to the convex body setting is not trivial, since restricting the distribution to
\mathcal{K}
places a strong constraint on the candidate algorithm. The discrepancies in complexity when going beyond
\mathsf{TV}
leads us to a fundamental question on the complexity of uniform sampling in a stronger metric:

Is it possible to achieve a query complexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
for uniform sampling in
\{\mathsf{KL},\mathcal{R}_{q},\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\}
-divergence?

In general, almost all algorithms take two phases: (
\mathsf{P1}
)
warm-start generation
and (
\mathsf{P2}
)
faster sampling under warm-start
.

\mathsf{P1}
finds a tractable initial distribution
\mu_{0}
towards the target
\pi
, whose closeness to
\pi
is measured by the
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
divergence at initialization,
M=\sup\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}=\exp(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi))
.
\mathsf{P2}
then samples from the target by leveraging the initial warmness.
This approach is also taken by
[
CV18
]
, the best known uniform-sampling algorithm in
\mathsf{TV}
. However, it fails to go beyond
\mathsf{TV}
, as a warm-start generated by the algorithm holds only in a
weak sense
, causing “
\mathsf{TV}
-collapse” (we return to this shortly). The resulting sampler can only have guarantees in
\mathsf{TV}
.

Ignoring
\mathsf{P1}
for the moment, one hopes that, towards stronger guarantees, there should be a sampler which converges in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
in
\mathsf{P2}
.
Recently,
[
KVZ24
]
proposed an algorithm with query complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(qMd^{2}\|\Sigma\|_{\mathsf{op}}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
for
\Sigma

\pi
, which seamlessly extends the best complexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(Md^{2}\|\Sigma\|_{\mathsf{op}}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
in
\mathsf{TV}
that is achieved by
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
.
However, this immediately raises a problem:
it requires a
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
guarantee at initialization in order to achieve
\mathcal{R}_{q}
-convergence.
One might want to sample from
\pi
directly from some simple feasible start, such as a uniform distribution on the unit ball. Unfortunately, such choices potentially incur exponential warmness in dimension (i.e.,
M\gtrsim\exp(d)
). This is where
\mathsf{P1}
demonstrates its importance, allowing us to address this issue through an
annealing scheme
.

Annealing algorithms

The strategy of annealing for volume computation and uniform sampling appeared in
[
DFK91
]
.
It proceeds with a sequence of distributions
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\leq k_{*}}
which steadily evolve from
\mu_{0}\propto\mathds{1}_{B_{1}(0)}
, the uniform distribution on a unit ball, towards
\mu_{k_{*}}=\pi
the uniform distribution on the entire body. This is done by fattening
\mu_{k}
, steadily increasing its variance, until it is close enough to
\pi
to be sampled directly with MCMC.
2
2

2

In the original paper, this is done by a sequence of uniform distributions, but this strategy has since been superseded by considering a sequence of truncated Gaussians with appropriate variance.
This general schema, wherein a tractable, well-understood distribution is gradually converted to a generic one, has been called
annealing
.

This scheme has been refined by annealing via exponential distributions
[
LV06c
]
and via Gaussians.
The latter, whose use of
\{\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{k}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}\}_{k\leq k_{*}}
was pioneered by the
\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
algorithm
[
CV18
]
, turns out to be a robust choice for
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\leq k_{*}}
under an appropriate update schedule for the variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
.
These intermediate distributions are “stepping stones” towards the desired uniform distribution, in the sense that each distribution is
\mathcal{O}(1)
close to the subsequent one in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, and no more than
k_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
annealing stages are required in total.

\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
can guarantee
M=\mathcal{O}(1)
in a weak sense with the total query complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(C^{2}d^{3}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
under a canonical set-up where
\mathcal{K}
is
well-rounded
(i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{2}d
for some constant
C
)
[
KLS97
,
LV06c
,
LV06a
,
CV18
]
.
Later work
[
JLLV21
]
claims that one can find an affine transformation which maps a skewed convex body to a well-rounded one with
C=\mathcal{O}(1)
, via a randomized algorithm with query complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
.

TV-collapse

Such annealing schemes are interwoven with an
approximate
sampler, typically an MCMC subroutine such as
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, requiring the sampler to approximately sample from evolving measures.
Hence, the warmness for a next target
\mu_{k+1}
is actually measured with respect to a distribution
\tilde{\mu}_{k}
, approximately close to
\mu_{k}
.
Standard constrained samplers like
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
or
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
are only able to provide guarantees (between
\tilde{\mu}_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
) in
\mathsf{TV}
for truncated Gaussian. However, due to failure of the triangle inequality through
\mathsf{TV}
and
\mathcal{R}_{q}
in general, the warmness of
\tilde{\mu}_{k}
with respect to
\mu_{k+1}
cannot be bounded. Even worse, it is essential that the warm-start for these samplers is in some Rényi divergence, preferably the
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
divergence. As a result, the annealing schemes fail to carry a strong enough notion of warmness across the evolving measures. Instead, it passes a
weakened form of warmness
to the last step via a coupling argument, causing any sampling guarantees based on this warmness to degrade to
\mathsf{TV}
distance.

Given this challenge, we state a refinement of (
\mathsf{P1}
):

Question 1
.

Can we generate a
true
warm-start
M=\mathcal{O}(1)
with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
queries?

The answer to this hinges upon the existence of a sampler which converges in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
. As the triangle inequality holds for
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, this would enable us to anneal the distribution
without compromising

\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-closeness throughout! Examining this sends us back to a more specific form of (
\mathsf{P2}
):

Question 2
.

Given
M=\mathcal{O}(1)
and uniform or truncated Gaussian target, is there a sampler with
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
guarantees
without
any overhead in query complexity?

Rényi divergence: theoretical gap and privacy

From our earlier discussion, we cannot help but circle back to the study of Rényi divergence in constrained sampling. This is theoretically important in its own right, since it leads us to examine a current gap between unconstrained and constrained sampling.
More specifically, the improvement in metric from
\mathsf{TV}
to
\{\mathsf{KL},\mathcal{R}_{q}\}
nicely parallels the work in unconstrained sampling, where convergence in weaker metrics (Wasserstein,
\mathsf{TV}
)
[
DT12
,
Dal17
]
paved the way for a characterization of rates in
\{\mathsf{KL},\mathcal{R}_{q}\}

[
CB18
,
MFWB22
,
DMM19
,
VW19
,
MCC+21
,
CEL+22
,
CCSW22
,
ZCL+23
,
AC23
]
. This research program led to many fruitful insights into algorithmic properties and mathematical techniques, and inspires us to attempt the same in the constrained setting. See §
1.3
for detail.

In purely practical terms, the
q
-Rényi divergence, particularly
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, has appeared in the literature most often in the context of differential privacy (DP)
[
Mir17
]
.
\mathcal{R}_{q}
can be used to establish
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP algorithms
[
HT10
]
, while
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
can directly establish
\epsilon
-DP algorithms. This connection has been well-explored in other sampling applications
[
GT20
,
GLL22
,
GLL+23
]
. However, convergence in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
has remained largely out of reach, and the existing algorithms are both suboptimal in complexity and limited in scope
[
HT10
,
MV22
]
.

1.1
Contributions

We close a gap in our current understanding of uniform sampling via a principled approach.
We first leverage recent results
[
KVZ24
]
for sampling on convex bodies, proposing a generalization of
[
KVZ24
]
that we call the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
. Using uniform ergodicity results from the study of Markov chains
[
DMLM03
]
, we show that it is possible to achieve convergence in the
very strong
notion of
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
for both uniform and truncated Gaussian distributions on
\mathcal{K}
from an
\mathcal{O}(1)
warm-start without suffering any loss in complexity. By using the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
inside an annealing scheme we call
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
, we show that it is possible to sample uniformly from a well-rounded convex body (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{2}d

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(C^{2}d^{3}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
queries in expectation. This convergence takes place in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, much stronger than the prior best results in
\mathsf{TV}
.

This framework is principled, and the essential benefits of the annealing scheme and sampler are made clearly evident.
Furthermore, the approach is simple, with no need to resort to algorithmic modifications as seen in other work
[
HT10
,
BDMP17
,
MV22
]
. We present our improvements in further detail below.

1.2
Techniques and challenges

1.2.1
Constrained samplers

Uniform sampling from a warm-start

Before worrying about the problem of obtaining a warm start, it is necessary to establish the best possible oracle complexity for the sampling routine in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
when given an
\mathcal{O}(1)
warm-start.

Previous works such as
[
LV06c
,
CV18
]
relied on
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run},\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
as underlying samplers with their annealing schemes. These possess several disadvantages. Firstly, the method of analysis is rather complicated. As for
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, the best analysis (in terms of complexity) makes statements about
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
, which is a biased variant of
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

[
KLS97
,
KLM06
]
. To implement
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
with
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
, an additional correction step for debiasing the stationary measure is required. This degrades rate estimates for
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
to
\mathsf{TV}
, making the rate estimates for
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
only in
\mathsf{TV}
as a result.
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, on the other hand, is only known to mix in
\{\mathsf{TV},\chi^{2}\}
for uniform and exponential distributions
[
LV06b
]
and in
\mathsf{TV}
for general log-concave distributions
[
LV06a
]
.

By contrast, the recent work of
[
KVZ24
]
demonstrated that
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
(Algorithm
1
) could achieve
\varepsilon
-mixing in any
\mathcal{R}_{q}
, requiring
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(qMd^{2}\|\Sigma\|_{\mathsf{op}}\operatorname{polylog}{\textstyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}})
queries in expectation from an
M
-warm start.

\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
and the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}

The algorithm of
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
(or
\mathsf{Prox\text{-}uniform}
) is remarkably simple. It alternately samples from two distributions, first getting
y
from a Gaussian centred at the current point, then
z
from a Gaussian centred at
y
but truncated to
\mathcal{K}
(Lines
2
and
3
in Algorithm
1
). This turns out to correspond to simulating the heat equation applied to the current measure, and then simulating a notion of time reversal of the heat equation (Lemma
20
). These dynamics cause any
\mathcal{R}_{q}
divergence to decay exponentially, with the speed of decay dependent on the isoperimetry of the target distribution.

The only remaining challenge is to bound the wasted queries in Line
3
. This follows a local conductance argument, where one quantifies the probability of
y
landing in a set that has a hard time returning to
\mathcal{K}
. This step requires both that
\mu_{0}
be warm (for reasons to be explained later) and that the variance
h=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{-2})
is appropriately small.

Gaussian sampling with
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}

Since we will end up annealing with Gaussians, we need to demonstrate that the guarantees for
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
are compatible with (truncated) Gaussian distributions
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}
. This is not necessarily trivial. While the rate estimates for
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
can be adapted for Gaussians
[
CV18
]
, those for
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
cannot be
[
LV06a
]
. To use
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
in an annealing scheme,
[
LV06c
]
resorts to exponential distributions, which have worse complexity.

In this work, we generalize the
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
algorithm for truncated Gaussians on
\mathcal{K}
. This can be done by adapting the forward and backward heat flow interpretations to incorporate a potential function in the target, in analogy with works in unconstrained sampling
[
CCSW22
]
. By the same analogy, we call our algorithm the (constrained)
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
.

While the calculations for the Gaussian case are somewhat involved, the approach to bounding the query complexity does not require any algorithmic modifications instead. The number of iterations needed to mix in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
for the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(q(d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2}))
, already follows as a consequence of a generic lemma shown in
[
KVZ24
]
(restated in Lemma
21
). As for the query complexity, a careful bound on the local conductance following the same approach as
[
KVZ24
]
(Lemma
23
) shows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(M)
membership queries suffices in each iteration. This makes
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(qM(d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2}))
queries in total. The complexity matches that of

\mathsf{TV}

\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
/
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}

We note that this is the first time that

\mathcal{R}_{q}
(and, as we shall see, in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}

\pi=e^{-f}|_{\mathcal{K}}

1.2.2
Warm starts and Rényi divergence

Importance of warm starts

We briefly digress to explain why warm starts are necessary in each of these contexts. The above samplers repeat a subroutine wherein a point is proposed, either from uniform distribution in a ball for
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
, and a Gaussian for
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
. However, naïvely using each of these points will bias the sampling algorithm. Thus, the subroutines must contain rejection steps which correct the distribution of the proposal. To preserve our complexity bounds, we must ensure that the number of rejections is small. Otherwise, the algorithm will waste the majority of its queries without moving.

How to control the number of rejections? If
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
is initialized at the target
\pi\propto\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
with suitable algorithmic parameters, then one can bound the expected number of rejections by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.
Instead, with
\exp(\mathcal{R}_{\infty})\leq M
at initialization, a straightforward calculation bounds the number of rejections by that experienced by
\pi
, multiplied by a factor of
M
. This explicitly reveals the importance of starting warm when bounding the number of rejections.

Given this context, we denote by
\mathsf{M}\to\mathsf{N}
when a sampler starting with warmness in a metric
\mathsf{M}
has convergence guarantees in a possibly weaker metric
\mathsf{N}
.
The guarantees of the previous samplers can be stated for the truncated Gaussian as
\mathsf{Ball\ walk},\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathsf{TV}
and
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}:\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{R}_{q}
. The previous discussion implies that we would require
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
to relay
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-guarantees across the annealing routine. Of the aforementioned works, only
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
is close to achieving this. Nonetheless, closing this gap from
\mathcal{R}_{q}
to
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
is far from trivial and requires the introduction of new analytical techniques, as we subsequently explain.

The difficulties of
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}

One would expect that convergence in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
is extremely difficult to show. By comparison, even for a continuous-time process such as the Langevin dynamics, convergence in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
is not known outside a few specific cases. Previous sampling guarantees in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
usually involve a complexity at least linear in
q

[
CEL+22
,
CCSW22
,
ZCL+23
,
AC23
,
KVZ24
]
, which render them useless for
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
.

The techniques for analyzing
q
-Rényi divergence usually involve constructing a Markov semigroup whose interpolation in discrete time yields the sampler of interest. The time evolution equations for this semigroup can then be used to derive exponential convergence of
\mathcal{R}_{q}
, with a constant depending on the isoperimetry of the stationary measure for the semigroup. This approach fails miserably for
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
. In its standard representation,
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, being the supremum of the density ratio, cannot be written as the expectation of a continuous quantity, and its decay properties are difficult to establish. Instead, one must look for proof strategies that use more information about the semigroup.

Any-start implies
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}

It is well known in the Markov chain literature that, if a Markov chain mixes rapidly in
\mathsf{TV}
from any deterministic starting point (a property known as
uniform ergodicity
), then the Markov chain causes the density to contract towards that of
\pi
in any arbitrarily strong norm. In particular, choosing the
L^{\infty}
-norm, it is possible to translate statements in a weak norm like “the algorithm converges to within
\varepsilon
in
\mathsf{TV}
distance of
\pi
within
K
iterations, given
any starting point
in the support of
\pi
” to an unconditional statement in an
extremely strong
norm — “the algorithm mixes in
L^{\infty}
distance within
K
iterations” (Theorem
14
). We call this analytical technique
boosting
for its remarkable strengthening of the performance metric.

This result had thus far been difficult to apply in most sampling settings. Consider for instance sampling from an unconstrained standard Gaussian, via some MCMC method like the Langevin diffusion. The
\mathsf{TV}
can always be bounded by the
\mathsf{KL}
-divergence, which contracts exponentially. However, because
\pi
is supported on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is always possible to pick a starting point arbitrarily far away from the Gaussian’s mode. This causes
\mathsf{KL}
at initialization to be arbitrarily large. The number of iterations that are needed to converge from a point-mass at
x
is roughly
\gtrsim\log\|x\|
, which is not bounded for all starting points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus, no finite number of iterations of the Markov chain will be sufficient to show uniform ergodicity across the entirety of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, even though the dynamics converge exponentially quickly in any
\mathcal{R}_{q}
.

In passing, we also mention another notion of boosting from
\mathsf{TV}
to
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
. This is done via a post-processing step that can be found in the work of
[
MV22
]
. Examining the guarantees of this approach, however, shows that the query complexity of the original algorithm unavoidably increases by a factor of
\poly(d)
. Thus, even if one could obtain warm-start at every step, this approach would give at minimum
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{5})
query complexity

One can use the

Proximal sampler mixes from any start

Thus, it suffices to establish convergence guarantees given any deterministic starting point within
\mathcal{K}
. When attempting to do this, a major issue arises in the analysis of the classic
\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
, which uses
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
. The mixing of
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
is established using a conductance-oriented proof. If the
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
is analyzed directly via
s

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
steps (rather than just the query complexity)
[
LV07
]
. This is problematic, since if we started at a bad initial point (for example, the tip of a cone), the resulting number of
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
steps can be exponentially large in dimension

\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
through
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}

KLM06
]
, requires a non-Markovian correction step, which rules out the boosting technique. Another candidate,
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}

[
LV06b
,
LV06a
]
. Hence, this disqualifies
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
as a sampler due to additional overhead of
\text{poly}(d)
from any feasible start.

For these discrete-time samplers, one could try to directly prove a
modified logarithmic-Sobolev inequality
(MLSI) of their corresponding Markov kernel (as opposed to for the stationary distribution
\pi
). While this approach can potentially bound the complexity overhead of these samplers from any feasible start by
\operatorname{polylog}(d)
(or worse), they require an involved analysis of the kernel as well as the target
\pi
. In contrast,
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
can be interpreted in terms of continuous forward and backward heat flows, so its mixing can be characterized by functional inequalities of just the target
\pi
. Thus,
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
enjoys substantially simpler analysis, with richer connections to established functional inequalities for log-concave measures.

Using
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
(Algorithm
1
and
2
), we can indeed achieve any-start results without overhead, thereby obtaining
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
guarantees via the boosting.
Leveraging the log-Sobolev inequality for the target, the number of iterations needs only depend doubly-logarithmically on the warmness. From a point-mass, it can be established that the warmness (after one iteration of
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
) is exponential in dimension, so this allows one to pay only a logarithmic factor when establishing mixing from any point-mass within
\mathcal{K}
. These factors combined allow
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
to do what
\mathsf{Ball\ walk},\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
could not: obtain
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
guarantees without any computational overhead or post-processing. To our knowledge, our paper is the first work in MCMC to accomplish this, and we hope that our methods would serve as a blueprint for obtaining similar guarantees, at least in the constrained sampling setting.

Related work

Constrained samplers

The previous approaches to constrained sampling via random walks date to
[
DFK91
]
. The most well-studied algorithms are
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}

[
LS93
,
KLS97
]
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}

[
Smi84
,
Lov99
]
.
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
is a simple scheme which samples
y
from a ball around the current point, accepting
y
if
y\in\mathcal{K}
and otherwise just remaining at the current point. Although it is possible to analyze
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
directly
[
LV07
]
, usually
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
refers to the the algorithm given by composing
\mathsf{Speedy\ walk}
with rejection sampling. The best known guarantee in
[
KLM06
]
gives a complexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(Md^{2}D^{2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
with
M=\exp\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi)
.

Another standard algorithm is
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
, which samples uniformly at random from a chord
\mathcal{K}\cap\ell
, where
\ell
is a random line passing through the current point.
Its query complexity for uniform and exponential distributions is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\operatorname{tr}\Sigma\log\frac{M}{\varepsilon})
, where
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^{\otimes 2}]
. See §
1.2
for the benefits and drawbacks of these two approaches.

Both
\mathsf{Ball\ walk}
and
\mathsf{Hit\text{-}and\text{-}Run}
can assume that
\mathcal{K}
is well-rounded, in the sense that
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{2}d
for a known constant
C
. A general convex body
\mathcal{K}
can be converted to a well-rounded body via an affine transformation. Finding this transformation requires an additional algorithmic ingredient called
rounding
. It has been claimed in the literature
[
JLLV21
]
that a randomized rounding algorithm exists with
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
complexity. Since this adds no computational overhead, we make the same assumption in the present work.

Apart from these general-purpose random walks, there also exist several samplers which exploit geometric information about
\mathcal{K}
, or which use stronger oracle models. The
\mathsf{Dikin\ walk}
algorithm makes use of a self-concordant barrier function
\phi
to draw samples from an anisotropic Gaussian (instead of isotropic as in
\mathsf{Ball\ walk},\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
), and converges in
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(md)
iterations for a convex body specified by
m
linear constraints
[
KN12
,
KV24
]
. Apart from this, Riemannian algorithms such as
\mathsf{Riemannian\ Hamiltonian\ Monte\ Carlo}
or
\mathsf{Riemannian\ Langevin\ Monte\ Carlo}
equip
\mathcal{K}
with a Riemannian metric, and then run a random walk using this geometry
[
GC11
,
LV18
,
LE23
,
KLSV23
,
CZS22
,
GKV23
]
.
Likewise,
\mathsf{Mirror\ Langevin}

[
ZPFP20
,
Jia21
,
AC21
,
LTVW22
,
SWW24
]
alters the geometry so that one can apply methods from unconstrained sampling, and can obtain
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
complexity with some additional assumptions.

Constrained samplers like
[
BEL18
,
Leh23
]
borrow algorithms from the unconstrained setting, and interweave them with projection steps onto
\mathcal{K}
. The best known overall complexity is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{d^{2}D^{3}}{\varepsilon^{4}})
in terms of projection oracle queries.
Finally,
[
BDMP17
,
GHZ22
]
use “soft” penalties rather than projections. All of the aforementioned techniques, however, require either additional assumptions on
\mathcal{K}
or the oracle model, or are otherwise inefficient terms of complexity.

Annealing strategies

Annealing as a computational tool dates back to
[
KGJV83
]
. Its original application was to combinatorial optimization, where the landscape of solutions is highly non-convex, and it has served as an efficient strategy for diverse problems
[
HJJ03
,
KV06
,
DCM19
]
.

The history of annealing for volume computation / uniform sampling dates back to the inception of constrained sampling, from the earliest works of
[
DFK91
]
through a long line of further improvements
[
Lov91
,
LS90
,
AK91
,
LS93
,
KLS95
,
KLS97
]
. The original incarnation of this algorithm, which remained unchanged through the listed references, samples uniformly from a sequence of bodies
\{\mathcal{K}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq k_{*}}
, with
\mathcal{K}_{k}=\mathcal{K}\cap 2^{k/d}B_{1}(0)
, with
k_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
iterations. The distribution at each prior phase is used as a warm start for the subsequent phase. Combined with a coupling argument for the approximate sampler, the law of the sample at iteration
k_{*}
can be viewed as a warm start for the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{K}
. While the number of phases is not severe, the bodies become increasingly difficult to sample as
k
increases. For instance, the complexity with
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
moves from
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2})
in the earliest phases to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
in the latest phases, with the total complexity being
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{4})
as a result.

To rectify this,
[
LV06c
]
proposed a similar multi-phase sampling strategy, but successively sampling with the exponential distributions
\mu_{k}\propto e^{-a_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}x}|_{\mathcal{K}}
where
\|a_{k}\|=2d(1-d^{-1/2})^{k}
. This needs only
k_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d})
phases to converge. However, these exponential distributions become increasingly ill-conditioned as
k
increases, and the warmness between
\mu_{k},\mu_{k+1}
is only in
\chi^{2}
. As a result, the best known complexity for obtaining a single sample with this approach is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{7/2})
.

[
CV18
]
proposes
\mathsf{Gaussian\ cooling}
, which avoids the pitfalls of both earlier approaches. Each stage
\mu_{k}
consists of a truncated Gaussian with variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
. As opposed to the previous annealing schemes, it is possible to accelerate the schedule (in the sense that
\sigma_{k}/\sigma_{k-1}
is increasing in
k
) while still maintaining
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-warmness between
\mu_{k},\mu_{k+1}
. As a result, while the total number of phases is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
, the overall complexity remains at
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
.

Warm starts and Rényi divergence

The convergence of samplers in Rényi divergences has been well studied in unconstrained sampling, beginning with results in continuous time
[
CLL19
,
VW19
]
. These were followed by Rényi guarantees of discrete time algorithms under log-Sobolev inequalities
[
GT20
,
EHZ22
,
CEL+22
]
, Poincaré inequalities
[
Leh23
,
CEL+22
]
and beyond
[
MHFH+23
]
. These algorithms are vital for warm starts in the unconstrained field as well, particularly for Metropolis adjusted algorithms
[
DCWY18
,
CDWY18
,
CLA+21
,
CG23
]
. In fact, the study of
\mathcal{R}_{q}
divergences and their connection to warm starts directly led to the present state-of-the-art algorithms in unconstrained sampling
[
AC23
]
.

Within constrained sampling,
the work of
[
HT10
]
proposed one method for obtaining guarantees in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
on transformations of the unit ball. This approach is based on grid walk, and unrelated to that in the current work. It has been extended by
[
BST14
]
to more general distributions. Secondly,
[
MV22
]
show a boosting scheme which mollifies the convex body by convolving it with a ball. Such a technique uses a sampler with
\mathcal{O}(e^{-d}\varepsilon)
-guarantees in
\mathsf{TV}
as an inner loop of the routine, which adds at least
\poly(d)
overhead when applied to a sampler with
\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
dependence.

The technique of proving
L^{\infty}
bounds using uniform ergodicity was well known in the study of Markov chains. Its history dates perhaps back to the works of Markov himself
[
Mar06
]
, among other venerable works
[
YK41
,
Doo53
,
MT12
]
. It is connected with Doeblin’s minorization condition and other fundamental properties of Markov chains
[
MT12
, §16]
, and has perhaps been most succinctly stated in
[
Rud11
, Proposition 3.23]
. The uniform ergodicity property, however, is difficult to establish, barring some exceptions such as exact Gibbs sampling or samplers on the lattice
[
LDM15
,
WL17
]
. As far as we know, we are the first to apply it in the constrained sampling setting.

Organization

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In §

2.2
Basic notions

Before proceeding, we reiterate our computational model.

Definition 5

(Membership oracle)
.

We are given a convex body
\mathcal{K}
which has
B_{1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{K}\subseteq B_{D}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
D>0
. We assume access to a
membership oracle
, which, given a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, answers
Yes
or
No
to the query “Is
x\in\mathcal{K}
?”

Where not otherwise specified, we will write
\pi=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
for the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{K}
. We introduce the following metrics between probability measures.

Definition 6

(Distance and divergence)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Their
total variation
distance is given by

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathsf{TV}}:=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lvert\mu(B)-\nu(B)\rvert\,,

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the collection of all Borel measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
q
-Rényi divergence
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\int\bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\bigr)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

if
\mu\ll\nu
, and
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\infty
otherwise. In the limit
q\to 1
, it converges to the
\mathsf{KL}
divergence,

\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\int\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,,

if
\mu\ll\nu
, and again
\mathsf{KL}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\infty
otherwise. The
\chi^{2}
-divergence is defined as
\chi^{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\pi):=\exp(\mathcal{R}_{2}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu))-1
. Finally, the limit
\lim_{q\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)
can be written as

\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu):=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\nu}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\,.

The
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
distance is especially important for us, through the concept of warmness.

Definition 7

(Warmness)
.

For
\mu\ll\nu
, we denote the warmness
M
of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
as

M:=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\nu}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}=\exp\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\bigr)\,.

Alternatively, if the above holds, then we also say that
\mu
is an
M
-warm start for
\nu
.

The following inequality for Rényi divergences will also be useful.

Lemma 8

(Data-processing inequality)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures,
P
a Markov kernel, and
q\geq 1
. Then,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu P\mathbin{\|}\nu P)\leq\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu\mathbin{\|}\nu)\,.

Functional inequalities for constrained distributions

The following functional inequalities, also known as isoperimetry inequalities, on the target distribution are vital for the analysis of sampling algorithms, being equivalent to the exponential mixing of a broad class of Markov chains.

Definition 9

(Log-Sobolev inequality)
.

A probability measure
\nu
satisfies a
log-Sobolev inequality
(
\mathsf{LSI}
) with parameter
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)
if for all smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\Ent_{\nu}(f^{2})\leq 2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\nabla f\|^{2}]\,,

(LSI)

with
\Ent_{\nu}(f^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f^{2}\log f^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f^{2}]\log\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f^{2}]
.

A weaker form of isoperimetry that is implied by the above is the Poincaré inequality.

Definition 10

(Poincaré inequality)
.

A probability measure
\nu
satisfies a
Poincaré inequality
(
\mathsf{PI}
) with parameter
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)
if for all smooth functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}f\leq C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\nu)\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|\nabla f\|^{2}]\,,

(PI)

with
\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}f:=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(f-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f)^{2}]
.

The following lemma summarizes the functional inequalities which are satisfied by (truncated) Gaussians and uniform distributions on
\mathcal{K}
, due to
[
Kla23
,
BGL14
]
. We refer readers to
[
KVZ24
, Appendix C]
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body with diameter
D
. Then, if
\pi\propto\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}
is the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{K}
,

C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\lesssim D^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\lesssim\|\Sigma\|\log d\,,

where
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[(X-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}X)(X-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}X)^{\mathsf{T}}]
is the covariance of
\pi
. For a Gaussian
\pi_{\sigma^{2}}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}

Total variation to Rényi infinity via LSI

In this section, we first review the work of

from

As a concrete example, we provide a

3.1
Strong contraction of a Markov chain

We recall standard notion in the theory of Markov semigroups.

Definition 12

(Markov kernel)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space. A
Markov kernel

P:\Omega\times\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
satisfies

1.

for each
x\in\Omega
, the map
P(\cdot|x):=P(x,\cdot)
from
\mathcal{F}
to
[0,1]
is a probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.

2.

for each
E\in\mathcal{F}
, the map
P(\cdot,E)
from
\Omega
to
[0,1]
is
\mathcal{F}
-measurable.

Given a probability measure
\mu
over
\Omega
and function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, a Markov kernel
P
acts on
\mu
and
f
to yield objects
\mu P
and
Pf
defined by

\displaystyle\mu P(\cdot)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}P(\cdot|x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\,,

\displaystyle Pf(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P(\cdot|x)}[f(Y)]=\int_{\Omega}f(y)\,P(\mathrm{d}y|x)\,.

A probability measure
\pi
is called
stationary
for
P
if
\pi P=\pi
.

In Markov semigroup theory, it is of major importance to study the
contractivity
of a Markov kernel, since it quantifies the convergence rate of its corresponding Markov chain toward the stationary distribution
\pi
. This contractivity can be captured via the contraction coefficient of
P
defined by

\|P\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}:=\sup_{0\neq f\in L^{p}_{0}}\frac{\|Pf\|_{L^{p}}}{\|f\|_{L^{p}}}\,,

where
\|f\|_{L^{p}}:=\|f\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}=(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|f|^{p}])^{1/p}
and
L^{p}_{0}:=\{f:\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[|f|^{p}]<\infty,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f=0\}
.

The most classical setting that has been studied is the
L^{2}(\pi)
space, whose contraction coefficient is given by
\gamma
if the
spectral gap
of the Markov kernel
P
is
1-\gamma
.
By substituting
f=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1
, it is also possible to quantify the convergence rate of a Markov chain in
\chi^{2}
-divergence with reference to the same constant
\gamma
.

This classical setting, however, is not sufficient for understanding
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, since we only have the one-sided bound
\mathcal{R}_{2}=\log(1+\chi^{2})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\infty}=\log\operatorname{ess\,sup}|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}|=\log\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Instead, it is natural to study the contraction coefficient in
L^{\infty}(\pi)
, defined by

\|P\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}}:=\sup_{0\neq f\in L^{\infty}_{0}}\frac{\|Pf\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}}\,,

where
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}:=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\pi}\lvert f\rvert
and
L^{\infty}_{0}:=\{f:\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\pi}\lvert f\rvert<\infty,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f=0\}
.
One observes that
L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}
contractivity implies the
uniform ergodicity
of a Markov chain, and that the opposite inequality also holds due to
[
Rud11
, Proposition 3.23]
.

Proposition 13

(
[
Rud11
, Proposition 3.23]
)
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel that is reversible with respect to the stationary distribution
\pi
. Then,

\|P^{n}-1_{\pi}\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}}\leq 2\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{x}\|P^{n}(\cdot|x)-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\,,

where
1_{\pi}
is the operator defined by
1_{\pi}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]
.

We can now deduce an explicit
L^{\infty}
-convergence result for a reversible Markov chain as follows.

Theorem 14
.

Consider a Markov chain with kernel
P
, initial distribution
\mu
and stationary distribution
\pi
. Then,

\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu P^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot 2\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{x}\|P^{n}(\cdot|x)-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\,

In particular,
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu P^{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot 2\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{x}\|P^{n}(\cdot|x)-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}
.

Proof.

Setting
f=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1
, we have

\|P^{n}-1_{\pi}\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}}\geq\frac{\|P^{n}f-1_{\pi}(f)\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}}=\frac{\|P^{n}f\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}}=\frac{\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu P^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}}\,,

where the last equality follows from
P(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi})=\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mu P)}{\mathrm{d}\pi}
due to the reversibility of
P
. Therefore,

\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu P^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot 2\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{x}\|P^{n}(\cdot|x)-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\,.

The Rényi-infinity bound immediately follows from
\log(1+x)\leq x
.
∎

3.2
LSI to uniform ergodicity without overhead

We now need to control the
\mathsf{TV}
-distance
uniformly
over any initial point
x\in\Omega
. That is, one should find the iteration number
n
of the Markov chain such that
\|\delta_{x}P^{n}-\pi\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\lesssim\varepsilon
for almost every
x\in\Omega
, where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x
. One expects that it is impossible in general to bound this quantity for arbitrary Markov chains and stationary measures, but we can get around this in our current setting, which only considers probability measures with compact support.

Mixing rates of many convex bodies samplers have been studied when
\pi
satisfies a Cheeger isoperimetric inequality (which is equivalent to a Poincaré inequality for log-concave distributions). For comparison, standard choices of Markov kernel in the unconstrained setting (such as the Langevin or underdamped Langevin dynamics) relate Poincaré inequalities for
\pi
to the convergence of the sampler in
\chi^{2}
, and have theoretical guarantees typically given by

\chi^{2}(\delta_{x}P^{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{n}{C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)}\bigr)\,\chi^{2}(\delta_{x}P^{1}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\,.

As
2\,\mathsf{TV}^{2}\leq\chi^{2}
, one can achieve
\varepsilon
-
\mathsf{TV}
in
C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\log(\chi^{2}(\delta_{x}P^{1}\mathbin{\|}\pi)/\varepsilon^{2})\lesssim C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)\log(\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\delta_{x}P^{1})}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\|_{L_{\infty}}/\varepsilon)
iterations. However, the initial
\chi^{2}
is typically
\exp(\Omega(d))
, so under (
PI
) this approach ends up incurring an additional overhead of
\Omega(d)
to the mixing rate.

While it would already be impressive to boost a mixing rate from the weakest metric (
\mathsf{TV}
) to the strongest metric (
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
) under (
PI
) at the cost of additional
\poly(d)
, one can achieve this with only additional
\operatorname{polylog}(d)
factors under (
LSI
). Just as a Poincaré inequality for
\pi
is normally sufficient to imply the exponential convergence of a corresponding Markov process to
\pi
in
\chi^{2}
, the log-Sobolev inequality is equivalent to exponential convergence of many process in entropy (or in
\mathsf{KL}
). Hence, under (
LSI
), theoretical guarantees of Markov chains are typically of the form

\mathsf{KL}(\delta_{x}P^{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\lesssim\exp\bigl(-\frac{n}{C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)}\bigr)\,\mathsf{KL}(\delta_{x}P^{1}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\,.

Since we know that
2\,\mathsf{TV}^{2}\leq\mathsf{KL}
(CKP inequality),
\varepsilon
-
\mathsf{TV}
can be achieved after
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\log(\mathsf{KL}(\delta_{x}P^{1}\mathbin{\|}\pi)/\varepsilon)\lesssim C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\|\frac{\mathrm{d}(\delta_{x}P^{1})}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\|_{L_{\infty}})
iterations. As noted earlier, this initial distance is at worst
\exp(d^{\mathcal{O}(1)})
, which only results in additional
\operatorname{polylog}(d)
factors after evaluating the double logarithm. Therefore, one can boost from
\mathsf{TV}
to
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
with only logarithmic overhead through (
LSI
).

\mu_{0}^{X}
and target
\pi^{X}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}
.
Then, for any
q\geq 1
,

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{k}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})}{(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{2k/q}}\,.

Proof.

For small
\epsilon>0
, as
\mu_{\epsilon}=(\mu_{0}^{X})_{\epsilon}=\mu_{0}^{X}*\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon I_{d})
and
\pi_{\epsilon}=\pi^{X}*\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon I_{d})
are
C^{\infty}
-smooth, we can invoke the decay result with step size
h-\epsilon
in Proposition
19
.
Thus, for contraction constants
C_{\epsilon}=(1+\frac{h-\epsilon}{\sigma^{2}+\epsilon})^{-2/q}
(since
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\bigl(\nu*\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon I_{d})\bigr)\leq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)+\epsilon
in general), it follows that

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{h-\epsilon}^{\leftarrow}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{\epsilon})\leq C_{\epsilon}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{\epsilon}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{\epsilon})\leq C_{\epsilon}\cdot\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{0})\,,

where we used the data-processing inequality (Lemma
8
) for the last inequality. By the lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{R}_{q}
as noted earlier, sending
\epsilon\to 0
leads to

\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{1}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})=\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{h}^{\leftarrow}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{0})\leq C\cdot\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}\mathbin{\|}\pi_{0})=C\cdot\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{0}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\,.

Repeating this argument
k
times completes the proof.
∎

4.1.2
Per-step guarantees

We can find an
effective domain
under
\pi^{Y}
, from which
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
escapes with only negligible probability. More precisely, we denote the
\delta
-blowup of
\mathcal{K}
by
\mathcal{K}_{\delta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,\mathcal{K})\leq\delta\}
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
R=(1+h\sigma^{-2})\,\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
with
\delta=t/d
and
h=\sigma^{2}c^{2}(d^{-1}\wedge(d^{2}\sigma^{2}-c^{2})^{-1})
, where
c
is any constant smaller than
d^{2}\sigma^{2}
and
t\geq 2c(c+1)
. Then,

\pi^{Y}(R^{c})\leq\exp\bigl(c^{2}-\frac{t^{2}}{8c^{2}}\bigr)\,.

Proof.

Using the density formula for
\pi^{Y}
in (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\cdot\pi^{Y}(R^{c})

\displaystyle=\int_{[(1+h\sigma^{-2})\,\mathcal{K}_{\delta}]^{c}}\frac{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})\|x-(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{-1}y\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\,\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2(h+\sigma^{2})}\|y\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{=}\frac{(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2}\|x-z\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2(h+\sigma^{2})}(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{2}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2}\|x-z\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\underset{(ii)}{\leq}\frac{(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{H}(z)}\exp\bigl(-\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2}\|x-z\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{d(z,\mathcal{K})}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})y^{2}}{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z\,,

where
(i)
follows from the change of variables
z=(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{-1}y
, and in
(ii)

\mathcal{H}(z)
denotes the supporting half-space at
\mathsf{proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(z)
containing
\mathcal{K}
for given
z\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
, given as

\mathcal{H}(z)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle\mathsf{proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(z)-z,x-\mathsf{proj}_{\mathcal{K}}(z)\rangle\geq 0\}\,,

when
z\not\in\mathcal{K}
.

We define the one dimensional Gaussian integral

\mathsf{T}(s)=\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})^{-1}\bigr)}(z\geq s)=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\int_{s}^{\infty}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})y^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y\,.

By the co-area formula and integration by parts, for
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{K}_{\delta}^{c}}\int_{d(z,\mathcal{K})}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})y^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\mathsf{T}(s)\int_{\partial\mathcal{K}_{s}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\underbrace{\mathsf{T}(s)\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\partial\mathcal{K}_{r}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}r}_{\eqqcolon\mathsf{I}}\Bigr]_{s=\delta}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})s^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\partial\mathcal{K}_{r}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\Bigr)\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}s\,.

The double integral above can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\partial\mathcal{K}_{c}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathrm{d}z)\,\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{K}_{s}\backslash\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}\int_{(1+s)\,\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=(1+s)^{d}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(1+s)^{2}(1+h\sigma^{-2})}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq(1+s)^{d}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

where we used
\mathcal{K}_{s}\subset(1+s)\mathcal{K}
in
(i)
, which follows from
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathcal{K}
. Hence, the double integral is bounded by
(1+s)^{d}\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})
.
Recall a standard tail bound for a Gaussian distribution:

\mathsf{T}(s)\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(s(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})^{1/2}-1\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\,.

Combining these two bounds, it holds that
\mathsf{I}
vanishes at
s=\infty
.

Upper bounding
\mathsf{I}
by
0
, we have just derived that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\cdot\pi^{Y}(R^{c})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}(1+s)^{d}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})s^{2}}{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s\cdot\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z\,.

Dividing both sides by the factor
\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x
, we obtain the following bound,

\displaystyle\pi^{Y}(R^{c})

\displaystyle\leq(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}(1+s)^{d}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})s^{2}}{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\exp(sd)\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1}}{2\pi}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})s^{2}}{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}\exp\bigl(\frac{h^{\prime}d^{2}}{2}\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}-d\sqrt{h^{\prime}}-1\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\,,

where in
(i)
we again use the tail bound for a Gaussian distribution.
Above, we introduced a new variable
h^{\prime}:=(\sigma^{-2}+h^{-1})^{-1}=\frac{h}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}
. Taking
\delta=t/d
and
h^{\prime}=c^{2}/d^{2}
subject to
t\geq 2c(c+1)
, we can make

\exp\bigl(\frac{h^{\prime}d^{2}}{2}\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{h^{\prime}}}-d\sqrt{h^{\prime}}-1\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\leq\exp\bigl(\frac{c^{2}}{2}-\frac{t^{2}}{8c^{2}}\bigr)\,.

Since
h\leq\sigma^{2}c^{2}/d
, we also have
(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}\leq\exp(c^{2}/2)
, and the claim follows.
∎

We can now provide the per-step complexity of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
under a warm start.

Lemma 23
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mu
an initial distribution
M
-warm with respect to
\pi^{X}
. For any given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta\in(0,1)
, set
Z=\frac{9nM}{\eta}(\geq 9)
,
h=\sigma^{2}\frac{\log\log Z}{\log Z}(d^{-1}\wedge(d^{2}\sigma^{2}-\frac{\log\log Z}{2\log Z})^{-1})
and
N=Z(\log Z)^{4}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{nM}{\eta})
. Then, the failure probability of one iteration is at most
\eta/n
. Moreover, the expected number of membership queries needed per iteration is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(M(\log\frac{nM}{\eta})^{4}\bigr)
.

Proof.

For
\mu_{h}:=\mu*\mathcal{N}(0,hI_{d})
, the failure probability is
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]
. Since
\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\pi^{X}\leq M
implies
\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}/\mathrm{d}(\pi^{X})_{h}=\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}/\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}\leq M
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq M\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]
.

We now bound the expectation. For the effective domain
R=(1+h\sigma^{-2})\mathcal{K}_{\delta}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\underbrace{(1-\ell)^{N}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}}_{\eqqcolon\mathsf{A}}

\displaystyle=\int_{R^{c}}\mathsf{A}+\int_{R\cap[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3nM/\eta)]}\mathsf{A}+\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3nM/\eta)]}\mathsf{A}

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}\pi^{Y}(R^{c})+\int_{[\ell\geq N^{-1}\log(3nM/\eta)]}\exp(-\ell N)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{R\cap[\ell<N^{-1}\log(3nM/\eta)]}\frac{(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{-d/2}\ell(y)}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2(h+\sigma^{2})}\|y\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\underset{(ii)}{\leq}e^{c_{1}^{2}-\frac{t^{2}}{8c_{1}^{2}}}+\frac{\eta}{3nM}+\frac{\log(3nM/\eta)}{N}\,\frac{\int_{R}(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{-d/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2(h+\sigma^{2})}\|y\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\underset{(iii)}{\leq}e^{c_{1}^{2}}\exp(-\frac{t^{2}}{8c_{1}^{2}})+\frac{\eta}{3nM}+\frac{\log(3nM/\eta)}{N}\cdot(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{d/2}(1+\delta)^{d}

\displaystyle\underset{(iv)}{\leq}e^{c_{1}^{2}}\exp(-\frac{t^{2}}{8c_{1}^{2}})+\frac{\eta}{3nM}+\frac{\exp(t+c^{2})}{N}\,\log\frac{3nM}{\eta}\,,

where in
(i)
we bounded the
(1-\ell)^{N}\leq 1
in the first term,
(1-\ell)^{N}\leq\exp(-\ell N)
in the second term, and again
(1-\ell)^{N}\leq 1
in the third term, and used the density formula (
4.2
) of
\pi^{Y}
in third term. In
(ii)
, the first bound follows from Lemma
22
, while the second and third uses the condition on
\ell
over each domain.
(iii)
follows from the change of variables. Lastly,
(iv)
follows from the setup
\delta=t/d
and
h=\sigma^{2}c^{2}(d^{-1}\wedge(d^{2}\sigma^{2}-c^{2})^{-1})\leq\sigma^{2}c^{2}d^{-1}
in Lemma
22
.

With
c^{2}=\frac{\log\log Z}{2\log Z}
,
t=\sqrt{8}\log\log Z
, and
N=Z(\log Z)^{4}
, the last line is bounded by
\frac{\eta}{nM}
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{h}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq M\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[(1-\ell)^{N}]\leq\frac{\eta}{n}\,.

We now bound the expected number of trials per iteration. Let
S
be the minimum of the threshold
N
and the number of trials until the first success. Then the expected number of trials per step is bounded by
M\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{Y}}[S]
due to
\mathrm{d}\mu_{h}/\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}\leq M
. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\ell}\wedge N\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R}\frac{1}{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{Y}+N\pi^{Y}(R^{c})=\frac{\int_{R}(1+h\sigma^{-2})^{-d/2}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2(h+\sigma^{2})}\|y\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}+N\pi^{Y}(R^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\exp(t+c_{1}^{2})+N\exp(-\Omega(t^{2}))\leq e(\log Z)^{3}+3(\log Z)^{4}=\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\bigl(\log\frac{nM}{\eta}\bigr)^{4}\Bigr)\,.

Therefore, the expected number of trials per step is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(M(\log\frac{nM}{\eta})^{4}\bigr)
, and the claim follows since each trial uses one query to the membership oracle of
\mathcal{K}
.
∎

4.1.3
Query complexity under a warm start

We now put together the mixing and per-step guarantees established above.

Proposition 24
.

For any
\eta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
q\geq 1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
defined below and convex body
\mathcal{K}
given by a well-defined membership oracle, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
(Algorithm
2
) with
h=\sigma^{2}(\frac{1}{d}\wedge\frac{1}{d^{2}\sigma^{2}-1})(\log\frac{9nM}{\eta})^{-1}
,
N=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{nM}{\eta})
, and initial distribution
\mu_{0}^{X}
which is
M
-warm with respect to
\pi^{X}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}
achieves
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu^{X}_{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\varepsilon
after
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(q(d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2})\log\frac{M}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)
iterations, where
\mu_{n}^{X}
is the law of the
n
-the iterate.
With probability
1-\eta
, the algorithm iterates
n
times without failure, using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(qM(d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2})(\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})^{5}\bigr)
expected number of membership queries in total. In particular, the query complexity is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(qMd^{2}\sigma^{2}(\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})^{5})
when
d^{-1}\lesssim\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
21
, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
should iterate

\mathcal{O}\bigl(q\sigma^{2}h^{-1}\log\frac{\log M}{\varepsilon}\bigr)
times to achieve
\varepsilon
-distance in
\mathcal{R}_{q}
. To ensure that the query complexity is bounded, we choose

h=\sigma^{2}\frac{\log\log Z}{\log Z}\bigl(\frac{1}{d}\wedge\frac{1}{d^{2}\sigma^{2}-1}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad N=\frac{9nM}{\eta}\bigl(\log\frac{9nM}{\eta}\bigr)^{4}\,.

By Lemma
23
, we need the following total number of membership queries in expectation:

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(qM(d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2})\bigl(\log\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)^{5}\Bigr)\,.

Hence, if
d^{-1}\lesssim\sigma^{2}
, then the query complexity is simply
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(qMd^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{5}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})
.
∎

4.2
Rényi-infinity guarantees for truncated Gaussians under warm start

To use the boosting technique, we need the uniform ergodicity of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
, bounding
\|\delta_{x}P^{n}-\pi^{X}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}
uniformly over
\mathcal{K}
, where
P
is the Markov kernel of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
for a truncated Gaussian. To this end, for any
x\in\mathcal{K}
, we bound
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\delta_{x}P^{1}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})

uniformly
by
\exp(\text{poly}(D,d))
, so that
\log\log\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\delta_{x}P^{1}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})
does not add more than a polylogarithmic factors to the complexity. In other words, we establish a Gaussian analogue of Lemma
15
.

Lemma 25
.

For any given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
for a truncated Gaussian
\pi^{X}
with variance
h
and any feasible start
x_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
achieves
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu_{n}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\varepsilon
for
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(h^{-1}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{d+h^{-1}D^{2}}{\varepsilon})
iterations.

Proof.

We bound the warmness of
\mu_{1}^{X}
towards
\pi^{X}
when
\mu_{0}^{X}=\delta_{x_{0}}
.
One can readily check that

\mu_{1}^{X}(x)=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(x)\cdot\int\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|x-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\|y-x_{0}\|^{2}\bigr)}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|z-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\,\mathrm{d}y\,,

and we should compare this with

\pi^{X}(x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\cdot\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{K}}(x)}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\,.

For
D=\text{diam}(\mathcal{K})
,

\displaystyle\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|z-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{z^{\mathsf{T}}y}{h}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\bigl(-\frac{D^{2}}{2h}\bigr)\cdot\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{z^{\mathsf{T}}y}{h}\bigr)\,.

As
|z^{\mathsf{T}}y|\leq\frac{1}{2}(2\|z\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2})
due to Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(-\frac{D^{2}}{2h}\bigr)\cdot\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{z^{\mathsf{T}}y}{h}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(-\frac{D^{2}}{2h}\bigr)\cdot\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}-\frac{\|z\|^{2}}{h}-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{4h}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(-\frac{3D^{2}}{2h}\bigr)\cdot\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{4h}\bigr)\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|x-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\|y-x_{0}\|^{2}\bigr)}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|z-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{3D^{2}}{2h}\bigr)}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\int\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|x-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\|y-x_{0}\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{4h}\bigr)}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\,\mathrm{d}y}_{\eqqcolon(\#)}\,.

The numerator of the integrand can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|x-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\|y-x_{0}\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{2h(1+h\sigma^{-2})}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{4h}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{4h}\|y-2(x_{0}+x)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2h}\bigl(\|x_{0}\|^{2}+\|x\|^{2}+\frac{h}{\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}-2\|x_{0}+x\|^{2}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(\frac{D^{2}}{h}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{4h}\|y-2(x_{0}+x)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,.

Putting this bound back to the integral above,

\displaystyle(\#)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{5D^{2}}{2h}\bigr)}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\frac{\int\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{4h}\|y-2(x_{0}+x)\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}y}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\cdot\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=2^{d/2}\exp\Bigl(\frac{5D^{2}}{h}\Bigr)\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\leq 2^{d/2}\exp\Bigl(\frac{5D^{2}}{h}\Bigr)\,.

Therefore, the ratio can be bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}\int\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|x-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2h}\|y-x_{0}\|^{2}\bigr)}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1+h\sigma^{-2}}{2h}\bigl\|z-\frac{1}{1+h\sigma^{-2}}y\bigr\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq 2^{d/2}\exp\Bigl(\frac{5D^{2}}{h}\Bigr)\,,

so
M=\operatorname{ess\,sup}\frac{\mu_{1}^{X}}{\pi^{X}}\leq 2^{d/2}\exp(5h^{-1}D^{2})
, and
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{1}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\lesssim\frac{q}{q-1}\log M\leq\frac{q}{q-1}\bigl(d+h^{-1}D^{2}\bigr)
. By Lemma
21
, one can achieve
\mathcal{R}_{q}(\mu_{n}^{X}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\varepsilon
for
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(qh^{-1}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{d+h^{-1}D^{2}}{\varepsilon})
.

Using
2\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{TV}}^{2}\leq\mathsf{KL}=\lim_{q\downarrow 1}\mathcal{R}_{q}
and
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi^{X})\leq\sigma^{2}
, one can achieve that after
n\gtrsim h^{-1}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{M(d+h^{-1}D^{2})}{\varepsilon}
iterations,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\|\mu^{X}_{n}-\pi^{X}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{M}\,.

By Theorem
14
with
\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\pi^{X}}-1\bigr\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq M
, we have
\bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{X}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\pi^{X}}-1\bigr\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon
and
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu^{X}_{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon
.
∎

Using the uniform ergodicity above, we can now obtain a guarantee in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
for a truncated Gaussian over
\mathcal{K}
, boosting the metric in Proposition
24
.

Theorem 26
.

For any
\eta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
defined below, and convex body
\mathcal{K}
given by a well-defined membership oracle, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
(Algorithm
2
) with
h=\sigma^{2}\bigl(\frac{1}{d}\wedge\frac{1}{d^{2}\sigma^{2}-1}\bigr)\bigl(\log\frac{9nM}{\eta}\bigr)^{-1}
,

, and initial distribution
\mu_{0}^{X}
that is
M
-warm with respect to
\pi^{X}
the truncated Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}
achieves
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\mu^{X}_{n}\mathbin{\|}\pi^{X})\leq\varepsilon
after
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl((d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2})\bigl(\log\frac{MD}{\eta\varepsilon}\bigr)^{2}\bigr)
iterations, where
\mu_{n}^{X}
is the law of the
n
-the iterate.
With probability

, the algorithm iterates

times without failure, using
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(M(d\vee d^{2}\sigma^{2})(\log\nicefrac{{D}}{{\eta\varepsilon}})^{6}\bigr)
expected number of membership queries in total. In particular, the query complexity is

when

.

Proof.

By Lemma
25
, the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
should iterate

times to output a sample whose law is

in

, we can conclude that throughout

Remark 27
.

It can be checked that, conditioning on the event that the algorithm has not failed, the distribution of the iterates remains the correct distribution

5
Successive proximal scheme: Rényi infinity guarantee for uniform sampling

We put together the ingredients prepared in previous sections, namely the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
for uniform distributions (Theorem
18
) and truncated Gaussian (Theorem
26
), along with the annealing scheme introduced in
[
CV18
]
, and obtain
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
(Algorithm
3
).

In §
5.1
, we outline
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
, together with our main result showing that it can sample a point approximately uniformly from a well-rounded convex body with query complexity
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\operatorname{polylog}(1/\varepsilon))
. In §
5.2
, we then provide a proof for the main claim.

5.1
Rényi infinity guarantee with cubic complexity

As mentioned earlier in §
1
, using the rounding algorithm in
[
JLLV21
]
, we can assume that a given convex body
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, presented by a well-defined membership oracle, is
well-rounded
. This means that
\mathcal{K}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{K}}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{2}d
with a known constant
C>0
, where
X\sim\mathcal{K}
indicates that
X
is drawn from the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
.
We state the main result of this paper below.

Theorem 28
.

Assume that a well-rounded convex body
\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{2}d
and
0\in\mathcal{K}
is presented by a well-defined membership oracle.
Let
\pi
be the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
.
For given
\eta,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
is a randomized algorithm, which succeeds with probability
1-\eta
in outputting a sample
X\sim\nu
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon
. Conditioned on its success,
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
uses
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(C^{2}d^{3}\log^{8}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon})
membership queries in expectation.

Preliminaries.

We first collect a series of observations that simplify our setup and arguments. For
\bar{X}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}}[X]
, by Jensen’s inequality,

\|\bar{X}\|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}}[\|X\|]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}}[\|X\|^{2}]}\leq C\sqrt{d}\,.

Also, for the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\mathcal{K}}
of the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{K}
,

\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{K}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}}[\|X-\bar{X}\|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{K}}[\|X\|^{2}]\leq C^{2}d\,.

Lastly, it is known from
[
LV07
, Theorem 5.17]
that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\|X-\bar{X}\|\geq t\cdot\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{K}})\bigr)^{1/2}\bigr)\leq\exp(-t+1)
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\|X-\bar{X}\|\geq t\cdot C\sqrt{d}\bigr)\leq\exp(-t+1)\,.

Therefore, we can actually work with a ‘truncated’ convex body instead of the full convex body
\mathcal{K}
:

Proposition 29
.

There exists a constant
L=C\log\frac{3e}{\varepsilon}
such that the volume of
\bar{\mathcal{K}}:=\mathcal{K}\cap B_{L\sqrt{d}}(0)
is at least
(1-\varepsilon/3)\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})
.

Sketch of
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
.

Let
\mu_{i}
be a truncated Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2}I_{d})|_{\mathcal{K}}
, and
\bar{\mu}_{i}
be an approximate measure close to
\mu_{i}
produced by the algorithm. Then,
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
consists of four phases:

•

Phase I
(\sigma^{2}=1/d
)

–

Initial distribution: Uniform measure over
B_{1}(0)
denoted by

\mu_{0}=\bar{\mu}_{0}
.

–

Target distribution:
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}=\mu_{1}
.

•

Phase II (
1/d\leq\sigma^{2}\leq 1
)

–

Run
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with initial dist.
\bar{\mu}_{i}
(not
\mu_{i}
) and
target dist.
\mu_{i+1}
.

–

Update
\sigma_{i+1}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{d}\bigr)
.

•

Phase III (
1\leq\sigma^{2}\leq L^{2}d
)

–

Run
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with initial dist.
\bar{\mu}_{i}
(not
\mu_{i}
) and
target dist.
\mu_{i+1}
.

–

Update
\sigma_{i+1}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\bigl(1+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{L^{2}d}\bigr)
.

•

Phase IV (
\sigma^{2}=L^{2}d
)

–

Run
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with initial distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}

and target distribution
\pi_{\mathcal{\bar{K}}}
, the uniform distribution over

\bar{\mathcal{K}}
.

Remark 30
.

Phases I-III can be viewed as “preprocessing steps” whose purpose is to generate a warm start for Phase IV. We also note that, while
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\varepsilon
, we do not have the (slightly stronger) property that, for
\varepsilon<1
,

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\pi}\Big|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\Bigr|\lesssim\varepsilon\,.

This is because the truncation of
\pi=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
to
\bar{\pi}=\pi_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
causes there to be
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{K}\backslash\bar{\mathcal{K}}
where
\bar{\pi}(\mathcal{A})=0<\pi(\mathcal{A})
. Since
\nu\ll\bar{\pi}
, we cannot establish a lower bound on
\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1
better than
0
. On the other hand, Corollary
31
shows that we can bound

\Bigl\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\Bigr\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}^{p}\leq\varepsilon\,.

Corollary 31
.

Under the same assumptions as Theorem
28
,
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
succeeds with probability
1-\eta
in outputting a sample
X\sim\nu
such that
\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}^{p}\leq\varepsilon
, so long as
\varepsilon<1
,
p\geq 1
.
Conditioned on its success,
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
uses
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(C^{2}d^{3}\log^{8}\frac{1}{\eta\varepsilon})
membership queries in expectation.

Proof.

As an intermediate step in the proof of Theorem
28
, we obtain that
\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\bar{\pi}}\bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\pi}}-1\bigr|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
, so that, replacing
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
with
\mathcal{K}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Bigl[\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}-1\Bigr|^{p}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K}\backslash\bar{\mathcal{K}})}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}+\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}\,\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\pi}}\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}{\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})}-1\Bigr|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\bigl(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{2\varepsilon}{3(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{3})}\Bigr)^{p}\leq\varepsilon\,,

so long as
\varepsilon<1
and
p\geq 1
.
∎

5.2
Proof details

The following subsections state a series of lemmas, each one of which proves a guarantee on each phase of the algorithm.

Failure probability.

For a target failure probability
\eta
of the entire algorithm, we can achieve this by setting the failure probability at each phase to be on the order of

\hat{\eta}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(\frac{\eta}{C^{2}d\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\Bigr)\,,

(5.1)

since the total number of inner phases is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(C^{2}d\log^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
.
This will be sufficient for the failure probability to be at most
\eta
, where we apply a union bound over all the phases. Since the dependence on
\hat{\eta}
of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
is polylogarithmic, this will not impact the resulting oracle complexity bounds by more than polylogarithmic factors.

Structure of lemmas.

We provide a lemma for each outer phase, where each lemma establishes three facts: (i) the number of updates (to
\sigma
), (ii) a quantitative guarantee on the warmness on each update, and (iii) the final query-complexity bound given by Theorem
26
.

Briefly, this theorem says that, starting from an
M
-warm distribution towards a truncated Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I)|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
, the oracle complexity to achieve
\varepsilon
error in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
, with success probability
1-\hat{\eta}
, is bounded by a quantity of asymptotic order

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(Md^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\varepsilon\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,.

Since
\sigma^{2}>d^{-1}
at all times, it suffices to choose
h^{-1}=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{2}\log\frac{M\log L}{\varepsilon\hat{\eta}})
and the number of
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
iterations to be
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{2}\frac{L}{\varepsilon\hat{\eta}})
.

Except for the last phase, we take
\varepsilon=\log 2
, in which case the query complexity is simply

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(Md^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,,

(5.2)

with
h^{-1}=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{2}\log\frac{M\log L}{\hat{\eta}})
and number of
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
iterations being
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{2}\frac{ML}{\hat{\eta}})
. Note that
\log 2
error in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
implies that the law of the resultant sample is at least
2
-warm with respect to the target.

5.2.1
Phase I

Lemma 32

(Phase I)
.

With probability at least
1-\hat{\eta}
, initial distribution
\mu_{0}=\mathsf{Unif}(B_{1}(0))
, and target distribution
\mu_{1}=\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d})|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
,
Phase I
outputs a sample with law
\bar{\mu}_{1}
satisfying

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{1}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}_{1}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}}-1\Bigr|\leq 1\,.

Its oracle complexity is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(d^{3/2}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,,

using
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with step size
h^{-1}=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{2}\log\frac{\log L}{\hat{\eta}})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\log^{2}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}})
iterations.

Proof.

# Inner phases:
The number of inner phases is clearly
1
.

Warmness:

The initial measure
\mu_{0}
has warmness given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}(x)}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1/\operatorname{vol}(B_{1}(0))}{\exp(-\frac{d}{2}\|x\|^{2})/\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp(-\frac{d}{2}\|z\|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}z}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2\pi/d)^{d/2}\exp(\frac{d}{2})}{\operatorname{vol}(B_{1})}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{(2\pi/d)^{d/2}}\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{d}{2}\|z\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z}_{\leq 1}

\displaystyle\underset{(i)}{\leq}(2/d)^{d/2}\exp\bigl(\frac{d}{2}\bigr)\Gamma\bigl(\frac{d}{2}+1\bigr)\underset{(ii)}{\leq}\gamma\sqrt{d}\exp\bigl(-\frac{d}{2}\log d+\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{2}\log d-\frac{d}{2}\bigr)\leq\gamma\sqrt{d}\,,

where in
(i)
we used
\operatorname{vol}(B_{1})=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma({\textstyle\frac{d}{2}}+1)
, and
(ii)
follows from Stirling’s approximation that

\Gamma\bigl(\frac{d}{2}+1\bigr)\leq\gamma ed^{\frac{d}{2}+1/2}(2e)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\quad\text{for some universal constant }\gamma>0\,.

Final complexity:
It follows from substituting
M=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
and
\sigma^{2}=d^{-1}
into (
5.2
).
∎

5.2.2
Phase II

Lemma 33

(Phase II)
.

With probability at least
1-\hat{\eta}i_{*}
for
i_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
, initial distribution
\bar{\mu}_{1}
(given by from Lemma
32
), and target distribution
\mu_{i_{*}}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
,
Phase II
outputs a sample with law
\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}}
satisfying

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{i_{*}}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{*}}}-1\Bigr|\leq 1\,,

Its oracle complexity is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,,

using
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
. The step size scheme for each inner phase is presented in the proof.

Proof.

Note that we slightly modify the scheme, which is to take
\sigma_{i+1}^{2}=\min(1,\sigma_{i}^{2}(1+\frac{1}{d}))
.

# Inner phases:

As
(1+d^{-1})^{d}\geq 2
for any
d\geq 1
, it takes at most
d
iterations to double
\sigma
. Thus, since we need to double
\sigma
on the order of
\mathcal{O}(\log d)
many times, the number of inner phases within Phase II is at most
i_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
.

Warmness:
By the construction of the algorithm, the following bound holds for all
x\in\bar{\mathcal{K}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}(x)}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i+1}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}\,\frac{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\frac{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\bigl(1+\frac{1}{d}\bigr)^{d/2}\int_{(1+\frac{1}{d})^{-1/2}\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\sqrt{e}\,.

Furthermore, if at each previous phase, we have

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{i}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu_{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}}-1\Bigr|\leq 1\,,

(5.3)

then this implies warmness of constant order between
\bar{\mu}_{i}
and
\mu_{i+1}
:

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{i+1}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu_{i}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i+1}}\Bigr|\leq 2\sqrt{e}\,.

Final complexity:
For any fixed
i
, the complexity in each inner phase is given by substituting
M\leq 2\sqrt{e}
and
\sigma^{2}_{i}\leq 1
into (
5.2
), to obtain a complexity of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(d^{2}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,.

Here, we run the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with
h_{i}^{-1}=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{2}\log\frac{\log L}{\hat{\eta}})
and
n_{i}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\sigma^{2}\log^{2}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}})
, to get
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
bounded by
\log 2
. Implicitly, this verifies the condition (
5.3
).

Secondly, as
i_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
, the total query complexity is bounded by the product of the worst-case complexity in each inner phase with the total number of inner phases, which is given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,.\qed

5.2.3
Phase III

Lemma 34

(Phase III)
.

With probability at least
1-\hat{\eta}j_{*}
for
j_{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
, initial distribution
\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}}
(given by from Lemma
33
), and target distribution
\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}=\mathcal{N}(0,L^{2}dI_{d})|_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
,
Phase II
outputs a sample with law
\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}+j_{*}}
satisfying

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}-1\Bigr|\leq 1\,,

Its oracle complexity is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(L^{2}d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,,

using the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
. The step size scheme for each inner phase is presented in the proof.

Proof.

In this phase, we will first perform the analysis over each doubling and then aggregate over the doublings.

# Inner phases:
We first partition
[1,L^{2}d]
by a sequence of doubling parts, where the terminal
\sigma^{2}
is at least double of the initial
\sigma^{2}
in each doubling part. Clearly, the number of doubling parts is
\log_{2}(L^{2}d)
.

Let
\sigma^{2}
be an initial variance of a given doubling part. Since we have

\Bigl(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{L^{2}d}\Bigr)^{\frac{L^{2}d}{\sigma^{2}}}\geq 2\quad\text{for all }d\geq 1\text{ and }\sigma\leq L\sqrt{d}\,,

the number of inner phases within the doubling part is at most
L^{2}d/\sigma^{2}\leq L^{2}d
. Therefore, the total number of inner phases during Phase III is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(L^{2}d)
.

Warmness:
Let
j\geq i_{*}
. By
the construction of the algorithm, for all
x\in\bar{\mathcal{K}}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j+1}(x)}=\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}\,\frac{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\frac{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\,.

As
\|x\|^{2}\leq L^{2}d
on
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
, we have

\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)=\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\cdot\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{2(L^{2}d+\sigma_{j}^{2})}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\leq\sqrt{e}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,.

As a result,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j+1}(x)}\leq\frac{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j+1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\frac{\sqrt{e}\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}=\sqrt{e}\,.

Furthermore, if at each previous phase, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{j}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu_{j}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}}-1\Bigr|\leq 1\,,

(5.4)

then this implies warmness of constant order

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{j+1}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu_{j}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j+1}}\Bigr|\leq 2\sqrt{e}\,.

Final complexity:

We first bound the total query complexity of one doubling part. Let
\sigma^{2}
be an initial variance of a given doubling part. For any intermediate variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
within the part, the query complexity of the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with
h^{-1}=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{2}\log^{2}\frac{L}{\eta})
, iteration number
n=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}\log^{2}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}})
, and warmness
M\geq 2\sqrt{e}
can be obtained from (
5.2
) as

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(d^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,.

This achieves
\log 2
-accuracy in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
in each update, which also implicitly verifies (
5.4
).
Since within the doubling part,
\sigma_{j}^{2}\leq 4\sigma^{2}
(where
\sigma^{2}
is the initial variance within the doubling part) and the number of inner phases is at most
L^{2}d/\sigma^{2}
, the total query complexity during Phase III, aggregating over all the doubling parts, is

\log_{2}(L^{2}d)\cdot\frac{L^{2}d}{\sigma^{2}}\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\Bigl(d^{2}(4\sigma^{2})\log^{6}\frac{L}{\hat{\eta}}\Bigr)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(L^{2}d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}}\bigr)\,,

achieving
\log 2
-accuracy in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
for the law of the final sample.
∎

5.2.4
Phase IV

Lemma 35

(Phase IV)
.

With probability at least
1-\hat{\eta}
, initial distribution
\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}+j_{*}}
(given by from Lemma
34
), and target distribution
\bar{\pi}\propto\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}
,
Phase IV
outputs a sample with law
\nu
satisfying

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\bar{\pi}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\pi}}-1\Bigr|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\,,

Its oracle complexity is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(L^{2}d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

using the
\mathsf{Proximal\ sampler}
with
h^{-1}=\widetilde{\Theta}(d^{2}\log\frac{\log(L/\varepsilon)}{\hat{\eta}})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(L^{2}d^{3}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}\varepsilon})
iterations.

Proof.

# Inner phases:
There is only a single update in this phase.

Warmness:

Let
\bar{\pi}
be the uniform distribution over
\bar{\mathcal{K}}
.
First, bounding
\exp(-\frac{1}{2L^{2}d}\|x\|^{2})\leq 1
and, since
\bar{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq B_{L\sqrt{d}}(0)
,

\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2L^{2}d}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\exp(-\frac{L^{2}d}{2L^{2}d})=\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})/\sqrt{e}\,.

Then, we can deduce

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}(x)}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\pi}(x)}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}(x)}{1/\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})}=\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})\cdot\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2L^{2}d}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{K}}}\exp\bigl(-\frac{1}{2L^{2}d}\|x\|^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\sqrt{e}\,.

As before, since we have

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}-1\Bigr|\leq 1\,,

then this implies warmness of constant order

\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\mu_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}+j_{*}}}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\pi}}}}\right\rvert\leq 2\sqrt{e}\,.

Final complexity:
Here, using Theorem
18
(instead of the Gaussian results) with
M=2\sqrt{e}
and
D=2L\sqrt{d}
, we obtain the query complexity of

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(L^{2}d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}\varepsilon}\bigr)\,.\qed

We now put together these four lemmas to prove Theorem
28
.

Proof of Theorem
28
.

Given the start
\mu_{0}
, we first apply Lemma
32
to produce
\bar{\mu}_{1}
. Then apply Lemma
33
to produce
\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}}
, Lemma
34
to produce
\bar{\mu}_{i_{*}+j_{*}}
, and finally Lemma
35
to produce
\nu
. Their complexity is dominated by that of Lemma
35
, which is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(L^{2}d^{3}\log^{6}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}\varepsilon}\bigr)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(C^{2}d^{3}\log^{8}\frac{1}{\hat{\eta}\varepsilon}\bigr)\,,

with the guarantee
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\bar{\pi})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
.

From our choice of
L
, it follows that
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\bar{\pi}\mathbin{\|}\pi)\leq\log(1+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3})
due to

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{\bar{\pi}(x)}{\pi(x)}=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{K})}{\operatorname{vol}(\bar{\mathcal{K}})}\leq 1+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\pi)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{\pi}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\leq\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\nu\mathbin{\|}\bar{\pi})+\mathcal{R}_{\infty}(\bar{\pi}\mathbin{\|}\pi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\log\bigl(1+\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}\bigr)\leq\varepsilon\,.

Secondly, we need to quantity the probability of successfully generating a sample. This is given, by a union bound, as
1-\hat{\eta}(2+i_{*}+j_{*})
. With
\hat{\eta}
chosen according to (
5.1
),
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
has at least
1-\eta
probability of success. Putting all these together concludes the proof.
∎

6
Conclusion

We have presented
\mathsf{Proximal\ Gaussian\ cooling}
, an algorithm which achieves
\varepsilon
-closeness in
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
with
\mathcal{O}(d^{3}\operatorname{polylog}\frac{1}{\varepsilon})
query complexity. Here we note some possible future directions for research.

•

In this work, we showed that it is possible to extend the guarantees for
\mathsf{In\text{-}and\text{-}Out}
for Gaussians of the form
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
. Naturally, one asks whether it is possible to sample from general distributions of the type
e^{-f}|_{\mathcal{K}}
, when
f
has some nice properties? The applications of this problem are innumerable, and the techniques required are far from trivial. It is likely that one would need to devise a different annealing scheme in order to control the initial warmness parameter, or find a new strategy which bypasses the need for annealing entirely.

•

This work assumed the membership oracle model for
\mathcal{K}
, but it is interesting to consider alternative oracle models. For instance, one can use more features of the geometry, by assuming access to a self-concordant barrier or a Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{K}
. Alternatively, one can assume access to a stronger oracle such as a separation oracle, which returns not just a binary response to if
x\in\mathcal{K}
, but when
x\not\in\mathcal{K}
also gives a hyperplane separating
x
and
\mathcal{K}
. It is as yet unknown if such a model can improve the query complexity, even for uniform sampling. It is also possible that a different annealing strategy would be beneficial in this context.
