A CLT for the LSS of large dimensional sample covariance matrices with unbounded dispersions
Zhijun Liulabel=e1, mark] liuzj037@nenu.edu.cn, Jiang Hulabel=e2, mark]huj156@nenu.edu.cn, Zhidong Bailabel=e3, mark]baizd@nenu.edu.cn, Haiyan Songlabel=e4, mark]songhy716@nenu.edu.cn

Abstract
In this paper, we establish the central limit theorem (CLT) for linear spectral statistics (LSS) of large-dimensional sample covariance matrix when the population covariance matrices are not uniformly bounded, which is a nontrivial extension of the Bai-Silverstein theorem (BST) (2004). The latter has strongly stimulated the development of high-dimensional statistics, especially the application of random matrix theory to statistics. However, the assumption of uniform boundedness of the population covariance matrices is found strongly limited to the applications of BST. The aim of this paper is to remove the blockages to the applications of BST. The new CLT, allows the spiked eigenvalues to exist and tend to inﬁnity. It is interesting to note that the roles of either spiked eigenvalues or the bulk eigenvalues or both of the two are dominating in the CLT. Moreover, the results are checked by simulation studies with various population settings. The CLT for LSS is then applied for testing the hypothesis that a covariance matrix 𝚺 \boldsymbol{\Sigma} is equal to an identity matrix. For this, the asymptotic distributions for the corrected likelihood ratio test (LRT) and Nagao’s trace test (NT) under alternative are derived, and we also propose the asymptotic power of LRT and NT under certain alternatives.

Introduction

Consider the general sample covariance matrix
{\bf B}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf T}_{p}{\bf X}_{n}{\bf X}_{n}^{\ast}{\bf T}_{p}^{\ast}
, where
{\bf X}_{n}
is a
p\times n
matrix with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) entries,
{\bf T}_{p}
is a
p\times p
deterministic matrix,
{\bf T}_{p}{\bf X}_{n}
can be considered a random sample from the population with the population covariance matrix
{\bf T}_{p}{\bf T}_{p}^{\ast}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and
∗
represents
the complex conjugate transpose. In this sequel, we will simply write
{\bf B}\equiv{\bf B}_{n}
,
{\bf T}\equiv{\bf T}_{p}
and
{\bf X}\equiv{\bf X}_{n}
when there is no confusion.
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p}
denotes the eigenvalues of
{\bf B}
. For a known kernel function
f
, we call
\sum_{j=1}^{p}f\left({\bf\lambda}_{j}\right)
the linear spectral statistic (LSS) of
{\bf B}
. As most of the classical test statistics in multivariate statistical analysis are associated with the eigenvalues of sample covariance matrices, LSSs are remarkable tools in many statistical problems (see
Anderson 2003
;
Yao et al. 2015
for details). By extensively studying high-dimensional data,
it was found that the performances of the LSSs are significantly different between low dimensions and high-dimensional data.
For example, under the low-dimensional setting, Wilks’ theorem (see
Wilks 1938
) provides the
\chi^{2}
approximation for the likelihood ratio statistics (LRT), which is a kind of LSS. However, when
p
is large compared with the sample size
n
, the LRTs have Gaussian fluctuations (see
Bai et al. 2009
;
Jiang and Yang 2013
).
More generally,
Bai and Silverstein 2004
proved the central limit theorem for the LSSs of high-dimensional
{\bf B}
under very mild conditions using the random matrix theory (RMT). We refer to this result as the Bai-Silverstein theorem (BST) for brevity. Following the development of
Bai and Silverstein 2004
, there are many extensions under different settings.
Pan and Zhou 2008
generalized the BST by removing the constraint on the fourth moment of the underlying random variables.
Zheng 2012
and
Yang and Pan 2015
extended the BST to multivariate
F
matrices and canonical correlation matrices, respectively.
Pan 2014
showed the CLT of the LSS for non-centered sample covariance matrices, and
Zheng et al. 2015
studied the unbiased sample covariance matrix when the population mean is unknown.
Chen and Pan 2015
focused on the ultra-high dimensional case in which the dimension
p
is much larger than the sample size
n
.
Gao et al. 2017
and
Li et al. 2021
studied the CLT for the LSSs of the high-dimensional Spearman correlation and Kendall’s rank correlation matrices, respectively. Without attempting to be comprehensive, we refer readers to other extensions
(
Bai et al. 2007
;
Bai et al. 2015
;
Bai et al. 2019
;
Zheng et al. 2019
;
Banna et al. 2020
;
Najim and Yao 2016
;
Baik et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Jiang and Bai 2021
)
.

Almost all the theories mentioned above have traditionally assumed that the spectral norms of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
are bounded in
n
.
This assumption severely limits their applications in data analysis because in many fields, such as economics and wireless communication networks, the leading eigenvalues may tend to infinity. We use three examples here.

•

Panel data model
(
Baltagi et al. 2017

y_{it}=x_{it}^{\prime}\beta+\mu_{i}+v_{it},~~i=1,\ldots,n,~~t=1,\ldots,T,

where
i
is the index of the cross-sectional units,
t
is the index of the time series observations,
\mu_{i}
represents the time invariant individual effects, and
v_{it}
is the idiosyncratic error term.
{\bf v}_{t}=(v_{1t},\dots,v_{nt})^{\prime}
are i.i.d.
N(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).
Assume that
v_{it}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\gamma_{ij}f_{tj}+\epsilon_{t}
, where
f_{tj}
is the factor
j
in period
t
,
\gamma_{ij}
is the factor loading of the individual
i
for factor
j
,
\epsilon_{t}
is the error term with i.i.d.
N(0,\sigma^{2}
), and

is the known number of factors.
The sphericity test in the fixed effect panel data model is

H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}{\bf I}_{n}~~\mbox{v.s.}~~H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\left({\bf I}_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\bm{\gamma}_{j}\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\prime}\right),

where
{\bf I}_{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional identity matrix.
{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}=(\gamma_{1j},\dots,\gamma_{nj})^{\prime}
is the vector of factor loading, and
\sigma_{j}^{2}
is the variance of factor

. Many efforts have been made to analyze the asymptotic power of sphericity tests in high-dimensional setups, where the number of cross-sectional units
n
in a panel is large, but the number of time series observations

could also be large. When

jointly tends to infinity with

, the norm of the perturbation term in the alternative hypothesis is greater than the threshold or even goes to infinity. In this case, the existing methods that assume

are bounded are not applicable.

•

Signal detection
(
Johnstone and Nadler 2017
): Consider a single signal model

{\bf x}=\chi_{s}^{1/2}u{\bf h}+\sigma\bm{v},

where
{\bf h}
is an unknown
p

is a random variable distributed as

,
\chi_{s}
is the signal strength,
\sigma
is the noise level, and
\bm{v}
is
a random noise vector that is independent of

It is easy to check that the covariance matrix of
{\bf x}
is
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}=\sigma^{2}\bm{\Sigma}_{v}+\chi_{s}{\bf h}{\bf h}^{\prime}
. When the noise level is low,
while the signal strength is large and sometimes tends to infinity, it is illogical to assume the boundedness of
\bm{\Sigma}_{x}
.

•

m
-factor structure
(
Li et al. 2020
): Consider the
m
-factor model

\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\bm{F}_{t}+\bm{E}_{t}

where the factors
\bm{F}_{t}\sim N\left(0,\mathbf{I}_{m}\right)
are independent of the idiosyncratic error terms
\bm{E}_{t}\sim

N\left(0,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{p}\right).
The loading matrix
\mathbf{A}_{p\times m}
is deterministic and of full rank such that
\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}
has eigenvalues

. The eigenvalues of the population covariance matrix

of

which follow the generalized spiked model. Because of the complexity of the real data, when
\sigma
is small, the signal-to-noise ratio may be large enough to give rise to the large spectral norm of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p}/\sigma^{2}
. Therefore, in this case, we assume the unbounded spectra of the population covariance matrices would be more realistic.

For these reasons, it is of practical value to obtain the asymptotic properties of the LSS when
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is unbounded. Therefore, in this paper,
we focus on the CLT for the LSS of a general covariance matrix structure

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}&0\\
0&{\bf D}_{2}\end{array}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\ast},

where
\mathbf{V}
is a unitary matrix,
{\bf D}_{1}
is a diagonal matrix consisting of the descending unbounded eigenvalues, and
{\bf D}_{2}
is the diagonal matrix of the bounded eigenvalues. The setting (
1
) is attributed to the famous spiked model
in which a few large eigenvalues of the population covariance matrix are assumed to be well separated from the rest
(
Johnstone 2001
)
. The spiked model has provided the foundations for a rich theory of principal component analysis through the performance of extreme eigenvalues as discussed in
Baik and Silverstein 2006
;
Paul 2007
;
Bai and Yao 2008
;
Nadler 2008
;
Jung and Marron 2009
;
Bai and Yao 2012
;
Onatski et al. 2014
;
Bloemendal et al. 2016
;
Wang and Yao 2017
;
Donoho et al. 2018
;
Perry et al. 2018
;
Johnstone and Paul 2018
;
Yang and Johnstone 2018
;
Yao et al. 2018
;
Dobriban 2020
;
Johnstone and Onatski 2020
;
Cai et al. 2020
;
Jiang and Bai 2021
. Recently,
Li et al. 2020
,
Yin 2021
and
Zhang et al. 2022
investigated the trace of the large sample covariance matrix with the spiked model assumption.

We highlight the main contributions of the present paper. First, we prove a non-trivial extension of the BST to the case in which the population covariance matrices are unbounded in the spectral norm. In particular, we show how the kernel functions and the divergence rate of the population spectral norm affect the new CLT. Second, it is known that Gaussian-like moments, i.e., the first fourth moments, coincide with a standard Gaussian distribution, or the diagonality of the population covariance matrix is necessary for the CLT of the LSSs (e.g.,
Zheng et al. 2015
). However, we prove that these restrictions can be completely removed by renormalization. More importantly, even if the limit of the LSS variance does not exist, the renormalized CLT still holds. Third,
by combining the technical strategy in

Bai and Silverstein 2004
and the analysis of the block decomposition of the sample covariance matrix in
Jiang and Bai 2021
, we prove that the LSSs of the unbounded and bounded parts are asymptotically independent.
The proof in this entire paper is built on the decomposition of the LSS, which is divided into two parts, an unbounded part and a bounded part. It is worth noting that the bounded part of the LSS cannot use the result in
Bai and Silverstein 2004
directly since off-diagonal sample covariance matrix blocks are not 0, which yields a bias between the bounded part of the LSS and the LSS of the bounded sample covariance matrix blocks. In facing this challenge, we make full use of the RMT and prove for the first time that bias can be measured in probability in the literature.

As an application, the established CLT is employed to study the asymptotic behavior of the LRT and Nagao’s trace (NT) test under the hypothesis

H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf I}_{p}~~\mbox{v.s.}~~H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\neq{\bf I}_{p}.

It is known that the LRT and NT are typical examples of LSSs with kernel functions
f(x)=x-\log x-1
and
x^{2}-2x+1
. In this paper, we start from a different perspective by studying the asymptotic distribution of the LRT and NT under the alternative that
{\bf D}_{1}
is composed of
M
spiked eigenvalues tending to infinity with multiplicity,
{\bf D}_{2}={\bf I}_{p-M}
, and then we establish their asymptotic power under the above alternative. Based on previous knowledge (e.g.,
(
Bai et al. 2009
)
), it seems that when the number of spiked eigenvalues is small, the influence caused by the spiked part is small, and the distribution of the LSS is mainly decided by the bulk part; however, that is not the case. After simulation, a surprising result is that when the spiked eigenvalues are very large, the LSS will also be affected by the spiked part even though the number of spikes is small.

\displaystyle\varsigma_{1}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\phi_{n}^{2}\left(\alpha_{k}\right)}{n}\left(f_{1}^{\prime}\left(\phi_{n}\left(\alpha_{k}\right)\right)\right)^{2}s_{k}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}f_{1}\left(z_{1}\right)f_{1}\left(z_{2}\right)\vartheta_{n}^{2}dz_{1}dz_{2}.

where

\displaystyle\Theta_{0,n}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\Theta_{1,n}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}_{n}(z_{1},z_{2})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{z_{1}z_{2}}{n}\underline{m}_{2n0}^{\prime}(z_{1})\underline{m}_{2n0}^{\prime}(z_{2})\mathrm{tr}{{\bf\Gamma}^{*}{\bf P}_{n}(z_{1}){\bf\Gamma}{\bf\Gamma}^{\prime}{\bf P}_{n}(z_{2})^{\prime}\bar{{\bf\Gamma}}}

.

\left(\frac{Y_{1}-\mu_{1}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}}},\dots,\frac{Y_{h}-\mu_{h}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{h}^{2}}}\right)^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}N_{h}\left(0,{\bf\Psi}\right),

with mean function

\displaystyle\mu_{l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2\pi i}\cdot\oint_{\mathcal{C}}f_{l}(z)\frac{c_{nM}\int\underline{m}_{2n0}^{3}(z)t^{2}\left(1+t\underline{m}_{2n0}(z)\right)^{-3}dH_{2n}(t)}{\left(1-c_{nM}\int\frac{\underline{m}_{2n0}^{2}(z)t^{2}}{\left(1+t\underline{m}_{2n0}(z)\right)^{2}}dH_{2n}(t)\right)\left(1-\alpha_{x}c_{nM}\int\frac{\underline{m}_{2n0}^{2}(z)t^{2}}{\left(1+t\underline{m}_{2n0}(z)\right)^{2}}dH_{2n}(t)\right)}dz

(3.6)

\displaystyle a\left(z_{1},z_{2}\right)

Note that

Remark 3.1

4
Application

In this section, we focus on testing the hypothesis that a high-dimensional covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is equal to an identity matrix, that is,

H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf I}_{p}\quad\text{vs}\quad H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}&0\\
0&{\bf I}_{p-M}\end{array}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\ast},

where
{\bf D}_{1}={\textrm{diag}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{K},\dots,\alpha_{K})
.
For this problem, the two most classical test statistics are the likelihood ratio test (LRT) statistic
(
Wilks 1938
)
and
Nagao’s trace (NT)
test statistic
(
Nagao 1973
)
. Specifically, the LR and NT statistics can be formalized as

L=\operatorname{tr}{\bf B}-\log\left|{\bf B}\right|-p~~\mbox{and}~~W=\mathrm{tr}({\bf B}-{\bf I}_{p})^{2},

respectively. Under the null hypothesis, we refer to
Bai et al. 2009
;
Jiang and Yang 2013
;
Ledoit and Wolf 2002
;
Wang and Yao 2013
;
Onatski et al. 2013
for the asymptotic properties of the LR and NT statistics for high-dimensional settings. In this section, we mainly focus on the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
. However, to provide a better comparison, we also present the asymptotic distributions under the null hypothesis in the following theorems:

Theorem 4.1

(CLT for LR)
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
4
with
c_{n}=p/n\rightarrow c\in(0,1)
, we have

•

(Under
H_{0}
,)

\dfrac{L-p\ell_{L}-\mu_{L}}{\sqrt{\varsigma_{L}^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1),

where

\displaystyle\ell_{L}=1-\frac{c_{n}-1}{c_{n}}\log\left(1-c_{n}\right),~~~\mu_{L}=-\frac{\log\left(1-c_{n}\right)}{2}\alpha_{x}+\frac{c_{n}}{2}\beta_{x}

and

\varsigma_{L}^{2}=\frac{\alpha_{x}+1}{2}(-2\log\left(1-c_{n}\right)-2c_{n}).

•

(Under
H_{1}
,)

\dfrac{L-(p-M)\breve{\ell}_{L}-\breve{\mu}_{L}}{\sqrt{\breve{\varsigma}_{L}^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1),

where

\displaystyle\breve{\ell}_{L}=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{c_{nM}-1}{c_{nM}}\log\left(1-c_{nM}\right)

\displaystyle\breve{\mu}_{L}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\log\left(1-c_{nM}\right)}{2}\alpha_{x}+\frac{c_{nM}}{2}\beta_{x}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}\left(\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)-\log\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)-1\right)-M(c_{n}+\log(1-c_{n}))

\displaystyle\breve{\varsigma}_{L}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{x}+1}{2}(-2\log\left(1-c_{nM}\right)-2c_{nM})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}\frac{2\left(\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)-1\right)^{4}}{n\phi_{n}^{2}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)}

\displaystyle\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)=

\displaystyle{\alpha}_{k}+\frac{{\alpha}_{k}(p-M)}{n({\alpha}_{k}-1)}.

Remark 4.1
.

If
c>1
, then
{\bf B}_{n}
is singular for large
n
, which gives rise to the undefined LR statistic
L
. Thus, in Theorem
4.1
, we add an additional restriction
c<1
.

Theorem 4.2

(CLT for NT)
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
4
, we have

•

(Under
H_{0}
,)

\frac{W-p\ell_{W}-\mu_{W}}{\sqrt{\varsigma_{W}^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1),

where

\displaystyle\ell_{W}=c_{n},~~\mu_{W}=\alpha_{x}c_{n}+\beta_{x}c_{n}~~\mbox{and}~~\varsigma_{W}^{2}=(\alpha_{x}+1)(4c_{n}^{3}+2c_{n}^{2})+4\beta_{x}c_{n}^{3}.

•

(Under
H_{1}
,)

\displaystyle\frac{W-(p-M)\breve{\ell}_{W}-\breve{\mu}_{W}}{\sqrt{\breve{\varsigma}_{W}^{2}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1),

where

\displaystyle\breve{\ell}_{W}

\displaystyle=c_{nM},

\displaystyle\breve{\mu}_{W}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{x}c_{nM}+\beta_{x}c_{nM}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}\left(\phi_{n}^{2}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)-2\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)+1\right)-Mc_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\breve{\varsigma}_{W}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{x}+1)(4c_{nM}^{3}+2c_{nM}^{2})+4\beta_{x}c_{nM}^{3}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{k}\frac{8\left(\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)-1\right)^{4}}{n}

\displaystyle\phi_{n}\left({\alpha}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle={\alpha}_{k}+\frac{{\alpha}_{k}(p-M)}{n({\alpha}_{k}-1)}.

Remark 4.2
.

For illustration,
we study the asymptotic properties of the LR and NT statistics under a simplified spiked population model
(
Johnstone 2001
)
, where the population covariance matrix is diagonal, with only one spiked eigenvalue, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{1},1,\dots,1)

(4.7)

Let
z_{a}
be the upper
a\%
quantile of the standard Gaussian distribution
\Phi
and
\phi_{1}=\alpha_{1}+\frac{c\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}-1}
. It is straightforward from Theorem
4.1
that the asymptotic power of the LR statistic under the alternative hypothesis (
4.7
) is

\Phi(\frac{-c+\left(\phi_{1}-\log\phi_{1}-1\right)-z_{a}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{x}+1}{2}(-2\log(1-c)-2c)}}{\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{x}+1}{2}(-2\log(1-c)-2c)+\frac{2}{n}\frac{(\phi_{1}-1)^{4}}{\phi_{1}^{2}}}}).

Analogously, the asymptotic power of the NT statistic under the alternative hypothesis (
4.7
) can be formulized as

\Phi(\dfrac{-2c+(\phi\left(\alpha_{1}\right)-1)^{2}-c^{2}-z_{a}\sqrt{(\alpha_{x}+1)(4c^{3}+2c^{2})+4\beta_{x}c^{3}}}{\sqrt{(\alpha_{x}+1)(4c^{3}+2c^{2})+4\beta_{x}c^{3}+\frac{8}{n}(\phi(\alpha_{1})-1)^{4}}}).

It is easy to find that if
\alpha_{1}\to\infty
, the asymptotic power of the LR and NT statistics tend to 1 of order
\min\{\alpha_{1},\sqrt{n}\}
and
\min\{\alpha_{1}^{2},\sqrt{n}\}
, respectively.

Technical proofs

In this section, we present the proofs of Theorems
3.1
,
3.2
,
4.1
and

6.1
Truncation and renormalization

Let
\hat{x}_{ij}=x_{ij}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\left|x_{ij}\right|<\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right\}}
and
\tilde{x}_{ij}=\frac{\hat{x}_{ij}-\mathbbm{E}\hat{x}_{ij}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}}
, where
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\mathbbm{E}\left|\hat{x}_{ij}-\mathbbm{E}\hat{x}_{ij}\right|^{2}
and
\eta_{n}\rightarrow 0
with a slow rate. Correspondingly, define
\hat{{\bf B}}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf T}\hat{{\bf X}}\hat{{\bf X}}^{\ast}{\bf T}^{\ast}
and
\tilde{{\bf B}}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf T}\tilde{{\bf X}}\tilde{{\bf X}}^{\ast}{\bf T}^{\ast}
, where
\hat{{\bf X}}=(\hat{x}_{ij})
and
\tilde{{\bf X}}=(\tilde{x}_{ij})
.
\hat{G}_{n}
and
\tilde{G}_{n}
denote the analogues of
G_{n}
with the matrix
{\bf B}
replaced by
\hat{{\bf B}}
and
\tilde{{\bf B}}
, respectively.
Next, we demonstrate that the entries of
\mathbf{X}
in the LSSs are equivalent to and can be replaced by the truncated and renormalized entries.

According to the Lindeberg-type condition in Assumption
1
, we obtain that as
\min\{n,p\}\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}({\bf B}\neq\hat{{\bf B}})\leq\sum_{i,j}\mathrm{P}\left(\left|x_{ij}\right|\geq\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right)\rightarrow 0.

It follows from the definition of LSSs that for any
l=1,\ldots,h
,

\displaystyle\left|\int f_{l}(x)d\hat{G}_{n}-\int f_{l}(x)d\tilde{G}_{n}(x)\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|+\sum_{i=M+1}^{p}\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|.

(6.8)

Using the same discussion in
Bai and Silverstein 2004
, we can easily obtain that the second term of (
6.8
) tends to 0 in probability.
For the first term of (
6.8
), from the arguments in Supplement B of
Jiang and Bai 2021
, we know that

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}\right|=o_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rho_{i}}).

(6.9)

Then, for brevity, we denote
\beta_{i}=({\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}}-\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}})/{\rho_{i}}
and
obtain that

\displaystyle f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})=\int_{0}^{\beta_{i}\rho_{i}}f_{l}^{\prime}(t+\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\beta_{i}\rho_{i}f_{l}^{\prime}(\beta_{i}\rho_{i}s+\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})ds=\beta_{i}\rho_{i}f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i}(\beta_{i}s+\frac{\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}}}{\rho_{i}}))}{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})}ds,

which, together with Assumption
4
and (
6.9
), implies

\sqrt{n}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\dfrac{\left|f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\hat{{\bf B}}})-f_{l}(\lambda_{i}^{\tilde{{\bf B}}})\right|}{f_{l}^{\prime}(\rho_{i})\rho_{i}}=o_{p}(1).

Therefore, in the following proofs, we can safely assume that
\left|x_{ij}\right|<\eta_{n}\sqrt{n}
.

6.2
Some primary definitions and lemmas

In this section, we provide some useful results that will be used later in the proofs of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
. For the population covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\bf T}{\bf T}^{\ast}
, consider the corresponding sample covariance matrix
{\bf B}={\bf T}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf T}^{\ast}
, where
{\bf S}_{x}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf X}{\bf X}^{\ast}
.
By singular value decomposition of
{\bf T}
(see (
2.3

{\bf B}={\bf V}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}&{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}&{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right){\bf V}^{\ast}.

Note that

{\bf S}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}&{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{1}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{1}{\bf D}_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}&{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bf U}_{2}^{\ast}{\bf S}_{x}{\bf U}_{2}{\bf D}_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{array}\right)\triangleq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bf S}_{11}&{\bf S}_{12}\\
{\bf S}_{21}&{\bf S}_{22}\end{array}\right).

Note that
{\bf B}
and
{\bf S}
have the same eigenvalues.

Recall that
\underline{{\bf B}}=\frac{1}{n}{\bf X}^{\ast}{\bf T}^{\ast}{\bf T}{\bf X}
(the spectral of which differs from that of
{\bf B}
by
\left|n-p\right|
zeros). Its limiting spectral distribution is
\underline{F}^{c,H}
,
\underline{F}^{c,H}\equiv\left(1-c\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left[0,\infty\right)}+cF^{c,H}
, and its Stieltjes transform is
\underline{m}\left(z\right)
.
Let
\widetilde{{\bf\lambda}}_{j}
be the eigenvalues of
{\bf S}_{22}
so that the LSS of
{\bf S}_{22}
is
\sum_{j=1}^{p-M}f(\widetilde{{\bf\lambda}}_{j})
. Correspondingly, recall that
c_{nM}:=\frac{p-M}{n}
,

Moreover, the power analysis for NT is similar to that for the LR; thus, we omit the detailed proof here.
