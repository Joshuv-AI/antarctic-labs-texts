Decentralized State Estimation In A Dimension-Reduced Linear Regression
Robin Forsling 1 , Fredrik Gustafsson 3 , Zoran Sjanic, and Gustaf Hendeby 2

Abstract
Decentralized state estimation in a communication-constrained sensor network is considered. The exchanged estimates are dimension-reduced to reduce the communication load using a linear mapping to a lower-dimensional space. The mean squared error optimal linear mapping depends on the particular estimation method used. Several dimension-reducing algorithms are proposed, where each algorithm corresponds to a commonly applied decentralized estimation method. All except one of the algorithms are shown to be optimal. For the remaining algorithm, we provide a convergence analysis where it is theoretically shown that this algorithm converges to a stationary point and numerically shown that the convergence rate is fast. A message-encoding solution is proposed that allows for efficient communication when using the proposed dimension reduction techniques. We also derive different properties from the proposed framework and show its superiority in relation to baseline methods. The applicability of the different algorithms is demonstrated using a simple fusion example and a more realistic target tracking scenario.

II
Background

In network-centric problems, multiple agents are cooperating for a common goal, e.g., estimating a common target. The focus is on estimation in decentralized SNs, where an agent consists of sensors and a processing unit. We begin by describing different network aspects. Prior work is reviewed at the end of this section.

II-A

Network-Centric Estimation

Network-centric estimation is a well-studied subject
[
10
]
. By utilizing multiple sensors and the communication of information, it is possible to enhance the quality of estimates. This information is then exchanged for further refinement. A centralized SN is illustrated in Fig.
2(a)
, where sensor measurements are communicated to a central processing unit where estimates are derived. Two major drawbacks of the centralized SN include vulnerability to the failure of critical nodes and a high communication load since all measurements are transmitted to the central node.

This paper considers decentralized SNs. A decentralized SN is illustrated in Fig.

. According to

(i)

There is no single point of failure.

(ii)

Agents communicate on a agent-to-agent basis without a central communication management.

(iii)

There is no global knowledge about the network topology.

In a decentralized SN, measurements are passed to a local processing unit, where local estimates are computed. The local estimates are then exchanged between the processing units to enhance the tracking quality through fusion. A decentralized SN has the following advantages over its centralized counterpart
[
13
,
1
]
:

•

Robustness
. Since there is no single point of failure, a decentralized design is inherently fault-tolerant.

•

Modularity
. The network is decomposed into smaller, self-contained subsystems, which reduces the overall complexity and makes the network scalable.

•

Flexibility
. Agents can connect to and disconnect from the network on-the-fly.

A decentralized SN is an example of distributed processing. However, the terms decentralized and distributed should not be used interchangeably. While distributed sensor networks have multiple interconnected processing units, there is typically a central coordinator or some global processing result is obtained, e.g., globally available estimates
[
14
]
. This work is limited to methods that are able to cope with the constraints imposed by (i)–(iii), thereby excluding many distributed processing techniques. For instance, the distributed Kalman filter algorithms of
[
15
,
16
]
assume that the implemented filters follow predefined schemes, and the diffusion-based strategies of
[
17
,
18
]
are developed under certain assumptions about the network topology.

Prior Work

A crucial aspect of network-centric estimation problems is communication constraints

19

III
Fusion of Dimension-Reduced Estimates

This paper considers a decentralized SN, where only DR estimates are allowed to be exchanged. The task is to reduce dimensionality so that fusion performance is promoted.

Mathematical preliminaries are given at first. Then the problem is formalized. Several methods applicable for the fusion of DR estimates are provided. At the end, we define two baseline methods for communication reduction, with which we will compare the developed methods.

(17)

is constant, minimization of

\displaystyle\underset{\Psi}{\maximize}

\displaystyle\trace\left(\Delta\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Psi S\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\Psi\Delta^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right).

(18)

Using the cyclic property of trace, this problem is equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\underset{\Psi}{\maximize}

\displaystyle\trace\left((\Psi S\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\Psi Q\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right),

(19)

where

. Since

and
\rank(\Psi)=m

\Psi S\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{m}
. This implies that there exists an invertible matrix

. Hence, without loss of generality (w.l.o.g.) it can be assumed that

implies that

, where

. Then the following problem is equivalent to (

\displaystyle\underset{\Phi}{\maximize}

\displaystyle\trace(\Phi A\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})

(20)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm subject\;to}

\displaystyle\Phi\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=I,

where

. The solution to (

[

]

.

Assume

and

. Let

, where

and
\rank(\Psi)=m
. The solution to

\displaystyle\underset{\Psi}{\maximize}

\displaystyle\trace\left((\Psi S\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}Q\Psi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right),

(21)

IV-A
1
Change of Basis

For efficient communication it is important that
\Psi
has orthogonal rows and that

is a diagonal matrix. This has to do with the encoding of
(y_{\Psi},R_{\Psi})
and
\Psi
upon transmission, and is further explained in Sec.

such that these criteria are satisfied. We start with the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Let

and

, where
m\leq n
. If

, then

\displaystyle A^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(ASA^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}A

\displaystyle=B^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(BSB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}B.

(22)

Proof.

Since
\rowspan(A)=\rowspan(B)
there exists an invertible matrix

such that

. Hence

\displaystyle A^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(ASA^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}A

\displaystyle=A^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}T^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}T^{-\mathsf{T}}(ASA^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}T^{-1}TA

\displaystyle=A^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}T^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(TASA^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}T^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}TA

\displaystyle=B^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(BSB^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}B.

∎

Consider

, where

are derived as in Theorem

. The rows of

is a transformation

(23)

For any two solutions

and

to

is not an orthogonal basis for

. Proposition

ensures that if

A

IV-C
1
Convergence Analysis

The AM method in Algorithm
5
alternates between solving two different kinds of optimization problems. Each separate problem is well-posed, and a global minimum is obtained. The solution to the problem in step 1 is given by the generalized eigenvalues associated with the largest generalized eigenvalues of a GEVP. Step 2 involves solving a convex optimization problem
[
53
]
for which any local minimum is also a global minimum. This does, however, not imply that the final iteration of Algorithm
5
is a global minimizer. Below, it is shown that the iterations of Algorithm
5
converge to a stationary point.

Let
J(\omega,\Phi)=\trace(P)
with
P
according to (
10
), i.e.,

\displaystyle J(\omega,\Phi)

\displaystyle=\trace\left(\left(\omega R_{1}^{-1}+(1-\omega)H_{2}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}(\Phi R_{2}\Phi^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\Phi H_{2}\right)^{-1}\right).

(30)

Define

\displaystyle J_{k-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=J(\omega_{k-1},\Phi_{k}),

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle=J(\omega_{k},\Phi_{k}).

(31)

Consider a sequence of
n_{k}
iterations and hence
2n_{k}
subiterations. Each iteration and subiteration have the same feasible set. Since in each subiteration a minimum is obtained it is concluded that

J_{\frac{1}{2}}\geq J_{1}\geq\cdots\geq J_{N-\frac{1}{2}}\geq J_{N},

which is a nonincreasing sequence
\{J_{k}\}
. Denote by
\{(\omega,\Phi)_{k}\}
the sequence of points generating
\{J_{k}\}
. Since
P\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n_{x}}
, there exists a lower bound
J_{\text{low}}>0
such that
J(\omega,\Phi)\geq J_{\text{low}},\forall(\omega,\Phi)
. Hence, the
monotonic convergence theorem

[
54
]
is applicable, which states that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}J_{k}=J(\bar{\omega},\bar{\Phi}),

(32)

where
(\bar{\omega},\bar{\Phi})
is a limit point. In the limit
J_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-J_{k-\frac{1}{2}}\rightarrow 0
and
J_{k+1}-J_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Since
J(\omega,\Phi)
is differentiable w.r.t.
\omega
and

a^{M_{i}}
and
a^{K_{i}}
, denote a vector that contains the components of a vector, e.g.,
a
, corresponding to the missing and available components of
\phi_{i}
, respectively. In particular, the union and intersection of the components of
\phi_{i}^{K_{i}}
and
\phi_{i}^{M_{i}}
correspond to
\phi_{i}
and the empty set, respectively. For arbitrary
i\in\{2,\dots,n_{2}\}
, the missing components
\phi_{i}^{M_{i}}
are computed by solving

\displaystyle A_{i}(\phi_{i}^{M_{i}})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=b_{i},\,\,A_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}^{M_{i}}\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{i-1}^{M_{i}}\end{bmatrix},\,\,b_{i}=-\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}^{K_{i}}(\phi_{i}^{K_{i}})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\\
\vdots\\
\phi_{i-1}^{K_{i}}(\phi_{i}^{K_{i}})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}.

(43)

Let
\Phi^{K}
denote the parts of
\Phi
that are actually transmitted using the proposed message encoding, i.e.,

\Phi^{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}&\phi_{2}^{K_{2}}&\ldots&\phi_{m}^{K_{m}}\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

Care must be taken to ensure that
\mathop{\rm det}(A_{i})\neq 0
, since otherwise the system of equations in (
43
) is not solvable. The condition
\mathop{\rm det}(A_{i})\neq 0
has to be checked by the transmitting agent. This means that it is not possible by default to always skip transmitting the first components of
\phi_{i}
since this could potentially imply that
\mathop{\rm det}(A_{i})=0
. Hence, alongside the transmission of the information contained in
\phi_{i}
, i.e.,
\phi_{i}^{K_{i}}
, the agent must also transmit information about which components are not transmitted, i.e., which elements of
\phi_{i}
are included in
\phi_{i}^{M_{i}}
. This requires the transmission of a few extra parameters. For example, if
m=3
, then
1+2=3
extra parameters must be transmitted that indicate which components of
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are not transmitted. For general
m
, a number of

n_{\text{ex}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(i-1)=\frac{m(m-1)}{2},

(45)

extra parameters must be included in the message. However, as described below, to enumerate the excluded components of the
\phi_{i}
, where
i=1,\dots,m
, only requires a few extra bits, which is negligible compared to the remaining parameters to be exchanged.

In summary, the transmitted message is given by
(y_{\Psi},\Phi^{K},\mathcal{J})
, where
\mathcal{J}
is an
n_{\text{ex}}
-dimensional vector containing the indices of the missing components of
\phi_{i}
, where
i=1,\dots,m
.

VII
Conclusions

Decentralized state estimation under communication constraints has been considered. The problem involved agents sharing information in a decentralized sensor network, where only DR estimates were allowed to be communicated. In particular, we have proposed the
generalized eigenvalue optimization
(GEVO) method as a framework for computing DR estimates optimized for fusion performance. Essentially, the GEVO method computes the matrix
\Psi
which defines the DR estimate to be communicated.

The GEVO framework was based on a generalization of the BSC formulas. The main focus has been on three variants of GEVO corresponding to the following fusion methods: KF, CI, and the LE method. We have shown how the optimal linear mapping is computed for BSC, KF, and LE. For CI, an alternating minimization algorithm has been proposed, which was shown to converge to a stationary point.

Different aspects related to the GEVO method were discussed. For instance, dealing with singularities when deriving
\Psi
and how to assign the number of rows in
\Psi
. A message-coding solution was provided for efficient communication when using DR estimates. We have also compared the GEVO framework, both theoretically and numerically, with two baseline methods. The numerical examples demonstrated the usability of the GEVO framework and its superiority to the baseline methods.
