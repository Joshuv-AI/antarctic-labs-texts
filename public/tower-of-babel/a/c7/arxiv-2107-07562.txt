On universal approximation and error bounds for Fourier Neural Operators
Nikola Kovachki and Samuel Lanthaler and Siddhartha Mishra

Abstract
. Fourier neural operators (FNOs) have recently been proposed as an effective framework for learning operators that map between infinite-dimensional spaces. We prove that FNOs are universal, in the sense that they can approximate any continuous operator to desired accuracy. Moreover, we suggest a mechanism by which FNOs can approximate operators associated with PDEs efficiently. Explicit error bounds are derived to show that the size of the FNO, approximating operators associated with a Darcy type elliptic PDE and with the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations of fluid dynamics, only increases sub (log)-linearly in terms of the reciprocal of the error. Thus, FNOs are shown to efficiently approximate operators arising in a large class of PDEs.



1.
Introduction

Deep neural networks have been extremely successful in diverse fields of science and engineering including image classification, speech recognition, natural language understanding, autonomous systems, game intelligence and protein folding,
[
16
]
and references therein. Moreover, deep neural networks are being increasingly used successfully in scientific computing, particular in simulating physical and engineering systems modeled by partial differential equations (PDEs). Examples include the use of physics informed neural networks
[
29
,
30
,
27
,
28
]
for solving forward and inverse problems for PDEs and supervised learning algorithms for high-dimensional parabolic PDEs
[
11
]
and parametric elliptic
[
14
,
32
]
and hyperbolic
[
22
,
23
]
PDEs, among others.

The success of deep neural networks at a wide variety of learning tasks can be attributed to a confluence of several factors such as the availability of massive labeled data sets, the design of novel architectures and training algorithms as well as the abundance of high-end computing platforms such as GPUs
[
12
]
. Still, it is fair to surmise that this edifice of success partly rests on the foundation of
universal approximation

[
1
,
9
,
13
]
, i.e., the ability of neural networks to approximate any continuous (even measurable) function, mapping a finite-dimensional input space into another finite-dimensional output space, to arbitrary accuracy.

However, many interesting learning tasks entail learning
operators
i.e., mappings between an infinite-dimensional input Banach space and (possibly) an infinite-dimensional output space. A prototypical example in scientific computing is provided by nonlinear operators that map the initial datum into the (time series of) solution of a nonlinear time-dependent PDE such as the Navier-Stokes equations of fluid dynamics. A priori, it is unclear if neural networks can be successfully employed for learning such operators from data, given that their universality only pertains to finite-dimensional functions.

The first successful use of neural networks in the context of such
operator learning
was provided in
[
8
]
, where the authors proposed a novel neural network based learning architecture, which they termed as
operator networks
and proved that these operator networks possess a surprising universal approximation property for infinite-dimensional nonlinear operators. Operator networks are based on two different neural networks, a
branch net
and a
trunk net
, which are trained concurrently to learn from data. More recently, the authors of
[
21
]
have proposed using deep, instead of shallow, neural networks in both the trunk and branch net and have christened the resulting architecture as a
DeepOnet
. In a recent article
[
15
]
, the universal approximation property of DeepOnets was extended, making it completely analogous to universal approximation results for finite-dimensional functions by neural networks. The authors of
[
15
]
were also able to show that DeepOnets can break the curse of dimensionality for a large variety of PDE learning tasks. Hence, in spite of the underlying infinite-dimensional setting, DeepOnets are capable of approximating a large variety of nonlinear operators efficiently. This is further validated by the success of DeepOnets in many interesting examples in scientific computing
[
26
,
6
,
20
]
and references therein.

An alternative operator learning framework is provided by the concept of
neural operators
, first proposed in
[
18
]
. Just as canonical artificial neural networks are a concatenated composition of multiple hidden layers, with each hidden layer composing an affine function with a scalar nonlinear activation function, neural operators also compose multiple hidden layers, with each hidden layer composing an affine
operator
with a local, scalar nonlinear activation operator. The infinite-dimensional setup is reflected in the fact that the affine operator can be significantly more general than in the finite-dimensional case, where it is represented by a weight matrix and bias vector. On the other hand, for neural operators, one can even use
non-local
linear operators, such as those defined in terms of an integral kernel. The evaluation of such integral kernels can be performed either with graph kernel networks
[
18
]
or with multipole expansions
[
17
]
.

More recently, the authors of
[
19
]
have proposed using convolution-based integral kernels within neural operators. Such kernels can be efficiently evaluated in the Fourier space, leading to the resulting neural operators being termed as
Fourier Neural Operators
(FNOs). In
[
19
]
, the authors discuss the advantages, in terms of computational efficiency, of FNOs over the other neural operators mentioned above. Moreover, they present several convincing numerical experiments to demonstrate that FNOs can very efficiently approximate a variety of operators that arise in simulating PDEs.

However, the theoretical basis for neural operators has not yet been properly investigated. In particular, it is unclear if neural operators such as FNOs are
universal
i.e., if they can approximate a large class of nonlinear infinite-dimensional operators. Moreover in this infinite-dimensional setting, universality does not suffice to indicate computational viability or efficiency as the size of the underlying neural networks might grow exponentially with respect to increasing accuracy, see discussion in
[
15
]
on this issue. Hence in addition to universality, it is natural to ask if neural operators can
efficiently
approximate a large class of operators, such as those arising in the simulation of parametric PDEs.

The investigation of these questions is the main rationale for the current paper. We focus our attention here on FNOs as they appear to be the most promising of the neural operator based operator learning frameworks. Our main result in this paper is to show that FNOs are
universal
in possessing the ability to approximate a very large class of continuous nonlinear operators. This result highlights the potential of FNOs for operator learning.

As argued before, a universality result is only a first step and by itself, does not constitute evidence for efficient approximation by FNOs. In fact, we show that in the worst case, the network size might grow exponentially with respect to accuracy, when approximating general operators. Hence, there is a need to derive explicit bounds on the network size in terms of the desired error tolerance. In this context, we consider a concrete computational realization of FNOs, that we term as
pseudospectral FNO
or
\Psi
-FNO (for short). In addition to proving universality for
\Psi
-FNOs, we will suggest a mechanism through which
\Psi
-FNOs can approximate operators arising from PDEs, efficiently. We also derive explicit error bounds for this architecture in approximating PDEs, for two widely used prototypical examples of PDEs i.e, a Darcy type elliptic equation and the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations of fluid dynamics. In particular, we prove that the size of
\Psi
-FNOs in approximating the underlying operators for both these PDEs, under suitable hypotheses, only scales polynomially (log-linearly) in the error. Thus, FNOs can approximate these operators efficiently and these results validate some of the computational findings of
[
19
]
. Together, these results constitute the first theoretical justification for the use of FNOs.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: in section
2
, we introduce FNOs and state the universality result. We also introduce
\Psi
-FNOs in this section. In section
3
, we show that
\Psi
-FNOs can efficiently approximate operators, stemming from the Darcy-type elliptic equation as well as the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations. In section
4
, we compare FNOs with DeepOnets and the results of the article are discussed in section
5
. The mathematical notation, used in this paper, is summarized in Appendix
A
and we present all the technical details and proofs in other appendices.

2.
Approximation by Fourier Neural Operators

In this section, we present Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) and discuss their approximation of a class of nonlinear operators specified below:

2.1.
Setting for Operator Learning.

Setting 2.1
.

We fix a spatial dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and denote by
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We consider the approximation of operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
a\mapsto u:=\mathcal{G}(a)
, where the input
a\in\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
,
d_{a}\in\mathbb{N}
, is a function
a:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
with
d_{a}
components, and the output
u\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
d_{u}\in\mathbb{N}
, is a function
u:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
with
d_{u}
components. Here
\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
and
\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
are Banach spaces (or suitable subsets of Banach spaces). Typical examples of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{U}
include the space of continuous functions
C(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, or Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
of order
s\geq 0
(see Appendix
B
for definitions.).

Concrete examples for operators
\mathcal{G}
, involving solution operators of PDEs, are given in section
3
.

2.2.
Neural Operators

With the above setting
2.1
and as defined in
[
18
]
, a neural operator
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
a\mapsto\mathcal{N}(a)
is a mapping of the form

\mathcal{N}(a)=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}(a),

for a given depth
L\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
,
d_{v}\geq d_{u}
, is a
lifting
operator (acting locally), of the form

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(a)(x)=Ra(x),\quad R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{a}},

and
\mathcal{Q}:\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
is a local
projection
operator, of the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(v)(x)=Qv(x),\quad Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}.

Remark 2.2
.

In practice, it has been found that improved results can be obtained if the simple
linear
lifting and projection operators
\mathcal{R}
(
2.1
) and
\mathcal{Q}
(
2.2
) are replaced instead by
non-linear
mappings of the form

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(a)(x)=\widehat{R}(a(x),x),\quad\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(v)(x)=\widehat{Q}(v(x),x),

where
\widehat{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\times D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
and
\widehat{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}\times D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
are neural networks with activation function
\sigma
. Our error estimates will rely on the (more restrictive) linear choice of lifting and projection operators, given by (
2.1
), (
2.2
). The linear choice has the theoretical benefit of ensuring
compositionality
, i.e. that a composition of neural operators can again be represented by a neural operator (cf. Lemma
D.4
). Despite this technical distinction, we emphasize that all of our error and complexity estimates continue to hold also for neural operators with non-linear lifting and projections, since linear operators can always be approximated by non-linear ones (cp. Lemma
C.1
). In fact, in the non-linear case, our results imply that
\widehat{Q}
,
\widehat{R}
can be chosen to be
shallow
networks.

In analogy with canonical finite-dimensional neural networks, the layers
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{L}
are non-linear operator layers,
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}:\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
,
v\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)
, which we assume to be of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)(x)=\sigma\bigg(W_{\ell}v(x)+b_{\ell}(x)+\big(\mathcal{K}(a;\theta_{\ell})v\big)(x)\bigg),\quad\forall\,x\in D.

Here, the weight matrix
W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
and bias
b_{\ell}(x)\in\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
define an affine pointwise mapping
W_{\ell}v(x)+b_{\ell}(x)
. The richness of linear operators in the infinite-dimensional setting can partly be realized by defining the following
non-local
linear operator,

\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{A}\times\Theta\to L\left(\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}),\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\right),

that maps the input field
a
and a parameter
\theta\in\Theta
in the parameter-set
\Theta
to a bounded linear operator
\mathcal{K}(a,\theta):\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, and the non-linear activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is applied component-wise. As proposed in
[
18
]
, the linear operators
\mathcal{K}(a,\theta)
are integral operators of the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{K}(a;\theta)v\big)(x)=\int_{D}\kappa_{\theta}(x,y;a(x),a(y))v(y)\,dy,\quad\forall\,x\in D.

Here, the integral kernel
\kappa_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{2(d+d_{a})}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
is a neural network parametrized by
\theta\in\Theta
. Specific examples of the integral kernel (
2.3
) include those evaluated with a graph kernel network as in
[
18
]
or with a multipole expansion
[
17
]
.

2.3.
Fourier Neural Operators

As defined in
[
19
]
, Fourier Neural operators (FNOs) are special cases of general neural operators (
2.3
), in which the kernel
\kappa_{\theta}(x,y;a(x),a(y))
is of the form
\kappa_{\theta}=\kappa_{\theta}(x-y)
. In this case, (
2.3
) can be written as a convolution

(2.4)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{K}({\theta})v\big)(x)=\int_{D}\kappa_{\theta}(x-y)v(y)\,dy,\quad\forall\,x\in D.

For concreteness, we consider the periodic domain
D=\mathbb{T}^{d}
(which we identify with the standard torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=[0,2\pi]^{d}
), although non-periodic, rectangular domains
D
can also be handled in a straightforward manner.

Given this periodic framework, the convolution operator in (
2.4
) can be computed using the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
and the inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
(see Appendix
B
(
B.1
) and (
B.2
) for notation and definitions), resulting in the following equivalent representation of the kernel (
2.3
),

(2.5)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}(\theta)v)(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Big(P_{\theta}(k)\cdot\mathcal{F}(v)(k)\Big)(x),\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.

Here,
P_{\theta}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
is a full matrix indexed by
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and is related to the integral kernel
\kappa_{\theta}(x)
in (
2.4
) via the Fourier transform,
P_{\theta}(k)=\mathcal{F}(\kappa_{\theta})(k)
. Note that we must impose that
P_{\theta}(-k)=P_{\theta}(k)^{\dagger}
coincides with the Hermitian transpose for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, to ensure that the image function
(\mathcal{K}(\theta)v)(x)
is a real-valued function for real-valued
v(x)
. Consequently, the form of Fourier neural operators (FNOs) for the periodic domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is that of a mapping
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, of the form

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(a):=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}(a),

where the lifting and projection operators
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are given by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively, and where the non-linear layers
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
are of the form

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)(x)=\sigma\bigg(W_{\ell}v(x)+b_{\ell}(x)+\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Big(P_{\ell}(k)\cdot\mathcal{F}(v)(k)\Big)(x)\bigg).

Here,
W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
and
b_{\ell}(x)
define a pointwise affine mapping (corresponding to weights and biases), and
P_{\ell}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
defines the coefficients of a non-local, linear mapping via the Fourier transform.

Remark 2.3
.

The simplest example for a FNO, as defined by (
2.6
),(
2.4
) is as follows; let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
be a canonical finite-dimensional neural network with activation function
\sigma
. We can associate to
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
the mapping
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, given by
a(x)\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a(x))
. We easily observe that
\mathcal{N}
is a FNO as we can write it in the form,

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{R}},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(y)=Ry
with
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{a}}
, and each layer
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}
is of the form
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(y)=\sigma(W_{\ell}y+b_{\ell})
for some
W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
,
b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
, with
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}
being an affine output layer of the form
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(y)=Qy+q
with
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}
,
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
. Replacing the input
y
by a function
v(x)
, these layers clearly are a special case of the FNO lifting layer (
2.1
), the non-linear layers (
2.7
) (with
P_{\ell}\equiv 0
and constant bias
b_{\ell}(x)\equiv b_{\ell}
), and the projection layer (
2.2
). Thus, any finite-dimensional neural network can be identified with a FNO as defined above.

For the remainder of this work, we make the following assumption,

Assumption 2.4

(Activation function)
.

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
2.7
) is assumed to be non-polynomial, (globally) Lipschitz continuous and
\sigma\in C^{3}
.

2.4.
Universal Approximation by FNOs

Next, we will show that FNOs (
2.6
) are
universal
i.e., given a large class of operators, as defined in setting
2.1
, one can find an FNO that approximates it to desired accuracy. To be more precise, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.5

(Universal approximation)
.

Let
s,s^{\prime}\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{G}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
be a continuous operator. Let
K\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
be a compact subset. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a FNO
\mathcal{N}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, of the form (
2.6
), continuous as an operator
H^{s}\to H^{s^{\prime}}
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon.

Sketch of proof.

The detailed proof of this universal approximation theorem is provided in Appendix
D.4
and we outline it here. For notational simplicity, we set
d_{a}=d_{u}=1
,
and first observe the following lemma, proved in Appendix
D.1
:

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume that the universal approximation Theorem
2.5
holds for
s^{\prime}=0
. Then it holds for arbitrary
s^{\prime}\geq 0
.

The main objective is thus to prove Theorem
2.5
for the special case
s^{\prime}=0
; i.e. given a continuous operator
\mathcal{G}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
K\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
compact, and
\epsilon>0
, we wish to construct a FNO
\mathcal{N}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, such that
{\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon}
.

To this end, we start by defining the following operator,

(2.8)

\mathcal{G}_{N}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\mathcal{G}_{N}(a):=P_{N}\mathcal{G}(P_{N}a),

with
P_{N}
being the orthogonal Fourier projection operator, defined in Appendix
B
(
B.6
). Thus,
\mathcal{G}_{N}
can be thought of loosely as the
Fourier projection
of the continuous operator
\mathcal{G}
.

Next, we can show that for any given
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{G}_{N}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon,\quad\forall\,a\in K.

Thus, the proof boils down to finding a FNO (
2.6
) that can approximate the operator
\mathcal{G}_{N}
to any desired accuracy.

To this end, we introduce a set of Fourier wavenumbers
k\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
, by

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{N}:={\left\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,\middle|\,|k|_{\infty}\leq N\right\}},

and define a
Fourier conjugate
or
Fourier dual
operator of the form

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}
,

(2.11)

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}(\widehat{a}_{k}):=\mathcal{F}_{N}\left(\mathcal{G}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}(\widehat{a}_{k})\right)\right)\right),

such that the identity

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{N}(a)=\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}\circ\mathcal{F}_{N}(P_{N}a),

holds for all
real-valued

a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Here,
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is the discrete Fourier transform and
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}
is the discrete inverse Fourier transform, with both being defined in Appendix
B
(
B.13
) and (
B.14
), respectively.

The next steps in the proof are to leverage the natural decomposition of the projection
\mathcal{G}_{N}
in (
2.12
) in terms of the discrete Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{N}\circ P_{N}
, the discrete inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}
and the Fourier conjugate operator
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
and approximate each of these operators by Fourier neural operators.

We start by denoting,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}=\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}(\simeq\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}),

as the set consisting of coefficients
\{(v_{1,k},v_{2,k})\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{N}}
, where
v_{\ell,k}\in\mathbb{R}
are indexed by a tuple
(\ell,k)
,
\ell\in\{1,2\}
,
k\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
, and interpreting the operator
\mathcal{F}_{N}\circ P_{N}
as a mapping
\mathcal{F}_{N}\circ P_{N}:a\mapsto\{(\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{a}_{k}),\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{a}_{k}))\}_{|k|\leq N}
, with input
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and the output
\{\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{a}_{k}),\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{a}_{k})\}_{|k|\leq N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
is viewed as a
constant
function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}})
. The approximation of this operator is a straightforward consequence of the following Lemma, proved in Appendix
D.2
,

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
B>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
be given. For all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}(T^{d};\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}})
,
v\mapsto\{\mathcal{N}(v)_{\ell,k}\}
, with
constant
output functions (constant as a function of
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
), and such that

\left.\begin{aligned} \|\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{v}_{k})-\mathcal{N}(v)_{1,k}\|_{L^{\infty}}&\leq\epsilon\\
\|\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{v}_{k})-\mathcal{N}(v)_{2,k}\|_{L^{\infty}}&\leq\epsilon\end{aligned}\right\}\quad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\;|k|_{\infty}\leq N,

for all
\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
, and where
\widehat{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
denotes the
k
-th Fourier coefficient of
v
.

In the next step, we approximate the (discrete) inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}
by an FNO. We recall that FNOs act on functions rather than on constants. Therefore, to connect
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}
and FNOs, we are going to interpret the mapping

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}:[-R,R]^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

as a mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}:\left\{\begin{aligned} &L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};[-R,R]^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\\
&\{\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{v}_{k}),\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{v}_{k})\}_{|k|\leq N}\mapsto v(x),\end{aligned}\right.

where the input
\{\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{v}_{k}),\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{v}_{k})\}_{|k|\leq N}\in[-R,R]^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
is identified with a
constant
function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};[-R,R]^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}})
. The existence of a FNO of the form (
2.6
) that can approximate (
D.19
) to desired accuracy is a consequence of the following lemma, proved in Appendix
D.3
,

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
B>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
be given. For all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, such that for any
v\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with
\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
, we have

\left\|v-\mathcal{N}\left(w\right)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon,

where
w(x):=\left\{(\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{v}_{k}),\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{v}_{k}))\right\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{N}}
, i.e.
w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}})
is a constant function collecting the real and imaginary parts of the Fourier coefficients
\widehat{v}_{k}
of
v
.

Finally, by setting
\widehat{K}:=\mathcal{F}_{N}(P_{N}K)\subset\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}
as the (compact) image of
K
under the continuous mapping
\mathcal{F}_{N}\circ P_{N}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}
and identifying
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
, where
\widehat{v}_{1,k}:=\mathrm{Re}(\widehat{v}_{k})
and
\widehat{v}_{2,k}:=\mathrm{Im}(\widehat{v}_{k})
for
k\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
, we can view
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
as a continuous mapping

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}:\widehat{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}},

on a compact subset. Hence, by the universal approximation theorem for finite-dimensional neural networks
[
1
,
13
]
, one can readily show that there exists an FNO, with only

local
weights (see remark
2.3
), which will approximate this continuous mapping
\widehat{G}_{N}
on compact subsets to desired accuracy.

Hence, each of the component operators of the decomposition (
2.12
) can be approximated to desired accuracy by FNOs and the universal approximation theorem follows by composing these FNOs and estimating the resulting error, with details provided in appendix
D.4
.
∎

In the following theorem, we will show that the universal approximation theorem
2.5
can be extended to include operators defined on function spaces with Lipschitz domains. In fact, the Lipschitz condition can be relaxed to include all locally uniform domains using ideas from
[
31
]
; we will, however, not pursue this for simplicity of the exposition. We show that one can construct a period extension of the input function and a FNO so that the restriction of the FNO’s periodic output to the domain of interest gives a suitable approximation to any continuous operator. Similar ideas have been pursued in the design of numerical algorithms for solving PDEs and usually go by the name of
Fourier continuations

[
5
,
24
]
. A major challenge for these methods is designing a suitable periodic function whose restriction gives the solution of interest. We show that FNOs can learn the output representation automatically.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
s,s^{\prime}\geq 0
and
\Omega\subset[0,2\pi]^{d}
be a domain with Lipschitz boundary. Let
\mathcal{G}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
be a continuous operator. Let
K\subset H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
be a compact subset. Then
there exists a continuous, linear operator
\mathcal{E}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
such that
\mathcal{E}(a)|_{\Omega}=a
for all
a\in H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
. Furthermore,
for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a FNO
\mathcal{N}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
of the form (
2.6
), such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)|_{\Omega}\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Since
\Omega
is open we have that
{\text{dist}(\Omega,\partial[0,2\pi]^{d})>0}
hence the conclusion of Lemma
B.3
in Appendix
B

follows with the hypercube
B=[0,2\pi]^{d}
, in particular,
there exists a continuous, linear operator
\mathcal{E}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s}([0,2\pi]^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
such that
\mathcal{E}(a)|_{\Omega}=a
and
\mathcal{E}(a)
is periodic on
[0,2\pi]^{d}
for all
a\in H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
. Therefore

\mathcal{E}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
. Similarly, we can construct an extension operator
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:H^{s^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
.

We can then associate to
\mathcal{G}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
another continuous operator
\overline{\mathcal{G}}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, by defining
\overline{\mathcal{G}}(a):=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{R}(a)
. Here
\mathcal{R}(a):=a|_{\Omega}
denotes the restriction to
\Omega
which is clearly linear and continuous.
By the continuity of
\mathcal{E}
, we have that
K^{\prime}:=\mathcal{E}(K)
is compact in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
. By the universal approximation theorem
2.5
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a FNO
\overline{\mathcal{N}}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, such that

\sup_{a^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}}\|\overline{\mathcal{G}}(a^{\prime})-\overline{\mathcal{N}}(a^{\prime})\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon.

But then, using the fact that
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{Id}
,
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Id}
, the mapping
\mathcal{N}:H^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
given by
\mathcal{N}:=\mathcal{R}\circ\overline{\mathcal{N}}\circ\mathcal{E}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in K}\|{\mathcal{G}}(a)-{\mathcal{N}}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\circ{\mathcal{G}}\circ\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)-\mathcal{R}\circ\overline{\mathcal{N}}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{R}\circ\overline{\mathcal{G}}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)-\mathcal{R}\circ\overline{\mathcal{N}}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{a\in K}\|\overline{\mathcal{G}}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)-\overline{\mathcal{N}}\circ\mathcal{E}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{a^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}}\|\overline{\mathcal{G}}(a^{\prime})-\overline{\mathcal{N}}(a^{\prime})\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.

∎

Remark 2.10
.

The form of the universal approximation theorem
2.5
stated above, shows that any continuous operator
\mathcal{G}:H^{s}\to H^{s^{\prime}}
can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by a FNO,
on a given compact subset
K\subset H^{s}
. The restriction to compact subsets may not always be very natural. For example, to train FNOs in practice, it might be more convenient to draw training samples from a measure
\mu
such as the law of a Gaussian random field, which does not have compact support. Furthermore, the operator
\mathcal{G}
may not always be continuous. To address these issues, one can follow the recent paper
[
15
]
, where the authors prove a more general version for the universal approximation of operators for DeepOnets; for any input measure
\mu
, and a Borel measurable operator
\mathcal{G}
, such that
\int\|\mathcal{G}(a)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\mu(a)<\infty
, it is shown that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a DeepOnet
\mathcal{N}(a)\approx\mathcal{G}(a)
such that

\int\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\mu(a)<\epsilon.

In particular, there are no restrictions on the topological support of
\mu
. The result of
[
15
]
was for the alternative operator learning framework of DeepOnets, but the ideas and the proof can be analogously extended to FNOs.

2.5.
\Psi
-Fourier neural operators.

In practice, one needs to compute the FNO, of form (
2.6
), both during training as well as for the evaluation of the neural operator. Thus, given any input function
a
, one should be able to readily calculate the FNO
\mathcal{N}(a)
, requiring the efficient computation of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
(
B.1
) and the inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
(
B.2
). In general, this is not possible as evaluating the Fourier transform (
B.1
) entails computing an integral exactly. Therefore, approximations are necessary to realize the action of FNOs on functions. Following
[
19
]
, one can efficiently approximate the Fourier transform and its inverse by the discrete Fourier transform (
B.13
) and the discrete inverse Fourier transform (
B.14
), respectively. This amounts to performing a pseudo(
\Psi
)-spectral Fourier projection between successive layers of the FNO and leading to the following precise definition,

Definition 2.11

(
\Psi
-FNO)
.

A
\Psi
-FNO
(or
\Psi
-spectral FNO) is a mapping

\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}),\quad a\mapsto\mathcal{N}(a),

of the form

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(a)=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{R}(a),

where
\mathcal{I}_{N}
denotes the pseudo-spectral Fourier projection onto trigonometric polynomials of degree
N\in\mathbb{N}
(
B.10
), the lifting operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, the projection
\mathcal{Q}:\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
are defined as in (
2.1
), (
2.2
), and the non-linear layers
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,\dots,N
, are of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma\bigg(W_{\ell}v(x)+b_{\ell}(x)+\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Big(P_{\ell}(k)\cdot\mathcal{F}(v)(k)\Big)(x)\bigg).

Here,
W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
and
b_{\ell}(x)\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
define a pointwise affine mapping
v\mapsto W_{\ell}v(x)+b_{\ell}(x)
, and the coefficients
P_{\ell}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}

(k\in\mathcal{K}_{N})
define a (non-local) convolution operator via the Fourier transform.

Note that a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}
is uniquely defined, as an operator, by its restriction to the finite-dimensional subspace
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\subset\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
(see Appendix
B
for the definition of
L^{2}_{N}
). Furthermore, we have that the image
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{N})\subset L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
. To indicate that a
\Psi
-FNO is of the form
2.14
, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we shall thus more simply say that “
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
is a
\Psi
-FNO”.

At the level of numerical implementation, a
\Psi
-FNO can be naturally identified with a
finite-dimensional
mapping

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}},\quad\bm{a}\mapsto\mathcal{N}(\bm{a}),

with input
\bm{a}=\{a_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}
corresponding to the point-values
a_{j}=a(x_{j})
on the grid
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
, and
\mathcal{J}_{N}:=\{0,\dots,2N\}^{d}
. Here,
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
is of the form

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(\bm{a})=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{L}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{a}),

where the lifting operator
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}
,
\bm{a}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{a})
, the projection
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}
,
\bm{v}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{v})
, are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\bm{a})

\displaystyle=\{Ra_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}},

\displaystyle(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{a}}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\{Qv_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}},\quad

\displaystyle(Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}),

and the non-linear layers
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,\dots,N
, are of the form

(2.15)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell}(\bm{v})_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma\bigg(W_{\ell}v_{j}+b_{\ell,j}+\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}\Big(P_{\ell}(k)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{N}(\bm{v})(k)\Big)_{j}\bigg)

for
j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}
. Here,
W_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
,
b_{\ell,j}=b_{\ell}(x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}
defines a pointwise affine mapping
W_{\ell}v_{j}+b_{\ell,j}
, the coefficients
P_{\ell}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}

(k\in\mathcal{K}_{N})
satisfy the Hermitian conjugacy condition
P_{\ell}(-k)=P_{\ell}(k)^{\dagger}
and define a (non-local) convolution operator via the discrete Fourier transform, and the non-linear activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is extended componentwise to a function
\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times\mathcal{J}_{N}}
. Comparing
\mathcal{N}
with the corresponding discretization
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
, it is easy to see that

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(\{a(x_{j})\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}})_{j}=\mathcal{N}(a)(x_{j}),\quad\forall\,j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}.

In particular, this implies that
\mathcal{N}(a)(x)
can in practice be computed for any
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
via the Fourier interpolation of the grid values
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(\{a(x_{j})\})_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
. In contrast to general FNOs,
\Psi
-FNOs therefore allow for efficient numerical implementation. Furthermore, the discrete (inverse) Fourier transforms in each hidden layer in (
2.15
) can be very efficiently computed using the fast Fourier transform (FFT).

The above discussion also leads to a very natural definition of the size of a
\Psi
-FNO below:

Definition 2.12

(Depth, width, lift and size)
.

The
depth
and
width
of a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}
(cp. Definition
2.11
), are defined by

0pt(\mathcal{N}):=L,\quad 0pt(\mathcal{N}):=d_{v}|\mathcal{J}_{N}|=d_{v}|\mathcal{K}_{N}|=(2N+1)^{d}d_{v}.

We refer to the dimension
d_{v}
, as the
lift
of
\mathcal{N}
, i.e. we set

\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N}):=d_{v}.

The
size
of a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}
is defined as the total number of degrees of freedom in a
\Psi
-FNO. A simple calculation shows that

\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{N})=\underbrace{d_{u}d_{v}}_{\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{Q})}+L\underbrace{\left(d_{v}^{2}+d_{v}|\mathcal{J}_{N}|+d_{v}^{2}|\mathcal{J}_{N}|\right)}_{\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{L}_{\ell})}+\underbrace{d_{a}d_{v}}_{\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{R})}.

The precise size of a
\Psi
-FNO will not be of any particular relevance for our asymptotic complexity estimates. Instead, we will usually content ourselves with the simple estimate

\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{N})\lesssim 0pt(\mathcal{N})\,0pt(\mathcal{N})\,\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N}),

where we assume that
\max(d_{a},d_{u})\leq d_{v}
; under this condition, the above estimate follows from the fact that
\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{N})\sim Ld_{v}^{2}|\mathcal{J}_{N}|
.

Given our discussion, it is natural to ask whether any FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L-1}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}
can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by an associated
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
,

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{R},

for sufficiently large
N\in\mathbb{N}
? An affirmative answer can be given for a natural class of FNOs of
finite width
, defined as follows.

Definition 2.13
.

A FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
is said to be
of finite width
, if
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
is a composition
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}
, with layers
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
of the form (
2.7
), and for which there exists a “width”
W\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the Fourier multiplier
P_{\ell}(k)\equiv 0
, for
|k|_{\infty}>W
.

We can now state the following theorem, which shows that
\Psi
-FNOs
\mathcal{N}
provide an arbitrarily close approximation of a given FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
:

Theorem 2.14
.

Assume that the activation function
\sigma\in C^{m}
is globally Lipschitz continuous.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
be a FNO of finite width, with
s>d/2
. and assume that
m>s
. Then for any
\epsilon,B>0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, such that

\sup_{\|a\|_{H^{s}}\leq B}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

For the proof, we refer to Appendix
D.5
. In particular, the last theorem implies an extension of the universal approximation theorem
2.5
to
\Psi
-FNOs
, provided that the input functions have sufficient regularity for the pseudo-spectral projection (
B.10
) to be well-defined:

Theorem 2.15

(Universal approximation for
\Psi
-FNOs)
.

Let
s>d/2
, and let
s^{\prime}\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{G}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
be a continuous operator. And let
K\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})
be a compact subset. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of the universal approximation theorem for FNOs, we again note that the general case
s^{\prime}\geq 0
can be deduced from the statement of Theorem
2.15
for the special case
s^{\prime}=0
. This is the content of the following lemma, whose proof is provided in Appendix
D.6
:

Lemma 2.16
.

Assume that Theorem
2.15
holds for
s^{\prime}=0
. Then it holds for arbitrary
s^{\prime}\geq 0
.

The special case
s^{\prime}=0
follows immediately from Theorem
2.14
and the observation that the FNO approximation constructed in the proof of the universal approximation theorem for FNOs, Theorem
2.5
, has finite width.
∎

2.5.1.
Structure and properties of
\Psi
-FNOs

We conclude this section by pointing out some aspects of the structure of
\Psi
-FNOs (
2.14
) that will be relevant in the following. To start with, we can simplify
\Psi
-FNOs by viewing them in terms of two types of layers. which we will refer to as
\sigma
- and
\mathcal{F}
-layers, respectively. A
\sigma
-layer
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
of a
\Psi
-FNO is a
local, non-linear
layer of the form
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(v)(x)=\mathcal{I}_{N}\sigma\left(A\mathcal{I}_{N}v(x)+b\right)
, or, in the numerical implementation (cp. (
2.15
))

\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}(v)_{j}=\sigma\left(Av_{j}+b_{j}\right),\quad\forall\,j\in\mathcal{J}_{N},

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
, and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}_{N}\times d_{v}}
defining an affine mapping. A
\mathcal{F}
-layer
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}}
of a
\Psi
-FNO is a
non-local, linear
layer of the form
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(P(k)\cdot\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{I}_{N}v)(k))(x)
, which in a practical implementation corresponds to

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{F}}(v)_{j}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}\Big(P(k)\cdot\mathcal{F}_{N}(v)(k)\Big)_{j},\quad\forall\,j\in\mathcal{J}_{N},

where
P:\mathcal{K}_{N}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{v}\times d_{v}}
is a collection of complex weights, with
P(-k)=P(k)^{\dagger}
the Hermitian transpose of
P(k)
, and
\mathcal{F}_{N}
(
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}
) denotes the discrete (inverse) Fourier transform.

The main point of these definitions is that each
\Psi
-FNO can be decomposed into a finite number of
\sigma
-layers and
\mathcal{F}
-layers, and that the converse is also true; i.e. any composition of
\sigma
-layers and
\mathcal{F}
-layers can be represented by a
\Psi
-FNO. These statements are made precise in a series of technical Lemmas, which are stated and proved in Appendix
D.7
.

3.
Approximation of PDEs by
\Psi
-FNOs

We have shown in the previous section that FNOs (
2.6
) as well as their computational realizations (
\Psi
-FNOs (
2.14
)) are universal i.e., they approximate any continuous operator, defined in the setting
2.1
, to desired accuracy. However, as repeatedly discussed in the introduction, universality alone does not suffice to claim that FNOs can approximate operators efficiently. In particular, it could happen that the size of the FNO is unfeasibly large to ensure a given accuracy of the approximation. That this is indeed the case is made precise in the following remark.

Remark 3.1
.

We observe from the proof of Theorem
2.5
that the desired FNO, approximating the operator
\mathcal{G}
, is constructed as
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{IFT}}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{N}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{FT}}
, with
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{FT}},\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{IFT}}
approximating the Fourier and Inverse Fourier transforms, respectively, whereas
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
is a canonical finite-dimensional neural network approximation of the “Fourier conjugate operator” (
2.11
):
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
. We note that
N
herein has to be chosen sufficiently large in order to yield the desired error tolerance of
\epsilon
. By Theorem
B.2
, this depends on the smoothness of the input space, i.e., if the input
a\in K\subset H^{s}
, for some
s>0
, then we need to choose
N
such that
N^{-s}\sim\epsilon
. Further assuming that the mapping
\mathcal{G}
is Lipschitz continuous, implies that the Fourier conjugate operator
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}
is also Lipschitz continuous as a mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2\mathcal{K}_{N}}
. Hence, neural network approximation results, such as those of
[
34
]
for ReLU activations or
[
10
]
for tanh activations, yield that the width of the approximating neural network
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
scales as
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}})\gtrsim\epsilon^{-D}
, where
D
is the dimension of the domain of
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
. In the present case, we have
D=|\mathcal{K}_{N}|\sim N^{d}\sim\epsilon^{-d/s}
, yielding that

(3.1)

0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}})\gtrsim\epsilon^{-\epsilon^{-d/s}}.

This scaling represents a
super-exponential
growth in the size of the FNO
\mathcal{N}
, with respect to the error
\epsilon
, incurred in approximating the underlying operator
\mathcal{G}
.

Given the above remark, we infer that in the worst case, a FNO approximating a generic Lipschitz continuous operator
\mathcal{G}
, can require extremely large sizes to achieve the desired accuracy, making it unfeasible in practice. The same holds for
\Psi
-FNOs of the form (
2.14
). This super-exponential growth appears as a form of
curse of dimensionality
i.e., exponential growth of complexity (measured here in the size of the FNO), with respect to the error.

Hence, it is reasonable to ask how these extremely pessimistic complexity bounds on FNOs (
\Psi
-FNOs), can be reconciled to their robust numerical performance for approximating PDEs, as reported in
[
19
]
. The rest of the section investigates this fundamental question.

The starting point of our explanation for the robustness of FNOs in approximating PDEs is the observation that operators which arise in the context of PDEs have a special structure and are not merely generic continuous operators mapping one infinite-dimensional function space to another. To see this, we point out that many time-dependent PDEs arising in physics can be written in the general abstract form,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\nabla\cdot F(u,\nabla u)=0,

where for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
is a vector of physical quantities, describing e.g. density, velocity or temperature of a fluid or other material at a given point
x\in D
in the domain
D
and at time
t\in[0,T]
. Equation (
3.2
) describes the general form of a conservation law for the physical quantities
u
with a flux function
F(u,\nabla u)
, which is typically
non-linear
, and can e.g. represent advection or diffusion terms. The flux function
F(u,\nabla u)
may also depend on
u
in a
non-local
manner. For example, for the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

u(x,t)=(u_{1}(x,t),\dots,u_{d}(x,t))\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

represent the fluid velocity at
(x,t)
, and the flux is defined by

F(u,\nabla u)=-u\otimes u-p+\nu\nabla u,

where
p=p(u)
depends on
u
in a non-local manner:

p=\mathcal{R}:(u\otimes u),\quad\mathcal{R}:=(-\Delta)^{-1}(\nabla\otimes\nabla),

where
\mathcal{R}
is a (non-local) operator closely related to the Riesz transform.

A popular numerical method for time-dependent PDEs, of the form (
3.2
), particularly on periodic domains
D=\mathbb{T}^{d}
, is the
pseudo-spectral method

[
7
]
, wherein (
3.2
) is discretized as,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{N}+\nabla\cdot\mathcal{I}_{N}F(u_{N},\nabla u_{N})=0,

where
u_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
\leq N
.

The resulting system of ODEs (
3.3
) can be further discretized in time using a time-marching scheme. For simplicity, the forward Euler discretization with time step
{\tau}
leads to,

(3.4)

\displaystyle u^{n+1}_{N}=u^{n}_{N}-{\tau}\nabla\cdot\mathcal{I}_{N}F(u^{n}_{N},\nabla u^{n}_{N}).

One might prove that the system (
3.4
) provides a convergent approximation for the underlying time-dependent PDE (
3.2
) for many different choices of the flux
F
.
In order to connect the approximation (
3.4
) with FNOs, we decompose the right hand side of (
3.4
) as,

\displaystyle u^{n}_{N}

\displaystyle\,\overset{\mathcal{R}}{\longmapsto}\,\begin{Bmatrix}u^{n}_{N}\\[3.0pt]
u^{n}_{N}\end{Bmatrix}\,\overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longmapsto}\,\begin{Bmatrix}u^{n}_{N}\\[3.0pt]
\nabla u^{n}_{N}\end{Bmatrix}

\displaystyle\,\overset{\sigma}{\mapsto}\,\dots\,\overset{\sigma}{\mapsto}\,\begin{Bmatrix}u^{n}_{N}\\[3.0pt]
F^{\sigma}_{N}(u^{n}_{N},\nabla u^{n}_{N})\end{Bmatrix}

\displaystyle\,\overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longmapsto}\,\begin{Bmatrix}u^{n}_{N}\\[3.0pt]
\nabla\cdot F^{\sigma}_{N}(u^{n}_{N},\nabla u^{n}_{N})\end{Bmatrix}

\displaystyle\,{\longmapsto}\;u^{n}_{N}-{\tau}\nabla\cdot F^{\sigma}_{N}(u^{n}_{N},\nabla u^{n}_{N}).

Here,
\mathcal{R}
is the lifting operator and
\sigma
,
\mathcal{F}
are the
\sigma
- and
\mathcal{F}
-layers, respectively, of a
\Psi
-FNO, that are defined in section
2.5.1
. The above representation suggests that the Fourier
\mathcal{F}
-layers of a
\Psi
-FNO allow us to take
exact
derivatives, and a composition of
\sigma
-layers of a
\Psi
-FNO allows us to approximate continuous functions to any desired accuracy (via the standard universal approximation theorem for finite-dimensional neural networks); in particular, a composition of
\sigma
-layers can provide an approximation

(u,\nabla u)\mapsto F^{\sigma}_{N}(u,\nabla u)\approx\mathcal{I}_{N}F(u,\nabla u).

Thus, by a suitable composition of
\sigma
- and
\mathcal{F}
-layers,
\Psi
-FNOs can
emulate
pseudo-spectral methods, providing a mechanism by which such neural operators can approximate solution operators for a large class of PDEs efficiently.

We will make this intuition precise in the following. However, instead of considering a generic abstract form of PDEs, we focus on two PDEs, often encountered in physics, which serve as prototypes for a wide variety of PDEs. We start with an elliptic PDE below.

3.1.
Stationary Darcy Flow

We consider the elliptic PDE of the form,

(3.5)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla u)=f.

Here,
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, can correspond to the steady-state pressure for a fluid, flowing according to the Darcy’s law, in a porous medium with the positive coefficient
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, denoting the rock permeability. Another model for (
3.5
) is that of a diffusion equation, with
u
modeling the temperature and
a
the thermal conductivity of the medium.

For simplicity, we assume periodic boundary conditions on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and we impose that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u\,dx=0
. Employing a suitable rescaling, we will furthermore assume that
a
can be written in the form:

a=1+\widetilde{a},\quad\widetilde{a}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\;s>d/2.

We note that the assumption
s>d/2
ensures that
\|\widetilde{a}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
, via the Sobolev embedding
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}){\hookrightarrow}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
(cp. Theorem
B.1
). To ensure that (
3.5
) is well-posed, we assume the following coercivity condition: There exists
\lambda>0
, such that
1+\widetilde{a}\geq\lambda
. In fact, we shall assume the slightly stronger condition that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{a}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\widetilde{a}\|_{H^{s}}\leq 1-\lambda,

where
C
is the norm of the embedding
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}){\hookrightarrow}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. The condition (
3.6
)
clearly implies the
\lambda
-coercivity of (
3.5
).

The underlying operator
\mathcal{G}:L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, maps a coefficient
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to the solution
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
of (
3.5
). Our aim is to
learn
this operator efficiently using a
\Psi
-FNO. To this end, we will follow the program discussed above and first present a pseudo-spectral method that approximates the Darcy flow PDE (
3.5
) accurately. Then, this pseudo-spectral method will be emulated by a suitable
\Psi
-FNO.

3.1.1.
A Fourier-Galerkin approximation of (
3.5
)

We fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that the coefficient field and right-hand side
a=1+\widetilde{a},f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, for some
s>d/2
, such that the pseudo-spectral projections
\mathcal{I}_{N}\widetilde{a}
and
\mathcal{I}_{N}f
are well-defined. We can now define a Fourier-Galerkin approximation of (
3.5
) as the (unique) solution
u_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, with
\int u_{N}\,dx=0
, and such that

(3.7)

\displaystyle-\dot{P}_{N}\nabla\cdot(a_{N}\nabla u_{N})=f_{N},

where we set
a_{N}:=1+\widetilde{a}_{N}
and

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \widetilde{a}_{N}&:=\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a},\\
f_{N}&:=\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}f.\end{aligned}\right.

See notation for
P_{N},\dot{P}_{N}
and
\mathcal{I}_{N}
in appendix
B
. We observe from (
3.8
) that
\widetilde{a}_{N}
and
f_{N}
are obtained by first carrying out a pseudo-spectral projection of
\widetilde{a}
,
f
on the regular grid
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{2N}}
(with
2N
grid points in each direction), yielding a representation of the form

\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a}=\sum_{|k|\leq 2N}\widehat{a}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},\quad\mathcal{I}_{2N}f=\sum_{|k|\leq 2N}\widehat{f}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},

for coefficients
\widehat{a}_{k},\widehat{f}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
, and then projecting these expressions onto
\dot{L}^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
:

\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a}=\sum_{0<|k|\leq N}\widehat{a}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},\quad\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}f=\sum_{0<|k|\leq N}\widehat{f}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}.

The reason for this particular choice of the projection (
3.8
) lies in the fact that the mapping

L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad(a_{N},u_{N})\mapsto-P_{N}\nabla\cdot\left(a_{N}\nabla u_{N}\right),

can be exactly represented by a pseudo-spectral method on
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{2N}}
, i.e., on a denser grid of
2N
grid points in each direction. Indeed, it is easy to see that if
a_{N}
,
u_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}
,
then
\nabla u_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}
, and the values
\nabla u_{N}(x_{j})
can be computed exactly via the discrete Fourier transform from the values
u_{N}(x_{j})
,
j\in\mathcal{J}_{2N}
. Since the product
a_{N}\nabla u_{N}\in L^{2}_{2N}
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
\leq 2N
, the Fourier coefficients of
a_{N}\nabla u_{N}
can also be recovered from knowledge of the point-values
a_{N}(x_{j})\nabla u_{N}(x_{j})
at the grid points
x_{j}\in\mathcal{J}_{2N}
. Finally, since the divergence and projection
P_{N}
can be evaluated exactly via discrete Fourier transforms, we conclude that the mapping

(a_{N},u_{N})\mapsto-P_{N}\nabla\cdot\left(a_{N}\nabla u_{N}\right)

can be computed based on knowledge of the grid values
a_{N}(x_{j})
, and
u_{N}(x_{j})
. The above procedure of computing a product of two trigonometric polynomials of degree
\leq N

exactly
, based on the point-values on a finer grid of size
2N+1
in each direction is well-known in the context of pseudo-spectral methods, and is usually referred in the numerical analysis literature as
de-aliasing
(cf. section 3.4.2 of the textbook
[
7
]
).

In order to computationally realize the Fourier-Galerkin method (
3.7
), we are going to recast it as a fixed point problem
u_{N}=F_{N}(u_{N};a_{N},f_{N})
, where
F_{N}({\,\cdot\,};a_{N},f_{N}):L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is defined by

(3.9)

\displaystyle F_{N}(u_{N};a_{N},f_{N})=\dot{P}_{N}(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla\cdot\left(\widetilde{a}_{N}\nabla u_{N}\right)+(-\Delta)^{-1}f_{N}.

In Lemma
E.1
Appendix
E
, we show that the map (
3.9
) is a
contraction
and can be efficiently realized by a Picard type fixed-point iteration scheme. This leads to the following algorithm for realizing the Fourier-Galerkin method (
3.7
) computationally,

Algorithm 3.2

(Approximate solution of Darcy equation)
.

a

Input:

N\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
f\in\dot{H}^{k-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, with
s>d/2+k>d
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 1
.

Output:

u_{N}\in H^{1}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, such that
u_{N}\approx u
, where
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
solves (
3.5
) with coefficient field
a
and right-hand side
f
.

(1)

Compute pseudo-spectral projections
\widetilde{a}_{N},f_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, defined via the values on “de-aliased” grid
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{2N}}
(cp. (
3.8
)):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \widetilde{a}_{N}&:=\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}(a-1),\\
f_{N}&:=\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}f\end{aligned}\right\}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

(2)

Set

K:=\left\lceil\frac{\log\left(\lambda^{-1}N^{-k}\right)}{\log\left(1-\lambda/2\right)}\right\rceil.

(3)

Set
u_{N}^{0}:=0\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

(4)

For
k=1,2,\dots,K
: compute

u_{N}^{k}:=\dot{P}_{N}(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla\cdot\left(\widetilde{a}_{N}\nabla u_{N}^{k-1}\right)+(-\Delta)^{-1}f_{N}.

(5)

Set
u_{N}:=u^{K}_{N}
.

We have the following theorem on the convergence of the algorithm
3.2
,

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
u
be the unique solution of (
3.5
). Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be given, with
k>d/2+1
, and assume that the coefficient
a\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for
s>d/2+k
satisfies the coercivity condition (
3.6
). Assume furthermore that
f\in H^{k-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Then there exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(s,d,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any integer
N\geq N_{0}
, there exists a unique solution of the discretized elliptic equation (
3.7
), and there exists a constant
C=C(s,d,\lambda,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\|f\|_{H^{k-1}})>0
, such that

\|u-u_{N}\|_{H^{1}}\leq CN^{-k},

where
u_{N}
is the output of Algorithm
3.2
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
relies only on standard techniques of numerical analysis and is presented in appendix
E.1
.

3.1.2.
\Psi
-FNO approximation of the Darcy equations

We will emulate the pseudo-spectral fixed point algorithm
3.2
by a
\Psi
-FNO, allowing us to derive approximation bounds. We consider the following setting,

Setting 3.4
.

Let
s\geq d/2+k+\delta
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
be a given Sobolev regularity parameter, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
be a (coercivity) constant. Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the set of
\lambda
-coercive coefficients
a\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
of the form
a=1+\widetilde{a}
, and such that

\|a\|_{H^{s}}\leq\lambda^{-1},\quad\|\widetilde{a}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\widetilde{a}\|_{H^{d/2+\delta}}\leq 1-\lambda.

Here
C>0
denotes the norm of the embedding
H^{d/2+\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}){\hookrightarrow}L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. We consider the operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{A}^{s}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
a\mapsto u
, where
u
solves the Darcy equation

-\nabla\cdot\left(a\nabla u\right)=f,\quad\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u(x)\,dx=0,

on the periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, with right-hand side
f\in\dot{H}^{k-1}
.

With this setting in place, we can now state our main FNO approximation theorem for the solution operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{A}^{s}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
of the Darcy problem:

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume Setting
3.4
, and assume that the activation function
\sigma
is non-linear and
\sigma\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(s,\lambda,d)>0
, such that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, such that

\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}^{s}}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq CN^{-k}

and

0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq CN^{d},\quad 0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq C\log(N),\quad\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N})\leq C.

In particular, we have

\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{N})\lesssim N^{d}\log(N).

The proof of this theorem, presented in Appendix
E.3
, relies crucially on the efficient approximation of quadratic nonlinearities by shallow neural networks with smooth activation functions, see Lemma
E.3
in Appendix
E
for the precise statement and proof.

Remark 3.6
.

To achieve a FNO approximation error of order
\epsilon
for the Darcy problem, Theorem
3.5
shows that a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}
with

(3.10)

\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{N})\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{d/k}\log(\epsilon^{-1}),\quad 0pt(\mathcal{N})\lesssim\log(\epsilon^{-1}),

is sufficient. Furthermore, the lifting dimension
d_{v}
can be kept uniformly bounded, independently of
\epsilon
. In particular, for
k>d
, the required total size of the
\Psi
-FNO scales sub log-linearly in the approximation accuracy
\epsilon
, indicating that
\Psi
-FNOs may provide a very efficient approximation to the solution operator of the Darcy problem, in this case.

As a concrete example for illustrating the approximation of Darcy equations by FNOs, we consider the following example.

Example 3.7
.

A possible model for coefficients
a
with a typical length scale
\ell>0
, is to assume an expansion of
a=a(x;Y)
in terms of random variables
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots)\in[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
(not necessarily i.i.d.), of the following form, similar to the ansatz in
[
32
]
:

a(x;Y)=1+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}b_{k}Y_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},

where we assume that the coefficients
b_{k}
satisfy a decay condition of the form
|b_{k}|\leq C_{b}\exp(-\ell|k|)
for a constant
C_{b}>0
. We also assume that
a(x;Y)
satisfy the coercivity condition

\|a({\,\cdot\,};Y)-1\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|a({\,\cdot\,};Y)-1\|_{H^{d/2+\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq 1-\lambda,

for some
\delta,\lambda\in(0,1)
, and that the source term
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Under these assumptions, we have
a\in H^{s}
for any
s>0
, and hence it follows from Theorem
3.5
that
for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C=C(r,\ell,\|f\|_{C^{k}},\delta)
, with the following property (cp. also Remark
3.6
): For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}
, such that

\sup_{Y\in[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}}\|\mathcal{G}(a({\,\cdot\,},Y))-\mathcal{N}(a({\,\cdot\,},Y))\|_{H^{1}}<\epsilon,

and

(3.11)

\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{N})\leq C\epsilon^{-1/r},\quad 0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq C\log(\epsilon^{-1}).

The complexity bounds (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) suggest that the size of a
\Psi
-FNO approximating the operator
\mathcal{G}
stemming from the Darcy equations, scales only sub-linearly (or even better) in the desired accuracy
\epsilon
. This should be contrasted with the fact that the size of a
\Psi
-FNO for a generic Lipschitz operator grows super-exponentially in the desired error (
3.1
). Thus, we are able to show that a
\Psi
-FNO can approximate this PDE solution operator much more efficiently than it might a generic infinite-dimensional operator.

3.2.
Incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.

The motion of a viscous, incompressible Newtonian fluid is modeled by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=\nu\Delta u,\\
{\mathrm{div}}(u)=0,\;u(t=0)=u_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the fluid velocity and
p\in\mathbb{R}
is the pressure of the fluid, acting as a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the divergence-free constraint
{\mathrm{div}}(u)=0
. The initial fluid velocity is denoted by
u_{0}
. For simplicity, we assume periodic boundary conditions in the domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. The viscosity is denoted by
\nu\geq 0
and we would like to state that the subsequent analysis also applies for
\nu=0
, where (
3.12
) reduces to the incompressible Euler equations modeling an ideal fluid. For definiteness, we recall the following well-known theorem for the well-posedness of the Navier-Stokes equations (
3.12
),

Theorem 3.8

(see e.g.
[
25
, Thm 3.4]
)
.

Let
r>d/2+2
. For any
u_{0}\in H^{r}
, there exists
T>0
and a unique classical solution of the Navier-Stokes equations (
3.12
), such that
u\in C([0,T];H^{r})\cap C^{1}([0,T];H^{r-2})
with
u(t=0)=u_{0}
.

It is well known that in two space dimensions
d=2
, the time interval for existence of solutions
[0,T]
can be extended to any finite
T
as long as
u_{0}\in H^{r}
, whereas the corresponding finite-time well-posedness result for three space-dimensions is an outstanding open problem
[
25
]
.

We recall that if the initial data
u_{0}
of (
3.12
) belongs to
\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, i.e. if

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u_{0}(x)\,dx=0,

then we also have that the corresponding solution
u(x,t)\in\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
(reflecting momentum conservation). Next, we introduce the
Leray-projection operator

\mathbb{P}:{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
, as the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto the subspace
\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})\subset\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, consisting of divergence-free vector fields; i.e. we have
u\in\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
if, and only if,
u\in\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}u(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\,dx=0,\quad\forall\,\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

In terms of Fourier series, the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}:\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
is explicitly given by

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{u}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}\right)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\left({1}-\frac{k\otimes k}{|k|^{2}}\right)\widehat{u}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}.

In terms of the Leray projection
\mathbb{P}
, we can now equivalently write the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (
3.12
) as the following equation on the Hilbert space
\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
as,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u=-\mathbb{P}\left(u\cdot\nabla u\right)+\nu\Delta u,\\
u(t=0)=u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Given this background, our main objective in this section is to construct a
\Psi
-FNO that will approximate the operator
\mathcal{G}
which maps the initial data
u_{0}
to the solution
u({\,\cdot\,},T)
(at the final time
T
) of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (
3.12
), (
3.14

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{gathered}\frac{u_{N}^{n+1}-u_{N}^{n}}{{\tau}}+\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla u^{n+1}_{N}\right)=\nu\Delta u_{N}^{n+1},\\

Here, we fix

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{u}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}\right):=\sum_{0<|k|_{\infty}\leq N}\left(1-\frac{k\otimes k}{|k|^{2}}\right)\widehat{u}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},

Next, in practice, one has to numerically approximate the solutions of the implicit equation (

\displaystyle w_{N}\mapsto F(w_{N}):=(1-\nu{\tau}\Delta)^{-1}u^{n}_{N}-{\tau}(1-\nu{\tau}\Delta)^{-1}\mathbb{P}_{N}(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla w_{N}).

(Pseudo-spectral approximation of the Navier-Stokes equations (

a

Remark 3.13
.

It is straightforward to observe from Theorem

Definition 4.1

In the following theorem (proved in Appendix

We note that due to the particular architecture of

5.
Summary and Discussion.

Many learning tasks, particularly, but not exclusively, in scientific computing, are naturally formulated as learning operators mapping one infinite-dimensional space to another. Neural operators have recently been proposed as a framework for operator learning. A particular form, the so-called Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) (
2.6
), have been shown to be efficient in approximating a wide variety of operators that arise in PDEs
[
19
]
. Our main aim in this paper was to analyze FNOs and
\Psi
-FNOs (
2.14
), which is a concrete computational realization of FNOs. To this end, we have presented the following results,

•

We showed in Theorem
2.5
and Theorem
2.15
that FNOs (resp.
\Psi
-FNOs) are
universal
i.e., they can approximate any continuous operator to desired accuracy. Our proof relies heavily on the ability of FNOs to approximate the Fourier transform and its inverse, together with the neural network approximation of the finite-dimensional Fourier conjugate operator (
2.11
). Thus, FNOs have the same universal approximation property as canonical neural networks for finite-dimensional functions and DeepOnets for operators
[
15
]
. This universality result paves the way for the widespread use of FNOs in the context of operator learning.

•

However as stated in remark
3.1
, in the worst case, the size of a FNO can grow
super-exponentially
in terms of the desired error for approximating a general Lipschitz continuous operator. This might inhibit the use of FNOs. On the other hand, we argue in the beginning of section
3
that
\Psi
-FNOs, which are a concrete computational realization of FNOs, can approximate the nonlinearities and differential operators that define PDEs, very efficiently. Hence, one can think of
\Psi
-FNOs as a new form of pseudo-spectral methods for PDEs, which in practice are adapted to, and optimized based on the given training data. Thus, one can expect that
\Psi
-FNOs can approximate PDEs efficiently.

•

We consider two widely used prototypical PDEs, namely the elliptic PDE (
3.5
) that arises in a stationary Darcy flow and the well-known incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for fluid dynamics. For both these PDEs, we prove rigorously that there exists a
\Psi
-FNO which can approximate the underlying nonlinear operators efficiently, as we can show that the size of the
\Psi
-FNO only needs to grow polynomially in terms of the error. In fact, we show that the size grows sub-linearly in terms of the error. Thus, FNOs can approximate these widely used PDEs efficiently, corroborating the empirical results presented in
[
19
]
.

Hence, our analysis provides very strong theoretical evidence that FNOs are an effective framework for operator learning. Moreover, we also compare FNOs to an alternative operator learning framework, that of DeepOnets
[
21
]
and show that
\Psi
-FNOs can be thought of a special case of DeepOnets with a trunk net approximating trigonometric functions and sensor points being equi-distributed Cartesian grid points. Given its special architecture, we argue that a
\Psi
-FNO can allow for a more parsimonious representation of operators than a DeepOnet, enabling a cheaper approximation of certain operators.

The comparison with DeepOnets also brings out some obvious limitations of FNOs. In particular, FNOs are efficient on rectangular domains as the
\Psi
-FNO can be evaluated efficiently with FFT. Although one can use FNOs for operators defined on arbitrary domains using suitable extension and restriction operators (see Theorem
2.9
), it is unclear if these operators can be realized computationally in an efficient manner. Moreover, FNOs fix the trigonometric basis as the trunk net in the underlying DeepOnet (see Theorem
4.2
). On the other hand, a general DeepOnet can learn trunk nets from the data during training, allowing the possibility of learning a more suitable representation from data. These considerations call for a more thorough computational comparison between DeepOnets and FNOs, and possibly other operator learning frameworks such as the one from
[
2
]
.

Similarly, extending the analysis of this article to other neural operators can be readily envisaged. The use of FNOs for more general operators, particularly those arising in non-scientific computing settings, such as images, text and speech, also needs to be investigated.

Appendix B
Notation and Technical Preliminaries.

In this section, we introduce frequently used notation in the article main text and recall some essential facts about Fourier analysis.

In the main text, our focus is functions defined on the periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, identified as
\mathbb{T}^{d}=[0,2\pi]^{d}
. Following standard practice, we denote by
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the space of square-integrable functions. For any such function
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we can define the
Fourier Transform
as,

(B.1)

\mathcal{F}(v)(k):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v(x)e^{-i\langle k,x\rangle}\,dx,\quad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the
k
-th Fourier coefficient of
v
is denoted by
\widehat{v}_{k}=\mathcal{F}(v)(k)
.

Given a set of Fourier coefficients
\{\widehat{v}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, the
inverse Fourier Transform
is defined as,

(B.2)

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\widehat{v})(x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{v}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.

Using the Fourier transform (
B.1
) and for
s\geq 0
, one can denote by
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the
Sobolev space
of functions
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, with Fourier coefficients
\{\widehat{v}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, having a finite
H^{s}
-norm:

(B.3)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{s}}^{2}:=\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|k|^{2s})|\widehat{v}_{k}|^{2}<\infty.

Note that with this definition, we have from Parseval’s identity, that
\|v\|_{H^{0}}=\|v\|_{L^{2}}
, so that
H^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

We also introduce the corresponding
homogeneous Sobolev spaces

\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
(and
\dot{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=\dot{H}^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
), consisting of functions
v(x)\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and with zero mean
\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v(x)\,dx=\widehat{v}_{0}=0
, and with norm

(B.4)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}:=\left((2\pi)^{d}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}|k|^{2s}|\widehat{v}_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, throughout this work, we will denote by
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the space of trigonometric polynomials
v_{N}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, of the form

(B.5)

\displaystyle v_{N}(x)=\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}c_{k}e^{i\langle x,k\rangle},

where the summation is over all
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that

|k|_{\infty}:=\max_{i=1,\dots,d}|k_{i}|\leq N.

The space
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is viewed as a normed vector space with norm
\|{\,\cdot\,}\|_{L^{2}}
. Similarly, for
s\geq 0
, we denote by
H^{s}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
the normed vector space of trigonometric polynomials
v_{N}
of degree
\leq N
, with norm
\|{\,\cdot\,}\|_{H^{s}}
.

We note that in order to ensure that
v_{N}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
is real-valued for all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the coefficients
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
must satisfy the relations
c_{-k}=\overline{c_{k}}
for all
|k|_{\infty}\leq N
, and where
\overline{c_{k}}
denotes the complex conjugate of
c_{k}
.

We denote by

(B.6)

\displaystyle P_{N}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad v\mapsto P_{N}v,

the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
; or more explicitly,

P_{N}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}\right)=\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq N}c_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},\quad\forall\,(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

In fact, the mapping
P_{N}
defines a projection
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to H^{s}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for any
s\geq 0
. We have the following spectral approximation estimate: Let
s>0
be given. There exists a constant
C=C(s,d)>0
, such that for any
v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we have

(B.7)

\displaystyle\|v-P_{N}v\|_{H^{\varsigma}}\leq CN^{-(s-\varsigma)}\|v\|_{H^{s}},\quad\text{for any $\varsigma\in[0,s]$.}

We also define a natural projection

(B.8)

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{N}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\dot{L}^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

by removing the mean, i.e.
\dot{P}_{N}v=P_{N}v-\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v(x)\,dx
, or equivalently:

\dot{P}_{N}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}\right)=\sum_{0<|k|_{\infty}\leq N}c_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle},\quad\forall\,(c_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

Furthermore, we denote by by

(B.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{N}:C(\mathbb{T}^{d})\mapsto L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad u\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{N}u,

the
pseudo-spectral projection
onto
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
; we recall that the pseudo-spectral projection
\mathcal{I}_{N}v
of a continuous function
v
is defined as the unique trigonometric polynomial
\mathcal{I}_{N}v\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, such that

(B.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{N}v(x_{j})=v(x_{j}),\quad\forall\,j\in\mathcal{J}_{N},

where
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
denotes the set of all regular grid points
x_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
of the form
x_{j}=2\pi j/(2N+1)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(cp. equation (
B.11
)).

We also recall the following embedding theorem for the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
:

Theorem B.1

(Sobolev embedding)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
s>d/2
, we have a compact embedding
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}){\hookrightarrow}C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
into the space of continuous functions. In particular, there exists a constant
C=C(s,d)>0
, such that

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|v\|_{H^{s}},\quad\forall\,v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

The Sobolev embedding theorem implies in particular that the pseudo-spectral projection
\mathcal{I}_{N}
is well-defined as an operator
\mathcal{I}_{N}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for
s>d/2
. In the following theorem, we recall a well-known approximation error estimate for the pseudo-spectral projection
\mathcal{I}_{N}
:

Theorem B.2

(Pseudo-spectral approximation estimate)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
s>d/2
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the spectral interpolation operator
\mathcal{I}_{N}:H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is well-defined. Furthermore, there exists a constant
C=C(s,d)>0
, such that the following approximation error estimate holds

\|(1-\mathcal{I}_{N})v\|_{H^{\varsigma}}\leq CN^{-(s-\varsigma)}\|v\|_{H^{s}},\quad\forall\,v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

for any
\varsigma\in[0,s]
.

Finally, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we fix a regular grid
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
of values

(B.11)

\displaystyle x_{j}=\frac{2\pi j}{2N+1},

where the index
j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}
belong to the index set

(B.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{N}:=\{0,\dots,2N\}^{d}.

Recall the set of Fourier wave numbers (
2.10
) and we define the
discrete Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}_{N}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}
by

(B.13)

\mathcal{F}_{N}(v)(k):=\frac{1}{(2N+1)^{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}v_{j}e^{-2\pi i\langle j,k\rangle/{N}},

with inverse
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{K}_{N}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{J}_{N}}
,

(B.14)

\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{N}(\widehat{v})(j):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{N}}\widehat{v}_{k}e^{2\pi i\langle j,k\rangle/N}.

Lemma B.3

(Periodic extension operator)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded, Lipschitz domain. There exists a continuous, linear operator
\mathcal{E}:W^{m,p}(\Omega)\to W^{m,p}(B)
for any
m\geq 0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
where
B\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a hypercube with
\Omega\subset B

such that, for any
u\in W^{m,p}(\Omega)
,

(1)

\mathcal{E}(u)|_{\Omega}=u
,

(2)

\mathcal{E}(u)
is periodic on
B
.

Proof.

By
[
33
, Chapter 6, Theorem 5]
, there exists a continuous, linear operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}:W^{m,p}(\Omega)\to W^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u)|_{\Omega}=u
for any
u\in W^{m,p}(\Omega)
. Let
\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a mapping
whose zeroth level set defines a curve
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
such that
\Omega\subset\Omega^{\prime}
and
\text{dist}(\partial\Omega^{\prime},\partial\Omega)>0
where
\Omega^{\prime}
is a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
. Furthermore, suppose that the
first level set of
\Phi
defines a curve
\partial\Lambda
such that
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Lambda

and
\text{dist}(\partial\Lambda,\partial\Omega^{\prime})>0

where, again,
\Lambda
is a bounded domain with boundary
\partial\Lambda
.
For example, we may take
\Phi(x)=x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{d}^{2}-r
for some
r>0
large enough then
\Omega^{\prime}
is
d
-ball of radius
\sqrt{r}
enclosing
\Omega
, and
\Lambda
is a
d
-ball of radius
\sqrt{1+r}
clearly enclosing
\Omega^{\prime}
. We will now follow
the approach of
[
3
]
and construct a windowing function
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular, we
define

\rho(x)=H\bigl(1-2\Phi(x)\bigl),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

where

H(t)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1+S(t)\bigl),\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

and

S(t)=\begin{cases}-1,&t<-1\\
\text{erf}\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\right),&-1\leq t\leq 1\\
1,&t>1\end{cases},\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}

with
erf
, the Gauss error function,

\text{erf}(t)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{t}\text{e}^{-z^{2}}\>\text{d}z,\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

Define
B\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be a large enough
d
-cube such that
\Lambda\subset B
and
\text{dist}(\partial\Lambda,\partial B)>0.

It is easy to check that
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and we have that
\rho|_{\Omega}=\rho|_{\Omega^{\prime}}=1
and
\rho|_{B\setminus\overline{\Lambda}}=0
with
\rho
and all of its
derivatives vanishing on
\partial\Lambda
. Furthermore,

\|\rho\|_{C^{m}(B)}<\infty

for any
m\geq 0
. We can thus define
\mathcal{E}:W^{m,p}(\Omega)\to W^{m,p}(B)
by

\mathcal{E}(u)=\rho\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u),\qquad\forall u\in W^{m,p}(\Omega).

By Leibniz’s rule and the generalized triangle inequality,
there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(d,m,p)>0
(independent of
p
in the case
p=\infty
) such that

\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{W^{m,p}(B)}\leq C_{1}\|\rho\|_{C^{m}(B)}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u)\|_{W^{m,p}(B)}.

Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is bounded, there is a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}(u)\|_{W^{m,p}(B)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\|\rho\|_{C^{m}(B)}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u)\|_{W^{m,p}(B)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\|\rho\|_{C^{m}(B)}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u)\|_{W^{m,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{2}\|\rho\|_{C^{m}(B)}\|u\|_{W^{m,p}(\Omega)}

hence
\mathcal{E}
is a continuous, linear operator. Since
\rho|_{\Omega}=1
,
we immediately have
\mathcal{E}(u)|_{\Omega}=\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u)|_{\Omega}=u
. Since
\text{ supp}(\partial^{\alpha}\rho)\subseteq\overline{\Lambda}
for any multi-index
0\leq|\alpha|\leq m
, we conclude
that
\partial^{\alpha}\mathcal{E}(u)|_{B\setminus\overline{\Lambda}}=0
hence
\mathcal{E}(u)
is periodic on
B
as desired.

∎

Appendix C
Non-linear lifting and projection operators

Lemma C.1
.

Assume that the activation function
\sigma\in C^{2}
is non-linear and that
\|\sigma\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty
. Let
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
be a neural operator of the form
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}
, with Lipschitz continuous layers
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}:L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, and with linear lifting/projection operators
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{Q}
of the form (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively. For any
K\subset L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
compact and
\epsilon>0
, there exist neural networks
\widehat{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\times D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
,
\widehat{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}\times D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
with a single hidden layer and
0pt(\widehat{R}),0pt(\widehat{Q})\leq 2d_{v}
, such that the mapping
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathcal{A}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to\mathcal{U}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
defined by

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a):=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(a),

where
\mathcal{R}(a)(x):=\widehat{R}(a(x),x)
,
\mathcal{Q}(v):=\widehat{Q}(v(x),x)
, approximates
\mathcal{N}
to order
\epsilon
:

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{N}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

We recall that, by our definition of a neural operator, we have
\mathcal{R}(a)(x)=Ra(x)
,
\mathcal{Q}(v)(x)=Qv(x)
, where
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{a}}
,
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}
. By assumption on the non-linearity of
\sigma
, there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})\neq 0
. For any
h>0
, we now define

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \widehat{Q}_{h}(v,x)&:=\frac{\sigma(z_{0}+hQv)-\sigma(z_{0}-hQv)}{2h\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})},\\
\widehat{R}_{h}(a,x)&:=\frac{\sigma(z_{0}+hRa)-\sigma(z_{0}-hRa)}{2h\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})}.\end{aligned}\right.

We remark that in the above expressions, the addition of
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
hQv\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
,
hRa\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
is carried out componentwise, as is the evaluation of
\sigma
.

Since the interior layers
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{L}
are fixed, we introduce the short-hand notation
\mathcal{L}:=\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}
. By assumption on the activation function and the layers, we have that
\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
is a Lipschitz continuous mapping. Our goal is to show that for any
K\subset L^{2}
compact and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a sufficiently small
h>0
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{N}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon,

where
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(a):=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{h}(a)
. To this end, we note that for any
a\in K
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}(a)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}}.

Introducing
\widetilde{K}:=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}(K)
, we note that
\widetilde{K}\subset L^{2}
is compact, and

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{N}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v\in\widetilde{K}}\|\mathcal{Q}(v)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h})\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L})\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{R}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}}.

The proof of this lemma thus follows form the following three claims:

Claim 1:

There exists
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that
\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h})\leq C
for all
h>0
.

Claim 2:

For any
\epsilon>0
and
\widetilde{K}\subset L^{2}
compact, there exists
h>0
, such that

\sup_{v\in\widetilde{K}}\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)-\mathcal{Q}(v)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

Claim 3:

For any
\epsilon>0
and
K\subset L^{2}
compact, there exists
h>0
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{h}(a)-\mathcal{R}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

Clearly, the only difference between Claims 2 and 3 is notational, hence it suffices show Claims 1 and 2 to conclude the proof of the present lemma.

Proof of Claim 1:

We note that for any
v(x),v^{\prime}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
, we have

(C.1)

\displaystyle|\widehat{Q}_{h}(v(x),x)-\widehat{Q}_{h}(v^{\prime}(x),x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2h|\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})|}2\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)|hQ(v(x)-v^{\prime}(x))|

(C.2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)\|Q\|}{|\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})|}|v(x)-v^{\prime}(x)|,

where
|{\,\cdot\,}|
denotes the Euclidean norm and
\|Q\|
denotes the operator norm of
Q
. Hence, it follows that for any
v,v^{\prime}\in L^{2}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, we have

\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)\|Q\|}{|\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})|}\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}},

and thus,

\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h})\leq\frac{\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)\|Q\|}{|\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})|},

is bounded independently of
h>0
.

Proof of Claim 2:

It follows form Taylor expansion that
\widehat{Q}_{h}(v(x),x)=Qv(x)+hR(h;Qv(x))
, where the remainder
R
satisfies a uniform bound

|R(h;Qv(x))|\leq\frac{C\|\sigma\|_{C^{2}}}{|\sigma^{\prime}(z_{0})|}|Qv(x)|^{2},\quad\forall h\in(0,1],\;v(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}.

In particular, considering the
L^{2}
-norm of the left-hand side and assuming
x\mapsto v(x)
to be
bounded
, we conclude that there exists a constant
C=C(\sigma,\|Q\|)>0
, depending only on the activation function
\sigma
and the (Euclidean) operator norm
\|Q\|
of
Q:(\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}},|{\,\cdot\,}|)\to(\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}},|{\,\cdot\,}|)
, such that

(C.3)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)-\mathcal{Q}(v)\|_{L^{2}}=\|R(h;Qv)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{2}},\quad\forall\,v\in L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}.

Next, for
M>0
we introduce a cut-off operator
p_{M}:L^{2}\to L^{2}\cap L^{\infty}
, by

p_{M}(v(x)):=\begin{cases}v(x),&(|v(x)|\leq M),\\
0,&(|v(x)|>M).\end{cases}

Since
\widetilde{K}\subset L^{2}
is compact, it follows that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\sup_{v\in\widetilde{K}}\|v-p_{M}(v)\|_{L^{2}}=0.

We can thus choose
M>0
sufficiently large, such that

\sup_{v\in\widetilde{K}}\|v-p_{M}(v)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\left(\sup_{h\in(0,1]}\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h})+\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{Q})\right)},

where we note that
\sup_{h\in(0,1]}\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h})<\infty
, by Claim 1.
Furthermore, and again by compactness of
\widetilde{K}\subset L^{2}
, there exists a constant
\widetilde{M}>0
, such that

\sup_{v\in\widetilde{K}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq\widetilde{M}.

With this
\widetilde{M}
, our choice of
M
and the estimate (
C.3
) on the remainder
R
, it follows that for any
v\in\widetilde{K}
:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)-\mathcal{Q}(v)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)-\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(p_{M}(v))\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(p_{M}(v))-\mathcal{Q}(p_{M}(v))\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|\mathcal{Q}(p_{M}(v))-\mathcal{Q}(v)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h})+\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{Q})\right)\|v-p_{M}(v)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+Ch\|p_{M}(v)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|p_{M}(v)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon/2+CM\widetilde{M}h.

Finally, choosing
h=\epsilon/(2CM\widetilde{M})
implies that

\sup_{v\in\widetilde{K}}\|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}(v)-\mathcal{Q}(v)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

This concludes the proof.

∎

D.1.
Proof of Lemma
2.6

The proof of Lemma
2.6
will rely on the following technical lemma:

Lemma D.1
.

Let
s^{\prime}\geq 0
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
be given. Let
K\subset H^{s^{\prime}}
be compact, and assume that
\sigma\in C^{m}
, where
m>s^{\prime}
is integer. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a single-layer FNO
\mathcal{L}:H^{s^{\prime}}\to H^{s^{\prime}}
, such that

\sup_{v\in K}\|P_{N}v-\mathcal{L}(v)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon.

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

First, we note that the Fourier projection
P_{N}:H^{s^{\prime}}\to H^{s^{\prime}}
is a continuous operator, and hence the image
P_{N}K\subset H^{s^{\prime}}
is compact. Furthermore,
P_{N}
maps into a finite-dimensional subspace of
H^{s^{\prime}}
. Due to the norm-equivalence on finite-dimensional spaces, there thus exists
C_{0}=C_{0}(N,K)>0
, such that

(D.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in K}\|P_{N}v\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{0},\quad\sup_{v\in K}\|P_{N}v\|_{H^{m}}\leq C_{0}.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\sigma^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
. We define, for
h>0
,

(D.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{h}(x):=\frac{\sigma(x_{0}+hx)-\sigma(x_{0}-hx)}{2h\sigma^{\prime}(x_{0})}.

One readily shows that
\psi_{h}\in C^{m}
, and that there exists a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\sigma,C_{0})>0
, such that

(D.3)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{h}\|_{C^{m}([-C_{0},C_{0}])}\leq C_{1},\quad\forall\,h\in(0,1].

Furthermore, by Taylor expansion, we have

(D.4)

\displaystyle|\psi_{h}(x)-x|\leq Ch,\quad\forall\,x\in[-C_{0},C_{0}],\;\forall\,h\in(0,1].

By the composition rule for Sobolev functions, we have that
\psi_{h}\circ P_{N}a\in H^{m}
, for
P_{N}a\in H^{m}
, and there exists a constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(C_{1},C_{0})>0
, such that

(D.5)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{h}(P_{N}v)\|_{H^{m}}\leq C_{2},\quad\forall\,v\in K.

We finally observe that the mapping
v\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{h}(v):=\psi_{h}(P_{N}v)
can be represented by a single-layer FNO, and by (
D.5
), we have

(D.6)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{h}(v)\|_{H^{m}}\leq C_{2},\quad\forall\,v\in K.

From the interpolation inequality between Sobolev spaces, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{h}(v)-P_{N}v\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{L}_{h}(v)-P_{N}v\|_{L^{2}}^{\theta}\|\mathcal{L}_{h}(v)-P_{N}v\|_{H^{m}}^{1-\theta},

where
\theta=1-s^{\prime}/m>0
. By (
D.6
) and (
D.1
), the second factor can be bounded independently of
h>0
. By (
D.4
) and (

\delta=\delta(M,s^{\prime},\mathbb{T}^{d},\epsilon)>0
at the end of this proof. We emphasize that
\delta
will depend only on parameters already introduced at this point of the proof. By assumption of the validity of the universal approximation theorem for
\Psi
-FNOs for
s^{\prime}=0
, we can choose
\widetilde{N}_{0}=\widetilde{N}_{0}(\mathcal{G},K,\delta)\in\mathbb{N}
, depending on
\mathcal{G}
,
K
and
\delta
, such that for any
N\geq\widetilde{N}_{0}
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\delta.

We define
N_{0}:=\max(\widetilde{N}_{0},M)
. By our choice of
\widetilde{N}_{0}
and
M
, the constant
N_{0}
depends only on the underlying operator
\mathcal{G}
, the compact set
K
and the parameter
\delta>0
. Let
N\geq N_{0}
. We claim that if
\delta=\delta(M,s^{\prime},\mathbb{T}^{d},\epsilon)>0
is been chosen sufficiently small, then for any
N\geq N_{0}
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
satisfying (
D.27
).

To see this, let
N\geq N_{0}
, and let
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
be a
\Psi
-FNO such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\delta
. Then we have

(D.29)

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in K}\|P_{M}\mathcal{G}(a)-P_{M}\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq CN^{s^{\prime}}\delta,

where
C=C(\mathbb{T}^{d},s^{\prime})>0
is independent of
N
and
\delta
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest natural number strictly bigger than
s^{\prime}
and
d/2
, i.e.
m>\max(s^{\prime},d/2)
. Let

(D.30)

\displaystyle K^{\prime}:=\mathcal{N}(K)\subset L^{2}_{N},

be the compact image of
K\subset H^{s}
under the (continuous) mapping
\mathcal{N}:H^{s}\to L^{2}_{N}
. We note that since
L^{2}_{N}
is a finite-dimensional space, the
L^{2}
-norm is equivalent to the
H^{m}
-norm on
L^{2}_{N}
. This implies that
K^{\prime}\subset H^{m}
is also compact when considered as a subset of
H^{m}
, with respect to norm
\|{\,\cdot\,}\|_{H^{m}}
. By Lemma
D.1
, there exists a single-layer FNO
\mathcal{L}:H^{m}\to H^{m}
, such that

(D.31)

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in K^{\prime}}\|P_{M}v-\mathcal{L}(v)\|_{H^{m}}\leq\delta.

We define a
\Psi
-FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
by the following composition

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:=\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{N},

where
\mathcal{I}_{N}
is the pseudo-spectral projection operator. We note that, since
N\geq N_{0}\geq M
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{N}P_{M}=P_{M}
, and recall that for
m>d/2
, the mapping
\mathcal{I}_{N}:H^{m}\to H^{m}
is continuous, with an operator norm that can be bounded independently of

.
Hence, we can estimate, for any

\displaystyle\|P_{M}\mathcal{N}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ P_{M}\mathcal{N}(a)-\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\underset{\downarrow}{(m>s^{\prime})}}}{\leq}\|\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ P_{M}\mathcal{N}(a)-\mathcal{I}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{N}(a)\|_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|P_{M}v-\mathcal{L}(v)\|_{H^{m}},

where
C=C(\mathbb{T}^{d},m)>0
is independent of
M
and
N
, and where
v:=\mathcal{N}(a)\in K^{\prime}
, with

defined in (

). From (

), we conclude that

(D.32)

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in K}\|P_{M}\mathcal{N}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq C\delta,

where
C=C(\mathbb{T}^{d},m)>0
is independent of
M
,
N
and
\delta
. In fact, since

is defined as the smallest integer
>\max(s^{\prime},d/2)
, the above constant only depends on
s^{\prime}
and the dimension

. Combining (
D.28
), (
D.29
) and (

), we find that for
N\geq N_{0}
and for any

, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon/2+C(1+N^{s^{\prime}})\delta,

where

is a constant independent of

.
Thus, if we choose

at the beginning of this proof sufficiently small to ensure that

then we conclude that for any

, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:L^{2}_{N}\to L^{2}_{N}
, such that

\sup_{a\in K}\|\mathcal{G}(a)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon.

This concludes the proof.
∎

D.7.
Technical Results on the structure of
\Psi
-FNOs

Recall section
2.5.1
where we introduced
\sigma
layers and
\mathcal{F}
-layers and claimed that
\Psi
-FNOs can be decomposed in terms of these layers. We have the following series of lemmas, which make this observation precise.

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
be a (ordinary) neural network with activation function
\sigma
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the mapping

\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}),\quad a(x)\mapsto\mathcal{N}(a)(x):=\mathcal{I}_{N}\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a(x)),

can be represented by a
\Psi
-FNO, with

0pt(\mathcal{N})=|\mathcal{J}_{N}|0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}),\quad 0pt(\mathcal{N})=0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}),\quad\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N})=0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}).

The proof is straight-forward and completely analogous to the same statement for FNOs in Remark
2.3
. We also note the following lemma, which allows us in practice to replace
\mathcal{F}
-layers by proper FNO layers:

Lemma D.3

(Linear approximation lemma)
.

Assume that the activation function
\sigma\in C^{2}
is twice continuously differentiable and non-constant. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
be a linear
\Psi
-FNO layer of the form

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}),\;\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(v_{N}):=Wv_{N}(x_{j})+b_{j}+\mathcal{F}_{N}^{-1}(P\mathcal{F}_{N}v_{N})_{j}.

Then there exists a constant
C>0
, such that for any
\epsilon,\,B>0
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, such that

\sup_{\|v_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B}\|\mathcal{N}(v_{N})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(v_{N})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon,

and

0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq CN^{d},\quad 0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq C,\quad\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N})\leq C.

Proof.

This follows from the observation that, by assumption, there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
\sigma^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
. By Taylor expansion, we have

\frac{\sigma(x_{0}+hy)-\sigma(x_{0}-hy)}{h\sigma^{\prime}(x_{0})}=y+R(h,y),

where
|R(h,y)|\leq C\overline{B}^{2}h
for any
y\in[-\overline{B},\overline{B}]
and
0<h\leq 1
. Replacing
y
by the output of the linear layer
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
, and choosing
\overline{B}
sufficiently large, we obtain with

\mathcal{N}_{h}(v_{n}):=\frac{\sigma(x_{0}+h\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(v_{N}))-\sigma(x_{0}-h\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(v_{N}))}{h\sigma^{\prime}(x_{0})},

that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(v_{N})-\mathcal{N}_{h}(v_{N})\|_{L^{2}}=O(h),

uniformly for all
v_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, such that
\|v_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
, and for
0<h\leq 1
. In particular, choosing
h=h(\epsilon)>0
sufficiently small, we can ensure that

\|\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(v_{N})-\mathcal{N}_{h}(v_{N})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon,

for all
v_{n}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}})
, such that
\|v_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
. The proof is concluded by observing that the mapping
v_{N}\mapsto\mathcal{N}_{h}(v_{N})
defines a
\Psi
-FNO, and that the size (width, depth, lift) is uniformly bounded in
h
.
∎

According to Lemma
D.2
, any composition of
\sigma
-layers can be identified with a
\Psi
-FNO. Lemma
D.3
shows that we can approximate
\mathcal{F}
-layers to arbitrary accuracy with a
\Psi
-FNO of uniformly bounded size. In the following lemma, we record the simple fact that a composition of
\Psi
-FNOs is again representable by a
\Psi
-FNO:

Lemma D.4

(Composition Lemma)
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{N}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{N}_{p}
be
\Psi
-FNOs, defined with respect to the same grid
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
, and defining operators
\mathcal{N}_{k}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{k-1}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}})
for
d_{0},\dots,d_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the composition
\mathcal{N}_{p}\circ\dots\mathcal{N}_{1}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}})
is well-defined. Then there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}})
, such that
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}_{p}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}
, and such that

0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p}0pt(\mathcal{N}_{k})
,
0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq\max_{k=1,\dots,p}0pt(\mathcal{N}_{k})
and
\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N})\leq\max_{k=1,\dots,p}\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N}_{k})
.

The proof of the previous lemma is straight-forward, and only requires a padding of each layer with zeros to achieve a uniform lifting dimension
d_{v}
across all layers. The following lemma will be central for our approximation and complexity estimates:

Lemma D.5

(Replacement Lemma)
.

Assume that
\sigma
is Lipschitz continuous. For
p\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{p}
be continuous operators
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{k}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{k-1}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}})
for
d_{0},\dots,d_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the composition
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:=\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{p}\circ\dots\circ\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}})
is well-defined. Assume that there exist constants
D_{k},W_{k},L_{k}>0
,
k=1,\dots,p
, such that for any
\epsilon,\,M>0
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}_{k}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{k-1}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}})
, such that

\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{k}(u)-\mathcal{N}_{k}(u)\|_{L^{2}_{N}}\leq\epsilon,

and
0pt(\mathcal{N}_{k})\leq D_{k}
,
0pt(\mathcal{N}_{k})\leq W_{k}
,
\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N}_{k})\leq L_{k}
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
and
M>0
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}})
, such that

\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(u)-\mathcal{N}(u)\|_{L^{2}_{N}}\leq\epsilon,

and
0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p}D_{k}
,
0pt(\mathcal{N})\leq\max_{k=1,\dots,p}W_{k}
,
\mathrm{lift}(\mathcal{N})\leq\max_{k=1,\dots,p}L_{k}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
p\in\mathbb{N}
. We start the induction at
p=2
: Let
\epsilon>0
and
M>0
be given, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
,
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
satisfy the hypotheses of this lemma. Since
B_{M}(0)\subset L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})
, the closed ball of radius
M
, is compact in the finite-dimensional space
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})
, and since
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
is continuous, it follows that the image
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(B_{M}(0))
is also compact; in particular, there exists
M_{1}>0
, such that
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(B_{M}(0))\subset B_{M_{1}}(0)
. By assumption, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}_{2}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
, such that

\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2M_{1}}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(u)-\mathcal{N}_{2}(u)\|_{L^{2}_{N}}\leq\epsilon/2.

We note that (for Lipschitz continuous
\sigma
)
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
is Lipschitz continuous on
B_{2M_{1}}(0)\subset L^{2}_{N}
. Let
\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})
denote the corresponding Lipschitz constant. By assumption on
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}_{1}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
, such that

\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(u)-\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}_{N}}\leq\min\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})},M_{1}\right).

Note that this estimate implies in particular that

\|\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(u)-\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2M_{1}.

Thus, we can estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(u)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{N}_{2}\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(u)-\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)-\mathcal{N}_{2}\circ\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})\,\sup_{\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq M}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(u)-\mathcal{N}_{1}(u)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sup_{\|v\|\leq 2M_{1}}\|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(v)-\mathcal{N}_{2}(v)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle<\epsilon.

The estimate on the depth, width and lift is immediate (cp. Lemma
D.4
). This proves the base case
p=2
of the induction.

Let now
p>2
, and assume that the claim holds for the composition of
p-1
operators. Given
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{p}
as in the statement of the lemma, we can apply the induction hypothesis for the composition of
p-1
operators, to see that the two continuous operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}:=\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}:=(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{p}\circ\dots\circ\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})
fulfill all assumptions of the lemma (with
p=2
), and with depths, widths and lifts of the approximating FNOs given by
\widetilde{D}_{1}=D_{1}
,
\widetilde{W}_{1}=W_{1}
,
\widetilde{L}_{1}=L_{1}
, and
\widetilde{D}_{2}=\sum_{k=2}^{p}D_{k}
,
\widetilde{W}_{2}=\max_{k=2,\dots,p}W_{k}
,
\widetilde{L}_{2}=\max_{k=2,\dots,p}L_{k}
. The proof of the induction step now follows from the base case (with
p=2
) already considered above.
∎

Appendix E
Technical Results and Proofs for Section
3.1

In this section, we collect some technical results and proofs for the material in section
3.1
of the main text. We start with the following Lemma on the contraction property of the map (
3.9
).

Lemma E.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k>d/2+1
, and let
s>d/2+k
be given. Assume that the coefficient
a\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
satisfies the
\lambda
-coercivity condition (
3.6
) for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and that the right-hand side of the stationary Darcy equation (
3.5
) belongs to the Sobolev space
f\in H^{k-1}
. Then there exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(s,d,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any
N\geq N_{0}
, we have
a_{N}\geq\lambda/2
and the mapping
F_{N}
defined by (
3.9
) is a contraction, with

\mathrm{Lip}\left(F_{N}:\dot{H}^{1}\to\dot{H}^{1}\right)\leq 1-\frac{\lambda}{2}.

Proof.

As
u_{N}\mapsto F_{N}(u_{N})
is an affine mapping, we can express the Lipschitz constant in terms of the following supremum

\mathrm{Lip}\left(F_{N}:\dot{H}^{1}\to\dot{H}^{1}\right)=\sup_{u_{N}\in\dot{H}^{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\|\dot{P}_{N}(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla\cdot(\widetilde{a}_{N}\nabla u_{N})\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}}{\|u_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}}.

We now observe that

\displaystyle\left\|\dot{P}_{N}(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla\cdot(\widetilde{a}_{N}\nabla u_{N})\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla\cdot(\widetilde{a}_{N}\nabla u_{N})\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\widetilde{a}_{N}\nabla u_{N}\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},

for all
u_{N}\in\dot{H}^{1}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. To finish the proof, we note that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{a}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}},

where the first term is bounded by
1-\lambda
by the coercivity assumption. By our definition of
\widetilde{a}_{N}
(cp. equation (
3.8
)), we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widetilde{a}-\dot{P}_{N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|\dot{P}_{N}\widetilde{a}-\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

But by the spectral/pseudo-spectral approximation estimates, we now have

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{a}-\dot{P}_{N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,\delta}\left\|\widetilde{a}-\dot{P}_{N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{H^{d/2+\delta}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s,d,\delta}N^{-(s-d/2-\delta)}\|a\|_{H^{s}},

and

\displaystyle\left\|\dot{P}_{N}\widetilde{a}-\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,\delta}\left\|\dot{P}_{N}\widetilde{a}-\dot{P}_{N}\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{H^{d/2+\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widetilde{a}-\mathcal{I}_{2N}\widetilde{a}\right\|_{H^{d/2+\delta}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s,d,\delta}N^{-(s-d/2-\delta)}\|a\|_{H^{s}}.

To be definite, let us choose
\delta:=(s-d/2)/2
. In particular, it follows that there exists a constant
C=C(s,d)
, and
N_{0}=N_{0}(s,d,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\|\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2},

for
N\geq N_{0}
, and hence
a_{N}=1+\widetilde{a}_{N}\geq 1+\widetilde{a}-\lambda/2\geq\lambda/2
, and

\mathrm{Lip}(F_{N})\leq\|\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\widetilde{a}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{a}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1-\frac{\lambda}{2},

for all
N\geq N_{0}
. The claim follows.
∎

Next, we provide the detailed proof of Theorem
3.3
which guarantees convergence of the algorithm
3.2
to the solutions of the Darcy equation (
3.5
).

E.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Proof.

Choose
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
as in Lemma
E.1
.

Step 1:

Well-posedness and error estimate of the FG approximation (
3.7
).

We consider the bilinear form
B:\dot{H}^{1}_{N}\times\dot{H}^{1}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

B(u_{N},w_{N}):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a_{N}(x)\nabla u_{N}(x)\cdot\nabla w_{N}(x)\,dx.

We note that
u_{N}
solves (
3.7
), if and only if,

(E.1)

\displaystyle B(u_{N},w_{N})=\langle f_{N},w_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}},

for all
w_{N}\in\dot{H}^{1}_{N}
. By Lemma
E.1
, we have
a_{N}\geq\lambda/2
for all
N\geq N_{0}
, and hence

B(w_{N},w_{N})\geq\lambda/2\|w_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}.

Thus,
B
is a coercive bilinear form. The existence and uniqueness of a solution
u_{N}^{\ast}\in\dot{H}^{1}_{N}
of (
E.1
) follows from this for any right-hand side
f_{N}
. Furthermore, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq 2\|u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\lambda}B(u_{N}^{\ast},u_{N}^{\ast})=\frac{4}{\lambda}\langle u_{N}^{\ast},f_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{\lambda}\|u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{{H}^{1}}\|f_{N}\|_{{H}^{-1}},

and thus,
\|u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{{H}^{1}}\leq 4\lambda^{-1}\|f_{N}\|_{{H}^{-1}}
.

It is also straightforward to show that by inductive differentiation of (
3.5
), one can also obtain higher-order estimates for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 1
for the (non-discretized) elliptic equation (
3.5
), of the form:

(E.2)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{k+1}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{k-1}},

where
C=C(k,d,\|a\|_{C^{k}})>0
.

To prove the claimed error estimate, we note that if
u
solves (
3.5
), then
P_{N}u\in\dot{H}^{1}_{N}
solves

\displaystyle-P_{N}\nabla\cdot(a_{N}\nabla P_{N}u)

\displaystyle=P_{N}f+P_{N}\nabla\cdot((a-a_{N})\nabla P_{N}u)

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{N}\nabla\cdot(a\nabla(1-P_{N})u).

It thus follows that
w_{N}=P_{N}u-u_{N}^{\ast}
solves

\displaystyle-P_{N}\nabla\cdot(a_{N}\nabla w_{N})

\displaystyle=(P_{N}f-f_{N})+P_{N}\nabla\cdot((a-a_{N})\nabla P_{N}u)

\displaystyle\qquad+P_{N}\nabla\cdot(a\nabla(1-P_{N})u)

\displaystyle=:(I)+(II)+(III).

The stability estimate then implies that
\|w_{N}\|_{{H}^{1}}\leq 4\lambda^{-1}\|(I)+(II)+(III)\|_{H^{-1}}
, can be bounded in terms of the
H^{-1}
-norm of the right-hand side. We can now estimate

\displaystyle\|(I)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\|(P_{N}f-f_{N})\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(1-P_{N})f\|_{H^{-1}}+\|f-f_{N}\|_{H^{-1}}\lesssim_{d,s}N^{-k}\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}.

The last inequality follows from the fact that
f\in H^{k-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, for
k-1>d/2
, and the (pseudo-)spectral approximation estimate.
For the second term, we fix a small
\delta>0
, and obtain

\displaystyle\|(II)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(a-a_{N})\nabla P_{N}u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\|a-a_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,\delta,\lambda}\|a-a_{N}\|_{H^{d/2+\delta}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}},

where we have used the embedding
H^{d/2+\delta}{\hookrightarrow}L^{\infty}
in the last step. Assuming that
\delta>0
is chosen sufficiently small, so that
s\geq d/2+\delta
, we can then estimate

\|(II)\|_{H^{-1}}\lesssim_{s,d}\lambda^{-1}N^{-(s-d/2-\delta)}\|a\|_{H^{s}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}}.

Finally, the third term can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\|(III)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\|a\nabla(1-P_{N})u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\|a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|(1-P_{N})u\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s,d}N^{-k}\|a\|_{H^{s}}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s,d,\lambda,k,\|a\|_{C^{k}}}N^{-k}\|a\|_{H^{s}}\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}.

In the last step, we used the higher regularity estimate (
E.2
). We note that for this step we assume that
a\in H^{s}
,
s>d/2+k
, so that we have an embedding
H^{s}{\hookrightarrow}C^{k}
. Under this condition, we can further estimate
(II)
by

\displaystyle\|(II)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s,d}N^{-(s-d/2-\delta)}\lambda^{-1}\|a\|_{H^{s}}\|f\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s,d,\lambda,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\|f\|_{H^{-1}}}N^{-k}.

Combining the above estimates for
(I)
,
(II)
and
(III)
, we conclude that for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
s>d/2+k
, we have

\|w_{N}\|_{H^{1}}=\|u_{N}^{\ast}-P_{N}u\|_{H^{1}}\leq CN^{-k},

provided that
a\in H^{s}
,
f\in H^{k-1}
, where
C=C\left(s,d,\lambda,k,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}\right)>0
is independent of
N
.

The proof is now finished by observing that

\|u-u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|(1-P_{N})u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|P_{N}u-u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{H^{1}}^{2},

and that, by the higher-regularity estimate (
E.2
), we have

\|(1-P_{N})u\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim_{k,d}N^{-k}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}}\lesssim_{s,k,d,\lambda,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\|f\|_{H^{k-1}}}N^{-k}.

Thus, it follows that there exists a constant
C=C(s,k,d,\lambda,\|a\|_{H^{s}},\|f\|_{H^{k-1}})>0
, such that

(E.3)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{N}^{\ast}\|_{H^{1}}\leq CN^{-k},

where
u^{\ast}_{N}
is the solution of the Fourier-Galerkin discretization (
3.7
).

Step 2:

Picard iteration estimate.

The output of Algorithm
3.2
is obtained by Picard iteration, i.e. by
K
-fold application of the mapping
F_{N}:\dot{H}^{1}_{N}\to\dot{H}^{1}_{N}
, yielding a recursively defined sequence

, and

, for

. Since
F_{N}
is a contraction with

for

, by Lemma
E.1
, and since

is the unique fixed point of

, this implies that

\|u^{K}_{N}-u^{\ast}_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq(1-\lambda/2)^{K}\|u^{\ast}_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}.

Using the definition of
H^{1}
,
\dot{H}^{1}
, and the regularity estimate (
E.2
), we can further estimate

\|u^{K}_{N}-u^{\ast}_{N}\|_{{H}^{1}}\leq 2\|u^{K}_{N}-u^{\ast}_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq 8\lambda^{-1}(1-\lambda/2)^{K}\|f_{N}\|_{H^{-1}}.

By definition of

and hence

. This yields

Combining Steps 1 and 2, implies that there exists a constant

, depending on

,

,

,

and

for all

, where

is the output of Algorithm

. This is the claimed estimate.
∎

E.2.
Neural network approximation of quadratic non-linearities

Our next aim is to prove Theorem

. As stated in the main text, the proof relies crucially on the following Lemmas, which show that neural networks can efficiently approximate certain quadratic non-linearities. We start with the following result,

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\sigma\in C^{3}
be a activation function. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
C=C(d)>0
, such that for any
\epsilon>0
and
B>0

(1)

a neural network
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that

\sup_{|a|,|b|\leq B}|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(a,b)-ab|\leq\epsilon,

and
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1})\leq C
,
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1})\leq C
,

(2)

a neural network
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\sup_{|a|,\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq B}|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(a,v)-av|\leq\epsilon,

and
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})\leq C
,
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})\leq C
,

(3)

a neural network
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\sup_{\|v\|_{\ell^{2}},\|U\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\leq B}|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{3}(v,U)-v\cdot U|\leq\epsilon,

and
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{3})\leq C
,
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{3})\leq C
.

Proof.

Points (2) and (3) easily follow from (1), by parallelizing multiple networks
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
. To see the first claim (1), we note that the quadratic function
y\mapsto y^{2}
can be approximated for
y\in[-B,B]
,
B>0
, to arbitrary precision by finite differences

y^{2}=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma(x+hy)-2\sigma(x)+\sigma(x-hy)}{h^{2}\sigma^{(2)}(x)}}_{\mathrm{sq}_{h}(y)}+R(h;y),

where we assume that
x
is chosen so that the second derivative
\sigma^{(2)}(x)\neq 0
, and
|R(h,y)|\leq Ch
for all
y\in[-B,B]
, and
C=C(B)
. Finally, following
[
34
]
, we observe that the product
ab
of two numbers
a
,
b
can be expressed in the form

ab=\frac{1}{2}\left((a+b)^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{sq}_{h}(a+b)-\mathrm{sq}_{h}(a)-\mathrm{sq}_{h}(b)\right)+\widetilde{R}(h;a,b),

where
\widetilde{R}
is related to
R
, and there exists a constant
C=C(B)
, such that
|\widetilde{R}(h;a,b)|\leq Ch
, for all
a,b\in[-B,B]
. Since
\mathrm{sq}_{h}
is a neural network of finite width and depth (independent of
h
), and since the last expression

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(a,b):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{sq}_{h}(a+b)-\mathrm{sq}_{h}(a)-\mathrm{sq}_{h}(b)\right),

is simply a linear combination of
\mathrm{sq}_{h}
, we conclude that
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}
is a neural network of uniformly bounded width and depth (uniform in
h
), and for sufficiently small
h>0
, we have

\sup_{a,b\in[-B,B]}|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{h}(a,b)-ab|\leq\epsilon.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Using the above lemma, one can prove the following result,

Lemma E.3
.

Assume that the activation function
\sigma\in C^{3}
is three times continuously differentiable and non-linear. There exists a constant
C>0
, such that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and for any
\epsilon,B>0
, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})

such that we have

\left\|P_{N}\left(a_{N}\nabla u_{N}\right)-\mathcal{N}(a_{N},u_{N})\right\|_{L^{2}_{N}}\leq\epsilon,

for all trigonometric polynomials
a_{N},u_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})
of degree

\|a_{N}\|_{L^{2}},\|u_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B

Proof.

First, we observe that there exists a linear FNO layer
\mathcal{L}:L^{2}_{2N}\to L^{2}_{2N}
, with a suitable choice of the Fourier multiplier matrix

\mathcal{L}(u_{N})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{2N}(P\mathcal{F}(u_{N}))=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{2N}\left(\sum_{|k|_{\infty}\leq 2N}ik\widehat{u}_{k}e^{i\langle k,x\rangle}\right)=\nabla u_{N},

is satisfied
exactly
for all

. We also note that if
\|u_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
, then
\|\nabla u_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq NB
. By the fact that all norms are equivalent on the finite-dimensional space
L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
(depending on
N
), such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|\nabla u_{N}(x)\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\nabla u_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}NB,

whenever
\|u_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
. We similarly see that by norm equivalence on
L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R})
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
(depending on
N
), such that we also have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}|a_{N}(x)|\leq C^{\prime\prime}\|a_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}B,

for any
\|a_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq B
. Let

. By Lemma
E.2

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}),0pt(\widehat{\mathcal{N}})\leq C(d)
(with
C=C(d)

independent
of
N

By Lemmas
D.2
,
D.3
and the composition lemma
D.4
, the composition

(a_{N},u_{N})\mapsto(a_{N},\mathcal{L}(u_{N}))=(a_{N},\nabla u_{N})\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(a_{N},\nabla u_{N}),

can be represented by a
\Psi
-FNO

, and by construction, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|a_{N}(x)\nabla u_{N}(x)-\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(a_{N},u_{N})(x)\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\epsilon,

for all
a_{N}
,
u_{N}\in L^{2}_{2N}

\|a_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
,

0pt(\widetilde{\mathcal{N}})\leq CN^{d},\quad 0pt(\widetilde{\mathcal{N}})\leq C,\quad\mathrm{lift}(\widetilde{\mathcal{N}})\leq C.

Finally, we note that the projection
P_{N}:L^{2}_{2N}\to L^{2}_{2N}

\Psi
-FNO layer
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
, and by Lemmas
D.3

, there exists a
\Psi
-FNO
\mathcal{N}:L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{2N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})

\|\mathcal{N}(a_{N},u_{N})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(a_{N},u_{N})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon

\displaystyle\|P_{N}(a_{N}\nabla u_{N})-\mathcal{N}(a_{N},u_{N})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|P_{N}(a_{N}\nabla u_{N})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}(a_{N},u_{N})\|_{L^{2}}

F.1.
Properties of the Pseudo-spectral scheme (
3.2.1
)

Our first aim is to show that the implicit operator equation that defines the scheme (
3.2.1
). To this end, we have following lemmas,

Lemma F.1
.

If
v\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
{\tau}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}N\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then we have

\|{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{N}(v\cdot\nabla w)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{L^{2}},

for all
w\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular, this estimate holds provided that

{\tau}\|v\|_{L^{2}}N^{d/2+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

We first note that for any
v\in L^{2}_{N}
, we have

{\tau}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}N\leq{\tau}\|v\|_{L^{2}}N^{d/2+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

The claim is now an immediate consequence of the fact that

\displaystyle\|{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{N}(v\cdot\nabla w)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\tau}v\cdot\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{\tau}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq{\tau}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}N\|w\|_{L^{2}},

for any
w\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

We can now state the following lemma on the well-posedness:

Lemma F.2
.

Let
U>0
. If
\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
for
n=0,\dots,n_{T}
, and if the CFL condition

(F.1)

\displaystyle{\tau}UN^{d/2+1}\leq\frac{1}{2},

is satisfied, then the recursion (
3.2.1
) is well-defined.

Proof.

The recursion (
3.2.1
) can be written in the form

(F.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}u^{n+1}_{N}=u^{n},

where the operator
\mathcal{T}_{n}:\dot{L}^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})\to\dot{L}^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
is given by

\mathcal{T}_{n}w_{N}:=w_{N}+{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla w_{N}\right)-\nu{\tau}\Delta w_{N}.

The claimed well-posedness of the recursion follows from the fact that, under the CFL assumption (
F.1
), the operator
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is invertible: Indeed, by Lemma
F.1
, this implies that for any
w_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
, we have

\|{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla w_{N}\right)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}.

But then, we have for any
w_{N}\in L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
, that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}_{n}w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\|(1-{\tau}\nu\Delta)w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}-\|{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla w_{N}\right)\|_{L^{2}_{N}}

\displaystyle\geq\|w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\|w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|w_{N}\|_{L^{2}},

where, in the first step, we have used the fact that
-{\tau}\nu\Delta
is a non-negative operator. In particular, the estimate
\|\mathcal{T}_{n}w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\|w_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
implies that
\mathcal{T}_{n}:L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})\to L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
is injective. Since
L^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
is finite-dimensional, we conclude that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is actually invertible, and hence the recursion (
3.2.1
) is well-defined, i.e. it can be solved for
u^{n+1}_{N}
, given
u^{n}_{N}
.
∎

By Lemma
F.2
, to prove the well-posedness of the scheme (
3.2.1
), it remains to be shown that with a suitable choice of the time-step
{\tau}
, we have a uniform

-energy bound of the form
\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
, for some
U>0
. This is the content of the following lemma:

Lemma F.3
.

Assume that
\|u^{0}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
, then the first-order scheme (
3.2.1
) is well-defined for any time-step satisfying the CFL condition
{\tau}UN^{d/2+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and we have
\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u^{0}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
for all
n=0,\dots,n_{T}
.

Proof.

We show inductively that if
\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
, then
\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
. The well-posedness then follows from Lemma
F.2
. To see that
\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
, we integrate (
3.2.1
) against
u^{n+1}_{N}
, to find

\displaystyle\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle u^{n+1}_{N},u^{n}_{N}\rangle-{\tau}\left\langle u^{n+1}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla u^{n+1}_{N}\right)\right\rangle-\nu{\tau}\|\nabla u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle u^{n+1}_{N},u^{n}_{N}\rangle-{\tau}\underbrace{\left\langle u^{n+1}_{N},u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla u^{n+1}_{N}\right\rangle}_{=0}-\nu{\tau}\|\nabla u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\langle u^{n+1}_{N},u^{n}_{N}\rangle\right|\leq\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}.

This proves the claim.
∎

Next, we have the following Lemma on the convergence of the iterations in algorithm

,

Lemma F.4
.

Given
\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq U
, assume that the CFL condition
{\tau}UN^{d/2+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
is satisfied. Define a recursive sequence
w^{n,k}_{N}\in\dot{L}^{2}_{N}(\mathbb{T}^{d};{\mathrm{div}})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by
w^{n,0}_{N}:=0
, and
w^{n,k+1}_{N}:=F(w^{n,k}_{N})
, where
F
is defined by (
3.19
). Then, we have
\|u^{n+1}_{N}-w^{n,k}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-k}\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\|w^{n,k}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq(1+2^{-k})\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
F.1
, and the fact that
(1-\nu{\tau}\Delta)^{-1}
is a contraction, it follows that
\mathrm{Lip}(F)\leq\frac{1}{2}
. By Picard iteration, and recalling that
u^{n+1}_{N}
is the unique fixed point of the recursion
w^{n,k}_{N}
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
), it immediately follows that

\|u^{n+1}_{N}-w^{n,k}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{k}}\|u^{n+1}_{N}-w^{n,0}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}}{2^{k}}.

The last step is a consequence of the a priori
L^{2}
-bound
\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}
proven in Lemma
F.3
. In particular, this estimate implies that

\|w^{n,k}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}+\|w^{n,k}_{N}-u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq(1+2^{-k})\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}.

∎

Remark F.5

Note that as a consequence of Lemma

\displaystyle\|u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|w^{n,\kappa_{0}}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq(1+2^{-\kappa_{0}})\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left(1+\frac{{\tau}^{2}}{T^{2}}\right)\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\dots\leq\left(1+\frac{{\tau}^{2}}{T^{2}}\right)^{n}\|u^{0}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\exp\left(\frac{{\tau}}{T}\right)U\leq eU,

for
n=0,\dots,n_{T}
. In particular, this ensures that the CFL condition (

) is satisfied for all

, generated by Algorithm
3.9
.

F.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.10

In this appendix, we provide a detailed proof of the convergence estimate of Theorem
3.10
, for the first-order scheme defined by Algorithm
3.9
.

To this end, let
u(t)
be an exact solution of (
3.12
), satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3.10
, and let
u^{0}_{N},\dots,u^{n_{T}}_{N}
denote the sequence generated by Algorithm
3.9
. For any
n=0,\dots,n_{T}-1
, we denote by
u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}
the solution of a single time-step with the semi-implicit scheme (
3.2.1
), starting from
u^{n}_{N}
, i.e. satisfying

(F.3)

\displaystyle\frac{u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}-u^{n}_{N}}{{\tau}}+\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}\right)=\nu\Delta u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}.

We recall that by Lemma
F.4
and Remark
F.5
, we have a uniform bound
\|u^{n}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq eU
. Since by assumption, the time-step
{\tau}eUN^{d/2+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
satisfies the relevant CFL condition, it follows that a unique solution
u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}
exists for all
n
. We also recall that by Lemma
F.4
, and by our choice of the number of iteration steps
\kappa
in Algorithm
3.9
, we have

\|u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}-u^{n+1}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2^{-k}eU\leq C{\tau}^{2},

where
C>0
depends only on
U
and the final time
T
.

Our first goal is to derive an estimate on the magnification of the approximation error due to a single timestep
u^{n}_{N}\mapsto u^{n+1}_{N}
. Let
u(t)
be the exact solution of (
3.12
). Observing that

(F.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|u^{n+1}_{N}-u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{2}}&\leq\|u^{n+1}_{N}-u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}+\|u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}-u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\leq C{\tau}^{2}+\|u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}-u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{2}},\end{aligned}

we only need to consider the error introduced by a single time-step
u^{n}_{N}\mapsto u^{n+1,\ast}_{N}
of the semi-implicit scheme (
3.2.1
). To this end, we can write

\frac{u(t^{n+1})-u(t^{n})}{{\tau}}+\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u(t^{n})\cdot\nabla u(t^{n+1})\right)=\nu\Delta u(t^{n+1})+\mathcal{E}^{n},

where
\mathcal{E}^{n}
collects all error terms:

\mathcal{E}^{n}=\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\tau}+\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL}+\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P}+\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\nu},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{u(t^{n+1})-u(t^{n})}{{\tau}}-\partial_{t}u(t^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(u(t^{n})\cdot\nabla\left(u(t^{n+1})-u(t^{n})\right)\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P}

\displaystyle=(1-\mathbb{P}_{N})\left(u(t^{n})\cdot\nabla u(t^{n})\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=-\nu\Delta\left(u(t^{n+1})-u(t^{n})\right).

Subtracting (
F.3
), and introducing the short-hand notation
e^{n}:=u(t^{n})-u_{N}^{n}
,
e^{n+1}:=u(t^{n+1})-u^{n,\ast}_{N}
, we find

\frac{e^{n+1}-e^{n}}{{\tau}}=-\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(e^{n}\cdot\nabla u(t^{n+1})\right)-\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1}\right)+\nu\Delta e^{n+1}+\mathcal{E}^{n}.

Next, integrate against
e^{n+1}_{N}:=P_{N}e^{n+1}
to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\tau}}

\displaystyle\left(\|e_{N}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|e_{N}^{n+1}-e_{N}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|e_{N}^{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|e^{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}\|e_{N}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}-\langle\mathbb{P}_{N}(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1}),e^{n+1}\rangle-\nu\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\langle\mathcal{E}^{n},e^{n+1}\rangle.

We note that

(F.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\mathbb{P}_{N}(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1}),e^{n+1}\rangle&=\langle P_{N}(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1}),e^{n+1}\rangle\\
&=\langle u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1},e^{n+1}\rangle-\langle(1-P_{N})u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1},e^{n+1}\rangle\\
&=0-\langle u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1},(1-P_{N})e^{n+1}\rangle\\
&=-\langle u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1},(1-P_{N})u(t^{n+1})\rangle\\
&=\langle e^{n+1},u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla(1-P_{N})u(t^{n+1})\rangle\\
&=-\langle e^{n+1},e^{n}\cdot\nabla(1-P_{N})u(t^{n+1})\rangle\\
&\qquad+\langle e^{n+1},u(t^{n})\cdot\nabla(1-P_{N})u(t^{n+1})\rangle\end{aligned}

We now note that we can rewrite the last term as follows:

\displaystyle\langle e^{n+1},u(t^{n})\cdot\nabla(1-P_{N})u(t^{n+1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(1-P_{N})\left(e^{n+1}\otimes u(t^{n})\right),\nabla u(t^{n+1})\right\rangle

Using the fact that

(1-P_{N})\left(e^{n+1}\otimes u(t^{n})\right)=(1-P_{N})\left((1-P_{N/2})e^{n+1}\otimes(1-P_{N/2})u(t^{n})\right),

it then follows that we have

\displaystyle\langle P_{N}(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1}),e^{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle e^{n+1},e^{n}\cdot\nabla(1-P_{N})u(t^{n+1})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\left\langle(1-P_{N})\left(e^{n+1}\otimes u(t^{n})\right),\nabla u(t^{n+1})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\|e^{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|(1-P_{N})\nabla u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\|(1-P_{N/2})u(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}}\|(1-P_{N})\nabla u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}}.

For
r>d/2+1
, we have a continuous embedding
H^{r}{\hookrightarrow}W^{1,\infty}
, and an inequality of the form

\|(1-P_{N/2})u(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{r,d}\frac{1}{N^{r}}\|u(t^{n})\|_{H^{r}}.

Hence we can estimate

\displaystyle\langle P_{N}(u^{n}_{N}\cdot\nabla e^{n+1}),e^{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim_{r,d}\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\|e^{n}\|_{L^{2}}\|u(t^{n})\|_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\frac{\|u(t^{n})\|_{H^{r}}^{2}}{N^{2r}}.

Estimating the products on the right-hand side using the inequality
ab\leq\epsilon a^{2}+\frac{1}{4\epsilon}b^{2}
with suitable
\epsilon>0
, it follows that there exists a constant
C>0
(independent of
\nu>0
,
N
and
n
), such that

(F.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|e^{n+1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}&\leq\left(1+C{\tau}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})}^{2}\right)\|e^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\nu\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
&\qquad+C{\tau}N^{-2r}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})}^{4}+C{\tau}|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n},e^{n+1}\rangle|.\end{aligned}

F.2.1.
Time-differencing error

We note that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{{\tau}},e^{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\tau}}\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\int_{t^{n}}^{t}\left\langle\partial_{t}^{2}u(s),e^{n+1}\right\rangle\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\tau}}\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\int_{t^{n}}^{t}\left\langle-P\left\{\partial_{t}u(s)\cdot\nabla u(s)+u(s)\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}u(s)\right\},e^{n+1}\right\rangle\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{{\tau}}\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\int_{t^{n}}^{t}\left\langle\nu\Delta u(s),e^{n+1}\right\rangle\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=:(I)_{\tau}+(II)_{\tau}.

The first term can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle(I)_{\tau}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\tau}\left(\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(L^{2}_{x})}\|u\|_{C_{t}(W^{1,\infty}_{x})}+\|u\|_{C_{t}(L^{\infty}_{x})}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{1}_{x})}\right)\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{r,d}{\tau}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r-2})}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r})}\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}},

provided that
r>d/2+2\geq 3
(the last bound is automatic for
d\geq 2
). For the second term, we derive the bound

(II)_{\tau}\lesssim{\tau}\nu\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{1}_{x})}\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus, for any
\epsilon>0
(to be specified later), we have for some constant
C=C(r,d)>0
:

(F.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{{\tau}},e^{n+1}\rangle|&\leq\epsilon\left(\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}+\nu\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}\right)\\
&\qquad+\frac{C{\tau}^{2}}{\epsilon}\left(\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r-2})}^{2}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r})}^{2}+\nu\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{1}_{x})}^{2}\right).\end{aligned}

F.2.2.
Non-linear time-differencing error

For the error associated with
\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL}
, we simply estimate (for
\epsilon>0
to be determined later)

|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL},e^{n+1}\rangle|\leq\epsilon\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{-1}\|\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

and we observe that

\|\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL}\|_{L^{2}}\leq{\tau}\|u\|_{C_{t}(L^{\infty}_{x})}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{1}_{x})}\leq{\tau}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r-2}_{x})},

assuming that
r>d/2+2\geq 3
. This yields

(F.8)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{NL},e^{n+1}\rangle|\leq\epsilon\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{{\tau}^{2}}{\epsilon}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})}^{2}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r-2}_{x})}^{2}

F.2.3.
Nonlinear projection error

Again, we estimate the error
\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P}
using the simple estimate

|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P},e^{n+1}\rangle|\leq\epsilon\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{-1}\|\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

with
\epsilon>0
to be specified later.
We furthermore note that

(1-P_{N})\left\{u(t^{n})\cdot\nabla u(t^{n+1})\right\}=(1-P_{N})\left\{(1-P_{N/2})u(t^{n})\cdot(1-P_{N/2})\nabla u(t^{n+1})\right\},

which implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(1-P_{N/2})u(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}}\|(1-P_{N/2})\nabla u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim_{r,d}N^{-r}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r})}^{2}.

In the last step, we have used the fact that
\|(1-P_{N/2})v\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{r,d}N^{-r}\|v\|_{H^{r}}
for
r\geq 0
, and that by Sobolev embedding

\displaystyle\|(1-P_{N/2})\nabla u(t^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\|(1-P_{N/2})u(t^{n+1})\|_{H^{r}}\leq\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})},

for any
r>d/2+1
. Thus, there exists
C=C(d,r)>0
, such that

(F.9)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{P},e^{n+1}\rangle|\leq\epsilon\|e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{C}{\epsilon N^{2r}}\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})}^{2}.

F.2.4.
Viscosity error

Finally, we note that

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n}_{\nu},e^{n+1}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\nu\|\nabla(u(t^{n+1})-u(t^{n}))\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\nu\epsilon\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\nu{\tau}^{2}}{\epsilon}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{1}_{x})}^{2}

(F.10)

\displaystyle\leq\nu\epsilon\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\nu{\tau}^{2}}{\epsilon}\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r-2}_{x})}^{2},

for any
r>d/2+2\geq 3
.

F.2.5.
The final stability estimate

Choosing
\epsilon=1/4
, it follows from (
F.7
), (
F.8
), (
F.9
) and (
F.10
), that the total error term
\langle\mathcal{E}^{n},e^{n+1}\rangle
can be estimated by

(F.11)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{E}^{n},e^{n+1}\rangle|\leq\|e^{n+1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\nu\|\nabla e^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C^{\ast}\left({\tau}^{2}+N^{-2r}\right).

for some constant
C^{\ast}>0
, depending only on
r>d/2+2
, the spatial dimension
d
and the norms
\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r})}
,
\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r})}
of the exact solution
u
.

Substitution of the error estimate (
F.11
) in (

) finally yields

\displaystyle(1-{\tau})\|e^{n+1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\left(1+C^{\ast}{\tau}\right)\|e^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+{\tau}C^{\ast}\left({\tau}^{2}+N^{-2r}\right),

where the constant

depends only on
r\geq d/2+2
, the dimension
d
and the norms
\|u\|_{C_{t}(H^{r}_{x})}
and
\|u\|_{C^{1}_{t}(H^{r-2}_{x})}

From this, we conclude that for a time-step

where the last estimate follows from (

We note that

. And hence, we finally find, for

, that

(F.14)

\displaystyle\|u^{n}_{N}-u(t^{n})\|_{L^{2}}=\sqrt{E^{n}}\leq C\left({\tau}+N^{-r}\right),

where

depends only on
T
,

,

,

and

\Psi
