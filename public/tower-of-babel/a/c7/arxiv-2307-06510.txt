Dimension-free Ergodicity of Path Integral Molecular Dynamics
Xuda Ye \comma \corrauth, Zhennan Zhou

Abstract
The quantum thermal average plays a central role in describing the thermodynamic properties of a quantum system. Path integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) is a prevailing approach for computing quantum thermal averages by approximating the quantum partition function as a classical isomorphism on an augmented space, enabling efficient classical sampling, but the theoretical knowledge of the ergodicity of the sampling is lacking. Parallel to the standard PIMD with N N ring polymer beads, we also study the Matsubara mode PIMD, where the ring polymer is replaced by a continuous loop composed of N N Matsubara modes. Utilizing the generalized Γ \Gamma calculus, we prove that both the Matsubara mode PIMD and the standard PIMD have uniform-in- N N ergodicity, i.e., the convergence rate towards the invariant distribution does not depend on the number of modes or beads N N .

1
Introduction

Calculation of the quantum thermal average plays an important role in quantum physics and quantum chemistry, not only because it fully characterizes the canonical ensemble of the quantum system, but also because of its wide applications in describing the thermal properties of complex quantum systems, including the idea quantum gases
[
1
]
, chemical reaction rates
[
2
,
3
]
, density of states of crystals
[
4
]
, quantum phase transitions
[
5
]
, etc. However, since the computational cost of direct discretization methods (finite difference, pseudospectral methods, etc.) grows exponentially with the spatial dimension
[
6
,
7
]
, the exact calculation of the quantum thermal average is hardly affordable in high dimensions. In the past decades, there have been numerous methods committed to compute the quantum thermal average approximately, and the path integral molecular dynamics (PIMD) is among the most prevailing ones.

The PIMD is a computational framework to obtain accurate quantum thermal averages. Using the imaginary time-slicing approach in Feynman path integral
[
8
]
,
the PIMD maps the quantum system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a ring polymer of
N
beads in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, where each bead represents a classical duplicate of the original quantum system, and adjacent beads are connected by a harmonic spring potential. When the number of beads
N
is large enough, the classical Boltzmann distribution of the ring polymer in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
is expected to yield an accurate approximation of the quantum thermal average.
Since the development of the PIMD in 1970s, this framework has been widely used in the calculations in the chemical reaction rates

[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, transition state theory
[
12
]
and tunneling splittings
[
13
,
14
]
.
Several variants of the PIMD, the ring polymer molecular dynamics (RPMD)
[
15
,
16
]
, the centroid molecular dynamics (CMD)
[
17
,
18
]
and the Matsubara dynamics
[
19
,
20
]
, have been employed to compute the quantum correlation function.
Recently, there have been fruitful studies on designing efficient and accurate algorithms to enhance the numerical performance of the PIMD
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
The PIMD can also be applied in multi-electronic-state quantum systems, see
[
25
,
26
]
for instance.
The general procedure to compute the quantum thermal average in the PIMD is demonstrated as follows:

1.

Ring polymer approximation
. Pick a positive integer
N
and map the quantum system to a ring polymer system of
N
beads, where each bead is a classical duplicate of the original system.

2.

Construction of sampling
. Equip the ring polymer system with a stochastic thermostat (e.g., Langevin, Andersen and Nosé–Hoover) so that the resulting stochastic process samples the Boltzmann distribution of the ring polymer.

3.

Stochastic simulation
. Evolve the stochastic process with a proper time discretization method, and approximate the quantum thermal average by the time average in the long-time simulation.

The PIMD framework based on the ring polymer beads is referred to as the standard PIMD in this paper.

Although the standard PIMD has become a mature framework to compute the quantum thermal average, a rigorous mathematical understanding of its convergence is still sorely lacking. A fundamental question is the dimension-free ergodicity, i.e., does the stochastic process sampling the Boltzmann distribution has a convergence rate which does not depend on the number of beads
N
?
The property of uniform-in-
N
ergodicity has been partially validated when the potential function is quadratic
[
27
,
28
]
, but a rigorous justification in the general case is still to be investigated.

An important goal of this paper is to justify the dimension-free ergodicity of the standard PIMD. Nevertheless, our proof mainly relies on an alternative PIMD framework, the Matsubara mode PIMD, which is more convenient for analysis.
As its name indicates, this framework is based on the Matsubara modes
[
29
,
19
]
rather than the discrete beads of the ring polymer. In the Matsubara mode PIMD, the ring polymer is expressed as the superpositions of
N
Matsubara modes, resulting in a continuous imaginary-time ring polymer loop rather than a ragged one (see Figure 3.2 of
[
19
]
for example).
The Matsubara mode PIMD has a close relation to the standard PIMD, because
the Matsubara mode PIMD with suitable discretization is equivalent to the standard PIMD
[
29
,
30
]
, except for the minor difference in the mode frequencies. In fact, the standard PIMD utilizes the normal mode frequencies
[
19
]
, whose continuum limit give rise to the Matsubara frequencies. It is also pointed in
[
29
,
30
]
that the standard PIMD has better performance than the Matsubara mode PIMD in computing the quantum thermal average.

The main contribution of this paper is that we prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the Matsubara mode PIMD and the standard PIMD, namely, the convergence rate towards the invariant distribution does not depend on
N
, which stands for the number of modes in the Matsubara mode PIMD and the number of beads in the standard PIMD. The authors believe that this is the first dimension-free egordicity result of the PIMD in the form of the underdamped Langevin dynamics. Our proof strategies include the Bakry–Émery calculus
[
31
]
and the recently developed generalized
\Gamma
calculus
[
32
,
33
]
.
It should be stressed that the convergence rate can still depend on the potential function
V(x)
and the temperature, and in particular the convergence rate is exponentially small in the low temperature limit. This a a common feature of the Bakry–Émery calculus with a non-convex potential function, referring to Proposition 5.1.6 of
[
31
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we derive the Matsubara mode PIMD from the Feynman path integral representation of the quantum thermal average. In Section
3
we prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity the Matsubara mode PIMD and the standard PIMD. In Section
4
we implement the numerical tests to show the performance of the Matsubara mode PIMD and the standard PIMD in computing the quantum thermal average and validate the dimension-free ergodicity.

2
Derivation of the Matsubara mode PIMD

In this section we derive the Matsubara mode PIMD from the Feynman path integral representation of the quantum thermal average. First, we show that the quantum equilibrium can be equivalently interpreted as the classical Boltzmann distribution of a continuous loop representing the ring polymer in the position space. Second, we represent the energy function
\mathcal{E}(\xi)
of the ring polymer loop in terms of the Matsubara coordinates
\xi
. Finally, we construct an infinite-dimensional underdamped Langevin dynamics to sample the distribution
\exp{-\mathcal E(\xi)}
, formulating the Matsubara mode PIMD.

Note that the our derivation does not rely on the normal mode transform, and is thus different from the traditional ways to generate the Matsubara modes
[
19
]
.

2.1
Quantum equilibrium as the Boltzmann distribution of a continuous loop

We consider the quantum system in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2}+V(\hat{q}),

(1)

where
\hat{q}
and
\hat{p}
are the position and momentum operators in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
V(\cdot)
is a real-valued potential function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When the quantum system is at a constant temperature
T=1/\beta
, the state of the system can be described by the canonical ensemble with the density operator
e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
, and thus the partition function
\mathcal{Z}=\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}]
. The quantum thermal average of the system means the canonical average of an observable operator
O(\hat{q})
, namely

\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}O(\hat{q})]}{\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}]},

(2)

Here, we assume the observable operator
O(\hat{q})
depends only on the position operator
\hat{q}
, where
O(\cdot)
is a real-valued function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Utilizing the Feynman path integral
[
34
,
8
]
, the partition function can be interpreted as the integral with respect to a continuous loop
x(\tau)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
parameterized by
\tau\in[0,\beta]
:

\mathcal{Z}=\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}]=\int\exp\{-\mathcal{E}(x)\}\mathcal{D}[x],

where
\mathcal{D}[x]
is the formal Lebesgue measure of the continuous loop in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the energy functional
\mathcal{E}(x)
is defined by

\mathcal{E}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}|x^{\prime}(\tau)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{0}^{\beta}V(x(\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau.

(3)

As a consequence, the quantum canonical ensemble
e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
is exactly mapped to a classical Boltzmann distribution of the continuous loop
x(\tau)
with the energy
\mathcal{E}(x)
.
Finally, the quantum thermal average
\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta}
can be formally written as

\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int\bigg[\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}O(x(\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg]\exp\{-\mathcal{E}(x)\}\mathcal{D}[x].

(4)

2.2
Represent the energy function with Matsubara modes

Consider the following eigenvalue problem with periodic boundary conditions:

-\ddot{c}_{k}(\tau)=\omega_{k}^{2}c_{k}(\tau),~~~~\tau\in[0,\beta],~~~~k=0,1,2,\cdots,

where the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are explicitly given by

\displaystyle\omega_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle c_{0}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\beta}};

\displaystyle\omega_{2k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k\pi}{\beta}

\displaystyle c_{2k-1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\sin\bigg(\frac{2k\pi\tau}{\beta}\bigg),

\displaystyle k=1,2,\cdots;

\displaystyle\omega_{2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k\pi}{\beta},

\displaystyle c_{2k}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}\cos\bigg(\frac{2k\pi\tau}{\beta}\bigg),

\displaystyle k=1,2,\cdots.

The eigenfunctions
\{c_{k}(\tau)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
form the orthonormal Fourier basis of
L^{2}([0,\beta];\mathbb{R})
, and thus any continuous loop
x(\cdot)
can be uniquely represented as

x(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau),~~~~\tau\in[0,\beta],

where
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the coordinates of
x(\cdot)
in different Matsubara modes. Conversely, for a given continuous loop
x(\cdot)
, the Matsubara coordinates are calculated from

\xi_{k}=\int_{0}^{\beta}x(\tau)c_{k}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau,~~~~k=0,1,2,\cdots.

Utilizing the Matsubara coordinates, the energy function
\mathcal{E}(x)
in (
3
) is written as

\mathcal{E}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\omega_{k}^{2}|\xi_{k}|^{2}+\mathcal{V}(\xi),~~~\text{with}~~\mathcal{V}(\xi):=\int_{0}^{\beta}V\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau)\bigg)\mathrm{d}\tau,

(5)

and the target Boltzmann distribution is formally given by
\exp\{-\mathcal{E}(\xi)\}
.

2.3
Formulation of the Matsubara mode PIMD

In the following, we construct an infinite-dimensional Langevin dynamics to sample the Boltzmann distribution
\exp\{-\mathcal{E}(\xi)\}
. Without any preconditioning, the vanilla underdamped Langevin dynamics for sampling
\exp\{-\mathcal{E}(\xi)\}
reads

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\omega_{k}^{2}\xi_{k}-\int_{0}^{\beta}\nabla V(x(\tau))c_{k}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau-\gamma\eta_{k}+\sqrt{2\gamma}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

where
\gamma>0
is the damping rate on each mode,
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are the auxiliary velocity variables in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\{B_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are independent Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, the high-frequency modes in the energy function
\mathcal{E}(\xi)
poses the infamous stiffness problem
[
27
,
35
]
in the time discretization of (
6
): the time step needs to be extremely small to stabilize of the high-frequency part of the dynamics.

In this paper, we employ the preconditioning

[
27
,
36
]
to scale the internal frequencies of the Matsubara modes. Introduce
a>0
and rewrite the energy function
\mathcal{E}(\xi)
as

\mathcal{E}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)|\xi_{k}|^{2}+\mathcal{V}^{a}(\xi),~~~\text{with}~~\mathcal{V}^{a}(\xi):=\int_{0}^{\beta}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau)\bigg)\mathrm{d}\tau,

(7)

where the potential function
V^{a}(q):=V(q)-a|q|^{2}/2
.
The positive coefficient
\omega_{k}^{2}+a
on each mode indicates that it is reasonable to apply the preconditioning

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\int_{0}^{\beta}\nabla V^{a}(x(\tau))c_{k}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau-\gamma\eta_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

which is an infinite-dimensional Langevin dynamics of the mode coordinates
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
.

Remark 2.1
.

If we neglect the gradient term in (
8
), the dynamics of
(\xi_{k},\eta_{k})
reads

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\gamma\eta_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.

which is a underdamped Langevin dynamics in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the invariant distribution

\exp\{-\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}{2}\big(|\xi_{k}|^{2}+|\eta_{k}|^{2}\big)\bigg\}.

Clearly, the preconditioning removes the stiffness of all Matsubara modes, and
\mathcal{O}(1)
time step is applicable for the time discretization.

Remark 2.2
.

The constant
a>0
is to ensure the frequency of the centroid mode
(k=0)
is nonzero, so that the preconditioning can be applied on all Matsubara modes.
An alternative preconditioning scheme for (
6
) without introducing
a
is defined as:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \xi_{0}&=\eta_{0},\\
\eta_{0}&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\nabla V(x(\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau-\gamma\eta_{0}+\sqrt{2\gamma}\dot{B}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \xi_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\eta_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\nabla V(x(\tau))c_{k}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau-\gamma\eta_{k}+\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{\omega_{k}}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.~~~~k=1,2,\cdots.

(10)

Moreover, the preconditioned Matsubara mode PIMD (
9
) and (
10
) can be employed in quantum systems defined on a periodic torus space
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

2.4
Implementation of the Matsubara mode PIMD in finite dimensions

At this stage, the infinite-dimensional Matsubara mode PIMD (
8
) is completely formal, and neither its existence or well-posedness is not justified.
For the purpose of numerical simulation, we need to truncate the number of Matsubara modes in (
8
) to a finite integer
N
. In this case the continuous loop
x(\tau)
is truncated as

x_{N}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau),~~~~\tau\in[0,\beta],

and the potential energy of the loop in (
7
) is replaced by

\mathcal{V}_{N}^{a}(\xi):=\int_{0}^{\beta}V^{a}(x_{N}(\tau))\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{\beta}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau)\bigg)\mathrm{d}\tau,~~~~\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}.

(11)

However, even for a finite
N
, the integral in (
11
) does not have an explicit expression, and the numerical integration is required to approximate
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{a}(\xi)
. Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
be the discretization size in the interval
[0,\beta]
, then we obtain the approximation

\mathcal{V}_{N}^{a}(\xi)\approx\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi):=\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg),~~~~\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

(12)

where
\beta_{D}=\beta/D
. The gradients of
\mathcal{V}^{a}_{N,D}(\xi)
with respect to each
\xi_{k}
are correspondingly

\nabla_{\xi_{k}}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)=\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\nabla V^{a}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))c_{k}(j\beta_{D}),~~~~k=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

Remark 2.3
.

We do not select the high-order numerical integration schemes mainly because the continuous loop
x_{N}(\tau)
becomes ragged when the number of modes
N
is large. The low-regularity of
x_{N}(\tau)
makes it ineffectual to apply high-order integration.

With the potential function
\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)
, the Matsubara mode PIMD (
8
) is approximated as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\frac{\beta_{D}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\nabla V^{a}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))c_{k}(j\beta_{D})-\gamma\eta_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.~~~k=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

(13)

whose invariant distribution is the classical Boltzmann distribution:

\pi_{N,D}(\xi)\propto\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)|\xi_{k}|^{2}-\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg)\bigg\}.

(14)

A typical choice of the discretization size
D
is the number of modes
N
, so that the computational cost per time step is equal to
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
in the Fast Fourier Transform. It is natural from (
4
) that the quantum thermal average
\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta}
is approximated as

\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta}\approx\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta,N,D}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\bigg[\frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}O\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg)\bigg]\pi_{N,D}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi.

The long-time simulation of the Matsubara mode PIMD (
13
) at finite
N
and
D
then provides an accurate estimate of the statistical average in the distribution
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
, which is denoted by
\langle{O(\hat{q})}\rangle_{\beta,N,D}
.

2.5
Relation to the standard PIMD

We demonstrate the connection between the Matsubara mode PIMD and the standard PIMD. Suppose the number of modes
N
is an odd integer and we view
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
as the normal mode coordinates
[
37
]
in the standard PIMD, then the Boltzmann distribution of the ring polymer beads in the standard PIMD is expressed as

\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a)|\xi_{k}|^{2}-\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{N})\bigg)\bigg\},

(15)

where
\{\omega_{k,N}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
are the normal mode frequencies

\omega_{0}=0,~~~~\omega_{2k-1,N}=\omega_{2k,N}=\frac{2}{\beta_{N}}\sin\bigg(\frac{k\pi}N\bigg),~~~~k=1,\cdots,\frac{N-1}{2}.

Furthermore, the preconditioned Langevin dynamics for sampling (
15
) is written as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\frac{\beta_{D}}{\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\nabla V^{a}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))c_{k}(j\beta_{D})-\gamma\eta_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma}{\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.~~~k=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

(16)

Comparing the dynamics (
13
) and (
16
), we observe the standard PIMD and the Matsubara mode PIMD (with odd
D=N
) are equivalent except for the mode frequencies.
Since the normal mode frequencies satisfy

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\omega_{k,N}=\omega_{k},~~~~k=0,1,2,\cdots,

we conclude that the Matsubara mode PIMD (
13
) and the
standard PIMD (
16
) have the identical continuum limit, namely the infinite-dimensional Langevin dynamics (
8
).
See Appendix C for the detailed derivation of the normal mode coordinates.

3
Uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the Matsubara mode PIMD

In this section, we prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the Matsubara mode PIMD. First, we present the assumptions and ergodicity results in Section 3.1. Then, we prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity in the overdamped and underdamped cases in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, respectively. Finally, in Section 3.4, we demonstrate that our proof of the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity in the Matsubara mode PIMD applies to the standard PIMD.

3.1
Assumptions and ergodicity results

For the convenience of analysis, in the Matsubara mode PIMD (
13
) we choose the damping rate
\gamma=1
, yielding the following underdamped Langevin dynamics:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\frac{\beta_{D}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\nabla V^{a}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))c_{k}(j\beta_{D})-\eta_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k}.\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

A closely related dynamics is the overdamped version of (
17
):

\dot{\xi}_{k}=-\xi_{k}-\frac{\beta_{D}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\nabla V^{a}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))c_{k}(j\beta_{D})+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},

(18)

which can be viewed as the overdamped limit of (
17
) as
\gamma\rightarrow\infty

[
38
]
.
The invariant distribution of (
18
) is
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
defined in (
14
), and the invariant distribution of (
17
) is

\mu_{N,D}(\xi,\eta)\propto\exp\bigg(-
\frac 12\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
(\omega_k^2+a) (|\xi_k|^2+|\eta_k|^2) -
\beta_D\sum_{j=0}^{D-1} V^a\bigg(
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \xi_k c_k(j\beta_D)
\bigg)
\bigg).

(19)

Clearly,
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
is the marginal distribution of
\mu_{N,D}(\xi)
in the variable
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
.

Under appropriate conditions on the potential
V(q)
, we prove that both the overdamped and underdamped Matsubara mode PIMD in (
18
) and (
17
) possess the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity, namely, the convergence rate to the invariant distribution does not depend on the number of modes
N
.

Before we conduct a detailed discussion on these results, we enumerate all the assumptions required in the proof, mainly on the potential function
V(q)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption
.

Given
a>0
, the potential function

V^{a}(q)=V(q)-\frac{a}{2}|q|^{2},~~~~q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

is twice differentiable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for some constants
M_{1},M_{2}\geqslant 0
:

(i)

V^{a}(q)
can be decomposed as
V^{c}(q)+V^{b}(q)
, where
\nabla^{2}V^{c}(q)\succcurlyeq O_{d}
and
|V^{b}(q)|\leqslant M_{1}
for any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(ii)

-M_{2}I_{d}\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}V^{a}(q)\preccurlyeq M_{2}I_{d}
for any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

A special assumption required in the underdamped case is:

(iii)

The number of modes
N
is no larger than the discretization size
D
, namely,
N\leqslant D
.

Here,
I_{d}
and
O_{d}
are the identity and zero matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Assumption (i) can be shortly interpreted as:
V^{c}(q)
is globally convex and
V^{b}(q)
is globally bounded.

3.2
Uniform ergodicity of overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD

We prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of (
18
) in the relative entropy using the Bakry–Émery calculus
[
31
]
.
The log-Sobolev inequality for the distribution
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
produces an explicit convergence rate in high-dimensions.

Theorem 3.3
.

Under Assumption (i), let
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the Markov semigroup of the overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD (
18
), then for any positive smooth function
f(\xi)
in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
,

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}}(P_{t}f)\leqslant\exp(-2\lambda_1 t)\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0,

where the convergence rate
\lambda_{1}=\exp(-4\beta M_1)
.

Proof 3.4
.

We study the overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD driven by the convex potential
V^{c}(q)
, and prove the corresponding uniform-in-
N
log-Sobolev inequality. Utilizing the bounded perturbation, we obtain the uniform-in-
N
log-Sobolev inequality for (
18
).

1. Ergodicity of overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD driven by
V^{c}(q)

For notational convenience, introduce the potential function of the convex part
V^{c}(q)
:

\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi):=\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}V^{c}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))=\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}V^{c}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg),

Since
V^{c}(q)
is globally convex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is easy to deduce
\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
is globally convex in the mode coordinates
\xi=\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
(see Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
).
Next consider the overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD driven by
\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
:

\dot{\xi}_{k}=-\xi_{k}-\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\nabla_{\xi_{k}}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},~~~~k=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

(22)

It is easy to see the generator of (
22
) is given by

L^{c}=-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigg(\xi_{k}+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\nabla_{\xi_{k}}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)\bigg)\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{k}}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\Delta_{\xi_{k}},

and the invariant distribution of (
22
) is

\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)\propto\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)|\xi_{k}|^{2}-\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}V^{c}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg)\bigg\}.

(23)

To establish the log-Sobolev inequality for the distribution
\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
, we compute the carré du champ operator
\Gamma_{1}(f,f)
and the iterated operator
\Gamma_{2}(f,f)
corresponding to the generator
L^{c}
(see Definition 1.4.2 and Equation (1.16.1) in
[
31
]
). By direct calculation,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{k}}g}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nabla_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}^{2}f:\nabla_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}^{2}g}{(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)(\omega_{l}^{2}+a)}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{k}}g}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}+\sum_{k,l=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f\cdot\nabla^{2}_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{l}}g}{(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)(\omega_{l}^{2}+a)}.

Here, the dot product
u\cdot B\cdot v
for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
means

u\cdot B\cdot v=u^{\mathrm{T}}Bv=\sum_{p,q=1}^{d}u_{p}B_{pq}v_{q},

and the double dot product
A:B
for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
means

A:B=\mathrm{Tr}[A^{\mathrm{T}}B]=\sum_{p,q=1}^{d}A_{pq}B_{pq}.

Utilizing the convexity of the potential function
\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
, we obtain

\Gamma_{2}(f,f)\geqslant\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}+\sum_{k,j=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f\cdot\nabla^{2}_{\xi_{k}\xi_{j}}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{j}}f}{(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)(\omega_{j}^{2}+a)}\geqslant\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}=\Gamma_{1}(f,f),

hence the log-Sobolev constant for
\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
is 1 according to Proposition 5.7.1 of
[
31
]
. The log-Sobolev inequality for
\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
then reads

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}^{c}}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{\Gamma_{1}(f,f)}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi_{N,D}^{c}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi_{N,D}^{c}.

(24)

Note that the relative entropy here is defined with respect to the distribution
\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
with
V^{c}(q)
rather than the distribution
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
with
V^{a}(q)
.

3. Ergodicity of overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD driven by
V^{a}(q)

Let
Z_{N,D}
and
Z_{N,D}^{c}
be the normalization constants of the distributions
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
and
\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
,

\displaystyle Z_{N,D}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\exp\bigg(-\frac 12\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (\omega_k^2+a) |\xi_k|^2 - \beta_D \sum_{j=0}^{D-1} V^a(x_N(j\beta_D))\bigg)\mathrm{d}\xi,

\displaystyle Z_{N,D}^{c}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\exp\bigg(-\frac 12\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (\omega_k^2+a) |\xi_k|^2 - \beta_D \sum_{j=0}^{D-1} V^c(x_N(\beta_D))\bigg)\mathrm{d}\xi.

Using the inequality
|V^{a}(q)-V^{c}(q)|=|V^{b}(q)|\leqslant M_{1}
, we have

|\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)-\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)|\leqslant\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}\big|V^{b}(x_{N}(j\beta_{D}))\big|\leqslant\beta M_{1},

and thus the constants
Z_{N,D}
and
Z_{N,D}^{c}
satisfy

\frac{Z_{N,D}}{Z_{N,D}^{c}}\in\big[\exp(-\beta M_1),\exp(\beta M_1)\big].

As a result, the density functions
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
and
\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)
satisfy

\frac{\pi_{N,D}^{c}(\xi)}{\pi_{N,D}(\xi)}=\frac{Z_{N,D}}{Z_{N,D}^{c}}\exp\bigg(
\beta_D \sum_{j=0}^{D-1} V^b(x_N(j\beta_D))
\bigg)\in\big[\exp(-2\beta M_1),\exp(2\beta M_1)\big].

Using the bounded perturbation (Proposition 5.1.6 of
[
31
]
),
we obtain from (
24
) that

\exp(-4\beta M_1)\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi_{N,D}.

Hence for the rate
\lambda_{1}=\exp(-4\beta M_1)
, the relative entropy has exponential decay,

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}}(P_{t}f)\leqslant\exp(-2\lambda_1 t)\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0,

for any positive smooth function
f(\xi)
.

In particular, the convergence rate
\lambda_{1}
does not depend on the number of modes
N
or the discretization size
D
, hence we conclude the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the overdamped Matsubara mode PIMD (
18
).

3.3
Uniform ergodicity of underdamped Matsubara mode PIMD

We prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of (
17
) in the entropy-like quantity (
21
), and the main technique is the generalized
\Gamma
calculus developed in
[
32
,
33
]
. The generalized
\Gamma
calculus is an extension of the Bakry–Émery calculus and can be applied on stochastic processes with degenerate diffusions. The generalized
\Gamma
calculus is largely inspired from the hypocoercivity theory
[
39
]
of Villani, and is able to produce an explicit convergence rate in the relative entropy rather than
H^{1}
or
L^{2}
.
For convenience, we present a brief review of the theory of the generalized
\Gamma
calculus in Appendix
C
.

Recall that the invariant distribution of the underdamped Matsubara mode PIMD (
17
) is
\mu_{N,D}(\xi,\eta)
defined in (
19
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Under Assumptions (i)(ii)(iii), let
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the Markov semigroup of the underdamped Matsubara mode PIMD (
17
), then for any positive smooth function
f(\xi,\eta)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2dN}
,

W_{\mu_{N,D}}(P_{t}f)\leqslant\exp(-2\lambda_2 t)W_{\mu_{N,D}}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0,

where the convergence rate
\lambda_{2}=\frac{a^{2}}{3M_{2}^{2}+5a^{2}}\exp(-4\beta M_1)
.

Proof 3.6
.

The proof is accomplished in several steps.
First, we establish the uniform-in-
N
log-Sobolev inequality for the distribution
\mu_{N,D}(\xi,\eta)
. Second, introduce the functions

\Phi_{1}(f)=f\log f,~~~~\Phi_{2}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}+|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}

and compute the generalized
\Gamma
operators
\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)
and
\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)
.
Finally, by validating the generalized curvature-dimension condition

\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)-\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{2}(f)+\bigg(\frac{M_{2}^{2}}{a^{2}}+1\bigg)\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)\geqslant 0,

we can apply Theorem
C.9
to prove the exponential decay of the quantity
W(P_{t}f)
.

1. Uniform-in-
N
log-Sobolev inequality for
\mu_{N}(\xi,\eta)

In Theorem
3.3
, the log-Sobolev inequality for the distribution
\pi_{N,D}(\xi)
holds true:

\lambda_{1}\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi_{N,D}}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi_{N,D},

where the convergence rate
\lambda_{1}=\exp(-4\beta M_1)
. For the velocity variables
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, define the Gaussian distribution
\nu_{N}(\eta)
by its density function:

\nu_{N}(\eta)\propto\exp\bigg(-\frac 12\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
(\omega_k^2+a)|\eta_k|^2\bigg),~~~~\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

then log-Sobolev inequality for
\nu_{N}
holds true,

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\nu_{N}}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}.

Since the distribution
\mu_{N,D}(\xi,\eta)=\pi_{N,D}(\xi)\otimes\nu_{N}(\eta)
is the tensor product, Proposition 5.2.7 of
[
31
]
yields the log-Sobolev inequality for the product distribution:

\lambda_{1}\mathrm{Ent}_{\mu_{N,D}}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}+|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D},

(25)

where the convergence rate is determined by the smaller one of the rates
\lambda_{1}=\exp(-4\beta M_1)
and
1
, which is
\lambda_{1}
itself.
Note that (
25
) does not imply the ergodicity of (
17
) directly, because (
17
) has degenerate diffusion in the
\eta
variable.

2. Calculation of the generalized
\Gamma
operators for
\Phi_{1}(f)
and
\Phi_{2}(f)

We still use the notation

\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)=\beta_{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg),~~~~\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

and the generator of (
17
) is given by

L=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\eta_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{k}}-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigg(\xi_{k}+\eta_{k}+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\nabla_{\xi_{k}}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)\bigg)\cdot\nabla_{\eta_{k}}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\Delta_{\xi_{k}}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}.

By direct calculation, the commutators
[L,\nabla_{\xi_{k}}]
and
[L,\nabla_{\eta_{k}}]
are given by

[L,\nabla_{\xi_{k}}]=\nabla_{\eta_{k}}+\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{l}^{2}+a}\nabla_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\eta_{l}},~~~~[L,\nabla_{\eta_{k}}]=\nabla_{\eta_{k}}-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}.

Inspired from Example 3 of
[
33
]
,
define the functions

\Phi_{1}(f)=f\log f,~~~\Phi_{2}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}+|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f},

(26)

where
\Phi_{2}(f)
can be viewed as a twisted form of

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\frac{|\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}+|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f},

which appears in the RHS of the log-Sobolev inequality (
25
).
From Example
C.4
in Appendix
C
, the generalized
\Gamma
operator
\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)
is given by

\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}.

(27)

To compute
\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)
, we write
\Phi_{2}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\Phi_{2,k}(f)
, where

\Phi_{2,k}(f)=\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}+|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f},~~~~k=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

According to Example
C.5
in Appendix
C
, we have

\displaystyle f\cdot\Gamma_{\Phi_{2,k}}(f)

\displaystyle\geqslant(\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f)\cdot[L,\nabla_{\eta_{k}}-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}]f+\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f\cdot[L,\nabla_{\eta_{k}}]f

\displaystyle=|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}-(\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f)\cdot\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{l}^{2}+a}\nabla_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{\eta_{l}}f.

Taking the summation over
k=0,1,\cdots,N-1
, we obtain

f\cdot\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)\geqslant\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}-\sum_{k,l=0}^{N-1}\frac{\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f}{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}\cdot\nabla_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\eta_{l}}f}{\omega_{l}^{2}+a}.

(28)

To further simplify the expression of
\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)
,
define the vectors
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
by

X=\bigg\{\frac{\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f}{\sqrt{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\bigg\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},~~~~Y=\bigg\{\frac{\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f}{\sqrt{\omega_{k}^{2}+a}}\bigg\}_{k=0}^{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

(29)

then the inequality (
28
) can be equivalently written as

\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)\geqslant\frac{|X|^{2}-X^{\mathrm{T}}\Sigma Y}{f},

(30)

where the symmetric matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}
is given by

\Sigma_{kl}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)(\omega_{l}^{2}+a)}}\nabla_{\xi_{k}\xi_{l}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{N,D}^{a}(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},~~~~k,l=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

By Lemma
A.3
we have
-\frac{M_{2}}{a}I_{dN}\preccurlyeq\Sigma\preccurlyeq\frac{M_{2}}{a}I_{dN}
, hence
(
30
) directly produces

\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)\geqslant\frac{|X|^{2}-\frac{M_{2}}{a}|X||Y|}{f}.

(31)

In conclusion, the functions
\Phi_{1}(f)
,
\Phi_{2}(f)
and their generalized
\Gamma
operators satisfy

\Phi_{1}(f)=f\log f,~~~~\Phi_{2}(f)=\frac{|X|^{2}+|Y|^{2}}{f},~~~~\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)=\frac{|Y|^{2}}{2f},~~~~\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)\geqslant\frac{|X|^{2}-\frac{M_{2}}{a}|X||Y|}{f},

where the vectors
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
are given in (
29
).

3. Generalized curvature-dimension condition produces ergodicity

Let us summarize the functional inequalities. The log-Sobolev inequality (
25
) implies

\lambda_{1}\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\mu_{N,D}}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\frac{|X+Y|^{2}+|Y|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}\leqslant\frac{3}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\frac{|X|^{2}+|Y|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D},

hence with the expressions of
\Phi_{1}(f)
and
\Phi_{2}(f)
, we can equivalently write

\frac{2\lambda_{1}}{3}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\Phi_{1}(f)\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}-\Phi_{1}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}f\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}\bigg)\bigg)\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\Phi_{2}(f)\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}.

(32)

The inequality (
31
) implies

\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)-\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{2}(f)\geqslant\frac{|X|^{2}-2\frac{M_{2}}{a}|X||Y|-|Y|^{2}}{2f},

and thus using the expression of
\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)
we have

\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)-\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{2}(f)+\bigg(\frac{M_{2}^{2}}{a^{2}}+1\bigg)\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f)\geqslant\frac{(|X|-\frac{M_{2}}{a}|Y|)^{2}}{2f}\geqslant 0.

(33)

Utilizing Theorem
C.9
in Appendix
C
, define the entropy-like quantity by

W_{\mu_{N,D}}(f)=\bigg(\frac{M_{2}^{2}}{a^{2}}+1\bigg)\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\Phi_{1}(f)\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}-\Phi_{1}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}f\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}\bigg)\bigg)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\Phi_{2}(f)\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,D}.

From the functional inequalities (
32
) and (
33
), we derive the exponential decay

W_{\mu_{N,D}}(P_{t}f)\leqslant\exp\bigg(-\frac{t}{1+\frac{3(M_2^2/a^2+1)}{2\lambda_1}}\bigg)W_{\mu_{N,D}}(f)\leqslant\exp(-2\lambda_2 t)W_{\mu_{N,D}}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0,

which completes the proof.

The convergence rate
\lambda_{2}
does not depend on the number of modes
N
or the discretization size
D
. Nevertheless, Theorem
3.5
requires the special Assumption (iii), i.e.,
N\leqslant D
.

3.4
Extension of the the standard PIMD

In Theorem
3.5
, we have proved the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the underdamped Matsubara mode PIMD (
17
) under Assumptions (i)(ii)(iii). In particular, Assumption (iii) is satisfied when the discretization size
D=N
or
D=\infty
. In these two cases, the Boltzmann distribution of the continuous loop is given by

\displaystyle(D=N)~:~\mu_{N,N}(\xi)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)(|\xi_{k}|^{2}+|\eta_{k}|^{2})-\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{D})\bigg)\bigg\},

\displaystyle(D=\infty)~:~~~~\mu_{N}(\xi)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k}^{2}+a)(|\xi_{k}|^{2}+|\eta_{k}|^{2})-\int_{0}^{\beta}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau)\bigg)\mathrm{d}\tau\bigg\}.

Theorem
3.5
directly shows the Matsubara mode PIMD for sampling
\pi_{N,N}(\xi)
and
\pi_{N}(\xi)
has the uniform-in-
N
convergence rate
\lambda_{2}=\frac{a^{2}}{3M_{2}^{2}+5a^{2}}\exp(-4\beta M_1)
.

Given the ergodicity results of the Matsubara mode PIMD, we can conveniently extend the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity to the standard PIMD. Let
N
be an odd integer, we employ the
N
normal mode coordinates
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
to represent the
N
beads of the ring polymer (see Appendix
B
). The Boltzmann distribution of the
N
beads is given in (
15
):

\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}(\xi,\eta)\propto\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a)(|\xi_{k}|^{2}+|\eta_{k}|^{2})-\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{N})\bigg)\bigg\},

and the standard PIMD for sampling
\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}(\xi,\eta)
is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\xi}_{k}&=\eta_{k},\\
\dot{\eta}_{k}&=-\xi_{k}-\frac{\beta_{N}}{\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\nabla V^{a}(x_{N}(j\beta_{N}))c_{k}(j\beta_{N})-\eta_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a}}\dot{B}_{k},\end{aligned}\right.

(34)

where we choose the damping rate
\gamma=1
. Similar to the quantity (
21
) in the Matsubara mode PIMD, we define the entropy-like quantity of
f(\xi,\eta)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2dN}
:

W_{\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}}(f):=\bigg(\frac{M_{2}^{2}}{a^{2}}+1\bigg)\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}}(f)+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2dN}}\frac{|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f-\nabla_{\xi_{k}}f|^{2}+|\nabla_{\eta_{k}}f|^{2}}{f}\mathrm{d}\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}.

Utilizing the same approaches with Theorem
3.5
, we can prove the uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the standard PIMD (
34
).

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
N
be an odd integer. Under Assumptions (i)(ii), let
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the Markov semigroup of the standard PIMD (
34
), then for any positive smooth function
f(\xi,\eta)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2dN}
,

W_{\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}}(P_{t}f)\leqslant\exp(-2\lambda_2 t)W_{\mu_{N,N}^{\mathrm{std}}}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0,

where the convergence rate
\lambda_{2}=\frac{a^{2}}{3M_{2}^{2}+5a^{2}}\exp(-4\beta M_1)
.

From Theorem
3.7
, we conclude that the standard PIMD has uniform-in-
N
ergodicity.

Remark 3.8
.

The uniform-in-
N
ergodicity of the standard PIMD (
34
) should also hold true when
N
is an even integer, and it requires a slight modification on the normal mode transform (
38
).

We note that Theorem 3.5 of
[
36
]
also provides a result of the dimension-free ergodicity of the PIMD, and the major difference is that
[
36
]
studies the preconditioned Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (pHMC) rather than the preconditioned Langevin dynamics, which is a more popular thermostat used in the PIMD
[
37
]
. Also, it is required in (3.10) that the duration parameter in the pHMC needs to be small enough.

Normal mode coordinates in path integral representation

We employ a slightly different approach from
[
37
]
to derive the normal mode coordinates. For simplicity, we assume the number of modes
N
be an odd integer.
Let
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
the mode coordinates, and
define the continuous loop by

x_{N}(\tau)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(\tau),~~~~\tau\in[0,\beta],

where
\{c_{k}(\cdot)\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
are the Fourier basis functions defined in Section
2.2
. Suppose the discretization size in
[0,\beta]
is also
N
, then
the
N
bead positions of the loop are

x_{j}=x_{N}(j\beta_{N})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}c_{k}(j\beta_{N}),~~~~j=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

(38)

From (
36
), the grid values of
c_{k}(\cdot)
satisfy the normalization condition

\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}c_{k}(j\beta_{N})c_{l}(j\beta_{N})=\delta_{k,l},~~~~k,l=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

where
\delta_{k,l}
is the Kronecker delta.
Then we have the equality

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|x_{j}|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\xi_{k}|^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}c_{k}^{2}(j\beta_{N})=\frac{1}{\beta_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\xi_{k}|^{2}.

(39)

The same procedure can be used to compute the spring potential

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|x_{j}-x_{j+1}|^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x_{j}\cdot(2x_{j}-x_{j-1}-x_{j+1})={4}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sin^{2}\bigg(\frac{\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil\pi}{N}\bigg)|\xi_{k}|^{2}.

By defining the normal mode frequencies

\omega_{k,N}=\frac{2}{\beta_{N}}\sin\bigg(\frac{\lceil\frac k2\rceil\pi}{N}\bigg),~~~~k=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

we can conveniently write the spring potential as

\frac{1}{\beta_{N}^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|x_{j}-x_{j+1}|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega_{k,N}^{2}|\xi_{k}|^{2}.

(40)

Recall that in the standard PIMD with
N
beads, the energy of the ring polymer is

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{std}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\beta_{N}^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|x_{j}-x_{j+1}|^{2}+\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V(x_{j}),

then we employ the equalities (
39
) and (
40
) to rewrite
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{std}}(x)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{std}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\beta_{N}^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|x_{j}-x_{j+1}|^{2}+\frac{a\beta_{N}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}|x_{j}|^{2}+\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V^{a}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a)|\xi_{k}|^{2}+\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{N})\bigg).

Therefore, the classical Boltzmann distribution in the standard PIMD is given by

\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\omega_{k,N}^{2}+a)|\xi_{k}|^{2}-\beta_{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}V^{a}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\xi_{k}c_{k}(j\beta_{N})\bigg)\bigg\}.

Appendix C
Generalized
\Gamma
calculus

We review the generalized
\Gamma
calculus developed in
[
32
,
33
]
, which deals with the ergodicity
of the Markov processes with degenerate diffusions, for example, the underdamped Langevin dynamics. The generalized
\Gamma
calculus
is based on the Bakry–Émery theory
[
31
]
.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
be a reversible Markov process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
(P_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the Markov semigroup. Let
L
be the infinitesimal generator of
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, and
\pi
be the invariant distribution. Then
L
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\pi)
.
In the classical Bakry–Émery theory, the carré du champ operator
\Gamma_{1}(f,g)
and the iterated operator
\Gamma_{2}(f,g)
are defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(L(fg)-gLf-fLg),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(L\Gamma_{1}(f,g)-\Gamma_{1}(f,Lg)-\Gamma_{1}(g,Lf)).

The curvature-dimension condition
CD(\rho,\infty)
is known as the function inequality

\Gamma_{2}(f,f)\geqslant\rho\Gamma_{1}(f,f),~~~~\mbox{for any smooth function $f$}.

For a given positive smooth function
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the relative entropy of
f
with respect to the invariant distribution
\pi
by

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\log f\mathrm{d}\pi-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\pi\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\pi.

If
\rho>0
, then
CD(\rho,\infty)
implies the log-Sobolev inequality (see Equation (5.7.1) of
[
31
]
)

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(f)\leqslant\frac{1}{2\rho}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\Gamma_{1}(f,f)}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi,~~~~\mbox{for any positive smooth function $f$}.

(41)

and thus the exponential decay of the relative entropy (see Theorem 5.2.1 of
[
31
]
)

\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leqslant e^{-2\rho t}\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0.

(42)

Inspired from the operators
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, we define the generalized
\Gamma
operator as follows.

Definition C.1
.

Suppose
f
is a smooth function, and
\Phi(f)
is a function of
f
and the derivatives of
f
. For a stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
with generator
L
, define the generalized
\Gamma
operator by

\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}(L\Phi(f)-\mathrm{d}\Phi(f)\cdot Lf),

where
\mathrm{d}\Phi(f)\cdot g
for two smooth functions
f,g
are given by

\mathrm{d}\Phi(f)\cdot g=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Phi(f+sg)-\Phi(f)}{s}.

The expression of
\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)
can be obtained via the following result (Lemma 5 of
[
33
]
).

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
C_{1},C_{2}
are two linear operators and
\Phi(f)=C_{1}f\cdot C_{2}f
, then

\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)=\Gamma_{1}(C_{1}f,C_{2}f)+\frac{1}{2}C_{1}f\cdot[L,C_{2}]f+\frac{1}{2}[L,C_{1}]f\cdot C_{2}f,

where
\Gamma_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the classical carré du champ operator.

In the following we assume the stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is solved by

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~~~~t\geqslant 0,

where
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the drift force,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
is a constant matrix, and
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then the generator of
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is given by

Lf(x)=b(x)\cdot f(x)+\nabla\cdot(D\nabla f),

(43)

where
D=\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the constant diffusion matrix.
For the generator
L
given in (
43
), we calculate the
\Gamma
operator
\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)
with some classical choices of
\Phi
.

Example C.3
.

If
\Phi(f)=|f|^{2}
, then we can take
C_{1}=C_{2}=1
in Lemma
C.2
and obtain

\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)=\Gamma_{1}(f,f)=\frac{1}{2}\big[\nabla(D\nabla(f^{2}))-2f\nabla(D\nabla f)\big]=(\nabla f)^{\mathrm{T}}D\nabla f.

In particular, since
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is positive semidefinite, we always have
\Gamma_{1}(f)\geqslant 0
.

Example C.4
.

If
\Phi(f)=f\log f
, then by direct calculation we have

\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\nabla\cdot\big(D(\log f+1)\nabla f\big)-(\log f+1)Lf\Big]=\frac{(\nabla f)^{\mathrm{T}}D\nabla f}{2f}.

Example C.5
.

If
\Phi(f)=|Cf|^{2}/f
for some linear operator
C
, then

\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)\geqslant\frac{Cf\cdot[L,Cf]}{f}.

(44)

The proof below is given in Lemma 7 of
[
33
]
.

Proof C.6
.

It is easy to verify for any smooth functions
f,g
, there holds

L(fg)=gLf+fLg+2\Gamma_{1}(f,g).

By replacing
f\rightarrow|Cf|^{2}
and
g\rightarrow 1/f
, we have

\displaystyle L\bigg(\frac{|Cf|^{2}}{f}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f}L(|Cf|^{2})+|Cf|^{2}L\bigg(\frac{1}{f}\bigg)+2\Gamma_{1}\bigg(|Cf|^{2},\frac{1}{f}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f}L(|Cf|^{2})+|Cf|^{2}\bigg(-\frac{Lf}{f^{2}}+\frac{2}{f^{3}}\Gamma_{1}(f)\bigg)+\frac{4Cf\cdot\Gamma_{1}(Cf,f)}{f^{2}}.

(45)

Note that

\mathrm{d}\bigg(\frac{|Cf|^{2}}{f}\bigg)\cdot|Cf|^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{d}(|Cf|^{2})\cdot Lf}{f^{2}}-|Cf|^{2}\frac{Lf}{f^{2}}.

(46)

Hence from (
45
)(
46
) and the definition of the generalized
\Gamma
operator in (
C.1
), we have

\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)\geqslant\frac{\Gamma_{|C\cdot|^{2}}(f)}{f}+\frac{1}{f^{3}}|Cf|^{2}\Gamma_{1}(f)+\frac{2Cf\cdot\Gamma_{1}(Cf,f)}{f^{2}},

(47)

where
\Gamma_{|C\cdot|^{2}}
is the generalized

\Gamma
operator induced by the function
|Cf|^{2}
.
Since the matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is positive semidefinite, we have the Cauchy-Swarchz inequality

|\Gamma_{1}(f,Cf)|^{2}\leqslant\Gamma_{1}(f,f)\Gamma_{1}(Cf,Cf),~~~~\mbox{for any smooth function $f$}.

(48)

From (
47
) and (
48
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\Gamma_{1}(Cf,Cf)+Cf\cdot[L,C]f}{f}+\frac{|Cf|^{2}\Gamma_{1}(f,f)}{f^{3}}

\displaystyle~~~~-2\sqrt{\frac{|Cf|^{2}\Gamma_{1}(f,f)}{f^{3}}}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{1}(Cf,Cf)}{f}}\geqslant\frac{Cf\cdot[L,C]f}{f}.

Hence we obtain the desired result.

Now we establish the curvature-dimension conditions for the generalized
\Gamma
operators. The following result relates the time derivative of
\Phi(f)
with
\Gamma_{\Phi}(f)
.

Lemma C.7
.

Given the constant
t>0
, for any
s\in[0,t]
, we have the equality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\big[P_{s}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)(x)\big]=2P_{s}\Gamma_{\Phi}(P_{t-s}f)(x).

As a consequence, for any
t\geqslant 0
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma_{\Phi}(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi.

Proof C.8
.

Using the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\big[P_{s}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)\big]

\displaystyle=LP_{s}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)+P_{s}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\big[\Phi(P_{t-s}f)\big]

\displaystyle=LP_{s}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)+P_{s}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Phi(P_{t-s-r}f)-\Phi(P_{t-s}f)}{r}

\displaystyle=LP_{s}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)-P_{s}\mathrm{d}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)\cdot LP_{t-s}f

\displaystyle=P_{s}\big(L\Phi_{t-s}f-\mathrm{d}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)\cdot LP_{t-s}f\big)

\displaystyle=2P_{s}\Gamma_{\Phi}(P_{t-s}f).

Integrating the equality over the distribution
\pi
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{s}\Phi(P_{t-s}f)\mathrm{d}\pi=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma_{\Phi}(P_{t-s}f)\mathrm{d}\pi.

(49)

Since
\pi
is the invariant distribution, replacing
t-s
by
s
in (
49
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma_{\Phi}(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi,

which completes the proof.

By choosing
\Phi(f)=f\log f
, Lemma
C.7
implies

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(\nabla f)^{\mathrm{T}}D\nabla f}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\Gamma_{1}(f,f)}{f}\mathrm{d}\pi.

(50)

If we have the log-Sobolev inequality (
41
), from (
50
) we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leqslant-2\rho\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(f),

which implies
\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leqslant e^{-2\rho t}\mathrm{\,Ent}_{\pi}(f)
, and we recover the result in (
42
).

Now we state the main theorem, which provides the generalized curvature-dimension condition for degenerate diffusion processes.

Theorem C.9
.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
be an ergodic stochastic process with the invariant distribution
\pi
. If for two functions
\Phi_{1}(f)
and
\Phi_{2}(f)
, there hold the functional inequalities

0\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{1}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi-\Phi_{1}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\pi\bigg)\leqslant c\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{2}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi,

(51)

\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}(f)\geqslant\rho\Phi_{2}(f)-m\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}(f),

(52)

for some constants
c,\rho,m>0
, then by defining the entropy-like quantity

W_{\pi}(f)=m\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{1}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi-\Phi_{1}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\pi\bigg)\bigg)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{2}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi,

we have the exponential decay

W_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leqslant\exp\bigg(
-\frac{2\rho t}{1+m c}
\bigg)W_{\pi}(f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0.

The proof below is given in Lemma 3 of
[
33
]
.

Proof C.10
.

Using Lemma
C.7
and (
52
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}W_{\pi}(P_{t}f)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg[m\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{1}(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{2}(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi\bigg]

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(m\Gamma_{\Phi_{1}}+\Gamma_{\Phi_{2}}\big)(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi\leqslant-2\rho\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{2}(P_{t}f)\mathrm{d}\pi.

(53)

Using (
51
) and the definition of
W(f)
, we have

W_{\pi}(f)=m\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{1}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi-\Phi_{1}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{d}\pi\bigg)\bigg)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{2}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi\leqslant(1+mc)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{2}(f)\mathrm{d}\pi.

Hence (
53
) implies

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}W_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leqslant-\frac{2\rho}{1+mc}W_{\pi}(P_{t}f),~~~~\forall t\geqslant 0,

yielding the desired result.
