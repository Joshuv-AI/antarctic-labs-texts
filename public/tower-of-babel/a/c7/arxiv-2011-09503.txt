Multifractal Fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Processes
Laurent Chevillard, Marc Lagoin, Stéphane G. Roux

Abstract
. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process can be seen as a paradigm of a finite-variance and statistically stationary rough random walk. Furthermore, it is defined as the unique solution of a Markovian stochastic dynamics and shares the same local regularity as the one of the Brownian motion. A natural generalization of this process able to reproduce the local regularity of a fractional Brownian motion of parameter H H is provided by the fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Based on previous works, we propose to include some Multifractal corrections to this picture using a Gaussian Multiplicative Chaos. The aforementioned process, called a Multifractal fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, is a statistically stationary finite-variance process. Its underlying dynamics is non-Markovian, although non-anticipating and causal. The numerical scheme and theoretical approach are based on a regularization procedure, that gives a meaning to this dynamical evolution, which unique solution converges towards a well-behaved stochastic process.

1.
Introduction

In many situations, for example some physical phenomena governed by thermal fluctuations such as the position of small particles suspended in a quiescent fluid, the standard Brownian motion, or Wiener process,
W(t)
is called to model and give account of certain quantities of interest. As often required by the nature of the situation, the phenomenon itself is statistically stationary and of finite-variance, therefore calling for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process. It is defined as the unique solution of the stochastic dynamics given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle dX(t)=-\frac{1}{T}X(t)dt+dW(t),

where the increment
dW(t)
of the Wiener process
W
can be viewed as a Gaussian white noise, and which meaning is clear as a random distribution. The large time scale
T>0
governs the correlation duration of the process. As a continuous-time stochastic process,
X(t)
is nowhere differentiable, and we can say that sample paths of
X
, as those of
W
, are H
\ddot{\text{o}}
lder continuous of any order strictly less than
H=1/2
, where
H
is the Hurst exponent.

Other physical phenomena, for example fluid turbulence
[
20
]
, or some time series encountered in finance
[
22
,
6
]
, exhibit in a statistically averaged sense a different Hurst exponent
H\in]0,1[
, a local regularity that can be reproduced by fractional Brownian motions
[
23
]
. Once again, as required for instance by the physics of turbulence which suggests a roughness of order
H\approx 1/3

[
14
]
, such phenomena are eventually statistically stationary and are of finite variance. In this case, using fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (fOU) processes
[
10
]
to model them seems more appealing. They could be defined as the unique solution of the following dynamics

(1.2)

\displaystyle dX_{H}(t)=-\frac{1}{T}X_{H}(t)dt+\text{``}dW_{H}(t)\text{''},

where the random noise
dW_{H}
entering in
1.2
is indicated between quotes to remind that its meaning has to be clarified. Whereas fractional motions can be written as stochastic integrals in a natural way
[
28
,
25
,
32
]
for any
H\in]0,1[
, the meaning pathwise of the random noise
dW_{H}(t)
, and consequently the meaning of the dynamics of
X_{H}
(
1.2
), is not obvious when
H<1/2
. As proposed in
[
11
]
, a regularization procedure over a small timescale,
\epsilon>0
, gives both a precise and explicit meaning to this random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H}(t)
. Accordingly, the unique solution
X_{\epsilon,H}(t)
of
1.2
once forced by such a random noise is shown in
[
11
]
to converge in law, as
\epsilon\to 0
, towards the canonical fOU process of
[
10
]
. Actually, the same regularized version of the noise
dW_{H}(t)
, as it is studied in
[
11
]
and that we are using in this communication, was previously proposed in the theoretical setup developed in
[
1
]
(see Remark
2.2
).

As we can see, this regularization procedure over
\epsilon
is necessary, when
H<1/2
, to define pathwise the dynamics schematically presented in
1.2
. This regularization turns out to be crucial when multifractal corrections, usually referred as the intermittency phenomenon in the literature of turbulence
[
14
]
, are added to the picture while considering a Gaussian multiplicative chaos
[
21
,
19
,
30
]
. It can be formally obtained as

(1.3)

\displaystyle M_{\gamma}(t)\;\text{``}=\text{''}\;e^{\gamma\widetilde{X}(t)},

where the Gaussian field
\widetilde{X}
entering in
1.3
is asked to be logarithmically correlated, and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
is a free parameter. Once again, the equality given in
1.3
is put in quotes because the meaning of the exponential of such a field, necessarily being of infinite variance, has yet to be clarified. In this article, we will show how to give a precise meaning to
M_{\gamma}
(
1.3
) while still relying on a regularization procedure over
\epsilon
, as it is usually done
[
30
]
. Moreover, we will see that the Gaussian process
\widetilde{X}
can actually be defined as a fOU process, solution of the stochastic dynamics provided in
1.2
, for a vanishing Hurst exponent
H=0
. This approach was already proposed and used in different setups to model some aspects of the random nature of fluid turbulence
[
11
,
26
,
27
,
33
,
13
]
. This method of construction differs from other ones found in the literature
[
15
,
24
,
16
]
. In particular, it includes the notion of causality, and its respective stochastic evolution, to this logarithmically correlated Gaussian field.

Finally, we propose a way to incorpore into the dynamics of the fOU process (
1.2
) a Multiplicative Chaos (
1.3
), and define respectively the stochastic evolution

(1.4)

\displaystyle dX_{H,\gamma}(t)=-\frac{1}{T}X_{H,\gamma}(t)dt+\text{``}dW_{H,\gamma}(t)\text{''},

which eventually makes sense only up to a regularization scale
\epsilon>0
. Taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, the asymptotic process obtained while considering the unique solution of
1.2
, will be referred as the Multifractal fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (MfOU) process. It can be seen as a generalization of the Multifractal random walk of
[
4
]
to a causal framework, and for any
H\in]0,1[
instead of the unique value
H=1/2
.

Organization of the paper.

In Section
2
, we present the general setup of the article, and especially the regularized version of the stochastic evolution proposed in
1.4
. We include several remarks on the various ingredients entering in the construction of this dynamics. Section
3
is devoted to the statement of our results. In particular, we list in Proposition
3.1
some important marginals of the MfOU process
X_{H,\gamma}
, including variance and high order moments of its increments. In Section
4
, we propose a method to synthesize a statistically stationary solution of the dynamics (
1.4
) in a periodical framework, and compare our theoretical predictions to our numerical estimations. We gather the proofs of Proposition
3.1
in Section
5
.

2.
Setup, notations and remarks

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a Wiener processes
W(t)
, note by
dW(t)
its increments over
dt
at time
t
. In the sequel, we will be studying the unique statistically stationary solution
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
of the following linear stochastic equation

(2.1)

dX_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)=-\frac{1}{T}X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)dt+dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t),

where enters a large timescale
T>0
and a small regularizing timescale
\epsilon>0
. For a given Hurst exponent
H\in]0,1[
, and an additional free parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, the dynamical evolution proposed in
2.1
is maintained by a random noise
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
that reads

(2.2)

dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)=\omega_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)dt+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)dW(t),

where we have introduced the statistically stationary random field

(2.3)

\omega_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-s+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(s)dW(s).

The random noise
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
2.2
) and field
\omega_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
2.3
) involve respectively a local version of and a linear operation on the white noise
dW
weighted by a positive random field
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)
given by

(2.4)

M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)=e^{\gamma\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}(t)-\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)}.

Finally, the zero-average statistically stationary Gaussian process
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}
that enters in the definition of the positive random weight
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
(
2.4
) is independent of the white noise
dW(t)
and is of finite variance for any
\epsilon>0
. We furthermore ask, in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, its covariance to diverge in a logarithmic fashion at small arguments, such that

(2.5)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}(t)\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}(t+\tau)\right)=\ln_{+}\left(\frac{T}{|\tau|}\right)+g(\tau),

with
\ln_{+}(s)=\max(\ln s,0)
and
g
a bounded and continuous function of its argument which goes to 0 at large arguments. A natural and causal way to achieve this is to define
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}
as the unique statistically stationary solution of an independent linear and Gaussian evolution which is based on the dynamics provided in
2.1
with
\gamma=0
, using furthermore the boundary value
H=0
. Defined such a way,
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}
can be seen as a regularized fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck of vanishing Hurst exponent.

Remark 2.2
.

For
\gamma=0
and thus
M_{\epsilon,0}(t)=1
, the noise
dW_{\epsilon,H,0}(t)
(
2.2
) is a well-posed Gaussian and causal process. Using the language developed in
[
1
]
, it is the stochastic differential, in the sense of Itô, of a continuous semimartingale
W_{\epsilon,H,0}(t)
which is a natural regularized form a fractional Brownian motion.

Remark 2.3
.

As we will see, the parameter
\gamma
eventually governs entirely the level of non Gaussianity and multifractality of the process
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
. To this regard, for
\gamma\neq 0
, the dynamics provided in
2.1
can be seen as a generalization of the one of the regularized fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process detailed in
[
11
]
. Using current notation, the unique statistically stationary solution of
2.1
with
\gamma=0
can be conveniently written as

(2.6)

X_{\epsilon,H,0}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{t-s}{T}}dW_{\epsilon,H,0}(s).

It is a finite variance Gaussian process for any
H\in]0,1[
and finite
\epsilon>0
, and it remains so as
\epsilon\to 0
. We note by
X_{H,0}
the Gaussian stochastic process which marginals coincide with the limiting values as
\epsilon\to 0
of those of
X_{\epsilon,H,0}
, and we call it a fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (fOU) process
[
10
]
. We have

(2.7)

\mathbb{E}\left(X_{H,0}^{2}\right)\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(X_{\epsilon,H,0}^{2}\right)=\frac{T^{2H}\left[\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}}{2\sin(\pi H)},

where enters the function
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{z-1}e^{-x}dx
. More generally, since
X_{\epsilon,H,0}
is a zero average Gaussian process, it is fully characterized by its covariance function which can be explicitly derived and behaves in the limit of vanishing regularizing scale
\epsilon\to 0
as, considering without loss of generality
\tau\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(X_{H,0}(t)X_{H,0}(t+\tau)\right)\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(X_{\epsilon,H,0}(t)X_{\epsilon,H,0}(t+\tau)\right)

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sin(\pi H)}\frac{\left[\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}}{\Gamma\left(2H\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{\tau+h}{T}}-\frac{\tau-h}{2|\tau-h|}e^{-\frac{|\tau-h|}{T}}\right]h^{2H-1}dh,

(2.9)

\displaystyle=T^{2}\left[\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2i\pi\omega\tau}\frac{|2\pi\omega|^{1-2H}}{1+4\pi^{2}\omega^{2}T^{2}}d\omega.

Notice that the expression of the correlation function (
2.8
), derived in
[
11
]
, is especially well suited to study its decay towards 0 at large arguments, in an alternative way as it is proposed in
[
10
]
, whereas its spectral form (
2.9
), that can be derived and justified using more general expressions of
[
11
]
, clarifies that indeed, the asymptotic process
X_{H,0}
coincides with the fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process of
[
10
]
, and differs from the so-called tempered fractional Brownian motion of
[
8
]
.

Finally, as a consequence of
2.8
, it is straightforward to see that fractional Brownian motions
[
23
]
and fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes
[
10
]
share the same local regularity, as it is quantified by the second moment of the increments. Defining the increment as
\delta_{\tau}X_{\epsilon,H,0}(t)\equiv X_{\epsilon,H,0}(t+\tau)-X_{\epsilon,H,0}(t)
, we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\delta_{\tau}X_{H,0}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\delta_{\tau}X_{\epsilon,H,0}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0}}\frac{1}{\sin(\pi H)}\frac{\left[\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}}{\Gamma\left(2H+1\right)}|\tau|^{2H}.

Remark 2.4
.

Let us now comment on the Gaussian field
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}

entering in the construction of the regularized Multifractal fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process defined in
2.1
. As already mentioned,
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}
can be viewed as a regularized fOU process, as it is recalled in remark
2.3
, with the boundary value
H=0
. More precisely, it can be written as the following stochastic integral

(2.11)

\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{t-s}{T}}d\widetilde{W}_{\epsilon,0,0}(s),

where

(2.12)

d\widetilde{W}_{\epsilon,0,0}(t)=-\frac{dt}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{t}(t-s+\epsilon)^{-\frac{3}{2}}d\widetilde{W}(s)+\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}d\widetilde{W}(t),

where the white noise
d\widetilde{W}(t)
is chosen independently of
dW(t)
. As we will see, the Gaussian process
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}
eventually shares similar statistical properties as those of the processes constructed in Refs.
[
15
,
24
]
in the asymptotic limit of vanishing
\epsilon
, although we recall that it is moreover defined as a stationary solution of a well-posed stochastic evolution.

In this case, the finiteness of variance is not guaranteed as the regularizing scale
\epsilon
goes to zero, and indeed, it is shown in Refs.
[
11
,
27
,
2
]
that instead,

(2.13)

\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)\equiv\mathbb{E}\left(X_{\epsilon,0,0}^{2}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right).

Whereas the variance of
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}
(
2.13
) diverges logarithmically with the regularizing parameter
\epsilon
, its covariance remains bounded, and we can write, as a direct application of Eqs.
2.8
and
2.9
, for any
\tau>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}(t)\widetilde{X}(t+\tau)\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(X_{\epsilon,0,0}(t)X_{\epsilon,0,0}(t+\tau)\right)

(2.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{\tau+h}{T}}-\frac{\tau-h}{2|\tau-h|}e^{-\frac{|\tau-h|}{T}}\right]h^{-1}dh,

(2.15)

\displaystyle=\pi T^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2i\pi\omega\tau}\frac{|2\pi\omega|}{1+4\pi^{2}\omega^{2}T^{2}}d\omega.

Starting from
2.14
, we recover that, once the limit
\epsilon\to 0
has been taken, the variance of
\widetilde{X}
is infinite while noticing that

(2.16)

\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}(t)\widetilde{X}(t+\tau)\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0}}\ln\left(\frac{1}{|\tau|}\right).

Because of the logarithmic behavior of the covariance function of
\widetilde{X}
for small arguments (
2.16
) and its decay towards 0 at large arguments
\tau\gtrapprox T
, we will find convenient, and useful for subsequent calculations, to write in a formal way

(2.17)

\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}(t)\widetilde{X}(t+\tau)\right)=\ln_{+}\left(\frac{T}{|\tau|}\right)+g(\tau),

with
\ln_{+}(s)=\max(\ln s,0)
and
g
a continuous, even and bounded function of its argument, as it is done in
[
30
]
. We could get an exact expression for the bounded function
g
entering in
2.17
using in particular
2.14
, although, as we will see, it will turn out to be sufficient to specify its value at the origin to understand with precision the statistical properties of the MfOU process at small scales. We find

(2.18)

g(0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln(h)e^{-h}dh\approx-0.577216,

which is known as minus the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

Remark 2.5
.

The last ingredient entering in the construction of the MfOU is the positive random weight
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
defined in
2.4
. It ressembles a regularized version of a Gaussian Multiplicative Chaos, as it is called in the probability theory literature
[
30
]
, although, the way it is renormalized makes it converging locally as
\epsilon\to 0
to a random distribution, which is zero-average and unit-variance. In the best situations, where subsequent integrals involve a deterministic function
f
that ensures dominated convergence, we will write, making use of the expression of the covariance of
\widetilde{X}
(
2.17
),

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\;\mathbb{E}\;\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)

\displaystyle dW(t)\bigg)^{2n}=

\displaystyle\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}T^{2\gamma^{2}n(n-1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(t_{i})\prod_{i<j=1}^{n}\frac{e^{4\gamma^{2}g(t_{i}-t_{j})}}{|t_{i}-t_{j}|_{+}^{4\gamma^{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}dt_{i},

where
|.|_{+}=\exp(\ln_{+}|.|)
and for a finite range of values of
\gamma^{2}
, depending on the regularity of
f
in the vicinity of the origin and on the requirement of the finiteness of this moment. As we will see, the very precise behavior of the random noise
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)dW(t)
when integrated against test functions, such as it is depicted in
2.19
, is eventually responsible of Multifractal corrections to the monofractal Gaussian process fOU.

Let us mention that defining a Multiplicative Chaos (
2.4
) in such a causal framework is far from anecdotal. In particular, causality is crucial when modeling several aspects of fluid turbulence
[
11
,
27
]
. Making use of Itô’s lemma, the regularization procedure allows to derive a stochastic evolution for the random weight
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
(
2.4
) itself, and we obtain

(2.20)

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)}{M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)}=-\frac{1}{T}\left[\ln M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)+\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)+\gamma\widetilde{\omega}_{\epsilon,0,0}(t)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2\epsilon}\right]dt+\gamma\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}d\widetilde{W}(t),

where the causal field
\widetilde{\omega}_{\epsilon,0,0}(t)
corresponds to the expression of
2.3
, with
H=0
,
\gamma=0
, and using the increment of the independent Wiener process
\widetilde{W}
instead of
W
. It is interesting to observe that each of the terms entering in the RHS of
2.20
diverges pathwise as
\epsilon\to 0
, whereas the variance of the stationary solution remains bounded, with
\mathbb{E}M^{2}_{\epsilon,\gamma}=1
. Notice also that the dynamical evolution of
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
(
2.20
) can be seen as a stochastic Volterra system
[
17
]
.

Finally, let us remark that the random weight
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
(
2.4
), which is a log-normal random field since it is taken as the exponential of a Gaussian process, can be seen as a particular case of the more general class of log-infinitely divisible measures
[
7
,
31
,
5
,
9
,
29
,
30
]
.

3.
Statement of the results

Proposition 3.1
.

The unique solution of the stochastic differential equation
2.1
goes at large time towards a statistically stationary process
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
that we can write

(3.1)

X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\frac{t-s}{T}}dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s),

where the integration is performed over the random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
defined in
2.2
. In this statistically stationary range, it is a zero-average process for any
H
and
\epsilon>0
. Note by
X_{H,\gamma}(t)
the process which marginals coincide with the limiting behavior of those of
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
as
\epsilon\to 0
, and call it a Multifractal fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (MfOU) process. For any
H\in]0,1[
, independently of
\gamma
, the covariance of
X_{H,\gamma}
coincides with the one of the underlying fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (fOU) process
X_{H,0}
. In particular, its variance is finite (
2.7
) and its covariance can be derived in an exact fashion (
2.8
).

More generally, considering the integer
n\geqslant 1
, and for any
H\in]0,1[
and
\gamma^{2}<\min(1/4,H/(n-1))
, the MfOU process has a finite moment of order
2n
, that is

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}\left(X_{H,\gamma}^{2n}\right)<\infty,

and we can obtain their exact expression as multiple integrals (see Section
5
devoted to proofs, and in particular Eqs.
5.30
,
5.31
and
5.37
). Furthermore, the MfOU process exhibits at small scales a multifractal behavior, as it can be characterized in a simple fashion by the behavior at small scales of the moments of its increments. We have, for an integer
n\geqslant 1
,
H\in]0,1[
and
\gamma^{2}<\min(1/4,H/(n-1))
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\delta_{\tau}X_{H,\gamma}\right)^{2n}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}c_{H,\gamma,2n}\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\,\left(\frac{\tau}{T}\right)^{2nH-2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}},

where the multiplicative constant
c_{H,\gamma,2n}
reads

(3.4)

\displaystyle c_{H,\gamma,2n}

\displaystyle=T^{2nH}e^{2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}g(0)}\times

\displaystyle\int_{\{u_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[(1-u_{i})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u_{i}\leqslant 1}-(-u_{i})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u_{i}\leqslant 0}\right]^{2}\prod_{i<j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|u_{i}-u_{j}|^{4\gamma^{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}du_{i},

with
g(0)
given in
2.18
.

Remark 3.2
.

Because of the independence of the multiplicative chaos
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
and the Wiener process
W
entering in the definition of the random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
2.2
), the MfOU process
X_{H,\gamma}
has a symmetric probability distribution. In consequence, all odd moments of
X_{H,\gamma}
and its increments vanish.

Remark 3.3
.

Let us examine the values of the factors
c_{H,\gamma,2n}
in simple cases. First, their expression (
3.4
) is understood when
n=1
as being

(3.5)

\displaystyle c_{H,\gamma,2}=c_{H,0,2}

\displaystyle=T^{2H}\int_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left[(1-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u\leqslant 1}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u\leqslant 0}\right]^{2}du

\displaystyle=\frac{T^{2H}}{\sin(\pi H)}\frac{\left[\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}}{\Gamma\left(2H+1\right)},

which is independent of
\gamma
, and coincides with the one of the fOU process (its statistical properties are recalled in Remark
2.3
), as it is claimed in Proposition
3.1
. Also, it is straightforward to get these factors for the non-intermittent
\gamma=0
case since the MfOU process corresponds to the fOU process, and is thus Gaussian. It is then clear, looking at the expression provided in
3.4
, that

(3.6)

\displaystyle c_{H,0,2n}=\left(c_{H,0,2}\right)^{n},

as it is expected for a Gaussian process. The last situation for which the expression of these factors
c_{H,\gamma,2n}
(
3.4
) gets simple is when
H=1/2
. In this case, we obtain

(3.7)

\displaystyle c_{1/2,\gamma,2n}

\displaystyle=T^{n}e^{2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}g(0)}\int_{\{u_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\in[0,1]^{n}}\prod_{i<j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|u_{i}-u_{j}|^{4\gamma^{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}du_{i},

that can be explicitly computed for the fourth-order moment (i.e.
n=2
) as

(3.8)

\displaystyle c_{1/2,\gamma,4}

\displaystyle=T^{2}e^{4\gamma^{2}g(0)}\int_{\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\in[0,1]^{2}}\frac{1}{|u_{1}-u_{2}|^{4\gamma^{2}}}du_{1}du_{2}

\displaystyle=T^{2}e^{4\gamma^{2}g(0)}\frac{1}{(1-4\gamma^{2})(1-2\gamma^{2})},

as it was already derived in
[
33
]
. Exact values of
c_{H,\gamma,2n}
for any appropriate
H
and
\gamma
are not known, but can be evaluated while performing a numerical estimation of the integrals entering in
3.4
.

Remark 3.4
.

Notice that the characterization of the sample path properties of
X_{H,\gamma}
and its multifractal nature, as it is announced in Proposition
3.1
, using only its even moments of integer orders, and those of its increments, is rather simple and incomplete. A more complete analysis of the statistical properties of the MfOU process, in the spirit of
[
12
]
, remains to be done.

4.
Numerical synthesis and statistical analysis

4.1.
Numerical synthesis

The purpose of this Section is to propose a simple and efficient numerical algorithm that generates a trajectory of the statistically stationary MfOU process
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
(
3.1
), using similar ideas as they are developed in
[
33
]
. As it is considered in the theoretical framework developed in Proposition
3.1
, trajectories are given for a finite
\epsilon>0
, and for
H\in]0,1[
and a certain value of the parameter
\gamma
, the values of
H
and
\gamma
being possibly conditioned on the existence of moments of a certain order as
\epsilon\to 0
. Alternatively, we could solve the underlying dynamics itself (
2.1
) and thus access the transient towards the statistically regime using a time-marching discretization, as it is proposed in
[
11
,
27
]
. In any case, we need to come up with a numerical approximation of the underlying random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
(
2.2
) which can be seen as a convolution of some random measure, that we will focus on in the sequel, with a causal and deterministic kernel, making the overall dynamics (
2.1
) non-Markovian. It is then tempting to use the Discrete Fourier transform (DFT) to design a numerical approximation
\widehat{dW}_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}[t]
of the continuous measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
, and incidentally, work in a framework for
T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}
-periodic functions.

The causal and deterministic kernel involved in the definition of
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
reads

(4.1)

h_{\epsilon,H}(t)=\mathfrak{h}_{\epsilon,H}(t)+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\delta(t),

where

(4.2)

\mathfrak{h}_{\epsilon,H}(t)=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)(t+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{t\geqslant 0}.

In this continuous setting, the function
h_{\epsilon,H}
(
4.1
) behaves differently depending of the value of the Hurst exponent
H
, as it is classically interpreted when considering fractional Gaussian noises
[
23
]
. For
H<1/2
, the function
h_{\epsilon,H}
is negative outside the origin, and is integrable over
t\in\mathbb{R}
, such that its integral vanishes. On the opposite, when
H>1/2
, its integral diverges, whereas it is equal to unity for
H=1/2
. Notice also that, in the vicinity of the origin,
h_{\epsilon,H}
is distributional, with a weight that depends on
\epsilon
.

From a numerical point of view, over a finite set
t\in[-T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2,T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]
, we need to make a compromise between enforcing integral properties of
h_{\epsilon,H}
(
4.1
), especially for
H<1/2
, and respecting the strength of its distributional nature in the vicinity of the origin. The most natural way to build a numerical estimation
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,H}
of the function
h_{\epsilon,H}
(
4.1
) is obtained in the Fourier space, such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,H}[t]=\text{DFT}^{-1}\left\{\text{DFT}\{\mathfrak{h}_{\epsilon,H}\}[\omega]+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}[t],

where the function
\mathfrak{h}_{\epsilon,H}
is defined in
4.2
, properly periodized over
[-T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2,T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]
, and the frequency
\omega
belonging to the discrete set
[-1/(2T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}),1/(2T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}})]
. It is clear that integral properties of
h_{\epsilon,H}
(
4.1
) are not exactly fulfilled by the proposed estimation
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,H}
. In particular, the integral over
[-T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2,T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2]
is equal, up to numerical errors regarding the finiteness of the time step, to
(T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/2+\epsilon)^{H-1/2}
, which eventually goes towards the expected limits as
T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\to\infty
. In the same time, the estimation reproduces accurately the distributional nature of the function
h_{\epsilon,H}
close to the origin.

An important numerical observation made in
[
33
]
shows that constraining the estimation
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,H}
to fulfill the integral properties of
h_{\epsilon,H}
is crucial for the development of logarithmic correlations for the particular case
H=0
(
2.16
), and brings numerical stability. Recall that for this boundary case
H=0
, very different behaviors are expected. In particular, whereas the variance of the fOU process (
2.6
), when
H\in]0,1[
, remains bounded as
\epsilon
goes to zero (
2.7
), it is expected to diverge when
H=0
(
2.11
) in a logarithmic fashion (
2.13
).
Because of this observation and for these reasons, we treat the
H=0
case separately and propose the following estimator
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,0}
of the function
h_{\epsilon,0}
as

(4.4)

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,0}[t]=\text{DFT}^{-1}\left\{\text{DFT}\{\mathfrak{h}_{\epsilon,0}\}[\omega]-\text{DFT}\{\mathfrak{h}_{\epsilon,0}\}[0]\right\}[t].

It is now clear that the vanishing integral properties of
h_{\epsilon,0}
is exactly fulfilled by its estimation
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,0}
(
4.4
), whereas an unavoidable error is made on the strength of the Dirac distribution near the origin.

Given these precisions, call
N
the number of collocation points and
\Delta t=T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}/N
the time step, we propose the following numerical estimation
\widehat{\widetilde{X}}_{\epsilon}[t]
of the regularized fOU process of vanishing Hurst exponent
\widetilde{X}_{\epsilon}(t)
(
2.11
) as

(4.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{X}}_{\epsilon}[t]=\Delta t\;\text{DFT}^{-1}\left\{\text{DFT}\left\{e^{-t/T}1_{t\geqslant 0}\right\}\text{DFT}\left\{\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,0}\right\}[\omega]\text{DFT}\left\{\widehat{d\widetilde{W}}\right\}\right\}[t],

where
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,0}
is given in
4.4
, and
\widehat{d\widetilde{W}}
a collection of
N
independent instances of a zero average Gaussian random variable of variance
\Delta t
. To prevent from additional aliasing errors, consider long trajectories such that
T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}\gg T
, where
T
is the characteristic large scale of the OU kernel entering in
4.5
. Accordingly, an estimation
\widehat{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}[t]
of the random positive weight
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)
(
2.4
) is obtained while exponentiating the estimation
\widehat{\widetilde{X}}_{\epsilon}
(
4.5
), such that

(4.6)

\widehat{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}[t]=e^{\gamma\widehat{\widetilde{X}}_{\epsilon}[t]-\gamma^{2}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left(\left.\widehat{\widetilde{X}}_{\epsilon}\right.^{2}\right)},

where enter the empirical expectation
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}
of the square of the discrete process
\widehat{\widetilde{X}}_{\epsilon}
. We could have used alternatively the exact expression of this expectation, that can be easily derived even for a given finite
\epsilon>0
(see A13 of
[
33
]
), which eventually gives undistinguishable results using the parameters of subsequent simulations.

Finally, the estimator
\widehat{X}_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}[t]
of the statistically stationary MfOU process
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
(
3.1
) reads

(4.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}[t]=\Delta t\;\text{DFT}^{-1}\left\{\text{DFT}\left\{e^{-t/T}1_{t\geqslant 0}\right\}\text{DFT}\left\{\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,H}\right\}[\omega]\text{DFT}\left\{\widehat{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}\widehat{dW}\right\}\right\}[t],

where
\widehat{h}_{\epsilon,H}
is given in
4.3
,
\widehat{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}
in
4.6
and
\widehat{dW}
a collection of
N
independent instances of a zero average Gaussian random variable of variance
\Delta t
, independent of those of
\widehat{d\widetilde{W}}
.

\widehat{X}_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
4.7
) taking without loss of generality
T_{\text{\tiny{tot}}}=1
. We consider
N=2^{30}

H
, namely
H=1/3,\;1/2
and
2/3

and

concerning the regularizing scale, and generate ten independent trajectories.

5.
Proofs

Proving the statistical properties of the MfOU process
X_{H,\gamma}
, as they are stated in Proposition
3.1
, boils down to computing the moments of a random variable defined by a linear operation on the random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
2.2
), and then studying their limit when
\epsilon\to 0
. That is why we consider in the sequel the random variable

(5.1)

Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s),

where
f
is an appropriate deterministic test function, say bounded and compactly supported for the sake of presentation. For the time being, assume furthermore that
f
is differentiable over
\mathbb{R}
, note by
f^{\prime}
its derivative, an assumption that we will revisit and adapt when we will treat the particular case of the MfOU process
X_{H,\gamma}
in Section
5.3
. We recall here for convenience the expression of the random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
that enters in the definition of the random variable
Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
5.1
), as it was done in Definition
2.1
. We have

(5.2)

dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s)=\omega_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s)ds+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(s)dW(s),

where

(5.3)

\omega_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s)=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{s}(s-u+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(u)dW(u),

and
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
a regularized version of a Multiplicative Chaos (see Remark
2.5
), normalized such that
\mathbb{E}M^{2}_{\epsilon,\gamma}=1
. Recall also that
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
(
2.4
) is chosen independently of
dW
. It is then straightforward to see that
Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
is a zero-average random variable, and has more generally vanishing odd moments. In the sequel, we will find convenient to express the random measure
dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
5.2
) as a convolution product of the random measure
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}dW
, i.e.

(5.4)

dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s)=ds\int_{\mathbb{R}}h_{\epsilon,H}(s-u)M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(u)dW(u),

with the deterministic kernel

(5.5)

h_{\epsilon,H}(s)=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)(s+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{s\geqslant 0}+\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\delta(s),

as it was already considered in Section
4
devoted to numerics (see
4.1
).

5.1.
Second-order statistical properties

To prepare for subsequent calculations, first consider second-order statistical properties, and compute the variance of
Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
, in a similar way, although more general, as it has been done in
[
11
]
. We have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(s_{1})f(s_{2})\mathbb{E}\left[dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s_{1})dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(s_{1})f(s_{2})h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{1}-u)h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{2}-u)\mathbb{E}\left[M^{2}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(u)\right]ds_{1}ds_{2}du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u)du,

where we have used that
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
is independent of
dW
and normalized such that
\mathbb{E}(M_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{2})=1
(see Remark
2.5
), and introduced the quantity

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(s_{1})f(s_{2})h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{1}-u)h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{1}-u)ds_{1}ds_{2}.

This shows that there are no multifractal corrections on the second-order statistical properties of the random variable
Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
, which coincide with those of
Y_{\epsilon,H,0}
. More explicitly, the deterministic function
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}
(
5.7
) reads

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u)=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u+\epsilon)^{H-3/2}(s_{2}-u+\epsilon)^{H-3/2}1_{s_{1}\geqslant u}1_{s_{2}\geqslant u}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle+2\epsilon^{H-1/2}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s_{1})f(u)(s_{1}-u+\epsilon)^{H-3/2}1_{s_{1}\geqslant u}ds_{1}+\epsilon^{2H-1}f^{2}(u).

For
H\in]1/2,1[
, we can see that, in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, all integrals entering in
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}
(
5.8
) involve singular kernels that are all locally integrable, thus only the first term of the RHS survives. Call this limit the function
\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)
. It is a continuous and bounded function, which is furthermore compactly supported if it is assumed so for the test function
f
. In this case, we can write

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{H>1/2,\gamma}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}^{2}\right]\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)du

\displaystyle=\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)(s-u)^{H-3/2}1_{s\geqslant u}ds\right)^{2}du.

This last expression could be even further simplified, as it was done in
[
11
]
, while introducing the correlation product, although, as we will see, this procedure is difficult to generalize for higher order moments. Notice that this expression makes perfect sense since all singularities are locally integrable, and
f
is compactly supported.

For
H=1/2
, the situation is even simpler since the process
\omega_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
5.3
) vanishes, and we obtain

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{H=1/2,\gamma}^{2}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(s)ds.

Take now
H\in]0,1/2[
. Going back to the expression of
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}
(
5.8
), we can see that the first term in the RHS has no pointwise limit when
\epsilon\to 0
since singularities are non locally integrable, neither do the two other terms. To extract finite and diverging contributions, split the integral of the first term of the RHS of
5.8
in two parts while considering the integration over the sets
s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}
and
s_{1}\geqslant s_{2}
, and notice that they give an equal contribution. Perform then an integration by parts over the dummy variable
s_{1}
and obtain

(5.11)

\displaystyle 2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u+\epsilon)^{H-3/2}(s_{2}-u+\epsilon)^{H-3/2}1_{s_{1}\geqslant u}1_{s_{2}\geqslant s_{1}}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon,H}(u)+\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon,H}(u),

where we have introduced the quantities

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\epsilon,H}(u)=\mathcal{I}_{1,H,\epsilon}(u)-\mathcal{I}_{2,H,\epsilon}(u)

with

(5.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1,H,\epsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int f^{2}(s)(s-u+\epsilon)^{2H-2}1_{u\leqslant s}ds

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2H-1}f^{2}(u)-2\int f(s)f^{\prime}(s)(s-u+\epsilon)^{2H-1}1_{u\leqslant s}ds,

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2,H,\epsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\epsilon^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\int f(u)f(s)(s-u+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{u\leqslant s}ds,

and

(5.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon,H}(u)=-2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int f^{\prime}(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}-u+\epsilon)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{u\leqslant s_{1}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}.

Adding everything up, noticing that
\mathcal{I}_{2,H,\epsilon}(u)
(
5.13
) will cancel with the second term of the RHS of
5.8
, and that the contribution of order
\epsilon^{2H-1}
of
\mathcal{I}_{1,H,\epsilon}(u)
(
5.12
) will cancel with the third one, we obtain

(5.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u)=\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon,H}(u)+\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon,H}(u),

where
\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon,H}
is defined in
5.14
and

(5.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon,H}(u)

\displaystyle=-2\int f(s)f^{\prime}(s)(s-u+\epsilon)^{2H-1}1_{u\leqslant s}ds

\displaystyle=-2\int f(s+u)f^{\prime}(s+u)(s+\epsilon)^{2H-1}1_{s\geqslant 0}ds.

Whereas the pointwise limit as
\epsilon\to 0
of the function
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}
as written in
5.8
is not obvious when
H<1/2
, it becomes clear once written as in
5.15
. To see this, remark that the function
\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon,H}
(
5.16
) is continuous, bounded and its integral over
u\in\mathbb{R}
vanishes. Note its pointwise limit as the function
\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)
. Similarly, while taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, the strongest singularity entering in the expression of
\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon,H}
(
5.14
) is obtained along the diagonal
s_{1}=s_{2}=s=u
and is of order
(s-u)^{2H-2}
, which is locally integrable in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as long as
H>0
. Call then
\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)
the pointwise limit of
\mathcal{J}_{\epsilon,H}(u)
.

We finally obtain, after taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
,

(5.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{H<1/2,\gamma}^{2}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)+\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)du

\displaystyle=-2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f^{\prime}(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u)^{H-1/2}(s_{2}-u)^{H-3/2}1_{u\leqslant s_{1}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}duds_{1}ds_{2}.

5.2.
Higher-order moments

Let us now develop and generalize our calculations, as they were done to derive the variance of the random variable
Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
5.1
), for the moment of order
2n
, with
n
and integer greater than unity. As mentioned in Proposition
3.1
, it is clear that its probability law is symmetrical, as a direct consequence of the independence of the white noise field
dW(t)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
and the random positive weight
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(t)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
(
2.4
). Thus all moments of odd order vanish.

Start with introducing the following quantity that encodes the correlation structure of the multiplicative chaos
M_{\epsilon,\gamma}
(
2.4
) and write

(5.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(\{u_{i}\}_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n})=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}M^{2}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(u_{i})\right],

which converges as
\epsilon\to 0
towards a locally integrable function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
when
\gamma^{2}<\min[1/4,1/(2(n-1))]
(see Remark
2.5
), and that reads

(5.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})=T^{2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}}\prod_{i<j=1}^{n}\frac{e^{4\gamma^{2}g(u_{i}-u_{j})}}{|u_{i}-u_{j}|_{+}^{4\gamma^{2}}}.

We have

(5.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{\epsilon,H\in]0,1[,\gamma}^{2n}\right)=\int_{\{s_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\prod_{i=1}^{2n}f(s_{i})\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2n}dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\{s_{i},u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{4n}}\prod_{i=1}^{2n}f(s_{i})h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{i}-u_{i})\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2n}dW(u_{i})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{2n}M_{\epsilon,\gamma}(u_{i})\right]\prod_{i=1}^{2n}ds_{i}du_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{s_{i},u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}}\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(s_{2i-1})f(s_{2i})h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{2i-1}-u_{i})h_{\epsilon,H}(s_{2i}-u_{i})ds_{2i-1}ds_{2i}du_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u_{i})du_{i},

where the function
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u)
is defined in
5.7
. Recall from Section

5.1
that the function
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon,H}(u)
converges pointwise as
\epsilon\to 0
towards a function
\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)
which expression depends on the value of the parameter
H
. In particular, we obtained

(5.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H>1/2}}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)(s-u)^{H-3/2}1_{s\geqslant u}ds\right)^{2}

(5.22)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H=1/2}}f^{2}(u)

(5.23)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H<1/2}}\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)+\mathcal{K}_{H}(u),

with

(5.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=-2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{\prime}(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{u\leqslant s_{1}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2},

(5.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s+u)f^{\prime}(s+u)s^{2H-1}1_{s\geqslant 0}ds.

Remark that for any
H\in]0,1[
, the functions
\mathcal{L}_{H}
and
f
share similar supports, which ensures that a product on them will be integrable at large arguments. So justifying the existence of the limit

(5.26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{H,\gamma}^{2n}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}^{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{H}(u_{i})du_{i},

where the pointwise limit
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\epsilon,\gamma}
is provided in
5.19
, and in particular determining the range of parameters
H
and
\gamma
for which this limit makes sense, requires more assumptions on the test function
f
than only continuity and boundedness. Instead of treating this question from a general point of view, we propose in the following Section to focus on the test function suggested by the MfOU process
X_{H,\gamma}
, and to justify its statistical properties as they are stated in Proposition
3.1
.

5.3.
Statistical properties of the MfOU process

5.3.1.
Variance and higher order moments of the process

Let us now go back to our initial problem, that is obtaining the marginals of the process
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
3.1
), and their limit as the regularizing scale
\epsilon
goes to zero. We make use of the statistical stationarity of this process, and consider expectations at the time
t=0
. Concerning the moments of the process, consider the particular test function

(5.27)

f(s)=e^{\frac{s}{T}}1_{s\leqslant 0},

such that indeed
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
(
3.1
) at the given time
t=0
can be written as

(5.28)

X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s).

As we have seen in Section
5.2
, for vanishing regularizing scale
\epsilon\to 0
, its moment of order
2n
(
5.26
) brings into play the function
\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)
which explicit expression depends of the value of
H
(Eqs.
5.21
,
5.22
and
5.23
). We have

(5.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H>1/2}}1_{u\leqslant 0}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\int_{s=u}^{0}e^{\frac{s}{T}}(s-u)^{H-3/2}ds\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to 0^{-}}}(-u)^{2H-1},

which shows that
\mathcal{L}_{H}
is a bounded function that goes to zero at the origin as fast as a power-law. This behavior in the vicinity of the origin weakens the singular behavior of the multiplicative chaos, and extends its range of integrability (
5.19
). Hence, the
2n
-order moment of the MfOU process is given by, for
H\in]1/2,1[
and
\gamma^{2}<\min\left(1/4,H/(n-1)\right)
,

(5.30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(X_{H>1/2,\gamma}^{2n}\right)=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{H}(u_{i})du_{i},

where the function
\mathcal{L}_{H}
is given in
5.29
and
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
in
5.19
. The case
H=1/2
is rather simple since
\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)=f^{2}(u)=\exp(2u/T)1_{u\leqslant 0}
, and we obtain for
\gamma^{2}<\min\left(1/4,1/(2(n-1))\right)

(5.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(X_{H=1/2,\gamma}^{2n}\right)=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(u_{i})du_{i}.

Take now
H\in]0,1/2[
, and remark that, using the expression of the test function
f
(
5.27
),

(5.32)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{T}f(s)-\delta(s),

which shows that the function
f
is not differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
in a classical sense, as expected from a causal kernel. For this reason, the expression of
\mathcal{J}_{H}
(
5.24
) where enters the derivative
f^{\prime}
is questionable. Nonetheless, we can argue, going back to the integration by parts procedure that takes place in the decomposition proposed in
5.11
, that it makes sense to use the distributional derivative (
5.32
) in the expression of the function
\mathcal{J}_{H}
(
5.24
) that enters the expression of
\mathcal{L}_{H}
(
5.23
). In this case, the Dirac distribution entering in the expression of
f^{\prime}
(
5.32
) gives no contribution, and we obtain

(5.33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{T}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{u\leqslant s_{1}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{T}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)1_{u\leqslant 0}\int_{[u,0]^{2}}e^{\frac{s_{1}+s_{2}}{T}}(s_{1}-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}.

It is a positive, bounded and integrable function. To see this, we have for large negative arguments

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to-\infty}}-\frac{2}{T}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)(-u)^{2H-2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{-})^{2}}e^{\frac{s_{1}+s_{2}}{T}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}=-T\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)(-u)^{2H-2},

which is indeed integrable. To see the behavior of
\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)
at the origin, rescale the dummy variables
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
of
5.33
by
-u
and get

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to 0^{-}}}-\frac{2}{T}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)(-u)^{2H}\int_{[-1,0]^{2}}(s_{1}+1)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}+1)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to 0^{-}}}-\frac{1}{T}\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{H\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)}(-u)^{2H}.

Whereas the function
\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)
remains bounded, the function
\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)
entering in the expression of
\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)
(
5.23
) will be singular at the origin, although integrable. To see this, notice first that we cannot make sense in a simple fashion of the expression of the function
\mathcal{K}_{H}
(
5.25
) while injecting the distributional derivative of
f
(
5.32
) in it. Instead, go back to the integration by parts procedure that takes place in the decomposition proposed in
5.11
, and obtain, using the causal kernel
f
(
5.27
),

(5.34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}1_{u\leqslant 0}\left[2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{u}^{0}e^{2\frac{s}{T}}(s-u+\epsilon)^{2H-2}ds+\epsilon^{2H-1}e^{2\frac{u}{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}1_{u\leqslant 0}\left[(-u+\epsilon)^{2H-1}-\frac{2}{T}\int_{u}^{0}e^{2\frac{s}{T}}(s-u+\epsilon)^{2H-1}ds\right]

\displaystyle=1_{u\leqslant 0}\left[(-u)^{2H-1}-\frac{2}{T}\int_{u}^{0}e^{2\frac{s}{T}}(s-u)^{2H-1}ds\right].

This shows that
\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)
is singular in the vicinity of the origin, such that

(5.35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to 0^{-}}}(-u)^{2H-1},

since the second term of the third equality of
5.34
is bounded in
u
, and actually behaves proportionally to
(-u)^{2H}
near the origin. To check the integrability at large arguments, write
\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)
(
5.34
) as

(5.36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=1_{u\leqslant 0}\left[\frac{2}{T}\int_{u}^{0}e^{2\frac{s}{T}}\left[(-u)^{2H-1}-(s-u)^{2H-1}\right]ds+e^{2\frac{u}{T}}(-u)^{2H-1}\right]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to-\infty}}-\frac{2}{T}(2H-1)(-u)^{2H-2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}s\;e^{2\frac{s}{T}}ds

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{u\to-\infty}}\frac{T}{2}(2H-1)(-u)^{2H-2},

which is indeed integrable at large arguments. Actually, it can be shown that its integral over
u\in\mathbb{R}
vanishes. To see this, before taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in
5.34
, observe that the integral vanishes for any
\epsilon>0
, which is also true in the limit.

Hence, the existence of the
2n
-order moment of the MfOU process is governed by the singular behaviors of
\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)
(
5.35
) and
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}
(
5.19
), and is given by, for
H\in]0,1/2[
and
\gamma^{2}<\min\left(1/4,H/(n-1)\right)
,

(5.37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(X_{H<1/2,\gamma}^{2n}\right)=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{H}(u_{i})du_{i},

where the function
\mathcal{L}_{H}
is given by
\mathcal{L}_{H}=\mathcal{J}_{H}+\mathcal{K}_{H}
, with
\mathcal{J}_{H}
given in
5.33
and
\mathcal{K}_{H}
in
5.34
.

5.3.2.
Variance and higher order moments of the increments of the process

Proceed now with the calculation of the moments of velocity increments, and determine in particular their behavior at small scales. To do so, consider the particular test function

(5.38)

\displaystyle f(s)\equiv f_{\tau}(s)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\tau-s}{T}}1_{\tau-s\geqslant 0}-e^{-\frac{-s}{T}}1_{-s\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{s}{T}}\left(e^{-\frac{\tau}{T}}1_{\tau-s\geqslant 0}-1_{-s\geqslant 0}\right).

such that the increment
\delta_{\tau}X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)\equiv X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t+\tau)-X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(t)
of the MfOU process
X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}
(
3.1
) at the given time
t=0
can be written as

(5.39)

\delta_{\tau}X_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)dW_{\epsilon,H,\gamma}(s).

First consider
H>1/2
. In this case, make use of
5.21
to get

(5.40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H>1/2}}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{s}{T}}\left(e^{-\frac{\tau}{T}}1_{\tau-s\geqslant 0}-1_{-s\geqslant 0}\right)(s-u)^{H-3/2}1_{s\geqslant u}ds\right)^{2}.

Rescaling by the positive quantity
\tau
, we get

(5.41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}(\tau u)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H>1/2}}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\tau^{2H-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{\tau s}{T}}\left(e^{-\frac{\tau}{T}}1_{s\leqslant 1}-1_{s\leqslant 0}\right)(s-u)^{H-3/2}1_{s\geqslant u}ds\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\tau^{2H-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(1_{s\leqslant 1}-1_{s\leqslant 0}\right)(s-u)^{H-3/2}1_{s\geqslant u}ds\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}\tau^{2H-1}\left[(1-u)^{H-1/2}1_{u\leqslant 1}-(-u)^{H-1/2}1_{u\leqslant 0}\right]^{2}.

Similarly for the term associated to the multiplicative chaos
\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})
(
5.19
), we have, once its variables rescaled by the scale factor
\tau
,

(5.42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{\tau u_{i}\})

\displaystyle=T^{2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}}\prod_{i<j=1}^{n}\frac{e^{4\gamma^{2}g[\tau(u_{i}-u_{j})]}}{|\tau(u_{i}-u_{j})|_{+}^{4\gamma^{2}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}\left(\frac{\tau}{T}\right)^{-2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}}e^{2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}g(0)}\prod_{i<j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|u_{i}-u_{j}|^{4\gamma^{2}}},

where
g(0)
is given in
2.18
, such that, for
H\in]1/2,1[
and
\gamma^{2}<\min\left(1/4,H/(n-1)\right)
, we have

(5.43)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\delta_{\tau}X_{H>1/2,\gamma}\right)^{2n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{H}(u_{i})du_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\tau^{n}\int_{\{u_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{M}_{\gamma}(\{\tau u_{i}\})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{H}(\tau u_{i})du_{i}

(5.44)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}c_{H,\gamma,2n}\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}n!}\,\left(\frac{\tau}{T}\right)^{2nH-2n(n-1)\gamma^{2}},

with
c_{H,\gamma,2n}
provided in
3.4
. This proves the power law announced in
3.3
. For
H=1/2
, since
\mathcal{L}_{H}
is rather simple in this case (
5.22
), we have

(5.45)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}(\tau u)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{H=1/2}}e^{2\frac{\tau u}{T}}\left(e^{-\frac{\tau}{T}}1_{u\leqslant 1}-1_{u\leqslant 0}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}\left(1_{u\leqslant 1}-1_{u\leqslant 0}\right)^{2},

which justifies the expression of the constant
c_{1/2,\gamma,2n}
(
3.4
) and of the power law announced in
3.3
for
H=1/2
, with again
\gamma^{2}<\min\left(1/4,1/(2(n-1))\right)
.

Take now
H\in]0,1/2[
and write the derivative of the kernel
f
(
5.38
) as

(5.46)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}f(s)-\delta(\tau-s)+\delta(s).

which makes sense as a distribution. Once again, it can be shown that we can inject safely this distribution into the expression of
\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)
(
5.24
), and obtain, while noticing that the Dirac function centered on
\tau
entering in
5.46
has no contribution,

(5.47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{H}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{T}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int f(s_{1})f(s_{2})(s_{1}-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{u\leqslant s_{1}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle-2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u\leqslant 0}\int f(s_{2})(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{0\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{2}.

Rescaling by the positive quantity
\tau
, we get

(5.48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{H}(\tau u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{T}\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\tau^{2H}\int f(\tau s_{1})f(\tau s_{2})(s_{1}-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{u\leqslant s_{1}}1_{s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{1}ds_{2}

\displaystyle-2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\tau^{2H-1}(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u\leqslant 0}\int f(\tau s_{2})(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{0\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}-2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\tau^{2H-1}(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u\leqslant 0}\int(1_{s_{2}\leqslant 1}-1_{s_{2}\leqslant 0})(s_{2}-u)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}1_{0\leqslant s_{2}}ds_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}-2\tau^{2H-1}(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}1_{u\leqslant 0}\left[(1-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-u)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right].

As encountered when deriving the variance of the process, we cannot make sense of the expression of
\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)
(
5.25
) when the derivative of
f
(
5.46
) is distributional. Instead, write
\mathcal{K}_{H}
as

(5.49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(u)\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[2\left(H-\frac{1}{2}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(s)(s-u+\epsilon)^{2H-2}1_{s\geqslant u}ds+\epsilon^{2H-1}f^{2}(u)\right]

\displaystyle=(\tau-u)^{2H-1}1_{u\leqslant\tau}+(-u)^{2H-1}\left(1-2e^{-\frac{\tau}{T}}\right)1_{u\leqslant 0}-\frac{2}{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(s)(s-u)^{2H-1}1_{s\geqslant u}ds.

Once rescaled by
\tau>0
, we obtain

(5.50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(\tau u)

\displaystyle=\tau^{2H-1}\left[(1-u)^{2H-1}1_{u\leqslant 1}+(-u)^{2H-1}\left(1-2e^{-\frac{\tau}{T}}\right)1_{u\leqslant 0}\right]

\displaystyle-\tau^{2H}\frac{2}{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(\tau s)(s-u)^{2H-1}1_{s\geqslant u}ds

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}}\tau^{2H-1}\left[(1-u)^{2H-1}1_{u\leqslant 1}-(-u)^{2H-1}1_{u\leqslant 0}\right].

Finally, summing up the equivalents of
\mathcal{J}_{H}(\tau u)
(
5.48
) and
\mathcal{K}_{H}(\tau u)
(
5.50
) as
\tau\to 0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}(\tau u)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits_{\tau\to 0^{+}}^{H<1/2}}\tau^{2H-1}\left[(1-u)^{H-1/2}1_{u\leqslant 1}-(-u)^{H-1/2}1_{u\leqslant 0}\right]^{2},

which justifies the expression of the constant
c_{H,\gamma,2n}
(
3.4
) and of the power law announced in
3.3
for
H<1/2
, with again
\gamma^{2}<\min\left(1/4,H/(n-1)\right)
.
