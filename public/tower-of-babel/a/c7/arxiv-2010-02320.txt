A category of Banach space functors
Mauricio GARAY and Duco van Straten

Abstract
. We introduce a sheaf theoretic viewpoint on functional analysis designed for infinite dimensional Lie group actions. We develop functional calculus for Banach valued functors and, in particular, prove the existence of an exponential map for a certain class of operators that generalise first order partial differential operators.



I
.
Introduction

I.1
.
Normal forms and versal deformations

Lie group actions play an important role in many branches of mathematics: classification problems, representation theory, constructions of moduli spaces and stacks etc. At the frontier between mathematics and physics, one encounters loop groups, gauge groups, diffeomorphism groups which all turn out to be infinite dimensional. There is no general theory for the study of group actions in the infinite dimensional context and, when hard analysis turns out to be efficient –for instance in KAM theory– the arguments are in general highly technical. Our aim is to provide a categorical framework from which one can easily deduces general results on group actions, such as normal forms and versal deformations.

To explain these two notions –which are at the origin of our investigations– consider the familiar finite dimensional situation of a Lie group
G
acting on a vector space
E
. The
normal form problem
consists in finding a “good representative” in each orbit. The Jordan normal form for the adjoint action of
G=Gl(n,\mathbb{C})
on
E=M(n,\mathbb{R})
provides such an example, although the notion of “goodness” is subjective and related to the type of applications one has in mind.

Once a normal form is chosen, say
x
, the
versal deformation problem
around the point
x
consists in finding an affine space
x+F\subset E
which is transversal to the
G
-orbits. The family
x+F
is then called
a versal deformation of
x
. Versal deformations might be thought as families of normal forms. Although for
G=GL(n,\mathbb{R})
the subject is classical, versal deformations of minimal dimension for the adjoint action were given by Arnold in 1971 and extended by Brieskorn and Slodowy to simple Lie algebras
[
3
,
6
,
30
]

I.2
.
Infinitesimal action

A basic technique for finding normal forms and versal deformations consists in looking at the infinitesimal action

\mathfrak{g}\times E\longrightarrow E

of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
on the tangent plane at the point
x
which we identify with the vector space
E
. The question is then: does the surjectivity of the map

\rho:\mathfrak{g}\times F\longrightarrow E,\ (v,t)\mapsto v(x)+t

implies that any element of a neighbourhood of
x
can be taken back to an element of
F
?

In finite dimensional spaces and even in Banach spaces, the answer is yes, as one can prove using the implicit function theorem.

I.3
.
The Lie iteration

There is a more traditional way to prove that a subspace
F
is transversal to the orbits than to use implicit function theory based on perturbation theory. We write an element in a neighbourhood of
x\in E
as

x_{0}=x+r_{0}

Then we search
v_{0}\in\mathfrak{g}
and
t_{0}\in F
solving the
homological equation
:

v_{0}(x)=r_{0}+t_{0}

and define a first element
g_{0}=e^{-v_{0}}
. We then have:

g_{0}(x+r_{0})=x+t_{0}+r_{1}.

We now repeat the procedure and try to find
v_{1},t_{1}
solving the equation:

v_{1}(x+t_{0})=r_{1}+t_{1}

and take
g_{1}=e^{-v_{1}}
. We get now:

g_{1}g_{0}(x+t_{0}+t_{1})=x+t_{0}+t_{1}+r_{2}

and so on. This process –that we call the
Lie iteration
– can be used in many places: differential equations, singularities of mappings, hypersurface singularities etc. The important point is that in many applications the homological equation can easily be solved along the transversal
x+F
.

I.4
.
Convergence of the iteration

The choice of a bounded operator

u:E\longrightarrow E

of a Banach algebra transforms the Banach space
E
into a module over the ring of convergent power series having a convergence radius
R>\|u\|.
This leads to the standard
functional calculus
which in particular establishes the existence of an exponential together with the estimate

\|e^{u}\|\leq e^{\|u\|}.

If we now assume that the solutions
(v_{n})
to the homological equations and the elements of the transversal
(t_{n})
might be chosen so to define summable norms sequences then the Lie iteration converges and

\|\prod_{i\geq 0}e^{v_{i}}\|\leq\prod_{i\geq 0}e^{\|v_{i}\|}

This is the only case where the convergence of the Lie iteration is elementary. We refer to
[
12
]
for details. In locally convex spaces, the situation is much more involved, as usually there is no functional calculus available.

I.5
.
The exponential map

In the sixties, infinite dimensional Lie groups appeared in gauge theories and in hydrodynamics. After Arnold proposed to include hydrodynamics as the geometry of the group of volume preserving diffeomorphisms, precise definitions were used to prove existence and unicity results
[
2
,
4
,
9
,
20
,
22
,
26
]
. Since then the study of infinite dimensional Lie group theory remained a subject of active research. We refer the interested reader to
[
16
,
18
,
25
,
27
]
.

Now, in the finite dimensional situation, a Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
is related to its group
G
via the exponential map

\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow G,\ v\mapsto e^{v}.

In the infinite dimensional situation, the situation is much more complicated. Even if we assume the exponential to be well-defined, it is in general not a local diffeomorphism. Milnor had already observed this fact for the group
Diff(\mathbb{S}^{1})
; his argument is quite simple: if a diffeomorphism turns out to be the exponential of a vector field, then linearising the vector field would linearise the mapping as well, but any neighbourhood of the identity contains many non-linearisable diffeomorphisms
[
23
, Warning 1.6]
. For locally convex spaces, no general theorem stating the existence of such a mapping is known (see
[
25
]
for a survey).

In particular, the manifold structure of these infinite dimensional Lie groups is not given by the exponential but rather by special parametrisations.
For instance, if
v
is the germ of a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

{\rm Id\,}+v
is the germ of a diffeomorphism and the map

v\mapsto{\rm Id\,}+v

can be used to define an infinite dimensional Lie group structure for germs of diffeomorphism.

The problem is then that for these special parametrisations, simple operations rapidly become complicated ; for instance the inverse map of
{\rm Id\,}+v
is given by Lagrange’s inversion formula which is already non-trivial. This contrasts with the inverse of
e^{v}
which is simply
e^{-v}
. Considerations of special subgroups like symplectomorphisms or volume-preserving diffeomorphisms require even more complicated arguments.

I.6
.
The Morse lemma

What happens to the notion of normal forms and versal deformation in the infinite dimensional context? To get an idea of the complexity involved in the convergence of the Lie iteration, let us consider the action of germs of biholomorphisms on the algebra
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
of convergent power series. To be even more precise, let us analyse the following elementary form of the Morse lemma:

Lemma I.6.1
.

Assume
f=x^{2}+o(x^{2})\in\mathbb{C}\{x\}
then there exists a biholomorphic map-germ
x=\varphi(y)
such that

f\circ\varphi(y)=y^{2}

Proof.

The proof is straightforward. We write

f(x)=x^{2}g(x),\ g(x)=1+o(1)

and define
y=x\sqrt{g}

∎

The locally convex Lie group of biholomorphic map germs

\varphi:(\mathbb{C},0)\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C},0)

acts on the space
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
. The lemma says that the affine subspace
x^{2}+{\mathcal{M}}^{3}
lies inside the orbit of
x^{2}
where

{\mathcal{M}}=\{f\in\mathbb{C}\{x\}:f(0)=0\}

stands for the maximal ideal.

Let us now look at the solution given by the Lie iteration.
If we choose any vector field
v=a(x)\partial_{x}
where
a
lies in
{\mathcal{M}}^{2}
(the square of the maximal ideal of the local ring
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
), it is not difficult to show the convergence of the formal power series

e^{v}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{v^{n}}{n!}

So let us write the Lie iteration starting from a particular case, say

f(x)=x^{2}+x^{3}

Here
E={\mathcal{M}}^{2}
and
F=\{0\}
and the Lie algebra is given by germs of vector fields
a(x)\partial_{x},\ a\in{\mathcal{M}}^{2}
. We have

v_{0}=\frac{x^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}.

Then we need to compute

e^{-v_{0}}f=x^{2}+o(x^{3})

and so on. Here the exponential might be interpreted either as the formal power series or as the time
1
flow of the vector field but how to prove the convergence of the Lie iteration in this case?

We would need estimates for the normalising fields
v_{0},v_{1},\dots
and therefore introduce Banach norms on the locally convex space
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
which induce operator norms on derivations. To construct these norms, one usually chooses open neighbourhoods in
\mathbb{C}
over which the functions are holomorphic and then try to control
the effect of taking the exponential and how it transforms these domains. Although this particular case of the Morse lemma is one of the simplest examples, the convergence of the iteration is already a non trivial exercise.

I.7
.
Lie iteration vs implicit function theorems

So in applications, the convergence of the Lie iteration or similar methods is hard to establish and is usually done by a long series of estimates, which require great skill. One of the first examples that displayed such virtuosity was given by Arnold in its original’s proof of the KAM theorem
[
1
]
.

The work of Moser represents a radical change. Moser proposed to base infinite dimensional group action on implicit function theorems. This project has been achieved with some success: Sergeraert proved versal deformation of mappings, Moser did find a simple proof to Arnold’s theorem for vector fields which is the toy model of KAM theory
[
5
,
10
,
24
,
28
,
31
]
.

So the use of implicit function theorems was regarded as the functional analytic solution to the long and tedious computations, as Zehnder enthusiastically wrote in 1972:
For
instance, while all previous proofs of the theorems in question involve infinitely many coordinate change of variables and consequently complicated convergence arguments, we shall avoid this inconvenience and work in a function space over a fixed set of variables
[
31
]
.

It took nevertheless more than 10 years before a baby model of the KAM theorem (Kolmogorov invariant torus theorem) was proven and, as far as we are aware of, no proof of the full KAM theorem of 1963 has been
published along these lines
[
5
]
(see also
[
10
]
).
So among the two roads –perturbation theory and Nash-Moser theorems– none of them seem to give a way out : whichever we choose heavy computations and tricky arguments are waiting for us!

In fact these two roads merge and Moser’s program can be followed together with perturbation theory in the analytic setting. We will develop a framework which surprisingly produces a functional calculus, that we call
functorial calculus
which goes beyond the case of Banach algebras. Both an exponential mapping with explicit estimates and a simple convergence criterion emerges from its definition. In fact we shall prove that, like in the finite dimensional situation:
the product of exponentials converges if and only if the elements of the Lie algebra we exponentiate are summable for some appropriate norms.
So the
complicated convergence arguments
of Zehnder will disappear.

I.8
.
Why categories and not just locally convex spaces?

The answer can already be found in the works of Siegel and Kolmogorov: one has to keep track of how the domains over which the maps are defined
[
19
,
29
]
.

The classical way to keep track of these domains is to take disks indexed according to their radii and show that although the radii become smaller they converge to a positive limit. So one easily imagines that by considering more complicated cases, the complexity of computations grows rapidly and like in tensor calculus, we rapidly run into a debauch of indices and estimates for the sizes of the different sets and the norms of the operators which are involved.

The categorical way to handle this problem is to consider indices as if they were the base of a fibration, like coordinates in a topological space. The fibre over a set may consist of holomorphic functions or vector fields defined over this set and we think of it as a kind of bundle. Of course, in the background, the sets are still indexed but the categorical setting looks somehow ”coordinate free”. There are many places in mathematics where the operation of ”dropping indices” involves a simplification of the setup : Matrices/Linear algebra, Tensors/Differential geometry, Equations/Schemes.

I.9
.
What is new? (or at least seems to be new)

In the classical case, one considers a single Banach chain indexed by an interval or by integers, the scale is sometimes increasing and sometimes decreasing. This is not the case here: starting from an interval, we construct Banach spaces over different kind of spaces like open triangles, closed triangles and subdomains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. These play the role of definition domains and the way they are mapped to each other is the key of understanding functorial calculus.

There is another point to be understood. While in the classical case, the norms are constructed a priori, in the categorical context, the norms emerge from general constructions. In particular, these are generally given by complicated formulas that would have been difficult to guess. Note also that unlike the classical approach, the formalism allows us to work with several scales at the same time. To see how this works in practise, we refer to
[
11
,
14
,
15
]
.

However, our framework is quite restrictive as we content ourselves to the case of linear mappings and therefore, this paper constitutes only the first steps towards a theory of Lie functors, which would go beyond the theory of locally convex Lie groups.

.
The Functor Category

Category theory is a language for a large part of mathematics and we assume the reader has some familiarity with notions from basic category theory and the
most basic categories like
Set
,
Vect
or
Ban
(with bounded linear maps as morphisms).
We refer to the first chapters of
[

]
or

[
17
]

II.1
.
Small categories

Recall that a
small category
is a category whose objects and morphisms form a set. We
will denote such categories by ordinary capitals like
A,B,C
and think
of them as sets enriched with an additional structure: for two objects

a,a^{\prime}\in A
there is specified a set of morphisms
Mor_{A}(a,a^{\prime})
, which
we may think of as arrows running from
a
to
a^{\prime}
.
The
opposite category

A^{op}
of
A
is defined to have the same
objects as
A
, but with all arrows reversed:

Mor_{A^{op}}(a,a^{\prime})=Mor_{A}(a^{\prime},a).

To an ordered set

1

We use the french convention. In most English texts, these objects are called
partially

(B,\geq)

a

, and empty otherwise. We will not make any distinction between an ordered set and the category it defines. The opposite category of

. Note that a plain set
B
can be seen as an example of an ordered set with trivial ordering (two elements can never be compared).

A (covariant) functor

between two small categories is the
natural generalisation of a map between sets.
In case

and
B
are ordered sets, the functor property of
\varphi:(A,\geq)\longrightarrow(B,\geq)

\varphi(a)\geq\varphi(a^{\prime})
.
The categorical approach contrasts with the naïve approach were compatibility relations need to be made explicit at every stage.

One can form the category
\mathcal{S}
of small categories, whose
objects are small categories, and where
Mor_{\mathcal{S}}(A,B)
consist of the

\varphi:A\longrightarrow B
.

.
Category of
B

We fix a small category
B
.
We denote by
{\bf Sets}
the category of sets with arbitrary maps as

{\bf Vect}
the category of vector spaces over a fixed field

K

{\bf Ban}
the category of
(real or complex) Banach spaces and continuous linear maps as morphisms.

Definition II.2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}

A

So a

F(\alpha\circ\beta)=F(\alpha)\circ F(\beta),

B

\mathcal{C}
. We call

B

\mathcal{C}
also

B
,
as one may think of such a functor as a family of objects in
\mathcal{C}

parametrised by
B
.

For instance a set over the ordered set
(\mathbb{N},\geq)
can be identified with an inverse system of sets and maps

\ldots\longrightarrow X_{n}\longrightarrow X_{n-1}\longrightarrow\ldots\longrightarrow X_{2}\longrightarrow X_{1}\longrightarrow X_{0}

and an object over the opposite category
(\mathbb{N},\leq)
can be identified with a directed system of sets

X_{0}\longrightarrow X_{1}\longrightarrow X_{2}\longrightarrow\ldots\longrightarrow X_{n}\longrightarrow X_{n+1}\longrightarrow\ldots

In general, if we consider a small category
B

B

{\bf Vect}

for each object of

The

II.3
.
Geometric picture

There is a simple way to associate to a relative set defined by a
functor
F:B\longrightarrow{\bf Sets}
an ordinary map of sets, that may provide some
additional geometrical intuition for the above discussion.

Definition II.3.1
.

Given a
B
-set defined by a functor
F:B\longrightarrow{\bf Sets}
,
we form the disjoint union

X(F):=\bigsqcup_{b\in B}X_{b},\;\;X_{b}:=F(b),

and call it the total space of the
B
-set.
The map

p:X(F)\longrightarrow B,\ X_{b}\ni x\mapsto b

is called the associated bundle and the set
X(F)_{b}=F(b)
is called the fibre
p^{-1}(b)
over
b\in B
.

We will denote elements of
X(F)
often by
pairs

(b,x)
, where

b\in B
and
x\in X_{b}
. This suppresses the (crucial)
information about the maps between the fibres. But these are often
clear from the context, in which case we often say that
p
or even
X
is
a
B
-set instead of specifying the functor
F
.

For a morphism
\varphi\in Mor_{B}(a,b)
there is a corresponding
map

\iota_{\varphi}:X_{a}\longrightarrow X_{b}

that we will call the
connecting morphism
of
\varphi
. If
B
is an ordered set this means simply that

a\geq b
and we may write
\iota_{\varphi}=\iota_{ba}
. The functor property translates
into the statements that for all
a,b,c\in B
with
a\geq b\geq c
.

\iota_{cb}\iota_{ba}=\iota_{ca}.

II.4
.
Relative vector spaces

For a
B
-object in
{\bf Vect}
or
{\bf Ban}
, we can form likewise its total space

E(F):=\bigsqcup_{b\in B}E_{b}.

which defines a
B
-set
p:E(F)\longrightarrow B
. All fibres
E_{b}=p^{-1}(b)
of this

B
-set now have a vector space or Banach space structure.

As such,
B
-vector spaces have some similarity with the idea
of a vector bundle or the étale space of a sheaf. Like for vector bundles, we sometimes identify the point
(b,x)
of the total space with the vector
x
on the fibre.

The fibres over different points

b\in B
can be very different, but the connecting morphism

F(\alpha):E_{a}\longrightarrow E_{b}
lying over a
morphism
\alpha\in Mor_{B}(a,b)
gives a way to compare fibres over
different points (“parallel transport”), and thus provides a
categorical version of a connection.

II.5
.
The section functor

Definition II.5.1
.

Let
p:X\longrightarrow B
be a set over a small category

B
, and
A
a subset of objects of
B
. Then
a section over
A
is a map

s:A\longrightarrow X

such that

p\circ s=Id_{A}.

We denote the set of all sections by
\Gamma(A,X)
.

So a section just consists of the choice of an element
x_{a}\in X_{a}
for each

a\in A
. In case of a relative vector space
p:E\longrightarrow B
, we may use
the vector space operations pointwise to define
the structure of a vector space on the set
\Gamma(A,E)
. Note that

\Gamma(A,E)=\prod_{a\in A}E_{a}

is just the
direct product
of all vector spaces
E_{a},a\in A
.
If we define the
support
of section
s:A\longrightarrow E
as the
set of
a\in A
for which
s(a)\neq 0
, then one can identify the
direct sum

\bigoplus_{a\in A}E_{a}\subset\prod_{a\in A}E_{a}

as the subspace of sections with
finite support
.

Note that in the definition of
\Gamma(A,X)
we ignored the arrows in
B
.
Of course these are usually important.

Definition II.5.2
.

Let
p:X\longrightarrow B
be a set over a small category
B
, defined by a
functor
F:B\longrightarrow{\bf Sets}
and
A
a sub-category of
B
.
A section
s\in\Gamma(A,X)
is called horizontal if for all

a,b\in A
and
\alpha\in Mor_{A}(a,b)
one has

F(\alpha)(s(a))=s(b).

We denote the subset of horizontal section by

\Gamma^{h}(A,X)\subset\Gamma(A,X).

II.6
.
The set of morphisms between
B
-objects are not
B
-objects

The contravariant nature of the Hom-functor in the first argument implies
that the morphisms of a category of
B
-objects are, in general, not
B
-objects. Let us clarify this point by a simple example.

Let
B
be the category
(\{0,1\},\leq)
. It has two objects
\{0,1\}
and one non trivial morphism in
Mor(0,1)
.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}={\bf Sets}
be the category of sets. A functor

F:B\longrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}

is nothing else than a map

f:X_{0}\longrightarrow X_{1}.

Consider two functors
F,G
associated to two mappings

f:X_{0}\longrightarrow X_{1},\ g:Y_{0}\longrightarrow Y_{1}

We have two associated bundles

X\longrightarrow B,\ Y\longrightarrow B

each one with two fibres and one connecting morphisms. If
Mor(X,Y)=Mor(F,G)
were a
B
-object, then it would have also a connecting morphism:

Mor(X_{0},Y_{0})\longrightarrow Mor(X_{1},Y_{1})

This means that to any morphism
\varphi_{0}\in Mor(X_{0},Y_{0})
can be transported to a morphism
\varphi_{1}\in Mor(X_{1},Y_{1})
in such a way that the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{X_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\varphi_{0}}
\textstyle{X_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi_{1}}
\textstyle{Y_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{g}
\textstyle{Y_{1}}

But this will not be possible in general. Take for instance

X_{0}=X_{1}=Y_{0}=Y_{1}=\{-1,1\}.

If
f
is a constant map and
g=\varphi_{0}=id
then
g\circ\varphi_{0}=\text{id},\ \varphi_{1}\circ f=\text{ constant}
.
Hence we find a contradiction.

In the theory of normal forms, we often deal with morphisms of a category and sometimes with morphisms of morphisms (say if we map a vector field to another), therefore the category of
B
-objects cannot be taken as our fundamental construction.

II.7
.
Construction of the functor category

We fix a category
\mathcal{C}
and consider
X\longrightarrow B
and
Y\longrightarrow B
two
{\mathcal{C}}
-objects over
B
, defined by functors

F,G:B\longrightarrow{\mathcal{C}}.

We observed that the set of morphisms between them does not form a
B
-set
in a natural way. There is no obvious way to associate to
\alpha\in Mor_{B}(a,b)
a morphism

u_{\alpha}:X_{a}=F(a)\longrightarrow Y_{b}=G(b).

To adress this problem, it turns out that instead of working over
a fixed category
B
, it is useful to consider consider the category of
all the relative objects of
\mathcal{C}
over all
possible small categories
B
. This defines a big category
\mathcal{C}_{*}
that we call the
functor category
of
{\mathcal{C}}
which we will now spell out.

Given two functors

F:B\longrightarrow\mathcal{C},\ G:C\longrightarrow\mathcal{C},

we define a set of morphisms which is
a
B^{op}\times C
-object in
\mathcal{S}
.
Here
B^{op}
stands for the opposite category obtained by reversing
the arrows.

Take two pairs
(a,b)
and
(c,d)
inside
B^{op}\times C
.
A morphism
(\alpha,\beta)\in Mor_{B^{op}\times C}((a,b),(c,d))

is defined as a pair of morphisms

III.1
.
Definition

Recall that we denote by

For relative Banach spaces

III.2
.
Norms matter

By definition, each Banach space

is equipped with a specific
norm
|-|_{b}

|x|=|v|_{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

and so we obtain a map

|-|:E\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\;\;x\mapsto|x|.

Together with the projection

to the base we obtain the

norm map

\nu:E\longrightarrow B\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ x=(b,v)\mapsto(b,|x|)=(b,|v|_{b}).

If

is a section, then for each

we can
consider the norm
|s(a)|
, hence we obtain a
norm function

|s|:=|-|\circ s:A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\;\;a\mapsto|s(a)|

of the section.

III.3
.
Banach spaces of sections

In perfect analogy with vector bundles equipped with a Riemannian metric, we consider the spaces of bounded sections and horizontal bounded
sections.

Definition III.3.1
.

Let

|s|_{A}:=\sup_{a\in A}|s(a)|.

Although elementary the following proposition is fundamental:

Proposition III.3.2

If

is a Banach space over
B
and
A
a subset of objects then
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
with
|-|_{A}

Proof.

We write temporarily

. Clearly, one has:

\displaystyle|s_{1}+s_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq|s_{1}|+|s_{2}|,

\displaystyle|\lambda\cdot s|

\displaystyle=|\lambda|\cdot|s|

and so
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
is a normed vector space.
Now consider a Cauchy sequence of sections

in
\Gamma^{b}(A,E)
, so for any

such that

|s_{n}-s_{m}|\leq\epsilon\;\;\;\textup{for}\;\;n,m\geq N.

As for all

it follows immediately that for any

are

and thus converge in

to an element

As

the sequence of norms

∎

If

The relative Banach space is defined by a functor

Consider a morphism

III.4
.
Rescaling

Rescaling the norm in a single Banach space is usually of no

as the rescaling may depend on the point

in question. For instance if we the derivative
for bounded holomorphic functions on a disk, then by rescaling the norm can be made bounded.

Definition III.4.1

A function

is called a weight function.
If

we define the rescaled Banach space
over

Note that the operation of rescaling has some formal resemblance

III.5
.
The derivative (part I)

From now on, we will use systematically the derivative to explain the various constructions leading to the notion of local operators.
We consider again the relative disc

D\longrightarrow(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\leq)

with fibre
D_{t}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<t\}.
Given an open set
U\subset\mathbb{C}
, let us denote by
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(U)
the Banach space of holomorphic bounded functions on
U
with supremum norm

|f|=\sup_{z\in U}|f(z)|.

We have a relative Banach space

E:={\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

with fibre
{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{t})
. For
t>s
, there are maps

u_{st}:{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{t})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{s}),\;\;f\mapsto\frac{df}{dz}

that are compatible under restrictions. This means that
the derivative is a global section

\frac{d}{dz}\in\Gamma^{h}(\Delta,Hom(E,E))

over the simplex

\Delta=\{(t,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{>0}:s<t\}.

However this section is not bounded, as for instance the function

f(z)=(1-z)\log(1-z)\in{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{t}),\ t=1

defines a unique horizontal section that we denote by:

\sigma=(1-z)\log(1-z)\in\Gamma^{h}(]0,1],{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D))

Its derivative defines a section only over
]0,1[
:

\sigma^{\prime}=-1-\log(1-z)\in\Gamma(]0,1[,{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D))

and for

close enough to

\sigma^{\prime}(s)=-1-\log(1-s)\xrightarrow[s\longrightarrow 0]{}+\infty

However one has the
Cauchy inequality

|\frac{d}{dz}f|_{s}\leq\frac{1}{t-s}|f|_{t}

as a direct consequence of Cauchy’s integral formula.
Therefore the derivative is an unbounded section over the simplex
\Delta

with a pole along the diagonal. This is the typical behaviour of the
norm for a differential operator of order
k
: the norm has a pole of order
k

along the diagonal. Now rescaling the space of operators by the function
\lambda(t,s)=t-s

transforms the unbounded section

into a bounded section in the rescaled Banach
space.

IV.2

Rescaled Kolmogorov spaces

By rescaling a Kolmogorov space

by a weight function
\lambda

is increasing, we get a new Kolmogorov space

with fibres
(E_{b},\lambda(b)|_{b}\cdot|)

However in many applications we encounter situations where

This is already the case for the derivative where we rescaled the relative space

In practise, this means that we lose completely our control on estimates. So we must find a way to construct a Kolmogorov space out of a relative Banach space and this is the Kolmogorification procedure. From a theoretical point of view, the construction might seem tautological but when we unwind the definition in concrete examples, we often end-up with complicated formulas for the norms which are constructed.

IV.3
.
Kolmogorification

For an ordered set
(B,\geq)
, the down-set of

consist of all
b^{\prime}\in B
for which

b\geq b^{\prime}
. Similarly we define in general

Definition IV.3.1
.

Let

We define the down-set of

]-\infty,b]:=\{b^{\prime}\in B\;|\;\exists\varphi\in Mor_{B}(b,b^{\prime})\},

and similarly the up-set

[b,+\infty[:=\{b^{\prime}\in B\;|\;\exists\varphi\in Mor_{B}(b^{\prime},b)\}.

Kolmogorov spaces have the following characteristic property:

Proposition IV.3.2
.

Let
E
be a Kolmogorov space
over
B
and
b\in B
. Then there is a natural isometry of Banach spaces

E_{b}=\Gamma^{\infty}(]-\infty,b],E).

Proof.

A vector

determines a unique horizontal section

s:]-\infty,b]\longrightarrow E,\ \varphi\mapsto\iota_{\varphi}(v)\in E_{\varphi(b)}

The Kolmogorov space property tells us that

|\iota_{\varphi}(v)|\leq|v|.

Therefore the norm function
|s(\varphi)|
attains its maximum at
\varphi=id
.
∎

To any Banach space
E
over
B
we can associate in a natural

over

which is the space of

of

over the

of

If

Clearly, if
\varphi inMor(b,b^{\prime})

we have

As the space of horizontal bounded sections is a Banach space (Proposition

we get that:

is a Kolmogorov space.

The space

If the vector space

The process of Kolmogorification has a similarity to the
sheafification of a presheaf.

Still we have not finished with the derivative. If

V
.
Functorial calculus

Like the well-known functional calculus in Banach spaces, we would like to
develop something similar in the context of Kolmogorov spaces, which we will
call
functorial calculus
. In contradistinction to functional calculus,
it turns out that for the moment a satisfactory functorial calculus can only
defined for a special class of operators, that we call
first order local operators
.
We will first define this class of operators and, to do this, we start
with two simple fundamental examples. The class we consider is much more general than what is usually understood by local operators (operators involving a finite number of derivatives). It includes for instance changes of scales, Hadamard products and division by holomorphic functions.

V.1
.
The derivative(part II)

Let us consider again the Kolmogorov space
E:={\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)
over
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
of
bounded holomorphic functions on the relative disk. We have seen that the derivative
\frac{d}{dz}
is not bounded as section of
{\rm Hom}(E,E)
, but will
be so as a section of

{\rm Hom}(E,E)^{op}[\lambda]\longrightarrow\Delta\times\Delta^{op}

where we rescale the norm
on the fibre
{\rm Hom}(E_{t},E_{s})
by a factor
\lambda(t,s)=t-s
and the Cauchy inequality implies that
|\frac{d}{dz}|=1
.

V.2
.
Division of holomorphic functions

For the second example, we consider the same Kolmogorov space

E:={\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

and let
M\subset E
be the subspace of functions
f
with
f(0)=0
.

We can divide a function
f\in M_{t}
by
z
and form the holomorphic
function
g(z):=f(z)/z\in E_{t}
and we have an estimate

|g|\leq\frac{1}{t}|f|.

As suggested by the above example of the derivative, it is natural to
expect that the categorical approach tells us to consider division also
as a two parameter family of operators
u_{st}\in{\rm Hom}(M_{t},E_{s})
:

u_{st}:M_{t}\longrightarrow E_{s},\ f\mapsto\frac{1}{z}f

parametrised by a closed simplex

\overline{\Delta}:=\{(t,s)\;\;|t\geq s\}.

Is the section bounded? Of course not. If we consider the horizontal section

\sigma=z\in\Gamma(]0,t],M)

then

|\sigma(t)|=t,\ |\frac{1}{z}\sigma(t)|=1

so the operator norm of the map

u_{st}:M_{t}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)_{s},f\mapsto\frac{1}{z}

equals
1/t
. This means that the horizontal section

u\in\Gamma^{h}(\overline{\Delta},{\rm Hom}(M,E))

has a simple pole along the coordinate axis
t=0
. We may however rescale the norm by a factor
\lambda(t,s)=t
and then division operator becomes an element of the Banach space:

\Gamma^{\infty}(\overline{\Delta},\mathcal{H}om(E,E)[\lambda]).

Back in 1944, H. Cartan showed that a similar phenomenon appears for division of systems of
holomorphic functions where one gets the same type of estimate
[
7
]
. So when performing divisions or when solving equations, we will, as a general rule, rescale by functions vanishing on the boundary of a simplex.

Sometimes instead of dividing by a function we multiply by some function and therefore we rescale by
s^{k}

instead of dividing by
t^{k}
. If we combine these three types of operations (multiplication, division and differentiation), we encounter operators
f\mapsto g

with estimates of the form

|g|_{s}\leq\frac{s^{l}}{t^{k}(t-s)^{m}}|f|_{t}.

So we will rescale the norms by factor of the form
s^{-l}t^{k}(t-s)^{m}
.
The theory of
local operators
is an abstraction and
generalisation of these examples.

V.3
.
Local operators

We now consider Kolmogorov spaces

. As before, we write

\Delta:=\{(t,s)\in B^{op}\times B:t>s\}.

From a categorical point of view the set

is to be considered as lying in
B^{op}\times B
and therefore the condition

(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})\leq(t,s)

and

The opposite Kolmogorov space

whose fibre above

The Kolmogorov space

the Banach space of bounded horizontal sections over the triangle

\Delta(t,s)=\{(t^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in\Delta\;|\;t^{\prime}\leq t,s^{\prime}\geq s\}.

V.4
.
Composition of local operators

If
E,F,G
are Kolmogorov spaces over
B
, we have a well
defined bilinear composition maps

\displaystyle\circ:{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(E,F)\times{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(F,G)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(E,G)

\displaystyle(u,v)

\displaystyle\mapsto v\circ u

where we used the notation

{\rm Hom}_{A}(-,-):=\Gamma(A,{\rm Hom}(-,-))

This composition is defined as follows: given
u\in{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(E,F)
and
v\in{\rm Hom}_{\Delta(t,s)}(F,G)

and
(p,q)\in\Delta(t,s)
, we set

(v\circ u)_{(p,q)}:=v_{p,r}\circ u_{r,q},

where
p<r<q
. It is easily checked, that by the horizontality of
u
and
v

the result does not depend on the choice of
r
.
Obviously, if
u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda}(E,F)
and
v\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\mu}(F,G)
then

v\circ u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\rho}(E,G)

if one has

\rho(t,s)\leq\sup_{t\geq m\geq s}(\lambda(t,m)\mu(m,s))

Definition V.4.1
.

A sequence of weight functions
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k},\ldots)
is called
submultiplicative if for all
p,q
there exist an
m\in[s,t]\subset B
with

\lambda_{p+q}(t,s)\leq\lambda_{p}(t,m)\lambda_{q}(m,s).

Let us consider the special case
B=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
One verifies that

\lambda_{k}(t,s):=(t-s)^{k}/k^{k},\ s,t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

is a submultiplicative weight sequence: for given
s<t
the maximum
of the function

m\mapsto(t-m)^{p}(m-s)^{q}
on the interval
[s,t]
is attained at the point

m=\frac{p}{p+q}s+\frac{q}{p+q}t,

Local operators behave well with respect to composition:

Proposition V.4.2
.

If the sequence
(\lambda_{k})
is submultiplicative then for
u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{k}}(E,F)
and
v\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{l}}(F,G)
then
we have

v\circ u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{k+l}}(E,G)

and

|v\circ u|\leq|v||u|.

In particular for

u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{1}}(E,E)

then

u_{1}\circ u_{2}\circ\ldots\circ u_{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{n}}(E,E)

and we have the estimate:

|u_{1}\circ u_{2}\circ\ldots\circ u_{n}|\leq|u_{1}||u_{2}|\ldots|u_{n}|,

Especially for
u_{1}=u_{2}=\ldots=u_{n}=u
:

|u^{n}|\leq|u|^{n}.

So the graded algebra

{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda}(E,F):=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{k}}(E,F)

behaves very much like a Banach algebra and this is the reason that
one is able to develop a functional calculus in this setting.

In reading these formulas one should be aware that for reasons of simplicity
we always write
|-|
for the norms, but which norm this in fact is,
depends on the element to which we apply it. For example,
if
u
is
\lambda_{1}
-local, the norm in the expression
|u|^{n}
refers to
the
n
-th power of the norm on
{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{1}}(E,E)
, whereas in
|u^{n}|
we are using the norm on
{\mathcal{L}}^{\lambda_{n}}(E,E)
!

Submultiplicative functions

Definition V.5.1
.

A weight function
\lambda(t,s)
is submultiplicative if the sequence

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots)

is a submultiplicative sequence, where

\lambda_{n}(t,s):=\frac{e^{n}\lambda^{n}(t,s)}{n^{n}}.

The factor

appears naturally when we compose local operators while
the factor

As has been remarked before, the function

Note that if

is submultiplicative, then

is also submultiplicative if

and

\beta(t)
are monotonously increasing and decreasing respectively.
In particular, for

\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}_{>0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\ (t,s)\mapsto C\,s^{p}t^{-q}(t-s)

is submultiplicative. Note that for
pq\neq 0
, the function is however no longer monotonous.

V.6

Existence of functorial calculus

From now on we fix a submultiplicative weight
\lambda
.
Given a power series

we use the notation

Definition V.6.1
.

The Borel transform of a formal power series is defined by

B:\mathbb{C}[[z]]\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}[[z]],\ \sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\mapsto\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}z^{n}.

We fix a Kolmogorov space
E
over
B
and a submultiplicative weight function

\lambda:B\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
This defines a Kolmogorov-space

{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(E,E)\longrightarrow\Delta^{op}

of 1-local operators.
We use the following notation:

{\mathcal{X}}(R):=\{(t,s,u)\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(E,E)\;|\;\;|u|<R\lambda(t,s)\}

Theorem V.6.2
.

Let

f=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}\{z\}
be a convergent power series with
R
as radius
of convergence. Then there is a well-defined map of Banach spaces over
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, that we call the Borel map:

{\mathcal{B}}f:{\mathcal{X}}(R)\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}(E,E),\ (t,s,v)\mapsto(t,s,\sum_{n=0}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}v^{n})

and one has the estimate

|{\mathcal{B}}f(u)|\leq|f|\left(\frac{|u|}{\lambda(t,s)}\right).

Proof.

By Proposition
V.4.2
, for
u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(E,E)
, we have
u^{n}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(E,E)
and

|u^{n}|\leq|u|^{n}

Thus there is an inclusion

\varphi_{n}:{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(E,E)\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}(E,E)

of Banach-spaces over
\Delta
and by definition of the rescaled norms

\|\varphi_{n}(u)\|\leq\frac{n^{n}}{e^{n}\lambda(t,s)^{n}}|u|

on the norm. Here
\|\cdot\|
stands for the standard operator norm inside the Banach space
{\rm Hom}(E_{t},E_{s})
of Banach operators and
u
is in fact a triple
(t,s,v)
.

The choice of the constant
e
gives a simple estimate
for this norm:

\frac{n^{n}}{e^{n}\lambda(t,s)^{n}}\leq\frac{n!}{\lambda(t,s)^{n}}.

We obtain:

\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}\varphi_{n}(u^{n})\|\leq\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}|\left(\frac{|u|}{\lambda(t,s)}\right)^{n}=|f|\left(\frac{|u|}{\lambda(t,s)}\right).

This proves the theorem.
∎

V.8
.
The derivative (Final part)

We will show how the formalism works by itself on domains in a trivial example where we can also make easy explicit computations.
Consider once more the Kolmogorov space

{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0},

where
D\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
has fibres
D_{t}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<t\}.

Let us consider the exponential of the derivative:

u(t,s):{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{t})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{s}),\ g(z)\mapsto g^{\prime}(z)

According to Taylor’s formula

e^{u}g(z)=g(z+1).

As an unbounded operator, the definition domain of the exponential consists of analytic series having a convergence radius
>s+1
.
What does functorial calculus tells us in such a simple example?

So we consider the weight

\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{>0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\ (t,s)\mapsto t-s

and consider the derivative as an element
u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(E,E)
.
Our theorem says that taking the exponential of the derivation, which corresponds to composition the flow
\varphi
:

(e^{u})_{t,s}:{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{t})\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}^{b}(D_{s})

gives a well defined homomorphism provided we have the estimate:

|u(t,s)|<t-s.

But again by Cauchy inequality we get that
|u(t,s)|=1
and then we recover the condition
t>s+1
.

So functorial calculus informs us on the way the disk
D_{s}
is translated under the flow of the vector field: if the estimates
t>s+1
holds then the image of the disk
D_{s}
under the flow of
\partial_{z}
at time
1
is contained inside the disk
D_{t}
.

In more complicated
situations domains might be shrinked and moved in a complicated way, we do not know exactly how: our formalism takes care by itself of the necessary information to have a well-defined exponential.

V.9
.
Infinite products

As we pointed out in the introduction, when dealing with infinite dimensional group actions, it is customary to use iterations where infinite number of transformations are involved (see e.g.
[
24
]
). Our formalism allows us to spell out a theorem which ensure convergence of such iterations. To generalise Theorem
V.6.2
to infinite products, we need the following elementary lemma, then functorial calculus will do the rest:

Lemma V.9.1
.

Let
f
a positive
C^{1}
-function such that
f(0)=1
.
Then for any real positive summable sequence
(x_{i})
, the infinite product
\prod_{i}f(x_{i})
is convergent.

Proof.

Indeed

\log\prod_{i\geq 0}f(x_{i})=\sum_{i\geq 0}\log(1+x_{i}+o(x_{i}))

and
\log(1+y)\sim y
.
∎

From this lemma we deduce easily:

Theorem V.9.2
.

Let

f=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathbb{C}\{z\}
be a convergent power series with
R
as radius
of convergence and
f(0)=1
. Let
(u_{i})\subset{\mathcal{X}}(R)
be such that
\sum_{i}|u_{i}|
is convergent. Then the product

g_{k}:={\mathcal{B}}f(u_{k}){\mathcal{B}}f(u_{k-1})\cdots{\mathcal{B}}f(u_{0})=\prod_{i=0}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}f(u_{i})

converges
to a horizontal section
g\in\Gamma(A,{\rm Hom}(E,E))

where
A=\{(t,s):R\lambda(t,s)>\max|u_{i}|\}
.
Moreover one has the estimate

|\prod{\mathcal{B}}f(u_{i})|\leq\prod_{i\geq 0}|f|\left(\frac{|u_{i}|}{\lambda(t,s)}\right).

Proof.

Consider again the inclusion:

\varphi_{n}:{\mathcal{L}}^{n}(E,E)\longrightarrow Hom(E,E)

We use multi-index notations:

u^{\alpha}=\prod_{i}u_{i}^{\alpha_{i}},\ a_{\alpha}=\prod_{i}a_{\alpha_{i}},\ |\alpha|=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\text{ etc. }

where the sequence
\alpha=(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{n},\dots)
has only a finite number of non-zero elements.

We have the formal equality :

\sum_{\alpha}\frac{a_{\alpha}}{|\alpha|!}x^{\alpha}=\prod_{i>0}Bf(x_{i})

to which we would like to give a meaning for

x_{i}=\frac{|u_{i}|}{\lambda(t,s)}<R.

The homomorphisms
f(x_{i})
exist by Theorem
V.6.2
.
The estimate:

\|\varphi_{|\alpha|}(u^{\alpha})\|\leq\frac{|\alpha|!}{\lambda(t,s)^{|\alpha|}}|u|^{\alpha}

shows that the series

S=\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}\frac{\varphi_{|\alpha|}(u^{\alpha})}{|\alpha|!}

is convergent. Indeed:

\|\sum_{|\alpha|>k}a_{\alpha}\frac{\varphi_{|\alpha|}(u^{\alpha})}{|\alpha|!}\|\leq\sum_{|\alpha|>k}|a_{\alpha}|\frac{|u|^{\alpha}}{\lambda(t,s)^{\alpha}}

is the rest of a convergent power series
Let us show that the products

g_{k}:={\mathcal{B}}f(u_{k}){\mathcal{B}}f(u_{k-1})\cdots{\mathcal{B}}f(u_{0})

converge to
S
. We denote by

deg(\alpha)=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\alpha_{k}\neq 0\}

the last term before the sequence becomes zero. We then have

\displaystyle S(t,s)-g_{k}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{deg(\alpha)>k}a_{\alpha}\frac{u^{\alpha}}{|\alpha|!}

\displaystyle\|S(t,s)-g_{k}(t,s)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{deg(\alpha)>k}|a_{\alpha}|\frac{|u|^{\alpha}}{\lambda(t,s)^{\alpha}}=\prod_{i>k}|f|\left(\frac{|u_{i}|}{\lambda(t,s)}\right)

which is rest of the the convergent infinite product. This proves the theorem.
∎

.

Let
