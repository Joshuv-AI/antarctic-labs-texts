Complex semiclassical theory for non-Hermitian quantum systems
Guang Yang # \#, Yong-Kang Li # \#, Yongxu Fu, Zhenduo Wang, Yi Zhang

Abstract
Non-Hermitian quantum systems exhibit fascinating characteristics such as non-Hermitian topological phenomena and skin effect, yet their studies are limited by the intrinsic difficulties associated with their eigenvalue problems, especially in larger systems and higher dimensions. In Hermitian systems, the semiclassical theory has played an active role in analyzing spectrum, eigenstate, phase, transport properties, etc. Here, we establish a complex semiclassical theory applicable to non-Hermitian quantum systems by an analytical continuation of the physical variables such as momentum, position, time, and energy in the equations of motion and quantization condition to the complex domain. Further, we propose a closed-orbit scheme and physical meaning under such complex variables. We demonstrate that such a framework straightforwardly yields complex energy spectra and quantum states, topological phases and transitions, and even the skin effect in non-Hermitian quantum systems, presenting an unprecedented perspective toward nontrivial non-Hermitian physics, even with larger systems and higher dimensions.

Discussions
—We have established a complex semiclassical theory more generally applicable to non-Hermitian quantum systems with an analytical continuation of physical variables. We have also provided a strategy to locate closed orbits and eigenstates. We demonstrate our framework’s applicability, accuracy, and efficiency on various continuous and lattice models, generally unattainable by previous ansatzes due to system sizes, dimensions, potentials, magnetic fields, and complex spectra.

There are several interesting directions for further generalizations. We discuss the introduction of multiple bands and the Berry curvature
{\bf\Omega}
in Ref.
Sup
. On the other hand, we may consider the analytical continuation of the remaining two physical quantities in the EOMs, the Berry curvature
Silberstein et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2020
and the magnetic field, to the complex domains. Indeed, the physics of the model in Eq.
13
in a complex-valued magnetic field
\bf B
can be satisfactorily captured by the complex semiclassical theory (Fig.
3
and Ref.
Sup
). The complex semiclassical theory may also apply beyond the framework of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians. For example, it is straightforward to incorporate dissipative friction, such as
{\bf F_{\mu}}=-\mu{\bf p}
, into the EOMs, which forbids closed orbits for
t\in\mathbb{R}
; the complex semiclassical theory opens up the possibility of
t\in\mathbb{C}
, thus potential approaches and insights for dissipative or driven quantum systems
Sup
.

As shown in the main text, we can re-express the Hamiltonian as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega\left(b^{\dagger}a+1/2\right),

Appendix D
IV. Wave functions from the complex semiclassical theory

D.1
IVA. Geometric phase under complex variables

Conventionally, the semiclassical theory can provide not only the energy spectrum but also approximate wave functions. Among the contributions over the semiclassical orbit, the geometric phase
\int{\bf p}\cdot d{\bf r}
plays a vital role
Cvitanović et al. 2020

Despite the generalization to the complex domains, the geometric phase in the complex semiclassical theory remains the form of
\oint{\bf p}\cdot d{\bf r}
along a closed orbit. To see this, we consider a loop around an infinitesimal plaquette

\displaystyle W(0,-\delta{\bf p}_{c})W(-\delta{\bf r}_{c},0)W(0,\delta{\bf p}_{c})W(\delta{\bf r}_{c},0)

\displaystyle e^{-i\hat{x}\cdot\delta{\bf p}_{c}}e^{i\hat{p}\cdot\delta{\bf r}_{c}}e^{i\hat{x}\cdot\delta{\bf p}_{c}}e^{-i\hat{p}\cdot\delta{\bf r}_{c}}=e^{-[\hat{p},\hat{x}]\delta{\bf p}_{c}\cdot\delta{\bf r}_{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\delta{\bf p}_{c}\cdot\delta{\bf r}_{c}},

to

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle 0|W(-{\bf r}_{c}-\delta{\bf r}_{c},-{\bf p}_{c}-\delta{\bf p}_{c})W(\delta{\bf r}_{c},\delta{\bf p}_{c})W({\bf r}_{c},{\bf p}_{c})|0\rangle.

We can obtain the wave functions with such geometric phases augmenting the wave packets, as we demonstrate in the following subsections. As we have defined

In addition, the single-valuedness of the wave function constrains the geometric phase
\oint{\bf p}\cdot d{\bf r}
along a closed orbit, hence the quantization condition. For scenarios with
{\bf p}\in\mathbb{C}
and
{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}

E.2
VB. Biorthogonal convention and expectation value in non-Hermitian quantum systems

We can diagonalize a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian as
\hat{H}=\sum_{n}\epsilon_{n}\ket{nR}\bra{nL}
, where
\ket{nR}
(
\bra{nL}
) is the right (left) eigenstate with respect to the energy eigenvalue
\epsilon_{n}
. Correspondingly, the time evolution operator is:

U\left(t\right)=\exp{(-i\hat{H}t)}=\sum_{n}\exp{\left(-i\epsilon_{n}t\right)}\ket{nR}\bra{nL}.

(42)

Alternatively, we can write the diagonalized
\hat{H}
in a more compact matrix form as
\Lambda=V^{-1}\hat{H}V
, where the
n^{th}
column (row) of
V
(
V^{-1}
) is exactly
\ket{nR}
(
\bra{nL}
).
\epsilon_{n}
, on the diagonal
\Lambda
, is the biorthogonal expectation value of
\hat{H}
:
\epsilon_{n}=\braket{nL|\hat{H}|nR}
. Consistently, it is natural to deduce that the expectation value of an operator
\hat{O}
is the biorthogonal counterpart,
\braket{\hat{O}}_{n}=\braket{nL|\hat{O}|nR}
, or more generally,
\braket{\hat{O}}_{nm}=\braket{nL|\hat{O}|mR}
.

Given an initial quantum state
\ket{\Phi(0)}
, we can decompose it in the biorthogonal basis
\ket{\Phi(0)}=\sum_{n}\ket{nR}\braket{nL|\Phi(0)}
. It evolves in time as:

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi\left(t\right)}

\displaystyle=U\left(t\right)\ket{\Phi(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{nm}\exp{\left(-iE_{n}t\right)}\ket{nR}\braket{nL|mR}\braket{mL|\Phi(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\exp{\left(-iE_{n}t\right)}\ket{nR}\braket{nL|\Phi(0)},

(43)

where we have used the property
\braket{nL|mR}=\delta_{nm}
of the biorthogonal basis. Similarly, we have its bra
\langle\Phi(0)|
’s time evolution as:

\displaystyle\bra{\Phi(t)}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Phi(0)}U^{-1}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\exp{\left(iE_{n}t\right)}\braket{\Phi(0)|nR}\bra{nL},

(44)

whereas the Hermitian conjugation of
\ket{\Phi(t)}
is different:

\displaystyle\bra{\Phi^{\prime}(t)}=\sum_{n}\exp{\left(iE_{n}^{*}t\right)}\braket{\Phi(0)|nL}\bra{nR},

(45)

as directly follows from Eq.
43
.

Thus, the amplitude of
\braket{\Phi(t)|\Phi(t)}
is conserved, while that of
\braket{\Phi^{\prime}(t)|\Phi(t)}
grows or decays exponentially with respect to
t
:

\displaystyle\braket{\Phi(t)|\Phi(t)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\Phi(0)|U^{-1}(t)U(t)|\Phi(0)}=1,

\displaystyle\braket{\Phi^{\prime}(t)|\Phi(t)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\Phi(0)|U^{\dagger}(t)U(t)|\Phi(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{mn}\exp{\left[i(E_{m}^{*}-E_{n})t\right]}\braket{\Phi(0)|mL}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\braket{mR|nR}\braket{nL|\Phi(0)}.

(46)

Therefore, the former convention is more convenient for analyzing inner products and expectation values in non-Hermitian quantum systems.

Employing the biorthogonal convention, we define and evaluate the time evolution of observable
\hat{O}
’s expectation value as:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{O}(t)}=\braket{\Phi(t)|\hat{O}|\Phi(t)}=\braket{\Phi(0)|U^{-1}(t)\hat{O}U(t)|\Phi(0)},

(47)

which converts back to the common definition in Hermitian systems:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{O}(t)}

\displaystyle=\braket{\Phi(0)|U^{-1}(t)\hat{O}U(t)|\Phi(0)}

(48)

\displaystyle=\braket{\Phi(0)|U^{\dagger}(t)\hat{O}U(t)|\Phi(0)}=\braket{\Phi(t)|\hat{O}|\Phi(t)}.

Whether the expectation value in non-Hermitian quantum systems should be defined and calculated within the biorthogonal basis or merely the right eigenstates remains a subtle matter. When we speak of the NHSE, the focus is solely on (the amplitude of) the right eigenstates:
\braket{\psi^{\prime}(x)|\psi(x)}
; in contrast, however, the NHSE disappears when we employ the biorthogonal basis:
\braket{\psi(x)|\psi(x)}
. Even when it comes to many-body non-Hermitian quantum systems, these two views are both present in the literature: the spatial density of an
n
-fermion state
\ket{\Psi_{\mu}}
is calculated via
\braket{\Psi^{\prime}_{\mu}|\hat{n}_{x}|\Psi_{\mu}}
with the right eigenstates in Ref.
Mu et al. 2020
, while the non-Hermitian many-body bulk polarization
P^{LR}
takes the biorthogonal form
Lee et al. 2020
.

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y},\sigma^{z}
are the Pauli matrices. Quantum mechanically, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{x}^{2}+\beta^{2}\hat{p}_{y}^{2}-\alpha\beta B_{z}\sigma^{z}

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(2\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+1-\sigma^{z})/2,

where
\hat{a}=(\alpha\hat{p}_{x}+i\beta\hat{p}_{y})/\sqrt{\omega}
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}=(\alpha\hat{p}_{x}-i\beta\hat{p}_{y})/\sqrt{\omega}
are ladder operators similar to the main text.
\omega=2\alpha\beta B_{z}
. Consequently, the spectrum is
\epsilon_{n}=\pm\sqrt{\omega n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\geq 0
, which serves as our benchmark for the complex semiclassical theory.

The semiclassical equations of motion in the presence of the Berry curvature
\Omega(\bf{p})
are given by
Xiao et al. 2010
:

\displaystyle\dot{\bf{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial\epsilon/\partial{\bf{p}}-\dot{\bf{p}}\times\Omega(\bf{p}),

\displaystyle\dot{\bf{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf E}+\dot{\bf r}\times\bf{B},

(56)

where
\bf{E}=-\partial\epsilon/\partial{\bf{r}}
. As mentioned in the main text, the complex semiclassical theory amounts to generalizing the variables to the complex domains. For the non-Hermitian quantum system in Eq.
54
, we obtain
\epsilon=\pm\sqrt{\alpha^{2}p_{x}^{2}+\beta^{2}p_{y}^{2}}
. With
\bf{E}=0
and the Berry curvature
\Omega(\bf{p})\propto\bf{p}
, the corresponding complex EOMs give:

\displaystyle\dot{\bf{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial\epsilon/\partial{\bf{p}})\times B_{z}\hat{z},

\displaystyle\dot{p_{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{z}\partial\epsilon/\partial{p_{y}}=\beta^{2}p_{y}B_{z}/\epsilon,

(57)

\displaystyle\dot{p_{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B_{z}\partial\epsilon/\partial{p_{x}}=-\alpha^{2}p_{x}B_{z}/\epsilon,

whose solution is:

\displaystyle p_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{1}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}+z_{2}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t},

\displaystyle p_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(z_{1}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}-z_{2}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t})\cdot i\alpha/\beta,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\alpha^{2}z_{1}z_{2},

(58)

where
\omega^{\prime}=\alpha\beta B_{z}/\epsilon
.
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are determined by the initial conditions.

Next, we calculate the Berry phase along the orbit. We obtain the left and right spinor states with
\epsilon>0
at time
t
from:

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{1}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}(\sigma^{x}+i\sigma^{y})+z_{2}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}(\sigma^{x}-i\sigma^{y})

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\alpha\begin{pmatrix}&z_{1}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t}\\
z_{2}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}&\end{pmatrix},

and evaluate the accumulated Berry phase via integrating over the connection:

\int_{0}^{\frac{2\pi}{\omega^{\prime}}}dt\cdot i(\sqrt{z_{1}z_{2}},z_{1}e^{i\omega^{\prime}t})\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{z_{1}z_{2}}\\
z_{2}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}t}\end{pmatrix}/2z_{1}z_{2}=\pi.

(60)

Note that we have employed the biorthogonal convention for expectation values; see Sec. VB.
2z_{1}z_{2}
is a normalization. Consequently, the quantization condition demands:

\displaystyle\oint{\bf p}\cdot d{\bf r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint-{\bf p}\times d{\bf p}\cdot\hat{z}/B_{z}=2\pi z_{1}z_{2}\cdot 2\alpha/\beta B_{z},

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi(n+1/2)-\pi=2\pi n,\;n\in\mathbb{Z},

where we have made use of
d{\bf p}=d{\bf r}\times B_{z}\hat{z}
from the EOMs for the first equality. Putting Eq.
61
and Eq.
58
together, we establish the energy spectrum from the complex semiclassical theory:

\epsilon=\sqrt{2\alpha\beta B_{z}n}=\sqrt{\omega n},

(62)

and similarly for the
\epsilon<0
states. Overall, the results based on the complex semiclassical theory in the presence of the Berry phase are fully consistent with the quantum benchmark.
