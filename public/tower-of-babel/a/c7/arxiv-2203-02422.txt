SOME RESULTS ON FACTORIZATION OF MONOIDS
Zsolt Adam Balogh and Tamar Mesablishvili

Abstract
. Factorizations of monoids are studied. Two necessary and sufficient conditions in terms of so-called descent 1-cocycles for a monoid to be factorized through two submonoids are found. A full classification of those factorizations of a monoid whose one factor is a subgroup of the monoid is obtained. The relationship between monoid factorizations and non-abelian cohomology of monoids is analyzed. Some applications of semi-direct product of monoids are given.

INTRODUCTION

Factorizations of mathematical objects is an important topic in mathematics, whose basic underlying idea is to represent
a mathematical object as a product of two (usually simpler) sub-objects with minimal intersection.
The factorization problem can be divided into two parts. The first part is to find all the factorizations of a given mathematical object through its sub-objects. The second part is to study the properties of a mathematical object that has already been factorized with respect to its two smaller sub-objects.

In this paper we deal with the first part of the factorization problem for monoids. Our methodology is inspired by the paper
[
2
]
.
As a first step, we consider
descent 1-cocycles
for monoids (
[
4
]
) and then give two necessary and sufficient
conditions in terms of descent 1-cocycles for a monoid to be factorized through two submonoids (see, Theorems
2.12

and
2.13
). Furthermore, we provide a full classification of those factorizations of a monoid whose one factor is a subgroup of the monoid (Theorem
2.18
). Next, Theorems
3.6
,
3.8
and
3.9
describe certain relationships between monoid factorizations, non-abelian cohomology of monoids and semi-direct product
of monoids. Finally, we give examples for calculating how many ways a monoid can be factorized.

MONOID FACTORIZATION AND DESENT COHOMOLOGY

Given a monoid
M
with unit
\textsf{1}_{M}
and elements
m_{1},m_{2}\in M
. We sometimes will write

m_{1}\cdot m_{2}
for
m_{1}m_{2}
if it helps to avoid confusion.
To indicate that
A
is a submonoid of the monoid
M
, we use the notation
A\leqslant M
.
In this case, we write
\imath_{A}
for the canonical embedding
A\hookrightarrow M
.
If
X
and
Y
are subsets of
M
, then
XY
is the set
\{xy:x\in X\,\,\text{and}\,\,y\in Y\}.

Given another monoid
N
. Let
0_{M,N}
denote the homomorphism
M\to N
that sends each
element of
M
to the unit element
\textsf{1}_{N}
of
N
. This mapping is called the
zero homomorphism
from

M
to
N
. Let us denote by
U(M)
the set of invertible elements of
M
.
Throughout this paper
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of positive natural numbers
\{1,2,\ldots\}
and let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
.

2.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two submonoids of a monoid
M
. A
monoid factorization
is a monoid
M
together with its submonoids
A,B
such that the multiplication map

[\imath_{A},\imath_{B}]:A\times B\to M,\,\,(a,b)\longmapsto ab

is bijective, or equivalently, each element
m\in M
can be written uniquely as a product
m=ab
with
a\in A,\,b\in B
.
In this case one says that
M

is factorized by its submonoids

A
and
B
, or that the couple
(A,B)
is a

factorization
of
M
.
A
and
B
are respectively called the
first
and
second factors

of the factorization. We shall sometimes use the term “
(A,B\subseteq M)
is a monoid factorization” to mean that
the monoid
M
is factorized by its submonoids
A
and
B
.
(A,B)
is a
proper factorization of

M

if
A
, and then also
B
, is a proper submonoid of
M
. The monoid
M

admits a factorization
, or is
factorizable
,
if
M
is factorized by its proper submonoids. Otherwise it is called
non-factorizable
.

2.2 Example
.

The set of positive natural numbers
\mathbb{N}

under multiplication is a monoid. It may readily be verified that the set
\mathbb{O}=\{2k-1|k\in\mathbb{N}\}
of odd numbers is a submonoid of
\mathbb{N}
. Write
\mathcal{C}
for the
cyclic submonoid

\{2^{i}:i\,\,\text{is a non-negative integer}\}\subset\mathbb{N}.

Since every positive natural number can be uniquely written as a product of a non-negative integer
power of 2 and an odd integer, it follows that the map

\mathbb{O}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{N},\,(k,2^{i})\mapsto 2^{i}k

is bijective.
This shows that
(\mathbb{O},\mathcal{C})
is a factorization of the monoid
\mathbb{N}
.

Let
M
be a monoid. We write
\textsf{FAC}(M)
for the set of all factorizations of
M
, i.e.

\textsf{FAC}(M)=\{(A,B):A\leq M,B\leq M\,\,\text{and}\,\,(A,B)\,\,\text{is a factorization of}\,\,M\}.

For a fixed submonoid
A
of
M
, let
\textsf{FAC}(A/M)
be denote the set of
those submonoids
B\leq M
for which the pair
(A,B)
is a monoid factorization of
M
.
Note that when there already exists a monoid factorization of
M
whose first factor
is
A
, then
\textsf{FAC}(A/M)
may be considered as a pointed set with the second factor
of the factorization as the base point. It is easy to see that

\textsf{FAC}(M)=\bigsqcup_{A\subsetneqq M}\textsf{FAC}(A/M).

The following proposition gives a useful necessary condition for a submonoid of a given monoid to be the
first or second factor in a factorization of the monoid.

2.3 Proposition
.

Let
(A,B)
be a monoid factorization of
M
and
a\in A
,
b\in B
and
m\in M
. If

am\in A
, then
m\in A
. Symmetrically, if
mb\in B
, then
m\in B
.

Proof.

According to the symmetry, it suffices to prove the result only for the first sentence. Suppose that
a\in A
and
m\in M
are such that
am\in A
. Since
(A,B)
is a monoid factorization
of
M
, there exists a unique pair
(a^{\prime},b)\in A\times B
such that
m=a^{\prime}b
. Then

am=aa^{\prime}b
and since
am,aa^{\prime}\in A
, it follows from the uniqueness in the monoid factorization condition
that
b=\textsf{1}_{M}
. Thus
m=a^{\prime}b=a^{\prime}\in A
.
∎

.

Let
(\mathbb{Z},+,0)

\mathbb{N}_{0}

\emph{\textsf{FAC}}(\mathbb{N}_{0}/\mathbb{Z})=\emptyset

For a fixed factorization
(A,B)

l^{B}_{A}

[\imath_{A},\imath_{B}]^{-1}(m)=(l^{B}_{A}(m),r^{B}_{A}(m)),\qquad(m\in M).

2.5 Proposition
.

Let
(A,B)
be a factorization of a monoid
M
. Then

(2.1)

\emph{\textsf{Ker}}(l)=B\quad\text{and}\quad\emph{\textsf{Ker}}(r)=A.

Proof.

We only prove the first equation, the second one can be proved similarly.
Let
(A,B)
be a factorization of a monoid
M
. Then for any
b\in B
,
b=\textsf{1}_{M}b
and we have

l(b)=\textsf{1}_{M}
for all
b\in B
. Thus,
B\subseteq\textsf{Ker}(l)
. Conversely, let
m\in M
be such that

l(m)=\textsf{1}_{M}
. Since
m=l(m)r(m)
, it follows that
m=r(m)\in B
. Hence
\textsf{Ker}(l)\subseteq B
and
the proof is completed.
∎

2.6 Proposition
.

Let
(A,B)
be a factorization of a monoid
M
. Then the map

l=l^{B}_{A}:M\to A

satisfies the following conditions:

(L1)

l(a)=a
for all
a\in A
;

(L2)

l(am)=al(m),\,
for all
a\in A
and
m\in M
;

(L3)

l(m_{1}m_{2})=l(m_{1}l(m_{2}))
, for all
m_{1},m_{2}\in M
.

Proof.

Since
a=a\textsf{1}_{M}
for all
a\in A
, the definition of
l
shows that (L1) holds.
Next, since
am=al(m)r(m)
and
al(m)\in A
, while
r(m)\in B
, by the definition of
l
we have that

l(am)=al(m)
, so (L2) holds. Finally, let
m_{1},m_{2}
be arbitrary elements of
M
. Clearly,

-

m_{1}=l(m_{1})\cdot r((m_{1})
;

-

m_{2}=l(m_{2})\cdot r(m_{2})
;

-

r(m_{1})\cdot l(m_{2})=ab
, where
a=l(r(m_{1})\cdot l(m_{2}))
and
b=r(r(m_{1})\cdot l(m_{2}))
.

As a consequence

m_{1}m_{2}=l(m_{1})\cdot r(m_{1})\cdot l(m_{2})\cdot r(m_{2})=l(m_{1})\cdot a\cdot b\cdot r(m_{2}).

Since
l(m_{1})\cdot a\in A
and
b\cdot r(m_{2})\in B
, it follows that

l(m_{1}m_{2})=l(m_{1})\cdot a.

On the other hand, we have:

m_{1}\cdot l(m_{2})=l(m_{1})\cdot r(m_{1})\cdot l(m_{2})=l(m_{1})\cdot a\cdot b,

whence – since
l(m_{1})\cdot a\in A
and
b\in B
– one concludes that

l(m_{1}l(m_{2}))=l(m_{1})\cdot a.

Consequently,
l(m_{1}m_{2})=l(m_{1}l(m_{2}))
and (L3) also holds.
∎

2.8 Example
.

The set of non-negative even integers
\mathbb{E}=2\mathbb{N}_{0}

under addition is a monoid and hence a submonoid of
(\mathbb{N}_{0},+,0)
. If
n
is a non-negative

m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is such that
n+m
is even, then clearly
m
is also even. Hence

\mathbb{E}
satisfies the condition of Proposition
2.3
. We claim that
\mathbb{E}
cannot
appear as the first factor in a monoid factorization of
(\mathbb{N}_{0},+,0)
; equivalently,
\emph{\textsf{FAC}}(\mathbb{E}/\mathbb{N}_{0})=\emptyset
.
Indeed, if
\,\emph{\textsf{FAC}}(\mathbb{E}/\mathbb{N}_{0})\neq\emptyset
, then by Proposition
2.6
, there
exists a map
l:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{E}
satisfying Conditions (L1) - (L3). Since
\mathbb{E}
is a commutative
monoid,
l
is a homomorphism of monoids (see,
[
2
, Proposition 3.9]
). This implies in particular that

2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L1)}}{{=}}l(2)=l(1+1)=l(1)+l(1).

Keeping in mind that
l
takes its values from
\mathbb{E}
it follows that
l(1)=1
. Then we have for all

l(2n+1)=l(2n)+l(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L1)}}{{=}}2n+l(1)=2n+1,

which contradicts the assumption that

\mathbb{N}_{0}

\emph{\textsf{FAC}}(\mathbb{E}/\mathbb{Z})=\emptyset

2.9 Proposition
.

Let
A
be a submonoid of a monoid

\,a_{0}\in U(A)

a_{0}\star-:\mathcal{D}_{l}(M,A)\to\mathcal{D}_{l}(M,A)

(a_{0}\star q)(m)=q(ma_{0})a^{-1}_{0}.

Proof.

Fix an element
a_{0}\in U(A)

the map
a_{0}\star q
lies in
\mathcal{D}_{l}(M,A)
, that is, satisfies the conditions (L1) - (L3).
To begin with, observe that as it follows easily from the definition of
a_{0}\star q

A

since

(a_{0}\star q)(\textsf{1}_{M})=q(\textsf{1}_{M}a_{0})a^{-1}_{0}=q(a_{0})a^{-1}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L1)}}{{=}}a_{0}a^{-1}_{0}=\textsf{1}_{M},

it follows that the map
a\star q
satisfies Condition (L1). For Condition (L2) we have

(a_{0}\star q)(am)=q(ama_{0})a^{-1}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}aq(ma_{0})a^{-1}_{0}=a((a_{0}\star q)(m)).

It now remains to verify Condition (L3).

\displaystyle(a_{0}\star q)(m_{1}m_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{by definition of}\,\,\star

\displaystyle=q(m_{1}m_{2}a_{0})a^{-1}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{by}\,\,(\textsc{L}3)

\displaystyle=q(m_{1}q(m_{2}a_{0}))a^{-1}_{0}

\displaystyle=q(m_{1}q(m_{2}a_{0})a^{-1}_{0}a_{0})a^{-1}_{0}

\displaystyle\text{by definition of}\,\,\star

\displaystyle=(a_{0}\star q)(m_{1}q(m_{2}a_{0})a^{-1}_{0})

\displaystyle=(a_{0}\star q)(m_{1}(a_{0}\star q)(m_{2})).

Finally, it is easy to see that the map

Recall that a
left action of a monoid

is a function

M\times X\to X,\,\,(m,x)\mapsto m\star x

such that

(A1)

\textsf{1}\!_{M}\star x=x;

(A2)

(m_{1}m_{2})\star x=m_{1}\star(m_{2}\star x)

for all

2.10 Proposition
.

Let
A
be a submonoid of a monoid
M
.
The association

(a,q)\longmapsto a\star q

is a left action of the group
U(A)
on the set

\mathcal{D}_{l}(M,A)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.9
, it suffices to show that
(a,q)\longmapsto a\star q

satisfies Conditions (A1) and (A2). There is no difficulty in verifying Condition (A1) and
the following calculation verifies Condition (A2):

\displaystyle(a_{1}\star(a_{2}\star q))(m)

\displaystyle=(a_{2}\star q)(ma_{1})\cdot a_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=q(ma_{1}a_{2})\cdot a_{2}^{-1}\cdot a_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=q(ma_{1}a_{2})\cdot(a_{1}a_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=((a_{1}a_{2})\star q)(m).

Here the first, second and
fourth equality follow from the definition of
\star
.
∎

The quotient of

for all

there exist submonoids

We now return to general factorizations of monoids. Quite obviously, if
(A,B)
is a factorization of a monoid

then
M=AB
and
A\cap B=\{\textsf{1}_{M}\}
. While the converse holds if
M
is a group and

and

are its subgroups (e.g.,

), it is not true in general, as the following example shows.

2.11 Example
.

The set
-\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
of non-positive integers
under addition is a monoid and hence a submonoid of
(\mathbb{Z},+,0)
. It is easy to see that

-\mathbb{N}_{0}\cap\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0\}
and that the map

-\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{Z},\,\,(m,n)\mapsto m+n

is surjective. But this map is not injective. Indeed, for
-7,-3\in-\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and

, one has
-7+5=-3+1
.

The following result gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a pair of submonoids of
a given monoid to be a factorization of the monoid.

2.12 Theorem
.

A monoid
M
is factorized by its submonoids
A
and
B
if and only if
the following conditions hold:

(i)

AB=M
;

(ii)

there exists a map
l:M\to A
satisfying Condition (L2) such that

\emph{\textsf{Ker}}(l)=B
;

(iii)

there exists a map
r\in M\to B
satisfying Condition (R2) such that

\emph{\textsf{Ker}}(r)=A
.

Proof.

If a monoid
M
is factorized by its submonoids
A
and
B
, then clearly

AB=M
and hence (i) holds. Next,
l=l^{B}_{A}:M\to A
satisfying Condition (L2) by Proposition
2.6

and
\textsf{Ker}(l)=B
by Proposition
2.1
. Thus, (ii) holds. Similarly,

r=r^{B}_{A}:M\to B
satisfying Condition (R2) by Propositions
2.7
and
\textsf{Ker}(r)=A

by Proposition
2.1
. Thus, (iii) also holds.

Conversely, suppose that Conditions (i), (ii) and (iii) hold. Condition (i) guarantees that
the map
[\imath_{A},\imath_{B}]:A\times B\to M
is surjective. We claim that this
map is injective. Indeed, if
(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A\times B
are such that
[\imath_{A},\imath_{B}](a,b)=[\imath_{A},\imath_{B}](a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, then
ab=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}
. Since
\textsf{Ker}(l)=B
and
\textsf{Ker}(r)=A
,
it follows that

l(b)=l(b^{\prime})=\textsf{1}_{M}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,r(a)=r(a^{\prime})=\textsf{1}_{M}.

We then have

a=al(b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}l(ab)=l(a^{\prime}b^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}a^{\prime}l(b^{\prime})=a^{\prime}

and

b=r(a)b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(R2)}}{{=}}r(ab)=r(a^{\prime}b^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(R2)}}{{=}}r(a^{\prime})b^{\prime}=b^{\prime}.

Thus, the map

[\imath_{A},\imath_{B}]
is injective and so it is bijective.
Consequently,
M
is factorized through
A
and
B
.
∎

The next result gives another necessary and sufficient condition for a monoid to be
factorized by its submonoids. It should be compared with
[
5
, Theorem 2]
.
(Given a map
f:X\to Y
of sets, we write
\textsf{K}[f]
for the set

\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X\times X:f(x_{1})=f(x_{2})\}

2.13 Theorem
.

A monoid
M
is factorized by its submonoids
A
and
B

if and only if there are maps
A\xleftarrow{l}M\xrightarrow{r}B
of sets such that:

(i)

l
satisfies Condition (L2);

(ii)

r
satisfies Condition (R2);

(iii)

r\imath_{A}=0_{A,B}
and
l\imath_{B}=0_{B,A}
;

(iv)

\emph{\textsf{K}}[l]\cap\emph{\textsf{K}}[r]=\emph{\textsf{K}}[\emph{\textsf{Id}}_{M}]
.

Proof.

”If”: If
M
is factorized through its submonoids
A
and
B
, then the maps

l=l^{B}_{A}
and
r=r^{B}_{A}
satisfy Conditions (i) and (ii) by Proposition
2.12
. Moreover,
\textsf{Ker}(l)=B
and

\textsf{Ker}(r)=A
by Proposition
2.5
. This implies that Condition (iii) is also satisfied.

For Condition (iv), suppose that
m,m^{\prime}\in M
are such that
(m,m^{\prime})\in\textsf{K}[l]\cap\textsf{K}[r]
.
Then
l(m)=l(m^{\prime})
and
r(m)=r(m^{\prime})
and we have:

m=l(m)r(m)=l(m^{\prime})r(m^{\prime})=m^{\prime}.

Therefore,
\textsf{K}[l]\cap\textsf{K}[r]=\textsf{K}[\textsf{Id}_{M}].

”Only if”: In the light of Proposition
2.12
, we have only to prove that

-

\textsf{Ker}(l)=B
;

-

\textsf{Ker}(r)=A
;

-

AB=M
.

In order to prove these equalities, observe first that Condition (iii) guarantees that there
are inclusions
B\subseteq\textsf{Ker}(l)
and
A\subseteq\textsf{Ker}(r)
. Next, since

r(m)\in B\subseteq\textsf{Ker}(l)
and
l(m)\in A\subseteq\textsf{Ker}(r)
for
any
m\in M
, we have

l(l(m)r(m))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}l(m)l(r(m))=l(m)\textsf{1}_{M}=l(m)

and

r(l(m)r(m))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(R2)}}{{=}}r(l(m))r(m)=\textsf{1}_{M}r(m)=r(m).

It follows that the pair
(l(m)r(m),m)
lies in
\textsf{K}[l]\cap\textsf{K}[r]
. Then
l(m)r(m)=m

by (iv). Since
m\in M
was an arbitrary element, this implies that
AB=M
.

Finally, if
m\in M
is such that
l(m)=\textsf{1}_{M}
(resp.
r(m)=\textsf{1}_{M}
), then

m=l(m)r(m)=r(m)
and hence
m
lies in
B

(resp.
m=l(m)r(m)=l(m)\in A)
. Therefore,
\textsf{Ker}(l)\subseteq B

Consequently,
\textsf{Ker}(l)=B

\textsf{Ker}(r)=A

∎

2.14 Proposition
.

Let

Since

2.15 Theorem
.

Let

be a monoid. A subgroup

if and only if

If this is the case

Proof.

The condition is necessary by Proposition

. If it is satisfied, then there exists

(L,\textsf{Ker}(q))
is a

\textsf{Ker}(q)

2.14
. Next note that for any

and

It follows that

Now, for every

and

lies in

m\in M

2.18 Theorem
.

In the situation described above, the assignment

Proof.

Let

Then, by Proposition
2.14
,
\textsf{Ker}(q)
is a submonoid of
M
. Since

is factorized through the

and

can be written uniquely in the form

\textsf{1}_{M}=q(k)=q(l^{B}_{A}(k)r^{B}_{A}(k))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}l^{B}_{A}(k)q(r^{B}_{A}(k)).

Since

and hence

by our assumption on

,
it follows that
l^{B}_{A}(k)=q(r^{B}_{A}(k))^{-1}
.
Thus, any element

(2.2)

It follows in particular from (

.

Suppose now

.
Evidently,

l^{B}_{A}(m)\cdot r^{B}_{A}(m)=l^{B}_{A}(m)\cdot q(r^{B}_{A}(m))\cdot q(r^{B}_{A}(m))^{-1}\cdot r^{B}_{A}(m)

and
l^{B}_{A}(m)\cdot q(r^{B}_{A}(m))\in A

(2.3)

is an
(A,\textsf{Ker}(q))
-decomposition of

If
a,a^{\prime}\in A
and

are such that
ak=a^{\prime}k^{\prime}
, then

\begin{split}a=a\textsf{1}_{A}&=aq(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}q(ak)\\
&=q(a^{\prime}k^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}a^{\prime}q(k^{\prime})=a^{\prime}\textsf{1}_{A}=a^{\prime}.\end{split}

Moreover, we have:

aq(r^{B}_{A}(k))^{-1}r^{B}_{A}(k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{spl})}}{{=}}ak=ak^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{spl})}}{{=}}aq(r^{B}_{A}(k^{\prime}))^{-1}r^{B}_{A}(k^{\prime}).

Since

-

aq(r^{B}_{A}(k))^{-1},aq(r^{B}_{A}(k^{\prime}))^{-1}\in A
;

-

r^{B}_{A}(k),r^{B}_{A}(k)\in B
;

-

M
is factorized through
A
and
B
,

it follows that
r^{B}_{A}(k)=r^{B}_{A}(k^{\prime})
and
k=k^{\prime}
, as we have already remarked.
Thus, each element
m\in M
is uniquely expressible in the form
m=ak
with
a\in A
and
k\in\textsf{Ker}(q)
.
This proves that
M
is factorized through
A
and
\textsf{Ker}(q)
, or equivalently,

\textsf{Ker}(q)\in\textsf{FAC}(A/M)
. Hence the map

is well defined.
Clearly, the map

is the base point of the pointed set

and
\textsf{Ker}(l^{B}_{A})=B
by Proposition
2.5
, it follows that

is a morphism of pointed sets. In order to show that this morphism

Suppose now that
C\in\textsf{FAC}(A/M)
. Then

is factorized through
A
and

,

lies in
\mathcal{D}_{l}(M,A)
. Thus we only have to show

. For this, we consider an arbitrary

. Then

(2.4)

b=l^{C}_{A}(b)r^{C}_{A}(b).

Similarly, since
M
is factorized through
A
and
B
, and

,

(2.5)

r^{C}_{A}(b)=l^{B}_{A}(r^{C}_{A}(b))r^{B}_{A}(r^{C}_{A}(b)).

The combination of the last two equalities gives that

Quite obviously,
l^{C}_{A}(b)l^{B}_{A}(r^{C}_{A}(b))\in A

\begin{split}r^{C}_{A}(b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{spl.com.1})}}{{=}}&l^{B}_{A}(r^{C}_{A}(b))r^{B}_{A}(r^{C}_{A}(b))\\

We claim that the maps

2.19 Lemma
.

Let a monoid

and

of

Suppose that

to

B^{\prime}=a_{0}Ba_{0}^{-1}
.
Since

is factorized through its submonoids
A
and

,
there exist elements
b\in B
and

for any

Quite obviously,

3.
SEMI-DIRECT PRODUCT AND NON-ABELIAN COHOMOLOGY OF MONOIDS

Let
A
and
B
be monoids. Recall that a
(left) action of
B
on
A
is a left action

B\times A\to A,\quad(b,a)\longmapsto{b\star a}

of the monoid
B
on the set
A

such that:

(A3)

b\star\textsf{1}\!_{A}=\textsf{1}\!_{A}
;

(A4)

b\star(a_{1}a_{2})=(b\star a_{1})(b\star a_{2})

for all
b\in B
and all
a,a_{1},a_{2}\in A.
Thus, a left action of
B
on
A
is a
map

\star:B\times A\to A

satisfying Conditions (A1)–(A4).

Note that Conditions (A3) and (A4) express the fact that for every
b\in B
, the map

b\star-:A\to A,\,a\mapsto b\star a

is a monoid morphism.
Hence to give a left action of a monoid
B
on a monoid
A
is to give a homomorphism

B\to\textsf{End}(A)
of monoids, where
\textsf{End}(A)
is the set of all endomorphisms of the
monoid
A
which is again a monoid under the usual composition of endomorphisms
as the monoid operation. Given a homomorphism
\phi:B\to\textsf{End}(A)
, one obtains
a (left) action of
B
on
A
by setting
b\star a:=\phi(b)(a)
.

When a monoid
B
acts on a monoid
A
from the
left, one sometimes says that
A
is a
(left)
B
-monoid
.

3.1 Remark
.

It can be seen immediately that if
\star:B\times A\to A

is a monoid action, then
A^{\text{op}}
becomes a left
B
-monoid via
b\star a^{\text{op}}=(b\star a)^{\text{op}}
. This
B
-monoid is called the
opposite
to the
B
-monoid
A
.

For an arbitrary monoid
B
acting on a monoid
A

via a homomorphism
\phi:B\to\textsf{End}(A)
,
a
0
-
cohomology monoid

\textbf{H}_{\phi}^{0}(B,A)
and a
1
-
cohomology pointed set

\textbf{H}_{\phi}^{1}(B,A)
were constructed in

[
2
]
as follows. The
zeroth cohomology of
B
with coefficients in
A
is the set

\{a\in A:b\star a=a\,\,\text{for all}\,\,b\in B\}.

Conditions (A3) and (A4) guarantee that this set is in fact a submonoid of
A
.

Next, the set
\mathcal{Z}_{\phi}^{1}(B,A)
of
1
-cocycles of
B
with coefficients in
A
is the set of those maps

\chi:B\to A
for which

\chi(\textsf{1}_{B})=\textsf{1}_{A}

and

\chi(b_{1}b_{2})=\chi(b_{1})(b_{1}\star\chi(b_{2}))

for all
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
. Clearly

We normally omit the subscript

An important class of examples of monoid factorizations are the ones associated to monoid actions.
For any monoids

\textsf{1}_{A}b

in

3.2 Lemma
.

Let
(A,B)
be a factorization of a monoid

is left

-normal in

Proof.

One direction is immediate from the definition. For the converse, we note first that

aA=A=Aa
for all

.
Now let

\begin{split}mA=l(m)r(m)A&\subseteq l(m)Ar(m)\\

Here the inclusion follows from the fact that

is left

for all

-normality of

∎

3.3 Theorem
.

For a factorization
(A,B)
of a monoid

the following sentences are equivalent:

(i)

r=r^{B}_{A}:M\to B
is a homomorphism of monoids.

(ii)

(A,B)
is left normal.

(iii)

The map
\phi=\phi^{B}_{A}:B\to\emph{\textsf{End}}(A),\,\,\phi(b)=(a\to l(ba))
is an action
of the monoid
B
on the monoid
A
for which
M
is isomorphic as a monoid to
A{\phi}B
via
the map
A\phi B\xrightarrow{(ab\mapsto\imath_{A}(a)\imath_{B}(b)}M
.

Proof.

First we prove that (i) and (ii) are equivalent. If
r:M\to B
is a homomorphism of monoids, then for any
a\in A
and
b\in B
, we have:

r(ba)=r(b)r(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{lr})}}{{=}}r(b)\textsf{1}_{M}=r(b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(R1)}}{{=}}b.

It then follows
that
ba=l(ba)r(ba)=l(ba)b
, implying – since
a\in A
and
b\in B
were arbitrary and since
l(ba)\in A
–
that
bA\subseteq Ab
for all

. Thus,
A
is left
B
-normal in
M
, or equivalently,
(A,B)
is left normal.

Conversely, suppose that
A
is left
B
-normal in
M
. Since
r(\textsf{1}_{M})=\textsf{1}_{M}
by (R1),

preserves units.
Next, consider arbitrary two elements
m,m^{\prime}\in M
. Then
m=l(m)r(m)
and
m^{\prime}=l(m^{\prime})r(m^{\prime})
and hence

mm^{\prime}=l(m)r(m)l(m^{\prime})r(m^{\prime})
. Since
r(m)l(m^{\prime})\in r(m)A
and
r(m)A\subseteq Ar(m)
by
B
-normality of

,
it follows that
r(m)l(m^{\prime})=a\cdot r(m)
for some

. Then

mm^{\prime}=l(m)\cdot a\cdot r(m)\cdot r(m^{\prime})

implying –
since

(and hence

by (
2.1

– that

\begin{split}r(mm^{\prime})=&r(l(m)\cdot a\cdot r(m)\cdot r(m^{\prime}))\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(R2)}}{{=}}&r(l(m)a)\cdot r(m)\cdot r(m^{\prime})\\
=&\textsf{1}_{B}\cdot r(m)\cdot r(m^{\prime})=r(m)\cdot r(m^{\prime}).\end{split}

Consequently,

is a monoid homomorphism.

(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(iii). We have to show that the monoid action corresponding to the map
\phi^{B}_{A}
satisfies Conditions from (A1) to (A4).

Since for any
a\in A
,
\textsf{1}\!_{B}\star a=l(\textsf{1}\!_{M}a)=l(a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L1)}}{{=}}a
, (A1) holds. Similarly, since
for any

b\star\textsf{1}\!_{A}=l(b\textsf{1}\!_{M})=l(b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{lr})}}{{=}}\textsf{1}\!_{A}
, (A3) also holds.

Next, for any
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
and

, we have:

\begin{split}(b_{1}b_{2})\star a=l((b_{1}b_{2})a)=&l(b_{1}(b_{2}a))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L3)}}{{=}}l(b_{1}l(b_{2}a))\\
=&l(b_{1}(b_{2}\star a))=b_{1}\star(b_{2}\star a),\end{split}

where the first, fourth and fifth equalities follow from the definition of the action, while the second
one holds by associativity of multiplication in

. Thus, (A2) holds.

Finally, to prove that (A4) also holds, we proceed as follows. For any
b\in B
and
a_{1},a_{2}\in A,

consider
b\star(a_{1}a_{2})=l(ba_{1}a_{2})
. Since

is left

-normal in

,
ba_{1}=a^{\prime}b
for some

. Then clearly
l(ba_{1})=a^{\prime}
and we have:

\begin{split}b\star(a_{1}a_{2})=l(ba_{1}a_{2})=&l(a^{\prime}ba_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(L2)}}{{=}}a^{\prime}\cdot l(ba_{2})\\
=&l(ba_{1})\cdot l(ba_{2})=(b\star a_{1})(b\star a_{2}).\end{split}

This proves that (A4) also holds.

(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(i). We have already pointed out that for the semi-direct product
A{\phi}B
, the
map
p_{B}=r^{B}_{A}:A{\phi}B\to B
is a homomorphism of monoids.
∎

A monoid

is (isomorphic as a monoid to) a semi-direct product of

3.5 Lemma

For a split homomorphism of monoids
\textstyle{M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

(i)

(ii)

In this section we present certain applications for the results obtained in the previous sections.

Recall that a monoid

For a ring

4.2 Theorem

Let

.

Proof.

of

A corollary follows immediately.

.

Suppose that
