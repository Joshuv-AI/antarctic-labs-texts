The L p L_{p} -discrepancy for finite p > 1 p>1 suffers from the curse of dimensionality
Erich Novak, Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
The L p L_{p} -discrepancy is a classical quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution of an N N -element point set in the d d -dimensional unit cube. Its inverse for dimension d d and error threshold ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \varepsilon\in(0,1) is the number of points in [ 0 , 1 ) d [0,1)^{d} that is required such that the minimal normalized L p L_{p} -discrepancy is less or equal ε \varepsilon . It is well known, that the inverse of L 2 L_{2} -discrepancy grows exponentially fast with the dimension d d , i.e., we have the curse of dimensionality, whereas the inverse of L ∞ L_{\infty} -discrepancy depends exactly linearly on d d . The behavior of inverse of L p L_{p} -discrepancy for general p ∉ { 2 , ∞ } p\not\in\{2,\infty\} was an open problem since many years. Recently, the curse of dimensionality for the L p L_{p} -discrepancy was shown for an infinite sequence of values p p in ( 1 , 2 ] (1,2] , but the general result seemed to be out of reach. In the present paper we show that the L p L_{p} -discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality for all p p in ( 1 , ∞ ) (1,\infty) and only the case p = 1 p=1 is still open. This result follows from a more general result that we show for the worst-case error of positive quadrature formulas for an anchored Sobolev space of once differentiable functions in each variable whose first mixed derivative has finite L q L_{q} -norm, where q q is the Hölder conjugate of p p .



Keywords:
Discrepancy, numerical integration, curse of dimensionality, tractability, quasi-Monte Carlo

1
Introduction and main result

For a set
\mathcal{P}
consisting of
N
points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
in the
d
-dimensional unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
the local discrepancy function
\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{t}})=\frac{|\{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\in[{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}})\}|}{N}-{\rm volume}([{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}})),

for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
, where
[{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{t}})=[0,t_{1})\times[0,t_{2})\times\ldots\times[0,t_{d})
. For a parameter
p\in[1,\infty]
the
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the point set
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the
L_{p}
-norm of the local discrepancy function
\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}
, i.e.,

L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{t}})|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p}\quad\mbox{for $p\in[1,\infty)$,}

and

L_{\infty,N}(\mathcal{P}):=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{t}}\in[0,1]^{d}}|\Delta_{\mathcal{P}}({\boldsymbol{t}})|\quad\mbox{for $p=\infty$.}

Traditionally, the
L_{\infty}
-discrepancy is called star-discrepancy and is denoted by
D_{N}^{\ast}(\mathcal{P})
rather than
L_{\infty,N}(\mathcal{P})
. The study of
L_{p}
-discrepancy has its roots in the theory of uniform distribution modulo one; see
[
1
,
4
,
9
,
10
]
for detailed information.
It has a close relation to numerical integration, see Section
2
.

Since one is interested in point sets with
L_{p}
-discrepancy as low as possible it is obvious to study for
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
the quantity

{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d):=\min_{\mathcal{P}}L_{p,N}(\mathcal{P}),

where the minimum is extended over all
N
-element point sets
\mathcal{P}
in
[0,1)^{d}
. This quantity is called the
N
-th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy in dimension
d
.

Traditionally, the
L_{p}
-discrepancy is studied from the point of view of a fixed dimension
d
and one asks for the asymptotic behavior for increasing sample sizes
N
.
The celebrated result of Roth
[
15
]
is the most famous result in this direction and can be seen as the initial point of discrepancy theory. For
p\in(1,\infty)
it is known that for every dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exist positive reals
c_{d,p},C_{d,p}
such that for every
N\geq 2
it holds true that

c_{d,p}\frac{(\log N)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{N}\leq{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d)\leq C_{d,p}\frac{(\log N)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{N}.

Similar results, but less accurate, are available also for
p\in\{1,\infty\}
. See the above references for further information. The currently best
asymptotical lower bound in the
L_{\infty}
-case can be found in
[
2
]
.

All the classical bounds have a poor dependence on the dimension
d
.
For large
d
these bounds are only meaningful in an asymptotic sense
(for very large
N
) and do not give any information about the
discrepancy in the pre-asymptotic regime (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
or
[
3
, Section 1.7]
for discussions). Nowadays, motivated from applications of point sets with low discrepancy for numerical integration, there is dire need of information about the dependence of discrepancy on the dimension.

This problem is studied with the help of the so-called inverse of
L_{p}
-discrepancy (or, in a more general context, the information complexity; see Section
2
). This concept compares the minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy with the initial discrepancy
{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)
, which is the
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the empty point set, and asks for the minimal number
N
of nodes that is necessary in order to achieve that the
N
-th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy is smaller than
\varepsilon
times
{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)
for a threshold
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. In other words, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
the inverse of the
N
-th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy is defined as

N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ {\rm disc}_{p}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ {\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)\}.

The question is now how fast
N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)
increases, when
d\rightarrow\infty
and
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

It is well known and easy to check that for the initial
L_{p}
-discrepancy we have

{\rm disc}_{p}(0,d)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{(p+1)^{d/p}}&\mbox{if $p\in[1,\infty)$},\\[5.0pt]
1&\mbox{if $p=\infty$}.\end{array}\right.

(1)

Here we observe a difference in the cases of finite and infinite
p
. While for
p=\infty
the initial discrepancy equals 1 for every dimension
d
, for finite values of
p
the initial discrepancy tends to zero exponentially fast with the dimension.

For
p\in\{2,\infty\}
the behavior of the inverse of
N
-th minimal
L_{p}
-discrepancy is well understood. In the
L_{2}
-case it is known that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

(1.125)^{d}(1-\varepsilon^{2})\leq N_{2}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq 1.5^{d}\varepsilon^{-2}.

Here the lower bound was first shown by Woźniakowski in
[
16
]
(see also
[
13
,
14
]
) and the upper bound follows from an easy averaging argument, see, e.g.,
[
14
, Sec. 9.3.2]
.

In the
L_{\infty}
-case it was shown by Heinrich, Novak, Wasilkowski and Woźniakowski in
[
6
]
that there exists an absolute positive constant
C
such that for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have

N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\leq Cd\varepsilon^{-2}.

The currently smallest known value of
C
is
6.23401\ldots
as shown in
[
5
]
. On the other hand, Hinrichs
[
7
]
proved that there exist numbers
c>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
we have

N_{\infty}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq cd\varepsilon^{-1}.

So while the inverse of
L_{2}
-discrepancy grows exponentially fast with the dimension
d
,
the inverse of the star-discrepancy depends only linearly on the dimension
d
.
One says that the
L_{2}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality.
In information based complexity theory the behavior of the inverse
of star-discrepancy is called “polynomial tractability” (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
).
Hence the situation is clear (and quite different) for
p\in\{2,\infty\}
.
But what happens for all other
p\not\in\{2,\infty\}
? This question was open for many years.

Quite recently we proved in
[
12
]
that the
L_{p}
-discrepancy
suffers from the curse for all values
p
of the
form

p=\frac{2\ell}{2\ell-1}\quad\mbox{ with $\ell\in\mathbb{N}$}.

We used ideas that only work if
q
(the Hölder conjugate of
p
) is an even integer and therefore could not handle other values of
p
. Now, with a different approach, we solve the question for all
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Theorem 1
.

For every
p
in
(1,\infty)
there exists a real
C_{p}
that is strictly larger than 1, such that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
we have

N_{p}^{{\rm disc}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon).

We have

C_{p}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p+1}{p}\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}\right)^{-1}>1.

In particular, for all
p
in
(1,\infty)
the
L_{p}
-discrepancy suffers from the curse of dimensionality.

Figure
1
shows the graph of
C_{p}
for
p\in[1,50]
. An improvement will be given in Section
3
.

Figure 1:
Plot of
C_{p}
for
p\in[1,50]
. Note that
C_{1}=1
and
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}C_{p}=1
. We have
C_{2}=1.0022\ldots
,
C_{3}=1.00248\ldots
,
C_{4}=1.00238\ldots
.

The result will follow from a more general result about the integration problem in the anchored Sobolev space with a
q
-norm that will be introduced and discussed in the following Section
2
. This result will be stated as Theorem
3
.
We end the paper with three open problems.

2
Relation to numerical integration

It is well known that the
L_{p}
-discrepancy is related to multivariate integration (see, e.g.,
[
14
, Chapter 9]
). Since this relation is essential for the present approach we repeat the brief summary from
[
12
, Section 2]
. From now on let
p,q\geq 1
be Hölder conjugates, i.e.,
1/p+1/q=1
. For
d=1
let
W_{q}^{1}([0,1])
be the space of absolutely continuous functions whose first derivatives belong to the space
L_{q}([0,1])
. For
d>1
consider the
d
-fold tensor product space which is denoted by

W_{q}^{\boldsymbol{1}}:=W_{q}^{(1,1,\ldots,1)}([0,1]^{d})

and which is the Sobolev space of functions on
[0,1]^{d}
that are once differentiable in each variable and whose first derivative
\partial^{d}f/\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}
has finite
L_{q}
-norm, where
\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}=\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}\ldots\partial x_{d}
. Now consider the subspace of functions that satisfy the boundary conditions
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
if at least one component of
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
equals 0 and equip this subspace with the norm

\|f\|_{d,q}:=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|^{q}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)^{1/q}\quad\mbox{for $q\in[1,\infty)$,}

and

\|f\|_{d,\infty}:=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{\partial^{d}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|\quad\mbox{for $q=\infty$.}

That is, consider the space

F_{d,q}:=\{f\in W_{q}^{\boldsymbol{1}}\ :\ f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0\ \mbox{if $x_{j}=0$ for some $j\in[d]$ and $\|f\|_{d,q}<\infty$}\}.

Now consider multivariate integration

I_{d}(f):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad\mbox{for $f\in F_{d,q}$}.

We approximate the integrals
I_{d}(f)
by linear algorithms of the form

A_{d,N}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{j}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}),

(2)

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
are in
[0,1)^{d}
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{N}
are real weights that we call integration weights. If
a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=a_{N}=1/N
, then the linear algorithm (
2
) is a so-called quasi-Monte Carlo algorithm, and we denote this by
A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}}
.

Define the worst-case error of an algorithm (
2
) by

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})=\sup_{f\in F_{d,q}\atop\|f\|_{d,q}\leq 1}\left|I_{d}(f)-A_{d,N}(f)\right|.

(3)

For a quasi-Monte Carlo algorithm
A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}}
it is well known that

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N}^{{\rm QMC}})=L_{p,N}(\overline{\mathcal{P}}),

where
L_{p,N}(\overline{\mathcal{P}})
is the
L_{p}
-discrepancy of the point set

\overline{\mathcal{P}}=\{\boldsymbol{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\ :\ k\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
is defined as the component-wise difference of the vector containing only ones and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
,
see, e.g.,
[
14
, Section 9.5.1]
for the case
p=2
.

For general linear algorithms (
2
) the worst-case error is the so-called generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})=\overline{L}_{p,N}(\overline{\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{A}),

where
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
is like in (
4
) and
\mathcal{A}
consists of exactly the coefficients from the given linear algorithm (see
[
14
]
).
Here for points
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}
and corresponding
coefficients
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{N}\}
the discrepancy
function is

\overline{\Delta}_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}{\bf 1}_{[\boldsymbol{0},{\boldsymbol{t}})}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})-t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{d}

for
{\boldsymbol{t}}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
and the generalized
L_{p}
-discrepancy is

\overline{L}_{p,N}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|\overline{\Delta}_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}}({\boldsymbol{t}})|^{p}\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{t}}\right)^{1/p}\quad\mbox{for $p\in[1,\infty)$,}

with the usual adaptions for
p=\infty
. If
a_{1}=a_{2}=\ldots=a_{N}=1/N
, then we are back to the classical definition of
L_{p}
-discrepancy from Section
1
.

From this point of view we now study the more general problem of numerical integration in
F_{d,q}
rather than only the
L_{p}
-discrepancy (which corresponds to quasi-Monte Carlo algorithms – although with suitably “reflected” points). We consider linear algorithms where we restrict ourselves to non-negative weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
(thus QMC-algorithms are included in our setting).

We define the
N
-th minimal worst-case error as

e_{q}(N,d):=\min_{A_{d,N}}|e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})|

where the minimum is extended over all linear algorithms of the form (
2
) based on
N
function evaluations along points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
from
[0,1)^{d}
and with non-negative weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
. Note that for all
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e_{q}(N,d)\leq{\rm disc}_{p}(N,d).

(5)

The initial error is

e_{q}(0,d)=\sup_{f\in F_{d,q}\atop\|f\|_{d,q}\leq 1}\left|I_{d}(f)\right|.

We call
f\in F_{d,q}
a worst-case function, if
I_{d}(f/\|f\|_{d,q})=e_{q}(0,d)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
q\in(1,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
with
1/p+1/q=1
. Then we have

e_{q}(0,d)=\frac{1}{(p+1)^{d/p}}

and the worst-case function in
F_{d,q}
is given by
h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})=h_{1}(x_{1})\cdots h_{1}(x_{d})
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}
, where
h_{1}(x)=1-(1-x)^{p}
. Furthermore, we have

\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{p}{p+1}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}=\frac{p}{(p+1)^{1/q}}.

For a proof of Lemma
2
see
[
12
]
.

Note that for all Hölder conjugates
q\in(1,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
and for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e_{q}(0,d)={\rm disc}_{p}(0,d).

Now we define the information complexity as the minimal number of function evaluations necessary in order to reduce the initial error by a factor of
\varepsilon
.
For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
put

N^{{\rm int}}_{q}(\varepsilon,d):=\min\{N\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ e_{q}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\ e_{q}(0,d)\}.

We stress that
N^{{\rm int}}_{q}(\varepsilon,d)

is a kind of restricted complexity since we only allow positive quadrature formulas.

From (
5
), (
1
) and Lemma
2
it follows that for all Hölder conjugates
q\in(1,\infty]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
and for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)^{d}
we have

N^{{\rm int}}_{q}(\varepsilon,d)\leq N^{{\rm disc}}_{p}(\varepsilon,d).

Hence, Theorem
1
follows from the following more general result.

Theorem 3
.

For every
q
in
(1,\infty)
put

C_{p}=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p+1}{p}\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}\right)^{-1},

where
p
is the Hölder conjugate of
q
. Then
C_{p}>1
and for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
we have

N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq C_{p}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon).

(6)

In particular, for all
q
in
(1,\infty)
the integration problem in
F_{d,q}
suffers from the curse of dimensionality for positive quadrature formulas.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
3
is based on a suitable decomposition of the worst-case function
h_{1}
from Lemma
2
. This decomposition depends on
q
and
p
, respectively, and will determine the value of
C_{p}
in (
6
).

For a decomposition point
a
in
(0,1)
that will be
determined in a moment define the functions

\displaystyle h_{1,1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-(1-a)^{p}}{a}\min(x,a),

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(0)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[0,a]}(x)\left((1-(1-x)^{p})-\frac{x}{a}(1-(1-a)^{p})\right),

\displaystyle h_{1,2,(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{[a,1]}(x)\left((1-(1-x)^{p})-(1-(1-a)^{p})\right).

Then we have
h_{1}(x)=h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(0)}(x)+h_{1,2,(1)}(x)
. See Figure
2
for an illustration.

Figure 2:
Decomposition functions
h_{1,1}
,
h_{1,2,(0)}
and
h_{1,2,(1)}
for
p=3
and decomposition point
a=1/2
.

We choose the decomposition point
a
such that

\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(0)}\|_{1,q}=\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(1)}\|_{1,q}=:\alpha.

We have

\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(0)}\|_{1,q}^{q}=\int_{0}^{a}|p(1-x)^{p-1}|^{q}\,{\rm d}x=p^{q}\frac{1-(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}

and

\|h_{1,1}+h_{1,2,(1)}\|_{1,q}^{q}=\int_{a}^{1}|p(1-x)^{p-1}|^{q}\,{\rm d}x=p^{q}\frac{(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}.

Thus we have to choose

a=1-\frac{1}{2^{1/(p+1)}}

and then

\alpha=\frac{1}{2^{1/q}}\frac{p}{(p+1)^{1/q}}=\frac{\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}}{2^{1/q}}.

Obviously,
\alpha<\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}
.
Put

\beta:=\max\left(\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(0)}(x)\,{\rm d}x,\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(1)}(x)\,{\rm d}x\right)

Then we have
\beta<\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{p}{p+1}
.

Consider a linear algorithm
A_{d,N}
based in nodes
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}

and non-negative weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
.
Then for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we define functions

P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(h_{1,1}(x_{k})+h_{1,2,(z_{k})}(x_{k})),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d},

where
z_{k}\in\{0,1\}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
are chosen in a way, such that

P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}).

The functions
P_{i}
are tensor products and therefore we have
the simple formulas

\|P_{i}\|_{d,q}=\alpha^{d}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\int_{[0,1]^{d}}P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq\beta^{d}

for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

In order to estimate the error of
A_{d,N}
we consider the two functions
h_{d}
and

f^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}.

For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we have

h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\leq f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}),

because
P_{k}\geq 0
for all
k
and
P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
.
This and the use of non-negative quadrature weights implies that

A_{d,N}(h_{d})\leq A_{d,N}(f^{*}).

Now for real
y
we use the notation
(y)_{+}:=\max(y,0)
. Then we have the error estimate

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})\geq\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{+}}{2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d,q},\|f^{*}\|_{d,q})},

(7)

which is trivially true if
\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
and which is easily shown if
\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}>\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
, because then

\displaystyle\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-A_{d,N}(h_{d})+A_{d,N}(f^{*})-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}\,e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})+\|f^{*}\|_{d,q}\,e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d,q},\|f^{*}\|_{d,q})\,e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N}).

From the triangle inequality we obtain

\|f^{*}\|_{d,q}\leq N\alpha^{d}=N\frac{\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}}{2^{d/q}},

and we also have

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq N\beta^{d}.

Inserting into (
7
) yields

e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})\geq\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}\right)_{+}}{2\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}\max(1,N/2^{d/q})}.

Since the right hand side is independent of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}
we obtain

e_{q}(N,d)\geq\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}\right)_{+}}{2\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}\max(1,N/2^{d/q})}.

(8)

Put
\gamma:=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{p}{p+1}
. Then we have
\gamma>1
.

Now let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and assume that
e_{q}(N,d)\leq\varepsilon\,e_{q}(0,d)
. This and
e_{q}(0,d)\|h_{d}\|_{d,q}=(\frac{p}{p+1})^{d}
implies that

2\varepsilon\left(\frac{p}{p+1}\right)^{d}\max\left(1,\frac{N}{2^{d/q}}\right)\geq\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}\right)_{+}.

If
N\leq\min(2^{d/q},\gamma^{d})
, then we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p}{p+1}\right)^{d}-N\beta^{d}=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}\right)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,2\varepsilon\left(\frac{p}{p+1}\right)^{d}\max\left(1,\frac{N}{2^{d/q}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2\varepsilon\left(\frac{p}{p+1}\right)^{d}.

Hence

N\geq\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{p}{p+1}\right)^{d}(1-2\varepsilon)\geq(\min(2^{1/q},\gamma))^{d}(1-2\varepsilon).

If
N\geq\min(2^{d/q},\gamma^{d})
, then we trivially have

N\geq(\min(2^{1/q},\gamma))^{d}(1-2\varepsilon).

This yields

N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq(\min(2^{1/q},\gamma))^{d}(1-2\varepsilon),

and we are done.

It remains to compute the values for
C_{p}:=\min(2^{1/q},\gamma)
. Obviously,
C_{p}>1
, since
\gamma>1
. For
a=1-\frac{1}{2^{1/(p+1)}}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(0)}(x)\,{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}1-(1-x)^{p}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{a}^{1}1-(1-a)^{p}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,a-\frac{1-(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}+(1-a)(1-(1-a)^{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{p}{p+1}

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}h_{1,1}(x)+h_{1,2,(1)}(x)\,{\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\frac{1-(1-a)^{p}}{a}x\,{\rm d}x+\int_{a}^{1}1-(1-x)^{p}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{a(1-(1-a)^{p})}{2}+1-a-\frac{(1-a)^{p+1}}{p+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}\frac{p}{p+1}+\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}.

Hence

\beta=\frac{1}{2}\frac{p}{p+1}+\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}.

Therefore

\gamma=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p+1}{p}\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}}

and

C_{p}=\min(2^{1/q},\gamma)=\min(2^{1-1/p},\gamma)=\gamma=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p+1}{p}\frac{1+2^{p/(p+1)}-2^{1/(p+1)}}{4}}.

∎

3
Remark and open problems

There are many spaces where the curse of dimensionality
is present for high dimensional integration,
see the recent survey paper
[
11
]
.
For
p=\infty
the discrepancy is tractable and
this property does not hold for many unweighted
spaces; for a recent example see Krieg
[
8
]
.

The value of
C_{p}
in Theorem
1
and
3
(see also Figure
1
) can be improved with the following “spline” method:

For
y\in[0,1]
define the linear splines

s_{y}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1-(1-y)^{p}}{y}x&\mbox{if $x<y$,}\\[5.0pt]
1-(1-y)^{p}&\mbox{if $x\geq y$.}\end{array}\right.

Then
s_{y}\in F_{1,q}
,
s_{y}\geq 0
and
s_{y}(y)=h_{1}(y)
, the worst-case function from Lemma
2
. Put

\alpha:=\max_{y\in[0,1]}\|s_{y}\|_{1,q}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta:=\max_{y\in[0,1]}\int_{0}^{1}s_{y}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

It is easily shown that

\alpha=\max_{y\in[0,1]}\frac{1-(1-y)^{p}}{y^{1/p}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\beta=\max_{y\in[0,1]}(1-(1-y)^{p})\left(1-\frac{y}{2}\right).

Furthermore, it is elementary but tedious to show that
\alpha<\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}
. On the other hand, since for every
y\in[0,1]
we have
0\leq s_{y}(x)<h_{1}(x)
, it is clear that
\beta<\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x
. This yields that

\widetilde{C}_{p}:=\min\left(\frac{\|h_{1}\|_{1,q}}{\alpha},\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{1}h_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x\right)>1.

(9)

Now we modify the approach in the proof of Theorem
3
in the following way: As before, consider a linear algorithm
A_{d,N}
based in nodes
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}
in
[0,1]^{d}
and with non-negative weights
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
. Let
x_{i,j}
be the
j
-th coordinate of the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we define functions

P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=s_{x_{i,1}}(x_{1})s_{x_{i,2}}(x_{2})\cdots s_{x_{i,d}}(x_{d}),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1]^{d}.

Consider the two functions
h_{d}
and
f^{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}
. Since
A_{d,N}
uses only non-negative weights we have

\displaystyle A_{d,N}(f^{*})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}P_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}s_{x_{i,1}}(x_{i,1})s_{x_{i,2}}(x_{i,2})\cdots s_{x_{i,d}}(x_{i,d})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=A_{d,N}(h_{d}).

In the same way as in the proof of Theorem
3
we obtain

\displaystyle e(F_{d,q},A_{d,N})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{+}}{2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d,q},\|f^{*}\|_{d,q})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}\right)_{+}}{2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d,q},N\alpha^{d})},

where we used that
\|f^{*}\|_{d,q}\leq N\alpha^{d}
and
\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f^{*}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq N\beta^{d}
. This yields

e_{q}(N,d)\geq\frac{\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}h_{d}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}-N\beta^{d}\right)_{+}}{2\max(\|h_{d}\|_{d,q},N\alpha^{d})}.

From here it follows in the same way as in the proof of Theorem
3
that

N_{q}^{{\rm int}}(\varepsilon,d)\geq\widetilde{C}_{p}^{d}\,(1-2\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{where $\widetilde{C}_{p}>1$ is given in \eqref{def:C1new}.}

This re-proves Theorem
3
(and Theorem
1
). The advantage of
C_{p}
in Theorem
1
and
3
is that it is stated explicitly for any
p\in(1,\infty)
. The value of
\widetilde{C}_{p}
can be computed numerically for every
p\in(1,\infty)
. Experiments show a strong improvement of
\widetilde{C}_{p}
over
C_{p}
. See the following table and Figure
3
:

\widetilde{C}_{p}&1.06066\ldots&1.07231\ldots&1.07276\ldots&1.07005\ldots\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{l||l|l|l|l}p&10&20&30&100\\

. The blue line is the graph from Figure

.

We end the paper with three open problems.

1.

In order to estimate the error of quadrature formulas, we only
considered two functions
h_{d}
and

f^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}.

The reason is that we have an exact formula for the norm of
P_{i}
,
while the norms of “better” fooling functions are difficult to estimate.
Our first Open Problem is to improve the lower bounds by finding bigger values
for the constant
C_{p}
in the main result and
\widetilde{C}_{p}
.

2.

The proof in
[
12
]
only works for even
q
.
However, for even
q
, it is more general since we
prove the lower bound for all quadrature formulas, the weights
a_{i}

do not have to be positive.
Hence we ask, this is Open Problem 2, whether the curse also holds
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
for arbitrary quadrature formulas.
We guess that the answer is yes, but our attempts to prove it failed.

3.

We already mentioned that the problem is still open for
p=1
.
Our technique does not work in this case and we even do not guess
an answer to this third Open Problem.
