Anisotropic acoustics in dipolar Fermi gases Preprint: APS/123-QED
Reuben R. W. Wang, John L. Bohn

Abstract
We consider plane wave modes in ultracold, but not quantum degenerate, dipolar Fermi gases in the hydrodynamic limit. Longitudinal waves present anisotropies in both the speed of sound and their damping, and experience a small, undulatory effect in their flow velocity. Two distinct types of shear waves appear, a “familiar” one, and another that is accompanied by nontrivial density and temperature modulations. We propose these shear modes as an experimental means to measure the viscosity coefficients, including their anisotropies.

(18)

Such a mode implies the existence of long-lived density modulations due to a balance between thermal and dipolar mean-field energies. We leave further analysis of this phenomenon to a future work.

V
Conclusions

We have formulated a comprehensive theory of normal dipolar Fermi gases in the hydrodynamic regime, including effects from both the DMF and collisions. When comparable to the thermal energy, the DMF introduces a significant anisotropy to the speed of sound.
That is, sound travels faster when parallel to the dipole orientation but slower when orthogonal.
If too large, the DMF can completely overpower thermal molecular motion, triggering dynamical instability (i.e. the speed of sound becomes imaginary).
As a consequence of the sound speed dipole angle dependence, these instabilities only occur in waves that propagate at angles within
\Theta\approx 54.7^{\circ}
and
125.3^{\circ}
, relative to the dipole orientation. Outside this angular range, linear wave excitations remain completely robust against dipolar collapse.

Collisions on the other hand, serve to return the gas to hydrodynamic equilibrium, the route of which is also anisotropic. As in the case of bosons
Wang and Bohn 2022a
;
Wang and Bohn 2022b
, thermal conductivity and viscosity encompass these local thermalization effects and result in the anisotropic damping of sounds waves. Additionally, we find that these transport tensors lead to a minute undulatory divergence between the wave propagation
\hat{\bm{K}}
and fluid flow
\hat{\bm{\xi}}
directions, an effect not possible in dipolar superfluids.
Not surprisingly, anisotropy is also present in shear excitations that are directly consequent of viscous stresses. We therefore suggest that experimental realizations of shear waves could permit measurements of the viscosity coefficients. A curiosity of laminar shearing a dipolar gas, is that the viscid flow could incite an anomalous density and temperature excitation we identify as shear-entropy waves. This effect was one we found no analogy to in the literature.

With dense long-lived samples of polar molecules now accessible to the ultracold community, we expect the phenomena presented in this work and more yet to be explored with our theory, are experimentally achievable with current technologies.
A direction for future work could therefore be to solve the full nonlinear fluid equations, where we expect to find a rich tapestry of hydrodynamic phenomena.
Additionally, recent experiments of electric field shielded KRb molecules have suggested suppression of both two and three-body losses
Matsuda et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Schmidt et al. 2022
. This opens opportunities for hydrodynamic studies after the onset of dipolar collapse
Bohn et al. 2009
, where sustaining a large fraction of molecules past the instability could permit turbulent cascades from strong nonlinear flows
Drazin and Reid 2004
.

Appendix A
Evaluation of the Transport Tensors

In a nondegenerate gas, local equilibrium occurs by means of dipolar collisions parameterized by the dipole length
a_{d}
.
Close to local thermal equilibrium, re-equilibration processes are encapsulated by the transport tensors of viscosity and thermal conductivity, derivable from a microscopic picture with methods established by Chapman and Enskog
Chapman and Cowling 1990
.
Within length scales on the order of the molecular mean-free path, molecular interactions are dominated by collisional processes. The local distribution of molecules thus has dynamics well described by the Boltzmann transport equation

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{i}\partial_{i}\right)f(\bm{r},\bm{v})={\cal C}[f(\bm{r},\bm{v})],

(19a)

\displaystyle{\cal C}[f]=\int d\Omega^{\prime}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega^{\prime}}\int d^{3}v_{1}\absolutevalue{\boldsymbol{v} - \boldsymbol{v}_1}\left(f^{\prime}f_{1}^{\prime}-ff_{1}\right),

(19b)

where
f(\bm{r},\bm{v})
is the phase space distribution function and
{\cal C}[f]
is the two-body collision integral. All repeated indices are summed over and primes denote post-collision velocities for pairs of colliding molecules with incoming velocities
\bm{v}
and
\bm{v}_{1}
. We also adopt the compact notation
f_{1}=f(\bm{r},\bm{v}_{1})
and
f^{\prime}=f(\bm{r},\bm{v}^{\prime})
.
The gas number density is given by
n(\bm{r},t)=\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)
, which is only dependent on temperature at thermal equilibrium,
n_{0}=n_{0}(\beta)
. Thermal equilibrium also imposes a Boltzmann velocity distribution

\displaystyle f_{0}(\bm{u},\beta)

\displaystyle=n_{0}(\beta)c_{0}(\bm{u},\beta)

\displaystyle=n_{0}(\beta)\left(\frac{m\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left(-\frac{m\beta}{2}\bm{u}^{2}\right),

(20)

where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
,
\bm{u}^{2}=u_{k}u_{k}
, and
\bm{u}(\bm{r})=\bm{v}-\bm{U}(\bm{r})
is the
peculiar velocity, defined as the molecular velocity
\bm{v}
relative to the flow velocity
\bm{U}(\bm{r},t)=n(\bm{r},t)^{-1}\int d^{3}vf(\bm{r},\bm{v},t)\bm{v}

Close to thermal equilibrium, the molecular distribution fluctuates as

\displaystyle f(\bm{r},\bm{u},\beta)\approx f_{0}(\bm{u},\beta)[1+\Phi(\bm{r},\bm{u},\beta)],

(21)

with a perturbation function
\Phi
, that must satisfy

\displaystyle\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})\Phi(\bm{r},\bm{u},\beta)m=0,

(22a)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})\Phi(\bm{r},\bm{u},\beta)m\bm{u}=0,

(22b)

\displaystyle\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})\Phi(\bm{r},\bm{u},\beta)\frac{1}{2}m\bm{u}^{2}=0,

(22c)

as a result of mass, momentum and energy conservation respectively. Enskog’s prescription of successive approximations then renders the Boltzmann equation, to leading non-trivial order, as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{i}\partial_{i}\right)f_{0}\approx C[f_{0}\Phi].

(23)

The left-hand side of Eq. (
23
) above evaluates to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{k}\partial_{k}\right)f_{0}=f_{0}\big[V_{k}\partial_{k}(\ln T)+m\beta W_{k\ell}D_{k\ell}\big],

(24)

as detailed in App.
B
, where

\displaystyle V_{i}(\bm{u})\equiv\left(\frac{m\beta\bm{u}^{2}}{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)u_{i},

(25a)

\displaystyle W_{ij}(\bm{u})\equiv u_{i}u_{j}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\bm{u}^{2},

(25b)

\displaystyle D_{ij}(\bm{U})\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{j}U_{i}+\partial_{i}U_{j}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\partial_{k}U_{k}.

(25c)

The collision integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (
23
) is then

\displaystyle C[f]\approx\int d^{3}u_{1}

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \boldsymbol{u} - \boldsymbol{u}_1 }f_{0}(\bm{u})f_{0}(\bm{u}_{1})\int d\Omega^{\prime}\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega^{\prime}}\Delta\Phi,

(26)

where
\Delta\Phi=\Phi^{\prime}+\Phi^{\prime}_{1}-\Phi-\Phi_{1}
. Since Eq. (
26
) is linear in
\Phi
and Eq. (
24
) is linear in the quantities
\partial_{i}\ln T
and
D_{ij}
, one can infer an
ansatz
for the scalar function
\Phi
, of the form

\displaystyle\Phi(\bm{u},\beta)

\displaystyle={\cal B}_{k}\partial_{k}(\ln T)+m\beta{\cal A}_{k\ell}D_{k\ell},

(27)

where
\bm{{\cal B}}
(vector) and
\bm{{\cal A}}
(2-rank tensor) are functions of
\bm{u}
and
\beta
. Upon comparing terms,
\bm{{\cal B}}
and
\bm{{\cal A}}
must have the forms

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{ij}(\bm{u},n_{0},\beta)=W_{k\ell}(\bm{u})a_{k\ell ij}(u,n_{0},\beta),

(28a)

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{i}(\bm{u},n_{0},\beta)=V_{j}(\bm{u})b_{ji}(u,n_{0},\beta),

(28b)

where
u=\absolutevalue{\boldsymbol{u}}
, and the coefficients
a_{k\ell mn}(u,n_{0},\beta)
and
b_{k\ell}(u,n_{0},\beta)
are introduced as variational
ansatz
. We are thus left with

\displaystyle\Phi(\bm{u},\beta)

\displaystyle=V_{\ell}(\bm{u})b_{\ell k}(n_{0},\beta)\partial_{k}(\ln T)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+2m\beta W_{ij}(\bm{u})a_{ijk\ell}(n_{0},\beta)D_{k\ell}.

(29)

Referring back to Eq. (
A
), an average is taken over the molecular distribution by multiplying Eq. (
23
) by
V_{i}(\bm{u})
and
W_{ij}(\bm{u})
, then integrating over
\bm{u}
to give

\displaystyle\left(\int d^{3}u\>f_{0}(\bm{u})V_{i}(\bm{u})V_{j}(\bm{u})\right)\partial_{j}(\ln T)

(30)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\approx\left(\int d^{3}u\>V_{i}(\bm{u})C[f_{0}V_{k}]\right)b_{kj}\partial_{j}(\ln T),

\displaystyle\left(\int d^{3}uf_{0}(\bm{u})W_{ij}W_{k\ell}\right)D_{k\ell}

(31)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\approx\left(-\frac{\beta}{n_{0}}\int d^{3}u\>W_{ij}{\cal C}[f_{0}W_{mn}]\right)\mu_{mnk\ell}D_{k\ell},

respectively. Evaluating the integrals with the methods in Refs.
Wang et al. 2020
;
Wang and Bohn 2021
and comparing them to the linear constitutive relations

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{ij}\partial_{j}T,

(32a)

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{ijk\ell}D_{k\ell},

(32b)

we obtain the transport tensor of thermal conductivity

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}=\frac{175\mu_{0}k_{B}}{8m}\begin{pmatrix}5+\cos(2 \Theta)&0&-\sin(2 \Theta)\\
0&6&0\\
-\sin(2 \Theta)&0&5-\cos(2 \Theta)\end{pmatrix},

(33)

and the 13 unique viscosity coefficients

\displaystyle\mu_{1111}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{8}(117\cos(4 \Theta)+84\cos(2 \Theta)+415),

(34a)

\displaystyle\mu_{1113}=-\frac{3\mu_{0}}{8}(39\sin(4 \Theta)+14\sin(2 \Theta)),

(34b)

\displaystyle\mu_{1122}=-\frac{7\mu_{0}}{2}(3\cos(2 \Theta)+11),

(34c)

\displaystyle\mu_{1133}=-\frac{\mu_{0}}{8}(117\cos(4 \Theta)+107),

(34d)

\displaystyle\mu_{1212}=\frac{9\mu_{0}}{2}(5\cos(2 \Theta)+9),

(34e)

\displaystyle\mu_{1223}=-\frac{45}{2}\mu_{0}\sin(2 \Theta),

(34f)

\displaystyle\mu_{1313}=-\frac{9\mu_{0}}{8}(13\cos(4 \Theta)-29),

(34g)

\displaystyle\mu_{1322}=\frac{21}{2}\mu_{0}\sin( 2 \Theta),

(34h)

\displaystyle\mu_{1333}=\frac{3\mu_{0}}{8}(39\sin(4 \Theta)-14\sin(2 \Theta)),

(34i)

\displaystyle\mu_{2222}=77\mu_{0},

(34j)

\displaystyle\mu_{2233}=\frac{7\mu_{0}}{2}(3\cos(2 \Theta)-11),

(34k)

\displaystyle\mu_{2323}=-\frac{9\mu_{0}}{2}(5\cos(2 \Theta)-9),

(34l)

\displaystyle\mu_{3333}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{8}(117\cos(4 \Theta)-84\cos(2 \Theta)+415),

(34m)

where

. Other non-trivial viscosity terms are specified by the tensor symmetry identities

\displaystyle\mu_{ijmn}

Appendix B
Equilibrium Boltzmann Equation

In this appendix section, we extend the derivation of the equilibrium Boltzmann Equation found in
Bond et al. 1965
, to include arbitrary external potentials.

At thermal equilibrium, the left-hand side of the Boltzmann equation is given as

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal D}}{{\cal D}t}f_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{i}\partial_{i}-\frac{\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\right)f_{0}

(36)

\displaystyle=f_{0}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v_{i}\partial_{i}-\frac{\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\right)\ln f_{0},

(37)

where
f_{0}
is of the form in Eq. (
A
) and
n_{0}
is determined by the form of
V(\bm{r})
, so that

\displaystyle\ln f_{0}=\frac{3}{2}\ln\left(\frac{m}{2\pi}\right)+\ln( n_0 \beta^{3/2} )-\beta\frac{m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}.

(38)

The material derivative is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+U_{i}\partial_{i},

(39)

so that the
{\cal D}/{\cal D}t
operator can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal D}}{{\cal D}t}=\frac{D}{Dt}+u_{i}\partial_{i}-\frac{\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}.

(40)

We now treat the derivatives term by term.

First considering
u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln f_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln f_{0}

\displaystyle=u_{i}\partial_{i}\left[\ln( n_0 )+\frac{3}{2}\ln( \beta)-\beta\frac{m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}\right],

\displaystyle=u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln( n_0 )+\left(\beta\frac{m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right)u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln T

\displaystyle\quad+\beta mu_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}U_{j}.

(41)

As for
{D\ln f_{0}/Dt}
, we first consider the equations of conservation at thermal equilibrium which read

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}\ln n_{0}=-\partial_{j}U_{j},

(42a)

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}\ln T=-\frac{2}{3}\partial_{j}U_{j},

(42b)

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}U_{i}=-\frac{k_{B}T}{m}\partial_{i}\ln( n_0 T )-\frac{1}{m}\partial_{i}V(\bm{r}).

(42c)

The material derivative of
\ln f_{0}
then becomes

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}\ln f_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{Dt}\left[\ln( n_0 )+\frac{3}{2}\ln( \beta)-\beta\frac{m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{3}m\bm{u}^{2}\partial_{j}U_{j}-\beta u_{i}\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\quad-u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln( n_0 )-u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln T.

(43)

Finally, the term explicit in the potential is

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})}{m}\frac{\partial\ln f_{0}}{\partial u_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})}{2}\frac{\partial\bm{u}^{2}}{\partial u_{i}}=\beta u_{i}\partial_{i}V(\bm{r}).

(44)

Putting all these terms together, we get

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal D}}{{\cal D}t}\ln f_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{Dt}\ln f_{0}+u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln f_{0}-\frac{\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\ln f_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigg[-\frac{\beta}{3}m\bm{u}^{2}\partial_{j}U_{j}-u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln T

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\beta u_{i}\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})-u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln( n_0 )\bigg]

\displaystyle+\bigg[\beta mu_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}U_{j}+u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln( n_0 )

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left(\beta\frac{m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right)u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln T\bigg]

\displaystyle+\bigg[\beta u_{i}\partial_{i}V(\bm{r})\bigg],

(45)

which gives the final result

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal D}}{{\cal D}t}\ln f_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\beta\frac{m\bm{u}^{2}}{2}-\frac{5}{2}\right)u_{i}\partial_{i}\ln T

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\beta m\left(u_{i}u_{j}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\bm{u}^{2}\right)\partial_{i}U_{j}.

(46)

This shows that the external potential does not affect the Chapman-Enskog derivation of transport tensors.
