Spatial Censored Regression Models in R: The CensSpatial Package
José Alejandro Ordoñez, Christian E. Galarza, Victor H. Lachos

Abstract
CensSpatial is an R package for analyzing spatial censored data through linear models. It offers a set of tools for simulating, estimating, making predictions, and performing local influence diagnostics for outlier detection. The package provides four algorithms for estimation and prediction. One of them is based on the stochastic approximation of the EM (SAEM) algorithm, which allows easy and fast estimation of the parameters of linear spatial models when censoring is present. The package provides worthy measures to perform diagnostic analysis using the Hessian matrix of the completed log-likelihood function. This work is divided into two parts. The first part discusses and illustrates the utilities that the package offers for estimating and predicting spatial censored data. The second one describes the valuable tools to perform diagnostic analysis. Several examples in spatial environmental data are also provided.



1
Introduction

Spatial data can be described as observations that are spatially located in a coordinated area. Their analysis constitutes an area of statistics that is characterized by the spatial correlation among individuals where the interest is to predict the response at non-sampling sites. Usually, spatial analysis assumes that observations are fully observed, but this is not always possible. Sometimes data are subject to upper or lower (or both) detection limits, beyond which they are not quantifiable
(
Schelin and
Sjöstedt-de Luna 2014
)
. An example of this can be seen in environmental (spatial) monitoring of different variables which often involves left-censored observations falling below the minimum limit of detection (LOD) of
the instruments used to quantify them.

Methods for handling this type of data have been widely discussed in the statistical literature.
Rathbun 2006

applied a Robbins-Monro stochastic approximation to estimate the parameters of a spatial regression model, obtain conditional simulations of left-censored observations and then obtain a predictor for data at unsampled sites by taking the weighted mean of kriging predictors via importance sampling.
Schelin and
Sjöstedt-de Luna 2014
proposed a semi-naive method that determines imputed values at censored locations in an iterative algorithm together with variogram estimation. They compared its predictive performance through a simulation study with Rathbun’s, Naive 1 and Naive 2 methods, which involve replacing the censored data by the LOD or the LOD/2 respectively.
Militino and
Ugarte 1999
developed an EM-type algorithm for maximum likelihood (ML) estimation in censored spatial
data. However, this approach suffers from several drawbacks that
restrict its applicability, such as the computation of non-closed form expressions, which depend on high dimensional integrals.

The EM algorithm proposed by
Dempster
et al. 1977
is an iterative procedure for finding the maximum likelihood (ML) estimators of models that involves unobserved latent variables. In our context, an EM-type algorithm uses the observed and censored data to obtain estimates from the mean and correlation structures of a spatial model. In some cases, this algorithm cannot be carried out because the calculation of the expectations involved in E-step cannot be computed analytically. An alternative is to approximate those expectations by Monte Carlo methods to replace the traditional E-step. This procedure is the so-called Monte Carlo Markov chain (MCEM) algorithm. Even though it is possible to approximate these expectations, this can involve heavy computational effort when some generating methods are used to sample from the conditional latent variable distributions at each iteration.

An alternative to deal with this problem is the SAEM algorithm proposed by
Delyon
et al. 1999
. This algorithm replaces the E-step by a stochastic approximation obtained using simulated data, while the M-step remains unchanged. In the framework of spatial models,
Jank 2006
showed that the computational effort of
SAEM is much smaller and it reaches convergence in just a fraction of
the simulation iterations when compared with the MCEM algorithm. This is due to the fact that a memory effect is contained in the SAEM method, in which the
previous simulations are considered in the computation of the posterior ones.

Another important topic in spatial censored data analysis is the study of influential observations. Influence analysis is an important statistical tool because it can
assess the goodness-of-fit and influential observations that can distort the parameter estimates leading in some cases to erroneous inference
(
Giménez and
Galea 2013
)
. For influential observation detection, we discuss two approaches; the first one is the case-deletion approach
(
Cook 1977
)
, an intuitively appealing method which has been applied to many statistical models. The second one is local influence diagnostics
(
Cook 1986
)
. This technique assesses the stability of the estimation outputs with respect to the model inputs and has recently received attention in the literature on spatial models
(
De Bastiani et al. 2014
, see, e.g.,)
.
Zhu and Lee 2001
developed an approach to perform local influence analysis for general statistical models with missing data, based on the
Q
-displacement function which is closely related to the conditional expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood in the E-step of the EM algorithm. This approach produces results very similar to those obtained from Cook’s method. Moreover, the case-deletion can be studied by the
Q
-displacement function, following the approach of
Zhu
et al. 2001
. So, we develop here methods to obtain case-deletion measures and local influence measures by using the method of
Zhu
et al. 2001

(
Lee and Xu 2004
;
Zhu and Lee 2001
, see also)
in the context of spatial censored models.

The aim of this paper is to present the utilities of the R package
CensSpatial
. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we derive all the theoretical framework for the estimation and prediction functions for spatial censored data implemented in the proposed package, along with the R functions for calling these methods. Also we present some useful graphical tools for interpretation as well as an application to real data. Section
4
presents the local influence diagnostic methods to detect influential observations. Graphical tools and an application are also provided in this section. Further details such as first and second derivatives of the spatial correlation, can be found in the Appendix.

2
Estimation and prediction utilities

First, we specify the spatial censored linear (SCL) model in a general sense and then we describe all the estimation and prediction tools implemented in the
CensSpatial
package.

2.1
Background of spatial linear models for censored response

As in
De Bastiani et al. 2014
, we will consider a Gaussian model with a linear specification for
the spatial trend, which allows the inclusion of polynomial trends or more generally, spatially referenced covariates. The linear model is defined as

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$},

(1)

where
\mathbf{X}
and
\beta
represent the trend matrix and the parameters considered for the mean structure respectively, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}\sim N_{n}(\mathbf{0},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
is the stochastic component. We consider for the spatial case,
\mathbf{X}
is a full rank matrix and
\Sigma
is a non-singular matrix with the form
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\sigma^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}
, with
\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}=(\nu^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mathbf{R}(\phi))
, where
\mathbf{I}_{n}
represents an
n\times n
identity matrix,

\mathbf{R}(\phi)
is the correlation matrix for the error term and
\nu^{2}=\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}
(see Appendix). We also assume that the response
Z_{i}
is not fully observed for all area
i
, so we call
(V_{i},C_{i})
the observed data for the
i
th area, while
V_{i}
represents either an uncensored observation
(V_{i}=V_{0i})
or the LOD of censoring level
(V_{i}=(V_{1i},V_{2i}))
and
C_{i}
is the censoring indicator such that:

\displaystyle C_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&if&V_{1i}\leq Z_{i}\leq V_{2i}\,,\\
0&if&Z_{i}=V_{0i}\,.\end{array}\right.

Note that when
C_{i}=1
, if
Z_{i}\in(-\infty,V_{2i}]
, then we get a left censored SCL model
(
Toscas 2010
)
, and if
Z_{i}\in[V_{1i},\infty)
, then we get a right censored SCL model. The model defined in (
1
)-(
2.1
) is called the spatial censored
linear (SCL) model.

3
Likelihood and SAEM implementation

To compute the likelihood function associated with the SCL model, the observed and censored components of
\mathbf{Z}
must be treated separately. Let
\mathbf{Z}^{o}
be the
n^{o}
-vector of observed responses and
\mathbf{Z}^{c}
be
the
n^{c}
-vector of censored observations, with
(n=n^{o}+n^{c})
, such that
C_{i}=0
for all elements in
\mathbf{Z}^{o}
, and
C_{i}=1
for all elements in
\mathbf{Z}^{c}
. After reordering,
\mathbf{Z}
,
V
,
\mathbf{X}
, and
\Sigma
can be partitioned as follows:

\mathbf{Z}=vec(\mathbf{Z}^{o},\mathbf{Z}^{c}),\quad\textbf{V}=vec(\mathbf{V}^{o},\mathbf{V}^{c}),\quad\mathbf{X}^{\top}=[\mathbf{X}^{o\top},\mathbf{X}^{c\top}]\quad{\rm and}\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo}&\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oc}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{co}&\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{cc}\end{array}\right],

where
vec(\cdot)
denotes the function which stacks vectors or matrices having the same number of columns. Consequently,
\mathbf{Z}^{o}\sim N_{n^{o}}(\mathbf{X}^{o}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo})
,
\mathbf{Z}^{c}|\mathbf{Z}^{o}\sim N_{n^{c}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$},\mathbf{S}),
where
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=\mathbf{X}^{c}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{co}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo})^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}^{o}-\mathbf{X}^{o}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})

and
\mathbf{S}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{cc}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{co}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oc}
. Now, let
\phi_{n}(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{a},\mathbf{A})
be the
pdf
of
N_{n}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{A})
evaluated at
\mathbf{u}
. From
Vaida and
Liu 2009
and
Jacqmin-Gadda et al. 2000
, the likelihood function (using conditional probability arguments) is given by

\displaystyle L(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\phi_{n^{o}}(\mathbf{Z}^{o};\mathbf{X}^{o}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo})P(\mathbf{Z}^{c}\in\mathbb{V}^{c}|\mathbf{Z}^{o}),

(5)

where

\mathbb{V}^{c}=\{\mathbf{Z}^{c}=(Z_{1}^{c},\ldots,Z_{n^{c}}^{c})^{\top}|V_{11}^{c}\leq Z_{1}^{c}\leq V_{21}^{c},\ldots,V_{1n^{c}}^{c}\leq Z_{n^{c}}^{c}\leq V_{2n^{c}}^{c}\}

and
P(\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{A}|\mathbf{Z}^{o})
denotes the conditional probability of
\mathbf{u}
being in the set
\mathbb{A}
given the observed response. This function can be evaluated without much computational burden through the
mvtnorm
routine available in the namesake R package
(
Genz et al. 2020
, see)
. Now, we can compare models using a likelihood based criterion, such as the Akaike
(
Akaike 1974
, AIC;)
and Schwarz
(
Schwarz 1978
, BIC;)
information criteria.

3.1
SAEM algorithm for censored spatial data

We propose an EM-type (SAEM) algorithm by considering
\mathbf{Z}

as a missing data or latent variable. In the estimation, the reading
\mathbf{V}
at the censored observations (
C_{i}=1
) is treated as hypothetical missing data, and augmented with the observed dataset
\mathbf{Z}_{c}=(\mathbf{C}^{\top},\mathbf{V}^{\top},\mathbf{Z}^{\top})^{\top}
. Hence, the
complete-data log-likelihood function is given by:

\displaystyle\ell_{c}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}|)+(\mathbf{Z}-\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}-\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})\right]+cte,

(6)

with
cte
being a constant, independent of the parameter vector
\theta
. Given the current estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)}
, the E-step computes the
conditional expectation of the complete data log-likelihood function, i.e.,
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)})=E[\ell_{c}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\mathbf{Z}_{c})|\mathbf{V},\mathbf{C},\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)}]
. Denote the two conditional first moments for the response
\mathbf{Z}
as
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}=E\{\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}|\textbf{V},\mathbf{C},\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)}\}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}=E\{\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}|\textbf{V},\mathbf{C},\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)}\}
. For the SCL model, we have

\displaystyle Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}|)+\widehat{A}^{(k)}\right],

where

\widehat{A}^{(k)}=tr\big(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\big)-2\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}.

\mathbf{Z}
is not fully observed, so the components of
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}
, corresponding to
C_{i}=1
will be estimated by the first two moments of the truncated normal distribution respectively. When
C_{i}=0
, these components can be obtained directly from the observed values, i.e,
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}=\mathbf{Z}^{o}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}=\mathbf{Z}^{o}\mathbf{Z}^{o\top}
. Although these expectations exhibit closed forms (as functions of multinormal probabilities; for further details, please refer to
Arismendi 2013
), the calculation is computationally expensive requiring high-dimensional numerical integrations, resulting in convergence issues when the proportion of censored observations is non-negligible. At each iteration, the SAEM algorithm successively simulates from the conditional distribution of the latent variable, and updates the unknown parameters of the model. Thus, at iteration
k
, the SAEM proceeds as follows:

•

Step E-1 (sampling)
: Sample
\mathbf{Z}^{c}
from a truncated normal distribution, denoted by
TN_{n^{c}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$},\mathbf{S};\mathbb{A}^{c})
, with

\mathbb{A}^{c}=\{\mathbf{Z}^{c}=(Z_{1}^{c},\ldots,Z_{n^{c}}^{c})^{\top}|V_{11}^{c}\leq Z_{1}^{c}\leq V_{21}^{c},\ldots,V_{1n^{c}}^{c}\leq Z_{n^{c}}^{c}\leq V_{2n^{c}}^{c}\},

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=\mathbf{X}^{c}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{co}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo})^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}^{o}-\mathbf{X}^{o}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})

and

\mathbf{S}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{cc}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{co}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oo})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{oc}
.
Here
TN_{n}(.;\mathbb{A})
denotes the
n
-variate truncated normal distribution in the interval
\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}=A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{n}
. The new observation
\mathbf{Z}^{(k,l)}=(Z^{(k,l)}_{1},\ldots,Z^{(k,l)}_{n^{c}},Z_{n^{c}_{i}+1},\ldots,Z_{n})
is a sample generated for the
n^{c}
censored cases and the observed values (uncensored cases), for
l=1,\ldots,M.

•

Step E-2 (stochastic approximation)
: Since we have the sequence
\mathbf{Z}^{(k,l)}
, at the
k
-th iteration we replace the conditional expectations
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}
by the following stochastic approximations:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k-1)}+\delta_{k}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l=1}^{M}\mathbf{Z}^{(k,l)}-\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k-1)}\right],

(7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k-1)}+\delta_{k}\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{l=1}^{M}\mathbf{Z}^{(k,l)}\mathbf{Z}^{(k,l)\top}-\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k-1)}\right],

(8)

where
\delta_{k}
is a smoothness parameter, i.e., a decreasing sequence of positive numbers as defined in
Kuhn and
Lavielle 2004
, such that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}^{2}<\infty
. For the SAEM, the E-Step coincides with the MCEM algorithm at the expense of a significantly smaller number of simulations
M
(suggested to be
M\leq 20
). For more details, see
Delyon
et al. 1999
.

Finally, the conditional maximization (CM) step maximizes
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)})
with
respect to
\theta
and obtains a new estimate
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k+1)}
, as follows:

CM-Step (conditional maximization)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}^{-1(k)}\mathbf{X}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}^{-1(k)}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\Big[tr\big(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}^{-1(k)}\big)-2\widehat{\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\widehat{\Psi}^{-1(k)}\mathbf{X}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{(k+1)}+\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{\top(k+1)}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}^{-1(k)}\mathbf{X}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{(k+1)}\Big]\,\,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log(|\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}|)-\frac{1}{2}\left[tr\big(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}^{-1}\big)-2\widehat{\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{(k+1)}\right.\right.

(9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\left.\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{\top(k+1)}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{X}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}^{(k+1)}\right]\right),

with
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=(\nu^{2},\phi)^{\top}
. Note that
\widehat{\tau}^{2}
can be recovered via

\widehat{\tau}^{2(k+1)}=\widehat{\nu}^{2(k+1)}\widehat{\sigma}^{2(k+1)}
. The CM-step (
9
) can be easily accomplished using the
optim
routine in
R
. This process is iterated until some absolute distance between two successive evaluations of the actual log-likelihood

\ell(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, such as
|\ell(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k+1)})-\ell(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)})|
or

|\ell(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k+1)})/\ell(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}^{(k)})-1|
, becomes small enough.

3.2
Prediction

Following
Diggle and
Ribeiro 2007
, let
\mathbf{Z}_{obs}
denote a vector of random variables with observed realized values, and let
\mathbf{Z}_{pred}
denote another random variable whose realized values we would like to predict from the observed values of
\mathbf{Z}_{obs}
. A prediction for
\mathbf{Z}_{pred}
could be any function of
\mathbf{Z}_{obs}
, which we denote by
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{pred}=t(\mathbf{Z}_{obs})
. The mean square error (MSE) of
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{pred}
is given by
MSE(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{pred})=E((\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{pred}-\mathbf{Z}_{pred})^{2})
; so we can use
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{pred}=E(\mathbf{Z}_{pred}|\mathbf{Z}_{obs})
to find the predictor that minimizes
MSE(\mathbf{Z}_{pred})
.

For a Gaussian process, we write
\mathbf{Z}_{pred}=(Z_{pred}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\ldots,Z_{pred}(\mathbf{s}_{n}))
for
the unobserved values of the signal at the sampling locations
\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{n}
. We want to predict the signal value at an
arbitrary location; thus our target for prediction is
Z_{pred}(\mathbf{s})
. Given that
(\mathbf{Z}_{obs},\mathbf{Z}_{pred})
is also a multivariate Gaussian process, we can use the results in Subsection
3
to minimize the
MSE(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{pred})
, i.e., if
\mathbf{X}^{*}=(\mathbf{X}_{obs},\mathbf{X}_{pred})
is the
(n_{obs}+n_{pred})\times p
design matrix corresponding to
\mathbf{Z}^{*}=(\mathbf{Z}_{obs}^{\top},\mathbf{Z}_{pred}^{\top})
, then
\mathbf{Z}^{*}\sim N_{n_{obs}+n_{pred}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{*}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\right)
, where

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\begin{bmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{obs,obs}&\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{obs,pred}\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{pred,obs}&\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{pred,pred}\end{bmatrix}

and
\mathbf{Z}_{pred}|\mathbf{Z}_{obs}\sim N_{n_{pred}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{p},\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{p})
, where

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{p}=\mathbf{X}_{pred}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{obs,pred}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{obs,obs})^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}_{obs}-\mathbf{X}_{obs}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})

(10)

and

\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{p}=\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{pred,pred}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{pred,obs}(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{obs,obs})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{obs,pred}.

Then, the predictor that minimizes the
MSE
of prediction will be the conditional expectation in (
10
).

3.2.1
Prediction methods

Using the previous results, we will now describe the algorithm for each of the three prediction methods that are implemented in this paper.

(a)

Naive 1 and Naive 2 algorithms

We proceed as follows:

1.

Impute the censored observations using
LOD
(called Naive 1 algorithm), or
LOD/2
(called Naive 2 algorithm), depending on the type of censoring (left or right).

2.

Compute the least squares or likelihood estimates for the mean (linear trend in our case) and covariance structure using the imputed data.

3.

Evaluate the estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
from step 2 in expression
(\ref{mudep})
to obtain the predicted values.

(b)

Seminaive algorithm

Here, we follow the method in
Schelin and
Sjöstedt-de Luna 2014
. Let
C_{i}
denote the indicator variable, indicating the presence (
C_{i}=1
) or absence (
C_{i}=0
) of a censored observation. Then we proceed as follows:

1.

Set
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{0}=(\mathbf{Z}_{obs}^{T},\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{T})
where
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{0i}=0
if
C_{i}=1
.

2.

Obtain
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{0}=(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}},\hat{\sigma^{2}},\hat{\phi})
from
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{0}
by least squares.

3.

Set
k=0
. Let
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{-i}_{k}
denote the data vector
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k}
where unit
i
is removed.

4.

Find the predictor
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}(h,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{k}})
for all
i
such that
C_{i}=1
using expression (
10
) and
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{-i}_{k}
, where
h
denotes the distance between coordinates (isotropy).

5.

Set
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k+1}=(\mathbf{Z}_{obs}^{T},\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}^{T})
where
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}=(\max(0,\min(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}(h,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{k}}),LOD)):C_{i}=1)
.

6.

Update
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{k}
from the new data
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k+1}
.

7.

For iteration
k+1
, repeat steps 4-6 until the convergence criterion is satisfied for some constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
chosen by the user.

8.

Once convergence is attained, evaluate the estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
in expression
(\ref{mudep})
to obtain the predicted values.

Note that this algorithm only works for the left-censored case. The constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
are chosen such that the following three conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\hat{\sigma^{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k+1})-\hat{\sigma^{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k})}{\hat{\sigma^{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k})}\right|\leq c_{1},

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma^{2}}(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k+1})\leq c_{2}\hat{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{Z}_{obs})\mbox{\quad and}

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}{(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_{k+1}})>c_{3}\hat{\xi}{(\mathbf{Z}_{obs})}

(11)

where
\xi(X)
represents the skewness of
X
.

(c)

SAEM algorithm

Here, we proceed as follows:

1.

Obtain the estimates of the mean and covariance structure by SAEM procedure. For further details, see
Ordonez et al. 2018
.

2.

Impute the censored observations by the approximate first moments
\hat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(k)}
, obtained from the SAEM procedure.

3.

Evaluate the above estimates in expression (
10
) to obtain predicted values for the unobserved locations. In this case, we use
\mathbf{Z}_{obs^{*}}
to denote the observed data and estimate the censored observations via SAEM. This way, we can easily differentiate it from the fully observed response
\mathbf{Z}_{obs}
.

3.3
Application: Estimation and prediction tools

Now, we exemplify the estimation and prediction methods described previously and implemented in the
CensSpatial
package. We use the rainfall dataset from Parana state (Brazil) analyzed in
Diggle and
Ribeiro Jr 2002
. We carried out some performance comparison for the four prediction methods by using a cross-validation study with the first
100
observations for estimation and the remaining
43
observations for comparison of the predictive power of the estimated methods.

The rainfall dataset does not contain censored data, so left censoring was generated artificially for the first
100
observations, following
Schelin and
Sjöstedt-de Luna 2014
, i.e., we fixed the censoring level at
\alpha
, sorting the data (in ascending order) and setting the LOD to the 100
\alpha\%
percentile of the generated sample, i.e., the smallest
\alpha n
value of
Z(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\ldots,Z(\mathbf{s}_{n})
. This dataset, called
paranacens25
, is available in the package
CensSpatial
with 25
\%
censoring level. The following instructions call the data, then observations that will be used for estimation are stored in the object
dataest
and those that will be used for prediction are stored in the object
datapred
.

> data(paranacens25)
> dataest=paranacens25[1:100,]
> datapred=paranacens25[101:143,]

4
Local influence diagnostic utilities

In this section, we describe all measures used for local diagnostics. All calculations are based in the Hessian matrix of log-likelihood conditional expectation and the SAEM estimates obtained through the
SAEMSCL
routine. We used two simulated processes to illustrate how the package works to detect influential observations.

Following
Zhu and Lee 2001
, we compute the Hessian matrix
\tfrac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top}}
in order to obtain local diagnostic measures for a particular perturbation scheme as described in later subsections. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top},\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}{{}^{\top}})^{\top}
with
\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})^{\top}
where
\alpha_{1}=\sigma^{2}
,
\alpha_{2}=\phi
and
\alpha_{3}=\tau^{2}
. Then, the necessary expressions to compute the Hessian matrix
\ddot{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{X},

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\partial\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\alpha_{k}\partial\alpha_{l}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{l}}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}}\right)-2\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}\alpha_{l}}\mathbf{X}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\right].

4.1
Local influence

We derive the normal curvature of the local influence for some common perturbation schemes that could be present either in the model or the data. We will consider perturbations in response, scale matrix and explanatory variables.

Consider a perturbation vector
\omega
varying in an open region
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The conditional log-likelihood expectation of the complete data for the perturbed model will be denoted by
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})=E(\ell(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\mathbf{Z}_{c})|\mathbf{V},\mathbf{C},\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
which reaches its maximum at
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
. Assume there is an
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}
in
\Omega
such that
\ell(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}|\mathbf{Z}_{c})=\ell(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\mathbf{Z}_{c})
for all
\theta
. We use the
Q
-displacement function defined as:

f_{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=2[Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})-Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}))],

to obtain the influence graph
\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{\top},f_{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}))^{\top}
. The local behavior of
f_{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
can be summarized using the curvature
C_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}}}
at
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}
in the direction of some unit vector
\mathbf{d}
. This curvature is defined by:

C_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}}}=-2\mathbf{d}^{\top}\ddot{Q}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}\mathbf{d}\hskip 5.69046pt\quad\textup{and}\quad\hskip 5.69046pt-\ddot{Q}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}=\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}^{\top}-\ddot{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})^{-1}\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}},

where
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}=\frac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{\top}}
and
\ddot{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
is the Hessian matrix with elements as in expressions (
13
)-(13).

As described in
Cook 1986
, the quantities
C_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}}}
and
\ddot{Q}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}
are useful for detecting influential observations. Let the spectral (orthonormal) decomposition of
-2\ddot{Q}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}}
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\lambda_{r}>\lambda_{r+1}=\cdots=\lambda_{n}=0
eigenvalues and
\mathbf{a}_{i},
for
i=1,\cdots,n
, eigenvectors of this matrix.
Zhu and Lee 2001
proposed inspecting all eigenvectors corresponding to nonzero eigenvalues to capture the relevant information about influential observations.

Consider the aggregated contribution of each eigenvector with a corresponding nonzero eigenvalue as
\tilde{\lambda_{i}}=\lambda_{i}/(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r})
. Let
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{2}=(a_{11}^{2},\cdots,a_{in}^{2})^{\top}
and
M(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{2}
with
M(0)_{l}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{il}^{2}
for
l=1,\cdots,n
, the
l
-th component of
M(0)
. The assessment of influential observations can be performed through visual inspection of
M(0)_{l}
plotted against the index
l
. The
l
-th case may be considered influential if
M(0)_{l}
is larger than a benchmark value.
Zhu and Lee 2001
also considered the conformal normal curvature
B_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=C_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})/tr[-2\ddot{Q}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}]
, whose computation is quite simple and has the property that
0\leq B_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\leq 1
. They also showed that for a perturbation vector
\mathbf{d}_{l}
whose
l
-th entry is 1 and the remaining entries are 0,
M(0)_{l}=B_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}_{l}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, so we can obtain
M(0)_{l}
through
B_{f_{Q,\mathbf{d}_{l}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
.

In general, there is no rule to judge how influential a specific case in the data is. Some measures have been proposed to determine the benchmark value in the visual inspection of
M(0)
. Let
\bar{M}(0)
and
SM(0)
be the mean and standard error of the vector
M(0)
respectively.
Poon and
Poon 1999
proposed using
2\bar{M}(0)
as a benchmark value while
Zhu and Lee 2001
proposed
\bar{M}(0)+2SM(0)
, for taking into account the standard deviation of
M(0)
. In the
CensSpatial
package, we use the benchmark proposed by
Lee and Xu 2004
as
\bar{M}(0)+c^{*}SM(0)
, where
c^{*}
is a constant, which can vary depending of the specific application.

4.2
Perturbation schemes

We consider three types of perturbation schemes: perturbation in the response variable, which can indicate influence in their own predicted values, perturbation in the scale matrix
\Sigma
, which may reveal the individuals that are influential in the estimation of the scale parameter
\alpha
and finally perturbation in explanatory variables. For all these schemes, we are interested in computing the
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}}
matrix with elements:

\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}=\frac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{\top}}\hskip 2.84544pt\quad\textup{and}\quad\hskip 2.84544pt\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=(\Delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\top},\Delta_{\alpha_{2}}^{\top},\Delta_{\alpha_{3}}^{\top}),

where
\Delta_{\alpha_{k}}=\frac{\partial^{2}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\partial\alpha_{k}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{\top}}
, for
k=1,2,3
.

4.2.1
Response perturbation

A perturbation in response variable
\mathbf{V}
can be introduced by replacing it with
\mathbf{V}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\mathbf{V}+\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
, recalling the non-perturbed model
V_{i}=V_{0i}
for
C_{i}=0
and that
V_{i}
belongs to
[V_{1i},V_{2i}]
for
C_{i}=1
. In this way we can write the perturbed model as:

\displaystyle C_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&if&V_{1i}(\omega_{i})\leq Z_{i}(\omega_{i})\leq V_{2i}(\omega_{i})\,,\\
0&if&Z_{i}(\omega_{i})=V_{0i}(\omega_{i})\,,\end{array}\right.

where
\mathbf{V}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\mathbf{V}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
,
\mathbf{V}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\mathbf{V}_{2}+\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
and
\mathbf{Z}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\mathbf{Z}-\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
. To obtain the perturbed function
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
, we replace
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}
with
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\omega}=\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}_{\omega}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}_{\omega}}^{\top}=\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}-\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}+\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}^{\top}
in the non-perturbed
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
from Subsection 2.3. Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
be the vector that represents the case of no perturbation in response. Then, the matrix
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}
has the elements
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}=-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}
and
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=[\Delta_{\alpha_{ki}}],
where

\Delta_{\alpha_{ki}}=\frac{1}{2}\left((\mathbf{Z}_{(i)}+\mathbf{Z}_{(i)}^{\top})\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\right)+\mathbf{Z}_{(i)}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}},

with
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{(i)}
the matrix with the
i
-th row being
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}
and the remaining rows being zero. Expressions for
\tfrac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}
are defined in the Appendix.

4.2.2
Scale matrix perturbation

Now, we consider the scheme perturbation of the form
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\mathbf{D}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}
to study the effects of perturbation on the scale matrix. In this case
\mathbf{D}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
is an
n\times n
diagonal matrix with elements
\omega
. The non-perturbed model is obtained when
\omega_{i}=1
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
and therefore
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}
is a
(p+3)\times n
matrix with components
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{i}}=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{d}(i)[\mathbf{I_{n}}-\mathbf{P}]\mathbf{Z}
and
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=[\Delta_{\alpha_{ki}}]
, with

\Delta_{\alpha_{ki}}=-\mathrm{tr}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{d}(i)\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\right)\\
-\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{d}(i)\right)+\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}[\mathbf{P}^{\top}-2\mathbf{I}]\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{d}(i)\mathbf{P}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}\right],

for
k=1,2,3
, where
\mathbf{d}(i)
is an
n\times n
matrix with the
i
-th diagonal element equal to 1 and the remaining elements equal to zero.

4.2.3
Explanatory variable perturbation

We consider for this scheme a perturbation of the form
\mathbf{X}(\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})=\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{W}
and replace the perturbed
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$})
function as in the response perturbation, in this case,
\mathbf{W}=\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
. Under the non-perturbed model,
\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}=\mathbf{0}
and therefore
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\omega$}_{0}}
has elements
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{i}}=\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}[\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{2P^{\top}}]\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{W_{\textit{{i}}}^{(1)}}
and
\Delta_{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=[\Delta_{\alpha_{ki}}]
, with

\Delta_{\alpha_{ki}}=\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{\top}[\mathbf{I-P^{\top}}]\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\mathbf{W_{\textit{{i}}}^{(1)}}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}\widehat{\mathbf{Z}},

where
\mathbf{W_{\textit{{i}}}^{(1)}}
is an
n\times p
matrix with the
i
-th row equal to 1 and the remaining rows equal to zero.

to define the spatial correlation function to use. The code below illustrates how we generate the samples and the atypical points used in this paper.

> nobs=200; npred=100
> r1=sample(seq(1,30,length=400),n+n1); r2=sample(seq(1,30,length=400),n+n1)
> coords=cbind(r1,r2); cov.ini=c(2,0.1); beta=c(5,3,1)
> xtot<-cbind(1,runif((n+n1)),runif((n+n1),2,3)); xobs=xtot[1:n,]

> obj=rspacens(cov.pars=c(3,.3,0),beta=beta,x=xtot,coords=coords,cens=0.15,
+ n=(nobs+ npred),n1=npred,cov.model="matern" ,cens.type="left",kappa=0.3)

> y=obj$datare[,3]

> y[91]=y[91]+ 5*sd(y); y[126]=y[126]+ 5*sd(y); y[162]=y[162]+ 5*sd(y)

To assess the influence of outliers in the estimation process, we use the
localinfmeas
function. This uses an object of class
"SAEMSpatialCens"
, i.e., an object as a result of the SAEM estimation procedure to compute the value of the vector
M(0)
for each perturbation scheme and detect the influential points using the
Lee and Xu 2004
criteria. The benchmark used for the routine is
c^{*}=3
by default. On the other hand, the function
atypical
filters the results to get only the atypical points detected by the
localinfmeas
routine. The following instructions generate the output of the
localinfmeas
and
atypical
functions, considering a linear process with Matérn covariance structure and three perturbation schemes discussed previously.

> sest=SAEMSCL(cc,y,cens.type="left",trend="other",x=xobs,coords=coords,
+ M=15,perc=0.25,MaxIter=5,pc=0.2,cov.model=type,kappa=0.3,fix.nugget=T,
+ nugget=0,inits.sigmae=cov.ini[1], inits.phi=cov.ini[2],search=T,
+ lower=0.00001,upper=50)

> w = localinfmeas(sest, fix.nugget = TRUE, c = 3)
> atypical(w)$RP

obs m0
32 atypical obs 0.04478987
91 atypical obs 0.09086417
126 atypical obs 0.10935255
162 atypical obs 0.09978107

In these lines,
w
is a
localinfmeas
object. Then
atypical(w)
will be a list with three elements:
EP
,
SP
and
RP
, containing the atypical detected observations for each perturbation scheme. By default,
localinfmeas
also generates a diagnostic plot with for the three perturbation schemes.

Local influence diagnostic plots for spherical (first row) and Matérn (second row) processes under (a,d) response perturbation (b,e) scale matrix perturbation and (c,f) explanatory variable perturbation schemes

Figure
4
(provided by the
localinfmeas
function) shows the diagnostic plots for the two simulated datasets. It can be seen that the artificial outliers were detected, as expected. For the response perturbation, the procedure detected two atypical observations under the spherical covariance structure and one under the Matérn covariance structure. Thus, we can conclude that the proposed local influence measures work quite well to detect atypical observations in the context of censored spatial models.

5
Conclusions

This work introduces an
R
package for censored spatial data analysis that allows estimation, prediction and local influence diagnosis for censored Gaussian processes considering different linear trends and covariance structures. We discuss in detail all functions available in this package as well as the theoretical framework. Plots provided by the package constitute one of the most powerful tools for comparing models, assessing predictions and analyzing local influence. Applications and several examples can be found in the manual of the
CensSpatial
package, available in the CRAN repository. To the best of our knowledge, this R package is the first proposal, available to practitioners, to perform statistical analysis of censored spatial data.

Future extensions of this work include the use of scale mixtures of normal (SMN) distributions to accommodate heavy-tailed features
(
De Bastiani et al. 2014
)
. We can also extend the proposal of
Diggle
et al. 1998
by including censoring in generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs).
