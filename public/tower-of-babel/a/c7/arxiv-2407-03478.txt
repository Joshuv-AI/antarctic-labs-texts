Periodic gravity-capillary roll wave solutions to the inclined viscous shallow water equations in two dimensions Thanks: N. Stevenson was supported by an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship
Noah Stevenson

Abstract
. We study periodic, two-dimensional, gravity-capillary traveling wave solutions to a viscous shallow water system posed on an inclined plane. While thinking of the Reynolds and Bond numbers as fixed and finite, we vary the speed of the traveling frame and the degree of the incline and identify a set of the latter two parameters that classifies from which combinations nontrivial and small amplitude solution curves originate. Our principal technical tools are a combination of the implicit function theorem and a local multiparameter bifurcation theorem. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this paper constitutes the first construction and mathematical study of properly two dimensional examples of viscous roll waves.

1.
Introduction

The phenomenon of roll waves are a frequently observed manifestation of the instability of a shallow layer of water driven down an incline and are caused by the interaction of the gravitational force and the friction between the fluid and the bottom. While the mathematics and physics literature has been exploring these features for over a century now, the vast majority of rigorous nonlinear results on the existence, stability, and instability of roll waves have been exclusively established for one dimensional models such as the Saint-Venant equations with empirical modifications. The purpose of this work is to explore some of the two dimensional nature of roll waves. We carry out the first construction of families of properly two dimensional small amplitude gravity-capillary roll wave solutions to a damped viscous shallow water model representing a thin film of tilted incompressible fluid. Our solution families are also examples of traveling wave solutions to a dissipative fluid system with the notable novel feature of existing purely in the presence of a gravitational force (see the end of Section
1.2
for further discussion).

1.1.
The tilted shallow water equations, traveling formulation

The fluid model under consideration in this work is the damped shallow water equations, built with the effects of viscosity, gravity, and capillarity, posed on a two-dimensional inclined plane. These are the system:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\overline{\upeta}(\partial_{t}\overline{\upupsilon}+\overline{\upupsilon}\cdot\nabla\overline{\upupsilon})+\alpha\overline{\upupsilon}-\upmu\nabla\cdot(\overline{\upeta}\mathbb{S}\overline{\upupsilon})+\overline{\upeta}\nabla(g\overline{\upeta}-\upsigma\Delta\overline{\upeta})-\upkappa e_{1}\overline{\upeta}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\overline{\upeta}+\nabla\cdot(\overline{\upeta}\overline{\upupsilon})=0.\end{cases}

Here the unknowns are the velocity vector field
\overline{\upupsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the free surface height
\overline{\upeta}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The matrix field
\mathbb{S}\overline{\upupsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, which is the shallow water version of the viscous stress tensor, is given by the formula

(1.2)

\mathbb{S}\overline{\upupsilon}=\nabla\overline{\upupsilon}+\nabla\overline{\upupsilon}^{\mathrm{t}}+2(\nabla\cdot\overline{\upupsilon})I_{2\times 2}.

The physical parameters of system (
1.1
) are inverse slip coefficient
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the surface tension coefficient
\upsigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the viscosity
\upmu\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and the gravitational acceleration’s components
g\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\upkappa\in\mathbb{R}
;
g
indicates the strength of the portion of gravity which is ‘normal’ to the fluid while
\upkappa e_{1}
is the component of gravity acting parallel to the fluid (which we choose to be in the
e_{1}
direction).

The shallow water model (
1.1
), which is also known as the Saint-Venant equations when
\alpha=\upmu=\upsigma=\upkappa=0
, is an important system of equations which approximates the incompressible free boundary Navier-Stokes in scenarios where the fluid depth is much smaller than its characteristic horizontal scale. Consequently, the model is both physically and practically relevant. One should therefore think of solutions to (
1.1
) as describing flow of a thin film of incompressible liquid down an inclined plane.

A derivation of the system (
1.1
) from the free boundary Navier-Stokes system appended with gravitational forcing of the form
-ge_{3}+\upkappa e_{1}
and Navier slip boundary conditions on the rigid bottom proceeds through an asymptotic expansion and vertical averaging procedure; for the full details in the case
\upkappa=0
we refer, for instance, to the surveys of Bresch
[
7
]
or Mascia
[
29
]
and, in the case of general applied forcing, to Appendix B.1 in Stevenson and Tice
[
38
]
.

System (
1.1
) evidently has a number of structural resemblances to the barotropic compressible Navier-Stokes system, with
\overline{\upupsilon}
and
\overline{\upeta}
analogous to the fluid velocity and density, respectively. The term
\overline{\upeta}\nabla(g\overline{\upeta})=\frac{g}{2}\nabla(\overline{\upeta}^{2})
corresponds to a quadratic pressure law while
\upmu\overline{\upeta}
corresponds to viscosity coefficients which depend linearly on the density. Continuing with this analogy, we shall refer to the first equation in (
1.1
) as the momentum equation and the second one as the continuity equation. This latter equation dictates how the free surface is deformed and transported by the velocity field.

The advective derivative
\overline{\upeta}(\partial_{t}\overline{\upupsilon}+\overline{\upupsilon}\cdot\nabla\overline{\upupsilon})
in the momentum equation of (
1.1
) is balanced by several notable terms. First, we have the shallow water limit’s manifestation of the Navier slip boundary condition, which is the zeroth order frictional damping term
\alpha\overline{\upupsilon}
. We note that there are other options for a friction term that appear in the literature; this effect appears to be commonly modeled with the choice of the Chézy drag term, which would have the form
\widetilde{\alpha}|\overline{\upupsilon}|\overline{\upupsilon}
. This is an empirically derived force which is meant to capture so-called turbulent friction. We disregard this option of frictional damping and adopt the linear choice
\alpha\overline{\upupsilon}
, as it is consistent with the aforementioned mathematical derivation of our shallow water model from the Navier-Stokes equations.

Next, the momentum equation inherits a viscous damping term,
-\upmu\nabla\cdot(\overline{\upeta}\mathbb{S}\overline{\upupsilon})
, which captures the dissipative effect of intra-fluid friction. The term
\overline{\upeta}\nabla(g\overline{\upeta}-\upsigma\Delta\overline{\upeta})
encodes how changes in the geometry of the free surface influence the velocity. We note that this term can be expressed as the divergence of a gravity-capillary stress tensor, namely
\overline{\upeta}\nabla(g\overline{\upeta}-\sigma\Delta\overline{\upeta})=\nabla\cdot\mathbb{G}_{g,\upsigma}(\overline{\upeta})
, where

(1.3)

\mathbb{G}_{g,\upsigma}(\overline{\upeta})=\frac{g}{2}\overline{\upeta}^{2}I_{2\times 2}-\frac{\upsigma}{2}(\Delta(\overline{\upeta}^{2})-|\nabla\overline{\upeta}|^{2})I_{2\times 2}+\upsigma\nabla\overline{\upeta}\otimes\nabla\overline{\upeta}.

Finally, the term
-\upkappa e_{1}\overline{\eta}
encodes that the shallow water system is posed on an inclined plane and the size of the coefficient
\upkappa
dictates the steepness level, with
\upkappa=0
corresponding to a flat plane.

We are interested in the question of whether or not system (
1.1
) admits certain nontrivial solutions called roll waves - which are stationary in a frame traveling at a constant velocity down the incline. Let us begin our search for these by first identifying and reformulating perturbatively around the equilibrium solutions. One notes that for any choice of equilibrium height
H\in\mathbb{R}
the system (
1.1
) admits a constant solution of the form
\overline{\upeta}=H
and
\overline{\upupsilon}=(\kappa H/\alpha)e_{1}
. Let us fix some choice
H\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, as the nonpositive choices are not physically meaningful.

We shall next perform a rescaling and perturbative reformulation in which
(\alpha,g,H)\mapsto(1,1,1)
. We set
\mathrm{L}=H\sqrt{gH}/\alpha
and
\mathrm{T}=H/\alpha
to be some characteristic length and time of the system. Using these we then define the following non-dimensional parameters: inverse Reynolds number
\mu=\upmu T/L^{2}
, inverse Bond number
\sigma=\upsigma/gL^{2}
, and incline steepness (or inverse Froude number)
\kappa=\frac{\upkappa HT}{\alpha L}
. We then define the non-dimensional velocity
\upupsilon:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and free surface
\upeta:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
via

(1.4)

\overline{\upupsilon}(t,x)=\frac{\mathrm{L}}{\mathrm{T}}(\kappa e_{1}+\upupsilon(t/T,x/L)),\quad\overline{\upeta}(t,x)=H(1+\upeta(t/T,x/L)).

In terms of
\upupsilon
and
\upeta
, system (
1.1
) takes the non-dimensional form

(1.5)

\begin{cases}(1+\upeta)(\partial_{t}\upupsilon+(\upupsilon+\kappa e_{1})\cdot\nabla\upupsilon)+\upupsilon-\mu\nabla\cdot((1+\upeta)\mathbb{S}\upupsilon)+(1+\upeta)\nabla(1-\sigma\Delta)\upeta-\kappa e_{1}\upeta=0,\\
\partial_{t}\upeta+\kappa\partial_{1}\upeta+\nabla\cdot((1+\upeta)\upupsilon)=0.\end{cases}

Note that
(\upupsilon,\upeta)=(0,0)
is a solution to these equations representing the previously mentioned trivial equilibrium. We are interested in nontrivial, traveling perturbations of this solution. We are thus lead to reformulate the equations yet again, but this time in a frame that is traveling in the
e_{1}
direction (which is parallel to the tilt direction) at a signed non-dimensional speed
\overline{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
. The following traveling ansatz is made: there are
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with the property that

(1.6)

\upupsilon(t,x)=u(x-t\overline{\gamma}e_{1}),\quad\upeta(t,x)=\eta(x-t\overline{\gamma}e_{1}).

Let us define the parameter
\gamma=\overline{\gamma}-\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
to represent the relative non-dimensional wave speed. From (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) we then derive the equations satisfied by
u
,
\eta
,
\gamma
, and
\kappa
:

(1.7)

\begin{cases}(1+\eta)(u-\gamma e_{1})\cdot\nabla u+u-\mu\nabla\cdot((1+\eta)\mathbb{S}u)+(1+\eta)\nabla(1-\sigma\Delta)\eta-\kappa e_{1}\eta=0,\\
-\gamma\partial_{1}\eta+\nabla\cdot((1+\eta)u)=0.\end{cases}

This is the form of the tilted shallow water system which we shall analyze in this work. We view the above system as a sort of nonlinear ‘eigenvalue’ problem: Thinking of
\mu,\sigma>0
as being fixed, we wish to find
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
(u,\eta)\neq 0
such that (
1.7
) is satisfied.

Figure 1.
Plotted above are the graphs (each having the same scale) of the free surface component within a selection of different roll wave solutions to system (
1.7
) (with
\mu=3/20
,
\sigma=2

1
. This result applied in this situation is essentially telling us that if we fix (to leading order in the amplitude of the solution) the incline strength
\kappa=10
, then we can expect nontrivial traveling wave solutions to exist for relative wave speeds
\gamma
which are to leading order belonging to the range

(\gamma,\kappa)
though the function (

). Notice that the slowest waves are independent of the direction perpendicular to propagation while the faster waves have very long wavelength oscillations in the traveling direction.

1.3.
Main results and discussion

The statements of this paper’s main results require the introduction of some notation and helper functions. We shall work in classes of spatially periodic functions, viewing them as defined on flat 2-tori of various side lengths. If
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
then the 1-torus of size
\ell
is the set
\ell\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/(\ell\mathbb{Z})
. Now if
L=(L_{1},L_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
, then the
2
-torus of side lengths
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
is the set
\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}=(L_{1}\mathbb{T})\times(L_{2}\mathbb{T})
.

The function spaces for our velocity and free surface functions are built from the following subspaces of Sobolev spaces which satisfy certain symmetry conditions. Firstly, we denote the vanishing average subspace via

(1.8)

{H^{s}_{\bullet}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})=\{{f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\;:\;\mathscr{F}[f](0,0)=0}\}.

Next we denote the subspaces of functions who are even or odd in the second variable:

(1.9)

H^{s}_{\pm}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})=\{{f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\;:\;f(x_{1},x_{2})=\pm f(x_{1},-x_{2}),\;\forall\;x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}}\}.

The spaces of functions for the velocity and free surface are denoted by

(1.10)

\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{velo}}_{L}=H^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\times H^{2}_{-}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}),\quad\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}=(H^{3}_{\bullet}\cap H^{3}_{+})(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}),\quad\mathbf{X}_{L}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{velo}}_{L}\times\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}

In other words we are restricting our attention to velocity fields which are second-argument even in the first component and second-argument odd in the second component along with free surface functions that are average zero and second argument even.

The following (nonempty) set of non-dimensional relative wave speed and incline strength tuples is related to the classification of when the linearized problem associated with (
1.7
) has a kernel in periodic function spaces for some choice of period lengths:

(1.11)

\mathfrak{E}=\Big\{{(\gamma,\kappa)\in(1,\infty)^{2}\;:\;0<\kappa-\gamma\leqslant\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1)}\Big\}.

On the set
\mathfrak{E}
we shall define numerous auxiliary functions:
\rho:\mathfrak{E}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\chi:\mathfrak{E}\to(0,1]
, and
\mathfrak{l}:\mathfrak{E}\to(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
:

(1.12)

\rho(\gamma,\kappa)=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa/\gamma-1}{16\pi^{2}\mu}},\quad\chi(\gamma,\kappa)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma}{4\mu}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}-1\Big)},

and

(1.13)

\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)=\frac{1}{\rho(\gamma,\kappa)}\begin{cases}(1,1)&\text{if }\kappa-\gamma=\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1),\\
(1/\chi(\gamma,\kappa),1/\sqrt{1-\chi(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}})&\text{if }\kappa-\gamma<\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1).\end{cases}

Now we are ready to introduce our main results, whose proofs can be found in Section
3.3
. We reemphasize that the inverse Reynolds number
\mu>0
and the inverse Bond number
\sigma>0
are fixed throughout and all open sets and constants implicitly depend on these values.

First we establish the existence of small roll wave solutions to the inclined shallow water equations with viscosity and surface tension for certain values of wave speed and incline. In other words, we show that nearby certain trivial solutions to (
1.7
) there exists a family of small nontrivial solutions to the equations provided that we vary the speed and tilt parameters. These roll wave families are semi-explicitly parametrized by the kernel of the linearized problem at the trivial solution and actually describe the totality of nearby small roll waves.

Theorem 1

(Existence and multiplicity of small amplitude roll waves)
.

Assume that
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
and let
L=\mathfrak{l}(|\gamma_{\star}|,|\kappa_{\star}|)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
where
\mathfrak{l}
is defined in (
1.13
). Then there exists a direct sum decomposition
\mathbf{X}_{L}=E\oplus Z
with
\mathrm{dim}E=2
, positive numbers
\varepsilon,\overline{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and an open set
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)\in\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{X}_{L}
such that upon setting
K=E\cap\partial B_{\mathbf{X}_{L}}(0,1)
the following hold.

(1)

There exists smooth functions
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\eta}):[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K\to(B_{\mathbf{X}_{L}}(0,\overline{r})\cap Z)
and
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\kappa}):[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K\to B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,\overline{r})
such that for all
(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
we have that upon defining

(1.14)

\begin{cases}(u,\eta)=\alpha(\upupsilon,\upeta)+\alpha^{2}(\widetilde{u}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta),\widetilde{\eta}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)),\\
(\gamma,\kappa)=(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})+\alpha(\widetilde{\gamma}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta),\widetilde{\kappa}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)),\end{cases}

the velocity and free surface tuple
(u,\eta)
is a solution to system (
1.7
) with speed and tilt parameters
(\gamma,\kappa)
; moreover
\eta\neq 0
whenever
\alpha\neq 0
.

(2)

The only other local solutions are the trivial ones, more precisely:

(1.15)

\{{(\overline{\gamma},\overline{\kappa},\overline{u},\overline{\eta})\in\mathcal{O}\;:\;(\overline{\gamma},\overline{\kappa},\overline{u},\overline{\eta})\text{ is a solution to~\eqref{traveling form of the equations}}}\}\\
=[{\{{(\gamma,\kappa,0,0)\;:\;(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\}\cup\{{(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)\;:\;\text{defined via }~\eqref{nontrivial solutions}}\}}]\cap\mathcal{O}.

The next result is a simple corollary of Theorem

Theorem 2

(On the shape of roll waves)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1
consider the curve of solutions
\{{(\gamma_{\alpha},\kappa_{\alpha},u_{\alpha},\eta_{\alpha})}\}_{\alpha\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
produced by the maps in (
1.14
) by varying
\alpha\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
for some fixed
(\upupsilon,\upeta)\in K
. There exists constants
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
(not both zero) and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

(1)

\upeta
has one of the formulae:

(a)

if
0<|\kappa_{\star}|-|\gamma_{\star}|=\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}|\gamma_{\star}|(|\gamma_{\star}|^{2}-1)
, then

(1.16)

\upeta(x_{1},x_{2})=A\cos(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})+B\sin(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1}),

(b)

if
0<|\kappa_{\star}|-|\gamma_{\star}|<\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}|\gamma_{\star}|(|\gamma_{\star}|^{2}-1)

(1.17)

\upeta(x_{1},x_{2})=A\cos(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\cos(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2})+B\sin(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\cos(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2});

(2)

\upupsilon=(\upupsilon_{1},\upupsilon_{2})
is determined via
\upeta
through the formulae

(1.18)

\upupsilon_{1}=-\kappa_{\star}(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{2}^{2}\upeta-\gamma_{\star}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{2}\upeta

and

(1.19)

\upupsilon_{2}=\kappa_{\star}(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{2}\upeta-\gamma_{\star}\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{2}\upeta,

where
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
are the Riesz transforms in the
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
directions, respectively;

(3)

and we have for all for all
\alpha\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
:

(1.20)

\lVert\eta_{\alpha}-\alpha\upeta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert u_{\alpha}-\alpha\upupsilon\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant c\alpha^{2}.

In our final result we consider the speed and tilt parameters lying in the complement of the region considered in Theorem

Theorem 3

(Nonexistence of roll waves)
.

Suppose that
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E}))
and let
L\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
be arbitrary. There exists an
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, depending on
\mu
and
\sigma
in addition to the aforementioned parameters, such that if

(1.21)

(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\text{ satisfies }|\gamma-\gamma_{\star}|+|\kappa-\kappa_{\star}|+\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}+\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}\leqslant\varepsilon

and is a solution to system (
1.7
), then
u=0
and
\eta=0
.

Now that the main results of this paper have been presented, we shall discuss a few remarks. A high level summary of our results is as follows. For Theorems
1
and
2
, we work in a class of functions obeying the following symmetry conditions in the second spatial variable: the free surface and the first component of the velocity are even while the second component of the velocity is odd; on the other hand, for Theorem
3
no parity assumptions are necessary. We classify for which initializations
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)
there exists or does not exist period lengths
(L_{1},L_{2})
for the spatial variables and arbitrarily close periodic and nontrivial tuples
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)+\alpha(\widetilde{\gamma}_{\alpha},\widetilde{\kappa}_{\alpha},\overline{u}_{\alpha},\overline{\eta}_{\alpha})
solving system (
1.7
) for a sequence
\alpha\to 0
.

It is proved that if
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})
belong to the region
\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
(see (
1.11
) and Figure
2
), then there exists such curves of nontrivial solutions with terminus
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)
, if one selects the period lengths
(L_{1},L_{2})
to depend on the initial wave speed and tilt through the function (
1.13
). Moreover, the totality of small amplitude solutions to (
1.7
) nearby the trivial one
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)
, within the class obeying the aforementioned second variable symmetry assumptions and prescribed periodicity, is described. This is the content of Theorem
1
.

The set of solutions
(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)
generated with terminus
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}
are in a simple reflective correspondence with the set of solutions
(\hat{\gamma},\hat{\kappa},\hat{u},\hat{\eta})
generated with the negative terminus
(-\gamma_{\star},-\kappa_{\star})\in-\mathfrak{E}
, specifically the mapping
(-\hat{\gamma},-\hat{\kappa},R\hat{u}\circ R,\hat{\eta}\circ R)=(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)
, with
R(y_{1},y_{2})=(-y_{1},y_{2})
being the reflection operator, gives a bijective correspondence between these two sets of solutions (see equations (
3.28
) and (
3.29
) for more details.). Note that appeals to one’s physical intuition as one expects the collection of leftward traveling roll waves to be in symmetric correspondence with the collection of rightward traveling ones.

One could equivalently formulate the set
\mathfrak{E}
of definition (
1.11
) in terms of the minimal traveling speed for the linearized problem at a fixed tilt strength. Specifically, we can let
\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\in(1,\kappa)
be the unique number satisfying the equation
(\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa))^{2}=1+\frac{\sigma}{4\mu}\big(\frac{\kappa}{\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)}-1\big)
and deduce that

(1.22)

\mathfrak{E}=\{{(\gamma,\kappa)\in(1,\infty)^{2}\;:\;\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(\kappa)\leqslant\gamma<\kappa}\}.

We now see that it is suggested by Theorem
2
that the ‘slowest’ wave families - i.e. the one’s generated with a terminus
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
satisfying
|\gamma_{\star}|=\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(|\kappa_{\star}|)
, which are covered by the first case (i.e. (
1.16
)) in the theorem - are one dimensional in the sense that they do not depend on the second variable. This is indeed the case since the kernel of the linearized problem in this case consists of functions of just the first variable; so a minor modification of the proof of Theorem 1 will show that the families of solutions generated in this case are one dimensional functions. We do not pursue the full justification of this fact, however, as our primary interest is in the generation of properly two dimensional roll waves. Theorem
2
also tells us, specifically in the second conclusion (i.e. (
1.17
)), that all families of roll waves generated by a terminus
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
with an initial wave speed exceeding the minimal value, i.e.
|\gamma_{\star}|>\gamma_{\mathrm{min}}(|\kappa_{\star}|)
, are necessarily properly two dimensional.

On the other hand, one observes that each component of
\mathfrak{l}(|\gamma_{\star}|,|\kappa_{\star}|)
(the period lengths function from (
1.13
)) tends to
\infty
as
|\gamma_{\star}|\to|\kappa_{\star}|
. So the ‘faster’ roll waves produced by Theorem
1
are extremely slowly varying in space.

We also remark that while the roll wave solutions produced by Theorem
1
live initially in the regularity classes
(u,\eta)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
, a straightforward a posteriori regularity promotion argument (omitted here for brevity) will show that these solutions are, in fact, smooth.

Theorem
3
considers what happens in the opposite case of when
(\gamma,\kappa)
do not belong to the region
\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
. Here it is simply established that the trivial solution is locally unique (no matter the choice of the spatial period lengths) and so there does not exist arbitrarily small amplitude roll waves in our functional framework for these combinations of wave speed and tilt. In particular, this rules out two type of solutions: there does not exist arbitrarily small roll waves for small tilt (
|\kappa|<1
) and there does not exist arbitrarily small roll waves which ‘travel uphill’. Indeed, a positive
\kappa
indicates tilting down to the right while negative
\kappa
corresponds to tilting down to the left. Recall that
\overline{\gamma}=\gamma+\kappa
, defined in (
1.6
), is the dimensionless speed of the traveling frame, with
\overline{\gamma}>0
indicating a rightward traveling wave and
\overline{\gamma}<0
indicating a leftward traveling wave. So if
\overline{\gamma}
and
\kappa
have opposite signs (which happens in either traveling uphill case), then
(\gamma,\kappa)
necessarily belongs to the complement of
\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
and hence there are not arbitrarily small roll waves in this case.

This construction of the first properly two dimensional roll wave solutions to (
1.7
) opens the door to several lines of further inquiry which are delayed for future work. For instance, one can ask: For which values of tilt strength
\kappa
is the equilibrium solution stable or unstable? Can the curves of small solutions be extended to large amplitudes? Which other fluid models admit similar families of nontrivial solutions?

To close this subsection, we shall briefly discuss the proof strategy for our main theorems and outline the paper. Theorem
3
is established via a simple inverse function theorem argument: the invertibility of the linearization is proved via explicitly inverting the symbol of the linearized PDE, which is possible for speed and tilt parameters in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E}))
. Theorem
2
is actually a straightforward consequence of Theorem
1
and its proof.

Theorem
1
is proved via a local bifurcation theory argument. Upon study of the linearized operator associated with (
1.7
), which we call
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot):\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
in (
2.1
), one finds that for
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
and
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)
(see (
1.13
)) the kernel is a two dimensional subspace in
\mathbf{X}_{L}
. On the other hand
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
is Fredholm of index zero and hence has a closed range in
\mathbf{Y}_{L}
of codimension 2. It is therefore not clear how our functional framework would support a classical ‘simple eigenvalue’-type bifurcation argument as in Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
11
]
. Instead we look to a multiparameter bifurcation style argument to make up for the codimension 2 range of the linearization.

The precise abstract bifurcation tool utilized in this work, which will not be a surprise to experts in the area, is recorded with proof, for clarity and convenience, in Section
3.1
. A straightforward synthesis of the ideas in Theorem I.19.6 in Kielhöfer
[
21
]
and Lemma 1.12 in Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
11
]
yields a multiparameter bifurcation theorem which is capable of classifying all small solutions.

In Section
2
we aim to verify the ‘linear hypotheses’ of the bifurcation theorem. Section
2.1
computes the kernel of
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
in terms of the kernel of a related scalar partial differential operator. This latter operator can be readily analyzed with Fourier analysis and it is found that its kernel is exactly the subspace of functions frequency supported on a special set of modes. In Section
2.2
we then turn our attention to the range of the operator
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
. We show that the variation of
P
in the speed and tilt parameters acting on nontrivial members of the kernel makes up the missing two dimensions of the range; in other words a transversality condition is satisfied. This leads to the satisfaction of the remaining linear hypotheses of the bifurcation tool, Theorem
3.1
. Section
2.3
is a synthesis of the linear analysis.

Section
3
first develops the abstract bifurcation tool, Theorem
3.1
, with the content of Section
3.1
. Next, in Section
3.2
, we perform some simple smoothness verification for the shallow water system’s nonlinearities, thereby checking the nonlinear bifurcation hypotheses. Finally, in Section
3.3
, we combine all of the previous material into proofs of the main results, Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
.

1.4.
Conventions of notation and function spaces

\mathbb{N}
is the set
\{{0,1,2,\dots}\}

\mathbb{Z}=\{{\dots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\dots}\}
. The notation
A\lesssim B
means that there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

A\leqslant CB
. We also express that two quantities
A
and
B
are equivalent, written
A\asymp B
, if
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
. We shall use the bracket notation:
\langle\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
:

(1.23)

\langle x\rangle=\sqrt{1+|x_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|x_{k}|^{2}},\quad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}.

The Fourier transform of a function
f:\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
is denoted by
\mathscr{F}[f]:(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1})\times(\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
and has the formula

(1.24)

\mathscr{F}[f](\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}L_{2}}}\int_{0}^{L_{1}}\int_{0}^{L_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{1}\xi_{1}}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{2}\xi_{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x_{1}\;\mathrm{d}x_{2}

while the corresponding inverse Fourier transform of a sequence
\varphi:(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1})\times(\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
is the function
\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\varphi]:\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}\to\mathbb{C}^{k}
and is given via

(1.25)

\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\varphi](x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}L_{2}}}\sum_{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2}}\varphi(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{1}\xi_{1}}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{2}\xi_{2}}.

With these definitions the Fourier reconstruction formula

(1.26)

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}L_{2}}}\sum_{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2}}\mathscr{F}[f](\xi_{1},\xi_{2})e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{1}\xi_{1}}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{2}\xi_{2}}

holds.

The vector of Riesz transforms, denoted by
\mathcal{R}
, is the Fourier multiplication operator with the symbol vanishing at the origin and satisfying
\xi\mapsto\mathrm{i}|\xi|^{-1}\xi
for
\xi\neq 0
. In other words
\mathcal{R}=|\nabla|^{-1}\nabla
. The Leray projection operator onto divergence free vector fields, denoted

, is the Fourier multiplication operator with the symbol equal to the identity at the origin and obeying
\xi\mapsto I-\frac{\xi\otimes\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}
for
\xi\neq 0
. Note that
\mathbb{P}=I+\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R}
.

For

we let

denote the standard

-based Sobolev space of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued functions with
s
(weak) derivatives in
L^{2}
. When

we shall denote
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R})=H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
. As a norm on this space we take

(1.27)

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2}}\langle\xi\rangle^{2s}|\mathscr{F}[f](\xi)|^{2}.

The notation for our domain spaces is introduced in the beginning of Section
1.3
. The notation for the codomain spaces is similar. We pose the momentum and continuity equations of (
1.7

\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{mome}}_{L}=H^{0}_{+}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\times H^{0}_{-}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}),\quad\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{cont}}_{L}=(H^{1}_{\bullet}\cap H^{1}_{+})(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}),\quad\mathbf{Y}_{L}=\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{mome}}_{L}\times\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{cont}}_{L}.

Finally we shall introduce notation for larger container spaces for the domain

and the codomain
\mathbf{Y}_{L}
which do not enforce the second argument parity assumptions. Let

(1.29)

\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}=H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}),\quad\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}=H^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H^{1}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}).

where we recall that the function spaces

and

2.1.
Analysis of the kernel

Here we explore the kernels of the operators
P
from (
2.1

(\gamma,\kappa)
and
L
. Our first task is to reduce the computation of the kernel of

to that of a related, but scalar, integro-differential operator
Q
, which we define next. By the map
Q:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\to H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
we shall mean

(2.2)

Q(\gamma,\kappa,\eta)=\Delta^{-2}[{((\gamma-\kappa)-4\mu\gamma\Delta)\partial_{1}\eta+(\Delta-\gamma^{2}\partial_{1}^{2}-\sigma\Delta^{2})\eta}],\quad(\gamma,\kappa,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}),

One can easily check that the operators
Q
are also parity preserving, as they map
Q:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}\to\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}
, with
\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}

In this subsection and the next, unless otherwise stated, we are viewing

; e.g. when talking about linear algebraic constructions like the kernel and range the context is for these restricted operators which enforce the parity assumptions.

Proposition 2.1

(Correspondence of kernels)
.

For
L\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
and
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
the restriction of the linear map
R_{\gamma,\kappa}:\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}\to\mathbf{X}_{L}
given via

(2.3)

R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)^{-1}(I+\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R})(\eta e_{1})-\gamma\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R}(\eta e_{1})\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}

to
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}
takes values in
\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}_{L}
and gives an isomorphism
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)\to\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
.

Proof.

The mapping
R_{\gamma,\kappa}
of (
2.3
) is evidently continuous and an injection, which means we need only to check that its restriction to
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
has image
\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
.

Suppose first that
(u,\eta)\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}_{L}
. The equations in the first and second components of
P
in (
2.1
) are then zero. We decompose the vector field
u=v+w
,
v=(I-\mathbb{P})u
and
w=\mathbb{P}u
into its potential and solenoidal parts. The second equation in
P
reveals to us that
\gamma\partial_{1}\eta=\nabla\cdot u
and hence
v=\gamma(I-\mathbb{P})(\eta e_{1})=-\gamma\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R}(\eta e_{1})
.

Upon returning to the first component of
P
and applying
\mathbb{P}
, we derive that
(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)w=\kappa\mathbb{P}(\eta e_{1})
and hence
w=\kappa(1-\gamma\partial_{1}\Delta)^{-1}(I+\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R})(\eta e_{1})
. After recalling that
u=v+w
we see that at this point we have established that
R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta=(u,\eta)
.

The goal now is to establish that
\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}
. We apply the divergence to the first component equation of
P
and substitute that
\nabla\cdot u=\gamma\partial_{1}\eta
to derive:

(2.4)

((\gamma-\kappa)-4\gamma\mu\Delta)\partial_{1}\eta+(\Delta-\gamma^{2}\partial_{1}^{2}-\sigma\Delta^{2})\eta=0.

Since
\eta
has vanishing average, we can apply
\Delta^{-2}
to (
2.4
) and obtain that
\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
.

To complete the proof, we shall argue now that if
\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
, then
(u,\eta)=R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
. From the definition of
R_{\gamma,\kappa}
, it is clear that
\nabla\cdot u=\gamma\partial_{1}\eta
so that the second component of
P(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)
vanishes. In order to show that the first component of
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
also vanishes, we can decompose it into potential and solenoidal parts with the Leray projector. As we calculated in the previous step, the solenoidal part is the expression:
(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)\mathbb{P}u-\kappa\mathbb{P}(\eta e_{1})
. The definition
(u,\eta)=R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta
ensures that this vanishes. The potential part is the expression:
\Delta^{-1}\nabla[{((\gamma-\kappa)-4\mu\gamma\Delta)\partial_{1}\eta+(\Delta-\gamma^{2}\partial_{1}^{2}-\sigma\Delta^{2})\eta}]=\Delta\nabla Q(\gamma,\kappa,\eta)
which also vanishes.
∎

Our next goal is to compute the kernel of the operator
Q
. Thanks to the previous result, once this is complete, we obtain the kernel of
P
via application of the map
R_{\gamma,\kappa}
. In the next result we recall the notation introduced in equations (
1.11
), (
1.12
), and (
1.13

(2.5)

V(\gamma,\kappa)=\{{\rho(\gamma,\kappa)(\sigma_{1}\chi(\gamma,\kappa),\sigma_{2}\sqrt{1-\chi(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}})\;:\;\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{{-1,1}\}}\}.

Notice that
V(\gamma,\kappa)
is a subset of the circle of radius
\rho(\gamma,\kappa)
that has 2 points in the case that

and has 4 points in the opposite case of
\kappa-\gamma<\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1)

has the property that
V(\gamma,\kappa)\subset(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus\{{0}\}
with

Proposition 2.2

(Computation of the reduced kernel)
.

Suppose that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
and that

(1)

A function

(2)

The subspace
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}
is two dimensional and is spanned by the smooth functions
\upvarphi_{+}
and

(a)

In the case that

(2.6)

\upvarphi_{\iota}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L_{1}L_{2}}}\begin{cases}\cos(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})&\text{if }\iota=+,\\

(b)

In the case that
0<\kappa-\gamma<\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1)

(2.7)

\upvarphi_{\iota}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{4}{L_{1}L_{2}}}\begin{cases}\cos(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\cos(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2})&\text{if }\iota=+,\\

(2.8)

(2.9)

[{2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi_{1}((\gamma-\kappa)+16\pi^{2}\mu\gamma|\xi|^{2})-4\pi^{2}(|\xi|^{2}-\gamma^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+4\pi^{2}\sigma|\xi|^{4})}]\mathscr{F}[\eta](\xi)=0.

It is then immediate that if
\mathscr{F}[\eta]
is supported in the set
\widetilde{V}(\gamma,\kappa)

\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)

\xi\in\mathrm{supp}\mathscr{F}[\eta]\;\Rightarrow\;\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}(1-\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}+16\pi^{2}\mu|\xi|^{2})\\

(2.11)

\char 12\relax{\xi}^{2}=\frac{\kappa/\gamma-1}{16\pi^{2}\mu}=\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{2},\quad\xi_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}|\xi|^{2}(1+4\pi^{2}\sigma|\xi|^{2})=\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}\chi(\gamma,\kappa)^{2},

. So indeed
V(\gamma,\kappa)=\widetilde{V}(\gamma,\kappa)
as claimed and the first item is now established.

Let us now consider the second item. Firstly, one readily verifies (as a consequence of the first item) that
\mathrm{span}\{{\upvarphi_{+},\upvarphi_{-}}\}\subseteq\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
. To show the opposite inclusion, we shall split into two cases. Consider first that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}\cap(\partial\mathfrak{E})
, in other words
0<\kappa-\gamma=\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1)
. In this case we have that
\chi(\gamma,\kappa)=1
and so
V(\gamma,\kappa)=\{{(-\rho(\gamma,\kappa),0),(\rho(\gamma,\kappa),0)}\}
. Since
\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)=\frac{1}{\rho(\gamma,\kappa)}(1,1)
we can use Fourier reconstruction paired with the first item to deduce that if
\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}
then

(2.12)

\eta(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}L_{2}}}(\mathscr{F}[\eta](-\rho(\gamma,\kappa),0)e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{1}/L_{1}}+\mathscr{F}[\eta](\rho(\gamma,\kappa),0)e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}x_{1}/L_{1}}).

\eta
is
\mathbb{R}
-valued and so
\mathscr{F}[\eta](-\rho(\gamma,\kappa),0)=\overline{\mathscr{F}[\eta](\rho(\gamma,\kappa),0)}
and the above expression simplifies to a linear combination of
\upvarphi_{+}
and
\upvarphi_{-}
.

The consideration of the case
0<\kappa-\gamma<\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1)
follows a similar argument. Now
\chi(\gamma,\kappa)\in(0,1)
and so
V(\gamma,\kappa)
consists of 4 points and Fourier reconstruction gives:

(2.13)

\eta(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}L_{2}}}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\{{-1,1}\}}\mathscr{F}[\eta](\sigma_{1}/L_{1},\sigma_{2}/L_{2})e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}x_{1}/L_{1}}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\sigma_{2}x_{2}/L_{2}}.

\eta
is again
\mathbb{R}
-valued, which means
\mathscr{F}[\eta](-\xi)=\overline{\mathscr{F}[\eta](\xi)}
, and so the expression above simplifies to

(2.14)

\eta(x_{1},x_{2})=A_{0}\cos(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\cos(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2})+A_{1}\sin(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\cos(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2})\\
+A_{2}\cos(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\sin(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2})+A_{3}\sin(2\pi x_{1}/L_{1})\sin(2\pi x_{2}/L_{2}),

for some coefficients
A_{0},A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
. Now we invoke that
\eta
is an even function in the second variable, which necessitates that
A_{2}=A_{3}=0
and hence
\eta
is a linear combination of
\upvarphi_{+}
and
\upvarphi_{-}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now that we have established an explicit representation for the kernel of
Q
, we would like to return to the study of
P
. In what follows the following projection operator notation will be used. Define, for
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
and
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)
, the linear map
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}:\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{surf}}_{L}\to\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
via

(2.15)

\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta=\upvarphi_{+}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}}\eta\upvarphi_{+}+\upvarphi_{-}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}}\eta\upvarphi_{-},

where the functions
\upvarphi_{\pm}
are as in the second item of Proposition
2.2
. Note that since
\upvarphi_{\pm}
are orthonormal for the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
scalar product, we have that
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\circ\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}=\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}
.

We now combine the previous two results to summarize the information we have on the kernel of
P
.

Theorem 2.3

(Kernel synthesis)
.

Suppose that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
and that
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
.

(1)

The subspace
\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\subset\mathbf{X}_{L}
is two dimensional and is spanned by the set
\{{R_{\gamma,\kappa}\upvarphi_{+},R_{\gamma,\kappa}\upvarphi_{-}}\}
with
\upvarphi_{\pm}
as described in the second item of Proposition
2.2
and
R_{\gamma,\kappa}
the operator from (
2.3
).

(2)

The following direct sum decomposition holds:

(2.16)

\mathbf{X}_{L}=\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\oplus\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)

where

(2.17)

\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)=\{{(u,\eta)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}\;:\;\mathscr{F}[\eta](\xi)=0,\;\forall\;\xi\in V(\gamma,\kappa)}\}.

Proof.

The first item is immediate from the combination of Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
: the former result shows that
R_{\gamma,\kappa}:\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)\to\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
is an isomorphism while the latter result shows that
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)=\mathrm{span}\{{\upvarphi_{+},\upvarphi_{-}}\}
.

Let us now focus on proving the second item. Suppose that
(u,\eta)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
and define
(u_{0},\eta_{0}),(u_{1},\eta_{1})\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
via
(u_{0},\eta_{0})=R_{\gamma,\kappa}\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta
(recall (
2.15
)) and
(u_{1},\eta_{1})=(u,\eta)-(u_{0},\eta_{0})
. By construction we have that
(u,\eta)=(u_{0},\eta_{0})+(u_{1},\eta_{1})
. Since
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
Proposition
2.1
then assures us that
(u_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
. To verify that
(u_{1},\eta_{1})\in\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)
, we need that
\mathscr{F}[\eta_{1}]
vanishes on
V(\gamma,\kappa)
. Since
\eta_{1}=(I-\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa})\eta
and
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}
is a projection operator, we find that
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta_{1}=0
. On the other hand, we can write
\eta_{1}=\eta_{1,0}+\eta_{1,1}
with
\eta_{1,0}=\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\mathds{1}_{V(\gamma,\kappa)}\mathscr{F}[\eta_{1}]]
and
\eta_{1,1}=\eta_{1}-\eta_{1,0}
. Since
\eta_{1,1}
contains exclusively frequencies outside of
V(\gamma,\kappa)
, we have
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta_{1,1}=0
as well and hence
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta_{1,0}=0
. On the other hand
\eta_{1,0}\in\mathrm{span}\{{\upvarphi_{+},\upvarphi_{-}}\}
since the proof of Proposition
2.2
showed that
\mathrm{span}\{{\upvarphi_{+},\upvarphi_{-}}\}=\{{h\in\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}\;:\;\mathrm{supp}\mathscr{F}[h]\subseteq V(\gamma,\kappa)}\}
. So we deduce that
\eta_{1,0}=0
and so the inclusion
(u_{1},\eta_{1})\in\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)
follows.

We have established that the subspaces on the right of (
2.16
) sum to
\mathbf{X}_{L}
. Let us now argue that the sum is direct. Suppose that
(u_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
and
(u_{1},\eta_{1})\in\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)
are such that
(u_{0},\eta_{0})+(u_{1},\eta_{1})=0
. Upon restricting our attention to the
\eta
component we learn that
\eta_{0}+\eta_{1}=0
with
\mathscr{F}[\eta_{0}]
supported in
V(\gamma,\kappa)
(thanks to Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
) and
\mathscr{F}[\eta_{1}]
supported in
(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus V(\gamma,\kappa)
. So necessarily we have
\eta_{0}=\eta_{1}=0
. We also know that
(u_{0},\eta_{0})=R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta_{0}
and so
u_{0}=0
as well. At last we can conclude that
u_{1}=0
and so the sum is indeed direct.
∎

2.2.
Analysis of the range

In this subsection we complement our previous analysis by now studying the ranges of the operators
P
from (
2.1
). We shall find the following auxiliary linear operator quite useful: define
S_{\gamma}:\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}\to H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
, for
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, via

(2.18)

S_{\gamma}(f,g)=\Delta^{-2}[{\nabla\cdot f-(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-4\mu\Delta)g}],\quad(f,g)\in\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}.

Note that
S_{\gamma}
is also a parity preserving operator in the sense that
S_{\gamma}:\mathbf{Y}_{L}\to\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}
. Let us now classify the image of the operators
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
, viewing them as mapping
\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
.

Proposition 2.4

(Computation of the range)
.

Suppose that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
and
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
. The following hold.

(1)

For
(f,g)\in\mathbf{Y}_{L}
we have that
(f,g)\in\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
if and only if
\mathrm{supp}\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)]\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus V(\gamma,\kappa)
.

(2)

The subspace
\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
is closed.

Proof.

Let us begin by establishing the first item. Assume first that
(f,g)\in\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
so that there exists
(u,\eta)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
such that
P(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=(f,g)
. We therefore have the equations

(2.19)

-\gamma\partial_{1}u+u-\mu\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}u+\nabla(1-\sigma\Delta)\eta=\kappa\eta e_{1}+f,\quad\nabla\cdot u=\gamma\partial_{1}\eta+g.

The first of these can be decomposed into solenoidal and potential parts via the Leray projection operator
\mathbb{P}
and the second can be written in terms of
(I-\mathbb{P})
:

(2.20)

(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)\mathbb{P}u=\kappa\mathbb{P}(\eta e_{1})+\mathbb{P}f,\quad(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-4\mu\Delta)(I-\mathbb{P})u+\nabla(1-\sigma\Delta)\eta=\kappa(I-\mathbb{P})(\eta e_{1})+(I-\mathbb{P})f,\\
(I-\mathbb{P})u=\gamma(I-\mathbb{P})(\eta e_{1})+\Delta^{-1}\nabla g.

We then substitute the final identity in (
2.20
) into the penultimate one; this permits us to derive that the above equations are equivalent to

(2.21)

Q(\gamma,\kappa,\eta)=S_{\gamma}(f,g)

and

(2.22)

u=(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)^{-1}(\kappa\mathbb{P}(\eta e_{1})+\mathbb{P}f)+\gamma(I-\mathbb{P})(\eta e_{1})+\Delta^{-1}\nabla g.

Recall that the operators
Q
are defined in (
2.2
). Equation (
2.22
) tell us that the velocity
u
is entirely determined from the data
(f,g)
and the free surface
\eta
while (
2.21
) is an isolated equation for the free surface in terms of the data.

Upon taking the Fourier transform of identity (
2.21
) we find that for all
\xi\in(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus\{{0}\}
.

(2.23)

(4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2})^{-2}q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)\mathscr{F}[\eta](\xi)=\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)](\xi)

for the symbol

(2.24)

q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)=2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi_{1}(\gamma-\kappa+16\pi^{2}\mu\gamma|\xi|^{2})-4\pi^{2}(|\xi|^{2}-\gamma^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+4\pi^{2}\sigma|\xi|^{4}).

The proof of Proposition
2.2
shows that

(2.25)

\{{\xi\in(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus\{{0}\}\;:\;q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)=0}\}=\widetilde{V}(\gamma,\kappa)=V(\gamma,\kappa),

where the middle set is given by (
2.8
). Therefore, a necessary condition for (
2.23
) to hold is that
\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)]
vanishes whenever
q_{\gamma,\kappa}
vanishes, which is the same as saying that
\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)]
is supported on the complement of
V(\gamma,\kappa)
. Thus we have established the necessary direction of the first item.

Let us now focus on the sufficient direction. Suppose that
(f,g)\in\mathbf{Y}_{L}
satisfy
\mathrm{supp}\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)]\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus V(\gamma,\kappa)
. We propose that one can define
(u,\eta)
via (
2.21
) and (
2.22
) under this hypothesis. We shall define
\eta\in\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}
via the following prescription of its Fourier modes
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2}
:

(2.26)

\mathscr{F}[\eta](\xi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\xi\in V(\gamma,\kappa)\cup\{{0}\},\\
(q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi))^{-1}(4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2})^{2}\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)](\xi)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that if
\xi\notin V(\gamma,\kappa)\cup\{{0}\}
then
q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)\neq 0
; additionally it holds that

(2.27)

\frac{(4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2})^{2}}{q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)}\to-\frac{1}{\sigma}\quad\text{as}\quad|\xi|\to\infty

and hence not only is
\eta\in\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}
well-defined, we also have the estimate
\lVert\eta\rVert_{\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}}\lesssim\lVert S_{\gamma}(f,g)\rVert_{\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}}\lesssim\lVert(f,g)\rVert_{\mathbf{Y}_{L}}
for an implicit constant depending only
\gamma
,
\kappa
,
\mu
, and
\sigma
. We then define
u
in terms of
\eta
,
f
, and
g
according to equation (
2.22
). It is then readily verified that
u\in\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{velo}}
with
\lVert u\rVert_{\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{velo}}}\lesssim\lVert(f,g)\rVert_{\mathbf{Y}_{L}}
. Let us now verify that
P(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=(f,g)
; by the reductions made at the beginning of the proof it is equivalent to show that (
2.21
) and (
2.22
) are satisfied. The latter of this is automatic while the former of these follows from (
2.26
) and the assumption that
\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)](\xi)=0
for
\xi\in V(\gamma,\kappa)\cup\{{0}\}
. Thus the first item’s proof is now complete.

The second item is easy now that we have the first. The range is a closed set since the first conclusion equates the range to the intersection of finitely many kernels of bounded linear maps on
\mathbf{Y}_{L}
; namely the functions
(f,g)\mapsto\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(f,g)](\xi)
for
\xi\in V(\gamma,\kappa)
.
∎

Next we shall consider certain complimented subspaces to the range of
P
which are related to the partial derivatives of
P
with respect to the
\gamma
and
\kappa
parameters and its kernel. The following notation is set: the mappings
P_{\mathrm{s}},P_{\mathrm{t}}:\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
are defined via

(2.28)

P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)=-(\partial_{1}u,\partial_{1}\eta),\quad P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=-(\eta e_{1},0).

Notice that

(2.29)

P(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=P(0,0,u,\eta)+\gamma P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\kappa P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta),

for
(u,\eta)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
and
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular we have
D_{1,1}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)=P_{\mathrm{s}}
and
D_{1,2}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)=P_{\mathrm{t}}
, where
D_{1,1}
and
D_{1,2}
refer to the coordinate partial derivatives with respect to the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
factor in the definition of
P
(which is (
2.1
)).

Lemma 2.5

(Bases for the kernel of
Q
)
.

Suppose that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
,
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
. The following hold:

(1)

S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}},S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}:\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\to\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
, where the operator
S_{\gamma}
is defined in (
2.18
) and the operators
P_{\mathrm{s}}
and
P_{\mathrm{t}}
are given in (
2.28
).

(2)

For all
(u,\eta)\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\setminus\{{0}\}
the linear map

(2.30)

\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\alpha S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\beta S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)

is invertible.

Proof.

We begin the proof by computing the operators
S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}
and
S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}
on
\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
, so let
(u,\eta)
belong to this latter set. Thanks to Proposition
2.1
we have
(u,\eta)=R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta
with
\eta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
. So we can directly compute that
\Delta^{2}S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)=-\nabla\cdot(\partial_{1}u)+(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-4\mu\Delta)\partial_{1}\eta=-2\gamma\partial_{1}^{2}\eta+(1-4\mu\Delta)\partial_{1}\eta
and
\Delta^{2}S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}\eta=-\nabla\cdot(\eta e_{1})=-\partial_{1}\eta
and hence (since
\eta
is frequency supported
V(\gamma,\kappa)
)

(2.31)

S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)=\frac{1}{(4\pi^{2})^{2}\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{4}}(8\gamma\pi^{2}\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}\chi(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}\eta+(1+16\pi^{2}\mu\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{2})\partial_{1}\eta),\\
S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}\eta=-\frac{1}{(4\pi^{2})^{2}\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{4}}\partial_{1}\eta.

Notice that the operations
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{1}^{2}
preserve the subspace
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
(as can be readily checked on the basis
\{{\upvarphi_{+},\upvarphi_{-}}\}
) and hence we can deduce from the expressions (
2.31
) the claimed mapping properties of the first item.

We now prove the second item by checking a matrix representation for the linear map (
2.30
). The domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is equipped with the standard basis while the codomain
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
shall be equipped with the basis
\{{\eta,\partial_{1}\eta}\}
. That this latter set is indeed a basis, we shall check now. Supposing that
(u,\eta)\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\setminus\{{0}\}
leads to
\eta\neq 0
thanks to the previously mentioned identity
(u,\eta)=R_{\gamma,\kappa}\eta
. This
\eta
belongs to
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
and (by Proposition
2.2
) has frequency support in the set
V(\gamma,\kappa)
. As this set does not intersect
\{{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2}\;:\;\xi_{1}=0}\}
we also have
\partial_{1}\eta\neq 0
. We also know that
\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot)
is two dimensional and can be equipped with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
that is given by the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
-inner product of functions. As
\langle\eta,\partial_{1}\eta\rangle=0
(by the divergence theorem) we necessarily have that
\eta
and
\partial_{1}\eta
are linearly independent and hence spanning. Written in these aforementioned bases, the matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
corresponding to the linear map (
2.30
) (which is computed from the expressions (
2.31
)) is given by

(2.32)

M=\frac{1}{(4\pi^{2})^{2}\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{4}}\begin{pmatrix}8\gamma\pi^{2}\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}\chi(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}&0\\
1+16\pi^{2}\mu\rho(\gamma,\kappa)^{2}&-1\end{pmatrix}.

As
\rho(\gamma,\kappa),\chi(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
whenever
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
, we see that
\det M\neq 0
and the second item now follows.
∎

Proposition 2.6

(Complements of the range)
.

Let
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
,
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)
. Then for all
(u,\eta)\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\setminus\{{0}\}
we have

(2.33)

\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\oplus\mathrm{span}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)\oplus\mathrm{span}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=\mathbf{Y}_{L}.

In particular,
\mathrm{codim}\;\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)=2
.

Proof.

Let us establish first that the sum of the three subspaces on the left of (
2.33
) is
\mathbf{Y}_{L}
. Let
(f,g)\in\mathbf{Y}_{L}
. Set
h=\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}S_{\gamma}(f,g)\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma,\kappa)
(recall that the projection operator
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}
is defined in equation (
2.15
) while the operator
S_{\gamma}
is from (
2.18
)). Thanks to the second item of Lemma
2.5
we are assured the existence of
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\alpha S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\beta S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=h
. We set
(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g})=(f,g)-\alpha P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)-\beta P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)\in\mathbf{Y}_{L}
and claim that
(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g})\in\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
. To check this we shall use the first item of Proposition
2.4
. By construction we have that

(2.34)

S_{\gamma}(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g})=S_{\gamma}(f,g)-\alpha S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)-\beta S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=(I-\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa})S_{\gamma}(f,g)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}.

Hence
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}S_{\gamma}(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g})=0
. Now by arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we deduce
\mathrm{supp}\mathscr{F}[S_{\gamma}(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g})]\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus V(\gamma,\kappa)
. So indeed
(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g})\in\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
.

It remains to show that the sum of subspaces
\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)+\mathrm{span}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\mathrm{span}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=\mathbf{Y}_{L}
is direct. So suppose that
(f_{0},g_{0})\in\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
and
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are such that

(2.35)

(f_{0},g_{0})+\alpha_{0}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\beta_{0}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=0.

We can apply
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}S_{\gamma}
to (
2.35
); the contribution by
(f_{0},g_{0})
vanishes thanks to the first item of Proposition
2.4
and so we are left with
\Uppi_{\gamma,\kappa}[\alpha_{0}S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\beta_{0}S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)]=0
. Thanks to the first item of Lemma
2.5
we then learn that
\alpha_{0}S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{s}}(u,\eta)+\beta_{0}S_{\gamma}P_{\mathrm{t}}(u,\eta)=0
and then the second item of the same result implies that
\alpha_{0}=\beta_{0}=0
. Upon returning to (
2.35
) we deduce that
(f_{0},g_{0})=0
. So directness is shown and the proof is complete.
∎

2.3.
Synthesis of linear analysis

In this final subsection on linear analysis we tie together our results of Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.7

(On the operator
P
, I)
.

Let
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
and
L=\mathfrak{l}(\gamma,\kappa)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
. Regarding the operator
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot):\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
defined in (
2.1
), the following hold.

(1)

\mathrm{dim}\;\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)=2
.

(2)

The range of
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
is closed and
\mathrm{codim}\;\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)=2
.

(3)

For every
X\in\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\setminus\{{0}\}
it holds that

(2.36)

\mathbf{Y}_{L}=\mathrm{ran}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)\oplus\mathrm{span}D_{1,1}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)X\oplus\mathrm{span}D_{1,2}P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)X.

Proof.

The first item is Theorem
2.3
. The second and third items are Propositions
2.4
and
2.6
.
∎

We now give a complementary result to Theorem
2.7
which essentially says the set
\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
contains exactly the interesting parameter values for the linearized operator. Note that the next result holds in the larger domain and codomain spaces of (
1.29
), i.e. there is no need for second variable parity assumptions.

Theorem 2.8

(On the operator P, II)
.

Suppose that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
L\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
. If
(\gamma,\kappa)\not\in\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
, then the operator
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot):\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\to\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

By following the reductions in the proof of Proposition
2.4
, which do not require parity assumptions, we find that for
(u,\eta)\in\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}
and
(f,g)\in\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
the equation
P(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=(f,g)
is equivalent to the satisfaction of equations (
2.21
) and (
2.22
). The latter equation reads
u
as a bounded linear function of the data and
\eta
, so injectivity and surjectivity of
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot)
follow as soon as we verify that
Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot):H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\to H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
is an isomorphism. This linear map corresponds to the Fourier multiplication operator
\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2}\ni\xi\mapsto\frac{q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)}{(4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2})^{2}}\in\mathbb{C}
(with the definition of
q_{\gamma,\kappa}
given in (
2.24
)) and so we are tasked with inversion of its symbol on nonzero frequencies. Note that the limit (
2.27
) shows that there is no issue for frequencies outside of a large enough bounded region. Therefore, we need only verify that
q_{\gamma,\kappa}
has no zeros in the set
(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus\{{0}\}
.

Let
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\{{\xi\in(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus\{{0}\}\;:\;q_{\gamma,\kappa}(\xi)=0}\}
. By arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.2
we readily see that the set
Z(\gamma,\kappa)
does not intersect the line
\{{\xi\;:\;\xi_{1}=0}\}
and so an equivalent description is

(2.37)

Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\Big\{{\xi\in(\mathbb{Z}/L_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/L_{2})\setminus\{{0}\}\;:\;\begin{pmatrix}\gamma-\kappa+16\pi^{2}\mu\gamma|\xi|^{2}\\
|\xi|^{2}-\gamma^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+4\pi^{2}\sigma|\xi|^{4}\end{pmatrix}=0}\Big\}.

We first show that if
\gamma=0
or
\kappa=0
, then
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\varnothing
. If
\gamma=0
then the second equation in (
2.37
) leads to
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\varnothing
. If
\kappa=0
but
\gamma\neq 0
, then the first equation in (
2.37
) leads to
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\varnothing
.

We next claim that
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\varnothing
if
\gamma
and
\kappa
are both non zero and have opposite signs. If there were to exist
\xi\in Z(\gamma,\kappa)
in this case, then the first equation in (
2.37
) would imply that
1-\kappa/\gamma+16\pi^{2}\mu|\xi|^{2}=0
and so the opposite sign condition forces
|\xi|^{2}<0
which is a contradiction.

So
Z(\gamma,\kappa)
is only possibly nonempty if
\gamma
and
\kappa
have the same sign. It is obvious that
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=Z(-\gamma,-\kappa)
and so it suffices to consider what happens if both
\gamma
and
\kappa
are positive. In this case we can argue as in the proof of Proposition
2.2
to obtain that that
\xi\in Z(\gamma,\kappa)
if and only if the identities

(2.38)

|\xi|^{2}=\frac{\kappa/\gamma-1}{16\pi^{2}\mu}\quad\text{and}\quad\xi_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}|\xi|^{2}(1+4\pi^{2}\sigma|\xi|^{2})

are satisfied. A necessary condition for the satisfaction of (
2.38
) is that
|\xi|^{2}>0
and
\xi_{1}^{2}\leqslant|\xi|^{2}
which implies
\gamma>1
,
\kappa>\gamma
, and
\kappa<\gamma+\frac{4\mu}{\sigma}\gamma(\gamma^{2}-1)
. In other words
Z(\gamma,\kappa)\neq\varnothing
implies that
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathfrak{E}
.

It is thus established that if
(\gamma,\kappa)\not\in\mathfrak{E}\cup(-\mathfrak{E})
, then
Z(\gamma,\kappa)=\varnothing
and therefore the operator
Q(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot):H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\to H^{3}_{\bullet}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
is an isomorphism and so too is
P(\gamma,\kappa,\cdot,\cdot):\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\to\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
.
∎

3.
Nonlinear Analysis

The purpose of this section of the document is to derive an abstract bifurcation theorem and then to use this tool to construct and classify all small amplitude solutions to (
1.7
) in our functional framework.

3.1.
Abstract bifurcation

The derivation of our main tool which allows us to find all small amplitude gravity-capillary roll wave solutions to the inclined viscous shallow water system is the content of this subsection. The bifurcation theorem presented here for operators with multiple parameters, which will certainly be no surprise to the expert reader, is based off of Theorem I.19.6 in Kielhöfer
[
21
]
. The main difference is that our result adds a classification of the totality of small solutions by appending an argument mimicking that of Lemma 1.12 in Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Multiparameter bifurcation)
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are real Banach spaces,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times X
is open,
f:U\to Y
is smooth, and
(\lambda_{\star},0)\in U
are such that:

(1)

f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)=0
whenever
(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)\in U
.

(2)

The linear map
D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0):X\to Y
has closed range and satisfies
\mathrm{dim}E_{\star}=n
and
\mathrm{codim}F_{\star}=n
where
E_{\star}=\mathrm{ker}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\subset X
and
F_{\star}=\mathrm{ran}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\subset Y
. Here
D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)
is the derivative with respect to the
X
factor.

(3)

The open set

(3.1)

A_{\star}=\{{v\in E_{\star}\setminus\{{0}\}\;:\;Y=F_{\star}\oplus\mathrm{span}D_{1,1}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)v\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{span}D_{1,n}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)v}\}.

is nonempty. Here
D_{1}=(D_{1,1},\dots,D_{1,n})
is the derivative with respect to the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
factor broken into the coordinate partials.

If
Z\subset X
is any subspace such that
E_{\star}\oplus Z=X
and
\varnothing\neq K\subset A_{\star}
is compact, then the following hold.

(1)

There exists
\varepsilon,r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and smooth functions
\Lambda:[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K\to B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)
,
W:[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K\to Z\cap B_{X}(0,r)
satisfying for all
(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
:

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\Lambda(0,v)=0,\\
W(0,v)=0,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad f(\lambda_{\star}+\Lambda(\alpha,v),\alpha(v+W(\alpha,v)))=0.

(2)

If
(\lambda,z)\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)\times(Z\cap B_{X}(0,r))
are such that for some
(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
with
\alpha\neq 0
we have that
f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha(v+z))=0
, then we have that
\lambda=\Lambda(\alpha,v)
and
z=W(\alpha,v)
.

(3)

Assume additionally that
K\supseteq\{{v\in E_{\star}\;:\;\lVert v\rVert_{X}=1}\}
. Then there exists an open set
(\lambda_{\star},0)\in U_{1}\subseteq U
with the property that

(3.3)

(f^{-1}\{{0}\})\cap U_{1}=[{\{{(\lambda,0)\;:\;\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\}\cup\{{(\lambda_{\star}+\Lambda(\alpha,v),\alpha(v+W(\alpha,v)))\;:\;(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K}\}}]\cap U_{1}.

Proof.

Let

(3.4)

V=\{{(\alpha,v,\lambda,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times A_{\star}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z\;:\;(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha(v+z))\in U)}\}.

Observe that
\{{0}\}\times A_{\star}\times\{{0}\}\times\{{0}\}\subset V
. Consider the mapping
F:V\subset\mathbb{R}\times A_{\star}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z\to Y
defined via

(3.5)

F(\alpha,v,\lambda,z)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{-1}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha(v+z))&\text{if }\alpha\neq 0,\\
D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)(v+z)&\text{if }\alpha=0.\end{cases}

Notice that the first hypothesis on
f
permits us to Taylor expand for
\alpha\neq 0
and write

(3.6)

\alpha^{-1}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha(v+z))=\alpha^{-1}(f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha(v+z))-f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0))\\
=D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)(v+z)+\alpha\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)D_{2}^{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\tau\alpha(v+z))(v+z)^{\otimes 2}\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

From this one then readily deduces that
F
is smooth. We also observe that for any
v\in A_{\star}\subset E_{\star}
it holds that
F(0,v,0,0)=0
.

Now consider the domain of
F
as grouped into the product of the factors
X_{0}=\mathbb{R}\times A_{\star}
and
X_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z
so that
V\subset X_{0}\times X_{1}
. We would like to establish that for
v\in A_{\star}
the derivative
D_{2}F(0,v,0,0):X_{1}\to Y
is an isomorphism of Banach spaces. We compute that for
(\overline{\lambda},\overline{z})\in X_{1}
it holds

(3.7)

D_{2}F(0,v,0,0)(\overline{\lambda},\overline{z})=D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\overline{z}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{\lambda}_{j}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)v.

Our hypotheses ensure that this map is an isomorphism. Indeed, by the definition of
A_{\star}
and the fact that
v\in A_{\star}
,
D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\overline{z}\in F_{\star}
it follows that for any
y\in Y
the identity

(3.8)

D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\overline{z}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{\lambda}_{j}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star})v=y

is equivalent to

(3.9)

D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\overline{z}=y_{0},\;\forall\;j\in\{{1,\dots,n}\}\;\overline{\lambda}_{j}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)v=y_{j}

where
y=y_{0}+y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n}
is the unique decomposition with
y_{0}\in F_{\star}
and for
j\in\{{1,\dots,n}\}

y_{j}\in\mathrm{span}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)v
. So if
y=0
, then
\overline{\lambda}=0
and
z\in Z\cap E_{\star}=\{{0}\}
, hence
D_{2}F(0,v,0,0)
is injective. On the other hand, for any value of
y
we can find
\overline{z}\in Z
and
\overline{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that equation (
3.9
) is satisfied; therefore surjectivity is too established.

The hypotheses of the implicit function theorem are satisfied for the map
F
at the point
(0,v,0,0)
. We are therefore granted radii
r^{0}_{v},r^{1}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the property that
B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r^{0}_{v})\times B_{X_{1}}((0,0),r^{1}_{v})\subset V
and we are also granted a unique function with
\upiota_{v}(0,v)=0
,

(3.10)

\upiota_{v}:B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r_{v}^{0})\to B_{X_{1}}((0,0),r_{v}^{1}),\quad F(\alpha,w,\upiota_{v}(\alpha,w))=0,\;\forall\;(\alpha,w)\in B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r_{v}^{0}).

Moreover the functions
\upiota_{v}
are smooth.

We now would like to glue together these maps. Notice that if
v,\overline{v}\in A_{\star}
are such that
B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r^{0}_{v})\cap B_{X_{0}}((0,\overline{v}),r_{\overline{v}}^{0})\neq\varnothing
then, by uniqueness, we must have that
\upiota_{v}=\upiota_{\overline{v}}
on this intersection. This allows us to define the smooth map

(3.11)

\upiota:\bigcup_{v\in A_{\star}}B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r_{v}^{0})\to X_{1},\quad\upiota=\upiota_{v}\text{ on }B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r^{0}_{v}).

Now consider a compact subset
K\subset A_{\star}
. The family of balls
\{{B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r_{v}^{0})}\}_{v\in K}
form a cover of
\{{0}\}\times K\subset X_{0}
and so there is a finite subcover, say indexed by
\{{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}}\}\subset K
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Let us set
\rho_{K}=\frac{1}{2}\min\{{r^{1}_{v_{1}},\dots,r^{1}_{v_{m}}}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The set

(3.12)

\upiota^{-1}(B_{X_{1}}((0,0),\rho_{K}))\subset\bigcup_{v\in A_{\star}}B_{X_{0}}((0,v),r_{v}^{0})

is open and
\{{0}\}\times K\subset\upiota^{-1}(B_{X_{1}}((0,0),\rho_{K}))
. Thanks to compactness again, there exists a
\delta_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the property that
[-\delta_{K},\delta_{K}]\times K\subset\upiota^{-1}(B_{X_{1}}((0,0),\rho_{K}))
. In other words, we can view
\upiota:[-\delta_{K},\delta_{K}]\times K\to B_{X_{1}}((0,0),\rho_{K})
as a smooth map with the property that
\upiota(0,v)=0
for all
v\in K
and
F(\alpha,v,\upiota(\alpha,w))=0
for all
(\alpha,w)\in[-\delta_{K},\delta_{K}]\times K
. This restriction of
\upiota
is the unique function with these two properties. To obtain the first and second conclusions of the theorem we need only to take
\Lambda
and
W
to be the components of the map
\upiota
.

We now turn our attention to the third conclusion of the theorem, whose proof will be split into the series of sub-claims. The first sub-claim that will be established is as follows: There exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
v\in K
and all
(\lambda,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z
we have

(3.13)

C^{-1}(\lVert z\rVert_{X}+|\lambda|)\leqslant\Big\lVert D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)z+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)v\Big\rVert_{Y}\leqslant C(\lVert z\rVert_{X}+|\lambda|).

Notice that the map in the center of inequality (
3.13
) is
D_{2}F(0,v,0,0)(\lambda,z)
(see (
3.7
)); earlier in the proof we have established that
D_{2}F(0,v,0,0)
is an isomorphism
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z\to Y
. Therefore there exists a constant
C_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that (
3.13
) holds with
C
replaced by
C_{v}
at a fixed
v\in K
and for all
(\lambda,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z
. By a simple absorption argument, we find that there actually exists
\upepsilon_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
\widetilde{v}\in B(v,\upepsilon_{v})\cap K
and all
(\lambda,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z
it holds that

(3.14)

(2C_{v})^{-1}(\lVert z\rVert+|\lambda|)\leqslant\lVert D_{2}F(0,\widetilde{v},0,0)(\lambda,z)\rVert_{Y}\leqslant 2C_{v}(\lVert z\rVert_{X}+|\lambda|).

The collection of open balls
\{{B(v,\upepsilon_{v})}\}_{v\in K}
is cover for the compact set
K
and so we may extract a finite collection
\{{v_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{p}\subseteq K
such that
K\subset\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{p}B(v_{\ell},\upepsilon_{v_{\ell}})
; hence upon setting
C=2\max_{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant p}C_{v_{\ell}}
we deduce the desired equivalence of (
3.13
).

The second sub-claim is that there exists an open set
(\lambda_{\star},0)\in U_{1}\subseteq U
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all
(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
and
(\lambda,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z
satisfying
(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v+z)\in U_{1}
and
f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v+z)=0
we have that

(3.15)

|\alpha\lambda|+\lVert z\rVert_{X}\leqslant c|\alpha|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad|\alpha|\leqslant r/2c,

where
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the radius granted by the first conclusion of the theorem.

To prove this claim we fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
sufficiently small suppose initially that
(\lambda,z)\in B(0,\kappa)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Z
,
(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
,
\alpha v\in B(0,\kappa)\subset X
. are such that
f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v+z)=0
. Now we consider

(3.16)

0=f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v+z)=[{f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v+z)-f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v)}]+[{f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v)-f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)}]=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{II}.

We now write

(3.17)

\mathbf{I}=[{f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v+z)-f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v)-D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v)z}]\\
+[{D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v)-D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)}]z+D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)z=\mathbf{I}_{1}+\mathbf{I}_{2}+D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)z

and

(3.18)

\mathbf{II}=[{f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,\alpha v)-f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)-D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)\alpha v}]\\
+[{D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,0)-D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)-\lambda\cdot D_{1}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)}]\alpha v+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\alpha v\\
=\mathbf{II}_{1}+\mathbf{II}_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}D_{1,j}D_{2}f(\lambda_{\star},0)\alpha v.

The terms
\mathbf{I}_{1}
,
\mathbf{I}_{2}
,
\mathbf{II}_{1}
and
\mathbf{II}_{2}
can be estimated as follows

(3.19)

\lVert\mathbf{I}_{1}\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\lVert z\rVert_{X}^{2}\lesssim\kappa\lVert z\rVert_{X},\;\lVert\mathbf{I}_{2}\rVert_{Y}\lesssim(|\lambda|+|\alpha v|)\lVert z\rVert_{X}\lesssim\kappa\lVert z\rVert_{X},\\
\lVert\mathbf{II}_{1}\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\lVert\alpha v\rVert^{2}_{X}\lesssim|\alpha|^{2},\;\lVert\mathbf{II}_{2}\rVert_{Y}\lesssim|\lambda|^{2}\lVert\alpha v\rVert_{X}\lesssim\kappa|\alpha\lambda|,

where the implicit constants depend on the second and third derivatives of the function
f
. Now we have that
0=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{II}=D_{2}F(0,v,0,0)(\alpha\lambda,z)+\mathbf{I}_{1}+\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{II}_{1}+\mathbf{II}_{2}
and so we may estimate according to the first claim (
3.13
) that

(3.20)

|\alpha\lambda|+\lVert z\rVert_{X}\lesssim\lVert D_{2}F(0,v,0,0)(\alpha\lambda,z)\rVert_{Y}=\lVert\mathbf{I}_{1}+\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{II}_{1}+\mathbf{II}_{2}\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\kappa(|\alpha\lambda|+\lVert z\rVert_{X})+\alpha^{2}.

So by taking
\kappa>0
sufficiently small we can absorb in (
3.20
) and acquire the desired bound (
3.15
). The open set
U_{1}
can then be taken to be product of
B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\lambda_{\star},\kappa)
and the preimage of
B(0,\kappa)\times B(0,\kappa)
under the direct sum identification map
X=E_{\star}\oplus Z\to E_{\star}\times Z
. By taking
\kappa
smaller (say
\kappa\leqslant\varepsilon
), if necessary, we can also ensure that

(3.21)

\{{\alpha v\in E_{\star}\;:\;(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K}\}\supseteq B_{E_{\star}}(0,\kappa).

The inclusion (
3.21
) is where we are using the hypothesis
K\subseteq\{{v\in E_{\star}\;:\;\lVert v\rVert_{X}=1}\}
. Finally, by making
\kappa
smaller again, if needed, we can also guarantee that if
\alpha v\in B(0,\kappa)
for some
(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
, then
|\alpha|\leqslant r/2c
, thanks to the fact that the set
K
is a positive distance from
0
.

We are now ready to prove the third item. It is clear that the inclusion ‘
\supseteq
’ in (
3.3
) holds, so we need only focus on justifying ‘
\subseteq
’. So let us assume that
f(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,x)=0
with
(\lambda_{\star}+\lambda,x)\in U_{1}
with
U_{1}
as constructed in the proof of the second sub-claim. We decompose
x=w+z
with
w\in E_{\star}
and
z\in Z
according to the direct sum
X=E_{\star}\oplus Z
. As inclusion (
3.21
) holds, we may find
(\alpha,v)\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K
such that
w=\alpha v
. We have now reached a situation in which the second sub-claim applies and we deduce that inequality (
3.15
) holds for our
\lambda
and
z
.

If
\alpha=0
, then we deduce that
z=0
and the solution belongs to the set of trivial solutions
U_{1}\cap\{{(\uplambda,0)\;:\;\uplambda\in\mathbb{R}}\}
. On the other hand, if
\alpha\neq 0
, we deduce the inequalities:
|\lambda|+\lVert z/\alpha\rVert_{X}\leqslant c|\alpha|\leqslant r/2
. Hence we are in a position to apply the uniqueness assertion of the second item and deduce that
\lambda=\Lambda(\alpha,v)
and
z/\alpha=W(\alpha,v)
.
∎

3.2.
Analysis of the shallow water system’s nonlinearities

We cast system (
1.7
) as a nonlinear operator equation with two parameters and then check that certain mapping properties are satisfied.

Recall the definitions of the spaces
\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}
,
\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
from (
1.29
) and
\mathbf{X}_{L}
,
\mathbf{Y}_{L}
from (
1.10
) and (
1.28
). For any
L\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
we define the map
N:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\to\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
via

(3.22)

N(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=\begin{pmatrix}(1+\eta)u\cdot\nabla u-\gamma\eta\partial_{1}u-\mu\nabla\cdot(\eta\mathbb{S}u)+\eta\nabla(1-\sigma\Delta)\eta\\
\nabla\cdot(\eta u)\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 3.2

(Analysis of the nonlinearity)
.

For
L\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}
the following hold.

(1)

The mapping
N
from (
3.22
) is well-defined and smooth.

(2)

N
is parity preserving in the sense that
N:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
.

(3)

N
is purely nonlinear in the sense that
D_{2}N(\gamma,\kappa,0,0)=0
for all
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
where
D_{2}
refers to the derivative with respect to the
\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}
factor of the domain.

(4)

A tuple
(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}
is a solution to system (
1.7
) if and only if
(P+N)(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=0
, where
P
is the family of linear maps defined in (
2.1
).

Proof.

The mapping
N
is the sum of bilinear and trilinear mappings. Therefore, to establish the first and second items, we need only verify that each constituent multilinearity of
N
is a bounded mapping
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\to\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
and that the defining symmetries of
\mathbf{X}_{L}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{L}
are respected. This latter point is rote checking, so let us only prove the former point. For the first component of
N
we need only look the embedding maps
H^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
and
H^{3}\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}
along with the elementary inequalities

(3.23)

\lVert(1+\eta)u\cdot\nabla u\rVert_{L_{2}}\leqslant\lVert 1+\eta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\langle\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}\rangle\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}^{2},

(3.24)

\lVert\eta\partial_{1}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leqslant\lVert\eta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}},

(3.25)

\lVert\nabla\cdot(\eta\mathbb{S}u)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\mathbb{S}u\rVert_{H^{1}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\lVert\mathbb{S}u\rVert_{H^{1}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}},

and

(3.26)

\lVert\eta\nabla(1-\sigma\Delta)\eta\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}^{2}.

While for the second component of
N
we shall use that
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is an algebra

(3.27)

\lVert\nabla\cdot(\eta u)\rVert_{H^{1}}\lesssim\lVert\eta u\rVert_{H^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{3}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}.

So the first item is established. The third and fourth items are immediate.
∎

3.3.
Conclusions

In this final subsection of the document we bring together our linear analysis of Section
2
with the nonlinear analysis of Section
3.2
and with either the inverse function theorem or local bifurcation theorem of Section
3.1
. We are studying solutions to the system of equations (
1.7
) which, thanks to the third item of Proposition
3.2
, are neatly encoded as zeros of the function
P+N
. Recall that the set
\mathfrak{E}\subset(1,\infty)^{2}
is defined in (
1.11
).

Our first result here, which is Theorem
3
of Section
1.3
, regards the nonexistence of small amplitude periodic gravity-capillary roll wave solutions for certain combinations of wave speed and tilt.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

This is a simple consequence of the implicit function theorem.
P
as defined in (
2.1
) is a family of bounded linear maps while
N
is smooth and satisfies
D_{2}N(\cdot,\cdot,0,0)=0
thanks to Proposition
3.2
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.8
we have that
D_{2}(P+N)(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)=P(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot,\cdot)
is an isomorphism
\overline{\mathbf{X}}_{L}\to\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{L}
. So the implicit function theorem applies and so we learn that
(P+N)(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=0
with
\gamma-\gamma_{\star}
,
\kappa-\kappa_{\star}
,
u
,
\eta
sufficiently small implies that
u=0
and
\eta=0
.
∎

Our next result, which is Theorem
1
of Section
1.3
, says that if the speed and tilt parameters are in the complement of the previous theorem’s nonexistence set, then one can select the period lengths of the variables in such a way to guarantee the existence of a two parameter family of periodic nontrivial small amplitude solutions to system (
1.7
) which emanate from the trivial solution.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We shall prove this result by way of multiparameter bifurcation using Theorem
3.1
. Let us first remark that it suffices to consider the case that
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}
. Consider the reflection operator
R(y_{1},y_{2})=(-y_{1},y_{2})
; given
(u,\eta)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
let us define
(w,h)\in\mathbf{X}_{L}
via
h=\eta\circ R
and
w=Ru\circ R
; in other words

(3.28)

h(x_{1},x_{2})=\eta(-x_{1},x_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad w(x_{1},x_{2})=(-u_{1}(-x_{1},x_{2}),u_{2}(-x_{1},x_{2})),\quad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L}.

A routine calculation shows that (for any choice of
(\gamma,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
)

(3.29)

(P+N)(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad(P+N)(-\gamma,-\kappa,w,h)=0.

Therefore, once the local theory of solutions for the parameter values
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}
has been established, we can deduce a corresponding result for
(-\gamma_{\star},-\kappa_{\star})\in-\mathfrak{E}
via this reflection procedure. So let us assume now that
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star})\in\mathfrak{E}
.

Our goal is to apply Theorem
3.1
to the smooth mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
defined via
f=P+N
at the point
(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)
. The first hypothesis that
f(\cdot,\cdot,0,0)=0
is satisfied by inspection. Next, we see that
D_{2}f(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)=P(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot,\cdot)
(by the second item of Proposition
3.2
) and so we can apply Theorem
2.7
and read off that the linear map
D_{2}f(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0):\mathbf{X}_{L}\to\mathbf{Y}_{L}
has a two dimensional kernel
E=\mathrm{ker}D_{2}f(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)
, has a closed range
F=\mathrm{ran}D_{2}f(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)
of codimension 2, and for all
(u,\eta)\in E\setminus\{{0}\}
it holds that
\mathbf{Y}_{L}=F\oplus\mathrm{span}D_{1,1}D_{2}f(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)(u,\eta)\oplus\mathrm{span}D_{1,2}D_{2}f(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},0,0)(u,\eta)
. Hence all of the hypotheses of the multiparameter bifurcation theorem are satisfied.

Let us take our complementing subspace:
Z=\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)\subset\mathbf{X}_{L}
as in Theorem
2.3
and the compact set
K=E\cap\partial B_{\mathbf{X}_{L}}(0,1)
. Theorem
3.1
grants us
\varepsilon,r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
along with smooth functions
\Lambda:[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K\to B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,r)
,
W:[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\times K\to Z\cap B_{\mathbf{X}_{L}}(0,r)
for which (
3.2
) is satisfied. To obtain the functions
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\kappa})
from
\Lambda
and
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\eta})
from
W
we note that because
\Lambda
and
W
vanish for
\alpha=0
we can divide by
\alpha
and retain smoothness, i.e.:

(3.30)

(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\kappa})(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)=\Lambda(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)/\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}D_{1}\Lambda(\tau\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)\;\mathrm{d}\tau,\\
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\eta})(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)=W(\alpha,\upupsilon,\eta)/\alpha=\int_{0}^{1}D_{1}W(\tau\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

We then take the parameter
\overline{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
to be large enough so that
(\widetilde{\gamma},\widetilde{\kappa})
and
(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{\eta})
map into balls of radius
\overline{r}
.

By construction we have
(P+N)(\gamma,\kappa,u,\eta)=0
whenever the argument is defined via (
1.14
). Thus to complete the proof of the first item, we need to argue that
\eta\neq 0
for
\alpha\neq 0
. As
\mathbf{X}_{L}=E\oplus Z
and
\alpha^{2}(\widetilde{u}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\eta),\widetilde{\eta}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta))\in Z
, we see that
\alpha(\upupsilon,\upeta)\neq 0
implies
(u,\eta)\neq 0
. This happens exactly when
\alpha\neq 0
because
\lVert(\upupsilon,\upeta)\rVert_{\mathbf{X}_{L}}=1
. Moreover, since
(\upupsilon,\upeta)\in E=\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot,\cdot)
we may quote Proposition
2.1
to see that
(\upupsilon,\upeta)=R_{\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star}}\upeta
and hence
1\lesssim\lVert\upeta\rVert_{\mathbf{X}_{L}^{\mathrm{surf}}}
. By definition of
Z=\mathbf{Z}_{L}(\gamma,\kappa)
we also observe that
\widetilde{\eta}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)
and
\upeta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot)
have disjoint Fourier supports and so in fact
\eta\neq 0
for
\alpha\neq 0
.

The validity of the second item of this theorem is immediate from the third conclusion of Theorem
3.1
(which we are permitted to apply here as
K
contains all kernel vectors of unit length) if we set
\mathcal{O}=U_{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Our final result, which is Theorem
2
from Section
1.3
, gives qualitative remarks about the solutions produced by the previous result.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Directly from equation (
1.14
) we know that
\eta_{\alpha}=\alpha\upeta+\alpha^{2}\widetilde{\eta}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)
and
u_{\alpha}=\alpha\upupsilon+\alpha^{2}\widetilde{u}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)
with
(\upupsilon,\upeta)\in K=\mathrm{ker}P(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot,\cdot)
. Hence, thanks to Proposition
2.1
, we have that
\upeta\in\mathrm{ker}Q(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot)\setminus\{{0}\}
, where
Q
is the operator from (
2.2
), and

(3.31)

\upupsilon=\kappa_{\star}(1-\gamma\partial_{1}-\mu\Delta)^{-1}(I+\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R})(\upeta e_{1})-\gamma_{\star}\mathcal{R}\otimes\mathcal{R}(\upeta e_{1}).

Equation (
3.31
) readily implies that (
1.18
) and (
1.19
) hold. The kernel of
Q(\gamma_{\star},\kappa_{\star},\cdot)
is computed in Proposition
2.2
and is spanned by the functions
\upvarphi_{+}
,
\upvarphi_{-}
from equations (
2.6
) or (
2.7
) and so
\upeta=A\upvarphi_{+}+B\upvarphi_{-}
for some
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
(not both zero). So the desired estimates of (
1.20
) follow by subtracting
\alpha\upeta
from
\eta_{\alpha}
and
\alpha\upupsilon
from
u_{\alpha}
and estimating the remainder in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
via the Sobolev embedding:
\widetilde{\eta}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)\in H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L})
,
\widetilde{u}(\alpha,\upupsilon,\upeta)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R}^{2})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{L};\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The constant
c
in the claimed inequalities can then be taken a function of
\overline{r}
and these embedding constants.
∎
