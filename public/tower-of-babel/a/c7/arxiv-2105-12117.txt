L 2 L^{2} -critical nonuniqueness for the 2D Navier-Stokes equations
Alexey Cheskidov, Xiaoyutao Luo

Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the 2D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations on the torus. It is well known that for any L 2 L^{2} divergence-free initial data, there exists a global smooth solution that is unique in the class of C t ​ L 2 C_{t}L^{2} weak solutions. We show that such uniqueness would fail in the class C t ​ L p C_{t}L^{p} if p < 2 p<2 . The non-unique solutions we constructed are almost L 2 L^{2} -critical in the sense that ( i ) (i) they are uniformly continuous in L p L^{p} for every p < 2 p<2 ; ( i ​ i ) (ii) the kinetic energy agrees with any given smooth positive profile except on a set of arbitrarily small measure in time.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Statement of the problem

The 2D Navier-Stokes equations are a fundamental mathematical model of fluid flow written as

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla p=0&\\
\D u=0&\\
u(0)=u_{0}\end{cases}

posed on a spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a suitable boundary condition, where
u:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the unknown velocity with initial data
u_{0}
and
p:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is a scalar pressure. We consider the Cauchy problem of (
1.1
) on a time interval
[0,T]
for some initial data
u_{0}
and
T>0
. The existence and uniqueness of smooth solutions have been proved by Leray
[
Ler33
]
for
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and Ladyzhenskaya
[
Lad58
]
for bounded domains.

In the paper, we focus on the periodic case
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and the solutions with zero spatial mean

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u(x,t)\,dx=0,

which is also conserved under the evolution of the equations (
1.1
).

For any divergence-free initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, a standard Galerkin method leads to the existence of a weak solution
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
, and the validity of the energy equality

(1.2)

\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla u(s)\|_{2}^{2}\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\|u(0)\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{for all $t\geq 0$},

follows from the Onsager criticality of the energy-enstrophy space in 2D. A weak-strong uniqueness argument and Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality imply the regularity and uniqueness of such Leray-Hopf solutions.

In fact, the uniqueness of the 2D Navier-Stokes equation can be stated in a much stronger way. More precisely, Leray-Hopf solutions are unique in a much larger class than the Leray-Hopf class itself. As discussed in
[
GP02
]
, the result
[
FLRT00
]
of Furioli, Lemarié-Rieusset, and Terraneo implies that in 2D any weak solution in
C_{t}L^{2}
is unique without any additional regularity assumptions:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
FLRT00
]
)
.

For any divergence-free
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, there is only one weak solution in the class
C_{t}L^{2}
with initial data
u_{0}
.

Concerning the sharpness of this existence/uniqueness result, we ask two questions:

(a)

Does the existence still hold if initial data
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
p<2
?

(b)

Are weak solutions in the class
C_{t}L^{p}
unique if
p<2
?

The aim of this paper is to provide a positive answer to the first question, and a negative answer to the second one. In fact, the main results in this paper will be stated in wider Sobolev spaces, but we choose the usual Lebesgue spaces
L^{p}
here as the most elementary and significant. For instance, the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is invariant under the natural scaling of the Navier-Stokes equations
u(x,t)\to u_{\lambda}(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
, and the square of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
norm represents the total kinetic energy, which is nonincreasing in time for smooth solutions due to (
1.2
).

It is easy to see that when
p<2
, the regularity
u\in C_{t}L^{p}
itself alone is not enough to make sense of the weak formulation. By a weak solution of (
1.1
), we mean a vector field
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,T])
satisfying all of the following conditions:

(1)

For
a.e\,t\in[0,T]
,
u(\cdot,t)
is weakly divergence-free;

(2)

For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{T}
,

(1.3)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u_{0}(x)\varphi(\cdot,t)\,dx+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u\cdot(\Delta\varphi+u\otimes u:\nabla\varphi-u\cdot\partial_{t}\varphi)\,dxdt=0,

where
\mathcal{D}_{T}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R})
is the space of all divergence-free test functions vanishing on
t\geq T
.

The above definition seems too weak at first glance. However, by
[
FJR72
, Theorem 2.1]
, the above definition of weak solutions is equivalent to the integral equation

(1.4)

u(t)=e^{t\Delta}u_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathcal{P}\D(u\otimes u)(s)\,ds,

where
e^{t\Delta}
is the heat semigroup and
\mathcal{P}
is the Leray projection onto the divergence-free vector fields. This equation (
1.4
) is often referred to as the
mild formulation
of the Navier-Stokes equations and has been used to construct unique local solutions, also known as the
mild solutions
, when the initial data is critical or subcritical. We will discuss this in the summary below.

The following theorem is one of the main results of this paper, which shows both the existence and nonuniqueness of weak solutions in
C_{t}L^{p}
for
p<2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
1\leq p<2
and let
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be divergence free and
e:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be strictly positive and smooth.

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist a weak solution
u
with initial data
u(0)=u_{0}
and regularity

u\in C([0,T];L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\quad\text{and}\quad u\in C((0,T];L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\quad\text{for all $p^{\prime}<2$},

and a Borel set
\mathcal{G}\subset[0,T]
with
\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,T]\setminus\mathcal{G})<\varepsilon
such that

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}=e(t),\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{G}.

Theorem
1.2
contributes to both the existence and nonuniqueness in the class
C_{t}L^{p}
for
1\leq p<2
. Thanks to the Lusin-type property of the kinetic energy of the constructed solutions, Theorem
1.2
is sharp in both of the following two aspects.

•

The regularity
C_{t}L^{p}
for any
1\leq p<2
is sharp: if
p=2
, then any
C_{t}L^{2}
weak solution is unique in the class
C_{t}L^{2}
with the same initial data;

•

The size of set
\mathcal{G}
is optimal, namely,
[0,T]\setminus\mathcal{G}
can not be of measure zero: if
\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,T]\setminus\mathcal{G})=0
and
\|e\|_{L^{\infty}}
were small, then
u
would have a small
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}
norm, which implies uniqueness
[
GP02
]
.

1.2.
Summary of existence and uniqueness results

Before we introduce the main theorems, let us review the existing existence and uniqueness results for the 2D Navier-Stokes equations.

Since the classical work of Leray and Ladyzhenskaya, there has been a vast amount of literature on extending the existence and uniqueness results to a wider class of solutions.

Existence

Since the global existence of Leray-Hopf solutions
[
Ler34
]
, the existence theory for 2D Navier-Stokes equations mainly focused on two aspects: using the mild formulation for initial data in scale-invariant spaces or using the vorticity formulation in the case where the initial vorticity is a measure.

Using the mild formulation (
1.4
) for the velocity, in the celebrated work
[
KT01
]
Koch and Tataru showed the existence of a unique solution for small initial data
u_{0}\in\text{BMO}^{-1}
in dimensions
d\geq 2
. In 2D, Gallagher and Planchon
[
GP02
]
constructed global unique solutions in scale invariant Besov spaces
u_{0}\in B^{-1+2/p}_{p,q}
for any finite
p,q
. This was later extended by Germain
[
Ger05
]
to
u_{0}\in\text{VMO}^{-1}
, where the space
\text{VMO}^{-1}
is the closure of Schwartz functions in
\text{BMO}^{-1}
. We also mention related works
[
Cal90
,
GMS01
,
Maz03
]
and refer to
[
BCD11
,
LR16
]
for the background in Fourier analysis in line of research.

At the level of vorticity
\omega=\curl u
, the critical scaling corresponds to
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}
, which suggests the consideration of measure-valued vorticity. The first existence result in this direction dates back to
[
BEP85
,
GMO88
]
where the existence of weak solutions was shown when
\omega_{0}
is a Radon measure. We refer to
[
BA94
,
Cot86
,
Kat94
]
and reference therein for this line of research. Heuristically, such results would correspond to the velocity of scaling
u_{0}\in B^{-1+2/p}_{p,q}

for
p\leq 2
and
q=\infty
.

Uniqueness

For the velocity formulation, the known uniqueness results, which are usually corollaries of the existence results, can be summarized as follows: if
u\in C_{t}B^{-1+2/p}_{p,q}
with finite
p,q
, then
u
is unique in the class of weak solution with regularity
C_{t}B^{-1+2/p}_{p,q}
. It should be noted that even though the existence results in
[
KT01
,
Ger05
]
surpass
[
GP02
]
, the uniqueness in
[
KT01
,
Ger05
]
can not be stated with only one continuity condition in time.

The uniqueness of the vorticity formulation however does not follow from the construction of the solutions. Indeed, in
[
BEP85
,
GMO88
,
BA94
,
Cot86
,
Kat94
]
, the uniqueness results all have either smallness or structural assumptions. Building upon
[
GGL05
]
, this problem has finally been settled in
[
GG05
]
: if
\omega_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, then there is only one solution
\omega(t)
which is continuous in
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
for
t>0
.

The existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for the 2D Navier-Stokes equations have more or less matured except at the endpoint
B^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
, which is the largest critical space with respect to the scaling the Navier-Stokes equations, independent of the space dimensions. The current existence results in this space require extra assumptions (see for instance
[
CG06
,
CGP11
,
PZ14
]
and reference therein). It is also not clear at the moment whether weak solutions in
C_{t}B^{-1}_{\infty,\infty}
are unique, though other types of ill-posedness results are available in dimensions
d\geq 3

[
BP08
,
Ger08
,
CS10
]
.

Supported by the development of the existence and uniqueness theories so far, we can summarize the existence and uniqueness in a non-rigorous principle:

Existence and uniqueness may hold in the critical and sub-critical regime.

This heuristic principle has also been proven quite successful in view of the recent development of the negative results thanks to the convex integration technique.

Convex integration technique

Convex integration is a technique originated in isometric embedding problems of geometry dating back to the work of Nash
[
Nas54
]

[
Kui55

[

[

Very recently, in a groundbreaking work

1.4.
Main results

In this subsection, we list additional results, complementary to Theorem

1.4.1.
Density of energy near
t=0

First, in addition to the Lusin-type property for the kinetic energy, we have the density property (
1.5
) at
t=0
, which we state in the case of
L^{2}
initial data for simplicity.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be divergence free and
e:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be strictly positive, smooth and such that
e(0)=\|u_{0}\|_{2}
.

There exist a weak solution with initial data
u(0)=u_{0}
and regularity

u\in C([0,T];L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\quad\text{for all $p^{\prime}<2$},

and a Borel set
\mathcal{G}\subset[0,T]

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=e(t),\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{G},

and the density of
\mathcal{G}
at

is
1
:

(1.5)

1.4.2.
Extension to rough initial data

Our method of constructing weak solutions allows for initial data
u_{0}
to be much rougher than
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Such a supercritical regularity persists over the whole time interval and is supplemented with an additional smoothing effect, a gain of almost two derivatives.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any divergence free
u_{0}\in W^{s,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
s>-1
there are infinitely many solutions in the class

u\in C((0,T];W^{s^{\prime},1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\quad\text{for all $s^{\prime}<1$}.

Remark 1.5
.

A few remarks are in order.

(1)

The density estimate (
1.5
) is a much stronger result than the epsilon result in Theorem
1.2
: the kinetic energy of the solution will look more and more like

as

(2)

For every constructed solution,

\mathcal{T}_{j}\subset[0,1]

(3)

Note that

1.4.4.
2D Euler equations and the Onsager conjecture

In general, if a weak solution
u
of the Euler equations belongs to the dimension independent Onsager’s class
L^{3}([0,T];B^{1/3}_{3,c_{0}}
), then it conserves the kinetic energy
[
CCFS08
]
. The
1/3
-Hölder regularity is known as the Onsager threshold and has been proven to be sharp in dimensions
d\geq 3

[
Ise18
]
but not yet in 2D.

However, in two dimensions there are additional regularization mechanisms that prevent anomalous dissipation, cf.
[
CFLS16
,
CCS20
,
LMPP21
]
. For instance, as shown in
[
CFLS16
]
, weak solutions of the 2D Euler equations obtained as the vanishing viscosity limit of Leray-Hopf solutions of the 2D Navier-Stokes equations conserve energy provided
\omega_{0}\in L^{p}
for some
p>1
. We state a version of this result as Theorem
A.1
, and demonstrate its sharpness in the class of weak solutions of the Navier-Stokes equation in the following theorem, which is a consequence of the main iteration scheme.

Theorem 1.8
.

There exists a weak solution of the 2D Euler equations
u\in C([0,T];W^{s,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
for all
s<1
with
\curl u(0)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
p>1
that does not conserve energy, and a family
\{u^{\nu}\}
of weak solutions to the 2D Navier-Stokes equations with

\|u^{\nu}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\quad\nu\to 0,

for all
p^{\prime}<2
.

1.5.
Comments on the proof

Let us briefly explain the main ideas and difficulties of the proof. The main construction is based on a convex integration scheme that consists of adding highly oscillatory and concentrated perturbations to produce a weak solution of the 2D Navier-Stokes equations in the limit and an energy pumping mechanism that allows us to achieve the Lusin-type property for the kinetic energy profile.

One of the biggest difficulties in any convex integration scheme designed for the Navier-Stokes equations is the presence of the Laplacian, which in 2D makes the equations
L^{2}
-critical. This is the very reason that we need to work in a regularity setting below this threshold. The framework of intermittent convex integration developed in
[
BV19
]
only works above the regularity level
C_{t}L^{2}
which is not sufficient for 2D Navier-Stokes equations.

The main building block in the convex integration scheme employed in this paper is a family of accelerating jet flows that are oscillatory and intermittent in space as well as in time. These jet flows combine the advantages of the two constructions from
[
BCV22
]
and
[
CL22
]
, and can be fully intermittent both in space and in time, in contrast to the ones in
[
BCV22
]
and
[
CL22
]
. The main nonuniqueness results are the manifestation of the wild behaviors of such jet flows:

(1)

Accelerating jet flows are at rest for the majority of the time and only move on a union of small time intervals whose length models the strength of temporal intermittency;

(2)

In space, these flows are divergence-free and supported in small moving balls. The extreme spatial intermittency is needed for achieving the
C_{t}L^{p}
regularity for
p
close to
2
.

(3)

On each time interval in the temporal supports, the jet flows first accelerate, travel along the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with large speed, and then come to a rest. They are periodic in both space and time.

(4)

The jet flows solve the Navier-Stokes equations with a small
L^{1}_{t}W^{-1,1}
error. In other words, the “Reynolds stress” of these jet flows is small in
L^{1}_{t,x}
.

Once the main building blocks are obtained, we employ a space-time convex integration reducing the Reynolds stress to a highly oscillating in time error, which can be balanced by appropriate temporal and acceleration correctors, taking advantage of the space-time intermittency of the building blocks. To obtain nonuniqueness in the class
C_{t}L^{p}
for
p<2
, we optimize the parameters in the accelerating flows so that they are fully intermittent in space but only slightly intermittent in time. The membership in this class and reducing the stress error at the same time require a very delicate choice of parameters in the accelerating flows. Once the accelerating flows are in the class
C_{t}L^{p}
, it is then fairly straightforward to obtain nonuniqueness in
\cap_{p<2}C_{t}L^{p}
by a limiting argument in the iteration scheme, at least for smooth initial data.

To achieve nonuniqueness for rough initial data, we take advantage of the smoothing property of the fractional heat flow. Choosing the power of Laplacian large enough, we show that any divergence-free initial data
u_{0}\in W^{s,1}
for some
s>-1
admits a solution
(u,R)
of the Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system with the stress error
R\in L^{1}_{x,t}
, which is compatible with our space-time convex integration scheme. Thanks to the smoothing property of the fractional heat flow, we can build a solution of the 2D Navier-Stokes equations on top of
(u,R)
, approaching the initial time as iterations progress.

Another key ingredient needed for the Lusin-type property is an energy pumping mechanism, done separately from the convex integration step, which essentially exploits the fact that the smallness in
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}
falls in the subcritical regime of the 2D Navier-Stokes equations. Due to the time-intermittent nature of the convex integration scheme, the solution is mostly intact on a large portion of the time axis, what we call the good set
\mathcal{G}
, whose complement is of arbitrarily small measure. During the convex integration step, the perturbation can be made arbitrarily small on the good set
\mathcal{G}
in any Sobolev space. On the other hand, we are able to correct the solution on the good set
\mathcal{G}
to achieve desired properties, such as the
L^{2}
-continuity and gaining the prescribed energy level.

,

,

2.
Outline of the proof

2.1.
Notations

Throughout the paper, we fix our spatial domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which we often identify with a periodic square
[0,1]^{2}
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}+:=\mathbb{N}\setminus{0}
,
\mathcal{L}^{d}
denotes the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the Lebesgue spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the Lebesgue norms are denoted by

\|f\|_{p}=\|f\|_{L^{p}}=\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

where we do not distinguish scalar, vector or tensor valued functions. If the function
f:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is time-dependent, we write

\|f(t)\|_{p}=\|f(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})},

to indicate that the spatial norm is taken at each time slice
t\in[0,T]
.

The differentiation operations such as
\nabla
,
\Delta
, and
\D
are meant for differentiation in space only, while for differentiation in time we use
\partial_{t}
.

In general, repeated indexed are summed without mentioning when the meaning is clear in the context. For matrix-valued function
A:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, the usual (spatial) divergence is defined by

\D A=\partial_{j}A_{ij}.

For two vector valued functions
f,g:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the tensor product
\otimes
is given by
f\otimes g=f_{i}g_{j}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
\mathring{\otimes}
denotes the usual traceless tensor product
f\mathring{\otimes}g=f_{i}g_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}f_{k}g_{k}
.

For any function
f
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we say
f
is
\sigma^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{2}
-periodic if

f(x+\sigma^{-1}k)=f(x)\quad\text{for any $k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}$},

and for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the notation
f(\sigma\cdot)
for the function
x\mapsto f(\sigma x)
, so
f(\sigma\cdot)
is at least
\sigma^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{2}
-periodic.

We use intensively the notation
X\lesssim Y
which means
X\leq CY
for some constant
C>0
. Sometimes the constant will depend on various parameters and in that case we will write
X\lesssim_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\dots}Y
to indicate
X\leq CY
for some constant
C>0
depending on
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\dots
. The notation
\gtrsim
is similarly defined and
X\sim Y
means both
X\lesssim Y
and
X\gtrsim Y
at the same time.

2.2.
The Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system

The proof of our main theorems is based on the construction of a sequence of approximate solutions. These approximate solutions (of (
1.1
)) satisfy the so-called Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system:

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla p=\D R&\\
\D u=0,\end{cases}

where
R:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,T]\to\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{0}
is a
2\times 2
symmetric traceless matrix, usually termed as the Reynolds stress in the literature. We refer to the discussion in
[
BV21
]
for the motivation of this system and its relation to mathematical turbulence. In a nutshell, the Reynolds stress
R
measures the approximateness of the solutions to exact solutions of the Navier-Stokes equations.

In what follows, we will say
(u,R)
is a smooth solution to (
2.1
) on
I
if
u
and
R
satisfy (
2.1
) and are both smooth on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
for some interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. Note that the pressure is intentionally left out in this formulation since one can take the divergence of (
2.1
) to obtain the following elliptic equation

\Delta p=\D\D(R-u\otimes u),

which uniquely determines the pressure when coupled with the usual zero mean condition
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}p=0
.

2.3.
Universality of the Navier-Stokes-Reynolds flows

We prove the following auxiliary result used in the proof of Theorem
1.2
concerning the existence of (weak) solutions of (
2.1
) for general rough data.

Throughout the paper, we say
(u,R)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
) on
[0,T]
if

(2.2)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u_{0}(x)\varphi(x,0)\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]}(u\cdot\Delta\varphi+u\otimes u:\nabla\varphi+u\cdot\partial_{t}\varphi)\,dxdt-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]}R:\nabla\varphi\,dxdt

holds for all test functions
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{T}
(smooth, divergence-free, and vanishing on
t\geq T
).

Theorem 2.1
.

For any divergence-free
u_{0}\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
s>-1
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, there exists a weak solution
(u,R)
of (
2.1
) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,T]
such that
(u,R)
is smooth on
(0,T]
and

u\in C([0,T];W^{s,p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\quad\text{and}\quad R\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,T]).

In fact, the velocity can be chosen to be
u(t)=e^{-\nu t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u_{0}
for any
\alpha\geq 2
and
\nu>0
.

Proof.

Take
u(t)=e^{-\nu t(-\Delta)^{\alpha}}u_{0}
for some
\alpha\geq 2
,
\nu>0
, and define

(2.3)

R:=\mathcal{R}(\partial_{t}u-\Delta u)+u\mathring{\otimes}u,

where
\mathcal{R}
is the antidivergence defined in Appendix
C
. Note that the smoothness of
R
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,T]
follows from that of
u
.

To prove the claims, we estimate each term in (
2.3
). For any
0<t\leq 1
, by (
C.2
),
L^{q}
boundedness of the Riesz transform for some
q>1
, and well-known estimates for the fractional heat semigroup, we have

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{R}\partial_{t}u(t)\|_{L^{1}}=\|\mathcal{R}(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u(t)\|_{L^{1}}&\lesssim\|(-\Delta)^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{q}}\\
&\lesssim t^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1-s}{2})}\||\nabla|^{s}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}},\end{split}

which is integrable by choosing
q
sufficiently close to 1 when
\alpha>0
and
s>-1
;
and

\|\mathcal{R}\Delta u(t)\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\||\nabla|u(t)\|_{L^{q}}\lesssim t^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}(s-1+2(1-\frac{1}{q}))}\||\nabla|^{s}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}},

which is integrable by choosing
q
sufficiently close to 1 when
\alpha\geq 1
and
s>-1
;
as well as

\|u\mathring{\otimes}u(t)\|_{L^{1}}\lesssim\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim t^{\frac{s-1}{\alpha}}\||\nabla|^{s}u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}^{2},

which is integrable when
\alpha\geq 2
and
s>-1
;

∎

2.4.
The main iteration proposition

We now state the main proposition of the paper.

Proposition 2.2
.

There exist a geometric constant
M>0
such that for any
1\leq p<2
, there exists
r>1
depending only on
p
with the following properties.

Let
(u,R)
be a weak solution of (
2.1
) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
such that
(u,R)
is smooth on
(0,1]
. Given any
\delta>0
and any closed interval
I\subset(0,1]
, there exists another weak solution
(u_{1},R_{1})
of (
2.1
) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
and an open set
E\subset I
such that

(1)

The velocity perturbation
w:=u_{1}-u
is smooth on
[0,1]
,

\Supp_{t}w\subset I,

and

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{1}\big([0,t]\cap E\big)\leq t\delta,\qquad\text{for all $0\leq t\leq 1$.}

(2)

For any
t\not\in E

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|w(t)\|_{W^{\frac{1}{\delta},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Big|\|u_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}-\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}.

(3)

There hold the estimates,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\leq M\|R\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\delta.

(4)

The new stress error
R_{1}
satisfies

\|R_{1}\|_{L^{1}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\delta.

In the above proposition, the role of the closed interval
I\subset(0,1]
is to progressively get to
t=0
along the iterations in the proof. For the current paper, one should think of
I
as approximations of
(0,1]
. It also helps us to achieve the exact energy profile of the solution near
t=0
.

Remark 2.3
.

The velocity perturbation in the above proposition enjoys the following additional properties.

(1)

There exists disjoint open intervals
I_{n}\subset I
such that
E\subset\cup_{n}I_{n}
and a small
r>0
, which goes to zero as
\delta\to 0
, such that each
I_{n}
in
\cup_{n}I_{n}
is of length at most
r
, and the number of intervals
I_{n}
is bounded by
\lesssim r^{-\frac{1}{9}}
.

(2)

In fact, thanks to Corollary
4.6
, estimate (
2.8
) can be replaced by

\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;W^{s_{p},1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\delta,

for some
0<s_{p}<1
and
s_{p}\to 1
as
p\to 2
. This is used to prove (sharp) nonuniqueness in Sobolev spaces
L^{\infty}_{t}W^{s,1}
,
s<1
, in Theorem
1.4
.

2.5.
Proof of main nonuniqueness results

We prove Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
assuming Proposition
2.2
. Without loss of generality we assume
T=1
and
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof of Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.4
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we construct a sequence of solutions
(u_{n},R_{n})
, positive numbers
\delta_{n}\to 0
, monotone decreasing
t_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and open sets
E_{n}\subset[0,1]
such that
(u_{n},R_{n})
converges to the desired weak solution
u
, and the union of
E_{n}
gives
[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{G}
.

The solutions
(u_{n},R_{n})
and sets
E_{n}
, constructed by induction, will satisfy the following assumptions.

(a)

(u_{n},R_{n})
is a smooth solution of (
2.1
) on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,1]
;

(b)

The Reynolds stress is small:

\displaystyle\|R_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}\leq\delta_{n};

(c)

u_{n}
achieves the prescribed the energy level:

\displaystyle e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle>0\quad\text{for all $t\in(0,1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}$},

\displaystyle e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{n}^{2}}{100}\quad\text{for all $t\in[t_{n},1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}$},

where we define the bad set
\mathcal{B}_{n}\subset[0,1]
as the union of all
E_{j}
,
j\leq n
:

\mathcal{B}_{n}=\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq n}E_{j};

(d)

The sets
E_{n}
are unions of open intervals and satisfy

\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,t]\cap E_{n})\leq t\delta_{n},\qquad\forall\,0\leq t\leq 1.

Here, the parameters
p_{n}
and
\delta_{n}
are given explicitly as

\qquad p_{n}=2-2^{-n-1}\quad\text{for $n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}$ },\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{n}=\varepsilon 2^{-n}\leq 1\quad\text{if $n\geq 2$}.

The value of
\delta_{1}>0
and the sequence
t_{n}
are chosen separately after we specify the initial solution
(u_{1},R_{1})
in Step 1 below.

In addition, the perturbation
w_{n}:=u_{n}-u_{n-1}
satisfies for
n\geq 2
:

(i)

Regularity estimates:

\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}\delta_{n-1}^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p_{n}})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n-1},

where
C_{M}>0
depends only on the initial solution
(u_{1},R_{1})
and
the geometric constant
M
from Proposition
2.2
;

(ii)

Support away from the origin:

\displaystyle\Supp w_{n}

\displaystyle\subset(0,1];

(iii)

Small energy outside of
E_{n}
:

\|w_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq\delta_{n},\qquad t\in[t_{n},1]\setminus E_{n}.

Step 1: Initialization

We choose the initial solution
(u_{1},R_{1})
according to the given energy profile and
\varepsilon>0
in the main theorems. Take

u_{1}(t)=e^{-\nu t(-\Delta)^{2}}u_{0},

for some
\nu>0
that will be fixed large enough depending on
e(t)
. Thanks to
Theorem
2.1
, there exists a Reynolds stress
R_{1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])
such that
(u_{1},R_{1})
is a smooth solution to the Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system (
2.1
) on
(0,1]
. The associated pressure can be obtained by a routine computation.

Now we define
E_{1}=[0,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]
if
u_{0}\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
(recall that
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
), and
E_{1}=\emptyset
when
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Note that
\|u_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}}
is bounded on any compact subset of
(0,1]
, and, in the case
p=2
, we also have
\|u_{1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}e^{-\nu t}
. Thus, in either case, we can choose
\nu
large enough so that

\|u_{1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}<e(t),\quad\text{for all}\ \ t\in(0,T]\setminus E_{1}.

Let us point out that
u_{1}
is responsible for the difference in final regularity of
u
between the case
p<2
and
p=2
.

Since
R_{1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])
, we can specify the value of
\delta_{1}>1
as

(2.9)

\delta_{1}=1+100\max\big\{\|R_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])},\sup_{t\in[0,1]\setminus E_{1}}\big[e(t)-\|u_{1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\},

and choose a strictly decreasing sequence of
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, such that
t_{n}\to 0
and

(2.10a)

\displaystyle 100\|R_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,t_{n}])}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n},

(2.10b)

\displaystyle t_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|e\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{n}.

It follows that
(u_{1},R_{1})
satisfies all the conditions
(a)
–
(d)
. We also remark that condition (
2.10b
) is to get the energy density statement at
t=0
, so it is only used for the case
p=2
, as when
p<2
, we have
0\in E_{1}\subset\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

In the step 2–4 below, we will construct the solution
(u_{n},R_{n})
and the sets
E_{n}
for
n\geq 2
by induction.

Step 2: Energy correction
(u_{n},R_{n})\mapsto(\overline{u}_{n},\overline{R}_{n})

In this step, our main objective is to correct the energy level of
(u_{n},R_{n})
to obtain a new solution pair
(\overline{u}_{n},\overline{R}_{n})
with a slightly larger
\overline{R}_{n}
. This is done by adding a small energy corrector
\overline{w}_{n+1}
to
u_{n}
.

In view of inductive assumptions, the correction
\overline{w}_{n+1}:=\overline{u}_{n}-{u}_{n}
needs to improve the energy level of
u_{n}
on both
[t_{n+1},t_{n}]
and
[t_{n},1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}
. On the set
[t_{n},1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the energy is already quite close to
e(t)
due to
(c)
, and hence
\overline{w}_{n+1}
is small in
L^{2}
in this region. On
[t_{n+1},t_{n}]
, the correction might be larger than
\delta_{n}
since it is the first time we correct the energy there, but due to
t_{n}\to 0
as in (
2.10b
) the correction will be small in
L^{2}_{t}
norm. These observations ensure the needed convergence of
\overline{w}_{n+1}
whose estimates are verified in the next step.

Now we turn to the specific construction of
\overline{w}_{n+1}
. Throughout the iteration, we fix a function
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\Supp\psi\subset(0,1)^{2}
and rescale it by a large parameter
\mu>0
, still denoting the resulting function by
\psi
, so that

(2.11)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=1,\qquad\|\nabla^{n}\psi\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu^{n-1}\mu^{2(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})}\quad\text{for all $1\leq q\leq\infty$}.

We will use
\psi
as the stream function on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for the energy corrector
\overline{w}_{n+1}
. The exact value of
\mu
depends on many factors and will be specified later.

Now we can introduce a smooth nonnegative energy profile
\rho:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that all of the following holds.

(1)

\rho(t)
pumps the exact amount of energy outside of the bad set:

(2.12)

[\rho(t)]^{2}=\left(1-\frac{\delta_{n+1}^{2}}{600\|e\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}}\right)\left[e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\right]\quad\text{if $t\in[t_{n+1},1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}$};

(2)

\rho(t)
is under control on
[0,1]
:

(2.13)

[\rho(t)]^{2}<\begin{cases}e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\quad&\text{ if $t\in[t_{n+2},t_{n+1}]$}\\
\big\|e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\big\|_{L^{\infty}([t_{n},1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n})}\quad&\text{ if $t\in[t_{n},1]$};\end{cases}

(3)

\rho(t)
is supported away from
t=0
:

(2.14)

\rho(t)=0,\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,t_{n+2}]$}.

Such a profile
\rho(t)
exists thanks to the fact that
\mathcal{B}_{n}\subset[t_{n},1]
and the inductive assumption
(c)
, and we use it together with the stream function
\psi
to define the energy corrector
\overline{w}_{n+1}
:

\overline{w}_{n+1}=\rho(t)\nabla^{\perp}\psi.

We remark that in (
2.13
) we use
t_{n}
to separate the two cases as
\rho(t)
could be large on
[t_{n+1},t_{n}]
when comparing to
\delta_{n}
, cf. (
2.16
) below. The exact choice of
\rho(t)
is not substantial to our purpose as long as it fulfills (
2.12
)–(
2.14
). Immediately, we have

(2.15)

\|\overline{w}_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}=\rho(t),\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,1]$}.

In addition, by assumption
(c)
and (
2.13
), it follows that

(2.16)

0\leq\|\overline{w}_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{10}\delta_{n},\quad\text{for all $t\in[t_{n},1]$}.

The new solution
(\overline{u}_{n},\overline{R}_{n})
will then be defined as

\overline{u}_{n}:=u_{n}+\overline{w}_{n+1},

and

(2.17)

\overline{R}_{n}:=R_{n}+\mathcal{R}(-\Delta\overline{w}_{n+1}+\partial_{t}\overline{w}_{n+1})+(\overline{w}_{n+1}\mathring{\otimes}u_{n}+u_{n}\mathring{\otimes}\overline{w}_{n+1})+(\overline{w}_{n+1}\mathring{\otimes}\overline{w}_{n+1}).

Step 3: Estimates of
(\overline{u}_{n},\overline{R}_{n})
and
\overline{w}_{n+1}

In this step we establish the following estimates that will be passed to the next step. The estimates that we need are:

(2.18)

\displaystyle\|\overline{R}_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}\leq C\delta_{n},

where
C>0
is independent of
n
, as well as

(2.19a)

\displaystyle\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(0,1;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n},

(2.19b)

\displaystyle\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p_{n}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n},

and

(2.20a)

\displaystyle e(t)-\|\overline{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle>0\quad\text{for all $t\in(0,1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}$},

(2.20b)

\displaystyle 0<e(t)-\|\overline{u}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{500}\delta^{2}_{n+1}\quad\text{for all $t\in[t_{n+1},1]\setminus\mathcal{B}_{n}$}.

The easiest one is (
2.19b
), which follow directly from (
2.11
) and (
2.16
) by taking the free parameter
\mu
sufficiently large. For (
2.19a
), we consider the split

\displaystyle\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(0,1;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(0,t_{n};L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(t_{n},1;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}.

The bound for the first term follows from (
2.10b
),
the first part in (
2.13
), and (
2.14
):

\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(0,t_{n};L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq t_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{t\in[0,t_{n}]}\rho(t)\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{n},

while the the second term follows from (

\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(t_{n},1;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta_{n}}{10}.

Next, let us examine the energy level of

we have

\displaystyle\leq C(u_{n}){\rho(t)}\mu^{-1},

which tends to

\begin{split}e(t)-\|\overline{u}_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}&=e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}-\|\overline{w}_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle u_{n},\overline{w}_{n+1}\rangle.\end{split}

Next, we will use (
2.22

) and (

For (

\displaystyle e(t)-\|u_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}-\|\overline{w}_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}

The remaining task is to obtain the estimate of the new stress

\displaystyle\|\overline{w}_{n+1}\mathring{\otimes}\overline{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

Since

(2.25)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\psi}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\rho(t)+\sup_{t\in[t_{n},1]}\rho(t)\right)\leq C_{\psi}{\delta_{n}},

where in the last step we have used bounds (

We apply Proposition

, and the velocity perturbation

\|\widetilde{w}_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}\leq\delta_{n+1}/100\quad\text{for all $t\not\in E_{n+1}$}

Now by (
2.7
), (
2.8
), and (

)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]))}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\overline{R}_{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

(2.27a)

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot C^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{M}\delta_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2.27b)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{w}_{n+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p_{n}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n},

and

(2.28)

\displaystyle\|R_{n+1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[t_{n+1},1]))}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n+1}.

The pair
({u}_{n+1},{R}_{n+1})
solves equation (
2.1
) on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{2}
and the total stress error satisfies

\|R_{n+1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]))}\leq\|R_{n+1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,t_{n+1}]))}+\|R_{n+1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[t_{n+1},1]))},

which by (

) and (

) reduces to

\|R_{n+1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]))}\leq\delta_{n+1}.

In addition, due to (

), the energy level for any

satisfies

(2.29)

\displaystyle\Big|\|u_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}-\|\overline{u}_{n}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\Big|\leq\frac{\delta^{2}_{n+1}}{100^{2}}.

Then for the inductive assumption
(c)
, we first observe that
e(t)-\|u_{n+1}(t)\|_{2}^{2}>0
on

by the fact that

and (

). The same

holds also on

thanks to the definition of

and (

. Similarly, the second bound in follows from (
2.29
) and (
2.20b
)

Step 5: Verification of inductive assumptions

From Step 3 and Step 4, we see that
(u_{n+1},R_{n+1})
satisfies the listed items
(a)
–
(d)
at level

. To close the induction argument, we only need to verify items

,
(ii)
and

for the total perturbation

Now, we can conclude that:

•

Item

follows from (

•

Step 6: Passing to the limit

Finally, we will conclude that the constructed sequence
(u_{n},R_{n})
converges to a weak solution
u
of (
1.1
), and

satisfies all the listed properties in the statement of Theorem

.

We define the final weak solution

as the point-wise in time limit of

in

:

(2.30)

u(t):=\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}(t),

which is well-defined due to the inductive assumption

.
In other words,

(2.31)

u(t)=u_{1}(t)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}{w}_{n}(t).

Since we know that
u_{1}\in C([0,1];L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times(0,T])
, to show the claimed regularity of

it suffices to prove that

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}{w}_{n}\in C([0,1];L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})),\quad\text{for any $p^{\prime}<2$}.

Indeed, for each
p^{\prime}<2
we can find
N_{p^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{n}>p^{\prime}
provided
n\geq N_{p^{\prime}}
. Then by the inductive estimate
(i)
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\|{w}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{p^{\prime}}}\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\delta_{n-1}=0.

Sobolev space
L^{\infty}_{t}W^{s,1}
regularity in Theorem
1.4
similarly follows from estimates in Remark
2.3
.

Next, we show that the weak formulation holds. Indeed, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{T}
, using weak formulation of (
2.1
) or integrating by parts we obtain

(2.32)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u_{0}(x)\varphi(x,0)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]}(u_{n}\cdot\Delta\varphi+u_{n}\otimes u_{n}:\nabla\varphi+u_{n}\cdot\partial_{t}\varphi)\,dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]}R:\nabla\varphi\,dxdt.

Inductive assumptions
(a)
–
(d)
imply the convergence of all terms to their natural limits in (
2.32
). So

is a weak solution on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
.

Step 7: Final conclusion

The good set in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
is defined as

\mathcal{G}=[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{j\geq 1}E_{j}.

By inductive assumption
(c)
, the energy achieves the prescribed level on
\mathcal{G}
:

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=e(t),\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{G}.

Now by inductive assumption
(d)
and definition of
\delta_{n}
,

\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,1]\setminus\mathcal{G})\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{L}^{1}(E_{n})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\sum_{n\geq 2}\delta_{n}=\varepsilon,

which implies the Lusin-type property for the kinetic energy in Theorem
1.2
.

In the case when
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the set
E_{1}
is empty by definition. Recall also that
E_{n}\subset[t_{n},1]
for some sequence
t_{n}\to 0
. Hence, employing the inductive assumption
(d)
again, we obtain

t^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,t]\setminus\mathcal{G})\leq t^{-1}\sum_{n:t_{n}<t}\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,t]\cap E_{n})\leq t^{-1}\sum_{n:t_{n}<t}t\delta_{n}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
, since
t_{n}\to 0
. This establishes density property (
1.5
) in Theorem
1.3
.

Finally, we prove the second statement in
Remark
1.5
concerning the
L^{2}
-continuity of
u
. Define the bad set

\mathcal{B}=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\bigcup_{j\geq n}E_{j}.

By continuity of the Lebesgue measure from above,

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{B})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{L}^{1}\big(\bigcup_{j\geq n}E_{j}\big)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon 2^{1-n}=0.\end{split}

In fact, it follows from Remark
2.3
that the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{B}
is less than or equal to
\frac{1}{9}
. Note that

[0,1]\setminus\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{n},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{T}_{n}=\{0\}\cup[t_{n},1]\setminus\bigcup_{j\geq n}E_{j}.

Clearly
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are increasing, and by inductive assumption
(iii)
,

\|w_{j}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq\delta_{j},\qquad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\ j\geq n.

Since
\delta_{j}\to 0
, we have that
u
continuously maps
\mathcal{T}_{n}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which establishes the second item in
Remark
1.5
.

∎

2.6.
Proof of other main results

Here we sketch the minor modifications needed to prove Theorem
1.6
and Theorem
1.8
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We can use the same iteration as in Subsection
2.5
, but without the inductive items
(c)
and
(iii)
which are only related to the kinetic energy.

Take the initial solution
u_{1}
to be
v
and compute the associated Reynolds stress
R_{1}
. The smallness of
u-v
in any Sobolev norm is then a consequence (
2.5
), which we can add to the inductive assumptions for
(u_{n},R_{n})
. To conclude, we just delete Step 2 of energy correction from the proof and pass to the limit as before.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

We use a standard approximation argument. First, we note that Proposition
2.2
holds for the Euler equations, and hence we can construct a weak solution of the 2D Euler equations
u\in C([0,T];W^{s,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
for all
s<1
with
\curl u(0)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
p>1
that does not conserve energy.

Now for each value of the viscosity
0<\nu<1
we mollify the Euler solution
u
at a lengthscale
\sim\nu
to obtain a smooth vector field
v^{\nu}
which we use as the initial solution to the Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system
u_{1}:=v^{\nu}
with an appropriate Reynolds stress
R_{1}
. Omitting the energy correction step again, we can construct a solution of the Navier-Stokes equations
u^{\nu}
with

\|u^{\nu}-v^{\nu}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2-\nu}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\nu.

Hence the family of Navier-Stokes solutions
u^{\nu}
approaches
u
in the desired spaces:

\|u^{\nu}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\|u^{\nu}-v^{\nu}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{p^{\prime}}}+\|u^{\nu}-v^{\nu}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{p^{\prime}}}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\quad\nu\to 0,

for all
p^{\prime}<2
.

∎

3.
Proof of main proposition: velocity perturbation

In this section, we initiate the proof Proposition
2.2
. Typical in any convex integration scheme, the goal is to design a suitable velocity perturbation
w
so that the new solution

u_{1}:=u+w

solves the equation (
2.1
) with a much smaller Reynolds stress
R_{1}
.

The main objective here is to introduce the necessary preparation to define the velocity perturbation and the associated Reynolds stress. The full estimates claimed in Proposition
2.2
will be verified in the next section.

3.1.
Building blocks in space

The velocity perturbation will consist of suitable superpositions of a family of vector fields with coefficients depending smoothly on the given stress error
R
. We recall a crucial lemma from
[
DS17
]
which motivates the construction of our building blocks in the convex integration.

Recall that
\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{+}
denotes the set of positive definite symmetric
2\times 2
matrices and
\mathbf{e}_{k}:=\frac{k}{|k|}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, we use the norm
|A|:=\sup_{|x|=1}Ax
for definiteness (the choice is insignificant for our purpose).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
B_{\frac{1}{2}}(\Id)
denote the ball of radius
1/2
centered at identity Id in
\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{+}
. There exists a finite set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and smooth functions
\Gamma_{k}\in C^{\infty}({B}_{\frac{1}{2}}(\Id))
for any
k\in\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle R=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\Gamma_{k}^{2}(R)\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}\qquad\text{for all }\,R\in{B_{\frac{1}{2}}(\Id)}.

To utilize Lemma
3.1
, we need to choose proper cutoffs functions for the stress error
R
. Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a positive smooth function such that
\chi
is monotone increasing with respect to
|x|
and

(3.1)

\chi(x)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $0\leq|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}$}\\
2|x|&\text{if $|x|\geq\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}$}.\end{cases}

Let
\rho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])
be

\rho=\chi(R).

Then we see that

\Id-\frac{R}{\rho}\in B_{\frac{1}{2}}(\Id)\qquad\text{for any $(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]$,}

so we can use it as the arguments in the amplitude functions given by Lemma
3.1
. In particular, we have the following useful decomposition

(3.2)

\rho\Id-R=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}R_{k},

where the “primitive” stresses
R_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{+}
are given by

(3.3)

R_{k}:=\rho\Gamma_{k}^{2}\Big(\Id-\frac{R}{\rho}\Big)\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}.

The main building block of the convex integration scheme is a family of periodic accelerating jet flows
\mathbf{W}_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This construction builds on previous work of
Buckmaster, Colombo, and Vicol
[
BCV22
]

and our temporal intermittency framework used in
[
CL21
,
CL22
]
.

We first construct a family of autonomous vector fields
W_{k}
and then use intermittent temporal phase shifts
\Phi_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
to obtain the final jets
\mathbf{W}_{k}:=W_{k}\circ\Phi_{k}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Stationary jets)
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a finite set. There exists
\mu_{0}>0
such that for any
\nu,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
with
\mu_{0}<\nu\leq\mu
the following holds.

For every
k\in\Lambda
, there exist smooth vector fields
W_{k},W_{k}^{(c)}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
with the following properties.

(1)

Each
W_{k}+W_{k}^{(c)}
is divergence-free on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and there is a stream function
\Psi_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that

W_{k}+W_{k}^{(c)}=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi_{k},

and for each
k\in\Lambda

\mathbf{e}_{k}\parallel W_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{e}_{k}\perp W_{k}^{(c)},

where
\mathbf{e}_{k}=\frac{k}{|k|}
.

(2)

Each
W_{k}
has disjoint support

\Supp W_{k}\cap\Supp{W}_{k^{\prime}}=\emptyset\quad\text{if $k\neq k^{\prime}$}.

and for any
k\in\Lambda
, the following identities hold

(3.4)

\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}W_{k}\otimes W_{k}=\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k},

(3.5)

\D(W_{k}\otimes W_{k})=\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla|W_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}.

(3)

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the estimates

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|W_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+{\mu}^{-1}\|\nabla W_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},

\displaystyle\|W_{k}^{(c)}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+{\mu}^{-1}\|\nabla W_{k}^{(c)}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\nu\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},

and

(3.7)

\|\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}},

holds uniformly in
\nu,\mu
.

Proof.

We choose a collection of distinct points
p_{k}\in[0,1]^{2}
for
k\in\Lambda
and a number
\mu_{0}>0
such that

\bigcup_{k\in\Lambda}B_{\frac{2}{\mu_{0}}}(p_{k})\subset[0,1]^{2},

where
B_{\frac{2}{\mu_{0}}}(p_{k})
denotes the ball of radius
\frac{2}{\mu_{0}}
and center
p_{k}
and

(3.8)

\dist(p_{i},p_{j})>\frac{2}{\mu_{0}}\quad\text{if}\quad i\neq j.

The points
p_{i}
will be the centers of
W_{k}
and
W_{k}^{c}
.

For
k\in\Lambda
, let us introduce unit vectors
\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(3.9)

\mathbf{e}_{k}=\frac{k}{|k|},\qquad\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\perp}=\frac{1}{|k|}(-k_{2},k_{1}),

and their associated coordinates: for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle=(x-p_{k})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k},

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=(x-p_{k})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\perp}.

Now we choose compactly supported nontrivial
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((-\frac{1}{\mu_{0}},\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}))
and define non-periodic potentials
\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and vector fields
\widetilde{W}_{k},\widetilde{W}_{k}^{(c)}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}

\displaystyle=c\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi(\nu x_{k})\psi(\mu y_{k}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{k}

\displaystyle=-c(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi(\nu x_{k})\psi^{\prime}(\mu y_{k})\mathbf{e}_{k},

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{k}^{(c)}

\displaystyle=c\nu\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi^{\prime}(\nu x_{k})\psi(\mu y_{k})\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\perp},

where
c>0
is a normalizing constant such that (
3.4
) holds.
Periodizing, we obtain

\Psi_{k}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{k}(x+n),\quad W_{k}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widetilde{W}_{k}(x+n),\quad W_{k}^{(c)}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widetilde{W}_{k}^{(c)}(x+n),

which will be used as periodic building blocks, as we can now identify them with corresponding functions on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
:

\Psi_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad W_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad W_{k}^{(c)}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Note that

\begin{split}\nabla^{\perp}\Psi_{k}&=-\partial_{y_{k}}\Psi_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}+\partial_{x_{k}}\Psi_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\perp}\\
&=W_{k}+W_{k}^{(c)},\end{split}

and the rest of the conclusions follow trivially by direct computations as well.

∎

Remark 3.3
.

It is clear that the derivative of
\Psi_{k}
in the direction
\mathbf{e}_{k}
is of order
\nu
(rather than
\mu
for the full gradient):

(3.10)

\|(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla)\Psi_{k}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\nu\cdot\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}.

This fact will be used in Lemma
4.8
later.

Apparently,
W_{k}
or
W_{k}+W_{k}^{(c)}
are not approximate solutions of the Navier-Stokes (or Euler) equations due to (
3.5
). This error term can not be absorbed by a pressure gradient due to its anisotropic nature. However, this very identity (
3.5
) allows for letting
W_{k}
travel along the geodesics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
so that (
3.5
) can be balanced by the acceleration of a small corrector.

Letting
W_{k}
move along the geodesics creates one addition problem in 2D: without a suitable discretization of the temporal velocity, different
W_{k}
might collide with each other and thus create harmful interactions. We will use a temporal concentration mechanism to get around this issue.

3.2.
Phase shift by acceleration

Next, we introduce a simple method to avoid the collision of the support sets of different
W_{k}
. This is based on our previous construction of temporal concentration in
[
CL22
]
. We concentrate each
W_{k}
on disjoint time intervals so that they have disjoint supports in
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
. The size of those time intervals then determines the level of temporal concentration.

Now we turn to the specifics. Let us first choose temporal functions
g_{k}
and
h_{k}
to oscillate the building blocks
W_{k}
intermittently in time.
Let
G\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,1)
be such that

(3.11)

\int_{0}^{1}G^{2}(t)\,dt=1,\qquad\int_{0}^{1}G(t)\,dt=0.

For any
\kappa\geq 1
, we define
\tilde{g}_{k}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
as the
1
-periodic extension of
\kappa^{1/2}G(\kappa(t-t_{k}))
, where
t_{k}
are chosen so that
g_{k}
have disjoint supports for different
k
. In other words,

(3.12)

\tilde{g}_{k}(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\kappa^{1/2}G(n+\kappa(t-t_{k})).

In the convex integration scheme below, we will also oscillate the velocity perturbation at a large frequency
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
. So we define

(3.13)

g_{k}(t)=\tilde{g}_{k}(\sigma t).

Note that
g_{k}
concentrates on different small time intervals, and we have

(3.14)

\|g_{k}\|_{W^{n,p}([0,1])}\lesssim(\sigma\kappa)^{n}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}\quad\text{for all $1\leq p\leq\infty$},\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

Remark 3.4
.

With
g_{k}
defined, we will essentially use
g_{k}W_{k}
as the “building blocks” for the convex integration. Due to the temporal concentration of
g_{k}
, all
g_{k}W_{k}
have disjoint supports on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
, and hence there will be no interference between each different
W_{k}
.

For the corrector term that we will be using to balance (
3.5
), define
h_{k}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

h_{k}(t)=\int_{0}^{\sigma t}(\tilde{g}_{k}^{2}(s)-1)\,ds.

In view of the zero-mean condition for
g_{k}^{2}(t)-1
, these
h_{k}
are
\sigma^{-1}
-periodic on
[0,1]
and we have

(3.15)

\|h_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leq 1

uniformly in
\kappa
.
Moreover, we have the identity

(3.16)

\partial_{t}\left(\sigma^{-1}h_{k}\right)=g_{k}^{2}-1,

which will imply the smallness of the temporal oscillation corrector, cf (
3.29
).

3.3.
Accelerating jets

Let us recall the five parameters used for the building blocks.

•

spatial concentrations
\mu\geq 1
and
\nu\geq 1
: these are reciprocals of the longitudinal and lateral dimension of the building blocks
W_{k}
.

•

Oscillation
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
: we use it to oscillate the “building blocks”
g_{k}W_{k}
both in space and in time so that the stress error
R
is canceled weakly.

•

Temporal concentration
\kappa\geq 1
: this parameter models the concentration in time of the perturbation (through
g_{k}
), which is crucial for controlling the new stress error emanating from the Laplacian.

•

Acceleration
\omega\geq 1
: this parameter represents the acceleration of the flow in the building block, we use it to define the phase shift in (
3.18
).

To make our notation more compact, let us introduce the phase function
\Phi_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
defined by

(3.17)

\Phi_{k}:(x,t)\mapsto\sigma x+\phi_{k}(t)\mathbf{e}_{k},

where
\phi_{k}(t)
is defined by the relation

(3.18)

\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)=\omega g_{k}(t).

Note that due to the zero-mean condition in (
3.11
), such
\phi_{k}
always exists and for definiteness, we fix one of such choices throughout the construction.

By design,
\Phi_{k}
is
\sigma^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{2}
-periodic in space and
\sigma^{-1}
-periodic in time. Also, by definition of the phase shift (
3.18
), we have the important identities

(3.19)

\displaystyle\nabla\left(f\circ\Phi_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\nabla f\circ\Phi_{k},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(f\circ\Phi_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{k}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla f\right)\circ\Phi_{k}=\sigma^{-1}\omega g_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla\left(f\circ\Phi_{k}\right).

Now we will let the stationary flows
W_{k}
travel along
\mathbb{T}^{2}
in time, relating the velocity of the moving support sets to the intermittent oscillator
g_{k}
. More precisely, we define

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{k}(x,t):=W_{k}\circ\Phi_{k}=W_{k}(\sigma x+\phi_{k}(t)\mathbf{e}_{k}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(c)}(x,t):=W_{k}^{(c)}\circ\Phi_{k}=W_{k}^{(c)}(\sigma x+\phi_{k}(t)\mathbf{e}_{k}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}(x,t):=\Psi_{k}\circ\Phi_{k}=\Psi_{k}(\sigma x+\phi_{k}(t)\mathbf{e}_{k}).

Hence

(3.21)

\sigma^{-1}\nabla^{\perp}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}=\mathbf{W}_{k}+\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(c)}.

Also, thanks to (
3.19
) and (
3.5
), we have the important identities

(3.22)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}&=\sigma^{-1}\omega g_{k}\D(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k})\end{split},

and

(3.23)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}&=\sigma^{-1}\omega g_{k}(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\Psi}_{k},\end{split}

3.4.
The velocity perturbation

We are in the position to define the velocity perturbation. In summary, the perturbation consists of three parts:

(3.24)

w=w^{(p)}+w^{(c)}+w^{(t)},

where
w^{(p)}
is the principle part, accounting for the main contribution in the nonlinear term,
w^{(c)}
is an incompresiblity corrector, rectifying the divergence of
w^{(p)}
, and
w^{(t)}
is a temporal corrector with zero divergence, balancing the errors introduced by (
3.5
) and (
3.17
) below.

Next, we choose a cutoff for the velocity perturbation so that it lives strictly within the interval
I
. Since the stress error might accumulate near the endpoints of
I
, the cutoff will be sufficiently sharp so that the “leftover” error is negligible.
Let
\theta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
be a smooth temporal cutoff function such that
\|\theta\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
and

(3.25)

\theta(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\dist(t,I^{c})\geq\frac{\delta}{8\|R\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}}\\
0&\text{if }\dist(t,I^{c})\leq\frac{\delta}{16\|R\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}}.\end{cases}

In view of (
3.3
), the amplitude functions of the perturbation are given by

(3.26)

a_{k}=\theta\rho^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma_{k}\Big(\Id-\frac{R}{\rho}\Big).

The principle part of the perturbation consists of super-positions of the building blocks
\mathbf{W}_{k}
oscillating with period
\sigma^{-1}
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and traveling with a velocity
\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)
.

(3.27)

w^{(p)}(x,t):=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}(x,t)g_{k}(t)\mathbf{W}_{k}.

In what follows, we will omit the set of the summation indexes
k\in\Lambda
so that

w^{(p)}=\sum_{k}a_{k}g_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}.

Note that (
3.27
) is not divergence-free. To fix this, we introduce a divergence-free corrector

w^{(c)}(x,t):=\sum_{k}a_{k}(x,t)g_{k}(t)\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(c)}+\sigma^{-1}\nabla^{\perp}a_{k}(x,t)g_{k}(t)\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}.

Then, thanks to (
3.21
), we have

(3.28)

\begin{split}w^{(p)}+w^{(c)}&=\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}g_{k}(t)\left[a_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}+\nabla^{\perp}a_{k}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\right]\\
&=\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\big[a_{k}g_{k}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big].\end{split}

Finally, we define a temporal corrector
w^{(t)}
. The goal of this corrector
w^{(t)}
is two-fold:
(i)
to balance the high temporal frequency part of the stress error; and
(ii)
to balance the error in the interaction due to the acceleration in the phase shifts
\Phi_{k}
.

To introduce these correctors, we first recall the well-known Leray projection on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Definition 3.5

(Leray projection)
.

Let
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a smooth vector field. Define the operator
\mathcal{Q}
as

\mathcal{Q}v:=\nabla f+\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}v,

where
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is the unique smooth zero-mean solution of

\Delta f=\D v,\qquad x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

Furthermore, let
\mathcal{P}=\Id-\mathcal{Q}
be the Leray projection onto divergence-free vector fields with zero mean.

The temporal corrector
w^{(t)}
is defined as

(3.29)

w^{(t)}=w^{(o)}+w^{(a)},

where
w^{(o)}
is the temporal oscillation corrector

(3.30)

\displaystyle w^{(o)}(x,t)

\displaystyle:=-\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\mathcal{P}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}(t)\D R_{k},

and
w^{(a)}
is the acceleration corrector

(3.31)

w^{(a)}(x,t):=-\omega^{-1}\sigma\mathcal{P}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}(x,t)^{2}g_{k}(t)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}.

Let us say a few words about the corrector
w^{(t)}
. The corrector
w^{(t)}
will be used to balance part of the new stress error through its temporal derivative
\partial_{t}w^{(t)}
, but the roles of each part
w^{(o)}
and
w^{(a)}
are very different. To see the leading order temporal derivative of
w^{(a)}
,
thanks to fact that
\phi^{\prime}(t)=\omega g(t)
and due to (
3.22
), we have the heuristic

\partial_{t}w^{(a)}=-\mathcal{P}\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\D\big(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\big)+\text{lower order terms},

which is needed for a cancellation in the oscillation error. On the other hand, thanks to (
3.16
),

\partial_{t}w^{(o)}=-\theta^{2}\mathcal{P}\Big(\sum_{k\in\Lambda}(g_{k}^{2}-1)\D R_{k}\Big)+\text{lower order terms},

which we use to cancel a highly time oscillating remainder of the Reynolds stress, specific to the use of temporal intermittency in our scheme. We compute this in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
, there holds

\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\,dx=\theta^{2}\left(\rho\Id-R\right)+\theta^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}(g_{k}^{2}-1)R_{k},

where
R_{k}
is defined by (
3.3
).

Proof.

First we note that by Theorem
3.2
, for any
t
,

\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathbf{W}_{k}(x,t)\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}(x,t)\,dx=\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}W_{k}(x)\otimes W_{k}(x)\,dx=\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}.

So by Lemma
3.1
, a direct computation gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\rho\sum_{k\in\Lambda}g_{k}^{2}\Gamma_{k}^{2}\Big(\Id-\frac{R}{\rho}\Big)\mathbf{e}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}R_{k}+\theta^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}(g_{k}^{2}-1)R_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\left(\rho\Id-R\right)+\theta^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}(g_{k}^{2}-1)R_{k}.

∎

3.5.
The new Reynolds stress

In this subsection, our goal is to design a suitable stress tensor
R_{1}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{0}
such that the pair
(u_{1},R_{1})
is a smooth solution of
2.1
for a suitable smooth pressure
p_{1}
.

We will define the new Reynolds stress by

R_{1}=R_{\Lin}+R_{\Cor}+R_{\Osc},

such that

(3.32)

\displaystyle\D R_{\Osc}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}w^{(t)}+\D(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+R)+\nabla P,

(3.33)

\displaystyle\D R_{\Cor}

\displaystyle=\D\left((w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\otimes w+w^{(p)}\otimes(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\right),

(3.34)

\displaystyle\D R_{\Lin}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})-\Delta u+\D\left(u\otimes w+w\otimes u\right).

To this end, we will use the antidivergence operator
\mathcal{R}:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{0})
defined in Appendix
C
. True to its name, this antidivergence operator
\mathcal{R}
satisfies

(3.35)

\D(\mathcal{R}v)=v-\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}v\quad\text{for any $v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

Since (
3.32
)-(
3.34
) are time-dependent, it should be understood that the antidivergence
\mathcal{R}
is applied for each time slice
t\in[0,1]
, and the resulting stresses are smooth on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]
.

In addition, we will also use a bilinear antidivergence operator
\mathcal{B}
in Appendix
C
, which satisfies

(3.36)

\displaystyle\D(\mathcal{B}(v,A))=vA-\fint vA,\quad\text{for $v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})$ and $A\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})$},

where we denote
vA=v_{j}A_{ij}
instead of the usual
Av
. In what follows the matrix
A
is often symmetric, so there will be no confusion in writing
vA
versus
Av
.

This bilinear antidivergence
\mathcal{B}
has the advantage of gaining derivative from the second argument
A
when it only has high spatial frequencies. This has a flavor of the classical stationary phase and has been a useful tool for many estimates in convex integration
[
BMS21
]
.

3.6.
Computing the oscillation error

Our first step of solving for
R_{1}
is to derive
R_{\Osc}
. Let us compute the nonlinear term
\D(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+R)
. Due to (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) (see Remark
3.4
), we have

\Supp g_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}\cap\Supp g_{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{W}_{k^{\prime}}=\emptyset\quad\text{if $k\neq k^{\prime}$}.

It follows that we only have self-interactions of the accelerating jets:

\D(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+R)=\D\Big[\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}+R\Big].

We use Lemma
3.6
to remove the leading order interaction given by the spatial mean of
\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}
and obtain

(3.37)

\begin{split}\D(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+R)&=\D\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\big(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}-\fint\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\big)+\nabla(\theta^{2}\rho)+(1-\theta^{2})\D R\\
&\qquad+\theta^{2}\sum_{k}(g^{2}_{k}-1)\D R_{k}.\end{split}

Putting
\partial_{t}w^{(t)}
into action, using
\mathcal{P}=\Id-\mathcal{Q}
and the product rule, we separate the terms into four different groups:

(3.38)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{(t)}+

\displaystyle\D(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+R)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{k}g_{k}^{2}\nabla(a_{k}^{2})\cdot\big(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}-\fint\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\big)+\mathcal{Q}\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\D(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k})}_{=:E_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\partial_{t}w^{(a)}+\mathcal{P}\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\D(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k})}_{=:E_{2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\partial_{t}w^{(o)}+\theta^{2}\sum_{k}(g^{2}_{k}-1)\D R_{k}}_{=:E_{3}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\nabla(\theta^{2}\rho)+(1-\theta^{2})\D R}_{=:E_{4}}.

Before we move on to the analysis of each term
E_{i}
, let us point out the following simple consequence of (
3.35
) and the definition of
\mathcal{P}
:

(3.39)

\mathcal{P}f=\D(Rf)-\nabla(\Delta^{-1}\D f)\quad\text{for any $f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})$},

where we note that
f
does not need to be mean-free.
We will use (
3.36
) and (
3.39
) to re-write each
E_{i}
as the sum of the divergence of a stress and the gradient of a pressure.

Analysis of
E_{1}
:

Recall that by Definition
3.5
,

(3.40)

\mathcal{Q}v=\nabla\Delta^{-1}\D v+\fint v,

and consequently
\fint\mathcal{Q}v=\fint v
, which implies that
E_{1}
has zero spatial mean.
Therefore, if we define

(3.41)

R_{\Osc,x}=\sum_{k\in\Lambda}g_{k}^{2}\mathcal{B}\Big(\nabla(a_{k}^{2}),\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}-\fint\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\Big),

and the pressure

p_{1}=\Delta^{-1}\D\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\D\big(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\big),

then by (
3.36
) and (
3.40
) we have

(3.42)

E_{1}=\D R_{\Osc,x}+\nabla p_{1},

where the cancellation of the means follow from the fact that
\fint E_{1}=0
.

Analysis of
E_{2}
:

We use (
3.31
) and (
3.22
) to compute

\partial_{t}w^{(a)}=-\mathcal{P}\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{2}\D(\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k})-\omega^{-1}\sigma\mathcal{P}\sum_{k}\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}.

which implies that

(3.43)

\begin{split}E_{2}&=-\omega^{-1}\sigma\mathcal{P}\sum_{k}\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}.\end{split}

In view of (
3.39
), we define an approximation stress

(3.44)

R_{\Osc,a}=-\omega^{-1}\sigma\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\mathcal{R}\left(\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\right)

and a pressure term

p_{2}=\omega^{-1}\sigma\Delta^{-1}\D\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k},

such that

E_{2}=\D R_{\Osc,a}+\nabla p_{2}.

Analysis of
E_{3}
:

For this term, let us first compute the time derivative of
w^{(o)}
(
3.30
) using (
3.16
):

\partial_{t}w^{(o)}=-\theta^{2}\mathcal{P}\Big(\sum_{k\in\Lambda}(g_{k}^{2}-1)\D R_{k}\Big)-\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\mathcal{P}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}\D\partial_{t}R_{k}.

Thus

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}w^{(o)}+\theta^{2}\sum_{k}(g^{2}_{k}-1)\D R_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\mathcal{Q}\sum_{k}(g^{2}_{k}-1)\D R_{k}-\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\mathcal{P}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}\D\partial_{t}R_{k}.

Using definition of
\mathcal{Q}
and (
3.39
) once again, we define

(3.45)

p_{3}=\theta^{2}\sum_{k}(g^{2}_{k}-1)\Delta^{-1}\D\D R_{k}+\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}\Delta^{-1}\D\D\partial_{t}R_{k}

and

(3.46)

R_{\Osc,t}=-\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\mathcal{R}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}\D\partial_{t}R_{k}

to obtain

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=\D R_{\Osc,t}+\nabla p_{3}.

Analysis of
E_{4}
:

We just define

(3.47)

p_{4}=\theta^{2}\rho,

and

R_{\Rem}=(1-\theta^{2})R.

Then

\displaystyle E_{4}

\displaystyle=\D R_{\Rem}+\nabla p_{4}.

Combining all the terms, we have thus proven:

Lemma 3.7
.

Define the oscillation error
R_{\Osc}

R_{\Osc}=R_{\Osc,x}+R_{\Osc,t}+R_{\Osc,a}+R_{\Rem}.

and the pressure
P:=-p_{1}-p_{2}-p_{3}-p_{4}
.
Then

\partial_{t}w^{(t)}+\D(w^{(p)}\otimes w^{(p)}+R)+\nabla P=\D R_{\Osc}.

3.7.
Finalizing the new solution

Finally, we define the correction error and the linear error in the usual way:

(3.48)

R_{\Cor}=\mathcal{R}\D\big((w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\otimes w+w^{(p)}\otimes(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\big),

and

(3.49)

R_{\Lin}=\mathcal{R}\big(\partial_{t}(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})-\Delta u+\D(u\otimes w)+\D(w\otimes u)\big).

Now we can conclude the construction of the new solution to the Navier-Stokes-Reynolds system.

Lemma 3.8
.

Define the new Reynolds stress by

R_{1}=R_{\Lin}+R_{\Cor}+R_{\Osc},

and the new pressure by

p_{1}=p+P.

Then
(u_{1},R_{1})
is a solution to (
2.1
),

\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta u_{1}+\D(u_{1}\otimes u_{1})+\nabla p_{1}=\D R_{1}.

Proof.

Since
(u,R)
solves (
2.1
) with pressure
p
, a direct computation gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta u_{1}+

\displaystyle\D(u_{1}\otimes u_{1})+\nabla p_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+\D(u\otimes u)+\nabla p

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}w-\Delta w+\D(u\otimes w)+\D(w\otimes u)+\D(w\otimes w)+\nabla P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\D R+\partial_{t}w-\Delta w+\D(u\otimes w)+\D(w\otimes u)+\D(w\otimes w)+\nabla P.

Now by Lemma
3.7
and definitions (
3.48
), (
3.49
) we conclude that
(u_{1},R_{1})
solves (
2.1
).
∎

4.2.
Estimates on velocity perturbation

Lemma 4.2
.

The coefficients

are smooth on

and

\|a_{k}\|_{C^{n}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}\leq C_{u,n}\quad\text{for any $n\in\mathbb{N}$, }

where

are constants independent of

.
In addition, the bound

\|a_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\theta(t)\Big(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\,dx\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

holds uniformly for all time

.

Proof.

The smoothness of
a_{k}
follows from definition (
3.26
). Since the implicit constant is allowed to depend on

, the first bound follows immediately from the definition of
a_{k}
.

The second bound follows from the definition of

as well:

\|a_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\theta\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho\Gamma_{k}^{2}\left(\Id-\frac{R}{\rho}\right)\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\theta\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

The principle part
w^{(p)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}\lesssim\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+C_{u}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}},

and

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma}.

In particular, for sufficiently large
\lambda
,

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|R\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)},

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

Proof.

We first show the estimate for
L^{2}_{t,x}
and then for
L^{\infty}L^{p}
.

L^{2}_{t,x}
estimate:

For each
t\in[0,1]
, we take
L^{2}
norm in space and use Lemma
B.1
to obtain

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\sum_{k}g_{k}\big(\|a_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\|\mathbf{W}_{k}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}C_{u}\big),

where we used the fact that
\mathbf{W}_{k}
is
\sigma^{-1}\mathbb{T}^{2}
-periodic in space.
Recall that
\|\mathbf{W}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}}\lesssim 1
. Then using Lemma
4.2
and taking
L^{2}
norm in time gives

(4.11)

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}\lesssim\sum_{k}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}^{2}\theta^{2}(t)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\,dx\,dt\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+C_{u}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Notice that

t\mapsto\theta^{2}(t)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\,dx

is a smooth map on
[0,1]
and
g_{k}(t)
is
\sigma^{-1}
-periodic. Thus, we may apply Lemma
B.1
once again (with
p=1
) to obtain that

(4.12)

\int_{0}^{1}g_{k}^{2}\theta^{2}(t)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\,dx\,dt\lesssim\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}+C_{u}\sigma^{-1},

where we used the fact that
\int g_{k}^{2}=1
,
\Supp\theta\subset I
, and the bound

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\rho(x,t)\,dx\lesssim\|R(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}.

Thus, combining (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) gives

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}^{\frac{1}{2}}+C_{u}\sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}
estimate:

For each
t\in[0,1]
, we take
L^{p}
norm in space and then use Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\sum_{k}\|a_{k}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}|g_{k}(t)|\|\mathbf{W}_{k}(t)\|_{L^{p}}.

Now, thanks to Lemma
4.2
, (
3.14
), and Theorem
3.2
, taking

in time, we get

\displaystyle\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k}\|a_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,t}}\|g_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{u}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

where we used (
4.2
) for the last inequality.
∎

The last estimate of the velocity perturbation concerns the temporal corrector

.

Proposition 4.4
.

The temporal corrector
w^{(t)}=w^{(o)}+w^{(a)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1;L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

\displaystyle\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

\displaystyle\|w^{(o)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;W^{\frac{1}{\delta},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

where

is as defined in (

).

In particular, for all sufficiently large

,

\displaystyle\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\leq\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)},

\displaystyle\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{4},

\displaystyle\|w^{(o)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;W^{\frac{1}{\delta},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

Proof.

Recall that the temporal corrector

consists of the temporal oscillation corrector

(

) and the acceleration corrector

(

\displaystyle w^{(o)}

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\mathcal{P}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}\D R_{k},

\displaystyle w^{(a)}

\displaystyle=-\omega^{-1}\sigma\mathcal{P}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}a_{k}^{2}g_{k}|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}.

The estimate of

Now we turn to the

and

∎

Then divergence-free corrector

\displaystyle\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{2}(0,1;L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

In particular, for sufficiently large

,

\displaystyle\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])}

\displaystyle\leq\|R\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)},

\displaystyle\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

Proof.

Using Theorem

, for any

and

, there holds

\begin{split}\|w^{(c)}(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}&\lesssim\sum_{k}\|a_{k}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}|g_{k}(t)|\|\mathbf{W}_{k}^{(c)}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}}+\sigma^{-1}\|\nabla^{\perp}a_{k}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}|g_{k}(t)|\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}}\\

Recall that

due to (

).

∎

4.3.
Sobolev estimates and the exceptional set
E

Finally we show the Sobolev estimates in
L^{\infty}(0,1;W^{s_{p},1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
and specify the exceptional set
E
with the estimates (
2.4
)–(
2.6
) in the main proposition.

The proof of the Sobolev estimate follows closely the estimation we have done so far, therefore we only sketch the outline.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
s_{p}:=1-40\gamma
. Then for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, there holds

\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;W^{s_{p},1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\delta.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.1
and the previous estimations, it is clear that the estimate of
w
is majorized by
w^{(p)}
. By a Sobolev interpolation, for any
t\in[0,1]
,

\|w^{(p)}(t)\|_{W^{s_{p},1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|w^{(p)}(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{1-s_{p}}\|w^{(p)}(t)\|_{W^{1,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{s_{p}}\leq C_{u}(\sigma\mu)^{s_{p}}\kappa^{1/2}(\nu\mu)^{-1/2}.

The conclusion would follow if the exponent is negative. Indeed, using the choice of parameters in Section
4.1
, we see that

(\sigma\mu)^{s_{p}}\kappa^{1/2}(\nu\mu)^{-1/2}\leq\lambda^{s_{p}(2\gamma+1)-1+20\gamma},

which implies

\|w^{(p)}(t)\|_{W^{s_{p},1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq\delta,\qquad t\in[0,1],

for all sufficiently large
\lambda
.
∎

For the exceptional set
E
, recall from the definition of
g_{k}
, (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), that

\displaystyle\Supp g_{k}\subset\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\Lambda}(t_{k},t_{k}+(\kappa\sigma)^{-1})+n\sigma^{-1}.

Note that these open intervals are disjoint by definition.
So we just define
E
as

(4.13)

\displaystyle E:=[0,1]\cap\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\Lambda}(t_{k},t_{k}+(\kappa\sigma)^{-1})+n\sigma^{-1}.

Then for any
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}([0,t]\cap E)\leq\sum_{n,k}(\kappa\sigma)^{-1},

where
n,k
are such that
t_{k}+n\sigma^{-1}\in[0,t]
. Since
\Lambda\ni k
is finite and fixed, the total number of pairs of
n,k
is bounded by
Ct\sigma
, where
C
is independent of
t
and
\kappa
. It follows that

\mathcal{L}([0,t]\cap E)\leq Ct\kappa^{-1}

which implies (
2.4
) provided
\lambda
is sufficiently large.

Next, we show (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), namely the Sobolev and energy level estimate away from
E
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
t\in[0,1]\setminus E

\displaystyle\|w(t)\|_{W^{\frac{1}{\delta},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta,

\displaystyle\Big|\|u_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}-\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2},

provided that
\lambda
is sufficiently large.

Proof.

By the definition of
E
, we see that

w^{(p)}=w^{(c)}=w^{(a)}=0\qquad\text{for all $t\in[0,1]\setminus E$}.

So, for all
t\in[0,1]\setminus E
we have

\displaystyle\Big|\|u_{1}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}-\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\Big|\langle w^{(o)},u\rangle\Big|+\|w^{(o)}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2},

\displaystyle\|w(t)\|_{W^{\frac{1}{\delta},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|w^{(o)}(t)\|_{W^{\frac{1}{\delta},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})},

and the conclusion follows from Proposition
4.4
.
∎

4.4.
Estimates on the new Reynolds stress

The last step of the proof is to estimate

. We will proceed with the decomposition in Lemma
3.8
and show that for any sufficiently large
\lambda
, each part of the stress

is less than

.

It is also worth noting that we estimate all the errors on

except the oscillation error, for which we only look at

.

4.4.1.
Linear error

Lemma 4.8
.

For sufficiently large
\lambda
,

\|R_{\Lin}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

Proof.

We split the linear error into three parts:

R_{\Lin}=\underbrace{-\mathcal{R}\left(\Delta w\right)}_{:=R_{\Lap}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{R}\big(\partial_{t}(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})\big)}_{:=R_{\Acc}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{R}\left(\D(w\otimes u)+\D(u\otimes w)\right)}_{:=R_{\Dri}}.

The estimate of the Laplacian error
R_{\Lap}
relies crucially on the temporal concentration
\kappa
, the
R_{\Acc}
error uses the stream functions
\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}
so that we gain a factor of
(\sigma\mu)^{-1}
, and the drifts
R_{\Dri}
can be handle by standard estimates.

Estimate of
R_{\Lap}
:

We recall that
w=w^{(p)}+w^{(c)}+w^{(t)}
and, using (
C.2
), decompose the Laplacian error into two parts:

\displaystyle\|R_{\Lap}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla w\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+2\|\nabla w^{(t)}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}.

Now recall from (
3.28
) that

(4.14)

w^{(p)}+w^{(c)}=\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\big[a_{k}g_{k}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big],

and hence we can use estimates in Lemma
4.2
, (
3.14
), and Theorem
3.2
to obtain

(4.15)

\begin{split}\|\nabla(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&=\sigma^{-1}\Big\|\sum_{k}\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\big[a_{k}g_{k}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big]\Big\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\|a_{k}\|_{C^{2}_{x,t}}\|g_{k}\|_{L^{1}}\big\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;W^{2,r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\sigma^{-1}\cdot\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\sigma\mu)^{2}\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}.\end{split}

As for
\nabla w^{(t)}
, the oscillation part (
3.30
) simply satisfies

\|\nabla w^{(o)}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\times[0,1])}\lesssim\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\|h_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\|\nabla\D R_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

due to the choice of parameter
\sigma
, and the acceleration part (
3.31
) enjoys the bound

\begin{split}\|\nabla w^{(a)}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&\lesssim\omega^{-1}\sigma\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\big\|a_{k}^{2}\big\|_{C^{1}_{x,t}}\|g_{k}\|_{L^{1}}\big\|\nabla|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\omega^{-1}\sigma\cdot\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\sigma\mu)(\nu\mu)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},\end{split}

where we used Lemma
4.2
, (
3.14
), Theorem
3.2
, and Condition (
4.7
) for the last inequality.

Estimate of
R_{\Acc}
:

For the acceleration part of the linear error,
taking time derivative of (
4.14
) and using identity (
3.23
), we obtain

\begin{split}\partial_{t}(w^{(p)}+w^{(c)})&=\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\big[\partial_{t}(a_{k}g_{k})\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big]+\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\big[a_{k}g_{k}\partial_{t}\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big]\\
&=\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\big[\partial_{t}(a_{k}g_{k})\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big]+\sigma^{-2}\omega\sum_{k}\nabla^{\perp}\big[a_{k}g_{k}^{2}(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big].\end{split}

Now thanks to the fact that
\mathcal{R}\nabla^{\perp}
is a Calderón-Zygmund operator on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
(see (
B.2
)), we can use Lemma
4.2
, (
3.14
), and Theorem
3.2
to estimate the first term:

\begin{split}\sigma^{-1}\Big\|\sum_{k}\mathcal{R}\nabla^{\perp}\big[\partial_{t}(a_{k}g_{k})\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big]\Big\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r})}&\lesssim\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}\big\|a_{k}\big\|_{C^{1}_{x,t}}\big\|g_{k}\big\|_{W^{1,1}}\big\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{r}}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\sigma^{-1}(\sigma\kappa)\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}.\end{split}

As for the second term, we recall that the derivative of
\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}
in the direction
\mathbf{e}_{k}
is of order
\sigma\nu
(rather than
\sigma\mu
for the full gradient, see (
3.10
)):

\|(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{r}}\lesssim\sigma\nu\cdot\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}.

This estimate together with Lemma
4.2
and (
3.14
) implies

\begin{split}\sigma^{-2}\omega\Big\|\sum_{k}\mathcal{R}\nabla^{\perp}\big[a_{k}g_{k}^{2}(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big]\Big\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r})}&\lesssim\sigma^{-2}\omega\sum_{k}\big\|a_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,t}}\big\|g_{k}^{2}\big\|_{L^{1}}\big\|(\mathbf{e}_{k}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{\Psi}_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{r}}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\sigma^{-2}\omega\cdot\sigma\nu\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}.\end{split}

Due to (
4.8
), combining both terms we get

\begin{split}\|R_{\Acc}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&\lesssim C_{u}\left(\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}+\omega\sigma^{-1}\nu\mu^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}}\right)\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma}.\end{split}

Estimate of
R_{\Dri}
:

Using the
L^{r}
boundedness of
\mathcal{R}
,

\begin{split}\|R_{\Dri}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&=\|\mathcal{R}\left(\D(w\otimes u)+\D(u\otimes w)\right)\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim\|w\otimes u\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim\|w\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,t}}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},\end{split}

by
L^{\infty}L^{p}
estimates on
w
in Section
4.2
, and the fact that
r\leq p
.

Combining the above estimates, we can conclude that for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, there holds

\displaystyle\|R_{\Lin}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

∎

4.4.2.
Correction error

Lemma 4.9
.

For sufficiently large
\lambda
,

\|R_{\Cor}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

Proof.

Using Hölder’s inequality, the fact that
\mathcal{R}\D
is bounded in
L^{r}
, and (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|R_{\Cor}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)})\otimes w\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{r}_{x}}+\|w^{(p)}\otimes(w^{(c)}+w^{(t)}))\|_{L^{1}_{t}L^{r}_{x}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}+\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\Big)\|w\|_{L^{2}_{x,t}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}_{x,t}}\Big(\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}+\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\Big).

Recall that
w^{(t)}=w^{(o)}+w^{(a)}
. Then by Propositions
4.3
,
4.4
, and
4.5
we have

\begin{split}\|w\|_{L^{2}_{x,t}}&\leq\|w^{(p)}\|_{L^{2}_{x,t}}+\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{2}_{x,t}}+\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{2}_{x,t}}\\
&\lesssim\|R\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1])},\end{split}

as well as

\|w^{(c)}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}+\|w^{(t)}\|_{L^{2}_{t}L^{q}_{x}}\leq C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma}.

Thus for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, we can conclude that

\|R_{\Cor}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

∎

4.4.3.
Oscillation error

As we mentioned before, we estimate the oscillation error on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
since the perturbations are only designed to balance the old stress on
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
, cf. the remainder
R_{\Rem}
below.

Lemma 4.10
.

For sufficiently large
\lambda
,

\|R_{\Osc}\|_{L^{1}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

Proof.

We will use the decomposition from Lemma
3.7

R_{\Osc}=R_{\Osc,x}+R_{\Osc,t}+R_{\Osc,a}+R_{\Rem}

where we recall

\displaystyle R_{\Osc,x}

\displaystyle=g_{k}^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\mathcal{B}\Big(\nabla(a_{k}^{2}),\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}-\fint\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\Big),

\displaystyle R_{\Osc,t}

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{-1}\theta^{2}\mathcal{R}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}h_{k}\D\partial_{t}R_{k}

\displaystyle R_{\Osc,a}

\displaystyle=-\omega^{-1}\sigma\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\mathcal{R}\left(\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle R_{\Rem}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta^{2})R.

Estimate of
R_{\Osc,x}
:

To reduce notations, denote
\mathbf{T}_{k}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
by

\mathbf{T}_{k}=\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}-\fint\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k},

so that

R_{\Osc,x}(x,t)=g_{k}^{2}\sum_{k\in\Lambda}\mathcal{B}\big(\nabla(a_{k}^{2}),\mathbf{T}_{k}\big).

Using Theorem
C.3
and the fact that
\mathbf{T}_{k}
has zero spatial mean, we can estimate the
L^{r}
norm of
R_{\Osc,x}
at each time
t\in[0,1]
as follows.

\begin{split}\|R_{\Osc,x}(t)\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}&=g_{k}^{2}\Big\|\sum_{k}\mathcal{B}\Big(\nabla(a_{k}^{2}),\mathbf{T}_{k}\Big)\Big\|_{L^{r}}\\
&\lesssim g_{k}^{2}\sum_{k}\|\nabla(a_{k}^{2})\|_{C^{1}}\|\mathcal{R}\big(\mathbf{T}_{k}\big)\|_{L^{r}}\\
&\lesssim\sigma^{-1}g_{k}^{2}\sum_{k}\|\nabla(a_{k}^{2})\|_{C^{1}}\|\mathbf{T}_{k}\|_{L^{r}},\end{split}

where the last inequality used the fact that
\mathbf{T}_{k}
has zero spatial mean.
Thanks to Theorem
3.2
, for any
k\in\Lambda

\|\mathbf{T}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{r}}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{W}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{r}}\lesssim\|\mathbf{W}_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2r}}^{2}\lesssim(\nu\mu)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}

Therefore, integrating in time and using Lemma
4.2
, (
3.14
), and (
4.9
), we obtain

\begin{split}\|R_{\Osc,x}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&\lesssim C_{u}\sigma^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma}.\end{split}

Estimate of
R_{\Osc,t}
:

Using the bound on
h_{k}
(
3.15
), we infer

\begin{split}\|R_{\Osc,t}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&\leq\Big\|\sigma^{-1}\sum_{k}h_{k}\D\partial_{t}R_{k}\Big\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim\sigma^{-1}\Big\|\sum_{k}h_{k}\Big\|_{L^{1}}C_{u}\\
&\leq C_{u}\sigma^{-1}.\end{split}

Estimate of
R_{\Osc,a}
:

Thanks to Theorem
3.2
,

\begin{split}\|R_{\Osc,a}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}=&\omega^{-1}\sigma\Big\|\sum_{k}\mathcal{R}\big(\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\big)\Big\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\\
&\lesssim\omega^{-1}\sigma\sum_{k}\left\|\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(0,1;C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\|(|\mathbf{W}_{k}|^{2}\mathbf{e}_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{r}}\\
&=\omega^{-1}\sigma\sum_{k}\left\|\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(0,1;C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\|\mathbf{W}_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2r}}\\
&\lesssim\omega^{-1}\sigma(\nu\mu)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\sum_{k}\left\|\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(0,1;C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}.\end{split}

Using the product rule,

\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)=(\partial_{t}a_{k}^{2})g_{k}+a_{k}^{2}g_{k}^{\prime},

thanks to Lemma
4.2
and (
3.14
) we obtain

(4.16)

\begin{split}\left\|\partial_{t}\left(a_{k}^{2}g_{k}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(0,1;C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}&\lesssim C_{u}\left(\|g_{k}\|_{L^{1}}+\|g_{k}^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}}\right)\\
&\lesssim C_{u}\big(\kappa^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\sigma\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\big).\end{split}

Hence

\|R_{\Osc,a}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim C_{u}\omega^{-1}(\nu\mu)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\cdot\sigma^{2}\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

due to (
4.10
).

Estimate of
R_{\Rem}
:

Finally, the estimate of
R_{\Rem}
follows from the definition of the cutoff
\theta
as in (
3.25
):

\displaystyle\|R_{\Rem}\|_{L^{1}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle=\|(1-\theta^{2})R\|_{L^{1}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{I\cap\{\theta\neq 1\}}\|R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq|\{t\in I:\theta\neq 1\}|\|R\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta/8.

Combining this estimate with

\|R_{\Osc,x}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+\|R_{\Osc,t}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}+\|R_{\Osc,a}\|_{L^{1}(0,1;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\lesssim C_{u}\lambda^{-\gamma},

we conclude that the desired bound

\|R_{\Osc}\|_{L^{1}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\leq\frac{\delta}{4}

holds for all
\lambda
large enough.
∎

Appendix A
Vanishing viscosity limit in
L^{\infty}L^{2}

Here, we adopt a proof from
[
CFLS16
]
to our settings and show that vanishing viscosity solution of the 2D Euler equations with initial vorticity in
L^{p}
,
p>1
, conserve the kinetic energy.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
u\in C([0,T];W^{1,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
be a weak solution of the 2D Euler equations with
\curl u(0)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
p>1
, such that there is a family
\{u^{\nu}\}
of weak solutions to the 2D Navier-Stokes equations with

\|u^{\nu}-u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))}\to 0\qquad\text{as}\quad\nu\to 0.

Then
u(t)
conserves the kinetic energy.

Proof.

Thanks to the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, we have that
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
, and hence
for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a decomposition
u^{\nu}=u^{\nu}_{1}+u^{\nu}_{2}
such that

\|u_{1}^{\nu}\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{2}^{\nu}\in L^{\infty}_{t,x},

for
\nu
small enough. Therefore, by the previous theorem, for all
\nu
small enough,
u^{\nu}
is a Leray-Hopf solution of the 2D NSE and is smooth for
t>0
.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\omega_{0}\notin L^{2}
, as otherwise the result is trivial. Note that

\frac{d}{dt}\|\omega^{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=-2\nu\|\nabla\omega^{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim-\nu\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{p}}^{\frac{2p}{p-2}}\|\omega^{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{2-p}}.

Therefore

\|\omega^{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim(\nu t)^{\frac{p-2}{p}}.

Hence

0\geq\|u^{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|u^{\nu}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq-c(\nu t)^{\frac{2p-2}{p}}.

Taking a limit as
\nu\to 0
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Some technical inequalities

B.1.
Calderon-Zygmund operators on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

For
1<p<\infty
, the classical Calderon-Zygmund estimates hold on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|\Delta f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

It is standard to transfer the estimates on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{T}^{d}
:

(B.1)

\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\|\Delta f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

In particular, (
B.1
) implies the bounds used in the paper:

(B.2)

\|\mathcal{R}\nabla f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\qquad\text{for any $f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})$},\\

where
\mathcal{R}
is the antidivergence operator defined in the next section.

B.2.
Improved Hölder’s inequality on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

We recall the following result due to Modena and Székelyhidi
[
MS18
, Lemma 2.1]
, which extends the first type of such result
[
BV19
, Lemma 3.7]
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
r\in[1,\infty]
, and
a,f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions. Then for every
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
,

(B.3)

\Big|\|af(\sigma\cdot)\|_{r}-\|a\|_{r}\|f\|_{r}\Big|\lesssim\sigma^{-\frac{1}{r}}\|a\|_{C^{1}}\|f\|_{r}.

This result is used to control the energy of the perturbations in Section
4
. Note that the error term on the right-hand side can be made arbitrarily small by increasing the oscillation
\sigma
.

Appendix C
Antidivergence operators

For any
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, there exists a
v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that

\Delta v=f-\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}f.

And we denote this solution
v
by
\Delta^{-1}f
. Note that if
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then by rescaling we have

\Delta^{-1}\big(f(\sigma\cdot)\big)=\sigma^{-2}v(\sigma\cdot)\quad\text{ for $\sigma\in\mathbb{N}$.}

C.1.
Tensor-valued antidivergence
\mathcal{R}

We recall the following antidivergence operator
\mathcal{R}
introduced in
[
DLS13
]
.

Definition C.1
.

\mathcal{R}:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{0})
is defined by

(C.1)

(\mathcal{R}v)_{ij}=\mathcal{R}_{ijk}v_{k}

where

\mathcal{R}_{ijk}=-\Delta^{-1}\partial_{k}\delta_{ij}+\Delta^{-1}\partial_{i}\delta_{jk}+\Delta^{-1}\partial_{j}\delta_{ik}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{R}
is well-defined since
\mathcal{R}_{ijk}
is symmetric in
i,j
and taking the trace gives

\displaystyle\Tr\mathcal{R}v

\displaystyle=-2\Delta^{-1}\partial_{k}v_{k}+\Delta^{-1}\partial_{k}v_{k}+\Delta^{-1}\partial_{k}v_{k}=0.

By a direct computation, one can also show that

\D(\mathcal{R}v)=v-\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}v\quad\text{for any $v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})$}

and

(C.2)

\mathcal{R}\Delta v=\nabla v+\nabla v^{T}\quad\text{for any divergence-free $v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})$}.

The antidivergence operator
\mathcal{R}
is bounded on
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty

[
MS18
]
.

Theorem C.2
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. For any vector field
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
, there holds

\|\mathcal{R}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

In particular, if
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

\|\mathcal{R}f(\sigma\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\sigma^{-1}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\quad\text{for any $\sigma\in\mathbb{N}$}.

C.2.
Bilinear antidivergence
\mathcal{B}

We can also introduce the bilinear version
\mathcal{B}:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\times C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}_{0})
of
\mathcal{R}
.

Let

(\mathcal{B}(v,A))_{ij}=v_{l}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk}-\mathcal{R}(\partial_{i}v_{l}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk})

or by a slight abuse of notations

\mathcal{B}(v,A)=v\mathcal{R}A-\mathcal{R}(\nabla v\mathcal{R}A).

This bilinear antidivergence
\mathcal{B}
allows us to gain derivative when the later argument has zero mean and a small period.

Theorem C.3
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. For any
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
A\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
,

(C.3)

\D(\mathcal{B}(v,A))=vA-\fint_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}vA,

and

\|\mathcal{B}(v,A)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|v\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\|\mathcal{R}A\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

Proof.

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\D(\mathcal{B}(v,A))

\displaystyle=v_{l}\partial_{j}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk}+\partial_{j}v_{l}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk}-\D\mathcal{R}(\partial_{i}v_{l}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk})

\displaystyle=v_{l}A_{il}+\fint\partial_{i}v_{l}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk}

where we have used the fact that
A
has zero mean and
\mathcal{R}
is symmetric.

Integrating by parts, we have

\displaystyle\fint\partial_{i}v_{l}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk}=-\fint v_{l}\partial_{i}\mathcal{R}_{ijk}A_{lk}=-\fint v_{l}A_{lj},

which implies that

\displaystyle\D(\mathcal{B}(v,A))=vA-\fint vA.

The second estimate follows immediately from the definition of
\mathcal{B}
and Theorem
C.2
.

∎
