Condensation in scale-free geometric graphs with excess edges
Remco van der Hofstad, Pim van der Hoorn, Céline Kerriou, Neeladri Maitra, Peter Mörters

Abstract
. We identify the upper large deviation probability for the number of edges in scale-free geometric random graph models as the space volume goes to infinity. Our result covers the models of scale-free percolation, the Boolean model with heavy-tailed radius distribution, and the age-dependent random connection model. In all these cases the mechanism behind the large deviation is based on a condensation effect. Loosely speaking, the mechanism randomly selects a finite number of vertices and increases their power, so that they connect to a macroscopic number of vertices in the graph, while the other vertices retain a degree close to their expectation and thus make no more than the expected contribution to the large deviation event. We verify this intuition by means of limit theorems for the empirical distributions of degrees and edge-lengths under the conditioning. We observe that at large finite volumes, the edge-length distribution splits into a bulk and travelling wave part of asymptotically positive proportions.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Motivation

The physical phenomenon of condensation corresponds to the formation of drops of liquid when a gas is cooled down or put under high pressure.
The mathematics
behind this phenomenon however turns out not to be constrained to mathematical models of gases, it is ubiquitous throughout probability
and the theory of stochastic processes, ranging from random walks
[
2
]
, random trees
[
16
]
, interacting particle systems
[
24
]
and Gaussian processes
[
25
]
to queueing networks
[
21
]
and extreme value statistics
[
9
]
, to name just a few examples.
In this paper we show how a condensation effect arises in the large deviation theory of scale-free geometric random graphs.

To compare the condensation effects we look at the simplest probabilistic model capturing a condensation effect, which is the
balls-in-bins
model, see
[
16
, Sections 11 and 19]
. In this model

m
particles (or balls) are placed into
n
labelled containers (or bins) and the probability of a configuration
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
with
m_{i}
particles in the
i
th
container (and hence
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=m
) is

\frac{1}{Z_{m,n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}w(m_{i}),

where
w(i)=ci^{-\beta}
are the weights of a zeta
distribution with
1/c=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-\beta}
and
Z_{m,n}
is a normalisation constant. As
n\to\infty
and
m/n\to\rho
for some particle density
0<\rho<\infty
, it is shown in
[
16
, Theorem 11.4]

that the proportion
N_{k}(n)
of containers containing

k
particles converges to a number
\pi_{k}
with
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}=1
and

\rhd

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\pi_{k}=\rho
if
\rho\leq\nu:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}iw(i)
;

\rhd

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\pi_{k}=\nu
if
\rho>\nu
.

As
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kN_{k}(n)=m/n\to\rho
, we observe in the second case that particle mass is escaping. It turns out, see
[
16
, Theorem 19.34]
, that the excess mass condenses in a
single
container, in other words, the container containing the largest number of particles contains asymptotically
(\rho-\nu)n
balls. The vastly increased particle density in this container is interpreted as a manifestation of the condensation phenomenon in a gas under high pressure.

In the balls-in-bins model, if
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
are independent and zeta distributed, then

Z_{m,n}={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=m\right)

and the probability of a configuration
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
is the conditional distribution of
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})

given the event

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=m
. If
\rho>\nu
this event has asymptotically decreasing probability and hence the condensation stems from conditioning on a rare event. In our main result a similar phenomenon arises in the context of large deviations for geometric random graphs. Here, loosely speaking, the bins correspond to vertices and the number of balls in a bin corresponds to the vertex degree. But other than in the balls-in-bins model, in the geometric random graphs we can have more than one vertex of macroscopic degree, depending on the number of excess edges generated in the large deviation event.

A further analogy arises in the context of Bose-Einstein condensation. Bose and Einstein predicted in 1925 that above a certain density a macroscopic fraction of particles in a Bose gas aggregate into a single quantum state. Based on Feynman’s representation of the Bose gas as a soup of interacting loops in a container, see for example
[
28
]
, the phenomenon takes the form that as the particle number goes to infinity, loops of macroscopic length occur in the loop soup. This is studied rigorously under simplifying modelling assumptions, for example in
[
3
,
1
,
8
]
, but a fully satisfactory mathematical result on a physical model is still missing. The results achieved so far show that in toy models, above a critical particle density, the empirical distribution of the loop lengths per particle in the limit as the volume of the container tends to infinity converges to a distribution of total mass strictly less than one. The missing mass escapes to infinity because a proportion of particles is associated with loops of a macroscopic length scale, see for example
[
23
]
. In our model of a random graph embedded in a
d
-dimensional torus of volume
n
, we look at the length of edges instead of loops and study the empirical distribution of the Euclidean length of all edges. Conditioning the random graph on having an edge density above the critical value we prove that a positive fraction of the mass of the empirical distribution converges to a limit distribution of mass strictly smaller than one, while the rest of the mass forms a travelling wave of macroscopic speed and width. In particular, asymptotically, a positive fraction of edge-lengths is of macroscopic order
n^{1/d}
, loosely analogous to the behaviour of loop lengths in the models above.

Beyond the relation to condensation phenomena, our results are of interest from a purely random graph perspective, providing insight in the nature of the most important models of scale-free random geometric graphs by giving precise asymptotics for the probability that such a graph has a large number of edges. We also give limit theorems for the
empirical edge-length distribution and the empirical degree distribution of the graph conditioned on having a large number of edges, which expose the condensation behaviour. Results of this nature have been found for non-geometric graph models like the Chung-Lu graphs
[
26
]
, very simple geometric models
[
19
]
, but also for Boolean models which are not scale-free, see the seminal paper of Chatterjee and Harel
[
4
]
. The behaviour of scale-free Boolean models, and more general scale-free random geometric graphs, is however fundamentally different from the latter and is explored in the present paper. Our results are set up in a general framework covering a wide range of scale-free geometric graphs.

1.2.
Geometric random graph models

In our general setup, the vertex set of the graph

is contained in the
d

\rhd

Lattice case:

is an integer and

the integer lattice on the torus,

\rhd

Poisson case:

is real and
V_{n}
a Poisson point process of intensity one on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
.

Fix
\beta>2
. To define the edge set
E_{n}

V_{n}
, we equip each vertex

with a random weight
W_{x}
such that the random variables
(W_{x})_{x\in V_{n}}
are independent and identically distributed to some
W
with density

\displaystyle f_{W}(t):=(\beta-1)t^{-\beta}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{t\geq 1\}}.

(1.1)

We fix a
profile function

\varphi\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
, which is nonincreasing and integrable,
and a
connection kernel

\kappa\colon[1,\infty)\times[1,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
,
which is symmetric, continuous and nondecreasing in each argument. For every unordered pair

\{x,y\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, we define a function
p_{x,y}\colon[1,\infty)\times[1,\infty)\to[0,1]
by
p_{x,y}:=0
if
x=y
, and

\displaystyle p_{x,y}(v,w):=\varphi\left(\frac{d_{n}(x,y)^{d}}{\kappa(v,w)}\right)\text{ otherwise.}

(1.2)

Given the vertex set and the weights, we include the edge
\{x,y\}
in
E_{n}
with probability
p_{x,y}(W_{x},W_{y})
, independently for every unordered pair
\{x,y\}
of distinct vertices in
V_{n}
with weights
W_{x},W_{y}
. More precisely, let
(A_{\{x,y\}})_{x,y}
be conditionally independent Bernoulli-distributed random variables with success parameter
p_{x,y}(W_{x},W_{y})
. For notational simplicity write
A_{x,y}
for
A_{\{x,y\}}
. The edge set
E_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle E_{n}:=\{\{x,y\}\subseteq V_{n}\colon A_{x,y}=1\}.

(1.3)

The degree of a vertex
x\in V_{n}
is the number of neighbours of
x
in
G_{n}
defined as

\displaystyle D_{x}:=D_{x}(n):=\sum_{y\in V_{n}}A_{x,y}.

(1.4)

This model was introduced under the name
weight-dependent random connection model
in
[
12
,
11
]
and under the name
kernel-based spatial random
graph
in
[
18
]
. We now explain how our principal examples fit in this
framework. In all our examples the empirical degree distributions of the graphs converge to a deterministic limit,
which is a heavy-tailed distribution with the same tail index
\beta
as the weight distribution.

(i) Scale-free percolation

Take the profile function
\varphi(x)=1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-x^{-\alpha}}
, for some
\alpha>1
, and connection kernel
\kappa(v,w)=vw
. For the lattice case this model was first introduced and studied in
[
6
]
, the Poisson case has been introduced in
[
7
]
.

(ii) Poisson Boolean model with heavy-tailed radii

Consider the Poisson case with profile function

\varphi(x)={\mathbbm{1}}_{[0,1]}(x)
and connection kernel

\kappa(v,w)=(v^{1/d}+w^{1/d})^{d}
. Thinking of

r_{x}:=W_{x}^{1/d}
as the radius of a ball
\mathcal{B}(x,r_{x})
centred in
x
, in this model two vertices
x,y
are connected by an edge if and only if
\mathcal{B}(x,r_{x})\cap\mathcal{B}(y,r_{y})\not=\emptyset
. Therefore this graph model shares the name with the union of the balls
\mathcal{B}(x,r_{x})
over all points
x\in V_{n}
, which is studied in stochastic geometry
[
27
]
.

(iii) Age-based preferential attachment graphs

We define a dynamical graph model
(G^{t}\colon t\geq 0)
with vertices embedded in the torus
\mathbb{T}_{1}^{d}
of volume one as in
[
10
]
. Let
\varphi(x)=1\wedge x^{-\alpha}
, for some
\alpha>1
, and fix
0<\gamma<1
. We take a standard Poisson process of arrival times for the vertices. Given the graph
G^{t-}
, at the arrival time
t
of a new vertex, we
give this vertex a random location
x\in\mathbb{T}_{1}^{d}
uniformly on the torus of volume one. Independently for every vertex of
G^{t-}
, we establish an edge to the new vertex with probability

\varphi(td_{1}(x,y)^{d}(s/t)^{\gamma}),

where
y
is its location and
s<t
its birth-time. As
(t/s)^{\gamma}
is the order of the expected degree of the vertex at time
t
, the new vertex prefers to attach itself to existing vertices with large expected degree or, equivalently, old age.

For any fixed time
n
, we rescale the graph
G^{n}
to the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
by multiplying all locations with the factor
n^{1/d}
. We denote the rescaled graph by
G_{n}
and explain how it fits our framework. If
s\in(0,n)
is the birth time of a vertex at the new location
x
, then we let its weight be
W_{x}:=(s/n)^{-\gamma}
. Then the new vertex locations are a standard Poisson point process on
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, the weights are independent Pareto-distributed with parameter
\beta=1+\frac{1}{\gamma}
, and the probability of two vertices at locations
x,y
in
G_{n}
being connected is

\varphi\Big(n\big(W_{x}^{-1/\gamma}\vee W_{y}^{-1/\gamma}\big)\,d_{1}\big(xn^{-1/d},yn^{-1/d}\big)^{d}\big(\tfrac{W_{x}^{-1/\gamma}\vee W_{y}^{-1/\gamma}}{W_{y}^{-1/\gamma}\wedge W_{x}^{-1/\gamma}}\big)^{\gamma}\Big)=\varphi\Big(\frac{d_{n}(x,y)^{d}}{\kappa(W_{x},W_{y})}\Big),

for
\kappa(v,w)=(v\wedge w)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}(v\vee w)
. Hence results for the number of edges in
G^{n}
as
time
goes to infinity can be transferred directly from results for the number of edges for the graph
G_{n}
as the
volume
tends to infinity. The latter fits our framework with the given
\kappa
.

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with an atom inserted at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In both cases, the infinite random graph, rooted at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
arises as the local (Benjamini-Schramm) limit in probability of

, see
[
14
, Theorem 1.11]
. Denote the degree of the root in
G_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
by
D_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
and the degree of the root in
G_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
conditioned on having weight

.

Our third main result describes the degree distribution of the graph

conditioned on the event
|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)
. We start with a version describing degree sequences of hubs and other vertices separately.

Theorem 1.8

(Degree sequences of hubs and normal vertices)
.

Consider the weight-dependent random connection model with kernel
\kappa
and profile
\varphi
satisfying Assumption

A
. Let
\rho>0
be a non integer, and
k
be the unique integer satisfying

, as

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{1}{n}(D_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})_{i\in[k]},(\pi_{a,b}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{0\leq a,0\leq b\leq k}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\left((\Lambda(Y_{i}))_{i\in[k]},(\pi_{a,b})_{0\leq a,0\leq b\leq k}\right),

(1.22)

in distribution on sequence space with the product topology, while

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{n}{D_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}}\stackrel{{\!{}_{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}0\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{ for each $i>k$,}

(1.23)

where

\displaystyle\pi_{a,b}=\pi_{a,b}((U_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[k]}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[D_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(W)=a,\sum_{i=1}^{k}B^{\scriptscriptstyle{(i)}}_{U,W}=b\,\Big|\,(U_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[k]}\Big],

(1.24)

and where the vector

on

and an independent Pareto-distributed weight

Combining the results in Theorem

, we get convergence of the degree distribution.

Corollary 1.9

(Degree distribution)
.

Conditionally on the rare event
|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)
, the empirical degree distribution

\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\sum_{x\in V_{n}}\delta_{D_{x}}

converges in distribution on the Polish space of probability measures with the weak topology to the random probability measure

\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\sum_{b=0}^{k}\pi_{a,b}\,\delta_{a+b}\,.

Remark 1.10

(The condensate)
.

Theorem
1.8
identifies where the surplus edges, whose limit in distribution equals
S
by
Remark
1.5
, are located. Indeed, using that the sum of the degrees equals
2|E_{n}|
, we see that the vertices of high-weight of order
n
form the condensate attracting roughly
nS
incident edges. The other ends of the edges are at vertices whose weights have the unconditional weight distribution
W
. Thus, the condensate consists of
k
vertices, but other vertices can be incident to this condensate. This makes the condensation phenomenon different, and arguably richer, than the one described in Section
1.1
.

2.
Overview and proof strategies

2.1.
Upper large deviations: Strategy and proof of Theorem
1.1

We call an edge
high-weight
if at least one of its endpoints has high weight, which we make precise below. Further,
light-weight
edges are edges where neither endpoints have high weight. We also divide edges into
long
and
short
edges, where long edges are those that connect vertices far away from each other. More precisely, let

(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be some sequence tending to zero, let
a\in(0,1)
and split the edge set
E_{n}
into the three disjoint sets

\displaystyle E_{\rm main}

\displaystyle:=\{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}\colon d_{n}(x,y)\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d},\,W_{x}\vee W_{y}\leq n^{a}\},

(2.1)

\displaystyle E_{\rm long}

\displaystyle:=\{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}\colon d_{n}(x,y)>\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d},\,W_{x}\vee W_{y}\leq n^{a}\},

(2.2)

\displaystyle E_{\rm high}

\displaystyle:=\{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}\colon W_{x}\vee W_{y}>n^{a}\},

(2.3)

where
x\vee y
denotes the maximum of
x
and
y
. These sets correspond to the sets containing short and light-weight edges, long and light-weight edges and lastly high-weight edges.
Theorem
1.1
follows from three key steps estimating the number of edges in these sets.

Step 1. Short and light-weight edges form the bulk.

Consider short, light-weight edges. These constitute the ‘bulk’ edge count, i.e., a typical edge of the graph falls into this category.
Proposition
2.1
shows that the number of these edges are well concentrated about the mean
n\mu
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\alpha>1
be the parameter in (
1.7
) and let

E_{\rm main}
be as in (
2.1
) with
\varepsilon_{n}
and
a
chosen such that

\rhd

\varepsilon_{n}^{d}=n^{-\gamma}
for some
0<\gamma<(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\wedge\tfrac{1}{d}
, and

\rhd

0<a(2\vee(\beta-1))<(1-\gamma-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\wedge\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Then, for all
\delta>0
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(||E_{\rm main}|-n\mu|\geq n\delta\right)=o({n^{-k(\beta-2)}}).

We prove Proposition
2.1
in Section
3
. In the proof we first fix the vertex weights (and in the Poisson case both the vertex locations and weights) and apply a conditional version of
McDiarmid’s
concentration inequality, using that the edges are included independently. Then we use McDiarmid’s inequality again to show concentration of the resulting functionals of the independent weights (and in the Poisson case also the vertex locations).

Step 2. Long and light-weight edges are negligible.

Proposition
2.2
shows that this contribution is asymptotically negligible.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
\delta>0
, there exist
\varepsilon_{n}
and
a

satisfying the conditions of Proposition
2.1

such that
E_{\rm long}
in (
2.2
) satisfies

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm long}|\geq n\delta\right)=o({n^{-k(\beta-2)}}).

We prove Proposition
2.2
in Section
4
for which we use that the connection probability of a pair of vertices is increasing with respect to the weights of the vertices, and decreasing with respect to the spatial distance between the vertices. This implies that the total number of long and light-weight edges can be stochastically dominated by a suitable binomial random variable and we can use a standard binomial concentration argument.

Step 3. High-weight edges form the condensate.

Using the first two steps, it remains to understand the probability that the total number of high-weight edges is larger than
n\rho
. This is accomplished in Proposition
2.3
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
E_{\rm high}
be as in (
2.3
). Then, for any
0<a<1
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm high}|\geq n\rho\right)=(F(\rho)+o(1))n^{-k(\beta-2)},

where
F(\rho)
is given by (
1.11
).

We prove Proposition
2.3
in Section
5
for which we use that the number of high-weight edges is well approximated by the sum of the degrees of the high-weight vertices. The degree of a vertex given its weight has Poisson-like statistics with the mean being an increasing function (depending on
n
) of the weight, see for example
[
14
, Proposition 1]
for a proof of this fact when the locations of the vertices are uniform, as in the Poisson models. As a consequence, again using concentration arguments, when the weight of a vertex diverges, its degree behaves like this function evaluated at its weight. This observation allows us to approximate the total number of high-weight edges by an i.i.d. sum of a deterministic (but
n
-dependent) function of the weights. To understand the probability that this sum is at least
n\rho
, we do a large deviation analysis similar to the one given in
[
19
]
, and show that this event occurs precisely when there are
k=\lceil\rho\rceil
vertices with weight of order
n
. This shows that
{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm high}|\geq n\rho\right)
behaves as stated in Proposition
2.3
.

We now prove continuity of
F
, which is the last ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, which then follows straightforwardly from the above propositions.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
b\geq 0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\rho\mapsto F_{b}(\rho):=\frac{(\beta-1)^{k}}{{k!}}\int_{b}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{b}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{{{\Lambda}(y_{1})+\cdots+{\Lambda}(y_{k})>\rho}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}^{-\beta}dy_{i}

defines a continuous function
F_{b}\colon(k-1,k)\to(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Finiteness for
b>0
follows from
\beta>2
. For
b=0
, we note that
\lim_{y\searrow 0}\Lambda(y)=0
. Choosing
b>0
such that
\Lambda(y)<\rho-(k-1)
for all
y\in(0,b)
, and using that
\Lambda(y)\leq 1
,
we see that
F_{0}(\rho)=F_{b}(\rho)
.
To see positivity, we lower bound
F_{b}(\rho)
by

\left(\int_{b}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{{\Lambda}(y)>\rho/k\}}y^{-\beta}dy\right)^{k}.

Note that
\lim_{y\uparrow\infty}{\Lambda}(y)=1
and hence the set
\{{\Lambda}(y)>\rho/k\}
contains an interval unbounded to the right and hence has positive measure.
Hence
F_{b}(\rho)>0
.

To see continuity, fix
\rho\in(k-1,k)
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, we need to find
\delta>0
with

\int_{0}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\rho+\delta>\Lambda(y_{1})+\cdots+{\Lambda}(y_{k})>\rho-\delta\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}^{-\beta}dy_{i}<\varepsilon.

For this, we note that if
\delta^{\prime}>0
is small enough, then

\rho+\delta^{\prime}>\Lambda(y_{1})+\cdots+{\Lambda}(y_{k})>\rho-\delta^{\prime}
implies that there exists
i
with

\delta^{\prime}<\Lambda(y_{i})<1-\delta^{\prime}
. Using that

\varphi
has no flat pieces except possibly at zero and one,
we find
\delta^{\prime}>\delta^{\prime\prime},\delta>0
such that

|\Lambda(y)-\Lambda(y^{\prime})|>2\delta
if

\delta^{\prime}<\Lambda(y)<1-\delta^{\prime}
and
|y-y^{\prime}|>\delta^{\prime\prime}
. Hence one of the integrations with respect to
dy_{1},\ldots,dy_{k}
is over an interval of length at most
\delta^{\prime\prime}
and choosing
\delta^{\prime}>0
small we can upper bound the integral by
\varepsilon
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Take
\delta>0
sufficiently small such that
(\rho-\delta,\rho+\delta)\subseteq(k-1,k)
. Then fix
0<a<1
and
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that Propositions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
can be applied.
Using the definitions of
E_{\rm main}
,
E_{\rm long}
and
E_{\rm high}
in (
2.1
)-(
2.3
), we can write

\displaystyle|E_{n}|

\displaystyle=|E_{\rm main}|+|E_{\rm long}|+|E_{\rm high}|.

(2.4)

The upper bound of Theorem
1.1
follows by noting that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm main}|\geq n(\mu+\delta/2)\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm long}|\geq n\delta/2\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm high}|\geq n(\rho-\delta)\right)

(2.5)

\displaystyle=(F(\rho-\delta)+o(1))n^{-k(\beta-2)},

where the last equality holds by
applying Propositions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
.
For the lower bound, remark that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)\right)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm high}|+|E_{\rm main}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)\right)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm high}|\geq n(\rho+\delta),\,|E_{\rm main}|\geq n(\mu-\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm high}|\geq n(\rho+\delta)\right)-{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm main}|<n(\mu-\delta)\right).

By Propositions
2.1
and
2.3
, the last line equals
(F(\rho+\delta)+o(1))n^{-k(\beta-2)}
.
Combining the upper and lower bounds and, recalling from Lemma
2.4
that
F
is continuous on
(k-1,k)
, we complete the proof by
letting
\delta
tend to zero.
∎

2.2.
Convergence of the empirical edge-lengths: Strategy and proof of Theorem

Throughout this section
k
is the unique integer with
k-1<\rho<k
. The key step in the proof of Theorem
1.6

(a)
is the following law of large numbers.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let

\lambda_{f}

Then, for all

In the proof of Proposition

, given in Section

, and the sum over the edges in

Proof of Theorem

(a)
.

Proposition

imply that for all

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|\int f(x)d\mu_{n}(x)-\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\right|>\delta,|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)\right)=o(1){\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{n}|\geq n(\rho+\mu)\right).

∎

For the proof of Theorem
1.6
(b)
, we first approximate the empirical edge-length distribution at macroscopic scale by a deterministic function of the
k
largest weights in the graph. Let

be continuous with compact support and define

\displaystyle\Lambda_{f}(w):=\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}}f(\|z\|){{\Lambda}}(w,z)\,dz,

where we recall

from (

). Our next result identifies the limiting distribution of long edges in terms of the largest weights.

Proposition 2.6
.

Conditionally on

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{}_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}}f\left(\frac{d_{n}(x,y)}{n^{1/d}}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{f}\left(\frac{W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}{n}\right)\stackrel{{\!{}_{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}0,

2.6
in Section

by using a concentration argument to approximate, for all vertices

exceeding

Proposition 2.7
.

Conditionally on

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}(W_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},\dots,W_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)})\stackrel{{\!{}_{\mathrm{d}}}}{{\rightarrow}}(Y_{{1}},\dots,Y_{{k}}),

, the random variables

Proof of Theorem
1.6
(b)
.

Note that
\Lambda_{f}
is bounded from above and continuous by means of the linearity of

in the first component. Thus the function
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{f}(x_{i})

2.6

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{}_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}}f\left(\frac{d_{n}(x,y)}{n^{1/d}}\right)\stackrel{{\!{}_{\mathrm{d}}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{f}\left(Y_{i}\right),

∎

Remark 2.8

(A hub clique)
.

As a consequence of Proposition
2.7
, conditionally on the event
|E_{n}|\geq(\mu+\rho)n
, there are
k
vertices with weight at least
\varepsilon n
, with probability at least

, where

as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. We call these high-weight vertices

. As the length of any edge is at most

, and the connection probability is

. Hence if
\lim_{r\to 0}\varphi(r)=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i\in[k]}\Lambda(W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n)<\rho\mid B\Big)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left.W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ for some }i\in[k]\;\right|\;B\right).

(2.14)

By Proposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ for some }i\in[k]\;\right|\;B\right)\to{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{i}\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ for some }i\in[k]\right).

Next, (

|{{\mathbb{P}}\left((W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n)_{i\in[k]}\in A\right)}/{{\mathbb{P}}\left(B\right)}-\mathbb{P}((W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n)_{i\in[k]}\in A\mid B)|.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left((W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n)_{i\in[k]}\in A\right)

\displaystyle=n^{k}(\beta-1)^{k}\int_{1}^{\infty}dt_{1}\cdots\int_{1}^{\infty}dt_{k}(t_{1}\cdots t_{k})^{-\beta}{\mathrm{e}}^{-nt_{k}^{1-\beta}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{t_{1}>\cdots>t_{k},(t_{1}/n,\ldots,t_{k}/n)\in A\}}

It remains to show that (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\mathbb{P}}\left(B\right)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}((W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}/n)_{i\in[k]})h\big(\tfrac{1}{n}(D_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})_{i\in[k]},(\pi_{a,b}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})\big)\right]

2.4.
Discussion: Why we assume
\rho
to be non-integer.

We now briefly discuss the nature of the problem when
\rho>0

is an integer in Theorem
1.1
.
The proof can be reduced to studying the deviations of a truncated i.i.d. sum
of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{n}(W_{i}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>n^{a}\}}
for a suitable
a>0
,
where we can think of
\lambda_{n}(w)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.D_{x}\;\right|\;W_{x}=w\right]
. It turns out that, for any
b>0
, we have
\lambda_{n}(bn)/n\to{\Lambda}(b)
for some function
{\Lambda}(b)
, which converges to
1
as
b\to\infty
.
This leads us to study the large deviations of
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}n{\Lambda}(W_{i}/n)
, with
(W_{i})_{i\geq 1}
an i.i.d. sequence with law
(
1.1
) and
{\Lambda}(w)\leq 1
with
{\Lambda}(w)\to 1
as
w\to\infty
. We claim that these large deviations are not universal at the integers.

We discuss the probability of the event
S_{n}\geq(\mu+k)n
, where
S_{n}/n\stackrel{{\!{}_{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}\mu=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}]
,
for three illustrative examples of
{\Lambda}
, which differ in the approach to
1
in the large
x
limit:

\displaystyle\text{(a)}\;{\Lambda}(x)=1\wedge x;\;\;\;\text{(b)}\;{\Lambda}(x)=1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-x};\;\;\;\text{(c)}\;{\Lambda}(x)=\frac{x}{1+x}.

In case (a), we note that if
W_{i}>n
, the corresponding summand contributes
n
to the sum
S_{n}
. Thus, we get
S_{n}\geq(\mu+k)n
if there are
k
values of
i
for which
W_{i}>n
. The remaining contribution comes from the ‘small’ weights remaining tightly concentrated around
\mu n
.
This strategy turns out to be optimal and has a probability of order
n^{-k(\beta-2)}
, similar in flavour to Theorem
1.1
.

In case (b), we can consider a strategy where there are exactly
k
weights being at least
cn\log{n}
for some large constant
c>0
. The contribution coming from these large weights is at least
nk(1-n^{-c})
. The contribution coming from the remaining small weights is again tightly concentrated around
\mu n
, so they contribute with good probability at least
\mu n+n^{1-c}
to the sum. This strategy has probability of order
(cn\log{n})^{-k(\beta-2)}
, which vanishes faster than
n^{-k(\beta-2)}
, but is still much cheaper than having
k+1
vertices with weight of order
n
, and we believe it to be optimal.

In case (c), as before, we can always choose
k+1
vertices of weight of order
n
. However, another strategy similar to case (b) can be obtained by choosing
k
weights to be at least
n^{b}
for some appropriate
b>1
, and arguing as before that the contribution from the small weights is highly concentrated. Depending on
k
and
\beta
, either of these strategies can be optimal, and we expect to see a phase transition depending on
k
and
\beta
in this case.

3.
Concentration of the bulk: Proof of Proposition
2.1

In this section, we show that the short edges are essentially those in
E_{\rm main}
and the measure
\mu_{n}
is asymptotically equivalent to the empirical measure of the edge lengths
in the unconditioned model. We define
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
to be the cube of volume
n
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centred at zero.

3.1.
Setup and main techniques

Recall the definition of
E_{\rm main}
given by (
2.1
),

E_{\rm main}(G_{n})=\{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}\colon d_{n}(x,y)\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d},\,W_{x}\vee W_{y}\leq n^{a}\},

where
\varepsilon_{n}^{d}=n^{-\gamma}
for some

0<\gamma<(1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\wedge\tfrac{1}{d}
and
a>0
such that
a(2\vee(\beta-1))<(1-\gamma-\tfrac{1}{\alpha})\wedge\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Our main goal is to prove that
|E_{\rm main}|
is well-concentrated around
n\mu
. The number of vertices in our graph, denoted by
N_{n}
, is equal to
n
in the lattice case, and a
\mathrm{Poi}(n)
-distributed random variable in the Poisson case. In this case it is helpful to condition on the number of vertices. Let
\mathbb{P}_{N_{n}}
denote the probability of our graph models conditioned on the number of vertices
N_{n}
and write
\mathbb{E}_{N_{n}}
for the associated expectation.

For
M>0
, define the interval

\displaystyle I_{M}(n):=[n-M\sqrt{n\log n},n+M\sqrt{n\log n}].

(3.1)

Standard Chernoff concentration bounds
[
17
, Remark 2.6]
for Poisson random variables give us, for Poisson models,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(N_{n}\notin I_{M}(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{M^{2}n\log n}{2(n+M\sqrt{n\log n})}\right)=n^{-\frac{M^{2}}{2+M\sqrt{\log n/n}}},

(3.2)

for all
M>0
. In particular, choosing
M
sufficiently large, for example such that
M^{2}>3k(\beta-2)
, we get that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(N_{n}\notin I_{M}(n)\right)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}),

(3.3)

for Poisson models.
Once we condition on the number of vertices
N_{n}
, the graph
G_{n}
is completely determined by a vector
{\mathbf{W}}_{N_{n}}=(W_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N_{n}}
of i.i.d. weights with law (
1.1
), the vector
{\mathbf{X}}_{N_{n}}=(X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N_{n}}
of conditionally i.i.d. uniform positions in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
in the Poisson case, resp. locations of all the lattice points in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
in the lattice case, and
the edge indicators

{\mathbf{A}}_{N_{n}}=(A_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq N_{n}}\in\{0,1\}^{\binom{N_{n}}{2}}.

We denote the degree of the
i
th vertex by
D_{i}=D_{X_{i}}
. This setup allows us to provide a single proof that covers both cases simultaneously. For notational simplicity we will abbreviate
N_{n}
to
N
for the rest of the paper.

In this section we actually prove a more general result than just for
E_{\rm main}
, which involves a continuous bounded function
f\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
evaluated on the edge-lengths. The first step is to define a function
h_{n}
that depends on the edge indicators as well as the positions and weights of vertices and the value of
f
on the distances between the positions. We let

{\mathbf{a}}_{N}={(a_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}\in\{0,1\}^{\binom{N}{2}},\phantom{X}{\bf w}_{N}\in[1,\infty)^{N},\phantom{X}\text{ and }{\mathbf{x}}_{N}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}.

We define the function
h_{n}:\{0,1\}^{\binom{N}{2}}\times[1,\infty)^{N}\times(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which depends on
f
as

h_{n}({\mathbf{a}}_{N},{\mathbf{w}}_{N},{\mathbf{x}}_{N})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}a_{i,j}f(d_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{w_{i}\vee w_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}.

(3.4)

The main result of this section is a concentration result for
h_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})
around the value
n{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]/2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
W
be a random variable as in (
1.1
) and
f\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
a continuous bounded function. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
, as
n\to\infty
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|h_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})-\frac{n}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\right|>\delta n\right)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

Note that if
f=1
, then
h_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})=|E_{\rm main}|
while
\frac{n}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]=n\mu
. Hence Proposition
2.1
is a direct corollary of Proposition
3.1
.

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

The key tool behind proving the concentration result is
two
applications of McDiarmid’s inequality on
h_{n}
. The first application conditions on the weights and positions, so that the edge indicators are independent Bernoulli random variables, and the second application is for the conditional expectation of
h_{n}
given the weights and positions.

It turns out to be convenient to include the indicator of a
good event
in the function
h_{n}
. For this, let
\varepsilon_{n}=n^{-\gamma}
for some
0<\gamma<1/d
, and define
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})
as the partition of
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
of equally-sized cubes, each of side length
\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}
. Note that
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})
contains at most
\varepsilon_{n}^{-d}
many such cubes. For
M>0
, consider the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}:=\{\forall\,\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n}):\ |\mathcal{C}\cap\mathbf{X}_{N}|\in I_{M}(N\varepsilon_{n}^{d})\},

(3.5)

where we recall the interval
I_{M}(N\varepsilon_{n}^{d})
from (
3.1
). In words,
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
corresponds to the event that the number of points of
\mathbf{X}_{N}
contained in each cube in the partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})
lies in the given interval
I_{M}(N\varepsilon_{n}^{d})
. Note that for a cube
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})
, and conditionally on
N
, the number of points in
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathbf{X}_{N}
is
{\rm Bin}(N,\varepsilon_{n}^{d})
-distributed.
Using standard Chernoff bounds, and a union bound for the number of cubes, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle 1-{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{d}\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})}{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(|\mathcal{C}\cap\mathbf{X}_{N}|\notin I_{M}(N\varepsilon_{n}^{d})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon_{n}^{d}\exp\left(-M^{2}\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{d}N)/3\right)\leq cN^{-M^{2}/3}n^{\gamma d(1+M^{2}/3)},

(3.6)

for some constant
c>0
. Hence, by taking an expectation over
N
,

\displaystyle 1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{N\in I_{M}(n)\}}(1-{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right))\right]+{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\notin I_{M}(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq c\,2^{M^{2}/3}n^{-M^{2}/3}n^{\gamma d(1+M^{2}/3)}+{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\notin I_{M}(n)\right).

Using (
3.3
) and the fact that
0<\gamma<1/d
, we can choose
M
sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle 1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right)

\displaystyle=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

(3.7)

Therefore it suffices to work conditionally on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
. Define the function

\displaystyle g_{n}\colon\{0,1\}^{\binom{N}{2}}\times[1,\infty)^{N}\times(\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d})^{N}

\displaystyle\to[0,\infty),

(3.8)

\displaystyle g_{n}(\mathbf{a}_{N},\mathbf{w}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{N})

\displaystyle={\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}h_{n}(\mathbf{a}_{N},\mathbf{w}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{N}).

The main technical result needed to prove Proposition
3.1
is the following concentration bound on
g_{n}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\delta>0
. For both the lattice and Poisson case, there exist constants
{\sf K}_{1},{\sf K}_{2}>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
, conditionally on the event
N\in I_{M}(n)
,

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})-\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\right]\right|\geq\delta n\right)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right))+2\exp\left(-{\sf K}_{1}\delta^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{-d}\right)+2\exp\left(-{\sf K}_{2}\delta^{2}n^{1-2a(2\vee(\beta-1))}\right).

Another technical lemma we need provides bounds on the conditional expectation of the degree of a vertex, given the number of vertices
N
and the weight of the vertex.

Lemma 3.3
.

For both the lattice and Poisson case, there exist positive constants
{\sf k},{\sf K}>0
such that, for any
1\leq i\leq N
and
w\geq 1
,

{\sf k}\left(\tfrac{N-1}{n}\wedge 1\right)w\leq\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left.D_{i}\;\right|\;W_{i}=w\right]\leq{\sf K}\left(\tfrac{N-1}{n}\vee 1\right)w.

We postpone the proofs of both lemmas till the end of this section and proceed with the proof of Proposition
3.1
based on these technical results.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Throughout this proof we let
\sf F
denote the upper bound for the function

We start by splitting the difference between

and the expectation into four parts as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt\left|h_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})-\frac{n}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\left|g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})-\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\right]\right|

Bound on (

\displaystyle\hskip-20.0pt{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left|g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})-\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\right]\right|\geq\delta n/4\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[2\exp\left(-\frac{{\sf K}_{1}\delta^{2}}{16}\varepsilon_{n}^{-d}\right){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{{N\in I_{M}(n)}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+{\mathbb{E}}\left[2\exp\left(-\frac{{\sf K}_{2}\delta^{2}}{16}n^{1-2a(2\vee(\beta-1))}\right){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{{N\in I_{M}(n)}\}}\right]

Bound on (

We will prove that, for all

Observe that

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Big]-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\right]\Big|

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))-g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[(1-{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}){\sf F}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}\Big]+{\sf F}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[A_{i,j}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})>\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+{\sf F}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[A_{i,j}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}>n^{a}\}}\right].

The first term on the right-hand side can be bounded by
{\sf F}N^{2}[1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right)]
, using that
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}A_{i,j}\leq N^{2}
.
For the second term on the right-hand side, we note that by (
1.5
) and (
1.7
),
when
W_{1}\vee W_{2}\leq n^{a}
and
d_{n}(X_{1},X_{2})>\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}
,

\displaystyle p_{X_{1},X_{2}}(W_{1},W_{2})\leq{\sf C}\left(\frac{\kappa(W_{1},W_{2})}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\sf C}^{1+\alpha}\left(\frac{n^{\xi-1}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\sf C}^{1+\alpha}n^{-\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)},

where
\xi:=a(2\vee(\beta-1))
.
It follows that

\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[{\sf F}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})>\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}\Big]\leq{\sf F}{\sf C}^{1+\alpha}N^{2}n^{-\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)}.

(3.17)

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}>n^{a}\}}\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>n^{a}\}}\Big]=N{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[D_{1}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{1}>n^{a}\}}\right].

By Lemma
3.3
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}_{N}[D_{1}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{1}>n^{a}\}}|W_{1}]\leq{\sf K}_{1}\frac{N}{n}W_{1}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{1}>n^{a}\}}
and hence

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[D_{1}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{1}>n^{a}\}}\right]\leq{\sf K}_{1}\frac{N}{n}\int_{n^{a}}^{\infty}wf_{W}(w)dw=\frac{{\sf K}_{1}(\beta-1)}{\beta-2}\frac{N}{n}n^{-a(\beta-2)}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[2{\sf F}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}>n^{a}\}}\Big]\leq\frac{2{\sf F}{\sf K}_{1}(\beta-1)}{\beta-2}N^{2}n^{-1-a(\beta-2)}.

(3.18)

Putting (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) back into the upper bound for (
3.16
) we get

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt\Big|\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\right]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\sf F}}{2}nN^{2-M/4}+\frac{{\sf F}{\sf C}^{1+\alpha}}{2}N^{2}n^{-\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)}+\frac{2{\sf F}{\sf K}_{1}(\beta-1)}{\beta-2}N^{2}n^{-1-a(\beta-2)}.

Now recall that on the event
N\in I_{M}(n)
, it holds that
N=(1+o(1))n
.
Therefore, on this event, the right-hand side can be bounded from above by a constant multiple of
n\left(n^{2-M/4}+n^{1-\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)}+n^{-a(\beta-2)}\right)
. By selecting
M
large enough, this is
o(n)
, using our assumptions that
\beta-2>0
and
\xi<(1-\gamma-1/\alpha)
and therefore
\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)>1
.
Hence, by picking a sufficiently large
M
, we use (
3.3
) to conclude that

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Big]\Big|\geq\delta n/4\Big)

\displaystyle\leq{{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\notin I_{M}(n)\right)}=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

Bound on (
3.13
).
For the final term,
recall
\lambda_{f}(w)
from (
1.19
) and
\lambda(w,z)
from (
1.18
), for
w\geq 1
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let us write

\lambda_{f}^{n}(w):=\begin{cases}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Q}_{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(\|z\|)\lambda(z,w)&\text{in the lattice case,}\\
\int_{\mathbb{Q}_{n}}f(\|z\|)\lambda(z,w)\,dz&\text{in the Poisson case.}\end{cases}

Recalling that
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
is the centred cube of volume
n
, we observe

\mathbb{E}_{N}\Bigg[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Bigg]=\begin{cases}\frac{n-1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}^{n}(W)\right]&\text{for lattice models,}\\
\frac{N(N-1)}{2n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}^{n}(W)\right]&\text{for Poisson models,}\end{cases}

where in the lattice case we use that
N=n
. Note that
\lambda_{f}^{n}(w)
converges pointwise to
\lambda_{f}(w)
as
n
tends to infinity. Together with the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{f}(W)]<\infty
, the dominated convergence theorem implies that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{f}^{n}(W)]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]
.
It follows that for the Poisson case, on the event
N\in I_{M}(n)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Big]=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right],

since
N=(1+o(1))n
. The same equality holds for the lattice case.
For Poisson models, this implies that for all
\delta>0
and sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Big]-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\Big|>\delta/4,\,N\in I_{M}(n)\Big)=0.

Using (
3.3
) we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{N}\Big[\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}A_{i,j}f(d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j}))\Big]-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\Big|>\delta/4\Big)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\notin I_{M}(n)\right)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

by choosing
M
sufficiently large. The bound above also holds for lattice models. This deals with the last term (
3.13
) and therefore finishes the proof of Proposition
3.1
subject to Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
.
∎

3.3.
Proofs of technical lemmas

The rest of this section is dedicated to the proof of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
. Since we condition on the number of vertices
N
, the graph
G_{n}
will be based on a vector
{\mathbf{W}}_{N}=(W_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
of i.i.d. Pareto weights and
{\mathbf{X}}_{N}=(X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
of uniform positions in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
for the Poisson case and
(X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
being just the locations of the lattice points of
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
for the lattice case. The key tool in proving Lemma
3.2
lies in applying McDiarmid’s inequality
[
22
]
twice.

We begin by recalling the version of McDiarmid’s inequality we shall use, given in
[
5
]
. Consider a function
g:\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Y}_{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, where each
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
is a probability space. Moreover, let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Y}_{m}
be in the domain of
g
. We call this the
good event
and say that
g
satisfies the
bounded difference inequality on
\mathcal{G}
with non-negative constants
(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
if for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and for all
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}),(y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{m}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{G}
with
y_{j}=y_{j}^{\prime}
for
j\neq i
,

\displaystyle|g(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})-g(y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y_{m}^{\prime})|\leq c_{i}.

(3.19)

For such a function
g
, for any independent random variables
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
on the spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{Y}_{m}
and for all
\delta>{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{P}}\left(|g(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})-{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})\mid(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m})\in\mathcal{G}\right]|>\delta\right)

(3.20)

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}\right)+2\exp\Big(-\tfrac{2\left(\delta-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{2}}\Big).

For the proof we apply McDiarmid’s inequality to
|g_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left.g_{n}\;\right|\;\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N}\right]|
and also to
|\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left.g_{n}\;\right|\;\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[g_{n}\right]|
, where we write
g_{n}
for
g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})
for notational simplicity. The first term considers the probability conditioned on the weights and edges. Under this conditioning the edge indicators are independent so that McDiarmid’s inequality can be applied without specifying a good event. The good event is however needed to apply McDiarmid’s inequality to the second term, where the good event will be
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
.

Both these steps will yield an upper bound in terms of
N
that is
o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})
when
N=(1+o(1))n
, which holds for the lattice as well as the Poisson case with a probability that converges to
1
, by (
3.3
) with an error of order
o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Recall
g_{n}(\cdot)
from (
3.8
). Following the discussion above, we bound the probability on the left hand side of (
3.9
), for
\delta>0
, by

\displaystyle\hskip-10.0pt{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})-\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})]\right|\geq n\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\,|\,{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}]\right|\geq n\delta/2\right)

(3.21)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\,|\,{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}]-\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})]\right|\geq n\delta/2\right).

(3.22)

Concentration bulk edges given the weights and positions.

We begin with a bound on the term (
3.21
). Choose
m=\binom{N}{2}
and let
{\mathcal{Y}}_{(i,j)}
be the canonical probability space
\{0,1\}
for
1\leq i<j\leq N
. Conditionally on the weights
\mathbf{W}_{N}
and the positions
\mathbf{X}_{N}
, we consider
g_{n}
as a function on
\{0,1\}^{m}
.

For the application of McDiarmid’s inequality we take the good event to be the entire space, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}=\{0,1\}^{m}
so that in particular
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{G}^{c}\right)=0
. Denote by
{\mathbf{A}}_{N}^{{}_{(i,j)}}
the vector obtained from
{\mathbf{A}}_{N}
where we replace
A_{i,j}
by
A^{\prime}_{i,j}=1-A_{i,j}
and leave all other coordinates unchanged.
Then, conditionally on
\mathbf{W}_{N}
and
\mathbf{X}_{N}
, the function
g_{n}
satisfies, for all
1\leq i<j\leq N
, the bounded difference inequality

\displaystyle|g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})-

\displaystyle g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N}^{(i,j)},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})|

\displaystyle\leq{\sf F}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}|A_{i,j}-A_{i,j}^{\prime}|{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}

\displaystyle\leq{\sf F}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}=:c_{ij}.

The sum of the square of differences is given by

\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}c_{ij}^{2}={\sf F}^{2}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}.

On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
, the number of points in every cube of side length
\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}
is at most
2N\varepsilon_{n}^{d}
, and any ball of radius
R
is covered by at most
(2Rn^{-1/d}\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})^{d}
such cubes. This implies that, for any
y\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{{d}}
,

{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{j},y)\leq R\}}\leq(2Rn^{-1/d}\varepsilon_{n}^{-1})^{d}\cdot 2N\varepsilon_{n}^{d}=2^{d+1}R^{d}n^{-1}N.

(3.23)

Taking
R=\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}
it follows that
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}c_{ij}^{2}\leq{{\sf F}^{2}}2^{d+1}N^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{d}
and hence McDiarmid’s inequality (
3.20
) implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{N}(\left|g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})-\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}({\mathbf{A}}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\,|\,{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}]\right|\geq n\delta/2\,|\,{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N})

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{\delta^{2}n^{2}}{2^{d+2}{\sf F}^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{d}N^{2}}\right).

Concentration of bulk for weights and positions.

We next establish an upper bound for (
3.22
) by again an application of McDiarmid’s inequality. For the setup, let
m=N
,
\mathcal{Y}_{i}=[1,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and take
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
as the good event. We define the function
\hat{g}_{n}\colon[1,\infty)^{N}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, for
\mathbf{w}_{N}\in[1,\infty)^{N}
and
\mathbf{x}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
, as

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{n}({\mathbf{w}}_{N},{\mathbf{x}}_{N})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left.g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{w}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{N})\;\right|\;\mathbf{W}_{N}=\mathbf{w}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N}=\mathbf{x}_{N}\right]

(3.25)

\displaystyle={\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}\sum_{{}_{1\leq i<j\leq N}}f(d_{n}(x_{i},x_{j}))p_{x_{i},x_{j}}(w_{i},w_{j}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{w_{i}\vee w_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}.

and observe that
\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\left.g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{n})\;\right|\;{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}\right]=\hat{g}_{n}({\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}).

In addition, we note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[\hat{g}_{n}(\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[\hat{g}_{n}(\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[\hat{g}_{n}(\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\mid\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right]{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right).

We claim that
\hat{g}_{n}
satisfies the bounded difference inequality on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
. To see this, for any two vectors
{\mathbf{w}}_{N}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{N}
of weights and positions, denote by
{\mathbf{w}}_{N}^{{}_{(i)}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{N}^{{}_{(i)}}
the vectors where the
i^{\rm th}
entry is replaced by any value
w_{i}^{\prime}\in[1,\infty)
and
x_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and all other coordinates are unchanged. Then,

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt\left|\hat{g}_{n}({\mathbf{w}}_{n},{\mathbf{x}}_{n})-\hat{g}_{n}({\mathbf{w}}_{n}^{(i)},{\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{(i)})\right|

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}{\sf F}\sum_{{{1\leq j\leq N}\atop{j\neq i}}}p_{x_{i},x_{j}}(w_{i},w_{j}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{w_{i}\vee w_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}{\sf F}\sum_{{1\leq j\leq N}\atop{j\neq i}}p_{x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j}}(w_{i}^{\prime},w_{j}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x_{i}^{\prime},x_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{w_{i}^{\prime}\vee w_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}.

We will show that there is a constant
{\sf K}_{0}>0
that does not depend on
w_{i}
and
x_{i}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}{\sf F}\sum_{j\neq i}p_{x_{i},x_{j}}(w_{i},w_{j})

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{w_{i}\vee w_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}

\displaystyle\leq{\sf K}_{0}n^{2a((\beta-2)\vee 1)}(1+n^{-1}N).

(3.26)

The other sum satisfies the same upper bound, which implies that
\hat{g}_{n}(\cdot)
satisfies a bounded difference inequality on the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
with bounds

c_{i}=2{\sf K}_{0}n^{a((\beta-2)\vee 1)}(1+n^{-1}N)

and thus

\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{2}\leq 4{\sf K}_{0}^{2}n^{2a(2\vee(\beta-1))}N(1+n^{-1}N)^{2}.

We will first finish the application of McDiarmid’s inequality (
3.20
) using (
3.26
). For this, we note that on the event
N\in I_{M}(n)
, it holds for
n
sufficiently large that

\frac{n\delta}{2}-(1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right))2{\sf K}_{0}n^{a(2\vee(\beta-1))}(1+n^{-1}N)>\frac{\delta}{4}n,

which implies
n\delta/2>(1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right))\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}
.
Moreover, there exists a constant
Q
such that

4{\sf K}_{0}^{2}n^{2a(2\vee(\beta-1))}N(1+n^{-1}N)^{2}\leq Qn^{1+2a(2\vee(\beta-2))}.

Therefore, using that
\hat{g}\leq{\sf F}N^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{N}(\left|\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{n})\,|\,{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}]-\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{n})]\right|\geq n\delta/2)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|\hat{g}_{n}(\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[\hat{g}_{n}(\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{N})\mid\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right]\right|\geq n\delta/4\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left({\sf F}N^{2}(1-{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right))\geq n\delta/4\right).

Noting that on the event
I_{M}(N)
we have
N^{-2}\geq n^{-2}/2
it follows from (
3.6
) that for
n
sufficiently large the last term is zero. Thus, by applying (
3.20
) to the first part we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{N}(\left|\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{n})\,|\,{\mathbf{W}}_{N},{\mathbf{X}}_{N}]-\mathbb{E}_{N}[g_{n}(\mathbf{A}_{N},\mathbf{W}_{N},\mathbf{X}_{n})]\right|\geq n\delta/2)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\leq 2(1-{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right))+2\exp\left(-\frac{\frac{\delta^{2}}{8}n^{2}}{Qn^{1+2a(2\vee(\beta-2))}}\right).

Combining this with (
3.24
), the main result (
3.9
) follows by picking
{\sf K}_{1}=2^{d+2}{\sf F}
and
{\sf K}_{2}=(8Q)^{-1}
.
It only remains to establish the bound (
3.26
). We first use (
1.7
) to get

\displaystyle p_{X_{i},X_{j}}(W_{i},W_{j})\leq\left(1\wedge{\sf C}\left(\tfrac{\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})}{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})^{d}}\right)^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq{\sf C}^{{1/(\alpha d)}}\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})^{1/d}\}}+{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})>{\sf C}^{{1/(\alpha d)}}\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})^{1/d}\}}{\sf C}\left(\tfrac{\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})}{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})^{d}}\right)^{\alpha}.

Thus, the left-hand side of (
3.26
) can be bounded from above by a constant multiple of

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}\sum_{{1\leq j\leq N}\atop{j\neq i}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq{\sf C}^{1/(\alpha d)}\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})^{1/d}\wedge\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle+{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}}\sum_{{1\leq j\leq N,}\atop{j\neq i}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{{\sf C}^{1/(\alpha d)}\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})^{1/d}<d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}\}}{\sf C}\left(\tfrac{\kappa(W_{i},W_{j})}{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})^{d}}\right)^{\alpha}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}\vee W_{j}\leq n^{a}\}}.

4.
Concentration of the long edges: Proof of Proposition

Recall that we denote the number of vertices in our graph by

.
Recall

from (

) for

.
For both lattice and Poisson models we remark that, for all

with
x\neq y

and
d_{n}(x,y)>\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}

\displaystyle p_{x,y}(W_{x},W_{y})\leq{\sf C}\left(\frac{\kappa(W_{x},W_{y})}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n}\right)^{\alpha}\leq{\sf C}^{1+\alpha}\left(\frac{n^{\xi-1}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}\right)^{\alpha},

where

, using (
1.5
) and (
1.7
). Recall that we suppose that
\varepsilon_{n}
and

satisfy the conditions of Proposition
2.1
. More precisely,
\varepsilon_{n}=n^{-\gamma}
for some

and
\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)>1
. Define, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle p_{n}:=(n^{\xi-1}/\varepsilon_{n}^{d})^{\alpha}=\delta^{-\alpha}n^{\alpha(\xi-1+\gamma)},

where
\delta>0
. We begin by proving the proposition for lattice models. Let

be a
\mathrm{Bin}(N^{2},p_{n})
-distributed random variable with mean

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|E_{\rm long}|\geq n\delta\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}\geq n\delta\right)
and the upper bound tends to zero stretched-exponentially fast again using a standard Chernoff bound
[
17

for binomial random variables, and hence faster than

for any

. This concludes the proof of Proposition
2.2
for lattice models.

For Poisson models, conditionally on the number of vertices

, let

be a random variable, which is
\mathrm{Bin}({N^{2}},p_{n})
-distributed and note again that

is stochastically dominated by
B_{n}
. Further

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}>\delta n\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}>{N^{2}}p_{n}+n\delta/2\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left({N^{2}}p_{n}\geq n\delta/2\right).

(4.1)

The conditional mean of

{N^{2}}p_{n}

) give

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(B_{n}>{N^{2}}p_{n}+n\delta/2\right)\leq\exp\left({-\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}}{8({N^{2}}p_{n}+\delta n/6)}}\right).

By taking expectations we can bound the first summand of the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left({-\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}}{8({N^{2}}p_{n}+\delta n/6)}}\right){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{N\in I_{M}(n)\}}\right]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{P}}\left(N\notin I_{M}(n)\right).

By choosing
M
sufficiently large, (
3.3
) gives us that the second summand above equals

. The first summand of the above inequality can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle\exp{\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}n^{2}}{8{n^{2}p_{n}}+4\delta n/3)}\right)}=\exp{\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{8\delta^{-\alpha}n^{-\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)}+4\delta/(3n)}\right)}.

This can be further upper bounded by a constant multiple of

for large

. Since
\alpha(1-\xi-\gamma)>1
, the above equals
o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})
. We are left to bound the second summand of (

). Note that, for the same
\delta_{1}>0

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left({N^{2}}p_{n}\geq n\delta/2\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left({N}\geq\sqrt{n\delta/(2p_{n})}\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left({N}\geq n^{1+\delta_{1}/2}\right),

using the fact that
\sqrt{n/p_{n}}=\delta^{\alpha/2}n^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha/2(1-\xi-\gamma)}>n^{1+\delta_{1}/2}
. Since

is a Poi
(n)
-distributed random variable, standard concentration bounds
[
17

imply

, which concludes the proof. ∎

5.3.
The few-jumps phenomenon: Proof of Proposition
5.3

It remains to prove Proposition
5.3
. We first state and prove a
result that shows that it is unlikely to have many small macroscopic contributions.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. For all
\delta>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}\lambda_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{x}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n\right)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

(5.17)

Lemma
5.6
tells us that the sum
\sum_{x\in V_{n}}\lambda_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{x}\leq\varepsilon n\}}
cannot contribute significantly at a linear scale. Let us discuss the proof strategy. First, we use Lemma
5.2
to reduce the question to understanding the probability

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>K\delta n\right),

for some fixed constant
K>0
. The sum inside the last probability cannot contribute at least
K\delta n
with good probability when we choose
\varepsilon
arbitrarily small. To show this, we use a Chernoff inequality to reduce the problem to understanding exponential moments of the form
\mathbb{E}[\exp{(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})}]
, where
\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
is the truncated random variable
W{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}<W<\varepsilon n\}}
and where
W
has density (
1.1
), and we take
s_{n}=b\log{n}/n
for a suitable
b>0
. This we achieve by a second order Taylor expansion and choosing the scale
b
appropriately.

Proof.

Let
(W_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be an i.i.d. collection with law (
1.1
). For both lattice and Poisson models, we write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}\lambda_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{x}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n/{\sf K}\bigg),

where the inequality holds by Lemma
5.2
, and we use that the
W_{x}
are independent of the location of
x
. Recall that for lattice models,
N
equals the number
n
of points on the integer lattice of
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{n}
and for Poisson models,
N
is a Poi(
n
)-distributed random variable. Recalling
I_{M}(n)
from (
3.1
) and using (
3.3
) we deduce that, for Poisson models,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n/{\sf K}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n/{\sf K},\,N\in I_{M}(n)\right)+o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})\phantom{wefillthisspacetoalign}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{N\in I_{M}(n)\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n/{\sf K}\;\right|\;N\right)\right]+o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

From this it is clear that to prove the lemma for both lattice and Poisson models, it suffices to show that, for all
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n\right)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}),

(5.18)

where
(m_{n})_{n}
is any deterministic sequence satisfying
m_{n}=(1+o(1))n
. To prove (
5.18
), define the truncated random variable
\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}:=W{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W<\varepsilon n\}}
. The Chernoff bound gives us that, for all
s_{n}>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}_{i}>m\right)\leq{\rm e}^{-s_{n}m_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})\right]^{m_{n}}.

Let
s_{n}:=b\log n/n
, for a constant
b>0
to be chosen later. By the Taylor expansion of
{\rm e}^{x}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+s_{n}\hat{\mu}_{n}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})^{k}}{k!}\right],

(5.19)

where
\hat{\mu}_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}]
converges to zero as
n
tends to infinity. We start by investigating the third term in (
5.19
). We choose constants

such that

for all

. Thus, for sufficiently large

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})^{k}}{k!}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cs_{n}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})^{2}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}<c/s_{n}\}}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp{(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\geq c/s_{n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cs_{n}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[W^{2}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W<c/s_{n}\}}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp{(s_{n}W)}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W\in[c/s_{n},\varepsilon n)\}}\right],

(5.20)

where the second inequality holds since

(\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})^{2}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}<c/s_{n}\}}=W^{2}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}<W<\varepsilon n\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W<c/s_{n}\}}

and
{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}>c/s_{n}\}}={\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}<W<\varepsilon n\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W>c/s_{n}\}}
. Note that
W
is

-bounded for
3-\beta<\ell<2
. Thus we can choose
\ell>0
such that

and

\displaystyle s_{n}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[W^{2}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W<c/s_{n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=s_{n}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[W^{\ell}W^{2-\ell}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W<c/s_{n}\}}\right]\leq c^{\ell}s_{n}^{2-\ell}{\mathbb{E}}\left[W^{2-\ell}\right]=o(s_{n}),

where the last step holds since

and
s_{n}
converges to

. Next, note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp{(s_{n}W}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W\in[c/s_{n},\varepsilon n)\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{s_{n}\varepsilon n}{\mathbb{P}}\left(W\in[c/s_{n},\varepsilon n)\right)\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{s_{n}\varepsilon n}{\mathbb{P}}\left(W\geq c/s_{n}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{e}}^{s_{n}\varepsilon n}(s_{n}/c)^{\beta-1}=s_{n}c^{1-\beta}{\mathrm{e}}^{s_{n}\varepsilon n}s_{n}^{\beta-2}.

Plugging in
s_{n}=b\log n/n
, we get that, for some constant

,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp{(s_{n}W^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W\in[c/s_{n},\varepsilon n)\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq s_{n}C^{\prime}n^{b\varepsilon+2-\beta}(\log n)^{\beta-2}.

For
b<(\beta-2)\varepsilon^{-1}
, we get

n^{b\varepsilon+2-\beta}(\log n)^{\beta-2}=o(1)
and so, by (
5.20
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})^{k}}{k!}\right]

\displaystyle=o(s_{n}),

(5.21)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. From (
5.21
) it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp{(s_{n}\hat{W}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+s_{n}\hat{\mu}_{n}+o(s_{n})=1+o(s_{n})\leq\exp{(o(s_{n}))}.

Putting everything together, it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{i\leq m_{n}}W_{i}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{i}<\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp{(-s_{n}\delta n+m_{n}o(s_{n}))}=\exp(s_{n}n(-\delta+o(1)))

\displaystyle=o(n^{1-b\delta}),

using that
s_{n}=b\log n/n
,
m_{n}=(1+o(1))n
and
n^{o(1)}=o(n)
. Choosing
b>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
(1-k(\beta-2))/\delta<b<(\beta-2)\varepsilon^{-1}
is satisfied, we obtain that
1-b\delta<k(\beta-2)
which concludes the proof of (
5.18
).
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{i}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>bn\}}>\rho n,\mathcal{A}_{k}\Big)

\displaystyle=\binom{m_{n}}{k}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{i}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>bn\}}>\rho n,W_{1},\dots,W_{k}>bn,W_{{k+1}},\dots,W_{{m_{n}}}\leq bn\Big)

\displaystyle=\binom{m_{n}}{k}(1-(\beta-1)(bn)^{1-\beta})^{m_{n}-k}(\beta-1)^{k}\int_{bn}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{bn}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\frac{1}{n}({\lambda}_{n}(t_{i})+\ldots+{\lambda}_{n}(t_{k}))>\rho\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}^{-\beta}dt_{i}.

Observe that
(1-(\beta-1)(bn)^{1-\beta})^{m_{n}-k}=1+o(1)
since
n^{-(\beta-1)}=o(n^{-1})
, using
(m_{n}-k)/n\to 1
. By a change of variables
y_{i}=t_{i}/n
, we can write the last expression as

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\binom{n}{k}n^{-k(\beta-1)}(\beta-1)^{k}\int_{b}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{b}^{\infty}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\frac{1}{n}({\lambda}_{n}(ny_{1})+\cdots+{\lambda}_{n}(ny_{k}))>\rho\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}^{-\beta}dy_{i},

since
m_{n}=(1+o(1))n
. By Lemma
5.7
and dominated convergence, the last expression is asymptotically equivalent to
n^{-k(\beta-2)}F_{b}(\rho)
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Proposition
5.3
.

Proof of Proposition
5.3
.

The following proof holds for both lattice and Poisson models. Recall the definition of
F_{b}(\rho)
given in Lemma
5.8
and recall that we write
F(\rho)
for
F_{0}(\rho)
. Let
k=\lceil\rho\rceil
. Fix
0<\delta<1
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
(\rho-\delta,\rho+\delta)\subset(k-1,k)
, and such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{x}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n\Big)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

(5.27)

By Lemma
5.6
such choices are possible. A lower bound then easily follows using Lemma
5.8
, by observing

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\geq n^{a}\}}>\rho n\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\geq\varepsilon n\}}>\rho n\Big)

(5.28)

\displaystyle=(F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)+o(1))n^{-k(\beta-2)},

since
n^{a}=o(n)
. Thus,

\displaystyle n^{k(\beta-2)}\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\geq n^{a}\}}>\rho n\right)\geq F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)+o(1).

The desired lower bound in Proposition
5.3
follows by letting
\varepsilon\searrow 0
and using monotone convergence. To establish an upper bound, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\geq n^{a}\}}>\rho n\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{n^{a}\leq W_{x}\leq\varepsilon n\}}>\delta n\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\geq\varepsilon n\}}>(\rho-\delta)n\Big)

(5.29)

\displaystyle=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})+(F_{\varepsilon}(\rho-\delta)+o(1))n^{-k(\beta-2)}\leq(F(\rho-\delta)+o(1))n^{-k(\beta-2)},

where the equality follows from (
5.27
) and Lemma
5.8
and the last inequality holds since
F_{\varepsilon}(\rho-\delta)\leq F(\rho-\delta)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle n^{k(\beta-2)}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x\in V_{n}}{\lambda}_{n}(W_{x}){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\geq n^{a}\}}>\rho n\Big)

\displaystyle\leq F(\rho-\delta)+o(1).

The desired upper bound follows by first letting
n\to\infty
, and then letting
\delta\searrow 0
and using the continuity of
F
on the interval
(k-1,k)
.∎

6.
Bulk in edge-length distribution: Proof of
Proposition
2.5

Proof of
Proposition
2.5
.

We need to show that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}f(d_{n}(x,y))-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\Big|>\delta,|E_{n}|>n(\mu+\rho)\bigg)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

Let
R>0
be such that
\text{supp}(f)\subseteq(0,R)
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}f(d_{n}(x,y))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}f(d_{n}(x,y)){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\vee W_{y}\leq n^{a}\}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}f(d_{n}(x,y)){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\vee W_{y}>n^{a}\}}.

Since
R<\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}
for
n
sufficiently large, Proposition
3.1
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}f(d_{n}(x,y)){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\vee W_{y}\leq n^{a}\}}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\lambda_{f}(W)\right]\Big|>\delta\bigg)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

Therefore it suffices to show that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}f(d_{n}(x,y)){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\vee W_{y}>n^{a}\}}>\delta\bigg)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

We bound
|f(d_{n}(x,y))|\leq{\sf F}
, and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}\vee W_{y}>n^{a}\}}\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{x,y\in V_{n}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}>n^{a}\}},

so that we are left to show that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{x,y\in V_{n}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}>n^{a}\}}>\frac{\delta}{2{\sf F}}n\bigg)=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}).

We first condition on the number of nodes
N
and then consider the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
, which indicates that the number of points in each each cube of length
\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/d}
is inside the interval
[\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n-M\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n)},\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n+M\sqrt{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{d}n)}]
. Recall that for
M
sufficiently large,
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}\right)=1-o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})
(see (
3.7
)). Hence, it is enough to study the sum under the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon_{n},M}
. Recall that on this event,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})<R\}}<2^{d+1}R^{d}n^{-1}N\;\text{ for each $1\leq i\leq N$, }

see (
3.23
). Hence, we can bound

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>n^{a}\}}\leq 2^{d+1}R^{d}n^{-1}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>n^{a}\}}.

We now consider

\mathbb{P}_{N}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>n^{a}\}}>\frac{\delta}{2^{2+d}R^{d}{\sf F}}n^{2}N^{-1}\Big).

Conditioned on
N
, the sum is just a
\mathrm{Bin}(N,{\mathbb{P}}\left(W>n^{a}\right))
random variable. Now we condition on the event that
N\in I_{M}(n)
, which happens with probability
1-o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})
for
M
large enough by (
3.3
). Then, on this event,
\frac{\delta}{2^{2+d}R^{d}{\sf F}}n^{2}N^{-1}>N{\mathbb{P}}\left(W>n^{a}\right)
for
n
sufficiently large. Hence, an application of Hoeffding’s inequality yields that (writing
Q:=\frac{\delta}{2^{2+d}R^{d}{\sf F}}
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{N}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{i}>n^{a}\}}>\frac{\delta}{2^{2+d}R^{d}{\sf F}}n^{2}N^{-1}\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(N,{\mathbb{P}}\left(W>n^{a}\right))>Qn^{2}N^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-2Q^{2}n^{4}N^{-3}\right).

Since the last term is bounded by
2\exp\left(-2^{-2}Q^{2}n\right)
on the event
N\in I_{M}(n)
, we conclude that, for
M
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{x,y\in V_{n}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\{x,y\}\in E_{n}\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{n}(x,y)\leq R\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{W_{x}n^{a}\}}>\frac{\delta}{2{\sf F}}n\Big)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{N\in I_{M}(n)\}}{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(N,{\mathbb{P}}\left(W>n^{a}\right))>Qn^{2}N^{-1}\right)\right]+o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-2^{-2}Q^{2}n\right)+o(n^{-k(\beta-2)})=o(n^{-k(\beta-2)}),

which is what we needed to show.
∎

7.
Travelling wave in edge-length distribution: Proof of
Proposition
2.6

Our proof strategy is to show that the edges of macroscopic length are those in
E_{\rm high}
and the form of the condensate emerges from the fact that
E_{\rm high}
originates from
k
uniform vertices that all have weight of order
n
. The total mass, that is at least
\rho n
, of these corresponds to edges with length of order
n^{1/d}
. For the proof, we rely on the proofs of the previous sections.

To make the intuition outlined above precise, define the function
g:(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow[0,1)
by

\displaystyle g(x,y):=\text{Vol}\left([-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]^{d}\cap\left(\mathcal{B}(0,y)\setminus\mathcal{B}(0,x)\right)\right).

(7.1)

Fix
0\leq b_{1}<b_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/2
. Define the vertex set
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(x)={\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{(b_{1},b_{2})}(x)
as the intersection of
V_{n}
and the annulus
\mathcal{B}(x,b_{2}n^{1/d})\setminus\mathcal{B}(x,b_{1}n^{1/d})
centred at
x\in V_{n}
.
Write
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
for
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}^{(b_{1},b_{2})}({0})
. Remark that for all
x\in V_{n}
, for the lattice case, we have that
|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(x)|=(1+o(1))g(b_{1},b_{2})n
, while for the Poisson case,
|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(x)|
is Poisson distributed with mean
g(b_{1},b_{2})n
. Recall that
(W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{i\in[N]}
is the order statistic of the weights
(W_{x})_{x\in V_{n}}
.
For
i\in[N]
, recall that
X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\in V_{n}
denotes the random vertex with the
i^{\text{th}}
largest weight, i.e.,
W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}=W_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
. Define
\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}:=\{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},\ldots,X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\}
, to be the set of locations of the first
i
largest weight vertices.

Remark that to prove
Proposition
2.6
, it suffices to prove the statement for
f={\mathbbm{1}}_{[b_{1},b_{2})}
, for any
b_{1},b_{2}
satisfying
0\leq b_{1}<b_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}/2
. For
w\in(0,\infty)
, define
\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w)
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w):=\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}\cap\left(\mathcal{B}(0,b_{2})\setminus\mathcal{B}(0,b_{1})\right)}{{\Lambda}}(w,z)\,dz,

(7.2)

where
{{\Lambda}}(w,z)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\varphi\left(\frac{\|z\|^{d}}{\mathcal{K}(w,W)}\right)\right]
, defined in (
1.9
).

Recall that
\mathbb{P}^{x}
denotes the probability
\mathbb{P}
conditioned on having a point at
x\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
w\in(0,\infty)
and
x\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
. For both lattice and Poisson models,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(x)}A_{x,z}\to\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w),

(7.3)

where the convergence is in probability with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{\,x}
conditionally on
W_{x}=wn
.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

Fix
w\in(0,\infty)
throughout the proof. We begin by proving the lemma for the lattice case.
Representing the neighbourhood
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}(x)
of
x
by
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\mathbb{Q}_{n}
with
x
mapped to the origin, we get
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\mathbb{Q}_{n}\cap\mathcal{B}({0},b_{2}n^{1/d})\setminus\mathcal{B}({0},b_{1}n^{1/d})
. Conditionally on
W_{{0}}=wn
, we note that
(A_{0,y})_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}
are independent Bernoulli-
{\mathbb{E}}\left[p_{{0},y}(wn,W_{y})\right]
distributed random variables.
Using standard concentration arguments (see e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 2.8.]
), it suffices to show that
\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}^{0}[\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}A_{{0},y}\mid{W_{0}=wn}]\to\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w)
as
n\to\infty
. We write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}^{\,{0}}\left[\left.\sum_{y\in U_{n}}A_{{0},y}\;\right|\;W_{{0}}=wn\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|}{n}\int_{1}^{\infty}f_{W}(t)\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}\varphi\left(\frac{\|y\|^{d}}{\kappa(wn,t)}\right)dt.

(7.4)

By a slight modification of Lemma
5.7
, working with
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}
instead of
V_{n}
, we get that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}\varphi\left(\frac{\|y\|^{d}}{\kappa(wn,t)}\right)=\frac{1}{g(b_{1},b_{2})}\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}\cap\left(\mathcal{B}(0,b_{2})\setminus\mathcal{B}(0,b_{1})\right)}\varphi\left(\tfrac{\|y\|^{d}}{\mathcal{K}(w,t)}\right)dy,

since
|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|=(1+o(1))g(b_{1},b_{2})n
. We can further conclude by the dominated convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}^{\,{0}}\left[\left.\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}A_{{0},y}\;\right|\;W_{{0}}=wn\right]\to\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}\cap\left(\mathcal{B}(0,b_{2})\setminus\mathcal{B}(0,b_{1})\right)}f_{W}(t)\,\varphi\left(\tfrac{\|z\|^{d}}{\mathcal{K}(w,t)}\right)dzdt.

The proof for the Poisson case is of a similar flavour. First note that given
{0}\in V_{n}
and the weight
W_{{0}}=wn
, the locations and the weights of the vertices contributing to
\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}A_{{0},y}
are distributed as a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity

\displaystyle h(y,t)=f_{W}(t)\,\varphi\left(\frac{\|y\|^{d}}{\kappa(wn,t)}\right){\mathbbm{1}}_{\{t\geq 1\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{y\in\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\cap(\mathcal{B}(0,b_{2}n^{1/d})\setminus\mathcal{B}(0,b_{1}n^{1/d}))\}}.

(7.5)

This implies that given
{0}\in V_{n}
and the weight
W_{{0}}=wn
, the sum
\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}A_{{0},y}
is a Poisson random variable with parameter

\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}\cap(\mathcal{B}(0,b_{2}n^{1/d})\setminus\mathcal{B}(0,b_{1}n^{1/d}))}h(y,t)dydt.

This is an analogue of Lemma
5.4
and we omit the proof as it is a simple adaptation of the proof of
[
15
, Lemma 2.1]
. Using Poisson concentration arguments
[
17
, Remark 2.6]
, it suffices to prove that the mean of the Poisson random variable (
7.5
) converges to the right-hand side of (
7.3
). Just as in the lattice case, this follows from a modified version of Lemma
5.7
, using that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|\right]=g(b_{1},b_{2})n
. We omit the details.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

For lattice and Poisson models and
w_{1}\geq\dots\geq w_{k}>0
, conditionally on
(W_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},\dots,W_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)})=(w_{1}n,\dots,w_{k}n)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}\stackrel{{\!{}_{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\rightarrow}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i}),

where
\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w)
is given by (
7.2
).

Proof.

The following proof holds for both lattice and Poisson models. Let
\varepsilon>0
. A union bound gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{y\in\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon\;\Bigg|\;(W_{(j)})_{j\in[k]}=(w_{j}n)_{j\in[k]}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in\,{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}-\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon/k\;\Bigg|\;(W_{(j)})_{j\in[k]}=(w_{j}n)_{j\in[k]}\Bigg).

(7.6)

We will show that, for any
i\in[k]
, the summand of the right-hand side above is
o(1)
. To be able to apply Lemma
7.1
requires still some work since the summand above conditions on the
k
highest weights instead of on
W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}
. Fix
i\in[k]
and recall
\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}:=\{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},\ldots,X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\}
. Using the triangle inequality we can upper bound the summand by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\end{subarray}\setminus\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}-\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon/2k\;\Big|\;(W_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)})_{j\in[k]}=(w_{j}n)_{j\in[k]}\Big)

(7.7)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},X_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}}\Big|>\varepsilon/2k\;\Big|\;(W_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)})_{j\in[k]}=(w_{j}n)_{j\in[k]}\Big).

(7.8)

Note that the second summand equals zero for sufficiently large
n
since we have the deterministic bound
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},X_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}}\leq k/n<\varepsilon/2k
. Therefore it remains to show that the first summand (
7.7
) tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity. The fact that the weights
(W_{x})_{x}
are independent and that the random variable
A_{x,y}
only depends on the locations
x,y
and weights
W_{x},W_{y}
, gives that, for
\ell\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\setminus\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}}}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}\geq\ell\;\Big|\;(W_{(j)})_{j\in[k]}=(w_{j}n)_{j\in[k]}\Big)

(7.9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\setminus\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}\end{subarray}}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}\geq\ell\;\Big|\;W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}=w_{i}n,\,W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\setminus\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\setminus\mathbf{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle(k)}\end{subarray}}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}\geq\ell\;\Big|\;W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}=w_{i}n,\,W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\end{subarray}}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}\geq\ell\;\Big|\;W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}=w_{i}n,\,W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\Big).

(7.10)

Similarly, we can lower bound (
7.9
) by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\end{subarray}}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}-k\geq\ell\;\Big|\;W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}=w_{i}n,\,W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\Big).

(7.11)

Thus, for sufficiently large
n
, (
7.7
) can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})}A_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)},y}-\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon^{\prime}\;\Big|\;W_{X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}}=w_{i}n,\,W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}({X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}})\Big),

for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon/2k-k/n
. Observing that the location of the vertex
X_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
is just a uniformly chosen (lattice) point in
\mathbb{T}_{n}^{d}
, the above equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\,{0}}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}A_{{0},y}-\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon^{\prime}\;\Big|\;W_{{0}}=w_{i}n,\,W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\,{0}}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}A_{{0},y}-\Lambda_{[b_{1},b_{2})}(w_{i})\Big|>\varepsilon^{\prime}\;\Big|\;W_{{0}}=w_{i}n\Big)}{\mathbb{P}^{\,{0}}\Big(W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\;\Big|\;W_{{0}}=w_{i}n\Big)}.

(7.12)

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\,{0}}\Big(W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\;\Big|\;W_{{0}}=w_{i}n\Big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}^{\,{0}}\Big(W_{y}\leq w_{k}n\,\forall y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\Big)={\mathbb{E}}^{\,{0}}\left[{\mathbb{P}}\left(W\leq w_{k}n\right)^{|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|}\right],

and recall that
|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|=(1+o(1))g(b_{1},b_{2})n
for lattice models and
|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|
is
{\rm Poi}(g(b_{1},b_{2})n)
-distributed for Poisson models. Using the distribution of
W
given by (
1.1
), we have
\mathbb{E}^{0}[{\mathbb{P}}\left(W\leq w_{k}n\right)^{|{\mathcal{U}}_{n}|}]\rightarrow 0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
. We can conclude that (
7.7
) tends to zero by applying Lemma
7.1
to the first factor of (
7.12
).
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

Conditionally on the event

Concluding remarks on local limits.

Note that the proof of Proposition
2.9
shows that the subgraph of the conditional graph
G_{n}
given
|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)
induced by removing the vertices with the
k
highest weights has a local limit which equals the unconditional local limit
G_{\infty}
of
G_{n}
. The local limit of the conditional graph
G_{n}
given
|E_{n}|\geq n(\mu+\rho)
itself, however, does not exist. This is because with asymptotically positive probability a randomly sampled vertex connects to one of the
k
high-weight vertices, and as these vertices have infinite degree in the limit, the two-neighbourhood of a randomly sampled vertex becomes infinite with positive probability. However, one can still give a local description of the conditional graph, which we now describe informally.

Sample a random vertex
o_{n}
in the conditional graph. With high probability, it is not one of the
k
high-weight vertices. Now start exploring the neighbourhood of this vertex in some order, e.g., the depth-first order. At every step of the exploration, when we discover an incidence to one of the
k
high-weight vertices, we
stop exploring from that vertex, and go back to the last visited vertex to restart exploring from there. In particular, this method of exploration avoids discovering the ‘forward’ neighbours of a high-weight vertex, of which there are linearly many, as we have seen. This ‘restricted’ exploration can be formalized easily, which we omit to keep the discussion informal.
Now, say we explore the conditional graph in this fashion, and look at the set of explored vertices that are at graph distance
r
from
o_{n}
, and call this explored graph
B_{n}(r)
. Then,
B_{n}(r)
, rooted at
o_{n}
, for any fixed
r
, converges in the local (Benjamini-Schramm) topology (see
[
13
, Chapter 2]
) to the graph
B_{r}
, whose construction we describe next.

We briefly describe the local limit
G_{\infty}
of the unconditional graph
G_{n}
. The vertex set of
G_{\infty}
is the corresponding infinite vertex set - the infinite integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in the lattice case and the Palm version
\Gamma\cup\{0\}
of a standard Poisson process
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the Poisson case. Then the weight for each vertex is sampled as an independent random variable with density (
1.1
), and conditionally on the weights and locations, edges are placed independently between vertex pairs with probability (
1.2
). The graph
G_{\infty}
is rooted at
0
.

Now we describe the graph
B_{r}
. Let
B_{\infty}(r)=(V_{\infty}(r),E_{\infty}(r))
be the graph neighbourhood of radius
r
about
0
in
G_{\infty}
. Let the weights of the vertices in
V_{\infty}(r)
be
(W(v))_{v\in V_{\infty}(r)}
. Note that the
W(v)
are not i.i.d., since the weights are tilted due to the fact that they are in the
r
-neighbourhood of
0
, and the precise law of
(W(v))_{v\in V_{\infty}(r)}
is rather involved. The construction of the graph
B_{r}
is as follows.

\rhd

Append
k
extra vertices, say
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
, to
V_{\infty}(r)
and call the new vertex set
V_{r}
.

\rhd

The vertices
(v_{i})_{i\in[k]}
have marks
(U_{i},Y_{i})
, where the first coordinates are i.i.d. uniform points of
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
independent of
(Y_{i})_{i\in[k]}
, and the second coordinates have joint distribution (
1.16
).

\rhd

Given the variables sampled above, we place an edge between each vertex
v
of
V_{\infty}(r)
and each
v_{i},

i\in[k],
independently, with probability
\varphi(\|U_{i}\|^{d}/\mathcal{K}(Y_{i},W(v))).
Denote the new edge set by
E_{r}
.

\rhd

The graph
B_{r}
has vertex set
V_{r}
and edge set
E_{r}
.

This description gives a way to compute the typical distance (graph distance between two randomly sampled vertices) in the conditional graph
G_{n}
: Independently sample two random vertices
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
in
G_{n}
.
Start exploring in the fashion described above from both
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
, and let
d_{1}
(resp.,
d_{2}
) be the distance from
o_{1}
(resp.,
o_{2}
) up to which one has to explore to see a high-weight vertex. Denote by
H_{i}
the set of high-weight vertices exactly at graph distance
d_{i}
from
o_{i}
, for
i=1,2
. Recalling from Remark
2.8
that the high-weight vertices form a clique, we note that the graph distance from
o_{1}
to
o_{2}
in the conditional graph is
d_{1}+d_{2}+{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{H_{1}\cap H_{2}=\emptyset\}}
with high probability.
This quantity as a sequence in
n
is in fact a tight sequence of random variables - starting from a random vertex in the conditional graph, one finds a high-weight vertex within tight graph distance from it.
