On Enriched Categories and Induced Representations
Joshua A. Leslie 1, Ralph A. Twum 2

Abstract
. We show that induced representations for a pair of diffeological Lie groups exist, in the form of an indexed colimit in the category of diffeological spaces.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 18D20; Secondary 22D30

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a group. A representation of
G
may be described as a functor

F:G\rightarrow Vect_{\mathbb{K}}
, where
G
is a single-set category with the elements of
G
forming the arrows of the category, and
Vect_{\mathbb{K}}
is the category of vector spaces over a field
\mathbb{K}
.
Any subgroup
H
of
G
is automatically a full subcategory of
G
, hence the representation of

G
restricts to a representation of
H
. Thus the functor
F
restricts to a subfunctor on
H
, which
we may also denote by
F
.

Now, given a functor
T:H\rightarrow Vect_{\mathbb{K}}
, we consider if
T
can be
canonically

extended to a functor from
G
to
Vect_{\mathbb{K}}
. This process of
induction
has been defined
for a variety of pairs
G,H
:

(a)

G,H
finite groups;

(b)

G,H
complex analytic groups, with
H
a closed subgroup of
G
. This led to the
Borel-Weil theorem and its extensions by Bott and Kostant(
[
Bot57
]
,
[
Kos61
]
,
[
Dem76
]
,
[
Lur
]
). Other cases have also been considered, for instance

(c)

G,H
direct limit groups, with
H
a closed subgroup of
G
. In this case both
G
and
H
are infinite dimensional (
[
Kum02
]
,
[
DP04
]
).

We want to consider the concept of induced representations of infinite dimensional Lie groups using results from enriched category
theory and diffeology. We will specifically deal with the pair of groups
G=D^{G^{\infty}}(M)
and
H=S^{G^{\infty}}(M)
defined below.

Our goal is the nonconstructive proof of the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S^{G^{\infty}}(M)
be the regular diffeological Lie group of super symplectic diffeomorphisms of a supermanifold
M
.
S^{G^{\infty}}(M)
is a Lie subgroup of the group of super diffeomorphisms
D^{G^{\infty}}(M)
; further, there exist induced representations for Hilbert space representations for these two regular Lie groups.

We use existing results from enriched category theory to show that the induced representation exists, first as a Kan extension, which can be written as an
indexed limit
. In order to show that the representation exists, we need to enlarge the category from that of smooth (super-)manifolds to a more convenient category. We use the category of
diffeological spaces
, whose properties we will recall in the following section.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall some useful facts about Diffeological spaces, and discuss the category of diffeological spaces, which include (super) manifolds. We also define the two groups in Theorem
1.1
above.

Sections 3 and 4 introduce the main categorical notions: Kan extensions and indexed limits, and we show that induced representations can be interpreted as Kan extensions, which can also be interpreted as indexed limits. We collect these facts and prove Theorem
1.1
in Section 5. Section 6 contains some remarks on Diffeological categories and regularity of (diffeological) Lie groups.

2.
Diffeology and Diffeological Spaces

2.1.
Diffeological Spaces

The axioms of a diffeological space, or a diffeology on a set, were set forth by Iglesias-Zemmour
[
IZ13
]
. In the definition of a smooth manifold, we define a maximal atlas consisting of compatible charts on a set
X
. In a diffeology, we focus on the maps themselves between coordinate patches of open sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 0
and the set
X
, satisfying three axioms. These maps are called
plots
of the diffeology, and the set
X
with the defined collection of plots is called a diffeological space. The advantage of considering a diffelogical space is that smooth manifolds have a natural diffeology, and so form a full subcategory of the category of diffeological spaces.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a set. An
n
-parametrization of
X
is defined as a map
f:U\rightarrow X
, where
U
is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 0
. A constant parametrization is a map of the form
f:U\rightarrow X
such that
f(u)=x
for all
u\in U
.

Definition 2.2
.

A diffeology
\mathcal{D}
on
X
is a collection of parametrizations of
X
satisfying the following axioms:

(D1)

\mathcal{D}
contains the constant parametrizations.

(D2)

If
p:U\rightarrow X
is a parametrization such that
\forall u\in U
, there exists a neighborhood
V
of
u
such that
p|_{V}
is in
\mathcal{D}
, then
p
itself is in
\mathcal{D}
. In other words, the collection
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the axioms of a set-valued sheaf.

(D3)

Given a parametrization
p:U\rightarrow X
in
\mathcal{D}
, if
f:V\rightarrow U
is a smooth map, then
p\circ f
is a parametrization in
\mathcal{D}
.

The parametrizations in
\mathcal{D}
are called
plots
of the diffeology on
X
and the pair
(X,\mathcal{D})
is called a
diffeological space
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a set and
\mathcal{F}
be a collection of parametrizations on
X
. The diffeology on
X
induced by
\mathcal{F}
is the intersection of all diffeologies on
X
containing
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{F})
and
(Y,\mathcal{G})
be diffeological spaces. A map
f:X\rightarrow Y
is
smooth
if
f
takes plots of the diffeology on
X
to plots of the diffeology on
Y
.

A diffeological group is defined naturally, as a group such that the product and inverse maps are smooth in the sense of diffeology. Since Lie groups (resp. super Lie groups) are manifolds (resp. supermanifolds), they are diffeological groups.

Definition 2.5
.

A difeological space
(S,F)
is
lattice type
(or
L-type
) when given any two plots
f:M_{1}\rightarrow S
,
g:M_{2}\rightarrow S

at a point
f(x)=g(y)
, there exists a third plot
h
through which the germs of
f
at
x
and
g

at
y
factor, that is there exists a map
h:N\rightarrow S
with
f=h\circ\varphi
,
g=h\circ\gamma
where
\varphi:\tilde{M_{1}}\rightarrow N
and
\gamma:\tilde{M_{2}}\rightarrow N
are plots such that
\tilde{M_{i}}\subset M_{i}
,
i=1,2
are neigborhoods of
x
and
y

respectively and
\varphi(x)=\gamma(y)
.

All diffeological groups are
L
-type (
[
Lau08
]
, Lemma
2.37
). The property of being
L
-type allows for the definition of the tangent space at the identity, and a diffeological structure on that space, which gives a diffeological vector space.

Definition 2.6
.

A diffeological group
G
is called a
diffeological Lie group
when
T_{e}G
is a diffeological vector space such that

(DL1)

For any nonzero vector
\alpha\in T_{e}G
, there exists a smooth real-valued map
T:T_{e}G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
T(\alpha)\neq 0
:

(DL2)

Every plot of
T_{e}G
factors smoothly through a smooth linear map of a compact Hausdorff topological vector space into
T_{e}G
.

Let us apply the above definitions to that of supermanifolds. Using the construction in the paper by Leslie
[
Les03
]
, we form a super vector space of super dimension
m,n
. We use this to define the open sets for the diffeology on super manifolds and super Lie groups. The parametrizations (and hence the plots) can be adapted similarly.

Let
\Gamma
be the Grassmann algebra over
\mathbb{R}
generated by the elements
1
and
\{\xi\}_{i\in I}
, where
I
is a countably infinite set. In line with the theory of super vector spaces, all
\xi_{i}
are odd, with degree 1. Let
\mathcal{J}
be the collection of finite subsets of
I
, ordered by inclusion. Each homogeneous element of
\Gamma
may be expressed as
x_{K}\xi_{i_{1}}\xi_{i_{2}}\dots\xi_{i_{n_{K}}}
,
x_{K}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
K\in\mathcal{J}
and
n_{K}
is the cardinality of
K
. We will use the notation in Rogers
[
Rog07
]
, in which even homogeneous elements are represented using latin letters and odd homogeneous elements are represented using Greek letters.

As seen in
[
Les03
]
,
\Gamma
is a super commutative algebra, with
ab=(-1)^{|a||b|}ba
, where
a,b
are elements of
\Gamma
, with the degree of elements of
\Gamma
extended by linearity from the degree of homogeneous elements of
\Gamma
.

Define
V
to be the
m,n
-dimensional superspace
\Gamma_{0}^{m}\oplus\Gamma_{1}^{n}
, where
\Gamma_{0}
is the subspace of
\Gamma
consisting of even elements and
\Gamma_{1}
is the subspace of
\Gamma
consisting of odd elements. Thus

V=\Gamma_{0}\times\Gamma_{0}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{0}\times\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{1}\cdots\times\Gamma_{1}\quad(m,n)\,\text{times}.

We define a topology on
V
as follows: define the inductive limit topology on
\Gamma
, that is the finest topology such that all inclusion maps
\Gamma_{K}\hookrightarrow\Gamma
are continuous, where
\Gamma_{K}
is the finite dimensional subspace generated by elements
\xi_{i_{1}},\dots,\xi_{i_{n_{K}}}
,
K\in\mathcal{J}
. The topologies on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
are defined by restricting the inductive limit topology to the even and odd subspaces of
\Gamma
respectively. Finally the topology on
V
is defined using the product topology on
\Gamma_{0}^{m}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{n}
.

For the objects under study, we have modified Iglesias’ definition of a diffeological space as follows: instead of using open sets in
n
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
n\geq 1
, we consider open subsets of the spaces
\Gamma_{0}^{m}\oplus\Gamma_{1}^{n}
, where
m,n\geq 0
.

We use the following definition of a supersmooth
(G^{\infty})
function from
[
Les11
]
:

Definition 2.7
.

Let
V
and
W
be topological graded modules over
\Gamma_{0}
. A continuous map
f:V\times\cdots\times V\rightarrow W
is said to be an
n
-multimorphism if

(M1)

f
is
n
-multilinear with respect to the ground field
\mathbb{R}
,

(M2)

f(v_{1},\dots v_{i}\gamma,v_{i+1},\dots v_{n})=f(v_{1},\dots,v_{i},\gamma v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n})
, where
v_{i}\in V,1\leq i\leq n
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
,

(M3)

f(v_{1}\dots v_{n}\gamma)=f(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\gamma
,
v_{i}\in V
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
V
and
W
be topological graded modules over
\Gamma_{0}
and let
U\subseteq V
be open in
V
. A function
f:U\rightarrow W
will be called supersmoooth or
G^{\infty}
when for every
n\geq 1
, there exist continuous maps which are regular
k
-multimorphisms in the k terminal variables for each fixed
x\in U
, denoted by
D^{k}f(x;\cdots):U\times V\times\cdots\times V\rightarrow W
, where
k\leq n
, such that the map

F_{k}(h):=f(x+h)-f(x)-Df(x;h)-\frac{1}{2!}D^{2}f(x;h,h)-\cdots-\frac{1}{k!}D^{k}f(x;h,\dots h),1\leq k\leq n

satisfies the property that

G_{k}(t,h):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{F_{k}(th)}{t^{k}},&t\neq 0\\
0,&t=0\end{array}\right.

is continuous at
(0,h)
in
V
.

Now, let
M
be a supermanifold modeled on
V=\Gamma_{0}^{m}\oplus\Gamma_{1}^{n}
, where
m
and
n
are fixed. Thus the charts are
G^{\infty}
maps from open subsets
U
of
V
into
M
. Then
M
is automatically a diffeological space, with the diffeology generated by the
(m,n)
-charts on
M
. This diffeology is by definition the smallest diffeology containing all such charts as plots.

2.2.
The category of Diffeological Spaces

Let us denote the category of diffeological spaces by
\mathfrak{D}
. By definition,
\mathfrak{D}

contains the category of smooth manifolds (and supermanifolds) as a full subcategory.
It also has several useful properties outlined below, as shown by Iglesias
[
IZ13
]
.

Proposition 2.9
.

\mathfrak{D}
is closed under coproducts. For a collection
\{X_{i}|i\in I\}
of
diffeological spaces, the coproduct of the collection is called the
sum
of the diffeological spaces
X_{i}
and is denoted
\displaystyle\sqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
(Iglesias
[
IZ13
]
, 1.39).

Proposition 2.10
.

\mathfrak{D}
is closed under products. Just as in the above result, given an arbitrary collection
\{X_{i}|i\in I\}
, the product object is denoted
\displaystyle\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}
and can be given a canonical diffeology (Iglesias
[
IZ13
]
, 1.55).

Proposition 2.11
.

\mathfrak{D}
is a symmetric monoidal category.

Proof.

The product map is derived from the definition of the product object. In addition, since
\mathfrak{D}
is essentially a concrete category, associativity of the product map holds. We can take any one-point set as a unit object, since all one-point sets are unique up to isomorphism of diffeological spaces (all plots are constant). The coherence axioms are clearly satisfied. The symmetry of
\mathfrak{D}
is given by the canonical map
\gamma:X\times Y\rightarrow Y\times X,(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
.
∎

Proposition 2.12
.

\mathfrak{D}
is complete with respect to (all small) limits and colimits.

Proof.

From Mac Lane (
[
ML98
]
, Corollary V.2.2), we know that
if a category
\mathcal{C}
has equalizers of all pairs of arrows and all small products, then
\mathcal{C}
is small-complete. In
\mathfrak{D}
, the equalizer of pairs of smooth maps
f,g:X\rightarrow Y
is defined to be the subspace
Z
of
X
such that
f(x)=g(x)
for
x\in Z
. The universal arrow is the canonical inclusion map. The coequalizer is defined for pairs
f,g:X\rightarrow Y
by considering the smallest equivalence relation
E\subset Y\times Y
containing all pairs
(f(x),g(x))
, for
x\in X
. The coequalizer is the quotient space
Z=Y/E
, with the universal arrow given by the canonical projection map. From the existence of equalizers, coequalizers, coproducts and products, we conclude that
\mathfrak{D}
is complete.
∎

Proposition 2.13
.

\mathfrak{D}
is Cartesian closed.

Proof.

The map
\phi:\mathfrak{D}(X\times Y,Z)\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}(X,\mathfrak{D}(Y,Z))
given by
(\phi(f)(x))(y)=f(x,y)
for
f:X\times Y\rightarrow Z
is a bijection, and shows that the functor
\mathfrak{D}(Y,-):\mathfrak{D}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
is the right adjoint to the functor
-\times Y:\mathfrak{D}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

The properties of
\mathfrak{D}
as a category make it suitable as an enriching category. This gives us the ability to use the results of Kelly
[
Kel05
]
on indexed limits, indexed colimits and Kan extensions in the case of enriched categories. These ideas are discussed in the following section.

The super Lie groups

and

In

3.
Induced Representations are Kan Extensions

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{M}
be small categories. Recall that (
[
ML98
]
, Chapter
X
) a
right Kan extension of a functor
T:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
along
K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is a functor
R=Ran_{K}T:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
and a natural transformation
\mu:RK\dot{\longrightarrow}T
such that for any other right extension
S
of
T
along
K
and
\sigma:SK\dot{\longrightarrow}T
, there exists a unique natural transformation
\varepsilon:S\dot{\longrightarrow}R
such that
\sigma=\mu\circ\varepsilon K:SK\dot{\longrightarrow}RK\dot{\longrightarrow}T
. This gives the following bijection of sets:

(3.1)

\mathcal{A^{C}}(S,Ran_{K}T)\simeq\mathcal{A^{M}}(SK,T)

This bijection is an adjunction between the functor categories
\mathcal{A^{C}}
and
\mathcal{A^{M}}
. By the universal property,
Ran_{K}T
is unique up to natural isomorphism.

Dually, a
left Kan extension of
T
along
K
is a functor
L=Lan_{K}T:\mathcal{C}\dot{\rightarrow}\mathcal{A}
and a natural transformation
\mu:T\dot{\longrightarrow}LK
such that for any other left extension
S
of
T
along
K
and
\sigma:T\dot{\longrightarrow}SK
, there exists a unique natural transformation
\varepsilon:L\dot{\longrightarrow}S
such that
\sigma=\varepsilon K\circ\mu:T\dot{\longrightarrow}LK\dot{\longrightarrow}SK
. We thus have the following adjunction

(3.2)

\mathcal{A^{C}}(Lan_{K}T,S)\simeq\mathcal{A^{M}}(T,SK).

If
\mathcal{A}
is a small complete category, then (
[
ML98
]
, Chapter
X
) the Kan extensions can be expressed as pointwise limits: for the functors
T:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
and

K:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, the left and right Kan extensions are functors
L,R:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, where for each object

of
\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle Lc

\displaystyle=\text{Colim\text{ }}\left[(K\downarrow c)\xlongrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{M}\xlongrightarrow{T}\mathcal{A}\right]=\text{Colim\text{ }}_{f}Tm,\qquad f\text{ in }(K\downarrow c)

\displaystyle Rc

\displaystyle=\text{Lim\text{ }}\left[(c\downarrow K)\xlongrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{M}\xlongrightarrow{T}\mathcal{A}\right]=\text{Lim\text{ }}_{f}Tm,\qquad f\text{ in }(c\downarrow K)

where
(K\downarrow c)
and
(c\downarrow K)
are the comma (or slice) categories of objects over and under
c
, respectively. Now, we can rewrite
(K\downarrow c)
and
(c\downarrow K)
as follows: consider the functor
F=\mathcal{C}(c,K-):\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\text{Set}
, which assigns to each object of
\mathcal{M}
the set of arrows of the form
f:c\rightarrow Km
, where
m
is an object of
\mathcal{M}
. The category of
elements of

has objects pairs
<m,f>
, where
m
is an object of
\mathcal{M}
and
f\in\mathcal{C}(c,Km)
. If
\tau:m\rightarrow m^{\prime}
is an arrow in
\mathcal{M}
, then we have an induced arrow
\tau^{\ast}:<m,f>\rightarrow<m^{\prime},f^{\prime}>
, given by the commutative diagram

\textstyle{c\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{f^{\prime}}
\textstyle{Km\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{K\tau}
\textstyle{Km^{\prime}}

Therefore the slice category
(c\downarrow K)
is precisely the category of elements of

. Similarly,
(K\downarrow c)
is the category of elements of
\mathcal{C}(K-,c)
.

Let us relate the above definitions to the problem of induced representations, using the categories

H
,
G
and
\mathcal{V}=Vect_{\mathbb{K}}
which were defined in the Introduction, in place of
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{M}
above. We notice immediately that the induced representation can be interpreted as a Kan extension of the functor

. The left and right Kan

representation of

We will focus on the left Kan extension in our case. We will prove that the left Kan extension does exist, hence prove Theorem
1.1
, using the tools provided by diffeology. In the next section, we discuss indexed limits, and illustrate how the Kan extension can be expressed as an indexed limit.

4.
Indexed Limits in Diffeological Categories

The properties of the category
\mathfrak{D}
of diffeological spaces enable us to define an
enriched category
with the morphisms between objects having extra structure:

Definition 4.1
.

(
[
Twu12
]
Definition 5.1.1)
A
Diffeological Category

\mathcal{D}
is a locally small category such that for any objects
x,y
in
\mathcal{D}
, the hom set
\mathcal{D}(x,y)
is a
diffeological space which satisfies the following axiom: for all objects
x,y
and
z
in
\mathcal{D}
and elements
g
in
\mathcal{D}(x,y)
and
f
in
\mathcal{D}(y,z)
, the composition
f\circ g
in
\mathcal{D}(x,z)
defines a smooth map:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(y,z)\times\mathcal{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(x,z)

\displaystyle f\times g

\displaystyle\mapsto f\circ g

where
\mathcal{D}(y,z)\times\mathcal{D}(x,y)
has the product diffeology.

Thus a diffeological category is an enriched category where arrows between objects have the structure of a diffeological space.
Functors defined between diffeological categories are called
smooth
when they induce maps between diffeological spaces of arrows. Smooth natural transformations, smooth adjunctions and smooth universal arrows are defined similarly.

We note that the concept of a diffeological category makes sense, since the category
\mathfrak{D}
of diffeological spaces is itself a diffeological category: the function space diffeology makes the set of functions between diffeological spaces into a diffeological space itself.

Let
G:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be a functor of diffeological categories. We observe, from
[
Kel05
]
and
[
Fre10
]
that the limits and colimits of
G
satisfy the following natural isomorphisms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(c,\text{Lim\text{ }}G)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{J}}(\Delta c,G)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\text{Colim\text{ }}G,c)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{J}}(G,\Delta c)

We can rewrite the cones
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{J}}(\Delta c,G)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{J}}(G,\Delta c)
as

\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{J}}(\Delta 1,\mathcal{C}(c,G-))
and
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{J}^{op}}(\Delta 1,\mathcal{C}(G-,c))
respectively, where in the second case,
\Delta 1
is regarded as a contravariant functor from
\mathcal{J}
to
\mathcal{C}
.

The idea of an indexed (or weighted) limit is to replace the functor

In some cases, we can express indexed limits as ordinary limits. Consider a smooth functor

in
\mathfrak{D}

Proposition 4.4

Let

be smooth functors. Then

We use the fact that the space of natural transformations between

and

can be expressed as an

Nat(F,G)\simeq\int_{c}\mathfrak{D}(Fc,Gc)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(c,\{F,G\})

\displaystyle\simeq\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{J}}(F,\mathfrak{D}(c,G-))=Nat(F,D(c,G-))

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{x}\mathfrak{D}(Fx,D(c,Gx))

\displaystyle\simeq\mathfrak{D}\left(c,\int_{x}\mathfrak{D}(Fx,Gx)\right)=\mathfrak{D}(c,Nat(F,G))

Therefore
\{F,G\}

∎

\mathcal{C}(c,G-)

to

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
and
G:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
be smooth functors. Then
Nat(F,G)\simeq\text{Lim\text{ }}(elF\xrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{G}\mathfrak{D})

Proof.

The limiting cone for the composite functor

consists of objects
\alpha_{(c,x)}:\star\rightarrow Gc
, where
\star
denotes a 1-point set. For a map

, since
\alpha
is a cone. Define a natural transformation
\beta:F\dot{\rightarrow}G
by
\beta_{c}(x):=\alpha_{(c,x)}
. For
f^{*}:(c,x)\rightarrow(c^{\prime},x^{\prime})

\displaystyle Gf(\beta_{c}(x))

\displaystyle=Gf\alpha_{(c,x)}=\alpha_{(c^{\prime},x^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\beta_{c^{\prime}}(Ff(x))

\displaystyle=\beta_{c^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})=\alpha_{(c^{\prime},x^{\prime})}

giving the following

\textstyle{Fc\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\beta_{c}}
\scriptstyle{Ff}
\textstyle{Gc\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{Gf}
\textstyle{Fc^{\prime}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Hence

∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
G:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a smooth functor of diffeological categories. Then given a smooth index functor
F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
,

\{F,G\}\simeq\text{Lim\text{ }}(elF\xrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{G}\mathcal{B})

Proof.

Equation (
4.6
) gives
F\simeq\text{Colim\text{ }}\left(elF\xrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{y^{-1}}\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{C}}\right)\simeq\text{Colim\text{ }}\left(C(c,-):elF\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{C}}\right)
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\{F,G\}\simeq\{\text{Colim\text{ }}\mathcal{C}(c,-),G\}

\displaystyle\simeq\{\Delta 1*\mathcal{C}(c,-),G\}

\displaystyle\simeq\left\{\Delta 1,\{\mathcal{C}(c,-),G\}\right\}

\displaystyle\simeq\{\Delta 1,Gc\}

\displaystyle\simeq\text{Lim\text{ }}_{(c\in elF)}{Gc}

where the canonical limit is indexed by the category
elF
of elements of
F
.
On the other hand,
\text{Lim\text{ }}(elF\xrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{G}\mathcal{B})=\text{Lim\text{ }}_{(c\in elF)}{Gc}
. This establishes the isomorphism.
∎

A similar result holds for the case of indexed colimits. Let
F:\mathcal{C}^{op}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
be a smooth index functor and
G:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a smooth functor of diffeological categories. Then the following result holds:

F*G\simeq\text{Colim\text{ }}\left((elF)^{op}\xrightarrow{Q^{op}}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{G}\mathcal{B}\right)

As before, Theorem (

) gives a representation of

\displaystyle F*G

\displaystyle\simeq(\Delta 1*\mathcal{C}(-,c))*G

\displaystyle\simeq\Delta 1*\left(\mathcal{C}(-,c)*G\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\Delta 1*(Gc)

\displaystyle\simeq\text{Colim\text{ }}_{(c\in elF)}(Gc)

The other side of the isomorphism is
\text{Colim\text{ }}\left((elF)^{op}\xrightarrow{Q^{op}}\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{G}\mathcal{B}\right)=\text{Colim\text{ }}_{(c\in elF)}Gc

∎

\text{Lim\text{ }}\left[(c\downarrow K)\xlongrightarrow{Q}\mathcal{M}\xlongrightarrow{T}\mathcal{A}\right]\simeq\{\mathcal{C}(c,K-),T\}

6.
Remarks

Remark 6.1
.

The definition of diffeological category raises a question; are there examples of categories with the objects not necessarily diffeological spaces, such that the hom-sets have the structure of a diffeological space? It seems that the underlying structures would need to have the structure of a diffeological space, so we can use the function space diffeology. This means that the category
\mathfrak{D}
is its own higher-category: for
G,H
diffeological spaces, the morphism
\mathfrak{D}(G,H)
is also a diffeological space. We can extend this to
2
-morphisms and so on.

Remark 6.2
.

Milnor discusses the issue of regularity of (infinite dimensional) Lie groups in
[
Mil83
]
. Essentially, a Lie group is regular if there exists an exponential map from the Lie algebra to the Lie group, relating representations of both the algebra and the group. In this paper, we have discussed diffeological Lie groups, and it is natural to ask whether they are regular. J-P. Magnot
[
Mag17
]
has shown that this is not the case, so not all diffeological Lie groups are regular. This leads to the question whether all regular diffeological Lie groups can be classified.
