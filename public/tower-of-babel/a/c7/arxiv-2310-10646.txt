Exploring spin of ultralight dark matter with gravitational wave detectors Preprint: KUNS-2979, YITP-23-122, IPMU23-0034
Yusuke Manita, Hiroki Takeda, Katsuki Aoki, Tomohiro Fujita, Shinji Mukohyama

Abstract
We propose a novel method for distinguishing the spin of ultralight dark matter (ULDM) using interferometric gravitational wave detectors. ULDM can be a bosonic field of spin-0, 1, or 2, and each induces distinctive signatures in signals. We find that the finite-time traveling effect causes a dominant signal for spin-0 and spin-1 ULDM, while not for spin-2. By using overlap reduction functions (ORF) of multiple detectors, we can differentiate between the spins of ULDM. Furthermore, we point out that the current constraint on the coupling constant of spin-1 ULDM to baryons becomes 30 times weaker when the finite-time light-travel effect on the ORF is taken into account.



I
Introduction

Dark matter is an unknown non-baryonic component accounting for approximately 20% of energy density in the current universe. Various dark matter models with different masses have been proposed so far. The ultralight dark matter (ULDM) is the lightest model with a mass range of about
10^{-22}-1~{\rm eV}
. While the ULDM must be composed of bosons because such a light fermion cannot form a high-density structure like dark matter halo due to the Pauli blocking
Tremaine and Gunn 1979
, a bosonic field with any integer spin can be a ULDM candidate in principle.

The spin-0 (scalar) ULDM is motivated by QCD axion
Weinberg 1978
;
Wilczek 1978
, as well as superstring theory
Arvanitaki et al. 2010
. Superstring theory expects various scalar fields such as axion, moduli, and dilaton, any of which can be a ULDM candidate
Arvanitaki et al. 2015
. The spin-1 (vector) ULDM, which is also called dark photon, was originally introduced by Ref.
Holdom 1986
and string theory anticipates a light mass dark photon
Goodsell et al. 2009
. In addition, a massive spin-2 field, which arises as a low-energy modification of Einstein’s gravity, is equally a viable candidate for ULDM
Aoki and Maeda 2018
;
Marzola et al. 2018
;
Manita et al. 2022
;
Gorji 2023
.

For each spin and mass, various methods to search for the ULDM have been suggested. The mass range of about
10^{-14}-10^{-10}~{\rm eV}
can be explored by the ground-based interferometric gravitational wave detectors, which is the main focus of this paper. The spin-0 ULDM generates detectable signals when it changes the fundamental constants such as the fine structure constant or the proton-electron mass ratio
Arvanitaki et al. 2015
;
Stadnik and Flambaum 2015
;
Morisaki and Suyama 2019
. The spin-1 ULDM coupled to the
U(1)_{B}
or
U(1)_{B-L}
current leaves signals by displacing the mirror in the detectors
Pierce et al. 2018
. In the case of the spin-2 ULDM, it couples to the energy-momentum tensor of standard model particles due to its gravitational origin and gives signals in the same way as the gravitational waves
Armaleo et al. 2021
;
Manita et al. 2022
.

The spin-0 and spin-1 ULDM have been explored by using real data from gravitational wave detectors. For the spin-0 ULDM, constraints on the coupling constants with the standard model particles have been provided by GEO600
Dooley et al. 2016
and LIGO’s O3 run
Fukusumi et al. 2023
. On the other hand, constraints on the spin-1 ULDM have been obtained by analyzing the data in LIGO’s O1 run
Guo et al. 2019
and O3 run
Abbott et al. 2022
.

While the spin-2 ULDM causes differential motions of the mirrors in detectors as with gravitational waves, the spin-1 and spin-0 ULDM induce common motions. As a result, the signal from the spatial displacement of the mirrors is well suppressed in the spin-0 and spin-1 ULDM, and the “finite-time traveling effect” becomes dominant if the ULDM mass is
m\gtrsim v/L~(\simeq 50{\rm Hz}
for LIGO) where
v
and
L
denote the velocity of ULDM and the arm length of detector
Morisaki and Suyama 2019
;
Morisaki et al. 2021
.

We propose a method to distinguish ULDM signals of spin-0, spin-1, and spin-2 signals by using cross-correlation analysis with multiple detectors. The difference in mirror motions is imprinted on the overlap reduction function (ORF), which is determined by the relative position and orientation of the two detectors. Notably, we find that the ORF for the spin-0 and 1 ULDM signals have a quite different dependency on the relative position and orientation compared to that for spin-2, owing to the finite-time traveling effect in the spin-0 and 1 ULDM signals. Note that the differences in power spectra in ULDM can be also used to distinguish spins
Miller et al. 2022
.

Furthermore, our results suggest that the current upper bound on the coupling constant
\epsilon_{B}
for the spin-1 ULDM should be weakened because the analysis in
Abbott et al. 2022
uses the ORF designed for gravitational waves, not for the spin-1 ULDM with the finite-time traveling effect. Specifically, the constraint on
\epsilon_{B}^{2}
from LIGO’s and Virgo’s O3 run
Abbott et al. 2022
, becomes approximately 30 times less stringent.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we review the ULDM models and their detectable signals in gravitational wave detectors. In Sec.
III
, we develop the method for the cross-correlation analysis of spin-1 and spin-2 ULDM signals. In Sec.
IV
, we show that the finite-time traveling effect induces an ORF whose position and orientation dependence on detectors are different from those for spin-2. We also discuss the current and future constraints on spin-0, spin-1 and spin-2 ULDM. In Sec.
V
, we examine the distinguishability of the signals originating from spin-0, spin-1, and spin-2 ULDM based on the difference of their ORFs. Finally, Sec.
VI
is devoted to a summary and discussion. Throughout this paper, we use the unit of
c=\hbar=1
and the notation
M_{\rm Pl}=1/\sqrt{8\pi G}
where
G
is Newton’s constant.

II
ULDM search in gravitational wave detectors

In this section, we review the models of ULDM and explain how they can be probed by gravitational wave detectors.

II.1
Spin-2 ULDM Search

Spin-2 dark matter is a class of dark matter models that arises from bigravity
Aoki and Mukohyama 2016
;
Babichev et al. 2016a
;
Babichev et al. 2016b
or multi-gravity theories
González Albornoz et al. 2018
. In the ultralight mass range (lighter than a few eV scales), there are viable generation mechanisms such as the misalignment mechanism
Marzola et al. 2018
, production from primordial magnetic fields
Aoki and Maeda 2018
, or production from the anisotropic universe
Manita et al. 2022
, which can account for all or a part of dark matter component of the universe.

In the present Universe, such dark matter can be modeled as a superposition of tensor non-relativistic waves
\varphi_{\mu\nu}(k)
where
k
is the four-momentum satisfying
k^{2}=-m^{2}
. For a plane wave, the linearized equations of motion for the massive graviton around the vacuum yield the relation
k_{\mu}\varphi^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=0,~\varphi^{\mu}{}_{\mu}=0
.
Since we are interested in the non-relativistic waves
k^{0}\simeq m,~k^{i}\simeq 0
, we have
\varphi^{0}{}_{\nu}\simeq 0
and
\varphi^{i}{}_{i}\simeq 0
. Hence, we only consider the traceless part of
\varphi_{ij}
. The spin-2 ULDM (dark graviton) can be modeled by

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}\bm{k}\,\varphi_{ij}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\int d^{3}\bm{k}\,\tilde{\varphi}^{\lambda}(\bm{k})e_{ij}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\cos(\omega t-\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}+\theta)\,,

(1)

where each plane wave
\varphi_{ij}(\bm{k})
have been defined by

\displaystyle\varphi_{ij}(\bm{k})=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\tilde{\varphi}^{\lambda}(\bm{k})e_{ij}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\cos(\omega t-\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}+\theta)\,.

(2)

Since spin-2 ULDM is composed of non-relativistic waves,
\omega
and
\bm{k}
can be approximated as

\displaystyle\omega\simeq m\left(1+\frac{v^{2}}{2}\right)\,,\quad\bm{k}\simeq mv\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,,

(3)

where
v,\hat{\bm{\Omega}},
and
\lambda
are the speed of dark matter, its direction, and the polarization mode, respectively.

\Lambda_{\rm DG}
represents the set of possible polarization modes of the spin-2 ULDM.
For each wave,
the amplitude
\tilde{\varphi}^{\lambda}(\bm{k})
follows an appropriate distribution, such as the Maxwell distribution.
However, the phase
\theta
is a random variable that adheres to a uniform distribution for each
\bm{k}
and
\lambda
.

Since they do not affect our result significantly, we will neglect these randomnesses and assume a constant speed as

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,\tilde{\varphi}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\left(\omega t-mv\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{x}+\theta\right)\,,

(4)

where
\theta
denotes a constant phase shift. In practice, the velocities of the plane waves constituting ULDM can take on a variety of values according to a certain distribution such as standard halo model
Evans et al. 2019
. However, within the scope of our present study, the variance of these velocities is not important and is therefore neglected.

The maximum number of polarization modes is five, denoted by
\Lambda_{\rm DG}=\{+,\times,V_{x},V_{y},S\}
, and
e^{\lambda}_{ij}
are the corresponding polarization bases. To define these polarization tensors, we choose an orthonormal coordinate system with the bases
\{\hat{\bm{m}},\hat{\bm{n}},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\}
. Here,
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
is defined as
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\equiv\bm{k}/|\bm{k}|
, and
\hat{\bm{m}}
and
\hat{\bm{n}}
are unit orthogonal vectors on the polarization plane defined by
\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
. Due to the traceless condition, the polarization bases are given by

\displaystyle e^{+}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\hat{m}_{i}\hat{m}_{j}-\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle e^{\times}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\hat{m}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}+\hat{n}_{i}\hat{m}_{j}\,,

(6)

\displaystyle e^{V_{x}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\hat{m}_{i}\hat{\Omega}_{j}+\hat{\Omega}_{i}\hat{m}_{j}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle e^{V_{y}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\hat{n}_{i}\hat{\Omega}_{j}+\hat{\Omega}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle e^{S}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\hat{m}_{i}\hat{m}_{j}+\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}-2\hat{\Omega}_{i}\hat{\Omega}_{j}\right)\,.

(9)

Note that the number of polarization modes depends on the underlying theoretical framework. For instance, the Hassan-Rosen bigravity theory encompasses all five polarization modes: two tensor modes
e^{+}_{ij},e^{\times}_{ij}
, two vector modes
e^{V_{x}}_{ij},e^{V_{y}}_{ij}
, and one scalar mode
e^{S}_{ij}

Hassan and Rosen 2012
. On the other hand, the Minimal Theory of Bigravity includes only the two tensor modes
e^{+}_{ij}
and
e^{\times}_{ij}~
De Felice et al. 2021
.

The spin-2 dark matter universally couples to the matter fields through

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm int}=\frac{\alpha}{M_{\rm Pl}}\Phi_{ij}T_{\rm m}^{ij}\,,

(10)

where
\alpha
is a dimensionless constant and
T_{\rm m}^{ij}
is the spatial component of the energy-momentum tensor of matter fields. The coupling is exactly the same as the coupling to gravitational waves. In other words, when we ignore all gravitational effects other than the spin-2 dark matter, the matter fields propagate on the metric
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+\alpha\Phi_{\mu\nu}/M_{\rm Pl}
with
\Phi_{0\mu}\simeq 0
. The oscillation of
\Phi_{ij}
then causes a motion of mirrors as with the standard gravitational waves.

In order to investigate the response of the detector, we adopt the proper reference frame to scrutinize the motion of the test mass mirrors. With the center of mass of two mirrors set to
x=0
, the motion of the test mass mirror at
x^{i}
is described by the geodesic deviation equation

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x^{j}}{dt^{2}}=-R_{j0k0}x^{k}\,,

(11)

where
R_{j0k0}
represents the components of the Riemann curvature of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+\alpha\Phi_{\mu\nu}/M_{\rm Pl}
. Note that the proper reference frame is viable only when
\omega L\ll 1
with the arm length
L
. This condition is generally satisfied for ground-based detectors such as LIGO. In the subsequent discussions, we focus only on ground-based detectors and assume
\omega L\ll 1
.

Thanks to the transverse condition
\Phi_{0\mu}=0
, the
j0k0
components of the linearized Riemann curvature are expressed by

\displaystyle R_{j0k0}\simeq-\frac{\alpha}{2M_{\rm Pl}}\ddot{\Phi}_{jk}\,.

(12)

We consider an arm directed along
\hat{\bm{X}}
, where
x^{j}=x\hat{X}^{j}
. For simplicity, the two test mass mirrors are assumed to have identical masses, thus the origin of the coordinate is the midpoint of the arm in the absence of spin-2 ULDM. Consequently, the geodesic equation (
11
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}}=-\frac{\alpha x}{2M_{\rm Pl}}\ddot{\Phi}_{jk}\hat{X}^{j}\hat{X}^{k}\,.

(13)

We denote the coordinates of the input and end mirror as

\displaystyle x_{\rm i}(t)=-\frac{L}{2}+\delta x_{-}(t)\,,\quad x_{\rm e}(t)=\frac{L}{2}+\delta x_{+}(t)\,.

(14)

Then, (
13
) can be perturbatively solved as

\displaystyle\delta x_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\mp\frac{\alpha L}{4M_{\rm Pl}}\Phi_{jk}\hat{X}^{j}\hat{X}^{k}\,.

(15)

Thus, the input and end mirrors move in different directions. In other words, spin-2 ULDM generates a differential motion in two mirrors as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

In the gravitational wave detector, these displacements of the mirrors are converted into a phase shift of a laser. In the case of an L-shaped detector, the signal is converted into a phase difference as

\displaystyle h(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\nu}\frac{\phi(t;\hat{\bm{X}})-\phi(t;\hat{\bm{Y}})}{2L}\,,

(16)

where
\nu
is the laser frequency,
\hat{\bm{X}}
and
\hat{\bm{Y}}
are the unit vectors along each arm, and
\phi(t;\hat{\bm{X}})
is the phase shift of the laser beam during the round trip of the
\hat{\bm{X}}
-arm.

The round-trip phase shift is given by

\displaystyle\phi(t;\hat{\bm{X}})=2\pi\nu(t-T_{r})+\phi_{0}\,,

(17)

where
\phi_{0}
is a constant phase, and
T_{r}
is the round-trip time, expressed by

\displaystyle T_{r}=-x_{\rm i}(t)+2x_{\rm e}(t-L)-x_{\rm i}(t-2L)\,.

(18)

Therefore, the phase shift of the laser, under the existence of the spin-2 ULDM, is

\displaystyle\phi(t;\hat{\bm{X}})=-2\pi\nu(\delta L_{\rm time}+\delta L_{\rm space})+2\pi\nu(t-2L)+\phi_{0}\,,

(19)

where

\displaystyle\delta L_{\rm time}

\displaystyle=-\delta x_{-}(t)+2\delta x_{-}(t-L)-\delta x_{-}(t-2L)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha L}{M_{\rm Pl}}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L}{2}\right)\hat{X}^{i}\hat{X}^{j}\Phi_{ij}(t-L)\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\delta L_{\rm space}

\displaystyle=2(\delta x_{+}(t-L)-\delta x_{-}(t-L))

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha L}{M_{\rm Pl}}\hat{X}^{i}\hat{X}^{j}\Phi_{ij}(t-L)\,.

(21)

The first effect is caused by the displacement of mirrors during the round-trip of the laser, which is known as the finite-time traveling effect. The second effect arises from the difference in displacement between the input and end mirrors, due to the differential motion of the mirror.

The ratio of these displacements is estimated as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L_{\rm time}}{\delta L_{\rm space}}

\displaystyle\simeq\sin^{2}\left(\frac{mL}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx 1.8\times 10^{-5}\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\times 100{\rm Hz}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{L}{4{\rm km}}\right)^{2}\,.

(22)

Thus,
\delta L_{\rm time}
is negligible in the ground-based detectors.

Therefore, we obtain the expression of the spin-2 dark matter signal in the detector as

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{M_{\rm Pl}}D^{ij}\Phi_{ij}(t-L)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{M_{\rm Pl}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\tilde{\varphi}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,,

(23)

where we have defined the antenna pattern function as

\displaystyle F^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\equiv D^{ij}e^{\lambda}_{ij}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,

(24)

with the detector tensor

\displaystyle D^{ij}\equiv\frac{\hat{X}^{i}\hat{X}^{j}-\hat{Y}^{i}\hat{Y}^{j}}{2}\,.

(25)

As shown in (
23
), the signal for spin-2 ULDM is obtained in the same way as the signal for regular massless gravitational waves.

II.2
Spin-1 ULDM Search

Figure 1:
The schematic illustration of mirrors in a gravitational wave detector.
The arrows show the motion of mirrors responding to spin-2 dark matter (left) and spin-0,1 dark matter (right). The former induces differential motion and the latter induces common motion of the mirrors.

We then consider the spin-1 ULDM (dark photon)
Holdom 1986
. Several production mechanisms have been proposed for the non-thermal production of spin-1 ULDM in the early universe, for example, production from cosmic string
Long and Wang 2019
, gravitational production during inflation
Graham et al. 2016
, and so on.

The spin-1 ULDM is also constituted by a superposition of classical waves
A_{\mu}
. For a free massive spin-1 field, the equations of motion yield
k_{\mu}A^{\mu}=0
and
k^{2}=-m^{2}
so we can ignore the temporal component of
A_{\mu}
in the non-relativistic limit. For simplicity, we employ a model with fixed
v
and
\theta
as

\displaystyle A_{i}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})e^{\lambda}_{i}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\left(\omega t-mv\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{x}+\theta\right)\,.

(26)

We have defined the set of polarization as
\Lambda_{\rm DP}=\{x,y,z\}
, and corresponding polarization bases as

\displaystyle e^{x}_{i}=\hat{m}_{i}\,,\quad e^{y}_{i}=\hat{n}_{i}\,,\quad e^{z}_{i}=\hat{\Omega}_{i}\,.

(27)

Suppose that the spin-1 ULDM is a gauge boson associated with a symmetry of the standard model,
B
or
B-L
. Then, the interaction between the spin-1 ULDM and the standard model particles is given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm int}=\epsilon_{D}eA_{\mu}j_{D}^{\mu}\,,

(28)

where
j_{D}^{\mu}
with
D=B
or
D=B-L
represents the corresponding current of standard model particles and
\epsilon_{D}
denotes the coupling constant, normalized by the elementary charge
e
. Consequently, in the presence of the spin-1 ULDM, test mass mirrors in the arm are subject to Coulomb-type force

\displaystyle\bm{F}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{D}eQ_{D}\dot{\bm{A}}(t,\bm{x})\,,

(29)

where
Q_{D}
denotes the
U(1)_{D}
charge of the mirror.

Projecting this force along the direction of the
\hat{\bm{X}}
-arm, the equation of motion for the test mass mirrors is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}}=-\epsilon_{D}e\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\bm{\hat{X}}\cdot\dot{\bm{A}}\,,

(30)

where
M
represents the mass of the mirror. Employing the same coordinate system as (
14
), this equation of motion can be solved as

\displaystyle\delta x_{\pm}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon_{D}e}{\omega}\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\hat{\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{e}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\times\sin\left(\omega t-mv\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{x}+\theta\right)\,.

(31)

Unlike the spin-2 ULDM, the spin-1 ULDM fluctuates the input and output mirrors in the same directions, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. While the motion of mirrors is not a differential motion, the phase shift of the laser is still caused by the following two reasons. Firstly, as explained in the case of the spin-2 ULDM, the positions of the detectors at the emission are different from those at the arrival time due to the finite light-travel time, which causes a change in traveling distance of the laser even for the common motion of the mirrors
Morisaki and Suyama 2019
. Secondly, when the distribution of the spin-1 ULDM has a spatial dependence, i.e.,
v\neq 0
, the forces acting on detectors at different positions are different, leading to a displacement of the arm length.

The net displacement of the arm length is given by a sum of these two effects:

\displaystyle\delta L_{\rm time}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\epsilon_{D}e}{\omega}\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\hat{\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{e}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\sin\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\delta L_{\rm space}

\displaystyle=2\epsilon_{D}evL\frac{Q_{D}}{M}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})(\hat{\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{e}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}))(\hat{\bm{X}}\cdot{\hat{\bm{\Omega}}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,.

(33)

The ratio of these displacements is roughly estimated as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L_{\rm time}}{\delta L_{\rm space}}\sim\frac{mL}{v}

\displaystyle\approx 12.6\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\times 100{\rm Hz}}\right)\left(\frac{L}{4{\rm km}}\right)\left(\frac{200{\rm km/s}}{v}\right)\,.

(34)

Hence, the finite-time traveling effect
\delta L_{\rm time}
dominates for the larger mass than
v/L~(\simeq 50
Hz for LIGO)
Morisaki and Suyama 2019
. This should be contrasted with the spin-2 ULDM (
22
). In the case of spin-2, the leading effect of the spatial displacement
\delta L_{\rm space}
is simply caused by the differential motion of mirrors and thus the finite-time traveling effect
\delta L_{\rm time}
is subdominant. On the other hand, the spatial displacement
\delta L_{\rm space}
appears as a result of a spatial inhomogeneity so
\delta L_{\rm space}
is suppressed by a power of
v
. Hence,
\delta L_{\rm time}
can give a dominant effect in the spin-1 ULDM.

For
\delta L_{\rm space}
, the angular dependence is the same as those of the scalar and vector polarization modes in the spin-2 ULDM. Therefore, using the detector tensor defined by (
25
), the corresponding antenna pattern function is expressed as

\displaystyle F^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})=2D^{ij}\Omega_{i}e^{\lambda}_{j}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}),\quad(\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP})\,.

(35)

In contrast,
\delta L_{\rm time}
exhibits a different angular dependence. Introducing the detector vector as

\displaystyle D^{i}\equiv\hat{X}^{i}-\hat{Y}^{i}\,,

(36)

the corresponding antenna pattern functions are given by

\displaystyle G^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\equiv D^{i}e^{\lambda}_{i}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,\quad(\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP})\,.

(37)

The phase shift of the laser also takes the form of (
19
), and the signal is expressed by (
16
). Therefore, the spin-1 ULDM signal in gravitational waves detectors is represented by

\displaystyle h(t)=h_{\rm time}(t)+h_{\rm space}(t),

(38)

where

\displaystyle h_{\rm time}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\epsilon_{D}e}{\omega L}\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})G^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\sin\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,,

(39)

\displaystyle h_{\rm space}(t)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{D}ev\frac{Q_{D}}{M}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\cos\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,.

(40)

II.3
Spin-0 ULDM Search

The spin-0 ULDM (scalar dark matter) is motivated by solving the strong CP problem
Peccei and Quinn 1977
or superstring theory
Arvanitaki et al. 2010
. They can be produced through the non-thermal mechanism, such as the misalignment mechanism
Abbott and Sikivie 1983
;
Preskill et al. 1983
;
Dine and Fischler 1983
.

As with the previous cases, the spin-0 ULDM is modeled by a superposition of a huge number of classical waves:

\displaystyle\phi(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\varphi(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\cos\left(\omega t-mv\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\cdot\bm{x}+\theta\right)\,.

(41)

We consider the effective interaction Lagrangian in the form of
Damour and Donoghue 2010
;
Arvanitaki et al. 2015

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rm int}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\phi\left[\frac{d_{e}}{4e^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}-\frac{d_{g}\beta_{3}}{2g_{3}}G^{A}_{\mu\nu}G^{A\mu\nu}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-d_{m_{e}}m_{e}\bar{e}e-\sum_{i=u,d}(d_{m_{i}}+\gamma_{m_{i}}d_{g})m_{i}\bar{\psi}_{i}\psi_{i}\right]\,,

(42)

where
F_{\mu\nu}
is a field strength of the electromagnetic fields, and
G^{A}_{\mu\nu}
is a gluon.
d_{i}
are dimensionless parameters whereas
\beta_{3}
and
\gamma_{m_{i}}
are the QCD beta function and the anomalous dimensions of the up and down quarks, and
\kappa=\sqrt{4\pi G}
.

The motion of the mirrors in the arms of the detectors is described by the equation of motion
Morisaki and Suyama 2019

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\bm{x}}{dt^{2}}\simeq-\kappa\alpha_{A}\bm{\nabla}\phi\,,

(43)

where
Damour and Donoghue 2010

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}\approx d_{g}^{*}+\left[-\frac{0.036}{A^{1/3}}-0.02\frac{(A-2Z)^{2}}{A^{2}}\right](d_{\hat{m}}-d_{g})\,.

(44)

Here,
A
and
Z
are the mass number and the atomic number, and we have defined

\displaystyle d_{g}^{*}\approx d_{g}+0.093\left(d_{\hat{m}}-d_{g}\right)\,,

(45)

and

\displaystyle d_{\hat{m}}\equiv\frac{d_{m_{u}}m_{u}+d_{m_{d}}m_{d}}{m_{u}+m_{d}}\,.

(46)

Since the mirrors are largely composed of
\text{SiO}_{2}
,
\alpha_{A}
can be approximately set as
Fukusumi et al. 2023

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}\approx d_{g}+0.083(d_{\hat{m}}-d_{g})\,.

(47)

In the following, we assume that all mirrors are composed by the same components for simplicity.

With the equation of motion (
43
), through a similar procedure as the spin-1 ULDM, the spin-0 ULDM gives the signal in the detectors as

\displaystyle h(t)=h_{\rm time}(t)+h_{\rm space}(t)\,,

(48)

where

\displaystyle h_{\rm time}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\kappa\alpha_{A}v}{\omega L}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L}{2}\right)\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,\varphi(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})G^{z}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\sin\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,,

(49)

\displaystyle h_{\rm space}(t)

\displaystyle=-\kappa\alpha_{A}v^{2}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}\,\varphi(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{z}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\times\cos\left(\omega(t-L)+\theta\right)\,.

(50)

Thus, as with the spin-1 ULDM signal, the finite-time traveling effect dominates if the ULDM mass is larger than
v/L~(\simeq
50 Hz for LIGO). We also note that the antenna pattern functions are the same as those of the scalar polarization mode of the spin-1 ULDM.

II.4
Distinguish ULDM Spin with Single Detector

As we have discussed, the ULDMs of spin-0, 1, and 2 may all give detectable signals in gravitational wave detectors. We now turn to the question of whether we can distinguish the spin of ULDM if signals are detected. At first glance, it apears to be easy to distinguish because the signals are composed of a different set of polarization modes depending on the spin of ULDM. In practice, however, it is difficult to discriminate the spins by
a single detector
. This is because, as explicitly shown in (
23
), (
39
), (
40
), (
49
), and (
50
), the ULDM signal for any spin can be regarded as a plane wave oscillating at angular frequency
\omega
, and therefore their waveforms are completely identical.

It is possible to find a spin-dependence of signals when considering the distribution of
v

Nakatsuka et al. 2022
. The signal in Fourier space is no longer monochromatic and has a width of about
\Delta f/f_{0}\sim v^{2}\sim 10^{-6}
due to the c distribution. Since the shape of the power spectrum depends on the spin, the spin may be distinguishable if we can precisely determine the power spectrum by experiments. In fact, Ref.
Miller et al. 2022
has demonstrated that spin-0 and spin-1 ULDMs can be distinguished in simulations. However, in the mass region where the finite-time traveling effect is dominant, the shapes of the power spectrum for spin-1 and spin-2 are almost the same because both power spectrum has the identical power of
v
. This makes them challenging to be distinguished.

III
Cross-correlation analysis

Let us shift our focus to cross-correlation analysis using
multiple detectors
. Cross-correlation is a method widely utilized in the data analysis of gravitational waves, and it has been employed in searches for dark photons and scalar dark matter
Guo et al. 2019
;
Abbott et al. 2022
;
Morisaki et al. 2021
. In cross-correlation searches, the dependence of the antenna pattern functions is integrated into the overlap reduction function (ORF).
In this section, we will express the signal-to-noise ratio of the cross-correlation signal in terms of ORF and the parameters of ULDM.

III.1
Optimal Filtering and Signal-to-Noise Ratio for Cross-correlation ULDM Search

First, we briefly review optimal filtering in cross-correlation analysis and discuss the application to the ULDM search. The technique employed here is adapted from the stochastic gravitational wave background search outlined by
Allen and Romano 1999
.

We initially define the cross-correlation signal as

\displaystyle S_{IJ}\equiv\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}dt\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}dt^{\prime}s_{I}(t)s_{J}(t^{\prime})Q(t-t^{\prime})\,,

(51)

where
s_{I}(t)
is the output from a given detector with
I
and
J
representing distinct detectors. The symbol
T
indicates the observation time, and
Q(t-t^{\prime})
is a filter function that will be optimized to maximize the value of
S_{IJ}
.

By the Fourier transformation,
s_{I}(t)
and
Q(t)
can be expanded as

\displaystyle s_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\tilde{s}_{I}(f)e^{2\pi ift}\,,

(52)

\displaystyle Q(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\tilde{Q}(f)e^{2\pi if(t-t^{\prime})}\,.

(53)

Note that, as
s_{I}(t)
and
Q(t-t^{\prime})
are real numbers, their Fourier coefficients satisfy
\tilde{s}_{I}(f)^{*}=\tilde{s}_{I}(-f)
and
\tilde{Q}(f)^{*}=\tilde{Q}(-f)
. Substituting these expressions, the cross-correlation signal
S
can be reformulated as

\displaystyle S=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df^{\prime}\delta_{T}(f-f^{\prime})\tilde{s}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{s}_{J}(f^{\prime})\tilde{Q}(f^{\prime}),

(54)

where we have defined
\delta_{T}(f)
as

\displaystyle\delta_{T}(f)\equiv\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}dt~e^{2\pi ift}=\frac{\sin(\pi fT)}{\pi f}\,.

(55)

In the limit of
T\to\infty
, the function
\delta_{T}
reduces to a Dirac’s delta function, thus it can be regarded as a finite approximation of a Dirac’s delta function.
Note that the optimal filter function is contingent on the positions and orientations of the detectors and exhibits a rapid decline if the time difference
t-t^{\prime}
exceeds the spatial distance between the two detectors. Given that the typical observation time is significantly larger than the spatial distance between the detectors, the range of the time integral may be approximated by infinity:

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}df\,\delta_{T}(f)F(f)\simeq F(0)\,,

(56)

for a function
F(f)
. We use (
56
) throughout the calculations but keep the finiteness of
T
for later convenience [see e.g. (
60
) below].

We define the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of the cross-correlation signal as

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}\equiv\frac{\mu}{\sigma}\,,

(57)

where
\mu
and
\sigma
represent the mean and variance of
S
respectively.
We assume the detector output
s_{I}(t)
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle s_{I}(t)=h_{I}(t)+n_{I}(t)\,.

(58)

where
h_{I}(t)
denotes a ULDM signal and
n_{I}(t)
denotes a detector noise, respectively.
Provided that noise is uncorrelated between different detectors, the correlation function of outputs reduces to
\left<\tilde{s}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{s}_{J}(f^{\prime})\right>=\left<\tilde{h}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{h}_{J}(f^{\prime})\right>
. For the ULDM signal, this can be assumed to be denoted with a certain function
F_{IJ}(f)
by

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{h}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{h}_{J}(f^{\prime})\right>=F_{IJ}(|f|)\delta_{T}(f-f^{\prime})\,.

(59)

Consequently, the mean value of the cross-correlation signal
\mu=\left<S\right>
is evaluated as

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=T\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfF_{IJ}(|f|)\tilde{Q}(f)\,.

(60)

To get this equation, we have applied
\delta_{T}(0)=T
, as derived from (
55
).

To estimate the variance of
S
, we assume that the magnitude of the detector noise is much larger than the amplitude of the ULDM signal. Then, the squared variance of
S
can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\left<S^{2}\right>-\left<S\right>^{2}\approx\left<S^{2}\right>

\displaystyle\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\times\delta_{T}(f-f^{\prime})\delta_{T}(k-k^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\times\left<\tilde{n}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{n}_{J}(f^{\prime})\tilde{n}^{*}_{I}(k)\tilde{n}_{J}(k^{\prime})\right>\tilde{Q}(f^{\prime})\tilde{Q}(k^{\prime}).

(61)

We also assume that
n_{I}(f)
is an uncorrelated Gaussian random field with the noise power spectrum
S_{n,I}(f)
, that is,

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{n}_{I}^{*}(f)\tilde{n}_{I}(f^{\prime})\right>=\frac{1}{2}S_{n,I}(|f|)\delta(f-f^{\prime}).

(62)

This allows us to express the four-point function of the noise in the integral in (
61
) in terms of the power spectrum as

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{n}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{n}_{I}(f^{\prime})\tilde{n}^{*}_{J}(k)\tilde{n}_{J}(k^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\tilde{n}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{n}_{I}(k)\right>\left<\tilde{n}^{*}_{J}(f^{\prime})\tilde{n}_{J}(k^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|k|)\delta_{T}(f-f^{\prime})\delta_{T}(k-k^{\prime})\,,

(63)

where we have assumed
I\neq J
.
As a result, we find

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df^{\prime}S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)\delta_{T}(f-f^{\prime})^{2}|\tilde{Q}(f^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{T}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfS_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)|\tilde{Q}(f)|^{2},

(64)

where (
56
) and
\delta_{T}(0)=T
are used to get the second line.

Substituting (
60
) and (
64
) into the definition of the SNR (
57
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}=2\sqrt{T}\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfF_{IJ}(|f|)\tilde{Q}(f)}{\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfS_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)|\tilde{Q}(f)|^{2}\right]^{1/2}}\,.

(65)

To identify the optimal filter that maximizes the SNR, we introduce the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
in the functional space as

\displaystyle(A,B)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dfA(f)^{*}B(f)S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)\,,

(66)

where
A
and
B
are arbitrary functions of
f
. With this inner product, the SNR can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}=2\sqrt{T}\frac{\left(\tilde{Q}(f),\frac{F_{IJ}(|f|)}{S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)}\right)}{\left(\tilde{Q}(f),\tilde{Q}(f)\right)^{1/2}}\,.

(67)

This can be regarded as the projection of
\frac{F_{IJ}(|f|)}{S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)}
in the direction of
\tilde{Q}(f)
in the functional space. Hence, the optimal filter function
\tilde{Q}(f)
, which maximizes the SNR, is

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(f)=K\frac{F_{IJ}(|f|)}{S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)}\,,

(68)

where
K
is an arbitrary constant. Therefore, the SNR under the optimal filtering is given by

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}=2\sqrt{T}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}df\frac{|F_{IJ}(|f|)|^{2}}{S_{n,I}(|f|)S_{n,J}(|f|)}\right]^{1/2}\,.

(69)

As we will see, the correlation for the ULDM signal can be approximated by

\displaystyle F_{IJ}(f)\approx{\cal F}_{IJ}\delta\left(|f|-\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)\,,

(70)

with
{\cal F}
which is independent of
f
. Note that when the observational time exceeds the coherent time of ULDM, the stochastic effect cannot be negligible which can be taken into account by replacing
T
with
T_{\rm eff}
defined by
Budker et al. 2014
;
Nakatsuka et al. 2022

\displaystyle T_{\rm eff}=\begin{cases}T&(T\lesssim\tau_{\rm coh})\,,\\
\sqrt{\tau_{\rm coh}T}&(T\gtrsim\tau_{\rm coh})\,.\end{cases}

(71)

All in all, SNR for the cross-correlation search of ULDM is expressed in the form

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}\approx\frac{2\sqrt{2}{\cal F}_{IJ}T_{\rm eff}}{\sqrt{S_{n,I}(\frac{m}{2\pi})S_{n,J}(\frac{m}{2\pi})}}\,.

(72)

III.2
Cross-correlation Search for Spin-2 ULDM

For the spin-2 ULDM signal (
23
), the Fourier transformation yields

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2M_{\rm Pl}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\int_{S^{2}}d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}~\tilde{\varphi}^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle+\left[\delta\left(f-\frac{m}{2\pi}\right)e^{i\omega L+i\theta}+\delta\left(f+\frac{m}{2\pi}\right)e^{-i\omega L-i\theta}\right]\,.

(73)

In the galaxy, the ULDM is expected to be approximately isotropic, non-polarized, and stationary. Consequently, the two-point function of
\varphi_{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})
is given by

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{\varphi}_{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\tilde{\varphi}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})\right>=H_{\rm DG}^{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\delta^{2}\left(\hat{\bm{\Omega}},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime}\right)\,,

(74)

where
\delta^{2}\left(\hat{\bm{\Omega}},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime}\right)
is the delta function on the celestial sphere and
H_{\rm DG}^{\lambda}
are non-negative real constants. By using this correlation, the energy density of spin-2 ULDM is

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm DG}=\frac{1}{4}\left<\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}_{ij}(t,\bm{x})\dot{\tilde{\varphi}}^{ij}(t,\bm{x})\right>=\pi\omega^{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}H_{\rm DG}^{\lambda}\,.

(75)

This indicates that the energy density of each polarization component is given by
\pi\omega^{2}H_{\rm DG}^{\lambda}
. Therefore, we define the ratio of each polarization component as

\displaystyle\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DG}\equiv\frac{\pi\omega^{2}H_{\rm DG}^{\lambda}}{\rho_{\rm DG}}\,,

(76)

for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}
. According to (
75
), the sum of these fractions equals one:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DG}=1\,.

(77)

From (
73
), (
74
), and (
76
), the two-point correlation function of the spin-2 dark matter signal in Fourier space is calculated as

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{h}^{*}_{I}(f)\tilde{h}_{J}(f^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}f_{\rm DG}{\cal A}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DG}\gamma^{\lambda}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\times\delta\left(|f|-\frac{m}{2\pi}\right)\delta\left(f-f^{\prime}\right)\,.

(78)

We have ignored the terms proportional to
\delta(f+f^{\prime})
because they vanish if we consider the randomness of
\theta
.

We have defined a dimensionless constant
{\cal A}
as

\displaystyle{\cal A}\equiv\frac{\rho_{\rm DM}}{5\omega^{2}M_{\rm Pl}^{2}}\,,

(79)

The ratio of the energy density of the spin-2 ULDM to the total local density of dark matter
\rho_{\rm DM}
is defined as

\displaystyle f_{\rm DG}\equiv\frac{\rho_{\rm DG}}{\rho_{\rm DM}}\,,

(80)

where
\rho_{\rm DM}\simeq 0.3\,{\rm GeV/cm^{3}}
around the solar system in our galaxy. We have also defined the overlap reduction function (ORF) for each polarization mode by

\displaystyle\gamma^{\lambda}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv 5\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}{4\pi}F^{\lambda}_{I}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{\lambda}_{J}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,

(81)

for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}
. They are normalized to be unity if
I=J
. These ORFs quantify the relative orientation and position of two detectors
Flanagan 1993
. The extension for the vector and scalar polarization modes in extended gravitational waves are provided by
Nishizawa et al. 2009
. ORFs for the spin-2 dark matter correspond to the long wavelength limit of such extended gravitational waves.
By applying (
78
) to the formula (
72
), we obtain the following formula of SNR for the spin-2 dark matter

\displaystyle{\rm SNR}^{\rm DG}_{IJ}

\displaystyle=\Big|\frac{2\sqrt{2}\alpha^{2}f_{\rm DG}{\cal A}T_{\rm eff}}{\sqrt{S_{n,I}(\frac{m}{2\pi})S_{n,J}(\frac{m}{2\pi})}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DG}}\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DG}\gamma^{\lambda}_{IJ}\Big|\,.

(82)

III.3
Cross-correlation Search for Spin-1 ULDM

The Fourier transformation of the signal (
38
) is expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(f)=\tilde{h}_{\rm time}(f)+\tilde{h}_{\rm space}(f)\,,

(83)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\rm time}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{D}e}{i\omega L}\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})G^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta\left(f-\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)e^{i\theta}-\delta\left(f+\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)e^{-i\theta}\right]\,,

(84)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\rm space}(f)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{D}ev\frac{Q_{D}}{M}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\int d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta\left(f-\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)e^{i\omega L+i\theta}+\delta\left(f+\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)e^{-\char 16\relax\omega L-i\theta}\right]\,.

(85)

Similar to the spin-2 ULDM case, the two-point function of the field amplitudes is expressed by

\displaystyle\left<A^{\lambda}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})A^{\lambda^{\prime}}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime})\right>=H_{\rm DP}^{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\delta^{2}\left(\hat{\bm{\Omega}},\hat{\bm{\Omega}}^{\prime}\right)\,.

(86)

From this expression, the energy density of the spin-1 ULDM is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm DP}=\frac{1}{2}\left<\dot{A}_{i}(t,\bm{x})\dot{A}_{i}(t,\bm{x})\right>=\pi\omega^{2}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}H_{\rm DP}^{\lambda}\,.

(87)

Furthermore, the density parameters of each polarization mode is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DP}=\frac{\pi\omega^{2}H_{\rm DP}^{\lambda}}{\rho_{\rm DP}}\,,

(88)

for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DP}=1\,.

(89)

The cross-correlation can be calculated as the sum of the following two terms

1
1

1

The cross terms of
h_{\rm time}(f)
and
h_{\rm space}(f)
such as
\left<\tilde{h}_{{\rm space},I}^{*}\tilde{h}_{{\rm time},J}\right>
and
\left<\tilde{h}_{{\rm time},I}^{*}\tilde{h}_{{\rm space},J}\right>
are neglected because their ORF vanishes as shown in Appendix
B
.

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{h}_{{\rm time},I}(f)^{*}\tilde{h}_{{\rm time},J}(f^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{D}^{2}f_{\rm DP}{\cal B}_{IJ}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\Omega^{\lambda}_{\rm DP}{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{IJ}\delta\left(|f|-\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,,

(90)

\displaystyle\left<\tilde{h}_{{\rm space},I}(f)^{*}\tilde{h}_{{\rm space},J}(f^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{D}^{2}f_{\rm DP}{\cal C}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\Omega_{\rm DP}^{\lambda}\gamma^{\lambda}_{IJ}+\frac{1}{3}\Omega^{z}_{\rm DP}{\gamma}^{z}_{IJ}\right]

\displaystyle\times\delta\left(|f|-\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\right)\delta(f-f^{\prime})\,,

(91)

In these expressions, we have defined the dimensionless coefficients
{\cal B}_{IJ}
and
{\cal C}
as

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{8\rho_{\rm DM}e^{2}}{3\omega^{4}L_{I}L_{J}}\left(\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L_{I}}{2}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega L_{J}}{2}\right)\,,

(92)

\displaystyle{\cal C}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\rho_{\rm DM}e^{2}v^{2}}{5\omega^{2}}\left(\frac{Q_{D}}{M}\right)^{2}\,,

(93)

and the density fraction of the spin-1 ULDM to the density of the total dark matter as

\displaystyle f_{\rm DP}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\rho_{\rm DP}}{\rho_{\rm DM}}\,.

(94)

We have also defined two types of ORF. One is induced by the finite-time traveling effects as

\displaystyle{\Gamma}^{\lambda}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{3}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}{4\pi}G^{\lambda}_{I}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})G^{\lambda}_{J}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,

(95)

for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}
. Another ORF arises from the spatial displacement of the mirrors:

\displaystyle\gamma^{x}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv 5\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}{4\pi}F^{x}_{I}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{x}_{J}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,

(96)

\displaystyle\gamma^{y}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv 5\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}{4\pi}F^{y}_{I}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{y}_{J}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,,

(97)

\displaystyle\gamma^{z}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{15}{4}\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}{4\pi}F^{z}_{I}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})F^{z}_{J}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,.

\displaystyle+{\cal C}\left[\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{\rm DP}}\Omega_{\rm DP}^{\lambda}\gamma^{\lambda}_{IJ}+\frac{1}{3}\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}\gamma_{IJ}^{z}\right]\Bigg|\,.

V.1
Distinguishing Signal Between Spin-2 and Spin-1 ULDM

We explore the distinguishability between signals from spin-2 and spin-1 ULDM. Recall that the SNR for spin-2 is given by the standard ORF
\gamma_{IJ}
while that for spin-1 is determined by
\gamma_{IJ}
and
\Gamma_{IJ}
where
\Gamma_{IJ}
is the ORF from the finite-time traveling effect. As shown in (
112
) and (
119
), these ORFs have different dependencies on the positions and orientations of the detectors (see also Table
1
). In order to quantify these differences, we introduce a measure for the deviation in the SNR of cross-correlation between spin-2 and spin-1 ULDM signals as

\displaystyle\Delta_{IJ}\equiv\left|{\rm SNR}^{\rm DG}_{IJ}-{\rm SNR}^{\rm DP}_{IJ}\right|\,.

(125)

As a case study, suppose that a ULDM signal is detected through the cross-correlation between LIGO-Livingston and Hanford with a certain SNR denoted by
{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}
. The corresponding coupling constants are determined to be

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}f_{\rm DG}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}\sqrt{S_{n,{\rm H}}(\frac{m}{2\pi})S_{n,{\rm L}}(\frac{m}{2\pi})}}{2\sqrt{2}|{\cal A}\gamma_{\rm HL}|T_{\rm eff}}\,,

(126)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{D}^{2}f_{\rm DP}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}\sqrt{S_{n,{\rm H}}(\frac{m}{2\pi})S_{n,{\rm L}}(\frac{m}{2\pi})}}{2\sqrt{2}|{\cal B}_{\rm HL}\Gamma_{\rm HL}+\frac{10}{9}{\cal C}\gamma_{\rm HL}|T_{\rm eff}}\,,

(127)

where we have set
\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}=\frac{1}{3}
for simplicity. From this,
\Delta_{IJ}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{IJ}}{{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\frac{\gamma_{IJ}}{\gamma_{\rm HL}}\right|-\left|\frac{{\cal B}_{IJ}\Gamma_{IJ}+\frac{10}{9}{\cal C}\gamma_{IJ}}{{\cal B}_{\rm HL}\Gamma_{\rm HL}+\frac{10}{9}{\cal C}\gamma_{\rm HL}}\right|\right|\sqrt{\frac{S_{n,{\rm H}}S_{n,{\rm L}}}{S_{n,{I}}S_{n,{J}}}}\,.

(128)

We have assumed the same
T_{\rm eff}
for all combinations of detectors.

Figure
5
illustrates
\Delta_{IJ}
normalized by
{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}
. In lower mass ranges (
m\lesssim v/L\simeq 5\times 10^{-13}{\rm eV}
), the signal from the spatial displacement of mirrors dominates over the signal from the finite-time traveling effect in the spin-1 ULDM signal. This leads to a suppression in the distinguishability of spin, as the ORF for spin-1 ULDM resembles that of spin-2 ULDM. Since
{\cal B}_{IJ}/{\cal C}
is approximately proportional to
m^{2}
,
\Delta_{IJ}
grows with mass in this region. In contrast, in higher mass ranges (
m\gtrsim v/L\simeq 5\times 10^{-13}{\rm eV}
), the finite-time traveling effect becomes the dominant factor in the spin-1 ULDM signal, enhancing the distinguishability of spins.

Note that the cross-correlation between LIGO-Livingston and Hanford lacks sensitivity to the spin-1 ULDM at
m\simeq 5\times 10^{13}{\rm eV}
, due to the cancellation of terms
{\cal B}_{\rm HL}\Gamma_{\rm HL}
and
\frac{10}{9}{\cal C}\gamma_{\rm HL}
. This specific cancellation leads to a divergence in
\Delta_{IJ}/{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}
. Likewise, similar cancellations occur in other combinations of detectors, resulting in
\Delta_{IJ}/{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}=0
.

V.2
Distinguishing Signal Between Spin-1 and Spin-0 ULDM

Both signals from spin-1 and spin-0 ULDM are composed of the sum of
h_{\rm time}
and
h_{\rm space}
. However, their ratios differ unless
\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}=1
. This suggests that signals from spin-1 and spin-0 ULDM can be distinguished only if the ULDM mass is close to the region at which
h_{\rm time}
and
h_{\rm space}
contribute equally.

Similarly to (
125
), we define
\tilde{\Delta}_{IJ}
to indicate the distinguishability of spin-0 and spin-1 ULDM as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{IJ}=|{\rm SNR}_{IJ}^{\rm DP}-{\rm SNR}_{IJ}^{\rm SD}|\,.

(129)

We again consider the case where LIGO-Hanford and LIGO-Livingston detect a ULDM signal with a certain SNR through their cross-correlation analysis. Assuming that this signal originates from spin-0 ULDM,
\alpha_{A}^{2}f_{\rm SD}
is determined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}^{2}f_{\rm SD}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}\sqrt{S_{n,{\rm H}}(\frac{m}{2\pi})S_{n,{\rm L}}(\frac{m}{2\pi})}}{2\sqrt{2}|{\cal B}^{\prime}_{\rm HL}\Gamma_{\rm HL}+{\cal C}^{\prime}\gamma_{\rm HL}|T_{\rm eff}}\,.

(130)

Thus,
\tilde{\Delta}_{IJ}
is rewritten by

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{IJ}}{{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\frac{{\cal B}_{IJ}\Gamma_{IJ}+(1+\frac{\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}}{3}){\cal C}\gamma_{IJ}}{{\cal B}_{\rm HL}\Gamma_{\rm HL}+(1+\frac{\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}}{3}){\cal C}\gamma_{\rm HL}}\right|\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\left|\frac{{\cal B}^{\prime}_{IJ}\Gamma_{IJ}+{\cal C}^{\prime}\gamma_{IJ}}{{\cal B}^{\prime}_{\rm HL}\Gamma_{\rm HL}+{\cal C}^{\prime}\gamma_{\rm HL}}\right|\right|\sqrt{\frac{S_{n,{\rm H}}S_{n,{\rm L}}}{S_{n,{I}}S_{n,{J}}}}\,.

(131)

Since
\frac{{\cal B}_{IJ}}{{\cal C}}=\frac{4}{3}\frac{{\cal B}^{\prime}_{IJ}}{{\cal C}^{\prime}}
for the any combination of the detectors, (
131
) vanished in the limit of
\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}=1
, it corresponds to the case that only longitudinal mode is excited.

The lower panels of Fig.
5
show
\tilde{\Delta}_{IJ}
normalized by
{\rm SNR}_{\rm HL}
. We have set
\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}=\frac{1}{3}
. These plots have two peaks around
f\sim 50
Hz that are caused by the cancellation of each denominator in (
131
). Apart from the mass region around
m\sim v/L
, the distinguishability is damped because the same effect is dominated for both ULDM signals, and there are only a few difference in the position and orientation dependence on the ORFs.

V.3
Maximal Likelihood

In a practical ULDM search, the method of likelihood is useful for detecting the ULDM signal. We only consider the likelihood analysis to distinguish the signal between spin-1 and spin-2 ULDM, but the same analysis can be applied for distinguishing the spin-0 ULDM from others. The likelihood for each ULDM is given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}(S|\mu^{M})

\displaystyle=\prod_{IJ\in{\cal D}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}_{IJ}}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{(S_{IJ}-\mu^{M}_{IJ}(\theta_{M}))^{2}}{2\sigma_{IJ}^{2}}\right]\,,

(132)

for
M=\{{\rm DG},{\rm DP}\}
. Here,
{\cal D}
denotes a set of detectors, and
S
is a cross-correlation signal defined by (
51
). The model parameters
\{\theta_{\rm DG},\theta_{\rm DP}\}=\{f_{\rm DG}\alpha^{2},f_{\rm DP}\epsilon_{D}^{2}\}
are decided by maximizing the likelihood for each ULDM.
\mu^{M}_{IJ}
represents the mean value of the signal, which is given by (
60
). More explicitly, for each ULDM model,
\mu^{M}_{IH}
are given by

\displaystyle\mu_{IJ}^{\rm DG}(\theta_{\rm DG})

\displaystyle\approx T_{\rm eff}\frac{\theta_{\rm DG}{\cal A}|\gamma_{IJ}|}{\sqrt{S_{n,I}S_{n,J}}}\,,

(133)

\displaystyle\mu_{IJ}^{\rm DP}(\theta_{\rm DP})

\displaystyle\approx T_{\rm eff}\frac{\theta_{\rm DP}|{\cal B}_{IJ}\Gamma_{IJ}+(1+\frac{\Omega_{\rm DP}^{z}}{3}){\cal C}\gamma_{IJ}|}{\sqrt{S_{n,I}S_{n,J}}}\,.

(134)

Therefore, the deviation of ORFs expressed in (
112
) and (
119
) are reflected in the likelihood through
\mu^{M}_{IJ}
.

One of the indicators to distinguish the spin-1 and spin-2 ULDM is the ratio of the maximized likelihoods

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{{\rm sup}_{\theta_{\rm DG}}\left\{{\cal L}(S|\mu^{\rm DG})\right\}}{{\rm sup}_{\theta_{\rm DP}}\left\{{\cal L}(S|\mu^{\rm DP})\right\}}\,.

(135)

Roughly speaking, if
\Lambda\gg 1
, the signal can be regarded as originating from the spin-2 ULDM whereas if
\Lambda\ll 1
, the signal can be regarded as originating from spin-1 ULDM. This ratio corresponds to the Bayesian factor with the same prior probability for each dark matter model.

VI
Summary and discussion

In this paper, we have addressed the distinguishability of ULDM signals with different spins with the gravitational wave detectors.
First, we have discussed how the oscillations of ULDM cause a motion of mirrors of the gravitational wave detectors.
For the spin-0 and spin-1 UDLM, the finite-time traveling effects are critical for the ULDM search in ground-based detectors, while for spin-2 dark matter, even in its vector and scalar polarization modes, finite-time traveling effects give a negligible contribution. This reflects the nature that the spin-2 ULDM exerts differential motion on the two mirrors, whereas the spin-1 and spin-0 ULDM exert common motion on them. Next, we pointed out that the finite-time traveling effects give rise to the different ORF from the regular gravitational waves.
As a result, the spin-2 ULDM and the lower spin ULDM yield different signals in the cross-correlation analysis.
As shown in Figure
5
, if the ULDM signal is detected at a certain SNR by at least three detectors, it is possible to distinguish the spin-2 ULDM from that of spin-0 or 1.

Furthermore, we have found that the current constraint on the spin-1 ULDM becomes about 30 times weaker than that reported in Ref.
Abbott et al. 2022
when we use the appropriate ORF, as shown in Fig.
3
. This is due to the fact that the ORF of the finite-time traveling effect between LIGO-Livingston (L) and LIGO-Hanford (H) is 0.035 times smaller than the standard ORF for their geometrical configurations. The simple solution to improve the constraints on the spin-1 ULDM would be to additionally utilize the data of Virgo (V) or KAGRA (K). At
m\sim 10^{-12}
eV, the cross-correlations of KV and LV expected to give better constraints than that of HL.

While we have considered multiple detectors, the relative position and orientation between a detector and dark matter change in time thanks to the rotation of the Earth. Within the coherent time
\tau_{\rm coh}\sim 9300\,{\rm s}\,(10^{-12}{\rm eV}/m)
,
we may analyze correlations between the data in different time intervals of the same detector and observe the changes in the ORF over time. From (
112
) and (
119
),
\gamma_{IJ}
and
\Gamma_{IJ}
are expected to have different time dependencies. Therefore, it may be beneficial to use different time data to distinguish between spin-1 and spin-2 ULDM based on the time dependence of the ORF. Since this method does not require multiple detectors, it could offer advantages for ULDM searches in frequency regions where detectors are limited. Specifically, in the bands of LISA, where the coherent time scale is relatively long as
\tau_{\rm coh}\sim 290\,{\rm yr}
, correlations between data at different times are expected to be effective.

For spin-0 and spin-1 ULDM signal,

\displaystyle\equiv\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}}{4\pi}F^{z}_{I}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})G^{z}_{J}(\hat{\bm{\Omega}})\,.

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{X}_{IJ}=D_{I}^{ij}D_{J}^{k}\tilde{{\cal G}}_{ijk}^{X}\,,\quad(X\in\{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{L}\})
