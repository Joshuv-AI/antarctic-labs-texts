A unified picture of medium-induced radiation
Johannes Hamre Isaksen, Adam Takacs, and Konrad Tywoniuk

Abstract
We revisit the picture of jets propagating in the quark-gluon plasma. In addition to vacuum radiation, partons scatter on the medium constituents resulting in induced emissions. Analytical approaches to including these interactions have traditionally dealt separately with multiple, soft, or rare, hard scatterings. A full description has so far only been available using numerical methods. We achieve full analytical control of the relevant scales and map out the dominant physical processes in the full phase space. To this aim, we extend existing expansion schemes for the medium-induced spectrum to the Bethe–Heitler regime. This covers the whole phase space from early to late times, and from hard splittings to emissions below the thermal scale. Based on the separation of scales, a space-time picture naturally emerges: at early times, induced emissions start to build from rare scatterings with the medium. At a later stage, induced emissions due to multiple soft scatterings result in a turbulent cascade that rapidly degrades energy down to, and including, the Bethe–Heitler regime. We quantify the impact of such an improved picture, compared to the current state-of-the-art factorization that includes only soft scatterings, by both analytical and numerical methods for the medium-induced energy distribution function. Our work serves to improve our understanding of jet quenching from small to large systems and for future upgrades of Monte Carlo generators.

Introduction

Short-lived droplets of hot and dense nuclear matter, called the quark-gluon plasma (QGP), are produced in relativistic heavy-ion collisions at RHIC and LHC. Embedded in the same high-energy collisions, hard QCD processes are also present, resulting in the production of collimated sprays of energetic particles that are commonly referred to as jets

;
Dokshitzer:1991wu

;
Marzani:2019hun
;
Dasgupta:2020fwr

;
Gyulassy:2003mc
;
Peigne:2008wu
;
Mehtar-Tani:2013pia
;
Ghiglieri:2015zma
;
Andrews:2018jcm
. Currently, a vigorous experimental program dedicated to quantifying jet modifications is ongoing at both RHIC and LHC, focusing on a broad set of observables which includes measurements of the modification of the jet spectrum, jet substructure observables, and jet correlations
Armesto:2015ioy
;
Connors:2017ptx
;
Kogler:2018hem
;
Cunqueiro:2021wls
;
Apolinario:2022vzg
(for a selection of predictions see Refs.
Chien:2015hda
;
Mehtar-Tani:2016aco
;
KunnawalkamElayavalli:2017hxo
;
Caucal:2019uvr
;
Casalderrey-Solana:2019ubu
;

High-energy jets are particularly suitable probes of the QGP because their energy scale
Q_{\rm jet}
is much larger than the typical momentum scale of the medium
Q_{\rm med}
. If this is the case, the impact of medium modifications should therefore not affect the internal structure of the jet, which would still rely on perturbative QCD
Casalderrey-Solana:2012evi
;
Caucal:2018dla
;
Vaidya:2020lih
. A key ingredient when considering jet modifications is the radiation induced by scatterings with the deconfined medium constituents. Such emissions typically appear at scales comparable to
Q_{\text{med}}
. Emission at scales much higher than
Q_{\rm med}
, on the other hand, are unaffected by the medium, resulting in a factorized picture between vacuum and medium processes
Mehtar-Tani:2017web
;
Caucal:2018dla
. Medium-induced emissions redistribute the original jet parton energy to multiple, soft particles over large angles, including out of the jet cone. This leads to a net jet energy loss which, in turn, is manifested as a suppression of the jet spectrum (for an updated discussion of jet quenching see Refs.
Mehtar-Tani:2021fud
;
Takacs:2021bpv
, and for applications to substructure see Refs.
Mehtar-Tani:2016aco
;
Caucal:2019uvr
;
Caucal:2021cfb
). Consequently, medium-induced emissions are a crucial component of jet energy loss and thus of phenomenological studies of jet observables in heavy-ion collisions.

The medium-induced emission spectrum was formulated a long time ago

;
Zakharov:1997uu
;
Arnold:2002ja

Gyulassy:1999zd
;
Wiedemann:2000za
;
Djordjevic:2003zk
;
Sievert:2018imd

Baier:1996kr
;
Zakharov:1997uu
;
Salgado:2003gb
;
Armesto:2003jh
. Meanwhile, several works focused on the underlying scales that separate the limiting cases
Baier:1996kr
;
Arnold:2008iy
;
Arnold:2009mr
;
Blaizot:2012fh
;
Kurkela:2014tla
;
Dominguez:2019ges
;
Andres:2020kfg
. The full problem has also been tackled by numerical techniques
Zakharov:2004vm
;
CaronHuot:2010bp
(or more recently in Refs.
Feal:2018sml
;
Ke:2018jem
;
Andres:2020vxs
;
Schlichting:2020lef
). Not long ago, analytical techniques were developed that provided a unified description of the multiple, soft and rare, hard scatterings in a dense medium
Mehtar-Tani:2019tvy
;
Mehtar-Tani:2019ygg
;
Barata:2020sav
;
Barata:2020rdn
;
Barata:2021wuf
, which better match the full numerical solutions. The main challenge, common to both the numeric and analytic approaches, resides in dealing with multiple interactions with the underlying medium.

In this paper, we revisit the different analytic approaches to resumming multiple interactions for calculating the medium-induced emission spectrum. These include the opacity expansion (OE) and the improved opacity expansion (IOE), which includes harmonic oscillator approximation as the leading term. Moreover, we rigorously derive the resummed opacity expansion (ROE) for the first time, which extends the description of the spectrum to low energy emissions in the Bethe–Heitler regime. We provide a novel unified picture of these resummation schemes by identifying their relevant emergent scales and demonstrating their respective regions of validity. For example, we show that the single scattering approximation, contained in the leading order of OE, is valid even for a big medium, where one would expect more than one scattering if the emitted energy is high enough. We show that the full phase space of medium-induced emissions, spanning from the maximal jet energy to the thermal scale, is covered by a union of these expansions, see also Ref.
Andres:2020kfg
. Each of the expansions is also associated with the corresponding physical scattering processes, and thus we reinterpret the frequently used terms such as GLV emissions, coherent scatterings, and Bethe–Heitler region in a unified framework. Our framework goes beyond previous attempts to describe all regimes of medium-induced bremsstrahlung by presenting a resummation framework that can be systematically improved and that is valid both in the dilute and dense regimes.

As a next step, we identify the regimes where not only multiple interactions are important, but also multiple emissions
Arnold:2002zm
;
Blaizot:2013vha
. These conditions are met for sufficiently soft emissions in a large medium. The previously established hierarchy of emergent scales plays a crucial role in mapping out early, rare, and relatively hard emissions and a successive cascade of soft splittings. In this context, hard medium-induced splittings can be thought of as extra sources, in addition to the parent parton, for the full cascade. This description is realized analytically in a novel scheme that combines a fixed order expansion of rare emissions with an all-order resummation of soft splittings. The resulting energy distribution links the asymptotic early and late time behaviors for which analytical solutions exist. Finally, we resum multiple induced emissions numerically, using the previously obtained precise determination of the in-medium splitting rates, to calculate the energy distribution function. We highlight the interplay of rare hard scatterings, coherent soft splittings, and Bethe–Heitler emissions in a finite medium, providing a state-of-the-art resummation.

Our reorganized picture helps not only with the physical understanding of induced emissions, but provides a fast and efficient way to calculate the medium-induced spectrum, which is a key ingredient for estimating jet energy loss. It also serves to inform Monte Carlo algorithms simulating full jet evolution inside the medium about how to implement multiple medium-induced emissions and how to combine them with vacuum-like emissions, e.g. see in Ref.
Caucal:2018dla
.

We revisit the opacity expansion and the improved opacity expansion schemes, and put on a firm footing a novel resummation scheme, dubbed resummed opacity expansion, which is valid for emissions below the Bethe–Heitler scale. Improving on previous discussions, we provide formulas for the spectrum at arbitrary order and calculate it exactly up to second order in all the expansions, allowing us for the first time to establish regions where they converge. Finally, in Sec.
4

We analyze induced particles coming from the full phase space and confirm the importance of considering multiple emissions, especially in the soft sector. In order to facilitate an improved analytical understanding of the problem, we finally suggest a resummation scheme of multiple emissions by iterating in rare, hard emissions and including an arbitrary number of soft splittings. This is compared to the full numerical results. We conclude with an outlook in Sec.
6

2
Heuristic discussion of the medium-induced spectrum

The spectrum of gluon emissions induced by scatterings on a deconfined medium plays a central role in the phenomenology of jet quenching. However, a full understanding of all regimes have so far been lacking analytically and was previously only achievable through numerical methods. Here, we present a unified view of all relevant medium scales and their related regimes. Similar heuristic discussions have previously been presented in, e.g.,
Baier:1996kr
;
Peigne:2008wu
;
Mehtar-Tani:2019tvy
;
Andres:2020kfg
. In Sec.
3
we will provide rigorous derivations of the findings argued for here.

We formulate the spectrum of induced emissions by focusing on the relevant length scales:

•

The
size
of the medium
L
(or the length of propagation
t<L
).

•

The
mean free path
of the medium
\lambda\sim\frac{1}{n\sigma_{\rm tot}}
, which combines the density
n
and the scattering strength
\sigma_{\rm tot}\sim\int{\rm d}\sigma
, and it describes the distance between typical scatterings.

•

The
formation time
of an emission
t_{\rm f}=\frac{2\omega}{\bm{k}^{2}}
, where
\omega
is the energy and
\bm{k}
is the transverse momentum of the emission.

In addition, the relation between the in-medium screening scale
\mu
and the range of available transverse momenta
|{\bm{k}}|
is also important.

The
opacity

\chi\equiv L/\lambda
characterizes the denseness of the medium. If the opacity is small (
L\ll\lambda
), the medium is “dilute”, or weakly interacting, while it is “dense”, or strongly interacting if
L\gg\lambda
.
1
1

1

From this perspective, we have “fixed”
L
and vary
\lambda
. Naturally, we could also have identified these two regimes as a “large” and “small” media, where we “fix” the mean free path and vary
L
instead.
The dilute medium barely consists of scattering centers, however, in the dense medium one should account for an arbitrary number of interactions.

A big part of this paper will be about calculating the spectrum of medium-induced emissions. For reference, the vacuum spectrum reads,

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}\sim\alpha_{s}\int\frac{{\rm d}{\bm{k}}^{2}}{{\bm{k}}^{2}}\,,

(1)

where currently we did not specify the limits of the transverse momentum integral.
This contains the well-known soft (
\omega\to 0
) and collinear (
{\bm{k}}^{2}\to 0
) divergences. In contrast, the collinear divergence in the medium spectrum is removed by the need to exchange transverse momentum with the medium.

Motivated by this, we introduce a heuristic model that captures some of the features of the medium-induced spectrum, given by

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}\sim\alpha_{s}L\int{\rm d}^{2}\bm{k}\,\sigma(\bm{k})\sim\alpha_{s}^{3}nL\int\frac{{\rm d}\bm{k}^{2}}{(\bm{k}^{2}+\mu^{2})^{2}}\,,

(2)

where
n
is the medium density and
\mu
a screening mass. The behavior at high-
{\bm{k}}
,
\sigma\sim\bm{k}^{-4}
, reproduces the expected Coulomb tail. The factor
L
arises since the emission can take place anywhere along the medium length. For a more accurate description of medium-induced emissions, we refer the reader to Sec.
3
.

In our effective description, we focus on the hierarchy among the introduced scales and show the separation of different scattering regions. Firstly, in the
t_{\rm f}\gg L
limit, the formation of the emission extends beyond the medium, where one naturally should expect vacuum physics to dominate.
2
2

2

For such soft emissions, medium effects can influence the color coherence properties leading to a modification of the phase space
Mehtar-Tani:2010ebp
;
Mehtar-Tani:2012mfa
.
We will hence not consider this possibility here. The remaining cases are listed below:

Dilute media
(
t_{\rm f}\leq L\ll\lambda
):

In case of a low medium opacity, we expect that roughly one scattering occurs. This process will typically transfer a momentum of order of the Debye mass to the emitted gluon, or
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle\sim\mu^{2}
, leading to
{t_{\text{f}}}=2\omega/\mu^{2}
. The formation of the gluon has to take place inside the medium, giving rise to the characteristic energy scale in the dilute regime, namely

\bar{\omega}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}L\,.

(3)

This separates two regimes of emissions that are sourced via different scattering processes: on the one side soft gluons with
\omega<\bar{\omega}_{c}
, generated via a soft scattering with the medium
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle\lesssim\mu^{2}
. Hard gluons with
\omega>\bar{\omega}_{c}
can also be generated, however those demand a large momentum exchange with the medium,
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle>\mu^{2}
, which is comparatively rare. Let us now consider how the spectrum behaves in these two distinct regimes.

According to our discussion above, the soft production should be dominated by soft transverse momentum exchanges with the medium. Hence, we expect that the spectrum of emitted gluons goes as

\left.\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}\right|_{\omega<\bar{\omega}_{c}}\sim\alpha_{s}L\,\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}\bm{k}^{2}\frac{\alpha_{s}^{2}n}{(\bm{k}^{2}+\mu^{2})^{2}}\sim\alpha_{s}\frac{L}{\lambda}\,,

(4)

where the integral is dominated by
{\bm{k}}^{2}\lesssim\mu^{2}
. This integral gives the proportionality with the inverse mean free path i.e.
n/\mu^{2}\sim 1/\lambda
, resulting in an overall factor of medium opacity
L/\lambda
. This parametric estimate misses an important logarithmic factor
\sim\ln\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega
, see a further discussion in Sec.
3.2
, which signals that the simplifications pertaining to the “soft” regime break down at
\omega\approx\bar{\omega}_{c}
.

For hard emissions,
\omega>\bar{\omega}_{c}
, we instead get that

\left.\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}\right|_{\omega>\bar{\omega}_{c}}\sim\alpha_{s}^{3}nL\,\int_{\omega/L}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}{\bm{k}}^{2}}{{\bm{k}}^{4}}\sim\alpha_{s}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\,,

(5)

where we used
{t_{\text{f}}}=2\omega/\bm{k}^{2}<L
, and neglected the screening mass
\mu^{2}
in this parametric regime, since
\langle\bm{k}^{2}\rangle\gg\mu^{2}
.
Compared to the soft regime from Eq. (
4
) it is suppressed by an additional power of
\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega\ll 1
. The complete spectrum in the dilute regime is sketched in Fig.
2
(left).

Dense media with long formation time
(
\lambda\ll t_{\rm f}\ll L
):

In a dense medium we should expect that typically many scatterings occur during the emission process, which is illustrated in the middle of Fig.
1
. This demands a more sophisticated model than what we suggested in Eq. (
2
). Nevertheless, we can approximate the total transferred transverse momentum by
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle\sim\hat{q}t
, which resembles a random walk for
t
time in two dimensions, with
\hat{q}
playing the role of a diffusion constant.
3
3

3

Arbitrary dense medium, would result in overlapping scatterings that description if beyond the scope of this paper. Multiple independent scatterings require well separated scattering centers (
1/\mu\ll\lambda
) see in Ref.
Baier:1996kr
.
This constant determines the typical transverse momentum accumulated per unit length, or
\hat{q}\sim n\sim\mu^{2}/\lambda
. In this case the formation time becomes

{t_{\text{f}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\omega}{\hat{q}}}\,.

(6)

This is often called the coherence length, since during the formation time, interference effects between multiple scattering with the medium are active and the gluon feels only one effective scattering center. The accumulated transverse momentum during the splitting process is in this case
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle=\sqrt{2\omega\hat{q}}
, which is the celebrated Landau-Pomeranchuk-Migdal (LPM) effect.

Again, comparing the formation time to the medium length, leads to the characteristic energy scale in the dense regime, namely

\omega_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{q}L^{2}\,,

(7)

and thus
\omega<\omega_{c}
for multiple soft scatterings. The maximal possible momentum accumulated via multiple soft scatterings is denoted
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle\sim Q_{s}^{2}=\hat{q}L
. The other limiting scale of the multiple scattering regime arises when considering the minimal formation time in this hierarchy, i.e.
{t_{\text{f}}}>\lambda
, giving rise the scale
\omega>\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
, (see later in Eq. (
10
)). In this case, the accumulated transverse momentum squared reduces to a single soft scattering
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle\sim\hat{q}\lambda\sim\mu^{2}
.

In the multiple soft scattering regime, characterized by
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\ll\omega\ll\omega_{c}
, the mean free path has to be replaced by the formation time in Eq. (
4
), leading to

\left.\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}\right|_{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\ll\omega\ll\omega_{c}}\sim\alpha_{s}\frac{L}{{t_{\text{f}}}}\sim\alpha_{s}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{q}L^{2}}{\omega}}\,.

(8)

This is also often referred to as the BDMPS-Z spectrum in the soft limit.

For hard gluon emissions,
\omega\gg\omega_{c}
, we also have to demand that
\langle{\bm{k}}^{2}\rangle\gg Q_{s}
. In other words, only a hard scattering can provide sufficient transverse momentum to fulfill all the conditions. The relevant contribution is therefore captured by Eq. (
5
) and, remarkably, the spectrum in this limit is identical to the hard tail in the dilute regime, namely

\left.\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}\right|_{\omega\gg\omega_{c}}\sim\alpha_{s}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\,.

(9)

This demonstrates that, even in a dense medium, hard emissions mostly are driven by single, rare hard scattering events. An illustration of this can be seen on the right in Fig.
1
.

3
Spectrum of medium-induced emissions

In this section, we derive the all order emission spectrum induced by elastic scatterings on a deconfined medium. Of the three expansions we present here, the opacity expansion and the improved opacity expansion have been discussed in depth in previous works, see Refs.
Gyulassy:1999zd
;
Wiedemann:2000za
and
Mehtar-Tani:2019tvy
;
Mehtar-Tani:2019ygg
;
Barata:2020sav
;
Barata:2020rdn
;
Barata:2021wuf
respectively. The resummed opacity expansion has been argued for before, see
Wiedemann:2000za
;
Andres:2020kfg
;
Schlichting:2021idr
, but is here derived rigorously for the first time. This paper strives to be a comprehensive reference for all of the expansions, and hence they are all presented in detail. Furthermore, we extend previous calculations to all orders and present results to order
N=2
for the opacity expansion and
N_{r}=2
for the resummed opacity expansion, and extract the relevant limits analytically. This provides valuable insight into the underlying structure of the expansions in different regimes.

\int_{\bm{x}}=\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{x}}

v({\bm{x}},t)=\int_{\bm{q}}\sigma({\bm{q}},t)\left(1-{\rm e}^{i{\bm{q}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\right)\,.

(14)

Here
\sigma({\bm{q}},t)=N_{c}n(t){\rm d}^{2}\sigma_{\rm el}/{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{q}}
is proportional to the in-medium elastic scattering cross section, where
n(t)\sim T^{3}
is the density of scattering centers.
7
7

7

We include the number density of the scattering centers
n(t)
into
v({\bm{x}},t)
and
\sigma({\bm{q}},t)
similarly to the previous works in Refs.
Mehtar-Tani:2019tvy
;
Mehtar-Tani:2019ygg
;
Barata:2021wuf
.
The color factor
N_{c}
appears because, in this limit, only the emitted gluon picks up transverse momentum in the medium.
The potential can be extracted from an effective theory that accounts for both large and small momentum exchanges with the medium
Moore:2021jwe
. In the main part of this paper, we will use the Gyulassy-Wang potential
Gyulassy:1993hr
that both contains a hard Coulomb-tail and implements screening in the infrared
\mu^{2}\gg{\bm{q}}^{2}
,

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\sigma_{\rm el}}{{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{q}}}=\frac{g^{4}}{({\bm{q}}^{2}+\mu^{2})^{2}}\,,

(15)

where
\mu
is a screening mass of the order of the Debye mass of a thermal medium. We also provide the spectrum in App.
E
with the LO hard thermal loop (HTL) potential
Aurenche:2002pd
. Also, in Eq. (
13
) we neglect quark and gluon thermal masses, which corresponds to taking the high-energy limit (
E\gg m_{q,g}^{4}/\hat{q}\sim\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
), see e.g. Refs.
Arnold:2008iy
;
Schlichting:2021idr
for further discussion.

When the medium is not present,
v=0
, one recovers the propagation of a single parton in vacuum,
{\cal K}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})\equiv{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}},t_{2}-t_{1})
with
{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}},t)=\frac{\omega}{2\pi i\,t}\exp[i\,\omega{\bm{x}}^{2}/(2t)]
. To only capture medium effects, the vacuum term is explicitly subtracted in Eq. (
12
).

The emission spectrum in Eq. (
12
) is the result of a path integral formalism in which arbitrarily many soft and hard scatterings are included. It does, however, not account for longitudinal momentum (
\sim
energy) exchange with the medium. Having written the expression as a spectrum we also implicitly assume that the creation of the initial parton is factorized from the induced process (for example it was created in a highly virtual vacuum process). Finally, the medium averages leading to the simple form of the three-point correlator, as in Eq. (
13
), assumes independent scatterings on the medium. This parametrically holds if the size of the potential is much smaller than the mean free path, i.e.
\mu^{-1}\ll\lambda
, where the typical exchanged momentum is
|\delta\bm{q}|\sim\mu

Baier:1996kr
.

We should also note that Eq. (
12
) emerges as the result of a momentum integral of the differential spectrum
{\rm d}I/({\rm d}\omega\,{\rm d}^{2}\bm{k})
in the soft limit
Salgado:2003gb
;
Barata:2021wuf
, with no kinematical constraint on the transverse momentum (
\bm{k}
) integral similarly to Refs.
Arnold:2002ja
;
Arnold:2002zm
. A more careful treatment of the kinematics would be important especially if one is interested in emissions inside or out of a given cone
Salgado:2003gb
;
Andres:2020vxs
;
Takacs:2021bpv
.

Let us now cast the equation for the spectrum in an equivalent form. On many occasions it is more practical to work in transverse momentum space,

{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t_{2};{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{1})=\int_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}}\,{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{x}}+i{\bm{p}}_{0}\cdot{\bm{y}}}{\cal K}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})\,.

(16)

The vacuum propagator
{\cal K}_{0}
then becomes a plane wave, i.e.
{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}},t)=\exp[-i\,{\bm{p}}^{2}t/(2\omega)]
.
In this representation, the solution to the Schrödinger equation (
13
) can be written as the recursive equation

\displaystyle{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t_{2};{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{1})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta({\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}}_{0}){\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t_{2}-t_{1})

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}s\int_{\bm{q}}\,{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t_{2}-s)v({\bm{q}},s){\cal K}({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}},s;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{1})\,,

(17)

where now

v({\bm{q}},s)=(2\pi)^{2}\delta({\bm{q}})\Sigma(s)-\sigma({\bm{q}},s)\,,

(18)

ensures probability conservation. Here,
\Sigma(s)\equiv\int_{\bm{q}}\,\sigma({\bm{q}},s)
can be interpreted as the inverse of the (local) mean free path
\lambda
along a trajectory of a propagating parton, or

\lambda(s)=\frac{1}{\Sigma(s)}\,.

(19)

In these expressions, we have assumed that the integral over the elastic scattering cross section exists. In many cases, e.g. for the HTL potential
Aurenche:2002pd
, one needs to introduce an IR regulator. However,
v({\bm{q}},s)
in Eq. (
18
) is not sensitive to this IR regulation and therefore the expansion in Eq. (
3.1
) is well-defined. We have provided a further discussion of the HTL potential in App.
E
.

The medium-induced spectrum now reads

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{2\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}}\,{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{q}}\,\big[{{\cal K}}({\bm{p}},t_{2};{\bm{q}},t_{1})-(2\pi)^{2}\delta({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}}){\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}},t_{2}-t_{1})\big]\,,

(20)

The vacuum contribution can then be removed by inserting Eq. (
3.1
) into Eq. (
20
), yielding

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{4\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}_{0},{\bm{q}}}\,\frac{{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{0}}{{\bm{p}}^{2}}v({\bm{q}},t_{2}){\cal K}({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}},t_{2};{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{1})\,,

(21)

where we regulated the integral over the latter time coordinate using an adiabatic turn-off at infinity (see also Ref.
Andres:2020kfg
). The other time integrals are limited by the extent of the medium
L
.
Noticing, that

\displaystyle\int_{{\bm{p}}}\,\frac{p^{i}}{{\bm{p}}^{2}}v({\bm{p}}-{\bm{k}},s)=\frac{k^{i}}{{\bm{k}}^{2}}\Sigma({\bm{k}}^{2},s)\,,

(22)

where
\Sigma({\bm{k}}^{2},s)=\int_{{\bm{q}}}\,\sigma({\bm{q}},s)\Theta({\bm{q}}^{2}-{\bm{k}}^{2})
,
8
8

8

Also
\Sigma(0,s)=\Sigma(s)
, consistent with the definition in Eq. (
18
).
we obtain

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{4\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}_{0}}\,\Sigma({\bm{p}}^{2},t_{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{0}}{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t_{2};{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{1})\,.

(23)

While the above results are valid for any medium potential, in this work we will focus on the GW scattering potential, defined in Eq. (
15
). In this case, we find that

\Sigma({\bm{k}}^{2},s)=\frac{\hat{q}_{0}(s)}{{\bm{k}}^{2}+\mu^{2}}\,,

(24)

where
{\hat{q}}_{0}(s)=4\pi\alpha_{s}^{2}N_{c}n(s)
is a measure of the scattering density. Currently, we consider a medium of constant density,
n(s)=n_{0}
.

The spectrum, given by Eq. (
12
) or Eq. (
23
), can be evaluated using numerical techniques
Zakharov:2004vm
;
CaronHuot:2010bp
;
Feal:2018sml
;
Ke:2018jem
;
Andres:2020vxs
;
Schlichting:2020lef
or by employing analytical approximations. As we will see the approximate approaches rely on expanding the problem as a series, which will give the true answer at infinite order. The different series have different radii of convergence, and none of them will alone converge for all
L
and
\omega
, meaning more than one have to be employed. In most cases, however, the first order expansion is sufficient to provide an accurate approximation of the all order result. In the following we discuss three well-defined approaches that together provide an accurate description of the true problem for all
L
and
\omega
, called: the opacity expansion, the resummed opacity expansion, and the improved opacity expansion. We will derive these, discuss their limits and their regions of validity.

We also point out that the medium parameters for the numerical evaluations in Figs.
4
-
6
(right) are chosen to maximally separate the relevant scales and to illustrate the main features of the spectrum. They are also similar to the ones used in phenomenological studies
Takacs:2021bpv
;
Barata:2021wuf
;
Caucal:2021cfb
. It is worth pointing out that, although this particular choice violates the assumption of non-overlapping scattering centers and should be treated with care, changing the values of the parameters would not alter the qualitative picture of separating different regimes in the
(\omega,t)
plane.

3.2
Opacity expansion (OE)

The opacity expansion of the spectrum arises when inserting Eq. (
3.1
) directly into Eq. (
23
), and was developed in Refs.
Gyulassy:2000er
;
Wiedemann:2000za
.
9
9

9

To be precise, expanding our formulas order by order in opacity reproduces the expansion defined in Ref.
Wiedemann:2000za
, which reproduces Ref.
Gyulassy:2000er
in the “incoherent” limit.
The truncation of this series at a given order
n
in the medium scattering potential gives the
N=n
term, which is by definition proportional to
(L/\lambda)^{n}
(see Eq. (
A.1
)). Physically this means, at
N=n
one counts
n
number of scatterings (both with and without momentum exchange) on the full elastic potential. The relevant energy scales that arise are
\bar{\omega}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}L
, and
\frac{L}{\lambda}\bar{\omega}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{q}_{0}L^{2}
as discussed in Sec.
2
. A general formula for the spectrum at any order is derived in App.
A
, and with finite-
z
corrections in App.
C
. These results are used in the following calculations, and we will refer to the appendices for more details.

First order (
N=1
):

The spectrum at first order of opacity is well known
Gyulassy:1999zd
;
Wiedemann:2000za
. Since Eq. (
23
) already includes at least one scattering, we obtain the
N=1
term by replacing the full propagator
{\cal K}
by the vacuum one. We then find,

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}=8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\int_{\bm{p}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}^{2})\,{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}{\rm d}t_{0}\,{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}^{2}(t_{1}-t_{0})}\,,

(25)

where we have switched to dimensionless integration variables by defining
{\bm{p}}^{2}\to{\bm{p}}^{2}L/(2\omega)
and
t\to t/L
, and where
\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}^{2})=({\bm{p}}^{2}+\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega)^{-1}
. This expression can also be obtained from the general
N=n
result in Eq. (
A.1
). After simplifications, the spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}=2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\frac{1}{p+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\frac{p-\sin{p}}{p^{2}}\,,

(26)

where
{\rm\bar{\alpha}}=\alpha_{s}C_{R}/\pi
and
\lambda=\mu^{2}/{\hat{q}}_{0}
. It also agrees with Eq. (6.7) in Ref.
Wiedemann:2000za
(see also in Ref.
Salgado:2003gb
). We recognize the dependence on the medium opacity
L/\lambda
and the ratio
\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega
. The remaining integral can be done analytically, but the resulting expression is not very illuminating. However, the limiting behavior can readily be extracted,

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\left(\ln\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}-1+\gamma_{E}\right),&\qquad\text{for }\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}\,,\\[0.2pt]
\frac{\pi}{2}{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega},&\qquad\text{for }\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}\,.\end{cases}

(27)

This agrees well with the heuristic discussion in Sec.
2
. In particular, we identify a logarithmic behavior
\sim\ln\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}
in the infrared. Notice the different expansion structures in the soft
\sim\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}
and in the hard
\sim\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}
limits, which we will come back to.

Second order (
N=2
):

The calculation for
N=2
follows in a similar way, leading to

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=-8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{2},{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{2}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{2}^{2}}\tilde{v}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}{\rm d}t_{0}\,{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{2}^{2}(t_{2}-t_{1})}{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}(t_{1}-t_{0})}\,,

(28)

with dimensionless integration variables, and where
\tilde{v}({\bm{p}})=(2\pi)^{2}\delta({\bm{p}})-\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}})
. In the GW model,
\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}})=4\pi/({\bm{p}}^{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})^{2}
.
After inserting
\tilde{v}
, doing the time integrals and simplifying this can be written as

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}=-4\bar{\alpha}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\left[{\cal I}_{1}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)-\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}{\cal I}_{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)\right]\,,

(29)

where we have defined the integrals

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{1}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\frac{1}{p+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\frac{1-\cos p-\frac{p}{2}\sin p}{p^{3}}\,,

(30)

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{1}\,\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\frac{1}{\left[\left(p_{2}+p_{1}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}-4p_{2}p_{1}\right]^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{p_{2}-p_{1}}\left[\frac{1}{p_{1}^{2}}\left(1-\cos{p_{1}}\right)-\frac{1}{p_{2}^{2}}\left(1-\cos{p_{2}}\right)\right]\,.

(31)

The
{\cal I}_{1}
integral can be done analytically, but
{\cal I}_{2}
is more complicated. It can be shown that it is much smaller than
{\cal I}_{1}
in the soft limit. In the hard limit,
{\cal I}_{1}
and
{\cal I}_{2}
cancel at the order of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})
, leaving a positive contribution going as
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})^{2}
. In summary,

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}-{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2},&\text{for }\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}\,,\\[0.5pt]
\sim{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2},&\text{for }\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}\,.\end{cases}

(32)

We notice that the
N=2
is proportional to
\bar{\alpha}(\frac{L}{\lambda})^{2}
in the soft limit, and goes like
\bar{\alpha}(\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})^{2}
in the hard limit. This immediately implies that
N=2
is always subleading to
N=1
if the medium is dilute
L\ll\lambda
or if the emission is hard
\omega\gg\frac{L}{\lambda}\bar{\omega}_{c}
. Given the structure of the expansion, we expect the previous statement to hold at arbitrary
N=n
order. This is in agreement with the earlier, heuristic observation in Refs.
Gyulassy:2000fs
;
Arnold:2008iy
.

Based on the limits for
N=1
and
N=2
, given by Eqs. (
27
) and (
32
), in the regimes where the expansion holds the all order OE spectrum is expected to take the form

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}=\begin{cases}{\bar{\alpha}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}h_{n}\left(\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\right)}\,,&\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}\,,\\
{\bar{\alpha}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\tilde{h}_{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)}\,,&\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}\,,\end{cases}

(33)

where the OE coefficients
h_{n},\tilde{h}_{n}
are finite and can be calculated order by order. Note that we have not strictly proven this for all orders, although our
N=1
and
N=2
results strongly indicate this structure. In the soft limit
\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}
, the OE expansion converges rapidly, defining the expected “naive” radius of convergence
L/\lambda<1
. However, in the hard limit
\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}
there is convergence even if the medium is big, provided
\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}<1
. The full region of convergence is shown in green in the left panel of Fig.
4
. Outside of this region we expect higher orders to grow uncontrollably and hence the OE is not valid when truncated at any finite order.

The resulting spectrum from Eqs. (
26
) and (
29
) is shown in the right panel of Fig.
4
for different propagation lengths (labeled with
t
). For short lengths
t<\lambda
the OE is valid for all
\omega
. For
t>\lambda
, the OE is only valid if
\frac{t}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}<1
(see also the green region on the left panel). We note that the
N=2
correction becomes important at
t>\lambda
and
\omega\approx\frac{t}{\lambda}\bar{\omega}_{c}
(the latter constraint shown as bullets in the figure). For larger media, the grey bullets, representing the minimal energy for achieving convergence, moves to higher values, and the truncated OE series at
\omega
smaller than this becomes ill-defined. This can be seen, for instance, in the upper line in Fig.
4
(right) for a medium length of
t=4
fm.
We have also compared to a full numerical evaluation of the spectrum from Ref.
Andres:2020vxs
;
Andres:2022
. The figure shows that this indeed is well approximated by the OE in its region of validity, as we have argued. Our curves are not expected to hold in the limit
\omega\ll 1
GeV, where several important effects were not taken into account such as thermal masses, realistic 2-2 elastic scatterings, and other non-perturbative effects. We still plot the curves down to very small
\omega
to compare the different expansion schemes.

3.3
Resummed opacity expansion (ROE)

Next, we turn to dense media,
L\gg\lambda
, where multiple scattering have to be accounted for. However, as depicted to the left in Fig.
1
and discussed in Sec.
2
, for soft emissions with short formation times, a single scattering still gives the leading contribution to the spectrum. This defines the so-called Bethe–Heitler regime named after the QED analogue of this process. A qualitative argument of this regime was first given in Ref.
Wiedemann:2000za
and later developed in Ref.
Andres:2020kfg
, see also in Ref.
Schlichting:2021idr
which coined the name ‘‘resummed opacity expansion’’ (ROE).
10
10

10

In Ref.
Baier:1996kr
, authors derive the opacity expansion from the all-order formula Eq. (
12
) in a similar way as we did by expanding
{\cal K}
. Accidentally, in one of their intermediate steps in Sec. 4, they kept the virtual interactions resummed, which corresponds to our ROE. Back then, however, they did not realize the importance of that formula and they expanded it to reproduce the OE.

Here, we provide for the first time a consistent framework for dealing with an expansion of the
real
scatterings with the medium, whereby real we mean interactions with a finite transverse momentum exchange. At the same time, an all-order resummation of the corresponding
virtual
interactions, with zero transverse momentum exchange, is performed.

Dividing Eq. (
3.1
) by the vacuum propagator leaves us with

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})}{{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t-t_{0})}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta({\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}}_{0})-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\rm d}s\int_{\bm{q}}\,v({\bm{q}},s)\frac{{\cal K}({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}},s;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})}{{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};s-t_{0})}\,.

(34)

Next, taking a derivative with respect to the latest time results in

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})}{{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t-t_{0})}+\Sigma(t)\frac{{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})}{{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t-t_{0})}=\int_{\bm{q}}\,\sigma({\bm{q}})\frac{{\cal K}({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}},t;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})}{{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t-t_{0})}\,,

(35)

where
\Sigma(t)=\int_{\bm{q}}\,\sigma({\bm{q}},t)
, as before.
This motivates defining the elastic Sudakov factor,

\Delta(t,t_{0})\equiv{\rm e}^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\rm d}s\,\Sigma(s)}={\rm e}^{-(t-t_{0})\Sigma}\,,

(36)

where the last equality holds for media with constant density.
11
11

11

For medium potentials with unscreened soft divergences, such as the HTL potential, one has to modify this prescription to include an IR regulator. We refer to App.
E
for a further discussion.
This represents the probability of no elastic scattering occurring between times
t_{0}
and
t
.
Integrating out the time, we arrive at a slightly modified iterative equation

\displaystyle{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta({\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}}_{0})\Delta(t,t_{0}){\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t-t_{0})

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\rm d}s\frac{\Delta(t,t_{0})}{\Delta(s,t_{0})}\int_{\bm{q}}\,{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{p}};t-s)\sigma({\bm{q}},s){\cal K}({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}},s;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{0})\,.

(37)

Compared to the standard opacity expansion in Eq. (
3.1
), this looks very similar. However, the expansion is not in the potential
v({\bm{q}},s)
, which contains both a “real” and a “virtual” part, but in the potential scattering
\sigma({\bm{q}},s)
which comes from the term that provides a finite momentum transfer in the process. The virtual contributions, where no net momentum was exchanged, are accounted for to all orders in the Sudakov factor. This is why this expansion referred as a
resummed
opacity expansion.

The relevant scale that appears at high opacity is
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}\lambda
, as we discussed in Sec.
2
. Interestingly, at low opacity
L\ll\lambda
, the scale changes to
\bar{\omega}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}L
, which we recognize from the opacity expansion. In this regime the ROE is actually equivalent to the OE if one gathers all the terms up to the same order in
(\frac{L}{\lambda})^{n}
. However, as we will see, the terms are reshuffled in the ROE compared to the OE.

It is possible to reach a general formula for the resummed opacity expansion at arbitrary order. This was done in App.
A.2
, and we refer to that section for detailed calculations.

First order (
N_{r}=1
):

The first order can be obtained from Eq. (
A.2
) with
n=1
, and reads

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}=-8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\int_{{\bm{p}}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}^{2})\,{\rm Im}\,T\left({\bm{p}}^{2}-i\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)\,,

(38)

in re-scaled, dimensionless variables. Equation (
38
) corresponds to the formula (Eq. (4.6)) in Ref.
Andres:2020kfg
, but is here derived more rigorously. Here, we have defined the function

\displaystyle T(x)=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}{\rm d}t_{0}\,{\rm e}^{-ix(t_{1}-t_{0})}=\frac{1-ix-{\rm e}^{-ix}}{x^{2}}\,.

(39)

The real and imaginary parts of
T({\bm{p}}^{2}-i\chi)
are given in Eq. (
A.2
). After doing the angular integral this becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}=-2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p\,\frac{1}{p+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\,{\rm Im}\,T\left(p-i\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)\,.

(40)

At low opacity
L\ll\lambda
, the function
T(p-iL/\lambda)
becomes

\left.-{\rm Im}\,T(p)\right|_{L\ll\lambda}=\frac{p-\sin(p)}{p^{2}}\,,

(41)

making it equivalent to the OE result in Eq. (
26
). The limiting behavior in the relevant limits of Eq. (
40
) can be extracted, leading to

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\left(\ln\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)-1+\gamma_{E}\right)-{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(1-\pi\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\right),&\text{for }\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}\,,\\[0.5pt]
\frac{\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{2}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}-\frac{\pi}{6}{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega},&\text{for }\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}\,.\end{cases}

(42)

At leading order in
\mathcal{O}(\frac{L}{\lambda})
this is the same as the
N=1
opacity expansion, presented in Eq. (
27
). However, in contrast to the OE, subleading “
N=2
”-like terms
\sim(\frac{L}{\lambda})^{2}
appear, which only will be relevant when compared to higher-order contributions at
N_{r}=2
.

In the high opacity limit
L\gg\lambda
, we have to extract the relevant limit of
T(p-i\chi)
in a careful way, yielding

\left.-{\rm Im}\,T(p-i\chi)\right|_{L\gg\lambda}\simeq\frac{p}{(L/\lambda)^{2}+p^{2}}\,.

(43)

Changing the integration variable to
q=p\lambda/L
, we observe that
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
replaces
\bar{\omega}_{c}
as the relevant scale, and Eq. (
40
) becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle\simeq 2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}q\,\frac{1}{q+\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}}\frac{q}{1+q^{2}}=2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\ln\left(\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\right)}{1+\left(\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\right)^{2}}\,.

(44)

Finally, one can extract the soft and hard limits of this expression, which are given by

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\left(\ln\left(\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\omega}{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}\right),&\text{for }\omega\ll\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\,,\\[0.5pt]
\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega},&\text{for }\omega\gg\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\,.\end{cases}

(45)

The soft limit agrees with the heuristic discussion in Sec.
2
. Strikingly, we see that the behavior in the soft and hard limit takes exactly the same form as for
N=1
except that
\bar{\omega}_{c}
has been replaced by
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
(note that
\bar{\omega}_{c}(L=\lambda)=\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
).

Second order (
N_{r}=2
):

The second order is found from Eq. (
A.2
) with
n=2
, and reads

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{2},{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{2}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{2}^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{{\bm{p}}_{2}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}}\left({\rm Re}\,T({\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}-i\chi)-{\rm Re}\,T({\bm{p}}_{2}^{2}-i\chi)\right)\,.

(46)

After going to polar coordinates and doing the angular integrals, this becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=4{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{1}\,\frac{1}{p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\frac{p_{1}}{\left[\left(p_{1}+p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}-4p_{1}p_{2}\right]^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{p_{2}-p_{1}}\left({\rm Re}\,T(p_{1}-i\chi)-{\rm Re}\,T(p_{2}-i\chi)\right)\,.

(47)

We study this expression separately in the low- and high-opacity limits.

In the low opacity limit
L\ll\lambda
, the spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle\simeq 4{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{1}\,\frac{1}{p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\frac{p_{1}}{\left[\left(p_{1}+p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}-4p_{1}p_{2}\right]^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{p_{2}-p_{1}}\left(\frac{1-\cos{p_{1}}}{p_{1}^{2}}-\frac{1-\cos{p_{2}}}{p_{2}^{2}}\right)\,,

(48)

where again the only relevant energy scale is
\bar{\omega}_{c}
, as it is in the OE.
The double momentum integral can be recognized as
\mathcal{I}_{2}
from
N=2
of the opacity expansion. The soft and hard limits are

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}},&\qquad\text{for }\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}\,,\\[0.5pt]
\frac{\pi}{6}{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega},&\qquad\text{for }\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}\,.\end{cases}

(49)

Summing up the two first orders of the ROE and OE we see that
N_{r}=1+2
agrees with
N=1+2
, but only when keeping the subleading
\sim(L/\lambda)^{2}
terms at order
N_{r}=1
. As mentioned before, the opacity expansion is arranged so that the order
N=n
only contains terms where the opacity scales as
\sim\chi^{n}
, where
\chi=L/\lambda
. The resummed opacity expansion also includes all of the same terms, but they are spread out over different orders of the expansion due to the resummation contained in the Sudakov factor. The orders
N_{r}<n
do contain terms going as
\chi^{n}
. To get the right term at order
\chi^{n}
in the ROE one therefore has to keep the subleading corrections going as
\chi^{n}
at all previous orders of the expansion. For this reason the opacity expansion is more convenient to use in the low opacity limit, as it does not mix orders of opacity.

In the high opacity limit
L\gg\lambda
, we get

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle\simeq 4{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}p_{1}\,\frac{1}{p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\frac{p_{1}}{\left[\left(p_{1}+p_{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}-4p_{1}p_{2}\right]^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{p_{2}+p_{1}}{(1+p_{2}^{2})(1+p_{1}^{2})}\,.

(50)

The soft and hard limits of this expression are given by

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\omega}{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}},&\qquad\text{for }\omega\ll\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\,,\\
\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega},&\qquad\text{for }\omega\gg\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\,,\end{cases}

(51)

where similarly to
N_{r}=1
, the relevant scale is now
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
. Both
N_{r}=1
and 2 goes as
\sim\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}
, however, in the soft limit
N_{r}=1
dominates, while in the hard limit
{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=2}\sim{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=1}
. This shows that ROE is quickly convergent if
\omega\ll\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
, while the expansion appears to break down for harder emissions. We expect this structure to appear to all orders in
N_{r}=n
. The resulting validity of the expansion is shown in the left of Fig.
5
in blue.
Based on our findings, the expansion scheme for the ROE at high opacity
L\gg\lambda
is

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}=\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}\right)\,,

(52)

where
f_{n}
is a finite function that can be obtained order by order for
\omega\ll\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
.

The resulting spectrum is shown in the right of Fig.
5
for different propagation lengths. For short times (
t<\lambda
), the ROE is valid for all
\omega
and it gives the same spectrum as the OE (compare to the right panel of Fig.
4
). For longer propagation the ROE is only valid if
\omega<\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
, which is denoted with bullets in the figure. Outside of the valid region, the curves turn to gray (see also the left panel). Based on the figure,
N_{r}=2
has negligible contribution to the spectrum until
t\approx\lambda
or
\omega\approx\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
. Again, the dashed line represents the full numerical evaluation of the spectrum from Ref.
Andres:2020vxs
;
Andres:2022
which is well approximated by the ROE in its region of validity.

3.4
Improved opacity expansion (IOE)

The final expansion scheme we consider is the improved opacity expansion, introduced in Refs.
Mehtar-Tani:2019tvy
;
Mehtar-Tani:2019ygg
;
Barata:2020sav
;
Barata:2020rdn
;
Barata:2021wuf
. We saw that the ROE at high opacity does not work for energies much higher than
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
. This makes sense since at higher energies the formation time
{t_{\text{f}}}
of the emission becomes bigger than the mean free path, implying that the parton will scatter many times on the medium. The main motivation for the improved opacity expansion is to resum multiple soft scatterings, while account perturbatively for rare, hard scatterings. This is achieved by introducing a scale
Q^{2}
that separates soft and hard scatterings in the scattering potential,

v({\bm{x}},t)\approx v_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t)+\delta v({\bm{x}},t)\,,

(53)

where
v_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t)=\frac{{\hat{q}}(t)}{4}{\bm{x}}^{2}
and
\delta v({\bm{x}},t)=\frac{{\hat{q}}_{0}(t)}{4}{\bm{x}}^{2}\ln{\frac{1}{Q^{2}{\bm{x}}^{2}}}
. Equation (
53
) is the
\mu|\bm{x}|\ll 1
expansion of Eq. (
14
) with the GW potential. The first term is referred to as the harmonic oscillator approximation (HO), where the jet quenching parameter is

{\hat{q}}(t)={\hat{q}}_{0}(t)\ln{\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu_{*}^{2}}}\,,

(54)

where
\mu_{\ast}^{2}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}{\rm e}^{-1+2\gamma_{E}}
for the GW potential. The logarithm in
\hat{q}
comes from the fact that the typical exchanged momentum
\langle\bm{k}^{2}\rangle=L\,\int_{\bm{q}}\bm{q}^{2}\sigma(\bm{q})
is divergent and thus it has to be regulated resulting in the leading logarithmic form in Eq. (
54
) (see in Ref.
Arnold:2009mr
). As long as
Q^{2}/\mu_{\ast}^{2}\gg 1/(Q^{2}\bm{x}^{2})
, the HO term dominates over
\delta v
, and the latter can be treated as a perturbation. This provides a big advantage, since the multiple scattering in the HO approximation can be resummed analytically.

The separation scale
Q^{2}
has to be fixed in a meaningful way to not to interfere with the expansion
Barata:2020sav
. A natural choice that achieves this is evaluating
Q^{2}
at the typical transverse momentum of the emission
\bm{k}^{2}\sim\hat{q}{t_{\text{f}}}
, that yields

Q_{r}^{2}(\omega)=\sqrt{\omega\hat{q}(\omega)}\,,

(55)

which constitutes an implicit equation for
Q^{2}_{r}(\omega)
, and for
\hat{q}(\omega)\equiv\hat{q}(Q_{r}(\omega))
, see Eq. (
54
).
12
12

12

Equation (
55
) has a solution only if
\omega>2e\frac{\mu^{4}_{\ast}}{\hat{q}_{0}}\simeq 0.925\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
. When this is satisfied, and
L>\lambda
, then
Q_{r}^{2}>\mu^{2}_{\ast}
by default. This is the necessary condition for the convergence of the IOE. The IOE therefore breaks down for
\omega\lesssim\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
.

The IOE corresponds to expanding the full medium solution for
{\cal K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}})
around the harmonic oscillator, in contrast to the conventional opacity expansion where one expands around the vacuum solution. It can be cast in the iterative equation

\displaystyle{\cal K}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})

\displaystyle={\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}s\int_{\bm{z}}\,{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{z}},s)\delta v({\bm{z}},s){\cal K}({\bm{z}},s;{\bm{y}},t_{1})\,.

(56)

Here,
{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}})
is itself the solution to an iterative equation, namely

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})

\displaystyle={\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}},t_{2}-t_{1})

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}s\,\int_{\bm{z}}\,{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{z}},t_{2}-s)v_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},s){\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},s;{\bm{y}},t_{1})\,.

(57)

The formal solution for
{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})
can also be cast as a path integral, namely

{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})=\int_{{\bm{r}}(t_{1})={\bm{y}}}^{{\bm{r}}(t_{2})={\bm{x}}}{\cal D}{\bm{r}}\,{\rm e}^{i\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}s\,\big[\frac{\omega}{2}\dot{\bm{r}}^{2}+iv_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{r}},s)\big]}\,,

(58)

which has a well-known analytical solution in a static medium,

\mathcal{K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}\left(\bm{x},t_{2};\bm{y},t_{1}\right)=\frac{\omega\Omega}{2\pi i\,\sin(\Omega\Delta t)}{\rm e}^{\frac{i\omega\Omega}{2\sin(\Omega\Delta t)}\left[\cos(\Omega\Delta t)\,({\bm{x}}^{2}+{\bm{y}}^{2})-2\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}\right]}\,,

(59)

where
\Delta t\equiv t_{2}-t_{1}
and
\Omega=\frac{1-i}{2}\sqrt{\hat{q}(\omega)/\omega}
is the characteristic oscillator frequency.

Inserting this expansion into the equation for the medium-induced spectrum Eq. (
12
) separates it into two parts,

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny HO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\,\partial_{\bm{x}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}\big[{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})-{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})\big]_{{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny IOE}}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}s\int_{0}^{s}{\rm d}t_{1}\,\int_{\bm{z}}\,

\displaystyle\times\partial_{\bm{x}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}[{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{z}},s)\delta v({\bm{z}},s){\cal K}({\bm{z}},s;{\bm{y}},t_{1})]_{{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}=0}\,.

(61)

The first term gives rise to the well-known HO spectrum, while the second constitutes an expansion in hard splittings around the harmonic oscillator. The IOE spectrum can be simplified further, giving

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny IOE}}}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{\bm{x}}\,{\rm e}^{-i\frac{\omega\Omega}{2}\tan{(\Omega(L-t_{2})){\bm{x}}^{2}}}\delta v({\bm{x}})\frac{{\bm{x}}}{{\bm{x}}^{2}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}{\cal K}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})|_{{\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(62)

where
{\cal K}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}})
should be iterated using Eq. (
3.4
) in order to generate higher orders of the expansion.
A general formula for the improved opacity expansion at arbitrary order can also be derived, which was done in Sec.
A.3
, see also in Ref.
Barata:2020sav
.

Harmonic oscillator (HO):

The harmonic oscillator approximation resums all coherent soft scatterings during the formation of the emission. The relevant scale is

\omega_{c}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\hat{q}(\omega_{c})L^{2}\,,

(63)

where the scale in the jet quenching parameter is set to
\omega_{c}
. This scale was already identified in Sec.
2
. The HO approximation is expected to be valid for
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\ll\omega\ll\omega_{c}
.

We derive here the familiar harmonic oscillator spectrum in a new way. The vacuum contribution can easily be subtracted by inserting Eq. (
3.4
) into Eq. (
12
), which gives

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny HO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}s\int_{0}^{s}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle\times\partial_{\bm{x}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}\big[{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{z}},s)v_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},s){\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},s;{\bm{y}},t_{1})\big]_{{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}=0}\,.

(64)

Using the fact that
\int_{s}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\,\partial_{\bm{x}}{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{z}},s)|_{{\bm{x}}=0}=-i\frac{\omega}{\pi}\frac{{\bm{z}}}{{\bm{z}}^{2}}
, the spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny HO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}=

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm\bar{\alpha}}{\hat{q}}(\omega)}{2\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{\bm{z}}\,{\bm{z}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})|_{{\bm{y}}=0}\,.

(65)

This can be further simplified by using that

\displaystyle\int_{\bm{z}}\,{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{\partial}_{\bm{y}}{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})|_{{\bm{y}}=0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(\omega\Omega)^{2}}{2\pi\sin^{2}(\Omega(t_{2}-t_{1}))}\int_{\bm{z}}\,{\bm{z}}^{2}{\rm e}^{i\frac{\omega\Omega}{2}{\bm{z}}^{2}\cot(\Omega(t_{2}-t_{1}))}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\cos^{2}(\Omega(t_{2}-t_{1}))}\,.

(66)

The time integration can be now be dealt with straightforwardly, yielding

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\,\frac{1}{\cos^{2}(\Omega(t_{2}-t_{1}))}=-\frac{\ln\cos\Omega L}{\Omega^{2}}\,,

(67)

and thus the spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny HO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=2\bar{\alpha}\,\ln\left|\cos\Omega L\right|\,,

(68)

which is the familiar BDMPS-Z spectrum
Baier:1996kr
;
Zakharov:1996fv
. The limits of this are

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny HO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{2\omega_{c}}{\omega}},&\text{for }\omega\ll\omega_{c}\,,\\[0.7pt]
\frac{{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{6}\left(\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2},&\text{for }\omega\gg\omega_{c}\,.\end{cases}

(69)

The soft limit agrees with the discussion in Sec.
2
, while the hard limit is subleading compared to the OE
N=1
in Eq. (
27
). Defining
\hat{q}
with a logarithm extends the region of validity, which was also found in Ref.
Arnold:2009mr
, leading to the curved
\omega_{c}(t)
line in Fig.
3
.

Next-to-harmonic oscillator (NHO):

Using the definition in Eq. (
62
) and the results of Sec.
A.3
, the first order of the improved opacity expansion can be written as

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny NHO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\pi}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}s\int_{\bm{u}}\,\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\frac{\mu^{2}}{2Q^{2}}\frac{1}{{\bm{u}}^{2}}\right)}{\rm e}^{\frac{i}{2}f(s){\bm{u}}^{2}}\,,

(70)

where we have defined the function
f(s)=\sigma\sqrt{\omega_{c}/\omega}[\cot(\sigma s\sqrt{\omega_{c}/\omega})-\tan(\sigma(1-s)\sqrt{\omega_{c}/\omega})]
, and
\sigma=\frac{1-i}{\sqrt{2}}
. After doing the
{\bm{u}}
integral this becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny NHO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}=2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}s\,\frac{1}{f(s)}\left[1-\gamma_{E}+\ln{\left(-i\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\frac{\mu_{*}^{2}}{Q^{2}}f(s)\right)}\right]\,.

(71)

The limits of this expression can readily be extracted. In the soft limit,
\omega\ll\omega_{c}
, we have
f(s)\to 2i\sigma\sqrt{\omega_{c}/\omega}
while in the hard limit,
\omega\gg\omega_{c}
, it becomes
f(s)\to 1/s
. These simplifications make it possible to do the last time integration. Hence, the extracted limiting behavior is,

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny NHO}}}{{\rm d}\omega}\simeq\begin{cases}{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{2\omega_{c}}{\omega}}\,\frac{1}{2\ln Q^{2}/\mu_{\ast}^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\gamma_{E}+\ln{\left(\frac{\sqrt{{\hat{q}}\omega}}{\sqrt{2}Q^{2}}\right)}\right),&\qquad\text{for }\omega\ll\omega_{c}\,,\\
\frac{\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{2}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega},&\qquad\text{for }\omega\gg\omega_{c}\,.\end{cases}

(72)

In the soft limit
\omega\ll\omega_{c}
, N
n
HO terms will take the form of the HO by using
Q_{r}
, and thus

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}={\rm\bar{\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{q}(\omega)L^{2}}{\omega}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{a_{0}}{\ln Q_{r}^{2}/\mu^{2}_{\ast}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\ln Q_{r}^{2}/\mu_{\ast}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)\,,

(73)

where we added the HO term, and used Eq. (
55
). The choice of
Q=Q_{r}(\omega)
is effective, when the medium is big enough
L\gg\lambda
. It is clear that the expansion parameter of the IOE is
\ln^{-1}(Q_{r}^{2}/\mu_{\ast}^{2})\ll 1
in the soft limit. Therefore, N
n
HO terms can be absorbed into an effective jet transport parameter,

\hat{q}_{\text{eff}}(Q^{2})=\hat{q}_{0}\ln\left(\frac{Q_{r}^{2}}{\mu^{2}_{\ast}}\right)\left[1+\frac{a_{0}}{\ln Q_{r}^{2}/\mu_{\ast}^{2}}+\frac{a_{1}}{\ln^{2}Q_{r}^{2}/\mu_{\ast}^{2}}+\dots\right]\,.

(74)

The coefficients
a_{0}=1.016
and
a_{1}=0.316
of the expansion and higher-order terms up to N
2
HO were found in Ref.
Barata:2020sav
.

In the hard limit of Eq. (
72
), one can see that the IOE reproduces the hard limit of
N=1
in the OE from Eq. (
27
). Furthermore, it is bigger than the HO contribution in Eq. (
68
) and thus NHO dominates for
\omega\gg\omega_{c}

3.5
Summary of the regimes and the induced emission spectrum

In this section, we have presented three distinct perturbative expansions (OE, ROE, and IOE) that provide different ways of calculating the induced emission spectrum
\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega}
in their respective regimes of convergence.
The OE and IOE are expansion schemes that were first developed in previous works, while the ROE is rigorously derived in this section for the first time. Their regions of validity are sketched previously in Figs.
4
–
6
, and at least one of the expansions is valid at every point in the phase space
(\omega,t)
. Here
\omega
is the emitted energy and
t
is the propagated length (
L
is the maximal length of the medium and
E
is the energy of the emitting particle). As a consequence, our description of the spectrum is complete in the full phase space, as one can always use one of the expansions to reach an approximation of the true spectrum, and one can reach better accuracy by including higher orders. Note that the expansions are overlapping: for
L<\lambda
both OE and ROE are valid, and for
\omega>\omega_{c}
both IOE the OE can be used.

4
Resumming multiple emissions in the medium

Section
3
presents a theoretical framework consisting of different perturbative expansions (namely the opacity expansion (OE), the resummed OE (ROE), and the improved OE (IOE)) to describe the full phase space (
\omega,t
) of the medium-induced gluon emission spectrum. The emitted energy
\omega
is limited by the energy of the emitter
\omega\ll E
, and the propagation time (length)
t
is in turn limited by the medium length
t<L
.
Our effective formalism accounts for arbitrarily many scatterings that can be arbitrarily hard or soft. Finally, Eq. (
75
) describes the emission spectrum up to arbitrary precision and recovers the full solution of Eq. (
12
) that has only been achieved numerically before
CaronHuot:2010bp
;
Feal:2018sml
;
Andres:2020vxs
.

It is now time to explore what consequences the full induced spectrum instills on a parton propagating through the medium. In this section, we will present analytical solutions of the evolution equation of the medium-induced cascade. We present the inclusive gluon energy distribution for different medium lengths, and we will focus on gluons for transparency. The numerical solutions are presented in Sec.
5
. Vacuum emissions belong outside of the scope of the current work.

4.1
The necessity of multiple emissions

Multiple emissions have to be taken into account whenever the multiplicity of gluons is large. We define the multiplicity of gluons above the energy
\omega
in terms of the spectrum
{\rm d}I/{\rm d}\omega
, as

N(\omega)=\int_{\omega}^{\infty}{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}\,.

(78)

The upper limit of the integral is taken to infinity because we will currently assume that the energy of the emitter
E
is much larger than the largest available medium energy scale. Also, for our current purposes, it suffices to consider the leading behavior of the spectrum in the various scattering regimes presented in Fig.
3
.

At low opacity
L\ll\lambda
and starting from
\omega<\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
, we find

N(\omega)\simeq\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}\ln^{2}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}+\frac{\pi\bar{\alpha}}{2}\frac{L}{\lambda}\,.

(79)

where
\bar{\omega}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}L
and we have only kept the leading terms.
The maximal multiplicity in the hard regime, see the second term, is always small for perturbative splittings with
\bar{\alpha}\ll 1
. Furthermore, the multiplicity in the soft (Bethe–Heitler) regime, given by the first term, becomes large only at very small energies, i.e.
\omega<{\rm e}^{\sqrt{1/(\bar{\alpha}L/\lambda)}}\bar{\omega}_{c}
. We can therefore safely neglect multiple emissions at low opacities.

For dense media,
L\gg\lambda
, we discuss the three pertinent cases. As usual, we will denote
\omega_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{q}L^{2}
. Then, for
\omega\gg\omega_{c}
(rare hard scattering regime), we find

N(\omega)\simeq\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\,,

(80)

where we again neglected subleading terms. Hence hard emissions, described by the OE expansion, can safely be considered to be rare.

Next, for
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\ll\omega\ll\omega_{c}
we get

N(\omega)\simeq 2^{\frac{3}{2}}\bar{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}\,,

(81)

where we introduced the leading behavior of the spectrum and neglected the multiplicity from hard emissions, following the discussion above. The multiplicity becomes large
N(\omega)\gg 1
at energies
\omega\ll\bar{\alpha}^{2}\omega_{c}
and thus multiple emission becomes dominant.
For large enough medium length
L\gg\lambda
, there is a significant phase space allowing for multiple emission (
\bar{\alpha}_{s}^{2}\omega_{c}(t)>\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
), resulting in the power enhancement.

Finally, for soft gluon energies
\omega<\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}\ll\omega_{c}
, the multiplicity is

N(\omega)\simeq\bar{\alpha}\frac{L}{\lambda}\ln^{2}\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}+2^{\frac{3}{2}}\bar{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}}\,,

(82)

where, again, only the leading terms from each regime were kept.
The second term in Eq. (
82
) scales as
\sim(L/\lambda)\sqrt{\hat{q}/\hat{q}_{0}}
. Based on the discussion above, this term is already large and the multiplicity continues to grow only logarithmically for small
\omega
, and therefore multiple emissions are going to happen.

4.2
Resummation of multiple emissions

Considering multiple emissions in a medium poses a tremendous theoretical challenge. The situation is quite analogous to the description of multiple gluon emissions in vacuum. Similar to QCD jets, the main challenge when considering medium effects lies in dealing with intricate interference effects between subsequent emissions, see e.g. Refs.
Arnold:2015qya
;
Arnold:2020uzm
;
Arnold:2021pin
.
However, when considering multiple soft emissions, that occur quasi-instantaneously, these effects can safely be neglected
Arnold:2002zm
;
Blaizot:2013vha
.
14
14

14

We can also extend this logic for the semi-hard emissions which are included in our formalism, since they are rare occurrences and therefore the resummation has no effect, see Sec.
4.3
.

In order to clarify the framework that we work in, let us briefly recall the main arguments for neglecting interference effects for a set of multiple induced emissions. For the time being, we stick to emissions in the HO region which dominate the multiplicity. The typical time it takes an emission to form, often referred to as a
formation time
(or in some works branching time), of a soft gluon is
{t_{\text{f}}}\sim\sqrt{\omega/\hat{q}}
. This time is much smaller than the extent of the medium
{t_{\text{f}}}\ll L
as long as
\omega\ll\omega_{c}
.

Another relevant quantity is the time between two subsequent emissions. This is related to the no emission probability (or Sudakov factor).
For a leading particle with energy
E
, the first emission is produced at time
{t_{\text{f}}}_{1}
, with energy
\omega_{1}
. A second, strongly ordered emission (
\omega_{2}\ll\omega_{1}
) of the original parton forms much quicker
{t_{\text{f}}}_{2}\ll{t_{\text{f}}}_{1}
. The time between the two emissions
t_{\text{split}}
can be estimated with
\int_{{t_{\text{f}}}_{1}}^{t_{\text{split}}}{\rm d}t\int_{\omega_{2}}^{\omega_{1}}{\rm d}\omega\,\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}t}\sim 1
, that is basically the probability of not having emissions between
\omega_{2}<\omega<\omega_{1}
, resulting in

t_{\text{split}}\sim{t_{\text{f}}}_{1}+\frac{1}{\bar{\alpha}}{t_{\text{f}}}_{2}\,.

(83)

Hence, our rough estimate implies that typically
t_{\text{split}}\gg{t_{\text{f}}}_{2}
(for
\bar{\alpha}\ll 1
). Therefore, the formation of emissions is short compared to the time that separates emissions and thus emissions form independently. This motivates the resummation of multiple independent emissions in terms of a rate equation.

Similar analysis can be done for hard emissions
\omega\gg\omega_{c}
, for which
t_{\rm split}
is very long due to the unlikeliness of hard scatterings, and therefore the emissions are formed independently.

For soft emissions
\omega\ll\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
,
t_{\rm split}\sim{t_{\text{f}}}_{1}+\frac{1}{\bar{\alpha}}\ln^{-2}\frac{\lambda}{{t_{\text{f}}}_{2}}
and thus emissions form independently. Close to the boundary in cases where
t_{\rm split}\approx{t_{\text{f}}}_{1}+{t_{\text{f}}}_{2}
, a more complicated structure appears in terms of resummation, as emissions might overlap. In this case, interference effects between the two emissions have to be included. A similar thing happens in vacuum for wide angle soft emissions, which result in angular ordering and in non-global effects for which the resummation has been understood just recently
Banfi:2021xzn
. We will use the rate equation to account for emissions with any
\omega
. However, it will not necessarily account correctly for interference among them and further study is needed in the future.

One question still remains open, namely the choice of the time scale used in the rate equation. In case of two emissions, the second emission experiences a shorter medium, of the scale
\sim L-t_{\rm f1}-t_{\rm split}
. We know, however, that in the soft limit
\omega\ll\omega_{c}
, the formation time is
t_{\rm f}\ll L
, and therefore, the length degradation should not matter for a large medium
Arnold:2002zm
;
Blaizot:2013vha
. For small media, or for emissions with comparable formation times, these corrections can become significant. It is an unresolved question how to incorporate these corrections into a rate equation see e.g. Ref.
Arnold:2020uzm
.
15
15

15

See also Sec. 4 in Ref.
Barata:2021byj
, where modifications of the rate due to finite formation time were studied.
However, as argued above, the corrections to the rate coming from finite-size effects can be treated in a perturbative fashion. While these issues merit further studies, perhaps within a Monte Carlo approach, we consider them to go beyond our present scope and we assume that all emissions experience the same length
L
. This matches the approximation in most of the current energy-loss models.

4.3
Analytic solutions of the evolution equation

The evolution equation (
85
) is readily solved by numerical evaluation, which will be discussed in Sec.
5
. Here we will discuss limiting cases where analytical solutions can be found. We can find such solutions at early times (considering only one emission) and at late times (considering many soft emissions). These are by now well-known limiting cases. Finally, we also consider the novel case of the evolution equation at intermediate times, where both rare, hard emissions and multiple, soft emissions can occur in sequence according to their respective allowed phase space of emissions, given in Fig.
3
.

To simplify our discussion, in this section we will neglect the Bethe–Heitler regime. We will nevertheless include it in the full numerical solutions presented in Sec.
5
.

Early time evolution:

At an early stage of medium propagation, the leading parton has little time to interact with the medium which also translates into a small probability of splitting. Given our previous discussion, the natural medium scale to compare with is the mean free path
\lambda
. Hence, at
t\lesssim\lambda
we consider a single splitting, leading to

\displaystyle D(x,t)

\displaystyle\simeq\delta(1-x)\left[1-\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\,z{\cal K}(z,xE,s)\right]+\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\int_{x}^{1}{\rm d}z\,{\cal K}\left(z,\frac{x}{z}E,s\right)\delta\left(1-\frac{x}{z}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}s\,x{\cal K}(x,E,s)\,,

(90)

where we dropped the term proportional to
\delta(1-x)
, which is only important for energy conservation, cf. Eq. (
87
). Using the results in Eq. (
88
), we find that

D(x,t)\simeq\begin{cases}2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{t}{\lambda}\frac{1}{1-x}\ln\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}(t)}{x(1-x)E}\right)&\quad\text{for }x\ll\bar{x}_{c}\,,\\
\frac{\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{4}\frac{{\hat{q}}_{0}}{E}\frac{t^{2}}{x(1-x)^{2}}&\quad\text{for }\bar{x}_{c}\ll x\ll 1-\bar{x}_{c}\,,\\
\end{cases}

(91)

where we have defined

\bar{x}_{c}=\bar{\omega}_{c}(t)/E
. For
x\ll\bar{x}_{c}
, corresponding to the single soft or Bethe–Heitler scattering regime, a characteristic
D\sim\ln 1/x
structure appears that is similar to the DGLAP energy distribution in vacuum. On the contrary, for
x\gg\bar{x}_{c}
, corresponding to the single hard scattering regime,
D\sim 1/x
, and the two regimes are separated by
\bar{x}_{c}
.
The limiting case of Eq. (
91
) is shown in Fig.
9
with dashed lines for different times (different panels). Since we, as we move onward, will largely neglect the description of the infared regime, we only used the single hard scattering (green) contribution that is valid
x>\bar{x}_{c}
.

Formally, the early-time expansion breaks down when
t>\lambda
which is also the characteristic time when multiple interactions with the medium become important. Finally, we do not expect the early time solution to hold for for
x<{\rm\bar{\alpha}}^{2}\omega_{c}/E
where multiple emissions play an important role.

Late time evolution:

The evolution equation can be solved exactly if one assumes coherent scatterings dominate for all momentum fractions
x
and all times
t
. This approximation is most sound when
t\gg L_{c}
, as seen in Fig.
3
. The analytical solution neglects the Bethe–Heitler, which will make the solution less reliable for very small
x<\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}/E
. We will call this solution
D_{0}
as it serves as a baseline for subsequent calculations. It is the solution to the evolution equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{0}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{1}{\rm d}z\,{\cal K}_{\rm coh}\left(z,\frac{x}{z}E,t\right)D_{0}\left(\frac{x}{z},t\right)-\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}z\,z{\cal K}_{\rm coh}\left(z,xE,t\right)D_{0}(x,t)

(92)

where
{\cal K}_{\rm coh}
is the soft limit of the harmonic oscillator regime, see the middle term in Eq. (
89

The solution from Ref.
Blaizot:2013hx
is

D_{0}(x,\tau)=\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{x}(1-x)^{3/2}}{\rm e}^{-\pi\frac{\tau^{2}}{1-x}}\,,

(93)

where we dropped the

6
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we studied emissions induced by elastic scatterings on the quark gluon plasma. We considered the interplay of the relevant length scales of the problem, which are the propagation length, the mean free path between scatterings, and the formation time. From these considerations, we derived the emergent energy scales that separate the induced emission spectrum into regimes governed by different scattering processes. The emerging hierarchy of scales and related processes is illustrated in Fig.
3
.

We presented a new theoretical framework consisting of different perturbative expansions, namely the opacity expansion (OE), the improved OE (IOE), and the resummed OE (ROE), which we derived rigorously. Together these are suitable to describe the induced emission spectrum in different regimes. We showed that at least one of the expansions is valid in every phase space (
\omega,t
) point, where
\omega
is the energy of the emitted gluon and
t
is the propagation time (length) in the medium.
19
19

19

For the general case valid beyond the strictly soft limit
\omega
refers to the reduced energy of the three-body evolution
\omega=z(1-z)E
, where
E
is the initial energy of the emitter and
z
is the momentum sharing fraction.
While these expansions are formally simply reorganizations of the multiple scattering series, it is also important to note that none of the expansions
when truncated at any fixed order
is valid everywhere. Relying on the multiple approaches to obtain the spectrum, our composite framework can account for an arbitrary number of medium interactions that can be soft or hard. Most importantly, it is systematically improvable and can, in principle, describe the emission spectrum (and rate) up to arbitrary precision, which has only been achieved numerically before
CaronHuot:2010bp
;
Feal:2018sml
;
Andres:2020vxs
.

In the current work, we have elucidated the convergence properties up to second order in the studied resummations. Identifying the expansion structure in the different regimes opens for the possibility of studying the accuracy of resummations in the medium. Finally, our new description provides a quick and efficient way to evaluate the induced emission spectrum (and rate), which is an essential ingredient of medium-induced cascades and jet quenching study. Our implementation is available in an online repository
MedKernels:2022
.

In order to tackle multiple emissions, we also studied the in-medium energy distribution
D(x,t)
within our new formalism. This is an essential ingredient of jet quenching phenomenology as it describes how the energy of a leading particle gets distributed within a cascade.
Having first identified the conditions to generate multiple emissions in the medium, we demonstrated the separation between early, rare emissions – generated mainly by a single momentum exchange with the medium – and a following cascade of soft splittings.
We also showed that emissions are formed independently up to suppressed terms, justifying a posteriori the formulation of the cascade via a rate equation.
We developed new analytic tools to combine the resummation of multiple soft with rare hard emissions, and thus we showed how different scattering processes appear on the level of the energy distribution. Finally, using numerical evaluation based on the previously derived full splitting kernels, we showed more rigorously the transition effects between different scattering regions. We identified the importance of the time dependent phase space separation, multiple Bethe–Heitler emissions, and the running of
\hat{q}
, to mention some.

Even though this work can immediately be applied to jet quenching phenomenology one should also tackle other challenges, such as vacuum emissions and coherence effects, which we have neglected here and left for future studies. We also acknowledge the importance of soft emissions for which one should study thermal masses, energy changing
2\to 2
scatterings, and thermalization
Ghiglieri:2015ala
;
Schlichting:2020lef
to correctly describe the very infrared regime close to the thermal scale and below. Moreover, other non-perturbative effects such as expanding, inhomogeneous medium are also important, in which direction our framework is extendable
Casalderrey-Solana:2012evi
;
Adhya:2019qse
;
Caucal:2020xad
;
Adhya:2021kws
.

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{\left({\bm{p}}^{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\bm{p}}^{2}+\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}}\,.

(110)

The momentum and time integrals only depend on the unitless combination
\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega
. This means that the spectrum will take the form
\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}={\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}h_{n}(\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}})
, for some function
h_{n}
. This would naively imply convergence for
L/\lambda<1
, but the exact for of the function
h_{n}
must also be taken into account. In Sec.
3.2
we showed for
N=1,2
at low energy
\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}
that
h_{n}(\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}})
is a finite function, meaning the series converges when
L/\lambda<1
. However, at high energy
\omega\gg\bar{\omega}_{c}
the function takes the form
h_{n}(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})\sim(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})^{n}\tilde{h}_{n}(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})
, where
\tilde{h}_{n}
is finite, implying convergence when
\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}<1
. Proving this is true to all orders is deferred to future work.

One can also derive a corresponding formula for the rate
\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=n}}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}L}
, by taking the length derivative of Eq. (
A.1
)

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=n}}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}L}=(-1)^{n-1}8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{1}{L}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\tilde{v}({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1})\dots\tilde{v}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{0}\int_{t_{0}}^{1}{\rm d}t_{n-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n-1}}{\rm d}t_{n-2}\dots\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\,{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}(1-t_{n-1})}{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{n-1}^{2}(t_{n-1}-t_{n-2})}\dots{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}(t_{1}-t_{0})}\,.

(111)

A.2
Resummed opacity expansion

One can also derive all order formulas for the resummed opacity expansion, by starting with Eq. (
3.3
) and following the same procedure,

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{4\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\Sigma({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\sigma({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1})\dots\sigma({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

(112)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{0}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{n-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n-1}}{\rm d}t_{n-2}\dots\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\,\Delta(t_{n},t_{0}){\rm e}^{-i\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}{2\omega}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})}\dots{\rm e}^{-i\frac{{\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}}{2\omega}(t_{1}-t_{0})}\,.

The ROE and OE expansions are equivalent at infinite order at low opacity
L/\lambda\ll 1
, but at finite order the terms are mixed up. The terms containing a delta function in the potential
v
in the OE are included in the Sudakov factor
\Delta
in the ROE. One can see this by taking
\Delta\to 1
and
v\to-\sigma
in Eq. (
112
) and Eq. (
108
) respectively, in which case the expansions become exactly the same. This is however not a good approximation at any order.

After changing to unitless variables we arrive at

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}=8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1})\dots\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

(113)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{0}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{n-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n-1}}{\rm d}t_{n-2}\dots\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{L}{\lambda}(t_{n}-t_{0})}{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})}\dots{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}(t_{1}-t_{0})}\,,

where we have used that in the static medium
\Delta(t_{n},t_{0})=\exp(-\frac{t_{n}-t_{0}}{\lambda})
. Again we use the unitless functions
\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}^{2})
and
\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}})
defined in Eq. (
A.1
) for the GW model. In this case the time integrals can be done analytically

\displaystyle i\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{0}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{n-1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{n-1}}{\rm d}t_{n-2}\dots\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{L}{\lambda}(t_{n}-t_{0})}{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}(t_{n}-t_{n-1})}\dots{\rm e}^{-i{\bm{p}}_{1}^{2}(t_{1}-t_{0})}

\displaystyle=i^{n}\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{{\rm e}^{-i\left({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-i\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)(t_{n}-t_{0})}}{\prod_{l\neq k}^{n}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{l}^{2})}=\left(\cos{\frac{n\pi}{2}}+i\sin{\frac{n\pi}{2}}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{T\left({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-i\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)}{\prod_{l\neq k}^{n}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{l}^{2})}\,,

(114)

where we have defined the function

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{0}\,{\rm e}^{-ix(t_{n}-t_{0})}=\frac{1-ix-{\rm e}^{-x}}{x^{2}}\,.

(115)

The reason this simplification was not possible in the OE is that the formula for the time integrals is only valid if
{\bm{p}}_{k}\neq{\bm{p}}_{l}
, while the OE has terms containing
\delta({\bm{p}}_{k}-{\bm{p}}_{k-1})
. However, in the ROE there are no such deltas, so the formula is valid.
In the end the spectrum is

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1})\dots\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\cos{\frac{n\pi}{2}}{\rm Re}\,T({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-i\chi)-\sin{\frac{n\pi}{2}}{\rm Im}\,T({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-i\chi)}{\prod_{l\neq k}^{n}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{l}^{2})}\,.

(116)

The remaining momentum integrals will in the end be some function of
\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega
and opacity
\chi=L/\lambda
. As a reference, the real and imaginary parts of
T({\bm{p}}^{2}-i\chi)
are

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,T({\bm{p}}^{2}-i\chi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi({\bm{p}}^{4}+\chi^{2})+{\bm{p}}^{4}-\chi^{2}-{\rm e}^{-\chi}\left(({\bm{p}}^{4}-\chi^{2})\cos{{\bm{p}}^{2}}+2\chi{\bm{p}}^{2}\sin{{\bm{p}}^{2}}\right)}{({\bm{p}}^{4}+\chi^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle-{\rm Im}\,T({\bm{p}}^{2}-i\chi)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}({\bm{p}}^{4}+\chi^{2})-2\chi{\bm{p}}^{2}-{\rm e}^{-\chi}\left(({\bm{p}}^{4}-\chi^{2})\sin{{\bm{p}}^{2}}-2\chi{\bm{p}}^{2}\cos{{\bm{p}}^{2}}\right)}{({\bm{p}}^{4}+\chi^{2})^{2}}\,.

(117)

It is also possible to look at the limits of this function when the opacity
\chi
is low or high. In the low opacity case
\chi\ll 1
we have

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle\simeq 8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1})\dots\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\cos{\frac{n\pi}{2}}(1-\cos{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}})+\sin{\frac{n\pi}{2}}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-\sin{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}})}{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{4}\prod_{l\neq k}^{n}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{l}^{2})}\,.

(118)

In this case the momentum integrals will give a function of
\bar{\omega}_{c}/\omega
. As mentioned in Sec.
3.3
is it preferable to use the OE (
A.1
) at low opacity, as the order of opacity is mixed up in the ROE.

In the high opacity case
\chi\gg 1
you get

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle\simeq 8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{L}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2},\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1},\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}})\dots\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1},\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\cos{\frac{n\pi}{2}}+\sin{\frac{n\pi}{2}}{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}}{(1+{\bm{p}}_{k}^{4})\prod_{l\neq k}^{n}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{l}^{2})}\,.

(119)

Here we see the emergence of the scale
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}\lambda
, which takes the place of
\bar{\omega}_{c}
in the functions
\tilde{\Sigma}
and
\tilde{\sigma}
, as emphasized in the above formula. The momentum integrals then only become some function of
\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}/\omega
. Notice that this scales as
\sim L/\lambda
at every order, meaning it can converge also when
L/\lambda>1
.

In Sec.
3.4
we calculated the high opacity limit to orders
N=1,2
. There it was clear that for low
\omega\ll\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
the
N=2
limit is subleading compared to the
N=1
limit, while at high
\omega\gg\omega_{\text{\tiny BH}}
the
N=2
and
N=1
limits are of the same order. This seems to imply that the expansion converges at low
\omega
, while it breaks down at high
\omega
. Again, proving this for all orders is deferred to future work.

Again the calculation for the rate
\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}L}
is similar

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{r}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}L}=8\pi{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\frac{1}{L}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int_{{\bm{p}}_{n},\dots,{\bm{p}}_{1}}\,\tilde{\Sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}^{2})\frac{{\bm{p}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{1}}{{\bm{p}}_{n}^{2}}\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{n}-{\bm{p}}_{n-1})\dots\tilde{\sigma}({\bm{p}}_{2}-{\bm{p}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\cos{\frac{n\pi}{2}}\left[\chi-{\rm e}^{-\chi}\left(\chi\cos{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}}-{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}\sin{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]+\sin{\frac{n\pi}{2}}\left[{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\rm e}^{-\chi}\left({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}\cos{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}}+\chi\sin{{\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}}\right)\right]}{({\bm{p}}_{k}^{4}+\chi^{2})\prod_{l\neq k}^{n}({\bm{p}}_{k}^{2}-{\bm{p}}_{l}^{2})}\,.

(120)

A.3
Improved opacity expansion

The spectrum for the improved opacity expansion can be written as the iterative equation

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\rm IOE}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\alpha_{s}C_{R}}{\omega^{2}}{\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}s\,\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle\times\partial_{\bm{x}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}[{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{z}},s)\delta v({\bm{z}},s){\cal K}({\bm{z}},s;{\bm{y}},t_{1})]_{{\bm{x}}={\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(121)

where the zeroth order solution is the HO from Eq. (
68
) has to be added. In the following we will make use of results from Ref.
Mehtar-Tani:2019tvy
;
Mehtar-Tani:2019ygg
. The harmonic oscillator propagator is

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{HO}}\left(\bm{x},t_{2};\bm{y},t_{1}\right)=\frac{\omega}{2\pi iS\left(t_{2},t_{1}\right)}\exp\left(\frac{i\omega}{2S\left(t_{2},t_{1}\right)}\left[C\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\bm{x}^{2}+C\left(t_{2},t_{1}\right)\bm{y}^{2}-2\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}\right]\right)\,.

(122)

The functions
S
and
C
are given implicitly by

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{~d}^{2}t}+\Omega^{2}(t)\right]S\left(t,t_{0}\right)=0,\quad S\left(t_{0},t_{0}\right)=0,\quad\partial_{t}S\left(t,t_{0}\right)_{t=t_{0}}=1}

(123)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{~d}^{2}t}+\Omega^{2}(t)\right]C\left(t,t_{0}\right)=0,\quad C\left(t_{0},t_{0}\right)=1,\quad\partial_{t}C\left(t,t_{0}\right)_{t=t_{0}}=0}\,,

where the frequency
\Omega(t)
is given by

\Omega(t)=\frac{1-i}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{q}(t)}{\omega}}

(124)

To continue it is useful to apply the formulas

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\partial_{\bm{x}}{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{z}},s)|_{{\bm{x}}=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i\omega}{\pi}\frac{{\bm{z}}}{{\bm{z}}^{2}}{\rm e}^{i\frac{\omega}{2}\Omega(s)^{2}\frac{S(s,L)}{C(s,L)}{\bm{z}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}{\rm d}t_{1}\partial_{\bm{y}}{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},s;{\bm{y}},t_{1})|_{{\bm{y}}=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{-i\omega}{\pi}\frac{{\bm{z}}}{{\bm{z}}^{2}}{\rm e}^{-i\frac{\omega}{2}\frac{C(0,s)}{S(0,s)}{\bm{z}}^{2}}\,.

(125)

Then the spectrum takes the following form

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\rm IOE}}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{x}}\,{\rm e}^{-i\frac{\omega\Omega}{2}\tan{(\Omega(L-t_{2})){\bm{x}}^{2}}}\delta v({\bm{x}})\frac{{\bm{x}}}{{\bm{x}}^{2}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{y}}{\cal K}({\bm{x}},t_{2};{\bm{y}},t_{1})|_{{\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(126)

where we have used that for the brick medium
{\hat{q}}(t)=\Theta(L-t){\hat{q}}
, the functions
S
and
C
are simply

S\left(t_{2},t_{1}\right)=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sin\Omega\left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right),\quad\text{ and }\quad C\left(t_{2},t_{1}\right)=\cos\Omega\left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)\,.

(127)

One can derive a formula for the IOE at arbitrary order, which was also explored in Ref.
Barata:2020sav
.

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{I}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\pi}\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{x}}_{n}\dots{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{x}}_{1}\,\frac{{\bm{x}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{x}}_{1}}{{\bm{x}}_{n}^{2}{\bm{x}}_{1}^{2}}\delta v({\bm{x}}_{n})\dots\delta v({\bm{x}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,\int_{0}^{L}{\rm d}t_{n}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}{\rm d}t_{n-1}\dots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}{\rm d}t_{1}\,{\rm e}^{i\frac{\omega\Omega}{2}\left[\cot{(\Omega t_{1})}{\bm{x}}_{1}^{2}-\tan{(\Omega(L-t_{n})){\bm{x}}_{n}^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}}_{n},t_{n};{\bm{x}}_{n-1},t_{n-1})\dots{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{x}}_{2},t_{2};{\bm{x}}_{1},t_{1})\,.

(128)

After changing to unitless variables by defining
{\bm{u}}_{k}=\sqrt{\mu^{2}\omega/(2\bar{\omega}_{c})}{\bm{x}}_{k}
and
s_{k}=\frac{t_{k}}{L}
,

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{I}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\pi}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{n}\dots{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{1}\,\frac{{\bm{u}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{1}}{{\bm{u}}_{n}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{1}^{2}}\delta\tilde{v}({\bm{u}}_{n})\dots\delta\tilde{v}({\bm{u}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}s_{n}\int_{0}^{s_{n}}{\rm d}s_{n-1}\dots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}{\rm d}s_{1}\,{\rm e}^{i\frac{\sigma}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}\left[\cot{(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}s_{1})}{\bm{u}}_{1}^{2}-\tan{(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}(1-s_{n})){\bm{u}}_{n}^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times\tilde{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{u}}_{n},s_{n};{\bm{u}}_{n-1},s_{n-1})\dots\tilde{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{u}}_{2},s_{2};{\bm{u}}_{1},s_{1})\,.

(129)

Here we have defined
\sigma=\frac{1-i}{\sqrt{2}}
and the unitless functions

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{v}({\bm{u}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{u}}^{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\frac{\mu^{2}}{2Q^{2}}\frac{1}{{\bm{u}}^{2}}\right)}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{u}}_{2},s_{2};{\bm{u}}_{1},s_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}}{2\pi i\sin{\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}(s_{2}-s_{1})\right)}}

\displaystyle\times{\rm e}^{\frac{i\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}}{2\sin{\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}(s_{2}-s_{1})\right)}}\left[\cos{\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}(s_{2}-s_{1})\right)({\bm{u}}_{2}^{2}+{\bm{u}}_{1}^{2})-2{\bm{u}}_{2}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{1}}\right]}\,.

(130)

As the integrals only depend on
\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}}
and
\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\frac{\mu^{2}}{2Q^{2}}
the IOE spectrum can be written as
\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{I}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}=\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\pi}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}f_{n}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}}{\omega}},\frac{\omega}{\bar{\omega}_{c}}\frac{\mu^{2}}{2Q^{2}}\right)
where the function
f_{n}
is given by the integrals.
The soft limit
\omega\ll\omega_{c}
of the IOE expansion was discussed in detail in
Barata:2020sav
, and also in Sec.
3.4
.

In the hard limit
\omega\gg\omega_{c}
, the spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N_{I}=n}}{{\rm d}\omega}

\displaystyle\simeq(-1)^{n-1}\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\pi}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)^{n}\int{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{n}\dots{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{1}\,\frac{{\bm{u}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{1}}{{\bm{u}}_{n}^{2}{\bm{u}}_{1}^{2}}\delta\tilde{v}({\bm{u}}_{n})\dots\delta\tilde{v}({\bm{u}}_{1})

\displaystyle\times{\rm Re}\,\int_{0}^{1}{\rm d}s_{n}\int_{0}^{s_{n}}{\rm d}s_{n-1}\dots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}{\rm d}s_{1}\,{\rm e}^{i\frac{{\bm{u}}_{1}^{2}}{2s_{1}}}

\displaystyle\times\tilde{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{u}}_{n},s_{n};{\bm{u}}_{n-1},s_{n-1})\dots\tilde{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{u}}_{2},s_{2};{\bm{u}}_{1},s_{1}),

(131)

where the BDMPS propagator has gone to the vacuum propagator

\tilde{\cal K}_{0}({\bm{u}}_{2},s_{2};{\bm{u}}_{1},s_{1})=\frac{1}{2\pi i(s_{2}-s_{1})}{\rm e}^{i\frac{i({\bm{u}}_{2}-{\bm{u}}_{1})^{2}}{2(s_{2}-s_{1})}}\,.

(132)

Notice that there is no remaining dependence on the BDMPS scale
\omega_{c}
. In Sec.
3.4
we calculated the hard limit to first order, and the resulting expression (
72
) is the same as the OE limit (
27
) for high
\omega
. Whether this correspondence is also true at higher orders is an interesting question that will be explored in future work.

Appendix B
General formulas for the emission rate in the soft limit

In this appendix, we gather the formulas relevant for computing the emission rate in the soft limit. The rate can be written as

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}t}=\frac{4{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\pi}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}_{0}}\,\Sigma({\bm{p}}^{2},t)\frac{{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{p}}_{0}}{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{\cal K}({\bm{p}},t;{\bm{p}}_{0},t_{1})\,,

(133)

in momentum-space representation of the three-point function, and

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}t}=\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{\bm{z}}\,v({\bm{z}},t)\frac{{\bm{z}}}{{\bm{z}}^{2}}\cdot\bm{\partial}_{\bm{y}}\left.{\cal K}({\bm{z}},t;{\bm{y}},t_{1})\right|_{{\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(134)

in coordinate-space representation. Equation (
133
) can be employed directly to derive expressions for the rate in the OE and ROE, by simply inserting the expansions (
3.1
) and (
3.3
). We will not attempt at deriving higher-order corrections to these rates here, since they can be also be found for a medium with constant density by taking the appropriate derivative with respect to length on the expression for the spectrum.

For the IOE, the harmonic oscillator spectrum is directly calculable, following the decomposition in Eqs. (
60
) and (
3.4
). For the IOE rates, we find

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny HO}}}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{2\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{\bm{z}}\,\hat{q}(t){\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{\partial}_{\bm{y}}\left.{\cal K}_{\text{\tiny HO}}({\bm{z}},t;{\bm{y}},t_{1})\right|_{{\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(135)

\displaystyle\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{\text{\tiny IOE}}}{{\rm d}\omega{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}}{\omega}{\rm Re}\,i\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t_{1}\int_{\bm{z}}\,\delta v({\bm{z}},t)\frac{{\bm{z}}}{{\bm{z}}^{2}}\cdot\bm{\partial}_{\bm{y}}\left.{\cal K}({\bm{z}},t;{\bm{y}},t_{1})\right|_{{\bm{y}}=0}\,,

(136)

where the three-point correlator
{\cal K}({\bm{z}};{\bm{y}})
is found from iterating (
3.4
). As a cross-check, for a medium with constant density we obtain

Medium-induced spectrum and rate with finite-

The process we study is a parton of energy

Reference

.

The starting equation for keeping finite-

corrections can be found in Ref.

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}I_{ba}^{\rm med}}{{\rm d}z}=\frac{\alpha_{s}}{{\omega}^{2}}P_{ba}(z){\rm Re}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}t_{2}\int^{t_{2}}_{0}{\rm d}t_{1}\,\bm{\partial}_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\partial}_{\bm{y}}\left[{\cal K}_{ba}(\bm{x},t_{2};\bm{y},t_{1})-{\cal K}_{0}(\bm{x},t_{2};\bm{y},t_{1})\right]_{\bm{x}=\bm{y}=0}\,,

\displaystyle P_{gq}(z)

\displaystyle=C_{F}\frac{1+(1-z)^{2}}{z}\,,\quad\quad P_{qq}(z)=P_{gq}(1-z)\,,\quad\quad

\displaystyle P_{gg}(z)

\displaystyle=C_{A}\frac{[1+z(1-z)]^{2}}{z(1-z)}\,,\quad\quad P_{qg}(z)=N_{f}T_{F}\left[z^{2}+(1-z)^{2}\right]\,,

Appendix E
HTL potential

In this appendix, we investigate what happens if employ the HTL potential in the multiple-scattering series. The potential now reads

\displaystyle\sigma({\bm{q}})=N_{c}n(t)\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\sigma_{\rm el}}{{\rm d}^{2}{\bm{q}}}=\frac{4\pi\,\hat{q}_{0}}{{\bm{q}}^{2}({\bm{q}}^{2}+m_{D}^{2})}\,,

(179)

where
m_{D}
is the Debye screening mass in a thermal medium with temperature
T
and
\hat{q}_{0}=4\pi\alpha_{s}m_{D}^{2}T

Aurenche:2002pd
.
In this case, we find that

\Sigma({\bm{p}}^{2})=\frac{\hat{q}_{0}}{m_{D}^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}+m_{D}^{2}}{{\bm{p}}^{2}}\right)\,,

(180)

where we have a logarithmic divergence as
{\bm{p}}^{2}\to 0
.
This implies that the mean free path has to be regulated by an IR cut-off so that
\lambda\sim\Sigma^{-1}(p^{2}_{\rm min})
.

In this case, the OE remains unmodified because the divergence at
p^{2}_{\rm min}\to 0
cancels order by order between the real and virtual contributions. The ROE, however, has to be treated with care when truncated at a finite order since we have to introduce an explicit IR cut-off in order to define the elastic Sudakov factor. Hence, the modified Sudakov reads

\Delta(t,t_{0})={\rm e}^{-\Sigma_{\text{reg}}(t-t_{0})}\,,

(181)

in a static medium, where
\Sigma_{\text{reg}}\equiv\Sigma(p^{2}_{\rm min})
with
p^{2}_{\rm min}
and unknown IR regulator. Clearly, an all-order resummation of the ROE series would remove the spurious IR dependence.

The role of the medium potential in the IOE was clearly elucidated in
Barata:2020sav
, where the information about the scattering potential is fully contained in the definition of
\mu_{\ast}^{2}
. We refer the reader to this paper for an exhaustive discussion.

In order to clarify what modifications arise in the OE and ROE, we compute the respective first-order terms of the expansions, i.e.
N=1
and
N_{r}=1
, explicitly here. In the OE, we find

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}=2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\,\frac{\hat{q}_{0}L}{m_{D}^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}u\,\ln\left(\frac{u+y}{u}\right)\,[-{\rm Im}\,T(u)]\,,

(182)

where now we defined

with
\bar{\omega}_{c}=m_{D}^{2}L/2
. The prefactor contains
\hat{q}_{0}/m_{D}^{2}
, which is similar to the inverse mean free path (in fact, in the GW model it would be exactly equal to
\lambda^{-1}

\omega\frac{{\rm d}I^{N=1}}{{\rm d}\omega}=2{\rm\bar{\alpha}}\,\frac{\hat{q}_{0}L}{m_{D}^{2}}\begin{cases}\ln(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega})\left[-1+\gamma_{E}+\ln\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}_{c}}{\omega}\right)\right]&\text{ for }\omega\ll\bar{\omega}_{c}\\
