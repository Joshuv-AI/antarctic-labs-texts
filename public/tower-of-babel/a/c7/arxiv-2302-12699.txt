Wall-and-chamber structures for finite-dimensional algebras and τ \tau -tilting theory


Abstract
Zusammenfassung The wall-and-chamber structure is a geometric invariant that can be associated to any algebra. In this notes we give the definition of this object and we explain its relationship with torsion classes and τ \tau -tilting theory.

1.1
Introduction

The main aim of representation theory of finite-dimensional algebras is to understand the category of (finitely presented) modules over a given algebra.
One of the founding results in this area is a result by Gabriel
[
21
]
, which states that the module category of any finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is equivalent, in the sense of Morita
[
28
]
, to the category of representations of a quiver with relations (which depend on the original algebra).
This was breakthrough in the field, not only because it reduces greatly the universe of algebras to be studied, but also because with the incorporation of quivers, Gabriel also allowed the introduction of important combinatorial tools that have played a central role in the theory ever since.

Several years later, at the turn of the century, Fomin and Zelevinski introduced
cluster algebras

[
18
]
as a new approach to understand Lusztig’s dual canonical bases.
These are algebras that are defined from starting data, known as the
the initial seed
, and then the reminding data is constructed using a combinatorial process known as
mutation
.
For a family of cluster algebras of particular importance, the
antisymmetric
cluster algebras, both the initial data and the mutation process can be encoded in terms of quivers.
As a consequence, many mathematicians started using the tools developed throughout the years in representation theory to understand cluster algebras and solve some of the standing conjectures in this new subject
[
22
,
12
,
13
,
2
]
.

Also, the study of cluster algebras made explicit certain patterns in the module category of every finite-dimensional algebra.
It was in this context that notions which are central in current representation theory, such as
\tau
-tilting theory
[
1
]
or higher homological algebra
[
25
]

[
24
]
, were defined.
For a detailed account of the origin of
\tau
-tilting theory, see
[
35
]
.

Cluster algebras are subalgebras of certain function fields.
This point of view allows a geometric approach to their study.
The basic idea states that a cluster algebra should be the coordinate ring of a variety, which is unknown a priori.
In this line of work one can find, for instance, the works of Fock and Goncharov
[
17
]
and Gross, Hacking, Keel and Kontsevich
[
23
]
.

In the latter the authors associate to each cluster algebra a
cluster scattering diagram
, a cone complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
where every cone comes with the element of a group satisfying equations that are determined by the cone complex itself.
They then used these scattering diagrams to show at once several open conjectures in cluster theory and to give new proofs for other conjectures that have been settled by different means.

Soon after, Bridgeland
[
9
]
defined for every finite-dimensional algebra a
stability
scattering diagram.
In this case the cone complex is defined using the stability conditions studied by King in
[
27
]
.
Bridgeland showed moreover that, if the algebra is hereditary (i.e.
A=KQ
for an acyclic quiver
Q
) over an algebraically closed field
K
, then the stability scattering diagram associated to
A
is isomorphic to the cluster scattering diagram associated to the cluster algebra of
Q
.

The support of the stability scattering diagram, that is, its underlying cone complex, is known as the
wall-and-chamber structure
of the algebra.

are dedicated to give a detailed definition of the wall-and-chamber structure of an algebra and to explain the relationship of this object and

.

The structure of these notes is the following. After recalling some basic facts about the module category of an algebra, we introduce stability conditions, the wall-and-chamber structure of an algebra and we study some of their basic properties.
Afterwards we give a brief overview on
\tau

We then explain the relationship between the wall-and-chamber structure of an algebra and its

1.2
Preliminaries

For any finite-dimensional algebra
A
we denote by
\mods A
the category of finitely presented (right)
A
-modules.
Let
M\in\mods A
, then we denote by
\add M
the full subcategory of
\mods A
additively generated by
M
, that is the category of all direct summands of direct sums of
M
. Furthermore, define the subcategories
\Fac M
and
\Sub M
to be given by

\Fac M\coloneqq\{X\in\mods A:\text{there exists an epimorphism }M^{p}\to X\to 0\text{ for some $p\in\mathbb{N}$}\},

\Sub M\coloneqq\{X\in\mods A:\text{there exists a monomorphism }0\to X\to M^{p}\text{ for some $p\in\mathbb{N}$}\}.

Finally, we need to introduce the following notation:

T^{\perp}\coloneqq\{X\in\mods A:\Hom(T,X)=0\},\quad{}^{\perp}T\coloneqq\{X\in\mods A:\Hom(X,T)=0\}.

In these notes we often work with a very particular type of subcategories of
\mods A
, the so-called
torsion classes

[
16
]
.

Definition 1
.

[
16
, Theorem 2.1]

A pair of full subcategories
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
of
\mods A
is a
torsion pair
if the following are satisfied:

(1)

\Hom_{A}(T,F)=0
for
T\in\mathcal{T}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
, and

(2)

for all
M\in\mods A
there exists a short exact sequence given by

0\to tM\to M\to fM\to 0

(1.1)

where
tM\in\mathcal{T}
and
fM\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then
\mathcal{T}
is called the
torsion class
and
\mathcal{F}
the
torsion-free class
.

It turns out that given a torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
this short exact sequence
1.1
is unique, see
[
5
, Proposition VI.1.5]
, meaning that
tM
and
fM
depend functorially on
M
. We then call
0\to tM\to M\to fM\to 0
the
canonical
short exact sequence of
M
with respect to
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
.
In this case we say that
tM
is the
torsion object
of
M
.

While torsion pairs are defined for an arbitrary abelian category, the following result gives us an additional characterisation of the torsion and torsion-free classes in our setting.

Proposition 2
.

[
16
, Theorem 2.3]

A subcategory
\mathcal{T}
of
\mods A
is a torsion class if and only if
\mathcal{T}
is closed under quotients and extensions. In this case, the corresponding torsion-free class
\mathcal{F}
such that
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is a torsion pair is
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
.

Dually, a subcateogory
\mathcal{F}
of
\mods A
is a torsion-free class if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is closed under subobjects and extensions. In this case, the associated torsion class is
\mathcal{T}={}^{\perp}\mathcal{F}
.

We furthermore assume that
A
is a basic algebra.
This means that there exist a collection of idempotents
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
such that
1_{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}
verifying
e_{i}A\not\cong e_{j}A
if
i\neq j
.
If we denote
e_{i}A
as
P(i)
, we can therefore write
A=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P(i)
.
Moreover, in this case, the set
\{P(1),\dots,P(n)\}
contains exactly one representative of each isomorphism class of indecomposable projective
A
-modules.

For a module
M\in\mods A
, let
|M|
denote the number of isomorphism classes of indecomposable direct summands.
In particular, with the notation above, we have that
|A|=n
.

The
Grothendieck group
of
\mods A
is the abelian group
K_{0}(\mods A)\coloneqq K_{0}(A)=\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{F}
is the free abelian group generated by the set of isomorphism classes of a module
M\in\mods A
. Then, the Grothendieck group
K_{0}(A)
is obtained by identifying
[M]=[L]+[N]
corresponding to all short exact sequences

0\to L\to M\to N\to 0

(1.2)

in
\mods A
.
The Jordan-Hölder Theorem for modules states that every module
M\in\mods A
has a composition series with unique length whose subfactors are simple modules which are unique up to permutation.
In other words there exists a composition series

0=M_{0}\subset M_{1}\subset\dots\subset M_{t}=M

which comes with short exact sequences

0\to M_{j-1}\to M_{j}\to\underbrace{M_{j}/M_{j-1}}_{\cong S(i)}\to 0.

We may therefore decompose the equivalence class of any module
M\in K_{0}(A)
as follows:

\displaystyle[M]=[M_{t}/M_{t-1}]+[M_{t-1}]=\dots=\sum_{j=1}^{t}[M_{j}/M_{j-1}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[S(i)],

where
a_{i}
is the multiplicity of the simple module
S(i)
at vertex
i
as a composition factor of
M
.
Then
\{[S(1)],\dots,[S(n)]\}
generates
K_{0}(A)
and there is an isomorphism of abelian groups given by
\bdim:K_{0}(A)\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
sending
[S(i)]\mapsto\mathbf{e}_{i}
, where
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Given an
A
-module
M
, we denote by
\bdim M
the vector
\bdim([M])\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and we call it the
dimension vector
of
M
.
The reason behind this name is the following: If
A=KQ/I
is the bounded path algebra of a quiver
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1})
with set of vertices
Q_{0}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
M
is an
A
-module, then
\bdim M=(\dim_{K}M_{1},\dots,\dim_{K}M_{n})
where
M_{i}
is the
K
-vector space
M\mathbf{e}_{i}
.

Let

D_{A}\coloneqq\text{diag}(\dim_{K}\End_{A}(S(1)),\dots,\dim_{K}\End_{A}(S(n)))

be the matrix whose diagonal entries equal the dimensions over
K
of the endomorphism algebras of the simples.
We can now define the inner product we use throughout these lecture notes as
\langle-,-\rangle:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\langle v,w\rangle=v^{T}D_{A}w.

Note that when
A=KQ/I
the matrix
D_{A}
is the identity matrix and this inner product corresponds with the classical dot product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Due to this remark, and by abuse of notation, the classical dot product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is also denoted by
\langle-,-\rangle
.

1.3
Stability conditions

The study of stability conditions of module categories is due to King
[
27
]
, who translated the geometric invariant theory of Mumford
[
29
]
to quiver representations. We begin by recalling some basic concepts and definitions. Given a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a so called
stability condition
, we get two notions of stability for modules.

Definition 3
.

[
27
]
Let
M\in\mods A
, take
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say
M
is
v
-semistable
if
\langle v,\bdim M\rangle=0
and if for any nonzero proper subobject
L
of
M
we have
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle\leq 0
or, equivalently, we have
\langle v,\bdim N\rangle\geq 0
for all nonzero proper quotients
N
of
M
. We say
M
is
v
-stable
if these inequalities are strict.

Definition 4
.

Define the
category of
v
-semistable objects

\mods_{v}^{ss}A
to be the full subcategory of
\mods A
whose objects are
v
-semistable.

The following result is a special case of
[
11
, Proposition 2.20]
and shows that
\mods_{v}^{ss}A
has desirable properties.

Theorem 5
.

[
11
, Proposition 2.20]
The category
\mods_{v}^{ss}A
is a wide subcategory of
\mods A
i.e. closed under kernels, cokernels and extensions.

Beweis.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
be a homomorphism between
v
-semistable modules
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. If
f
is zero or an isomorphism the result follows immediately. Otherwise, we want to show that
\ker f
and
\coker f
are
v
-semistable. Consider the short exact sequences

0\to\ker f\to M_{1}\to\image f\to 0,

0\to\image f\to M_{2}\to\coker f\to 0.

By definition, the
v
-semistable modules
M_{i}
satisfy
\langle v,\bdim M_{i}\rangle=0
and
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle\leq 0
for any subobject
L
of
M_{i}
or equivalently
\langle v,\bdim N\rangle\geq 0
for any quotient
N
of
M_{i}
. So in particular we get

\langle v,\bdim\image f\rangle\geq\langle v,\bdim M_{1}\rangle=0,

\langle v,\bdim\image f\rangle\leq\langle v,\bdim M_{2}\rangle=0.

Thus
\langle v,\bdim\image f\rangle=0
. Furthermore, from the exact sequences it follows that

\bdim\ker f=\bdim M_{1}-\bdim\image f\quad\text{and}\quad\bdim\coker f=\bdim M_{2}-\bdim\image f,

which tells us that

\langle v,\bdim\ker f\rangle=\langle v,\bdim M_{1}\rangle-\langle v,\bdim\image f\rangle=0,

and similarly
\langle v,\bdim\coker f\rangle=0
. Since subobjects of
\ker f
are subobjects of
M_{1}
and quotients of
\coker f
are quotients of
M_{2}
, they satisfy the necessary inequalities. Thus
\ker f,\coker f\in\mods_{v}^{ss}A
.

For extensions, we assume that
X,Z\in\mods_{v}^{ss}A
in the diagram below and want to show that
Y\in\mods_{v}^{ss}A
. Similar to above we have that
\langle v,\bdim Y\rangle=\langle v,\bdim X\rangle+\langle v,\bdim Z\rangle=0
because of exactness of the sequence. What is left to prove is that for all subobjects
L
of
Y
we have
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle\leq 0
, which can be seen from the following diagram:

The following definition of a brick is essential in studying stability conditions and related notions.

Definition 6
.

An object
B\in\mods A
is called a
brick
if its endomorphism algebra
\End_{A}(B)
is a division ring i.e. every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

The first connection between stable modules and bricks is the following.

Proposition 7
.

[
30
, Theorem 1]

If
B
is
v
-stable then
B
is a brick.

Beweis.

Let
B
be
v
-stable. Similar to the proof of the previous theorem, if we assume that
f:B\to B
is not zero and not an isomorphism, then the following short exact sequences

0\to\ker f\hookrightarrow B\twoheadrightarrow\image f\to 0,

0\to\image f\hookrightarrow B\twoheadrightarrow\coker f\to 0

imply

\langle v,\bdim\image f\rangle>\langle v,\bdim B\rangle>\langle v,\bdim\image f\rangle,

which is a contradiction. Hence,
f
is either 0 or an isomorphism, thus any morphism
f\in\End(B)
is invertible and thus
\End_{A}(B)
a division ring when
B
is
v
-stable.
∎

We observe the following connection between
v
-stables and
v
-semistables.

Lemma 8
.

The (relatively) simple modules in
\mods_{v}^{ss}A
are exactly the
v
-stable objects.

Beweis.

Let
M
be a
v
-stable module, then
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle<0
for all nonzero proper submodules
L
of
M
. Hence it cannot have nonzero
v
-semistable proper submodule
L
which would have to satisfy
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle=0
. Now assume that a
v
-semistable module
M
does not have any nonzero proper submodules, then it trivially satisfies
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle<0
for all nonzero proper submodules
L
and is therefore
v
-stable.
∎

The following theorem by Rudakov
[
30
]
is reminiscent of the “Jordan-Hölder-Theorem” for semistable modules because of the previous lemma.

Theorem 9
.

[
30
, Theorem 3]

Let
M\in\mods_{v}^{ss}A
. Then there exists a filtration

0=M_{0}\subset M_{1}\subset\dots\subset M_{t}=M

such that
M_{i}/M_{i-1}
is
v
-stable. Moreover, any two such filtrations have the same length and the multiset
\{M_{i}/M_{i-1}\}
is independent of the filtration chosen.

Beweis.

We only show the existence of such a filtration. Since the
v
-stable modules are relatively simple in
\mods_{v}^{ss}A
the “uniqueness” of the filtration can be proven following arguments similar to those used to show the classical Jordan-Hölder Theorem.

If
M
is
v
-stable, then we may take the filtration
0\subseteq M
and we are done.
Assume that
M
is
v
-semistable, but not
v
-stable, then there exists some nonzero proper submodule
L
of
M
such that
\langle v,\bdim L\rangle=0
. Take
L
to be a maximal submodule with that property, then
M/L
satisfies

\langle v,\bdim M/L\rangle=\langle v,\bdim M\rangle-\langle v,\bdim L\rangle=0.

Moreover any submodule
N/L
of
M/L
corresponds to a submodule
N
of
M
containing
L
. Thus

\langle v,\bdim N/L\rangle=\underbrace{\langle v,\bdim N\rangle}_{<0}-\underbrace{\langle v,\bdim L\rangle}_{=0}<0.

Then
M/L
is
v
-stable.
If
L
is
v
-stable, then we are done. Otherwise we repeat this process with
L
,
which eventually ends since
\mods A
is a length category.
∎

Baumann, Kamnitzer and Tingley
[
8
]
showed that we can associate to every stability condition two torsion pairs in the following way.

Proposition 10
.

[
8
, Proposition 3.1]
Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a stability conditon, then there exist torsion pairs
(\mathcal{T}_{v},\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{v})
and
(\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{v},\mathcal{F}_{v})
where

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{v}=\{0\}\cup\{Y\in\mods A:\forall Y\to Z\to 0,\langle v,\bdim Z\rangle>0\}
,

(2)

\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{v}=\{0\}\cup\{Y\in\mods A:\forall Y\to Z\to 0,\langle v,\bdim Z\rangle\geq 0\}
,

(3)

\mathcal{F}_{v}=\{0\}\cup\{Y\in\mods A:\forall 0\to X\to Y,\langle v,\bdim X\rangle<0\}
,

(4)

\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{v}=\{0\}\cup\{Y\in\mods A:\forall 0\to X\to Y,\langle v,\bdim X\rangle\leq 0\}
.

Moreover,
\mods_{v}^{ss}A=\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{v}\cap\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{v}
.

\mathcal{T}_{v}
,
\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{v}
,
\mathcal{F}_{v}
and
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{v}

\mods A
. First, let us use

of
T
. Then any quotient

of
T^{\prime}
is also a quotient of
T
and hence satisfies
\langle v,\bdim T^{\prime\prime}\rangle>0
as required. Thus
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{v}
. Furthermore if
T
and
T^{\prime}
are in
\mathcal{T}_{v}
consider the following extension

and consider

. Assume there is a morphism

and

(\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{v},\mathcal{F}_{v})

Instead of asking which modules
M
are
v
-semistable for a given
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let us now consider for which
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a module
M
is
v
-semistable.
It turns out that the collection of vectors (i.e. stability conditions) for which a module is semistable, called the
stabiliy space
, forms a rich structure.
We now introduce the main object of study of these notes: the
wall-and-chamber structure
of an algebra.

Definition 11
.

Fix a nonzero module
M\in\mods A
, then the
stability space

\mathcal{D}(M)
of
M
is

\mathcal{D}(M)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:M\text{ is $v$-semistable}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We say
\mathcal{D}(M)
is a
wall
if
\codim\mathcal{D}(M)=1
. A
chamber
is an open connected component of

\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mods A\\
0\neq M\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(M)}.

The combination of all stability spaces
\mathcal{D}(M)
for indecomposable modules
M
and the corresponding chambers they define is called the
wall-and-chamber structure
. Because of the following result it is sufficient to calculate the stability spaces of the indecomposable modules to obtain the whole wall-and-chamber structure.

Proposition 12
.

Let
M,N\in\mods A
, then
\mathcal{D}(M\oplus N)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(M)\cap\mathcal{D}(N)
.

Beweis.

Let

1.4
\tau
-tilting theory

The area of
\tau
-tilting theory is a recent development in the representation theory of finite dimensional algebras.
The name is a combination of (classical) tilting theory and Auslander-Reiten theory, where
\tau
represents the Auslander-Reiten translation.
It was first introduced in the early 2010s by Adachi, Iyama and Reiten
[
1
]
and has since become an active area of research.
Many connections with other mathematical subfields have been established and continue to be discovered.
This new theory can be viewed as a completion of classical tilting theory with respect to mutations.
We begin by introducing some definitions.

Definition 16
.

[
1
]

Let
T,P\in\mods A
, where
P
is projective. Then

(1)

T
is
\tau
-rigid
if
\Hom_{A}(T,\tau T)=0
;

(2)

T
is
\tau
-tilting
if it is
\tau
-rigid and
|T|=|A|
;

(3)

a pair
(T,P)
is
\tau
-rigid
if
T
is
\tau
-rigid and
\Hom_{A}(P,T)=0
;

(4)

a
\tau
-rigid pair
(T,P)
is
\tau
-tilting
if
|T|+|P|=|A|=n
.

Sometimes, the term
support
\tau
-tilting module
is used to describe a
\tau
-rigid module
T
which is part of a
\tau
-tilting pair
(T,P)
. For comparison, let us give the definition of a (classical) tilting module.

Definition 17
.

Let
T\in\mods A
. Then
T
is called
tilting
if

(1)

\pd T\leq 1
, i.e the projective dimension of
T
is less than or equal to 1, and

(2)

\Ext_{A}^{1}(T,T)=0
, i.e.
T
is
rigid
, and

(3a)

there exists a short exact sequence
0\to A\to T^{\prime}\to T^{\prime\prime}\to 0
, where
T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}\in\add T
.

At first glance there may not seem to be many similarities between
\tau
-tilting modules and tilting modules. However, by
[
5
, Corollary VI.4.4]
, the third condition is equivalent to the following:

(3b)

|T|=|A|
.

Moreover,
[
7
, Proposition 5.8]
states that
\Hom_{A}(T,\tau T)=0
implies
\Ext_{A}^{1}(T,T)=0
and
[
5
, Corollary IV.2.14]
implies that the converse holds when
\pd T\leq 1
. This means that, when the projective dimension of a module
M
is at most one, a module
M
is tilting if and only if it is
\tau
-tilting. Therefore, one can view
\tau
-tilting as a possible generalisation of tilting theory.
The following result is sometimes called Skowroński’s Lemma.

Lemma 18
.

[
1
, Proposition 1.3]

If
(T,P)
is
\tau
-rigid then
|T|+|P|\leq n
.

Beweis.

Let
e
be an idempotent of
A
such that
\add P=\add Ae
and write
B=A/\langle e\rangle
. Then
[
1
, Lemma 2.1b]
implies that
\Hom_{A}(T,\tau T)=0
if and only if
\Hom_{B}(T,\tau_{B}T)=0
. It follows from
[
7
, Theorem 5.10]
and from
[
32
]
that
|T|\leq|B/\textnormal{ann}(T)|\leq|B|=|A|-|P|=n-|P|
.
∎

A natural question to ask is what the minimal torsion class containing a given module
M\in\mods A
is. To answer this, we must first introduce some new notions. Recall, that for a module
M
we denote by
\Fac M
the full subcategory of
\mods A
of all quotient modules of finite direct sums of copies of
M
. Now, if
\mathcal{X}
is a subcategory of
\mods A
, then the full subcategory
\Filt(\mathcal{X})
is defined by

\Filt(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\{X\in\mods A:\exists 0=X_{0}\subset X_{1}\subset\dots\subset X_{t}=X\text{ such that }X_{i+1}/X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\}.

We are now able to answer the question above with the following well-known result first stated in
[
15
]
and proved in
[
33
]
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
M\in\mods A
, the minimal torsion class containing
M
is
\Filt(\Fac M)
.

Beweis.

Let us first show that
\Filt(\Fac M)
is indeed a torsion class by showing it is closed under quotients and extensions.

Take
N\in\Filt(\Fac M)
with composition series
0=N_{0}\subset N_{1}\subset\dots\subset N_{t}=N
such that
N_{i+1}/N_{i}\in\Fac M
. Consider a quotient of
N
denoted by
N^{\prime}\coloneqq N/L
. Then we obtain the following filtration of
N^{\prime}
,

0=N_{0}^{\prime}\subset N_{1}^{\prime}\subset\dots\subset N_{r}^{\prime}=N^{\prime},

where
N_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq(N_{i}+L)/L
. Thus

\displaystyle N_{i+1}^{\prime}/N_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left((N_{i+1}+L)/L\right)/\left((N_{i}+L)/L\right)

\displaystyle\cong(N_{i+1}+L)/(N_{i}+L),

where we made use of the third isomorphism theorem to obtain the second line. Hence there is a well-defined homomorphism
N_{i+1}/N_{i}\to(N_{i+1}+L)/(N_{i}+L)
given by
n+N_{i}\mapsto n+(N_{i}+L)
which is surjective. Therefore
N_{i+1}^{\prime}/N_{i}^{\prime}
is a quotient of
N_{i+1}/N_{i}
and thus also an element of
\Fac M
. So
N^{\prime}\in\Filt(\Fac M)
.

Now assume we have
N,N^{\prime}\in\Filt(\Fac M)
, and consider the short exact sequence
0\to N\to E\to N^{\prime}\to 0
. Then
N^{\prime}\cong E/N
and submodules of
N^{\prime}
are in bijective correspondence with submodules of
E
containing
N
. Let
N\subseteq L_{i}\subseteq E
be the submodules corresponding to the filtration
0=N_{0}^{\prime}\subset N_{1}^{\prime}\subset\dots\subset N_{t}^{\prime}=N^{\prime}
of
N^{\prime}
such that
N_{i}^{\prime}\cong L_{i}/N
. Then we can obtain a filtration for
E
by taking

0=N_{0}\subset N_{1}\subset\dots\subset N_{s}=N=L_{0}\subset L_{1}\subset\dots\subset L_{t}=E.

Clearly the composition factors of the filtration of
N
are in
\Fac M
by definition. And the components of the right half of the filtration satisfy

\displaystyle L_{i+1}/L_{i}

\displaystyle\cong(L_{i+1}/N)/(L_{i}/N)

\displaystyle\cong N_{i+1}^{\prime}/N_{i}^{\prime},

which is in
\Fac M
as it is a composition factor of the filtration of
N^{\prime}
. Thus
E\in\Filt(\Fac M)
. This is the smallest torsion class because every element is an iterated extension of elements in
\Fac M
, which must be contained in every torsion class containing
M
.
∎

However,
\Fac(\Filt(\add T))
need
not
be a torsion class since it might not be closed under extensions.

1.4.1
Finiteness conditions on subcategories

In this subsection we show a close connection between
\tau
-tilting theory and a particular type of torsion classes in
\mods A
. We begin by introducing two definitions.

Definition 20
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mods A
be a full subcategory. Given a module
M\in\mods A
, a
right
\mathcal{X}
-approximation
of
M
is a map
f_{M}:X_{M}\to M
with
X_{M}\in\mathcal{X}
such that for any map
g:Y\to M
with
Y\in\mathcal{X}
, there is a map
g^{\prime}:Y\to X
such that the following diagram commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\forall g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists g^{\prime}}

In other words, every map
g:Y\to M
factors through the map
f_{M}:X_{M}\to M
. Dually, a
left
\mathcal{X}
-approximation
of
M
is a map
g_{M}:M\to X_{M}
with
X_{M}\in\mathcal{X}
such that for any map
h:M\to Y
with
Y\in\mathcal{X}
, there is a map
h^{\prime}:X_{M}\to Y
such that the following diagram commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\forall h}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists h^{\prime}}

Definition 21
.

We say that a full subcategory
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mods A
is
contravariantly finite (in
\mods A
)
if every
M\in\mods A
admits a right
\mathcal{X}
-approximation. Dually
\mathcal{X}
is
covariantly finite
if every
M\in\mods A
admits a left
\mathcal{X}
-approximation. The subcategory
\mathcal{X}
is called
functorially finite
if it is both contravariantly and covariantly finite.

It turns out that we have been studying a particular example of such subcategories.

Proposition 22
.

Every torsion class in
\mods A
is contravariantly finite.

Beweis.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a torsion class such that
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
is the torsion-free class. Take any module
M\in\mods A
. Then by the definition of torsion pairs, there exists a canonical short exact sequence

0\to tM\xrightarrow{f_{M}}M\xrightarrow{g_{M}}fM\to 0

where
tM\in\mathcal{T}
. For any
T \in\mathcal{T}
consider the morphism
h:T\to M
, then since
fM\in\mathcal{F}
,
g_{M}\circ h=0
. So,
\image h\in\ker{g_{M}}=tM
. Hence
h
factors through
tM
like desired.
∎

Dually, one can show that any torsion-free class in
\mods A
is covariantly finite. The following theorem by Auslander and Smalø
[
7
]
lets us characterise the modules
T\in\mods A
such that
\Fac T
is a (functorially finite) torsion class.

Theorem 23
.

[
7
, Theorem 5.10]

Let
T\in\mods A
. Then
\Fac T
is a torsion class if and only if
T
is
\tau
-rigid. In this case,
\Fac T
is functorially finite. Moreover, every functorially finite torsion class arises this way.

The previous classification of functorially finite torsion classes was refined by Adachi, Iyama and Reiten in
[
1
]
using the notion of
\tau
-tilting pair. This is considered as one of the more fundamental results in
\tau
-tilting theory.

Theorem 24
.

[
1
, Theorem 2.7]

There is a one-to-one correspondence

\displaystyle\{\text{$\tau$-tilting pairs}\}

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\{\text{functorially finite torsion classes}\}.

\displaystyle(T,P)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\quad\Fac T

A natural question in representation theory is to consider the algebras having a finite number of objects with a certain property.
An algebra
A
is said to be
\tau
-tilting finite
if there are finitely many basic
\tau
-tilting pairs in
\mods A
.
The following characterisation of
\tau
-tilting finite algebras was given by Demonet, Iyama and Jasso in
[
15
]
.

Theorem 25
.

[
15
, Theorem 3.8]

Let
A
be a finite dimensional algebra. The following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is
\tau
-tilting finite.

(2)

Every torsion class in
\mods A
is functorially finite.

(3)

Every torsion-free class in
\mods A
is functorially finite.

In this case, there are only finitely many torsion and torsion-free classes in
\mods A
.

Recall, that for a torsion class
\mathcal{T}
we say

is
\Ext

if

\Filt(\Fac({\begin{array}[]{c}1\end{array}}\oplus{\begin{array}[]{c}2\end{array}}))=\add\{{\begin{array}[]{c}1\end{array}}\oplus{\begin{array}[]{c}2\end{array}}\oplus{\begin{array}[]{cc}1\\
2\end{array}}\}=\Fac({\begin{array}[]{c}2\end{array}}\oplus{\begin{array}[]{cc}1\\
2\end{array}}).

In this example, we also showcased a
\tau
-rigid module that is not support
\tau
-tilting, namely
{\begin{array}[]{cc}1\\
2\end{array}}
. In general, the following two torsion pairs associated to a
\tau
-rigid pair

are of particular interest:

(\Fac T,T^{\perp})\quad\text{and}\quad({{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}},\Sub(\tau T\oplus\nu P)),

where
\nu
is the Nakayama functor. Adachi Iyama and Reiten
[

]
showed the following relation between these two torsion-classes.

Proposition 27
.

[
1
, Corollary 2.13]

Let
(T,P)
be
\tau
-rigid, then
\Fac T\subseteq{{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}
. Moreover the equality holds if
(T,P)
is
\tau
-tilting.

Beweis.

We only show here the inclusion
\Fac T\subseteq{{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}
.
Let
M\in\Fac T
, then there exists an epimorphism
T^{r}\xrightarrow{p}M\to 0
for some

. Assume there exists a nonzero

, then

is a nonzero map, contradicting the fact that

is
\tau
-rigid. On the other hand, if
g\in\Hom(P,M)
is nonzero, then the projectivity of

implies the existence of a map

such that the following diagram commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{r}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exists h}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}

Again, since

is
\tau
-rigid

and thus

. In conclusion

.
∎

Since
\Fac T
is contained in
{{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}
a natural question is to ask how many functorially finite torsion classes
\mathcal{T}
there are such that
\Fac T\subset\mathcal{T}\subset{{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}
. In other words, how many completions are there of a
\tau
-rigid
(T,P)
into a
\tau
-tilting pair? We call a
\tau
-tilting pair

\tau
-tilting
if
|T|+|P|=|A|-1
. The following property of almost
\tau
-tilting pairs is one of the motivating factors that lead to the introduction of
\tau
-tilting theory.

Theorem 28
.

[
1
, Theorem 2.18]

Let
(T,P)
be an almost
\tau
-tilting pair. Then there are exactly two completions of
(T,P)
into a
\tau
-tilting pair.

The theorem above allows us to define

of
\tau
-tilting pairs, where we delete one indecomposable direct summand of
(T,P)
and complete it to the unique different
\tau
-tilting pair. The following definition formalises this and distinguishes between the two completions of an almost
\tau
-tilting pair.

Definition 29
.

Let
(T,P)
be an almost
\tau
-tilting pair, and let
(T^{\prime},P^{\prime})
and
(T^{\prime\prime},P^{\prime\prime})
be the two completions of
(T,P)
into a
\tau
-tilting pair. Then we say that
(T^{\prime},P^{\prime})
and
(T^{\prime\prime},P^{\prime\prime})
are
mutations
of each other. And we say that
(T^{\prime\prime},P^{\prime\prime})
is the
left mutation
of
(T^{\prime},P^{\prime})
if
\Fac T^{\prime\prime}=\Fac T
and
\Fac T^{\prime}={{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}
.

Jasso
[
26
]
generalised
28

\tau
-tilting reduction
.

Theorem 30
.

[
26
, Theorem 1.1]

For every
\tau
-rigid pair
(T,P)
there exists an algebra
B_{(T,P)}
(the
\tau
-tilting reduction of
A
by
(T,P)
) and a bijection

\{\tau\text{-tilting pairs in $\mods B_{(T,P)}$}\}\longleftrightarrow\{\text{completions of $(T,P)$ to a $\tau$-tilting pair}\}.

The module category of this
\tau
-tilting reduction may be expressed in the following way.

Theorem 31
.

[
26
, Theorem 1.4]

The category
\mods B_{(T,P)}
is equivalent to the
\tau
-perpendicular category
{T^{\perp}\cap{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}
of
(T,P)
and

|B_{(T,P)}|=|A|-|T|-|P|.

1.4.2
g-vectors

Beside the dimension vector
\bdim M
of an
A
-module
M
, we can also associate another integer vector to the module, called the
g
-vector
of
M
. The
g
-vector was first introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
19
]
in the context of cluster algebras. Later, it was shown that the
g
-vector is encoded in the projective presentation of
\tau
-rigid modules. It turns out that
g
-vectors of
\tau
-rigid pairs have a lot of underlying structure to them.

Definition 34
.

Let
M\in\mods A
be an
A
-module, and
P_{-1}\to P_{0}\to M\to 0
be a minimal projective presentation of
M
where
P_{0}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P(i)^{a_{i}}
and
P_{-1}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P(i)^{b_{i}}
, then the
g
-vector
of
M
is

g^{M}\coloneqq(a_{1}-b_{1},a_{2}-b_{2},\dots,a_{n}-b_{n}).

We call
g^{T}-g^{P}
the
g
-vector of the
\tau
-rigid pair
(T,P)
. The following result is fundamental in the study of
g
-vectors and its idea can already be found in
[
6
]
, but was first shown by Dehy and Keller
[
14
]
by using a geometric approach in the context of 2-Calabi-Yau categories over an algebraically closed field. Later, Adachi, Iyama and Reiten
[
1
]
adapted it to the language of
\tau
-tilting theory and Demonet, Iyama and Jasso
[
15
]
extended it to an arbitrary field.

Theorem 35
.

[
14
, Theorem 2.3]

[
15
, Theorem 6.5]

Let
(T,P)
and
(T^{\prime},P^{\prime})
be
\tau
-rigid pairs then
g^{T}-g^{P}=g^{T^{\prime}}-g^{P^{\prime}}
if and only if
T\cong T^{\prime}
and
P\cong P^{\prime}
.

It follows trivially that if two
\tau
-rigid modules
T
and
T^{\prime}
share a
g
-vector
g^{T}=g^{T^{\prime}}
, then
T\cong T^{\prime}
. On top of that, the
g
-vectors of
\tau
-tilting pairs exhibit many desirable properties. The first of which is the following.

Theorem 36
.

[
1
, Theorem 5.1]

If
(T,P)
is a
\tau
-tilting pair then
\{g^{T_{i}},-g^{P_{i}}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

The following theorem was proven by Auslander and Reiten in the 1980s, but with the development of
\tau
-tilting theory has suddenly become a key result in this field.
Here recall from
Section

1.2

that
\langle v,w\rangle=v^{T}D_{A}w
.

Theorem 37
.

[
6
, Theorem 1.4]

Let
M,N\in\mods A
, then

\langle g^{M},\bdim N\rangle=\dim_{K}\Hom_{A}(M,N)-\dim_{K}\Hom_{A}(N,\tau M).

Beweis.

Let
P_{-1}\to P_{0}\to M
be a minimal projective presentation of
M
.
Let us write
P_{0}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P(i)^{a_{i}}
and
P_{-1}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P(i)^{b_{i}}
.
Since
\langle-,-\rangle
is bilinear we have that

\langle g^{M},\bdim N\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-b_{i})\dim_{K}\Hom_{A}(P(i),N).

We know that
\dim_{K}\Hom_{A}(P(i),N)=\dim_{K}\End_{A}(S(i))\dim_{K}N_{i}
, where
N_{i}
is the vector space at vertex
i
of the representation
N
. Furthermore
g^{P(i)}=\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
has nonzero entry equal to 1 only in the
i
-th position. Therefore,

\langle g^{P(i)},\bdim N\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{e}_{i},\bdim N\rangle=\dim_{K}\End_{A}(S(i))\dim_{K}N_{i}=\dim_{K}\Hom_{A}(P(i),N).

∎

Let us now study the arrangement of
g
-vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, we consider the cones spanned by
g
-vectors of
\tau
-rigid objects in the following manner.

Definition 38
.

Let
(T,P)
be a
\tau
-rigid pair so
T=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}T_{i}
and
P=\bigoplus_{i=k+1}^{t}P_{i}
for some
t\leq n
, then we define the polyhedral cone
\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}
to be given by

\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}g^{T_{i}}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{t}\alpha_{j}g^{P_{j}}:\alpha_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq t\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The interior cone

is defined as

Recall, that

is the Grothendieck group of an algebra

, the free abelian group isomorphic to

having as basis the set

of isomorphism classes of simple right

40

is a
\tau
-rigid pair then we have an equivalence of categories

\mods_{v}^{ss}A=T^{\perp}\cap{{}^{\perp}\tau T\cap P^{\perp}}

for every

. The following result tells us how many
v
-stable modules there are when

is in the interior cone of a
\tau
-rigid pair
(T,P)
.

Proposition 42
.

[
10
, Theorem 3.14]
Let
(T,P)
be a
\tau
-rigid pair and let
v\in\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}^{o}
. Then there are exactly

\rank\left(K_{0}\left(T^{\perp}\cap{{}^{\perp}\tau T}\cap P^{\perp}\right)\right)=|A|-|T|-|P|

v
-stable modules. In particular, there are no nonzero
v
-stable modules when
(T,P)
is
\tau
-tilting.

Beweis.

This is a combination of
31
and
40
.
∎

The following result establishes a bijection between chambers and
\tau
-tilting pairs and was first shown by Brüstle, Smith and Treffinger
[
10
]
who proved that every
\tau
-tilting pair gives rise to a unique chamber. Later, Asai
[
4
]
showed the converse.

Theorem 43
.

[
10
, Proposition 3.15]
[
4
, Theorem 3.17]

Let
(T,P)
be a
\tau
-tilting pair. Then
\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}^{o}
is a chamber, that is, a connected open component of
\mathfrak{R}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mods A\\
0\neq M\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(M)}

Beweis.

Take

. Since
(T,P)
is a
\tau
-tilting pair, we know
\mods_{v_{1}}^{ss}A=\{0\}
from

cannot be in the stability space of any module, thus

belongs to a chamber
\mathfrak{C}
. Moreover, every vector of
\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}^{o}
belongs to the same chamber, since
\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}^{o}
is connected. Hence
\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}^{o}\subset\mathfrak{C}
.

On the other hand, take a vector

in the boundary i.e.

. Therefore there is smaller-dimensional cone corresponding to a
\tau
-rigid pair
(T^{\prime},P^{\prime})
. From
42

\mods_{v_{2}}^{ss}A=(T^{\prime})^{\perp}\cap{{}^{\perp}\tau T^{\prime}}\cap(P^{\prime})^{\perp}\neq\{0\}.

In other words,

is in the stability space of some module, and thus is in a wall, hence no vectors outside of
\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)}^{o}
are in the chamber since it is connected and therefore

]
.

In this subsection, to avoid technicalities, we assume that

-vectors was introduced by Fu
[

]
and was motivated by the

of cluster algebras. Let us begin by giving the following constructive proof of
36
by Treffinger
[

]
.

Theorem 45
.

[
34
, Theorem 3.5]
Let
(T,P)
be a
\tau
-tilting pair such that
T\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}T_{i}
and
P\cong\bigoplus_{j=k+1}^{t}P_{j}
, then the set of
g
-vectors
\{g^{T_{1}},\dots,g^{T_{k}},-g^{P_{k+1}},\dots,-g^{P_{n}}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

Beweis.

Let
G_{(T,P)}\coloneqq(g^{T_{1}}|\dots|g^{T_{k}}|-g^{P_{k+1}}|\dots|-g^{P_{n}})
be the
n\times n
matrix whose columns are the
g
-vectors of the indecomposable direct summands of
(T,P)
. Set
(T,P)_{\ell}=\left(\bigoplus_{i\neq\ell}T_{i},\bigoplus_{i\neq\ell}P_{i}\right)
for
1\leq\ell\leq n
. From
40
it follows that for all
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
there exists a unique brick
B_{\ell}
which is
v
-semistable for all
v\in\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)_{\ell}}
given by

B_{\ell}\in(\bigoplus_{i\neq\ell}T_{i})^{\perp}\cap{{}^{\perp}(\bigoplus_{i\neq\ell}\tau T_{i})}\cap(\bigoplus_{i\neq l}P_{i})^{\perp}.

Treffinger showed in
[
34
, Lemma 3.3]
that the collection of these bricks
B_{\ell}
is such that

G_{(T,P)}^{T}\cdot(\bdim B_{1}|\bdim B_{2}|\dots|\bdim B_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\delta_{2}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&0\\
0&\dots&0&\delta_{n}\end{pmatrix}

where
\delta_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. In other words, the product of the
g
-matrix and the matrix of bricks is an invertible diagonal matrix in
\text{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus the
g
-vectors form a basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
∎

Remark.
The only place in the previous proof where the hypothesis of

being algebraically closed is used, is to show that
\delta_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. However, the fact that the multiplication of these two matrices is diagonal with nonzero determinant is true for every finite dimensional algebra. See for instance
[

.

the

-vectors
of

the

. The construction by Treffinger
[

]
above shows that every

for some brick
B
, which means the
c
-vectors are sign-coherent.

Let us now define a class of algebras for which we can obtain all
\tau
-tilting pairs by mutation from the
\tau
-tilting pair
(A,0)
.

Definition 47
.

An algebra is said to be
\tau
-tilting finite
(also called
g
-finite
) if the number of
\tau
-tilting pairs is finite.

There are many equivalent conditions for an algebra to be
\tau
-tilting finite, let us state the following two.

Theorem 48
.

[
15
, Theorem 1.4]

An algebra is
\tau
-tilting finite if and only if the number of bricks in
\mods A
is finite.

Theorem 49
.

[
31
, Theorem 1.1]

An algebra is
\tau
-tilting finite if and only if there exists a number
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\dim_{K}B\leq d
for every brick
B\in\mods A
.

Let us conclude by giving some more insight into
c
-vectors and their associated bricks. Asai
[
3
]
introduced semibricks, defined as follows.

Definition 50
.

[
3
, Definition 1.1]

A set
\{B_{i}:i\in I\}
is a
semibrick
if
B_{i}
is a brick for each
i\in I
and
\Hom_{A}(B_{i},B_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
.

Treffinger
[
34
]
showed that in fact the collection of positive (resp. negative)
c
-vectors form a semibrick. The following statement is in the setting of
45
.

Theorem 51
.

[
34
, Lemma 3.10]

Let
(T,P)
be a
\tau
-tilting pair and let
\{B_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\}
be the bricks associated to the almost
\tau
-rigid pairs
(T,P)_{i}
. Define

C_{(T,P)}^{+}\coloneqq\{B_{i}:c_{i}=\bdim B_{i}\},\quad C_{(T,P)}^{-}\coloneqq\{B_{i}:c_{i}=- \bdim B_{i}\}.

Then
C_{(T,P)}^{+}
and
C_{(T,P)}^{-}
are semibricks. Moreover,
\Filt(\Fac(C_{(T,P)}^{+}))=\Fac T
and
\Filt(\textnormal{Sub}(C_{(T,P)}^{-}))=T^{\perp}
.

Beweis.

Let us begin by showing that
C_{(T,P)}^{+}
is a semibrick. Take
B_{s},B_{t}\in C_{(T,P)}^{+}
such that
s\neq t
. By construction the bricks
B_{s}
and
B_{t}
are
v
-semistable for
v\in\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)_{s}}^{o}
and
v\in\mathcal{C}_{(T,P)_{t}}^{o}
, respectively, and satisfy

B_{s}\in\left(\bigoplus_{i\neq s}T_{i}\right){}^{\perp}\cap{}^{\perp}\left(\bigoplus_{i\neq s}\tau T_{i}\right),\qquad B_{t}\in\left(\bigoplus_{i\neq t}T_{i}\right){}^{\perp}\cap{}^{\perp}\left(\bigoplus_{i\neq t}\tau T_{i}\right).

Then
[
34
, Proposition 3.2]
implies that
B_{s},B_{t}\in\Fac T
. Combining this with the above, it follows that there exists an epimorphism
p_{t}:T_{t}\to B_{t}
. Thus every morphism
f\in\Hom(B_{t},B_{s})
can be composed with
p_{t}
to get a nonzero map
fp_{t}:T_{t}\to B_{s}
. But since
B_{s}\in(\bigoplus_{i\neq s}M_{i})^{\perp}
and
s\neq t
this is a contradiction. Therefore
f=0
and
\Hom(B_{t},B_{s})=0
for all
B_{t},B_{s}\in C_{(T,P)}^{+}
.

A dual argument shows that elements
B_{s},B_{t}\in C_{(T,P)}^{-}
such that
s\neq t
satisfy
B_{s},B_{t}\in\Sub(\tau T)
. In particular there is a monomorphism
\iota_{s}:B_{s}\to\tau T_{s}
Thus any morphism
f\in\Hom(B_{t},B_{s})
can be composed with
\iota_{s}
to obtain a map
\iota_{s}f:B_{t}\to\tau T_{s}
which is a contradiction and
f=0
. For the moreover part, see
[
34
, Lemma 3.13]
.
∎
