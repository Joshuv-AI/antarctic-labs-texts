Optimal parabolic upper bound for the energy-momentum relation of a strongly coupled polaron
David Mitrouskas, Krzysztof Myśliwy i and Robert Seiringer

Abstract
We consider the large polaron described by the Fröhlich Hamiltonian and study its energy-momentum relation defined as the lowest possible energy as a function of the total momentum. Using a suitable family of trial states, we derive an optimal parabolic upper bound for the energy-momentum relation in the limit of strong coupling. The upper bound consists of a momentum independent term that agrees with the predicted two-term expansion for the ground state energy of the strongly coupled polaron at rest, and a term that is quadratic in the momentum with coefficient given by the inverse of twice the classical effective mass introduced by Landau and Pekar.



1
Introduction

1.1
The Model

The large polaron provides an idealized description for the motion of a slow band electron through a polarizable crystal. The analysis of the polaron is a classic problem in solid state physics that first appeared in 1933 when Landau introduced the idea of self-trapping of an electron in a polarizable environment
[
28
]
. Since it provides a simple model for a particle interacting with a nonrelativistic quantum field, the polaron has been of interest also in field theory and mathematical physics. In particular the strong coupling theory of the polaron and Pekar’s adiabatic approximation have been the source of interesting and challenging mathematical problems.

Following H. Fröhlich
[
20
]
the Hamiltonian of the model acts on the Hilbert space

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}\,=\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\textnormal{d}x)\otimes\mathcal{F},

(1.1)

with
\mathcal{F}
the bosonic Fock space over
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and is given by

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}\,=\,-\Delta_{x}+\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{N}+\alpha^{-1}\phi(h_{x}).

(1.2)

Here
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the coordinate of the electron,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the number operator on Fock space, and the field operator
\phi(h_{x})=a^{\dagger}(h_{x})+a(h_{x})
with coupling function

\displaystyle h_{x}(y)\,=\,-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}

(1.3)

accounts for the interaction between the electron and the quantum field. The creation and annihilation operators satisfy the usual canonical commutation relations

\displaystyle\big[a(f),a^{\dagger}(g)\big]\,=\,\big\langle f|g\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},\quad\big[a(f),a(g)\big]\,=\,0.

(1.4)

Since we set
\hbar=1
and the mass of the electron equal to
1/2
, the only free parameter is the coupling constant
\alpha>0
.

By rescaling all lengths by a factor
1/\alpha
, one can show that
\alpha^{2}H_{\alpha}
is unitarily equivalent to the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{\rm{Polaron}}\,=\,-\Delta_{x}+\mathbb{N}+\sqrt{\alpha}\phi(h_{x}),

(1.5)

which is more common in the polaron literature and also explains why
\alpha\to\infty
is called the strong coupling limit.

The Fröhlich Hamiltonian defines a translation invariant model, i.e., it commutes with the total momentum operator,

\displaystyle[H_{\alpha},-i\nabla_{x}+P_{f}]\,=\,0

(1.6)

where
P_{f}=\textnormal{d}\Gamma(-i\nabla)
denotes the momentum operator of the phonons. This allows the definition of the energy-momentum relation
E_{\alpha}(P)
as the lowest possible energy of
H_{\alpha}
when restricted to states with total momentum
P\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. To this end, it is convenient to switch to the Lee–Low–Pines representation

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(P)

\displaystyle\,=\,(P_{f}-P)^{2}+\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{N}+\alpha^{-1}\phi(h_{0}),

(1.7)

where
H_{\alpha}(P)
acts on the Fock space only
[
30
]
. The Fröhlich Hamiltonian
H_{\alpha}
is unitarily equivalent to the fiber decomposition
\int^{\oplus}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}H_{\alpha}(P)\textnormal{d}P
, which follows easily from transforming
H_{\alpha}
with
e^{iP_{f}x}
and diagonalizing the obtained operator in the electron coordinate. The energy-momentum relation is then defined as the ground state energy of the fiber Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(P)

\displaystyle\,=\,\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha}(P)),

(1.8)

which by construction satisfies
E_{\alpha}(RP)=E_{\alpha}(P)
for all rotations
R\in\textnormal{SO}(3)
. It is known that
E_{\alpha}(0)\leq E_{\alpha}(P)
and hence
E_{\alpha}(0)=\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha})
(in fact it is expected that
E_{\alpha}(0)<E_{\alpha}(P)
for all
P\neq 0

[
12
]
). Further properties, such as the domain of analyticity, existence of ground states and the value of the bottom of the continuous spectrum, were analyzed in
[
21
,
51
,
42
,
23
,
10
]
.

The aim of this work is to analyze the quantitative behavior of the energy-momentum relation for large coupling
\alpha\to\infty
. Our main result provides an upper bound for
E_{\alpha}(P)
. The upper bound consists of a momentum independent part coinciding with the optimal upper bound for the ground state energy of the strongly coupled polaron at rest, and a momentum dependent part. In more detail, the momentum independent part is given by the classical Pekar energy and the corresponding quantum fluctuations that are described by the energy of a system of harmonic oscillators with frequencies determined by the Hessian of the corresponding classical field functional. This part agrees with the expected asymptotic form of
E_{\alpha}(0)
, see (
1.25
). The momentum dependent part, on the other hand, describes the energy of a free particle with mass
M(\alpha)=\frac{2\alpha^{4}}{3}\int|\nabla\varphi|^{2}
, where
\varphi
denotes the self-consistent polarization field, which coincides with the classical polaron mass introduced by Landau and Pekar
[
29
]
, see (
1.24
). As will be explained in Section
1.3
, our result confirms the heuristic picture of the polaron (the electron and the accompanying classical field) as a free quasi-particle with largely enhanced mass. To our best knowledge, the upper bound we present in this work is the first rigorous result about the connection between the energy-momentum relation
E_{\alpha}(P)
and the classical polaron mass
M(\alpha)
.

Starting from the works in the 30’s and 40’s
[
28
,
29
,
19
]
there has been a large number of publications in the physics literature that studied the ground state energy
E_{\alpha}(0)
and the effective mass, that is, the inverse curvature of
E_{\alpha}(P)
at
P=0
. For a comprehensive summary of the earlier results, we refer to
[
39
]
. More recent developments are reviewed in
[
3
]
. Mathematically rigorous results for the leading order asymptotics of
E_{\alpha}(0)
, for
\alpha
large, were obtained by Lieb and Yamazaki
[
38
]
(with non-matching upper and lower bounds) and by Donsker and Varadhan
[
11
]
as well as Lieb and Thomas
[
37
]
. The effective mass has been studied in
[
50
,
12
,
14
,
36
,
35
,
6
]
. Further improvements have been obtained for confined polarons or polaron models with more regular interaction
[
18
,
15
,
45
]
. For completeness, let us also mention recent progress in the understanding of the polaron path measure
[
44
,
5
]
as well as the increased interest in the analysis of the Schrödinger time evolution of strongly coupled polarons
[
25
,
32
,
33
,
40
,
13
,
16
,
17
]
.

1.2
Pekar functionals

The semiclassical theory of the polaron has been introduced by Pekar
[
48
]
. It arises naturally in the context of strong coupling, based on the expectation that the electron and the phonons are adiabatically decoupled, similarly as the electrons are adiabatically decoupled from the heavy nuclei in the famous Born–Oppenheimer theory
[
8
,
7
]
. With this in mind, one can minimize the Fröhlich Hamiltonian over product states of the form

\displaystyle\Psi_{u,v}\,=\,u\otimes e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha v)}\Omega

(1.9)

where
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is a normalized electron wave function,
\Omega=(1,0,0,\ldots)
the Fock space vacuum and
e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha v)}\Omega
the coherent state, up to normalization, that is associated with a classical field
\alpha v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. A simple computation leads to the Pekar energy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(u,v)\,=\,\frac{\big\langle\Psi_{u,v}|H_{\alpha}\Psi_{u,v}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}}{\big\langle\Psi_{u,v}|\Psi_{u,v}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}}\,=\,\big\langle u|(-\Delta+V^{v})u\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|v|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

(1.10)

with polarization potential

\displaystyle V^{v}(x)\,=\,-2\operatorname{Re}\big\langle v|h_{x}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,-\operatorname{Re}\int\frac{v(y)}{\pi^{2}|x-y|^{2}}\textnormal{d}y.

(1.11)

By completing the square, one can further remove the field variable and obtain the energy functional for the electron wave function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle\,=\,\inf_{v\in L^{2}}\mathcal{G}(u,v)\,=\,\int|u(x)|^{2}\textnormal{d}x-\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint\frac{|u(x)|^{2}|u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|}\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}y,

(1.12)

which is known
[
34
]
to admit a unique rotational invariant minimizer
\psi>0
(the minimizing property is unique only up to translations and multiplications by a constant phase). Alternatively, one can minimize the Pekar energy functional w.r.t. the electron wave function first. This leads to the classical field functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v)

\displaystyle\,=\,\inf_{|\hskip-1.0pt|u|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=1}\mathcal{G}(u,v)\,=\,\inf\text{spec}\,(-\Delta+V^{v})+|\hskip-1.0pt|v|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

(1.13)

whose unique rotational invariant minimizer is readily shown to be

\displaystyle\varphi(z)\,=\,-\big\langle\psi\big|h_{\cdot}(z)\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,\int\frac{|\psi(y)|^{2}}{2\pi^{2}|z-y|^{2}}\textnormal{d}y.

(1.14)

The corresponding classical ground state energy is called the Pekar energy

\displaystyle e^{\rm Pek}\,=\,\mathcal{E}(\psi)\,=\,\mathcal{F}(\varphi),\quad e^{\rm Pek}<0,

(1.15)

and by the variational principle it provides an upper bound for
\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha})
. The validity of Pekar’s ansatz was rigorously verified by Donsker and Varadhan
[
11
]
who proved that
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha})=e^{\rm Pek}
and subsequently by Lieb and Thomas
[
37
]
who added a quantitative bound for the error by showing that

\displaystyle\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha}^{\rm F})\,\geq\,e^{\rm Pek}+O(\alpha^{-1/5}).

(1.16)

Given the potential
V^{\varphi}
for the field
\varphi
, one can define the Schrödinger operator

\displaystyle h^{\rm Pek}\,=\,-\Delta+V^{\varphi}(x)-\lambda^{\rm pek},\quad\lambda^{\rm Pek}\,=\,e^{\rm Pek}-|\hskip-1.0pt|\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2}

(1.17)

with
\lambda^{\rm Pek}=\inf\sigma(-\Delta+V^{\varphi}(x))<0
and
\psi
the corresponding unique ground state. It follows from general arguments for Schrödinger operators that
h^{\rm Pek}
has a finite spectral gap above zero, and thus the reduced resolvent

\displaystyle R\,=\,Q_{\psi}(h^{\rm Pek})^{-1}Q_{\psi}\quad\text{with}\quad Q_{\psi}\,=\,1-P_{\psi},\quad P_{\psi}\,=\,|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,

(1.18)

defines a bounded operator (
P_{\psi}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the state
\psi
).

The last object to be introduced in this section is the Hessian
H^{\rm Pek}
of the energy functional
\mathcal{F}
at its minimizer
\varphi
, defined by

\displaystyle\big\langle v\big|H^{\rm Pek}v\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\big(\mathcal{F}(\varphi+\varepsilon v)-\mathcal{F}(\varphi)\big)\quad\forall v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

(1.19)

In the following lemma we collect some important properties of
H^{\rm Pek}
.

Lemma 1.1
.

The operator
H^{\rm Pek}
has integral kernel

\displaystyle H^{\rm Pek}(y,z)\,=\,\delta(y-z)-4\operatorname{Re}\big\langle\psi\big|h_{\cdot}(y)Rh_{\cdot}(z)\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

(1.20)

and satisfies the following properties.

(i)

0\leq H^{\rm Pek}\leq 1

(ii)

\textnormal{Ker}H^{\rm Pek}=\textnormal{Span}\{\partial_{i}\varphi\,:\,i=1,2,3\}

(iii)

H^{\rm Pek}\geq\tau>0
when restricted to
(\textnormal{Ker}H^{\rm Pek})^{\perp}

(iv)

{\rm Tr}_{L^{2}}(1-\sqrt{H^{\rm Pek}})<\infty
.

The proof of the lemma, in particular Item (ii), is based on the analysis of the Hessian of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}

[
31
]
. The details are given in Section
4
.

1.3
Motivation and goal of this work

In this work, we are interested in the behavior of the energy-momentum relation
E_{\alpha}(P)
for large values of the coupling
\alpha
. In general,
E_{\alpha}(P)
is expected to interpolate between two distinct regimes (see for instance
[
24
,
22
,
53
,
51
]
): The
quasi-particle regime
and the
radiative regime
. The former corresponds to small momenta, and the expectation is that the system behaves effectively like a free particle with energy

\displaystyle E^{\rm eff}_{\alpha}(P)=E_{\alpha}(0)+\frac{P^{2}}{2M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)}

(1.21)

where the effective mass is determined by the inverse curvature of
E_{\alpha}(P)
at
P=0
(which is known to be well-defined),

\displaystyle M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)\,:=\,\frac{1}{2}\lim_{P\to 0}\bigg(\frac{E_{\alpha}(P)-E_{\alpha}(0)}{P^{2}}\bigg)^{-1}.

(1.22)

It is easy to verify that
M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)\geq 1/2
(the mass of the electron in our units), and one can further show that the inequality is strict if
\alpha>0
, so that the emerging quasi-particle is heavier than the bare electron. The heuristic idea is that the electron drags along a cloud of phonons when it moves through the crystal and thus appears to be heavier than it would be without the interaction. The radiative regime, on the other hand, describes a polaron at rest and an unbound/radiative phonon carrying the total momentum
P
. It is expected to be valid for large momenta and it is characterized by a flat energy-momentum relation that equals or approaches the bottom of the continuous spectrum
[
42
]
,

\displaystyle\inf\sigma_{\rm cont}(H_{\alpha}(P))=E_{\alpha}(0)+\alpha^{-2}.

(1.23)

The two regimes cross at
|P|=P_{\rm c}(\alpha):=\sqrt{2M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)}/\alpha
which marks a characteristic momentum scale of the polaron. While the quasi-particle picture is expected to be accurate for
|P|\ll P_{\rm c}(\alpha)
, the radiative regime should hold for
|P|\gtrsim P_{\rm c}(\alpha)
(see also Remark
1.2
below). Between the two regimes there is no concrete prediction for the behavior of
E_{\alpha}(P)
. A schematic plot is provided in Figure
1.1
.

Figure 1.1:
The energy-momentum relation
E_{\alpha}(P)
is expected to have two characteristic regimes: The parabolic quasi-particle regime for small momenta (dark area) and the radiative regime for large momenta (light area). For the transition between the two there is no concrete prediction. The dashed lines denote the quasi-particle energy (
1.21
) and the bottom of the continuous spectrum (
1.23
). Their intersection defines the momentum scale
P_{c}(\alpha)
that is proportional to
\alpha
for large coupling.

One aspect of this work is to show that the quasi-particle picture is mathematically rigorous, insofar as it provides a parabolic upper bound on
E_{\alpha}(P)
that coincides with the expected form of the quasi-particle energy in the limit of large coupling. Since the quasi-particle energy (
1.21
) is determined by the values of
E_{\alpha}(0)
and
M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)
, it is instructive to recall two long-standing open conjectures concerning their behavior for
\alpha\to\infty
. As explained in the previous section, the phonon field behaves classically for large coupling, and thus it is expected that
M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)
should asymptotically tend to the expression that follows from the corresponding semiclassical counterpart of the problem. This semiclassical theory of the effective mass was introduced by Landau and Pekar in 1948
[
29
]
, and, based on this work (see also
[
50
,
14
]
), it is conjectured that

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{4}}\,=\,M^{\rm LP}\quad\text{with}\quad M^{\rm LP}\,=\,\frac{2}{3}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}.

(1.24)

Although this problem is many decades old, the best available rigorous result is that
M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)
is divergent
[
36
]
, with a recent proof that it diverges at least as fast as
\alpha^{2/5}

[
6
]
. Regarding the ground state energy
E_{\alpha}(0)
the prediction from the physics literature (see e.g.
[
4
,
41
,
52
,
26
]
) is that

E_{\alpha}(0)\,=\,e^{\rm Pek}+\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}{\rm Tr}_{L^{2}}(\sqrt{H^{\rm Pek}}-1)+O(\alpha^{-2-\delta})\quad\text{as}\quad\alpha\to\infty

(1.25)

for some
\delta>0
(in fact it is predicted that
\delta=2

[
26
]
). Compared to the semiclassical expansion this includes a subleading correction of order
\alpha^{-2}
, which we call the
Bogoliubov energy
, and which arises from quantum fluctuations of the field around its classical value. For a nice heuristic derivation of this correction, we recommend the study of
[
41
]
. Now inserting (
1.24
) and (
1.25
) into (
1.21
), and based on the expectation that the quasi-particle regime is restricted to
|P|\ll\sqrt{2M^{\rm eff}(\alpha)}/\alpha\sim\alpha
, it is clear that the Bogoliubov energy needs to be taken into account in order to see the quasi-particle energy shift given by
P^{2}/(2\alpha^{4}M^{\rm LP})\ll\alpha^{-2}
. Mathematically, the validity of (
1.25
) has been established only for confined polaron models
[
18
,
15
]
. The corresponding upper bound for the unconfined model is a corollary of our main result.

As a summary of the above we arrive at the following claim.

Conjecture
.

Let
M^{\rm LP}
be the Landau–Pekar mass defined in (
1.24
). There exists a continuous function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, satisfying
f(s)\to 1
as
s\to\infty
and

\displaystyle f(s)

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{s}{2M^{\rm LP}}+O(s^{2})\quad\text{as}\quad s\to 0,

(1.26)

such that for all
P\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}\alpha^{2}\left(E_{\alpha}(\alpha P)-e^{\rm Pek}-\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}{\rm Tr}_{L^{2}}\big(\sqrt{H^{\rm Pek}}-1\big)\right)\,=\,f(P^{2}).

(1.27)

Our main result, Theorem
2.1
below, provides an upper bound for
E_{\alpha}(\alpha P)
that is compatible with the conjecture in the quasi-particle regime. To be more precise, our result implies that the left side of (
1.27
), with the limit replaced by the
\limsup
, is bounded from above by
P^{2}/(2M^{\rm LP})
for all
P\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This shows that the corrections to the quasi-particle energy are always negative, a conclusion that is not entirely obvious a priori.

Remark 1.1
.

An immediate consequence of the conjecture would be that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lim_{P\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha^{2}\bigg(\frac{E_{\alpha}(\alpha P)-E_{\alpha}(0)}{P^{2}}\bigg)^{-1}\,=\,M^{\rm LP}

(1.28)

which is to be compared with (
1.24
) where the limits are taken in reversed order.

Remark 1.2
.

Even though our analysis is focused on the quasi-particle regime, let us mention an interesting problem concerning the radiative regime. The question is whether
E_{\alpha}(P)
enters the continuous part of the spectrum, i.e. whether the spectral gap closes at some finite momentum, or not. The answer may in fact depend on the dimension and possibly also on the value of
\alpha
. It is known that in two dimensions
E_{\alpha}(P)
remains an isolated eigenvalue for all
P
, meaning that the curve approaches
\inf\sigma_{\rm cont}(H_{\alpha}(P))
only in the limit
|P|\to\infty

[
51
]
. To our knowledge in three dimensions the question is not completely settled. While for small momenta it is known that
E_{\alpha}(P)
corresponds to a simple eigenvalue
[
51
]
, there is indication from results obtained for weak coupling that
E_{\alpha}(P)
agrees with the bottom of the continuous spectrum when
|P|
is sufficiently large
[
10
]
.

2
Main Result

We are now ready to state the main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
E_{\alpha}(P)=\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha}(P))
,
M^{\rm LP}=\frac{2}{3}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
with
\varphi
defined in (
1.14
) and choose
c>0
. For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C_{c,\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(P)\,\leq\,e^{\rm Pek}+\frac{{\rm Tr}_{L^{2}}(\sqrt{H^{\rm Pek}}-1)}{2\alpha^{2}}+\frac{P^{2}}{2\alpha^{4}M^{\rm LP}}+C_{c,\varepsilon}\,\alpha^{-\frac{5}{2}+\varepsilon}

(2.1)

for all
|P|/\alpha\leq c
and all
\alpha
large enough.

Since the operator
\sqrt{H^{\rm Pek}}-1
is trace class, non-zero and non-positive (see Lemma
1.1
), the second term on the right side is finite and lowers the energy. It corresponds to the predicted quantum corrections of the ground state energy of the Fröhlich Hamiltonian
[
4
,
41
,
52
,
26
]
. Since
E_{\alpha}(0)=\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha})
, our theorem implies a two-term upper bound for the ground state energy of the Fröhlich Hamiltonian that agrees with this prediction. For momenta in the range
\alpha^{-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\ll|P|/\alpha\leq c
, the last term in (
2.1
) is subleading for large
\alpha
when compared to the momentum dependent term. In this region the upper bound describes a quadratic dispersion relation for a free quasi-particle with mass
\alpha^{4}M^{\rm LP}
. The upper restriction on the range of
|P|
is natural, since for
|P|/\alpha\geq\sqrt{2M^{\rm LP}}
the right side of (
2.1
) would be larger than the value of the bottom of the continuous spectrum (
1.23
). The lower restriction
|P|/\alpha\gg\alpha^{-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}
, on the other hand, could in principle be improved by deriving a better error term in (
2.1
).

The derivation of a matching lower bound is, of course, more involved. To our knowledge the best known parabolic lower bound is still the one obtained by Lieb and Yamazaki
[
38
]
in 1958 stating that
E_{\alpha}(P)\geq c_{1}e^{\rm Pek}+c_{2}P^{2}/(2\alpha^{4}M^{\rm LP})
with
c_{1}\approx 3.07
and
c_{2}\approx 0.11
. Even for
P=0
it remains a challenging problem to improve the Lieb–Thomas bound (
1.15
) such that it includes the quantum corrections of order
\alpha^{-2}
. Progress in this direction has been achieved in
[
18
,
15
]
for simplified polaron models in which the electron and the quantum field are confined to suitable finite size regions.

In the next two sections we provide the definition of our trial state and formulate our main statement as a variational estimate. The remainder of the paper is devoted to the proof of the variational estimate. A sketch of the strategy of the proof is given in Section
3.2
.

2.1
Bogoliubov Hamiltonian

In this section we introduce and discuss a quadratic Hamiltonian defined on the Fock space. For its definition we set
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
to be the orthogonal projectors onto
\textnormal{Ker}H^{\rm Pek}=\text{Span}\{\partial_{i}\varphi:i=1,2,3\}
and
(\textnormal{Ker}H^{\rm Pek})^{\perp}
, that is

\displaystyle\text{Ran}(\Pi_{0})\,=\,\text{Ker}H^{\rm Pek},\quad\text{Ran}(\Pi_{1})\,=\,(\text{Ker}H^{\rm Pek})^{\perp}.

(2.2)

Even though we will not make explicit use of it, it is convenient to keep in mind that the decomposition
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,=\,\text{Ran}(\Pi_{0})\oplus\text{Ran}(\Pi_{1})

implies the factorization

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{1}\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{F}(\text{Ran}(\Pi_{0}))\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{1}=\mathcal{F}(\text{Ran}(\Pi_{1})).

(2.3)

For technical reasons, which are explained in Section
3.4.3
, we introduce the Bogoliubov Hamiltonian
\mathbb{H}_{K}
with a momentum cutoff
K\in(0,\infty]
. Setting
\mathbb{N}_{1}=\textnormal{d}\Gamma(\Pi_{1})
(the number operator on
\mathcal{F}_{1}
) we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{K}\,=\,\mathbb{N}_{1}-\big\langle\psi\big|\phi(h_{K,\cdot}^{1})R\phi(h_{K,\cdot}^{1})\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},

(2.4)

where the new coupling function

\displaystyle h_{K,x}^{1}(y)\,=\,\int\textnormal{d}z\,\Pi_{1}(y,z)h_{K,x}(z)\quad\text{with}\quad h_{K,x}(y)\,=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{|k|\leq K}\frac{e^{ik(x-y)}}{|k|}\textnormal{d}k

(2.5)

results from the coupling function
h_{x}
by removing all momenta larger than
K
and then projecting to
\text{Ran}(\Pi_{1})
. The second term in (
2.4
) defines the quadratic operator given by

\displaystyle\big\langle\psi\big|\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})R\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

2.2
Trial state and variational estimate

As starting point for the definition of our trial state consider the Fock space wave function obtained from the fiber decomposition of the classical Pekar product state
\Psi_{\psi,\varphi}
, that is

\displaystyle\Psi^{\rm Pek}_{\alpha}(P)

\displaystyle\,=\,\int\textnormal{d}x\,e^{i(P_{f}-P)x}\psi(x)e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha\varphi)}\Omega.

(2.15)

Testing the energy of
H_{\alpha}(P)
with
\Psi^{\rm Pek}_{\alpha}(P)
, one would in fact obtain that
E_{\alpha}(P)
is bounded from above by

\displaystyle e^{\rm Pek}-\frac{3}{2\alpha^{2}}+\frac{P^{2}}{\alpha^{4}M^{\rm LP}}+o(\alpha^{-2}).

(2.16)

For
E_{\alpha}(0)
this provides already a better bound compared to the semiclassical approximation for
\inf\sigma(H_{\alpha})
. The improvement comes from taking into account the translational symmetry and can be interpreted as the missing zero-point energy of three quantum oscillators (that turned into translational degrees of freedom). As a side remark, we find it somewhat surprising that fiber decompositions of this form have been employed very rarely in the polaron literature, exceptions being
[
27
]
and
[
46
]
. We think they could be of interest also for other translation-invariant polaron type models.

To obtain the desired bound for
E_{\alpha}(P)
, we need to add several modifications to the integrand in (
2.15
). On the one hand, we have to replace the classical field
\varphi
by a suitably shifted
\varphi_{P}
in order to get the correct momentum dependent term (note that (
2.16
) is missing a factor
\frac{1}{2}
in the quadratic term). The missing part of the rest energy (compare with (
2.13
)), on the other hand, is caused by two types of correlations that need to be added to the Pekar product state. First, we include correlations between the electron and the phonons. This is done in the spirit of first-order adiabatic perturbation theory. Second, we rotate the vacuum by the unitary transformation (
2.11
) that diagonalizes the Bogoliubov Hamiltonian (
2.4
). As discussed, the latter describes the quantum fluctuations of the phonons around the classical field. For technical reasons, briefly explained in Section
3.2
, we also need to introduce suitable momentum and space cutoffs in the trial state.

Explicitly, we consider the family of Fock space wave functions
\Psi_{K,\alpha}(P)\in\mathcal{F}
, depending on the coupling
\alpha
, the total momentum
P\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the cutoff
K\in(K_{0},\infty)
, given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{K,\alpha}(P)

\displaystyle\,=\,\int\textnormal{d}x\,e^{i(P_{f}-P)x}\,e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha\varphi_{P})-a(\alpha\varphi_{P})}\big(G^{0}_{K,x}-\alpha^{-1}G^{1}_{K,x}\big)

(2.17)

where

\displaystyle\varphi_{P}\,=\,\varphi+i\xi_{P}\quad\text{with}\quad\xi_{P}\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}M^{\rm LP}}(P\nabla)\varphi,\quad M^{\rm LP}=\frac{2}{3}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},

(2.18)

and

\displaystyle G^{0}_{K,x}

\displaystyle\,=\,\psi(x)\Upsilon_{K},\quad G^{1}_{K,x}=u_{\alpha}(x)(R\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})\psi)(x)\Upsilon_{K}\quad\text{and}\quad\Upsilon_{K}=\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}\Omega.

(2.19)

Here

is a radial function, satisfying

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(x)\,=\,\begin{cases}1&\forall\ |x|\leq\alpha\\
0&\forall\ |x|\geq 2\alpha\end{cases}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla u_{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\Delta u_{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}\,\leq\,C\alpha^{-1}

(2.20)

for some
C>0
.
For completeness, we recall that
\psi>0
and
\varphi
are the unique rotational invariant minimizers of the Pekar functionals (
1.12
) and (
1.13
).

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|\varphi_{P}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-|\hskip-1.0pt|\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{4}(M^{\rm LP})^{2}}|\hskip-1.0pt|(P\nabla)\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{P^{2}}{2\alpha^{4}M^{\rm LP}}

(3.22)

since
|\hskip-1.0pt|(P\nabla)\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=\frac{P^{2}}{3}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=\frac{P^{2}}{2}M^{\rm LP}
because of rotational invariance of
\varphi
. With (
3.1
) inserted into (
3.20
), the stated formula for the energy follows from (
3.16
) and (
3.18
).
∎

3.2
A short guide to the proof

3.2.1
Heuristic picture

Given Lemma
3.1
, the remaining task is to show that
(\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{G}+\mathcal{K})/\mathcal{N}
coincides, up to small errors, with the energy contribution of order
\alpha^{-2}
in (
2.23
). Although our proof is somewhat technical, the main idea is a simple one, and we explain the corresponding heuristics here in order to facilitate the reading. The main point is that the integrals appearing in the terms given in Lemma
3.1
turn out to be, as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
and
|P|/\alpha\leq c
, sharply localized around zero at the length scale of order
\alpha^{-1}
. In this regime, as formally
w_{P,y}(z)\approx y\nabla\varphi(z)
for
y
small, the Weyl operator
W(\alpha w_{P,y})
effectively acts non-trivially only on the
\mathcal{F}_{0}
part of the Fock space (at this point it is convenient to recall the factorization (
2.3
)). Moreover, we shall show that
e^{A_{P,y}}
can be effectively replaced by the identity operator and it suffices to consider
T_{y}\approx 1+y\nabla
. Since our trial state coincides with the vacuum on
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, we thus expect for
|y|
small that

\displaystyle T_{y}e^{A_{P,y}}W(\alpha w_{P,y})G^{i}_{K}\approx e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\left(1+y\nabla\right)e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha y\nabla\varphi)}G^{i}_{K},\quad i=0,1

(3.23)

with
\lambda=\frac{1}{6}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2}
. (Since
T_{y}
acts on the electron coordinate, it commutes with
e^{A_{P,y}}
and
W(\alpha w_{P,y})
). Taking this approximation for granted, and considering only the term with
i=j=0
in (
3.17
), would lead to

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\,\approx\,\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle G_{K}^{0}|T_{y}e^{A_{P,y}}W(\alpha w_{P,y})G_{K}^{0}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,=\,\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\ +\ \text{Errors}.

(3.24)

With the above replacement, and keeping only the terms of order
\alpha^{-2}
(relative to the factor from the norm), the energy terms are found to be given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\big\langle\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\ +\ \text{Errors}

(3.25a)

\displaystyle\quad+\,\frac{1}{\alpha}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\big\langle\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}|\big(\phi(h_{\cdot}+\varphi)(1+(y\nabla)a^{\dagger}(\alpha y\nabla\varphi))\big)\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

(3.25b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\,=\,-\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}}\operatorname{Re}\big\langle\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}|\phi(h_{\cdot}^{1})u_{\alpha}R\phi(h_{K,\cdot}^{1})\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\ +\ \text{Errors}

(3.25c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\big\langle\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}|\phi(h_{\cdot}^{1})Ru_{\alpha}h^{\rm Pek}u_{\alpha}R\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\ +\ \text{Errors}.

(3.25d)

From here the Bogoliubov energy is obtained by setting
u_{\alpha}=1
and
K=\infty
in the leading-order terms, and using
Rh^{\rm Pek}R=R
, since this would imply (omitting the errors)

\displaystyle\eqref{eq: heuristic E1}+\eqref{eq: heuristic G}+\eqref{eq: heuristic K}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{\infty}|(\mathbb{N}_{1}-\phi(h_{\cdot}^{1})R\phi(h^{1}_{\infty,\cdot}))\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{\infty}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\inf\sigma(\mathbb{H}_{\infty})}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}.

(3.26)

The remaining
-\frac{3}{2\alpha^{2}}
term stems from the part of the interaction involving the zero modes. In (
3.25b
), the term not involving
y\nabla
vanishes due to
\langle\psi|h_{\cdot}\psi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=-\varphi
. Moreover,
\langle\psi|h_{\cdot}\nabla\psi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\varphi
using
\nabla h_{\cdot}=-(\nabla h)_{\cdot}
via integration by parts (in the sense of distributions). Thus, since
[a^{\dagger}(y\nabla\varphi),\mathbb{U}_{\infty}^{\dagger}]=0
,

(
3.25b
)

\displaystyle\,=\,\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\big\langle\Omega|\phi(\langle\psi|h_{\cdot}y\nabla\psi\rangle)a^{\dagger}(y\nabla\varphi)\Omega\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\,=\,-\frac{1}{2}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}|\hskip-1.0pt|y\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=-\frac{3}{2\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}.

(3.27)

Equations (
3.26
) and (
3.27
) now add up to the desired energy of order
\alpha^{-2}
, see (
2.24
). Note that for estimating the error induced by replacing
e^{A_{P,y}}
by unity we require the momentum cutoff
K
in the definition of the trial state, see Lemma
3.14
.

The main issue in (
3.23
) is that while for small enough
y
one can use the first-order approximation
W(\alpha w_{P,y})\approx W(\alpha y\nabla\varphi)
, for
y
large, on the other hand, the higher-order terms in
w_{P,y}
begin to play an important part, ultimately killing the Gaussian factor. Writing

\displaystyle\big\langle G_{K}^{i}|\widetilde{H}_{\alpha}(P)e^{A_{P,y}}W(\alpha w_{P,y})T_{y}G^{j}_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

(3.28)

\displaystyle\hskip 7.11317pt=e^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}}\big\langle G_{K}^{i}|\widetilde{H}_{\alpha}(P)e^{A_{P,y}}e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha w_{P,y})}e^{-a(\alpha w_{P,y})}T_{y}G_{K}^{j}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}},\quad i,j=0,1,

we notice that, since

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2}=2\int\textnormal{d}k\,|\hat{\varphi}_{P}(k)|^{2}(1-\cos(ky))\to 2|\hskip-1.0pt|\varphi_{P}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2}\quad\text{for}\quad|y|\to\infty,

(3.29)

the prefactor should lead to a
y
-independent, exponentially small constant.
In order to make use of this exponential decay in
\alpha
, however, we need to ensure that

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle G_{K}^{i}|\widetilde{H}_{\alpha}(P)e^{A_{P,y}}e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha w_{P,y})}e^{-a(\alpha w_{P,y})}T_{y}G_{K}^{j}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\big|\leq C\alpha^{n}g(y)

(3.30)

is polynomially bounded in
\alpha
with some integrable function
g(y)
, which heuristically can be expected to be true since the average number of particles in the state
\widetilde{H}_{\alpha}(P)G_{K}^{i}
is of order one w.r.t.
\alpha
. To obtain the required integrability in
y
is also the reason for introducing the cutoff function
u_{\alpha}
in the definition of
G^{1}_{K}
.

3.2.2
Outline of the proof

Although the replacement (
3.23
) illustrates the main idea behind extracting the leading order terms, in our proof we do not directly perform this replacement and estimate the resulting error. Instead, when taking inner products, we commute the exponential operators
e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha w_{P})}
and
e^{-a(\alpha w_{P})}
in
W(\alpha w_{P,y})
to the left resp. to the right until they hit the vacuum state in
G_{K}^{i}
. This involves the Bogoliubov transformation, cf. Lemma
3.10
, and gives rise to a slight modification of
w_{P,y}
, which we denote by
\widetilde{w}_{P,y}
. These manipulations naturally lead to a multiplicative factor
\exp(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}})
which, as we shall see, indeed behaves like the Gaussian function in (
3.23
) for
|y|
small and tends to a constant exponentially small in
\alpha
as
|y|\rightarrow\infty
. In Lemma
3.4
we prove the large
\alpha
asymptotics of integrals of the type
\int g(y)\exp(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2})\textnormal{d}y
for a suitable class of functions
g
. The major part of the proof, apart from extracting the leading order terms, is to establish that the resulting error terms in the integrands are, in fact, functions in this class. This is, for the most part, achieved by use of elementary estimates combined with the commutator method by Lieb and Yamazaki
[
38
]
in the form stated in Lemma
3.8
. As already mentioned, for certain terms this makes the introduction of the space cutoff
u_{\alpha}
and the momentum cutoff
K
necessary, while for others, it is enough to use the well-known regularity properties of
\psi
, the relevant consequences of which are summarized in Lemma
3.6
.

In the next two sections, we state the remaining necessary lemmas. The main proof is then carried out in Sections
3.5
–
3.9
.

Throughout the remainder of the proof we will abbreviate constants by the letter
C
and write
C_{\tau}
whenever we want to specify that it depends on a parameter
\tau
. As usual, the value of a constant may change from one line to the next.

3.3
The Gaussian lemma

We recall that
w_{P,y}=(1-e^{-y\nabla})\varphi_{P}
and
\Theta_{K}=(H^{\rm Pek}_{K})^{1/4}
and set

\displaystyle w_{P,y}^{0}

\displaystyle\,=\,\Pi_{0}w_{P,y}\in\text{Ker}H^{\rm Pek}

(3.31a)

\displaystyle w_{P,y}^{1}

\displaystyle\,=\,\Pi_{1}w_{P,y}\in(\text{Ker}H^{\rm Pek})^{\perp}

(3.31b)

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{P,y}

\displaystyle\,=\,w_{P,y}^{0}+\Theta_{K}\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{1})+i\Theta_{K}^{-1}\operatorname{Im}(w_{P,y}^{1}).

(3.31c)

Remark 3.1
.

Note that
(y,z)\mapsto\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y})(z)
is even as a function on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, while
\operatorname{Im}(w_{P,y})(z)
is odd on the same space. Since
\Pi_{0}
and
\Theta_{K}
have real-valued kernels that are even as functions on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, they preserve the parity properties just mentioned. That
\Pi_{0}
has the desired properties follows directly from its explicit form. To see this for
\Theta_{K}
, it is enough to check this for
H_{K}^{\rm Pek}
, which can be easily done using the fact that the resolvent
R
commutes with the reflection operator, which, on the other hand, follows from the invariance of
h^{\rm Pek}
and
\Pi_{i}
under parity. Thus
(y,z)\mapsto\operatorname{Re}(w^{i}_{P,y})(z)
is even as a function on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
for
i=0,1
while the corresponding imaginary parts are odd on the same space. These facts will be of relevance below where they lead to the vanishing of several integrals.

The following lemma is proven in Section
4
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\lambda=\frac{1}{6}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
and
K_{0}>0
large enough. For every
c>0
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C\big(\alpha^{-2}y^{2}+y^{4}\big)

(3.32a)

\displaystyle\big||\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}^{0}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-2\lambda y^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C\big(\alpha^{-2}y^{2}+y^{4}+y^{6}\big)

(3.32b)

\displaystyle\big||\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-2\lambda y^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C\big(\alpha^{-2}y^{2}+y^{4}+y^{6}\big)

(3.32c)

for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
|P|/\alpha\leq c
,
K\in(K_{0},\infty]
and
\alpha>0
.

For
0\leq\delta<1
and
\eta>0
we introduce the weight function

\displaystyle n_{\delta,\eta}(y)

\displaystyle\,=\,\exp\bigg(-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}}{2}\bigg)

(3.33)

where, for ease of notation, the dependence on
\alpha
,
P
and
K
is omitted. Using the arguments laid down in Remark
3.1
, it is easy to see that
n_{\delta,\eta}(y)
is even as a function of
y
. Moreover in the limit of large
\alpha
the dominant part of the weight function when integrated against suitably decaying functions comes from the term in the exponent that is quadratic in
y
, cf. (
3.32c
). This is a crucial ingredient in our proofs and the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\eta_{0}>0
,
c>0
,
\lambda=\frac{1}{6}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
and

defined in (
3.33
). For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exist constants

such that

\displaystyle\int|y|^{n}g(y)\,\Big|n_{\delta,\eta}(y)-e^{-\eta\lambda\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}y^{2}}\Big|\textnormal{d}y\,\leq\,C_{n}\frac{|\hskip-1.0pt|g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}}{\alpha^{(4+n)(1-\delta)+\delta}}+e^{-d\alpha^{-2\delta+1}}|\hskip-1.0pt||\cdot|^{n}g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}

(3.34)

for all non-negative functions
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
,
\delta\in[0,1)
,
|P|/\alpha\leq c
and all
K,\alpha
large enough.

At first reading, one should think of
n=0
,
\delta=0
,
\eta=1
and
g
a suitable
\alpha
-independent non-negative function. In this case the integral involving the Gaussian is of order
\alpha^{-3}
whereas the term on the right hand side is of order
\alpha^{-4}
and thus contributing a subleading error. The proof of the lemma is given in Section
4
. As a direct consequence that will be useful to estimate error terms, we find

Corollary 3.5
.

Given the same assumptions as in Lemma
3.4
, for every

there exist constants

such that

\displaystyle\int|y|^{n}g(y)n_{\delta,\eta}(y)\textnormal{d}y\,\leq\,C_{n}\frac{|\hskip-1.0pt|g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}}{\alpha^{(3+n)(1-\delta)}}+e^{-d\alpha^{-2\delta+1}}|\hskip-1.0pt||\cdot|^{n}g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}

(3.35)

for all non-negative functions
g\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

,
\delta\in[0,1)
,

and all

large enough.

Proof of Corollary

.

Since

\displaystyle\int\textnormal{d}y\,|y|^{n}e^{-\eta\lambda\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}y^{2}}\,=\,(\eta\lambda\alpha^{2(1-\delta)})^{-\frac{3+n}{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,|y|^{n}e^{-y^{2}}\,=\,C_{n}\alpha^{-(3+n)(1-\delta)},

(3.36)

the statement follows immediately from Lemma
3.4
.
∎

3.4.1
Estimates involving the Pekar minimizers

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\psi>0
be the rotational invariant unique minimizer of the Pekar functional (
1.12
), and let

\displaystyle H(x)\,:=\,\big\langle\psi|T_{x}\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,(\psi\ast\psi)(x).

(3.37)

We have that
\psi
,
|\nabla\psi|
and
H
are
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3},(1+|x|^{n})\textnormal{d}x)
functions for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
and all
n\geq 0
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x
we have

\displaystyle|H(x)-1|\,\leq\,Cx^{2}.

(3.38)

Proof.

As follows from
[
34
]
,
\psi(x)
is monotone decreasing in
|x|
; moreover, it is smooth and bounded and vanishes exponentially at infinity, i.e. there exists a constant
C0
such that
\psi(x)\leq Ce^{-|x|/C}
for all
|x|
large enough (for the precise asymptotics see
[
43
]
). This clearly implies the statement for
\psi
. It further implies that all the derivatives of
\psi
are bounded. Hence, in order to show the desired result for
|\nabla\psi|
, it suffices to show that
\int\textnormal{d}x|x|^{n}|\nabla\psi(x)|
is finite for all
n\geq 0
. Since
\psi
is radial, i.e. there is a function
\psi^{\rm rad}:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that
\psi(x)=\psi^{\rm rad}(|x|)
, and monotone decreasing, we have

\displaystyle\int\textnormal{d}x\,|x|^{n}|\nabla\psi(x)|

\displaystyle\,=\,-4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\textnormal{d}\psi^{\rm rad}(r)}{\textnormal{d}r}r^{n+2}\textnormal{d}r\,=\,(n+2)\int\frac{|\psi(x)|}{|x|}|x|^{n}\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle\,\leq\,4\pi\left(R_{0}^{n+2}|\hskip-1.0pt|\psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}+\frac{n+2}{R_{0}}|\hskip-1.0pt||\cdot|^{n}\psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}\right)

(3.39)

for all
R_{0}>0
. Clearly
H
is bounded, and hence, by
|x+y|^{n}\leq 2^{1-n}\left(|x|^{n}+|y|^{n}\right)
, we can easily bound

\displaystyle\int|x|^{n}H(x)\textnormal{d}x\,\leq\,2^{2-n}|\hskip-1.0pt|\psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt||\cdot|^{n}\psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}

(3.40)

from which the statement follows also for
H
. To show (
3.38
), use the Fourier representation

\displaystyle H(x)\,=\,\int|\widehat{\psi}(k)|^{2}\cos(kx)\textnormal{d}k,

(3.41)

together with
H(x)\leq 1
,
\cos(kx)\geq 1-\frac{(kx)^{2}}{2}
and
\nabla\psi\in L^{2}
.
∎

The next lemma contains suitable bounds for the potential

and the resolvent

introduced in (

), (

) and (

).

Lemma 3.7
.

There is a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle(V^{\varphi})^{2}\,\leq\,C(1-\Delta),\quad\pm V^{\varphi}\,\leq\,\frac{1}{2}(-\Delta)+C\quad\text{and}\quad|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla R^{1/2}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}\leq C.

(3.42)

Proof.

For the proof of the first two inequalities, we refer to

(3.43)

where we made use of the second inequality in (

3.4.2
The commutator method

In the course of the proof we are frequently faced with bounding field operators like
\phi(h_{x})
. From the standard estimates for creation and annihilation operators, we would obtain

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|a(f)\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|f|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|\mathbb{N}^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}},\ |\hskip-1.0pt|a^{\dagger}(f)\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|f|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}},\ \Psi\in\mathscr{H},

(3.44)

which is not sufficient since
h_{0}(y)
is not square-integrable. With the aid of the commutator method introduced by Lieb and Yamazaki
[
38
]
one obtains suitable upper bounds by using in addition some regularity in the electron variable of the wave function
\Psi
. For our purpose, the version summarized in the following lemma will be sufficient.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
h_{K,\cdot}
for
K\in(1,\infty]
as defined in (
2.5
), let
A
denote a bounded operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(
acting on the field variable
)
and
a^{\bullet}\in\{a,a^{\dagger}\}
. Further let
X,Y
be bounded symmetric operators in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(
acting on the electron variable
)
that satisfy
D_{0}:=|\hskip-1.0pt|X|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|Y|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla X|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|Y|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|X|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla Y|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}<\infty
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|Xa^{\bullet}(Ah_{K,\cdot+y})Y\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,CD_{0}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

(3.45a)

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|Xa^{\bullet}(Ah_{\Lambda,\cdot+y}-Ah_{K,\cdot+y})Y\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\frac{CD_{0}}{\sqrt{K}}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

(3.45b)

for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\Psi\in\mathscr{H}
and
\Lambda>K>1
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that
Ah_{K,\cdot+y}=T_{y}(Ah_{K,\cdot})
and in case that
A
has an integral kernel,

\displaystyle(Ah_{K,x})(z)=\int\textnormal{d}u\,A(z,u)h_{K,x}(u).

(3.46)

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

To obtain the first inequality, write

and apply the second inequality (with

(3.47)

\displaystyle\leq\int\textnormal{d}x|\hskip-1.0pt|Ah_{1,x+y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}(Y\Psi)(x)|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}\leq|\hskip-1.0pt|A|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|h_{1,0}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|Y|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}.

To verify the second inequality, write the difference as a commutator

\displaystyle h_{\Lambda,x}(z)-h_{K,x}(z)=[-i\nabla_{x},j_{K,\Lambda,x}(z)],\quad j_{K,\Lambda,x}(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\limits_{K\leq|k|\leq\Lambda}\textnormal{d}k\frac{ke^{ik(x-z)}}{|k|^{3}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|X\nabla|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|a^{\bullet}(Aj_{K,\Lambda,\cdot+y})Y\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|X|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|a^{\bullet}(Aj_{K,\Lambda,\cdot+y})\nabla Y\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|A|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}\big(|\hskip-1.0pt|X\nabla|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|Y|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|X|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla Y|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}\big)\,|\hskip-1.0pt|j_{K,\Lambda,0}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}.

(3.49)

The desired bound now follows from

.

\displaystyle\leq C|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}e^{-A_{P,y}}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\leq C|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}.

(3.83)

To estimate the second operator, we write out the inner product, use Cauchy–Schwarz twice, apply Corollary
3.9
(with
A=1
,
X=R
and
Y=P_{\psi}
) and use (
3.32a
),

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|R_{1,y}^{2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,

\displaystyle=\,4\alpha^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|\int\textnormal{d}z\,\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{1}(z))P_{\psi}h_{K,\cdot}(z)Ru_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}e^{-A_{P,y}}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\,4\alpha^{2}\int\textnormal{d}u\,|w_{P,y}^{1}(u)|^{2}\int\textnormal{d}z\,|\hskip-1.0pt|P_{\psi}h_{K,\cdot}(z)R|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}^{2}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}e^{-A_{P,y}}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\,C\alpha^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}e^{-A_{P,y}}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\,C\alpha^{2}(y^{4}+\alpha^{-4})|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}.

(3.84)

Combining the above estimates we arrive at

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|R_{1,y}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}(1+\alpha y^{2})|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}.

(3.85)

Since
\psi(x)
decays exponentially for large
|x|
, the function
f_{\alpha}(y):=|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}P_{\psi}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt||\cdot|^{n}f_{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}\,\leq\,\int\textnormal{d}y\,|y|^{n}\bigg(\int\textnormal{d}x\,\psi(x+y)^{2}u_{\alpha}(x)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\,\leq\,C_{n}\alpha^{3+n}\ \ \text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.86)

With this at hand we can estimate the part containing the tail. Invoking Corollary
3.12

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{1}^{>}|\,\leq\,\frac{C}{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)^{1/2}\Upsilon_{K}^{>}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{\alpha}(y)(1+\alpha y^{2})\,\leq\,C_{\delta}\,\alpha^{-5}.

(3.87)

To estimate the first term in (
3.81
), we proceed similarly as in the bound for
\mathcal{N}_{02}
. We insert the identity (
3.70
), apply Cauchy–Schwarz and employ (
3.74
). This leads to

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{1}^{<}|

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\frac{2}{\alpha}\int\textnormal{d}y\,|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{\kappa\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{U}^{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}.}}_{K}(e^{-A_{P,y}}R_{1,y}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}^{<})|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{-\kappa\mathbb{N}}W(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y})\Omega|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\frac{2}{\alpha}\int\textnormal{d}y\,|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{\kappa\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{U}^{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}.}}_{K}(e^{-A_{P,y}}R_{1,y}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}^{<})|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,n_{\delta,\eta}(y).

(3.88)

In the remaining norm we use the fact that
R_{1,y}
changes the number of phonons at most by one, and thus we can apply Lemma
3.13
and (
3.85
), together with (
3.55a
), to get

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{\kappa\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{U}^{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}.}}_{K}(e^{-A_{P,y}}R_{1,y}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}^{<})|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,\leq\,\sqrt{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|R_{1,y}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,\leq\,Cf_{\alpha}(y)\big(1+\alpha y^{2}\big).

(3.89)

With Corollary
3.5
, (
3.86
) and
|\hskip-1.0pt|f_{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}\leq 1
, this leads to

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{1}^{<}|

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\frac{C}{\alpha}\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{\alpha}(y)\big(1+\alpha y^{2}\big)\,n_{\delta,\eta}(y)\,\leq\,C\alpha^{-1-3(1-\delta)}.

(3.90)

Term
\mathcal{N}_{2}
. The strategy for estimating this term is similar to the one for
\mathcal{N}_{1}
. Proceeding as described before (
3.81
), one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{2}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle R_{2,y}\psi\otimes\big(\Upsilon_{K}^{<}+\Upsilon_{K}^{>}\big)\big|W(\alpha w_{P,y})G_{K}^{0}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,=\,\mathcal{N}_{2}^{<}+\mathcal{N}_{2}^{>}

(3.91)

with
R_{2,y}=R_{2,y}^{1}+R_{2,y}^{2}
and

\displaystyle R_{2,y}^{1}

\displaystyle\,=\,P_{\psi}\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})Re^{-A_{P,y}}u_{\alpha}T_{-y}u_{\alpha}R\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})P_{\psi},

(3.92a)

\displaystyle R_{2,y}^{2}

\displaystyle\,=\,2\alpha P_{\psi}\langle h_{K,\cdot}|\operatorname{Re}(w^{1}_{P,y})\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}Re^{-A_{P,y}}u_{\alpha}T_{-y}u_{\alpha}R\phi(h^{1}_{K,\cdot})P_{\psi}.

(3.92b)

It follows in close analogy as for
R_{1,y}
in (
3.82a
)–(
3.82b
) that given any
\Psi\in\mathscr{H}
,

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|R_{2,y}\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}u_{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}(1+\alpha y^{2})|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)\Psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}},

(3.93)

and since
|\hskip-1.0pt|u_{\alpha}T_{-y}u_{\alpha}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}\leq\mathbbm{1}(|y|\leq 4\alpha)
, we can use Corollary
3.12
to estimate

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{2}^{>}|\,\leq\,\frac{C}{\alpha^{2}}|\hskip-1.0pt|(\mathbb{N}+1)\Upsilon_{K}^{>}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\mathbbm{1}(|y|\leq 4\alpha)(1+\alpha y^{2})\,\leq\,C_{\delta}\,\alpha^{-6}.

(3.94)

To bound the first term in (
3.91
) we proceed similarly as for
\mathcal{N}_{01}
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{2}^{<}|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{-2}\int\textnormal{d}y\,|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{\kappa\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{U}_{K}(R_{2,y}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}^{<})|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{-\kappa\mathbb{N}}W(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y})\Omega|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,|\hskip-1.0pt|R_{2,y}\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,n_{\delta,\eta}(y)\leq\frac{C}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\mathbbm{1}(|y|\leq 4\alpha)(1+\alpha y^{2})\,n_{\delta,\eta}(y).

(3.95)

The last integral is estimated again via Corollary
3.5
, and thus
|\mathcal{N}_{2}^{<}|\leq C\alpha^{-5+3\delta}
.

Collecting all relevant estimates and choosing
\delta>0
small enough completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

3.6
Energy contribution
\mathcal{E}

In this section we prove the following estimate for the energy contribution
\mathcal{E}
defined in (
3.8a
).

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
\mathbb{N}_{1}=\textnormal{d}\Gamma(\Pi_{1})
and choose
c>0
. For every
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0

(
we omit the dependence on
c
)
such that

\displaystyle\bigg|\,\mathcal{E}-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\bigg(\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}-\frac{3}{2}\bigg)\,\mathcal{N}\,\bigg|\,\leq\,C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{K}\alpha^{-6+\varepsilon}

(3.96)

for all
|P|/\alpha\leq c
and
K,\alpha
large enough.

Proof.

Since
G_{K}^{0}=\psi\otimes\Upsilon_{K}
,
h^{\rm Pek}\psi=0
and
\mathbb{N}\Upsilon_{K}=\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\,=\,\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle G_{K}^{0}|\big(\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{N}_{1}+\alpha^{-1}\phi(h_{\cdot}+\varphi_{P})\big)T_{y}e^{A_{P,y}}W(\alpha w_{P,y})|G_{K}^{0}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathscr{H}$}}}\,=\,\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2},

(3.97)

where both terms provide contributions to the energy of order
\alpha^{-2}
.

Term
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. Recall that
H(y)=\langle\psi|T_{y}\psi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
and use this to write

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}(e^{A_{P,y}}-1)W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,=\,\mathcal{E}_{11}+\mathcal{E}_{12}.

(3.98)

With (
3.63
), (
3.3
) and (
3.33
) it follows that

\displaystyle W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\,=\,\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}W(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y})\Omega\,=\,n_{0,1}(y)\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}\,e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha w_{P,y}^{0})}e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})}\Omega,

(3.99)

and since
e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha w_{P,y}^{0})}
commutes with
\mathbb{U}_{K}\mathbb{N}_{1}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}
and
e^{a(\alpha w_{P,y}^{0})}\Upsilon_{K}=\Upsilon_{K}
(we use
\mathbb{U}_{K}a^{\dagger}(f^{0})\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger}(f^{0})
for
f^{0}\in\text{Ran}(\Pi_{0})
), this leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{11}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)n_{0,1}(y)\big\langle\Omega|\mathbb{U}_{K}\mathbb{N}_{1}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}e^{a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})}\Omega\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}.

(3.100)

Because
\mathbb{U}_{K}\mathbb{N}_{1}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}
is quadratic in creation and annihilation operators, we can expand the exponential in the inner product and use that only the zeroth and second order terms give a non-vanishing contribution,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{11}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)n_{0,1}(y)\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\ +\,\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)n_{0,1}(y)\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})\Omega\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}=\mathcal{E}_{111}+\mathcal{E}_{112}.

(3.101)

Next we add and subtract the Gaussian to separate the leading-order term,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{111}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)\,e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)\big(n_{0,1}(y)-e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}\big)\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,=\,\mathcal{E}_{111}^{\rm lo}+\mathcal{E}_{111}^{\rm err}.

(3.102)

In
\mathcal{E}_{111}^{\rm lo}
we use
|H(y)-1|\leq Cy^{2}
and Corollary
3.12
to replace
H(y)
by unity at the cost of an error of order
\alpha^{-7}
. In the term where
H(y)
is replaced by unity, we perform the Gaussian integral and use Proposition
3.15
and again Corollary
3.12
. This leads to

\displaystyle\Big|\,\mathcal{E}_{111}^{\rm lo}-\mathcal{N}\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,\Big|\,\leq\,C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{K}\alpha^{-6+\varepsilon}.

(3.103)

The error in (
3.102
) is bounded with the help of Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{111}^{\rm err}|\,\leq\,\frac{C}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)|n_{0,1}(y)-e^{-\lambda\alpha^{2}y^{2}}|\,\leq\,C\alpha^{-6}.

(3.104)

In
\mathcal{E}_{112}
we use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, Corollary
3.12
and Lemma
3.3
, to obtain

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\mathbb{N}_{1}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})\Omega\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\big|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\,\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})a^{\dagger}(\alpha\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1})\Omega|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,\leq\,2\alpha^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,\leq\,C\alpha^{2}(y^{4}+\alpha^{-4}).

(3.105)

With
|\hskip-1.0pt||\cdot|^{n}H|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}\leq C_{n}
we can now apply Corollary
3.5
to obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{112}|\,\leq\,C\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)(y^{4}+\alpha^{-4})n_{0,1}(y)\,\leq\,C\alpha^{-7}.

(3.106)

In order to bound
\mathcal{E}_{12}
in (
3.98
), we decompose
\Upsilon_{K}=\Upsilon_{K}^{<}+\Upsilon_{K}^{>}
for some
\delta>0
, see (
3.67
), and then follows similar steps as described below (
3.69
). This way we can estimate

|\mathcal{E}_{12}|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{\kappa\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{U}_{K}(e^{-A_{P,y}}-1)\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,n_{\delta,\eta}(y)+\,\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}}|\hskip-1.0pt|\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}^{>}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y).

(3.107)

While the second term is bounded via (
3.55b
) by
C_{\delta}\,\alpha^{-12}
, in the first term we apply Lemma
3.13
and use the functional calculus for self-adjoint operators,

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|e^{\kappa\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{U}_{K}(e^{-A_{P,y}}-1)\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\sqrt{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|(e^{-A_{P,y}}-1)\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\sqrt{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|(P_{f}y+g_{P}(y))\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}.

(3.108)

Since
P_{f}
changes the number of phonons in
\mathcal{F}_{1}
at most by one, we can proceed by

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt|\hskip-1.0pt|(P_{f}y+g_{P}(y))\mathbb{N}_{1}\Upsilon_{K}^{<}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\leq(\alpha^{\delta}+1)|\hskip-1.0pt|(P_{f}y+g_{P}(y))\Upsilon_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\leq C\alpha^{\delta}(\sqrt{K}|y|+\alpha|y|^{3}),

(3.109)

where we used
1\leq\alpha^{\delta}
, Lemma
3.14
and (
3.77
) in the second step. We conclude via Corollary
3.5
that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{12}|\,\leq\,\frac{C}{\alpha^{2}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,H(y)(\sqrt{K}|y|+\alpha|y|^{3})n_{\delta,\eta}(y)+C_{\delta}\,\alpha^{-12}\,\leq\,C_{\delta}\,\sqrt{K}\alpha^{-6+4\delta}.

(3.110)

Term
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Here we start with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle\,=\,\alpha^{-1}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|L_{1,y}W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha^{-1}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|L_{1,y}(e^{A_{P,y}}-1)W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,=\,\mathcal{E}_{21}+\mathcal{E}_{22},

(3.111)

where

\displaystyle L_{1,y}\,=\,\big\langle\psi|\phi(h_{\cdot}+\varphi_{P})T_{y}\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=\phi(l_{y})+\pi(j_{y})

(3.112)

with

\displaystyle l_{y}\,=\,H(y)\varphi+\big\langle\psi|h_{\cdot}T_{y}\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},\quad j_{y}\,=\,H(y)\xi_{P},

(3.113)

and
\xi_{P}
defined in (
2.18
). We record the following properties of
l_{y}
and its derivative. The proof of the lemma is postponed until the end of the present section.

Lemma 3.17
.

For
k=0,1
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{y}\|\nabla^{k}l_{y}\|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,<\,\infty,\quad\int|y|^{n}\|\nabla^{k}l_{y}\|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,\textnormal{d}y\,<\,\infty.

(3.114)

Note that, by Lemma
3.6
,
j_{y}
clearly has these properties as well. We proceed by writing
\mathcal{E}_{21}=\mathcal{E}_{21}^{0}+\mathcal{E}_{21}^{P}
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{21}^{0}

\displaystyle\,=\,\alpha^{-1}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\phi(l_{y})W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}

(3.115a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{P}_{21}

\displaystyle\,=\,\alpha^{-1}\int\textnormal{d}y\,\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|\pi(j_{y})W(\alpha w_{P,y})\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}},

(3.115b)

and estimate the two parts separately. Using the canonical commutation relations and (
3.53c
), we evaluate

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{21}^{0}

\displaystyle\,=\,\int\big\langle\underline{l_{y}}|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}n_{0,1}(y)\textnormal{d}y

\displaystyle\,=\,\int\Big(\big\langle l_{y}^{0}|w_{P,y}^{0}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+\big\langle l_{y}^{1}|\mathrm{Re}(w_{P,y}^{1})\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+i\big\langle l_{y}^{1}|\Theta^{-2}_{K}\mathrm{Im}(w_{P,y}^{1})\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\Big)n_{0,1}(y)\textnormal{d}y

(3.116)

where we used that
l_{y}
is real-valued. Note that
l_{-y}(-z)=l_{y}(z)
. As discussed in Remark
3.1
,
n_{0,1}(y)
is even, and using the arguments therein one can conclude that
\Theta^{-2}_{K}\mathrm{Im}(w_{P,y}^{1})
and
\mathrm{Im}(w_{P,y}^{0})
are odd functions on
\mathbb{R}^{6}
since
(y,z)\mapsto\mathrm{Im}(w_{P,y})(z)
is odd on this space, and hence

\displaystyle\int\big\langle l_{y}^{0}|\mathrm{Im}(w_{P,y}^{0})\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}n_{0,1}(y)\textnormal{d}y\,=\,\int\big\langle l_{y}^{1}|\Theta^{-2}_{K}\mathrm{Im}(w_{P,y}^{1})\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}n_{0,1}(y)\textnormal{d}y\,=\,0.

(3.117)

Thus, with
\mathrm{Re}(w_{P,y})=w_{0,y}
, and with

\displaystyle v(y)\,:=\,\big\langle l_{y}|w_{0,y}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

(3.118)

we finally have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{21}^{0}\,=\,\int\big\langle l_{y}^{0}+l_{y}^{1}|\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{0})+\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{1})\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}n_{0,1}(y)\textnormal{d}y\,=\,\int v(y)n_{0,1}(y)\textnormal{d}y.

(3.119)

Note that
v\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
since
y\mapsto|\hskip-1.0pt|l_{y}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
is, while
|\hskip-1.0pt|w_{0,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
is uniformly bounded in
y
. Because of

\varphi(z)=-\langle\psi|h_{\cdot}(z)\psi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

and

\nabla_{z}h(x-z)\,=\,-\nabla_{x}h(x-z)

we have by integration by parts

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi\,=\,-2\big\langle\nabla\psi|h_{\cdot}\psi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}.

(3.120)

Thus

\displaystyle l_{y}\,=\,-\frac{1}{2}y\nabla\varphi+\varphi(H(y)-1)+\big\langle\psi|h_{\cdot}(T_{y}\psi-\psi-y\nabla\psi)\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}.

(3.121)

Since
\psi
is a smooth function with uniformly bounded derivatives, there exists a
C>0
such that for all
y

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|T_{y}\psi-\psi-y\nabla\psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}\,\leq\,Cy^{2}.

(3.122)

Moreover, for
k=0,1
and every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\displaystyle x\mapsto(h_{\cdot}(z)\nabla^{k}\psi)(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\textnormal{d}x)\quad\text{and}\quad z\mapsto|\hskip-1.0pt|h_{\cdot}(z)\nabla^{k}\psi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{1}$}}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\textnormal{d}z).

(3.123)

The first statement follows easily from Lemma
3.6
; to show the second one, use

\displaystyle\int\textnormal{d}z\,\frac{1}{|u-z|^{2}|v-z|^{2}}\,=\,\frac{1}{\pi^{3}|u-v|}

(3.124)

and apply the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality. This, together with (
3.38
), shows that there exists a function
f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\textnormal{d}z)
such that

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C\big(y^{4}+\alpha^{-4}y^{2}P^{2}\big)

(4.22)

for some constant
C>0
, and with
|P|\leq\alpha c
we obtain the stated estimated. The bound for
|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
follows from

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=|\hskip-1.0pt|\Theta_{K}\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{1})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\Theta_{K}^{-1}\operatorname{Im}(w_{P,y}^{1})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\leq C|\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}^{1}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},

(4.23)

where we used that
\Theta_{K}
is real-valued and satisfies

\displaystyle 0\,<\,\beta\,\leq\,\Theta_{K}^{2}\,\leq\,1

(4.24)

when restricted to
\text{Ran}(\Pi_{1})
; see Lemma
2.2
. To bound
|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{0}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}
we use

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{0}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{0}_{0,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\Pi_{0}(1-e^{-y\nabla})\xi_{P}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},

(4.25)

since
\varphi
,
\xi_{P}
and
\Pi_{0}
are all real-valued. Expanding
1-e^{-y\nabla}
as in (
4.21
), it is easy to conclude that
|\hskip-1.0pt|\Pi_{0}(1-e^{-y\nabla})\xi_{P}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\leq CP^{2}y^{2}\alpha^{-4}
. Using the explicit form of
\Pi_{0}
and
\langle\nabla\varphi|\varphi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=0
, we can write

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{0}_{0,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,\frac{3}{|\hskip-1.0pt|\nabla\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\big|\big\langle\nabla_{i}\varphi|e^{-y\nabla}\varphi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\big|^{2}.

(4.26)

Using the Fourier representation and rotation invariance, we have

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle\nabla_{i}\varphi|e^{-y\nabla}\varphi\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\big|\,=\,\bigg|\int p_{i}|\hat{\varphi}(p)|^{2}\sin(py)~\textnormal{d}y\bigg|.

(4.27)

By the elementary inequality
|\sin z-z|\leq Cz^{3}
, the formula
|\hskip-1.0pt|(y\nabla)\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}^{2}=2\lambda y^{2}
and the finiteness of
\|\Delta\varphi\|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},
we conclude that

\displaystyle\big||\hskip-1.0pt|w_{P,y}^{0}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-2\lambda y^{2}\big|\,\leq\,C\big(y^{4}+y^{6}+\alpha^{-4}y^{2}P^{2}\big).

(4.28)

To prove the last bound, we use

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\,=\,|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{0}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\Theta_{K}\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{1})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+|\hskip-1.0pt|\Theta_{K}^{-1}\operatorname{Im}(w_{P,y}^{1})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},

(4.29)

and hence with (
4.24
),

\displaystyle\beta|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{1}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\leq|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{0}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\beta^{-1}|\hskip-1.0pt|w^{1}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}.

(4.30)

The desired bound now follows from (
4.22
) and (
4.28
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

From Lemma
3.3
, we have

\displaystyle\big||\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-2\lambda y^{2}\big|

\displaystyle\,\leq\,C(\alpha^{-2}y^{2}+y^{4}+y^{6})\,\leq\,C\frac{y^{2}}{\alpha}\quad\text{for all}\quad\frac{|P|}{\alpha}\leq c,\ y^{2}\leq\alpha^{-1}.

(4.31)

Hence there is a constant
\mu>0
such that for all
y^{2}\leq\alpha^{-1}
the weight function (
3.33
) satisfies

\displaystyle n_{\delta,\eta}(y)

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\exp(-(\lambda\eta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}-\mu\alpha^{-2\delta+1})y^{2})

(4.32a)

\displaystyle n_{\delta,\eta}(y)

\displaystyle\,\geq\,\exp(-(\lambda\eta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}+\mu\alpha^{-2\delta+1})y^{2}).

(4.32b)

In the remainder let us abbreviate
f_{n}(y)=|y|^{n}g(y)
and
Z(y)=|n_{\delta,\eta}(y)-e^{-\lambda\eta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}y^{2}}|
. We then decompose the integral into

\displaystyle\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)Z(y)\,=\,\int_{B_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)Z(y)+\int_{B^{c}_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)Z(y)

(4.33)

with
B_{\alpha}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:y^{2}\leq\alpha^{-1}\}
. The bounds (
4.32a
) and (
4.32b
) imply that

\displaystyle|Z(y)|\leq e^{-\lambda\eta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}}\left(e^{\mu\alpha^{-2\delta+1}y^{2}}-1\right)\quad\forall y\in B_{\alpha}

(4.34)

and thus by
|e^{z}-1|\leq ze^{z}
for
z>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)Z(y)\,\leq\,\mu\alpha^{-2\delta+1}\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)y^{2}e^{-(\eta\lambda-\mu\alpha^{-1})\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}y^{2}}.

(4.35)

The last expression is further bounded by

\displaystyle\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)y^{2}e^{-(\eta\lambda-\mu\alpha^{-1})\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}y^{2}}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,|y|^{n+2}e^{-(\eta\lambda-\mu\alpha^{-1})\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}y^{2}}

(4.36)

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{C_{n}|\hskip-1.0pt|g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}}{\alpha^{(5+n)(1-\delta)}}\left(\eta\lambda-\mu\alpha^{-1}\right)^{-(n+5)/2}

and since the resulting expression is uniformly bounded in
\eta\geq\eta_{0}
and
\alpha
large, we get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)Z(y)\,\leq\,C_{n}\frac{|\hskip-1.0pt|g|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$L^{\infty}$}}}}{\alpha^{(4+n)(1-\delta)+\delta}}.

(4.37)

To bound the second term in (
4.33
), we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{B^{c}_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)Z(y)\,\leq\,\int_{B^{c}_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)n_{\delta,\eta}(y)+e^{-\lambda\eta\alpha^{-2\delta+1}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y).

(4.38)

To see that the first summand is exponentially small as well, we use (
4.29
), (
4.24
) and
\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{i})=\Pi_{i}\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y})=\Pi_{i}\operatorname{Re}(w_{0,y})
for
i=0,1
,

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|\widetilde{w}_{P,y}|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}

\displaystyle\geq|\hskip-1.0pt|\operatorname{Re}(w_{P,y}^{0})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}+\beta|\hskip-1.0pt|\operatorname{Re}(w^{1}_{P,y})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}\geq\beta|\hskip-1.0pt|\mathrm{Re}(w_{0,y})|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=\beta|\hskip-1.0pt|(1-e^{-y\nabla})\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}},

(4.39)

and hence

\displaystyle n_{\delta,\eta}(y)\,\leq\,\exp\Big(-\eta\beta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}q(y)\Big)\quad\text{with}\quad q(y)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|(1-e^{-y\nabla})\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}.

(4.40)

Since
\varphi
is real-valued, we have
\langle\varphi|e^{-y\nabla}|\varphi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=\langle\varphi|e^{y\nabla}|\varphi\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}=(\varphi\ast\varphi)(y)
and thus

\displaystyle q(y)=|\hskip-1.0pt|\varphi|\hskip-1.0pt|^{2}_{\text{\tiny{$L^{2}$}}}-(\varphi\ast\varphi)(y).

(4.41)

Recall that, as shown in
[
34
]
, the electronic Pekar minimizer
\psi
is radial and non-increasing and hence
\varphi
, cf. (
1.14
), is radial and non-increasing as well, as convolutions of radial non-increasing functions are themselves radial non-increasing functions. Consequently,
q(y)
is radial and monotone non-decreasing, and thus
q(y)\geq q(y^{\prime})
for all
y\in B_{\alpha}^{c}
,
y^{\prime}\in B_{\alpha}
. On the other hand, by a simple computation, using the regularity of
\varphi
, one finds that
q(y)\geq C_{0}y^{2}
for some
C_{0}>0
and all
|y|
small enough, and thus
q(y)\geq C_{0}\alpha^{-1}
for all
y\in B_{\alpha}^{c}
and
\alpha
large. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{B^{c}_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)n_{\delta,\eta}(y)

\displaystyle\,\leq\,\int_{B^{c}_{\alpha}}\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)e^{-\eta\beta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)}q(y)}

\displaystyle\,\leq\,e^{-C_{0}\eta\beta\alpha^{2(1-\delta)-1}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)\,\leq\,e^{-d\alpha^{-2\delta+1}}\int\textnormal{d}y\,f_{n}(y)

(4.42)

for some
d>0
, which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.14
.

Let
p=-i\nabla
. By a straightforward computation using the transformation property (
2.11
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{U}_{K}P_{f}\mathbb{U}_{K}^{\dagger}\Omega=\sum_{n}a^{\dagger}(A_{K}u_{n})a^{\dagger}(B_{K}p\overline{u_{n}})\Omega+\textnormal{Tr}_{L^{2}}(B_{K}pB_{K})\Omega

(4.43)

for some orthonormal basis
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. That
B_{K}pB_{K}
is trace-class can be seen via

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{L^{2}}{|B_{K}pB_{K}|}\,\leq\,|\hskip-1.0pt|B_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm HS$}}}\,|\hskip-1.0pt|pB_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm HS$}}}\,\leq\,CK,

(4.44)

where the second step follows from Lemma
2.2
, implying
|\hskip-1.0pt|B_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm HS$}}}\leq C
, and

\displaystyle|\hskip-1.0pt|pB_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm HS$}}}^{2}\,=\,\textnormal{Tr}_{L^{2}}(pB_{K}B_{K}p)\,\leq\,\textnormal{Tr}_{L^{2}}(p(1-H_{K}^{\rm Pek})p)\,\leq\,CK.

(4.45)

By rotation invariance
\textnormal{Tr}_{L^{2}}(B_{K}pB_{K})=0
. The first term in (
4.43
), on the other hand, is seen to be a two-particle wave function
\Phi_{K}
given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{K}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(A_{K}pB_{K}+B_{K}pA_{K}\right)(x,y).

(4.46)

Thus

\displaystyle\big\langle\Upsilon_{K}|(P_{f})^{2}\Upsilon_{K}\big\rangle_{\text{\tiny{$\mathcal{F}$}}}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{2}\|A_{K}pB_{K}+B_{K}pA_{K}\|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm HS$}}}^{2}\,\leq\,2|\hskip-1.0pt|A_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm op$}}}^{2}|\hskip-1.0pt|pB_{K}|\hskip-1.0pt|_{\text{\tiny{$\rm HS$}}}^{2}\,\leq\,CK,

(4.47)

where we invoked again (
4.45
).
∎
