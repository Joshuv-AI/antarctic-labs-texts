A Class of Homeomorphisms on Homogeneous Spaces of a Group Action
Samuel A. Hokamp

Abstract
. We develop a class of homeomorphisms on a compact homogeneous space of a transitive group action and show how the class sheds new light on a decomposition problem. We further use this class to show that every such homogeneous space in a locally convex topological vector space which is also convex must necessarily be trivial, ie. a singleton set. Additionally, this class of homeomorphisms allows us to relate the induced group action on the space of continuous functions to the action on the homogeneous space.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin by presenting some standard definitions concerning group actions. Let
X
be a set and
G
be a group that acts on
X
. That is, there exists a map

\Phi:G\times X\to X

with the following properties:

(1)

\Phi(e,x)=x
for all
x\in X
, where
e\in G
is the identity element, and

(2)

\Phi(\alpha,\Phi(\beta,x))=\Phi(\alpha\beta,x)
for all
x\in X
and
\alpha,\beta\in G
.

When we wish to be explicit, the map
\varphi_{\alpha}:X\to X
shall denote the action of the element
\alpha\in G
on
X
; that is,
\varphi_{\alpha}(x)=\Phi(\alpha,x)
for
x\in X
. When there is no confusion, we shall write
\varphi_{\alpha}(x)
simply as
\alpha x
.

Below are some basic properties of group actions found in a variety of classical texts.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
X
.

•

The action is
faithful
if
\alpha x=x
for all
x\in X
implies that
\alpha=e
.

•

The action is
free
if
\alpha x=x
for some
x\in X
implies that
\alpha=e
.

•

The action is
transitive
if for each
x,y\in X
, there exists
\alpha\in G
such that
y=\alpha x
.

In proceeding,
G
shall be a compact group with Haar measure
m
. We say that
G
acts
continuously
on a topological space
X
if the map
\Phi
is continuous with respect to the product topology. When
G
acts continuously and transitively on a compact Hausdorff space
X
, each
\varphi_{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism of
X
, and we say
X
is a
homogeneous space
. So

shall be throughout the paper.

Let
C(X)
denote the space of continuous complex functions with domain
X
and uniform norm
||\cdot||
. The action of
G
on
X
induces an action of
G
on
C(X)
via the map

(\alpha,f)\mapsto f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}.

Note that this map need not be continuous, but it can be shown to be
separately continuous
; that is, each map induced by fixing a component element is continuous: for
\alpha\in G
, the map
f\mapsto f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}
is a bijective linear isometry on

, and for

, the continuity of the map

is a consequence of Lemma 5.1 of
[
4
]
.

Throughout the paper,
\mu
shall denote the unique regular Borel probability measure on

that is invariant under the action of
G
. Specifically,

(2.1)

\int_{X}f\,d\mu=\int_{X}f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\,d\mu,

for all
f\in C(X)
and
\alpha\in G
. The existence of such a measure is a result of André Weil from
[
5
]
. A construction of

can be found in
[
6
]
(Theorem 6.2), but existence can also be established non-constructively using the Riesz Representation Theorem (for reference, Theorem 6.19
[
7
]
). Since this technique will be useful to us in Section
3
, we provide the result and its proof as Theorem
6.1
in the appendix (Section
6
).

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the notation

shall denote the usual Lebesgue spaces of measurable functions on

.

To discuss the proof from
[
4
]
in Section

, we need the following definitions:

Definition 2.2
.

A space of complex functions

defined on

is

(

-invariant

f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\in Y
for every

.

Remark 2.3
.

Since the action of

is

is

Explicitly, the invariance property (

Definition 2.5

A space

Finally, all classical results used in the paper can be found in a variety of texts, with the reference given being only one option.

3.
A Class of Homeomorphisms on Homogeneous Spaces

In this section we establish the titular class of homeomorphisms on
X
. Each homeomorphism
\phi_{x}
of this class is induced by an element of
x\in X
and is constructed as follows. Fix
x\in X
. We define the set

S(x)=\{\alpha\in G:\alpha x=x\}.

This is the set of elements in
G
that stabilize
x
, which is a closed subgroup of
G
called the
isotopy subgroup
for
x
. A theorem of Weil states that
X
is homeomorphic to
G/S(x)
with the quotient topology (for reference, Theorem 6.1 of
[
6
]
), the homeomorphism
\phi
given by

\phi(\alpha x)=\alpha S(x).

Note the importance of transitivity here. Further, the map
\iota:G/S(x)\to G/S(x)
given by

\iota(\alpha S(x))=\alpha^{-1}S(x)

is a homeomorphism on
G/S(x)
in the quotient topology
1
1

1

In fact, using the strong continuity of the quotient map, one can show that if
G
is a topological group and
H
a subgroup, then for any homeomorphism
f
of
G
, the map

xH\mapsto f(x)H

is a homeomorphism of
G/H
.
. We compose the homeomorphisms
\phi
,
\iota
, and
\phi^{-1}
together to get the homeomorphism
\phi_{x}
:

\phi_{x}=\phi^{-1}\circ\iota\circ\phi.

In particular, for all
\alpha\in G
,

\phi_{x}(\alpha x)=\alpha^{-1}x,

so that
\phi_{x}(y)=\beta^{-1}x
, where
y\in X
is such that
y=\beta x
.

Theorem
3.1
now establishes some basic properties of the maps
\phi_{x}
:

Theorem 3.1
.

For each
x\in X
, let
\phi_{x}
denote the corresponding homeomorphism defined previously. Then,

(1)

\phi_{x}(x)=x
.

(2)

\phi_{x}^{-1}=\phi_{x}
.

(3)

For all
\alpha\in G
,
\phi_{\alpha x}=\varphi_{\alpha}\circ\phi_{x}\circ\varphi_{\alpha^{-1}}

(4)

For all
\alpha\in G
,
\phi_{\alpha x}=\phi_{x}\circ\phi_{\alpha^{-1}x}\circ\phi_{x}
.

Proof.

To prove (1), let
\alpha\in G
be any element such that
\alpha x=x
. The identity element will work fine. Observe then

\phi_{x}(x)=\phi_{x}(\alpha x)=\alpha^{-1}x=x,

since
\alpha^{-1}x=x
if and only if
\alpha x=x
. To show (2), let
y\in X
and
\beta\in G
such that
y=\beta x
. Then

\phi_{x}(\phi_{x}(y))=\phi_{x}(\phi_{x}(\beta x))=\phi_{x}(\beta^{-1}x)=\beta x=y.

To show (3), fix
\alpha\in G
, and let
y\in X
and
\beta\in G
such that
y=\beta x
. Observe then

\alpha\phi_{x}(\alpha^{-1}y)=\alpha\phi_{x}(\alpha^{-1}\beta x)=\alpha\beta^{-1}\alpha x=\phi_{\alpha x}(\beta\alpha^{-1}\alpha x)=\phi_{\alpha x}(\beta x)=\phi_{\alpha x}(y).

To show (4), let
\alpha
,
y
, and
\beta
be as defined above. Then

\phi_{x}(\phi_{\alpha^{-1}x}(\phi_{x}(y)))=\phi_{x}(\phi_{\alpha^{-1}x}(\phi_{x}(\beta x)))=\phi_{x}(\phi_{\alpha^{-1}x}(\beta^{-1}x))

=\phi_{x}(\phi_{\alpha^{-1}x}(\beta^{-1}\alpha\alpha^{-1}x))=\phi_{x}(\alpha^{-1}\beta\alpha^{-1}x)=\alpha\beta^{-1}\alpha x=\phi_{\alpha x}(y),

by jumping into the middle of the equality proving (3).
∎

A very useful result of the homeomorphisms
\phi_{x}
is that each one induces a bijective linear isometry
U_{x}
on each space
C(X)
or
L^{p}(\mu)
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, via
U_{x}f=f\circ\phi_{x}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be any of the spaces
C(X)
or
L^{p}(\mu)
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and
x\in X
. The map
U_{x}:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{X}
given by
U_{x}f=f\circ\phi_{x}
is a bijective linear iosmetry.

Proof.

The bijectivity of each
U_{x}
is clear, since each is its own inverse by Theorem
3.1
(2), and linearity is clear as well. To show that
U_{x}
is an isometry on
C(X)
, observe that

|(U_{x}f)(y)|=|f(\phi_{x}(y))|\leq||f||\hskip 18.06749pt\text{and}\hskip 18.06749pt|f(y)|=|(U_{x}f)(\phi_{x}(y))|\leq||U_{x}f||

for all
y\in X
and
f\in C(X)
. In particular,
||U_{x}f||=||f||
.

To show that
U_{x}
is an isometry on each
L^{p}(\mu)
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we show that

(3.1)

\int_{X}U_{x}f\,d\mu=\int_{X}f\,d\mu

holds for all
f\in C(X)
. From the proof of Theorem
6.1
in the appendix, we have

\int_{X}U_{x}f\,d\mu=\int_{G}(U_{x}f)(\gamma x)\,dm(\gamma)=\int_{G}f(\phi_{x}(\gamma x))\,dm(\gamma)=\int_{G}f(\gamma^{-1}x)\,dm(\gamma),

for
f\in C(X)
, where
m
denotes the Haar measure on
G
. However, the Haar measure on
G
is invariant under inversion in
G
, and thus

\int_{G}f(\gamma^{-1}x)\,dm(\gamma)=\int_{G}f(\gamma x)\,dm(\gamma)=\int_{X}f\,d\mu.

This verifies property (
3.1
). We then apply property (
3.1
) alongside Lusin’s Theorem and the Dominated Convergence Theorem (for reference, Theorem 2.24 and Theorem 1.34, respectively, of
[
7
]
) to get that
\mu(\phi_{x}(E))=\mu(E)
for all Borel sets
E\subset X
. Consequently,

\int_{X}U_{x}f\,d\mu=\int_{X}f\,d\mu

holds for every
f\in L^{p}(\mu)
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
. That
U_{x}
is an isometry follows directly.
∎

The following theorem establishes some basic properties of the operators
U_{x}
, the third of which is the key piece in the results that follow.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
\mathscr{X}
is any of the spaces
C(X)
or
L^{p}(\mu)
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
x\in X
, and
\alpha\in G
. For all
f\in\mathscr{X}
, we have that

(1)

\displaystyle(U_{\alpha x}f)\circ\varphi_{\alpha}=U_{x}(f\circ\varphi_{\alpha})
,

(2)

\displaystyle U_{\alpha x}f=U_{x}U_{\alpha^{-1}x}U_{x}f
, and

(3)

(U_{x}f)\circ\varphi_{\alpha}=U_{x}f
when
\alpha x=x
.

Proof.

Equalities (1) and (2) follow from (3) and (4) of Theorem
3.1
, respectively:

(U_{\alpha x}f)\circ\varphi_{\alpha}=f\circ\phi_{\alpha x}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}=f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\circ\phi_{x}=U_{x}(f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}).

U_{\alpha x}f=f\circ\phi_{\alpha x}=f\circ\phi_{x}\circ\phi_{\alpha^{-1}x}\circ\phi_{x}=U_{x}U_{\alpha^{-1}x}U_{x}f.

To show (3), we note that since
\alpha x=x
, we have
\alpha^{-1}x=x
. Let
y\in X
and
\beta\in G
such that
y=\beta x
. We then have that

[(U_{x}f)\circ\varphi_{\alpha}](y)=f(\phi_{x}(\alpha\beta x))=f(\beta^{-1}\alpha^{-1}x)=f(\beta^{-1}x)=f(\phi_{x}(\beta x))=(U_{x}f)(y).

Since this can be done for any
y\in X
, the proof is complete.
∎

Replacing
f
with
U_{x}f
in Theorem
3.3
(3) yields Theorem
1.2
. A more particular statement of this result is given below as Corollary
3.4
.

Corollary 3.4
.

If
\alpha\in G
and
x\in X
such that
\alpha x=x
, then
f=f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}
for all
f\in C(X)
.

Theorem
1.3
, relating the actions of
G
on
X
and
G
on
C(X)
, immediately follows.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose that the action of
G
on
C(X)
is faithful, and let
\alpha\in G
and
x\in X
such that
\alpha x=x
. To show that the action of
G
on
X
is free, we must prove that
\alpha
is the identity element of
G
.

Since
\alpha
stabilizes some element of
X
, we must have that
f=f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}
for all
f\in C(X)
by Corollary
3.4
. Since the action of
G
on
C(X)
is faithful, we get that
\alpha
must be the identity element of
G
, as desired.
∎

4.
Discussing a Decomposition Problem

In this section, we revisit Conjecture
1.1
as studied in
[
4
]
and discuss the additional condition imposed on
X
that allowed for the main result of that paper. To begin, we show how the Peter-Weyl Theorem from
[
3
]
is used to establish the existence of a collection
\mathscr{G}
of closed
G
-minimal spaces of continuous functions over
X
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a compact group acting continuously and transitively on a compact Hausdorff space
X
. Then there exists a collection
\mathscr{G}
of closed, pairwise orthogonal,
G
-minimal subspaces of
C(X)
whose direct sum is
L^{2}(\mu)
.

The statement of the key part of the Peter-Weyl Theorem used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(given below as Theorem
4.2
) is taken from
[
8
]
. One can also find this result as Theorem 5.2 from
[
9
]
.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
8
]
Theorem 1.12(e))
.

Let
\Phi
be a unitary representation of
G
on a Hilbert space
V
. Then
V
is the orthogonal direct sum of finite-dimensional irreducible invariant subspaces.

Further treatment of unitary representations can be found in
[
9
]
. Note that a subspace
M\subset V
is
invariant
for
\Phi
if
\Phi(\alpha)M\subset M
for all
\alpha\in G
. Further, a subspace of
V
is
irreducible
if it contains no non-trivial invariant subspaces.

Lemma 5.3 of
[
4
]
, given below as Lemma
4.3
, is also used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
4
]
Lemma 5.3)
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be any of the spaces
C(X)
or
L^{p}(\mu)
, for
1\leq p<\infty
. If
Y
is a closed
G
-invariant space in
\mathscr{X}
, then
Y\cap C(X)
is dense in
Y
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

One easily checks that the map
\Phi
given by

\Phi:G\to\mathscr{U}(L^{2}(\mu))\hskip 9.03374pt\text{such that}\hskip 9.03374pt\Phi(\alpha)f=f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\hskip 9.03374pt\text{for all }f\in L^{2}(\mu),

is a unitary representation of
G
, where
\mathscr{U}(L^{2}(\mu))
denotes the group of unitary operators on
L^{2}(\mu)
. Thus, by the part of the Peter-Weyl Theorem given above as Theorem
4.2
,
L^{2}(\mu)
decomposes into the orthogonal direct sum of finite-dimensional irreducible invariant subspaces. To translate into our terminology, an invariant subspace of the representation is equivalent to the subspace being
G
-invariant, and a subspace being irreducible is equivalent to it being
G
-minimal.

Let
\mathscr{G}
denote this collection of pairwise orthogonal,
G
-minimal spaces. Since each element of
\mathscr{G}
is finite-dimensional, each is closed. Finally, Lemma
4.3
yields that each element of
\mathscr{G}
is in fact a space of continuous functions. Note the role that the finite-dimensionality of the elements of
\mathscr{G}
plays here. ∎

In
[
4
]
, the collection
\mathscr{G}
was affixed with the additional property that

(4.1)

\dim(H\cap H(x))=1

for each
x\in X
and each
H\in\mathscr{G}
, where

H(x)=\{f\in C(X):f=f\circ\varphi_{\alpha},\text{ for all }\alpha\in G\text{ such that }\alpha x=x\}.

That is,
H(x)
is the space of continuous functions on
X
that are stabilized in
C(X)
by every stabilizer of
x
from
G
. However, Corollary
3.4
immediately yields the following:

Theorem 4.4
.

For each
x\in X
, we have that
H(x)=C(X)
.

Thus, property (
4.1
) amounts to requiring each space

to have dimension 1:

1=\dim(H\cap H(x))=\dim(H\cap C(X))=\dim H.

This property occurs in some important cases, such as when
G
is abelian (
[
9
]
, Corollary 3.6), but is not the case in general, as we see in the work of Nagel and Rudin in
[
1
]
. Further, the work of Nagel and Rudin shows that this condition is not strictly necessary to achieve the decomposition of closed
G
-invariant spaces into
G
-minimal spaces. Currently, Conjecture
1.1
in its full generality remains unproven.

5.
Convex Homogeneous Spaces of a Group Action

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.4
, the proof of which uses Corollary
3.4
alongside the Schauder-Tychonoff Theorem (for reference, Theorem 5.28 of
[
10
]
) and Urysohn’s Lemma (for reference, Theorem 33.1 of
[
11
]
). Note that Urysohn’s Lemma applies because
X
, as a compact Hausdorff space, is a normal space (Theorem 32.3 of
[
11
]
).

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

To begin, let
G
be the compact group which acts continuously and transitively on
X
to make it a homogeneous space. We note that since
X
is a convex, compact subset of a locally convex topological vector space, every homeomorphism of
X
has a fixed point, by the Schauder-Tychonoff Theorem. In particular, for every
\alpha\in G
, the map
\varphi_{\alpha}
has a fixed point; that is, for each
\alpha\in G
there exists
x\in X
such that
\alpha x=x
. Applying Corollary
3.4
, for all
f\in C(X)
we have that
f=f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}
.

However, due to the transitivity of the group action, we get that each function
f\in C(X)
must be constant: Let
x,y\in X
and
\alpha\in G
such that
y=\alpha x
. Then

f(y)=f(\alpha x)=(f\circ\varphi_{\alpha})(x)=f(x).

This can be done for each pair of points in
X
, so
f
must be constant.

Finally, if
X
were to contain more than one point, for each pair of distinct points in
X
there would exist a continuous function separating them, according to Urysohn’s Lemma. However, as we just established, all continuous functions on
X
are constant, meaning
X
cannot contain any pairs of distinct points. Thus,
X
must be a singleton set.
∎

6.
Appendix: Existence of a
G
-Invariant Measure on
X

In this section we present a non-constructive proof of the following result of André Weil from
[
5
]
. Recall
m
denotes the Haar measure of
G
.

Theorem 6.1
.

There exists a unique regular Borel probability measure
\mu
on
X
that is invariant under
G
in the sense that

(6.1)

\int_{X}f\,d\mu=\int_{X}f\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\,d\mu,

for every
f\in C(X)
and every
\alpha\in G
.

Proof.

We define the map
\Lambda
on
C(X)
by

\Lambda f=\int_{G}f(\gamma x)\,dm(\gamma),

for some
x\in X
. The transitivity of the action and the invariance of the Haar measure yield that
\Lambda
is well-defined: If
y\in X
, then there exists
\beta\in G
such that
\beta x=y
. Thus,

\int_{G}f(\gamma y)\,dm(\gamma)=\int_{G}f(\gamma\beta x)\,dm(\gamma)=\int_{G}f(\gamma x)\,dm(\gamma).

Clearly,
\Lambda
is a linear functional on
C(X)
. Further, it is bounded:

|\Lambda f|\leq\int_{G}|f(\gamma x)|\,dm(\gamma)\leq||f||.

Thus, by the Riesz Representation Theorem (Theorem 6.19
[
7
]
), there exists a unique regular Borel measure
\mu
on
X
such that

\int_{X}f\,d\mu=\Lambda f

for all
