Improved Performance of Stochastic Gradients with Gaussian Smoothing
A. Starnes, C. Webster

Abstract
This paper formalizes and analyzes Gaussian smoothing applied to two prominent optimization methods: Stochastic Gradient Descent (GSmoothSGD) and Adam (GSmoothAdam) in deep learning. By attenuating small fluctuations, Gaussian smoothing lowers the risk of gradient-based algorithms converging to poor local minima. These methods simplify the loss landscape while boosting robustness to noise and improving generalization, helping base algorithms converge more effectively to global minima. Existing approaches often rely on zero-order approximations, which increase training time due to inefficiencies in automatic differentiation. To address this, we derive Gaussian-smoothed loss functions for feedforward and convolutional networks, improving computational efficiency. Numerical experiments demonstrate the enhanced performance of our smoothing algorithms over unsmoothed counterparts, confirming the theoretical benefits.

Introduction

Optimization problems in machine learning often involve minimizing the expectation or sum of stochastic functions dependent on underlying observations. In many cases, the total loss is computed as the average loss over individual data samples. However, a significant challenge arises when optimal solutions for specific samples fail to generalize to the broader dataset or other observations. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is a widely used method to address these issues by computing gradients from random samples of the data. Despite its advantages, like gradient descent (GD), SGD can become trapped in suboptimal local minima. The methods proposed in this paper aim to overcome this limitation by introducing GSmooth techniques that enhance the performance of stochastic gradient-based algorithms.

In general, given

Our goal is to apply smooth stochastic gradient techniques to deep learning problems, specifically by training neural networks using Gaussian smoothing. To achieve this, we need to compute the expectation in (
1.9
), typically approximated via numerical integration, as direct computation is intractable due to high dimensionality. However, this approach is computationally expensive. Inspired by
[
16
]
, we instead derive the analytical form of the neural network after Gaussian smoothing. With this closed form, the smoothed network can be coded and trained as efficiently as its unsmoothed counterpart. Moreover, having the exact smoothed gradient, rather than an approximation, aligns with our theoretical results.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

Formalize GSmoothSGD and prove convergence results for
L
-smooth functions and arbitrary smoothing parameter sequences (Section
3
);

•

Introduce GSmoothAdam and prove almost sure convergence of gradients to a stationary point for L-smooth functions (Section
4
);

•

Derive the mathematical formulation of Gaussian-smoothed feedforward (FFNN) and convolutional (CNN) neural networks and implement the new architecture in Python (Section
5
); and

•

Provide numerical evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of smoothing in stochastic gradient settings (Section
6
).

Related Works

The use of Gaussian smoothing has roots in gradient-free optimization, homotopy continuation, and partial differential equations.
Gaussian smoothing has been applied to the non-stochastic (deterministic) gradient setting.
For an overview of smoothing gradient descent, see, e.g.,
[
22
]
and the references therein.

While not the focus of this paper, Gaussian smoothing is popular in gradient-free optimization.
One of the seminal papers,
[
21
]
, proposes Gaussian smoothing in order to construct a zero-order SGD-type method to optimize, since (
1.9
) can clearly be approximated with numerical integration techniques.
The work
[
21
]
also provides several of the foundational results that we utilize in our work, see, e.g., Lemma
2.1 (a)
, and the discussion that follows, with a focus on using the gradient of the smoothed function as a surrogate for the original gradient with a fixed (small) smoothing parameter value.
A zero-order Adam method is proposed in
[
1
]
, where they also show that this method converges.
For a good overview of zero-order methods including those related to Gaussian smoothing, see
[
13
]
.

From the perspective of exploiting partial differential equations (PDEs) to assist with optimization, a common approach is to use a PDE where the initial condition is the objective function and, as time increases, the PDE transforms the objective function into an improved version of itself.
An alternate definition for
g_{\sigma}
is to define a convolution between
g
and a Gaussian kernel.
Functions of this form are solutions to the heat equation with initial condition given by
g
(see Section 2.3 of
[
4
]
).

The PDE version of smoothing is a particular case of homotopy continuation, where the homotopy is given by the solution to the PDE and is often at least differentiable.
The standard optimization by homotopy continuation algorithm (OGHC) finds the minimizers of the homotopy at
t
, starting at
t=1
and iteratively reducing
t
to 0. The homotopy is chosen so that optimizing the homotopy at
t=1
is very easy.
Our modification of SGD is a generalized form of OGHC, now allowing for
\sigma
to change at will rather than when close enough to the inner loops minima.
The majority of the theoretical results for OGHC come from the works
[
20
,
19
,
15
,
18
,
17
]
.
Aside from the theory, the effort
[
16
]
trains recurrent neural networks using OGHC.
In the abstract,
[
16
]
mentions that this approach is “applicable to FFNNs”.
This work is what primarily motivated us to explicitly smooth FFNNs as well as CNNs.

To the best of our knowledge, no other works have attempted to combine Gaussian smoothing with Adam.
On the other hand, a number of other papers have modified OGHC as well and proven convergence results; we discuss the three most related which can be viewed as particular examples of GSmoothSGD.
For perspective, our convergence results for GSmoothSGD only assume that
f
is
L
-smooth (the proof can be modified to show similar results if
f
is just Lipschitz as was done in
[
22
]
).
The work
[
5
]
focuses on the noisy problem where evaluations of the function or its gradient has noise, i.e.,
f(\bm{x})+\xi
or
\nabla f(\bm{x})+\xi
with
\xi

The authors’ modification of OGHC uses
\sigma_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t-1}
, which can be viewed as using GSmoothSGD where
\sigma_{t}
repeats as many times as they run SGD.
They also add an additional step after the SGD steps where an average is taken over a decision set that decreases between iterations.
Their convergence results have very strong assumptions, in particular

These assumptions restrict the possibilities for

]
.
Zero-th order Perturbed Stochastic gradient descent (ZPSGD) was proposed in
[
8
]
, which follows GSmoothSGD with a fixed

The convergence results in

The most related paper to our analysis of GSmoothSGD is

The authors convert OGHC from a double loop into a single loop that follows GSmoothSGD where

Gaussian smoothing SGD (GSmoothSGD)

Here we prove convergence results for GSmoothSGD.
Further, if
\sigma_{t}=0
(i.e., no smoothing occurs), then we recover the standard convergence guarantees as with SGD.
If

is constant, then we show that the expectation of the gradient from GSmoothSGD ends up in to a ball centered at zero whose radius depends of this constant.

Theorem 3.1

Consider GSmoothSGD in Algorithm

(3.11)

The proof modifies the standard SGD proof (see, e.g.,

Compared to the standard convergence results for SGD (the terms in (

5
Explicitly Smoothing Neural Networks

Gaussian smoothing in deep learning is often implemented using gradient-free approaches, such as Monte Carlo estimates of (
1.9
). This method is computationally expensive, as each point in the gradient-free approximation requires randomly perturbing the model, a process more complex than simple function queries. To circumvent the computational burden of integral approximation, we explicitly compute the output of Gaussian smoothing applied to a network. Our approach is inspired by
[
16
]
, which derives the explicit form of a Gaussian-smoothed recurrent neural network. We start by introducing the notation for explicit smoothing and then present the main results, detailing how Gaussian smoothing modifies the formulation of a neural network.

For a neural network, we use
C
and
L
to denote the number of convolutional and dense layers, respectively.
Given a convolutional layer
C_{l}
, let
k_{l}
be the kernel size and
s_{l}
be the stride length.
Then
w_{l}=\frac{\text{width}(x_{l-1})-k_{l}}{s_{l}}+1
gives the width of the output of the convolution with a single kernel.
When we explicitly smooth a convolutional layer, we end up with an additional regularization term that depends on the input to that layer.
Define the convolutional norm of
x
with respect to
C_{l}
as

\|x\|_{C_{l}}^{2}=\|x*J_{k_{l}}\|^{2}

where
J_{k_{l}}
is the
k_{l}\times k_{l}
matrix whose entries are all 1.

Two additional regularization terms come from smoothing the dense and activation layers, which we define as

\displaystyle r_{1}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\|\theta\textup{diag}(\sqrt{(h^{2})_{\sigma}}(x))\|^{2}_{F}-\|\theta\textup{diag}(h_{\sigma}(x))\|^{2}_{F}

(5.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\textup{dim}(x)\|\sqrt{(h^{2})_{\sigma}}(x)\|^{2}

(5.13)

\displaystyle\hskip-3.0ptr_{2}(x,\theta,l)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\|h_{\sigma}(x)\|_{C_{l}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{(h^{2})_{\sigma}}(x)\|_{F}^{2}

(5.14)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\|h_{\sigma}(x)\|_{F}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}w_{l}^{2}\|\theta\|_{F}^{2},

(5.15)

where

is the Frobenius norm.

With our notation defined, we are ready to state the optimization problem that Gaussian smoothed neural networks solve.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we provided theoretical convergence proofs for smoothing two stochastic gradient algorithms, along with sufficient conditions for performance improvements. We first defined the GSmoothSGD algorithm and proved that it converges to a ball around the minimizer under specific sequences of smoothing parameters (e.g., when constant). We then introduced GSmoothAdam and demonstrated its almost-sure convergence to a stationary point, showing that smoothing does not impose additional computational costs. These results offer a general framework for applying smoothing to other stochastic gradient algorithms.

Our numerical experiments on neural networks further confirmed that smoothing improves performance and robustness to noise in training. To align with our theoretical findings, we explicitly derived Gaussian-smoothed versions of feedforward and convolutional neural networks, which can be adapted to a broad range of architectures. Future work will explore smoothing with other distributions beyond Gaussian, such as compactly supported distributions, to extend these results to cases involving only local Lipschitz constants of the gradient.

Definition B.1

\sigma\geq 0
and suppose
f_{\sigma}
has a unique minimizer
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}
.
Let
(\sigma_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[\sigma,\infty)
with
\sigma_{t}\to\sigma
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\big(\sigma,(\sigma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty};(k_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bm{x}_{t}\to\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}\text{ where }\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{x}_{t}-\eta\nabla f_{k_{t},\sigma_{t+1}}(\bm{x}_{t})\text{ and }\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}\}

(B.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\big(\sigma,(\sigma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle=\bigcap\left\{\mathcal{B}\big(\sigma,(\sigma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty};(k_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\big):k_{t}\sim\text{Unif}([K])\right\}

(B.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\left\{\mathcal{B}\big(\sigma,(\sigma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\big):(\sigma_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[\sigma,\infty)\text{ with }\sigma_{t}\to\sigma\right\}

(B.24)

We call
\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
the basin of attraction of
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}
.

While we assume that there are unique minimizers, the proof can be generalized to the setting where the minimizers form a connected, bounded set. Regardless, for simplicity, we assume uniqueness of the minimizers.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
chains together the iterates from traveling though
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{N}
by running GSmoothSGD until the iterate is close enough to
\bm{x}_{\sigma_{n}}^{\star}
so that it is also in the basin of attraction of
\bm{x}_{\sigma_{n+1}}^{\star}
.
The following proposition provides the proof of this chaining idea.
In particular, Proposition
3.1
is actually a corollary of the following result, which provides how we transition from
\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{n})
to
\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{n+1})
.

Proposition B.1

Assume
f_{\sigma}
has a unique minimizer
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}
for
0\leq\sigma\leq\Sigma
.
Let
0\leq\tau<\sigma\leq\Sigma
be such that

\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{B}(\tau).

(B.25)

Suppose further that there is an open set around
\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}
contained in
\mathcal{B}(\tau)
.
Then
\mathcal{B}(\sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\tau)
.

Let
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
, so there exists
(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[\sigma,\infty)
such that

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t-1}-\eta\nabla f_{k_{t},\alpha_{t+1}}(\bm{x}_{t-1})

(B.26)

with
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}
and
k_{t}\sim\text{Unif}([K])
satisfies
\bm{x}_{t}\to\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}
.
Let
\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},...)
be a realization of samples where
k_{t}\sim\text{Unif}([K])
.
By assumption, there exists
R>0
such that

B(\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma},R)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\tau).

(B.27)

Since
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{n})
, there exists
(\alpha_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[\sigma,\infty)
so that if

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t-1}-\eta\nabla f_{k_{t},\alpha_{t+1}}(\bm{x}_{t-1})

(B.28)

with
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}
then
\bm{x}_{t}\to\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}
.
As such, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\|\bm{x}_{N}-\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma}\|<R
.
Hence,

\bm{x}_{N}\in B(\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma},R)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\tau).

(B.29)

This means that there exists
(\beta_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[\tau,\infty)
with
\beta_{t}\to\tau
such that if

\bm{y}_{t}=\bm{y}_{t-1}-\eta\nabla f_{k_{t+N},\beta_{t+1}}(\bm{y}_{t-1})

(B.30)

with
\bm{y}_{0}=\bm{x}_{N}
then
\bm{y}_{t}\to\bm{x}_{\tau}^{\star}
.
Define

\gamma_{t}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{t}&\text{for }t=1,...,N-1\\
\beta_{t}&\text{for }t\geq N\end{array}\right.

(B.31)

and

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{t-1}-\eta\nabla f_{k_{t},\gamma_{t+1}}(\bm{x}_{t-1})

(B.32)

with
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}
.
Then
\bm{x}_{t}\to\bm{x}_{\tau}^{\star}
and
(\gamma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}\subseteq[\tau,\infty)
with
\gamma_{t}\to\tau
.
Therefore,

x\in\mathcal{B}(\tau,(\gamma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty};\bm{k})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\tau).

(B.33)

Since this can be done for any
\bm{k}
, the proof is complete.

Practically, this does not provide a sequence of smoothing parameters because
(\beta_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
(and hence
(\gamma_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
) depends on
\bm{k}
.
Next, we state a more general corollary of the previous proposition compared to Proposition
3.1
.
In fact, Proposition
3.1
is just a particular example of the following corollary that leverages the convexity of
f_{\Sigma}
to increase the initial basin of attraction.

Corollary B.1

Suppose there exists an increasing sequence,
(\sigma_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
, starting at 0 such that for
n=2,\dots,N
,
f_{\sigma_{n}}
has a unique minimizer
\bm{x}_{\sigma_{n}}^{\star}
and there exists
R_{n}>0
so that

B(\bm{x}^{\star}_{\sigma_{n}},R_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{n-1}).

(B.34)

Then
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(0)
.

The previous proposition shows that
\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{n-1})
for each
n=2,...,N
.
Therefore,

\mathcal{B}(\Sigma)=\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{2})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{N})=\mathcal{B}(0).

(B.35)

Finally, we prove Proposition
3.1
.

[Proof of Proposition
3.1
]
Since each
f_{k,\Sigma}
is convex and they share a minimizer,
f_{\Sigma}
is also convex with the same minimizer.
As such,
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By the previous corollary, we have

\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathcal{B}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(0)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(B.36)

D
Details Regarding Explicitly Smoothing Neural Networks

In order to calculate the explicit form that a smoothed neural network has, we need to be able to combine all of the model’s components (including its constraint equations) into a single function that we can take the expectation of.
We begin our analysis by writing out the constrained and unconstrained optimization problems for both FFNNs and CNNs.

A FFNN satisfies the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\theta,b}\quad&\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{L}^{n}-y^{n}\|^{2}\\
\text{subject to}\quad&x_{1}^{n}=\theta_{1}x_{0}^{n}+b_{1}\\
&x_{l}^{n}=\theta_{l}h(x_{l-1}^{n})+b_{l}\text{ for }l=2,...,L\end{split}

(D.105)

Converting this to an unconstrained optimization problem means that a FFNN satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\theta,b}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{L}^{n}-y^{n}\|^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\theta_{1}x_{0}^{n}+b_{1}-x_{1}^{n}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=2}^{L}\lambda_{l}\|\theta_{l}h(x_{l-1}^{n})+b_{l}-x_{l}^{n}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(D.106)

A CNN satisfies the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\theta,b}\quad&\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{L+C}^{n}-y^{n}\|^{2}\\
\text{subject to}\quad&x_{1}^{n}=x_{0}^{n}*\theta_{1}+b_{1}\\
&x_{l}^{n}=h(x_{l-1}^{n})*\theta_{l}+b_{l}\text{ for }2\leq l\leq C\\
&x^{n}_{l}=\theta_{l}h(x^{n}_{l-1})+b_{l}\text{ for }C+1\leq l\leq C+L\end{split}

(D.107)

Again converting this into an unconstrained problem shows that a CNN satisfies:

\min_{\theta,b}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|x_{L+C}^{n}-y^{n}\|^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|x_{0}^{n}*\theta_{1}+b_{1}-x_{1}^{n}\|_{F}^{2}+\sum_{l=2}^{C}\lambda_{l}\|h(x_{l-1}^{n})*\theta_{l}+b_{l}-x_{l}^{n}\|_{F}^{2}\\
+\sum_{l=C+1}^{C+L}\lambda_{l}\|\theta_{l}h(x^{n}_{l-1})+b_{l}l-x_{l}^{n}\|^{2}.

(D.108)

To smooth these loss functions, we need to smooth each term of its sum.

\displaystyle=\|\theta_{+}x+b_{+}-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{+}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}(\theta_{+})_{ij}(\theta_{+})_{ik}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{j}u_{k}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.139)

\displaystyle=\|\theta_{+}x+b_{+}-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\theta_{+})_{ij}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{j}^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.140)

\displaystyle=\|\theta_{+}x+b_{+}-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\theta_{+})_{ij}^{2}

(D.141)

\displaystyle=\|\theta_{+}x+b_{+}-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\theta_{+}\|_{F}^{2}.

(D.142)

The second term of (
D.136
), smoothes to

\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}\left(\|x\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}\right)=\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}\|x\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{4}dd_{+}}{4},

(D.143)

which has been done several times already in this proof. Therefore, we end up with

l_{\sigma}(b_{+},x_{+},\theta_{+},x)=\|\theta_{+}x+b_{+}-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|\theta_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}\|x\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{4}dd_{+}}{4}+\sigma^{2}d_{+}.

(D.144)

Before we smooth the convolutional layer, we want to write out the “convolutional norm”:

\|x\|_{C_{+}}^{2}=\sum_{a,b=0}^{w_{+}-1}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k_{+}}\Big(x^{(as_{+}+i)(bs_{+}+j)}\Big)^{2}=\|x*J_{k_{+}}\|^{2}.

(D.145)

Proposition D.3
(Smoothing Convolutional Layer)

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x_{+},b_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{+}}
,
\theta_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}\times c_{+}}
, and

l(\theta_{+},x_{+},x)=\|x*_{+}\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}

(D.146)

were
*_{+}
represents the convolution with stride
s_{+}
.
Let
C_{+}
represent the convolutional layer with these parameters.
If
x=x_{0}
represents the input data (i.e. we cannot smooth with respect to
x
), then

\displaystyle l_{\sigma}(\theta_{+},x_{+},x)=\|x*_{+}\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\sigma^{2}d_{+}.

(D.147)

Otherwise,

\displaystyle l_{\sigma}(\theta_{+},x_{+},x)=\|x*_{+}\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}w_{+}^{2}\|\theta_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\frac{\sigma^{4}}{4}\|1\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\sigma^{2}d_{+}.

(D.148)

For the proof, we will simplify notation and write
*
instead of
*_{+}
since all of the convolutions will use the same stride.
Recall that
w_{+}=\left(\frac{\text{width}(x)+c_{+}}{s_{+}}+1\right)
.
First, we smooth with respect to
b_{+}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d_{+}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{+}}}}\|x*\theta_{+}+(b_{+}+\sigma U)-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.149)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d_{+}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{+}}}}\Big(\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+2\sigma\langle x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+},U\rangle_{F}+\sigma^{2}\|U\|_{F}^{2}\Big)e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.150)

\displaystyle=\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}.

(D.151)

Second, we smooth with respect to
\theta_{+}
. For first term from the previous equation, we have

\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{c_{+}^{2}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}^{2}}}\|x*(\theta_{+}+\sigma U)+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU\\
=\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{c_{+}^{2}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}^{2}}}\|x*U\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.152)

Focusing on the convolution term, (here
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}=0,s_{+},2s_{+},...,w_{+}s_{+}
)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{c_{+}^{2}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}^{2}}}\|x*U\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.153)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{c_{+}^{2}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}^{2}}}\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{c_{+}}U^{ij}x^{(a^{\prime}+i)(b^{\prime}+j)}\right)^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.154)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{c_{+}^{2}}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}^{2}}}\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{c_{+}}\sum_{k,l=1}^{c_{+}}U^{ij}U^{kl}x^{(a^{\prime}+i)(b^{\prime}+j)}x^{(a^{\prime}+k)(b^{\prime}+l)}\right)e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.155)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{c_{+}^{2}}}{{2}}}}\sum_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{c_{+}}\big(x^{(a^{\prime}+i)(b^{\prime}+j)}\big)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{c_{+}^{2}}}(U^{ij})^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.156)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|x\|_{C_{+}}^{2}.

(D.157)

So after smoothing with respect to
b_{+}
and
\theta_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}.

(D.158)

Third, we smooth with respect to
x_{+}
, but as with
b_{+}
we end up with

\displaystyle\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\sigma^{2}d_{+}.

(D.159)

Finally, we smooth with respect to
x
. For the first term of (
D.159
), we have

\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|(x+\sigma U)*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU\\
=\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|U*\theta_{+}\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.160)

Now, for the second term, we repeat the process in (
D.153
) and arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|(x+\sigma U)*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.161)

\displaystyle=\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{a,b=1}^{w_{+}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{c_{+}}(\theta_{+}^{ij})^{2}

(D.162)

\displaystyle=\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}w_{+}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{c_{+}}(\theta_{+}^{ij})^{2}

(D.163)

\displaystyle=\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}w_{+}^{2}\|\theta_{+}\|_{F}^{2}.

(D.164)

For the second term of (
D.159
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x+\sigma U\|^{2}_{C_{+}}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.165)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{a,b=1}^{w_{+}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{c_{+}}\Big(x^{(a+i)(b+j)}+\sigma U^{(a+i)(b+j)}\Big)^{2}e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.166)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b=1}^{w_{+}}\sum_{i,j}\Big(x^{(a+i)(b+j)}\Big)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{a,b=1}^{w_{+}}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{2}

(D.167)

\displaystyle=\|x\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|1\|^{2}_{C_{+}}.

(D.168)

So, after smoothing with respect to all variables, we end up with

\displaystyle\|x*\theta_{+}+b_{+}-x_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}w_{+}^{2}\|\theta_{+}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\frac{\sigma^{4}}{4}\|1\|^{2}_{C_{+}}+\sigma^{2}d_{+}.

(D.169)

Proposition D.4
(Smoothing Dropout Layer)

Let
p\in[0,1]
and
drop
be given by
\mathcal{P}(\textup{drop}(x)=0)=p
and
\mathcal{P}(\textup{drop}(x)=x)=1-p
.
For
x,x_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the unconstrained loss associated with the dropout layer as

l(x,x_{+})=\|\textup{drop}(x)-x_{+}\|^{2}.

(D.170)

Then

l_{\sigma}(x,x_{+})=p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+(1-p)\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+\left(1-\tfrac{p}{2}\right)\sigma^{2}d.

(D.171)

Let
x,x_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that if
x
and
x_{+}
are matrices, the norms and inner products in this proof are the Frobenius-type.
First, we will smooth with respect to
x_{+}
. So,

\displaystyle E_{u}[l(x,x_{+}+\sigma u)]

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x_{+}+\sigma u\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du+\frac{1-p}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-(x_{+}+\sigma u)\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du.

(D.172)

The first integral can be computed as

\displaystyle\frac{p}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x_{+}+\sigma u\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

\displaystyle=p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{p\sigma^{2}d}{2}.

(D.173)

The second integral is

\displaystyle\frac{1-p}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-(x_{+}+\sigma u)\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

\displaystyle=(1-p)\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{(1-p)\sigma^{2}d}{2}.

(D.174)

So,

\displaystyle E_{u}[l(x,x_{+}+\sigma u)]

\displaystyle=p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{p\sigma^{2}d}{2}+(1-p)\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{(1-p)\sigma^{2}d}{2}

(D.175)

\displaystyle=p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+(1-p)\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}.

(D.176)

Second, we will smooth with respect to
x
. We have

\displaystyle E_{u}(l(x+\sigma u,x_{+}))

(D.177)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+(1-p)\|x+\sigma u-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}\right)e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.178)

\displaystyle=p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}+\frac{1-p}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x+\sigma u-x_{+}\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.179)

\displaystyle=p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}+(1-p)\left(\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}\right)

(D.180)

\displaystyle=(1-p)\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}+p\|x_{+}\|^{2}+(2-p)\frac{\sigma^{2}d}{2}.

(D.181)

This shows the claimed result.

Proposition D.5
(Smoothing Average Pooling)

Define

\Big(\textup{pool}(x)\Big)_{i}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x^{i_{j}},

(D.182)

that is the pooling layer averages over
x^{i_{j}}
for
j=1,...,k
.
Let

l(x,x_{+})=\|\textup{pool}(x)-x_{+}\|^{2}.

(D.183)

Then

l_{\sigma}(x,x_{+})=\|\textup{pool}(x)-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2k}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}.

(D.184)

First, we smooth with respect to
x_{+}
, which has been done numerous times throughout this note and we end up with

\|\textup{pool}(x)-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{+}}{2}.

(D.185)

Second, we smooth with respect to
x
:

\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\textup{pool}(x+\sigma u)-x_{+}\right\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du\\
=\|\textup{pool}(x+\sigma u)-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\textup{pool}(u)\right\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du.

(D.186)

Focusing on the last term,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\textup{pool}(u)\right\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}u^{i_{j}}\right)^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.187)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{+}}\sum_{j,m=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{i_{j}}u^{i_{m}}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.188)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u^{i_{j}})^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du

(D.189)

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{+}\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}{2k}.

(D.190)

So,

\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\|\textup{pool}(x+\sigma u)-x_{+}\right\|^{2}e^{-\|u\|^{2}}\;du=\|\textup{pool}(x+\sigma u)-x_{+}\|^{2}+\frac{d_{+}\sigma^{2}}{2k}.

(D.191)

Combining this with the constant term from the previous equation gives the result.

Note D.1
(Comment about pooling layers)

If a max pooling layer is used, then the smoothing function is difficult (if not impossible to compute). In particular, let
\mathcal{I}
is the index that a single part of the pooling layer takes the maximum over and

h(x)=\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}x^{ij}.

(D.192)

Since
h
is nonlinear, we need to use Proposition
D.1
(e) which requires computing
h_{\sigma}
.
Then

\displaystyle h_{\sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{|\mathcal{I}|}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|}}\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\Big(x^{ij}+\sigma U_{ij}\Big)e^{-\|U\|_{F}^{2}}\;dU

(D.193)

\displaystyle=E\left(\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}X_{ij}\right)

(D.194)

where
X_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{l}^{ij},\sigma^{2})
.
Even in the case where all of the
x_{l}^{ij}
are the same, this does not have a closed form.
Hence, we cannot compute
h_{\sigma}
.

D.2
Proofs of Mathematical Formulation of Smoothed Neural Networks

Now that we have smoothed all of the components of both of the unconstrained problems (
D.106
) and (
D.108
), we are ready to explicitly write out their smoothed mathematical formulations.

[Proof of Theorem
5.1
]
The unconstrained FFNN is given in (
D.106
), which finds the minimum of the sum (over
n
) of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|x_{L}^{n}-y^{n}\|^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\theta_{1}x_{0}^{n}+b_{1}-x_{1}^{n}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=2}^{L}\lambda_{l}\|\theta_{l}h(x_{l-1}^{n})+b_{l}-x_{l}^{n}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(D.195)

Based on the previous section, this smoothes to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|x_{L}^{n}-y^{n}\|^{2}&+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{L}}{2}+\lambda_{1}\left(\|\theta_{1}x_{0}^{n}+b_{1}-x_{1}^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}d_{1}}{2}\|x_{0}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}d_{1}\right)\\
&+\sum_{l=2}^{L}\lambda_{l}\Bigg(\|\theta_{l}h(x_{l-1}^{n})+b_{l}-x_{l}^{n}\|^{2}+\|\theta_{l}\textup{diag}(\sqrt{(h^{2})_{\sigma}}(x_{l-1}))\|^{2}_{F}\\
&\phantom{+\sum_{l=2}^{L}\lambda_{l}\Bigg(}-\|\theta_{l}\textup{diag}(h_{\sigma}(x_{l-1}))\|^{2}_{F}+\sigma^{2}d_{l}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\|\sqrt{(h^{2})_{\sigma}}(x_{l-1})\|^{2}\right)\Bigg).\end{split}

(D.196)

Now, we can reconstrain to get the result. The proof for the CNN case is exactly the same.

E
Additional Details of Numerical Experiments and Practical Guide to Smooth Neural Network Implementation

We begin with a description of how to implement the explicitly smooth neural networks.
In order to use TensorFlow’s built-in layer regularizers, we avoid regularization terms that mix weights and layer inputs (e.g.,
\|\theta\textup{diag}(\sqrt{(h^{2})_{\sigma}}(x))\|^{2}_{F}
).
This is why took a non-standard approach and we separated the activation function from the layer.
Hence, we split up terms like
\|\theta h(x)+b\|
into two terms like
\|h(x)-y\|
and
\|\theta y+b\|
.
Then we use the results from Section
D.1
to smooth.
The original unconstrained terms and their smoothed constraints and regularization terms can be found in Table
3
.

Another modification of SGD that addresses this the variation in the gradient near a minimum is stochastic variance reduced gradient (SVRG)
[
9
]
, which modifies the gradient used in the update step using inner and outer loops.
The outer loop computes a control variate that reduces the variance of the iterates in the inner loop.
The inner loop performs SGD steps, but with the control variate added to each step.
So, the inner update becomes

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla f_{k_{t}}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{k_{t}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\widetilde{\mu}

(F.197)

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{x}_{t-1}-\eta\bm{v}_{t},

(F.198)

where
\widetilde{x}
is the output of the previous inner iteration and
\widetilde{\mu}
is the full gradient at
\widetilde{x}
.
Since the motivation for SVRG is variance reduction, just like SGD, SVRG has a tendency converge to non-global minima, making it another good candidate for using Gaussian smoothing.
GSmoothSGD suffers the same variance issues as SGD, so in order to combine the benefits of variance reduction and smoothing, we propose Gaussian smoothed SVRG (GSmoothSVRG) which can be found in Algorithm
3
.

In this section, we provide convergence results for GSmoothSVRG and then provide numerical experiments that show how the variance is reduced compared to both SGD and GSmoothSGD.

F.1
Convergence of GSmoothSVRG

This section provides the proof of Theorem
F.1
and the additional background results needed for the proof.
The original convergence result for SVRG is stated for strongly convex function, we will do the same for GSmoothSVRG, which means we need to show that smoothing preserves strong convexity as well.
Our second result shows just that.

Lemma F.1

If
f
is
\gamma
-strongly convex, then so is
f_{\sigma}
.

The proof is the same as the proof when
f
is convex (see Lemma
2.1 (a)
) where only a minor modification is made for the strong convexity which now includes the quadratic function that
f
grows as fast as. We still provide the proof here.

[Proof of Lemma
F.1
]
Assume that
f
is
\gamma
-strongly convex.
Then

\displaystyle f_{\sigma}(t\bm{x}+(1-t)\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t\bm{x}+(1-t)\bm{y}+\sigma\bm{u})e^{-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}}\;du

(F.199)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\Big(t(\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{u})+(1-t)(\bm{y}+\sigma\bm{u})\Big)e^{-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}}\;du

(F.200)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(tf(\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{u})+(1-t)f(\bm{y}+\sigma\bm{u})

(F.201)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{2}t(1-t)\|(\bm{x}+\sigma\bm{u})-(\bm{y}+\sigma\bm{u})\|^{2}\Big)e^{-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}}\;du

(F.202)

\displaystyle=tf_{\sigma}(\bm{x})+(1-t)f_{\sigma}(\bm{y})+\frac{\gamma}{2}t(1-t)\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}

(F.203)

shows that
f_{\sigma}
is also
\gamma
-strongly convex.

We now show that adding variance reduction using SVRG to GSmoothSGD or equivalently smoothing SVRG does not change the convergence rate from the original SVRG for strongly convex and
L
-smooth functions.
In particular, compared with GSmoothSGD, GSmoothSVRG will converge for a fixed learning rate.

Theorem F.1

Consider GSmoothSVRG in Algorithm
3
.
Assume
f_{i}
is convex and
L
-smooth and
f=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}
is
\gamma
-strongly convex (for some
\gamma>0
).
Assume
m
is sufficiently large so that

\alpha=\frac{1+2L\eta^{2}m}{\eta\gamma(1-2L\eta)m}<1.

(F.204)

Then

E(f(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\leq\alpha^{s}E(f_{\sigma_{0}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+\frac{Ld}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha^{i}\max(0,\sigma_{i-1}^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}).

(F.205)

The proof of this theorem is broken into four lemmas whose proofs follow the same structure as in
[
9
]
with adaptions for smoothing.
In particular, we mimic the proof that SVRG converges for strongly convex and
L
-smooth functions from
[
9
]
, which is broken into four lemmmas, and smooth when necessary.
The four lemmas build on each other culminating in the fact that the iterates are getting closer to the minimum.
The proof of the theorem then iteratively applies this result to get the claimed bound.
We begin by justifying the first lemma which states a bound between the difference in the gradients of the iterates and the minimum.

Lemma F.2

For each
i\in\{1,...,K\}
, let
f_{i}
be
L
-smooth and convex.
Then for any
\sigma\geq 0
,

E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\|^{2})\leq 2L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.206)

Furthermore, for
\sigma\geq\tau\geq 0
,

E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i,\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})\|^{2})\leq 4L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))

(F.207)

where
\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau}
is the minimizer of
f_{\tau}
.

We can adapt the above to get the following statements as well:

E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i,\tau}(\bm{x}_{*})\|^{2})\\
\leq 2L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}L^{2}d

(F.208)

E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i,\tau}(\bm{x})\|^{2})\leq 4L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))

(F.209)

Note that since each
f_{k}
is convex,
f
and
f_{k,\sigma}
are convex for any
\sigma\geq 0
. This means that
f_{\sigma}
is also convex for any
\sigma\geq 0
.
Let

g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})=f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})-\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{*}\rangle.

(F.210)

Then since
f_{i}
is convex,

f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\geq f_{i}(\bm{x})-f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\geq\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{*}\rangle.

(F.211)

This means
g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})\geq 0
for any
i
and
\sigma
.
Since
f_{i,\sigma}
is
L
-smooth, so is
g_{i}^{\sigma}
.
So,

0\leq g_{i}^{\sigma}\left(\bm{x}-\tfrac{1}{L}\nabla g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})\right)\leq g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})-\frac{1}{2L}\|g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})\|^{2}

(F.212)

and rearranging we have

\|g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})\|^{2}\leq 2Lg_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x}).

(F.213)

Since

\nabla g_{i}^{\sigma}(\bm{x})=\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*}),

(F.214)

we have

\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\|^{2}\leq 2L\Big(f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})-\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{*}\rangle\Big).

(F.215)

Therefore, taking the expectation over
i
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\|^{2})&\leq 2LE(f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})-\langle\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{*}\rangle)\\
&=2L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).\end{split}

(F.216)

The furthermore statement can be seen by

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i,\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})\|^{2})&\leq E(\|\nabla f_{i,\sigma}(\bm{x})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\|^{2})+E(\|\nabla f_{i,\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{x}_{*})\|^{2})\\
&\leq 2L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+2L(f_{\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&\leq 2L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+2L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&=4L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})-f(\bm{x}_{*})),\end{split}

(F.217)

since
f_{\sigma}(\bm{x})\geq f_{\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})
.

Next, we bound the GSmoothSVRG gradient update by a linear combination of function outputs.
The previous lemma is applied in the proof in order to derive the bound.

Lemma F.3

For each
i\in\{1,...,K\}
, let
f_{i}
be
L
-smooth and convex.
For
\sigma\geq\tau\geq 0
,

E(\|\bm{v}_{t}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\leq 4L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*})+f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.218)

Recall that

\bm{v}_{t}^{\sigma,\tau}=\nabla f_{i_{t},\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}.

(F.219)

This means

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(\bm{v}_{t}^{\sigma,\tau}|\bm{x}_{t-1})&=\nabla f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\nabla f_{\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})\\
&=\nabla f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1}).\end{split}

(F.220)

Observe

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(\|\bm{v}_{t}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})&=E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\leq E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\qquad+E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(1)}}{{\leq}}E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\qquad+E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-E(f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}))\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(2)}}{{\leq}}E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})+E(\|\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau})-\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem~\ref {lem:ssvrgstep1}}}}{{\leq}}4L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+4L(f_{\tau}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(3)}}{{\leq}}4L(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*})+f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\end{split}

(F.221)

where step (1) is due to
E(\nabla f_{i_{t},\tau}(\bm{x}_{*}^{\tau}))=0
, step (2) follows from
E(\|\xi-E(\xi)\|^{2})=E(\|\xi\|^{2})-\|E(\xi)\|^{2}\leq E(\|\xi\|^{2})
for any random vector
\xi
, and step (3) is because
f
is convex and
\sigma\geq\tau
.

With this bound on the GSmoothSVRG gradient update, we are ready to bound the expected value of the iterates.

Lemma F.4

For each
i\in\{1,...,K\}
, let
f_{i}
be
L
-smooth and convex.
For
\sigma\geq\tau\geq 0
,

2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\leq E(\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2})+4L\eta^{2}mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.222)

First,

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(\|\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def. }\bm{x}_{t}}}{{=}}\|\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*},E(\bm{v}_{t}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\rangle+\eta^{2}E(\|\bm{v}_{t}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{eqn. \eqref{eqn:expectationofvt}}}}{{=}}\|\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*},\nabla f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})\rangle+\eta^{2}E(\|\bm{v}_{t}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{t-1})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem. \ref {lem:ssvrgstep2}}}}{{\leq}}\|\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*},\nabla f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})\rangle\\
&\qquad+4L\eta^{2}(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*})+f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{conv.}}}{{\leq}}\|\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&\qquad+4L\eta^{2}(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*})+f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&=\|\bm{x}_{t-1}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta(1-2L\eta)(f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+4L\eta^{2}(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).\end{split}

(F.223)

Since
P(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s}=\bm{x}_{t})=\frac{1}{m}
for
t=0,...,m-1
, then

mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1})=\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{t}).

(F.224)

So, summing over the
m
steps gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(\|\bm{x}_{m}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1})&\leq\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*})|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1})\\
&\qquad+4L\eta^{2}m(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).\end{split}

(F.225)

Rearranging shows

E(\|\bm{x}_{m}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1})+2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*})|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1})\\
\leq\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}+4L\eta^{2}m(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.226)

Since
\|\bm{x}_{m}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}\geq 0
,

2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*})|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1})\leq\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}+4L\eta^{2}m(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.227)

Finally, taking the expectation gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}&2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&=2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*})|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1}))\\
&\leq E(E(\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2}|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1}))+4L\eta^{2}mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})|\bm{x}_{0},...,\bm{x}_{m-1}))\\
&=E(\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2})+4L\eta^{2}mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).\end{split}

(F.228)

All of the work so far now culminates in showing that, for strongly convex functions, expected difference between the iterates and minimum decreases by a multiplicative factor smaller than one each time.

Lemma F.5

Assume
f_{i}
is convex and
L
-smooth and
f
is
\gamma
-strongly convex (for some
\gamma>0
).
For
\sigma\geq\tau\geq 0
, if
f
is
\gamma
-strongly convex, then

E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\leq\frac{1+2L\eta^{2}m}{\eta\gamma(1-2L\eta)m}E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.229)

Since
f
is
\gamma
-strongly convex, so is
f_{\sigma}
.
As
\bm{x}_{0}=\widetilde{\bm{x}}
,

E(\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2})\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f_{\sigma}(\bm{x}_{*}))\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).

(F.230)

Combining this with Lemma
F.4
shows

\displaystyle\begin{split}2\eta(1-2L\eta)mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))&\leq E(\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}_{*}\|^{2})+4L\eta^{2}mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&\leq\frac{2}{\gamma}E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+4L\eta^{2}mE(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&=2\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}-2L\eta^{2}m\right)E(f_{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})-f(\bm{x}_{*})).\end{split}

(F.231)

Arithmetic gives the result.

With the result of the previous lemma in hand, we are finally ready to prove that GSmoothSVRG converges.

[Proof of Theorem
F.1
]
Using Lemma
F.5
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}E(f_{\sigma_{s}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))&\leq\alpha E(f_{\sigma_{s}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))\\
&\leq\alpha E(f_{\sigma_{s-1}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{s-1})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+\frac{Ld}{2}\alpha\max(0,\sigma_{s-1}^{2}-\sigma_{s}^{2})\\
&\vdots\\
&\leq\alpha^{s}E(f_{\sigma_{0}}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{0})-f(\bm{x}_{*}))+\frac{Ld}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha^{i}\max(0,\sigma_{i-1}^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}).\end{split}

(F.232)

Note that the only condition that
\tau
in GSmoothSVRG needs to satisfy is
\sigma_{t}\geq\tau\geq 0
at each iteration.
The two obvious choices for
\tau
are
\sigma_{s}
or
0
.
If
\tau=\sigma_{s}
, then we are performing SVRG on
f_{\sigma_{s}}
.
On the other hand, if
\tau=0
, then we are making the control variate of SVRG include information about the gradient of the original, non-smoothed function.

F.2
Numerical Experiments for GSmoothSVRG

For the GSmoothSVRG experiments, we repeat the noisy MNIST experiments using a batch size of 1.
Since our primary focus was to compare variance of the norm of the iterative update and the rate of convergence between GSmoothSVRG and SGD, we only trained for one epoch (of 5000 steps in total).
Additionally, we wanted to use the same learning rate for both algorithms; after a hyperparameter search, we chose to use 0.01 for both methods.
A hyperparameter search was also done to pick regularization weights as well as the smoothing parameter for the inner GSmoothSVRG update.
The regularization weights for GSmoothSVRG were
10^{-13}
for both the first ReLU layer and first dense layer and
10^{-11}
for both the second ReLU layer and the output layer, and we use the same smoothing parameter for both the inner and outer updates.
The standard deviation of the norm of the gradient update for all of the GSmoothSVRG experiments can be found in Figure
9
.
