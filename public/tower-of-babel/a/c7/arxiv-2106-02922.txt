A finite element method for two-phase flow with material viscous interface
Maxim Olshanskii ∗, Annalisa Quaini ∗ and Qi Sun

Abstract
This paper studies a model of two-phase flow with an immersed material viscous interface and a finite element method for numerical solution of the resulting system of PDEs. The interaction between the bulk and surface media is characterized by no-penetration and slip with friction interface conditions. The system is shown to be dissipative and a model stationary problem is proved to be well-posed. The finite element method applied in this paper belongs to a family of unfitted discretizations. The performance of the method when model and discretization parameters vary is assessed. Moreover, an iterative procedure based on the splitting of the system into bulk and surface problems is introduced and studied numerically.



keywords

two-phase flow, surface Stokes equations, finite elements, cutFEM, TraceFEM

1
Introduction

We consider two immiscible, viscous, and incompressible fluids
separated by a viscous inextensible material interface modeled as a Boussinesq–Scriven surface fluid.
The following coupling conditions are prescribed
between the bulk two-phase flow and the surface fluid: (i) the immiscibility condition, i.e. the bulk fluid does not penetrate through
the interface; (ii) slip with friction between the bulk fluid and the viscous interface; and (iii) the
load exerted from the bulk fluid onto the surface fluid defined by the jump of the normal stress across the interface.
To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that the slip with friction conditions are imposed in this context.
This extends the Navier boundary condition to the interface case and interpolates between no-slip of the bulk phase and embedded material layer, as considered in

[
4
,
10
,
38
,
45
]
, and uncoupled lateral dynamics
of fluidic surfaces as studied, for example, in
[
46
,
48
,
51
]
. The model contributes to characterizing the interaction between a rather viscous and dense interface material and less viscous and dense bulk fluid,
as is the case of, e.g., a lipid vesicle or cell
membrane with its content and surrounding extracellular fluid.

We first state the problem in terms of incompressible two-phase Navier–Stokes flow in the bulk coupled to
surface fluid equations and show the energy balance for this system. This balance
demonstrates that the system with the proposed coupling conditions is thermodynamically consistent, i.e. in the absence of external forces
and with no energy inflow through the boundary the system is dissipative.
Then, we introduce a simplifying assumption: the coupled system
has reached a steady state and inertia terms can be neglected. Although the resulting surface–bulk Stokes problem
is a strong simplification of the original problem, it still retains interesting features for the purpose of numerical analysis.

For the simplified model, we show well-posedness following the abstract framework for
generalized saddle point problems proposed in, e.g.,
[
5
,
37
]
.
Next, we present a sharp interface, geometrically unfitted finite element (FE) method.
Unfitted methods allow the sharp interface to cut through the elements of a fixed background grid.
Their main advantage is the relative ease in handling time-dependent domains, implicitly defined interfaces,
and problems with strong geometric deformations
[
9
]
.
For the bulk two-phase Stokes problem, we apply an isoparametric unfitted finite element approach
[
32
]
of the CutFEM (or Nitsche-XFEM) family
[
14
,
17
,
25
,
26
,
35
,
52
]
. For a (bulk only) interface Stokes problem with slip between phases, the approach was studied in
[
40
]
.
CutFEM uses overlapping fictitious domains in combination with ghost
penalty stabilization
[
11
]
to enrich and stabilize the solution.
In this paper, we consider the unfitted generalized Taylor–Hood finite element pair

\mathbf{P}_{k+1}-P_{k}
,
k\geq 1
. For more details on the isoparametric unfitted finite element, we refer to

[
33
,
34
]
.
For the discretization of the surface Stokes problem, we apply the trace finite element method
[
41
,
29
]
,
which uses traces of the bulk finite element functions. In a set of numerical experiments, we address
the dependence of the discretization error of the method on variations in the friction coefficient, ratio of bulk fluid viscosity, surface fluid viscosity, mesh size, and position of the interface relative to the fixed computational mesh.

One option to solve the coupled bulk-surface problem is to adopt
a monolithic approach. However, such an approach would quickly lead to
high computational costs as the mesh gets refined. Instead, we introduce a partitioned scheme based on fixed-point iterations
[
44
]
. With this algorithm,
the bulk and surface flow problems are solved separately and sequentially
and the coupling conditions are enforced in an iterative fashion. We choose this algorithm for its
simplicity of implementation and study its performance when model and discretization parameters vary. Our numerical experiments provide evidence of the robustness of the proposed approach with respect to the contrast in viscosity in the bulk fluid, surface fluid viscosity, and position of the interface relative to the background mesh. At the same time, convergence slowdown was observed for certain values of the slip coefficients.

The outline of the paper is as follows.
In Sec.
2
, we introduce the strong formulation of the coupled problem
and the associated energy balance. Sec.
3
presents
the strong and weak formulations of the simplified problem, together with the finite element discretization.
In Sec.
4
, we propose a partitioned algorithm for the numerical
solution of the coupled problem.
Numerical results in 3 dimensions are reported in Sec.
5
.
For all the simulations in this paper, we have used NGsolve
[
2
,
19
]
,
a high performance multiphysics finite element software with a Python interface,
and add-on library ngsxfem
[
3
]
.

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm div}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\pm}+\mathbf{f}^{\pm}

49

28

36

[

To introduce these equations, we need some further definitions.
Let
\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})^{T}

For a scalar function

and

are independent of the particular smooth extension of

and
\mathbf{U}
off

.
On

\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U}):=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}(\nabla\mathbf{U}+(\nabla\mathbf{U})^{T})\mathbf{P}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{U}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{U})^{T}).

(2.4)

The surface divergence for a vector
\mathbf{g}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
a tensor

{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{g}:={\rm tr}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\mathbf{g})

{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{A}:=\mbox{\small$\left({\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{A}),\,{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{e}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{A}),\,{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{e}_{3}^{T}\mathbf{A})\right)^{T}$ },
where

is the
i

With this notation, the surface Navier–Stokes equations take the form:

\displaystyle\rho_{\Gamma}\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\partial}_{t}\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\Gamma}\pi+2\mu_{\Gamma}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U})+\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}+\mathbf{b}^{e}+\pi\kappa\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.6)

where

Next, we turn to the coupling conditions between equations (

)–(

) posed in the bulk

First, the immiscibility condition means that the bulk fluid does not penetrate through

Normal velocity

In fluid vesicles and cells, typically a viscous and dense lipid membrane, represented by

, is

Thus, we consider Navier-type conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=f^{+}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}-\mathbf{U}_{T})

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=-f^{-}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}-\mathbf{U}_{T})

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

Conditions (

2.1
Energy balance of the continuous coupled problem

We look for the energy balance of the coupled system (
2.12
)–(
2.14
).
We make use of the following identities for time-dependent domains
\Omega_{\pm}(t)
, whose
only moving part of the boundary is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega_{\pm}(t)}|\mathbf{u}^{\pm}|^{2}\,dV

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\pm}(t)}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\cdot{}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}^{\pm}}{\partial t}\,dV+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma(t)}|\mathbf{u}^{\pm}|^{2}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{\pm}\,dS,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\pm}(t)}(\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{\pm}\,dV

Identity (

Above, we have used (

), (

), (

We repeat similar steps for the kinetic energy of the fluid in

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-2\mu^{-}\int_{\Omega_{-}}\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}^{-})\|^{2}\,dV-2\mu^{+}\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left\lVert\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}^{+})\right\rVert^{2}dV}_{\text{Bulk fluid viscous dissipation}}\underbrace{-2\mu_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\|\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U}_{T})\|^{2}\,dS}_{\text{Surface fluid viscous dissipation}}

3
A simplified steady problem

In this section, we consider a (strongly) simplified version of the problem presented in Sec.
2
. Our main assumption is that the coupled bulk and surface fluid system has reached a steady state and inertia terms can be neglected. Since the steady state implies
\Gamma(t)=\Gamma(0)
, we have
U_{N}=0
and hence
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{T}
.
This simplified surface–bulk Stokes problem models a viscosity dominated two-phase flow with the viscous interface in a dynamical equilibrium; see also Remark
1
. It is an interesting model problem for the purpose of numerical analysis.
With these simplifications, the equations (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) become:

\displaystyle-\mu^{\pm}\Delta\mathbf{u}^{\pm}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}^{\pm}\qquad\text{in }\Omega_{\pm},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm div}{}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=0\qquad~~\text{in }\Omega_{\pm}.

(3.2)

We impose a non-homogeneous Dirichlet condition on the entire outer boundary of
\Omega
, i.e. problem
(
3.1
)–(
3.2
) is supplemented with boundary condition (
2.13
) with
\mathbf{g}\in[H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{D})]^{3}
and

\partial\Omega_{D}=\partial\Omega
.
Under our assumption, the momentum equation for the surface fluid simplifies to

-2\mu_{\Gamma}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U}_{T})+\nabla_{\Gamma}\pi-\pi\kappa\mathbf{n}=[\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}]^{+}_{-}+\mathbf{b}^{e}.

The tangential part of the above momentum equation together with the inextensibility condition (
2.6
) leads to the surface Stokes problem

\displaystyle-2\mu_{\Gamma}\mathbf{P}{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U}_{T})+\nabla_{\Gamma}\pi

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}]^{+}_{-}+\mathbf{P}\mathbf{b}^{e}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{U}_{T}

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(3.4)

while the normal part simplifies to

[\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}]^{-}_{+}=\pi\kappa\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma.

(3.5)

The interface condition above is standard in many models of two-phase flows, where
\pi
has the meaning of the surface tension coefficient.

Coupling condition (
2.7
) is replaced by:

\mathbf{u}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{u}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma,

(3.6)

while conditions (

\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\pm}\mathbf{n}}=\pm f^{\pm}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{\pm}-\mathbf{U}_{T})\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma.

(3.7)

Finally, we will see that the (weak formulation of the) problem is well-posed under two mean conditions for the bulk pressure:

\int_{\Omega^{\pm}}p^{\pm}\,dx=0.

Remark 1
.

Since
\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
, condition (
3.6
) allows the flow
through the steady interface
\Gamma
. This is inconsistent with (
2.7
), which assumes
immiscibility of fluids. For a physically consistent formulation that describes the true equilibrium one has to set
\mathbf{u}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{u}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\Gamma
, but allow the shape of
\Gamma
to be the unknown, i.e. to be determined as a part of the problem. For such equilibrium to exist, external forces and boundary conditions may have to satisfy additional constraints. Finding such constraints
and solving the resulting non-linear problem is outside the scope of this paper. We rather follow a common convention in the analysis
of models for steady two-phase problems and allow (
3.6
) for the steady interface; see, e.g.
[
18
,
42
,
21
,
8
]
.

3.1
Variational formulation

The purpose of this section is to derive the variational formulation of coupled problem (
3.1
)–(
3.7
).
Let us introduce some standard notation. The space of functions whose square is integrable in a domain
\omega

is denoted by
L^{2}(\omega)
.
The space of functions whose distributional derivatives of order up to
m\geq 0
(integer)
belong to
L^{2}(\omega)
is denoted by
H^{m}(\omega)
.
The space of vector-valued functions with components in
L^{2}(\omega)
is denoted with
L^{2}(\omega)^{3}
.

H^{1}(\textrm{div}\ \!,\omega)
is the space of functions in
L^{2}(\omega)
with divergence in
L^{2}(\omega)
.
Moreover, we introduce the following functional spaces:

\displaystyle V^{-}

\displaystyle=H^{1}(\Omega_{-})^{3},~V^{+}=\{\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{+})^{3},\mathbf{u}{\big|_{\partial\Omega_{D}}}=\mathbf{g}\},~V^{+}_{0}=\{\mathbf{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{+})^{3},\mathbf{u}{\big|_{\partial\Omega_{D}}}=\boldsymbol{0}\},

\displaystyle V^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}^{-},\mathbf{u}^{+})\in V^{-}\times V^{+},\mathbf{u}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{u}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}~\text{on}~\Gamma\},

\displaystyle V^{\pm}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}^{-},\mathbf{u}^{+})\in V^{-}\times V^{+}_{0},\mathbf{u}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{u}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}~\text{on}~\Gamma\},

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\{p=(p^{-},p^{+})\in L^{2}(\Omega_{-})\times L^{2}(\Omega_{+}),~s.t.~\int_{\Omega^{\pm}}p^{\pm}\,dx=0\},

\displaystyle V_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{U}\in H^{1}(\Gamma)^{3}:\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\}.

The space
V^{\pm}
can be also characterized as
(V^{-}\times V^{+})\cap H^{1}(\textrm{div}\ \!,\Omega)
.
We use

and

to denote the

product and the duality pairing, respectively.

Multiplying (

) by

and (

\displaystyle-(p^{-},\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{v}^{-})_{\Omega_{-}}-(p^{+},\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{v}^{+})_{\Omega_{+}}+2(\mu^{-}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}^{-}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{-}))_{\Omega_{-}}+2(\mu^{+}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}^{+}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{+}))_{\Omega_{+}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\langle\pi\kappa,\mathbf{v}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rangle_{\Gamma}+\langle f^{-}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{-}\rangle_{\Gamma}+\langle f^{+}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle(\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{u}^{-},q^{-})_{\Omega_{-}}+(\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{u}^{+},q^{+})_{\Omega_{+}}=0

(3.9)

for all
(\mathbf{v},q)\in V^{\pm}_{0}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
The interface terms in (

) and (

\displaystyle-\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}\mathbf{n},\mathbf{v}^{-}\rangle_{\Gamma}+\langle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}\mathbf{n},\mathbf{v}^{+}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=-\langle\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}\mathbf{n},\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{-}\rangle_{\Gamma}+\langle\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}\mathbf{n},\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}\rangle_{\Gamma}-\langle[\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}]^{-}_{+},\mathbf{v}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\quad-\langle\pi\kappa,\mathbf{v}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rangle_{\Gamma}.

Likewise, we find that the surface velocity and pressure satisfy the following integral identities:

\displaystyle-(\pi,{\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{V})_{\Gamma}+2(\mu_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}))_{\Gamma}-\langle f^{-}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\langle f^{+}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\Gamma}=(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{b}^{e},\mathbf{V})_{\Gamma}

(3.10)

\displaystyle({\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{U},\tau)_{\Gamma}=0

(3.11)

for all
(\mathbf{V},\tau)\in V_{\Gamma}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
.

The weak formulation of the coupled problem (

)–(
3.7
) follows by combining (

)–(
3.9
) and (

)–(
3.11
). In order to write it,
we introduce the following forms for all

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{V}^{\pm}_{0}

\displaystyle a(\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}\},\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\})=

\displaystyle 2(\mu^{-}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}^{-}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{-}))_{\Omega_{-}}+2(\mu^{+}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}^{+}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{+}))_{\Omega_{+}}

\displaystyle+2(\mu_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}))_{\Gamma}+\langle f^{-}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{-}-\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle+\langle f^{+}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}-\mathbf{U}),\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\Gamma},

for all

Note that test and trial pressure spaces both involve two (different) gauge conditions.

3.2
Well-Posedness

With the goal of proving the well-posedness of the stationary problem,
we start by showing that
a(\{\cdot,\cdot\},\{\cdot,\cdot\})
is coercive.
Let
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm})}=\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{+}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{+})}+\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{-}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega_{-})}
.
Let
\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm})}^{2}=\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\Omega_{+}}^{2}+\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\Omega_{-}}^{2}
. We
define the following additional norms:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm})}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{2},\quad{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}=\left\lVert p\right\rVert^{2}_{\Omega_{\pm}}+\left\lVert\pi\right\rVert^{2}_{\Gamma}

The coercivity result is formulated in the form of a lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For any
\mathbf{v}\in V^{\pm}_{0}
and
\mathbf{V}\in V_{\Gamma}
it holds

a(\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\},\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\})\geq C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

(3.13)

with a positive constant
C
, which may depend on the viscosity values and
\Omega_{\pm}
.

Proof.

One readily computes that

a(\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\},\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\})=2(\mu^{-}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{-}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{-}))_{\Omega_{-}}+2(\mu^{+}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{+}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{+}))_{\Omega_{+}}\\
+2(\mu_{\Gamma}\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}),\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}))_{\Gamma}+f^{-}\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{-}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}^{2}+f^{+}\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}^{2}.

(3.14)

Since function
\mathbf{v}^{+}
satisfies homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{+}\setminus\Gamma
, we apply the following Korn’s inequality in
\Omega_{+}
:

\left\lVert\mathbf{v}^{+}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega_{+})}\leq C\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{+})\|_{\Omega_{+}}

(3.15)

By the triangle and trace inequalities in
\Gamma
, we get

\left\lVert\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\Gamma}\leq\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}+\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}\|_{\Gamma}\leq\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}+C\left\lVert\mathbf{v}^{+}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega_{+})}.

(3.16)

We further apply Korn’s inequality on
\Gamma

[
28
]
:

\left\lVert\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{\Gamma}+\|\mathbf{D}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V})\|_{\Gamma}\right).

(3.17)

Next, we can estimate the trace of
\mathbf{v}^{-}
on
\Gamma
through the triangle inequality:

\left\lVert\mathbf{v}^{-}\right\rVert_{\Gamma}\leq\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{-}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}+\|\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}\leq\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}^{-}-\mathbf{V}\|_{\Gamma}+\left\lVert\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}.

(3.18)

We finally apply the following Korn’s inequality in
\Omega_{-}
:

\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega_{-})}\leq C\left(\|\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}^{-})\|_{\Omega_{-}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{v}^{-}\right\rVert_{\Gamma}\right).

(3.19)

Identity (
3.14
) and inequalities (
3.15
)–(
3.19
) lead to (
3.13
) after easy computations.

∎

The continuity of the bilinear forms
a(\{\cdot,\cdot\},\{\cdot,\cdot\})
,
b(\{\cdot,\cdot\},\{\cdot,\cdot\})
and
s(\cdot,\cdot)
follows from standard arguments based on the Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle inequalities:

\begin{split}a(\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}\},\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\})\leq C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\quad\text{for all}~\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in V^{\pm}_{0},~\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in V_{\Gamma},\\
b(\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}\},\{p,\pi\})\leq C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\quad\text{for all}~\mathbf{u}\in V^{\pm}_{0},~\mathbf{U}\in V_{\Gamma},~p\in L^{2}(\Omega),~\pi\in L^{2}(\Gamma),\\
s(\mathbf{v},\pi)\leq C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},0\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{0,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\quad\text{for all}~\mathbf{v}\in V^{\pm},~\pi\in L^{2}(\Gamma).\end{split}

(3.20)

Problem (
3.12
) falls into the class of so-called generalized saddle point problems.
An abstract well-posedness result for such problems
can be found, e.g. in
[
5
,
37
]
, which
extend the Babuşka–Brezzi theory. Applied to (
3.12
), this well-posedness
result requires coercivity (
3.13
), continuity (
3.20
) and two inf-sup conditions formulated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

The following inf-sup conditions hold with positive constants
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in V^{\pm}_{0},\mathbf{V}\in V_{\Gamma}}\frac{b(\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi\})+s(\mathbf{v},\pi)}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}}\geq\gamma_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},\quad\forall~p\in L^{2}_{\pm},~\pi\in L^{2}(\Gamma),

(3.21)

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in V^{\pm}_{0},\mathbf{V}\in V_{\Gamma}}\frac{b(\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi\})}{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}}\geq\gamma_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},\quad\forall~p\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega),~\pi\in L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma).

Proof.

The proof follows by combining well-known results about the existence of a continuous right inverse of the divergence operator in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{3}

[
7
]
and
V_{\Gamma}

[
28
]
: For arbitrary

\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{3}

\begin{split}p=\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{v}~\text{~in}~\Omega,\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{v}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq c_{\Omega}\|p\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\\
\pi={\mathop{\,\rm div}}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{V}~\text{~on}~\Gamma,\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{V}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq c_{\Gamma}\|\pi\|_{\Gamma}.\end{split}

(3.23)

Letting

,
(\mathbf{v}^{-},\mathbf{v}^{+})^{T}\in V^{\pm}_{0}

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

This proves (

.

To show (
3.21

\pi_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)

\pi_{0}

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi_{0}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq b(\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi_{0}\})+s(\mathbf{v},\pi),\qquad{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq(c_{\Omega}+c_{\Gamma}){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi_{0}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

Let

Such

can be built, for example, as follows: Let
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{-}=\nabla\psi

from

[

]

\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{w}=C^{+}-\mathop{\rm div}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega^{+})

\|\mathbf{w}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega^{+})}\leq c_{\Omega^{+}}\|\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{+})}\leq C

[
7
]
. The desired

is given in

by
\mathbf{v}_{1}^{+}=\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{1}+\mathbf{w}
.
Since
\mathop{\rm div}\mathbf{v}_{1}=-C^{\pm}
, for

and
\pi\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
we have identities

(3.28)

We also note the equality
\|\pi^{\perp}\|_{\Gamma}^{2}=\hat{c}\,s(\mathbf{v}_{1},\pi^{\perp})

The denominator above is positive, since

&=b(\{\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi\})+s(\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\pi)-(\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\pi_{0})_{\Gamma}\\
&\leq b(\{\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi\})+s(\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\pi)+\frac{\beta^{2}\hat{c}^{2}}{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\|^{2}_{\Gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\|\pi_{0}\|^{2}_{\Gamma}\\
&\leq b(\{\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi\})+s(\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\pi)+c_{3}\,\beta^{2}\|\pi^{\perp}\|_{\Gamma}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\pi_{0}\|^{2}_{\Gamma}.\end{split}

with some
c_{3}>0
depending only on
\Gamma
and
\Omega
. For
\beta>0
sufficiently small such that

\frac{\beta}{2}-c_{3}\,\beta^{2}\geq 0
, we get

c{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leq b(\{\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{V}\},\{p,\pi\})+s(\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\pi),

(3.29)

with
c>0
depending only on
\Gamma
and
\Omega
.
Thanks to the triangle inequality, the second estimate in (
3.26
) and the definition of
\hat{c}
and
\mathbf{v}_{1}
, we find the bound

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{v}+\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{V}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{V}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+\beta\hat{c}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\beta\hat{c}\mathbf{v}_{1},0\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq(c_{\Omega}+c_{\Gamma}){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi_{0}\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}+C\,\|\pi^{\perp}\|_{\Gamma}\leq C\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\{p,\pi\}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

with
C>0
depending only on
\Gamma
and
\Omega
. The combination of the above bound and (
3.29
) completes
the proof of the lemma.  ∎

Finite element discretization

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

\Gamma
.
We consider a family of shape regular tetrahedral triangulations

of

We adopt the convention that the elements
T
and edges

Let

The set of elements intersecting

and the set of elements

(3.30)

respectively.
We assume

The domain formed by all tetrahedra in

We define the

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{h}^{\Gamma,\pm}=\{e=\text{int}(\partial T_{1}\cap\partial T_{2}):T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\pm}~\text{and}~T_{1}\cap\Gamma\neq\emptyset~\text{or}~T_{2}\cap\Gamma\neq\emptyset\}.

For the space discretization of the bulk fluid problems, we restrict our attention to inf-sup stable
finite element pair
\mathbf{P}_{k+1}-P_{k}

, i.e. Taylor-Hood elements. Specifically,
we consider the spaces of continuous finite element pressures given by:

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{-}=\{p\in C(\Omega_{h}^{-}):q|_{T}\in P_{k}(T)~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}^{-}_{h}\}.

Space
Q_{h}^{+}

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\pm}(\Omega)_{h}=\{p=(p^{-},p^{+})\in Q_{h}^{-}\times Q_{h}^{+}\,:\,\int_{\Omega^{-}}p^{-}=\int_{\Omega^{+}}p^{+}=0\},

Let

\displaystyle V_{h}^{-}=\{\mathbf{u}\in C(\Omega_{h}^{-})^{3}:\mathbf{u}|_{T}\in\mathbf{P}_{k+1}(T)~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}^{-}_{h}\},

V_{h}^{+}

\displaystyle V^{\pm}_{h}=\{\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}^{-},\mathbf{u}^{+})\in(V_{h}^{-}\times V_{h}^{+})\}.

Functions in
L^{2}_{\pm}(\Omega)
and
V^{\pm}_{h}
and their derivatives are multivalued in
\Omega^{\Gamma}_{h}
, the overlap of
\Omega_{h}^{-}

and
\Omega_{h}^{+}
. The jump of a multivalued function over the
interface is defined as the difference of components coming from
\Omega_{h}^{-}

and
\Omega_{h}^{+}
, i.e.
[\mathbf{u}]=\mathbf{u}^{-}-\mathbf{u}^{+}
on
\Gamma
. Note that this is the
jump that we have previously denoted with
[\cdot]^{-}_{+}
.
Moreover, we define the following averages:

\{\mathbf{u}\}=\alpha\mathbf{u}^{+}+\beta\mathbf{u}^{-},\quad\langle\mathbf{u}\rangle=\beta\mathbf{u}^{+}+\alpha\mathbf{u}^{-},

(3.33)

where

are weights to be chosen such that

.
For example, in
[
14

[
13

, the authors choose
\alpha=0
,

if

\alpha=1

Below, in (

\displaystyle[ab]=[b]\{a\}+\left\langle b\right\rangle[a].

(3.34)

For the discretization of the surface Stokes problem, we first consider the generalized Taylor–Hood bulk spaces in the strip
\Omega_{h}^{\Gamma}

\displaystyle V_{\Gamma,h}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{U}\in C(\Omega_{h}^{\Gamma})^{3}:\mathbf{U}|_{T}\in\mathbf{P}_{k+1}(T)~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}^{\Gamma}_{h}\},

\displaystyle Q_{\Gamma,h}

\displaystyle=\{\pi\in C(\Omega_{h}^{\Gamma}):\pi|_{T}\in P_{k}(T)~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}^{\Gamma}_{h}\},

Q_{\Gamma,h}^{0}=Q_{\Gamma,h}\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma)
.
In the trace finite element method, we use the traces of functions from
V_{\Gamma,h}
and
Q_{\Gamma,h}
on
\Gamma
. The inf-sup stability of the resulting trace FEM was analyzed in
[
41
]
for

and extended to higher order isoparametric trace elements in
[
29
]
.

In the treatment of the surface Stokes problem, one has to enforce the tangentiality condition

on

. In order to enforce it while avoiding locking, we follow

23
,
24
,
28

]

and add a penalty term to the weak formulation.

A discrete variational analogue of problem (

) reads:
Find
(\mathbf{u}_{h},p_{h})\in V^{\pm}_{h}\times L^{2}_{\pm}(\Omega)_{h}
, and
(\mathbf{U}_{h},\pi_{h})\in V_{\Gamma,h}\times Q_{\Gamma,h}
such that

\left\{\begin{split}a_{h}(\{\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{U}_{h}\},\{\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h}\})+b_{h}(\{\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h}\},\{p_{h},\pi_{h}\})+s_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h},\pi_{h})&=r_{h}(\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h})\\
b_{h}(\{\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{U}_{h}\},\{q_{h},\tau_{h}\})-b_{p}(p_{h},q_{h})-b_{s}(\pi_{h},\tau_{h})&=0\end{split}\right.

(3.35)

for all
(\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h})\in V_{0,h}^{\pm}\times Q_{h}^{\pm}
and
(\mathbf{V}_{h},\tau_{h})\in V_{\Gamma,h}\times Q_{\Gamma,h}^{0}
.
We define all the bilinear forms in (
3.35
) for all
\mathbf{u}_{h}\in V_{h}^{\pm}
,
\mathbf{v}_{h}\in V^{\pm}_{0,h}
,
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in V_{\Gamma,h}
,

,

. Let us start from form

\displaystyle a_{h}(\{\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{U}_{h}\},\{\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h}\})=

\displaystyle a_{i}(\{\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{U}_{h}\},\{\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h}\})+a_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})

\displaystyle+a_{p}(\{\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{U}_{h}\},\{\mathbf{v}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h}\})+a_{s}(\mathbf{U}_{h},\mathbf{V}_{h}),

(3.36)

where we group together the terms that arise from the integration by parts of the divergence of the stress tensors:

\displaystyle a_{i}(\{\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{U}_{h}\},\{\mathbf{v}_{h},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{h}\})=2(\mu^{-}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}_{h}^{-}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}_{h}^{-}))_{\Omega_{-}}+2(\mu^{+}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u}_{h}^{+}),\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}_{h}^{+}))_{\Omega_{+}}

\displaystyle a_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v}_{h})=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{h_{T}}\{\mu\}([\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}],[\mathbf{v}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}])_{\Gamma}-2\langle\{\mu\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}\},[\mathbf{u}_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}]\rangle_{\Gamma},

As for the parameters required by the discretization of the surface Stokes problem, we allow:

(3.44)

where

,

Stokes interface and surface Stokes problems. For the sake of brevity, we do not work out these details here, but will
present them in a follow-up paper.

4
A partitioned method for the coupled bulk-surface flow

For the solution of the coupled problem described in Sec.
3
we intend to use a partitioned strategy, i.e. each
sub-problem is solved separately and the coupling conditions are enforced in an iterative fashion.
Partitioned method are appealing for solving coupled problems because they allow to reuse existing solvers with minimal modifications.
In order to devise such a method for the simplified problem in Sec.
3
,
let us take a step back and look at the original problem (
2.12
).

Discretize problem (
2.12
) in time with, e.g., the Backward Euler method and consider the coupled problem at
a particular time
t=t^{n+1}
. Let
S_{b}
be the map that associates the jump in the normal stress across
the interface to any given surface flow velocity
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}_{T}+U_{N}\mathbf{n}
:

S_{b}(\mathbf{U})=[\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}]^{+}_{-}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}(\mathbf{u}^{+},p^{+})\mathbf{n}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}(\mathbf{u}^{-},p^{-})\mathbf{n}\quad\text{on}~\Gamma,

where
(\mathbf{u}^{+},p^{+})
and
(\mathbf{u}^{-},p^{-})
represent the solution of the two-phase time-discrete Navier-Stokes problem
at time
t
associated to (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) endowed with interface conditions (
2.7
), (
2.9
),
and (
2.10
). Moreover, let
S_{s}
be the operator associated to the surface flow such that to any given
surface flow velocity
\mathbf{U}
it associates the load
\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}
:

S_{s}(\mathbf{U})=\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}\quad\text{on}~\Gamma,

through the time-discrete surface Navier-Stokes problem
at time
t
associated to (
2.5
)–(
2.6
). Note that
S_{b}
and
S_{s}
are nonlinear and
their definitions can involve also forcing terms and, in the case of the bulk fluid problem, terms due to the boundary conditions.
For the surface operator, we can define
S^{-1}_{s}
as the map that associates the surface flow velocity
\mathbf{U}

to any given load
\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
.

With the above definitions, we can express the time discrete version of coupled problem (
2.12
)
in terms of the solution
\mathbf{U}
of a nonlinear equation defined only on
\Gamma
. This interface equation is usually
presented in one of three formulations that are equivalent from the mathematical point of view, but give rise to different
iterative algorithms. The first and perhaps most used formulation is the fixed-point one: Find
\mathbf{U}
such that

S_{s}^{-1}(S_{b}(\mathbf{U}))=\mathbf{U}\quad\text{on}~\Gamma.

(4.1)

The second formulation is a slight modification of (
4.1
), which lends itself to
a Newton iterative method: Find
\mathbf{U}
such that

S_{s}^{-1}(S_{b}(\mathbf{U}))-\mathbf{U}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on}~\Gamma.

The third approach is given by the Steklov-Poincaré equation: Find
\mathbf{U}
such that

S_{b}(\mathbf{U})-S_{s}(\mathbf{U})=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on}~\Gamma.

See, e.g.,
[
44
]
for more details on these three formulations.

A standard algorithm for eq. (
4.1
) uses fixed-point iterations: Given
\mathbf{U}^{k}
, compute

\mathbf{U}^{k+1}=\mathbf{U}^{k}+\omega^{k}(\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{k}-\mathbf{U}^{k})\quad\text{with}~\overline{\mathbf{U}}^{k}=S_{s}^{-1}(S_{b}(\mathbf{U}^{k})).

(4.2)

The choice of the relaxation parameter
\omega^{k}
determines the efficiency of the algorithm or it might be crucial for convergence
in certain ranges of the physical parameters. An effective strategy for setting
\omega^{k}
is the Aitken’s acceleration method.

For simplicity, we present algorithm (
4.2
) applied to the time discrete version of coupled problem (
2.12
)
with
\omega^{k}=1
for all
k
(i.e., no relaxation). At time
t=t^{n+1}
, assuming that
\mathbf{U}^{k}
is known, perform the following steps:

-

Step 1
: solve the two-phase time-discrete Navier-Stokes problem
at time
t
associated to (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) for the bulk flow variables
(\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1},p^{-}_{k+1})
and
(\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1},p^{+}_{k+1})

with interface conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=U_{N}^{k}=\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=f^{+}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1}-\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k})

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=-f^{-}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1}-\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k})

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma.

-

Step 2
: solve the time-discrete surface Navier-Stokes problem
at time
t
associated to (
2.5
)–(
2.6
) for variables
(\mathbf{U}^{k+1},\pi^{k+1})

with interface condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

-

Step 3
: Check the stopping criterion

\displaystyle||\mathbf{U}^{k+1}-\mathbf{U}^{k}||_{\Gamma}<\epsilon||\mathbf{U}^{k}||_{\Gamma},

where
\epsilon
is a given stopping tolerance.

Notice that the bulk and surface flow problems are solved separately and sequentially. In general, this algorithm is easy
to implement but convergence could be slow in certain ranges of the physical parameters and require relaxation for speed-up.

The above algorithm adapted to simplified problem (
3.1
)–(
3.7
),
reads as follows. At iteration
k+1
, assuming that
(\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k},\pi^{k})
are known, perform the following steps:

-

Step 1
: solve two-phase problem (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) for the bulk flow variables
(\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1},p^{-}_{k+1})
and
(\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1},p^{+}_{k+1})

with interface conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=f^{+}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1}-\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k})

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle=-f^{-}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1}-\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k})

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}\right]^{-}_{+}

\displaystyle=\pi^{k}\kappa

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma.

(4.6)

-

Step 2
: solve surface flow problem (
3.3
)–(
3.4
) for variables
(\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k+1},\pi^{k+1})

with interface condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=[\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k+1}\mathbf{n}]^{+}_{-}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

(4.7)

-

Step 3
: Check the stopping criterion

\displaystyle||\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k+1}-\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k}||_{\Gamma}<\epsilon||\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k}||_{\Gamma}.

(4.8)

Notice that only interface condition (
4.4
)–(
4.7
) are coupling conditions for bulk and surface flows.
If one was to compute the load exerted on the surface fluid in (
4.7
) directly from the solution of the
problem at Step 1, the overall accuracy of the method would be spoiled. Instead, one can compute

\mathbf{P}\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}^{k+1}
by plugging (
4.4
)–(
4.5
) into (
4.7
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f^{+}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1}+f^{-}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1}-(f^{+}+f^{-})\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

However, we prefer to use a more implicit version of the above condition:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\mathbf{f}_{\Gamma}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=f^{+}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{+}_{k+1}+f^{-}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}^{-}_{k+1}-(f^{+}+f^{-})\mathbf{U}_{T}^{k+1}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

since it could help have a better control of approximate rigid rotations (Killing vector fields).

5
Numerical results

The aim of the numerical results collected in this section is to provide evidence of the robustness of the proposed finite element approach
with respect to the contrast in viscosity in the bulk fluid, surface fluid viscosity, value of the slip coefficients, and position of the interface relative to the fixed computational mesh.

For the averages in (

\alpha=0
