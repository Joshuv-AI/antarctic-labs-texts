Hydrodynamic Limit for Spatially Structured Interacting Neurons
Aline Duarte, Guilherme Ost, Andrés A. Rodríguez

Abstract
We study the hydrodynamic limit of a stochastic system of neurons whose interactions are not of mean-field type and are produced by chemical and electrical synapses, and leak currents. The system consists of ε − 2 \varepsilon^{-2} neurons embedded in [ 0 , 1 ) 2 [0,1)^{2} , each spiking randomly according to a point process with rate depending on both its membrane potential and position. When neuron i i spikes, its membrane potential is reset to 0 0 while the membrane potential of j j is increased by a positive value ε 2 ​ a ​ ( i , j ) \varepsilon^{2}a(i,j) , if i i influences j j . Furthermore, between consecutive spikes, the system follows a deterministic motion due both to electrical synapses and leak currents. The electrical synapses are involved in the synchronization of the membrane potentials of the neurons, while the leak currents inhibit the activity of all neurons, attracting simultaneously their membrane potentials to 0. We show that the empirical distribution of the membrane potentials converges, as ε \varepsilon vanishes, to a probability density ρ t ​ ( u , r ) \rho_{t}(u,r) which is proved to obey a non linear PDE of Hyperbolic type.

Introduction

In this paper we present a stochastic process which describes a population of spatially structured interacting neurons. Our aim is to study the hydrodynamical limit of such process and characterize its limit law as well. Despite of its own interest in mathematics, the analysis of hydrodynamical behavior of neuronal systems is an important issue in neurobiology. For instance, the most common imaging techniques, including EEG and fMRI, do not measure individual neuron activity but rather a resulting effect driven by interactions of large subpopulations of neurons. Thus, the rigorous mathematical modeling of EEG and fMRI data requires a collective description (a “macroscopic equation”) derived from many interacting neurons (“large microscopic systems”), a typical setting of study on hydrodynamical limits of stochastic particle systems.

In a nutshell, neurons are electrically excitable cells whose activity consist in sudden peaks, called action potentials and often referred to as
spikes
. More specifically, spikes are short-lasting electrical pulses in the membrane potential of the cell and the higher the membrane potential the higher the probability of a spike to occur.
Thus, it is quite natural to assume that the generating mechanism of spikes is given by a point process in which the spiking rate of a given neuron depends on its membrane potential.
In this paper, we work under that assumption and additionally, assume that the membrane potential evolves under the effect of chemical and electrical synapses, and leak currents.

Electrical synapses
are due to so-called gap-junction channels between neurons which induce a constant sharing of potential. The unique aspect of electrical synapses is their reciprocity. This means they are neither excitatory nor inhibitory but rather synchronizer. For each pair of neurons
(i,j)
, we modulate this synchronizing strength by
b(i,j)
, where
(i,j)\mapsto b(i,j)
is a nonnegative symmetric function. For instance, if
N
is the size of the set of neurons and
b(i,j)=N^{-1}
for
i\neq j
and
b(i,i)=0
, the electrical synapses would push the membrane potential of each neuron to the average membrane potential of the system. In the general case, the membrane potential of each neuron is also attracted to a mean value, although this value may vary for each neuron depending on the shape of the function
b(i,j).

In contrast with electrical synapses,
chemical synapses
are point events which can be described as follows. Each neuron
i
with membrane potential
\mathrm{U}
spikes randomly
at rate
\varphi(\mathrm{U},i)
, where
\mathrm{U}\mapsto\varphi(\mathrm{U},i)
is a non decreasing function, positive at
\mathrm{U}>0
and vanishing at
0
. This last assumption implies the absence of external stimuli.
When neuron
i
spikes, its membrane potential is immediately reset to a resting potential
0
. Simultaneously, the neurons which are influenced by neuron
i
receive an additional positive value to their membrane potential. Specifically, the membrane potential of neuron
j
is increased by the value
a(i,j)
in each spike of
i
, if the latter influences the former. The positiveness of the function
(i,j)\mapsto a(i,j)
means that all chemical synapses are of the excitatory type.

Additionally to the synapses, neurons loose potential to the environment along time due to leakage channels which pushes down the membrane potential of each neuron toward the resting state.
This constant outgoing flow of potential is referred to, in the neurobiological literature, as
leak currents.
For an account on these subjects we refer the reader to
[
9
]
.

In the study of Hydrodynamic limits a
mean-field
type assumption is quite frequent. This means that
a(i,j)=b(i,j)=N^{-1}
for any pair of neurons
(i,j),
with
N
being the size of the population of neurons.
For recent neuromathematical models adopting the mean-field assumption see, among others, the models in
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
. However, a more realistic description should incorporate the mutual distance among neurons. In order to achieve such accurate description, we use Kac potentials ideas
and techniques developed for such potentials in statistical mechanics. In our context, this means that the functions

and

considered here are quite general but are scaled by factor

, if

For an account on hydrodynamic limits and Kac potentials we refer respectively to

-
[

and
[

]

To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that stochastic modeling of spatially structured neuronal networks whose occurrences of spikes are described by Poisson processes has been addressed. Most of the mathematical models of neuronal system taking into account also spatial locations have been done with Brownian random components, see for instance
[
15

For each
\varepsilon>0,
the set of neurons is denoted by
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap[0,1)^{2}
and the state of our system at time

, with
\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For each neuron

and time

represents the membrane potential of neuron

at time

, to a law having, at each time

The strategy for proving this theorem can be described in the following way. We identify the process with its empirical distribution and, as a first step, we show that the sequence of laws of the empirical distributions is tight.
Once tightness is proven, we identify the limiting law as supported by the solutions of the PDE by a coupling argument. Specifically, we first approximate the true process by a discrete space and time family of processes
Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}
for which the analysis of the Hydrodynamic limit is somehow easier.
Once established the convergence to
Y^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}
, we obtain the result by taking
\delta,\ell,E,\tau\to 0.
A similar approach was recently used in
[
3
]
, however, in the present work, we generalize their approach to the case of spatially structured interacting neurons. Finally,
we show the solutions of the PDE are unique to get full convergence.

Proof.

Indeed, for any test function

The criterion requires the existence of a positive constant

By its definition,

\gamma_{t}^{(\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon^{4}\sum_{j}\sum_{i\neq j}\varphi(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{i}(t),i)a(j,i)+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j}\varphi(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)\phi(0,j)\\
-\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j}\varphi(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)\phi(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)-\alpha\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j}\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t)\\
-\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j}\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)\lambda_{j}[\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t)-\bar{\mathrm{U}}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t)]+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\hskip 56.9055pt

with

O(\varepsilon^{2}):=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j}\sum_{i\neq j}\varphi(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{j}(t),j)\left[\phi\left(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{i}(t)+\varepsilon^{2}a(j,i),i\right)-\phi(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{i}(t),i)-\varepsilon^{2}a(j,i)\phi^{\prime}(\mathrm{U}^{(\varepsilon)}_{i}(t),i)\right].

Now, Assumption

\mathcal{L}=L_{\rm fire}+L_{(\alpha+\lambda)}

The equality above is directly verified. Thus, it follows that

5.2
Consequences of the Coupling Algorithm

The Theorem
4
is the main result of this section. It states that typically the difference of the potentials
\Delta_{i}(n)=|\mathrm{U}(n\delta)(i)-Y^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(n\delta)(i)|
is small (proportionally to
\delta
). In addition, it claims that the proportion of neurons having large values of
\Delta_{i}(n)
is also small (again proportional to
\delta
).

Definition 5
.

A label
i\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
is called “good at time
k\delta
” if for all
n=1,\ldots,k
the following is true:

(i)

Either
\xi^{1}_{i}
rings first and
\xi_{i}
does not ring on interval
[(n-1)\delta,n\delta];

(ii)

or neither
\xi^{1}_{i}
nor
\xi^{2}_{i}
ring on the interval
[(n-1)\delta,n\delta].

We denote by
\mathcal{G}_{n}
the set of good labels at time
n\delta
and
\mathcal{B}_{n}=\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
the set of bad labels.
For
i\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
we set
\Delta_{i}(n):=|\mathrm{U}(n\delta)(i)-Y^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(n\delta)(i)|
so that the maximum distance between the membrane potential of the true and auxiliary process, for the good labels, is

\theta_{n}=\max\{\Delta_{i}(k),\,i\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\,,k\leq n\}.

We now enunciate the Theorem
4
. Its proof is postponed to the Appendix
9
.

Theorem 4
.

Grant Assumption
3
, for any given
T>0,

there exist
\delta_{0}>0
and a constant
C
depending on
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}

and on
T
such that for all
\delta\leq\delta_{0},

\theta_{n}\leq C\delta\quad\mbox{ and}\quad\varepsilon^{2}|\mathcal{B}_{n}|\leq C\delta\quad\mbox{ for all $n$ such that $n\delta\leq T,$ }

with probability
\geq 1-c_{1}\delta^{-1}e^{-c_{2}\epsilon^{-2}\delta^{4}}.
The constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
do not depend on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
.

For any test function
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
and
n\geq 1
, we write

\nu^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n\delta}(\phi)=\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}\phi(Y^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{i}(n\delta),i_{m}).

As a by product of Theorem
4
, we obtain an upper bound for the
L_{1}
- distance between the variables
\mu_{n\delta}(\phi)
and
\nu^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n\delta}(\phi)
, for each test function
\phi\in\mathcal{S}.
This result will be used, in section
7
, in the analysis of the Hydrodynamic for
\mathrm{U}
. Let

\mathcal{T}=\Big\{t\in[0,T]:t=n2^{-q}T,n,q\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

(5.10)

Remember that
P^{(\varepsilon)}_{u}
denotes the law under which the true process
U^{(\varepsilon)}(t)
satisfies the condition
\mathrm{U}(0)=u.
We write
\tilde{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{u}
to denote the law under which the process
Y^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(\cdot)
satisfies
\Phi_{0}(u)=(\Phi_{0}(u_{i}),i\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon})
and write
Q^{(\varepsilon)}_{u}
to denote the joint law of the true and auxiliary processes induced by coupling algorithm provided above. We shall denote the associated expectations by
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{u}
and
\tilde{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{u}
, and, by abuse of notation, the joint expectation by
Q^{(\varepsilon)}_{u}.

Proposition 3
.

Take
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\delta\in\{2^{-q}T,q\in\mathbb{N}\}
and let

and fix

.
Then, there exists a constant

, not depending on
\delta
, such that

Q_{u}^{(\varepsilon)}\left[\big|\mu_{t}(\phi)-\nu^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{t}(\phi)\big|\right]\leq C||\varphi||_{\rm Lip}\Big(\frac{e^{-C\epsilon^{-2}\delta^{4}}}{\delta}+\delta\Big).

(5.11)

The proof is given in Appendix
10
. Next, in section

, we study the hydrodynamic limit for the approximating process and, in section

2
and

.

6
Hydrodynamic Limit for the Auxiliary Process

In this section, we initially describe the random evolution of the membrane potentials in the auxiliary process. Next, we define a deterministic version of this evolution taking into account the average behaviour of the auxiliary process in each time interval
\big[n\delta,(n+1)\delta\big).

Beside, we also consider the random variables which compute the number of neurons of the auxiliary process in a given square with a given potential and, from the dynamics of these variables, we define a second deterministic evolution.
The main theorem of this section, Theorem
5
, states that both the random potentials and the counting variables becomes deterministic as
\varepsilon\to 0
and they are described respectively by the first and second deterministic evolutions.

In the remaining of the section these deterministic evolutions will be used to define the hydrodynamic evolution for the auxiliary processes.
When necessary
we shall stress the dependence both on
\varepsilon
and
n
writing
Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}
,
N^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h)
,
\bar{y}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m)
,
S^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h)
and
S^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}\big(m,\delta).

6.1
Hydrodynamic Evolution of the Auxiliary Process

Throughout the subsection the parameters
\delta,\ell,E,\tau
are kept fixed, so that we omit the superscript in all variables considered below. In what follows we work in
C_{m}
and doing so we drop also the dependency on
m
unless some confusion may arise.

We denote by
\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}
the random set of potentials which the auxiliary process (restricted to
C_{m}
) assume at time
n\delta
. By (
5.2
), we have
\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}=\{E^{(\varepsilon)}_{0,k}:k=1\ldots,R_{0}E^{-1}\}
where we set
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{0,k}=E_{k}.

At time
\delta
,
the potential of neurons which spike in the
J_{h}=\big[\delta-h\tau,\delta-(h-1)\tau\big)
, independently of their initial membrane potentials, will be a value
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,h}\in\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{1}.
By (
5.9
), we immediately see that

E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,h}=\Phi_{(h-1)\tau,\bar{y}^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}(m)}(0)+S^{(\varepsilon)}_{1}(m,h),\ h=1,\ldots,\delta\tau^{-1}.

(6.1)

On the other hand, at time
\delta
, the membrane potential of those neurons which initially had potential
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{0,k}
and do not spike will be a value
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}\in\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{1}.
Recalling (
5.8
), it is readily verified that

E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}=\Phi_{\delta,\bar{y}^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}(m)}\Big(E^{(\varepsilon)}_{0,k}\Big)+S^{(\varepsilon)}_{1}(m,\delta),\ k=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}|

(6.2)

where
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{0,k}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
.
Thus, we may split the elements of the finite set
\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{1}
into two groups. The first group consists of those potentials satisfying (
6.2
), reached for only by neurons which do not spike in
[0,\delta)
. On the other hand, due to spikes of neurons in the interval
[0,\delta)
some potentials are “created” at time
\delta
. This leads to the second group of potentials, those satisfying (
6.1
). Moreover, the following chain of inequalities holds

0=E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,1}<\ldots<E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,\delta\tau^{-1}}<E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,1+\delta\tau^{-1}}<\ldots<E^{(\varepsilon)}_{1,R_{0}E^{-1}+\delta\tau^{-1}}.

Iterating the argument above, for each
n\delta\leq T
, we may also split the elements of the set
\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}
into two groups. Those potentials belonging to the first group satisfy

E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}=\Phi_{\delta,\bar{y}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m)}\Big(E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}\Big)+S^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,\delta),\ k=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}|,

(6.3)

where
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}\in\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}
,
while the potentials of the second group satisfy

E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,h}=\Phi_{(h-1)\tau,\bar{y}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m)}(0)+S^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h),\ h=1,\ldots,\delta\tau^{-1}.

(6.4)

From our definitions, we have also that

0=E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,1}<\ldots<E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,\delta\tau^{-1}}<E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,1+\delta\tau^{-1}}<\ldots<E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,R_{0}E^{-1}+n\delta\tau^{-1}}.

Now, writing

e^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m)=\tilde{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(0)}\big[\bar{Y}^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{i_{m}}(n\delta)\big],\ 1\leq m\leq\ell^{2},\ n\delta\leq T,

(6.5)

to denote the expected value of the local average membrane potential
\bar{Y}^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{i_{m}}
at time
n\delta
, we set
\mathcal{D}^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}=\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}
and then recursively define for
k=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}|,

D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}:=\Phi_{\delta,e^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m)}\big(D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}\big)+\tilde{E}_{Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(0)}^{(\varepsilon)}\big[S^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,\delta)\big],\ \mbox{with}\ D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}\in\mathcal{D}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1},

(6.6)

D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,h}:=\Phi_{(h-1)\tau,e^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m)}(0)+\tilde{E}_{Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(0)}^{(\varepsilon)}\Big[S^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h)\Big],\ h=1,\ldots,\delta\tau^{-1}.

(6.7)

Given
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}\in\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}
, we write
\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k)
to denote the number of neurons of
Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}
, in
C_{m}
, with membrane potential
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
at time
n\delta
.
Finally, we write

\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}(m,k)=\tilde{E}_{Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(0)}^{(\varepsilon)}\Big[\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}(m,k)\Big],

to denote the expected number of neurons of the
Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}
in the square
C_{m}
whose potential at time
0
is
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{0,k}
, and iteratively we set

\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k+\delta\tau^{-1})=\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m,k)e^{-\delta\varphi\big(D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)},\ D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}\in\mathcal{D}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1},

(6.8)

and for
h=1,\ldots\delta\tau^{-1}
,

\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h)=\sum\limits_{k}\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m,k)\Big(e^{-(\delta-h\tau)\varphi\big(D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}-e^{-(\delta-(h-1)\tau)\varphi\big(D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}\Big).

(6.9)

Suppose we have computed the number neurons of
Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}
, in
C_{m}
, with a given potential
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}
. Then, the probability of a neuron with such potential does not spike in the interval
[0,\delta)
is exactly
e^{-\delta\varphi\big(E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}.
Thus, we expect that the number of neurons having potential
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
at the next step satisfies

\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k+\delta\tau^{-1})\approx\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1}(m,k)e^{-\delta\varphi\big(E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}.

This relation explains (
6.8
). Similarly, we notice that the expected fraction of those neurons having potential
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k}
, which spike in the interval
J_{h}=\big[\delta-h\tau,\delta-(h-1)\tau\big)
is precisely

\eta_{n}(m,k)\Big(e^{-(\delta-h\tau)\varphi\big(E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}-e^{-(\delta-(h-1)\tau)\varphi\big(E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}\Big).

Then, summing over
k
we get the random version of (
6.9
).

We shall show that the random membrane potentials
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
are close
(proportionally to
\varepsilon^{1/2}
)
to the deterministic values
D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
define above. Furthermore, it will be shown that the
collection of counting variables

\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k)
are close to the values
\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k)
. Here, close means again to be proportional to
\varepsilon^{1/2}.

Theorem 5
.

There exist positive constants
C
,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, not depending on
\varepsilon
such that
for all
n
with
0\leq n\delta\leq T
,
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}\in\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}
and
D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}\in\mathcal{D}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n},

\big|E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}-D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}\big|\leq C\varepsilon^{1/2},\quad\varepsilon^{2}\big|\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k+\delta\tau^{-1})-\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,k+\delta\tau^{-1})\big|\leq E\ell^{2}\varepsilon^{1/2}

for
k=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{E}^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}|
and

\varepsilon^{2}\Big|\eta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h)-\zeta^{(\varepsilon)}_{n}(m,h)\Big|\leq\tau\ell^{2}\varepsilon^{1/2},h=1,\ldots,\delta\tau^{-1},

with probability
\geq 1-c_{1}e^{c_{2}\varepsilon^{-1}}.

The proof is given in the Appendix
11
.

Remark 4
.

The constant
C,c_{1}
and
c_{2}
given in the Theorem
5
, which does depend on
\varepsilon
, turns out to have a bad dependency on the parameters
\delta,\ell,E
and
\tau
. However all these parameters are fixed in this section, so that the Theorem
5
implies that both
E^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
and
D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
, as well as
\varepsilon^{2}\eta_{n}(m,k)
and
\varepsilon^{2}\zeta_{n}(m,k)
are close to each other as
\varepsilon\to 0
(keeping
\delta,\ell,E,\tau
fixed).

6.2
The Limit Trajectory of the Auxiliary Process

As a consequence of the Theorem
5
we shall prove that the law of
\nu_{n\delta}
converges in the Hydrodynamic limit to a limit law denote by
\rho^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n\delta}(u,r)
to be defined below. The limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of
D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}
and
\zeta_{n,m}\big(D^{(\varepsilon)}_{n,k}\big)
will appear in its definition. In what follows we make explicit the dependence on
\delta,\ell,E,\tau
writing
D^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k}
,
\zeta^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,k)
,
e^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m),

S^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,\delta)
and
S^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,h.)

We set for each
1\leq k\leq R_{0}E^{-1}
and
1\leq m\leq\ell^{-2}
,

\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0}(m,k):=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0}(m,k),\ I_{0,k}=I_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{E}.

By Assumption
2
this limit exits and it is equal to
\int_{C_{m}}\int_{I_{k}}\psi_{0}(u,i_{m})du.

The value
\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0}(m,k)
has the nice probabilistic meaning of being
the limit fraction of neurons, inside
C_{m}
, whose membrane potential is
D^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0,k}=D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0,k}=E_{k}.

The function
\rho^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0}(u,r)
is then obtained by distributing the number
\zeta_{0,m}\big(D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0,k}\big)
uniformly over the rectangle
I_{0,k}\times C_{m}:

\rho^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0}(u,r):=\frac{\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{0}(m,k)}{E\ell^{2}},\ \ (u,r)\in I_{0,k}\times C_{m}.

(6.10)

We now give its definition at a general step
n\delta
. We first compute the limit potentials
D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{1,h}
and
D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}
. Taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of in the expressions (
6.6
) and (
6.7
), it follows that

D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}=\Phi_{\delta,e^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1}(m)}\Big(D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1,k}\Big)+s^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m),

(6.11)

D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,h}=\Phi_{(h-1)\tau,e^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1}(m)}(0)+s^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau,h)}_{n}(m),

(6.12)

where for each
n\geq 0
, the functions
e^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m)
,
s^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m)
and
s^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau,h)}_{n}(m)
are obtained by letting
\varepsilon\to 0:

e^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}e^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m),

(6.13)

s^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{E}_{Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(0)}^{(\varepsilon)}\big[S^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,\delta)\big],

(6.14)

s^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau,h)}_{n}(m)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{E}_{Y^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}(0)}^{(\varepsilon)}\big[S^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,h)\big].

(6.15)

We need also to compute the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of the numbers
\zeta^{(\varepsilon,\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,k).
By letting
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
6.8
), it is clear that

\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,k+\delta\tau^{-1})=\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1}(m,k)e^{-\delta\varphi\Big(D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\Big)}.

(6.16)

Similarly, sending
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
6.9
), we have that

\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,h)=\sum\limits_{k}\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1}(m,k)\Big(e^{-(h-1)\tau\varphi\big(D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}-e^{-h\tau\varphi\big(D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1,k},i_{m}\big)}\Big).

(6.17)

Now, consider the set of intervals
\mathcal{I}^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k}=\Big\{I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k}\Big\}
where for
h=1,\ldots,\delta\tau^{-1}
, the intervals are of the form

I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,h}=\Big[D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,h},D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,h+1}\Big),

(6.18)

while for
k=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1}|,

I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}
is the interval having center in the value
D^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}
whose length satisfies

|I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}|=e^{-(\alpha+\lambda_{m})\delta}|I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n-1,k+\delta\tau^{-1}}|.

(6.19)

Finally, we set

\rho^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n\delta}(u,r)=\frac{\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,k)}{|I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k}|\ell^{2}},\ (u,r)\in I^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n,k}\times C_{m}.

(6.20)

Notice that
\rho^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n\delta}(u,r)
is obtained by distributing the number
\zeta^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n}(m,k)
uniformly over the rectangle

.
Furthermore, for all

, the function

is a probability density on

, i.e,

1=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{(\delta,\ell,E,\tau)}_{n\delta}(u,r)du.

(6.21)

,

Appendix 4: proof of Theorem 2 for general firing rates

The proof is analogous to the proof presented in Appendix 4 of
[
3
]
. For sake of completeness we shall give it here.

Let
\varphi,R,T
and
C
as in the statement of Theorem
1
and take
\phi
be any bounded continuous functions on
D\big([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\big).
We have to show that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi)=\phi(\rho).

Let
A
be the set
A=\{||U^{(\varepsilon)}(t)||\leq C,t\in[0,T]\}.
Theorem
1
implies that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\big|\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi)-\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi 1_{A})\big|=0.

(12.1)

Now, consider
\mathcal{P}^{(*,\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}
the distribution of the process with a spiking rate
\varphi^{*}(\cdot,\cdot)
which fulfils the Assumption
3
and it is equal to
\varphi
for
u\leq C
. By definition, it follows that

\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi 1_{A})=\mathcal{P}^{(*,\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi 1_{A}).

(12.2)

Having proved Theorem
2
under the Assumption
3
, we get the desired convergence to a limit density
\rho^{*}=(\rho^{*}_{t}dudr)_{t\in[0,T]}
, for the process whose spiking rate is
\varphi^{*}
.
It follows then, from (
12.1
) and (
12.2
), that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi)=\psi(\rho^{*}1_{A}).

We claim that
\rho^{*}=\rho^{*}1_{A}
. Indeed, by considering
\phi(w)=\sup\{w_{t}(1),t\leq T\}\wedge 1,

we immediately see that
1=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi)=\phi(\rho^{*}1_{A}).
This last equalty implies that
\rho^{*}
have support in
[0,C].
As a consequence,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{P}^{(\varepsilon)}_{[0,T]}(\phi)=\phi(\rho^{*}1_{A})=\phi(\rho^{*}),

which concludes the proof of the Theorem.
