Leptonic CP violation from the seesaw
C. C. Nishi, J. I. Silva-Marcos

Abstract
The seesaw extension of the SM explains the tiny neutrino masses and it is accompanied by many CP violating phases. We study the case where all leptonic CP violation arises from the soft breaking in the heavy Majorana mass matrix. Parameter counting reveals that one less parameter is needed to describe this case. This reduction leads to restrictions on the parameter space of heavy neutrinos. We analyze the minimal seesaw case in detail and find that mass degeneracy of heavy neutrinos is not possible for certain values of the CP phases.



I
Introduction

After the discovery that neutrinos have tiny masses and mix, one of our next major goals is to determine the existence of leptonic CP violation.
Hints for CP violating values of the Dirac CP phase
\delta
—analogous to the CP odd phase of the CKM mixing matrix—
is already appearing in neutrino oscillations

Large planned experiments such as DUNE

The existence of leptonic CP violation at low energy may be linked to the fundamental question of the origin of the matter-antimatter asymmetry of the universe which may be attributed without much effort to a leptonic CP violation at the high scale generated by the decay of the same heavy righthanded neutrinos

It is indeed possible to have successful leptogenesis sourced by low energy CP violation, either from the Dirac or Majorana phases in the PMNS, specially in the flavored regime

;

If we consider the usual case of the seesaw with three righthanded neutrinos (RHNs), the theory possesses 21 physical parameters, of which six are physical CP odd phases in the usual counting.
Three reside in the PMNS matrix: one Dirac CP phase and two Majorana phases.
The other three may be accessed through processes involving the RHNs at high energy or, in EFT language, in the suppressed lepton number conserving dimension six operator

.
In the Casas-Ibarra parametrization

It is straightforward to attribute this soft breaking to scalars breaking CP spontaneously.

Our study may have implications for scenarios where CP is in fact an exact symmetry of nature at high energies and CP violation at the low scale is spontaneous.

No restriction on the PMNS and simple restrictions

In Sec.

However, once we

13

v^{2}\lambda_{R}^{\dagger}\lambda_{R}=V\hat{M}_{\nu}^{1/2}R^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{R}R\hat{M}_{\nu}^{1/2}V^{\dagger}\sim\text{real}\,,

(28)

modulo rephasing.
This means that the imaginary part of
R
needs to somehow cancel the imaginary part of the PMNS
V
. This constraint is hard to implement.

As a final discussion of this section, let us consider the simple consequences on the CP asymmetry responsible for leptogenesis.
Let us write the flavored CP asymmetry in the decay
N_{1}\to\ell_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}

cp.asym
:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\neq 1}\frac{\im(\lambda^{\dagger}_{\alpha 1}(\lambda\lambda^{\dagger})_{1k}\lambda_{k\alpha})}{8\pi(\lambda\lambda^{\dagger})_{11}}\frac{1}{1-x_{k}}

(29)

\displaystyle-\ \sum_{k\neq 1}\frac{\im(\lambda^{\dagger}_{\alpha 1}(\lambda\lambda^{\dagger})_{k1}\lambda_{k\alpha})}{8\pi(\lambda\lambda^{\dagger})_{11}}\sqrt{x_{k}}\left[\frac{1}{1-x_{k}}+1-(1+x_{k})\ln\Big(1+\frac{1}{x_{k}}\Big)\right]\,,

where
x_{k}=M_{k}^{2}/M_{1}^{2}
.
In these expressions
\lambda
means
\lambda_{R}
.
The unflavored CP asymmetry is obtained from (
29
) by summing in
\alpha
.
Then the first line in (
29
), which conserves total lepton number, vanishes.
The second line has the imaginary part dependent only on
\im[(\lambda_{R}\lambda_{R}^{\dagger})_{k1}]^{2}
.
Then, given the structure
\lambda_{R}=U_{R}^{\dagger}\hat{\lambda}U_{e}
in the generic CPV case, cf. (
16
),
or
\lambda_{R}=U_{R}^{\dagger}\hat{\lambda}O_{e}
in the soft CPV case, the unflavored CP asymmetry does not depend on the additional phase present in
U_{e}
of the generic CPV case.
The difference between generic CPV and soft CPV would appear only through possible restrictions on the spectrum of the RHNs and on
U_{R}
due to the restriction of
U_{\nu}
. We show this restrictions for the minimal case in Sec.
VII
.

VII
The minimal seesaw case:
n_{R}=2

Here we study in detail the soft CPV case for the minimal case of two RHNs
min.seesaw
where the reduced number of parameters will allow us to be quantitative and often we will be able to extract analytical expressions.
Comparison to the generic CPV case will be made on appropriate places.

VII.1
Parametrization in the diagonal-
\lambda
basis

Let us device an explicit parametrization for
n_{R}=2
starting from the diagonal-
\lambda
basis in (
9
).
Considering that for NO (IO) the massless neutrino is
\nu_{1}
(
\nu_{3}
), we choose the first (third) column of
\lambda
to be zero:

\text{NO:}\quad\hat{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda_{1}&0\cr 0&0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\Bigg(\begin{array}[]{c}0\cr 0\end{array}\Bigg|~\tilde{\lambda}~\Bigg)\,,\quad\text{IO:}\quad\hat{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&0\cr 0&\lambda_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}=\Bigg(~\tilde{\lambda}~\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{c}0\cr 0\end{array}\Bigg)\,,

(30)

where
\tilde{\lambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is the nonsingular square block.
The charged lepton Yukawa has the form (
9
) for the soft CPV case and (
14
) for the generic CPV case. The heavy mass matrix
M_{R}
is generic.

With these choices for
\lambda
, the light neutrino mass matrix from the seesaw formula (
8
) yields the block diagonal form:

\text{NO:}\quad M_{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cr&\tilde{M}_{\nu}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\text{IO:}\quad M_{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{M}_{\nu}&\cr&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(31)

where, for both cases, the nontrivial
2\times 2
block is

\tilde{M}_{\nu}=-v^{2}\tilde{\lambda}M_{R}^{-1}\tilde{\lambda}\,.

(32)

The matrices in (
31
) are evidently diagonalized by a block diagonal unitary matrix

\text{NO:}\quad U_{\nu}=i\begin{pmatrix}1&\cr&\tilde{U}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\text{IO:}\quad U_{\nu}=i\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}&\cr&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(33)

We have included the factor
i
for convenience and conventionally keep
\tilde{U}
within
SU(2)
.

The PMNS matrix is given by the mismatch (
23
) of
U_{\nu}
and the diagonalizing matrix of the charged lepton Yukawa
Y
.
Given the block structure in (
33
), discarding the
i
factor,
it follows that the PMNS matrix for the CPV case has the first (third) column real for NO (IO).
For a proper comparison, we should use the same phase convention for the generic CPV case.

To extract the consequences on
M_{R}
, we can invert (
32
).
If we take NO, it reads

M_{R}=v^{2}\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{U}\diag(m_{2}^{-1},m_{3}^{-1})\tilde{U}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}\tilde{\lambda}\,.

(34)

For IO, we just replace the light neutrino masses
m_{2}\to m_{1},m_{3}\to m_{2}
.
For further analysis, it is more convenient to normalize with respect to

M_{0}\equiv|\det M_{R}|^{1/2}=\sqrt{M_{1}M_{2}}=v^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}}{m_{2}m_{3}}}\,,

(35)

where
M_{1},M_{2}
are the heavy neutrino masses.
Then we can parametrize

\frac{M_{R}}{M_{0}}=\diag(\kappa,\kappa^{-1})\tilde{U}\diag(\rho,\rho^{-1})\tilde{U}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}\diag(\kappa,\kappa^{-1})\,,

(36)

where
\rho_{\rm NO}\equiv\sqrt{m_{3}/m_{2}}=(\Delta m^{2}_{\rm atm}/\Delta m^{2}_{\rm sol})^{1/4}\approx 2.41
for NO.
For IO, we should use
\rho_{\rm IO}\equiv\sqrt{m_{2}/m_{1}}\approx 1.0076
.
The other variable is
\kappa=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}}
.

Parametrization of
V

Given the special feature of the PMNS matrix having the first (third) column real for NO (IO), it is more
convenient to adopt a parametrization that makes this feature explicit.

We will adopt

\displaystyle V\big|_{\rm NO}

\displaystyle=R_{23}(-\theta_{1})R_{12}(-\theta_{2})\begin{pmatrix}1&\cr&W(\alpha,\theta_{3},\beta)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle V\big|_{\rm IO}

\displaystyle=R_{13}(\theta_{1})R_{23}(\theta_{2})\begin{pmatrix}W(\alpha,\theta_{3},\beta)&\cr&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
R_{ij}
are defined in (
1
) and (
2
), while

is a
2\times 2
representation of

defined by

W(\alpha,\theta,\beta)\equiv e^{-i\sigma_{3}\alpha/2}e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta}e^{-i\sigma_{3}\beta/2}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\theta}e^{-i(\alpha+\beta)/2}&-s_{\theta}e^{-i(\alpha-\beta)/2}\cr s_{\theta}e^{+i(\alpha-\beta)/2}&c_{\theta}e^{+i(\alpha+\beta)/2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(38)

using the notation with the Pauli matrices
\sigma_{i}
.
The parametrization assumes
\theta_{i}\in[0,\pi/2]
,
\alpha\in[0,2\pi]
and
\beta\in[0,\pi]
.
We can note that
\beta
in (
37
) is the single Majorana phase of the minimal case and it is not currently constrained.
An equivalent form for the last matrix in (
38
) is
\diag(1,e^{i\beta})
, in which case it is clear that
\beta
goes with
\nu_{3}
for NO and with
\nu_{2}
for IO.

The parametrization for NO in (
37
) has indeed the first column real and additionally positive entries when
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
lie in the first quadrant.
Similarly, the parametrization for IO has the third column real and positive when
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
lie in the first quadrant.
The NO parametrization has the additional advantage that
\theta_{3}
, the angle that is accompanied by complex phases, is ensured to be small as
\theta_{3}=0
when
V_{13}=0
.
Figure

\delta

We can see that the angles
\theta_{i}
depend only mildly on
\delta
.
The extraction of these parameters is similar to the case of standard-like parametrizations
denton

VII.3
Restrictions on
\tilde{U}

As shown in Sec.
VI
, the unitary matrix
U_{\nu}
that diagonalizes the light neutrino mass matrix
M_{\nu}
in the diagonal-
\lambda
basis is restricted in the soft CP case once we take into account the physical PMNS, and provided that the latter violates CP.
For the minimal case, the nontrivial block of
U_{\nu}
is
\tilde{U}
, cf. (
33
).
Here, we will show the explicit restrictions on
\tilde{U}
.
For the generic CPV case,
\tilde{U}
is free.

Focusing on NO and discarding the global
i
factor in (
33
), the PMNS matrix is given by

V=O_{e}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\cr&\tilde{U}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(39)

It should be clear that the first column of
V
coincides with the first column of
O_{e}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}
, hence it is real. For IO, the same occurs for the third column.
As a general restriction,
\tilde{U}
cannot be the identity if we want a complex
V
.
In contrast, for the generic CPV case we understand that
\tilde{U}
is free in the following manner: the real
O_{e}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}
becomes the unitary
U_{e}^{\dagger}
and this matrix can cancel any
\tilde{U}
and can still give the correct
V
.

Given the form of
V
in (
37
), it is convenient to adopt the following parametrizations for

O_{e}
:

\displaystyle O_{e}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}\Big|_{\rm NO}

\displaystyle=R_{23}(-\theta_{1})R_{12}(-\theta_{2})R_{23}(-\theta_{3}^{\prime})\,,

(40)

\displaystyle O_{e}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}\Big|_{\rm IO}

\displaystyle=R_{13}(\theta_{1})R_{23}(\theta_{2})R_{12}(-\theta_{3}^{\prime})\,.

We can adopt
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,\pi/2]
to keep the first (third) column positive for NO (IO).
We can also take
\theta_{3}^{\prime}\in[0,\pi]
as some sign flips can be absorbed by Majorana fields.

With the previous parametrization of
O_{e}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}
, we can identify in
V
the structure

\displaystyle V\big|_{\rm NO}

\displaystyle=R_{23}(-\theta_{1})R_{12}(-\theta_{2})\begin{pmatrix}1&\cr&U_{\rm eff}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(41)

\displaystyle V\big|_{\rm IO}

\displaystyle=R_{13}(\theta_{1})R_{23}(\theta_{2})\begin{pmatrix}U_{\rm eff}&\cr&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

where the effective subblock is

U_{\rm eff}=e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta_{3}^{\prime}}\tilde{U}\,.

(42)

Comparing to the parametrization of
V
in
(
37
), we conclude that the angles
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
are determined from the current knowledge of the PMNS, except for
\delta
. They are given in Fig.
1
.
The effective subblock
U_{\rm eff}
is also mostly determined.
Using the parametrization (
38
) of
SU(2)
,
we have

U_{\rm eff}=W(\alpha,\theta_{3},\beta)\,,

(43)

where
\theta_{3}
and
\alpha
are also given in Fig.
1
as a function of
\delta
.
Therefore, inverting (
42
),
\tilde{U}
is given by

\tilde{U}=e^{+i\sigma_{2}\theta_{3}^{\prime}}W(\alpha,\theta_{3},\beta)\,,

(44)

where
\theta_{3}^{\prime}
and
\beta
are free.

If we parametrize
\tilde{U}
as

\tilde{U}=W(\alpha_{\nu},\theta_{\nu},\beta_{\nu})\,,

(45)

then
\alpha_{\nu},\theta_{\nu}
will be restricted by the fact that
\alpha,\theta_{3}
are functions of
\delta
in (

).
The latter leads to the relation

e^{-i\sigma_{3}\alpha_{\nu}/2}e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta_{\nu}}e^{-i\sigma_{3}(\beta_{\nu}-\beta)/2}=e^{+i\sigma_{2}\theta_{3}^{\prime}}e^{-i\sigma_{3}\alpha/2}e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta_{3}}\,,

(46)

or

e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta_{3}^{\prime}}e^{-i\sigma_{3}\alpha_{\nu}/2}e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta_{\nu}}=e^{-i\sigma_{3}\alpha/2}e^{-i\sigma_{2}\theta_{3}}e^{-i\sigma_{3}(\beta-\beta_{\nu})/2}\,.

(47)

In these equations, both sides are generic parametrizations of
SU(2)
and then one side can be written as a function of the other side.
One parametrization is the usual phase-angle-phase parametrization in (
38
) and the other makes use of the orthogonal decomposition of
appendix

.
For definiteness, we will use (
46
) for most of the calculations.

We clearly see that some regions are not allowed.

VII.4
Constraints on mass degeneracy of RHNs

In the soft CPV case, we will show here that due to the restrictions on
\tilde{U}
that enters in
M_{R}
in (
34
), mass degeneracy of
N_{1},N_{2}
is not possible for certain values of CP phases
\delta
and
\beta
of the PMNS.

For comparison, in the generic CPV case, the form of
M_{R}
in (
34
) is still valid in the diagonal-
\lambda
basis. The difference is that
\tilde{U}
is unconstrained.
Therefore, if we choose
\tilde{U}
diagonal in (
34
),
M_{R}
will be also diagonal so that we can easily adjust the values of the diagonal of
\lambda
to make the masses degenerate.
Diagonal
\tilde{U}
means that
\theta_{\nu}=0

in (
45
) (
\theta_{\nu}=\pi/2
is analogous).
As shown in Fig.
3
,
for the soft CPV case,
\theta_{\nu}=0
cannot be reached for CP violating values of
\delta
.

To analyze the constraints on mass degeneracy of the heavy neutrinos, we take
M_{R}
in the normalized form (
36
) and define

H_{R}\equiv\frac{M_{R}M_{R}^{\dagger}}{M_{0}^{2}}\,.

(49)

Then take the characteristic polynomial

\det\bigg(H_{R}-\lambda{\mathbbm{1}}_{2}\bigg)=\lambda^{2}-\gamma_{1}\lambda+1\,.

(50)

The unity comes from
\det(H_{R})=1
and the only variable coefficient is the trace

\gamma_{1}=\tr(H_{R})\,.

(51)

The roots of the characteristic polynomial, which are the eigenvalues of
H_{R}
, are

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\right)^{2}-1}\,.

(52)

Since
H_{R}
is hermitean and positive definite, it is guaranteed that

\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\geq 1\,.

(53)

For equality,
\lambda_{\pm}=1
and the masses are degenerate.
Given the normalization factor
M_{0}
in (
35
), we conclude that

\lambda_{+}=\frac{M_{2}^{2}}{M_{0}^{2}}=\frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}}\,,

(54)

is the mass ratio of the heavier to the lighter mass of
M_{R}
.
Note that
\lambda_{-}=1/\lambda_{+}
.

Taking the explicit form for
M_{R}/M_{0}
in
(
36
) and minimizing
\gamma_{1}
with respect to
\kappa
, we obtain

\min_{\kappa}\big(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\big)=|1+z^{2}|+|z|^{2}\,,

(55)

where

z\equiv\sin(2\theta_{\nu})\mbox{\large$\tfrac{1}{2}$}\Big(\rho e^{i\beta_{\nu}}-\frac{1}{\rho e^{i\beta_{\nu}}}\Big)\,.

(56)

Note that
\gamma_{1}
does not depend on
\alpha_{\nu}
of
\tilde{U}
in (
45
) even without minimization.
In Fig.
5
we plot (
55
) for various values of
\sin(2\theta_{\nu})
for both NO and IO using best-fit values for
\rho_{\rm NO}
and
\rho_{\rm IO}
.
We clearly see that the minimum occurs at
\beta_{\nu}=\pi/2
and that this minimum is unity up to a critical value of
\sin(2\theta_{\nu})
.
This critical value is
\sin^{2}(2\theta_{\nu})|_{\rm crit}=0.5
for NO and
\sin^{2}(2\theta_{\nu})|_{\rm crit}=0.999943
for IO.
See appendix
D
for analytical details.

VIII
Conclusions

We have studied the simple case where the seesaw mechanism is simultaneously responsible for the light neutrino masses and the leptonic CP violation. The only source for CP breaking is soft and resides in the Majorana mass term of the heavy righthanded neutrinos.
We denoted this case as the soft CP violation case in contrast to the generic CP violation case.
This soft breaking can be easily attributed to scalar field vevs.

For the realistic case of two or more RHNs, the soft CP violating Lagrangian depends on one less parameter compared to the generic CP violation case.
This reduction of one less parameter is common to other scenarios where CP violation in the SM are generated spontaneously
nb-vlq
;
nb-vll
.
For the seesaw case, this parameter reduction leads to subtle restrictions of the seesaw parameter space once leptonic CP violation is assumed and the current knowledge of neutrino masses and mixing are taken into account.
The restrictions act on the high energy sector and the low energy parameters are not restricted in any way when seen in isolation.
For the minimal seesaw case, we analyzed the restricted parameters in detail, most notably,
there are correlations between the low energy CP phases of the PMNS with the mass splitting of RHNs. For certain values, mass degeneracy is forbidden and resonant leptogenesis is severely restricted unless freeze-in lepetogenesis effects are taken into account
shaposhnikov
;
drewes
.

Appendix A
Orthogonal decomposition

One can show that a
n\times n
unitary matrix
U
can be always decomposed into

U=\mathcal{O}_{2}\hat{U}\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}\,,

(60)

where
\mathcal{O}_{i}
are real orthogonal matrices and
\hat{U}
is a diagonal matrix of phases (they are not the eigenvalues of
U
).
The number of parameters are
n\times(n-1)/2
in each
\mathcal{O}_{i}
and
n
phases in
\hat{U}
, so
n^{2}
in total as it should be. One phase in
\hat{U}
can be removed if global rephasing is allowed.

The proof is simple.
We first check that

4i[\re(U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U),\im(U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U)]=[U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U+(U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U)^{*},U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U-(U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U)^{*}]=-2[U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U,(U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U)^{*}]=0\,.

(61)

Then the real and imaginary parts of the unitary symmetric matrix
U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U
can be diagonalized simultaneously by a real orthogonal matrix:

\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U\mathcal{O}_{1}=\diag(e^{i2\lambda_{j}})=\hat{U}^{2}\,.

(62)

The diagonalizing matrix is
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and the diagonal matrix is
\hat{U}^{2}
.
Then
U\mathcal{O}_{1}
coincides with
\hat{U}
except for a rotational freedom from the left which we denote as
\mathcal{O}_{2}
resulting in

U\mathcal{O}_{1}=\mathcal{O}_{2}\hat{U}\,.

(63)

As the eigenvectors in
\mathcal{O}_{1}
has no definite global sign, we can conventionally choose the phases in
\hat{U}
to lie in
[0,\pi)
.

As a special case, let us assume
U
admits rephasing transformations from the left.
Then we can always take, e.g., the first column real and non-negative. In this case
U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U
is block diagonal

U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\dots\cr 0&*&*\cr\vdots&*&*\end{pmatrix}\,,

(64)

because
(U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U)_{11}=(U^{\dagger}U)_{11}=1
already. In this case
\mathcal{O}_{1}
only needs to rotate in
n-1
dimensions and depends only on
(n-1)(n-2)/2
angles. It is also clear from (
64
) that
\hat{U}
has
\hat{U}_{11}=1
and
\hat{U}
depends only on
n-1
phases.
As
\mathcal{O}_{2}
contains the usual
n(n-1)/2
angles, the total amounts to
n(n-1)
parameteres, a reduction in
n
parameters corresponding to the rephasing of
n
phases.

For
n=3
, this result has implications on the parametrization of the PMNS matrix
V
which is physically equivalent under rephasing from the left.
The parametrization (
60
) allows us to parametrize the PMNS matrix with
only two phases
.
Specifically in the rephasing convention (
64
) we can write

V=\mathcal{O}_{2}\diag(1,e^{i\alpha/2},e^{i\beta/2})R_{23}(\theta_{23})\,,

(65)

where
R_{23}
is a rotation in the
(23)
plane and
\alpha,\beta
are confined to
[0,2\pi]
due to sign flip freedom from the right. The angle
\theta_{23}\in[0,2\pi)
as well. The matrix
\mathcal{O}_{2}
is a generic
SO(3)
matrix that can be parametrized with three Euler angles. Hence
V
in (
4
) is parametrized with 4 angles and 2 phases.
Therefore, one phase (CP odd) is traded by one angle (CP even) if compared to the standard parametrization.

Appendix B
Orthogonal decomposition: rephasing from both sides

The analysis is slightly more complicated if the unitary matrix
U
is allowed rephasing transformations from both sides. Here we analyze this case specifically for
n=3
.
The result is that the orthogonal decomposition (
60
) is possible with
only one phase
.
This suggests a different parametrization for the CKM matrix but the number of phases is unchanged.

Let us denote the columns of
U
as

U=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}u_{1}&u_{2}&u_{3}\end{array}\right)\,,

(66)

and analogously for
U^{\dagger}

U^{\dagger}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}v_{1}&v_{2}&v_{3}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}^{\dagger}\cr\hline\cr u_{2}^{\dagger}\cr\hline\cr u_{3}^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)\,.

(67)

If
e_{i}
are the canonical column vectors, we can write

\displaystyle Ue_{i}=u_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\quad Uv_{i}=e_{i}\,,

(68)

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}e_{i}=v_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\quad U^{\dagger}u_{i}=e_{i}\,,

(69)

for
i=1,2,3
.

By choosing
u_{1}
real by rephasing from left, we obtain

U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}u_{1}=e_{1}\implies U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}Ue_{1}=e_{1}\,,

(70)

and
U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U
is block diagonal as in (
64
).
If additionally
v_{1}=U^{\dagger}e_{1}
is real by rephasing from the right, then also
v_{1}=U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}e_{1}
and

U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}Uv_{1}=U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}e_{1}=v_{1}\,.

(71)

Assuming
v_{1}\neq e_{1}
, which is equivalent to
U
non-block-diagonal, we conclude that the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
of
U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U
has multiplicity at least two. Discarding real
U
, the multiplicity should be exactly two.

Now construct
\mathcal{O}_{1}
with columns
e_{1},v_{1}^{\prime},v_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, where
(e_{1},v_{1}^{\prime})
is the orthogonal basis for the space spanned by
(e_{1},v_{1})
while
v_{1}^{\prime\prime}
is the orthogonal direction (
\mathcal{O}_{1}
is block diagonal).
It is clear that

\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U^{\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathsf{T}$}}U\mathcal{O}_{1}=\diag(1,1,e^{i2\beta})=\hat{U}^{2}\,,

(72)

where incidentally
e^{i\beta}=\det U
.
Equation (
63
) tells us that the first column of
\mathcal{O}_{2}
is
u_{1}
, i.e., the same as
U
’s.
Since
Uv_{1}=e_{1}
, the second column of
\mathcal{O}_{2}
is the piece of
e_{1}
orthogonal to
u_{1}
.
So we can obtain
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
from orthogonalization of

\mathcal{O}_{1}\leftarrow(e_{1},v_{1},e_{1}\times v_{1})\,,\quad\mathcal{O}_{2}\leftarrow(u_{1},e_{1},u_{1}\times e_{1})\,.

(73)

In this case
\mathcal{O}_{1}
is block diagonal and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
has vanishing
(13)
entry.
We can reverse the roles of
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
making the latter block diagonal. Other choices of real columns and rows lead to similar forms.

The parameter counting goes as follows:
\hat{U}
contains one phase,
\mathcal{O}_{1}
depends on one angle to define
v_{1}
in the subspace orthogonal to
e_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
depends on two angles to define
u_{1}
. The total amounts to the correct number of 4 parameters

For
n>3
, the construction of the first two columns in
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
continues to be as in (
73
). For
\mathcal{O}_{1}
, the first two columns depend on the pair
(e_{1},v_{1})
which requires
n-2
angles to define the part of
v_{1}
orthgonal to
e_{1}
. The rest of the
n-2
columns span an
n-2
dimensional space which can be rotated by the
O(n-2)
group with
(n-2)(n-3)/2
angles. For
\mathcal{O}_{2}
, the reasoning is the same, except that the pair
(u_{1},e_{1})
needs
n-1
angles to define
u_{1}
.
As the number of phases in (
72
) becomes
n-2
, the total number of parameters is
2\times(n-2)+(n-1)+2\times(n-2)(n-3)/2=(n-1)^{2}
, matching the number of parameters for a CKM-like mixing matrix with
n
families
branco:book
.
The number of phases here, however, differ from the standard counting (parametrization).
The orthogonal decomposition uses
n-2
phases. The standard parametrization requires
(n-1)(n-2)/2
.
For
n=3
, one phase is required in both cases (the KM counting) but for larger
n
the scaling is linear rather than quadratic and much less phases appear.

For

\begin{pmatrix}A&-B^{*}\cr B&A^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\theta}&s_{\theta}\cr-s_{\theta}&c_{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&-b^{*}\cr b&a^{*}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(74)

with fixed
a,b

\theta

These matrices belong to
SU(2)
so that
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1
and
|A|^{2}+|B|^{2}=1

Parametrizing

|a|=c_{\theta^{\prime}}\,,\quad|b|=s_{\theta^{\prime}}\,,\quad ba^{*}=|ab|e^{i\alpha^{\prime}}\,,

(76)

(c_{2\theta},s_{2\theta})=\pm\frac{1}{N}(c_{2\theta^{\prime}},s_{2\theta^{\prime}}c_{\alpha^{\prime}})\,,

(77)

where

is the normalization factor.
Hence, the minimal and maximal values of
|B|^{2}
are respectively given by

2|B|^{2}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scriptsize{min}}}}{{\text{\scriptsize{max}}}}}=1\mp\sqrt{1-s_{2\theta^{\prime}}^{2}s^{2}_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\,.

(78)

Note that
|A|^{2}_{\rm min}=1-|B|^{2}_{\rm max}
and analogously for the maximum.

We can check that, at the extrema,

BA^{*}\big|_{\rm extr}=\frac{i}{2}s_{2\theta^{\prime}}s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\,,

(79)

so that the relative phase of
A,B
is
\pm\pi/2
.
Therefore, for the minimum of
|B|
, the matrix in (
74
) can be written as

e^{\mp i\sigma_{3}\pi/4}\begin{pmatrix}|A|_{\max}&-|B|_{\min}\cr|B|_{\min}&|A|_{\max}\end{pmatrix}e^{-i\sigma_{3}\beta/2}\,,

(80)

where the minus (plus) sign is for
s_{\alpha^{\prime}}>0
(
s_{\alpha^{\prime}}<0
).
The phase
\beta
depends on other parameters.
The case of maximum
|B|
is similar.

If we want to extremize
BA^{*}
with respect to
\theta

(c_{2\theta},s_{2\theta})=\pm\frac{1}{N}(-s_{2\theta^{\prime}}c_{\alpha^{\prime}},c_{2\theta^{\prime}})\,.

(81)

The extrema are

BA^{*}\big|_{\rm ext}=is_{2\theta^{\prime}}s_{\alpha^{\prime}}\mp\sqrt{1-s^{2}_{2\theta^{\prime}}s^{2}_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\,.

(82)

At these extrema,

|A|^{2}=|B|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

(83)
