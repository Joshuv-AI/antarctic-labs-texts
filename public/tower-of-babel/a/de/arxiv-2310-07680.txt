Hamiltonian Dynamics of Bayesian Inference Formalised by Arc Hamiltonian Systems
Takuo Matsubara, Takuo Matsubara, Takuo Matsubara, Takuo Matsubara

Abstract
This paper advances theoretical understanding of infinite-dimensional geometrical properties associated with Bayesian inference. First, we introduce a novel class of infinite-dimensional Hamiltonian systems for saddle Hamiltonian functions whose domains are metric spaces. A flow of this system is generated by a Hamiltonian arc field, an analogue of Hamiltonian vector fields formulated based on (i) the first variation of Hamiltonian functions and (ii) the notion of arc fields that extends vector fields to metric spaces. We establish that this system obeys the conservation of energy. We derive a condition for the existence of the flow, which reduces to local Lipschitz continuity of the first variation under sufficient regularity. Second, we present a system of a Hamiltonian function, called the minimum free energy, whose domain is a metric space of negative log-likelihoods and probability measures. The difference of the posterior and the prior of Bayesian inference is characterised as the first variation of the minimum free energy. Our result shows that a transition from the prior to the posterior defines an arc field on a space of probability measures, which forms a Hamiltonian arc field together with another corresponding arc field on a space of negative log-likelihoods. This reveals the underlying invariance of the free energy behind the arc field.

2.1
Notations and Terminologies

We introduce basic notations and terminologies.

Functional-Analytic

By a “curve", we mean a map from a time interval to a metric space, where it can be equivalently thought of as a time-dependent variable in a metric space.
We use subscripts to indicate the time argument of a curve; for example
t\mapsto x_{t}
denotes a curve.
A curve is said continuous if it is a continuous map.
We denote by
\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}
the limit with respect to a scalar
s\in\mathbb{R}
approaching to
0
from the right.
For any metric space
(M,d)
, we denote by
B_{r}(x)
an open ball in
(M,d)
around a point
x\in M
with a radius
r
.
For any map
\Phi:M\times[0,1]\to M
defined for a set
M
and the time interval
[0,1]
, we use subscripts to indicate the time argument of
\Phi
, that is,
\Phi_{s}(x)
denotes the value at
(x,s)\in M\times[0,1]
.

Measure-Theoretic

Let
\Theta
be a separable completely metrizable space (i.e. a Polish space).
By a “measure", we mean a non-negative Borel measure on
\Theta
.
By a “signed measure", we mean a signed Borel measure on
\Theta
.
Any signed measure

admits the Jordan decomposition
P=P^{+}-P^{-}
by two uniquely associated (non-negative) measures
P^{+}
and
P^{-}
.
For each signed measure

, we define a measure

—often referred to as the variation of

— by

.
This means that
\int_{O}d|P|(\theta)=\int_{O}dP^{+}(\theta)+\int_{O}dP^{-}(\theta)

.
A signed measure

is said finite if and only if the measure
|P|
is finite.
If a measure

admits the Radon-Nikodym derivative

with respect to a measure
Q
, we indicate the relationship by

for better presentation.
Denote by
\mathcal{M}
,

, and

a set of all finite signed measures, all finite measures, and all probability measures.

2.2
Hamiltonian Systems

Hamiltonian systems have an extensive history of the development; we refer readers to
Goldstein, Poole and
Safko 2002
for a detailed introduction of classical mechanics and to
Arnold 1978
for the rigorous mathematical foundation.
Hamiltonian systems enable us to describe the evolution of the objects through the first-order evolution equation, by introducing a variable called the momentum.
For illustration, consider an object moving in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose location at time
t
is denoted
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The momentum, denoted
z_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, can be associated with a physical quantity calculated by the product of the mass and velocity at time
t
.
A Hamiltonian function
H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
corresponds to the total energy that the system has at each state
(x,z)
.
How the pair of the location and momentum
(x_{t},z_{t})
evolves over time
t
is determined through
Hamilton’s equation
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(x_{t},z_{t})=(-\nabla_{2}H(x_{t},z_{t}),\nabla_{1}H(x_{t},z_{t}))

(5)

where
\nabla_{1}
and
\nabla_{2}
denote the gradient operator with respect to the first and second argument.
Hamilton’s equation (
5
) is a differential equation
(d/dt)(x_{t},z_{t})=F(x_{t},z_{t})
driven by the
Hamiltonian vector field

F(x,z)=(-\nabla_{2}H(x,z),\nabla_{1}H(x,z))
, which can be solved as an initial value problem.
Under sufficient regularity, it generates a unique solution flow
t\mapsto(x_{t},z_{t})
from any initial state
(x_{0},z_{0})
in the domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In particular, any flow
t\mapsto(x_{t},z_{t})
generated by the Hamiltonian vector field conserves the energy, meaning that the value of the Hamiltonian function
H(x_{t},z_{t})
remains constant along the flow.

2
illustrates a Hamiltonian system of a simple pendulum; it can be observed that the values of the Hamiltonian function remain constant along every generated flow.

Chernoff and Marsden 1974
—and later
Marsden and Hughes 1983
for application to mechanics of elastic materials—established an infinite-dimensional extension of Hamiltonian systems to Banach spaces and, more generally, to symplectic manifolds built on smooth Banach manifolds.
Here, we do not consider the case of Banach manifolds.
As
Kriegl and Michor 1997
and
Schmeding 2022
pointed out, there are known limitations of Banach manifolds.
For example,
Eells and Elworthy 1970
proved that an infinite-dimensional smooth Banach manifold is smoothly embedded as an open set in a Banach space used to define the atlas
4
4

4

Although an atlas of a Banach manifold can be defined using multiple Banach spaces in principle, the Banach spaces turn out to be all isomorphic in most cases to guarantee Fréchet-differentiability. This means that it suffices to use one Banach space to define an atlas of a Banach manifold
(see
Lang 1985
, p.22)
.
.
Analysis on smooth Banach manifolds then reduces to that on Banach spaces.

Consider a setting where an object of interest
(x_{t},z_{t})
belongs to a Banach space
E_{1}\times E_{2}
.
In the extended Hamiltonian system, the evolution of the system is governed by an evolution equation
(d/dt)(x_{t},z_{t})=F(x_{t},z_{t})
driven by a map
F:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to E_{1}\times E_{2}
which depends on the gradient of the Hamiltonian function
H(x,z)
analogous to (
5
) but taken in a sense of the Fréchet derivative under appropriate conditions of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.
By the Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem
(
Brezis 2011
, e.g.)
, the evolution equation admits a unique solution from any initial state if the map

is Lipschitz continuous in
E_{1}\times E_{2}
.
However, this formulation based on a norm topology is not suitable for dynamics involving the set of measures
\mathcal{M}^{+}
.
In a Banach space of signed measures
\mathcal{M}
under e.g. the total variation norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}}

(
Cohn 2013
)
, it is easy to prove that the subset
\mathcal{M}^{+}
has no interior point.
That is, if we use the norm topology on
\mathcal{M}
, no open set can be defined inside
\mathcal{M}^{+}
to use important topological notions—such as the Fréchet derivative or local Lipschitz continuity—of functions defined only on
\mathcal{M}^{+}
.
One trivial approach to define open sets inside
\mathcal{M}^{+}
is to use a metric topology on
\mathcal{M}^{+}
instead of the norm topology introduced to the vector space
\mathcal{M}
.
For example, the set
\mathcal{M}^{+}
will be a metric space
(\mathcal{M}^{+},d)
under the total variation metric
d
on
\mathcal{M}^{+}
defined by restricting the norm distance
\|P-Q\|_{\mathcal{M}}
to
\mathcal{M}^{+}\times\mathcal{M}^{+}
.
This motivates us to introduce a metric on the domain of interest and define the evolution within the metric space.
To achieve this, we capitalise on the notion of arc fields
(
Calcaterra and
Bleecker 2000
)
recapped next.

2.3
Arc Fields

The notion of arc fields
(
Calcaterra and
Bleecker 2000
)
, that extends vector fields to metric spaces, is used to formulate an analogue of Hamiltonian vector fields and Hamilton’s equation in metric spaces.
The underlying idea behind arc fields has a close connection to mutational equations
(
Aubin 1999
;
Lorenz 2010
)
whose common application includes the evolution of set-valued objects and objects that belongs to complicated regions of vector spaces
(
Aubin 1993
)
.
To illustrate the idea, consider a vector field
F:E\to E
in a Banach space
(E,\|\cdot\|_{E})
and a solution curve
t\mapsto x_{t}
of the corresponding evolution equation
(d/dt)x_{t}=F(x_{t})
.
At each
x\in E
, define a curve
\Phi_{s}(x)=x+sF(x)
over time
s\in[0,1]
.
Informally, the evolution equation can be thought of as the first-order approximation of the solution curve

at each
t
over small

, specified by the curve
s\mapsto\Phi_{s}(x_{t})
:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\left\|\frac{x_{t+s}-x_{t}}{s}-F(x_{t})\right\|_{E}=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\|x_{t+s}-\Phi_{s}(x_{t})\|_{E}}{|s|}=0.

A key observation is that the vector-space structure of the set
E
—where the curve
t\mapsto x_{t}
belongs—is not essential in this formulation.
In the above condition, consider that the vector space
E
is replaced with any set, such as
\mathcal{M}^{+}
, and the norm
\|x_{t+s}-\Phi_{s}(x_{t})\|_{E}
is replaced with a metric

on the replaced set
E
.
The above condition can be still well-defined if we have a premise that the curve
s\mapsto\Phi_{s}(x_{t})
stays within the set
E
over all
s
in some interval.
This observation leads to the extension of vector fields to metric spaces.

Arc Hamiltonian Systems

Extending Hamiltonian systems to infinite-dimensional metric spaces may open up a new horizon for formulating the dynamics of intriguing phenomena.
A focus in this work is on saddle Hamiltonian functions whose domains are equipped with metrics.
This section will establish the general framework of arc Hamiltonian systems.
The first variation (c.f.

3.1

Hamiltonian arc field
(c.f.

It will be shown that the generated flow obeys the conservation of energy.

In

Convex-Analytic Preliminaries

Define the extended real number
\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
.
It is standard convention in convex analysis to consider extended-valued functions from a vector space

\overline{\mathbb{R}}

We denote by

An extended-valued function

f((1-\lambda)x+\lambda v)\leq(1-\lambda)f(x)+\lambda f(v)

An extended-valued function

is said

-f

3.2

Hamiltonian systems describe the dynamics of a paired variable

in some space.
We consider a setting where the variable

The framework of arc Hamiltonian systems is developed for saddle Hamiltonian functions.

•

Denote by

It is convenient to introduce a condition,

, of a saddle function

In what follows, the duplet

6

It is possible to define the symplectic variation as

alternatively.
of a saddle function

Definition 4

(Compatibility)

Definition 6

5
Conclusion

This paper made two theoretical contributions.
First, we established the framework of arc Hamiltonian systems for saddle Hamiltonian functions whose domains are infinite-dimensional metric spaces.
We derived the general condition, under which a Hamiltonian arc field generates energy-conserving flows everywhere in a metric space.
We showed that the general condition reduces to local Lipschitz continuity of the first variation of the Hamiltonian function when the metric on
M
has an extension to a norm-induced metric on
E_{1}\times E_{2}
.
Second, we presented the arc Hamiltonian system of the minimum free energy, in which the difference of the posterior and the prior of Bayesian inference is characterised as the first variation of the minimum free energy
H(f,P)
with respect to
f
.
Pairing this first variation with the other first variation with respect to
P
, we revealed the underlying Hamiltonian arc field behind the transition from the prior and the posterior, as highlighted in
Remark

4
.
The invariance of the conservation of the minimum free energy was established.

In this work, convexity of saddle Hamiltonian functions plays a vital role in the construction of our energy-conserving systems.
Arc Hamiltonian systems satisfy the conservation of energy, using only a topologically weak notion of derivative and gradient, i.e., the variational derivative and the first variation.
In contrast to the Fréchet derivative, weak notions of derivative, such as the Gâteaux derivative, no longer satisfy the chain rule in general
(
Yamamuro 1974
)
.
Nonetheless, convexity acts in place of the chain rule to guarantee the energy-conserving property of arc Hamiltonian systems.
We focused on saddle Hamiltonian functions that are independent of time
t
.
Relaxing time-independence of saddle Hamiltonian functions represents one of natural directions for future work.
An extension of arc fields to time-dependent cases was considered in
Kim and Masmoudi 2013
.
In this paper, we applied the framework of arc Hamiltonian systems to formalise the infinite-dimensional geometrical property associated with Bayesian inference.
Apart from our application, there may be other intriguing dynamical phenomena or optimisation methodologies that benefit from infinite-dimensional Hamiltonian systems in metric spaces.
The general framework of arc Hamiltonian systems can naturally lead to the development of further applications.

The function

We introduce the first intermediate lemma used in the main proof.

(*_{a})=1/F(f,P)
.
It then follows from
Lemma

9
that we have

\displaystyle D_{1}G_{f,P}(g)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{F(f,P)}\cdot D_{1}F_{f,P}(g)=\int_{\Theta}g(\theta)\frac{\exp(-f(\theta))}{\int_{\Theta}\exp(-f(\theta))dP(\theta)}dP(\theta).

By definition of the duality
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, this establishes that
D_{1}G_{f,P}(g)=\langle g,P_{*}\rangle
.
Here it is clear that
P_{*}\in E_{2}
since
\exp(-f(\theta))
is bounded as
\exp(-f(\theta))\leq 1
by the condition of
M
.

Second, we derive
D_{2}G_{f,P}(Q)
.
By definition of the variational derivative, we have

\displaystyle D_{2}G_{f,P}(Q)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log(F(f,P+\epsilon Q))-\log(F(f,P))}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log(F(f,P+\epsilon Q))-\log(F(f,P))}{F(f,P+\epsilon Q)-F(f,P)}\cdot\frac{F(f,P+\epsilon Q)-F(f,P)}{\epsilon}.

By abuse of notation, set
\delta:=F(f,P+\epsilon Q)-F(f,P)
.
Notice again that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(\delta/\epsilon)
corresponds to of the variational derivative
D_{2}F_{f,P}(Q)
.
We have
\delta\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
because
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(\delta/\epsilon)
exists from
Lemma

9
.
Therefore, the above limit can be written as

\displaystyle D_{2}G_{f,P}(Q)

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\log(F(f,P)+\delta)-\log(F(f,P))}{\delta}}_{=:(*_{b})}\cdot\underbrace{\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{F(f,P+\epsilon Q)-F(f,P)}{\epsilon}}_{=D_{2}F_{f,P}(Q)}.

The term
(*_{b})
equals to the term
(*_{a})
above, meaning that we have
(*_{b})=1/F(f,P)
.
Since
D_{2}F_{f,P}(Q)=\int_{\Theta}\exp(-f(\theta))dQ(\theta)
by
Lemma

9
, we have

\displaystyle D_{2}G_{f,P}(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{F(f,P)}\cdot\int_{\Theta}\exp(-f(\theta))dQ(\theta)=\int_{\Theta}\frac{-\exp(-f(\theta))}{\int_{\Theta}\exp(-f(\theta))dP(\theta)}dQ(\theta).

By definition of the duality
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, this establishes that
D_{2}G_{f,P}(Q)=\langle-f_{*},Q\rangle
.
Here it is clear that
f_{*}\in E_{1}
since
\exp(-f(\theta))
is bounded as
\exp(-f(\theta))\leq 1
as aforementioned.
∎

D.3
The Main Proof of
Theorem

5

Finally, we provide the main proof of
Theorem

5
.
The proof uses the second lemma in
Section

D.2
, where the first lemma in
Section

D.1
was implicitly used as an intermediate result for the second lemma in
Section

D.2
.

As shown in (
S14
), we have
H(f,P)=G(f,P)-\langle f,P\rangle
for the function
G
in (
S24
).
First, we derive the variational derivative
D_{1}H_{f,P}(g)
.
We have

\displaystyle D_{1}H_{f,P}(g)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{G(f+\epsilon g,P)-G(f,P)}{\epsilon}-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\langle f+\epsilon g,P\rangle-\langle f,P\rangle}{\epsilon}.

Notice that the second limit immediately equals to
\langle g,P\rangle
because the function
f\mapsto\langle f,P\rangle
is linear due to the bilinearlity of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
The first limit is nothing but the variational derivative
D_{1}G_{f,P}(g)
of the function
G
in (
S24
).
It hence follows from
Lemma

10
that

\displaystyle D_{1}H_{f,P}(g)

\displaystyle=\langle g,P_{*}\rangle-\langle g,P\rangle=\langle g,P_{*}-P\rangle.

This establishes the first variation
\partial_{1}H(f,P)=P_{*}-P
by definition of the first variation.

Second, we derive the variational derivative
D_{2}H_{f,P}(g)
.
We have

\displaystyle D_{2}H_{f,P}(Q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{G(f,P+\epsilon Q)-G(f,P)}{\epsilon}-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\langle f,P+\epsilon Q\rangle-\langle f,P\rangle}{\epsilon}.

Again, notice that the second limit immediately equals to
\langle f,Q\rangle
because the function
P\mapsto\langle f,P\rangle
is linear due to the bilinearlity of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
The first limit is nothing but the variational derivative
D_{2}G_{f,P}(g)
of the function
G
in (
S24
).
It hence follows from
Lemma

10
that

\displaystyle D_{2}H_{f,P}(Q)

\displaystyle=\langle-f_{*},Q\rangle-\langle f,Q\rangle=\langle-f_{*}-f,Q\rangle.

This establishes the first variation
\partial_{2}H(f,P)=-f_{*}-f
by definition of the first variation.
It is equivalent to that
-\partial_{2}H(f,P)=f_{*}+f
, which completes the proof.
∎

E.1
The Proof of
Lemma

Define a norm
\|P\|_{E_{2}}:=\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P|(\theta)
on
E_{2}
.
By the reverse triangle inequality, we have
\|P\|_{E_{2}}-\|P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}\leq\|P-P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}=\gamma(P,P_{0})
.
We apply a trivial inequality
\gamma(P,P_{0})\leq d((f,P),(f_{0},P_{0}))
, which follows from definition of
d
, to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P|(\theta)-\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P_{0}|(\theta)=\|P\|_{E_{2}}-\|P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}\leq d((f,P),(f_{0},P_{0}))<r

where the last inequality follows from

.
This implies
\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P|(\theta)\leq\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P_{0}|(\theta)+r
.
Since

, setting

completes the proof.
∎

E.2
The Proof of
Lemma

7

Since
x\mapsto\exp(-x)
is convex, Jensen’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle Z(f,P)=\int_{\Theta}\exp(-f(\theta))dP(\theta)\geq\exp\left(-\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)dP(\theta)\right)

This lower bound is equivalent to an upper bound
1/Z(f,P)\leq\exp(\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)dP(\theta))
.
Define a norm
\|f\|_{E_{1}}:=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|f(\theta)|/w(\theta)
and a norm
\|P\|_{E_{2}}:=\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P|(\theta)
.
We upper bound the term
(*):=-\int_{\Theta}f(\theta)dP(\theta)
inside the exponential function as follows:

\displaystyle|(*)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{|f(\theta)|}{w(\theta)}\int_{\Theta}w(\theta)d|P|(\theta)=\|f\|_{E_{1}}\|P\|_{E_{2}}.

It follows from the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle|(*)|

\displaystyle\leq(\|f-f_{0}\|_{E_{1}}+\|f_{0}\|_{E_{1}})(\|P-P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}+\|P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}).

We have trivial bounds
\|f-f_{0}\|_{E_{1}}\leq d((f,P),(f_{0},P_{0}))
and
\|P-P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}\leq d((f,P),(f_{0},P_{0}))
by definition of
d
.
Since
(f,P)\in B_{r}(f_{0},P_{0})
and
r\leq 1
, we further have
\|f-f_{0}\|_{E_{1}}\leq 1
and
\|P-P_{0}\|_{E_{2}}\leq 1
.
Plugging this in the upper bound of
1/Z(f,P)
, we establish that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z(f,P)}\leq\exp\left((1+\|f_{0}\|_{E_{1}})(1+\|P_{0}\|_{E_{2}})\right).

Setting the right-hand side to
W_{0}
, which depends only on
(f_{0},P_{0})
, completes the proof.
∎

E.3
The Proof of
Lemma

8

Let
(f,P),(g,Q)
be arbitrary points in
B_{r}(f_{0},P_{0})
.
Denote
(*):=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\exp(-f(\theta))/Z(f,P)-\exp(-g(\theta))/Z(g,Q)|

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle(*)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left|\frac{\exp(-f(\theta))}{Z(f,P)}-\frac{\exp(-g(\theta))}{Z(f,P)}+\frac{\exp(-g(\theta))}{Z(f,P)}-\frac{\exp(-g(\theta))}{Z(g,Q)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Z(f,P)}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\exp(-f(\theta))-\exp(-g(\theta))|+\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\exp(-g(\theta))|\left|\frac{1}{Z(f,P)}-\frac{1}{Z(g,Q)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(f,P)}\underbrace{\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\exp(-f(\theta))-\exp(-g(\theta))|}_{=:(*_{1})}+\frac{\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\exp(g(\theta))|}{Z(f,P)Z(g,Q)}\underbrace{\vphantom{\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}}\left|Z(f,P)-Z(g,Q)\right|}_{=:(*_{2})}.

Denote
(*_{a}):=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|\exp(-g(\theta))|
.
We have
(*_{a})\leq 1
since
g(\theta)\geq\log w(\theta)\geq 0
by the condition of
M
.

Lemma

7
shows that
1/Z(f,P)\leq W_{0}
for all
(f,P)\in B_{r}(f_{0},P_{0})
.
Therefore

\displaystyle(*)

\displaystyle\leq W_{0}\,(*_{1})+W_{0}^{2}\,(*_{2}).

(S25)

We upper bound each term
(*_{1})
and
(*_{2})
in the remainder.

Term
(*_{1})
:
The mean value theorem implies

At each

\displaystyle|\exp(-f(\theta))-\exp(-g(\theta))|\leq\max(\exp(-f(\theta)),\exp(-g(\theta)))|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|.

It then follows from taking the supremum of both the sides that

\displaystyle(*_{2})
