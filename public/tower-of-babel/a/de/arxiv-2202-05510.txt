Support Vectors and Gradient Dynamics of Single-Neuron ReLU Networks
Sangmin LeeDepartment of Mathematical SciencesKAIST leeleesang@kaist.ac.kr, Byeongsu SimDepartment of Mathematical SciencesKAIST byeongsu.s@kaist.ac.kr Jong Chul YeKim Jaechul Graduate School of AIKAIST jong.ye@kaist.ac.kr

Abstract
Understanding implicit bias of gradient descent for generalization capability of ReLU networks has been an important research topic in machine learning research. Unfortunately, even for a single ReLU neuron trained with the square loss, it was recently shown impossible to characterize the implicit regularization in terms of a norm of model parameters Vardi & Shamir 2021 . In order to close the gap toward understanding intriguing generalization behavior of ReLU networks, here we examine the gradient flow dynamics in the parameter space when training single-neuron ReLU networks. Specifically, we discover an implicit bias in terms of support vectors, which plays a key role in why and how ReLU networks generalize well. Moreover, we analyze gradient flows with respect to the magnitude of the norm of initialization, and show that the norm of the learned weight strictly increases through the gradient flow. Lastly, we prove the global convergence of single ReLU neuron for d = 2 d=2 case.



1
Introduction

Recently, many researchers have investigated the intriguing generalization capability of ReLU networks even without explicit regularization
Goodfellow et al. 2016
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2018
;
Alom et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Calin 2020
. In particular, the number of trainable parameters in deep neural networks is often greater than the training data set, this situation being notorious for overfitting from the point of view of classical statistical learning theory. However, empirical results have shown that a deep neural network generalizes well in the test phase, resulting in high performance for the unseen data
Jiang et al. 2019
.

This apparent contradiction has raised questions about the mathematical
foundations of machine learning and their relevance to practitioners.
A number of theoretical papers have been published to understand the generalization capability of deep learning
models
Neyshabur et al. 2015
;
Bartlett et al. 2017
;
Nagarajan & Kolter 2019
;
Arora et al. 2018
;
Golowich et al. 2018
;
Neyshabur et al. 2018
;
Wei & Ma 2019
.
In particular, the recent discovery of “double descent”
Belkin et al. 2019
;
Belkin et al. 2020
extends the classical U-shaped bias-variance trade-off curve by showing that increasing the model capacity beyond the interpolating regime leads to improved performance in the test phase.
It was further suggested that the implicit bias by optimization algorithms may lead to simpler solutions that improve generalization in the over-parameterized regime
Gunasekar et al. 2018
.

Accordingly, many machine learning researchers have studied implicit bias and gradient flow.
For linear networks,
Gunasekar et al. 2017
showed that gradient flows with infinitesimally small norm converge to the minimum nuclear norm.

Azulay et al. 2021
studied the initialization scale of gradient flows and obtained closed-form implicit regularizers for several types of networks.
Cornacchia et al. 2021
showed that noise labels guide the network to a sparse solution and reduce test error.
The focus of these works is to find a regularization function of the model parameters, so that if we apply gradient descent on the average loss, then it converges in some
sense to a global optimum that minimizes the regularization term.
Unfortunately, when we consider problems beyond simple linear classification and regression, the situation gets more complicated.
For example,
Vardi & Shamir 2021
showed that even for a simple single-neuron ReLU network,
the implicit regularization cannot be expressed by any explicit function of the norm of model parameters.

In order to address the discrepancy between the theory and the empirical generalization power of ReLU networks,
we are interested in investigating the gradient flow dynamics when training single-neuron ReLU networks.
While most of the theoretical analysis of ReLU networks focus on the input space partition
Hanin & Rolnick 2019c
;
Hanin & Rolnick 2019a
;
Park et al. 2021
, here we are particularly interested in the analysis in the
parameter space
since it has provided additional insight.
Specifically,
Xu et al. 2021
studied the partitioned parameter space by ReLU networks in terms of polytopes and suggested a traversing algorithm to visit all polytopes sequentially.
Similarly,
Lacotte & Pilanci 2020
consider the partitioned parameter space in two-layer ReLU networks. They provide an exact characterization of the set of all global optima of the non-convex loss landscape, and find explicit paths for non-increasing loss under
L^{2}
regularization term.
Unlike the aforementioned works that mostly focus on the expressiveness and optimization landscape in terms of parameter space,
the main focus of this paper is extending these ideas to understand the implicit bias and the dynamics of gradient flows of single-neuron ReLU networks.
As such, our findings and contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:

•

We discover an implicit bias in terms of
support vectors
for single-neuron ReLU networks that play a key role in why and how ReLU networks can generalize well.
We further prove that the global minimum of single-neuron ReLU networks has smaller losses than linear ones.

•

Under proper initialization, we showed that gradient flow avoids bad local minimum. This explains why a ReLU network is trained well, although there are many spurious minima.

•

We provide simple proofs for norm-increasing property of single-neuron linear networks, and extend it to ReLU networks. More precisely, for a gradient flow of ReLU networks initialized with infinitesimally small norm, under some conditions, the norm of the gradient flow is shown to strictly increase until it converges.

•

Finally, we show the global convergence of gradient flow for special case
d=2
. Specifically, for a gradient flow initialized with infinitesimally small norm and positive gradient, we show that it converges to a global minimum with increasing norm of the weights.

Most of the proofs can be found in Appendix. In addition,
main theoretical findings of linear single-neuron networks are included in Appendix
D
, which are the basis of the main analysis for ReLU networks.

Partitions and support vectors of single-neuron ReLU networks

The main goal of this section is to extend the analysis of the loss landscape of single-neuron linear networks in Appendix
D

Due to the existence of ReLU, one of important tools for ReLU network analysis is understanding parameter space partition.
Specifically, let

Inspired by

Finally, for a given partition

Proposition

Specifically, for a partition

It is worth noting that this terminology is closely related with the support vector machine

Furthermore, the following result suggests an important advantage of ReLU networks in terms of support vectors.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under

, the global minimum of a single-neuron ReLU network has smaller loss than a single neuron linear network.

This states that in order to have a smaller loss, some data could be deactivated during the training of ReLU networks.
The following toy example shows the deactivation of data during the training of a single neuron ReLU networks.

Example 3.5

(Deactivation in ReLU networks.)
.

Consider the three data (
n=3
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
(

) described in Figure

. Let
P_{0}
be the partition where all three data are activated. Since the virtual minimum
{\bm{w}}_{P_{0}}^{*}
(orange star) is in the deactivated half space of the green data, the gradient flow must deactivate it (at the purple dot) and move to the next partition

. Then, the virtual minimum is changed to the yellow star (
{\bm{w}}_{P_{1}}^{*}
) which is the optimal solution of two data (blue and red). i.e., the gradient flow finally disregards the green data and converges to the optimal point of the remained data. See also Example

Theorem
3.4
and Example
3.5

clearly show that a gradient flow gives up to fit some data and rather focuses on the best-fit of the remained data (i.e., support vectors) to learn larger common tendency of data.
This may explain why and how ReLU networks generalize better than linear networks.
Then, one may wonder whether the large number of deactivation (i.e. smaller number of support vectors) is preferable. However, the following theorem says that if several local minima with different number of support vectors are feasible, the one with more support vectors is preferrable.

Theorem 3.6
.

Consider a single-neuron ReLU network (
1
) with
A2
. Let
{\bm{w}}_{1}^{*}
be a local minimum which has set of support vectors
S_{1}
, and
{\bm{w}}_{2}^{*}
be another local minimum which has set of support vectors
S_{2}
such that
S_{2}\subset S_{1}
. Then,
L({\bm{w}}_{2}^{*})\geq L({\bm{w}}_{1}^{*})
.

Later in Theorem

, we will show that the gradient flow dynamics tends to avoid bad local minima and converges to the one with a large number of support vectors, which is another important implicit bias of the gradient flow.

4
Gradient flow dynamics

4.1
Weight initialization

Recall Figure
2(a)
where a gradient flow initialized with small norm goes through the partition with all the data being active, after which some of the data become deactivated to reach a local minimizer. It turns out that weight initialization plays the key roles in this dynamics.
In particular, the norm of the initial weight plays key role to the activation of data, as explained in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

For a single-neuron ReLU network (
1
), consider
{\bm{w}}_{0}
in a partition
P
and define

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{*}:=\frac{{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{q}}}{{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{H}}{\bm{w}}_{0}}

for a data
{\bm{x}}_{j}
, where
{\bm{H}}
and
{\bm{q}}
are given by (
2
).
Then,
\nabla L(\alpha{\bm{w}}_{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}_{j}\geq 0
if and only if
\alpha\geq\alpha_{j}^{*}
.

Proposition
4.1
leads to a conclusion that a gradient flow with sufficiently large norm (i.e.
\alpha\geq\alpha_{j}^{*}
) satisfies the deactivation condition of Proposition
3.1
.
On the other side, for a gradient flow with small norm, we can expect that all data being activated, which is indeed true as shown in Lemma
F.5
. See also Figure
2(b)
.

Activation of data is a significant issue since Theorem
3.6
guarantees lower loss value for larger number of support vectors.

In the following theorem, we further suggest a condition of initialization point
{\bm{w}}_{0}
such that one specific data
{\bm{x}}_{j}
is kept activated on the gradient flow
{\bm{w}}(t)
.

Theorem 4.2

(No Deactivation)
.

Consider a gradient flow (
3
) in a single-neuron ReLU network under
A1
and
A2
. Suppose there exists
{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
such that
L({\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*})=0
.
Then,
{\bm{x}}_{j}
is always activated on the gradient flow initialized at
{\bm{w}}_{0}
if

{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{w}}_{0}>0
and

\displaystyle\frac{y_{j}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\|}>\left\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}\right\|.

(6)

In particular, if (
6
) holds for all
j=1,\cdots,n
, then the gradient flow initialized at
{\bm{w}}_{0}
coincides with that of a single-neuron linear network initialized at the same point
{\bm{w}}_{0}
, which converges to the global minimum.

The existence of
{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
that activates all data in Theorem
4.2
is easily satisfied in the overparameterized neural network. If (
6
) does not hold for some
1\leq j\leq n
, gradient flows of linear and ReLU networks do not coincide and the gradient flow of the ReLU network may not converge to the global minimum (see Example
5.2
).
Nonetheless, the following theorem shows that a gradient flow initialized under some conditions does not converge to a bad local minimum.

Theorem 4.3

(Gradient flow does not converge to a bad local minimum)
.

Consider a gradient flow (
3
) under the same condition of Theorem
4.2
. Let
{\bm{w}}_{loc}^{*}
be a local minimum and
S
be the set of its support vectors.
Now suppose the initialization point
{\bm{w}}_{0}
satisfies

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{x}}_{j}\in S^{c}}\left[\frac{y_{j}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\|}\right]\geq\left\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}\right\|.

(7)

Then, the gradient flow initialized at
{\bm{w}}_{0}
does not converge to
{\bm{w}}_{loc}^{*}
.

For example, consider two local minima
{\bm{w}}_{1}^{*}
and
{\bm{w}}_{2}^{*}
with their sets of support vectors
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
such that
S_{2}\subset S_{1}
. Then,

\max_{{\bm{x}}_{j}\in S_{1}^{c}}\left[\frac{y_{j}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\|}\right]\leq\max_{{\bm{x}}_{j}\in S_{2}^{c}}\left[\frac{y_{j}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\|}\right]

implies that (
7
) looks more feasible for
S_{2}
than
S_{1}
. This suggests that a gradient flow may not converge to a ‘bad’ local minimum that has few support vectors without crucial data in the sense of Theorem
3.6
. That is, we can say that a gradient flow does not converge to a local minimum with large loss value.
There is another interpretation of this theorem. Since
L({\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*})=0
, i.e.,
y_{j}={\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
for all
j
, (
7
) can be converted to

\displaystyle\max\limits_{{\bm{x}}_{j}\in S^{c}}\cos\theta_{j}>\frac{\left\|{\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}\right\|}{\left\|{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}\right\|}

(8)

where
\theta_{j}
is the angle between
{\bm{x}}_{j}
and
{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
, i.e.
\cos\theta_{j}=\frac{{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{w}}^{*}_{GM}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}_{j}\right\|\cdot\left\|{\bm{w}}_{GM}\right\|}
. Then (
8
) says that a data
{\bm{x}}_{j}
which has large
\cos\theta_{j}
value is not deactivated on the gradient flow, which is a candidate of crucial data.

In terms of Proposition
4.1
, the gradient flow with small norm tends to activate all data and the one with large norm does the opposite.
Therefore, the data which aligns well on the
{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
easily satisfies (
8
) and would be kept activated after it is activated with the help of small norm initialization.
On the other hand, the gradient flow with large norm initialization does not allow the data to satisfy (
8
) and may converge to the minimum with few support vectors.
In the middle of two realms, there is a critical region where
\left\|{\bm{w}}_{0}\right\|\approx\left\|{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}\right\|
. If the direction of
{\bm{w}}_{0}
is close with
{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
, then all data is activated, and the gradient flow easily converge to the global minimum. In contrast, if the direction of
{\bm{w}}_{0}
is far from
{\bm{w}}_{GM}^{*}
, the right hand side of (
8
) is much larger. Thus, we can conjecture that the convergence is sensitive to the direction of
{\bm{w}}_{0}
in this critical region.
Accordingly, our observations extends the existing results on why the gradient flow initialized with infinitesimally small norm is preferred in the gradient flow dynamics
Gunasekar et al. 2017
;
Razin & Cohen 2020
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
.

Finally, it is worth noting that even though
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{W}
is a cusp, every gradient flow initialized with infinitesimally small norm converges to the same local minimum.

Proposition 4.4
.

Consider a gradient flow (
3
) of a single-neuron ReLU network under
A1
,
A2
, and
A3
. Further suppose that the Hessian matrix
{\bm{H}}({\bm{w}}(t))
has full rank on the gradient flow until it converges. Then, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
{\bm{w}}_{0}
with
\left\|{\bm{w}}_{0}\right\|<\delta
and
-\nabla L({\bm{w}}_{0})>\mathbf{0}
, every gradient flow initialized at
{\bm{w}}_{0}
converges to the same point.

4.2
Norm increasing property

In Appendix
D
, we show
that linear regression has implicit biases that the gradient flow initialized at zero converges to the minimum norm solution, with strictly increasing its norm until it converges. In Theorem
F.6
, we extend this norm increasing property to single-neuron ReLU networks.
By the balancedness property of deep ReLU networks shown by
Du et al. 2018
, the norm of each intermediate layers increase together. Therefore, if one intermediate layer and its backpropagated labels defined in Appendix
C
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
F.6
, then every layer has norm increasing property together.
Also note that this norm increasing property does not contradict with the result of
Vardi & Shamir 2021
, since it does not need to converge to the minimum norm solution.
We provide such example in Example
5.2
.

Although the conditions for Theorem
F.6
look complicated,
for the special case of
d=2
, the condition becomes trivial
as there exists a special structure in the partitioned parameter space. More precisely, there is an ‘order’ for activation and deactivation, as the following lemma states.

Lemma 4.5

(Ordering of partitions in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
)
.

Consider the partitioned parameter space
\mathcal{W}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
under
A1
. Then we can impose an relative order between partitions. In particular, for any two partitions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
in the 2nd (or 4th) quadrant, either
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}~|~{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim P_{1}\}\subset\{{\bm{x}}_{i}~|~{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim P_{2}\}
or
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}~|~{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim P_{1}\}\supset\{{\bm{x}}_{i}~|~{\bm{x}}_{i}\sim P_{2}\}
holds.

This provides some useful geometric insights to understand learning dynamics of gradient flow.
We start with introducing an interesting lemma.

Lemma 4.6

(No revisit lemma for
d=2
)
.

Consider a single-neuron ReLU network with
d=2
under
A1
,
A2
, and
A3
. Then a gradient flow initialized with infinitesimally small norm does not re-activate any deactivated data.

With this lemma, we can obtain the global convergence and norm-increasing property in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Theorem 4.7

(Global convergence for
d=2
)
.

Consider a single-neuron ReLU network with
d=2
under
A1
,
A2
, and
A3
. Then, a nontrivial (
{\bm{q}}({\bm{w}}_{0})>\mathbf{0}
) gradient flow initialized with infinitesimally small norm converges to the global minimum with strictly increasing its norm.

6
Conclusion and future work

Understanding implicit bias of gradient descent has been an important goal in machine learning research. In this paper,
we investigated implicit bias of gradient flow dynamics in single-neuron ReLU networks with square loss and provided following observations.
First, we showed the implicit bias of gradient flows in terms of support vectors of ReLU networks to answer why and how ReLU networks generalize well.
Second, we revealed an implicit bias of gradient flow dynamics with respect to the norm of initialization. Specifically, we provided an initialization condition when a gradient flow keeps some data activated. Using this, we showed that a gradient flow with some condition on initialization does not converge to bad local minima.
Third, we extended the norm-increasing property of single-neuron linear networks to single-neuron ReLU networks under some conditions, revealing another implicit bias of gradient flows.
Finally, for a special case
d=2
, we proved the global convergence of the gradient flow.

Limitations.

This work is not free of limitations. It is worth to seek conditions so that Theorem
4.7
can be generalized to guarantee the global convergence in higher dimension
d>2
.
Second, perhaps using the label-backpropagation we proposed in Appendix
C
, we should generalize this result in deep ReLU networks.

Appendix B
Multi-neuron ReLU networks

In this section, we prove that the result of single neuron ReLU networks can be directly extended to sigle-layer multineuron case. Let
f_{\bm{W}}({\bm{x}})=[{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}]_{+}
be a single layer multineuron case with input dimension
d_{in}
and output dimension
d_{out}
. Let
{\bm{w}}_{1}^{T},{\bm{w}}_{2}^{T},\cdots,{\bm{w}}_{d_{out}}^{T}
be the row vectors of
{\bm{W}}
.
Then, the loss function (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle L({\bm{W}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|[{\bm{W}}{\bm{x}}_{i}]_{+}-{\bm{y}}_{i}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{out}}([{\bm{w}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}]_{+}-({\bm{y}}_{i})_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{out}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}([{\bm{w}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}]_{+}-({\bm{y}}_{i})_{j})^{2}.

Thus for each row vector
{\bm{w}}_{j}
, its gradient is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\bm{w}}_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}([{\bm{w}}_{j}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}]_{+}-({\bm{y}}_{i})_{j}){\bm{x}}_{i},

which is equivalent to the loss of single-neuron ReLU networks.
Therefore, a single-layer multi-neuron ReLU network is just a set of independent
d_{out}
single-neuron ReLU networks, and the result of single-neuron ReLU networks (Lemma
3.2
, Theorem
3.4
, Theorem
4.3
, Theorem
F.6
, Lemma
4.6
, Theorem

Appendix C
Label-backpropagation of deep ReLU networks

In this section, we show how single-neuron linear and ReLU networks form building blocks of deep ReLU networks.

:

l

Proposition C.1

(Label-backpropagation)
.

Consider an
l
-layer ReLU network
f(\cdot\;;{\bm{W}}^{(1)},\cdots,{\bm{W}}^{(l)})
with one data pair
({{\bm{x}}},{\bm{y}})
under square loss. Let
{\bm{x}}^{(i)}
and
{\bm{o}}^{(i)}
be input and output of
i
-th layer
(i=1,2,\cdots,l-1)
, which are defined by

\displaystyle{\bm{o}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle:=[{\bm{W}}^{(i)}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}]_{+}

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle:={\bm{o}}^{(i-1)}

where
{\bm{o}}^{(0)}:={\bm{x}}
,
{\bm{x}}^{(l)}:={\bm{o}}^{(l-1)}
and
{\bm{o}}^{(l)}:={\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{x}}^{(l)}
.
Define
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(l)}:={\bm{o}}^{(l)}-{\bm{y}}^{(l)}
and
{\bm{y}}^{(l)}:={\bm{y}}
. Now for
m=1,2,\cdots,l-1
, recursively define
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(m)}
and backpropagated-label
{\bm{y}}^{(m)}
by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{{\bm{x}}^{(m+1)}>\mathbf{0}\}}\odot{\bm{W}}^{(m+1)T}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(m+1)},

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}^{(m)}

\displaystyle:={\bm{o}}^{(m)}-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(m)}.

Then the gradient of
{\bm{W}}^{(i)}
of the
l
-layer ReLU network given by the data pair
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is the same with the gradient of
{\bm{W}}^{(i)}
of a single-neuron ReLU network
f^{(i)}
given by the data pair
({\bm{x}}^{(i)},{\bm{y}}^{(i)})
for
i=1,2,\cdots,l-1
.

Proof.

An
l
-layer ReLU network
f(\cdot\;;{\bm{W}}^{(1)},\cdots,{\bm{W}}^{(l)})
is modeled by

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}}\;;{\bm{W}}^{(1)},\cdots\!,{\bm{W}}^{(l)})={\bm{W}}^{(l)}[\cdots{\bm{W}}^{(2)}[{\bm{W}}^{(1)}{\bm{x}}]_{+}\cdots]_{+}

For a given data pair
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
, the square loss function is defined by

L({\bm{W}}^{(1)},\cdots\!,{\bm{W}}^{(l)})\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\left\|f({\bm{x}}\;;{\bm{W}}^{(1)},\cdots\!,{\bm{W}}^{(l)})-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Then, the gradient of
{\bm{W}}^{(m)}
is computed by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(l)}{\bm{x}}^{(l)\,T},

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{(m)}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)\,T}\quad\text{for}\quad m=1,2,\cdots,l-1.

(10)

See
Calin 2020
for a detail derivation. Now we focus on the last layer
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
. Consider a single-neuron linear network
f^{(l)}(\cdot\;;{\bm{W}}^{(l)})
with a given data pair
({\bm{x}}^{(l)},{\bm{y}}^{(l)})
. The square loss provides gradient of
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|f^{(l)}({\bm{x}}^{(l)}\;;{\bm{W}}^{(l)})-{\bm{y}}^{(l)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{x}}^{(l)}-{\bm{y}}^{(l)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=({\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{x}}^{(l)}-{\bm{y}}^{(l)}){\bm{x}}^{(l)\,T}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(l)}{\bm{x}}^{(l)\,T},

which is equal to (
9
). Therefore, the single-neuron linear network
f^{(l)}(\cdot\;;{\bm{W}}^{(l)})
with a given data pair
({\bm{x}}^{(l)},{\bm{y}}^{(l)})
provides the same gradient (
9
) for
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
.

Similarly, we can apply this argument for intermediate layers. For
m=1,\cdots,l-1
, consider a single-neuron ReLU network
f^{(m)}(\cdot\;;{\bm{W}}^{(m)})
with a data pair
({\bm{x}}^{(m)},{\bm{y}}^{(m)})
. Then, the gradient of
{\bm{W}}^{(m)}
is computed by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{(m)}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|f^{(m)}({\bm{x}}^{(m)}\;;{\bm{W}}^{(m)})-{\bm{y}}^{(m)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{W}}^{(m)}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|[{\bm{W}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)}]_{+}-{\bm{y}}^{(m)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{{\bm{W}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)}>\mathbf{0}\}}\odot({\bm{W}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)}-{\bm{y}}^{(m)})\right){\bm{x}}^{(m)\,T}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{[{\bm{W}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)}]_{+}>\mathbf{0}\}}\odot([{\bm{W}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)}]_{+}-{\bm{y}}^{(m)})\right){\bm{x}}^{(m)\,T}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{{\bm{o}}^{(m)}>\mathbf{0}\}}\odot({\bm{o}}^{(m)}-{\bm{y}}^{(m)})\right){\bm{x}}^{(m)\,T}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{{\bm{x}}^{(m+1)}>\mathbf{0}\}}\odot({\bm{o}}^{(m)}-{\bm{y}}^{(m)})\right){\bm{x}}^{(m)\,T}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(m)}{\bm{x}}^{(m)\,T},

which is (
10
). Therefore, the single-neuron ReLU network
f^{(m)}(\cdot\;;{\bm{W}}^{(m)})
with a data pair
({\bm{x}}^{(m)},{\bm{y}}^{(m)})
provides the same gradient (
10
) for
{\bm{W}}^{(m)}
.

To sum up, gradient of the
l
-layer ReLU network
f
can be equivalently obtained from
(l-1)
single-neuron ReLU networks
f^{(m)}
with
m=1,2,\cdots,l-1
and one single-neuron linear network
f^{(l)}
.
∎

This proposition means that training

single-neuron ReLU networks and one linear network. See Figure

Lemma C.2

(
Du et al. 2018
)
.

For an
l
-layer ReLU network, on a gradient flow, the difference of Frobenius norm of weight matrices of adjoined layers is invariant, i.e.,

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\left\|{\bm{W}}^{(m)}\right\|_{F}^{2}-\left\|{\bm{W}}^{(m+1)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right)=0.

By this property of deep ReLU networks, if an intermediate single-layer ReLU network has norm-increasing property (Theorem

This is one reason why studying a single-layer ReLU network is crucial.

Appendix D
Single-neuron linear networks

Single-neuron linear networks are trained as follows:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}L({\bm{w}}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}-y_{i})^{2},

(11)

Then, the gradient flow initialized at
{\bm{w}}_{0}
is given by solution of the following differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{w}}(t)}{dt}=-\nabla_{\bm{w}}L=-{\bm{H}}{\bm{w}}+{\bm{q}},\qquad{\bm{w}}(0)={\bm{w}}_{0}.

(12)

where
{\bm{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{1}&\cdots&{\bm{x}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
and

{\bm{H}}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{T}={\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{T},\quad{\bm{q}}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}={\bm{X}}{\bm{y}}.

Since
{\bm{H}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{T}
is positive semidefinite,
L
is convex with respect to
{\bm{w}}
. Therefore, the set of minima of (
11
) is equal to the set of stationary points of
L
.

Proposition D.1

(The manifold of stationary points)
.

Consider the loss function
L({\bm{w}})
in (
11
) and suppose the data matrix
{\bm{X}}
has rank
r
. Then,

(i)

every gradient flow converges to the global minimum.

(ii)

If
r\geq d
, the stationary point is unique, which is the global minimum given by
{\bm{w}}^{*}={\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{q}}
.

(iii)

Otherwise (i.e.,
r<d
), the set of stationary points forms a
(d-r)
-dimensional connected linear manifold containing
{\bm{w}}^{*}={\bm{H}}^{\dagger}{\bm{q}}
.

Proof.

This is a basic property of linear regression. See
Anton & Busby 2003
for detail.
∎

D.1
Norm-increasing property of single-neuron linear networks.

The goal of this subsection is to prove the norm-increasing property of single-neuron linear networks.
Thanks to the reduction principle (Theorem
E.1
) which will be explained later, without loss of generality, we can assume
{\bm{H}}
has the full rank.

Definition D.2

(Hyperrectangle of
{\bm{H}}
)
.

Consider (
11
) and suppose the Hessian matrix
{\bm{H}}
has full rank. For the unique global minimum
{\bm{w}}^{*}={\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{q}}
, let
{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{e}}_{2},\cdots{\bm{e}}_{d}
be the eigenvectors of
{\bm{H}}
with direction such that

. Then,

is defined by

\left\{{\bm{w}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}{\bm{e}}_{k}\in\mathcal{W}~|~0\leq c_{k}\leq c_{k}^{*}\right\}\subset\mathcal{W}.

.

Consider (
11
) and a gradient flow
{\bm{w}}(t)
. Define a function
g({\bm{w}}):=-{\bm{w}}^{T}\dot{{\bm{w}}}
. Then, the

is defined by the set

\{{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{W}~|~g({\bm{w}})<0\}\subset\mathcal{W}.

Proposition D.4

(Properties of the hyperrectangle of
{\bm{H}}
and the norm-increasing subset)
.

Suppose we have a unique minimum
{\bm{w}}^{*}
for (

).
Then the following statements hold.

1.

The norm of a gradient flow increases if and only if it is inside the norm-increasing subset.

2.

The norm-increasing subset forms a
d
-dimensional ellipsoid, where axes of the ellipsoid are parallel to the eigenvectors of
{\bm{H}}
.

3.

If a gradient flow initializes in the hyperrectangle of
{\bm{H}}
, it never escapes the hyperrectangle.

4.

The ellipsoid contains the hyperrectangle. In particular, their center points coincide at
\frac{1}{2}{\bm{w}}^{*}
and every vertex of the hyperrectangle lies on the boundary of the norm-increasing subset.

Proof.

1.

Consider the time derivative of
\|{\bm{w}}(t)\|^{2}
.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\|{\bm{w}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2{\bm{w}}^{T}\dot{{\bm{w}}}

\displaystyle=-2g({\bm{w}})

Therefore, the norm of the gradient flow
{\bm{w}}
increases if and only if

, which means
{\bm{w}}
is inside the norm-increasing subset from Definition

.

2.

Since
\nabla_{\bm{w}}L={\bm{H}}{\bm{w}}-{\bm{q}}={\bm{H}}({\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{*})
by Proposition

,

\displaystyle g({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=-{\bm{w}}^{T}\dot{{\bm{w}}}

\displaystyle={\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{H}}({\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{*})

\displaystyle=({\bm{w}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{w}}^{*})^{T}{\bm{H}}({\bm{w}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{w}}^{*})-\frac{1}{4}{\bm{w}}^{*T}{\bm{H}}{\bm{w}}^{*}.

Therefore,
g({\bm{w}})<0
is an ellipsoid centered at
\frac{1}{2}{\bm{w}}^{*}
, where its axes are parallel to eigenvectors of
{\bm{H}}
by
Anton & Busby 2003
.

3.

Since
{\bm{H}}{\bm{w}}^{*}={\bm{q}}
, (

) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{w}}}=-{\bm{H}}({\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{*}),\quad{\bm{w}}(0)={\bm{w}}_{0}.

(13)

For

of

.
From

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-{\bm{H}}t}({\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*})+{\bm{w}}^{*}

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}{\bm{e}}_{k}{\bm{e}}_{k}^{T}({\bm{w}}_{0}-{\bm{w}}^{*})+{\bm{w}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigg(e^{-\lambda_{k}t}{\bm{e}}_{k}(c_{k}^{0}-c_{k}^{*})+c_{k}^{*}{\bm{e}}_{k}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\bigg(c_{k}^{*}-e^{-\lambda_{k}t}(c_{k}^{*}-c_{k}^{0})\bigg){\bm{e}}_{k}.

Since

is inside the hyperrectangle, we get

for all
t\geq 0
. Therefore, the gradient flow never escapes the hyperrectangle.

4.

Since the hyperrectangle is convex, it is enough to show that the vertices of the hyperrectangle lie on the boundary of the ellipsoid.
Let

be one vertex of the hyperrectangle among
2^{d}
vertices. Then, it can be represented by

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{\bm{j}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}j_{k}c_{k}^{*}{\bm{e}}_{k},\qquad j_{k}=0\text{ or }1

where

. Subsequently,

\displaystyle g({\bm{v}}_{\bm{j}})

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}_{\bm{j}}^{T}{\bm{H}}({\bm{v}}_{\bm{j}}-{\bm{w}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}j_{k}c_{k}^{*}{\bm{e}}_{k}\right)^{T}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}{\bm{e}}_{k}{\bm{e}}_{k}^{T}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}(1-j_{k})c_{k}^{*}{\bm{e}}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}j_{k}(1-j_{k})\lambda_{k}c_{k}^{*2}

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore, all vertices of the hyperrectangle lie on the boundary of the norm-increasing ellipsoid, thus the hyperrectangle is contained in the ellipsoid.

We now rewrite the well-known properties of least-square linear regression in

Theorem E.1

(Reduction principle)
.

In the last case of Proposition

and

.

2.

The gradient flow (
12

{\bm{w}}_{0}+\overline{\mathcal{W}}

3.

L
has a unique minimum
\overline{{\bm{w}}}^{*}
in
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
.

For every minimum
{\bm{w}}^{*}
of

.

In other words, the dynamics of the gradient flow
{\bm{w}}(t)
in

Proof.

Since

has

Now consider spectral decomposition of

Define a projection operator
\varphi:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\varphi~:~

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\quad\longrightarrow\qquad\quad\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle\,{\bm{w}}\quad\longmapsto\quad\sum_{k=1}^{r}({\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{e}}_{k}){\bm{e}}_{k}^{T}.

Then it is easily checked that

and a direct sum decomposition

Now we are ready to prove the statements of the theorem.

1.

For any

,

\displaystyle L({\bm{w}}+{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(({\bm{w}}+{\bm{v}})^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}+{\bm{v}}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{x}}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}

2.

Let
{\bm{w}}_{\infty}
be a stationary point of

\varphi(\dot{{\bm{w}}})=\dot{{\bm{w}}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\varphi({\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\varphi({\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}(0))

\displaystyle=\varphi(\int_{0}^{t}\dot{{\bm{w}}}(s)ds)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(\dot{{\bm{w}}}(s))ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{{\bm{w}}}(s)ds

\displaystyle={\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}(0)

\displaystyle={\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}_{0}.

Therefore,
({\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}_{0})
is in
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
, which means the gradient flow lies on
{\bm{w}}_{0}+\overline{\mathcal{W}}
.

3.

From the construction,
\overline{\mathcal{W}}=
range
({\bm{H}})
. Therefore, Proposition
D.1
gives the unique stationary point
\overline{{\bm{w}}}^{*}
in
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
.

4.

Let
{\bm{w}}^{*}
be a minimum of
L
. From orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{W}=\overline{\mathcal{W}}\oplus\textup{ker}(\varphi)
,
{\bm{w}}^{*}=\varphi({\bm{w}}^{*})+({\bm{w}}^{*}-\varphi({\bm{w}}^{*}))

L({\bm{w}}^{*})=L(\varphi({\bm{w}}^{*})+({\bm{w}}^{*}-\varphi({\bm{w}}^{*})))=L(\varphi({\bm{w}}^{*}))

\varphi({\bm{w}}^{*})
is a minimum in
\overline{\mathcal{W}}

\overline{\mathcal{W}}
has a unique minimum
\overline{{\bm{w}}}^{*}

In particular, by (ii), we can say that the geometry of the gradient flow can be reduced to

∎

Lemma F.5

Consider a single-neuron ReLU network under

Proof.

For

∎

Consider a single-neuron ReLU network under

Proof.

By Lemma
F.5
, we can assume that the initialization point

For a data
{\bm{x}}_{i}

for all

For the Hessian matrix

\displaystyle h_{i}(t)

However, we show that this global convergence does not hold in high dimension (

Lemma F.10
.

For some constants
a_{i},b_{i}
, and
c
, the equation
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e^{b_{i}t}=c
has at most
n
zeros.

Proof.

We prove a weaken version of this Lemma first, which is
c=0
case.

Claim
:
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e^{b_{i}t}=0
has at most
n-1
solution.

Proof of Claim
: We use mathematical induction on
n
. For
n=1
, equation
a_{1}e^{b_{1}t}=0
has no solution and for
n=2
, it has at most one solution
t=\frac{1}{b_{1}-b_{2}}\log\frac{-a_{2}}{a_{1}}
if
\frac{-a_{2}}{a_{1}}>0
. Now suppose it holds for
n
and consider
n+1
step. WLOG assume
a_{n+1}\neq 0
and deform the equation
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}e^{b_{i}t}=0
to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}e^{b_{i}t}=a_{n+1}e^{b_{n+1}t}(1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{n+1}}e^{(b_{i}-b_{n+1})t})=0.

(18)

Now let
g(t)=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{n+1}}e^{(b_{i}-b_{n+1})t}
. Then,

g^{\prime}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i}(b_{i}-b_{n+1})}{a_{n+1}}e^{(b_{i}-b_{n+1})t}=0

has at most
n-1
solutions, by the induction. Therefore, (
18
) has at most
n
solutions, by Rolle’s Theorem. This completes the proof of the claim.
\hfill\qed

Now we return to prove the original lemma. For given
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e^{b_{i}t}=c
, it has a solution if and only if
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e^{(b_{i}+b_{n+1})t}-ce^{b_{n+1}t}=0
, which has at most
n+1
zeros by the claim.

Finally, we provide one example that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e^{b_{i}t}=c
has exactly
n
zeros. Take
c=1
, then there exists
a_{i}
’s such that
t=0,1,2,\cdots,n-1
are zeros for some distinct
b_{i}
’s because

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
e^{b_{1}}&e^{b_{2}}&\cdots&e^{b_{n}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e^{(n-1)b_{1}}&e^{(n-1)b_{2}}&\cdots&e^{(n-1)b_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}

above matrix is called a Vandermonde matrix, which is known to be invertible
Anton & Busby 2003
.
∎

Theorem F.11

(Reactivation of linear networks for
d>2
)
.

Consider single-neuron linear networks under
A1
,
A2
, and
A3
. There exists dataset
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that for a hyperplane
V
, a gradient flow crosses
V
at most
d
times.

Proof.

Recall (
15
). By translation and rotation, WLOG we can assume
{\bm{w}}^{*}
to be origin and
{\bm{H}}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_{1}},\cdots,e^{\lambda_{d}})
. Therefore, the gradient flow is given by

e^{\lambda_{2}t}w_{02}\\
\vdots\\
e^{\lambda_{d}t}w_{0d}\end{bmatrix}

Now suppose that we have a hyperplane

determined by
\{{\bm{w}}~|~{\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{v}}=c\}

(19)

Finally, by Lemma
F.10

19
) has at most
d
zeros. Therefore, a gradient flow of single-neuron linear networks can across the hyperplane
V
at most
d
times.
∎
