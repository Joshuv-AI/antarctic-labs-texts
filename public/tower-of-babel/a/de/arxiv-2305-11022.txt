Massively Parallel Reweighted Wake-Sleep
Thomas Heap, Gavin Leech, Laurence Aitchison

Abstract
Reweighted wake-sleep (RWS) is a machine learning method for performing Bayesian inference in a very general class of models. RWS draws K K samples from an underlying approximate posterior, then uses importance weighting to provide a better estimate of the true posterior. RWS then updates its approximate posterior towards the importance-weighted estimate of the true posterior. However, recent work [ Chatterjee and Diaconis 2018 ] indicates that the number of samples required for effective importance weighting is exponential in the number of latent variables. Attaining such a large number of importance samples is intractable in all but the smallest models. Here, we develop massively parallel RWS, which circumvents this issue by drawing K K samples of all n n latent variables, and individually reasoning about all K n K^{n} possible combinations of samples. While reasoning about K n K^{n} combinations might seem intractable, the required computations can be performed in polynomial time by exploiting conditional independencies in the generative model. We show considerable improvements over standard “global” RWS, which draws K K samples from the full joint.



1
Introduction

Many machine learning tasks involve inferring the latent variables from underlying observations
[
Jaynes 2003
,
MacKay et al. 2003
]
.
One approach to inferring these latent variables from data is to use Bayesian inference.
In Bayesian inference, we define a generative model which consists of a prior,
\operatorname{P}\left(\text{latents}\right)
, describing the probability of the latent variable before seeing data, and a likelihood,
\operatorname{P}\left(\text{data}\middle|\text{latents}\right)
, describing the probability of the data given the latents.
The goal is then to compute the posterior using Bayes theorem,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}\left(\text{latents}\middle|\text{data}\right)\propto\operatorname{P}\left(\text{data}\middle|\text{latents}\right)\operatorname{P}\left(\text{latents}\right).

(1)

However, computing this posterior is typically intractable, especially in more complex models where the likelihood or prior is parameterised by a neural network.

As an alternative, modern approaches such as variational inference
[
Jordan et al. 1999
,
Wainwright et al. 2008
,
Kingma and Welling 2013
,
Rezende et al. 2014
,
Blei et al. 2017
,
Nguyen et al. 2017
,
Zhang et al. 2018
,
Kingma et al. 2019
,
Gayoso et al. 2021
, VI; ]
and reweighted wake-sleep
[
Bornschein and Bengio 2014
,
Le et al. 2020
, RWS; ]
learn the parameters,
\phi
, of an approximate posterior,
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(\text{latents}\middle|\text{data}\right)
.
In VI, we learn this posterior by optimizing the evidence lower-bound objective (ELBO) using the reparameterisation trick
[
Kingma and Welling 2013
,
Rezende et al. 2014
]
.
This bound often has considerable slack, which can bias inferences.
To address this issue importance weighted auto-encoders
[
Burda et al. 2015
,
Cremer et al. 2017
, IWAEs; ]
draw multiple samples from the approximate posterior and use importance weighting to provide a tighter bound on the model evidence.
In RWS, we draw multiple samples from the approximate posterior, reweight those samples to approximate the true posterior, then update the approximate posterior towards the reweighted approximation of the true posterior (specifically, this is the wake-phase Q update; see
Bornschein and Bengio 2014
).

However, recent work
[
Chatterjee and Diaconis 2018
]
showed that the number of samples required to get accurate importance weighted estimates is very large.
Specifically, they showed that the required number of samples scales as
e^{\operatorname{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\operatorname{P}\left(z\middle|x\right)\middle\|{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z\middle|x\right)\right)}
.
This is particularly problematic because we expect the KL divergence to scale linearly in the number of latent variables,
n
.
Indeed, if
\operatorname{P}\left(z\middle|x\right)
and
{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z\middle|x\right)
are IID over the
n
latent variables, then the KL-divergence is exactly proportional to
n
.
Overall, this implies that we expect the required number of samples to be exponential in the number of latent variables, which is clearly infeasible for larger models.

This problem has been addressed in the IWAE context using TMC
[
Aitchison 2019
]
, which draws
K
samples for each of the
n
latent variables, and individually reasons about each of the
K^{n}
combinations of samples.
Here, we develop an analogous approach for RWS, which we call massively parallel (MP) RWS.
Critically, this is not a simple extension of the derivations in
Aitchison 2019
.
The derivations in
Aitchison 2019
are either restricted to factorised approximate posteriors, or use an augmented state-space viewpoint which cannot be applied to RWS.
We therefore give very different and considerably more general derivations in Sec.
4
.
Indeed, these more general derivations allow us to use a more general class of approximate posteriors, even in the original VI setting.

2
Related Work

Of course, our methods are based on fundamental work on VI
[
Jordan et al. 1999
,
Wainwright et al. 2008
,
Kingma and Welling 2013
,
Rezende et al. 2014
,
Blei et al. 2017
,
Nguyen et al. 2017
,
Zhang et al. 2018
,
Kingma et al. 2019
,
Gayoso et al. 2021
]
, IWAE
[
Burda et al. 2015
,
Cremer et al. 2017
]
and RWS
[
Bornschein and Bengio 2014
,
Le et al. 2020
]
.

Perhaps the most obvious related work is TMC
[
Aitchison 2019
]
, which also draws
K
samples for each of the
n
latent variables, and considers all
K^{n}
combinations.
The key difference to our work is that TMC only applies to VI, while our work applies to RWS.
However, our more general derivations improve on TMC itself.
Specifically, TMC is restricted to approximate posteriors that are IID across the
K
particles for one latent variable.
In contrast, our derivations allow us to couple the distribution over
K
particles for a single latent variable (Appendix
B
), which gives scope for e.g. applying variance reduction strategies.

Further, there is a body of work improving VI, but not RWS in specific restricted classes of model.

The first model class is a single-level hierarchical model, with a Bayesian parameter,
z_{0}
, common to all datapoints, and latent variables,
z_{1}\dotsc z_{n}
, each associated with a different datapoint.

Geffner and Domke 2022
propose a “local” importance weighting (LIW) scheme for this class of model, which contrasts with standard importance weighting schemes that they describe as “global”.
We adopt their “global” terminology for standard IWAE and RWS, which draw
K
samples from the full joint approximate posterior.
LIW in effect does IWAE separately for each datapoint: it separately draws
K
IWAE samples for the latent variables,
z_{1}\dotsc z_{n}
, associated with each datapoint,
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}
.
This looks very similar to TMC and massively parallel RWS, which draw
K
samples for the Bayesian parameter,
z_{0}
and the latent variables,
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}
, and reasons about all
K^{n+1}
combinations of all samples on
z_{0},z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}
.
However LIW differs from TMC and massively parallel RWS in that LIW draws only a single sample of the Bayesian parameter,
z_{0}
.
Of course, there are additional differences.
In particular, LIW, like TMC, ultimately performs VI, while massively parallel RWS applies RWS.
Further, massively parallel RWS (like TMC) can be applied to a very broad class of models, while LIW is restricted to these single-level hierarchical models.

A second class of models is timeseries models.
Massively parallel methods in timeseries may bear some resemblance to particle filtering/sequential Monte-Carlo (SMC)
[
Gordon et al. 1993
,
Doucet et al. 2009
,
Andrieu et al. 2010
,
Maddison et al. 2017
,
Le et al. 2017
,
Lindsten et al. 2017
,
Naesseth et al. 2018
,
Lai et al. 2022
]
, in that SMC/particle filters also reason over multiple samples for each latent variable.
However, work which learns a proposal/approximate posterior in the particle filtering setting focuses on VI rather than RWS
[
Maddison et al. 2017
,
Le et al. 2017
,
Lindsten et al. 2017
,
Naesseth et al. 2018
,
Lai et al. 2022
]
.
Moreover, most work in SMC / particle filtering considers only a restrictive class of timeseries model, while massively parallel methods operate in a very general class of models.
While there is some work extending SMC to more general generative models
[
Lindsten et al. 2017
, e.g. ]
, this work does not, for instance, give a mechanism to learn an approximate posterior using e.g. IWAE or RWS, let alone to have an approximate posterior whose structure differs from that of the underlying generative model.

3
Background

Here, we give background on IWAE and RWS, which are methods for performing Bayesian inference in a probabilistic generative model.
Both IWAE and RWS work with a collection of
K
samples of the latent variables.
The full collection of
K
samples is denoted
z
, while an individual sample (specifically the
k
th sample) is denoted
z^{k}
,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=(z^{1},z^{2},\dotsc,z^{K})\in\mathcal{Z}^{K}.

(2)

For standard global VI and RWS,
K
samples are drawn by sampling
K
times from the underlying single-sample approximate posterior,
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
, which has parameters,
\phi
,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z|x\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right),

(3)

where
\mathcal{K}=\{1,\dotsc,K\}
.

IWAE and RWS can be written in terms of an unbiased estimator of the marginal likelihood (Appendix
C.1.2
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}r_{k}(z),

(4)

\displaystyle r_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{k}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right)},

(5)

where
\operatorname{P}\left(x,z^{k}\right)
is the generative probability, and
r_{k}(z)
is the ratio of generative and approximate posterior probabilities,
r(z^{k})
.

3.1
Importance weighted autoencoder

In IWAE
[
Burda et al. 2015
]
, we optimize
\phi
and
\theta
using the IWAE objective,
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{global}}
, which forms a lower-bound on the marginal likelihood,
\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)
,

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)\geq\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{global}}(\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\log\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)\right]

(6)

Differentiating this objective wrt the parameters of the generative model is straightforward, as
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)
does not depend on
\theta
so we can interchange the expectation and gradient operators.
In contrast, the distribution over which the expectation is taken does depend on
\phi
, so the
\phi
update is more difficult to implement and requires reparameterisation
[
Kingma and Welling 2013
,
Rezende et al. 2014
]
.

3.2
Reweighted wake-sleep

In RWS
[
Bornschein and Bengio 2014
]
, we do not have a single unified objective.
Instead, we update the generative model and approximate posterior by drawing
K
samples from an approximate posterior,
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
.
We then use importance reweighting to bring those samples closer to the true posterior,
\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z|x\right)
, and do a maximum likelihood-like update with those reweighted samples.
In particular, the
\operatorname{P}
update resembles the M-step in EM, and maximizes
\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k},x\right)
for the reweighted samples.
Likewise, the (wake-phase)
{\operatorname{Q}}
update maximizes
\log{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
for the reweighted samples mirroring the true posterior, and therefore brings
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
closer to the true posterior,

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{\textrm{global}}

\displaystyle=

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{r_{k}(z)}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)}\nabla_{\theta}\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k},x\right)\right],

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{\textrm{global}}

\displaystyle=

(7b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{r_{k}(z)}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)}\nabla_{\phi}\log{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{k}|x\right)\right].

See Appendix
C.2.1
for a derivation of these updates.
However, it turns out that implementing the updates in (Eq.
7
) directly is difficult, as it requires us to separately compute the gradients for each sample,
z^{k}
.
Instead, we typically use,

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{\textrm{global}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\mathcal{P}_{\text{global}}(z)\right],

(8a)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{\textrm{global}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\left(-\log\mathcal{P}_{\text{global}}(z)\right)\right].

(8b)

See Appendix
A
for a proof of equivalence.

4
Methods

These previous approaches draw
K
samples from the full joint latent space.
However, the required number of samples scales exponentially in the number of latent variables
[
Chatterjee and Diaconis 2018
]
.
Thus, we define a massively parallel scheme in which we draw
K
samples for each latent variable, then effectively obtain
K^{n}
samples by considering all combinations of
K
samples for each of the
n
latent variables.
To that end, we denote each of the separate samples for separate latent variables
z_{i}^{k}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
, where
k
indexes the sample and
i
indexes the latent variable.
We can write the collection of
K
samples for a single latent variable (the
i
th) as,

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=(z_{i}^{1},z_{i}^{2},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K})\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{K}.

(9)

To sample all
K
copies of the full joint latent space, TMC
[
Aitchison 2019
]
uses an IID distribution over the
K
samples,
z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K}
,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z\middle|x\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z_{i}^{k}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right).

(10)

Here,
{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}
are the indices of parents of the
i
th latent variable under the approximate posterior.
In contrast, massively parallel methods allow for dependencies between the
K
samples for the
i
th latent variable,
z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K}
(Appendix
B
),

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z\middle|x\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right).

(11)

There are no formal constraints on these dependencies.
However, there are practical constraints, namely that we need to be able to efficiently compute the single-particle marginals,
{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)
.
In Appendix
B
, we give more specifics about choices of
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)
and
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z_{i}^{k}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)
.
At a high level, these distributions are constructed by mixing the underlying single-sample approximate posterior,
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}
, for different combinations of parent particles.

The generative model is more complicated, because we want to evaluate the generative probability for any of the
K^{n}
possible combinations of the
K
samples of the
n
latent variables.
To facilitate writing down these generative probabilities, we begin by defining a vector of indices,

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle=\left(k_{1},k_{2},\dotsc,k_{n}\right)\in\mathcal{K}^{n},

(12)

which has one index,
k_{i}
, for each of the
n
latent variables.
The latent variables specified by these indices is known as the “indexed” latent variables and can be written,

\displaystyle z^{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(z_{1}^{k_{1}},z_{2}^{k_{2}},\dotsc,z_{n}^{k_{n}}\right)\in\mathcal{Z}.

(13)

The generative probability can thus be written,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\mathbf{k}}\right)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k_{i}}_{i}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right).

Here,
{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}
are the indices of parents of the
i
th latent variable under the generative model, and
{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}
are the parents of the data under the generative model.
Our use of
{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}
mirrors our use of
{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}
to denote indices of parents of the
i
th latent variable under the approximate posterior.
Of course, the structure of the generative model and approximate posterior may differ, so
{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}
and
{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}
can also differ.

Looking at
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)
(Eq.
4
) we average only over
K
terms, corresponding to our
K
samples from the full joint latent space.
Our key contribution is to adapt RWS for the case where we average over all
K^{n}
combinations of samples for each latent variable, indexed
\mathbf{k}
.

We can define an alternative unbiased marginal likelihood estimator,
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
.
This estimator is obtained by averaging over all
K^{n}
combinations of all samples of all latent variables,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}}r_{\mathbf{k}}(z),

(15)

\displaystyle r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(x,z^{\mathbf{k}}\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)}

(16)

For the proof that
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
is an unbiased marginal likelihood estimator, see Appendix
C.1.3
.
By analogy with global IWAE, we can define an objective for massively parallel IWAE,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z\middle|x\right)}\left[\log\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)\right].

(17)

We prove that this quantity has the required properties (specifically, that it is a lower-bound on the log marginal likelihood) in Appendix
C.1.3
.
This quantity is very similar to that given in
[
Aitchison 2019
]
, except that it allows for a slightly more general proposal,
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}
, which allows for dependencies between the
K
samples for a single latent variable,
z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K}
.
Our key contribution is to design massively parallel updates for RWS,

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle{=}\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z\middle|x\right)}\left[\frac{1}{K^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}}\frac{r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)}\nabla_{\theta}\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}},x\right)\right]

(18a)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle{=}\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z\middle|x\right)}\left[\frac{1}{K^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}}\frac{r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)}\nabla_{\phi}\log{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}},x\right)\right]

(18b)

These updates are derived in Appendix
C.2.2
, and they can
be implemented using,

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z\middle|x\right)}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)\right],

(19a)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z\middle|x\right)}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\left(-\log\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)\right)\right],

(19b)

(see Appendix
C.2.1
).

Efficiently averaging exponentially many terms

It should be surprising that we can compute
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
(Eq.
15
) efficiently, as it involves summing over exponentially many (
K^{n}
) terms.
However, it turns out that efficient computation is possible if we exploit structure in the generative model.
To exploit structure, we first need to write down the generative probability for the
\mathbf{k}
th sample of all latent variables,
z^{\mathbf{k}}
.
This looks alot like Eq. (
14
), as it follows the same graphical model structure, with
{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}
and
{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}
giving the indices of parents of the data and the
i
th latent variable respectively,

\displaystyle r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k_{i}}_{i}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right).}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)}

If we fix
z
(i.e. all
K^{n}
samples of all
n
latent variables), then
r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
can be regarded as a big tensor with
K^{n}
elements, indexed by
\mathbf{k}
.
In that case, each term in the product defining
r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
(Eq.
20
) can also be regarded as a tensor.
The key observation is that the individual tensors in the product typically have only a few indices.
For instance, the probability of the data,
x
, depends only on the indices of samples of the parents (i.e.
(k_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)})
).
These indices of the samples of the parents can be written,

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}}

\displaystyle=\left(k_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}\right)\in\mathcal{K}^{\left\lvert{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}\right\rvert},

(21a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}

\displaystyle=\left(k_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right)\in\mathcal{K}^{\left\lvert{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right\rvert}.

(21b)

and where
\left\lvert{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right\rvert
are the number of parents latent variables.
To make explicit the idea that the individual terms in Eq. (
20
) can be understood as tensors, we define
f^{x}_{\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}}}(z)
as the tensor for the data and
f^{i}_{k_{i},\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}}(z)
as the tensor for the
i
th latent variable,

\displaystyle f^{x}_{\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}}}(z)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}\right),

(22a)

\displaystyle f^{i}_{k_{i},\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k_{i}}_{i}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)}.

(22b)

Thus, we can write
r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
as a product of these factors,

\displaystyle r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)

\displaystyle=f^{x}_{\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}}}(z)\prod_{i}f^{i}_{k_{i},\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}}(z),

(23)

and
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
can be understood as a big tensor product,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{K^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{n}}f^{x}_{\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(x\right)}}}(z)\prod_{i}f^{i}_{k_{i},\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}}(z).

(24)

This tensor product can be efficiently computed in polynomial time by ordering the sums and products using Python packages such as opt-einsum
[
Daniel et al. 2018
]
.

5.3
Comparing MP VI with TMC

Even though our main contribution is in developing massively parallel RWS, our derivations also allow for slightly more general massively parallel approaches to VI.
In particular, our derivations allow us to couple the proposal for the
K
samples of the
i
th latent variable,
z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K}
, while TMC
[
Aitchison 2019
]
forces these
K
samples to be IID.
This coupling in massively parallel methods allows us to introduce variance-reduction strategies inspired by methods for reducing particle degeneracy in particle filters
[
Carpenter et al. 1999
,
Li et al. 2012
,
Li et al. 2014
,
Zhou et al. 2016
,
Wang et al. 2017
]
(see Appendix
B
for further details).

To highlight these advantages, we considered two toy timeseries models: a single observation and a multi- observation model.

5.3.1
Single Observation

In the single observation model, there is a latent timeseries
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{30}
(we use
N=30
), and an observation,
x
, only at the last timestep,

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(26)

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{i-1},1/N),

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{N},1)

We use the prior to define the proposal (see Appendix
B
).
Results can be seen in figure
4
a.
For large
K
, all methods converge to the same value, as the ELBOs are all bounded by the true model evidence.
To compare the methods, we therefore need to consider their relative performance for smaller values of
K
.
We can see that the TMC (orange)
[
Aitchison 2019
]
performs considerably worse than massively parallel VI (red) and IWAE (blue)
[
Burda et al. 2015
]
.
We believe that TMC is performing poorly because of particle degeneracy
[
Carpenter et al. 1999
,
Li et al. 2012
,
Li et al. 2014
,
Zhou et al. 2016
,
Wang et al. 2017
]
.
In particular, the TMC proposal for
z_{i}
is given by a mixture of the prior, conditioned particles from the previous timestep,
z_{i-1}
.
In sampling from this mixture, in essence, we first sample a parent particle,
z_{i-1}^{k_{i-1}}
, then we sample from the prior, conditioned on that parent sample,
\operatorname{P}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|z_{i-1}^{k_{i-1}}\right)
.
In TMC, we choose these parent sample IID, which means that one parent particle,
z_{i-1}^{k_{i-1}}
may have zero, one or multiple children.
This is problematic: whenever a parent sample has zero children, then this reduces diversity in the samples of
z_{i}
, and this issue builds up over timesteps.
Massively parallel methods circumvent this issue by ensuring that each parent sample has one and only one child sample (which requires us to couple the distribution over
z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K}
), and IWAE avoids the issue by simply sampling
z_{i}^{k}
conditioned on
z_{i-1}^{k}
.
Massively parallel is comparable to IWAE in this setting due to conditioning only a single scalar value at the end of the timeseries.
These methods separate when we consider multiple observations (next).

5.3.2
Multiple Observations

Next, we considered a more standard timeseries with multiple observations.
We are implementing these methods in the context of a new probabilistic programming language.
This language currently has limitations on the number of latent variables that are inherited from the opt-einsum implementation.
As such, we were not able to do the obvious thing of having one observation at every timestep.
Instead, we had an observation every third timestep.

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(27)

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}((1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})z_{i-1},2/\tau),

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{i},1)\quad\quad\text{if $i$ divisible by 3}.

Again, we use
N=30
.
Results can be seen in Fig.
4
.
Again, the methods converge as
K
increases, but this time, massively parallel VI (red) gives better performance than both alternatives for lower values of
K
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced massively parallel RWS, in which we draw
K
samples for
n
latent variables, and efficiently consider all
K^{n}
combinations by exploiting conditional independencies in the generative model.
We showed that massively parallel RWS represents a considerable improvement over previous RWS methods that draw
K
samples from the full joint latent space.

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\Delta\phi_{\text{RWS}}\right]

\mathcal{P}_{\text{global}}(z)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\Delta\phi_{\text{RWS}}\right]

Appendix B
TMC vs massively parallel approximate posteriors

TMC approximate posteriors draw the
K
samples of the
i
th latent variable IID,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z_{i}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k_{i}\in\mathcal{K}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)

(37)

Specifically, TMC draws each sample from an equally weighted mixture over all parent particles,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{K^{|{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}|}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right).

(38)

In contrast, massively parallel methods do not force us to sample particles IID.
The key issue with IID sampling is that it introduces the risk of particle degeneracy
[
Carpenter et al. 1999
,
Li et al. 2012
,
Li et al. 2014
,
Zhou et al. 2016
,
Wang et al. 2017
]
.
In particle degeneracy, some of the parent samples (e.g.
z_{j}^{1}
where
j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}
) might have multiple children, in the sense that multiple
z_{i}^{k}
are sampled from the mixture component arising from
z_{j}^{1}
.
At the same time, some of the parents, (e.g.
z_{j}^{2}
) might have no children, in the sense that no
z_{i}^{k}
are sampled from a mixture component arising from
z_{j}^{2}
.
This is problematic because it reduces diversity in the population of samples,
z_{i}=(z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K})
, and this reduction in diversity can be especially problematic in models with long chains of latent variables, such as timeseries models.
To reduce the risk of particle degeneracy, the massively parallel methods considered here couple the distribution over each of the
K
particles,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\neq\prod_{k_{i}\in\mathcal{K}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{TMC}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right).

(39)

However, we do ensure that the marginal for a single particle is the same as for TMC,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{K^{|{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}|}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{global}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right).

(40)

To achieve this, we sample a permutation,
\pi
for each latent variable, and the permutation tells us which parent particle to consider.
To give an example for one parent,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}\middle|\pi,z_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k_{i}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|\pi,z_{j}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|\pi,z_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k_{i}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}^{\pi_{k_{i}}}\right)

(42)

Critically, if we marginalise over the permutation, the distribution over a single
z_{i}^{k_{i}}
has the same density as that from a uniform mixture,

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|\pi,z_{j}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Q}}_{\textrm{MP}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k_{j}}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\right).

(44)

Finally, if we have multiple parent latent variables, we independently sample a permutation for each latent variable.

\displaystyle=\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k_{i}}_{i}\middle|z_{j}^{k_{j}}\text{ for all }j\in{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}\right)

(49)

Note that in Eq. (

), we allow for the possibility of a slightly more general form for the approximate posterior, where the distribution over
z_{i}^{k_{i}}
may depend on any of the parent samples.
This generalisation ensures that the subsequent derivations generalise to other possible forms for the approximate posterior, such as those for TMC (Eq.
11
).

z

z^{\mathbf{k}}
).
The
i

z_{i}^{/\mathbf{k}_{i}}
,

\displaystyle z_{i}^{/k_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left(z_{i}^{1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{k_{i}-1},z_{i}^{k_{i}+1},\dotsc,z_{i}^{K}\right)\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}^{K-1}.

(50)

and
z^{/\mathbf{k}}
are all non-indexed latents,

\displaystyle z^{/\mathbf{k}}

(51)

C.1
IWAE

C.1.1
Single-Sample VI

We begin by building intuition by looking at the derivation for the ELBO in the standard single-sample VAE.
We start by writing the marginal likelihood as an integral,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\int dz^{\prime}\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\prime}\right).

(52)

Here, we use
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
to denote a single sample from the full joint state space; we use
z^{\prime}
instead of
z
because
z
is reserved for
K
samples (Eq.
2
). Next, we divide and multiply by the approximate posterior probability,
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\int dz^{\prime}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\prime}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}.

(53)

Now, we can rewrite the integral as an expectation under the approximate posterior,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\left[\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\prime}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\right].

(54)

Now we take the logarithm on both sides and apply Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\log\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\left[\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\prime}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\right]\geq\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\left[\log\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\prime}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\right]=\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}

(55)

Of course, this derivation is specific to the single-sample VAE.
But we can pull out an underlying strategy that generalises to the multi-sample setting.
In particular, we first come up with an unbiased estimator of the marginal likelihood.
In our VAE, this is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{VAE}}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\prime}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}

(56)

Following Eq. (
54
) we can see that this quantity is an unbiased estimator of the marginal likelihood if
z^{\prime}
is sampled from
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\left[\mathcal{P}_{\text{VAE}}(z^{\prime})\right]

(57)

Then we apply Jensen’s inequality (Eq.
55
),

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}}=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z^{\prime}|x\right)}\left[\log\mathcal{P}_{\text{VAE}}(z^{\prime})\right].

(58)

However, this approach highlights key issues with the usual single-sample bound.
In particular, the single-sample estimator,
\mathcal{P}_{\text{VAE}}(z^{\prime})
can be very high-variance, and variance in the unbiased estimator causes the Jensen bound to become looser.

C.1.2
Global IWAE

To reduce variance in the unbiased estimator, a natural approach is to average
K
independent samples, and this is exactly what global IWAE does,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}(z)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{P}_{\text{VAE}}(z^{k})

(59)

This is of course an unbiased estimator, as it is the average of
K
unbiased estimators,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)\right].

(60)

Therefore, applying Jensen’s inequality gives a new lower-bound on the log-marginal likelihood,

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\log\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)\right]\geq\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\log\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)\right]=\mathcal{L}_{\text{IWAE}}

(61)

which is tighter than the usual single-sample ELBO
[
Burda et al. 2015
]
, and which matches Eq. (
6
) in the main text.

C.1.3
Massively Parallel IWAE

Our proposed
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
(Eq.
15
) is the average of
K^{n}
terms, rather than
K
terms in global IWAE.
To prove that our massively parallel strategy is valid, our strategy is to show that every term in this average is an unbiased estimator of
\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)
, in which case the average is also an unbiased estimator, and we can again apply Jensen.

Each term in the average
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
(Eq.
15
) is of the form
r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
(Eq.
16
).
The expectation of each term is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\mathbf{k}}\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}\right].

(62)

We can rewrite the expectation as an integral,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\int dz\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\mathbf{k}}\right)\prod_{i}\frac{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}.

(63)

Bayes theorem tells us,

\displaystyle\frac{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}},z_{i}^{/k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}={\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{/k_{i}}|x,z_{i}^{k_{i}},z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right),

(64)

Applying Bayes theorem,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\int dz\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x,z^{\mathbf{k}}\right)\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{/k_{i}}|x,z_{i}^{k_{i}},z_{{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}}\right).

(65)

Importantly, the integrand is a valid joint distribution over
x
and
z
, or equivalently over
x
,
z^{\mathbf{k}}
and
z^{/\mathbf{k}}
.
Thus, integrating over
z^{/\mathbf{k}}
then
z^{\mathbf{k}}
, we find,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right).

(66)

As such, each of the
r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
terms is an unbiased estimator of the marginal likelihood.
As
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)
(Eq.
15
) is just an average of
K^{n}

r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
terms, it is also an unbiased estimator.
Applying Jensen’s inequality to this unbiased estimator,

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\log\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)\right]=\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{MP}},

(67)

which mirrors Eq. (
17
) in the main text.

C.2
RWS

C.2.1
Global RWS

To build intuition, we first give a derivation of the standard RWS updates.
Ideally the updates would use samples drawn from the true posterior,
\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z|x\right)
,

\displaystyle\Delta\theta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z,x\right)\right]

(68a)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\log{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)\right]

(68b)

The
\operatorname{P}
update is exactly the M-step in EM, and the
{\operatorname{Q}}
step trains
{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)
using maximum likelihood based on samples from the true posterior.
To simplify the derivations, we note that both of these updates can be understood as computing a moment under the true posterior,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\left[\Delta(z^{k})\right].

(69)

For the
\operatorname{P}
update, we have
\Delta_{\textrm{post}}=\Delta\theta_{\textrm{post}}
and
\Delta(z^{k})=\nabla_{\theta}\log\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z,x\right)
.
For the
{\operatorname{Q}}
update, we have
\Delta_{\textrm{post}}=\Delta\phi_{\textrm{post}}
and
\Delta(z^{k})=\nabla_{\theta}\log{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z,x\right)
.
Of course, in practice, the true posterior is intractable, so instead we must use some form of importance weighting.
We begin by writing the generic form for the updates as an integral,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\int dz^{k}\operatorname{P}\left(z^{k}|x\right)\Delta(z^{k}).

(70)

We then multiply and divide by an approximate posterior,
{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\int dz^{k}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\Delta(z^{k}).

(71)

We can rewrite the integral as expectation over the approximate posterior,
{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\left[\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\Delta(z^{k})\right].

(72)

This quantity is difficult to use directly, because computing the posterior,
\operatorname{P}\left(z^{k}|x\right)
involves the marginal likelihood,
\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)
, which is intractable,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k}|x\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k},x\right)}{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)}

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)=\int dz^{k}\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{k},x\right).

(73)

As the true marginal likelihood is intractable, we instead use
\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)
(Eq.
4
), which is an unbiased estimator of
\operatorname{P}\left(x\right)
, and is correct in the limit as
K\rightarrow\infty

[
Burda et al. 2015
]
.
This gives updates of the form,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{global}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}\left[\frac{\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{k},x\right)}{{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z^{k}|x\right)}}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)}\Delta(z^{k})\right].

(74)

Remembering the definition of
r_{k}(z)
(Eq.
5
), this can be written,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{global}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{r_{k}(z)}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{global}}(z)}\Delta(z^{k})\right].

(75)

Finally as the expectation is the same for all
k
, we can average over
k
, which gives the expression in the main text (Eq.
7
)

C.2.2
Massively Parallel RWS

Now, we can move on to massively parallel RWS.
In the previous derivation for global RWS, we showed that each sample,
z^{k}
, individually constituted an unbiased estimator.
In the massively parallel setting, the key difference is that instead of having
K
samples
z^{k}
, we have
K^{n}
samples,
z^{\mathbf{k}}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)}\left[\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}})\right]=\int dz^{\mathbf{k}}\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}}).

(76)

Now, we multiply and divide by
\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\int dz^{\mathbf{k}}\left(\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)\right)\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)}\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}}).

(77)

Now, we introduce and integrate out a distribution over the non-indexed latent variables,
\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{/k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{i}^{k_{i}},z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int dz^{/\mathbf{k}}\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{/k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{i}^{k_{i}},z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right),

(78)

Multiplying Eq. (
77
) by
1
(Eq.
78
),

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\int dz^{\mathbf{k}}\left(\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)\right)\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)}\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}})\int dz^{/\mathbf{k}}\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{/k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{i}^{k_{i}},z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right).

(79)

Combining the integrals over
z^{\mathbf{k}}
and
z^{/\mathbf{k}}
into a single integral over
z
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\int dz{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z\middle|x\right)\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}\middle|x,z_{\textrm{qa}\left(i\right)}\right)}\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}}).

(80)

Writing the integral as an expectation,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}})\right].

(81)

Again, the posterior can be written,

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}}|x\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}},x\right)}{\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)}

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)=\int dz^{\mathbf{k}}\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}},x\right).

(82)

Again, the marginal likelihood,
\operatorname{P}_{\theta}\left(x\right)
is intractable.
Instead, we use the massively parallel estimate of the marginal likelihood, which was shown to be unbiased in Sec.
C.1.3
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{\frac{\operatorname{P}\left(z^{\mathbf{k}},x\right)}{\prod_{i}{\operatorname{Q}}\left(z_{i}^{k_{i}}|x,z_{{\textrm{pa}\left(i\right)}}\right)}}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)}\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}})\right].

(83)

Remembering the definition of
r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)
(Eq.
16
), this can be written,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\textrm{MP}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{{\operatorname{Q}}_{\phi}\left(z|x\right)}\left[\frac{r_{\mathbf{k}}(z)}{\mathcal{P}_{\textrm{MP}}(z)}\Delta(z^{\mathbf{k}})\right].

(84)

Finally, note that the expectation is the same for every value of
\mathbf{k}
.
Averaging over all
K^{n}
values of
\mathbf{k}
, we get the form in the main text (Eq.
18
).
