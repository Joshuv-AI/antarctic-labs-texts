On highly degenerate CR maps of spheres
Giuseppe della Sala, Bernhard Lamel, Michael Reiter, Duong Ngoc Son

Abstract
. For N ≥ 4 N\geq 4 we classify the ( N − 3 ) (N-3) -degenerate smooth CR maps of the three-dimensional unit sphere into the ( 2 ​ N − 1 ) (2N-1) -dimensional unit sphere. Each of these maps has image being contained in a five-dimensional complex-linear space and is of degree at most two, or equivalent to one of the four maps into the five-dimensional sphere classified by Faran. As a byproduct of our classification we obtain new examples of rational maps of degree three which are ( N − 3 ) (N-3) -degenerate only along a proper real subvariety and are not equivalent to polynomial maps. In particular, by changing the base point, it is possible to construct new families of nondegenerate maps.

32H02, 32V40

1.
Introduction

The sphere plays an extremely important role in CR geometry as it is the homogeneous “flat model” for
strictly pseudoconvex hypersurfaces in the sense of the canonical Cartan geometry
developed by Tanaka
[
43
]
and Chern and Moser
[
5
]
. We will denote the sphere in

\C^{n}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}=\bigl\{z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\C^{n}:\|z\|^{2}=|z_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|z_{n}|^{2}=1\bigr\},

and its representation as the Heisenberg group by

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{2n-1}\coloneqq\left\{(z,w)=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1},w)\in\C^{n-1}\times\C:\im w=\|z\|^{2}\right\}.

There is one more special role for
\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}
, which is that in higher codimension
m\geq n
,
there is an abundance of holomorphic
maps taking
\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}
, and yet, the
space of these holomorphic maps carries a rich structure not yet well understood,
and we will discuss some of the results in
order to put our results in context.

We will call holomorphic maps taking
\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}

sphere maps
from now on. Any sphere map can be composed with automorphisms of the spheres to obtain a new sphere map. In order to exclude these trivial constructions, we consider the class of sphere maps up to equivalence with respect to the composition of automorphisms of the source sphere and the target spheres.

Two notions of equivalence are important here: The first one with respect
to the isotropy groups at a distinguished point, and the second one with respect to the full groups, which include
translations
, i.e., automorphisms belonging to the transitive part of the groups with respect to a distinguished point. In order to distinguish the two, we are going to refer to the first one as
isotropical equivalence
, and the second one simply as
equivalence
.

We note that we might as well talk about smooth CR maps between spheres, and work by Forstnerič
[
26
]
and Cima–Suffridge
[
7
]
shows that such maps are rational with poles outside of the sphere.

It turns out that in certain cases that (excluding constant maps) the linear embedding
L\colon\C^{n}\to\C^{m}
given by
L(z)=(z,0)
is the only sphere map up to equivalence. This phenomenon is referred to as
rigidity
and was observed by Alexander
[
1
]
in the case
m=n\geq 2
, Webster
[
45
,
46
]
for
m=n+1\geq 3
, and finally Faran
[
25
]
and Huang
[
28
]
when
3\leq n\leq m\leq 2m-2
. These conditions are typically
thought of as ensuring “low codimension” in a suitable sense.

The situation is wildly different in “high codimension”.
When
n\geq 3
and
m=2n-1
Huang–Ji
[
30
]
found that there are two equivalence classes of sphere maps: The linear embedding
L
and the
Whitney map

W
given by

\displaystyle W(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},z_{1}z_{n},z_{2}z_{n},\ldots,z_{n}^{2}).

When
m=2n
, there exists the so-called “D’Angelo” family. These maps are of the form

\displaystyle F_{s}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\bigl(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},\sqrt{s}z_{n},\sqrt{1-s}z_{1}z_{n},\sqrt{1-s}z_{2}z_{n},\ldots,\sqrt{1-s}z_{n}^{2}\bigr),

and are pairwise inequivalent for
0\leq s\leq 1
, so that there are infinitely many equivalence classes. This phenomenon persists for
m\geq 2n
, see D’Angelo
[
9
]
;
Hamada
[
27
]
showed that for
m=2n
(and
n\geq 4
) these exhaust all equivalence classes. Note that
F_{0}
is equivalent to the map
W\oplus 0
, while
F_{1}=L
.
It was discovered by Huang–Ji–Xu
[
31
]
that for
n\geq 4
and
n\leq m\leq 3n-4
there are no “new maps” apart from
F_{s}
composed with the linear embedding
L
, i.e., maps equivalent to
F_{s}\oplus 0
, where
0
is the zero vector of appropriate length. This observation is now know as the
gap phenomenon
, see the survey
[
32
]
. There are further instances given in
[
33
]
and
[
2
]
.

D’Angelo’s result and the gap phenomenon show that for
n=2
rigidity fails for every
m\geq 3
,
we obtain “new maps” at every step, and we
have to think about every
m\geq n
as a “high codimension” case. Faran
[
24
]
proved that there are four equivalence classes of sphere maps when
n=2
and
m=3
, given by

(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto\begin{cases}\left(z_{1}^{2},\sqrt{2}z_{1}z_{2},z_{2}^{2}\right)\\
\left(z_{1},z_{2}z_{1},z_{2}z_{1}^{2}\right)\\
\left(z_{1}^{3},\sqrt{3}z_{1}z_{2},z_{2}^{3}\right)\\
\left(z_{1},z_{2},0\right).\end{cases}

Other proofs were provided by Cima–Suffridge
[
6
]
, Ji
[
34
]
and the third author
[
41
]
. The case where the real dimension of the source sphere is three has been examined
by many authors from different angles: D’Angelo
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
, D’Angelo–Kos–Riehl
[
13
]
, D’Angelo–Lebl
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
, D’Angelo–Grundmeier–Lebl
[
12
]
, D’Angelo–Xiao
[
17
]
, Lichtblau
[
39
]
and Meylan
[
40
]
.

Let us highlight the following structural results:
Lebl has shown in
[
38
]
that any rational sphere map of degree two from
\mathbb{S}^{3}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}
for
m\geq 4

belongs to the
family of monomial maps
D^{m}_{s,t}:\mathbb{S}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}
, given by

(1)

\displaystyle D^{m}_{s,t}(z_{1},z_{2})=\bigl(\sqrt{s}z_{1},\sqrt{t}z_{2},\sqrt{1-s}z_{1}^{2},\sqrt{2-s-t}z_{1}z_{2},\sqrt{1-t}z_{2}^{2},0,\ldots,0\bigr),

where
s
and
t
are two real parameters satisfying
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1

and
(s,t)\neq(1,1)
. Two maps of this family are equivalent if and only if they have the same parameters.
When
m=4
, then necessarily either
s=0
or
t=1
and we obtain the two one-parameter families of maps listed in
[
44
]
. This classification generalizes previously obtained results in
[
3
,
31
]
and
[
35
]
.

The complexity of sphere maps from
\mathbb{S}^{3}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}
is an intriguing, but widely open problem. When
m=4
there exists the classification of monomial maps by D’Angelo
[
9
]
, see also
[
44
]
.
So far, a complete list of sphere maps from
\mathbb{S}^{3}
into
\mathbb{S}^{7}
remains open.

Our main motivation in this paper is to investigate a particular
case of sphere mappings from
\mathbb{S}^{3}
in complete detail.
We call these maps
tangentially degenerate
at
p
. Roughly this means that their derivatives are
linearly dependent along the complex tangent plane at
p
and we
refer the reader to
Definition

3
for the technical definition.

Theorem 1
.

If
H:(\mathbb{H}^{3},0)\rightarrow(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1},0)
is a smooth CR map which is tangentially
(2,N-3)
-degenerate at
0
,
then
H
is either quadratic or isotropically equivalent to one of the following families of maps:

i)

\displaystyle H=(f,z^{2}\phi,w(a_{3}z+b_{3}w)\phi,b_{4}w^{2}\phi,0,\dots,0,g)/\delta,

where

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=z-\frac{i}{2}(2\mu-1)wz+\frac{\lambda}{2}w^{2}+\lambda^{2}w^{2}z+i\lambda wz^{2},

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=1+2\lambda z,

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=w(1-i\mu w),

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=1-i\mu w-i\lambda wz-w^{2}\left(2\lambda^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)-2\lambda\mu w^{2}z+2i\lambda^{2}\mu w^{3},

satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle\mu>1/6,\qquad 0<\lambda\leq\sqrt{\mu(6\mu-1)/3},

and
a_{3}=\sqrt{6\mu-1}/2
and
b_{3}=i\lambda/(4a_{3})
. Additionally if
N\geq 5
, then
b_{4}=(2\mu^{2}-\lambda^{2})/4-|b_{3}|^{2}
, and if
N=4
, then
\lambda=\sqrt{\mu(6\mu-1)/3}
.

ii)

\displaystyle H=(f,(z^{2}+aw^{2})\phi,a_{3}w(z-i\lambda w)\phi,0,\ldots,0,g)/\delta,

where

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=2z+i(4a+1)wz+\lambda w^{2}-8az^{3}+2i(8a+1)\lambda wz^{2}

\displaystyle-w^{2}z\left(2a^{2}-8a\lambda^{2}-a-2\lambda^{2}\right)-ia\lambda w^{3},

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=2(1+2\lambda z-iaw),

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=2w\left(1+2iaw-4az^{2}+8ia\lambda wz+aw^{2}\left(4\lambda^{2}-a\right)\right),

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=2+4iaw-8az^{2}+2i\lambda(8a-1)wz-2w^{2}\left(a^{2}-4a\lambda^{2}-a+2\lambda^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\penalty\ -4iawz^{2}-6a\lambda w^{2}z+2ia^{2}w^{3},

and
\lambda\geq 0,a\leq-1/16
and
a_{3}=\sqrt{-(1+16a)}/2
. If
N=3
, then

a=-1/16
.

Remark 1
.

We observe that, when restricting the maps in (ii) to
a=-1/16
, the image of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
projects to
\mathbb{H}^{5}
and we obtain, after setting
\lambda=s/2
the one-parameter family of maps of degree
3
from
[
41
, Theorem 4.1]
.

The proof of
Theorem

1
is provided in
section

5
.

It turns out that the two-parameter families of maps obtained as isotropical
equivalence classes in
Theorem

1
are not equivalent with respect
to (small) translations.

Corollary 1
.

Any map listed in
Theorem

1
is locally rigid, i.e., isolated in the quotient space of
(2,N-3)
-tangentially degenerate maps with respect to the full group of automorphisms. In other words, there is at most a discrete set of translations, which transform one given map into other maps of the list.

See also
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
and
[
21
]
for more information on local rigidity of CR maps.

The previous
Theorem

1
allows to solve one part of the classification of sphere maps and we prove the following result:

Theorem 2
.

Let
H:\mathbb{S}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
be a smooth CR map. Assume that the image of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
under
H
is not contained in a complex three-dimensional subspace of
\C^{N}
. Then
H
is constantly
(N-3)
-degenerate in an open set of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
if and only if
H
is quadratic.

A sphere map
H:\mathbb{S}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
is
constantly
s
-degenerate
in the sense of
[
36
]
if, at least generically, the space of holomorphic vector fields tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
along the image of
H
is of dimension
s
. When
s=0
the map is called
finitely nondegenerate
. See
[
42
]
and
section

2.3
for more details and an equivalent definition of degeneracy.

The motivation for our work was inspired by the analogue to
Theorem

2
in higher dimensions, which was obtained in
[
4
]
, when the source dimension
n
is greater than
4
.

It is well-known that if the map is
(N-1)
-degenerate on an open set, then it must be a constant map, see
section

2.5
below. If the map is
(N-2)
-degenerate on an open set, then it must be a linear map, see
section

2.6
below. The degree of the maps stated in
Theorem

2
does not follow from general considerations or known degree bounds. Together with
Theorem

2
we want to propose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1
.

If a sphere map is
s
-degenerate for
s>0
having linearly independent components, then it is of degree
N-s-1
.

Note that when
s=0
, the above conjecture cannot hold: The
group-invariant sphere map
from
\mathbb{S}^{3}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
is finitely nondegenerate and of degree
2N-3
, see
[
8
]
and
[
42
]
.

We would also like to point out that the target dimension of the maps given in
Theorem

2
cannot be deduced from the known bounds on the size of the image of sphere maps given in
[
4
]
(see also
section

2.8
below), which shows that these bounds are not sharp.

The proof of
Theorem

2
follows the line of thought carried out in
[
41
]
: We first provide a (partial) normal form for
(N-3)
-degenerate sphere maps with respect to automorphisms, which belong to the stabilizer of a base point.

Then we classify the normalized sphere maps, by using the fact that they are
(N-3)
-degenerate. Initially, this allows to reduce the complexity of the map by showing that all but two components of the map are a multiple of one holomorphic function by a linear or quadratic factor, depending on the case (Case I or II in
section

5
) under consideration as stated in
Lemma

6
below. A lot of computations aim to determine the map up to its
4
-jet. Again by the
(N-3)
-degeneracy the map must satisfy a system of three holomorphic functional equations, which allow us to solve for the components of the map.

We end up with two two-parameter families of rational maps of degree three as given in
Theorem

1
, which contain all normalized sphere maps, when we exclude those maps which are quadratic or which embed into
\mathbb{S}^{5}
. An interesting fact appearing in the proof of
Theorem

1
is, that case II, when most of the components factor by a linear term, actually is a subcase of case I, when most of the components factor by a quadratic factor. This follows from tedious computations: It would be interesting to be able to conclude this directly from the degeneracy condition.

The obtained maps in
Theorem

1
turn out to be
(N-3)
-degenerate at most along a proper real subvariety of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, hence all constantly
(N-3)
-degenerate sphere maps are quadratic or embed into
\mathbb{S}^{5}
.

Let us close this introduction by giving an overview of each section.
In
section

2
we provide all relevant definitions, in particular of the definition of degeneracy and give a bound on the size of the image of a sphere map, depending on the degeneracy of the map. In
section

3
we obtain a partial normal form for the
(N-3)
-degenerate sphere maps with respect to automorphisms fixing a point. In
section

4
we use the degeneracy to obtain an initial form of the map and show that all but two components are a multiple of a holomorphic function and a linear or quadratic factor. In
section

5
we give the proof of our main result
Theorem

1
. Let us point out that, that we used Mathematica in
section

5
. Nevertheless, all computations are elementary and straight-forward, but often tedious and contain expressions with many terms involved. We tried to find the shortest path to achieve our goal, although often a high level of complexity is inevitable, but we hope our representation meets this aspiration. Finally, in
section

5.3
we deduce some properties of the maps and the proofs of
Corollary

1
and
Theorem

2
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we provide relevant notions and definitions, and introduce the notation that we
are going to use.

2.1.
The unit sphere and its automorphisms

For coordinates in
\C^{2}
and
\C^{N}
,
N\geq 2
, respectively, and their conjugates as well as
coordinates in the complexification we write
Z=(z,w),\bar{Z}=(\chi,\tau)
and

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(z^{\prime},w^{\prime})=(z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{N-1}^{\prime},w^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\bar{Z}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\chi^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})=(\chi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\chi_{N-1}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}).

For vectors
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\C^{m}
and
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})\in\C^{m}
we write
\langle a,b\rangle=a_{1}b_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}b_{m}
and
\|a\|^{2}=\langle a,\bar{a}\rangle
.

The
unit sphere

\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
in
\C^{N}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}\coloneqq\left\{z\in\C^{N}:\|z\|^{2}=1\right\}.

Another representation of the sphere is given by the
Heisenberg hypersurface

\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
in
\C^{N}
, defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}\coloneqq\left\{(z,w)\in\C^{N-1}\times\C:\im w=\|z\|^{2}\right\}.

\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}\setminus\left\{(0,-1)\right\}
is biholomorphically equivalent to

\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
via the Cayley transform

\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}\setminus\left\{(0,1)\right\}\ni(z_{1},\dots,z_{N-1},z_{N})\mapsto\left(\frac{z_{1}}{1+z_{N}},\dots,\frac{z_{N-1}}{1+z_{N}},i\frac{1-z_{N}}{1+z_{N}}\right)\in\mathbb{H}^{2N-1},

with inverse

\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}\ni\left(z_{1},\dots,z_{N-1},w\right)\mapsto\left(\frac{2iz_{1}}{i+w},\dots,\frac{2iz_{N-1}}{i+w},\frac{i-w}{i+w}\right).

We are going to work with the CR vector field on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
given by

L=\frac{\partial}{\partial\chi}-2iz\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}
.
For a smooth function
h
depending on two complex variables
z
and
w
we write
h^{(k,\ell)}
for
\frac{\partial^{k+\ell}h(0)}{\partial z^{k}\partial w^{\ell}}
.

We denote by
\Aut(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1})
the automorphisms of
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
, that is, the linear fractional holomorphic maps taking
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}

into itself. Those have at most one pole on
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
.
We also denote by
\Aut(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1},0)
the collection of those
automorphisms which are germs of biholomorphisms at
0
of
\C^{N}
mapping
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
into itself.
The automorphisms of
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
which fix
0
form a group, which we denote by
\Aut_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1},0)
and refer to as the
stability group
of
(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1},0)
. Elements of the stability group can be parametrized as follows:

\displaystyle\phi_{\lambda,U,c,r}(z,w)=\frac{\left(\lambda U(z+cw),\lambda^{2}w\right)}{1-2i\langle\bar{c},z\rangle+(r-i\|c\|^{2})w},

where
\lambda>0
,
U
is a unitary matrix of size
N-1
,
c\in\C^{N-1}
and
r\in\R
. In addition, we are going to use the following automorphisms
of
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
which send
0
to

p_{0}=(z_{0},u_{0}+i\|z_{0}\|^{2})\in\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
:

\displaystyle t_{p_{0}}(z,w)=\bigl(z+z_{0},w+u_{0}+i\|z_{0}\|^{2}+2i\langle\bar{z}_{0},z\rangle\bigr).

The collection of all such automorphisms of
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
together with their inverses we refer to as (proper)
translations
; they actually can be thought of as elements of the stability group of the
point at infinity. We shall also refer to the map
t_{\infty}\colon(z,w)\mapsto(\frac{z}{w},-\frac{1}{w})
sending the point
0
to the point
at infinity as a “translation”. The proper translations form a subgroup of
\Aut(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1})
, which will be denoted by
T(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1})
.

Equivalently, we can work with the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
; its automorphisms are again the
linear fractional maps of
\C^{N}
taking
\mathbb{S}^{2N-1}
into itself. While in the unbounded
representation, a map can well have a pole on
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
(corresponding to sending a
point to the point at infinity), the pole set in the compact representation is outside of the
sphere.

2.2.
CR maps and spherical equivalence

Let
H
be a smooth CR map from

\mathbb{H}^{3}
into
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
written as

H=(f,g)=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N-1},g)
. We recall again that works of Forstnerič
[
26
]
and Cima–Suffridge
[
7
]
show that these maps are (restrictions of) rational holomorphic maps with poles outside of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Meylan
[
40
]
showed that their degree is at most
N(N-1)/2
, where the
degree
of a rational map is the maximal degree of the polynomials in the numerators and denominators of each component of the map. Degree bounds
for sphere maps are an interesting topic in themselves, and
we refer the reader to D’Angelo’s recent book
[
11
]
for
more information on them.

The map
H
satisfies the
mapping equation
(also
referred to as the
basic equation
), which in its “complexified”
form is given by

\displaystyle g(z,\tau+2iz\chi)-\bar{g}(\chi,\tau)=2i\langle f(z,\tau+2iz\chi),\bar{f}(\chi,\tau)\rangle.

This equation holds for all
z,w,\chi,\tau\in\C
for which the
expression is defined (we have to avoid the poles, of course).
An equivalent notion for the mapping equation is as follows:
H
sends
\mathbb{H}^{3}
into
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
if there is a
rational function
Q
with no pole at
0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{g(z,w)-\bar{g}(\chi,\tau)}{2i}-\langle f(z,w),\bar{f}(\chi,\tau)\rangle=Q(z,w,\chi,\tau)\left(\frac{w-\tau}{2i}-z\chi\right).

Composing a map with automorphisms trivially generates new maps. One is therefore interested
in the equivalence classes of maps with respect to “spherical equivalence”.

Definition 1
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}\colon\C^{n}\to\C^{N}
be sphere
maps. We say that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are
equivalent
if there exist
automorphisms
\psi\in\Aut(\mathbb{H}^{2n-1})
and
\psi^{\prime}\in\Aut(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1})
such that
H_{2}=\psi^{\prime}\circ H_{1}\circ\psi^{-1}
.

Equivalently, this means that there are open, connected subsets
U_{1},U_{2}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2n-1}
on which
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are actually
defined, if there are translations
t_{p}\in T(\mathbb{H}^{2n-1})
for
p\in\mathbb{H}^{2n-1}
and
t_{q}^{\prime}\in T(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1})
for
q\in\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
,
\phi\in\Aut_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{3},0)
and
\phi^{\prime}\in\Aut_{0}(\mathbb{H}^{2N-1},0)
such that

\displaystyle H_{2}=\phi^{\prime}\circ t^{\prime}_{q}\circ H_{1}\circ t_{p}\circ\phi.

Remark 2
.

By a Hopf lemma argument which seems to be “folklore” (see e.g.
[
28
, section 2]
and
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
for proofs), it follows
that a nonconstant CR map
H=(f,g)
of

\mathbb{H}^{2n-1}
into
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
satisfies
g^{(0,1)}>0
, which means that
H
is
CR transversal
at all points. Now the mapping
equation implies
g^{(0,1)}=\langle f^{(1,0)},{\bar{f}}^{(1,0)}\rangle
, so
H
is
immersive.

2.3.
Degeneracy of a CR map

A useful biholomorphic invariant in the study of CR mappings in positive codimension is their degeneracy as introduced in
[
36
]
. We first
recall the general definition:

Definition 2
.

Let
M\subset\C^{N}
and
M^{\prime}\subset\C^{N^{\prime}}
be real-analytic generic CR submanifolds of codimension
d
and
d^{\prime}
respectively. Close to
p\in M
and
p^{\prime}\in M^{\prime}
we assume that
M
and
M^{\prime}
are given by the vanishing of
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
respectively.
Write
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
for a basis of CR vector fields of
M
. Consider a smooth CR map
H:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
. For each
k\in\N
define the following subspaces of
\C^{N^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle E_{k}^{\prime}(p)\coloneqq\Span_{\C}\{L^{\alpha}{\rho^{\prime}_{jz^{\prime}}}(H(z),\overline{H(z)})|_{z=p}:\alpha\in\N^{n},|\alpha|\leq k,1\leq j\leq d^{\prime}\}.

The number
s(p)\coloneqq N^{\prime}-\max_{k}\dim_{\C}E_{k}^{\prime}(p)
is referred to as
degeneracy
of

at
p
. The map
H
is of
constant degeneracy

at

if
p\mapsto s(p)
is a constant function in a neighborhood of

. For the smallest integer
k_{0}
such that

for all
\ell\geq k_{0}
we say the map

is
(k_{0},s)
-degenerate
at
p
. When
s=0
we say the map is
finitely nondegenerate
at
p
or more precisely, that it is
k_{0}
-nondegenerate
at
p
.

Note that for a map
H=(f,g)
from
\mathbb{H}^{3}
into

\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
we have

E_{k}(0)=\Span_{\C}\left\{(0,1),(f^{(1,0)},0),\dots,(f^{(k,0)},0)\right\}.

Remark 3
.

It follows directly from the definition of degeneracy and the transitivity of the automorphism group that any map can be assumed to be of constant degeneracy
0\leq s\leq N-1
in a neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and sending
0
to
0
, when considering maps up to equivalence.

2.4.
Tangential degeneracy

In the following, we will be concerned with maps degenerating along
specific subsets; depending on how these are situated with respect
to the CR vector fields, the degeneration along these sets has strong
implications. For this, we work directly with
the Heisenberg hypersurface, denote its complexification by

\mathcal{M}
, with defining equation
equal to
w-\tau=2iz\chi
. The CR field
L
extends naturally
to a vector field on the complexification, again denoted by
L
, if
we set

L=\frac{\partial}{\partial\chi}-2iz\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau},

and consider a map
H=(f,g)
as before.
We note that for
(p,\bar{q})=(z,w,\chi,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}
, we can also
define

{\tilde{E}}_{\ell}(p,\bar{q})={\rm span}\left\{(L^{k}\bar{f})(p,\bar{q})\colon k\leq\ell\right\}\subset\C^{N-1},\quad{\tilde{E}}(p,\bar{q})=\bigcup_{\ell}{\tilde{E}}_{\ell}(p,\bar{q}).

The
degeneracy
of
H
at
(p,\bar{q})
is then defined to be
d(p,\bar{q})=N-1-\dim\tilde{E}(p,q)
,
and the
generic degeneracy
is given by
d_{H}=\max_{(p,\bar{q})\in\mathcal{M}}d(p,\bar{q})
, realized outside of a complex-analytic
subvariety of
\mathcal{M}
.
Note that generically (outside of a complex analytic subvariety of
\mathcal{M}
),

\tilde{E}(p,\bar{q})=\tilde{E}_{N-1-d_{H}}(p,\bar{q})
.

We also recall that with the Segre variety
S_{q}
associated to
q=(\bar{\chi},\bar{\tau})
,
which in our case is
the complex hyperplane given by
w=\tau+2iz\chi
, we have
(p,\bar{q})\in\mathcal{M}

if and only if
p\in S_{q}
. We now define the tangential degeneracy of a sphere map
H
.

Definition 3
.

The
tangential degeneracy
of
H
at
q
is defined to be

\tdeg_{H}(q)=\max_{p\in S_{q}}d(p,\bar{q}).

We say that
H
is
tangentially
(k,\tdeg_{H}(q))
-degenerate
if
\tilde{E}_{k}(p,\bar{q})=\tilde{E}(p,\bar{q})
for generic
p\in S_{q})
and
k
is the smallest integer with that property.

2.5.
(N-1)
-degenerate maps

We first recall that the maps of highest degeneracy are necessarily constant. We don’t yet need the
concept of tangential degeneracy for that.

Lemma 1
.

Let
H=(f,g)=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N-1},g):\mathbb{H}^{2n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
be a map.
If
H
is of degeneracy
N-1
at any point, then
H
is constant.

Proof.

Constant degeneracy
N-1
means in particular that
L\bar{f}=0
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. If
H
is nonconstant, then it is
transversal at
0
,
which implies that
\bar{f}^{(1,0)}=L\bar{f}(0)\neq 0
(see
Remark

2
). Hence
H
must be constant.
∎

2.6.
Tangentially
(1,N-2)
-degenerate maps

We note that a similar characterization appears in
[
4

using different results and methods, under a slightly stronger assumption than here.

Lemma 2
.

Let
H=(f,g)=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N-1},g):\mathbb{H}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
be a map with

\tdeg_{H}(q)=N-1
for some

Proof.

After translating
q
to
0
, we partially normalize the map. Since

is transversal, we may assume that

after applying a unitary matrix to

. Next, we consider
\Phi^{\prime}\circ H
, where

\Phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(z^{\prime},w^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{\left(z^{\prime}+c^{\prime}w^{\prime},w^{\prime}\right)}{1-2i\langle z^{\prime},\bar{c}^{\prime}\rangle-i\|c^{\prime}\|^{2}w^{\prime}},\qquad c^{\prime}\coloneqq-f^{(0,1)}\in\C^{N-1},

so that we may assume

.
From the degeneracy assumption we have

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}L\bar{f}_{1}&L\bar{f}_{k}\\
L^{m}\bar{f}_{1}&L^{m}\bar{f}_{k}\end{array}\right|(z,0,0,0)=0,

for

. We follow
[

, Lemma 16]
and use the fact that for a power series

we have

(4)

\displaystyle\phi(z,w,\chi,w-2iz\chi)=\phi(z,w,0,w)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k!}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial\chi^{k}}\biggl|_{\chi=0}\phi(z,w,\chi,w-2iz\chi)\chi^{k}

\displaystyle=\phi(z,w,0,w)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k!}(L^{k}\phi)(z,w,0,w)\chi^{k}.

Using (
2.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle(L\bar{f}_{1})(z,0,0,0)(L^{m}\bar{f}_{k})(z,0,0,0)-(L\bar{f}_{k})(z,0,0,0)(L^{m}\bar{f}_{1})(z,0,0,0)=0.

We divide by

, multiply with

and use the above fact (

\displaystyle L\bar{f}_{1}(z,0,0,0)

\displaystyle(\bar{f}_{k}(\chi,-2iz\chi)-\bar{f}_{k}(0))

\displaystyle-L\bar{f}_{k}(z,0,0,0)(\bar{f}_{1}(\chi,-2iz\chi)-\bar{f}_{1}(0))=0,

i.e.

\displaystyle\left(\bar{f}_{1}^{(1,0)}-2iz\bar{f}_{1}^{(0,1)}\right)\bar{f}_{k}(\chi,-2iz\chi)-\left(\bar{f}_{k}^{(1,0)}-2iz\bar{f}_{k}^{(0,1)}\right)\bar{f}_{1}(\chi,-2iz\chi)=0.

Using the normalization conditions

and

we get

for
2\leq k\leq N-1
. This implies that
f_{k}=0
for
2\leq k\leq N-1
and
so

gives rise to the map
(f_{1},g)
from
\mathbb{H}^{3}
into itself, which is a linear automorphisms by a well-known result of Alexander
[
1
]
.
∎

2.8.
Bounds on the size of image

In this section we study degenerate maps in general. The following estimate on the size of the image of a degenerate map was given before in
[
4
]
(see also
[
33
, section 5, step (II)]
, which itself is based on
[
28
]
, proof of Lemma 4.3). For the sake of completeness we provide a proof here.

Lemma 3
.

Let
H
be a nonconstant map, which is constantly
(k_{0},s)
-degenerate in a neighborhood of the origin and sends
\mathbb{H}^{3}\subset\C^{2}
into
\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}\subset\C^{N}
. Then

(a)

N=s+k_{0}+1

(b)

The image of
H
is contained in a subspace of
\C^{N}
of dimension at most
\binom{k_{0}+2}{2}
.

Remark 4
.

The estimate in
[
4
]
differs from the one obtained in
lemma

3
. By the normal form in
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
when the geometric rank
\kappa_{0}\leq n-2
, one has the property that pure
w
-derivatives (of order bigger than
1
) at
0
vanish. In fact, using the notation of
[
4
]
, it is assumed that
n\geq 5
and
\kappa_{0}\leq 2
. Hence, in
[
4
, (4.2)]
the derivatives when
|\alpha|=0
(assuming
1\leq b\leq l_{0}-1
) can be ignored, which results in the bound
\xi(l_{0})\leq\binom{l_{0}}{2}
. Thus it is estimated that the image of the map is contained in a subspace of dimension
d_{l_{0}}+2+\xi(l_{0})\leq l_{0}-1+2+\binom{l_{0}}{2}=\binom{l_{0}+1}{2}+1<\binom{l_{0}+2}{2}
.

Proof of
lemma

3
.

To show (a), we write
H=(f,g)\in\C^{N-1}\times\C
and

\displaystyle v_{0}=(\bar{f},i/2)\in\C^{N-1}\times\C.

and define for
1\leq m\leq k
,

\displaystyle v_{m}=(L^{m}\bar{f},0)\in\C^{N-1}\times\C,

and for
k\geq 1
we define the subspaces

\displaystyle E_{k}\coloneqq\Span_{\C}\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}\subseteq\C^{N}.

By
[
36
, Lemma 23–24]
we have the generic bounds
0\leq s\leq N-2
and
1\leq k_{0}\leq N-1-s
, which imply that the vectors
\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k_{0}}\}
, spanning
E_{k_{0}}
, (generically) are linearly independent. Indeed,
E_{k_{0}}
must span an
(N-s)
-dimensional subspace, which is only achieved if
k_{0}+1=N-s
.

For (b) we consider the tangential component
f
of
H=(f,g)
and define the subspace

\displaystyle F_{k_{0}}(p)\coloneqq\Span_{\C}\{L^{a}\bar{f}(p):a\leq k_{0}\}\subseteq\C^{N-1}.

Observe, that by the degeneracy, it holds that
L^{b}\bar{f}(p)\in F_{k_{0}}(p)
for all
b\in\N
. Write
T=\partial/\partial w+\partial/\partial\bar{w}
such that
T=\frac{1}{2i}[L,\bar{L}]
commutes with
L
and
\bar{L}
and
T\bar{f}=1/(2i)\bar{L}(L\bar{f})
.
For
k\geq 0
we define the subspaces

\displaystyle D_{k}(p)\coloneqq\Span_{\C}\{T^{a}L^{b}\bar{f}(p):a+b\leq k\}\subseteq\C^{N-1}.

Let
(c,d)\in\N^{2}
. Then we have, since
L^{c+d}\bar{f}\in F_{k_{0}}
and
\bar{L}^{j}L^{k}\bar{f}=0
if
j>k
, that

\displaystyle T^{c}L^{d}\bar{f}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{(2i)^{c}}\bar{L}^{c}L^{c+d}\bar{f}}_{\in\bar{L}^{c}F_{k_{0}}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq\min(c,k_{0})\\
k\leq k_{0}\end{subarray}}e_{jk}\bar{L}^{j}L^{k}\bar{f}\in D_{k_{0}},\qquad e_{jk}\in\C.

This shows
D_{k}\subseteq D_{k_{0}}
for all
k\geq 0
and thus

\displaystyle f(Z)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}f(0)}{\partial Z^{\alpha}}Z^{\alpha}\in\Span_{\C}\left\{\frac{\partial^{\alpha}f(0)}{\partial Z^{\alpha}}:\alpha\in\N^{2}\right\}=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}{D_{k}(0)}={D_{k_{0}}(0)}.

This implies that the image of
H
is contained in
D_{k_{0}}(0)
. To compute the latter, we note that
D_{k_{0}}(0)
is spanned by the derivatives of
H
w.r.t.
z
and
w
at
0
up to order
k_{0}
. Thus
\dim_{\C}D_{k_{0}}(0)
is at most the dimension of the space of polynomials in two variables of degree at most
k_{0}
, which is
\binom{k_{0}+2}{2}
.
∎

Initial form and statement of classification

In this section we study
(N-3)
-degenerate maps and reduce their complexity in a first step.

Lemma 5
.

Let
H=(f,g)=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N-1},g):\mathbb{H}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2N-1}
be a map which is tangentially
(2,N-3)
-degenerate at the origin, and satisfying the partial normal form of
lemma

4
. Then, for

, the component

satisfies

(10)

\displaystyle\left(2z^{2}+w^{2}f_{2}^{(0,2)}\right)f_{k}(z,w)=w\left(2zf_{k}^{(1,1)}+wf_{k}^{(0,2)}\right)f_{2}(z,w).

Proof.

We proceed similarly as in the proof of
lemma

2
and consider the determinant in (

), which is

\displaystyle\left|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}L\bar{f}_{1}&L\bar{f}_{2}&L\bar{f}_{k}\\

L^{m}\bar{f}_{1}&L^{m}\bar{f}_{2}&L^{m}\bar{f}_{k}\end{array}\right)(z,0,0,0)\right|=0.

Divide this equation by
m!
, multiply with
\chi^{m}
, sum over
m\geq 0
and use the fact from (
4
), to get

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}L\bar{f}_{1}(z,0,0,0)&L\bar{f}_{2}(z,0,0,0)&L\bar{f}_{k}(z,0,0,0)\\
L^{2}\bar{f}_{1}(z,0,0,0)&L^{2}\bar{f}_{2}(z,0,0,0)&L^{2}\bar{f}_{k}(z,0,0,0)\\
\bar{f}_{1}(\chi,0-2iz\chi)&\bar{f}_{2}(\chi,-2iz\chi)&\bar{f}_{k}(\chi,-2iz\chi)\end{array}\right|=0.

Setting

, assuming the normalization conditions
f^{(1,0)}=e_{1},f^{(0,1)}=0
, such that
L\bar{f}(z,0,0,0)=e_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}L^{2}\bar{f}_{2}(z,0,0,0)&L^{2}\bar{f}_{k}(z,0,0,0)\\
\bar{f}_{2}(\chi,-2iz\chi)&\bar{f}_{k}(\chi,-2iz\chi)\end{array}\right|\equiv 0.

It follows that

\displaystyle\left(\bar{f}_{2}^{(2,0)}-4iz\bar{f}_{2}^{(1,1)}-4z^{2}\bar{f}_{2}^{(0,2)}\right)\bar{f}_{k}(\chi,-2iz\chi)

By the normal form we have that

\displaystyle\left(1-2z^{2}\bar{f}_{2}^{(0,2)}\right)\bar{f}_{k}(\chi,-2iz\chi)=-2z\left(i\bar{f}_{k}^{(1,1)}+z\bar{f}_{k}^{(0,2)}\right)\bar{f}_{2}(\chi,-2iz\chi).

Thus we obtain after setting

We write

Lemma 6

With the notations introduced above in (

or

Next, assume that all of the

Let

and

5.1.

,

where

are constants. Then

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{g-\bar{g}}{2i}-|f|^{2}-|\phi|^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{4}|q_{k}|^{2}=Q(z,w,\bar{z},\bar{w})\left(\frac{w-\bar{w}}{2i}-|z|^{2}\right),

\displaystyle g(z,w)

\displaystyle=w(1+w\eta(z,w)),

\displaystyle f(z,w)

\displaystyle=z+wz\eta(z,w)+w\psi(z,w).

In particular
f(z,0)=z
.
Differentiating (

) with respect to
\bar{z}
twice and setting

Differentiating (

\bar{z}
and

\widetilde{H}\bigl|_{\Sigma}=(z,\phi(z,0),0)
