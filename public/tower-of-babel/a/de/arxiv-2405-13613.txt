Enumerating Graphlets with Amortized Time Complexity Independent of Graph Size
Alessio Conte, Roberto Grossi, Yasuaki Kobayashi, Kazuhiro Kurita, Davide Rucci, Takeaki Uno, Kunihiro Wasa

Abstract
Graphlets of order k k in a graph G G are connected subgraphs induced by k k nodes (called k k -graphlets) or by k k edges (called edge k k -graphlets). They are among the interesting subgraphs in network analysis to get insights on both the local and global structure of a network. While several algorithms exist for discovering and enumerating graphlets, the amortized time complexity of such algorithms typically depends on the size of the graph G G , or its maximum degree. In real networks, even the latter can be in the order of millions, whereas k k is typically required to be a small value. In this paper we provide the first algorithm to list all graphlets of order k k in a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) with an amortized time complexity depending solely on the order k k , contrarily to previous approaches where the cost depends also on the size of G G or its maximum degree. Specifically, we show that it is possible to list k k -graphlets in O ⁡ ( k 2 ) O(k^{2}) time per solution, and to list edge k k -graphlets in O ⁡ ( k ) O(k) time per solution. Furthermore we show that, if the input graph has bounded degree, then the amortized time for listing k k -graphlets is reduced to O ⁡ ( k ) O(k) . Whenever k = O ⁡ ( 1 ) k=O(1) , as it is often the case in practical settings, these algorithms are the first to achieve constant time per solution.

On the technical side, our contribution is the following. We note that enumeration algorithms based on the so-called binary partition approach often exhibit simplicity in both correctness and algorithmic behavior: binary partition involves determining how to expand each partial solution by either including an additional element

2

2.2
Basic techniques for efficient enumeration

Let us introduce in this section the main algorithmic techniques used in the paper.

Binary Partition
(also

or

\mathcal{S}
into two sets
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
such that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
.
Consider for example the enumeration of all
k
-graphlets in a graph
G=(V,E)
.
We first pick a vertex
v\in V
and partition
\mathcal{S}
, the space of all
k
-graphlets of
G
, into two sets: one containing all solutions that include
v
, the other containing all solutions that do not include
v
.
If the aforementioned sets contain more than one solution, we recursively proceed until we can print a unique
k
-graphlet.
This process defines an implicit tree structure
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
that we call the
enumeration tree
or recursion tree.
We will refer to a
X\in\mathcal{V}
as a tree
node
or an
iteration
, and we call a node
Y\in\mathcal{V}
a
child
of
X
if and only if there exists an arc
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{E}
.
The set of all children of
X
is called
ch(X)
.
The parent-child relation of
\mathcal{T}
can be defined as follows. Let
X
be a node of
\mathcal{T}
, corresponding to an instance of the problem on the graph
G_{X}=(V_{X},E_{X})
.
The children nodes of
X
either consider
v\in V
as part of the solution, or remove it from the graph.
Thus, the left child
X_{l}
of
X
obtains the same input graph
G_{X}
, but includes
v
in all subsequent solutions (e.g. by keeping it in a special set
S
), while the right child obtains the input graph
G_{X_{r}}=(V_{X}\setminus\{v\},\{(u,w)\in E_{X}\mid u,w\in V_{X}\setminus\{v\}\}
.

A key factor when designing an efficient enumeration algorithm based on this technique, is to ensure that each internal node in
\mathcal{T}
has at least two children.
Suppose that any internal node
X\in\mathcal{T}
can generate all its children in
T(X)
time, and that each leaf node produces exactly one solution.
When every internal node has exactly two children, the number of solutions, i.e. of leaves, linearly bounds the number of internal nodes, allowing us to conclude that the
amortized time complexity
of this enumeration algorithm is
O(\max_{X\in\mathcal{T}}T(X))
.
Notice that if there exists an internal node with exactly one child in
\mathcal{T}
, the amortized time complexity of the algorithm worsens to
O(d\cdot\max_{X\in\mathcal{T}}T(X))
, where
d
is the depth of
\mathcal{T}
.

This technique has been widely used in the literature for the enumeration of structures like

-
t

-

We now introduce another technique that can be used for the time complexity analysis of binary partition algorithms called
push-out amortization

Uno 2015
.
The idea of this kind of analysis is to speed up the computation of the nodes in the bottom level of the enumeration tree.
In fact, as each internal node has at least two children, the number of nodes in the bottom part is exponentially larger than that of nodes in top part.
Therefore, by speeding up the lowest nodes, we expect to exponentially improve the time complexity of the whole algorithm.
With this idea in mind, one has to design an algorithm respecting the following conditions: (1) the overall time complexity is dominated by the complexity of leaf nodes, and (2) leaves have “small” time complexity.
For reference, the independent set enumeration problem can also be solved efficiently by push-out amortization, as well as other enumeration problems
Kurita et al. 2021
;
Uno 2015
.

In order to prove that the total time of the bottom levels of an enumeration tree dominate the total running time of an algorithm, it is important to ensure that the total time of children nodes is somewhat
larger
than that of the parent node.
However, at the same time we must also ensure that the running time of leaf nodes is kept small, and satisfying both of these conditions is not always easy.
This is because usually when designing an algorithm in a way that the time complexity of a child node is large, it is difficult to ensure condition (2).
On the other hand, when we design the leaves with a small time complexity, it becomes difficult to ensure condition (1).
Uno
Uno 2015
established
a sufficient condition to solve the above situation called the
push-out (PO) condition
.

Theorem 1

(The PO condition
Uno 2015
)
.

If, for every internal node
X
of
\mathcal{T}
, it holds that

\bar{T}(X)=\sum_{Y\in ch(X)}T(Y)\quad\geq\quad\alpha T(X)-\beta(|ch(X)|+1)T^{*},

where
\alpha>1
and
\beta\geq 0
are constants and
T^{*}
is the worst case running time of processing any leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
,
then, the amortized time of each node
X
in
\mathcal{T}
is
O(T^{*})
.

Suppose, for example, that for every leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
, the algorithm outputs a distinct solution (we assume, for the sake of convenience, that one solution corresponds to exactly one leaf and vice versa).
If every internal node of
\mathcal{T}
has at least two children and
T^{*}
is upper bounded by a constant, then the algorithm runs in amortized constant time per solution.
The algorithm presented in
Section

4
is designed according to the push-out amortization strategy.

Finally, a note on data structures is in order: we are describing recursive algorithms that make changes to a graph as they proceed. Since creating copies of the graph for children recursive calls would dominate the complexity, we keep a single dynamic copy of the graph and edit it as necessary, undoing the changes as we backtrack.

We assume using a graph data structure that allows the key operation of

•

Iterating over the neighbors of a node
v
in
O(d_{G}(v))

•

Deleting or inserting an edge in
O(1)
time.

•

Deleting or inserting a vertex

O(d_{G}(v))
time.

•

Contracting an edge

O(d_{G}(u))
or

In particular observe how the first two items are the essential ones, as we can delete a vertex

This type of structure is typically considered folklore knowledge, but more details on how to maintain it can be found, e.g., in

2.3
Obtaining Delay Bounds

As pointed out earlier, it is possible to transform these amortized time bounds into delay. Let us briefly observe how.
In particular, we can use the
Output Queue Method

Uno 2003
.
It consists in creating a bounded queue
Q
of length
\left\lceil 2T^{*}/\bar{T}\right\rceil+1
, where
T^{*}
is the maximum delay, i.e. the cost of all the ancestors of a leaf iteration in the recursion algorithm that outputs a solution, and
\bar{T}
the average time between two outputs (i.e. the amortized cost).
During the execution of our algorithm, instead of outputting a solution directly, we place the solution into the end of
Q
.
If
Q
is full, then we pop the front and output that solution.
If we reach the end of the algorithm and
Q
is not empty, output all the remaining solutions.
By using this technique we can regularize the delay and make sure to output every a solution every
\bar{T}
time. In particular, we can obtain
O(k^{2})
and
O(k)
delay respectively for
k
-graphlet enumeration and edge
k
-graphlet enumeration.
We can then proceed to give amortized bounds on our algorithms, knowing that they can always be translated into delays with the Output Queue Method.
For more details on this technique, we refer the reader to
Uno 2003
.
In what follows, we will not explicitly adopt this technique in the pseudocode in order not to further complicate the analysis of our algorithms, but we note that the regularized delay is achievable through small modifications to each algorithm.

O(n+m+k^{2}\cdot N)

O(k^{2})
amortized time per solution.
The space consumption of the whole strategy is
O(n+m)
, needed by

by

does not require asymptotically more space.
∎

We remark that, as shown in

, which technically could be lower than

, although this is likely to be the case only in bounded-degree graphs.

4
Enumeration of edge
k
-graphlets and
k
-subtrees

In this section we address the problem of enumerating all edge
k
-graphlets for a given graph
G
. We observe that we can transform this problem into the
k
-graphlet enumeration presented in Section
3
by replacing
G
with its line graph (which represents the adjacency between the edges of
G
rather than between its nodes)
Harary and Norman 1960
.
This gives an amortized
O(k^{2})
-time algorithm for enumerating all edge
k
-graphlets, but we are able to achieve a better complexity for this task, namely
O(k)
amortized time per solution and
O(n+m)
overall space. A slight variation of what we propose in this section also gives an enumeration algorithm for all
k
-subtrees with the same time and space complexity.

For ease of reading, we summarize the key operations of the algorithm:

•

We use binary partition, where we select an edge
e
and enumerate (i) all solutions containing
e
, then (ii) all solutions not containing
e
.

•

We select
e
so that the partial solution
\mathit{In}
chosen so far is connected.

•

Every time we select or exclude an edge, we identify special types of edges (
unnecessary
,
mandatory
,
far
) and process them with dedicated efficient strategies, that allow us to amortize the time complexity via push-out amortization.

4.1
Trimming mandatory and unnecessary edges

In the following, given a graph
G=(V,E)
and two disjoint sets
\mathit{In},\mathit{Ex}\subseteq E
of its edges, such that
G[\mathit{In}]
is connected, we denote by
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},\mathit{Ex},k)
the set of edge
k
-graphlets in
G
that contain
\mathit{In}
and do not contain
\mathit{Ex}
. We denote by
V_{\mathit{In}}
the set of vertices
V(G[\mathit{In}])
, and we recall that
V(G[\mathit{In}])
contains all and only the vertices that are incident to at least one edge in
\mathit{In}
.

As
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},\mathit{Ex},k)=\mathcal{S}(G-\mathit{Ex},\mathit{In},\emptyset,k)
, we can simply ignore
\mathit{Ex}
and hereafter use the reduced notation
S(G,\mathit{In},k)
, and denoting the exclusion of a set of edges
\mathit{Ex}
by simply removing them from
G
, i.e.,
S(G-\mathit{Ex},\mathit{In},k)
. Recall that the removal of
\mathit{Ex}
can be done in
O(\left|\mathit{Ex}\right|)
time as remarked in Section
2.2
.

Moreover, as edges from
G
are removed, some edges may become
mandatory
: an edge
e
is mandatory if the connected component in
G-e
containing
V_{\mathit{In}}
has less than
k
edges (hence,
e
must be part of
all
solutions containing
G[\mathit{In}]
).
In principle, we could immediately add all mandatory edges directly to
\mathit{In}
, however, since we want to preserve
\mathit{In}
to be a connected subgraph, the algorithm will only add the mandatory edges adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
(and iteratively add any mandatory edge that may become adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
in this process).
while all mandatory edges must eventually be added to
\mathit{In}
, it is not necessary to do this immediately, as by only recurring into instances that have at least one solution, we will always finally reach a point where all the mandatory edges are adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
and are thus added to
\mathit{In}
. Let us consider the complexity of this operation:

We also want the algorithm to remove
unnecessary
edges : these are the edges that cannot be part of solutions containing
\mathit{In}
, as reaching them would require including more than
k
edges. We define these as edges
e
such that
dist(V_{\mathit{In}},e)\geq k-\left|\mathit{In}\right|
, i.e., those whose distance from the closest vertex of
V_{\mathit{In}}
to an endpoint of
e
is at least
k-\left|\mathit{In}\right|
.

We call the identification and removal of unnecessary edges from
G
, as well as the (iterative) addition to
\mathit{In}
of all mandatory edges adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
, the
trimming operation
, and denote it by
trim(
G,\mathit{In},k
)
.

More formally, the operation
trim(
G,\mathit{In},k
)
will return a pair
\langle H,\mathit{In}^{\prime}\rangle
where
\mathit{In}^{\prime}
is
\mathit{In}
plus the mandatory edges that were adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
, and
H
is the graph obtained by removing the unnecessary edges from
G
, i.e.,
E(H)=\{e\in E(G)|dist(V_{\mathit{In}^{\prime}},e)\geq k-\left|\mathit{In}^{\prime}\right|\}
. We now show that this operation can be performed in
O(|E(H)|)
time.

Lemma 4
.

Removing unnecessary edges in the instance
G,\mathit{In},k
can be performed in
O(\left|E(H)\right|)
time, where
H
is the graph obtained after removing such edges.

Proof.

We show how to find the edges of
H
in
O(\left|E(H)\right|)
time, from which we can directly construct a copy of
H
. Firstly, all edges of
\mathit{In}
are part of
H
. Moreover, if
\left|\mathit{In}\right|=k
then there is no other edge and we are done; otherwise, as
\left|\mathit{In}\right|\leq k
, any edge neighboring
\mathit{In}
can potentially be added and thus will be part of
H
.

To find the remaining edges of
H
let us contract all edges of
\mathit{In}
: as contracting an edge
\{x,y\}
can be done in either
O(d(x))
or
O(d(y))
(see Section
2.2
), we can complete the operation in
\sum_{v\in V_{\mathit{In}}}(d(v))
time by choosing one arbitrary vertex
r\in V_{\mathit{In}}
and iteratively contracting every edge
\{r,x\}\in\mathit{In}
adjacent to
r
in
O(d(r))
time. Indeed since
\mathit{In}
is connected, this will finally contract all its edges, and by the above observation all edges incident to
V_{\mathit{In}}
are part of
H
, so
\sum_{v\in V_{\mathit{In}}}(d(v))\leq\left|E(H)\right|
.

Let
r
be the vertex obtained by contracting
\mathit{In}
:
We can now perform a breadth-first search from
r
limited to distance
k-\left|\mathit{In}\right|
, which will identify all edges that belong to
H
without traversing any of the farther away ones that do not, i.e., we can identify all edges of
H
in
O(\left|E(H)\right|)
time.
∎

Now it is crucial to observe that, by definition, any edge that is mandatory cannot be also unnecessary. This means that the trimming operation can first remove unnecessary edges, obtaining
H
, and only then add the mandatory ones to
\mathit{In}
. Moreover, adding the mandatory edges does not increase distances from
V_{\mathit{In}}
, so it does not in turn create more unnecessary edges, and there is no need to reiterate the process. Thus, given
H
, we now focus on how to process the mandatory edges:

Lemma 5
.

Iterative identification and addition to
\mathit{In}
of all mandatory edges adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
, in the instance
H,\mathit{In},k
, can be performed in
O(|E(H)|)
time.

Proof.

Firstly, if
|E(H)|=k
then all edges are mandatory and we are done. Otherwise, any mandatory edge
e
must be a
bridge
, i.e., an edge whose removal disconnects the graph, causing the loss of more than just
e
. In the following let us call the number of edges unreachable from
\mathit{In}
when removing
e
the
loss
of
e
.

We can identify all bridges in
O(|E(H)|)
time
Tarjan 1974
, and these actually give us a tree-like decomposition where nodes are 2-edge-connected component (2ECC), and they are connected by the bridges.

Choose as root of this tree an arbitrary 2ECC that contains at least one node of
V_{\mathit{In}}
, and let us proceed in a bottom up way: for each leaf component
C
, its size is exactly the loss of the bridge connecting
C
to the tree (plus one, for the bridge itself). Going up, each internal component will cause a loss equal to its own size, plus the size of all its children bridges, which we have already computed in the bottom-up procedure. Of course, once we find a bridge in
\mathit{In}
or a component containing edged of
\mathit{In}
we can stop in this subtree since they cannot be removed. Clearly, a bridge is mandatory if its loss is more than
|E(H)|-k
, thus we can immediately label every such bridge as mandatory. This process takes
O(|E(H)|)
time as 2ECC are disjoint and the size of each is only computed once.

Finally, we go top-down from the components containing vertices in
V_{\mathit{In}}
and add to
\mathit{In}
all mandatory bridges adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
, as well as iteratively the mandatory bridges adjacent to them (as they became part of
\mathit{In}
). This part also takes
O(|E(H)|)
time as it can be done via a breadth-first search. This completes the proof.
∎

From Lemmas
4
and
5
we immediately obtain the following:

Corollary 6
.

The trimming operation
trim(
G,\mathit{In},k
)
, i.e., iterative addition of mandatory edges adjacent to
V_{\mathit{In}}
, and removal of unnecessary edges, can be performed in
O(\left|E(H)\right|)
time, where
H
is the graph obtained from
G
after removing unnecessary edges. Note that
|E(H)|\leq|E(G)|
.

For this reason, in the rest of this section we focus on the problem of enumerating the edge
k
-graphlets of a generic instance
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},k)
, with the understanding that any excluded edges (i.e.,
\mathit{Ex}
) and unnecessary edges will be handled by the trimming operation. We say that an instance is
trimmed
if it has no unnecessary edges and
\mathit{Ex}=\emptyset
.

4.2
Far edges

Given an instance of
G
, our partially formed solution
\mathit{In}
and
k
, our goal is to enumerate
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},k)
. However, to optimize the running time, we enumerate some of the solutions with a different strategy, using
far edges
.

We say that an edge
e
is a
far edge
if
dist(V_{\mathit{In}},e)=k-|\mathit{In}|-1
, and define the set of all far edges as
E_{\tt far}=\{e\in E\mid dist(V_{\mathit{In}},e)=k-|\mathit{In}|-1\}
. Intuitively, this means that the only way to find a solution including the edge
e
, is to include a shortest path from
V_{\mathit{In}}
to
e
. Thus we can transform the problem of enumerating the solutions that contain a far edge into a shortest-path enumeration problem, then remove
E_{\tt far}
from
G
to enumerate the rest.

Formally, we partition
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},k)
into two disjoint sets, which are enumerated separately:

•

\mathcal{S}_{\tt far}(G,\mathit{In},k)
is the set of solutions in
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},k)
that contain a far edge.

•

\mathcal{S}_{\tt near}(G,\mathit{In},k)
are the remaining solutions, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}(G,\mathit{In},k)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\tt far}(G,\mathit{In},k)
.

Observation 7
.

Let
H
be an edge
k
-graphlet in
\mathcal{S}_{\tt far}(G,\mathit{In},k)
. Then
H-e
must be a shortest path between
V_{\mathit{In}}
and an endpoint of
e
.

From
7
, we can enumerate all edge
k
-graphlets in
\mathcal{S}_{\tt far}(G,\mathit{In},k)
using an algorithm for enumerating all shortest paths.
Enumeration of all shortest paths can be done in constant amortized time with linear time preprocessing (see
(
Conte et al. 2023
, Section 4.1)
).
The authors of
Conte et al. 2023
gave a constant amortized time enumeration of all
s
-
t
paths on DAGs.
On the other hand, our problem is multi-source and multi-sink shortest path enumeration, but it is known that this problem can be reduced to a
s
-
t
path enumeration on DAGs (see, for instance,
Birmelé et al. 2013
).

As
\mathcal{S}_{\tt far}(G,\mathit{In},k)
can be enumerated efficiently, we then focus on the enumeration of
\mathcal{S}_{\tt near}(G,\mathit{In},k)
. Firstly, observe that we can find
\mathcal{S}_{\tt near}(G,\mathit{In},k)
by simply removing
E_{\tt far}
from

:

Lemma 8
.

\mathcal{S}_{\tt near}(G,\mathit{In},k)=\mathcal{S}(G-E_{\tt far},\mathit{In},k)
.

Proof.

For any
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\tt near}(G,\mathit{In},k)
,
H
cannot be in
\mathcal{S}_{\tt far}(G,\mathit{In},k)
as it does not contain any edge from

; hence,
H\in\mathcal{S}(G-E_{\tt far},\mathit{In},k)
. Vice versa, for any
H\in\mathcal{S}(G-E_{\tt far},\mathit{In},k)
,

is contained in
\mathcal{S}_{\tt near}(G,\mathit{In},k)
by definition.
∎

By Lemma
8
, it suffices to perform
trim(
G-E_{\tt far},\mathit{In},k
)
and enumerate all edge
k
-graphlets in the resulting trimmed instance.

4.3
Partition strategy

Algorithm

Recall that we select

A key idea to make the amortization work are

Lemma 9

Let

Firstly, since the instance is trimmed,

Observe also how

Suppose that

. Thus, this shortest path is not broken by the removal of

. This means

. Moreover,

and

are directly adjacent to

so

and

.

Let

∎

We first consider how trimming after removal of far edges does not remove further edges:

From the definition of the operation

satisfies the PO condition (Theorem
1
) by setting
\alpha=\beta=5/4
and
T^{*}=O(k)
.

Proof.

Let
F_{X}
be the set of far edges in
G_{X}
.
The number of children of
X
, i.e.,
\left|ch(X)\right|
, is at least
\left|F_{X}\right|+2
(at least one per far edge, plus two nested recursive calls).
The graph obtained by

G-F_{X},\mathit{In},k
)
has at least
\left|E_{X}\right|-\left|F_{X}\right|
edges by

adds

\left|E_{X}\right|-\left|F_{X}\right|
. The child on

4
removes

has size
\geq(\left|E_{X}\right|-\left|F_{X}\right|)/2
, each child derived from a far edge is a leaf, which takes

\left|F_{X}\right|
children of size
O(k)
each. Recalling as noted above that the constant factors in the big

, the cost of each recursive call given by

, and that
k\geq 2
(as

ch(X)
of
X
is:

\displaystyle\sum_{Y\in ch(X)}T(Y)

\displaystyle\geq c^{*}(\left|E_{X}\right|-\left|F_{X}\right|)+c^{*}(\left|E_{X}\right|-\left|F_{X}\right|)/2+c^{*}\cdot k\cdot\left|F_{X}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}c^{*}\left|E_{X}\right|-\frac{3}{2}c^{*}\left|F_{X}\right|+c^{*}\cdot k\cdot\left|F_{X}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}c^{*}\left|E_{X}\right|+c^{*}(k-\frac{3}{2})\left|F_{X}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{2}c^{*}\left|E_{X}\right|

\displaystyle\alpha T(X)-\beta(\left|ch(X)\right|+1)T^{*}

4.6

In this section, we propose an algorithm for enumerating all

4
.
Let
X
be a node of the recursion tree as seen in the previous sections,
G_{X}
be the associated graph,
\mathit{In}_{X}
be a set of edges, and
k
be an integer.
The main difference between this algorithm, and
Algorithm

4
lies in the partition strategy.
Indeed, for an edge
e=\{u,v\}\in\Gamma_{G_{X}}(\mathit{In}_{X})
such that
u
is incident to any edge of
\mathit{In}_{X}
,
an edge that connects vertices
v
and a vertex in
G_{X}[\mathit{In}_{X}]
cannot be added to
\mathit{In}_{X}\cup\{e\}
(otherwise it would form a cycle), hence we remove such edges from
G_{X}
.
The correctness of this algorithm can be shown using the same arguments adopted in Section
4
.
We can also prove that the PO condition holds for this algorithm: in particular, when we add an edge
e=\{u,v\}
to
\mathit{In}_{X}
, and there exist many edges that connect
v
and another vertex in
G_{X}[\mathit{In}_{X}]
, we can generate all subproblems (i.e. children nodes in the recursion tree) in linear time.
Therefore, we can enumerate also
k
-subtrees in
O(k)
amortized time, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Let
G
be a graph and
k
be an integer.
We can enumerate all
k
-subtrees of

is the number of

5
Conclusions and Future Work

We provided new algorithms for enumerating both vertex-induced and edge-induced subgraphs/subtrees in any simple, undirected graph, improving the state of the art for both problems.
This improvement over the fastest known algorithms in the literature was possible by the use of both traditional amortized analysis and the more recent push-out amortized analysis.
Our algorithms are the first to have time complexity depending
solely
on the size of the subgraph to be enumerated, i.e.,
O(k^{2})
amortized time for
k
-graphlets and
O(k)
amortized time for edge
k
-graphlets, in contrast with existing approaches that also include the size of the graph or its maximum degree in their complexity bounds.

The question on whether
O(k)
amortized time is attainable for
k
-graphlets is still open, in contrast to what we did with edge
k
-graphlets/subtrees, because a
k
-graphlet can have a quadratic number of edges, making this open problem challenging.
