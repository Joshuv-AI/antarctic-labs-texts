1.
Introduction

Define the Artin-Hasse exponential:

(1.1)

\displaystyle E(x)=\exp(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{p^{k}}}{p^{k}})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{n}x^{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{p}\cap\mathbb{Q})[[x]].

This exponential, and variations of it, play an essential role in the Dwork theory of exponential sums and the explicit computation of
L
-functions over finite fields. Its coefficients also have a rich combinatorial interpretation which will be the focus of this paper. Let
h_{n}
be the number of
p
-elements in the symmetric group
S_{n}
. It can be shown (Lemma 2.11 in
[
5
]
) that

u_{n}=\frac{h_{n}}{n!}.

To date, there are few explicit results involving these coefficients.
One can use the power series definition from Equation (
1.1
) and compute an explicit expansion of
u_{n}
(see Equation (2.9) in
[
5
]
). It is also known that the coefficients satisfy a recursive formula (Lemma 1 in
[
2
]
):

u_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}u_{n-p^{i}},

where we initially take
u_{0}=1
. More generally, Conrad has shown (see the proof of Theorem 2.10 in
[
3
]
) that the Artin-Hasse exponental
\bmod\ p
is not rational over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, that is,
(E(x)\bmod\ p)\not\in\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
.

Other properties about the coefficients are less resolved, and the main mystery is how the coefficients behave
p
-adically.
Conrad has conjectured (Section 5 of
[
3
]
) that they are uniformly distributed in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with respect to the Haar measure. Thakur
[
6
]
has also asked whether the Artin-Hasse exponential
\bmod\ p
is transcendental over
\mathbb{F}_{p}(x)
. In terms of the coefficients, Thakur’s question is whether or not the set of sequences of coefficients
\bmod\ p
,

\displaystyle\{({u}_{p^{i}n+j}\bmod\ p)_{n\geq 0}|i\geq 0\textrm{ and }0\leq j<p^{i}\},

called the
p
-kernel of the sequence
(u_{n}\bmod\ p)_{n\geq 0}
, is finite. See Section 6.6 in
[
1
]
for further information on
p
-kernels and Section 12.2 for automatic sequences over finite fields in general.

In this paper, we present a new identity involving the Artin-Hasse exponential coefficients. Before we state our result, let us motivate it. Define the Cartier operator on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[x]]
by:

\displaystyle U_{p}:\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[x]]

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[x]]

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{pn}x^{n},

and construct an operator:

\displaystyle\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[x]]\

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[x]]

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\mapsto U_{p}(E(x)\cdot f(x)),

where the action inside the
U_{p}
is just multiplication. The Cartier operator appears in both the Dwork theory of exponential sums (Chapter 5, §3 of
[
4
]
) and Christol’s theory of automatic sequences over finite fields (Section 12.2 of
[
1
]
). The matrix of this
\phi
operator with respect to the basis
\{1,x,x^{2},\cdots\}
is then:

\begin{bmatrix}1&u_{p}&u_{2p}&u_{3p}&\\
0&u_{p-1}&u_{2p-1}&u_{3p-1}&\cdots\\
0&u_{p-2}&u_{2p-2}&u_{3p-2}&\\
&&\vdots&\end{bmatrix}.

Our main result is the explicit computation of the leading principal minors of the transpose of this matrix. Given the general difficulty in dealing with the coefficients
u_{n}
, especially when
n>p-1
, it is surprising that a closed-form formula for these determinants exists. Explicitly,

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
1\leq\ell
,

|u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{k!p^{k}}{(pk)!}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times},

where we take
u_{n}=0
if
n<0
.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we reduce the determinant
|u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}
to one involving only binomials. Then, using a result of Tonne
[
7
]
, we are able to explicitly compute the determinant by counting certain types of Young tableaux.

This paper has three main sections. The first section is essentially independent from the rest of the paper, and in it we count the Young tableaux needed in order to get a closed-form formula from Tonne’s theorem. In the second section, we compute an expansion of
h_{n}
, Lemma
3.6
, which we use in the third section after exploiting a determinant trick, Lemma
4.1
, to finally prove Theorem
1.1
.

\sigma\in C_{t}^{n-1}
, because there are
(n-1)-|t|
numbers not appearing in
\sigma
,
\sigma
itself appears in the set
K
with multiplicity
(n-1)-|t|+1=n-|t|
.
On the other hand, suppose
\sigma\in C_{t}^{n}-C_{t}^{n-1}
so
\sigma
has an
n
in its cycle somewhere. By replacing
n
with all the numbers not appearing in
\sigma
, we can see there are
n-|t|
many
\tau\in C_{t}^{n-1}
such that
r_{n}(\tau,j)=\sigma
, some
j
.

Therefore since every element in
K
has multiplicity
n-|t|
and
K
has cardinality
nC_{t}^{n-1}
, the claim follows.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

If
t\neq 1
, then:

\displaystyle C_{t}^{n}=\binom{n}{|t|}C_{t}^{|t|}.

Proof.

Inductively applying Lemma
3.4
yields:

\displaystyle C_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-|t|}C_{t}^{n-1}=\frac{n}{n-|t|}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-1-|t|}C_{t}^{n-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-|t|}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-1-|t|}\cdot\frac{n-2}{n-2-|t|}\cdots\frac{|t|+1}{1}C_{t}^{|t|}.

∎

Lemma 3.6
.

For
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle h_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\binom{n}{kp}C_{k},

where
C_{k}=\sum_{t\in I^{k}}C_{t}^{kp}
.

Proof.

The lemma follows from Corollary
3.5
, and because:

\displaystyle h_{n}=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in I_{n}\\
t\neq 1\end{subarray}}C_{t}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\sum_{t\in I^{k}}C_{t}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\sum_{t\in I^{k}}\binom{n}{kp}C_{t}^{kp}=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\binom{n}{kp}\cdot(\sum_{t\in I^{k}}C_{t}^{kp}).

∎

4.
The Determinant

In this section we will piece together the results of the previous two sections and prove our main theorem.
We start with a technical lemma which is key to proving Proposition
4.2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any formal variables
E_{j,k}
and
X_{i,k}
,

|\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}E_{j,i-k+1}X_{i,k}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|E_{j,k}|_{1\leq j,k\leq\ell}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i,1}.

Proof.

Consider the matrix
M
:

\displaystyle M=|E_{j,i}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}\cdot|X_{i,i-j+1}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell},

taking
X_{i,k}=0
if
k<1
. The entry
(M)_{i,j}
is given by:

(M)_{i,j}=\sum_{v=1}^{\ell}X_{i,i-v+1}E_{j,v}=\sum_{v=1}^{i}X_{i,i-v+1}E_{j,v}.

The claim then follows since
|X_{i,i-j+1}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}
is lower triangular and equals

\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i,1}
.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
i,j\geq 1
and
\alpha_{j},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
. For any
\ell\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|\alpha_{j}\beta_{i}u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|\frac{\alpha_{j}\beta_{i}}{(pk-j)!}|_{1\leq j,k\leq\ell}.

Proof.

Let
i,j\geq 1
with
pi-j\geq 0
. By Lemma
3.6
:

\displaystyle h_{pi-j}=\sum_{k=1}^{i-\left\lfloor j/p\right\rfloor}\binom{ip-j}{kp}C_{k}.

Hence:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{j}\beta_{i}}{(pi-j)!}h_{pi-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{i-\left\lfloor j/p\right\rfloor}\left(\frac{\alpha_{j}}{(p(i-k)-j)!}\right)\left(\beta_{i}\frac{C_{k}}{(pk)!}\right).

Thus if
X_{i,k}=\beta_{i}\frac{C_{k}}{(pk)!}
and

\displaystyle E_{j,k}=\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{(pk-j)!}&k\geq\left\lfloor j/p\right\rfloor\\
0&k<\left\lfloor j/p\right\rfloor\end{cases},

then Equation (
4.1
) becomes:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{j}\beta_{i}}{(pi-j)!}h_{pi-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}E_{j,i-k}X_{i,k}.

So by Lemma
4.1
,

\displaystyle|\alpha_{j}\beta_{i}u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}

\displaystyle=|\frac{\alpha_{j}\beta_{i}}{(pi-j)!}h_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|E_{j,k}|_{1\leq j,k\leq\ell}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i,0}

\displaystyle=|\frac{\alpha_{j}\beta_{k}}{(pk-j)!}|_{1\leq j,k\leq\ell}.

∎

Corollary 4.3
.

For any
\ell\geq 1
,

|\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|\binom{pi}{j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}.

Proof.

Taking
\alpha_{j}=1/j!
and
\beta_{i}=(pi)!
in Lemma
4.2
yields:

\displaystyle|\frac{(pi)!}{j!}u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}

\displaystyle=|\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|\frac{(pi)!}{j!(pi-j)!}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|\binom{pi}{j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}.

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

For any
\ell\geq 1
,

|\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=p^{\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}k}.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.3
and Theorem 1 in
[
7
]
,

\displaystyle|\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=|\binom{pi}{j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=p^{\ell}\cdot|T_{n-1}|,

and the theorem follows by Corollary
2.9
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

For any
\ell\geq 1
,

|u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{k!p^{k}}{(pk)!}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{\times}.

Proof.

Observe that

\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}=\frac{(pi)!}{j!(pi-j)!}h_{pi-j}=\frac{(pi)!}{j!}u_{pi-j},

so that
u_{pi-j}=\frac{\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}}{\frac{(pi)!}{j!}}
. Then, by Theorem
4.4
,

|u_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}=\frac{|\binom{pi}{j}h_{pi-j}|_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{(pi)!}{i!}}=\frac{p^{\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}k}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{(pi)!}{i!}}=\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{k!p^{k}}{(pk)!},

and so since

\displaystyle\ordp_{p}\left(\frac{k!p^{k}}{(kp)!}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{k}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor+k-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{kp}{p^{j}}\right\rfloor=0,

the claim follows.
∎
