Low Rank Matrix Approximation in Linear Time
Sariel Har-Peled

Abstract
Given a matrix ℳ \mathcal{M} with n n rows and d d columns, and fixed k k and ε \varepsilon , we present an algorithm that in linear time (i.e., O ⁡ ( 𝖭 ) O(\mathsf{N}{}) ) computes a k k -rank matrix ℬ \mathcal{B} with approximation error ‖ ℳ − ℬ ‖ F 2 ≤ ( 1 + ε ) ​ μ opt ​ ( ℳ , k ) \left\|{\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{B}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(\mathcal{M},k\right) , where 𝖭 = n ​ d \mathsf{N}{}=nd is the input size, and μ opt ​ ( ℳ , k ) \mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(\mathcal{M},k\right) is the minimum error of a k k -rank approximation to ℳ \mathcal{M} . This algorithm succeeds with constant probability, and to our knowledge it is the first linear-time algorithm to achieve multiplicative approximation.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of computing a low rank
approximation to a given matrix
\mathcal{M}
with
n
rows and
d
columns.
A
k
-rank approximation matrix is a matrix of rank
k
(i.e., the
space spanned by the rows of the matrix is of dimension
k
). The
standard measure of the quality of approximation of a matrix
\mathcal{M}

by a matrix
\mathcal{B}
is the squared Frobenius norm
\left\|{\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{B}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
, which is the sum of the squared entries of the matrix

\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{B}
.

The optimal
k
-rank approximation can be computed by
SVD
in time
O(\min(nd^{2},dn^{2}))
. However, if
d
and
n
are large
(conceptually, consider the case
d=n
), then this is unacceptably
slow. Since one has to read the input at least once, a running time of

\Omega(nd)
is required for any matrix approximation algorithm. Since

\mathsf{N}{}=nd
is the size of the input, we will refer to running time
of
O(\mathsf{N}{})
as being linear.

Previous work.

Frieze
et al.

[
FKV04
]
showed how to sample the rows of
the matrix, so that the resulting sample span a matrix which is a good
low rank approximation. The error of the resulting approximation has
an additive term that depends on the norm of the input matrix.
Achlioptas and McSherry
[
AM01
]
came up with an alternative
sampling scheme that yields a somewhat similar result.

Note, that a multiplicative approximation error which is proportional
to the error of the optimal approximation is more desirable as it is
potentially significantly smaller than the additive error. However,
computing quickly a small multiplicative low-rank approximation
remained elusive. Recently, Deshpande
et al.

[
DRVW06
]
,
building on the work of Frieze
et al.

[
FKV04
]
, made a
step towards this goal. They showed a multipass algorithm with an
additive error that decreases exponentially with the number of passes.
They also introduced the intriguing concept of volume sampling.

Bădoiu and Indyk
[
BI06
]
had recently presented a linear
time algorithm for this problem (i.e.,
O(\mathsf{N}{}+\left(d+\log n\right)^{O(1)})
), for the special case
k=1
, where
\mathsf{N}{}=nd
.
They also mention that, for a fixed
k
, a running time of
O(\mathsf{N}{}k\log(n/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon))
is doable. In fact, using the Lanczos
method or Power method yields a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation in time
roughly
O(\mathsf{N}{}(k/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}\log n)
. For
k=1
, this was
proved in the work of Kuczynski and Wozniakowski
[
KW92
]
,
and the proof holds in fact for any fixed
k
. We also point out
another algorithm, with similar performance, using known results, see
Remark
2.6
. Thus, the challenge in getting a linear
running time is getting rid of the
O(\log n)
factor in the running
time.

In practice, the Power method requires
O(\mathsf{N}{}kI)
time, where
I

is the number of iterations performed, which depends on the
distribution of the eigenvalues, and how close they are to each other.
In fact, the
\mathsf{N}{}
term can be replaced by the number of non-zero
entries in the matrix. In particular, if the matrix is sparse (and
I

is sufficiently small) the running time to compute a low-rank
approximation to the matrix is sublinear.

One can interpret each row of the matrix
\mathcal{M}
as a point in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This results in a point-set
P
of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
problem now is to compute a
k
-flat (i.e., a
k
-dimensional linear
subspace) such that the sum of the squared distances of the points of

P
to the
k
-flat is minimized. This problem is known as the

L_{2}
-fitting problem. The related
L_{1}
-fitting problem was recently
studied by Clarkson
[
Cla05
]
and the
L_{\infty}
-fitting
problem was recently studied by Har-Peled and Varadarajan

[
HV04
]
(see references therein for the history of these
problems).

Our Results.

The extensive work on this problem mentioned above still leaves open
the nagging question of whether one can get a multiplicative low-rank
approximation to a matrix in linear time (which is optimal in this
case). In this paper, we answer this question in the positive and
show a linear-time algorithm with small multiplicative error, using a
more geometric interpretation of this problem. In particular, we
present an algorithm that with constant probability computes a matrix

\mathcal{B}
such that
\left\|{\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{B}}\right\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(\mathcal{M},k\right)
, where
\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
is the minimum error of a

k
-rank approximation to
\mathcal{M}
, and
\varepsilon>0
is a prespecified
error parameter. For input of size
\mathsf{N}{}=nd
the running time of
the new algorithm is (roughly)
O(\mathsf{N}{}k^{2}\log k)
; see
Theorem
4.1
for details.

Our algorithm relies on the observation that a small random sample
spans a flat which is good for most of the points. This somewhat
intuitive claim is proved by using
\varepsilon
-nets and VC-dimension
arguments performed (conceptually) on the optimal (low-dimensional)

k
-flat (see Lemma
3.1
below). Next, we can filter the
points, and discover the outliers for this random flat (i.e., those
are the points which are “far” from the random flat). Since the
number of outliers is relatively small, we can approximate them
quickly using recursion. Merging the random flat together with the
flat returned from the recursive call results in a flat that
approximates well all the points, but its rank is too high. This can
be resolved by extracting the optimal solution on the merged flat, and
improving it into a good approximation using the techniques of Frieze

et al.

[
FKV04
]
and Deshpande
et al.

[
DRVW06
]
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

A
k
-
flat

\mathcal{F}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
dimension
k
(in particular, the origin is included in
\mathcal{F}
).

For a point
q
and a flat
\mathcal{F}
, let
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(q\right)
denote
the
distance
of
q
to
\mathcal{F}
. Formally,
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(q\right)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|{q-x}\right\|
. We will denote the squared distance
by
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(q\right)=\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(q\right)\right)^{2}
.

The
price
of fitting a point set
P
of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

to a flat
\mathcal{F}
is

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)=\sum_{p\in P}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(p\right)\right)^{2}.

The
k
-flat realizing
\displaystyle\min_{\dim(\mathcal{F})=k}\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, and its price is

\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
.

The
span
of a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
\mathrm{span}(S)
,
is the smallest linear subspace that contains
S
.

The input is a set
P
of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its size is

\mathsf{N}{}=nd
.

2.2
Computing a
(s,\mathcal{F})
-sample.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a flat. For a parameter
s
, a
(s,\mathcal{F})
-sample
from
P
is generated by picking point
p\in P
to
the random sample with probability

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{c}\frac{\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(p\right)\right)}{\sum_{q\in P}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(q\right)\right)},

where
\mathsf{c}
is an appropriate constant, and the size of the sample
is at least
s
.

It is not immediately clear how to compute a
(s,\mathcal{F})
-sample
efficiently since we have to find the right value of
\mathsf{c}
such
that the sample generated is of the required size (and not much bigger
than this size). However, as described by Frieze
et al.

[
FKV04
]
, this can be achieved by bucketing the basic
probabilities (i.e.,
{{\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(p\right)}}/\sum_{q\in P}{\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(q\right)}
for a point
p\in P
), such that each bucket
corresponds to points with probabilities in the range

[2^{-i},2^{-i+1}]
. It is now easy to verify that given a target size

s
, one can compute the corresponding
\mathsf{c}
in linear time, such
that the expected size of the generated sample is, say,
4s
. We also
note, that by the Chernoff inequality, with probability
\geq 1/2
,
the sample is of size in the range
[s,8s]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given a flat
\mathcal{F}
and a parameter
s
, one can compute a

(s,\mathcal{F})
-sample from a set
P
of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
O(\mathsf{N}{}\dim(\mathcal{F}))
time. The sample is of size in the range

[s,8s]
with probability
\geq 1/2
.

Furthermore, if we need to generate
t
independent

(s,\mathcal{F})
-samples, all of size in the range
[s,8s]
, then this
can be done in
O(\mathsf{N}{}\dim(\mathcal{F})+n\cdot t)
time, and
this bound on the running time holds with probability
\geq 1-\exp(-t)
.

Proof:

The first claim follows from the above description. As for the
second claim, generate
16t
samples independently. Note, that we
need to compute the distance of the points of
P
to
\mathcal{F}
only
once before we compute these
16t
samples. By the Chernoff
inequality, the probability that less than
t
samples, out of the

16t
samples generated, would be in the valid size range is
smaller than
\exp(-t)
.

2.3
Extracting the best
k
-flat lying inside another flat

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
u
-flat in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
P
be a set of
n

points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, one can extract the
k
-flat

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
that minimizes
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)
in
O(\mathsf{N}{}u+nu^{2})
time, where
k\leq u
.

Proof:

We project the points of
P
onto
\mathcal{F}
. This takes
O(\mathsf{N}{}u)
time, and let
P^{\prime}
denote the projected set. Next, we compute,
using
SVD
, the best approximation to
P^{\prime}
by a
k
-flat
\mathcal{G}

inside
\mathcal{F}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is
u
dimensional, we can treat
the point of
P^{\prime}
as being
u
dimensional and this can be done in

O(nu^{2})
time overall.

For a point
p\in P
, let
p^{\prime}
be its projection onto
\mathcal{F}

and let
p^{\prime\prime}
be the projection of
p
onto
\mathcal{G}
. By
elementary argumentation, we know that the projection of
p^{\prime}
onto

\mathcal{G}
is
p^{\prime\prime}
. As such,
\left\|{p-p^{\prime\prime}}\right\|^{2}=\left\|{p-p^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|{p^{\prime}-p^{\prime\prime}}\right\|^{2}
implying that
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)=\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)+\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)
. Since we computed the
flat
\mathcal{G}
that minimizes
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)
, it follows that
we computed the
k
-flat
\mathcal{G}
lying inside
\mathcal{F}
that
minimizes the approximation price
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)
.

2.4
Fast additive approximation

We need the following result (the original result of

[
DRVW06
]
is in fact slightly stronger).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{F}
be a

u
-flat,
\mathcal{R}
be a
(s,\mathcal{F})
-sample of size
r
, and
let
\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R})
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the

k
-flat lying inside
\mathcal{G}
that minimizes
\mu_{\mathcal{H}}\left(P\right)
.
Then, we have that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mu_{\mathcal{H}}\left(P\right)}\right]\leq\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)+\frac{k}{r}\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right).

Furthermore,
\mathcal{H}
can be computed in
O(\mathsf{N}{}(r+u))

time.

Proof:

The correctness follows immediately from the work of Deshpande
et al.
[
DRVW06
]
.

As for the running time, we compute the distance of the points of

P
to
\mathcal{F}
. This takes
O(\mathsf{N}{}u)
time. Next, we
compute
\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{R})
. Finally, we
compute the best
k
-flat
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
that
approximates
P
using Lemma
2.2
, which takes

O(\mathsf{N}{}(r+u)+n(r+u)^{2})=O(\mathsf{N}{}(r+u))
since
r+u\leq 2d
.

We need a probability guaranteed version of Theorem
2.3
, as
follows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{F}
be a

O(k)
-flat, and parameters
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, one
can compute a
k
-flat
\mathcal{H}
, such that, with probability at
least
\geq 1-\delta
, we have
\mu_{\mathcal{H}}\left(P\right)\leq\beta
,
where

\displaystyle\beta=(1+\varepsilon/2)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)+(\varepsilon/2)\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right).

The running time of the algorithm is
O(\mathsf{N}{}(k/\varepsilon)\log(1/\delta))
. The algorithm succeeds with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

Proof:

Set
s=4k/\varepsilon
. Generate
t=O((1/\varepsilon)\ln(1/\delta))

independent
(s,\mathcal{F})
-samples, of size in the range
[s,8s]
,
using the algorithm of Lemma
2.1
. Next, for each
random sample
\mathcal{R}_{i}
, we apply the algorithm of
Theorem
2.3
to it, generating a
k
-flat
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, for

i=1,\ldots,t
. By the Markov inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[{\Bigl.\mu_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\left(P\right)\geq(1+\varepsilon/4)\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mu_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\left(P\right)}\right]}\right]\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon/4}.

As such, with probability
\geq 1-1/(1+\varepsilon/4)\geq 1-(1-\varepsilon/8)=\varepsilon/8
we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\mu_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\left(P\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\left(\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)+\frac{k}{s}\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)+\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta.

Clearly, the best
k
-flat computed, which realizes the price

\displaystyle\min_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
, has a price which is at
most
\beta
, and this holds with probability
\geq 1-(1-\varepsilon/8)^{t}\geq 1-\delta/2
. Note, that the time to generate
the samples is
O(\mathsf{N}{}\dim(\mathcal{F})+n\cdot t)
, and this
bounds holds with probability
\geq 1-\delta/4
. As such, the
overall running time of the algorithm is
O(\mathsf{N}{}\dim(\mathcal{F})+n\cdot t+\mathsf{N}{}kt)=O(\mathsf{N}{}(k/\varepsilon)\ln(1/\delta))
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a given
O(k)
-flat,
P
a set of points
n
in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
c
a given parameter. Assume that
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)\leq c\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
. Then, one can compute a
k
-flat

\mathcal{G}
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right).

The running time of the algorithm is
O(\mathsf{N}{}\cdot(k/\varepsilon)\log(c/\delta))
and it succeeds with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

Proof:

Let
t=16\left\lceil{\lg(c/\delta)}\right\rceil
, and set
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{F}
.
In the
i
th step of the algorithm, we generate a

(s,\mathcal{F}_{i-1})
-sample
\mathcal{R}_{i}
with
s=20k
. If the
generated sample is not of size in the range
[s,8s]
, we
resample. Next, we apply Theorem
2.3
to
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
and

\mathcal{R}_{i}
. If the new generated flat
\mathcal{G}_{i}
is more
expensive than the
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
then we set
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
. Otherwise, we set
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathcal{G}_{i}
. We
perform this improvement loop
t
times.

Note, that if
\mu_{P}\left(\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right)\geq 8\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
then

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mu_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}\left(P\right)}\right]\leq\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)+\frac{k}{20k}\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}}\left(P\right)\leq\left(\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{20}\right)\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}}\left(P\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}}\left(P\right),

by Theorem
2.3
. By Markov’s inequality, it follows that

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[{\mu_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}\left(P\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{i-1}}\left(P\right)}\right]\geq 1/2
. Namely, with
probability half, we shrink the price of the current
k
-flat by a
factor of
2
(in the worst case the price remains the same).
Since each iteration succeeds with probability half, and we
perform
t
iterations, it follows that with probability larger
than
1-\delta/10
(by the Chernoff inequality), the price of the
last flat
\mathcal{F}_{t}
computed is
\leq 8\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
.
Similarly, we know by the Chernoff inequality that the algorithm
performed at most
4t
sampling rounds till it got the required

t
good samples, and this holds with probability
\geq 1-\delta/10
.

Thus, computing
\mathcal{F}_{t}
takes
O(\mathsf{N}{}{}kt)=O(\mathsf{N}{}\,k\log(c/\delta))
time. Finally, we apply Lemma
2.4

to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
with
\varepsilon/10
and success probability
\geq 1-\delta/10
. The resulting
k
-flat has price

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{H}}\left(P\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon/20)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)+(\varepsilon/20)\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\left(P\right)\leq(1+\varepsilon/20+8(\varepsilon/20))\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right),

as required. Note, that overall this algorithm succeeds with
probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

Remark 2.6
.

Har-Peled and Varadarajan
[
HV04
]
presented an
algorithm that computes, in
O(\mathsf{N}{}k)
time, a
k
-dimensional
affine subspace (i.e., this subspace does not necessarily contains
the origin) which approximates, up to a factor of
k!
, the best
such subspace that minimizes the maximum distance of a point of

P
to such a subspace. Namely, this is an approximation algorithm
for the
L_{\infty}
-fitting problem, and let
\mathcal{F}
denote the
computed
(k+1)
-flat (i.e., we turn the
k
-dimensional affine
subspace into a
(k+1)
-flat by adding the origin to it).

It is easy to verify that
\mathcal{F}
provides a
O\left((nk!)^{2}\right)

approximation to the optimal
k
-flat realizing
\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)

(i.e., the squared
L_{2}
-fitting problem). To see that, we remind
the reader that the
L_{\infty}
and
L_{2}
metrics are the same up
to a factor which is bounded by the dimension (which is
n
in
this case).

We can now use Lemma
2.5
starting with
\mathcal{F}
, with
c=O\left((nk!)^{2}\right)
. We get the required
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation,
and the running time is
O(\mathsf{N}{}(k/\varepsilon)(k\log k+\log(n/\delta)))
. The algorithm succeeds with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

2.5
Additional tools

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(x\right)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|{q-x}\right\|^{2}
be
the squared distance function of a point
x
to a given
u
-flat

\mathcal{F}
. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any two points. We have:

(i)

For any
0\leq\beta\leq 1
,
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(\beta x+(1-\beta)y\right)\leq\beta\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(x\right)+(1-\beta)\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(y\right)
. That is,

\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(\cdot\right)
is convex.

(ii)

Let
\ell
be the line through
x
and
y
, and
let
z
be a point on
\ell
such that
\left\|{x-z}\right\|\leq k\cdot\left\|{x-y}\right\|
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(z\right)\leq 9k^{2}\cdot\max\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(x\right),\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(y\right)\right).

Proof:

The claims of this lemma follow by easy elementary arguments and
the proof is included only for the sake of completeness.

(i) Rotate and translate space, such that
\mathcal{F}
becomes the

u
-flat spanned by the first
u
unit vectors of the standard
orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. Let
x^{\prime}
and

y^{\prime}
be the images of
x
and
y
, respectively. Consider the line

\ell(t)=tx^{\prime}+(1-t)y^{\prime}
. We have that
g(t)=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}^{2}\left(\ell(t)\right)=\sum_{i=t+1}^{d}(tx_{i}^{\prime}+(1-t)y_{i}^{\prime})^{2}
, which is a convex function since it is a sum of convex
functions, where
x^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{d}^{\prime})
and
y^{\prime}=(y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{d}^{\prime})
. As such,
g(0)=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(y\right)
and

g(1)=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(x\right)
, and
g(\beta)=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(\beta x+(1-\beta)y\right)
. By the convexity of
g(\cdot)
, we
have
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(\beta x+(1-\beta)y\right)=g(\beta)\leq\beta g(1)+(1-\beta)g(0)=\beta\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(x\right)+(1-\beta)\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(y\right)
, as claimed.

(ii) Let
{x}^{\prime}
be the projection of
x
to
\mathcal{F}
, and
let
\ell^{\prime}
be the parallel line to
\ell
passing through

{x}^{\prime}
. Let
y^{\prime}\in\ell^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}\in\ell^{\prime}
be the
translation of
y
and
z
onto
\ell^{\prime}
by the vector
x^{\prime}-x
. By
similarity of triangles, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(z^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\left\|{x^{\prime}-z^{\prime}}\right\|}{\left\|{x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}}\right\|}\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y^{\prime}\right)\leq k\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y^{\prime}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\|{x^{\prime}-x}\right\|+\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(z^{\prime}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(x\right)+k\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y^{\prime}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left((k+1)\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(x\right)+k\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y\right)\right)^{2}\leq 9k^{2}\cdot\max\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(x\right),\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(y\right)\right),

since
\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y^{\prime}\right)\leq\left\|{x-x^{\prime}}\right\|+\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y\right)=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(x\right)+\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(y\right)
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a
flat in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)\leq c\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
, where
c\geq 1
is a constant. Then, there exists
a
k
-flat
\mathcal{G}
that lies inside
\mathcal{F}
such that

\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)\leq 5c\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
.

Proof:

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the projection of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
onto
\mathcal{F}
, where

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
denotes the optimal
k
-flat that approximates
P
.
Let
P^{\prime}
be the projection of
P
onto
\mathcal{F}
. We have that

\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)\leq\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\left(\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(p\right)\right)^{2}+\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{G}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\right)^{2}\right)=\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)+\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)\leq\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)+\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P^{\prime}\right),

where
p^{\prime}
denotes the projection of a point
p\in P
onto

\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{p\in P}\left(\left\|{p-p^{\prime}}\right\|+\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(p\right)\right)^{2}\leq 2\sum_{p\in P^{\prime}}\left(\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(p\right)\right)^{2}+\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(p\right)\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)+\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P\right)\right)\leq(2c+2)\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P\right).

We conclude that
\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(P\right)\leq\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(P\right)+\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P^{\prime}\right)\leq(c+2c+2)\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P\right)\leq 5c\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
.

3
A sampling lemma

The following lemma, testifies that a small random sample induces a
low-rank flat that is a good approximation for most points. The random
sample is picked by (uniformly) choosing a subset of the appropriate
size from the input set, among all subsets of this size.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\delta>0
a
parameter, and let
\mathcal{R}
be a random sample of
P
of size

O\left(k^{2}\log(k/\delta)\right)
. Consider the flat
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{R})
, and let
Q
be the set of the
(3/4)n
points of

P
which are closest to
\mathcal{F}
. Then
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(Q\right)\leq 96k^{2}\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

Proof:

Let
r=\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)/n
be the average contribution of a point
to the sum
\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P\right)=\sum_{p\in P}\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}^{2}\left(p\right)
, where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
is the optimal
k
-flat
approximating
P
. Let
U
be the points of
P
that contribute
at most
8r
to
\mu_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}\left(P\right)
; namely, those are the
points in distance at most
\sqrt{8r}
from
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
. By
Markov’s inequality, we have
\left\lvert{U}\right\rvert\geq(7/8)n
. Next,
consider the random sample when restricted to the points of
U
;
namely,
R=\mathcal{R}\cap U
. By the Chernoff inequality, with
probability at least
1-\delta/4
, we have that
\left\lvert{R}\right\rvert=\Omega(k^{2}\log(k/\delta))
.

Next, consider the projections of
R
and
U
onto
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
,
denoted by
R^{\prime}
and
U^{\prime}
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be the
largest volume ellipsoid, contained in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
that is enclosed
inside
{\mathcal{CH}}(R^{\prime})
, where
{\mathcal{CH}}(R^{\prime})
denotes the convex-hull of
R^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be the expansion of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
by a factor of
k
. By
John’s theorem
[
Mat02
]
, we have
R^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{CH}}(R^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{E}
.

Since
R^{\prime}
is a large enough random sample of
U^{\prime}
, we know that

R^{\prime}
is a
(1/24)
-net for ellipsoids for the set
U^{\prime}
. This
follows as ellipsoids at Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
have VC-dimension

O(k^{2})
, as can be easily verified. (One way to
see this is by lifting the points into
O(k^{2})
dimensions, where
an ellipsoid in the original space is mapped into a half space.)

Figure 1
:
The solid discs represent point in
R^{\prime}
, the
circle represents the points of
U^{\prime}
.

As such, we can use the
\varepsilon
-net theorem of Haussler and Welzl

[
HW87
]
, which implies that
\left\lvert{U^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{E}}\right\rvert\geq(23/24)\left\lvert{U^{\prime}}\right\rvert
, with probability at least
1-\delta/4
.
This holds since no points of
R^{\prime}
falls outside
\mathcal{E}
, and as
such only
(1/24)\left\lvert{U^{\prime}}\right\rvert
points of
U^{\prime}
might fall outside

\mathcal{E}
by the
\varepsilon
-net theorem. (Strictly speaking, the
VC-dimension argument is applied here to ranges that are
complements of ellipsoids. Since the VC dimension is persevered
under complement the claim still holds.)

For
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, let
f(p^{\prime})=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(p^{\prime}\right)
denote
the squared distance of
p^{\prime}
to
\mathcal{F}
. If
p^{\prime}\in R^{\prime}
then the
corresponding original point
p\in R\subseteq U
and as such
p\in\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{span}(R)
. Implying that
f(p^{\prime})=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\leq\left\|{p-p^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}}^{2}\left(p\right)\leq 8r
since
p\in U
. By Lemma
2.7
(i), the
function
f(\cdot)
is convex and, for any
x\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle f(x)\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}f(y)\leq\max_{z\in{\mathcal{CH}}(R^{\prime})}f(z)\leq\max_{w\in R^{\prime}}f(w)\leq 8r,

since
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{CH}}(R^{\prime})
.

Let
v
denote the center of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, and consider any point
p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
. Consider the line
\ell
connecting

v
to
p^{\prime}
, and let
u
be one of the endpoints of
\ell\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. Observe that
\left\|{\ell\cap\mathcal{E}}\right\|\leq k\cdot\left\|{\ell\cap\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\right\|
. This implies by Lemma
2.7
(ii) that

\displaystyle\forall p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\;\;\;\;f(p^{\prime})=\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\leq 9k^{2}\max(\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(u\right),\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(v\right))\leq 72k^{2}r.

In particular, let
Y
be the set of points of
U
such that their
projection onto
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
lies inside
\mathcal{E}
. For
p\in Y
, we
have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(p\right)\leq\left(\left\|{p-p^{\prime}}\right\|+\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(p^{\prime}\right)\right)^{2}=\left(\sqrt{8r}+\sqrt{72k^{2}r}\right)^{2}\leq 128k^{2}r,

where
p^{\prime}
is its the projection of
p

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{opt}}
.
Furthermore,
\left\lvert{Y}\right\rvert=\left\lvert{U^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{E}}\right\rvert\geq(23/24)\left\lvert{U^{\prime}}\right\rvert\geq(23/24)(7/8)8\geq(3/4)n
.

Specifically, let
Q
be the set of

points of
P

closest to
\mathcal{F}
. Since
\left\lvert{Q}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert{Y}\right\rvert

that for any

\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(q\right)\leq\max_{y\in Y}\mathbf{d}_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\left(y\right)\leq 128k^{2}r
and as such

.

Theorem 4.1
.

For a set
P
of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and parameters

\delta

ApproxFlat
(P,k,\delta,\varepsilon)

\mathcal{I}

Proof:

Let us first bound the price of the generated approximation. The
proof is by induction. For
\left\lvert{P}\right\rvert\leq 20k

trivially holds. Otherwise, by induction, we know that

\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(Y\right)\leq 2\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(Y,k\right)\leq 2\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)
.
Also,
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(X\right)\leq 96{}k^{2}\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)

. As such,
\mu_{\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G})}\left(P\right)\leq\mu_{\mathcal{F}}\left(X\right)+\mu_{\mathcal{G}}\left(Y\right)\leq 98k^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)

, the

-flat
\mathcal{H}

is of price
\leq 500k^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{opt}}\left(P,k\right)

2.5
, we have

, as required.

Next, we bound the probability of failure. We picked
\mathcal{R}

such that Lemma
3.1
holds with probability
\geq 1-(\delta/10)
. By induction, the recursive call succeeds with
probability
\geq 1-\delta/2
. Finally, we used the algorithm of

2.5
so that it succeeds with probability

\displaystyle T(n,\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(n/4,\delta/2)+O\left(\mathsf{N}{}k^{2}\log(k/\delta)+n\left(k^{2}\log(k/\delta)\right)^{2}+\mathsf{N}{}(k/\varepsilon)\log(k/(\varepsilon\delta))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\mathsf{N}{}k\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+k\right){\log\frac{k}{\varepsilon\delta}}\right),

assuming

If the input is given as a matrix

5
Conclusions

In this paper we presented a linear-time algorithm for low-rank matrix
approximation. We believe that our techniques and more geometric
interpretation of the problem is of independent interest. Note, that
our algorithm is not pass efficient since it requires
O(\log n)

passes over the input (note however that the algorithm is IO
efficient). We leave the problem of how to develop a pass efficient
algorithm as an open problem for further research.

Surprisingly, the current bottleneck in our algorithm is the sampling
lemma (Lemma
3.1
). It is natural to ask if it can be further
improved. In fact, the author is unaware of a way of proving this
lemma without using the ellipsoid argument (via
\varepsilon
-net or random
sampling
[
Cla87
]
techniques), and an alternative proof
avoiding this might be interesting. We leave this as an open problem
for further research.
