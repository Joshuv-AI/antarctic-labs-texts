Abstract
. Mean field approximation is a powerful technique to study the performance of large stochastic systems represented as n n interacting objects. Applications include load balancing models, epidemic spreading, cache replacement policies, or large-scale data centers. Mean field approximation is asymptotically exact for systems composed of n n homogeneous objects under mild conditions. In this paper, we study what happens when objects are heterogeneous . This can represent servers with different speeds or contents with different popularities. We define an interaction model that allows obtaining asymptotic convergence results for stochastic systems with heterogeneous object behavior, and show that the error of the mean field approximation is of order O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) . More importantly, we show how to adapt the refined mean field approximation, developed by the authors of ( Gast et al. 2019 ) , and show that the error of this approximation is reduced to O ⁡ ( 1 / n 2 ) O(1/n^{2}) . To illustrate the applicability of our result, we present two examples. The first addresses a list-based cache replacement model RANDOM( m m ), which is an extension of the RANDOM policy. The second is a heterogeneous supermarket model. These examples show that the proposed approximations are computationally tractable and very accurate. They also show that for moderate system sizes ( n ≈ 30 n\approx 30 ) the refined mean field approximation tends to be more accurate than simulations for any reasonable simulation time.



1.
Introduction

Mean field approximation method is a widely used tool to analyze large-scale and complex stochastic models composed of interacting objects. The idea of the approximation is to assume that objects within the system evolve independently. Following this assumption, interactions of objects in the system are approximated by a “mean” behavior, which allows to model the system’s evolution as a set of deterministic ordinary differential equations. Mean field approximation finds widespread use in fields such as epidemic spreading
(
Montalbán et al. 2020
;
de
Arruda et al. 2018
)
, load balancing strategies
(
Mukhopadhyay and
Mazumdar 2015
;
Mitzenmacher 2001
)
, caching
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
or SSDs
(
Van Houdt 2013
)
. Building on this approximation, a refined mean field approximation is introduced in
(
Gast 2018
;
Gast
et al. 2019
)
that greatly improves the accuracy of mean field approximations for populations of
n=10
to
n=100
objects. The popularity of mean field approximation lies in the ease of defining and solving the differential equations as well as the increasingly high accuracy for large systems.

Most of the theoretical work, however, has been done for systems where the interacting objects have homogeneous transitions, as for density-dependent population processes of Kurtz
(
Kurtz 1978
)
, or can be clustered into a finite number of groups with similar statistical behavior. Yet, in many models, heterogeneity plays an important role. This is particularly relevant to model caches, where object popularities vary broadly among contents, or epidemic spreading, where variations of sensibility among agents can greatly influence the long-term dynamics and vaccination strategies
(
Gomes
et al. 2020
)
. Using a finite number of clusters with homogeneous behavior simplifies the underlying models and essentially ignores the actual heterogeneity. Up to now, there are virtually no fully heterogeneous models with theoretical guarantees on why mean field approximation is a valid technique.

In this paper, we generalize the notion of mean field approximation and refined mean field approximation to stochastic systems composed of
n
heterogeneous objects and show that similar asymptotic results as for the homogeneous case hold. For such a system, we show that it is possible to construct a set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) which approximate
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)
, the probability for an object
k
to be in a state
s
at time
t
. This can be used to approximate the expectation of a function of the state of an object (such as the average queue length in a queuing system).

To give some intuition, the way we construct our approximations is to consider a scaled model with
C
identical copies of each object. This allows one to define the mean field approximation
x_{(k,s)}(t)
and a
1/C
-expansion term
v_{(k,s)}(t)
defined in
(
Gast
et al. 2019
)
. These approximations are shown in
(
Gast
et al. 2019
)
to be asymptotically accurate as the number of copies
C
goes to infinity. As the fully heterogeneous system corresponds to having one copy, the heuristic reasoning is then to apply the approximation with
C=1
. Up to now, there was no theoretical foundation on why this should work because all results assume that the number of copies
C
goes to infinity.

We provide the first rigorous justification of the validity of this approach. The main contribution of our paper is to show that if
x_{(k,s)}(t)
is the mean field approximation and
v_{(k,s)}(t)
is the expansion term defined in
(
Gast
et al. 2019
)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)

\displaystyle=x_{(k,s)}(t)+O(1/n),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)

\displaystyle=x_{(k,s)}(t)+v_{(k,s)}(t)+O(1/n^{2}).

As an important corollary, if the system is composed of
C
copies of
n
heterogeneous objects, we then have
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)=x_{(k,s)}(t)+(1/C)v_{(k,s)}(t)+O(1/(Cn)^{2})
. This shows that the accuracy of the mean field and refined mean field approximation does not depend on the level of heterogeneity of the system but only on the total number of objects (being
n
or
nC
).

To do so, we develop a heterogeneous interaction model in which each object changes state either unilaterally or by interacting with
d-1
other objects. The main assumption that we make in our model is that the rate at which a given tuple of
d
individuals interact scales as
O(1/n^{d-1})
. As there are
O(n^{d})
such tuples, this guarantees a uniform bound on the interactions between tuples. This model covers an extensive range of models with pairwise interactions, such as infection models, load balancing strategies, or cache replacement policies. These approximations can be computed by solving a differential equation that can be easily integrated numerically. For the mean field approximation, the number of variables grows linearly with the number of different objects
n
. For the refined approximation, it grows quadratically with
n
. Our proposed framework naturally extends mean field models for homogeneous population processes and the results are comparable with
(
Gast 2017
;
Kurtz 1978
;
Ying 2016
)
. Our approach does not assume any homogeneity in the system and does not cluster objects into a finite number of classes. Hence, it can be applied to interacting systems where all objects are different.

To illustrate our results we provide two examples that show how the mean field and refined mean field approximation can be applied. They also show that the hidden constants in the
O(1/n)
and
O(1/n^{2})
error terms given by the theorems are small in practice. Our first example is a list-based cache replacement algorithm studied in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
consisting of
n
objects whose popularities follow a Zipf-like distribution. We study how the cache popularities depend on the replacement policy for the transient and steady-state regime. For transient results, we compare the mean field and refined mean field approximation with simulations, which indicates that the refined mean field provides a significant improvement of accuracy. The results are even more striking for the steady-state regime for which it is possible to compute the exact steady-state distribution if the system size is small. This allows us to compare the accuracy of the two approximations and the simulation to the exact value. We observe that, for any reasonable computational power, the confidence intervals provided by the simulation are higher than the error of the refined mean field approximation as soon as
n
exceeds a few tens. In a second example, we apply the approximation techniques to a heterogeneous two-choice load balancing model. The heterogeneity in the model is introduced by considering varying server rates. As for the previous examples, we give a full description justifying the use of the mean field models and show by numerical computation that the obtained results confirm the theoretical statements. Numerical calculations for values such as the average queue length and queue length tail distribution are given. We also compare the approximation results to a homogeneous variant of the system where the server rates are set to the average server rate of the heterogeneous model. This shows that, as expected, taking heterogeneity into account strikingly improves the accuracy of the results. For both examples, we adapt numerical methods from the toolbox developed by
(
Gast 2018
)
, which allowed us to implement and solve the differential equations with relative ease.

Related Work

Generator and Stein’s method

Our paper builds on the recent line of work regarding the use of Stein’s method
(
Stein 1986
)
. This method allows one to estimate precisely the distance between two random variables by looking at the distance between the generators of two related stochastic systems. This method has seen a resurgence of interest in the stochastic network community since the work of
(
Braverman and Dai 2017
;
Braverman
et al. 2017
)
. There is still an active development in this area. For instance, this method has been used to develop higher-order diffusion approximations
(
Braverman
et al. 2020
;
Braverman 2017
)
. It is used in
(
Hodgkinson
et al. 2018
)
to develop a normal approximation of a heterogeneous discrete time population process. One of the key differences between our work and theirs is that the two aforementioned papers consider one-dimensional processes (i.e., the state of each object of the system is either
0
or
1
), and the extension to more complex dynamics is not direct, at least from a computational point of view. One contribution of our work is to demonstrate how to deal with multiple states, by changing the state representation.

Refined mean field methods

Stein’s method has been successfully used to study the accuracy of mean field methods in
(
Gast 2017
;
Kolokoltsov
et al. 2012
;
Ying 2016
;
Ying 2017
)
. These works show that the accuracy of the mean field approximation is
O(1/n)
for a system with
n

homogeneous
objects. By using an expansion of the generator, these results have been extended in
(
Gast
et al. 2019
;
Gast and
Van Houdt 2017a
)
to propose what the authors called a
refined
mean field approximation, that is similar to the system size expansion introduced in mathematical biology
(
van Kampen 2007
;
Grima 2010
;
Grima
et al. 2011
)
.

In fact, there exists a close link between the refined approximation that we propose in the paper and the approach of
(
Gast
et al. 2019
;
Gast and
Van Houdt 2017a
)
. Since the results of
(
Gast
et al. 2019
;
Gast and
Van Houdt 2017a
)
only apply to systems composed of homogeneous objects, let us consider a hypothetical model composed of
C
identical copies of each of the
n
object, and let us denote by
X_{(k,s)}^{[C\text{ copies}]}(t)
the number of copies of the object
k
that are in state
s
at time
t
. By
(
Gast
et al. 2019
;
Gast and
Van Houdt 2017a
)
, there exists a constant
v_{(k,s)}(t)
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\E\left[X_{(k,s)}^{[C\text{ copies}]}(t)\right]=x_{(k,s)}(t)+\frac{1}{C}v_{(k,s)}(t)+O(1/C^{2}).

Since our original model corresponds to
C=1
, the rationale behind our approximation is to use
x_{(k,s)}(t)
as a first order approximation and
x_{(k,s)}(t)+v_{(k,s)}(t)
as the refined approximation. Yet, this is no a priori guarantee of why
O(1/C^{2})
should be small for
C
equal to one. As a key technical contribution, our paper gives the theoretical foundation of this method.

This requires to overcome several difficulties, that are our main contributions compared to the aforementioned papers:

(1)

We define an interaction model that can be dealt with by bounding the interaction rates.

(2)

A key idea of our work is to use the indicators
X_{k,s}(t)\in\{0,1\}
and not the proportion of objects in a given state. This allows us to construct the expansion terms and do the proofs.

(3)

Similar to previous works
(
Gast 2017
;
Kolokoltsov
et al. 2012
;
Ying 2015
)
, we use generators to reduce the analysis of the mean field error to the study of the sensibility of an ODE with respect to its initial conditions (Section
6.2
). The extra difficulty in our case is to carefully analyze the remainder terms: while to analyze (
1
), the error is a finite sum of
O(1/C)
terms, here we have a sum of
n
(or
n^{2}
) terms, some of them being of order
O(1/n^{2})
and others being of order
O(1/n^{3})
. Dealing with all these different cases requires quite some care and is the subject of Appendix
D
.

Note that to study heterogeneous population models, it is quite common to assume that there are
n
classes with
C
copies of the same objects of class
k\in\{1\dots n\}
. This has been used for instance to study load-balancing strategies
(
Mukhopadhyay and
Mazumdar 2015
)
or cache replacement policies
(
Hirade and
Osogami 2010
;
Tsukada
et al. 2012
)
. Our approach generalizes such methods as we assume that the objects can be fully heterogeneous. This is for instance what is used in replica models
(
Mézard
et al. 1987
;
Baccelli and
Taillefumier 2019
)
.

Heterogeneous populations and caches

In the performance evaluation community, heterogeneous population models are very common when studying cache replacement policies, where the popularity of objects is typically assumed to follow a Zipf-like distribution. As the dynamics of caches are intrinsically complicated, many mean field like approximations have been proposed, such as the famous TTL-approximation of
(
Fagin 1977
)
(sometimes misleadingly called the Che-approximation after it was rediscovered in
(
Che
et al. 2001
)
) or fixed-point approximation like
(
Dan and Towsley 1990
)
. Theoretical support exists to prove that these approximation are asymptotically correct
(
Fricker
et al. 2012
;
Jiang
et al. 2018
;
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
.

The generic method that we propose in this paper has two advantages: First, we prove that the accuracy of the mean field method is
O(1/n)
whereas the above papers only obtain bounds in
O(1/\sqrt{n})
. Second, we develop a refined approximation that can greatly improve the accuracy compared to the cited method, at the price of being computationally more expensive.

In particular, our result applies directly to the cache replacement model of
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
in which a mean field approximation for the RAND(
\mathbf{m}
) policy is derived. This paper also contains a theorem that shows that the mean field approximation is
O(1/\sqrt{n})
-accurate. Yet, we do not think that the proof of the main result of
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
is correct because of the use of a martingale inequality combined with the infinite-norm (and not a
L_{2}
-norm). More precisely, we believe that the problem in their proof is just below their Equation (13) when Lemma 1(ii) is used. What their Lemma 1 implies is that
M(t)
is a Martingale such that
\E\left[\left\|M(t+1)-M(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq c
. This is used below their Equation (13) to imply that
\E\left[\left\|M(t)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq ct
. The problem is that this is true if the norm
\left\|M\right\|
is a
L_{2}
norm (or any norm that can be written as a scalar product
\left\|M\right\|^{2}=\langle M,M\rangle
) but not if
\left\|M\right\|
it is a supremum norm. The norm used in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
is a supremum norm and we do not believe it can be derived from a scalar product. The approach that we take in this paper is radically different as we work with a comparison of generators. This allows us to correct the proof of
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
by obtaining a tighter bound. Note that we do not claim that their result is false, but only that the proof is false. We explain in Appendix
C.2
that their result is a consequence of ours (and that our results give a finer bound).

k\in\{1\ldots n\}
and a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we define

\displaystyle X^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}^{(n)}(t)=s\}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if object $k$ is in state $s$ at time $t$},\\

\mathbf{X}^{(n)}
is less compact than the original representation
\mathbf{S}^{(n)}

\mathbf{x}
in
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X}^{(n)})
.

4.2.
Accuracy of the Mean Field Approximation

To obtain asymptotic properties we require that there exists a uniform bound
\bar{r}
, independent of
n
, such that for all
s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime},...\in\mathcal{S}
and
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have:

(4)

\displaystyle r^{(n)}_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{d},(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\rightarrow(s_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,s_{d}^{\prime})}\leq\bar{r}.

Theorem 4.1.

Assume that the model, defined in Section
3.2
, satisfies (
4
). Let
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x},t)
be the solution of the ODE introduced in Section
4.1
with initial condition
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}(0)=\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(n)}
and drift
f^{(n)}
. Then, for
(k,s)\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\times\mathcal{S}
and
t<\infty
,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[X^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(t)\right]=\phi^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+O(1/n).

Key ideas of the proof.

A complete proof is provided in Section
6.3
. Here we only give a brief overview. Note that by definition of the initial value problem and the drift being a polynomial, the differentiability of the solution
\phi
w.r.t. the initial condition and time is given. In the first part of the proof, we borrow ideas from
(
Kolokoltsov
et al. 2012
;
Gast 2017
)
and use a Taylor’s expansion of
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}^{(n)}
to show that
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)-\phi^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)
can be bounded by a weighted sums of
\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}\partial x_{(k^{\prime\prime},s^{\prime\prime})}}
. The key technical difficulty of the proof is then to show that these terms are small when the number of objects is large. To do so, we distinguish the cases where
k^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime\prime}
refer to the same object as
k
or not.
∎

The statement of the theorem can be interpreted as saying that the probability of an object
k
in the Markov chain
\mathbf{X}
to be in state
s
is approximated by
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{(k,s)}
with an accuracy of
O(1/n)
. Indeed, with this result we can obtain similar statements as for the homogeneous case where in many cases asymptotic results are proven for
Z^{(n)}_{s}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{(k,s)}(t)
, the stochastic process describing the fraction of objects which are in state
s
. It should be noted that the solution of the ODE
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{(k,s)}
, taking values in
[0,1]
, is not close to the value of
\mathbf{X}_{(k,s)}
, which indicates if object
k
is in state
s
and takes the values zero or one. Hence, single trajectories of the stochastic process are not comparable to the approximation.

To illustrate this result, let us consider a cache model with a total of
n=4
objects and a cache that can store
2
objects. We assume that the popularities follow a Zipf distribution of parameter
0.8
, meaning that object
k
is requested at rate
\lambda_{k}=1/k^{0.8}
and use the RANDOM replacement policy. The policy exchanges objects the following way: When an object is requested and inserted in the cache, we evict another object picked uniformly at random among the two objects in the cache. Initially, the cache contains the objects
3
and
4
.

In Figure
1
, we plot the behavior of the cache as a function of time. Each plot corresponds to a different object and contains three curves: In grey we plot one stochastic trajectory of the cache,
X_{(k,\text{in})}(t)
, where
X_{(k,\text{in})}=1
means that the object
k
is in the cache and
0
that it is not. In blue, we plot the probability for object
k
to be in the cache at time
t
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\text{object $k$ in cache at time $t$}\right)=\E\left[X_{(k,\text{in})}(t)\right]
, which is computed by averaging over
1000
trajectories. In green, we plot the solution of the mean field approximation,
\phi^{(n)}_{(k,\text{in})}(\mathbf{x},t)
. We emphasize that
X_{(k,\text{in})}(t)
is never close to
\phi^{(n)}_{(k,\text{in})}(\mathbf{x},t)
, because the former can only take the values
0
and
1
whereas the latter takes values between
0
and
1
. Moreover, the latter, which is the mean field approximation, seems to provide a very good approximation for the object to be in the cache.

4.3.
Accuracy of the Refined Mean Field Approximation

In
(
Gast
et al. 2019
;
Gast and
Van Houdt 2017a
)
, Gast et al. introduced a refined mean field approximation which provides significantly more accurate approximations in the homogeneous case. The idea is to study higher moments of
X_{(k,s)}-\phi_{(k,s)}
and to derive refinement terms. Taking the refinements into account is especially important for small to moderate system sizes, i.e.
n\approx 10-50
, where the mean field approximation does not capture the dynamics of the stochastic system well. In this section, we show how to derive the refinement for our heterogeneous model.

To construct the refinement term, we consider an (imaginary) system in which there are
C
replicas of the same object. Let us denote
X^{[C\text{ copies}]}_{(k,s)}(t)
the fraction of replicas of type
k
that are in state
s
at time
t
. The process
\mathbf{X}^{[C\text{ copies}]}
is a density-dependent population process and our original process is given by
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}^{[1\text{ copy}]}
. The mean field approximation of
\mathbf{X}^{[C\text{ copies}]}
is also
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}^{(n)}
. The idea of
(
Gast
et al. 2019
)
is to study the stochastic fluctuation of
X^{[C\text{ copies}]}_{(k,s)}(t)
around its mean field approximation. The authors show that there exists a set of deterministic values
v^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(t)
such that

(6)

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{[C\text{ copies}]}_{(k,s)}(t)\right]=\phi^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{C}v^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+O(1/C^{2}).

The values
v^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(t)
are shown in
(
Gast
et al. 2019
)
to satisfy a system of linear ordinary differential equations whose solution can be expressed in integral form as:

\displaystyle v_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\atop\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\times\mathcal{S}}Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)d\tau

where
Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x})
corresponds to the expected change of the covariance between the values of
X_{(k_{1},s_{1})}
and
X_{(k_{2},s_{2})}
of the stochastic system at some given point
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}
. We formally introduce and elaborate more on the refinement terms in Appendix
B.2
.

In our heterogeneous population model, we have no replica which corresponds to setting
C=1
. Hence, the above bound does not guarantee that the
O(1/C^{2})
should be small for
C=1
. The next theorem shows that, surprisingly, using the refined approximation (
6
) with
C=1
copy leads to an approximation that is an order of magnitude more accurate than the mean field approximation provided before. When comparing Equation (
5
) and (
7
), what this theorem shows is that the correction
v^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(x,t)
is of order
O(1/n)
and is the leading term of the
O(1/n)
-term of Equation (
5
).

Note that to obtain the accuracy bound, no further assumption is needed compared to the case of the mean field approximation. That is, we assume that the parameters
r
are uniformly bounded.

Theorem 4.2.

Assume that the model, defined in Section
3.2
, satisfies (
4
). Let
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x},t)
be the solution of the ODE introduced in Section
4.1
with initial condition
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}(0)=\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{(n)}
and drift
f^{(n)}
. Let
\mathbf{v}^{(n)}(\mathbf{x},t)
be the solution of the refinement term explicitly defined in Appendix
B.2
. Then, for
(k,s)\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\times\mathcal{S}
and
t<\infty
,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}(t)=s\right)

\displaystyle=\phi^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+v^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+O(1/n^{2}).

Key ideas of the proof.

A complete proof is provided in Section
6.4
. The proof of Theorem
4.2
uses the same methodology as the proof of Theorem
4.1
but refines the analysis to extract the term
v
. First, we use a second-order Taylor expansion instead of the first order expansion used in the proof of Theorem
4.2
, this allows us to derive an expansion term in integral form. Second, we show that this expansion term is essentially equal to the refinement term
v
. Last, we show that the remainder terms are of order
O(1/n^{2})
by carefully studying how small the third and fourth derivatives of
\phi
with respect to its initial condition are.
∎

The theorem shows that, by adding the refinement term, the error of the refined approximation is of order
O(1/n^{2})
, which is an order of magnitude better than the
O(1/n)
of the classical mean field approximation. This implies that both equations are asymptotically exact as the number of interacting objects goes to infinity. Hence, the refinement is especially interesting to approximate systems with few interacting objects. Note that in theory, it is possible to obtain a refined-refined approximation that has an accuracy of
O(1/n^{3})
. For that, one can adapt the
1/N^{2}
-expansion of
(
Gast
et al. 2019
)
to compute a second expansion term. This expansion depends on up to the fourth derivative of
\phi
. Yet, proving carefully that this expansion is
O(1/n^{3})
-accurate seems difficult as it requires obtaining precise estimates of up to the sixth derivative of
\phi
. Also, from a practical point of view, computing such an expansion involves solving an ODE with
O((nS)^{4})
variables which seems difficult as soon as
n
grows. Hence, in this paper, we restrict our attention to the first expansion term.

To illustrate how this refinement improves the accuracy compared to the classical mean field, we consider the same cache replacement policy as the one studied in Figure
1
, with four objects and a cache of size
2
. Compared to the previous figure, we now added an orange curve that corresponds to the refined mean field approximation. We observe that, if the mean field approximation was good, the refined mean field approximation seems almost exact.

In fact, the refined mean field approximation lies within the confidence interval of the sample mean which is calculated from
1000
sample trajectories of the underlying system. It is noticeable that computing the mean field and refinement term takes about 150ms whereas simulating
1000
sample paths and calculating the sample mean takes several seconds.

This suggests that for the same computational budget, the refined mean field approximation is more accurate than the simulation. We will elaborate more on that in Section
5.1
.

On the applicability to partially heterogeneous systems

Consider the case that the stochastic system is not fully heterogeneous, i.e. there exists a finite number of classes (say
n
), each class having
C
objects that have the same behavior. Such a model corresponds to our model of
C
copies. As this model is a density dependent process, the results from
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2017b
;
Gast
et al. 2019
)
show that the mean field approximation is
O(1/C)
-accurate and that the refined mean field approximation is
O(1/C^{2})
accurate for the
C
-copy model. We now show that Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
can be used to obtain the following much sharper bound:

(8)

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{[C\text{ copies}]}_{(k,s)}(t)\right]=\phi^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{C}v^{(n)}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)+O\left(\frac{1}{(Cn)^{2}}\right).

The (only) difference between this equation and (
6
) is that the term
O(1/C^{2})
of (
6
) is replaced by the much smaller term
O(1/(Cn)^{2})
. This implies that the accuracy of mean field interaction model does not depend on the number of homogeneous copies but only on the total number of objects.

To see why (
8
) works, we remark that our model with
C
copies of
n
classes can be represented by a fully heterogeneous model with
n^{\prime}=Cn
objects (one just have to use equal rates for objects that are similar). The result of Theorem
4.1
and
4.2
imply that the mean field
\phi^{(n^{\prime})}
and refined mean field
\phi^{(n^{\prime})}+v^{(n^{\prime})}
approximations are
O(1/n^{\prime})
and
O(1/(n^{\prime})^{2})
accurate. By replacing
n^{\prime}
by
nC
and summing over identical objects, one obtains (
8
).

4.4.
Numerical Complexity

From a computational point of view, the mean field and refined mean field approximations greatly fasten the estimation of transient or steady-state values compared to a direct study of the original Markov process
\mathbf{S}^{(n)}
. Indeed, the continuous-time Markov chain
\mathbf{S}^{(n)}
has up to
|\mathcal{S}|^{n}
states where the mean field approximation can be computed by solving a non-linear ODE with
n|\mathcal{S}|
variables. As shown in Appendix
B.2
, the refinement term
\mathbf{v}^{(n)}
is the solution of a linear ODE with
n|\mathcal{S}|+(n|\mathcal{S}|)^{2}
variables. This means that both approximations can be solved by using standard numerical integrators. Moreover, their complexity grows linearly (for the mean field) or quadratically (for the refinement) in the number of objects whereas an exact analysis grows exponentially with the number of objects.

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we illustrate our main results with two examples, a cache replacement model and a two-choice load balancing model. We will see that both models fulfill the requirements for Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
and that the hidden constant given in the theorems is small. The two examples are chosen to illustrate models for which the classical, homogeneous mean field approximation cannot be used but our heterogeneous framework applies.

5.1.
Application to a Cache Replacement Algorithm: the RANDOM(
\mathbf{m}
) Model

As a first example we consider the list-based RANDOM(
\mathbf{m}
) cache replacement policy which was introduced in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
;
Hazewinkel et al. 1987
)
. List-based cache replacement policies are used to order content in a cache separated into lists. The cache is separated into
\ell:=|\mathcal{S}|-1
lists with sizes
m_{1}^{(n)},\ldots,m_{\ell}^{(n)}
. When an object is requested, if it is not in the cache, it is inserted in the first list and replaces a randomly chosen object from it. If the object is in a list
s
, it is promoted to list
s+1
and a randomly chosen object from list
s+1
is moved to list
s
. If an object that is not in the cache is requested we call it a ‘miss’ otherwise a ‘hit’. It is shown in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
that list-based cache replacement policies can greatly improve the hit rate compared to the classical RANDOM or LRU policies, at the price of being less responsive. The authors of
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
used a mean field approximation for which some theoretical support was given (essentially by showing that the error of the mean field approximation is
O(1/\sqrt{n})
). In this section, we push this analysis further in two directions. First, we show that our framework improves on the bound of
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
by showing that the error of the mean field approximation is
O(1/n)
and not
O(1/\sqrt{n})
. Second, we show that our refined approximation provides an extremely accurate approximation (essentially more accurate than simulation). While this last fact was empirically observed in
(
Casale and Gast 2020
)
, Theorem
4.2
provides theoretical support by showing that the error of the refined approximation is
O(1/n^{2})
.

5.1.1.
Model and Approximations

We consider that there are
n
objects with identical sizes. Requests for an object
k
arrive according to a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda_{k}
. In our framework, the state of object
k
at time
t
is
S^{(n)}_{k}(t)\in\{0,1\dots\ell\}
, that represents the list in which object
k
is ("0" means that the object is not stored in the cache). Following our framework, we denote by
X_{(k,s)}(t)
the random variable that equals
1
if object
k
is in list
s
and
0
otherwise. According to the RANDOM(
\mathbf{m}
) policy, if object
k
is in list
s\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
and gets requested, then it is moved into list
s+1
and a randomly selected object (say
k_{1}
) from list
s+1
moves into list
s
. The corresponding transitions for the Markov chain
\mathbf{X}
are:

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{e}_{(k,s+1)}-\mathbf{e}_{(k,s)}+\mathbf{e}_{(k_{1},s)}-\mathbf{e}_{(k_{1},s+1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ at rate }\frac{\lambda_{k}}{m_{s+1}^{(n)}}X_{(k,s)}X_{(k_{1},s+1)}.

Here,
\lambda_{k}X_{(k,s)}
is the rate at which object
k
is requested while being in list
s
, and
X_{(k_{1},s+1)}/m_{s}^{(n)}
is the probability that object
k_{1}
is in list
s+1
and is chosen to be exchanged.

The drift of the stochastic system is the vector
(f_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x}))_{(k,s)}
. By definition, for object
k
in list
s
, the
(k,s)
component of the drift is

\displaystyle f_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}x_{(k,s-1)}-\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k_{1}}}{m_{s}}x_{(k_{1},s-1)}x_{(k,s)}+\bigl(\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k_{1}}}{m_{s+1}}x_{(k_{1},s)}x_{(k,s+1)}-\lambda_{k}x_{(k,s)}\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{\{s<\ell\}}.

The mean field approximation is the solution of the ODE
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x})
. Note that this ODE is the same as the one given in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
.

The transitions of this model are pairwise interactions with
b^{(n)}_{k,k_{1},s,s+1,s+1,s}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{m^{(n)}_{s+1}/n}
as defined in (
2b
). There is no unilateral transition. To apply Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, we assume that the list sizes
m_{i}^{(n)}
grow linearly with
n
which guarantees that
b
remains bounded by
\bar{b}=\frac{\max_{k}\lambda_{k}}{\min_{s}(m_{s}/n)}
. Therefore, the assumptions of the theorems are satisfied as soon as the values
\lambda_{k}
are bounded and the list sizes grow linearly with the number of objects. This guarantees that the mean field approximation is
O(1/n)
accurate whereas the refined mean field approximation is
O(1/n^{2})
accurate.

5.1.2.
Transient Analysis

We calculate the solution of the ODEs for the mean field and refined mean field approximation and compute the simulations by adapting the toolbox
(
Gast 2018
)
. We implement the Markov chain, the drift
f
, the drift derivatives and
\mathbf{Q}
for the approximations based on the transitions (
9
). For our numerical example, we consider a cache with
n=20
objects for which the request rates follows a Zipf distribution with parameter
\alpha=0.8
, that is,
\lambda_{k}=A/k^{\alpha}
with

being a normalizing constant. We consider a cache with three lists of sizes
m_{1}=5
,
m_{2}=3
, and

.

In Figure

n
different objects following a Zipf popularity with parameter
\alpha=0.5

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=s\right)
for all

.

We show in Appendix
C.1

5.2.
Application to a Load Balancing Algorithm: The two-choice Model

5.2.1.
Model and Approximations

In our second example, we consider a variation of the well studied two-choice model
(
Mitzenmacher 2001
)
. In contrast to the homogeneous case, where all servers have equal service rate parameters, we consider a heterogeneous setup in which processors can have different speeds. We study the impact of the heterogeneity of servers on their performance. Note that a similar analysis was done in
(
Mukhopadhyay and
Mazumdar 2015
)
by using two classes of servers. The purpose of this example is twofold. It illustrates that our framework can incorporate load balancing models with heterogeneous servers. It also shows that taking heterogeneity into account in such systems is important if one wants to characterize the performance precisely.

The model consists of
n
servers with heterogeneous service rate parameters
\mu_{k},k=1,\ldots,n
and a finite buffer of size
b
, including the job in service. Jobs arrive according to a Poisson process of rate
\lambda n
, and we call
\lambda
the arrival rate. For each incoming job, we randomly pick two servers. The job is then assigned to the server which has the least number of unfinished jobs. If both servers have the same queue length and a full buffer, the job is discarded. Otherwise, the assignment between the two servers is done at random. The service time of a job in the queue of server
i
is exponentially distributed with mean
\mu_{i}
. The state of a server
k
at time
t
is its queue length
S_{k}(t)\in\mathcal{S}=\{0,1,\ldots,b\}
, state
0
is referring to the idle state.

We denote by
X_{(k,s)}
the random variable that equals
1
if server
k
has
s
jobs. The process
\mathbf{X}=(X_{(k,s)})_{(k,s)}
is a Markov chain whose transitions are (for all
k,k_{1}\in\{1\dots n\}
)
:

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\mapsto X-e_{(k,s)}+e_{(k,s-1)}

\displaystyle\text{ at rate }\mu_{k}X_{(k,s)},

(11b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\mapsto X+e_{(k,s+1)}-e_{(k,s)}

\displaystyle\text{ at rate }(2\lambda n\mathbf{1}_{\{s_{1}\geq s+1\}}+\lambda n\mathbf{1}_{\{s_{1}=s\}})\frac{X_{(k,s)}}{n}\frac{X_{(k_{1},s_{1})}}{n}.

In the above equation, the first type of transition (
11a
) corresponds to the completion of a job by server
k
when the job queue is of size
1\leq s\leq b
. It reduces the queue length from
s
to
s-1
which sets
X_{(k,s)}
to
0
and
X_{(k,s-1)}
to
1
. The second type of transitions, in equation (
11b
), corresponds to adding a job to server
k
having
0\leq s\leq b-1
jobs in the buffer. In this case, the queue size is increased by one from
s
to
s+1
. To explain the transition rate we see that the servers
k,k_{1}
can be selected in two ways, by selecting
k
or
k_{1}
first and the other second. In the case that both queues have equal length, the chance to add the job to server
k
is
1/2
. If both buffers are full, the job is discarded.

This model has both unilateral transitions with
r_{k,(s)\rightarrow(s-1)}^{(n)}=\mu_{k}
and pairwise interactions with
r_{k,k_{1},(s,s_{1})\rightarrow(s+1,s_{1})}^{(n)}=(2\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\{s_{1}\geq s+1\}}+\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\{s_{1}=s\}})/n
. The bound (
4
), required to apply Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, is verified when the values of
\lambda
and
\mu_{k}
are bounded independently of
n
. Moreover, we assume a possibly very buffer for the servers.

To simplify notations, let
g_{s}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\tilde{s}\geq s}X_{(k,\tilde{s})}(t)/n
be the fraction of servers at time
t
with queue length at least
s
. By summing over all possible values of
k_{1}
and
s_{1}
, the transition (
11b
) can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle X\mapsto X+e_{(k,s+1)}-e_{(k,s)}

\displaystyle\text{ at rate }\lambda\ X_{(k,s)}(g_{s}+g_{s+1}).

By using this notation, the drift for index
(k,s)
is

\displaystyle f_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\mu_{k}x_{(k,s+1)}-\lambda x_{(k,s)}(g_{s}+g_{s+1})-\bigl(\mu_{k}x_{(k,s)}-\lambda x_{(k,s-1)}(g_{s-1}+g_{s})\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{\{s\geq 1\}}.

Proof of Theorem
4.1

By Lemma
6.1
, we have

(12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\E[\mathbf{X}(t)-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(0),t)]=\int_{0}^{t}\E[\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}&K_{\mathbf{X}(\tau),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\left(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t-\tau)-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),t-\tau)\right)\\

The above expression involves terms of the form
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau)

(13)

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=D_{\mathbf{x}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})+R_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau),

\displaystyle R_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-\nu)\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x}+\nu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}),\tau)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j})d\nu.

Moreover, by definition of the drift, one has
\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
. Combining this with (
13
) and plugging this into equation (

) shows that

(14)

\displaystyle\E[\mathbf{X}(t)-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(0),t)]=\int_{0}^{t}\E[\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{X}(\tau),x^{\prime}}R_{1}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t-\tau)]d\tau.

To conclude the proof, we show in Lemma
D.2
that
\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}R_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)
is of order

The proof of Theorem

The first is to use a second-order Taylor expansion instead of the first order expansion used in (

as an integral of quantities that depend on the second derivative of

By using a second order Taylor expansion of
\phi

(15)

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau)=D_{\mathbf{x}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial^{2}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},\tau)+R_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau),

where the remainder term

is equal to

\displaystyle R_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1{-}\nu)^{2}\sum_{i,j,u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{3}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{x}+\nu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x}),\tau)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}{-}\mathbf{x}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}{-}\mathbf{x}_{j})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{u}{-}\mathbf{x}_{u})d\nu

and

is given by
\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})(x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j})
which we formally introduce in Appendix
B.2

To simplify notations, let
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x},\tau):=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial^{2}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},\tau)
. Similarly to (
14
), we have

(16)

\displaystyle\E[\mathbf{X}(t)]{-}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(0),t)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\E[\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),t{-}\tau)]d\tau}_{\text{$\approx\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t){+}O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})$ by \ref{lemma:connection_ode_integral_form} and \ref{lemma:refinement_bound}.}}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\E[\underbrace{\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{X}(\tau),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}R_{2}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t{-}\tau)}_{\text{$O(1/n^{2})$ by Lemma~\ref{lemma:taylor_remainder}.}}]d\tau.

By using the approach we used for

, we prove in Lemma
D.2
that the last term of the above equation (that involves a sum of
R_{2}
) is of order
O(1/n^{2})
. This is quite technical and done by carefully bounding the first, second and third derivatives of
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t)
with respect to its initial condition. We are then left with the first term of Equation (
16
). By Lemma
D.3
, the refinement term
\mathbf{v}
can be expressed in integral form as
v_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),\tau)d\tau
. This shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\E[g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\tau)]d\tau-v_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{X}(0),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\E[g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\tau)-g_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(0),\tau),\tau)]d\tau.

We show in Lemma
D.4
that the above term is of order
O(1/n^{2})
. This requires to bound up to the fourth derivative of
\phi
with respect to its initial condition. Plugging everything into Equation (
16
) shows that
\E[\mathbf{X}(t)]-\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(0),t)-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X}(0),t)=O(1/n^{2})
and concludes the proof.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we show how to derive mean field and a refined mean field approximation for systems composed of
n
heterogeneous objects. Most of the results which guarantee that mean field approximation is asymptotically correct assume that the system is composed of a population of
n
homogeneous objects, or at least can be clustered into a finite number of classes of objects and let the number of objects in each class goes to infinity. A possible approach to derive a (refined) mean field approximation for a heterogeneous is to consider a scaled model with
C
copies of each of the
n
objects. Classical methods show that the (refined) mean field approximations are asymptotically exact as
C
grows.

Our paper is the first to show that applying this method for the original system (with
C=1
object of each of the
n
class) is indeed valid. The main results of our paper, namely Theorem
4.1
and
4.2
, show that the accuracy of the mean field and refined mean field approximation is
O(1/n)
and
O(1/n^{2})
. We illustrate our results by considering two examples: a model of cache replacement policies, and a load balancing model. These examples show that the proposed approximations can be computed efficiently and are very accurate. They also show that taking heterogeneity into account is important to characterize precisely the quantitative behavior of such systems.

When studying the performance of large computer systems, heterogeneity is often neglected because it increases the complexity of the model and because there are few tools to analyze such systems. We believe that our work has potential application in many models and will foster the development of the analysis of heterogeneous systems (such as load balancing or epidemic models).

C.1.
Computation of the exact steady-state probabilities

It is shown in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
that the steady-state distribution of the RANDOM(
\mathbf{m}
) cache replacement policy has a product-form, which the authors use to derive the per-object miss probability. Here, we show how to adapt the same methodology to compute the steady-state probability for an object to be in list

(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
but leads to a slightly different recurrence equation.

Recall that

denotes the list in which object

is (where

means that the object is not in the cache). We say that a state
\mathbf{S}
is admissible for
\mathbf{m}
if the number of objects in list

is exactly

for all

. Theorem 6 of
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
can be rephrased as follows: For any admissible state
\mathbf{S}
, the steady-state probability of
\mathbf{S}
is equal to

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{S})=\frac{1}{C(\mathbf{m},n)}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k})^{S_{k}},

where
C(\mathbf{m},n)=\sum_{\text{$\mathbf{S}$ admissible for $\mathbf{m}$}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k})^{S_{k}}
is a constant such that the probabilities
\pi(\mathbf{S})
sum to one. Note that the constant
C(\mathbf{m},n)
is not the same as the constant

defined in
(
Gast and
Van Houdt 2015
)
because our configuration
\mathbf{S}
does not take into account the position in a list in which an object is but only takes into account the list in which an object is: there is a
\prod_{s}m_{s}!
factor between the two.

By decomposing the set of admissible configurations depending on the list in which object
n
is (either outside the cache or in list
s
), we get that:

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{m},n)=\sum_{\text{$\mathbf{S}$ admissible for $\mathbf{m}$}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k})^{S_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\sum_{\text{$\mathbf{S}$ admissible for $\mathbf{m}$ and object $n$ is in list $s$}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k})^{S_{k}}

\displaystyle=C(\mathbf{m},n-1)+\sum_{s=1}^{S}(\lambda_{n})^{s}C(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{e}_{s},n-1),

with the convention that

if
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{0}
or if

. Indeed, there is a bijection between the admissible configurations for
\mathbf{m}
with
n
objects in which object
n
is in list
s
and the configurations for

Similarly, the probability for object

to be in list

is the sum over all admissible configurations such that object

is in list
s
which corresponds to the set of admissible configurations for

\displaystyle\pi_{n,s}^{\text{exact}}=\frac{(\lambda_{n})^{s}C(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{e}_{s},n-1)}{C(\mathbf{m},n)}.

The above recurrence equations can be used to compute the exact value of

for all

. By reordering the objects, it can be also used to compute the recurrence equation for all objects

and can be lowered to

by carefully reordering the objects. This means that for relatively small values of

, it is possible to compute an exact value for

Appendix D
Technical lemmas

D.1.
Bounds for Partial Derivatives of
\phi

In Lemma
D.1
we analyze the properties of the partial derivatives of
\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)
with respect to the initial condition
\mathbf{x}
. We introduce the set
\mathcal{I}:=\{1,\dots,n\}\times\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{k\}\times\mathcal{S}
to simplify notations for frequently appearing sums in the lemma and proof. The set
\mathcal{I}
encompasses all object-state tuples
(k,s)
, the set
\mathcal{I}_{k}
includes tuples
(k,s)
with fixed object
k
. We emphasize that the bounds for the partial derivatives differ substantially depending on whether
\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)
is derived with respect to
(k,\hat{s})\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
or
(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{k}
. Our results show that if the sum over the states of the absolute values of the partial derivatives of
\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)
,
\sum_{\hat{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\phi_{(k,\hat{s})}}{x_{(k,\hat{s})}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
, is derived with respect to the same object
k
, i.e. in direction of a object-state pair
(k,\hat{s})
, it can be bounded independent of
n
. However, if the same sum is derived with respect to a pair
(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{k}
it is of order
O(1/n)
. Subsequently, we can show similar properties for sums of higher partial derivatives such as
\sum_{\hat{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,\hat{s})}}{\partial x_{(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})}\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
. For the second partial derivatives we see that if at least one of the derivative direction is in
\mathcal{I}_{k}
the sum is bounded by
O(1/n)
and otherwise, if
(\tilde{k},\tilde{s}),(\hat{k},\hat{s})\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{k}
, the sum is of order
O(1/n^{2})
. Our analysis considers partial derivatives up to the fourth order for which we establish bounds with likewise properties. A direct consequence we frequently use is that the absolute value of the partial derivative of
\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)
can be bounded by the previously mentioned sums, for example
\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\leq\sum_{\hat{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\phi_{(k,\hat{s})}}{\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
. Thus, same bounds hold for
\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
and absolute values of higher order partial derivatives.

Lemma D.1.

Given the solution
\phi
of the ODE defined in section
4.1
. For the partial derivatives of
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{(k,s)}
with respect to the initial condition
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
(k,s)\in\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,n\}\times\mathcal{S}
the following properties hold:

(a)

If
i,j,w
and
l
are in
\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{1,\ldots,k-1,k+1,\ldots,n\}\times\mathcal{S}
, i.e. none of the tuples
i,j,w
or
l
refer to object
k
, then

\displaystyle(a.1)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n),

\displaystyle(a.2)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n^{2}),

\displaystyle(a.3)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n^{3}),

\displaystyle(a.4)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{4}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{w}\partial x_{l}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n^{4}).

(b)

Otherwise, if any tuple
i,j,w
or
l
is in
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{k\}\times\mathcal{S}
then, for the same summations,

\displaystyle(b.1)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1),

\displaystyle(b.2)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n),

\displaystyle(b.3)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n^{2}),

\displaystyle(b.4)\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{4}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{w}\partial x_{l}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n^{3}).

Proof.

We will prove this lemma by bounding the derivative with respect to time of
\frac{\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)
(and of the derivative of the higher order terms). The result will then follow by using Grönwall’s Lemma in differential form.

First derivative – proof of (a.1) and (b.1)
– Recall that
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t)
satisfies the differential equation
\frac{d}{dt}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t)=f(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))
. Hence, the partial derivatives of
\frac{\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t),\ i\in\mathcal{I}
with respect to the time
t
are

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{d\phi_{(k,s)}}{dt}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\partial(f_{(k,s)}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial\phi_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t).

Having a closer look at the partial derivatives of
f
(see Appendix
B.2
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{1}

\displaystyle\text{ for }u\in\mathcal{I}_{k},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{2}/n

\displaystyle\text{ for }u\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{k}.

Let
L_{1}:=\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
and define
c^{k}_{i}:=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\\
1/n\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial\phi_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\leq L_{1}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}c_{u}^{k}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|.

To obtain the bound on the max term, we start by bounding the change of the
\max
with respect to time for the first partial derivatives.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\leq L_{1}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}c_{u}^{k}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|+L_{1}\frac{1}{n}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|(n-1)\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

Furthermore, for
t
equal to zero,
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{x}
which implies that
\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},0)}{\partial x_{i}}=1
if
(k,s)=i
and
0
otherwise. From this it follows directly that
\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in S}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},0)\right|=1
. Applying Grönwalls Lemma to the obtained results yields
\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\leq\exp\bigl(2L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|t\bigr)=O(1)
.

We bound
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
in the same manner. First, for the time derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}c^{k}_{u}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|+\frac{1}{n}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|^{2}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\right).

Here we know that the second summand is
O(1)
. By definition of
\phi
, at time zero
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},0)\right|
is equal to one if
i
is in
\mathcal{I}_{k}
and zero otherwise. Using Grönwalls Lemma it follows

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(c^{k}_{i})=\begin{cases}O(1)&\text{ for }i\in\mathcal{I}_{k},\\
O(1/n)&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

This shows (a.1) and (b.1). Note that as an important direct consequence, the same is true for
\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
.

Second derivative – proof of (a.2) and (b.2)
– For the second partial derivatives we repeat the procedure of bounding the second derivative of
\phi
with respect to time
t
. Deriving the second partial derivative of
\phi
with respect to time
t
gives

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{v}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t).

To bound the above term, we observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}C_{3}/n\text{ if }i\text{ or }j\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\\
C_{4}/n^{2}\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

for
C_{3},C_{4}\geq 0
. We define
L_{2}=\max\{C_{3},C_{4}\}
with which we bound the first sum by

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{v}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}}{n}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\sum_{v\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{L_{2}}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{k}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\right)

By previous observations
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1)
and
\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(c^{k}_{i})
which implies that the first sum is
\frac{1}{n}L_{2}(O(c^{k}_{i})+O(c^{k}_{j}))+O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})=O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{i}+c^{k}_{j}))
.

The second sum can be bounded, similar to the first partial derivatives, by

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f^{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\leq L_{1}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}c_{u}^{k}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|.

Now we can derive bounds for
\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in S}\sum_{k}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
and
\sum_{s}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
. The procedure is the same as before. For the
\max
term we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{i}+c^{k}_{j}))+L_{1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}c_{u}^{k}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|\}

\displaystyle\leq O(1/n)+2L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|.

Recall that
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{x}
which implies that
\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},0)}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}=0
. Hence,
\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},0)\right|=0
which allows to conclude, by applying Grönwalls Lemma, that

\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(1/n)
.
For
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|
we infer

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{i}+c^{k}_{j}))+L_{1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}c_{u}^{k}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{i}+c^{k}_{j}))+L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|+\frac{1}{n}L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|^{2}\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle=O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{i}+c^{k}_{j}))+L_{1}\left|\mathcal{S}\right|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|+O(1/n^{2}).

With
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},0)\right|=0
and Grönwall, we have
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|=O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{i}+c^{k}_{j}))
.

. In order to obtain bounds for the

\displaystyle+\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{v}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{v}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial^{3}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}_{u}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)

and

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{l}}\bigl(\sum_{u,v,o\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{3}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{v}\partial x_{o}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{o}}{\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{v}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{v}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{w}}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)

The above quantity is equal to

Lemma D.2.

For

and

, the remainder terms satisfy

\displaystyle\E[\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}R_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi^{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right|\left|Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{x})\right|

\displaystyle=O(1/n),\quad

\displaystyle\E[\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}R_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})}\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i,j,u\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi^{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right|\left|R_{i,j,u}(\mathbf{x})\right|

\displaystyle=O(1/n^{2}).

Before starting the proof, recall that the first and second order remainder terms
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
defined in Section
6
are expressed as:

\displaystyle R_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)=\phantom{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1{-}\nu)\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x}+\nu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}),\tau)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}{-}\mathbf{x}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}{-}\mathbf{x}_{j})d\nu,

\displaystyle R_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1{-}\nu)^{2}\sum_{i,j,u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{3}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{x}+\nu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x}),\tau)(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}{-}\mathbf{x}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}{-}\mathbf{x}_{j})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{u}{-}\mathbf{x}_{u})d\nu,

and that, as defined in Appendix
B.2
,
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{R}
are given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\text{\qquad and \qquad}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 3},

where
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}
and
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 3}
are Kronecker products of
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})
with itself, i.e.
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}_{i,j}=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}{-}\mathbf{x}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}{-}\mathbf{x}_{j})
and
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}{-}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 3}_{i,j,u}=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}{-}\mathbf{x}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{j}{-}\mathbf{x}_{j})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{u}{-}\mathbf{x}_{u})
. The two tensors
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{R}
can be naturally extended to
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
due to their entries being polynomials.

Proof.

To prove the two statements we first introduce some simplifying notations. We define
c^{k}_{k_{1}}
, with
k,k_{1}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, to be one if
k
equals
k_{1}
and
1/n
otherwise. By the definition of
\mathbf{Q}
it follows that for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
:

\displaystyle\left|Q_{(k,s),(k_{1},s_{1})}(\mathbf{x})\right|=O(c^{k}_{k_{1}})=\begin{cases}O(1)&\text{ if }k=k_{1},\\
O(1/n)&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This can be seen by writing the elements of
\mathbf{Q}
based on the two transition types (
2a
) and (
2b
), as shown in Section
B.2
.

From Lemma
D.1
we know
\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right|=O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}))
which holds for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
. Here, the big
O
notation hides the dependence on
\tau,L_{1},L_{2}
and
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|
. By noting that
\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}}c_{k_{2}}^{k_{1}}c^{k}_{k_{1}}=O(1)
, we conclude

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x})\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi^{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right|=\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}O(c^{k_{1}}_{k_{2}})O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}))=O(1/n),

where we hide the dependence on
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|
.
To prove the second statement we define

\displaystyle c_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }k_{1}=k_{2}=k_{3}\\
\frac{1}{n}&\text{ if }k_{1}=k_{2}\neq k_{3}\text{ or }k_{2}=k_{3}\neq k_{1}\text{ or }k_{1}=k_{3}\neq k_{2}\\
\frac{1}{n^{2}}&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

By explicitly rewriting the entries of
\mathbf{R}
as done in Section
B.2
for
\mathbf{Q}
, the tensor
\mathbf{R}
is such that

\displaystyle\left|R_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2}),(k_{3},s_{3})}(\mathbf{x})\right|=O(c_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}})

Lemma
D.1
states that the third partial derivatives of
\phi
are bounded by
\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi^{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}\partial x_{(k_{3},s_{3})}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right|=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}+c^{k}_{k_{3}}))
. From
\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}c_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}+c^{k}_{k_{3}})=O(1)
, it follows that the sum of the two terms above behaves as

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2}),(k_{3},s_{3})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|R_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2}),(k_{3},s_{3})}(\mathbf{x})\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi^{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}\partial x_{(k_{3},s_{3})}}(\mathbf{y},\tau)\right|=O(1/n^{2}).

∎

D.3.
Connection of Differential and Integral Form for the Refinement Term

The following Lemma
D.3
shows how to express the refinement term
v
and
w
in integral form. Both representations are of importance since we exploit the differential form for numerical computations whereas we use the integral form in the proofs of Theorem
4.2
and Lemma
D.4
which are related to the accuracy of the refined mean field approximation.

Lemma D.3.

The solutions to the system of ODEs

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{v}_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))v_{u}(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,l\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{l}\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t)))w_{u,l}(\mathbf{x},t),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{w}_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}w_{u,(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial f_{(k_{1},s_{1})}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}w_{u,(k_{1},s_{1})}(\mathbf{x},t)\frac{\partial f_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))

\displaystyle\phantom{\dot{w}_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x},t)==}+Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))

can be expressed in integral form as

\displaystyle v_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)d\tau,

\displaystyle w_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial\phi_{(k_{1},s_{1})}}{\partial x_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)\frac{\partial\phi_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)d\tau.

Proof.

For a sufficiently differentiable function
h:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{t}h(\tau,t)d\tau=h(t,t)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}(\tau,t)d\tau.

We define
h(\tau,t)=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)
. Recall that
\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(x,t),0)=0
which implies
h(t,t)=0
. To calculate
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}(\tau,t)
, we use the identity

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}f_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau))

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}f_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,l\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{l}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)\frac{\partial\phi_{l}}{\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau),

where the last term is the same as the one derived in (
21
).

Combining these results and rearranging terms leads to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)d\tau

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)d\tau}_{v_{u}(\mathbf{x},t)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{u,l\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{l}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},t))\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)\frac{\partial\phi_{l}}{\partial x_{j}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),t-\tau)d\tau}_{w_{u,l}},

which is the ODE describing
v_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)
. We obtain the integral form for
w_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{x},t)
by application of the same steps.

∎

D.4.
Comparison of the Refinement Term
\mathbf{v}
and the Quadratic Taylor Term

In Lemma
D.4
below, we bound the difference of the refinement term
\mathbf{v}
and the quadratic term of the second order Taylor expansion appearing in the proof of Theorem
4.2
. By defining
g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{y})\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{y},t-\tau)
we see that the entries of the refinement term
\mathbf{v}
in integral form can be expressed as
v_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),\tau)d\tau
. Similarly, the time integral over the expectation of the quadratic term of the Taylor expansion is given by
\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\ \E[g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\tau)]d\tau
. The latter arises due to the comparison of generator approach used in the proof of Theorem
4.2
and the subsequent Taylor expansion of order two. The lemma shows that the difference of the two terms decreases quadratically with the system size
n
and allows, in combination with Lemma
D.2
, to obtain the accuracy bounds for the refined mean field approximation.

Lemma D.4.

Define
g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{y},\tau)=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{y})\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{y},t-\tau)
with
\phi
being the solution to the ODE defined in Section
4.1
and
\mathbf{Q}
as defined in Appendix
B.2
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\E[g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\tau)-g_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),\tau)]d\tau=O(1/n^{2}).

Proof.

We follow a similar proof concept as in Theorem
4.1
. First, we define
h_{\tau}(\mathbf{y})=g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{y},\tau)
and rewrite

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\E[g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{X}(\tau),\tau)-g_{(k,s)}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau),\tau)]d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\E[h_{\tau}(\mathbf{X}(\tau))-h_{\tau}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))]d\tau.

By definition of
g_{(k,s)}
,
h_{\tau}
is twice continuously differentiable. Second, using Lemma
6.1
, we see that
\E[h_{\tau}(\mathbf{X}(\tau))-h_{\tau}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x},\tau))]
is equal to

(23)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\E[\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{X}(\nu),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\bigl(h_{\tau}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau{-}\nu))-h_{\tau}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau{-}\nu))\bigr)-D_{x}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau{-}\nu)f(\mathbf{X}(\nu))]d\nu.

We use a second order Taylor expansion to express
h_{\tau}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau-\nu))=(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tau-\nu)
around
\mathbf{X}(\nu)
. The constant and linear term of the expansion are
h_{\tau}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau-\nu))
and

D_{x}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau-\nu)\Delta X(\nu)
respectively. By realizing that the sum
\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{X}(\nu),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}D_{x}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau-\nu)\Delta X(\nu)
is equal to
D_{x}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau-\nu)f(\mathbf{X}(\nu))
, it follows that equation (
23
) is equal to the remainder of the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\E[\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}K_{\mathbf{X}(\nu),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\Delta X_{i}(\nu)\Delta X_{j}(\nu)\int_{0}^{1}(1-\omega)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{X}(\nu)+\omega\Delta\mathbf{X}(\nu),\tau-\nu)d\omega]d\nu.

Taking the supremum over all possible values of
\mathbf{X}(\tau)
as well as
\mathbf{x}
and using the definition of
\mathbf{Q}
, the above term is bounded by

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sup_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{y})\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|d\nu.

The rest of the proof is then essentially a careful analysis of the above sum. For that, we use again Lemma
D.1
but also need bounds on up to the second derivative of
\mathbf{Q}
(This is needed because in the above expression the function
h_{\tau}
is defined as a function of
\mathbf{Q}
). The latter makes the rest of the proof long and technical but the main ideas are essentially similar to the ones used in Lemma
D.1
.

The second derivative of
h\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$}
satisfies (for
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\bigl(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu)\bigr)

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu)+\sum_{u,r\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu)\frac{\partial\phi_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{z},\tau{-}\nu).

To bound the above term, we need to study
\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial g_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z},\tau)
and
\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial^{2}g_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\mathbf{z},\tau)
. Applying the chain rule to the definition of
h_{\tau}
shows that the first partial derivative of
h_{\tau}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{u}}\left(\sum_{q,l}Q_{m,l}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{m}\partial x_{l}}(\mathbf{z},t{-}\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,l\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial Q_{q,l}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)+\sum_{q,l\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{q,l}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu).

Similarly, the second partial derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{r}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{r}}\left(\sum_{q,l\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial Q_{q,l}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)+\sum_{q,l\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{q,l}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,l\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}Q_{q,l}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)+\frac{\partial Q_{q,l}}{\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}\partial x_{r}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\partial Q_{q,l}}{\partial x_{r}}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}\partial x_{u}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)+Q_{q,l}(\mathbf{z})\frac{\partial^{4}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{q}\partial x_{l}\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu).

What remains is to bound the sums appearing in the above derivatives. We use the notations
c^{k}_{k_{1}}
and
c_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}
, as in the proof of Lemma
D.2
. From the representation of
\mathbf{Q}
given in Appendix
B.2
, it can be seen that
Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(z)=O(c^{k_{1}}_{k_{2}})
,
\frac{\partial Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{(k_{3},s_{3})}}=O(c_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}})
and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{(k_{3},s_{3})}\partial x_{(k_{4},s_{4})}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}O(1/n)&\text{ if }(k_{1},k_{2})=(k_{3},k_{4})\text{ or }(k_{4},k_{3}),\\
O(1/n^{2})&\text{ if }k_{1}=k_{3},k_{4}\text{ or }k_{2}=k_{3},k_{4},\\
O(1/n^{3})&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Lemma
D.1
gives bounds for the partial derivatives of
\phi
. This enables us to develop an upper bound for
|\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(z)|
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(z)\right|\leq\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(z)\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(z,t-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle\phantom{\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(z)\leq}\quad+\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(z)\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(z,t-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}O\bigl(c_{k_{1},k_{2},k^{\prime}}\bigr)O\bigl(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}})\bigr)+\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}O\bigl(c^{k_{1}}_{k_{2}}\bigr)O\bigl(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}+c^{k}_{k_{3}})\bigr)=O(\frac{1}{n}c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}).

With the above observations we bound the first sum of (
25
) by

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu))\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial g_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu),\tau)\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}O(\frac{1}{n}c^{k}_{k^{\prime}})O(\frac{1}{n}(c^{k^{\prime}}_{k_{1}}+c^{k^{\prime}}_{k_{2}}))=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}})).

For the second partial derivatives of
h_{\tau}(\mathbf{z})=g_{(k,s)}(\mathbf{z},t-\tau)
, we note that all sums which appear in the explicit form of the partial derivative are bounded by
O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}}))
. Using the bounds for
\mathbf{Q}
and
\phi
and their respective partial derivatives we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}\left|Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})(\mathbf{z})}\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{4}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}O(c^{k_{1}}_{k_{2}})O(\frac{1}{n^{3}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}+c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}}))=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}})),

and that

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}\left|\frac{\partial Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\mathbf{z})\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{3}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}O(c_{k_{1},k_{2},\hat{k}})O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}+c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}})),

and

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(z)\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{z},t-\nu)\right|=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}})).

The last bound follows with careful case-by-case analysis for the second derivative of
\frac{\partial^{2}Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}{\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}
. As a direct consequence
\left|\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu))\right|=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}}))
. This enables us to establish a bound for the second sum of (
25
),

\displaystyle\sum_{u,r\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu))\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime}),(\hat{k},\hat{s})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}g_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu),\tau)\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}{\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime}),(\hat{k},\hat{s})\in\mathcal{I}}O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k^{\prime}}+c^{k}_{\hat{k}}))O(c^{k^{\prime}}_{k_{1}})O(c^{\hat{k}}_{k_{2}})=O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}})).

For the last part of the proof, we use the obtained results to bound
\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{y})\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(h_{\tau}\circ\text{\boldmath$\phi$})(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|
.
By equation (
25
) we see that (
24
) is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sup_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{i,j}(\mathbf{y})\right|\left|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu))\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sum_{u,r\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{\tau}}{\partial x_{u}\partial x_{r}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu))\frac{\partial\phi_{u}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\frac{\partial\phi_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|d\nu.

Indeed, we bound the supremum by the two following terms

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{y})\right|\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial g_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu),\tau)\right|\left|\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|

(26)

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}=\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}O(c^{k_{1}}_{k_{2}})O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}))=O(1/n^{2})

and

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(\mathbf{y})\right|\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime}),(\hat{k},\hat{s})\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}g_{(k,s)}}{\partial x_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}\partial x_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu),\tau)\right|

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}\left|Q_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}(z)\right|\sum_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime}),(\hat{k},\hat{s})\in\mathcal{I}}\ldots}\times\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(k^{\prime},s^{\prime})}}{\partial x_{(k_{1},s_{1})}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|\left|\frac{\partial\phi_{(\hat{k},\hat{s})}}{\partial x_{(k_{2},s_{2})}}(\mathbf{z},\tau-\nu)\right|

(27)

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}}=\sum_{(k_{1},s_{1}),(k_{2},s_{2})}O(c^{k_{1}}_{k_{2}})O(\frac{1}{n^{2}}(c^{k}_{k_{1}}+c^{k}_{k_{2}}))=O(1/n^{2}).

The bounds (
26
) and (
27
) show that (
24
) is of order
O(1/n^{2})
, where the hidden constant depends on
\tau,\bar{r},\left|\mathcal{S}\right|
, from which the claim of the Lemma follows. ∎
