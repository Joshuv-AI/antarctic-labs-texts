Abstract
. We study the distribution of zeroes of power series with infinite radius of convergence. The coefficients of the series have the form ξ ⁡ ( n ) ​ a ​ ( n ) \xi(n)a(n) , where a a is a smooth sequence of positive numbers, and ξ \xi is a sequence of complex-valued multipliers having binary correlations and no gaps in the spectrum. We show that under certain assumptions on the smoothness of the sequence a a and on the binary correlations of the multipliers ξ \xi , the zeroes of the power series are equidistributed with respect to a radial measure defined by the sequence a a . We apply our approach to several examples of the sequence ξ \xi : (i) IID sequences, (ii) sequences e ⁡ ( α ​ n 2 ) e(\alpha n^{2}) with Diophantine α \alpha , (iii) random multiplicative sequences, (iv) the Golay–Rudin–Shapiro sequence, (v) the indicator function of the square-free integers, (vi) the Thue–Morse sequence.



1.
Introduction and main results

In this work, we study the
following problem:
how does the sequence of multipliers
\xi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}

affect the zero distribution of the entire function represented by the
power series

F_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)a(n)z^{n}
?
The theory of entire functions has no general results pertaining to this classical
question, there are only several case studies initiated by Lévy, Littlewood, Offord,
and others. The most studied and the only relatively well understood instances
are the cases of IID sequences
\xi
(see Littlewood–Offord
[
22
]
, Offord
[
32
,
35
]
, Kabluchko–Zaporozhets
[
14
]
, Nazarov–Nishry–Sodin
[
28
]
) and of
lacunary sequences
a
(see Hayman
[
10
]
, Hayman–Rossi
[
12
]
, and Offord
[
33
,
34
]
), in which case the sequence of multipliers
\xi
plays no essential role.

A new approach to this problem, which is based on spectral properties of the sequence
\xi
,
was launched in
[
1
,
2
]
. It appears that certain estimates for the
autocorrelations

\frac{1}{B-A}\,\sum_{A\leq n<B}\xi(n)\bar{\xi}(n+h),\qquad h\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\,,

yield the angular equidistribution of zeroes of the entire functions of exponential type

(1.1)

F_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)\,\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\,.

More precisely,

n_{F_{\xi}}(r;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}+o(1)}{2\pi}\,r\,,\qquad r\to\infty,

where
n_{F_{\xi}}(r;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is the number of zeroes of
F_{\xi}
(counted with multiplicities) in the closed sector
\{z\colon 0\leq|z|\leq r,\theta_{1}\leq\arg z\leq\theta_{2}\}
.
In
[
1
]
the authors proved that the same angular equidistribution
holds under the assumption that
\xi
is
a Wiener sequence without lacunas in its spectrum. Recall that the sequence
\xi
is called

a Wiener sequence
, if for every
h\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the limit

r(h)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{N}\,\sum_{0\leq n<N}\xi(n)\bar{\xi}(n+h)

exists.
Extending the sequence
r
to
\mathbb{Z}
by setting
r(-h)=\bar{r}(h)
, we get a positive-definite sequence,
which is, therefore, given by the sequence of Fourier coefficients of a non-negative measure

\chi_{\xi}
on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
,
r=\widehat{\chi}_{\xi}
,
\chi_{\xi}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
.
The measure
\chi_{\xi}
is called
the spectral measure
of the Wiener sequence
\xi
, and the
closed support of
\chi_{\xi}
is called
the spectrum
of
\xi
.
This approach permitted us to prove the angular equidistribution of zeroes of the function
F_{\xi}

defined in (
1.1
) for many sequences
\xi
that were intractable using
the previously known techniques.

In this work, we advance in several directions, studying the zero distribution of
F_{\xi}

on local scales and replacing the sequence
a(n)=\tfrac{1}{n!}
by a rather wide class of “smooth sequences”. The uniform transportation distance provides a convenient set-up for this study.

It is worth mentioning that our interest in
the zero distribution on local scales was a result of a conversation on some aspects of the work Lester–Matomäki–Radziwiłł
[
18
]
, which one of the authors had
several years ago with Steve Lester and Maks Radziwiłł.

1.1.
Smooth sequences
a

A positive sequence
a
will be called
smooth
if

(1.2)

a(n)=\exp\Bigl[-\int_{0}^{n}\varphi\Bigr]\,,

where
\varphi
is a non-negative, increasing, concave
C^{2}
-function on
[0,\infty)

satisfying

\varphi(0)=0,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\varphi(t)=\infty,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(t)=0\,,

with some quantitative bounds on
|\varphi^{\prime\prime}|
.
Different sequences
\xi
will require different bounds on
|\varphi^{\prime\prime}|
, but
the following condition (
1.3
) will suffice for all instances of the sequence

\xi
considered in this work (except for the Thue–Morse sequence,
which requires a stronger restriction):

(1.3)

(\varphi^{\prime})^{2+{\varepsilon}}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}|\varphi^{\prime\prime}|\lesssim_{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{\prime})^{2-{\varepsilon}}\qquad{\rm for\ every\ }{\varepsilon}>0.

These assumptions are not too restrictive, and the class
of entire functions with smooth Taylor coefficients contains functions of zero and infinite order of
growth.

In what follows, we will mostly use the inverse
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
rather than
\varphi
itself.
This is a convex function, which, together with its derivative, grows to
+\infty
, and satisfies the
regularity condition

(1.4)

(\psi^{\prime})^{1-{\varepsilon}}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\psi^{\prime\prime}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}(\psi^{\prime})^{1+{\varepsilon}}\qquad{\rm for\ every\ }{\varepsilon}>0,

which readily follows from (
1.3
). In turn, (
1.4
) yields similar bounds for the
function
\psi^{\prime}
:

(1.5)

\psi^{1-{\varepsilon}}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\psi^{\prime}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\psi^{1+{\varepsilon}}\qquad{\rm for\ every\ }{\varepsilon}>0.

1.2.
The reference measure
\gamma

To measure the growth of the entire function
F_{\xi}
, we use a slightly smoothed maximal term
of the power series
\sum_{n\geq 0}a(n)z^{n}
, letting

\mu(R)=\max_{t\geq 0}\,\exp\Bigl[t\log R-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi\Bigr].

Then, assuming that
R\geq 1
, we get

\log\mu(R)=\int_{0}^{\log R}\psi(s)\,{\rm d}s=\int_{1}^{R}\frac{\nu(r)}{r}\,{\rm d}r\,,

where the function
\nu=\psi\circ\log
is the smoothed central index of the same power series.

We expect that for Wiener sequences
\xi
without lacunas in their spectrum, the subharmonic functions
\log|F_{\xi}(z)|
and
\log\mu(|z|)
are sufficiently close to each other, and therefore, the counting
measure of zeroes of
F_{\xi}
,

n_{F_{\xi}}=\,\sum_{\lambda\colon F_{\xi}(\lambda)=0}\,\delta_{\lambda},

is close to the radial measure

\gamma=(2\pi)^{-1}\,\Delta\log\mu(|z|)=\sigma(r)r^{-1}{\rm d}r\otimes\,{\rm d}\theta,\qquad z=re(\theta)\,,

where
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log r
, and
e(\theta)=e^{2\pi{\rm i}\theta}
.

Note that we could
equally use the functions
\mu_{1}(R)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a(n)R^{n}
or

\mu_{2}(R)=\bigl(\sum_{n\geq 0}a(n)^{2}R^{2n}\bigr)^{1/2}
, and the corresponding Riesz measures

\gamma_{i}=(2\pi)^{-1}\Delta\log\mu_{i}(|z|)
,
i=1,2
. The difference between the measures

\gamma
,
\gamma_{1}
, and
\gamma_{2}
is inessential for our purposes.

1.3.
(
\gamma,\rho
)-equidistribution

To measure the proximity of
n_{F_{\xi}}
and
\gamma
, we use a classical lattice-point-counting idea usually attributed to Gauss: for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
,

m(K_{-})\leq\#(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap K)\leq m(K_{+})\,,

where
m
is the planar Lebesgue measure,
K_{+}
is the
\sqrt{2}
-Euclidean neighbourhood of
K
,
and
K_{-}
is a subset of
K
, which consists of points that are
\sqrt{2}
-separated from the boundary of
K
.
Thus,
|\#(Z^{2}\cap K)-m(K)|
is bounded by the Lebesgue measure of the
\sqrt{2}
-neighbourhood of the the boundary of
K
.

Equivalently, the uniform transportation distance between the counting
measure
\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\delta_{a}
and the Lebesgue measure
m
does not exceed
\sqrt{2}
.
In our setting, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
play the role of lattice points, the measure
\gamma
, defined
above, replaces the Lebesgue measure
m
, and the Euclidean metric is replaced by a slowly varying metric, which locally looks Euclidean.

We introduce a distance
d_{\rho}
on
\mathbb{C}
, letting

d_{\rho}(z_{1},z_{2})=\inf_{\ell}\,\int_{\ell}\,\frac{|{\rm d}w|}{\rho(w)}\,,\qquad z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C},

where the infimum is taken over all
C^{1}
-curves
\ell
connecting the points
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
.
Here and elsewhere,
\rho
is a positive
C^{1}
-smooth radial function on
\mathbb{C}

such that
\rho^{\prime}(R)=o(1)
for
R\to\infty
. We will call the function

\rho
a
radial gauge
. For any set
X\subset\mathbb{C}
, we denote by

X_{+\tau}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon d_{\rho}(z,X)<\tau\}

the
\tau
-neighbourhood of
X
.

Definition 1
.

The counting measure
n_{F_{\xi}}
is said to be

(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed if
there exist positive constants
C
and
\tau
such that, for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
,

|n_{F_{\xi}}(K)-\gamma(K)|\leq C\gamma((\partial K)_{+\tau})\,.

Note that
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistribution is an asymptotic characteristic of zeroes of

F_{\xi}
, which is not affected by multiplying the gauge
\rho
by a positive constant.

It is worth mentioning that, under our assumptions,
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistribution of
zeroes of
F_{\xi}

is equivalent to the seemingly stronger finiteness of the uniform transportation
distance,
\operatorname{Tra}_{d_{\rho}}(n_{F_{\xi}},\gamma)<\infty
. We will
not be using the transportation distance
\operatorname{Tra}_{d_{\rho}}
in the bulk of
this work, so we recall its definition and prove the aforementioned equivalence in Appendix A.

1.3.1.
… and how to use it

First, we consider the disks
R\bar{\mathbb{D}}=\{|z|\leq R\}
. Recalling that
\mu(R)
vanishes on
[0,1]
,
we get

\gamma(R\bar{\mathbb{D}})=\int_{1}^{R}\frac{\sigma(r)}{r}\,{\rm d}r=\nu(R)\,.

Furthermore, it is easy to see that if
R
is large enough,

(R\mathbb{T})_{+\tau}\subset\bigl\{R-2\tau\rho(R)\leq|z|\leq R+2\tau\rho(R)\bigr\}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\gamma((R\mathbb{T})_{+\tau})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{R-2\tau\rho(R)}^{R+2\tau\rho(R)}\frac{\sigma(r)}{r}\,{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\simeq\sigma(R)\rho(R)/R\,,

whenever
\sigma
stays nearly constant on the intervals

[R-O(\rho(R)),R+O(\rho(R))]
, which will be always the case in this work.
Under our regularity assumptions, the functions
\nu=\psi\circ\log
and
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log

have almost the same rate of growth (see (
1.5
)), while for the most part, in this work,
(except of the case when
\xi
is the Thue–Morse sequence) we may take
\rho\simeq R\sigma(R)^{-\kappa}
with
0<\kappa<\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Hence, in these cases, we get

|n_{F_{\xi}}(R\bar{\mathbb{D}})-\nu(R)|=o(\nu(R)^{1-\kappa^{\prime}}),\quad R\to\infty\,,

for any
\kappa^{\prime}<\kappa
. In the Thue–Morse case, our estimate becomes worse but still meaningful:

|n_{F_{\xi}}(R\bar{\mathbb{D}})-\nu(R)|=o\bigl(\nu(R)e^{-c\sqrt{\log\nu(R)}}\,\bigr),\quad R\to\infty\,,

with some
c>0
.

Now we turn to estimates on local scales and consider the disks

\bar{D}(z;r)=\{w\colon|w-z|\leq r\}
.
If
r
is comparable to
\rho(|z|)
, then

(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistribution yields the upper bound

\displaystyle n_{F_{\xi}}(\bar{D}(z;r))

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(\bar{D}(z;r))

\displaystyle\simeq\sigma(|z|)\Bigl(\frac{\rho(|z|)}{|z|}\Bigl)^{2}\,.

When
r
becomes much larger than
\rho(|z|)
but in such a way that

\sigma
remains nearly constant on
\bar{D}(z;r)
, the main term is

\gamma(\bar{D}(z;r))\simeq\frac{\sigma(|z|)}{|z|^{2}}\cdot r^{2}\,,

while the error term is bounded by

\gamma((\partial D(z;r))_{+\tau})\lesssim\frac{\sigma(|z|)}{|z|^{2}}\cdot\rho(|z|)r\,,

which is much smaller than the main term. Hence, in such disks, the number of zeroes

n_{F_{\xi}}(\bar{D}(z;r))
approximately equals
\gamma(\bar{D}(z;r))
,
with an explicit control of the
o(1)
term, namely

|n_{F_{\xi}}(D(z,r))-\gamma(D(z,r))|\lesssim\gamma(D(z,r))\cdot\frac{\rho(|z|)}{r}.

1.4.
Main results

We consider several instances of sequences
\xi
having different origins.
For each of these sequences, we prove that the zeroes of the function
F_{\xi}
are

(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed for some explicitly computed radial gauge
\rho
.

Recall that the measure
\gamma
has the density
(2\pi)^{-1}\sigma(R)/R^{2}
, where
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log
,
with respect to the area measure on
\mathbb{C}
. Hence, the gauge
\rho
has to be at least

\gtrsim R(\sigma(R))^{-1/2}
. Our technique needs slightly more:

\rho(R)\gtrsim R(\log\sigma(R)/\sigma(R))^{1/2}
,
and in two instances (the IID sequences
\xi
, and
\xi(n)=e(\alpha n^{2})
with irrational
\alpha
badly approximated by rationals), we achieve that scale. In most other instances, our method stops
at the scale
\rho(R)=R(\sigma(R))^{-c}
with some
0<c<\tfrac{1}{2}
, which we will estimate.
It is worth mentioning that we lack examples which would clarify whether these estimates
reflect the correct order of discrepancy of zeroes of
F_{\xi}
.

To better digest the precision of our results, it
is instructive to consider the special
case
a(n)=(n!)^{-1/2}
, when the smoothed central index of the Taylor series
is
\nu(R)=\psi(\log R)=R^{2}+O(R)
, and

\sigma(R)=\psi^{\prime}(\log R)=2R^{2}+O(1)
, and, up to an inessential correction,

\gamma=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\times{\rm area\ measure}
.

In order to simplify the statements, in this section we give our results in a somewhat weaker
form than what will be proven afterwards. For this reason, we enumerate them in accordance with
their numeration in the bulk of the paper, adding the letter “
\sf a
”. For instance,
Theorem
\ref{thm:IID}\sf a
is a simplified version of the more general result given in Theorem
1
.

In this section, we always tacitly assume that the sequence of smooth coefficients
a(n)
is defined by (
1.2
), with the function
\varphi
satisfying the regularity condition (
1.3
).

1.4.1.
Non-degenerate IID sequence

A

Theorem
\bf\ref{thm:IID}\sf a
.

Suppose that
\xi
is a non-degenerate IID sequence satisfying
1
1

1

Note that, generally speaking, IID sequences are not Wiener sequences,
unless
\xi(0)
has a finite second moment.

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\xi(0)|^{\varepsilon}\bigr]<\infty,\quad{with\ some\ }{\varepsilon}>0.

Then, almost surely, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
are
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed,
provided that

(1.6)

\rho(R)\simeq R\,\sqrt{\frac{\log\sigma(R)}{\sigma(R)}}\,.

In the case
a(n)=(n!)^{-1/2}
the radial gauge
\rho

boils down to
\rho(R)\simeq\sqrt{\log R}
. Interestingly, this estimate cannot be essentially improved.
Theorem

\xi
is
a sequence of complex Gaussian independent random variables and if
\delta
is sufficiently small, then,
a.s., the function

\{|z-j^{2}|\leq\delta(\log j)^{1/4}\}
,
j\geq 2
.

1.4.2.
The sequence
\xi(n)=e(\alpha n^{2})
with irrational
\alpha

This is a Wiener sequence whose spectral measure is the Lebesgue measure.
For
a(n)=1/n!
the angular equidistribution of the zeroes of
the corresponding entire function (
1.1
) was proven by
Eremenko and Ostrovskii in
[
6
]
.
A more general result, pertaining to the sequences
\xi(n)=e(Q(n))
, where
Q(x)=\sum_{j=2}^{d}q_{j}x^{j}
is a polynomial of degree
d\geq 2
with real coefficients at least one of which
is irrational, was proven in
[
2
, Theorem 1]
.
Here, we will consider the case of a Diophantine irrational
\alpha
, and
our estimates will depend on the Diophantine properties of
\alpha
.

Let
\|t\|
denote the distance from
t
to the closest integer.

Theorem
\bf\ref{thm:diophnatine}\sf a
.

Let
\xi(n)=e(\alpha n^{2})
.

(i)
Suppose that for any positive integer
q\geq 2
,

\|q\alpha\|\geq\frac{c(\alpha)}{q(\log q)^{a}}\,,\quad with\ some\ a\geq 0.

Then the zeroes of the entire function
F_{\xi}
are
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed
with the radial gauge
\rho=R\sigma^{-1/2}(\log\sigma)^{(a+1)/2}
.

(ii)
Suppose that for any positive integer
q\geq 2
,

\|q\alpha\|\geq\frac{c(\alpha)}{q^{1+b}}\,,\quad with\ some\ b>0\,.

Then, for every
b^{\prime}>b
, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
are
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed
with the radial gauge
\rho=R\sigma^{-1/(2+b^{\prime})}
.

Note that Nassif

]
studied the case

and, using a technique developed by Hardy and Littlewood in

,

(later, Littlewood

form a “slowly varying lattice”.

1.4.3.
Random multiplicative and completely multiplicative functions

Random multiplicative functions we deal with here are sequences
\xi
defined
by

\xi(n)=\begin{cases}\prod_{j=1}^{k}X_{p_{j}},&{\rm if\ }n=\prod_{j=1}^{k}p_{j},\\
0&{\rm if\ }n{\rm\ is\ not\ square-free},\end{cases}

in the multiplicative case, and

\xi(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(X_{p_{j}})^{m_{j}},\qquad{\rm if\ }n=\prod_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}^{m_{j}},

in the completely multiplicative case. Here
X_{p}
are symmetric and unimodular IIDs parameterized by the primes. A.s., the sequence
\xi
a Wiener sequence whose spectral measure is the Lebesgue measure.

Theorem
\bf\ref{thm:random-multiplicative}\sf a
.

Let
\xi
be a random multiplicative function. Then, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
are

(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed with the radial gauge
\rho=R\sigma^{-c}
, for any
c<1/6
.

The proof of this theorem uses a randomized version of the binary Chowla conjecture (Lemma
6.7
), which might be of independent interest.

1.4.4.
The Golay–Rudin–Shapiro sequence

Here we consider the sequence
\xi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\{\pm 1\}
, which at each
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

equals the parity of the number of (possibly overlapping) pairs of consecutive
ones in the binary expansion of
n
, i.e., if
n=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}r_{\ell}(n)2^{\ell}

is a binary expansion of
n
, then
\xi(n)=(-1)^{\tau(n)}
, where

\tau(n)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}r_{l}(n)r_{\ell+1}(n)
. Equivalently, this sequence
can be defined by
\xi(0)=1
,
\xi(2n)=\xi(n)
,
\xi(2n+1)=(-1)^{n}\xi(n)
,
n\geq 0
.
This is a Wiener sequence, and,
as in the previous cases, its spectral measure is the Lebesgue measure.

Theorem
\bf\ref{thm:GRS-sequences}\sf a
.

Let
\xi
be the Golay–Rudin–Shapiro sequence.
Then, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
are
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed
with the radial gauge
\rho=R\sigma^{-c}
for any
c<1/3
.

1.4.6.
The Thue–Morse sequence

The Thue–Morse sequence
\xi\colon\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\{\pm 1\}

is defined by
\xi(0)=1
,
\xi(2n)=\xi(n)
,
\xi(2n+1)=-\xi(n)
,
n\geq 0
.
Equivalently,
\xi(n)=(-1)^{\omega(n)}
, where
\omega(n)
is the number of ones in the binary expansion of
n
. It is well-known since the works by Mahler
[
23
]
and
Kakutani
[
15
]
, that this is a Wiener sequence with a singular continuous spectral
measure having no gaps in its support.

Theorem
\bf\ref{thm:TM}\sf a
.

Let
\xi
be the Thue–Morse sequence.
Let the function
\varphi
, which defines the smooth coefficients
a
,
in addition to the regularity assumption (

), satisfy the

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\lesssim(\log t)^{-C}
with some
C>1
.
Then, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
are
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed
with the radial gauge
\rho=R\exp(-c\sqrt{\log\sigma}\,)
, provided that the constant

is sufficiently small.

This corresponds to
\rho(R)=R\exp(-c\sqrt{\log R}\,)
in the case

.

3.
A subharmonic lemma

Let

Proposition 1

(\gamma-\gamma_{1})(K)=\int_{K}\Psi\,{\rm d}(\gamma-\gamma_{1})+\int_{K}(1-\Psi)\,{\rm d}(\gamma-\gamma_{1}).

Estimate of the second integral on the RHS is straightforward:

\int_{K}(1-\Psi)\,{\rm d}(\gamma-\gamma_{1})=\int_{K\setminus K_{-8}}(1-\Psi)\,{\rm d}(\gamma-\gamma_{1})\leq\gamma(K\setminus K_{-8}).

Next, by Green’s identity, we get

\displaystyle\int_{K}\Psi\,{\rm d}(\gamma-\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{K}\Delta\Psi\cdot(V-V_{1})\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{K\setminus K_{-8}}\Delta\Psi\cdot(V-V_{1})\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{K\setminus K_{-8}}|\Delta\Psi|\cdot|V-V_{1}|\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w\in W\cap(K_{-4}\setminus K_{-11})}\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\Delta\psi_{w}|\cdot|V-V_{1}|\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{w\in W\cap(K_{-4}\setminus K_{-11})}\rho(w)^{-2}\,\int_{2D_{w}}|V-V_{1}|\,{\rm d}m\,.

By Lemma
3.3
, the RHS is

\lesssim\sum_{w\in W\cap(K_{-4}\setminus K_{-11})}\gamma(2D_{w})\lesssim\gamma(K_{-1}\setminus K_{-14}),

proving (
3.13
).

Now, we verify the opposite bound

(3.14)

(\gamma_{1}-\gamma)(K)\lesssim\gamma(K_{+8}\setminus K_{-8}).

Set
\Psi=\Psi_{K}
. Since
\Psi\big|_{K}=1
and
\operatorname{supp}(\Psi)\subset K_{+8}
, we have

(\gamma_{1}-\gamma)(K)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Psi\,{\rm d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma)+\int_{K_{+8}\setminus K}\Psi\,{\rm d}(\gamma-\gamma_{1}).

The second integral on the RHS is

\leq\int_{K_{+8}\setminus K}\Psi\,{\rm d}\gamma\leq\gamma(K_{+8}\setminus K).

At last, arguing as above, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Psi\,{\rm d}(\gamma_{1}-\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Delta\Psi\cdot(V_{1}-V)\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{K_{+8}\setminus K}\Delta\Psi\cdot(V_{1}-V)\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{K_{+8}\setminus K}|\Delta\Psi|\cdot|V_{1}-V|\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{w\in W\cap(K_{+4}\setminus K_{-4})}\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{2D_{w}}|\Delta\psi_{w}|\cdot|V_{1}-V|\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{w\in W\cap(K_{+4}\setminus K_{-4})}\rho(w)^{-2}\,\int_{2D_{w}}|V_{1}-V|\,{\rm d}m

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{w\in W\cap(K_{+4}\setminus K_{-4})}\gamma(2D_{w})

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma(K_{+8}\setminus K_{-8}),

proving (
3.14
). Clearly, estimates (
3.13
) and (
3.14
)
together yield estimate (
3.5
), completing the proof of Lemma
3.4
, and hence,
of Proposition
1
as well.
∎

4.
From the power series
F_{\xi}(z)
to Weyl-type sums
W_{R}(\theta)

In view of Proposition
1
, we are after a lower bound for
|F|

on a sufficiently dense set of points in
\mathbb{C}
.
The main result of this section, Proposition
2
, reduces this question to the problem of
obtaining lower bounds for certain Weyl-type exponential sum.

4.1.
Regularity of
\varphi
and
\psi

Definition 3

(
\Delta
-regularity)
.

Let
\Delta\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a non-decreasing function satisfying

\Delta(2s)\simeq\Delta(s)
and
\Delta(s)=o(\sqrt{s}\,(\log s)^{-3/2})
as
s\to\infty
.
The function
\varphi
will be called
\Delta
-regular,
if it is a non-negative, increasing, concave
C^{2}
-function on
[0,\infty)
, satisfying

\varphi(0)=0,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\varphi(t)=\infty,\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(t)=0\,,

with

(4.1)

|\varphi^{\prime\prime}|\leq(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}\,\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime})\,.

By
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
we denote the inverse function to
\varphi
.
This is a convex function, which, together with its derivative, grows to
+\infty
.

We start with some simple estimates for
\Delta
-regular functions
\varphi
and their inverses

\psi
, which will be used throughout this work.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular.
Then,

(a)

\psi^{\prime\prime}\leq\psi^{\prime}\Delta(\psi^{\prime})
;

(b)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)=(1+o(1))\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
, for

\tau\to\infty
and

|t-\tau|\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau))=o(1)
;

(c)

\psi^{\prime}(u)=(1+o(1))\psi^{\prime}(v)
, for

v\to\infty
and
|u-v|\Delta(\psi^{\prime}(v))=o(1)
;

(d)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(t))\gtrsim 1/t
;

(e)

\psi^{\prime}/\Delta(\psi^{\prime})\lesssim\psi
.

Proof.

Estimate (a) follows from (
4.1
) combined with the formulas

\psi^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime}(\psi)}\,,\qquad\psi^{\prime\prime}=\frac{|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\psi)|}{(\varphi^{\prime}(\psi))^{3}}\,.

Suppose that estimate (b) does not hold, i.e., that there exists a function
{\varepsilon}(\tau)\to 0

as
\tau\to\infty
, such that

\limsup_{\tau\to\infty}\,\max\{|\varphi^{\prime}(t)/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)-1|\colon|t-\tau|\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau))\leq{\varepsilon}(\tau)\}>0.

Then, for some
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, there exist an arbitrarily large
\tau
and a

\vartheta
such that
|\varphi^{\prime}(\vartheta)/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)-1|=\delta
, while

|\varphi^{\prime}(t)/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)-1|<\delta
everywhere on the open interval
I
with endpoints
\tau
and
\vartheta
and with
|I|\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau))\leq{\varepsilon}(\tau)
.
Then,

\delta\lesssim\Bigl|\log\,\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\vartheta)}{\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)}\,\Bigr|\leq\int_{I}\,\Bigl|\,\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\,\Bigr|\leq|I|\cdot\max_{I}\Bigl|\,\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}{\varphi^{\prime}}\,\Bigr|\leq|I|\cdot\max_{I}\varphi^{\prime}\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}).

Since
\delta
was chosen less than
\tfrac{1}{2}
,
everywhere on
I
we have
\varphi^{\prime}\leq 2\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
. Hence,

\max_{I}\varphi^{\prime}\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime})\lesssim\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau))
, and therefore,

\delta\lesssim|I|\cdot\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau))\lesssim{\varepsilon}(\tau)\,,

arriving at a contradiction as
\tau\to\infty
, which proves (b).
The proof of estimate (c) follows the same pattern, so we skip it.

Estimate (d) easily follows from (b). Indeed, assume that (d) does not hold.
That is, there is a sequence
\tau_{j}\uparrow\infty
such that

\tau_{j}\varphi^{\prime}(\tau_{j})\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau_{j}))\to 0
. Consider the intervals

[0,\tau_{j}]
. By (b),
|\varphi^{\prime}(+0)/\varphi^{\prime}(\tau_{j})-1|\to 0
. Since
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau_{j})\to 0
,
we get
\varphi^{\prime}(+0)=0
, which is impossible since
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
monotonically decreases to
0

as
\tau
grows. At last, estimate (e) is just a restatement of (d).
∎

4.2.
The central group of terms of the power series
F_{\xi}

Let

F_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)a(n)z^{n},

with

a(n)=\displaystyle\exp\Bigl[-\int_{0}^{n}\varphi\Bigr]
, and let

\mu=\mu(R)=\max_{t\geq 0}\,\exp\Bigl[t\log R-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi\Bigr].

Then, for
R\geq 1
,

\log\mu(R)=\int_{0}^{\log R}\psi=\int_{1}^{R}\frac{\nu(r)}{r}\,{\rm d}r\,,

where
\nu=\nu(R)=\psi(\log R)
.
We also let
\sigma=\sigma(R)=\psi^{\prime}(\log R)
.
Set

\omega(t)=\omega_{R}(t)=t\log R-\int_{0}^{t}\varphi.

Then
a_{k}R^{k}=e^{\omega(k)}
,
\omega(\nu)=\log\mu
,

\omega^{\prime}(\nu)=0
, and
\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)=-\varphi^{\prime}(\nu)=-1/\sigma
.
We will need simple estimates for the function
\omega
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular.
Then, we have

(i)

\bigl|\omega(\nu+t)-\omega(\nu)-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)t^{2}\bigr|\lesssim\sigma^{-2}\Delta(\sigma)\,|t|^{3}
,
|t|\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
;

(ii)

\bigl|\omega(\nu+t)-\omega(\nu)\bigr|=(1+o(1))\sigma^{-1}\,t^{2}
,

|t|\simeq\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
;

(iii)

\bigl|\omega^{\prime}(\nu+t)\bigr|=(1+o(1))\sigma^{-1}\,|t|
,

|t|\simeq\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
.

Proof.

To prove (i), we note that, by Taylor’s formula,

\omega(\nu+t)-\omega(\nu)-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)t^{2}=\tfrac{1}{6}\,\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}(\nu+x)t^{3}=\tfrac{1}{6}\,|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\nu+x)|\,t^{3},

with some
x
,
|x|\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
. Therefore, applying first
estimate (
4.1
) and then
Lemma
4.1
(b), we see that the LHS of (i) is

\leq(\varphi^{\prime}(\nu+x))^{2}\Delta(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\nu+x))\,|t|^{3}\,\simeq\sigma^{-2}\Delta(\sigma)\,|t|^{3}\,,

which gives us (i).

Estimate (ii) follows from (i): for
|t|\simeq\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
, we have

|\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)|t^{2}\simeq\log\sigma
, while

\sigma^{-2}\Delta(\sigma)\,|t|^{3}=o(1)
.

To prove (iii), we again apply Taylor’s formula:

\omega^{\prime}(\nu+t)=\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu+x)\,t=|\varphi^{\prime}(\nu+x)|t

with some
x
,
|x|\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
. By Lemma
4.1
(b),
we get (iii).
∎

Next, we observe that, by
\Delta
-regularity of
\varphi
,
for large
R
, we have
\nu\gg\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}

(indeed, by Lemma
4.1
(e),

\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}\leq\nu\cdot\Delta(\sigma)\sqrt{\sigma^{-1}\log\sigma}=o(\nu)
).
We choose

N=A\,\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
with
A\gg 1
, and define the Weyl-type sum

W_{R}(\theta)=\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq N}\xi(k)e(k\theta)e^{-(k-\nu)^{2}/(2\sigma)}\,.

Proposition 2
.

Let
F_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)a(n)z^{n}

be an entire function with smooth coefficients
a
and with
bounded coefficients
\xi
. Suppose that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular.
Then, for
R\gg 1
and
A\gg 1
,

\max_{\theta\in[0,1]}\bigl|F_{\xi}(Re(\theta))-\mu W_{R}(\theta)\bigr|\lesssim\mu\,\Delta(\sigma)(\log\sigma)^{3/2}.

This proof of this Proposition is a simple application of the classical Laplace method.

Proof.

Recalling that
\mu=e^{\omega(\nu)}
and that
\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)=-1/\sigma
, we
have

\bigl|F_{\xi}(Re(\theta))-\mu W_{R}(\theta)\bigr|\lesssim\mu\Bigl(\sum_{0\leq k<\nu-N}+\sum_{k>\nu+N}\Bigr)\exp\bigl[\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)\bigr]\\
+\mu\,\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq N}\Bigl|\exp\bigl[\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)\bigr]-\exp\bigl[\,\frac{1}{2}\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)(k-\nu)^{2}\bigr]\Bigr|\,.

We claim that the first two sums on the RHS tend to zero as
R\to\infty
.

Indeed, for
0\leq k<\nu-N
, using concavity of
\omega
and Lemma
4.2
(ii) and (iii),
we get

\displaystyle\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)

\displaystyle=\omega(k)-\omega(\nu-N)+\omega(\nu-N)-\omega(\nu)

\displaystyle\leq-(\nu-N-k)|\omega^{\prime}(\nu-N)|-c_{1}\sigma^{-1}N^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-c_{2}(\nu-N-k)\sigma^{-1}N-c_{1}\sigma^{-1}N^{2}

\displaystyle=-c_{2}(\nu-N-k)A\,\sqrt{\sigma^{-1}\log\sigma}-c_{1}A^{2}\log\sigma\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k<\nu-N}e^{\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)}

\displaystyle<e^{-c_{1}A^{2}\log\sigma}\,\sum_{\ell\geq 0}e^{-c_{2}\ell A\,\sqrt{\sigma^{-1}\log\sigma}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma^{-c_{1}A^{2}}\cdot A^{-1}\,\sqrt{\sigma/\log\sigma}

\displaystyle=o(1)\,,

provided that
A
is large enough that
c_{1}A^{2}>\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The case
k>\nu+N
follows by almost the same argument and we skip it.

Finally, by Lemma
4.2
(i), for
|k-\nu|\leq N
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\exp\bigl[\omega(k)

\displaystyle-\omega(\nu)\bigr]-\exp\bigl[\frac{1}{2}\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)(k-\nu)^{2}\bigr]\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl[-(k-\nu)^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\,\Bigl|\exp\bigl[\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)-\frac{1}{2}\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)(k-\nu)^{2}\bigr]-1\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sigma^{-2}\Delta(\sigma)N^{3}\,\exp\bigl[-(k-\nu)^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]

\displaystyle=A^{3}\sigma^{-1/2}\Delta(\sigma)(\log\sigma)^{3/2}\,\exp\bigl[-(k-\nu)^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\,.

Hence,

\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq N}\Bigl|\exp\bigl[\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)\bigr]-\exp\bigl[\frac{1}{2}\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(\nu)(k-\nu)^{2}\bigr]\Bigr|\\
\lesssim\sigma^{-1/2}\Delta(\sigma)(\log\sigma)^{3/2}\,\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\exp\bigl[-\ell^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\lesssim\Delta(\sigma)(\log\sigma)^{3/2}\,,

proving Proposition
2
.
∎

The following modification of Proposition
2
will be used in the next section,
when we will deal with random independent coefficients
\xi
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
F_{\xi}
be an entire function with smooth coefficients
a
.
Suppose that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular and
that the sequence

\xi
has at most power growth:
|\xi(k)|\lesssim(k+1)^{B}
.
Then, for
R\gg 1
,

\bigl|F_{\xi}(R\,e(\theta))\bigr|\lesssim\nu^{B}\sigma^{1/2}\,\mu\,.

Proof.

As in the proof of the previous lemma, we write

|F_{\xi}(Re(\theta))|\leq\mu\,\Bigl(\sum_{0\leq k<v-N}+\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq N}+\sum_{k>\nu+N}\Bigr)|\xi(k)|\exp\bigl[\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)\bigr]\,.

Arguing as in the proof of that lemma, we see that the first sum on the RHS is bounded by
o(1)\nu^{B}
, while the second sum is

\lesssim\nu^{B}(\Delta(\sigma)(\log\sigma)^{3/2}+\sigma^{1/2})\lesssim\nu^{B}\sigma^{1/2}
.
At last, the third sum is

\lesssim\nu^{B}\,\sum_{k>\nu+N}\Bigl(\frac{k}{\nu}\Bigr)^{B}\,\exp\bigl[-c_{1}A^{2}\log\sigma-c_{2}(k-\nu-N)A\,\sqrt{\sigma^{-1}\log\sigma}\,\bigr]\\
\lesssim\nu^{B}\,\sigma^{-c_{1}A}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\max\{1,(\ell/\nu)^{B}\}\,e^{-c_{2}\ell A\,\sqrt{\sigma^{-1}\log\sigma}}=o(\nu^{B}),

proving the lemma.
∎

5.
IID sequences
\xi

Our first station is the case of IID sequences
\xi
. In this well-studied setting
the result on equidistribution of zeroes on local scales appears to be new.

5.1.
Equidistribution of zeroes on local scales

The main result of this section is the following

Theorem 1
.

Let
\xi
be a sequence of independent identically distributed complex-valued random
variables with a non-degenerate distribution satisfying the moment condition

(5.1)

\exists\delta>0,\quad\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\xi(0)|^{\delta}\bigr]<\infty,

and let
F_{\xi}(z)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)a(n)z^{n}

be a random entire function with smooth coefficients

a(n)=\displaystyle\exp\Bigl[-\int_{0}^{n}\varphi\Bigr]
.
Suppose that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular
and that

(5.2)

\varphi^{\prime}(t)\lesssim t^{-c}

with some
c>0
. Let
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log
, where
\psi=\varphi^{-1}

is the inverse function to
\varphi
.

Then, almost surely, the zeroes of
F_{\xi}
are

(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed with the gauge

(5.3)

\rho(R)=R\,\sqrt{\sigma^{-1}\log\sigma\,}.

Note that condition (
5.2
) is equivalent to the bound
\psi^{\prime}\gtrsim\psi^{c}
(i.e.,
to
\sigma\gtrsim\nu^{c}
). By convexity of
\psi
, this yields
\psi^{\prime}(s)\gtrsim s^{c}
, that is,

\sigma(R)\gtrsim(\log R)^{c}
.

Proof.

We will show that, a.s., the subharmonic functions
V(z)=\log\mu(|z|)
and

V_{1}(z)=\log|F_{\xi}(z)|
satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
1
, provided that
the radial gauge
\rho
is chosen according to (
5.3
) (note that
the function
\rho
defined by (
5.3
) satisfies
\rho^{\prime}(R)=o(1)
as
R\to\infty
, that is,
\rho
is indeed a radial gauge).

Recall that the Riesz measure
\gamma=(2\pi)^{-1}\,\Delta V
equals

{\rm d}\gamma=\sigma(R)R^{-1}{\rm d}R\otimes{\rm d}\theta
,
z=Re(\theta)
, so its
density
\Gamma
equals
(2\pi)^{-1}\sigma(R)R^{-2}
, and therefore,

\Gamma(R)\rho^{2}(R)=(2\pi)^{-1}\log\sigma(R)
satisfies condition (
3.1
).

The verification of condition (
3.2
) is also straightforward. We have

\frac{\Gamma(R)}{\Gamma(R^{\prime})}=\Bigl(\frac{R^{\prime}}{R}\Bigr)^{2}\cdot\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\log R)}{\psi^{\prime}(\log R^{\prime})}\,.

Both factors on the RHS tend to
1
uniformly in
|R^{\prime}-R|\leq\rho(R)
.
For the first factor this holds since
\rho(R)=o(R)
, while for the second factor
this follows from Lemma
4.1
(c).

The upper bound (
3.3
) follows from (
5.1
). Indeed,
the moment condition yields that

\mathbb{P}\bigl[|\xi(k)|\geq(k+1)^{2/\delta}\bigr]\leq(k+1)^{-2}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\xi(k)|^{\delta}\bigr]\leq C(k+1)^{-2},

whence, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, a.s., we have
|\xi(k)|\lesssim(k+1)^{2/\delta}
,

k\geq 0
. Then, by Proposition
3
,

\displaystyle\log|F_{\xi}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\log\mu(|z|)+O(\log\sigma(|z|))

\displaystyle=V(z)+O((\Gamma\rho^{2})(|z|))\,,

which gives us (
3.3
).

The proof of the lower bound (
3.4
) relies on a version of the Nguyen-Vu
anti-concentration estimate for trigonometric sums.

Proposition 4

(Nguyen–Vu)
.

Let

S(\theta)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\xi_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}e(\lambda\theta),\qquad\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z},\ |\Lambda|=n,

be a random trigonometric sum with complex-valued IID coefficients
\xi_{\lambda}

having a non-degenerate
distribution, and with non-random coefficients
(c_{\lambda})\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

|c_{\lambda}|\geq\kappa
. Then, for any
\alpha\geq 1
, there exists
\beta<\infty
such that,
for any interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
of length
|I|\gtrsim 1/n
, we have

\inf_{\theta\in I}\,\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S(\theta)-Z|<\kappa n^{-\beta}\bigr]\lesssim n^{-\alpha}\,.

The proof of this lemma is an almost verbatim repetition of the original one
[
31
, Lemma 9.2]
,
so we relegate it to Appendix B, proceeding with the proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix
R\geq 1
. Note that, by Lemma
4.2
(i),
for
|k-\nu|\leq\sqrt{\sigma}
, we have

|\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)|\lesssim 1
, whence

a_{k}R^{k}/\mu(R)=\exp\bigl[\omega(k)-\omega(\nu)\bigr]\simeq 1
,
so we can apply Proposition
4
with
c_{k}=a_{k}R^{k}/\mu(R)
.
Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since the random variables

F_{\xi}(Re(\theta))-\mu S(\theta)
and
\mu S(\theta)
are independent, we conclude
that there exists
\vartheta
so that

|\vartheta-\theta|\lesssim 1/\sqrt{\sigma}
, and

\mathbb{P}\bigl[|F_{\xi}(Re(\vartheta))|<\mu\sigma^{-\beta/2}\bigr]\lesssim\sigma^{-\alpha/2}

with
\alpha
to be chosen later, and
\beta=\beta(\alpha)
.
We take the sequence
(R_{j})
such that
R_{j+1}=R_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho(R_{j})
,
split the circle
R_{j}\mathbb{T}
into
\simeq\sqrt{\sigma(R_{j})}
arcs
R_{j}I_{j,k}
of the angular
size
|I_{j,k}|\simeq 1/\sqrt{\sigma(R_{j})}
, and denote by
\zeta_{j,k}
the centers of
these arcs. Then the union of the disks
D_{j,k}=\{|z-\zeta_{j,k}|\leq\rho(R_{j})\}
covers
the whole complex plane, except for a bounded set.
Rarefying the set
\{\zeta_{j,k}\}
(cf. Section
3.2
), we assume that the
disks
D_{j,k}
have bounded multiplicity of covering.

Consider the events

X_{j,k}=\bigl\{\max_{D_{j,k}}\log|F_{\xi}|\leq\log\mu(R_{j})-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta\log\sigma(R_{j})\bigr\}.

Our next step is to show the convergence of the series

(5.4)

\sum_{j,\,k}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl[X_{j,k}\bigr]<\infty\,.

By construction,
\mathbb{P}\bigl[X_{j,k}\bigr]\lesssim\sigma(|\zeta_{j,k}|)^{-\alpha/2}
,
that is,

\sum_{j,\,k}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl[X_{j,k}\bigr]\lesssim\sum_{j,\,k}\sigma(\zeta_{j,k})^{-\alpha/2}\,.

To conclude that the series on the RHS converges, we observe that

\iint_{D_{j,k}}\frac{{\rm d}m(z)}{|z|^{2}\log^{2}|z|}\gtrsim\frac{\rho^{2}(\zeta_{j,k})}{|\zeta_{j,k}|^{2}\log^{2}|\zeta_{j,k}|}\\
=\frac{\log\sigma(|\zeta_{j,k}|)}{\sigma(|\zeta_{j,k}|)}\cdot\frac{1}{\log^{2}|\zeta_{j,k}|}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:21}}}{{\gtrsim}}\,\sigma(|\zeta_{j,k}|)^{-\alpha/2}\,,

provided that
\alpha
was chosen sufficiently large. That is, condition (
5.4
) holds.
Then, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, a.s., only finitely many events
X_{j,k}
may occur, that is,
a.s., all but finitely many disks
D_{j,k}
contain a point
w_{j,k}
such that

\displaystyle\log|F_{\xi}(w_{j,k})|

\displaystyle\geq\log\mu(|w_{j,k}|)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\beta\log\sigma(|w_{j,k}|)

\displaystyle=V(w_{j,k})-O((\Gamma\rho^{2})(w_{j,k})).

It remains to take the set
W=(w_{j,k})
, to observe that the union of the
disks
\{|z-w_{j,k}|\leq 6\rho(w_{j,k})\}
covers all but finitely many of the disks

D_{j,k}
, so applying Proposition
1
with the radial gauge
6\rho
,
we complete the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

5.2.
A Gaussian example

In this section we will provide an example, which shows that the result of Theorem
1

cannot be essentially improved with respect to the size of the local scale. We consider the case
when
a(n)=1/\sqrt{n!}
and
\xi
is a sequence of independent standard complex-valued Gaussian random
variables, and let

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)\,\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\,.

This function is called the Gaussian Entire Function, GEF, for short.
It is distinguished from other Gaussian entire functions by the remarkable
distribution invariance of its zero set with respect to isometries of the plane,
see
[
13
, Ch. 2]
or
[
29
]
.

In this case, a straightforward computation shows that the function
\varphi
satisfies

\varphi(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\log t+O(t^{-1})
, and
\varphi^{\prime}(t)=(2t)^{-1}+O(t^{-2})
.
Then,

\psi(s)=e^{2s}+O(1)
, and
\psi^{\prime}(s)=2e^{2s}+O(1)
,
whence, for large
|z|
,

V(z)=\log\mu(|z|)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\log|z|}\psi=\tfrac{1}{2}\,|z|^{2}+O(\log|z|),

and
\sigma(R)=\psi^{\prime}(\log R)=2R^{2}+O(1)
.
Furthermore, the density of the Riesz measure of the function
V
equals

(2\pi)^{-1}\sigma(R)/R^{2}=\pi^{-1}+O(R^{-2})
.
Hence, by Theorem
1
, a.s.,
the number of zeroes of the GEF
F
in any disk of radius

r
and center
z
, with sufficiently large
|z|
and with
r\gtrsim\sqrt{\log|z|}
,
is close to
r^{2}
.

The following theorem shows that this local equidistribution breaks down at the scale
\log^{1/4}t
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
F
be a GEF, and let
D_{j}=D(j^{2},\kappa\log^{1/4}j)
,
j\geq 2
,
with a sufficiently small parameter
\kappa>0
.
Then a.s.
F
does not vanish on infinitely many disks from the collection
(D_{j})
.

Proof.

We will be using the asymptotic independence property of the zero set of the GEF
F
.
For
w\in\mathbb{C}
, set

T_{w}F(z)=F(z+w)e^{-z\bar{w}-\frac{1}{2}|w|^{2}}\,.

Let
r_{j}=\kappa\log^{1/4}j
. Then, by Lemma 5 in
[
30
]
, there exist independent GEFs

F_{j}
,
j\geq 2
, such that

T_{j^{2}}F=F_{j}+H_{j}
and

\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\sup_{D(0,r_{j})}|H_{j}(z)|e^{-|z|^{2}/2}\geq e^{-j^{2}}\Bigr]\leq 2e^{-j^{2}/2}.

By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, a.s., there exists
j_{0}
so that, for
j\geq j_{0}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{D(0,r_{j})}|H_{j}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Now we argue as in
[
38
, Section 1]
. Let

F_{j}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi_{j}(n)\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\,.

For every
j
, we consider the event

Y_{j}
that

|\xi_{j}(0)|\geq 2
,
|\xi_{j}(n)|\leq e^{-r_{j}^{2}}
for
1\leq n\leq 16r_{j}^{2}
,
and
|\xi_{j}(n)|\leq 2^{n}
for
n>16r_{j}^{2}
. Then

\mathbb{P}[Y_{j}]\geq Ce^{-cr_{j}^{4}}\gtrsim j^{-1}
,
provided that
\kappa
is such that
c\kappa^{4}\leq 1
. Denote by
J
the set of
j
s
such that the events
Y_{j}
occur.
Since
\sum_{j}\mathbb{P}[Y_{j}]=+\infty
, and the events
Y_{j}
are independent,
by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, a.s.,
\operatorname{card}J=\infty
.

Finally, if
j\in J
and
j\geq j_{1}
, then, by a straightforward computation
[
38
, Section 1]
,

\displaystyle\inf_{D(0,r_{j})}|F_{j}|\geq 1
,
and therefore, for
j\geq\max(j_{0},j_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{D(0,r_{j})}|T_{j^{2}}F(z)|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Thus,
F
does not vanish on
D_{j}
for
j\in J
,
j\geq\max(j_{0},j_{1})
,
and we are done.
∎

It might be interesting to construct similar examples for other distributions
of the IID sequence
\xi
(for instance, Rademacher or Steinhaus ones),
at least, for the same choice
a(n)=1/\sqrt{n!}
.

6.
Wiener sequences
\xi
with the Lebesgue spectral measure

In Proposition
5
we give a lower bound for the Weyl-type sum
W_{R}(\theta)
on
a sufficiently dense set of points
(R,\theta)
. The crucial role in this bound will be
played by a quantitative smallness of autocorrelations

\frac{1}{B-A}\sum_{A\leq k<B}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h),\qquad h\neq 0.

Combined
with Proposition
2
and Proposition
1
,
it will guarantee
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistribution
of zeroes of
F_{\xi}
with an appropriate radial gauge
\rho
(Theorem
3
).
We will demonstrate how this approach works for three different instances of Wiener sequences

\xi
with Lebesgue spectral measure: (i)
\xi(n)=e(\alpha n^{2})
with irrational non-Liouville
\alpha
, (ii) random multiplicative sequences, and (iii) the Golay–Rudin–Shapiro sequence.

6.1.
Auxiliary estimates

Here, we collect estimates of the function

V_{R}(k;h)
, which will be defined
momentarily. These estimates will be used in the proofs of Proposition
5

and Proposition
6
.

Throughout this section we assume that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular.
As above,
\nu=\nu(R)
and
\sigma=\sigma(R)
. Let
\beta=\beta(R)
be a small parameter
satisfying

\sigma^{-1/2}=o(\beta),\quad\beta\Delta(\sigma)=o(1)\,,

as
R\to\infty
. Set

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle=\int_{R}^{R(1+\beta)}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k-\nu(s))^{2}+(k+h-\nu(s))^{2}}{2\sigma(s)}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}\nu(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k-t)^{2}+(k+h-t)^{2}}{2\sigma(\nu^{-1}(t))}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}t\,.

A simple and useful observation is that the function
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log
stays
approximately constant on the integration interval
[R,R(1+\beta)]
:

Lemma 6.1
.

We have

(i)

\sigma(s)=(1+o(1))\sigma
everywhere on
[R,R(1+\beta)]
;

(ii)

\nu(R(1+\beta))-\nu(R)=(1+o(1))\beta\sigma
.

Proof.

Since
|\log(R(1+\beta))-\log R\,|\leq\beta=o(1/\Delta(\sigma))
,
relation (i) readily follows from Lemma
4.1
(c).
To prove (ii), we recall that
\nu^{\prime}(r)=\sigma(r)/r
. Hence,
(ii) follows from (i).
∎

The next lemma provides us with crude upper and lower bounds on
V_{R}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

We have

(i)

V_{R}(k;h)\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}
;

(ii)

V_{R}(k;0)\gtrsim\sqrt{\sigma}
,
provided that
\nu(R)\leq k\leq\nu(R(1+\beta))
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
(i),

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\bigl[-(1+o(1))\,(k-t)^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle<\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\bigl[-(1+o(1))u^{2}/{2\sigma}\bigr]\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}\,,

proving (i).

The proof of (ii) is also straightforward. By Lemma
6.1
(i),

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\bigl[-(1+o(1))(k-t)^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)-k}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-k}\exp\bigl[-(1+o(1))u^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\,{\rm d}u\,.

Observe that either
\nu(R)-k\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\,L
, or
\nu(R(1+\beta))-k\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\,L
,
where
L=\nu(R(1+\beta))-\nu(R)
. By Lemma
6.1
(ii),
L=(1+o(1))\beta\sigma
, which is

\gg\sqrt{\sigma}
. Thus,

\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}\,L}\exp\bigl[-(1+o(1))u^{2}/(2\sigma)\bigr]\,{\rm d}u\gtrsim\sqrt{\sigma}\,,

proving the lemma.
∎

The next lemma gives us more accurate upper bounds.

Lemma 6.3
.

(i)

V_{R}(k;h)\lesssim e^{-ch^{2}/\sigma}\sqrt{\sigma}
;

(ii)

V_{R}(k;h)\lesssim\begin{cases}e^{-c(\nu(R)-k)^{2}/\sigma}\sqrt{\sigma},\ &k<\nu(R),\\
e^{-c(k-\nu(R(1+\beta)))^{2}/\sigma}\sqrt{\sigma},\ &k>\nu(R(1+\beta))\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)-k-\frac{h}{2}}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-k-\frac{h}{2}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1+o(1)}{2\sigma}\,\bigl((u-\tfrac{1}{2}h)^{2}+(u+\tfrac{1}{2}h)^{2}\bigr)\Bigr]\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)-k-\frac{h}{2}}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-k-\frac{h}{2}}\exp\bigl[-(1+o(1))(u^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}h^{2})/\sigma\bigr]\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle<e^{-(1+o(1))h^{2}/(4\sigma)}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-(1+o(1))u^{2}/\sigma}\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-ch^{2}/\sigma}\,\sqrt{\sigma}\,.

To prove the second estimate, we note that, for
k<\nu(R)
, we have

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle<\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1+o(1)}{2\sigma}\,(t-k)^{2}\Bigr]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle<\int_{\nu(R)-k}^{\infty}e^{-(1+o(1))u^{2}/(2\sigma)}\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle<e^{-(1+o(1))(\nu(R)-k)^{2}/(2\sigma)}\sqrt{\sigma}\,.

The case
k>\nu(R(1+\beta))
is very similar and we skip it.
∎

The next lemma estimates the oscillation of the function
k\mapsto V_{R}(k;h)
along
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|V_{R}(k;h)-V_{R}(k-1;h)|\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Set
m_{1}=\nu(R)-A\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
,
m_{2}=\nu(R(1+\beta))+A\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}

with sufficiently large
A
. By Lemma
6.3
(ii), the sums over
k<m_{1}
and

k>m_{2}

are
o(1)
if the constant
A
was chosen big enough. Hence, we need to show that

\sum_{m_{1}\leq k\leq m_{2}}|V_{R}(k;h)-V_{R}(k-1;h)|\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}\,.

We will represent the function
V_{R}(k;h)
as a difference of two increasing
functions in
k
. Recalling that
\sigma(\nu^{-1}(t))=1/\varphi^{\prime}(t))
,
we get

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\Bigl[\,-\frac{(k-t)^{2}+(k+h-t)^{2}}{2\sigma(\nu^{-1}(t))}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\bigl[\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varphi^{\prime}(t)((k-t)^{2}+(k+h-t)^{2})\,\bigr]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)-k-\frac{1}{2}h}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-k-\frac{1}{2}h}\exp\bigl[\,-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varphi^{\prime}(x+k+\tfrac{1}{2}\,h)(x^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\,h^{2})\,\bigr]\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\,\int_{\nu(R)-k-\frac{1}{2}h}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-m_{1}-\frac{h}{2}}-\int_{\nu(R(1+\beta))-k-\frac{1}{2}h}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-m_{1}-\frac{h}{2}}\,\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\exp\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\varphi^{\prime}(x+k+\tfrac{1}{2}\,h)(x^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\,h^{2})\,\bigr]\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=V_{1,R}(k;h)-V_{2,R}(k;h).

First, observe that both functions
V_{1,R}
and
V_{2,R}
have uniform upper bounds

(6.1)

V_{1,R},V_{2,R}\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}\,.

Indeed, in the integration range,
\nu(R)\leq x+k+\tfrac{1}{2}\,h\leq\nu(R(1+\beta))+(m_{2}-m_{1})=\nu+O(\beta\sigma)
. Since
\beta\sigma\Delta(\sigma)\varphi^{\prime}(\nu)=\beta\Delta(\sigma)=o(1)
, by Lemma
4.1
(b), in this range,

\varphi^{\prime}(x+k+\tfrac{1}{2}\,h)=(1+o(1))\varphi^{\prime}(\nu)=(1+o(1))\sigma^{-1}
,
which yields (
6.1
).

Next, we observe that since the function
t\mapsto\varphi^{\prime}(t)
decreases,
the functions
V_{1,R}
and
V_{2,R}
are increasing functions in
k
.

The rest is straightforward:

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{1}\leq k\leq m_{2}}|V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle-V_{R}(k-1;h)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq 2}\,\sum_{m_{1}\leq k\leq m_{2}}|V_{j,R}(k;h)-V_{j,R}(k-1;h)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j\leq 2}\,\sum_{m_{1}\leq k\leq m_{2}}\bigl[V_{j,R}(k-1;h)-V_{j,R}(k;h)\bigr]

\displaystyle<\sum_{1\leq j\leq 2}V_{j,R}(m_{1};h)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}\,,

proving the lemma.
∎

Set

V_{R}(h)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}V_{R}(k;h)\,.

Lemma 6.5
.

We have

V_{R}(0)\simeq\beta\sigma^{3/2}\,.

Proof.

To prove the lower bound, we write

V_{R}(0)\geq\sum_{\nu(R)\leq k\leq\nu(R(1+\beta))}V_{R}(k;0)

and note that by Lemma
6.1
(ii) and Lemma
6.2
,
the RHS is

\simeq\sqrt{\sigma}\bigl[\nu(R(1+\beta))-\nu(R)\bigr]\simeq\beta\sigma^{3/2}.

To prove the upper bound, we split the sum into three parts:

V_{R}(0)=\Bigl(\sum_{k<\nu(R)}\ +\sum_{\nu(R)\leq k\leq\nu(R(1+\beta))}+\sum_{k>\nu(R(1+\beta))}\,\Bigr)V_{R}(k;0)\,.

By Lemma
6.3
(ii), the first and the third sums are
O(\sigma)=o(\beta\sigma^{3/2})
,
while, by the above, the middle sum is
\simeq\beta\sigma^{3/2}
.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

For
h^{2}\lesssim\sigma
, we have

|V_{R}(h)-V_{R}(0)|\lesssim(1+h^{2}\beta)\sqrt{\sigma}\,.

Proof.

First, we observe that

\Bigl|V_{R}(h)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{R}(\kappa;h)\,{\rm d}\kappa\Bigr|\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}\,.

Indeed, as in the proof of Lemma
6.4
, we set

m_{1}=\nu(R)-A\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
,
m_{2}=\nu(R(1+\beta))+A\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}

with sufficiently large
A
. Then, by Lemma
6.3
(ii),

V_{R}(h)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{R}(\kappa;h)\,{\rm d}\kappa=\sum_{m_{1}\leq k\leq m_{2}}\Bigl[V_{R}(k;h)-\int_{k}^{k+1}V_{R}(\kappa;h)\,{\rm d}\kappa\Bigr]+o(1)\,,

provided that the constant
A
was chosen sufficiently big. It remains to recall that,
as we have shown in the proof of Lemma
6.4
, the total variation of the function

k\mapsto V_{R}(k;h)
on
[m_{1},m_{2}]
is
\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}
.

Thus, we need to bound the difference of the integrals

\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{R}(\kappa;h)\,{\rm d}\kappa-\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{R}(\kappa;0)\,{\rm d}\kappa\Bigr|\leq\int_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\bigl|V_{R}(\kappa;h)-V_{R}(\kappa-\tfrac{1}{2}\,h;0)\bigr|\,{\rm d}\kappa+o(1)\,.

Estimating the integrand on the RHS, we get

\displaystyle\bigl|

\displaystyle V_{R}(\kappa;h)-V_{R}(\kappa-\tfrac{1}{2}\,h;0)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{\nu(R)-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\,h}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\,h}\bigl[\exp\bigl(-\varphi^{\prime}(u+\kappa+\tfrac{1}{2}\,h)(u^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\,h^{2})\,\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\exp\bigl(-\varphi^{\prime}(u+\kappa+\tfrac{1}{2}\,h)u^{2}\bigr]\,{\rm d}u\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\nu(R)-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\,h}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\,h}\exp\bigl(-(1+o(1))u^{2}/\sigma\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\bigl|\exp\bigl(-(1+o(1))h^{2}/(4\sigma)\bigr)-1\bigr|\,{\rm d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim(h^{2}/\sigma)\cdot\sqrt{\sigma}=h^{2}/\sqrt{\sigma}\,,

Thus,

\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{R}(\kappa;h)\,{\rm d}\kappa-\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{R}(\kappa;0)\,{\rm d}\kappa\Bigr|\lesssim(m_{2}-m_{1})h^{2}/\sqrt{\sigma}+o(1)\\
\lesssim(\beta\sigma+\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma})h^{2}/\sqrt{\sigma}\lesssim h^{2}\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\,,

completing the proof.
∎

6.2.
Lower bound for Weyl-type sums
W_{R}

Throughout this section we assume that the function
\varphi
is
\Delta
-regular.
As above,
\nu=\nu(R)
and
\sigma=\sigma(R)
. Let
\beta=\beta(R)
be a small parameter
satisfying

(6.2)

\sqrt{\frac{\log\sigma}{\sigma}}\ll\beta,\quad\beta\Delta(\sigma)=o(1)

(later, in applications, we set
\rho(R)=R\beta(R)
).
We aim to estimate from below the exponential sum

W_{R}(\theta)=\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq N}\xi(k)e(k\theta)e^{(k-\nu)^{2}/(2\sigma)}\,,\qquad N\gg\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}\,,

on a sufficiently dense set of points
(R,\theta)
.

For
M_{1}<\nu(R)
and
M_{2}>\nu(R(1+\beta))
, we set

S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)=\max_{M_{1}\leq k\leq M_{2}}\,\Bigl|\sum_{k\leq s\leq M_{2}}\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+h)\Bigr|\,.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose that

(6.3)

\sum_{\nu\leq k\leq\nu+\frac{1}{2}\beta\sigma}\,|\xi(k)|^{2}\gtrsim\beta\sigma\,,

and that, for some
p>1
,

(6.4)

\sum_{h\geq 1}(1+\beta h)^{-p}S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)\ll\beta\sigma,

with

M_{1}=\nu(R)-\beta\sigma,\quad M_{2}=\nu(R(1+\beta))+\beta\sigma\,.

Then, for every
\vartheta\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
, there exist

R^{\prime}\in[R,R(1+\beta)],\quad\theta^{\prime}\in(\vartheta-\beta,\vartheta+\beta),

such that

|W_{R^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})|\gtrsim\sigma^{1/4}\,.

Note that, by Lemma
6.1
(ii),

M_{2}-M_{1}\lesssim\beta\sigma
, and therefore, we have

S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)\lesssim\beta\sigma
. Hence,
for
1<q<p/(p-1)
, the terms in the sum on the LHS of (
6.4
) with
h\geq\beta^{-q}
can be discarded.
Lemma
6.3
(i) implies that
the terms with
h\gg\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
can be discarded as well.

Furthermore, the
\Delta
-regularity of
\varphi
and our conditions (
6.2
) on
\beta
yield that
M_{1},M_{2}\simeq\nu(R)
.

Proof.

To simplify our notation, we extend the sequence
\xi
, letting
\xi
equal
0

on negative integers, and set

\widetilde{W}_{R}(\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi(k)e(k\theta)\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k-\nu)^{2}}{2\sigma}\Bigr].

It’s easy to see that, for
N=A\,\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}
with sufficiently large positive

A
(used in the definition of the sum
W_{R}
), we have

\sum_{|k-\nu|\geq N}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k-\nu)^{2}}{2\sigma}\Bigr]\lesssim\int_{N}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/(2\sigma)}\,{\rm d}x=o(1),

so that, in order to prove Proposition
5
, it will be enough to estimate the sum

\widetilde{W}_{R}
, rather than
W_{R}
, from below.

We fix a non-negative function
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
\operatorname{supp}(g)\subset(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})
, and
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g=1
, fix
\vartheta\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
,
and set

\widetilde{X}=\int_{R}^{R(1+\beta)}\,\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|\widetilde{W}_{s}(\theta)|^{2}\,g(\beta^{-1}(\vartheta-\theta))\,{\rm d}\theta\,{\rm d}\nu(s)\,.

By Lemma
6.1
(ii),
\nu(R(1+\beta))-\nu(R)=(1+o(1))\beta\sigma
, so, to prove Proposition
5
,
we need to show that
\widetilde{X}\gtrsim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}
.

First, we rewrite
\widetilde{X}
as a Fourier series

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}

\displaystyle=\int_{R}^{R(1+\beta)}\,\underset{k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}{\sum\,\sum}\,\xi(k_{1})\bar{\xi}(k_{2})\,\Bigl[\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e((k_{1}-k_{2})\theta)g(\beta^{-1}(\vartheta-\theta))\,{\rm d}\theta\Bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k_{1}-\nu(s))^{2}+(k_{2}-\nu(s))^{2}}{2\sigma(s)}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}\nu(s)

\displaystyle=\beta\,\int_{R}^{R(1+\beta)}\,\underset{k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}{\sum\,\sum}\,e((k_{1}-k_{2})\vartheta)\widehat{g}(\beta(k_{2}-k_{1}))\,\xi(k_{1})\bar{\xi}(k_{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k_{1}-\nu(s))^{2}+(k_{2}-\nu(s))^{2}}{2\sigma(s)}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}\nu(s)

\displaystyle=\beta\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}(\beta h)e(-h\vartheta)\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)V_{R}(k;h)\,,

where

\displaystyle V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle=\int_{R}^{R(1+\beta)}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k-\nu(s))^{2}+(k+h-\nu(s))^{2}}{2\sigma(s)}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}\nu(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\nu(R)}^{\nu(R(1+\beta))}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(k-t)^{2}+(k+h-t)^{2}}{2\sigma(\nu^{-1}(t))}\,\Bigr]\,{\rm d}t\,.

Then, we apply a usual strategy: in order to estimate the sum

\widetilde{X}=\beta\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}(\beta h)e(-h\vartheta)\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)V_{R}(k;h)\,,

from below, we split it into two parts, estimate the diagonal terms (
h=0
) from below,
and the non-diagonal terms (
|h|\geq 1
) from above:

\displaystyle|\widetilde{X}|

\displaystyle\geq\beta\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\xi(k)|^{2}V_{R}(k;0)-\beta\sum_{|h|\geq 1}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\,\Bigl|\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)V_{R}(k;h)\,\Bigr|

\displaystyle={\rm DT}-{\rm NDT}\,.

By Lemma
6.2
(ii),

{\rm DT}\gtrsim\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\,\sum_{\nu\leq k\leq\nu+\frac{1}{2}\,\beta\sigma}|\xi(k)|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:condition1}}}{{\gtrsim}}\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}\,,

so it remains to show that the non-diagonal terms are
\ll\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}
.

Next, we cut the non-diagonal sums. Recalling that the Fourier transform
\widehat{g}(\lambda)

decays faster than any negative power of
\lambda
, and using Lemma
6.3
, given
p>1
,
we get

{\rm NDT}\lesssim\beta\sum_{1\leq h\leq A\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}}\frac{1}{(1+\beta h)^{p}}\,\Bigl|\,\sum_{M_{1}\leq k\leq M_{2}}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)V_{R}(k;h)\,\Bigr|+O(1)\,.

Applying first summation by parts and then Lemma
6.2
(i) and Lemma
6.4
,
we estimate the inner sum by

\displaystyle\Bigl|

\displaystyle\sum_{M_{1}\leq k\leq M_{2}}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)V_{R}(k;h)\,\Bigr|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Bigl(V_{R}(M_{1};h)+\sum_{M_{1}<k\leq M_{2}}|V_{R}(k;h)-V_{R}(k-1;h)|\Bigr)\cdot S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)\,,

where, as before,

S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)=\max_{M_{1}\leq k\leq M_{2}}\Bigl|\,\sum_{k\leq s\leq M_{2}}\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+h)\,\Bigr|\,.

Hence,

{\rm NDT}\lesssim\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\,\sum_{1\leq h\leq A\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}}(1+\beta h)^{-p}\,S^{*}(M_{1},M_{2};h)+O(1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:condition2}}}{{\ll}}\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}\,,

completing the proof of Proposition
5
.
∎

6.5.
Random multiplicative and completely multiplicative sequences

Denote by
\mathsf{P}
the set of primes.
Let

be a sequence of independent identically distributed

Consider two random multiplicative functions:

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\prod_{p|n}X_{p},&n{\rm\ is\ square-free},\\

\displaystyle=\prod_{p^{a}||n}X^{a}_{p}.

\xi_{1}

the function
\xi_{2}
is completely multiplicative.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\xi
be a random multiplicative sequence
\xi_{1}
or
\xi_{2}
.
Let
F_{\xi}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)a(n)z^{n}

\Delta

\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log

\psi
is the function inverse to

.
Then, almost surely,

(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed with the radial gauge

The proof will use the following estimate for the binary correlations of

which improves Lemma 9 in
[
1

Lemma 6.7
.

Let

,
b>0

\mathbb{E}\,\Bigl[\,\Bigl|\,\sum_{x\leq k<(1+\eta)x}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)\,\Bigr|^{2}\,\Bigr]\lesssim\eta x^{1+b},

and

Proof.

Let
h>0

We have

Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\,\Bigl[\,\Bigl|\sum_{x\leq k<(1+\eta)x}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr]\\
=\sumsum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in[x,(1+\eta)x]}\,\mathbb{E}\,\bigl[\xi(n_{1})\bar{\xi}(n_{1}+h)\bar{\xi}(n_{2})\xi(n_{2}+h)\bigr].

Observe that if

(6.8)

\mathbb{E}\,\bigl[\xi(n_{1})\bar{\xi}(n_{1}+h)\bar{\xi}(n_{2})\xi(n_{2}+h)\bigr]\not=0,

Denote

d_{2}=\gcd(n_{2},n_{2}+h)

,

Since
d_{1}
and
d_{2}

\tau
is the

and

Case 1:
\xi=\xi_{1}

Therefore, for every

and, hence,

Case 2:
\xi=\xi_{2}
.
Let
e_{1}^{2},f_{1}^{2},e_{2}^{2},f_{2}^{2}

,

\frac{k_{1}(k_{1}+\frac{h}{d_{1}})}{e_{1}^{2}f_{1}^{2}}=\frac{k_{2}(k_{2}+\frac{h}{d_{2}})}{e_{2}^{2}f_{2}^{2}}.

First, the left hand side of (

and

verifying the equations

For fixed

is
\lesssim_{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}
.
Given

Throughout this section we assume that

(7.1)

\Bigl|\,\frac{1}{X}\,\sum_{0\leq s<X}\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\Bigr|\lesssim{\varepsilon}_{1}(X;h),\qquad 0\leq h\leq H=H(X),

with
X\mapsto{\varepsilon}_{1}(X;h)
decreasing to
0
and
X\mapsto H(X)
increasing to
\infty
,
as
X\to\infty
, and that

(7.2)

\inf\bigl\{\chi(J)\colon J\subset[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\,]{\rm\ an\ interval},|J|=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta\bigr\}\gtrsim{\varepsilon}_{2}(\eta),

with a positive non-decreasing function
{\varepsilon}_{2}
.
The first condition quantifies the fact that
\chi
is a spectral measure
of the Wiener sequence
\xi
, while the second condition is a quantitative version
of the statement that
\chi
has no gaps in its support.

In Proposition
6
we provide a set of conditions which will yield a lower
bound on the Weyl-type sum
W_{R}(\theta)
on a sufficiently dense set of points

(R,\theta)
. Then, we combine Proposition
6
with Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
and show (in Theorem
7
) that these
conditions guarantee equidistribution of zeroes of
F_{\xi}
on appropriate local scales.
This set of conditions looks somewhat cumbersome, but then, to demonstrate how neatly it works,
we consider two instances of Wiener sequences
\xi
with singular spectral measures
having no gaps in their support: the indicator-function of the square-free integers
and the Thue–Morse sequence.

7.1.
Another lower bound for Weyl-type sums

Denote by
\mathcal{G}
the class of non-negative test-functions
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\operatorname{supp}(g)\subset\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigr)
,
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g=1
, and

g=1
on
\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{1}{4}\,\bigr]
.
As before, we assume that
\varphi
is a
\Delta
-regular function, and
that
\nu=\psi\circ\log
,
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log
, where
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
is the inverse
function.

Proposition 6
.

Let
R\gg 1
. Suppose that there exist
q>1
,
\beta=\beta(R)\ll 1/\Delta(\sigma)
,
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, satisfying the following set of conditions:

(a)

\beta^{-q}\leq\min\bigl\{\sqrt{\sigma},H(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu)\bigr\}
;

(b)

\beta^{-(1+2q)}\ll\sigma{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
;

(c)

\displaystyle\nu\sum_{0\leq h\leq\beta^{-q}}{\varepsilon}_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu;h)\ll\beta\sigma{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
;

(d)

\displaystyle\sum_{h>\beta^{-q}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\ll{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
.

Then, for every
\vartheta\in\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bigr]
, there exist

R^{\prime}\in[R,R(1+\beta)],\quad\theta^{\prime}\in(\vartheta-\beta,\vartheta+\beta),

such that

|W_{R^{\prime}}(\theta^{\prime})|\gtrsim\sigma^{1/4}\,\sqrt{{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)}\,.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
5
, we estimate from below the average

\widetilde{X}=\int_{R}^{R(1+\beta)}\,\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|\widetilde{W}_{s}(\theta)|^{2}\,g(\beta^{-1}(\vartheta-\theta))\,{\rm d}\theta\,{\rm d}\nu(s)\,,

where

\widetilde{W}_{R}(\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi(k)e(k\theta)\exp\bigl[\,\frac{(k-\nu)^{2}}{2\sigma}\,\bigr]\,.

By Lemma
6.1
(ii),
\nu(R(1+\beta))-\nu(R)=(1+o(1))\beta\sigma
, so, to prove Proposition
6
we need to show that

\widetilde{X}\gtrsim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
.
As before, we rewrite
\widetilde{X}
as a Fourier series

\widetilde{X}=\beta\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}(\beta h)e(-h\vartheta)\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)V_{R}(k;h)\,.

Recalling the notation

\displaystyle V_{R}(h)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}V_{R}(k;h)
, we split the RHS into three parts:

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}

\displaystyle=\beta V_{R}(0)\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}(\beta h)\widehat{\chi}(h)e(-h\vartheta)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}(\beta h)e(-h\vartheta)\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}(\beta h)\widehat{\chi}(h)e(-h\vartheta)\,(V_{R}(h)-V_{R}(0))

\displaystyle=I+II+III\,.

We will show that
I\gtrsim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
, while
the terms
|II|
and
|III|
are
\ll\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
.

Lower bound of
I
:
We set
g_{\beta}(\theta)=\beta^{-1}g(\beta^{-1}\theta)
, and denote by

(\chi*g_{\beta})^{\prime}
the density of the convolution of
\chi
with

g_{\beta}
. Then,

I=\beta V_{R}(0)(\chi*g_{\beta})^{\prime}(-\vartheta).

By Lemma
6.5
,
V_{R}(0)\gtrsim\beta\sigma^{3/2}
. Since
g_{\beta}=\beta^{-1}
on

\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{4}\,\beta,\tfrac{1}{4}\,\beta\bigr]
, we have

(\chi*g_{\beta})^{\prime}(-\vartheta)\gtrsim\chi\bigl[-\vartheta-\tfrac{1}{4}\,\beta,-\vartheta+\tfrac{1}{4}\,\beta\bigr]\gtrsim{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta).

Thus,
I\gtrsim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
.

Upper bound of
II
:
Recalling that
\beta\sigma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\gg}}\sqrt{\sigma\log\sigma}

and using Lemma
6.3
(ii), we cut the sum in
k
, getting

\displaystyle|II|

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta\,\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot\bigl|\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\,\bigr|+o(1)

\displaystyle=\beta\,\bigl(\,\sum_{|h|\leq\beta^{-q}}+\sum_{|h|>\beta^{-q}}\,\bigr)\,|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\bigl|\,\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\,\bigr|+o(1)\,.

For
|h|>\beta^{-q}
, using the crude estimate

\bigl|\,\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\,\bigr|\lesssim\beta\sigma\cdot\sqrt{\sigma}=\beta\sigma^{3/2}\,,

we get

\beta\,\sum_{|h|>\beta^{-q}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot\bigl|\,\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\,\bigr|\\
\lesssim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}\sum_{|h|>\beta^{-q}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\,,

which is
\ll\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
by assumption (d).

Now, we consider the sum over
|h|\leq\beta^{-q}
.
Applying summation by parts and using Lemma
6.4
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|

\displaystyle\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl(\max_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}V_{R}(k;h)+\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}|V_{R}(k;h)-V_{R}(k-1;h)|\,\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\max_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta}\,\bigl|\,\sum_{k\leq s\leq\nu+2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)\,\bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}\,\max_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta}\,\bigl|\,\sum_{k\leq s\leq\nu+2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)\,\bigr|\,.

First, we assume that
0\leq h\leq\beta^{-q}
. Then, by estimate (
7.1
),
the maximum on the RHS is
\lesssim(\nu+2\beta\sigma){\varepsilon}_{1}(\nu-2\beta\sigma;h)
.
Since
\beta\sigma\ll\sigma/\Delta(\sigma)\lesssim\nu
, the latter expression is

\lesssim\,\nu{\varepsilon}_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu;h)
. The application of estimate (
7.1
) was legal since,

\beta^{-q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}H(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta\sigma\ll\nu}}{{\leq}}\,H(\nu-2\beta\sigma)
.
Thus,

\beta\,\sum_{0\leq h\leq\beta^{-q}}\,|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot\bigl|\,\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\,\bigr|\\
\lesssim\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\cdot\nu\,\sum_{0\leq h\leq\beta^{-q}}{\varepsilon}_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu,h)\,,

which is
\ll\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
by assumption (c).

The sum over
-\beta^{-q}\leq h\leq-1
needs only a minor modification.
In this case we have

\displaystyle\bigl|\,\sum_{k\leq s\leq\nu+2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+h)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)\,\bigr|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\,\sum_{k-|h|\leq s\leq\nu+2\beta\sigma-|h|}\,\bigl(\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+|h|)-\widehat{\chi}(|h|)\bigr)\,\bigr|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\,\sum_{k\leq s\leq\nu+2\beta\sigma}\,\bigl(\xi(s)\bar{\xi}(s+|h|)-\widehat{\chi}(|h|)\bigr)\,\bigr|+O(|h|)\,.

Therefore,

\beta\,\sum_{-\beta^{-q}\leq h\leq-1}\,|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot\bigl|\,\sum_{|k-\nu|\leq 2\beta\sigma}\bigl(\xi(k)\bar{\xi}(k+h)-\widehat{\chi}(h)\bigr)V_{R}(k;h)\,\bigr|\\
\lesssim\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\bigl(\,\nu\,\sum_{0\leq h^{\prime}\leq\beta^{-q}}{\varepsilon}_{1}(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu,h^{\prime})+\beta^{-2q}\,\bigr)\,,

and, by assumptions (c) and (b), both terms on the RHS are
\ll\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)
.

Upper bound of
III
:
We have

|III|\lesssim\beta\,\bigl(\,\sum_{|h|\leq\sqrt{\sigma}}+\sum_{|h|>\sqrt{\sigma}}\,\bigr)|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot|(V_{R}(h)-V_{R}(0))|\,.

To estimate the first sum, we apply Lemma
6.6
and use that the Fourier transform of
g
decays faster than any negative power. We get

\beta\,\sum_{|h|\leq\sqrt{\sigma}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot|(V_{R}(h)-V_{R}(0))|\lesssim\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\,\sum_{|h|\leq\sqrt{\sigma}}\,|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|(1+h^{2}\beta)\\
\lesssim\beta\sqrt{\sigma}\cdot\beta^{-2}=\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}}{\beta}\,.

To estimate the second sum, we use the crude bound
V_{R}(h)\lesssim\beta\sigma^{3/2}
,
which follows from Lemma
6.2
(i) and Lemma
6.3
(ii).
Using again that the Fourier transform of
g
decays faster than any negative power, we get

\beta\,\sum_{|h|>\sqrt{\sigma}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\cdot|(V_{R}(h)-V_{R}(0))|\lesssim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}\,\sum_{|h|>\sqrt{\sigma}}|\widehat{g}(\beta h)|\\
\lesssim\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}\,\sum_{\ell\geq\beta\sqrt{\sigma}}\frac{1}{\ell^{3}}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}}{\beta}\,.

It remains to recall that
condition (b) guarantees that

\beta^{-1}\sqrt{\sigma}\ll\beta^{2}\sigma^{3/2}\,{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta).

This completes the proof of Proposition
6
.
∎

7.3.
The indicator-function of square-free integers

Here, we consider
\xi(n)=\mu^{2}(n)
, where
\mu
is the Möbius function.
The main result of this section is Theorem
8
below.
The key ingredient in its proof is Mirsky’s classical estimate for binary correlations.
Set

D=\prod_{p}\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{p^{2}}\Bigr)\,.

Lemma 7.1

(Mirsky
[
25
]
)
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\Bigl|\sum_{0\leq k\leq x}\mu^{2}(k)\mu^{2}(k+h)-D(h)x\Bigr|\leq C_{\varepsilon}x^{2/3+\varepsilon},\qquad 0\leq h\leq x,

where
D(0)=6\pi^{-2}
, and for
h\neq 0
,

D(h)=D\,\prod_{p^{2}|h}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\Bigr)\,.

The only difference with Mirsky’s result is he did not specify the dependence of
the constant on the shift
h
. For the reader’s convenience, we give the
proof in Appendix C. We will follow Mirsky’s work very closely.

Having Lemma
7.1
at hand, it is not difficult to compute the spectral measure
\chi

of the sequence
\mu^{2}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

The spectral measure of the sequence
\mu^{2}
equals

\chi=D\,\sum_{\mu^{2}(d)=1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\prod_{p|d}\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\Bigr)\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}\delta_{e(j/d^{2})}.

Since the proof is only a few lines, we give it here:

Proof.

We need to check that
\widehat{\chi}(h)=D(h)
. For
h=0
this is obvious. For
h\geq 1
, we have

\widehat{\chi}(h)=D\,\sum_{\mu^{2}(d)=1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\prod_{p|d}\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\Bigr)\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}e(jh/d^{2}).

Since

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}e(jk/m)=\begin{cases}m,\qquad m\,|\,k,\\
0,\qquad m{\not|}\,k,\end{cases}

we obtain that

\widehat{\chi}(h)=D\,\sum_{\mu^{2}(d)=1,\,d^{2}|h}\ \prod_{p|d}\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}=D\,\prod_{p^{2}|h}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\Bigr)=D(h),

completing the proof.
∎

The next lemma tells us how thin the measure
\chi
can be, i.e, how
estimate (
7.2
) looks in this case:

Lemma 7.3
.

For any interval
I\subset\bigl[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
,
we have
\chi(I)\gtrsim|I|^{3/2}
.

Proof.

We have

\chi(I)\gtrsim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{2}(d)=1,\\
d^{2}|I|>1\end{subarray}}\,\Bigl(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\prod_{p|d}\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\Bigr)d^{2}|I|\\
=|I|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{2}(d)=1,\\
d^{2}|I|>1\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{p|d}\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\gtrsim|I|\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{2}(d)=1,\\
d^{2}|I|>1\end{subarray}}\,\frac{1}{d^{2}}\gtrsim|I|\,\sum_{\ell>|I|^{-1}}\,\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\gtrsim|I|^{3/2},

where in the inequality before last, we used that the square-free numbers have positive density.
∎

Combining Theorem
7
with Lemma
7.1
and Lemma
7.3
, we arrive at

Theorem 8
.

Let

F(z)=\sum_{\mu^{2}(n)=1}a(n)z^{n}

be an entire function with smooth coefficients

a(n)=\displaystyle\exp\Bigl[-\int_{0}^{n}\varphi\Bigr]
with a
\Delta
-regular function

\varphi
, such that, for every
{\varepsilon}>0
,
\varphi^{\prime}(t)=o(t^{-1+{\varepsilon}})
as
t\to\infty
.
Let
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log
, where
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
is the inverse function to
\varphi
.
Then the zero set of
F
is
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed with
\rho=R\sigma^{-c}
, provided that
0<c<\tfrac{2}{21}
and
\Delta(s)=o(s^{c})
as
s\to\infty
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.1
, condition (
7.1
) holds with

{\varepsilon}_{1}(X)=X^{-\frac{1}{3}+{\varepsilon}}
and
H(X)=X
.
By Lemma
7.3
,
condition (
7.2
) holds with
{\varepsilon}_{2}(\beta)=\beta^{\frac{3}{2}}
.
We take
\beta=\sigma^{-c}
and verify that, for
c<\tfrac{2}{21}
,
the assumptions of Theorem
7
hold, provided that
q>1
is
chosen sufficiently close to
1
.

The verification is quite straightforward.
Since
H(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\nu\gtrsim\sigma/\Delta(\sigma)\gg\sigma^{1-c}
,
assumption (a) boils down to
cq<\min\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2},1-c\bigr)
, that is, to
c<\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Assumption (b) holds for
c(1+2q)<1-\tfrac{3}{2}\,c
, that is, for
c<\tfrac{2}{9}
.

Assumption (c) is true when
\nu^{\frac{2}{3}+{\varepsilon}}\ll\beta^{1+\frac{3}{2}+q}\sigma=\sigma^{1-(\frac{5}{2}+q)c}
. Since we are assuming that
\nu=o(\sigma^{1+{\varepsilon}})
,
this boils down to
\tfrac{7}{2}\,c<\tfrac{1}{3}
, that is, to
c<\tfrac{2}{21}
.

Since the Fourier transform
\widehat{g}
decays faster than
any negative power, assumption (d) holds for any choice of
c>0
.
At last, to satisfy condition (
7.3
), we need
-\tfrac{1}{4}+c<-\tfrac{3}{4}\,c
,
i.e.,
c<\tfrac{1}{7}
.
∎

Likely, using more advanced analytic number theory techniques, one can improve
the exponent
\tfrac{2}{21}
.

7.4.
The Thue–Morse sequences

The Thue–Morse sequence is defined in an inductive way by the relations
\xi(0)=1
,
\xi(2n)=\xi(n)
,
\xi(2n+1)=-\xi(n)
,
n\geq 0
. The Thue–Morse sequence is a Wiener sequence with
purely singular continuous spectral measure. This fact goes back to Mahler
[
23
]
. In that work Mahler proved that
the Thue–Morse sequence is a Wiener sequence, computed its spectral measure, and
proved that it has no discrete component and has a non-trivial singular continuous component. The fact that the spectral measure is purely absolutely continuous was proven later by Kakutani
[
15
]
.
Curiously, Mahler published his result in 1927, as a follow-up to Winer’s celebrated work
[
41
]
, in which Wiener introduced the class of sequences, which today bears his name.

Lemma 7.4

(Mahler
[
23
]
)
.

Let
\xi
be the Thue–Morse sequence. Then,

\Bigl|\sum_{0\leq k<x}\xi(k)\xi(k+h)-\sigma(h)x\Bigr|\leq Ch\log(x+1),\qquad 0\leq h<x,

where the even sequence
\sigma\colon\mathbb{Z}\to[-1,1]
is defined by the recurrence relations

\sigma(0)=1
, and
\sigma(2h)=\xi(h)
,

\sigma(2h+1)=-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma(h)+\sigma(h+1))
.

Our formulation is slightly different from the original one, since Mahler did not specify
the rate of convergence of binary correlations. We give the proof, which follows
Mahler’s one mutatis mutandis, in Appendix D.

Let
\chi
be the spectral measure of the Thue–Morse sequence, that is,
\widehat{\chi}(h)=\sigma(h)
,
h\in\mathbb{Z}
. The following lemma is probably well-known to experts.
Its proof exploits the identity for the generating function of
\xi
:

\sum_{n\geq 0}\xi(n)z^{n}=\prod_{\ell\geq 0}\Bigl(1-z^{2^{\ell}}\Bigr)\,.

Lemma 7.5
.

For any interval
I\subset[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\,]
,

(7.4)

\chi(I)\gtrsim\exp(-c(\log|I|^{2})).

Proof.

Given
N\geq 1
, we define

\displaystyle P_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq k<N}\xi(k)e(kt),

\displaystyle{\rm d}\chi_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}|P_{N}(t)|^{2}\,{\rm d}t.

Then
\chi_{N}([0,1])=1
and for
h\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\widehat{\chi_{N}}(h)=\frac{1}{N}\,\sum_{0\leq k<N}\xi(k)\xi(k+h)\to\sigma(h),\qquad N\to\infty.

Hence, the measures
\chi_{N}
tend to
\chi
weakly, and to verify (
7.4
), it suffices to check that for any interval

I=[p2^{-m},(p+1)2^{-m}]
with integer
p
we have

\chi_{2^{n}}(I)\geq\exp(-Cm^{2}),\qquad n>m.

Since
P_{1}(t)=1
and

P_{2^{n+1}}(t)=\sum_{0\leq k<2^{n+1}}\xi(k)e(kt)\\
=\sum_{0\leq k<2^{n}}\xi(2k)e(2kt)+\sum_{0\leq k<2^{n}}\xi(2k+1)e((2k+1)t)\\
=\sum_{0\leq k<2^{n}}\xi(k)e(2kt)-\sum_{0\leq k<2^{n}}\xi(k)e((2k+1)t)=(1-e(t))P_{2^{n}}(2t),

we obtain that

P_{2^{n}}(t)=\prod_{0\leq j<n}\bigl(1-e(2^{j}t)\bigr).

Therefore,

{\rm d}\chi_{2^{n}}(t)=\Bigl(\prod_{0\leq j<n}\bigl(2\sin^{2}(2^{j}\pi t)\bigr)\Bigr)\,{\rm d}t.

Set
I^{\prime}=[(p+\frac{1}{4})2^{-m},(p+\frac{3}{4})2^{-m}]
. We have

|\sin(2^{j}\pi t)|\geq c2^{j-m},\qquad 0\leq j\leq m,\,t\in I^{\prime}.

Hence,

\prod_{0\leq j\leq m}\bigl(2\sin^{2}(2^{j}\pi t)\bigr)\geq 2^{-m^{2}-Cm},\qquad t\in I^{\prime}.

Furthermore, for
n>m
, the measure

d\gamma_{m,n}(t)=\Bigl(\prod_{m<j<n}\bigl(2\sin^{2}(2^{j}\pi t)\bigr)\Bigr)\,{\rm d}t

is
2^{-(m+1)}
-periodic, and
\gamma_{m,n}([0,1])=1
. Hence,
\gamma_{m,n}(J)=2^{-(m+1)}
for any interval
J
of length
2^{-(m+1)}
.
In particular,
\gamma_{m,n}(I^{\prime})=2^{-(m+1)}
, and, finally,

\chi_{2^{n}}(I^{\prime})\geq 2^{-m^{2}-Cm},\qquad n>m.

This gives us (
7.4
).
∎

Theorem 9
.

Let

be an entire function with smooth coefficients

with a

,

.
Let
\sigma=\psi^{\prime}\circ\log
, where
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
is the inverse function to
\varphi
.
Then the zero set of

is
(\gamma,\rho)

Appendix A:
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistribution and the uniform transportation

The idea to measure the proximity between
n_{F_{\xi}}
and
\gamma
by
the uniform transportation distance was used in Sodin–Tsirelson
[
37
]
in the case

a(n)=(n!)^{-1/2}
when
\xi
is a complex Gaussian IID sequence
(see also
[
39
]
). In a somewhat different set-up, a
similar idea was used by Sjöstrand, see
[
36
, Ch 12]
and references therein.

A.1 The uniform transportation distance and its dual version

Let
\gamma
and
\gamma_{1}
be locally finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{C}
. We call a non-negative locally finite measure
\mathfrak{n}
on
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}

a transportation
from
\gamma_{1}
to
\gamma
, if
\mathfrak{n}
has marginals
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma
, that is,

\iint_{\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}}h(x)\,{\rm d}\mathfrak{n}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}h(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma_{1}(x)\,,

and

\iint_{\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}}h(y)\,{\rm d}\mathfrak{n}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}h(y)\,{\rm d}\gamma(y)\,,

for all compactly supported continuous functions
h\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Note that if there exists a map
T\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
that pushes forward the
measure
\gamma_{1}
to
\gamma
, then the corresponding transportation
\mathfrak{n}
is defined
by

\iint_{\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}}H(x,y)\,{\rm d}\mathfrak{n}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}H(x,Tx)\,{\rm d}\gamma_{1}(x)

for an arbitrary compactly supported continuous function

H\colon\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
.

The better
\mathfrak{n}
is concentrated near the diagonal of
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
, the closer
the measures
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma
are to each other. We shall measure such a concentration in the
L^{\infty}
-sense, and set

\mathsf{Tra}_{\,d}(\gamma_{1},\gamma)=\inf\,\sup\{{d}(x,y)\colon x,y\in\operatorname{supp}(\mathfrak{n})\}\,,

where
{d}\colon\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a distance function on
\mathbb{C}
, and the infimum
is taken over all transportations
\mathfrak{n}
from
\gamma_{1}
to
\gamma
.
Note that the distance
\mathsf{Tra}_{\,d}
might be infinite.

There exists a dual version of the transportation distance,
which measures the discrepancy between the measures
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma
.
The distance
\mathsf{Di}_{\,d}(\gamma_{1},\gamma)
is defined to be the infimum of
\tau>0
such that, for each bounded Borel set
U\subset\mathbb{C}
,

(A.1)

\gamma_{1}(U)\leq\gamma(U_{+\tau})\quad{\rm and}\quad\gamma(U)\leq\gamma_{1}(U_{+\tau}),

where
U_{+\tau}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon d(z,U)<\tau\}
is a
\tau
-neighbourhood of
U
.
The equality
\mathsf{Tra}=\mathsf{Di}
is classical (Strassen, Sudakov, Laczkovich);
its proof can be found, for instance, in
[
39
, Appendix A-1]
.

A.2 Equivalence of two notions

Clearly, for any Borel set
U\subset\mathbb{C}
, we have

|\gamma_{1}(U)-\gamma_{2}(U)|\leq 2\gamma_{1}((\partial U)_{+2\tau})

with
\tau=2\mathsf{Di}_{\,d}(\gamma_{1},\gamma)
. Thus, the measures

n_{F_{\xi}}
and
\gamma
are
(\gamma,\rho)
-equidistributed provided that

\mathsf{Di}_{\,d_{\rho}}(n_{F_{\xi}},\gamma)<\infty
. The converse is less obvious:

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that we are given the measure
\gamma=\Gamma m
, where
m
is the area measure, and the radial gauge
\rho
satisfies estimates (
3.1
)
and (
3.2
) in Proposition
1
, and let
\gamma_{1}
be a locally finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{C}
.
Suppose that there exist positive constants
\tau
and
C
such that, for any compact
set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
,

(A.2)

|\gamma_{1}(K)-\gamma(K)|\leq C\gamma((\partial K)_{+\tau}).

Then

\mathsf{Di}_{\,d_{\rho}}(\gamma_{1},\gamma)<\infty
.

A.2.1 Proof of estimate (
A.1
) for compact sets which are far from the origin

Lemma A.2
.

Under conditions of Lemma
A.1
,
there exist positive values
r_{1}
and
\tau_{1}
such that, for any compact set

K\subset\{|z|\geq r_{1}\}
,

\gamma(K)\leq\gamma_{1}(K_{+\tau_{1}})\quad{\rm and}\quad\gamma_{1}(K)\leq\gamma(K_{+\tau_{1}})

The idea of the proof of this lemma is borrowed from Laczkovich
[
17
]
(see also
[
39
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Set
R_{1}=1
,
R_{j+1}=R_{j}+M\rho(R_{j})
, where
M
is a large parameter to be fixed. Clearly,

R_{j}\uparrow\infty
(otherwise,
\lim_{j}\rho(R_{j})=\rho(\lim_{j}R_{j})>0
,
which would lead us to a contradiction). Set
A_{j}=\{z\colon R_{j}\leq|z|\leq R_{j+1}\}
. We partition the annulus
A_{j}
into
equal closed sectors of size
\simeq M\rho(R_{j})
, and denote these sectors
by
Q=Q_{jk}=\{z\colon R_{j}\leq|z|\leq R_{j+1},\theta_{k}\leq\arg(z)\leq\theta_{k+1}\}

We denote the “center” of the sector

by

w(Q)=\sqrt{R_{j}R_{j+1}}\,e^{{\rm i}(\theta_{k}+\theta_{k+1})/2}

We say that the sectors
Q
and

are

we denote this relation by
Q\sim Q^{\prime}
, and set
\widetilde{Q}=\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\sim Q}Q^{\prime}
.

Next, we note that, since
\rho^{\prime}(r)\to 0

(A.3)

\rho(R_{j+1})/\rho(R_{j})\to 1,\quad j\to\infty\,,

and that, by our assumption (
3.2

(A.4)

\Gamma(R)/\Gamma(R_{j})\to 1,\quad R_{j-1}\leq R\leq R_{j+1},\ j\to\infty\,.

Furthermore, by (
A.3
) and (
A.4
), for

and for any sector

,
the hollowing holds:

•

there are at most
9
neighbouring sectors

•

{\rm diam}_{\rho}(\widetilde{Q})\leq 10M
(as usual,

•

\tfrac{1}{2}\leq\Gamma(z_{1})/\Gamma(z_{2})\leq 2
,

is the constant from (

).

Let

,

By (

) we have

\gamma_{1}(K)\leq\gamma_{1}(A)\leq\gamma(A)+C\gamma\bigl((\partial A)_{+\tau}\bigr).

Furthermore, since

For each boundary sector

, we have

\gamma\bigl((\partial Q)_{+\tau}\bigr)\lesssim M\rho(w)^{2}\Gamma(w),\quad w=w(Q),

while for the sectors

\gamma_{1}(Q^{\prime})\geq\gamma(Q^{\prime})-C\gamma\bigl((\partial Q^{\prime})_{+\tau}\bigr)\\

Appendix B: Proof of Proposition
4

As we have already mentioned, here, we will closely follow
the original proof given by Nguyen and Vu. They work not with the sum of
exponentials, as we do, but with the sum of cosines

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\xi_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\cos(2\pi\lambda\theta)
.
Another difference is that we assume that the independent random variables
(\xi_{\lambda})

are identically distributed, while Nguyen and Vu only assume their independence, but
additionally assume that, for some
m>2
, the
m
th central moment of the
\xi_{\lambda}
s is uniformly bounded.

The proof is carried out in three steps. First, one considers the case
when
(\xi_{\lambda})
are Rademacher random variables
(r_{\lambda})
, that is,
independent random variables taking the values

\pm 1
with equal probability
1/2
. This step is based on a variant of
Halász’ anti-concentration result
[
31
, Lemma 9.3]
(we will recall it
below). In the second step, applying Kahane’s reduction principle, one extends the result
to independent symmetric complex-valued random variables
(\xi_{\lambda})
.
Finally, the general case will be reduced to the symmetric one using the symmetrization device, that is,
taking independent copies
\xi^{\prime}_{\lambda}
of
\xi_{\lambda}
and applying the result of the second
step to the symmetric random variables
\xi_{\lambda}-\xi^{\prime}_{\lambda}
.

Fix an integer
\alpha\geq 1
. Assume that

\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|c_{\lambda}|=1
.

Step 1
.

Assuming that
(r_{\lambda})
are Rademacher random variables, and letting

S(\theta)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}r_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}e(\lambda\theta)
,
we show that, for every
\theta\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
, except for a set of Lebesgue measure
at most
O(n^{-\beta/(4\alpha)+2\alpha+1/4})
, we have

\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl[\bigl|S(\theta)-Z\bigr|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\lesssim n^{-\alpha}\,.

Let
c_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}^{\prime}+{\rm i}c_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}
,
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(|c_{\lambda}^{\prime}|^{2}+|c_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}|^{2})=1
,
and let
c(\theta)=\cos(2\pi\theta)
,

, i.e.,

e(\theta)=c(\theta)+{\rm i}s(\theta)
. Set
a_{\lambda}={\rm Re}\,\bigl[c_{\lambda}e(\lambda\theta)\bigr]=c_{\lambda}^{\prime}c(\lambda\theta)-c_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}s(\lambda\theta)

Since, for every

,

\mathbb{P}\bigl[\bigl|S(\theta)-Z\bigr|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl[\bigl|{\rm Re\,}S(\theta)-{\rm Re\,}Z\bigr|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\,,

it suffices to estimate the size of the set of
\theta
s, for which

\sup_{X\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\Bigl|\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}r_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}-X\bigr|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\lesssim n^{-\alpha}\,.

We will use a Halász-type lemma borrowed from

\mathbb{P}\bigl[\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(X_{\lambda}-\mathbb{E}[X_{\lambda}])\geq nt\bigr]\leq e^{-cnt^{2}}
. Noting that
\mathbb{E}[X_{\lambda}]\leq 1-\delta
and letting
t=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta
,
we see that with probability at least
1-e^{-cn\delta^{2}/4}
there are at least
\tfrac{1}{2}\,n

indices
\lambda\in\Lambda
for which
|\xi_{\lambda}|\geq\delta
. On the event that
this happens, denote by
\Lambda_{1}
the set of
\lambda\in\Lambda
for which
|\xi_{\lambda}|\geq\delta
,

S_{1}(\theta)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}r_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}e(\lambda\theta)
.
Since
\Lambda_{1}
has at least
\tfrac{1}{2}\,n
elements,
by Step 1, we get

\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}^{({\varepsilon}_{\lambda})}\bigl[\bigl|S_{1}(\theta)-Z\bigr|<\delta n^{-\beta}\bigr]\lesssim n^{-\alpha}\,,

outside a set of
\theta
s of Lebesgue measure at most
O(n^{-\beta/(4\alpha)+2\alpha+1/4})
.
Since
S_{1}(\theta)
and
S(\theta)-S_{1}(\theta)
are independent, we obtain that again with probability at least
1-e^{-cn\delta^{2}/4}

outside a set of
\theta
s of Lebesgue measure at most
O(n^{-\beta/(4\alpha)+2\alpha+1/4})
we have

\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}^{({\varepsilon}_{\lambda})}\bigl[\bigl|S(\theta)-Z\bigr|<\delta n^{-\beta}\bigr]\lesssim n^{-\alpha}\,,

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}^{(\xi_{\lambda})}\Bigl[\frac{1}{|I|}\,\int_{I}\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}^{({\varepsilon}_{\lambda})}\bigl[\bigl|S(\theta)-Z\bigr|<\delta n^{-\beta}\bigr]\,{\rm d}\theta\Bigr]\\
\leq O(n^{-\alpha})+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{|I|}\cdot n^{-\beta/(4\alpha)+2\alpha+1/4}\Bigr)+O(e^{-c\delta^{2}n/4})=O(n^{-\alpha})\,,

provided that
\beta\geq 20\alpha^{2}
.
Finally, we observe that
covering the disk centered at
Z
of radius
n^{-\beta}
by
O(\delta^{-2})

disks of radius
\delta n^{-\beta}
, we get

\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S(\theta)-Z|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\leq O(\delta^{-2})\cdot\sup_{Z\in\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S(\theta)-Z|<\delta n^{-\beta}\bigr]\,,

which, together with (
Appendix B: Proof of Proposition
), completes the second step.

In particular, we get a value
\theta\in I
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S(\theta)|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\lesssim n^{-\alpha}.

Step 3
.
Here, we consider the general case. Let
\xi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
be independent copies of
\xi_{\lambda}
,

\lambda\in\Lambda
. Then the random variables
\xi_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}=\xi_{\lambda}-\xi_{\lambda}^{\prime}

are symmetric and non-degenerate. Denote by
S_{\xi}
,
S_{\xi^{\prime}}
and
S_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}
the corresponding
trigonometric sums, and note that

|S_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(\theta)|<2n^{-\beta}
provided that, for some
Z\in\mathbb{C}
,

|S_{\xi}(\theta)-Z|<n^{-\beta}
and
|S_{\xi^{\prime}}(\theta)-Z|<n^{-\beta}
.
Therefore,

\Bigl(\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S_{\xi}(\theta)-Z|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\Bigr)^{2}=\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S_{\xi}(\theta)-Z|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\cdot\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S_{\xi^{\prime}}(\theta)-Z|<n^{-\beta}\bigr]\\
\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl[|S_{\xi^{\prime\prime}}(\theta)|<2n^{-\beta}\bigr]\,.

By Step 2 (applied with
2\alpha
instead of
\alpha
),
there exist

Appendix C: Proof of Lemma
7.1

As we have already mentioned, we will closely follow Mirsky’s paper
[
25
]
.
Since

\mu^{2}(n)=\sum_{d^{2}|n}\mu(d),

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq x}\mu^{2}(k)\mu^{2}(k+h)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}c-b^{2}d=h,\\
b^{2}d\leq x\end{subarray}}\,\mu(a)\mu(b)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}c-b^{2}d=h,\\
b^{2}d\leq x,\,ab\leq y\end{subarray}}\ +\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}c-b^{2}d=h,\\
b^{2}d\leq x,\,ab>y\end{subarray}}\,\Bigr)\mu(a)\mu(b)=I_{1}+I_{2},

for a large parameter
y
to be fixed later on.

First,

I_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h,\\
ab\leq y\end{subarray}}\,\mu(a)\mu(b)\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}c-b^{2}d=h,\\
1\leq b^{2}d\leq x\end{subarray}}1.

Set

t=\gcd(a,b),\ a^{\prime}=\frac{a^{2}}{t^{2}},\ a^{\prime\prime}=\frac{a}{t},\ b^{\prime}=\frac{b^{2}}{t^{2}},\ b^{\prime\prime}=\frac{b}{t},\ h^{\prime}=\frac{h}{t^{2}},\ x^{\prime}=\frac{x}{t^{2}},

and choose
c_{0},d_{0}
so that
a^{\prime}c_{0}-b^{\prime}d_{0}=h^{\prime}
.
Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{\prime}c-b^{\prime}d=h^{\prime},\\
1\leq b^{\prime}d\leq x^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\,1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{\prime}(c-c_{0})=b^{\prime}(d-d_{0}),\\
1-b^{\prime}d_{0}\leq b^{\prime}(d-d_{0})\leq x^{\prime}-b^{\prime}d_{0}\end{subarray}}\,1=\frac{x^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}+q

with
|q|<2
.
Therefore,

I_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h,\\
ab\leq y\end{subarray}}\,\mu(a)\mu(b)\Bigl(\frac{x\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})}{a^{2}b^{2}}+O(1)\Bigr)\\
=x\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h,\\
ab\leq y\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\mu(a)\mu(b)\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})}{a^{2}b^{2}}+O(y\log y).

Since

\Bigl|\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h,\\
ab>y\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\mu(a)\mu(b)\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})}{a^{2}b^{2}}\,\Bigr|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h,\\
ab>y\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})}{a^{2}b^{2}}\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{2}|h,\\
t^{2}a^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime}>y\end{subarray}}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}a^{\prime\prime 2}b^{\prime\prime 2}}\leq\sum_{a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\geq 1}\,\frac{1}{a^{\prime\prime 2}b^{\prime\prime 2}}\,\sum_{t>\sqrt{y/(a^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime})}}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}}\\
\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}\,\sum_{a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\geq 1}\,\frac{1}{(a^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime\prime})^{3/2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}\,,

we conclude that

I_{1}=x\sum_{\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h}\frac{\mu(a)\mu(b)\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})}{a^{2}b^{2}}+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{\sqrt{y}}+y\log y\Bigr).

Next,

\sum_{\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})|h}\frac{\mu(a)\mu(b)\gcd(a^{2},b^{2})}{a^{2}b^{2}}\,=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})=1,\,\gcd(a^{\prime\prime},t)=1,\\
\gcd(b^{\prime\prime},t)=1,\,t^{2}|h\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\mu(ta^{\prime\prime})\mu(tb^{\prime\prime})}{t^{2}a^{\prime\prime 2}b^{\prime\prime 2}}\,=:A.

If
s^{2}
is the largest square divisor of
h
, then

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu^{2}(t)=1,\,t|s}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})=1,\,\gcd(a^{\prime\prime},t)=1,\\
\gcd(b^{\prime\prime},t)=1\end{subarray}}\,\frac{\mu(a^{\prime\prime})\mu(b^{\prime\prime})}{a^{\prime\prime 2}b^{\prime\prime 2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu^{2}(t)=1,\,t|s}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,\sum_{\gcd(d,t)=1}\,\frac{\mu(d)\tau(d)}{d^{2}},

where
\tau(d)
is the number of the divisors of
d
.
Therefore,

A=\sum_{\mu^{2}(t)=1,\,t|s}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\prod_{p\,\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.97221pt\kern-4.20757pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\mid}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.97221pt\kern-4.20757pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\mid}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.54443pt\kern-3.04367pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\mid}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 0.38889pt\kern-2.49922pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\mid}}}\,t}\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{p^{2}}\Bigr)=\prod_{p}\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{p^{2}}\Bigr)\sum_{\mu^{2}(t)=1,\,t|s}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\prod_{p|t}\Bigl(1-\frac{2}{p^{2}}\Bigr)^{-1}\\
=D\,\prod_{p|s}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}(1-2/p^{2})}\Bigr)=D\,\prod_{p^{2}|h}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{p^{2}-2}\Bigr)=D(h).

Thus,

(C.1)

I_{1}=D(h)x+O\Bigl(\frac{x}{\sqrt{y}}+y\log y\Bigr).

Next,

|I_{2}|=\Bigl|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}c-b^{2}d=h,\\
b^{2}d\leq x,\,ab>y\end{subarray}}\mu(a)\mu(b)\Bigr|\leq\sum_{cd<x(x+h)/y^{2}}\,\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{2}c-b^{2}d=h,\\
b^{2}d\leq x\end{subarray}}\,1.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By
[
4
, Lemma 4]
, the equation
a^{2}cd-(bd)^{2}=hd
has at most
C_{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}
solutions
(a,bd)
if

cd
is not a perfect square. If
cd=m^{2}
, then the equation
(am)^{2}-(bd)^{2}=hd
has at most
C_{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}
solutions
(am,bd)
. Therefore,

(C.2)

|I_{2}|\leq C_{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}\,\sum_{cd<2x^{2}/y^{2}}\,1\lesssim C_{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}\,\frac{x^{2}}{y^{2}}\,\log(2x^{2}/y^{2}).

Finally, set
y=x^{2/3}
. Now, (
C.1
) and (
C.2
) yield

\Bigl|\sum_{k\leq x}\mu^{2}(k)\mu^{2}(k+h)-D(h)x\Bigr|\leq C_{\varepsilon}x^{(2/3)+2\varepsilon},\qquad 1\leq h\leq x,

completing the proof.
\Box

Appendix D: Proof of Lemma
7.4

The following argument reproduces rather closely Mahler’s original proof.
Set

S(x,h)=\sum_{0\leq k<x}\xi(k)\xi(k+h).

Then

S(x,h)=\sum_{0\leq k<x/2}\xi(2k)\xi(2k+h)+\sum_{0\leq k<x/2}\xi(2k+1)\xi(2k+h+1)+\Delta(x,h),

where

\Delta(x,h)=\begin{cases}-\xi(x)\xi(x+h)&x{\rm\ is\ odd};\\
0&{\rm\ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle S(2x,2h)

\displaystyle=2S(x,h),

\displaystyle S(2x+1,2h)

\displaystyle=2S(x+1,h)+\Delta(2x+1,2h),

\displaystyle S(2x,2h+1)

\displaystyle=-S(x,h)-S(x,h+1),

\displaystyle S(2x+1,2h+1)

\displaystyle=-S(x+1,h)-S(x+1,h+1)+\Delta(2x+1,2h+1)

for positive
x
and
h
.
Since
S(x,0)=x
,
x\geq 0
, we obtain by induction in
h
that

\bigl|S(x,h)-\sigma(h)x\bigr|\leq Ch\log(x+1),\qquad x\geq 1,

for some positive numerical constant
C
.
\Box
