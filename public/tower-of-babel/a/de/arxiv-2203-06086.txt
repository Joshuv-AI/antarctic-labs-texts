On the Insensitivity of Bit Density to Read Noise in One-bit Quanta Image Sensors
Stanley H. Chan

Abstract
The one-bit quanta image sensor is a photon-counting device that produces binary measurements where each bit represents the presence or absence of a photon. The sensor quantizes the analog voltage into the binary bits using a threshold value q q . The average number of ones in the bitstream is known as the bit-density and is the sufficient statistics for signal estimation. An intriguing phenomenon is observed when the quanta exposure is at the unity and the threshold is q = 0.5 q=0.5 . The bit-density demonstrates an insensitivity as long as the read noise level does not exceed a certain limit. In other words, the bit density stays at a constant independent of the amount of read noise. This paper provides a mathematical explanation of the phenomenon by deriving conditions under which the phenomenon happens. It was found that the insensitivity holds when some forms of the symmetry of the underlying Poisson-Gaussian distribution holds.

The Quantization Threshold of QIS

The subject of this paper is related to the quantization threshold of a one-bit QIS. The starting point of the problem is the familiar Poisson-Gaussian distribution

1

The one-bit QIS produces a quantized version of the signal

Y=\begin{cases}1,&\qquad X\geq q,\\
0,&\qquad X<q.\end{cases}

(3)

For example, in Figure

Since

is a binary random variable, its probability masses can be determined. All probabilities in the shaded region in Figure

follows the integral

\displaystyle p_{Y}(1)

\displaystyle=\int_{q}^{\infty}p_{X}(x)\;dx

\displaystyle=\int_{q}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}e^{-\theta}}{k!}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-k)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\;dx,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^{k}}{k!}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{q-k}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right),

(4)

and
p_{Y}(0)=1-p_{Y}(1)

The statistical expectation of the random variable

\mathbb{E}[Y]

The mathematical expression of the bit density is straightforward. Since

\displaystyle D\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle=1\cdot p_{Y}(1)+0\cdot p_{Y}(0)

\displaystyle=p_{Y}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^{k}}{k!}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{q-k}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right).

(5)

Note that

q
, the read noise
\sigma
, and the underlying exposure
\theta

I-B

An Unexpected Observation

Consider a threshold
q=0.5
. If we plot the bit-density as a function of
\theta
, how does the plot look like?

Without much deep analysis, we can quickly anticipate that in the extreme case when the read noise is
\sigma=0
, the error function
\text{erfc}(\cdot)
will become a step function, and hence the bit density will be as simple as

D^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}e^{-\theta}}{k!}=1-e^{-\theta}.

(6)

As
\theta
increases, the bit density
D^{*}
also increases. If we plot the function in the semilog-
x
scale, it will look like one of the curves shown in Figure
3
.

Now, consider the case where the read noise
\sigma
is no longer zero. Figure
3
shows a few of these cases. The observation is that regardless of the read noise level
\sigma
(at least for
\sigma\leq 0.5
considered in this plot), the bit density at
\theta=1
appears to be a
constant
. In other words, it appears that there is an insensitivity of the bit density to the read noise!

The insensitivity to the read noise implies that if we set the threshold to
q=0.5
and observe an average bit density that is equal to
D^{*}=1-e^{-1}
, then it is guaranteed that the underlying exposure is
\theta=1
. So, the insensitivity to the read noise has the potential to offer a perfect estimate of the
analog
signal by just using the
digital
measurements that have been severely quantized.

Fig. 3:
The bit density
D
as a function of the exposure
\theta
, for different levels of read noise. The threshold is chosen as
q=0.5
. Notice that when
\theta=1
, the bit densities of different read noise all intersect at the same point. The goal of this paper is to mathematically explain this phenomenon and provide conditions under which the intersection occurs.

The above observation was first mentioned by Fossum
[
29
]
. The intuitive argument was that when
\sigma
is sufficiently small, the symmetry of the Poisson and the Gaussian will make the loss of probability mass before the threshold compensated for the gain of the probability mass after the threshold. For this phenomenon to hold, it was mentioned that
\sigma\leq 0.5
would be a sufficient condition. This paper is a follow up work of
[
29
]
, where it was commented that “
This interesting insensitivity has been proven mathematically by Chan [10] after a discussion of this paper
”. Here, we present the proof by answering two questions:

•

Where does the insensitivity of read noise come from? Is there a mathematical proof of the existence?

•

Under what conditions will the insensitivity exist? Will the insensitivity exist for exposures other than
\theta=1
and thresholds other than
q=0.5
?

I-C

Other QIS Threshold Analyses in Literature

The analysis of the threshold of one-bit QIS has been reported in various occasions since early 2010. In the first theory paper

[
28
]
.

As far as algorithms are concerned, a few threshold update schemes have been proposed using Markov chain and other statistical techniques
[
31
,
32
]
. The algorithm presented in
[
20
]
uses a bisection approach by checking the percentage of ones and zeros.

For SPAD-based QIS, the interaction between threshold and read noise is irrelevant because a SPAD has zero read noise. However, the large dark current is a bigger challenge for SPAD-based QIS, although recent advancements in SPAD have demonstrated improvements in dark current
[
33
,
34
]
. For SPAD, there is more considerations about the dead time
[
23
]
. On the algorithmic side, SPAD-based QIS largely share the same mathematical results as CIS-based QIS
[
23
,
35
]
. The bigger question, which is not the subject of our present paper, is the scene motion. The work by Ma et al.
[
24
]
gave a good assessment of how much image reconstruction can we expect using image registration techniques. Another line of work about using SPAD-based QIS for high dynamic range imaging can be found in

,
37
]
.

In the electronic device literature, the focus is slightly different. Instead of analyzing the quantized Poisson statistics, the interest is about stabilizing the threshold to a fixed value, say

The present paper is a mathematical analysis of the threshold. Specific considerations are put into the presence of read noise which were not analyzed in the previous theoretical work such as

Main Result

II-A

Statement and Numerical Inspection

The main result is stated in Theorem 1. The theorem provides a mathematical condition under which the constant bit density
D
can be observed. The theorem also predicts that when the read noise is above the limit predicted by the theorem, the bit density will drop.

Theorem 1

Define the bit density of a 1-bit Poisson-Gaussian random variable as

D=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^{k}}{k!}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{q-k}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right).

(7)

(8)

(D^{*}-D)/D^{*}\leq 0.0001

(D^{*}-D)/D^{*}\leq 0.0001

\sigma

II-B

Mathematical Tools

The proof of the main theorem requires several elementary probabilistic tools. To make the calculus well-defined, the variable
k
is relaxed from being integers to real numbers.

The first one is the relationship between the complementary error function (
erfc
) and the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard Gaussian.

Lemma 1

The complementary error function can be written equivalently through the CDF of the standard Gaussian:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{q-k}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{q}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(y-k)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\;dy

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}1-\Phi\left(\frac{q-k}{\sigma}\right),

(9)

where
\Phi\left(\cdot\right)
is CDF of the standard Gaussian, defined as

\Phi\left(x\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right\}\;dy.

Proof

Note that
1-\Phi\left(x\right)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right\}\;dy
. Then by letting
{x=(q-k)/\sigma}
, the result is proved.

\square

The shape of the the complementary error function and the CDF are shown in Figure
5
. They are related by a simple amplitude and time scaling.

Fig. 5:
The complementary error function
\text{erfc}(x)
and the CDF
\Phi(x)
are related by an amplitude and time scaling.

The next lemma is about flipping the roles of
q
and
k
in the Gaussian distribution. This allows us to re-center the Gaussian to
q
and evaluate it up to
k
.

Lemma 2

The CDF evaluated with respect to
q
can be switched to the CDF evaluated with respect to
k
:

\displaystyle\int_{q}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(y-k)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\;dy

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{-\infty}^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(y-q)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\;dy.

(10)

Proof

The left-hand side is
1-\Phi((q-k)/\sigma)
whereas the right-hand side is
\Phi((k-q)/\sigma)
. Since
\Phi(x)=1-\Phi(-x)
, the equality in (
10
) is proved.

\square

A direct consequence of the lemma is that the error function in (
9
) can be simplified to
1-\Phi((q-k)/\sigma)=\Phi((k-q)/\sigma)
, which is the Gaussian CDF with mean of
q
evaluated at
k
. The intuition can be seen from Figure
6
. The integral on the left-hand side of (
10
) is the black curve which is a Gaussian with mean
k=4
. The area under the curve is colored in gray. The integral on the right-hand side of (
10
) is the red curve which is a Gaussian with mean
q=5
. The area under the curve is colored in pink. The lemma asserts that the area of the gray region is identical to the area of the pink region.

Fig. 6:
The black and red curves are the two Gaussian probability density functions centered at
q
and
k
, respectively. The figure highlights the equivalence between the two shaded areas.

The third mathematical tool is the derivative of the CDF.

Lemma 3

The derivative of the CDF is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dk}\left\{\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(k-q)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

(11)

If the derivative is evaluated at
k=q
, then the exponential term is eliminated, leaving a constant
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}
.

Proof

The proof is goes as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dk}\left\{\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dk}\int_{-\infty}^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(y-q)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\;dy

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(k-q)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\},

(12)

where (a) is due to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

\square

II-C

Proof of Main Result

The key idea of the proof is to zoom into the transient of the Gaussian CDF and evaluate the residue compared to an ideal sharp cutoff.

First, define the following notations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)=\frac{\theta^{k}e^{-\theta}}{k!},\;\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\;\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)=\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right).

(13)

To make the bit density
D
explicitly depending on the read noise
\sigma
, we use Lemma 1 and 2 to define
D(\sigma)
as

\displaystyle D(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^{k}}{k!}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{q-k}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^{k}}{k!}\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k).

(14)

When the read noise is zero, i.e.,
\sigma=0
, the Gaussian part
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
will become a unit step function with the transition occurring at
q=0.5
. The bit density
D(\sigma)
in this case is the
ideal
bit density such that

\displaystyle D^{*}=D(0)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k),

(15)

where the summation starts at
k=1
(instead of
k=0
) if the threshold is
q=0.5
.

Since the read noise insensitivity is the phenomenon that
D(\sigma)\approx D^{*}
, any error made in such approximation needs to be measured by

\displaystyle D^{*}-D(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.61108pt=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)-\Big[\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip-8.61108pt=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\Big)-\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0).

(16)

Therefore, the task now becomes how to evaluate the sum.

Figure
7
shows the behavior of the infinite sum for the case
q=0.5
and
\theta=1
. For entries with the index
k\geq 3
, it is almost sure that the ideal Gaussian CDF
\mathcal{G}_{0}(k)
is identical to the actual Gaussian CDF
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
for any reasonably small
\sigma
. That is, the pink color region is exactly the same as the region covered by the red stems for any
k\geq 3
. Thus, for
k\geq 3
, one should expect that the residue
D^{*}-D(\sigma)
can be completely described by the terms with
k=0,1,2
only.

Fig. 7:
The behavior of the Poisson part
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)
and the Gaussian part
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
for
\sigma=0.8
and
\theta=1
.

The precise relationship between
k
and
\sigma
is given as follows. Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)=\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)\geq\Phi\left(\frac{3-q}{\sigma}\right),

because
\Phi(\cdot)
is monotonically increasing. Setting
q=0.5
and
\Phi\left(\frac{3-q}{\sigma}\right)\geq 0.999
will give
\sigma\leq\Phi^{-1}\left(2.5\right)=0.8090
. Therefore, for all
\sigma\leq 0.8090
and
k\geq 3
,
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\geq 0.999
, the term
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
will be at the unity for
k\geq 3
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\approx 1
, and so the infinite sum in (
16
) can be simplified to just the terms for
k=0,1,2
. This means

\displaystyle D^{*}-D(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\Big)-\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)

\displaystyle=\underset{=0}{\underbrace{\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\Big)}}

(17)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\Big)-\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0).

The finite sum can be broken down into two parts:
k=0,1
, and
k=2
. Consider the term
k=1
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)\Big)-\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)-\Big(\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)+\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)\Big).

(18)

However, notice that
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)
when
\theta=1
because

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta^{1}e^{-\theta}}{1!}=e^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta^{0}e^{-\theta}}{0!}=e^{-1}.

(19)

Also, notice that
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)=1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)
when
q=0.5
because

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{0-0.5}{\sigma}\right)=\Phi\left(\frac{-0.5}{\sigma}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{1-0.5}{\sigma}\right)=\Phi\left(\frac{0.5}{\sigma}\right).

(20)

Using these two facts, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)+\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)+\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1),

and so the right hand side of (
18
) is zero.

Based on the above observations, the residue is essentially determined by the term
k=2
:

\displaystyle D^{*}-D(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)\Big)-\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(2)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)\Big).

(21)

To this end, set a tolerance level
\alpha=0.0001
, the criteria will become

\displaystyle\frac{D^{*}-D(\sigma)}{D^{*}}\leq\alpha,

(22)

which implies
D^{*}-D(\sigma)\leq\alpha D^{*}
. Using (
21
), one can show that
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(2)\Big(1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)\Big)\leq\alpha D^{*}
. Rearranging the terms will yield

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\alpha D^{*}}{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(2)}.

(23)

Since
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)=\Phi\left(\frac{2-q}{\sigma}\right)
, it follows that for
q=0.5
,

\displaystyle\sigma\leq\frac{2-q}{\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha D^{*}}{\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(2)}\right)}\approx 0.4419,

(24)

where
\theta=1
,
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(2)=\theta^{2}e^{-\theta}/2!=0.1839
,
D^{*}=1-e^{-1}=0.6321
, and
\alpha=0.0001
. This completes the main proof.

II-D

An Alternative and (Coarser) Estimate

The result in (
24
) is arguably intimidating. Thus, it would be useful to obtain a slightly more “civilized” version. The goal here is to derive a simpler estimate of
\sigma
.

Corollary 1

Under the same conditions as Theorem 1, if
\sigma\leq 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\approx 0.4
, it holds that
D(\sigma)\approx D^{*}
.

Proof

The intuition is to use a piecewise linear function to approximate the Gaussian CDF as shown in Figure
8
. The linear portion approximates the CDF as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)=\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)\approx ak+b\overset{\text{def}}{=}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\sigma}(k),

(25)

where
a
is the slope to be determined, and
b
is the
y
-intercept to be determined.

Fig. 8:
The function
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
when
\theta=1
,
q=0.5
, and
\sigma=0.2
. The transition point can be theoretically derived.

To determine the piecewise linear function, first consider the slope of
\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)
. By Lemma 3, it is known that

\frac{d}{dk}\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(k-q)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}

(26)

The function
\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)
is symmetric at
k=q
. At
k=q
, the slope is

a=\frac{d}{dk}\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)\Bigg|_{q=k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}.

(27)

The
y
-intercept is chosen such that the linear function is 0.5 when
k=q
, i.e.,
aq+b=0.5
. This gives

b=0.5-\frac{q}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}.

(28)

Therefore, the CDF is approximated

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\sigma}(k)=\begin{cases}0,&\;k\leq\ell,\\
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right)k+\left(0.5-\frac{q}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right),&\;\ell\leq k\leq u,\\
1,&\;k\geq u\end{cases}

(29)

where
\ell
and
u
are the lower and the lower limits, respectively.

The upper limit can be determined by evaluating the expression when
\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)=1
. This yields

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right)k+\left(0.5-\frac{q}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right)=1,

(30)

which translates to

\displaystyle u\overset{\text{def}}{=}q+0.5\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma.

Similarly, the lower limit is determined by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right)k+\left(0.5-\frac{q}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right)=0,

(31)

which gives

\displaystyle\ell\overset{\text{def}}{=}q-0.5\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma.

The more conservative estimate is derived by enforcing

\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)=0,\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)=1,\;\;\mbox{for}\;k\geq 1.

If this can be enforced, then the actual bit density in (
14
) will be exactly the same as the ideal bit density in (
15
). To ensure this happens,
\sigma
can be approximately chosen such that
u=1
and
\ell=0
. This, in turn, implies that

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{2(u-q)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}},

(32)

by substituting
u=1
and
q=0.5
. Therefore, as long as
\sigma\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\approx 0.4
,
D
will be sufficiently close to
D^{*}
.

\square

III
Generalization to Arbitrary
\theta
and
q

The analysis presented in the previous section is a special case where the quanta exposure is
\theta=1
and the threshold is
q=0.5
. A natural question is how does the analysis generalize to other situations. Clearly, as we can see in the proof above, the key of the insensitivity is due to the
symmetry
of certain special cases of the Poisson distribution and the Gaussian CDF. When such symmetry is broken (as will be discussed next), the insensitivity will not appear.

III-A

Insensitivity does not appear when
q\not=0.5
for
\theta=1
.

Consider again the special case where
\theta=1
, but this time a threshold
q\not=0.5
. Tracing back to the proof, one can follow the same argument to show that for any
\sigma\leq 0.4419
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)=\Phi\left(\frac{k-q}{\sigma}\right)\geq 0.999,\quad k\geq 3,

for any
0<q<1
. Therefore, the residue is characterized by

\displaystyle D(\sigma)-D^{*}=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(0)[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)-0]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(1)[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)-1]

\displaystyle+\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(2)\left[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)-1\right].

For different choices of the threshold
0<q<1
, both the Gaussian CDF
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)
and the ideal CDF
\mathcal{G}_{0}(0)
will appear differently as shown in Figure
9
.

Fig. 9:
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
for
\sigma=0.8
and
\theta=1
. Notice the values of
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
at
k=0,1,2
, and the location of the pink region.

A sufficient condition for the residue
D(\sigma)-D^{*}
to vanish is to find a
\sigma
such that

\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(0)\approx 0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)\approx 1,

(33)

because then
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)\approx 1
since
1\geq\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(2)\geq\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(1)\approx 1
. For (
33
) to hold, pick a tolerance
\alpha
(say
\alpha=0.001
). Then the two conditions in (
33
) become

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\frac{0-q}{\sigma}\right)\geq\alpha,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Phi\left(\frac{1-q}{\sigma}\right)\leq 1-\alpha,

which is equivalent to

\sigma\leq\min\left\{-\frac{q}{\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)},\frac{1-q}{\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)}\right\}.

(34)

For example, if
\alpha=0.001
, the required
\sigma
is
\sigma\leq 0.0647
for
q=0.2
or
q=0.8
. But such a small
\sigma
basically means that the insensitivity only exists for an extremely small read noise.

Figure
10
shows the bit density
D(\sigma)
as a function of the read noise
\sigma
for a set of thresholds
q=0.1,\ldots,0.9
. This can be seen as a “zoomed out” version of Figure
4
. As predicted by the theory, the bit density
D(\sigma)
stays at its ideal value
D^{*}
only briefly as
\sigma
grows. The maximum range of
\sigma
is attained when
q=0.5
, but for other choices of
q
, the affordable read noise
\sigma
is quite small.

Fig. 10:
Bit density
D(\sigma)
as a function of
\sigma
for
\theta=1
.

The upper and the lower half of Figure
10
demonstrates a symmetric behavior for
q>0.5
and
q<0.5
. This is attributed to the symmetry of the Gaussian CDF. As one adjusts the threshold
q
, the transition of
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
changes. However, shifting
q
upward by a certain amount versus shifting
q
downward by the same amount will result in identical residues as demonstrated in the two cases of Figure
9
.

To further elaborate on the statement that the insensitivity is a special event for
q=0.5
, one can estimate the range of
\sigma
such that the bit density
D(\sigma)
remains close to
D^{*}
. Figure
11
shows the result. Here, the “closedness” is measured by checking the relative error
(D(\sigma)-D^{*})/D^{*}
to be within a certain tolerance level of
\alpha=0.0001
. As one can see in this experiment, the range of
\sigma
has a sharp and tall spike at
q=0.5
. This spike quickly goes off and sees a linear decay on the two sides as
q
deviates from
0.5
. Therefore, the insensitivity is largely a special event for
\theta=1
and
q=0.5
(among other integer value
\theta
which will be discussed next). For arbitrarily chosen
\theta
and
q
, the insensitivity does not occur because the symmetry is broken.

Fig. 11:
The range of
\sigma
such that
(D(\sigma)-D^{*})/D^{*}\leq 0.0001
for
\theta=1
. Notice the sharp peak at
q=0.5
, which means that the insensitivity is only observed for the special case of
\theta=1
and
q=0.5
.

III-B

Insensitivity appears for any integer
\theta
and
q=\theta-0.5

If the key reason for the read noise insensitivity is the symmetry, it is natural to expect the insensitivity to occur when quanta exposure
\theta
is any integer and when the threshold
q
is
\theta-0.5
. The requirement of an integer
\theta
is that the Poisson random variable is symmetric when
\theta
is an integer. This is due to a standard approximation of Poisson using Gaussian:

Lemma 4
(Gaussian approximation of Poisson)

For large
\theta
(i.e.,
\theta\gg 1
), it holds that

p_{X}(x)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\theta^{x}e^{-\theta}}{x!}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\theta}}e^{-\frac{(x-\theta)^{2}}{2\theta}}.

(35)

See
[
28
]
for proof. If
\theta
is not an integer, the symmetry of the Poisson is broken and so the insensitivity will not appear.

When
\theta
is an integer, choosing
q=\theta-0.5
ensures that symmetry is preserved. Define

\displaystyle\overline{q}=q+0.5,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\underline{q}=q-0.5

as the ceiling and floor operations of the threshold
q
. Then it holds that for any
\sigma\leq 0.8090
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)

\displaystyle\geq 0.999,\qquad k-\overline{q}\geq 2,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)

\displaystyle\leq 0.001,\qquad k-\underline{q}\leq 2.

(36)

The residue
D(\sigma)-D^{*}
in this case is

\displaystyle D(\sigma)-D^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)-\mathcal{G}_{0}(k)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\underline{q}-\ell)[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\underline{q}-\ell)-0]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\ell=0}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\overline{q}+\ell)[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\overline{q}+\ell)-1].

(37)

Since
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\underline{q}-\ell)\approx\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\overline{q}+\ell)
for large
\theta
, it follows that the residue is simplified to

\displaystyle D(\sigma)-D^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\overline{q}+\ell)\Big[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\overline{q}+\ell)+\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\underline{q}-\ell)-1\Big].

Again, by the symmetry of the Gaussian CDF that
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\overline{q}+\ell)=1-\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\underline{q}-\ell)
for
q=\theta-0.5
, it follows that

\displaystyle D(\sigma)-D^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(\overline{q}+\ell)\underset{\approx 0}{\underbrace{\Big[\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\overline{q}+\ell)+\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(\underline{q}-\ell)-1\Big]}}.

Figure
12
shows an example where the quanta exposure is
\theta=10
and the threshold is
q=9.5
. The read noise in this example is
\sigma=0.8
. For such a large quanta exposure
\theta
, the Poisson random variable is approximately a Gaussian with symmetric probability masses. The ideal bit density
D^{*}
is calculated by summing the Poisson masses over the region highlighted in pink, whereas the actual bit density
D(\sigma)
is calculated by summing the Poisson masses weighted by the Gaussian CDF (over the entire index set
k=0,1,\ldots
). Because of the symmetry,
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(9)+\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(10)=1
and
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(8)+\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(11)=1
. Thus it can be visually justified that the actual bit density will be the same as the ideal bit density. Similar arguments hold for other integer valued
\theta
. For small integer
\theta
(such as
\theta=1,2,3
), the clipping near the origin needs to be taken care of but those are minor.

Fig. 12:
The behavior of the Poisson part
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(k)
and the Gaussian part
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma}(k)
for
\sigma=0.8
and
\theta=10
. For such a large quanta exposure
\theta=10
, the Poisson mass is symmetric. If one chooses
q=\theta-0.5=9.5
, the symmetry of the Gaussian CDF and the symmetry of the Poisson mass will make the bit density to become insensitive to the read noise.

IV
Conclusion

The insensitivity of the bit density of a 1-bit quanta image sensor is analyzed. It was found that for a quanta exposure
\theta=1
and an analog voltage threshold
q=0.5
, the bit density
D
is nearly a constant whenever the read noise satisfies the condition
\sigma\leq 0.4419
. The proof is derived by exploiting the symmetry of the Gaussian cumulative distribution function, and the symmetry of the Poisson probability mass function at the threshold
k=0.5
. An approximation scheme is introduced to provide a simplified estimate where
\sigma\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.4
.

In general, the analysis shows that the insensitivity of the bit density is more of a (very) special case of the 1-bit quantized Poisson-Gaussian statistics. Insensitivity can be observed when the quanta exposure
\theta
is an integer
and
the threshold is
q=\theta-0.5
. As soon as the pair
(\theta,q)
deviates from this configuration, the insensitivity will no longer appear.
