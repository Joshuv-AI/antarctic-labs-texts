Multiple orthogonal polynomials, d d -orthogonal polynomials, production matrices, and branched continued fractions
Alan D. Sokal

Abstract
I analyze an unexpected connection between multiple orthogonal polynomials, d d -orthogonal polynomials, production matrices and branched continued fractions. This work can be viewed as a partial extension of Viennot’s combinatorial theory of orthogonal polynomials to the case where the production matrix is lower-Hessenberg but is not necessarily tridiagonal.



Key Words:

Multiple orthogonal polynomial,
d
-orthogonal polynomial,
production matrix, branched continued fraction,
lower-Hessenberg matrix, Łukasiewicz path.

Mathematics Subject Classification (MSC 2010) codes:

42C05 (Primary);
05A15, 05A19, 15A24, 15A99, 15B99, 30B70, 30E05, 33C45, 41A21 (Secondary).

1
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to point out, and then analyze in detail,
an unexpected connection between multiple orthogonal polynomials
and
d
-orthogonal polynomials on the one hand,
and production matrices and branched continued fractions on the other
— objects that arose over the past few decades
in the special-functions and enumerative-combinatorics communities,
respectively.
It is appropriate to begin, therefore,
by explaining briefly each of these four concepts.

Multiple orthogonal polynomials

[
5
,
51
,
75
]

[
36
, Chapter 23]

are a generalization of conventional orthogonal polynomials

[
72
,
14
,
36
]

in which the polynomials satisfy orthogonality relations
with respect to several measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}

rather than just one.
Multiple orthogonal polynomials first arose in Hermite–Padé approximation

[
52
, Chapter 4]

[
73
]
;
they have applications to number theory

[
67
,
68
,
74
]
,
random matrices
[
42
,
43
,
44
]

and nonintersecting random paths

[
41
,
42
,
43
]
,
among other fields.
See Section
2.1
for a brief summary.

Closely related to multiple orthogonal polynomials
are the so-called
d
-orthogonal polynomials.
A sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of monic polynomials
is said to be
d
-orthogonal
[
77
,
50
]

with respect to a sequence
\Gamma_{0},\ldots,\Gamma_{d-1}
of linear forms
in case
{\Gamma_{k}(x^{\ell}\,P_{n}(x))=0}
whenever
n>d\ell+k
.
(To avoid trivialities, it is also usually required that

{\Gamma_{k}(x^{\ell}\,P_{n}(x))\neq 0}
when
n=d\ell+k
.)
For
d=1
this reduces to the ordinary concept of orthogonality.
It turns out that the sequence of multiple orthogonal polynomials of type II
taken along the so-called stepline
is
d
-orthogonal (for
d=r
) with respect to
the linear forms associated to the measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
.

Production matrices
[
23
,
24
]

have become in recent years an important tool in enumerative combinatorics
(see Section
2.2
for a brief summary).
In the special case of a tridiagonal production matrix,
this construction goes back to Stieltjes’
[
70
,
71
]

work on continued fractions:
the production matrix of a classical S-fraction or J-fraction is tridiagonal.
Moreover, the classical J-fraction and tridiagonal production matrix
associated to the moment sequence of a measure
\mu

are closely related to the sequence of orthogonal polynomials
associated to
\mu
.
This connection was comprehensively investigated
by Viennot
[
80
,
81
]
in the early 1980s,
who developed a general combinatorial theory of orthogonal polynomials,
building on Flajolet’s
[
31
]
combinatorial theory of
continued fractions.
Our work here can be viewed as a partial extension of Viennot’s theory
to the case where the production matrix is lower-Hessenberg
(i.e. vanishes above the first superdiagonal)
but is not necessarily tridiagonal.
Indeed, this extension was already begun by Viennot himself

[
80
, sections III.5 and V.6]
.

Various types of branched continued fractions have been introduced
in the analysis literature

[
22
,
10
,
11
]

[
49
, pp. 274–280, 285]

[
20
, p. 28]
,
but we are not concerned here with these.
Rather, we are concerned with the branched continued fractions
that have been introduced by combinatorialists
and whose Taylor coefficients are the generating polynomials
for selected types of lattice paths,
generalizing the work of Flajolet
[
31
]

on classical continued fractions.
This investigation was also initiated
by Viennot
[
80
, section V.6]
,
who briefly considered the branched continued fractions
(
fractions multicontinuées
)
generated by Łukasiewicz paths.
This work was carried forward in the Ph.D. theses of
Roblet
[
59
]
and Varvak
[
78
]
.
Further applications were made by
Gouyou-Beauchamps
[
33
]
and Drake
[
28
]
.
Subsequently, Albenque and Bouttier
[
4
]

introduced the branched continued fractions generated by
m
-Dyck paths
and proved many interesting results about them.
Most recently, Pétréolle, Sokal and Zhu
[
56
]

carried out a comprehensive analysis of the branched continued fractions
associated to
m
-Dyck,
m
-Schröder and Łukasiewicz paths,
with emphasis on questions related to total positivity;
see also
[
55
,
21
]
for further applications.
For these branched continued fractions, the production matrix
is lower-Hessenberg but not (except in the classical cases) tridiagonal.
See Section
2.4
for a brief summary.

Finally, let us mention the remarkable Ph.D. thesis of Drake
[
27
]
,
who initiated the combinatorial theory of multiple orthogonal polynomials
and who foresaw the link with branched continued fractions

[
27
, p. 1]
.
Our work here can be viewed as an extension,
and to some extent a completion, of his.

Throughout this paper, we fix a commutative ring
(with identity element
1\neq 0
)
R
:
we will use sequences and matrices with entries in
R
,
and polynomials and formal power series with coefficients in
R
.
The analyst reader should feel free to imagine,
without too much loss of generality, that
R={\mathbb{R}}
.
However, in applications of this formalism there will often be parameters,
and we will usually prefer to treat these parameters as
algebraic indeterminates
{\bm{\xi}}
; then
R
will be either
the ring
{\mathbb{R}}[{\bm{\xi}}]
of polynomials in these indeterminates
or the field
{\mathbb{R}}({\bm{\xi}})
of rational functions in these indeterminates.

In particular, to any (positive or signed) measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}

that has finite moments of all orders, there is canonically associated
a linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}
on the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
,
defined by
{\mathcal{L}}(x^{n})=\int\!x^{n}\,d\mu(x)
.
It is well known
[
14
]
that the theory of orthogonal polynomials
(or at least the simplest parts of it)
can be expressed entirely in terms of this linear functional
— or equivalently, in terms of the sequence of moments
a_{n}={\mathcal{L}}(x^{n})
—
without reference to the measure
\mu
.
We shall adopt this approach here, and also replace
{\mathbb{R}}

by an arbitrary commutative ring
R
.

The plan of this paper is as follows:
In Section
2
we collect some basic definitions and facts
concerning multiple orthogonal polynomials, production matrices
and branched continued fractions.
In Section
3
we use the theory of production matrices
to demonstrate some very simple relations between
sequences of monic polynomials, the linear recurrences they satisfy,
and their dual sequences of linear functionals.
We also generalize Viennot’s
[
80
]
formula
for the expectation of products of orthogonal polynomials.
In Section
4

we analyze sequences of monic polynomials that are orthogonal
to a sequence of linear functionals.
In Section
5
we show how this theory
applies to ordinary orthogonal polynomials,
and in Section
6

we apply it to multiple orthogonal polynomials.
Finally, in Section
7
we examine some concrete examples.
In the Appendix we prove a basic result concerning

LU
factorization for matrices over a commutative ring.

,

2.1
Multiple orthogonal polynomials

We begin by giving a brief introduction to the theory of
multiple orthogonal polynomials,
following
[
36
, Chapter 23]

but making a few comments about “algebraizing” the theory
to allow coefficients in an arbitrary commutative ring
R
.
We limit attention to the multiple orthogonal polynomials of type II,
since these are the only ones that will arise in the remainder of the paper.
(I leave it to others to investigate whether there is any analogue
of the connections discussed here for the
multiple orthogonal polynomials of type I.)

Fix an integer
r\geq 1
,
and let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
be positive measures on the real line
with finite moments of all orders.
We use multi-indices
{\mathbf{n}}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}

and write
|{\mathbf{n}}|=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}
.
The
multiple orthogonal polynomial of type II

for the multi-index
{\mathbf{n}}
is the degree-
|{\mathbf{n}}|
monic polynomial

P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)=x^{|{\mathbf{n}}|}+\ldots\,
satisfying
the orthogonality relations

\int\!x^{k}\,P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)\,d\mu_{j}(x)\;=\;0\quad\hbox{for all $1\leq j\leq r$ and $0\leq k\leq n_{j}-1$}\;,

(2.1)

whenever such a polynomial exists and is unique.
The equations (
2.1
) give a system of
|{\mathbf{n}}|

linear equations for the
|{\mathbf{n}}|
non-leading coefficients of
the polynomial
P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)
;
the multi-index
{\mathbf{n}}
is said to be
normal

whenever the solution exists and is unique.
Note that the coefficient matrix of this system is the
transpose of the
|{\mathbf{n}}|\times|{\mathbf{n}}|
matrix

{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{n}}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}H^{(1)}_{|{\mathbf{n}}|,n_{1}}&H^{(2)}_{|{\mathbf{n}}|,n_{2}}&\cdots&H^{(r)}_{|{\mathbf{n}}|,n_{r}}\end{pmatrix}

(2.2)

where
H^{(j)}_{M,N}
is the
M\times N
Hankel matrix
of the moments of
\mu_{j}
: that is,

H^{(j)}_{M,N}=(m^{(j)}_{r+s})_{0\leq r\leq M-1,\,0\leq s\leq N-1}

where

m^{(j)}_{k}\;=\;\int\!x^{k}\,d\mu_{j}(x)\;.

(2.3)

Therefore, the multi-index
{\mathbf{n}}
is normal
if and only if
\det{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{n}}\neq 0
.

A system of measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
is said to be
perfect

in case all
{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
are normal.
Several general sufficient conditions for a system to be perfect are known
(Angelesco systems, AT systems, Nikishin systems, …):
see
[
36
, Chapter 23]

[
75
]
.
We shall henceforth restrict attention to perfect systems.

The orthogonality conditions (
2.1
)
can be trivially re-expressed in terms of
the linear forms
{\mathcal{L}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathcal{L}}^{(r)}

associated to the measures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r}
,
which are defined by
{\mathcal{L}}^{(j)}(x^{n})=\int\!x^{n}\,d\mu_{j}(x)
:
it suffices to replace
m^{(j)}_{k}
by
{\mathcal{L}}^{(j)}(x^{k})
.
Moreover, from this point of view,
the measures
\mu_{j}
need not be positive measures;
indeed, the linear forms
{\mathcal{L}}^{(j)}

need not come from (signed) measures at all.
Provided that one can show, one way or another,
that all
{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
are normal,
the multiple orthogonal polynomials are well-defined.

Having done this, we can go farther and “algebraize” the theory
by considering polynomials with coefficients in an arbitrary commutative ring
(with identity element
1\neq 0
)
R
, rather than just
R={\mathbb{R}}
.
We fix linear forms
{\mathcal{L}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathcal{L}}^{(r)}

on the polynomial ring
R[x]
,
and define “moments”
m^{(j)}_{k}={\mathcal{L}}^{(j)}(x^{k})
;
then the multi-index
{\mathbf{n}}
is normal
if and only if
\det{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{n}}
is an invertible element of the ring
R
.

Let us now make a simple but important observation.
Fix a multi-index
{\mathbf{n}}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
,
and suppose that the polynomial
P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)
satisfies the
orthogonality relations (
2.1
)
with respect to some family of (not necessarily positive)
measures
{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r})
.
Then
P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)
also satisfies the
orthogonality relations (
2.1
)
with respect to any family of (not necessarily positive)
measures
{\bm{\mu}}^{\prime}=(\mu^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mu^{\prime}_{r})

where
\mu^{\prime}_{i}
is any linear combination of
\{\mu_{j}\colon n_{j}\geq n_{i}\}
.
In particular, if
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\ldots\geq n_{r}
,
then we can take
\mu^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}c_{ij}\mu_{j}

for any lower-triangular matrix
C=(c_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
.
That is,
\mu^{\prime}_{i}
is an arbitrary linear combination of

\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{i}
.
This observation will play an important role in what follows
(see Section
4
ff.).

The collection
(P_{\mathbf{n}}(x))_{{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}}
of
(monic) multiple orthogonal polynomials
of type II satisfies a variety of recurrences, generalizing the well-known
three-term recurrence for conventional orthogonal polynomials.
Here is one
[
36
, Theorem 23.1.7
et seq.
]
:
We denote by
{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
the multi-index with entry 1 in position
k

and 0 elsewhere.
For a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we write

{\mathbf{s}}^{(\pi)}_{j}={\mathbf{e}}_{\pi(1)}+{\mathbf{e}}_{\pi(2)}+\ldots+{\mathbf{e}}_{\pi(j)}

for
1\leq j\leq r
.
Then there exist real numbers
a_{{\mathbf{n}},0}^{(k)}

(
{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
,
1\leq k\leq r
)
and
a_{{\mathbf{n}},j}^{(\pi)}

(
{\mathbf{n}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
,
\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}
,
1\leq j\leq r
) such that

x\,P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)\;=\;P_{{\mathbf{n}}+{\mathbf{e}}_{k}}(x)\>+\>a_{{\mathbf{n}},0}^{(k)}\,P_{\mathbf{n}}(x)\>+\>\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{{\mathbf{n}},j}^{(\pi)}\,P_{{\mathbf{n}}-{\mathbf{s}}^{(\pi)}_{j}}(x)\;,

(2.4)

with the convention that
P_{{\mathbf{n}}-{\mathbf{s}}^{(\pi)}_{j}}(x)=0

whenever one or more of the entries in
{\mathbf{n}}-{\mathbf{s}}^{(\pi)}_{j}
is negative.
(Note that the coefficients
a_{{\mathbf{n}},j}^{(\pi)}
do not depend on
k
,
and the coefficients
a_{{\mathbf{n}},0}^{(k)}
do not depend on
\pi
.
But we will never use this fact.)

Now let
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots
be an infinite sequence
of elements of
\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
and define a sequence
({\mathbf{n}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of multi-indices in
{\mathbb{N}}^{r}

by
{\mathbf{n}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbf{e}}_{j_{i}}
.
These multi-indices satisfy
|{\mathbf{n}}_{k}|=k

and describe an increasing nearest-neighbor path in
{\mathbb{N}}^{r}

in which the
i
th step is along direction
j_{i}
.
Now let
{\widehat{P}}_{k}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}P_{{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}(x)

be the multiple orthogonal polynomial of type II along this path in
{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
.
It then follows from (
2.4
)
that the singly-indexed sequence
({\widehat{P}}_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

satisfies an
(r+2)
-term recurrence of the form

x{\widehat{P}}_{n}(x)\;=\;\sum_{k=n-r}^{n+1}\pi_{nk}\,{\widehat{P}}_{k}(x)

(2.5)

where
\pi_{n,n+1}=1
and
\pi_{nk}=0
for
k<0
;
of course the coefficients
\pi_{nk}
depend
on the choice of nearest-neighbor path.
The recurrence (
2.5
)
will play a central role in the remainder of this paper.
Please observe that the coefficients
\pi_{nk}
in this recurrence
can be collected into a matrix
\Pi=(\pi_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}

that is unit-lower-Hessenberg and
(r,1)
-banded:
that is,
\pi_{nk}=0
if
k>n+1
or
k<n-r
, and
\pi_{n,n+1}=1
.

A particularly important role is played by
the multi-indices
{\mathbf{n}}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
lying on the
stepline
:
this is the near-diagonal sequence starting at
(0,0,\ldots,0)

and following the path

(n,n,\ldots,n)\to(n+1,n,\ldots,n)\to(n+1,n+1,\ldots,n)\to\ldots\to(n+1,n+1,\ldots,n+1)\to\ldots\;.

In other words, we define a singly-indexed sequence
({\widetilde{P}}_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

by

{\widetilde{P}}_{n}(x)\;=\;P_{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})}(x)\quad\hbox{where }n_{i}\>=\>\Bigl\lfloor{n+r-i\over r}\Bigr\rfloor\;\;\hbox{for }1\leq i\leq r\;.

(2.6)

The stepline polynomials
({\widetilde{P}}_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

are a special case of the nearest-neighbor-path polynomials

({\widehat{P}}_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
,
so they satisfy an
(r+2)
-term recurrence
of the form (
2.5
).

2.2
Production matrices

In this subsection we give a brief introduction to the theory of
production matrices
[
23
,
24
]
;
see also
[
65
]

[
56
, sections 8.1 and 9.2]

for further discussion.
In the general theory, the production matrix can be any row-finite
or column-finite matrix. Here, however, we shall restrict attention
to production matrices that are unit-lower-Hessenberg.
Also, in the general theory the production matrix is usually called
P
;
but here we shall call it
\Pi
in order to avoid confusion with the
sequence of polynomials
P_{n}(x)
.

So let
\Pi=(\pi_{ij})_{i,j\geq 0}
be a unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix
(indexed by
{\mathbb{N}}
) with entries in a commutative ring
R
:
that is,
\pi_{i,i+1}=1
and
\pi_{ij}=0
for
j>i+1
.
Then let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be the matrix defined by

a_{nk}=(\Pi^{n})_{0k}
.
It is easy to see that
A
is unit-lower-triangular,
i.e.
a_{nn}=1
and
a_{nk}=0
for
k>n
.
Writing out the matrix multiplications explicitly, we have

a_{nk}\;=\;\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1}}\pi_{0i_{1}}\,\pi_{i_{1}i_{2}}\,\pi_{i_{2}i_{3}}\,\cdots\,\pi_{i_{n-2}i_{n-1}}\,\pi_{i_{n-1}k}\;,

(2.7)

so that
a_{nk}
is the total weight for all
n
-step walks in
{\mathbb{N}}

from
i_{0}=0
to
i_{n}=k
, in which the weight of a walk is the
product of the weights of its steps, and a step from
i
to
j

gets a weight
\pi_{ij}
.
(Since
\Pi
is lower-Hessenberg, these are
Łukasiewicz walks
,
i.e. the allowed steps are
i\to j
with
0\leq j\leq i+1
.)
Yet another equivalent formulation is to define the entries
a_{nk}

by the recurrence

a_{nk}\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{n-1,i}\,\pi_{ik}\qquad\hbox{for $n\geq 1$}

(2.8)

with the initial condition
a_{0k}=\delta_{0k}
.
We shall call
\Pi
the
production matrix

and
A
the
output matrix
, and we write
A={\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)
.

These definitions can be given a compact matrix formulation.
Let
\Delta
be the matrix with 1 on the superdiagonal and 0 elsewhere,
i.e.
\Delta_{n,n+1}=1
and
\Delta_{nk}=0
for
k\neq n+1

(of course it is unit-lower-Hessenberg).
Then for any matrix
M
with rows indexed by
{\mathbb{N}}
,
the product
\Delta M
is simply
M
with its zeroth row removed
and all other rows shifted upwards.
(Some authors use the notation
\overline{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\Delta M
.)
The recurrence (
2.8
) can then be written as

\Delta A\;=\;A\Pi\;.

(2.9)

Since
A
is unit-lower-triangular, it is invertible,
so (
2.9
) is equivalent to

\Pi\;=\;A^{-1}\Delta A\;.

(2.10)

Conversely, it is not difficult to see that
for any unit-lower-triangular matrix
A
,
the matrix
A^{-1}\Delta A
is unit-lower-Hessenberg.
It therefore follows that for each unit-lower-triangular matrix
A
,
there is a unique unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix
\Pi

such that
A={\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)
,
and it is given by
\Pi=A^{-1}\Delta A
.

Now let
B=A^{-1}
be the inverse of
A

(which is of course also unit-lower-triangular).
We then have a one-to-one correspondence between
unit-lower-Hessenberg matrices
\Pi
,
unit-lower-triangular matrices
A

and unit-lower-triangular matrices
B
, defined by

A\>=\>{\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)\>=\>B^{-1}\>,\quad B\>=\>{\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)^{-1}\>=\>A^{-1}\>,\quad\Pi\>=\>A^{-1}\Delta A\>=\>B\Delta B^{-1}\;.

(2.11)

In Section
3
we will see how the matrices
A
,
B
and
\Pi

arise in different characterizations of sequences of monic polynomials.

Remarks.

1. Production matrices are nowadays widely used in enumerative combinatorics:
thus, for instance, the entry for a triangular array
in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
[
53
]

often gives its production matrix.

2. Several subclasses of lower-Hessenberg production matrices
are of especial combinatorial interest:

•

Tridiagonal production matrices correspond to
Motzkin walks

(i.e. the allowed steps are
i\to j
with
j\in\{i-1,i,i+1\}
)
and thence to classical J-fractions
[
31
]
,
as will be explained in Section
2.3
.

•

Toeplitz lower-Hessenberg production matrices generate
Bell-subgroup
Riordan arrays
; and more generally,
1-almost-Toeplitz lower-Hessenberg production matrices
(i.e. lower-Hessenberg matrices that are Toeplitz
except for the zeroth column) generate Riordan arrays.
See
[
61
,
69
,
8
,
62
]

for introductions to Riordan arrays,
and
[
23
,
34
,
65
]
for the just-quoted theorems
on their production matrices.

•

Lower-Hessenberg production matrices of the form

\pi_{nk}=(n!/k!)\,(z_{n-k}+ka_{n-k+1})

generate
exponential Riordan arrays

[
8
, pp. 217–218]

[
55
, Theorem 8.2]
.

3. When the commutative ring
R
is equipped with a partial order,
production matrices are also a powerful tool for attacking problems
related to total positivity
[
65
]
.
In particular, the total positivity of the production matrix
\Pi

is a sufficient (but far from necessary) condition for the
total positivity of its output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)

and for the Hankel-total positivity of the zeroth-column sequence
of
{\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)
.
See
[
65
]

[
56
, sections 8.1 and 9.2]

[
55
,
21
,
64
]

for precise statements, proofs, and further discussion
and applications.

\blacksquare

2.3
Classical continued fractions

As preparation for the discussion of branched continued fractions
in Section
2.4
,
as well as for some applications later in this paper,
we begin by giving a very brief review of selected aspects of the theory
of classical continued fractions (J-fractions and S-fractions).
We will follow the notation and terminology used nowadays by
combinatorialists
[
31
]
,
as this is the most appropriate for our work;
but we will also point out the translation to the formalism
employed in the classical analysis literature
on continued fractions

[
54
,
82
,
38
,
49
,
20
]

and the moment problem

[
71
,
63
,
1
,
3
,
60
]
.

We shall consider continued fractions of either Stieltjes (S) type,

f(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;,

(2.12)

or Jacobi (J) type,

f(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}t-\cfrac{\beta_{1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;.

(2.13)

Here these expressions are to be interpreted as
formal power series in the indeterminate
t
;
we do
not
wish to address questions of convergence.
Thus, the continued-fraction coefficients
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,

{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}=(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 0}

are sequences in a commutative ring
R
,
and they determine the sequence
{\bm{a}}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of Taylor coefficients
by formal expansion of the continued fraction.
Indeed, it is conceptually simplest to consider

{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}}
as algebraic indeterminates;
then the
a_{n}
are polynomials with integer coefficients
in these indeterminates:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S_{n}({\bm{\alpha}})\,t^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}J_{n}({\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}})\,t^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}t-\cfrac{\beta_{1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}

(2.15)

We call
S_{n}({\bm{\alpha}})
the
Stieltjes–Rogers polynomials
,
and
J_{n}({\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}})
the
Jacobi–Rogers polynomials
.

In a seminal 1980 paper, Flajolet
[
31
]

gave a combinatorial interpretation of the Stieltjes–Rogers
and Jacobi–Rogers polynomials in terms of lattice paths.
We recall that a
Motzkin path
of length
n

is a path in the right quadrant
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
,
starting at
(0,0)
and ending at
(n,0)
,
using steps
(1,1)
[“rise”],
(1,0)
[“level step”]
and
(1,-1)
[“fall”].
More generally, a
Motzkin path at level
\bm{k}

is a path in
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq k}
,
starting at
(0,k)
and ending at
(n,k)
,
using the same steps.
A Motzkin path is called a
Dyck path
if it has no level steps;
obviously a Dyck path must have even length.

Theorem 2.1

(Flajolet
[
31
]
)
.

(a)

The Jacobi–Rogers polynomial

is the generating polynomial for Motzkin paths of length
n
,
in which each rise gets weight 1,
each level step at height
i
gets weight
\gamma_{i}
,
and each fall from height
i
gets weight
\beta_{i}
.

(b)

The Stieltjes–Rogers polynomial

is the generating polynomial for Dyck paths of length
2n
,
in which each rise gets weight 1
and each fall from height
i
gets weight
\alpha_{i}
.

Proof

[
31
]
.
(a) For each
k\geq 0
, let
f_{k}(t)
be the generating function
for Motzkin paths at level
k
(of arbitrary length)
in which each rise gets weight 1,
each level step at height
i
gets weight
\gamma_{i}
,
each fall from height
i
gets weight
\beta_{i}
,
and each step of any kind gets an additional weight
t
.
It is a formal power series in the indeterminate
t
,
with coefficients that are polynomials in
{\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}}
.

Now let
{\mathcal{P}}
be any Motzkin path at level
k
;
and if it is of nonzero length,
split it at its first return to height

,
yielding

.
Then

is either a single level step at height

or else a path of the form

is a rise

is an arbitrary Motzkin path at level

and

is a fall

.
Furthermore,

is an arbitrary Motzkin path at level

We thus deduce the functional equation

f_{k}(t)\;=\;1\>+\>\gamma_{k}t\,f_{k}(t)\>+\>\beta_{k+1}t^{2}\,f_{k+1}(t)\,f_{k}(t)

(2.16)

or equivalently

f_{k}(t)\;=\;{1\over 1\>-\>\gamma_{k}t\>-\>\beta_{k+1}t^{2}\,f_{k+1}(t)}\;.

(2.17)

Iterating (

), we see immediately that

is given by the continued fraction

f_{k}(t)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{k}t-\cfrac{\beta_{k+1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{k+1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{k+2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}

(2.18)

and in particular that

).

(b) This follows from part (a) by setting

,
renaming
{\bm{\beta}}
as
{\bm{\alpha}}
, and renaming

as

.

\square

3.1
Linear functionals

Let
R[x]
be the ring of polynomials in one indeterminate
x
,
with coefficients in
R
; it is an
R
-module.
(If
R
is a field, then
R[x]
is a vector space over
R
.)
A
linear functional
(more precisely, an
R
-linear functional)
on
R[x]
is a map
{\mathcal{L}}\colon R[x]\to R

satisfying
{\mathcal{L}}(ap(x)+bq(x))=a{\mathcal{L}}(p(x))+b{\mathcal{L}}(q(x))

for all
a,b\in R
and
p(x),q(x)\in R[x]
.
To each linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}\colon R[x]\to R

there is naturally associated a sequence
(\ell_{n})_{n\geq 0}

of elements of
R
, defined by
\ell_{n}={\mathcal{L}}(x^{n})
.
And conversely, to every sequence
(\ell_{n})_{n\geq 0}

there is associated a unique linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}

satisfying
{\mathcal{L}}(x^{n})=\ell_{n}
,
namely
{\mathcal{L}}(\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}x^{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}c_{n}\ell_{n}
.
We call
(\ell_{n})_{n\geq 0}
the
moment sequence

of the linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Now let
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of such linear functionals.
We form the matrix
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
whose columns are the
moment sequences of these linear functionals,
i.e.
a_{nk}={\mathcal{L}}_{k}(x^{n})
.
We call
A
the
moment matrix

for the sequence
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of linear functionals.
We say that the sequence
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is
normalized

in case the matrix
A
is unit-lower-triangular,
i.e.
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(x^{n})=0
for
n<k
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(x^{k})=1
.

3.2
Sequences of monic polynomials

By a
sequence of monic polynomials

we mean a sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of polynomials
(with coefficients in
R
)
such that
P_{n}(x)
has degree
n
and leading coefficient 1.
We can assemble the coefficients of these polynomials
into a unit-lower-triangular matrix
B=(b_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}

by writing
P_{n}(x)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}b_{nk}\,x^{k}
.
There is obviously a one-to-one correspondence between
unit-lower-triangular matrices and sequences of monic polynomials.
We call
B
the
coefficient matrix

for the sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of polynomials.

Now let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be the inverse matrix to
B
,
i.e.
A=B^{-1}
.
Then we obviously have
x^{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}a_{nk}\,P_{k}(x)
.

3.3
Duality

Let
{\mathbf{P}}=(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of monic polynomials,
and let
{\mathbf{L}}=({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of linear functionals.
We say that
{\mathbf{P}}
and
{\mathbf{L}}
are
dual
to each other
in case
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(P_{n}(x))=\delta_{kn}
for all
k,n\geq 0
.
The fundamental result concerning such duality is very simple:

Proposition 3.1

(Sequence of monic polynomials and its dual sequence
of linear functionals)
.

Given any sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of monic polynomials,
there exists a unique sequence
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of
linear functionals that satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(P_{n}(x))=\delta_{kn}
,
and it is normalized.

Conversely, given any normalized sequence
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of
linear functionals, there exists a unique sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

of monic polynomials that satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(P_{n}(x))=\delta_{kn}
.

The relation between these sequences is:
The moment matrix
A
of the sequence
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}

and the coefficient matrix
B
of the sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

are inverses of each other.

Proof.
Using
P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{nj}\,x^{j}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(x^{j})=a_{jk}
,
we see that the condition
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(P_{n}(x))=\delta_{kn}

is equivalent to the matrix equation
BA=I
.
If
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of monic polynomials,
then
B
is unit-lower-triangular, and
BA=I
has the unique solution

A=B^{-1}
; moreover,
A
is unit-lower-triangular.
And conversely, if
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is a
normalized sequence of linear functionals,
then
A
is unit-lower-triangular, and
BA=I
has the unique solution

B=A^{-1}
; moreover,
B
is unit-lower-triangular.

\square

3.4
Linear recurrence
\longleftrightarrow
production matrix

The next result, which is only slightly more complicated,
connects a sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of monic polynomials
with the unique linear recurrence (of a certain standard form) that defines it:

Proposition 3.2

(Sequence of monic polynomials and its defining recurrence)
.

Given any sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of monic polynomials,
there exists a unique unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix

\Pi=(\pi_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
such that

P_{n+1}(x)\;=\;(x-\pi_{nn})\,P_{n}(x)\>-\>\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_{nk}\,P_{k}(x)

(3.1)

or equivalently

xP_{n}(x)\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\pi_{nk}\,P_{k}(x)\;.

(3.2)

And conversely, given any unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix

\Pi=(\pi_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
,
there exists a unique sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of polynomials
satisfying (
3.1
)/(
3.2
)
with the initial condition
P_{0}(x)=1
, and it is monic.

The relation between these objects is:
The coefficient matrix
B
of the sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}

satisfies
B={\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)^{-1}
or equivalently
\Pi=B\Delta B^{-1}
.

Proof.
Let
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of monic polynomials
with coefficient matrix
B
.
Substituting
P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{nj}\,x^{j}

into (
3.2
)
and extracting the coefficient of
x^{j}
,
we see that (
3.2
) is equivalent to

b_{n,j-1}\;=\;(\Pi B)_{nj}

(3.3)

or in other words

B\Delta\;=\;\Pi B\;.

(3.4)

Since
B
is unit-lower-triangular and hence invertible,
this equation has the unique solution
\Pi=B\Delta B^{-1}
.

The converse assertion is obvious, using (
3.1
).

\square

2. The recurrence (

can also be written in vector form as
x{\mathbf{P}}=\Pi{\mathbf{P}}
,
where
{\mathbf{P}}
is the column vector whose entries are the sequence

(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
.
Iterating this, we see that

x^{r}\,{\mathbf{P}}=\Pi^{r}\,{\mathbf{P}}
for any integer

or concretely

x^{r}P_{n}(x)\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{n+r}(\Pi^{r})_{nj}\,P_{j}(x)\;.

(3.5)

It then follows that for any polynomial

(3.6)

\blacksquare

The monic polynomials
P_{n}(x)
defined by the recurrence
(
3.1
) are also the characteristic polynomials
of the leading principal submatrices of the production matrix
\Pi
.
To state this result, let us introduce notation as follows:
For any matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\geq 0}
,
we write
A_{n}=(a_{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}

for its
n\times n
leading principal submatrix,
and
\Delta_{n}(A)=\det A_{n}

for its
n\times n
leading principal minor,
with the convention
\Delta_{0}(A)=1
.
When the matrix is lower-Hessenberg,
these leading principal minors satisfy a recurrence
that is reasonably well known

[
32
, pp. 251–252]

[
84
, pp. 410–411]
,
though perhaps not as well known as it should be:

Lemma 3.3

(Leading principal minors of a lower-Hessenberg matrix)
.

The leading principal minors
\Delta_{n}(H)

of a lower-Hessenberg matrix
H=(h_{ij})_{i,j\geq 0}

satisfy the recurrence

\Delta_{n}\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-j}\,h_{n-1,j}\,\Biggl(\prod_{i=j}^{n-2}h_{i,i+1}\!\Biggr)\,\Delta_{j}\;.

(3.7)

Proof.
Laplace-expand
\det H_{n}
in the last (i.e.
(n-1)
st) row.
When row
n-1
and column
j
are deleted from
H_{n}
, what remains is

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|cccc}&&&&&&\\
&\scalebox{1.5}{$H_{j}$}&&&&\\
&&&&&&\\[-5.69054pt]
\hline\cr&&&&&&\\[-8.53581pt]
\times&\cdots&\times&h_{j,j+1}&&&\\
\times&\cdots&\times&\times&h_{j+1,j+2}&&\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\\
\times&\cdots&\times&\times&\times&\cdots&h_{n-2,n-1}\\
\end{array}\right]

(3.8)

(blank entries are zero),
so that its determinant is

\Bigl(\prod\limits_{i=j}^{n-2}h_{i,i+1}\!\Bigr)\,\Delta_{j}
.

\square

For tridiagonal matrices, (
3.7
)
becomes a three-term recurrence that is much better known

[
35
, p. 35]
.

Proposition 3.4

(Monic polynomials as characteristic polynomials of production matrix)
.

Let
\Pi=(\pi_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
be a unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix,
and let
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of monic polynomials
defined by the recurrence (
3.1
)
with the initial condition
P_{0}(x)=1
.
Then
P_{n}(x)=\det(xI-\Pi_{n})
.

Proof.
Applying (
3.7
) to the matrix
H=xI-\Pi
,
we obtain

\Delta_{n}\;=\;(x-\pi_{n-1,n-1})\Delta_{n-1}\>-\>\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\pi_{n-1,j}\,\Delta_{j}\;,

(3.9)

which matches the recurrence (
3.1
).

\square

3.5
Summary

To summarize the results obtained thus far:
There is a one-to-one correspondence between
sequences
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of monic polynomials
(with coefficient matrix
B
),
their dual sequences
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of linear functionals
(with moment matrix
A
),
and their defining linear recurrences
(
3.1
)/(
3.2
)
(with production matrix
\Pi
);
and these correspondences are given by (
2.11
).

3.6
Expectation values of products

Fix now a unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix
\Pi
:
this defines (by Proposition
3.2
)
a sequence
(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
of monic polynomials
with coefficient matrix
B={\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)^{-1}
,
which in turn defines (by Proposition
3.1
)
a dual sequence
({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of linear functionals
with moment matrix
A={\mathcal{O}}(\Pi)
.
Our goal is to find a general combinatorial or algebraic formula
for quantities of the form
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(q(x)\,P_{m}(x)\,P_{n}(x))
,
where
q(x)
is a polynomial,
in terms of the coefficients
\Pi
.
By analogy to probability theory,
we refer colloquially to quantities like

{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(q(x)\,P_{m}(x)\,P_{n}(x))

as “expectation values”.

In the tridiagonal case with
k=0
,
Viennot
[
80
, p. I-15, Proposition I.17]

found a beautiful formula for these expectation values:

Proposition 3.6

(Viennot
[
80
]
)
.

When the unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix
\Pi
is tridiagonal, we have

{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(q(x)\,P_{m}(x)\,P_{n}(x))\;=\;\pi_{10}\pi_{21}\cdots\pi_{n,n-1}\,(q(\Pi))_{mn}

(3.10)

for any polynomial
q(x)
.
In particular, when
q(x)=1
we have the orthogonality relation

{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(P_{m}(x)\,P_{n}(x))\;=\;h_{n}\,\delta_{mn}

(3.11)

with the normalizing constant
h_{n}=\pi_{10}\pi_{21}\cdots\pi_{n,n-1}
.

Please note
[
80
, p. I-15]

that the right-hand side of (
3.10
) with
q(x)=x^{r}
,
namely
\pi_{10}\pi_{21}\cdots\pi_{n,n-1}\,(\Pi^{r})_{mn}
,
can be interpreted as the total weight for Motzkin paths of length
r+m+n

from height
0\to 0
in which the first
m
steps are “up” steps
(getting weight 1) and the last
n
steps are “down” steps
(getting weight
\pi_{n,n-1}\cdots\pi_{10}
).
Now, in a Motzkin path that starts and ends at the same height,
each “up” step
i\to i+1
can be paired with a “down” step
i+1\to i
;
it follows that the weight of such a path
equals the weight of the reversed path.
These considerations show that the right-hand side of (
3.10
)
is indeed symmetric in
m\leftrightarrow n
.

Viennot
[
80
, pp. I-16–I-19]

proved Proposition
3.6

by a rather intricate combinatorial argument;
here we give a simple algebraic proof:

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Let
{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the moment sequence of
the linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
, i.e.

\ell_{n}\;=\;{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(x^{n})\;=\;a_{n0}\;=\;(\Pi^{n})_{00}\;.

(3.12)

And let
H_{\infty}({\bm{\ell}})=(\ell_{i+j})_{i,j\geq 0}

be the Hankel matrix associated to the sequence
{\bm{\ell}}
.
Since the unit-lower-Hessenberg matrix
\Pi
is tridiagonal,
it is of the form (
2.25
)
with
\gamma_{n}=\pi_{nn}
and
\beta_{n}=\pi_{n,n-1}
.
Proposition
2.2
therefore gives

H_{\infty}({\bm{\ell}})\;=\;ADA^{\rm T}

(3.13)

where
D=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(1,\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\beta_{2},\ldots)
.
Since
B=A^{-1}
, we can rewrite this as

B\,H_{\infty}({\bm{\ell}})\,B^{\rm T}\;=\;D\;,

(3.14)

or concretely

\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}b_{mi}\,b_{nj}\,\ell_{i+j}\;=\;\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}\,\delta_{mn}\;.

(3.15)

But the left-hand side of (
3.15
)
is exactly
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(P_{m}(x)\,P_{n}(x))
.
This proves (
3.11
).

If we further left-multiply (
3.14
) by
q(\Pi)
,
we obtain

q(\Pi)\,B\,H_{\infty}({\bm{\ell}})\,B^{\rm T}\;=\;q(\Pi)\,D\;,

(3.16)

or concretely

\sum_{i,j,m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(q(\Pi))_{mm^{\prime}}\,b_{m^{\prime}i}\,b_{nj}\,\ell_{i+j}\;=\;(q(\Pi))_{mn}\,\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}\;.

(3.17)

But, using (
3.6
),
we see that the left-hand side of (
3.17
)
is exactly
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(q(x)\,P_{m}(x)\,P_{n}(x))
.
This proves (
3.10
).

\square

4
Sequence of monic polynomials orthogonal to a sequence of
linear functionals

Let
{\bm{\Gamma}}=(\Gamma_{k})_{k\geq 0}

be a sequence of linear functionals on
R[x]
,
with moment matrix
\Gamma=(\gamma_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}

given by
\gamma_{nk}=\Gamma_{k}(x^{n})
.
And let
{\mathbf{P}}=(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of monic polynomials,
with unit-lower-triangular coefficient matrix
B=(b_{nj})_{n,j\geq 0}

given by
P_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{nj}\,x^{j}
.
We say that
{\mathbf{P}}
is orthogonal to
{\bm{\Gamma}}

in case each
P_{n}(x)
is orthogonal to
\Gamma_{k}
for
0\leq k\leq n-1
,
i.e.
\Gamma_{k}(P_{n}(x))=0
for
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
Since

\Gamma_{k}(P_{n}(x))\;=\;\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{nj}\,\gamma_{jk}\;=\;(B\Gamma)_{nk}\;,

(4.1)

we see that
{\mathbf{P}}
is orthogonal to
{\bm{\Gamma}}

if and only if
B\Gamma
vanishes below the diagonal,
or in other words
B\Gamma
is an upper-triangular matrix
U
,
or equivalently
\Gamma=B^{-1}U
.
We record this simple fact:

Proposition 4.1

(Orthogonality between a sequence of linear functionals and
a sequence of monic polynomials)
.

Let
{\bm{\Gamma}}=(\Gamma_{k})_{k\geq 0}

be a sequence of linear functionals on
R[x]
,
with moment matrix
\Gamma
;
and let
{\mathbf{P}}=(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of monic polynomials,
with unit-lower-triangular coefficient matrix
B
.
Then
{\mathbf{P}}
is orthogonal to
{\bm{\Gamma}}

if and only if the matrix
B\Gamma
is upper-triangular,
or equivalently if there exists an upper-triangular matrix
U

such that
\Gamma=B^{-1}U
.

We can interpret this result in two ways,
depending on whether we start from
{\bm{\Gamma}}
or from
{\mathbf{P}}
:

Starting from
{\bm{\Gamma}}
.

Suppose that the moment matrix
\Gamma

has a factorization
\Gamma=LU

where
L
is unit-lower-triangular and
U
is upper-triangular.
Then
B=L^{-1}
is the coefficient matrix
for a sequence
{\mathbf{P}}
of monic polynomials that is orthogonal to
{\bm{\Gamma}}
.
Furthermore, the sequence
{\mathbf{L}}=({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}

of linear functionals dual to
{\mathbf{P}}

(given by Proposition
3.1
)
then has moment matrix
A=B^{-1}=L
.
It follows that
\Gamma_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(U^{\rm T})_{kj}\,{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
;
and if the diagonal elements of
U
are invertible,
then
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(U^{-\rm T})_{kj}\,\Gamma_{j}
.
In other words, each
\Gamma_{k}
is a linear combination of

{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
;
and if the diagonal elements of
U
are invertible,
then each
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
is a special linear combination
of
\Gamma_{0},\ldots,\Gamma_{k}
, namely,
one that makes the first
k
elements of its moment sequence zero
and the next element 1.

Starting from
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Let
{\mathbf{P}}=(P_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of monic polynomials,
with coefficient matrix
B
.
Then there is a
canonically associated
sequence of linear functionals
with respect to which
{\mathbf{P}}
is orthogonal,
namely, the dual sequence
{\mathbf{L}}=({\mathcal{L}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
,
with moment matrix
A=B^{-1}
.
But
{\mathbf{P}}
is also orthogonal with respect to
any
sequence

{\bm{\Gamma}}=(\Gamma_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of linear functionals
whose moment matrix
\Gamma
is of the form
\Gamma=AU
,
where
U
is
any
upper-triangular matrix;
or in other words, each
\Gamma_{k}
is an arbitrary linear combination
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
.

Remark.
When
R={\mathbb{R}}
, it is often the case in applications
that
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is the moment functional of a positive measure.
But
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
for
k\geq 1

cannot
be the moment functional
of a positive measure, because the zeroth component
of its moment sequence vanishes
but the sequence is not identically zero (since the
k
th component is 1).
Analyst readers may be interested in the following problem:
Given a unit-lower-triangular matrix
A

whose zeroth column is the moment sequence of a positive measure,
find an upper-triangular matrix
U

such that every column of
\Gamma=AU

is the moment sequence of a positive measure;
or in other words, find linear combinations
\Gamma_{k}

of
{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
that are all moment functionals
of positive measures.
This problem of course has trivial solutions:
we could take all the
\Gamma_{k}
to be zero, or to be equal to
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
.
But suppose we further insist that
all the functionals
\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{1},\ldots
be linearly independent.
Then the problem seems to be nontrivial.

\blacksquare

is a linear combination of

Application to ordinary orthogonal polynomials

Let us begin by showing how the general theory from the preceding section

Fix a linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}

{\bm{\ell}}=(\ell_{n})_{n\geq 0}

\Gamma

{\bm{\Gamma}}
in case

for

is a sequence of monic orthogonal polynomials
in the usual sense associated to the linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}

[
14
, Chapter 1]

In particular, by Corollary

(b),

of

Let us now relate this to production matrices
and classical continued fractions.

[

[

where

[this follows from (
2.33
)],
while the

are given by other determinants

{\bm{\ell}}

[
80

.

7.3
Final remarks

I suspect that the foregoing examples are just the tip of the iceberg,
and that the connection between multiple orthogonal polynomials,
production matrices and branched continued fractions will be fruitful
in both directions.
For instance, using known techniques
(such as vector Pearson equations

[
26
,
9
,
76
,
17
,
18
,
47
,
48
]
)
it may be possible to devise new examples of multiple orthogonal polynomials;
these will then automatically provide a production matrix
for the sequence of moments of
\mu_{1}
;
this production matrix will in turn automatically arise from
a branched J-fraction
[
56
, Sections 4–8]
,
and in some cases this branched J-fraction may arise
from contraction of a branched S-fraction

[
56
, Propositions 7.2 and 7.6]
.
And conversely, combinatorial or algebraic methods leading to new
production matrices or branched continued fractions
may point the way to new examples of multiple orthogonal polynomials.

L=(\ell_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
be an
n\times n
lower-triangular matrix
over a commutative ring
R
;
and assume that none of the diagonal elements
\ell_{ii}

is zero or a divisor of zero.
Then for each vector
b\in R^{n}
,
the equation
Lx=b
has at most one solution
x\in R^{n}
.

Proof.
\ell_{11}x_{1}=b_{1}
has at most one solution
x_{1}
,
since
\ell_{11}
is neither zero nor a divisor of zero.
Continuing inductively, we see that

\ell_{ii}x_{i}=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\ell_{ij}x_{j}

has at most one solution
x_{i}
.

\square
