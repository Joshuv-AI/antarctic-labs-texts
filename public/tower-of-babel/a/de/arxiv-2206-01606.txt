Excess risk analysis for epistemic uncertainty with application to variational inference
Futoshi Futami, Tomoharu Iwata, Naonori Ueda, Issei Sato, Masashi Sugiyama

Abstract
Bayesian deep learning plays an important role especially for its ability evaluating epistemic uncertainty (EU). Due to computational complexity issues, approximation methods such as variational inference (VI) have been used in practice to obtain posterior distributions and their generalization abilities have been analyzed extensively, for example, by PAC-Bayesian theory; however, little analysis exists on EU, although many numerical experiments have been conducted on it. In this study, we analyze the EU of supervised learning in approximate Bayesian inference by focusing on its excess risk. First, we theoretically show the novel relations between generalization error and the widely used EU measurements, such as the variance and mutual information of predictive distribution, and derive their convergence behaviors. Next, we clarify how the objective function of VI regularizes the EU. With this analysis, we propose a new objective function for VI that directly controls the prediction performance and the EU based on the PAC-Bayesian theory. Numerical experiments show that our algorithm significantly improves the EU evaluation over the existing VI methods.



1
Introduction

As machine learning applications spread, understanding the uncertainty of predictions is becoming more important to increase our confidence in machine learning algorithms
[
5
]
. Uncertainty refers to the variability of a prediction caused by missing information. For example, in regression problems, it corresponds to the error bars in predictions; and in classification problems, it is often expressed as the class posterior probability, entropy, and mutual information
[
24
,
15
]
. There are two types of uncertainty
[
5
]
: 1) Aleatoric uncertainty (AU), which is caused by noise in the data itself, and 2) Epistemic uncertainty (EU), which is caused by a lack of training data. In particular, since EU can tell us where in the input space is yet to be learned, integrated with deep learning methods, it is used in such applications as dataset shift
[
40
]
, adversarial data detection
[
52
]
, active learning
[
23
]
, Bayesian optimization
[
22
]
, and reinforcement learning
[
26
]
.

Mathematically, AU is defined as Bayes risk, which expresses the fundamental difficulty of learning problems
[
10
,
25
,
50
]
. For EU, Bayesian inference is useful because posterior distribution updated from prior distribution can represent a lack of data
[
24
]
. In practice, measurements like the variance of the posterior predictive distribution, and associated conditional mutual information represented EU in practice
[
28
,
10
]
.

In Bayesian inference, since posterior distribution is characterized by the training data and the model using Bayes’ formula, its prediction performance and EU are determined automatically. However, due to computational issues, such exact Bayesian inference is difficult to implement; we often use approximation methods, such as variational inference (VI)
[
6
]
, especially for deep Bayesian models. Since the derived posterior distribution also depends on the properties of approximation methods, the prediction performance and EU of deep Bayesian learning are no longer automatically guaranteed through Bayes’ formula. The prediction performance has been analyzed as generalization error, for example, by PAC-Bayesian theory
[
1
]
. Since EU is also essential in practice, we must obtain a theoretical guarantee of the algorithm- and the sample- dependent non-asymptotic theory for EU, similarly to generalization error analysis.

Unfortunately, study has been limited in that direction. Traditional EU analysis has focused on the properties of the exact Bayesian posterior and predictive distributions
[
12
,
30
]
as well as large sample behaviors
[
9
]
. Since Bayesian deep learning uses approximate posterior distributions, we cannot apply such traditional EU analysis based on Bayes’ formula to Bayesian deep learning. The asymptotic theory of a sufficiently large sample may overlook an important property of the EU that is due to the lack of training data.

Recently, analysis of EU focusing on loss functions was proposed for supervised learning
[
51
,
25
]
. EU was defined as the
excess risk
obtained by subtracting the Bayes risk corresponding to the AU from the total risk. Thus, excess risk implies the loss due to insufficient data when the model is well specified. Although this approach successfully defines EU with loss functions, the following limitation still remains.
[
51
]
assume that the data generating mechanism is already known and that we can precisely evaluate Bayesian posterior and predictive distribution. A correct model is not necessarily a realistic assumption, and the assumption about an exact Bayesian posterior hampers understanding EU in approximation methods.

To address these limitations, it appears reasonable to analyze excess risk under a similar setting as PAC-Bayesian theory and apply it to EU of approximate Bayesian inference.
However, as shown in Sec.
2
, analyzing excess risk in such a way leads to impractical theoretical results, and the relations between excess risk and the widely used EU measurements remain unclear. This greatly complicates EU analysis. Because of this difficulty, to the best of our knowledge, no research exists on excess risk for EU for approximation methods.

In this paper, we propose a new theoretical analysis for EU that addresses the above limitations of these existing settings.
Our contributions are the followings:

•

We show non-asymptotic analysis for widely used EU measurements (Theorems
2
and
3
). We propose computing the Bayesian excess risk (BER) (Eq.(
9
)) and show that this excess risk equals to widely used EU measurements. Then we theoretically show the convergence behavior of BER using PAC-Bayesian theory (Eqs. (
13
)) and (
3
)).

•

Based on theoretical analysis, we give a new interpretation of the existing VI that clarifies how the EU is regularized (Eqs.(
3.3
) and (
3.3
)). Then we propose a novel algorithm that directly controls the prediction and the EU estimation performance simultaneously based on PAC-Bayesian theory (Eq.(
21
)). Numerical experiments suggest that our algorithm significantly improves EU evaluation over the existing VI.

2
Background of PAC-Bayesian theory and epistemic uncertainty

Here we introduce preliminaries. Such capital letters as
X
represent random variables, and such lowercase letters as
x
represent deterministic values. All the notations are summarized in Appendix
A
.

2.1
PAC-Bayesian theory

We consider a supervised setting and denote input-output pairs by
Z=(X,Y)\in\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. We assume that all the data are i.i.d. from some unknown data-generating distribution
\nu(Z)=\nu(Y|X)\nu(X)
.
Learners can access
N
training data,
\mathbb{Z}^{N}:=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})
with
Z_{n}:=(X_{n},Y_{n})
, which are generated by
\mathbb{Z}^{N}\sim\nu(Z)^{N}
. We express
\nu(Z)^{N}
as
\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})
. We express conditional distribution as
\nu(Y|X=x)
as
\nu(Y|x)
for simplicity. We introduce loss function
l:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{A}
is an action space. We express loss of action
a\in\mathcal{A}
and target variable
y
is written as
l(y,a)
. We introduce a model
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
, parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When we put a prior
p(\theta)
over
\theta
, the PAC-Bayesian theory
[
1
,
16
]
guarantees the prediction performance by focusing on the average of the loss with respect to posterior distribution
q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})\in\mathcal{Q}
.
\mathcal{Q}
is a family of distributions and
q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})
is not restricted to Bayesian posterior distribution. In this work we consider the log loss and the squared loss. For the log loss, we consider model
p(y|x,\theta)
, and the loss is given as
l(y,p(y|x,\theta))=-\ln p(y|x,\theta)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is probability distributions.
For the squared loss, we use model
f_{\theta}(x)
and
l(y,f_{\theta}(x))=|y-f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}
, where
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}
.

PAC-Bayesian theory provides a guarantee for the generalization of test error
\displaystyle R^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}l(Y,f_{\theta}(X))
and training error
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}r^{l}(\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}l(Y_{n},f_{\theta}(X_{n}))
. A typical PAC-Bayesian error bound takes form
R^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}r^{l}(\mathbb{Z}^{N})+\mathrm{Gen}^{l}(\mathbb{Z}^{N})
.
\mathrm{Gen}^{l}(\mathbb{Z}^{N})
is called
generalization error
. Under suitable assumptions
[
1
]
,
\mathrm{Gen}^{l}(\mathbb{Z}^{N})
is upper-bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{\alpha})
for
\alpha\in(1/2,1]
. In many cases, it depends on the complexity of the posterior distribution, such as Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})|p(\theta))
. When
\mathrm{Gen}^{l}(\mathbb{Z}^{N})=\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})|p(\theta))/\lambda+\mathrm{c}
, where
\lambda
and
c
are positive constants, given training data
\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N}
, we get a posterior distribution for the prediction by

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.95}{$\displaystyle\hat{q}(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})=\mathop{\mathrm{arg~min}}\limits_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})\in\mathcal{Q}}r(\mathbb{z}^{N})+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})|p(\theta))}{\lambda}$}.

(1)

When the log loss and
\lambda=N
is used, this minimization is closely related to variational inference (VI) in Bayesian inference. See
[
16
]
for details.

Under additional moderate assumptions, using
\hat{q}(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})
for the test error, we can derive the following
excess risk (ER)
bound from the PAC-Bayesian generalization bound
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.95}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{ER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N},\theta^{*}):=R^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-R^{l}(Y|X,\theta^{*})\leq C_{1}\frac{\ln N}{N^{\alpha}}$},

(2)

where
R^{l}(Y|X,\theta^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}l(Y,f_{\theta^{*}}(X))
and
\theta^{*}=\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}l(Y,f_{\theta}(X))
.
Constant
C_{1}
depends only on the problem. Since we aim to analyze EU, we do not further discuss the details of the PAC-Bayesian bound. See Appendix
C.2
for the explicit conditions of this bound.

Although PAC-Bayesian theory focuses on the average test error over posterior distribution, we use predictive distribution for predictions in Bayesian inference. Thus we define
Prediction Risk (PR)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{PR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(X)).

(3)

When the loss is log loss,
\mathrm{PR}^{\log}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}\log p^{q}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
where
p^{q}(y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}):=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}p(y|x,\theta)
is the approximate predictive distribution. Thus,
\mathrm{PR}^{\log}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
corresponds to the log loss of the predictive distribution, which is commonly used in the analysis of Bayesian inference
[
48
,
49
]
.

2.2
Epistemic uncertainty measurements

Here, we introduce widely used EU measurements in approximate Bayesian inference. For the log loss, conditioned on
(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, the approximate mutual information has been widely used for uncertainty estimation
[
10
]
:

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=H[p^{q}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})]-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}H[p(Y|x,\theta)],

(4)

where
H[p^{q}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})]:=-\mathbb{E}_{p^{q}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})}\log p^{q}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
is the entropy of the approximate predictive distribution and
\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}H[p(Y|x,\theta)]
is the conditional entropy.
I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
has been used in Bayesian experimental design
[
13
]
and reinforcement learning
[
10
]
. Note that by taking the expectation, we have
I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X=x)}I_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})}(\theta;Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
.

In the case of squared loss, the variance of the model is often used for EU. This is a common practice in VI, Monte Carlo (MC) dropout
[
28
]
, and deep ensemble methods
[
29
]
. Conditioned on
(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, it is written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}(f_{\theta}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x))^{2}.

(5)

Although Eqs.(
4
) and (
5
) are widely used in application, there have been limited theoretical study for them as discussed in Sec.
1
.

2.3
Excess risk analysis and epistemic uncertainty

Recently, the analysis of EU based on excess risk was proposed
[
51
]
.
The key idea of this analysis is to assume that our statistical model
p(y|x,\theta)
is correct and address the average performance of this model by assuming a prior distribution over
\theta
with distribution
p(\theta)
. Specifically, the joint distribution of the training data, the test data, and parameter of the model is given as
p_{B}(\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z,\theta):=p(\theta)\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(Y_{n}|X_{n},\theta)\nu(X_{n})p(Y|X,\theta)\nu(X)
. Under this setting, the goal of learning is to infer decision rule
\psi:\mathcal{Z}^{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
that minimizes expected loss
\mathbb{E}_{p_{B}(\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z,\theta)}[l(Y,\psi(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}))]
. They refer to this setting as Bayesian learning since we marginalize out parameter
\theta
. With this notation,
[
51
]
defined
minimum excess risk
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\inf_{\psi:\mathcal{Z}^{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{B}(\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z,\theta)}[l(Y,\psi(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}))]

\displaystyle-\inf_{\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{p_{B}(\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z,\theta)}[l(Y,\phi(\theta,X))],

(6)

where the first term is the minimum achievable risk using the training data and the second term is the Bayes risk since it uses learning rule
\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
, which takes true parameter
\theta
instead of the training data. Thus, the second term is the aleatroic uncertainty (AU) since it expresses the task’s fundamental difficulty.
Then
\mathrm{MER}
can be regarded as the EU since it is the difference between the total risk and the AU
[
51
,
18
]
.

For the log loss, the first term is
H[p(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})]
and the second term is
\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)}H[p(Y|X,\theta)]
. Thus,
\mathrm{MER}^{\log}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=I(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
, which is the conditional mutual information.
Other than the log loss, if the loss function satisfies the
\sigma^{2}
sub-Gaussian property conditioned on
(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
,
\mathrm{MER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\leq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}I(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})}
holds
[
51
]
. In many practical settings
I(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
is upper-bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\ln N/N)
. Thus, EU converges with
\mathcal{O}(\ln N/N)
under this settings. See Appendix
C
for more details.

Although this analysis successfully defined EU with rigorous theoretical analysis, the assumptions are clearly impractical since we assume that the correct model, exact Bayesian posterior, and predictive distributions are available. To extend this analysis into approximate Bayesian inference, it is tempting to combine the theory of MER with PAC-Bayesian theory where the data are generated i.i.d from
\nu(Z)
. For that extension, here we introduce the
Prediction Excess Risk (PER)
using Eq.(
3
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathrm{PR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(X))],

(7)

where the second term corresponds to the Bayes risk. Although we introduced this definition inspired by Eq.(
2.3
), it is impractical for evaluating EU. In practice, we are interested in evaluating EU using only input
x
, as shown in Eqs.(
4
) and (
5
). However, we cannot use Eq.(
7
) for that purpose since we do not know both
\nu(Y|x)
and the Bayes risk in the second term. Despite less practical definition, as shown in Sec.
3
, PER plays a fundamental role in understanding the algorithm-dependent behavior of the widely used EU measurements in Eqs.(
4
) and (
5
).

3
Analysis of epistemic uncertainty based on excess risk

In this section, we develop theories for analyzing the widely used EU measurements introduced in Sec
2.2
. We focus on the following questions.
(Q1)
The convergence behaviors of those measurements are not apparent. As the number of training data points increases, we expect these measurements to converge to zero.
(Q2)
The relationship between these measurements and the generalization error is unclear. Since these measurements depend on the training data and the algorithm, we expect some meaningful relationships must exist. All the proofs in this section are shown in Appendix
D
.

3.1
Relation between epistemic uncertainty and Bayesian excess risk

First, to connect the practical EU evaluation methods in Sec.
2.2
with the excess risk analysis in Sec.
2.3
, we introduce the approximate joint distribution of test data, training data, and parameters:

\displaystyle\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})q({\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N}})\nu(Z)\approx p^{q}(\theta,\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z):=\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})q({\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N}})\nu(X)p(Y|X,\theta).

(8)

When the log loss is used, we employ model for
p(y|x,\theta)
in Eq.(
8
). When the squared loss is used, we assume Gaussian distribution
p(y|x,\theta)=N(y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})
for some
v\in\mathbb{R}
.

When a model is well specified, that is,
\nu(y|x)=p(y|x,\theta^{*})
holds for some
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
, we expect that the predictive distribution converges to
p(y|x,\theta^{*})
and the approximation of Eq.(
8
) becomes accurate as
N
increases. We discuss the quality of this approximation in Sec.
3.2
. Under this setting, we define
Bayesian Excess risk (BER)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathrm{BPR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-\inf_{\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{p^{q}(\theta,\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z)}l(Y,\phi(\theta,X))

(9)

where
\mathrm{BPR}
is the
Bayesian Prediction Risk
defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{BPR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathbb{E}_{p^{q}(\theta,\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}f_{\theta^{\prime}}(X)),

(10)

and the second term is the Bayes risk under the approximate joint distribution of Eq.(
8
). Note that
\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
is always larger than 0; see Appendix
D.2
for details. We also show the formal definitions of BER and BPR conditioned on
(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
in Appendix
D.1
.

\mathrm{BER}
and
\mathrm{BPR}
are defined, motivated by PER, PR, and MER. The difference is the mechanism of through which the test data are generated. In
\mathrm{BER}
, we assume that our model
p(y|x,\theta)
is correct, and the parameters follow the approximate posterior distribution
q({\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}})
. Thus, the data-generating mechanism resembles the setting in Sec.
2.3
. Therefore, similar to MER, BER implies the loss due to insufficient data under the assumption that our current model
q({\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}})p(y|x,\theta)
is correct.

The next theorem elaborate this intuition and connects BER to widely used EU measurements:

Theorem 1
.

Conditioned on
(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, we express
\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
for the log loss and
\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
for the squared loss. Under the definition of Eq.(
9
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}),\quad\quad\quad\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x).

(11)

Thus, by studying BER, we can analyze the widely used EU measurements. We point out that
\mathrm{BPR}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=H[p^{q}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})]
, and the Bayes risk is given as
\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}H[p(Y|x,\theta)]
.

Remark 1
.

BER captures EU when our model is well specified. On the other hand, PER (Eq. (
7
)) represents the prediction performance, which has the relation to the quality of approximation of Eq. (
8
) under the given loss function. This intuition leads to our new VI in Sec.
3.3
.

3.2
Analysis of excess risk based on PAC-Bayesian theory

Based on the definitions introduced in Sec.
3.1
, here we develop a novel relation between BER and the generalization. First, we show the results of the squared loss. For simplicity, assume
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
. See Appendix
D.6
for
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 2
.

Conditioned on
(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, assume that a regression function is well specified, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}[Y|x]=f_{\theta^{*}}(x)
holds. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{(2)}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=\mathrm{ER}^{(2)}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N},\theta^{*})\leq R^{(2)}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}).

(12)

Furthermore assume that the PAC-Bayesian bound Eq.(
2
) holds, and then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=\mathrm{ER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N},\theta^{*})\leq\frac{C_{1}\ln N}{N^{\alpha}}.

(13)

Proof.

We use the following relation about the Jensen gap and BER:

Lemma 1
.

For any
(x,y)
and any posterior distribution conditioned on
\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle|y-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}|y-f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}.

(14)

From this lemma, the theorem follows directly.
∎

Remark 2
.

When we use a flexible model, such as a deep neural network for
f_{\theta}(x)
, assumption
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}[Y|x]=f_{\theta^{*}}(x)
holds even when we misspecify noise function
\nu(Y|x)
.

From Eq.(
12
),
\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)
clearly is a lower bound of excess risk and test error, consistent with the well-known result that the variance of the predictor often underestimates EU
[
29
]
. From Eq.(
13
),
\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
converges to 0 with the same order as the PAC-Bayesian bound.
Finally, we remark that from Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle R^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=\mathrm{PR}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}).

(15)

This indicates that the test error is decomposed into PR and BER. As pointed out in Remark
1
, BER is EU under the approximation of Eq.(
8
), and PER represents the quality of that approximation, Eq.(
15
) suggests that the test error simultaneously regularizes those BER and PER.

Next we show the log loss result. Our analysis requires additional assumption about model
p(y|x,\theta)
. We define log density ratio
L(y,x,\theta,\theta^{*}):=-\ln p(y|x,\theta)+\ln p(y|x,\theta^{*})
.

Assumption 1
.

Conditioned on
(x,\theta,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, there exists convex function
h(\rho)
for
[0,b)
such that cumulant function
L(y,x,\theta,\theta^{*})
is upper-bounded by
h(\rho)
,i.e., the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}e^{\rho(L(Y,x,\theta,\theta^{*})-\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}L(Y,x,\theta,\theta^{*}))}\leq h(\rho).

(16)

For example, if
h(\rho)=\rho^{2}\sigma^{2}(x,\theta)/2
and
b=\infty
, this assumption resembles the
\sigma^{2}
sub-Gaussian property given
(x,\theta,\mathbb{z}^{N})
. When considering Gaussian likelihood
p(y|x,\theta)=N(y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})
, we have

h(\rho)=\frac{\rho^{2}}{2v^{2}}|f_{\theta}(x)-f_{\theta*}(x)|^{2}

. Thus,
\sigma^{2}(x,\theta)
depends on
x
and
\theta
, and we refer to this
\sigma^{2}(x,\theta)
as a sub-Gaussian property. Other than the Gaussian likelihood, when the log loss is bounded, it satisfies the sub-Gaussian property. In this paper, we focus on this sub-Gaussian setting for Assumption
1
to clarify the presentation. We show an example of the logistic regression in Appendix
D.11
.

Theorem 3
.

When the model is well specified, that is,
\nu(y|x)=p(y|x,\theta^{*})
holds and
\sigma^{2}(x,\theta)
sub-Gaussian property is satisfied for
L(y,x,\theta,\theta^{*})
, as discussed above. Assume that
\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\sigma^{2}(x,\theta)<\sigma_{p}^{2}<\infty
. Conditioned on
(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})\leq\sqrt{2\sigma_{p}^{2}\mathrm{ER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N},\theta^{*})}.

(17)

Moreover, assume PAC-Bayesian bound Eq.(
2
) and

\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{z}^{N})q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})\nu(X)}\sigma^{2}(X,\theta)<\sigma_{q}^{2}<\infty

hold, and then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\sigma_{q}^{2}\mathrm{ER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N},\theta^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{q}^{2}C_{1}\ln N}{N^{\alpha}}}.

(18)

Proof.

Using Assumption
1
, we apply change-of-measure inequalities
[
39
]
to control the approximation error of Eq.(
8
).
∎

Remark 3
.

When we use a generalized linear model, the assumption of a well-specified model is relaxed so that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}[Y|x]
is well specified, similar to Remark
2
. See Appendix
D.9
for details.

From Eq.(
17
),
I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
is a lower bound of the excess risk and test error. From Eq.(
3
),

I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})

converges in the order of

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\ln N/N^{\alpha}})

if we can upper-bound

\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})q({\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N}})\nu(X)}\sigma^{2}(X,\theta)<\sigma_{q}^{2}<\infty

. For the Gaussian likelihood, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})\nu(X)}\sigma^{2}(X,\theta)\leq\!2\mathrm{ER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N},\theta^{*})\!\leq\frac{2C_{1}\ln N}{N^{\alpha}}:=\sigma_{q}^{2}

. See Appendix
D.8
for a detail.

In a similar way, we can derive the convergence rate of the entropy of the predictive distribution, which shows
H[p^{q}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})]=\scalebox{0.95}{$H[p(Y|X,\theta^{*})]+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\ln N/N^{\alpha}})$}
. See Appendix
D.10
for a formal statement. We show similar results for Theorem
3
under sub-exponential property in Appendix
E
.

In summary, we obtained the convergence of widely used EU measurements and the entropy in the approximate Bayesian inference for the first time. They converges faster than excess risks. Moreover we obtained two messages from Theorems
2
and
3
. First, the widely used EU measurements are the lower bounds of the test error and excess risks. This is consistent with the experimental fact that these EU measurements often underestimate EU. Second, the sum of PER and BER is upper-bounded by excess risk (test error). Thus, when minimizing the test error, we also simultaneously minimize PER and BER. This interpretation extends the intuition of Remark
1
and leads to a new VI in Sec.
3.3
.

3.3
Novel EU regularization method for variational inference

As seen in Sec.
3.2
, minimizing the test error leads to minimizing BER and PER. In this section, we discuss this relation using the objective function of VI. As an explicit example, consider a regression problem using
N(y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})
. Then from Lemma
1
, the loss function of the standard VI (eliminating
\mathrm{KL}(q({\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}})|p(\theta))
) can be written:

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\ln N(y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}\frac{|Y-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(X)|^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(X)}{2v^{2}}+\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{PR}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{z}^{N})}{2v^{2}}+\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{2}}{2}.

(19)

Eq.(
3.3
) implies that the standard VI tries to fit the mean of the predictive distribution to target variable
y
with the regularization term about the variance of the predictor. These terms corresponds to
\mathrm{PR}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{z}^{N})
and
\mathrm{BER}^{(2)}(Y|X,\mathbb{z}^{N})
. Note that there is a relation
\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})\leq\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)/v^{2}
for the Gaussian likelihood. This interpretation is consistent with Remark
1
.

It has been numerically reported that the standard VI often underestimates EU. Alternative objective functions have been proposed to address this issue. For example, the entropic loss defined as
\mathrm{Ent}_{\alpha}^{l}(y,x):=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}e^{-\alpha l(y,f_{\theta}(x))}
for
\alpha>0
, which is used in the
\alpha
-divergence dropout (
\alpha
-DO)
[
31
]
and the second order PAC-Bayesian methods (
2^{\mathrm{nd}}
-PAC)
[
34
,
14
]
, can capture EU better than the standard VI. Note that when
\alpha=1
and the log loss is used, the entropic risk corresponds to the log loss using the predictive distribution. For the Gaussian likelihood, we can upper-bound the entropic risk:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}\mathrm{Ent}_{\alpha=1}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y,X)

\displaystyle\leq\!\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}\frac{|Y\!-\!\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(X)|^{2}}{v^{2}}\!+\!\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(X)}{v^{2}}\!-\!\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{z}^{N})\!+\!\ln 2\pi v^{2},

(20)

where we used Eq.(
17
). See Appendix
D.12
for the derivation. Compared to Eq.(
3.3
), the entropic risk implicitly introduces a smaller regularization term for BER. This explains why
\alpha
-DO and
2^{\mathrm{nd}}
-PAC showed larger EU than the standard VI. We show a similar result for the entropic risk of the general log loss other than the Gaussian likelihood in Appendix
D.12
.

From these relations, balancing BER and PR appropriately leads to a solution that better evaluates the EU. Motivated by the decomposition in Eqs.(
3.3
) and (
3.3
), we directly control the prediction performance and the Bayesian excess risk for the Gaussian likelihood:

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.93}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{rBER}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|y_{i}\!-\!\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x_{i})|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+\lambda\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x_{i})}{2v^{2}}\!+\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{2}}{2}\!+\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{KL}(q({\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}})|p(\theta))$},

(21)

where
0<\lambda\leq 1
is the coefficient of the BER regularizer.
\mathrm{KL}(q({\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}})|p(\theta))
is a regularization term motivated by PAC-Bayesian theory. We select
\lambda
by cross-validation and it should be smaller than 1 since
\lambda=1
corresponds to the standard VI from Eq.(
3.3
) and the standard VI often underestimates the EU. We call Eq.(
21
) the regularized Bayesian Excess Risk VI (rBER) and show the PAC-Bayesian generalization guarantee for our rBER in Appendix
F
. In Sec.
5.2
, we numerically evaluated this objective function.

rBER can also be seen as an extension of the standard VI. In the standard VI, the test loss is lower-bounded by the sum of the PR and BER with equal weights (Eq.(
15
)). rBER has the flexible weights between PR and BER. See Appendix
F
for a detailed comparison.

4
Relation to existing work

The existing theoretical analysis of uncertainty focused on the calibration performance and clarified when a model over- and underestimates uncertainty
[
38
,
4
,
36
,
17
]
. Other than calibration, the analysis of Gaussian processes (GP) has been gaining attention since GP’s posterior predictive distribution can be expressed analytically
[
12
,
30
]
. Some research focused on the distance or geometry between the test and training data points to derive EU
[
32
,
46
]
. Other approaches connect the randomness of the posterior distribution to predictions by the delta method
[
37
]
.
Differently, the information-theoretic approach
[
51
]
focused on the loss function of the problem and defined the excess risk as the EU. Loss function-based analysis was proposed in the deterministic learning algorithm
[
25
]
.
Our theory, which can be regarded as an extension of the information-theoretic approach
[
51
]
to approximate Bayesian inference, derived the convergence properties of the variance and the entropy of the posterior predictive distributions.

Although the excess risk bound in Eq.(
2
) has been discussed by PAC-Bayesian theory
[
1
]
, its relation to the EU has not been investigated. The relationship between PAC-Bayesian theory and Bayesian inference has been investigated in terms of marginal likelihood
[
16
,
43
]
. Our work established new relationships that connect the uncertainty of the Bayesian predictive distribution and the PAC-Bayesian generalization bound. The information-theoretic approach
[
51
]
clarified that EU can be expressed by conditional mutual information. This relation was extended to meta-learning
[
27
]
. However, the researchers assumed that correct models and exact posterior distributions are available. Our proposed analysis relaxes these assumptions.

6
Conclusion

We theoretically and numerically analyzed the epistemic uncertainty of approximate inference. We clarified the novel relations among excess risk, epistemic uncertainty, and generalization error. We then showed the convergence rate of the widely used uncertainty measures for the first time. Motivated by theoretical analysis, we proposed a novel variational inference (VI) and applied it to the particle VI. In future work, it would be interesting to explore the relation between BER and evidential learning.

p(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})

\displaystyle MER_{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\leq\mathbb{E}l(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,\theta^{\prime}))-l(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,\theta)),

(22)

where
\Psi^{*}(X,\theta^{\prime})
is a plug-in decision rule, first we draw
\theta^{\prime}
from posterior distribution and substitute it to
\Psi^{*}
.
Then if the moment generating function of
l(Y,\Psi^{*}(X,\theta^{\prime}))
under
P(Y,\theta^{\prime}|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})
satisfies regularity conditions, we can upper bound MER. For example, a loss function satisfies
\sigma^{2}
-subGaussian conditioned on
(X,\mathbb{z}^{N})=(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
for all
(x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
, then

\displaystyle MER_{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})=\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}I(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})}.

(23)

Thus, we can treat zero-one loss and some squared loss.

Thus, CMI plays a central role in the Bayesian excess risk analysis.
Then existing work
[
51
]
shows that

\displaystyle I(\theta;Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\leq\frac{1}{N}I(\theta;\mathbb{Z}^{N}).

(24)

This is because the mutual information is upper-bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\ln N)
for many practical settings. Thus, the excess risk converges to 0 as
N\to\infty
.

C.2
Preliminaries of the PAC-Bayesian theory

We briefly introduce the PAC-Bayesian theory. The typical PAC-Bayesian bound provides us the high-probability guarantee about the gap between the test error
\tilde{R}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}l(Y,f_{\theta}(X))
and
\tilde{r}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}l(Y_{n},f_{\theta}(X_{n}))
(Here we do not take the expectation over
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
);

Theorem 4
.

[
2
]

Given a data generating distribution
\nu
, for any prior distribution
p(\theta)
over
\Theta
independent of
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
and for any
\xi\in(0,1)
and
c>0
, with probability at least
1-\xi
over the choice of training data
\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})
, for all probability distributions
q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})
over
\Theta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\tilde{R}(\theta)\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\tilde{r}(\theta)+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(q|p)+\ln{\xi}^{-1}+\Omega_{p,\nu}(c,N)}{cN},

(25)

where
\Omega_{p,\nu}(c,N):=\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathrm{exp}[cN(\tilde{R}(\theta)-\tilde{r}(\theta))]
.

This constant
\Psi_{p,\nu}
depends on the property of the loss function and the data generating distribution and prior. For example, when
l(y,f_{\theta}(x))-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}l(Y,f_{\theta}(X))
satisfies the
\sigma^{2}
sub-Gaussian property, and by setting
c=1/\sqrt{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\tilde{R}(\theta)\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\tilde{r}(\theta)+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(q|p)+\ln{\xi}^{-1}+\sigma^{2}/2}{\sqrt{N}}.

(26)

On the other hand, we introduced the bound in expectation in the main paper
[
1
]
. For example, under the similar setting as Eq.(
26
), when we assume the
\sigma^{2}
sub-Gaussian property and
c=\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle R^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\leq r(\theta)+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})|p(\theta))}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda\sigma^{2}}{2N},

(27)

see
[
1
]
for the proof and other settings.

Next, we introduce the PAC-Bayesian bound Eq.(
2
). When
r(\theta)
satisfies the
L
-lipschitz property and setting the prior as
N(\theta|0,\beta^{2}I_{d})
and
\lambda=1/\sqrt{N}
, we have

\displaystyle R^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-R^{l}(Y|X,\theta^{*})\leq L\beta\sqrt{\frac{d}{N}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\frac{\|\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}+\frac{d}{2}\log N}{\sqrt{N}}.

(28)

See
[
1
]
for the proof and other settings.

In the main paper, we considered the squared loss. For the squared loss, above Lipschitz bound cannot be used. Here we introduce different PAC-Bayesian bound. In stead of Lipschitz property, we assume that for asny
\theta,\ \theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, there exists a measurable function
M(x)
such that

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(x)-f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)\leq M(x)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2},

(29)

and assume
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X)}M(X)<L<\infty
. From the almost identical derivation of Example 2.2 in
[
1
]
, we have

\displaystyle R^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-R^{l}(Y|X,\theta^{*})\leq L\beta^{2}\frac{d}{N}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\frac{\|\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}+\frac{d}{2}\log N}{\sqrt{N}}.

(30)

D.1
Conditional expectation of excess risks

In this section, we define the conditional version of the excess risks. Note that PER and BER was defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathrm{PR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(X))],

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}):=\mathrm{BPR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})-\inf_{\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{p^{q}(\theta,\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z)}l(Y,\phi(\theta,X)).

(31)

We define the conditional excess risk as

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}):=\mathrm{PR}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})-\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(x))],

(32)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}):=\mathrm{BPR}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})-\inf_{\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})p(Y|x,\theta)}l(Y,\phi(\theta,x)).

(33)

It has been proved in Lemma 3.4 in
[
45
]
that if the action space is
\mathbb{R}
following relation holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\mathrm{PER}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=\mathrm{PER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}),

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})=\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}).

(35)

Moreover, for the log loss, from Theorem 3 in
[
8
]
, above relation holds. Thus, we can naturally connect the conditional and unconditional definitions of PER and BER for the squared loss and log-loss.

In the following, we explicitly calculate how relations Eqs. (
34
) and (
35
) holds.
The first terms in (
32
) and (
33
) can easily be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{PR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q({\theta}|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(X))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|X=x)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\mathrm{PR}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}),

(36)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{BPR}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X)q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N})p(Y|X,\theta)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}f_{\theta^{\prime}}(X))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})p(Y|X=x,\theta)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\mathrm{BPR}^{l}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N}).

(37)

Next we calculate the Bayes risks in the second terms. First, for the squared loss, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(X))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}(Y-\tilde{\phi}(X))^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}(Y-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y^{\prime}|X)}Y^{\prime}|X)^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X)}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y^{\prime}|X)}Y^{\prime}|X-\tilde{\phi}(X))^{2},

(38)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y^{\prime}|X)}Y^{\prime}|X
is the conditional expectation. Thus, infimum is achieved by setting
\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y^{\prime}|X)}Y^{\prime}|X=\tilde{\phi}(X)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(X))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}(Y-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y^{\prime}|X)}Y^{\prime}|X)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X=x)}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|X=x)}(Y-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y^{\prime}|x)}Y^{\prime}|x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X=x)}\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(x))].

(39)

We can show the same statement as

\displaystyle\inf_{\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{p^{q}(\theta,\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z)}l(Y,\phi(\theta,X))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z}^{N}=\mathbb{z}^{N})\nu(X=x)}\inf_{\phi:\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})p(Y|x,\theta)}l(Y,\phi(\theta,x)).

(40)

Combined these relations, we get Eqs. (
34
).

Next, we discuss the conditional Bayes risk for the log-loss. We can proceed the calculation in the same way as the squared loss. Then we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(X))]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|X)\nu(X)}\log\nu(Y|X)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(X=x)}\inf_{\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}[l(Y,\tilde{\phi}(x))].

(41)

The Bayes risk in BPR has the similar relation. Thus, it is clear that the relations shown in Eqs. (
34
) and (
35
) hold.

D.2
Proof of
\mathrm{BER}^{l}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\geq 0

We remark that for the squared loss and log-loss, BPR can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p^{\mathrm{f}}(\theta,\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z)}l(Y,\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{Z}^{N})}f_{\theta^{\prime}}(X))=\inf_{\psi:\mathcal{Z}^{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{p^{q}(\mathbb{Z}^{N},Z,\theta)}[l(Y,\psi(X,\mathbb{Z}^{N}))].

Lemma 2

W

and let for every

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}e^{\rho(W-\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}W)}\leq h(\rho),

(52)

is satisfied, for any probability distribution
q
, which is absolutely continuous with respect to
p
such that
\mathrm{KL}(q|p)\leq\infty

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(W)}W-\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}W\leq h^{*-1}(\mathrm{KL}(q|p)).

(53)

The proof of this lemma is shown in

as Theorem 4. Also, this lemma previously appeared in
[
7

When

and

\sigma^{2}
sub-Gaussian property and

Then under the assumption, from the Lemma

\theta

\mathbb{z}^{N}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta^{*})}L(Y,x,\theta)-\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}L(Y,x,\theta)\leq h^{*-1}(\mathrm{KL}(p(Y|x,\theta^{*})|p(Y|x,\theta))).

(54)

When we focus on a sub-Gaussian property, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta^{*})}L(Y,x,\theta)-\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}L(Y,x,\theta)\leq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\mathrm{KL}(p(Y|x,\theta^{*})|p(Y|x,\theta)))}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta^{*})}L(Y,x,\theta)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}2\sigma^{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathrm{KL}(p(Y|x,\theta^{*})|p(Y|x,\theta)))},

(56)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta^{*})}L(Y,x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta^{*})}[-\ln p(Y|x,\theta)+\ln p(Y|x,\theta^{*})]

D.11
Discussion about the logistic regression

Here we discuss the relation among Bayesian excess risk, frequentist excess risk, and the generalization ability for the logistic regression. For logistic regression, we define the model as

where
\mathrm{sig}=1/(1+e^{-x})
is the sigmoid function and
\phi(x)
is the feature vector.

We consider applying the following change-of-measure inequality; let
W
be a real-valued integrable random variable. If

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}e^{(W-\mathbb{E}W)}<\infty,

(79)

is satisfied, then, for any probability distribution

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(W)}W-\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}W\leq\ln\frac{\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}e^{\rho(W-\mathbb{E}_{p(W)}W)}+\mathrm{KL}(q|p)}{\rho}.

(80)

Here we assume that
q:=p(Y|x,\theta^{*})dy
,

, and
W=L(Y,x,\theta,\theta^{*})
conditioned on
(x,\theta,\mathbb{z}^{N})

.

Conditioned on
(x,\theta,\mathbb{z}^{N})

, we have

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}e^{\rho(L(Y,x,\theta,\theta^{*})-\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}L(Y,x,\theta,\theta^{*}))}

\displaystyle=\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}e^{\rho(-\ln p(Y|x,\theta)+\ln p(Y|x,\theta^{*}))}+\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}(\ln p(Y|x,\theta)-\ln p(Y|x,\theta^{*}))

\displaystyle=(\rho-1)D_{\rho}(p(Y|x,\theta^{*})|p(Y|x,\theta))+\rho\mathrm{KL}(p(Y|x,\theta)|p(Y|x,\theta^{*}))

\displaystyle\leq\rho\mathrm{KL}(p(Y|x,\theta)|p(Y|x,\theta^{*})),

(81)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P|Q):=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\int\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)^{\alpha-1}dP\geq 0.

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(p(Y|x,\theta)|p(Y|x,\theta^{*}))

(83)

(84)

D.12
Discussion of entropic risk

Before showing the relation between posterior variance and mutual information, we introduce an important lemma used in the analysis.

Lemma 3

(Lemma 1 in
[
19
]
)
.

Let
P(w)
be a measure on a set
W
and
Q(v)
be a measure on a set
V
. For any real-valued function
u(w,v)
, we have

\displaystyle-\int_{V}dQ(v)\ln\int_{W}dP(w)e^{u(w,v)}\leq-\ln\int_{W}dP(w)e^{\int_{V}dQ(v)u(w,v)}.

(92)

For completeness, we show the proof.

Proof.

For any real valued functions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{W}dP(w)e^{\alpha u_{1}(w)+(1-\alpha)u_{2}(w)}

\displaystyle=\int_{W}dP(w)\left(e^{u_{1}(w)}\right)^{\alpha}\left(e^{u_{2}(w)}\right)^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{W}dP(w)e^{u_{1}(w)}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\int_{W}dP(w)e^{u_{2}(w)}\right)^{1-\alpha},

(93)

where we used Hölder’s inequality. Taking logarithmic function, this shows that
\ln dP(w)e^{u(w,v)}
is convex in
u
. Thus, the result follows by using the Jensen inequality.
∎

Thus, this theorem is not restricted to probability distributions.

We first show the relation between posterior variance and the mutual information (
\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
).

Lemma 4
.

For the Gaussian likelihood
p(y|x,\theta)=N(y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}f_{\theta}(x)}{v^{2}}.

(94)

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})

\displaystyle=I_{\nu}(\theta;Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}\left[-\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}p(Y|x,\theta^{\prime})+\ln p(Y|x,\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}e^{\ln p(Y|x,\theta^{\prime})-\ln p(Y|x,\theta)}.

(95)

Then, applying Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq-\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}e^{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{p(Y|x,\theta)}\ln p(Y|x,\theta^{\prime})-\ln p(Y|x,\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq-\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}e^{-\frac{1}{2v^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})p(Y|x,\theta)}(Y-f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x))^{2}-(Y-f_{\theta}(x))^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}e^{-\frac{1}{2v^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}(v^{2}+f^{2}_{\theta}(x)-2f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)f_{\theta}(x)+f_{\theta^{\prime}}^{2}(x)-(v^{2}+f^{2}_{\theta}(x)+f^{2}_{\theta}(x)-2f_{\theta}^{2}(x)))}

\displaystyle=-\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}e^{-\frac{1}{2v^{2}}(\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}^{2}(x)-2\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)+f_{\theta^{\prime}}^{2}(x))}

(96)

). We use the transportation inequality Eq.(

(98)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Y|x)}\ln\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}N(Y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})

c>0
, then we have

\displaystyle h^{*-1}(y)=\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}y}+cy.

(110)

This is called as sub-gamma property. If
\sigma^{2}
and
c
are upper bounded by positive constants
\sigma^{2}<\sigma_{0}^{2}<\infty
and
c<c_{0}<\infty
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{PER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})+\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N})\leq\sqrt{2\sigma_{0}^{2}\mathrm{ER}^{\log}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N},\theta^{*})}+c_{0}\mathrm{ER}^{\log}(Y|X,\mathbb{Z}^{N},\theta^{*}).

(111)

Appendix F
Detailed description of the proposed method

First, we show the PAC-Bayesian bound for our proposed method. Following the high-probability bound of Theorem
4
, given a distribution
\nu(Z)
, for any prior distribution
p(\theta)
over
\Theta
independent of
\mathbb{z}^{N}
and for any
\xi\in(0,1)
and
c>0
, with probability at least
1-\xi
over the choice of training data
\mathbb{z}^{N}
, for all probability distributions
q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})
over
\Theta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}\left[\frac{|Y-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(X)|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+\lambda\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(X)}{2v^{2}}\right]+\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}|y_{i}\!-\!\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x_{i})|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+\!\lambda\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x_{i})}{2v^{2}}\right]\!+\!\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\!+\!\frac{\mathrm{KL}(q|p)\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{\xi}\!+\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{p,\nu}(c\!,\!N)}{cN},

(112)

where

\displaystyle\Omega_{p,\nu}(c,N):=\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)p(\theta^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z})^{N}}\mathrm{exp}[cN(\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}L(Z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}L(Z_{n},\theta,\theta^{\prime}))],

(113)

\displaystyle L(z,\theta,\theta^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\frac{|y-f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+(\lambda-1)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}^{2}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}{2v^{2}}.

(114)

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
35
]
.
First note that

\displaystyle\frac{|y-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+\lambda\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)}{2v^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\frac{|y-f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+(\lambda-1)\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)}{2v^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\frac{|y-f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+(\lambda-1)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}^{2}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}{2v^{2}}.

(115)

Based on this, we define the tandem loss as

\displaystyle L(z,\theta,\theta^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\frac{|y-f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+(\lambda-1)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}^{2}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)f_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}{2v^{2}}.

(116)

Then by considering the prior
p(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=p(\theta)p(\theta^{\prime})
, using Theorem
4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}L(Z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})}L(z_{n},\theta,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})|p(\theta)p(\theta^{\prime}))+\ln{\xi}^{-1}+\Omega_{p,\nu}(c,N)}{cN},

(117)

where

\displaystyle\Omega_{p,\nu}(c,N):=\ln\mathbb{E}_{p(\theta)p(\theta^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\mathbb{Z})^{N}}\mathrm{exp}[cN(\mathbb{E}_{\nu(Z)}L(Z,\theta,\theta^{\prime})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}L(Z_{n},\theta,\theta^{\prime}))].

(118)

Since
\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})q(\theta^{\prime}|\mathbb{z}^{N})|p(\theta)p(\theta^{\prime}))=2\mathrm{KL}(q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})|p(\theta))
, by setting c=2c’, we get the result.
∎

Thus, the constant
\Omega_{p,\nu}
depends only on the setting of the problem. We optimize the right-hand side of Eq.(
F
) as the objective function.

Next, we discuss the relation between rBER and standard VI. The objective function of standard VI is

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}\ln N(y|f_{\theta}(x),v^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{|y-\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x)|^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N}}f_{\theta}(x)}{2v^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\ln 2\pi v^{2}.

(119)

Thus, we can interpret that the log loss of the Gaussian likelihood corresponds to the prediction risk and Bayesian excess risk. Since the prediction risk corresponds to the prediction performance, the standard VI implicitly controls the prediction performance and the Bayesian excess risk. Our rBER can be regarded as

\displaystyle\mathrm{rBER}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2v^{2}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{PR}^{\mathrm{(2)}}(y_{n}|x_{n},\mathbb{z}^{N})+\lambda\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{(2)}}(y_{n}|x_{n},\mathbb{z}^{N})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln 2\pi v^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{KL}(q|p).

(120)

Thus, BER has a flexible weight for regularizing the uncertainty. Numerically, when
\lambda=0
, this corresponds to the setting where we simply optimize
\mathrm{PR}^{\mathrm{(2)}}(y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})
. This means we only consider the fitting performance. We numerically found that
\lambda=0
results in large uncertainty due to the lack of regularization. When
\lambda=1
, we found that the uncertainty is underestimated.

Finally, we remark the relation between Bayesian excess risk and
\mathrm{Var}f_{\theta}(x)
. Since we focus on the log loss, thus we can consider the following type of objective function.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}|y_{n}\!-\!\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|\mathbb{z}^{N})}f_{\theta}(x_{n})|^{2}}{2v^{2}}\!+\!\lambda\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(y_{n}|x_{n},\mathbb{z}^{N})\right]\!+\!\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{KL}(q|p)\!,

(121)

where we use the Bayesian excess risk directly, instead of
\mathrm{Var}f_{\theta}(x)
. Note that from Appendix
D.12
,
\mathrm{BER}^{\mathrm{log}}(Y|x,\mathbb{z}^{N})\leq\mathrm{Var}f_{\theta}(x)/v^{2}
holds for the Gaussian likelihood. Thus, Eq.(
121
) and our BER behaves in a similar way. From the numerical point of view, implementing
\mathrm{Var}f_{\theta}(x)
is easier than Eq.(
121
) since we simply calculate the variance of the prediction.

G.1
Particle variational inference

Since we applied our BER to particle variational inference (PVI), we briefly introduce the PVI and existing methods. In PVI, we use the empirical distribution
\rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{\theta_{i}}(\theta)
as the posterior distribution. Here
\delta_{\theta_{i}}(\theta)
is the Dirac distribution that has a mass at
\theta_{i}
. We refer to these
M
samples as particles. PVI
[
33
,
47
]
approximates the posterior through iteratively updating the empirical distribution by interacting them with each other:

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{\mathrm{new}}\xleftarrow{}\theta_{i}^{\mathrm{old}}+\eta v_{i}(\{\theta_{i^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{old}}\}_{i^{\prime}=1}^{M}),

(122)

where
v(\{\theta\})
is the update direction. Basically,
v
is composed of the gradient term and the repulsion term to enhance the divesity of the posterior distribution since we are often interested in the multi-modal information of the posterior distribution. For the update direction about
v
, see f-SVGD in
[
47
]
and VAR in
[
14
]
for details.

We follow the approach in VAR in
[
14
]
. They proposed using the gradient of the PAC-Bayesian bound for the update direction
v
in Eq.(
122
). For example, VAR uses

\displaystyle v_{i}=\partial_{i}\mathcal{F}(\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}),

(123)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\{\theta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}):=-\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\ln p(y_{n}|x_{n},\theta_{i})\!+R(y_{n},x_{n})\right]+\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{KL}(\rho_{\mathrm{E}}|\pi),

(124)

where
R
is the repulsion term to enhance the diversity. See
[
14
]
for details. Following their setting, we consider using the following update direction

\displaystyle v_{i}=\partial_{i}\mathrm{rBER}(\lambda)

(125)

\displaystyle\scalebox{0.95}{$\displaystyle\mathrm{rBER}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{|y_{n}\!-\!\mathbb{E}_{\rho(\theta)}f_{\theta}(x_{n})|^{2}}{2v^{\prime 2}}\!+\lambda\frac{\mathrm{Var}f_{\theta}(x_{n})}{2v^{\prime 2}}\!+\frac{\ln 2\pi v^{{}^{\prime}2}}{2}\!+\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{KL}(\rho(\theta)|p(\theta))$},

(126)

where
\rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{\theta_{i}}(\theta)
. We optimize
v^{\prime}
by gradient descent.

G.4
Contextual bandit tasks

Here we explain the setting of the task. Our experiments follow the setting in
[
47
,
14
]
. Denote the context set as
\mathcal{S}
. For each time step
t
, an agent recieves context
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
from the environment. The agent choose action
a_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,A\}
based on the context
s_{t}
and get a reward
r_{a_{t},t}
. We would like to minimize the pseudo regret

\displaystyle R_{T}=\max_{\phi:\mathcal{S}\to\{1,\ldots,A\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{g(s_{t}),t}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{a_{t},t}\right],

(127)

where
\phi
maps the context to the action. We consider a prior
\mu_{s,i,0}
over a reward of context
s
and action
i
. Then, we update the prior to a posterior distribution using the observed reward. Following the previous work, we use Thompson sampling to select the action as

\displaystyle r_{t}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arg~max}}\limits_{i=\{1,\ldots,K\}}\hat{r}_{i,t},\quad\hat{r}_{i,t}\sim\mu_{s,i,t}.

(128)

We consider a neural network regression model following the previous work
[
47
,
14
]
, where the input is the context, and the output is the
K
-dimensional action. We place a prior distribution over the parameters of the network. We approximate the posterior distribution of the neural network parameters by PVI. All the hyperparameters are precisely the same as in the previous work
[
47
]
.
