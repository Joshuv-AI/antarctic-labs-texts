Modular flats of oriented matroids and poset quasi-fibrations
Paul Mücksch

Abstract
. We study the combinatorics of modular flats of oriented matroids and the topological consequences for their Salvetti complexes. We show that the natural map to the localized Salvetti complex at a modular flat of corank one is what we call a poset quasi-fibration – a notion derived from Quillen’s fundamental Theorem B from algebraic K K -theory. As a direct consequence, the Salvetti complex of an oriented matroid whose geometric lattice is supersolvable is a K ⁡ ( π , 1 ) K(\pi,1) -space – a generalization of the classical result for supersolvable hyperplane arrangements due to Falk, Randell and Terao. Furthermore, the fundamental group of the Salvetti complex of a supersolvable oriented matroid is an iterated semidirect product of finitely generated free groups – analogous to the realizable case. Our main tools are discrete Morse theory, the shellability of certain subcomplexes of the covector complex of an oriented matroid, a nice combinatorial decomposition of poset fibers of the localization map, and an isomorphism of covector posets associated to modular elements. We provide a simple construction of supersolvable oriented matroids. This gives many non-realizable supersolvable oriented matroids and by our main result aspherical CW-complexes.

1.
Introduction

Complements of complex hyperplane arrangements provide a rich source of interesting topological spaces.
One of the most intricate and fascinating problems is to decide when arrangement complements are
Eilenberg-MacLane
K(\pi,1)
-spaces where
\pi
is the fundamental group of the space.
By definition, this is the case if their universal covering space is contractible.
Synonyms are aspherical space or classifying space for the group
\pi
.
They play an important role in algebraic topology e.g. as their cohomology coincides with
the group cohomology of
\pi
.
Moreover, if a
K(\pi,1)
-space is homotopy equivalent to a finite CW-complex then the group
\pi
is torsion free.
In the following, we call complex arrangements whose complements are aspherical simply
K(\pi,1)
or aspherical arrangements.

One of the most prominent positive results regarding
K(\pi,1)
-arrangements
is Deligne’s theorem
[
Del72
]
, stating that complexified real simplicial arrangements
are aspherical.
Subsequently, Salvetti
[
Sal87
]
introduced a regular cell complex modeling the homotopy type of the
complement of a complexified real arrangement.
Gel’fand and Rybnikov
[
GR90
]
realized that
the construction of the Salvetti complex only depends on the oriented matroid
associated to the real arrangement (see Section
2.6
).
This in turn led to a generalization of Deligne’s result to oriented matroids in general, i.e.
the Salvetti complex of an oriented matroid whose covector complex is a simplicial cell decomposition of the sphere is indeed aspherical,
independently established by Cordovil
[
Cor94
]
and Salvetti
[
Sal93
]
.

The combinatorial notions of a modular flat in a geometric lattice and supersolvability
were introduced by Stanley
[
Sta71
,
Sta72
]
(see Section
2.3
).
Regarding the
K(\pi,1)
problem for hyperplane arrangements,
a further corner stone in the theory is due to Falk and Randell
[
FR85
]

and Terao
[
Ter86
]
, see also
[
OT92
, Ch. 5]
. Thanks to their theorem,
complex arrangements with supersolvable geometric lattices are aspherical.
Their proof is entirely geometric and does not use any complexes modeling the homotopy type of the complement.
More precisely, they showed that the natural projection of the ambient space of the arrangement
to the quotient space by a modular intersection of corank one, restricted to the complement space
is a fiber bundle map with fiber a punctured complex plane.
The associated long exact sequence of homotopy groups then yields their result.
As a special case for real arrangements one obtains that supersolvability of their intersection lattices
implies that their Salvetti complexes are concrete instances of finite
K(\pi,1)
-complexes.
A nice generalization of Falk, Randell and Terao’s result to arrangements on connected abelian Lie groups
is obtained in the very recent work of Bibby and Delucchi
[
BD22
]
.

The natural question appears whether the “real” version of Falk, Randell and Terao’s theorem
extends to all oriented matroids as does Deligne’s theorem.

It is exactly the aim of this paper to provide a positive answer to this question and a novel combinatorial
proof of the “supersolvability theorem” for real arrangements.
More precisely, we show that the natural projection map from the Salvetti complex to its localization
at a modular corank one flat in the geometric lattice of an oriented matroid (see Section
2.6
)
is what we call a
poset quasi-fibration
.
The poset fibers of this localization map are homotopy equivalent to the affine Salvetti complex
of an oriented matroid of rank two (Theorem
6.4
).
The notion of a poset quasi-fibration is directly derived from Quillen’s fundamental
Theorem B from algebraic
K
-theory
[
Qui73
]
(see Section
5
).

Since every poset quasi-fibration comes with a long exact homotopy sequence,
as an application we obtain the following theorem which indeed extends
Falk, Randell and Terao’s result to oriented matroids.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Salvetti complex of a supersolvable oriented matroid is aspherical.

We also provide a simple argument that the Salvetti complex of each
localization of an oriented matroid with aspherical Salvetti complex is
aspherical as well (Lemma
2.26
).
In the case of hyperplane arrangements this useful necessary condition for asphericity
is due to Oka , cf.
[
Par93
, Lem. 1.1]
.
Furthermore, we show that the fundamental group of the Salvetti complex of a supersolvable
oriented matroid is an iterated semidirect product of finitely generated free groups (see Section
7
)
which is completely analogous to the realizable case.

A main ingredient in our study is discrete Morse theory which was established by
Forman
[
For98
]
(see Section
2.7
). We use its reformulation in terms of acyclic matchings
on face posets of cell complexes due to Chari
[
Cha00
]
,
which is now standard for applications to combinatorial topology, see also Kozlov’s book
[
Koz08
]
.
The acyclic matchings which are relevant to us are derived from the shellability
of the covector complex of an oriented matroid (see Section
2.5
),
a central result in the combinatorial topology of oriented matroids.

In the recent breakthrough work by Paolini and Salvetti
[
PS21
]

which settled the
K(\pi,1)
conjecture for affine Artin groups, discrete Morse theory
in combination with certain shellability results also played an important role.

In a separate erratum
[
DM22
]

we comment on results stated by Delucchi in
[
Del08
]
which are unfortunately not correct.
Resolving these issues would lead to a generalization of our results, similar to the more general
fiber bundles for hyperplane arrangements described in the work of Falk and Proudfoot
[
FP02
]
.

This article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we recall all the required notions from combinatorial
topology and oriented matroid theory.
In Section
3
we further elaborate on the special properties
of modular flats of oriented matroids.
Section
4
describes a useful stratification of the poset fibers of the
localization map between Salvetti complexes.
Section
5
introduces the special class of order
preserving maps which play a central role in our study.
In Section
6
we derive certain acyclic matchings which are then applied
to prove our main result: Theorem
6.4
.
In the following Section
7
we describe the structure of the fundamental group of
the Salvetti complex of a supersolvable oriented matroid.
To conclude, in Section
8
we provide a simple construction
which yields many non-realizable supersolvable oriented matroids.

2.
Preliminaries

In this preliminary section we review all the objects and notions required
for our study.

2.1.
Posets and regular CW-complexes

We introduce some notation and collect basic informations on the combinatorial topology of posets and regular CW-complexes.
Throughout this paper, all partially ordered sets (or posets for short) and all complexes are assumed to be finite.
A concise reference for us is
[
BLVS+99
, App. 4.7]
.

We start by recalling some basic poset terminology.
Let
P=(P,\leq)
be a poset. Its
dual
or
opposite
poset
P^{\vee}

has the same ground set as
P
but its order relation
\leq^{\vee}

is reversed:
x\leq^{\vee}y:\iff y\leq x
.

To emphasize the order relation of a particular poset
P
we occasionally use the notation
\leq_{P}
.

We denote by
\lessdot
the
cover relations
of
P
.
That is
x\lessdot y
if and only if
x<y
, and for all
z\in P
:

x<z\leq y
implies
z=y
.

A subset
I\subseteq P
is called an
order ideal
if
y\in I
and
x\in P
with

x\leq y
implies
x\in I
.
Dually,
F\subseteq P
is called an
order filter
if
F^{\vee}
is an order ideal in
P^{\vee}
.
We write
P_{\leq y}=\{x\in P\mid x\leq y\}
for the
principal
order ideal generated by
y\in P

and similarly
P_{\geq x}=\{y\in P\mid x\leq y\}
for the principal order filter
generated by
x\in P
.

A map
f:P\to Q
between two posets is
order preserving
or a
poset map

if for all
x\leq_{P}y

(x,y\in P)
we have
f(x)\leq_{Q}f(y)
.
If
q\in Q
then we write
(f\downarrow q):=f^{-1}(Q_{\leq q})
for the
poset fibers
of
f
.

A
linear extension

\dashv
of
P
is a total order on the ground set
P
such that
for all
x,y\in P
:
x\leq y
implies
x\dashv y
. Linear extensions of finite posets always exits.
Given an order ideal
I\subseteq P
, a linear extension
\dashv_{I}
on
I

can always be extended to a linear extension
\dashv
on
P
with

x\dashv y
if and only if
x\dashv_{I}y
for all
x,y\in I
.
Moreover, a linear extension on
I
can always be extended to
P
in such a way
that all the elements of
I
come first.
An analogue statement holds for order filters.

We assume familiarity with the notion of
CW-complexes
, also called
cell complexes
, e.g. see
[
Hat02
]
.
Important for our context are the
regular
cell complexes
since they are completely determined by their
face poset
of closed cells ordered by inclusion.
Let
\Sigma
be a regular cell complex and
\sigma,\tau\in\Sigma
.
If
\tau\subseteq\overline{\sigma}
then
\tau
is called a
face

of
\sigma
and we simply write
\tau\leq\sigma
.
In what follows, we thus regard a regular cell complex
\Sigma
itself as a poset.
Cells of dimension zero are referred to as
vertices
.

Conversely, to every poset
P
is associated its
order complex

\Delta(P)
.
It is the simplicial complex with

n
-simplices given by all chains of length
n
in
P
, i.e.

\Delta(P)_{n}:=\{\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\}\mid x_{i}\in P\text{ with }x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}\}.

For a simplicial complex
\Delta
we denote its topological realization by
|\Delta|
.
The composition of forming the order complex and taking its realization yields the

simplicial realization
functor

|\Delta(-)|:\Pos\to\Top

from the category
\Pos
of posets and order preserving maps
to the category
\Top
of topological spaces and continuous maps.
For a regular cell complex
\Sigma
the image of its face poset under this functor
is naturally homeomorphic to
\Sigma
:
|\Delta(\Sigma)|\cong\Sigma
.

We call an order preserving map
f:P\to Q
a homotopy equivalence
provided
|\Delta(f)|
is a homotopy equivalence.

Maps between finite regular cell complexes are given as order preserving maps
between their face posets. A poset map between the face posets of
regular cell complexes which is a homotopy equivalence in the above sense
is indeed a homotopy equivalence of their defining topological spaces.

A regular cell complex
\Sigma
is called
pure
if
all maximal cells have the same dimension.

A subset
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
is called a (closed) subcomplex
if with a cell
\sigma\in\Gamma
all of its faces also belong to
\Gamma
,
i.e.
\Gamma
is an order ideal of
\Sigma
.
For a subset of cells
S\subseteq\Sigma

we denote by
\Sigma(S):=\{\tau\in\Sigma\mid\tau\leq\sigma
for a
\sigma\in S\}
the subcomplex
of all faces of cells in
S
.
In poset terminology,
\Sigma(S)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in S}\Sigma_{\leq\sigma}
is the order ideal generated by
S
.
For a cell
\sigma\in\Sigma
we denote by
\delta\sigma
the subcomplex consisting of
all proper faces of
\sigma
.

2.2.
Oriented Matroids

We recall some basics from oriented matroid theory.
An oriented matroid can be regarded as a combinatorial abstraction of
a real hyperplane arrangement, i.e. a finite set of hyperplanes in a finite dimensional real vector space.
The standard reference is the book by Björner et al.
[
BLVS+99
]
.
In our study, we adopt the point of view that oriented matroids constitute a
special class of regular cell complexes, see Remark
2.4
below.

First, let us recall some notation for sign vectors.
Let
E
be some finite set and consider a sign vector
\sigma\in\{+,-,0\}^{E}
.
We denote by
z(\sigma):=\{e\in E\mid\sigma_{e}=0\}
its
zero set
.
The
opposite
vector
-\sigma
is defined by

(-\sigma)_{e}:=\begin{cases}-&\text{ if }\sigma_{e}=+,\\
+&\text{ if }\sigma_{e}=-,\\
0&\text{ if }\sigma_{e}=0.\end{cases}

The
composition

\sigma\circ\tau
of two sign vectors
\sigma,\tau\in\{+,-,0\}^{E}

is defined by

(\sigma\circ\tau)_{e}:=\begin{cases}\sigma_{e}&\text{ if }\sigma_{e}\neq 0,\\
\tau_{e}&\text{ else},\end{cases}

and their
separating set

S(\sigma,\tau)
is defined as

S(\sigma,\tau):=\{e\in E\mid\sigma_{e}=-\tau_{e}\neq 0\}.

If
A\subseteq E
and
\sigma\in\{+,-,0\}^{E}
we write
\sigma|_{A}:=(\sigma_{e}\mid e\in A)\in\{+,-,0\}^{A}

for its restriction to
A
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
oriented matroid

\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
is given by a finite
ground set
E
and a subset of sign vectors
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\{+,-,0\}^{E}
called
covectors
of
\mathcal{M}
,
subject to satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

\mathbf{0}:=(0,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathcal{L}
,

(2)

if
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}
, then
-\sigma\in\mathcal{L}
,

(3)

if
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{L}
, then
\sigma\circ\tau\in\mathcal{L}
,

(4)

if
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{L}
and
e\in S(\sigma,\tau)

there exists an
\eta\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\eta_{e}=0
and

\eta_{f}=(\sigma\circ\tau)_{f}=(\tau\circ\sigma)_{f}
for all
f\in E\setminus S(\sigma,\tau)
.

Let
\leq
be the partial order on the set
\{+,-,0\}
defined by
0<+
,
0<-
, with

+
and
-
incomparable. This induces the product partial order on
\{+,-,0\}^{E}
in which sign vectors
are compared component wise.
The
face poset
or covector poset
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
of
\mathcal{M}
is obtained by considering the induced partial order on the
subset
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\{+,-,0\}^{E}
.

The
rank
of
\mathcal{M}
is defined as the length of a maximal chain in
\mathcal{L}
.

The maximal elements in
\mathcal{L}
are called
topes
; they are denoted by
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})
.

Note that this is only one of several equivalent ways to define oriented matroids, cf.
[
BLVS+99
, Ch. 3]
.

An element
e\in E
is called a
loop
of the oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})

if
\sigma_{e}=0
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}
.
Two elements
e,f\in E
which are not loops are
parallel
if
\sigma_{e}=0

implies
\sigma_{f}=0
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}
.

In the rest of the sequel, without loss of generality, we always assume an oriented matroid to
be simple, i.e. it contains no loops or parallel elements.
This is justified by the fact that the covector poset of an oriented matroid and its simplification are isomorphic,
cf.
[
BLVS+99
, Lem. 4.1.11]
.

We have the following principal examples of oriented matroids.

Example 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of hyperplanes in a real vector space
V\simeq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.
For
H\in\mathcal{A}
choose defining linear forms
\alpha_{H}\in V^{*}
with
\ker(\alpha_{H})=H
.
Then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}))
with

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}):=\{(\sgn(\alpha_{H}(v))\mid H\in\mathcal{A})\mid v\in V\}\subseteq\{+,-,0\}^{\mathcal{A}}

is an oriented matroid.

We recall the definition of an affine oriented matroid.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
be an oriented matroid and
g\in E
.
Define
\mathcal{M}_{\aff,g}:=(E,\mathcal{L}^{+},g)
by

\mathcal{L}^{+}:=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{L}\mid\sigma_{g}=+\}.

Then
\mathcal{M}_{\aff,g}
is called an
affine
oriented matroid
or the
decone
of
\mathcal{M}
with respect to
g
.

Remark 2.4
.

For our purposes, oriented matroids are best thought of as special instances of regular cell complexes.
This is justified by the topological representation theorem due to Folkman and Lawrence
[
FL78
]
,
see also
[
BLVS+99
, Thm. 5.2.1]
.
Its core implication that the reduced covector poset
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\Zero\}

is the face poset of a regular cell decomposition of a sphere induced by
an arrangement of tamely embedded codimension one subspheres suffices for our study, see also Theorem
2.21
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\Zero\}
is a PL-sphere, so
\mathcal{L}^{\vee}
represents a regular cell decomposition
of a ball, cf.
[
BLVS+99
, Prop. 4.7.26]
.

There is another poset associated to an oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
which is essential for our considerations.

Definition 2.5
.

The
geometric lattice

L(\mathcal{M})
of
\mathcal{M}
is defined as

L(\mathcal{M}):=\{z(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{L}\}\subseteq 2^{E},

with partial order given by inclusion.
Elements of
L(\mathcal{M})
are called
flats
.
For two elements
X,Y\in L(\mathcal{M})
, their join is
X\vee Y=\inf\{Z\in L(\mathcal{M})\mid X\cup Y\subseteq Z\}
,
their meet is
X\wedge Y=X\cap Y
.

Moreover, for
X,Y\in L=L(\mathcal{M})
we write
L_{X}:=L_{\leq X}
for the lattice of the
localization
at
X
,

L^{Y}:=L_{\geq Y}
for the lattice of the
contraction
to
Y

and
L_{X}^{Y}:=[X,Y]:=L_{\leq X}\cap L_{\geq Y}
for an interval.

The lattice
L=L(\mathcal{M})
is graded by
rank
, i.e. the rank of
X\in L
is the length of a maximal chain in
L_{\leq X}
.

Note that the map
z:\mathcal{L}\to L
is a cover and rank preserving, order reversing surjection
[
BLVS+99
, Prop. 4.1.13]
.
The associated order preserving map
\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\to L
is also denoted by
z
.

Definition 2.6

(Restriction, contraction, and localization)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
be an oriented matroid.

(1)

For a subset
A\subseteq E
the
restriction

\mathcal{M}|_{A}=(A,\mathcal{L}|_{A})
of
\mathcal{M}
to
A
is
defined by

\mathcal{L}|_{A}:=\{\sigma|_{A}\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{L}\}.

(2)

For
X\subseteq E
the
contraction

\mathcal{M}/X=(E\setminus X,\mathcal{L}/X)
is defined by

\mathcal{L}/X:=\{\sigma|_{E\setminus X}\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{L}\text{ with }X\subseteq z(\sigma)\}.

(3)

For a flat
X\in L(\mathcal{M})
the
localization
of
\mathcal{M}
at
X
is
\mathcal{M}_{X}:=\mathcal{M}|_{X}
.
The natural projection of
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathcal{L}_{X}:=\mathcal{L}|_{X}
is denoted by
\rho_{X}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}_{X},\sigma\mapsto\sigma|_{X}
.

Remark 2.7
.

For the covectors of the contraction
\mathcal{M}/X
we also use the notation

\mathcal{L}^{X}:=\mathcal{L}/X
where
L^{X}=\{Y\in L(\mathcal{M})\mid Y\geq X\}
.
We often identify
\mathcal{L}^{X}
with
z^{-1}(L^{X})
by the obvious isomorphism

z^{-1}(L^{X})\simeq\mathcal{L}^{X}
.
Therefor, we write
\mathcal{L}^{X}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
or
\mathcal{L}^{X}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}
.

If
L_{X}^{Y}=[Y,X]\subseteq L(\mathcal{M})
we frequently write
\mathcal{L}_{X}^{Y}:=\mathcal{L}_{X}/Y
.

Remark 2.8
.

Let
X\in L
and let
\rho_{X}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}_{X}
be the localization map.
Then for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}
with
z(\alpha)=X
we have a section

\iota_{\alpha}:\mathcal{L}_{X}\to\mathcal{L}
to
\rho_{X}
which is defined by:

\iota_{\alpha}(\sigma)_{e}:=\begin{cases}\sigma_{e}&\text{if }e\in X,\\
\alpha_{e}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

An easy calculation shows that both the localization map
\rho_{X}
and the section
\iota_{\alpha}
preserve
the composition of covectors, i.e. we have
\rho_{X}(\sigma\circ\tau)=\rho_{X}(\sigma)\circ\rho_{X}(\tau)

for all
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{L}
,
and
\iota_{\alpha}(\sigma^{\prime}\circ\tau^{\prime})=\iota_{\alpha}(\sigma^{\prime})\circ\iota_{\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})

for all
\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{X}
.

We recall the notion of an extension of an oriented matroid.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
be an oriented matroid.
Then another oriented matroid
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=(E^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
with
E\subseteq E^{\prime}
is called an
extension
of
\mathcal{M}

if
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|_{E}=\mathcal{M}
.

If
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is an extension of
\mathcal{M}
, then we have a natural injective map

L(\mathcal{M})\hookrightarrow L(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}),X\mapsto\widetilde{X}
,
where
\widetilde{X}=\inf\{X^{\prime}\in L(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})\mid X\subseteq X^{\prime}\}
.

2.3.
Modular flats and supersolvable geometric lattices

For this section, let
L=L(\mathcal{M})
be the geometric lattice of a simple oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
.
The notion of a modular flat in a geometric lattice was introduced
by Stanley
[
Sta71
]
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
X\in L
.
Then
X
is
modular
if
for all
Y,Z\in L
with
Z\leq Y
we have

Z\vee(X\wedge Y)=(Z\vee X)\wedge Y.

The following notion is also due to Stanley
[
Sta72
]
.

Definition 2.11
.

The geometric lattice
L
is called
supersolvable
if there is a maximal chain

X_{0}<X_{1}<\ldots<X_{r}
of flats in
L
such that
X_{i}
is modular for all
0\leq i\leq r
.

In this case, we call
\mathcal{M}
itself supersolvable.

Supersolvable oriented matroids of rank
3
are easily describes by the following lemma,
the proof of which is left to the reader.

Lemma 2.12
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
to be of rank
3
.
Then
\mathcal{M}
is supersolvable if and only if there is a flat
X\in L
of rank
2

such that for all flats
Y\in L
of rank
2
we have

X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
.

Modular flats have the following property (which is actually equivalent to modularity).

Lemma 2.13

(
[
Bry75
, Thm. 3.3]
)
.

Let
X\in L
be a modular flat.
Then for
Y\in L
the map
p_{X}:[Y,X\vee Y]\to[X\wedge Y,X],Z\mapsto Z\wedge X
is a poset isomorphism
with inverse
s_{Y}:[X\wedge Y,X]\to[Y,X\vee Y],W\mapsto W\vee Y
.

2.4.
Topes and convex subsets

Recall that we denote the topes of an oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
,
i.e. the maximal elements of
\mathcal{L}
by
\mathcal{T}
.
As remarked earlier, without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathcal{M}
is simple.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
B\in\mathcal{T}
.
We define a partial order on
\mathcal{T}

Definition 2.15
.

Let

2.5.
Shellability and subcomplexes

We briefly elaborate on shellability of regular cell complexes, cf.
[
BLVS+99
, App. 4.7]
.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
\Sigma
be a pure
d
-dimensional regular cell complex.
A linear ordering
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{t}
of its maximal cells is called
a

if either
d=0
, or
d\geq 1
and the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

\delta\sigma_{j}\cap(\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}\delta\sigma_{i})
is pure of dimension
d-1
for
2\leq j\leq t
,

(ii)

\delta\sigma_{j}
has a shelling in which the
(d-1)
-cells of
\delta\sigma_{j}\cap(\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}\delta\sigma_{i})

come first for
2\leq j\leq t
,

(iii)

\delta\sigma_{1}
has a shelling.

A complex which admits a shelling is called

.

For our investigation, the principal example of a shellable regular cell complex is the
covector complex
\mathcal{L}
of an oriented matroid.
More precisely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.21

(
[
BLVS+99
, Thm. 4.3.3]
)
.

The reduced covector poset
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\Zero\}
of an oriented matroid of rank

is isomorphic to
the face poset of a shellable regular cell decomposition of the
(r-1)
-sphere.
Furthermore, every linear extension of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M},B)
is a shelling of
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\Zero\}
.

We list the following useful fact, cf.
[
BLVS+99
, Prop. 4.7.26]
.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
\Sigma
be a regular cell decomposition of the
d
-sphere which has a shelling

\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}
.
Then for
k<t
the subcomplex
\Sigma(\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}\})

is a shellable
d
-ball with shelling
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
.

With the preceding results we can delineate the following property of subcomplexes
generated by subsets of the topes

of an oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}
.

Corollary 2.23
.

Let
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
be a convex subset of topes.
Then both the subcomplexes
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q})
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{Q})

Proof.

Let

By Lemma

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M},B)

Conversely, with respect to the opposite linear extension of

This, in turn, yields the statement with Theorem
2.21

and Proposition

∎

2.7.
Discrete Morse theory

We review some basic concepts from Forman’s discrete Morse theory
[
For98
]
from the viewpoint
of acyclic matchings on posets respectively regular cell complexes adopted by Chari
[
Cha00
]
.
For a comprehensive exposition of the theory we refer to
Kozlov’s book
[
Koz08
]
.

Definition 2.29
.

Let
P=(P,\leq)
be a poset.
We associate a directed graph
G(\Sigma)=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with vertex set
\mathcal{V}=P
the ground set
of
P
and directed edges
\mathcal{E}:=\{(a,b)\mid a\lessdot b\}
its cover relations,
i.e. the Hasse diagram of the poset.

A subset
\mtc\subseteq\mathcal{E}
is called a
matching
on
P
if every element of
P
appears
in at most one
(a,b)\in\mtc
.

Let
G(P,\mtc)=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
be the new directed graph with
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{E}\setminus\mtc\cup\{(b,a)\mid(a,b)\in\mtc\}
,
i.e. the directed graph constructed from
G(P)
by inverting all edges in
\mtc
.
A matching
\mtc
on
\Sigma
is called
acyclic
if
G(P,\mtc)
has no directed cycles.

For an acyclic matching
\mtc
on
P
its
critical elements

C(\mtc)\subseteq P
are defined
as

C(\mtc):=\{a\in P\mid a\notin e\text{ for all }e\in\mtc\}.

In the same way we define acyclic matchings for a regular cell complex
\Sigma
(which is identified with its poset of faces).

Note that each acyclic matching
\mtc
on a poset
P
gives an acyclic matching

\mtc^{\vee}
on the dual poset
P^{\vee}

with
C(\mtc)=C(\mtc^{\vee})
.

We record the following special case of the main theorem of discrete Morse theory,
cf.
[
For98
]
,
[
Cha00
]
,
[
Koz08
, Ch. 11]
.

Theorem 2.30
.

Let
\Sigma
be a regular cell complex and let
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
be a subcomplex.
Assume that
\mtc
is an acyclic matching on
\Sigma
with
C(\mtc)=\Gamma
.
Then
\Gamma
is a strong deformation retract of
\Sigma
.
In particular, the inclusion
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Sigma
is a homotopy equivalence.

The next proposition, due to Chari
[
Cha00
]
, connects
shellability with discrete Morse theory.
Another, more explicit description of acyclic matchings for
shellable regular cell complexes is due to Delucchi
[
Del08
]
.

Proposition 2.31

(
[
Cha00
, Prop. 4.1]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a regular cell decomposition of the
d
-ball with shelling
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t}
.
Further, let
v\in\sigma_{1}
be a vertex.
Then there is an acyclic matching
\mtc
on
\Sigma
with
C(\mtc)=\{v\}
, i.e.
\Sigma
collapses onto
v
.

We conclude by recalling the following useful tool to construct acyclic matchings on posets, and hence on cell complexes.

Theorem 2.32

(Patchwork theorem
[
Koz08
, Thm. 11.10]
)
.

Let
f:P\to Q
be a poset map. Assume that for all
q\in Q
we have an acyclic matching
\mtc(q)

on
f^{-1}(q)
. Then
\mtc=\bigcup\limits_{q\in Q}\mtc(q)
is an acyclic matching on
P
.

A stratification of subcomplexes of the Salvetti complex

Again, let

be a modular flat of corank

We now describe a special stratification of the poset fiber

for

.
This is analogous to a result by Delucchi
[

Unfortunately, Delucchi’s more general statement is not correct, cf.

Recall that by Lemma

we have a natural linear order on the subset of topes

Lemma 4.1
.

For all

Proof.

This follows from the fact that

See the proof of

5.
Poset quasi-fibrations

We introduce a new class of poset maps with a desirable topological property.

Firstly, we record the following theorem from Quillen’s foundational
work
[
Qui73
]
on algebraic
K
-Theory, originally expressed in terms
of small categories and functors.
We state it in its special incarnation in terms of posets and order preserving maps.

Theorem 5.1

(Theorem B for posets
[
Qui73
, pp. 89]
)
.

Let
f:P\to Q
be an order preserving map between posets.
If for all
a\leq b

(a,b\in Q)
the inclusion

(f\downarrow a)=f^{-1}(Q_{\leq a})\hookrightarrow f^{-1}(Q_{\leq b})=(f\downarrow b)
is a homotopy equivalence,
then for
x\in P
with
f(x)=a
the homotopy fiber of

F(|\Delta(f)|,a)
is homotopy equivalent to
|\Delta(f\downarrow a)|
.

Consequently, we have a long exact homotopy sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i+1}(|\Delta(Q)|,a)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}(|\Delta(f\downarrow a)|,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}(|\Delta(P)|,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}(|\Delta(Q)|,a)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ldots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}(|\Delta(j)|)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{i}(|\Delta(f)|)}

where
j:(f\downarrow a)\hookrightarrow P
is the natural inclusion.

Theorem
5.1
motivates the following notion.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
f:P\to Q
be a poset map such that for all
a\leq b

(a,b\in Q)

the inclusion
(f\downarrow a)\hookrightarrow(f\downarrow b)

is a homotopy equivalence.
Then
f
is called a
poset quasi-fibration
.

Invoking a homotopy colimit construction
[
WZŽ99
, Prop. 3.12 & Rem.]
,
every poset quasi-fibration can be combinatorially “turned into”
a proper quasi-fibration in the sense of Dold and Thom
[
DT58
]
(after simplicial realization)
with fibers homotopy equivalent to the poset fibers of the original order preserving map.

7.
Fundamental groups

We analyze the structure of the fundamental group of the Salvetti complex of a
supersolvable oriented matroid.
This is completely analogous to the realizable case, cf.
[
FR85
]
.

Let
P
be a poset and
x\in P
. Then we write
\pi_{1}(P,x)=\pi_{1}(|\Delta(P)|,x)
for the fundamental group of
P
.
Similarly, if
f:P\to Q
is an order preserving map, then we write

\pi_{1}(f):=\pi_{1}(|\Delta(f)|):\pi_{1}(|\Delta(P)|,x)\to\pi_{1}(|\Delta(Q)|,f(x))
for the induced map
between fundamental groups.

Firstly, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume that
f:P\to Q
is a poset quasi-fibration which has a section
s:Q\to P
.
Let
x\in P
and
a=f(x)
and assume further that
|\Delta(Q)|
and
|\Delta(f\downarrow a)|
are
K(\pi,1)
-spaces.
Then the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(P,x)
is a semi-direct product:

\pi_{1}(P,x)\cong\pi_{1}((f\downarrow a),x)\rtimes\pi_{1}(Q,a).

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
, the long exact sequence of the poset quasi-fibration degenerates
to the short exact sequence:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}((f\downarrow a),x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(P,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(Q,a)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(f)}

The map
\pi_{1}(s)
gives a section to
\pi_{1}(f)
and so the above short exact sequence splits.
∎

With the preceding lemma and our main result from the last section we can say the following.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal{L})
be an oriented matroid and assume that
X\in L(\mathcal{M})
is modular and
of corank
1
.
Assume further that
\mathcal{S}_{X}
is aspherical.
Then for

and

we have

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S},x)\cong F\rtimes\pi_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{X},a),

where

is a free group with
|E\setminus X|
generators.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.4
the map
\widetilde{\rho}_{X}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}_{X}

is a poset quasi fibration with
(\widetilde{\rho}_{X}\downarrow a)
homotopy equivalent to
the affine Salvetti complex of an oriented matroid of rank
2
on
|E\setminus X|+1

Hence
(\widetilde{\rho}_{X}\downarrow a)\simeq S^{1}\vee\ldots\vee S^{1}
is a wedge of

Thus,
\pi_{1}((\widetilde{\rho}_{X}\downarrow a),a)=F

Furthermore,
\widetilde{\rho_{X}}
has a section by Remark
2.8
and
Lemma

yields the result.

.

the oriented matroid
\mathcal{M}
is already supersolvable and there is nothing to show.
Otherwise, let
Y\in L(\mathcal{M})
be a flat of rank
2
with
X\cap Y=\emptyset
.
By Lemma
8.1
we can extend
\mathcal{M}
by a single element
g
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}

such that
\widetilde{X}\cap\widetilde{Y}=\{g\}
.
Then we have

|\{Y^{\prime}\in L(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})\mid Y^{\prime}\cap\widetilde{X}=\emptyset\}|<|\{Y\in L(\mathcal{M})\mid X\cap Y=\emptyset\}|.

Hence, after a finite number of single element extensions via Lemma
8.1
we arrive at
an extension
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{M}
which is supersolvable thanks to Lemma
2.12
.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

Note that there is no analogue of Lemma
8.1
for oriented matroids of rank
\geq 4
.
E.g. the Edmonds-Fukuda-Mandel oriented matroid
\mathtt{EFM}(8)
–
an orientation of the uniform matroid of rank
4
on
8
points –
gives an example that has three corank
1
flats which cannot be connected
by any extension, cf.
[
BLVS+99
, Prop. 10.4.5]
.
Consequently, it is not clear to us that supersolvable extensions for higher rank oriented matroids
exist in general.
In contrast, in the realizable case one easily sees that any hyperplane arrangement is a subarrangement
of a supersolvable arrangement.

The remark leaves the following question.

Question 8.4
.

Do oriented matroids of rank
\geq 4
always have supersolvable extensions?

Starting with a non-realizable oriented matroid, Proposition
8.2

yields a non-realizable supersolvable oriented matroid.
Consequently, we obtain many non-realizable supersolvable oriented matroids.

Corollary 8.5
.

There are infinitely many supersolvable oriented matroids
which are not realizable.

All of these non-realizable oriented matroids have aspherical Salvetti complexes by Theorem
1.1
.

.

Example 8.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the oriented matroid of rank
3

associated to some orientation of the pseudoline arrangement,
illustrated as a projective picture in Figure
7
.
By Pappus’ theorem, the oriented matroid is not realizable by an arrangement of linear hyperplanes.

By Proposition
8.2
,
\mathcal{M}
can be extended to a supersolvable
oriented matroid
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
. A possible extension is illustrated by the dotted lines in Figure
7
.
The intersection point marked by a solid circle represents a modular flat
X
of corank one. It is connected to
every other intersection point of pseudolines in the arrangement by some line passing through
X
.
The resulting supersolvable oriented matroid
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is evidently not realizable.
