A two-way approach to out-of-time-order correlators
Yingfei Gu, Alexei Kitaev, Pengfei Zhang

Abstract
Out-of-time-order correlators (OTOCs) are a standard measure of quantum chaos. Of the four operators involved, one pair may be regarded as a source and the other as a probe. A usual approach, applicable to large- N N systems such as the SYK model, is to replace the actual source with some mean-field perturbation and solve for the probe correlation function on the double Keldysh contour. We show how to obtain the OTOC by combining two such solutions for perturbations propagating forward and backward in time. These dynamical perturbations, or scrambling modes, are considered on the thermofield double background and decomposed into a coherent and an incoherent part. For the large- q q SYK, we obtain the OTOC in a closed form. We also prove a previously conjectured relation between the Lyapunov exponent and high-frequency behavior of the spectral function.

Introduction

Out-of-time-order correlators (OTOCs) are interesting objects for several reasons. First, they characterize quantum chaos in a way that is comparable to the classical picture of divergent trajectories
[
1
]
. For systems in which connected correlators are suppressed by a large factor
C
, OTOCs behave as
1-C^{-1}e^{\varkappa t}
as long as
C^{-1}e^{\varkappa t}\ll 1
; at later times, they decay to zero. The number
\varkappa
, called the Lyapunov exponent, is bounded by
\frac{2\pi}{\beta}

[
2
]
. OTOCs give access to the near-horizon region of black holes
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, in which case
\varkappa=\frac{2\pi}{\beta}
. Another reason to study OTOCs is their relevance to quantum scrambling and certain information-theoretic tasks
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

In this paper, we give a conceptual picture and a general expression for OTOCs in many-body systems with all-to-all interactions such as the SYK model
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. For such systems, the previously mentioned parameter
C
is proportional to the number of elementary degrees of freedom
N
. The early-time behavior is already well-studied. For the SYK model at low temperature, the Lyapunov exponent
\varkappa
matches that of a black hole
[
10
]
. Maldacena and Stanford
[
13
]
found a finite-temperature correction to
\varkappa
and calculated the Lyapunov exponent at an arbitrary temperature in the large-
q
limit. A general ansatz for the early-time OTOC was introduced in
[
12
]
, and a relation between the ratio
C/N
and the Lyapunov exponent was established in
[
14
]
.

In principle, the calculation of a correlator like
\langle{(A(t)B(0)C(t)D(0)}\rangle
can be carried out by propagating
B(0)
,
D(0)
forward or
A(t)
,
C(t)
backward in time using the Heisenberg equation. This leads to the operator growth picture
[
15
]
. At infinite temperature, the decomposition of the Heisenberg-evolved operator
\mathcal{O}
into basis operators (i.e. products of
\chi_{j}
in the SYK case) may be interpreted as a quantum wave function
[
16
]
. Qi and Streicher
[
17
]
have extended this method to finite temperatures by constructing the quantum state
|\Psi\rangle=\big|\mathcal{O}\rho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle
, where
\rho
is the thermal density matrix and
\big|\rho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle
is the thermofield double state (TFD). However, they still define the “size” of
|\Psi\rangle
relative to the infinite-temperature TFD. We will adjust their definition as follows. Consider the free-fermion state
\varrho
(referred to as the “naive model” in
[
12
]
) reproducing the Wightman function of the SYK model at a given temperature. The corresponding state
\big|\varrho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle
of the double system may be regarded as a fermionic vacuum. Then the size of a perturbed TFD is defined as the average number of quasiparticles with some energy-dependent weights.

At low temperatures, essential properties of the SYK model are governed by the Schwarzian action
[
11
,
13
,
12
]
, which describes a black hole in
1+1
dimensions
[
18
]
. The relevant part of black hole physics goes back to the work of Dray and ’t Hooft
[
19
]
, who discovered that an infalling object generates a gravitational “shock wave” at the past horizon. A classical shock affects quantum correlation functions between the two sides of an eternal black hole
[
3
]
. In fact, a two-sided black hole perturbed by a shock is analogous to the state
|\Psi\rangle
we have previously discussed. ’T Hooft also proposed a fully quantum model of gravitational shocks, which mediate the scattering between incoming and outgoing particles
[
20
]
. The scattering amplitude is
a(p^{\mathrm{out}},p^{\mathrm{in}})=\exp(ifp^{\mathrm{out}}p^{\mathrm{in}})
, where
p^{\mathrm{out}}
,
p^{\mathrm{in}}
are the particles’ null momenta and
f
depends on their angular coordinates. Since the states of particles inside a black hole are inaccessible, the physical meaning of this theory is rather elusive and was understood long after ’t Hooft’s work. If one wants to operate with quantum observables only on one side, the relevant quantity is an out-of-time-order correlator. Such correlators were calculated by Shenker and Stanford for black holes
[
5
]
and by Maldacena, Stanford, and Yang for the SYK model at low temperature
[
18
]
. This includes the nonlinear, late-time regime. Stanford, Yang, and Yao
[
21
]
conjectured that the general form of these solutions is valid for large-
N
models at any temperature. We will show it is indeed the case.

A surprising connection exists between OTOCs and two-point functions. In a nutshell, chaotic dynamics contributes to equilibrium noise. Parker, Cao, Avdoshkin, Scaffidi, and Altman
[
22
]
conjectured a lower bound on high-frequency tails of the spectral function in terms of the Lyapunov exponent. They proved it at infinite temperature, and Avdoshkin and Dymarsky extended the derivation to finite temperatures
[
23
]
. We will give a different proof in section
7
.

can also be considered. To ensure the finiteness of correlation functions, operators should be ordered by
\tau

\zeta=0,1

X_{1},X_{2}
have parity

X_{3},X_{4}
have parity
\zeta_{3}=\zeta_{4}

\displaystyle\OTOC_{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4})

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle{\TT X_{1}(\theta_{1})X_{2}(\theta_{2})X_{3}(\theta_{3})X_{4}(\theta_{4})}\bigr\rangle

(2.1)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\zeta_{2}\zeta_{3}}\bigl\langle{X_{1}(\theta_{1})X_{3}(\theta_{3})X_{2}(\theta_{2})X_{4}(\theta_{4})}\bigr\rangle,

where (see also figure

\theta_{j}=\tau_{j}+it_{j},\qquad\tau_{1}\geqslant\tau_{3}\geqslant\tau_{2}\geqslant\tau_{4}\geqslant\tau_{1}-2\pi,\qquad t_{1}\approx t_{2}>t_{3}\approx t_{4}.

(2.2)

Using the time translation symmetry, we can arrange that
t_{1},t_{2}
are positive,
t_{3},t_{4}
are negative, and all four times are approximately equal in magnitude. This allows for a symmetric interpretation of the OTOC, whereby
X_{3}(\theta_{3})
and
X_{4}(\theta_{4})
create a perturbation to the thermofield double that propagates forward in time, while
X_{1}(\theta_{1})
and
X_{2}(\theta_{2})
create a backward-propagating perturbation. These counter-propagating “waves” interact at times around
0
, where they are both relatively weak. More exactly, let us assume that

|t_{1}-t_{2}|,\,|t_{3}-t_{4}|\lesssim 1,\qquad\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2},\,-\frac{t_{3}+t_{4}}{2}\gg 1,\qquad C^{-1}e^{(t_{1}+t_{2})/2},\,C^{-1}e^{-(t_{3}+t_{4})/2}\ll 1.

(2.3)

The quantity relevant to the OTOC,
C^{-1}e^{(t_{1}+t_{2}-t_{3}-t_{4})/2}
, is generally of the order of one.

We will show that the OTOC has the same general form as in the black hole problem
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\OTOC_{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4})\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dy_{\mathrm{A}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dy_{\mathrm{R}}\,e^{-\lambda y_{\mathrm{A}}y_{\mathrm{R}}}\,h^{\mathrm{R}}_{X_{1},X_{2}}\bigl(y_{\mathrm{A}};\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\bigr)\,h^{\mathrm{A}}_{X_{3},X_{4}}\bigl(y_{\mathrm{R}};\theta_{3}-\theta_{4}\bigr),\end{aligned}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\text{where}\qquad\lambda=C^{-1}e^{i\varkappa(\pi-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}.

(2.5)

Let us explain this result before deriving it. The integration variables
y_{\mathrm{A}}
and
y_{\mathrm{R}}
are analogous to the null momenta on the past and future horizons (
p^{\mathrm{out}}
,
p^{\mathrm{in}}
in
[
20
]
or
p^{u}_{1}
,
p^{v}_{2}
in
[
5
]
), which are due to particles moving and generating gravitational shocks along the opposite horizons. More exactly,
y_{\mathrm{A}}
(representing a perturbation source in the future) and
y_{\mathrm{R}}
(representing a similar source in the past) determine the magnitudes
z_{\mathrm{A}}
,
z_{\mathrm{R}}
of the backward-propagating (advanced) and forward-propagating (retarded) modes, respectively:

z_{\mathrm{A}}=C^{-1}e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}y_{\mathrm{A}},\qquad z_{\mathrm{R}}=C^{-1}e^{i\varkappa(\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}y_{\mathrm{R}}.

(2.6)

The exponential factor in (
2.4
),

a(y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}}):=e^{-\lambda y_{\mathrm{A}}y_{\mathrm{R}}}=\exp\bigl(-Ce^{i\varkappa\pi/2}z_{\mathrm{A}}z_{\mathrm{R}}\bigr),

(2.7)

describes interaction between the counter-propagating modes and may be understood as a scattering amplitude.

For a sharper interpretation, let us assume that

\tau_{1}=\tau_{3}=\frac{\tau}{2},\qquad\tau_{2}=\tau_{4}=-\frac{\tau}{2},\qquad 0\leqslant\tau\leqslant 2\pi

(2.8)

so that
z_{\mathrm{A}}=C^{-1}e^{\varkappa(t_{1}+t_{2})}y_{\mathrm{A}}
and
z_{\mathrm{R}}=C^{-1}e^{-\varkappa(t_{3}+t_{4})}y_{\mathrm{R}}
are real and positive.

. In this case, one can arrange for

, we will interpret the quantities

). In the situation we have described, where

3.13
), we find that

\Upsilon_{X_{1},X_{2}}^{\mathrm{R},m}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{m}\,h_{X_{1},X_{2}}^{\mathrm{R}}(y;\theta)\,dy,\qquad\Upsilon_{X_{3},X_{4}}^{\mathrm{A},m}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{m}\,h_{X_{3},X_{4}}^{\mathrm{A}}(y;\theta)\,dy.

(3.14)

Plugging these expressions into (

), we obtain equation (
2.4
).

4
Small perturbations to the thermofield double

In the linear (early-time) regime, scrambling modes are treated as small perturbations to the thermofield double state. A mode is generally in a superposition of mean-field states of different amplitudes, but in the linear approximation, only the average amplitude
c
and the corresponding change in the two-point function
\delta\langle{\chi_{\alpha}(\tau_{1})\chi_{\beta}(\tau_{2})}\rangle\propto c
matter. (Here,
\alpha,\beta,\ldots
refer to Majorana modes in an SYK-like setting.)

We find it convenient to use a Hilbert space formulation of mean-field theory, known as the “naive model”
[
12
]
. Formally, it is just a representation of the two-point function by a Gaussian fermionic state. To give it more meaning, consider the operators
\chi_{\alpha}(it)
with
t
in an interval much shorter than the scrambling time. Their thermal expectation values obey Wick’s theorem with
1/N
accuracy, and so it is tempting to say that the quantum state is almost Gaussian. This is actually not correct because
1/N
errors add up; for example, one cannot calculate the expectation value of the SYK Hamiltonian
\sum_{j<k<l<m}J_{jklm}\chi_{j}\chi_{k}\chi_{l}\chi_{m}
using Wick’s theorem. (The accumulation of errors can be illustrated by constructing
N
linear combinations of basic Majorana operators that almost anticommute pairwise but not in the bigger set. Indeed, there exist
N
unit vectors with
O(1/N)
mutual inner products that are nonetheless linearly dependent.) To avoid this problem, we assume that
\alpha,\beta,\ldots
belong to a small subset of modes. Then the operators
\chi_{\alpha}(it)
may be regarded as free-fermion, i.e.
\{\chi_{\alpha}(it),\chi_{\beta}(it^{\prime})\}
is just a number with acceptable accuracy. Furthermore, the restriction of the thermal state to the subalgebra generated by these operators is Gaussian. For the double system and similarly doubled subalgebra, the TFD is reduced to a pure Gaussian state, which may be called a free-fermion vacuum.

In this section, we mathematically define the double system and consider the perturbed TFD. Although it is still a pure state, its restriction
R
to the aforementioned subalgebra is not. We examine the structure of
R
and decompose it into a coherent and an incoherent part. (This is the only task that requires the use of the naive model, and it is tangential to our main goal.) Then we introduce “size operators”
Q_{\mathrm{R}}
,
Q_{\mathrm{A}}
that measure the magnitudes of forward-growing (retarded) and backward-growing (advanced) scrambling modes, respectively. These operators are positive-semidefinite, vanish on the TFD, and are sensitive only to the incoherent perturbation parts, which are related to the commutator OTOC.

4.1
The double system

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space of a physical system, and let us introduce its almost identical copy with the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{*}
, that is, the space of linear functional on
\mathcal{H}
(or equivalently, bra-vectors). The algebra
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
of operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
is canonically isomorphic to
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}
. Thus, any operator
A\in\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
may be interpreted as a vector
|A\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}
. In particular,
|{\rm TFD}\rangle=|\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle
corresponds to the square root of the thermal density matrix
\rho=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta H}
. The Hermitian inner product on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}
has the following expression in the operator language:

\langle A|B\rangle=\Tr(A^{{\dagger}}B).

(4.1)

Now, we consider operators acting on the double system. In the bosonic case, the formalism is quite simple. Note that the operator algebra
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H}^{*})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
with the multiplication order reversed; this new algebra is denoted by
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\rm op}
. Thus,
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*})\cong\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})\otimes\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\rm op}
. An element
X\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y\in\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})\otimes\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\rm op}
acts as follows:

(X\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y)|A\rangle=|XAY\rangle.

(4.2)

In the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded case, we define the left and right actions of
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
on itself so as to produce a representation of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebra product
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})\otimes_{\rm gr}\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\rm op}
:

\displaystyle(X\boldsymbol{\cdot}I)|A\rangle=|XA\rangle\qquad\text{(left action)},

(4.3)

\displaystyle(I\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y)|A\rangle=i^{\deg(AY)-\deg(A)}|AY\rangle\qquad\text{(right action)}.

Here, the phase factor
i^{\deg(AY)-\deg(A)}
is designed to ensure that the left and right actions commute up to
(-1)^{\deg X\cdot\deg Y}
. For a general element
X\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y=(X\boldsymbol{\cdot}I)(I\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y)
of
\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})\otimes_{\mathrm{gr}}\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\rm op}
, we have

\displaystyle(X_{1}\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y_{1})(X_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y_{2})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\deg Y_{1}\cdot\deg X_{2}}(X_{1}X_{2}\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y_{2}Y_{1}),

(4.4)

\displaystyle(X\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\deg X\cdot\deg Y}(X^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\cdot}Y^{\dagger}).

For an arbitrary
X\in\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{H})
, we define an “annihilation operator”
a(X)
such that
a(X)|\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle=0
:

a(X)=\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}X\rho^{-\frac{1}{4}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}I-i^{-\deg X}I\boldsymbol{\cdot}\rho^{-\frac{1}{4}}X\rho^{\frac{1}{4}},

(4.5)

where the phase factor
i^{-\deg X}
is consistent with the choice in (
4.3
). Note that

a(X)\,|\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}Y\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}\rangle=\big|\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}[X,Y]_{\mathrm{gr}}\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}\bigr\rangle,

(4.6)

where

[X,Y]_{\mathrm{gr}}=XY-(-1)^{\deg X\cdot\deg Y}YX.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(4.7)

Now, let
\chi_{\alpha}(it)
be some Majorana fields satisfying Wick’s theorem. (The example of interest is
\chi(it)=\chi_{j}(it)
for a fixed site
j
of the SYK model, considered at the time scale of the order of
1
so that
1/N
corrections are not exponentially amplified.) We work in the operator algebra generated by
\chi_{\alpha}(it)
, or equivalently, by
\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{\alpha}(it)e^{i\omega t}dt
with the commutation relations
\{\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega),\tilde{\chi}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\}=A_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\,2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})
, where the spectral function
A
also determines the correlation function:

\langle\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)\tilde{\chi}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{A_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{1+e^{-\beta\omega}}\,2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime}).

(4.8)

The thermal state
\rho
restricted to this subalgebra will be denoted by
\varrho
. In most cases,
\varrho
and
\rho
can be used interchangeably; for example,

\varrho^{-s}\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)\varrho^{s}=\rho^{-s}\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)\rho^{s}=e^{s\beta H}\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)e^{-s\beta H}=e^{-s\beta\omega}\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega).

(4.9)

The distinction between
\varrho
and
\rho
will be important in the next section, when we will consider perturbations.

The next piece of formalism is useful to describe the structure of the perturbed TFD. For this purpose, it is convenient to represent
\chi_{\alpha}(it)
as a delayed signal coming from the “past horizon”, i.e. a standard heat bath with a flat spectral function. More exactly (see appendix
A
for detail),

\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}(\omega),

(4.10)

where
\{\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega),\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})\}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})
. The last condition is equivalent to the equation
L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)^{\dagger}=A(\omega)
, and we also require that
L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)
admits an analytic continuation to the upper half-plane and has reasonable asymptotic behavior at
\omega\to\infty
. Due to the analyticity condition,
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega)
is defined for complex
\omega
with positive imaginary part by inverting the transformation (
4.10
) and using the regularization
\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)\approx\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{\alpha}(it)e^{i\omega t}u(t)\,dt
, where
u
is a broad Gaussian. But if
\Im\omega<0
, then
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega)
is hard to express in terms of the original operators of the model. Nonetheless, we will use it as a formal construct in equations (
4.28
), (
4.29
) for quantum states. This is legitimate because by definition, a state is a linear functional on observables. If one uses this definition directly, as in (
4.26
), (
4.27
), the past horizon representation is not necessary, though it helps avoid carrying the spectral function around. In the derivation of our main result, we will not use it at all.

The canonical annihilation operators for the double system are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{\beta\omega}{2\pi}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Bigl({\underbrace{\varrho^{\frac{1}{4}}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega)\varrho^{-\frac{1}{4}}}_{e^{\beta\omega/4}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega)}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}I+iI\boldsymbol{\cdot}{\underbrace{\varrho^{-\frac{1}{4}}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega)\varrho^{\frac{1}{4}}}_{e^{-\beta\omega/4}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(\omega)}}\Bigr),

(4.11)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega)|\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle=0,\quad\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega^{*})^{\dagger}|\varrho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{\beta\omega}{2\pi}\right)}\,\big|\varrho^{\frac{1}{4}}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}(-\omega)\varrho^{\frac{1}{4}}\bigr\rangle,

(4.12)

where the overall factor is chosen such that
\{\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega),\tilde{a}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime})^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
when
\omega
,
\omega^{\prime}
are real. In the OTOC analysis, we will use a variant of this equation:

\bigl\{\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr),\,\tilde{a}_{\beta}\bigl(\omega^{\prime}-i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)^{\dagger}\bigr\}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\qquad\text{for }\,\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.13)

4.2
Retarded vertex function as a deformation of the TFD

Let us again set
\beta=2\pi
and consider fermionic operators
X_{j}
at complex times
\theta_{j}=\tau_{j}+it_{j}
with

\tau_{1}=\pi,\quad\tau_{3}=\frac{\pi}{2},\quad\tau_{2}=0,\quad\tau_{4}=-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad t_{1}\approx t_{2}>t_{3}\approx t_{4}.

(4.14)

This configuration is shown in Fig.
1
b. It contains a pair of operators in the future (namely,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
) and another pair in the past (
X_{3}
and
X_{4}
). We may interpret either pair as a source that perturbs the thermofield double
|\rho^{1/2}\rangle
, whereas the other pair measures the resulting effect. Here, we choose to regard
X_{3}
and
X_{4}
as sources and interpret the OTOC as a matrix element of a combination of
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
acting on the double system:

\OTOC_{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4})=\bigl\langle\rho^{1/4}X_{4}(it_{4})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/4}\big|-iX_{1}(it_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}X_{2}(it_{2})\big|\rho^{1/4}X_{3}(it_{3})\rho^{1/4}\bigr\rangle.

(4.15)

We assume that
t_{1}\approx t_{2}\approx 0
, whereas
t_{3},t_{4}
are large and negative. Thus, we focus on the perturbation effect while pushing the sources to distant past (or distant future in other cases). For simplicity, let
X_{4}=X_{3}^{\dagger}
and
t_{4}=t_{3}
; then the above expression becomes (up to an overall factor) the expectation value of
-iX_{1}(it_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}X_{2}(it_{2})
on the pure state
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
, where

|\Psi\rangle=b^{-1/2}\big|\rho^{1/4}X_{3}(it_{3})\rho^{1/4}\bigr\rangle,\qquad b=\bigl\langle\rho^{1/4}X_{3}(it_{3})\rho^{1/4}\big|\rho^{1/4}X_{3}(it_{3})\rho^{1/4}\bigr\rangle=\langle{X_{3}(\theta_{3})X_{4}(\theta_{4})}\rangle.

(4.16)

If
X_{1}=\chi_{\alpha}
and
X_{2}=\chi_{\beta}
are elementary Majorana operators, we may restrict
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
to the previously defined subalgebra, i.e. trace out the other degrees of freedom. (This amounts to using the “naive model” when detecting the TFD deformation.) Thus, we obtain a mixed state
R
of a system of free fermions, and the OTOC is expressed as follows:

b^{-1}\OTOC_{\chi_{\alpha},\chi_{\beta},\cdots,\cdots}(\pi+it_{1},it_{2},\cdots,\cdots)=\Tr\bigl((-i\chi_{\alpha}(it_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi_{\beta}(it_{2}))\,R\bigr).

(4.17)

The expression on the right-hand side is a perturbed version of the Wightman function,

\langle{\chi_{\alpha}(\pi+it_{1})\chi_{\beta}(it_{2})}\rangle=\Tr\bigl((-i\chi_{\alpha}(it_{1})\cdot\chi_{\beta}(it_{2}))\,|\varrho^{1/2}\rangle\langle\varrho^{1/2}|\bigr).

(4.18)

In this section, we study the deformation of the TFD in the linear order. Thus, the OTOC is given by equation (
3.1
), which can be simplified as follows:

b^{-1}\OTOC_{\chi_{\alpha},\chi_{\beta},\cdots,\cdots}(\pi+it_{1},it_{2},\cdots,\cdots)=\langle{\chi_{\alpha}(\pi+it_{1})\chi_{\beta}(it_{2})}\rangle-c\,e^{\varkappa\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}}\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}_{\alpha\beta}(\pi+i(t_{1}-t_{2})).

(4.19)

The coefficient
c
depends on the deformation source, i.e. on
X_{3}
and
t_{3}
, but we may assume that
c>0
by fixing the normalization of
\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}
. The Fourier transform of this function,
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}(\pi+it)e^{i\omega t}dt
, can be shown to satisfy the following relations:

\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)=\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(-\omega)^{T}=\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(-\omega)^{*}=\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)^{\dagger},\qquad\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)\geqslant 0.

(4.20)

The last inequality can be derived from the positivity of the incoherent deformation part, defined below, which is proportional to
\cos\frac{\pi\varkappa}{2}
and related to commutator OTOCs.

The last term in (

) characterizes the deformation of the free-fermion vacuum:

\delta\langle{-i\chi_{\alpha}(it_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi_{\beta}(t_{2})}\rangle:=\Tr\bigl((-i\chi_{\alpha}(t_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi_{\beta}(t_{2}))\,\delta R\bigr)=-c\,e^{\varkappa\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}}\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}_{\alpha\beta}(\pi+i(t_{1}-t_{2})),

(4.21)

where

\delta R=R-|\varrho^{1/2}\rangle\langle\varrho^{1/2}|.

(4.22)

An arbitrary infinitesimal deformation of a pure state can be decomposed into a “coherent” and an “incoherent” part:

\delta R=|\xi\rangle\langle\varrho^{1/2}|+|\varrho^{1/2}\rangle\langle\xi|+\sum_{j}\bigl(|\eta_{j}\rangle\langle\eta_{j}|-\langle\eta_{j}|\eta_{j}\rangle|\varrho^{1/2}\rangle\langle\varrho^{1/2}|\bigr),\qquad|\xi\rangle,|\eta_{j}\rangle\perp|\varrho^{1/2}\rangle.

(4.23)

In the case of a free-fermion system,
|\xi\rangle
is a linear combination of

, whereas
|\eta_{j}\rangle
is a combination of
a^{\dagger}_{\alpha}|\varrho^{1/2}\rangle
. To compute
|\xi\rangle
and
|\eta_{j}\rangle
, we will consider
\delta\langle\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega+i\frac{\varkappa}{2})\,\tilde{a}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}+i\frac{\varkappa}{2})\rangle
and
\delta\langle\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega+i\frac{\varkappa}{2})^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{\beta}(\omega^{\prime}+i\frac{\varkappa}{2})\rangle
, respectively.

Since the annihilation and creation operators are defined in terms of the “past horizon” operators
\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}(\omega)
(see Eq. (
4.10
)), the first calculation step is to pass from
\delta\langle{-i\chi_{\alpha}(\pi+it_{1})\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi_{\beta}(t_{2})}\rangle
in (
4.21
) to

\delta\bigl\langle{\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}\bigl(\omega^{\prime}+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)}\bigr\rangle=-ic\,\widetilde{\Xi}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\,\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime}),

(4.24)

where

\widetilde{\Xi}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)=L^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)^{-1}\,\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)\left(L^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)^{-1}\right)^{\dagger}.

(4.25)

The function
\widetilde{\Xi}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)
has properties similar to (
4.20
).

The next step is to express the products of
a
and
a^{\dagger}
using the definition (
4.11
). Each such expression has four terms, but it is sufficient to consider only the terms with
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}
on different sides. (Indeed, the expectation value of any one-sided observable is not affected by the deformation.) Thus,

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\delta\bigl\langle{\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)\tilde{a}_{\beta}\bigl(\omega^{\prime}+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)}\bigr\rangle

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varkappa}{2}-i\omega)\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varkappa}{2}-i\omega^{\prime})}{2\pi}\begin{aligned} \Bigl(\;&ie^{\pi(\omega-\omega^{\prime})/2}\,\delta\bigl\langle{\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}\bigl(\omega^{\prime}+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2})}\bigr\rangle\\
{}-{}&ie^{\pi(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)/2}\,\delta\bigl\langle{\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}\bigl(\omega^{\prime}+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha}\bigl(\omega+i\tfrac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)}\bigr\rangle\Bigr)\end{aligned}

4.3
Size operator

A “size operator” is any positive-semidefinite operator that measures the magnitude of the TFD deformation. Since we consider a one-parameter family of quantum states, and only to the first order in the parameter
c
(as defined by Eq. (
4.21
)), there are many ways to measure. This is based on an underlying assumption: the measurement takes place around a certain time
t_{0}
, say,
t_{0}=0
, whereas the deformation is produced by a relatively weak source at a distant time. Within the naive model, we may use the operator

Q_{\mathrm{R}}^{(\text{naive})}=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}v_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,\tilde{a}_{\alpha}\bigl(\omega+i\frac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)^{\dagger}\tilde{a}_{\beta}\bigl(\omega^{\prime}+i\frac{\varkappa}{2}\bigr)

with some positive-semidefinite
v
. To assure convergence (in view of the exponential factors in the definition (
4.11
) of
\tilde{a}_{\alpha}(\omega)
), we may require that
v_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
decay sufficiently fast as
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\to\infty
. In the time domain, the previous equation becomes

Q_{\mathrm{R}}^{(\text{naive})}=\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}u_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\,e^{-\varkappa(t+t^{\prime})/2}a(\chi_{\alpha}(it))^{{\dagger}}a(\chi_{\beta}(it^{\prime})),\qquad u\geqslant 0,

(4.33)

where, according to the definition (
4.5
),

a(\chi_{\alpha}(it))=\chi_{\alpha}\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}I+iI\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi_{\alpha}\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it\bigr).

(4.34)

The “window function”
u
can be, for example, Gaussian to eliminate any potential divergence. (In practice, it is not an issue.) In the SYK case, the indices
\alpha,\beta
are redundant because the naive model consists of a single field,
\chi(it)=\chi_{j}(it)
for some fixed
j
.

In the actual SYK model, the TFD deformation affects all Majorana modes equally, and therefore, the size operator may be averaged over the modes. Changing the overall normalization, we arrive at the following definition:
6
6

6

For infinite temperature and
u_{\mathrm{R}}(t,t^{\prime})=\delta(t)\delta(t^{\prime})
, we get
Q_{\mathrm{R}}=\sum_{j}a(\chi_{j})^{{\dagger}}a(\chi_{j})
, where
a(\chi_{j})=\chi_{j}\boldsymbol{\cdot}I+iI\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi_{j}
. In this case,
\langle\mathcal{O}|Q_{\mathrm{R}}|\mathcal{O}\rangle
is the “size” of the operator
\mathcal{O}
in the sense of Ref.
[
17
]
. See Ref.
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
for more proposals of operator size at finite temperature.

Q_{\mathrm{R}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,u_{\mathrm{R}}(t,t^{\prime})\,e^{-\varkappa(t+t^{\prime})/2}a(\chi_{j}(it))^{{\dagger}}a(\chi_{j}(it^{\prime})),\qquad u_{\mathrm{R}}\geqslant 0.

(4.35)

By construction,
Q_{\mathrm{R}}
is positive-semidefinite, annihilates
|\rho^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle
, and measures the magnitude of the forward-propagating scrambling mode. The definition of the operator
Q_{\mathrm{A}}
measuring the backward-propagating scrambling mode is similar but involves the coefficient function
u_{\mathrm{A}}(t,t^{\prime})e^{\varkappa(t+t^{\prime})/2}
.

To elaborate a bit, the “magnitude” is understood as the coefficient
c
. However, it is convenient to normalize
Q_{\mathrm{R}}
by the condition

\delta\bigl\langle{Q_{\mathrm{R}}}\bigr\rangle=Cc.

(4.36)

Let us express it more explicitly. For each filed
\chi=\chi_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\delta\bigl\langle{a(\chi(it))^{{\dagger}}a(\chi(it^{\prime}))}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta\bigl\langle{i\chi\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it^{\prime}\bigr)+i\chi\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it^{\prime}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\chi\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it\bigr)}\bigr\rangle

(4.37)

\displaystyle=c\,2\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\pi\varkappa}{2}\bigr)e^{\varkappa(t+t^{\prime})/2}\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}(\pi+i(t-t^{\prime})).

Thus, the function
u_{\mathrm{R}}
in (
4.35
) is normalized as follows:

\int dt\,dt^{\prime}u_{\mathrm{R}}(t,t^{\prime})\,\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}(\pi+i(t-t^{\prime}))=\frac{C}{2\cos(\frac{\pi\varkappa}{2})\,N}=k_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(-\varkappa)\,(\Upsilon^{\mathrm{A}},\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}).

(4.38)

The last equality is a result from Ref.
[
14
]
. It involves a kinetic coefficient
k_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(-\varkappa)
called “branching time” and a certain inner product between the advanced and retarded vertex functions.

Finally, we remark that the coefficient
c
is, actually, the average value of the true TFD deformation magnitude
z_{\mathrm{R}}
that is measured by
C^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{R}}
. Formally,
z_{\mathrm{R}}
is an eigenvalue of
C^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{R}}
, which is positive because
Q_{\mathrm{R}}
is positive. More intuitively,
z_{\mathrm{R}}
is a random variable that is generated by a quantum process, amplified to become essentially classical, and affecting all Majorana modes. If
z_{\mathrm{R}}
is fixed, all terms in (
4.35
) have the same expectation value
\frac{C}{N}z_{\mathrm{R}}
, while their fluctuations are independent. Since
N
is large, the fluctuations are not important.

5
Nonlinear theory

In this section, we interpret the operator
e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{R}}}
(where
z
is some number) as a source of a backward-propagating scrambling mode that can be treated using mean-field theory. Likewise, the operator
e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{A}}}
generates a forward-propagating mode of the given magnitude
z
, whose exact form is obtained by solving a certain equation on the double Keldysh contour. The operators in question have norm less or equal to
1
if
\Re z\geqslant 0
and vanish as
\Re z\to+\infty
. Furthermore, they are analytic in
z
, which implies the desired analytic properties of their matrix elements mentioned in section
3
.

5.1
Matrix element interpretation of
F^{\mathrm{R}}
,
F^{\mathrm{A}}
,
h^{\mathrm{R}}
,
h^{\mathrm{A}}

We now consider the perturbation created by a pair of operators beyond the linear order. Let us insert the operators at complex times
\theta_{3}=\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{3}
and
\theta_{4}=-\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{4}
with
t_{3}\approx t_{4}<0
(as we did before) and probe the resulting forward-propagating mode with
e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{R}}}
at times around
0
. The matrix element of the operator
e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{R}}}
has an expression similar to (
4.15
) and may be interpreted in a dual way, as a measure of the backward-propagating mode generated by that operator:

F^{\mathrm{A}}_{X_{3},X_{4}}\bigl(z;\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{3},\,-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{4}\bigr)=\bigl\langle\rho^{1/4}X_{4}(it_{4})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/4}\big|e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{R}}}\big|\rho^{1/4}X_{3}(it_{3})\rho^{1/4}\bigr\rangle.

(5.1)

This function can be analytically continued in
t_{3}
,
t_{4}
, and a similar function arises from probing the perturbation created by
X_{1}(\theta_{1})
,
X_{2}(\theta_{2})
with
e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{A}}}
:

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{A}}_{X_{3},X_{4}}(z;\theta_{3},\theta_{4})

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\rho^{1/2}X_{4}(\theta_{4})^{\dagger}\big|e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{R}}}\big|\rho^{1/2}X_{3}(\theta_{3})\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\text{for }\,\pi\geqslant\Re\theta_{3}\geqslant 0\geqslant\Re\theta_{4}\geqslant-\pi,

(5.2)

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{R}}_{X_{1},X_{2}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle X_{1}(\theta_{1})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2}\big|e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{A}}}\big|X_{2}(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\text{for }\,\pi\geqslant\Re\theta_{1}\geqslant 0\geqslant\Re\theta_{2}\geqslant-\pi.

(It is also true that
F^{\mathrm{R}}_{X_{1},X_{2}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\bigl\langle\rho^{1/2}X_{2}(\theta_{2})^{\dagger}\big|e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{A}}}\big|\rho^{1/2}X_{1}(\theta_{1})\bigr\rangle
.)

The Taylor series

e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{A}}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{m}}{m!}Q_{\mathrm{A}}^{m},\qquad e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{R}}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{m}}{m!}Q_{\mathrm{R}}^{m}

(5.3)

imply similar expansions for the functions
F^{\mathrm{R}}
,
F^{\mathrm{A}}
. They are given by Eq. (
3.9
) with

\displaystyle\bigl(e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}\bigr)^{m}\,\Upsilon_{X_{1},X_{2}}^{\mathrm{R},m}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle X_{1}(\theta_{1})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2}\big|Q_{\mathrm{A}}^{m}\big|X_{2}(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\bigl(e^{i\varkappa(\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}\bigr)^{m}\,\Upsilon_{X_{3},X_{4}}^{\mathrm{A},m}(\theta_{3}-\theta_{4})

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\rho^{1/2}X_{4}(\theta_{4})^{\dagger}\big|Q_{\mathrm{R}}^{m}\big|\rho^{1/2}X_{3}(\theta_{3})\bigr\rangle.

Following the convention of section
4.3
, we denote the eigenvalues of
C^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{A}}
,
C^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{R}}
by
z_{\mathrm{A}},z_{\mathrm{R}}
. Let us consider the eigenvalue decompositions

C^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{A}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}z_{\mathrm{A}}\Pi_{\mathrm{A}}(z_{\mathrm{A}})\,dz_{\mathrm{A}},\qquad C^{-1}Q_{\mathrm{R}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}z_{\mathrm{R}}\Pi_{\mathrm{R}}(z_{\mathrm{R}})\,dz_{\mathrm{R}}.

(5.5)

The first of them, together with the first equation in (
5.4
), implies that

\Upsilon_{X_{1},X_{2}}^{\mathrm{R},m}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(e^{i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}Cz_{\mathrm{A}}\bigr)^{m}\bigl\langle X_{1}(\theta_{1})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2}\big|\Pi_{\mathrm{A}}(z_{\mathrm{A}})\big|X_{2}(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle\,dz_{\mathrm{A}}

(5.6)

On the other hand,
\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R},m}
is related to the inverse Laplace transforms of
F^{\mathrm{R}}
, that is, to the function
h^{\mathrm{R}}
in (
3.13
), (
3.14
). Thus, we obtain the first equation below (the second one is similar):

\displaystyle\bigl\langle X_{1}(\theta_{1})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2}\big|\Pi_{\mathrm{A}}(z_{\mathrm{A}})\big|X_{2}(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}C\,h_{X_{1},X_{2}}^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(\underbrace{e^{i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}Cz_{\mathrm{A}}}_{y_{\mathrm{A}}};\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\bigr),

(5.7)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\rho^{1/2}X_{4}(\theta_{4})^{\dagger}\big|\Pi_{\mathrm{R}}(z_{\mathrm{R}})\big|\rho^{1/2}X_{3}(\theta_{3})\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}C\,h_{X_{3},X_{4}}^{\mathrm{A}}\bigl(\underbrace{e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}Cz_{\mathrm{R}}}_{y_{\mathrm{R}}};\theta_{3}-\theta_{4}\bigr).

Note that we have reproduced the relation (
2.6
) between the magnitudes
z_{\mathrm{A}},z_{\mathrm{R}}
of scrambling modes and the random variables
y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}}
pertaining to their sources. If
y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}}>0
and
0\leqslant\tau\leqslant 2\pi
, then
h_{X^{\dagger},X}^{\mathrm{R}}(y_{\mathrm{A}};\tau)\geqslant 0
and
h_{X^{\dagger},X}^{\mathrm{A}}(y_{\mathrm{R}};\tau)\geqslant 0
. Upon suitable normalization (as in equation (
2.9
)), these functions may be interpreted as the probability distributions of
y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}}
.

5.2
Kinetic equation on the double Keldysh contour

Let us now focus on the SYK model,

H=i^{\frac{q}{2}}\sum_{1<j_{1}\cdots j_{q}<N}J_{j_{1},...,j_{q}}\chi_{j_{1}}...\chi_{j_{q}},\qquad\{\chi_{j},\chi_{k}\}=\delta_{jk},\qquad\overline{J^{2}_{j_{1},...,j_{q}}}=\frac{(q-1)!}{N^{q-1}}J^{2}.

(5.8)

The ambiguities in the definition of
Q_{\mathrm{A}}
(and similarly,
Q_{\mathrm{R}}

7
7

7

We could further set
t_{0}=0

|z|\ll 1

z

F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})

e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}z

\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}

Q_{\mathrm{A}}=u_{\mathrm{A}}e^{\varkappa t_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a(\chi_{j}(it_{0}))^{\dagger}a(\chi_{j}(it_{0})),\qquad u_{\mathrm{A}}=\frac{C}{2\cos(\frac{\pi\varkappa}{2})\Upsilon^{\mathrm{A}}(\pi)\,N}=k_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}(-\varkappa)\,\frac{(\Upsilon^{\mathrm{A}},\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}})}{\Upsilon^{\mathrm{A}}(\pi)}.

\displaystyle e^{-zQ_{\mathrm{A}}}\approx 1

\displaystyle+\frac{-zu_{\mathrm{A}}e^{\varkappa t_{0}}}{1!}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a(\chi_{j}(it_{0}))^{\dagger}a(\chi_{j}(it_{0}))

(5.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{(-zu_{\mathrm{A}}e^{\varkappa t_{0}})^{2}}{2!}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{N}a(\chi_{j_{1}}(it_{0}))^{\dagger}a(\chi_{j_{2}}(it_{0}))^{\dagger}a(\chi_{j_{2}}(it_{0}))a(\chi_{j_{1}}(it_{0}))+\cdots

zu_{\mathrm{A}}e^{\varkappa t_{0}}\ll 1

(5.11)

(5.12)

6
Example: the large-
q
SYK model

6.1
Preliminaries

The large-
q
SYK model was introduced by Maldacena and Stanford, who computed, among other things, the two-point function and the Lyapunov exponent
[
13
]
. The four-point function was calculated in
[
30
]
using the mean-field approximation, which captures the early-time OTOC. We will compute the OTOC in the general case,
C^{-1}e^{\varkappa t}\sim 1
.

By definition,
N
is taken to infinity before the
q\to\infty
limit. To obtain sensible results, the coupling parameter
J
should scale as follows:

J^{2}=\frac{2^{q-1}}{q}\mathcal{J}^{2},

(6.1)

where
\mathcal{J}
is fixed. The factor
2^{q-1}
is to compensate the adopted normalization of Majorana operators,
\chi^{2}=1/2
. The
1/q
factor in
J^{2}
implies that the equilibrium self-energy is proportional to
1/q
. We will arrange that
u_{\mathrm{R}}=u_{\mathrm{A}}=2/q
so that the perturbation scales in the same way.

Instead of
q
and
\mathcal{J}
, it is often convenient to use
\Delta
and
v
that are defined as follows:

\Delta=\frac{1}{q},\qquad\quad\frac{v}{2\cos\frac{\pi v}{2}}=\mathcal{J},\quad 0<v<1.

(6.2)

The number
v
determines the natural time scale in the system. For example, the Lyapunov exponent is
\varkappa=v
and the two-point function is as follows:

G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\langle{\chi(\theta_{1})\chi(\theta_{2})}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\cos\frac{v\pi}{2}}{\cos\frac{v(\pi-\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})}{2}}\right)^{2\Delta},\qquad 2\pi\geqslant\Re(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\geqslant 0.

(6.3)

Note one subtlety about the derivation of equation (
6.3
). The method of
[
13
]
, which we will also use, is based on the ansatz
G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{1}{2}e^{g(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})/q}
and the assumption
|g(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})|\ll q
. This assumption is satisfied in the region where the self-energy is non-negligible. Indeed
\Sigma=J^{2}G^{q-1}=\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{q}(e^{g/q})^{q-1}\approx\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{q}e^{g}
. We have
|\Im g|\leqslant\frac{v\pi}{2}
; the real part of
g
is always negative, and if it is large in magnitude, then
\Sigma
is small. More specifically, the self-energy and all its variants are concentrated in the region
v|\Im(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})|\lesssim 1
. However
G
and other correlation functions extend farther out. To verify the extended solution, one can first check that the retarded and advanced Green functions,

iG^{\mathrm{R}}(t_{1},t_{2})=-iG^{\mathrm{A}}(t_{2},t_{1})\approx\theta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,e^{-v(t_{1}-t_{2})/q},

(6.4)

Differential form and solution of the kinetic equation

Let us rewrite equations (

(6.7)

\partial_{1}\partial_{2}\frac{g(t_{1},t_{2})}{2}=\mathcal{J}^{2}e^{g(t_{1},t_{2})}-2ze^{\varkappa t_{0}}\delta(t_{1}-t_{0})\delta(t_{2}-t_{0}).

(6.8)

where the function

f_{1}(t)=e^{2ze^{vt_{0}}}\coth\frac{v(t-t_{0})}{2}\,,\qquad f_{2}(t)=e^{-2ze^{vt_{0}}}\tanh\frac{v(t-t_{0})}{2}\,.

(6.14)

Thus, we have found the non-equilibrium Wightman function for

and
t_{2}>0

W(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\cos\frac{\pi v}{2}}{e^{2ze^{vt_{0}}}\cosh\frac{v(t_{1}-t_{0})}{2}\cosh\frac{v(t_{2}-t_{0})}{2}-e^{-2ze^{vt_{0}}}\sinh\frac{v(t_{1}-t_{0})}{2}\sinh\frac{v(t_{2}-t_{0})}{2}}\right)^{2\Delta}.

\frac{1}{2}e^{\Delta(g_{1}(t_{1})-vt_{2})}&\text{if }v(t_{2}-t_{1})\gg 1.\end{cases}

(6.16)

The function
W
we have found has this property. Since the Liouville region and the tail regions overlap, the exact tails (i.e.
g_{1},g_{2}
) for our

and for the actual solution coincide. Therefore, our results are valid in the entire plane.

6.3
Vertex functions and late-time OTOC

We calculate the OTOC using the general formula (
2.4
) and the relations involved in its derivation. In terms of the kinetic equation,
F^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(z;\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},\,-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{2}\bigr)=W(t_{1},t_{2})
in the
t_{0}\to-\infty
limit (that is, in the case where the source is infinitely small but its effect at given
t_{1},t_{2}
is finite). Thus,

F^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(z;\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},\,-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{2}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\cos\frac{\pi v}{2}}{\cosh\frac{v(t_{1}-t_{2})}{2}+ze^{v\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}}}\right)^{2\Delta}.

(6.17)

The analytic continuation to complex times is conveniently written as follows:

F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,\biggl(1+\frac{z_{12}}{\vartheta_{12}}\biggr)^{-2\Delta},

(6.18)

where

z_{12}=e^{-iv\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}}{2}}z,\qquad\vartheta_{12}=\cos\frac{v(\pi-\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})}{2}.

(6.19)

Let us comment on analytic properties of the function
F^{\mathrm{R}}
. First,
F^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(z;\,\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},\,-\tfrac{\pi}{2}+it_{2}\bigr)
is analytic in
z
in the entire plane, except for a branch cut from
-\infty
to
-(e^{-vt_{1}}+e^{-vt_{2}})/2
. More generally, if
\pi\geqslant\Re\theta_{1}\geqslant 0\geqslant\Re\theta_{2}\geqslant-\pi
, then
F^{\mathrm{R}}\bigl(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is analytic in the region
|\arg z|<\bigl(1-\frac{v}{2}\bigr)\pi
. This is consistent with the abstract result based on the consideration of matrix elements, which asserts the analyticity in the right half-plane under the same conditions. For the large-
q
SYK model, the matrix element argument can be strengthened as follows. The vector
|\chi(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\rangle
is well-defined for
\frac{1-v}{2v}\pi>\Re\theta_{2}>-\frac{1+v}{2v}\pi
(vs.
0\geqslant\Re\theta_{2}\geqslant-\pi
in the general case) because
\langle\chi(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}|\chi(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\rangle=G(-\theta_{2}^{*},\theta_{2})
is bounded for all
\theta_{2}
in the indicated range. If we rather keep
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{2}
the same, the range of possible
\arg z
is extended by
\frac{1-v}{2}\pi
in both directions, matching the actual analyticity domain.

The vertex functions
\Upsilon^{R,m}
entering equation (
3.9
) can be found by Taylor expanding
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
in
z_{12}
:

F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z_{12})^{m}}{m!}\cdot\underbrace{G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\frac{\Gamma(2\Delta+m)}{\Gamma(2\Delta)}\vartheta_{12}^{-m}}_{\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R},m}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})}.

(6.20)

Due to the analyticity and decay at infinity in the right half-plane (and slightly beyond),
F^{\mathrm{R}}
can be represented as a Laplace transform in
z_{12}
:

F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}h^{\mathrm{R}}(y;\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\exp(-z_{12}y)\,dy.

(6.21)

For the specific function (
6.18
), the inverse Laplace transform is:

h^{\mathrm{R}}(y;\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})=G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\,\frac{\vartheta_{12}^{2\Delta}}{\Gamma(2\Delta)}\,y^{2\Delta-1}\exp(-\vartheta_{12}y).

(6.22)

Note that
\Upsilon^{\mathrm{A},m}(\theta)=\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R},m}(\theta)
and
h^{\mathrm{A}}(y,t)=h^{\mathrm{R}}(y,t)
due to the time reversal symmetry of the SYK model.

Now, we use our main formula (
2.4
) and find the OTOC:

\displaystyle\OTOC(\theta_{1},\theta_{2};\theta_{3},\theta_{4})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!dy_{\mathrm{A}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\!dy_{\mathrm{R}}\,e^{-\lambda y_{\mathrm{A}}y_{\mathrm{R}}}h^{\mathrm{R}}(y_{\mathrm{A}},\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\,h^{\mathrm{A}}(y_{\mathrm{R}},\theta_{3}-\theta_{4})

(6.23)

\displaystyle=G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})G(\theta_{3},\theta_{4})\,\biggl(\frac{\vartheta_{12}\vartheta_{34}}{\lambda}\biggr)^{2\Delta}U\biggl(2\Delta,1;\,\frac{\vartheta_{12}\vartheta_{34}}{\lambda}\biggr),

where

\lambda=C^{-1}e^{iv(\pi-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})},\qquad C=N\cdot 4\Delta^{2}\cos\frac{\pi v}{2},

(6.24)

and
U(a,b,z)
is the confluent hypergeometric function. For the symmetric configuration (
4.14
), the factor
\lambda
is real,
\lambda=C^{-1}e^{v\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}-t_{3}-t_{4}}{2}}
.

The OTOC can also be written in the form (
3.7
). In the large-
q
SYK case, it amounts to the asymptotic expansion

x^{-2\Delta}U(2\Delta,1;x^{-1})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(2\Delta+n)^{2}}{\Gamma(2\Delta)^{2}}\frac{(-x)^{n}}{n!}.

(6.25)

7
Discussion

Our study adds to the well-established relation between quantum chaos, information scrambling, and the instability of the thermofield double. In particular, we have described the production of scrambling modes by operator pairs using the functions
h^{\mathrm{A}}
and
h^{\mathrm{R}}
. This is a quantum process even in the large-
N
limit, though the dual picture (involving
F^{\mathrm{R}}
and
F^{\mathrm{A}}
, respectively) is mean-field. The large-
N
approximation also leads to a simple interaction form between counter-propagating modes,
a(y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}})=\exp\bigl(-C^{-1}e^{i\varkappa(\pi-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}y_{\mathrm{A}}y_{\mathrm{R}}\bigl)
, but again,
a(y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}})
may be interpreted as a quantum scattering amplitude. These results support the idea of “scramblon” as a quantum object (essentially, a Bose field) and suggest the possibility of a special form of quantum mechanics describing scrambling, and maybe even gravity. It must be non-unitary because it is meant to provide a coarse-grained description, but it should not introduce an “arrow of time”.

As a rather simple idea along these lines, one may use a variant of ’t Hooft’s action for gravitational shocks
[
20
]
and represent the scattering amplitude as follows:

a(y_{\mathrm{A}},y_{\mathrm{R}})=\frac{e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}C}{2\pi i}\int dz_{\mathrm{R}}\,dz_{\mathrm{A}}\,\exp\Bigl(e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}\bigl(Cz_{\mathrm{R}}z_{\mathrm{A}}-e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}y_{\mathrm{A}}z_{\mathrm{R}}-e^{i\varkappa(\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})/2}y_{\mathrm{R}}z_{\mathrm{A}}\bigr)\Bigr).

(7.1)

The integral is taken over a suitable surface in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and the saddle point of the action (i.e. the exponent in the above expression) is given by (
2.6
). Combining equations (
2.4
) and (
7.1
), we get

\displaystyle\OTOC_{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4})

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}C}{2\pi i}\int dz_{\mathrm{R}}\,dz_{\mathrm{A}}\,\exp\bigl(e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}Cz_{\mathrm{R}}z_{\mathrm{A}}\bigr)\,F^{\mathrm{R}}_{X_{1},X_{2}}\bigl(e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}z_{\mathrm{R}};\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\bigr)\,F^{\mathrm{A}}_{X_{3},X_{4}}\bigl(e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}z_{\mathrm{A}};\theta_{3},\theta_{4}\bigr).

This is, essentially, the effective model proposed by Stanford, Yang, and Yao, see Eq. (2.6) in
[
21
]
. In comparison, our equation (
2.4
) is more similar to Eq. (2.4) (or (8) in the arXiv version) of
[
5
]
because in both cases, the integral is taken over null energies running from
0
to
\infty
.

An interesting extension of our results, leading to further questions, has to do with analytic properties of the function
F^{\mathrm{R}}
and the spectral function. In the most general case,
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is analytic in the half-plane
\Re z>0
, provided
\pi\geqslant\theta_{1}\geqslant 0\geqslant\theta_{2}\geqslant-\pi
. The last condition can be relaxed by applying the argument we have used for the large-
q
SYK model. Suppose that the spectral function decays as

A(\omega)\sim e^{-\tau_{*}|\omega|}\quad\text{for }\,\omega\to\infty

(7.3)

or even faster. Then the two-point function
G(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is well-defined for
2\pi+\tau_{*}>\Re(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})>-\tau_{*}
, and therefore, the states
|X_{1}(\theta_{1})^{\dagger}\rho^{1/2}\rangle
and
|X_{2}(\theta_{2})\rho^{1/2}\rangle
have bounded norm for

\pi+\frac{\tau_{*}}{2}>\theta_{1}>-\frac{\tau_{*}}{2},\qquad\frac{\tau_{*}}{2}>\theta_{2}>-\pi-\frac{\tau_{*}}{2}.

(7.4)

If
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are fixed and satisfy these inequalities, then
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is analytic in the half plane
\Re z>0
. But
F^{\mathrm{R}}
actually depends on certain combinations of its variables:
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=f^{\mathrm{R}}(e^{-i\varkappa(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})/2}z;\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})
(see (
3.11
)). Hence,
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},-\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{2})
is analytic in the region

|\arg z|<\biggl(1+\varkappa\biggl(1+\frac{\tau_{*}}{\pi}\biggr)\biggr)\frac{\pi}{2}.

(7.5)

On the other hand,
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\frac{\pi}{2}+it,-\frac{\pi}{2}+it)=f^{\mathrm{R}}(e^{\varkappa t}z;\pi)
must have a singularity at some negative value of
x=e^{\varkappa t}z
. Let us first prove a weaker statement: as
x
goes to
-\infty
, the function in question either diverges at some finite value of the variable or grows faster than any exponential. Indeed,
f^{\mathrm{R}}(x;\pi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-yx}h^{\mathrm{R}}(y,\pi)\,dy
(see (
3.13
)), where
h^{\mathrm{R}}(y,\pi)\geqslant 0
(see remark at the end of section
5.1
). It follows that for any positive
y_{*}
such that
h^{\mathrm{R}}(y_{*},\pi)\not=0
, we have
f^{\mathrm{R}}(x;\pi)\geqslant ce^{-y_{*}x}
, where
c=h^{\mathrm{R}}(y_{*},\pi)>0
. Such a
y_{*}
can be arbitrarily large because
h^{\mathrm{R}}(y,\ldots)
is analytic in a neighborhood of the positive real axis, and therefore, can vanish only at a discrete set of points.

To proceed, we will use the the integral equations (
5.23
), (
5.24
) for the function
W(t_{1},t_{2})=F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},-\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{2})
, where
z<0
is fixed. So far we have proved that
W(t,t)
grows super-exponentially (namely, faster than
\exp(ae^{\varkappa t})
for any
a
), but the goal is to show that it diverges at some finite
t
. Initially (i.e. at times around
t_{0}
),
W(t_{1},t_{2})
varies at the characteristic time scale of the order of
1
. As larger times, it grows increasingly faster as a function of
\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}
, while the dependence on
t_{1}-t_{2}
remains less sharp. In the regime where the effective growth exponent tends to infinity, we may assume that
W(t_{1},t_{2})\approx W\bigl(\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2})
and that
G^{\mathrm{R}}(t_{1},t)\approx-i\theta(t_{1}-t)
,
G^{\mathrm{A}}(t^{\prime},t_{2})\approx i\theta(t_{2}-t^{\prime})
. Thus, the
W\to\infty
asymptotic behavior is described by a simple equation:

W(t)=4J^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(t-t^{\prime})\,\theta(t-t^{\prime})\,W(t^{\prime})^{q-1}\,dt^{\prime}.

(7.6)

It can be reduced to the second-order differential equation
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}W=4J^{2}W^{q-1}
, whose solution diverges as
W(t)\sim(t_{*}-t)^{-2/(q-2)}
. Note that the temperature does not matter here because we discuss short-time dynamics. This argument was inspired by the calculation of high-frequency noise at infinite temperature in Ref.
[
33
]
.

Given that
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},-\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{2})
is analytic in the region (
7.5
) but singular at some negative real
z
, we conclude that
\tau_{*}\leqslant\pi(\varkappa^{-1}-1)
, or in dimensional units,

\framebox{$\displaystyle\tau_{*}\leqslant\frac{\pi}{\varkappa}-\frac{1}{2T}.$}

(7.7)

This is a nontrivial relation because it connects high-frequency properties to the Lyapunov exponent, which is bounded by
2\pi T
. It was derived by a completely different method (not specific to SYK-like models) in
[
22
]
for
T=\infty
and in
[
23
]
in general. In the maximal chaos case, we get
\tau_{*}=0
, that is, the spectral function has subexponential (for example, polynomial) decay. In general, and in particular for the
q=4
SYK model, the bound (
7.7
) is not tight, see appendix
C.2
.

These are some questions we have come across:

1.

How to include dynamics in ’t Hooft’s action? This seems possible but not straightforward because the forward-propagating mode is described by the kinetic equation on the double Keldysh contour, and the backward-propagating mode has a similar but separate description. A special case of the effective model (
7.2
) was derived in
[
21
]
from the
G\Sigma
action on a certain contour. It would be interesting to generalize that derivation and track the origin of the factor
e^{i\varkappa\pi/2}
.

2.

What is the best theoretical bound on the analyticity domain of
F^{\mathrm{R}}
? We have explained the fact that for the large-
q
SYK model,
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},-\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{2})
is analytic in
z
in the entire plane with a branch cut from
-\infty
to
0
. Furthermore, the numerical results in appendix
C
indicate that the same is true for
q=4,6
. Is this a general property?

3.

How to compute
1/N
corrections to the OTOC? Such corrections are due to Feynman diagrams with ladders joining not only at the ends, but also in the middle.

Appendix A
Past and future horizon representations

For concreteness, we consider the fermionic case, namely, a set of Majorana fields
\chi_{\alpha}(t)
that satisfy Wick’s theorem and have spectral function
A
. The idea is to represent them in terms of (
1+1
)-d chiral Majorana fields
{\psi}_{\beta}(x,t)={\psi}_{\beta}(x-t)
whose spectral function is

A^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})=\{{\psi}_{\alpha}(t),{\psi}_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),\qquad A^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(A.1)

A general representation of this kind has the form

\chi_{\alpha}(t)=\int\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime}){\psi}_{\beta}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},\qquad L_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R},

(A.2)

or

\tilde{\chi}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\sum_{\beta}L_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)\tilde{\psi}_{\beta}(\omega),\qquad L_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)^{*}=L_{\alpha\beta}(-\omega),

(A.3)

where

L(\omega)L(\omega)^{\dagger}=A(\omega)\quad\;\text{for }\,\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

(A.4)

Let us now impose a causality condition,
L_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})=0
if
t-t^{\prime}<\mathrm{const}
. (One interpretation is that
\psi_{\beta}(t)
is a field on the past horizon of a black hole, whereas
\chi_{\alpha}(t)
is the same type of field at a fixed spatial location outside the horizon.) We will denote this representation by
L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)
; it admits an analytic continuation to the upper half plane. The future horizon representation
L^{\mathrm{A}}(\omega)
is defined similarly. Thus,

\displaystyle A(\omega)=L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)^{\dagger}=L^{\mathrm{A}}(\omega)L^{\mathrm{A}}(\omega)^{\dagger}\quad\;\text{for }\,\omega\in\mathbb{R},

(A.5)

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{R}}(-\omega^{*})^{*}=L^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)\;\,\text{for }\Im\omega\geqslant 0,\qquad L^{\mathrm{A}}(-\omega^{*})^{*}=L^{\mathrm{A}}(\omega)\;\,\text{for }\Im\omega\leqslant 0.

(A.6)

Equation (
A.5
) is related to the Birkhoff factorization problem: Given a continuous matrix-valued function
M
on the unit circle with
\det M(z)\neq 0
, find a decomposition

M(z)=L(z)\cdot R(z)\quad\;\text{for }\,|z|=1,

(A.7)

where
L
is analytic for
|z|\leqslant 1
and
R
is analytic for
|z|\geqslant 1
, and both functions are non-degenerate in their definition domains. If
M(z)>0
on the unit circle, then there exists a solution such that

R(z)=L(1/z^{*})^{*},

(A.8)

and it is unique up to a unitary transformation
[
34
]
.

Appendix B
Brownian OTOC

Another example that can be solved analytically is the Brownian SYK model
[
35
]
. The OTOC has been computed numerically (using the operator growth picture) in
[
36
]
and analytically in
[
21
]
. Here, we give a shorter derivation using our general method.

C.2
High-frequency noise vs. Lyapunov exponent

In the main text, we have derived inequality (
7.7
), which connects the exponential decay rate
\tau_{*}
of the spectral function to the Lyapunov exponent
\varkappa
. The numerical results in the last subsection for
q=4,6
indicate that
F^{\mathrm{R}}(z;\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{1},-\frac{\pi}{2}+it_{2})
is analytic in
z
in the entire plane with a branch cut from
-\infty
to
0
. One possible reason for this is that the bound (
7.7
) might be tight. In this subsection, we numerically compare
\tau_{*}
and
\frac{\pi}{\varkappa}-\frac{1}{2T}
for the
q=4
SYK in a broad range of temperatures.

To find
\tau_{*}
, we first numerically determine the spectral function
A(\omega)
using the Schwinger-Dyson equation on the (single) Keldysh contour. At thermal equilibrium, the functions
G^{\mathrm{K}}(t,t^{\prime})
and
G^{\mathrm{R}}(t,t^{\prime})
depend only on
t-t^{\prime}
; hence,

\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega-\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)},\qquad i\Sigma^{\mathrm{R}}(t)=J^{2}\Bigl(\bigl(iG^{\mathrm{K}+\mathrm{R}}(t)/2\bigr)^{q-1}-\bigl(iG^{\mathrm{K}-\mathrm{R}}(t)/2\bigr)^{q-1}\Bigr),

(C.11)

where the second equation is a special case of (
5.21
). The retarded Green function
G^{\mathrm{R}}
is used to compute the spectral function
A
and then the Keldysh Green function
G^{\mathrm{K}}
:

A(\omega)=-2\Im\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega),\qquad\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{K}}(\omega)=-iA(\omega)\tanh\frac{\beta\omega}{2}.

(C.12)

Equations (
C.11
) and (
C.12
) are solved iteratively with discretized
t
and
\omega
. The parameter
\tau_{*}
is obtained from the best exponential fit to the tails of the spectral function.

We then compute the Lyapunov exponent
\varkappa
by solving the linearized kinetic equation
[
13
,
14
]
:

\left|\tilde{G}^{\mathrm{R}}\Bigl(\omega+i\frac{\varkappa}{2}\Bigr)\right|^{2}\int\tilde{R}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega^{\prime})\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}=\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)\,,\quad\quad R(t)=(q-1)J^{2}\bigl(W^{(0)}(t,0)\bigr)^{q-2}.

(C.13)

Here we have introduced
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt~\Upsilon^{\mathrm{R}}(\pi+it)
. In numerics, the left-hand side of (
C.13
) becomes the vector
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{\mathrm{R}}
multiplied by a matrix
K(\varkappa)
. The Lyapunov exponent
\varkappa\in(0,2\pi/\beta)
is determined by requiring that the largest eigenvalue of
K(\varkappa)
is
1
.
