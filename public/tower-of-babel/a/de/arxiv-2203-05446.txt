Algorithms for the Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition Problem Anais do LIII Simpósio Brasileiro de Pesquisa Operacional, Vol 53, 2021 - 139228 https://proceedings.science/sbpo-2021/


Abstract
Given an Eulerian graph G G , in the Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition problem, we are interested in finding a collection of edge-disjoint cycles { E 1 , E 2 , … , E k } \{E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{k}\} in G G such that all edges of G G are in exactly one cycle and k k is maximum. We present an algorithm to solve the pricing problem of a column generation Integer Linear Programming (ILP) model introduced by [ Lancia and Serafini 2016 ] . Furthermore, we propose a greedy heuristic, which searches for minimum size cycles starting from a random vertex, and a heuristic based on partially solving the ILP model. We performed tests comparing the three approaches in relation to the quality of solutions and execution time, using distinct sets of Eulerian graphs, each set grouping graphs with different numbers of vertices and edges. Our experimental results show that the ILP based heuristic outperforms the other methods.

RESUMO

Dado um grafo Euleriano
G
, no problema de Decomposição Máxima de Ciclos em Grafos Eulerianos, estamos interessados em encontrar uma coleção de ciclos aresta-disjuntos
\{E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{k}\}
em
G
tal que todas as arestas de
G
pertençam a exatamente um ciclo e
k
seja máximo. Apresentamos um algoritmo para resolver o problema de
pricing
da geração de colunas para um modelo de Programação Linear Inteira (PLI) apresentado por [Lancia e Serafini, 2016]. Além disso, propomos uma heurística gulosa, que busca ciclos de tamanho mínimo partindo de um vértice aleatório, e uma heurística baseada na resolução parcial do modelo PLI.
Realizamos testes comparando as três abordagens em relação à qualidade das soluções e ao tempo de execução, usando conjuntos distintos de grafos Eulerianos aleatótios agrupados de acordo com a quantidade de vértices e arestas.
Nossos resultados experimentais mostram que a heurística baseada no modelo PLI supera os outros métodos.

1
Introduction

The problem of Maximum Graph Decomposition consists of partitioning the set of edges of a graph into a collection of subsets of edges
\{E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{k}\}
, such that
k
is maximum and each subset
E_{i}
induces a specific type of subgraph (e.g., trails, cycles). This is a well studied problem in combinatorial optimization
[
Ganesamurthy and Paulraja 2018
;
Caprara 1999
;
Mynhardt and van Bommel 2016
;
Heinrich 1992
;
Rodger 1990
]
.

In this work, we deal with the version of the problem where each subset induces a cycle. To guarantee that a decomposition always exists, we assume that the input graph is always Eulerian. In this way, we call this problem the
Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition
(
MAX-ECD
).
[
Caprara 1999
]
showed that
MAX-ECD
is NP-hard.
[
Caprara et al. 2003
]
presented an ILP model with an exponential number of variables. To cope with this,
[
Lancia and Serafini 2016
]
proposed a column generation approach to solve the ILP model of
[
Caprara et al. 2003
]
. Below we discuss our motivation to study the
MAX-ECD
.

In comparative genomics, the genome rearrangement distance problem receives two genomes
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
as input and consists in finding a minimum length sequence of rearrangements (mutations that alter segments of a genome) that transforms
\mathcal{G}_{1}
into
\mathcal{G}_{2}
. One common structure used to represent genomes is the Breakpoint Graph
[
Hannenhalli and Pevzner 1999
;
Bafna and Pevzner 1995
]
. In the Breakpoint Graph, there exists one vertex for each gene of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
. For each pair of adjacent elements in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
that are not adjacent in
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, there exists a black edge connecting these elements, and for each pair of adjacent elements in
\mathcal{G}_{2}
that are not adjacent in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, there exists a gray edge connecting these elements.

Most algorithms for the genome rearrangement distance need the decomposition of a Breakpoint Graph into cycles of alternating colors
[
Bafna and Pevzner 1995
;
Hannenhalli and Pevzner 1999
;
Rahman et al. 2008
]
.
[
Pinheiro et al. 2020
]
developed heuristics for finding decompositions of Breakpoint Graphs based on the Tabu Search metaheuristic
[
Glover 1989
;
Glover 1990
]
, and presented experimental results on simulated data.

[
Caprara 1999
]
showed a polynomial-time reduction from the Maximum Breakpoint Graph Decomposition problem to the Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition problem. Therefore, the results presented here have direct application in the genome rearrangement distance problem.

In this work, we present two heuristics for the
MAX-ECD
. The first one is a greedy algorithm that, at each step, finds a minimum sized cycle starting at a random vertex and adds it to the current decomposition. The second heuristic is based on solving a version of the ILP model of
[
Caprara et al. 2003
]
, whose original size is exponential in the number of edges, by restricting it to only a few variables. We evaluate the performance of these algorithms in practice comparing them with the column generation presented by
[
Lancia and Serafini 2016
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
formally introduces the problem and the definitions used in the text. Section
3
presents an integer linear programming (ILP) model for the problem. Section
4
describes the two heuristics we developed and Section
5
discusses the experimental results of the ILP model and the heuristics. Finally, in Section
6
we highlight our conclusions and give directions for future investigations.

Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition

A graph
G
is an ordered pair
(V,E)
, where
V
is a set of vertices and
E
is a set of edges (unordered pairs of vertices). Given two vertices
u,v\in V
, we say that they are
adjacent
(or
neighbors
) if
(u,v)\in E
. In this work, we consider only simple graphs, i.e., graphs such that there are no duplicated edges and with
u\neq v
, for all edges
(u,v)\in E
.

We say that an edge
(u,v)
is
incident
to the vertices
u
and
v
.
The
degree
of a vertex
v
is equal to the number of edges incident to
v
and it is denoted by
d(v)
.

A
trail
in a graph
G=(V,E)
is a sequence of vertices
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
, such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in E
, for all
1\leq i<n
, and there are no repetitions of edges (consecutive pairs of vertices in the trail). The
length
of a trail is equal to the number of edges in it. A
circuit
in a graph
G=(V,E)
is a trail
(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})
such that
v_{1}=v_{n}
. A
cycle
is a circuit without repeated vertices. A graph is called
connected
if, for all pair of vertices
u
and
v
, there exists a trail from
u
to
v
.

An
Eulerian circuit
in a graph
G=(V,E)
is a circuit that contains all edges of
G
. An
Eulerian graph
is a graph that has an Eulerian circuit.

A
decomposition
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a collection of subsets of edges
\{E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{k}\}
, such that the subsets are disjoint and their union is equal to
E
. If every subset of the collection induces a cycle in
G
, the partition forms a
cycle decomposition
of
G
, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

Now, given an Eulerian graph
G=(V,E)
, in the Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition problem
MAX-ECD
, we seek a cycle decomposition of
G
with maximum size. Note that, since the problem is defined only for Eulerian graphs, the existence of a cycle decomposition is guaranteed.

Integer Linear Programming Model

Before showing the ILP model presented by
[
Caprara et al. 2003
]
, we first introduce some additional notations. We use
\mathcal{H}
to represent the collection of cycles related to the solution constructed by the ILP model.
Given an Eulerian graph
G=(V,E)
, let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of all cycles in
G
. For each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
, let
x_{C}
be a binary variable such that
x_{C}=1
, if and only if
C\in\mathcal{H}
. Then, the model presented by
[
Caprara et al. 2003
]
reads:

\max\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}x_{C}

(1)

subject to:

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}:e\in C}x_{C}

\displaystyle\penalty\ \leq 1,

\displaystyle\forall e\in E

(2)

\displaystyle x_{C}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle\penalty\ \forall C\in\mathcal{C}

(3)

Constraint (
2
) guarantees that the cycles of the solution are edge-disjoint, and equation (
3
) defines the variables
x_{C}
as binary.
Note that a set of edge-disjoint cycles that do not cover all edges in the graph is a feasible solution of this model. However, such solution can not be optimal. Indeed, as the objective function (
1
) maximizes the number of cycles and
G
is Eulerian, in any optimal solution every edge must be covered by a cycle in
\mathcal{H}
.

Since the size of
\mathcal{C}
grows exponentially with the size of
E
, it is not viable to create all variables of the model for large instances. Therefore, we resort to column generation and branch-and-price techniques. At the beginning of the branch-and-bound algorithm the model is fed a small subset of
x_{C}
variables and more variables are added as needed until an optimal solution of the linear relaxation is reached.
The decision whether a variable is brought to the model or not is made by computing the
pricing subproblem
.
For this, consider the dual of the linear relaxation of the formulation given by equations (
1
)–(
3
):

\min\sum_{e\in E}y_{e}

(4)

subject to:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in C}y_{e}

\displaystyle\penalty\ \geq 1,

\displaystyle\forall C\in\mathcal{C}

(5)

\displaystyle y_{e}\penalty\

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\penalty\ \forall e\in E

(6)

The pricing subproblem amounts to decide if there exists a cycle
C
whose associated constraint in (
5
) is violated by the current dual variables
y^{*}
.
Since the size of
\mathcal{C}
is exponential in
|E|
, it is impractical to check all such constraints.
Instead, we use a combinatorial algorithm described below to solve the pricing subproblem.

For each cycle
C\in\mathcal{C}
, constraints (
5
) require that the sum of variables
y^{*}_{e}
, for
e\in C
, to be greater than or equal to
1
. Therefore, given a cycle
C
, if
\sum_{e\in C}y^{*}_{e}<1
, the associated constraint is violated.
Hence, in the separation algorithm, we create a weighted graph
G^{\prime}
with the same vertices and edges of
G
, where each edge
e
has weight
y^{*}_{e}
. A constraint of the dual model is violated if, and only if, the minimum weight cycle of
G^{\prime}
has weight less than one. Thus, the algorithm finds a minimum weight cycle
C^{*}
in
G^{\prime}
and, if its weight is less than one, the constraint related to
C^{*}
is violated and the variable
x_{C^{*}}
is added to the primal model. If the weight of
C^{*}
is greater than or equal to one, no variables need to be added to the linear relaxation of the primal model
[
Lancia and Serafini 2016
]
, since optimality has been reached.

So, the pricing problem is to find a minimum weight cycle
C^{*}
in the weighted graph
G^{\prime}
, without considering cycles related to variables
x_{C}
that were fixed to zero by the branch-and-bound algorithm, since we want to find new cycles to consider in the primal model. When no variables
x_{C}
are fixed to zero, we can use the
MINIMUM-CYCLE
algorithm described in Algorithm
1
. For each edge
(u,v)
, the algorithm finds a minimum weight path
P
from
u
to
v
, which does not have the edge
(u,v)
, and creates the cycle
P\cup{(u,v)}
. Note that this is the minimum weight cycle that contains the edge
(u,v)
. Then, the algorithm returns the generated cycle with minimum weight.

When the variables
\{x_{C_{1}},x_{C_{2}},\ldots,x_{C_{k}}\}
are fixed to zero, we need to forbid these cycles in the pricing problem. For this, we create copies
G_{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k}}
of
G^{\prime}
, such that
1\leq z_{i}\leq|C_{i}|
for
1\leq i\leq k
. In each copy
G_{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{k}}
, we forbid the cycles
C_{1}
to
C_{k}
by updating the weight of the
z_{i}
-th edge of
C_{i}
to
\infty
. The algorithm
MINIMUM-CYCLE
is executed for each altered copy of
G^{\prime}
and the minimum weight cycle found in all these executions is the solution of the pricing problem.

4
Heuristics for
MAX-ECD

In this section, we present two heuristics developed to solve the problem, which are named
GREEDY
and
ILP-HEURISTIC
.

The
GREEDY
heuristic finds a cycle decomposition as described in Algorithm
2
. At each iteration, a random vertex
v
with degree
d(v)>0
is chosen. The algorithm then performs a breadth-first search to find a minimum size cycle
C
starting at
v
, adds this cycle to the decomposition, and removes all edges of
C
from the graph.

Since the edges of a cycle added to the solution
\mathcal{H}
are removed before starting a new iteration, all cycles in
\mathcal{H}
are edge-disjoint. Note that after removing all edges of a cycle from the graph, all vertices remain with an even degree. Therefore, at each iteration, any vertex
v
with
d(v)>0
is in at least one cycle. Furthermore, since the algorithm always finds a cycle, eventually
|E|=0
, and the solution covers all edges of the original graph. The
GREEDY
heuristic has a time complexity of
O(|E|^{2})
.

The
ILP-HEURISTIC
consists in the following. Given a set of cycles
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{C}
, we create the model from Section
3
using only variables
x_{C}
such that
C\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. To ensure that the modified model is feasible, we can add the cycles of at least one valid decomposition in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Given a parameter

, we create the set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with the cycles of

This strategy guarantees that the optimal solution of the
ILP-HEURISTIC
is better than or equal to the best of the
k

GREEDY
heuristic.
However, a solution from the
ILP-HEURISTIC
may not cover all edges of the input graph, since we run the ILP model using a subset of
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, given a solution
\mathcal{H}
from the

for an Eulerian graph
G=(V,E)
, we apply a post-processing step with the
GREEDY
heuristic that finds a decomposition
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
for the graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
, where
E^{\prime}=\{(u,v)\in E\penalty\ |\penalty\ (u,v)\notin\mathcal{H}\}
, and returns
\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, which is then a valid decomposition for
G
. When we refer to the
ILP-HEURISTIC
, we assume that the post-processing step is also performed.

6
Conclusion

In this work we presented algorithms for the Maximum Eulerian Cycle Decomposition problem, alongside an experimental study of these algorithms and a column generation ILP model for the problem.

Our experiments showed good results for the ILP heuristic, which is an heuristic that partially solves the ILP model activating only the variables related to good cycles found by a greedy heuristic.

For future work, we intend to propose new algorithms based on metaheuristics used in the literature for combinatorial problems, such as Genetic Algorithms (GA) and
Greedy Randomized Adaptive Search Procedure (GRASP).
