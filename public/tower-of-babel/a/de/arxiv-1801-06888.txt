On Lagrangians with Reduced-Order Euler–Lagrange Equations
David SAUNDERS

Abstract
If a Lagrangian defining a variational problem has order k k then its Euler–Lagrange equations generically have order 2 ​ k 2k . This paper considers the case where the Euler–Lagrange equations have order strictly less than 2 ​ k 2k , and shows that in such a case the Lagrangian must be a polynomial in the highest-order derivative variables, with a specific upper bound on the degree of the polynomial. The paper also provides an explicit formulation, derived from a geometrical construction, of a family of such k k -th order Lagrangians, and it is conjectured that all such Lagrangians arise in this way.

Introduction

If
L
is a Lagrangian function depending on some independent variables
x^{i}
, some dependent variables
u^{\alpha}
, and some first derivative variables, then the resulting Euler–Lagrange equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\alpha}}-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x^{i}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{i}}=0

are generically of second order: the total derivative operator
{\rm d}/{\rm d}x^{i}
maps first-order variables to second-order variables. For a Lagrangian depending also on higher-order derivative variables
u^{\alpha}_{ij},u^{\alpha}_{ijh},\ldots
(of order up to
k
) the Euler–Lagrange equations, written in a multi-index notation as

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert I\rvert=0}^{k}(-1)^{\lvert I\rvert}\frac{{\rm d}^{\lvert I\rvert}}{{\rm d}x^{I}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{I}}=0,

are generically of order
2k
. This paper considers the case of
k
-th order Lagrangians whose Euler–Lagrange equations have order strictly less than
2k
.

The existence of Lagrangians with reduced-order Euler–Lagrange equations has been known for a long time. The Einstein–Hilbert Lagrangian from general relativity (see, for example,
[
2
]
) is second-order but its Euler–Lagrange equations, the Einstein field equations, are again second-order rather than fourth-order. In this case, though, the Lagrangian may be written (although not invariantly) as the sum of a first-order Lagrangian and a total divergence
[
2
, Sections 3.3.1 and 3.3.2]
, so that these second-order Euler–Lagrange equations may in fact be derived from a first-order Lagrangian.

Some examples with more substance may be found in
[
4
,
5
]
in the context of Lagrangians involving a single independent variable. Any such
k
-th order Lagrangian which is linear in the derivative variables of highest order
k
will give rise to Euler–Lagrange equations of order strictly less than
2k
. Of course any total derivative
L={\rm d}f/{\rm d}x
will satisfy this condition trivially, because its Euler–Lagrange equations will vanish identically. But not every Lagrangian linear in the highest order derivatives is a total derivative (or a total derivative plus a lower-order function); the simplest such example is the first-order Lagrangian
L_{1}=uv_{x}-vu_{x}
, giving rise to the first-order Euler–Lagrange equations
u_{x}=v_{x}=0
. On the other hand, a Lagrangian involving a single independent variable which is not linear in the derivative variables of highest order
k
will necessarily give rise to Euler–Lagrange equations of order
2k
.

With more than one independent variable, the situation becomes more complicated. It remains the case that linearity in the highest order derivatives is a sufficient condition for reduced-order Euler–Lagrange equations; but now the condition is no longer necessary. For example, in
[
8
]
a class of ‘special Lagrangians’ is defined. These are differential forms (integrands of the variational problem) rather than functions, and they are constructed using a procedure of horizontalization. The coefficient function (the Lagrangian function) is a polynomial of degree
m
in the derivative variables of highest order
k
, where
m
is the number of independent variables, and indeed the polynomial is a linear combination of determinants in those variables. Once again, the Euler–Lagrange equations have order strictly less than
2k
. A simple example is the Lagrangian
L_{2}=u_{x}\big(u_{xx}u_{yy}-u_{xy}^{2}\big)
which gives rise to a third order Euler–Lagrange equation; the
2
-form
L_{2}{\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}y
is the horizontalization of
u_{x}{\rm d}u_{x}\wedge{\rm d}u_{y}
.

These special Lagrangians do not, though, exhaust the possibilities. Consider a problem with two independent variables
x
,
y
and three dependent variables
u
,
v
,
w
. The second order Lagrangian

\displaystyle L_{3}=u_{xx}v_{xy}w_{yy}-u_{xx}v_{yy}w_{xy}+u_{xy}v_{yy}w_{xx}-u_{xy}v_{xx}w_{yy}+u_{yy}v_{xx}w_{xy}-u_{yy}v_{xy}w_{xx}

gives rise to third order Euler–Lagrange equations but is not a special Lagrangian in the sense of
[
8
]
because it is cubic rather than quadratic in the second derivative variables (although it is again a determinant). The same comment applies to the fourth order Lagrangian

\displaystyle L_{4}=u_{xxxx}u_{xxyy}u_{yyyy}+2u_{xxxy}u_{xxyy}u_{xyyy}-u_{xxxx}u_{xyyy}^{2}-u_{xxxy}^{2}u_{yyyy}-u_{xxyy}^{3},

which gives rise to a sixth order Euler–Lagrange equation. This suggests that some more general alternating structure might be needed.

We obtain such a structure by using a version of the theory of differential hyperforms
[
6
]

These are tensors with symmetry properties corresponding to Young diagrams; if the diagram contains a single column then the tensor is purely alternating and so corresponds to an ordinary differential form. We shall make particular use of hyperforms which are alternating combinations of symmetric tensors. It is known that hyperforms give rise to particular types of determinant known as hyperjacobians (see

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2

5

Our conjecture is that every Lagrangian with reduced-order Euler–Lagrange equations may be constructed as a sum of Lagrangians obtained in this way, and finally in Section
6
we present some evidence in support of that conjecture. It would also be interesting, for future work, to attempt to extend this approach to give geometrical constructions of Lagrangians of order
k
whose Euler–Lagrange equations have order less than
2k-1

2k-2

3
The polynomial property

Our first result is that if a Lagrangian density of order
k
gives rise to a projectable Euler–Lagrange form then in any coordinate system the corresponding Lagrangian function must be a polynomial of order at most
p_{k}
in the highest order derivative variables
u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}}
, where
p_{k}
is the number of distinct multi-indices
\mathrm{K}
of length
k
.

To illustrate our approach, we describe the procedure for two special cases.

In the first special case we take
k=2
, so that we want to show that
L
must be a polynomial of degree at most
p_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}m(m+1)
in the second order derivative variables. We know, as a consequence of projectability, that the expression

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert=2}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{\mathrm{K}}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}

has order strictly less than
4
, so if we expand the second order total derivatives we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert\mathrm{J}\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert=2}u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{J}+\mathrm{K}}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{J}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}+\cdots,

where the dots indicate terms whose order is less than
4
. It follows that, for each multi-index
H
with
\lvert H\rvert=4
, we must have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{J}+\mathrm{K}=H}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{J}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}=0.

(3.1)

Fix an index
i
with
1\leq i\leq m
, and let
H_{i}
be the multi-index satisfying
H_{i}(i)=4
,
H_{i}(j)=0
for
j\neq i
; here and more generally we call a multi-index with only a single nonzero entry a
pure multi-index
. We then see immediately from equation (
3.1
) that if
\mathrm{K}_{i}
is the pure multi-index satisfying
\mathrm{K}_{i}(i)=2
,
\mathrm{K}_{i}(j)=0
for
j\neq i
then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}}=0.

(3.2)

Now fix indices
i
,
j
with
j\neq i
, and let
H_{ij}
be the multi-index satisfying
H_{ij}(i)=H_{ij}(j)=2
,
H_{ij}(h)=0
for

; we call this a
mixed multi-index
. If
\mathrm{K}_{ij}
is the mixed multi-index satisfying
\mathrm{K}_{ij}(i)=\mathrm{K}_{ij}(j)=1
,
\mathrm{K}_{ij}(h)=0
for

then we see from (

) that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{ij}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{ij}}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{j}}}-\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{j}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}},

so that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{4}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{ij}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{ij}}\partial u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}_{ih}}\partial u^{\delta}_{\mathrm{K}_{ih}}}=\frac{\partial^{4}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{j}}\partial u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\delta}_{\mathrm{K}_{h}}}+\frac{\partial^{4}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{j}}\partial u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}_{h}}\partial u^{\delta}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\frac{\partial^{4} L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{ij}} \partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{ij}} \partial u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}_{ih}} \partial u^{\delta}_{\mathrm{K}_{ih}}}=}{}+\frac{\partial^{4}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{j}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\delta}_{\mathrm{K}_{h}}}+\frac{\partial^{4}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}_{j}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}\partial u^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}_{h}}\partial u^{\delta}_{\mathrm{K}_{i}}}=0.

(3.3)

Thus if the expression

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{r}L}{\partial u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathrm{J}_{1}}\partial u^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mathrm{J}_{2}}\cdots\partial u^{\alpha_{r}}_{\mathrm{J}_{r}}}

does not vanish then in the list
(\mathrm{J}_{1},\mathrm{J}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{J}_{r})
each distinct pure multi-index
\mathrm{K}_{i}
can appear at most once (from (

)), and each distinct mixed multi-index
\mathrm{K}_{ij}

\mathrm{K}_{ij}

\mathrm{K}_{ih}

Let

be the number of pure multi-indices

in the list, and let

be the number of mixed multi-indices

with, respectively, multiplicities

\displaystyle r=a+b+2c+3d\leq m-2(c+d)+\tfrac{1}{2}m(m-1)+c+2d\leq\tfrac{1}{2}m(m+1)=p_{2}.

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{k-h}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{(k-l,l)}\partial u^{\beta}_{(h+l,k-h-l)}}=0.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\lvert\mathrm{J}\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{K}_{1}\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{K}_{2}\rvert=k

This is just the parallelogram rule for any Euclidean norm, that

\displaystyle 2\lVert x\rVert^{2}\leq 2\lVert x\rVert^{2}+2\lVert y\rVert^{2}=\lVert x+y\rVert^{2}+\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}

with equality only when

2k

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert\mathrm{J}\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert=k}u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{J}+\mathrm{K}}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{J}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}+\cdots,

\lvert H\rvert=2k
, we must have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{J}+\mathrm{K}=H}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{J}}\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}=0.

(3.6)

Now consider the repeated derivative of order
p+1

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{r}L}{\partial u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathrm{J}_{1}}\partial u^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mathrm{J}_{2}}\cdots\partial u^{\alpha_{p+1}}_{\mathrm{J}_{p+1}}},

(3.7)

where
\lvert\mathrm{J}_{1}\rvert=\lvert\mathrm{J}_{2}\rvert=\cdots=\lvert\mathrm{J}_{p+1}\rvert=k
, and suppose that in the list of multi-indices
(\mathrm{J}_{1},\mathrm{J}_{2},{\ldots},\mathrm{J}_{p+1})
we have
\mathrm{J}_{r}=\mathrm{J}_{s}=\mathrm{J}
. Use (
3.6
) to write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha_{r}}_{\mathrm{J}}\partial u^{\alpha_{s}}_{\mathrm{J}}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{K}_{1}+\mathrm{K}_{2}=2\mathrm{J}\\
\mathrm{K}_{1},\mathrm{K}_{2}\neq\mathrm{J}\end{subarray}}-\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial u^{\alpha_{r}}_{\mathrm{K}_{1}}\partial u^{\alpha_{s}}_{\mathrm{K}_{2}}},

so that by Lemma
3.1
we have in each term on the right-hand side
\lVert\mathrm{K}_{1}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\mathrm{K}_{2}\rVert^{2}>2\lVert\mathrm{J}\rVert^{2}
. By repeating this process we must eventually be able to write (
3.7
) as a sum or difference of terms, each of which has
m+1
pure multi-indices of maximum weight
k^{2}
(so that two of its pure multi-indices must be equal) and therefore each of which must vanish.
∎

4
Hyperforms

The necessary condition given above for a Lagrangian density
\lambda
on
J^{k}\pi
to have projectable Euler–Lagrange equations is obviously not sufficient; but the requirement that
\lambda
be the horizontalization of some
m
-form on
J^{k-1}\pi
is, as noted in the Introduction, too strong. We shall define a weaker condition on
\lambda
which will still be sufficient to ensure that the Euler–Lagrange equations are projectable, using the idea of a horizontal
(p,q)
hyperform introduced in Section
2
.

We first consider horizontal
(1,q)
hyperforms; any such hyperform
\theta
may be written in coordinates on
U^{k}
as
\theta_{\mathcal{J}}{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}}
where
\theta_{\mathcal{J}}\in C^{\infty}\big(U^{k}\big)
. We have mentioned that
\pi_{k,k-1}\colon J^{k}\pi\to J^{k-1}\pi
is an affine bundle so that for any point of
J^{k-1}\pi
the fibre over that point is an affine space. The restriction of
\theta
to that fibre takes its values in the appropriate fibre of
S^{q}T^{*}M
, a vector space, so it makes sense to ask whether that restriction is an affine map. If this is the case for every fibre of
\pi_{k-1,k}
then we say that
\theta
is an
affine
(1,q)
hyperform
; the coordinate representation of
\theta
then becomes

\displaystyle\big(\theta^{\mathrm{K}}_{\alpha\mathcal{J}}u^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{K}}+\theta_{\mathcal{J}}\big){\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}},

where now
\theta^{\mathrm{K}}_{\alpha\mathcal{J}},\theta_{\mathcal{J}}\in C^{\infty}(U^{k-1})
,
U^{k-1}\subset J^{k-1}\pi
. (Recall here that the roman multi-indices
\mathrm{K}
have length
k
, whereas the calligraphic multi-indices
\mathcal{J}
have length
q
.)

The collection of affine hyperforms is, however, too large for our purposes. To obtain a suitable restriction, we note that the map
\theta\colon J^{k}\pi\to S^{q}T^{*}M
is affine precisely when the associated difference map
\bar{\theta}\colon V\pi\otimes_{J^{k-1}\pi}S^{k}T^{*}M\to S^{q}T^{*}M
is linear on each fibre over
J^{k-1}\pi
. We say that
\theta
is a
special affine hyperform
if there is a tensor
\tilde{\theta}\in V\pi^{*}\otimes_{J^{k-1}\pi}S^{k-q}TM
such that the fibre-linear map
\bar{\theta}
is given by the contraction of elements of
V\pi\otimes_{J^{k-1}\pi}S^{k}T^{*}M
with
\tilde{\theta}
. We shall let
\Omega^{1,q}_{\mathrm{sa}}\big(J^{k}\pi\big)
denote the collection of such special affine hyperforms; it is a module over
C^{\infty}\big(J^{k-1}\pi\big)
, though not of course over
C^{\infty}\big(J^{k}\pi\big)
. A special affine hyperform may therefore be written in coordinates as

\displaystyle\bigl(\theta^{I}_{\alpha}u^{\alpha}_{I+\mathcal{J}}+\theta_{\mathcal{J}}\bigr){\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}},

where
\theta^{I}_{\alpha},\theta_{\mathcal{J}}\in C^{\infty}\big(U^{k-1}\big)
,
U^{k-1}\subset J^{k-1}\pi
. (Here the italic multi-indices
I
have length
k-q
.)

As examples of affine and special affine hyperforms, consider the case where
m=2
and
n=1
, with coordinates
x
,
y
,
u
, and where
q=k=2
, so that in this case each multi-index
I
is zero. An affine hyperform will have a coordinate representation

\displaystyle\bigl(\theta^{xx}_{xx}u_{xx}+\theta^{xy}_{xx}u_{xy}+\theta^{yy}_{xx}u_{yy}+\theta_{xx}\bigr){\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+\bigl(\theta^{xx}_{xy}u_{xx}+\theta^{xy}_{xy}u_{xy}+\theta^{yy}_{xy}u_{yy}+\theta_{xy}\bigr){\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\bigl(\theta^{xx}_{yy}u_{yy}+\theta^{xy}_{yy}u_{xy}+\theta^{yy}_{yy}u_{yy}+\theta_{yy}\bigr){\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y,

where the functions
\theta^{xx}_{xx},\theta^{xy}_{xx},\ldots
are at most first order, whereas a special affine hyperform will have a coordinate representation

\displaystyle\bigl(\theta_{0}u_{xx}+\theta_{xx}\bigr){\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+\bigl(\theta_{0}u_{xy}+\theta_{xy}\bigr){\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+\bigl(\theta_{0}u_{yy}+\theta_{yy}\bigr){\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y,

where each term involves only a single second order coordinate, and where the (at most first order) function
\theta_{0}
is the same for all three terms.

We may see the relationship between this definition and the operation of horizontalization on ordinary
1
-forms by considering the special case where
q=1
. In this case a special affine hyperform
\theta
(now just a horizontal
1
-form) may be written in coordinates as
\big(\theta^{I}_{\alpha}u^{\alpha}_{I+1_{i}}+\theta_{i}\big){\rm d}x^{i}
(where
1_{i}
denotes the multi-index with a single
1
in position
i
) and is the horizontalization of, for instance, the
1
-form
\theta^{I}_{\alpha}{\rm d}u^{\alpha}_{I}+\theta_{i}{\rm d}x^{i}
. There is, however, no well-defined horizontalization operator mapping forms to hyperforms when
q\geq 2
.

We now consider horizontal
(p,q)
hyperforms, where
p
is fixed to equal the number
p_{q}
of distinct multi-indices
I\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
of length
q
, so that

\displaystyle p_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}m+q-1\\
q\end{pmatrix}=\frac{(m+q-1)!}{q!(m-1)!};

the fibre dimension of
S^{q}T^{*}M
is then equal to
p_{q}
, so that
\textstyle\bigwedge^{p_{q}}S^{q}T^{*}M\to J^{k}\pi
is a line bundle. We shall say that such a section of this bundle, a horizontal
(p_{q},q)
hyperform
\omega
, is
hyperaffine
if it can be written as a sum of wedge products of special affine
(1,q)
hyperforms, and we shall let
\Omega^{p_{q},q}_{\mathrm{ha}}\big(J^{k}\pi\big)
denote the collection of such hyperforms; again this is a module over
C^{\infty}\big(J^{k-1}\pi\big)
. If for a single wedge product

\displaystyle\omega=\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{p_{q}},

where

\displaystyle\theta_{r}\in\Omega^{1,q}_{\mathrm{ha}}\big(J^{k}\pi\big),\qquad\theta_{r}=\bigl(\theta^{I}_{r,\alpha}u^{\alpha}_{I+\mathcal{J}}+\theta_{r,\mathcal{J}}\bigr){\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}},

then in the coordinate expression for
\omega
the coefficient of the single local basis element
{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}_{1}}\wedge{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}_{p_{q}}}
will be given as a linear combination (by functions projectable to
J^{k-1}\pi
) of determinants in the highest order derivative variables
u^{\alpha}_{I+\mathcal{J}}
, ranging in size up to
(p_{q}\times p_{q})
; for instance the largest determinant will take the form

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}u^{\alpha_{1}}_{I_{1}+\mathcal{J}_{1}}&u^{\alpha_{1}}_{I_{1}+\mathcal{J}_{2}}&\cdots&u^{\alpha_{1}}_{I_{1}+\mathcal{J}_{p_{q}}}\\
u^{\alpha_{2}}_{I_{2}+\mathcal{J}_{1}}&u^{\alpha_{2}}_{I_{2}+\mathcal{J}_{2}}&\cdots&u^{\alpha_{2}}_{I_{2}+\mathcal{J}_{p_{q}}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
u^{\alpha_{p_{q}}}_{I_{p_{q}}+\mathcal{J}_{1}}&u^{\alpha_{p_{q}}}_{I_{p_{q}}+\mathcal{J}_{2}}&\cdots&u^{\alpha_{p_{q}}}_{I_{p_{q}}+\mathcal{J}_{p_{q}}}\end{vmatrix},

(4.1)

where
u^{\alpha_{1}}_{I_{1}},u^{\alpha_{2}}_{I_{2}},\ldots,u^{\alpha_{p_{q}}}_{I_{p_{q}}}
are derivative variables of order
k-q
, and smaller determinants, arising when one or more of the
(1,q)
hyperforms
\theta_{r}
is projectable to
J^{l}\pi
with
l<k
, will be obtained as suitably-sized minors. (If those derivative variables are not distinct then the largest determinant will vanish, and this always happens when
n<p_{q}
. It is nevertheless the case that sufficiently small minors will be nonzero.)

As examples, we may see that the four Lagrangian functions mentioned in the Introduction all arise as such coefficients. For
L_{1}
we take
q=p_{q}=k=1
and for
L_{2}
we take
q=1
,
p_{q}=2
,
k=2
; both the corresponding Lagrangian densities arise from conventional horizontalization. For
L_{3}
we take
q=k=2
,
p_{q}=3
, the variables
u
,
v
,
w
and the hyperform obtained from the wedge product

\displaystyle\bigl(u_{xx}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+u_{xy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+u_{yy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y\bigr)\wedge\bigl(v_{xx}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+v_{xy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+v_{yy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\wedge\bigl(w_{xx}{\rm d}x\odot dx+w_{xy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+w_{yy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y\bigr),

so that

\displaystyle L_{3}=\begin{vmatrix}u_{xx}&u_{xy}&u_{yy}\\
v_{xx}&v_{xy}&v_{yy}\\
w_{xx}&w_{xy}&w_{yy}\end{vmatrix};

for
L_{4}
we take
q=2
,
p_{q}=3
,
k=4
, the variables
u_{xx}
,
u_{xy}
,
u_{yy}
and the hyperform obtained from the wedge product

\displaystyle\bigl(u_{xxxx}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+u_{xxxy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+u_{xxyy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\wedge\bigl(u_{xxxy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+u_{xxyy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+u_{xyyy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\wedge\bigl(u_{xxyy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+u_{xyyy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+u_{yyyy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y\bigr),

so that

\displaystyle L_{4}=\begin{vmatrix}u_{xxxx}&u_{xxxy}&u_{xxyy}\\
u_{xxxy}&u_{xxyy}&u_{xyyy}\\
u_{xxyy}&u_{xyyy}&u_{yyyy}\end{vmatrix}.

5
Hyperaffine Lagrangians

The examples at the end of the previous section suggest how we might relate the construction of hyperaffine
(p_{q},q)
hyperforms to Lagrangian densities. We note that such a hyperform may be written in coordinates as

\displaystyle\omega=\omega_{q}{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}_{1}}\wedge{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}x^{\mathcal{J}_{p_{q}}},

and when
q>1
then this is obviously different from an ordinary horizontal
m
-form such as
\lambda
. The two types of object are, nevertheless, related: they are both sections of line bundles, and in coordinates each has a single coefficient function,
L
or
\omega_{q}
. Furthermore, under a change of coordinates
(x^{i},u^{\alpha})\mapsto(\tilde{x}^{i},\tilde{u}^{\alpha})
,
L
is altered by the Jacobian of the transformation
x^{i}\mapsto\tilde{x}^{i}
, whereas
\omega_{q}
is altered by a power of that Jacobian. As the condition for
\omega
to be hyperaffine may be expressed in terms of
\omega_{q}
in a way which is independent of transformations of the base coordinates
x^{i}
, it makes sense to say that a Lagrangian density
\lambda
is hyperaffine if, in any coordinate system, the corresponding local Lagrangian function
L
may be written as a sum
L=\sum\limits_{q=1}^{k}\omega_{q}
where each
\omega_{q}
is the coefficient in that coordinate system of a hyperaffine
(p_{q},q)
hyperform with
p_{q}=(m+q-1)!/q!(m-1)!
.

For example, in the case where
m=2
and
n=1
with coordinates
x
,
y
,
u
, we might consider the third-order Lagrangian function

\displaystyle L=u_{xxx}u_{yyy}-u_{xxy}u_{xyy}.

We may write
L
as
\omega_{2}
, the scalar coefficient of
\omega=\theta_{1}\wedge\theta_{2}\wedge\theta_{3}
, where

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=u_{xxx}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+u_{xxy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+u_{xyy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y,

\displaystyle\theta_{2}=u_{xxy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}x+u_{xyy}{\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y+u_{yyy}{\rm d}y\odot{\rm d}y,

\displaystyle\theta_{3}={\rm d}x\odot{\rm d}y,

so that
\omega_{2}
is a (non-vanishing)
2\times 2
minor of the (vanishing) determinant

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}u_{xxx}&u_{xxy}&u_{xyy}\\
u_{xxy}&u_{xyy}&u_{yyy}\\
u_{xxy}&u_{xyy}&u_{yyy}\end{vmatrix}.

We see that
L
is a null Lagrangian, so its Euler–Lagrange equations are trivially projectable. Indeed the significance of our definition comes from the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\lambda
is a hyperaffine Lagrangian density on
J^{k}\pi
then
\lambda
has projectable Euler–Lagrange equations.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove that a function
L
given in coordinates as an
h\times h
minor of the determinant (
4.1
) gives rise to Euler–Lagrange equations of order strictly less than
2k
. As terms in those equations of order
2k
can arise only when considering

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert=k}\frac{{\rm d}^{\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert}}{{\rm d}x^{\mathrm{K}}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}},

where we have written the sum over the multi-indices
\mathrm{K}
explicitly, it is sufficient to show that each such term (for a given index
\beta
) vanishes when
L
is such a determinant.

Write
L
in the form

\displaystyle L=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}}\varepsilon_{\sigma}u^{\alpha_{1}}_{I_{1}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(1)}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{I_{2}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(2)}}\cdots u^{\alpha_{h}}_{I_{h}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(h)}},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{h}
is the permutation group and
\varepsilon_{\sigma}=\pm 1
is the parity of the permutation
\sigma
; then for any given multi-index
\mathrm{K}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert}}{{\rm d}x^{\mathrm{K}}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r,s\leq h\\
s\neq r\end{subarray}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}}\delta^{\alpha_{r}}_{\beta}\delta^{K}_{I_{r}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(r)}}\varepsilon_{\sigma}\Phi_{rs\sigma}u^{\alpha_{s}}_{I_{r}+I_{s}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(r)}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(s)}},

where the coefficient functions
\Phi_{rs\sigma}
are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{rs\sigma}=u^{\alpha_{1}}_{I_{1}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(1)}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{I_{2}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(2)}}\cdots\widehat{r}\cdots\widehat{s}\cdots u^{\alpha_{h}}_{I_{h}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(h)}}

with the circumflex denoting the omission of a factor in the product. As the multi-indices
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{h}
and
\mathcal{J}_{1},\mathcal{J}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{h}
are given, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert=k}\frac{{\rm d}^{\lvert\mathrm{K}\rvert}}{{\rm d}x^{\mathrm{K}}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial u^{\beta}_{\mathrm{K}}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r,s\leq h\\
s\neq r\end{subarray}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}}\delta^{\alpha_{r}}_{\beta}\varepsilon_{\sigma}\Phi_{rs\sigma}u^{\alpha_{s}}_{I_{r}+I_{s}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(r)}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(s)}},

where the factor
\delta^{K}_{I_{r}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(r)}}
on the right-hand side is omitted.

Fix values for
r
and
s
; we shall show that

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}}\delta^{\alpha_{r}}_{\beta}\varepsilon_{\sigma}\Phi_{rs\sigma}u^{\alpha_{s}}_{I_{r}+I_{s}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(r)}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(s)}}=0.

To see this, for each
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{h}
let
\tilde{\sigma}
be the permutation given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(r)=\sigma(s),\qquad\tilde{\sigma}(s)=\sigma(r),\qquad\tilde{\sigma}(t)=\sigma(t)\qquad\text{for $1\leq t\leq h$, $t\neq r,s$}.

We obtain in this way a partition of
\mathfrak{S}_{h}
into equivalence classes of the form
\{\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}\}
, where each equivalence class contains exactly two elements because
r\neq s
. As
\Phi_{rs\tilde{\sigma}}=\Phi_{rs\sigma}
,
\mathcal{J}_{\tilde{\sigma}(r)}+\mathcal{J}_{\tilde{\sigma}(s)}=\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(r)}+\mathcal{J}_{\sigma(s)}
and
\varepsilon_{\sigma}+\varepsilon_{\tilde{\sigma}}=0
, the result follows.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

The upper bound
p_{k}
given in Theorem
3.2
, for the polynomial degree of a Lagrangian in the highest order derivatives, is sharp if the number of independent variables
n
satisfies
n\geq p_{k}
.

Proof.

Take
q=k
, and let
\mathcal{J}_{1},\mathcal{J}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{J}_{p_{k}}
be the distinct multi-indices of length
k
. If

\displaystyle L=\begin{vmatrix}u^{1}_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}&u^{1}_{\mathcal{J}_{2}}&\cdots&u^{1}_{\mathcal{J}_{p_{k}}}\\
u^{2}_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}&u^{2}_{\mathcal{J}_{2}}&\cdots&u^{2}_{\mathcal{J}_{p_{k}}}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
u^{p_{k}}_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}&u^{p_{k}}_{\mathcal{J}_{2}}&\cdots&u^{p_{k}}_{\mathcal{J}_{p_{k}}}\end{vmatrix}

(so that all the multi-indices
I_{r}
are zero) then
L
is the coefficient of a hyperaffine hyperform, so that it gives rise to projectable Euler–Lagrange equations by Theorem
5.1
.
∎

6
Discussion

The arguments above show that there is a correspondence between hyperaffine
(p_{q},q)
hyperforms and Lagrangian densities with projectable Euler–Lagrange equations. The correspondence is certainly not injective, even locally in a fixed coordinate system. For instance, with
m=k=2
and
n=3
the Lagrangian functions

\displaystyle L_{3}=\begin{vmatrix}u_{xx}&u_{xy}&u_{yy}\\
v_{xx}&v_{xy}&v_{yy}\\
w_{xx}&w_{xy}&w_{yy}\end{vmatrix}\!,\!\!\qquad L_{5}=w\begin{vmatrix}u_{xx}&u_{xy}\\
v_{xy}&v_{yy}\end{vmatrix}\!,\!\!\qquad L_{6}=w\begin{vmatrix}u_{xx}&u_{xy}\\
v_{xx}&v_{xy}\end{vmatrix}=w\begin{vmatrix}u_{xx}&u_{xy}&u_{yy}\\
v_{xx}&v_{xy}&v_{yy}\\
0&0&1\end{vmatrix}

are all hyperaffine; we have (temporarily omitting the symmetric product symbol
\odot
)

\displaystyle L_{3}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\wedge{\rm d}y{\rm d}y=\bigl(u_{xx}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x+u_{xy}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+u_{yy}{\rm d}y{\rm d}y\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{L_3 {\rm d}x {\rm d}x \wedge{\rm d}x {\rm d}y \wedge{\rm d}y {\rm d}y =}{}\wedge\bigl(v_{xx}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x+v_{xy}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+v_{yy}{\rm d}y{\rm d}y\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{L_3 {\rm d}x {\rm d}x \wedge{\rm d}x {\rm d}y \wedge{\rm d}y {\rm d}y =}{}\wedge\bigl(w_{xx}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x+w_{xy}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+w_{yy}{\rm d}y{\rm d}y\bigr),

\displaystyle L_{5}{\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}y=\bigl(w(u_{xx}{\rm d}x+u_{xy}{\rm d}y)\bigr)\wedge\bigl(v_{xy}{\rm d}x+v_{yy}{\rm d}y\bigr),

but

\displaystyle L_{6}{\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}y=\bigl(w(u_{xx}{\rm d}x+u_{xy}{\rm d}y\bigr)\wedge\bigl(v_{xx}{\rm d}x+v_{xy}{\rm d}y\bigr),

\displaystyle L_{6}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x\wedge{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\wedge{\rm d}y{\rm d}y=\bigl(u_{xx}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x+u_{xy}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+u_{yy}{\rm d}y{\rm d}y\bigr)

\displaystyle\hphantom{L_6 {\rm d}x {\rm d}x \wedge{\rm d}x {\rm d}y \wedge{\rm d}y {\rm d}y =}{}\wedge\bigl(v_{xx}{\rm d}x{\rm d}x+v_{xy}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y+v_{yy}{\rm d}y{\rm d}y\bigr)\wedge{\rm d}y{\rm d}y.

We do, however, make the conjecture that the correspondence is surjective: that is, that if a Lagrangian density has projectable Euler–Lagrange equations then, in any coordinate system, its Lagrangian function must be a sum of determinants of the form (
4.1
), or of minors of such determinants with essentially the same format. One might clearly attempt to establish such a conjecture by considering the homogeneous components of the Lagrangian, and it is certainly the case that the quadratic component satisfies the condition.

Proposition 6.1
.

If the Lagrangian density
\lambda
on
J^{k}\pi
has projectable Euler–Lagrange equations then the quadratic terms of the polynomial Lagrangian function
L
are determinants with a hyperaffine structure.

Proof.

Suppose the quadratic terms of
L
are
A^{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}u^{\alpha_{1}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}
where
\lvert{\mathrm{H}}_{1}\rvert=\lvert{\mathrm{H}}_{2}\rvert=k
. Partition the set of quadratic terms according to the multi-index
H={\mathrm{H}}_{1}+{\mathrm{H}}_{2}
, and consider the terms

\displaystyle\Psi_{H}=\sum_{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}+{\mathrm{H}}_{2}=H}A^{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}u^{\alpha_{1}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}

in a single component of the partition. There must be at least two distinct terms; for if there were only a single term then there would have to be an index
i
such that
{\mathrm{H}}_{1}(i)={\mathrm{H}}_{2}(i)=k
, and then (
3.6
) would imply
A^{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=0
.

Choose, arbitrarily, one term
A^{\mathrm{K}_{1}\mathrm{K}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathrm{K}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mathrm{K}_{2}}
, so that (
3.6
) now gives

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{K}_{1}\mathrm{K}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathrm{H}}_{1}+{\mathrm{H}}_{2}=H\\
({\mathrm{H}}_{1},{\mathrm{H}}_{2})\neq(\mathrm{K}_{1},\mathrm{K}_{2})\end{subarray}}-A^{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}},

and hence

\displaystyle\Psi_{H}=\sum_{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}+{\mathrm{H}}_{2}=H}A^{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\bigl(u^{\alpha_{1}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}-u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathrm{K}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mathrm{K}_{2}}\bigr),

so that each
\Psi_{H}
is a sum of determinants. (The restriction
({\mathrm{H}}_{1},{\mathrm{H}}_{2})\neq(\mathrm{K}_{1},\mathrm{K}_{2})
is omitted from the latter sum because if
({\mathrm{H}}_{1},{\mathrm{H}}_{2})=(\mathrm{K}_{1},\mathrm{K}_{2})
then the term vanishes.)

To see that each determinant has a hyperaffine structure (that is, can be written in the form (
4.1
)) consider a single expression

\displaystyle u^{\alpha_{1}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{{\mathrm{H}}_{2}}-u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathrm{K}_{1}}u^{\alpha_{2}}_{\mathrm{K}_{2}}

(6.1)

and let
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
