Non-Euclidean Differentially Private Stochastic Convex Optimization: Optimal Rates in Linear Time Thanks: This work is an extension of [ 7 ] .
Raef Bassily, Cristóbal Guzmán, Anupama Nandi

Abstract
Differentially private (DP) stochastic convex optimization (SCO) is a fundamental problem, where the goal is to approximately minimize the population risk with respect to a convex loss function, given a dataset of n n i.i.d. samples from a distribution, while satisfying differential privacy with respect to the dataset. Most of the existing works in the literature of private convex optimization focus on the Euclidean (i.e., ℓ 2 \ell_{2} ) setting, where the loss is assumed to be Lipschitz (and possibly smooth) w.r.t. the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} norm over a constraint set with bounded ℓ 2 \ell_{2} diameter. Algorithms based on noisy stochastic gradient descent (SGD) are known to attain the optimal excess risk in this setting. In this work, we conduct a systematic study of DP-SCO for ℓ p \ell_{p} -setups under a standard smoothness assumption on the loss. For 1 < p ≤ 2 1<p\leq 2 , under a standard smoothness assumption, we give a new, linear-time DP-SCO algorithm with optimal excess risk. Previously known constructions with optimal excess risk for 1 < p < 2 1<p<2 run in super-linear time in n n . For p = 1 p=1 , we give an algorithm with nearly optimal excess risk. Our result for the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -setup also extends to general polyhedral norms and feasible sets. Moreover, we show that the excess risk bounds resulting from our algorithms for 1 ≤ p ≤ 2 1\leq p\leq 2 are attained with high probability. For 2 < p ≤ ∞ 2<p\leq\infty , we show that existing linear-time constructions for the Euclidean setup attain a nearly optimal excess risk in the low-dimensional regime. As a consequence, we show that such constructions attain a nearly optimal excess risk for p = ∞ p=\infty . Our work draws upon concepts from the geometry of normed spaces, such as the notions of regularity, uniform convexity, and uniform smoothness.

Stochastic Convex Optimization (SCO) is one of the most fundamental problems in optimization, statistics, and machine learning. In this problem, the goal is to minimize the expectation of a convex loss with respect to a distribution given a dataset of i.i.d. samples from that distribution. 1.1
Overview of Results

We formally study DP-SCO beyond Euclidean setups. More importantly, we identify the appropriate structures that suffice to attain nearly optimal rates in these settings. A crucial ingredient of our algorithms and lower bounds are the concepts of uniform convexity and uniform smoothness in a normed space. More concretely, we use the notion of
\kappa
-regularity
of a normed space
[
23
]
, which quantifies how (close to) smooth is its squared norm (see Section
2
for a formal definition). This concept has been applied in (nonprivate) convex optimization to design strongly convex regularizers, and to bound the deviations of independent sums and martingales in normed spaces. In this work, we make use of these ideas and we further show that
\kappa
-regular spaces have a natural noise addition DP mechanism that we call the
generalized Gaussian mechanism
(see Section
3
). We remark that this mechanism may be of independent interest.

Now, we focus on
\ell_{p}
-setups and describe our results for the different values of
1\leq p\leq\infty
:

Case of
1<p\leq 2
:
This regime of
p
is interesting to investigate given the fact that the dependence of the optimal excess risk on the dimension of the problem is logarithmic in the
\ell_{1}
-setup
[
7
,
3
]
and polynomial in the Euclidean setup
[
6
]
. Our work provides nearly tight upper and lower bounds on the excess risk of DP-SCO in this regime. Our lower bound shows a surprising phenomenon: there is a
sharp phase transition
of the excess risk around
p=1
. In fact, when
1+\Omega(1)<p<2
,
our lower bound is essentially the same as those of the
\ell_{2}
case. This shows that the dependence on
\sqrt{d}
is necessary in this regime, thus solving an open question posed in
[
34
]
.
Our upper bound is attained via a new, linear-time algorithm. The best known previous construction for this problem for
1<p<2

[
2
]
runs in super-linear, namely,
O(\min\{n^{3/2},n^{2}/\sqrt{d}\})
time.

Our algorithm is based on a new variant of variance-reduced
Stochastic Frank-Wolfe
(SFW) algorithm with noisy gradient estimates. The noisy gradient estimates are generated using our novel generalized Gaussian mechanism. This algorithm enjoys many attractive features: it is projection free and makes implicit use of gradients through a linear optimization oracle. The structure of our algorithm is significantly different from the non-private variants
[
20
,
36
]
, and crucially uses the regularity of the dual space to control the bias of the noisy gradient estimates. In particular, our algorithm involves a binary-tree-based variance reduction technique proposed by Asi et al.
[
3
]
. The binary tree is constructed such that more samples (larger batch sizes) are assigned to vertices that are closer to the root and a gradient estimate at each vertex is then calculated using the samples of that vertex and the prior gradient estimates along
the path to the root. The tree-based structure and batch sizing, together with carefully tuned step sizes, effectively control the privacy budget while limiting the down stream influence of the error in the gradient estimates. Moreover, our algorithm makes a single pass on the
input sample, i.e., it runs in linear time. The resulting excess risk of our algorithm is nearly optimal, namely,
\tilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon n})
. Furthermore, we show that our upper bound is attained with high probability over the input sample and the internal randomness of the algorithm.

Case of
p=1
:
We provide an algorithm that attains nearly-optimal excess population risk with high probability.
Our algorithm here is based on the variance-reduced one-sample stochastic Frank-Wolfe algorithm
[
36
]
. This algorithm uses a single data point per iteration, allowing for larger number of iterations; and it achieves the optimal excess risk in non-private SCO in the Euclidean setting.
Despite its advantages,
this algorithm does not immediately apply to DP-SCO for the
\ell_{1}
-setup. The most important reason being that this algorithm was designed for the
\ell_{2}
-setup, so our first goal is to show that a recursive gradient estimator used in
[
36
]
(which is a variant of the Stochastic Path-Integrated Differential EstimatoR, SPIDER
[
20
]
) does indeed work in the
\ell_{1}
-setup.
Similarly to the previous case, by making use of the regularity of the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm to obtain
variance bound on the gradient estimator, we are able to extend the SFW method to the
\ell_{1}
-setup. A second challenge comes from the requirement of differential privacy. First, we use the fact that at each iteration, only a linear optimization oracle is required, and when the feasible set is polyhedral, we can construct such an oracle privately by
the report noisy max mechanism
[
18
,
10
]
. This technique was first used by
[
34
]
in their construction for the DP-ERM version of this problem which was based on “ordinary” full-batch FW. However, the recursive estimator in our construction is queried multiple times where a growing batch is used each time. This requires a different privacy analysis that entails bounding the sensitivity of the gradient estimator by analyzing a recursive bound on the same. The analysis crucially relies on the fact that our construction, unlike the non-private SFW, uses a constant step size and uses a large batch at the first iteration to reduce the sensitivity.

Case of
2<p\leq\infty
:
Another interesting question is what happens in the range of
p>2
. For comparison, it is known that non-privately the excess risk behaves as
\Theta(\frac{1}{n^{1/p}}+\frac{d^{1/2-1/p}}{\sqrt{n}})

[

[
21

attains nearly optimal excess risk for smooth convex losses. This implies that for

O(n^{5/4}d^{1/8})

[
21

To conclude our overview, we note that the SFW-based algorithms for the cases
p=1
and
1<p\leq 2

n
and are
projection-free
, which are desirable properties for large dataset and high-dimensional regimes. Moreover, all of our SFW-style algorithms enjoy high-probability excess risk guarantees. Instrumental to these guarantees is the use of concentration properties of martingale difference sequences in regular spaces
[
23
]

Preliminaries

Normed Spaces and Regularity.

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space of dimension
d,
and let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
an arbitrary inner product over
\mathbf{E}
(not necessarily inducing the norm
\|\cdot\|
). Given
x\in\mathbf{E}
and
r>0
, let
{\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|}(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbf{E}:\|y-x\|\leq r\}
.
The dual norm over
\mathbf{E}
is defined as usual,
\|y\|_{\ast}:=\max_{\|x\|\leq 1}\langle y,x\rangle
. With this definition,
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{\ast})
is also a
d
-dimensional normed space. As a main example, consider the case of
\ell_{p}^{d}\triangleq(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
, where
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
\|x\|_{p}\triangleq\big(\sum_{j\in[d]}|x_{j}|^{p}\big)^{1/p}
. As a consequence of the Hölder inequality, one can prove that the dual of
\ell_{p}^{d}
corresponds to
\ell_{q}^{d}
, where
1\leq q\leq\infty
is the conjugate exponent of
p
, determined by
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
.

The algorithms we consider in this work can be applied to general spaces whose dual has a sufficiently smooth norm. To quantify this property, we use the notion of
regular spaces
, following
[
23
]
. Given
\kappa\geq 1
, a normed space
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
is
\kappa
-regular if there exists
1\leq\kappa_{+}\leq\kappa
and a norm
\|\cdot\|_{+}
such that
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{+})
is
\kappa_{+}
-smooth, i.e.,

\|x+y\|_{+}^{2}\leq\|x\|_{+}^{2}+\langle\nabla(\|\cdot\|_{+}^{2})(x),y\rangle+\kappa_{+}\|y\|_{+}^{2}\qquad(\forall x,y\in\mathbf{E}),

(1)

and
\|\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{+}
are equivalent with constant
\sqrt{\kappa/\kappa_{+}}
:

\|x\|^{2}\leq\|x\|_{+}^{2}\leq(\kappa/\kappa_{+})\|x\|^{2}\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbf{E}).

(2)

As basic example, Euclidean spaces are
1
-regular. Other examples of regular spaces are

\ell_{q}^{d}
where
2\leq q\leq\infty
: these spaces are
\kappa
-regular with

\kappa=\min\{q-1,2e\log(d)\}
; in this case,
\|x\|_{+}=\|x\|_{r}
with
r=\min\{q,2\log(d)+1\}
and
\kappa_{+}=(r-1)

[
23
,
15
]
(smoothness of this norm squared is proved e.g. in
[
9
, Example 5.11]
.) Finally, consider a polyhedral norm
\|\cdot\|
with unit ball
{\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|}=\mbox{conv}({\cal V})
.
Then
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{\ast})
is
(2e\ln|{\cal V}|)
-regular. More precisely, note that
\|x\|_{\ast}=\max_{v\in{\cal V}}|\langle v,x\rangle|
, hence
the norm
\|x\|_{+}:=\big(\sum_{v\in{\cal V}}|\langle v,x\rangle|^{q}\big)^{1/q}
, with
q=2\ln|{\cal V}|+1
, satisfies (
1
) with
\kappa_{+}=(q-1)
(e.g., follows from
[
9
, Example 5.11]
), and satisfies (
2
) with
\sqrt{\kappa/\kappa_{+}}\leq\sqrt{e}
(using the equivalence of
\|\cdot\|_{q}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
), thus
\kappa=e\kappa_{+}=2e\ln|{\cal V}|
.

An interesting property of regular spaces are the variance and concentration bounds of their vector-valued martingales
[
23
]
. These will allow us to naturally extend some estimators proposed in Euclidean settings to non-Euclidean regular settings.

Definition 2.2

(Differential Privacy
[
16
,
17
,

.

Let

. A (randomized) algorithm

Differentially Private Stochastic Convex Optimization.

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space, and
{\cal X}\subseteq\mathbf{E}
a closed convex set of diameter
M>0
. Given
L_{0},L_{1}>0
, denote
{\cal C}(L_{0},L_{1})
the class of functions
f:{\cal X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
which are convex;
L_{0}
-Lipschitz,
f(x)-f(y)\leq L_{0}\|x-y\|
, for all
x,y\in{\cal X}
; and
L_{1}
-smooth,
\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|_{\ast}\leq L_{1}\|x-y\|
, for all
x,y\in{\cal X}
.
Given a
loss function

f:{\cal X}\times\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
s.t.
f(\cdot,z)\in{\cal C}(L_{0},L_{1})
for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, and a distribution
{\cal D}
over
\mathcal{Z}
, the SCO problem corresponds to the minimization of the
population risk
,
F_{\cal D}(x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim{\cal D}}[f(x,z)]
over
{\cal X}
. Let,
F_{\cal D}^{\ast}\triangleq\min_{x\in{\cal X}}F_{\cal D}(x)
.
Given an algorithm
{\cal A}:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\mapsto\mathbf{E}
, define its
excess population risk
as

{\cal E}[{\cal A}]=F_{\mathcal{D}}({\cal A}(S))-F_{\mathcal{D}}^{\ast}.

(3)

Our guarantees on the excess population risk will be expressed in terms of
upper bounds on (
3
) that hold with high probability over the randomness of both
S
and the random
coins of the algorithm. Additionally, the expected excess population risk of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as
{\cal R}_{\cal D}[{\cal A}]=\underset{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\cal E}[{\cal A}]\right].
The DP-SCO problem in the
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
-setup corresponds to the setting above, where algorithms are constrained to satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy.
We distinguish the problem above from its empirical counterpart (DP-ERM), where we are interested in the minimization of the
empirical
average of the loss w.r.t. the input dataset.

3
Generalized Gaussian Distribution and Mechanism

One important requirement for the application of DP stochastic first-order methods is designing the proper private mechanism for an iterative method. If we want to achieve privacy by adding noise to gradients, then we need to do it in a way to achieve privacy from
gradient sensitivity
, w.r.t. the dual norm. With this purpose in mind, we design a new noise addition mechanism that leverages the regularity of the
dual space

(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{\ast})
.

Definition 3.1

(Generalized Gaussian distribution and mechanism)
.

Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{\ast})
be a
d
-dimensional
\kappa
-regular space with smooth norm
\|\cdot\|_{+}
. We define the generalized Gaussian (GG) distribution
{\cal GG}_{\|\cdot\|_{+}}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, as the one with density
g(z)=C(\sigma,d)\exp\{-\|z-\mu\|_{+}^{2}/[2\sigma^{2}]\}
, where
C(\sigma,d)=\big[\mbox{Area}(\{\|x\|_{+}=1\})\frac{(2\sigma^{2})^{d/2}}{2}\Gamma(d/2)\big]^{-1}
, and
Area
is the
\left((d-1)\text{-dim}\right)
surface measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Given an algorithm
{\cal A}:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\mapsto\mathbf{E}
with bounded
\|\cdot\|_{\ast}
-sensitivity:

\sup_{S\simeq S^{\prime}}\|{\cal A}(S)-{\cal A}(S^{\prime})\|_{\ast}\leq s
, we define the generalized Gaussian mechanism of
{\cal A}
with variance
\sigma^{2}
as

{\cal A}_{\cal GG}(S)\sim{\cal GG}_{\|\cdot\|_{+}}({\cal A}(S),\sigma^{2}).

We first list some basic properties.

Proposition 3.2
.

(a)

For any
m\geq 1
, if
z\sim{\cal GG}_{\|\cdot\|_{+}}(0,\sigma^{2})
, then
\mathbb{E}[\|z\|_{+}^{m}]\leq(2\sigma^{2})^{m/2}\Gamma\big(\frac{m+d}{2}\big)/\Gamma\big(\frac{d}{2}\big).
In particular,
\mathbb{E}[\|z\|_{\ast}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|z\|_{+}^{2}]\leq d\sigma^{2}
.

(b)

For any
\nu\geq 0
, if
z\sim{\cal GG}_{\|\cdot\|_{+}}(0,\sigma^{2})
, then
\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\|z\|_{\ast}^{2}/\nu^{2}\}]\leq\exp\big\{\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{\nu^{2}-2\sigma^{2}}\big\}.
In particular, if
\nu=\sigma\sqrt{d+2}
, then
\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\frac{\|z\|_{\ast}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\frac{\|z\|_{+}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\}]\leq\exp(1).

(c)

For any
\alpha>1
,
D_{\alpha}({\cal GG}_{\|\cdot\|_{+}}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2})||{\cal GG}_{\|\cdot\|_{+}}(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2}))\leq\frac{\kappa\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}(\alpha-1)}\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{\ast}^{2}
, where
D_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-Rényi divergence.

The last property exhibits a behavior analogous to the Gaussian r.v.s in the
\ell_{2}
-setup.

Proof.

(a)

Notice that for any
m
(below
\Gamma(\cdot)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\exp\big\{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\big\}r^{m}dr=\frac{(2\sigma^{2})^{m+1/2}}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-u}u^{m-1/2}du=\frac{(2\sigma^{2})^{(m+1)/2}}{2}\Gamma\big(\frac{m+1}{2}\big).

This implies that the
m
-th moment w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{+}
can be computed as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|z\|_{+}^{m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\sigma,d)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|z\|_{+}^{m}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\|z\|_{+}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big\}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\sigma,d)\mbox{Area}(\{\|x\|_{+}=1\})\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{m+d-1}\exp\big\{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\big\}dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\sigma^{2})^{m/2}\Gamma\big(\frac{m+d}{2}\big)/\Gamma\big(\frac{d}{2}\big).

We conclude using that
\|z\|_{\ast}\leq\|z\|_{+}
. On the other hand, the bounds for the second moment are obtained by using that
\Gamma(1+d/2)=(d/2)\Gamma(d/2)
.

(b)

Using
\|z\|_{\ast}\leq\|z\|_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big\{\frac{\|z\|_{\ast}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\big\}\big]

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big\{\frac{\|z\|_{+}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\big\}\big]=C(\sigma,d){\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\exp\big\{\frac{\|z\|_{+}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\big\}\exp\big\{-\frac{\|z\|_{+}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\big\}dz

\displaystyle\textstyle=C(\sigma,d){\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\exp\big\{\|z\|_{+}^{2}\big(\frac{1}{\nu^{2}}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\big)\big\}dz=C(\sigma,d)/C\big(\big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\big)^{-1/2},d\big)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\nu^{2}}{\nu^{2}-2\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{d/2}=\Big(1+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\nu^{2}-2\sigma^{2}}\Big)^{d/2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big\{\frac{d}{2}\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\nu^{2}-2\sigma^{2}}\big\}.

Note that, this is upper bounded by
\exp(1)
when
\nu=\sigma\sqrt{d+2}
.

(c)

Let
\mathbb{P}={\cal GG}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2})
and
\mathbb{Q}={\cal GG}(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2})
.

\displaystyle\exp\{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}||\mathbb{Q})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\sigma,d)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\frac{d\mathbb{P}}{d\mathbb{Q}}\Big)^{\alpha}d\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\sigma,d)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z-\mu_{1}\|_{+}^{2}+\frac{\alpha-1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z-\mu_{2}\|_{+}^{2}\Big\}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(\sigma,d)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z-\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}\|_{+}^{2}+\frac{\alpha-1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|z\|_{+}^{2}\Big\}dz.

Let now
\mu=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}
and
p(\cdot)=\|\cdot\|_{+}^{2}
. Now, by convexity and smoothness of
\|\cdot\|_{+}^{2}

-\alpha\|z-\mu\|_{+}^{2}\leq-\alpha\|z\|_{+}^{2}+\langle\nabla p(z),\alpha\mu\rangle\leq-\alpha\|z\|_{+}^{2}+[\|z\|_{+}^{2}-\|z-\alpha\mu\|_{+}^{2}+\kappa_{+}\|\alpha\mu\|_{+}^{2}].

Plugging this in the integral above, we get

\displaystyle e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}||\mathbb{Q})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textstyle\exp\big\{\frac{\kappa_{+}\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mu\|_{+}^{2}\big\}C(\sigma,d){\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\|z-\alpha\mu\|_{+}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big\}dz\leq\exp\big\{\frac{\kappa\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\mu\|_{\ast}^{2}\big\},

Hence,
D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}||\mathbb{Q})\leq\frac{\kappa\alpha^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}(\alpha-1)}\|\mu\|_{\ast}^{2}.

∎

The generalized Gaussian mechanism applied to a function with

-sensitivity bounded by

is
(\alpha,\rho)
-RDP, where

. In particular, choosing

, the GG mechanism is

-DP.

Proof.

The first part follows directly from Proposition

. The second part can be obtained from the first part, together with the DP/RDP reduction in

, Propos. 3]
.

High Probability Bias of Recursive Gradient Estimators

Our DP-SCO algorithms in the
\ell_{p}
setups when
1\leq p\leq 2
, are based on the variance-reduced stochastic Frank-Wolfe algorithm variants. These algorithms use a variant of the following recursive gradient estimator:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}=(1-\alpha)(\widetilde{\nabla}_{t-1}+\Delta_{t})+\alpha\nabla_{t}+\mathbf{g}_{t}

(4)

For any
t
, let
\mathbf{B}_{t}
denote a mini-batch of data points drawn from the input dataset without replacement. Note that, since the input dataset consists of i.i.d. draws from an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the mini-batches
\{\mathbf{B}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{t}\}
are disjoint and independent. Here, given a mini-batch
\mathbf{B}_{t}
,
\nabla_{t}
is the unbiased gradient estimator given by
\nabla_{t}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{B}_{t}|}\sum_{z\in\mathbf{B}_{t}}\nabla f(x^{t},z)
, and
\Delta_{t}
is the gradient variation given by
\Delta_{t}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{B}_{t}|}\sum_{z\in\mathbf{B}_{t}}[\nabla f(x^{t},z)-\nabla f(x^{t-1},z)]
. Here
\alpha\in(0,1)
is an averaging factor. Finally,
\mathbf{g}_{t}
is the noise, which is a centered random variable,
\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{g}_{t}\right]=0
, and satisfies a light-tail property

\displaystyle\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\{\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|^{2}/\nu_{t}^{2}\}\right]\leq\exp(1)\quad(\exists\nu_{t}\geq 0).

(5)

Recall that
\mathcal{X}
is a closed convex set of diameter
M>0
, and for any datapoint
z
in the input dataset, we have the following properties:

•

Unbiasedness:
\underset{z\in{\cal D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla f(x,z)\right]=\nabla F_{\cal D}(x)

•

Boundedness:
\|\nabla f(x,z)\|_{\ast}\leq L_{0}
, a.s..

•

Smoothness:
\|\nabla f(x,z)-\nabla f(y,z)\|_{\ast}\leq L_{1}\|x-y\|
.

We will use Proposition
2.1
and the above properties to derive high probability bounds on the variance of the recursive gradient estimator
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}
, which is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and step sizes,
\eta_{t}\in(0,1),~\forall t
. Let
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space such that
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{\ast})
is
\kappa
-regular. Suppose the noise
\mathbf{g}_{t}
, is a centered random variable,
\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{g}_{t}\right]=0
, and satisfies (
5
) for some
\nu_{t}\geq 0
. For any
0<\beta<1
, with probability at least
1-\beta
, the recursive gradient estimate
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}
satisfies for all
t\in[T]
:

\textstyle\|\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{t})\|_{\ast}\leq C_{\beta}\big[\frac{(1-\alpha)^{t}L_{0}}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{B}_{0}|}}+(L_{1}M\eta_{t}+\alpha L_{0})\sum\limits_{s<t}\frac{(1-\alpha)^{t-(s+1)}}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{B}_{s}|}}+\sum\limits_{s<t}(1-\alpha)^{t-(s+1)}\nu_{s}\big],

where
C_{\beta}=\big(\sqrt{e\kappa}+\sqrt{3\log(2T/\beta)}\big)
.

Proof.

Note that, the recursive gradient estimator is given by
4
. Now, to prove a high probability bound on
\|\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{t})\|_{\ast}
, we unravel this bias in terms of a martingale difference sequence. Below, for a compact notation, let
\widebar{\Delta}_{t}\triangleq\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t})-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t-1}).

\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\big[\widetilde{\nabla}_{t-1}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t-1})\big]+(1-\alpha)\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t-1})-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t})+(1-\alpha)\Delta_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{t}+\alpha\mathbf{g}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle(1-\alpha)\big[\widetilde{\nabla}_{t-1}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t-1})\big]+\left[\Delta_{t}-\widebar{\Delta}_{t}\right]+\alpha\left[\nabla_{t}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t})+\mathbf{g}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)^{t}(\widetilde{\nabla}_{0}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{0}))+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-(j+1)}[(\Delta_{j}-\widebar{\Delta}_{j})+\alpha(\nabla_{j-1}-\nabla F(x^{j-1})+\mathbf{g}_{j})].

Given
t\in[T]
and
z\in\mathbf{B}_{t}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{<z,t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra induced by all datapoints preceding
z
(in the order used by the algorithm), as well as
(\mathbf{g}_{s})_{s<t}
.
Here, we assume that
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\mathcal{D}}(x^{t})
is a sum of martingale-difference terms and hence, we need to establish the following bounds for each summand in the estimators.

1.

First term:
\widetilde{\nabla}_{0}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{0})
.

For the initial batch
\mathbf{B}_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{0}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{0})=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{B}_{0}|}

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbf{B}_{0}}[\nabla f(x^{0},z)-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{0})]

Moreover,
\|\nabla f(x^{0},z)-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{0})\|_{\ast}\leq{2L_{0}}
, for each
z\in\mathbf{B}_{0}
, a.s.

In particular, each summand of the first term satisfies, for
\psi_{0}^{2}=4L_{0}^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2t}/\mathbf{B}_{0}^{2}
,

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\Big\{(1-\alpha)^{2t}\|\nabla f(x^{0},z)-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{0})\|^{2}_{\ast}/[\psi_{0}\mathbf{B}_{0}]^{2}\Big\}\Big|{\cal F}_{<z,0}\right]\leq\exp(1).

2.

Second term:
[\Delta_{j}-\widebar{\Delta}_{j}]+\alpha[\nabla_{j-1}-\nabla F(x^{j-1})]
.

Given a mini-batch
\mathbf{B}_{j}
, we can write this term as follows

\displaystyle\textstyle[\Delta_{j}-\widebar{\Delta}_{j}]+\alpha[\nabla_{j-1}-\nabla F(x^{j-1})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{B}_{j}|}\sum_{z\in\mathbf{B}_{j}}\Big[(\nabla f(x^{j},z)-\nabla f(x^{j-1},z)-\widebar{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle\textstyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\alpha(\nabla f(x^{j-1},z)-\nabla F(x^{j-1}))\Big].

Now, using that
f(\cdot,z)
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
L_{1}
-smooth, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x^{j},z)-\nabla f(x^{j-1},z)-\widebar{\Delta}_{j}\|_{\ast}\leq 2L_{1}M\eta_{j}

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x^{j-1},z)-\nabla F(x^{j-1})\|_{\ast}\leq 2L_{0}.

Thus, the dual norm of each summand is a.s. bounded by
2L_{1}M\eta_{j}+2\alpha L_{0}
. Finally, choosing

\psi_{j}^{2}=\frac{4}{|\mathbf{B}_{j}|^{2}}(1-\alpha)^{2t-2(j+1)}[L_{1}M\eta_{j}+\alpha L_{0}]^{2},

for each summand of the second term, using datapoint
z\in\mathbf{B}_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big\{\frac{1}{(\psi_{t}|\mathbf{B}_{j}|)^{2}}

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)^{2t-2(j+1)}\Big\|(\nabla f(x^{j},z)-\nabla f(x^{j-1},z)-\widebar{\Delta}_{j})

\displaystyle+\alpha[\nabla f(x^{j-1},z)-\nabla F(x^{j-1})\Big\|_{\ast}^{2}\Big\}\Big|{\cal F}_{<z,j}\Big]\leq\exp(1),

3.

Third term:
\mathbf{g}_{j}

It is given that
\mathbf{g}_{j}
is a centered random variable,
\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{g}_{t}\right]=0
, and it satisfies

\underset{}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\{\|\mathbf{g}_{t}\|^{2}/\nu_{t}^{2}\}\right]\leq\exp(1).

Therefore, by Proposition
2.1
, we have that for every
t
and any
\beta\in(0,1)
:

\mathbb{P}\Big[\|\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{t})\|_{\ast}\geq\sqrt{2}C_{\beta}\Big(|\mathbf{B}_{0}|\psi_{0}^{2}+\sum_{s<t}(1-\alpha)^{2t-2(s+1)}\big[|\mathbf{B}_{s}|\psi_{s}^{2}+\nu_{s}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}\Big]\leq\beta,

where
C^{\prime}_{\beta}=(\sqrt{e\kappa}+\sqrt{3\log(2/\beta)}).
Using Jensen’s inequality and union bound over
t
, with probability
1-\beta
, the recursive gradient estimate
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}
satisfies for all
t\in[T]
(letting
C_{\beta}=(\sqrt{e\kappa}+\sqrt{3\log(2T/\beta)})
):

\textstyle\|\widetilde{\nabla}_{t}-\nabla F_{\cal D}(x^{t})\|_{\ast}\leq C_{\beta}\big[\frac{(1-\alpha)^{t}L_{0}}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{B}_{0}|}}+(L_{1}M\eta_{t}+\alpha L_{0})\sum\limits_{s<t}\frac{(1-\alpha)^{t-(s+1)}}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{B}_{s}|}}+\sum\limits_{s<t}(1-\alpha)^{t-(s+1)}\nu_{s}\big].

∎

5
Differentially Private SCO:
\ell_{p}
-setup for
1<p\leq 2

5.1
Noisy Variance-Reduced Stochastic Frank-Wolfe

In this section, we study DP-SCO in the
\ell_{p}
-setup when
1<p\leq 2
and provide a high probability upper bound on the excess risk. We give a differentially private stochastic Frank-Wolfe algorithm that is based on the variance reduction proposed in
[
36
]
. In our algorithm we combine a binary-tree-based variance reduction technique proposed by Asi et al. for the
\ell_{1}
setup
[
2
]
with privacy-preserving gradient noise addition by our generalized Gaussian mechanism. Our algorithm has
T
phases, where in each phase
t\in{1,\ldots,T}
we construct a binary tree of depth
t
. Next, the algorithm traverses through the vertices of the tree according to the Depth-First-Search (DFS) approach. Following the notation in
[
2
]
, vertices are denoted by
u_{t,s}
where
s\in\{0,1\}^{\leq t}
is the path to the vertex. Each vertex
u_{t,s}
is associated with a parameter
x_{t,s}
, a gradient estimate
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,s}
, and a set of samples
S_{t,s}
. At a high level, each phase
t
starts by computing a fresh estimate for the gradient of the population risk at root vertex (i.e.
s=\varnothing
) based on a large batch of samples. Next the vertices are traversed according to the Depth-First-Search approach and the gradient at each vertex is estimated using the samples of that vertex and the gradients along the path to the root. When a left child is visited, the parent node passes the parameter
x_{t,s}
and gradient estimate
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,s}
with no update. At every right child, the algorithm improves the gradient estimate using the estimate at the parent node. When a leaf vertex is visited in the DFS order, the algorithm updates the current iterate using the Frank-Wolfe step with the gradient estimate at the leaf. The vertices that are closer to the root are assigned more samples, i.e. each vertex is associated with a sample of size
\frac{b}{2^{j}}
, where
j
is the depth of the vertex. Hence, the variance of the gradient estimate is reduced along the path to the root.

Following are some of the notations we use in this section for convenience. Let
\mathsf{DFS}(t)
denote the DFS order of the vertices in a binary tree of depth
t
(root not included). For
s\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, let
\ell(s)\in[0,2^{t}-1]
denote the integer whose binary representation is
s
. In the description of the algorithm, let
x_{t,s}
denote the iterates, where
t
is the phase and
s\in\{0,1\}^{t}
is the path from the root.

Our algorithm, denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{noisySFW}}
, is provided in pseudocode in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{noisySFW}}
: Noisy Private Stochastic Frank-Wolfe Algorithm

0:

Private dataset:
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
, privacy parameters:
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, convex set:
\mathcal{X}

T

b

Set

for

of
b

Set

(Privacy Guarantee of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{noisySFW}}

.

Let
\eta_{t,s}=\frac{1}{2^{t-1}+\ell(s)+1}~\forall s,t
. Then, Algorithm
1

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private.

Proof.

Let
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
be neighboring datasets. Let
\widetilde{\nabla}_{t,s}
and
\widetilde{\nabla}^{\prime}_{t,s}
denote the private gradient estimates corresponding to
S
and
S^{\prime}

t
, let
\nabla_{t,\varnothing}=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{z\in S_{t,\varnothing}}\nabla f(x_{t,\varnothing},z)
be the gradient estimator for the root of the tree which has a sample size of
b
. Note that the global
\|\cdot\|_{*}
-sensitivity of
\nabla_{t,\varnothing}
is bounded by
\frac{2L_{0}}{b}
. Hence, by Corollary
3.3

we obtain that Step

in Algorithm

is

-differentially private..

Next, for the iterate
x_{t,s}
, let
S_{t,s}
denote the mini-batch given in Step

1
.
Let
\Delta_{t,s}=\frac{2^{|s|}}{b}\sum_{z\in S_{t,s}}\left(\nabla f(x_{t,s},z)-\nabla f(x_{t,\hat{s}},z)\right)
.
Also, let

Here for the current iterate

denotes the iterate of its parent vertex. Recall that whenever the algorithm visits a leaf node, it applies a Frank-Wolfe step to calculate the next iterate and puts its value in the next vertex in the DFS order. Hence, the total number of (consecutive) iterates between

.

, Algorithm 2]
runs in linear time and attains expected excess population risk

{\cal R}_{\cal D}[{\cal A}_{\mathsf{PhasedSGD}}]=O\big(L_{0}M\big(\frac{d^{1/2-1/p}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{d^{1-1/p}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\big)\big).

Proof.

We start by bounding the
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-diameter and Lipschitz constant for the
\ell_{p}
-setup. First, since the
\|\cdot\|_{p}
-diameter of
{\cal X}
is bounded by
M
, then the
\|\cdot\|_{2}
-diameter of
{\cal X}
is bounded by
d^{1/2-1/p}M
. Next, if
f
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{p}
, i.e.
\bigcup_{x\in{\cal X}}\partial f(x)\subseteq{\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|_{p^{\ast}}}(0,L_{0})
, then
f
is also
L_{0}
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Therefore,
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
implies

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\cal D}[{\cal A}_{\mathsf{noisySGD}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle d^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}L_{0}M\cdot O\Big(\max\Big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle L_{0}M\cdot O\Big(\frac{d^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{d^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big).

∎

We conclude this section observing that in the low-dimensional regime, i.e.,
d\lesssim n
, the above upper bound is optimal since it matches the optimal non-private lower bound of
\Omega\left(\frac{d^{1/2-1/p}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

[
1
]
. Note that in the
\ell_{\infty}
setting, the low-dimensional regime is the only interesting regime since the excess risk is
\Omega(1)
if
d>n
. Hence, our result implies that phased SGD
[
21
]
attains nearly optimal excess risk for DP-SCO in the
\ell_{\infty}
setting. We formally state this observation below.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
2<p\leq\infty
and
{\cal X}={\cal B}_{\|\cdot\|_{p}}(0,M)
. Let
d\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2}\leq n
. The phased SGD algorithm
{\cal A}_{\mathsf{PhasedSGD}}

[
21
, Algorithm 2]
runs in linear time and attains the optimal excess population risk for DP-SCO in the
\ell_{p}
-setup. For
p=\infty
, this algorithm attains nearly optimal excess population risk.

Remark 2
.

Our results here are based on the excess risk guarantees of
[
21
]
, which are expectation guarantees rather than high-probability guarantees. However, we note that it is possible to provide high-probability guarantees for the phased SGD algorithm
{\cal A}_{\mathsf{PhasedSGD}}

[
21
, Algorithm 2]
by slightly modifying the algorithm (where the mini-batch size in each phase is larger by a logarithmic factor) together with a more careful analysis that uses a standard martingale argument to show high-probability convergence guarantee for the projected SGD invoked in each phase of
{\cal A}_{\mathsf{PhasedSGD}}
.

Note that, for general losses, Kulkarni et. al.
[
25
]
give an algorithm for DP-SCO in the Euclidean setup that attains the optimal excess population risk. Their algorithm runs in
O(\min\{n^{5/4}d^{1/8},\frac{n^{3/2}}{d^{1/8}}\})
time. This algorithm combines a DP-ERM algorithm
[
25
, Algorithm 4]
with an iterative localization approach from
[
21
]
. Following the same argument as in the smooth case, we have the following result for general convex losses.

Proposition 7.3
.

For general convex losses, consider the problem of DP-SCO in the
\ell_{p}=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{p})
-setup, with
2<p\leq\infty
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{RndSmth}}
denote the algorithm proposed in
[
25
]
. Then,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{RndSmth}}
attains expected excess population risk

{\cal R}_{\cal D}[\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{RndSmth}}]=O\big(L_{0}M\big(\frac{d^{1/2-1/p}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{d^{1-1/p}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\big)\big)

and runs in
O(\min\{n^{5/4}d^{1/8},n^{3/2}/d^{1/8}\})
time.

Moreover, as in Corollary
7.2
, when
d\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2}\leq n
, this algorithm attains the optimal excess risk for any
\ell_{p}
setup, where
p>2
. Hence, when
p=\infty
, this algorithm attains nearly optimal excess risk.

Appendix A
Lower Bound for the
\ell_{p}
Setup for
1<p<2

In this section, we give lower bounds on the excess risk of DP-SCO and DP-ERM in the
\ell_{p}
setting for
1<p<2
. Our lower bound for DP-SCO has the form

\tilde{\Omega}\big(\max\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},(p-1)\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon n}\big)\big)
.
The first term follows directly from the non-private lower bound for SCO in the same setting
[
28
]
. To establish a lower bound
with the second term,
we show a lower bound of essentially the same order (up to a logarithmic factor in
1/\delta
) on the excess empirical error for DP-ERM in the
\ell_{p}
setup (Theorem
A.2
). By the reduction in
[
6
, Appendix C]
, we conclude the claimed lower bound on DP-SCO.

Problem setup:
Let
p\in(1,2)
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{B}_{p}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{p}^{d}
is the unit
\ell_{p}
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{Z}=\{-\frac{1}{d^{1/q}},\frac{1}{d^{1/q}}\}^{d}
where
q=\frac{p}{p-1}
. Let
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow[-1,1]
defined as:

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x,z)=-\langle x,z\rangle,~x\in\mathcal{X},z\in\mathcal{Z}.

Note that for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
f(\cdot,z)
is convex, smooth, and
1
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{p}
over
\mathcal{X}
. Recall that for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{Z}
, we define the population risk of
x\in\mathcal{X}
w.r.t.
\mathcal{D}
as
F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)\triangleq\underset{z\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x,z)\right]
, and for any dataset
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n},
we define the empirical risk of
x\in\mathcal{X}
w.r.t.
S
as
F_{S}(x)\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x,z_{i})
.

Our lower bound for DP-SCO is formally stated in the following theorem.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
p\in(1,2)
and
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\delta<\frac{1}{n^{1+{\Omega(1)}}}
. Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z},
and
f
be as defined in the setup above. There exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{Z}
such that for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP-SCO algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, we have

\textstyle\underset{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{n},\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}(S))\right]-\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\mathcal{D}}(x)=\tilde{\Omega}\Big(\max\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},(p-1)\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon n}\Big)\Big).

As mentioned earlier, given the reduction from
[
6
]
, it suffices to prove a lower bound of essentially the same order for DP-ERM w.r.t. the problem described above. The rest of this section will be devoted to this goal; namely, to prove the following.

Theorem A.2
.

Under the same setup in Theorem
A.1
, there exists a dataset
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
such that for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP-ERM algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{Z}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, we have

\textstyle\underset{\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{S}(\mathcal{A}(S))\right]-\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F_{S}(x)=\Omega\Big((p-1)\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big).

Proof.

Let the spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z},
and the loss function
f
be as defined in the problem setup above. For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let
x_{j}
denote the
j
-th coordinate of
x
, where
j\in[d]
. Let
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
, and let
z_{ij}
denote the
j
-th coordinate of
z_{i}
, where
i\in[n],j\in[d]
. Define
\bar{z}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}
, and similarly, let
\bar{z}_{j}
denote the
j
-th coordinate of
\bar{z}
.

Let
x^{\ast}\triangleq\arg\min\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}F_{S}(x)=\arg\max\limits_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle x,\bar{z}\rangle
. Note that we have

\displaystyle x^{\ast}_{j}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\big(|\bar{z}_{j}|^{q-1}/\|\bar{z}\|_{q}^{q-1}\big)\mathsf{sign}(\bar{z}_{j}),\quad j\in[d],

(8)

where
\|\cdot\|_{q}
denote the
\ell_{q}
norm (recall that
q\triangleq\frac{p}{p-1}
). To see this, note that by Hölder’s inequality
\forall x\in\mathcal{X},

F_{S}(x)\geq-\|\bar{z}\|_{q}
, and on the other hand, note that
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
since
\|x^{\ast}\|_{p}=1
and
F_{S}(x^{\ast})=-\|\bar{z}\|_{q}
.
Next, we make the following claim.

Claim A.3
.

Let
\alpha>0
. Let
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{X}
be such that
F_{S}(\widehat{x})-F_{S}(x^{\ast})\leq\alpha
. Then,
\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|_{p}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\alpha}{(p-1)\|\bar{z}\|_{q}}}.

The proof of this claim relies on the uniform convexity property of the
\ell_{p}
norms for
p\in(1,2]
(see
[
4
]
). We formally restate this property below:

Fact A.4

(see Eq. (1.6) in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
x,y
be any elements of an
\ell_{p}
-normed space
(\mathbf{E},\|\cdot\|_{p})
,
where
1<p\leq 2
. We have
\|\frac{x+y}{2}\|_{p}\leq 1-\frac{p-1}{8}\|x-y\|_{p}^{2}
.

Now, observe that for any
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
F_{S}(\widehat{x})-F_{S}(x^{\ast})\leq\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle 1-\frac{\alpha}{2\|\bar{z}\|_{q}}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\langle\frac{\widehat{x}+x^{\ast}}{2},\frac{\bar{z}}{\|\bar{z}\|_{q}}\rangle\leq\|\frac{\widehat{x}+x^{\ast}}{2}\|_{p}\leq 1-\frac{p-1}{8}\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|_{p}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from the above fact. Rearranging terms leads to the above claim.

Fix values for
\varepsilon
and
\delta
as in the theorem statement. Next, we will show the existence of a dataset
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
with
\|\bar{z}\|_{q}=\Omega\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big)
such that for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP-ERM algorithm for the above problem that outputs a vector
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we must have
\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|_{p}=\Omega(1)
with probability
2/3
over the algorithm’s random coins. Note that, by Claim
A.3
, this implies the desired lower bound. To see this, suppose, for the sake of a contradiction, that there exists an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP-ERM algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that outputs
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\underset{\widehat{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F_{S}(\widehat{x})\right]-F_{S}(x^{\ast})=o\Big((p-1)\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big)
. Then, by Markov’s inequality, with probability
\geq 0.9,
we have
F_{S}(\widehat{x})-F_{S}(x^{\ast})=o\Big((p-1)\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big)
. Hence, Claim
A.3
would imply that, with probability
\geq 0.9
,
\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|_{p}=o(1)
, which contradicts with the claimed
\Omega(1)
lower bound on
\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|
. Hence, to conclude the proof of Theorem
A.2
, it remains to show the claimed lower bound on
\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|
, which we do next.

In the final step of the proof, we resort to a construction based on the fingerprinting code argument due to
[
12
]
. We use the following lemma, which is implicit in the constructions of
[
11
,
32
]
.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
n,d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\delta<\frac{1}{n^{1+{\Omega(1)}}}
. Let
\mathcal{T}=\{-1,1\}^{d}
. There exists a dataset
T=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathcal{T}^{n}
where
\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq c\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}
for some universal constant
c>0
such that for any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private algorithm
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{T}^{n}\rightarrow[-1,1]^{d},
the following is true with probability
2/3
over the random coins of
\mathcal{M}
:
\exists J\subseteq[d]
with
|J|=\Omega(d)
such that

(\forall j\in J),\quad\Big\lvert\mathcal{M}_{j}(T)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{ij}\Big\rvert=\Omega\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\Big\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{ij}\Big\rvert=c\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n},

where
\mathcal{M}_{j}(T)
and
v_{ij}
denote the
j
-th coordinates of
\mathcal{M}(T)
and
v_{i}
, respectively.

We consider a normalized version of the dataset
T=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
in the above lemma. Namely, we consider a dataset
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
, where
z_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{d^{1/q}},~i\in[n].
Note that the above lemma implies the existence of a subset
J\subseteq[d]
with
|J|=\Omega(d)
such that for all
j\in J,

|\bar{z}_{j}|=\frac{c}{d^{1/q}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}
for some universal constant
c>0
. Note also that
\forall~j\in[d]\setminus J,

|\bar{z}_{j}|\leq\frac{c}{d^{1/q}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}
since
\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq c\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{|J|}{d}\,c^{q}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\right)^{q}\leq\|\bar{z}\|_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq c^{q}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\right)^{q},

which, given the fact that
|J|=\Omega(d)
, implies that
\|\bar{z}\|^{q}_{q}=c^{\prime}\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}
for some universal constant
c^{\prime}>0
. Hence, by the fact that
q>2
, we have
\|\bar{z}\|_{q}=\Theta\big(\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon n}\big)
. Moreover, note that for all
j\in[J]
, we have
\frac{|\bar{z}_{j}|^{q-1}}{\|\bar{z}\|_{q}^{q-1}}=\frac{\left(\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{q}}}{d^{1-\frac{1}{q}}}=\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{d^{1/p}}
for some universal constant
c^{\prime\prime}
, where the last equality follows from the fact that
q>2
and
\frac{1}{q}=1-\frac{1}{p}
.

Let
\bar{v}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}
, and let
\bar{v}_{j}
denote the
j
-th coordinate of
\bar{v}
for
j\in[d]
. Given the above observations and the expression of the minimizer
x^{\ast}
in eq.
8
, it is not hard to see that for all
j\in J,

\displaystyle x^{\ast}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{d^{1/p}}\mathsf{sign}(\bar{v}_{j})=\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{d^{1/p}}\cdot\frac{\bar{v}_{j}}{|\bar{v}_{j}|}=\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon n}{d^{1/2+1/p}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\bar{v}_{j}.

(9)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP-ERM algorithm that takes the dataset
S
described above as input, and let
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{X}
denote its output. Construct an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private algorithm
\mathcal{M}
for the dataset
T
of Lemma
A.5
by first running
\mathcal{A}
on
S=\frac{1}{d^{1/q}}\cdot T
, which outputs
\widehat{x}
, then releasing
\mathcal{M}(T)=\frac{c}{c^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\frac{d^{1/2+1/p}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon\,n}\cdot\widehat{x}
. Now, using
9
and given the description of
\mathcal{M}
, observe that

\displaystyle\textstyle\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\textstyle\geq\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{c}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon n}{d^{1/2+1/p}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\cdot\Big(\sum_{j\in J}|\mathcal{M}_{j}(T)-\bar{v}_{j}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\textstyle=\Omega\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{d^{1/2+1/p}\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\,d^{1/p}\,\frac{\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon\,n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega(1)

where the third step follows from Lemma
A.5
and the fact that
\mathcal{M}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. This establishes the desired lower bound on
\|\widehat{x}-x^{\ast}\|_{p}
, and hence by the argument described earlier, the proof of Theorem
A.2
is now complete.
∎
