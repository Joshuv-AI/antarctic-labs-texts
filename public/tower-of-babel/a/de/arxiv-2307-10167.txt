𝚅𝙸𝚃𝚂 \mathtt{VITS} : Variational Inference Thompson Sampling for contextual bandits
Pierre Clavier, Tom Huix, Alain Durmus

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce and analyze a variant of the Thompson sampling (TS) algorithm for contextual bandits. At each round, traditional TS requires samples from the current posterior distribution, which is usually intractable. To circumvent this issue, approximate inference techniques can be used and provide samples with distribution close to the posteriors. However, current approximate techniques yield to either poor estimation (Laplace approximation) or can be computationally expensive (MCMC methods, Ensemble sampling…). In this paper, we propose a new algorithm, Varational Inference TS ( 𝚅𝙸𝚃𝚂 \mathtt{VITS} ), based on Gaussian Variational Inference. This scheme provides powerful posterior approximations which are easy to sample from, and is computationally efficient, making it an ideal choice for TS. In addition, we show that 𝚅𝙸𝚃𝚂 \mathtt{VITS} achieves a sub-linear regret bound of the same order in the dimension and number of round as traditional TS for linear contextual bandit. Finally, we demonstrate experimentally the effectiveness of 𝚅𝙸𝚃𝚂 \mathtt{VITS} on both synthetic and real world datasets.

Introduction

In traditional Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) problems, an agent, has to sequentially choose between several actions (referred to as ”arms”), from which he receives a reward from the environment. The arm selection process is induced by a sequence of policies, which is inferred and refined at each round from past observations. These policies are designed to optimize the cumulative rewards over the entire process. The main challenge in this task is to effectively manage a suitable exploitation and exploration trade-off
(
Robbins 1952
;
Katehakis & Veinott 1987
;
Berry & Fristedt 1985
;
Auer et al. 2002
;
Lattimore & Szepesvári 2020
;
Kveton et al. 2020
)
. Here, exploitation refers to selecting an arm that is currently believed to be the best based on past observations, while exploration refers to selecting arms that have not been selected frequently in the past in order to gather more information.

Contextual bandit problems is a particular instance of MAB problem, which supposes, at each round, that the set of arms and the corresponding reward depend on a
d
-dimensional feature vector called a contextual vector or context. This scenario has been extensively studied over the past decades and learning algorithms have been developed to address this problem
(
Langford & Zhang 2007
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
;
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
;
Kveton et al. 2020
)
, and they have been successfully applied in several real-world problem such as recommender systems, mobile health and finance
(
Li et al. 2010
;
Agarwal et al. 2016
;
Tewari & Murphy 2017
;
Bouneffouf et al. 2020
)
. The existing algorithms for addressing contextual bandit problems can be broadly categorized into two groups. The first category is based on maximum likelihood and the principle of optimism in the face of uncertainty (OFU) and has been studied in
(
Auer et al. 2002
;
Chu et al. 2011
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011b
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Ménard & Garivier 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Foster & Rakhlin 2020
;
Zenati et al. 2022
)
.

The second category consists in randomized probability matching algorithms, which is based on Bayesian belief and posterior sampling. Thompson Sampling (TS) is one of the most famous algorithms that fall into this latter category. Since its introduction by
Thompson 1933
, it has been widely studied, both theoretically and empirically
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2012
;
Kaufmann et al. 2012
;
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
;
Russo & Van Roy 2014
;
Russo & Van Roy 2016
;
Lu & Van Roy 2017
;
Riquelme et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
. Despite the fact that OFU algorithms offer better theoretical guarantees compared to classic TS-based algorithms, traditional TS methodologies still appeal to us due to their straightforward implementation and empirical advantages. In
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2012
)
, the authors claimed that: ”In applications like display advertising and news article recommendation, TS is competitive with or better than popular methods such as UCB”. Similarly,
(
Chapelle & Li 2011
)
has examined the empirical performances of TS on both simulated and real data. Their experiments demonstrate that TS outperforms OFU methods, leading them to conclude: ”In any case, TS is very easy to implement and should thus be considered as a standard baseline”. Taking all these factors into account, we have decided to focus on TS-based algorithms for addressing contextual bandit problems.

Despite its relative simplicity, effectiveness and convergence guarantees, TS comes with a computational burden which is to sample, at each iteration
t\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, from an appropriate Bayesian posterior distribution
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}
defined from the previous observations. Indeed, these posteriors are usually intractable and
approximate inference methods have to be used to obtain samples with distributions ”close” to the posterior. The family of TS methods using approximate inference methods will be referred to as approximate inference TS in the sequel. Among the simplest approximate
inference methods, Laplace approximation has been proposed for TS in

Chapelle & Li 2011
. This method consists of approximating
the posterior distribution
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}
by a Gaussian distribution with a
carefully chosen mean and covariance matrix. More precisely, the mean
is a mode of the target distribution which is typically found using an
optimization algorithm, while the covariance matrix is taken to be the
negative Hessian matrix of the log posterior at the considered
mode. Despite this method is easy to implement, it may lead to poor posterior representations. Indeed, while Laplace method achieves minimal optimality in terms of regret
(
Faury et al. 2022
)
, it doesn’t dictate the posterior convergence rate. More precisely, in
(
Katsevich & Rigollet 2023
)
it has been demonstrated that VI outperforms Laplace in terms of mean convergence by a factor of
1/n
. It is worth noting that the covariance rates remain the same for both methods. This discrepancy can lead to inadequate approximations, especially in high-dimensional settings, as highlighted in section I.4 of
(
Katsevich & Rigollet 2023
)
.

Another class of popular approximate inference
methods are Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, such as Metropolis or Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) algorithms. In the bandit literature, LMC has been proposed to get approximate samples from TS posteriors for solving traditional bandit problem in
(
Mazumdar et al. 2020
)
and for contextual
bandit problems in
Xu et al. 2022
;
Huix et al. 2023
. Also,
Lu & Van Roy 2017
have proposed to adapt Ensemble Methods to the bandit setting. Roughly, the idea here is to maintain and
incrementally update an ensemble of statistically plausible models and
to draw a uniform sample from this family at each iteration.

Finally, Variational Inference (VI)
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
is another class of approximate method that could be used to get samples from the posterior distribution. The core concept behind VI is to find a distribution
\tilde{\mathrm{q}}
, referred to as the variational posterior, to closely match the true posterior
\hat{\mathrm{p}}
in terms of Kullback-Leibler divergence (
\mathrm{KL}
) within a predefined family of distributions known as the variational family
\mathcal{G}
. In general, the variational family is chosen to make the optimization of the
\mathrm{KL}
tractable and to be easy to sample from. In their work
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
propose the mean-field mixture of Gaussian variational family for TS. This family of distributions is quite extensive and provides an accurate approximation for a wide range of posterior distributions. However, in our perspective, it might not be the most suitable choice for TS. Firstly, the optimization algorithm at each time step can be computationally expensive. Secondly, the mean-field assumption assumes that the parameters are independent, a premise that holds true in the regime of large, overparameterized models. In our perspective, this regime may not align with the Bandit problem, which often operates in a setting where the number of data points tends towards infinity in comparison to the model size.
Finally,
Yu et al. 2020
also employs VI in more general graphical models but focuses on structured arms and rewards, where the rewards are correlated through latent variables.

In this paper, we develop an efficient VI method that makes use of the whole family of non-degenerate Gaussian distributions. This choice of VI family is supported by
the Bernstein-Von Mises theorem
(
Van der Vaart 2000
)

. This theorem, subject to specific regularity conditions, asserts that a properly scaled version of the posterior converges to a Gaussian as the sample size grows. When applied to contextual bandits, the data points progressively accumulate over time, leading to the gradual concentration of the posterior around a dominant mode. As a consequence, the Gaussian approximation becomes increasingly suitable for representing the posterior in this particular setting. Furthermore, the covariance of the rescaled posterior distribution tends to converge towards the inverse Fisher information matrix, which may not necessarily be diagonal, thus justifying the need for a non-mean-field hypothesis. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

Our
first contribution
is methodological. We develop a novel variant of the TS algorithm, referred to as Variational Inference TS (
\mathtt{VITS}
). Our method addresses the main challenges encountered by the existing approximate TS algorithms and can be applied to a very large class of TS posteriors. Moreover, it enjoys a low computational cost both theoretically and empirically, since it boils down to adding a few optimization steps per round. We also propose two approximate versions of
VITS
, called
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
, that scale with the problem dimension.

Our
second contribution
is theoretical. We establish that our proposed methodology achieves a sub-linear regret of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3/2}\sqrt{T})
(up to logarithmic term) in the linear contextual bandit framework, where
T
is the number of rounds and
d
is the dimension of the policy parameter. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first regret bound derived for VI in the context of sequential learning.

Finally, our
last contribution
is to illustrate the empirical performances of our method on a synthetic and on the real world dataset MovieLens (
Lam & Herlocker
). It has been shown that in many cases,
VITS
outperforms existing approximate TS algorithms such as LMC algorithm.

Related work.

The theoretical foundations of TS for linear contextual bandits were initially explored by
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
. In this paper, the authors establish a sub-linear cumulative regret bound
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3/2}\sqrt{T})
for Linear TS (Lin-TS). Compared to this study, our method achieves a similar regret bound in the linear framework. However, it should be noted that Lin-TS is a specialized algorithm that can be only used when the posterior is known and can be efficiently sampled from.

As mentioned previously, VI has been suggested for TS in
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
. This paper introduces a TS algorithm called VTS that utilizes a mixture of mean-field Gaussian distributions to approximate the sequence of posteriors. In comparison to this work, the setting and the variational family we consider are richer than
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
. A more detailed comparison is postponed in Appendix
D
. Moreover, the methodology developed in
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
does not come with any convergence guarantees.
An empirical and theoretical study of using LMC as approximate inference method for TS for contextual bandit problems was carried out in
Xu et al. 2022
. This paper establishes that the resulting algorithm, called LMC-TS, achieves a state-of-the-art sub-linear cumulative regret for linear contextual bandits. Compared to this method, our approach yields a similar sub-linear regret in the same setting.
Finally,
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
suggests a TS method based on Neural Tangent Kernel. While this performs well on real datasets, their method is much more expensive than previously mentioned approaches, as it requires training a neural network.

2
Thompson sampling for contextual bandits

Contextual bandit:

We now present in more details the
contextual bandit framework. Let
\mathsf{X}
be a contextual space and
consider
\mathcal{A}:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow 2^{\mathsf{A}}
a set-valued action map, where

2^{\mathsf{A}}
stands for the power set of the action space
\mathsf{A}
. For
simplicity, we assume here that

\sup_{x\in\mathsf{X}}\mathrm{Card}(\mathcal{A}(x))<+\infty
. A (deterministic or
random) function
\pi:\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{A}
is said to be a policy if for any

x\in\mathsf{X}
,
\pi(x)\in\mathcal{A}(x)
. Then, for a fixed horizon

T\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, a contextual bandit process can be defined as follows:
at each iteration
t\in[T]
and given the past observations

\mathrm{D}_{t-1}=\{(x_{s},a_{s},r_{s})\}_{s<t}
:

•

The agent receives a contextual feature
x_{t}\in\mathsf{X}
;

•

The agent chooses an action
a_{t}=\pi_{t}(x_{t})
where
\pi_{t}
is a policy sampled from
\mathbb{Q}_{t}(\cdot|\mathrm{D}_{t-1})
;

•

Finally, the agent receives a reward
r_{t}
sampled from
\mathcal{R}(\cdot|x_{t},a_{t})
given
\mathrm{D}_{t-1}
. Here,
\mathcal{R}
is a Markov kernel on
(\mathsf{A}\times\mathsf{X})\times\mathsf{R}
, where
\mathsf{R}\subset\mathbb{R}

For a fixed family of conditional distributions
\mathbb{Q}_{1:T}=\{\mathbb{Q}_{t}\}_{t\leq T}
, this process defines a random sequence of policies,
\pi_{1:T}=\{\pi_{t}\}_{t\leq T}
with distribution still denoted by
\mathbb{Q}_{1:T}
by abuse of notation. Let’s defined the optimal expected reward for a contextual vector
x\in\mathsf{X}
and the expected reward given
x
and any action
a\in\mathcal{A}(x)
as follow

\displaystyle f_{\star}(x)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}(x)}f(x,a)\;,f(x,a)=\int r\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d}r|x,a)\;.

(1)

The main challenge of a contextual bandit problem is to find the distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{1:T}
that minimizes the cumulative regret defined as

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{Q}_{1:T})=\sum_{s\leq T}\mathrm{Regret}_{s}^{\pi_{s}}\;

(2)

\displaystyle\text{ with}\quad\textstyle\mathrm{Regret}_{s}^{{\pi_{s}}}=f_{\star}(x_{s})-f(x_{s},\pi_{s}(x_{s})).

The main difficulty in the contextual bandit problem, comes from the fact that the reward distribution
\mathcal{R}
is intractable and must be inferred to find the best policy to minimize the instantaneous regret
\pi\mapsto f_{\star}(x)-f(x,\pi(x))
for a context
x\in\mathsf{X}
. However, the estimation of
\mathcal{R}
may be in contradiction with the primary objective to minimize the cumulative regret (
2
), since potential non-effective arms has to be chosen to obtain a complete description of
\mathcal{R}
. Therefore, bandit learning algorithms have to achieve an appropriate trade-off between
exploitation of arms which have been confidently learned and exploration of misestimated arms.

Thompson sampling:

To achieve such a trade-off, we consider the popular Thompson Sampling (TS) algorithm. Consider a parametric model
\{\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\,:\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
for the reward distribution, where for any
\theta
,
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
is a Markov kernel on
(\mathsf{A}\times\mathsf{X})\times\mathsf{R}
parameterized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume in this paper that
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
admits a density with respect to some dominating measure
\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}
. An important example are
generalized linear bandits
(
Filippi et al. 2010
;
Kveton et al. 2020
)
.
In particular, it assumes that
\{\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(\cdot|x,a)\,:\,\theta\in\Theta\}
is an exponential family with respect to
\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}
, i.e., for
x\in\mathsf{X}
and
a\in\mathsf{A}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}_{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}}(r|x,a)=\mathrm{h}(r)\exp(g(\theta,x,a)\mathrm{T}(r)-\mathrm{C}(\theta,x,a)),

(3)

for
h:\mathsf{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, natural parameter and log-partition function
g,\mathrm{C}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and sufficient statistics
\mathrm{T}:\mathsf{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. The family is said to be in canonical form if
g(\theta,x,a)=\left\langle\phi(x,a),\theta\right\rangle
for some feature map
\phi:\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\mathrm{C}(\theta,x,a)=\sigma(\left\langle\phi(x,a),\theta\right\rangle)
for some link function
\sigma
.
Linear contextual bandits
(
Chu et al. 2011
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011b
)
fall into this model taking
\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathrm{Leb}
,
\mathrm{T}
equals to the identity function,

\text{$\mathrm{h}(r)=\exp(-\eta r^{2}/2)$ and $g(\theta,x,a)=\eta\left\langle\phi(x,a),\theta\right\rangle$},

(4)

for some
\eta>0
.
As a result,
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(\cdot|x,a)
is simply the Gaussian distribution with mean
\left\langle\phi(x,a),\theta\right\rangle
and variance
1/\eta
. Finally
(
Riquelme et al. 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2020
)
introduced an extension of linear contextual bandits, referred to as linear neural contextual bandits where
g
is a neural network with weights
\theta
and taking as input a pair
(x,a)
. With the introduced notations, the likelihood function associated to the observations
\mathrm{D}_{t}
at step
t>1
is given by

\mathrm{L}_{t}(\theta)\propto\exp\left\{\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\ell(\theta|x_{s},a_{s},r_{s})\right\}\;,

(5)

where the log-likelihood is given by
\ell(\theta|x_{s},a_{s},r_{s})=\log(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}_{\theta}/\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}})(r_{s}|x_{s},a_{s})\;.

Choosing a prior on
\theta
with density
\mathrm{p}_{0}
with respect to
\mathrm{Leb}
, and applying Bayes formula, the posterior distribution at round
t\in[T]
is given by

\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}=\mathrm{L}_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{p}_{0}(\theta)/\mathfrak{Z}_{t}

(6)

where
\mathfrak{Z}_{t}=\int\mathrm{L}_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{p}_{0}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\theta
denotes the normalizing constant and we used the convention that
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{1}=\mathrm{p}_{0}
. Moreover we define the potential function
U(\theta)\propto-\log\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}(\theta)
. Then, at each iteration
t\in[T]
, TS consists in sampling a sample
\theta_{t}
from the posterior
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}
and from it, use as a policy,
\pi_{t}^{(\mathrm{TS})}(x)
defined for any
x
by

\displaystyle\small\pi_{t}^{(\mathrm{TS})}(x)=a^{\theta_{t}}(x)\;,a^{\theta}(x)=\argmax_{a}\int r\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(\mathrm{d}r|x,a)

(7)

Since
\mathfrak{Z}_{t}
is generally intractable, sampling from the posterior distribution is not in general an option.

Variational inference TS:

To address this challenge, practitioners often employ approximate inference methods to generate samples from a distribution that is expected to be ”close” to the actual posterior distribution. In this context, we specifically concentrate on the application of VI. In this scenario, we consider a variational family
\mathcal{G}
which is a set of probability densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure, from which it is typically easy to sample from. Then ideally, at each round
t\in[T]
, the posterior distribution
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}
is approximated by the variational posterior distribution
\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}
which is defined as:

\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}=\argmin_{\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{p}|\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t})\;,

(8)

where
\mathrm{KL}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
However, we have to determine at each round a solution to the problem specified in (
8
).
In this paper, we consider as variational family the set of non-degenerate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathrm{N}(\mu,\Sigma)\,:\,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{+}\}
where
\mathrm{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
is the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{+}
is the set of symmetric positive definite matrices. As explained in the introduction, this Gaussian variational family is particularly relevant in bandit framework according to Bernstein-Von Mises theorem.

Presentation of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}
:

As we will see, this choice of variational family will allow to derive an efficient method for solving (
8
) using the Riemannian structure of
\mathcal{G}
. As noted in
(
Lambert et al. 2022b
)
,
\mathcal{G}
equipped with the Wasserstein distance of order 2 is a complete metric space as a closed subset of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the set of probability distributions with finite second moment. Recall that for two Gaussian distributions
p_{0}=\mathrm{N}(\mu_{0},\Sigma_{0})
and
p_{1}=\mathrm{N}(\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1})
, their Wasserstein distance has a closed form:

\displaystyle\small W_{2}^{2}\left(p_{0},p_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{0}+\Sigma_{1}-2(\Sigma_{0}^{1/2}\Sigma_{1}\Sigma_{0}^{1/2})^{1/2})\;.

This Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{G}
allows to derive a Riemannian metric denoted
\mathfrak{g}
. The corresponding geodesic is given through the exponential map. More precisely, for a Gaussian distribution
p=\mathrm{N}(\mu_{p},\Sigma_{p})
, this map is defined as

\displaystyle\exp_{p}(\mu_{v},\Sigma_{v})=

\displaystyle(\mu_{p}+\mu_{v}+(\Sigma_{v}+\mathrm{I})(\cdot-\mu_{p}))_{\#}p

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{N}(\mu_{p}+\mu_{v},\;(\Sigma_{v}+\mathrm{I})\;\Sigma_{p}\;(\Sigma_{v}+\mathrm{I}))\;.

With all these preliminaries, we can now present and motivate the algorithm developed in

(
Lambert et al. 2022b
)
to efficiently solve (
8
). This method can be formalized as a Riemannian gradient descent scheme on
\mathcal{G}
. Firstly, we define the loss function
\mathcal{F}_{t}:p\rightarrow\mathrm{KL}(p|\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t})
. Then, following
(
Lambert et al. 2022b
)
, we derive the gradient operator of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
on
\mathcal{G}
equipped with
\mathfrak{g}
as

\small\nabla_{\mathfrak{g}}\mathcal{F}_{t}(p)=(\int\nabla U_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}p(\theta),\;\int\nabla^{2}U_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}p(\theta)-\Sigma_{p}^{-1})

(10)

where
\Sigma_{p}
is the covariance matrix of
p
. From this expression, the corresponding Riemannian gradient descent
(
Bonnabel 2013
)
using a step size
h_{t}>0
defines the sequence of iterates
\{\mathrm{q}_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
recursively as:

\mathrm{q}_{t,k+1}=\exp_{\mathrm{q}_{t,k}}(-h_{t}\nabla_{\mathfrak{g}}\mathcal{F}_{t}(\mathrm{q}_{t,k}))\;.

At each time step
t
, this sequence is initialized with variational posterior at the previous step, ie,
\mathrm{q}_{t,0}=\mathrm{q}_{t-1,K_{t-1}}
. Please note that this warm initialization of the posterior results in an efficient algorithm and has been directly used in our main theoretical result (see (
31
)). Combining (
9
) and (
10
), this recursion amounts defining a sequence of means
\{\mu_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
and covariance matrices
\{\Sigma_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
by the recursions

\displaystyle\mu_{t,k+1}=\mu_{t,k}-h_{t}\int\nabla U_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{q}_{t,k}(\theta),

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t,k+1}=A_{t,k}\Sigma_{t,k}A_{t,k},

\displaystyle\mathrm{q}_{t,k+1}=\mathrm{N}(\mu_{t,k+1},\Sigma_{t,k+1})

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad A_{t,k}=\mathrm{I}-h_{t}(\int\nabla^{2}U_{t}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{q}_{t,k}(\theta)-\Sigma_{t,k}^{-1})

The main computational challenge in this recursion stems is that the integrals involved are typically intractable. To overcome this issue, we employ a Monte Carlo procedure to approximate these integrals. Subsequently, we consider a sequence of mean values denoted as
\{\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
and covariance matrices
\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{1}}
such that:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{t,k+1}=\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}-h_{t}\nabla U_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}),\quad\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k+1}=\tilde{A}_{t,k}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}\;\tilde{A}_{t,k}

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\tilde{A}_{t,k}=\mathrm{I}-h_{t}(\nabla^{2}U_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k})-\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}^{-1})\;,

where
\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}\sim\mathrm{N}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,k},\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k})
. Consequently, following
(
Lambert et al. 2022b
)
we obtain an algorithm capable of addressing the problem defined in (
8
). However, this algorithm exhibits computational inefficiency, particularly in high-dimensional scenarios. This inefficiency arises from the necessity to sample from a Gaussian distribution with a non-diagonal covariance matrix during each updating step
k\in[K_{t}]
. As a result, it becomes impractical for use in a contextual bandit problem, where, at each time step
t
, we must solve the problem described in (
8
). This paper introduces an improved version of the earlier algorithm, designed to efficiently address the problem presented in (
8
). To achieve this, we begin by examining a sequence of matrices denoted as
B_{t,k}
, defined by the following

B_{t,k+1}=\big\{\mathrm{I}-h_{t}\nabla^{2}U_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k})\big\}B_{t,k}+h_{t}{(B_{t,k}^{-1})^{\top}}\;.

(11)

It is important to note that
B_{t,k}
is a square-root matrix of the covariance of the variational distribution
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}
, ie,
B_{t,k}B_{t,k}^{\top}=\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}
. Then we can sample efficiently from the variational distribution using
B_{t,k}
with

3.1
Linear Bandit

In this section, we are interested in convergence guarantees for

applied to the linear contextual bandit framework.
This framework consists in assuming that
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
has form (
3

\lambda_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathrm{Leb}

\mathrm{T}

\mathrm{h}

\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Leb}}(r|x,a)\propto\exp[\eta(r-\left\langle\phi(x,a),\theta\right\rangle)^{2}/2]\;.

(14)

Assumption on the reward kernel
\mathcal{R}
is the following:

Assumption 3.1
.

(Sub-Gaussian Reward Distribution)
There exists
R>1
such that for any
x\in\mathsf{X}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}(x)
,
\rho>0
,
\log\int\exp\{\rho(r-f(x,a))\}\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d}r|x,a)\leq R\rho^{2}\;,
where

1

We could only assume that

in Assumption
3.1
since if a distribution is
R
-sub-Gaussian, it is also
R^{\prime}
-sub-Gaussian for any
R^{\prime}\geq R
, however, we choose to set
R\geq 1
to ease the presentation of our main results. We also assume that the model is well-specified.

Assumption 3.2
.

There exists
\theta^{\star}
such that
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta^{\star}}
and satisfying
\lVert\theta^{\star}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Feature map
\phi
satisfies the boundedness condition.

Assumption 3.3
.

For any contextual vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and action
a\in\mathcal{A}(x)
, it holds that
\lVert\phi(x,a)\rVert_{2}\leq 1
.

Uniform boundedness condition on the feature map is relatively common for obtaining regret bounds for linear bandit problems
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Kveton et al. 2020

Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011b

Assumption 3.4
.

The prior distribution is assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian distribution with variance
1/(\lambda\eta)

\eta
also appears in the definition
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
in (
14
),

While our theoretical results can readily be extended to accommodate a non-zero mean Gaussian prior, for the sake of simplicity, we have chosen to center the prior. Under Assumption
3.4
, combining (
6
) and (
14
),
the negative log posterior
-\log\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}
denoted by

\displaystyle\textstyle U_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}(\phi(a_{s},x_{s})^{\top}\theta-r_{s})^{2}+\lambda\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{2}(\theta^{\top}V_{t}\theta-2\theta^{\top}b_{t}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}r_{s}^{2})\;,

(16)

\displaystyle V_{t}=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathrm{I}_{d}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\phi_{s}\phi_{s}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\;b_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}r_{s}\phi_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 1}.

\nabla U_{t}(\theta)=\eta(V_{t}\theta-b_{t})

\nabla^{2}U_{t}(\theta)=\eta V_{t}

\hat{\mu}_{t}=V_{t}^{-1}b_{t}
and covariance matrix
\hat{\Sigma}_{t}=(\eta V_{t})^{-1}

Denote by
\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T}
the distribution on the sequence of policies induced by the sequence of variational posterior
\{\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}=\mathrm{N}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}},B_{t,K_{t}}^{\top}B_{t,K_{t}})\}_{t\in[T]}
obtained with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume Assumptions
3.1
to
3.4
hold. For the choice of hyperparameters
\{K_{t},h_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
and
\eta
specified in Section
A.2
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the cumulative regret is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T})\leq\frac{CR^{2}d\sqrt{dT}\log(3T^{3})}{\lambda^{2}}\log\Big(\frac{(1+T/\lambda d)}{\delta}\Big)

where
C\geq 0
is a constant independent of the problem. Our main result shows that the distribution of the sequence of policies generated by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}
results in a cumulative regret of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\sqrt{dT})
. It is in the same order as the state-of-the-art cumulative regret obtained in
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
)
for LinTS.
The number of optimization steps
K_{t}
we found are of order
\kappa_{t}^{2}\log(dT\log(T))
where

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}

\log(dT\log(T))
. Finally,
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
derived similar bounds for TS using LMC for linear contextual bandit problems.
Although our proof is based on the linear case, it could be extended to more general cases insofar as our updates remain Gaussian by definition of the variational family. This allows the use of Gaussian (anti) concentration bound in the theoretical analysis. This is in contrast to other approximation methods, which do not possess this advantage.

Numerical experiments

4.1
Linear and quadratic bandit

Our initial investigation focused on a toy setting where contextual vectors are sampled from a Gaussian distribution. However, in this specific setting, the contextual vectors exhibit high diversity, resulting in a posterior covariance matrix with a condition number of
O(1)
. This condition makes the optimization problem overly simplistic, as a result, all approximation methods seem to perform identically in this simple well-conditioned problem. So we introduce a novel setting in which the diversity of arms is controlled by a parameter, denoted as
\zeta
. Firstly, we consider a fixed pool of arms denoted as
P=[\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n}]
with
n=50
, where each arm
\tilde{x}_{i}
follows a normal distribution
\mathrm{N}(0_{d},\mathrm{I}_{d})
. This fixed pool is relevant in real-world scenarios, such as in a Recommender system, where this pool corresponds to the concept of a meta-user. Then, at each step
t\in[T]
, for every arm, we randomly sample a vector
\tilde{x}_{i}
from the pool
P
, and the contextual vector associated with this arm is defined as
x=\tilde{x}_{i}+\zeta\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},\mathrm{I}_{d})
. When
\zeta
has a high value, the corresponding user is far from the meta-user. Consequently, the diversity among arms is high, resulting in a well-conditioned problem. However, in cases where
\zeta
is low, the problem is ill-conditioned and the optimization becomes challenging.

We consider the linear bandit and the quadratic bandit problems. In both settings, the bandit environment is simulated using a random vector
\theta^{\star}
sampled from a normal distribution
\mathrm{N}(0_{d},\sigma^{\star}\mathrm{I}_{d})
. We opted for
\sigma^{\star}=1/d
to ensure that the variance of the scalar product
x^{\top}\theta^{\star}
remains independent of the dimension
d
. The parameter dimension
d
is set to
20
and we consider a number of arms
K=50
. In the linear bandit setting, the reward associated with the contextual vector
x
, is
r=x^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\alpha\epsilon
where
\epsilon\sim\mathrm{N}(0_{d},\mathrm{I}_{d})
. However, to maintain problem complexity independent of
\zeta
, we have set the signal-to-noise ratio to a fixed value of 1, meaning
\mathbb{E}[(x^{\top}\theta^{\star})^{2}]/\mathbb{E}[(\alpha\epsilon)^{2}]=1
. This implies that
\sqrt{1+\zeta^{2}}=\alpha
. See Appendix
H
for more details about the setting. In these experiments, we have chosen to compare
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
,
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
, Linear TS (LinTS), and LMC-TS, with 10 and 50 iterations of Langevin diffusion at each step. For
VITS
based algorithm, we have only used 10 updating steps. We have omitted the performance of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}
since it experimentally performs identically to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
. For the algorithm
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
, we approximate the integral presented in (
13
) using 20 Monte Carlo samples. This choice is made due to the observed instability caused by the Monte Carlo error when considering high values of
\eta
. However, in our setting, even with 20 Monte Carlo samples,
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
remains a faster method compared to
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
. We also attempted to assess the performance of VTS, but, in the ill-conditioned setting, it exhibited a linear and notably high cumulative regret. Consequently, we have opted to exclude it from the figure for the sake of clarity and visibility. The mean and standard error are reported for all experiments over 50 runs. The hyperparameter is provided in Appendix
E

MovieLens Dataset

In this section, we evaluate
VITS
on the MovieLens dataset, consisting of one million ratings by 6040 users for 3952 movies. We adopt the setup proposed in
(
Aouali et al. 2022
)
, involving a low-rank factorization of the rating matrix to yield 5-dimensional representations for users (
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
) and movies (
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
). Movies are treated as potential actions, and context
x_{t}
is uniformly sampled from the pool of user vectors. We consider logistic rewards, sampled from
\text{Ber}(\mu(x_{j}^{\top}\theta_{i}))
, where
\mu
is the sigmoid function. We conduct 50 simulations, each involving 100 randomly selected movies.Our prior distribution employs a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu_{0}
and covariance
\Sigma_{0}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{0})
. Here,
\mu_{0}
and
\sigma_{0}
represent the mean and variance of movie vectors across all dimensions. This setting deviates somewhat from our theoretical framework, where we consider a unified posterior distribution for all arms using a feature map function
\phi
representing context-action pairs. In the MovieLens context, each arm possesses an individual posterior distribution. These two settings closely align when the feature map is the vector concatenation function. In practice, we can apply
VITS
or LMC at each arm to obtain posterior samples. In this experiment, we compare LinTS against LMC-TS,
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
, and the
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
variant. LMC-TS uses 10 Langevin updating steps. It’s crucial to note that for each time step
t
and each arm
a
, LMC-TS requires running Langevin diffusion to obtain a new parameter with low correlation to the previous one. This leads to a high computational complexity for LMC-TS. In contrast,
VITS
for each arm only involves sampling from a low-dimensional Gaussian distribution and updating the variational posterior corresponding to the chosen arm. This approach offers significant computational efficiency.

6
Conclusion and perspectives

This paper presents a novel TS algorithms called
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{I}}
, that
use VI as an approximation method. This algorithms provide robust theoretical guarantees, in particular a
cumulative regret bound of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\sqrt{dT})
in the linear setting.
One limitation of our theoretical analysis is that the regret bound derived is limited to the linear setting while the interest of our algorithm relies on nonlinear tasks. Additionally, we introduce two other algorithms, named
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
, which offer enhanced computational efficiency. The
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
algorithm uses a Taylor expansion to remove the computation of inverse matrix and the
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II Hessian-free}}
algorithm removes the computations of Hessian, resulting in faster execution. Finally, all algorithms have been extensively evaluated in both simulated and real problems.

)

(
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
)

(
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
)
to
VITS
. We give in this section the main steps of our proofs. Each step is based on Lemmas which are stated and proved in the next sections. First, we define the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}}
such that for any
t\in[T]
,
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the
\sigma

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
and
x_{t}
where

t-1
and

t
.
For some feature map

and
for any
t\in[T]

\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}=\phi(x_{t},a^{\star}_{t})\;,\,\phi_{t}=\phi(x_{t},a_{t})\;,

the features vector of the best arm
a^{\star}_{t}
and the features vector of the arm
a_{t}
chosen by
VITS
at time
t
respectively.
the difference between the best expected reward and the expected reward obtained by
VITS

At each round
t\in[T]
, we consider the set of saturated arms
\mathcal{S}_{t}
and unsaturated arms
\mathcal{U}_{t}
defined by

\mathcal{S}_{t}=\bigcap_{a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t})}\{\Delta_{t}(a)>g(t)\;\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\}\;,

(17)

and
\mathcal{U}_{t}=\mathcal{A}(x_{t})\backslash\ \mathcal{S}_{t}
where

) and

for some constant

independent of

,
t
and

. In addition, consider the events
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
and
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
such that

\bigcap_{a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t})}\{|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\theta^{\star}|\leq\;g_{1}(t)\;\;\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\}\subset\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}

\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}=\bigcap_{a\subset\mathcal{A}(x_{t})}\{|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}|\leq\;g_{2}(t)\;\;\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\}\;,

where
\hat{\mu}_{t}
is given by
\hat{\mu}_{t}=V^{-1}b_{t}
and

is given in (
15
).
The specific definitions of
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
,
g,g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are given in Section
A.3
of the supplementary. Nevertheless, by definition, it holds that
g_{1}(t)+g_{2}(t)\leq g(t)
.

1.

For ease of notation, the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{1:T}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{1:T}}[\cdot]
and probabilities
\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{1:T}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{1:T}}(\cdot)
with respect to the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
are denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]
and
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\cdot)
respectively.
Therefore, with these notations, we have by definition of the cumulative regret:

\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{t}\;.

We now bound for any
t\in[T]
, with high probability,
\Delta_{t}(a_{t})
.
To this end, in the next step of the proof, we show that the stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in[T]}
defined below is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[T]}
super-martingale.

X_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}Y_{s}

with

Y_{s}=\Delta_{s}-cg(s)\|\phi_{s}\|_{V^{-1}_{s}}/p-2/s^{2}\;,

where
p\in(0,1)
and
c
is a sufficiently large real number, independent of
d
,
T
and
s
.

2.

Showing that
(X_{t})_{t\in[T]}
is a super-martingale.

We consider the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}]+\;\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})|\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{true}}]\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{true}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}]+\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{true}})\;,

(18)

where we used for the last inequality that
\lVert\theta^{\star}\rVert_{2}\leq 1
and Assumption
3.3
.
Then, since
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})|\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}]\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t})+\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})|\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{var}}]\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{var}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}[\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})|\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}]+\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{var}})]

(19)

where in the last line we have used that
\Delta_{t}(a_{t})\leq 1
again.
Denote by
\bar{a}_{t}=\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{U}_{t}}\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}
and
\overline{\phi}_{t}=\phi(x_{t},\bar{a}_{t})
. Then, given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
and
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}(a_{t})

\displaystyle=\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}-\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}

\displaystyle=\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}-\overline{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\overline{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}-\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}g(t)\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}+\overline{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}-\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}g(t)\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}+(\overline{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}+g(t)\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}})-(\phi_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-g(t)\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}(2\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}+\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}})g(t)

(20)

where inequality
(a)
is due to
\bar{a}_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}
, and therefore
\Delta_{t}(\bar{a}_{t})\leq g(t)\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}
,
inequality
(b)
uses that given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
and
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
, for any
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|\phi^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\phi^{\top}\theta^{\star}|\leq g(t)\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}
since by definition
g_{1}(t)+g_{2}(t)\leq g(t)
; finally, the arm
a_{t}
maximizes the quantity
\phi(x_{t},a_{t})^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}
,
\overline{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\phi_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}
is obviously negative, which implies inequality
(c)
.

Moreover, given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
and
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}|a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}]\mathbb{P}_{t}(a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t})+\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}|a_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}]\mathbb{P}_{t}(a_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\mathbb{P}_{t}(a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}(p-1/t^{2})\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

where
(a)
is due to the definition of
\overline{\phi}_{t}
, i.e. for any
a\in\mathcal{U}_{t},\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\leq\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}
, and
(b)
uses Lemma
A.4
with
p\in(0,1)
. Here is one of the main differences with the proof conducted by
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
)
. Indeed, to obtain such a bound, we need to carefully dig into the convergence of the the sequence of means
\{\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}\}_{k\in[1,K_{t}]}
and covariance matrices
\{\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,K_{t}}\}_{k\in[1,K_{t}]}
to obtain a fine-grained analysis of the distribution of
\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}
.
Therefore, using equations (
19
) and (
2
)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})]\leq\Big(\frac{2}{p-1/t^{2}}+1\Big)g(t)\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}]+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\leq\frac{cg(t)}{p}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}]+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\;,

where
c
is a sufficiently large real number independent of the problem. Plugging this bounds in
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{cg(t)}{p}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}]+\frac{1}{t^{2}}+\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{t}^{\textrm{true}})

Applying Lemma
A.3
yields

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta_{t}(a_{t})]\leq\frac{cg(t)}{p}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}]+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\;.

This is another important difference with the original proof of
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2012
)
which uses our precise convergence study for
\{\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}\}_{k\in[1,K_{t}]}
.
Then, it follows that
(X_{t})_{t\in[T]}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[T]}
-super martingale.

3.

Concentration for
(X_{t})_{t\in[T]}
.

Note that
(X_{t})_{t\in[T]}
is a super-martingale with bounded increments: for any
t\in[T]

\displaystyle|X_{t+1}-X_{t}|

\displaystyle=|Y_{t+1}|

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{t}(a_{t})-\frac{cg(t)}{p}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}-\frac{2}{t^{2}}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}|\Delta_{t}(a_{t})(a_{t})-\frac{cg(t)}{\sqrt{\lambda}p}-\frac{2}{t^{2}}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3cg(t)}{\sqrt{\lambda}p}\;,

where in
(a)
we have used that

\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\leq\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{1}}\leq 1/\sqrt{\lambda}

and inequality
(b)
is due to
\Delta_{t}(a_{t})\leq 1
,
2/t^{2}\leq 2
and
3cg(t)/(p\sqrt{\lambda})>2
for an appropriate choice of the numerical constant
c
. Therefore, applying Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (Lemma (
B.3
)), with probability
1-\delta
it holds that

\displaystyle X_{T}\leq\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)\sum_{s=1}^{T}\frac{9c^{2}g(s)^{2}}{p^{2}\lambda}}\leq\sqrt{18\log(1/\delta)\frac{c^{2}}{p^{2}\lambda}g(T)^{2}T}\;,

using that
g(T)\geq g(t)
.

4.

Conclusion.

The super-martingale
(X_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is directly linked to the cumulative regret by

\displaystyle X_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\Delta_{t}-cg(t)\|\phi_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}/p-2/t^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}cg(t)\|\phi_{t}\|_{V_{t}^{-1}}/p+2/t^{2}

then taking the expectation and using the super-martingale previous argument of the proof, we obtain the following upper bound for the cumulative regret :

\displaystyle\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{cg(t)}{p}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}+\sqrt{18\log(1/\delta)\frac{c^{2}}{p^{2}\lambda}g(T)^{2}T}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\;.

using that
\sum_{t=1}^{+\infty}1/t^{2}\leq\pi^{2}/6
. As a result, applying Lemma
A.5
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T})\leq\frac{cg(T)}{p}\sqrt{2dT\log\Big(1+T/(\lambda d})\Big)+\frac{cg(T)}{p\sqrt{\lambda}}\sqrt{18\log(1/\delta)T}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\;.

Using the definition of
g(T)
in (
A.1
), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{CRegret}(\mathbb{\tilde{Q}}_{1:T})\leq\frac{CR^{2}d}{\lambda^{2}}\log(3T^{3})\sqrt{dT\log\big(1+T/(\lambda d)\big)\log\big(1/\delta\big)}\;,

where
C\geq 0
is a constant, independent of the problem, which completes the proof.

A.2
Hyperparameters choice and values

In this section, we define and discuss the values of the main hyperparameters.

Parameter

\bm{\eta:}

is the inverse of the temperature. The lower is
\eta
, the better is the exploration. It is fixed to

\eta=4\lambda^{2}/(81R^{2}d\log(3T^{3}))\leq 1

(21)

Parameter

\bm{\lambda:}

is the inverse of the standard deviation of the prior distribution. It controls the regularization. The lower is
\lambda
, the better is the exploitation. This parameter is fixed but lower than 1.

Parameter

\bm{h_{t}:}

is the step size used in all Algorithms. It is fixed to

h_{t}=\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})/(2\eta(\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})^{2}+2\lambda_{\text{max}}(V_{t})^{2}))

(22)

Parameter

\bm{K_{t}:}

is the number of gradient descent steps performed. It is fixed to

K_{t}=1+2(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})\log\Big(2R\kappa_{t}d^{2}T^{2}\log^{2}(3T^{3})\Big)\;.

(23)

Therefore the number of gradient descent steps is
K_{t}\leq\mathcal{O}(\kappa_{t}^{2}\log(dT\log(T)))
.

A.3
Useful definitions

Definition A.1
.

(Variational approximation)

Recall that
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t}(\theta)\propto\exp(-U_{t}(\theta))
is the posterior distribution. And
\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}
is the variational posterior distribution in the sense that

\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}=\argmin_{\mathrm{p}\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{p}|\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{t})\;,

where
\mathcal{G}
is a variational family. In this paper we focus on the Gaussian variational family and we denote by
\tilde{\mu}_{t}
and
B_{t}
respectively the mean and the square root covariance matrix of the variational distribution, ie,

\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t}=\mathrm{N}(\tilde{\mu}_{t},B_{t}B_{t}^{\top})\;.

The values of
\tilde{\mu}_{t}
and
B_{t}
are obtained after running
K_{t}
steps of algorithm
2
or
3
. Note that the sequence of means
\{\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
is defined recursively by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{t,k+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}-h_{t}\nabla U_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}-h_{t}\eta V_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}-\hat{\mu}_{t})

where
\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}\sim\mathrm{N}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,k},B_{t,k}^{\top}B_{t,k})
and we have used that
\nabla U_{t}(\theta)=\eta(V_{t}\theta-b_{t})
(see equation (
15
)). Consequently,
\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}
is also Gaussian and we denote by
\tilde{m}_{t,k}
and
\tilde{W}_{t,k}
its mean and covariance matrix, ie,
\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}\sim\mathrm{N}(\tilde{m}_{t,k},\tilde{W}_{t,k})
.
Furthermore, the sequence of square root covariance matrix
\{B_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
is defined recursively in Algorithm
2
by

\displaystyle B_{t,k+1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathrm{I}-h_{t}\nabla^{2}(U_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}))\right\}B_{t,k}+(B_{t,k}^{\top})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathrm{I}-\eta h_{t}V_{t}\right\}B_{t,k}+h_{t}(B_{t,k}^{\top})^{-1}

where we have used that
\nabla^{2}(U_{t}(\theta))=\eta V_{t}
for the linear bandit case (see (
15
)). Let denote by
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}=B_{t,k}B_{t,k}^{\top}
the covariance of the variational posterior
\tilde{\mathrm{q}}_{t,k}
. For ease of notation we denote by
A_{t}=\mathrm{I}-\eta h_{t}V_{t}
, it follows that

\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k+1}=A_{t}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}A_{t}+2h_{t}A_{t}+h_{t}^{2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}_{t,k}

If
\Lambda_{t,k}=\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}-1/\eta V^{-1}_{t}
denotes the difference between the covariance matrix of the varational posterior and the true posterior, therefore it holds that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t,k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{t}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,k}A_{t}+2h_{t}A_{t}+h_{t}^{2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}_{t,k}-1/\eta V^{-1}_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}\Lambda_{t,k}A_{t}+2h_{t}A_{t}-2h_{t}\mathrm{I}+\eta h_{t}^{2}V_{t}+h_{t}^{2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}_{t,k}

\displaystyle=A_{t}\Lambda_{t,k}A_{t}-\eta h_{t}^{2}V_{t}+h_{t}^{2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}_{t,k}

\displaystyle=A_{t}\Lambda_{t,k}A_{t}-h_{t}^{2}\eta V_{t}\Lambda_{t,k}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}_{t,k}

Definition A.2
.

(Concentration events)

The main challenge for the proof of Theorem
3.5
, is to control the probability of the following events:
for any
t\in[T]
we define

•

\widehat{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}=\left\{\text{for any }a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t}):\;|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\theta^{\star}|\leq\;g_{1}(t)\;\;\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\right\}

•

\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}=\widehat{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}\bigcap\Big\{|\xi_{t}|<R\sqrt{1+\log 3t^{2}}\Big\}\bigcap\Big\{\|\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})\|\leq\sqrt{4d\log 3t^{3}}\Big\}

•

\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}=\left\{\text{for any }a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t}):\;|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}|\leq\;g_{2}(t)\;\;\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\right\}
,

where
g_{1}(t)=R\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})}+\sqrt{\lambda}
and
g_{2}(t)=10\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})/(\eta\lambda)}
and
\xi_{t}
is the R-sub Gaussian noise of the reward definition defined by the relation

r_{t}=\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi_{t}\;.

(24)

The first event
\widehat{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}
controls the concentration of
\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}
around its mean. Similarly, event
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
controls the concentration of
\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}
around its mean. Note that compared to
(
Agrawal & Goyal 2013
)
, in our case, it is important to include within
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, the concentration of the distributions
\xi_{t}
and
\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})
. Consequently, conditionally on
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
and
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
it holds that: for any
a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t})

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\theta^{\star}|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(R\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})}+\sqrt{\lambda}+10\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})/(\eta\lambda)}\Bigg)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq 12R\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})/(\eta\lambda)}\;\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{108dR^{2}}{\lambda^{3/2}}\sqrt{\log(3t^{3})\log(3T^{3})}\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle:=g(t)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\;,

(25)

where in
(a)
, we have used that
\eta=4\lambda/\Big(81R^{2}d\log(3T^{3})\Big)
and in the last inequality we have used that
g(t)=CR^{2}d\sqrt{\log(t)\log(T)}/\lambda^{3/2}
.

A.4
Main lemmas

Lemma A.3
.

(Concentration lemma for
\hat{\mu}_{t}
)

Recall the definition of the event
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
in (
A.2
). Therefore, for any
t\in[T]
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t})\geq 1-\frac{1}{t^{2}}

(26)

This lemma shows that the mean of the posterior distribution
\hat{\mu}_{t}
is concentrated around the true parameter
\theta^{\star}
with high probability.

Proof.

Firstly, we apply Lemma
B.4
, with
m_{t}=\phi_{t}/\sqrt{\lambda}=\phi(x_{t},a_{t})/\sqrt{\lambda}
and
\epsilon_{t}=\big(r_{a_{t}}(t)-\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}\big)/\sqrt{\lambda}
, where
r_{a_{t}}(t)
is sampled from the R-sub-Gaussian reward distribution of mean
\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}
. Let’s define the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\prime}=\{a_{\tau+1},m_{\tau+1},\epsilon_{\tau}\}_{\tau\leq t}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\prime}
,
m_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{\prime}
-measurable. Moreover,
\epsilon_{t}
is conditionally
R/\sqrt{\lambda}
-sub-Gaussian due to Assumption
3.1
and is a martingale difference process because
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{\prime}]=0
. If we denote by

M_{t}=\mathrm{I}_{d}+1/\lambda\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}m_{\tau}m_{\tau}^{\tau}=1/\lambda V_{t+1}\;,

and

\zeta_{t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}m_{\tau}\epsilon_{\tau}\;,

Then, Lemma
B.4
shows that
\|\zeta_{t}\|_{M_{t}^{-1}}\leq R/\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{d\log(\frac{t+1}{\delta^{\prime}})}
with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
.
Moreover, note that

\displaystyle M_{t-1}^{-1}(\zeta_{t-1}-\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle=M_{t}^{-1}(1/\lambda b_{t}-1/\lambda\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\phi_{\tau}\phi_{\tau}^{\top}\theta^{\star}-\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle=M_{t-1}^{-1}(1/\lambda b_{t}-M_{t-1}\theta^{\star})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{t}-\theta^{\star}\;.

Note that
||\theta^{\star}||_{M_{t-1}^{-1}}=\lVert\theta^{\star}M_{t-1}^{-1/2}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\theta^{\star}\rVert_{2}\lVert M_{t-1}^{-1/2}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\theta^{\star}\rVert_{2}
, where the last inequality is due to Assumption
3.2
. Then, for any arm

we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\theta^{\star}|

\displaystyle=|\phi(x_{t},a)M_{t-1}^{-1}(\xi_{t-1}-\theta^{\star})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{M_{t-1}^{-1}}\|\xi_{t-1}-\theta^{\star}\|_{M_{t-1}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{M_{t-1}^{-1}}(\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{M_{t-1}^{-1}}+\|\theta^{\star}\|_{M_{t-1}^{-1}})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\left(R/\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{d\log(\frac{t}{\delta^{\prime}})}+1\right)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda}\left(R/\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})}+1\right)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\left(R\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})}+\sqrt{\lambda}\right)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle:=g_{1}(t)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\;.

This inequality holds with probability at least
\delta^{\prime}=1/(3t^{2})
.

Moreover, recall the definition of the R-subGaussian noise of the reward definition in section
A.2

r_{t}=\phi_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi_{t}

Then it holds that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{t}|>x)\leq\exp(1-x^{2}/R^{2})

\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{t}|\leq R\sqrt{1+\log 3t^{2}})\geq 1-1/(3t^{2})

t\leq 1
. Finally, recall the definition of
\tilde{W}_{t,k}
,
\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}
and
\tilde{m}_{t,k}
in section
A.1
. Consequently, the term
\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})
is gaussian with mean
0
and an identity covariance matrix. Therefore, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})\|\leq\sqrt{4d\log 3t^{3}}\Big)\geq 1-1/(3t^{2})

(27)

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\widehat{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}\bigcap\left\{|\xi_{i}|<R\sqrt{1+\log 3t^{2}}\right\}\bigcap\left\{\|\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})\|\leq\sqrt{4d\log 3t^{3}}\right\}\Big)\geq 1-\frac{1}{t^{2}}\;,

where
\widehat{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}
is defined is
A.2

Lemma A.4
.

Probability of playing an unsaturated arm

Given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
defined in section (
A.2
), the conditional probability of playing an unsaturated arm is strictly positive and is lower bounded as

\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}\mathbb{P}_{t}(a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}):=\mathbb{P}(a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}}(p-1/t^{2})\;,

(28)

where
p=1/\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{e}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{t}
is defined in (
17
).

Proof.

If we suppose that
\forall a\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
,
\;\phi(x_{t},a^{\star}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}
, then
a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}
. Indeed, The optimal arm
a^{\star}_{t}
is obviously in the unsaturated arm set (
\mathcal{U}_{t}
) and
\phi(x_{t},a_{t})^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\phi(x_{t},a^{\star}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}
by construction of the algorithm. Hence we have

\mathbb{P}(a_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq\mathbb{P}(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t},\forall a\in\mathcal{S}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

Subsequently, given events
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
and
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
we have

Indeed, for any
a\in\mathcal{S}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\theta^{\star}+g(t)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{\hat{\Sigma}_{t}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\theta^{\star}\;,

and

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\theta^{\star}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\theta^{\star}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t})\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t})+\mathbb{P}(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\theta^{\star}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\mathbb{P}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\geq\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t},\forall a\in\mathcal{S}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})+\mathbb{P}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)

Lemma A.5
.

(Upper bound of
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\phi_{t}\|_{\hat{\Sigma}_{t}}
)

The following lemma we will be useful in the derivation of the regret bound later in the proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\leq\sqrt{2dT\log\Big(1+\frac{T}{\lambda d}\Big)}

Proof.

Recall the relation between the 1-norm and 2-norm for a d-dimensional vector, ie,
\|\cdot\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{d}\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}^{2}}

Firs, recall the definition of
V_{t}=\lambda\mathrm{I}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\phi_{s}\phi_{s}^{\top}
in (
15
).
Therefore, we apply Lemma 11 and Lemma 10 of
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011b
)
, then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\log\frac{\det V_{t}}{\det\lambda\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\log\frac{(\lambda+T/d)^{d}}{\lambda^{d}}

\displaystyle=2d\log\big(1+\frac{T}{\lambda d}\big)\;.

Consequently,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\phi_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\leq\sqrt{2dT\log\Big(1+\frac{T}{\lambda d}\Big)}

∎

Lemma A.6

Given

(29)

where

Proof.

Recall the definitions of
\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}
and
\tilde{m}_{t,k}
in section
A.1
. Moreover, this section also presents the sequence

Lemma A.7
.

Given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, for any
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

|\phi(\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{t})|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}(1-\eta h\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{i}))^{K_{i}-1}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}\|\phi_{j}\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}\left(g_{1}(t)/\sqrt{\lambda}+R\sqrt{1+\log(3t^{2})}\right)

A.2

Proof.

A.6

\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}A_{i}^{K_{i}-1}(\hat{\mu}_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{j+1})+\hat{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle|\phi^{\top}(\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{t})|

(31)

We can notice that the previous term only depends on the difference between the mean posterior at time j and the one at time j+1, which can be upper bounded. Recall the different relations between
V_{j}
,
b_{j}
,
r_{j}
,
\phi_{j}
and
\hat{\Sigma}_{j}

V^{-1}_{j+1}=(V_{j}+\phi_{j}\phi_{j}^{\top})^{-1}=V^{-1}_{j}-\frac{V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}}{1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}}

The difference between the mean posterior at time
j+1
and the one at time
j
becomes:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{j+1}-\hat{\mu}_{j}

\displaystyle=V^{-1}_{j+1}b_{j+1}-V^{-1}_{j}b_{j}

\displaystyle=(V^{-1}_{j}-\frac{V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}}{1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}})(b_{j}+r_{j}\phi_{j})-V^{-1}_{j}b_{j}

\displaystyle=r_{j}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}-\frac{V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}}{1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}}(b_{j}+r_{j}\phi_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}}{1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}}\left\{-\phi_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{j}-r_{j}\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}+r_{j}(1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}^{\top})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}(r_{j}-\phi_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{j})}{1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}(\phi_{j}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi_{j}-\phi_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{j})}{1+\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}}

(33)

where in
(a)

with
\xi_{j}
is sampled from a R-Subgaussian distribution. Inequality
(b)
is due to
\phi_{j}^{\top}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{i}=\|\phi_{j}\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}^{2}>0
.

Subsequently, combining equations (
31
) and (
33

\displaystyle|\phi(\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}|\phi^{\top}A_{i}^{K_{i}-1}(\hat{\mu}_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{j+1})|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}\left|\phi^{\top}A_{i}^{K_{i}-1}V^{-1}_{j}\phi_{j}(\phi_{j}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi_{j}-\phi_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{j})\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}(1-\eta h\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{i}))^{K_{i}-1}\phi^{\top}V^{-1/2}_{j}V^{-1/2}_{j}\phi_{j}|(\phi_{j}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi_{j}-\phi_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{j})|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}(1-\eta h\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{i}))^{K_{i}-1}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}\|\phi_{j}\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}|(\phi_{j}^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\xi_{j}-\phi_{j}^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{j})|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t-1}(1-\eta h\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{i}))^{K_{i}-1}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}\|\phi_{j}\|_{V^{-1}_{j}}\left(g_{1}(t)/\sqrt{\lambda}+R\sqrt{1+\log(3t^{2})}\right)

In the inequality
(a)
we have used equation (
33
),
in
(b)
the relation
A_{i}^{K_{i}-1}=(\mathrm{I}-\eta h_{i}V_{i})^{K_{i}-1}\preceq(1-\eta h_{i}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{i}))^{K_{i}-1}\;\mathrm{I}_{d}

\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}=\sqrt{\phi^{\top}V^{-1}_{t}\phi}=\sqrt{\phi^{\top}V_{t}^{-1/2}}\sqrt{V_{t}^{-1/2}\phi}=\phi^{\top}V_{t}^{-1/2}
,
and finally

is due to
|\xi_{i}|<R\sqrt{1+\log 3t^{2}}

holds and

Lemma A.8
.

Given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}

A.7
, it gives us

. Moreover, for any

) and (

). The point (b) comes that

\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}=(1-\eta h_{t}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t}))^{2}<(1-\frac{3\eta h_{t}}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t}))

\eta h_{t}\leq 1/(4\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t}))
. Consequently, the geometric sum has a common ratio strictly lower than 1, then it is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{(1-\frac{3\eta h_{t}}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t}))}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{(1-\frac{3\eta h_{t}}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t}))}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\frac{3\eta h_{t}}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})}{1-\frac{3\eta h_{t}}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})-\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\frac{3\eta h_{t}}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})}{1/2\;\eta h_{t}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})-\eta^{2}h_{t}^{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{\eta h_{t}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})}\;,

(41)

where in the first inequality we have used that the ratio of the previous sum is positive. In the last inequality we have used that
\eta h_{t}\leq 1/(6\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t}))
in the denominator and we can remove the negative part of the numerator. Therefore, it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{t,k+1}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2k}\lVert\Gamma_{t,k}\rVert_{2}+6\eta\kappa_{t}h_{t}D_{t}^{k-1}\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\;,

where the last inequality comes from (
41
) and the definition of
\kappa_{t}=\lambda_{\text{max}}(V_{t})/\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})
. Finally, iterating over
t
yields to:

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{t,k+1}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2k}\prod_{j=1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{j}\rVert_{2}^{2(K_{j}-1)}\lVert\Gamma_{1,1}\rVert_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2k}\prod_{i=j+1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2(K_{i}-1)}\Big(6\eta\kappa_{j}h_{j}D_{j}^{K_{j}-1}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+6\eta\kappa_{t}h_{t}D_{t}^{k-1}\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2k}\prod_{i=j+1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2(K_{i}-1)}\Big(6\eta\kappa_{j}h_{j}D_{j}^{K_{j}-1}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\Big)+6\eta\kappa_{t}h_{t}D_{t}^{k-1}\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\;,

where in the last inequalities we have used that
W_{1,1}
is initialized such that
W_{1,1}=1/(11\eta\lambda)\mathrm{I}
and that
J_{1}V_{1}=h_{1}(2\mathrm{I}-\eta h_{1}\lambda\mathrm{I})^{-1}=1/(11\eta\lambda)\mathrm{I}
because
h_{1}=1/(6\eta\lambda)
.
Finally, we can conclude

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{t,K_{t}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2(K_{t}-1)}\prod_{i=j+1}^{t-1}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2(K_{i}-1)}\Big(6\eta\kappa_{j}h_{j}D_{j}^{K_{j}-1}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\Big)+6\eta\kappa_{t}h_{t}D_{t}^{K_{t}-1}\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\prod_{i=j+1}^{t}\lVert A_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2(K_{i}-1)}\Big(6\eta\kappa_{j}h_{j}D_{j}^{K_{j}-1}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}\prod_{i=j}^{t}D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}\Big(6\eta\kappa_{j}h_{j}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}\lVert\Lambda_{t,1}\rVert_{2}\Big)\;,

where in the last inequality we have used that
\lVert A_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq D_{t}
. Moreover, equation (
40
) gives us that
\lVert\Lambda_{j,1}\rVert_{2}\leq 1/(\eta\lambda)\sum_{r=1}^{j}\prod_{l=r}^{j-1}D_{l}^{K_{l}-1}
. Consequently, it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{t,K_{t}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{6\kappa_{j}h_{j}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}}{\lambda}\sum_{r=1}^{j}\prod_{l=r}^{j-1}D_{l}^{K_{l}-1}\prod_{i=j}^{t}D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{6\kappa_{j}h_{j}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}}{\lambda}\sum_{r=1}^{j}\prod_{i=r}^{t}D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}\;.

∎

Lemma A.15
.

For any
t\geq 2
, given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, if the number of gradient descent steps is
K_{t}\geq 1+4(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})\log\Big(2\kappa_{t}d^{2}T\log^{2}(3T^{3})\Big)/3
, therefore it holds that

|\phi^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}\right)|\leq\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{3}{\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}}+\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})/(11\eta)}\Bigg)\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\;.

Proof.

For any
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|\phi^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}\right)|\leq\lVert\phi^{\top}\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}\lVert\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}))\rVert_{2}\;,

(42)

where
\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{1/2}
is the unique symmetric square root of
\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}
. Firstly, given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, the term
\lVert\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})\rVert_{2}<\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})}
.

Then, we observe that

\displaystyle\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})}\lVert\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}^{1/2}\phi\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})\phi\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}\phi^{\top}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})\phi\Gamma_{t,K_{t}}\phi^{\top}}+\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})\phi J_{t}V^{-1}_{t}\phi^{\top}}\;,

(43)

where
J_{t}=h_{t}(2\mathrm{I}-\eta h_{t}V_{t})^{-1}V_{t}=(2V^{-1}_{t}/h_{t}-\eta\mathrm{I})^{-1}
and
\Gamma_{t,k}=\tilde{W}_{t,k}-J_{t}V^{-1}_{t}
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\phi J_{t}V^{-1}_{t}\phi^{\top}}

\displaystyle=\lVert(J_{t}V^{-1}_{t})^{1/2}\phi\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\lVert J_{t}^{1/2}V_{t}^{-1/2}\phi\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert J_{t}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\;,

where in inequality
(a)
we have used that
J_{t}
and
V^{-1}_{t}
commute.

Recall that
V_{t}
is a symmetric matrix, therefore we have
\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})\mathrm{I}\preceq V_{t}\preceq\lambda_{\text{max}}(V_{t})\mathrm{I}
. It follows that

\frac{2}{h_{t}\lambda_{\text{max}}(V_{t})}\mathrm{I}\preceq\frac{2}{h_{t}}V^{-1}_{t}\preceq\frac{2}{h_{t}\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})}\mathrm{I}.

Recall the definition of
h_{t}=\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})/\big(2\eta(\lambda_{\text{min}}(V_{t})^{2}+\lambda_{\text{max}}(V_{t})^{2})\big)
. Consequently, the previous relation becomes

\Big(\frac{4\eta(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})}{\kappa_{t}}-\eta\Big)\mathrm{I}\preceq\frac{2}{h_{t}}V^{-1}_{t}-\eta\mathrm{I}\preceq\Big(3\eta+8\eta\kappa_{t}^{2}\Big)\mathrm{I}\;.

(44)

The left hand term is obviously positive, therefore it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert J_{t}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert(\frac{2}{h_{t}}V^{-1}_{t}-\eta\mathrm{I})^{-1/2}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{t}}{\eta(4+8\kappa_{t}^{2}-\kappa_{t})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta(4+7\kappa_{t}^{2})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{11\eta}}\;.

Finally,

\displaystyle\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})\phi J_{t}V^{-1}_{t}\phi^{\top}}\leq\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})/(11\eta)}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\;.

Now, we focus on the first term of equation
43
. Lemma
A.14
gives us that

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{t,K_{t}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{6\kappa_{j}h_{j}\lVert V_{j}\rVert_{2}}{\lambda}\sum_{r=1}^{j}\prod_{i=r}^{t}D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{\kappa_{j}}{\eta\lambda}\sum_{r=1}^{j}\prod_{i=r}^{t}D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}\;,

where in the last inequality we have used that
h_{t}\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}=\kappa_{t}/\big(2\eta(1+2\kappa_{t})\big)\leq 1/(6\eta)
.
For any
j\in[2,t]
, let’s define
\epsilon_{j}=1/(2(\kappa_{j}d^{2}t^{2}\log^{2}(3t^{3})))
. Additionally, let’s fix
K_{i}
such that
D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}\leq\epsilon_{j}
(this condition will be explained later in the Lemma). Subsequently, we have

\displaystyle 4d\log(3t^{3})\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}/(\eta\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{t}\kappa_{j}\sum_{r=1}^{j}\prod_{i=r}^{t}D_{i}^{K_{i}-1}

\displaystyle\leq 4d\log(3t^{3})\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}/(\eta\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{t}\kappa_{j}\sum_{r=1}^{j}\epsilon_{j}^{t-r+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}}{t^{2}\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}\sum_{r=1}^{t}\sum_{j=r}^{t}\epsilon_{j}^{t-r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}}{t^{2}\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}\sum_{r=1}^{t}\sum_{j=r}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2d^{2}t^{2}\log^{2}(3t^{3})}\Big)^{t-r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}}{t\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}\sum_{r=1}^{t}\frac{t-r+1}{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2d^{2}t^{2}\log^{2}(3t^{3})}\Big)^{t-r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}}{t\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}\sum_{r=1}^{t}\Big(\frac{1}{2d^{2}t^{2}\log^{2}(3t^{3})}\Big)^{t-r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}}{\eta t\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}\sum_{u=0}^{t-1}\Big(\frac{1}{25}\Big)^{u}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}\;,

where in
(a)
we have used that
\epsilon_{j}\leq 1/(2(d^{2}t^{2}\log^{2}(3t^{3}))
. Inequality
(b)
is due to
t-r+1\leq t
. The inequality
(c)
is obtained because
1/(2d^{2}t^{2}\log^{2}3t^{3})\leq 1/(4\times\log^{2}(8))\leq 1/25
and

\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\lambda\mathrm{I}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\phi\phi^{\top}\rVert_{2}\leq\lambda+t-1\leq t
, because
\lambda\leq 1
.

Consequently, as
\lVert\phi\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert V_{t}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})\phi\Gamma_{t,K_{t}}\phi^{\top}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{3}{\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}}\|\phi\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\;.

(45)

Moreover, the previous inequalities hold if

, it follows that we need

∎

Appendix B
Concentration and anti-concentration

Lemma B.1
.

(Concentration lemma for
\tilde{\theta}_{t}
)

For any
t\in[T]
, given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, the following event is controlled

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq 1-\frac{1}{t^{2}}

Proof.

Firstly, if
t=1
, the condition is obvious because
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\geq 0
. For the rest of the proof, we assume that
t\geq 2
. Recall the definition of the event
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}
:

\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{var}}_{t}=\left\{\text{for any }a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t}),|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\hat{\mu}_{t}|\leq g_{2}(t)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}\right\}\;.

with
g_{2}(t)=10\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})/(\eta\lambda)}
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t})
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}(\tilde{\theta}_{t}-\hat{\mu}_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}})|+|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})|+|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}(\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}-\hat{\mu})|\;,

where
\tilde{\theta}_{t}=\tilde{\theta}_{t,K_{t}}
is a sample from the variational posterior distribution trained after
K_{t}
steps of Algorithm
2
.
\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,K_{t}}
are, respectively, the mean and covariance matrix of the variational posterior. Moreover,
\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}
is gaussian with mean
\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}
and covariance matrix
\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}
(see section
A.1
). If the number of gradient descent steps is
K^{(1)}_{t}\geq 1+4(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})\log(2T)/3
, then Lemma
A.12
shows that with probability at least
1-1/t^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{4d\log(t^{3})/\eta}\Big(1+1/\sqrt{\lambda}\Big)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{d\log(t^{3})/(\eta\lambda)}\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}},

where the last inequality is due to
\lambda\leq 1
.

Similarly, Lemma
A.15
shows that for any
t\geq 2
, given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, if
K^{2}_{t}\geq 1+4(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})\log\Big(2\kappa_{t}d^{2}T^{2}\log^{2}(3T^{3})\Big)/3
, therefore we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{3}{\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}}+\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})/(11\eta)}\Bigg)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

where in the last simplification we have used
\lambda\leq 1
.

Finally, Given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, let’s apply Lemma
A.8
with a number of gradient descent steps such
K^{(3)}_{t}\geq 1+2(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})\log\left(4R\sqrt{dT\log(3T^{3})}\right)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle|\phi(\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq 2/\lambda\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}.

Note that
K_{t}=1+2(1+2\kappa_{t}^{2})\log\Big(2R\kappa_{t}d^{2}T^{2}\log^{2}(3T^{3})\Big)\geq\max\{K^{(1)}_{t},K^{(2)}_{t},K^{(3)}_{t}\}

23

1-1/t^{2}
we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}-\hat{\mu}_{t}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}-\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}\right)|+|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mu}_{t,k}-\tilde{m}_{t,k}\right)|+|\phi(x_{t},a)^{\top}\left(\tilde{m}_{t,k}-\hat{\mu}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(4\sqrt{d\log(t^{3})/(\eta\lambda)}+\sqrt{\frac{3}{\eta\lambda d\log(3t^{3})}}+\sqrt{4d\log(3t^{3})/(11\eta)}+2/\lambda\Big)\|\phi(x_{t},a)\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

where the last inequality holds because
t\geq 2
,
\lambda\leq 1
and
\eta\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

(Anti-concentration lemma)

Given
\mathrm{E}^{\textrm{true}}_{t}
, if the number of gradient steps is
K_{t}=1+2(1+\kappa_{t}^{2})\log\Big(2R\kappa_{t}d^{2}T^{2}\log^{2}(3T^{3})\Big)

Therefore, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}>\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}\right)\leq p\;,

p=1/(2\sqrt{2\pi e})

Proof.

Firstly, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t,K_{t}}>\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t,K_{t}}-\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}}{\sqrt{\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t,K_{t}}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}+\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}}}>\frac{\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\theta^{\star}-\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}}{\sqrt{\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}+\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{W}_{t,K_{t}}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}}}\right)\;.

\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\mu}_{t}\sim\mathrm{N}\big(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{m}_{t},\;\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\tilde{W}_{t,k}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\big)\;\text{and}\quad\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t,K_{t}}\sim\mathrm{N}\big(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\mu}_{t,k},\;\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\big)\;.

\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{t}\sim\mathrm{N}\big(\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\tilde{m}_{t},\;\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}+\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\tilde{W}_{t}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\big)\;.

). First, in this lemma, we suppose that

A.2
is upper than

\displaystyle|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}(\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{t})|

\displaystyle|\phi_{t}^{*\top}(\theta^{\star}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\leq}}|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}(\theta^{\star}-\hat{\mu}_{t,K_{t}})|+|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}(\hat{\mu}_{t,K_{t}}-\tilde{m}_{t,K_{t}})|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\Bigg(R\sqrt{d\log(3t^{3})}+\sqrt{\lambda}+\frac{2}{\lambda}\Bigg)\|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

Regarding the denominator of
Y_{t}
, we need a lower bound for
\|B_{t,k}\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\|_{2}

A.10
gives us that

\|B_{t,K_{t}}\phi\|_{2}\geq 1/\sqrt{\eta}\Big(1-\sqrt{\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{2}C_{t}}\Big)\|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}

with

(46)

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\geq}}\frac{\|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{9t^{1/4}}\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\geq}}\frac{\|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{9}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{8\|\accentset{\ast}{\phi}_{t}\|_{V^{-1}_{t}}}{9\sqrt{\eta}}

with (a) is
46

Where (b) we use

Appendix C
Approximation of our algorithm and complexity

In this section, the objective is to approximate the inversion of the matrix
B_{t,k}
of Algorithm
2
. Indeed, Algorithm
2
, requires to compute the inversion of a
d\times d
matrix at each step
t
and
k
, which represents a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
. In the approximated version of Algorithm
2
, we consider both the sequence of square root covariance matrix
\{B_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
and the sequence of their approximations
\{C_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
such that: for any
t\in[T]
and
k\in[K_{t}]

C_{t,k}\approx B_{t,k}^{-1}\;.

Recall the recursive definition of
B_{t,k}
,

\displaystyle B_{t,k+1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathrm{I}-h_{t}A_{t,k})\right\}B_{t,k}+h_{t}(B_{t,k}^{\top})^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx\left\{\mathrm{I}-h_{t}A_{t,k})\right\}B_{t,k}+h_{t}C_{t,k}^{\top}\;,

(48)

where
A_{t,k}=B_{t,k}^{2}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}-\tilde{\mu}_{t,k})(\nabla U_{t}(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k})^{\top}
if the hessian free algorithm is used or
A_{t,k}=\nabla^{2}U(\tilde{\theta}_{t,k})
otherwise. Recall that
\tilde{\theta}_{t,k}\sim\mathrm{N}(\tilde{\mu}_{t,k},B_{t,k}B_{t,k}^{\top})
. Furthermore, we can now focus on the definition of the sequence
\{C_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
. Firstly, we recall that

\displaystyle B_{t,k+1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathrm{I}-h_{t}A_{t,k}\right\}B_{t,k}+h_{t}(B_{t,k}^{\top})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{I}+h_{t}((B_{t,k}^{\top})^{-1}(B_{t,k})^{-1}-A_{t,k})\}B_{t,k}.

Then, let’s use a first order Taylor expansion of the previous equation in
h_{t}
, we obtain the approximated inverse square root covariance matrix:

\displaystyle C_{t,k+1}=C_{t,k}\{(\mathrm{I}-h_{t}(C_{t,k}^{\top}C_{t,k}-A_{t,k})\}.

(49)

Note that the lower is
h_{t}
, the better is the approximation and in our case the step size
h_{t}
is decreasing with
t
. The approximated recursive definition of the square root covariance matrix defined in equation (
48
) and its approximated inverse defined in equation (
49
) are used to defined our the approximated version of
VITS
called
{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\texttt{VITS}-\texttt{II}}
and is presented in Algorithm
3
. Moreover, note that the updating step of Algorithm
3
uses only matrix multiplication and sampling from independent Gaussian distribution
\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
. Therefore the global complexity of the overall algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
.

Appendix D
Discussion on the difference between the algorithm of
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
and our algorithm
\mathrm{VITS}
.

The main difference between our setting and the one of
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
is the bandit modelisation. Indeed, given a context
x
and an action
a
, in our setting, the agent receives a reward
r\sim\mathrm{R}(\cdot|x,a)
. Consequently, a parametric model
\mathrm{R_{\theta}}
is used to approximate the reward distribution and it yields to a posterior distribution
\hat{\mathrm{p}}
. In the setting of
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
, the agent receives a reward
r\sim\mathrm{R}_{a}(\cdot|x)
. Then, it considers a set of parametric models
\{\mathrm{R_{\theta_{a}}}\}_{a=1}^{K}
and a set of posterior distributions:
\{\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{K}
. The setting we have used in this paper is richer as it consider the correlation between the arms distributions compared to
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
which consider that the arm distributions are independents. For example, if we consider the case of the Linear bandit. In this setting, the posterior distribution is Gaussian. With the modelisation of
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
, we have for any
a\in[K]
,
\hat{\mathrm{p}}_{a}:=\mathrm{N}(\mu_{a},\Sigma_{a})
, where
\mu_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma_{a}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d}
. However, with our modelisation,
\hat{\mathrm{p}}:=\mathrm{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
, where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
and
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times K}
. We can see that the covariance matrix
\Sigma
encodes the correlations between the different arms, which is not the case of
\{\Sigma_{a}\}_{a=1}^{K}
.
In addition, in our setting, we can consider any model for the mean of the reward distribution. For example we can choose
g(\theta,x,a)
as a Neural Networks. This kind of model is unusable in the formulation of
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
.

Moreover, the approximate families used in both papers are different. Indeed, we consider the family of non-degenerate Gaussian distributions, and
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
is focused on the family of mixture of mean-field Gaussian distribution. The mixture of Gaussian distribution is richer than the classic Gaussian distribution. However, the non mean-field hypothesis allow to keep the correlation between arms distributions.

Furthermore,
\mathtt{VTS}
from
(
Urteaga & Wiggins 2018
)
scales very poorly with the size of the problem. The variational parameters are very large:
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M}
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M\times d}
,
V\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times M\times d\times d}
where
K
is the number of arms,
M
is the number of mixtures and
d
the parameter dimension. In addition, the parameter updating step is also very costly in term of memory and speed. We have re-implemented an efficient version of their algorithm in JAX in order to scale as much as possible but many memory problems occur.

Finally, our algorithm comes with theoretical guarantees in the Linear Bandit case and outperforms empirically the others approximate TS methods.
\mathtt{VTS}
performs poorly in practice and has no theoretical guarantee, even in the Linear case.

Appendix H
Details about experiences in synthetic contextual bandits

with synthetic data

In this subsection, we provide more details about the toy example derive in this paper. Firstly, we consider a fixed pool of arms denoted as
P=[\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n}]
with
n=50
, where each arm
\tilde{x}_{i}
follows a normal distribution
\mathrm{N}(0_{d},\mathrm{I}_{d})
. Then, at each step
t\in[T]
, for every arm, we randomly sample a vector
\tilde{x}_{i}
from the pool
P
, and the contextual vector associated with this arm is defined as
x=\tilde{x}_{i}+\zeta\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},\mathrm{I}_{d})
.
The bandit environment is simulated using a random vector
\theta^{\star}
sampled from a normal distribution
\mathrm{N}(0_{d},\sigma^{\star}\mathrm{I}_{d})
. We opted for
\sigma^{\star}=1/d
to ensure that the variance of the scalar product
x^{\top}\theta^{\star}
remains independent of the dimension
d
. Indeed, both linear and quadratic settings, the reward only depends on the scalar product between the context and the true parameter. If we denote by
x[i]
and
\theta^{\star}[i]
the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate of the vector
x
and
\theta^{\star}
respectively, then the scalar product is defined by

x^{\top}\theta^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x[i]\theta^{\star}[i],

and its variance is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[x^{\top}\theta^{\star}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}[\sum_{i=1}^{d}x[i]\theta^{\star}[i]]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{V}[x[i]]\mathbb{V}[\theta^{\star}[i]]

\displaystyle=d\sigma^{\star}\;\mathbb{V}[x[i]].

In the previous equations we have used that all coordinates are independents identically distributed and centered. Therefore, taking
\sigma^{\star}=1/d
ensure that the variance of the scalar product remains independent of d. In the linear bandit setting, the reward depends linearly on the contextual vector
x
, more precisely,

r=x^{\top}\theta^{\star}+\alpha\epsilon\;,

where
\epsilon\sim\mathrm{N}(0_{d},\mathrm{I}_{d})
. However, to maintain problem complexity independent of
\zeta
, we have set the signal-to-noise ratio to a fixed value of 1. This signal-to-noise ratio is the ratio between
\mathbb{E}[(x^{\top}\theta^{\star})^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}[(\alpha\epsilon)^{2}]
.
Firstly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(x^{\top}\theta^{\star})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}[x^{\top}\theta^{\star}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}[x[i]]

\displaystyle=1+\zeta^{2}\;,

where in the last equation we have used that
x=\tilde{x}_{i}+\zeta\epsilon
and
\mathbb{V}[x[i]]=1+\zeta^{2}
. Moreover, the denominator of the signal-to-noise ratio is
\mathbb{E}[(\alpha\epsilon)^{2}]=\alpha^{2}
. Consequently, a signal-to-noise ratio equals to
1
implies that
\sqrt{1+\zeta^{2}}=\alpha
.

In the quadratic bandit setting, the reward depends quadratically on the contextual vector
x
, more precisely,

r=(x^{\top}\theta^{\star})^{2}+\alpha\epsilon\;,

where
\epsilon\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\mathrm{I})
. In this setting, the reward also depends only on the scalar product between
x
and
\theta^{\star}
, thus, we also choose
\sigma^{\star}=1/d
. We also ensure a signal-to-noise equal to 1, it implies a more sophisticated condition on the noise:
\alpha=(\zeta^{2}+1)\sqrt{3+6/d}
. More precisely, in the quadratic setting, the signal-to-noise ratio is defined as follow

\frac{\mathbb{E}[(x^{\top}\theta^{\star})^{4}]}{\mathbb{E}[(\alpha\epsilon)^{2}]}=1.

Firstly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x^{\top}\theta^{\star})^{4}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x[i]\theta^{\star}[i])^{4}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x[i]\theta^{\star}[i])^{4}+4\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\neq i}(x[i]\theta^{\star}[i])^{3}x[j]\theta^{\star}[j]+6\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j<i}(x[i]\theta^{\star}[i])^{2}(x[j]\theta^{\star}[j])^{2}

\displaystyle+12\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{k\neq i,k<j}(x[i]\theta^{\star}[i])^{2}x[j]\theta^{\star}[j]x[k]\theta^{\star}[k]+24\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j<i}\sum_{k<j}\sum_{l<k}x[i]\theta^{\star}[i]x[j]\theta^{\star}[j]x[k]\theta^{\star}[k]x[l]\theta^{\star}[l]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[x[i]^{4}]\mathbb{E}[\theta^{\star}[i]^{4}]+6\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j<i}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[x_{j}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[\theta^{\star}[i]^{2}]\mathbb{E}[\theta^{\star}[j]^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{9(\zeta^{2}+1)^{2}}{d}+6\binom{d}{2}\frac{(\zeta^{2}+1)^{2}}{d^{2}}

\displaystyle=(\zeta^{2}+1)^{2}(\frac{9}{d}+\frac{3(d-1)}{d})

\displaystyle=(\zeta^{2}+1)^{2}(\frac{6}{d}+3)

which gives that
\alpha=(\zeta^{2}+1)\sqrt{3+6/d}
