On the Correspondence Between Domination and the Spectrum of Jacobi Operators Thanks: The authors were in part supported by NSF grant DMS-1764154.
Kateryna Alkorn, Zhenghe Zhang

Abstract
. In this paper, we first develop a notion of dominated splitting for 𝕄 ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) {\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -sequences and show it is a stable property under ∥ ⋅ ∥ ∞ \|\cdot\|_{\infty} -perturbation. Then we show an energy parameter belongs to the spectrum of a Jacobi operator, possibly singular, if and only if the associated Jacobi cocycle does not admit dominated splitting. This generalizes the results obtained by the second author [ Z ] in the scenario of Schrödinger operators. Finally, we consider dynamically defined Jacobi operators whose base dynamics is only assumed to be topologically transitive. We show an energy parameter belongs to the spectrum of the operator defined by the base point with a dense orbit if and only if the dynamically defined Jacobi cocycle does not admit dominated splitting. This includes the original Johnson’s theorem obtained by R. Johnson [ J ] for Schödinger operators and the main theorem obtained by C. Marx [ Ma ] for Jacobi operators as special cases.



1.
Introduction and Statement of Main Results

In this paper we study the relationship between the spectrum of the Jacobi operator and the dynamics of its cocycle. We set
\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})=\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{C}})
for
p\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
or
p=\infty
and let
\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{R}})\subset\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})
be real-valued sequences. The Jacobi operator
J_{a,b}:\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
is given by

(1)

(J_{a,b}\psi)(n)=\overline{a_{n-1}}\psi(n-1)+a_{n}\psi(n+1)+b_{n}\psi(n),

where
\psi=(\psi_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
,
a=(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
, and
b=(b_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{R}})
. We say the Jacobi operator is singular if
a_{n}=0
for some
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Jacobi operators are bounded self-adjoint operators. Hence, the spectrum
\sigma(J_{a,b})
of the operator
J_{a,b}
which is defined as

\sigma(J_{a,b}):=\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}:J_{a,b}-E\textit{ does not have bounded inverse}\}

is a nonempty compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
. The resolvent,
\rho(J_{a,b})
, of the operator
J_{a,b}
is defined as:

\rho(J_{a,b})={\mathbb{C}}-\sigma(J_{a,b})

Another type of Jacobi operators we will study are dynamically defined operators which are given as follows. Let
\Omega
be a compact metric space and
T
be a homeomorphism on
\Omega
. Let
a\in C(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}})
and
b\in C(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
, i.e.
a
and
b
are complex and real valued continuous functions on
\Omega
, respectively. Then for each
\omega\in\Omega
, we may define a Jacobi operator as

(2)

(J_{\omega}\psi)(n)=\overline{a(T^{n-1}\omega)}\psi(n-1)+a(T^{n}\omega)\psi(n+1)+b(T^{n}\omega)\psi(n).

In this case, we let
\sigma(J_{\omega})
and
\rho(J_{\omega})
denote its spectrum and resolvent set, respectively.

Jacobi operators are natural extension of one-dimensional discrete Schrödinger operators, the definition of which is to set
a_{n}=1
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
in (
1
). Just like Schrödinger operators, Jacobi operators arise naturally in various ways in mathematics and physics. For instance, they play a fundamental role in the study of completely integrable nonlinear lattices, in particular the Toda lattice and its modified counterpart, the Kac-van Moerbeke lattice. In the study of inverse spectral theory, one has to go to Jacobi operators even if one starts with Schrödinger operators. We refer the readers to
[
T
]
for more information regarding Jacobi operators.

In many ways, the study of spectral analysis of Schrödinger or Jacobi operators may be reduced to the study of dynamics of the associated cocycles. One of the basic relations between spectral analysis of operators and dynamics of cocycles is the Johnson’s type of theorems where one can identify the spectrum by these energies whose cocycles admit certain dynamics structure. For Schrödinger operators, this dynamics structure is the so-called uniform hyperbolicity. In the scenario of Jacobi operators, it turns out that the dynamics structure is the so-called dominated splitting which generalizes uniform hyperbolicity. These type of correspondences in particular play a key role in the analysis of the so-called Cantor spectrum phenomenon. For works related to these topics, we refer the readers to
[
ABD1
,
ABD2
,
WZ
]
in the Schrödinger case and to
[
FOZ
,
AJM
,
JM
,
Ma
]
in the Jacobi case and the references therein. Cantor spectrum phenomenon, on the other hand, has a deep physcis background. It is closely related to the so-called quantum hall effect, see e.g.
[
H
,
TKNN
]
. The present paper concerns Johnson type of theorem for Jacobi operators. In the next two subsections, we first review the Johnson’s theorem for Schrödinger operators. Then we discuss the existing Johnson’s type of results for Jacobi operators due to C. Marx
[
Ma
]
. Finally, we state the main results of this paper and discuss the strategy of their proofs.

1.1.
Review of Johnson’s theorem for Schrödinger operators

Let
b\in C(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
. Consider the Schrödinger operators
H_{\omega}
with potentials given by
b_{n}=b(T^{n}\omega)
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Again, to define such operators, we just need to set
a(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in\Omega
in (
2
). Let
A^{E}:\Omega\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
be the Schrödinger cocycle map at the energy
E
. For
\omega\in\Omega
, we define its orbit under
T
to be
\mathrm{Orb}(\omega):=\{T^{n}\omega:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. Then the following theorem goes back to
[
J
, Theorem 3.1]
:

Theorem 1
.

Let
(T,\Omega)
be topologically transitive. In other words, there is a
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
such that
\overline{\mathrm{Orb}(\omega_{0})}=\Omega
. Then
(T,A^{E})
is uniformly hyperbolic if and only if
E\in\rho(H_{\omega_{0}})
.

We wish to point out that Johnson’s theorem does not necessarily need to involve a base dynamics
(T,\Omega)
. Correspondingly, the notion of uniform hyperbolicity may be defined for a sequence of
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
-matrices. Let
H_{b}
be the Schrödinger operator with the potential
b\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
and let
A^{E}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
be the cocycle map defined on
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then the following theorem is from
[
Z
, Theorem 3]
:

Theorem 2
.

A^{E}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is uniformly hyperbolic if and only if
E\in\rho(H_{b})
.

Assume
(T,A^{E})
is a uniformly hyperbolic cocycle defined over a base dynamics
(T,\Omega)
. Then for each
\omega\in\Omega

A^{E}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, where
A^{E}(n)=A^{E}(T^{n}\omega)
, is a uniformly hyperbolic
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
-sequence. Thus the following corollary is a direct consequence of Theorem
2
:

Corollary 1
.

If
(T,A^{E})
is uniformly hyperbolic, then
E\in\bigcap_{\omega\in\Omega}\rho(H_{\omega})
.

Note that Corollary
1
does not have any restrictions on the base dynamics. It is also clear that Theorem
2
implies:

If
E\in\bigcap_{\omega\in\Omega}\rho(H_{\omega})
, then
A^{E}(T^{(\cdot)}\omega):{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is uniformly hyperbolic for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

In general, for a continuous cocycle map
A:\Omega\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, one may not be able to lift the uniform hyperbolicity from sequences (i.e. uniform hyperbolicity of
A(T^{(\cdot)}\omega):{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
for all
\omega\in\Omega
) to uniform hyperbolicity of cocycle
(T,A)
. This is because one may lose the uniformity of certain constants. However, when the base dynamics is topologically transitive, one can indeed pass the uniform hyperbolic from sequences to the cocycle. In fact, if
\overline{\mathrm{Orb}(\omega_{0})}=\Omega
, then the uniform hyperbolicity of
A(T^{(\cdot)}\omega):{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
implies the uniform hyperbolicity of
(T,A)
. This is due the fact that uniform hyperbolicity is equivalent to a uniform exponential growth condition which can easily be passed uniformly from a dense orbit to the whole space.

Alternatively, one may use the following relatively simple fact, see e.g.
[
Z
, Theorem 6]
, to show that if
E\in\rho(H_{\omega_{0}})
, then
(T,A^{E})
is uniformly hyperbolic.

Proposition 1
.

If
\overline{\mathrm{Orb}(\omega_{0})}=\Omega
, then
\sigma(H_{\omega})\subset\sigma(H_{\omega_{0}})
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

In particular, if
E\in\rho(H_{\omega_{0}})
, then
d(E,\sigma(H_{\omega}))\geq d(E,\sigma(H_{\omega_{0}}))>0
for all
\omega\in\Omega
where
d(E,\sigma(H_{\omega}))
denotes the distance between
E
and
\sigma(H_{\omega})
. Then using the strategy of
[
Z
, Section 3.3.2]
, one can show that the constants appear in the definition of uniform hyperbolicity of
A(T^{(\cdot)}\omega):{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

depend only on
\|(H_{\omega}-E)^{-1}\|^{-1}=d(E,\sigma(H_{\omega}))
. In particular, those constants can be made uniform for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

1.2.
The case with Jacobi operators and strategy of proofs

For Jacobi operators, the notion of uniform hyperbolicity is not sufficient as the associated cocycle map takes values in
{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, which denotes the set of all
2\times 2
matrices. Not only they might be not in
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, they might even be singular, i.e. have zero determinant. Motivated by the study of the extended Harper model
[
JM
,
AJM
]
, C. Marx
[
Ma
]
first noted that in such scenario the right choice should be cocycles admitting dominated splitting, which is a natural generalization of uniformly hyperbolic cocycles to those allowing
{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-values. Domination, on the other hand, is an intensively studied notion in dynamical systems. It was introduced by and plays a key role in the works of Mañé
[
M
]
and Liao
[
L
]
on Smale’ s stability conjecture. The term
dominated splitting
was introduced by Mañé in
[
M
]
. We refer the readrs to
[
BV
,
BDP
,
P
]
and the referenes therein regarding recent works in smooth dynamical systems involving domination. For recent works related to domination for cocyles defined over base dynamics, we refer the readers to
[
AJS
,
BG
,
BM
]
and the references therein.

Regarding the correspondence between domination and spectrum of Jacobi operators, Marx
[
Ma
]
showed the following version of Johnson’s theorem. We again let
T:\Omega\to\Omega
be homeomorphism on a compact metric space
\Omega
. We say
T
is minimal if
\overline{\mathrm{Orb}(\omega)}=\Omega
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. We say
T
is uniquely ergodic if
T
has only one ergodic probablity measure. We say
T
is strictly ergodic if
T
is uniquely ergodic and minimal. By Proposition
2
,
\rho(J_{\omega})
is independent of
\omega\in\Omega
if
T
is minimal. Let
B^{E}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be the Jacobi cocycle map that is associated with the energy
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
T:\Omega\to\Omega
be strictly ergodic. Assume
B^{E}\in C^{0}(\Omega,{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
and
\int_{\Omega}\big|\log|\det(B^{E}(\omega))|\big|d\mu<\infty
for all
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Assume that the map
\omega\mapsto J_{\omega}
is continuous with respect to the operator norm on
J_{\omega}
. Then
E\in\rho(J_{\omega})
if and only if
(T,B^{E})
admits dominated splitting.

The original version of Marx’s theorem was stated for quasiperiodic Jacobi cocycles whose base dynamics are minimal translations on the
d
-dimensional torus. Such base dynamics satisfy all the conditions stated in Theorem
3
. But as the author indicated in
[
Ma
, Remark 1.2.ii.]
, the proof works for a more general dynamics as stated in Theorem
3
. The author also mentioned in
[
Ma
, Remark 1.2.iii.]
that he believes the results should hold true for merely minimal base dynamics. We wish to point out that there are Johnson theorems for CMV matrices. Regarding Johnson’s theorem for the standard CMV matrices, which may be considered as the unitary analog of Schrödinger operators, we refer the readers to
[
DFLY
, Theorem 1.2]
. Regarding Johnson’s theorem for generalized CMV matrices, which is the unitary analog of Jacobi operators, we refer the readers to
[
FOZ
, Theorem 6.1]
. It in particular plays a key role in studying the spectral properties of the unitary Almost Mathieu operator in
[
FOZ
]
which arises natually in modeling a one-dimensional quantum walk whose coins are distributed quasi-periodically.

However, as we explained in Section
1.1
and similar to the case of Schrödinger operators, there should be a Johnson’s theorem for Jacobi operators defined by any sequences
a\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
b\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{R}})
. Using such a theorem, one can already deduce some partial Johnson type of results for any base dynamics. Moreover, once one has the sequence version, it should not be far away to get a full version of Johnson’s theorem for Jacobi operators like the Schrödinger case. In other words, topological transitivity should be sufficient. In particular, Propostion
1
can be easily seen to hold true for Jacobi operators as well (see e.g. Proposition
2
of Section
5
). Hence, one should be able to control all the necessary constants associated with conditions of Definition
1
to make them uniform.

These are the main goals of this paper: to establish thorough versions of the Johnson’s theorem for Jacobi operators, both for sequence-defined and dynamically defined operators.

In the case of
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
matrices, the definition of uniform hyperbolicity for cocycles defined over base dynamics and for
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
-sequences are almost identical, see
[
Z
]
. However, though the definition of dominated splitting for
{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-cocycles over base dynamics exists (see. e.g. Definition
2
of Section
5
), it’s not immediately clear what the one for
{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences should be. A good such definition should at least satisfy the following conditions:

(a)

It generalizes the definition of uniform hyperbolicity for
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
-sequences.

(b)

If a dynamically defined cocycle
(T,B)
admits dominated splitting, then

B(T^{(\cdot)}\omega):{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

should be a sequence that admits dominated splitting for each
\omega\in\Omega
.

(c)

It should be stable under
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-perturbation.

(d)

It should be the right definition for one to show a Johnson’s theorem for the Jacobi operator
J_{a,b}
with any choice of
a\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
b\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{R}})
.

Thus, our first task is to find such a definition. Throughout the paper, we consider cocycle maps
B
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
or on a compact metric space
\Omega
to be in
\ell^{\infty}
. We also let
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the superemum norm in various scenarios. In particular, we may let
M>0
to be a universal upbound for all the cocycle maps
B
, that is
\|B\|_{\infty}<M
. We define

(3)

B_{n}(j)=\begin{cases}B(j+n-1)\cdots B(j),&n\geq 1,\\
I_{2},&n=0,\end{cases}

where
I_{2}
is the identity matrix. We also define

B_{-n}(j)=[B_{n}(j-n)]^{-1}=B(j-n)^{-1}\cdots B(j-1)^{-1},\ n\geq 1,

if all matrices involved are invertible. It turns out that the following definition of domination for
{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences serves our purposes perfectly:

Definition 1
.

We say that
B\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
admits
dominated splitting

({\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}})
if for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there are one-dimensional spaces
E^{u}(j)
and
E^{s}(j)
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with the following properties.

(1)

E^{u},E^{s}
are
B
–invariant in the sense that for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, it holds that

B(j)[E^{u}(j)]\subseteq E^{u}(j+1)\mbox{ and }B(j)[E^{s}(j)]\subseteq E^{s}(j+1).

(2)

There exist
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
\lambda>1
such that

\|B_{N}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|>\lambda\|B_{N}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|

for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all unit vectors
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)
and
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
.

(3)

\textit{There exists }\delta>0
such that
d\big(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j)\big)>\delta
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(4)

Let
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
be from condition (2). Then it holds
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B_{N}(j)\|>0
.

Clearly, condition (2) implies that
E^{s}(j)\neq E^{u}(j)
, hence
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}=E^{s}(j)\oplus E^{u}(j)
, for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In condition (3),
d(W,V)
denotes a distance between two one-dimensional spaces
W
and
V
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. For its definition, see equation (
11
) at the beginning of Section
2
. Here we say the space
E^{u}
dominates the space
E^{s}
. Throughout this paper, without loss of generality, we set
\lambda=2
in condition (2) in Definition
1
. From now on,
B\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
means
B
admits dominated splitting.

Remark 1
.

Conditions (1) and (2) of Definition
1
are standard conditions when one defines
\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-valued cocycles defined over a base dynamics that admit dominated splitting, see again Definition
2
of Section
5
. We also notice that in Definition
2
, condition (3) is automatically satisfied. However, when it comes to
\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
sequences, neither conditions (1) and (2) imply conditions (3) and (4), nor conditions (3) and (4) imply each other under conditions (1) and (2). But conditions (3) and (4) are necessary to guarantee the stability of the domination. Indeed, we have two examples where the first example satisfies conditions (1)-(3) while condition (4) fails. In the second example, we have conditions (1), (2), and (4) while condition (3) fails. Moreover, our examples are actually
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-valued.

Example one
. We defined
B
as follows:

B(j)=\begin{pmatrix}2^{-|j|}&0\\
\ 0&2^{-|j|-1}\end{pmatrix}\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

It is clear that for this
B
we have
E^{s}(j)=\mathrm{span}\{\binom{0}{1}\}
and
E^{u}(j)=\mathrm{span}\{\binom{1}{0}\}
where
E^{u}
dominates
E^{s}
at step
1
. By definition of distance between complex lines at the begining of Section
2
, specifically, equations (
11
) and (
9
), we can easily see
d(E^{s}(j),E^{u}(j))=2
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Evidently, condition (4) fails since it is clear that

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B_{n}(j)\|=0\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

Example two
. We define
B
as follows.

\Lambda(j)=\begin{pmatrix}2^{2-|j|}&0\\
\ 0&2^{-|j|}\end{pmatrix},\ D(j)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&2^{-|j|}\end{pmatrix},\mbox{ and }

B(j):=D(j+1)\Lambda(j)D(j)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2^{2-|j|}&-3\\
0&2^{-|j+1|}\end{pmatrix}.

It’s clear from the construction that the first and second column vectors of
D(j)
generates
E^{u}(j)
and
E^{s}(j)
of
B(j)
, respectively. It’s simple calculation to see
E^{u}(j)
dominates
E^{s}(j)
at step 1 with the correponding
\lambda>2
. Moreover, it is clear that
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B(j)\|>3
which is nothing other condition (4) of Definition
1
. However, by (
11
) and (
9
), it is clear that

d(E^{s}(j),E^{u}(j))<2^{-|j|}\to 0\mbox{ as }j\to\pm\infty.

For the examples above, the domination can easily be perturbed away by an arbitrarily small
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-perturbation.

Finally, we notice that under conditions (1)-(3), condition (4) is equivalent to

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B_{n}(j)\|>0\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

Indeed,
1
clearly implies condition (4). On the other hand, conditions (1)-(3) implies that if we define
D:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
such that the first and second column vectors are unit vectors in
E^{u}(j)
and
E^{s}(j)
, respectively, then
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\det(D(j))|>0
,
\|D\|_{\infty}\leq 2
, and

D(j+1)^{-1}B(j)D(j):=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{+}_{j}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-}_{j}\end{pmatrix}

for some
\lambda^{+}_{j}
and
\lambda^{-}_{j}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
. It clearly implies

(4)

D(j+N)^{-1}B_{n}(j)D(j):=\begin{pmatrix}\prod^{j+N-1}_{k=j}\lambda^{+}_{k}&0\\
0&\prod^{j+N-1}_{k=j}\lambda^{-}_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

By condition (2), we must have for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
:

\bigg|\prod^{j+N-1}_{k=j}\lambda^{+}_{k}\bigg|>2\bigg|\prod^{j+N-1}_{k=j}\lambda^{-}_{k}\bigg|.

By the properties of
D
we mentioned above and by condition (4), we must have

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|D(j+N)^{-1}B_{N}(j)D(j)\|>0.

Combine the two estimates above, we obtain

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigg|\prod^{j+N-1}_{k=j}\lambda^{+}_{k}\bigg|>0

which in turn implies
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\lambda^{+}_{j}|>0
since
\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\lambda^{+}_{j}|<C
for some
C>0
. Hence

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigg|\prod^{j+n-1}_{k=j}\lambda^{+}_{k}\bigg|>0\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1.

By (
4
) and properties of
D
, we then obtain
1
. It turns out that often times in the remaining part of the paper, to obtain condition (4) of Definition
1
, we instead prove the stronger condition
1
above.

(T,B)\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
for

.

Consider the family of dynamically defined Jacobi operators

,

, defined by (

It turns out that the key step to show Theorem

{\mathbb{D}}_{r}=\{z:|z|<r\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
. For
A\in{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, we let
A\cdot
denote certain projectivized action on

Theorem 6
.

Let
A:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|A(j)\|>0
. If there exist

Domination for

-Sequences and Its Stability

Let
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
be the one-dimensional complex projective space, or the Rieman sphere. We mainly use the following projection maps from
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
:

(7)

\pi:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\mbox{ where }\pi\binom{z_{1}}{z_{2}}=\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}.

Through this projection, each one-dimensional space in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
can be identified with a point in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Hence, we may view a point
z\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
as an one-dimension space
\mathrm{span}\{\binom{1}{z}\}
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Note
\infty
is considered to be
\mathrm{span}\{\binom{0}{1}\}
. For instance, by
\vec{v}\in z
we mean
\vec{v}
is a vector in the one-dimensional space
z
. In particular, we let
\vec{z}
denotes a unit vector in
z
. We shall mainly use the following metric on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
:

(8)

d(z,z^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\frac{2|z-z^{\prime}|}{\sqrt{(1+|z|^{2})(1+|z^{\prime}|^{2})}},&z,\ z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}};\\
\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}},&z^{\prime}=\infty.\end{cases}

It’s a standard fact the metric above on the Rieman sphere is induced by the stereographic projection

P:\mathbb{S}^{3}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1\}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

so that
d(z,z^{\prime})=\|P^{-1}(z)-P^{-1}(z^{\prime})\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
for
z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, where

P(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\begin{cases}\infty&\mbox{if }(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(0,0,1);\\
\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}i}{1-x_{3}},&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note
d(z,z^{\prime})\leq 2|z-z^{\prime}|
for all
z
and
z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
\vec{v}
and
\vec{v}^{\prime}
be two nonzero vectors in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. We let
(\vec{v},\vec{v}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
denotes the matrix whose column vectors are
\vec{v}
and
\vec{v}^{\prime}
. Then a direct computation shows that

(9)

d(\pi(\vec{v}),\pi(\vec{v}^{\prime}))=\frac{2|\det(\vec{v},\vec{v}^{\prime})|}{\|\vec{v}\|\cdot\|\vec{v}^{\prime}\|}.

In particular, if
\vec{v}
and
\vec{v}^{\prime}
are two unit vectors, then

(10)

d(\pi(\vec{v}),\pi(\vec{v}^{\prime}))=2|\det(\vec{v},\vec{v}^{\prime})|.

Since one dimensional space can be identified by the points in
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, abusing the notation slightly, for two one-dimensional subspaces
V
and
W
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, we define

(11)

d(V,W):=d(\pi(\vec{v}),\pi(\vec{w}))

where
\vec{v}\in V
and
\vec{w}\in W
are nonzero vectors.

Throughout this paper, we make the following assumptions:
C,\ c
will be universal constants, where
C
is large and
c
is small.

Let
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be a nonzero matrix such that
\|A\|<M
. Under the projection
\pi
as described in (
7
), there is an induced projectivized map of
A
acting on projective space
({\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1})\setminus\{\alpha\}
, where
\alpha
is the eigenspace of the
0
eigenvalue of
A
, if such exists. We denote the induced map by
A\cdot z
. Then a direct computation shows that

(12)

A\cdot z:({\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1})\setminus\{\alpha\}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1},\ A\cdot z=\frac{c+dz}{a+bz}.

Recall
\vec{z}
denotes a unit vector in the one-dimensional space
z\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. By (
9
) and (
10
), we have

\displaystyle d(A\cdot z,A\cdot z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d(A\cdot\pi(\vec{z}),A\cdot\pi(\vec{z}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=d(\pi(A\vec{z}),\pi(A\vec{z}^{\prime}))

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{2|\det(A\vec{z},A\vec{z}^{\prime})|}{\|A\vec{z}\|\cdot\|A\vec{z}^{\prime}\|}

\displaystyle=\frac{2|\det(A)|}{\|A\vec{z}\|\cdot\|A\vec{z}^{\prime}\|}|\det(\vec{z},\vec{z}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\frac{2|\det(A)|}{\|A\vec{z}\|\cdot\|A\vec{z}^{\prime}\|}|d(z,z^{\prime})|

If
|\det(A)|>\delta>0
, then it holds that

\inf_{\|\vec{v}\|=1}\|A\vec{v}\|=\frac{|\det(A)|}{\|A\|}>\frac{\delta}{M}

which in turn implies that (change

to

if necessary)

(14)

\frac{\delta}{M^{2}}d(z,z^{\prime})\leq d(A\cdot z,A\cdot z^{\prime})\leq\frac{M^{4}}{\delta^{2}}d(z,z^{\prime})\mbox{ for all }z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}.

Sometimes, we may need to use an another projection

\bar{\pi}:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\mbox{ where }\bar{\pi}\binom{z_{1}}{z_{2}}=\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}}.

Then the projectivized map of
A
under
\bar{\pi}
, denoted by

, becomes the well-known Möbius transformation:

(15)

\bar{A}(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

All the computation above still hold true for

. In fact,

.

2.1.
Invariant cone field implies domination: proof of Theorem
6

In this section, we will prove Theorem
5
. We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}\to{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
be a holomorphic function for some
0<\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
. Then there exists a
0<\rho=\rho({\alpha},{\alpha}^{\prime})<1
such that it holds for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}
that

\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|<\rho\left|\frac{z_{2}-z_{1}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{z_{1}}z_{2}}\right|.

Proof.

For simplicity, we set
{\mathbb{D}}={\mathbb{D}}_{1}
. Define
g:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}
to be

(16)

g(z):=\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}f(\alpha z)

which is a holomorphic function on the unit disc. By Schwarz–Pick theorem (see
[
D
]
), for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

\left|\frac{g(z_{2})-g(z_{1})}{1-\overline{g(z_{1})}g(z_{2})}\right|\leq\left|\frac{z_{2}-z_{1}}{1-\overline{z_{1}}z_{2}}\right|.

Fom (
16
), it holds for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}
that

f(z)=\alpha^{\prime}g\left(\frac{z}{\alpha}\right).

Hence, for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\alpha^{\prime}g\left(\frac{z_{1}}{\alpha}\right)-\alpha^{\prime}g\left(\frac{z_{2}}{\alpha}\right)}{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{\alpha^{\prime}g\left(\frac{z_{1}}{\alpha}\right)}\alpha^{\prime}g\left(\frac{z_{2}}{\alpha}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}\left|\frac{g\left(\frac{z_{1}}{\alpha}\right)-g\left(\frac{z_{2}}{\alpha}\right)}{1-\overline{g\left(\frac{z_{1}}{\alpha}\right)}g\left(\frac{z_{2}}{\alpha}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}\left|\frac{\frac{z_{2}}{\alpha}-\frac{z_{1}}{\alpha}}{1-\overline{\frac{z_{1}}{\alpha}}\frac{z_{2}}{\alpha}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\left|\frac{z_{2}-z_{1}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{z_{1}}z_{2}}\right|.

We can rewrite

\displaystyle\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{\prime 2}}\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{2}-(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}})^{2}\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{\prime 2}}\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})}\right|\left|\frac{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})}{\alpha^{2}-(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}})^{2}\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})}\right|

Hence, combining the two estimates above, we obtain for all
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
in
{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}
that

(17)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|\cdot\left|\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\left|\frac{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|\cdot\left|\frac{z_{2}-z_{1}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{z_{1}}z_{2}}\right|.

All that is left to show is that

(18)

\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\left|\frac{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|<\rho<1\mbox{ for all }z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}.

Since
|\overline{f(z_{1})}f(z_{2})|<\alpha^{\prime 2}
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}
, it is clear that the estimate above can be reduced to find an upbound of the following function

h:{\mathbb{D}}_{a}\to{\mathbb{R}}\mbox{ where }\ h(z)=\left|\frac{a-z}{b-z}\right|\mbox{ and }0<a<b.

Set
z=te^{i{\theta}}
for some
0\leq t<a
. It is then straightforward to see that

h(te^{i{\theta}})=\left(\frac{a^{2}+t^{2}-2at\cos{\theta}}{b^{2}+t^{2}-2bt\cos{\theta}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and
\max_{{\theta}\in[0,2\pi)}h(te^{i{\theta}})=h(te^{i\pi})=\frac{a+t}{b+t}
which is monotone increasing in
t\in[0,\infty)
. Hence we obtain

\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{a}}|h(z)|\leq\frac{2a}{a+b}.

Thus, we have for the left hand side of (
18
) that

\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\left|\frac{\alpha^{\prime 2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}{\alpha^{2}-\overline{f(z_{1})}{f(z_{2})}}\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\frac{2\alpha^{\prime 2}}{\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{\prime 2}}=\frac{2\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}}{1+(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}})^{2}}<1\mbox{ for all }z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}.

In other words, we can set
\rho=\rho({\alpha},{\alpha}^{\prime})=\frac{2\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}}{1+(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}})^{2}}<1
.

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

Replacing
{\alpha}
by a smaller number if necessary, we may assume that for all

By Lemma

, there exists a

Lemma 2
.

Let

. Define

. Then,
B\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
if and only if there exists

, such that

Proof.

We start by showing the
only if

. Fix
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that it satisfies condition (2) in definition
1
. For simplicity, we define

as

A^{(m)}(j)=B^{(m,N)}(j)=B_{N}(jN+m).

By condition (4) of Definition
1
, it is clear that
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|A^{(m)}(j)\|>0
for all

For every

Now we show the

for all

\vec{s}^{(m)}(j)

\vec{u}^{(m)}(j)

and a

so that for all

and all

(29)

\displaystyle\|E^{s}_{m}(j)-E^{u}_{m}(j)\|_{{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}}>\delta

(30)

\displaystyle\|A^{(m)}_{K}(j)\vec{u}^{(m)}(j)\|>2\|A^{(m)}_{K}(j)\vec{s}^{(m)}(j)\|

and

for

, we have

which is always a one-dimesional subspace of

. We define

That is, if

One readily checks that
E^{u,m}(j)
and
E^{s,m}(j)
are
B
-invariant for each

. Moreover,

for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We now have
N

. We claim that the

choices must be the same. To this end, we first note that it holds that for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
that

E^{u,m}(kN+m)=E^{u}_{m}(k)\mbox{ and }E^{s,m}(kN+m)=E^{s}_{m}(k).

We define
\vec{s}_{m}(j)\in E^{s,m}(j)
and
\vec{u}_{m}(j)\in E^{u,m}(j)

for some

We assume that

\vec{u}_{m}(j)\in E^{u}_{m}(k)

\vec{s}_{m}(j)\in E^{s}_{m}(k)

E^{u}_{m}

\displaystyle\|B_{n_{2}}(j)\vec{s}_{\ell}(j)\|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\|B_{n_{2}}(j)\vec{u}_{\ell}(j)\|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\|DB_{n_{1}}(j)\vec{u}_{\ell}(j)\|

\|B_{\ell-m}(j+n_{1})\vec{u}^{(m)}(k+p)\|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\lambda}

\|B_{KN}((k+p)N+m)\|=\|A^{(m)}_{K}(k+p)\|<\frac{C_{3}}{\lambda}

\vec{s}_{\ell}(j)=e^{it}\vec{s}_{m}(j)\mbox{ for some }t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Now, we just need to define

Lemma 3
.

Let
\Lambda=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda^{+}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be such that

|\lambda^{+}|>\gamma>0
and
\left|\lambda^{+}\right|>2|\lambda^{-}|
.
Fix any
r>0
. Then there exist
c=c(\gamma,r)>0
and
C=C(\gamma,r)>0
so that if
\widetilde{\Lambda}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
satisfies
\|\widetilde{\Lambda}-\Lambda\|\leq c
, then

(34)

\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{r}}|\Lambda\cdot z-\widetilde{\Lambda}\cdot z|<C\|\widetilde{\Lambda}-\Lambda\|.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\Lambda}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and let
\delta=\|\widetilde{\Lambda}-\Lambda\|
. Then,
|\lambda^{+}-a|\leq\delta
,
|\lambda^{-}-d|\leq\delta
,
|b|\leq\delta
and
|c|\leq\delta
which in turn imply that

\displaystyle|c+dz-\lambda^{-}z|

\displaystyle\leq|c|+|z||d-\lambda^{-}|\leq\delta(1+|z|)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle|a+bz-\lambda^{+}|

\displaystyle\leq|a-\lambda^{+}|+|b||z|\leq\delta(1+|z|).

In particular, we have

|a+bz|\geq\lambda^{+}-\delta(1+|z|)\geq\gamma-\delta(1+r)\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}

provided
\delta<\frac{\gamma}{2(1+r)}
. Thus, for such
\delta
, we have for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{r}
that

\displaystyle|\Lambda\cdot z-\widetilde{\Lambda}\cdot z|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\lambda^{-}z}{\lambda^{+}}-\frac{c+dz}{a+bz}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\lambda^{-}z}{\lambda^{+}}-\frac{\lambda^{-}z}{a+bz}\right|+\left|\frac{\lambda^{-}z}{a+bz}-\frac{c+dz}{a+bz}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\lambda^{-}z}{\lambda^{+}(a+bz)}\right||a+bz-\lambda^{+}|+\left|\frac{1}{a+bz}\right||\lambda^{-}z-(c+dz)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|a+bz|}\delta(1+|z|)\bigg(\left|\frac{\lambda^{-}_{j}}{\lambda^{+}_{j}}z\right|+1\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}(1+r)(2+r)\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}(1+r)(2+r)\|\Lambda-\widetilde{\Lambda}\|.

In other words, (
34
) holds true with
c=\frac{\gamma}{2(1+r)}
and
C=\frac{1}{\gamma}(1+r)(2+r)
.
∎

E^{s}

\Lambda:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is diagonal, i.e.

\Lambda(j)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{j}^{+}&0\\
\ 0&\lambda_{j}^{-}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, since
E^{u}
dominates
E^{s}
at step
1
, we must have

\left|\lambda_{j}^{+}\right|>2\left|\lambda_{j}^{-}\right|\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

By condition (4) of Definition
1
,

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j))>\delta>0

which implies

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\det D(j)|=d(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j))>\delta>0.

Thus, there exist
c,C
, depending on
\delta
only, such that

(36)

c<\|D(j)^{\pm}\|<C\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}

By condition (3) of Definition
1
,
m_{1}<\|B(j)\|<M
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence, we must have

(37)

c_{1}<\|\Lambda(j)\|<C_{1}\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
c_{1}
and
C_{1}
depend on
m_{1},M
and
\delta
. Since
\left|\lambda_{j}^{+}\right|>2|\lambda_{j}^{-}|
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there must exist a
\gamma=\gamma(m_{1},M,\delta)>0
such that
|\lambda_{j}^{+}|>\gamma
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, for any
{\alpha}>0
, we have

(38)

\Lambda(j)\cdot({\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha})\subset{\mathbb{D}}_{\frac{{\alpha}}{2}}\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

For
\widetilde{B}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, we define

(39)

\widetilde{\Lambda}(j):=D(j+1)^{-1}\widetilde{B}(j)D(j).

Then by (
36
), we have for some
C=C(\gamma,{\alpha})>0
that

\|\widetilde{\Lambda}(j)-\Lambda(j)\|<C\|\widetilde{B}-B\|.

Hence, if
\|\widetilde{B}-B\|
small, we have the following two properties. First, by (
37
), we have

(40)

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|\widetilde{\Lambda}(j)\|>c.

Second, by (
34
) of Lemma
3
, we have

\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}}|\Lambda(j)\cdot z-\widetilde{\Lambda}(j)\cdot z|<C\|\widetilde{\Lambda}(j)-\Lambda(j)\|<C^{2}\|\widetilde{B}(j)-B(j)\|.

Thus for all
\frac{{\alpha}}{2}<{\alpha}^{\prime}<{\alpha}
, there exists a
{\varepsilon}={\varepsilon}(\delta,{\alpha},{\alpha}^{\prime},\gamma)>0
such that if
\|\widetilde{B}-B\|<{\varepsilon}
, then

(41)

\widetilde{\Lambda}(j)\cdot{\mathbb{D}}_{\alpha}\subset{\mathbb{D}}_{{\alpha}^{\prime}}\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

(
40
) and (
41
) imply that
\widetilde{\Lambda}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
satisfies all the conditions of Theorem
6
. Thus, we have
\widetilde{\Lambda}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
with the corresponding constants
\delta(\widetilde{\Lambda})
and
N(\widetilde{\Lambda})
depend only on
{\varepsilon}
, hence only on
m_{N(B)}
,
M
,
\delta(B)
,
{\alpha}
and
{\alpha}^{\prime}
. However, the choice of
{\alpha}
and
{\alpha}^{\prime}
are independent of
B
.

Since
\widetilde{\Lambda}
is conjugate to
\widetilde{B}
via
D
, one readily check that
\widetilde{B}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
. Indeed, condition (4) of Definition
1
for
\widetilde{\Lambda}
clearly implies
\widetilde{B}
satisfies condition (4) as well. Moreover, if
\tilde{E}^{u}
and
\tilde{E}^{s}
are the invariant spaces of
\widetilde{\Lambda}
, then it is straightforward to see that
\bar{E}^{u}(j)=D(j)\cdot E^{u}(j)
and
\bar{E}^{s}(j)=D(j)\cdot E^{s}(j)
are the invariant spaces of
\widetilde{B}
which meet all other conditions of Definition
1
. Finally, since
{\alpha}
and
{\alpha}^{\prime}
can be chosen independent of
B
and since constants associated with

depend only on
\delta
, one readily checks that the dependence of the constants
\delta(\widetilde{B})
and
N(\widetilde{B})
depend on
B
only through
\delta(B)
,
N(B)
,
m_{N(B)}
, and
M
.
∎

3.
Domination Implies Invertibility of the Operator

In this section, we show that

\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}:B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}\ \}\subset\rho(J_{a,b}).

Consider the spectral equation

(42)

J_{a,b}\psi=E\psi,

where
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
is the
energy parameter
. A direct computation shows that if
\psi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
solves equation (
42
), then

(43)

B^{E}(j)\binom{\psi(j)}{\psi(j-1)}=a_{j}\binom{\psi(j+1)}{\psi(j)}\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
B^{E}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is called the
Jacobi cocycle map
and is defined as

(44)

B^{E}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}E-b_{j}&-\overline{a_{j-1}}\\
a_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}.

In case
a_{j}\neq 0
, we define
A^{E}(j)=\frac{1}{a_{j}}B^{E}(j)
. By (
43
),
(J_{a,b}\psi)_{j}=(E\psi)_{j}
is equivalent to

(45)

A^{E}(j)\binom{\psi(j)}{\psi(j-1)}=\binom{\psi(j+1)}{\psi(j)}.

In other words,
A^{E}(j)
becomes the transfer matrix of the operator (
1
) when it exists. First, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
is such that
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
with
E^{u}
and
E^{s}
being its invariant directions. Then there exists a
\lambda_{0}>1
so that the following holds true.

(1)

Suppose there is
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
a_{j}\neq 0
for all
j>j_{0}
. Then for each
j>j_{0}
, there exists a
k=k(j)>0
such that it holds for all unit vectors
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)
that

(46)

\|A^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\geq k\lambda_{0}^{n}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1,

(2)

Suppose there is a
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
a_{j}\neq 0
for all
j<j_{0}
. Then, for all
j\leq j_{0}
, there exists
k=k(j)>0
such that it holds for all unit vectors
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
that

(47)

\|A^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|\geq k\lambda_{0}^{n}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1.

Proof.

By conditions (1)-(2) of Definition
1
,
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
guarantees that there exist
c>0
and
\lambda>1
such that

(48)

\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|>c\lambda^{n}\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|

for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all unit vectors
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)
and
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
.
By condition (3) of Definition
1
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\det\begin{pmatrix}\vec{u}(j),\vec{s}(j)\end{pmatrix}|=d(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j))>\delta>0
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\delta\big|\prod_{i=j}^{j+n-1}a_{i}\overline{a_{i-1}}\big|

\displaystyle\leq|\det(B^{E}_{n}(j)|d(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j))

\displaystyle=|\det(B^{E}_{n}(j))|\cdot|\det\begin{pmatrix}\vec{u}(j),\vec{s}(j)\end{pmatrix}|

\displaystyle=|\det(B^{E}_{n}(j)\begin{pmatrix}\vec{u}(j),\vec{s}(j)\end{pmatrix})|

\displaystyle=|\det\begin{pmatrix}B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j),B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\end{pmatrix}|

\displaystyle=\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\cdot\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|\cdot|\det(\vec{u}(j+n),\vec{s}(j+n))|

\displaystyle=\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\cdot\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|\cdot d\big(E^{u}(j+n),E^{s}(j+n)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\delta\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\cdot\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|.

Combining the estimate above and (
48
), we have for some
\tilde{c}>0
, the choice of which is independent of
j
, that

(49)

\left|\widetilde{c}\prod_{i=j}^{j+n-1}a_{i}\overline{a_{i-1}}\right|\leq\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|^{2}\lambda^{-n}\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\mbox{ and all }n\geq 1.

If
a_{j}\not=0
for all
j>j_{0}
, then by (
49
) it holds for all
j>j_{0}+1
that

\|A^{E}_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|^{2}\geq\widetilde{c}\lambda^{n}\left|\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_{j+n-1}}\right|\geq\widetilde{c_{1}}\lambda^{n}|a_{j-1}|\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1

where the last inequality comes from the fact that
a\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}})
. It clearly implies (
46
) with
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{\lambda}
when
j>j_{0}+1
. No matter
a_{j_{0}}=0
or not, the estimate above can then be extended to
j_{0}+1
since

A^{E}(j_{0}+1)E^{u}(j_{0}+1)=E^{u}(j_{0}+2).

Similarly, suppose for some
j
,
B^{E}_{-n}(j)
is well-defined for all
n\geq 1
. Then
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
guarantees that there exists a
c>0
, the choice of which is independent of
j
, such that

\|B^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|>c\lambda^{n}\|B^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|

for all unit vectors
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)
and
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
. Following the same logic as above, we get that

\left|\frac{\widetilde{c}}{\prod_{i=j-n}^{j-1}a_{i}\overline{a_{i-1}}}\right|\leq\|B^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\|B^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|\leq\|B^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|^{2}\lambda^{-n}

which in turn implies for all
n\geq 1

\|A^{E}_{-n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|^{2}\geq\widetilde{c}\lambda^{n}\left|\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_{j-n-1}}\right|\geq\widetilde{c_{1}}\lambda^{n}|a_{j-1}|.

If
a_{j}\neq 0
for all
j<j_{0}
, then
B^{E}_{-n}(j)
is well-defined for all
j\leq j_{0}
and for all
n\geq 1
. Thus, the estimate above clearly implies (
47
) with
\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{\lambda}
for all
j\leq j_{0}
.
∎

For
j_{2}>j_{1}
, we define
J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}
to be the restriction of
J_{a,b}
to the subspaces
\ell^{2}(j_{1},j_{2}]
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. That is
j_{(j_{1},j_{1}+1]}=b_{j_{1}+1}
; for
j_{2}>j_{1}+1
, we have

(50)

(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}\phi)(j)=\begin{cases}a_{j_{1}+1}\phi(j_{1}+2)+b_{j_{1}+1}\phi(j_{1}+1),&j=j_{1}+1,\\
(J_{a,b}\phi)(j),&j_{1}+1<j<j_{2}\mbox{ if }j_{2}>j_{1}+2,\\
\overline{a_{j_{2}-1}}\phi(j_{2}-1)+b_{j_{2}}\phi(j_{2}),&j=j_{2}.\end{cases}

We also denote restrictions of
J_{a,b}
on half lines
(j_{0},+\infty)
and
(-\infty,j_{0}]
with Dirichlet boundary conditions by
J_{(j_{0},+)}
and
J_{(-,j_{0}]}
, respectively. In other words, we have

(51)

(J_{(j_{0},+)}\phi)(j)=\begin{cases}a_{j_{0}+1}\phi(j_{0}+2)+b_{j_{0}+1}\phi(j_{0}+1),&j=j_{0}+1,\\
(J_{a,b}\phi)(j),&j>j_{0}+1,\end{cases}

(52)

(J_{(-,j_{0}]}\phi)(j)=\begin{cases}\overline{a_{j_{0}-1}}\phi(j_{0}-1)+b_{j_{0}}\phi(j_{0}),&j=j_{0},\\
(J_{a,b}\phi)(j),&j<j_{0}.\end{cases}

Note for all those restrictions, we write the intervals of integers to be half open without the left end point. They may certainly be written as other type of intervals. We define
p_{N}(j,E)=\det(E-J_{[j,j+N)})
for
N\geq 1
,
p_{0}(j,E)=1
, and
p_{-1}(j,E)=0
. Then it is a standard result that the following is true for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all
N\geq 1
:

B_{N}^{E}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{N}(j,E)&-\overline{a_{j-1}}p_{N-1}(j+1,E)\\
a_{j+N-1}p_{N-1}(j,E)&-\overline{a_{j-1}}a_{j+N-1}p_{N-2}(j+1,E)\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
is such that
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
with
E^{u}
,
E^{s}
being its invariant directions. We consider the following restrictions of
J_{a,b}
:

(1)

There is
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
a_{j_{0}}=0
and
a_{j_{0}}\neq 0
for all
j>j_{0}
. Then we consider
J_{(j_{0},+)}
.

(2)

There is
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
a_{j_{0}}=0
and
a_{j_{0}}\neq 0
for all
j<j_{0}
. Then we consider
J_{(-,j_{0}]}
.

(3)

For all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
a_{j}\neq 0
. Then we consider the whole line operator
J_{a,b}
.

(4)

There exist
j_{1}<j_{2}
such that
a_{j_{1}}=a_{j_{2}}=0
. Then we consider
J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}
.

In cases (1) and (2), the solution space of
J_{(j_{0},+)}\phi=E\phi
or
J_{(-,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
is one-dimensional. Moreover, any of their nontrivial solutions grows exponentially along some subsequences that go to
\pm\infty
, respectively. In case (3), the solution space of
J_{a,b}\psi=E\psi
is two dimensional and any nontrivial solution either grows exponentially along a subsequence that goes to
\infty
or along a subsequence that goes to
-\infty
. In case (4),
J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}\phi=E\phi
has only zero solution.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may focus on case (1) since case (2) can be done similarly. Consider the solution space
\{\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}_{j>j_{0}}\to{\mathbb{C}}:J_{(j_{0},+)}\phi=E\phi\}
. By (
51
), the solution is uniquely determined by
\phi_{j_{0}}
which implies the space is one-dimensional. More precisely, for all
j\geq j_{0}+2
,
\phi(j_{0})
is determined by equation (
51
) which by (
45
) is equivalent to

\binom{\phi(j+1)}{\phi(j)}=A^{E}_{j-j_{0}-1}(j_{0}+1)\binom{\phi(j_{0}+1)}{\phi(j_{0})}.

Note in the equation above, the choice of
\phi(j_{0})
is not relevant as it will be canceled by
a_{j_{0}}=0
. In particular, if we define
\phi^{u}
to be a solution generated by some
\binom{\phi^{u}(j_{0}+1)}{\phi^{u}(j_{0})}\in E^{u}(j_{0}+1)
, then by Lemma
4
, we will have for all
j\geq j_{0}+1
:

(53)

\left\|\binom{\phi^{u}(j+1)}{\phi^{u}(j)}\right\|=\left\|\binom{\phi^{u}(j_{0}+1)}{\phi^{u}(j_{0})}\right\|\|A^{E}_{j-j_{0-1}}(j_{0}+1)\vec{u}(j)\|\geq\tilde{k}\lambda^{j-j_{0}}.

It is clear that the above estimate implies that
|\phi^{u}(n_{l})|>\tilde{k}\lambda^{n_{l}-j_{0}}
for a strictly monotone increasing sequence
n_{l}>j_{0}
. In particular, it is a nontrivial solution which must form a basis of the solution space. Hence, all nontrivial solutions of
J_{(j_{0},+)}\phi=E\phi
are some
\phi^{u}
generated by a nonzero vector in
E^{u}(j_{0}+1)
and it holds that for some
\tilde{k}=\tilde{k}(\phi^{u})>0
,

(54)

|\phi^{u}(n_{l})|>\tilde{k}\lambda^{n_{l}-j_{0}}\mbox{ for all }l\geq 1.

Going backwards in time
j
and running the same proof above, we obtain the following information in case (2). Any nontrivial solution
\phi^{s}
of
J_{(-,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
is generated by a vector in
E^{s}(j_{0})
. By case (2) of Lemma
4
and the estimate above we obtain for some
n_{t}<j_{0}
, which is strictly monotone decreasing in
t\geq 1
, that

(55)

|\phi^{s}(n_{t})|>\tilde{k}\lambda^{j_{0}-n_{t}}\mbox{ for all }t\geq 1.

To consider case (3), we fix any
j_{0}
. Then all solutions are of the form
\psi=\alpha\phi^{u}+\beta\phi^{s}
where
\phi^{u}
is generated by a vector in
E^{u}(j_{0})
and
\phi^{s}
is generated by a vector in
E^{s}(j_{0})
. Since
a_{j}\neq 0
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the same proof as in the first two cases yield that
\phi^{u}(n_{l})
grows exponentially fast as
n_{l}\to\infty
and
\phi^{s}(n_{t})
grows exponentially fast as
n_{t}\to-\infty
where
\{n_{l}\}
and
{n_{l}}
are some sequences. Hence, if
\psi
is nontrivial, it must grow exponentially fast at least along one of the sequences
\{n_{l}\}
and
\{n_{t}\}
.

Now, we consider case (4), i.e. the finite restrictions
J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}
. We want to show

\dim\{\phi^{f}(n),j_{1}<n\leq j_{2}:(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)\phi^{f}(n)=0\}=0.

If not, then
(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)\phi^{f}=0
for some nonzero vector
\phi^{f}
. Set
N=j_{2}-j_{1}
. Then we have
p_{N}(j_{1}+1,E)=\det(E-J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]})=0
since
E\in\sigma(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]})
. Recall
a_{j_{1}}=a_{j_{2}}=0
. Thus, we have

B_{N}^{E}(j_{1}+1)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{N}(j_{1}+1,E)&-\overline{a_{j_{1}}}p_{N-1}(j_{1}+2,E)\\
a_{j_{2}}p_{N-1}(j_{1}+1,E)&-\overline{a_{j_{1}}}a_{j_{2}}p_{N-2}(j_{1}+2,E)\end{pmatrix}

is a zero matrix, which contradicts condition (4) of Definition
1
.
Hence, the solutions of
(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)\phi^{f}=0
are all trivial.
∎

Let
\mathcal{E}_{g}(J_{a,b})
be the set of generalized eigenvalues of
J_{a,b}
, i.e. all
E\subset{\mathbb{C}}
that admit a nontrivial polynomially bounded solution of
J_{a,b}\psi=E\psi
. From the theorem of Sch’nol-Berezanskii
[
B
,
Sc
]
, it is well-known that

\overline{\mathcal{E}_{g}(J_{a,b})}=\sigma(J_{a,b}).

In particular, it suffices to show for any
E
such that
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
, all nontrivial solutions of
J_{a,b}\psi=E\psi
are not polynomially bounded. Indeed, by Theorem
5
,
B^{E^{\prime}}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
for all
E^{\prime}
close to
E
. Hence, for all
E^{\prime}
close
E
, all nontrivial solutions of
J_{a,b}\psi=E^{\prime}\psi
are not polynomially bounded which implies
E\in\overline{{\mathcal{E}}_{g}(J_{a,b})}^{\complement}=\rho(J_{a,b})
.

Hence, to prove

\{E:B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}\ \}\subset\rho(J_{a,b}),

we only need to show all nontrivial solutions of
J_{a,b}\psi=E\psi
are not polynomially bounded when
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
. To this end, we define

(56)

j_{\max}:=\sup\{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a_{j}=0\}\mbox{ and }j_{\min}:=\inf\{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a_{j}=0\},

if they exist. Note we allow
j_{\max}
to be
\infty
and
j_{\min}
to be
-\infty
.

Proof of the first direction of Theorem
4
.

Now we are ready to prove (
5
). We may divide the proof into the following cases.

Case I.
a_{j}\neq 0
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then case (3) of Lemma
5
directly implies that all nontrivial solutions of
J_{a,b}\psi=E\psi
are not polynomially bounded.

Case II.
-\infty<j_{\min}\leq j_{\max}<\infty
. If
j_{\min}=j_{\max}
, then we can decompose
J_{a,b}
as the following direct sum

J_{a,b}=J_{(-,j_{\min}]}\oplus J_{(j_{\max},+)}.

If
j_{\min}<j_{\max}
, then we have

J_{a,b}=J_{(-,j_{\min}]}\oplus J_{(j_{\min},j_{\max}]}\oplus J_{(j_{\max},+)}.

By cases (1), (2), and (4) of Lemma
5
, in both cases, the solution space is spanned by

\{(\phi^{s},0,0,\ldots),(\ldots,0,0,\phi^{u})\}

where
\phi^{s}
and
\phi^{u}
are any nontrivial solutions of
J_{(-,j_{\min}]}\phi=E\phi
and
J_{(j_{\max},+)}\phi=E\phi
, respectively. Hence, by (
54
) and (
55
) and similar to Case I, all nontrivial solutions of the eigenvalue equation in both cases must grow exponentially fast at least along some subsequences go to
\infty
or
-\infty
.

Case III. Either
j_{\min}=-\infty
and
j_{\max}<\infty
; or
j_{\min}>-\infty
and
j_{\max}=\infty
. In the first case, there exists a strictly monotone decreasing sequence of integers
\{j_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that
j_{1}=j_{\max}
and
a_{j_{k}}=0
for all
k\geq 1
. Hence we may decompose the operator
J_{a,b}
as

J_{a,b}=\ldots\oplus J_{(j_{k+1},j_{k}]}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{(j_{2},j_{1}]}\oplus J_{(j_{1},+)}.

Then by cases (1) and (4) of Lemma
5
, the solution space of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
is spanned by
(\ldots,0,0,\phi^{u})
, where
\phi^{u}
is any nontrivial solution of
J_{(j_{\max},+)}\phi=E\phi
. By (
54
), all nontrivial solutions of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
grows exponentially fast along
\{n_{l}\}
where
n_{l}\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
.

In the second case, we may decompose the operator
J_{a,b}
as

J_{a,b}=J_{(-,j_{1})}\oplus J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{(j_{k},j_{k+1}]}\oplus\cdots

where
j_{1}=j_{\min}
and
a_{j_{k}}=0
for all
k\geq 1
. Hence, the solution space of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
is spanned by
(\phi^{s},0,0\ldots)
, where
\phi^{s}
is any nontrivial solution of
J_{(-,j_{\min}]}\phi=E\phi
. By (
55
), all nontrivial solutions of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
grow exponentially along
\{n_{t}\}
where
n_{t}\to-\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

Case IV.
j_{\min}=-\infty
and
j_{\max}=\infty
. Then there exists a subsequence
(j_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
such that
j_{k}\to\pm\infty
as
k\to\pm\infty
and
a_{j_{k}}=0
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence, we may decompose
J_{a,b}
as

J_{a,b}=\bigoplus^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}J_{(j_{k},j_{k+1}]}.

By case (4) of Lemma
5
, all solutions of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
are trivial.

Since the four cases exhaust all the possibilities, the proof is completed.

∎

4.
Domination Away from the Spectrum

In this section we prove the second half (
6
) of Theorem
4
. That is,

\rho(J_{a,b})\subset\{E:B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}\ \}.

4.1.
Lower bound of the norm of iterations of Jacobi cocycles

In this section, we show if
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
, then
B^{E}
satifies the stronger version
1
of condition (4) of Definition
1
. For simplicity, we denote by
J_{N}(j)=J_{[j,j+N)}
, the restriction of
J_{a,b}
on
[j,N)
. Recall we have
p_{N}(j,E)=\det(E-J_{N}(j))
. Let
\mathrm{diam}(S)
be the diameter of a subset
S\subset{\mathbb{C}}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
and fix a
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
. For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta=\delta({\varepsilon})>0
such that the following is true: if
|p_{N}(j,E)|\leq\delta
for some
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then there exists an
E_{0}\in\sigma(J_{N}(j))
such that
|E-E_{0}|<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

To prove present lemma, it suffices to show the following result.

Fix
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and define
I_{N}(j,\delta):=\{E\in{\mathbb{C}}:|p_{N}(j,E)|\leq\delta\}
. For all
{\varepsilon}>0
, there exists
\delta=\delta({\varepsilon})>0
such that for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all connected components
{\mathcal{K}}
of
I_{N}(j,\delta)
, we have

\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{K}})<{\varepsilon}.

Moreover, there is a zero of
p_{N}(j,E)
in
{\mathcal{K}}
.

To show that each connected component of
I_{N}(j,\delta)
contains a zero of
p_{N}(j,E)
, we just need to show that
|p_{N}(j,E)|
has no positive local minimum. Suppose
|p_{N}(j,E)|
has a positive local minimum at
E_{0}
. Then
p_{N}(j,E):B_{r}(E_{0})=\{E:|E-E_{0}|<r\}\to{\mathbb{C}}
has no zeros for small
r>0
. Hence, the holomorphic function
\frac{1}{p_{N}(j,E)}:B_{r}(E_{0})\to{\mathbb{C}}
attains its maximum modulus at an interior point
E_{0}
. This contradicts the maximum principle since
p_{N}(j,E)
is nonconstant for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

Next we show for all
{\varepsilon}>0
the existence of desired
\delta
. Here we use the following version of the so-called Markov’s inequality, see. e.g.
[
Si
, Lemma 3.1]
:

For any connected compact set
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{K}})>\eta>0
, there exist positive constants
M,\alpha
, depending only on
\eta
, such that it holds for all polynomials
p(z)
and all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
that

(57)

\left\|\frac{d^{r}p_{n}}{dz^{r}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{K}}}\leq M(\deg p)^{r\alpha}\|p_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{K}}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the supremum norm on
{\mathcal{K}}
and
\deg p
is the degree of the polynomial
p
. Now suppose the statement at the begining of this proof is not true. Then there exists
{\varepsilon}_{0}>0
such that for all
\delta>0
, there exists
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and a connected component
{\mathcal{K}}
of
I_{N}(j,\delta)
whose diameter satisfies

\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{K}})\geq{\varepsilon}_{0}.

Applying (
57
) to
p_{N}(j,E)
on
{\mathcal{K}}
, we obtain

\left\|\frac{d^{N}p_{N}(j,E)}{dE^{N}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{K}}}\leq MN^{r\alpha}\|p_{N}(j,E)\|_{{\mathcal{K}}}\leq MN^{N\alpha}\delta.

It is clear that the coefficient of the highest order term
E^{N}
in
p_{N}
is
1
. Hence, we obtain for all
\delta>0
that

N!\leq MN^{N\alpha}\delta

where
N
is fixed and
M
and
{\alpha}
depend only on
{\varepsilon}_{0}
. This is clearly not possible if we choose
\delta
to be smaller than
\frac{N!}{MN^{N{\alpha}}}
.
∎

First, we note the following facts from functional analysis. Let
H
be a self-adjoint operator, i.e.
H^{*}=H
, on a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. Then Weyl’s criterion (see e.g.
[
RS
]
) says:

(58)

E\in\sigma(H)\mbox{ if and only if : for all }{\varepsilon}>0,\ \|(H-E)u\|<{\varepsilon}\mbox{ for some unit vector }u\in\mathbb{H}.

Moreover, for each
z\in\rho(H)
, we have

(59)

\|(H-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1}=d(z,\sigma(H)).

Clearly,
J_{a,b}
is self-adjoint. We define
\delta_{j}(n)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
to be the sequence such that

\delta_{j}(n)=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{ if }m=j\\
0,&\mbox{ if }m\neq j.\end{cases}

Throughout this section, we fix
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
and we let
M>0
be that

\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\{|a_{j}|,|b_{j}|,|b_{j}-E|,|E|,\|J_{a,b}\|\}<M.

Moreover, we define

(60)

\delta:=d(E,\sigma(J_{a,b}))=\|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}\|^{-1}

Lemma 7
.

Let
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
. Then for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, it holds that

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\|>0.

Proof.

We will prove this lemma by induction. First, we set
n=1
. We need to show

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B^{E}(j)\|>0.

Suppose
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B^{E}(j)\|=0
. Then, for all
{\varepsilon}>0
, there exists
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\|B^{E}(j_{0})\|<\epsilon
. Hence, we have

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}E-b_{j_{0}}&-\overline{a_{j_{0}-1}}\\
\ a_{j_{0}}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|<{\varepsilon}\mbox{ implies }|E-b_{j_{0}}|<{\varepsilon},\ |a_{j_{0}-1}|<{\varepsilon},\mbox{ and }|a_{j_{0}}|<{\varepsilon}.

Now, let’s consider
(J_{a,b}-E)\delta_{j_{0}}.
Clearly,

(J_{a,b}-E)\delta_{j_{0}}(n)=\overline{a_{n-1}}\delta_{j_{0}}(n-1)+a_{n}\delta_{j_{0}}(n+1)+(b_{n}-E)\delta_{j_{0}}(n)

which is 0 for all
n\not=j_{0}-1,j_{0},j_{0}+1
. On the other hand, it is easy to see that

(J_{a,b}-E)\delta_{j_{0}}(n)=\begin{cases}a_{j_{0}-1},&\mbox{ if }n=j_{0}-1\\
b_{j_{0}}-E,&\mbox{ if }n=j_{0}\\
\overline{a_{j_{0}}},&\mbox{ if }n=j_{0}+1.\end{cases}

Hence,
\|(J_{a,b}-E)\delta_{j_{0}}\|_{\ell^{2}}<3{\varepsilon}
. By Weyl’s Criterion, we have
E\in\sigma(J_{a,b})
since
\|\delta_{j_{0}}\|_{\ell^{2}}=1
, which contradicts
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
.

Next, we do the induction: assuming

\inf_{1\leq n<N}\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B^{E}_{n}(j)\|=c>0,

we want to show
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B^{E}_{N}(j)\|>0
. Recall we have
p_{N}(j,E)=\det(E-J_{N}(j))
and

B_{N}^{E}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{N}(j,E)&-\overline{a_{j-1}}p_{N-1}(j+1,E)\\
a_{j+N-1}p_{N-1}(j,E)&-\overline{a_{j-1}}a_{j+N-1}p_{N-2}(j+1,E)\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
6
, for all
{\varepsilon}>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that if
|p_{N}(j,E)|<\delta
, then there exists
E_{0}\in\sigma(J_{N}(j))
such that
|E-E_{0}|<{\varepsilon}
. Assume
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B^{E}_{N}(j)\|=0
. Choose
0<\gamma<\max\{\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2},\delta,\frac{1}{M^{2}}\}
. Then, there exists
j_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\|B^{E}_{N}(j_{0})\|<\gamma^{3N}
which implies all the following four terms

|p_{N}(j_{0},E)|,\ |a_{j_{0}-1}p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)|,\ |a_{j_{0}+N-1}p_{N-1}(j_{0},E)|,\mbox{ and }

|a_{j_{0}-1}a_{j_{0}+N-1}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+1,E)|

are smaller than
\gamma^{3N}
. We will divide the discussion into three different cases.

Case I.
|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma
,
|a_{j_{0}-1}|<\gamma
. Since
|P_{N}(j_{0},E)|<\gamma^{3N}<\delta
, by Lemma
6
, there exists
E_{0}\in\sigma(J_{N}(j_{0}))
such that
|E-E_{0}|<{\varepsilon}
. Choose a unit eigenvector
\vec{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})
of
J_{N}(j_{0})
for the eigenvalue
E_{0}
. We define
\phi=(\ldots,0,0,\vec{u},0,0,\ldots)
which is a unit vector in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Clearly,

(J_{a,b}-E)\phi=(J_{a,b}-E_{0})\phi+(E-E_{0})\phi
where
\|(E-E_{0})\phi\|<{\varepsilon}
.
A direct computation shows that

[(J_{a,b}-E_{0})\phi]_{j}=\begin{cases}0,&j_{0}\leq j\leq j_{0}+N-1,\ j\geq j+N+1,\mbox{ or }j\leq j_{0}-2\\
a_{j_{0}-1}u_{1},&j=j_{0}-1\\
\overline{a_{j_{0}+N-1}}u_{N},&j=j+N,\end{cases}

which implies
\|(J_{a,b}-E_{0})\phi\|<2\gamma<2{\varepsilon}
. Hence, it holds for the unit vector
\phi
that

\|(J_{a,b}-E)\phi\|<3{\varepsilon}.

Case II. Either
|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma
and
|a_{j_{0}-1}|\geq\gamma
; or
|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|\geq\gamma
and
|a_{j_{0}-1}|<\gamma
. Without loss of generality, we focus on the case
|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma
and
|a_{j_{0}-1}|\geq\gamma
. First, we have

\gamma|p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)|\leq|a_{j_{0}-1}p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)|<\gamma^{3N}

which implies
|p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)|<\gamma^{3N-1}
. If
|a_{j_{0}}|<\sqrt{2\gamma}<{\varepsilon}
, then together with

|p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)|<\gamma^{3N-1}<\delta\mbox{ and }|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma<{\varepsilon}.

We may apply the same argument as in case I to the operator
J_{N-1}(j_{0}+1)
and get a unit vector
\phi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
such that

\|(J_{a,b}-E)\phi\|<3{\varepsilon}.

If
|a_{j_{0}}|\geq\sqrt{2\gamma}
, then it is a standard result that

|p_{N}(j_{0},E)|=|(E-b_{j_{0}})p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)-|a_{j_{0}}|^{2}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N}

which implies

|a_{j_{0}}^{2}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N-2}+\gamma^{3N}<2\gamma^{3N-2},

which in turn implies

|p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N-3}.

Repeating this procedure, then either at some step we obtain
\|(J_{a,b}-E)\phi\|<3{\varepsilon}
for some unit vector
\phi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
, or we eventually get

\big|p_{2}(j_{0}+N-2,E)|=|(E-b_{j_{0}+N-2})(E-b_{j_{0}+N-1})-|a_{j_{0}+N-2}|^{2}\big|<\gamma^{9}

and

|p_{1}(j_{0}+N-1,E)|=|E-b_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma^{6}.

So we have that
|E-b_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma^{6}<{\varepsilon}
,
|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|<\gamma<{\varepsilon}
and
|a_{j_{0}+N-2}|<\sqrt{2\gamma^{5}}<{\varepsilon}
, which again yields

\|(J_{a,b}-E)\phi\|<3{\varepsilon}.

Case III.
|a_{j_{0}+N-1}|\geq\gamma
and
|a_{j_{0}-1}|\geq\gamma
. In this case, we must have
|p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)|<\gamma^{3N-1}
,
|p_{N-1}(j_{0},E)|<\gamma^{3N-1}
and
|p_{N-2}(j_{0}+1,E)|<\gamma^{3N-2}
. Note we have

|p_{N}(j_{0},E)|=|(E-b_{j_{0}})p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)-|a_{j_{0}}|^{2}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N}\mbox{ and }

|p_{N-1}(j_{0},E)|=|(E-b_{j_{0}})p_{N-2}(j_{0}+1,E)-|a_{j_{0}}|^{2}p_{N-3}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N-1}.

So we must have that

|a_{j_{0}}^{2}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)|<2\gamma^{3N-2}\mbox{ and }|a_{j_{0}}^{2}p_{N-3}(j_{0}+2,E)|<2\gamma^{3N-3}

If
|a_{j_{0}}|>\sqrt{2\gamma}
, then
|p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N-3}
and
|p_{N-3}(j_{0}+2,E)|<\gamma^{3N-4}
. We have

B^{E}_{N-1}(j_{0}+1)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{N-1}(j_{0}+1,E)&-\overline{a_{j_{0}}}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+2,E)\\
a_{j_{0}+N-1}p_{N-2}(j_{0}+1,E)&-\overline{a_{j_{0}}}a_{j_{0}+N-1}p_{N-3}(j_{0}+2,E)\end{pmatrix}

Hence, combine all the estimates above, we obtain the following estimate

\|B^{E}_{N-1}(j_{0}+1)\|<5\gamma^{3N-5}<5\gamma<5{\varepsilon},

which cannot happen if we choose
{\varepsilon}<\frac{5}{c}
. Thus we must have
|a_{j_{0}}|<\sqrt{2\gamma}<{\varepsilon}
. By the same argument as above, we see that
|a_{j_{0}+N-2}|<\sqrt{2\gamma}<{\varepsilon}
. Recall
|p_{N-2}(j_{0}+1,E)|<\gamma^{3N-2}<\delta
. Applying the same argument as in case I to
J_{N-2}(j_{0}+1)
, we again obtain

\|(J_{a,b}-E)\phi\|<3{\varepsilon}

for some unit vector
\phi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Combining all the possible cases, if
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}B_{N}(j)=0
, then for all
{\varepsilon}>0
we can find a unit vector
\phi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
such that

\|(J_{a,b}-E)\phi\|<3{\varepsilon},

which by Weyl’s criterion implies
E\in\sigma(J_{a,b})
. This contradicts
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
, concluding the proof.
∎

4.2.
Estimates on and structure of the Green’s Function

Now, to prove that
B^{E}\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
, we only need to construct the two invariant directions and show they satisfy conditions (1)-(3) of Definition
1
. First, we perform a Combes-Thomas type of estimate
[
CT
]
concerning exponential decay of the Green’s Function.

Lemma 8
.

For each
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
. There exists a positive constant
\gamma=\gamma(\delta,M)>0
such that

(61)

|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}(p,q)|\leq\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\gamma|p-q|}\mbox{ for all }p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Proof.

Define
M_{\beta}
to be the multiplication operator
(M_{\beta}\phi)_{n}=e^{{\beta}n}\phi_{n}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
|\beta|\leq 1
. Let
T
be the left shift operator. That is,
(T\phi)_{n}=\phi_{n+1}
. A direct computation shows that:

\displaystyle(M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)M_{\beta}\phi)_{n}

\displaystyle=e^{-{\beta}n}(J_{a,b}-E)(e^{{\beta}n}\phi_{n})

\displaystyle=e^{-{\beta}n}(\overline{a_{n-1}}e^{{\beta}(n-1)}\phi_{n-1}+a_{n}e^{{\beta}(n+1)}\phi_{n+1}+(b_{n}-E)e^{{\beta}n}\phi_{n})

\displaystyle=\overline{a_{n-1}}e^{-{\beta}}\phi_{n-1}+a_{n}e^{{\beta}}\phi_{n+1}+(b_{n}-E)\phi_{n}

\displaystyle=(J_{a,b}-E)\phi_{n}+a_{n}(e^{\beta}-1)\phi_{n+1}+\overline{a_{n-1}}(e^{-{\beta}}-1)\phi_{n-1}

\displaystyle=(J_{a,b}-E)\phi_{n}+a_{n}(e^{\beta}-1)(T\phi_{n})+\overline{a_{n-1}}(e^{-{\beta}}-1)(T^{-1}\phi_{n}).

Hence,

M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)M_{\beta}=J_{a,b}-E+a_{n}(e^{\beta}-1)T+\overline{a_{n-1}}(e^{-{\beta}}-1)T^{-1}=J_{a,b}-E+S.

The operator
S
is bounded on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
and it holds for some
C=C(M)>0
that

\|S\|\leq|a_{n}||(e^{\beta}-1)|+|a_{n-1}||(e^{-{\beta}}-1)|\leq C|{\beta}|.

Clearly,
\|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}S\|\leq\frac{1}{2}
if
|{\beta}|\leq\|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}\|^{-1}/(2C)=\delta/(2C)
. Then

M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)M_{\beta}=J_{a,b}-E+S=(J_{a,b}-E)[I+(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}S]

is invertible. Moreover

(M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)M_{\beta})^{-1}=M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}M_{\beta}=[I+(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}S]^{-1}(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1},

which implies

\|M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}M_{\beta}\|\leq 2\|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}\|=\frac{2}{\delta}.

Hence, it holds for all
p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
that

\displaystyle|\langle\delta_{p},M_{-{\beta}}(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}M_{\beta}\delta_{q}\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle M_{-{\beta}}\delta_{p},(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}M_{\beta}\delta_{q}\rangle|

\displaystyle=|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}(p,q)|e^{-{\beta}(p-q)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\delta}.

By choosing the sign of
\beta
appropriately, it clearly holds that for
\gamma=|{\beta}|
that

|(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}(p,q)|\leq\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\gamma|p-q|}

which is nothing other than (
61
).
∎

We define
g_{j}(n)=(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}(n,j)
. By (
61
), it holds that

(62)

|g_{j}(n)|<\frac{2}{\delta}e^{-\gamma|n-j|},\mbox{ for all }n,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

It is clear that
(g_{j}(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
(g_{(j)}(n))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
are the unique solutions of the equations

(63)

(J_{a,b}-E)g_{j}=\delta_{j}\mbox{ and }g_{(\cdot)}(n)(J_{a,b}-E)=\delta_{n}.

In other words, we have for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
that

(64)

\overline{a_{j-1}}g_{j}(j-1)+a_{j}g_{j}(j+1)+(b_{j}-E)g_{j}(j)=1\mbox{ and }

(65)

\overline{a_{j}}g_{j+1}(j)+a_{j-1}g_{j-1}(j)+(b_{j}-E)g_{j}(j)=1.

In particular, we have for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
that

(66)

|g_{j}(j-1)|+|g_{j}(j+1)|+|g_{j}(j)|\neq 0\mbox{ and }|g_{j-1}(j)|+|g_{j}(j)|+|g_{j+1}(j)|\neq 0.

For each
j
,
g_{j}
may be obtained by connecting pieces which are related to different solutions of
(J_{a,b}-E)\psi=0
. Such information will be the key to construct the two invariant directions of
B^{E}
. To get more such information about
g_{j}
, we divide the discussion into several different cases depending on whether
a_{j}=0
or not. We denote by
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{\pm})
all the two-sided infinite sequences who is square summable on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\pm}
, respectively.

Case I.

a_{n}\not=0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Fix a
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. By (
66
),
|g_{j}(j-1)|+|g_{j}(j+1)|+|g_{j}(j)|\neq 0
. We further subdivide it into three different cases.

Case I.a.

g_{j}(j)\neq 0
or
g_{j}(j-1)g_{j}(j+1)\neq 0
. If we use
\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}
as an initial condition, then we obtain get a nonzero solution
\phi^{u}(n)
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
that coincides with
g_{j}(n)
for
n\leq j
. Hence,
\phi^{u}(n)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-})
. If we use
\binom{g_{j}(j+1)}{g_{j}(j)}
as an initial condition, then we obtain a nonzero solution of
\phi^{s}(n)
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
that coincides with
g_{j}(n)
for
n\geq j
. Hence,
\phi^{s}(n)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
.

Case I.b.

g_{j}(j-1)\neq 0
and
g_{j}(j)=g_{j}(j+1)=0
. First, we have a nonzero solution
\phi^{u}_{j}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-})
that is generated by
\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}
. We claim that in this case we must have

|g_{j-1}(j)|+|g_{j-1}(j+1)|\neq 0.

Indeed, if
g_{j-1}(j)=g_{j-1}(j+1)=0
. Then
g_{j}(j)=g_{j-1}(j)=0
and (
65
) together imply
g_{j+1}(j)\neq 0
. Hence
\binom{g_{j+1}(j)}{g_{j+1}(j-1)}
is linearly independent with
\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}=\binom{0}{g_{j}(j-1)}
which can generate another
\phi^{u}_{j+1}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-})
. It is clear that
\phi^{u}_{j}
and
\phi^{u}_{j+1}
are linear independent. This is impossible since

\displaystyle\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{u}_{j}(j)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j)\\
\phi^{u}_{j}(j-1)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-1)\end{pmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left(A_{j-n}(n)\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{u}_{j}(j-n)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-n)\\
\phi^{u}_{j}(j-n-1)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-n-1)\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=|\det A_{n-j}(n)|\cdot\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{u}_{j}(j-n)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-n)\\
\phi^{u}_{j}(j-n-1)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-n-1)\end{pmatrix}\right|

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{|a_{j-n}|}{|a_{j-1}|}\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{u}_{j}(j-n)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-n)\\
\phi^{u}_{j}(j-n-1)&\phi^{u}_{j+1}(j-n-1)\end{pmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle\to 0\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

while the first term is a fixed positive number, a contradiction. Thus, we may use
\binom{g_{j-1}(j+1)}{g_{j-1}(j)}
to generate a nonzero solution
\phi^{s}
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
that coincides with
g_{j-1}(n)
for all
n\geq j
. Hence, we must have
\phi^{s}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
.

Case I.c.

g_{j}(j+1)\neq 0
and
g_{j}(j)=g_{j}(j-1)=0
. It is basically the dual case of case 1.b. First, we may construct a
\phi^{s}_{j}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
, solution of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
, via
\binom{g_{j}(j+1)}{g_{j}(j)}
. Then we must have

|g_{j+1}(j)|+|g_{j+1}(j-1)|\neq 0.

Otherwise,
g_{j+1}(j)=g_{j}(j)=0
and (
65
) together imply
g_{j-1}(j)\neq 0
. Thus
\binom{g_{j-1}(j+1)}{g_{j-1}(j)}
can be used to generate another solution
\phi^{s}_{j-1}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
that is linearly independent of
\phi^{s}_{j}
. This is again impossible since

\displaystyle\left|\frac{a_{j+1}}{a_{n}}\right|\cdot\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{s}_{j-1}(j+1)&\phi^{s}_{j}(j+1)&\\
\phi^{s}_{j-1}(j)&\phi^{s}_{j}(j)\end{pmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left(A_{n-j-1}(j+1)\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{s}_{j-1}(j+1)&\phi^{s}_{j}(j+1)&\\
\phi^{s}_{j-1}(j)&\phi^{s}_{j}(j)\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|

(68)

\displaystyle=\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{s}_{j-1}(n)&\phi^{s}_{j}(n)\\
\phi^{s}_{j-1}(n-1)&\phi^{s}_{j}(n-1)\end{pmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle\to 0\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

while the first term in this chain of equations can be bounded below by a fixed positive number. Thus we may use
\binom{g_{j+1}(j)}{g_{j+1}(j-1)}
to generate a nonzero solution
\phi^{u}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-})
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
.

To sum up, in case I, we always obtain two nonzero solutions
\phi^{s}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
and
\phi^{u}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-})
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
. It is clear that
\phi^{s}
and
\phi^{u}
must be linear independent. Otherwise, they both decay exponentially as
n\to\pm\infty
which implies they are eigenvectors which constradicts
E\in\rho(J_{a,b})
. Since
\{\phi:J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi\}
is a two dimensional space, we have for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(69)

g_{j}(n)=\begin{cases}\phi_{j}^{s}(n),&n\geq j,\\
\phi_{j}^{u}(n),&n\leq j\end{cases}

where
\phi^{s(u)}_{j}\in\mathrm{span}\{\phi^{s(u)}\}
, respectively.

Case II
. There is a
j_{0}
so that
a_{j_{0}}=0
and
a_{n}\neq 0
for all
n<j_{0}
. We consider the operator
J_{(-,j_{0}]}
. We may fix any
j<j_{0}
. We again divide the discussion into three different cases.

Case II.a.

g_{j}(j)\neq 0
or
g_{j}(j-1)g_{j}(j+1)\neq 0
, then similar to case I.a, we may first use the vector
\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}
to generate a vector
(\phi^{-}(j))_{j\leq j_{0}}
such that

(70)

[(J_{(-,j_{0}]}-E)\phi^{-}](j)=0\mbox{ for all }j<j_{0}\mbox{ and }\phi^{-}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-}).

Note by the same computation as in (
67
), the set of all such
\phi^{-}
is a one-dimension space. Moreover,
\phi^{-}
cannot be a solution of
J_{(-,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
or else we can find a
\ell^{2}
-solution of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
via
\phi=(\phi_{-},0,0,\ldots,)
. In other words, we must have

(71)

\overline{a_{j_{0}-1}}\phi^{-}(j_{0}-1)+(E-b_{j_{0}})\phi^{-}(j_{0})\not=0.

Next, we can use
\binom{g_{j}(j+1)}{g_{j}(j)}
to generate a solution
(\phi^{s}(j))_{j\leq j_{0}}
of
J_{(-,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
. Note the set of such vectors form a one-dimensional space as well since they are uniquely determined by
\phi^{s}(j_{0})
. Note that it must hold
\phi^{s}\notin\ell^{2}((-\infty,j_{0}])
. Otherwise, we can again construct a nontrivial
\ell^{2}
-solution of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
.

Case II.b.

g_{j}(j)=g_{j}(j+1)=0
and
g_{j}(j-1)\neq 0
, then we can again use the similar argument of case I.b. First, we can first obtain a nontrivial
\phi^{-}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{-})
via
\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}
which satisfies (
70
). Then the same argument of case I.b would imply
\binom{g_{j-1}(j+1)}{g_{j-1}(j)}\neq\vec{0}
which can be used to generate a nontrivial
\phi^{s}
solving
J_{(-\infty,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
.

Case II.c.

g_{j}(j)=g_{j}(j-1)=0
and
g_{j}(j+1)\neq 0
, then we can first use
\binom{g_{j}(j+1)}{g_{j}(j)}
to generate a solution
\phi^{s}
of
J_{(-\infty,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
. Then we can show that

|g_{j+1}(j)|+|g_{j+1}(j-1)|\neq 0.

Indeed, otherwise we have
g_{j+1}(j)=g_{j}(j)=0
which together with (
65
) imply
g_{j-1}(j)\neq 0
. Then
\binom{g_{j-1}(j+1)}{g_{j-1}(j)}
can be used to generate another solution of
J_{(-\infty,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
which is linearly independent with
\phi^{s}
. This is impossible since the solution space of
J_{(-\infty,j_{0}]}\phi=E\phi
is one-dimensional. Hence
\binom{g_{j+1}(j)}{g_{j+1}(j-1)}
can be used to generate a nonzero
\phi^{-}
as described in (
70
).

To summarize, in case 2, we always obtain a nonzero
\phi^{s}
solving
J_{(-,j_{0}]}\phi^{s}=E\phi^{s}
and a nonzero
\phi^{-}
as described in (
70
). Now for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we consider
g_{j}(n)
for
n\leq j_{0}
. If
j>j_{0}
, then we must have

(72)

g_{j}(n)=0\mbox{ for all }n\leq j_{0}.

Otherwise,
((g_{j}(n))_{n\leq j_{0}},0,0,\ldots,)
will be nonzero
\ell^{2}
-solution of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
. If
j\leq j_{0}
, then we have

(73)

g_{j}(n)=\begin{cases}\phi^{-}_{j}(n),&n\leq j,\\
\phi_{j}^{s}(n),&j\leq n\leq{j_{0}}.\end{cases}

where
\phi_{j}^{s}\in\mathrm{span}\{\phi^{s}\}
and
\phi_{j}^{-}\in\mathrm{span}\{\phi^{-}\}
.

Case III.
There is a
j_{0}
such that
a_{j_{0}}=0
and
a_{n}\neq 0
for all
n>j_{0}
. We consider the operator
J_{(j_{0},+)}
. We fix a
j>j_{0}+1
. The discussion is completely analagou to case II. Roughly speaking, depending on which of
\{g_{j}(j-1),g_{j}(j),g_{j}(j+1)\}
is nonzero, we can have three different cases:

a.

g_{j}(j)\neq 0
or
g_{j}(j-1)g_{j}(j+1)\neq 0
;

b.

g_{j}(j)=g_{j}(j+1)=0
and
g_{j}(j-1)\neq 0
;

c.

g_{j}(j)=g_{j}(j-1)=0
and
g_{j}(j+1)\neq 0
.

In all these cases, we can obtain a nonzero solution
(\phi^{u}(j))_{j>j_{0}}
which solves
J_{(j_{0},\infty)}\phi=E\phi
via
\binom{g_{p}(j)}{g_{p}(j-1)}
where
p=j
or
j+1
. Such solution form a one-dimensional space as they can be uniquely determined by
\phi^{s}(j_{0}+1)
. Moreover, we can obtain a nonzero
(\phi^{+}(j))_{j>j_{0}}
via
\binom{g_{q}(j+1)}{g_{q}(j)}
where
q=j
or
j-1
such that

(74)

[(J_{(j_{0},+)}-E)\phi^{+}](j)=0\mbox{ for all }j>j_{0}+1\mbox{ and }\phi^{+}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+}).

By the computation of (
68
), we obtain the set of all such
\phi^{+}
form a one-dimensional space. Moreover,
\phi^{+}
cannot be a solution of
J_{(j_{0},+)}\phi=E\phi
. Otherwise, we can construct a
\ell^{2}
-solution
\phi=(\ldots,0,0,(\phi^{+}(j))_{n>j_{0}}
of
J_{a,b}\phi=E\phi
. That is, we must have

(75)

a_{j_{0}+2}\phi^{+}(j_{0}+2)+(b_{j_{0}+1}-E)\phi^{+}(j_{0}+1)\neq 0.

Then for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we consider
g_{j}(n)
for
n>j_{0}
. If
j\leq j_{0}
, we must have

(76)

g_{j}(n)=0\mbox{ for all }n>j_{0}.

If
j>j_{0}
, then we have

(77)

g_{j}(n)=\begin{cases}\phi^{+}_{j}(n),&n\geq j,\\
\phi_{j}^{u}(n),&j_{0}<n\leq j.\end{cases}

Case IV.
There is a
j_{1}<j_{2}
such that
a_{j_{1}}=a_{j_{2}}=0
and
a_{n}\neq 0
for all
j_{1}<n<j_{2}
if such
n
exists. Then we consider the operator
J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}
. It is clear that in this case, we have

(J_{a,b}-E)^{-1}=(J_{(-,j_{1}]}-E)^{-1}\oplus(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)^{-1}\oplus(J_{(j_{2},+)}-E)^{-1}

where
(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)^{-1}
is a finite matrix of order
j_{2}-j_{1}
. Then we have the following subcases.

Case IV.a.

j_{2}-j_{1}=1
, then it clearly holds that

(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)^{-1}=\frac{1}{b_{j_{2}}-E}.

Case IV.b.

j_{2}=j_{1}+2
, then it is clear that
(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)^{-1}
is a
2\times 2
bounded, invertible matrix which may be written as

\begin{pmatrix}g_{j_{1}+1}(j_{1}+1)&g_{j_{1}+2}(j_{1}+1)\\
g_{j_{1}+1}(j_{1}+2)&g_{j_{1}+2}(j_{1}+2)\end{pmatrix}.

It is clear what are
g_{j}(n)
for
j_{1}<n\leq j_{2}
and for all
j
in the two cases above.

Case IV.c
If
j_{2}>j_{1}+2
, then we want to explore a bit more the structure of
(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)^{-1}
. It is clear that if we start with

a_{j_{1}+1}\phi(j_{1}+2)+(b_{j_{1}+1}-E)\phi(j_{1}+1)=0,

then we can find a nonzero vector
(\phi^{(1)}(j))_{j\in(j_{1},j_{2}]}
such that
[(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)\phi^{(1)}](j)=0
for all
j_{1}<j<j_{2}
and

(78)

\overline{a_{j_{2}-1}}\phi^{(1)}(j_{2}-1)+(b_{j_{2}}-E)\phi^{(1)}(j_{2})\neq 0.

The set of such
\phi^{(1)}
is clearly a one-dimensional space. Similarly, if we start with

\overline{a_{j_{2}-1}}\phi(j_{2}-1)+(b_{j_{2}}-E)\phi(j_{2})=0,

then we can obtain a nonzero vector
(\phi^{(2)}(j))_{j\in(j_{1},j_{2}]}
such that

[(J_{(j_{1},j_{2}]}-E)\phi^{(2)}](j)=0
for all
j_{1}+1<j\leq j_{2}
and

(79)

a_{j_{1}+1}\phi^{(2)}(j_{1}+2)+(b_{j_{1}+1}-E)\phi^{(2)}(j_{1}+1)\neq 0.

The set of such
\phi^{(2)}
is a one-dimensional space. Thus we obtain the following information of
g_{j}
. If
j\leq j_{1}
and
j>j_{2}
, then it is clear that

g_{j}(n)=0\mbox{ for all }j_{1}<n\leq j_{2}.

If
j_{1}<j\leq j_{2}
, then it holds that

(80)

g_{j}(n)=\begin{cases}\phi^{(2)}_{j}(n),&j\leq n\leq j_{2},\\
\phi_{j}^{(1)}(n),&j_{1}<n\leq j.\end{cases}

where
\phi^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathrm{span}\{\phi^{(i)}\}
for
i=1,2
.

It is clear that cases I-IV together describe
g_{j}(n)
for all
j,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

4.3.
Construction of the dominated splitting

We begin this section by the following estimates which is the consequence of the classfications in Section
4.2
.

Lemma 9
.

There exists a
C=C(\delta,M)>0
such that for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, it holds that

(81)

\max\big\{|g_{j-1}(j)|,\ |g_{j-1}(j-1)|,\ |g_{j-2}(j)|,\ |g_{j-2}(j-1)|\big\}>C^{-1}\mbox{ and }

(82)

\max\left\{|g_{j}(j)|,\ |g_{j}(j-1)|,\ |g_{j+1}(j)|,\ |g_{j+1}(j-1)|\right\}>C^{-1}.

Proof.

We focus on the proof of (
81
) as the proof of (
82
) is completely analogous. By (
64
) and (
65
), we have for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
that

(83)

\overline{a_{j-2}}g_{j-1}(j-2)+a_{j-1}g_{j-1}(j)+(b_{j-1}-E)g_{j-1}(j-1)=1\mbox{ and }

(84)

\overline{a_{j-1}}g_{j}(j-1)+a_{j-2}g_{j-2}(j-1)+(b_{j-1}-E)g_{j-1}(j-1)=1.

Suppose (
81
) is not true. Then we may choose
{\varepsilon}>0
small and
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
so that

\max\big\{|g_{j-1}(j)|,\ |g_{j-1}(j-1)|,\ |g_{j-2}(j)|,\ |g_{j-2}(j-1)|\big\}<{\varepsilon},

which together with (
83
) and (
84
) implies

|\overline{a_{j-2}}g_{j-1}(j-2)|>\frac{1}{2}\mbox{ and }|\overline{a_{j-1}}g_{j}(j-1)|>\frac{1}{2}.

In particular, there is a
C=C(\delta,M)>0
so that

\min\big\{|a_{j-2}|,\ |a_{j-1}|,\ |g_{j-1}(j-2)|,\ |g_{j}(j-1)|\big\}>C^{-1}.

If we choose
{\varepsilon}>0
small enough, then

\displaystyle\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}g_{j-1}(j-1)&g_{j}(j-1)\\
g_{j-1}(j-2)&g_{j}(j-2)\end{pmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle=|g_{j-1}(j-1)g_{j}(j-2)-g_{j}(j-1)g_{j-1}(j-2)|

\displaystyle\geq|g_{j}(j-1)g_{j-1}(j-2)|-|g_{j-1}(j-1)g_{j}(j-2)|

\displaystyle\geq C^{-2}-{\varepsilon}\frac{2}{\delta}

\displaystyle>0.

Since
a_{j-1}a_{j-2}\neq 0
, we may use
\binom{g_{p}(j-1)}{g_{p}(j-2)}
as an initial condition to generate a
\phi_{p}
for
p=j
or
j-1
where
\phi_{j}
and
\phi_{j-1}
are linearly independent by the estimate above.

On the other hand, if
a_{n}\neq 0
for all
n<j-1
, then we are in case I or II of Section
4.2
. Thus, we have either
\phi_{p}=\phi^{u}_{p}
in case I or
\phi_{p}=\phi^{-}_{p}
in case II. If there is a
j_{1}<j-2
such that
a_{j_{1}}=0
and
a_{n}\neq 0
for all
j_{1}\leq n\leq j-1
, then we are in case III or IV.c of Section
4.2
. Thus, we have either
\phi_{p}=\phi^{u}_{p}
in case III or
\phi_{p}=\phi^{(1)}_{p}
in case IV.c. However, in all these cases
\phi_{j}
and
\phi_{j-1}
belong to a same one-dimensional space which implies they must be linear dependent, a contradiction.
∎

We define for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
a pair of vectors

\vec{s}(j):=\begin{cases}\dbinom{g_{j-1}(j)}{g_{j-1}(j-1)}&\mbox{ if }|g_{j-1}(j)|+|g_{j-1}(j-1)|>\frac{C^{-1}}{2};\\
\dbinom{g_{j-2}(j)}{g_{j-2}(j-1)}&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}

and

\vec{u}(j):=\begin{cases}\dbinom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}&\mbox{ if }|g_{j}(j)|+|g_{j}(j-1)|>\frac{C^{-1}}{2};\\
\dbinom{g_{j+1}(j)}{g_{j+1}(j-1)}&\mbox{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

By (
81
) and (
82
) and by choosing
C=C(M,\delta)>0
appropriately, we have

(85)

C^{-1}<\|\vec{s}(j)\|<C\mbox{ and }C^{-1}<\|\vec{u}(j)\|<C\mbox{ for all }j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Note by (
83
), it holds that

(86)

\vec{s}(j)=\binom{g_{j-1}(j)}{g_{j-1}(j-1)}\mbox{ if }a_{j-2}=0.

Similarly, by
\overline{a_{j-1}}g_{j}(j-1)+a_{j}g_{j}(j+1)+(b_{j}-E)g_{j}(j)=1
, we must have

(87)

\vec{u}(j)=\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}\mbox{ if }a_{j}=0.

Thus for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we may define two one-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
as

E^{s}(j)=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{s}(j)\}\in E^{s}(j)\mbox{ and }E^{u}(j)=\mathrm{span}\{\vec{u}(j)\}.

Since cases I-IV of Section
4.2
describe
g_{j}(n)
for all
j,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we can in pariticular have the following corollary which will be heavily used in the remain part of this section.

Corollary 3
.

Fix a
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
so that
a_{j-1}a_{j}\neq 0
. We define

j_{1}=\max\{n<j-1:a_{n}=0\}\mbox{ and }j_{2}=\min\{n\geq j:a_{n}=0\}.

Then, it holds that
g_{p}(n)=\phi^{\alpha}_{p}(n)
for all
p\leq n<j_{2}+1
and
g_{q}(n)=\phi^{\beta}_{q}(n)
for all
j_{1}<n\leq q
where
q=j-1
or
j-2
,
q=j
or
j+1
, and the choices of
{\alpha}
and
{\beta}
are as follows:

(1)

j_{1}=-\infty
and
j_{2}=\infty
: case I of Section
4.2
which implies
{\alpha}=s
and
{\beta}=u
.

(2)

j_{1}=-\infty
and
j_{2}<\infty
: case II of Section
4.2
which implies
{\alpha}=s
and
{\beta}=-
.

(3)

j_{1}>-\infty
and
j_{2}=\infty
: case III of Section
4.2
which implies
{\alpha}=+
and
{\beta}=u
.

(4)

j_{1}>-\infty
and
j_{2}<\infty
: case IV.c of Section
4.2
which implies
{\alpha}=(2)
and
{\beta}=(1)
.

Moreover, if
j_{1}=j-2
, then by (
86
) we have
p=j-1
. So it still holds that
g_{p}(n)=\phi^{\alpha}_{p}(n)
for all
p\leq n<j_{2}+1
. Similarly, if
j_{2}=j
, then (
87
) implies
q=j
and
g_{q}(n)=\phi^{\beta}_{q}(n)
for all
j_{1}<n\leq q
.

Now, we first show the invariance of
E^{s}
and
E^{u}
.

Lemma 10
.

E^{s}
and
E^{u}
are
B^{E}
–invariant. In other words, for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, it holds that

B^{E}(j)\cdot E^{u}(j)=E^{u}(j+1)\mbox{ and }B^{E}(j)\cdot E^{s}(j)\subseteq E^{s}(j+1).

Proof.

Fix a
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. First, we show
E^{s}(j)\subseteq E^{s}(j+1)
. It suffices to show
B^{E}(j)\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j+1)
. We divide the proof into the following three cases.

Case 1.s
. Assume
a_{j-1}a_{j}\neq 0
. By Corollary
3
, it holds that

B^{E}(j)\vec{s}(j)=\binom{\phi_{p}^{\alpha}(j)}{\phi_{p}^{\alpha}(j-1)}=a_{j}\binom{\phi_{p}^{\alpha}(j+1)}{\phi^{\alpha}_{p}(j)}\in E^{s}(j+1)

Case 2.s
. Assume
a_{j-1}=0
. Then
J_{a,b}=J_{(-,j-1]}\oplus J_{[j,+)}
. Thus we always have
g_{p}(j)=0
for
p=j
or
j-1
. In other words, we have

B^{E}(j)\vec{s}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}E-b_{j}&0\\
a_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}\binom{0}{g_{p}(j-1)}=\binom{0}{0}\in E^{s}(j+1).

Case 3.s
. Assume
a_{j}=0
. Then it always true that
\overline{a_{j-1}}g_{p}(j-1)+(E-b_{j})g_{p}(j)=0
for
p=j-1
or
j-2
. Thus, we have

B^{E}(j)\vec{s}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}E-b_{j}&-\overline{a_{j-1}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\binom{g_{p}(j)}{g_{p}(j-1)}=\binom{0}{0}\in E^{s}(j+1).

Next, we show
B^{E}(j)E^{u}(j)=E^{u}(j+1)
. Clearly, it suffices to show
\binom{0}{0}\neq B^{E}(j)\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j+1)
. We again divide the proof into three different cases.

Case 1.u
. Assume
a_{j-1}a_{j}\neq 0
. Then by Corollary
3
, it holds that

B^{E}(j)\vec{u}(j)=\binom{\phi_{q}^{\beta}(j)}{\phi_{q}^{\beta}(j-1)}=a_{j}\binom{\phi_{q}^{\beta}(j+1)}{\phi^{\beta}_{q}(j)}\in E^{u}(j+1).

It is clear that
B^{E}(j)\vec{u}(j)\neq\binom{0}{0}
since
\det(B^{E}(j))\neq 0
and
\vec{u}(j)\neq\binom{0}{0}
.

Case 2.u
. Assume
a_{j}=0
. Then by (
87
), we must have
\vec{u}(j)=\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}
.

B^{E}(j)\vec{u}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}E-b_{j}&-\overline{a_{j-1}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\binom{g_{j}(j)}{g_{j}(j-1)}=\binom{-\overline{a_{j-1}}g_{j}(j-1)+(E-b_{j})g_{j}(j)}{0}=\binom{1}{0}.

On the other hand,
J_{a,b}=J_{(-,j]}\oplus J_{(j,+)}
implies
g_{m}(j)=0
for
m>j
. Hence, we have

\vec{u}(j+1)=\binom{g_{q}(j+1)}{g_{q}(j)}=\binom{g_{q}(j+1)}{0}\neq\vec{0}

where
q=j+1
or
j+2
. Thus, it holds that
\vec{0}\neq B^{E}(j)\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j+1)
.

Case 3.u
. Assume
a_{j-1}=0
and
a_{j}\neq 0
. First,
J_{a,b}=J_{(-,j-1]}\oplus J_{[j,+)}
implies
g_{m}(j-1)=0
for
m\geq j
. Since
a_{j}\neq 0
, we must have

B^{E}(j)\vec{u}(j)=\begin{pmatrix}E-b_{j}&0\\
