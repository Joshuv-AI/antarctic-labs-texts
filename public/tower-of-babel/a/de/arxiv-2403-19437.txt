The largest- K K -norm for general measure spaces and a DC Reformulation for L 0 L^{0} -Constrained Problems in Function Spaces
Bastian Dittrich a , Daniel Wachsmuth a

Abstract
We consider constraints on the measure of the support for integrable functions on arbitrary measure spaces. It is shown that this non-convex and discontinuous constraint can be equivalently reformulated by the difference of two convex and continuous functions, namely the L 1 L^{1} -norm and the so-called largest- K K -norm. The largest- K K -norm is studied and its convex subdifferential is derived. An exemplary problem is solved by applying a DC method to the reformulated problem.

Introduction

We are interested in solving optimal control problems with constraints on the measure of the support of the control.
These constraints are formulated in terms of the so-called
L^{0}
-norm, which is defined by

\|u\|_{0}:=\mu(\operatorname{supp}(u))=\mu\left(\{x\in\Omega\mid u(x)\neq 0\}\right),

where
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable, and
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a measure space.
Optimal control problems that take the size of the support of the control into account were studied
for instance in
[
12
,
15
,
21
,
25
,
27
]
.
Due to the lack of weakly lower semicontinuity of
u\mapsto\|u\|_{0}
in
L^{p}
-spaces, existence of solutions
of problems involving
\|u\|_{0}
in an
L^{p}
-setting cannot be proven. Nevertheless, optimality conditions
in terms of Pontryagin’s maximum principle are available
[
12
,
27
]
.
In addition, algorithms based on topological derivatives are feasible
[
15
,
21
,
26
]
.

In this paper, we will investigate problems with constraints on
\|u\|_{0}

in spaces that are compactly embedded into
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
As an example, the following problem will be studied

\displaystyle\min_{u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}^{2}-\left\langle g,u\right\rangle\quad\text{ subject to}\quad\|u\|_{0}\leq K,

(1.1)

where
K\in(0,\mu(\Omega))
, and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded domain.
In this setting, we can prove existence of solutions.
Such a formulation has been used in
[
3
]
in connection to shape optimization problems.
However, neither the Pontryagin’s maximum principle nor the above mentioned optimization methods are applicable.
For the sake of general validity, we will work in this paper with the following abstract optimization problem

\min_{u\in X}f(u)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|u\|_{0}\leq K,

where
X
is compactly embedded into
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, and
f:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is convex and lower semicontinuous.
In order to solve such a problem numerically we follow the ideas of
[
9
]
, where optimization problems in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with cardinality constraints are studied.
They proposed a penalization scheme based on the largest-
K
-norm, which in the case of a general measure space can be defined as

|u|_{K}:=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}:\>\mu(A)\leq K}\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu.

Instead of the cardinality constrained problem, a sequence of penalized problems

\min_{u\in X}f(u)+\rho\left(\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K}\right),\quad\rho>0,

are solved, where
\rho>0
is a penalization parameter.
The penalized problems have a particular structure: the objective functional is a difference of convex functions (DC-function),
which enables the use of specialized optimization methods
[
18
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
After presenting some important preliminary results in
Section

2
, the largest-
K
-norm is studied in detail in
Section

3
.
This is at first done for the case of an atom-free and
\sigma
-finite measure space, and afterwards for a general
\sigma
-finite measure space.
This is due to the fact that atom-free measure spaces, like the Lebesgue space, allow to prove more elegant properties, e.g., a complete characterization of the convex subdifferential.
Nevertheless, the equivalent reformulation of the
L^{0}
-constraint

\|u\|_{0}\leq K\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K}=0

can be shown to hold true for every
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and every
\sigma
-finite measure space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)
, see
Theorem

3.22
.

Next we prove convergence results for the DC-Algorithm in general Banach spaces in
Section

4
, including an inexact convergence result.

Section

5
is then devoted to the
L^{0}
-constrained problem.
We start by showing existence of solutions for the original as well as the penalized problem and prove a classical penalty method result.
In the second part we investigate optimality conditions of the penalized problem and generalize the classical penalty method result to sequences of critical points instead of global minima.

Lastly, the goal of
Section

6
is to numerically solve a specific
L^{0}
-constrained problem, namely (
1.1
).
Reformulating and penalizing this specific problem follows the ideas of the previous sections based on the largest-
K
-norm.
We report about the numerical results in
Section

6.5
.

Notation

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space.
The
L^{p}(\mathrm{\Omega})
-norm of a measurable function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\|u\|_{p,\mu}:=\left(\int|u|^{p}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\right)^{1/p}.

The
L^{0}
-norm will be denoted by

\|u\|_{0,\mu}:=\mu(\operatorname{supp}(u)).

With the notation

|u|_{0}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }u\neq 0\\
0&\text{ if }u=0\\
\end{cases}

we can write

\|u\|_{0,\mu}=\int|u(x)|_{0}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu(x).

Given
K\in(0,\mu(\Omega))
the largest-
K
-norm of
u
is defined by

|u|_{K,\mu}:=\sup\left\{\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\mid A\in\mathcal{A},\ \mu(A)\leq K\right\}.

We will omit the subscript
\mu
if the measure is clear from the context.

2
Preliminary results

2.1
DC reformulation in finite dimensions

First, let us recall the definition of DC functions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a vector space,
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
a mapping. Then
f
is called DC function (difference-of-convex function) if there are convex functions
f_{1}:X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
and
f_{2}:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f=f_{1}-f_{2}
.

Let us briefly review the developments of
[
9
]
.
There, the cardinality-constrained problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|x\|_{0}\leq K,\>x\in S

(2.1)

is studied, where

\|x\|_{0}:=\left|\{i\in\{1,...,n\}\mid x_{i}\neq 0\}\right|

is the cardinality of the support of
x
,

K\in\{1,...,n\}
is given,

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a DC function, and
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex set.
Based on the largest-
K
-norm

|x|_{K}:=|x_{(1)}|+\ldots+|x_{(K)}|=\max_{I\subseteq\{1,...,n\}:|I|\leq K}\sum_{i\in I}|x_{i}|,

where
x_{(i)}
is the
i
-th largest entry of
x
in absolute value, they consider the problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|x\|_{1}-|x|_{K}=0,\>x\in S,

which is equivalent to (
2.1
)
due to the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
[
9
, Theorem 1]
)
For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and arbitrary integers
K,h
with
1\leq K<h\leq n
the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\|x\|_{0}\leq K
,

2.

|x|_{h}-|x|_{K}=0
,

3.

\|x\|_{1}-|x|_{K}=0
.

Based on this result, the associated penalized problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in S}f(x)+\rho\left(\|x\|_{1}-|x|_{K}\right),\quad\rho>0,

(2.2)

is used as an approximation.
Since
x\mapsto|x|_{K}
is convex, the objective functional in (
2.2
)
is a DC function.
Then the penalized problems can be solved by a DC algorithm in order to solve the original problem (
2.1
).
For the convergence of the penalty method, they have the following result.

Theorem 2.3
.

(
[
9
, Theorem 2]
)
Let
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}<...
with
\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho_{t}=\infty
and suppose that
x^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a global minimizer of the penalized problem (
2.2
) with penalty parameter
\rho=\rho_{t}
. Then every limit point
x^{*}
of the sequence
(x^{t})
is a global solution of the cardinality-constrained problem (
2.1
).

Moreover, in the case of
f
being continuously differentiable with Lipschitz-continuous gradient and, e.g.,
S=\mathbb{R}^{n}
they provide an exact penalty result
[see
9
, Theorem 3]
.
Unfortunately, we were not able to translate this result to the infinite dimensional setting.
However, the exact penalty result
[
9
, Theorem 4, Corollary 4]
for
f
being a quadratic function can be generalized to our discretized problem, see
Section

6.2
.

2.2
Atom-free measure spaces

Let us recall the definition and basic properties of atom-free measure spaces.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space.
A set
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)>0
is called atom, if every set
B\in\mathcal{A}
with
B\subseteq A
either satisfies
\mu(B)=0
or
\mu(A\setminus B)=0
.
The measure space
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
is called atom-free if there are no atoms.
The measure space
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
is called purely atomic if for every
B\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(B)>0
there exists an atom
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
A\subseteq B
.

These definitions are consistent with
[
6
, 211I-K]
.
We will frequently use the following results.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be an atom-free measure space.
Then for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)<\infty
and
t\in[0,\mu(A)]
there exists a set
B\in\mathcal{A}
with
B\subseteq A
and
\mu(B)=t
.

Proof.

[See
6
, 215D]
. ∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be an atom-free and
\sigma
-finite measure space.
Then for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
t\in[0,\mu(A)]
there exists a set
B\in\mathcal{A}
with
B\subseteq A
and
\mu(B)=t
.

Proof.

We only have to consider the case
t<\mu(A)=\infty
.
Let
\mathrm{\Omega}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{\Omega}_{n}
be a decomposition of
\mathrm{\Omega}
in pairwise disjoint sets
\mathrm{\Omega}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
with
0<\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n})<\infty
.
Since
t<\infty
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t\leq\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n}\cap A)\right)<\infty
.
Applying
Theorem

2.5
to the set
\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n}\cap A)
then shows the claim.
∎

3
The Largest-
K
-norm

In this section, we will investigate basic properties of the largest-
K
-norm.

Definition 3.1

(largest-
K
-norm)
.

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space.
For every
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in[0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})]
we define

|u|_{K}:=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}:\>\mu(A)\leq K}\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu.

Clearly,
|\cdot|_{K}\equiv 0
for
K=0
and
|\cdot|_{K}=\|\cdot\|_{1}
for
K=\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
In addition,
K\mapsto|u|_{K}
is monotonically increasing for fixed
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
As
|\cdot|_{K}
is the supremum of convex and lower semicontinuous functions it is convex and lower semicontinuous.

The connection to the
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
-norm directly implies continuity.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space and
K\in[0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})]
.
Then the function
u\mapsto|u|_{K}
is a continuous semi-norm on
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
|\cdot|_{K}
satisfies the triangle inequality and is absolutely homogeneous.
Let
(u_{n})\subseteq L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
with
\|u_{n}-u\|_{1}\to 0
.
Then

||u_{n}|_{K}-|u|_{K}|\leq|u_{n}-u|_{K}\leq\|u_{n}-u\|_{1},

which shows the claim.
∎

The main goal of this section is to show that the supremum in
Definition

3.1
is attained, which is surprisingly difficult.
First, we state a sufficient condition that a set
A
realizes the supremum.
As it turns out, upper level sets play an important role for the largest-
K
-norm.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
. Define for every
t\in[0,\infty)
the sets

\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}:=\{x\in\mathrm{\Omega}\mid|u(x)|>t\},\quad\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}:=\{x\in\mathrm{\Omega}\mid|u(x)|\geq t\}.

Then the function
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t})
is monotonically decreasing and continuous from the right, and the function
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t})
is monotonically decreasing and continuous from the left.

Proof.

First, note that for any
s,t\in[0,\infty)
with
s<t
it holds
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{>s}
and
\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq s}
.
Let
t\in[0,\infty)
,
s\in(0,\infty)
. Take sequences
(s_{n})
and
(t_{n})
with
t_{n}\searrow t
and
s_{n}\nearrow s
.
W.l.o.g. we can assume that the sequences
(t_{n})
and
(s_{n})
are monotone and
s_{1}>0
.
Then the claim follows directly from the continuity of the measure from above and below since

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t_{n}},\qquad\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq s}=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq s_{n}},

where we have used that
\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq s_{1}})\leq s_{1}^{-1}\|u\|_{1}<\infty
.
∎

Now we are in the position to prove a sufficient condition that a given set
A
realizes the supremum in the definition of the largest-
K
-norm.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
be given.
Suppose there exist
t\geq 0
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}
and
\mu(A)=K
.
Then we have
|u|_{K}=\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
.

Proof.

Let
t\geq 0
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}
and
\mu(A)=K
.
Take
B\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(B)\leq K
.
Then we have

\begin{split}\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu-\int_{B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu&=\int_{A\setminus B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu-\int_{B\setminus A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\\
&\geq t(\mu(A\setminus B)-\mu(B\setminus A))\\
&=t(\mu(A)-\mu(A\cap B)-(\mu(B)-\mu(A\cap B)))\\
&=t(K-\mu(B))\geq 0.\end{split}

This proves
\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}
.
∎

We start our investigation with the special case of an atom-free and
\sigma
-finite measure space.
Later on, we will generalize the results to a general
\sigma
-finite measure space.
Most of the following results hold trivially for
K\in\{0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})\}
. Hence, we only consider
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
.

3.1
The Atom-Free Case

In this section let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be an atom-free and
\sigma
-finite measure space.
Then there are pairwise disjoint sets
(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n})
with
\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n})<\infty
and
\mathrm{\Omega}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{\Omega}_{n}
.

For such a measure space,
u\mapsto|u|_{K}
is a norm.

Theorem 3.5
.

For every
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
the function
|\cdot|_{K}:L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\to\mathbb{R}
is a norm on the space
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.2

|\cdot|_{K}
is already a semi-norm.
Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
and take
u\neq 0
in
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
. Then there exists a set
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)>0
and
u(x)\neq 0
a.e. on
A
.
Due to
Corollary

2.6
we can choose
B\subseteq A
such that
0<\mu(B)\leq K
.
It follows
|u|_{K}\geq\int_{B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu>0
.
This shows that
|\cdot|_{K}
is a norm on
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
∎

We will now use the upper level sets to show that a set satisfying the sufficient condition of
Lemma

3.4
exists, and hence
the supremum in the definition of the largest-
K
-norm is always attained.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
be given. Then there are
t\geq 0
and
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}
,
\mu(A)=K
, and
|u|_{K}=\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
.
Here,
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}
and
\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}
are defined as in
Lemma

3.3
.

Proof.

First, let us observe that
\mu(\Omega_{\geq 0})=\mu(\Omega)
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu(\Omega_{\geq t})=0
.
Since
s\mapsto\mu(\Omega_{\geq s})
is monotonically decreasing and continuous from the left by
Lemma

3.3
,
t:=\sup\{s\geq 0\mid\mu(\Omega_{\geq s})\geq K\}
is well-defined and it holds

\mu\left(\Omega_{\geq t+\varepsilon}\right)<K\leq\mu\left(\Omega_{\geq t}\right)\quad\forall\varepsilon>0.

Since
\mu\left(\Omega_{>t}\right)=\mu\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{\geq t+\frac{1}{k}}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu\left(\Omega_{\geq t+\frac{1}{n}}\right)
, it follows

\mu\left(\Omega_{>t}\right)\leq K\leq\mu\left(\Omega_{\geq t}\right).

Due to
Corollary

2.6
, we can choose
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mu(A)=K
and
\Omega_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\Omega_{\geq t}
.
Then the claim follows with
Lemma

3.4
.
∎

This enables us to prove the key result for the reformulation of a
L^{0}
-constrained problem by the largest-
K
-norm.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Then the following four statements are equivalent:

1.

\|u\|_{0}\leq K
.

2.

\|u\|_{1}=|u|_{K}
,

3.

|u|_{H}=|u|_{K}
for all
H\in(K,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})]
,

4.

|u|_{h}=|u|_{K}
for one
h\in(K,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})]
.

Proof.

(
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
):
Let
A:=\operatorname{supp}(u)
. Then
\mu(A)\leq K
and
|u|_{K}\geq\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\|u\|_{1}\geq|u|_{K}
.
(
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
): This follows from the monotonicity of
H\mapsto|u|_{H}
and
\|u\|_{1}=|u|_{\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})}
.
(
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
4
) is trivial.

(
4
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
):
Let
A
be given by
Theorem

3.6
, i.e.,

\mu(A)\leq K
and
\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}
.
By replacing
A
with
A\cap\operatorname{supp}u
, we can assume
A\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u
.
Let
B
be given such that
A\subseteq B\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u
and
\mu(B)\leq h
.
Then
|u|_{h}\geq\int_{B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\geq\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}=|u|_{h}
,
which implies
u=0
a.e. on
B\setminus A
.
Due to the inclusion
B\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u
it follows
\mu(B\setminus A)=0
and
\mu(B)=\mu(A)\leq K
.
Since
\mu
is atom-free,
all subsets of
\operatorname{supp}u
have measure
\leq K
, and
\|u\|_{0}\leq K
follows.
∎

.

In
Theorem

3.7
only the implication (

)

(

) needed that the measure space is atom-free.

3.1.1
The Convex Subdifferential

Let us now investigate the subdifferential of
u\mapsto|u|_{K}
.
The first step is to prove that the supremum in the definition of
|\cdot|_{K}
can be written as the
supremum of a linear functional on a convex set.
We start with the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
be given. Let us define

D:=\left\{d\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid d\geq 0,\,\|d\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\,\|d\|_{1}\leq K\right\}.

Then
D
is convex and it holds

\ext(D)=\left\{\chi_{A}\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid A\in\mathcal{A},\,\mu(A)\leq K\right\},

where
\ext(D)
denotes the set of extreme points of
D
.

Proof.

Obviously,
D
is convex.
Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)\leq K
, which implies
\chi_{A}\in D
.
Let
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
be given such that
\lambda d_{1}+(1-\lambda)d_{2}=\chi_{A}
.
Then, for almost all
x\in\mathrm{\Omega}
we have
d_{1}(x),d_{2}(x)\in[0,1]
and
\chi_{A}(x)\in\ext([0,1])
.
It follows
\chi_{A}=d_{1}=d_{2}
almost everywhere, and hence
\chi_{A}
is an extreme point of
D
.

Now let
d\in D\setminus\{\chi_{A}\mid A\in\mathcal{A},\,\mu(A)\leq K\}
.
Then
d
cannot be a characteristic function.
Therefore, there exist a set
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
0<\mu(A)<\infty
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon<d<1-\varepsilon
a.e. on
A
.
Since
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
is atom-free there exists a set
B\subseteq A
with
\mu(B)=\frac{1}{2}\mu(A)
.
We define

d_{1}:=d+\varepsilon(\chi_{B}-\chi_{A\setminus B}),\quad d_{2}:=d+\varepsilon(\chi_{A\setminus B}-\chi_{B}).

Then it holds
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
,
\frac{1}{2}d_{1}+\frac{1}{2}d_{2}=d
, and
d_{1}\neq d\neq d_{2}
, which proves
d\notin\ext(D)
.
∎

The supremum in
Definition

3.1
of the largest-
K
-norm can be taken over the whole set
D
instead of just the characteristic functions without changing the result.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
,
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Let
D
be as in
Lemma

3.9
. Then it holds

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}:\mu(A)\leq K}\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}=\sup_{d\in D}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu.

Both suprema are attained.
Moreover, if
\|u\|_{0}\geq K
and
d\in D
attains the supremum then it holds
\|d\|_{1}=K
.

Proof.

Let
t
and
A
be as in
Theorem

3.6
,
i.e.,
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}
with
\mu(A)=K
and
|u|_{K}=\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
.
Define the sets
\mathrm{\Omega}_{<t}
and
\mathrm{\Omega}_{=t}
analogously to
Lemma

3.3
.

Let
d\in D
be given. Using the properties of
d
and
A
, we find

\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu-|u|_{K}=\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}(d-\chi_{A})|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\\
\begin{aligned} &=\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}}(d-1)|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{=t}}(d-\chi_{A})|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{<t}}(d-0)|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\\
&\leq t\left(\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}}(d-1)\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{=t}}(d-\chi_{A})\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{<t}}d\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\right)\\
&=t(\|d\|_{1}-\mu(A))\leq 0.\end{aligned}

(3.1)

This proves

|u|_{K}\geq\sup_{d\in D}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu.

Since for
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)\leq K
we have
\chi_{A}\in D
, the reverse inequality follows.

Let
\|u\|_{0}\geq K
.
Let the supremum be attained in
d\in D
.
The inequalities in (
3.1
) are satisfied with equality if and only if

d=1
a.e. on
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}
,
d=0
a.e. on
\mathrm{\Omega}_{<t}
, and
t(\|d\|_{1}-K)=0
.
If
t>0
then we get
\|d\|_{1}=K
immediately.
If
t=0
then these conditions imply
\|d\|_{1}\geq\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{>0})=\|u\|_{0}\geq K
.
Since
d\in D
, it follows
\|d\|_{1}=K
.
∎

The proof implies that all sets which attain the supremum in the largest-
K
-norm satisfy, up to null sets, the sufficient condition from
Lemma

3.4
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
,
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
and
t\geq 0
from
Theorem

3.6
.
If
\|u\|_{0}\geq K
and
B
is a set with
\mu(B)\leq K
and
\int_{B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}
then
\mu(B)=K
and
\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\setminus B)=0=\mu(B\setminus\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t})
.

Proof.

Theorem

3.10
directly implies
\mu(B)=K
and the corresponding proof shows
\chi_{B}=1
a.e. on
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}
and
\chi_{B}=0
a.e. on
\mathrm{\Omega}_{<t}
which is the claim.
∎

This characterization by
Theorem

3.10
moreover has the following two consequences.
First, in the case of a finite measure space, we have that the largest-
K
-norm is an equivalent norm on
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let the measure space
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be finite and atom-free.
Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Then it holds

\frac{K}{\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})}\|u\|_{1}\leq|u|_{K}\leq\|u\|_{1}\quad\forall u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),

i.e.,
|\cdot|_{K}
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
are equivalent norms for
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

3.5

|\cdot|_{K}
is a norm on
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Define
d:=\frac{K}{\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})}\chi_{\mathrm{\Omega}}\in D
. We then have for every
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
that

\frac{K}{\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})}\|u\|_{1}=\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq\sup_{d\in D}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}.

∎

Note that setting
u=\chi_{\mathrm{\Omega}}
and
u=\chi_{A}
for some
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)=K
shows that these two inequalities are sharp.
A second consequence of
Theorem

3.10

is the following generalization of the Hölder inequality.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
,
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Then it holds

\int_{\Omega}u\cdot v\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq|u|_{K}\cdot\max\left(\|v\|_{\infty},K^{-1}\|v\|_{1}\right).

Proof.

This follows directly from
Theorem

3.10
, since for
v\neq 0
we have

\left(\max\left(\|v\|_{\infty},K^{-1}\|v\|_{1})\right)\right)^{-1}|v|\in D.

∎

Moreover, using
Theorem

3.10
we can fully characterize the convex subdifferential.
We need the following well-known fact, see, e.g.,
[
1
, Proposition 16.18]
for a similar result.
We present its short proof for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
X
be a normed space and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and positively homogeneous, i.e.,

f(\lambda x)=\lambda f(x)
for all
x\in X
and all
\lambda\geq 0
.
Then for every
x\in X
it holds

\partial f(x)=\left\{s\in\partial f(0)\mid\langle s,x\rangle=f(x)\right\}.

Proof.

Let
s\in\partial f(0)
with
\langle s,x\rangle=f(x)
, then

f(y)-f(x)\geq\langle s,y\rangle-\langle s,x\rangle=\langle s,y-x\rangle

for every
y\in X
, i.e.,
s\in\partial f(x)
.
Now let
s\in\partial f(x)
, i.e.,

f(y)-f(x)\geq\langle s,y-x\rangle\quad\forall y\in X.

Setting
y=0
and
y=2x
implies
f(x)\leq\langle s,x\rangle
and
f(x)\geq\langle s,x\rangle
, respectively, which shows
\langle s,x\rangle=f(x)
.
Inserting this in the above inequality yields

f(y)\geq\langle s,y\rangle\quad\forall y\in X,

i.e.,
s\in\partial f(0)
.
∎

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
0\neq u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)

\displaystyle=\left\{s\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid\|s\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\,\|s\|_{1}\leq K\right\},

\displaystyle\partial|\cdot|_{K}(u)

\displaystyle=\left\{s\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid\max\left(\|s\|_{\infty},\,K^{-1}\|s\|_{1}\right)=1,\,\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}su\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}\right\}.

Proof.

Since
u\mapsto|u|_{K}
is convex and positively homogeneous, it follows

\partial|\cdot|_{K}(u)=\left\{s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)\mid\ \int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}su\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}\right\}

by
Lemma

3.14
. Therefore, it suffices to compute
\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)
.

Let
s\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
be given with
\|s\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\|s\|_{1}\leq K
.
Then
Corollary

3.13
implies
\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}s\cdot v\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq|v|_{K}
for all
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, i.e.,
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)
.
Now let
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)
.
This is equivalent to

\int_{\Omega}s\cdot v\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq|v|_{K}

for all
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Suppose
\|s\|_{\infty}>1
, take
A\subseteq\{x\in\Omega\mid|s(x)|>1\}
such that
\mu(A)\in(0,K)
and set
v=\chi_{A}\sign(s)
. Then
\int_{\Omega}s\cdot v\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu>\mu(A)=|v|_{K}
.
Hence
\|s\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Let
v_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sign(s)\chi_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{k}}\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, where
(\mathrm{\Omega}_{k})
is defined at the beginning of
Section

3.1
.
Then the sequence
(sv_{n})
is non-negative and converges pointwise a.e. to
|s|
.
Fatou’s Lemma implies

\|s\|_{1}=\int|s|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int sv_{n}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}|v_{n}|_{K}\leq K.

Let
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(u)
.
Then
s\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
with
\int su\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}
and

Corollary

3.13
implies that
\max\left(\|s\|_{\infty},\,K^{-1}\|s\|_{1}\right)=1
, which shows the claim.
∎

3.1.2
The Dual Norm

In the atom-free case the largest-
K
-norm is in fact a norm by
Theorem

3.5
and for a finite measure space by
Corollary

3.12
even equivalent to the
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
-norm.
For
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
the dual norm

|s|_{K}^{\prime}:=\sup_{u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}):|u|_{K}\leq 1}\left|\langle s,u\rangle\right|

on
(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}
is then characterized by the following result.
In the finite dimensional setting this was already proven in
[
29
]
.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
.
Then
(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}=L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
for every
s\in(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}
it holds

|s|_{K}^{\prime}=\max\left(\|s\|_{\infty},K^{-1}\|s\|_{1}\right).

Proof.

Since
|u|_{K}\leq\|u\|_{1}
for all
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
it follows
(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}\subseteq(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),\|\cdot\|_{1})^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Let
s\in(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}
with
|s|_{K}^{\prime}\leq 1
.
Then
s\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and it follows

\int sv\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\langle s,v\rangle\leq|s|_{K}^{\prime}|v|_{K}\leq|v|_{K}

for all
v\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, i.e.,
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)
.
Now let
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)
.
Then
s\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
by
Theorem

3.15
and
Corollary

3.13
implies

|\langle s,v\rangle|=\left|\int sv\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\right|\leq\int s\sign(s)|v|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\leq|v|_{K}\cdot\max\left(\|s\|_{\infty},K^{-1}\|s\|_{1}\right)

for all
v\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Hence,
s\in(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}
with
|s|_{K}^{\prime}\leq\max\left(\|s\|_{\infty},K^{-1}\|s\|_{1}\right)\leq 1
by
Theorem

3.15
.
This proves

\left\{s\in(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}\mid|s|_{K}^{\prime}\leq 1\right\}=\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)

and in particular
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\subseteq(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}
.

Using the result of
Theorem

3.15
, we get

\begin{split}|s|_{K}^{\prime}&=\inf\{t>0\mid|s|_{K}^{\prime}\leq t\}\\
&=\inf\{t>0\mid t^{-1}s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)\}\\
&=\inf\{t>0\mid\|s\|_{\infty}\leq t,\,\|s\|_{1}\leq tK\}\\
&=\max\left(\|s\|_{\infty},K^{-1}\|s\|_{1}\right),\end{split}

for any
s\in(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}
and in particular
(L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}),|\cdot|_{K})^{\prime}\subseteq L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, which shows the claim.
∎

Using this characterization together with
[
24
, Proposition 4.6.2]
for convex subdifferentials of norms reproduces our elementarily proven
Theorem

3.15
.

3.2
The General Case

In this section let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be an arbitrary
\sigma
-finite measure space, and let

\mathrm{\Omega}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{\Omega}_{n}
with
(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n})
pairwise disjoint and
\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{n})<\infty
its decomposition.
Due to
[
14
]
, we can decompose the measure
\mu
into an atom-free and a purely atomic part.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space.
Then there are measures
\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}
such that
\mu=\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}+\mu_{\mathrm{af}}
, where
\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}
is purely atomic, and
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}
is atom-free.
These measures are mutually singular (
\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}\perp\mu_{\mathrm{af}}
), i.e.,
there exists
Y\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}(A)=\mu(A\cap Y)
and
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(A)=\mu(A\setminus Y)
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
In addition,
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(A)=0
for all atoms
A\in\mathcal{A}
of
\mu
.

Proof.

The decomposition was constructed in
[
14
, Corollary 2.6]
in a more general setting, while the mutual singularity is a consequence of
\sigma
-finiteness and
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(A)>0
. Then there is
B\subseteq A
with
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(B)\in(0,\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(A))
.
It follows
\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}(B)\in[0,\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}(A)]
and
\mu(B)\in(0,\mu(A))
, and
A
is not an atom of
\mu
.
∎

Obviously,
|\cdot|_{K}
is not a norm in general.
If there is an atom
X\in\mathcal{A}
of
\mu
with
\mu(X)>K
then
|\chi_{X}|_{K}=0
.

The goal of this section is to generalize the results of the atom-free case.
We will prove the existence of a set that attains the supremum in the definition of
|\cdot|_{K}
.
In addition, we will prove that the reformulation of the
L^{0}
-constraint is applicable in the general case.

Moreover, we also give some counter examples to results from
Section

3.1
, which are no longer valid. These counterexamples will be based on the following setup.

Example 3.18
.

We choose

\mathrm{\Omega}:=(0,1)\cup(1,3)\cup(3,6),\quad\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}\left(\mathcal{L}((0,1))\cup\{(1,3),(3,6)\}\right),

so that
\mathcal{A}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{(1,3),(3,6)\}
and the Lebesgue measurable subsets of
(0,1)
.
In addition, we take
\mu
to be the Lebesgue measure.
The resulting measure space is separable and finite, but neither atom-free nor purely atomic.
Define the function

u:=4\left(\chi_{(0,1)}+\chi_{(1,3)}\right)+3\chi_{(3,6)}.

For
K:=4
we then obviously have

|u|_{K}=\int_{(0,1)\cup(3,6)}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=13.

Note that for all the following counter examples a purely atomic measure space generated by three atoms, e.g.,
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}\left(\{(0,1),(1,3),(3,6)\}\right)
, would be sufficient.
Nevertheless, we use this example to demonstrate that even if the measure space contains an atom-free part
some results from
Section

3.1
are not true in a general setting.
∎

Since the measure space is
\sigma
-finite, we have
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})=L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})^{\prime}
.
The set
D
from
Lemma

3.9
is compact in the weak-* topology on
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
be given.
Then the set

D=\left\{d\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid d\geq 0,\,\|d\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\,\|d\|_{1}\leq K\right\}

from
Lemma

3.9
is weak-* compact in
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Proof.

We can write

D=\{d\geq 0\}\cap\{\|d\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}\cap\{\|d\|_{1}\leq K\}=:D_{1}\cap D_{2}\cap D_{3}.

The closed unit ball
D_{2}
is known to be weak-* compact by Banach-Alaoglu
[
2
, Theorem 3.16]
.
Therefore, it suffices to show that
D
is weak-* closed.

For any
v\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
define the function
f_{v}:L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\to\mathbb{R}
by
f_{v}(d):=\int dv\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
.
Then, since
f_{v}
is weak-* continuous for every
v\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, and

D_{1}=\bigcap_{v\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega}):v\geq 0}f_{v}^{-1}([0,\infty)),\quad D_{3}=\bigcap_{v\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega}):\|v\|_{\infty}\leq 1}f_{v}^{-1}([-K,K]),

this shows that
D_{1}
and
D_{3}
and therefore
D
is weak-* closed.
∎

This result enables us to prove the existence of a set that attains the supremum in the largest-
K
-norm.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
be given.
Then there exists a set
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)\leq K
and
|u|_{K}=\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
.

Proof.

Let

C:=\left\{\chi_{A}\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid A\in\mathcal{A},\,\mu(A)\leq K\right\}.

Let
\overline{C}^{*}
denote the weak-* closure of
C
in
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Due to
C\subseteq D
, the set
\overline{C}^{*}
is weak-* compact by
Lemma

3.19
.
Since the functional
f\mapsto\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
is weak-* continuous, we have

|u|_{K}=\sup_{f\in C}\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\sup_{f\in\overline{C}^{*}}\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu

and hence there exists
f\in\overline{C}^{*}\subseteq D
, which attains the supremum.

Let
X\subseteq\Omega
be an atom of
\mu
. Define the weak-* continuous functional
g:L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\to\mathbb{R}
by
g(f):=\int_{X}f\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
.
For any
\chi_{A}\in C
it holds
g(\chi_{A})=\mu(A\cap X)\in\{0,\mu(X)\}
and hence the continuity of
g
implies

g\left(\overline{C}^{*}\right)\subseteq\overline{g(C)}=\{0,\mu(X)\}.

It follows
\int_{X}f\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\in\{0,\mu(X)\}
, so that
f|_{X}=0
or
f|_{X}=1

\mu
-a.e. on
X
.
Hence, there is
B\subseteq Y
, with
Y
from
Proposition

3.17
, such that
f=\chi_{B}

\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}
-almost everywhere. Due to
Proposition

3.17
, we have
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(B)=0
.

Since the measure
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}
is atom-free, we can apply
Theorem

3.6
and
Theorem

3.10
.
Let us set
K^{\prime}:=\int f\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{af}}
so that
K^{\prime}\in[0,K]
.
Then there is
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu_{\mathrm{af}}(A)\leq K^{\prime}
such that

\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{af}}\geq\int g|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{af}}

for all
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
g\geq 0
,
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\int g\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{af}}\leq K^{\prime}
.
Due to
Proposition

3.17
, we can assume
\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}(A)=0
.
This implies
\mu(A\cup B)\leq K
and
\mu(A\cap B)=0
.
In addition, we get

\int_{A\cup B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{af}}+\int_{B}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}\geq\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{af}}+\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu_{\mathrm{pa}}=\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K},

which is the claim.
∎

The following example shows that there is not always a set which has both or even only one of the sufficient properties of
Lemma

3.4
and attains the supremum in
|\cdot|_{K}
.

Example 3.21
.

In the setup of
Example

3.18
we have

\displaystyle\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t})

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})=6>K\quad\text{for all }t<3,

\displaystyle\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t})

\displaystyle=\mu((0,1)\cup(1,3))=3<K\quad\text{for all }4\geq t>3,

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Omega}_{>3}

\displaystyle=(0,1)\cup(1,3),

\displaystyle\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq 3}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\Omega}.

Therefore, there can only exist a set
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}
with
\mu(A)=K
for
t=3
.
In this case no such set
A
exists, since the only possible sets are
(0,1)\cup(1,3)
and
\mathrm{\Omega}
.

Moreover, the supremum is not even attained by a feasible upper level set since

\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}_{>3}}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=12<13=|u|_{K}.

Furthermore, if we choose
K=4.5
the supremum is still exclusively attained by
(0,1)\cup(3,6)
, i.e., for a set
A
with
\mu(A)<K
.
∎

As already mentioned in
Remark

3.8
, the proof for the equivalent reformulations of the
L^{0}
-constraint only required the measure space to be atom-free in one step.
Therefore, the reformulation as
\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K}=0
is still possible in the general case.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\|u\|_{0}\leq K
,

2.

\|u\|_{1}=|u|_{K}
,

3.

|u|_{H}=|u|_{K}
for all
H\in(K,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})]
.

In addition, these statements imply

4.

|u|_{h}=|u|_{K}
for one
h\in(K,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})]
.

Proof.

The implications (
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
)
\Rightarrow
(
4
) follow
as in the proof of
Theorem

3.7
.
Since (
3
) trivially implies (
2
), we only have to show (
2
)
\Rightarrow
(
1
):
By
Theorem

3.20
there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(A)\leq K
and
\int_{A}|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u|_{K}
.
By considering

Lemma 3.24
.

Let

be given.
Then the extreme points

of the set

Proof.

Let

be in the set on the right. Take

,
\lambda\in(0,1)
with

Using the arguments of

Because of

Theorem 3.27
.

Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Then it holds

\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)=\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{*}\big(\left\{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})\mid|f|=\chi_{A},\ A\in\mathcal{A},\ \mu(A)\leq K\right\}\big),

where

denotes the weak-* closed convex hull in

Proof.

As argued in the proof of

, we have

|u|_{K}=\sup_{f\in C}\int f|u|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\sup_{|f|\in C}\int fu\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\sup_{f\in\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{*}(\{|f|\in C\})}\int fu\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu,

where

.
This directly implies
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}^{*}(\{|f|\in C\})\subseteq\partial|\cdot|_{K}(0)
.

Let us take

. Then by a consequence of Hahn-Banach theorem
[

(in the weak-* topology of

[

i.e.,

The following auxiliary result shows that the subdifferential of specific functions does not change if a dense subspace of
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
is considered instead.
This particularly applies to the largest-
K
-norm.

Theorem 3.29
.

Let
f:L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and continuous and
X\subseteq L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
a dense subspace. Then it holds

\partial^{X}f(0)=\partial^{L^{1}}f(0).

Proof.

The inclusion
\partial^{L^{1}}f(0)\subseteq\partial^{X}f(0)
follows directly from the fact that
X\subseteq L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Now let
s\in\partial^{X}f(0)
, i.e.,
s
is linear and continuous on
X
, and

\langle s,v\rangle\leq f(v)\quad\forall v\in X.

Because of the density of
X
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, we can uniquely extend
s
to
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Moreover, there exists for every
u\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
a sequence
(u_{n})\subseteq X
with
\|u_{n}-u\|_{1}\to 0
.
It follows

\langle s,u\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle s,u_{n}\rangle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}f(u_{n})=f(u),

which shows
s\in\partial^{L^{1}}f(0)
.
∎

4
DC method in Function Space

The penalization of the constraint
\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K}=0
leads to a DC function.
The minimization of such functions can be attempted using the celebrated DC-Algorithm.
For the convenience of the reader, we prove some important results on the DC-Algorithm in a (possibly) infinite dimensional setting.
For further reading on (mostly finite dimensional) DC problems and the DC-Algorithm we refer to
[
17
]
and references therein.

Let us consider the general DC-problem

\min_{u\in X}f(u):=g(u)-h(u),

where

•

X
is a Banach space,

•

g:X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is proper, convex and lower semi-continuous,

•

h:X\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and continuous.

The basic DC-Algorithm for this problem reads:

Algorithm 4.1

(DC-Algorithm)
.

(S.0)

Choose
u^{0}\in X
and set
k:=0
.

(S.1)

Choose
s^{k}\in\partial h(u^{k})
and determine
u^{k+1}
as a solution to

\min_{u\in X}g(u)-h(u^{k})-\langle s^{k},u-u^{k}\rangle

(4.1)

(S.2)

If a suitable termination criterion is satisfied: STOP.

(S.3)

Set
k\leftarrow k+1
and go to (S.1).

Therefore, in step (S.1) the function
h
is approximated by its linearization based on the subgradient
s^{k}
, and the resulting convex problem (
4.1
) is solved.
Note that the convex function
u\mapsto g(u)-h(u^{k})-\langle s^{k},u-u^{k}\rangle
majorizes
f
.
The following necessary optimality conditions can be derived analogously to the finite dimensional setting.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
u\in X
be a local minimum of
f=g-h
. Then

\emptyset\neq\partial h(u)\subseteq\partial g(u),

and in particular
\partial g(u)\cap\partial h(u)\neq\emptyset
, see
[
22
, Theorem 2]
.

In general, limit points of iterates of
Algorithm

4.1
will only satisfy the weaker optimality condition
\partial g(u)\cap\partial h(u)\neq\emptyset
.

Definition 4.3
.

A point
u\in X
is called a critical point of
f=g-h
, if

\partial g(u)\cap\partial h(u)\neq\emptyset.

u
is called a strongly critical point of
f=g-h
, if

\emptyset\neq\partial h(u)\subseteq\partial g(u).

A strongly critical point is also often called directional-stationary or d-stationary, see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.
The following convergence results for
Algorithm

4.1
are well-known, see, e.g.,
[
22
, Theorem 3]
.
For the convenience of the reader, we give short elementary proofs.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(u^{k})
be a sequence generated by
Algorithm

4.1
for
f=g-h
. Then

1.

the sequence
(f(u^{k}))
of function values is monotonically decreasing.

2.

if
u^{k+1}=u^{k}
the current iterate
u^{k}
is a critical point of
f=g-h
.

3.

if
f(u^{k})=(g-h)(u^{k})=(g-h)(u^{k+1})=f(u^{k+1})
the current iterate
u^{k}
is a critical point of
f=g-h
.
If in addition
g
or
h
is strongly convex it holds
u^{k+1}=u^{k}
.

Proof.

(1)
Since
u^{k+1}
minimizes the DC subproblem (
4.1
) in each step it holds

\displaystyle f(u^{k+1})

\displaystyle=g(u^{k+1})-h(u^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq g(u^{k+1})-h(u^{k})-\langle s^{k},u^{k+1}-u^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq g(u^{k})-h(u^{k})=f(u^{k}).

(2) The optimality of
u^{k+1}
implies
s^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k+1})
.
If
u^{k+1}=u^{k}
we directly have
s^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k})\cap\partial h(u^{k})
.

(3)
Now let
f(u^{k})=f(u^{k+1})
, i.e.,

g(u^{k})-g(u^{k+1})=h(u^{k})-h(u^{k+1}).

Since
s^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k+1})\cap\partial h(u^{k})
, this implies

\displaystyle g(u^{k})-g(u^{k+1})=\langle s^{k},u^{k}-u^{k+1}\rangle.

(4.2)

Let
v\in X
. Then we obtain

\begin{split}g(v)-g(u^{k})&=g(v)-g(u^{k+1})+g(u^{k+1})-g(u^{k})\\
&\geq\langle s^{k},v-u^{k+1}\rangle-\langle s^{k},u^{k}-u^{k+1}\rangle=\langle s^{k},v-u^{k}\rangle,\end{split}

which implies
s^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k})
. Therefore,
u^{k}
is a critical point.
If in addition
g
is strongly convex, we get

g(u^{k})-g(u^{k+1})\geq\langle s^{k},u^{k}-u^{k+1}\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u^{k}-u^{k+1}\|_{X}^{2}

for some
\alpha>0
, and therefore
u^{k+1}=u^{k}
by (
4.2
).
If
h
is strongly convex we can argue similarly.
∎

Under suitable assumptions, accumulation points of the iterates of the DC-Algorithm are critical points of
f=g-h
.
Note that the assumption of the existence of strong and weak-* convergent subsequences is quite strong.
We prove that they are fulfilled for some problem classes in
Section

6
below.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that
f=g-h
is bounded from below and that at least one of
g
and
h
is strongly convex.
Let
(u^{k})
be a sequence generated by the DC-Algorithm,
Algorithm

4.1
, with the associated sequence
(s^{k})
of subgradients.

Suppose that there is a subsequence indexed by
(k_{n})
such that
u^{k_{n}}\to\bar{u}
in
X
and

s^{k_{n}}\rightharpoonup^{*}\bar{s}
in
X^{\prime}
.
Then
\bar{s}\in\partial g(\bar{u})\cap\partial h(\bar{u})
, i.e.,
\bar{u}
is a critical point of
f=g-h
.

Proof.

Since
u^{k+1}
minimizes the DC subproblem (
4.1
) in each step, it holds
s^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k+1})
.
By construction
s^{k}\in\partial h(u^{k})
.
This implies

\displaystyle g(v)-g(u^{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\langle s^{k},v-u^{k+1}\rangle\quad\forall v\in X,

(4.3)

\displaystyle h(v)-h(u^{k})

\displaystyle\geq\langle s^{k},v-u^{k}\rangle\quad\forall v\in X.

In particular, we get

\displaystyle g(u^{k})-g(u^{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\langle s^{k},u^{k}-u^{k+1}\rangle,

\displaystyle h(u^{k+1})-h(u^{k})

\displaystyle\geq\langle s^{k},u^{k+1}-u^{k}\rangle.

Since one of
g
and
h
are assumed to be strongly convex, we get an additional term
\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u^{k}-u^{k+1}\|_{X}^{2}

with
\alpha>0
on the right-hand side of at least one of the inequalities.
Then we find

f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1})=g(u^{k})-g(u^{k+1})+h(u^{k+1})-h(u^{k})\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{X}^{2}

for some
\alpha>0
.
Since
(f(u^{k}))
is monotonically decreasing by
Lemma

4.4
and bounded from below, it is converging.
Then the above inequality proves
\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{X}\to 0
,
which implies
u^{k_{n}+1}\to\bar{u}
in
X
.
Since
s^{k_{n}}\rightharpoonup^{*}\bar{s}
in
X^{\prime}
,
u^{k_{n}}\to\bar{u}
and
u^{k_{n}+1}\to\bar{u}
in
X
, we can pass to the limit
n\to\infty
in (
4.3
)
to obtain

\displaystyle g(v)-g(\bar{u})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\bar{s},v\rangle-\langle\bar{s},\bar{u}\rangle\quad\forall v\in X,

\displaystyle h(v)-h(\bar{u})

\displaystyle\geq\langle\bar{s},v\rangle-\langle\bar{s},\bar{u}\rangle\quad\forall v\in X,

i.e.,
\bar{s}\in\partial g(\bar{u})\cap\partial h(\bar{u})
.
∎

In the following we show that for similar convergence results it suffices to solve the subproblem (
4.1
) only approximately.

Algorithm 4.6

(Inexact DC-Algorithm)
.

(S.0)

Choose
u^{0}\in X
and set
k:=0
.

(S.1)

Choose
s^{k}\in\partial h(u^{k})
and let
u^{k+1}
be an
\varepsilon^{k}
-stationary point for the convex problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in X}g(u)-\langle s^{k},u\rangle,

(4.4)

i.e., it holds
s^{k}+\varepsilon^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k+1})
.

(S.2)

If a suitable termination criterion is satisfied: STOP.

(S.3)

Set
k\leftarrow k+1
and go to (S.1).

Usually the inexactness in the DC-Algorithm is incorporated via the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential see,
[
4
,
17
]
.
In our formulation
\varepsilon^{k}
plays the role of a residuum to the necessary and sufficient condition
0\in\partial g(u^{k+1})-s^{k}
.
The definition of
\varepsilon^{k}
implies

\operatorname{dist}\left(0,\partial g(u^{k+1})-s^{k}\right)\leq\|\varepsilon^{k}\|.

Like in most inexact DC-Algorithms there is no guarantee that the subproblem can be solved in a finite number of iterations for a prescribed maximal value of
\|\varepsilon^{k}\|
.
For a detailed discussion see for example
[
33
]
.

We have the following inexact convergence result.

Theorem 4.7

(Inexact DC)
.

Assume that
f=g-h
is bounded from below and that at least one of
g
and
h
is strongly convex.
Let
(u^{k})
be a sequence generated by the inexact DC-Algorithm,
Algorithm

4.6
, with the associated sequences
(s^{k})
and
(\varepsilon^{k})
.

Suppose that there is a subsequence indexed by
(k_{n})
such that
u^{k_{n}}\to\bar{u}
in
X
and

s^{k_{n}}\rightharpoonup^{*}\bar{s}
in
X^{\prime}
. Suppose in addition

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|\varepsilon^{k}\|_{X^{\prime}}^{2}<\infty.

Then
\bar{s}\in\partial g(\bar{u})\cap\partial h(\bar{u})
, i.e.,
\bar{u}
is a critical point of
f=g-h
.

Proof.

The proof works analogously to the proof of the previous theorem.
From
s^{k}+\varepsilon^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k+1})
, and the uniform convexity of
g
or
h
we get

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{X}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1})+\langle\varepsilon^{k},u^{k+1}-u^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq f(u^{k})-f(u^{k+1})+\frac{1}{\alpha}\|\varepsilon^{k}\|_{X^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{4}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{X}^{2}

for some
\alpha>0
.
Since
g
is proper and
\partial g(u^{1})\neq\emptyset
it follows
f(u^{1})<\infty
.
As
f
is bounded from below by some
M\in\mathbb{R}
, we get after summing the above inequality

\frac{\alpha}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{X}^{2}\leq f(u^{1})-M+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|\varepsilon^{k}\|_{X^{\prime}}^{2}<+\infty.

This implies
u^{k_{n}+1}\to\bar{u}
in
X
.
As in the proof of
Theorem

4.5
, we can pass to the limit in the inclusions

s^{k}+\varepsilon^{k}\in\partial g(u^{k+1})
and
s^{k}\in\partial h(u^{k})

along the subsequence
(k_{n})

to obtain

.
∎

5
DC Reformulation in Function Space

In the following we now apply the results on the largest-
K
-norm to the general
L^{0}
-constrained problem, prove solvability and study the penalized problem.
For this purpose let
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
be again a
\sigma
-finite measure space.
We consider the general problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in X}f(u)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|u\|_{0}\leq K

(5.1)

with
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
under the assumption that

•

X
is a reflexive Banach space that is compactly embedded in
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
,

•

f:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is proper, lower semicontinuous, convex, bounded from below and coercive, and

•

there is
u_{0}\in X
such that
f(u_{0})<\infty
and
\|u_{0}\|_{0}\leq K
.

By
Theorem

3.22
problem (

) is equivalent to

\min_{u\in X}f(u)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K}=0.

In the following, we want to study the associated penalized problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in X}f(u)+\rho(\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K}),\qquad\rho>0,

(5.2)

where
\rho
is a penalty parameter.
Note that this problem is a DC problem.

5.1
A Penalty Method

Let us define for every
u\in X
the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle P(u)

\displaystyle:=\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K},

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(u)

\displaystyle:=f(u)+\rho(\|u\|_{1}-|u|_{K})=f(u)+\rho P(u).

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
the functions
\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
|\cdot|_{K}
, and
P
are weakly continuous on
X
.

Proof.

Since
X
is compactly embedded in
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
this is a direct consequence of the continuity of all the functions with respect to the
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
-norm by

Lemma

3.2
.
∎

The weak continuity implies the global solvability of the original problem (
5.1
) and the penalized problem (
5.2
).

Theorem 5.2
.

The
L^{0}
-constrained problem (
5.1
) has a global solution.

Proof.

The global solvability follows by standard arguments:
Let
M:=\{u\in X\mid\|u\|_{0}\leq K\}
be the feasible set of problem (
5.1
),
which is non-empty.
Let
(u_{n})\subseteq M
be a minimizing sequence, i.e.,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(u_{n})=\inf_{u\in M}f(u)
.
Due to the assumptions on
f
it follows
\inf_{u\in M}f(u)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
f
is bounded from below, it follows from the coercivity of
f
that
(u_{n})
is bounded in
X
.
Since
X
is reflexive, we can extract a weakly convergent subsequence
(u_{n_{k}})
with
u_{n_{k}}\overset{X}{\rightharpoonup}u^{*}
for some

.
As

is proper, convex and lower semicontinuous, and therefore weakly lower semicontinuous, we get

f(u^{*})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}f(u_{n_{k}})=\lim_{k\to\infty}f(u_{n_{k}})=\inf_{u\in M}f(u).

By
Theorem

3.22

it holds
M=\{u\in X\mid P(u)=0\}
.
The weak continuity of

by
Lemma

5.1
implies
u^{*}\in M
, and
u^{*}
solves (
5.1
).
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
\rho>0
the penalized problem (
5.2
) has a global solution.

Proof.

The proof can be carried out using similar arguments as that of
Theorem

5.2
.
∎

For the corresponding penalty method, we have the following result.

3.

for all

,

,

,

6.

of the sequence

).

Proof.

The statements (

[

.

(
4
) (
5
) By assumption, there is
u_{0}\in X
with
P(u_{0})=0
and

.
Let

be a feasible point for (

), which exists by assumption.
Since

is a global minimum of

.

(

5.2
Optimality conditions

In the convergence result for the penalty method
Theorem

5.4
, we assume that we have access to a sequence of global solutions of the penalized problem (
5.2
).
However, the DC-Algorithm in general does not deliver global solutions but only critical points, see
Theorem

4.5
.
Therefore, we want to consider sequences of critical points of the penalized problem and
study their limiting behavior.

For this purpose, we first investigate optimality conditions of the penalized problem (
5.2
) under the additional assumptions that

•

X
is dense in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, and

•

f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is Gateaux-differentiable.

Since
X\subseteq L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
is dense, we know by
Theorem

3.29
that
\partial^{X}|\cdot|_{K}(u^{*})=\partial^{L^{1}}|\cdot|_{K}(u^{*})
and

\partial^{X}\|\cdot\|_{1}(u^{*})=\partial^{L^{1}}\|\cdot\|_{1}(u^{*})
.
Therefore, we simply write
\partial|\cdot|_{K}(u^{*})
and
\partial\|\cdot\|_{1}(u^{*})
for these subdifferentials.

We consider the DC-decomposition of problem (
5.2
) into
f+\rho\|\cdot\|_{1}
and
\rho|\cdot|_{K}
.
Strongly critical points give rise to the following necessary optimality condition.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
u^{*}
be a local minimum or a strongly critical point of problem (
5.2
).
Then for every
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(u^{*})
there exists
r\in\partial\|\cdot\|_{1}(u^{*})
such that

\rho\cdot\langle s,v\rangle=\langle f^{\prime}(u^{*}),v\rangle+\rho\cdot\langle r,v\rangle

for every
v\in X
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
Proposition

4.2
.
∎

Let us now utilize the developments of the subgradient of
|\cdot|_{K}
from
Theorem

3.15
and
Theorem

3.28
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
u^{*}
be a local minimum or strongly critical point of problem (
5.2
).
Let
A\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u^{*}
be given such that
\mu(A)\leq K
and
\int_{A}|u^{*}|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u^{*}|_{K}
.

Then the following statements are satisfied:

1.

f^{\prime}(u^{*})\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
,

2.

f^{\prime}(u^{*})(x)=0
for almost every
x\in\operatorname{supp}u^{*}\cap A
.

3.

f^{\prime}(u^{*})(x)=-\rho\sign(u^{*})(x)
for almost every
x\in\operatorname{supp}u^{*}\setminus A
.

4.

|f^{\prime}(u^{*})(x)|\leq\rho
for almost every
x\not\in\operatorname{supp}u^{*}
.

In addition,
A
is unique up to sets of measure zero, i.e., if
B\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u^{*}
satisfies
\mu(B)\leq K
and
\int_{B}|u^{*}|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u^{*}|_{K}

then
\mu(A\triangle B)=0
.

Proof.

Define
s:=\sign(u^{*})\chi_{A}
. Then it follows
\|s\|_{0}\leq K
and
s\in\partial|\cdot|_{K}(u^{*})
by
Theorem

3.28
.
By
Corollary

5.5
there exists
r\in\partial\|\cdot\|_{1}(u^{*})
such that

\displaystyle\rho\int_{A}\sign(u^{*})\cdot v\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\langle f^{\prime}(u^{*}),v\rangle+\rho\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}r\cdot v\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu

(5.3)

for every
v\in X
.
Analogously to the proof of
Theorem

3.29
we can argue that
f^{\prime}(u^{*})\in L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
The density of
X
in
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and the continuity of (
5.3
) with respect to
v
show that (
5.3
) holds for every
v\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Testing with
v=\chi_{B}\in L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
for

yields

\rho\int_{B}\sign(u^{*})\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=\int_{B}f^{\prime}(u^{*})\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\rho\int_{B}\sign(u^{*})\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu,

since
r=\sign(u^{*})
a.e. on
\operatorname{supp}u^{*}
. This shows
f^{\prime}(u^{*})=0
a.e. on
A
.

On the other hand, testing with
v=\chi_{B}u^{*}
for

gives

0=\int_{B}f^{\prime}(u^{*})\cdot u^{*}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\rho\int_{B}|u^{*}|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu,

which shows that
f^{\prime}(u^{*})(x)=-\rho\sign(u^{*})(x)
for almost every
x\in\operatorname{supp}u^{*}\setminus A
.

Lastly, testing with
v=\chi_{B}
for
B\subseteq(\operatorname{supp}u^{*})^{c}
with

results in

0=\int_{B}f^{\prime}(u^{*})\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\rho\int_{B}r\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu.

Since

, this shows
|f^{\prime}(u^{*})(x)|\leq\rho
for almost every
x\not\in\operatorname{supp}u^{*}
.

Let now
B\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u^{*}
be such that
\mu(B)\leq K
and
\int_{B}|u^{*}|\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu=|u^{*}|_{K}
.
Then (
2
) and (
3
) imply
0=f^{\prime}(u^{*})=-\rho\sign(u^{*})
almost everywhere on
A\triangle B
.
This implies
\mu(A\triangle B)=0
.
∎

For an atom-free measure space this can be strengthened by examining the upper level sets.

Corollary 5.7

Let

for almost every

(or
x\in\{0<|u^{*}|<t\}
),

3.

|f^{\prime}(u^{*})(x)|\leq\rho
for almost every

,

where

if

.

Proof.

By

3.6
there exist

and
\mathrm{\Omega}_{>t}\subseteq A\subseteq\mathrm{\Omega}_{\geq t}

\mu(A)=K
and

.

Let

be a critical point of problem (

6
Aspects of discretization

Let
\mathrm{\Omega}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain. We will work with the Lebesgue measure, which is atom-free, see, e.g.,
[

, 211M]
.
We are interested in numerically solving the

-constrained problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})}\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}^{2}-\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}gu\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|u\|_{0}\leq K

(6.1)

for
g\in L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
.
This is motivated by a shape optimization problem considered in
[

]
.

Note that the assumptions of
Section

5
are satisfied and hence (
6.1
) is globally solvable by
Theorem

5.2
.
Moreover, we can prove that the assumptions of
Theorem

4.5
are satisfied.
We even have strong convergence of the subgradients.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(u^{k})
be a sequence generated by the DC-Algorithm,
Algorithm

4.1
, applied to the penalization of problem (
6.1
) with the associated sequence
(s^{k})
of subgradients.
Then the assumptions of
Theorem

are met, i.e., the objective is a DC function which is bounded from below such that at least one convex component is strongly convex and there is a subsequence indexed by

such that

in

and

in
H^{-1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

Proof.

The first term of the objective functional is the squared norm of the Hilbert space
H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and hence strongly convex.
The boundedness from below follows directly from Poincare’s inequality, see, e.g.,
[
5
, Satz 6.13]
.

Let
r^{k}\in\partial\|\cdot\|_{1}(u^{k})
be such that

-\triangle u^{k+1}=g-\rho(r^{k}-s^{k}),

which exists since
u^{k+1}
minimizes the DC subproblem.
By
Lemma

3.14
the sequences
(s^{k})
and
(r^{k})
are bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Since
\mathrm{\Omega}
is bounded,
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
is continuously embedded into
L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})
is compactly embedded into
H^{-1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
This shows that there exists a strongly convergent subsequence of the right-hand side of the above equation in
H^{-1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.

The strong convergence of the associated subsequence of
(u^{k+1})
in
H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
now follows by the continuity of the map
(-\triangle)^{-1}:H^{-1}(\mathrm{\Omega})\to H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, cf.
[
5
, Satz 6.29]
.
Hence, there is a subsequence indexed by
(k_{n})
such that
u^{k_{n}}\to\bar{u}
in
H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Because of the boundedness of the associated subsequence of subgradients
(s^{k_{n}})
in
L^{\infty}(\mathrm{\Omega})
, the claim follows analogously to above after possibly extracting another strong convergent subsequence in
H^{-1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
∎

At first we take a closer look at the discretization of the
L^{0}
-constraint in the context of finite element methods.

6.1
Reformulation of
L^{0}
-Constraints in the Space of Piecewise Linear Continuous Functions

We consider a standard finite element discretization with simplices.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a shape-regular mesh with
d
-simplex elements
T_{i}
,
i=1,...,m
,
and nodal basis functions
\varphi_{j}
,
j=1,...,N
, which generate the space
V_{N}
of piecewise linear continuous functions
u_{h}
.

Define the vector
\mu(\mathcal{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by

\mu(\mathcal{T})_{i}:=\mu(T_{i})

and the matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
by

D_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if the node }j\text{ is a vertex of the element }T_{i},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Given
u_{h}\in V_{N}
, we denote by
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the vector of coefficients with respect to the basis
(\varphi_{j})
.
Furthermore, for
u_{h}\in V_{N}
we define the vector

w_{u}:=D|u|\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

where the absolute value is taken elementwise.
Therefore,
w_{u,i}
is the sum of the absolute values of
u_{h}
at the
d+1
vertices of the element
T_{i}
.

For these vectors, we consider norms weighted by
\mu(\mathcal{T})
, i.e.,

\|w_{u}\|_{0,\mu(\mathcal{T})}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu(\mathcal{T})_{i}|w_{u,i}|_{0},

and similarly for
\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
and
|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
,
which corresponds to defining the measure
\mu
on
\{1\dots m\}
by
\mu(\{i\})=\mu(\mathcal{T})_{i}
.
Using these weighted norms,
we can express the
L^{0}(\mathrm{\Omega})
-norm of a function
u_{h}\in V_{N}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
u_{h}\in V_{N}
, then
\|u_{h}\|_{0}=\|w_{u}\|_{0,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
.

Proof.

Let
T_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
be a simplex.
Since
u_{h}
is piecewise linear and continuous, it holds either
u_{h}=0
a.e. on
T_{i}
or
u_{h}\neq 0
a.e. on
T_{i}
. Moreover,
u_{h}=0
a.e. on
T_{i}
is the case if and only if
u_{h}=0
at all vertices of
T_{i}
, i.e., if
w_{u,i}=0
. This shows the claim.
∎

Then the following equivalent reformulation holds for the discretized
L^{0}
-constraint
\|u_{h}\|_{0}\leq K
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
u_{h}\in V_{N}
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
. Then it holds

\|u_{h}\|_{0}\leq K\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}-|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}=0.

Proof.

Due to
Lemma

6.2
it holds
\|u_{h}\|_{0}\leq K
if and only if
\|w_{u}\|_{0,\mu(\mathcal{T})}\leq K
.
By
Theorem

3.22
this is equivalent to
\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}-|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}=0
.
∎

Let us define the following mesh-dependent approximations of the
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
- and the largest-
K
-norm.

Definition 6.4
.

For every
u_{h}\in V_{N}
define

\displaystyle\|u_{h}\|_{1,h}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|u_{i}|\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}\varphi_{i}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu,

\displaystyle|u_{h}|_{K,h}

\displaystyle:=\max_{I\subseteq\{1,...,m\}:\sum_{i\in I}\mu(T_{i})\leq K}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}|\int_{\cup_{i\in I}T_{i}}\varphi_{j}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu.

\|u_{h}\|_{1,h}
is a well-known approximation for the
L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})
-norm, see, e.g.,
[
28
]
.
These approximations connect to our reformulation in the following way.

Theorem 6.5
.

For every
u_{h}\in V_{N}
it holds

\displaystyle\|u_{h}\|_{1,h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})},

\displaystyle|u_{h}|_{K,h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}.

Proof.

If
j
is a vertex of
T_{i}
, the integral
\int_{T_{i}}\varphi_{j}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu
equals the
d+1
-dimensional volume of a hyper-pyramid with base
T_{i}
and height
1
.
Therefore, we get

\begin{split}\int_{T_{i}}\varphi_{j}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu&=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d+1}\mu(T_{i}),&\text{if }j\text{ is a vertex of }T_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\
&=\frac{1}{d+1}D_{i,j}\mu(T_{i}).\end{split}

Let
I\subseteq\{1,...,m\}
. Then we get

\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}|\sum_{i\in I}D_{i,j}\mu(T_{i})=\sum_{i\in I}\mu(T_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{i,j}|u_{j}|=\sum_{i\in I}\mu(T_{i})w_{u,i}.

This yields

\displaystyle|u_{h}|_{K,h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}\max_{I\subseteq\{1,...,m\}:\sum_{i\in I}\mu(T_{i})\leq K}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}|\sum_{i\in I}D_{i,j}\mu(T_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}\max_{I\subseteq\{1,...,m\}:\sum_{i\in I}\mu(T_{i})\leq K}\sum_{i\in I}\mu(T_{i})w_{u,i}=\frac{1}{d+1}|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}.

Setting
K=\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})
proves the claim for
\|u_{h}\|_{1,h}
.
∎

6.2
Exact Penalty Parameter for Discretized Problem

In
[
9
]
an exact penalty result is derived for the special case of a quadratic objective function.
Applying
Theorem

6.3
to the finite element discretization

\displaystyle\min_{u_{h}\in V_{N}}\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}^{2}-\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}gu_{h}\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|u_{h}\|_{0}\leq K

(6.2)

of problem (
6.1
) results in the finite dimensional problem

\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Au-b^{T}u\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}-|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}=0,

where the objective function is also quadratic.
Here,
A
and
b
denote the stiffness matrix and load vector of the finite element discretization.

We are interested in solving the penalized problem

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Au-b^{T}u+\rho\left(\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}-|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}\right)

(6.3)

for large
\rho>0
.
Note that this problem is always globally solvable.
Then the exact penalty results
[
9
, Theorem 4, Corollary 4]
can be adapted to the weighted norms in (
6.3
):
There is
\rho^{*}>0
such that for all
\rho>\rho^{*}
any solution of the penalized problem (
6.3
) is a solution of the original discretized problem (
6.2
).
Unfortunately,
\rho^{*}
depends on the discretization, and
\rho^{*}\to\infty
for
h\searrow 0
.

6.3
Convex Subdifferentials in Finite Dimensions

Solving the penalized problem (
6.3
) by a DC-Algorithm requires the computation of a subgradient of the function
u\mapsto|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
.
The following results give sufficient explicit characterizations of the necessary subdifferentials.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
K\geq 0
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Moreover, let
I^{*}
be an optimal set for the maximization problem in
|x|_{K,\lambda}
and
I_{0}^{*}:=\{i\in I^{*}\mid x_{i}=0\}
.
Then it holds

\partial|\cdot|_{K,\lambda}(x)\supseteq\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid s_{i}\begin{cases}=\lambda_{i}\sign(x_{i}),&i\in I^{*}\setminus I_{0}^{*}\\
\in[-\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}],&i\in I_{0}^{*}\\
=0,&i\notin I^{*}\end{cases}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
s
be in the set on the right-hand side.
Then for any vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds

s^{T}y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}y_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in I^{*}}|s_{i}||y_{i}|\leq\sum_{i\in I^{*}}\lambda_{i}|y_{i}|\leq|y|_{K,\lambda},\quad s^{T}x=\sum_{i\in I^{*}}\lambda_{i}|x_{i}|=|x|_{K,\lambda},

which shows the claim.
∎

For a complete characterization of the subdifferential of the normal finite-dimensional largest-
K
-norm see
[
29
, Theorem 3]
.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
K\geq 0
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then

\partial|w_{u}|_{K,\lambda}(u)=\{(\rho_{i}s_{i})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\rho=D^{T}r,\>r\in\partial|\cdot|_{K,\lambda}(w_{u}),\>s_{i}\in\partial|\cdot|(u_{i})\>\forall i\}.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle g_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\quad

\displaystyle x\mapsto|Dx|_{K,\lambda},

\displaystyle f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\quad

\displaystyle x\mapsto|x_{i}|\text{ for every }i\in\{1,...,N\},

\displaystyle F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad

\displaystyle x\mapsto(f_{1}(x),...,f_{N}(x))^{T}=|x|.

Then
g_{K}
is convex and componentwise non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
and
f_{i}
is convex for every
i\in\{1,...,N\}
.
Moreover, it holds
(g_{K}\circ F)(u)=|w_{u}|_{K,\lambda}
for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Therefore, it follows by the chain rule for the convex subdifferential
[see
11
, Theorem 4.3.1]
that

\partial|w_{u}|_{K,\lambda}(u)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}s^{i}\mid(\rho_{1},...,\rho_{N})^{T}\in\partial g_{K}(F(u)),\>s^{i}\in\partial f_{i}(u)\>\forall i\in\{1,...,N\}\right\}.

Furthermore, it holds

\partial g_{K}(x)=D^{T}\partial|\cdot|_{K,\lambda}(Dx)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
by
[
11
, Theorem 4.2.1]
. Obviously

\partial f_{i}(u)=\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid s_{i}\in\partial|\cdot|(u_{i}),\>s_{j}=0\text{ for all }j\neq i\right\}\text{ for every }i\in\{1,...,N\},

holds, which finishes the proof.
∎

Using
Lemma

6.6
to determine
r\in\partial|\cdot|_{K,\lambda}(w_{u})
, this result provides a subgradient
s\in\partial|w_{u}|_{K,\lambda}(u)
for the DC-Algorithm.
However, to be able to use
Lemma

6.6
numerically, one has to determine a set
I^{*}\subseteq\{1,...,n\}
, which attains the maximum in

\max_{I\subseteq\{1,...,n\}:\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\leq K}\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}|x_{i}|.

Such problems are known as knapsack problems see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.
In our implementation, we used a greedy algorithm, cf.
[
19
, section 2.4]
.

To solve the subproblem (
6.4
) of the DC-Algorithm we also need the convex subdifferential of the function
u\mapsto\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then

\partial\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}(u)=\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid s_{i}\in\mu(\triangle_{i})\partial|\cdot|(u_{i})\ \forall i\in\{1,...,N\}\right\},

where
\triangle_{i}
is the union of all simplices in the grid of which the node
i
is a vertex, i.e.,
\mu(\triangle_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{j,i}\mu(T_{j})
.

Proof.

It holds

\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu(T_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{i,j}|u_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}|\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{i,j}\mu(T_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|u_{j}|\mu(\triangle_{j}).

Hence,
\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
equals a weighted
\ell^{1}
-norm of
u
, which proves the claim.
∎

6.4
Algorithms

The explicit DC-Algorithm for problem (
6.3
) is:

Algorithm 6.9

(DC algorithm for problem (
6.3
))
.

(S.0)

Choose
u^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and set
k:=0
.

(S.1)

Define
s^{k}:=\rho s
with
s\in\partial|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}(u^{k})
and determine
u^{k+1}
as a solution to

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Au-b^{T}u+\rho\|w_{u}\|_{1,\mu(\mathcal{T})}-(s^{k})^{T}u.

(6.4)

(S.2)

If a suitable termination criterion is satisfied: STOP.

(S.3)

Set
k\leftarrow k+1
and go to (S.1).

Analogously to
[
20
]
the necessary optimality condition of (
6.4
) can be formulated as

F_{\tau}(u):=g(u)-\mathcal{P}_{[-\rho\mu(\triangle),\rho\mu(\triangle)]}\left(g(u)-\frac{1}{\tau}u\right)=0,\quad\tau>0,

where
g(u):=Au-b-s^{k}
and
\mathcal{P}_{[-\rho\mu(\triangle),\rho\mu(\triangle)]}
denotes the elementwise projection onto
[-\rho\mu(\triangle_{i}),\rho\mu(\triangle_{i})]
.
For solving the DC subproblem (
6.4
) we use a local semismooth Newton method applied to the equation
F_{\tau}(u)=0
.

We determine the subgradient
s\in\partial|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}(u^{k})
in step (S.1) of
Algorithm

6.9
in the following way.

Algorithm 6.10
.

(S.1)

Determine the set
I^{*}\subseteq\{1,...,m\}
, which attains the maximum in
|w_{u^{k}}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}
.

(S.2)

Define
r\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
r_{i}:=\mu(T_{i})
for
i\in I^{*}
and
r_{i}:=0
otherwise.

(S.3)

Define
a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
by
a_{i}:=\sign(u^{k}_{i})
.

(S.4)

Compute
s:=(D^{T}r)\star a
, where
\star
denotes elementwise multiplication.

By
Lemma

6.6
and
Theorem

6.7
the vector
s
from step (S.4) then satisfies
s\in\partial|w_{u}|_{K,\mu(\mathcal{T})}(u^{k})
.
As already mentioned, in our implementation the set
I^{*}
is approximated by a greedy algorithm for knapsack problems.

6.5
Numerical Experiments

As already mentioned we are interested in solving the exemplary problem

\min_{u\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})}\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}^{2}-\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}gu\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu\quad\text{subject to}\quad\|u\|_{0}\leq K

for
g\in L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})
and
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
.

For our experiments we choose
\mathrm{\Omega}:=(0,1)^{2}
and the measure space
(\mathrm{\Omega},\mathcal{A},\mu)
to be be the standard Lebesgue-space.
Note that the Lebesgue-space is atom-free and hence fits our assumptions from
Section

3.1
.
For the parameter function

we use

In accordance with

we discretize our exemplary problem by means of a standard triangular finite element method with continuous piecewise linear functions.

[

.

In
[
9
]
they also tested a schedule for the constraint parameter
K
, which produced better results.
This means, that instead of using a fixed
K
during the algorithm, one starts with
K=\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})
and decreases
K
at every iteration until the target parameter is met.

Let
K\in(0,\mu(\mathrm{\Omega}))
be the actual constraint parameter and
\lambda\in(0,1)
a scheduling parameter.
Then in each iteration
k
we replace the actual
K
by

K_{k}:=\max\left(\lambda K_{k-1},K\right)

where
K_{-1}:=\mu(\mathrm{\Omega})
.
Obviously, in the implementation the stopping criterion
u^{k+1}=u^{k}
is only active as soon as
K_{k}=K
.

In
Section

6.5.4
one can see the results for different scheduling parameters
\lambda
and different levels of discretization.
The last column indicates how many iterations are needed, until the target parameter
K
is met, i.e.,
K_{k}=K
.
It shows that the number of iterations needed by the algorithm is (up to three iterations) equal to the iterations needed by the schedule.

In
Figure

2
the computed solutions for two different scheduling parameters are depicted.
It indicates that the significantly better functional value for larger
\lambda
, and also compared to no schedule cf.
Figure

1
, is due to a change in the support of the computed solution.

As shown by
Section

6.5.4
the size of the scheduling parameter has to be larger for a finer mesh to reach the better solution.
Moreover, at lower levels of discretization, we can observe some sort of convergence in the objective function values
f(u_{\lambda}^{*})
as
\lambda\nearrow 1
.
It can be expected that this can also be observed for higher levels of discretization, if
\lambda\nearrow 1
.

6.5.5
Comparison with
L^{1}
-penalization

The most famous method to induce sparsity is a
L^{1}
-penalization.
A possible relaxation for our original problem (
6.1
) would hence be

\min_{u\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})}\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}^{2}-\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}gu\,\text{\rm{}d}\mu+\beta\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathrm{\Omega})},\qquad\beta>0,

with the approximate discretization

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Au-b^{T}u+\beta\|u_{h}\|_{1,h}=:\phi(u),

(6.5)

with
\|u_{h}\|_{1,h}
from
Definition

6.4
and
Theorem

6.5
.

It is well known that for large enough
\beta
the only solution of such
L^{1}
-penalized problems is zero, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
.
For problem (
6.5
) it follows analogously to
[
16
]
that this is the case as soon as

\beta\geq\max_{i\in\{1,...,N\}}\frac{3|b_{i}|}{\mu(\triangle_{i})}.

Note that the DC subproblem is of the form (
6.5
) as well, with
b=b+\frac{1}{3}s^{k}
.
The reason why the zero-solution does not occur nevertheless is that
s^{k}
also scales with the penalty parameter
\beta
in this case: For a suitable element of the corresponding vectors it holds

\frac{3|b_{i}+\frac{1}{3}s_{i}^{k}|}{\mu(\triangle_{i})}=\frac{3|b_{i}|}{\mu(\triangle_{i})}+\frac{|\beta\mu(\triangle_{i})\sign(u_{i}^{k})|}{\mu(\triangle_{i})}=\frac{3|b_{i}|}{\mu(\triangle_{i})}+\beta>\beta.

Problem (
6.5
) can for example be solved by using SpaRSA
[
31
]
.
We use the simple non-monotone scheme with
u^{0}=A^{-1}b
and the following choice of parameters:

M=5,\quad\alpha_{\max}=10^{20},\quad\alpha_{\min}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{\max}},\quad\eta=2,\quad\sigma=0.01

Furthermore, we stop the algorithm if

\frac{|\phi(u^{k})-\phi(u^{k-1})|}{|\phi(u^{k-1})|}\leq 10^{-5}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\|u^{k}-u^{k-1}\|_{2}}{\|u^{k}\|_{2}}\leq 10^{-5}.

Note that the penalty parameter
\beta
has to be tuned to reach a desired level of sparsity.

The results for different levels of sparsity can be seen in
Section

6.5.5
, where
iter.
is the number of iterations needed and
\beta
is the tuned penalty parameter.
The computed solution for
1/h=512
is depicted in
Figure

3a
.
With respect to the objective function value, SpaRSA produces better results than our plain algorithm in lower levels of discretization.
However, if we use the scheduling from
Section

6.5.4
, our algorithm clearly outperforms the
L^{1}
-approach.
Besides that, no tuning of the penalty parameter has to be done.
Moreover, it seems that the
L^{1}
-approach cannot handle larger levels of discretization that well as the objective function value increases again for
h\searrow 0
.
Nevertheless, with respect to the support, the computed solutions look similar to our solutions with scheduling, see
Figure

3a
and
Figure

2
.

Another generic starting point is

.
However, this does not work without some changes.
This is due to the fact that we usually choose
s_{i}^{k}=0
if
u_{i}=0
, see
Algorithm

6.10
, and hence the DC-subproblem degenerates to a
L^{1}
-penalized problem with zero solution, since
\rho
is usually large in the sense of
Section

6.5.5
.
This problem can be overcome by choosing the subgradient
s^{k}
in another way.

For
u^{0}=0
we always apply the schedule from
Section

6.5.4
with
\lambda=0.995
and stop the semismooth Newton method if
\|F_{\tau}(u)\|_{2}\leq 10^{-10}
.
It turns out that the subgradient selection has a huge impact in the case
u^{0}=0
.
Natural choices for
a_{i}
with
u_{i}^{k}=0
in step (S.3) of
Algorithm

6.10
would be
a_{i}=1
and
a_{i}=-1
.
A more problem specific choice is for example
a_{i}=\sign(b_{i})
since, in order to minimize the objective functional,
\sign(u_{i})=\sign(b_{i})
seems to be advantageous.
The computed solutions for these choices of
a_{i}
are depicted in
Figure

4
.
The choice
a_{i}=\sign(b_{i})
seems to work very well since it reproduces the best functional value so far.

Furthermore, the non-negative (non-positive) computed solutions for
u^{0}=0
and
a_{i}=1
(
a_{i}=-1
), see
Figure

4
, are reproduced independently of the subgradient selection when using
u^{0}=1
(
u^{0}=-1
).

u^{*}_{L^{1}}
of the
L^{1}
-penalized problem from
Section

6.5.5

This produced the solution from

6.5.6
.
With respect to objective value the solution is not quite as good as the one starting at

\lambda=0.995
.
Nevertheless, this shows that our algorithm is first capable of and second needs very few iterations to significantly improve the solution of the
L^{1}
