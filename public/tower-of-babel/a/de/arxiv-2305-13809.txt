Towards the horizons of Tits’s vision — on band schemes, crowds and 𝔽 1 {\mathbb{F}}_{1} -structures
Oliver Lorscheid, Koen Thas

Abstract
. This text is dedicated to Jacques Tits’s ideas on geometry over 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} , the field with one element. In a first part, we explain how thin Tits geometries surface as rational point sets over the Krasner hyperfield, which links these ideas to combinatorial flag varieties in the sense of Borovik, Gelfand and White and 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} -geometry in the sense of Connes and Consani. A completely novel feature is our approach to algebraic groups over 𝔽 1 {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}} in terms of an alteration of the very concept of a group. In the second part, we study an incidence-geometrical counterpart of (epimoprhisms to) thin Tits geometries; we introduce and classify all 𝔽 1 {\mathbb{F}}_{1} -structures on 3 3 -dimensional projective spaces over finite fields. This extends recent work of Thas and Thas [ 32 ] on epimorphisms of projective planes (and other rank 2 2 buildings) to thin planes.

Introduction

The idea of a field with one element was first perceived by Jacques Tits in the 1950s who saw a parallel between incidence geometries that stem from algebraic geometry over finite fields and those that stem from combinatorial group theory. He communicated his ideas in several talks and included a short section in his paper
[
37
]
(as reproduced in the epigraph), which is the only published account of what he had in mind.

The central theme of Tits’s paper is a framework for incidence geometries that captures both worlds — geometry over Galois fields
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
with
q
elements and combinatorial group theory — which behaves analogously, but with “parameter”
q=1
. In particular, Tits asks for an algebro-geometric explanation of these combinatorial geometries in terms of algebraic geometry over the elusive field
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
with one element.

These varieties are, in essence, closed subschemes of a product of projective spaces over

Band schemes

To give a brief impression on band schemes: a
band
is a multiplicatively written commutative monoid
B
with neutral element
1
and absorbing element
0
, together with a
null set

N_{B}
, which is an ideal of the semiring
{\mathbb{N}}[B]/{\langle 0\rangle}=\{\sum n_{a}a\mid n_{a}\in{\mathbb{N}},\,a\in B-\{0\}\}
(where only finite sums are considered) such that every
a\in B
has a unique
additive inverse

-a\in B
that satisfies
a+(-a)\in N_{B}
.

For example, a ring
R
is a band with null set
N_{R}=\{\sum n_{a}a\mid\sum n_{a}a=0\text{ in }R\}
. Two important examples of bands that are not rings are

•

the
regular partial field

{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{0,1,-1\}
with null set
N_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{n_{1}1+n_{-1}(-1)\mid n_{1}=n_{-1}\}
;

•

the
Krasner hyperfield

{\mathbb{K}}=\{0,1\}
with null set
N_{\mathbb{K}}=\{n_{1}\cdot 1\mid n_{1}\neq 1\}
.

Bands come with a suitable notion of morphism, which defines the category
\Bands
. A
band scheme
is a functor
\mathscr{X}:\Bands\to\Sets
that is locally representable in a suitable sense. Examples of band schemes are representable, or
affine
, band schemes
\Hom(C,-)
where
C
is a band.

Another example is the
projective
n
-space
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}(B)\ =\ \big\{\,(a_{0},\dotsc,a_{n})\in B^{n+1}\,\big|\,a_{i}\in B^{\times}\text{ for some }i\,\big\}\;/\;B^{\times}

where the unit group
B^{\times}=\{a\in B\mid ab=1\text{ for some }b\in B\}
acts diagonally on
B^{n+1}
(and
B
is a band).

Grassmannians
\Gr(r,n)
and flag varieties
\Fl({\mathbf{r}};n)
of type
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
are defined by the usual Plücker and incidence relations as subfunctors of (products of) projective spaces. They come with coordinate projections
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},n)
whenever

is a subtype of

The band schemes

extend the usual flag varieties in the sense that
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)(k)
agrees with the usual flag varieties over a field

in terms of flag varieties over

\coprod_{{\mathbf{r}}\in\Theta}\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\quad\text{where}\quad\Theta\ =\ \big\{\,(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})\,\big|\,s>0,\;0<r_{1}<\dotsb<r_{s}<n\,\big\},

and the face relations of simplices is given by the projections
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},n)({\mathbb{F}}_{q})

Taking

-rational points recovers in an analogous fashion the combinatorial flag variety

Theorem 1.1
.

The simplices of the combinatorial flag variety

Crowds

The aspect that is completely novel in this paper is the realization, and extension, of the group action of
S_{n}
on its Coxeter complex and its combinatorial flag variety in terms of an action of band schemes. This theory starts with the generalization of groups to “crowds.”

A
crowd
is a set
G
with an
identity

1\in G
and a
crowd law

R\subset G^{3}
, which is assumed to satisfy some suitable axioms that we explain in section
5.1
. A group
G
is a crowd with crowd law
R=\{(a,b,c)\in G^{3}\mid abc=1\}
. Note that we can recover the group multiplication from the crowd law: the product
ab
of two elements
a,b\in G
is the unique element
c\in G
for which there is a
\bar{c}\in G
with
(a,b,\bar{c}),\,(c,\bar{c},1)\in R
.

A natural notion of morphism turns crowds into a category
\Crowds
. This allows for an extension of algebraic groups to band schemes: we define an
algebraic crowd
as a band scheme
{\mathscr{G}}:\Bands\to\Crowds
for which the crowd law
{\mathscr{R}}:\Bands\to\Sets
is also a band scheme.

The main example of this text is the algebraic crowd
\SL_{n}
with

\SL_{n}(B)\ =\ \big\{(a_{i,j})\in B^{n^{2}}\mid\det(a_{i,j})-1\in N_{B}\big\}

where we write
a=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\dotsc,n}
, whose crowd law
{\mathscr{R}}(B)
consists of all triples
(a,b,c)\in\SL_{n}(B)^{3}
for which

\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\ a_{i,k}b_{k,l}c_{l,j}-\updelta_{i,j}\ \in\ N_{B}

for all
i,j=1,\dotsc,n
, as well as for all cyclic permutations of
(a,b,c)
. Note that the crowd
\SL_{n}(B)
is in general not a group, which underlines the role of crowds for this theory.

Crowd activities

Replacing the action
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
of a group
G
on a set
X
by its graph
T=\{(a,x,y)\in G\times X\times X\mid a.x=y\}
leads to the notion of a
crowd activity
of a crowd
G
on a set
X
as a subset
T\subset G\times X\times X
. An
algebraic crowd activity
of an algebraic crowd

on a band scheme

is a subscheme

of
{\mathscr{G}}\times\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{X}
.

The classical action of the special linear group on flag varieties extends in a natural way to a crowd activity of the algebraic crowd
\SL_{n}
on the band scheme
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)
. Taking
{\mathbb{K}}
-rational points exhibits a new type of symmetries for combinatorial flag varieties. More precisely, we have:

Theorem 1.2
.

The crowd
\SL_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
contains the symmetric group
S_{n}
as the subcrowd of all permutation matrices. The crowd activity of

whose restriction to

is the usual action on
\Upomega_{S_{n}}

We consider it as a problem of high interest to investigate the properties of this crowd action, which might be a suitable tool to tackle various combinatorial conjectures with a proven analogue in finite field geometry. Another interesting task is to extend the insights of this text to algebraic groups and flag varieties of other Dynkin types.

In this section we state some algebraic definitions and properties of

The “field”
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}

One way of defining
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
— to fix ideas — is as the set

(3)

\Big(\{0,1\},\cdot\Big),

where only the classical multiplication is present (so one does not allow addition).
Although this definition is very simple, we will show in the next subsection that by relaxing (or better: releasing) the addition of
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
to obtain
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, its absolute galois group already is an object of interest.

First note that the algebraic closure
\overline{{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}}
of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
is the group of all roots of unity (which is isomorphic to
({\mathbb{Q}}/{\mathbb{Z}},+)
); this is because the only polynomial equations we consider in this simple setting are of the form

(with
m
a positive integer). Another way to look at it goes as follows: let
p
be any prime, and consider the algebraic closure
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
; it consists of all finite field extensions
{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{n}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
. If we represent
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
as above, one standard way to define
{\mathbb{F}}_{1^{n}}

n
a positive integer, is as the set

(4)

\Big(\{0\}\cup\upmu_{n},\cdot\Big),

where
\upmu_{n}
is the cyclic group of order
n
. Now let
n
vary to obtain
\overline{{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}}
. And note that this approach is independent of the chosen prime
p
.

Absolute Galois group of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}

We can now easily calculate the absolute Galois group

(5)

\mathsf{Gal}(\overline{{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}}/{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}})\cong\Aut(\overline{{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}})

of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
. First note that

is the direct sum of its so-called

-groups

{\mathbb{Z}}(p^{\infty})
, where
p
ranges over the primes. (One can define

simply as the Sylow

; alternatively, it is the group of

-roots of unity, where

induces an endomorphism in each component

and vice versa, each choice of a set of endomorphisms

(where

) as

It is well known that

Note that

Algebraic groups over

The vessel for our realization to Tits’s vision on algebraic groups over

is the concept of band schemes. For the versed reader, band schemes are particular types of ordered blue schemes. But we will give an independent non-technical account that allows the new-comer to

Our approach is example driven. Conceptually this theory promises to lead to a new interesting perspective on algebraic groups over

3.
Bands

Bands and band schemes are introduced in the forthcoming paper
[
1
]
by Baker, Jin and the first author. They provide a simplified account to
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry (opposed to ordered blue schemes) that is tailored to certain central applications of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry. In particular, band schemes serve for the purposes of this text.

3.1.
Bands

A
pointed monoid
is a (multiplicatively written) commutative monoid
A
with neutral element
1
together with an absorbing element
0
that satisfies
0\cdot a=0
for all
a\in A
. We can embed
A
as a multiplicative submonoid in the semiring

\textstyle A^{+}\ =\ {\mathbb{N}}[A]/{\langle 0\rangle}\ =\ \big\{\sum n_{a}a\,\big|\,n_{a}\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ and }n_{a}=0\text{ for all but finitely many }a\in A-\{0\}\big\}.

An
ideal
of
A^{+}
is a submodule of
A^{+}
, i.e. a subset
I
that contains
0
, is closed under addition and that contains
xy
for all
x\in A^{+}
and
y\in I
. We write
I={\langle S\rangle}
for the ideal generated by a subset
S
of
A^{+}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
band
is a pointed monoid
B
together with a semiring ideal
N_{B}
(the
null set
) of
B^{+}
such that for every
a\in B
there is a unique
b\in A
with
a+b\in N_{B}
. A
band morphism
is a multiplicative map
f:B_{1}\to B_{2}
with
f(0)=0
and
f(1)=1
such that
\sum n_{a}f(a)\in N_{B_{2}}
for every
\sum n_{a}a\in N_{B_{1}}
. This defines the category
\Bands
.

We denote the unique
b
for which
a+b\in N_{B}
by
-a
and call it the
additive inverse of
a
. We write
a-b
for
a+(-b)\in B^{+}
. The axioms imply that
-0=0
,
(-1)^{2}=1
and
-a=(-1)\cdot a
for all
a\in A
.

The category of bands contains all limits and colimits. Its terminal object is the
zero band

B=\{0\}
with
0=1
and
N_{B}=\{0\}=B^{+}
. Its initial object is
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{0,1,-1\}
with

N_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\ =\ \{n.1+n.(-1)\mid n\geq 0\},

which can be seen as a quadratic field extension of
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
.

Examples

A (commutative and unital) ring
R
is naturally a band with pointed monoid
B=R
and null set

\textstyle N_{B}\ =\ \big\{\sum n_{a}a\in B^{+}\,\big|\,\sum n_{a}a=0\text{ as equality in $R$}\big\}.

Since a map
f:R_{1}\to R_{2}
is a ring homomorphism if and only if it is a band morphism, this establishes a fully faithful embedding
\Rings\to\Bands
. We typically denote the band with the same symbol as the ring, e.g. we use
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
aned
{\mathbb{Z}}
for the band associated with the epinonymous ring.

This embedding extends, in fact, to hyperrings. Namely, a hyperring
R
(with hypersum
\boxplus
) defines the band with pointed monoid
B=R
and null set

\textstyle N_{B}\ =\ \big\{\sum a_{i}\in B^{+}\,\big|\,0\in\bigboxplus a_{i}\big\}.

The most relevant examples of hyperrings for our text are the
Krasner hyperfield

{\mathbb{K}}=\{0,1\}
with null set
N_{\mathbb{K}}=\{n\cdot 1\mid n=0\text{ or }n\geq 2\}
, the
tropical hyperfield

{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
with null set

\textstyle N_{\mathbb{T}}\ =\ \big\{\sum n_{a}a\,\big|\,n_{a}=0\text{ for all }a\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\text{ or }n_{b}\geq 2\text{ for }b=\max\{a\mid n_{a}\neq 0\}\big\}

and the
hyperring
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}=[0,1]
of tropical integers
whose null set is
N_{\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}}=N_{\mathbb{T}}\cap\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{+}
.

Other examples of bands are partial fields, fuzzy rings and idylls. On the other hand, a band
B
is naturally an ordered blueprint with underlying monoid
B
, ambient semiring
B^{+}
and partial order
{\langle 0\leq\sum a_{i}\mid\sum a_{i}\in N_{B}\rangle}
.

Base extension to rings

There is a natural functor
(-)_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}:\Bands\to\Rings
, which sends a band
B
to the ring

B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}\ =\ B^{+}\otimes_{\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{Z}}\ =\ {\mathbb{Z}}[B]/{\langle N_{B}\rangle}

and a band morphism
f:B\to C
to the ring homomorphism
f^{+}\colon B^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to C^{+}_{\mathbb{Z}}
with

\textstyle f^{+}\big(\big[\sum n_{a}\cdot a\big]\big)\ =\ \big[\sum n_{a}\cdot f(a)\big].

This functor is left adjoint to the fully faithful embedding
\Rings\to\Bands
. In particular,
B_{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}\simeq R
if
B
is the band associated with the ring
R
.

3.2.
Algebras and quotients

Let
k
be a band. A
k
-algebra
is a band
B
together with a band morphism
\upalpha_{B}:k\to B
. A (
k
-linear
)
morphism
of
k
-algebras

and
C
is a band morphism
f:B\to C
for which
\upalpha_{C}=f\circ\upalpha_{B}
. This defines the category
\Alg_{k}
of
k
-algebras.

Tensor products

The tensor product
B\otimes_{k}C
is a particular instance of a colimit of bands: its monoid consists of the equivalence classes
b\otimes c
of elements
(b,c)\in B\times C
for the equivalence relation
\sim
generated by
(ab,c)\sim(b,ac)
for
a\in k
,
b\in B
and
c\in C
. Its null set is the semiring ideal of
(B\otimes_{k}C)^{+}
generated by expressions of the forms
\sum b_{i}\otimes 1
and
\sum 1\otimes c_{j}
for which
\sum b_{i}\in N_{B}
and
\sum c_{j}\in N_{C}
.

Free algebras

Let
k
be a band and
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a set. Let
{\langle T_{i}\rangle}_{\mathbb{N}}
be the free monoid generated by the
T_{i}
, which consists of all finite products of the form
\prod T_{i}^{e_{i}}
with
e_{i}\geq 0
. The
free
k
-algebra in
\{T_{i}\}
is the monoid

\textstyle k[T_{i}]\ =\ k[T_{i}\mid i\in I]\ =\ \big\{c\prod T_{i}^{e_{i}}\,\big|\,c\in k,\,e_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ with all but finitely many }e_{i}=0\big\}

with the identification
0\prod T_{i}^{e_{i}}=0\prod T_{i}^{0}
and its null set is generated by the expressions
\sum c_{i}\prod T_{i}^{0}
for which
\sum c_{i}\in N_{k}
. The map
c\mapsto c\prod T_{i}^{0}
defines an injection
k\to k[T_{i}]
of bands, and we identify
c\in k
with
c\prod T_{i}^{0}\in k[T_{i}]
. Note that every map
\{T_{i}\}\to B
into a
k
-algebra
B
extends uniquely to a
k
-linear band morphism
k[T_{i}]\to B
.

Quotients

Let
B
be a band and
S
a subset of
B^{+}
. The
quotient of
B
by
S
is the band whose monoid

B\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle S\rangle}\ =\ B/\sim

consists of the equivalence classes
\bar{a}
of
a\in B
, where
\sim
is generated by the relations
ac\sim bc
for which
a-b\in S
and
c\in B
, and whose null set is

\textstyle N_{B\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle S\rangle}}\ =\ \big\langle\sum n_{\bar{a}}\bar{a}\,\big|\,\sum n_{a}a\in N_{B}\cup S\big\rangle.

The quotient map
\pi:B\to B\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle S\rangle}
with
\pi(a)=\bar{a}
is a surjective band morphism, and every band morphism
f:B\to C
with
\sum n_{a}f(a)\in N_{C}
for all
\sum n_{a}a\in S
factors uniquely through
\pi
.

Examples

The constructions of free algebras and quotients allow us to write every band in the form
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{i}]\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle S\rangle}
. For instance,

{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle 1+1\rangle},\qquad{\mathbb{F}}_{3}\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle 1+1+1\rangle},\qquad{\mathbb{K}}\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle 1+1,\,1+1+1\rangle},

{\mathbb{F}}_{4}\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T]\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle 1+1,\ T^{3}+1,\ T^{2}+T+1\rangle},\qquad{\mathbb{F}}_{5}\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T]\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle T^{2}+1,\ T+T+1\rangle}.

4.
Band schemes

For the versed reader in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry, we define a band scheme as an ordered blue scheme

for which
\mathscr{O}_{X}(U)
is a band for all open subsets

of

4.1.
Band schemes as functor of points

For our purposes, we define a
band scheme
as a functor
\mathscr{X}:\Bands\to\Sets
. This functor is assumed to have an
affine open covering
, but we grossly neglect this condition in this text. A
morphism
of band schemes is a morphism of functors.

An
affine band scheme
is a
representable
functor, i.e. a functor isomorphic to
\Hom(B,-)
for some band
B
. We write
\Spec B=\Hom(B,-)
. By the Yoneda lemma, a morphism
\upvarphi:\Spec C\to\Spec B
of affine band schemes is induced by a band morphism
f:B\to C
.

Let
k
be a band. A
k
-scheme
is a functor
\mathscr{X}:\Alg_{k}\to\Sets
. We denote the category of
k
-schemes by
\Sch_{k}
. Since
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
is initial in
\Bands
, a band scheme is the same as an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-scheme, and
\Sch_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
corresponds to the category of band schemes.

4.2.
Projective band schemes

Let
k
be a band and
n\geq 0
. The
projective
n
-space over
k
is the functor
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}:\Alg_{k}\to\Sets
that sends a
k
-algebra

to the set

{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}(B)\ =\ \big\{[x_{0}:\dotsc:x_{n}]\,\big|\,(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n})\in B^{n+1}\text{ with }x_{i}\in B^{\times}\text{ for some }i\big\}

of equivalence classes of
B^{n+1}/B^{\times}
. Note that a
k
-linear morphism
f:B\to C
induces the map
\upvarphi:{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}(B)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}(C)
with
\upvarphi([x_{0}:\dotsc:x_{n}])=[f(x_{0}):\dotsc:f(x_{n})]
, which defines
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
as a functor.

A formal expression
P=\sum c_{e}T_{0}^{e_{0}}\dotsb T_{n}^{e_{n}}
with
c_{e}\in k^{+}
and multi-index

is called a

if there is a

) such that
\sum_{i=0}^{n}e_{i}=d
whenever

. Substituting

by

evaluates

in a tuple
(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n})\in k^{n+1}
, and the condition
\sum c_{e}x_{0}^{e_{0}}\dotsb x_{n}^{e_{n}}\in N_{k}
is well defined for equivalence classes
[x_{0}:\dotsc:x_{n}]
if

is homogeneous.

A
projective
k
-scheme
is a subfunctor
\mathscr{X}
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}
of the form

\textstyle\mathscr{X}(B)\ =\ \big\{[x_{0}:\dotsc:x_{n}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{k}(B)\,\big|\,\sum\upalpha_{B}(c_{i,e})x_{0}^{e_{0}}\dotsb x_{n}^{e_{n}}\,\in\,N_{B}\text{ for all }i\in I\big\}

where
\upalpha_{B}:k\to B
is the structure map of the
k
-algebra
B
and where
\{\sum c_{i,e}x_{0}^{e_{0}}\dotsb x_{n}^{e_{n}}\}_{i\in I}
is a set of homogeneous polynomials over
k
.

In the following, all projective band schemes are defined over
k={{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which allows us to omit the subscript
k
. In particular, we write
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
for
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
.

Grassmannians

Let
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
0\leq r\leq n
. Let
\binom{E}{r}
be the family of
r
-subsets of
E
. The Grassmannian over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
is the functor
\Gr(r,n):\Bands\to\Sets
that sends a band
B
to the set
\Gr(r,n)(B)
of equivalence classes
[x_{I}]_{I\in\binom{E}{r}}\in B^{N}/B^{\times}
for which
x_{I}\in B^{\times}
for some
I\in\binom{E}{r}
and that satisfy the
Plücker relation

\sum_{k=0}^{r}\,(-1)^{\epsilon(j_{k},I)+\epsilon(j_{k},J)}\,\cdot\,x_{J-\{j_{k}\}}\,\cdot\,x_{J^{\prime}\cup\{j_{k}\}}\ \ \in\ \ N_{B}

for all
J=\{j_{0},\dotsc,j_{r}\}
and
J^{\prime}=\{j^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,j^{\prime}_{r-1}\}
where
\epsilon(j,I)=\#\{i\in I\mid i<j\}
and
x_{I}=0
if
\#I<r
. Choosing a linear order on
\binom{E}{r}
defines an inclusion
\Gr(r,n)
into
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
for
N=\binom{n}{r}-1
, which we call the
Plücker embedding
and which gives
\Gr(r,n)
the structure of a projective band scheme.

If
k
is a field, then
\Gr(r,n)(k)
stays naturally in bijection with the usual Grassmannian. We recover other well-known spaces:

(1)

\Gr(r,n)({\mathbb{K}})
is canonically bijective to the set of all matroids of rank
r
on
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
;

(2)

\Gr(r,n)({\mathbb{T}})
is canonically bijective to the Dressian
\Dr(r,n)
;

(3)

\Gr(r,n)(F)
is canonically bijective to all
F
-matroids of rank
r
on
E
for every idyll
F
.

For more details, we refer the reader to
[
2
]
.

Flag varieties

Also flag varieties extend to band schemes; cf.
[
16
]
for more details. Given a finite set
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and a tuple
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
of increasing integers
r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s}\in E
(the
type
), we define the flag variety
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},E)=\Fl(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s},E)
as the functor
\Bands\to\Sets
that sends a band
B
to the set of all tuples

\big([x_{r_{1},I}],\dotsc,[x_{r_{s},I}]\big)\quad\in\quad\prod_{i=1}^{s}\Gr(r_{i},E)(B)

that satisfy the incidence relations

\sum_{k=0}^{r_{i^{\prime}}}\,(-1)^{\epsilon(j_{k},J)+\epsilon(j_{k},J^{\prime})}\,\cdot\,x_{r_{i^{\prime}},J-\{j_{k}\}}\,\cdot\,x_{r_{i},J^{\prime}\cup\{j_{k}\}}\ \ \in\ \ N_{B}

for all
i\leq i^{\prime}
and
J=\{j_{0},\dotsc,j_{r_{i^{\prime}}}\}
and
J^{\prime}=\{j^{\prime}_{2},\dotsc,j^{\prime}_{r_{i}}\}
where we define
x_{r,I}=0
if
\#I<r
.

5.
Crowds

In the existing approaches to algebraic groups over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, the group law
\upmu:G\times G\to G
of an algebraic group
G
does not descend without some yoga to allow for such morphisms over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
; cf. the solutions in
[
20
]
and
[
23
]
. The reason for this difficulty is that matrix groups typically involve the additive structure of the tensor powers of its coordinate algebra. In contrast to usual algebraic geometry where tensor products are rings, tensor products fail to be closed under addition in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry.

We present in this text a different viewpoint that aligns with the very idea of bands (and, more generally, ordered blueprints): instead of considering the group law as a function
\upmu:G\times G\to G
, we consider it as a collection of triples
(a,b,c)
whose product is
1
, i.e.
abc=1
, respectively. This allows us to recover the product
ab=\upmu(a,b)
as the unique element
c
for which there is a
d
with
1cd=1
and
abd=1
. We formalize this type of structure in the following.

5.1.
Definitions

Definition 5.1
.

A
crowd
is a set
G
together with a element
1\in G
(the
identity
) and a subset
R\subset G^{3}
(the
crowd law
) that satisfy the following axioms for all
a,b,c\in G
:

(C1)

(a,1,1)\in R
if and only if
a=1
;

(C2)

(a,b,1)\in R
implies
(b,a,1)\in R
;

(C3)

(a,b,c)\in R
implies
(c,a,b)\in R
.

A
crowd morphism
is a map
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
between crowds
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that
f(1)=1
and
\big(f(a),f(b),f(c)\big)\in R_{2}
for all
(a,b,c)\in R_{1}
. This defines the category
\Crowds
.

Let
G
be a crowd. The
inversion of
G
is the subset

R^{(2)}\ =\ \big\{(a,b)\in G\times G\,\big|\,(a,b,1)\in R\big\}

of
G^{2}
. For
a,b\in G
, the
inverse of
a
is the subset

a^{-1}\ =\ \big\{b\in G\,\big|\,(a,b)\in R^{(2)}\big\}

of
G
and the
product of
a
and
b
is the subset

ab\ =\ \big\{c\in G\,\big|\,(a,b,d)\in R\text{ and }(d,c)\in R^{(2)}\text{ for some }d\in G\big\}.

of
G
.

Remark 5.2
.

We can formulate the axioms of a crowd in a more symmetric form as follows. Let
G
be a set,
1\in G
and
R\subset G^{3}
. Then
G
, together with
1
and
R
, is a crowd if and only if it satisfies the following axioms.

(C1
∗
)

The following conditions for
a\in G
are equivalent:

(a)\quad a=1;\qquad(b)\quad(a,1,1)\in R;\qquad(c)\quad(1,a,1)\in R;\qquad(d)\quad(1,1,a)\in R.

(C2
∗
)

The following conditions for
a,b\in G
are equivalent:

\displaystyle(a)\quad(a,b,1)\in R;\qquad

\displaystyle(b)\quad(b,1,a)\in R;\qquad

\displaystyle(c)\quad(1,a,b)\in R;

\displaystyle(d)\quad(b,a,1)\in R;\qquad

\displaystyle(e)\quad(a,1,b)\in R;\qquad

\displaystyle(f)\quad(1,b,a)\in R.

(C3
∗
)

The following conditions for
a,b,c\in G
are equivalent:

(a)\quad(a,b,c)\in R;\qquad(b)\quad(c,a,b)\in R;\qquad(c)\quad(b,c,a)\in R.

Remark 5.3
.

Additional properties that are satisfied by many crowds of interest are the following:

(C4)

a^{-1}\neq\varnothing
for all
a\in G
.

(C5)

(a,b,c),\ (a,\bar{a},1),\ (b,\bar{b},1),\ (c,\bar{c},1)\in R
implies
(\bar{c},\bar{b},\bar{a})\in R
for all
a,\bar{a},b,\bar{b},c,\bar{c}\in G
.

The concept of abelian groups extends naturally to crowds in the form of the axiom:

(C6)

(a,b,c)\in R
implies
(b,a,c)\in R
for all
a,b,c\in G
.

We collect some additional properties of a crowd without proof.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
G
be a crowd. Then:

(1)

1^{-1}=\{1\}
and
1\notin a^{-1}
for
a\neq 1
;

(2)

1\in ab
if and only if
b\in a^{-1}
.

Remark 5.5
.

The category of crowds has good properties such as the following. The
trivial crowd

G=\{1\}
with
R=\{(1,1,1)\}
is the initial and terminal object in
\Crowds
. The functor
\Crowds\to\Sets_{\ast}
to pointed sets that sends a crowd
(G,1,T)
to the pointed set
G
with base point
1
, has a fully faithful left adjoint
\Sets_{\ast}\to\Crowds
, which sends a pointed set
X
with base point
\ast
to the
free crowd
G[X]=(X,\ast,T)
on
X
with
T=\{(1,1,1)\}
.

Remark 5.6
.

There is a natural alternative to axiomatize a crowd: consider a set
G
and subsets
R^{(i)}\subset G^{i}
for
i=1,2,3
such that
R^{(1)}=\{1\}
is a singleton. Consider
G^{i}
embedded in
G^{i+1}
as
G^{i}\times\{1\}
. Then the crowd axioms
(C1)
–
(C3)
are equivalent to

(1)

R^{(i)}
is invariant under cyclic permutation of the factors for
i=1,2,3
;

(2)

R^{(i)}=R^{(i+1)}\cap G^{i}
as subsets of
G^{i+1}
for
i=1,2
.

This reformulation of the crowd axioms suggests a generalization of crowds to relations
R^{(i)}\subset G^{i}
for larger
i
(e.g. all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
). At the moment of writing, we do not see the need for such a generalization and dismiss this viewpoint from this text.

5.2.
Groups as crowds

The principal example of crowds are groups: given a group
G
with neutral element
1
, we define
R=\{(a,b,c)\in G^{3}\mid abc=1\}
. Then
G
together with
1
and
R
is a crowd:
(C1)
is evident;
(a,b,1)\in R
if and only if
b=a^{-1}
is the (unique) inverse of
a
, and thus also
(b,a,1)\in R
, which shows
(C2)
;
(a,b,c)\in R
if and only if
c^{-1}=ab
is the (unique) product of
a
and
b
, and thus also
(c,a,b)\in R
, which shows
(C3)
.

This discussion should also serve as some intuition for the definition of
a^{-1}
and
ab
for more general crowds: for groups, these sets are singletons, and their respective elements coincide with the usual inverse and product of elements of a group.

We sharpen these insights into the relation between groups and crowds in the following result.

Proposition 5.7
.

A map
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
between groups is a group homomorphism if and only if it is a crowd morphism. This yields a fully faithful embedding
\Groups\to\Crowds
. A crowd
G
is isomorphic to a group if and only if it satisfies the property that for all
a,b,c,d,e\in G
we have:

(1)

a^{-1}
and
ab
are singletons;

(2)

ad=ec
if
d\in bc
and
e\in ab
.

Proof.

We begin with the second claim. Let
G
be a group and
R
the associated crowd law. We refer to the inverse element of
a\in G
by
\upiota(a)
and to the product of
a
and
b
by
\upmu(a,b)
to differentiate it from the subsets
a^{-1}=\{\upiota(a)\}
and
ab=\{\upmu(a,b)\}
that belong to the crowd
(G,1,R)
. This verifies, in particular, property (
(1)
). In order to verify property (
(2)
), consider
d\in bc
and
e\in ab
. Then
d^{-1}=\{\upiota(d)\}
and
e^{-1}=\{\upiota(e)\}
as well as
\upmu(\upmu(b,c),\upiota(d))=1
and
\upmu(\upmu(a,b),\upiota(e))=1
. Thus
d=\upmu(b,c)
and
e=\upmu(a,b)
. By (
(1)
),

ad\ =\ \{\upmu(a,d)\}\ =\ \{\upmu(a,\upmu(b,c))\}\ =\ \{\upmu(\upmu(a,b),c)\}\ =\ \{\upmu(e,c)\}\ =\ ec

where the middle equality follows from the associativity of
\upmu
. Thus (
(2)
).

Conversely, assume that a crowd
(G,1,R)
satisfies (
(1)
) and (
(2)
). We define
\upmu(a,b)
as the unique element of
ab
and
\upiota(a)
as the unique element of
a^{-1}
. We claim that
(G,\upmu,1)
is a group with inversion
\upiota
.

We verify the group axioms, starting with the associativity of
\upmu
. Consider
a,b,c\in G
. Let
d=\upmu(b,c)
and
e=\upmu(a,b)
, i.e.
d\in bc
and
e\in ab
. By (
(2)
),

\{\upmu(a,\upmu(b,c))\}\ =\ \{\upmu(a,d)\}\ =\ ad\ =\ ec\ =\ \{\upmu(e,c)\}\ =\ \{\upmu(\upmu(a,b),c)\},

which implies the associativity of
\upmu
since the sets in this equality are singletons. In order to prove that
1
is right neutral, we consider
a\in G
and
a1=\{c\mid(a,1,\upiota(c))\in R\}
. Then also
(a,\upiota(c),1)\in R
by axioms
(C2)
and
(C3)
of a crowd. Thus
\upiota(c)\in a^{-1}=\{\upiota(a)\}
and in conclusion
c=a
. This shows that
a1=\{a\}
and therefore
\upmu(a,1)=a
, as desired. To prove that
\upiota(a)
is right-inverse to
a
, note that
(a,\upiota(a),1)\in R
and
\upiota(1)=1
by the definition of
\upiota
. Therefore
1\in a\upiota(a)=\{\upmu(a,\upiota(a))\}
, which shows that
\upmu(a,\upiota(a))=1
. Thus
(G,\upmu,1)
is a group. Note that this group structure for
G
induces indeed the crowd law
R
since
(a,b,c)\in R
if and only if
\upmu(a,b)=\upiota(c)
and thus
\upmu(\upmu(a,b),c)=1
. This concludes the proof of the second claim of the proposition.

We continue with the first claim. Consider a group homomorphism
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
and
(a,b,c)\in R_{1}
, i.e.
\upmu(\upmu(a,b),c)=1
in
G_{1}
. Then
\upmu(\upmu(f(a),f(b)),f(c))=1
in
G_{2}
and thus
(f(a),f(b),f(c))\in R_{2}
, which shows that
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
is a crowd morphism.

Conversely, assume that
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
is a crowd morphism between groups, i.e. the crowd structures of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
satisfy (
(1)
) and (
(2)
). Consider
a,b\in G_{1}
and
c=\upmu(a,b)
, i.e.
ab=\{c\}
(by (
(1)
)) and
(a,b,\upiota(c))\in R_{1}
. Thus
(f(a),f(b),f(\upiota(c))\in R_{2}
. Since a crowd morphism preserves inverses, i.e.
f(\upiota(c))=\upiota(f(c))
, we find that
\upiota(f(c))\in f(a)f(b)
and thus
\upmu(f(a),f(b))=f(c)
, which shows that
f
is a group homomorphism. In particular, this implies that the functor
\Groups\to\Crowds
is fully faithful, which completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Example 5.8

(Special linear group)
.

The leading example in this text is the special linear group
G=\SL_{n}(R)
over a ring
R
. Its elements are all
(n\times n)
-matrices
a=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\dotsc,n}
with coefficients
a_{i,j}\in R
whose determinant

\det(a)\ =\ \sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\ \sign\sigma\,\cdot\ \prod_{k=1}^{n}\ a_{k,\sigma(k)}

is equal to
1
. Its neutral element is the identity matrix
{\mathbf{1}}=(\updelta_{i,j})
where
\updelta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker symbol. The crowd law
R\subset G^{3}
consists of all triples
(a,b,c)
of elements of
\SL_{n}(R)
with product
{\mathbf{1}}
, i.e. which satisfy

\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}a_{i,k}\cdot b_{k,l}\cdot c_{l,j}\ =\ \updelta_{i,j}

for all
i,j=1,\dotsc,n
.

5.3.
Algebraic crowds

We denote by
\mathscr{F}_{G}
the functor
\mathscr{F}_{G}:\Crowds\to\Sets
that sends a crowd
(G,1,R)
to its underlying set
G
and by
\mathscr{F}_{R}:\Crowds\to\Sets
the functor that sends
(G,1,R)
to its crowd law
R
.

Definition 5.9
.

An
algebraic crowd
is a functor
{\mathscr{G}}:\Bands\to\Crowds
such that both
\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ{\mathscr{G}}:\Bands\to\Sets
and
\mathscr{F}_{R}\circ{\mathscr{G}}:\Bands\to\Sets
are band schemes. An algebraic crowd
{\mathscr{G}}
is
affine
if both
\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ{\mathscr{G}}
and
\mathscr{F}_{R}\circ{\mathscr{G}}
are affine band schemes.

Remark 5.10
.

By a standard application of Yoneda’s lemma, algebraic crowds can equivalently be characterized as a band scheme
\mathscr{X}
(which is
\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ{\mathscr{G}}
) together with a subscheme
{\mathscr{R}}
of
\mathscr{X}
(which is
\mathscr{F}_{R}\circ{\mathscr{G}}
as subfunctor of
\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ{\mathscr{G}}
) and a morphism
\Spec{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\to\mathscr{X}
(which corresponds to the unit
1\in{\mathscr{G}}(B)
for all bands
B
) that satisfy certain axioms that correspond to
(C1)
–
(C3)
.

If
{\mathscr{G}}
is affine, say
\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ{\mathscr{G}}\simeq\Hom(C,-)
and
\mathscr{F}_{R}\circ{\mathscr{G}}\simeq\Hom(Q,-)
for bands
C
(the
coordinate band
) and
Q
, then the crowd structure of
{\mathscr{G}}
is equivalent to epimorphisms
\eta:C\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
(the
counit
) and
{\mathbf{r}}:C^{\otimes 3}\to Q
(the
colaw
) that satisfy the following axioms for all morphisms
\upalpha,\beta,\gamma:C\to B
into a band
B
and
\eta_{B}:C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eta}}{{\to}}{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}\to B
:

(C1
enumi
)

The morphism
\upalpha\otimes\eta_{B}\otimes\eta_{B}:C^{\otimes 3}\to B
factors through
{\mathbf{r}}:C^{\otimes 3}\to Q
if and only if
\upalpha=\eta_{B}
.

(C2
enumi
)

If
\upalpha\otimes\beta\otimes\eta_{B}
factors through
{\mathbf{r}}
, then also
\beta\otimes\upalpha\otimes\eta_{B}
factors through
{\mathbf{r}}
.

(C3
enumi
)

If
\upalpha\otimes\beta\otimes\gamma
factors through
{\mathbf{r}}
, then also
\gamma\otimes\upalpha\otimes\beta
factors through
{\mathbf{r}}
.

5.4.
The special linear group as an algebraic crowd

The special linear algebraic group
\SL_{n}
, considered as a functor
\Rings\to\Groups\to\Crowds
, extends to an affine algebraic crowd
\SL_{n}:\Bands\to\Crowds
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which can be described explicitly as follows, using the notation from Remark
5.10
.

The coordinate band of
\SL_{n}
is

C\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{i,j}\mid i,j=1,\dotsc,n]\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle\det(T_{i,j})-1\rangle}.

Its counit is the band morphism
\eta:C\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
that sends
T_{i,j}
to
\updelta_{i,j}
. Its colaw is the projection map
{\mathbf{r}}:C^{\otimes 3}\to Q
where

\textstyle Q\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T^{(m)}_{i,j}\mid m=1,2,3,\,i,j=1,\dotsc,n]\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle S\rangle}

with

\textstyle S\ =\ \big\{\sum_{k,l}\ T^{(\sigma(1))}_{i,k}T^{(\sigma(2))}_{k,l}T^{(\sigma(3))}_{l,j}-\updelta_{i,j}\,\big|\,\sigma\in A_{3},i,j=1,\dotsc,n\big\}.

where
A_{3}\subset S_{3}
is the alternating group. It follows from our discussion in Example
5.8
that for rings
R
, the crowd
\SL_{n}(R)
is the crowd associated with the special linear group of
(n\times n)
-matrices with coefficients in
R
.

This description of
\SL_{n}(R)
extends from rings
R
to bands
B
. Namely,
\SL_{n}(B)
is identified with all
(n\times n)
-matrices
a=(a_{i,j})_{i,j=1,\dotsc,n}
with coefficients
a_{i,j}\in B
such that
\det(a_{i,j})-1\in N_{B}
. The crowd colaw
R(B)
of
\SL_{n}(B)
consists of all triples of matrices
(a^{(1)},a^{(2)},a^{(3)})\in\SL_{n}(B)^{3}
such that

\sum_{k,l}\ a^{(\sigma(1))}_{i,k}a^{(\sigma(2))}_{k,l}a^{(\sigma(3))}_{l,j}-\updelta_{i,j}\in N_{B}

for all
\sigma\in A_{3}
and
i,j=1,\dotsc,n
.

In the light of Proposition
5.7
, this shows that the algebraic crowd
\SL_{n}:\Bands\to\Crowds
extends the special linear group from rings to bands. In the following, we describe the crowd
\SL_{n}(B)
for some bands
B
of particular interest.

Example 5.11
.

As a first example, we consider the regular partial field
B={{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
, which is a subband of the integers
{\mathbb{Z}}
. This allows us to describe
\SL_{n}({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}})
as the subset of matrices in
\SL_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
whose coefficients are in
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}=\{0,\pm 1\}
. A triple of matrices
(a,b,c)
over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
belongs to the colaw if and only if their product as matrices in
\SL_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
is the identity matrix. This also implies that inverses
a^{-1}
and products
ab
are singletons or empty.

As a concrete example,
\SL_{2}({{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}})
has 20 elements, which are, up to a simultaneous sign change of all coefficients:

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\quad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\big].

In this case, all inverses
\big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\big]^{-1}=\big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}d&-b\\
-c&a\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\}
are singletons, but certain products are empty, e.g.
\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big]\cdot\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big]=\varnothing
.

Example 5.12
.

As the second example, we consider the Krasner hyperfield
{\mathbb{K}}
. The elements of
\SL_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
are
(n\times n)
-matrices
a
with coefficients
a_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
that satisfy the condition
\det(a_{i,j})-1\in N_{\mathbb{K}}
, which means that
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,\sigma(i)}=1
for some permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. For
n=2
, we have

\SL_{2}({\mathbb{K}})\ =\ \big\{\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big]\ \big\}.

In this case, inverses
\big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\big]^{-1}=\big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}d&b\\
c&a\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\}
are singletons and the product of two matrices
a,b\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{K}})
is given by

\textstyle ab\ =\ \big\{c\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{K}})\,\big|\,c_{i,j}+\sum_{k=1}^{2}a_{i,k}b_{k,j}\in N_{\mathbb{K}}\text{ for }i,j=1,2\big\},

which turns
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{K}})
into a (non-commutative) canonical hypergroup in the sense of Mittas (
[
25
]
).

This fact is, however, coincidental to
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{K}})
. For
n=3
, inverses are not singletons anymore. For example,

\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\Big]^{-1}\ =\ \Big\{\ \Big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&0\\
1&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\Big],\quad\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\Big],\quad\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\Big]\ \Big\}.

Example 5.13

(Tropical special linear group)
.

As a third example, we consider
\SL_{2}
for the tropical hyperfield
{\mathbb{T}}
and the hyperring
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
of tropical integers. In the latter case
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
, we have

\SL_{2}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}})\ =\ \big\{\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\
b&1\end{smallmatrix}\big],\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&1\\
1&b\end{smallmatrix}\big]\ \big|\,a,b\in\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}\,\big\},

with unique inverses

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\
b&1\end{smallmatrix}\big]^{-1}\ =\ \big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\
b&1\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\},\qquad\big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&1\\
1&b\end{smallmatrix}\big]^{-1}\ =\ \big\{\big[\begin{smallmatrix}b&1\\
1&a\end{smallmatrix}\big]\big\},

and products

\textstyle ab\ =\ \big\{c\in\SL_{2}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}})\,\big|\,c_{i,j}+\sum_{k=1}^{2}a_{i,k}b_{k,j}\in N_{\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}}}\text{ for }i,j=1,2\big\}.

In particular,
\SL_{2}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{T}})
forms a canonical hypergroup, which extends the corresponding result for
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{K}})
. The tropical points behave less well:

\SL_{2}({\mathbb{T}})\ =\ \big\{\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\big]\ \big|\,ad\leq bc=1\text{ or }bc\leq ad=1\text{ or }1\leq ad=bc\,\big\},

which contains tropically singular matrices of the form
\big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&a\\
a&a\end{smallmatrix}\big]
with
a\geq 1
, whose inverse

\big[\begin{smallmatrix}a&a\\
a&a\end{smallmatrix}\big]^{-1}\ =\ \big\{\ \big[\begin{smallmatrix}b&c\\
b&c\end{smallmatrix}\big]\ \big|\,ab,ac\geq 1\,\big\}

is not a singleton. From the outset, this seems pathological. However, we find back the better behaved set of quasi-invertible tropical matrices with permanent
1
, in the sense of Izhakian and Rowen in
[
15
]
, as the subset

\big\{\,a\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{T}})\,\big|\,\det(a)\notin N_{\mathbb{T}}\,\big\},

which should be thought of as an open subset of
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{T}})
in the algebro-geometric sense. It is not clear if this subset has a natural structure of a tropical scheme, but it might fit into Friedenberg-Mincheva’s framework of tropical adic spaces in
[
14
]
.

5.5.
Descending linear algebraic groups to
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}

The previous formalism extends to closed subgroups of
\SL_{n,{\mathbb{Z}}}
, i.e. an
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
-model of a linear algebraic group is defined in terms of a representation on
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}
with determinant
1
.

Namely, a closed immersion
\upiota:G\to\SL_{n,{\mathbb{Z}}}
of a linear algebraic group
G
as a subgroup of
\SL_{n,{\mathbb{Z}}}
identifies the coordinate algebra of
G
with a quotient of
{\mathbb{Z}}[T_{i,j}\mid i,j=1,\dotsc,n]
by an ideal
I
that contains
\det(T_{i,j})-1
. We define the affine algebraic crowd
{\mathscr{G}}:\Bands\to\Crowds
as follows: its coordinate algebra is the band

\textstyle B\ =\ {{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}[T_{i,j}\mid i,j=1,\dotsc,n]\hskip-2.0pt\sslash\hskip-2.0pt{\langle\sum n_{a}a\mid\sum n_{a}a\in I\rangle}.

Its counit is the morphism
B\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
with
T_{i,j}\mapsto\updelta_{i,j}
. Its colaw
R
is the quotient of
B^{\otimes 3}
by the relations

\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}a_{i,k}\cdot b_{k,l}\cdot c_{l,j}\ -\ \updelta_{i,j}

for
i,j=1,\dotsc,n
and all cyclic permutations of
a
,
b
and
c
where
a_{i,j}
,
b_{i,j}
and
c_{i,j}
are the classes of
T_{i,j}
in the respective factors of
B^{\otimes 3}
. The following result is immediate from the construction.

Theorem 5.14
.

The band
B
together with the counit
B\to{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}^{\pm}}
and the colaw
B^{\otimes 3}\to R
defines an affine algebraic crowd
{\mathscr{G}}:\Bands\to\Crowds
such that
{\mathscr{G}}(R)
is the crowd associated with the group
G(R)
for every ring
R
.

6.
Crowd activities

In this section, we introduce the natural extension of group actions from groups to crowds, which allows us to extend the action of the algebraic group
\SL_{n,{\mathbb{Z}}}
on the schemes
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)_{\mathbb{Z}}
to the realm of band schemes.

6.1.
Definitions

Definition 6.1
.

A
crowd activity
(
of
G
on
X
)
is a triple
(G,X,T)
where
G
is a crowd,
X
a set and
T
a subset of
G\times X\times X
. We define

a.x\ =\ \{y\in X\mid(a,x,y)\in T\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad a.S\ =\ \{y\in X\mid(a,x,y)\in T\text{ for some }x\in S\}

for
a\in G
,
x\in X
and
S\subset X
. A
morphism of crowd activities
(G,X,T)
and
(G^{\prime},X^{\prime},T^{\prime})
is a pair
(f,g)
of a crowd morphism
f:G\to G^{\prime}
and a map
g:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
(f(a),g(x),g(y))\in T^{\prime}
for all
(a,x,y)\in T
. This defines the category
\CrowdActivities
of crowd activities.

Note that the collection of subsets
a.x
of
X
for varying
a\in G
and
x\in X
determine
T
as

T\ =\ \{(a,x,y)\in G\times X\times X\mid y\in a.x\}.

Note further that a pair
(f,g)
of a crowd morphism
f:G\to G^{\prime}
and a map
g:X\to X^{\prime}
is a morphism between crowd activities
(G,X,T)
and
(G^{\prime},X^{\prime},T^{\prime})
if and only if
g(a.x)\subset f(a).g(x)
for all
a\in G
and
x\in X
.

Remark 6.2
.

The crowd activities of primary interest for this text satisfy some properties, such as the following:

(A1)

1.x=\{x\}
for all
x\in X
.

(A2)

y\in a.x
if and only if
x\in b.y
for all
x,y\in X
and
a,b\in G
with
(a,b,1)\in R
.

(A3)

1.x\subset a.(b.(c.x))
for all
x\in X
and
a,b,c\in G
with
(a,b,c)\in R
.

We consider it a highly interesting task to study crowd activities with these properties, possibly in combination with suitable additional axioms for the underlying crowds, such as
(C4)
and
(C5)
.

We do not request properties
(A1)
–
(A3)
as axioms for crowd activities since they fail to hold for crowd activities of a crowd
G
on itself by left multiplication, which is given by

a.b\ =\ \{c\in G\mid\text{there is a }d\in c^{-1}\text{ such that }(a,b,d)\in R\}.

for
a,b\in G
. In general, this crowd activity does satisfy neither of
(A1)
–
(A3)
. Note that if
a^{-1}\neq\varnothing
for all
a\in G
, then
(A2)
is equivalent to
(C5)
.

6.2.
Group actions as crowd activities

Crowd activities generalize group actions in the following sense. Let
\GroupActions
be the category of group actions
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
of a group
G
on a set
X
. A
morphism
of group actions
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
and
\uptheta^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\times X^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
is a pair
(f,g)
of a group morphism
f:G\to G^{\prime}
and a map
g:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
g\circ\uptheta=\uptheta^{\prime}\circ(f,g)
, i.e.
g:X\to X^{\prime}
is
G
-equivariant with respect to the induced
G
-action
(g,x)\mapsto\uptheta^{\prime}(f(g),x)
of
G
on
X^{\prime}
.

Let
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
be a group action. The
crowd activity associated with
\uptheta
is the crowd activity
(G,X,T)
with

T\ =\ \big\{(a,x,y)\in G\times X\times X\,\big|\,\uptheta(a,x)=y\big\}.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
G
and
G^{\prime}
be groups.

(1)

A crowd activity
(G,X,T)
is induced by a group action if and only if it satisfies properties
(A1)
and
(A3)
and if
a.x
is a singleton for all
a\in G
and
x\in X
.

(2)

Let
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
and
\uptheta^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\times X^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
be group actions with associated crowd activities
(G,X,T)
and
(G^{\prime},X^{\prime},T^{\prime})
, respectively. Then a pair
(f,g)
of a group homomorphism
f:G\to G^{\prime}
and a map
g:X\to X^{\prime}
is a morphism of group actions if and only if it is a morphism of crowd activities. In other words, the association
\uptheta\mapsto(G,X,T)
defines a fully faithful embedding
\GroupActions\to\CrowdActivities
.

Proof.

We begin with claim (
(1)
). Assume that
(G,X,T)
is induced by a group action
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
. Then
a.x=\{\uptheta(a,x)\}
is a singleton for all
a\in G
and
x\in X
. Moreover,
1.x=\{\uptheta(1,x)\}=\{x\}
for all
x\in X
, which verifies
(A1)
. If
(a,b,c)\in R
, then
abc=1
as elements of the group
G
. Thus

a.(b.(c.x))=\{\uptheta(a,\uptheta(b,\uptheta(c,x)))\}=\{\uptheta(a,\uptheta(bc,x))\}=\{\uptheta(abc,x)\}=\{\uptheta(1,x)\}=\{x\}=1.x

for all
x\in X
, which verifies
(A3)
.

Conversely, assume that
(G,X,T)
satisfies
(A1)
and
(A3)
and that
a.x
is a singleton for all
a\in G
and
x\in X
. Define
\uptheta(a,x)
as the unique element
y
in
a.x
. We claim that
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
is a group action, i.e.
\uptheta(1,x)=x
and
\uptheta(ab,x)=\uptheta(a,\uptheta(b,x))
for all
a,b\in G
and
x\in X
. By
(A1)
,
1.x=\{x\}
for all
x\in X
, and thus
\uptheta(1,x)=x
, as required.

As the next step consider
c\in G
and
x\in X
, and let
c^{-1}
be the inverse element of
G
, i.e.
(c^{-1},c,1)\in R
. Then by
(A1)
and
(A3)
, we have

\uptheta(c^{-1},\uptheta(c,x))\ =\ \uptheta(c^{-1},\uptheta(c,\uptheta(1,x)))\ =\ x.

Thus for
a,b\in G
with product
ab=c
as elements of
G
, i.e.
(a,b,c^{-1})\in R
, and for
y=\uptheta(c,x)
, we have
y=\uptheta(a,\uptheta(b,\uptheta(c^{-1},y)))
by
(A1)
and
(A3)
. Therefore

\uptheta(a,\uptheta(b,x))\ =\ \uptheta(a,\uptheta(b,\uptheta(c^{-1},\uptheta(c,x))))\ =\ \uptheta(1,\uptheta(c,x))\ =\ \uptheta(c,x),

which completes the verification that
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
is a group action.

This proves claim (
(1)
) of the proposition. Claim (
(2)
) follows at once from the observation that
g(a.x)\subset f(a).g(x)
is equivalent with
g(\uptheta(a,x))=\uptheta(f(a),g(x))
since both
g(a.x)=\{g(\uptheta(a,x))\}
and
f(a).g(x)=\{\uptheta(f(a),g(x))\}
are singletons.

\blacksquare

6.3.
Algebraic crowd activities

Let us review the concept of an algebraic group acting on a scheme. The category of group actions comes with two forgetful functors
\mathscr{F}_{G}:\GroupActions\to\Groups
and
\mathscr{F}_{X}:\GroupActions\to\Sets
, which send a group action
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
to
G
and to
X
, respectively. An
algebraic group action
is a functor
\Theta:\Rings\to\GroupActions
such that
\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ\Theta:\Rings\to\Groups
is an algebraic group and such that
\mathscr{F}_{X}\circ\Theta:\GroupActions\to\Sets
is a scheme.

This concept generalizes to crowd activities in the following way. First note that the forgetful functors
\mathscr{F}_{G}
and
\mathscr{F}_{X}
extend to functors
\mathscr{F}_{G}:\CrowdActivities\to\Crowds
and
\mathscr{F}_{X}:\CrowdActivities\to\Sets
in the obvious way. Crowd activities come with a third forgetful functor
\mathscr{F}_{T}:\CrowdActivities\to\Sets
, which sends a crowd activity
(G,X,T)
to
T
.

Definition 6.4
.

An
algebraic crowd activity
is a functor
\Theta:\Bands\to\CrowdActivities
such that
{\mathscr{G}}=\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ\Theta
is an algebraic crowd and such that both
\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{F}_{X}\circ\Theta
and
\mathscr{T}=\mathscr{F}_{T}\circ\Theta
are band schemes. We say that
{\mathscr{G}}
acts on
\mathscr{X}
via
\Theta
.

Remark 6.5
.

Note that an algebraic group action
\Theta:\Rings\to\GroupActions
satisfies the analogon of the last requirement of an algebraic crowd activity automatically:
\mathscr{F}_{T}
sends a group action
\uptheta:G\times X\to X
to the set
\{(a,x,y)\in G\times X\times X\mid\uptheta(a,x)=y\}
, which is bijective to
G\times X
via
(a,x,y)\mapsto(a,x)
. Thus
\mathscr{F}_{T}\circ\Theta
is isomorphic to the scheme
({\mathscr{G}}\circ\Theta)\times(\mathscr{X}\circ\Theta)
.

Given an algebraic crowd
{\mathscr{G}}
and a band scheme
\mathscr{X}
, an algebraic crowd activity
\Theta
with
{\mathscr{G}}\simeq\mathscr{F}_{G}\circ\Theta
and
\mathscr{X}\simeq\mathscr{F}_{X}\circ\Theta
is determined by a subfunctor
\mathscr{T}
of
{\mathscr{G}}\times\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{X}
that is a band scheme. We describe the algebraic crowd activities of
\SL_{n}
on projective spaces, Grassmannians and flag varieties in the following.

Crowd activities on projective spaces

The action of the algebraic group
\SL_{n,{\mathbb{Z}}}
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{Z}}
extends to an algebraic crowd activity of
\SL_{n}
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
via the subfunctor
\mathscr{T}
of
\SL_{n}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
that sends a band
B
to

\textstyle\mathscr{T}(B)\ =\ \big\{(a,x,y)\in\SL_{n}(B)\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}(B)\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}(B)\,\big|\,\sum_{k}a_{i,k}x_{k}-y_{i}\,\in\,N_{B}\text{ for }i=1,\dotsc,n\big\}.

Crowd activities on Grassmannians

The algebraic crowd activity of
\SL_{n}
on
\Gr(r,n)
is as follows. Given a band
B
, a
B
-matrix
a=(a_{i,j})\in\SL_{n}(B)
and
r
-subsets
I
and
J
of
E=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, we define the
(I,J)
-minor of
a
as

a_{I,J}\ =\ (-1)^{\epsilon(I)+\epsilon(J)}\det(a_{i,j})_{i\in I,j\in J}.

where
\epsilon(I)
is the minimal number of transposition on
E
needed to map
I
to
\{1,\dotsc,r\}
. Then the algebraic group action of
\SL_{n}
on the scheme
\Gr(r,n)
extends to a algebraic crowd activity
\Theta:\Bands\to\CrowdActivities
by sending a band to

\textstyle\mathscr{T}(B)\ =\ \big\{(a,x,y)\in\SL_{n}(B)\times\Gr(r,n)(B)\times\Gr(r,n)(B)\,\big|\,\sum_{J}a_{I,J}x_{J}-y_{I}\,\in\,N_{B}\text{ for }I\in\binom{E}{r}\big\}.

Crowd activities on flag varieties

Eventually the algebraic crowd activity of
\SL_{n}
on the Grassmannians
\Gr(r,n)
for various
r
extend to an algebraic crowd activity
\mathscr{T}
on flag varieties
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)
of type
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})
, which is a closed subscheme of
\prod\Gr(r_{i},n)
. A triple of elements
a\in\SL_{n}(B)
and
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}),\,(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{r})\in\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)(B)
is in
\mathscr{T}(B)
if and only if

\sum_{J}a_{I,J}x_{r_{i},J}-y_{r_{i},I}\,\in\,N_{B}

for all
i=1,\dotsc,r
and all
I\subset E
of cardinality
r_{i}
.

7.2.
Rational points of flag varieties

The band schemes
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)
come with projection maps: if
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}=(r_{i_{1}},\dotsc,r_{i_{t}})
is a subtype of
{\mathbf{r}}=(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})

1\leq i_{1}<\dotsb<i_{t}\leq s
, then the projection
\pi_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},n)
sends a
B
-rational point
\big([x_{r_{1},I}],\dotsc,[x_{r_{s},I}]\big)
to
\big([x_{r_{i_{1}},I}],\dotsc,[x_{r_{i_{t}},I}]\big)
.

Given a band
B

\Updelta_{n}(B)
as follows. Its simplices are the elements of

\coprod_{{\mathbf{r}}\in\Theta}\ \Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)(B)\quad\text{where}\quad\Theta\ =\ \big\{\,(r_{1},\dotsc,r_{s})\,\big|\,s>0,\;0<r_{1}<\dotsb<r_{s}<n\,\big\},

and the dimension of a simplex

with

is
\dim\updelta=s-1
. The faces of
\updelta

\pi_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},B}:\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)(B)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},n)(B)
for subtypes
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}

. This defines
\Updelta_{n}
as a functor from
\Bands

\Updelta_{n}

7.3.
Recovering Tits geometries

Borovik, Gelfand and White observe in
[
5
, section 7.14]
that the Coxeter complex
\Uppi_{n,{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}}
of
S_{n}
, which Tits envisioned as a geometry over
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
, appears as a subcomplex of the combinatorial flag variety
\Upomega_{S_{n}}=\Updelta_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
. We recover this subcomplex from the viewpoint of band schemes as follows: a point
\big([x_{r_{1},I}],\dotsc,[x_{r_{s},I}]\big)
of
\Gr({\mathbf{r}},n)({\mathbb{K}})
lies in
\Uppi_{n,{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}}
if and only if for
i=1,\dotsc,s
, the tuple
[x_{r_{i},I}]
has precisely one non-zero entry.

More accurately, there is a simplicial subscheme
\Upgamma_{n}
of
\Updelta_{n}
, which is defined by the vanishing of terms of the form
x_{r,I}x_{r,J}
with
I\neq J
and which satisfies the following two properties:

•

\Upgamma_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
is the apartment of the canonical basis of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
in
\mathscr{B}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
;

•

\Upgamma_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
is the Coxeter complex of
S_{n}
as a subcomplex of
\Upomega_{S_{n}}
.

7.4.
Extending the symmetry group

The action of
S_{n}
on the Coxeter complex
\Uppi_{n,{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}}=\Upgamma_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
and the combinatorial flag variety
\Upomega_{S_{n}}=\Updelta_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
can be recovered from the crowd activity of
\SL_{n}
on
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)
(for various
{\mathbf{r}}
) as follows.

As a first observation note that the componentwise definition of the crowd activity
\SL_{n}
on
\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)
implies that the projections
\pi_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}}\colon\Fl({\mathbf{r}},n)\to\Fl({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},n)
are morphisms of algebraic crowd activities (with respect to the algebraic crowd activities of
\SL_{n}
on either flag variety). This defines an algebraic crowd activity of
\SL_{n}
on the simplicial band scheme
\Updelta_{n}
.

We define
\mathscr{N}
as algebraic subcrowd of all monomial matrices of
\SL_{n}
. As a band scheme, it is defined as

\mathscr{N}(B)\ =\ \big\{\,(a_{i,j})\in\SL_{n}(B)\,\big|\,\text{there is an $\sigma\in S_{n}$ such that }a_{i,j}=0\text{ whenever }j\neq\sigma(i)\,\big\}

for every band
B
. Its crowd law is the restriction of the crowd law of
\SL_{n}(B)
to
\mathscr{N}(B)
.

Over a ring
R
, the group
\mathscr{N}(R)
is the normalizer of the diagonal torus of
\SL_{n}(R)
. In fact, for every band
B
, the crowd
\mathscr{N}(B)
is a group, namely the group of monomial matrices of determinant
1
. In other words, the crowd law of
\mathscr{N}(B)
consists of all triples of monomial matrices whose product is
{\mathbf{1}}
. Since
{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}=\{1\}
and
-1=1
in
{\mathbb{K}}
, the group
\mathscr{N}({\mathbb{K}})
consists of all permutation matrices and thus
\mathscr{N}({\mathbb{K}})\simeq S_{n}
.

The algebraic crowd activity of
\SL_{n}
on
\Updelta_{n}
restricts to a group action of
\mathscr{N}
on
\Updelta_{n}
. Under the identifications
\mathscr{N}({\mathbb{K}})={\mathbb{S}}_{n}
and
\Updelta_{n}({\mathbb{K}})=\Upomega_{S_{n}}
, this group action recovers the usual action of
S_{n}
on
\Upomega_{S_{n}}
.

Turning this observation around, we see that we have extended the group action of
S_{n}
on
\Upomega_{S_{n}}
in a natural way to the crowd activity of
\SL_{n}({\mathbb{K}})
on
\Upomega_{S_{n}}
.

Remark 7.2
.

We consider it a highly interesting task to study the properties of this crowd activity and see potential applications to conjectures in combinatorics with a finite field analogon.

7.6.
Other Dynkin types

In this text, we have demonstrated in the sample case of
\SL_{n}
how our proposed solution to Tits’s dream leads to interesting geometric structures with the potential for future applications.

We expect that the formalism of this text extends to other
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-models of
\SL_{n}
and other algebraic groups. The subtlety here is that different linear presentations of the same algebraic group lead to different
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-models. In this sense,
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-geometry encodes the representation theory of an algebraic group.

In particular, we expect that Tits geometries of Dynkin types
{\mathsf{B}}
,
{\mathsf{C}}
and
{\mathsf{D}}
appear as a subcomplex of the space of
{\mathbb{K}}
-rational points of suitable simplicial band schemes that come equipped with suitable algebraic crowd activities. The space of all
{\mathbb{K}}
-rational points should reflect the concept of Coxeter matroids by Borovik, Gelfand and White (cf.
[
5
]
).

We hope that these remarks stimulate a rigorous treatment of Tits geometries, their
{{\mathbb{F}}_{1}}
-models and the relation to matroid theory in the proposed language of band schemes and algebraic crowds.

8.3.
Morphisms and epimorphisms

A

\Upgamma=(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{L},\mathbf{I})

is a map

\upalpha

\upalpha(\mathscr{P})=\mathscr{P}^{\prime}

\Upgamma:\Upgamma\mapsto\Upgamma
with
\Upgamma

of
\Upgamma

\Upgamma

Remark 8.4
.

In categorical language, an

is any morphism which is right-cancellative.
In the category of sets, this is trivially equivalent to asking that the morphism (map) is surjective. Since morphisms between generalized polygons are defined by the underlying maps between the point sets and line sets, it follows that in the categorical sense, epimorphisms between polygons are indeed as above.
