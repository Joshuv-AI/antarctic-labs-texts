Subscedastic weighted least squares estimates
Jordan Bryan, Haibo Zhou, Didong Li

Abstract
In the heteroscedastic linear model, the weighted least squares (WLS) estimate of the model coefficients is more efficient than the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate. However, the practical application of WLS is challenging because it requires knowledge of the error variances. Feasible weighted least squares (FLS) estimates, which use approximations of the variances when they are unknown, may either be more or less efficient than the OLS estimate depending on the quality of the approximation. A direct comparison between FLS and OLS has significant implications for the application of regression analysis in varied fields, yet such a comparison remains an unresolved challenge. In this study, we address this challenge by identifying the conditions under which FLS estimates using fixed weights demonstrate greater efficiency than the OLS estimate. These conditions provide guidance for the design of feasible estimates using random weights. They also shed light on how certain robust regression estimates behave with respect to the linear model with normal errors of unequal variance.



1
Introduction

Accounting for heteroscedasticity to improve the precision of regression estimates is an old, but not outdated practice. Indeed, modern statistical methods are still being adapted to incorporate information about heterogeneous variance in outcome variables. For instance,
Shah et al., (2023)
develop a consistent estimate of the error variance in a model for individualized treatment rules in order to stabilize their parameter estimates. In a different setting,
Bryan et al., (2023)
and
Bryan et al., (2024)
apply principles of variance estimation to devise more efficient estimates of water quality using fluorescence spectroscopy data. The question of how to address heteroscedasticity has continued to inspire new methodological developments primarily because, while classical least squares theory provides optimal estimates when the error variances are known, optimal procedures are more difficult to identify when the error variances must be estimated.

Such challenges arise even in the context of the standard linear model with independent errors:

{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(1)

where
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is known and full-rank, and

{\rm{E}}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]={\mathbf{0}},~{\rm{Cov}}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]={\rm{E}}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}]=\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}).

In this setting, the
weighted least squares estimate
(WLS)
({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}
has minimum variance among all linear unbiased estimates of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. However, computing the WLS estimate requires knowledge of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, which is unknown in practice. By contrast, the
ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate

({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\mathbf{y}}
can be computed in practice, since it is a function of
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
alone. OLS, though, can be significantly less efficient than WLS if there is a high degree of heteroscedasticity.

A so-called
feasible weighted least squares (FLS) estimate
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is obtained by plugging a computable estimate of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, denoted by
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, into the vector-valued function
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}:\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
, defined as

b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})=({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}},

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
denotes the set of
n\times n
diagonal positive definite matrices. This function yields the OLS estimate,
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n})
, and the WLS estimate,
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
, as special cases. FLS estimates have the benefit of being computable, and they have the potential to be more efficient than the OLS estimate. However, they also have the potential to be arbitrarily less precise than the OLS estimate if
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, the feasible substitute for
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, is far from the truth.

As only feasible and ordinary least squares estimates are available in practical settings, it is important to understand when the extra effort of designing feasible weights leads to a gain in efficiency relative to OLS. However, FLS estimates using random weights generally depend on
{\mathbf{y}}
in a non-linear fashion, which makes explicit derivation of their covariance matrices difficult. Perhaps in part because of this difficulty, one approach to dealing with heteroscedastic errors in the linear model has been to focus on estimating standard errors for the OLS estimate, which are asymptotically valid even when the form of heteroscedasticity is unknown.
White, 1980a ()
devised such standard errors, and subsequent work from
Arellano, (1987)
extended this approach to the case of temporal dependence between errors corresponding to repeated measures.
Driscoll and Kraay, (1998)
and
Vogelsang, (2012)
address consistent standard error estimation under both temporal and spatial dependence. While this line of research has provided several clever means of asymptotically valid inference, it generally does not address the issue of point estimation for the linear model coefficients, as pointed out by
Romano and Wolf, (2017)
. One exception is
Liang and Zeger, (1986)
, although they address estimation efficiency through simulation rather than theory.

On the other hand, most of the FLS literature related to point estimation uses the WLS estimate
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
, not the OLS estimate
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n})
, as reference. Early work on the finite-sample properties of FLS established upper bounds on the inefficiency of the OLS estimate
(
Anderson,, 1948
;
Watson,, 1967
;
Watson,, 1972
;
Knott,, 1975
)
or fixed-weight FLS estimates
(
Khatri and Rao,, 1981
;
Wang and Yang,, 1989
)
relative to the WLS estimate.
Kurata and Kariya, (1996)
used the Loewner partial order to bound the covariance matrix of a limited class of FLS estimates between two scalar multiples of the optimal
{\rm{Cov}}[b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})]
. Other authors, including
Fuller and Rao, (1978)
,
Carroll and Ruppert, (1988)
, and
Hansen, (2007)
examined the asymptotic properties of feasible weighted and generalized least squares estimates. These authors use the optimal
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
as the point of reference in the sense that they consistently estimate the true error covariance matrix, so that their estimates have the same asymptotic properties as
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
.

In this article, we offer new insights into the comparison of FLS estimates to the OLS estimate in the context of point estimation. Our starting point is the finite-sample perspective initiated by
Szroeter, (1994)
, in which we consider fixed-weight FLS estimates. We then use these results to draw conclusions about certain large-sample cases. Our primary contribution is to characterize a class of FLS estimates, which are guaranteed to be more efficient than the OLS estimate. We then demonstrate that certain robust regression estimates that are simple to compute satisfy a type of oracle efficiency property with respect to this class. This latter result puts our work in conversation with recent articles
(
Feng et al.,, 2024
;
Wiens,, 2024
)
that make explicit comparisons between OLS and alternatives; however, these articles do not discuss heteroscedasticity per-se.

In the case of a single regressor, we use variance as the measure of an estimate’s efficiency. In the case of multiple regressors, we primarily work with the
generalized variance
, although we show that our results also apply to the
total variance
as well. Following
Bloomfield and Watson, (1975)
, we define the generalized variance of a multivariate estimate as the determinant of its covariance matrix. For any FLS estimate that uses non-random weights
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, the covariance matrix of
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
with respect to (
1
) will be an instance of the matrix-valued function
H_{{\mathbf{X}}}:\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}\times\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{+}^{p}
defined as

H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})=({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}})^{-1},

(3)

where
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{p}
refers to the set of
p\times p
positive definite matrices. The FLS estimates forming our subclass of interest therefore take the form

b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}),~\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},

where

\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}:|H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\Omega})|\leq|H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})|\leq|H_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})|,~\forall{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\}.

(4)

We call
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
a
subscedastic set
because its elements are—in a sense made precise in Section
2
—less dispersed than
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. That
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is non-empty is guaranteed by a matrix Cauchy inequality
(
Marshall and Olkin,, 1990
)
, which says

{\rm{Cov}}[b_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{\Omega}})]=H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\preceq H_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})={\rm{Cov}}[b_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n})],~\forall{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},

where
\preceq
denotes the Loewner partial order on
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{p}
. Hence, both
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
{\mathbf{I}}_{n}
are in
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. In fact, a consequence of the Gauss-Markov Theorem
(
Aitken,, 1936
)
is that
H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\preceq H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}),~\forall{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},~\forall\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{+}
, so that
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}
may be equivalently defined using only the second inequality in (
4
).

In Section
2
of this article, we examine the subscedastic set
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
in the case of a single regressor and provide a sufficient condition so that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. Building on this result, we then develop the necessary and sufficient condition so that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
for
1\leq p<n
. In Section
3
we discuss the implications of these results for estimation in the linear model. In particular, we show directly that a FLS estimate need not use consistent weights in order to outperform the OLS estimate. In Section
4
, we provide a link between the results of Section 2 and the asymptotic variance of two robust regression estimates, one of which is derived from the
t
-distribution. We then conduct numerical experiments in Section
5
that demonstrate how the
t
estimate behaves in the context of the normal linear model with heteroscedasticity. We see that it performs favorably relative to parametric FLS estimates, especially when the parametric form of heteroscedasticity is misspecified. Finally, in Section
6
, we conclude with a discussion of possible extensions to this work. The proofs of all results may be found in Section
A
of the Appendix.

2
Properties of subscedastic weights

The subscedastic set
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is defined using the determinant inequality

|H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})|\leq|H_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})|.

(5)

Because the determinant is invariant to multiplication of its matrix argument by any
p\times p
orthogonal matrix, the inequality (
5
) is unchanged when
{\mathbf{U}}
, the matrix whose columns are the left singular vectors of
{\mathbf{X}}
, is substituted for
{\mathbf{X}}
itself. In this section, it will be convenient to work in terms of
{\mathbf{U}}
rather than
{\mathbf{X}}
.

To build intuition for the properties of subscedastic sets, consider the case of a single regressor so that
p=1
. Let
\mathcal{V}^{n}
denote the set of
n
-dimensional unit vectors, let
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
and define the functions

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\omega_{i},

\displaystyle\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}(\tilde{\omega}_{i}-\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}))(\omega_{i}-\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})).

Because
{\mathbf{u}}
is a unit vector, the functions
\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
and
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
behave, respectively, like the expectation and covariance functions of discrete random variables with supports determined by the diagonal entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and probability mass functions determined by the squared magnitude of the entries of
{\mathbf{u}}
. By rearranging terms in the inequality
H_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\leq H_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
, we can express the condition
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
in terms of the functions
\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
and
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
as follows:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\iff\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega})+\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\leq 0,~\forall{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n},

(6)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}
. This formulation is useful because it points to an intuitive sufficient condition so that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, namely that both
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega})
and
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
are non-positive for all
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
. Now, for any
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
,
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
will be non-positive if the diagonal entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
are in monotone non-decreasing relation with the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
(
Schmidt,, 2014
, Corollary 3.1). The condition in the following proposition is sufficient so that
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega})
is also non-positive for any choice of
{\mathbf{u}}
, implying that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a function such that for each
\omega>0

\displaystyle 1.

\displaystyle g(\omega)\leq g(\omega^{\prime}),~\forall\omega^{\prime}\geq\omega

(GRM)

\displaystyle 2.

\displaystyle g(\omega^{\prime})/\omega^{\prime}\leq g(\omega)/\omega,~\forall\omega^{\prime}\geq\omega.

Then if
\tilde{\omega}_{i}=g(\omega_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
.

In other words, for any
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
, it is guaranteed that
H_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\leq H_{{\mathbf{u}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
if the ranks of the diagonal entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
and the ranks of the diagonal entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
agree with those of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Another way of phrasing (
GRM
) is to say that the function
g(\omega)
is non-decreasing, while the function
g(\omega)/\omega
is non-increasing. Functions that satisfy (
GRM
) include, but are not limited to, fractional powers (
g(\omega)=\omega^{1/q},~q\geq 1
), translations by a positive constant (
g(\omega)=\omega+\lambda,~\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
), and functions of the form
g(\omega)=\log(\omega+\lambda),~\lambda>1
. Some examples are displayed in Figure
1
.

Functions of this type appear in the literature on robust covariance estimation
(
Maronna,, 1976
;
Romanov et al.,, 2023
)
. By
Rosenbaum, (1950)
Theorem 1.4.3, they constitute a subset of the class of
subadditive
functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. While they may be unbounded from above, a simple transformation can produce bounded versions of such functions.

Proposition 2
.

If
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a function satisfying (
GRM
), then for non-negative constants
\lambda,\gamma
the function

f(\omega)=\frac{1}{1/g(\omega)+\lambda}+\gamma

(7)

also satisfies it, where
f
has the additional property of being bounded from above and below by
1/\lambda+\gamma
and
\gamma
, respectively.

Functions satisfying (
GRM
) also have a connection with the theory of
majorization
, which has many applications to matrix trace and determinant inequalities. Letting
{\mathbf{a}}\prec{\mathbf{b}}
denote that
{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
majorizes
{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
Marshall et al.,, 2011
for a definition), we have the following result:

Proposition 3
.

Let
g
be a function satisfying (
GRM
). Then

\left(\frac{g(\omega_{1})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\omega_{i})},\dots,\frac{g(\omega_{n})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\omega_{i})}\right)\prec\left(\frac{\omega_{1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}},\dots,\frac{\omega_{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}}\right).

Figure 1:
Three functions satisfying (
GRM
). From left to right, the functions are
\omega^{1/q}
,
\sqrt{w}/\{\int_{-k}^{k}\mathrm{exp}(-z^{2}/2\omega)dz\}
, and
g_{1,\nu}(\omega)
, where this last function is defined in Theorem
2
. For the purposes of visualization, the functions have been normalized to attain a maximum value of 1 at
\omega=10
.

While (
GRM
) is an interesting property, it can only provide a partial characterization of
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. This is because
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega})\leq 0,~\forall{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
is not necessary for the right hand side of (
6
) to hold. On the other hand, the inequality

\displaystyle\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega})-\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega})-\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})^{2}\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\boldsymbol{\Omega})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\omega_{i}(\tilde{\phi}_{i}-\mathrm{e}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}))^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0

demonstrates that
\mathrm{c}_{{\mathbf{u}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})\leq 0,~\forall{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
is necessary to ensure
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. Hence, a necessary and sufficient condition for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
must include a monotonicity requirement on the diagonal entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
along with a weaker growth restriction than that of (
GRM
). The following theorem shows that this relaxed restriction can be expressed in terms of pairs of diagonal entries of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
and let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\omega}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\omega}_{n})\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
. Then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
if and only if

1\leq\frac{\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{\tilde{\omega}_{j}}\leq 2\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}-1

(8)

for all
\omega_{i}\geq\omega_{j}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\},j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

The monotonicity requirement on the diagonal elements of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is expressed through the first inequality in (
8
), while the weak growth restriction is expressed through the second.

As it is stated, Theorem
1
only refers to the variance of a single regression coefficient. The next results show that (
8
) is not unique to the univariate case, nor is it merely a peculiarity of the generalized variance criterion used to define
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
1\leq p<n
. Then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
if and only if (
8
) holds for all
\omega_{i}\geq\omega_{j}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\},j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
1\leq p<n
. Define the set

\mathcal{K}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}:\text{\rm trace}[H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})]\leq\text{\rm trace}[H_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})],~\forall{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\}.

Then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}\iff\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}
.

Corollary
1
says that the subscedastic set induced by a given
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
does not depend on the number of regressors, provided that number is less than
n
. Corollary
2
shows that the total variance of a FLS estimate using subscedastic weights is less than that of the OLS estimate. While it is not the focus of this article, the next result shows that the case of non-diagonal error covariance can also be partially addressed by (
8
).

Corollary 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}
be simultaneously diagonalizable, and let
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}
and
\{\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\lambda}_{n}\}
be the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, respectively. Then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
if and only if

1\leq\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{i}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{j}}\leq 2\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{j}}-1

(9)

for all
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{j}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\},j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

We conclude this section by stating some additional properties of subscedastic weights that can be derived from (
8
). First, Corollary
1
implies that the conclusion of Proposition
1
also holds for
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}
when
p>1
. This can be seen by the following reasoning: if the diagonal elements of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
satisfy (
GRM
), then
\tilde{\omega}_{i}/\tilde{\omega}_{j}\geq 1
for all
\omega_{i}\geq\omega_{j}
, and
\tilde{\omega}_{i}\omega_{j}/(\tilde{\omega}_{j}\omega_{i})\leq 1\leq 2-(\omega_{j}/\omega_{i})
for all
\omega_{i}\geq\omega_{j}
. Multiplying the latter inequalities by
\omega_{i}/\ \omega_{j}
shows that (
GRM
) implies (
8
). Next, because (
8
) depends only on pairwise ratios of diagonal elements,
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
has what
Bilodeau, (1990)
and
Kariya and Kurata, (2004)
call the “symmetric inverse property,” meaning

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\iff\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}}.

Finally,
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}
is a convex cone on
\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
. The cone property of
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is clear from the fact that
H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(s\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})=H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}),~\forall s>0
. The convexity of
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
can be derived directly from (
8
): given
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
,

\tilde{\omega}_{j}\leq\tilde{\omega}_{i}\leq 2\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}\tilde{\omega}_{j}-\tilde{\omega}_{j},~\text{ and }~\tilde{\psi}_{j}\leq\tilde{\psi}_{i}\leq 2\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}\tilde{\psi}_{j}-\tilde{\psi}_{j},

for all
\omega_{i}\geq\omega_{j}
. For any
0\leq t\leq 1
, this implies

t\tilde{\omega}_{j}+(1-t)\tilde{\psi}_{j}\leq t\tilde{\omega}_{i}+(1-t)\tilde{\psi}_{i}\leq 2\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}\{t\tilde{\omega}_{j}+(1-t)\tilde{\psi}_{j}\}-\{t\tilde{\omega}_{j}+(1-t)\tilde{\psi}_{j}\},

for all
\omega_{i}\geq\omega_{j}
, so
t\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+(1-t)\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. Along with the cone property, convexity implies that

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\implies\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+s{\mathbf{I}}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},~\forall s>0,

(10)

so regularized subscedastic weights are also subscedastic weights.

3
Estimation in the linear model

The matrix
H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
used to define the notion of a subscedastic set is equal to the covariance matrix of the FLS estimate
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
under (
1
) when
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is any fixed matrix in
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{+}
. As seen in the previous section, the conditions so that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is a member of
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
depend on the unknown
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. A natural question is then: to what extent is
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
actually feasible? More broadly, what is the relevance of Section
2
to estimation in practice if one must know
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
to choose an appropriate
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
?

To begin, we observe that one does not need to know the values of the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. One implication of Corollary
1
is that knowing the ranks of
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
along with a lower bound on the minimum ratio between consecutive ordered elements
\gamma<\min_{i=j+1}\{\omega_{(i)}/\omega_{(j)}\}
would be sufficient to construct an
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
and a corresponding
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
that is guaranteed to outperform the OLS estimate. By contrast, to reproduce
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
itself up to a scale factor, it would be necessary to know the ranks of the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
along with
n-1
additional ratios, for instance the collection of all ratios between consecutive ordered elements. Thus, the task of finding an optimal estimate (the WLS estimate) depends on more unknowns than the task of finding an estimate that is at least better than the OLS estimate.

This latter, more modest goal, brings otherwise impossible tasks into the feasible realm in certain simple cases. For instance, consider the groupwise heteroscedastic linear model with error covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\theta_{1}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n_{1}}&&{\mathbf{0}}\\
&\ddots&\\
{\mathbf{0}}&&\theta_{K}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n_{K}}\end{array}\right],

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
,
n=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}
. While it is implausible that one knows the exact values of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in advance, it is at least more plausible that, for small
K
, one knows the ordering of the elements of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and that no group’s error variance is within some factor
\gamma\geq 1
of another’s. Let
\tilde{\theta}_{(1)}^{2}=1
, and set
\tilde{\theta}_{(i)}^{2}:=(2\gamma-1)\tilde{\theta}_{(i-1)}^{2}
for each
i>1
. Then, using the notation above,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
defines a matrix whose diagonal elements are subscedastic weights.

Alternatively, consider the linear model with error variances depending on a single covariate through a parameterized scedastic function

\omega_{i}=v_{\theta}(x_{i,1}),~i=1,\dots,n.

Common examples of
v_{\theta}
, all of which are used in the simulation studies of
Romano and Wolf, (2017)
, include

\displaystyle v_{{\theta}}(x)

\displaystyle=|x|^{\theta}

(11)

\displaystyle v_{{\theta}}(x)

\displaystyle=\{\log|x|\}^{\theta}

\displaystyle v_{{\theta}}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{\theta|x|+\theta|x|^{2}}.

Conveniently, in each specification above, the ranks of
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
are equivalent to the ranks of
(|x_{1,1}|,\dots,|x_{n,1}|)
, which are known. Hence, if one can identify a lower bound
\gamma
for the minimum plausible value of
\theta
, one can simply take
\tilde{\omega}_{i}=v_{\gamma}(x_{i,1})
, and the corresponding
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
will outperform the OLS estimate. This is due to the fact that, for each of the scedastic functions above,
\gamma\leq\theta
implies that
v_{\gamma}(x)
is a fractional power of
v_{\theta}(x)
and thus satisfies (
GRM
).

Finally, there are simple yet common examples of linear models with non-diagonal covariance for which the subscedastic property can serve as a guide for the design of feasible weights. Suppose that data are recorded on biological specimens, which are processed in
K
distinct batches. Sources of idiosyncratic variation due to the processing of the specimens may introduce marginal correlation among observations within batches. This may be modeled as follows

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta}+{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{z}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(12)

\displaystyle{\rm{E}}[{\mathbf{z}}]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}},{\rm{Cov}}[{\mathbf{z}}]=\theta_{1}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{K}

\displaystyle{\rm{E}}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle={\mathbf{0}},{\rm{Cov}}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]=\theta_{0}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n},

where
{\mathbf{A}}
is an
n\times K
indicator matrix such that
A_{ik}=1
if observation
i
is in batch
k
, and
A_{ik}=0
otherwise. Model (
12
) specifies a linear mixed effects model with random intercepts, which induces a non-diagonal marginal error covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\theta_{1}^{2}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+\theta_{0}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n}.

Conveniently, both the eigenvectors of this matrix and the ordering of its eigenvalues are known as long as
{\mathbf{A}}
is known. Assume that the number of observations in batch
k
is
n_{k}
, and that
n_{1}\geq\dots\geq n_{K}
. Then the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are, respectively,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{U}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{A}}&{\mathbf{Q}}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(1/\sqrt{n_{1}},\dots,1/\sqrt{n_{K}},1,\dots,1),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(n_{1}\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{0}^{2},\dots,n_{K}\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{0}^{2},\theta_{0}^{2},\dots,\theta_{0}^{2}),

where
{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n,n-K}
is a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the null space of
{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
. Here, subscedastic weights may be designed if one has knowledge of, or a conservative upper bound on
\theta_{0}^{2}/\theta_{1}^{2}
, the ratio of the isotropic variability to the batch variability. Specifically, if
\gamma\geq\theta_{0}^{2}/\theta_{1}^{2}
, then it can be checked that

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{U}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top},

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(n_{1}+\gamma,\dots,n_{K}+\gamma,\gamma,\dots,\gamma),

with
{\mathbf{U}}
defined as above satisfies (
8
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Of course this same reasoning applies to the case of a single (
K=1
) “batch,” which for small
\theta_{1}^{2}
would correspond to weak equi-correlation between all errors.

While the conclusions above have the appeal of being valid for any sample size, they do not yield much insight into the behavior of FLS estimates using random weights. Still, the subscedastic property may be used to assess such estimates when
n
is large. One conclusion is that a matrix of random weights
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{n}
need not be consistent for
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
in order to produce an estimate that eventually outperforms the OLS estimate. Informally, if
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{n}
is asymptotically equal to some
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}
, then
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{n})
outperforms the OLS estimate if
n
is large enough. Both
Atkinson et al., (2016)
and
Romano and Wolf, (2017)
provide numerical evidence for this claim by evaluating the variance of FLS estimates when they are misspecified with respect to the true form of heteroscedasticity. Here, we give sufficient conditions on the probability limit of feasible weights
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{n}
so that they yield an estimate that outperforms the OLS estimate as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Since the dimension of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
grows with
n
, the statement of these conditions requires a modified notation that replaces matrices with infinite sequences.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\{\tilde{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be sequences of positive scalars, let
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of
p
-dimensional vectors, and let
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be sequences of random variables. Assuming that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}~\text{ and }~\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\tilde{\omega}^{2}_{i}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}

exist, define the coefficient estimates

b^{n}_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\{\tilde{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty})=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\right\}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}}{\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right\}

and

b^{n}_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty})=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\hat{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\right\}^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}}{\hat{\omega}_{i}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right\},

and suppose that
\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1/\hat{\omega}_{i}-1/\tilde{\omega}_{i}){\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbf{0}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1/\hat{\omega}_{i}-1/\tilde{\omega}_{i})(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}){\mathbf{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbf{0}}.

Then if
\{\tilde{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are such that

\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\omega}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\omega}_{n})\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\mathrm{diag}({\omega}_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})}

for any positive integer
n\geq 2
, it follows that

\frac{|{\rm{Cov}}[b^{n}_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty})]|}{|{\rm{Cov}}[b^{n}_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\{1\})]|}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|{\rm{Cov}}[b^{n}_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\{\tilde{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty})]|}{|{\rm{Cov}}[b^{n}_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\{1\})]|}\leq 1,

where
\{1\}
denotes the constant sequence.

Proposition
4
says that a feasible estimate for the error variances need not have the same parametric form as the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in order to yield coefficient estimates that eventually outperform the OLS estimate. Here again, the benefits of moderating one’s goals in estimation become apparent. Any feasible estimate satisfying the consistency properties of Proposition
4
will be asymptotically optimal for exactly one sequence of error variances. On the other hand, the same estimate will outperform OLS for a whole family of such sequences.

This observation motivates a general prescription for designing FLS estimates that are conservative with respect to misspecification of the scedastic function. Namely, one can add a small multiple of the identity to the matrix of feasible weights. For simplicity, consider the finite sample case where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
is non-random. Then for any
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\implies\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+s{\mathbf{I}}_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},~\forall s>0.

This is due to the fact that the identity is the unique matrix that is in
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
for any choice of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
, and the fact that
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is a convex cone.
If one defines the set

\mathcal{W}^{p}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}=\{\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{+}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\},

then it follows that

\mathcal{W}^{p}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{p}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+s{\mathbf{I}}_{n}},

with the inclusion above being strict as long as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
is not proportional to the identity. The subset of model (
1
) under which
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}+s{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
outperforms
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n})
is evidently larger than that under which
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}})
outperforms
b_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n})
. This idea can be combined with the ideas of Proposition
4
to obtain a similar statement for FLS estimates with random weights that converge in probability to some fixed
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
.

4
Robust estimates of regression coefficients

Another informal prescription for dealing with heteroscedasticity when the scedastic function is unknown is to use a robust regression estimate rather than the OLS estimate. Here we demonstrate that subscedastic sets provide an explanation for some of the favorable properties of two robust estimates in the context of the heteroscedastic linear model with normally distributed errors.

First, we consider the maximum marginal likelihood estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
under the hierarchical linear model

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}|\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle\sim N_{n}({\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\Omega})

(13)

\displaystyle\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{iid}{\sim}IG(\nu/2,\nu\omega_{0}/2),

where
IG
denotes the inverse gamma distribution. Marginalizing over the
\omega_{i}
’s, the
y_{i}
’s in this model are independent realizations of
t
-distributed random variables, each with mean
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
, scale
\omega_{0}
, and degrees of freedom
\nu
. The independent
t
model and its maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
have previously been studied in the context of robust regression, in particular by
Lange et al., (1989)
who derived several of its properties in the well-specified case.
Lange and Sinsheimer, (1993)
also discuss how
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
may be computed using an iteratively reweighted least squares algorithm (see also Section
B
of the Appendix).

Our interest lies in the asymptotic behavior of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
in the misspecified case, specifically when the true model is the heteroscedastic linear model (
1
) with normally distributed errors. Because
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
is the solution to a maximization problem, its asymptotic distribution under this type of misspecification can be understood through the framework of
M
-estimation
(
Huber,, 1973
;
Huber and Ronchetti,, 2009
)
. Applying the
M
-estimation results of
Stefanski and Boos, (2002)
, we provide the asymptotic covariance matrix of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

The asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
in the model (
1
) with normally distributed errors is described by

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N_{p}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})

where

\displaystyle{\mathbf{B}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f_{\omega_{0},\nu}(\omega_{i})}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{g_{\omega_{0},\nu}(\omega_{i})}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top},

and

\displaystyle f_{\omega_{0},\nu}(\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=2\omega_{i}\left\{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega_{0}}{\omega_{i}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i}}{\nu\omega_{0}}}\right)e^{\frac{\nu\omega_{0}}{2\omega_{i}}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega_{0}}{\omega_{i}}}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz\right)-1\right\}^{-1},

\displaystyle g_{\omega_{0},\nu}(\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}\left\{1-\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega_{0}}{\omega_{i}}}e^{\frac{\nu\omega_{0}}{2\omega_{i}}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega_{0}}{\omega_{i}}}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz\right)\right\}^{-1}.

Furthermore,
g_{\omega_{0},\nu}
satisfies (
GRM
) for any
\omega_{0},\nu>0
.

The last part of the result above pertaining to the (
GRM
) property allows us to obtain insight into how
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
behaves relative to the OLS estimate. Specifically, if
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a bounded sequence, then the limiting generalized variance of
\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
can be bounded above by a constant multiple of the limiting generalized variance of
\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}
. Likewise with the limiting total variance.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose that
\sup\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}=\omega_{\max}
. Then define

C(\omega_{0},\nu,\omega_{\mathrm{max}})=\frac{g_{\omega_{0},\nu}(\omega_{\max})^{2}}{\omega_{\max}f_{\omega_{0},\nu}(\omega_{\max})}.

Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{\rm trace}[{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]]\leq C(\omega_{0},\nu,\omega_{\mathrm{max}})\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{\rm trace}[{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}]]

(14)

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]|\leq C(\omega_{0},\nu,\omega_{\mathrm{max}})^{p}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}]|.

(15)

Similar calculations may be done for another well-known robust regression estimate, the Huber estimate, defined as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\mathrm{argmin}}~~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{k}(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}),

where

\rho_{k}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}z^{2}/2&~~|z|<k\\
k|z|-k^{2}/2&~~|z|\geq k\end{array}\right..

The asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}
with respect to (
1
) is easily obtained from the formulae of
Huber, (1964)
. The next result describes this distribution and shows that it too contains weights satisfying the (
GRM
) property.

Proposition 5
.

The asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}
in the model (
1
) with normally distributed errors is described by

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N_{p}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{V}}^{-1}{\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{V}}^{-1})

where

\displaystyle{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f_{k}(\omega_{i})}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{g_{k}(\omega_{i})}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top},

and

\displaystyle f_{k}(\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\omega_{i}}}\int_{-k}^{k}z^{2}e^{-z^{2}/2\omega_{i}}dz+\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\omega_{i}}}\int_{-\infty}^{-k}e^{-z^{2}/2\omega_{i}}dz+\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\omega_{i}}}\int_{k}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}/2\omega_{i}}dz\right\}^{-1}

\displaystyle g_{k}(\omega_{i})

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\omega_{i}}}\int_{-k}^{k}e^{-z^{2}/2\omega_{i}}dz\right\}^{-1}.

Furthermore,
g_{k}
satisfies (
GRM
) for all
k>0
.

In analogy to the result in Corollary
4
, Proposition
5
implies that the asymptotic generalized and total variance of
\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}
are bounded above by a constant times those of a FLS estimate using subscedastic weights.

Corollary 5
.

Suppose that
\sup\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}=\omega_{\max}

C(k,\omega_{\mathrm{max}})=\frac{g_{k}(\omega_{\max})^{2}}{\omega_{\max}f_{k}(\omega_{\max})}.

Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{\rm trace}[{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}]]\leq C(k,\omega_{\mathrm{max}})\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{\rm trace}[{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}]]

(16)

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}]|\leq C(k,\omega_{\mathrm{max}})^{p}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|{\rm{Cov}}[\sqrt{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}]|.

(17)

4.1
Inference for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}

Often, it is of interest to calculate confidence intervals for the coefficients in a regression to accompany point estimates. In what follows we propose a method for calculating asymptotically valid confidence intervals for the
t
-derived estimates examined above. Our confidence intervals are based on standard errors derived from an empirical approximation to the asymptotic covariance matrix in Theorem
2
.

Let
\ell(y_{i};\boldsymbol{\beta})
refer to the log-likelihood of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
for a single observation in the independent
t
error model (
13
). From the calculations in the proof of Theorem
2
, we have

\nabla\ell(y_{i};\boldsymbol{\beta})\nabla\ell(y_{i};\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}=(\nu+1)^{2}\frac{(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}}{\{\nu\omega_{0}+(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}\}^{2}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top},

and

\nabla^{2}\ell(y_{i};\boldsymbol{\beta})=(\nu+1)\frac{(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}-\nu\omega_{0}}{\{\nu\omega_{0}+(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}\}^{2}}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}.

Replacing the expectations of these quantities with their empirical counterparts evaluated at
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
yields our estimate of the covariance matrix of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}

\widehat{{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}\ell(y_{i};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T})\right\}^{-1}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell(y_{i};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T})\nabla\ell(y_{i};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T})^{\top}\right\}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla^{2}\ell(y_{i};\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T})\right\}^{-1}.

Theorems from
Hoadley, (1971)
,
White, 1980b ()
, and
Iverson and Randles, (1989)
suggest that
\widehat{{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]}
consistently estimates
{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]
, so that

\left[(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T})_{j}-Z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]}_{jj}^{1/2},(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T})_{j}+Z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{{\rm{Cov}}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}]}_{jj}^{1/2}\right]

defines a confidence interval for the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
regression coefficient with asymptotic coverage at level
1-\alpha
.

Simulations and real-world examples

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{H}

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}
and its asymptotic standard errors can substantially outperform common-use variants of heteroscedasticity-consistent intervals based on the OLS estimate.

6
Discussion

The experiments of the previous section suggest that
t
-derived estimates can be substantially more efficient than the OLS estimate in the heteroscedastic linear model with normally distributed errors. The theoretical results in this article suggest that this improvement in efficiency can be attributed to a quasi-oracle property of the
t
-derived estimates: in the limit, these estimates are sub-optimal, but are still preferable to OLS because they are similar to a FLS estimate using subscedastic weights. From the perspective of point estimation in the heteroscedastic linear model, we contend that
t
-derived estimates—perhaps with fixed degrees of freedom for
n<100
—may be used as a default estimate preferable to OLS. For inference purposes, we present evidence that asymptotically valid confidence intervals for
t
-derived estimates may be obtained from an empirical approximation to the asymptotic covariance matrix. A more careful theoretical justification for the use of these intervals may be warranted in future work. However, the empirical results presented here are promising in that the average interval width tends to be much smaller than that of popular heteroscedasticity-consistent alternatives.

In other future theoretical work, we hope to establish more results that apply to non-diagonal covariance matrices. Part of the challenge with doing so is that such results must depend on simultaneous conditions on the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, which may be more opaque than the relatively simple subscedastic criterion in (
8
). It is also possible that subscedastic sets described by different criteria than (
8
) might arise by using other measures of relative multivariate variance besides the generalized and total variances considered in this article. However, we conjecture that (
8
) is the necessary and sufficient subscedastic condition for at least one additional measure of multivariate relative variance, described by

\frac{1}{p}\text{\rm trace}\left\{H_{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\boldsymbol{\Omega})H_{{\mathbf{X}}}({\mathbf{I}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega})^{-1}\right\}.

(19)

While it is relatively easy to verify the necessity of (
8
) in this case, we have not yet found a proof of its sufficiency.

Finally, we have also shown that subscedastic sets can be a lens through which to analyze regression
M
-estimates other than those derived from the
t
-distribution. It would be interesting to investigate more connections between subscedasticity, the influence functions of various robust regression estimates, and the distribution of the error terms.

Proof of Theorem
1

To prove Theorem
1
, we first present the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{V}^{n}
denote the set of all
n
-dimensional unit vectors. Define the function
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

k({\mathbf{u}})={\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{u}}-({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{u}})^{2}({\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{u}}).

\sup_{{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}}~k({\mathbf{u}})=\sup_{{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n},\|{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0}\leq 2}~k({\mathbf{u}}).

Proof.

We will prove the statement by providing, for any
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
, a corresponding
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
with
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|_{0}\leq 2
such that

k({\mathbf{u}})\leq k({\mathbf{v}}).

Given
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{n}

{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
and let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}={\mathbf{u}}\odot{\mathbf{u}}
, where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. Further, let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{1/2}
denote the entrywise positive square root of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
so that
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{1/2})_{i}=|u_{i}|
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,

may be written as

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\odot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\odot\boldsymbol{\omega})-(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}})^{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}),

(20)

where
\tilde{\phi},\boldsymbol{\omega}
are
n
-dimensional vectors containing the diagonal elements of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, respectively.

If
\|{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0}\leq 2
, then setting
{\mathbf{v}}:={\mathbf{u}}
yields a trivial bound satisfying the norm constraint. Suppose instead that
\|{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0}=d>4
, and let
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{u}}}
be the index set of the non-zero entries of
{\mathbf{u}}
. Then there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\odot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\odot\boldsymbol{\omega})=0,~\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}=0,~\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=0,~\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}{\mathbf{1}}_{n}=0

(21)

and
\eta_{i}=0
for

exists because these restrictions define a system of at most

independent linear equations in

21

\eta_{i}=0
for

. As

. If
\boldsymbol{\omega},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}

An
\boldsymbol{\eta}
satisfying the restrictions above must also have at least one negative entry and one positive entry among its non-zero entries. This is due to the fact that
\boldsymbol{\omega},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
and
{\mathbf{1}}_{n}

\boldsymbol{\eta}
cannot lie in either the positive orthant or the negative orthant. Consequently, there exists an

\epsilon=\min_{\{i:\eta_{i}<0\}}|\alpha_{i}/\eta_{i}|,

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

{\mathbf{v}}:=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\epsilon\boldsymbol{\eta})^{1/2}
produces a unit vector with

norm equal to

. The fact that
{\mathbf{v}}
is a unit vector follows from

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\alpha_{i}+\epsilon\eta_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\epsilon\eta_{i})

\displaystyle={\mathbf{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\epsilon{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle=1,

where we used the last linear equation in (

{\mathbf{v}}

. Finally,

because the first three linear equations in (

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Each of the steps above can be repeated until one begins the process with

It remains to address the case that

.

\|{\mathbf{u}}\|_{0}=4
, then there exists a non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\odot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\odot\boldsymbol{\omega})>0,~\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}=0,~\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=0,~\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}{\mathbf{1}}_{n}=0

and
\eta_{i}=0
for each

and

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{z}}_{j}}{({\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{z}}_{j})^{2}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\right).

1
, each of the summands in the last line above is less than or equal to zero. Therefore,

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n,p}}{\sup}~\text{\rm trace}\left\{({\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{U}})^{-1}{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{U}})^{-1}-{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{U}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\underset{{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathcal{V}^{n,p}}{\sup}~\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{z}}_{j}}{({\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{z}}_{j})^{2}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\underset{{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}}{\sup}\left(\frac{{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{z}}_{j}}{({\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\mathbf{z}}_{j})^{2}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\mathbf{z}}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0,

which shows that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\in\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{p}
.

(

do not satisfy

. Then there exists at least one pair of indices,

, for which

and
j

A.9
Proof of Theorem
2

To prove Theorem
2
, we first present and prove the following four technical lemmas A2 to A5.

Lemma 2
.

Let
c>0
. Then

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi/2}}{c}+\frac{\pi(1-c)}{2c^{3/2}}e^{c/2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2}),

where
\mathrm{erfc}(c)=(2/\sqrt{\pi})\int_{c}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}}dr
is the complementary error function.

Proof.

The expectation and variance of an
\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
random variable are
1/\lambda
and
1/\lambda^{2}
, respectively. So

\int_{0}^{\infty}x\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=\frac{1}{\lambda}~\text{ and }~\int_{0}^{\infty}(x-1/\lambda)^{2}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}.

From this, we obtain the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}.

Apply this identity to see that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}(c+z^{2})e^{-(c+z^{2})t}e^{-z^{2}/2}dtdz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}e^{-ct}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(c+z^{2})e^{-tz^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dzdt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-1/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(c+z^{2})\frac{\sqrt{2t+1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(2t+1)z^{2}/2}dzdt.

Recognizing the interior integral as an expectation with respect to a normal density with mean
0
and variance
1/(2t+1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-1/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}\{c+(2t+1)^{-1}\}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\left\{c\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-1/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-3/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt\right\}.

For both integrals above, substitute
r=\sqrt{2t+1}
so that
dr=(2t+1)^{-1/2}dt
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-1/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\{(r^{2}-1)/2\}^{2}e^{-c(r^{2}-1)/2}dr,

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-3/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt=\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-2}\{(r^{2}-1)/2\}^{2}e^{-c(r^{2}-1)/2}dr.

This yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}e^{c/2}}{8}\int_{1}^{\infty}(r^{-2}+c)(r^{2}-1)^{2}e^{-cr^{2}/2}dr

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}e^{c/2}}{8c^{3/2}}\int_{\sqrt{c}}^{\infty}(r^{-2}+1)(r^{2}-c)^{2}e^{-r^{2}/2}dr

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{c/2}}{4c^{3/2}}\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}\{4r^{4}+2(1-2c)r^{2}+(c^{2}-2c)+c^{2}/(2r^{2})\}e^{-r^{2}}dr,

where in the second and third lines we rescaled the variable of integration without changing its symbol. Evaluating each of the summands above using integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}4r^{4}e^{-r^{2}}dr=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\pi}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})+\frac{\sqrt{c}e^{-c/2}(c+3)}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}2(1-2c)r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}dr=\frac{1-2c}{2}\left\{\sqrt{\pi}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})+\sqrt{2c}e^{-c/2}\right\},

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}(c^{2}-2c)e^{-r^{2}}dr=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(c^{2}-2c)}{2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2}),

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}\{c^{2}/(2r^{2})\}e^{-r^{2}}dr=c^{2}\left\{-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})+\frac{e^{-c/2}}{\sqrt{2c}}\right\}.

From the display above, the sum of the constants involving the complementary error function is

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{1-2c}{2}\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{c^{2}-2c}{2}\sqrt{\pi}-(c^{2}/2)\sqrt{\pi}=2\sqrt{\pi}\left\{1-c\right\}.

The sum of the constants in front of
e^{-c/2}
is

\frac{\sqrt{c}(c+3)}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{c}(1-2c)}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{c^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}}=4\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}}.

Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi/2}}{c}+\frac{\pi(1-c)}{2c^{3/2}}e^{c/2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
c>0
. Then

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2}}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\pi(\sqrt{c}+1/\sqrt{c})e^{c/2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})-\sqrt{2\pi}\right\}.

Proof.

The expectation and variance of an
\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
random variable are
1/\lambda
and
1/\lambda^{2}

\int_{0}^{\infty}x\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=\frac{1}{\lambda}~\text{ and }~\int_{0}^{\infty}(x-1/\lambda)^{2}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}.

From this, we obtain the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}.

Apply this identity to see that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2}}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}(c+z^{2})e^{-(c+z^{2})t}z^{2}e^{-z^{2}/2}dtdz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2}e^{-ct}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{2}(c+z^{2})e^{-tz^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dzdt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-1/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(cz^{2}+z^{4})\frac{\sqrt{2t+1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(2t+1)z^{2}/2}dzdt.

Recognizing the interior integral as an expectation with respect to a normal density with mean
0
and variance
1/(2t+1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-1/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}\{c(2t+1)^{-1}+3(2t+1)^{-2}\}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\left\{c\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-3/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt+3\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-5/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt\right\}.

For both integrals above, substitute
r=\sqrt{2t+1}
so that
dr=(2t+1)^{-1/2}dt
. Then

\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-3/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt=\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-2}\{(r^{2}-1)/2\}^{2}e^{-c(r^{2}-1)/2}dr.

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}(2t+1)^{-5/2}t^{2}e^{-ct}dt=\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-4}\{(r^{2}-1)/2\}^{2}e^{-c(r^{2}-1)/2}dr.

This yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}e^{c/2}}{8}\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{-2}(3r^{-2}+c)(r^{2}-1)^{2}e^{-cr^{2}/2}dr

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}e^{c/2}}{8\sqrt{c}}\int_{\sqrt{c}}^{\infty}r^{-2}(3r^{-2}+1)(r^{2}-c)^{2}e^{-r^{2}/2}dr

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{c/2}}{4\sqrt{c}}\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}\{2r^{2}+(c^{2}-6c)/(2r^{2})+(3-2c)+3c^{2}/(4r^{4})\}e^{-r^{2}}dr

where in the second and third lines we rescaled the variable of integration without changing its symbol. Evaluating each of the summands above using integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}2r^{2}e^{-r^{2}}dr=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sqrt{\pi}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})+\sqrt{2c}e^{-c/2}\right\},

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}(c^{2}-6c)/(2r^{2})e^{-r^{2}}dr=(c^{2}-6c)\left\{-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})+\frac{e^{-c/2}}{\sqrt{2c}}\right\},

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}(3-2c)e^{-r^{2}}dr=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}(3-2c)}{2}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2}),

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{c/2}}^{\infty}\{3c^{2}/(4r^{4})\}e^{-r^{2}}dr=\frac{c^{2}}{2}\left\{\sqrt{\pi}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})-\frac{\sqrt{2}(c-1)e^{-c/2}}{c^{3/2}}\right\}.

From the display above, the sum of the constants involving the complementary error function is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi}-\frac{c^{2}-6c}{2}\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{3-2c}{2}\sqrt{\pi}+(c^{2}/2)\sqrt{\pi}=2\sqrt{\pi}\left\{1+c\right\}.

The sum of the constants in front of
e^{-c/2}
is

\frac{\sqrt{2c}}{2}+\frac{c^{2}-6c}{\sqrt{2c}}-\frac{\sqrt{2c}(c-1)}{2}=-\frac{4\sqrt{c}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2}}{(c+z^{2})^{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(1+c)e^{c/2}}{2\sqrt{c}}\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{c/2})-\sqrt{\pi/2},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
z
be a truncated standard normal random variable taking values on
(a,\infty)
with probability density function

p(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}\bigg/\left(1-\int_{-\infty}^{a}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz\right),

for some
a>0
.
Then the expectation of
z
is bounded below and above as follows

\frac{a(a^{2}+3)}{a^{2}+2}\leq{\rm{E}}[z]\leq a+1/a.

Proof.

From the definition

{\rm{E}}[z]=\int_{a}^{\infty}p(z)dz=\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}ze^{-z^{2}/2}dz\bigg/\left(1-\int_{-\infty}^{a}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz\right).

Due to the fact that
z/a\geq 1
on the domain of integration, it follows that

\displaystyle{\rm{E}}[z]

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}ze^{-z^{2}/2}dz\bigg/\left(1-\int_{-\infty}^{a}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz\right)

\displaystyle=a+\frac{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(z-a)e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}

\displaystyle\leq a+\frac{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(z^{2}/a-z)e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}

\displaystyle=a+\frac{1}{a}\frac{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}z^{2}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}-\frac{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}ze^{-z^{2}/2}dz}{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}

\displaystyle=a+\frac{1}{a}\left(1+a\frac{e^{-a^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}}{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}\right)-\frac{e^{-a^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}}{\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}

\displaystyle=a+1/a,

where in the fifth line we applied the formulae for the first and second moments of a truncated standard normal random variable
(
Johnson et al.,, 1994
)
.

For the lower bound, we apply an upper bound on the so-called
Q
-function, defined as

Q(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{erfc}(x/\sqrt{2})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz.

This bound, developed by
Perić et al., (2019)
, states that

Q(x)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{x^{2}+2}{x(x^{2}+3)}e^{-x^{2}/2},~x>0.

(32)

By rearranging terms, see that

{\rm{E}}[z]=\frac{e^{-a^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}}{Q(a)},

so that applying (
32
) yields

{\rm{E}}[z]\geq\frac{a(a^{2}+3)}{a^{2}+2},

and the proof is done.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Define the function

h(x):=\sqrt{2\pi}xe^{x^{2}/2}\int_{-\infty}^{-x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz.

The following properties hold

1.

h(x)
is non-decreasing for
x>0
.

2.

x^{2}/(x^{2}+1)\leq h(x)\leq(x^{2}+2)/(x^{2}+3)
for
x>0
.

3.

h(x)
is concave for
x>0
.

4.

x^{2}\{1-h(x)\}
is non-decreasing for
x>0
.

Proof.

To prove the first property, note that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\log h(x)

\displaystyle=x+1/x-\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}}{\int_{-\infty}^{-x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}

\displaystyle=x+1/x-\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}}{\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2}dz}

\displaystyle=x+1/x-x/h(x),

where we used the symmetry of the standard normal probability density function to arrive at the second line. By Lemma
4
, the expression in the second line is non-negative for all
x>0
. Hence,
\log h(x)
is non-decreasing for
x>0
, which implies that
h(x)
is non-decreasing for
x>0
.

For the second property, both the inequality
\frac{d}{dx}\log h(x)\geq 0,~\forall x>0
, and the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}h(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{d}{dx}\log h(x)\right\}h(x)

\displaystyle=(x+1/x)h(x)-x

\displaystyle\geq 0

for
x>0
yield the lower bound

\displaystyle h(x)\geq\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+1},~\forall x>0

upon rearrangement. A direct application of the
Perić et al., (2019)
inequality (see the proof of Lemma
4
) then yields the upper bound

h(x)\leq\frac{x^{2}+2}{x^{2}+3},~x>0.

To see that
h(x)
is concave, take its second derivative to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}h(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\left\{(x+1/x)h(x)-x\right\}

\displaystyle=h(x)+x\{(x+1/x)h(x)-x\}-h(x)/x^{2}+(1+1/x^{2})h(x)-2

\displaystyle=(x^{2}+3)h(x)-(x^{2}+2).

By the previously established upper bound on
h(x)
, we conclude that

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}h(x)\leq 0,~\forall x>0,

so

is concave for

.

Finally, note that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}[x^{2}\{1-h(x)\}]

\displaystyle=2x-2xh(x)-x^{2}\{(x+1/x)h(x)-x\}

\displaystyle=x\left\{(x^{2}+2)-(x^{2}+3)h(x)\right\},

For all
x>0
the sign of the expression above is equal to the sign of
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}h(x)
. By the concavity of
h(x)
, we conclude that
x^{2}\{1-h(x)\}
is non-decreasing for
x>0
.
∎

.

Proof of Theorem

.

The estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
maximizes the marginal likelihood of (

(33)

Appendix B
EM algorithm for
t
regression

The algorithm described here has been developed in more detail in
Liu and Rubin, (1995)
. We include its derivation here for completeness and for consistency with our notation. Consider the hierarchical linear model

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}|\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\Omega}

\displaystyle\sim N({\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\Omega}),

\displaystyle\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{iid}{\sim}IG(\nu/2,\nu\omega_{0}/2).

Given
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
, one can find the maximizers
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*},\nu^{*},\omega_{0}^{*}
of the likelihood function

L(\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu,\omega_{0};\boldsymbol{\Omega},{\mathbf{y}})=p({\mathbf{y}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega},\boldsymbol{\beta})p(\boldsymbol{\Omega}|\nu,\omega_{0}).

(37)

We also have a simple form for the conditional distribution of
\omega_{i}
given the unknown parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu,\omega_{0}
and the data
{\mathbf{y}}

\omega_{i}|{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu,\omega_{0}\sim IG((\nu+1)/2,((y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}+\nu\omega_{0})/2).

(38)

This suggests an EM algorithm as a means to obtain maximum marginal likelihood estimates of
\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu,\omega_{0}
.

The E-step computes the expectation of the log of (
37
) with respect to the distribution in (
38
) given a current set of iterates
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},\nu^{(t)},\omega_{0}^{(t)}
. The log likelihood is

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\log(2\pi\omega_{i})+(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}/\omega_{i}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\nu\log(\nu\omega_{0}/2)-2\log\Gamma(\nu/2)-(\nu+2)\log\omega_{i}-\nu\omega_{0}/\omega_{i}].

Up to addition of constants, this can be written more compactly as

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[-(\nu+3)\log\omega_{i}-((y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}+\nu\omega_{0})/\omega_{i}+\nu\log(\nu\omega_{0}/2)-2\log\Gamma(\nu/2)].

With respect to (
38
), this has expectation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-(\nu+3)(\log(((y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)})^{2}+\nu^{(t)}\omega_{0}^{(t)})/2)-\psi((\nu^{(t)}+1)/2))-\right.

(39)

\displaystyle\left.(\nu^{(t)}+1)\frac{(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}+\nu\omega_{0}}{(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)})^{2}+\nu^{(t)}\omega_{0}^{(t)}}+\nu\log(\nu\omega_{0}/2)-2\log\Gamma(\nu/2)\right].

The M-step maximizes (
39
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu,\omega_{0}
. The maximizer in
\boldsymbol{\beta}
of (
39
) can be obtained as the solution to a WLS regression

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\mathrm{argmin}}~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=[(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)})^{2}+\nu^{(t)}\omega_{0}^{(t)}]^{-1}.

In relation to the discussion at the beginning of Section
4
, it is apparent that at the final step of the algorithm,
T
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(T)}
is precisely a regularized FLS estimate, with weights equal to
(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(T-1)})^{2}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and regularization term equal to
\nu^{(T-1)}\omega_{0}^{(T-1)}
.

A closed form expression for the maximizer of (
39
) in
\omega_{0}
also exists

\omega_{0}^{(t+1)}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\nu^{(t)}+1)/n}{(y_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)})^{2}+\nu^{(t)}\omega_{0}^{(t)}}\right]^{-1}.

The maximizer of (
39
) in
\nu
has no such closed form expression, but it may be obtained numerically with standard software.
