Optimising Hard Prompts with Few-Shot Meta-Prompting
Sayash Raaj Hiraou

Abstract
Prompting is a flexible and adaptable way of providing instructions to a Large Language Model (LLM). Contextual prompts include context in the form of a document or dialogue along with the natural language instructions to the LLM, often constraining the LLM to restrict facts to that of the given context while complying with the instructions. Masking the context, it acts as template for prompts. In this paper, we present an iterative method to generate better templates using an LLM from an existing set of prompt templates without revealing the context to the LLM. Multiple methods of optimising prompts using the LLM itself are explored to check the effect of few shot sampling methods on iterative propagation while maintaining linguistic styles and syntax on optimisation of prompt templates, yielding a 103.87% improvement using the best performing method. Comparison of the results of multiple contextual tasks demonstrate the ability of LLMs to maintain syntax while learning to replicate linguistic styles. Additionally, the effect on the output with different methods of prompt template generation is shown.



1
Introduction

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has paved the way for a new era of natural language processing, particularly with an increase in accessibility to the models with chat and similar user interfaces. These models, like GPT-3 have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in diverse tasks
Zhou et al. 2022
, from answering questions from a given context to text completion.

Harnessing the potential of these models through natural language instructions lies in the effective design of prompts- guiding LLMs in generating relevant outputs through input instructions. Prompting techniques vary widely, usually specific to a task and domain
White et al. 2023
. Contextual prompts include additional information in the form of dialogue or a document, guiding the LLM to refer or constrain its knowledge to that given in the context.

Template-based prompts have a predefined structure which controls the input given to the LLM with respect to its format, context or any additional information required for the task in question. Contextual prompts can have a predefined structure, thus acting as a template-based prompt with instructions and context in a specific format.

Meta-prompting, the act of asking the LLM itself to design prompts, is another method of importance in this paper. Recent research in the field of prompt tuning focuses on soft prompts, their initialisation problems, and related nuanced issues. Hard prompt tuning is a relatively unexplored avenue, which this paper tries to explore to generate better prompt templates. This paper experimentally measures the impact of linguistic styles of prompts on their outputs using meta-prompting.

In this work, LLMs and their capability to learn and maintain linguistic styles and syntax while adapting to different prompt templates are explored. The nuanced aspects of prompt engineering specifically for templates of contextual prompts are studied. The objective is to, without revealing the underlying context, generate enhanced templates. Understanding how LLMs respond to prompts with reference to changing linguistic styles and syntaxes helps reveal the dynamics between LLM outputs and linguistic inputs.

2
Related Work

Prompt optimisation, one of the most resource-efficient ways of increasing the accuracy of answers generated by an LLM, involves tweaking natural language instructions to obtain better-quality answers without changing the parameters of an LLM.

Prompts can be of two types- soft prompts and hard prompts. Soft prompt tuning
Qin and Eisner 2021
involves fine-tuning vectors which mostly lead to incoherent sentences understood by the LLM but unreadable by humans. Hard prompts on the other hand are written by and are interpretable by humans, forming coherent sentences following the syntax of a language. They have a comparatively rigid structure, usually explicitly specifying instruction guiding the model towards a controlled output. In this paper, the focus is on tuning coherent hard prompts using LLMs such that they do not turn into soft prompts incomprehensible to humans. Soft prompts respond well to tuning, being flexible and providing a refined interaction with LLMs. Hard prompts have the inherent property of being manually curated. Intricate linguistic details influence the output obtained using the hard prompts.

Certain efforts in the past have been made to optimise hard prompts, using various methods of producing better quality hard prompts
Ma et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2023
, some of them resulting in incoherent sentences which cannot be understood by humans or are task-irrelevant
Wen et al. 2023
.

There is some consensus when it comes to LLMs being able to improve upon prompts used for the same LLMs themselves.
Fernando et al. 2023
talks about mutating prompts, which helps bring some variability into the prompts, and by extension, the syntactical structure of providing an instruction in natural language.
Yang et al. 2024
talks about LLMs being general optimisers for multiple tasks including optimising prompts. Existing techniques to improve prompts in general preserve the intent and meaning behind the instruction but change the way it is asked.
Brown et al. 2020
emphasises on the improvement few-shot examples bring to the quality of answers of an LLM.
Wei et al. 2023
advocates for the improvement chain of thought prompting brings to the table, and
Wang et al. 2023
expands upon it to produce a consistent and reasoned path for producing answers from an LLM.

Thus, there is a strong case made for using LLMs when optimising for tasks, including optimising natural language instructions to mutate prompts and produce better quality answers while preserving the underlying meaning and human-readability of the prompt.

5
Conclusion

Comparing prompts similar in structure of language, when looked at by humans, is a subjective task. Defining rules and metrics of evaluation is crucial for a fair comparison. For the set of feeder and propagation methods, a difference in the performance in the outputs from each corresponding method which arises from their combinations is found. The study indicates a significant role played by the feeder method, propagation method and the interaction when combining them to optimise prompts. Comparing the mean scores in a batch of prompts, the best improvement was seen for the task of question-answering, with a 103.87 per cent improvement when compared with the manual set of prompts.

The combination of Feeder method A with either propagation method provides an output with greater mean and maximum scores when compared to the baseline sets
S_{-1}
and when compared to the same propagation methods combined with Feeder method B. The result indicates a possibility of the lowest performing prompt-scores pairs not having an effect when generating prompts for the latest iteration, which means negative meta-prompting could prove futile where the LLM does not learn what to actively avoid and instead focuses only on the positive examples to learn from.

The similarity scores are significantly lower than the manual baseline sets for methods A, B and C. This hints towards the possible selection and emphasis by the LLM on only a select few prompts, learning from and propagating them. Looking at the performance of the methods while maintaining diversity, the experiment finds method C to be the most effective at optimising prompt templates in our experiment.

The experiment could be extended and checked on multiple LLMs and different combinations for a more generalised result, diving deeper into the details of the interaction between feeder and propagation methods.
