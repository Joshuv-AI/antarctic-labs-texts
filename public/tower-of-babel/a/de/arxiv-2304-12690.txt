The Generations of Classical Correlations via Quantum Schemes
Zhenyu Chen, Lijinzhi Lin, Xiaodie Lin, Zhaohui Wei, Penghui Yao

Abstract
Suppose two separated parties, Alice and Bob, share a bipartite quantum state or a classical correlation called a seed , and they try to generate a target classical correlation by performing local quantum or classical operations on the seed, i.e., any communications are not allowed. We consider the following fundamental problem about this setting: whether Alice and Bob can use a given seed to generate a target classical correlation. We show that this problem has rich mathematical structures. Firstly, we prove that even if the seed is a pure bipartite state, the above decision problem is already NP-hard and a similar conclusion can also be drawn when the seed is also a classical correlation, implying that this problem is hard to solve generally. Furthermore, we prove that when the seed is a pure quantum state, solving the problem is equivalent to finding out whether the target classical correlation has some diagonal form of positive semi-definite factorizations that matches the seed pure state, revealing an interesting connection between the current problem and optimization theory. Based on this observation and other insights, we give several necessary conditions where the seed pure state has to satisfy to generate the target classical correlation, and it turns out that these conditions can also be generalized to the case that the seed is a mixed quantum state. Lastly, since diagonal forms of positive semi-definite factorizations play a crucial role in solving the problem, we develop an algorithm that can compute them for an arbitrary classical correlation, which has decent performance on the cases we test.

Introduction

Local state transformation is a fundamental problem in quantum information theory, which has wide applications in communication complexity, resource theory, quantum distributed computing, quantum interactive proof systems, etc. In the setting of local state transformation, given two bipartite states
\rho
and
\sigma
, two separated parties, Alice and Bob, share
\rho
, and their goal is to generate
\sigma
. In this paper, we concern ourselves about the case where the target state
\sigma
is a classical correlation
(X,Y)
, which is a pair of random variables distributed according to a joint probability distribution
P
, for convenience, we call half of the total number of bits needed to record the labels of
(X,Y)
the
size
of
P
. Suppose
P
is not a product probability distribution, then Alice and Bob have to share some resource beforehand, such as another classical correlation
P^{\prime}
called a
seed
correlation or a shared quantum state
\rho
called a
seed
state. In the former case, all their operations are classical and local (each operation involves only one party), while in the latter case, they could perform local quantum operations, i.e., each party can make proper quantum measurements on the quantum subsystem and output the outcomes as
X
or
Y
. Similar to the classical case, we define the size of
\rho
to be half of the total number of qubits in
\rho
, denoted
\mbox{\sf{size}}(\rho)
. Here we stress that when Alice and Bob are generating a target classical correlation
P
based on their shared resources, any communication between them is not allowed. In the current paper, we will focus on this kind of setting.

In such settings, the minimum size of such seed correlation (state) that can generate
P
has been defined as the
classical (quantum) correlation complexity
of
P
, denoted
\mbox{\sf{R}}(P)
(
\mbox{\sf{Q}}(P)
). In fact, it has been proved that
[
Zha12
,
JSWZ13
]

(1.1)

\mbox{\sf{R}}(P)=\lceil\log_{2}\mbox{\tt{rank}}_{+}(P)\rceil,

and

(1.2)

\mbox{\sf{Q}}(P)=\lceil\log_{2}\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)\rceil.

For any nonnegative matrix
P\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}_{+}^{n\times m}
, its nonnegative rank
\mbox{\tt{rank}}_{+}(P)
is defined as the minimum number
r
such that
P
can be decomposed as the summation of
r
nonnegative matrices of rank
1
, and
\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)
is its positive semi-definite rank (PSD-rank), which is the minimum
r
such that there are
r\times r
positive semi-definite matrices
C_{x}
,
D_{y}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{r\times r}
satisfying that
P(x,y)=\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}D_{y})
for all
x
and
y
, and this is called a
PSD decomposition

[
FMP+12
,
FGP+15
]
.

In such a situation, a natural further question arises: given a specific quantum state
\rho_{0}
and
\mbox{\sf{size}}(\rho_{0})\geq\mbox{\sf{Q}}(P)
, can we generate
P
by measuring
\rho_{0}
locally? Similarly, if we have a classical correlation
P_{0}
such that
\mbox{\sf{size}}(P_{0})\geq\mbox{\sf{R}}(P)
, can we generate
P
based on
P_{0}
using local operations only? Note that the work in
[
JSWZ13
]
cannot answer these questions. In the current manuscript, we will show that these problems have very rich mathematical structures.

Contributions

Our main contributions are as follows.

•

We show that even for the special cases that the seed is a bipartite pure state

•

Related work

Non-interactive simulations of joint distributions
, the classical counterpart of local state transformation, is a fundamental task in information theory. In this setting, two separated parties are provided with sequences
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
, respectively, where
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are identically distributed draws from a probability distribution
P(x,y)
. The goal is to determine whether they can generate a pair of random variables
(U,V)_{u,v}
possessing a joint distribution that closely approximates a target distribution
Q(u,v)
without any communication between them. The history of research on non-interactive simulations of joint probability distributions dates back to the pioneering works by Gács and Körner
[
GK73
]
and Wyner
[
Wyn75
]
. Interested readers may refer to subsequent work such as
[
GKS16
,
KA16
]
and the references therein for more information. Local state transformations, first investigated by Beigi
[
Bei13
]
, have gained increasing attention recently. However, characterizing the possibility of local state transformations has proved challenging, leading to various studies on necessary and sufficient conditions, as well as algorithm designs for this problem
[
MBG+19
,
QY21
,
CFL+12
,
QY23
]
.

Most research on non-interactive simulations of joint distributions and local state transformations focuses on the asymptotic setting, where the parties share infinite copies of the source states
[
DB14
,
Bei13
]
. In this scenario, two powerful tools are studied: quantum maximal correlation
[
Bei13
]
and hypercontractivity ribbon
[
DB14
]
. These are utilized to demonstrate nontrivial applications, particularly in proving the impossibility of local state transformations. However, one limitation of migrating these two approaches to the one-shot case is that these two quantities remain the same for all entangled pure states. For example, the quantum maximal correlation equals
1
for all entangled pure states, which implies that they contain the strongest correlation, thus failing in providing nontrivial results in the one-shot case.

To the best of our knowledge, research on one-shot non-interactive simulations of joint probability distributions and one-shot local state transformations is much less. Jain, Shi, Wei, and Zhang proved that the minimum sizes of seed classical states or quantum states required to generate a given joint distribution are tightly captured by nonnegative ranks and positive semidefinite ranks respectively. However, both of them are NP-hard to compute
[
Vav10
,
Shi17
]
.

Let
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Let

. If A is a self-adjoint matrix, let

be its spectral decomposition. Given a function

from complex numbers to complex numbers, we define

. A matrix

is said to be positive semi-definite if all its eigenvalues are nonnegative, and we write

to indicate matrix

is a PSD matrix. If
A,B\geq 0
such that
\mathrm{tr}(AB)=0
, then
AB=0

[
FGP+15
]
.

If
P
is an
m
-by-
n
classical correlation, let
P(x)
denote the marginal probability of getting
x
, i.e.,
P(x)\equiv\sum_{y}P(x,y)
, and similarly,
P(y)\equiv\sum_{x}P(x,y)
.
P(x|y)\equiv\frac{P(x,y)}{{P(y)}}
denotes the conditional probability of getting
x
given
y
. Define the classical fidelity for two nonnegative vectors

and

as
F(p,q)=\sum_{x}\sqrt{p(x)}\cdot\sqrt{q(x)}
.

A quantum state
\rho
in Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a trace-one positive semi-definite operator acting on
\mathcal{H}
. A quantum state

is called pure if it is rank-one as a matrix, namely

for some unit vector
|\psi\rangle
. In this special case, we also write

as

. For a pure state

, its Schmidt decomposition is defined as
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}|\alpha_{i}\rangle\otimes|\beta_{i}\rangle

in the Schmidt decomposition are called the Schmidt coeﬀicients of

. For quantum states

and

, the fidelity between them is defined as
\mathrm{F}(\rho,\sigma)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\sqrt{\sigma^{1/2}\rho\sigma^{1/2}}\right)
.

2
The quantum case that
\rho
is pure

We first consider the following special case: If the seed is a bipartite pure state
|\psi\rangle
, can a target classical correlation
P
be produced from it by local operations only? We now show that this special case already has very rich mathematical structures.

2.1
On the computational complexity

We first prove that solving this special case generally is already NP-hard, implying that it is hard to solve the original problem efficiently.

As mentioned in the introduction, for a given classical correlation
P
, its quantum correlation complexity, the minimum size of a quantum seed state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
that can generate
P
, has been characterized completely.

Lemma 2.1
(
[
JSWZ13
]
)

Let
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
be a classical correlation, and
\rho=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\cdot|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|
be a quantum state in
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mbox{\sf{Q}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\lceil\log_{2}\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)\rceil

\displaystyle=\min_{\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}},\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}}\{\big\lceil\log_{2}\mbox{\tt{S-rank}}(|\psi\rangle)\big\rceil:|\psi\rangle\text{ is a pure state}

\displaystyle\text{ in }\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}},\rho=\mbox{\tt{tr}}_{\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\},

where
\mbox{\tt{S-rank}}(|\psi\rangle)
is the Schmidt rank of
|\psi\rangle
with respect to the partition
AA_{1}|BB_{1}
.

That is to say, a given pure state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can generate
P
only when the size of
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is at least
\lceil\log_{2}\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)\rceil
. In fact,
[
JSWZ13
]
proves a further conclusion, that is,
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can generate
P
if and only if
|\psi_{0}\rangle
has the same Schmidt coefficients with some purification of
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
, where the partition is chosen to be
AA_{1}|BB_{1}
.

This can be explained as follows. On the one hand, if
|\psi_{0}\rangle
has the same Schmidt coefficients with a purification
|\phi\rangle
of
\rho
, then Alice and Bob can transform
|\psi_{0}\rangle
to
|\phi\rangle
by local unitaries (attach some blank ancilla qubits if needed). Then by throwing away the qubits in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
, the obtained quantum state will be exactly
\rho
. On the other hand, if
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can generate
P
, it means that Alice and Bob can produce
\rho
by performing local quantum operations on
|\psi_{0}\rangle
. Note that these local operations can be simulated by attaching necessary ancilla qubits, performing local unitary operations, and then dropping part of the qubits, which indicates that before dropping part of the qubits, the overall quantum state is actually a purification of
\rho
, and it has the same Schmidt coefficients with
|\psi_{0}\rangle
, as local unitaries do not change them.

Unfortunately, we now prove that it is generally NP-hard to determine whether or not
|\psi\rangle
has the same Schmidt coefficients will some purification of
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
.

Theorem 2.1

The problem of deciding whether a given pure state
|\psi\rangle
can generate a given correlation
P
is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard.

Proof.

We first recall the fact that the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
problem is NP-hard
[
Har82
]
. Suppose we have a set of positive integers
S=\{a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{{r}}\}
, and
T=\sum_{i}a_{i}/2
. The
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
problem is asking whether any subset of
S
sums to precisely
T
. We now try to reduce the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
problem to the problem we are studying, denoted as
\mathsf{CORRELATION\mathchar 45\relax GENERATION}
problem for convenience.
We specify the inputs of
\mathsf{CORRELATION\mathchar 45\relax GENERATION}
to be the squared Schmidt coefficients of the pure seed state instead of its amplitudes in the computational basis;
this does not sacrifice generality as any two pure states with the same Schmidt coefficients are equivalent to each other under local isometries.

Consider the following classical correlation:

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{2}I_{2\times 2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Let the Schmidt decomposition of
|\psi\rangle
be

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}|\alpha_{i}\rangle|\beta_{i}\rangle,

where
\lambda_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{\sum_{k}a_{k}}
for any
i
. Also let
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\cdots,\sqrt{\lambda_{r}})
.
We remark that a finite representation of
\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}\}
can be obtained from
\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{r}\}
in polynomial steps due to each
\lambda_{i}
being a rational number.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq...\geq\lambda_{r}>0
.
Then
|\psi\rangle
can generate correlation
P
if and only if Alice and Bob can perform binary-outcome quantum measurements with
operators
\{A,I-A\}
and
\{B^{T},I-B^{T}\}
respectively such that it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|(A\otimes B^{T}))=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda A\Lambda B)=

\displaystyle 1/2,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda(I-A)\Lambda(I-B))=

\displaystyle 1/2,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda A\Lambda(I-B))=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda(I-A)\Lambda B)=

\displaystyle 0,

where
A
and
B
are
r\times r
PSD matrices satisfying
0\leq A,B\leq I
, and
B^{T}
is the transpose of
B
.

Note that
\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda A\Lambda(I-B))=0
implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{(}(\Lambda^{1/2}A\Lambda^{1/2})(\Lambda^{1/2}(I-B)\Lambda^{1/2}){)}=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{(}(\Lambda^{1/2}(I-B)\Lambda^{1/2})(\Lambda^{1/2}A\Lambda^{1/2}){)}=

\displaystyle 0.

Since both
\Lambda^{1/2}A\Lambda^{1/2}
and
\Lambda^{1/2}(I-B)\Lambda^{1/2}
are PSD matrices,
we immediately have that

\displaystyle(\Lambda^{1/2}A\Lambda^{1/2})(\Lambda^{1/2}(I-B)\Lambda^{1/2})=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle(\Lambda^{1/2}(I-B)\Lambda^{1/2})(\Lambda^{1/2}A\Lambda^{1/2})=

\displaystyle 0.

By similar argument,
\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda(I-A)\Lambda B)=0
implies that

\displaystyle(\Lambda^{1/2}(I-A)\Lambda^{1/2})(\Lambda^{1/2}B\Lambda^{1/2})=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle(\Lambda^{1/2}B\Lambda^{1/2})(\Lambda^{1/2}(I-A)\Lambda^{1/2})=

\displaystyle 0.

Since
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{1/2}
are invertible, the above conditions indicate that

\displaystyle A\Lambda B=

\displaystyle A\Lambda=\Lambda B,

\displaystyle B\Lambda A=

\displaystyle B\Lambda=\Lambda A.

This gives
B=\Lambda^{-1}A\Lambda=\Lambda A\Lambda^{-1}
.
Since
\Lambda
is diagonal, we have
B=A
and when
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
it
holds that
A_{ij}=0
, which means that
A
and
B
are block diagonal matrices and each block corresponds to each distinct value of
\lambda_{i}
.

Furthermore, we also have

\displaystyle B=\Lambda^{-1}A\Lambda=\Lambda^{-1}A\Lambda B=\Lambda^{-1}A^{2}\Lambda=B^{2},

hence both
A
and
B
are projectors. Combining this fact and that
A
and
B
are block diagonal matrices, we
know that each block of
A
and
B
are also projectors.

Finally, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda A\Lambda B)=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda^{2}A^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda^{2}A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\lambda\cdot\mathrm{rank}(A|_{\mathrm{ker}(\Lambda^{2}-\lambda I)}),

where
\lambda
in the summation runs through all distinct values of
\lambda_{i}
, and
A|_{\mathrm{ker}(\Lambda^{2}-\lambda I)}
is the block of
A
that corresponds to the value of
\lambda
.
It can be seen that this summation is exactly a subset sum of
\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}\}
, hence
\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{\lambda}\lambda\cdot\mathrm{rank}(A|_{\mathrm{ker}(\Lambda^{2}-\lambda I)})\right)
is a subset sum of
\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{r}\}
.
Therefore if
|\psi\rangle
can generate
P
, a solution of the corresponding
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
instance also exists. Conversely, if the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
instance has a solution, then by measuring
|\psi\rangle
in its Schmidt basis and grouping the outcomes,
we can transform
|\psi\rangle
into
P
.
Hence we obtain a polynomial-time reduction of the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
problem to the
\mathsf{CORRELATION\mathchar 45\relax GENERATION}
problem.

\square

2.2
The diagonal form of PSD factorizations

For a given classical correlation
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
and the corresponding quantum state
\rho=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\cdot|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, we now know that generally it is hard to determine whether a seed quantum state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
has the same Schmidt coefficients with some purification of
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
. Here the major challenge is to characterize the Schmidt coefficients for all possible purifications of
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
, and any results of this kind will be very useful to determine whether a given
|\psi\rangle
can produce
P
by local quantum operations only.

Interestingly, it turns out that there exists a close relation between Schmidt coefficients of purifications of
\rho
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
and a special form of PSD factorizations for
P
. Recall that the concept of PSD factorization plays an important role in optimization theory, which determines the computational power of semi-definite programming in combinatorial optimization problems
[
Yan88
,
BPT12
]
.

Specifically, we call this special form of PSD factorizations
the diagonal form of PSD factorizations
.

Definition 2.1

A
diagonal form of PSD factorizations
for a nonnegative matrix
P\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}_{+}^{n\times m}
is a collection of PSD matrices
C_{x},D_{y}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{k\times k}
that satisfy

P(x,y)=\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}D_{y}),\ x=1,...,n,\ y=1...,m

and

\sum_{x=1}^{n}C_{x}=\sum_{y=1}^{m}D_{y}=\Lambda,

where
\Lambda\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}^{k\times k}
is a diagonal nonnegative matrix. And if
k=\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)
, we say this is an optimal diagonal form of PSD factorizations.

We remark that for an arbitrary nonnegative matrix
M
, one can always find an optimal diagonal form of PSD factorizations, which is implied by the following theorem. Note that to make the entries of
M
sum to
1
, a proper renormalization for
M
by a constant factor may be needed.

Lemma 2.2

Let
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
be a classical correlation, and
\rho=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\cdot|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Then there exists a purification
|\psi\rangle
of
\rho
with Schmidt coefficients

\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\sqrt{\lambda_{2}},...\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}
if and only if
there exists a diagonal form of PSD factorization
\{C_{x},D_{y}\}
of
P
such that
\sum_{x=1}^{n}C_{x}=\sum_{y=1}^{m}D_{y}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\cdots,\sqrt{\lambda_{r}})
.

Proof.
Suppose the Schmidt decomposition of
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
can be written as

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{x=1}^{n}|x\rangle\otimes|v_{x}^{i}\rangle\right)\otimes\left(\sum_{y=1}^{m}|y\rangle\otimes|w_{y}^{i}\rangle\right),

where
|v_{x}^{i}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}
and
|w_{y}^{i}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
are unnormalized quantum states. According to the definition of Schmidt decomposition, one can choose the norms of
|v_{x}^{i}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}
and
|w_{y}^{i}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
properly such that

(2.3)

\sum_{x=1}^{n}\langle v_{x}^{j}|v_{x}^{i}\rangle=\sum_{y=1}^{m}\langle w_{y}^{j}|w_{y}^{i}\rangle=0,\ \ \text{for any }i\neq j,

and

(2.4)

\sum_{x=1}^{n}\langle v_{x}^{i}|v_{x}^{i}\rangle=\sum_{y=1}^{m}\langle w_{y}^{i}|w_{y}^{i}\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}},\ \ \text{for any }i\in[r].

Since
|\psi\rangle
is a purification of
\rho
, it holds that

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt{tr}}_{\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\langle v_{x}^{j}|v_{x}^{i}\rangle\cdot\langle w_{y}^{j}|w_{y}^{i}\rangle\right).

We now define
r\times r
matrices
C_{x}
such that
C_{x}(j,i)=\langle v_{x}^{j}|v_{x}^{i}\rangle
for all
i,j\in[r]
, and
r\times r
matrices
D_{y}
such that
D_{y}(i,j)=\langle w_{y}^{j}|w_{y}^{i}\rangle
for all
i,j\in[r]
. Then for any
x\in[n]
and
y\in[m]
,
C_{x}
and
D_{y}
are positive semi-definite matrices, and Eqs.(
2.3
) and (
2.4
) imply that

\sum_{x=1}^{n}C_{x}=\sum_{y=1}^{m}D_{y}=\Lambda,\ \ \text{for any }i\in[r],

where
\Lambda=\text{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},...,\sqrt{\lambda_{r}})
.

For the other direction, let
C_{x},D_{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
be positive semidefinite matrices with
\operatorname{tr}\left(C_{x}D_{y}\right)=P(x,y)
for all
x\in[n],\ y\in[m]
, and this decomposition satisfy
\sum_{x=1}^{n}C_{x}=\sum_{y=1}^{m}D_{y}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\cdots,\sqrt{\lambda_{r}})
. For
i\in[r]
, let
\left|v_{x}^{i}\right\rangle
be the
i
-th column of
\sqrt{C_{x}^{T}}
and let
\left|w_{y}^{i}\right\rangle
be the
i
-th column of
\sqrt{D_{y}}
. Define
|\psi\rangle
in
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
as follows:

|\psi\rangle\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{ def }}}{{=}}\!\sum_{i=1}^{r}\!\left(\!\sum_{x}|x\rangle\!\otimes\!|x\rangle\!\otimes\!\left|v_{x}^{i}\right\rangle\!\right)\!\otimes\!\left(\!\sum_{y}|y\rangle\!\otimes\!|y\rangle\!\otimes\!\left|w_{y}^{i}\right\rangle\!\right)\!.

Then, by defining
|V_{i}\rangle=\sum_{x}|x\rangle\otimes|x\rangle\otimes\left|v_{x}^{i}\right\rangle
and
|W_{i}\rangle=\sum_{y}|y\rangle\otimes|y\rangle\otimes\left|w_{y}^{i}\right\rangle
, it can be shown directly that

\langle V_{i^{\prime}}|V_{i}\rangle=\sum_{x}C_{x}^{T}(i^{\prime},i)=0,\ \ \text{for any }i\neq i^{\prime}\in[r],

and

\langle V_{i}|V_{i}\rangle=\sum_{x}C_{x}^{T}(i,i)=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}},\ \ \text{for any }i\in[r].

Similarly, it holds that
\langle W_{i^{\prime}}|W_{i}\rangle=\sum_{y}D_{y}(i^{\prime},i)=0
and
\langle W_{i}|W_{i}\rangle=\sum_{y}D_{y}(i,i)=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
for all
i\neq i^{\prime}\in[r]
. The above four equations imply
|\psi\rangle
is a pure state with Schmidt coefficients

\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\sqrt{\lambda_{2}},...\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}
with respect to the partition
AAA_{1}|BBB_{1}
, also

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}}{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\left\langle v_{x}^{j}\mid v_{x}^{i}\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle w_{y}^{j}\mid w_{y}^{i}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|\cdot\operatorname{tr}\left(C_{x}D_{y}\right)=\rho,

which shows
|\psi\rangle
is a purification of
\rho
.

\square

Since Lemma
2.2
gives a complete description for the Schmidt coefficients of all possible purifications of
\rho=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\cdot|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|
, we directly obtain the following Theorem that determines whether a pure state
|\psi\rangle
can be transformed to
P
under local transformation.

Theorem 2.2

A pure state
|\psi\rangle
with Schmidt coefficients
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\sqrt{\lambda_{2}},...\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}
can generate a classical correlation
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
if and only if there exists a diagonal form of PSD factorization
\{C_{x},D_{y}\}
of
P
such that
\sum_{x=1}^{n}C_{x}=\sum_{y=1}^{m}D_{y}=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},\cdots,\sqrt{\lambda_{r}})
.

An interesting fact is that a nonnegative matrix can have multiple optimal diagonal forms of PSD factorizations. For example, let

P=\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},

then it holds that
\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)=2
. We define two pure states in
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
as

|\psi_{1}\rangle={\sum_{(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{2}}}\sqrt{P(x,y)}|x\rangle|y\rangle|x\rangle|y\rangle

and

{|\psi_{2}\rangle}={\sum_{(x,y)\in\{0,1\}^{2}}}\sqrt{P(x,y)}|x\rangle|y\rangle{\rm CNOT}|x\rangle|y\rangle,

where the CNOT gate is performed on the subsystems
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
. Then it can be verified that
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
are two different purifications of
\rho=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\cdot|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
. By calculating their Schmidt coefficients, we can obtain two different optimal diagonal forms of PSD factorizations for
P
, where the
\Lambda
matrices are
\Lambda_{1}\approx\text{diag}(0.9342,0.3568)
and
\Lambda_{2}\approx\text{diag}(0.8165,0.5774)
respectively.

2.3
Several necessary conditions for that
|\psi\rangle
generates
P

Let
m(\cdot)
be a measure of bipartite states which is monotone non-increasing under local quantum operations. Then it is easy to see that
m(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)\geq m(P)
is a necessary condition for which
|\psi\rangle
can be locally transformed to the target classical correlation
P
.
Several information-theoretic quantities are known to be monotone non-increasing under local operations such as mutual information. Thus, if the mutual information
I(A:B)
of
|\psi\rangle
is less than that of
P
, then
P
cannot be generated from
|\psi\rangle
under local operations only.

Another such measure of correlation is the
sandwiched
{\alpha\mathchar 45\relax}
Rényi divergence

[
MLDS+13
]
, which is defined as

\tilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mbox{\tt{tr}}(\rho)}\mbox{\tt{tr}}\left[(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}})^{\alpha}\right]\right),

where
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty]
. Let
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}|i_{A}\rangle|i_{B}\rangle,\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
and
\sigma=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\cdot|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|
, where
|i_{A}\rangle,|x\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{A}
(
|i_{B}\rangle,|y\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{B}
) are orthonormal bases for systems
A
(
B
). Since the data processing inequality holds for the order-
\alpha
Rényi divergence with
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2},1)\cup(1,\infty]
, i.e., it cannot be increased by local quantum operations, by requiring
\tilde{D}_{\alpha}(\rho\|\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})\geq\tilde{D}_{\alpha}(\sigma\|\sigma_{A}\otimes\sigma_{B})
where
\rho_{A},\rho_{B},\sigma_{A},\sigma_{B}
are reduced density matrices for
\rho
and
\sigma
respectively, it derives

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-1}\right)^{\alpha}\leq\sum_{xy}\frac{P(x,y)^{\alpha}}{(P(x)P(y))^{\alpha-1}},&\alpha\in[\frac{1}{2},1)\\
\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-1}\right)^{\alpha}\geq\sum_{xy}\frac{P(x,y)^{\alpha}}{(P(x)P(y))^{\alpha-1}},&\alpha\in(1,\infty)\\
{\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\geq\max\limits_{x,y}\frac{P(x,y)}{\sum_{j}P(x,{j})\sum_{i}P({i},y)},}&{\alpha=\infty.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Therefore, if
|\psi\rangle
and
P
violate any inequality in Eq.(
2.5
),
|\psi\rangle
cannot generate
P
under local operations.

In addition to the above natural conclusions, we now show that the insight provided by Theorem
2.2
allows us to obtain several new necessary conditions that
|\psi\rangle
has to satisfy to generate
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
, which can be stronger than the above two necessary conditions. Again, we suppose the Schmidt rank of
|\psi\rangle
is
r
, and the squared Schmidt coefficients are
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq...\geq\lambda_{r}
. For simplicity, in this subsection we suppose that
r=\mbox{\tt{rank}${}_{\tt psd}$}(P)
, i.e.,
\lambda_{r}>0
.

Proposition 2.1

(
A necessary condition for
\lambda_{r}
)
If
|\psi\rangle
can produce
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
by local operations only, then it holds that

(2.6)

\lambda_{r}\leq\min_{x,y}\frac{\sum_{{j}}P(x,{{j}})\sum_{{i}}P({{i}},y)}{P(x,y)}.

Proof.

As mentioned before,
|\psi\rangle
can generate
P
if and only if
|\psi\rangle
has the same Schmidt coefficients with a purification of
\rho
. According to Theorem
2.2
, this means that
P
has a diagonal form of PSD factorizations
C_{{i}}
and
D_{{j}}
with
\sum_{{i}}C_{{i}}=\sum_{{j}}D_{{j}}=\Lambda=\text{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}},...,\sqrt{\lambda_{r}})
, where
i\in[n]
and
j\in[m]
. For arbitrary
x\in[n]
and
y\in[m]
, we have that

\sum_{{j}}P(x,{j})=\sum_{{j}}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}D_{{j}})=\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}\cdot\Lambda).

Since
\Lambda\geq\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}\cdot I_{r\times r}
, it holds that
\sum_{{j}}P(x,{j})\geq\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x})
, where
I_{r\times r}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix. Similarly, we also have
\sum_{{i}}P({i},y)\geq\sqrt{\lambda_{r}}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(D_{y})
.

Meanwhile, according to the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the relation
P(x,y)=\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}D_{y})
implies that

P(x,y)\leq\sqrt{\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{2})}\sqrt{\mbox{\tt{tr}}(D_{y}^{2})}\leq\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x})\mbox{\tt{tr}}(D_{y}),

where we have utilized the fact that for any PSD matrix
M
it holds that
(\mbox{\tt{tr}}(M))^{2}\geq\mbox{\tt{tr}}(M^{2})
. Combining the above facts, we have that

\sum_{{j}}P(x,{j})\sum_{{i}}P({i},y)\geq\lambda_{r}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x})\mbox{\tt{tr}}(D_{y})\geq\lambda_{r}\cdot P(x,y).

Therefore, we conclude the proof.

\square

Interestingly, Proposition
2.1
can be compared with the result given by the
sandwiched
\alpha\mathchar 45\relax
Rényi divergence
. Recall that when
\alpha=\infty
, Eq.(
2.5
) yields

\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\geq\max\limits_{x,y}\frac{P(x,y)}{\sum_{j}P(x,{j})\sum_{i}P({i},y)},

which is always weaker than Eq.(
2.6
).

Example 1

Let us see an example showing that this condition can be stronger than the ones given by the mutual information and the sandwiched
{\alpha\mathchar 45\relax}
Rényi divergence. Suppose one tries to generate
P=\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0\\

from

satisfy the conditions given by the mutual information condition and the sandwiched

\lambda_{r}
of

is 0.3, implying that Eq.(
2.6
) is violated and the possibility of producing

by performing local operations on

is ruled out.

Proposition 2.2

(A necessary condition for the entropy

of
\{\lambda_{i}\}
)
If
|\psi\rangle
can produce
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
by local operations only, then it holds that

(2.7)

I(P)\leq-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\log(\lambda_{i}).

Before we prove this proposition, it is worth mentioning that the necessary condition provided by the monotonicity of mutual information directly reads

I(P)\leq-2\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\log(\lambda_{i}),

indicating that the above new condition is always stronger.

Proof.

Define
V=\text{diag}(\sqrt[4]{\lambda_{1}},\sqrt[4]{\lambda_{2}},...,\sqrt[4]{\lambda_{r}})
. Since
C_{x}
and
D_{y}
are a diagonal form of PSD factorizations for
P
, it holds that
C_{x}^{\prime}=VC_{x}V
and
D_{y}^{\prime}=V^{-1}D_{y}V^{-1}
are still a valid PSD decomposition for
P
, i.e.,
P(x,y)=\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime}D_{y}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, we now have that
\sum_{x}C_{x}^{\prime}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{r})
and
\sum_{y}D_{y}^{\prime}=I_{r\times r}
.

At the same time, let
P_{x}
denote the transpose of the
x
-th row of
P
, where
x\in[n]
. Define
\text{sum}(P_{x})
to be the sum of all the entries of
P_{x}
, then it holds that
\text{sum}(P_{x})=\sum_{y}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime}D_{y}^{\prime})=\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime})
. Since
C_{x}^{\prime}
is PSD for any
x
and
\sum_{y}D_{y}^{\prime}=I_{r\times r}
, we now view
\{D_{y}^{\prime}\}
as a set of a positive-operator-valued measure (POVM), and
\rho_{x}=C_{x}^{\prime}/\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime})
as a quantum state, then
P_{x}/\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime})
is actually the outcome probability distribution if we measure
\rho_{x}
with the POVM
\{D_{y}^{\prime}\}
.

We now consider the following virtual protocol performed by Alice and Bob. With probability
\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime})
, Alice prepares and sends the quantum state
\rho_{x}
to Bob. After receiving the quantum state, he measures it using the POVM
\{D_{y}^{\prime}\}
and records the outcome

. This process can be regarded as a protocol that classical information is transformed from Alice to Bob. According to the Holevo bound (
[
NC11
]
, chapter 12), it holds that

\displaystyle I(P)

\displaystyle\leq S\left(\sum_{x}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime})\cdot\rho_{x}\right)-\sum_{x}\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}^{\prime})\cdot S(\rho_{x})

\displaystyle\leq S\left(\sum_{x}C_{x}^{\prime}\right)=-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\log(\lambda_{i}),

where

is the mutual information between
x
and
y
,

is the von Neumann entropy of
\rho
, and we have utilized the fact that
S(\rho_{y})\geq 0
for any
y
.

\square

3
The general case that the seed state is mixed

3.1
A general necessary condition for the case of mixed seed

We now turn to the general case of the classical correlation generation problem: If Alice and Bob share a mixed seed quantum state

|\psi_{0}\rangle
, and then determine whether
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can generate
P
by using the results we have obtained. If we find out that
|\psi_{0}\rangle
cannot generate
P
by local operations only, we immediately know that
\rho_{0}
cannot generate
P
. In other words, a necessary condition for
\rho
can generate
P
is that
any
specific purification
|\psi\rangle
of
\rho_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}_{A_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B_{1}}
can also generate
P
.

Example 6

Suppose the target correlation is
P=\begin{bmatrix}0.3&0\\
0&0.7\end{bmatrix}
. Alice and Bob share a two-qubit mixed state
\rho=\frac{1}{2}|0_{A}0_{B}\rangle\langle 0_{A}0_{B}|+\frac{1}{2}|1_{A}1_{B}\rangle\langle 1_{A}1_{B}|
, and want to generate
P
by local operations. It can be verified that the pure state
|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0_{A_{1}}0_{A}0_{B}0_{B_{1}}\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1_{A_{1}}1_{A}1_{B}1_{B_{1}}\rangle
is a specific purification of
\rho
. According to Eq.(
2.6
), we know that the square of the minimal Schmidt coefficient of the pure seed state must be less than or equal to 0.3, which rules out the possibility of generating
P
from
|\psi\rangle
, hence the original mixed state
\rho
cannot generate
P
.

3.2
When the seed is also a classical correlation

In this subsection, we study the case that both the seed and target are classical correlations, denoted by
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
and
Q=[Q(x,y)]_{x,y}
, respectively. We will show that this case is still NP-hard.

3.2.1
Quantum has no advantage in reachability

Suppose
P=[P(x,y)]_{x,y}
and
Q=[Q(x,y)]_{x,y}
are two different classical correlations with the same size. We now prove that quantum operation has no advantage in the task of transforming
P
to
Q
locally. A similar result was also reported in
[
CFH18
]
.

Theorem 3.1

If
P
can generate
Q
by local quantum operations, then the same can be achieved by local classical operations.

Proof.

Suppose
\{E_{i}\}
and
\{F_{j}\}
are the Kraus operators for the quantum operations performed by Alice and Bob respectively such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}(E_{i}\otimes F_{j})\rho(E_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes F_{j}^{\dagger})=

\displaystyle\sigma,

where

\displaystyle\rho=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|,

\displaystyle\sigma=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}Q(x,y)|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes|y\rangle\langle y|.

We expand the above relation with three pairs of indices and obtain

\displaystyle\sigma=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}}\!P(x,y)\langle x^{\prime}|E_{i}|x\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|F_{j}|y\rangle\langle x|E_{i}^{\dagger}|x^{\prime\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\langle y|F_{j}^{\dagger}|y^{\prime\prime}\rangle|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime\prime}|\otimes|y^{\prime}\rangle\langle y^{\prime\prime}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\!P(x,y)\langle x^{\prime}|E_{i}|x\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|F_{j}|y\rangle\langle x|E_{i}^{\dagger}|x^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\cdot\langle y|F_{j}^{\dagger}|y^{\prime}\rangle|x^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|\otimes|y^{\prime}\rangle\langle y^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}P(x,y)|\langle x^{\prime}|E_{i}|x\rangle|^{2}|\langle y^{\prime}|F_{j}|y\rangle|^{2}|x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\rangle\langle x^{\prime}y^{\prime}|.

This implies that

\displaystyle Q(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,x,y}P(x,y)|\langle x^{\prime}|E_{i}|x\rangle|^{2}|\langle y^{\prime}|F_{j}|y\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\!

\displaystyle P(x,y)\!\left(\!\sum_{i}|\langle x^{\prime}|E_{i}|x\rangle|^{2}\!\right)\!\left(\!\sum_{j}|\langle y^{\prime}|F_{j}|y\rangle^{2}|\!\right)\!.

We define

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(x^{\prime}|x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}|\langle x^{\prime}|E_{i}|x\rangle|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(y^{\prime}|y)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}|\langle y^{\prime}|F_{j}|y\rangle|^{2}.

Then it can be verified that both
\mathrm{P}(x^{\prime}|x)
and
\mathrm{P}(y^{\prime}|y)
are valid conditional probabilities. Thus if Alice and Bob take them as local classical operations, they can generate
Q
based on the seed classical correlation
P
.

\square

3.2.2
The NP-hardness of the case of classical seed

We now show that even if the seed state is restricted to a classical correlation, determining whether it can generate a target classical correlation is NP-hard.

Theorem 3.2

The problem of deciding whether a given correlation
P_{1}
can generate another given correlation
P_{2}
via local quantum operations is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard.

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we reduce the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
problem, which takes as input a set of positive integers
\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r}\}
, to this problem. We consider the case that

\displaystyle P_{1}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}),

\displaystyle P_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}I_{2\times 2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\lambda_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{\sum_{k}a_{k}}
, hence
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}=1
. According to Theorem
3.1
, we can assume that the local operations performed by Alice and Bob are classical. As a result, the correlation
P_{2}
can be generated from
P_{1}
if and only if there exists
2\times r
matrices
A,B
such that

\displaystyle A(i,j)\geq

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in[2],j\in[r],

\displaystyle B(i,j)\geq

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\forall i\in[2],j\in[r],

\displaystyle A(1,j)+A(2,j)=

\displaystyle B(1,j)+B(2,j)=1,\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\forall j\in[r],

\displaystyle P_{2}=

\displaystyle AP_{1}B^{T}.

Suppose such
A
and
B
indeed exist.
We now fix some
j\in[r]
and suppose
{A(1,j)}>0
.
By
{P_{2}(1,2)}=0
, we have
{B(2,j)}=0
, hence
{B(1,j)}=1
.
Since
{P_{2}(2,1)}=0
, this implies that
{A(2,j)}=0
, hence
{A(1,j)}=1
.
Due to the symmetry of the problem, a similar conclusion can be drawn for
{A(2,j)}
,
{B(1,j)}
, and
{B(2,j)}
.
Therefore, there exists
S\subseteq[r]
such that
(j\in S\implies{A(1,j)=B(1,j)}=1)
and
(j\not\in S\implies{A(2,j)=B(2,j)=1})
.
This gives
{P_{2}(1,1)}=\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{2}
, hence
\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}
is a solution to the corresponding
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
instance. Conversely, if the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
instance has a solution
\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}
, then let
A
and
B
be constructed samely as above, we obtain that
P_{2}=AP_{1}B^{T}
.

Therefore, we successfully construct a reduction from the
\mathsf{SUBSET\mathchar 45\relax SUM}
problem to the problem that generates
P_{2}
from
P_{1}
, implying that the latter is NP-hard.

\square

4
An algorithm that computes diagonal forms of PSD factorizations

According to Theorem
2.2
, to determine whether or not
|\psi\rangle
can produce
P
by local operations only, one needs to characterize all possible diagonal forms of PSD factorizations for
P
, and find out whether one of them is consistent with
|\psi\rangle
.
In the current section, we propose an algorithm to compute the diagonal forms of PSD factorizations for an arbitrary correlation
P
with respect to a given diagonal matrix
\Lambda
.

For this, we now formulate the task of computing diagonal forms of PSD factorizations as an optimization problem, where the variables are two sets of matrices
\{C_{x}\}
and
\{D_{y}\}
belonging to the positive semidefinite cone:

(4.14)

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}C_{x},D_{y}\geq 0\\
x=1,\ldots,n\\
y=1,\ldots,m\end{subarray}}\sum_{x=1}^{n}\sum_{y=1}^{m}\left(P(x,y)-\mbox{\tt{tr}}(C_{x}D_{y})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\sum_{x=1}^{n}C_{x}=\sum_{y=1}^{m}D_{y}=\Lambda.

This optimization problem is non-convex and NP-hard
[
Shi17
]
.
Since the constraints and objective function are polynomial functions of the variables,
some established methods such as quantifier elimination
[
Tar98
]
and Lasserre’s hierarchy
[
Las01
]
could be employed to solve the optimization problem, albeit with exponential running time.
To obtain a heuristic approach for this problem, we fix one of the two sets of matrices to simplify it to a convex optimization problem,
similar to the algorithm in
[
VGG18
]
for computing PSD factorization.
We accordingly develop an algorithm that computes diagonal forms of PSD factorizations by alternately optimizing over
\{C_{x}\}
and
\{D_{y}\}
.
The pseudo-code is represented in Algorithm
1
.

.
