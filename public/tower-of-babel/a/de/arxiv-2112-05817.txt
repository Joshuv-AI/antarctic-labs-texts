Exposure-Referred Signal-to-Noise Ratio for Digital Image Sensors
Abhiram Gnanasambandam, and Stanley H. Chan

Abstract
The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is a fundamental tool to measure the performance of an image sensor. However, confusions sometimes arise between the two types of SNRs. The first one is the output-referred SNR which measures the ratio between the signal and the noise seen at the sensor’s output. This SNR is easy to compute, and it is linear in the log-log scale for most image sensors. The second SNR is the exposure-referred SNR, also known as the input-referred SNR. This SNR considers the noise at the input by including a derivative term to the output-referred SNR. The two SNRs have similar behaviors for sensors with a large full-well capacity. However, for sensors with a small full-well capacity, the exposure-referred SNR can capture some behaviors that the output-referred SNR cannot. While the exposure-referred SNR has been known and used by the industry for a long time, a theoretically rigorous derivation from a signal processing perspective is lacking. In particular, while various equations can be found in different sources of the literature, there is currently no paper that attempts to assemble, derive, and organize these equations in one place. This paper aims to fill the gap by answering four questions: (1) How is the exposure-referred SNR derived from first principles? (2) Is the output-referred SNR a special case of the exposure-referred SNR, or are they completely different? (3) How to compute the SNR efficiently? (4) What utilities can the SNR bring to solving imaging tasks? New theoretical results are derived for image sensors of any bit-depth and full-well capacity.



I
Introduction

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is a basic tool to measure a device’s performance when acquiring, transmitting, and processing raw data in the presence of noise. In as early as 1949, when Claude Shannon derived the information capacity of a noisy Gaussian channel, the concept of SNR was already presented
[
1
]
. As the name suggests, the SNR is the ratio between the signal power and the noise power

\text{SNR}=\frac{\text{signal power}}{\text{noise power}},

(1)

which is sometimes expressed in the logarithmic scale via
10\log_{10}\text{SNR}
with the unit decibel (dB). Assuming that the signal follows the equation
Y=\theta+W
where
\theta
is a scalar and
W\sim\text{Gaussian}(0,\sigma^{2})
is the white noise, one can measure the SNR at the
output
by defining

\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\frac{\text{signal at output}}{\text{noise at output}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Y]}},

(2)

where the conversion from power to magnitude is taken care by changing the log from
10\log_{10}\text{SNR}
to
20\log_{10}\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}
. In this equation,
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation and
\mathrm{Var}[\cdot]
denotes the variance of the random variable
Y
. The SNR defined in (
2
) is known as the
output-referred
SNR.

In the sensor’s literature, there is an alternative definition of the SNR which is based on the exposure (i.e., the input). The definition of this
exposure-referred
SNR is
[
2
]

\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\frac{\text{signal at input}}{\text{noise at input}}=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Y]}\cdot\frac{d\theta}{d\mathbb{E}[Y]}}.

(3)

The denominator of the equation is called the exposure-referred noise or the input-referred noise
[
3
]
. In computer vision, an early work of Mitsunaga and Nayar in 1999
[
4
]
mentioned the equation (Equation (
3
)) although they did not give it a name.

The interpretation of the derivative
d\theta/d\mathbb{E}[Y]
is known to the sensor’s community
[
2
]
. The argument is that the input-referred noise is computed by back-propagating the signal from the output to the input via the transfer function
d\theta/d\mathbb{E}[Y]
which approximates the relationship between the input
\theta
and the output
\mathbb{E}[Y]

[
5
, supplementary report]
. As mentioned in multiple papers
[
6
,
5
]
, the presence of this derivative term is particularly important for sensors with a small full-well capacity because when a sensor saturates, the SNR has to drop. The exposure-referred SNR can capture this phenomenon whereas the output-referred SNR cannot.

Both versions of the SNRs are widely used in the industry
[
2
]
. People use the SNRs to evaluate sensors and to design sensor parameters so that the performance is maximized. However, from a theoretical point of view, besides the usual interpretation of treating the derivative as a transfer function, mathematically there is no rigorous proof trying to unify the two SNRs. The paper by Yang et al.
[
7
]
introduced the SNR from a statistical estimation perspective, whereas the paper by Elgendy and Chan
[
5
]
proved the equivalence between the SNR in
[
7
]
and
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}
for the case of one-bit sensors under a zero read noise. The connections between
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}
and
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}
and their generalizations to multi-bit sensors remain unanswered. The goal of this paper is to fill the gap by answering four questions:

(i)

How is the exposure-referred SNR derived from the first principle?

(ii)

Under the unified framework addressed in (i), is it possible to show that
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}
is a special case of
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}
?

(iii)

How to efficiently compute
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}
for complex forward imaging models?

(iv)

What are the utilities of
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
?

II
Mathematical Background

II-A

Limitations of Output-Referred SNR

To begin the discussion, it would be useful to first study the output-referred SNR and understand its limitations. Consider a Poisson-Gaussian random variable which is commonly used in modeling image sensors
[
8
,
9
]
:

X\sim\text{Poisson}(\theta)+\text{Gaussian}(0,\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}),

where
\theta
denotes the average number of photons (i.e., the flux integrated over the surface area and the exposure time), and
\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}
is the standard deviation of the read noise. This equation is a simplified model. Factors such as dark current, pixel non-uniformity, defects, analog-to-digital conversion, and color filter arrays are not considered to make the theoretical derivations tractable. Later in the paper, when the Monte Carlo simulation is introduced, some of these factors will be included.

Assuming that the sensor has a full-well capacity
L
(with the unit electrons), the output produced by the sensor follows the equation

Y=\begin{cases}X,&\qquad X<L,\\
L,&\qquad X\geq L.\end{cases}

(4)

If the full-well capacity is
L=\infty
, the measurement
Y
will never saturate, and hence the expectation is
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta
and the variance is
\mathrm{Var}[Y]=\theta+\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2}
. Then, according to (
2
),
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}
can be computed via

\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\theta+\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}}}.

(5)

If the read noise is negligible, i.e.
\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}\ll\theta
, the SNR can be simplified to
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\sqrt{\theta}
. This equation can be found in many standard texts, e.g.,
[
8
]
.

The problem arises when the full-well capacity is
finite
. When
L<\infty
,
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}
in (
2
) is still adequate to capture the sensor’s behavior when the exposure
\theta
is smaller than the full-well capacity
L
. However, if
\theta
reaches the full-well and goes beyond it, the mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
will stop growing with
\theta
as illustrated in Figure
1
. The variance
\mathrm{Var}[Y]
will gradually drop to zero because when
Y
goes beyond the full-well capacity, it will be capped. As a result,
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}
according to (
2
) will eventually go to infinity (because
\mathrm{Var}[Y]\rightarrow 0
). This is not a desirable behavior because the SNR beyond the saturation is supposed to be poor.

Fig. 1:
With a finite full-well capacity, the mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
will stop growing when the exposure
\theta
exceeds the full-well capacity
L=10^{2}
.

II-B

One-bit and Multi-bit Sensors

If the full-well capacity is the source of the issue, the next question is how commonly does it happen. The full-well capacity of a conventional CMOS image sensor (CIS) is thousands of electrons. Therefore, unless the scene is bright and the integration is long, the sensor generally does not need to operate near the full-well capacity. However, for a newer type of image sensors based on single-photon detectors, the full-well capacity can be as small as only one electron. Their idea is to acquire binary bits at a very high frame rate and use computational methods to reconstruct the image. This class of sensors is broadly known as the quanta image sensors (QIS) that can be implemented using the single-photon avalanche diodes (SPAD-QIS) or the existing CMOS technology (CIS-QIS)
[
10
]
.

From a mathematical modeling point of view, QIS is no different from a conventional CIS if it operates in the multi-bit mode because the equation will follow (
4
)
[
6
]
. The difference is that for QIS, the read noise is about 0.2 electrons whereas for CIS, the read noise can range from 2 to tens of electrons. If QIS operates in the one-bit mode,
Y
will follow

Y=\begin{cases}0,&\qquad X<q,\\
1,&\qquad X\geq q,\end{cases}

(6)

where
q
is the threshold (usually set as
q=0.5
). Because of the generality of equations (
4
) and (
6
), the theoretical results of this paper is applicable to sensors of
any
bit-depth, including QIS and the conventional CIS.

II-C

Truncated Poisson and the Incomplete Gamma Function

The multi-bit sensor equation in (
4
) requires some statistical tools to handle the truncated Poisson random variable. To further simplify notations, in this section, the read noise is
\sigma_{\text{read}}=0
so that
X\sim\text{Poisson}(\theta)
. In this case, the probability mass function of
Y
is

p_{Y}(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{\theta^{y}}{y!}e^{-\theta},&\qquad y<L,\\
\sum_{\ell=L}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{\ell}}{\ell!}e^{-\theta},&\qquad y=L.\end{cases}

(7)

By construction, the random variable
Y
will never take a value greater than
L
. The probability that
Y=L
is given by the sum of the Poisson tail, which can be conveniently expressed via the incomplete Gamma function as shown in Figure
2
.

Fig. 2:
Incomplete Gamma function
\Psi_{L}(\theta)
as a function of
\theta
.

Definition 1
(Incomplete Gamma function,
[
11
]
)

The upper incomplete Gamma function is defined as
\Psi_{L}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
, with

\Psi_{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(L)}\int_{\theta}^{\infty}t^{L-1}e^{-t}dt=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\frac{\theta^{\ell}e^{-\theta}}{\ell!},

(8)

for
\theta>0,L\in\mathbb{N}
where
\Gamma(L)=(L-1)!
is the standard Gamma function.

A few useful properties of
\Psi_{L}(\theta)
can be derived. First, the first-order derivative of
\Psi_{L}(\theta)
is

\Psi^{\prime}_{L}(\theta)=-\frac{\theta^{L-1}e^{-\theta}}{(L-1)!}<0,\;\;\text{for all}\;\theta,

(9)

which means that
\Psi_{L}(\theta)
is a strictly decreasing function in
\theta
. The steepest slope can be determined by analyzing the curvature

\Psi^{\prime\prime}_{L}(\theta)=-(L-1)\theta^{L-2}e^{-\theta}+e^{-\theta}\theta^{L-1}.

Equating this to zero will yield
\theta^{*}=L-1
. At this critical point and assume
L\gg 1
, a new result using Stirling’s formula can be shown
1
1

1

The proof of this result is given in the Appendix.
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{L}^{\prime}(\theta^{*})\approx-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\theta^{*}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\theta^{*}-(L-1))^{2}}{2\theta^{*}}\right\}.

(10)

Therefore,
\Psi_{L}^{\prime}(\theta^{*})=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(L-1)}}
. Hence, the slope of the incomplete Gamma function reduces as
L
increases.

Remark 1
: Most papers in the image sensor’s literature plot curves with respect to
\log_{10}\theta
instead of
\theta
, like the one shown in Figure
2
. The
x
-axis compression caused by
\log_{10}\theta
will make the transient of the incomplete Gamma function to appear steeper. The reason is that for any function
f(\theta)
, the slope in the
\log_{10}\theta
space is determined by
\frac{d}{d\log_{10}\theta}f(\theta)=\theta f^{\prime}(\theta)\cdot\log 10
. So for large
\theta
, the slope appears steeper.

II-D

Delta Method

A mathematical tool that will become useful later in the paper is the
Delta Method
in statistics. It approximates the variance when a random variable undergoes a nonlinear transformation.

Lemma 1
(Delta Method,
[
12
]
)

Consider a sequence of independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
with a common mean
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=\mu
. Define
\overline{X}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}
be the sample average, and assume that
\sqrt{N}(\overline{X}-\mu)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\text{Gaussian}(0,\tau^{2})
, where
\tau^{2}
is the standard deviation of a Gaussian random variable, and
\overset{d}{\rightarrow}
denotes convergence in distribution. Suppose that there is a continuously differentiable function
f
such that
f^{\prime}(\mu)
exists and is not zero. Then
\sqrt{N}[f(\overline{X})-f(\mu)]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\text{Gaussian}(0,\tau^{2}(f^{\prime}(\mu))^{2})
. In other words,

\mathbb{E}[(f(\overline{X})-f(\mu))^{2}]\approx[f^{\prime}(\mu)]^{2}\mathrm{Var}[\overline{X}].

(11)

Proof:

The complete proof can be found in
[
12
, Theorem 2.5.2]
. The two key arguments in the proof are (1)
\overline{X}
converges in distribution to
\mu
due to the Central Limit Theorem; (2) Taylor approximation gives

\displaystyle f(\overline{X})\approx f(\mu)+f^{\prime}(\mu)(\overline{X}-\mu)+o_{p}(\overline{X}-\mu),

where
o_{p}
denotes the little-
o_{p}
notation
[
12
, Definition 2.1.3]
and hence

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left[f(\overline{X})-f(\mu)\right]\approx\sqrt{N}f^{\prime}(\mu)(\overline{X}-\mu)+o_{p}(\sqrt{N}(\overline{X}-\mu)).

Taking squares on both sides gives (
11
).
∎

III
SNR: A Statistical Definition

III-A

Defining the SNR

When defining the SNR, it is important to clarify the notion of signal and noise. In most of the imaging problems, the underlying signal is the scene exposure
\theta
. The signal
\theta
defines a probability distribution
p_{Y}(y;\theta)
from which the i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
are drawn.

Reconstruction of the signal
\theta
from
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
is based on an
estimator

\widehat{\theta}
. An estimator can be any mapping that maps
\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}]
to
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
. However, if
\theta
is a fixed scalar, most sensors will produce an estimate based on the sample average
\overline{Y}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}
because the on-chip processing today is largely limited to simple operations such as addition. In this case, the estimator can be written as
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})
, which can also be interpreted as an estimator based on the sufficient statistics.

The noise term in the SNR is the deviation between the estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
and the true parameter
\theta
. Since the estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
is random, the noise power is the expectation

\text{noise power}=\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})-\theta)^{2}],

(12)

which is also the mean squared error.

Definition 2
(SNR, formal definition)

Let
\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}]
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from the distribution
p_{Y}(y;\theta)
. Construct an estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
. Then the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is

\text{SNR}(\theta)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})-\theta)^{2}]}}.

(13)

The above definition is not a new invention. When Yang et al. presented the analysis of the quanta image sensor in 2011
[
7
]
, this definition was already used to derive the Cramer-Rao lower bound. Subsequent papers such as
[
5
]
and
[
13
]
by Chan and colleagues also rely on this definition. A version for single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) is presented by Gupta and colleagues
[
14
]
.

To elaborate on this formal definition of the SNR, consider the two examples below.

Example 1
(Poisson)

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Poisson}(\theta)
, and consider the maximum-likelihood estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\overline{Y}
. (The derivation of the ML estimator for a Poisson random variable is skipped for brevity.) Since
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\overline{Y}
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})-\theta)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Y}-\theta)^{2}]=\frac{\theta}{N},

where the second equality holds because the variance of a Poisson random variable is
\theta
. Therefore,
\text{SNR}(\theta)=\sqrt{N}\cdot\sqrt{\theta}
, which is consistent with (
5
) when
\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}=0
.
\square

Example 2
(Poisson + Gaussian)

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Poisson}(\theta)+\text{Gaussian}(0,\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2})
. Consider the ML estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\overline{Y}
. It then follows that

\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})-\theta)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Y}-\theta)^{2}]=\frac{1}{N}\left(\theta+\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2}\right),

where the second equality holds because the variance of a Poisson-Gaussian is the sum of the two variances. Therefore,
\text{SNR}(\theta)=\sqrt{N}\cdot\theta/\sqrt{\theta+\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2}}
. This result is consistent with (
5
) for a general
\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}
.
\square

III-B

Mean Invariance

The formal definition of the SNR is general for
any
estimator. However, the statistical noise model for an actual image sensor can be complicated. In fact, except for a few special occasions where the maximum-likelihood (ML) estimator can be expressed in a closed form, in most other situations the ML estimator cannot be obtained in a closed form. In this case, a more convenient way is to define the estimator from the mean.

Definition 3
(Mean invariant estimator)

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from the distribution
p_{Y}(y;\theta)
. Let
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\int y\cdot p_{Y}(y;\theta)\,dy
be the mean of
Y
. An estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
is mean invariant if

\mu(\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y}))=\overline{Y}.

(14)

If
\mu^{-1}
exists, the mean invariant estimator is
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y})
.

The following two examples shows that many estimators are mean invariant.

Example 3

Let
Y_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\text{Gaussian}(\theta,\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2})
for
n=1,\ldots,N
, where
\theta
is the unknown parameter. It can be shown that the maximum-likelihood (ML) estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{Y})=\mathop{\underset{\theta}{\mbox{argmax}}}\;\prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(Y_{n}-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{\scriptsize read}}^{2}}\right\}=\overline{Y}.

Notice that if
Y
is Gaussian, the mean is
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta
. Therefore, the mean invariance property holds:

\displaystyle\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{Y}))\overset{(a)}{=}\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{Y})=\overline{Y}.

where
(a)
is due to the fact that
\mu(\theta)=\theta
.
\square

Example 4

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be i.i.d. one-bit measurements defined according to (
6
), with
q=0.5
and
\sigma_{\text{read}}=0
. In this case,
Y_{n}
is a Bernoulli random variable such that
Y_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(1-e^{-\theta})
. The ML estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{Y})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\underset{\theta}{\mbox{argmax}}}\;\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1-e^{-\theta})^{Y_{n}}(e^{-\theta})^{1-Y_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\log(1-\overline{Y}).

Since the mean of
Y
is
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[Y]=1-e^{-\theta}
, it follows that

\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{Y}))=1-e^{-\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{Y})}=\overline{Y}.

Again, the mean invariance property is satisfied.
\square

A mean invariant estimator is easy to construct. Even if
p_{Y}(y;\theta)
has a complex form, the mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
can be obtained through Monte Carlo simulation. Once the mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
is determined, the mean invariant estimator
\widehat{\theta}
can be
constructed
from
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y})
, assuming that
\mu^{-1}
exists.

Follow up of Example
3
. Let
Y_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\text{Gaussian}(\theta,\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2})
for
n=1,\ldots,N
where
\theta
is the unknown parameter. Since the mean is
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta
, it follows that
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta
. This
\mu
is the identity mapping, and so the inverse mapping is
\mu^{-1}(s)=s
for any
s
. Thus, one can define an estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y})=\overline{Y}
. As seen, it is the same as the ML estimator derived in Example
3
. Moreover,
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})
is mean invariant because
\widehat{\theta}
is constructed in that way.
\square

Follow up of Example
4
. Let
Y_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(1-e^{-\theta})
be the one-bit measurements for
n=1,\ldots,N
. The mean is
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[Y]=1-e^{-\theta}
, and so the inverse is
\mu^{-1}(s)=-\log(1-s)
. Therefore, one can define the estimator as
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y})=-\log(1-\overline{Y})
The result is identical to Example
4
. Furthermore, since the estimator is constructed from the mean invariance property, it has to satisfy the property.
\square

Based on Example
3
and Example
4
, one may conjecture that any ML estimator is also the mean invariant estimator. The observation is correct for any distributions in the exponential family. The proof is given in the Appendix. Outside the exponential family the two can be different. The following is a counter example.

Example 5
(Mean invariant estimator
\not=
ML estimator)

Consider a truncated Poisson distribution

\displaystyle p_{Y}(y;\theta)=\begin{cases}\frac{\theta^{y}e^{-\theta}}{y!},&\qquad y<L,\\
1-\Psi_{L}(\theta),&\qquad y\geq L.\end{cases}

Using (
23
) (to be proved in the next section), the mean is
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))
. Let
\mu(\theta)=\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))
. So, the mean invariant estimator can be defined as

\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y}),

which is a function of
\overline{Y}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}
.

Now consider the ML estimator. The ML estimator is

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\underset{\theta}{\mbox{argmax}}}\;\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigg[(Y_{n}\log\theta-\theta)\cdot\underset{Z_{n}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{I}\{Y_{n}<L\}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\log(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))\cdot\underset{1-Z_{n}}{\underbrace{\mathbb{I}\{Y_{n}\geq L\}}}\Bigg],

(15)

where
Z_{n}=\mathbb{I}\{Y_{n}<L\}
is the indicator function that returns 1 if
Y_{n}<L
or 0 if otherwise. Taking derivative and setting it to zero implies that
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}
must satisfy the equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}Z_{n}\right)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}\right)\cdot\frac{\Psi_{L}^{\prime}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})}{1-\Psi_{L}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})}.

(16)

Therefore, the ML estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y})
must be a function of
(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}Z_{n}
and
(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Z_{n}
, not
\overline{Y}
.
\square

While the ML estimator and the mean invariant estimator are generally not the same, they are asymptotically equivalent. As
N\rightarrow\infty
, the consistency of the ML estimator implies that
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y})\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\theta

[
12
]
. On the other hand, the law of large numbers implies that
\overline{Y}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mu(\theta)
. So, a mean invariant estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y})\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\mu^{-1}(\mu(\theta))=\theta
. Therefore, as
N\rightarrow\infty
, both
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y})
and
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
will converge in probability to the true parameter
\theta
and hence they are equivalent asymptotically.

Mean invariance is also not the same as the invariance principle of the ML estimator. The invariance principle of the ML estimator says that if there is a monotonic mapping
h
that maps
\theta
to
h(\theta)
, then
h(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})
will be the ML estimator of
h(\theta)
. The following example shows a case where mean invariance is different from the invariance of ML.

Example 6

Consider
X_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(\theta)
and
Y_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny{i.i.d.}}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(e^{\theta})
for
n=1,\ldots,N
. The ML estimator of
\theta
using
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
is

\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\overline{X})=\overline{X}.

According to the invariance of the ML estimator, a monotonic mapping
h_{1}(\cdot)=e^{(\cdot)}
will ensure that
\widehat{\theta}_{1}\overset{\text{def}}{=}h_{1}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})=e^{\overline{X}}
is the ML estimator of
e^{\theta}
. By the same principle, if there is a different monotonic mapping
\widehat{\theta}_{2}\overset{\text{def}}{=}h_{2}(\cdot)=\log(\cdot)
, it holds that
h_{2}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})=\log(\overline{X})
is the ML estimator of
\log(\theta)
.

Now consider the mean
\mu(\theta)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}[Y]=e^{\theta}
. The invariance principle says nothing about whether
\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{1}(\overline{Y}))=\overline{Y}
or
\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{2}(\overline{Y}))=\overline{Y}
. In fact,
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
does not satisfy the mean invariance property because
\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{1}(\overline{Y}))=e^{\widehat{\theta}_{1}(\overline{Y})}=e^{e^{\overline{Y}}}\not=\overline{Y}
. However,
\widehat{\theta}_{2}
satisfies the mean invariance property because
\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{2}(\overline{Y}))=e^{\log(\overline{Y})}=\overline{Y}
. Therefore, the invariance principle of the ML estimator is completely different from the mean invariance property.
\square

To summarize, the mean invariance is a property that specifically focuses on whether the mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]
can be nonlinearly mapped to recover the true parameter
\theta
. This is the property required for the exposure-referred SNR. Whether the estimator is the ML estimator is not of concern.

In the statistics literature, the mean invariance property presented in this paper is related to the
link function
for the generalized linear models. Specifically, the mapping
\mu(\theta)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}[Y]
from the true parameter
\theta
to the mean
\mu
is known as the link function, and the inverse mapping
\mu^{-1}
is known as the response function. Readers interested in details of this topic can consult
[
15
]
.

III-C

Exposure-referred SNR

With all the mathematical tools ready, the exposure-referred SNR can now be formally derived.

Theorem 1

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from the probability density function
p_{Y}(y;\theta)
. Define
\overline{Y}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}
. Let
\mu(\theta)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}[Y]
and assume that
\mu^{-1}
exists. Let
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})
be the mean invariant estimator such that
\widehat{\theta}(\boldsymbol{Y})=\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y})
. Then the SNR defined in (
13
) is related to
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}
as

\text{SNR}(\theta)\approx\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}.

(17)

Proof:

By the Delta Method, the mean squared error can be approximated by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})-\theta\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})-\widehat{\theta}(\mu(\theta))\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\left[\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}(\mu(\theta))\right]^{2}\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}],

where the derivative is taken with respect to
\mu(\theta)
.

Since
\widehat{\theta}(\mu(\theta))=\mu^{-1}(\mu(\theta))=\theta
, it follows that
\frac{d\widehat{\theta}(\mu)}{d\mu}=\frac{d\theta}{d\mu}
. So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})-\theta\right)^{2}\right]=\left[\frac{d\theta}{d\mu}\right]^{2}\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}].

(18)

Using the fact that
\frac{d\theta}{d\mu}=1/\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}
, the SNR can be written as

\displaystyle\text{SNR}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})-\theta\right)^{2}\right]}}\approx\underset{\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)}{\underbrace{\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}}},

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 1

Under the same conditions listed in Theorem
1
, the exposure-referred SNR is related to the output-referred SNR as

\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)\cdot\frac{\theta}{\mu}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}.

(19)

Proof:

The proof follows from the substitution

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\underset{=\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)}{\underbrace{\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}}}\cdot\frac{\theta}{\mu}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}.

This completes the proof.
∎

As one can see from (
18
), the derivative
d\mu/d\theta
is added because of the Delta Method. It is the first-order approximation of a nonlinear mapping from the output to the input. Using the argument of Elgendy and Chan
[
5
]
, this first-order derivative can be regarded as a transfer function relating the output
\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}]
to the input
\theta
. If the input-output has a linear relationship
\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}]=\theta
, which is the case of a CIS with a large full-well capacity, then the derivative is
d\mu/d\theta=1
and so
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
.

III-D

Illustrating the SNR via one-bit QIS

To elaborate on the difference between
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
and
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)
, it would be instructive to consider the statistics of a one-bit quanta image sensor. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Poisson}(\theta)
and let
Y_{n}
be the random variables defined in (
6
).

First, consider the case where
q=1
. Since
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(1-e^{-\theta})
, the mean is
\mathbb{E}[Y]=1-e^{-\theta}
. Define the mean as
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[Y]=1-e^{-\theta}
. As shown in Example
4
, the maximum-likelihood estimate of
\theta
is
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=-\log(1-\overline{Y})
and it satisfies the mean invariance property. The derivative
d\mu/d\theta
is

\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left[1-e^{-\theta}\right]=e^{-\theta}.

Substituting into Theorem
1
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}=\sqrt{N}\cdot\theta\cdot\sqrt{\frac{e^{-\theta}}{1-e^{-\theta}}}.

For cases where
q>1
, one can use the incomplete Gamma function so that

p_{Y}(y;\theta)=\begin{cases}1-\Psi_{q}(\theta),&\qquad y=1,\\
\Psi_{q}(\theta),&\qquad y=0.\end{cases}

It then follows that
\mathbb{E}[Y]=1-\Psi_{q}(\theta)
and the estimator can be chosen such that
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})=\Psi_{q}^{-1}(1-\overline{Y})
. The mean invariance property is therefore validated. The derivative
d\mu/d\theta
is

\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}=\frac{d}{d\theta}(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta))=\frac{\theta^{q-1}e^{-\theta}}{(q-1)!}.

Hence, the SNR is

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\frac{\sqrt{N}\cdot\theta}{\sqrt{\Psi_{q}(\theta)(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta))}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{q-1}e^{-\theta}}{(q-1)!},

(20)

of which the visualization is shown in Figure
3
. This result is consistent with the one shown by Elgendy and Chan by deriving the Fisher Information
[
5
]
.

Fig. 3:
Exposure-referred SNR for a one-bit quanta image sensor, assuming that
\sigma_{\text{read}}=0
. As
\theta
goes beyond the threshold
q
,
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
starts to drop as expected.

Unlike
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
, the output-referred SNR goes to infinity when
\theta
grows. For the same one-bit statistics, the output-referred SNR is simply the ratio between
\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}]
and
\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]
, which is

\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}=\sqrt{N}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-\Psi_{q}(\theta)}{\Psi_{q}(\theta)}}.

(21)

As shown in Figure
4
,
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)
grows indefinitely as
\theta
grows. This does not reflect the reality because when
\theta
grows beyond the threshold
q
, the measurements
\{Y_{n}\,|\,n=1,\ldots,N\}
will have more one’s. The signal degrades and hence eventually the SNR drops to zero.

\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)

IV
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
for Finite full-well Capacity

The subject of this paper is to understand the exposure-referred SNR for digital (CCD and CMOS) image sensors with a finite full-well capacity
L
. In particular, the goal is to understand the situation when
L
is small, e.g., a few bits. This section presents the main result for such a scenario.

IV-A

\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
for Truncated Poisson

To make the analysis tractable, the derivation in this section will be focusing on a truncated Poisson distribution assuming
\sigma_{\text{read}}=0
. Extension to the more complex noise model will be analyzed later.

Theorem 2
(
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
for truncated Poisson)

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables following the truncated Poisson statistics defined in (
4
) where
X_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d.}}{\sim}\text{Poisson}(\theta)
for
n=1,\ldots,N
. Let
\widehat{\theta}(\overline{Y})
be an estimator satisfying the mean invariance property. Then the exposure-referred SNR is

\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\sqrt{N}\cdot\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Y_{n}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta},

(22)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mu,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y]

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi_{L-2}(\theta)+\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L^{2}(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))-\mu^{2},

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi_{L-1}^{\prime}(\theta)+\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)-L\Psi_{L}^{\prime}(\theta).

(23)

Proof:

The proof is presented in the Appendix.
∎

To illustrate the predicted
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
as a function of
\theta
, Figure

shows several curves evaluated at different full-well capacity
L
. As is consistent with the one-bit QIS example shown in Section III.D, the exposure-referred SNR for a truncated Poisson random variable also demonstrates a drop in
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
after the pixel saturates. What is more interesting is that as

increases,

becomes a straight line in the log-log plot with a sharp decay after saturation.

V
Monte Carlo Simulation

So far, the theoretical derivations have been focusing on the Poisson distribution only. Read noise, dark current, quantization error, and other sources of noise have not been considered. When including these factors, seeking an analytic expression would be significantly more challenging. A more reasonable approach is to resort to numerical schemes to estimate the SNR approximately.

V-A

General Principle

In general, the measurement
Y
generated by an image sensor is the result of a sequence of optical-electronic operations such as

Y=\text{clip}\left\{\text{round}\left\{\text{Poisson}(\theta+\theta_{\text{dark}})+\text{Gauss}(0,\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2})\right\}\right\},

(26)

where
\theta_{\text{dark}}
denotes the dark current, “round” denotes the analog-to-digital (A/D) conversion, and “clip” denotes the saturation due to a finite full-well capacity. Assuming a sufficiently large full-well capacity
L
, the output-referred SNR is given by
[
16
,
17
]

\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\theta+\theta_{\text{dark}}+\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}}},&\quad\theta<L,\\
0,&\quad\theta\geq L,\end{cases}

(27)

where
L
is the full-well capacity.

To compute
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
, the numerical approach is to draw samples from the the distribution defined by the forward model:

Y_{m}=\text{forward model}\left(\theta\;|\;\theta_{\text{dark}},\sigma_{\text{read}},L\right),

(28)

for
m=1,\ldots,M
, where
M
denotes the number of Monte Carlo samples. As stated in (
28
), the
m
th sample
Y_{m}
is a function of the underlying signal
\theta
, along with other model parameters. Do not confuse
M
with the number of i.i.d. measurements
N
used in the previous subsections when defining the average
\overline{Y}
.

The Monte Carlo sampling scheme goes as follow. Consider
N=1
. For every
\theta
, the sample average is an estimate of
\mathbb{E}[Y]
and the sample variance is an estimate of
\mathrm{Var}[Y]
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(\theta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m},\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(\theta)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(Y_{m}-\widehat{\mu})^{2}.

Once
\widehat{\mu}(\theta)
has been determined for every
\theta
, the derivative
d\mu/d\theta
can be approximated by

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\mu}}{d\theta}=\frac{\widehat{\mu}(\theta_{k+1})-\widehat{\mu}(\theta_{k})}{\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}},

where
\{\theta_{k}\,|\,\theta_{k}<\theta_{k+1},\;k=1,\ldots,K\}
is the discrete set of exposures used to evaluate the mean and variance. Consequently, if there are
N
i.i.d. samples,
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
can be approximately estimated by

\widehat{\text{SNR}}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)=\sqrt{N}\cdot\frac{\theta}{\widehat{\sigma}}\cdot\frac{d\widehat{\mu}}{d\theta}.

(29)

V-B

\theta_{\text{dark}}
and
\sigma_{\text{read}}

With the Monte Carlo simulation technique, complex forward models can be visualized. Consider the following two demonstrations.

\theta_{\text{dark}}=0.016
(which is consistent with the quanta image sensor

L=15
electrons, and 4-bit A/D converter. The read noise level

.

The utility of the Monte Carlo simulation is that it bypasses the complication of seeking for an analytic expression of
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
. To account for even more difficult modelings such as the pixel response non-uniformity,

VI
Utilities

After elaborating on the details of the exposure-referred SNR, readers may ask: what are the utilities of this SNR? The answer is simple. As a performance metric of an image sensor, the primary utility of the exposure-referred SNR is to describe how well an image sensor performs. Because of this primary goal, three points should be noted:

•

The exposure-referred SNR is a generalized version of the output-referred SNR. The latter is a special case of the former when the bit-depth
L
is large as shown in Figure
8
. The output-referred SNR and the exposure-referred SNR are very similar for large
L
, whereas, for a small bit-depth, the output-referred SNR cannot capture the phenomenon when the exposure goes beyond the full-well capacity.

•

Because of the first point, any subsequent low bit-depth sensors applications need to use the exposure-referred SNR. Using the output-referred SNR will lead to sub-optimal performance, and this will be illustrated in Application 1 on high-dynamic range imaging.

Fig. 8:
The output-referred SNR is a special case of the exposure-referred SNR when the full-well capacity
L
is large. This figure plots the SNRs of a truncated Poisson random variable. For the output-referred SNR, the curve is generated by
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=\sqrt{\theta}
for
\theta\leq L
and
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)=0
for
\theta>L
.

•

Again, because of the first point, the exposure-referred SNR can be used as the objective function to tune the parameters of the one-bit and few-bit image sensors. Since closed-form expressions of the SNR are available for some cases, the optimal parameters can also be expressed in closed-form. Application 2 on the threshold design will illustrate this point.

VI-A

High Dynamic Range Imaging Using a 3-bit Sensor

The majority of the high dynamic range (HDR) image reconstruction algorithms today are designed for digital image sensors with a large full-well capacity, and so
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)
is adequate. However, for image sensors with a small full-well capacity, e.g.,
L=7
, a fusion algorithm based on
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
will produce a better image.

To illustrate the impact of
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
in HDR imaging, consider the problem of reconstructing one HDR image (a single pixel)
\widehat{\theta}
from
M
exposure brackets
\boldsymbol{Y}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{Y}^{M}
. Each of these exposure brackets
\boldsymbol{Y}^{m}=[Y_{1}^{m},\ldots,Y_{N}^{m}]
has
N
frames. The formation of each frame follows the equation

Y^{m}_{n}\overset{\text{\tiny i.i.d.}}{\sim}\begin{cases}\text{Poisson}(\tau_{m}\theta),&\quad\tau_{m}\theta<L,\\
L,&\quad\tau_{m}\theta\geq L,\end{cases}

for
n=1,\ldots,N
, where
\tau_{m}
is the
m
th integration time. To reconstruct the
m
th low-dynamic range (LDR) image, one can substitute the sample average
\overline{Y}^{m}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}^{m}
into the inverse mapping of the mean
\mu^{-1}
. Then the HDR image is fused by a linear combination of the estimates
2
2

2

The problem here does not assume any motion so that the theoretically optimal solution can be analytically derived. Handling motion remains an open problem in computer vision, although significant progress has been made over the past decade.

\widehat{\theta}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}\frac{\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y}^{m})}{\tau_{m}},

(30)

where
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{M}\}
is a set of linear combination weights. The development of the idea can be traced back to Mann and Picard
[
19
]
, Debevec and Malik
[
20
]
, among other works
[
21
,
16
,
22
]
. For a conventional CIS with a large full-well capacity,
\mu^{-1}
is an identity mapping and so the reconstruction is simplified to
\widehat{\theta}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{w_{m}}{\tau_{m}}\overline{Y}^{m}
. For sensors with a small full-well capacity, the nonlinearity of the mean function should be taken into account.
This reconstruction scheme is elementary and predates all the deep learning methods.

As previously proved by Gnanasambandam and Chan in
[
13
]
, the optimal weight
w_{m}
is

w_{m}=\frac{\text{SNR}_{m}^{2}(\theta)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\text{SNR}_{m}^{2}(\theta)},

(31)

where
\text{SNR}_{m}(\theta)
is the SNR of the
m
th low dynamic range image. If
\text{SNR}_{\text{out}}(\theta)
is used, the weight will become

\displaystyle w_{m}=\frac{\text{SNR}_{\text{out},m}^{2}(\theta)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\text{SNR}_{\text{out},m}^{2}(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{\tau_{m}\theta\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}})^{2}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\sqrt{\tau_{m}\theta\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{m}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\tau_{m}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}},

(32)

where the function
\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}
is a binary indicator showing whether the total exposure
\tau_{m}\theta
has exceeded the full-well capacity.
\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}=1
if the argument is true and
\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{m}\theta<L\}}=0
if the argument is false. If
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp},m}(\theta)
is used, the optimal weight will become

w_{m}=\frac{\text{SNR}_{\text{exp},m}^{2}(\theta)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\text{SNR}_{\text{exp},m}^{2}(\theta)},

(33)

where one can substitute (
22
) into this equation.

Once the combined image is formed, the overall SNR can be computed via

\text{SNR}_{\text{HDR}}(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\frac{w_{m}}{\tau_{m}}\right)^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}(\theta)}}.

(34)

where
\sigma_{m}^{2}(\theta)=\mathrm{Var}[\mu^{-1}(\overline{Y}^{m})]
is the noise variance of the
m
th reconstructed LDR image. The intuitive interpretation of (
34
) is that it weighs the noise according to the integration time
\tau_{m}
and combination weight
w_{m}
to produce a calibrated noise. Thus, the ratio is the SNR with respect to the optimally combined image
\widehat{\theta}
.

Optimal

\theta\gg 1

The context of this operating regime is that the sensor sees a sufficient amount of photons but it chooses to operate in a single-bit mode to earn the speed. In this case, the threshold

When
\theta\gg 1

0\leq\Psi_{q}(\theta)\leq 1

\Psi_{q}(\theta)(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta))\leq 1/4
where the maximum is attained when
\Psi_{q}(\theta)=1/2

\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\Psi_{q}(\theta)(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta))}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{q-1}e^{-\theta}}{(q-1)!}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\theta}{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{q-1}e^{-\theta}}{(q-1)!}.

(35)

The following lemma shows the optimal
q
for the lower bound.

Lemma 2
(Maximizing the lower bound)

Consider the SNR lower bound for one-bit QIS given by (

q^{*}=\lfloor\theta\rfloor+1
.

Proof:

The proof of this lemma can be found in
[

]
.
∎

What was not proved in

\theta

\Psi_{q}(\theta)
can be approximated by the cumulative distribution function of a Gaussian via the Stirling’s formula. This can be seen from (

\theta

The integral is
\Psi_{q}(\theta)=1/2
when

\Psi_{q}(\theta)=1/2
and so

. This is a new result. The context is that the photon flux is small and so the goal of the sensor is to count the number of photons. However, for small

VII
Discussions and Conclusion

VII-A

Alternatives to SNR?

While SNR is a natural choice for analyzing the performance of an image sensor, it is by no means the
only
option. Especially for one-bit devices such as the quanta image sensor, there are other ways to characterize the performance.

VII-A
1
Entropy

As far as one-bit measurements are concerned, the entropy is a natural substitute of the SNR. If
Y
is binary with
p_{Y}(1)=1-\Psi_{q}(\theta)
and
p_{Y}(0)=\Psi_{q}(\theta)
, the entropy is

\displaystyle H(Y)

\displaystyle=-p_{Y}(1)\log_{2}p_{Y}(1)-p_{Y}(0)\log_{2}p_{Y}(0)

\displaystyle=-(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta))\log_{2}(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta))-\Psi_{q}(\theta)\log_{2}\Psi_{q}(\theta).

It is relatively easy to show that the derivative of the entropy with respect to
\Psi_{q}(\theta)
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\Psi_{q}(\theta)}H(Y)=-\log\left(\frac{1-\Psi_{q}(\theta)}{\Psi_{q}(\theta)}\right).

Setting it to zero will yield
\Psi_{q}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore, the entropy is maximized when
\mathbb{E}[Y]=1-\Psi_{q}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[Y]
is the expected value of the measurement,
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{1}{2}
means that the entropy is maximized when there are 50% one’s and 50% zero’s in a set of independent measurements. So, if the application goal is to identify a threshold
q
such that the performance of the sensor is maximized, then instead of optimizing for the SNR as in Section VI.B.1, the alternative is to optimize the entropy. The solution to
\Psi_{q}(\theta)=1/2
is
q^{*}=\theta+1
, which is consistent with Section VI.B.1.

VII-A
2
Bit Error Rate (BER)

In the presence of read noise, the bit error rate is another commonly used criterion to evaluate the performance of a sensor. For one-bit quanta image sensor, the BER measures the probability of making a wrong decision (i.e., declaring a 0 as a 1, or declaring a 1 as a 0). It can be readily computed as

\displaystyle\text{BER}(\theta)

\displaystyle=p_{Y}(0)\cdot\int_{q}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle\quad+p_{Y}(1)\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(t-1)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\text{read}}^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{q}{\sigma_{\text{read}}\sqrt{2}}\right)\Psi_{q}(\theta)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{1-q}{\sigma_{\text{read}}\sqrt{2}}\right)(1-\Psi_{q}(\theta)).

(39)

Therefore, if
q=1/2
, the BER is simplified to

\text{BER}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{read}}\sqrt{8}}\right),

(40)

which does not depend on
\theta
. If
\text{BER}(\theta)
can be empirically measured, then by inverting (
40
) one can estimate the read noise
\sigma_{\text{read}}
. For a fixed
\theta
, one can also optimize (
39
) by finding an appropriate
q
.

VII-B

Conclusion

The exposure-referred SNR is a concept motivated by the need to capture the sensor’s behavior near and beyond the full-well capacity. For small image sensors with one or few bits, exposure-referred SNR provides a natural characterization of the performance without showing an infinite SNR due to the artificial squeezing of the noise. In order to establish the exposure-referred SNR, the paper introduces new mathematical concepts and showed a few results:

•

The mean invariance property is introduced. The property asserts that when an estimator
\widehat{\theta}(\cdot)
is applied to the mean of the measurement, the mapped value is the true parameter
\widehat{\theta}(\mu)=\theta
.

•

Exposure-referred SNR calibrates the noise variance
\mathrm{Var}[Y]
by the derivative
d\mu/d\theta
, where the derivative is the result of the first-order approximation used in the Delta Method.

•

SNR of a sensor with a finite full-well capacity is analytically derived via the incomplete Gamma function. The new result generalizes the conventional ones. For sensors with a large full-well capacity, the new result recovers the classical one that shows a linear response. For sensors with a small full-well capacity, the new result shows how the transient of the SNR looks like.

•

Monte Carlo simulation is presented for complex noise models. The general procedure is to estimate the mean of the measurement
\mu(\theta)
and the variance of the measurement
\mathrm{Var}[Y]
. Then by using a numerical finite difference operator, the derivative
d\mu/d\theta
can be approximated.

•

Optimal high dynamic range image reconstruction is shown. The new result generalizes the classical linear response.

•

Threshold analysis of one-bit sensors is revisited and generalized. For
\theta\gg 1
, the optimal threshold is
q=\theta+1
. For
\theta\approx 1
, the optimal threshold needs to overcome the read noise and so the optimal value is
q=1/2
.

•

For one-bit sensors, it was found that the entropy of the bits and the bit error rate can be used as alternatives to characterize the sensor.

As the full-well capacity of the image sensors is becoming small, it is anticipated that the exposure-referred SNR will become a useful utility for theoretical analysis of the sensors, hence allowing signal processing

]
, algorithm development
[
27
,

,

,

,

]
, and computer vision applications
[

,
33
]
. Specific problems such as sensor gain control, exposure analysis, bit-depth to speed trade off, and color filter array, will be important questions to answer next.

VIII-A

Gaussian approximation of Poisson

When deriving the first-order derivative of the incomplete Gamma function, it was mentioned that the Poisson distribution can be approximated by a Gaussian. The formal statement is as follows.

Lemma 3
(Gaussian approximation of Poisson)

For large
\theta
(i.e.,
\theta\gg 1
), it holds that

p_{X}(x)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\theta^{x}e^{-\theta}}{x!}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\theta}}e^{-\frac{(x-\theta)^{2}}{2\theta}}.

(41)

Note that this is
not
the Central Limit theorem because it does not involve any sample average. The approximation compares the two functions.

Proof:

First of all, take the log on the Poisson equation:

\log p_{X}(x)=\log\left\{\frac{\theta^{x}e^{-\theta}}{x!}\right\}=x\log\theta-\theta-\log x!

Stirling’s formula states that for
x\rightarrow\infty
, we have
x!\approx x^{x}e^{-x}\sqrt{2\pi x}
. Substitute into the previous equation yields

\displaystyle\log p_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\approx x\log\theta-\theta-\log\left(x^{x}e^{-x}\sqrt{2\pi x}\right)

\displaystyle=x\log\theta-\theta-x\log x+x-\log\sqrt{2\pi x}.

The Gaussian has to fit the Poisson well around the mean, which is
\theta
. Thus define
x=\theta+\epsilon
with
\theta\gg\epsilon
. Then,

\displaystyle\log p_{X}(x)

\displaystyle={x}\log\theta-\theta-{x}\log{x}+{x}-\log\sqrt{2\pi{x}}

\displaystyle\hskip-30.1388pt={(\theta+\epsilon)}\log\theta-\theta-{(\theta+\epsilon)}\log{(\theta+\epsilon)}

\displaystyle\qquad+{(\theta+\epsilon)}-\log\sqrt{2\pi{(\theta+\epsilon)}}

\displaystyle\hskip-30.1388pt=\epsilon+(\theta+\epsilon)\log\frac{\theta}{\theta+\epsilon}-\log\sqrt{2\pi(\theta+\epsilon)}

\displaystyle\hskip-30.1388pt=\epsilon-(\theta+\epsilon)\log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}\right)-\log\sqrt{2\pi\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-30.1388pt=\epsilon-\log\sqrt{2\pi\theta}-\left(\theta+\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}\right){\log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}\right)}.

For
\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}\ll 1
, it holds that
\log(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\theta})\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}+\ldots
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\log p_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\approx\epsilon-\log\sqrt{2\pi\theta}-\left(\theta+\epsilon+\frac{1}{2}\right){\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}+\ldots\right)}

\displaystyle=\epsilon-\log\sqrt{2\pi\theta}-\epsilon-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\theta}-\frac{\epsilon}{2\theta}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\theta}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\theta^{2}}+\ldots.

By canceling terms, and removing
\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\theta^{2}}
and
\frac{\epsilon}{2\theta}
(because
\frac{\epsilon}{\theta}\ll 1
), it follows that
\log p_{X}(x)\approx-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\theta}-\log\sqrt{2\pi\theta}
.
This implies that
p_{X}(x)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\theta}}e^{-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\theta}}
. Substituting
x=\theta+\epsilon
completes the proof.
∎

Mean Invariant = ML for Exponential Family

A mean invariant estimator is generally not the same as an ML estimator. However, for distributions in the exponential family, they are identical. Recall the definition of the exponential family. A sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\boldsymbol{Y}=[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}]
is said to be in the exponential family if

p_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y};\theta)=h(\boldsymbol{y})\exp\left\{\eta(\theta)T(\boldsymbol{y})-A(\theta)\right\},

(42)

for some functions
h
,
\eta
,
T
, and
A

[
12
]
. The function
T(\boldsymbol{y})
is the sufficient statistic. Since a necessary condition for an estimator to be mean invariant is that it is a function of the sample average
\overline{Y}
, in the subsequent discussions it is assumed that
T(\boldsymbol{y})=\overline{y}
where
\overline{y}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}y_{n}
with
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
as the realizations of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
.

Example 9

For one-bit QIS, the probability density function can be written as

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y};\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\exp\left\{\log\left[(1-e^{-\theta})^{y_{n}}(e^{-\theta})^{1-y_{n}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{N\overline{y}\log(1-e^{-\theta})+N(1-\overline{y})\log(e^{-\theta})\right\}

\displaystyle=\left[\exp\left\{\overline{y}\log(e^{\theta}-1)-\theta\right\}\right]^{N}.

So, one can associate
h(\boldsymbol{y})=1
,
T(\boldsymbol{y})=\overline{y}
,
\eta(\theta)=\log(e^{\theta}-1)
,
A(\theta)=\theta
.

Lemma 4

\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y})
is the ML estimate if and only if it satisfies the equation

g(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y}))\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{A^{\prime}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y}))}{\eta^{\prime}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y}))}=\overline{y},

(43)

where
g(\theta)\overset{\text{def}}{=}A^{\prime}(\theta)/\eta^{\prime}(\theta)
is a function, provided that
A^{\prime}(\theta)
and
\eta^{\prime}(\theta)
exist and are not zero. If
g^{-1}
exists, then
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y})=g^{-1}(\overline{y})
.

Proof:

\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}
is the ML estimate if and only if
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y};\theta)\big|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}}=0
. The derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y};\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left\{\log h(\boldsymbol{y})+\eta(\theta)\overline{y}-A(\theta)\right\}

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}(\theta)\overline{y}-A^{\prime}(\theta).

Setting it to zero, the condition is equivalent to
\frac{A^{\prime}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})}{\eta^{\prime}(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}})}=\overline{y}
. Defining
g(\theta)=\frac{A^{\prime}(\theta)}{\eta^{\prime}(\theta)}
, it follows that
g(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y}))=\overline{y}
. If
g^{-1}
exists, then estimator is
\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y})=g^{-1}(\overline{Y})
.
∎

Lemma 5

For any distributions in the exponential family and for any parameter
\theta
,

\frac{A^{\prime}(\theta)}{\eta^{\prime}(\theta)}=\mathbb{E}[T(\boldsymbol{Y})]\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mu(\theta).

(44)

If
A^{\prime}(\theta)
and
\eta^{\prime}(\theta)
exist and are not zero, and
\mu^{-1}
exists, then
\theta=\mu^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[T(\boldsymbol{Y})])
.

Proof:

The function
A(\theta)
is the normalization constant, defined as
A(\theta)=\log\int h(\boldsymbol{y})e^{\eta(\theta)T(\boldsymbol{y})}d\boldsymbol{y}
. Therefore, its derivative is

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left\{\log\int h(\boldsymbol{y})e^{\eta(\theta)T(\boldsymbol{y})}d\boldsymbol{y}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int h(\boldsymbol{y})e^{\eta(\theta)T(\boldsymbol{y})}T(\boldsymbol{y})\eta^{\prime}(\theta)d\boldsymbol{y}}{\int h(\boldsymbol{y})e^{\eta(\theta)T(\boldsymbol{y})}\boldsymbol{y}}

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}(\theta)\int T(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot p_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{y};\theta)d\boldsymbol{y}=\eta^{\prime}(\theta)\mathbb{E}[T(\boldsymbol{Y})].

Thus, if
\mu^{-1}
exists, then
\theta=\mu^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[T(\boldsymbol{Y})])
.
∎

Comparing (
43
) and (
44
), it follows that the mapping is
g(\theta)=\mu(\theta)
. Therefore,
\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y}))=g(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y}))
. But since
g(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y}))=\overline{Y}

\mu(\widehat{\theta}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{Y}))=\overline{Y}

Proof of Theorem 2

Proof:

Since
\overline{Y}=(1/N)\sum_{n=1}^{N}Y_{n}
, the mean
\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]
. It then follows that the derivative
d\mu/d\theta
remains unchanged. For the variance, it is easy to show that
\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]=\mathrm{Var}[Y_{1}]/N
. Substituting these results into (
22
) would yield

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[\overline{Y}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}=\sqrt{N}\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Y_{1}]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}.

Therefore, it suffices to prove the SNR for
N=1
. In what remains, let
Y
be any of the random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
since they are i.i.d.

Recall the probability density function of
Y
:

\displaystyle p_{Y}(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{\theta^{y}}{y!}e^{-\theta},&\qquad y<L,\\
\sum_{k=L}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}e^{-\theta}=1-\Psi_{L}(\theta),&\qquad y\geq L,\end{cases}

where
\Psi_{L}(\theta)
is the incomplete Gamma function. The mean of
Y
can be shown as

\displaystyle\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}k\cdot\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}e^{-\theta}+L\cdot\left(\sum_{k=L}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}e^{-\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{\theta^{k}}{(k-1)!}e^{-\theta}+L\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\theta\sum_{k=0}^{L-2}\frac{\theta^{k-1}}{k!}e^{-\theta}+L\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta)).

The derivative
d\mu/d\theta
is therefore

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left\{\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))\right\}

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi_{L-1}^{\prime}(\theta)+\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)-L\cdot\Psi^{\prime}_{L}(\theta).

For the variance, since
\mathrm{Var}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]-\mu^{2}
, it remains to determine
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}k^{2}\cdot\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}e^{-\theta}+L^{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{k=L}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}e^{-\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}k\frac{\theta^{k}}{(k-1)!}e^{-\theta}+L^{2}\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}(k-1+1)\frac{\theta^{k}}{(k-1)!}e^{-\theta}+L^{2}\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{L-1}\frac{\theta^{k}}{(k-2)!}e^{-\theta}+\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{\theta^{k}}{(k-1)!}e^{-\theta}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+L^{2}\cdot(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta))

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi_{L-2}(\theta)+\theta\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)+L^{2}(1-\Psi_{L}(\theta)).

This completes the proof.
∎

VIII-D

Proof of Corollary
2

Proof:

When
L
is large,
\Psi_{L}(10^{\phi})
and
\Psi_{L-1}(10^{\phi})
are close enough that they can be considered approximately equal. Denote the value
\Psi_{L}(10^{\phi})
as
\Psi
. Then by (
24
) it holds that
\Psi\rightarrow 0
for
\phi>\log_{10}L
and
\Psi\rightarrow 1
for
\phi\leq\log_{10}L
. In either case, since
\Psi
is a constant, it follows that the derivative
\Psi^{\prime}_{L}(10^{\phi})=0
as long as
\phi>\log_{10}L
or
\phi<\log_{10}L
. Therefore, by denoting
\theta=10^{\phi}
, the two cases can be derived as follows.

When
\phi\leq\log_{10}L
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi+L(1-\Psi)=\theta,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y]

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi+\theta\Psi+L^{2}(1-\Psi)-\mu^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\cdot 1+\theta\cdot 1+L^{2}\cdot 0-\mu^{2}=\theta,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\Psi-(L-\theta)\Psi_{L-1}^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=1-(L-\theta)\cdot 0=1.

So, the SNR for
\phi\leq\log_{10}L
is

\displaystyle 20\log_{10}\text{SNR}_{\exp}(10^{\phi})=20\log_{10}\sqrt{10^{\phi}}=10\phi.

When
\phi>\log_{10}L
,
\Psi\rightarrow 0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi+L(1-\Psi)\approx\theta\Psi+L,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[Y]

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi+\theta\Psi+L^{2}(1-\Psi)-\mu^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi+\theta\Psi+L^{2}(1-\Psi)-(\theta\Psi+L)^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi+\theta\Psi+L^{2}-\theta^{2}\Psi^{2}-2\theta\Psi L-L^{2}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}\Psi+\theta\Psi-2\theta\Psi L

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi(\theta+1-2L),

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\theta\Psi_{L-1}^{\prime}(\theta)+\Psi_{L-1}(\theta)-L\Psi_{L}^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\theta\cdot 0+\Psi-L\cdot 0=\Psi.

By taking the limit that
\Psi\rightarrow 0
, it follow that

\displaystyle\lim_{\Psi\rightarrow 0}\text{SNR}_{\exp}(10^{\phi})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Psi\rightarrow 0}\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[Y]}}\cdot\frac{d\mu}{d\theta}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Psi\rightarrow 0}\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\theta\Psi(\theta+1-2L)}}\cdot\Psi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Psi\rightarrow 0}\frac{\sqrt{\theta\Psi}}{\sqrt{(\theta+1-2L)}}=0.

Combining with the case where
\phi\leq\log_{10}L
, the overall SNR is proved.
∎

VIII-E

MATLAB Code for Monte Carlo Simulation

The MATLAB code below illustrates the Monte Carlo simulation of how
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
is generated for a truncated Poisson distribution. Adding other factors to the forward model can be done by modifying the random variable
Y
.

N = 100000;

L = 10;

theta_set = logspace(-2,3,100);

mu = zeros(1,100);

sigma = zeros(1,100);

for i=1:100

theta = theta_set(i);

Theta = theta*ones(N,1);

Y = poissrnd(Theta);

Y(Y>L) = L;

mu(i) = mean(Y);

sigma(i) = std(Y);

end

dmu_dt = [diff(mu)./diff(theta_set) 1];

SNR = theta_set./sigma.*dmu_dt;

loglog(theta_set, SNR);

For plotting the theoretical
\text{SNR}_{\text{exp}}(\theta)
, one just needs to call the incomplete Gamma function.

theta = logspace(-2,3,100);

Psi = gammainc(theta,L,’upper’);

Psi1 = gammainc(theta,L-1,’upper’);

Psi2 = gammainc(theta,L-2,’upper’);

dPsi = -theta.^(L-1).*exp(-theta) ...

/gamma(L);

/gamma(L-1);

mu = theta.*Psi1 + L.*(1 - Psi);

sigma = sqrt(theta.^2 .* Psi2 + ...

theta.*Psi1 + L^2*(1-Psi) - ...

mu_theory.^2);
