Abstract
. Let 𝒞 d , n \mathcal{C}_{d,n} be the convex cone consisting of real n n -variate degree d d forms that are strictly positive on ℝ n ∖ { 𝟎 } \mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\} . We prove that the Lebesgue volume of the sublevel set { g ≤ 1 } \{g\leq 1\} of g ∈ 𝒞 d , n g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n} is a completely monotone function on 𝒞 d , n \mathcal{C}_{d,n} and investigate the related properties. Furthermore, we provide (partial) characterization of forms, whose sublevel sets have finite Lebesgue volume. Finally, we discover an interesting property of a centered Gaussian distribution, establishing a connection between the matrix of its degree d d moments and the quadratic form given by the inverse of its covariance matrix.

Let

denote the space of real
n
-variate forms (homogeneous polynomials) of degree
d
. The space
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
is endowed with
the Bombieri inner product

(1.1)

\displaystyle\langle g,h\rangle\ =\ \sum_{|\alpha|=d}{d\choose\alpha}^{-1}g_{\alpha}h_{\alpha},\quad{d\choose\alpha}=\frac{d\mathrm{!}}{\alpha_{1}\mathrm{!}\cdots\alpha_{n}\mathrm{!}},

where
g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{|\mathbf{\alpha}|=d}g_{\mathbf{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{\alpha}}
and
h(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{|\mathbf{\alpha}|=d}h_{\mathbf{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{\alpha}}
are two forms written in the basis of monomials of degree
d
.
For a form
g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
we consider its sublevel set

\mathbf{G}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(\mathbf{x})\leq 1\}.

We are interested in
the volume function

f
that to a given
g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
associates the Lebesgue volume
f(g)={\rm vol}\,(\mathbf{G})
of its sublevel set.
The volume of
\mathbf{G}
is infinite if
g
takes negative values.
Thus, it is natural to apply
f
only to polynomials that are non-negative on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We call a form
g

positive definite
(PD for short), if it is positive on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
. The sublevel set
\mathbf{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a PD form is compact and hence it has a finite Lebesgue volume.
In this regard, we are concerned with the open convex cone
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of PD forms.
We implicitly assume that the degree
d
is even, as only in this case
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is non-empty.
The closure
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
of
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
with respect to the norm topology on
(\mathcal{H}_{d,n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
consists of forms that are non-negative on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1.1.
Complete monotonicity of the volume function

The cone
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is a natural domain of definition of the volume function

(1.2)

\displaystyle f:\mathcal{C}_{d,n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ \mathbb{R},

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto\ {\rm vol}\,(\mathbf{G})\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{G}}\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},

which is strictly convex and admits the following integral representation

(1.3)

\displaystyle f(g)\ =\ {\rm vol}\,(\mathbf{G})\ =\ \frac{1}{\Gamma\left(1+n/d\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},

see
[
12
, Thm. 2.2]
.
In our first result we show that (
1.2
) is
completely monotone
,
that is, it is
the Laplace transform
of some Borel measure on the closed dual cone
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}=\{L\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{*}:L(g)\geq 0\ \forall g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\}
to
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
.
To state it, let us recall (from, e.g.,
[
5
, Thm. 19]
) that
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}
is the conic hull of the image of the
Veronese map

(1.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ \mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{*},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}

\displaystyle\mapsto\ [g\mapsto g(\boldsymbol{\ell})].

By
[
21
, Thm. 17.10]
,
\mathcal{C}^{\,*}_{d,n}
, also known as
the moment cone
, consists of
(truncated) moment functionals
, that is, such
L\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{*}
with
L(g)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}g(\mathbf{z})\,\textrm{d}\nu(\mathbf{z})
,
g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
, for some measure
\nu
supported on
the unit sphere

\mathbb{S}^{n-1}=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}=1\}
.
Then, an element
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}(\ell)
,
\ell\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, corresponds to
the Dirac measure
at
\ell
.
An alternative perspective on
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}
comes with the identification of
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{*}
and
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
via the inner product (
1.1
).
Under this identification, one has
g(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\big\langle\big(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\big)^{d},g\big\rangle
(see, e.g.,
[
21
, (19.6)]
) and, in particular, (
1.4
) sends a vector
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the
d
th power of a linear form,
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\big(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\big)^{d}\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{*}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 2
and any even
d\geq 2
the volume function (
1.2
) admits an integral representation

(1.5)

\displaystyle f(g)\ =\ \int_{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}}\exp(-\langle\theta,g\rangle)\,\frac{\textrm{{d}}\mu(\theta)}{\Gamma\left(1+n/d\right)},

where
\mu
is the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the Veronese map (
1.4
).
In particular,
f:\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is completely monotone and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

we have that

(1.6)

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}D_{v_{1}}\dots D_{v_{k}}f(g)\ \geq\ 0,\quad g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n},

where
D_{v}
denotes the directional derivative along
v\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Complete monotonicity of a differentiable function

Thanks to Bernstein-Hausdorff-Widder-Choquet theorem, recalled in Section

In

The results from

A particular focus in their study was given to elementary symmetric polynomials and to products of linear forms.

In physics one often considers exponential probability density functions of the form

It is in general difficult to find closed-form expressions for

(respectively, for (

In

For example, for

1.2.
Moment matrices of central Gaussian vectors and sums of squares

A form
g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
is called
a sum of squares
(or SOS), if
g(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}h_{j}(\mathbf{x})
for some forms
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\in\mathcal{H}_{d/2,n}
of degree
d/2
.
Equivalently, one can write
g
as

(1.8)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})\ =\ \mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\,G\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=d/2}
is the column-vector of monomials
1
1

1

From now on we fix an order on the set of monomials of a given degree.
of degree
d/2
and
G\in\textrm{PD}_{M}
is a positive semi-definite matrix of size
M\times M
,
M={d/2+n-1\choose n-1}
(called
a Gram matrix
of
g
).
Below by
\textrm{PD}_{M}
and
\textrm{PSD}_{M}
we denote the convex cone of positive definite, respectively positive semi-definite,
M\times M
real symmetric matrices.
By definition, sums of squares are non-negative, and, if
g
is given by (
1.8
) with a positive definite matrix
G
, then the form
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is also positive definite.

Remark 1.4
.

It is well-known that not every non-negative form is a sum of squares, see
[
19
]
. However, by a celebrated result of Artin
[
2
]
, after multiplying a non-negative form by a suitable sum of squares form, one obtains a sum of squares.
Moreover, by a result
[
18
, Thm. 3.12]
of Reznick, if a form
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is positive definite, there exists
s\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2s}g\in\mathcal{C}_{d+2s,n}
is a sum of squares, where
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
is the Euclidean norm of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In fact, by the same result,
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2s}g
is even a sum of
(d+2s)
-th powers of linear forms and hence
\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2s}g\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d+2s,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is identified with a point of the moment cone
\mathcal{C}_{d+2s,n}^{\,*}
.
Thus, sums of squares constitute an important subclass of the class of non-negative forms.
Furthermore, with the representation (
1.8
) for sums of squares, one can approach
polynomial optimization problems
via
semi-definite programming
, see
[
10
]
.

A centered multivariate normal distribution is defined by its covariance matrix
Q^{-1}
, with the probability density function given by

(1.9)

\displaystyle p^{\,}_{Q}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ \frac{\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y}}{2}\right),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The covariance matrix equals
the Hankel matrix
of moments of
p^{\,}_{Q}
of degree
2
, i.e.,

(1.10)

\displaystyle Q^{-1}\ =\ \mathbf{M}_{2}[Q]\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}p^{\,}_{Q}(\mathbf{y})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ =\ \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}y_{i}y_{j}p^{\,}_{Q}(\mathbf{y})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right).

Motivated by this property of Gaussian distributions, in

2.5

(1.11)

\displaystyle P_{G}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ \frac{\exp\left(-k\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\right)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-k\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\right)\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}},\quad\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

associated to a positive definite SOS form
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})G\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
,
G\in\textrm{PD}_{M}
, and
the Hankel-like matrix

\mathcal{M}_{d}[G]\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}P_{G}(\mathbf{y})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}

of degree
d
moments of (
1.11
), where
(2k)^{-1}={d/2+n-1\choose n}
. When
d=2

and (

(1.12)

\displaystyle G\in\textrm{PD}_{M}\ \mapsto\ ({\rm det}(G))^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-k\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\right)\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Theorem
1.5

Remark 1.6

By

Sums of squares of degree
d
form a closed convex subcone (called
the
SOS
cone
)
\Sigma_{d,n}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
of the cone of non-negative homogeneous polynomials. Its dual cone
\Sigma_{d,n}^{*}=\{L\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{d,n}\,:\,L(g)\geq 0\ \forall\,g\in\Sigma_{d,n}\}
contains
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}
and is known as
the pseudo-moment cone
.
For any
(truncated) pseudo-moment functional

L\in\Sigma_{d,n}^{*}
let

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{d}(L)\ =\ L\left(\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\ =\ \left(L(\mathbf{y}^{\alpha+\beta})\right)_{|\alpha|=|\beta|=d/2}

be the
M\times M

Hankel-like matrix
of pseudo-moments of degree
d
associated to
L
.
One has
\mathbf{M}_{d}(L)\in\textrm{PSD}_{M}
, as
\mathbf{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}(L)\mathbf{h}=L(\mathbf{h}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{h})=L(h^{2})\geq 0
holds for any form
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d/2,n}
whose coefficients in the basis of monomials form a column vector
\mathbf{h}=(h_{\alpha})_{|\alpha|=d/2}
.
Conversely, any matrix
\mathbf{M}_{d}\in\textrm{PSD}_{M}
that satisfies
(\mathbf{M}_{d})_{\alpha\beta}=(\mathbf{M}_{d})_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}
whenever
\alpha+\beta=\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}
,
|\alpha|=|\beta|=|\alpha^{\prime}|=|\beta^{\prime}|=d/2
, is of the form (
1.15
) for
L\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{*}
defined by
L(\mathbf{y}^{\alpha+\beta})=(\mathbf{M}_{d})_{\alpha\beta}
,
|\alpha|=|\beta|=d/2
.
Furthermore, for a linear functional
L\in\Sigma_{d,n}^{*}
that lies in the interior of the pseudo-moment cone, the associated pseudo-moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{d}(L)\in\textrm{PD}_{M}
is positive definite and hence invertible.
If
L\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}
is even a moment functional, that is,

(1.16)

\displaystyle L(g)\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}g(\mathbf{z})\,\textrm{d}\nu(\mathbf{z}),\quad g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n},

for some measure
\nu
supported on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, then the entries of the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{d}(L)
are moments of
\nu
of degree
d
.

By a result of Nesterov (see
[
16
, Thm.
2
]
), a SOS form

belongs to the interior of the cone
\Sigma_{d,n}
if and only if there is some linear functional

in the interior of the pseudo-moment cone such that
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{M}_{d}(L)^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
. Moreover,

1.3.
Comparison of

Given a positive definite form

\mu_{g}
the Lebesgue measure restricted to the sublevel set
\mathbf{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
g
.
Integrating
\mu_{g}
against any form
h\in\mathcal{H}_{e,n}
of degree
e
, one obtains by
[
12
, (2.1)]
that

\mu_{g}(h)\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{G}}h(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ =\ \frac{1}{\Gamma\left(1+(n+e)/d\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(\mathbf{x})\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

In this perspective,
\mu_{g}
can be identified with a measure with exponential density
\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We consider
L^{1}
- and
L^{2}
-norms associated to the measure
\mu_{g}
,

(1.17)

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{G}}|h(\mathbf{x})|\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}\ =\ \left(\int_{\mathbf{G}}|h(\mathbf{x})|^{2}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{1/2},

where
h
is any Lebesgue measurable function on
\mathbf{G}
.
We show that
g
minimizes the ratio of
L^{2}
- and
L^{1}
-norms over all non-zero
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
. Then

g\ =\ \arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}}\left\{\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}\,:\,\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}=\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}\right\}

or, equivalently,

\frac{\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}}{\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}}\ =\ \min\left\{\frac{\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}}{\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}}\,:\,h\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}\setminus\{0\}\right\}.

Moreover, up to a factor,
g
is the unique minimizer of these optimization problems.

In particular, the positive definite form
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
has the smallest
L^{2}(\mu_{g})
-norm among all degree
d
forms
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
that have the same
L^{1}(\mu_{g})
-norm as
g
.

Remark 1.10
.

The above result also holds when
g
and
h
are positively homogeneous functions of degree
d
with
g(\mathbf{x})
being positive for
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
, see
[
11
, Lemma 3]
and our proof of Theorem
1.9
in Section
4
.

1.4.
Non-negative forms with sublevel sets of finite volume

It turns out that, for some non-negative forms
g\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}=\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
in the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
, the sublevel set
\mathbf{G}
has finite Lebesgue volume
f(g)={\rm vol}\,(\mathbf{G})
, while it is not the case for all
g\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
for general
d
and
n
.
Motivated by this observation, we consider the set
\mathcal{V}_{d,n}
of forms
g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
with
f(g)<\infty
.
One naturally has
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{d,n}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
is a convex cone by
[
12
, Thm. 2.1]
.
It is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{V}_{2,n}=\mathcal{C}_{2,n}
, that is, only positive definite quadratic forms
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{2,n}}
have finite
f(g)
.
We give a complete characterization of binary forms in
\mathcal{V}_{d,2}
in terms of multiplicities of their real zeros.

Theorem 1.11
.

A non-negative binary form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,2}}
is in
\mathcal{V}_{d,2}
if and only if
g
has zeros of order at most
d/2-1
. In particular,
\mathcal{V}_{4,2}=\mathcal{C}_{4,2}
.

We also provide some sufficient conditions for membership in
\mathcal{V}_{d,n}
for
n>2
.
For this let us call a non-negative form
g\in\overline{C}_{d,n}

generic
, if it is
round at every its real zero
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
(see
[
6
, p. 47]
), that is, the Hessian matrix
\mathrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})\right)
is positive definite when restricted to the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{x}

(equivalently,
\mathrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g
is of corank one).
Thus, a non-negative (but not positive definite) quadratic form
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{x}
is generic if and only if the associated positive semi-definite matrix
G
is of corank one.
Also, for a non-negative binary form
g\in\overline{C_{d,2}}
the condition of being generic means that all real zeros of
g
are of order two.

Theorem 1.12
.

For
d\geq 4
,
n\geq 3
a generic non-negative form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
is in
\mathcal{V}_{d,n}
.

Theorems
1.11
and
1.12
are proven in Section
5
.
Now, we consider some examples of generic non-negative forms that lie on the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
.

Example 1
.

The
Motzkin form

(1.18)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})\ =\ x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}+x_{3}^{6}-3x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2},\quad\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),

was historically the first explicit example of a non-negative form that is not a sum of squares, see
[
19
]
. A point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is a zero of
g
if and only if
|x_{1}|=|x_{2}|=|x_{3}|
. Setting
x_{3}=1
in
g
gives
the Motzkin polynomial

\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})=y_{1}^{4}y_{2}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{4}+1-3y_{1}^{2}y_{2}^{2}
, whose real zeros are
(1,1)
,
(1,-1)
,
(-1,1)
,
(-1,-1)
. At any real zero
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
(say,
\mathbf{x}=(1,1,1)
) of
g
the Hessian
\textrm{{Hess}}_{\mathbf{x}}g
is positive semi-definite and has rank
2
and hence the Motzkin form
g\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{6,3}
is generic in the above sense.

As the following example shows, it is not difficult to find generic forms in
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
also for higher
n\geq 3
and even
d\geq 4
.

Example 2
.

Consider a sum of squares form

g(\mathbf{x})\ =\ x_{n}^{d-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{2}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{d},\quad\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n}).

Then a real zero
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
g
must satisfy
x_{1}=\dots=x_{n-1}=0
. At
\mathbf{x}=(0,\dots,0,1)
the Hessian of
g
has rank
n-1
and hence
g\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is generic.

By Theorem
1.12
, the sublevel sets of the Motzkin form (
1.18
) and of forms constructed in Example
2
are of finite Lebegue volume.

One might wonder, whether the term “generic” is in place in the above context.
The following remark motivates our choice for the terminology.

Remark 1.13
.

The boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of the cone of non-negative forms is a semialgebraic subset of codimension one.
In Proposition
5.4
we show that the set of non-generic forms is a semialgebraic subset of
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of codimension at least
2
and that it is nowhere dense in the boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
, endowed with the topology induced from
(\mathcal{H}_{d,n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

Structure of the paper

There are 4 sections, each corresponding to a subsection of the above introductory section. Thus, in the next section we prove complete monotonicity of the volume function (
1.2
) and discuss a generalization of this result as well as treat the case of sums of squares. In Section
3
we discuss moment matrices of centered Gaussian vectors and prove Theorem
1.5
and Corollary
1.8
. In Section
4
a proof of the extremal property from Theorem
1.9
is presented. And in the last section we characterize non-negative forms, whose sublevel sets have finite Lebesgue volume.

2.
Complete monotonicity of the volume function

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open convex cone and denote by
C^{*}

the dual cone
to
C
,

C^{*}\ =\ \left\{\mathbf{L}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)^{*}\,:\,\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{g})\geq 0\ \forall\ \mathbf{g}\in C\right\}.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f:C\to\mathbb{R}
is
completely monotone
, if it is
C^{\infty}
-differentiable and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and all vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{k}\in C

(2.1)

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}D_{\mathbf{v}_{1}}\cdots D_{\mathbf{v}_{k}}\,f(\mathbf{g})\,\geq\,0,\quad\mathbf{g}\in C,

where
D_{\mathbf{v}}
denotes the directional derivative along the vector
\mathbf{v}
.

The Bernstein-Hausdorff-Widder theorem
[
25
, Thm. 12a]
gives a characterization of completely
monotone functions in one variable, these are exactly Laplace transforms of Borel
measures on the positive reals. Choquet
[
4
, Thm. 10]
found a generalization of this result to convex cones in higher dimensional spaces.

Theorem 2.2

(Bernstein-Hausdorff-Widder-Choquet theorem)
.

A smooth function
f:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on an open convex cone
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is completely monotone if and only if it is
the Laplace transform
of a unique Borel measure
\mu
supported on
C^{*}
, that is,

(2.2)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{g})\ =\ \int_{C^{*}}\exp(-\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{g}))\,\textrm{{d}}\mu(\mathbf{g}).

For a non-negative form
h\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{e,n}}
of degree
e\geq 0
the function
f_{h}
defined in (
1.7
) admits an integral representation (see
[
11
, Thm. 1]
)

(2.3)

\displaystyle f_{h}(g)\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{G}}h(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ =\ \frac{1}{\Gamma(1+(n+e)/d)}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(\mathbf{x})\exp(-g)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}.

In particular, the Lebesgue volume of the sublevel set
\mathbf{G}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(\mathbf{x})\leq 1\}
of
g
is obtained with
h=1
as

(2.4)

\displaystyle f(g)\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{G}}\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ =\ \frac{1}{\Gamma(1+n/d)}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}.

Moreover,
f_{h}
is
C^{\infty}
-differentiable and its derivatives are expressed as follows.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
h\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{e,n}}
. Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
we have for
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}

(2.5)

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}D_{v_{1}}\dots D_{v_{k}}f_{h}(g)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\Gamma\left(1+k+\frac{n+e}{d}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+e}{d}\right)}\int_{\mathbf{G}}h(\mathbf{x})v_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots v_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+e}{d}\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(\mathbf{x})v_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots v_{k}(\mathbf{x})\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{x}.

Proof.

Since
g(\mathbf{x})
is positive for
\mathbf{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, its minimum
g_{\min}=\min_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}g(\mathbf{x})>0
over the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is positive.
By homogeneity,
g(\mathbf{x})\geq g_{\min}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{d}
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Therefore, derivatives of the integrand in (
2.3
) read

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}D_{v_{1}}\dots D_{v_{k}}\left(h(\mathbf{x})\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\right)\,=\,h(\mathbf{x})v_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots v_{k}(\mathbf{x})\exp(-g(\mathbf{x})),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since this is majorized by the function
h(\mathbf{x})|v_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots v_{k}(\mathbf{x})|\exp(-g_{\min}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{d})
, which is clearly integrable, dominated converge theorem implies that

(2.6)

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}D_{v_{1}}\dots D_{v_{k}}f_{h}(g)\,=\,\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+e}{d}\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(\mathbf{x})v_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots v_{k}(\mathbf{x})\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{x}.

For any multiindex
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
formula
[
11
, (20)]
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\exp(-g(\mathbf{x}))\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{x}\ =\ \Gamma(1+(n+e+|\alpha|)/d)\int_{\mathbf{G}}h(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{x}.

From multilinearity of (
2.6
) in
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
the remaining equality in (
2.5
) follows.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Proposition
2.3
implies that
f_{h}:\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and hence the volume function (
1.2
) are completely monotone. Indeed, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and positive definite forms
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
the derivative
(-1)^{k}D_{v_{1}}\dots D_{v_{k}}f_{h}(g)
is non-negative for all
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
by (
2.5
).
In fact, it is even strictly positive as one can see directly.

Next, we give an alternative proof of complete monotonicity of the volume function, that also delivers an integral representation predicted by Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

As already mentioned, complete monotonicity of the volume function (
1.2
) is guaranteed by Proposition
2.3
.

Denote by
\mu
the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the Veronese map (
1.4
), that is,
\mu(B)\ =\ \lambda(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}^{-1}(B))
for any Borel measurable subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then the definition (
1.4
) of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}
and
[
3
, Thm. 3.6.1]
give

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp(-g(\boldsymbol{\ell}))\,\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp(-\langle\theta_{\boldsymbol{\ell}},g\rangle)\,\textrm{d}\lambda(\boldsymbol{\ell})\ =\ \int_{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}}\exp(-\langle\theta,g\rangle)\,\textrm{d}\mu(\theta),

which combined with (
1.3
) yields (
1.5
).
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Analogously, when
h\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
has degree
d
, one can show that

f_{h}(g)\ =\ \int_{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}}\exp\left(-\langle\theta,g\rangle\right)\frac{\langle\theta,h\rangle\,\textrm{{d}}\mu(\theta)}{\Gamma(1+(n+d)/d)},

where
\mu
is the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the Veronese map (
1.4
).
Thus, the unique Borel measure from Theorem
2.2
, that is associated to the completely monotone function
f_{h}
is proportional to
\langle\cdot,h\rangle\mu
.

By the Leibniz rule for derivatives, the product of two completely monotone functions is completely monotone.
In particular, for any integer
s\in\mathbb{N}
the
s
-th power
f^{s}
of the volume function (
1.2
) is completely monotone.
Interestingly, in the setting of quadratic forms
(d=2)
,
f^{s}
is completely monotone for all sufficiently large (not necessarily integer) powers
s>>1
.

Proposition 2.6
.

The function
g\mapsto f(g)^{s}
on
\mathcal{C}_{2,n}
is completely monotone if and only if
s=0,1,2,\dots,n-2
or
s\geq n-1
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.10
below, we have that

f(g)^{s}\ =\ \left(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}^{n}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}\right)^{s}{\rm det}(G)^{-s/2},\quad g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{x},

where
G
is the positive definite matrix representing the quadratic form
g\in\mathcal{C}_{2,n}
.
The claim now directly follows from
[
22
, Theorem 1.3]
.
∎

By its construction, the representing measure
\mu
from Theorem
1.1
is supported on the set
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of
d
-th powers of linear forms, which is also known as (the real part of)
the Veronese cone
.
By
[
8
, Prop. 5.7]
, the representing measure of the power
f^{s}
of (
1.2
) is proportional to
the
s
-th convolution power
\mu^{*s}
of
\mu
,

\displaystyle f^{s}\ =\ \prod_{j=1}^{s}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\,*}_{d,n}}\exp(-\langle\theta,g\rangle)\,\frac{\textrm{d}\mu(\theta)}{\Gamma\left(1+n/d\right)}\ =\ \int_{\mathcal{C}^{\,*}_{d,n}}\exp(-\langle\theta,g\rangle)\,\frac{\textrm{d}\mu^{*s}(\theta)}{\Gamma\left(1+n/d\right)^{s}}.

The support of the measure
\mu^{*s}
is
the
s
-th Minkowski power of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\rm supp}\,(\mu^{*s})\,=\,\{\theta_{\boldsymbol{\ell}^{1}}+\dots+\theta_{\boldsymbol{\ell}^{s}}\,:\,\boldsymbol{\ell}^{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\ell}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

By Richter’s theorem, for
s={n-1+d\choose d}
the support (
2.7
) of
\mu^{*s}
fills the cone
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}^{\,*}
, see
[
20
, Satz 4]
and
[
5
, Thm. 19]
.
Actually, by
[
5
, Thm. 57]
, this already happens for some
s\leq{n-1+d\choose d}-n+1
.

2.1.
Volume function of sums of squares

Recall from (
1.8
) that a non-negative form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
is SOS if and only if it can be written as
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some positive semi-definite (Gram) matrix
G
. Moreover, if
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is positive definite, it has a positive definite Gram matrix
G\in\textrm{PD}_{M}
.
Given a non-negative form
h\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{e,n}}
, we consider a composed map

(2.8)

\displaystyle f_{h}^{\,\mathrm{sos}}:\mathrm{PD}_{M}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ (0,\infty),

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\mapsto\ f_{h}(\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\,G\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})),

where
f_{h}
is defined in (
1.7
).
When
h=1
, we call

(2.9)

\displaystyle f^{\,\mathrm{sos}}(G)\ =\ f_{1}^{\,\mathrm{sos}}(G)\ =\ f(\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\,G\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})),\quad G\in\mathrm{PD}_{M},

the
SOS
volume function
.
The map (
1.8
) is linear in
G
and therefore, by Proposition
2.3
, (
2.8
) (and hence also (
2.9
)) must be completely monotone on its domain
\textrm{PD}_{M}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

The function
f_{h}^{\,\mathrm{sos}}:\textrm{{PD}}_{M}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is completely monotone.

Proof.

The claim follows directly from Proposition
2.3
.
Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\in\mathrm{PD}_{M}
be any positive definite matrices. Then by the definition of a directional derivative,

(2.10)

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}D_{V_{1}}\dots D_{V_{k}}f_{h}^{\,\mathrm{sos}}(G)\ =\ (-1)^{k}D_{v_{1}}\dots D_{v_{k}}f_{h}(g)\ \geq 0,

where
g
is given by (
1.8
) and
v_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\,V_{j}\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is the positive definite form with Gram matrix
V_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,k
.
∎

Next, we describe an integral representation of (
2.9
), suggested by Theorem
2.2
.
For this we consider the evaluation of a sum of squares (
1.8
) at
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(2.11)

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\ell})\ =\ \mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{\mathsf{T}}\,G\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\ =\ \textrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{\mathsf{T}}\,G\right),

where
\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{\mathsf{T}}
is a
M\times M
rank-one positive semi-definite matrix.
This motivates us to study “the matrix analogue” of the Veronese map (
1.4
),

(2.12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{d,n}^{\,\textrm{sos}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ \textrm{PD}_{M}^{\,*},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}

\displaystyle\mapsto\ \mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{\mathsf{T}},

where
\textrm{PSD}_{M}=\textrm{PD}_{M}^{\,*}=\{\Theta\in\textrm{{Mat}}(M,\mathbb{R}):\textrm{Tr}(\Theta\,G)\geq 0\ \forall\ G\in\textrm{PD}_{M}\}
is the dual cone to
\textrm{PD}_{M}
, which coincides with the closure of
\textrm{PD}_{M}\subset\textrm{Mat}(M,\mathbb{R})
and consists of positive semi-definite matrices.
Here we identify the dual space
\textrm{Mat}(M,\mathbb{R})^{*}
and
\mathrm{Mat}(M,\mathbb{R})
with the help of
the trace inner product

\Theta,G\in\textrm{Mat}(M,\mathbb{R})\mapsto\textrm{Tr}(\Theta\,G)
.
The main result of this subsection is an analogue of (
1.5
) for SOS forms.

Theorem 2.8
.

The
SOS
volume function (
2.9
) admits an integral representation

(2.13)

\displaystyle f^{\,\textrm{{sos}}}(G)\ =\ \int_{\textrm{{PSD}}_{M}}\exp(-\textrm{{Tr}}(\Theta\,G))\,\frac{\textrm{{d}}\mu^{\textrm{{sos}}}(\Theta)}{\Gamma\left(1+n/d\right)},

where
\mu^{\textrm{{sos}}}
is the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under (
2.12
).

Proof.

Denote by
\mu^{\textrm{sos}}
the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the map (
2.12
).
Then formula (
2.11
) and
[
3
, Thm. 3.6.1]
give

(2.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp(-\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{\mathsf{T}}\,G\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\boldsymbol{\ell}))\,\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp(-\textrm{Tr}\left(\Theta_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\,G\right))\,\textrm{d}\lambda(\boldsymbol{\ell})

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\textrm{PSD}_{M}}\exp(-\textrm{Tr}\left(\Theta\,G\right))\,\textrm{d}\mu^{\textrm{sos}}(\Theta),

which combined with (
1.3
) and (
2.9
) yields (
2.13
).
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Analogously, when
h(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}H\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\in\Sigma_{d,n}
is a sum of squares of degree
d
, one can show that

(2.15)

\displaystyle f^{\,\mathrm{sos}}_{h}(g)\ =\ \int_{\textrm{{PSD}}_{M}}\exp\left(-\textrm{{Tr}}\left(\Theta\,G\right)\right)\frac{\textrm{{Tr}}\left(\Theta\,H\right)\,\textrm{{d}}\mu^{\textrm{{sos}}}(\Theta)}{\Gamma(1+(n+d)/d)},

where
\mu^{\textrm{{sos}}}
is the push-forward measure of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under (
2.12
).
Thus, the unique Borel measure from Theorem
2.2
, that is associated to the completely monotone function
f^{\,\mathrm{sos}}_{h}
is proportional to
\textrm{{Tr}}\left(\,\cdot\,H\right)\mu^{\textrm{{sos}}}
.

The case of quadratic (
d=2
) forms deserves a special attention.
In this case any
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
can be written as
g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{x}
for a unique positive definite matrix
G
(cf. (
1.8
)).
By (
1.3
) and elementary Gaussian integration, one obtains a closed form expression for the volume function of
g
(see also
[
15
, p. 38]
).

Proposition 2.10
.

For any
n\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle f^{\,\mathrm{sos}}(G)\,=\,f(g)\,=\,\frac{\sqrt{\pi}^{n}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\rm det}(G)}}.

Remark 2.11
.

Complete monotonicity of the function
G\mapsto({\rm det}(G))^{-1/2}
on the cone of positive definite matrices is a quite known fact, see
[
22
, pp. 355-356]
.

We conclude this chapter with an explicit formula for (
2.8
) in the case
d=2
.

Theorem 2.12
.

For any
n\geq 2
and
h(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}H\mathbf{x}
,
H\in\textrm{{PSD}}_{n}
, we have

(2.16)

\displaystyle f_{h}^{\,\mathrm{sos}}(G)\ =\ \frac{\sqrt{\pi}^{n}}{2\,\Gamma\left(1+(n+2)/2\right)}\frac{\textrm{{Tr}}(G^{-1}H)}{\sqrt{{\rm det}(G)}},\quad G\in\textrm{{PD}}_{n}.

Proof.

Spectral theorem applied to real symmetric matrices
G
and
H
yields decompositions
G=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
H=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{j}\,\mathbf{d}_{j}\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Here
\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{d}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\{\mathbf{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{d}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are orthonormal bases consisting of eigenvectors of
G
and, respectively,
H
, whose associated eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}>0
and, respectively,
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n}\geq 0
.
Performing an orthogonal change of variables
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}\mathbf{c}_{k}
in the last integral of (
2.3
), we write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}H\mathbf{x}\exp(-\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}G\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\eta_{j}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{j}\right)^{2}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}^{2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{j}\left(\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{n}y_{k}y_{\ell}\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{j}\mathbf{c}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j}\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}^{2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}^{2}\left(\mathbf{c}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j}\right)^{2}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}^{2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y},

where in the last step we use the fact that the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}y_{k}y_{\ell}\exp(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}^{2})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}
equals zero whenever
k\neq\ell
.
After one more change of variables
y_{i}=z_{i}/\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and elementary Gaussian integration, the above integral reads

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\eta_{j}}{\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}z_{i}^{2}\exp\left(-\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}\right)\,\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}}}\ =\ \frac{\sqrt{\pi}^{n}}{2\sqrt{{\rm det}(G)}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\eta_{j}}{\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j}\right)^{2}.

The inverse matrix of
G\in\textrm{PD}_{n}
is given by
G^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Using properties of the trace, we can rewrite the double sum in the last expression above as

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\eta_{j}}{\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j}\right)^{2}\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\eta_{j}}{\lambda_{i}}\textrm{Tr}\left((\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j})(\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}_{i})\right)\ =\ \sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\lambda^{-1}_{i}\eta_{j}\textrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{d}_{j}\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ \textrm{Tr}\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{j}\,\mathbf{d}_{j}\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right)\ =\ \textrm{Tr}\left(G^{-1}H\right).

By gathering all together and combining with (
2.3
) we obtain the claim (
2.16
).
∎

3.
Moment matrices of central Gaussian vectors and sums of squares

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem
1.5
and then derive Corollary
1.8
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let us consider the vector
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\sqrt{{d/2\choose\alpha}}\,\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\right)_{|\alpha|={d/2}}
of normalized monomials of degree
d/2
.
The advantage of working with the normalized monomials consists in the fact that they (unlike
\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}
,
|\alpha|=d/2
) form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{d/2,n}
with respect to the Bombieri inner product (
1.1
). It is a well-known fact (see, e.g.,
[
9
, p. 17]
) that (
1.1
) is invariant under the standard action of
O(n)
on
\mathcal{H}_{d/2,n}
by changes of variables.
Thus, an orthogonal change of variables
\mathbf{x}\mapsto a\mathbf{x}
,
a\in O(n)
, induces a change of basis in
\mathcal{H}_{d/2,n}
given by some “big” orthogonal matrix
A\in O(M)
,
M={d/2+n-1\choose n-1}
, that is,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a\mathbf{x})\ =\ A\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x}).

The entries of the “big” matrix
A\in O(M)
are some forms of degree
d/2
in the entries of the “small” matrix
a\in O(n)
.
The matrix

\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[Q]\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}p^{(d)}_{\,Q}(\mathbf{y})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ =\ \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sqrt{{d/2\choose\alpha}}\sqrt{{d/2\choose\beta}}\mathbf{y}^{\alpha+\beta}p^{(d)}_{\,Q}(\mathbf{y})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)

of normalized moments of degree
d
of
p_{\,Q}^{(d)}
is related to
\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]
via the formula

(3.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[Q]\ =\ S\,\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]\,S,

where

S\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}\ddots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\sqrt{{d/2\choose\alpha}}&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

is the diagonal matrix of square roots of multinomial coefficients.
We now observe that forms
\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
and
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
coincide.
Indeed, the relation (
3.2
) together with
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})=S\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
imply

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})S^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]^{-1}S^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x}).

Next, we show that it is enough to prove the claim (
1.14
) in the case when
Q
is diagonal.
For this, let us choose an orthogonal
a\in O(n)
so that the conjugate to
Q
matrix
a^{\mathsf{T}}Qa=\Lambda
is the diagonal matrix of its eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}>0
.
Using formula (
3.1
), we derive

(3.4)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}^{-1}[Q]\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}a\Lambda a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a\mathbf{z})\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm det}(\Lambda)}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda\mathbf{z}}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}\right)^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}A\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}A^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm det}(\Lambda)}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda\mathbf{z}}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}\right)^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}A\,\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[\Lambda]^{-1}A^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\ =\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[\Lambda]^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}),

where we use orthogonality of matrices
a\in O(n)
,
A\in O(N)
, linearity of the integral and the fact that
\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})=A^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
.
Therefore, (
1.14
) holds for
\Lambda
if and only if it holds for
Q=a\Lambda a^{\mathsf{T}}
, since by the last derivation and (
3.3
) we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{x}\right)^{d}\

\displaystyle=\ \left(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}a\Lambda a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right)^{d}\ =\ \left((a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})\right)^{d}\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{d}[\Lambda]^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\ \mathbf{m}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\Lambda]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(a^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}).

Now, we reduce proving the claim (
1.14
) for a diagonal matrix
Q=\Lambda
to proving it for the identity matrix
Q=\mathrm{Id}
.
Since the eigenvalues of
Q=\Lambda
are positive we can write them as
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}^{2}
for some
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Let us observe that, under the coordinate-wise multiplication
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto\mu*\mathbf{x}=(\mu_{1}x_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}x_{n})
by real numbers
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, the vector of monomials transforms as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mu*\mathbf{x})\ =\ \left(\mu^{\alpha}\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}\right)_{|\alpha|=d}\ =\ \mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mu)*\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\ =\ \mathcal{N}(\mu)\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathcal{N}(\mu)
is the diagonal matrix with numbers
\mu^{\alpha}
,
|\alpha|=d
, on its diagonal.
We relate the forms
\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
with
Q=\Lambda
and
Q=\mathrm{Id}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\Lambda]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\frac{\sqrt{{\rm det}(\Lambda)}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\mu*\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}(\mu*\mathbf{y})}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mu^{-1}*\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mu^{-1}*\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{N}(\mu^{-1})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{N}(\mu^{-1})\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{z}}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\frac{\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\right)^{-1}\hskip-8.5359pt\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{N}(\mu)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}\mathcal{N}(\mu)\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\,=\,\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mu*\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mu*\mathbf{x}),

where we perform a change of variables
\mathbf{z}=\mu*\mathbf{y}
, use linearity of the integral and the formula
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{-1})=\mathcal{N}(\mu)^{-1}
with
\mu^{-1}=(\mu_{1}^{-1},\dots,\mu_{n}^{-1})
.
This together with the formula
(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda\mathbf{x})^{d}=((\mu*\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}(\mu*\mathbf{x}))^{d}
imply that (
1.14
) holds for a diagonal matrix
Q=\Lambda
if and only if it holds for the identity matrix
Q=\mathrm{Id}
.

To prove (
1.14
) for
Q=\mathrm{Id}
, we first note that the form
\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})
is invariant under orthogonal changes of variables.
Indeed, this property follows from formula (
3.4
) with
Q=\Lambda=\mathrm{Id}
and (
3.3
), since (by definition)
a\,\mathrm{Id}\,a^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathrm{Id}
holds for all orthogonal matrices
a\in O(n)
. As an
O(n)
-invariant form of degree
d
is proportional to
(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{d/2}
(see, e.g.,
[
7
, Lemma
2.1
]
), we obtain

\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\ =\ c\,(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{d/2}

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We now integrate this equality against the measure
p^{(d)}_{\,\mathrm{Id}}(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}
. Using linearity of the integral and properties of the trace and performing elementary integration, we compute

\displaystyle{d/2+n-1\choose n-1}\

\displaystyle=\ \textrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]\right)\ =\ \sum_{|\alpha|=|\beta|=d/2}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}_{\alpha\beta}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{|\alpha|=|\beta|=d/2}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}_{\alpha\beta}\,\mathbf{x}^{\alpha+\beta}\,p^{(d)}_{\,\mathrm{Id}}(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}_{d}[\mathrm{Id}]^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\,p^{(d)}_{\,\mathrm{Id}}(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\ c\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{d/2}\,p^{(d)}_{\,\mathrm{Id}}(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\ c\,\sigma_{d}^{d}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x})^{d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}}{2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\ c\,\sigma_{d}^{d}\,\frac{{\rm vol}\,(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+n-1}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)\,\textrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\ c\,\frac{{d/2+n-1\choose n-1}}{\sqrt{2}^{d}\prod_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\left(\frac{n}{2}+i\right)}\sqrt{2}^{d}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d+n}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}\ =\ c{d/2+n-1\choose n-1},

where we use the formula
{\rm vol}\,(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})=2\sqrt{\pi}^{n}/\,\Gamma\left(n/2\right)
for the volume
of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
This derivation yields
c=1
and hence completes our proof of (
1.14
).

It remains to prove that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]\ =\ \frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp\left(-k(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{x})^{d/2}\right)\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-k(\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y})^{d/2}\right)\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}}

is the matrix of degree
d
moments of the Gaussian-like density (
1.11
), associated to the SOS form
g(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{x})^{d/2}
. Applying a change of variables
\mathbf{y}=\sqrt{2}\sigma_{d}\,\mathbf{z}
in the definition (
1.13
) of
\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]
and then using
[
11
, (20)]
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sigma_{d}^{n}\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{y})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y}}{2\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\sqrt{2}^{d}\sigma_{d}^{d}\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sqrt{\pi^{n}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp\left(-\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{z}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\sqrt{2}^{d}\sigma_{d}^{d}\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sqrt{\pi^{n}}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+d}{2}\right)\int_{\left\{\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{z}\leq 1\right\}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\sqrt{2}^{d}\sigma_{d}^{d}\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sqrt{\pi^{n}}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+d}{2}\right)\int_{\left\{(\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{z})^{d/2}\leq 1\right\}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{\sqrt{2}^{d}\sigma_{d}^{d}\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sqrt{\pi^{n}}}\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{n+d}{d}\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp\left(-(\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{z})^{d/2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{k^{1+\frac{n}{d}}\sqrt{2}^{d}\sigma_{d}^{d}\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{\sqrt{\pi^{n}}}\frac{d\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+d}{2}\right)}{2\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+d}{d}\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp\left(-k(\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y})^{d/2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y},

where in the last step we change variables
\mathbf{z}=k^{1/d}\mathbf{y}
. Now, using the definition of
\sigma_{d}
and formulas
k^{-1}=2{d/2+n-1\choose n}={d/2+n-1\choose n-1}d/n
,
{\rm vol}\,(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})=2\sqrt{\pi^{n}}/\Gamma(n/2)
, we simplify the constant in front of the last integral and write

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{k^{\frac{n}{d}}n\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}}{{\rm vol}\,(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\Gamma\left(\frac{n+d}{d}\right)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{m}_{d/2}(\mathbf{z})^{\mathsf{T}}\exp(-k(\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y})^{d/2})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

Performing a sequence of changes of variables and using basic properties of Gamma function, one can see that the mass of the measure
\exp(-k(\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y})^{d/2})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}
equals

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\exp\left(-k(\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y})^{d/2}\right)\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ =\ \frac{{\rm vol}\,(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\Gamma\left(\frac{n+d}{d}\right)}{k^{\frac{n}{d}}n\sqrt{{\rm det}(Q)}},

which together with (
3.7
) yields the claimed representation (
3.6
) for
\mathbf{M}_{d}[Q]
.∎

We conclude this section with a proof of Corollary
1.8
.

Proof of Corollary
1.8
.

Since the matrix
Q\in\textrm{PD}_{n}
is positive definite, we have that
\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{y}>0
for non-zero
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. We first perform spherical change of variables
\mathbf{y}=r\,\mathbf{z}
,
r\in(0,\infty)
,
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, in the integral (
1.13
) defining

4.
Comparison of
L^{1}
- and
L^{2}
-norms associated to a PD form

In this section we prove an extremal property of positive definite forms, that is stated in Theorem
1.9
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

For a positive definite form
g\in\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
and any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
one has by formula
(56)
in
[
11
, Lemma 3]
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{G}}|h(\mathbf{x})|g(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{n+d}{n+2d}\int_{\mathbf{G}}|h(\mathbf{x})|\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ =\ \frac{n+d}{n+2d}\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}

and in particular with
h=g
one obtains
\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}=\frac{n+d}{n+2d}\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}
.

Let now

be such that
\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}=\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}
. Then we derive

\displaystyle 0\

\displaystyle\leq\ \||h|-g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}\ =\ \|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}-2\int_{\mathbf{G}}|h(\mathbf{x})|g(\mathbf{x})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}

\displaystyle=\ \|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}-2\frac{n+d}{n+2d}\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}+\frac{n+d}{n+2d}\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}

\displaystyle=\ \|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}-\frac{n+d}{n+2d}\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mu_{g})}\ =\ \|h\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})}-\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu_{g})},

which yields the claim.
Moreover, if

or

.
∎

5.
Non-negative forms with sublevel sets of finite volume

In this section we prove Theorems
1.11
and
1.12
and start with some auxiliary discussion.
Let
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
be a non-negative form and consider the dehomogenized non-negative polynomial
\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ g(\mathbf{y},1)
, where
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
We consider the following change of variables on
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}\neq 0\}
,

\mathbf{x}\ =\ (x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n})\ =\ r(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1},1),\quad(\mathbf{y},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\right),

which allows us to write
g(\mathbf{x})=r^{d}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})
.
Then the sublevel set of
g
is expressed as

\mathbf{G}\ =\ \{g(\mathbf{x})\leq 1\}\ =\ \left\{(\mathbf{y},r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\right):-\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-1/d}\leq r\leq\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-1/d}\right\}

and, as a consequence, its Lebesgue volume equals

(5.1)

\displaystyle f(g)\ =\ \int_{\mathbf{G}}\textrm{d}\mathbf{x}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{-\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-1/d}}^{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-1/d}}|r|^{n-1}\textrm{d}r\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ =\ \frac{2}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{n/d}}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

As we show in the following lemma, the question about finiteness of
f(g)
has a “local nature”, at least for some class of non-negative forms
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}]
be a non-negative polynomial of degree
d
with only isolated real zeros
\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\dots\mathbf{y}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and whose degree
d
part is positive definite.
Then for any
\alpha>\frac{n-1}{d}
the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}
is finite if and only if integrals
\int_{U_{i}}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}
over some neighborhoods
U_{i}
of
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are finite.

Proof.

The only if direction is obvious, since
\int_{U}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}
for any open subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

Let us now consider such open neighborhoods
U_{i}
of
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, that satisfy
\int_{U_{i}}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}<\infty
and take a ball
B_{R}=\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:|\mathbf{y}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}^{2}<R^{2}\right\}
that contains
\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{(m)}
.
First, we show that the integral of
\tilde{g}^{-\alpha}
over the complement of
B_{R}
is finite for large
R>0
.
By our assumption, the degree
d
homogeneous part
\tilde{g}_{d}
of
\tilde{g}=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\tilde{g}_{j}
is a positive definite form and hence
\tilde{g}_{d}(\mathbf{y})\geq\varepsilon_{d}|\mathbf{y}|^{d}
holds for some
\varepsilon_{d}>0
.
For
j=0,1,\dots,d-1
the degree
j
homogeneous part
\tilde{g}_{j}
of
\tilde{g}
satisfies
|\tilde{g}_{j}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\varepsilon_{j}|\mathbf{y}|^{j}
for some
\varepsilon_{j}>0
.
2
2

2

The constant
\varepsilon_{j}
,
j=0,1,\dots,d-1
, can be chosen to be the maximum value of the restriction of
|\tilde{g}_{j}|
to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and
\varepsilon_{d}
is a positive constant not greater than the minimum of
\tilde{g}_{d}
over
\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
.
We put all these inequalities together and obtain

\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})\ =\ \tilde{g}_{d}(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\tilde{g}_{j}(\mathbf{y})\ \geq\ \tilde{g}_{d}(\mathbf{y})-\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|\tilde{g}_{j}(\mathbf{y})|\ \geq\varepsilon_{d}|\mathbf{y}|^{d}-\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\varepsilon_{j}|\mathbf{y}|^{j},\quad\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

For sufficiently large
R>0
and all points
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with
|\mathbf{y}|\geq R
the rightmost expression in the last formula can be estimated as

(5.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})\ \geq\ \varepsilon_{d}|\mathbf{y}|^{d}-\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\varepsilon_{j}|\mathbf{y}|^{j}\ \geq\ \varepsilon|\mathbf{y}|^{d}

for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Using (
5.2
) and writing
\mathbf{y}=r\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\setminus B_{R}
,
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
,
r\geq R
, in spherical coordinates, we estimate the integral,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{\alpha}}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}\ \leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\alpha}|\mathbf{y}|^{d\alpha}}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ =\ \frac{\textrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^{n-2})}{\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{d\alpha-n+2}}\,\textrm{d}r<\infty,

where convergence of the last integral follows from the condition
\alpha>(n-1)/d
.
Since the function
\tilde{g}^{-\alpha}
is bounded over the compact set
K=\overline{B_{R}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}U_{i}\right)}
, the integral
\int_{K}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}
is finite.
This, together with (
5.3
) and finiteness of integrals
\int_{U_{i}}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{-\alpha}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{y}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, finally yield

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{\alpha}}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}\ \leq\ \int_{K}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{\alpha}}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{\alpha}}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{U_{i}}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})^{\alpha}}\,\textrm{{d}}\mathbf{y}\ <\ \infty.

∎

Remark 5.2
.

For any
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
the form
g(\mathbf{y},0)
is the degree
d
part of the (inhomogeneous) polynomial
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}]
defined by
\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y},1)
.
Therefore, the degree
d
part of
\tilde{g}
is positive definite if and only if a non-negative form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
has no zeros in
\{(\mathbf{y},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}\}
.

For a non-negative binary form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,2}}
, the non-negative univariate polynomial
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y]
has only isolated real zeros, each of which is of even order. Moreover,
\tilde{g}
is of degree
d
if and only if
g
does not vanish at
(1,0)
. This can be achieved by composing
g
with a rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Under such an operation the sublevel set
\mathbf{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
g
gets rotated and, in particular, its Lebesgue volume remains preserved.
Thus, by (
5.1
) and Lemma
5.1
, finiteness of
f(g)={\rm vol}\,(\mathbf{G})
can be decided by looking at the integrals of
\tilde{g}^{-n/d}
over small neighborhoods of real zeros of
\tilde{g}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

Let us consider a non-negative binary form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,2}}
.
By Remark
5.2
and the above discussion, without loss of generality we can assume that
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y]
satisfies assumptions of Lemma
5.1
.

By (
5.1
) the volume of
\mathbf{G}=\{g\leq 1\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is proportional to the one-dimensional integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{g}(y)^{-2/d}\,\textrm{d}y
, where
\tilde{g}(y)=g(y,1)
is the dehomogenization of
g
.
If
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
is a real zero of
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y]
of order
k
, it is possible to write
\tilde{g}(y)=(y-y^{\prime})^{k}h(y)
, where
h\in\mathbb{R}[y]
is a polynomial that does not vanish at
y^{\prime}
.
Performing an affine change of variables
y=z+y^{\prime}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}
, we are left with the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}z^{-2k/d}\tilde{h}(z)^{-2/d}\,\textrm{d}z
, where
\tilde{h}(z)=h(z+y^{\prime})
.
For a small
\delta>0
, the function
\tilde{h}^{-2/d}
is bounded (from below and from above by some positive constants) over the interval
(-\delta,\delta)
.
Thus,
\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}z^{-2k/d}\tilde{h}(z)^{-2/d}\,\textrm{d}z<\infty
if and only if
\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}z^{-2k/d}\,\textrm{d}z<\infty
. The latter condition is in turn equivalent to
2k/d<1
, which means that the order
k
of the zero
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
is smaller than
d/2
. Since the argument applies to an arbitrary real zero of
\tilde{g}
, the main claim follows from Lemma
5.1
.

For
d=4
the above condition on the order of real zeros reads
k<2
. Since the order of a real zero of a non-negative binary form is always an even number, only positive definite forms
g
in
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{4,2}}
have finite
f(g)
and hence
\mathcal{V}_{4,2}=\mathcal{C}_{4,2}
.
∎

If
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
is a generic non-negative form, the polynomial
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
can have only isolated real zeros.
In fact, a stronger result holds.

Lemma 5.3
.

The restriction of a generic non-negative form
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has only finitely many zeros.
In particular,
\tilde{g}
has finitely many zeros in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and, after possibly an orthogonal change of variables,
g
has no zeros in
\{(\mathbf{y},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathbf{y}\neq 0\}
.

Proof.

If
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
is a generic form and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is a zero of
g
, then for all unit vectors
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
that are orthogonal to
\mathbf{x}
, the Hessian satisfies

\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g\,\mathbf{v}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})v_{i}v_{j}\geq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
. Since a real zero
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of a non-negative form
g
must also be its singular point, that is,
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x})=0
,
i=1,\dots,n
, we have

g(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{v}+o(t))\ =\ t^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})\,v_{i}v_{j}+o(t^{3})\ \geq\ t^{2}(\varepsilon+o(t))

for points
\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{v}+o(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
In particular, for a sufficiently small
t>0
and any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{x}
we have
g(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{v}+o(t))>0
and hence the zero
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is isolated.
By compactness of the sphere,
g
can have only finitely many isolated zeros in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Injectivity of the mapping

\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\mapsto\frac{(\mathbf{y},1)}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{y}|^{2}+1}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

together with the homogeneity of
g
implies that
\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})=g(\mathbf{y},1)=0
holds for only finitely many
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
Finally, after possibly an orthogonal change of variables, the last coordinate of any real zero
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
is non-zero.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.12
.

Proof of Theorem
1.12
.

Let
g\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{d,n}}
be a generic non-negative form. This means in particular that for any real zero of
g
of the form
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{y},1)
the kernel of the positive semi-definite
n\times n
matrix
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})\right)
is one-dimensional and spanned by
\mathbf{x}
.
Note that
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is a zero of
\tilde{g}
and the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
Hessian matrix
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{g}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{g}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(\mathbf{y})\right)
is positive definite.
Indeed, because
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1}]
is non-negative, the matrix
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{g}
must be at least positive semi-definite.
If
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is a vector in the kernel of
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{g}
, then
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{u},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
must be in the kernel of
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g
, because

\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g\,\mathbf{w}\ =\ \mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{g}\,\mathbf{u}\ =\ \boldsymbol{0}

and both Hessian matrices are positive semi-definite.
But then
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{u},0)
has to be proportional to
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{y},1)
and hence
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
.
Positive definitedness of
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{g}
means in particular that

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{g}\,\mathbf{v}\ =\ \sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{g}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(\mathbf{y})v_{i}v_{j}\ \geq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
and all unit vectors
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

Now, by Lemma
5.3
and Remark
5.2
, we can assume that the non-negative polynomial
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]
satisfies assumptions of Lemma
5.1
.
Thus, it is enough to prove that the integral
\int_{U}\tilde{g}(\mathbf{z})^{-n/d}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}
over some neighborhood of
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
converges.
Take
U=B(\mathbf{y},\delta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
to be the open ball of radius
\delta>0
centered at
\mathbf{y}
.
Since the real zero
\mathbf{y}
of the non-negative polynomial
\tilde{g}
is also its singular point, that is,
\frac{\partial\tilde{g}}{\partial y_{i}}(\mathbf{y})=0
,
i=1,\dots,n-1
, the Taylor expansion of
\tilde{g}
at the point of the ball
\mathbf{y}+t\mathbf{v}
,
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
,

\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y}+t\mathbf{v})\ =\ t^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{g}}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(\mathbf{y})v_{i}v_{j}+o(t^{3})\ \geq\ t^{2}(\varepsilon+o(t)),

where we invoke (

For a sufficiently small

This leads to an estimate

\displaystyle\int_{B(\mathbf{y},\delta)}\frac{1}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{z})^{n/d}}\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}\

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{t^{n-2}}{\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y}+t\mathbf{v})^{n/d}}\,\textrm{d}t\,\textrm{d}\mathbb{S}^{n-2}(\mathbf{v})

and

.

5.1.
On the denseness of generic forms in the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}

We conclude this section with a proposition, that justifies the term “generic”.
Note that our proof of this result exploits facts from classical algebraic geometry.

Proposition 5.4
.

Non-generic non-negative forms in
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
form a semialgebraic subset of
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of codimension at least
2
. Moreover, this set is nowhere dense in
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
, endowed with the topology induced from
(\mathcal{H}_{d,n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

Proof.

Throughout this proof,
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{H}_{d,n}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
denotes the space of complex
n
-variate forms of degree
d
.
Those forms that are singular at some point of
the complex projective
(n-1)
-space
\mathbb{C}\textrm{{P}}^{n-1}
form an irreducible algebraic hypersurface

D_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\ =\ \left\{g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\,:\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=\dots=\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{n}}(\mathbf{x})=0\ \textrm{for some}\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}\textrm{P}^{n-1}\right\},

that can be retrieved as the Zariski closure of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
of the cone of non-negative forms, see
[
17
, Thm. 4.1]
.
Let us consider the set

(5.5)

X_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\ =\ \left\{g\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\ \large\colon\ \begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{1}}(\mathbf{x})=\dots=\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{n}}(\mathbf{x})=0\ \textrm{and}\\
&\textrm{rank}\left(\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}(g)\right)\leq n-2\ \textrm{for some}\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}\textrm{P}^{n-1}\end{aligned}\right\}

of forms with a singular point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}\textrm{P}^{n-1}
, at which the Hessian matrix
\textrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}(g)=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(\mathbf{x})\right)
has corank at least
2
.
Note that
X_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
is obtained by projecting to the first coordinate the algebraic variety of pairs
(g,\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathbb{C}\textrm{P}^{n-1}
satisfying conditions in (
5.5
).
Thus, the set
X_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
is algebraic by
[
23
, Thm. 1.11]
.

Let
g(\mathbf{x})=x_{n}^{d-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{2}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{d}
be a degree
d
form that we considered in Example
2
.
Apart from the unique real zero
(0:\dots:0:1)
, the form
g
(in general) has other singular points in
\mathbb{C}\textrm{P}^{n-1}
.
All these points are described by the equations

(5.6)

\displaystyle x_{1}(x_{1}^{d-2}+x_{n}^{d-2})\ =\ 0,\ \dots,\ x_{n-1}(x_{n-1}^{d-2}+x_{n}^{d-2})\ =\ 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}\ =\ 0.

One verifies directly that the Hessian matrix
\mathrm{Hess}_{\mathbf{x}}g
at any solution
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
of (
5.6
) has corank one.
This implies that
g\in D_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\setminus X_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
.
Since the variety
X_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
is properly (as just shown) contained in the irreducible hypersurface
D_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
, its codimension in
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}
must be at least 2.
In particular, the real part
X_{d,n}:=X_{d,n}^{\mathbb{C}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
is of (real) codimension at least 2 in
\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
.
By definition, a non-negative form
g\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is non-generic if and only if it is in
X_{d,n}
.
Thus, the semialgebraic subset
X_{d,n}\cap\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
of non-generic forms has codimension at least 2.

To prove the second claim, let
U\subset\mathcal{H}_{d,n}
be any open subset with
U\cap\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}\neq\varnothing
.
By
[
24
, Rem. 2.4]
and the proof of
[
24
, Lemma 2.5]
, the open subset
U\cap\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
of the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
has codimension
1
.
Since the set
U\cap(X_{d,n}\cap\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n})
is of codimension at least
2
, it is not dense in
U\cap\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
and hence
X_{d,n}\cap\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
is nowhere dense in
\partial\mathcal{C}_{d,n}
.
∎
