From multivalued to Boolean functions: preservation of soft nested canalization
Élisabeth Remy, Paul Ruet

Abstract
Nested canalization (NC) is a property of Boolean functions which has been recently extended to multivalued functions. We study the effect of the Van Ham mapping (from multivalued to Boolean functions) on this property. We introduce the class of softly nested canalizing (SNC) multivalued functions, and prove that the Van Ham mapping sends SNC multivalued functions to NC Boolean functions. Since NC multivalued functions are SNC, this preservation property holds for NC multivalued functions as well. We also study the relevance of SNC functions in the context of gene regulatory network modelling.



1
Introduction

Dynamical properties of biological systems, such as stability and robustness, are often associated with the canalizing property. This notion is at the basis of the work of Waddington in embryology, which describes, in the 1940s, epigenetic landscapes representing embryogenesis by canalizing configurations
[
27
]
. In the 1990’s, S. Kauffman introduced the class of canalizing Boolean functions
[
5
,
6
]
to formalize the canalizing behaviour observed in gene regulatory networks. In short, canalizing Boolean functions are functions
f:(\mathbb{Z}/{2\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/{2\mathbb{Z}}
(or
\mathbb{R}
) such that at least one input variable, say
x_{i}
(
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
), has a value
a=0
or
1
which determines the value of
f(x)
. Nested canalizing (NC) functions provide a “recursive” version of canalizing functions: an NC function
f
is canalizing and, moreover, its restriction
f\mathpunct{\mathpunct{\restriction}}_{x_{i}\neq a}
is itself NC.
NC functions are particularly interesting because of their “low complexity”: in particular, their average sensitivity (a measure of complexity related to spectral concentration, learning properties, decision tree complexity
[
14
]
and stability properties
[
5
,
7
]
) is bounded above by a constant
[
9
,
8
]
.

A natural question is the extension of this canalizing property to multivalued functions, involving more than two expression levels, which are often needed in the modeling of biological systems to circumvent the too restrictive Boolean representation
[
24
]
. The present paper investigates the property of nested canalization for multivalued functions
f:(\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}}
or
\mathbb{R}
for some
k\geqslant 2
. A definition of nested canalizing (NC) multivalued function has been proposed in
[
11
,
12
]
. In
[
17
]
, we introduced a more general class of multivalued functions, which we called weakly nested canalizing (WNC), for which we proved that the average sensitivity is bounded above by a constant.

Multivalued modelling allows to distinguish different thresholds of action of a gene on its targets, and is of course more complex to analyse than the Boolean case. Boolean mappings have been proposed, associating one Boolean variable to each threshold. In this way, the various actions of the multivalued component in the network are deployed, each represented by a Boolean variable. The behavior of the Boolean system thus obtained can be related to that of the initial multivalued model. This gives access to a large number of theoretical works and numerical tools specific to the analysis of Boolean models. Thus we are interested in the relationship between the notions of canalization for multivalued and Boolean functions. Clearly an NC Boolean function is NC multivalued, but what can we say about the other direction? More precisely, a mapping
\beta
from multivalued functions to Boolean functions has been defined by Van Ham in
[
26
]
, and it has been proved in
[
2
,
25
]
to be in some sense unique (it is the only injective such mapping preserving neighbours in the state space, and for which the regulatory graphs are unchanged).

We prove here that
\beta
maps NC multivalued functions to NC Boolean functions. To this end, we introduce in Section
2
the notion of softly nested canalizing (SNC) multivalued functions, and prove that NC
\Rightarrow
SNC
\Rightarrow
WNC (Proposition
2
and Section
2.3
). Then we actually prove in Theorem
3
that SNC functions are mapped by
\beta
to NC Boolean functions. On the other hand, we show that Theorem
3
does not extend to WNC functions: we shall see in Section
3.5
an example of a WNC function which is mapped by
\beta
to a non NC Boolean function.

NC functions appear as appropriate rules in Boolean models of gene regulatory networks. Indeed, Boolean networks have been proposed as models for gene regulatory networks
[
23
]
, with different nodes corresponding to different genes. The activity of a gene is regulated by the activity of other genes to which it is connected through a Boolean function. These networks are built from biological data and knowledge from the literature, and their associated Boolean functions are far from random. It turns out that NC functions are predominant in the large databases of Boolean gene networks
[
22
]
. In Section
4
we extract the multivalued genes from this database, and we study the canalizing properties of their multivalued functions and their corresponding Boolean functions.

Nested canalizing multivalued functions

The concept of nested canalization, originally considered for Boolean functions
[
5
]
, has been extended to multivalued functions in
[
11
,
12
,
4
]
. We first recall here this definition, before introducing soft nested canalization in Section

.

Let
k,n
be positive integers,
k\geqslant 2
.
\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}}
is the ring of integers modulo

A function

is said to be
canalizing with respect to coordinate

if there exists a function

different from the constant

such that

f(x)=\begin{cases}b&\text{if }x_{i}=a\\
g(x)&\text{if }x_{i}\neq a.\end{cases}

We shall simply say that
f
is
canalizing
if it is canalizing with respect to some
i,a,b
.

A

is a (nonempty, strict) subset of

of the form

or
\{i,\ldots,k-1\}
, with
0\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a permutation,
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be segments, and

be such that

. Then

is said to be
nested canalizing (NC) with respect to

,

f(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1}&\text{if }x_{\sigma(1)}\in A_{1}\\
c_{2}&\text{if }x_{\sigma(1)}\notin A_{1},x_{\sigma(2)}\in A_{2}\\
\;\vdots&\quad\vdots\\
c_{n}&\text{if }x_{\sigma(1)}\notin A_{1},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n-1)}\notin A_{n-1},x_{\sigma(n)}\in A_{n}\\
c_{n+1}&\text{if }x_{\sigma(1)}\notin A_{1},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n-1)}\notin A_{n-1},x_{\sigma(n)}\notin A_{n}.\end{cases}

We shall simply say that
f
is
NC
if it is NC with respect to some
\sigma
,
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
,
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n+1}
.

In Sections
3
and
4
, we shall be interested in multivalued networks. These networks are represented by functions
F:(\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rightarrow(\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}})^{n}

and each

gives the target value of gene number

. We shall say that such a network

is

if all functions

are.

Note that the segment

plays the role of a threshold for the variable

. For an NC multivalued function

as defined in
[

,
12
,
4
]
, each variable

is used exactly once in the process of canalization (and the value of

is determined by the threshold corresponding to

). It is possible to relax the definition by allowing canalization along successive thresholds for a same variable. This is essentially what we shall do in defining softly nested canalizing functions below. In doing so, we shall also remove the condition
c_{n}\neq c_{n+1}
.

2.1
Softly nested canalizing multivalued functions

Let
n
be a positive integer. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
k_{i}>0
,
\Omega_{i}
be a set of
k_{i}
integers,
\Omega=\prod_{i}\Omega_{i}
, and

. Note that we do not require

for all

. If
k_{j}=1
for some

,

could be viewed as a function with one less variable,

\prod_{i\neq j}\Omega_{i}
, but we still consider it as a function defined on
\prod_{i}\Omega_{i}

We shall say that

is
softly canalizing with respect to coordinate

and
(a,b)\in\Omega_{i}\times\mathbb{R}
if

whenever

, and simply that it is
softly canalizing
if it is softly canalizing with respect to some
i,a,b
. We shall also say that
F:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is
SNC
if all functions
F_{i}:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega_{i}
are.

Note that this definition differs slightly from the usual definition by the absence of condition on the values of

and

. In particular, constant functions are softly canalizing, though not canalizing.

If

is canalizing with respect to

and

, we shall consider

f\mathpunct{\mathpunct{\restriction}}_{x_{i}\neq a}:\Omega\cap\{x\mid x_{i}\neq a\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

the restriction of

to the set of

.

The class of softly nested canalizing on
\Omega=\prod_{i}\Omega_{i}
is then defined by induction on the cardinality

of
\Omega
. If
|\Omega|=1
,
i.e.

for all

, any

is
softly nested canalizing (SNC) on
\Omega
. If
|\Omega|>1
,

is
SNC on
\Omega
if there exist

such that

•

k_{i}\geqslant 2
,

•

is either the min or the max of
\Omega_{i}
,

•

is softly canalizing with respect to

,

•

is SNC on

, a strict subset of

.

Intuitively, a function

.

Letting

, numbers

and

for each

such that:

f(x)=\begin{cases}b_{1}&\text{if }x_{v(1)}=a_{1}\\
b_{2}&\text{if }x_{v(1)}\neq a_{1},x_{v(2)}=a_{2}\\
\;\vdots&\quad\vdots\\
b_{K}&\text{if }x_{v(1)}\neq a_{1},\ldots,x_{v(K-1)}\neq a_{K-1},x_{v(K)}=a_{K}\\
\end{cases}

and for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
\Omega^{\prime}_{v(i)}=\Omega_{v(i)}\setminus\{a_{j}\ |\ j<i,v(i)=v(j)\}
is not a singleton and

is either the min or the max of
\Omega^{\prime}_{v(i)}
.

In decomposing an NC function

is considered exactly once (in some order prescribed by a permutation

) and the value of

in some segment

. This can be realized by successively fixing the value of

for each

, and therefore, the class of SNC functions contains the class of NC functions, as stated in the following Proposition:

Proposition 2
.

If
f:(\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}}
is NC, then it is SNC.

Proof.

Assume

is NC with respect to
\sigma
,
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
,
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n+1}
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha_{i}^{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i}^{|A_{i}|}\}

\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}})\setminus A_{i}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha_{i}^{1+|A_{i}|},\ldots,\alpha_{i}^{k-1}\}.

More precisely, the
K=n(k-1)

are defined according to the following ordering which depends on the nature of the segment

:

•

either

, and then we choose the numbers

so that
0=\alpha_{i}^{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{i}^{|A_{i}|}

k-1=\alpha_{i}^{1+|A_{i}|}>\cdots>\alpha_{i}^{k-1}

•

or
k-1\in A_{i}
, and we choose the

If both

Then finally, a function

Some examples

•

We have noticed in Section

•

An easy induction on

However, they are not NC

]

(

•

For the same reason, the identity from

to

is SNC but not NC. As we shall see in Section

\bm{\mathsf{Dsor}}

\bm{\mathsf{Drk}}
,

•

In Section

2.3
WNC multivalued functions and average sensitivity

It is possible to consider a slightly more general notion of multivalued canalization, by removing the condition on canalizing values in the definition of SNC functions. This is what we do in
[
17
]
:
weakly nested canalizing (WNC) functions

f:(\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}})^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/{k\mathbb{Z}}
are defined like SNC functions, but the values

used to define

for

need not be extreme values (initially

or

), they can be intermediate values:

.

By Proposition
2
, NC
\Rightarrow
SNC
\Rightarrow

[
17
]
that WNC (hence NC and SNC) multivalued functions have “low complexity” in the sense that their

(see
[
14
, Chapter 8]
) is bounded above by a constant (independent of

), while the average sensitivity of an arbitrary multivalued function is of order

.

It is worth noticing that the above implications are strict. For instance, the function

defined by the following table:

\hline\cr 0&2&0&0\\

(and determines

), then the second canalization takes

(determining

) or

(determining

), and finally

or

.

Relation between
\beta
and nested canalization

Theorem 3
.

If
F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}):\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
is SNC, then
F^{\beta}:\beta(\Omega)\rightarrow\beta(\Omega)
is NC.

Proof.

As in the definition of
\beta
, we shall use double indices for Boolean functions, and let

\displaystyle F^{\beta}

\displaystyle=(F^{\beta}_{1,1},\ldots,F^{\beta}_{1,k_{1}-1},\ldots,F^{\beta}_{n,1},\ldots,F^{\beta}_{n,k_{n}-1})

\displaystyle=(F^{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\ldots,F^{\beta}_{n}),

where
F^{\beta}_{j,a}=\beta_{j,a}\circ F:\beta(\Omega)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and

F^{\beta}_{j}=\beta_{j}\circ F=(F^{\beta}_{j,1},\ldots,F^{\beta}_{j,k_{j}-1}):\beta(\Omega)\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{k_{j}-1}.

Therefore
F^{\beta}_{j}(\beta(x))=1^{F_{j}(x)}0^{k_{j}-1-F_{j}(x)}
and
F^{\beta}_{j,a}(\beta(x))=1\Leftrightarrow F_{j}(x)\geqslant a
.

Let us fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
1\leqslant a\leqslant k_{j}-1
and prove that
F^{\beta}_{j,a}
is NC.

By assumption,
F_{j}:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega_{j}
is SNC, hence by Proposition
1
, there exist a function
v:\{1,\ldots,K\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,n\}
, with
K=\sum_{i}k_{i}-n
, and numbers
a_{i}\in\Omega_{v(i)}
and
b_{i}\in\Omega_{j}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
such that, for all
x\in\Omega
:

F_{j}(x)=\begin{cases}b_{1}&\text{if }x\in\Omega^{(1)}\text{ and }x_{v(1)}=a_{1}\\
b_{2}&\text{if }x\in\Omega^{(2)}\text{ and }x_{v(2)}=a_{2}\\
\;\vdots&\quad\vdots\\
b_{K}&\text{if }x\in\Omega^{(K)}\text{ and }x_{v(K)}=a_{K},\end{cases}

where for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
\Omega^{(i)}\subseteq\Omega
is the set of
x
such that

x_{v(1)}\neq a_{1},\ldots,x_{v(i-1)}\neq a_{i-1}.

Note that
\Omega=\Omega^{(1)}\supset\Omega^{(2)}\supset\cdots\supset\Omega^{(K)}
form a decreasing sequence of subsets of
\Omega
.

Moreover, by Proposition
1
, the numbers
a_{i}
are assumed to satisfy the following constraint:

either
a_{i}=\min\Omega^{(i)}_{v(i)}
or
a_{i}=\max\Omega^{(i)}_{v(i)}
.

Let
\varepsilon_{i}=0,a^{\prime}_{i}=a_{i}+1
in the first case (
min
case) and
\varepsilon_{i}=1,a^{\prime}_{i}=a_{i}
in the second case (
max
case). Letting
y=\beta(x)
, we observe that in the min case, when
x\in\Omega^{(i)}
we have

x_{v(i)}=a_{i}\Leftrightarrow x_{v(i)}\leqslant a_{i}\Leftrightarrow y_{v(i),a_{i}+1}=\beta_{v(i),a_{i}+1}(x)=0,

and that in the max case, we have

x_{v(i)}=a_{i}\Leftrightarrow x_{v(i)}\geqslant a_{i}\Leftrightarrow y_{v(i),a_{i}}=\beta_{v(i),a_{i}}(x)=1.

These two equivalences are summarized in the following property:

x_{v(i)}=a_{i}\Leftrightarrow y_{v(i),a^{\prime}_{i}}=\varepsilon_{i}\text{ whenever }x\in\Omega^{(i)}.

(
P
)

Let us prove by induction on
i
that

x\in\Omega^{(i)}\Leftrightarrow\;\;\begin{cases}y_{v(1),a^{\prime}_{1}}\neq\varepsilon_{1}\\
\;\vdots\\
y_{v(i-1),a^{\prime}_{i-1}}\neq\varepsilon_{i-1}.\end{cases}

(
Q_{i}
)

Q_{1}
is trivial because
\Omega^{(1)}=\Omega
, and by property
P
, we have

\displaystyle x\in\Omega^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\left(x\in\Omega^{(i)}\text{ and }x_{v(i)}\neq a_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\left(x\in\Omega^{(i)}\text{ and }y_{v(i),a^{\prime}_{i}}\neq\varepsilon_{i}\right),

hence
Q_{i}
entails
Q_{i+1}
, and we have shown that
Q_{i}
holds for any
i
. Combining properties
P
and
Q_{i}
, we then obtain:

\begin{cases}x\in\Omega^{(i)}\\
x_{v(i)}=a_{i}\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow\;\;\begin{cases}y_{v(1),a^{\prime}_{1}}\neq\varepsilon_{1}\\
\;\vdots\\
y_{v(i-1),a^{\prime}_{i-1}}\neq\varepsilon_{i-1}\\
y_{v(i),a^{\prime}_{i}}=\varepsilon_{i}.\end{cases}

On the other hand,
F^{\beta}_{j,a}(y)=1\Leftrightarrow F_{j}(x)\geqslant a
. Hence, letting
\chi_{b\geqslant a}=1
if
b\geqslant a
and
0
otherwise, we have
F^{\beta}_{j,a}(y)=\chi_{F_{j}(x)\geqslant a}
. In particular

F_{j}(x)=b_{i}\Rightarrow F^{\beta}_{j,a}(y)=\chi_{b_{i}\geqslant a}.

Therefore, the condition that
F_{j}
is SNC gives, for any
y\in\beta(\Omega)
:

F^{\beta}_{j,a}(y)=\begin{cases}\chi_{b_{1}\geqslant a}&\text{if }y_{v(1),a^{\prime}_{1}}=\varepsilon_{1}\\
\chi_{b_{2}\geqslant a}&\text{if }y_{v(1),a^{\prime}_{1}}\neq\varepsilon_{1},y_{v(2),a^{\prime}_{2}}=\varepsilon_{2}\\
\;\vdots&\quad\vdots\\
\chi_{b_{K}\geqslant a}&\text{if }y_{v(1),a^{\prime}_{1}}\neq\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,y_{v(K-1),a^{\prime}_{K-1}}\neq\varepsilon_{K-1},y_{v(K),a^{\prime}_{K}}=\varepsilon_{K}.\end{cases}

This means that
F^{\beta}_{j,a}
is NC in the sense of Section
3.3
. Since this holds for all
j,a
,
F^{\beta}
is NC.
∎

3.5
Counterexample for WNC functions

Let
f:\Omega=\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
be the function defined in Section
2.3
. It is WNC but not SNC, and
\beta(\Omega)\subseteq\{0,1\}^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{2}
. The function
f^{\beta}:\beta(\Omega)\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{2}
is given by the following table:

f^{\beta}(x,y)=\quad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&00&10&11\\
\hline\cr 00&11&00&00\\
10&10&10&10\\
11&11&00&11\end{array}

where pairs in
\{0,1\}^{2}
are written without commas and parentheses. This table is simply obtained from the one defining
f
by the substitutions
0\mapsto 00,1\mapsto 10,2\mapsto 11
.

Now, the two Boolean functions
f_{1}^{\beta},f_{2}^{\beta}:\beta(\Omega)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that
f^{\beta}=(f_{1}^{\beta},f_{2}^{\beta})
are therefore:

f_{1}^{\beta}(x,y)=\quad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&00&10&11\\
\hline\cr 00&1&0&0\\
10&1&1&1\\
11&1&0&1\end{array}\quad\quad f_{2}^{\beta}(x,y)=\quad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&00&10&11\\
\hline\cr 00&1&0&0\\
10&0&0&0\\
11&1&0&1\end{array}

and it is easy to see that
f_{1}^{\beta}
is NC but that
f_{2}^{\beta}
is not NC. Therefore Theorem
3
does not extend to WNC functions.

4
Canalization of biological networks

Boolean NC functions appear predominantly in databases of Boolean genetic networks

3

22
]

Thus, in order to have an insight into the characteristics of multivalued functions specified in the context of biological systems, we considered a sample of such functions from biological models.

4.3
Enrichment in SNC functions

To get a flavour of the canalizing properties of biological functions, we investigated whether the distribution of the 48 multivalued rules that we considered (reported in Tables
1
to
4
) is as expected or shifted towards more than expected canalization.

Deriving a formula to calculate the exact number of SNC or WNC functions (similar to the formula given in
[
12
]
for the number of NC multivalued functions) is not trivial and is beyond the scope of this work. Here, we proposed a method for estimating an upper bound on the maximum number of SNC functions (
B
), and elaborated a python program that calculates it (
C
).

Thus, we calculated, for each value of arity
n\in\{1,\ldots,9\}
,

•

the proportion of SNC functions among our functions of arity
n
(Tables
1
to
4
),
i.e.
functions from
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}
to
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
, where
\Omega_{i}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
;

•

the upper bound on the expected proportion of SNC functions among all functions of arity
n
.

We were able to calculate the upper bound on the number of SNC functions only for

(computation for

reaches the precision limit of floating point numbers). The results are reported in Table

5
Concluding remarks and prospects

We prove in this manuscript that Booleanization preserves the SNC (and therefore the NC) property (Theorem
3
). On the other hand, the preliminary analysis of the canalizing property of multivalued biological functions (section
4.3
) suggests a certain relevance of canalization, notably SNC, among models arising from gene networks, as is the case for Boolean biological functions. It is worth observing that within the study sample, SNC and WNC properties are not distinguished. The significant number of multivalued functions that are not WNC, but whose Booleanizations are NC (gathered in Table
3
), calls for further study.

Importantly, it should be noted that we have not specified the assumptions for updating the system while they play an important role in the dynamical behavior and its properties. Implicitly, we considered here that the genes directly reach their target values: for instance,
\bm{\mathsf{Drk}}
can be activated at level
2
when
\bm{\mathsf{Der}}
is at level
2
, and if the current state of
\bm{\mathsf{Drk}}
is
0
, it will update directly to value
2
. However, in the context of biological systems, this is not a realistic hypothesis, steps of
1
are more common: in the previous example, if
\bm{\mathsf{Der}}
is at level
2
and
\bm{\mathsf{Drk}}
is not active, its activity will first reach level
1
. The introduction of the “steps of
1
” hypothesis in the definition of the function would obviously change the canalizing properties.

Appendix B
Method for computing an upper bound on the proportion of SNC functions

We first observe that, by Proposition
1
, each SNC function
f:\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
can be defined by a sequence of at most
K=\sum_{i}(k_{i}-1)
steps of canalization (
k_{i}=|\Omega_{i}|
). This representation is obviously not unique, and can be improved by grouping some commuting steps: for instance, consecutive steps which fix
f(x)
to the same value commute. This leads to a (still non-unique) representation of
f
by a matrix

(m_{i}^{j},M_{i}^{j},v^{j})_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}^{j}

where the step number
j
is at most
K
: at each step
j
, canalization of variables
x_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,n
)

•

m_{i}^{j}
times to its minimal value in its range

•

and
M_{i}^{j}
times to its maximal value

leads to
f(x)=v^{j}
. Consecutive values
v^{j},v^{j+1}
are assumed to be different, except possibly for the last two values. For each
i
,
\sum_{j}m_{i}^{j}+M_{i}^{j}=k_{i}-1
, so the above matrices can be produced by enumerating lists of given length
K
whose sum equals a given number,
i.e.
ordered integer partitions (with fixed length and possibly null entries). Then void steps (in which
m_{i}^{j}=M_{i}^{j}=0
for all
i
) are removed.
