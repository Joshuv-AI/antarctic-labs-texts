Theoretical smoothing frameworks for general nonsmooth bilevel problems
Jan Harold Alcantara Akiko Takeda

Abstract
Bilevel programming has recently received a great deal of attention due to its abundant applications in many areas. The optimal value function approach provides a useful reformulation of the bilevel problem, but its utility is often limited due to the nonsmoothness of the value function even in cases when the associated lower-level function is smooth. In this paper, we present two smoothing strategies for the value function associated with lower-level functions that are not necessarily smooth but are Lipschitz continuous. The first method employs quadratic regularization for partially convex lower-level functions, while the second utilizes entropic regularization for general lower-level objective functions. Meanwhile, the property known as gradient consistency is crucial in ensuring that a designed smoothing algorithm is globally subsequentially convergent to stationary points of the value function reformulation. With this motivation, we prove that the proposed smooth approximations satisfy the gradient consistent property under certain conditions on the lower-level function. Keywords. bilevel optimization; value function; smooth approximation; gradient consistency; quadratic regularization; entropic regularization

Introduction

Consider the bilevel optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}&f(x,y)\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\displaystyle y\in\argmin_{\bar{y}\in Y}g(x,\bar{y})\end{array}

where
f,g:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\times{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
are Lipschitz continuous but possibly nonsmooth functions, and
X,Y
are closed and convex subsets of
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
and
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}
, respectively. The minimization of
g(x,\cdot)
over
Y
is known as the
lower-level problem
, while the minimization of
f
is called the
upper-level problem
. The bilevel optimization problem (
1
) has recently gained significant attention due to its numerous applications, including hyperparameter optimization
[
Franceschi et al. 2018
,
Okuno et al. 2021
]
, meta-learning
[
Franceschi et al. 2018
]
, and Stackelberg games
[
von Stackelberg 2010
]
, among others. We refer the reader to
[
Dempe et al. 2015
,
Dempe and Zemkoho 2020
]
and the references therein for more applications of bilevel optimization.

Traditional methods for solving the bilevel problem (
1
) involve reformulation or relaxation of (
1
) as a one-level problem, thereby permitting the use of solution methods from nonlinear programming
[
Dempe et al. 2015
]
. A widely used approach is to replace the lower-level minimization problem by its optimality conditions. This yields an equivalent problem if
g(x,\cdot)
is convex for each
x\in X
. Among the recent trends that gained considerable attention is the case when
f
is continuously differentiable,
g
is a twice continuously differentiable,
g(x,\cdot)
is strongly convex for all
x\in X
, and
Y={\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}
. In this case, the lower-level problem of (
1
) defines a smooth map
x\mapsto y(x)
that corresponds to the unique minimizer of
g(x,\cdot)
. In turn, (
1
) simplifies to the minimization of a smooth function
f(x,y(x))
over
x\in X
, which can be handled by typical smooth optimization methods such as the projected gradient descent algorithm. Indeed, this framework has been the basis of some recent works in the literature
[
Ghadimi and Wang 2018
,
Ji et al. 2021
,
Pedregosa 2016
]
, but the main drawback is that this approach is heavily dependent on the smoothness of
f
and
g
, as well as the strong convexity of
g(x,\cdot)
. In many applications, such smoothness requirements are not met
[
Okuno et al. 2021
]
. Moreover, we note that when
g(x,\cdot)
is nonconvex, a local optimal solution of (
1
) may not even correspond to a stationary point of its reformulation via optimality conditions of the lower-level problem
[
Mirrlees 1999
]
.

An alternative stream of research, which is the direction pursued in this paper, employs the value function reformulation approach. The
value function
associated with the lower-level problem of (
1
) is defined as

v(x)\coloneqq\min_{y\in Y}~g(x,y).

(1.3)

With this, the bilevel problem (
1
) is equivalent to the nonlinear programming problem

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}&f(x,y)\\
\text{s.t.}&\displaystyle g(x,y)-v(x)\leq 0,\end{array}

(VFP)

which was first proposed by
Outrata 1990
.
Note that the reformulation (
VFP
) is independent of any smoothness and convexity assumptions on
g
, so that this framework is theoretically applicable to any bilevel optimization problem. However, one difficulty that comes with this flexible reformulation is determining under what conditions local solutions of (
VFP
) correspond to its stationary points. In nonlinear programming, these conditions pertain to constraint qualifications (CQs), and unfortunately, usual CQs such as the Mangasarian-Fromovitz constraint qualification (MFCQ) are violated by feasible points of (
VFP
)
[
Ye and Daoli 1995
]
. One promising alternative is to consider an approximate bilevel program

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\min_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}&f(x,y)\\
\text{s.t.}&\displaystyle g(x,y)-v(x)\leq\epsilon.\end{array}

(
\text{VFP}_{\epsilon}
)

where
\epsilon>0
. The above perturbed problem is a reasonable approximation of (
VFP
) as its local and global solutions are arbitrarily close to the solution set of (
VFP
)
[
Lin et al. 2014
,
Ye et al. 2023
]
.
Moreoever, unlike (
VFP
), (nonsmooth) MFCQ is automatically satisfied by feasible points of (

VFP
ϵ

), which is a sufficient constraint qualification for local solutions of (

VFP
ϵ

) to be stationary
[
Lampariello and Sagratella 2020
,
Lin et al. 2014
,
Ye et al. 2023
]
.

While the approximate bilevel problem (

VFP
ϵ

) addresses the CQ issue, another hurdle is the need for an algorithmic framework that is capable of obtaining stationary points. Solution methods for solving nonlinear programming problems often hinge on the assumption that both the objective and constraint functions involved are smooth. Unfortunately, the value function reformulation (
VFP
) of the bilevel problem introduces a challenge, since the value function
v
defined by (
1.3
), in general, is nonsmooth even when
g
is a smooth function (see
Theorem

2.1
). In the absence of smoothness, a popular adopted strategy involves employing smooth approximations, which have been extensively utilized in many nonsmooth optimization problems, complementarity problems and variational inequalities; see
Chen 2012
and the references therein. As a consequence, it is natural to expect that smooth approximations for the functions
f
and
g
in the bilevel problem (
1
) may be readily available, since these often correspond to loss functions with a nonsmooth regularizer in many applications; for instance, see
Chen 2012
. However, as previously mentioned, the smoothness of
g
is in general not adequate to ensure the smoothness of
v
. Consequently, employing traditional methods for the nonlinear programming reformulation (
VFP
) or (

VFP
ϵ

) necessitates smoothing techniques for the value function. Unfortunately, to date, smooth approximations of the value functions have not been thoroughly investigated yet in the literature. One work that pursued this direction in the context of bilevel optimization is studied by
Lin et al. 2014
. The authors adopted the entropic regularization approach originally formulated in
[
Fang and Wu 1996
]
, which yields an approximation (in the sense of
Definition

2.5
) that is valid for a smooth function
g
and a compact set
Y
.
Gradient consistency
(see
Definition

2.6
) of the proposed smoothing family is also proved, which is an essential property to guarantee convergence to stationary points. Apart from this work, there has been no other existing works on smoothing methods for bilevel problems, to the best of our knowledge.

In this paper, we present theoretical frameworks of smoothing strategies for solving the bilevel problem that do not require smoothness assumptions on
g
. In particular, we focus on the value function reformulation, and propose smooth approximations of the value function for Lipschitz continuous functions
g
. We propose two different techniques: First, we present a simple quadratic regularization approach for nonsmooth lower-level function
g
that is convex in
y
. The other technique is a more general approach that has no partial convexity requirement on
g
. More specifically, we extend the framework of
Fang and Wu 1996
,
Lin et al. 2014
to obtain a smooth approximation of the value function for a nonsmooth function
g
and a compact set
Y
. In addition, we also present a possible extension to unbounded closed sets
Y
. Meanwhile, as mentioned above, gradient consistency is a very important property to guarantee that a smoothing algorithm is globally (subsequentially) convergent to stationary points (see
Proposition

2.13
). Motivated by this, we prove that under certain conditions on
g
, all the proposed smooth approximations possess gradient consistency. Instrumental to this goal are Danskin-type theorems for arbitrary closed sets
Y
, which are also derived in this paper.

2.1
Nonsmooth and variational analysis

We recall some important concepts and results from nonsmooth and variational analysis. Most of these materials can be found in
[
Clarke 1983
, Chapter 2]
and
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
.

Let
\phi
be a Lipschitz continuous function on an open set
O\subseteq{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
. The
Clarke generalized directional derivative
of
\phi
at
\bar{x}\in O
in the direction
d
, denoted by
\phi^{\circ}(x;d)
, is defined as

\phi^{\circ}(\bar{x};d)=\limsup_{x\to\bar{x},t\searrow 0}\frac{\phi(x+td)-\phi(x)}{t},

and the (usual)
directional derivative
of
\phi
at
\bar{x}
in the direction
d
is

\phi^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\phi(\bar{x}+td)-\phi(\bar{x})}{t}.

We say that
\phi
is at
\bar{x}

Clarke regular
if for all
d\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
,
\phi^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)
exists and
\phi^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)=\phi^{\circ}(\bar{x};d)
. For instance, if
\phi
is differentiable at
\bar{x}
or if
\phi
is convex, then it is Clarke regular at
\bar{x}
.

The
Clarke generalized gradient
of
\phi
at
\bar{x}
, denoted by
\partial\phi(\bar{x})
, is given by

\partial\phi(\bar{x})\coloneqq\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}:\phi^{\circ}(\bar{x};d)\geq\langle\xi,d\rangle~\forall d\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\},

which is a compact and convex set. When
\phi
is convex, this coincides with the subdifferential in the sense of convex analysis:

\partial^{c}\phi(\bar{x})\coloneqq\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}:\phi(x)\geq\phi(\bar{x})+\langle\xi,x-\bar{x}\rangle~\forall x\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\}.

The Clarke generalized directional derivative
\phi^{\circ}(\bar{x};\cdot)
can be expressed as the support function of
\partial\phi(\bar{x})
, that is,

\phi^{\circ}(\bar{x};d)=\sigma_{\partial\phi(\bar{x})}(d)\coloneqq\max\{\langle d,\xi\rangle:\xi\in\partial\phi(\bar{x})\},

(2.2)

and so when
\phi
is convex,
\phi^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)=\sigma_{\partial^{c}\phi(\bar{x})}(d)
.

As a set-valued mapping,
\partial\phi(\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous at any
\bar{x}\in O
. That is, given any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

\partial\phi(x)\subseteq\partial\phi(\bar{x})+\varepsilon B_{1}(0)\quad\forall x\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x}).

By a standard argument using Heine-Borel theorem and noting the boundedness of
\partial\phi(x)
for any
x\in O
, note that
\bigcup_{x\in\Omega}\partial\phi(x)
is a bounded set for any compact subset
\Omega
of
O
.

For the Clarke subdifferential of the value function, we have the following celebrated result due to Danskin (see
Danskin 1967
and
[
Clarke et al. 1998
, Ex. 9.13]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Danskin’s Theorem)
.

Let
g:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\times Y\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
be given, where
Y
is a compact subset of
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}
. Suppose that for a neighborhood
\Omega
of
\bar{x}\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
, the derivative
\nabla_{x}g(x,y)
exists and is continuous (jointly) as a function of
(x,y)\in\Omega\times Y
. Then, the value function
v
given by (
1.3
) is Lipschitz and Clarke regular near
\bar{x}
, and

\partial v(\bar{x})=\co\{\nabla_{x}g(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\bar{y}\in S(\bar{x})\},

where
S:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\rightrightarrows Y
is given by

S(x)\coloneqq\argmin_{y\in Y}g(x,y).

An extension of the above result to the nonsmooth case was also provided in
Bertsekas 1971
under additional structural assumption on
g
.

Theorem 2.2

(Extended Danskin’s Theorem)
.

Let
g:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\times Y\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
be given, where
Y
is a compact subset of
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}
. Suppose that for a neighborhood
\Omega
of
\bar{x}\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
,
g
is continuous on
\Omega\times Y
, and for every
y\in Y
, the function
g(\cdot,y)
is a concave function on
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
. Then
v
given by (
1.3
) is concave on
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
and

\partial v(\bar{x})=P(\bar{x})\coloneqq\co\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}:\xi\in\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},\bar{y})~\text{and}~\bar{y}\in S(\bar{x})\}.

(2.3)

Proof.

The original result is stated for the maximum value function. Denoting
\displaystyle V(x)\coloneqq\max_{y\in Y}\left(-g(x,y)\right)
, which is a convex function, we have from
[
Bertsekas 1971
, Proposition A.22]
that

\partial^{c}V(\bar{x})=\co\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}:\xi\in\partial_{x}^{c}(-g(\bar{x},\bar{y}))~\text{and}~\bar{y}\in S(\bar{x})\}.

Noting that
\partial v=\partial(-V)=-\partial V=-\partial^{c}V
, we get the desired result.
∎

We recall the following important result on uniform level-bounded functions; see

Theorem 2.4

Let

If there exists

2.2
Smooth approximations

We formally recall some concepts related to smooth approximations. The definitions herein are adopted from
Chen et al. 2011
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
O\subseteq{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{d}
be an open set. We say that
\{\phi_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
is a
family of smooth approximations
for a continuous function
\phi:O\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
over the set
O
if
\phi_{\mu}:O\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
is continuously differentiable and if for all
\bar{z}\in O
, we have
\lim_{z\to\bar{z},\mu\to 0}\phi_{\mu}(z)=\phi(\bar{z})
.

With the availability of a smooth approximation, a traditional smoothing method is as follows: Given a nonsmooth optimization problem (P) involving a nonsmooth function
g
, we consider a smooth approximate problem (P
μ
) obtained by replacing
g
with
g_{\mu}
. This is a convenient approach as there are many powerful algorithms for dealing with smooth optimization problems, which may then be used for solving (P
μ
).

Intuitively, we expect that a sequence of solutions (or stationary points) of (P
μ
) with
\mu
decreasing to zero will converge to a solution (or stationary point) of the original problem (P). Theoretically, this is achieved through the notion of gradient consistency defined as follows. Note that this property is very important in characterizing stationary points of any smoothing method.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
O\subseteq{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{d}
be an open set and
\{g_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
be a family of smoothing functions for a continuous function
g:O\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
over the set
O
. This family is said to satisfy the
gradient consistent property
at
\bar{z}\in O
if

\emptyset\neq\limsup_{z\to\bar{z},\mu\searrow 0}\nabla g_{\mu}(z)\subseteq\partial g(\bar{z}),

where

\limsup_{z\to\bar{z},\mu\searrow 0}\nabla g_{\mu}(z)\coloneqq\left\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{d}:\exists\{z^{k}\},\exists\{\mu_{k}\}~\emph{suchthat}~z^{k}\to\bar{z},\mu_{k}\searrow 0~\emph{and}~\nabla g_{\mu_{k}}(z^{k})\to\xi\right\}.

We now provide some examples of smooth approximations that satisfy gradient consistency. We also highlight the uniform convergence of these smoothing families, to illustrate the satisfiability of the hypotheses of some results in
Sections

3
and
4
.

Example 2.7
.

Chen and Mangasarian 1995
proposed a smooth approximation of the plus function
\phi(z)=z_{+}=\max\{z,0\}
,
z\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
, using symmetric density functions
\rho
with
\kappa\coloneqq\int_{{\rm I}\!{\rm R}}|s|\rho(s)ds<+\infty
. In particular, they defined

\phi_{\mu}(z)\coloneqq\int_{{\rm I}\!{\rm R}}(z-\mu s)_{+}\rho(s)ds,

which satisfies the inequality
0\leq\phi_{\mu}(z)-\phi(z)\leq\kappa\mu
for all
z\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
. With this, it is straightforward to see that
\{\phi_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
is a family of smooth approximations of
\phi
in the sense of
Definition

2.5
, and in addition,
\phi_{\mu}\to\phi
uniformly on
{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
. Moreover, it also holds that

\limsup_{z\to\bar{z},\mu\searrow 0}\phi_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\begin{cases}=\sgn(\bar{z})&\text{if }\bar{z}\neq 0\\
\subseteq[0,1]&\text{if }\bar{z}=0\end{cases},

and thus gradient consistency is satisfied. This smoothing is particularly useful since many other loss functions and structure-promoting regularizers are expressible in terms of the plus function (see
Alcantara et al. 2021
,
Chen 2012
).

Example 2.8
.

The Moreau envelope of a closed proper function
\phi:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is given by

e_{\mu}\phi(z)\coloneqq\min_{u\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}}\phi(u)+\frac{1}{2\mu}{\left\|{z-u}\right\|}^{2}.

When
\phi
is a convex function,
e_{\mu}\phi
is
(\mu^{-1})-
smooth with
\nabla e_{\mu}\phi(z)=\frac{1}{\mu}(z-p_{\mu}(z))
where
p_{\mu}(z)\coloneqq\argmin_{u\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}}\phi(u)+\frac{1}{2\mu}{\left\|{z-u}\right\|}^{2}

[
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
, Theorem 2.26(b)]
. Moreover, for any open subset
\Omega\subseteq\interior(\dom(\phi))
, we have
0\leq\phi(z)-e_{\mu}\phi(z)\leq\frac{L_{\phi,\Omega}^{2}}{2}\mu
for all
z\in\Omega
, where
L_{\phi,\Omega}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
on
\Omega
(see
[
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
, Example 9.14]
and
[
Beck 2017
, Theorem 10.51]
). Hence,
\{e_{\mu}\phi:\mu>0\}
is a family of smooth approximations of
\phi
, and we also note that
e_{\mu}\phi
converges to
\phi
uniformly on
\Omega
. Using
[
Liu et al. 2019
, Lemma 1]
and noting that
\nabla e_{\mu}\phi(z)\in\partial\phi(p_{\mu}(z))
and
\partial\phi
is upper semicontinuous, it is not difficult to check that the gradient consistent property is satisfied.

Example 2.9
.

Let
g:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\times{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
be a convex function (that is, convex in
(x,y)
) and level-bounded in
y
locally uniform in
x
. Then the value function

given by (
1.3
) is convex
[
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
, Theorem 10.13]
and by
Example

2.8
,
\{v_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
where

v_{\mu}(x)\coloneqq e_{\mu}(v(x))=\min_{z\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}}\min_{y\in Y}g(z,y)+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|z-x\|^{2}

is a family of smooth approximations of the value function satisfying gradient consistency.

Example 2.10
.

Let
g:{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}\times{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}\to{\rm I}\!{\rm R}
be such that
g(\cdot,y)
is a concave function for all
y\in Y\subseteq{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{m}
. Then
-v
is a convex function and we may consider

v_{\mu}(x)\coloneqq-e_{\mu}(-v(x))=\max_{z\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}}\min_{y\in Y}g(z,y)-\frac{1}{2\mu}\|z-x\|^{2}

as a family of smooth approximations of the value function satisfying gradient consistency.

Aside from the restrictive structural assumptions on
g

3.1

Proposition 3.4

Under the hypotheses of

\hat{v}(x)\coloneqq\min_{y\in\hat{Y}}g(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{S}(x)\coloneqq\argmin_{y\in\hat{Y}}g(x,y).

Since the global minimizers of

, it follows that
\hat{S}\equiv S

\cl(B_{\alpha}(\bar{x}))
. In particular,
\hat{v}\equiv v
on the open set

so that their Clarke subdifferentials agree. Hence, together with Danskin’s theorem,
\partial v(\bar{x})=\partial\hat{v}(\bar{x})=\co\{\nabla_{x}g(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\bar{y}\in\hat{S}(\bar{x})\}=\co\{\nabla_{x}g(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\bar{y}\in{S}(\bar{x})\}
. This completes the proof.
∎

On the other hand, the following theorem is an extension of

Theorem 3.8

Let

\displaystyle\partial v(\bar{x})=P(\bar{x})=\co\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}:\xi\in\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},\bar{y})~\text{and}~\bar{y}\in S(\bar{x})\}.

Proof.

Define

, so that

\rho

Let

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{V}(\bar{x}+t_{k}d)-\hat{V}(\bar{x})}{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{V}(\bar{x}+t_{k}d)-\hat{V}(\bar{x})}{t_{k}}

\hat{h}^{\circ}(\bar{x},\bar{y};d,0)=\max\{\langle\zeta,(d,0)\rangle:\zeta\in\partial\hat{h}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\}=\max\{\langle\zeta_{x},d\rangle:\zeta_{x}\in\pi_{x}(\partial\hat{h}(\bar{x},\bar{y}))\},

We also take note of the following special case which should also be well-known, but a proof is provided for completeness.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let

be convex in

Proof.

Since

that
\partial v(\bar{x})=\{\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}:(\xi,0)\in\partial\left(g(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\delta_{Y}(\bar{y})\right)\}

We note that for a convex function

With the above characterizations of the Clarke subdifferential of the value function, we can now derive the gradient consistency of the proposed smoothing family

We start by proving that

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that

Proof.

Let
\Omega

\|\nabla_{x}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g_{\mu}(x,y)\right)\|

\nabla v_{\mu}(x)=\int_{Y}\alpha_{\mu}(x,y)\nabla_{x}g_{\mu}(x,y)dy\quad\forall x\in\Omega,

(u,y)
by
[
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Proposition 7.1.4(a)]
. Hence, with the upper-semicontinuity of the Clarke subdifferential (see
[
Facchinei and Pang 2003
, Proposition 7.1.4(b)]
or
[
Clarke 1983
, Proposition 2.1.5(d)]
), we conclude that
\bigcup_{(u,y)\in\cl(\Omega)\times Y}\partial g(u,y)
is bounded. (Note that here, the Lipschitz continuity of
g
over the open set
O_{X}\times O_{Y}
containing the compact set
\cl(\Omega)\times Y
plays an important role; see also
Section

2.1
). Since
\partial_{x}g(u,y)\subseteq\pi_{x}\left(\partial g(u,y)\right)
by
[
Clarke 1983
, Proposition 2.3.16]
, we conclude that the set
\bigcup_{(u,y)\in\cl(\Omega)\times Y}\partial_{x}g(u,y)
is likewise bounded, which together with the continuity of
g
implies that
M\coloneqq\sup\left\{\exp(-g(u,y))\|v\|:v\in\partial_{x}g(u,y)~\text{and}~(u,y)\in\cl(\Omega)\times Y\right\}
is finite. Going back to (
4.6
) and using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\min_{y\in Y_{j}}(-g(x^{*},y))-M\|x^{k}-x^{*}\|\leq\min_{y\in Y_{j}}\exp(-g(x^{k},y))\leq\min_{y\in Y_{j}}\exp(-g(x^{*},y))+M\|x^{k}-x^{*}\|.

At this point, we follow arguments analogous to those in the proof of
[
Fang and Wu 1996
, Lemma 2]
to obtain a contradiction, thus completing the proof. We note, however, that showing that equation (17) in
Fang and Wu 1996
does not require the “super uniform continuity” assumption imposed by the authors, as it automatically follows from
Theorem

2.4
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that the hypotheses of
Lemma

4.2
hold and
\{g_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
is a family of smooth approximations of
g
over
O_{X}\times O_{Y}
such that
g_{\mu}
converges uniformly to
g
on
\cl(\Omega)\times Y
for any open subset
\Omega
with
\cl(\Omega)\subseteq O_{X}
. Then,
\{v_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
is a family of smooth approximations for the value function over
O_{X}
.

Proof.

Proposition

4.1
already provides the continuous differentiability of each
v_{\mu}
on
O_{X}
. We just need to show that for any
\bar{x}\in O_{X}
,
v_{\mu}(x)\to v(\bar{x})
as
\mu\to 0
and
x\to\bar{x}
. Take any
\bar{x}\in O_{X}
and let
\varepsilon>0
be given, and
\tau\in(\exp(-\varepsilon),1)
. By
Lemma

4.2
there exists
\delta>0
such that (
4.4
) holds for all
\mu\in(0,\delta)
. Together with
[
Fang and Wu 1996
, Theorem 1]
, we have

\tau(\mu\vol(Y))^{\mu}\max_{y\in Y}\exp(-g(x,y))\overset{\eqref{eq:integral_lower_bound}}{\leq}\left(\int_{Y}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g(x,y)\right)dy\right)^{\mu}\leq\vol(Y)^{\mu}\max_{y\in Y}\exp(-g(x,y))

for all
x\in\cl(\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is an open set containing
\bar{x}
whose closure is contained in
O_{X}
. Noting the monotonicity of the logarithmic function, we have

-\mu\ln\vol(Y)-\max_{y\in Y}(-g(x,y))\leq-\mu\ln\left(\int_{Y}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g(x,y)\right)dy\right)\\
\leq-\ln\tau-\mu\ln(\mu\vol(Y))-\max_{y\in Y}(-g(x,y)).

By the choice of
\tau
and the definition of
v
, we have

v(x)-\mu\ln\vol(Y)\leq-\mu\ln\left(\int_{Y}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g(x,y)\right)dy\right)\leq v(x)-\mu\ln(\mu\vol(Y))+\varepsilon.

We may choose
\delta>0
to be sufficiently small enough so that for all
\mu\in(0,\delta)
, we have
\mu\ln\vol(Y)<2\varepsilon
and
-\mu\ln(\mu\vol(Y))<\varepsilon
. Hence,

\left|-\mu\ln\left(\int_{Y}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g(x,y)\right)dy\right)-v(x)\right|<2\varepsilon,

(4.7)

valid for all
x\in\cl(\Omega)
and
\mu\in(0,\delta)
. On the other hand, invoking the uniform convergence of
g_{\mu}
to
g
on
\cl(\Omega)\times Y
, we may choose
\delta
to be small enough so that (
4.7
) holds and
|g_{\mu}(x,y)-g(x,y)|<\varepsilon
for all
\mu\in(0,\delta)
and
(x,y)\in\cl(\Omega)\times Y
. Consequently, we have

\left|-\mu\ln\left(\int_{Y}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g_{\mu}(x,y)\right)dy\right)+\mu\ln\left(\int_{Y}\exp\left(-\mu^{-1}g(x,y)\right)dy\right)\right|<\varepsilon

for all
x\in\cl(\Omega)
and
\mu\in(0,\delta)
. Together with (
4.7
) and the definition of
v_{\mu}
, we have

|v_{\mu}(x)-v(x)|<3\varepsilon\qquad\forall x\in\cl(\Omega),~\forall\mu\in(0,\delta).

(4.8)

By the continuity of the value function on
\cl(\Omega)
(see
Theorem

2.4
(a)), we may choose
\delta>0
so that
|v(x)-v(\bar{x})|<\varepsilon
for all
x\in\cl(\Omega)
with
\|x-\bar{x}\|<\delta
. Together with (
4.8
), we have
|v_{\mu}(x)-v(\bar{x})|<4\varepsilon
for all
\mu\in(0,\delta)
and
x\in\cl(\Omega)
with
\|x-\bar{x}\|<\delta
. This completes the proof.
∎

We next show that
v_{\mu}
satisfies the gradient consistent property. To this end, we need the following lemmas. Note that
Lemma

4.4
is an extension of
[
Lin et al. 2014
, Theorem 5.3]
, but we provide a simpler and shorter proof as shown below. Moreover, we also need another technical result shown in
Lemma

4.5
, which will be helpful in our proof of gradient consistency.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\bar{x}\in O_{X}
and
y^{\prime}\in Y\setminus S(\bar{x})
. Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

4.3
, we have
\alpha_{\mu}(x,y^{\prime})\to 0
as
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
, where
\alpha_{\mu}
is given by (
4.3
).

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.3
,
-g_{\mu}(x,y^{\prime})+v_{\mu}(x)\to-g(\bar{x},y^{\prime})+v(\bar{x})
as
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
. Meanwhile, observe that we can rewrite
\alpha_{\mu}
as
\alpha_{\mu}(x,y)=\exp\left(\mu^{-1}(-g_{\mu}(x,y)+v_{\mu}(x))\right).

Hence, letting
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
and noting that
-g(\bar{x},y^{\prime})+v(\bar{x})<0
since
y^{\prime}\notin S(\bar{x})
, we see that
\alpha_{\mu}(x,y^{\prime})\to 0
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\bar{x}\in O_{X}
and let
Z
be any closed subset of
Y\setminus S(\bar{x})
. Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

4.3
,
\int_{Z}\alpha_{\mu}(x,y)dy\to 0
as
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
.

Proof.

First, we claim that
g_{\mu}(x,\cdot)\to g(\bar{x},\cdot)
as
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
uniformly on
Y
. Note that by hypothesis,
g_{\mu}\to g
as
\mu\to 0
uniformly on
\cl(B_{\delta_{0}}(\bar{x}))\times Y
, where
\delta_{0}
is any positive number such that
\cl(B_{\delta_{0}})\subseteq O_{X}
. Thus, given any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
such that

\mu\in(0,\delta)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|g_{\mu}(x,y)-g(x,y)|<\varepsilon/2\quad\forall(x,y)\in\cl(B_{\delta_{0}}(\bar{x}))\times Y.

(4.9)

Meanwhile, since
g
is uniformly continuous on the compact set
\cl(B_{\delta_{0}}(\bar{x}))\times Y
, we may choose a smaller
\delta>0
, if necessary, so that

|g(x,y)-g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|<\varepsilon/2\quad\forall(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})~\text{with}~{\left\|{(x,y)-(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right\|}<\delta.

(4.10)

Since
\delta<\delta_{0}
and
{\left\|{(x,y)-(\bar{x},y)}\right\|}={\left\|{x-\bar{x}}\right\|}
, then for any
(x,\mu)
such that
{\left\|{x-\bar{x}}\right\|}<\delta
and
\mu\in(0,\delta)
, it follows from triangle inequality and (
4.9
)-(
4.10
) that
|g_{\mu}(x,y)-g(\bar{x},y)|<\varepsilon
for any
y\in Y
. This completes that
g_{\mu}(x,\cdot)\to g(\bar{x},\cdot)
as
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
uniformly on
Y
.

Now, let
\varepsilon\coloneqq\displaystyle\max_{y\in Z}g(\bar{x},y)-v(\bar{x})
. Note that since
Z
is compact and lies outside
S(\bar{x})
, then
\varepsilon
is a finite positive number. Since
\lim_{(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)}v_{\mu}(x)=v(\bar{x})
by
Theorem

4.3
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
v_{\mu}(x)-v(\bar{x})<\varepsilon/2
for all
(x,\mu)\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x},0)
. On the other hand, by the claim proved above, we may choose a smaller
\delta>0
if necessary so that
g(\bar{x},y)-g_{\mu}(x,y)<\varepsilon/2
for all
y\in Y
and for all
(x,\mu)\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x},0)
. Consequently,
-g_{\mu}(x,y)+v_{\mu}(x)<-g(\bar{x},y)+v(\bar{x})+\varepsilon
for all
y\in Y
and for all
(x,\mu)\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x},0)
. On the other hand, by our choice of
\varepsilon
, we have
-g(\bar{x},y)+v(\bar{x})+\varepsilon\leq 0
for all
y\in Z
, so that
-g_{\mu}(x,y)+v_{\mu}(x)<0
for all
(x,\mu)\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x},0)
and
y\in Z
. In turn,
\alpha_{\mu}(x,\cdot)=\exp\left(\mu^{-1}(-g_{\mu}(x,\cdot)+v_{\mu}(x))\right)
is uniformly bounded above by 1 on
Z
for all
(x,\mu)\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x},0)
. Finally, we obtain the desired limit
\int_{Z}\alpha_{\mu}(x,y)dy\to 0
as a direct consequence of
Lemma

4.4
and the bounded convergence theorem.
∎

We now prove the following result which is instrumental in showing gradient consistency, as proved in
Theorem

4.10
. Note that unlike the smooth analogue
[
Lin et al. 2014
, Theorem 5.5]
, we do not assume compactness of the set
X
. As mentioned above, we also need the additional result
Lemma

4.5
to prove the following lemma. We also point out that due to the nonsmoothness of
g
, the main challenge and difficulty lie in finding an element of
P(\bar{x})
(refer to (
2.3
)) that is arbitrarily close to the set
\limsup_{x\to\bar{x},\mu\searrow 0}\nabla v_{\mu}(x)
. Moreover, the subsequent verification that the identified element is indeed close requires some subtle techniques.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that the hypotheses of
Theorem

4.3
hold and that there exists a neighborhood
\Omega
of
\bar{x}\in O_{X}
such that
\partial_{x}g(\cdot,\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous on
\Omega\times O_{Y}
. If
\dist(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu}(x,\cdot),\partial_{x}g(x,\cdot))
converges to
0
uniformly on
Y
as
(x,\mu)\to(\bar{x},0)
, then

\emptyset\neq\limsup_{x\to\bar{x},\mu\searrow 0}\nabla v_{\mu}(x)\subseteq P(\bar{x}),

where
P(\bar{x})
is given in (
2.3
).

Proof.

First, we show that
\limsup_{x\to\bar{x},\mu\searrow 0}\nabla v_{\mu}(x)
is indeed nonempty. By the uniform convergence assumption, there exists
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that
\dist(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu}(x,y),\partial_{x}g(x,y))<1
for all
\mu\in(0,\delta^{\prime})
and
(x,y)\in\cl(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\bar{x}))\times Y.

It follows that

\|\nabla_{x}g_{\mu}(x,y)\|\leq 1+M\qquad\forall\mu\in(0,\delta^{\prime}),~\forall(x,y)\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\bar{x})\times Y

(4.11)

where

M\coloneqq\sup\{\|\xi\|:\xi\in\partial_{x}g(x,y)~\text{and}~(x,y)\in\cl(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\bar{x}))\times Y\}.

(4.12)

We may choose
\delta^{\prime}>0
to be sufficiently small so that
\cl(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\bar{x}))\subseteq\Omega
. Thus,
M<+\infty
by the upper semicontinuity of
\partial_{x}g(\cdot,\cdot)
and compactness of
\cl(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\bar{x}))\times Y
. From (
4.2
) and noting that
\int_{Y}\alpha_{\mu}(x,y)dy=1
, we see that
\{\nabla v_{\mu}(x):x\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\bar{x}),\mu\in(0,\delta^{\prime})\}
is a bounded set, thus proving the first claim.

To prove the inclusion, suppose that
\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})\to\xi\in{\rm I}\!{\rm R}^{n}
for some sequences
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
and
\mu_{k}\searrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. We need to show that
\xi\in P(\bar{x})
. To this end, let
\varepsilon>0
be given. We will find an element
\xi^{k}
of
P(\bar{x})
such that
\|\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})-\xi^{k}\|<\varepsilon
for sufficiently large
k
. For any
y\in S(\bar{x})
, we have from upper semicontinuity that there exists
\delta_{y}>0
such that

\partial_{x}g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\subseteq\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y)+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}B_{1}(0)\quad\forall x^{\prime}\in B_{\delta_{y}}(\bar{x}),~y^{\prime}\in B_{\delta_{y}}(y).

(4.13)

Meanwhile, since
Y
is compact and
g
is continuous,
S(\bar{x})
is compact. Hence, there exist points
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{r}\in S(\bar{x})
and numbers
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots,\delta_{r}>0
such that
S(\bar{x})\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}B_{\delta_{i}}(y_{i})
. Denote
Y_{1}\coloneqq Y\cap B_{\delta_{1}}(y_{1})
,
Y_{i}\coloneqq Y\cap\left(B_{\delta_{i}}(y_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}B_{\delta_{j}}(y_{j})\right)
for
i=2,\dots,r
, and
Y_{r+1}\coloneqq Y\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}B_{\delta}(y_{i})
. Note that since
Y_{r+1}\subseteq Y\setminus S(\bar{x})
, it follows from
Lemma

4.4
that
\int_{Y_{r+1}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)dy\to 0
.

Now, let
\delta>0
be such that
\delta<\min\{\delta^{\prime},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{r}\}
and

\dist(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu}(x,y),\partial_{x}g(x,y))<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\quad\forall\mu\in(0,\delta)~\text{and}~\forall(x,y)\in\cl(B_{\delta}(\bar{x}))\times Y.

(4.14)

Choose
k
large enough so that
x^{k}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{x})
,
\mu_{k}\in(0,\delta)
and

\int_{Y_{r+1}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)dy<\frac{\varepsilon}{3(2M+1)}

(4.15)

where
M
is given by (
4.12
). We now define
\xi^{k}
as

\xi^{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}\int_{Y_{i}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)\right)dy,

where
y_{r+1}
is an arbitrarily chosen element of
S(\bar{x})
,
\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}
denotes the projection operator onto the convex set
\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})
, and
\varphi^{(i)}_{k}
is any single-valued mapping from
Y_{i}
to
\partial_{x}g(x^{k},y)
. Note that since
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r+1}Y_{i}=Y
,
\int_{Y}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)dy=1
, and the range of
\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}
is contained in
P(\bar{x})
, it follows that
\xi^{k}
is indeed in
P(\bar{x})
by Jensen’s inequality.

We now show that
\|\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})-\xi^{k}\|<\varepsilon
.
For each
i=1,\dots,r
, denote
E_{i}\coloneqq\{y\in Y_{i}:g~\text{is not differentiable at }(x^{k},y)\}
. By Rademacher’s theorem, note that the Lebesgue measure of
E_{i}
is zero since
g
is Lipschitz continuous on
O_{X}\times O_{Y}
. Moreover,
\partial_{x}g(x^{k},y)=\{\nabla_{x}g(x^{k},y)\}
and
\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)=\nabla_{x}g(x^{k},y)
for any
y\in Y_{i}\setminus E_{i}
where
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
. Hence, for all
y\in Y_{i}\setminus E_{i}
with
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
,

\displaystyle{\left\|{\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\nabla_{x}g(x^{k},y)}\right\|}

\displaystyle={\left\|{\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(x^{k},y)}(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y))}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\dist(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y),\partial_{x}g(x^{k},y))

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

(4.16)

where the last inequality holds by (
4.14
). By (
4.13
) and our choice of
\delta
, we have for any
y\in Y_{i}\setminus E_{i}
(
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
) that there exists
\nu(y)\in\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})
such that
{\left\|{\nabla_{x}g(x^{k},y)-\nu(y)}\right\|}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. It follows that
{\left\|{\nabla_{x}g(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\nabla_{x}g(x^{k},y)\right)}\right\|}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. This, together with (
4.16
), implies that

{\left\|{\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)\right)}\right\|}<\frac{2\varepsilon}{3},\quad\forall y\in Y_{i}\setminus E_{i},~i\in\{1,\dots,r\},

(4.17)

by triangle inequality. Meanwhile, we also have

\displaystyle{\left\|{\int_{Y_{r+1}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\left(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{r+1})}\left(\varphi^{(r+1)}_{k}(y)\right)\right)dy}\right\|}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{Y_{r+1}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\left({\left\|{\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)}\right\|}+{\left\|{\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{r+1})}\left(\varphi^{(r+1)}_{k}(y)\right)}\right\|}\right)dy

\displaystyle\quad\leq(2M+1)\int_{Y_{r+1}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)dy

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\eqref{eq:integral_alpha_epsilon}}{<}\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

(4.18)

where the second inequality holds by (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) since
\delta<\delta^{\prime}
. Finally, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})-\xi^{k}\|

\displaystyle={\left\|{\int_{Y}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)dy-\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}\int_{Y_{i}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)\right)dy}\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq{\left\|{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{Y_{i}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\left(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)\right)\right)dy}\right\|}

\displaystyle\qquad+{\left\|{\int_{Y_{r+1}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)\left(\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{r+1})}\left(\varphi^{(r+1)}_{k}(y)\right)\right)dy}\right\|}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:outside_S(x)_integral}}{<}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{Y_{i}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y){\left\|{\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)\right)}\right\|}dy+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{Y_{i}\setminus E_{i}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y){\left\|{\nabla_{x}g_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)-\Pi_{\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},y_{i})}\left(\varphi^{(i)}_{k}(y)\right)}\right\|}dy+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dist_S(x)_integral}}{<}\frac{2\varepsilon}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{Y_{i}}\alpha_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k},y)dy+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

(4.19)

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, it follows that we can extract a subsequence
\{\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})\}_{k\in K}
where
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}
together with
\{\xi^{k}\}_{k\in K}\subseteq P(\bar{x})
such that
\|\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})-\xi^{k}\|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
with
k\in K
. Since
P(\bar{x})
is closed (due to the compactness of
S(\bar{x})
), any accumulation point
\xi^{*}
of
\{\xi^{k}\}_{k\in K}
belongs to
P(\bar{x})
. Since
\|\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})-\xi^{*}\|\leq\|\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})-\xi^{k}\|+\|\xi^{k}-\xi^{*}\|
and
\nabla v_{\mu_{k}}(x^{k})\to\xi
, we conclude that
\xi=\xi^{*}
, so that
\xi\in P(\bar{x})
as desired. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We note that the uniform convergence assumption in the above lemma can be achieved in many situations. Suppose that
g
takes the form
g(x,y)=a(x,y)+b(p(x),q(y))
where
a
and
b
are smooth,
b
has Lipschitz continuous derivative,
p
and
q
are Lipschitz continuous functions, and the chain rule applies for
b(p(\cdot),q(y))
for each
y
; for instance, under the conditions stipulated in
[
Clarke 1983
, Theorem 2.3.9]
or simply when
p
is a smooth function. Let
\{p_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
and
\{q_{\mu}:\mu>0\}
be smooth approximations of
p
and
q
, respectively, such that
\dist(\nabla p_{\mu}(\cdot),\partial p(\cdot))\to 0
pointwise (uniform convergence is not required), and
q_{\mu}\to q
uniformly on
Y
. Note that these assumptions are satisfied by the Chen-Mangasarian smoothing and the Moreau envelope smoothing described in
Example

2.7
and
Example

2.8
. Under these circumstances, we obtain the uniform convergence required in
Lemma

4.6
.

Remark 4.8
.

Upper semicontinuity of
\partial_{x}g
can be attained under any condition that will guarantee that if
(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\in\partial g(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, then
\xi\in\partial_{x}g(\bar{x},\bar{y})
for any
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
. For instance, the latter condition can be satisfied under Clarke regularity of
g
(see
[
Clarke 1983
, Proposition 2.3.15]
) or convexity/concavity of
g
with respect to
x
(see
[
Clarke 1983

5
Concluding Remarks

We have presented two techniques for deriving smooth approximations of the value function corresponding to a nonsmooth function. One approach is through the use of quadratic regularization, which is applicable when the lower-level objective is convex in the decision variable of the lower-level problem. In the absence of this convexity structure, we also present another technique by entropic regularization, which does not require any other structural assumption other than Lipschitz continuity. While smoothing approaches are often employed for the practical purpose that optimization toolboxes usually require smoothness, it is equally important to theoretically investigate whether or not the smoothing algorithm will converge to a stationary point. In this work, we also proved the gradient consistent property for the proposed smoothing techniques, which serve as a primary tool for convergence analysis of a smoothing algorithm. This paper provides the theoretical framework for smoothing approaches for bilevel optimization problems. In our future work, we will further investigate if we can relax the Lipschitz continuity assumption on the lower-level objective function, which played a pivotal role in almost all of our analyses. Existence of dominating functions which were required in entropic regularization for unbounded constraint sets also deserve further investigation. Finally, it is a subject of our future work to look into practical implementations of gradient-based algorithms for solving the bilevel optimization problem using the proposed smoothing functions for the value function.
