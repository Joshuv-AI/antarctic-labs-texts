Hadamard Product and Resurgence Theory
Y. Li, D. Sauzin, S. Sun

Abstract
We discuss the analytic continuation of the Hadamard product of two holomorphic functions under assumptions pertaining to Écalle’s Resurgence Theory, proving that if both factors are endlessly continuable with prescribed sets of singular points A A and B B , then so is their Hadamard product with respect to the set { 0 } ∪ A ⋅ B \{0\}\cup A\cdot B . This is a generalization of the classical Hadamard Theorem in which all the branches of the multivalued analytic continuation of the Hadamard product are considered.



1
Introduction

1.1
The Hadamard product of two power series

f(\xi),g(\xi)\in\mathbb{C}[[\xi]]

is defined by the formula

f(\xi)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\xi^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad g(\xi)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}\xi^{n}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\odot g(\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}b_{n}\xi^{n}.

(1.1)

If the radii of convergence of
f
and
g
are positive,
i.e.

f(\xi),g(\xi)\in\mathbb{C}\{\xi\}
, then
so is the radius of convergence of
f\odot g
.
From now on, we identify a convergent
power series with the holomorphic germ at
0
that it defines.
J. Hadamard published in 1898 the proof of his now classical theorem
[
Ha99
]
,
to the effect that:

Given finite subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
K,L>0
, if
f

extends analytically to
\mathbb{D}_{K}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\alpha\in A}R_{\alpha}

and
g
to
\mathbb{D}_{L}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\beta\in B}R_{\beta}
,
then
f\odot g
extends analytically to
\mathbb{D}_{KL}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{\gamma\in A\cdot B}R_{\gamma}
.

Here, we have denoted by
\mathbb{D}_{M}
the open disc centred at the origin of
radius
M
,
by
R_{\omega}
the portion of the ray
\omega\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

from
\omega
onward,
and by
A\cdot B
the “product set” of
A
and
B
:

\mathbb{D}_{M}\coloneqq D(0,M),\qquad R_{\omega}\coloneqq\omega\,[1,+\infty),\qquad A\cdot B\coloneqq\{\alpha\beta\mid(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B\}.

(1.2)

So, this is a statement about the “principal branches” of
f
,
g

and
f\odot g
:
we are considering their analytic continuation to a part of their
Mittag-Leffler stars, assuming that any singular point that appears
for
f
or
g
when following a ray emanating from the origin is
isolated
(in fact, J. Hadamard also proves a more general version, in
which the various
R_{\omega}
’s are interpreted as portion of logarithmic spirals
of the same polar slope).

In
[
Bo98
]
, É. Borel studied the analytic continuation of
f\odot g
, also giving a proof of Hadamard’s theorem, with a view to giving
more information on the nature of the singularities of
f\odot g
in
terms of the nature of the singularities of
f
and
g
, at least in
the case of “uniform singularities” (
i.e.
with trivial monodromy:
poles or essential singularities).
We refer the reader to
[
PM20a
]
,
[
PM20b
]
for a modern study and a
generalization of this result.

Interestingly, Borel also mentions something about the analytic
continuation of
f
,
g
and
f\odot g
to other sheets than the
principal one,

In this article, we are concerned with the case when

satisfy a property pertaining to Écalle’s Resurgence Theory

Definition 1.1

Given a non-empty closed discrete subset
\Omega

, what we call
“

\gamma\colon\thinspace[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega

in the disc of convergence
of

This is the definition that has been employed in

]

, or in
[

]
,
[

]

[

.
It already covers a range of interesting examples,

in complex

The simplest examples of
\Omega

the analytic continuation of an
\Omega
-continuable germ, only on

More general is the definition of “endless continuability”

[

]
,
[
KS20
]
, and even more general that of
“continuability without a cut”
[
Eca85
]
.

Our main result is

Theorem A
.

Given non-empty closed discrete subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{C}
, if
f
is an

A
-continuable germ and
g
a
B
-continuable germ, then

\Omega\coloneqq\{0\}\cup(A\cdot B)

(1.3)

is closed and discrete and
f\odot g
is an
\Omega
-continuable germ.

The proof is given in Sections
2
–
3
.

Note that in Definition
1.1
we may have
0
belonging
to
\Omega
or not, but in all cases does an
\Omega
-continuable germ have a
principal branch regular at
0
. Simple examples with
\Omega=\{0,1\}

are

f_{1}(\xi)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\xi}\log(1-\xi)
and

\operatorname{Li}_{2}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\xi}f_{1}(\xi_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}\xi_{1}
.
So, Theorem
A
is compatible with the Hadamard
Theorem, and J. Hadamard is right not to include
0
among the possibly
singular points of
f\odot g

as far as only the principal branches are concerned.

The necessity of including
0
among the possibly singular points of

f\odot g
when we consider its analytic continuation on other sheets
was already noted by Borel, who gives credit to E. Lindelöf for that point

[
Bo98
, ftn 2]
.
A simple example that illustrates it is

f_{0}(\xi)\coloneqq-\log(1-\xi)
:
the germ
f_{0}
is
\{1\}
-continuable, but the germ
f_{0}\odot f_{0}
is
not; in fact,
f_{0}\odot f_{0}=\operatorname{Li}_{2}
is
\{0,1\}
-continuable.

1.3
The formal Borel transform
\mathcal{B}\colon\thinspace t\,\mathbb{C}[[t]]\to\mathbb{C}[[\xi]]
is defined
by:

\widetilde{f}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}t^{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{B}\widetilde{f}(\xi)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{c_{n}}{n!}\xi^{n}.

(1.4)

In other words,
\mathcal{B}\widetilde{f}(\xi)=\mathrm{e}^{\xi}\odot\frac{\widetilde{f}(\xi)}{\xi}
.
A formal power series
\widetilde{f}(t)\in t\,\mathbb{C}[[t]]
is said to be an

\Omega
-resurgent series
when its Borel transform
\mathcal{B}\widetilde{f}
is an

\Omega
-continuable germ
[
Eca81
]
,
[
Sau13
]
.
The singularities of
\mathcal{B}\widetilde{f}
then give us information on the
divergence of
\widetilde{f}(t)
, inasmuch as the Borel transform of a
convergent power series has no singularity at all.

Now,

f=\mathcal{B}\widetilde{f}\quad\text{and}\quad g=\mathcal{B}\,\widetilde{g}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\odot g=\mathcal{B}(e_{1}\odot\widetilde{f}\odot\widetilde{g}),

(1.5)

where
e_{1}(t)\coloneqq t\,\mathrm{e}^{t}
, hence our result can be rephrased as

Given non-empty closed discrete subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{C}
, if
\widetilde{f}(t)
is an

A
-resurgent series and
\widetilde{g}(t)
a
B
-resurgent series, then

e_{1}\odot\widetilde{f}(t)\odot\widetilde{g}(t)
is an
\Omega
-resurgent
series with
\Omega
as in (
1.3
).

This is to be compared with Theorem 6.27 of
[
MS16
]
, which can
be rephrased as:

Given non-empty closed discrete subsets
A,B\subset\mathbb{C}
such that

\Omega\coloneqq A\cup B\cup(A+B)
is closed and discrete, if
\widetilde{f}(t)
is an

A
-resurgent series and
\widetilde{g}(t)
a
B
-resurgent series, then
their product
\widetilde{f}(t)\cdot\widetilde{g}(t)
is an
\Omega
-resurgent
series.

The latter statement is a variation on a result on the multiplication
of
\Omega
-resurgent series proved in
[
Sau13
]
.
More generally, the stability under multiplication of the space of
resurgent series (defined as Borel preimages of endlessly continuable
germs or of germs continuable without a cut) is a key fact of
Écalle’s Resurgence Theory.

1.4
The shift of indices in the definition of
\mathcal{B}
(mapping
t^{n+1}
to

\xi^{n}/n!
) is a convenient normalization which results in the
following relation between the ordinary multiplication in
\mathbb{C}[[t]]

and the convolution
*
in
\mathbb{C}[[\xi]]
:

\mathcal{B}(\widetilde{f}\cdot\widetilde{g})=f*g(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\xi}f(\xi_{1})g(\xi-\xi_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}\xi_{1}.

(1.6)

This is the usual “additive” convolution (associated with Laplace
transform and Borel-Laplace summation), whereas the Hadamard product
may be considered as a “multiplicative” convolution. Indeed, as was
well-known already in Hadamard’s time,

0<\rho<R_{f}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\lvert\xi\rvert<\rho R_{g}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\odot g(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{C_{\rho}}f({\zeta})g\Big(\frac{\xi}{{\zeta}}\Big)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}{\zeta}}{{\zeta}},

(1.7)

where
C_{\rho}
is the parametrized circle:
s\in[0,1]\mapsto{\zeta}=\rho\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}s}

(we have denoted by
R_{f}
and
R_{g}
the radii of convergence of
f
and
g
).

Actually, Formula (
1.7
) is the basis of Hadamard’s and Borel’s arguments in

[
Ha99
]
and
[
Bo98
]
, and it will be the starting point of our
analysis as well.
The idea, translated from Borel’s own words, is that “this expression
of
f\odot g
stays valid
if one deforms the integration contour without
letting it cross any singular point of the integrand
” and, “the
contour having been fixed in an arbitrary manner, one obtains the
analytic continuation of
f\odot g

by moving
\xi
in the
plane, provided the singular points of the integrand do not cross
the integration contour”
.

It seems that, throughout
[
Bo98
]
, Borel keeps in mind the possibility of
going to the non-principal sheets and dealing with multivalued
analytic continuation, yet reluctantly so, since when he explicitly
mentions that possibility (in the aforementioned footnote 2 or
later in Sec. III when he writes “if the functions
f
and
g
were
multivalued…”) he tends to recommend to discard it (see the last
paragraph of his Sec. III: “It seems to us useless to insist on the
latter point… one would be led to complicated statements…”).
However,
he puts in a footnote (footnote 3) an idea that
has been successfully
adapted to study the analytic continuation of the
additive

convolution (
1.6
) of endlessly continuable germs in the context of
Resurgence
[
Eca81
]
,
[
CNP93
]
.

We reproduce here the content of footnote 3 of
[
Bo98
]
, with minor notational
changes. Borel writes (
1.7
) as

f\odot g(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{C}f\big(\frac{1}{x}\big)g(\xi x)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}x}{x}
,
with
C=C_{\rho^{-1}}
.

“Let us conceive the closed contour
C
as a flexible extensible
thread, the singular points of
f\big(\frac{1}{x}\big)
as pins stuck
into the plane, the singular points of
g(\xi x)
as pins that travel as

\xi
moves.
It is necessary and sufficient that the thread always part the two
systems of pins. Now, this will always be possible, by means of a
suitable deformation, if, while travelling, the second pins never come
to
hit
the first ones (…); the thread may acquire a very
complicated form, but this is harmless.”
[
Bo98
, ftn 3, p. 240]

In the case of the additive convolution product (
1.6
),
instead of the closed contour
C=C_{\rho^{-1}}
, it is the line segment

[0,\xi]
that must be deformed.
In
[
Sau13
]

and
[
MS16
]
, it was shown how to contruct explicitly this

when
\xi
moves in the complex plane without meeting
A\cup B\cup(A+B)
;

this is done
by applying to the initial integration contour a
homeomorphism that is a deformation of the identity obtained as the flow
of an explicit non-autonomous vector field tailored to the situation.

A benefit of such a detailed rigorous proof with respect to the arguments given
in
[
Eca81
]
or
[
CNP93
]
is that it allows for quantitative
estimates.
These estimates, in turn, can be

used to show the stability of the space of resurgent series under
nonlinear operations and not only

[
Sau15
]
,
[
MS16
]
,
[
KS20
]
.

1.5
Our proof of Theorem
A
is spread over

–

Section
2
, which gives details on the kind of

),

This proof bears some resemblance with the proof of the theorem on

We were led to Theorem
A
while studying the Borel
transform of the Moyal star product of two resurgent series
\widetilde{f}(t,q,p)
and
\widetilde{g}(t,q,p)

[

]
. Indeed, the Borel counterpart of the Moyal star
product can be written in terms of the Borel transforms of the

f(\xi,q,p)
and
g(\xi,q,p)
, and the formula appears as a
mixture of additive convolution with respect to
\xi
and Hadamard product; more
specifically, it involves the Hadamard product with respect to
{\zeta}
of the
Taylor expansions

f(\xi_{1},q,p+{\zeta})\odot g(\xi_{2},q+{\zeta},p)

and then a convolution-like integration with respect to
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
.
However, in such a many-variable context, the restrictions one needs
to put on the singular locus of

and
g
are more stringent than in
Definition
1.1
, namely one requires
“algebro-resurgence”
[

]
,
[

]
:
f,g\in\mathbb{C}\{\xi,q,p\}
have analytic
continuation away from a proper algebraic subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{3}

(or

if one is interested in deformation quantization of

with coordinates
q_{1},\ldots,q_{N},p_{1},\ldots,p_{N}
).
As a result, the functions of
{\zeta}
involved in the Hadamard product
part of the formula have at most finitely many singularities.

Our work appears as complementary to the one of
[
PM20a
]
and

[
PM20b
]
, which is devoted to the nature of the singularities of
the principal branch of

and contains new formulas for the
monodromy at a given point
\omega=\alpha\beta
.
Considering the monodromy operator
\Delta_{\omega}
forces to go around
\omega
and visit other
sheets of the Riemann surface of
f\odot g
, but only the nearby
sheets (the “next-to-principal” one for
\Delta_{\omega}(f\odot g)
, or the
ones reached by circling several times around
\omega
if one wants to
iterate

).
Dealing with these nearby branches of the analytic continuation of the
Hadamard product requires a
deformation of the integration contour in (

) that is
tractable by means of relatively elementary geometry;
the deformation becomes much more intricate if the
variable
\xi
is allowed to travel to arbitrary sheets, but it can still be
mathematically described and studied by following the method of

–
3
.
Therefore, it would be interesting to see whether this can be used to study the nature of
the singularities of the non-principal branches of

in the spirit of

[
PM20a
]
and
[
PM20b
]
.
Moreover, as noted in
[

]
, one might try to explore more deeply
the connection with Écalle’s Resurgence Theory; the so-called
“alien operators”, designed to study the singularities of endlessly
continuable functions, behave particularly well in relation with the

) and they lead to contour

[

]
—see

[

]
.

Another avenue of research that naturally suggests itself would be
to consider the Hadamard product of endlessly continuable germs

and

which are not

-continuable in the sense of
Definition
1.1
for any discrete closed set
\Omega
.
One may conjecture that

is still endlessly continuable
because, during the process of analytic continuation by means of

many singularities of

and

at the same time, which is why
(additive) convolution (and even iterated convolutions) could be handled in
[

]
; one
might try to apply similar techniques to the Hadamard product.

2
Deformations of the identity adapted to a path
\gamma

We now begin the proof of Theorem
A
.
We thus give ourselves
A
and
B
non-empty closed discrete subsets
of
\mathbb{C}
, and we define
\Omega
by (
1.3
).
We also introduce

A^{\prime}\coloneqq A\setminus\{0\},\quad a\coloneqq\min\{\lvert\alpha\rvert\mid\alpha\in A^{\prime}\},\qquad B^{\prime}\coloneqq B\setminus\{0\},\quad b\coloneqq\min\{\lvert\beta\rvert\mid\beta\in B^{\prime}\},

(2.1)

regardless of
0
belonging to
A\cup B
or not.
Note that

\Omega=\{0\}\cup(A^{\prime}\cdot B^{\prime}).

(2.2)

2.1
The first claim to be proved is

Lemma 2.1
.

The set
\Omega
is closed and discrete.

Proof.

Clearly, this is equivalent to saying that

\text{For any $R\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$,}\quad\Omega\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R}\kern 5.0pt\text{is finite.}

(2.3)

Let
R\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Any pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in A^{\prime}\times B^{\prime}
such that
\alpha\beta\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R}

must satisfy
\lvert\alpha\rvert\geq a>0
,
\lvert\beta\rvert\geq b>0
and

\lvert\alpha\beta\rvert\leq R
.
Hence,
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R/b}
and
\beta\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R/a}
.
But
A\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R/b}
and
B\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R/a}
are finite,
by the rephrasing (
2.3
) of our assumption on
A

and
B
, hence there only finitely many such pairs
(\alpha,\beta)
and

\Omega\cap\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{R}
is finite.
∎

2.2
Let
I\coloneqq[0,1]
. We now give ourselves an
A
-continuable
germ
f
, a
B
-continuable germ
g
, and a path

\gamma\colon\thinspace I\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
that has its initial point close enough
to the origin,
specifically

0<\lvert\gamma(0)\rvert<ab.

(2.4)

Let us choose
\rho>0
such that

\frac{\lvert\gamma(0)\rvert}{b}<\rho<a.

(2.5)

Note that the radius of convergence
R_{f}
of
f
is at least
a
, and
the radius of convergence
R_{g}
of
g
at least
b
,
hence Formula (
1.7
) implies that

f\odot g(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{C_{\rho}}f({\zeta})g\Big(\frac{\xi}{{\zeta}}\Big)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}{\zeta}}{{\zeta}}\quad\text{for}\kern 5.0pt\xi\;\text{close enough to}\;\gamma(0).

(2.6)

To prove Theorem
A
, we just need to prove that

f\odot g
admits analytic continuation along
\gamma
.
Without loss of generality, we may suppose that
\gamma
is
C^{1}
with
a derivative
\gamma^{\prime}
that is Lipschitz.

2.3
We now make precise the notion of deformation of the identity that we
have alluded to in the introduction:

Definition 2.2
.

We call “deformation of the identity adapted to
\gamma
” any family

(\Psi_{t})_{t\in I}
of Lipschitz homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}
such that

\Psi_{0}=\mathop{\hbox{{\rm Id}}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{C}}
and

\displaystyle\alpha\in\{0\}\cup A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\quad\kern 5.0pt\Psi_{t}(\alpha)=\alpha\qquad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\beta\in B^{\prime}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Psi_{t}\Big(\frac{\gamma(0)}{\beta}\Big)=\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I.

(2.8)

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose we have found
(\Psi_{t})_{t\in I}
, a deformation of the
identity adapted to
\gamma
, then the analytic continuation of

f\odot g
along
\gamma
exists and is given, at each
\gamma(t)
,
t\in I
, by the formula

\operatorname{cont}_{\gamma\mid t}(f\odot g)(\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\mathrm{i}}}\oint_{\Psi_{t}(C_{\rho})}f({\zeta})g\Big(\frac{\xi}{{\zeta}}\Big)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}{\zeta}}{{\zeta}}\quad\text{for}\kern 5.0pt\xi\;\text{close enough to}\;\gamma(t).

(2.9)

Proof.

Since each
\Psi_{t}
is a homeomorphism of
\mathbb{C}
,
condition (
2.7
)
entails
\Psi_{t}^{-1}(\alpha)=\alpha
for

\alpha\in A
, whence

{\zeta}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Psi_{t}({\zeta})\in\mathbb{C}\setminus A\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I.

(2.10)

We also have
\Psi_{t}^{-1}(0)=0
and, by
condition (
2.8
),
\Psi_{t}^{-1}\big(\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}\big)=\frac{\gamma(0)}{\beta}

for all
\beta\in B^{\prime}
, whence

{\zeta}\neq 0\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\frac{\gamma(0)}{{\zeta}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus B^{\prime}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Psi_{t}({\zeta})\neq 0\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt}\frac{\gamma(t)}{\Psi_{t}({\zeta})}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus B^{\prime}\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I

(2.11)

(indeed:
\frac{\gamma(t)}{\Psi_{t}({\zeta})}=\beta\in B^{\prime}
would require
{\zeta}=\Psi_{t}^{-1}\big(\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}\big)=\frac{\gamma(0)}{\beta}
).
Therefore, since by (
2.5
) all the points of
C_{\rho}
satisfy the requirements
indicated in the left-hand sides of (
2.10
)–(
2.11
), the
integral in the right-hand side of (
2.9
) is
a well-defined
function
h_{t}(\xi)
of
\xi
, which

represents a holomorphic germ at
\gamma(t)
.

The Cauchy theorem entails that, for
t<t^{\prime}
close enough, the
functions
h_{t}
and
h_{t^{\prime}}
coincide in a neighbourhood of
\gamma_{\mid[t,t^{\prime}]}
, hence
h_{t}
is the analytic continuation along
\gamma

at
\gamma(t)
of
h_{0}
, and
h_{0}=f\odot g
by (
2.6
).
∎

3
Construction of a non-autonomous vector field

3.1
To conclude the proof of Theorem
A
, it is thus
sufficient to find a deformation of the identity in the sense of
Definition
2.2
. Our tool will be the flow map induced
by a non-autonomous vector field.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
X\colon\thinspace I\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is locally Lipschitz
(identifying
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) and satisfies

\lvert X(t,{\zeta})\rvert\leq K\lvert{\zeta}\rvert

(3.1)

with some
K>0
. Then,
for each
t^{*}\in I
and each initial condition
{\zeta}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
,
the non-autonomous vector field

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}{\zeta}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=X(t,{\zeta})

(3.2)

has a unique maximal solution
t\mapsto\Phi^{t^{*},t}({\zeta}_{0})
such
that
\Phi^{t^{*},t^{*}}({\zeta}_{0})={\zeta}_{0}
.
This maximal solution is defined for all
t\in I
.

Moreover, the map
(t^{*},t,{\zeta})\in I\times I\times\mathbb{C}\mapsto\Phi^{t^{*},t}({\zeta})\in\mathbb{C}
thus defined is locally Lipschitz,
and
(\Phi^{t^{*},t})_{t^{*},t\in I}
is a family of locally Lipschitz
homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\Phi^{t,t^{**}}\circ\Phi^{t^{*},t}=\Phi^{t^{*},t^{**}},\qquad\Phi^{t^{*},t^{*}}=\mathop{\hbox{{\rm Id}}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{C}}.

(3.3)

Proof.

Apply the classical Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem about the existence and
uniqueness of solutions to systems of ordinary differential equations

and their regular dependence upon the initial condition
.
The maximal solutions are defined for all times because
of the bound (
3.1
).

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that we have found
c\colon\thinspace I\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
locally
Lipschitz, bounded by
K\lvert{\zeta}\rvert
with some
K>0
, such that

\displaystyle\alpha\in\{0\}\cup A^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\quad\kern 5.0ptc(t,\alpha)=0\qquad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\beta\in B^{\prime}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c\Big(t,\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}\Big)=\frac{1}{\beta}\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I.

(3.5)

Then the flow map
\Psi_{t}\coloneqq\Phi^{0,t}
of the non-autonomous
vector field

X(t,z)\coloneqq c(t,z)\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)

(3.6)

gives rise to a deformation of the identity adapted to
\gamma
.

Proof.

We have
\Psi_{t}(\alpha)=\Phi^{0,t}(\alpha)=\alpha
for all
\alpha\in A

by (
3.4
),
and (
3.5
) shows that
t\mapsto\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}
is a
particular solution of
X
for each
\beta\in B^{\prime}
, hence

\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}=\Phi^{0,t}\big(\frac{\gamma(0)}{\beta}\big)=\Psi_{t}\big(\frac{\gamma(0)}{\beta}\big)
.
∎

3.2

Thus, we are just left with the question of finding a function
c

meeting the requirements of Corollary
3.2
.
Since
\Omega
is closed and
\gamma(I)
is compact, we can pick
\delta,M>0
so
that, for each
t\in I
,

\delta\leq\lvert\gamma(t)\rvert\leq M,\qquad\lvert\gamma(t)-\alpha\beta\rvert\geq\delta\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0pt(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B.

(3.7)

Lemma 3.3
.

If
{\varepsilon}>0
is chosen small enough so that

{\varepsilon}<\frac{a}{2},\qquad\frac{a\delta}{{\varepsilon}}>M+\frac{2M}{a}{\varepsilon},

(3.8)

then

\beta\in B^{\prime}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{dist}\Big(\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta},A^{\prime}\Big)\geq{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all}\kern 5.0ptt\in I.

(3.9)

Proof.

Suppose
\lvert\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}-\alpha\rvert<{\varepsilon}
with
\alpha\in A^{\prime}
. We
would have

\delta\leq\lvert\gamma(t)-\alpha\beta\rvert<{\varepsilon}\lvert\beta\rvert

(3.10)

by (
3.7
b).
We also have
\lvert\alpha\beta\rvert\geq a\lvert\beta\rvert
by (
2.1
) and

\lvert\gamma(t)\rvert\leq M
by (
3.7
a),
whence

a\lvert\beta\rvert\leq{\varepsilon}\lvert\beta\rvert+M

(3.11)

by the triangle inquality.
Now, either
\lvert\beta\rvert\geq\frac{2M}{a}
, but then

a\lvert\beta\rvert\leq{\varepsilon}\lvert\beta\rvert+M\leq({\varepsilon}+\frac{a}{2})\lvert\beta\rvert

leads to a contradiction with (
3.8
a);
or
\lvert\beta\rvert<\frac{2M}{a}
, but then

\frac{a\delta}{{\varepsilon}}<a\lvert\beta\rvert
by (
3.10
),
which is
\leq M+\lvert\beta\rvert{\varepsilon}
by (
3.11
),
whence the inequality

\frac{a\delta}{{\varepsilon}}<M+\frac{2M}{a}{\varepsilon}
,
which contradicts (
3.8
b).
∎

Let us choose a Lipschitz function
\eta=\eta_{{\varepsilon},A^{\prime}}\colon\thinspace\mathbb{C}\to[0,1]
such that

\alpha\in A^{\prime}\kern 5.0pt\Longrightarrow\kern 5.0pt\eta(\alpha)=0,\qquad\operatorname{dist}({\zeta},A^{\prime})\geq{\varepsilon}\kern 5.0pt\Longrightarrow\kern 5.0pt\eta({\zeta})=1.

(3.12)

For instance, we may pick a Lipschitz function
\chi_{\varepsilon}\colon\thinspace\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to[0,1]
such that
\chi_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
and

d\geq{\varepsilon}\kern 5.0pt\Longrightarrow\kern 5.0pt\chi_{\varepsilon}(d)=1,

e.g.

\chi_{\varepsilon}(d)\coloneqq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\min}\big\{\frac{d}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\varepsilon}}},1\big\}
, and define

\eta({\zeta})\coloneqq\chi_{\varepsilon}\big(\operatorname{dist}({\zeta},A^{\prime})\big)
.

We conclude by checking that the function defined by

c(t,{\zeta})\coloneqq\eta({\zeta})\frac{{\zeta}}{\gamma(t)}

(3.13)

fulfils all the requirements of Corollary
3.2
:

–

The function
c
is locally Lipschitz and bounded by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{\delta}}\lvert{\zeta}\rvert
, by virtue of (
3.7
a), because

\lvert\eta\rvert\leq 1
.

–

We have
c(t,0)=0
.

–

If
\alpha\in A^{\prime}
, then
c(t,\alpha)=0
because (
3.12
a)
says that
\eta(\alpha)=0
.

–

If
\beta\in B^{\prime}
, then (
3.9
) says that

\operatorname{dist}\big(\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta},A^{\prime}\big)\geq{\varepsilon}
, hence

c\big(t,\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}\big)=\eta\big(\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta}\big)\frac{1}{\beta}=\frac{1}{\beta}

by (
3.12
b).

Therefore, the proof of Theorem
A
is complete.
