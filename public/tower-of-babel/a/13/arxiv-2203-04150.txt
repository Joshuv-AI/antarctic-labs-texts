The principle of maximum entropy and the distribution of mass in galaxies
Jorge Sánchez Almeida*0000-0003-1123-6003

Abstract
We do not have a final answer to the question of why galaxies choose a particular internal mass distribution. Here we examine whether the distribution is set by thermodynamic equilibrium (TE). Traditionally, TE is discarded for a number of reasons including the inefficiency of two-body collisions to thermalize the mass distribution in a Hubble time, and the fact that the mass distribution maximizing the classical Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy is unphysical. These arguments are questionable. In particular, when the Tsallis entropy that describes self-gravitating systems is used to define TE, the mass distributions that result (i.e., the polytropes) are physically sensible. This work spells out this and other arguments for TE, and presents the polytropes and their properties. It puts forward empirical evidence for the mass distribution observed in galaxies to be consistent with polytropes. It compares polytropes with Sérsic functions and it shows how the DM halos resulting from cosmological numerical simulations become polytropes when efficient collisions are allowed. It also discusses pathways to thermalization bypassing two-body collisions. It finally outlines future developments including deciphering whether or not DM particles collide efficiently.

Purpose and organization of this review

Galaxies are self-gravitating structures approaching mechanical equilibrium, where kinetic energy (
\langle T\rangle
) and gravitational energy (
\langle U\rangle
) tend to balance each other to meet the virial condition (
2\langle T\rangle+\langle U\rangle=0
). The constraint imposed by this condition is very loose, though. It allows for a large number of ways to distribute the mass internally
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
. This freedom contrasts with the fact that real galaxies choose only very specific internal mass distributions, namely, those consistent with stellar mass surface density profiles looking like Sérsic functions
(
Sersic 1968
;
Caon et al. 1993
;
Trujillo et al. 2001
;
Blanton et al. 2003
;
Graham and Driver 2005
;
van der Wel et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
. These functions embrace from exponential disks
(
de Jong and van der Kruit 1994
, observed in dwarf galaxies; e.g.,)
to
de Vaucouleurs

R^{1/4}
-profiles
(
de Vaucouleurs 1948
, characteristic of massive ellipticals; e.g.,)
.

Why do galaxies choose this particular mass distribution?
Although it sounds a trivial question, we do not know the answer yet. It could be due either to satisfy some fundamental physical principle (e.g., thermodynamic equilibrium; hereinafter TE) or to the very particular initial conditions that gave rise to the system
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
. Contrarily to what one may naively think, the mass distribution in galaxies is commonly explained as the outcome of cosmological initial conditions
(
Ascasibar et al. 2004
;
Henriksen 2004
;
Cen 2014
;
Nipoti 2015
;
Ludlow and Angulo 2017
;
Brown et al. 2020
, and Sect.
4
)
. The option of a fundamental process like TE determining the internal configuration is traditionally disregarded with two different arguments which, however, seem to become frailer as time goes by and more observational and theoretical work is available.

The first argument against TE has to do with the nature of dark matter (DM). According to the current concordance cosmological model, DM provided most of the gravity that allowed galaxies to form in time, and now it holds them together. In the simplest model, DM is collision-less and cold (CDM). Under these hypotheses, the timescale for the DM particles to reach TE is set by the two-body relaxation timescale, which is extremely long; typically much larger than the Hubble time
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
;
Power et al. 2003
;
Ludlow et al. 2019
;
Sánchez Almeida and Trujillo 2021
, i.e., than the age of the Universe;)
. Thus, taken literally, this result would discard mass distributions arising from TE. However, the collision-less nature assumed for the DM causes some of the so-called small-scale problems of the CDM model
(
Weinberg et al. 2015
;
Bullock and Boylan-Kolchin 2017
;
Del Popolo and Le Delliou 2017
, e.g.,)
, in particular, the
cusp–core problem
. Simulated CDM haloes have
cusps
in their central mass distribution
(e.g.,
Navarro et al. 1997
;
Wang et al. 2020
, ; see Sect.
4
)
which disagree with the central plateau or
core
often observed in galaxies
(
Oh et al. 2015
;
Carlsten et al. 2021
;
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. This well known difficulty of the CDM model is bypassed invoking physical processes which essentially shorten the timescale to reach TE, making it shorter than the Hubble time. The proposed pathways to thermalization are of very different nature (Sect
6
): feedback of the baryons on the DM particles through gravitational forces
(
Governato et al. 2010
;
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Freundlich et al. 2020
)
, scattering with massive gas clumps
(
Elmegreen and Struck 2013
;
Struck and Elmegreen 2019
)
, forcing by a central bar
(
Hohl 1971
)
, merger of two super massive black holes
(
Merritt et al. 2006
;
Nasim et al. 2021
;
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
, SMBHs;)
, or assuming an artificially large DM to DM collision cross section
(
Spergel and Steinhardt 2000
;
Davé et al. 2001
;
Elbert et al. 2015
)
.
Whether or not it is collision-less, the DM seems to find a way for thermalization faster than the inefficient two-body relaxation collisions, an observational fact that weakens the original criticism.

The second argument against TE setting the internal structure of galaxies is related to the use of the classical Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy to define equilibrium. Following the principles of statistical physics, the structure in TE corresponds to the most probable configuration of a self-gravitating system and, thus, it should result from maximizing its entropy

This is precisely the meaning given in the paper to the term
thermodynamic equilibrium
. It is used for distributions that maximize the funcional describing the entropy of the system.
. The use of the classical Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy to characterize self-gravitating systems leads to a distribution with infinite mass and energy
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
;
Padmanabhan 2008
, and Sect.

)
, disfavoring TE as explanation. However, this difficulty of the theory has been overcome as follows. In the standard Boltzmann-Gibbs approach, the long-range gravitational forces that govern self-gravitating systems are not taken into account. These forces are not subordinate but a fundamental ingredient of the physical system. Fortunately, systems with long-range interactions admit long-lasting meta-stable states described by the Tsallis (
S_{q}
) non-additive entropy
(
Tsallis 1988
;
Tsallis 2009
;
Chavanis and Sire 2005
)
. In particular, the maximization under suitable constraints of the Tsallis entropy of a self-gravitating N-body system leads to a polytropic distribution
(
Plastino and Plastino 1993
;
Lima and de Souza 2005
)
, which can have finite mass and a shape resembling the DM distribution found in numerical simulations of galaxy formation
(
Navarro et al. 2004
;
Calvo et al. 2009
;
An and Zhao 2013
;
Sánchez Almeida and Trujillo 2021
;
Merritt et al. 2005
, and Sect.
4
)
. The polytropic shape has lately gained practical importance because of its association with real self-gravitating astrophysical objects (Sect.
5
). The mass density profiles in the centers of dwarf galaxies are reproduced by polytropes without any tuning or fitting
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2020
)
. Polytropes also explain the stellar surface density profiles observed in galaxies
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
)
and in globular clusters
(
Trujillo and Sánchez Almeida 2022
)
.

In short, the arguments against TE setting galaxy shapes are questionable. Moreover, empirical evidence supports that the mass distribution expected from this equilibrium is indeed observed in self-gravitating astronomical objects. These two facts justify the writing of the present review. It basically expands the above arguments, gathering the works suggesting the fundamental role played by TE and spelling out the arguments leading to this conclusion.

Sect.

2.1
) and later on (Sect.
2.2
) the Tsallis entropy that yields polytropic density profiles. The physically relevant properties of polytropes are summarized in Sect.
2.2.1
. In order to compare polytropes with observations, they have to be projected in the plane-of-the-sky, an exercise carried out in Sect.
2.2.2
.
Section
3
discuses the relation between projected polytropes (
propol
s) and Sérsic profiles.
Section
4
briefly mentions the relation between polytropes and the profiles inferred from N-body numerical simulations of structure formation in the Universe.
Section
5
summarizes all the evidence for polytropic profiles reproducing real galaxies, thus providing observational support for the theory described in previous sections.
Section
6

Finally, Sect.
7

Self-gravitating systems of maximum entropy

This section follows arguments and notation taken from
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
;
Plastino and Plastino 1993
;
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
)
.

2.1
Solution using the classical Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy: isothermal sphere

Assume a spherically symmetric self-gravitating system of identical particles. The classical Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy of the system is defined as

S=-\int f\ln f\,d^{6}{\bf w}+C,

(1)

with
f
the distribution function (DF),
d^{3}{\bf w}
the volume element in the 6D phase space (of position and velocity), and
C
an integration constant. The integral extends to all the phase space. The maximum entropy solution would be the distribution
f
that maximizes
S
under the constraints that the total mass
M
and energy
E
are constant,

M=\int f\,d^{6}{\bf w},

(2)

E=\int\epsilon\,f\,d^{6}{\bf w},

(3)

with
\epsilon=\Phi+v^{2}/2
the total energy per unit mass. The symbols
v
and
\Phi
stand for the velocity and the gravitational potential, respectively. Using Lagrange multipliers and variational calculus, the condition for
S
to be extreme follows from,

\frac{\partial}{\partial f}[f\ln f+\alpha f+\beta\epsilon f]=0,

(4)

with
\alpha
and
\beta
two Lagrange multipliers. Satisfying this condition requires
f
to be an exponential function of the energy, namely,

\ln f=-(1+\alpha+\beta\epsilon).

(5)

Since the system is self-gravitating, the gravitational potential,
\Phi
, and the density,

\rho=\int f\,d^{3}{\bf v},

(6)

corresponding to
f
have to satisfy the Poisson equation for spherically symmetric systems,

\nabla^{2}\Phi=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{d}{dr}\Big(r^{2}\frac{d\Phi}{dr}\Big)=4\pi G\rho,

(7)

with
G
the gravitational constant and
r
the radial distance to the center of the potential. Equations (
5
), (
6
), and (
7
) are better solved in terms of the relative potential
\Psi=\Phi_{0}-\Phi
and the relative energy
\varepsilon=\Phi_{0}-\epsilon
, with the constant
\Phi_{0}
chosen so that
\varepsilon>0
for
f>0

(see
Binney and Tremaine 2008
, Chap. 4)
. Then,

\ln f=\beta(\Psi-\frac{v^{2}}{2})+C^{\prime},

(8)

and so through the relation (
6
),

\ln\rho=\beta\Psi+C^{\prime\prime},

(9)

which transforms Eq. (
7
) into

\frac{d}{dr}\Big(r^{2}\frac{d\ln\rho}{dr}\Big)=-4\pi G\beta\,r^{2}\rho.

(10)

The previous equation describes an
isothermal sphere
, i.e., it is formally identical to the density structure arising in a self-gravitating ideal gas of constant temperature
T
, with

\beta=\frac{m_{g}}{kT},

(11)

where
m_{g}
is the mass of each gas molecule and
k
the Boltzmann constant. In general,
Eq. (
10
)
has to be integrated numerically, however, it admits a solution called
singular isothermal sphere
,

\rho\propto\frac{1}{r^{2}},

(12)

which is important in the present context since it describes the asymptotic behavior of all solutions of Eq. (
10
) when
r\rightarrow\infty

(
Chandrasekhar 1967
;
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
. This implies that the total mass of the system is always infinity,

M\propto\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{r}r^{\prime 2}\rho(r^{\prime})dr^{\prime}\simeq\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty.

(13)

If rather than the total mass one works out the total energy, it also turns out to be infinity. Finally, the entropy of the system (Eq. [
1
]) tends to infinity as well,

S\propto-\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\ln\rho-\beta\frac{v^{2}}{2})\,\rho\,\exp(-\beta\frac{v^{2}}{2})\,v^{2}dv\,r^{\prime 2}dr^{\prime}

\simeq\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big[\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{r}(2\ln r^{\prime}+\beta\frac{v^{2}}{2})dr^{\prime}\Big]\,\exp(-\beta\frac{v^{2}}{2})\,v^{2}dv\,\rightarrow\infty.

(14)

Therefore, any self-gravitating system asked to reach maximum Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy has infinite mass, infinite energy, and infinite entropy, and so, it is non-physical.
Binney and Tremaine 2008
explain that no
f
with finite
M
and
E
maximizes
S
because a trivial re-arrangement of the mass distribution can augment
S
boundlessly. Given
M
and
E
,
S
always can be increased by increasing the degree of central concentration and then transferring the resulting gain of potential energy to an arbitrarily small amount of mas placed in a large outer envelope.

2.2
Solution using the Tsallis entropy: polytropes

The Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy neglects long range interactions leading to the inconsistencies pointed out in Sect.
2.1
. Back in
Tsallis 1988
,
Tsallis 1988
introduced another entropy, now called Tsallis entropy,

S_{q}=-\int f\,\frac{1-f^{q-1}}{q-1}\,d^{6}{\bf w}+C^{\prime\prime\prime}.

(15)

The Tsallis entropy has been successfully used in many different physical contexts going from plasmas and particle physics to geophysics and astrophysics
(e.g.,
Tsallis 2011
, Sect. 6)
. It works because
S_{q}
is able to describe systems having long range interactions among their constituents
(
Tsallis 2009
;
Tsallis 2011
)
. Tsallis entropy actually represents a full family of entropies since it depends on the numerical coefficient
q
. In the special case when
q\rightarrow 1
it becomes the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy in Eq. (
1
)
2
2

2

Consider the identity
(1-f^{q-1})/(q-1)=(1-\exp[(q-1)\ln f])/(q-1)
, expand
\exp
in its Taylor series, and take the limit
q\rightarrow 1
.
.
Following the standard approach described in Sect.
2.1
,
Plastino and Plastino 1993
worked out the DF
f
that minimizes
S_{q}
for a system with constant mass and energy. In this case the Lagrange Eq. (
4
) becomes,

\frac{\partial}{\partial f}[f\,\frac{1-f^{q-1}}{q-1}+\alpha f+\beta\epsilon f]=0,

(16)

which provides,

f=\Big[\frac{1}{q}\Big(1-(\alpha+\beta\epsilon)(1-q)\Big)\Big]^{\frac{1}{q-1}},

(17)

or, redefining
\Phi_{0}
,

f\propto\Big[\Phi_{0}-\epsilon\Big]^{\frac{1}{q-1}}=\Big[\Psi-\frac{v^{2}}{2}\Big]^{\frac{1}{q-1}}.

(18)

The DF in Eq. (
18
) corresponds to a
polytrope

(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
, Eqs. (4.41) and (4.83) in )
with the polytropic index
m
set by
q
as

m=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{q-1},

(19)

and with the density (Eq. [
6
]) given by

\rho\propto\Psi^{m}.

(20)

For the system to be self-gravitating, the density
\rho
and the gravitational potential
\Phi
(
=\Psi-\Phi_{0}
) have to be linked through the Poisson Eq. (
7
). After some algebra, the Poisson equation can be re-written as the so-called Lane-Emden equation
(
Chandrasekhar 1967
;
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
, which in practice is taken to define polytropes. Explicitly, a polytrope of index
m
is defined as the spherically-symmetric self-gravitating structure resulting from the solution of the Lane-Emden equation for the (normalized) gravitational potential, namely,

\frac{1}{s^{2}}\frac{d}{ds}\Big(s^{2}\frac{d\psi}{ds}\Big)=\begin{cases}-3\psi^{m}&\psi>0,\\
0&\psi\leq 0.\end{cases}

(21)

The symbol
\psi
stands for the normalized relative potential (
\propto\Psi
),
s
represents the scaled radial distance,

r=b\,s,

(22)

and the density at radial distance
r
is recovered from
\psi
as

\rho(r)=\rho(0)\,\psi(s)^{m},

(23)

where
\rho(0)
and
b
are two constants. In general, Eq. (
21
) has to be solved numerically under the initial conditions

\psi(0)=1~~~{\rm and}~~~d\psi(0)/ds=0.

(24)

Solutions with
d\psi(0)/ds\not=0
are discarded because they have infinite central density and total mass
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
, e.g.,)
3
3

3

Plugging into Eq. (
21
) the expansion of
\psi
around
s=0
,
\psi(s)\simeq\psi(0)+[d\psi(0)/ds]\,s+\dots
, one ends up with the identity
\psi(0)\simeq-(3/2)[d\psi(0)/ds]\,s^{-1}
, which implies that either
d\psi(0)/ds=0
or
\psi(0)\rightarrow\infty
when
s\rightarrow\infty
.
.

In contrast with the maximum entropy solutions for the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy, the maximum entropy solution for the Tsallis entropy are physically meaningful provided
m
(and so
q
) is within a fairly narrow range of values,

3/2<m\leq 5.

(25)

The upper limit (
m\leq 5
) comes from requesting the mass to be finite
(
Chandrasekhar 1967
;
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
. The cause for the lower limit is more subtle. Polytropes with
m<3/2
have an
f
which increases with increasing energy per particle, which is physically unreliable
(
Plastino and Plastino 1993
)
.
In addition, the stability of polytropes is analyzed in various works
(
Chavanis 2002
;
Taruya and Sakagami 2002
;
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
which set the condition
m>3/2
for the polytropes to be stable for radial pulsations.

shows the polytropes resulting from solving the Lane-Emden Eq. (

). These curves just provide the shape of the density profile, which can be shifted horizontally and vertically by arbitrary amounts (Eqs. [

] and [

]). The color code of the solid lines gives the polytropic index as indicated in the inset. The curve with

The DF
f
in Eq. (
6
), integrated over all velocities at a given point, renders the density in Eq. (

). The same exercise can be used to derive the velocity dispersion
\sigma_{v}
,

\rho\,\sigma_{v}^{2}=\int v^{2}\,f\,d^{3}{\bf v},

(26)

and it leads to

\sigma_{v}^{2}(r)=\sigma_{v}^{2}(0)\,\psi,

(27)

with the velocity dispersion in the center of the gravitational potential,
\sigma_{v}^{2}(0)
, connected with the central density through

\sigma^{2}_{v}(0)=4\pi G\rho(0)b^{2}/(m+1).

(28)

Since the physical systems is axi-symmetric, the velocity dispersion in one particular direction (e.g.,
z
) is just
\sigma_{vz}=\sigma_{v}/\sqrt{3}
.

shows the radial variation of the velocity dispersion corresponding to the polytropic radial density variation represented in Fig.

(

2.2.1
Properties of the polytropes

The solutions of the Lane-Emden equation (
\psi
in Eq. [
21
]) are well studied in literature because of their connection with the stellar structure produced by polytropic gases
(
Fowler and Hoyle 1964
;
Chandrasekhar 1967
;
Horedt 2004
)
. Some of these properties are of relevance for the study of the structure of galaxies, and they are compiled next.

\bullet

Analytic solutions.

The case
m=5
is known as the
Schuster 1884
sphere or
Plummer 1911
model and has an analytic solution,

\psi(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+s^{2}}},

(29)

which extends to infinity but has finite mass. Analytic solutions also exists for
m=0
and
m=1

(
Chandrasekhar 1967
)
. They are useful for the purpose of testing numerical solutions of Eq. (
21
), but they are no so relevant in the present context because of Eq. (
25
).

\bullet

Connection with the isothermal sphere.
Since the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy approaches the Tsallis entropy in the limit
q\rightarrow 1
, and this limit corresponds to
m\rightarrow\infty
(see Eq. [
19
]), one can recover the isothermal sphere (Eqs. [
10
] and [
12
]) taking the limit of the Lane-Emden Eq. (
21
) when
m\rightarrow\infty

(
Hunter 2001
)
.

\bullet

Analytic approximations
. Most polytropic indexes do not admit an analytic solution and either Eq. (
21
) has to be integrated numerically or one has to resort to one of the analytic approximations existing in literature. The original approximations date back to pre-computer days when polytropes were fundamental to model stelar structure
(
Chandrasekhar 1967
)
. However, these approximations may still be useful nowadays to fit large datasets of galaxies. Fitting requires to evaluate polytropes thousands of times, and this computation usually determines the speed of the algorithm.

Chandrasekhar 1967
expands
\psi
as a polynomial around
s=0
. The first terms are

\psi(s)\simeq 1-\frac{1}{2}\,s^{2}+\frac{3\,m}{40}\,s^{4}-\dots,

(30)

an expression which holds for
s\lesssim 1
. Through Eq. (
23
), one recovers the volume density.
An expansion reaching out to the term
s^{10}
is explicitly given by
Horedt 2004
. This approximation eventually breaks down for
s
large enough and so other alternatives have being worked out. Approximations in terms of exponentials have being tried
(
Fowler and Hoyle 1964
;
Beech 1987
)
. Through an Euler transformation of Eq. (
21
),
Hunter 2001
works out a series that converges all the way to the outer radius. A comprehensive review of the possibilities put forward in literature are compiled by
Horedt 2004
. Among them, it is shown that for large
s
,
\psi(s)
approaches the solution

\psi(s)\simeq A_{m}\,s^{2/(1-m)}

(31)

in an oscillatory manner, with the coefficient
A_{m}
depending just on
m

(see
Horedt 2004
, Eq. (2.4.88))
. Thus, due to Eqs. (
23
), (
22
), and (
31
),

\rho\propto r^{-2}

(32)

for
m\gg 1
. Figure
1
also includes for reference the curve
\rho\propto r^{-2}
(the blue dashed line) to represent the asymptotic behavior of high-
m
polytropes. Likewise, the asymptotic behavior of the velocity dispersion for
m\gg 1
inferred from Eqs. (
27
) and (
31
) is a constant independent of
s
, as it should be in this limit when polytropes become isothermal spheres.

\bullet

Central cores
. All politropes have a
core
(a central plateau in the density distribution) with the same shape independently of the index
m

(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2020
;
Sánchez Almeida and Trujillo 2021
)
. Equations (
23
) and (
22
), and the first two terms in the approximation (
30
) lead to,

\frac{\rho(r)}{\rho_{\alpha}}\simeq\Big[1+\frac{\alpha}{2m}\Big(1-\frac{r^{2}}{r_{\alpha}^{2}}\Big)\Big]^{m}\simeq 1+\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big(1-\frac{r^{2}}{r_{\alpha}^{2}}\Big),

(33)

with the characteristic radius,
r_{\alpha}
, and characteristic density,
\rho_{\alpha}=\rho(r_{\alpha})
, defined in terms of a particular value (
-\alpha
) for the logarithmic derivative of the density profile,

\frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln r}(r_{\alpha})=-\alpha.

(34)

The second approximate identity in the right-hand side of Eq. (
33
), which holds for
\alpha\ll 2m
, indicates that after normalization by
\rho_{\alpha}
and
r_{\alpha}
, all polytropes collapse to a single shape independent of
m
. In other words, except for a trivial normalization, all the polytropes look the same in their cores. This property is followed by haloes from numerical simulations of self-gravitating systems (Sect.
4
) as well as in real galaxies (Sect.
5
).

\bullet

Finite size.

We also note that for
m<5
there is always a truncation, i.e.,
\rho
goes to zero at a finite radius
(
Chandrasekhar 1967
)
. Thus, these polytropes have a finite size. The radii have to be computed numerically, and are tabulated elsewhere
(e.g.,
Chandrasekhar 1967
, Table 4)
.

\bullet
Relation with polytropic gases.
As we explain above, the properties of polytropes are well known because of the formal equivalence with polytropic gases, traditionally used to model stars analytically
Chandrasekhar 1967
. They are formally identical provided that the exponent
\gamma
that defines the relation between pressure (
P
) and
\rho
,

P\propto\rho^{\gamma},

(35)

is related with the polytropic index in the Lane-Emden Eq. (
21
) as
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
, e.g.,)
,

\gamma=1+\frac{1}{m}.

(36)

In this equivalence,
\sigma_{v}^{2}
plays the role of gas temperature (
T
) so that the perfect gas law,
P\propto\rho\,T
, turns into,

P\propto\rho\,\sigma_{v}^{2}.

(37)

\bullet
Relation between density and velocity dispersion.
Putting together Eqs. (
23
) and (
27
), one finds a one-to-one relation between
\rho
and
\sigma_{v}
, namely,

\rho(r)=\rho(0)\,[\sigma_{v}(r)/\sigma_{v}(0)]^{2m}.

(38)

2.2.2
Plane-of-the-sky projected polytropes

The polytropes describe a 3D mass distribution whereas the comparison with astronomical observations is often made in terms of the surface density projected in the plane of the sky (see Sects.
3
and
5
). Thus, the 2D projection of the polytropes
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
, called
propol
s by)
deserves analysis. The surface density
\Sigma(R)
corresponding to the volume density
\rho(r)
is given by its Abel transform, with
R
the projected distance from the center
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
, e.g.,)
. Then,
\Sigma(R)
can be expressed in terms of the normalized Abel transform
f(x,m)
,

\Sigma(R)=a\,f(R/b,m),

(39)

with

f(x,m)=2\,\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{s\,\psi^{m}(s)\,ds}{\sqrt{s^{2}-x^{2}}},

(40)

x=R/b
, and
a=b\,\rho(0)
. The variable
a
has units of surface density. In general, for an arbitrary index
m
, the function
f(x,m)
has to be evaluated numerically. However,
f
has a close analytic expression for
m=5

(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
)
, namely,

f(x,5)=\frac{4/3}{(1+x^{2})^{2}},

(41)

which has its half-mass radius at
x=1
. As it happens with the polytropes, all
propol
s show a central plateau
(see
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
, Fig. 1, as well as Fig.
3
in here)
.

3
Relation between polytropes and Sérsic profiles

The observed stellar mass surface density within galaxies drops with radial distance following a law approximately given by Sérsic functions
(
Sersic 1968
;
Graham and Driver 2005
)
,

\Sigma(R)=\Sigma(0)\,\exp{\large[}-c_{n}\,(R/R_{e})^{1/n}{\large]},

(42)

with
R_{e}
the radius enclosing half of the mass and
c_{n}
a constant which depends only on the so-called Sérsic index
n
. The Sérsic index controls the shape of the profile, and has been observed to vary from 0.5 to 6
(
Blanton et al. 2003
;
van der Wel et al. 2012
)
, approximately going from disk-like galaxies
(
de Jong and van der Kruit 1994
,
n\simeq 1
;)
to elliptical galaxies
(
de Vaucouleurs 1948
,
n\simeq 4
;)
.
The question arises as to whether the theoretical polytropes account for the empirical Sérsic profiles. Since Sérsic functions describe surface densities they have to be compared with
propol
s (Sect.
2.2.2
). Both
propol
s and Sérsic profiles present cores, however, they are not similar. Except for
n\lesssim 1
, the cores of the Sérsic profiles are too small compared with the cores of the
propol
s
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
)
. This is shown in Fig.
3
, which shows three different Sersic profiles covering the whole range of observed indexes together with fits to them using
propol
s. There is a large mismatch in the cores, but their outskirts are indistinguishable within any realistic observational error.
The equivalence between Sérsic index
n
and polytropic index
m
depends somewhat on the range of radii used for comparison, but it approximately goes from
m\simeq 2
for
n=0.5
to
m\simeq 5
for
n=6

(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
)
. Thus, the range of physically sensible polytropes (Eq. [
25
]) seem to naturally yield the range of observed Sérsic indexes
(
Blanton et al. 2003
;
van der Wel et al. 2012
)
. Because of the mismatch in the cores, it is at present unclear whether the range of observed Sérsic indexes truly arises from the range of sensible polytropic indexes.

Sérsic profiles describe stellar mass whereas
propol
s model total mass, i.e., gas, stars, and DM all together. For the similarity between Sérsic profiles and
propol
s to be of relevance for real galaxies, a scaling between stellar mass and total mass must exist. Fortunately, the similarity still holds even when the scaling is not a constant and the ratio between stellar and total mass varies radially
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
, see)
.

4
Relation between polytropes and CDM mass density profiles

According to the current cosmological model, the DM provides most of the gravitational force that drives galaxy formation and evolution. The DM particles in this model are
cold
, and only interact with each other and with the baryons through gravity
(
Peebles 2021
, for an up-to-date critical review, see)
. Given the expected low mass of the DM particles
(
Carr and Kühnel 2020
)
, gravitation alone cannot thermalize the particle distribution within a Hubble time
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
so the current DM mass distribution in galaxy haloes should still reflect the initial conditions.
Back in
Navarro et al. 1997
,
Navarro et al. 1997
found that the DM haloes in numerical simulations follow a universal law, now called NFW profile, where the density drops with radius
r
as

\rho_{\rm NFW}(r)=\frac{4\,\rho_{\rm NFW}(r_{s})}{(r/r_{s})(1+r/r_{s})^{2}},

(43)

where
r_{s}
is a characteristic radius. Because the NFW profile results from cold DM numerical simulations, its shape is commonly explained as the outcome of the cosmological initial conditions
(
Ascasibar et al. 2004
;
Cen 2014
;
Nipoti 2015
;
Ludlow and Angulo 2017
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
.
It is repeatedly found in literature that DM halos are reproduced by Einasto profiles as well as, if not better than, the NFW profiles
(
Navarro et al. 2004
;
Merritt et al. 2005
;
An and Zhao 2013
)
. The Einasto profiles,
\rho_{ei}
, are formally identical to a Sérsic profile but with the variable representing the 3D radial distance,
r
, rather than the projected distance,
R
, i.e.,

\rho_{ei}(r)=B\,\exp(-A\,r^{\mu}),

(44)

where
A
,
B
, and
\mu
are the three parameters that define the profile.

Cosmological numerical simulations of collision-less DM particles produce halos without core (Eq. [
43
]). However, when collisions are included, then the resulting DM haloes always develop a polytropic central core
(
Sánchez Almeida and Trujillo 2021
)
. There are two ways in which collisions between particles have been considered in simulations. One of them is actually an artifact due to the need of using DM particles with artificially large masses. Then the two-body gravitationally induced collisions become unrealistically important, and the inner DM halo has to be discarded to recover proper NFW profiles
(
Power et al. 2003
)
. On the other hand, collisions are imposed to model the so-called self-interacting DM
(
Spergel and Steinhardt 2000
)
, finding that the shape of the resulting halo has a core whose shape is independent of the assumed collisional cross-section
(
Elbert et al. 2015
)
. In both cases the resulting DM haloes have a polytropic core (see Fig.

). This result reinforces the ansatz that the Tsallis entropy is indeed adequate to describe self-gravitating systems in thermal equilibrium.

5
Observational support for galaxies reaching Tsallis maximum entropy

The use of Tsallis entropy is not new to the literature on galaxies, and it has already shown quite some success. It provides a common framework linking a number of seemingly disconnected observational properties of galaxies.
The mass density profiles observed in the centers of dwarf galaxies are very well reproduced by polytropes without any degree of freedom or tuning
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2020
)
. In other words, the so-called
core-cusp
problem of the CDM cosmology (see Sect.
1
) is automatically solved if the mass distribution within dwarfs is in TE as defined by the Tsallis entropy. Similar conclusions have also being found elsewhere
(
Novotný et al. 2021
)
.
The same type of profile also explains the stellar surface density profiles observed in globular clusters
(
Trujillo and Sánchez Almeida 2022
)
.

Claims in the literature that polytropes do not provide good fits to observed galaxies
(
Chavanis and Sire 2005
;
Féron and Hjorth 2008
, e.g.,)
have been disproved lately
(
Novotný et al. 2021
;
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. It has been shown that
propol
s account for the mass distribution in low mass galaxies (
M_{\star}<10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
), and they do it better than classical Sérsic profiles
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2021
)
. The goodness of
propol
s to reproduce the observed mass distribution in galaxies parallels the equivalence between
propol
s and Sérsic profiles analyzed in Sect.
3
. Although with scatter, the observed polytropic indexes increase with increasing mass and tend to cluster around
m=5
. For the most massive galaxies,
propol
s are very good at reproducing their central parts, but they do not handle well cores and outskirts overall. It seems like if the central parts are already in TE, a condition not reached in the outskirts yet
(
Henriksen 2004
)
.

The range of physically sensible polytropes (Eq. [
25
]) seems to naturally yield the range of observed Sérsic indexes
(
Blanton et al. 2003
;
van der Wel et al. 2012
, from 0.5 to 6;)
. As we discuss in Sect.
4
, because of the mismatch in the cores between
propol
s and Sérsics, it is at present unclear whether this agreement is pure coincidence or if it provides the long-sought explanation for why observed galaxies follow Sérsic profiles. Even with this caveat, we argue that the equivalence between sensible polytropic indexes and observed Sérsic indexes supports that galaxies develop polytropic mass distributions.

The star counts and the kinematic data of the Milky Way (MW) stellar halo are well represented by an Einasto profile with
\mu^{-1}\simeq 2
and an effective radius
\simeq 20
kpc
(
Evans and Williams 2014
)
. Because of the equivalence between Einasto profiles and polytropes (Sect.
4
and Fig.
4
), this MW halo corresponds to a polytrope with
m
between 4 and 5.

6
Pathways to thermalization

As we put forward in Sect.
5
, some observations suggest that TE, as described by the Tsallis entropy, sets the internal distribution of mass in some galaxies. Therefore, the arguments in Sect.
1
against galaxies being able to reach TE are questionable. In particular, there should be alternatives to the two-body relaxation collisions, a mechanism unable to thermalize the gravitational potential within the Hubble time. What are these alternative pathways to thermalization?

Violent relaxation

(
Lynden-Bell 1967
;
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
can do the work. If for some reason the self-gravitating system is driven far from equilibrium, then the gravitational potential varies in time, and this variation allows the particles to exchange their energy and momentum in a timescale comparable with the dynamical timescale for the variation of the potential. For instance, if the system starts off out the virial equilibrium and collapses, this timescale will be set by the free-fall time, which is much shorter than the Hubble time
5
5

5

For a MW-like halo of mass and radius
10^{13}{\rm M}_{\odot}
and 100 kpc, respectively, the free-fall time is only 0.2 Gyr.
. This idea permeates various physical processes invoked to turn the
cusps
expected from the CDM simulations into the observed
cores
. For example, the feedback of the baryons on the DM particles through gravitational forces
(
Governato et al. 2010
;
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Freundlich et al. 2020
)
. Supernova explosions suffice to expel a significant fraction of the gas existing in the central regions of dwarf galaxies in a very short timescale, changing the overall gravitational potential and forcing the DM distribution to readjust. Gas gets re-accreted, new stars are formed, and the process starts over rendering a cored density profile after several of these cycles.

Another pathway to thermalization may be through the mergers of SMBHs expected to occur at the center of massive ellipticals
(
López-Cruz et al. 2014
;
Mazzalay et al. 2016
)
. The motion of two merging black holes produces scouring of stars. In addition, the recoil kicks the merged SMBH out of the center, forcing a final swing of the SMBH that stirs the global gravitational potential
(
Merritt 2006
;
Nasim et al. 2021
)
. Thus, scouring plus recoil may allow the self-gravitating system to reach TE in a timescale much shorter than the two-body relaxation timescale. The fast scouring of the inner region can also be achieved through scattering with massive gas clumps
(
Elmegreen and Struck 2013
;
Struck and Elmegreen 2019
)
or by the forcing produced by a central bar
(
Hohl 1971
)
. We note that central BHs may also be present in dwarf galaxies
(
Mezcua 2017
;
Davis et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, therefore, this thermalizing process may work for them as well.

Another extremely interesting pathway to thermalization has to do with the unknown nature of the DM particles. Thermalization in a short timescale is possible if two-body collisions between particles are efficient enough. This would happen if DM particles had masses in the stellar-mass realm (e.g., if they are stellar-mass primordial BHs), a possibility seemingly discarded by observations
(
Carr and Kühnel 2020
)
. A more appealing possibility is DM not being collision-less, i.e., having an additional large DM particle – DM particle collision cross section that shortens the two-body collision timescale below the Hubble time
(
Spergel and Steinhardt 2000
;
Davé et al. 2001
;
Elbert et al. 2015
)
.
When the DM particles of numerical simulations are allowed to interact through any of these two mechanisms, it leads to a gravitational potential conforming with polytropes
(
Sánchez Almeida and Trujillo 2021
)
.

7
Conclusions

We still do not have a final answer to the original question of
why do real galaxies choose a particular mass distribution?

Here we have examined whether
thermodynamic equilibrium (TE) is setting the mass distribution
.
The standard answer discards the role of TE for a number of reasons.
Firstly, the TE described by the classical Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy produces mass distributions with unphysical properties (Sect.
2.1
).
Secondly, two-body gravitational collisions are quite inefficient and so TE cannot be set and should not be relevant (Sect.
1
).
Finally, the DM halos coming from numerical simulations (i.e., the NFW profile and its relatives) have a mass distribution set by initial conditions rather than by TE (Sect.
1
).
However, these arguments are questionable for a number of reasons.
Firstly, the Boltzmann-Gibbs entropy does not describe systems with long range interaction. When the Tsallis entropy is used to define TE, then the resulting mass distribution (polytropes; Sect.
2.2
) turns out to be physically sensible.
Secondly, there are alternatives to the two-body collisions to thermalize the potential. They go from feedback of baryons on DM through gravity to the stirring of the potential produced by the merging of SMBHs (Sect.
6
).
Thirdly, the standard DM halos produced in numerical simulations assume collision-less DM particles thus, by construction, they have to reflect initial conditions rather than TE. However, when collisions are allowed, the numerical haloes are consistent with polytropes and so with TE (Sect.
4
). This result reinforces the ansatz that the Tsallis entropy is indeed adequate to describe self-gravitating systems in thermal equilibrium.

In addition to the above arguments rebutting the original criticisms, a number of observations indicate that polytropic profiles reproduce real galaxies (Sect.
5
). In particular, the outskirts of plane-of-the-sky projected politropes are extremely similar to Sérsic profiles (Sect.
3
), and they do reproduce the stellar mass distribution in galaxies. Even the halo of the MW seems to be a polytrope. Moreover, the cores in the observed mass distribution of dwarf galaxies are polytropes so solving the so-called
core – cusp
problem seems to be equivalent to explaining why the mass distribution in these objects is thermalized (Sects.
1
and
5
).

Section
2.2
summarizes a number of properties of polytropes that are of interest in the context of galaxy structure. In particular, the cores of all polytropes have the same shape, independently of polytropic index
m
(Eq. [
33
]), the velocity dispersion scales as a power of the density (Eq. [
38
]), and there is a fairly narrow range of valid values of
m
(Eq. [
25
]).

As a way to foretell future developments in the field, I conclude with a few interesting and open lines of research. Ordered from more general to more specific, they are:
(1) From first principles, derive the entropy describing self-gravitating systems
(
Tsallis 2011
;
Amigó et al. 2018
)
.
(2) Include angular momentum in the polytropic formalism
(
Barnes et al. 1986
;
Nguyen and Lingam 2013
;
Herpich et al. 2017
)
.
(3) Are the haloes in fully DM dominated galaxies thermalized? I so, then the DM cannot be collision-less as assumed in the standard cosmological model
(
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Oñorbe et al. 2015
;
Peebles 2021
)
.
(5) Observationally, the central mass surface density in galaxies seems to have an upper limit
(
Salucci and Burkert 2000
;
Burkert 2020
,
\Sigma(0)\lesssim 75\,{\rm M_{\odot}\,pc^{-2}}
, e.g.)
. Is this a consequence of the galaxies being polytropes
(
Chavanis 2002
)
?
