Uniform Distribution of Hardy Sums
Alessandro Lägeler

Abstract
. We employ the spectral theory of Eisenstein series to prove that the Hardy sums, integer-valued analogs of the classical Dedekind sums, are uniformly distributed in 𝐙 / m ​ 𝐙 \mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z} for any integer m > 1 m>1 .



1.
Introduction

The classical Dedekind sums

(1.1)

s(d,c)=\frac{1}{4c}\sum_{k=1}^{|c|-1}\cot\frac{\pi k}{c}\cot\frac{\pi kd}{c},\;(d,c)=1,

have been objects of interest ever since Dedekind first introduced them in his study
[
3
]
of the transformation behavior of the logarithm of
\eta(z)=e^{\frac{\pi iz}{12}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{2\pi inz}\right)
. A lot is known about their distributional behavior: Hickerson
[
6
]
showed that the set
\{(d/c,\;s(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c>0\}
is dense in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}
, a result of Vardi
[
15
]
asserts that their fractional parts are equidistributed on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
, and Myerson
[
11
]
strenghtened Vardi’s result to
(d/c,\;s(d,c))
being equidistributed in
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
.

A natural analog of the Dedekind sums in (
1.1
) are the so-called Hardy sums, which arise from the transformation behavior of the logarithm of
\theta
-functions. More precisely, let
\theta(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}e^{\pi in^{2}z}
and
\theta_{4}(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}(-1)^{n}e^{\pi in^{2}z}
. Their logarithms transform as

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\log\theta(g.z)-\log\theta(z)&=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{i}\right)+\frac{\pi i}{4}S(d,c)\\
&\mathrm{for}\;g={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}),\;c>0,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd},\end{split}

and

\displaystyle\log\theta_{4}(g.z)-\log\theta_{4}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{cz+d}{i}\right)-\frac{\pi i}{4}S_{4}(d,c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{for}\;g={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}),\;c>0,\;d\;\mathrm{odd},

where
S(d,c)
and
S_{4}(d,c)
are integer-valued correction factors. Berndt
[
1
]
proved that these correction factors have the following representation as finite sums:

(1.3)

S(d,c)=\sum_{k=1}^{|c|-1}(-1)^{k+1+\left\lfloor\frac{dk}{c}\right\rfloor},\;\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd},

and

(1.4)

S_{4}(d,c)=\sum_{k=1}^{|c|-1}(-1)^{\left\lfloor\frac{dk}{c}\right\rfloor},\;\;(d,c)=1,\;d\;\mathrm{odd},

which we refer to as ”Hardy sums” (as they already appeared in early work of Hardy
[
5
]
).

Equivalently to (
1.2
), the Hardy sum
S(d,c)
might be viewed in terms of the multiplier system of
\theta(z)^{8r}=e^{8r\log\theta(z)}
for a rational number
0<r<1
under the action of the group

\Gamma_{\theta}=\left\{{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}):(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\right\}.

Namely, the function
\theta(z)^{8r}
transforms for
g={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma_{\theta}
like
\theta(g.z)^{8r}=\nu_{r}(g)(cz+d)^{4r}\theta(z)^{8r}
, where

(1.5)

\nu_{r}(g)=\begin{cases}e^{2\pi ir\left(S(d,c)-\mathrm{sign}(c)\right)},&c\neq 0,\\
e^{2\pi ir(\mathrm{sign}(d)-1)},&c=0.\end{cases}

Similarly as for the Dedekind sums
s(d,c)
, it is known that the sets
\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c>0,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
and
\{(d/c,\;S_{4}(d,c)):(d,c)=1,\;c>0,\;d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
are dense in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{Z}
; see
[
9
,
8
]
. However, an analog of Vardi’s result for
s(d,c)
being equidistributed in
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
has been missing so far, a gap which we would like to fill with this note.

To show that a real sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is equidistributed in
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
is equivalent to showing that for each
m\neq 0
the Weyl sums

(1.6)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e(ma_{n})\to 0,\;\mathrm{as}\;N\to+\infty,

where we set
e(z)=e^{2\pi iz}
as usual.

As the Hardy sums are integer-valued, the question of equidistribution on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
is trivial – clearly, they are not equidistributed. Hence, instead of considering the Lebesgue-measurable set
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
we look at the distribution of
S(d,c)
and
S_{4}(d,c)
on the natural analog
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
for
m>1
with the counting measure. We get the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m>1
be an integer.

(1)

The set

\{(d/c,\;S(d,c)\pmod{m}):1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}

is uniformly distributed on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
as
c\to+\infty
.

(2)

The set

\{(d/c,\;S_{4}(d,c)\pmod{m}):1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1,\;d\;\mathrm{odd}\}

is uniformly distributed on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
as
c\to+\infty
.

As an immediate corollary to Theorem
1.1
we get that the sets
\{S(d,c)\pmod{m}:1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
and
\{S_{4}(d,c)\pmod{m}:1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1,\;d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
are both uniformly distributed on
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
as
c\to+\infty
.

It should be noted that the distribution in
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
of the sequence of the integer-valued Rademacher symbols

\Psi\left({\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\right)=\frac{a+d}{c}-12\;s(d,c)-3\mathrm{sign}(c(a+d)),\;{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}),

ordered by absolute value of the trace is known to be uniform
[
14
]
. Moreover, other distributional results have been obtained for the Rademacher symbol
\Psi
by Mozzochi
[
10
]
, based on an idea of Sarnak (see also Von Essen’s generalization to other multiplier systems
[
16
]
, which is relevant in our case).

In the next section, we will recall the generalization of equidistribution to compact abelian groups. The first statement of Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to showing that for all
n\in\mathbf{Z}
and every rational number
0<r<1
:

(1.7)

\frac{1}{\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)}\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(-n\frac{d}{c}+rS(d,c)\right)\to 0,\;\mathrm{as}\;N\to+\infty,

where
\sum_{d(c)^{*}}
means that we sum over
d\pmod{c}
so that
(d,c)=1
and

\Phi_{\theta}(N)=\{d/c:1\leq d<c\leq N,\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}.

The second part of Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the correspondent statement for
S_{4}(d,c)
in (
1.4
). As the methods are virtually the same for
S(d,c)
and
S_{4}(d,c)
, we will only focus on the Hardy sum
S(d,c)
in this note.

The idea for the proof is straightforward: The Dirichlet series of the Weyl sums in (
1.7
) appear in the Fourier expansion of certain Eisenstein series of weight
4r
with multiplier system
\nu_{r}
given in (
1.5
). The spectral theory of Eisenstein series gives the analytic continuation of these Dirichlet series and information about its poles. Analytically, this encodes information about the growth rate of the Weyl sums (
1.7
).

In section
2
, we will first recall the notion of equidistribution on
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
, give the asymptotic growth rate of
\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)
, and then prove Theorem
1.1
elementarily for
m=2
. In section
3
, we recall the some facts from the spectral theory of Eisenstein series, which we will use to give the estimates of the Weyl sums for the case
m>2
. In section
4
we apply Perron’s formula to prove that the exponential sums in (
1.7
) grow slower than
\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)
, hence proving Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Weyl Sums

2.1.
Equidistribution on Compact Abelian Groups

The theory of equidistribution generalizes to the more general setting of compact abelian groups.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a compact abelian group. A sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
is called equidistributed in
G
if the measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{g_{n}}
converges weakly to the Haar measure
\mu
in
G
as
N\to+\infty
, i.e. if for every continuous function
f:G\to\mathbf{C}
we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(g_{n})\to\int_{G}f(g)d\mu(g),\;\mathrm{as}\;N\to+\infty.

Equidistribution modulo
1
is a special case of Definition
2.1
for the compact abelian group
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
(the Haar measure on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
is the Lebesgue measure). In our discussion of the integer-valued Hardy sums, we will then no longer consider
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
, but the compact abelian group
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
for an integer
m>1
instead. The Haar measure on
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
is the counting measure.

Theorem 2.2

(Eckmann
[
2
]
)
.

Let
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
be a sequence in a compact abelian group
G
. The sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
is equidistributed in
G
if and only if
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\chi(g_{n})\to 0
as
N\to+\infty
for all non-trivial characters
\chi
on
G
.

The Weyl criterion (
1.6
) is a special case of Theorem
2.2
, as the non-trivial characters on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
are given by
\{e(nx):n\neq 0\}
. In the case
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
for
m>1
, the non-trivial characters are given by
n\mapsto e\left(\frac{j}{m}n\right)
for
j=1,...,m-1
; see
[
12
,
13
]
.

To show equidistribution of the Hardy sums
S(d,c)
on, say,
\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}
, we must show that

(2.1)

\frac{1}{\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)}\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{1}{2}S(d,c)\right)\to 0,\;\mathrm{as}\;N\to+\infty,

and, more generally, that

\frac{1}{\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)}\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(rS(d,c)\right)\to 0,\;\mathrm{as}\;N\to+\infty

for each rational
0<r<1
to show equidistribution of
S(d,c)
on
\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
for all
m>1
.

Finally, for the coupled equidistribution in the first part of Theorem
1.1
we need to prove (
1.7
).

2.2.
Estimating
\Phi_{\theta}(N)

Naturally, to prove equidistribution we need to understand the growth rate of the function

(2.2)

\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)=\#\{d/c:1\leq d<c\leq N,\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}.

We may also write the function
\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)
as a sum of special Euler-totient functions

\varphi_{\theta}(c)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}1=\begin{cases}\varphi(n),&\mbox{if }c\;\mathrm{even},\\
\frac{1}{2}\varphi(n),&\mbox{if }c\;\mathrm{odd}.\end{cases}

Note that
\varphi_{\theta}(c)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(-nd/c\right)
for
n=0
. In this notation, we have
\#\Phi_{\theta}(N)=\sum_{c=1}^{N}\varphi_{\theta}(c)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have
\Phi_{\theta}(N)\asymp N^{2}
as
N\to+\infty
.

Proof.

First, we notice that

(2.3)

\Phi_{\theta}(N)=\sum_{c=1}^{N}\varphi_{\theta}(c)=\sum_{c=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\varphi(2c)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\varphi(2c-1).

Let us denote the first sum on the right hand side of (
2.3
) by
\Lambda_{1}(N)
and the second sum by
\Lambda_{2}(N)
.

We get for
\Lambda_{1}(N)
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\varphi(2k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\sum_{dd^{\prime}=2k}d^{\prime}\mu(d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}^{N}\mu(d)\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{\left\lfloor N/d\right\rfloor}d^{\prime}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\mu(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=1,\\
d^{\prime}\;\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor N/d\right\rfloor}d^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\mu(d)\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2d\right\rfloor}d^{\prime}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\mu(d)\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2d\right\rfloor}d^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\mu(d)\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{\left\lfloor N/2d\right\rfloor}d^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor}\mu(d)\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2d}\right\rfloor\left(\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2d}\right\rfloor+1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{8}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{2}}+o(N^{2}).

Similarly, one can show that
\Lambda_{2}(N)=\frac{N^{2}}{8}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d=1,\\
d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{2}}+o(N^{2})
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Since the asymptotic leading terms of
\Lambda_{1}(N)
and
\Lambda_{2}(N)
in the proof of Lemma
2.3
are equal, we have
\Lambda_{1}(N)-\Lambda_{2}(N)=o(N^{2})
as
N\to+\infty
.

Thus, by Lemma
2.3
, to prove Theorem
1.1
, we need to show that for all rational numbers
0<r<1
and
n\neq 0
the Weyl sums

(2.4)

\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e(-nd/c+rS(d,c))=o(N^{2})

as
N\to+\infty
.

2.3.
The case
m=2

We start by reviewing the proof of the equidistribution of the sequence
\{d/c:1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1\}
in
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
. The proof amounts to finding non-trivial cancellation in the Ramanujan sums
R_{c}(n)=\sum_{d(c)^{*}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)
. This follows from

(2.5)

R_{c}(n)\leq n\;\mathrm{for\;all\;}n\neq 0,

which can easily be seen from the von Sterneck formula
R_{c}(n)=\mu\left(\frac{c}{(c,n)}\right)\frac{\varphi(c)}{\varphi\left(\frac{c}{(c,n)}\right)}
, where
\mu
is the Möbius-function. Hence,

(2.6)

\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{d(c)^{*}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}Nn\to 0,\;\mathrm{as}\;N\to+\infty,

by (
2.5
) and thus
\{d/c:1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1\}
is equidistributed on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
.

Let us now consider the sequence
\{d/c:1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
instead. This sequence is also equidistributed in
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
. Consider the Weyl sums

(2.7)

\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\leq N,\\
c\;\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{d(c)^{*}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\leq N,\\
c\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
d\;\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)

for
n\neq 0
. The first sum on the right hand side of (
2.7
) is seen to be a sum of Ramanujan sums
R_{c}(n)
, which is
o(N^{2})
by (
2.6
). For the second sum, it is easy to show that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
d\;\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}e\left(-n\frac{d}{c}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<d<\left\lfloor\frac{c}{2}\right\rfloor,\\
(d,c)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(-2n\frac{d}{c}\right)\leq\frac{|R_{c}(2n)|+1}{\left|1+e\left(-2n\frac{\left\lfloor c/2\right\rfloor}{\left\lfloor c/2\right\rfloor+1}\right)\right|}\ll n

uniformly in
c
. This proves that the Weyl sum
\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(n\frac{d}{c}\right)=o(N^{2})
as well and thus proves equidistribution of
\{d/c:1\leq d<c,\;(d,c)=1,\;c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\}
on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
.

We now give an elementary proof of the equidistribution of
(d/c,S(d,c)\pmod{2})
on
\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}
. To this end, we need to bound the Weyl sum

\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(nd/c\right)e^{\pi iS(d,c)}.

As we showed in a previous paper
[
8
, Lem. 4.1]
, the Hardy sum
S(d,c)
is odd when
c
is even and
S(d,c)
is even when
c
is odd.

1

Similarly, for the value of
S_{4}(d,c)
for

,
(d,c)=1
, and

.
Thus, we have

\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\

For

Albeit it is probably possible to give an elementary proof of Theorem

3.
Eisenstein Series with Multiplier Systems

Let
0<r<1
denote a rational number. Our main tool to prove the uniform distribution of Hardy sums will be the theory of Eisenstein series with a multiplier system. By a multiplier system of weight
r
on a subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
we mean a function
\nu:\Gamma\to\mathbf{C}
such that the cocycle
j(g,z)=cz+d
with
g={\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\in\Gamma
satisfies
\nu(gh)j(gh,z)^{r}=\nu(g)\nu(h)j(g,h.z)^{r}j(h,z)^{r}
for all
g,h\in\Gamma
.

An example of a multiplier system
\nu_{r}
for
\Gamma_{\theta}
was already given in (
1.5
), which is the multiplier system of the weight
4r
modular form
\theta(z)^{8r}=e^{8r\log\theta(z)}
under
\Gamma_{\theta}
. It is not difficult to see that
\nu_{1/2}
is a multiplicative homomorphism.

For the group
\Gamma_{\theta}
, let
\Gamma_{\infty}<\Gamma_{\theta}
denote the subgroup of its parabolic elements, i.e. the subgroup generated by
\pm{\tiny\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}}
. The multiplier system
\nu_{r}
is singular with respect to the cusp
i\infty
, i.e.
\nu_{r}\left({\tiny\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}}\right)=1
(see also
[
8
, Prop. 3.4]
). We may hence view
\nu_{r}
as a function on the cosets
\Gamma_{\infty}\setminus\Gamma
.

For a multiplier system
\nu
, consider the following Dirichlet series:

(3.1)

Z_{\nu}(n,s)=\sum_{c>0}\frac{1}{c^{2s}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(-n\frac{d}{c}\right)\nu\left({\tiny\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}}\right).

For our bounds on the Weyl sums (
1.7
), we will be interested in the special case

Z_{r}(n,s)=Z_{\nu_{r}}(n,s)=e^{-2\pi ir}\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{c^{2s}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e\left(-n\frac{d}{c}+rS(d,c)\right).

For a multiplier system
\nu
of weight
4r
, which is singular with respect to
i\infty
, define

(3.2)

E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)=\sum_{g\in\Gamma_{\infty}\setminus\Gamma_{\theta}}\overline{\nu(g)}j(g,z)^{-4r}\mathrm{Im}(g.z)^{s-2r},\;\mathrm{Re}(s)>1,

which is the non-holomorphic Eisenstein series of weight
4r
and multiplier system
\nu
. The series is absolutely convergent for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
and uniformly convergent on compact subsets of
\mathbf{H}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The Fourier expansion of the non-holomorphic Eisenstein series is given by

E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)=y^{s-2r}+\varphi_{4r,\nu}(s)y^{1-s-2r}+\sum_{n\neq 0}y^{-2r}\varphi_{4r,\nu}(n,s)W_{\mathrm{sign}(n)2r,s-1/2}(2\pi|n|y)e^{\pi inx},

where

\displaystyle\varphi_{4r,\nu}(s)

\displaystyle=\pi\frac{2^{2-2s}\Gamma(2s-1)e^{2\pi ir}}{\Gamma(s-2r)\Gamma(s+2r)}Z_{\nu}(0,s),

\displaystyle\varphi_{4r,\nu}(n,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{s}|n|^{s-1}e^{2\pi ir}}{\Gamma(s+\mathrm{sign}(n)2r)}Z_{\nu}(n,s),

and
W_{\kappa,\mu}(z)
denotes the Whittaker
W
-function.

Proof.

See, for instance, Fay’s paper
[
4
, Thm. 3.4]
; note that the Eisenstein series considered in Fay’s paper is
y^{-4r}E_{-4r}(z,s;\nu)
in our notation.
∎

Proposition
3.1
is the special case of the Fourier expansion around the cusp
\mathfrak{a}=i\infty
. More generally, for any cusp
\mathfrak{a}
of
\Gamma_{\theta}
, let
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
be its scaling matrix such that
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}.i\infty=\mathfrak{a}
. The Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
has a Fourier expansion around
\mathfrak{a}
given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}j(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}},z)^{4r}E_{4r}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}.z,s;\nu)&=\delta_{\mathfrak{a},\infty}\mathrm{Im}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}.z)^{s-2r}+\varphi_{4r,\mathfrak{a},\nu}(s)\mathrm{Im}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}.z)^{1-s-2r}\\
&+\sum_{n\neq 0}y^{-2r}\varphi_{4r,\mathfrak{a},\nu}(n,s)W_{\mathrm{sign}(n)2r,s-\frac{1}{2}}(4\pi|n|y)e^{\pi inx}\end{split}

for certain functions
\varphi_{4r,\mathfrak{a},\nu}(s)
and
\varphi_{4r,\mathfrak{a},\nu}(n,s)
.

It is well-known that the Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
has an analytic continuation in
s
to the whole complex plane. From the Fourier expansion in Proposition
3.1
, the poles of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
correspond to poles of the analytically continued Dirichlet series
Z_{\nu}(n,s)
or – e.g. in the case
s=\frac{1}{2}
– to poles of the
\Gamma
-factors. For a pole
s_{0}
of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
, we shall be interested in giving an upper bound for
\mathrm{Re}(s_{0})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose the Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
has a pole at
s_{0}\in\mathbf{C}
with
\mathrm{Re}(s_{0})>\frac{1}{2}
. Then its residue
\mathrm{Res}_{s=s_{0}}E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
is a non-zero square-integrable eigenfunction of
\Delta_{4r}
with eigenvalue
(s_{0}-2r)(1-s_{0}-2r)
.

Proof.

This can be seen from looking at the Fourier expansion of the residues around each cusp
\mathfrak{a}
of
\Gamma_{\theta}
in (
3.3
).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

For
r=\frac{1}{8}
the Eisenstein series
E_{1/2}(z,s;\nu_{r})
has a pole at
s=\frac{3}{4}
and its residue is equal to
\theta(z)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

The Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
is holomorphic on the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\max\{1-2r,2r\}
.

Proof.

Suppose
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
has a pole at
s_{0}\in\mathbf{C}
. By Proposition
3.2
, the residue of the Eisenstein series at
s_{0}
is a non-zero square-integrable function
\varphi(z)
with eigenvalue
(s_{0}-2r)(1-s_{0}-2r)
under
\Delta_{4r}
, i.e. a Maass form. Each Maass form has non-negative eigenvalue. An elementary analysis of the polynomial
(s-2r)(1-s-2r)=-s^{2}+s+4r^{2}\geq 0
gives that for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}
the point
s
needs to be real and that its value will be negative if
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\max\{1-2r,2r\}
. Hence, we must have
\mathrm{Re}(s_{0})\leq\max\{1-2r,2r\}
.
∎

Generally, the Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
has a pole at
s=\max\{1-2r,2r\}
for
0<r<\frac{1}{2}
. The Eisenstein series
E_{2}(z,s;\nu)
does not have a pole at
s=1
and is a harmonic function at that point.

Moreover, Proposition
3.4
does not give any new information about the poles of the Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
in the strip
0<\mathrm{Re}(s)<1
for
\frac{1}{2}<r<1
. However, using lowering operators, we can easily get information about the poles of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
in the strip through Proposition
3.4
.

Corollary 3.5
.

For
\frac{1}{2}<r<1
, the Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
is holomorphic for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>2r-1
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
3.4
, using

2iy^{2}\partial_{\overline{z}}E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)=(s-2r)E_{4r-2}(z,s;\nu)

and looking at the Laurent-expansion around a pole of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
.
∎

From the Fourier expansion of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
we see that if the Eisenstein series is holomorphic at some point
s
with
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}
, then the Dirichlet series
Z_{\nu}(n,s)
are holomorphic at
s
as well. We rephrase Proposition
3.4
and Corollary
3.5
in a separate corollary in the special case of the Dirichlet series
Z_{r}(n,s)
, which we will apply in the next section.

Corollary 3.6
.

The functions
Z_{r}(n,s)
are holomorphic for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>\max\{1-2r,2r\}
for
0<r<\frac{1}{2}
and for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>2r-1
for
\frac{1}{2}<r<1
.

The Eisenstein series
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
as such is not square-integrable with respect to the Petersson inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}\setminus\mathbf{H}}f(z)\overline{g(z)}y^{4r}\frac{dxdy}{y^{2}},

the problematic part of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
being the zeroth Fourier coefficient in (
3.3
). To make it square-integrable, we consider the truncated Eisenstein series instead.

Let
Y>0
. For each cusp
\mathfrak{a}
, let
F_{\mathfrak{a}}(Y)=\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}.F_{Y}
be the cuspidal zone around
\mathfrak{a}
, where
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
is such that
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}.i\infty=\mathfrak{a}
and
F_{Y}=\{z\in\mathbf{H}:-1<\mathrm{Re}(s)<1,\mathrm{Im}(z)>\max\{1,Y\}\}
. We define the truncated Eisenstein series by

E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu)=\begin{cases}E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)-\delta_{\mathfrak{a},\infty}\mathrm{Im}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}.z)^{s-2r}-\varphi_{4r,\mathfrak{a},\nu}(s)\mathrm{Im}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}.z)^{1-s-2r},&z\in F_{\mathfrak{a}}(Y),\\
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu),&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The truncated Eisenstein series
E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu)
is now a square-integrable function on
\Gamma_{\theta}\setminus\mathbf{H}
of weight
4r
. Moreover, it satisfies the Maass-Selberg relations.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
with
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
be regular points of
E_{4r}(z,s;\nu)
and let
E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu)
be the truncated Eisenstein series. We have

\displaystyle\langle E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s_{1};\nu),E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s_{2};\nu)\rangle

\displaystyle=(s_{1}-s_{2})^{-1}\varphi_{4r,\nu}(s_{1})+(s_{2}-s_{1})^{-1}\varphi_{4r,\nu}(s_{2})

\displaystyle+(s_{1}+s_{2}-1)^{-1}Y^{s_{1}+s_{2}-1}-(s_{1}+s_{2}-1)^{-1}\varphi_{4r,\nu}(s_{1})\overline{\varphi_{4r,\nu}(s_{2})}.

Proof.

This is an application of Green’s theorem; see, e.g., Iwaniec’s book
[
7
, Prop. 6.8]
.
∎

The Maass-Selberg relations in the case
\nu=\nu_{r}
give a growth estimate for
Z_{r}(n,\sigma+it)
with
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<\frac{3}{2}
as
t\to\pm\infty
, which we will use for giving a growth estimate for the Weyl sums (
1.7
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma<\frac{3}{2}
and
n\in\mathbf{Z}
. We have

Z_{r}(n,\sigma+it)\ll_{\sigma,\;r,\;n}\sqrt{|t|}

as
|t|\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Write
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma\in\mathbf{R}
and
t>0
. From Theorem
3.7
for
\nu=\nu_{r}
with
s_{1}=s
and
s_{2}=\overline{s}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}\setminus\mathbf{H}}|E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu_{r})|^{2}d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma-1}\left(Y^{2\sigma-1}+|\varphi_{4r,\nu_{r}}(s)|^{2}Y^{1-2\sigma}\right)+\frac{1}{it}\left(\overline{\varphi_{4r,\nu_{r}}(s)}Y^{it}+\varphi_{4r,\nu_{r}}(s)Y^{-it}\right),

from which it follows immediately that
\varphi_{4r,\nu_{r}}(s)
is bounded as
|t|\to+\infty
. By Stirling’s approximation, we have

\frac{\Gamma(2s-1)}{\Gamma(s+2r)\Gamma(s-2r)}\sim\frac{|t|^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\;\mathrm{as}\;t\to\pm\infty,

hence the claim for
n=0
follows by Proposition
3.1
.

To prove the assertion for
Z_{r}(n,s)
with
n\neq 0
, we again use the Maass-Selberg relations to estimate for
Y>1
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}\setminus\mathbf{H}}|E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu_{r})|^{2}y^{4r}\frac{dxdy}{y^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{Y}^{\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}|E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu_{r})|^{2}y^{4r}\frac{dxdy}{y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\neq 0}\left|\frac{\pi^{s}|m|^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s+\mathrm{sign}(m)2r)}\right|^{2}|Z_{r}(m,s)|^{2}\int_{Y}^{\infty}|W_{\mathrm{sign}(m)2r,s-\frac{1}{2}}(4\pi|m|y)|^{2}\frac{dy}{y^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\left|\frac{\pi^{s}|n|^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s+\mathrm{sign}(n)2r)}\right|^{2}|Z_{r}(n,s)|^{2}\int_{Y}^{Y+1}|W_{\mathrm{sign}(n)2r,s-\frac{1}{2}}(4\pi|n|y)|^{2}\frac{dy}{y^{2}}.

Using the asymptotic formula

W_{\mathrm{sign}(n)2r,s-\frac{1}{2}}(4\pi|n|y)\sim\pi^{-1/2}\Gamma(\mathrm{sign}(n)2r+s-1/2)(\pi|n|y)^{1-s}

as
|t|\to+\infty
(which converges uniformly for
y
in a compact set), we see that

(3.4)

\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}\setminus\mathbf{H}}|E_{4r}^{Y}(z,s;\nu_{r})|^{2}y^{4r}\frac{dxdy}{y^{2}}\gg\left|\frac{\Gamma(s+\mathrm{sign}(n)2r-1/2)}{\Gamma(s+\mathrm{sign}(n)2r)}\right|^{2}|Z_{r}(n,s)|^{2}\int_{Y}^{Y+1}\frac{1}{y^{2\sigma}}dy.

The left hand side of (
3.4
) is bounded by what we showed in the first part of the claim. Again, by Stirling’s approximation, the claim follows for
n\neq 0
.
∎

4.
Uniform Distribution of Hardy Sums

In this section, we will use the results from section
3
to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Let
0<r<1
be a rational number and
n\in\mathbf{Z}
. Since the case
r=\frac{1}{2}
was already proved in section
2.3
, we may assume that
r\neq\frac{1}{2}
.

To work out the explicit growth rate of the Weyl sums, we will use Perron’s formula, i.e. that for any
\alpha>1
, we have

(4.1)

\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e(nd/c+rS(d,c))=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\alpha-i\infty}^{\alpha+i\infty}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
T>0
,
\alpha=1+\varepsilon
, and
s_{1},...,s_{l}\in\mathbf{C}
be the location of the poles of
Z_{r}(n,s)
. Using Cauchy’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\alpha-iT}^{\alpha+iT}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon+iT}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds+\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon+iT}^{\alpha+iT}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds

\displaystyle-\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon-iT}^{\alpha-iT}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Res}_{s=s_{j}}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s_{j}}}{2s_{j}}.

The integrals along the horizontal lines satisfy

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\alpha-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon-iT}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds\right|\leq\int_{\alpha-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon-iT}\left|Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}\right|ds\leq\frac{N^{2\alpha}}{T}\int_{\alpha-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon-iT}\left|Z_{r}(n,s)\right|ds\ll\frac{N^{2\alpha}}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}}

as
T\to+\infty
by Lemma
3.8
. The integral along the vertical line
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon+i\mathbf{R}
can, again by Lemma
3.8
, be bounded by

(4.2)

\left|\int_{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon-iT}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon+iT}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s}}{2s}ds\right|\ll_{r,\;\varepsilon,\;n}N^{1+\varepsilon}T^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, we get

\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(c)^{*},\\
c+d\;\mathrm{odd}\end{subarray}}e(nd/c+rS(d,c))=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{Res}_{s=s_{j}}Z_{r}(n,s)\frac{N^{2s_{j}}}{2s_{j}}+O\left(N^{2+\varepsilon}/T^{1/2}+N^{1+\varepsilon}T^{1/2}\right)

as
T\to+\infty
. Theorem
1.1
then follows by Corollary
3.6
and choosing
T=N
.
