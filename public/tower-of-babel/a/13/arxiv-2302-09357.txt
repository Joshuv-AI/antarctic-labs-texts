Stochastic Online Instrumental Variable Regression: Regrets for Endogeneity and Bandit Feedback
Riccardo Della Vecchia, Debabrota Basu

Abstract
Endogeneity, i.e. the dependence of noise and covariates, is a common phenomenon in real data due to omitted variables, strategic behaviours, measurement errors etc. In contrast, the existing analyses of stochastic online linear regression with unbounded noise and linear bandits depend heavily on exogeneity, i.e. the independence of noise and covariates. Motivated by this gap, we study the over- and just-identified Instrumental Variable (IV) regression, specifically Two-Stage Least Squares, for stochastic online learning , and propose to use an online variant of Two-Stage Least Squares, namely 𝖮 ​ 2 ​ 𝖲𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{O\text{2}SLS} . We show that 𝖮 ​ 2 ​ 𝖲𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{O\text{2}SLS} achieves 𝒪 ​ ( d 𝒙 ​ d 𝒛 ​ log 2 ​ T ) \text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T) identification and 𝒪 ~ ​ ( γ ​ d 𝒛 ​ T ) \widetilde{\text{O}}(\gamma\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T}) oracle regret after T T interactions, where d 𝒙 d_{\boldsymbol{x}} and d 𝒛 d_{\boldsymbol{z}} are the dimensions of covariates and IVs, and γ \gamma is the bias due to endogeneity. For γ = 0 \gamma=0 , i.e. under exogeneity, 𝖮 ​ 2 ​ 𝖲𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{O\text{2}SLS} exhibits 𝒪 ​ ( d 𝒙 2 ​ log 2 ​ T ) \text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}\log^{2}T) oracle regret, which is of the same order as that of the stochastic online ridge. Then, we leverage 𝖮 ​ 2 ​ 𝖲𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{O\text{2}SLS} as an oracle to design 𝖮𝖥𝖴𝖫 ​ - ​ 𝖨𝖵 \mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV} , a stochastic linear bandit algorithm to tackle endogeneity. 𝖮𝖥𝖴𝖫 ​ - ​ 𝖨𝖵 \mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV} yields 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d 𝒙 ​ d 𝒛 ​ T ) \widetilde{\text{O}}(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T}) regret that matches the regret lower bound under exogeneity. For different datasets with endogeneity, we experimentally show efficiencies of 𝖮 ​ 2 ​ 𝖲𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{O\text{2}SLS} and 𝖮𝖥𝖴𝖫 ​ - ​ 𝖨𝖵 \mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV} .

(Fig.

Like this example, online regression is a founding component of online learning
[

[

]

[

In linear regression, the
outcome
(or output variable)
Y\in\mathbb{R}
, and the
input features
(or covariates, or treatments)
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Y=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\eta
,
where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the

and
\eta
is the observational noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
.

The goal is to estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}
from an observational dataset.

Two common assumptions in the analysis of linear regression are (i) bounded observations and covariates
[
Vovk 1997
,
Bartlett et al. 2015
,
Gaillard et al. 2019
]
, and (ii)
exogeneity
, i.e. independence of the noise
\eta
and the input features
\mathbf{X}
(
\mathbb{E}[\eta|\mathbf{X}]=0
)
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011b
,
Ouhamma et al. 2021
]
. Under exogeneity, researchers have studied scenarios where the observational noise is unbounded and has only bounded variance
\sigma^{2}
. But this setting asks for a different technical analysis than the bounded adversarial setting popular in online regression literature. For example,
Ouhamma et al. 2021
analyse online forward and online ridge regressions in the unbounded stochastic setting.

Instrumental Variable (IV) regression.
Historically, IVs are introduced to identify and quantify the causal effects of endogenous covariates
[
Newey and Powell 2003
]
. IVs are widely used in economics
[
Wright 1928
,
Mogstad et al. 2021
]
, causal inference
[
Rubin 1974
,
Hernan and Robins 2020
,
Harris et al. 2022
]
, bio-statistics and epidemiology
[
Burgess et al. 2017
]
.
One popular framework is to choose IVs which heavily influence the covariates but are independent of the exogenous noise.
Following that, one aims to learn the relations between IVs and covariates in one stage, then uses the predicted values of covariates to learn the relation between covariates and the outcome variables in the next stage.
This approach to conducting two stages of linear regression using IVs is called
Two Stage Least Squares Regression
(
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
)
[
Angrist and Imbens 1995
,
Angrist et al. 1996
]
.
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
has become the standard tool in economics, social sciences, and statistics to study the effect of treatments on outcomes involving endogeneity.
Recently, in machine learning, researchers have extended traditional
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
to nonlinear structures, non-compliant instruments, and corrupted observations using deep learning
[
Liu et al. 2020
,
Xu et al. 2020
,
Xu et al. 2021
]
, graphical models
[
Stirn and Jebara 2018
]
, and kernel regression
[
Zhu et al. 2022
]
.

To rectify the error due to endogeneity, the analyst further studies the main factors behind pricing and observes that
\mathrm{Price}_{t}
depends on material cost of the required components
\mathrm{MaterialCost}_{t}
. She also observes that
\mathrm{MaterialCost}_{t}

\mathrm{Event}_{t}
.
To rectify the error due to endogeneity, the analyst observes that
\mathrm{MaterialCost}_{t}
is independent of the omitted variable
\mathrm{Event}_{t}
. Thus, she chooses
\mathrm{MaterialCost}_{t}
to be an
Instrumental Variable (IV)
. She runs an online linear regression to learn the relation between
\mathrm{Price}_{t}
and
\mathrm{MaterialCost}_{t}
. This is equivalent to learning
\theta
in the first-stage
\mathrm{Price}_{t}=\theta\times\mathrm{MaterialCost}_{t}+\epsilon_{t}
, where exogeneity is obeyed. She learns that an increase in material cost increases the price, say
\theta=1
.
Now, she uses the prices predicted at time
t
, by the first-stage regressor, as the second-stage covariates, and uses them further the relation between price and sales in the second-stage. This leads her to an estimate of the second-stage parameter
\beta
, say
-1.01
, which is accurate even under endogeneity.

Thus, we are interested in studying the
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
approach for online learning. But the existing analyses of
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
are asymptotic, i.e. what can be learned if we have access to an infinite number of samples in an offline setting
[
Singh et al. 2020
,
Liu et al. 2020
,
Nareklishvili et al. 2022
]
. In applications, this analysis is vacuous as one has access to only finite samples. Additionally, in practice, it is natural to acquire the data sequentially as treatments are chosen on-the-go and then to learn the structural equation from the sequential data
[
Venkatraman et al. 2016
]
. This motivates us to analyse the online extension of
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
, named
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
.

Additionally, as stated in Example
1.1
, the goal of the analyst might be to aid a restaurant owner or food supplier to strategically decide on a price of the food item from a price range and also corresponding raw material cost among multiple suppliers to improve the sales as much as possible. At this point, the estimation of the first- and second-stage parameters aid in sequential decision making, leading to the maximisation of accumulated outcomes over time. This is exactly a linear bandit problem.
In this case, the analyst only observes the outcome corresponding to the choice of price and material cost. This is referred to as bandit feedback in online learning literature and studied under the linear bandit formulation
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
]
. This motivates us to extend
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
to
linear bandits, where both bandit feedback and endogeneity occur
. In this paper, we investigate two questions:

1.
What is the upper bound on the loss in performance for deploying parameters estimated by
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
instead of the true parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
? How does estimating the true parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
influence different performance metrics under endogeneity?

2.
Can we design efficient algorithms for linear bandits with endogeneity by using
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
?

Our contributions.
Our investigation has led to

1.
A non-asymptotic analysis of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
:
First, we identify three notions of regret:
identification regret
,
oracle regret
, and
population regret
. Though all of them are of same order under exogeneity, we show that the relations are more nuanced under endogeneity and unbounded noise
(see Appendix
B.9
)
. We focus specifically on the identification regret, i.e. the sum of differences between the estimated parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and
oracle regret
, i.e. the sum of differences between the losses incurred by the estimated parameters
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. In Section
3
, we theoretically show that
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
achieve
\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T)

\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T+\gamma\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T\log T})

T
samples from the observational data. Identification regret of

is

multiplicative factor higher than regret of online linear regression under exogeneity, and oracle regret is

additive factor higher. These are the costs that

pay for tackling endogeneity in two stages. In our knowledge,

Due to two-stages and endogeneity, we can’t rely on standard techniques and need to prove concentration bounds for dependent r.v. (Appendix

We also experimentally demonstrate efficiency of

Related work: Online regression without endogeneity.
Our analysis of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
extends the tools and techniques of online linear regression without endogeneity. Analysis of online linear regression began with
[

Littlestone et al. 1991

Bartlett et al. 2015

Gaillard et al. 2019

These works perform an adversarial analysis with bounded outcomes, covariates, and observational noise, while we focus on the stochastic setting.

Ouhamma et al. 2021

In this paper, we analyse online

Venkatraman et al. 2016

\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}

Linear bandits generalise the setting of online linear regression under bandit feedback

Here, we use

2
Background: Instrumental variables and offline two-stage least squares

We are given an observational dataset
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
consisting of
n
pairs of input features and outcomes. Here,
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
. Inputs and outcomes are stochastically generated using a linear model

y_{i}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\eta_{i},

(Second stage)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is the
unknown true parameter vector
of the linear model, and
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
is the
unobserved error
representing all causes of
y_{i}
other than
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. It is assumed that the error terms
\eta_{i}
are independent and identically distributed with bounded variance
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
.
The parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
quantifies the causal effect on
y_{i}
due to a unit change in a component of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, while retaining other causes of
y_{i}
constant.
The goal of linear regression is to estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}
by
minimising the square loss over dataset

[
Brier 1950
]
, i.e.
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\triangleq\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{2}
.

The obtained solution is called the Ordinary Least Square (OLS) estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}

[
Wasserman 2004
]
, and is a corner stone of online regression
[
Gaillard et al. 2019
]
and linear bandit algorithms
[
Foster and Rakhlin 2020
]
.
Specifically, if we define the input feature matrix to be
\mathbf{X}_{n}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, the outcome vector to be
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\triangleq[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]^{\top}
, and the noise vector is
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}\triangleq[\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}]^{\top}
, OLS estimator is

\displaystyle{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}

\displaystyle\triangleq(\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\beta}+(\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\eta_{i}
are independent
, i.e. under exogeneity, the OLS estimator is unbiased:
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}]=\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Furthermore, it is also asymptotically consistent since
\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}0
implies
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{\mathrm{OLS}}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\boldsymbol{\beta}

[
Greene 2003
]
.

In practice, the input features
\boldsymbol{x}
and the noise
\eta
are often correlated
[
Greene 2003
, Chapter 8]
. As in Figure
1(c)
, this dependence, called
endogeneity
, is modelled with
a confounding unobserved random variable

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.
To compute an unbiased estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
under endogeneity, the Instrumental Variables (IVs)
\boldsymbol{z}
are introduced
[
Angrist et al. 1996
,
Newey and Powell 2003
]
. IVs are chosen such that they are highly correlated with endogenous components of
\boldsymbol{x}
(relevance condition) but are independent of noise
\eta
(exogeneity of
\boldsymbol{z}
). This leads to Two-stage Least Squares (
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
) approach to IV regression
[
Angrist and Imbens 1995
,
Angrist et al. 1996
]
. We further assume that IVs,
\mathbf{Z}_{n}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}
, cause linear effects on the endogenous covariates

\mathbf{X}_{n}=\mathbf{Z}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Theta}+\mathbf{E}_{n}.

(First stage)

\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\times d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is the unknown first-stage parameter matrix and
\mathbf{E}_{n}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is the unobserved noise matrix leading to confounding in the second stage.
First-stage is a “classic”
multiple regression
with covariates
\boldsymbol{z}
independent of the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})

[
Wasserman 2004
, Ch. 13]
. Thus, OLS in first-stage yields an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}\triangleq(\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{n}
, leading to the
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{\top}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{\top}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{n}.

(2SLS)

Here,
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}
is the predicted covariate from first-stage. Given
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\eta_{i}]=0
in the true model, we observe that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{2SLS}}=\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\boldsymbol{\beta},

as
n\to\infty
.
Since
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are correlated,
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
estimator is not unbiased in finite-time. When
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}=d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, we call
\boldsymbol{z}

just-identified IVs
. When
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}<d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, we call
\boldsymbol{z}

over-identified IVs
. In this paper, we analyse both of the conditions.

Assumption 2.1
.

The assumptions for conducting
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
are
[
Greene 2003
,
Hernan and Robins 2020
]
:

1.

Endogeneity of
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The second stage input features
\boldsymbol{x}
and noise
\eta
are not independent:
\boldsymbol{x}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp\eta
.

2.

Exogeneity of
\boldsymbol{z}
.
IV random variables are independent of the noise in the second stage:
\boldsymbol{z}\perp\!\!\!\perp\eta
.

3.

Relevance Condition of IVs
. For every
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the variables
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
are correlated:
\boldsymbol{z}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{x}
.

[
Greene 2003
, Sec.8.2]
and
[
Hernan and Robins 2020
, Ch. 16]
state these three as the
strictly necessary conditions for the existence of IVs
in offline IV-regression. Thus, we assume only these necessary conditions to hold for our analysis of online
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
.

3
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
: Online two-stage least squares regression

In this section, we describe the problem setting and schematic of
Online Two-Stage Least Squares Regression
, in brief
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
. Following that, we first define two notions of regret:
identification
and
oracle
, measuring accuracy of estimating the true parameter and the predicted outcomes, respectively. We provide a theoretical analysis of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
and upper bound the two types of regret (Section
3.2
).

\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
.
In the online setting of IV regression, the data
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t},y_{t}),\ldots
arrives in a stream.
Following
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
model (Fig.
1(c)
), data is generated endogeneously

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}

3.1
Regrets for estimation & prediction: Identification & oracle regrets

To analyse the online regression algorithms, it is essential to define proper performance metrics, specifically
regrets
.
Regret quantifies what an online (or sequential) algorithm cannot achieve as it does not have access to the whole dataset rather observes it step by step.
Here, we discuss and define different regrets that we leverage in our analysis of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
.

In econometrics and bio-statistics, where
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
is popularly used the focus is the accurate identification of the underlying structural model
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Identifying
\boldsymbol{\beta}
leads to understanding of the underlying economic or biological causal relations and their dynamics. In ML,
Venkatraman et al. 2016
applied
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
for online linear system identification. Thus, given a sequence of estimators
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and a sequence of covariates
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, the cost of identifying the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be quantified by

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}.

(3)

We refer to
\widetilde{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
as
identification regret
over horizon
T
. In the just identified setting that we are considering, the identification regret is equivalent to the regret of counterfactual prediction (Eqn. 5,
Hartford et al. 2016
). Counterfactual predictions are important to study the causal questions: what would have changed in the outcome if Treatment
a
had been used instead of treatment
b
? One of the modern applications of IVs is to facilitate such counterfactual predictions
[
Hartford et al. 2016
,
Bennett et al. 2019
,
Zhu et al. 2022
]
.

Alternatively, one might be interested in evaluating and improving the quality of prediction obtained using an estimator
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
with respect to an underlying oracle (or expert), which is typically the case in statistical learning theory and forecasting
[
Foster 1991
,
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
]
. If the oracle has access to the true parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, the cost in terms of prediction that the estimators pay with respect to the oracle is
\bar{r}_{t}\triangleq(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1})^{2}-(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}
.
Thus, the regret in terms of the quality of prediction is

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1})^{2}-(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}.

(4)

We refer to
\overline{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
as the
oracle regret
. This regret is studied for stochastic analysis of online regression
[
Ouhamma et al. 2021
]
and for analysing bandits
[
Foster and Rakhlin 2020
]
.

As
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
is interesting for learning causal structures
[
Hartford et al. 2016
,
Bennett et al. 2019
]
, we focus on the identification regret. On the other hand, to compare with the existing results in online linear regression, we also analyse the oracle regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
. Though they are of similar order (w.r.t.
T
) in the exogenous setting, we show
that they differ significantly for
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
under endogeneity
(
Section

3.2
)
.

Remark 3.2

(
Hardness of exogeneity vs. endogeneity for prediction
)
.

In online learning theory focused on Empirical Risk Minimisation (ERM), another type of regret is considered where the oracle has access to the best offline estimator
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\triangleq\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}
given the observations over
T
steps
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
. Thus, the new formulation of regret becomes

R_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1})^{2}-\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}.

We refer to it as the
population regret
.
Under exogeneity,
Ouhamma et al. 2021
show that oracle regret and population regret differs by
o(\log^{2}T)
. We show that under endogeneity, their expected values differ by
\Omega(T)
. Thus, we avoid studying population regret in this paper, and detail it in
Appendix
B.9
.

Confidence set.

Lemma 3.3

(Confidence ellipsoid for the second-stage parameters)

Let us define the

Discussion.

1.
Hardness of endogeneity vs. exogeneity.

Under exogeneity and unbounded stochastic noise, the oracle regret of online linear regression is
\text{O}(d^{2}\log^{2}T)

[
Ouhamma et al. 2021
]
. If we take the just-identified IVs, i.e.
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}=d_{\boldsymbol{z}}=d
, due to endogeneity,
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
incurs an additive factor of
\text{O}(\gamma\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T\log T})
in the oracle regret. This term appears due to the correlation between the second and the first-stage noises, and it is proportional to the degree of correlation between the noises in these two stages. Thus, the bias due to the correlation of noises acts as the dominant term. In
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
literature, this phenomenon is called the self-fulfilling bias
[
Li et al. 2021
]
. But we did not find any explicit bound on it in a stochastic and non-asymptotic analysis.

2.
Tightness of analysis.
In the existing literature, we do not have any lower bound for online regression with exogeneity. But we can indicate tightness of the proposed analysis by observing two things. (i) Our identification regret under endoegeneity is of the same order as that of exogeneous case
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2012
]
. (ii) If we assume
\gamma=0
, i.e. the noises are independent, and we retrieve also the oracle regret of the same order as that of the exogenous case
[
Ouhamma et al. 2021
]
.

3.
Bounded vs. unbounded noise.
For bounded noise and covariates, the adversarial setting would subsume the endogeneous setting where the responses are sampled according to dependent bounded noise since covariates and responses are adversarially chosen sequences. If we consider the adversarial linear regression for bounded responses
y_{t}\leq Y
and covariates, the the Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth Regressor (VAWR) achieves almost optimal
\mathcal{O}(Y^{2}\log T)
regret
[
Orabona 2019
]
. Thus, the
\gamma\sqrt{T}
term disappears as we obtain in our oracle regret bound with unbounded and stochastic noise. This contrast indicates non-triviality of our analysis for the unbounded noise and unbounded outcomes.

\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
: Algorithm design.

If the agent had full information in hindsight, she could infer the best arm (a.k.a. action or intervention) in
\mathcal{A}_{t}
as
a^{*}_{t}=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{t,a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}].

Choosing

is equivalent to choosing
\boldsymbol{z}_{t,a_{t}^{*}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t,a_{t}^{*}}
.
But the agent does not know them and aims to select
\{a_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
to minimise regret:
R_{T}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,a_{t}^{*}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t,a_{t}})]
. Now, we extend the
\mathsf{OFUL}
algorithm minimising regret in linear bandits with exogeneity
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
]
. The core idea is that the algorithm maintains a confidence set
\mathcal{B}_{t-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
around the estimated parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}
, which is computed only using the observed data. In order to tackle endogeneity, we choose to use the
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}
computed using data observed till
t-1
(Equation (
3
)). Then, we build an ellipsoid
\mathcal{B}_{t-1}
around it, such that
\mathcal{B}_{t-1}\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}}\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{t-1}(\delta)}\right\}
, where
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{t-1}(\delta)\triangleq 2\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\log\left(\nicefrac{{\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\right)^{1/2}\lambda^{-d_{\boldsymbol{z}}/2}}}{{\delta}}\right)
and
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}
. Then, the algorithm chooses an optimistic estimate
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t-1}
from that confidence set:
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t-1}\triangleq\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{t-1}}\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\right).

Then, she chooses the action
A_{t}
corresponding to
\boldsymbol{x}_{t,A_{t}}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t-1}
, which maximises the reward as per the estimate
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t-1}
. In brief, the algorithm chooses the pair

(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,A_{t}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t-1})=\argmax\nolimits_{{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}_{t}\times\mathcal{B}_{t-1}}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}.

Given confidence interval, we optimistically choose

by solving

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\left\{\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{t,a},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}\right\rangle+\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}_{t-1}(\delta)}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,a}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}}\right\}

(6)

1.
Handling unbounded outcomes.
We do not need bounded outcomes (
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\in[-1,1]
) as OFUL
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2011a
]
. Similar to
[
Ouhamma et al. 2021
]
, which removes this assumption for exoegeneity, our analysis works for unbounded outcomes showing that the bounded outcome assumption is not needed even under endogeneity.

2.
Two-stage OFUL-IV vs. one-stage OFUL under endogeneity.
An alternative proposal than using a two-stage approach for LBE is to reduce it to a one-level linear bandit, i.e.
y_{t}=(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}+\eta_{t}
.
This leads to a composite unknown parameter
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and a new sub-Gaussian noise with component-wise variance
\sigma_{new}^{2}=2(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
.
Now, if we apply OFUL to this setup, we observe that the confidence bound around the new hidden parameter
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\beta}
is proportional to
\sigma_{new}
.
Thus, the confidence interval blows up by
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}
. Also, using this interval in Equation (
6
) requires us to know either
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
or a tight upper bound on it. In real-life, we cannot assume access to such knowledge. Additionally,
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
grows linearly with
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Thus, this approach can incur significantly higher regret for high-dimensional covariates. In contrast,
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
does not require to know either
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}
or any bound on it. Additionally, the confidence interval of
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
depends on
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
, which is significantly smaller than
\sigma_{new}^{2}
. These observations demonstrate the benefit of using
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
under endogeneity than
\mathsf{OFUL}
with a single-stage reduction.
Single-stage reduction might be helpful if the confounding noise is covariate dependent but is independent of the unknown parameter
[
Krishnamurthy et al. 2018
]
. Numerical results in Fig.

further validates this benefit of
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
over an one-stage reduction.

6
Conclusion and future works

We study online IV regression, specifically online
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
, for unbounded noise and endogenous data. We analyse the finite-time identification and oracle regrets of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
. We observe that
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
incurs
\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T)
identification regret, and
\text{O}(\gamma\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T\log T})
oracle regret as the correlation between the noises in the two-stages dominate the identification regret. For just-identified IVs, identification regrets are of the same order for exogeneity and endogeneity. We further propose
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
for stochastic linear bandits with endogeneity that uses
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
to estimate the parameters. We show that
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
achieves
\text{O}(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T}\log T)
regret. We experimentally show that
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
and
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
are more accurate than Ridge and
\mathsf{OFUL}
, respectively.
In future, it would be interesting to extend the stochastic analysis of online IV regression and bandits with endogeneity to non-parametric
[
Newey and Powell 2003
]
, non-linear
[
Xu et al. 2020
]
, and non-stationary
[
Russac et al. 2019
]
settings.

A.1
Vectors, matrices, and norms

Definition A.1

(
\ell_{p}
-norms)
.

For a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we express its
\ell_{p}
-norm as
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{p}
for
p\geq 0
. A special case is the Euclidean
\ell_{2}
-norm denoted as
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
, which is induced by classical scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Given a rectangular matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
n\geq m
, we write its ordered singular values as

\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{A})=\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\sigma_{2}(\mathbf{A})\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{m}(\mathbf{A})=\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\geq 0

The minimum and maximum singular values have the variational characterisation

\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{A})=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\|\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}}\|\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2},

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\triangleq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1\right\}
is the Euclidean unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition A.2

(
\ell_{2}
-operator norm)
.

The spectral or
\ell_{2}
-operator norm of
\mathbf{A}
is defined as

\vvvert\mathbf{A}\vvvert_{2}\triangleq\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{A})\;.

(7)

Since covariance matrices are symmetric, we also focus on the set of symmetric matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted
\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mid\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}^{\mathrm{T}}\right\}
, as well as the subset of positive semidefinite matrices given by

\mathcal{S}_{+}^{d\times d}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}\mid\mathbf{Q}\succeq 0\right\}.

From standard linear algebra, we recall the facts that any matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{S}^{d\times d}
is diagonalisable via a unitary transformation, and we use
\lambda(\mathbf{Q})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote its vector of eigenvalues, ordered as

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{Q})=\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{Q})\geq\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{Q})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{Q})=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q}).

Note that a matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is positive semidefinite-written
\mathbf{Q}\succeq 0
for short-if and only if
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})\geq 0
.

Remark A.3

(Rayleigh-Ritz variational characterisation of eigenvalues)
.

We remind also the Rayleigh-Ritz variational characterisation of the minimum and maximum eigenvalues-namely

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{Q})=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}.

Remark A.4
.

For any symmetric matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, the
\ell_{2}
-operator norm can be written as

\vvvert\mathbf{Q}\vvvert_{2}=\max\left\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{Q}),\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{Q})\right|\right\},

by virtue of which it inherits the variational representation

\vvvert\mathbf{Q}\vvvert_{2}=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{v}\right|.

Corollary A.5
.

Given a rectangular matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
n\geq m
, suppose that we define the
m

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{A}
. We then have the relationship

\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{R})=\left(\sigma_{j}(\mathbf{A})\right)^{2}\quad\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,m

We now introduce norms that are induced by positive semi-definite matrices in the following way.

Definition A.6
.

For any vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n\times n}
, let us define the norm
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbf{A}}\triangleq\sqrt{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{y}}=\sqrt{\langle\boldsymbol{y},\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle}

Throughout the paper we will need often to bound matrix induced norms using

Proposition A.7
.

Take
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, with
\mathbf{B}\succeq 0
positive semi-definite and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{B}}^{2}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}}^{2}\leq\vvvert\mathbf{B}\vvvert_{2}\vvvert\mathbf{A}\vvvert_{2}^{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}

(8)

Proof.

The equality holds since we can rewrite

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{B}}^{2}=\langle\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{x},\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}}^{2}.

The inequality follows by the definition of

-norms, where we further substitute

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}{\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{y},\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle}{\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\frac{\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\vvvert\mathbf{B}\vvvert_{2}\vvvert\mathbf{A}\vvvert_{2}^{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

We note that the inequality holds trivially for

\mathbf{A}

therefore, in the previous case, we can safely divide by

and
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}

∎

Lemma A.8

(Determinant-Trace Inequality)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for any
1\leq s\leq t
,
\left\|\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\right\|_{2}\leq L_{z}
. Let
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}=\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top}
for some
\lambda>0
. Then,

\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\leq\left(\lambda+tL^{2}_{z}/d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\right)^{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}

(9)

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}
. Since
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}
is positive definite, its eigenvalues are positive. Also, note that
\operatorname{det}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t})=\prod_{s=1}^{{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}}\alpha_{s}
and
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)=\sum_{s=1}^{{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}}\alpha_{s}
. By inequality of arithmetic and geometric means,

\sqrt[d_{\boldsymbol{z}}]{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}}{{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}}.

Therefore,
\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\leq\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)/{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\right)^{{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}}
.
Now, it remains to upper bound the trace:

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)=\operatorname{Tr}(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}})+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top}\right)={d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\lambda+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\lambda+tL^{2}_{z}.

A.2
Random variables and concentration bounds

Random variables follow the previous convention if they are scalar, vectors, or random matrix variables.
We adopt the following convention when we talk about sub-Gaussians and sub-exponential random variables.

Definition A.10

(Sub-Gaussian r.v.)
.

A random variable
X
with mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
is sub-Gaussian if there is a positive number
\sigma
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(X-\mu)}\right]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}\quad\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\text{. }

Definition A.11

(Sub-exponential r.v.)
.

A random variable
X
with mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
is sub-exponential if there are non-negative parameters
(\nu,\alpha)
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(X-\mu)}\right]\leq e^{\nu^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}\quad\text{ for all }|\lambda|<\frac{1}{\alpha}

Lemma A.12

(Square and product of non-independent sub-Gaussian random variables)
.

Given two non-independent random variables
X\sim
sub-Gaussian
(\sigma_{X})
and
Y\sim
sub-Gaussian
(\sigma_{Y})
, we have the following properties:

1. the squared random variable

X^{2}
is sub-exp
\left(4\sqrt{2}\sigma^{2},4\sigma^{2}\right);

2. the recentered random variable

XY
is sub-exp
\left(4\sqrt{2}\left(\sigma_{X}^{2}+\sigma_{Y}^{2}\right),2\left(\sigma_{X}^{2}+\sigma_{Y}^{2}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

We establish the proof for both statements sequentially, leveraging the first result to demonstrate the second in the scenario of non-independent random variables. This distinction gives rise to distinct constants compared to the outcome observed for independent random variables.

For the first point we bound the rescaled
p
-th power of
X

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sigma_{X}\sqrt{2}}}\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sigma_{X}\sqrt{2}}}\right|^{p}\geq u\right\}du

(integral identity for positive r.v.)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{X}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left\{|X|\geq t\sqrt{2}\sigma_{X}\right\}pt^{p-1}dt

(change of variable
u=t^{p}
)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}2e^{-t^{2}}pt^{p-1}dt

(by
\sigma_{X}
-sub-Gaussianity)

\displaystyle=p\Gamma(p/2)

( set
t^{2}=s
and use definition of Gamma function) )

By multiplying both sides by
(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{p}
we obtain

{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X\right|^{p}\right]}\leq p2^{\frac{p}{2}}\sigma^{p}\Gamma(p/2).

Let
W=X^{2}
and
\mu_{W}=\mathbb{E}[W]
. By power series expansion and since
\Gamma(r)=(r-1)!
for an integer
r

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\left(W-\mu_{W}\right)}\right]

Appendix B
Regret analysis of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}

Instrumental variables is a powerful technique employed in econometrics to address endogeneity concerns when estimating causal relationships between variables. One computational approach commonly used is
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
. In the first stage of 2SLS, each endogenous covariate in the equation of interest is regressed on all exogenous variables in the model, including both exogenous covariates within the equation of interest and the excluded instruments. This step aims to establish the relationship between the endogenous variables and the instruments. In the second stage, the regression of interest is estimated, wherein each endogenous covariate is substituted with the predicted values obtained from the first stage. By employing this two-stage procedure, 2SLS effectively addresses endogeneity issues and provides consistent estimates of the causal effects of interest.

In the following we show the relation between the just-identified and over-identified case of IVs and show the form that the
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
assumes in the two cases.

B.1
A primer on just-identified and over-identified IVs and
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}

Just-identified case [
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}=d_{\boldsymbol{z}}=d
]:

In the just-identified case, instruments and covariates have the same dimension
d
, i.e. for each covariate, there is an instrument that is correlated to it. Given an observational dataset
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{s},y_{s}\}_{s=1}^{t}
consisting of
t
pairs of input features and outcomes, such that
y_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These inputs and outcomes are stochastically generated using a linear model

y_{s}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}+\eta_{s},

It is assumed that the error terms
\eta_{i}
are independently and identically distributed and have bounded variance
\sigma^{2}
.
In the presence of endogeneity (
\boldsymbol{x}
and the noise
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are correlated) Ordinary Least Square
\boldsymbol{\beta}

[
Wasserman 2004
]
defined as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\triangleq\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\sum_{s=1}(y_{s}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{s})^{2}

is biased and asymptotically inconsistent.
To compute an unbiased estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
under endogeneity, a popular technique is to introduce the Instrumental Variables (IVs). IVs are chosen such that they are highly correlated with endogenous components of
\boldsymbol{x}
(relevance condition) but are independent of the noise
\eta
(exogeneity condition for
\boldsymbol{z}
).
In practice, this dependence is modelled with a confounding unobserved random variable
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
by

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Theta}+\mathbf{E}_{t},

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\times d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is an unknown first-stage parameter matrix and
\mathbf{E}_{t}\triangleq[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}]^{\top}
is the unobserved noise matrix leading to confounding in the second stage.
The most common IV specification uses the following estimator:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{IV},t}\triangleq\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}.

(IV)

As long as
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\eta_{i}]=\boldsymbol{0}
in the true model,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{IV},t}=\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\boldsymbol{\beta},

as
t\to\infty
.
This works because IV solves for the unique parameter that satisfies
\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\boldsymbol{0}
. Since
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are correlated, IV estimator is not unbiased in finite-time.

Relevance condition
for IVs ensures this. It basically means that
IVs and covariates must have some correlation
, and thus, IVs explain some of the variance observed in the endogenous covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}

[
Hernan and Robins 2020
, Chapter 16]
. Since we are interested in finite time guarantees, we assume “positive-definite covariance" between IVs and covariates at any finite time.

Specifically, we use the following form of the relevance condition:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},\mathbf{X}_{t})\right)=\sigma_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}^{\top}\right)\geq{\mathfrak{r}}>0\,,\qquad\forall t\in\mathbb{N}.

(12)

Remark B.1

(Requirement of the relevance condition)
.

Here, we discuss different perspectives and results justifying the use of the relevance condition in the online IV regression setting like its offline counterpart.

1)

Causality perspective:
Relevance is one of the three
strictly necessary conditions for the existence of IVs
even in offline IV-regression
[
Greene 2003
,
Hernan and Robins 2020
]
. Without relevance, IV regressor is not defined. For linear case, [Martens et al.’06, Epidemiology] show that relevance exists for IVs causally associated with covariates.

2)

Experimental grounding:
We confirm that this assumption is good and realistic. Because our regression analysis and the bandit algorithm’s design are based on this assumption, and in both settings, our algorithms outperform the natural baselines (plus the one-stage reduction in fig) while not needing to know the exact amount of relevance.

3)

Expressing relevance:

[
Greene 2003
]
states that the relevance condition can also be written as a condition of rank maximality for covariance matrices, which we express as the minimum singular value being bounded away from zero a.s.

Over-identified case [
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}<d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
]:

Suppose that there are more instruments than there are covariates in the equation of interest, so that
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
is a
t\times d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
matrix with
d_{\boldsymbol{z}}>d_{\boldsymbol{x}}
. This is often called the over-identified case. In this case, the generalised method of moments (GMM) can be used. The GMM IV estimator is

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{GMM},t}\triangleq\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{t},

(GMM IV)

where
\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}
refers to the projection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\triangleq\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}
.
This expression collapses to the first when the number of instruments is equal to the number of covariates in the equation of interest. The over-identified IV is, therefore a generalisation of the just-identified IV. Defining GMM IV estimator requires an additional well-behavedness assumption on IVs.

Assumption B.2

(Well-behaved IVs)
.

For any
t>0
, the matrix
\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}
is full rank with
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})\triangleq\lambda_{0}>0
.

Proposition B.3
.

If
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}=d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, i.e. in the just-identified case,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{GMM},t}
reduces to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV},t}
.

Proof.

Expanding the
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{GMM}
expression:

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{GMM},t}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

In the just-identified case, we have as many instruments as covariates, so that the dimension of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
is the same as that of
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
. Hence,
\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t},\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}
are all squared matrices of the same dimension. We can expand the inverse, using the fact that, for any invertible
n
-by-n matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B},(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})^{-1}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\mathrm{GMM},t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{IV},t}

∎

Interpretation as two-stage least-squares.

One computational method which can be used to calculate IV estimates is two-stage least squares
\mathsf{\text{2}SLS}
. In the first stage, each explanatory variable that is an endogenous covariate in the equation of interest is regressed on all of the exogenous variables in the model, including both exogenous covariates in the equation of interest and the excluded instruments. The predicted values from these regressions are obtained. Then, in the second stage, the regression of interest is estimated as usual, except that in this stage each endogenous covariate is replaced with the predicted values from the first stage.

Proposition B.4
.

For
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, i.e. in both just- and over-identified cases,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{GMM},t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\textrm{2SLS},t}
.

Proof.

The proof follows in two steps by simple substitution of the relevant quantities.

Stage 1:
Regressing each column of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
on
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
with a regularisation parameter
\lambda
, in the first stage

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Theta}+\mathbf{E}_{t}

yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}\triangleq\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}

(13)

and save the predicted values:

\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}\triangleq\mathbf{Z}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}=\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\triangleq\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{X}_{t}.

Stage 2:
Regress
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
on the predicted values from the first stage:

\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}

which gives:

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\textrm{2SLS},t}=\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

where the second equality follows from the fact that
the usual OLS estimator is:
(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
, and replacing
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}=\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{X}_{t}
and noting that
\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}
is a symmetric and idempotent matrix, so that
\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}=\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}=\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\textrm{2SLS},t}

\displaystyle=\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\textrm{GMM},t}

∎

(15)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{t}(\delta)

(16)

Proof.

Step 1:
First, we start by observing the
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
estimate of the second-stage parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}+\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}

We would like to remind that following the classical GMM estimator, we assume
\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}
is invertible for any
t>0
, and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})=\lambda>0
.

Step 2:
Thus, for any vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, we can express

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)(\underbrace{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}})^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x},(\underbrace{\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}}_{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x},\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\underset{(a)}{\leq}\lVert\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}\lVert{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}}

\displaystyle\underset{(b)}{=}\lVert\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t})\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\underset{(c)}{=}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t})^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\rVert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

Here, we define the first-stage design matrix to be
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\triangleq\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}
, and the second-stage design matrix to be
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\triangleq\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}
for any
t>0
.

Inequality (a) is obtained from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.
Equality (b) is due to the fact that
\lVert\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}^{-1}}
. Equality (c) is due to the definition of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t}
(Eq. (
13
)).

Step 3:
Now, let us choose
\boldsymbol{x}\triangleq\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)
.

This leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}=\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)=\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\|^{2}_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}},

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{RHS}

\displaystyle=\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\rVert_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\vvvert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\vvvert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\rVert_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}}\vvvert\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\vvvert_{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}}^{-1}}

Step 4:
Combining the two results above lead to

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbf{Z}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}}

We bound the last term assigning
\eta_{0}=0
, using
Theorem

A.15
for the first inequality, and
Lemma

A.8
in the second inequality.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}}=\left\lVert\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\right\rVert_{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{det}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)^{1/2}\lambda^{-d_{\boldsymbol{z}}/2}}{\delta}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1+tL^{2}_{z}/d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\lambda}{\delta}\right)}.

The second inequality is obtained from the concentration of the vector-valued martingales with probability at least
1-\delta
.
The final inequality is obtained by assuming
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}=\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}
.
∎

B.4
Concentration of the minimum eigenvalue of the design matrices

The aim of the section is to find a concentration result for the minimum eigenvalue of the design matrix, which, in turn, gives us a concentration of the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the inverse of the design matrix
\left\vvvert\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}
.
We use a classical concentration result for the covariance matrix
Theorem

A.14
together with the bound on the difference of the minimum eigenvalues of two symmetric matrices
Lemma

A.9
in order to bound the maximum eigenvalue of the inverse of the design matrix.

Remark B.7

(Diversity condition of IVs)
.

In this section, we consider the covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, i.e. the true covariance matrix of the IVs vectors, to be only positive definite a.s.
This is widely used in the stochastic online regression and linear bandit literature
[
Papini et al. 2021
,
Tirinzoni et al. 2022
]
, and is known as the
diversity condition
.

Lemma B.8

(Well-behavedness of First-stage Design Matrix)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
be i.i.d. zero-mean random vectors with covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\right\|_{2}\leq L_{\boldsymbol{z}}
almost surely. We denote the regularised design matrix as
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}=\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top}
.
For all
\delta>0
and regularisation parameter
\lambda>0
,
we observe that

\displaystyle\left\vvvert\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}=\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\right)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda}\text{ if }t\leq T_{0}\\
\frac{2}{t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}\text{ if }t>T_{0}\end{cases}.

Here,
T_{0}>0
is a constant defined by Equation (
18
) and
\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
is the minimum eigenvalue of the true covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{z}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}]
.

Proof.

First, we aim to find a lower bound for the smallest eigenvalue of the design, matrix where we set the regularisation parameter
\lambda
to zero. We denote the ‘non-regularised’ design matrix as
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{\lambda=0}
. For
t\geq 1
, we observe that
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{\lambda=0}/t\triangleq\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}
.
Thus, by applying Equation (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\nicefrac{{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{\lambda=0}}}{{t}}\right)-\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\right|\leq\frac{4L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}{t}\log\left(\frac{2d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}{\delta}\right)+2\sqrt{\frac{2L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}{t}\log\left(\frac{2d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}{\delta}\right)\left\vvvert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right\vvvert_{2}}\,.

Further substituting
A\triangleq 2L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}\log\left(\frac{2d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}{\delta}\right)
leads to the following lower bound for the minimum eigenvalue

\displaystyle\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{\lambda=0}\right)\geq\max\left\{0,t\left(\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})-\nicefrac{{2A}}{{t}}-2\sqrt{\nicefrac{{A\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}}{{t}}}\right)\right\}.

Here,
\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
and
\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
is the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of the true covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{z}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{\top}]
.

Now, from the variational definition of the minimum eigenvalues, we have
\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\geq\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{\lambda=0}\right)+\lambda,
which implies that
\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\geq\lambda
for all
t\geq 0
, with equality for
t=0
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\lambda,\lambda+t\left(\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})-\nicefrac{{2A}}{{t}}-2\sqrt{\nicefrac{{A\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}}{{t}}}\right)\right\}.

(17)

Let us consider the second term inside the maximum of Equation (
17
), and we split it in the following way

\displaystyle\lambda+t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})-2A-2\sqrt{tA\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}

\displaystyle=t\underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}{2}}_{\text{Term (A)}}+\underbrace{\left(\frac{t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}{2}-2\sqrt{tA\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}+\lambda-2A\right)}_{\text{Term (B)}}

Now we study for which values Term (B) is non-negative. The corresponding second-order polynomial equation is obtained substituting
u=\sqrt{t}
, and is

u^{2}\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})-4u\sqrt{A\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}+2(\lambda-2A)=0\;,

which has two solutions given by

u_{\pm}=\nicefrac{{2\sqrt{A\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}\pm\sqrt{4A\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)+2(2A-\lambda)\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}}}{{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}}\;.

In particular
for
t>\lceil u_{+}\rceil
,
\text{Term (B)}\geq 0
, and
Equation

17
simplifies in

\displaystyle\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\lambda,t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})/2\right\}.

Therefore, for
t>\lceil 2\lambda/\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\rceil
and
t>\lceil u_{+}\rceil
we have that

\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\geq t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})/2
.

Putting the results together, we conclude that

\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\geq t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})/2\quad\text{for}\quad t>T_{0}\triangleq\max\left\{\lceil 2\lambda/\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\rceil,\lceil u_{+}\rceil\right\},

(18)

while for
t\leq T_{0}
, we retain the trivial lower bound of the minimum eigenvalue, i.e.
\lambda
.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\right)\geq\begin{cases}\lambda\text{ if }t\leq T_{0}\\
t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})/2\text{ if }t>T_{0}\end{cases}\iff\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\right)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\lambda}\text{ if }t\leq T_{0}\\
\frac{2}{t\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}\text{ if }t>T_{0}\end{cases}.

∎

Corollary B.9

(Bound on the sum of maximum eigenvalues of the inverses of the first-stage design matrices)
.

For every
T>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{T}\left\vvvert\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},s}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}\leq f(T)=\mathcal{O}(\log(T))\>,

where
f(t)\triangleq\frac{T_{0}+1}{\lambda}+\frac{2(\log(T)+1)}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}
.

Proof.

For
T\leq T_{0}
,

\sum_{s=0}^{T}\left\vvvert\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},s}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}=\sum_{s=0}^{T}\frac{1}{\lambda}\leq\frac{T_{0}+1}{\lambda}=\mathcal{O}(1).

(19)

If
T>T_{0}

\sum_{s=0}^{T}\left\vvvert\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},s}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}=\sum_{s=0}^{T_{0}}\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sum_{s=T_{0}+1}^{T}\frac{2}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)s}\leq\frac{T_{0}+1}{\lambda}+\frac{2(\log(T)+1)}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}=\mathcal{O}(\log(T))

(20)

where the last inequality follows from
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\leq\int_{1}^{n}\frac{dx}{x}+1=\log(n)+1
.
∎

B.5
Upper bounding the feature norms

In this section, we present some useful Lemmas that we used in the proofs of the regret bounds of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
and
\mathsf{OFUL\text{-}IV}
.

Remark B.10
.

In
Section

B.2
, we describe that the first stage regression in
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
can be expressed as running
d
independent ridge regressions for each column of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
(Equation (
14
)). Since the standard analysis of each of the ridge regressions assume independent and sub-Gaussian noise added in the linear model (cf.
Theorem

B.5
;
[
Ouhamma et al. 2021
]
), we assume that each component of the first stage noise, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t,i}
, corresponding to the
i
-th ridge regression is
\operatorname{sub-Gauss(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})}
. Thus, we obtain that
\mathbb{E}\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}
. We use this result throughout this section.

Lemma B.11

(Bounding the First-stage Estimates)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and IVs being bounded
\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{2}^{2}\leq L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}
, the minimum singular value of the estimated parameter in the first-stage regression (
Equation

14
) is lower bounded by:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t})\geq\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}},

(21)

where we have from the relevance condition of IVs,
\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},\mathbf{X}_{t})\right)=\sigma_{\min}\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}^{\top}\right)\geq{\mathfrak{r}}>0
(Eq. (
12
))
.

Proof.

We start with the variational definition of the minimum singular value and then we lower bound this quantity:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\min}\left(\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}-1}}\lVert\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\underset{(a)}{=}\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}-1}}\frac{\lVert\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2}}\lVert\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\min}\left(\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\right)\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\min}\left(\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)^{-1}\right)\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)}{\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)}\>.

(a)
holds because according to
2.1
it is assumed that
\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)
has maximum rank and therefore we are not dividing by a zero norm vector.
Then, by the variational definition of the biggest eigenvalue

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}-1}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}-1}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\right\rangle^{2}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\lVert\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq tL_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}

Finally, we note that the quantity
\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\geq t\mathfrak{r}

by the definition of relevance, which implies

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)}{\lambda^{2}_{\max}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}\right)}\geq\frac{t\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{tL_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\geq\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\>.

∎

Lemma B.12
.

For any
t\geq 1
, we get

\displaystyle\left\vvvert\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}=\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}\right)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\right)\>\sigma_{\min}{{}^{2}}\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}}\right)}.

(22)

Proof.

The equalities follow directly from
Corollary

A.5
, by the definition of the operator norm of a symmetric matrix, and using the fact that the maximum eigenvalue of an inverse matrix equals the inverse of the minimum eigenvalue of the matrix.
For the inequality, we start by lower bounding the minimum eigenvalue of the second stage design matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}
and applying the definitions:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}\right)=\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}-1}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}-1}}\frac{\left\langle\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}}\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle}\left\langle{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}}\boldsymbol{v},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\right)\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}-1}}\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\right)\>\sigma_{\min}{{}^{2}}\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}}\right)

and we conclude taking the inverses.
∎

Lemma B.13

(Bounding the impact of first-stage noise)
.

For first stage noises that is component-wise sub-Gaussian(
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
), and bounded IVs, i.e.
\lVert\boldsymbol{z}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}
,
we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{\mathfrak{r}{{}^{4}}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}{{}^{4}}}\left(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}f(T)+C_{5}\right)}_{\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log T)}

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Here,
C_{5}
is a constant of
\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}})
as defined in
Equation

24
.

Proof.

The proof follows using chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\lambda_{\max}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}}\right)}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{2}}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\right)\>\sigma_{\min}{{}^{2}}\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t-1}}\right)}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{r}{{}^{4}}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}{{}^{4}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{2}}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}\right)}

,

B.11

Term I:

The squared

-norms of sub-Gaussian random variables are sub-exponential
[

]
, therefore
\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\sim\operatorname{sub-exp}(\nu,\alpha)
where the correct values of

are derived in

A.12
, in the result on the square of sub-Gaussians variables, and gives
\nu\triangleq 4\sqrt{2}d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
and
\alpha\triangleq 4\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
.
Furthermore, given an
X\sim\operatorname{sub-exp}(\nu,\alpha)
and a constant

\nicefrac{{X}}{{c}}\sim\operatorname{sub-exp}\left(\nicefrac{{v}}{{c}},\nicefrac{{c}}{{\alpha}}\right)
which follows by considering a new parameter
\lambda/c

in the definitions of sub-exponentiality

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda X/c}\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}/2c^{2}}
,
where

|\lambda/c|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}

iff

|\lambda|\leq\frac{c}{\alpha}

Using this, we rescale by the factor
t

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=T_{0}}^{T}\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{t}\geq\mu\right]

\displaystyle\underset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\sum_{t=T_{0}}^{T}\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\epsilon_{t}\right\|^{2}}{t}}\right]e^{-\lambda\mu}

\displaystyle\underset{(b)}{\leq}e^{\sum_{t=T_{0}}^{T}\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}/2t^{2}-\lambda\mu}

\displaystyle\underset{(c)}{\leq}e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{T_{0}-1}-\frac{1}{T}\right)-\lambda\mu}

where (a) is Markov’s inequality, (b) uses the product rule for expectations and

\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\sim\operatorname{sub-exp}(\nu=4\sqrt{2}d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\alpha=4\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})
, and (c) holds
\forall|\lambda|\leq\frac{m}{\alpha}
thanks to the following series of inequalities

\sum_{T_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\leq\int_{t_{0}-1}^{T}1/t^{2}dt=(-\frac{1}{t}|_{T_{0}-1}^{T}=-\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{T_{0}-1}.

This proves that

\sum_{t=T_{0}}^{T}\frac{\left\|\eta_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\eta_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{t}\sim\operatorname{sub-exp}\Big({\nu\sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{0}-1}-\frac{1}{T}}},{\frac{T_{0}}{\alpha}}\Big)

(23)

We bound the following summation using

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\vvvert\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t-1}^{-1}\right\vvvert_{2}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\\
\begin{aligned} &=\sum_{t=0}^{T_{0}}\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\right)\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\sum_{t=T_{0}+1}^{T-1}\operatorname{\lambda_{max}}\left(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}^{-1}\right)\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\\

Since Term I can be upper-bounded by a constant

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{2}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}\left(4\sqrt{2}d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T_{0}}\log(1/\delta)}+\frac{2T_{0}}{4\sigma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}\log(1/\delta)\right)\triangleq C_{5},

Term II:

The proof step-wise applies: (d) sub-Gaussianity of

Term II

B.6
Upper bounding correlation between first and second stage noises

We start deriving a concentration result for the quantity

S_{t}\triangleq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1}^{\top}\allowbreak\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right).

We can prove that
S_{t}
is a martingale adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}\triangleq\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s:t},\eta_{1:t},z_{1:t}\right)
,
by proving that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|S_{t}\right|\right]\leq\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=S_{t}
. The first condition is immediate and the second can be easily verified since:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t_{1}}\eta_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)+\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=0+S_{t}.

Lemma B.15

(Concentration of correlated first and second-stage noise)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, and for sub-Gaussian first- and second-stage noises with parameters
\sigma_{\eta}
and
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
, for given
T>1

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\eta_{t}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 8e^{2}\left(\sigma_{\eta}^{2}+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}\left(\sqrt{2f(T)\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\sqrt{\max\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda},\frac{2}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}\right\}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta\in[0,1)
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three steps and the main technical difficulty arises from the dependencies between the random variables, which we tackle using
Lemma

A.12
plus some techniques derived from the equivalent characterisations of sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential random variables
[
Vershynin 2018
]
.

Step 1: Finding the sub-exponential parameters.
We start deriving the sub-exponential parameters for
\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})
.

First, we bound the p-th moment of the random variable

\phi_{i}\triangleq\left(\epsilon_{s,i}\eta_{s}-\gamma_{i}\right)
. This is the centred product of two sub-Gaussians non-independent random variables
\eta_{s}
and
\epsilon_{s,i}
and from
Lemma

A.12
is
\phi_{i}\sim\operatorname{sub-exp}\left(\nu_{i},\alpha_{i}\right)
with
\nu_{i}\triangleq 4\sqrt{2}\left(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\right),\alpha_{i}\triangleq 2\left(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\right)
.
We can rewrite the previous sub-exponentiality condition using a unique parameter
K_{1}\triangleq\max\left\{\alpha_{i},\nicefrac{{\nu_{i}}}{{\sqrt{2}}}\right\}=4\left(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\right)

which implies

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\phi_{i}}\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}K_{1}^{2}}
for all
|\lambda|\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{K_{1}}}
. Using the general inequality
|x|^{p}\leq p^{p}\left(e^{x}+e^{-x}\right)
valid for
x\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{\phi_{i}}{K_{1}}\right|^{p}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[p^{p}\left(\exp\left({\frac{\phi_{i}}{K_{1}}}\right)+\exp\left({-\frac{\phi_{i}}{K_{1}}}\right)\right)\right]{\leq}p^{p}2e^{K_{1}^{2}/K_{1}^{2}}\leq 2ep^{p}

The previous inequality directly implies an inequality on the p-th norm of the
\phi_{i}

\sqrt[p]{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\epsilon_{s,i}\eta_{s}-\gamma_{i}\right|^{p}\right]}\leq\sqrt[p]{2e}K_{1}p\leq 2eK_{1}p=4e\left(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\right)p\triangleq K_{2}p

(26)

Now, we find the sub-exponential parameters for the scalar product
\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})
. Using the sub-exponential characterisation with
L^{p}
norm that we just derived in
Equation

26
we have
\forall p\geq 1

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)\right\|_{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1,i}\right|\left\|\epsilon_{s,i}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\|_{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1,i}\right|K_{2}p\triangleq K_{3,s}p\quad

(27)

where
K_{3,s}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1,i}\right|K_{2}=4e\left(\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1,i}\right|
.

We are ready to bound the moment-generating function and derive the

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\sum_{i}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1,i}\left(\epsilon_{s,i}\eta_{s}-\gamma_{i}\right)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{p}\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{p}}{p!}\right]\underset{(b)}{=}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{p}}{p!}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\underset{(c)}{=}1+\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})\right)^{p}\right]}{p!}\underset{(d)}{\leq}1+\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{p}K_{3,s}^{p}p^{p}}{p!}

\displaystyle\underset{(e)}{\leq}1+\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\lambda^{p}{K_{3,s}}^{p}e^{p}\underset{(f)}{=}1+\frac{\left(\lambda K_{3,s}e\right)^{2}}{1-\lambda K_{3,s}e}

\displaystyle\underset{(g)}{\leq}1+2\left(\lambda K_{3,s}e\right)^{2}\underset{(h)}{\leq}e^{2\lambda^{2}K_{3,s}^{2}e^{2}}

where
(a)
is a series expansion,
(b)
is from the linearity of expectation,
(c)
comes from the first moment equal to zero,
(d)
is obtained by substituting
Equation

27
,
(e)
is Stirling’s approximation
p!\geq\frac{p^{p}}{e^{p}}
,
(f)
is valid
\forall|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{K_{3,s}e}
,
(g)
is valid for
|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{2K_{3,s}e}
,
(h)
uses
1+x\leq e^{x}\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Therefore we have that
\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{s-1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}\eta_{s}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is sub-exp(
2K_{3,s}e

2K_{3,s}e

Lemma B.16

(Bias of Correlated First and Second-stage Noise)

Under the same hypothesis as that of

, in

, in
(d)

Lemma

and finally in

B.7
Upper bounding the identification regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}

In this section, we analyse the identification regret of

Theorem
3.4

(Identification regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
)
.

If Assumption

first-stage noise is a component-wise sub-Gaussian(
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
) r.v., second-stage noise is also sub-Gaussian(

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Estimation}\\

with probability at least
1-\delta\in[0,1)
.
Here,
\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)
is the confidence interval defined by
Lemma

3.3
,
f(T)
is defined in
Corollary

B.9
,
C_{5}
in
Lemma

B.13
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

We bound it (a) using the confidence bound to control the concentration of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
around

\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and (b) by bounding the sum of feature norms according to the following decomposition.

Step 1:
By applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we first decouple the effect of parameter estimation and the feature norms

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}

\displaystyle{\leq}\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}_{t-1}(\delta)}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}}

3.3
, and holds

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}.

Step 2:
Now, we need to bound the sum of the feature norms, for which we can directly use the result of

B.14

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{4}}}d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)f(T)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{4}}}C_{5}=\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log T)

Step 3:
By combining the results of the previous steps and considering the definition of

, we conclude that we can bound the Identification Regret as follows, and its orders is

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log T}\underbrace{\left(\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{4}}}d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)f(T)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{4}}}C_{5}\right)}_{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log T}

B.8
Upper bounding the oracle regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}

In this section, we elaborate on the proofs and techniques to bound the oracle regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
.

Theorem
3.5

(Oracle regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
)
.

Under the same assumptions of
Theorem

3.4
, the oracle regret of
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
at step
T>1
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\centering\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Identif.}\\
\text{Regret}\\
\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T)\end{subarray}}{\underbrace{\widetilde{R}_{T}}}+\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Estimation}\\
\text{O}(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log T})\end{subarray}}{\underbrace{\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{First-stage}\\
\text{feature norm}\\
\text{O}(\sqrt{\log T})\end{subarray}}{\left(\underbrace{C_{1}\sqrt{f(T)}}\right.}+\underbrace{C_{2}\sqrt{2d_{\boldsymbol{x}}f(T)}+\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}C_{3}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Correlated noise}\\
\text{Concentration term}\\
\text{O}(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log T})\end{subarray}}+\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Correlated noise}\\
\text{Bias term}\\
\text{O}(\gamma\sqrt{T})\end{subarray}}{\left.\underbrace{\gamma C_{4}\sqrt{T}}\right)}\@add@centering

with probability at least
1-\delta\in(0,1)
.
Here,
\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)
is the confidence interval defined by
Lemma

3.3
,
f(T)
is defined in
Corollary

B.9
,
C_{5}
in

B.13
,
\gamma\triangleq\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}]\|_{2}

C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
C_{3}
, and
C_{4}
are
d_{\boldsymbol{z}}
,
d_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and

Proof.

Using Equation (
4
), and defining
\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}\triangleq\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)
, the instantaneous regret at step
t
is

\displaystyle\overline{r}_{t}

\displaystyle\triangleq\ell_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}\right)-\ell_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)=\left(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}-\left(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\eta_{t}\right)^{2}-\eta_{t}^{2}=\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}+2\eta_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}+2\eta_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}

Since
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}
by (
First stage
), the second term can be rewritten substituting as

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=2\eta_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}+2\eta_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}

Therefore, the cumulative oracle regret
\overline{R}_{T}
by horizon
T
is

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\overline{r}_{t}=\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{2}}_{(\bullet 1\bullet)}+2\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}}_{(\bullet 2\bullet)}+2\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}}_{(\bullet 3\bullet)}

The proof proceeds by bounding each of the three terms individually.

Term 1: Second-stage regression error.

The first term
(\bullet 1\bullet)

Theorem

3.4
,

\displaystyle(\bullet 1\bullet)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log T}\underbrace{\left(\left(L_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{4}}}d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)f(T)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{4}}}C_{5}\right)}_{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log T}=\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T).

Term 2: Coupling of first-stage features and second-stage parameter estimation.

Now, we bound
(\bullet 2\bullet)
using martingale inequalities similar to the ones used for the confidence intervals to derive a uniform high probability bound.

Step 1:
Following
Theorem

A.16
, we define

It is immediate to verify that the hypothesis are satisfied, since

is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}

Bearing in mind this substitution we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}{w}_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\left(1+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{w}_{t}^{2}\right)\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{w}_{t}^{2}}}{\delta}\right)}

(30)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Step 2:
Now, the problem reduces to upper bounding

We proceed like for the first term in the
Step 2
for
Term 1

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}^{2}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\underset{(a)}{\leq}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathfrak{b}_{t-1}(\delta)\left\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\underset{(b)}{\leq}\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\right\|_{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{t-1}^{-1}}^{2}

where

Lemma

3.3
,
(b)
from monotonicity of
\mathfrak{b}_{t-1}(\delta)
and
(c)
from Equation (
25

Step 3:
Thus, substituting the bound of
Step 2
into

\displaystyle 2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sqrt{2\left(1+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{L_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{2}\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}f(T)}\right)}}_{\text{O}(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\log T)}\underbrace{\sqrt{\log\Bigg({\delta}^{-1}\sqrt{1+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{L_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{2}\mathfrak{r}^{4}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}f(T)}\Bigg)}}}_{\text{O}\left(\sqrt{\log(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\log T)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\text{O}\left(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\log T\sqrt{\log(\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\log T)}\right)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Thus, the order of
(\bullet 2\bullet)
is negligible with respect to term
(\bullet 1\bullet)
.

Term 3: Coupling of First- and Second-stage Noises.

We bound the term
(\bullet 3\bullet)
containing the self-fulfilling bias, i.e. the correlation between the first- and second-stage noise, by splitting it into two contributions.

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\eta_{t}\right)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\eta_{t}\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\eta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\eta_{t}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}

Now we can use

B.15
and

B.16

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{8e^{2}\left(\sigma_{\eta}^{2}+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{2}\right)\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}\left(\sqrt{2f(T)\log(2/\delta)}+\sqrt{\max\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda},\frac{2}{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}\right\}}\log(2/\delta)\right)}_{\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}\log(T)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}_{T}(\delta)}\left(\frac{T_{0}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+2\frac{\sqrt{2T}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}}\right)}_{\text{O}(\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T\log T})}

Thus, by assembling the upper bounds on the three terms, we get

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\overline{r}_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\mathfrak{r}^{2}}{L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}}\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}_{T-1}(\delta)}\left(\frac{T_{0}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+2\frac{\sqrt{2T}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)}}\right)}_{\text{O}(\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}\sqrt{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}T\log T})}

Remark B.19

(A discussion on
\lambda
)
.

In the design matrix, we use the fact that
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}\triangleq\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top}+\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}
, such that
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})\geq\lambda
. We discuss different ways to achieve it and standard rectifications, if we remove this condition.

1. Adding a regulariser:
We can ensure
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0}=\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}}
, and thus,
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})=\lambda
. This can be achieved by using a
\ell_{2}
regulariser, i.e.
\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
, in the second-stage, such that
\lambda>0
.

2. Data augmentation:
The other way to ensure is to consider a data augmentation approach. Rather than begining with
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, i.e. a vector with all zero entries, we can choose a
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
during initialisation, such that
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})=\vvvert\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{\top}\vvvert_{2}\geq\lambda
, and
\lVert\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\leq L_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}
.

If we want to remove this condition that
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},0})\geq\lambda
, and redefine
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}
as
\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top}
, then we can modify the bounds in two ways:

1. Well-behavedness of IVs:
It is common in econometrics and OLS regression literature to assume well-behavedness of the covariates for simplifying the analysis (Assumption A.2. in Chapter 8.2
[
Greene 2003
]
; Theorem 13.13.
[
Wasserman 2004
]
. In our case, well-behavedness means that for any
t>0
,
\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top})>0
, i.e. the first-stage design matrix dictated by the IVs is invertible at every step. In this case, we can proceed with our analysis by substituting
\lambda=\inf_{t\geq 0}\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\boldsymbol{z}_{s}^{\top})
.

2. Added cost for initial design matrices:
Now, if we want to be more flexible and not to consider the well-behavedness of IVs assumption, we have to follow the rectifications as in the analysis of unregularised OLS by
Maillard 2016
or the analysis of unregularised forward (VAWR) algorithm by
Ouhamma et al. 2021
. Specifically, if we assume
t=T_{0}>1
is the first time step when the design matrix becomes positive definite, the identification regret bound becomes
\widetilde{R}_{T}=\text{O}\left(\log(\delta^{-1}C_{\text{peeling}}(TL_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}/\gamma d_{\boldsymbol{z}})^{d_{\boldsymbol{z}}})\log(\mathrm{det}(G_{\boldsymbol{z},T})/\mathrm{det}(G_{\boldsymbol{z},T_{0}}))\right)
with probability at least
1-\delta
as
T\gg T_{0}
. Here,
C_{\text{peeling}}
is some dimension and
T
-independent constant invoked by the peeling technique in Theorem 5.4. of
[
Maillard 2016
]
. Thus, we can asymptotically maintain the
\text{O}(d_{\boldsymbol{x}}d_{\boldsymbol{z}}\log^{2}T)
identification regret bound for
\mathsf{O\text{2}SLS}
. Similar relaxations can be derived for all other bounds as an interesting theoretical exercise.

B.9
Discussion on different definitions of regret

Under exogeneity,
[
Ouhamma et al. 2021
, Theorem 3.1]
show that the oracle and population regrets differ by
o(\log^{2}T)
.
In this section, we show it to be not true under endogeneity
.
Thus,
studying the three regrets shows
that the
hardness of identification of underlying model (the identification regret
\widetilde{R}_{T}
), quality of predictions due to unavailability of oracle/true model (oracle regret
\overline{R}_{T}
), and quality of predictions w.r.t. a fixed dataset/population (population regret
R_{T}
) can differ significantly under endogeneity
, whereas they are of similar order under exogeneity.

Historically, econometrics has focused on (asymptotic) correct identification of the estimator
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in presence of endogeneity in order to assign causal meaning to the parameters in a regression model
[
Angrist and Imbens 1995
]
.

Instead, the primary concern of the statistics and statistical learning community has arguably been generalisation (see for example
[
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
]
), with the online learning literature initiated by the works by
Foster 1991
and
Littlestone et al. 1991
.

Under exogeneity, the problem of identifying the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is solved at the same time as minimizing the
\mathsf{MSE}
by the
\mathsf{OLS}
estimator, which is in fact a consistent estimator and is the Minimum Mean Squared Error Estimator (
\mathsf{MMSE}
). This is not true if we introduce the more realistic assumption of endogeneity. In fact the estimator is biased and the
\mathsf{MMSEE}
is different from the oracle
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in this case:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\mathsf{MMSEE}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}y]=\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}\eta],

where we isolated the bias term
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}\eta]
.
The expression simply follows by solving for
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
in

\partial_{\beta^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}y}\left[\left(y-\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right]=0
.
Under exogeneity,
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}\eta]=0
and the
\mathsf{MMSEE}
conicedes with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, while, in the more realistic case of endogenous noise
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}\eta]\neq 0
and the estimator is biased for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Therefore, the oracle is not the best estimator in terms of prediction accuracy.
Instead, we should aim for a consistent estimator of the
\mathsf{MMSE}
, like the Empirical Risk Minimiser (
\mathsf{ERM}
) which leads to the
\mathsf{OLS}
estimator.
In the endogenous setting, the
\mathsf{OLS}
estimator is biased for the oracle
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and this poses the problem of assigning causal meaning to it.

We are interested in finding notions of regret that preserve the causal interpretation of the estimators while allowing a study of their generalisation properties
. This has led us to extend the
instrumental variables
analysis to the online setting and to the inspection of different notions of regret. The regrets that we introduce below, differ from the
population regret
which is usually considered in online learning under the exogeneity assumption. The population regret is defined as

R_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T})\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1})^{2}-\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}

(32)

for a time-dependent estimator
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}
and where we indicated with
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\triangleq\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}
.
Instead, we introduce two alternative regrets that measure the performance of an estimator
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}
with respect to the oracle
\boldsymbol{\beta}
(instead of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}
). Respectively, they are the
oracle regret

\overline{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and the
identification regret

\widetilde{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
below

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1})^{2}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_{t}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2},\quad\quad\quad\widetilde{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}.

Theorem

B.20
shows that an estimator that performs well in terms of
oracle regret
is instead a bad choice for the
population regret
, and the other way around.

Theorem B.20
.

For any estimator, the quantity
\Delta\mathfrak{R}_{T}\triangleq R_{T}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\right)-\overline{R}_{T}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is lower bound in expectation by

\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathfrak{R}_{T}]=\Omega(T)

Proof Sketch.
Solving for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}
leads to the OLS estimator for data up to time
T
:

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}=\boldsymbol{\beta}+\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\eta_{t}
provided that we can invert the design matrix in the previous expression.
Using this expression we ca rewrite
\Delta\mathfrak{R}_{T}
using
\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\triangleq\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}
and
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},T}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}
as follows

\displaystyle\Delta\mathfrak{R}_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\right)^{2}+2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\top}\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}=3\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},T}}.

Thanks to
Lemma

B.8
, we know that the minimum eigenvalue of
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},T}
is
\Omega(T)
which implies
\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},T}}\geq\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\|^{2}_{2}\operatorname{\lambda_{min}}(\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{x},T})\gtrsim\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\|^{2}_{2}T
. Furthermore, we can bound away from zero in expectation
\|\Delta\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}\|_{2}^{2}
by using Cramér–Rao bound on each component of the estimator
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}
, for which we have
\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T,i}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})^{2}\right]\geq\frac{\left[1+b^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})\right]^{2}}{I(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})}+b(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})^{2}
where
b(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T,i}]-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}
is the bias of the estimator and
I(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i})
is the Fisher Information evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}
. We know that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{T}
is a biased estimator of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in the endogenous setting, therefore the bias is strictly positive for at least one component, and this concludes the proof.

D.4
An experiment on real data: Online estimation of gasoline consumption

Motivation in real-life applications.

A real-life example, where applying linear bandits with endogeneity would be important and relevant is the Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAI).

Example D.1

(Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAI)
Tewari and Murphy 2017
)
.

JITAI framework is evoked to build mobile health platforms where a number of decisions have to be taken (e.g. “whether or not to send an activity encouraging message") depending “on a variety of behavior change domains including alcohol abuse, depression, obesity, and substance abuse", and the data regarding a patient’s health condition (“such as GPS location, calendar busyness, and heartrate") is collected through mobile or IoT devices.
[
Tewari and Murphy 2017
]
states that “Contextual bandit algorithms can be used for personalizing JITAIs" (page 2 of
Tewari and Murphy 2017
).

In our contextual bandit formulation, a decision corresponds to an arm, the data regarding a patient form the covariates
X
, and a proximal outcome of a decision (e.g. whether the person gets into an activity or not) leads to the outcome
y
. In this setting, at step
t
, choosing an intervention/decision corresponds to choosing an instrument
Z
. Our goal is to choose a set of instruments at each step
t
so that the health condition of the patient improves the most. This is an online problem by nature.

Performing a stochastic analysis of an online learning algorithm before deployment is important as it tells us expected gains and limitations, which correspond to the health of actual people.
Tewari and Murphy 2017
echo our motivation as “Algorithms designed for the worst-case adversarial framework can perform sub-optimally when data is actually generated stochastically". They also state that one of the “assumptions that make repeated appearance in the theoretical analyses of contextual bandit algorithms" is “independence" (or exogeneity as we call it), and “any candidate online learning algorithm needs to be tested for reasonable departures from these ideal assumptions in simulations before being deployed in a real study with users".

We exactly aim to do that by studying how online regression behaves under endogeneity and what are the departures from well-known behaviours under exogeneity.
Tewari and Murphy 2017
further state that “New algorithms that are more robust to failure of assumptions need to be designed and associated guarantees provided." This discussion makes it evident why studying endogeneity is important and is not a theoretical exercise.

Experiments on LBE with real-world data.

Since we do not have access to datasets like Just-In-Time interventions, which are hardly available in public due to their sensitive nature, we demonstrate the practical experiments on simulated datasets. This is not our construct but a known phenomenon in the existing literature on instrumental variable based bandits
[
Kallus 2018
,
Stirn and Jebara 2018
]
, or tackling action-independent endogeneity
[
Krishnamurthy et al. 2018
]
. But for regression, we use the publicly available dataset from the “Economic Report of the President 2000 and Census Bureau and Department of Energy" on motor gasoline consumption in the United States between 1970 and 1999.
