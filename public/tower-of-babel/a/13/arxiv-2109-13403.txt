Abstract
We calculate the stellar evolution of both white dwarfs (WDs) in AM CVn binaries with orbital periods of P orb ≈ 5 − 70 P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 5-70 minutes. We focus on the cases where the donor starts as a M He < 0.2 ​ M ⊙ M_{\mathrm{He}}<0.2\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}} Helium WD and the accretor is a M WD > 0.6 ​ M ⊙ M_{\mathrm{WD}}>0.6\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}} WD. Using Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (MESA), we simultaneously evolve both WDs assuming conservative mass transfer and angular momentum loss from gravitational radiation. This self-consistent evolution yields the important feedback of the properties of the donor on the mass transfer rate, M ˙ \dot{M} , as well as the thermal evolution of the accreting WD. Consistent with earlier work, we find that the high M ˙ \dot{M} ’s at early times forces an adiabatic evolution of the donor for P orb < 30 P_{\mathrm{orb}}<30 minutes so that its mass-radius relation depends primarily on its initial entropy. As the donor reaches M He ≈ 0.02 − 0.03 ​ M ⊙ M_{\mathrm{He}}\approx 0.02-0.03\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}} at P orb ≃ 30 P_{\mathrm{orb}}\simeq 30 minutes, it becomes fully convective and could lose entropy and expand much less than expected under further mass loss. However, we show that the lack of reliable opacities for the donor’s surface inhibit a secure prediction for this possible cooling. Our calculations capture the core heating that occurs during the first ≈ 10 7 \approx 10^{7} years of accretion and continue the evolution into the phase of WD cooling that follows. When compared to existing data for accreting WDs, as seen by Cheng and collaborators for isolated WDs, we also find that the accreting WDs are not as cool as we would expect given the amount of time they have had to cool.



I
Introduction

AM Canum Venaticorum (AM CVn) systems are ultracompact binaries undergoing helium mass-transfer with orbital periods,
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
, between 5 and 68 minutes
(
Nather et al. 1981
;
Nelemans et al. 2001
;
Nelemans et al. 2004
;
Ramsay et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. Their orbital evolution is dominated by loss of angular momentum from gravitational wave radiation
(
Tutukov & Yungelson 1979
;
Tutukov & Yungelson 1981
, e.g.,)
, and the gravitational wave signal from AM CVn systems should be detectable by missions such as the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna
(
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2013
;
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2017
;
Nelemans et al. 2004
;
Kremer et al. 2017
;
Breivik et al. 2018
, LISA;)
. The study of AM CVn systems is of interest for several reasons. Their orbital evolution can help constrain the efficiency of tides in degenerate stars
(
Piro 2019
, e.g.,)
. Unstable helium burning is expected to occur in some AM CVn systems, leading to helium novae
(
Ashok & Banerjee 2003
, e.g.,)
, and in the case of dynamical helium-burning, faint thermonuclear “.Ia” supernovae
(
Bildsten et al. 2007
;
Shen & Bildsten 2009
)
. Accretion is expected to occur via an accretion disk
(
Marsh et al. 2004
, except at shortest periods where direct-impact accretion occurs; e.g., )
, which allows AM CVn systems to be good testing grounds for the theory of nearly pure helium accretion disks
(
Kotko et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
.

There are three possible channels for the formation of AM CVn systems. In the helium white dwarf (He WD) donor scenario, the initial donor is a degenerate, low-mass (
\approx 0.1-0.2\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
) helium white dwarf
(
Deloye et al. 2007
, e.g.,)
. In the helium star (He star) donor scenario, the donor starts mass-transfer as a non-degenerate, helium-burning star with masses ranging from
\approx 0.3
to
0.7\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}

(
Iben & Tutukov 1991
;
Yungelson 2008
, e.g.,)
. Note that at long periods
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 40\,\mathrm{min}
, the thermal properties of the He WD and He star donors are predicted to converge
(
Deloye et al. 2007
;
Yungelson 2008
)
. The two remain distinguishable by their compositions, with the He star donors expected to contain products of helium-burning. In the cataclysmic variable (CV) donor channel
(
Podsiadlowski et al. 2003
, e.g.,)
, the donor starts as a
\approx 1\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
star, initiates hydrogen-rich mass transfer around the end of core hydrogen-burning, and eventually loses its hydrogen-rich envelope. In all three channels, the accretor is often assumed to be a
0.6-1.0\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
carbon-oxygen white dwarf (CO WD).

In this work, we revisit the calculations by
Bildsten et al. 2006
on the thermal evolution of the CO WD accretor in the context of the He WD donor channel. In Section
II
, we describe our computational setup and construction of initial stellar models for both the donor and the accretor. We evolve donor models of various initial central entropies under mass transfer to a point-mass, and describe their thermal evolution in Section
III
. We show that the mass-radius relation of the donor, as well as the time evolution of
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
, depends on their initial central entropy and whether they can cool. In Section
IV
, we evolve the CO WD accretor along with the donor and the binary orbit. Our results agree with
Bildsten et al. 2006
but are more consistent as our models track the time-dependent cooling of the donor.
At high mass transfer rates,
\dot{M}
, near period minimum, the accretor is reheated due to accretion; as the orbit widens and
\dot{M}
drops, the accretor luminosity eventually becomes just that of a cooling WD. We compute synthetic color-magnitude diagrams for our accretor models for comparisons with observations in Section
V
. We show that observed AM CVn systems appear bluer and brighter than expected, which corresponds to a younger WD cooling age. We conclude in Section
VI
.

In the following, we denote the accretor and the donor by the subscripts WD and He. Quantities at the center of the stars are denoted by the subscript
c
. Initial and final quantities are denoted by the superscripts
i
and
f
. Age since initiation of mass transfer will be used synonymously with age.

II
Computational Setup and Initial Stellar Model Construction

To obtain realistic, time-dependent mass transfer histories for AM CVn binaries, we self-consistently evolve a He WD donor and a CO WD accretor using the
\tt binary
capability of the stellar evolution instrument Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (
{\tt MESA}
version 12778;
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Paxton et al. 2013
;
Paxton et al. 2015
;
Paxton et al. 2018
;
Paxton et al. 2019
).
The equation of state (EOS) adopted for the WDs in this study is a blend among OPAL
(
Rogers & Nayfonov 2002
)
and SCVH
(
Saumon et al. 1995
)
at low densities (
\log_{10}(\rho/\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3})\lesssim 2.9
), and PC at high densities
(
Potekhin & Chabrier 2010
,
\log_{10}(\rho/\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3})\gtrsim 2.9
)
. Radiative opacities, where available, are taken from OPAL
(
Iglesias & Rogers 1993
;
Iglesias & Rogers 1996
)
at
\log_{10}(T/\mathrm{K})\gtrsim 3.8
and
Ferguson et al. 2005
at
\log_{10}(T/\mathrm{K})\lesssim 3.8
, and extrapolated otherwise (see Section
III.3
). Electron conductive opacities are from
Cassisi et al. 2007
with corrections from
Blouin et al. 2020
for He under moderate coupling and moderate degeneracy. The latter corrections are implemented by modifying our copy of the
{\tt MESA}
source code, but will be included in future releases of
{\tt MESA}
. Our nuclear net includes the NCO reaction chain
(
Bauer et al. 2017
)
which may impact the helium shell thickness for unstable burning on the accretor. The models presented in this work differ slightly from an earlier version of models presented in
van Roestel et al. 2021
. The latter adopted the HELM EOS
(
Timmes & Swesty 2000
)
during the early phases of mass transfer for the He WD donor, and included no electron conduction opacity correction from
Blouin et al. 2020
for both WDs. However, difference in quantities like luminosity is insignificant (of order 10%). Our
{\tt MESA}
input files are available at Zenodo
1
1

1

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5532940
.

The He WD models are made using a modified version of the
\tt make\_he\_wd
test suite, where a 1.5
\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
star with metallicity
Z=0.02
is evolved from the zero-age main sequence (ZAMS) to the formation of an inert helium core of mass
0.15-0.18\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
. We then strip off the envelope, and allow the bare He core to cool to the desired central entropy. Extremely low mass (ELM) He WDs are expected to have a hydrogen envelope of mass
\approx 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}

(
Istrate et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. When accreted onto the CO WD the hydrogen may undergo unstable nuclear burning
(
Kaplan et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
, which is computationally expensive to follow and has no thermal impact on the accretor, hence we neglect it in our study. We leave the He WD with almost no hydrogen envelope (
M_{\mathrm{H}}\lesssim 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
), which only impacts the initial mass transfer phase, but has no impact on the mass transfer at late times
(
Kaplan et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
.

The He WD models have initial specific central entropies of
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=2.23,2.61,3.07,3.52
, where
N_{\mathrm{A}}
is Avogadro’s number.
In
Deloye et al. 2007
, the donor models are labeled by their initial central degeneracy parameter,
\psi^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}=E_{\mathrm{F,c}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\rm c}\approx\rho_{\rm c}/(1.2\times 10^{-8}T_{\rm c}^{3/2})\,\mathrm{K}^{3/2}\mathrm{cm}^{3}\mathrm{g}^{-1}
, where
T_{\rm c}
,
\rho_{\rm c}
and
E_{\mathrm{F,c}}
are the initial temperature, density and electron Fermi energy at the center of the He WD. Our models correspond to
\log_{10}(\psi^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}})=2.89,2.39,1.82,1.34
respectively.
Our models are chosen to be similar in range of
\psi^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}
to those in
Deloye et al. 2007
.
Deloye et al. 2007
determined the distribution of
\psi^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}
at contact (see their Fig. 3) based on post-common envelope conditions of the surviving double WD systems in the population synthesis study of
Nelemans et al. 2001
, and modeled the mass transfer of He WDs with
\log_{10}(\psi^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}})\approx 1.1-3.5
. Our coldest model is less degenerate than theirs due to inadequate EOS coverage in
{\tt MESA}
(see below).

The CO WD models are similarly made using a modified version of the
\tt make\_co\_wd
test suite. We evolve a ZAMS model of mass
3-6\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
with metallicity
Z=0.02
up to helium shell burning and the formation of a CO core of the desired mass. We then remove the envelope and allow the CO core to cool to the desired starting core temperature.

The He WD and CO WD models are then taken as initial models in
{\tt MESA}

\tt binary
which self-consistently evolves the stellar structures of both stars and the orbital parameters. We assume fully conservative mass transfer at a rate
\dot{M}
and model it using the Ritter scheme
(
Ritter 1988
)
. We assume that orbital angular momentum loss is only due to the emission of gravitational waves. While the accretor may be significantly spun up by the accreted material at the expense of orbital angular momentum
(
Marsh et al. 2004
;
Gokhale et al. 2007
)
, we choose to model both stars as non-rotating as a first approximation. Tides may play an important role in synchronizing both components, which helps stabilize the orbit, and in impacting their thermal evolution through tidal dissipation
(
Marsh et al. 2004
;
Fuller & Lai 2012
;
Fuller & Lai 2014
, e.g.,)
.
Observations show that at long periods (
P_{\mathrm{orb}}=46\,\mathrm{min}
for GP Com and
65\,\mathrm{min}
for V396 Hya) the accretor is rotating far slower than critical
(
Kupfer et al. 2016
)
.

The binary simulations begin with an initial orbital period of
\approx 20
minutes with both components detached. The orbit decays due to gravitational wave radiation and the He WD eventually fills its Roche lobe. We follow the evolution of the system until the donor has cooled down sufficiently that
\log Q=\log\rho-2\log T+12\geqslant 5
in any of the cells. This choice was made because for
\log Q\geqslant 5
,
{\tt MESA}
switches to an ideal gas equation of state which affects the donor mass-radius relation and subsequently the orbital evolution. This typically occurs only when
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 60\,\mathrm{min}
, allowing us to compare our predictions with all known AM CVn systems. Future work with
{\tt MESA}
incorporating the EOS’s of
Chabrier et al. 2019
and
Jermyn et al. 2021
can probe the further evolution of the donor at even lower temperatures with better numerical accuracy.

For the accretor, we set
\tt tau\_factor=30
for numerical convenience, which places the outermost cell at an optical depth of
\tau=2/3\times 30=20
. We also opted to adopt a grey Eddington
T-\tau
relation instead of interpolating from the DB tables provided by Odette Toloza and Detlev Koester. The level of discrepancy in the accretor effective temperature for these two approaches is
\lesssim 10\%
. We allow the latent heat of crystallization to be released at a Coulomb coupling parameter
\Gamma=\langle Z^{5/3}_{i}\rangle e^{2}/a_{e}k_{\mathrm{B}}T
between 225 and 235
(
Bauer et al. 2020
, see equation 1 of)
, where
\langle Z^{5/3}_{i}\rangle
is an average of
Z^{5/3}_{i}
over all ion species with charge
Z_{i}
, and
a_{e}=(3/4\pi n_{e})^{1/3}
is the electron separation.

As we show in Section
III
, the initial thermal properties and subsequent thermal evolution of the donor are important in setting the binary orbital evolution and
\dot{M}
history.
Therefore, for each
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.15\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
model of fixed
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}
, we generate an adiabatic mass-radius relation, by stripping the
0.15\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
He WD model to the desired mass via the
\tt relax\_mass
control in
\tt MESA
. The rate of mass change for this construction is chosen such that the core of the model evolves adiabatically.
We obtain the cold WD (fully degenerate) limit similarly by stripping the
0.15\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
He WD models to the desired mass, and subsequently allowing them to cool to a central temperature of
T_{\mathrm{c,He}}=10^{5}\,\mathrm{K}
. To avoid the
\log Q\geqslant 5
stopping condition, we irradiate the surface layer of the He WD model. This is done in part for numerical convenience, but is also expected for realistic AM CVn systems where, on geometrical grounds, the accreting WD should strongly irradiate the donor.

For comparison, we also evolved a
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.35\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
He star model taken from
Brooks et al. 2015
. We chose
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.5\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
and
P^{i}_{\mathrm{orb}}=20\,\mathrm{min}
, which corresponds to the first model shown in Table 1 of their paper and of
Yungelson 2008
.
We also obtained an adiabatic mass-radius relation for the He star using the
\tt relax\_mass
control described above. The initial model for this construction is taken from the binary simulation when core He burning is quenched in the He star and its mass decreases to
0.2\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
.

III.1
Donor Mass-Radius Relation

The resulting donor’s mass-radius relations are shown in the top panel of Figure
2
, and the associated power-law index
n=\mathrm{d}\ln R_{\mathrm{He}}/\mathrm{d}\ln M_{\mathrm{He}}
in the bottom panel. Initially, the donor contracts (
n>0
) as the outermost radiative layer is stripped off (see
Deloye et al. 2007
and
Kaplan et al. 2012
for in-depth discussions). Eventually, the underlying layers drive an expansion in radius (
n<0
), with the lowest entropy model closest to
n=-1/3
as expected for a fully degenerate object. In general, the less degenerate, higher entropy models show a more positive
n
. As
M_{\mathrm{He}}
decreases, the power-law slope becomes more positive, in part because Coulomb effects become more important
(
Deloye & Bildsten 2003
)
.

For less degenerate donors (especially the
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.52
donor),
n
becomes more positive when they start to cool. Initially, the donors evolve adiabatically as the mass change timescale,
\tau_{\mathrm{m}}=M_{\mathrm{He}}/\dot{M}
, is much shorter than the thermal timescale,
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=\int c_{\mathrm{p}}T\mathrm{d}m/L_{\mathrm{He}}
. This is confirmed by the agreement of the
R(M)
relations from the binary calculations (solid lines) and the adiabatic models (dot-dashed lines) in Figure
2
, below
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 30\,\mathrm{min}
when
\tau_{\mathrm{m}}\ll\tau_{\mathrm{th}}
.
As
\dot{M}
drops with time, eventually
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}\lesssim\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
(
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
is labeled by square symbols). Then the donor becomes fully convective (labeled by circle symbols) and starts to cool
(
Deloye et al. 2007
)
, as we discuss in the end of this section. Therefore, less degenerate donors start to converge to the mass-radius relation of a fully degenerate He WD, as seen in Figure
2

(
Deloye et al. 2007
, see also Fig. 8 of)
.

The mass-radius relation depends only slightly on
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}
, as seen in Figure
2
. At a given
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}
, the difference between the
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.15\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
(solid lines) and
0.18\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
(dashed lines) tracks is only evident at larger
M_{\mathrm{He}}
(
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\lesssim 20\,\mathrm{min}
), and is negligible at small
M_{\mathrm{He}}
especially when donor cooling happens.

III.2
Mass Accretion Rate Histories

We show the time evolution of
\dot{M}
in the top panel of Figure
4
. As the He WD donor fills its Roche lobe, the mass transfer rate rapidly rises to
\sim 10^{-7}-10^{-8}

\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
.
All
\dot{M}
’s peak within
10^{6}
yrs after mass transfer initiates, with more degenerate donors having higher
\dot{M}
due to their smaller radii
(
Kaplan et al. 2012
, e.g., )
. Afterwards,
\dot{M}\propto t^{-1.3}
as analytically expected
(
Bildsten et al. 2006
)
. At a fixed age,
\dot{M}
is lower for more degenerate donors. The ordering of
\dot{M}
with donor degeneracy both at peak
\dot{M}
and in the power-law phase agrees with
Deloye et al. 2007
(their Fig. 6). The top panel of Figure
4
also shows that
\dot{M}
does not vary significantly with
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}
, as shown by the models with
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
and
1.05\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
at
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.07
.

The time evolution of
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
is tied intimately to the donor’s mass-radius relation and its thermal evolution, as shown in the middle panel of Figure
4
. Less degenerate donors have a larger
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
at period minimum, but
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
evolution with time slows down after an age of
\approx 10^{8}
yrs, so that at the end, more degenerate donors catch up and have larger
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
at a fixed age. For the less degenerate donors, the reason for their slower time evolution is that they eventually cool when
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}\lesssim\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
.

Due to increased orbital angular momentum loss from gravitational wave emission, a higher total mass also speeds up the
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
evolution with time.
This is more clearly shown by the pair of
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.07
models with different
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}
, and barely observable in the pairs of models with different
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}
. However, a higher total mass increases the likelihood of a helium flash being triggered on the accretor, since less accumulated mass on the accretor is required given a higher
\dot{M}
(a higher
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}
leads to a higher peak
\dot{M}
; see top panel of Fig
4
) and a higher
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}

(
Bauer et al. 2017
, e.g., )
. Indeed, our calculations that simultaneously evolve the stellar structure of the accretor show that the
M_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
accretor undergoes a helium flash for the
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.18\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
,
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.07
model at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 6\,\mathrm{min}
, while the
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.52
model avoids a helium flash due to the countering effect of a higher central entropy.

We show the evolution of
\dot{M}
with
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
in Figure
6
. For
P_{\mathrm{orb}}<40
min, an initially hotter donor gives a higher
\dot{M}
at fixed
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
. But starting at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 30

\dot{M}

\dot{M}
drops more sharply with
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
. As analytically derived by
Cannizzo & Nelemans 2015

\dot{M}\propto P_{\mathrm{orb}}^{\xi}
, where
\xi=4(5-6n)/3(3n-1)
. Initially as
n\approx-1/3
,
\xi\approx-4.67
, but during cooling,
n\approx 0
and
\xi\approx-6.67
. Thus, the sharp drop in
\dot{M}
with
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
is consistent with analytical expectations. Even though hotter donors are at longer
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
at an age of
10^{8}
yrs (due to a larger minimum
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
), this ordering reverses before an age of
10^{9}
yrs.

III.3
Donor Thermal Evolution Uncertainties Under Mass Transfer

The impact of the thermal evolution of the donor is substantial for the observed
\dot{M}(P_{\mathrm{orb}})
and
P_{\mathrm{orb}}(t)
relations and so deserves some additional scrutiny. This evolution is set by both the initial entropy and whether entropy can be lost as mass is transferred, which we now discuss.

The donor can only cool when its thermal timescale,
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}
, is comparable to or shorter than the mass transfer timescale
\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
.
The former is inversely proportional to the donor luminosity,
L_{\mathrm{He}}
, so lowering
L_{\mathrm{He}}
may delay the cooling of the donor, and speed up the evolution of
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
with age.

Figure 7:

Figure 8:
Density-temperature profiles of the donor (left panel), and entropy profiles of the donor (right panel), both color-coded by
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
. Convection zones are labeled by thick blue lines. All profiles range from
P_{\mathrm{orb}}=10
to 60 min in 10 min intervals, and we also include the initial model (
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 20

The thermal evolution of the donor with
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
and
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}/N_{\mathrm{A}}k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.07
is shown in Figure
8
. The left panel shows a sequence of temperature-density profiles color-coded by
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
, and the right panel shows the entropy profiles of the same models. The black dashed line in the left panel shows the evolution of the core in
T-\rho
space. At
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\lesssim 30\,\mathrm{min}
, the core evolves adiabatically as
\tau_{\mathrm{m}}\lesssim\tau_{\mathrm{th}}
, and starts to cool for
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 30\,\mathrm{min}
. This is corroborated by the entropy profiles, which show that the central entropy is roughly constant for
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\lesssim 30\,\mathrm{min}
and only drops due to cooling at longer periods.
This decrease of central entropy happens even before the donor becomes fully convective. Due to radiative diffusion, heat is always lost from the center, i.e.,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{grav},c}\equiv-T_{c}(\mathrm{d}S_{c}/\mathrm{d}t)>0
. The change in central entropy becomes apparent when
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 30\,\mathrm{min}
as the timescale increases from
\sim 10^{7}\,\mathrm{yr}
to
\sim 10^{8}-10^{9}\,\mathrm{yr}
and
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}
eventually becomes comparable to, or shorter than,
\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
. However, becoming fully convective accelerates the loss of entropy from the center, the occurrence of which we now describe.

Before the donor becomes fully convective and cools, it has an outer convective zone (blue thick lines in Figure
8
), and a radiative core. The outgoing luminosity in the thin radiative surface is set by
L/M_{\mathrm{He}}=(64\pi G/3)(\sigma_{\mathrm{sb}}T^{4}/\kappa P)\nabla_{\mathrm{ad}}\propto 1/\kappa
, at the top of the convective boundary
(
Arras & Bildsten 2006
)
.
Thus, the cooling luminosity of the donor is inversely proportional to the opacity at the top radiative-convective boundary for a fully convective star. At
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 20\,\mathrm{min}
, this is at
\log_{10}(\rho/\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3})\approx 0
and
\log_{10}(T/\mathrm{K})\lesssim 4
. However, there are no radiative opacity tables currently at this range, and instead
{\tt MESA}
extrapolates from the
Ferguson et al. 2005
low-temperature opacity tables at the same
\log_{10}T
. We defer investigation of the opacity to future studies, and note that a higher opacity at the top radiative-convective boundary can delay cooling of the donor and affect the age-period relation. We also note that irradiation can play an important role in keeping the top radiative-convective boundary at a higher
T
(and potentially
\kappa
), but this is subject to the same opacity uncertainties described above.

The same uncertainty in the donor cooling physics exists for the He star donors at late times. From table 1 of
Yungelson 2008
, the typical age and
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
for
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.35-0.4\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
are a few hundred Myrs and
\approx 40
min at the end of their calculations. If we take the corresponding binary parameters, and integrate forward taking
n=-0.16
, we get an age since mass transfer between
\approx 1.5
and
3
Gyrs at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}=65
min. These are lower limits nonetheless, since
n=-0.16
is fitted for
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 10-35
min. Eventually the He star donors should start to cool
(
Deloye et al. 2007
)
, as hinted by
n\gtrsim 0
at low masses for the mass-radius relation in Fig. 4 of
Yungelson 2008
.
Indeed, this is explicitly shown by our He star model in the middle panel of Figure
4
, where the age increases significantly around
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 40\,\mathrm{min}
as the He star starts to cool (see also Figure
2
).

To summarize, we have quantified the scatter in the age-
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
relation considering all He WD models with donor cooling. In the middle panel of Figure
4
, the range of ages at which
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 65
min is
4-5
Gyrs.
The importance of the period-age relation, itself tied closely to the thermal properties of the donor, will become clear once we discuss the heating and cooling of the accreting WD in Section
IV
.

We now focus on the effects of mass transfer on the accretor properties, particularly

T^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}

M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75

Once settled onto the more massive CO WD, the accreted He releases heat while it is compressed deep in the envelope. At the initially high
\dot{M}\sim 10^{-8}-10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
, the rate of compression is higher than that of heat transport, resulting in a local temperature increase
(
Nomoto 1982
;
Townsley & Bildsten 2004
)
. This “compressional heating” creates a temperature inversion in the envelope, clearly seen in Panel (a) of Figure
9
, as the sequence of temperature-density profiles of the accretor at various times (color-coded by accretor mass). Heat transport from the temperature peak occurs both towards the surface and into the colder core. The latter can be seen as a thermal front propagating to the center in Figure
9
, which reaches the center in a few
10^{7}
years after the start of mass transfer. Before then, the core evolves adiabatically. The
(1\mathrm{-}3)\times 10^{7}
years for the core to become heated is consistent with the heat conduction time,
t_{\mathrm{cond}}
, as derived in
Townsley & Bildsten 2004
(their equation A2). All models presented in this section avoided the occurrence of a helium flash, as the temperature of the helium layer never reached a value adequate to trigger a thermonuclear instability (see Figure
9
).

When the age since mass transfer is comparable to
t_{\mathrm{cond}}\approx(1\mathrm{-}3)\times 10^{7}
yr,
T_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
reaches a maximum and the WD cools.
By then,
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 20
minutes and the donor has reached
0.03\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
. The mass accretion rate,
\dot{M}
, has dropped from peak by 1-2 orders-of-magnitude (see Figure
4
), diminishing the importance of compressional heating and leading to a transition from core heating to core cooling
(
Bildsten et al. 2006
)
. Panel (a) of Figure
9
shows that near peak
T_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
, the temperature inversion gradually disappears as the core is heated due to the inward heat flow. We show the accretor profiles in the subsequent cooling phase in Panel (b) of Figure
9
, color-coded by orbital period. As the WD further cools, the surface convection zone deepens and eventually crystallization starts when
\Gamma_{c}=225
, at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 50
min.

The time evolution of
T_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
and
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
is shown in Figure
11
. Consistent with Figure
9
, the core first evolves adiabatically, then becomes significantly heated once the conduction front reaches the center at
10^{7}\,\mathrm{yrs}
, and cools thereafter. An initially cooler WD is heated to a lower peak
T_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
and hence shows a lower
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
around peak
T_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
. We note that, if a steadily accreting disk is present, its bolometric accretion luminosity
L_{\mathrm{acc}}\equiv GM_{\mathrm{WD}}\dot{M}/2R_{\mathrm{WD}}
would always dominate
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
. We discuss the challenges of differentiating between luminosity from accretion and from the cooling WD in the Appendix.

Recent calculations by
Blouin et al. 2020
show a lower conductive opacity in WD envelopes, than the
Cassisi et al. 2007
conductive opacities which are used in typical WD cooling models
(
Bédard et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. We incorporated this correction in all AM CVn models presented in this work. Compared to a model without this correction, the reheated accretor reaches a slightly lower peak central temperature, due to faster transport of heat out of the envelope. In the cooling phase, the accretor has a higher luminosity at the same central temperature (by
\approx 30\%
) and cools faster. Equivalently, in well agreement with
Blouin et al. 2020
, this leads to a lower luminosity at a fixed cooling age. However, the difference in inferred cooling ages is much less than 1 Gyr, for a
0.9\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
DB WD with
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 10^{4}\,\mathrm{K}
(their Fig. 4), so its effect on our calculations is small.

We compare our results with
Bildsten et al. 2006
, who modeled the thermal state of the accretor using quasistatic envelope methods. For early times, before the core gets heated, they found that the WD luminosity varies only with
\dot{M}
and
M_{\mathrm{WD}}
as
L_{\mathrm{WD}}\propto\dot{M}^{1.4}M_{\mathrm{WD}}^{-0.3}
for
\dot{M}>10^{-9}\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
. We compare this expression with our
{\tt MESA}
results in Figure
13
. For
\dot{M}>3\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
, the
{\tt MESA}
models show no dependence on
T^{i}_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
as expected
(
Bildsten et al. 2006
)
. Compressional heating dominates
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
at early times. The power-law dependence of
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
on
\dot{M}
is slightly shallower than predicted. For
\dot{M}\lesssim 3\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
, the WD cooling luminosity starts to dominate, and an initially cooler WD is heated to a smaller peak
T_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
(see Figure
11
). Thus between
\dot{M}=10^{-10}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
and
3\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
, a dependence of
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
on
T^{i}_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
is seen, with a lower
T^{i}_{c,\mathrm{WD}}
giving a lower
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
at fixed
\dot{M}
.

However, inferring the cooling ages of AM CVn accretors (see Section
V
) is subject to the caveat that, compared to normal CO WDs, AM CVn accretors may have a significant fraction of their total mass in a thick He shell, if unstable He burning can be avoided (e.g., our
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.15\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
,
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
model would have
q_{\mathrm{He}}=M_{\mathrm{He}}/M_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.15/0.9\approx 0.17
in the end).
Since He has a different specific heat capacity than a C/O mixture, the cooling luminosity of an AM CVn accretor would be different than that of a CO WD of the same total mass, at a given cooling age.

If we combine
L_{\mathrm{WD}}=-C_{\mathrm{V}}(\mathrm{d}T_{\rm c}/\mathrm{d}t)
, where
C_{\mathrm{V}}
is the total heat capacity, with
L_{\mathrm{WD}}\propto T_{\rm c}^{5/2}

(
Townsley & Bildsten 2004
, e.g.,)
and solve for the cooling time
\tau_{\mathrm{cool}}
, then
\tau_{\mathrm{cool}}\propto C_{\mathrm{V}}L_{\mathrm{WD}}^{-3/5}
.
As seen in Figure
9
, at the ages
1-5
Gyrs that we are interested in, the C/O is near the crystallization limit and the He is approximately in the liquid state. Then we can approximate, for an AM CVn accretor that has a C/O core mass
M_{\mathrm{CO}}
and He shell mass
M_{\mathrm{He}}
,
C_{\mathrm{V}}\approx 3(k_{\mathrm{B}}M_{\mathrm{CO}}/\mu_{\mathrm{CO}}m_{\mathrm{p}})+2(k_{\mathrm{B}}M_{\mathrm{He}}/\mu_{\mathrm{He}}m_{\mathrm{p}})
, where
\mu_{\mathrm{He}}=4
,
\mu_{\mathrm{CO}}\approx 14
, and the specific heat capacities for C/O and He are motivated by our
{\tt MESA}
models
(
Baiko & Yakovlev 2019
, see also,)
. Similarly, for a CO WD of the same total mass
M_{\mathrm{tot}}=M_{\mathrm{CO}}+M_{\mathrm{He}}
,
C_{\mathrm{V}}\approx 3(k_{\mathrm{B}}M_{\mathrm{tot}}/\mu_{\mathrm{CO}}m_{\mathrm{p}})
.
Hence, for a CO WD and an AM CVn accretor of the same total mass
M_{\mathrm{tot}}
and cooling luminosity
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
, the ratio of their cooling ages is
\tau_{\mathrm{cool,AM\,CVn}}/\tau_{\mathrm{cool,C/O}}\approx(M_{\mathrm{CO}}/M_{\mathrm{tot}})+(2/3)(\mu_{\mathrm{CO}}/\mu_{\mathrm{He}})(M_{\mathrm{He}}/M_{\mathrm{tot}})
. For our fiducial case,
M^{i}_{\mathrm{He}}=0.15\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
and
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
, this ratio is about
1.22
.

Therefore, when comparing the contours of constant age for CO WDs with thin He envelopes (
q_{\mathrm{He}}=10^{-2}
) in Figure
19
to observed data points, the AM CVn accretors may have older actual cooling ages than inferred, by tens of percent. Nonetheless, as we will show in Section
V
, there exists a cooling age discrepancy of
\approx 3-4
Gyrs at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 65\,\mathrm{min}
between theory and observations, so the corrections to the cooling age presented here are insignificant.

V
Comparison to Observations

We showed that at
\dot{M}\lesssim 3\times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
, the accretor luminosity is just that of a cooling WD.
If
in addition we assume that, active accretion is not contaminating the data
(
Bildsten et al. 2006
)
, then combining a cooling WD model with the age-
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
relation yields a theoretical prediction for the luminosity of an AM CVn system at a given
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
. We compare this prediction for the accretor to observed systems, and show that the observed systems appear
\approx 3-4\,\mathrm{Gyrs}
younger than predicted at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 65\,\mathrm{min}
.

To compare with observations, we compute absolute magnitudes from our
{\tt MESA}
models. Given an effective temperature,
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
, and surface gravity,
\log g
, from
{\tt MESA}
, we interpolate from the pure-helium model atmosphere table from the Montreal group
(
Bergeron et al. 2011
;
Blouin et al. 2018
;
Bédard et al. 2020
)
2
2

2

https://www.astro.umontreal.ca/ bergeron/CoolingModels/ (catalog https://www.astro.umontreal.ca/$∼$bergeron/CoolingModels/)
and obtain a bolometric correction for various photometric systems.

Our V-band absolute magnitude,
M_{\mathrm{V}}
, when plotted against
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
, follows closely the “cool” donor track of
Bildsten et al. 2006
. However, we discovered that their
M_{\mathrm{V}}
is always lower by
\approx 0.7\,\mathrm{mag}
(i.e., brighter) than the
M_{\mathrm{V}}
we obtain using the Montreal bolometric corrections with the corresponding
T_{\mathrm{eff}}
and an assumed
\log g=8.5
. For example, at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 63\,\mathrm{min}
, their “cool” donor model has
T_{\mathrm{eff,WD}}\approx 6000\,\mathrm{K}
which should give
M_{\mathrm{V}}\approx 14.949
, but their
M_{\mathrm{V}}
reads
\approx 14.3
instead. This explains why our
M_{\mathrm{V}}-P_{\mathrm{orb}}
track agrees better with their “cool” donor track than with their “hot” donor track, despite better agreement in
T_{\mathrm{eff}}-P_{\mathrm{orb}}
with the latter.

Regardless, as shown by
Ramsay et al. 2018
(their Fig. 2), our
V
or
g
-band absolute magnitudes,
M_{\mathrm{V}}
or
M_{\mathrm{g}}
, are lower than observed by 2 magnitudes at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 60
min. We show this discrepancy instead by comparing our
{\tt MESA}
tracks on a color-magnitude diagram (
M_{\mathrm{g}}
vs
g-r
for SDSS and Pan-STARRS-1 filters; Fig
19
), to observed AM CVn systems with
P_{\mathrm{orb}}>50
min, both color-coded by
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
. By
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 20
min, our
M^{i}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.75\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
(or
0.85\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
) accretor has accumulated most of the donor’s mass, and starts to cool as a
M^{f}_{\mathrm{WD}}=0.9\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
(or
1.0\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
) WD. This explains the good agreement of our
{\tt MESA}
tracks even before
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 40
min with the corresponding
0.9\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
(or
1.0\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
) cooling tracks from
Bédard et al. 2020
.

Nevertheless, even though the observed long-period systems lie close to the WD cooling tracks, they appear brighter and bluer than expected from our
{\tt MESA}
tracks for the corresponding
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
.
If
we interpret the observed source as dominated by the luminosity of the cooling WD, then their photometric cooling ages, as inferred from the CMD, are between 0.5 and 2 Gyr. In contrast, the

age-period relation (Sec

4-5
Gyr at

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we perform binary calculations with
{\tt MESA}
for AM CVn binaries with a He WD donor. In Section
III
, we evolved He WD donor models of various initial central specific entropies,
S^{i}_{c,\mathrm{He}}
, and showed that the initial entropy and the subsequent thermal evolution of the donor dictates the mass transfer history and hence the age-period evolution of the binary. We find that at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 40\,\mathrm{min}
, the donor starts to cool as its thermal timescale,
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}
, is comparable to or shorter than its mass transfer timescale,
\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
, although this is subject to uncertainties in the surface opacity as described in Section
III.3
. We then evolve the accretor along with the donor and the orbit in Section
IV
. At the initially high
\dot{M}
near period minimum, the accretor is heated due to compressional heating. Later as
\dot{M}
drops while the orbit widens, the accretor behaves simply as a cooling WD (at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 30\,\mathrm{min}
). Our calculations show well agreement with the semi-analytic predictions of
Bildsten et al. 2006
, but are more accurate since we self-consistently consistent both binary components.

Given that we theoretically expect that accretor is simply a cooling WD at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 30\,\mathrm{min}
, we compute synthetic color-magnitude diagrams for our accretor models and compare with observations
(
Ramsay et al. 2018
)
in Section
V
. We show that the observed systems,
if
interpreted as the cooling WD, appear much younger (in terms of cooling age) than expected at the corresponding
P_{\mathrm{orb}}
(by
\approx 3-4
Gyrs at
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\approx 65
min).
This may be attributed in part to uncertainties in the initial entropy distribution and subsequent cooling of the donor, which affects the age-period relation of our models. Better understanding of the donor cooling can be obtained by incorporating opacities of warm dense helium in
{\tt MESA}
. Another possibility is that the observed luminosities are contaminated by the accretion disk or boundary layer (discussed in the Appendix), although this appears unlikely in the case of Gaia14aae as revealed by eclipse modeling
(
Green et al. 2018
)
. Finally, isolated WDs show evidence of a cooling delay
(
Cheng et al. 2019
;
Kilic et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, and we speculate that a similar phenomenon in the accretor may well explain the observed discrepancy. The recent discovery of 5 new eclipsing AM CVn systems with
P_{\mathrm{orb}}=35-62\,\mathrm{min}
by
van Roestel et al. 2021
, which constrains the luminosity contributions from different components and the degree of cooling of the donor, will help disentangle these various scenarios.

Appendix A
Accretion Luminosity

We now address the possibility that observed AM CVn systems have contributions from the accretion disk or boundary layer. As the bottom panel of Figure
11
shows, if accretion is steady, the accretion luminosity,
L_{\mathrm{acc}}\equiv GM_{\mathrm{WD}}\dot{M}/2R_{\mathrm{WD}}
, would be more than 5 times greater than the WD luminosity,
L_{\mathrm{WD}}
, at all times.

If
we assume steady-state accretion, and model the disk as a multicolor blackbody, we would expect the disk to dominate over the cooling WD in the V-band. We note that
Bildsten et al. 2006
proposed the opposite, because they assumed a uniform, averaged disk temperature defined by
L_{\mathrm{acc}}=S\sigma T^{4}
, where
S
is the disk surface area. Their approach underestimated the temperature in the inner disk and hence the disk contribution to the
V
-band (again, assuming steady state). This implies we cannot expect
a priori
that the cooling WD dominates over the disk contribution in the V-band.

However, the disk may not be in a steady state between 20 and 60 minutes
(
Ramsay et al. 2018
;
Rivera Sandoval et al. 2020
;
Rivera Sandoval et al. 2021
)
, as it is expected to be thermally and viscously unstable if the mass transfer rate,
\dot{M}
, is between the limits
\dot{M}^{+}_{\mathrm{cr}}
and
\dot{M}^{-}_{\mathrm{cr}}

(
Kotko et al. 2012
, their eqn. A2 for
Z=0.02
)
, shown in Figure
6
.
The upper limit,
\dot{M}^{+}_{\mathrm{cr}}
, is evaluated at the tidal radius
R_{\mathrm{t}}=0.6a/(1+q)
, where
a
is the binary separation and
q=M_{\mathrm{He}}/M_{\mathrm{WD}}
is the mass ratio.
The lower limit,
\dot{M}^{-}_{\mathrm{cr}}
, is evaluated at
R_{\mathrm{WD}}
, the accretor radius at the end of the simulation (essentially the radius of a
0.75+0.15=0.9\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
WD).

As noted by
Cannizzo & Nelemans 2015
,
Kotko et al. 2012
did not account for the zero-torque inner boundary condition in converting from
T_{\mathrm{eff}}(R)
to
\dot{M}(R)
locally in the disk. This affects the lower stability condition
\dot{M}^{-}_{\mathrm{cr}}
, since the global condition for the disk to be cold and stable is
T_{\mathrm{eff}}(R)<T^{-}_{\mathrm{eff,cr}}(R)
for every radius
R
within the disc. Because the disk effective temperature peaks at
(49/36)R_{\mathrm{WD}}
, with peak value given by
0.488T_{*}
where
T_{*}=3GM_{\mathrm{WD}}\dot{M}/(8\pi R_{\mathrm{WD}}^{3}\sigma_{\mathrm{sb}})
, the lower stability condition should actually be
0.488T_{*}<T^{-}_{\mathrm{eff,cr}}(R=(49/36)R_{\mathrm{WD}})
3
3

3

The
Kotko et al. 2012
approach essentially takes
T_{*}<T^{-}_{\mathrm{eff,cr}}(R=R_{\mathrm{WD}})
and solves for
\dot{M}
.
. Assuming
T^{-}_{\mathrm{eff,cr}}\propto R^{\beta}
, where
\beta\approx-0.09
, and solving for
\dot{M}
, the actual
\dot{M}^{-}_{\mathrm{cr}}
should be a factor
(49/36)^{4\beta}/0.488^{4}\approx 15.8
times
\dot{M}^{-}_{\mathrm{cr}}(R=R_{\mathrm{WD}})
. Thus, the green dashed line in the top panel of Figure
6
should be 15.8 times higher if we account for the zero-torque inner boundary condition, and we would expect no disk outbursts for
P_{\mathrm{orb}}\gtrsim 50
min. However, this is to be taken with a grain of salt since AM CVn disk outbursts are yet to be fully understood
(
Kotko et al. 2012
;
Rivera Sandoval et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.

Moreover, observations suggest an insignificant disk contribution for long-period AM CVn systems. Eclipse modeling of Gaia14aae suggests that the WD contribution is
\approx 80\%

(
Green et al. 2018
, though a He WD donor is unlikely for this system)
. In addition, optical spectra and photometry of long-period systems can be fitted with a single blackbody of temperature
T_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 10^{4}\,\mathrm{K}

(
Carter et al. 2014
, e.g., SDSS J1137+4054,
P_{\mathrm{orb}}=59.6
min, and SDSS J1505+0659,
P_{\mathrm{orb}}=67.8
min;)
, whereas an accretion disk spectrum is expected to be flatter
(
Nagel et al. 2009
, e.g.,)
.

These observational constraints can be met by an optically thick boundary layer. A boundary layer can be consistent with eclipse modeling by virtue of its compact emitting area. Coincidentally, the “accretion temperature”
T_{\mathrm{acc}}
, defined by
L_{\mathrm{acc}}=4\pi R_{\mathrm{WD}}^{2}T_{\mathrm{acc}}^{4}
, gives the inferred blackbody temperature
10^{4}\,\mathrm{K}
. Note that, however, this is difficult from a theoretical point-of-view, because the boundary layer may be optically thin for
\dot{M}\lesssim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}
, and it may not spread over the entire WD surface which implies an effective temperature much higher than
T_{\mathrm{acc}}

(
Piro & Bildsten 2004
, e.g., )
.
