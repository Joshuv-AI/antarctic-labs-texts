A new behavioral model for portfolio selection using the Half-Full/Half-Empty approach
F Cesarone, M Corradini, L Lampariello, J Riccioni

Abstract
We focus on a behavioral model, that has been recently proposed in the literature, whose rational can be traced back to the Half-Full/Half-Empty glass metaphor. More precisely, we generalize the Half-Full/Half-Empty approach to the context of positive and negative lotteries and give financial and behavioral interpretations of the Half-Full/Half-Empty parameters. We develop a portfolio selection model based on the Half-Full/Half-Empty strategy, resulting in a nonconvex optimization problem, which, nonetheless, is proven to be equivalent to an alternative Mixed-Integer Linear Programming formulation. By means of the ensuing empirical analysis, based on three real-world datasets, the Half-Full/Half-Empty model is shown to be very versatile by appropriately varying its parameters, and to provide portfolios displaying promising performances in terms of risk and profitability, compared with Prospect Theory, risk minimization approaches and Equally-Weighted portfolios. Keywords: Portfolio optimization; Behavioral models; Prospect theory; Performance analysis.

Introduction

Theoretical models of individual behavior in decision-making under risk conditions begun to have a solid foundation in the mid-1900s with the development of the Expected Utility Theory (EUT) by von Neumann
(
von Neumann et al. 1944
)
.
According to EUT, if the preferences of individuals in risky decision making satisfy some fundamental axioms, the Decision Maker (DM) maximizes the expected utility function, and such choice is considered to be rational
(
Keeney et al. 1993
)
: in this framework, individuals are taken to be rational agents.
However, many criticisms have followed over the years due to the discrepancy between the theorized rational behavior and the real one, which can actually be influenced by multiple factors.

The evolution of the literature on decision-making under risk has long been driven by the discovery of paradoxes
(
Fox and Poldrack 2014
;
Luce et al. 2008
;
Wu et al. 2004
)
. Old paradoxes such as the Allais one
(
Allais 1953
)
and its variations
(
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
)
challenged the descriptive power of EUT.
For this reason, over the last 60 years, decision theorists have been trying to develop a theory of choice that can give a satisfactory description of decision-makers’ behavior in risky situations.

Although several theories have been proposed over time
(
Fox and Poldrack 2014
;
Starmer 2000
;
Wu et al. 2004
, see, e.g.,)
, Prospect Theory (PT)
(
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
)
and Cumulative Prospect Theory (CPT)
(
Tversky and Kahneman 1992
)
have emerged as the leading descriptive paradigm for modeling decision-making under risk
(
Camerer 1998
;
Fox and Poldrack 2014
;
Starmer 2000
;
Wu et al. 2004
)
and have inspired a large body of theoretical and empirical work
(
Abdellaoui 2000
;
Abdellaoui 2002
;
Camerer 1992
;
Diecidue and Wakker 2001
;
Gonzalez and Wu 1999
;
Karni and Safra 1987
;
Luce 2014
;
Luce 2001
;
Machina 1982
;
Prelec 1998
;
Quiggin 2012
;
Schmeidler 1989
;
Starmer and Sugden 1989
;
Tversky and Wakker 1995
;
Viscusi 1989
;
Wakker 1994
;
Wakker 1996
;
Wakker 2001
;
Wakker et al. 1994
;
Wu and Gonzalez 1996
;
Wu and Gonzalez 1998
;
Wu and Gonzalez 1999
;
Yaari 1987
)
.
Despite this apparent success, several scholars have questioned the underlying assumptions and applicability of PT and CPT, which seem not to describe some empirical evidence
(
Baltussen et al. 2006
;
Blavatskyy 2005
;
Brandstätter et al. 2006
;
Hertwig et al. 2004
;
Humphrey 1995
;
Levy and Levy 2002
;
Lopes and Oden 1999
;
Marley and Luce 2005
;
Neilson and Stowe 2002
;
Payne 2005
;
Starmer 2000
;
Starmer and Sugden 1993
;
Weber and Kirsner 1997
;
Wu and Gonzalez 1999
;
Wu and Markle 2008
;
Wu et al. 2005
)
.

PT and CPT are, thus, at the same time, “one of the most confirmed and falsified decision-making models currently in circulation”
(
Wakker 2010
)
.
However,
Birnbaum 2008
has recently produced systematic empirical cases that confute both PT and CPT approaches and have received considerable attention in the relevant literature
(
Fox and Poldrack 2014
;
Luce et al. 2008
;
Wakker 2010
)
.
In fact, the author has presented 11 new paradoxes showing “self-contradiction or systematic false predictions”
(
Birnbaum 2008
, p. 463)
, leading to the conclusion that PT and CPT cannot “be retained as a descriptive model of decision making”
(
Birnbaum 2008
, p. 464)
.

The transfer of attention exchange (TAX) model “correctly predicts results with all 11 new paradoxes”
(
Birnbaum and Stegner 1979
;
Birnbaum and Chavez 1997
;
Birnbaum 2008
, p. 463)
.
But, while this model fits the data better than PT and CPT
(
Birnbaum 2008
;
Fox and Poldrack 2014
;
Wakker 2010
)
, it is less parsimonious
(
Wu et al. 2004
, p. 408)
, involving the presence of a large number of free parameters, and does not have “the particularly tractable form of prospect theory, with psychological interpretations for its parameters”
(
Wakker 2010
, p. 351)
.

In
Cenci et al. 2015
, a simple decision-making under risk approach for positive lotteries has been proposed to explain the well-known “old and new” paradoxes at a stroke.
The rationale behind the resulting model can be easily understood through the metaphor of the “glass half-full, glass half-empty”: different individuals may evaluate the same situation differently and are typically divided between those who focus on positive aspects (the optimists/glass half-full) and those who focus on negative ones (the pessimists/glass half-empty).
The Half-Full/Half-Empty (HF/HE) model suggests that a DM’s evaluation of a choice situation may depend on how the DM assesses the difference between outcomes above and below the mean value of the lottery considered.
The DM’s evaluation of the lottery (and the consequent attitude towards risk) depends on two parameters only: the DM’s degree of optimism/pessimism, and a probability distortion parameter that determines the DM’s decision weights.
In
Corradini 2022
, the author investigates how to suitably calibrate this model to explain “old and new” paradoxes, even in the case of negative lotteries (Note that, in
Corradini 2022
, only calibration of negative lotteries in
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
is considered since negative lotteries in
Birnbaum 2008
are trivially explained).

Compared to other decision-making models that have been recently proposed to address the paradoxes in
Birnbaum 2008
, the advantages of the HF/HE approach are twofold.
On the one hand, it is very intuitive, as it is closely related to the Expected Value (EV) criterion, and the related parameters have a clear and well-established behavioral interpretation
(
Hurwicz 1951
;
Arrow and Hurwicz 1972
;
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
;
Tversky and Wakker 1995
, see, e.g.,)
.
On the other hand, the HF/HE approach is highly parsimonious and flexible, providing an extremely simple way of modeling behavior, based on just three parameters.

Behavioral models for portfolio selection incorporate investor’s perception of risk into the decision-making process, avoiding the pitfalls of suboptimal investment choices.
Theoretical evidence exists in the literature on risk reduction in portfolio selection models based on the behavior of DMs who are naturally loss averse
(
Hens and Bachmann 2008
;
Grishina et al. 2017
)
.

Benartzi and Thaler 1995
focus their study on a particular loss aversion concept, i.e. the so-called myopic loss aversion, which is key in PT, to explain the equity premium puzzle
(
Mehra and Prescott 1985
, see also)
for DMs who evaluate their portfolios annually.

Levy and Levy 2003
, among the Pareto-optimal mean-variance portfolios, propose to select the ones with the highest prospect utility, in case of normally distributed asset returns.

Pirvu and Schulze 2012
generalize the results of
Levy and Levy 2003
for elliptic symmetric distributions of risky assets.

De Giorgi et al. 2007
apply the PT approach to portfolio selection, assuming equally likely historical scenarios for the asset returns.
The PT portfolios are obtained by solving a nonconvex optimization problem through a heuristic.
Other scholars investigate portfolio selection models based on CPT

(
Bernard and Ghossoub 2010
;
He and Zhou 2011
;
Consigli et al. 2019
, see)
.

Barro et al. 2020
compare different portfolio selection models, including models based on PT and CPT, and approaches based on risk minimization, where risk is measured by variance and mean absolute deviation.

In this paper, we refine the approach in
Cenci et al. 2015
and
Corradini 2022
by tailoring it to portfolio selection problems.
More precisely, we generalize the HF/HE approach to the context of mixed (i.e. positive and negative) lotteries and discuss financial and behavioral interpretation of the HF/HE-related parameters.
We develop a portfolio selection model based on the HF/HE approach that results in a nonconvex optimization problem, for which we suggest an alternative, equivalent Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) formulation.
By means of the empirical analysis we give, based on three real-world data sets, the HF/HE model is shown to be very versatile by appropriately setting its parameters, providing portfolios that display promising performances in terms of risk and profitability, compared with Prospect Theory and risk minimization approaches and the Equally-Weighted portfolio.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we describe how to apply the HF/HE approach to portfolio selection.
More in detail, in Section
2.1
, we introduce some preliminary concepts related to the decision-making model under risk in
Cenci et al. 2015
and
Corradini 2022
.
In Section
2.2
, we extend the HF/HE approach to the context of positive and negative lotteries and present a detailed discussion about the financial interpretation of HF/HE parameters.
In Section
2.3
, we adapt the HF/HE approach for portfolio selection purposes, obtaining a nonconvex optimization problem, for which, in Section
2.4
, we propose a (MILP) equivalent formulation.
In Section
3
, we present the PT-based portfolio selection model and two minimum-risk portfolio strategies that referred to for comparative purposes.
In Section
4
, we provide an extensive empirical analysis based on three real-world datasets, where the performance of the HF/HE portfolio is compared with that of Prospect Theory, Equally-Weighted, minimum variance and Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) portfolios.
Conclusions are drawn in Section
5
.

2
Half-Full/Half-Empty approach

After gaining insight, in Section
2.1
, about the decision-making model under risk that has been developed in
Cenci et al. 2015
and
Corradini 2022
, we show in Section
2.3
how to tailor it to portfolio selection purposes: as for the resulting nonconvex optimization problem, we propose, in Section
2.4
, a MILP equivalent formulation.

2.1
Preliminary concepts

We recall here some preliminary notions of the HF/HE approach in
Cenci et al. 2015
, where the authors consider only nonnegative random variables.

Denoting by
Y
a generic nonnegative lottery,
we can express its EV as follows:

H_{EV}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mu+\mathbb{E}[Y-\mu]\,,

(1)

where obviously
\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y]
.
Distinguishing the part of
Y
below the mean from the part above the mean, we can write
(Y-\mu)=(Y-\mu)_{+}+(Y-\mu)_{-}
, where
(Y-\mu)_{+}=\max\{Y-\mu,0\}
and
(Y-\mu)_{-}=\min\{Y-\mu,0\}
. Expression (
1
) becomes

H_{EV}[Y]=\mu+2\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)_{+}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)_{-}]\Bigr\}.

(2)

The expected value of
Y
can be generalized by assuming that the DM assigns a weight
\lambda
different from
\frac{1}{2}
for realizations of
Y
above and those below the mean, according to the DM’s degree of optimism/pessimism.
Therefore, expression (
2
) becomes

H_{\lambda}[Y]=\mu+2\bigl\{\lambda\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)_{+}]+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)_{-}]\bigr\},

(3)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
and represents the DM’s degree of optimism/pessimism.
More precisely, an
optimistic
DM sets
\lambda\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
, thus assigning a weight, that is larger than
\frac{1}{2}
, to the average of the values of
Y
above
\mu
.
If a DM is
pessimistic
, then
\lambda\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, i.e. the DM attributes a weight, that is larger than
\frac{1}{2}
, to the average of the values of
Y
below
\mu
.
If
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
, (
3
) coincides with (
2
).

2.2
The HF/HE functional with positive and negative lotteries

To rely on the HF/HE functional
H_{\lambda}
in portfolio selection, we need to reformulate it in the context of mixed lotteries, namely when
Y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Suffice it to repeat the rationale behind (
1
) and (
2
) for a random variable
Y
, which admits both positive and negative values.

Let
Y_{+}=\max\{Y,0\}
and
Y_{-}=\min\{Y,0\}
, then
Y=Y_{+}+Y_{-}
,

|Y|=Y_{+}-Y_{-}
, and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mu_{+}+\mu_{-}
, where
\mu_{+}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{+}]
and
\mu_{-}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{-}]
.
Starting from (
1
), we can write

\begin{split}H_{1}[Y]&=\mathbb{E}[Y-\mu]+\mu=\mathbb{E}[Y_{+}+Y_{-}-\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}]+\mu=\\
&=\mu+\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})]+\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})].\end{split}

(4)

Considering the relations

\begin{split}Y_{+}-\mu_{+}&=(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}+(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{-}\\
Y_{-}-\mu_{-}&=(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}+(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{-}\,,\\
\end{split}

(5)

and substituting them in (
4
), we have

\begin{split}H_{1}[Y]&=\mu+\bigl\{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}+(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{-}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}+(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{-}\bigr]\bigr\}=\\
&=\mu+\bigl\{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{-}\bigr]\bigr\}+\bigl\{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{-}\bigr]\bigr\}=\\
&=\mu+2\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{-}]\Bigr\}+2\Bigl\{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{-}]\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(6)

Similar to (
3
), the functional
H_{1}[Y]
can be generalized as follows

H_{2}[Y]=\mu+2\Bigl\{\lambda_{+}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}]+(1-\lambda_{+})\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{-}]\Bigr\}+2\Bigl\{\lambda_{-}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}]+(1-\lambda_{-})\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{-}]\Bigr\},

(7)

where
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
are the weights that the DM assigns to the outcomes above the mean in the case of gains and losses, respectively;
1-\lambda_{+}
and
1-\lambda_{-}
are the weights when the lotteries are below the mean in the case of gains and losses, respectively.

Using relations (
5
),
H_{2}[Y]
can be expressed as

\begin{split}H_{2}[Y]&=\mu+2\Bigl\{\lambda_{+}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}]-(1-\lambda_{+})\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}]\Bigr\}+\\
&+2\Bigl\{\lambda_{-}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}]-(1-\lambda_{-})\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}]\Bigr\}=\\
&=\mu+2\Bigl\{(2\lambda_{+}-1)\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}]\Bigr\}+2\Bigl\{(2\lambda_{-}-1)\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}]\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(8)

Again from (
5
), we have
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]=2\mathbb{E}[(Y_{+}-\mu_{+})_{+}]
and
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr]=2\mathbb{E}[(Y_{-}-\mu_{-})_{+}]
.
Thus,

\begin{split}H_{2}(Y)=\mu+(2\lambda_{+}-1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr].\end{split}

(9)

We point out that the HF/HE functional (
9
) depends on two parameters only, which, as shown in the next section, have a clear and well-established financial interpretation.

2.2.1
Financial interpretation of the HF/HE parameters

If a DM adopts the value function
h(Y)
to evaluate the lottery
Y
, then the certainty equivalent of
Y
,
CE_{Y}
, is defined as
h(CE_{Y})=h(Y)
: the DM is risk-adverse (risk-seeking) when
CE_{Y}<\mu
(
CE_{Y}>\mu
), while for
CE_{Y}=\mu
, the DM is risk-neutral.

In case
h(Y)=H_{2}(Y)
, we have
H_{2}(CE_{Y})=CE_{Y}
, hence
CE_{Y}=H_{2}(Y)
. Thus, accordingly, a DM is risk-adverse (risk-seeking) when
H_{2}(Y)<\mu
(
H_{2}(Y)>\mu
), while if
H_{2}(Y)=\mu
, the DM is risk-neutral.

For positive lotteries (
Y>0
), (
9
) becomes
H_{2}(Y)=\mu+(2\lambda_{+}-1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]
.
Therefore, a DM is

•

risk-adverse, when
2\lambda_{+}-1<0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{+}\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
;

•

risk-seeking, when
2\lambda_{+}-1>0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{+}\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
;

•

risk-neutral, when
2\lambda_{+}-1=0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{+}=\frac{1}{2}
.

For negative lotteries (
Y<0
), we have
H_{2}(Y)=\mu+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr]
.
Hence, a DM is

•

risk-adverse, when
2\lambda_{-}-1<0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{-}\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
;

•

risk-seeking, when
2\lambda_{-}-1>0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{-}\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
;

•

risk-neutral, when
2\lambda_{-}-1=0\Leftrightarrow\lambda_{-}=\frac{1}{2}
.

In the general case of mixed lotteries (i.e.
Y\in\mathbb{R}
), the DM is risk-averse when
H_{2}(Y)<\mu
, thus, if

(2\lambda_{+}-1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr]<0.

Hence,
(2\lambda_{+}-1)<(1-2\lambda_{-})\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]}
yields

\lambda_{+}<\frac{1}{2}+(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda_{-})\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]}.

(10)

Inequality (
10
) can be rewritten as

\lambda_{+}<-m\lambda_{-}+k\,,

(11)

where
m=\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]}>0
and
k=\frac{1}{2}(1+m)>\frac{1}{2}
.

2.2.2
About the distortion of probabilities

A refinement of the functional defined in (
9
) can be obtained by including the distortion of the lottery’s probabilities
(
Fehr-Duda and Epper 2012
;
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
;
Tversky and Wakker 1995
, see, e.g.,)
.
In the case of a discrete random variable
Y=\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{T}\}
with probabilities
p=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{T}\}
, it consists of transforming the probabilities
p_{i}
by an appropriate weighting function. An example of weighting function is given by

w(p_{i},p,q)=\frac{p_{i}^{q}}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}p_{j}^{q}}\,,

(12)

where
q
is a suitable positive parameter that needs to be calibrated.

In Fig.
3
, we exhibit the behavior of the weighting function (
12
) for a binary lottery
Y=\{(y_{1},p);(y_{2},1-p)\}
in the case when
0<q<1
e
q>1
.

(a)
q=0.7

(b)
q=1.4

Figure 3:
Graph of the weighting function (
12
).

If
0<q<1
(left side), the probability of the most likely events is underestimated, and that of the least likely events is overestimated;
vice versa
, in case
q>1
(right side).
When
q=1
, there is no probability distortion (see the red lines in Fig.
3
).
Then, expression (
9
) can be generalized as follows:

H[Y]=\mu_{q}+(2\lambda_{+}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\lvert Y_{+}-\mu_{+}\rvert\bigr]+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\lvert Y_{-}-\mu_{-}\rvert\bigr],

(13)

where
\mu_{q}=\mathbb{E}_{q}[Y]=\sum_{j=1}^{T}w_{q}(p_{j})y_{j}
,
\mathbb{E}_{q}[g(Y)]=\sum_{j=1}^{T}w_{q}(p_{j})g(y_{j})
, and
w_{q}(p_{j})=\frac{p_{j}^{q}}{\sum_{i=1}^{T}p_{i}^{q}}
.

2.3
The HF/HE portfolio selection model

In this section, we adapt the HF/HE approach to portfolio selection problems.
We consider asset returns defined on a discrete probability space
with
T
states of nature, where each state has a probability of occurrence equal to
\pi_{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,T
.
Furthermore, we use a look-back approach where the realizations of the discrete random returns correspond to the historical scenarios, which are equally likely, i.e.,
\pi_{t}=\frac{1}{T}

\forall t
, as typically assumed in portfolio optimization
(see, e.g.,
Mansini et al. 2007
;
Carleo et al. 2017
;
Bellini et al. 2021
, and references therein)
.
If
p_{kt}
is the price of asset
k
at time
t
, the return of asset
k
at time
t
is denoted by
r_{kt}=\frac{p_{kt}-p_{k(t-1)}}{p_{k(t-1)}}
.
Assuming an investment universe composed of
n
assets and denoting by
x
the vector of portfolio weights, the portfolio return at time
t
is given by
R_{t}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}
.
For all models we consider, feasible portfolios are those identified by the budget constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1
and the no-short-selling requirements
x_{k}\geq 0\quad k=1,\dots,n
.

Note that in case of equally likely scenarios, the weighting function (
12
) is
w(\pi_{t},\pi,q)=\pi_{t}\equiv\frac{1}{T}
,
t=1,\ldots,T
, for every
q
.

Thus, the HF/HE functional (
9
) applied to the portfolio return becomes

H[R(x)]=\mu(x)+(2\lambda_{+}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert R_{t}^{+}(x)-\mu^{+}(x)\rvert+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert R_{t}^{-}(x)-\mu^{-}(x)\rvert,

(14)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}R(x)&=R^{+}(x)+R^{-}(x)\\
\mu(x)&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mu_{k}\,,\qquad\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{kt}\\
R_{t}^{+}(x)&=\max\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\Bigr)\\
\mu^{+}(x)&=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}^{+}(x)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\Bigr)\\
R_{t}^{-}(x)&=\min\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\Bigr)\\
\mu^{-}(x)&=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}^{-}(x)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\Bigr).\end{split}

Therefore, we obtain the following portfolio optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{x}{\mbox{maximize}}&\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mu_{k}+(2\lambda_{+}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\max\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\bigr)-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\bigr)\rvert+\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert\min\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\bigr)-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\bigr)\rvert\\[8.0pt]
\mbox{s.t.}&\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1\\[5.0pt]
&x_{k}\geq 0\quad\qquad k=1,\dots,n,\end{array}

(15)

which is a nonconvex nonsmooth optimization problem.
However, as shown in the next section, it can be equivalently formulated as a MILP problem.

Furthermore, under specific (but unrealistic) assumptions, optimal solutions of (
15
) can coincide with efficient portfolios obtained by relying on the Mean-MAD model developed by
Konno and Yamazaki 1991
(see Remark
1
).

2.4
A MILP reformulation of the HF/HE model

The nonconvex nonsmooth optimization problem (
15
) can be equivalently recast according to a suitable MILP formulation, as shown below. Relying on such an alternative version of the model opens the door to different possible numerical treatments in order to address the problem in practice.

Preliminarily, one can introduce:
(
i
)
T
auxiliary variables
d_{t}^{+}
defined as the deviation of the portfolio return
\sum_{i=k}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}
from
0
when
\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}>0
, and
0
otherwise;
(
ii
)
T
auxiliary variables
d_{t}^{-}
defined as the deviation of the portfolio loss
-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}
from
0
when
-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}>0
, and
0
otherwise.

We remark that the sets

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathcal{F}}\triangleq\Bigg\{(x,d^{+},d^{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{T}&:&\displaystyle d_{t}^{+}=\max\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\right),\,d_{t}^{-}=\max\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},0\right),\\[5.0pt]
&&\,t=1,\ldots,T,\,x\geq 0,\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1\Bigg\},\\[10.0pt]
{\mathcal{S}}\triangleq\Bigg\{(x,d^{+},d^{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{T}&:&\displaystyle d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt},\,d_{t}^{+}d_{t}^{-}=0,\,d_{t}^{+},d_{t}^{-}\geq 0,\,t=1,\ldots,T,\\[5.0pt]
&&x\geq 0,\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1\Bigg\}\end{array}

are equal. The inclusion
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
follows easily from the definitions. As for
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, suffice it to observe that, if
(y,b^{+},b^{-})\in{\mathcal{S}}
,
b_{t}^{+}>0
yields
b_{t}^{-}=0
and, in turn,
b_{t}^{+}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}r_{kt}
, while
b_{t}^{+}=0
entails
b_{t}^{-}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}r_{kt}
. Hence, the HF/HE model (
15
) can be equivalently recast as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cll}\displaystyle\underset{(x,d^{+},d^{-})}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mu_{k}-(2\lambda_{+}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert d_{t}^{+}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+}\rvert-(2\lambda_{-}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert d_{t}^{-}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{-}\rvert&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}&\hskip-60.0ptt=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}^{+}d_{t}^{-}=0&\hskip-60.0ptt=1,\ldots,T\\
&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1&\\
&x_{k}\geq 0&\hskip-60.0ptk=1,\ldots,n\\
&d_{t}^{+},d_{t}^{-}\geq 0&\hskip-60.0ptt=1,\ldots,T.\end{array}

(16)

As shown in Section
2.2.1
, considering a DM that is risk-adverse for gains and risk-seeking for losses, we have
(2\lambda_{+}-1)<0
and
(2\lambda_{-}-1)>0
.
In this case, we notice that
-(2\lambda_{+}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert d_{t}^{+}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+}\rvert
is a convex function, while
-(2\lambda_{-}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert d_{t}^{-}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{-}\rvert
is concave. Via the epigraph form of Problem (
16
)
(see, e.g.,
Grippo and Sciandrone 2023
, Section 2.5)
, we get the following equivalent version of it:

\begin{array}[]{cll}\displaystyle\underset{(x,d^{+},d^{-},z^{+},z^{-})}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mu_{k}+(1-2\lambda_{+})\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z_{t}^{+}+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z_{t}^{-}&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&-z_{t}^{+}-(d_{t}^{+}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+})\leq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&-z_{t}^{+}+(d_{t}^{+}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+})\leq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&-z_{t}^{-}-|d_{t}^{-}-\frac{1}{T}d_{t}^{-}|\leq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}^{+}d_{t}^{-}=0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1&\\
&x_{k}\geq 0&k=1,\ldots,n\\
&d_{t}^{+},d_{t}^{-}\geq 0&t=1,\ldots,T.\end{array}

(17)

We finally deal with the difficult-to-handle disjunctive-type constraints
-z_{t}^{-}-|d_{t}^{-}-\frac{1}{T}d_{t}^{-}|\leq 0
and complementarity conditions
d_{t}^{+}d_{t}^{-}=0
,
t=1,\ldots,T
, in (
17
) through the big-M reformulation, thus obtaining the mixed-integer linear program

\begin{array}[]{cll}\displaystyle\underset{(x,d^{+},d^{-},z^{+},z^{-},\bar{y},\tilde{y},w)}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mu_{k}+(1-2\lambda_{+})\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z_{t}^{+}+(2\lambda_{-}-1)\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z_{t}^{-}&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&-z_{t}^{+}-(d_{t}^{+}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+})\leq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&-z_{t}^{+}+(d_{t}^{+}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+})\leq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&-z_{t}^{-}\leq M(1-\bar{y}_{t})&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&-z_{t}^{-}-(d_{t}^{-}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{-})\leq M(1-\bar{y}_{t})&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&z_{t}^{-}\leq M(1-\tilde{y}_{t})&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&-z_{t}^{-}+(d_{t}^{-}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{-})\leq M(1-\tilde{y}_{t})&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\bar{y}_{t},\tilde{y}_{t}\in\{0,1\}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\bar{y}_{t}+\tilde{y}_{t}=1&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}^{+}\leq Mw_{t}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}^{-}\leq M(1-w_{t})&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&w_{t}\in\{0,1\}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1&\\
&x_{k}\geq 0&k=1,\ldots,n\\
&d_{t}^{+},d_{t}^{-}\geq 0&t=1,\ldots,T,\end{array}

(18)

where
M
is a suitably large positive scalar.

3
Other portfolio selection models to be compared with

We introduce several portfolio selection models to be compared with the HF/HE approach.
More precisely, in Section
3.1
, we briefly describe the behavioral portfolio selection model based on PT.
In Section
3.2
, we present two minimum risk approaches, using variance and MAD, as a risk measures, respectively.
Furthermore, we also test the so-called Equally Weighted (EW) portfolio, defined by
x_{k}=\frac{1}{n}
,
k=1,\cdots,n
, whose performances would seem to be barely improved by optimized portfolios in practice
(
DeMiguel et al. 2009
)
.

3.1
Prospect Theory portfolio

Kahneman and Tversky 1979
suggest to interpret individuals’ experimentally observed economic choices by modeling their satisfaction level through a nonconcave function.
A DM exhibits a risk-averse attitude toward gains and a risk-seeking attitude toward losses. A crucial property of PT is loss aversion: individuals are more sensitive to losses than gains
(
Sun 2009
, see)
.
Furthermore, among different alternatives, investors evaluate potential gains and losses with respect to a reference point. They overestimate the probabilities of significant gains and losses.
Accordingly, on the one hand, the satisfaction levels of a DM are modeled by a function that is concave for gains and convex for losses, thus incorporating a risk-averse attitude for gains and a risk-seeking attitude for losses.
The value function is steeper below the reference since people tend to focus more on losses than gains.
On the other hand, a DM transforms objective probabilities through a weighting function, by underestimating medium-high probabilities and overestimating low probabilities associated with extreme events (see Section
2.2.2
).

To make comparisons, we refer to the PT-based behavioral portfolio selection model proposed by
De Giorgi et al. 2007
:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{x}{\mbox{maximize}}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl[\Bigl(\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}\Bigr)_{+}\Bigr)^{\alpha}-\beta\Bigl(-\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}r_{kt}\Bigr)_{-}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\Bigr]\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1\\
&x_{k}\geq 0\quad k=1,\dots,n,\end{array}

(19)

where
\alpha\leq 1
leads to a concave objective for gains and convex for losses, and controls the DM’s risk attitude. Furthermore,
\beta
represents loss aversion. Also, if
\beta>1
, the function is asymmetric, so that DM is more loss-averse than gain-averse.
Model (
19
), thus, is a nonconvex nonsmooth optimization problem. As in
De Giorgi et al. 2007
, we fix
\alpha=0.88
and
\beta=2.25
.

3.2
Minimum variance and minimum MAD portfolios

The long-only minimum variance portfolio
(
Markowitz 1952
;
Markowitz 1959
)
can be obtained by solving the following convex quadratic programming problem:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{x}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{k,j}x_{k}x_{j}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1\\
&x_{k}\geq 0\quad k=1,\ldots,n,\end{array}

(20)

where
\sigma_{k,j}
is the covariance of returns of asset
k
and asset
j
,
k,j=1,\dots n
.

An alternative deviation risk measure in the sense of
Rockafellar et al. 2006
, which has been often used in the literature
(see, e.g.,
Ararat et al. 2021
;
Carleo et al. 2017
;
Cesarone et al. 2015
;
Cesarone et al. 2016
;
Cesarone 2020
, and references therein)
, is the MAD: it is defined as the expected value of the absolute deviation of the portfolio return from its mean.
Thus, the long-only minimum MAD portfolio
(
Konno and Yamazaki 1991
)
can be obtained by solving the following optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{x}{\mbox{minimize}}&\mbox{MAD}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k,t}x_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}x_{k}\right\rvert\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1\\
&x_{k}\geq 0,\quad k=1,\ldots,n,\end{array}

(21)

where
\mu_{k}
represents the expected return of asset
k
.
As in
Konno and Yamazaki 1991
, one can linearize problem (
21
) by introducing
T
auxiliary variables

d_{t}
defined as the absolute deviation of the portfolio return from its mean, thus obtaining, following a similar logic as in Section
2.4
, the Linear Programming (LP) problem

\begin{array}[]{cll}\underset{(x,d)}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\displaystyle d_{t}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}(r_{k,t}-\mu_{k})x_{k}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\displaystyle d_{t}\geq-\sum_{k=1}^{n}(r_{k,t}-\mu_{k})x_{k}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}=1&\\
&x_{k}\geq 0&k=1,\ldots,n.\end{array}

(22)

Remark 1
(HF/HE vs. Mean-MAD)

Assuming
R(x)>0
, if
\lambda_{+}\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, the HF/HE portfolio coincides with the Mean-MAD portfolio

x^{*}=\argmin\{-\mu(x)+(1-2\lambda_{+})MAD(x):\,x\in\Delta\}.

(23)

Assuming
R(x)<0
, if
\lambda_{-}\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, the HF/HE portfolio coincides with the Mean-MAD portfolio

x^{*}=\argmin\{-\mu(x)+(1-2\lambda_{-})MAD(x):\,x\in\Delta\}.

(24)

Whenever
R(x)\in\mathbb{R}
, Mean-MAD and HF/HE portfolios are different.
Indeed, the HF/HE model weighs positive and negative outcomes differently, while
MAD is a symmetric risk measure and equally penalizes outcomes above and below the portfolio expected return.

4
Empirical analysis

By means of a thorough empirical analysis, we test the performances of HF/HE portfolios in comparison with EW, minimum variance and MAD, and PT ones.

Summing up, in Section
4.1
, we describe datasets and performance measures. We, then, discuss the computational results: more precisely, in Section
4.2
, we test and validate HF/HE portfolios, choosing parameters
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
values that satisfy old and new paradoxes
(
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
;
Birnbaum 2008
)
; in Section
4.3
, we explore the flexibility of the HF/HE approach in terms of the out-of-sample performances by varying
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
outside the paradoxes’ constraints.

For the out-of-sample performance analysis, we adopt a rolling time window mechanism: using a time window of 500 (daily) observations (in-sample period), we solve the problem for overlapping windows constructed by moving forward in time in steps of 20 days. The optimal portfolio that is computed in an in-sample period is then maintained for the next 20 days (out-of-sample period), so that we can evaluate its performances by considering the aforementioned measures.

All experiments have been implemented on a laptop with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-7500U CPU @ 2.70 GHz processor and 8 GB of RAM, using MATLAB R2022b. We address problems (
15
) and (
19
) using a multi-start heuristic, while the remaining models are coped with by means of standard built-in solvers.

4.1
Description of datasets and performance measures

We consider the following datasets that are publicly available on
https://www.francescocesarone.com/data-sets
and consist of daily prices, adjusted for dividends and stock splits and retrieved from Refinitiv
(
Cesarone et al. 2022
, see, for more details,)
:

•

DIJA
(Dow Jones Industrial Average, USA): composed of 28 assets and 3715 observations (October 2006 - December 2020);

•

NASDAQ 100
(National Association of Securities Dealers Automated Quotation, USA): composed of 54 assets and 3715 observations (October 2006 - December 2020);

•

FTSE 100
(Financial Times Stock Exchange, UK): composed of 80 assets and 3715 observations (October 2006 - December 2020).

To evaluate the out-of-sample performances of the portfolio strategies we analyze, we use the following measures that are typically resorted to in the literature
(see, e.g.,
Cesarone et al. 2015
;
Cesarone et al. 2016
;
Cesarone et al. 2020
;
Bruni et al. 2017
;
Cesarone and Colucci 2017
, and references therein)
:

•

out-of-sample daily expected return
\hat{\mu}^{out}
(
ExpRet
) and out-of-sample daily volatility
\hat{\sigma}^{out}
(
Vol
).

•

Sharpe
ratio
(
Sharpe 1966
;
Sharpe 1998
)
, i.e. gain per unit of risk:

\mbox{\bf{Sharpe}}=\frac{\hat{\mu}^{out}-r_{f}}{\hat{\sigma}^{out}}\,,

where we fix
r_{f}=0
;

•

Maximum Drawdown
(
Chekhlov et al. 2005
,
MaxDD
, see, e.g.,)
, i.e.

\mbox{\bf{MaxDD}}=\min_{T^{in}+1\leq t\leq T}DD_{t},\quad DD_{t}=\frac{W_{t}-\max\limits_{T^{in}+1\leq\tau\leq t}W_{\tau}}{\max\limits_{T^{in}+1\leq\tau\leq t}W_{\tau}}\qquad{t\in\{T^{in}+1,\dots,T}\},

where
T^{in}
is the length of the in-sample window,
W_{\tau}=W_{\tau-1}(1+R_{\tau}^{out})
is the value of wealth after
\tau

periods,
W_{T^{in}}=1
is the initial wealth, and
R_{\tau}^{out}
denotes the out-of-sample portfolio returns for each portfolio strategy;

•

Sortino
ratio
(
Sortino and Satchell 2001
)
, which, again, is a gain-risk ratio performance measure defined as follows:

\mbox{\bf{Sortino}}=\frac{\hat{\mu}^{out}-r_{f}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E},[(r^{out}-r_{f})_{-}^{2}]}}

where
{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(r^{out}-r_{f})_{-}^{2}]}}
represents the Target Downside Deviation and
r_{f}=0
;

•

Rachev
ratio
(
Biglova et al. 2004
)
, which measures the upside potential and is defined as the ratio between expected return in the best
\alpha
% values of
R^{out}-r_{f}
and expected losses in the worst
\beta
% values of
R^{out}-r_{f}
:

\mbox{\bf{Rachev}}=\frac{\mbox{CVaR}_{\alpha}(r_{f}-{R}^{out})}{\mbox{CVaR}_{\beta}(R^{out}-r_{f})},

where
\alpha=\beta=5
and
r_{f}=0
;

•

average Return On Investment
(
Phillips and Phillips 2005
,
aveROI
, see, e.g.,)
, which indicates the return generated by each portfolio strategy for a given time horizon
\Delta\tau
:

ROI_{\tau}=\frac{W_{\tau}-W_{\tau-\Delta\tau}}{W_{\tau-\Delta\tau}}\qquad\tau=\Delta\tau+1,\dots,T,

where
W_{\tau-\Delta\tau}
represents the capital invested at the beginning of the time horizon,
W_{\tau}=W_{\tau-\Delta\tau}\prod_{t=\tau-\Delta\tau+1}^{\tau}(1+R_{t}^{out})
is the portfolio wealth, and
T
is the entire number of the available realizations;

•

Herfindahl index
(
Rhoades 1993
)
, which is a concentration measure, defined as
\mbox{HI}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}^{2}
.
The normalized version of the index (
NHI
) is

\mbox{\bf{NHI}}=\displaystyle\frac{1-HI}{1-\frac{1}{n}}.

Hence,
\mbox{\bf{NHI}}=0
represents the maximum concentration, while
\mbox{\bf{NHI}}=1
indicates the maximum diversification;

•

average number of assets (
ave #
) selected by each portfolio strategy.

5
Conclusions

Taking a cue from
Cenci et al. 2015
and
Corradini 2022
, we have extended the so-called HF/HE behavioral approach to encompass the general case of mixed positive and negative lotteries. Also, we have been able to present a novel and detailed discussion about the financial meaning of the model’s relevant parameters.
Such analysis, in turn, prompted us to tailor this strategy to portfolio selection purposes: as a result, we devised a nonconvex optimization model together with its MILP reformulation.
Concerning testing and validation, we have presented an extensive empirical analysis based on several real-world datasets, which confirms, by means of numerical evidences, the out-of-sample competitiveness of the method.

Future research will be directed toward (a) a better understanding of the role played by the probabilistic bias parameter
q
in portfolio selection, (b) a thorough investigation of different algorithmic strategies to address the problem in practice, and (c) a “behavioral evaluation” of financial derivative contracts.
