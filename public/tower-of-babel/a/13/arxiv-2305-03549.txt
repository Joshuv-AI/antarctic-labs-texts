Around the Gauss circle problem: Hardy’s conjecture and the distribution of lattice points near circles
Stephen Lester, Igor Wigman

Abstract
. Hardy conjectured that the error term arising from approximating the number of lattice points lying in a radius- R R disc by its area is O ⁡ ( R 1 / 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) O(R^{1/2+o(1)}) . One source of support for this conjecture is a folklore heuristic that uses i.i.d. random variables to model the lattice points lying near the boundary and square-root cancellation of sums of these random variables. We examine this heuristic by studying how these lattice points interact with one another and prove that their autocorrelation is determined in terms of a random model. Additionally, it is shown that lattice points near the boundary which are “well separated” behave independently. We also formulate a conjecture concerning the distribution of pairs of these lattice points.



1.
Introduction

Gauss initiated the study of the number of lattice points inside a radius-
R
disc,
B(R)
, and showed that the number of such lattice points equals its area up to an error term of size
O(R)
. Numerous researchers have worked on improving the bound for the error term, including more recent works of Huxley
[
7
]
and Bourgain-Watt
[
3
]
, the latter of which shows that the error term is
O\left(R^{\frac{1034}{1648}+o(1)}\right)
(note that
\tfrac{1034}{1648}=0.6274\ldots
). Hardy
[
6
]
conjectured that

(1.1)

N(R):=\#\{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\lambda\in B(R)\}=\operatorname{Area}(B(R))+O(R^{1/2+o(1)}).

If true, this estimate would be nearly optimal, since Hardy proved the error term above is

\neq O\left(R^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log R)^{\frac{1}{4}}\right),

which has been refined by Hafner
[
5
]
and Soundararajan
[
9
]
.

To see why one might expect (
1.1
) should hold, recall Gauss’ argument and consider squares centered at
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-lattice points with unit area and sides parallel to the coordinate axes.
The area of the intersection of all the squares with
B(R)
accounts for the main term
\operatorname{Area}(B(R))
and the error term arises
from the squares that are centered at lattice points lying in the annulus

(1.2)

\mathscr{D}_{R;1/\sqrt{2}}:=B(R+1/\sqrt{2})\setminus\overline{B(R-1/\sqrt{2})}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\>R-1/\sqrt{2}<\|z\|<R+1/\sqrt{2}\},

of constant width
\sqrt{2}
. One might expect that each of these lattice points belongs “randomly”, with probability
\frac{1}{2}
to the inner circle
B(R)
, independently of the other lattice points. Then, by
the standard square root cancellation laws (or, for example, the central limit theorem), a sequence of such growing sums of
\sim\operatorname{Area}(\mathscr{D}_{R;1/\sqrt{2}})=\sqrt{8}\pi R
i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables, is a.s. bounded by a function growing only slightly faster than
R^{1/2}
. Of course, the events that correspond to whether a lattice point lies in the inner circle
cannot be literally independent, since that would imply too far fetched conclusions, contradicting, in part, Hardy’s lower bound; clearly, the relative positions of two neighboring lattice points are not independent.
However, a weaker statement, e.g. independence of the positions of sufficiently far apart lattice points, could still asymptotically hold.

In this article we explore this heuristic, and examine under what circumstances lattice points lying near the boundary of
B(R)
interact independently from one another. Let

(1.3)

\Gamma_{R}:=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap\mathscr{D}_{R;1/\sqrt{2}}

be the set of lattice points lying in the annulus
\mathscr{D}_{R;1/\sqrt{2}}
. Also, let

S=\left[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right]^{2}
be the unit square, and for

\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
denote
S(\lambda):=S+\lambda
to be the unit square shifted by
\lambda
. Also, for
\lambda\in\Gamma_{R}
let

\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda):=\operatorname{Area}\left(S(\lambda)\cap B(R)\right)=\left(\chi_{B(R)}*\chi_{S}\right)(\lambda)

where
\chi_{B(R)}
and
\chi_{S}
are the characteristic functions of the disc
B(R)
and the square
S
respectively, and
\chi_{B(R)}*\chi_{S}
is their convolution. Namely,
\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda)
is the area of the portion of
S(\lambda)
lying inside
B(R)
.
The main focus of this work is the distribution, as
R\rightarrow\infty
, of the (deterministic) numbers

(1.4)

\Lambda_{R}:=\left\{\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda):\>\lambda\in\Gamma_{R}\right\},

that appear naturally in the Gauss circle problem.

3.
Discussion

3.1.
On the proofs of the main results

Given
\lambda=(R+r_{\lambda})e^{i\theta_{\lambda}}
as in (
2.1
), it will be shown that as
R\rightarrow\infty
the value of
\pazocal A_{R}(\lambda)
depends, up to lower order terms, only on
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda})
and not
R
, see (
4.4
). Since the leading term in the r.h.s. of (
4.4
) is a continuous functional
\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r,\theta)
as in (
2.3
),
it is possible to infer the distribution of
\{\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda})\}_{\lambda\in\Gamma_{R}}
from the equidistribution of Proposition
2.1
of the shifted polar coordinates

\{(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda})\}_{\lambda\in\Gamma_{R}}\subseteq G

of lattice points, via an application of the continuous mapping theorem. This approach will yield the asymptotic distribution part of Theorem
2.2
. The variance part of Theorem
2.2
is done by a direct and explicit, alas somewhat long and tedious computation of
\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r,\theta)
, and its variance.

To prove Theorem
2.7
one aims to rigorously justify the limit model of Section
2.2
.
In analogy to the above, the correlations

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K}\pazocal A_{R}(\lambda_{j})\pazocal A_{R}(\lambda_{j+k})

may also be understood in terms of the joint distribution of the tuples
(r_{j},\theta_{j})
and
(r_{j+k},\theta_{j+k})
with
j\in[1,\ldots,K]
. Rather, given
k\geq 1
and
(r_{j},\theta_{j})
, one has
\theta_{j}\approx\theta_{j+k}
, and the random model suggests that
r_{j+k}
is almost fully determined by
(r_{j},\theta_{j})
essentially via

(3.1)

r_{j+k}=\rho_{k}(r_{j},\theta_{j})+O_{k}\bigg(\frac{1}{R}\bigg),

which holds for a subset of
\{j:1\leq j\leq K\}
of full asymptotic density.
The function
\rho_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
is somewhat implicit, and difficult to use for practical purposes, though perhaps it could be employed for numerical computations of the values of
\pazocal C_{k}
. However, for the
existence
of the limit of Theorem
2.7
, only the continuity of
\rho_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
outside a measure zero sets of values of
(r,\theta)\in G
will be needed in order to apply the continuous mapping theorem again.

A number of obstacles exist to justify the above. First, the lattice points in the annulus
\mathscr{D}_{R;1/\sqrt{2}}
next to a given
\lambda_{j}
might, in general, fail to be the same as those lying in the approximating rectangle. Second, even if these two sets coincide, the ordering, w.r.t. the polar angle in the annulus might differ from the ordering and w.r.t. the projection on the long side of the approximating rectangle. If either of these two problematic scenarios occur, then one may no longer infer the important approximate identity (
3.1
). Proposition
5.5
and its proof shows that either of these two obstacles is rare: Discrepancies between the said two sets would imply a lattice point in a narrow sector, whereas the ordering violation would imply a very restrictive condition on the angle of
\lambda_{j}
or one of its neighbors.

The strategy used to prove theorems
2.4
and
2.5
is similar, and
we will only describe the ideas used in the proof of Theorem
2.5
.
Let

\pazocal B=\left\{(\lambda,\mu)\in\Gamma_{R}\times\Gamma_{R}:\theta_{\lambda}-\theta_{\mu}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)\in\left[\frac{k}{\pazocal K},\frac{k+2\pi}{\pazocal K}\right]\right\},

where
\pazocal K=\pazocal K(R)=\sqrt{8}\pi R
.
By our earlier observation, to prove Theorem
2.5
it suffices to compute the joint distribution of

(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})_{(\lambda,\mu)\in\pazocal B}
, that is, we wish to asymptotically estimate

(3.2)

\frac{1}{\pazocal K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\lambda,\mu)\in\pazocal B\\
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})\in(I_{1},J,I_{2})\end{subarray}}1

as
R\rightarrow\infty
where
I_{1},I_{2}\subseteq[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]
and
J\subseteq[0,2\pi)
are intervals.
To analyze the above sum, we expand the condition
\theta_{\lambda}-\theta_{\mu}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi)\in[\frac{k}{\pazocal K},\frac{k+2\pi}{\pazocal K}]
as, roughly, a trigonometric polynomial of degree
\pazocal K
, i.e.

\frac{1}{\pazocal K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\lambda,\mu)\in\pazocal B\\
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})\in(I_{1},J,I_{2})\end{subarray}}1\approx\frac{1}{\pazocal K^{2}}\sum_{|\ell|\leq\pazocal K}e^{-2\pi i\ell k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu,\lambda\in\Gamma_{R}\\
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})\in(I_{1},J,I_{2})\end{subarray}}e^{i\ell(\theta_{\lambda}-\theta_{\mu})}.

We then transform the inner sum on the r.h.s. using Poisson summation (to make this rigorous we use a smooth function that approximates the condition
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})\in(I_{1},J,I_{2})
) and collect a main term from
\ell=0
, which corresponds to the equidistribution of
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})
. In our range of interest the dual sum after applying Poisson summation will be shorter than the original sum, providing a gain at this step. However if one bounds the
\ell\neq 0
terms individually this gives a worse than trivial estimate, since our sum over
\ell
is long and even the optimal conjectural bounds for the individual sums would not suffice here.

We proceed to average the dual sums over
|\ell|\leq\pazocal K
and this procedure yields a secondary main term. For fixed
k
the aforementioned secondary term is the same size as the main term, which reflects the rigidity of neighboring lattice points, whereas for
k\rightarrow\infty
the analysis is more subtle. We express the secondary main term as an oscillatory integral with a phase function that includes the parameter
k
as a linear factor. Applying a stationary phase estimate, we conclude that the secondary main term decays relative to our main term as
k\rightarrow\infty
. This establishes the equidistribution of
(r_{\lambda},\theta_{\lambda},r_{\mu})_{(\lambda,\mu)\in\pazocal B}
from which Theorem
2.5
follows.

3.2.
Second order results for the Gauss circle problem

Gauss’ classical argument for estimating lattice points inside a circle shows that a bound for the remainder term in the asymptotic
expression (
1.7
) for the expectation

\frac{1}{K(R)}\sum\limits_{\lambda\in\Gamma_{R}}\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda)

and their number (
1.5
) give a bound for the error term
\Delta(R)
in the Gauss circle problem.
Therefore, the finer aspects of the distribution of the numbers
\{\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda)\}
are not expected to
improve the known bounds for
\Delta(R)
, and the assertions presented within this manuscript are
second-order
results giving an important insight on the Gauss circle problem.

3.3.
Equidistribution of lattice points in Diophantine rectangles

Though not explicitly stated, the essence of the vanishing
on average
of the
\pazocal{C}_{k}
in Theorem
1.4
has to do with the following equidistribution fact, encapsulated within the proof of Theorem
2.4
. Let
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
be an angle,
r\in[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]
and consider the tilted and shifted semi-infinite rectangle
\mathscr{R}=\mathscr{R}_{r,\theta}
as in (
2.7
). Then, for
“generic”

\theta
, the projection of
\{\kappa_{\ell}\}
, the lattice points (
2.8
) lying in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap\mathscr{R}
, ordered w.r.t. the projection onto the long side of
\mathscr{R}
, onto the short side of
\mathscr{R}
, are equidistributed in
I:=[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]
,
on average
w.r.t.
\ell
. That is, the set of the projections of
\{\kappa_{\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq L}
becomes equidistributed in
I
as
L\rightarrow\infty
, for a set of
\theta
of almost full measure (depending on
L
) inside
[0,2\pi)
.

The generic
\theta
above are those so that
\tan\theta
is far from a rational number with small denominator, or, what is equivalent, the direction
(\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})
is far from rational direction. Otherwise, if
(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
is rational, then
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cap\mathscr{R}
is periodic, so that their projections only the short side are far from equidistributed; in some extreme cases, such as, for example
\theta=0
or
\theta=\pi/4
, the distribution of the projections is dominated by a single atom at
-r
. The major challenge in proving Conjecture
1.3
is showing the same equidistribution, without averaging w.r.t.
\ell
, and exploiting the averaging w.r.t.
r
(which is not used in the aforementioned argument).

3.4.
Random tilts vs. random lattices

Taking the fixed lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and intersecting it with a randomly tilted semi-infinite rectangle is equivalent to intersecting the fixed semi-infinite rectangle with nonstandard, randomly tilted lattices. This construction is reminiscent of the random lattice problems that could be addressed using techniques involving ergodicity and the equidistibution of the given lattices w.r.t. the Haar measure defined on the space of lattices, see e.g. the recent survey
[
8
]
. Unfortunately, the (random) transformations applied on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, namely the random tilts (and the random shifts) are unlikely to equidistribute in the space of lattices w.r.t. the Haar measure, since the stretch transformation is not present within the allowed repertoire. Hence these ergodic methods are unlikely to be directly applicable in our problem, and the new techniques developed within this manuscript are necessary.

3.5.
Independence and uncorrelatedness at large distance

Since the
\pazocal{C}_{k}
, the output of Theorem
1.2
, is an outcome of a limit, as
R\rightarrow\infty
, of the correlations between
\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda_{j})
and
\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda_{j+k})
with
k

fixed
, that implies that the angles
\theta_{j}
and
\theta_{j+k}
(polar representation of
\lambda_{j}
and
\lambda_{j+k}
respectively) are asymptotic, and cannot possibly be independent. However, as a by-product of our analysis presented within the proofs of Theorem
2.4
and Theorem

, it follows that the
(r_{j},r_{j+k})
components of the shifted polar representation (

) of

and
\lambda_{j+k}
respectively, are still asymptotically independent, on average w.r.t.

, leading to Conjecture

, with

as in

independent
copies of
\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r,\theta)
.

Using the same reasoning as above, it makes sense to expect that as
R,k\rightarrow\infty

simultaneously
, with
k=t\cdot R
for some

fixed, the vector
(\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda_{j}),\pazocal{A}_{R}(\lambda_{j+k}))
converges in distribution to the limit random vector

\left(\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r,\theta),\pazocal{A}_{\infty}\left(r,\theta+t/(\sqrt{2}\pi)\right)\right).

On the other hand, since the distribution of
\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r,\theta)
, conditioned on
\theta
, genuinely depends on the value of
\theta
, the random variables
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
are not independent, and their being uncorrelated has to do with the symmetric distribution conditioned on
\theta
, for
every

\theta

The setting of Conjecture
2.3
(or Conjecture
1.3
), where no averaging w.r.t.

takes place, is very different from the averaging regime of Theorem
1.4
. Here, given

, the

is
fully dependent
on
r
, and, a forteriori, so does
\pazocal{A}_{\infty,k}(r,\theta)
fully depend on
\pazocal{A}_{\infty}(r,\theta)

though it quickly becomes unfeasible. Since, as

to grow, with a growing number of different ranges, it seems plausible that the map

exhibits statistical pseudorandomness, giving some confidence in Conjecture

9.1.
A summation formula

Notation 9.1
.

Given a Schwartz function
g:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
we define the Hankel type transform

\mathscr{B}_{l}(g)(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(y)J_{l}(2\pi\sqrt{\xi y})\,dy.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
we also write

\tau_{4\ell}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
\|\lambda\|^{2}=n\end{subarray}}e^{4i\ell\theta_{\lambda}}.

We note that
\tau_{-4\ell}(n)=\tau_{4\ell}(n)
and
|\tau_{4\ell}(n)|\leq\tau_{0}(n)\ll n^{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose
F
is a Schwartz function supported on the positive real numbers. Then for
X>0
we have that

(9.1)

\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{4\ell}(n)F\bigg(\frac{n}{X}\bigg)=\delta_{\ell=0}\cdot\pi X\int_{0}^{\infty}F(r)\,dr+\pi X\sum_{n\geq 1}\tau_{4\ell}(n)\mathscr{B}_{4\ell}(F)(nX),

where
\delta_{\ell=0}
is one if
\ell=0
and is zero otherwise.

Proof.

Let
\vartheta:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow[-2\pi,2\pi]
be given by

\vartheta(u,v)=\begin{cases}4\arctan(v/u)\,&\text{ if }\,u\neq 0,\\
0\,&\text{ if }\,u=0.\end{cases}

For any non-zero
\lambda=(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
note that
e^{i4\theta_{\lambda}}=e^{i\vartheta(a,b)}
. The l.h.s. of (
9.1
) is

=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}e^{i\ell\vartheta(a,b)}F\bigg(\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{X}\bigg).

We now apply Poisson summation to see the preceding expression is

(9.2)

=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i\ell\vartheta(u,v)}F\bigg(\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{X}\bigg)e(-au-bv)\,dudv.

We treat the term with
(a,b)=0
separately and use polar coordinates to see that it equals

(9.3)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{4i\theta\ell}F\bigg(\frac{r^{2}}{X}\bigg)rd\theta dr&=\frac{X}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(r)\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{4i\theta\ell}d\theta\bigg)dr=\delta_{\ell=0}\cdot\pi X\int_{0}^{\infty}F(r)\,dr.\end{split}

The terms with
\lambda=(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
in (
9.2
) are

(9.4)

\begin{split}&=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{4i\theta\ell}F\bigg(\frac{r^{2}}{X}\bigg)e(-\|\lambda\|r(\cos(\theta)\cos(\theta_{\lambda})+\sin(\theta)\sin(\theta_{\lambda})))\,rd\theta dr\\
&=\pi X\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(r)\bigg(\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{4i\theta\ell}e(-\|\lambda\|\sqrt{Xr}(\cos(\theta-\theta_{\lambda})))\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\bigg)dr.\end{split}

Also

(9.5)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{4i\theta\ell}e(-\|\lambda\|\sqrt{Xr}(\cos(\theta-\theta_{\lambda})))\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=e^{4i\ell\theta_{\lambda}}J_{4\ell}(2\pi\|\lambda\|\sqrt{Xr}),

and note
[
4
, 8.404.2,8.411.1]
that
J_{4\ell}(\cdot)=J_{-4\ell}(\cdot)
.
Combining (
9.4
) and (
9.5
) then using the resulting formula with (
9.3
) in (
9.2
) we complete the proof.
∎

To quantify how the summation formula (
9.1
) transforms our sum let us record the following estimate, that essentially follows from integrating by parts.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
l
be an integer, and let
\mathscr{B}_{l}(W_{R,j})
be the Hankel type transform of
W_{R,j}
as in Notation
9.1
.
Then, for
j=1,2
and any integer
A\geq 1
, and real number
\xi\geq 1
, we have

\mathscr{B}_{l}(W_{R,j})(\xi)\ll\bigg(\frac{|l|+RP}{\sqrt{\xi}}\bigg)^{A}\frac{1}{R}.

Additionally, if we also have that
\xi\geq 10l^{2}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|W_{j}^{(m)}(x)|dx\ll P^{m-1}
for each integer
m\geq 1
then

\mathscr{B}_{l}(W_{R,j})(\xi)\ll\frac{|l|^{A}+R^{A}P^{A-1}}{R\xi^{A/2+1/4}}.

Our range of interest will be

and the lemma implies that the dual sum after applying Poisson summation is essentially supported on integers
n
with
n\leq P^{2}R^{\varepsilon}
. Coincidentally, this range is at the transition regime for the Bessel function.

Proof.

By
[
4
, 8.472.3]
we have

\frac{d}{dx}(x^{v+1}J_{v+1}(x))=x^{v+1}J_{v}(x).

For brevity write
W_{R}=W_{R,j}
.
We integrate by parts to get

(9.6)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}W_{R}(y)J_{l}(\sqrt{\xi y})\,dy=&\frac{2}{\xi}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{-l}W_{R}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)d(y^{l+1}J_{l+1}(y))\\
=&\frac{2}{\xi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{2y^{2}}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)-lW_{R}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)\bigg)J_{l+1}(y)\,dy.\end{split}

Integrating by parts once more, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)J_{l+1}(y)\,dy&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{-l}}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)d(y^{l+2}J_{l+2}(y))\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y}\bigg(\frac{2y^{4}}{\xi^{2}}W_{R}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)-\frac{ly^{2}}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)\bigg)J_{l+2}(y)\,dy.\end{split}

Similarly,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)J_{l+2}(y)\,dy&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{-l-2}}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)d(y^{l+3}J_{l+3}(y))\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2y^{4}}{\xi^{2}}W_{R}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)-(l+2)\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}W_{R}^{\prime}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)\bigg)J_{l+3}(y)\,dy.\end{split}

Thus repeatedly integrating by parts, and arguing as above one sees that

\begin{split}\mathscr{B}_{l}(W_{R})(\tfrac{\xi}{4\pi^{2}})\ll&\frac{1}{\xi}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{-A+1}|J_{l+A}(y)|\sum_{j=0}^{A}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)^{j}(|l|+1)^{A-j}\bigg|W_{R}^{(j)}\bigg(\frac{y^{2}}{\xi}\bigg)\bigg|\,dy\\
\ll&\frac{1}{\xi^{A/2}R}\sum_{j=0}^{A}R^{j}(|l|+1)^{A-j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+y/R)^{j-A/2}|J_{l+A}(\sqrt{\xi(1+\tfrac{y}{R}})||W^{(j)}(y)|\,dy,\end{split}

where in the second step we made the change of variables
y\rightarrow\sqrt{\xi(1+\tfrac{y}{R})}
and used that
W_{R}^{(l)}(y)=R^{l}W^{(l)}((y-1)R)
.
Noting that
J_{l}(x)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e(lt)e^{-ix\sin 2\pi t}\,dt
, we see
|J_{l}(x)|\leq 1
.
Additionally, we have that
J_{l}(x)\ll 1/\sqrt{x}
for

[
4
, 8.453.1]
. Using these estimates in the above equation and bounding

completes the proof.
∎

|\xi-a|>\frac{1}{10^{4}\Delta_{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\xi|(\xi-a)^{2}}\,d\xi\ll\frac{\log(\Delta_{1}+1)}{\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}}.\end{split}

By (

(10.27)
