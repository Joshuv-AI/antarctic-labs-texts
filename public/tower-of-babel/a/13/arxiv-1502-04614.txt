Entire Functions Sharing Small Functions With Their Difference
Operators

Abstract




Abstract.
We investigate uniqueness problems for an
entire function that shares two small functions of finite order with their
difference operators. In particular, we give a generalization of a result in

[2]
.

2010
Mathematics Subject Classification
:30D35, 39A32.

Key words
: Uniqueness, Entire functions, Difference
operators.

1
Introduction and Main Results

Throughout this paper, we assume that the reader is familiar with
the fundamental results and the standard notations of the Nevanlinna’s value
distribution theory
(\left[7\right],

\left[9\right],

\left[12\right])
. In addition, we will use
\rho\left(f\right)
to denote the
order of growth of
f
and
\tau\left(f\right)
to denote the type of
growth of
f
, we say that a meromorphic function
a\left(z\right)
is a
small function of
f\left(z\right)
if
T\left(r,a\right)=S\left(r,f\right),
where
S\left(r,f\right)=o\left(T\left(r,f\right)\right),
as
r\rightarrow\infty
outside of a possible exceptional set of
finite logarithmic measure, we use
S\left(f\right)
to denote the family
of all small functions with respect to
f\left(z\right)
. For a
meromorphic function
f\left(z\right),
we define its shift by
f_{c}\left(z\right)=f\left(z+c\right)

\left(\text{Resp. }f_{0}\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)\right)
and its difference operators by

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)=f\left(z+c\right)-f\left(z\right),\text{ \ \ }\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}^{n-1}\left(\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)\right),\text{ }n\in\mathbb{N},\text{ }n\geq 2.

In particular,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=\Delta^{n}f\left(z\right)
for the case
c=1.

Let
f\left(z\right)
and
g\left(z\right)
be two
meromorphic functions, and let
a\left(z\right)
be a small function with
respect to
f\left(z\right)
and
g\left(z\right).
We say that
f\left(z\right)
and
g\left(z\right)
share
a\left(z\right)
CM (counting
multiplicity), provided that
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)
and
g\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)
have the same zeros with the same
multiplicities.

The problem of meromorphic functions sharing small
functions with their differences is an important topic of uniqueness theory
of meromorphic functions
\left(\text{see, }\left[1,4-6\right]\right)
.
In 1986, Jank, Mues and Volkmann
\left(\text{see, }\left[8\right]\right)
proved:

Theorem A

Let
f
be a nonconstant
meromorphic function, and let
a\neq 0
be a finite constant. If
f,

f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
share the value
a
CM, then
f\equiv f^{\prime}.

In
\left[11\right],
P. Li and C. C. Yang gives the following
generalization of Theorem A.

Theorem B

Let
f
be a nonconstant
entire function, let
a
be a finite nonzero constant, and let
n
be a positive integer. If
f
,
f^{\left(n\right)}
and
f^{\left(n+1\right)}
share the value
a
CM, then
f\equiv f^{\prime}.

In
\left[2\right],
B. Chen et al proved a difference
analogue of result of Theorem A and obtained the following results:

Theorem C
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic
entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f
and
\Delta_{c}^{2}f
share
a\left(z\right)
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\equiv\Delta_{c}^{2}f.

Theorem D
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let
a\left(z\right),

b\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be periodic entire functions with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{2}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\equiv\Delta_{c}^{2}f.

Recently in
\left[3\right],
B. Chen and S. Li
generalized Theorem C and proved the following results:

Theorem E
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic
entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f

\left(n\geq 2\right)

share
a\left(z\right)
CM, then
\Delta_{c}f\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f.

Theorem F
Let
f\left(z\right)

be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=C\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right),
where
C
is a
nonzero constant.

It is interesting now to see what happening when
f\left(z\right)
,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 1\right)
share
a\left(z\right)
CM. The main of this paper is to give a difference analogue of
result of Theorem B. In fact, we prove that the conclusion of Theorems E and
F remains valid when we replace
\Delta_{c}f\left(z\right)
by
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
, and we obtain the following results.

Theorem 1.1

Let
f\left(z\right)

be a nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let
a\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a
periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right)
,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 1\right)

share
a\left(z\right)

CM, then
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right).

Example 1.1
Let
f\left(z\right)=e^{z\ln 2}
and
c=1.

Then, for any
a\in\mathbb{C},
we notice that
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
a
CM for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we can easily see that
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right).
This example satisfies Theorem 1.1.

Remark 1.1
In Example 1.1, we have
\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
for any
integer
m>n+1.
However, it remains open when
f\left(z\right)
,
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

and

\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z\right)

\left(m>n+1\right)
share

a\left(z\right)
CM, the claim
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
in Theorem 1.1 can be replaced by
\Delta_{c}^{m}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
in general.

Theorem 1.2
Let
f\left(z\right)
be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order, and let
a\left(z\right),

b\left(z\right)\left(\not\equiv 0\right)\in S\left(f\right)
be a periodic entire function with period
c
. If
f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-b\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right).

Theorem 1.3
Let
f\left(z\right)
be a
nonconstant entire function of finite order. If
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM, then
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv C\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right),
where
C
is a nonzero constant.

Example 1.2
Let
f\left(z\right)=e^{az}
and
c=1

where
a\neq 2k\pi i

\left(k\in\mathbb{Z}\right),
it is clear that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)=\left(e^{a}-1\right)^{n}e^{az}
for any integer
n\geq 1.
So,
f\left(z\right),

\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)

and
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)
share
0
CM for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and we can easily see that
\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)\equiv C\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)
where
C=e^{a}-1.
This example satisfies
Theorem 1.3.

2
Some lemmas

Lemma 2.1
\left[10\right]

Let
f
and
g
be meromorphic functions such that
0<

\rho\left(f\right),\rho\left(g\right)<\infty
and
0<\tau\left(f\right),\tau\left(g\right)<\infty.
Then we have

\left(\text{i}\right)

If
\rho\left(f\right)>\rho\left(g\right),

then we obtain

\tau\left(f+g\right)=\tau\left(fg\right)=\tau\left(f\right).

\left(\text{ii}\right)

If
\rho\left(f\right)=\rho\left(g\right)

and
\tau\left(f\right)\neq\tau\left(g\right),

then we get

\rho\left(f+g\right)=\rho\left(fg\right)=\rho\left(f\right)=\rho\left(g\right).

Lemma 2.2
\left[12\right]

Suppose
f_{j}\left(z\right)

(j=1,2,\cdots,n+1)
and
g_{j}\left(z\right)

(j=1,2,\cdots,n)

(n\geq 1)
are
entire functions satisfying the following conditions:

\left(\text{i}\right)

\overset{n}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}f_{j}\left(z\right)e^{g_{j}\left(z\right)}\equiv f_{n+1}\left(z\right);

\left(\text{ii}\right)
The order of
f_{j}\left(z\right)
is less than the order of
e^{g_{k}\left(z\right)}
for
1\leq j\leq n+1,

1\leq k\leq n.
And
furthermore, the order of
f_{j}\left(z\right)
is less than the
order of
e^{g_{h}\left(z\right)-g_{k}\left(z\right)}
for
n\geq 2
and
1\leq j\leq n+1,

1\leq h<k\leq n.

Then
f_{j}\left(z\right)\equiv 0,

\left(j=1,2,\cdots n+1\right).

Lemma 2.3
\left[5\right]

Let
c\in\mathbb{C},

n\in\mathbb{N},
and let
f\left(z\right)
be a meromorphic function
of finite order. Then for any small periodic function
a\left(z\right)
with period
c,
with respect to
f\left(z\right),

m\left(r,\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n}f}{f-a}\right)=S\left(r,f\right),

where the exceptional set associated with
S\left(r,f\right)
is of at most finite logarithmic measure.

3
Proof of the Theorems

Proof of the Theorem 1.1.
Suppose on the contrary to the
assertion that
\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)\not\equiv\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right).
Note that
f\left(z\right)
is a nonconstant
entire function of finite order. By Lemma 2.3, for
n\geq 1
, we have

T\left(r,\Delta_{c}^{n}f\right)=m\left(r,\Delta_{c}^{n}f\right)\leq m\left(r,\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n}f}{f}\right)+m\left(r,f\right)\leq T\left(r,f\right)+S\left(r,f\right).

Since
f\left(z\right)
,
\Delta^{n}f\left(z\right)
and
\Delta^{n+1}f\left(z\right)

\left(n\geq 1\right)
share
a\left(z\right)

CM, then

\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}=e^{P\left(z\right)}

(3.1)

and

\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}=e^{Q\left(z\right)},

(3.2)

where
P
and
Q
are polynomials. Set

\varphi\left(z\right)=\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f\left(z\right)-\Delta_{c}^{n}f\left(z\right)}{f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right)}.

(3.3)

From
\left(3.1\right)
and
\left(3.2\right),
we get
\varphi\left(z\right)=e^{Q\left(z\right)}-e^{P\left(z\right)}.
Then, by supposition
and
(3.3)
, we see that
\varphi\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
. By Lemma
2.3, we deduce that

T\left(r,\varphi\right)=m\left(r,\varphi\right)\leq m\left(r,\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n+1}f}{f-a}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{\Delta_{c}^{n}f}{f-a}\right)+O\left(1\right)=S\left(r,f\right).

(3.4)

Note that
\frac{e^{Q\left(z\right)}}{\varphi\left(z\right)}-\frac{e^{P\left(z\right)}}{\varphi\left(z\right)}=1.
By using the second
main theorem and
(3.4)
, we have

T\left(r,\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}\right)\leq\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{\varphi}{e^{Q}}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}-1}\right)+S\left(r,\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}\right)

=\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{\varphi}{e^{Q}}\right)+\overline{N}\left(r,\frac{\varphi}{e^{P}}\right)+S\left(r,\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}\right)

=S\left(r,f\right)+S\left(r,\frac{e^{Q}}{\varphi}\right).

(3.5)

Thus, by
(3.4)
and
(3.5)
, we have
T(r,e^{Q})

=S(r,f)
. Similarly,
T(r,e^{P})=S(r,f)
. Setting now
g\left(z\right)=f\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right),
we have from
\left(3.1\right)
and
\left(3.2\right)

\Delta_{c}^{n}g\left(z\right)=g\left(z\right)e^{P\left(z\right)}+a\left(z\right)

(3.6)

and

\Delta_{c}^{n+1}g\left(z\right)=g\left(z\right)e^{Q\left(z\right)}+a\left(z\right).

(3.7)

By
\left(3.6\right)
and
\left(3.7\right),
we have

g\left(z\right)e^{Q\left(z\right)}+a\left(z\right)=\Delta_{c}\left(\Delta_{c}^{n}g\left(z\right)\right)=\Delta_{c}\left(g\left(z\right)e^{P\left(z\right)}+a\left(z\right)\right).

Thus

g\left(z\right)e^{Q\left(z\right)}+a\left(z\right)=g_{c}\left(z\right)e^{P_{c}\left(z\right)}-g\left(z\right)e^{P\left(z\right)},

which implies

g_{c}\left(z\right)=M\left(z\right)g\left(z\right)+N\left(z\right),

(3.8)

where
M\left(z\right)=e^{-P_{c}\left(z\right)}\left(e^{P\left(z\right)}+e^{Q\left(z\right)}\right)
and
N\left(z\right)=a\left(z\right)e^{-P_{c}\left(z\right)}.
From
\left(3.8\right),
we have

g_{2c}\left(z\right)=M_{c}\left(z\right)g_{c}\left(z\right)+N_{c}\left(z\right)=M_{c}\left(z\right)\left(M\left(z\right)g\left(z\right)+N\left(z\right)\right)+N_{c}\left(z\right),

hence

g_{2c}\left(z\right)=M_{c}\left(z\right)M_{0}\left(z\right)g\left(z\right)+N^{1}\left(z\right),

where
N^{1}\left(z\right)=M_{c}\left(z\right)N_{0}\left(z\right)+N_{c}\left(z\right).
By the same method, we can deduce that

g_{ic}\left(z\right)=\left(\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}M_{kc}\left(z\right)\right)g\left(z\right)+N^{i-1}\left(z\right)\text{
}\left(i\geq 1\right),

(3.9)

where
N^{i-1}\left(z\right)

\left(i\geq 1\right)
is an entire
function depending on
a\left(z\right),e^{P\left(z\right)},e^{Q\left(z\right)}
and their differences. Now, we can rewrite
\left(3.6\right)

as

\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}g_{ic}\left(z\right)=\left(e^{P\left(z\right)}-\left(-1\right)^{n}\right)g\left(z\right)+a\left(z\right).

(3.10)

By
\left(3.9\right)
and
\left(3.10\right),
we have

\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}\left(\left(\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}M_{kc}\left(z\right)\right)g\left(z\right)+N^{i-1}\left(z\right)\right)-\left(e^{P\left(z\right)}-\left(-1\right)^{n}\right)g\left(z\right)=a\left(z\right)

which implies

A\left(z\right)g\left(z\right)+B\left(z\right)=0,

(3.11)

where

A\left(z\right)=\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}M_{kc}\left(z\right)-e^{P\left(z\right)}+\left(-1\right)^{n}

and

B\left(z\right)=\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}N^{i-1}\left(z\right)-a\left(z\right).

It is clear that
A\left(z\right)
and
B\left(z\right)
are small
functions with respect to
f\left(z\right).
If

A\left(z\right)\not\equiv 0
, then
\left(3.11\right)
yields the contradiction

T\left(r,f\right)=T\left(r,g\right)=T\left(r,\frac{B}{A}\right)=S\left(r,f\right).

Suppose now that
A\left(z\right)\equiv 0,
rewrite the equation
A\left(z\right)\equiv 0
as

\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}e^{-P_{\left(k+1\right)c}}\left(e^{P_{kc}}+e^{Q_{kc}}\right)=e^{P}-\left(-1\right)^{n}.

We can rewrite the left side of above equality as

\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{-\overset{i}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}P_{kc}}\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}\left(e^{P_{kc}}+e^{Q_{kc}}\right)

=\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{-\overset{i}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}P_{kc}}e^{\overset{i-1}{\underset{k=0}{\sum}}P_{kc}}\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}\left(1+e^{Q_{kc}-P_{kc}}\right)

\overset{n}{=\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{P-P_{ic}}\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}\left(1+e^{Q_{kc}-P_{kc}}\right).

So

\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{P-P_{ic}}\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}\left(1+e^{h_{kc}}\right)=e^{P}-\left(-1\right)^{n},

(3.12)

where
h_{kc}=Q_{kc}-P_{kc}.
On the other hand, let
\Omega_{i}=\left\{0,1,\cdots,i-1\right\}
be a finite set of
i
elements, and

P\left(\Omega_{i}\right)=\{\varnothing,\left\{0\right\},\left\{1\right\},\cdots,\left\{i-1\right\},\left\{0,1\right\},\left\{0,2\right\},\cdots,\Omega_{i}\},

where
\varnothing
is an empty set. It is easy to see that

\underset{k=0}{\overset{i-1}{\prod}}\left(1+e^{h_{kc}}\right)=1+\underset{A\in P\left(\Omega_{i}\right)\backslash\left\{\varnothing\right\}}{\sum}\exp\left(\underset{j\in A}{\sum}h_{jc}\right)

=1+\left[e^{h}+e^{h_{c}}+\cdots+e^{h_{\left(i-1\right)c}}\right]+\left[e^{h+h_{c}}+e^{h+h_{2c}}+\cdots\right]+\cdots+\left[e^{h+h_{c}+\cdots+h_{\left(i-1\right)c}}\right].

(3.13)

Dividing the proof on two parts:

Part (1).
h\left(z\right)
is non-constant polynomial.
Suppose that
h\left(z\right)=a_{m}z^{m}+\cdots+a_{0}

\left(a_{m}\neq 0\right),
since
P\left(\Omega_{i}\right)\subset P\left(\Omega_{i+1}\right),
then by
\left(3.12\right)
and
\left(3.13\right)
we
have

\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{P-P_{ic}}+\alpha_{1}e^{a_{m}z^{m}}+\alpha_{2}e^{2a_{m}z^{m}}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e^{na_{m}z^{m}}=e^{P}-\left(-1\right)^{n}

which is equivalent to

\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}e^{a_{m}z^{m}}+\alpha_{2}e^{2a_{m}z^{m}}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e^{na_{m}z^{m}}=e^{P},

(3.14)

where
\alpha_{i}

\left(i=0,\cdots,n\right)
are entire functions of
order less than
m.
Moreover,

\alpha_{0}=\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{P-P_{ic}}+\left(-1\right)^{n}

=e^{P}\left(\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}C_{n}^{i}\left(-1\right)^{n-i}e^{-P_{ic}}+\left(-1\right)^{n}e^{-P}\right)=e^{P}\Delta_{c}^{n}e^{-P}.

\left(\text{i}\right)
If
\deg P>m
, then we obtain from
\left(3.14\right)
that

\deg P\leq m

which is a contradiction.

\left(\text{ii}\right)
If
\deg P<m,
then by using Lemma 2.1
and
\left(3.14\right)
we obtain

\deg P=\rho\left(e^{P}\right)=\rho\left(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}e^{a_{m}z^{m}}+\alpha_{2}e^{2a_{m}z^{m}}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e^{na_{m}z^{m}}\right)=m,

which is also a contradiction.

\left(\text{iii}\right)
If
\deg P=m,
then we suppose that
P\left(z\right)=dz^{m}+P^{\ast}\left(z\right)
where
\deg P^{\ast}<m.

We have to study two subcases:

\left(\ast\right)
If
d\neq ia_{m}

\left(i=1,\cdots,n\right),
then we have

\alpha_{1}e^{a_{m}z^{m}}+\alpha_{2}e^{2a_{m}z^{m}}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e^{na_{m}z^{m}}-e^{P^{\ast}}e^{dz^{m}}=-\alpha_{0}.

By using Lemma 2.2, we obtain
e^{P^{\ast}}\equiv 0,
which is impossible.

\left(\ast\ast\right)
Suppose now that there exists at most
