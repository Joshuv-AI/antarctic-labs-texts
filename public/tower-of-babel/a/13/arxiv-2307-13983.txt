Pleijel nodal domain theorem in non-smooth setting
Nicolò De Ponti, Sara Farinelli, Ivan Yuri Violo

Abstract
We prove the Pleijel theorem in non-collapsed RCD spaces, providing an asymptotic upper bound on the number of nodal domains of Laplacian eigenfunctions. As a consequence, we obtain that the Courant nodal domain theorem holds except at most for a finite number of eigenvalues. More in general, we show that the same result is valid for Neumann (resp. Dirichlet) eigenfunctions on uniform domains (resp. bounded open sets). This is new even in the Euclidean space, where the Pleijel theorem in the Neumann case was open under low boundary-regularity.



1
Introduction

Given a continuous eigenfunction
u
of a linear operator
L
, there is a lot of interest in studying the properties of its nodal domains, the latter being defined as the connected components of the set
\{u\neq 0\}
. In the usual setting
L
is an operator of differential nature, with discrete spectrum
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq\ldots,
and a classical problem is to bound the number of nodal domains of
u_{k}
in terms of
k
. Here
u_{k}
is an eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

There are two main results known in this direction. The first one, due to Courant
[
35
]
(see also
[
36
]
), provides a pointwise bound:
for every

k
the number of nodal domains of
u_{k}
is less than or equal to
k
. The second one is due to Pleijel
[
78
]
and provides an asymptotic upper bound, which implies that
for sufficiently large

k
the number of nodal domains of
u_{k}
is strictly less than
k
.

The theorems of Courant and Pleijel have been deeply investigated in a different number of situations, see
[
10
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
25
,
30
,
37
,
40
,
45
,
56
,
66
,
69
,
79
,
86
]
for a non-exhaustive list. The main goal of the present paper is to obtain an asymptotic upper bound on the number of nodal domains for Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacian eigenfunctions in the setting of possibly non-smooth metric measure spaces.
In particular, we focus on the class of
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces consisting of metric measure spaces satisfying a synthetic notion of Ricci curvature bounded from below by
K
and dimension bounded from above by
N
(see
[
3
]
and Section
2.5
for more details). Nevertheless, our analysis is of interest already in the Euclidean case since we prove the Pleijel theorem for Lipschitz and even more rough domains (see Corollary
1.3
below and the subsequent discussion). Indeed, the validity of a Pleijel result in the Neumann case with boundary regularity below
C^{1,1}
was an open question in the field (see the comments after Remark 1.2 in
[
56
]
).

About the Courant nodal domain theorem, let us just briefly mention that its validity is open for
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces. This is mainly due to the fact that the
weak unique continuation property
for the Laplacian in this setting is currently not known. We refer to
[
44
,
43
]
for more on this problem, where also the failure of the
strong
unique continuation property in the
\mathrm{RCD}
setting is shown. We remark that a worse, but still pointwise, upper bound on the number of nodal domains can be easily deduced from the variational characterization of the eigenvalues (see
[
66
]
).

Before stating our main result, let us first introduce the setting and some notations referring to Section
2
for the precise definitions. Our investigation deals with eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet or Neumann Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
,
\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}
in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
in a
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
, where
\mathcal{H}^{N}
denotes the
N
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
({\rm X},{\sf d})
. As usual in this kind of problems, some additional assumptions are required to deal with the Neumann case and we will demand that
\Omega
is a
uniform domain
(see Definition
3.1
). We will clarify below why we need to restrict our attention to RCD spaces endowed with the Hausdorff measure, called
non-collapsed
in the literature, instead of considering the full RCD class. Here we limit ourselves to mention that these assumptions are sufficient for the Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacian in
\Omega
to have discrete spectrum and for the eigenfunctions to be continuous. We list the Dirichlet and Neumann eigenvalues respectively by

\begin{split}&0\leq\lambda_{1}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\rightarrow+\infty,\\
&0=\lambda_{1}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\ldots\rightarrow+\infty,\end{split}

counted with multiplicity. Thanks to the continuity of a Laplacian eigenfunction
u
in our setting, it makes sense to define its
nodal domains
, which are the connected components of
\Omega\setminus\{u=0\}
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we can now define

M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{D}}(k):=\sup\left\{\#\text{ of nodal domains of }u:u\ \textrm{Dirichlet eigenfunction of eigenvalue }\ \lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\right\}

and analogously
M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{N}}
in the Neumann case (see Def.
6.3
for more detailed definitions of
M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{D}},M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{N}}
).
We finally denote by
j_{\alpha}
the first positive zero of the Bessel function of index
\alpha>0
and by
\omega_{N}
the volume of the unit ball in the
N
-dimensional Euclidean space.

Theorem 1.1

(Pleijel theorem in RCD setting - Neumann and Dirichlet cases)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space, with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\geq 2
, and let
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be an open and bounded set. Then

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{D}}(k)}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega^{2}_{N}j^{N}_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}}}<1\,.

(1.1)

If moreover
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
is a uniform domain, then

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{N}}(k)}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega^{2}_{N}j^{N}_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}}}<1\,.

(1.2)

In particular, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
large enough every Dirichlet (resp. Neumann) eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
(resp.
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)
) in any
\Omega
bounded open set (resp. uniform domain) has less than
k
nodal domains.

There has been recently a growing interest in the study of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Laplacian and their zero set in the setting of RCD spaces (see
[
7
,
6
,
61
,
62
,
42
,
18
,
28
,
44
,
92
,
8
,
43
]
). However, to the best of our knowledge, Theorem
1.1
is the first non-trivial result related to nodal domains.

The class of non-collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space includes non-collapsed Ricci limit spaces
[
32
,
33
]
and finite dimensional Alexandrov spaces
[
77
,
91
]
, and our result is new also for these classes of spaces where the Courant’s nodal domain theorem is not known.
Additionally, thanks to the recent
[
83
]
, we know that every
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space contains a rich class of non-trivial uniform domains, hence it is possible to find many sets that satisfy the assumptions of our result also in the Neumann case.
We recall that, in the somewhat easier Dirichlet case, Theorem
1.1
goes back to the work of Pleijel
[
78
]
in the Euclidean plane and to Bérard and Meyer
[
17
]
for smooth Riemannian manifolds.

When
{\rm X}
is bounded it is allowed to take
\Omega={\rm X}
in the Theorem
1.1
. In this case Neumann eigenfunctions coincide with the usual Laplacian eigenfunctions on
{\rm X}
and we have the following.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
be a compact
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space, with
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\geq 2
. Denote by
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
the eigenvalues of the Laplacian in
{\rm X}
and by
M(k)
the maximal number of nodal domains of any Laplacian eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}.

Then

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{M(k)}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega^{2}_{N}j^{N}_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}}}<1\,.

(1.3)

The result in Theorem
1.1
in the case of Neumann eigenfunctions is interesting already when taking
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
to be the
N
-dimensional Euclidean space. We extract this version below in a self-contained statement, for the convenience of the reader.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 2,
be a uniform domain. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
denote by
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)
the Neumann Laplacian eigenvalues in
\Omega
and by
M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{N}}(k)
the maximal number of nodal domains of a Neumann eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega).
Then

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{N}}(k)}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega^{2}_{N}j^{N}_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}}}<1.

(1.4)

Recall that the class of uniform domains in the Euclidean space includes bounded
Lipschitz domains
, but also more irregular domains such as
quasi disks
and in particular the interior of a
Koch Snowflake
(see Section
3.1
for more details and references).
A Pleijel theorem for Neumann eigenfunctions of Euclidean domains was firstly proved by Polterovich
[
79
]
, who considered planar domains with piecewise real analytic boundary. The general
N
-dimensional case was obtained in
[
69
]
for domains
\Omega
with
C^{1,1}
boundary, where the regularity assumption is required in order to apply to eigenfunctions a reflection procedure across the boundary of
\Omega
. The same limitation on the regularity of the boundary appears in
[
56
]
(in the context of more general Robin problems), where it is explicitly stated the problem of the validity of Pleijel theorem under a weaker regularity of the boundary. Very recently, the techniques introduced in
[
69
]
were employed and refined in
[
15
]
in the planar case, where the authors were able to treat
2
-dimensional domains with smooth boundaries except for a finite number of vertices.

Our work introduces a different strategy and avoids any reflection argument, allowing us to handle more general domains without imposing any restriction on the dimension. To explain the basic idea of our method we recall that a key step in the original proof of the Pleijel theorem is to exploit the fact that an eigenfunction
u
in
\Omega
, when restricted to one of its nodal domains
U\subset\Omega
, satisfies a zero-Dirichlet boundary condition in
U
itself, thus allowing to apply the Faber-Krahn inequality and get a lower bound for the volume of
U
. While this is true for a Dirichlet-eigenfunction and for all its nodal domains, it is in general
false
for a Neumann eigenfunction and a nodal domain that touches the boundary. The reflection procedure in
[
69
]
is needed precisely to handle this issue, but requires smoothness of the boundary.
Instead our observation is that, by the very definition of nodal domain, an eigenfunction
u
(even in the Neumann case) has indeed zero-Dirichlet boundary conditions in
U
but
relative to the ambient domain
\Omega
, i.e. ignoring the portion of
\partial U
which is contained in
\partial\Omega
. The key point is then to view
\Omega
as a metric space in its own right and prove that it is regular enough to satisfy a version of the Faber-Krahn inequality, which then allows to carry out the rest of the argument. This is where the uniform condition will enter into play ensuring the required analytical properties of
\Omega
.

Even if we use mostly techniques coming from the metric setting, we also develop some purely-Euclidean technical tools that we believe could be useful to show other Pleijel-type results in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
under low boundary-regularity. In particular, we prove
a Faber-Krahn-tpye inequality and a Green’s formula for eigenfunctions of uniform domains (see Corollary
5.2
and Corollary
6.2
respectively). Both the results were previously available only assuming
C^{1,1}
-boundary.

We now comment further on the assumptions and the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The uniformity hypothesis on the domain guarantees the discreteness of the spectrum of the Neumann Laplacian, a fact even needed to state the theorem. Additionally we will make a crucial use of analytical properties of uniform domains in metric measure spaces, such as Sobolev extension properties, the validity of a Poincaré inequality and a Sobolev embedding (see Section
3
for more details).

The non-collapsed assumption is more technical in nature, and we leave for future investigations the general case of possibly collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces. Let us notice that in collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces the spectrum of the Laplacian can produce a singular and in some sense unexpected behaviour in the asymptotic regime (see the recent
[
38
]
), and thus this generalization seems non-trivial as we are going to further clarify in the next lines commenting the proof.

The main scheme of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is similar to the one usually employed in the smooth setting, e.g. in
[
69
,
86
,
78
,
56
,
45
,
17
]
. The two primary ingredients are the Weyl law and an almost-Euclidean Faber-Krahn inequality for small volumes.

The Weyl law has already been investigated in the setting of
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces (see
[
8
]
and
[
92
]
). In the non-collapsed case it takes the usual formulation

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\frac{N(\lambda)}{\lambda^{N/2}}=\frac{\omega_{N}}{(2\pi)^{N}}\mathcal{H}^{N}(\Omega)\,,

where
N(\lambda):=\sharp\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda^{{\mathcal{D}}}_{k}(\Omega)\leq\lambda\}
is the eigenvalues counting function and
\{\lambda^{{\mathcal{D}}}_{k}(\Omega)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the domain
\Omega
(see Definition
2.5
). We stress that the Weyl law in the Dirichlet case is sufficient for our purposes, even if in our main statement we consider both Dirichlet and Neumann eigenfunctions. This thanks to the elementary inequality
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
between Neumann and Dirichlet eigenvalues (see Lemma
2.8
).
We remark that suitable forms of the Weyl law on the whole space have been studied under slightly more general assumptions than non-collapsing, but the situation is more intricate and there exist compact
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces for which
N(\lambda)
is not asymptotic to
\lambda^{\beta}
for any
\beta\geq 0
. We refer to
[
8
,
38
]
for the details.

Concerning the almost-Euclidean Faber-Krahn inequality, it roughly states that the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of an open set
U\subset{\rm X}
, of sufficiently small volume, is bounded below by the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of the Euclidean ball having the same volume and up to a small error. This will be obtained starting from an almost-Euclidean isoperimetric inequality for small volumes (similar to the one obtained in
[
17
]
in the smooth setting) and rearrangement methods. In contrast with the proof in the smooth case, our situation requires to deal with a set
C
of possibly “bad” points, and to work with sets
U
that stay sufficiently far from
C
. We refer to Theorem
5.3
and Theorem
3.2
for the precise statements, and we suggest to compare them with
[
17
, Lemme 16,15]
. For both these results the non-collapsed assumption also plays a key role to ensure a more regular infinitesimal behaviour of the ambient space.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Calculus in metric measure spaces

The triple
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
will denote a metric measure space, where
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a complete and separable metric space and
\mathfrak{m}
is a non-negative Borel measure, finite on bounded sets. We will also always assume
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\mathfrak{m})=X
. For every set
A\subset{\rm X}
we will denote by
\overline{A}
its topological closure, by
A^{c}\coloneqq{\rm X}\setminus A
its complement and by
\partial A
its topological boundary. We denote by
B_{r}(x):=\{y\in{\rm X}:{\sf d}(x,y)<r\}
the ball of radius
r
and center
x
. The same set is also denoted by
B^{{\rm X}}_{r}(x)
whenever we want to emphasize the role of the space
{\rm X}
. By
{\sf d}(A,B):=\inf\{{\sf d}(x,y)\ :\ x\in A,y\in B\}
we denote the distance between two sets
A,B\subset{\rm X}
, so that
{\sf d}(A,\emptyset)=+\infty
. The open
\varepsilon
-enlargement of a set
A\subset{\rm X}
is denoted by
A^{\epsilon}:=\{x\in{\rm X}:{\sf d}(A,x)<\varepsilon\}
. Given a set
C\subset{\rm X}
, we denote by
{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$}:={\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C\times C}$}
the restriction of the distance to the set
C
. We will say that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is proper if closed and bounded subsets of
{\rm X}
are compact.

Given a metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
and a rectifiable curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to{\rm X}
, we denote by
l(\gamma)
its length (see e.g.
[
57
, Chapter 5.1]
). We say that
\gamma
joins
x\in{\rm X}
and
y\in{\rm X}
if
\gamma(a)=x
and
\gamma(b)=y.

Definition 2.1

(Nodal domain)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a metric space,
A\subset{\rm X}
be any subset and
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. The
nodal domains
of
f
(in
A
) are the connected components of
A\setminus\{x\in A\ :\ f(x)=0\}.

In the next result we recall some elementary properties of nodal sets.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a metric space,
A\subset{\rm X}
be any subset and
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. Let
U\subset A
be a nodal domain of
f.
Then either
f>0
or
f<0
in
U.
Moreover if
A
is open and
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is locally connected then
U
is also open.

Proof.

The set
U
is connected by definition, hence
f(U)\subset\mathbb{R}
is also connected and does not contain zero. It follows that
f(U)\subset(0,\infty)
or
f(U)\subset(-\infty,0).
For a proof that
({\rm X},{\sf d})
locally connected implies that
U
is open whenever
A
is open see e.g.
[
75
, Theorem 25.3]
.
∎

For every open set
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
we denote by
\mathsf{LIP}(\Omega)
,
\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\Omega)
and
\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega)
respectively the space of Lipschitz functions, locally Lipschitz functions and Lipschitz functions with compact support in
\Omega
. We also denote by
\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega)
the subset of
\mathsf{LIP}({\rm X})
of functions having support bounded and contained in
\Omega.

The slope
{\rm lip}(f)(x)
of a locally Lipschitz function
f\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\Omega)
at a point
x\in\Omega
is defined as

{\rm lip}(f)(x):=\varlimsup_{y\to x}\frac{|f(y)-f(x)|}{{\sf d}(y,x)},

taken to be 0 when
x
is isolated.
The slope satisfies the following Leibniz rule:
{\rm lip}(fg)\leq f{\rm lip}(g)+g{\rm lip}(f)
, for every
f,g\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(\Omega).

Given
p\in[1,\infty]
, we use the notation
L^{p}({\rm X},\mathfrak{m})
(resp.
L^{p}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X},\mathfrak{m})
) for the space of Lebesgue
p
-integrable (resp.
p
-locally integrable) real functions on
X
endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. For brevity, the same function space is also denoted by
L^{p}(\mathfrak{m})
. When
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
is an open set, we set
L^{p}(\Omega):=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})
where
\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}
is the restriction of the measure
\mathfrak{m}
to
\Omega
. For a function
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
we define its essential support
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u)
as the smallest closed set
C
such that
u=0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\Omega\setminus C.

The Cheeger energy
{\sf Ch}\colon L^{2}(\mathfrak{m})\to[0,\infty]
is defined as the convex and lower semicontinuous functional

{\sf Ch}(f):=\inf\Big\{\varliminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{{\rm X}}{\rm lip}^{2}(f_{n})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\colon(f_{n})\subset L^{2}(\mathfrak{m})\cap\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X}),\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathfrak{m})}=0\Big\}.

The Sobolev space
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
(or
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
for short) is then defined as
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m}):=\{{\sf Ch}<\infty\}
equipped with the norm
\|f\|^{2}_{\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})}:=\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathfrak{m})}^{2}+{\sf Ch}(f)
, which makes it a Banach space. This approach to the definition of Sobolev space was introduced in
[
5
]
, where it is also shown to be equivalent to the previous definitions given in
[
31
,
85
]
. For every
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
there exists a notion of modulus of the gradient called
minimal weak upper gradient
, minimal w.u.g. for short, denoted by
|Df|\in L^{2}(\mathfrak{m})
and satisfying

{\sf Ch}(f)=\int_{{\rm X}}|Df|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\,.

For every
f\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X})
we have
|Df|\leq{\rm lip}(f)

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e.. Moreover, the following calculus rules are satisfied (see e.g.
[
51
]
): for every
f,g\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
it holds

\begin{split}&\text{\emph{locality:} $|Df|=|Dg|$ $\mathfrak{m}$-a.e.\ in $\{f=g\}$,}\\
&\text{\emph{chain rule:} for every $\varphi\in\mathsf{LIP}(\mathbb{R})$ with $\varphi(0)=0$, $\varphi(f)\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})$ and $|D\varphi(f)|=|\varphi^{\prime}(f)||Df|,$}\\
&\text{\emph{Leibniz rule:} for every $\eta\in\mathsf{LIP}\cap L^{\infty}({\rm X})$, $\eta f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})$ and $|D(\eta f)|\leq|\eta||Df|+|D\eta||f|.$}\end{split}

(2.1)

Given
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
open we also define the following local Sobolev spaces

\begin{split}&\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\coloneqq\overline{\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega)}^{\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})},\\
&\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\coloneqq\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\ :\ f\eta\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X}),\,\forall\eta\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega),\,|Df|\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},\end{split}

where in the definition of
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
the minimal w.u.g.
|Df|\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is defined by

|Df|\coloneqq|D(f\eta_{n})|,\quad\text{$\mathfrak{m}$-a.e.\ in $\{\eta_{n}=1\}$},

(2.2)

with
\eta_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega)
is any sequence satisfying
\{\eta_{n}=1\}\uparrow\Omega
(there is no dependence on the chosen sequence, by the locality property of the minimal weak upper gradient). We endow
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
with the norm given by

\|f\|_{\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)}^{2}\coloneqq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\||Df|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

which makes it a Banach space. Observe that by the Leibniz rule we have that for every
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
it holds that
f\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and also
|Df|\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=|Df\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}|

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\Omega
(by the locality). Moreover, for every
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
we have
f=0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
X\setminus\Omega
and thus
\|f\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\|_{\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)}=\|f\|_{\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})}
, which shows that the map

T:\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega),\qquad T(f):=f\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}

(2.3)

is a linear isometry. For these reasons, with a little abuse of notation, sometimes we identify
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
with
T(\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega))\subset\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and think to
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
as an element of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

If we choose
\Omega={\rm X}
, then
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})=\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
with the same norm and minimal w.u.g., so the notation is consistent with the one given above.

Remark 2.3
.

It can be shown (see e.g.
[
6
, Remark 2.15]
) that
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
coincides, up to
\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. equivalence of functions, with the Newtonian Sobolev space
N^{1,2}(\Omega,{\sf d},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})
defined in
[

,

]
(see also
[

]
). The norms of the two spaces coincide as well thanks to the equivalence proved in

]
between the various notions of minimal weak upper gradients.

\blacksquare

Following
[
48
]
we say that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is
infinitesimally Hilbertian
if
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
is a Hilbert space or equivalently if the Cheeger energy satisfies the parallelogram identity:

{\rm Ch}(f+g)+{\rm Ch}(f-g)=2{\rm Ch}(f)+2{\rm Ch}(g),\quad\forall f,g\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X}).

(2.4)

If
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is infinitesimally Hilbertian, then
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space as well for every
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
open (see e.g.
[
27
, Remark A.3]
).
Moreover, we can give a notion of
scalar product between gradients
of functions
f,g\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
by setting

L^{1}(\Omega)\ni\nabla f\cdot\nabla g\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(|D(f+g)|^{2}-|Df|^{2}-|Dg|^{2}\right),

(2.5)

which is bilinear and satisfies

\begin{split}&|\nabla f\cdot\nabla g|\leq|Df||Dg|,\quad\mathfrak{m}\text{-a.e.,}\quad\forall\,f,g\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega),\\
&|\nabla f\cdot\nabla f|=|Df|^{2},\quad\mathfrak{m}\text{-a.e.,}\quad\forall\,f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega).\end{split}

(2.6)

Under the infinitesimally Hilbertian assumption we can define a notion of Laplacian via integration by parts.

Definition 2.4

(Neumann Laplacian)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure space and
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be open. We say that
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
belongs to the domain of the Neumann Laplacian, and we write
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{N}},\Omega)
, if there exists
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}hg\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla f\cdot\nabla g\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\,,\quad\forall\,g\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega).

(2.7)

If
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{N}},\Omega)
then the function
h
is unique and is denoted by
\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}f.

Definition 2.5

(Dirichlet Laplacian)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure space and
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be open. Then
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
belongs to the domain of the Dirichlet Laplacian, and we write
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{D}},\Omega)
, if there exists
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}hg\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla f\cdot\nabla g\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\,,\quad\forall\,g\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.8)

If
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{D}},\Omega)
then the function
h
is unique and is denoted by
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}f.

Since under the infinitesimally Hilbertian assumption Lipschitz and bounded functions are dense in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
(see
[
5
]
) we have
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}({\rm X})=\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
and so the Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacian coincide for
\Omega={\rm X}
. In this situation we simply write
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}=\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
and call it simply Laplacian operator and write
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta)
in place of
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{N}},{\rm X})
or
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{D}},{\rm X})
.

Definition 2.6

(Eigenfunctions)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure space and
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be open.
We say that a non-null
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{D}},\Omega)
(resp.
{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{N}},\Omega)
) is a Dirichlet (resp. Neumann)
eigenfunction of the Laplacian
in
\Omega
of

eigenvalue

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
if
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}f=-\lambda f
(resp.
\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}f=-\lambda f
). In the case
\Omega={\rm X}
we simply write that
f
is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian of eigenvalue
\lambda.

Remark 2.7

(Compatibility with Euclidean Laplacian)
.

If
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})=(\mathbb{R}^{N},|\cdot|,{\mathscr{L}}^{N})
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is open, the spaces
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
coincide with the usual ones, also with the same norms, as shown in
[
85
, Theorem 4.5]
(see also
[
19
, Theorem A.2 and Corollary A.4]
or
[
51
, Section 2.1.5]
). In particular, by polarization, the right-hand side of both (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) coincides with the integral of the scalar product between weak gradients in the classical sense. This shows that the definition of eigenfunction (and eigenvalue) of the Neumann or Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega
given above coincides with the usual one in the Euclidean case.

\blacksquare

For later use we observe that, whenever Lipschitz functions are dense in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
, for every bounded and open set
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
it holds that

\{f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})\ :\ {\sf d}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(f),{\rm X}\setminus\Omega)>0\}\subset\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.9)

Indeed there exist
\eta\in\mathsf{LIP}({\rm X})
such that
\eta=1
in
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(f)
and
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\eta)\subset\Omega
and a sequence
f_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}({\rm X})
with
f_{n}\to f
in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
, by density. Then
\eta f_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega)
and
\eta f_{n}\to f
in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
, which shows that
f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

We state in the next lemma an inequality between Neumann and Dirichlet eigenvalues that will play a key role in the sequel. Note that in the statement by
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
we mean, more precisely, that
T(\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
where
T
is defined in (
2.3
).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an infinitesimally Hilbertian metric measure space and let
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be open. Let us suppose that
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
with compact inclusion. Then
-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
has discrete spectrum, i.e. the eigenvalues form a diverging sequence (counted with multiplicity) that we denote by

0\leq\lambda_{1}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\rightarrow+\infty\,.

(2.10)

If moreover
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
with compact inclusion, then also

-\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}
has discrete spectrum denoted by

0=\lambda_{1}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\rightarrow+\infty\,,

(2.11)

and it holds

\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\,,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.12)

Proof.

Let us introduce the local Cheeger energies

\displaystyle\sf{\displaystyle Ch}_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\Omega}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow[0,+\infty],\ \qquad{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\Omega}(f):=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}|Df|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\qquad&\textrm{if}\ f=g\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\,\textrm{for some}\ g\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\,,\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\sf{\displaystyle Ch}_{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\Omega}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow[0,+\infty],\qquad{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\Omega}(f):=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}|Df|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\qquad&\textrm{if}\ f\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\,,\\
+\infty&\textrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

and notice that they define two Dirichlet forms, i.e. two densely defined, Markovian, closed, quadratic forms
[
24
,
46
]
. To check this, it is sufficient to recall the calculus rules given in (
2.1
) and, for the
L^{2}
-lower semicontinuity, the equivalent definition through relaxation (see
[
27
]
for all the details). We denote by
L_{{\mathcal{D}}}
(resp.
L_{{\mathcal{N}}}
) the infinitesimal generator of
{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\Omega}
(resp.
{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\Omega}
) with its associated domain
{\sf D}(L_{{\mathcal{D}}})
(resp.
{\sf D}(L_{{\mathcal{N}}})
). Notice that, by the very definition,
{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{N}},\Omega)={\sf D}(L_{{\mathcal{N}}})
with
\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}=L_{{\mathcal{N}}}
. Regarding the Dirichlet Laplacian, we have
f\in{\sf D}(\Delta_{\mathcal{D}},\Omega)
if and only if
f\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in{\sf D}(L_{{\mathcal{D}}})
with
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}f=L_{{\mathcal{D}}}(f\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})
. In particular,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
(resp.
-\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}
) if and only if it is and eigenvalue of
-L_{{\mathcal{D}}}
(resp.
-L_{{\mathcal{N}}}
).

From the classical theory of Dirichlet forms
[
24
,
46
]
we know that
-L_{\mathcal{D}}
and
-L_{\mathcal{N}}
are non-negative, densely defined, linear, self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Under these assumptions, it is well known (see e.g.
[
39
]
) that the compactness of the embedding of
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
(resp.
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
) in
L^{2}(\Omega)
implies the discreteness of the spectrum of
-L_{{\mathcal{D}}}
(resp.
-L_{{\mathcal{N}}}
) and thus of
-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
(resp.
-\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}
).

Since
T(\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega))\subset\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
as Hilbert spaces, we also know that whenever
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
with compact inclusion both the spectra are discrete.

We also have at disposal the variational characterization of the eigenvalues , see e.g.
[
39
, Theorems 4.5.1, 4.5.3]
. More precisely, defined

\displaystyle\lambda^{{\mathcal{N}}}(\Omega)[M]:=\sup\{{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\Omega}(f)\ :\ f\in M,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\},

(2.13)

\displaystyle\lambda^{{\mathcal{D}}}(\Omega)[M]:=\sup\{{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\Omega}(f)\ :\ f\in M,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1\},

we know that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)=\inf\{\lambda^{{\mathcal{N}}}(\Omega)[M]\ :\ M\subset\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega),\ \dim(M)=k\},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)=\inf\{\lambda^{{\mathcal{D}}}(\Omega)[M]\ :\ M\subset T(\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)),\ \dim(M)=k\},

The inequality (
2.12
) thus follows immediately from (
2.14
) since the infimum is taken on a larger set and
{\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\Omega}(f)={\sf Ch}_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\Omega}(f)
for every
f\in T(\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)).

∎

We will use in the sequel the notation introduced in the previous lemma, i.e. whenever
-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
(resp.
-\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}
) has discrete spectrum in
\Omega
we will denote by
\{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(resp.
\{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
) the sequence of its eigenvalues. In the case
\Omega={\rm X}
, assuming the discreteness of the spectrum of
-\Delta
, we will simply write
\lambda_{k}
in place of
\lambda_{k}^{{\mathcal{N}}}({\rm X})
.

For an arbitrary m.m.s.
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
and any
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
open subset we also introduce

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\frac{\int|Du|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int u^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}:u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega),\ u\not\equiv 0\right\}

(2.15)

and we call
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
the
first eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
\Omega
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions
.
Recalling the definition definition of
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
we have the following characterization of
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
:

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)=\inf\left\{\frac{\int|Du|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int u^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}:u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega),\ u\not\equiv 0\right\}.

(2.16)

Note that differently from
\lambda_{1}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
, which we defined only when the inclusion
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact,
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)
is always defined. Nevertheless, even if not needed, we stress that whenever
\lambda^{{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}(\Omega)
exists we do have

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)=\lambda^{{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}(\Omega),

as follows by (
2.16
) and (
2.14
).

2.2
Sets of finite perimeter

Let
f\in L^{1}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X},\mathfrak{m})
and let
U\subset{\rm X}
be open. Following
[
73
,
4
]
we define

|{\bf D}f|(U):=\inf\left\{\varliminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{U}{\rm lip}(f_{n})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\ :\ f_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(U),\ f_{n}\to f\ \textrm{in}\ L^{1}_{\mathsf{loc}}(U,\mathfrak{m})\right\}\,,

(2.17)

and we say that
f
is of locally bounded variation if
|{\bf D}f|(U)<+\infty
for every
U
open and bounded. We also set

|{\bf D}f|(A)\coloneqq\inf\{|{\bf D}f|(U)\ :\ U\subset{\rm X}\text{ open, }A\subset U\},\quad\forall\,\text{$A\subset{\rm X}$ Borel}

(note that this coincides with (
2.17
) if
A
is open).
For every Borel set
E\subset{\rm X}
and
A\subset{\rm X}
Borel we define
{\rm{Per}}(E,A):=|{\bf D}\chi_{E}|(A)<+\infty
, where
\chi_{E}:X\to\{0,1\}
denotes the characteristic function of
E
. We say that
E
is of finite perimeter if
{\rm{Per}}(E)\coloneqq{\rm{Per}}(E,{\rm X})<+\infty
.

When
f
is of locally bounded variation (respectively,
E
is a set of finite perimeter), the map
A\mapsto|{\bf D}f|(A)
(respectively,
A\mapsto{\rm{Per}}(E,A)
) defines a Borel measure (see
[
4
,
73
]
). Every
f\in\mathsf{LIP}({\rm X})
is of locally bounded variation and
|{\bf D}f|\leq{\rm lip}(f)\mathfrak{m}
(see
[
4
, Remark 5.1]
).

From the definitions it immediately follows that
E
is of finite perimeter if and only if
E^{c}
is of finite perimeter, in which case
{\rm{Per}}(E,\cdot)={\rm{Per}}(E^{c},\cdot)
holds.

In the sequel we will take advantage of the following coarea-type inequality.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space and fix
x\in{\rm X}.
Then for a.e.
r>0
the ball
B_{r}(x)
has finite perimeter and for every Borel set
A\subset{\rm X}
it holds

\int_{0}^{R}{\rm{Per}}(B_{r}(x),A)\,{\mathrm{d}}r\leq\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x)\cap A),\quad\forall\,R>0.

(2.18)

Proof.

Since the function
{\sf d}_{x}(\cdot)\coloneqq{\sf d}(x,\cdot)
is 1-Lipschitz, it is of locally bounded variation and
|{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}|\leq{\rm lip}({\sf d}_{x})\mathfrak{m}\leq\mathfrak{m}.
Then by the coarea formula (see Proposition 4.2 in
[
73
]
) we get directly that
B_{r}(x)=\{{\sf d}_{x}(\cdot)<r\}
has finite perimeter for a.e.
r>0
and that

\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\rm{Per}}(B_{r}(x),E){\mathrm{d}}r=|{{\bf D}{\sf d}_{x}}|(E)\leq\mathfrak{m}(E),\quad\forall\,\,E\subset{\rm X}\text{ Borel}.

Then (
2.18
) follows taking
E\coloneqq B_{R}(x)\cap A
and observing that by the very definition in (
2.17
) it holds
{\rm{Per}}(B_{r}(x),B_{R}(x))=0
for every
r>R
.
∎

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space. Given a Borel set
E\subset{\rm X}
we define the upper and lower densities at
x
as

\overline{D}(E,x)\coloneqq\varlimsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))},\quad\underline{D}(E,x)\coloneqq\varliminf_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}.

Clearly, if
x\in{\rm X}
is such that
\overline{D}(E,x)>0
, by definition of limit superior we have
x\in\overline{E}
(since every open ball with center
x
must intersect
E
).

The
essential boundary
and the
essential interior
are given respectively by

\displaystyle\partial^{e}E\coloneqq\{x\in{\rm X}\ :\ \overline{D}(E,x)>0,\,\overline{D}(E^{c},x)>0\},

\displaystyle E^{(1)}\coloneqq\{x\in{\rm X}\ :\ \overline{D}(E,x)=\underline{D}(E,x)=1\},

which are both Borel sets. As a direct consequence of the definition of these sets, notice that if
E\subset F
, then
E^{(1)}\subset F^{(1)}
. Moreover,
\partial^{e}E=\partial^{e}(E^{c})
.

We collect in the next lemma all the elementary facts that we will need about the essential boundary and the essential interior.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a metric space and
E,F\subset{\rm X}
be Borel sets. We have the following:

(i)

If
E
is open,
E\subset E^{(1)}
.

(ii)

\partial^{e}E\subset\partial E
.

(iii)

(E\cap F)^{(1)}\subset E^{(1)}\cap F^{(1)}
.

(iv)

(E^{c})^{(1)}\subset(E^{(1)})^{c}
. In particular, if
E
is open then
(E^{c})^{(1)}\subset E^{c}
.

(v)

If
E
and
F
are disjoint, then also
E^{(1)}
and
F^{(1)}
are disjoint.

(vi)

\partial^{e}(E\cap F)\cup\partial^{e}(E\cup F)\subset\partial^{e}E\cup\partial^{e}F
.

Proof.

(i)

Let
x\in E
. Since
E
is open,
B_{r}(x)\subset E
for sufficiently small
r>0
, thus
\underline{D}(E,x)=1
and
x\in E^{(1)}
.

(ii)

As we have already observed, if
x\in\partial^{e}E
it holds
x\in\overline{E}
and
x\in\overline{E^{c}}
, thus
x\in\partial E
.

(iii)

The result is a direct consequence of the fact that
\underline{D}(E\cap F,x)\leq\underline{D}(E,x)\leq 1
for every
x\in{\rm X}
.

(iv)

Let
x\in(E^{c})^{(1)}
, i.e.
\underline{D}(E^{c},x)=1
. In particular,

\frac{2}{3}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))<\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E^{c})=\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))-\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)

for sufficiently small
r>0
. Thus
\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap E)<\frac{1}{3}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))
for
r>0
small enough, which implies
x\notin E^{(1)}
. The second conclusion follows from what we have just proven and point
({i})
.

(v)

The assumption
E\cap F=\emptyset
is equivalent to
E\subset F^{c}
. Passing to the essential interior it holds
E^{(1)}\subset(F^{c})^{(1)}
and using
({iv})
one deduces that
E^{(1)}\subset(F^{(1)})^{c}
which gives the desired conclusion.

(vi)

This is proven e.g. in
[
23
, Prop. 1.16]
(note that the doubling assumption on
\mathfrak{m}
is not used in that statement).

∎

We conclude this part with the following elementary and well known result. Since we could not find it stated exactly in this form in the literature, we include a proof.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space and let
C\subset{\rm X}
be closed. Then for every
E\subset C
Borel satisfying
{\sf d}(E,{\rm X}\setminus C)>0
it holds

{\rm{Per}}(E)={\rm{Per}}_{C}(E),

where
{\rm{Per}}_{C}(E)
denotes the perimeter of
E
computed in the metric measure space
(C,{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$}).

Proof.

First observe that for every
f\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X})
it holds
f\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(C)
and
{\rm lip}_{C}(f)\leq{\rm lip}(f)
, where
{\rm lip}_{C}(f)
denotes the slope of
f
computed in the metric space
(C,{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$})
. This and the definitions implies
{\rm{Per}}_{C}(E)\leq{\rm{Per}}(E).
For the other inequality it is sufficient to find a sequence
f_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(C,{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$})
such that
{\sf d}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(f_{n}),{\rm X}\setminus C)>0
,
f_{n}\to{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{E}
in
L^{1}(\mathfrak{m})
and
\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\to{\rm{Per}}_{C}(E)
. Indeed extending
f_{n}
by zero to the whole
{\rm X}
we have
\int_{\rm X}{\rm lip}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}
, so that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\leq\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{X}{\rm lip}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}={\rm{Per}}_{C}(E).

To produce such sequence we consider a sequence
g_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(C,{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$})
such that
g_{n}\to{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{E}
in
L^{1}(C,\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$})
and
\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(g_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\to{\rm{Per}}_{C}(E)
, which exists by definition. Then we take any
\eta\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(C)
satisfying
{\sf d}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\eta),{\rm X}\setminus C)>0
,
\eta=1
in a neighbourhood of
E
,
0\leq\eta\leq 1
and set
f_{n}\coloneqq\eta g_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(C,{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$}).
Clearly
f_{n}\to{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{E}
in
L^{1}(C;\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$})
. Moreover by the Leibniz rule for the slope
{\rm lip}_{C}(f_{n})\leq g_{n}{\rm lip}_{C}(\eta)+{\rm lip}_{C}(g_{n})
. In addition
{\rm lip}_{C}(\eta)=0
in
E
. Therefore

\displaystyle{\rm{Per}}_{C}(E)\leq\varliminf_{n}\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\leq\varlimsup_{n}\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(f_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle\leq\varlimsup_{n}\int_{C}{\rm lip}_{C}(g_{n}){\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}+{\rm lip}_{C}(\eta)\int_{{\rm X}\setminus E}g_{n}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}={\rm{Per}}_{C}(E),

where the second term vanishes because
g_{n}\to{\raise 1.29167pt\hbox{$\chi$}}_{E}
in
L^{1}(C;\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{C}$})
.
∎

2.3
PI spaces

Most of the arguments along the note will be carried out in the general setting of locally doubling m.m. spaces supporting a Poincaré inequality, also called PI spaces. We refer to
[
19
,
57
]
and references therein for a thorough introduction on this topic and recall here only the properties of these spaces that will be used in this note.

Definition 2.12

(PI space)
.

A metric measure space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is said to be a
PI space
if:

i)

it is
uniformly locally doubling
, i.e. if there exists a function
C_{D}\,:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

\mathfrak{m}\big(B_{2r}(x)\big)\leq C_{D}(R)\,\mathfrak{m}\big(B_{r}(x)\big)\,,\quad\text{for every }0<r<R\text{ and }x\in{\rm X},

ii)

supports a
weak local
(1,1)
-Poincaré inequality
, i.e there exists a constant
\lambda\geq 1
and a function
C_{P}\,:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that for any
f\in\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}(X)
it holds

\fint_{B_{r}(x)}\bigg|f-\fint_{B_{r}(x)}f\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\bigg|\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\leq C_{P}({R})\,r\fint_{B_{\lambda r}(x)}{\rm lip}(f)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\,,\quad\text{for every }0<r<R\text{ and }x\in{\rm X}.

Observe that the uniformly locally doubling assumption implies that PI spaces are proper.
Additionally PI spaces are connected and locally connected (see e.g.
[
19

).

Lemma 2.13

(Approximation with non-vanishing slope)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a bounded PI space. Then for every open subset
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
and any non-negative
u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega)
there exists a sequence of non-negative
u_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega)
satisfying
{\rm lip}(u_{n})\neq 0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\{u_{n}>0\}
and such that
u_{n}\to u
in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X}).

Proof.

Since
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is a bounded PI space it admits a geodesic distance
\tilde{\sf d}
that is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
{\sf d},
i.e.
L^{-1}\tilde{\sf d}\leq{\sf d}\leq L\tilde{\sf d}
for some constant
L\geq 1
(see e.g.
[
57
, Corollary 8.3.16]
).
Hence we can apply
[
74
, Lemma 3.6]
to deduce that the conclusion of the lemma holds in the m.m.s.
({\rm X},\tilde{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
. However
u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega;{\sf d})
if and only if
\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega;\tilde{\sf d})
, with
{\rm lip}(u)\geq L^{-1}\tilde{\rm lip}(u)
, where
\tilde{\rm lip}(\cdot)
denotes the slope computed in the metric space
({\rm X},\tilde{\sf d})
. Hence if
\tilde{\rm lip}(u)\neq 0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\{u>0\}
, then
{\rm lip}(u)\neq 0
in
\{u>0\}

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e.. Moreover, since
\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X},{\sf d})=\mathsf{LIP}_{\mathsf{loc}}({\rm X},\tilde{\sf d})
and
{\rm lip}(\cdot)\leq L\tilde{\rm lip}(\cdot)
we have that
\|u\|_{\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})}\leq L\|u\|_{\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X},\tilde{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})}
for all
u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}({\rm X},{\sf d})
. Therefore the conclusion holds also for the m.m.s.
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m}).

∎

The above lemma allows to give the following characterization of

(Characterization of

(2.19)

Proof.

For every for every

, setting

\mathfrak{m}

Proposition 2.18

(Isoperimetric inequality for small volumes)

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))}\geq c\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{s},\quad\forall\,x\in{\rm X},\quad\forall\,0<r<R.

Then there exist constants

By

2.4
Pólya-Szegő inequality in metric measure spaces

Here we report a version of the Pólya-Szegő rearrangement inequality for metric measure spaces, following
[
74
,
76
]
.
The main difference with the classical version in the Euclidean space
[
80
]
is that, even if the initial function lives in a metric space, the symmetrization will be defined in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The result is a generalization of the Pólya-Szegő inequality introduced in
[
17
]
in the case of Riemannian manifolds.

Definition 2.20

(Distribution function)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space,
\Omega\subseteq{\rm X}
be an open set with
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
and
u:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
be a non-negative Borel function. We define
\mu:[0,+\infty)\to[0,\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)]
, the distribution function of
u
, as

\mu(t):=\mathfrak{m}(\{u>t\}).

(2.25)

For
u
and
\mu
as above, we let
u^{\#}
be the generalized inverse of
\mu
, defined by

u^{\#}(s):=\begin{cases}{\rm ess}\sup u&\text{if $s=0$},\\
\inf\left\{t:\mu(t)<s\right\}&\text{if $s>0$}.\end{cases}

It can be checked that
u^{\#}
is non-increasing and left-continuous.

Next, we define the
Euclidean monotone rearrangement
into the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{N},|\cdot|,{\mathscr{L}}^{N})
, where
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}
is the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. From now on, we denote by
\omega_{N}\coloneqq{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(B_{1}(0))
the Lebesgue measure of the unit ball in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Definition 2.21

(Euclidean monotone rearrangement)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space and
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be open with
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)<+\infty
and
N\in\mathbb{N}.

For any Borel function
u\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we define
\Omega^{*}:=B_{r}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, choosing
r>0
so that
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(B_{r}(0))=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
(i.e.
r^{N}=\omega_{N}^{-1}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
) and the monotone rearrangement
u^{*}_{N}:\Omega^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
by

u^{*}_{N}(x):=u^{\#}({\mathscr{L}}^{N}(B_{|x|}(0)))=u^{\#}(\omega_{N}|x|^{N}),\qquad\forall x\in\Omega^{*}.

In particular
u
and
u_{N}^{*}
are equimeasurable, i.e.
\mathfrak{m}(\{u>t\})={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\{u^{*}_{N}>t\})
for all
t>0.

In the sequel, whenever we fix
\Omega
and
u\colon\Omega\to[0,\infty)
, the set
\Omega^{*}
and the rearrangement
u^{*}_{N}
are automatically defined as above.
Observe also that, given
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, its monotone rearrangement must be defined by fixing a Borel representative of
u
. However, this choice does not affect the outcome object
u^{*}_{N}
, as clearly the distribution function
\mu(t)
of
u
is independent of the representative.

The following result is essentially contained in
[
76
]
, see in particular
[
76
, Remark 3.7]
, (see also
[
74
]
for a similar result), since the only difference is that here the rearrangement is defined in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
instead that on an interval. Nevertheless we include a short argument outlining the main points of the proof.

Theorem 2.22

(Euclidean Pólya-Szegő inequality)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a bounded PI space,
\Omega\subsetneq{\rm X}
be open and fix
N\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}.
Suppose there exists a constant
\tilde{C}>0
such that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq\tilde{C}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}\,,\quad\forall\,E\subset\Omega\text{ Borel.}

(2.26)

Then:

i)

For every
u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega)
non-negative,
u\not\equiv 0
, with
{\rm lip}(u)\neq 0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\{u>0\}
, then
u^{*}_{N}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega^{*})
and it holds

\int_{\{u\leq s\}}|Du|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\geq\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{{\rm{Per}}(\{u>t\})}{N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|Du_{N}^{*}|{\mathrm{d}}{\rm{Per}}(\{u^{*}_{N}>t\})\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\quad\forall s\in(0,\max u].

(2.27)

ii)

The Euclidean-rearrangement maps
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
to
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega^{*})
and

\int_{\Omega}|{Du}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\geq\Big(\frac{\tilde{C}}{N\omega_{N}^{1/N}}\Big)^{2}\int_{\Omega^{*}}|{Du_{N}^{*}}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N},\quad\forall\,u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.28)

Proof.

It is enough to prove
i)
, since
ii)
then follows by approximation with Lipschitz functions using Lemma
2.13
as in
[
76
, Theorem 3.6]
(see also
[
74
]
).

We fix
u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega)
,
u\not\equiv 0
, with
{\rm lip}(u)\neq 0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e.
\{u>0\}
. Set
M\coloneqq\sup u.

Under these assumptions
\mu
is strictly monotone, absolutely continuous (hence differentiable almost everywhere) and

\int_{\{u\leq s\}}|Du|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\geq\int_{0}^{s}\frac{{\rm{Per}}(\{u>t\})^{2}}{-\mu^{\prime}(t)}\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\quad\forall s\in(0,M].

(2.29)

This can be seen arguing exactly as in the proof of
[
74
, Prop. 3.12 and (3.23)]
(see also
[
76
]
), recalling also that
|{\bf D}u|\leq{\rm lip}(u)\mathfrak{m}=|Du|\mathfrak{m}
(see (
2.20
)). Next we claim that
u^{*}_{N}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega^{*}).
Recall that by definition
\Omega^{*}=B_{r}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
r>0
satisfies
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(B_{r}(0))=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega).
From the definitions
u^{*}_{N}(x)=\tilde{u}^{*}_{N}(|x|)
, where
\tilde{u}^{*}_{N}:[0,r]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the rearrangement into the space
([0,\infty),|.|,N\omega_{N}t^{N-1}{\mathrm{d}}t)
as defined in
[
76
, Definition 3.1]
. Then the fact that
u^{*}_{N}\in\mathsf{LIP}(\Omega^{*})
follows directly from
\tilde{u}^{*}_{N}\in\mathsf{LIP}[0,r]
which is proved in
[
76
, Prop. 3.4]
under the same assumptions on
u
and
\Omega
. Finally
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u^{*}_{N})\subset\Omega^{*}
. Indeed
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u)\subsetneq\Omega
, otherwise
\Omega
would be closed and would coincide with
{\rm X}
(as
{\rm X}
is connected). This implies that
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\{u_{N}^{*}>t\})\leq\mathfrak{m}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u))<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega^{*}),
for all
t>0,
because
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega\setminus\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u))>0
, as non-empty open sets in
{\rm X}
have positive measure. Since
u_{N}^{*}
is a radial function centered at the origin this shows
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u^{*}_{N})\subset\Omega
.
Next we observe that
\tilde{u}_{N}^{*}
is strictly decreasing in
(0,\mathfrak{m}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u)))
(since
\mu(t)
is continuous) and in particular
\{u^{*}_{N}>t\}=B_{r_{t}}(0)
(and
\{u^{*}_{N}=t\}=\partial B_{r_{t}}(0)
) for some
r_{t}\in[0,\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)]
, for every
t\in(0,M)
. Note that
r_{t}
can be computed explicitly to be
r_{t}=(\omega_{N}^{-1}\mu(t))^{1/N}
, which also shows that
(0,M)\ni t\mapsto r_{t}
is a strictly monotone and locally absolutely continuous map. In particular

\mathcal{H}^{N}(\partial B_{r_{t}}(0))=N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}.

(2.30)

Combining these observations with the expression for the derivative of
\mu
given in
[
74
, Lemma 3.10]
(see also
[
76
, Lemma 3.5]
) we have

-\mu^{\prime}(t)=\int_{\partial B_{r_{t}}(0)}({\rm lip}(u_{N}^{*}))^{-1}{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{N-1}=\frac{N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}}{{\rm lip}(\tilde{u}_{N}^{*})(r_{t})}\,\quad\text{for a.e.\ $t\in(0,M)$},

(2.31)

where we have used (
2.30
) and that
{\rm lip}(u_{N}^{*})(x)={\rm lip}(\tilde{u}_{N}^{*})(|x|)
which easily follows from the identity
u^{*}_{N}(x)=\tilde{u}^{*}_{N}(|x|)
.
Plugging the above in (
2.29
) we reach

\displaystyle\int_{\{u\leq s\}}|Du|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{s}\frac{{\rm{Per}}(\{u>t\})^{2}{\rm lip}(\tilde{u}_{N}^{*})(r_{t})}{N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}}\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{{\rm{Per}}(\{u>t\})}{N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}}\right)^{2}{\rm lip}(\tilde{u}_{N}^{*})(r_{t})N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t))^{\frac{N-1}{N}}\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\left(\frac{{\rm{Per}}(\{u>t\})}{N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mu(t)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}}\right)^{2}\int_{\partial B_{r_{t}}(0)}{\rm lip}(u_{N}^{*}){\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,,

where for the last step we argue as in (
2.31
). This concludes the proof.
∎

2.5
RCD spaces

For brevity we do not recall the definition of
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
spaces (with
N\in[1,\infty)
and
K\in\mathbb{R}
) since it will not be directly used in this note, instead we recall here all the properties of these spaces that will be needed. For further details on the definition and on the theory of metric measure spaces with synthetic Ricci curvature lower bound we refer to the surveys
[
3
,
49
]
and references therein.

First recall that every
\mathrm{RCD}
space is infinitesimally Hilbertian from the very definition.
In every
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space the
Bishop-Gromov inequality
holds
[
87
]
, that is

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{v_{K,N}(r)}\geq\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))}{v_{K,N}(R)},\quad\forall x\in{\rm X},\,\forall 0<r<R,

(2.32)

where the quantities
v_{K,N}(r)
coincides, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, with the volume of the ball of radius
r
in the model space of curvature
K
and dimension
N
. For the definition of
v_{K,N}
for non integer
N
see
[
87
]
, however in the results of this note only the case
N\in\mathbb{N}
will be relevant.

As a consequence of (
2.32
) we obtain that for every
R_{0}>0
,
N<+\infty
and
K\in\mathbb{R}
there exists a constant
C_{R_{0},K,N}
such that for every
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
it holds:

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))}\geq C_{R_{0},K,N}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{N},\quad\forall x\in{\rm X},\,\forall 0<r<R\leq R_{0}.

(2.33)

Taking
R=2r
this shows also that every
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space with
N<+\infty
is uniformly locally doubling (recall Definition
2.12
).
It is also proved in
[
82
,
81
]
that every
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space supports also a weak local (1,1)-Poincaré inequality. Combining the last two observations we conclude that every
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space with
N<+\infty
is an infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space.

We recall the following embedding result.

Proposition 2.23

(
[
50
, Theorem 6.3,
ii)
]
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space and let
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be bounded. Then the inclusion
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathfrak{m})
is compact.

Given an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
we define the
Bishop-Gromov
density function
\theta_{N}:{\rm X}\to(0,+\infty]
by

\theta_{N}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{\omega_{N}r^{N}}=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{v_{N,K}(r)}\,,

(2.34)

where the existence of the limits is ensured by (
2.32
) (see
[
41
, Def. 1.9]
). As shown in
[
41
, Lemma 2.2]
the function
\theta_{N}
is lower-semicontinuous in
{\rm X}.

A key property that we will need is the validity of a local almost-Euclidean isoperimetric inequality. We will use the following version essentially proved in
[
76
]
(see also
[
29
,
11
]
for similar results in the setting of
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces and
[
17
]
for the Riemannian setting).

Theorem 2.24

(Local almost-Euclidean isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space for some
N\in(1,\infty),K\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for every
x\in{\rm X}
with
\theta_{N}(x)<+\infty
and every
\varepsilon\in(0,\theta_{N}(x))
there exists
\rho=\rho(\varepsilon,x,N)
such that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}(\theta_{N}(x)-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{N}}(1-\varepsilon),\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\rho}(x)\text{ Borel.}

(2.35)

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the statement with
\varepsilon\in(0,\theta_{N}(x)/2\wedge 1/2)
. From
[
76
, Theorem 3.9]
there exists
\bar{R}=\bar{R}(\varepsilon,K,N)
such that for every
x\in{\rm X}
,
R\in(0,\bar{R}]
it holds

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\theta_{N,R}(x)^{\frac{1}{N}}(1-(2C^{1/N}_{\varepsilon,R}(x)+1)\varepsilon-\varepsilon),\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\varepsilon R}(x),

where
\theta_{N,\rho}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{\rho}(x))}{\omega_{N}\rho^{N}}
and
C_{\varepsilon,R}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\theta_{N,\varepsilon R}(x)}{\theta_{N,R}(x)}.
Since
\theta_{N}(x)=\lim_{\rho\to 0}\theta_{N,\rho}(x)<+\infty
there exists
\bar{r}=\bar{r}(x,\varepsilon)
so that
\theta_{N,\rho}(x)\in(\theta_{N}(x)-\varepsilon,\theta_{N}(x)+\varepsilon)
for all
\rho\leq\bar{r}
. Moreover since
\varepsilon<\theta_{N}(x)/2
we have that for every
R\leq\bar{r}
it holds
C_{\varepsilon,R}(x)\leq\frac{\frac{3}{2}\theta_{N}(x)}{\frac{1}{2}\theta_{N}(x)}=3.
Hence choosing
\rho=\rho(x,\varepsilon,K,N)\coloneqq\varepsilon(\bar{r}(\varepsilon,x)\wedge\bar{R}(\varepsilon,K,N))
, we have that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}(\theta_{N}(x)-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{N}}(1-8\varepsilon),\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\rho}(x),

from which the conclusion follows.
∎

Next we introduce the subclass of non-collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space.

Definition 2.25

(
[
41
]
)
.

An
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is said to be
non-collapsed
if
\mathfrak{m}=\mathcal{H}^{N}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{N}
denotes the
N
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
({\rm X},{\sf d})
.

After the works
[
60
,
26
]
this definition is known to be equivalent to one given in
[
67
]
.
As showed in
[
41
, Theorem 1.12]
if
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is a non-collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)

N\in\mathbb{N}.
Moreover by
[
41
, Corollary 1.7]
(see also
[
8
, Theorem 1.4]
) it holds that

\begin{split}&\theta_{N}(x)=1,\quad\mathfrak{m}\text{-a.e.\ $x\in{\rm X}$,}\\
&\theta_{N}(x)\leq 1,\quad\forall x\in{\rm X}.\end{split}

(2.36)

Remark 2.26

(Consistency with the smooth setting)
.

It is worth to recall that
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces are compatible with the smooth setting in the following sense.
Any
N
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with Ricci curvature bounded below by a number
K\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
{\rm Ric}_{g}\geq Kg
, endowed with the Riemannian distance and volume measure is a non-collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)

,

. In particular the metric measure space

, where

\blacksquare

We conclude recalling the validity of the Weyl law in non-collapsed setting proved in
[
8
,
92
]
.

Theorem 2.27

(Weyl law in
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space and
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be open and bounded. Then

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{k}{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)^{N/2}}=\frac{\omega_{N}}{(2\pi)^{N}}\mathcal{H}^{N}(\Omega),

(2.37)

where
\{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the spectrum of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega
defined in (
2.10
).

Proof.

By the results in
[
8
,
92
]
it holds that

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\frac{N(\lambda)}{\lambda^{N/2}}=\frac{\omega_{N}}{(2\pi)^{N}}\mathcal{H}^{N}(\Omega),

(2.38)

where
N(\lambda)\coloneqq\#\{k\in\mathbb{N}\ :\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda\}
. This implies (
2.37
). To see this, set
N^{-}(\lambda)\coloneqq\#\{k\in\mathbb{N}\ :\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)<\lambda\}
, and observe

N(\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)-1)\leq N^{-}(\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega))\leq k\leq N(\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)),\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that in
[
92
]
formula (
2.38
) is stated with the further assumption that
\text{diam}(\Omega)<\text{diam}({\rm X})
(when
\Omega\neq{\rm X}
), however this assumption is needed in
[
92
]
only to ensure the discreteness of the spectrum, for which we know by Proposition
2.23
that the boundedness of
\Omega
is sufficient.
∎

3
Sobolev spaces and Neumann eigenfunctions in uniform domains

Our method to deal with Neumann eigenfunctions in domains with irregular boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(or in more abstract
\mathrm{RCD}
spaces) will be to translate the problem to a global one, by viewing the domain as a metric measure space. The idea is that if the boundary satisfies an appropriate regularity condition, then the resulting m.m. space is a PI space and in particular has good analytic properties, like the isoperimetric inequality and embedding theorems (recall Section
2.3
). We stress that the possibility of using abstract metric spaces to deal with Neumann and mixed boundary value problems in irregular domains of the Euclidean space was noted before (see e.g.
[
19
, Page 33]
).

The key notion that we will use is the one of
uniform domain
that we now introduce.

Definition 3.1

(Uniform domains)
.

A bounded open subset
\Omega
of a metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is called a
uniform domain
if there exists a constant
C>1
such that every pair of points
x,y\in\Omega
can be joined by a rectifiable curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\Omega
such that
l(\gamma)\leq C{\sf d}(x,y)
and

{\sf d}(\gamma(t),\partial\Omega)\geq C^{-1}\min\left(l(\gamma\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{[0,t]}$}),l(\gamma\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{[t,1]}$})\right),\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

Uniform domains were introduced in
[
72
]
and
[
65
]
(see also
[
89
]
) and are central in the theory of BV and Sobolev extension domains (see
[
65
,
47
,
58
,
59
,
22
,
68
]
).
Uniform domains are also equivalent to one-sided non tangentially accessible (1-sided NTA) domains
(see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 2.15]
or the Appendix in
[
55
]
).
They include Lipschitz domains, but also more irregular domains such as the quasi-disks, i.e. images of the unit ball under a global quasi-conformal maps (see
[
72
, Theorem 2.15]
,
[
47
]
,
[
63
, Section 3]
or also
[
88
, Remarks 2.1]
). In particular the interior of the Koch snowflake is an example of uniform domain. It has also been proved recently that in every doubling quasi-convex metric space (in particular any bounded
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space) any bounded open set can be approximated from inside and outside by uniform domains, see
[
83
]
for the precise statement.

It easily follows from the definition that every uniform domain is both connected and locally connected (see e.g.
[
71
]
).

The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem. It says that a uniform domain in a non-collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space (and in particular in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
) when viewed as a m.m. space admits an almost Euclidean isoperimetric inequality near almost every point (point
(ii)
) and the eigenvalues of the Laplacian satisfy a weak version of the Weyl law (point
(iii)
). Item
(i)
is a technical condition that we will need to apply the Faber-Krahn inequality in Proposition
5.1
. We remark that, differently from the preceding sections, in the following statement we will denote by
{\rm Y}
the ambient space, while the notation
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is reserved to the metric measure space associated to the closure of the domain
\Omega\subset{\rm Y}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
({\rm Y},\tilde{{\sf d}},\mathcal{H}^{N})
be an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space and let
\Omega\subset{\rm Y}
be a uniform domain. Then the metric measure space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})\coloneqq(\overline{\Omega},\tilde{{\sf d}}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathcal{H}^{N}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is an infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space and satisfies the following properties:

(i)

there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B^{{\rm X}}_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B^{{\rm X}}_{R}(x))}\geq c\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{N},\quad\forall\,x\in{\rm X},\,\forall\,0<r<R.

(ii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a closed set
C_{\varepsilon}\subset{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(C_{\varepsilon})=0
such that for every
x\in{\rm X}\setminus C_{\varepsilon}
there exists a constant
\rho=\rho(x,N,\varepsilon)>0
satisfying

{\rm{Per}}_{\rm X}(E)\geq(1-\varepsilon)N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}},\quad\forall\,E\subset B^{\rm X}_{\rho}(x)\,\text{ Borel,}

(3.1)

where
{\rm{Per}}_{\rm X}
and
B^{\rm X}_{\rho}(x)
are respectively the perimeter and the metric ball in the space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m}).

(iii)

denoted by
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
the spectrum of the Laplacian in
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
(recall (
2.21
)) it holds
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{{\mathcal{N}}}(\Omega)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{N/2}}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega_{N}\mathfrak{m}({\rm X})}.

(3.2)

Remark 3.3

(The ‘bad’ set
C_{\varepsilon}
)
.

If the ambient space
({\rm Y},\tilde{{\sf d}},\mathcal{H}^{N})
is the Euclidean space then item
ii)
in Theorem
3.2
is immediate by taking
C_{\varepsilon}=\partial\Omega
and by the isoperimetric inequality (indeed
\partial\Omega
is negligible as we will show in Lemma
3.6
). More generally if
({\rm Y},\tilde{{\sf d}},\mathcal{H}^{N})
is a Riemannian manifold with
\tilde{{\sf d}}
the geodesic distance then item
ii)
follows taking again
C_{\varepsilon}=\partial\Omega
and applying the local almost-Euclidean isoperimetric inequality in
[
17
, Appendice C]
.
In the general case, as will be shown in the proof, the set
C_{\varepsilon}
in Theorem
3.2
can be taken to be

C_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{x\in\overline{\Omega}\ :\ \theta_{N}(x)\leq 1-\varepsilon\}\cup\partial\Omega,

where
\theta_{N}:{\rm X}\to(0,+\infty]
is the Bishop-Gromov density function defined in (
2.34
). In other words the set
C_{\varepsilon}
contains
\partial\Omega
plus a subset of the singular points, the latter being the points where
\theta_{N}<1
. Note that
\theta_{N}\equiv 1
if
({\rm Y},\tilde{{\sf d}},\mathcal{H}^{N})
is a Riemannian manifold, so that
C_{\varepsilon}
reduces to
\partial\Omega,
in accordance to what we said above.

\blacksquare

We start with some basic properties of uniform domains.

Definition 3.4

(Corkscrew-condition)
.

A bounded open subset
\Omega
of a metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
satisfies the corkscrew-condition if there exists a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that
for every point
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and all
0<r\leq\text{diam}(\Omega)
, the set
\Omega\cap B_{r}(x)
contains a ball of radius
\varepsilon r.

In Definition
3.4
it is equivalent to require only
x\in\partial\Omega.

For the proof of the following well known fact see e.g.
[
22
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Every uniform domain satisfies the corkscrew-condition.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a uniformly locally doubling metric measure space and let
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
satisfy the corkscrew-condition. Then
\mathfrak{m}(\partial\Omega)=0.
In particular this holds if
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
is a uniform domain.

Proof.

Thanks to the corkscrew-condition there exists a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that for every
x\in\partial\Omega
and
r>0
there exists
B_{\varepsilon r}(y)\subset B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
. Then by the uniformly locally doubling assumption we have

\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega)\geq\mathfrak{m}(B_{\varepsilon r}(y))\geq C_{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{m}(B_{2r}(y))\geq C_{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)),\quad\forall r\in(0,1),

(3.3)

where
C_{\varepsilon}>0
is a constant depending only on
\varepsilon
. Therefore no point of
x\in\partial\Omega
can be a one-density point for
\partial\Omega
. The conclusion follows by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem for locally doubling metric measure spaces (see e.g.
[
57
, Section 3.4]
). The result applies to uniform domains, since by Lemma
3.5
they satisfy they corkscrew-condition.
∎

The following lemma gives a lower bound on the measure of balls in domain satisfying the corkscrew-condition.

Lemma 3.7
.

Fix
s>0
. Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure space such that for every
R_{0}>0
there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))}\geq c_{0}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{s},\quad\forall x\in{\rm X},\,\,\forall 0<r<R\leq R_{0}.

(3.4)

Then for every bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
satisfying the corkscrew-condition there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega)}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x)\cap\Omega)}\geq C\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{s},\quad\forall x\in\overline{\Omega},\,\,\forall 0<r<R.

(3.5)

In particular this holds if
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
is a uniform domain.

Proof.

Taking
R=2r
in (
3.4
) shows that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is uniformly locally doubling. Then as in (
3.3
) we have the existence of a constant
\tilde{C}
such that for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega)\geq\tilde{C}\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))\overset{\eqref{eq:dim doubling}}{\geq}c_{0}\tilde{C}\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle\geq c_{0}\tilde{C}\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x)\cap\Omega)\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{s},\quad\forall 0<r<R\leq\text{diam}(\Omega).

This proves (
3.5
) for
0<r<R\leq\text{diam}(\Omega)
.
If instead
r>\text{diam}(\Omega)
we have

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))\cap\Omega}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x)\cap\Omega)}=\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}=1\geq\frac{r^{s}}{R^{s}},\quad\forall x\in\overline{\Omega},\quad\forall R\geq r.

This shows (
3.5
) also for
\text{diam}(\Omega)<r<R
and concludes the proof.
∎

The next step is to show that the Sobolev space on a uniform domain coincides with the Sobolev space on its closure. In what follows, given a m.m. space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
and an open subset
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
, we denote by
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
the m.m. space obtained by endowing
\overline{\Omega}
with the restriction distance
{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}\coloneqq{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}\times\overline{\Omega}}$}
and measure
\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}
, obtained by restricting
\mathfrak{m}
to the induced Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\overline{\Omega}.
Note that by definition
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is a complete and separable metric measure space with
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})=\overline{\Omega}.
Moreover for a function
u\in L^{2}(\overline{\Omega},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
we will denote by
u\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{m})
the function which agrees
\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. with
u
in
\Omega.

Theorem 3.8

(Equivalence between
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
and
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a PI space and
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be a uniform domain. Then
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})=\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
as function spaces. More precisely, for every
u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
it holds that
\Phi(u)\coloneqq u\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and the map

\Phi:\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})\to\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a surjective isometry. Moreover if
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is infinitesimally Hilbertian, so is
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
and

\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla(u\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})\cdot\nabla(v\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m},\quad\forall u,v\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}).

(3.6)

Proof.

The result is well known, but for convenience of the reader we include a short argument which is a combination of results already present in literature. Fix
u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
. The fact that
\Phi(u)=u\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is checked e.g. in
[
6
, Remark 2.15]
. Moreover by
[
9
, Prop. 6.4]
for every
\eta\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}(\Omega)
, since
{\sf d}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\eta u),{\rm X}\setminus\Omega)>0,
it holds that
\eta u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
(when extended by zero in the whole
{\rm X}
) and also that
|D(\eta u)|_{\rm X}=|D(\eta u)|_{\overline{\Omega}}

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\Omega
, where
|D(\cdot)|_{\overline{\Omega}}
is the w.u.g. in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
and
|D(\cdot)|_{{\rm X}}
is the w.u.g. in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
. Hence by the arbitrariness of
\eta
and by locality we deduce that
|Du|_{\overline{\Omega}}=|D(u\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})|_{\Omega}

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\Omega
, where
|D(\cdot)|_{\Omega}
is the w.u.g. in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
(as defined in (
2.2
)). This together with the fact that
\mathfrak{m}(\partial\Omega)=0
(recall Lemma
3.6
) shows that
\Phi(u)
preserves the norm.
It remains to show that
\Phi
is surjective (note that everything we said up to now holds for an arbitrary open set
\Omega
with
\mathfrak{m}(\partial\Omega)
=0).
Now since
\Omega
is a uniform domain, by
[
22
, Prop. 5.9]
,
\Omega
is an extension domain and in particular for every
u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
there exists
\tilde{u}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X})
such that
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=u
. By density of Lipschitz functions (see
[
85
]
or
[
19
, Theorem 5.1]
) there exists a sequence
u_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{bs}({\rm X})
such that
u_{n}\to\tilde{u}
in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}({\rm X}).
In particular it holds
u_{n}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\to u
in
L^{2}(\overline{\Omega};\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
. Moreover
u_{n}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}\in\mathsf{LIP}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
and by definition of slope we have
{\rm lip}_{\overline{\Omega}}(u_{n}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})(x)\leq{\rm lip}(u_{n})(x)
for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, where
{\rm lip}_{\overline{\Omega}}
denotes the slope with respect to the metric space
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}).
Therefore

\sup_{n}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}|{\rm lip}_{\overline{\Omega}}(u_{n}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}\leq\sup_{n}\int_{{\rm X}}|{\rm lip}(u_{n})|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}<+\infty
, which by definition proves that
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}).
Since
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=u
and by the arbitrariness of
u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
this shows that
\Phi
is surjective. Finally if
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is infinitesimally Hilbertian then
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space and since
\Phi
is an isometry, then
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is a Hilbert space as well and so
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is infinitesimally Hilbertian. Then identity (
3.6
) follows by polarization using the definition of scalar product between gradients.
∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space and let
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be a uniform domain. Then the metric measure space
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is an infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space.

Proof.

The fact that
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is a PI space is proved in
[
22
, Theorem 4.4]
(see also
[
1
, Prop. 7.1]
and
[
19
, Theorem A.21]
), recalling also that
\mathfrak{m}(\partial\Omega)=0
. The infinitesimal Hilbertianity follows from Theorem
3.8
(alternatively we could apply
[
48
, Prop. 4.22]
).
∎

From the previous results about the compatibility of the two Sobolev spaces
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
, we can deduce a compatibility between eigenfunctions and eigenvalues in
\Omega
and in
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
.

Corollary 3.10

(Equivalence between
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)
and
\lambda_{k}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be an infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space and let
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be a uniform domain. Then:

i)

u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is an eigenfunction for the Neumann Laplacian in
\Omega
of eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only there exists an eigenfunction
\tilde{u}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
of the Laplacian in the metric measure space
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
of eigenvalue
\lambda
and satisfying
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=u
. In particular, if this is the case, then
u
has a Hölder continuous representative in
\Omega.

ii)

the embedding
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact and in particular the Neumann Laplacian in
\Omega
has a discrete spectrum
\{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(counted with multiplicity) satisfying

0=\lambda_{1}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\dots\rightarrow+\infty.

iii)

denoted by
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
the spectrum for the Laplacian in
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$}),
it holds that

\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)=\lambda_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

First observe that the statement makes sense since by Theorem
3.9
we have that
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is a bounded infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space and so by (
2.21
) we have that the Laplacian in
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
has a discrete spectrum
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}.
It is sufficient to show
i)
and
ii)
, because then
iii)
would follow from the definitions. Suppose that
u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is an eigenfunction for the Neumann Laplacian in
\Omega
of eigenvalue
\lambda
. Then by Theorem
3.8
there exists
\tilde{u}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
such that
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=u.

Moreover for every
v\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
, again by Theorem
3.8
, we have that
v\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Then applying (
3.6
) we obtain

\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\nabla\tilde{u}\cdot\nabla v\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=-\lambda\int_{\Omega}uv\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=-\lambda\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\tilde{u}v{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m},

where in the second identity we used the definition of eigenfunction and in the last one that
\mathfrak{m}(\partial\Omega)=0,
because
\Omega
is a uniform domain. This shows that
\tilde{u}
is an eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda
for the Laplacian in
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
. Then by Theorem
2.17

\tilde{u}
has a Hölder continuous representative in
\overline{\Omega}
, which implies that
u
has also a continuous representative in
\Omega
. Conversely suppose that
\tilde{u}
is an eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda
for the Laplacian in
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
. Then by Theorem
3.8
we have
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Moreover, again by Theorem
3.8
, for every
v\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
there exists
\tilde{v}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
such that
\tilde{v}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=v.
Therefore as above using (
3.6
)

-\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}v{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=-\lambda\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\tilde{u}\tilde{v}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\nabla\tilde{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{v}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}\cdot\nabla v\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}.

This shows that
\tilde{u}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}
is an eigenfunction of eigenvalue
\lambda
for the Neumann Laplacian in
\Omega
and completes the proof of
i).
For
ii)
recall that by Theorem
3.8
the map
\Phi:\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})\to\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, given by
\Phi(u)=u\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}
is an isometry and that by Theorem
2.16
the inclusion
\iota:\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\overline{\Omega},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is compact. Let now
u_{n}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
be a sequence bounded in
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega).
Then the sequence
\Phi^{-1}(u_{n})\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
is also bounded, hence it has a converging subsequence in
L^{2}(\overline{\Omega},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
. However by definition
\Phi^{-1}(u_{n})\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$}=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(u_{n})=u_{n}
for every
n
. Hence
u_{n}
has also a converging subsequence in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})
, which shows that the embedding
\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\Omega}$})
is compact. This completes the proof of
ii).

∎

Remark 3.11
.

If the ambient space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space, with
N<+\infty
, then the Neumann and Dirichlet eigenfunctions are actually locally Lipschitz in the interior of the domain, as follows directly from
[
64
, Theoem 1.1]
(see also
[
7
, Prop. 7.1]
). Recall that the continuity of eigenfunctions is crucial to define their nodal domains.

\blacksquare

Next we show that for a uniform domain
\Omega
there is a one to one correspondence between the nodal domains in
\Omega
and the nodal domains in its closure.

Proposition 3.12

(Compatibility of nodal domains)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d})
be a metric space,
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
be a uniform domain and
f:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. Denote by
\mathcal{C}
(resp.
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
) the set all the connected components of
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
(resp.
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
). Then

\overline{\mathcal{C}}=\{U\cup(\partial U\cap(\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}))\ :\ U\in\mathcal{C}\}.

(3.7)

In particular the sets
\mathcal{C}
and
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
have the same cardinality.

Proof.

For every
U\in\mathcal{C}
we put
\varphi(U)\coloneqq U\cup(\partial U\cap(\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}))
and we want to show that
\varphi
defines a bijective map
\varphi:\mathcal{C}\to\overline{\mathcal{C}}
. We note immediately that
\varphi
is injective, because if
\varphi(U)=\varphi(V)
then
U=\varphi(U)\cap\Omega=\varphi(V)\cap\Omega=V
.

To conclude it is sufficient to prove that the sets
\{\varphi(U)\}_{U\in\mathcal{C}}
are open, closed and connected in the topology of
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
and that their union is
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
. Indeed this would show that
\{\varphi(U)\}_{U\in\mathcal{C}}
are exactly the connected components of
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
, which would imply (
3.7
) and in particular that
\varphi
is surjective.

Note first that the elements of
\mathcal{C}
are open in the topology of
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
(and thus also in the one of
{\rm X}
) because
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
is locally connected (recall Lemma
2.2
) since it is an open subset of
\Omega
which is a uniform domain and thus locally connected.

The key observation is that for every
x\in\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
there exists
r>0
and
U\in\mathcal{C}
such that

B_{r}(x)\cap\overline{\Omega}\subset\varphi(U)=U\cup(\partial U\cap(\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})).

(3.8)

To prove this note that
f(x)\neq 0
. Then by continuity there exists
r_{0}>0
so that
B_{r}(x)\cap\overline{\Omega}\subset\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
for every
r\in(0,r_{0}]
. In particular for every
r\in(0,r_{0}]
the set
B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
is contained in
\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{C}}U
. Suppose that for some
r>0
the set
B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
intersects at at least two distinct sets
U_{r},V_{r}\in\mathcal{C}
and take two points
u_{r}\in U_{r}\cap B_{r}(x),v_{r}\in V_{r}\cap B_{r}(x)
. Since
\Omega
is a uniform domain there exists a rectifiable curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to{\rm X}
contained in
\Omega
, connecting
u_{r}
and
v_{r}
and of length
l(\gamma)\leq C{\sf d}(u_{r},v_{r})\leq 2Cr
, where
C>0
is some constant independent of
r.
Then
\gamma([0,1])
must intersect
\{f=0\}
otherwise
\gamma([0,1])\subset\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
and
U_{r}\cup V_{r}\cup\gamma([0,1])
would be a connected subset of
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
, which contradicts the fact that
U_{r},V_{r}
are distinct connected components of
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}.
Therefore, since
\gamma([0,1])\subset B_{(2C+1)r}(x)
, it holds
B_{(2C+1)r}(x)\cap\Omega\cap\{f=0\}\neq\emptyset
and so by the choice of
r_{0}
we must have
(2C+1)r>r_{0}
. This proves that for
r
small enough
B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega
intersects at most one set in
\mathcal{C}
. However, as observed above,
B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega\subset\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{C}}U
for every
r\in(0,r_{0}]
. Therefore for
r>0
small enough we must have that
B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega\subset U
for some
U\in\mathcal{C}.
Fix one such
r>0
and fix
y\in B_{r}(x)\cap\partial\Omega
. Then for every
s>0
small enough
B_{s}(y)\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
and
B_{s}(y)\cap\Omega\subset B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega\subset U
. This shows that
y\in\partial U
. Therefore for every
r>0
small enough
B_{r}(x)\cap\Omega\subset U
,
B_{r}(x)\cap\partial\Omega\subset\partial U
for some
U\in\mathcal{C}
and as observed above
B_{r}(x)\cap\overline{\Omega}\subset\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
. Combining these three facts proves (
3.8
).

Consider now any
U\in\mathcal{C}
and note that

\varphi(U)
is precisely the closure of
U
in the topology of
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
.
Indeed

\displaystyle\overline{U}\cap(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\})

\displaystyle=\left(U\cap(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\})\right)\cup\left(\partial U\cap(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\})\right)

\displaystyle=U\cup\left(\partial U\cap(\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})\right)\cup\left(\partial U\cap(\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})\right)

\displaystyle=U\cup\left(\partial U\cap(\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})\right)=\varphi(U),

where we used that
U\subset\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
and that
\partial U\cap(\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})=\emptyset
because
U
is closed in the topology of
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
, being a connected component, but also open as observed above. Moreover
U
is connected in
\Omega\setminus\{f=0\}
and thus also in
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
, hence
\varphi(U)
is also connected in the same topology, being the closure of a connected set. Additionally (
3.8
) implies that
\varphi(U)
is open in the topology of
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
. It remains to prove that the union of the sets
\{\varphi(U)\}_{U\in\mathcal{C}}
is
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}
, which can be seen as follows

\displaystyle\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{f=0\}

\displaystyle=(\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})\cup(\partial\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})\overset{\eqref{eq:key}}{\subset}(\Omega\setminus\{f=0\})\cup\left(\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{C}}\varphi(U)\right)

\displaystyle\subset\left(\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{C},}U\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{C}}\varphi(U)\right)=\bigcup_{U\in\mathcal{C}}\varphi(U).

∎

We pass now to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The fact that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
is an infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space is contained in Theorem
3.9
. Recall also that by Lemma
3.6
it holds
\mathcal{H}^{N}(\partial\Omega)=0.
Item
(i)
follows immediately combining (
2.33
) and Lemma
3.7
and recalling that
\Omega
is bounded.

We pass to the proof of
\ref{it:isop}.
Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
arbitrary.
We choose

C_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{x\in\overline{\Omega}\ :\ \theta_{N}(x)\leq 1-\varepsilon\}\cup\partial\Omega

(see (
2.34
) for the definition of
\theta_{N}
). From the lower semicontinuity of the function
\theta_{N}(\cdot)
, it follows that
C_{\varepsilon}
is a closed subset of
\overline{\Omega}
. Moreover, since
\theta_{N}(x)=1
for
\mathcal{H}^{N}
-a.e.
x
(recall (
2.36
)) and
\mathcal{H}^{N}(\partial\Omega)=0,
it follows
\mathcal{H}^{N}(C_{\varepsilon})=0
. Note that by construction
1-\varepsilon<\theta_{N}(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus C_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
. Therefore we can apply the local almost-Euclidean isoperimetric inequality given by Theorem
2.24
(recall also Lemma
2.11
) and obtain that for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus C_{\varepsilon}
and every
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
there exists
\rho=\rho(x,N,\varepsilon)<\tilde{\sf d}(x,\Omega^{c})
such that

{\rm{Per}}_{\rm X}(E)\geq\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}}N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}(1-2\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{N}}(1-\varepsilon),\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\rho}(x)=B^{\rm X}_{\rho}(x)\text{ Borel},

which shows (
3.1
). It remains to show
(iii)
. Denote by
\lambda_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
the spectrum of the Laplacian in
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
(counted with multiplicity and in non-decreasing order). Recall that by Corollary
3.10

\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)
is the
k
-th Neumann eigenvalue of
\Omega
(in non-decreasing order). Recalling Lemma
2.8
we know that
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\leq\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
is the
k
-th Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue of
\Omega.
Then (
3.2
) follows from the Weyl law for the Dirichlet Laplacian (see Theorem
2.27
):

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\lambda_{k}^{N/2}}{k}=\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega))^{N/2}}{k}\leq\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{(\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega))^{N/2}}{k}=\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega_{n}\mathcal{H}^{N}(\Omega)}=\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega_{n}\mathfrak{m}({\rm X})},

having used again
\mathfrak{m}(\partial\Omega)=0.

∎

4
From local to global isoperimetric inequality

In this section we prove the following crucial result. Informally speaking, it says that in a PI space satisfying an almost-Euclidean isoperimetric inequality around almost-every point, the same isoperimetric inequality extends to all sets having sufficiently small volume and avoiding a ‘bad’ but small region of the space.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a bounded PI space and fix
N>1
. Suppose that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a closed set
C_{\varepsilon}\subset{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(C_{\varepsilon})=0
such that for every
x\in{\rm X}\setminus C_{\varepsilon}
there exists a constant
\rho=\rho(x,N,\varepsilon)>0
satisfying

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq(1-\varepsilon)N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}},\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\rho}(x)\,\text{ Borel.}

(4.1)

Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
there exists an open set
U_{\varepsilon,\eta}\subset{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(U_{\varepsilon,\eta})<\eta
and constants
\beta=\beta({\rm X},\varepsilon,N,\eta)>0
,
\beta^{\prime}=\beta^{\prime}(\varepsilon)>0
such that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq(1-\varepsilon)N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}},

(4.2)

for every
E\subset{\rm X}
Borel satisfying

0<\mathfrak{m}(E)\leq\beta,\quad\frac{\mathfrak{m}(E\cap U_{\varepsilon,\eta})}{\mathfrak{m}(E)}\leq\beta^{\prime}.

Observe that assumption (
4.1
) is the same as item
ii)
in Theorem
3.2
.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
takes inspiration from the arguments in Appendix
C
of
[
17
]
in the smooth setting, but requires also to deal with the technical issues arising from working in a non-smooth metric space.

We start with an estimate for the perimeter of the complement of the union of a finite number of balls.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a PI space. Suppose that
B_{i}\coloneqq B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\subset{\rm X}
,
i=1,...,k
,
k\in\mathbb{N},
have all finite perimeter and satisfy
{\rm{Per}}(B_{i},\partial^{e}B_{j})=0
for all
i\neq j
. Then

{\rm{Per}}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c},.)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\rm{Per}}(B_{i},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c}}$}.

(4.3)

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
. By (
2.22
) we have that for every ball
B\subset{\rm X}
of finite perimeter

{\rm{Per}}(B^{c},.)={\rm{Per}}(B,.)={\rm{Per}}(B,.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B^{c}}$},

which shows that the statement holds for
k=1
. Suppose that the statement is true for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
B_{i}\coloneqq B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\subset{\rm X}
,
i=1,...,k+1
be as in the statement. By a repeated application of
(vi)
in Lemma
2.10
we get

\partial^{e}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c})\subset\partial^{e}B_{i}\cup...\cup\partial^{e}B_{k}.

(4.4)

Using (
4.4
) and the assumption
{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},\partial^{e}B_{j})=0
for all
j\neq k+1
gives

{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},\partial^{e}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c}))=0.

Hence we can apply Proposition
2.19
and obtain

{\rm{Per}}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k+1}^{c},.)\leq{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c})^{(1)}}$}+{\rm{Per}}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{(B_{k+1}^{c})^{(1)}}$}.

(4.5)

Since balls are open sets, by using
(iii)
and
(iv)
in Lemma
2.10
it holds

(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c})^{(1)}\subset B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c},

that combined with (
4.5
) and the induction hypothesis gives

\displaystyle{\rm{Per}}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k+1}^{c},.)

\displaystyle\leq{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c}}$}+{\rm{Per}}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{(B_{k+1}^{c})^{(1)}}$}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c}}$}+{\rm{Per}}(B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{k+1}^{c}}$}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c}\cap B_{k+1}^{c}}$}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\rm{Per}}(B_{i},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{1}^{c}\cap B_{2}^{c}\cap...\cap B_{k}^{c}\cap B_{k+1}^{c}}$},

where in the second line we used that
(B_{k+1}^{c})^{(1)}\subset B_{k+1}^{c}
(recall
(iv)
in Lemma
2.10
) and in the last line that
{\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},.)={\rm{Per}}(B_{k+1},.)\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{B_{k+1}^{c}}$}
(recall (
2.22
)) for the first term and the induction hypothesis for the second term. This concludes the proof.
∎

Combining the above estimate with a covering argument we can prove the following proposition, from which Theorem
4.1
will easily follow.

Proposition 4.3

(From local-to-global isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a PI space. Suppose there exist constants
\lambda>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
and a compact set
{\sf K}\subset{\rm X}
such that for all
x\in{\sf K}
there exists
\rho(x)>0
so that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq\lambda\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\alpha},\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\rho(x)}(x)\,\text{ Borel.}

(4.6)

Then there exists a constant
C=C({\sf K},\alpha,\lambda)
such that

{\rm{Per}}(V)\geq\lambda\mathfrak{m}(V\cap{\sf K})^{\alpha}-C\mathfrak{m}(V),\quad\forall\,V\subset{\rm X}\text{ Borel.}

(4.7)

Proof.

We start by extracting once and for all a finite covering
{\sf K}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{M}B_{\frac{\rho(x_{i})}{2}}(x_{i})
, with
x_{i}\in{\sf K}
, and we set
\overline{\rho}\coloneqq\min_{i}\rho(x_{i})>0
. It is enough to prove (
4.7
) for sets
V
of finite perimeter. Fix one such set
V.

We claim that there exist
r_{1},\dots,r_{M}
, with
r_{i}\in\left(\frac{\rho(x_{i})}{2},\rho(x_{i})\right)
such that the following hold:

a)

B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})
has finite perimeter, for every
i=1,...,M,

b)

{\rm{Per}}(V,\partial B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}))=0
, for every
i=1,...,M,

c)

{\rm{Per}}(B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}),V^{(1)})\leq\frac{3\mathfrak{m}(V)}{\rho(x_{i})}
, for every
i=1,...,M
,

d)

{\rm{Per}}(B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}),\partial B_{r_{j}}(x_{j}))=0
, for every
i,j=1,...,M
with
i\neq j.

It is sufficient to prove that:

(i)

for any
i\in\{1,...,M\}
, there exists
A_{i}\subset\left(\frac{\rho(x_{i})}{2},\rho(x_{i})\right)
with

5
Almost Euclidean Faber-Krahn inequality for small volumes

Similarly to the classical Faber-Krahn inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, combining the almost Euclidean isoperimetric inequality for small volumes in Section
4
and the Pólya-Szegő inequality of Section
2.4
, we deduce here an almost-Euclidean Faber-Krahn inequality for small volumes similar to
[
17
, Lemme 16]
in the case of Riemannian manifolds. However it is not possible to apply directly the Pólya-Szegő inequality as in
[
17
]
, because our isoperimetric inequality applies only to sets that have small volumes
and avoid
a bad set with small measure.
This technical difficulty will require a more careful argument, which will eventually lead to a Faber-Krahn inequality that applies only to sets that again avoid a portion of the space with small measure (see Theorem
5.3
).

We start by recalling the well known expression of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of a ball in the
N
-dimensional Euclidean space (see e.g.
[
17
]
):

\lambda_{1}(B_{r}^{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(0))=\left(\frac{\omega_{N}}{{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(B_{r}^{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(0))}\right)^{2/N}j^{2}_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}},\qquad\forall\,r>0,

(5.1)

where
j_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}}
denotes the first positive zero of the Bessel function (of the first kind) of index
\frac{(N-2)}{N}
.

Next we obtain a weaker Faber-Krahn inequality, i.e. with a rough constant, but that applies to sets also with large volume.

Proposition 5.1

(Faber-Krahn inequality in PI spaces)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a bounded PI space satisfying for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and some constant
c>0

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))}\geq c\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{N},\quad\forall\,x\in{\rm X},\,\forall\,0<r<R.

Then there exist constants
v_{0}=v_{0}({\rm X})>0
and
C=C({\rm X},N)>0
such that

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geq\frac{C}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)^{\frac{2}{N}}},\quad\forall\,\Omega\subset{\rm X}\text{ open, }0<\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\leq v_{0}.

(5.2)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.18
there exist constants
w_{0}=w_{0}({\rm X})>0
and
C_{I}=C_{I}({\rm X},N)>0
such that

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq C_{I}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}},\qquad\forall\,E\subset{\rm X}\text{ Borel such that $\mathfrak{m}(E)\leq w_{0}$}

(5.3)

In particular if
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\leq v_{0}\coloneqq w_{0}
, then (
5.3
) holds for every
E\subset\Omega
Borel. Moreover taking
v_{0}<\mathfrak{m}({\rm X})
we have
\Omega\neq{\rm X}.

Therefore we can apply the Pólya-Szegő inequality (
2.28
) and deduce

\frac{\int_{\Omega}|{Du}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}\geq\Big(\frac{C_{I}}{N\omega_{N}^{1/N}}\Big)^{2}\frac{\int_{\Omega^{*}}|{Du_{N}^{*}}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}}{\int_{\Omega^{*}}|u_{N}^{*}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}}\geq\frac{C}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)^{\frac{2}{N}}},\quad\forall u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega),\,u\geq 0,\,u\not\equiv 0,

(5.4)

where we have also used that
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathfrak{m})}=\|u_{N}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{*},{\mathscr{L}}^{N})}
,
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega^{*})
, and in the last passage the identity (
5.1
) and the fact that
u_{N}^{*}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega^{*})
. By taking the infimum with respect to all the possible
u
in the characterization (
2.16
), we get the result.
∎

From the previous proposition we can deduce the following version of the Faber-Krahn in the Euclidean setting. Even if it will be not used in this note, we think it is worth to be isolated in a separate statement. Indeed it has been pointed out repeatedly in the previous literature that one of the major difficulties in counting nodal domains for non-Dirichlet boundary condition in subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with irregular boundary is the absence of a suitable Faber-Krahn inequality for subdomains close to the boundary (see e.g. discussions in
[
56
, Section 2]
,
[
69
, Section 1.2]
,
[
53
, Section 1.2]
,
[
15
, Section 1.1]
). This was one of the main issues faced
[
69
]
which also forced the assumption of a
C^{1,1}
boundary (see also
[
56
]
).
Here we show precisely that a Faber-Krahn-type inequality does hold in any uniform domain, no matter how close is the support of the function to the boundary.

Corollary 5.2

(Faber-Krahn inequality for uniform domains)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a uniform domain. Then there exist constants
v_{0}\in(0,{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega))
and
C>0
, depending only on
\Omega,
such that

\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}}\geq\frac{C}{({\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u)))^{\frac{2}{N}}},\quad\forall u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega),u\not\equiv 0,\ \text{such that }{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u))\leq v_{0}.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Then by Theorem
3.8
there exists
\tilde{u}\in L^{2}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathscr{L}}^{N})
such that
\tilde{u}=u

{\mathscr{L}}^{N}
-a.e. in
\Omega
and such that
\tilde{u}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},{\mathscr{L}}^{N}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
where
{\sf d}
denotes the Euclidean distance (recall also Remark
2.7
). Moreover by Theorem
3.2
the m.m.s.
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})\coloneqq(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},{\mathscr{L}}^{N}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
5.1
. Finally by Lemma
3.6
we have
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\partial\Omega)=0.
Let
v_{0}<\mathfrak{m}({\rm X})={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega)
and
C>0
be the constants given by Proposition
5.1
, which depend only on
\Omega.

Consider the open set
U_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\tilde{u}))^{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon>0
. Then
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(U_{\varepsilon})\to{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\tilde{u}))={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u))
as
\varepsilon\to 0,
having also used that
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\partial\Omega)=0.
Hence assuming that
{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(u))<v_{0}({\rm X})
we have that
\mathfrak{m}(U_{\varepsilon})\leq v_{0}
for
\varepsilon
small enough. Clearly
\tilde{u}\in\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(U_{\varepsilon})
(recall (
2.9
)), hence we can apply (
5.2
) and obtain

\frac{\int_{\overline{\Omega}}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}}{\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\tilde{u}^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}}\geq\frac{C}{\mathfrak{m}(U_{\varepsilon})^{\frac{2}{n}}}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and recalling that
\int_{\overline{\Omega}}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}
concludes the proof.
∎

We pass to the statement of our main Faber-Krahn inequality for small volumes.

Theorem 5.3

(Almost Euclidean Faber-Krahn inequality for small volumes)
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a bounded PI space satisfying for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and some constant
c>0

\frac{\mathfrak{m}(B_{r}(x))}{\mathfrak{m}(B_{R}(x))}\geq c\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{N},\quad\forall\,x\in{\rm X},\,\forall 0<r<R.

Suppose that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a closed set
C_{\varepsilon}\subset{\rm X}
with
\mathfrak{m}(C_{\varepsilon})=0
such that for every
x\in{\rm X}\setminus C_{\varepsilon}
there exists a constant
\rho=\rho(x,N,\varepsilon)>0
satisfying

{\rm{Per}}(E)\geq(1-\varepsilon)N\omega_{N}^{\frac{1}{N}}\mathfrak{m}(E)^{\frac{N-1}{N}},\quad\forall\,E\subset B_{\rho}(x)\,\text{ Borel,}

(5.5)

Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
there exists an open set
U_{\varepsilon,\eta}
with
\mathfrak{m}(U_{\varepsilon,\eta})<\eta
and constants
\delta=\delta({\rm X},\varepsilon,N,\eta)>0
,
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}({\rm X},\varepsilon,N)>0
such that
for every
\Omega\subset{\rm X}
open satisfying

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\leq\delta,\quad\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega\cap U_{\varepsilon,\eta})}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}\leq\delta^{\prime},

and, denoted by
\Omega^{*}\coloneqq B_{r}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
the ball satisfying
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega^{*})
, it holds

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\geq(1-\varepsilon){\lambda_{1}}(\Omega^{*}).

(5.6)

The key point of Theorem
5.3
is that the constant
\delta^{\prime}

does not depend
on
\eta
. This will be crucial in the proof of the main result, which will be done in Section
6
. Indeed we will eventually need to get rid of
\delta^{\prime}
by sending
\eta\to 0
(see in particular (
6.8
)).

Remark 5.4
.

Thanks to Theorem
3.2
we know that, given
({\rm Y},\tilde{\sf d},\mathcal{H}^{N})
an
\mathrm{RCD}(K,N)
space and
\Omega\subset{\rm Y}
a uniform domain, the metric measure space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})\coloneqq(\overline{\Omega},\tilde{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},\mathcal{H}^{N}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
satisfies the hypotheses of the above Theorem
5.3
. This will actually be the way in which we will apply this result in the sequel.

\blacksquare

In the proof of Theorem
5.3
we will make use of the following elementary observation.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a m.m.s. and
f\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(X)
,
f\not\equiv 0
,
f\geq 0
. It holds

\frac{\int_{X}|Df|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\,\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{X}|f|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}\geq\lambda_{1}(\{f>0\}),

where
\{f>0\}\coloneqq\{x\in{\rm X}\colon f(x)>0\}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{n}\coloneqq(f-\frac{1}{n})^{+}
. Then
\psi_{n}\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\{f>0\})
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large,
\psi_{n}\not\equiv 0
. Then for
n
large
\psi_{n}
is a competitor in (
2.15
), so

\lambda_{1}(\{f>0\})\leq\varliminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\int_{X}|D\psi_{n}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{X}|\psi_{n}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}=\varliminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\int_{\{f\geq 1/n\}}|Df|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{\{f\geq 1/n\}}|f-1/n|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}=\frac{\int_{X}|Df|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{X}|f|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}},

where the first equality follows from the locality of the weak upper gradient.
∎

We are now ready to prove the Faber-Krahn inequality for small volumes.

, while
\delta^{\prime}
only on
{\rm X},N,\varepsilon.

open such that

\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\leq\delta,\quad\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega\cap U)}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)}\leq\delta^{\prime},

where

{\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega^{*})=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega).
Up to choosing

\Omega\neq{\rm X}.

Let
u\in\mathsf{LIP}_{c}(\Omega)
be a competitor in the infimum of (
2.19
). In particular
\ u\not\equiv 0
,
u\geq 0
and
{\rm lip}(u)\neq 0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. in
\{u>0\}

We divide two cases:

Case 1

\mathfrak{m}(\{u>0\})\leq 3\sqrt{\delta^{\prime}}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)

5.5
and Proposition

One last ingredient, contained in the next statement, is a crucial inequality relating the eigenvalue of an eigenfunction with the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of one of its nodal domains. Proposition 6.1
.

Let
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
be a bounded infinitesimally Hilbertian PI space,

be open and

be a Dirichlet or Neumann eigenfunction of the Laplacian in
U
of eigenvalue
\lambda
. If
\Omega\subset U
is a nodal domain of (the continuous representative of)
f
, then
\Omega
is open in
{\rm X}
and it holds

\lambda_{1}(\Omega)\leq\lambda=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|Df|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}.

(6.1)

In
[
17
]
(in Riemannian setting) it is shown that the first in (
6.1
) is actually an equality in the Dirichlet case, however we do not know whether the same is true also in this more general setting. Nevertheless (
6.1
) will be sufficient for our purposes.

Specializing Proposition
6.1
to the Euclidean setting we also obtain the following result which, even if not needed in the sequel, we believe it is interesting on its own. In particular it extends previous results in
[
69
, Proposition 1.7]
and
[
15
, Lemma 3.3]
, where the same was proved respectively for
C^{1,1}
domains and for planar piecewise smooth domains.

Corollary 6.2

(Green’s formula for eigenfunctions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a uniform domain and let

be a Neumann eigenfunction in
\Omega
of eigenvalue
\lambda
. Then for every

nodal domain of

\int_{U}|\nabla f|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}=\lambda\int_{U}f^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathscr{L}}^{N}.

Proof.

Let
f
be as in the statement and
U
be a nodal domain of
f.

From Corollary
3.10
there exists
\tilde{f}\in L^{2}(\overline{\Omega},{\mathscr{L}}^{N})
such that
\tilde{f}=f

{\mathscr{L}}^{N}
-a.e. in
\Omega
and such that
\tilde{f}
is an eigenfunction for the Laplacian of eigenvalue
\lambda
in the m.m.s.
(\overline{\Omega},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$},{\mathscr{L}}^{N}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{\Omega}}$})
where

denotes the Euclidean distance (recall also Remark

Proof of Proposition

from the chain rule (see (

Now we observe that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\||D\psi_{n}|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(X)}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega\cap\left\{f>\frac{1}{n}\right\}}|Df|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{\Omega}|Df|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m},

converges to

in

.
In particular

\varliminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\psi_{n}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{\Omega}\psi_{n}^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla f|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}{\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathfrak{m}}.

To see this by definition of eigenfunction (both in the Dirichlet case and the Neumann case) and since

), (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) we reach

\frac{M(u)}{k}\leq\frac{\#\mathcal{S}_{1}+\#\mathcal{S}_{2}+\#\mathcal{S}_{3}}{k}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\overline{\Omega})}{k\delta(\Omega,\varepsilon,N,\eta)}+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)^{\frac{N}{2}}}{k}\frac{1}{C({\Omega})^{\frac{N}{2}}}\frac{\eta}{\delta^{\prime}(\Omega,\varepsilon,N)}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{N}}\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\overline{\Omega})}{k}.

Note that the right hand side is independent of
u
, hence we can take the supremum among all Neumann eigenfunctions in
\Omega
of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)
and obtain

\frac{M_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{N}}(k)}{k}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\overline{\Omega})}{k}\frac{1}{\delta(\Omega,\varepsilon,N,\eta)}+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)^{\frac{N}{2}}}{k}\frac{1}{C({\Omega})^{\frac{N}{2}}}\frac{\eta}{\delta^{\prime}(\Omega,\varepsilon,N)}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)}{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{N}}\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}\frac{\mathfrak{m}(\overline{\Omega})}{k},

where
M_{\Omega}^{{\mathcal{N}}}(k)
is as in Definition
6.3
.
Passing to the
\varlimsup
as
k\to+\infty
, using (
3.2
),

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{M_{\Omega}^{{\mathcal{N}}}(k)}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega_{N}\mathfrak{m}(\overline{\Omega})}\frac{1}{C(\overline{\Omega})^{\frac{N}{2}}}\frac{\eta}{\delta^{\prime}(\Omega,\varepsilon,N)}+\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega_{N}}\frac{1}{\left((1-\varepsilon)\alpha_{N}\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}},

(6.8)

sending first
\eta\to 0
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
(note that both
\delta^{\prime}
and
\varepsilon
are independent of
\eta
) we conclude that

\varlimsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{M_{\Omega}^{{\mathcal{N}}}(k)}{k}\leq\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega_{N}\alpha_{N}^{\frac{N}{2}}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{N}}{\omega^{2}_{N}j^{N}_{\frac{(N-2)}{N}}}<1,

where for the last inequality we refer to
[
17
, Lemme 9]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
in the Dirichlet case.

Let
u\in{W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)}
be a Dirichlet eigenfunction in
\Omega
of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
.
Without loss of generality we can assume that
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
5.3
. Otherwise we can take a uniform domain
U\subset{\rm X}
such that
\Omega\subset U
(which exists by
[
83
]
) and replace
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
with
(\overline{U},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{U}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{U}$})
, which by Theorem
3.2
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
5.3
. Moreover it is a direct verification that the function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
remains a Dirichlet eigenfunction in
\Omega
of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
also in the new space
(\overline{U},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{U}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{U}$})
. Indeed
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
viewed as a subset of
L^{2}(\Omega)
in the space
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
coincides with
\mathit{W}^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)
viewed as a subset of
L^{2}(\Omega)
in the space
(\overline{U},{\sf d}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{\overline{U}}$},\mathfrak{m}\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$|_{U}$})
, and the corresponding minimal w.u.g. also coincide (see
[
9
, Prop. 6.4]
).

Fix now
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\eta>0
and let
\delta=\delta({\rm X},\varepsilon,N,\eta)>0,

\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}({\rm X},\varepsilon,N)>0
and
U_{\varepsilon,\eta}\subset{\rm X}
be the constants and the set given by Theorem
5.3
applied to
({\rm X},{\sf d},\mathfrak{m})
. Recall that
\mathfrak{m}(U_{\varepsilon,\eta})\leq\eta.

From here the proof proceeds almost verbatim as in the Neumann case, by considering the sets

\mathcal{S}_{1}\coloneqq\{i\in 1,...,M(u)\ :\ \mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i})\geq\delta\}\,,

\mathcal{S}_{2}\coloneqq\{i\in 1,...,M(u)\ :\ \mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i}\cap U_{\varepsilon,\eta})\geq\delta^{\prime}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i})\}\,,

\mathcal{S}_{3}\coloneqq\{i\in 1,...,M(u)\ :\ \mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i})\leq\delta,\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i}\cap U_{\varepsilon,\eta})\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i})\},

where
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M(u)}
is an enumeration of the nodal domains of the continuous representative of
u
, and then exploiting the inequalities

\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{N}}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{i}),\quad\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{i})\geq\frac{C_{\rm X}}{\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i})^{\frac{2}{N}}},\quad\text{ for all $i\in{1,\dots,M(u)}$}

which hold by Proposition
5.1
and Proposition
6.1
(that we can apply again by Theorem
3.2
), together with

\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{i})\geq\lambda_{1}(\Omega_{i}^{*})(1-\varepsilon),\quad\text{for all $i\in\mathcal{S}_{3}$},

(
\Omega_{i}^{*}\coloneqq B_{r}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
being the ball satisfying
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega_{i})={\mathscr{L}}^{N}(\Omega_{i}^{*})
) that holds by Theorem
5.3
.
∎

For completeness, we conclude with the proofs of the Corollaries
1.2
and
1.3
, even if they are essentially already included in Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

We apply Theorem
1.1
with
\Omega={\rm X}
. Notice that the choice is admissible since
{\rm X}
is assumed to be compact, and thus
\Omega
is trivially a uniform domain inside
{\rm X}
. From the discussion in Section
2.1
we know that
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathcal{N}}
in this situation, and thus the result follows.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

The metric measure space
(\mathbb{R}^{N},|\cdot|,\mathcal{H}^{N})
, where
\mathcal{H}^{N}
is the
N
-dimensional Hausdorff measure is a non-collapsed
\mathrm{RCD}(0,N)
(recall Remark
2.26
). Hence the result follows applying Theorem
1.1
, recalling also the compatibility between Neumann eigenfunctions in the metric setting and the usual ones (see Remark
2.7
).
∎
