Active Transport of Cargo-Carrying and Interconnected Chiral Particles
Bhavesh Valecha, Hossein Vahid, Pietro Luigi Muzzeddu, Jens-Uwe Sommer, Abhinav Sharma

Abstract
Directed motion up a concentration gradient is crucial for the survival and maintenance of numerous biological systems, such as sperms moving towards an egg during fertilization or ciliates moving towards a food source. In these systems, chirality—manifested as a rotational torque—plays a vital role in facilitating directed motion. While systematic studies of active molecules in activity gradients exist, the effect of chirality remains little studied. In this study, we examine the simplest case of a chiral active particle connected to a passive particle in a spatially varying activity field. We demonstrate that this minimal setup can exhibit rich emergent tactic behaviors, with the chiral torque serving as the tuning parameter. Notably, when the chiral torque is sufficiently large, even a small passive particle enables the system to display the desired accumulation behavior. Our results further show that in the dilute limit, this desired accumulation behavior persists despite the presence of excluded volume effects. Additionally, interconnected chiral active particles exhibit emergent chemotaxis beyond a critical chain length, with trimers and longer chains exhibiting strong accumulation at sufficiently high chiral torques. This study provides valuable insights into the design principles of hybrid bio-molecular devices of the future.

The dynamics of the active-passive composite can be equivalently described by the collective coordinates, which we identify as the center of friction
\bm{R}
and the bond coordinates
\bm{r}
, defined as:

\begin{split}\bm{R}&=\frac{1}{1+q}\bm{r}_{1}+\frac{q}{1+q}\bm{r}_{2},\\
\bm{r}&=\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}.\end{split}

(3)

To analyze the spatial regions where the active-passive composite preferentially accumulates in the long-time limit, it is convenient to express the evolution equation for the one-time joint probability density
P(\bm{R},\bm{r},\theta,t)\equiv P
. Since the stochastic dynamics in Eq. (
1
) is Markovian, this is simply given by the Fokker-Planck equation
[
46
]
:

\begin{split}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\,P=&-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bigg[\dfrac{1}{1+q}\dfrac{1}{\gamma}f_{s}\bm{p}P-\dfrac{1}{1+q}\dfrac{T}{\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{R}}P\bigg]\\
&-\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot\bigg[\dfrac{1+q}{q}\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\bm{F}P+\dfrac{1}{\gamma}f_{s}\bm{p}P-\dfrac{1+q}{q}\dfrac{T}{\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{r}}P\bigg]\\
&-\omega\partial_{\theta}P+D_{R}\partial_{\theta}^{2}P.\end{split}

(4)

Here, the symbol
\cdot
represents a single contraction,
\nabla_{\bm{R}}
and
\nabla_{\bm{r}}
represent derivatives with respect to
\bm{R}
and
\bm{r}
, and
\partial_{\theta}
is the rotation operator in two dimensions.

We are interested in the spatial regions where the active-passive composite preferentially accumulates in the steady state. To this end, we attempt a coarse-grained description of our system at the mean-field level akin to the analysis in Refs.
[
25
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
, where we use the center of friction as a proxy for the position of the composite. This interpretation holds whenever the typical distance between the two particles is sufficiently small compared to the characteristic length scale of variation of the activity field. This gives us access to physically relevant fields such as the position density, orientation, etc. Particularly, we begin by performing a Cartesian multipole expansion of the probability density
P
in the eigenfunctions of the operator
\partial_{\theta}^{2}

[
48
]
:

P(\bm{R},\bm{r},\theta,t)=\phi+\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{p}+\bm{\mu}\colon\big(\bm{p}\bm{p}-\mathds{1}/2\big)+\bm{\Theta}(P),

(5)

where
\phi,\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\mu}
are functions of
\bm{R}
and
\bm{r}
, and
\colon
denotes a double contraction. Specifically, these correspond to the positional probability density, the average polarization, and the nematic tensor, respectively. The term
\bm{\Theta}(P)
contains the dependencies on all the higher-order modes. Projecting Eq. (
4
) onto the respective eigenfunctions, we obtain a hierarchy of time-evolution equations for the modes (
\phi,\bm{\sigma},\bm{\mu},\ldots
), details of which can be found in the Supplementary Material (SM). Importantly, we note that the slowest mode of the dynamics is the positional probability density
2\pi\phi(\bm{R,r},t)=\int\differential\theta P(\bm{R,r},\theta,t)
, obtained by integrating out the rotational degree of freedom
\bm{p}
. In fact,
\phi(\bm{R,r},t)
is a conserved quantity and satisfies a continuity equation:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\,=&-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bigg[-\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\phi+\frac{1}{1+q}\frac{1}{2\gamma}f_{s}\bm{\sigma}\bigg]\\
&-\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2\gamma}f_{s}\bm{\sigma}+\frac{1+q}{q}\frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{F}\phi-\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\phi\right].\end{split}

(6)

Additionally, the orientation field
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{R,r},t)=\int\differential\theta\,\bm{p}P(\bm{R,r},\theta,t)
, which is related to the conditional average of the polarization vector
\bm{p}
at fixed position of the active-passive composite, evolves in times as:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}}{\partial t}=&-\big(D_{R}\,\bm{\mathds{1}}-\omega\,\bm{\varepsilon}\big)\cdot\bm{\sigma}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)(f_{s}\phi)\\
&-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{\sigma})+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}),\end{split}

(7)

where the dependence on the higher-order modes is captured in
\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2})
. It is important to note that
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{R,r},t)
is a fast mode due to the presence of the sink term
\big(D_{R}\,\bm{\mathds{1}}-\omega\,\bm{\varepsilon}\big)\cdot\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\mathds{L}}^{-1}\bm{\sigma}
, where
\bm{\varepsilon}
is the
Levi-Civita
tensor in two dimensions. The eigenvalues of the matrix
\bm{\mathds{L}}
govern the decay-timescale of the orientation field
\bm{\sigma}
. This implies that
\bm{\sigma}
can be approximated to be quasi-static at the timescale of variations in
\phi
. Furthermore, we assume that the activity field is slowly varying in space, i.e., the gradients of
f_{s}(\bm{R})
are i) small compared to the persistence length of the chiral active particle, and ii) small compared to the separation between the active and passive particles. This assumption implies that
\phi(\bm{R},\bm{r},t)
also has small spatial variations, as well as the contribution from the higher-order modes
\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2})
to Eq. (
7
) can be neglected. The details of the coarse-graining and the validity of the approximation can be found in the SM.

The quasi-stationarity of
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{R,r},t)
, combined with the assumption of small spatial variations in the activity field, allows for the closure of the hierarchy of the system of equations without requiring information from higher-order modes. These are known as the adiabatic approximation and the small gradients approximation, respectively. Furthermore, we integrate out the bond coordinate
\bm{r}
from the continuity equation in Eq. (
6

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\rho(\bm{R},t)}{\partial t}&=-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bm{J},\\
&=-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bigg[\bm{V}(\bm{R})\rho(\bm{R})-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})\rho(\bm{R})\right)\bigg].\end{split}

(8)

Here, the effective diffusion coefficient
\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})

\bm{R}

\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})=\dfrac{1}{1+q}\frac{T}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{1}}+\dfrac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\dfrac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}f_{s}^{2}(\bm{R})\bm{\mathds{L}}^{T}.

(9)

\begin{split}\bm{V}(\bm{R})&=\left(\bm{\mathds{1}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\big[(1-q)\bm{\mathds{1}}+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}\big]\bm{\mathds{L}}^{-1}\right)\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R}).\end{split}

(10)

where the matrix
\bm{\mathds{B}}
reads

\bm{\mathds{B}}=\frac{qk}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\left(q\bm{\mathds{1}}+\frac{(1+q)k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\right)^{-1}.

(11)

x
-direction in the remainder of this study. Then, symmetry arguments dictate that the steady state density also varies only along the
x

\begin{split}J_{x}(x)=-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\rho\partial_{x}\mathds{D}_{xx}-\mathds{D}_{xx}\partial_{x}\rho,\end{split}

(12)

where
\mathds{D}_{xx}
denotes the

9

\epsilon

, determines the accumulation behavior of the composite. Upon imposing a zero-flux condition along this direction, we obtain the steady state density:

\rho(x)\propto\bigg[1+\dfrac{D_{R}}{D_{R}^{2}+\omega^{2}}\dfrac{1}{1+q}\dfrac{1}{2\gamma T}f_{s}^{2}(x)\bigg]^{-\displaystyle\epsilon/2}.

(13)

We find that the steady state density of our active-passive composite is determined by the sign of the exponent
\epsilon

\epsilon<0\,(>0)
,
\rho(x)
follows the same (opposite) trend as

, i.e., the composite accumulates where the activity is high (low). As shown in the following, the tactic parameter depends on the interaction
\bm{F}
between the chiral active particle and the passive particle, the ratio of mobilities
q
and the chiral torque
\omega
. Specifically, we consider two cases for the force
\bm{F}
in our analytical treatment: force due to a spring with zero rest length and, force due to an infinitely stiff spring.

We first consider the case of a harmonic spring with zero rest length. In this case, the tactic parameter is given by the expression:

\begin{split}\epsilon=1-q\dfrac{(1+\Omega^{2})(1+\tau)}{\Omega^{2}+(1+\tau)^{2}},\end{split}

(14)

where we have introduced non-dimensional parameters

\begin{split}\Omega&=\omega\tau_{p},\\
\tau&=\frac{(1+q)k\tau_{p}}{q\gamma},\end{split}

(15)

which express the chiral torque and the spring relaxation time in units of the persistence time
\tau_{p}=D_{R}^{-1}
of the active particle due to rotational diffusion, respectively.

The preferential accumulation of the composite can be qualitatively understood as follows. Consider that the persistence time of the chiral active particle is smaller than the relaxation time of the spring connecting the two particles. The chiral active particle probes the neighborhood of the passive particle, locally sampling the activity gradients. This gives rise to a net pull on the passive particle towards the high-activity region. Since the persistence time of the active particle can be tuned via the chiral torque, i.e., it becomes smaller than the spring relaxation time with increasing
\Omega
, the tactic behavior of the composite can be switched from accumulation in low-activity regions to high-activity regions by increasing the chiral torque. It also follows that there is no tactic transition in the extreme case of
k\rightarrow\infty
(
\epsilon\rightarrow 1
), in which case the spring relaxes instantaneously. This scenario corresponds to the chiral active particle and the passive particle being on top of each other.

Tuning the persistence time of the active particle via the chiral torque provides a handle on controlling the tactic behavior of composites. However, when fixing the distance between the chiral active particle and the passive particle via a rigid bond, the tactic behavior of the composite is independent of the persistence time and is determined by
q
alone. The tactic parameter (See SM) then reads

\epsilon=1-\frac{q}{2},

(17)

same as reported in Ref.
[
25
]
for two dimensions. This is shown in Fig.
4
for a sinusoidal variation of the activity field, where the transition in the accumulation behavior is obtained only by changing the ratio
q
.

III
Conclusion & Outlook

Directed motion toward regions of high concentration is a defining feature of living systems, spanning length scales from protein complexes to bacteria. In this work, we demonstrate that a minimal model system composed of a chiral active particle coupled to a passive particle, can exhibit preferential accumulation as an emergent property. Particularly, when the chiral active particle and the passive particle are connected by a rigid rod, the transition from accumulation in low-activity regions to high-activity regions depends solely on the size of the passive particle, similar to the previous studies
[
25
,
47
]
. In contrast, when the coupling is mediated by a harmonic spring of zero rest length, the crossover is determined by the chiral torque. Additionally, we observe that this preferential accumulation is possible even when the chiral active particle is coupled to a small passive particle, provided the chiral torque is sufficiently large, which deviates from previous findings.
Our results demonstrate that even when excluded volume effects are considered, accumulation in high-activity regions remains robust in the dilute limit, further underscoring the effect of chirality as a control parameter. The mechanism underlying the preferential accumulation involves the anchoring effect of the passive particle, which allows the chiral active particle to explore the activity gradients and leads to the composite effectively drifting up the gradient. Moreover, our results show that when chiral active particles are connected into chains, they accumulate in high-activity regions at sufficiently high
\Omega
for chain lengths exceeding three monomers (cf. Fig. SI 1 of the SM). Previous theoretical studies suggested that directed motion can emerge when two chiral active particles with equal and opposite torques are coupled, creating a tug-of-war effect
[
48
]
. In contrast, we show that dimers with the same torque direction experience a cumulative chiral effect that suppresses chemotaxis, even at very high chiral torques. This can be attributed to the reduction of the persistence time of the active monomers in the presence of chirality, in which case short chains preferentially accumulate in low activity regions
[
27
]
.

.2
Eliminating the orientational degrees of freedom

We begin with the stochastic equations of motion that govern the dynamics of the active-passive composite (Eq. (1) in the main text). These are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\bm{r}_{1}}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{F}+\frac{1}{\gamma}f_{s}(\bm{r}_{1})\bm{p}+\sqrt{\frac{2T}{\gamma}}\,\bm{\xi}_{1}(t),

(SI 1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\theta}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=\omega+\sqrt{2D_{R}}\,\eta(t),

(SI 1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\differential\bm{r}_{2}}{\differential t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q\gamma}\bm{F}+\sqrt{\frac{2T}{q\gamma}}\,\bm{\xi}_{2}(t).

(SI 1c)

It is convenient to make a coordinate transformation to the collective coordinate of the composite, which are identified as the center of friction and the bond coordinates, given by:

\begin{split}\bm{R}&=\frac{1}{1+q}\bm{r}_{1}+\frac{q}{1+q}\bm{r}_{2},\\
\bm{r}&=\bm{r}_{1}-\bm{r}_{2}.\end{split}

(SI 2)

We now switch to an equivalent description in terms of the Fokker-Planck equation (FPE) for the probability density
P(\bm{R},\bm{r},\theta,t)\equiv P
, given by:

\begin{split}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\,P=&-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bigg[\dfrac{1}{1+q}\dfrac{1}{\gamma}f_{s}\bm{p}P-\dfrac{1}{1+q}\dfrac{T}{\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{R}}P\bigg]\\
&-\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot\bigg[\dfrac{1+q}{q}\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\bm{F}P+\dfrac{1}{\gamma}f_{s}\bm{p}P-\dfrac{1+q}{q}\dfrac{T}{\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{r}}P\bigg]\\
&-\omega\partial_{\theta}P+D_{R}\partial_{\theta}^{2}P,\end{split}

(SI 3)

where the symbol
\cdot
represents a single contraction, and
\nabla_{\bm{R}}
and
\nabla_{\bm{r}}
represent derivatives with respect to
\bm{R}
and
\bm{r}
, respectively.
\partial_{\theta}
is the rotation operator in two dimensions and
\partial_{\theta}^{2}
is the Laplacian on a unit circle.

Following the analysis introduced in
[
25
]
, we start by expanding the probability density in the eigenfunctions of
\partial_{\theta}^{2}
operator:

P(\bm{R},\bm{r},\theta,t)=\phi+\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{p}+\bm{\mu}\colon\bm{Q}+\bm{\Theta}(P).

(SI 4)

Here, the
1
,
\bm{p}
and
\bm{Q}=\bm{p}\,\bm{p}-\bm{\mathds{1}}/2
are the first three eigenfunctions of
\partial_{\theta}^{2}
operator with eigenvalues 0, -1 and -4 respectively, and
\colon
denotes double contraction. Note that these eigenfunctions are equivalent to the
0^{\text{th}}
,
1^{\text{st}}
and
2^{\text{nd}}
order moments in the angular multipole expansion in two dimensions, and correspond to physically relevant observables. Specifically,
\phi(\bm{R},\bm{r},t)
is the positional probability density,
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{R},\bm{r},t)
is proportional to the average orientation and
\bm{\mu}(\bm{R},\bm{{r},t})
is related to the nematic order parameter.
\bm{\Theta}(P)
contains the dependencies on all the higher order moments.

Since we eventually want to know the collective position of the active-passive composite in the steady state, we will integrate out the orientational degrees of freedom. This forms our first coarse-graining step. To this end, we introduce the inner product:

\big\langle\,f(\bm{p}(\theta)),\,g(\bm{p}(\theta))\,\big\rangle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta f(\bm{p}(\theta))\,g(\bm{p}(\theta)).

(SI 5)

We can now project the FPE onto the eigenfunctions of
\partial_{\theta}^{2}
operator to get the time evolution of the moments. To do this, we will need to use the following relations involving the orthogonality of the eigenfunctions of our expansion:

\begin{split}\langle\,1,1\,\rangle&=2\pi,\\
\langle\,\bm{p},1\,\rangle&=0,\\
\langle\,\bm{Q},1\,\rangle&=0,\\
\langle\,1,P\,\rangle&=2\pi\phi,\\
\langle\,\bm{p},P\,\rangle&=\pi\bm{\sigma},\\
\langle\,\bm{Q},P\,\rangle&=\frac{\pi}{2}\bm{\mu},\\
\langle\,\bm{p},\partial_{\theta}P\,\rangle&=-\pi\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{\sigma},\\
\langle\,1,\bm{\Theta}(P)\,\rangle&=0,\\
\langle\,\bm{p},\bm{\Theta}(P)\,\rangle&=0,\\
\langle\,\bm{Q},\bm{\Theta}(P)\,\rangle&=0.\\
\end{split}

(SI 6)

Note that, we have evaluated the inner product
\langle\,\bm{p},\partial_{\theta}P\,\rangle
as follows:

\langle\,\bm{p},\partial_{\theta}P\,\rangle=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\big(\partial_{\theta}\bm{p}\big)P=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{p}P=-\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\langle\,\bm{p},P\,\rangle=-\pi\,\bm{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{\sigma},

where we have used integration by parts and introduced the two dimensional
Levi-Civita
tensor
\bm{\varepsilon}
:

\bm{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

To get the equation of motion for the positional probability density
2\pi\phi(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},t)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\differential\theta\,P(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\bm{p},t)
, we project eq. (
SI 4
) onto the identity eigenfunction and get:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\phi=-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bigg[-\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\phi+\frac{1}{1+q}\frac{1}{2\gamma}f_{s}\bm{\sigma}\bigg]-\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2\gamma}f_{s}\bm{\sigma}+\frac{1+q}{q}\frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{F}\phi-\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\phi\right].\end{split}

(SI 7)

Similarly, taking the inner product of eq. (
SI 4
) with
\bm{p}
gives:

\begin{split}\bigg\{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\big(D_{R}\,\bm{\mathds{1}}-\omega\,\bm{\varepsilon}\big)\cdot\bigg\}\bm{\sigma}=&-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)(f_{s}\phi)-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{\sigma})\\
&+\frac{T}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}+\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}\right)\bm{\sigma}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)\cdot(f_{s}\bm{\mu}).\end{split}

(SI 8)

Finally, we can consider the inner product with the nematic tensor
\bm{Q}
to get:

\begin{split}\bigg\{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+4D_{R}\bigg\}\bm{\mu}=&-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{\mu})+\frac{T}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}+\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}\right)\bm{\mu}\\
&-\frac{2}{\pi\gamma}\bigg[\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)f_{s}\bm{\sigma}\bigg]\colon\langle\,\bm{Q},\bm{Q}\,\rangle-\frac{2}{\pi}\omega\langle\bm{Q},\partial_{\theta}P\rangle\\
&-\frac{2}{\pi\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)\cdot\langle f_{s}\bm{p}\bm{Q},\Theta(P)\rangle.\end{split}

(SI 9)

The equations (
SI 7
), (
SI 8
) and (
SI 9
), together with the evolution equations for higher order expansion coefficients, form a hierarchical structure and exactly determine the full probability distribution
P(\bm{R,r},\theta,t)
. Unfortunately, such a hierarchy cannot be solved exactly. However, we will see in the following, this hierarchy can be closed by employing the small gradients approximation, which implies that all terms involving the nematic tensor are of order at least
\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2})
and thus can be ignored.

.3
Eliminating the bond coordinate

As we are interested in the marginal distribution of the position of the center of mass, we integrate out the information about the relative distance between the chiral active particle and the passive particle. This results in a continuity equation for the probability distribution
\rho(\bm{R})=2\pi\int\differential\bm{r}\phi(\bm{R,r},t)
of the collective coordinate
\bm{R}
. This is done as follows:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\differential\bm{r}\phi(\bm{R,r},t)=&\,\,\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}\int\differential\bm{r}\phi-\frac{1}{1+q}\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int\differential\bm{r}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot(f_{s}\bm{\sigma})\\
&-\int\differential\bm{r}\bigg\{\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2\gamma}f_{s}\bm{\sigma}+\frac{1+q}{q}\frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{F}\phi-\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\phi\right]\bigg\}.\end{split}

(SI 10)

Under periodic boundary conditions, the above equation reduces to the continuity equation:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho=-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bm{J},

(SI 11)

where the probability current can be broken down into a part due to diffusion and a part due to the polarization:

\bm{J}=\bm{J}_{D}+\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}},

(SI 12)

with

\bm{J}_{D}=-\frac{T}{\gamma}\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho,

(SI 13)

and

\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}=\frac{2\pi}{1+q}\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int\,\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\bm{\sigma}.

(SI 14)

.4
Approximations

To find the steady state density distribution
\rho(\bm{R})
from eq. (
SI 11
), we need to calculate
\bm{J_{\sigma}}
, which involves closing the hierarchy in eqs. (
SI 7
), (
SI 8
) and (
SI 9
). To do so, we employ two approximations that are central to this analysis. These are the adiabatic approximation and the small gradients approximation.

.4.1
Adiabatic Approximation

We identify the position probability distribution
\phi(\bm{R,r},t)
as the slowest mode of our dynamics. Indeed,
\phi(\bm{R,r},t)
satisfies a continuity equation, meaning that it is locally conserved and decays on timescales which are
\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{-1})
. Additionally, all the higher order moments decay at much faster timescales due to the presence of sink-terms in their dynamics. Thus, they are effectively quasi-static at the timescales at which our quantity of interest
\phi(\bm{R,r},t)
evolves. In particular,
\bm{\sigma}
decays on a timescale given by the eigenvalues of the matrix
\big(D_{R}\,\bm{\mathds{1}}-\omega\,\bm{\varepsilon}\big)^{-1}\equiv\bm{\mathds{L}}
and
\bm{\mu}
decays on timescales of the order of
(4D_{R})^{-1}
. This allows us to set the time derivative terms in eq. (
SI 8
) and eq. (
SI 9
) to zero, giving us:

\begin{split}\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg\{&-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)(f_{s}\phi)-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{\sigma})\\
&+\frac{T}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}+\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}\right)\bm{\sigma}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)\cdot(f_{s}\bm{\mu})\bigg\}\end{split}

(SI 15)

and

\begin{split}\bm{\mu}=\frac{1}{4D_{r}}\bigg\{&-\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\bm{F}\bm{\mu})+\frac{T}{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}+\frac{1+q}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}^{2}\right)\bm{\mu}\\
&-\frac{2}{\pi\gamma}\bigg[\Big(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\Big)f_{s}\bm{\sigma}\bigg]\colon\langle\,\bm{Q},\bm{Q}\,\rangle-\frac{2}{\pi}\omega\langle\,\bm{Q},\mathcal{\partial_{\theta}}P\,\rangle\\
&-\frac{2}{\pi\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)\cdot\langle\,f_{s}\bm{p}\,\bm{Q},\Theta(P)\,\rangle\bigg\}.\end{split}

(SI 16)

Plugging eq. (
SI 15
) back into eq. (
SI 14
), we get:

\begin{split}\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}=2\pi\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg\{&-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}\phi)-\frac{1}{1+q}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{r}}(f_{s}\phi)\\
&-\frac{1}{q}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\bm{F\sigma})+\frac{1}{(1+q)}\frac{T}{2\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\bigg[\frac{1}{(1+q)}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot(\nabla_{\bm{R}}\bm{\sigma})+\frac{(1+q)}{q}\nabla_{\bm{r}}\cdot(\nabla_{\bm{r}}\bm{\sigma})\bigg]\\
&-\frac{1}{1+q}\frac{1}{4\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\left(\frac{1}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}+\nabla_{\bm{r}}\right)\cdot(f_{s}\bm{\mu})\bigg\}.\end{split}

(SI 17)

.4.2
Small Gradients Approximation

Next, we are interested in the limits where the gradients in the activity field are small compared to the persistence length of the chiral active particle as well as small compared to the separation between the active and the passive particles.
This approximation will allow us to decouple eq. (
SI 11
), (
SI 12
) and (
SI 17
) to get a closed form solution of the marginal probability density
\rho(\bm{R})
.

To apply this approximation, it is important to realize that, in the absence of gradients in the activity field, our system has no directional preferences and will be isotropic in space. This implies that the nematic tensor
\bm{\mu}
and higher order moments are at least
\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}})
. This allows us to significantly simplify eq. (
SI 17
), wherein we only have to evaluate the first three terms. We will also convert the gradients in
\nabla_{\bm{r}}
to gradients in
\nabla_{\bm{R}}
by applying integration by parts:

\nabla_{\bm{r}}f_{s}(\bm{r}_{1})=\frac{q}{1+q}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}(\bm{r}_{1}).

(SI 18)

We then get the following expression for
\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}
:

\begin{split}\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}=2\pi\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg\{-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}\phi)+\frac{q}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}+\frac{1}{1+q}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\bm{I}\bigg\},\end{split}

(SI 19)

where we have defined the quantity
\bm{I}
as:

\bm{I}=\int\differential\bm{r}(\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s})\bm{\sigma}.

(SI 20)

We solve this integral as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{I}

\displaystyle=\int\differential\bm{r}(\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s})\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\int\differential\bm{r}\big[\bm{F}\cdot(\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s})\nabla_{\bm{r}}\big]\cdot\bigg(\bm{\mathds{1}}\phi f_{s}+\frac{1+q}{q}\bm{F\sigma}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}f_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\int\differential\bm{r}\nabla_{\bm{r}}(\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s})\cdot\bigg(\bm{\mathds{1}}\phi f_{s}+\frac{1+q}{q}\bm{F\sigma}\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}f_{s}),

(SI 21)

where we have used integration by parts in the last line. Now, we use

\nabla_{\bm{r}}(\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s})=\nabla_{\bm{r}}\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}f_{s}).

The interaction between the chiral active particle and the passive particle is modeled as a harmonic spring with rest length
l_{0}
. So we can evaluate
\nabla_{\bm{r}}\bm{F}
as:

\nabla_{\bm{r}}\bm{F}=-k\nabla\bm{r}[(r-l_{0})\bm{\hat{r}}]=-k\big[\bm{\hat{r}\hat{r}}+(1-l_{0}/r)(\bm{1}-\bm{\hat{r}\hat{r}})\big]\equiv-k\bm{\mathds{A}},\,\,\text{where}\,\,r=|\bm{r}|.

With this,
\bm{I}
becomes

\displaystyle\bm{I}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg[\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\bm{\mathds{A}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}+\frac{1+q}{q}\int\differential\bm{r}(\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s})\bm{\sigma}\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg[\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\bm{\mathds{A}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}+\frac{1+q}{q}\bm{I}\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{qk}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg(q\bm{\mathds{1}}+\dfrac{(1+q)k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\bigg)^{-1}\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\bm{\mathds{A}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\mathds{B}}\cdot\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\bm{\mathds{A}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}.

(SI 22)

where we used the fact that the matrix
\mathds{A}
is symmetric, the identity
\mathds{A}\cdot\bm{F}=\bm{F}
and we have simplified the notation by introducing the quantity
\bm{\mathds{B}}
:

\bm{\mathds{B}}=\dfrac{qk}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\bigg(q\bm{\mathds{1}}+\dfrac{(1+q)k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\bigg)^{-1}.

We can now finally write the closed form expression for the polarisation current
\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}

\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}=-\frac{2\pi}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg[\int\differential\bm{r}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}\phi)-q\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}\int\differential\bm{r}\phi f_{s}\bm{\mathds{A}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\bigg].

(SI 23)

Now, we are in the position to get the expression of the steady state density distribution
\rho
. In particular, we analyze two specific forms of the force
\bm{F}
between the active particle and the passive particle: the rigid bond case and the harmonic potential with zero rest length. We do this in the following section.

.5
Steady state density distribution

.5.1
Spring with zero rest length

In this case, the quantity
\bm{\mathds{A}}
defined for the spring force is the identity matrix, i.e.,
\bm{\mathds{A}}(l_{0}=0)=\bm{\mathds{1}}
. Since the spring has zero rest length, the separation between the active and passive particles is small compared to the gradients of the activity field, provided that
k
is not too small. So, we can approximate
\phi(\bm{R,r},t)
as:

\phi(\bm{R,r},t)\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\rho(\bm{R})\delta(\bm{r}).

(SI 24)

Moreover, we can also Taylor-expand the activity field about the collective coordinate
\bm{R}
as:

f_{s}=f_{s}(\bm{r}_{1})=f_{s}\left(\bm{R}+\bm{r}\frac{q}{1+q}\right)=f_{s}(\bm{R})+\frac{q}{1+q}\bm{r}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}(\bm{R}),

(SI 25)

and thus move it out of the
\bm{r}
integral in all terms.

With these steps, we can write
\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}
as:

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg[f_{s}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho\int d\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta(\bm{r})+\rho f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\int d\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta(\bm{r})

\displaystyle-q\rho f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\int d\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta(\bm{r})+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\rho\int d\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta(\bm{r})\bigg]+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}f_{s}^{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{\rho}{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\big[(1-q)\bm{\mathds{1}}+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}\big]\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}}^{2}),

(SI 26)

We can now evaluate the total flux up to the drift/diffusion order as:

\displaystyle\bm{J}=

\displaystyle\,\bm{J}_{D}+\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+q)}\frac{T}{\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}f_{s}^{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{\rho}{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\big[(1-q)\bm{\mathds{1}}+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}\big]\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}^{2}).

(SI 27)

In particular, the total flux
\bm{J}
has the following structure

\bm{J}=\bm{V}(\bm{R})\rho(\bm{R})-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})\rho(\bm{R})\right),

(SI 28)

where the effective diffusion coefficient depends on
\bm{R}
and is given by:

\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})=\dfrac{1}{1+q}\frac{T}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{1}}+\dfrac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\dfrac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}f_{s}^{2}(\bm{R})\bm{\mathds{L}}^{T},

(SI 29)

and the effective drift
\bm{V}(\bm{R})
can be written in terms of
\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})
as:

\begin{split}\bm{V}(\bm{R})=\left(\bm{\mathds{1}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\big[(1-q)\bm{\mathds{1}}+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}\big]\bm{\mathds{L}}^{-1}\right)\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R}).\end{split}

(SI 30)

In this study, the activity gradients are assumed to exist only in the
x
-direction. Since there is translational invariance in the
y
direction, the stationary density
\rho
has variations only along the
x
-direction. Thus, the stationary probability flux along the
x
-direction is given by:

\begin{split}J_{x}(x)=&-\frac{1}{(1+q)}\frac{T}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\rho(x)-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}f_{s}^{2}(x)\bm{\mathds{L}}_{xx}\partial_{x}\rho(x)\\
&-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{\rho(x)}{2}\bigg\{\bm{\mathds{L}}\big[(1-q)\bm{\mathds{1}}+(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}\big]\bigg\}_{xx}\partial_{x}(f_{s}(x)^{2})\\
=&-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\rho\partial_{x}\mathds{D}_{xx}-\mathds{D}_{xx}\partial_{x}\rho,\end{split}

(SI 31)

where we denoted with
\mathds{D}_{xx}
the
xx
element of the effective diffusion coefficient.
We define the quantity
\epsilon
as the
tactic parameter
, which determines the accumulation behavior of the active-passive composite. Particularly, for
\epsilon<0
, the composite accumulates in regions of high activity and vice-versa. Imposing a zero-flux condition along the direction of the activity gradient, we obtain the steady state density:

\rho(x)\propto\bigg[1+\dfrac{D_{R}}{D_{R}^{2}+\omega^{2}}\dfrac{1}{2\gamma T}\dfrac{1}{(1+q)}f_{s}^{2}(x)\bigg]^{-\displaystyle\epsilon/2},

(SI 32)

where the tactic parameter enters as the exponent, and is dependent on the chiral torque
\Omega
. Specifically, we get

\begin{split}\epsilon=1-q\dfrac{\big(1+\Omega^{2}\big)\big(1+\tau\big)}{\Omega^{2}+(1+\tau)^{2}},\end{split}

(SI 33)

where we have introduced the following non-dimensional parameters in units of the persistence time
\tau_{p}=D_{R}^{-1}
of the active particle due to rotational diffusion,

\Omega=\omega\tau_{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau=\frac{(1+q)k\tau_{p}}{q\gamma}.

.5.2
Infinitely stiff spring

This is implemented by taking the limit of the spring constant
k\rightarrow\infty
. The only term where the spring constant appears is
\bm{\mathds{B}}
and we can now evaluate it in this limit as:

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(1+q)\bm{\mathds{B}}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{q(1+q)k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\bigg(q\bm{\mathds{1}}+\dfrac{(1+q)k}{\gamma}\bm{\mathds{L}}\bigg)^{-1}=q\bm{\mathds{1}}.

(SI 34)

Note that in this limit, the spring force between the chiral active particle and the passive particle is so strong that the separation between them approximately remains the same as the spring rest length
l_{0}
, i.e.,
r^{\prime}\approx l_{0}
and so we can write:

\phi(\bm{R},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\pi l_{0}}\rho(\bm{R},t)\delta(r^{\prime}-l_{0}).

(SI 35)

Additionally, we can also Taylor-expand the activity field in the same way as Eq. (
SI 25
). With these steps,
\bm{J_{\sigma}}
now becomes

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg[f_{s}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho\int\differential\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi l_{0}}\delta(r-l_{0})+\rho f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\int\differential\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi l_{0}}\delta(r-l_{0})

\displaystyle-q\rho f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\int\differential\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi l_{0}}\delta(r-l_{0})+q\,\bm{\mathds{1}}f_{s}\nabla_{\bm{R}}f_{s}\rho\int\differential\bm{r}\frac{1}{2\pi l_{0}}\delta(r-l_{0})\bm{\hat{r}\hat{r}}\bigg]+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}^{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\bigg[f_{s}^{2}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho+\frac{\rho}{2}\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}^{2})-\frac{q\rho}{2}\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}^{2})+\frac{q\rho}{4}\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}^{2})\bigg]+\mathcal{O}(\nabla_{\bm{R}^{2}}),

(SI 36)

where we have used the normalization of the delta distribution and

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\differential\bm{r}\bm{\hat{r}\hat{r}}=\frac{\bm{\mathds{1}}}{2}.

(SI 37)

The total flux then becomes:

\displaystyle\bm{J}=

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{D}+\bm{J}_{\bm{\sigma}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(1+q)}\frac{T}{\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}f_{s}^{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}\rho

\displaystyle-\left(1-\frac{q}{2}\right)\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{\rho}{2}\bm{\mathds{L}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{R}}(f_{s}^{2}).

(SI 38)

Using this flux, we can write an effective drift-diffusion equation:

\bm{J}=\bm{V}(\bm{R})\rho(\bm{R})-\nabla_{\bm{R}}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathds{D}}(\bm{R})\rho(\bm{R})\right),

We again consider activity to be varying only along the
x
-direction with the stationary flux given by:

\begin{split}J_{x}(x)=&-\frac{1}{(1+q)}\frac{T}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\rho(x)-\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{D_{R}}{D_{R}^{2}+\omega^{2}}f_{s}^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\rho(x)\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{q}{2}\right)\frac{1}{(1+q)^{2}}\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\frac{D_{R}}{D_{R}^{2}+\omega^{2}}\partial_{x}(f_{s}^{2}(x))\\
=&-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\rho\partial_{x}\mathds{D}_{xx}-\mathds{D}_{xx}\partial_{x}\rho,\end{split}

(SI 39)

Similar to the previous case, we find that the effective drift and diffusion coefficient are related by a derivative relation.
Upon imposing a zero-flux condition, we obtain the same structure for the steady state density:

\rho(x)\propto\bigg[1+\dfrac{D_{R}}{D_{R}^{2}+\omega^{2}}\dfrac{1}{2\gamma T}\dfrac{1}{(1+q)}f_{s}^{2}(x)\bigg]^{-\displaystyle\epsilon/2},

(SI 40)

However, the tactic parameter
\epsilon
is now given by:

\epsilon=1-\frac{q}{2}.

(SI 41)

We see that the tactic parameter
\epsilon
is only dependent on the size of the passive particle via
q
.

.7
Simulation Details

The chiral active particle and the passive particle were simulated using Langevin dynamics: The equations of motion (eq. (1) in the main text) were first discretized up to linear order in the integration time-step using the Euler method. The increments at each time-step were then summed up using the Itó rule with a time-step size of
\differential t=D_{R}\times 10^{-5}
for the case of harmonic spring with zero rest length and
\differential t=5D_{R}\times 10^{-6}
for the case of the infinitely stiff spring. The simulation box size was chosen to be
L=100
with periodic boundary conditions. The activity field was also chosen to be periodic with sinusoidal variations.
