Abstract
A subset S S of vertices in a planar graph G G is a free set if, for every set P P of | S | |S| points in the plane, there exists a straight-line crossing-free drawing of G G in which vertices of S S are mapped to distinct points in P P . In this survey, we review • several equivalent definitions of free sets, • and applications of free sets in graph drawing. The survey concludes with a list of open problems in this still very active research area.

Introduction

In 2005, an online game called
Planarity
became wildly popular

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planarity

\Gamma
of a planar graph

is planar, the drawing

is not crossing-free; some pairs of edges cross each other, and an edge may even contain a vertex in its interior. Since
\Gamma

The act of moving some vertices in

\move(\Gamma)

2
2

2

The Planarity game actually measures the amount of time the player takes to accomplish this task, not the number of vertices moved.
Equivalently, what is the maximum number,

of vertices that can be kept fixed while untangling

\fix(G):=\min\{\fix(\Gamma):\text{$\Gamma$ is a straight-line drawing of $G$}\}

For a family

\fix_{\mathcal{F}}(n):=\min\{\fix(G):G\in\mathcal{F},\,|V(G)|=n\}
.
3
3

3

Technically

Determining the asymptotic growth of

\mathcal{G}
of planar graphs, the problem has been studied since

asked for a polynomial lower bound over

\fix_{\mathcal{G}}(n)\in\Omega(n^{1/4})\cap O(n^{0.4948})

.

In this survey, we study various kinds of vertex subsets of a planar graph
G
: proper-good sets, collinear sets, free-collinear sets, and free sets. Each of these definitions is, at first glance, more stringent than the one that precedes it. Indeed, it follows immediately from definitions that every free set is a free-collinear set, every free-collinear set is a collinear set, and every collinear set is a proper-good set. The definition of a proper-good set is the most relaxed, which makes it easy to find large proper-good sets, even by hand using a pencil and a paper that contains a crossing-free drawing of
G
. At the other extreme, free sets satisfy a very strong property that says we can place the vertices of a free set on any given pointset and then find locations for the other vertices of
G
that result in a non-crossing straight-line drawing of
G
. This makes free sets incredibly useful for many applications, including untangling.

A key result in this area, discovered over roughly 15 years, is that all of these definitions are equivalent: A vertex subset in a planar graph is proper-good if and only if it is collinear, if and only if it is free-collinear, if and only if it is free. Meanwhile, proper-good sets, collinear sets, free-collinear sets, and free sets were being used to resolve problems in graph drawing and related areas. With the benefit of hindsight, we attempt to organize and present two decades of results on free sets and their applications in a way that makes them as easy to understand and as useful as possible.

The rest of this survey is structured as follows: In
Section

2

Section

3

Section

4
we describe applications of free sets to a variety of graph drawing problems. In
Section

5
, we introduce a one-bend variant of free sets.
Section

6
concludes with a list of open problems and directions for further research.

Four Definitions of Free Sets

For definitions of standard graph theoretic terms and notations used in this survey (such a treewidth,
k
-trees, independent set, etc.) the reader is referred to the textbook by
Diestel 2018
. Throughout this survey, a
graph
is undirected, has no self-loops, and has no parallel edges, unless specified otherwise.

A
drawing
of a graph is a representation of the graph in which each vertex
v
is represented by a distinct point in the plane and each edge
vw
is represented by a simple open curve in the plane, the closure of which has
v
and
w
as endpoints. When discussing a particular drawing, we do not distinguish between a vertex and the point that represents it, or an edge and the curve that represents it. A drawing is a
straight-line
drawing if each edge is an open line segment. In a drawing, two edges may have a non-empty intersection, and an edge may contain a vertex that is not one of its endpoints. A drawing is a
crossing-free drawing
if this does not occur: any two distinct edges are disjoint and no vertex is contained in any edge.
4
4

4

What we call a crossing-free drawing is sometimes called a
plane
drawing or an embedding (in the plane). What we call a straight-line crossing-free drawing is sometimes called a
geometric embedding
or

.

Recall that for any
n

G_{0}
, on at least
3
vertices, it is possible to add edges to

to obtain a planar graph

with

is a

: in any crossing-free drawing of this graph, each of the faces has exactly three edges on its boundary. A
near-triangulation
is a biconnected embedded graph whose inner faces are all
triangles
, i.e., bounded by three edges. The
dual

G^{*}
of an embedded graph

is a graph whose vertices are the faces of
G
that contains an edge between two vertices
f
and
g
if these two faces of
G
have a common edge on their boundaries. For a planar graph
G
with outer face

, the

G^{+}
of

is defined as

, the graph obtained from the dual

by removing the vertex corresponding to the outer face
f_{0}
of

. A graph is

if it has a crossing-free drawing with all its vertices on the outer face. A graph equipped with such a crossing-free drawing is an

of a cycle

Under our definitions, a

graph is a graph that has a crossing-free drawing.
Fáry’s Theorem

[
59
,
35
,
54
]
asserts that a graph is planar if and only if it has a straight-line crossing-free drawing. Today, we use Fáry’s Theorem without thinking about it, but it is worthwhile spending a moment reflecting on what would happen if Fáry’s Theorem were not true. There would be two kinds of planar graphs, topological and straight-line, with the latter being a special case of the former. Any result proven for straight-line planar graphs would need a separate proof for topological planar graphs (assuming it was also true for topological planar graphs). Edge contractions, which obviously preserve topological planarity and are commonly used in inductive proofs for planar graphs, would be off-limits for proofs about straight-line planar graphs.

Indeed, there are a number of questions in which the difference between (not necessarily crossing-free) drawings and straight-line drawings of graphs is profound. A famous example of this is Conway’s
Thrackle Conjecture

[
60
]
, which asserts that if
G
has a drawing in which each pair of edges share exactly one point (possibly a common endpoint) then the number of edges of
G
is no more than the number of vertices of
G
. This conjecture has been open since the 1960s but the straight-line version of the question (the
Linear Thrackle Problem
) has an easy solution, first observed by Erdős
[
29
]
.

Let

5

Let

3.

We note that our definitions deviate from much of the literature by treating

is an

is an

and

, but there may be no permutation of

that is an ordered free set in

and in

A few properties of free sets are immediate from the definition, and we will use them throughout. If

is an ordered free set in a graph

•

the reversal of

is an ordered free set in

•

is an ordered free set in any subgraph of

•

Unordered collinear sets and unordered free-collinear sets were defined first by

6
6

6

Although they don’t use this terminology,

In fact,

Before continuing, we make a remark about pointsets whose

-coordinates are not all distinct, since the definition of (ordered) free set appears to disallow these. However, we claim that for any unordered free set

Next we turn to the relationship between free sets and the untangling problem in the introduction. In the untangling problem, we are given a straight line drawing
\Gamma
of
G
(with crossings) and the goal is to find a large set
F\subseteq V(G)
and a straight-line crossing-free drawing
\Gamma^{\prime}
of
G
such that
\Gamma^{\prime}(v)=\Gamma(v)
for each
v\in F
. Note that it is not sufficient to choose
F
to be a free set in
G
for the following reason: The definition of a free set would only guarantee that we can find a straight-line crossing-free drawing
\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\{\Gamma^{\prime}(v):v\in F\}=\{\Gamma(v):v\in F\}
. That is,
\Gamma^{\prime}
draws the vertices of
F
on the same point set
P:=\{\Gamma(v):v\in F\}
in both drawings, but may change the permutation that maps the vertices in
F
to points in
P
. The following lemma shows that free sets in
G
are nevertheless useful in obtaining lower bounds on
\fix(G)
. The proof of this lemma also illustrates the usefulness of definitions based on ordered sets. Another example, discussed in
Section

4
, is the problem of simultaneous embedding with mapping.

Lemma 1
.

Let
G
be a planar graph that has a free set of size at least
k
. Then
\fix(G)\geq\sqrt{k}
.

Proof.

Let
S
be an ordered free set in
G
of size
k
. Fix any straight-line drawing
\Gamma
of
G
and, without loss of generality (by a slight rotation, if necessary) assume that no two vertices of
\Gamma
have the same
{x}
-coordinate. Let
(S,\prec)
be the partial order in which
v\prec w
if and only if
v
appears before
w
in
S
and the
{x}
-coordinate of
v
in
\Gamma
is less than the
{x}
-coordinate of
w
in
\Gamma
.

By Dilworth’s Theorem,
(S,\prec)
contains a chain of length at least
\sqrt{k}
or
(S,\prec)
contains an antichain of size at least
\sqrt{k}
. First, suppose
(S,\prec)
contains a chain
v_{1}\prec\cdots\prec v_{\ell}
of length
\ell\geq\sqrt{k}
. Then
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})
is a free set since it is a subsequence of
S
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, let
(x_{i},y_{i})
be the coordinates given to
v_{i}
in
\Gamma
. By the definition of free set,
G
has a straight-line crossing-free drawing in which
v_{i}
is mapped to
(x_{i},y_{i})
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
. Therefore,
\fix(\Gamma)\geq\ell\geq\sqrt{k}
.

To deal with the case where
(S,\prec)
contains an antichain of size at least
\sqrt{k}
, we use the fact that the ordered set obtained by reversing the order of
S
is also a free set. Let
(S,\prec^{\prime})
be the partial order on the elements of
S
in which
v\prec^{\prime}w
if and only if
v
appears
after

w
in
S
and the
{x}
-coordinate of
v
in
\Gamma
is less than the
{x}
-coordinate of
w
in
\Gamma

(S,\prec)
form a chain in
(S,\prec^{\prime})
. Now we proceed as in the previous paragraph to show that
\fix(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{k}
.
Therefore
\fix(\Gamma)\geq\sqrt{k}
for any straight-line drawing
\Gamma
of
G
. Therefore
\fix(G)\geq\sqrt{k}
.

As discussed in the introduction, the following theorem provides the main motivation for this survey.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
be a planar graph and let
S
be an ordered subset of
V(G)
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

S
is an (ordered) proper-good set.

2.

S
is an (ordered) collinear set.

3.

S
is an (ordered) free-collinear set.

4.

S
is an (ordered) free set.

Note that, unlike the other three definitions, the definition of proper-good set is purely combinatorial/topological; it has no mention of straight-lines or vertex coordinates. In this respect,
1
plays a role similar to Fáry’s Theorem. Using the proper-good set definition allows purely combinatorial methods to be used in the search for free sets. For any proper-good set, there is a short easily-verifiable certificate (a crossing-free drawing of
G
and a description of the curve
C
) that
S
is indeed a proper-good set. In contrast, in order to show directly that
S
is a free-collinear set (or a free set) involves proving a fact that must hold for all choices of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
(or
(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{k},y_{k})
, respectively). Working with this definition directly would involve (at the very least) a significant amount of linear algebra.

Every collinear set is a free-collinear set:

The proof that every collinear set is a free-collinear set is due to

The proof of

If

and

that defines two trees

\Omega(|C|)

∎

If

is the dual of a triangulation

is the degree of the corresponding vertex in

Corollary 2
.

Let

be an
n
-vertex triangulation of maximum degree

be the dual of

. Then

has a free set of size
\Omega(c(G^{*})/\Delta^{4})
.

2

A result of

shows that one can add edges to any planar graph

of maximum degree

to obtain a triangulation

of maximum degree at most

. Combining this with the best known lower bounds on the circumference of cubic triconnected graphs

Corollary 3
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex planar graph of maximum degree

4
Applications

In this section, we discuss applications of free sets. For each of these (
s
) applications, and each of the (
t
) classes of planar graphs known to have large free sets, the use of free sets gives a result. Rather than providing an exhaustive list of

4.1
Untangling

As discussed in the introduction, the original application of free sets was untangling. By
1
, each lower bound (on the size of the largest free set in a graph family) obtained in
Sections

3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
, readily turns into the square root of the same bound for the untangling problem on that family. For example,
5
and
1
yield:

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the class of all planar graphs. For every
n
-vertex graph
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
,
\fix(G)\geq(n/2)^{1/4}
. In other words,
\fix_{\mathcal{G}}(n)\geq(n/2)^{1/4}
.

We do not know if this bound is asymptotically optimal.
Cano et al. 2014
proved that for the class of planar graphs
\mathcal{G}
,
\fix_{\mathcal{G}}(n)\in O(n^{0.4965})
.

In contrast, applying the known bounds on the largest free sets coupled with
1
gives tight bounds for some graph families. Specifically, the linear bounds on free sets in
Section

3.1
and
1
imply an
\Omega(n^{1/2})
untangling bound for these families.
Bose et al. 2009
showed that there is a class of trees, namely forests of stars,

, such that
\fix_{\mathcal{S}}\in O(n^{1/2})
. Thus the
\Omega(n^{1/2})
untangling bound is tight for all families in
Section

3.1
, except for the class of cubic triconnected planar graphs. The stars in
{\mathcal{T}}
have unbounded degree. Thus a tight bound on untangling cubic triconnected planar graphs is unknown.

4.2
Universal point subsets

A set of points
P
is
universal
for a set of planar graphs if every graph from the set has a straight-line crossing-free drawing where all of its vertices map to distinct points in
P
. Arguably, the most famous open problem in graph drawing, attributed to Bojan Mohar (1988), asks if there exists a constant
c
such that, for every
n
there exists a pointset of size
c\cdot n
that is universal for the class of all
n
-vertex planar graphs. It is known that for all large enough
n
, no universal pointset of size
n
exists for the class of
n
-vertex planar graphs – as first proved by
de Fraysseix et al. 1990
. The same authors also proved that the
O(n)\times O(n)
integer grid is universal for all
n
-vertex planar graphs and thus universal pointsets of size
O(n^{2})
exist. Currently the best known lower bound on the size of a smallest universal pointset for
n
-vertex planar graphs
is
1.239n-o(n)

[
52
]

and the best known upper bound is
n^{2}/4-O(n)

[
5
]
. Closing the gap between
\Omega(n)
and
O(n^{2})
is a major, and likely very difficult, graph drawing problem, open since 1988
[
33
,
34
]
.

Various related notions have been introduced and studied in the literature with the aim of better understanding the universal pointset problem. A set
P
of
k
points in the plane is a
universal point subset
for a family of planar graphs, if for every graph
G
in the family, there exists a straight-line crossing-free drawing in which
k
vertices of
G
are placed at the
k
points in
P
. Universal point subsets were introduced by
Angelini et al. 2012a
. They proved that a particular convex chain of
\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
points is a universal point subset for the class of all
n
-vertex planar graphs.
Di Giacomo et al. 2012
then proved that every set of
\Omega(\log n)
points, as well as, every set of
\lceil n^{1/3}\rceil
points in convex position is a universal point subset for the class of
n
-vertex planar graphs.

The following lemma and its consequences, first observed by
Dujmović 2017
, is immediate from the definition of free sets.

Lemma 9
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and
\mathcal{F}
a family of planar graphs such that every graph in the family has a free set of size at least
k
. Then every set of
k
points in the plane is a universal point subset for
\mathcal{F}
.

Note that this lemma does not just prove that there exists a set of
k
points that is a universal point subset for
\mathcal{G}
, it proves that
every
set of
k
points is a universal point subset for
\mathcal{G}
. This distinction turns out to be useful in other applications. Combining

and
5
gives:

Corollary 5
.

Every set of
\lceil\sqrt{n/2}\rceil
points is a universal point subset for the class of
n
-vertex planar graphs.

It has been known for a long time that every set of
n
points in general position is a universal point (sub)set for the family of
n
-vertex outerplanar graphs
[
41
,
15
,
22
]
.

implies that all the families studied in
Section

3.1
, also admit universal point subsets of linear size. This includes, for example, planar graphs of treewidth at most
3
by
2
, a strict superclass of the class of outerplanar graphs.

4.3
Simultaneous Geometric Embeddings

Simultaneous geometric embeddings were introduced by

Braß et al. 2007
. Since then, there has been a plethora of work on the subject on many variants of the problem– see, for example,
a survey by
Bläsius et al. 2013
. Common variants of the problem include those in which the mapping between the vertices of the two graphs is given and those in which the mapping is not given.

4.3.1
Without Mapping

A sequence of graphs
G_{1},G_{2},\dots,G_{r}
are said to have a
simultaneous geometric embedding without mapping (SGE-nomap)
if there exists a pointset
P
of size
\max\{|V(G_{1})|,\ldots,|V(G_{r})|\}
such that
each of
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
has a straight-line crossing-free drawing where all of its vertices are mapped to distinct points in
P
. The following well known and still wildly open problem was asked by
Braß et al. 2007
in 2003, and is also listed among selected list of graph drawing problems in
[
18

n
-vertex planar graphs have a SGE-nomap (for every positive integer
n

not
to be true when “pair” (that is,
r=2
) is replaced by a bigger constant
[
21
,
52
]
, currently being
r=30

[
55
]
. More generally,
Steiner 2023
showed that for every large enough

there exists a sequence of at most
(3+o(1))\log_{2}(n)

-vertex planar graphs that do not have a SGE-nomap.

While the problem of
Braß et al. 2007
still seems to be out of reach, in a different direction

write: “What is the largest

such that every

-vertex planar graph and every

k
-vertex planar graph admit a geometric simultaneous embedding without mapping? Surprisingly, we are not aware of any super-constant lower bound for the value of

Dujmović 2017
, answered their question with the help of free sets, as detailed in the next lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Let
G_{1}
be a planar graph with a free set of size at least
k

G_{2}
be a planar graph on at most

and
G_{2}
admit SGE-nomap.

Proof.

By Fáry’s theorem,
G_{2}
has a straight-line crossing-free drawing on some set,

|V(G_{2})|\leq k
points. Since
G_{1}
has a free set

of size at least
k

G_{1}
has a straight-line crossing-free drawing where
|P_{2}|
vertices of

are mapped to distinct points in

. Consider now the set of points,

, defined by the vertices in the drawing of
G_{1}
. This set is our desired pointset as it is a set of
|V(G_{1})|
points such that each of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
has a straight-line crossing-free drawing where all of its vertices are mapped to distinct points of
P
.
∎

10
and

immediately imply the following, aforementioned result.

Corollary 6

For every
n
and every
k\leq\sqrt{n/2}
, every
n
-vertex planar graph and every
k
-vertex planar graph admit a SGE-nomap.

One can combine the many lower bounds (on the size of the largest free set in a graph family) obtained in

,

to obtain results of similar flavour to

-vertex planar graph and every

Note that universal point subsets of size

do not imply results on SGE-nomap, akin to

The

Let

4.4
Column planarity

Given a planar graph
G
, a set
R\subseteq V(G)
is

in

if the vertices of
R
can be assigned distinct
{x}
-coordinates such that for an assignment of
{y}
-coordinates to the vertices in
R
such that the resulting

points have no three on a line, there exists a straight-line crossing-free drawing of
G
in which each vertex

is placed to its assigned coordinates.
R
is
strongly column planar
if the “no three on a line condition” is removed from the definition. The following lemma is immediate from these definition and

:

Lemma 13
.

A (sub)set of vertices of a planar graph is a strongly column planar set if and only if it is an unordered free set.

Being strongly column planar implies being column planar, thus all the bounds from

,

and

implies that every

-vertex planar graph has a column planar set of size

implies that every

; and so on.

Column planar sets were introduced by

motivated by applications to partial simultaneous geometric embeddings. Notions similar to column planarity were studied by
Estrella-Balderrama et al. 2009
and

.

Barba et al. 2017
proved that

is not strongly column planar.
