OARelatedWork: A Large-Scale Dataset of Related Work Sections with Full-texts from Open Access Sources
Martin Docekal, Martin Fajcik, Pavel Smrz

Abstract
This paper introduces OARelatedWork 1 1 1 The code and data are available at https://github.com/KNOT-FIT-BUT/OAPapers . , the first large-scale multi-document summarization dataset for related work generation containing whole related work sections and full-texts of cited papers. The dataset includes 94 450 papers and 5 824 689 unique referenced papers. It was designed for the task of automatically generating related work to shift the field toward generating entire related work sections from all available content instead of generating parts of related work sections from abstracts only, which is the current mainstream in this field for abstractive approaches. We show that the estimated upper bound for extractive summarization increases by 217% in the ROUGE-2 score, when using full content instead of abstracts. Furthermore, we show the benefits of full content data on naive, oracle, traditional, and transformer-based baselines. Long outputs, such as related work sections, pose challenges for automatic evaluation metrics like BERTScore due to their limited input length. We tackle this issue by proposing and evaluating a meta-metric using BERTScore. Despite operating on smaller blocks, we show this meta-metric correlates with human judgment, comparably to the original BERTScore.



1
Introduction

Related work generation is a growing subfield of multi-document summarization. The related work section is not a plain summary of referenced papers, since the proper related work section is positioning a given work in the context of related papers, discussing the differences and similarities.
This places significant demands on models, generating related work sections, and datasets for training those models. As shown in Figure
1
, a model needs to process large text input consisting of a target paper and all references to be cited. A dataset has to include all the necessary data, which might be challenging, as some scientific papers are behind paywalls. Furthermore, even when they are available, it is hard to automatically process them, as they are usually in PDF format. Fortunately, there are existing tools for conversion to more machine-friendly formats. For example, GROBID
GRO 2008–2023
, which was specifically designed to process scientific papers and corresponds to the state-of-the-art in its field
Meuschke et al. 2023
.

Although the pioneering work of
Hoang and Kan 2010
originally used full-text references and a topic hierarchy tree for target papers, nowadays only abstracts are usually used
Lu et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
, because abstractive summarization of long inputs is technically demanding, given the constraints of existing models. Furthermore, the task of generation of related work is commonly divided into the generation of separate paragraphs. This makes the task easier, but also, such a simplification makes it impossible to assess whether a model is able to generate a well-structured related work section, as it only benchmarks models on a short sub-summary.

Generating whole related work is not demanding just for a generator but also for an evaluation metric. It is still an open problem how to properly automatically evaluate summarization
Fabbri et al. 2020
, and this problem becomes even more difficult when it comes to long-form summarization.

This work addresses the mentioned issues by introducing a large-scale dataset (Section
2.2
) for generating whole related work sections, providing automatically processed full-text of cited and target papers. To overcome the difficulties with embedding-based evaluation metrics limited in length, such as BERTScore
Zhang et al. 2020
, we suggest and evaluate a meta-metric for the evaluation of long-form summaries (Section
4
). We created several baseline models and used them to analyze the benefits of different types of content (Section
6.2
). Using the full inputs, we found that the models are able to utilize this extra context and produce significantly better results. The PRIMERA
Xiao et al. 2022
baseline improved from 0.08 ROUGE-2 when using just abstracts to 0.15 ROUGE-2 when using all available data.

2.1.1
Bibliography Linking

We decided to perform bibliography linking for two reasons. First, we used a data source containing documents without identified bibliography (CORE). The second reason is to enrich existing links.

For this task, we used MAG, the Semantic Scholar citation graph, and our own searcher. We consider Semantic Scholar as our main source; however, during processing we discovered that we can obtain additional links using also MAG.

We used our own searcher even though its contribution was marginal. The number of identified bibliographic entries increased only by 0.6%, which is about 11.60 million new links. Our search is done in multiple stages. The first stage is candidate retrieval. It consists of a string-match search on document titles using a SQLite database. We allow different additional postfixes for the matched title. Additionally, approximate matching is performed. It is looking for the k-nearest neighbors in the vector space of embeddings created using the feature locality-sensitive hashing
Weinberger et al. 2010
. We used this method because of its low computational complexity. The last step is the filtering stage.

The filter works with title, authors, and year of a record. The title and authors fields are mandatory. If the year field is known for both records that are supposed to be matched, there cannot be more than a two-year difference. Following
Lo et al. 2020
, we calculate the similarity score for a
title
as the harmonic mean of the Jaccard index and containment metric:

C=\frac{|N_{1}\cap N_{2}|}{\min(|N_{1}|,|N_{2}|)}\,,

(1)

where
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are word sets from matched strings, which are also used for the Jaccard index.

Only titles with similarity above the
0.75
threshold are considered as a match. When matching
authors
, we require that at least one pair of authors have a match. The match is again done using a similarity metric (using the same threshold), but now it is just the containment metric because we want to obtain high similarity even when there is just, e.g., a surname in one source. We observe that it is often the case that the names are expressed using initials in one source and the full form in the other. For that reason, we also create an initial version of each name and consider it when matching.

For similarity computation via Jaccard / containment metric and string-match search, we first normalize the title and authors (authors are not used for string-match search) by converting the string into its close ASCII form using Unidecode

To evaluate our search, we decided to perform a manual evaluation of the results and found that the search was correct in 47 of 50 cases.

2.1.2
Content Hierarchy

In the sources used, the documents were already parsed into sections and paragraphs. Nevertheless, we decided to add more levels to this shallow hierarchy, as we believe that it will increase the usability of our data. Ideally, we would like a document divided into sections, subsections, paragraphs, and sentences. However, parsing subsections is a very difficult task, as it is often the case that section headlines from existing resources are already not parsed correctly.

To parse subsections, we use the numbering present in a headline. Our method is applied to the list of headlines, some of which might be subsections. It first tries to create an internal numbering by guessing section numbers for headlines with missing numbers by exploiting writing habits and format. See Figure
2
for an example of conversion. Then comes identifying the longest sparse
7
7

7

We allow at most 3 missing numbers in between.
sequence of consecutive numberings (which we call the anchor). Then, it fits the rest of the headlines into a constructed hierarchy, as in the example in Figure
2
where the non-bold headlines are put in the
II.3 metrics
section. The algorithm leaves the original, shallow hierarchy if it fails. The method stays with the original when the anchor consists of less than three elements or when the numbering is not matching levelwise. For example, assume that the previous subsection was 2.3.1 and that section 3.5 follows. It also considers the numeric format; thus e.g., for previous subsection 2.3.1, the following subsection 2.3.B would be considered problematic. Because an exhaustive description of the conversion would be beyond the scope of this paper, we direct the reader to investigate our code.

We manually analyzed our subsection parser on 100 papers. As we wanted only to evaluate the subsection parser, we only considered the provided headlines from original corpora and not the true section headlines from the original PDF. We saw that for 20 papers the algorithm failed and left the original shallow hierarchy. From the rest of the 80 papers, we considered 89% parsed correctly. Our analysis also showed that 55% of these 80 papers did not use subsections.

To perform sentence segmentation, we used the scispaCy model
Neumann et al. 2019
. It was trained on papers from the biomedical domain, so it is more suitable for our case as standard (web) models usually have problems with this type of document. One of the problems with standard models is that the citation spans are misclassified as sentence boundaries.

2.1.3
Citation Spans

In our data, we leave the citation spans in their original form, except for the merged citations (e.g., [3-6]). We decided to expand these to each individual citation, as we are providing character offsets to identify the citation span. Otherwise, it would mean that two citation spans could be in the same position.

The original sources already provide parsed citation spans. However, we decided to also employ our own simple parser for Harvard-style citations. It is a regular expression searcher that checks whether the information in searched spans corresponds to at least a single bibliographic entry. The matching is done on author and year. The year must match exactly, and the matching of the author is done in the same way as described in Section
2.1.1
. This step is done after bibliography identification. We found an additional 13.66 million citation spans, which is a
1.8\%
increase.

To create a suitable dataset for the generation of related work sections, we search in our corpus for documents containing related work-like section. This means that we are not looking just for sections with the
“Related Work”
headline, but also, e.g.,
“Background”
,

“Background”

“Introduction”

In this way, we were able to find more than half a million documents with related work-like sections. However, we decided to filter them further to increase the quality of our final set. Our pipeline filters out each related work that violates the following conditions:

•

Contains at least three sentences.

•

Must cite at least two documents.

•

Every citation group in the related work section must have at least one linked document.

We define a citation group as a sequence of subsequent citation spans (e.g.,
[1][4][6]
). The condition to groups is used because having full coverage of cited papers is unrealistic in an open-access setup. We believe that having at least one representative of each group is a good approximation, as it is often the case that citations in a group are cited for similar reasons.

We also make sure that every target document, for which the related work is generated, and all cited documents have an abstract.

In this way we obtain 94 450 papers with related work section and 5 824 689 unique cited papers in selected related work sections.

Of all of these unique cited papers, 82% are abstract-only papers. As we want to provide a dataset that is suitable for evaluation of models on full-texts we decided to make sure that every related work section in validation and test set splits has all cited documents with full-text. To achieve that, we found all documents satisfying this condition and used 50% of them as our test set, 30% of them as our validation set, and we assigned the rest to the train set.

However, as can be seen in Table
1
, this way of splitting caused that train set contains longer targets, as it is more probable to cover all citations in shorter text, because shorter related works have less citations on average. Nevertheless, this does not present fundamental problem given our task definitions (see Section
3

Y=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{L})

A system is provided with input

We distinguish the following inputs:

•

Full-texts of cited papers and full-text of target paper (w/o the related work section).

•

Full-texts of cited papers.

It calculates a matrix

5
Related Work

5.1
Datasets

Multiple datasets exist that differ in input types, task definitions, and sample numbers.

The very first work
Hoang and Kan 2010
introduced the related work generation task with a dataset of 20 papers. The dataset consisted of related work summary references, full-texts of cited articles, and topic trees. The task, in this case, was to generate a whole related work section.

However, for abstractive approaches, it is more difficult to generate long text and process long inputs. This leads to a creation of datasets focusing on just abstract-only inputs used for generating single paragraph summaries
Lu et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
. A comparison with our data is available in Table
1
.

Some works focus on citation text generation, which is the task of generating a sentence (or multiple sentences) citing a given reference. Various inputs are being used, like the title and abstract of cited paper
AbuRa’ed et al. 2020
or context of citation sentence and cited abstract
Xing et al. 2020
. Recently,
Funkquist et al. 2023
released
CiteBench
to unify existing citation text datasets.

Our work goes in a different way, as our dataset aims to provide all the available sources to generate whole related work section. We believe that for that reason, we need to go beyond abstracts, when possible, and also provide the rest of the target paper to allow us to generate the contrasting parts of related work sections (paragraphs like this one).

5.2
Evaluation

As the
related work
generation is a
summarization
task
Hoang and Kan 2010
, it uses commonly used metrics in this field. The evaluation of summarization is difficult task, even when having human ratings, as the inter-annotator agreement tends to be small, especially when non-experts are involved
Fabbri et al. 2020
;
Gillick and Liu 2010
.

The most widely used metric is ROUGE
Lin 2004
, which comes with many variants such as ROUGE-N-F1 and ROUGE-L. The ROUGE-N is based on the lexical overlap of n-grams. The ROUGE-L uses the length of the longest common subsequence. There are many other lexical overlap-based metrics, e.g., BLEU
Papineni et al. 2002
or METEOR
Lavie and Agarwal 2007
. Nevertheless, all have the obvious drawback of not matching semantically the same texts in different text forms. This might be mitigated by having multiple references, which might be hard to obtain, or by considering synonyms, such as in the METEOR case.

Another stream of work is not based on lexical overlap but uses language models instead. The BERTScore
Zhang et al. 2020
uses contextual BERT
Devlin et al. 2019
embeddings to obtain cosine similarity between candidate and reference tokens and then aggregates them into the final score. On the other hand, the BARTScore
Yuan et al. 2021
is formulating the evaluation as a text generation task, where the score is the log probability of the target sequence given input sequence. Depending on what is target and source sequence it can be used to obtain faithfulness, precision, recall, or
F_{1}
. The problem of those metrics is that they have a limit on input length (512 tokens for BERT), which is problematic for our task. Thus, we decided to construct a meta-metric, described in Section
4.1
, operating on smaller blocks and aggregating the results.

6
Experiments

We investigate several baselines on our dataset and ablate the impact of different types of information-rich inputs. These cover naive and oracle approaches, traditional systems, and transformer-based models.

6.1
Models

6.1.1
LEAD

LEAD selects the first
n
sentences from each document, therefore from abstract. In our experiments we selected only the first sentence from each document and also add citation span referring to document from which the sentence was extracted from.

Such a baseline is useful to see how salient is the beginning of a document. It is expected that in the domain of scientific literature the beginning has high saliency because of the abstract section.

6.1.2
Greedy Oracle

The Greedy Oracle (GO) is an extractive method that greedily selects sentences, maximizing a given metric, in our case, ROUGE-2-F1. It is an estimation of the upper bound for extractive models. The greedy selection strategy is the same as described in
Nallapati et al. 2017
.

In our experiments we use greedy extractive oracle as a standalone summarizer and also we use it in combination with other models. This allows as to test neural network based model on long input types and analyze usefulness of additional content.

There is nuance when using Greedy Extractive Oracle as a standalone summarizer and when we use it together with another model. In the former case we create one summary from all sentences in all given documents and in the latter case we create sub-summaries for each document separately.

6.1.3
TextRank

TextRank
Mihalcea and Tarau 2004
is a traditional extractive graph-based summarizer. It establishes a weighted graph with nodes representing each sentence and edge weights representing similarity between two sentences. The similarity is based on length-normalized lexical overlap. It than runs ranking algorithm on constructed graph and selects top sentences as a summary.

We use the TextRank separately for each input document and set the approximate target length of each sub-summary to be a fair part of the ground truth summary length.

For each sub-summary, we add a citation span referring to the document from which the sub-summary was created.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

The dataset proposed here was made to shift the generation of related work to the more realistic scenario as we define the task as generating the whole section, not just a paragraph. Our work goes beyond abstracts and shows benefits of using additional context, and in the same time brings challenges of processing very large inputs and generating long summaries.

We also excluded some author-subjective aspects, such as the length of the related work or dividing the related work into subsections. Training models using suggested setup, using length and structure information as input, may also lead to more user friendly tools that allow to set those properties.

Our work also proposes and evaluates a meta-metric
BlockMatch
that is useful with combination of neural based metrics, which can’t fit whole context to their input.

In future work, this dataset can also be used to train retrieval-augmented models, which would be used for selecting related work papers.

8
Limitations and Ethical Considerations

The presented dataset is limited to open-access papers, mainly from computer science domains. Our dataset is only focusing on text data, which might be limiting when the essential information is, e.g., in a plot. The automatic processing of documents is imperfect and the errors made during it might miss or change information contained in a paper.

The baseline models used are intended for professionals, and their outputs should be considered critically. Our resources were limited, and we haven’t checked the performance of commercial models like GPT-4
OpenAI et al. 2024
.
