External columns and chambers of vector partition functions
Stefan Trandafir

Abstract
. The vector partition function p A p_{A} associated to a d × n d\times n matrix A A with integer entries is the function ℤ d → ℕ \mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N} defined by 𝐛 → # ⁡ { 𝐱 ∈ ℕ n : A ​ 𝐱 = 𝐛 } \mathbf{b}\to\#\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\} . It is known that vector partition functions are piecewise quasi-polynomials whose domains of quasi-polynomiality are maximal cones (chambers) of a fan called the chamber complex of A A . In this article we introduce external columns and external chambers of vector partition functions. Our main result is that (up to a saturation condition) the quasi-polynomial associated to a chamber containing external columns arises from a vector partition function with k k fewer equations and variables. In the case that the chamber is external – that is, when the number of external columns in a chamber is as large as possible without being trivial – the quasi-polynomial arises from a coin exchange problem. By exploiting this we are able to obtain a determinantal formula, characterize when the quasi-polynomial is polynomial, and show that in this case it is actually given by a negative binomial coefficient. We then apply these results to the enumeration of loopless multigraphs satisfying some degree conditions. Finally, we suggest a generalization to a result of Baldoni and Vergne for polynomials arising from chambers that we call semi-external chambers .



1.
Introduction

The vector partition enumeration problem is the natural
d
-dimensional analogue of the well-studied coin exchange problem (see for example
[
BeRo15
, Chapter 1]
). More precisely, for some full rank
d\times n
matrix
A
with integer entries, the number of vector partitions of
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the number of solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
of the equation
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
. If this number is finite for all
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we say that the function
p_{A}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
yielding the number of vector partitions of
\mathbf{b}
for all
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the vector partition function associated to the matrix
A
.

Vector partition functions appear in many different contexts with applications in statistics, representation theory, algebraic geometry, and more. More concretely, the enumeration of Contingency Tables
[
DeSt03
]
, the computation of Littlewood-Richardson coefficients
[
Ra04
]
, and of Kronecker coefficients
[
MiRoSu21
]
, constitute some examples where vector partition functions play a major role.

It is known that the vector partition function is a piecewise quasi-polynomial whose domains of quasi-polynomiality are the maximal cones (chambers) of a fan
[
Stur94
]
. In this article we introduce the notion of
external columns
of
A
and
external chambers
of
A
. Our main result (Theorem
4.2
) is that (up to a saturation condition) one may compute the quasi-polynomial for a chamber
\gamma
(henceforth denoted
p_{A}^{\gamma}
) containing external columns by computing a quasi-polynomial for a corresponding chamber in a simpler vector partition function. More precisely, if there are
k
external columns of
A
in
\gamma
, then the quasi-polynomial arises from a vector partition function
p_{B}
for some matrix
B
with
k
fewer rows and columns than
A
.

In the case that
\gamma
is an external chamber (contains the maximal amount of external columns without being trivial), Theorem
4.2
implies that the quasi-polynomial associated to
\gamma
arises from a coin exchange problem. Through this observation we are able characterize when the quasi-polynomial associated to
\gamma
is actually polynomial. Moreover, in these instances the formulae are particularly simple: they are given by negative binomial coefficients (Theorem
5.4
). Unimodular matrices satisfy the required saturation conditions, and so we immediately obtain results for two notions of unimodularity (Corollaries
5.7
and
5.10
).

By identifying external chambers in a family of vector partition fuctions arising from the enumeration of multigraphs, we are able to obtain explicit counting formulae for loopeless multigraphs satisfying certain degree requirements (Theorem
6.4
).

We also general set of chambers (of which the external chambers take part) that we call
semi-external chambers
and suggest a generalization of a result of Baldoni & Vergne (Conjecture
) related to linear factors of polynomials of
p_{A}
. This could potentially be used to better understand the vector partition functions associated to the enumeration of multigraphs, as well as in the analysis of certain polynomials associated to the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients.

The article is organized as follows: in Section
2
we recall background on cones, fans, and vector partition functions. In Section
3
, we introduce external columns and chambers and derive some useful properties associated to them. Next, in Section
4
, we derive our main result: Theorem
4.2
. In Section

5.1
), as well as to characterize when the quasi-polynomial associated to such a chamber is actually polynomial, in which case we show that it is actually given by a negative binomial coefficient (Theorem
5.4
). We further show that this formula can be simplified if the given matrix is unimodular (Corollary
5.10
). In Section
6
we give an application of our work to the enumeration of multigraphs. In Section
7
we prove the main ingredient of Theorem
4.2
– a technical result: Lemma
4.1
. In Section
we consider a more general set of chambers (of which the external chambers take part) that we call
semi-external chambers
. Finally, in Section
we discuss possible generalizations and applications of our work.

Background

2.1.
Cones

We mostly follow Cox-Little-Schenck
[
CoLiSc11
]
and Fulton
[
Fu93
]
in this section. If the reader is familiar with cones, we advise them to read Remark
2.1
before skipping to the following section.

A
convex polyhedral cone
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a set of the form

\sigma=\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k})=\{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mathbf{u}_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}:\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{k}\geq 0\}

and we say that
\sigma
is
generated
by
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k}
, and that
\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k}
are
generators
of
\sigma
.
We also define
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\emptyset)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. The
dimension
of cone
\sigma
is the dimension of the subspace generated by
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
.
Let
\sigma
be a convex polyhedral cone in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that the set

\sigma^{\vee}:=\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{u}\geq 0\hbox{ for all }\mathbf{u}\in\sigma\}

is the
dual cone of
\sigma
. Note that although it is not immediately obvious from the defintion,
\sigma^{\vee}
is also a convex polyhedral cone. A convex polyhedral cone
\sigma
is a
pointed convex polyhedral cone
(or
strongly convex polyhedral cone
) if it contains no
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\sigma
is
rational
if it can be generated by a finite number of integer points. In general in this article, we deal with rational strongly convex polyhedral cones, so we simply call these
cones
for short.

For
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbf{m}\neq\mathbf{0}
, let

H_{\mathbf{m}}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{u}=0\}

and

H_{\mathbf{m}}^{+}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{u}\geq 0\}.

We say that
H_{\mathbf{m}}
is a
supporting hyperplane
of a cone
\sigma
if
\sigma\subseteq H_{\mathbf{m}}^{+}
, and in this case we also say that
H_{\mathbf{m}}^{+}
is a
supporting half-space
of
\sigma
. A
face
of cone
\sigma
is a set of the form
H_{\mathbf{m}}\cap\sigma
. A
ray
(or
edge
) of
\sigma
is a face of dimension 1 and a
facet
of
\sigma
is a face of codimension 1. We also use the term ray on its own to refer to any
1
-dimensional cone. Any proper face of
\sigma
is the intersection of all the facets containing it.

Given a ray
r
, we say that
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
is a
ray generator
of
r
if
r=\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{w})
. If additionally,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\gcd(\{w_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\})=1
, then we say that
\mathbf{w}
is the minimal ray generator of
r
. Any set of generators of a cone
\sigma
contains some minimal subset of generators, which still generate
\sigma
. This subset consists of exactly the vectors which generate the edges of
\sigma
. We call such a subset a
minimal generating set
of
\sigma
, and call its elements
ray generators of
\sigma
. If a minimal generating set
S
of
\sigma
additionally has the property that each element is a minimal ray generator, then we say that
S
is the set of
minimal ray generators
of
\sigma
. Each cone
\sigma
has a unique set of minimal ray generators. We say that
\sigma
is
simplicial
if its set of ray generators is linearly independent.

Remark 2.1
.

We remark here that we have deviated slightly from the notation of
[
CoLiSc11
]
and
[
Fu93
]
. In
[
Fu93
]
what we call “ray generators” are called “minimal generators”. In
[
CoLiSc11
]
what we refer to as the “minimal ray generator” is simply called the “ray generator”, and what we call “ray generators” of

are called “minimal generators” of
\sigma
.
Our choice is dictated by our introduction of the terms “external ray generators” and “minimal external ray generators” for which either notation scheme (i.e that of
[

]
or
[
Fu93
]
) would cause confusion.

For a cone
\sigma
with supporting hyperplane
H
, we say that
\mathbf{u}
is an
inner facet normal
of
\sigma
if
\mathbf{u}\in H^{+}
is normal to
H
, and that
\mathbf{u}
is an
outer facet normal
of
\sigma
if
\mathbf{u}\in H^{-}
is normal to
H
. The following result is given in
[

, Proposition 1.2.8]
and the discussion appearing immediately after Proposition 1.2.8.

Proposition 2.2

(Cox-Little-Schenck, Proposition 1.2.8)
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a polyhedral cone such that
\sigma=H_{\mathbf{m}_{1}}^{+}\cap H_{\mathbf{m}_{2}}^{+}\cap\dots\cap H_{\mathbf{m}_{s}}^{+}
. Then
\sigma^{\vee}=\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{s})
. In particular,
\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{s}
are the inner facet normals of

if and only if
\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{s}

\sigma^{\vee}
.

A
fan

\Sigma
is a set of cones such that if
\sigma\in\Sigma
, then every face of
\sigma
is in
\Sigma
, and the intersection of
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma

and
\sigma_{2}

.

2.2.
Vector partition functions

Here we give a brief primer on vector partition functions, generally following the notation of
[
Stur94
]
. My
\mathbb{N}
we denote the set of non-negative integers
\{0,1,2,\dots,\}
. Throughout this document
A
will denote a
d\times n
matrix of rank
d
with integer entries and

\ker(A)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

We begin by recalling the definition of the vector partition function.

Definition 2.3
.

The
vector partition function of
A

p_{A}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}

is defined by

p_{A}(\mathbf{b}):=\#\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\}.

One can view this problem as enumerating the number of “partitions” of the vector
\mathbf{b}
whose parts are the columns of
A
– hence the name vector partition function.

Remark 2.4
.

Recall that the condition
\ker(A)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
is imposed so that
p_{A}
is indeed a function. Otherwise we may have that
p_{A}(\mathbf{b})
is not finite for some
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Given a matrix
M
with columns
\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{k}
, we define the
cone associated to
M
, denoted
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
to be the set
\{\lambda_{1}\mathbf{m}_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mathbf{m}_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{k}\mathbf{m}_{k}:\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\geq 0\}
. In words,
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
is the cone generated by the columns of
M
.
1
1

1

Some authors use
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(M)
to indicate the cone generated by the rows of
M
.

For each
s\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, we define
A_{s}
to be the submatrix of
A
composed of the columns of
s
. For each
s
satisfying
|s|~=~\operatorname{rank}(A_{s})~=~\operatorname{rank}(A)~=~d
, we say that
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
is a
simplicial cone of
A
. The
chamber complex
of
A
is the fan obtained as the common refinement of the simplicial cones of
A
(viewed as fans).
Explicitly, the chamber complex is the set of cones

\left\{\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}\\
|s|~=~\operatorname{rank}(A_{s})~=~d\\
\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})\end{subarray}}\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s}):\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\right\}

along with all of their faces. In words, for each
\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, form the cone obtained by taking the intersection of all simplicial cones containing
\mathbf{b}
. The chamber complex is exactly the set of cones obtained along with their faces.

The cones of maximal dimension of the chamber complex are called
geometrical chambers
– we call them
chambers
for short
2
2

2

In the literature chambers are usually defined to be the interiors of what we call chambers (i.e the maximal cells of the chamber complex). We use this terminology since we are, much more often than not, interested in the closed sets and not their interiors.
. Equivalently, these are the cones of dimension
d
of the chamber complex.

We list some facts about the chamber complex that we exploit throughout this thesis. These facts can be derived directly from the definition of the chamber complex.

(1)

Any chamber is exactly the intersection of all simplicial cones containing it.

(2)

For all
\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, the intersection of all simplicial cones containing
\mathbf{b}
(
\operatorname{cone}(\mathbf{b})
) is a cone of the chamber complex – in particular if the intersection is
d
-dimensional, it is a chamber.

(3)

For a given chamber
\gamma
, if
\mathbf{b}\in\gamma^{\circ}
(where
\gamma^{\circ}
denotes the interior of
\gamma
), then
\gamma
is the intersection of all simplicial cones containing
\mathbf{b}
.

(4)

If a chamber
\gamma
of
A
intersects a simplicial cone
\sigma
of
A

d
-dimensionally, then
\gamma\subseteq\sigma
.

(5)

Let
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. If column
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is in a chamber
\gamma
, then
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is a ray generator of
\gamma.

The vector partition function
p_{A}
can be described explicitly as a piecewise function whose domains are the chambers of
A
. The functions that are valid on the chambers are
quasi-polynomials
, which are finite sums of the form

q(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in S}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})z_{1}^{i_{1}},\dots,z_{k}^{i_{k}}

where
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is finite and the
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
are non-zero periodic functions in
(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
. That is, there exist positive integers
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
such that
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=c_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}(z^{\prime}_{1},\dots,z^{\prime}_{k})
whenever
z_{\ell}\equiv z^{\prime}_{\ell}\mod n_{\ell}
for
\ell=1,\dots,k
.
The
degree
of the quasi-polynomial
q
is the maximum over the sums
i_{1}+\dots+i_{k}
, and the
period
of
q
is the minimal positive integer
N
such that
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=c_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})
whenever
i_{\ell}\equiv j_{\ell}\mod N
for
\ell=1,\dots,k
for all
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in S
. We remark that a quasi-polynomial with period equal to one is just a polynomial.
To indicate the quasi-polynomial associated to a chamber
\gamma
, we write
p_{A}^{\gamma}
.

The explicit characterization of the form of the vector partition function is due to Sturmfels, and we record it in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.5

(Sturmfels, 1994
[
Stur94
]
)
.

Let
A
be a
d\times n
matrix of rank
d
.
The vector partition function of
A
,
p_{A}
, is a piecewise quasi-polynomial of degree
n-d
whose domains of quasi-polynomiality are the maximal cones (chambers) in the chamber complex of
A
.

For each choice of
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the evaluation
p_{A}(\mathbf{b})
is the number of integer points in the polytope

\mathcal{P}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{0}\}.

The funcion
L_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
that enumerates integer points in the
t^{th}
dilate of
\mathcal{P}

t\mathcal{P}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:A\mathbf{x}=t\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{0}\}

is a quasi-polynomial, which is called the
Ehrhart quasi-polynomial
.
This is equal to
p_{A}(t\mathbf{b})
(viewed as a function of

Computing the continuous discretely
by Beck and Robbins
[
BeRo15
]
gives a wonderful overview of this topic).

Barvinok’s algorithm
[
Barv94
]
allows one to explicitly compute the piecewise quasi-polynomial

in polynomial time for fixed dimension

. Multiple implementations of Barvinok’s algorithm exist:
Latte

[
DeHe04
]
developed by De Loera, Hemmecke, Tauzer, and Yoshida can be used to (among many other things) compute the quasi-polynomial

for each

and is integrated in

;

[

. While Barvinok’s algorithm is polynomial time for fixed dimension

3.
External columns and chambers

In this section, we introduce the main objects of study of this document: external columns, rays, facets, and chambers. Additionally, we describe some properties of these objects that will be necessary for our main result, Theorem
4.2
. We begin by defining some notation which will appear throughout the rest of this article.

3.1.
Notation

Recall that
A
denotes a
d\times n
matrix with integer entries, of rank
d
, and satisfying

\ker(A)\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

We denote the columns of
A
by
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
. Often we informally identify a matrix with its set of columns, so that we may write
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n})
in lieu of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(A)
. Additionally, we often refer to a
chamber of the matrix

A
instead of saying a
chamber in the chamber complex
of
A
for short.

Given a
d\times n
matrix
M
, by
M_{\hat{i},\hat{j}}
we denote the
(d-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix obtained by removing row
i
and column
j
. We also denote by
M_{\hat{i},\cdot}
the
(d-1)\times n
matrix obtained by removing row
i
, and by
M_{\cdot,\hat{j}}
the
d\times(n-1)
matrix obtained by removing column
j
. Similarly for a vector
\mathbf{v}
, by
\mathbf{v}_{\hat{i}}
, we denote
\mathbf{v}
with the
i
th coordinate removed.

3.2.
External columns

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be the
j
th column of
A
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. We define
\mathbf{a}_{j}
to be an
external column
of
A
if
\mathbf{a}_{j}\notin\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
.

If
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is an external column of
A
, then the cone
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
is a proper subset of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be a column of
A
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is an external column of
A
if and only if
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is a ray generator of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
and no other column of
A
is in the span of
\mathbf{a}_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be an external column of
A
. Since
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})\neq\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
,
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is part of a minimal generating set of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, and thus is a ray generator of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
. Also, no other column
\mathbf{c}
of
A
is in its span. Otherwise, either
\mathbf{c}
is a positive multiple of
\mathbf{a}_{j}
in which case
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
or
\mathbf{c}
is a negative multiple of
\mathbf{a}_{j}
in which case
\operatorname{ker}(A)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

Conversely, let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be a ray generator of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
with no other column of
A
in its span. Then
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is in a minimal generating set of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, and since no other column of
A
is in its span,
\mathbf{a}_{j}\notin\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
. Therefore,
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is an external column of
A
.
∎

A straightforward consequence of this proposition is that external columns of
A
lie on facets of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
. The following proposition will be useful in the following section, and is also a straightforward consequence of Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be an external column of
A
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and let
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
for some
s\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
. If
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
, then
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is a ray generator of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
. In particular,
j\in s
.

One can also define the external columns in terms of the vector partition function
p_{A}
. They are exactly the columns of
A
for which
p_{A}(t\mathbf{a}_{j})\leq 1
for all non-negative integers

(i.e for which the Ehrhart quasi-polynomial
p_{A}(t\mathbf{a}_{j})
has degree
0
).

3.3.
External chambers

We now introduce external chambers, the main objects of study of our work.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\gamma
be a chamber of
A
. We define
\gamma
to be an
external chamber
of
A
if all but one ray of
\gamma
is generated by an external column of
A
. Further, we define the rays that are generated by external columns of

to be
external rays
of
\gamma
and the other ray to be the
internal ray
of
\gamma
. Moreover, we define any generator of an external ray to be an

of
\gamma

of
\gamma
.

Remark 3.5
.

The reader may wonder what happens in the case that

is generated solely by external columns. This case is degenerate:
A
has a single chamber and

for all
\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the following matrix

given in

]

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be an external column of
A
for some

. Then any chamber of
A
containing
\mathbf{a}_{j}
has a single facet
f
not containing
\mathbf{a}_{j}
. Moreover, if
H
is the supporting hyperplane of

, then

separates
\mathbf{a}_{j}
and
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}}).

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is an external column of
A
, by definition
\mathbf{a}_{j}\not\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
denote the faces of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
of dimension

.
We note that these faces are not necessarily facets of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
: if
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
is
(d-1)
-dimensional, then

and

. However, the proof proceeds in the same way regardless of whether
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
is of dimension
d
or
d-1
.

For
i=1,\dots,k
, the faces

can be described as

for some supporting hyperplanes

. Without loss of generality, let
H_{1},H_{2},\dots,H_{\ell}
be the set of hyperplanes that separate
\mathbf{a}_{j}
and the cone
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
and also do not contain
\mathbf{a}_{j}
. For
i=1,\dots,\ell
, let
\kappa_{i}
be the cone generated by
\mathbf{a}_{j}
and the columns of

that generate

. Since
f_{i}
is generated by columns of

spanning

, it follows that
\kappa_{i}
is a union of simplicial cones of

– that is,
\kappa_{i}=\cup_{s\in S}\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
for some

with each

.

Let
\gamma
be a chamber of
A
containing
\mathbf{a}_{j}
. Then
\gamma
has a
d
-dimensional intersection with a simplicial cone of
A
that
is contained in
\kappa_{i^{*}}
for some

, and so
\gamma\subseteq\kappa_{i^{*}}
.
Let
\tau
be a facet of
\gamma
not contained in

, so
\tau
is not contained in
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
. By the definition of the chamber complex,

must be contained in a facet

of a simplicial cone
\sigma
of

. Thus, we can write
\tau^{\prime}=\sigma\cap H^{\prime}
for some hyperplane

. Then
\gamma\cap H^{\prime}
defines a proper face of
\gamma
containing the facet

, and so
\tau=\gamma\cap H^{\prime}
. Since
\tau^{\prime}
is generated by columns of

and not contained in

,
\mathbf{a}_{j}
is a ray generator of

. Thus,

not containing

is

Proposition 3.11

(Constructing external chambers)
.

Assume that the chamber complex of
A
contains at least two chambers, and let
f
be a
(d-1)
-dimensional cone in the chamber complex.
Then
f
is an external facet of
A
if and only if
f
is a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
containing exactly
d-1
columns of
A
. Moreover, if the columns of
A
generating
f
are
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}
, then the unique external chamber containing
f
is

\gamma:=\bigcap\limits_{k=0}^{n-d}\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{a}_{d+k}).

Proof.

We begin with the forward direction. Suppose that
f
is an external facet of
A
, and assume towards a contradiction that
f
is not a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
containing exactly
d-1
columns of
A
. Since
f
is an external facet of
A
, it is a facet of an external chamber
\gamma^{\prime}
of
A
. Since
\gamma^{\prime}
is simplicial, it has
d
facets, say
f_{1},\dots,f_{d-1},f_{d}=f
. Assume moreover that
f_{i}
is the unique facet of
\gamma^{\prime}
not containing
\mathbf{a}_{i}
for each
i=1,\dots,d-1
. Let
\bm{\iota}_{1},\dots,\bm{\iota}_{d}
be the inner facet normals (with respect to
\gamma^{\prime}
) corresponding to the facets
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}
, and
H_{1},\dots,H_{d}
be the corresponding supporting hyperplanes. Since
f
is not a facet of
A
containing exactly
d-1
columns of
A
, there are two options to consider

(1)

f
is not a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
,

(2)

f
is a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, but contains more than
d-1
columns of
A
.

In the first case there is some column
\mathbf{a}
of
A
with
\bm{\iota}_{d}\cdot\mathbf{a}\leq 0
and
\mathbf{a}\notin\gamma
. By Lemma
3.10
, the hyperplane
H_{j}
separates the column
\mathbf{a}_{j}
from the cone
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{j}})
for each
j=1,\dots,d-1
. Therefore,
\bm{\iota}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{a}\leq 0
for each
j=1,\dots,d-1
. Then
-\mathbf{a}\in\gamma^{\prime},
since
\bm{\iota}_{j}\cdot(-\mathbf{a})\geq 0
for each
j=1,\dots,d
. Now, let
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
be a simplicial cone of
A
for some
s\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
with
|s|=\operatorname{rank}(A_{s})=d
so that
\gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s}).
Since
-\mathbf{a}\in\gamma^{\prime}
,
-\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
, and so
-\mathbf{a}=\sum_{i\in s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}\geq 0.
But then,
\sum_{i\in s}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}+\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
, and so
\ker(A)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}
. This is a contradiction. Therefore,
f
is indeed a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, and
f
contains exactly the
d-1
columns of
A
,
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}.

In the second case, there is some column
\mathbf{a}\in f
with
\mathbf{a}\notin\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots\mathbf{a}_{d-1}\}
. However, since
\mathbf{a}
is a column of
A
, it generates a 1-dimensional cone of the chamber complex of
A
, and so
f=\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots\mathbf{a}_{d-1})
cannot be a cone of the chamber complex. This contradicts the fact that
f
is an external facet.

We now prove the reverse direction. If
f
is a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
containing exactly
d-1
columns of
A
, then each of these columns is a ray generator of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
and no pair is linearly dependent. Therefore, each of
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}
are external columns of
A
.

Finally, we show that
\gamma
is indeed a chamber of
A
. First note that none of the columns
\mathbf{a}_{d},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}
lie on
f
, and so
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{a}_{d+k})
is a simplical cone of
A
for each
k~=0,~\dots,n-d
. Therefore
\gamma
is the intersection of simplicial cones, and since each of these simplicial cones lie on the same side of the facet
f
, the cone
\gamma
must be
d
-dimensional. Consider the point

(2)

\mathbf{b}:=\mathbf{a}_{1}+\dots+\mathbf{a}_{d-1}.

Since
\{\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}\}
is a linearly independent set, and
f
is a facet of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, the formulation of (
2
) is the unique way to represent
\mathbf{b}
as a
\mathbb{N}
-linear combination of the columns of
A
. Therefore, any simplicial cone
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
of
A
containing
\mathbf{b}
must contain each of the external columns
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}
. Moreover,
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}
are each ray generators of
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{s})
by Proposition
3.3
. Therefore,
\gamma
is a chamber of
A
since it is a
d
-dimensional cone obtained as the intersection of all simplicial cones containing
\mathbf{b}
. Furthermore,
\gamma
is the unique external chamber containing
f
, since any other
d
-dimensional cone containing
\mathbf{b}
and obtained by an intersection of simplicial cones of
A
(necessarily containing all of
\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1}
as ray generators) must contain
\gamma
as a subset.

By Lemma
3.10
, there is a unique facet of
\gamma
not containing
\mathbf{a}_{i}
for each
1~\leq~i~\leq~d~-~1
, and so
\gamma
is simplicial. Therefore,
\gamma=\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{v})
for some ray generator
\mathbf{v}
. Since
A
has at least two chambers, it must have some column
\mathbf{c}\notin\gamma
. As well,
\mathbf{c}~\notin~f
, so
\gamma^{\prime}~:=~\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{c})
is a simplicial cone of
A
, and
\gamma\subsetneq\gamma^{\prime}
. Therefore, it follows that
\mathbf{v}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{c})
and so
\mathbf{v}
is an internal ray generator for
\gamma
. Thus,
\gamma
is an external chamber of
A
and

is an external facet of
A
.
∎

3.4.
A vector partition function preserving transformation

We now prove some results that allow us to transform the matrix
A
while preserving the vector partition function (up to an appropriate change of variables) and the structure of the chamber complex. We use these results in Section
4
in order to transform
A
into a form well-suited for analysis (described in Lemma
4.1
). Propositions
3.12
and
3.13
are straight-forward results, and have been assumed by other authors, so we give them without proof.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}
be an invertible matrix with integer entries. Then
p_{A}(\mathbf{b})=p_{MA}(M\mathbf{b})
for all
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For a cone
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and invertible matrix
M\in\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}
, define the cone
M\sigma:=\{M\mathbf{b}:\mathbf{b}\in\sigma\}
. We note that
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
is a generating set of
\sigma
if and only if
\{M\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots M\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
is a generating set of
M\sigma
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}
be an invertible matrix. The cone
\gamma
is a chamber of
A
if and only if
M\gamma
is a chamber of
MA
. Moreover,
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
is a minimal generating set of
\gamma
if and only if
\{M\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,M\mathbf{u}_{k}\}
is a minimal generating set of
M\gamma
.

Remark 3.14
.

The previous proposition does not always hold if we replace “minimal generating set” with “minimal ray generators”. In the case that
\gamma
is a simplicial cone with minimal ray generators
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{d}
, then
M\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,M\mathbf{v}_{d}
are minimal ray generators of
M\gamma
if and only if
M
is invertible over
\mathbb{Z}
(equivalently
\det(M)=\pm 1
).

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}
be an invertible matrix. Let
\mathbf{a}_{j}
be a column of
A
for some
j~\in~\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

\mathbf{a}_{j}
is an external column of
A
if and only if
M\mathbf{a}_{j}
is an external column of
MA
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
j=1
.
Points
\mathbf{b}
in
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{1}})
map to points in
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(MA_{\cdot,\hat{1}})
under the invertible mapping
\mathbf{b}\mapsto M\mathbf{b}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{1}})

\displaystyle\iff\mathbf{b}=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}

\displaystyle(\text{for }\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}\geq 0)

\displaystyle\iff M\mathbf{b}=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}M\mathbf{a}_{i}

\displaystyle\iff M\mathbf{b}\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(MA_{\cdot,\hat{1}})

Therefore
\mathbf{a}_{1}\notin\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A_{\cdot,\hat{1}})
if and only if
M\mathbf{a}_{1}\notin\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(MA_{\cdot,\hat{1}})
, and so
\mathbf{a}_{1}
is an external column if and only if
M\mathbf{a}_{1}
is an external column.
∎

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}
be an invertible matrix. Then

\gamma
is an external chamber of

if and only if
M\gamma
is an external chamber of
MA
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Propositions
3.13
and

.

In the following section, we show that one can always construct an invertible matrix

with integer entries that maps each of the external columns of

in

4.
Reduction of dimension

In this section, we consider chambers of
A
that contain external columns. Up to a lattice condition, we show that the quasi-polynomial
p_{A}^{\gamma}
for such a chamber
\gamma
can be obtained via a vector partition function of lower dimension. In particular, if
\gamma
has
k
external columns, then (up to a change of variables)
p_{A}^{\gamma}=p_{B}^{\gamma^{\prime}}
for a matrix
B
of
k
fewer rows and columns than
A
, and chamber
\gamma^{\prime}
of
B.
When this result is applied to external chambers, we find that
B
has a single row, so that
p^{\gamma}_{A}
is obtained from a coin exchange problem. As a consequence, such a
p_{A}^{\gamma}
is a univariate quasi-polynomial. Indeed,
p_{A}^{\gamma}
is precisely the Ehrhart quasi-polynomial associated to the internal ray of
\gamma
.

Let
\mathcal{L}{(}A)
denote the lattice generated by the columns of
A
, and
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(A)
denote the affine semigroup generated by the columns of
A
– that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}{(}A):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}:\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(A):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}:\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

An affine semigroup
\mathcal{S}
is
saturated
in a lattice
\mathcal{L}
if for each positive integer
k
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{L}
,
k\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{S}
only if
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{S}
. In our setting, we are generally interested in showing that affine semigroup generated by a subset of columns of
A
is saturated in the lattice generated by the columns of
A
– that is, that
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(A_{s})
is saturated in the lattice
\mathcal{L}(A)

In the previous section we showed that if

Lemma 5.3

Let

be a

integer matrix for some positive integer

, and assume that
\ker(B)\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\geq 0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Then
p_{B}
is a polynomial of degree

on
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(B)
if and only if each of the
k

are equal to some non-zero integer

. In this case,

p_{B}(b)=\binom{\frac{b}{\beta}+k-1}{k-1}

for all
b\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(B)
.

Proof.

We begin by proving the reverse implication. Assume
B
is a
1\times k
integer matrix with each of the
k
entries equal to some non-zero integer
\beta
. Then for each
b\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(B)
,
p_{B}(b)
is the number of ways of partitioning
b/{\beta}
into
k
equal non-negative integral parts. Therefore,

(7)

p_{B}(b)=\binom{\frac{b}{\beta}+k-1}{k-1}

is a polynomial in
b
for
b\in\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(B)
.

We now prove the forward implication.
Suppose
p_{B}
is a polynomial of degree
k-1
. We may assume that
k\geq 2
, since if
k=1
,
B
has a single entry. We note further that the entries of
B
must be either all positive or all negative or else
\ker(B)\cap\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\geq 0}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}
. We assume that all entries are positive, noting that the negative case follows a similar argument. For all
1\leq j\leq k
, the vector partition function
p_{B_{\cdot,\hat{j}}}
is a polynomial of degree
k-2
since it is the difference of two polynomials:

p_{B_{\cdot,\hat{j}}}(b)=p_{B}(b)-p_{B}(b-b_{1,j})

for all
b\in\mathcal{L}{(}B)\cap\mathbb{N}
. In particular, by repeated application of this fact, it follows that for each
1\times 2
submatrix of
B
, the vector partition function is a polynomial of degree
1
.

Assume towards a contradiction that
B
has two distinct entries, say, without loss of generality,
b_{1,1}
and
b_{1,2}
. Let
B^{\prime}=[b_{1,1},b_{1,2}]
be the

submatrix consisting of the two distinct entries, so that
p_{B^{\prime}}
is a polynomial of degree
1
.
Also,
p_{B^{\prime}}(0)=p_{B}(\min(b_{1,1},b_{1,2}))=1
, so
p_{B^{\prime}}(b)=1
for all
b\in\mathbb{N}
since
p_{B^{\prime}}
is linear. However,
p_{B^{\prime}}(b_{1,1}b_{1,2})\geq 2
since both
\mathbf{x}=(b_{1,2},0)
and
\mathbf{x}=(0,b_{1,1})
are solutions to
B^{\prime}\mathbf{x}=b_{1,1}b_{1,2}
with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
. This contradicts that
p_{B^{\prime}}
is a polynomial of degree
1
, and thus that
p_{B}
is a polynomial of degree
k-1
. Therefore, the entries of

must be the same as required.

5.3.
Unimodularity

We now consider the case of unimodular matrices – these matrices have the special property that
p_{A}
is a piecewise polynomial, so
p_{A}^{\gamma}
is polynomial for each chamber.

A full rank
d\times n
matrix
A
with integer entries is
unimodular
if every
d\times d
submatrix of
A
has determinant
1
,
-1
, or
0
(see for example
[
Schr86
, Section 19.1]
). If
A
is unimodular, then
\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{N}}(A)=\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

In
[
DeSt03
]
, De Loera and Sturmfels introduce a generalization of matrix unimodularity given by a geometrical criterion. Both of these definitions appear in this section, so we distinguish them by refering to the older definition simply as unimodular and the one introduced by De Loera and Sturmfels as DeLS-unimodular. A
d\times n
matrix
A
with integer entries is defined to be
DeLS-unimodular
if the polyhedron
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{0}\}
associated to the vector partition function
p_{A}(\mathbf{b})
has only integral vertices whenever
\mathbf{b}
is in the lattice spanned by the columns of
A
. Under these conditions,
p_{A}
is piecewise polynomial by the following result of De Loera and Sturmfels. We remark that unimodular matrices are DeLS-unimodular.

Theorem 5.6

(De Loera, Sturmfels 2003
[
DeSt03
]
)
.

Let
A
be a
d\times n
DeLS-unimodular matrix of rank
d
. Then
p_{A}
is a piecewise polynomial of degree
n-d
on
\mathcal{L}{(}A)\cap\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
and is zero everywhere else on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\operatorname{pos}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
.

If
A
is DeLS-unimodular, then each subset of columns of
A
generates an affine semigroup that is saturated in
\mathcal{L}(A)
. If
A
is unimodular then it is also DeLS-unimodular, and therefore if
A
is a unimodular matrix, then the same holds true. The following corollary now follows immediately.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
A
be a

d

be an external chamber of

(13)

p_{A}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{b})=\binom{\frac{\det(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{b})}{\det(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{d-1},\mathbf{a}_{d+\ell})}+n-d}{n-d}

for all

6.
Multigraph enumeration

In this section we consider the problem of enumerating the number of labelled multigraphs with vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
and a given sequence
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}
so that
\deg(v_{i})=d_{i}
for
1~\leq i~\leq m
. In order that this may be encoded as a vector partition function, we allow multiple edges between any pair of vertices but do not allow loops. Most known results for enumerating the number of graphs or multigraphs with a given degree sequence are asymptotic (see for example
[
BaHa13
,
GrMc13
,
McWaWo02
]
). We give an exact result for a relatively simple case, that we found by identifying external chambers of the corresponding vector partition functions. We have not seen this result in the literature, nor any attempts to approach this problem via the vector partition function formulation. This is somewhat surprising since it is well known that the enumeration of simple graphs with a given degree sequence can be viewed as counting integer points in polytopes.

For a positive integer
m
, define
M_{m}(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
to be the number of multigraphs on the vertex set
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
with degree sequence
(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
. We note that we do not assume that the degree sequence is monotonically decreasing unless explicitly stated.

For each pair of distinct vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
(
1\leq i\neq j\leq m
), let
x_{i,j}
denote the number of edges joining
v_{i}
and
v_{j}.

A multigraph on the vertex set
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
has degree sequence
(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
if the following
m
linear equations are satisfied:

(16)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}x_{i,j}=d_{j}\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,m.

The number of edges between any pair of vertices is a non-negative integer, and so one can describe
M_{m}(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
as the number of solutions
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1,2},\dots,x_{m-1,m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
satisfying the linear equations of (
16
). This description leads to the following vector partition function formulation.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
m
be a positive integer, and let
G_{m}
denote the incidence matrix of the complete graph
K_{m}
. Then

M_{m}(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})=p_{G_{m}}(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})

for each degree sequence
(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
on the lattice
d_{1}+d_{2}+\dots+d_{m}\equiv 0\mod 2
.

Example 6.2
.

Let us compute
M_{4}(5,4,3,2)

By Proposition

, this is equivalent to computing

in the polytope

.
Using

we compute the solutions explicitly; there are six of them. In Table

Since

.
