Generalized Grassmann Algebras and Applications to Stochastic Processes
D. Alpay † , P. Cerejeiras ‡ , U. Kähler ‡

Abstract
. In this paper we present the groundwork for an Itô/Malliavin stochastic calculus and Hida’s white noise analysis in the context of a supersymmentry with ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3} -graded algebras. To this end we establish a ternary Fock space and the corresponding strong algebra of stochastic distributions and present its application in the study of stochastic processes in this context.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 30G35; Secondary: 60H45, 60G22, 15A75.

1.
Introduction

Classic theories like Bose-Einstein or Fermi-Dirac statistics are based on SU(2)-symmetries and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded algebras. But theories like Quantum Chromodynamics where quarks are considered as fermions require a setting with
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
- (or
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
-)graded algebras for a convenient generalisations of Pauli’s exclusion principle and the establishment of the corresponding statistics
[
28
]
. Such algebras and the corresponding Dirac operators have been studied in the recent past. But these theories raise an additional question about the necessary extension of the corresponding supersymmetry. While standard supersymmetry combines bosonic fields with fermionic fields to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded Lie super algebra a super-symmetric extension involving quarks requires a
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-graded algebra of Grassmannian type which leads to a kind of hypersymmetry
[
1
]
.

One important aspect of supersymmetry lies in its combination with stochastic dynamics, also called topological supersymmetry initiated by the seminal works of Parisi and Sourlas in 1979 (see e.g.
[
32
]
or
[
26
]
), in particular the more recent supersymmetric theory of stochastic dynamics
[
30
,
31
]
. But for the establishment of such a theory in the context of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
- (or
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
-)graded algebras, i.e. the development of topological hypersymmetry, a major problem arises. One needs the counterpart of the classic Itô/Malliavin stochastic calculus and Hida’s white noise analysis in this context. This is the problem we are going to study in this paper. While differentiation and integration has been studied in superspace using Clifford algebras
[
22
,
21
]
no such study has been made in the context of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-graded algebras to the knowledge of the authors.

To this end let us recall the classic (finite or infinite) Grassmann algebra.
\mathcal{G}_{d}
is the unital algebra over the complex numbers generated by
\mathbf{e}_{0}=1
and a finite set of elements
\mathbf{e}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, which do not belong to and are linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, they satisfy

(1.1)

\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}=0,

where
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, and in particular

(1.2)

\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}=0.

An element of
\mathcal{G}_{d}
is often referred to as a supernumber. The number of generators can be taken to be infinite. To do so, closures of the algebra with respect to a norm are considered – see, e.g.,
[
33
,
35
,
6
,
7
]
.

It is well-known that the classic Grassmann algebra is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded. To introduce a
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-grading we present a generalization of the classic Grassmann algebra to a ternary Grassmanian setting. We consider once again the real generator
\mathbf{e}_{0}=1
and basis elements
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
. Instead of (
1.1
), we assume the generators satisfy

(1.3)

\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\omega\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}

for every
i<j
, where
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
and where
\omega\neq 1
is a (fixed) non-zero complex number. As will be clear from the next section these algebras have a natural
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-grading.

Using these algebras we are going to create the necessary algebraic and analytic tools for the establishment of the counterpart of Hida’s white noise space theory, including the construction of a topological algebra associated with the above mentioned algebra based on a decreasing family of Hilbert spaces which allows us to obtain the link between this algebra and the Fock space. We will finish by showing the application to stochastic processes.

2.
Finite Ternary Grassmann Algebras

Let us start by introducing ternary Grassmann algebras. Let
\mathfrak{G}=\{\mathbf{e}_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a countable set of linearly independent vectors over
\mathbb{C}.
We denote by
V_{d}
the complex linear space generated by the first
d
of such vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We define the ternary Grassmann algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
associated to
V_{d}
as the free (non-trivial) algebra over
\mathbb{C}
containing a copy of
\mbox{\rm Span }\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d}\}
and of
\mathbb{C}
, and satisfying to the following relations:

(i)

there exists a complex
\omega\not=0
such that

(2.1)

\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\omega\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i},\qquad\mbox{\rm for all }i<j;

(ii)

the basis elements satisfy

(2.2)

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{k})=0,\qquad\mbox{\rm for all }i,j,k=1,\ldots,d,

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the ternary form (based on the anti-commutator):

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i}\{\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{k}\}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\{\mathbf{e}_{k},\mathbf{e}_{i}\}+\mathbf{e}_{k}\{\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{k}+\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{k}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq j\leq k\leq d.

Remark 2.2
.

By “non-trivial” we mean that the product of any two arbitrary basic elements of the algebra is either a new element of the algebra or it is zero. We will say that
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is generated by
\mathfrak{G}_{d}=\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d}\}\subset\mathfrak{G}.
Such algebras have already been studied in the literature. They are often referred to as ternary Grassmann algebras
[
17
,
27
]
.

An immediate consequence of the definition is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Under the conditions of Definition
2.1
it holds

(i)

\mathbf{e}_{i}^{3}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,d;

(ii)

\omega
is a third root of the unit.

Proof.

Proposition
(i)
is immediate. For the second proposition we have for
1\leq i<j\leq d

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j})=\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=2(\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}+\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2})=2(1+\omega+\omega^{2})\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}.

Under the assumption of non-triviality (i.e.
\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}\not=0
) we obtain
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
and therefore,
\omega
is a root of third order the unity.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

In what follows we assume
\omega=e^{i2\pi/3}.

Due to Lemma
2.3
one observes that the relevant powers of the vector basis elements are given by
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{m},m=0,1,2,
since
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{m}=0
for all
m\geq 3
. Furthermore, we identify
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{0}=1,
the identity of the field
\mathbb{C}.
Hence, we have

(2.4)

\mathbf{e}_{j}^{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&m=0\\
\mathbf{e}_{j},&m=1\\
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2},&m=2\\
0,&m\geq 3\end{array}\right.

for all
j=1,\ldots,d.
In consequence, a basis for the finite ternary Grassmann algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is expressible in terms of appropriated ordered
d-
tuples of powers less than
3,
that is,

(2.5)

\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d}^{\nu_{d}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d})\in\{0,1,2\}^{d}.

Under the previous convention we have
\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=1
with
\boldsymbol{0}=(0,\ldots,0).
We shall denote by
\mathcal{I}_{d}
the set of all such
d-
tuples, that is,

(2.6)

\mathcal{I}_{d}=\{0,1,2\}^{d}.

Notice that we have
\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
if and only if
\nu_{j}=\mu_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,d.

Since there are
3^{d}
such basis elements every
z\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
can be written as

(2.7)

\mathbf{z}=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=0}^{2d}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\qquad z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathbb{C},~|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{d}.

We observe that
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is a
Z_{3}
-graded algebra, that is, under multiplication the grades add up modulus
3
. This leads to the following blade decomposition of the generalised ternary Grassmann algebra:

(2.8)

\mathcal{G}_{3,d}=[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{0}\oplus[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{2d}

where each
k-
blade is

(2.9)

[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{k}=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}:\mathbf{z}=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right\},~k=0,\ldots,2d.

In what follows we denote by
[\mathbf{z}]_{k}:=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
the projection of
\mathbf{z}
into the blade
[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{k},k=0,\ldots,2d.

Example 2.5
.

For dimension
d=2
we have

\mathbf{z}=\underbrace{z_{00}}_{\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,2}]_{0}}+\underbrace{z_{10}\mathbf{e}_{1}+z_{01}\mathbf{e}_{2}}_{\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,2}]_{1}}+\underbrace{z_{20}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}+z_{11}\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}+z_{02}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}}_{\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,2}]_{2}}+\underbrace{z_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+z_{12}\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}}_{\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,2}]_{3}}+\underbrace{z_{22}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}}_{\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,2}]_{4}},

where

[\mathbf{z}]_{0}=z_{00},\quad[\mathbf{z}]_{1}=z_{10}\mathbf{e}_{1}+z_{01}\mathbf{e}_{2},\quad\quad[\mathbf{z}]_{2}=z_{20}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}+z_{11}\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}+z_{02}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2},

[\mathbf{z}]_{3}=z_{21}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+z_{12}\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2},\quad\quad[\mathbf{z}]_{4}=z_{22}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2}.

The generalized finite ternary Grassmann algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
decomposes itself into the sum of three spaces

(2.10)

\mathcal{G}_{3,d}=\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{1}\oplus\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{2},

where each
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{k}

\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{k}={\rm span}\,\{\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}:|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k\mbox{\rm~(mod 3) }\},\quad k=0,1,2.

We observe that these spaces obey the following multiplication rules:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0}\subset\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{1}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{2}\subset\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{2}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{1}\subset\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0}.

Hence, only
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{0}
is a subalgebra of
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
while the spaces
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{1},\mathcal{G}_{3,d}^{2}

do not form an algebra
.

2.1.
Properties

We now present results on products in
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
. Due to (
2.1
), we have

(2.11)

\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}=\omega\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}=\omega^{2}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j},

for all
i<j.

Hence, for each component we have

(2.12)

\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\nu_{j}}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\mu_{j}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\nu_{j}+\mu_{j}},&\mbox{if ~}0\leq\nu_{j}+\mu_{j}\leq 2\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.,\quad j=1,\ldots,d.

We now observe that for all
\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}
we have

(2.13)

\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=0,

whenever
\nu_{j}+\mu_{j}\geq 3
for some
j.
However, if
0\leq\nu_{j}+\mu_{j}<3
for all
j
then by (
2.11
) we obtain

(2.14)

\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})~\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}},

where
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})=\omega^{2\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\sum_{j=s+1}^{d}\nu_{j}\mu_{s}}
corresponds to
\omega=\exp(\frac{2\pi i}{3})
to the power of the number of permutations of the basis elements.
This leads to the following multiplication rule:

(2.15)

\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}},\qquad\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{\rm if }\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\notin\mathcal{I}_{d}\\
\omega^{2\sum_{s=1}^{d-1}\sum_{j=s+1}^{d}\nu_{j}\mu_{s}},&\mbox{\rm otherwise}\end{array}\right..

Lemma 2.6
.

For every
\mathbf{z}\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{k},\mathbf{w}\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{s},

(0\leq k,s\leq 2d),
we have that
\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}=0
if
k+s>2d
and

(2.16)

\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}\in\{0\}\oplus[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{k+s},\quad{\rm if~}0\leq k+s\leq 2d.

Proof.

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}=(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}})(\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mu}|=s}w_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\sum_{{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k,|\boldsymbol{\mu}|=s}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|=k,|\boldsymbol{\mu}|=s}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})~z_{\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{d}}w_{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\nu_{1}+\mu_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d}^{\nu_{d}+\mu_{d}}.

The result follows trivially from (
2.15
). ∎

Lemma 2.7
.

For every vector
\mathbf{z}=z_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+z_{d}\mathbf{e}_{d}\in[\mathcal{G}_{3,d}]_{1}
it holds

(2.17)

\mathbf{z}^{3}=0.

Proof.

By direct computation we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{3}=\sum_{i,j,k}z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{k})=0.

since
\omega
is the 3rd root of the unit and
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{3}=0.

∎

We now present some decomposition results for the finite ternary Grassmann algebra. The results will be expressed in terms of the basis element
\mathbf{e}_{d}
but are easily extendable to any basis element
\mathbf{e}_{j}
with appropriate modifications.

Lemma 2.8
.

Every element
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
admits the following decomposition

(2.18)

\mathbf{z}=A+B\mathbf{e}_{d}+C\mathbf{e}_{d}^{2},\quad A,B,C\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d-1}.

The result is straightforward and its proof will be omitted.

Lemma 2.9
.

For every
A\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d-1},
there exists
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d-1}
such that

(2.19)

A\mathbf{e}_{d}=\mathbf{e}_{d}A^{\prime}.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{d}=\omega\mathbf{e}_{d}\mathbf{e}_{j},~j=1,\cdots,d-1.
Hence, we have for
A=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1}}a_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\mathbf{e}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d-1}

\displaystyle A\mathbf{e}_{d}=\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1}}a_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right)\mathbf{e}_{d}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1}}a_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d-2}^{\nu_{d-2}}(\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{\nu_{d-1}}\mathbf{e}_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1}}a_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d-2}^{\nu_{d-2}}\left(\omega^{\nu_{d-1}}\mathbf{e}_{d}\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{\nu_{d-1}}\right)=\cdots=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1}}a_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\omega^{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nu_{j}}\mathbf{e}_{d}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d-2}^{\nu_{d-2}}\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{\nu_{d-1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{d}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-1}}a_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\omega^{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nu_{j}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d-2}^{\nu_{d-2}}\mathbf{e}_{d-1}^{\nu_{d-1}}\right):=\mathbf{e}_{d}A^{\prime}.

∎

Corollary 2.10
.

If we have
A=0
in decomposition (
2.18
) then
\mathbf{z}^{3}=0.

This is an obvious consequence of the two previous lemmas, as
\mathbf{z}=(B+C\mathbf{e}_{d})\mathbf{e}_{d}.

Lemma 2.11
.

For every
1\leq n\leq d
there exists an element
I_{n}=\mathbf{e}^{2}_{d-n+1}\cdots\mathbf{e}^{2}_{d}
such that it satisfies:

i)

I_{n}
is nilpotent, that is
I_{n}^{2}=0;

ii)

I_{n}
acts as a projector of
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
onto the subalgebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d-n},
that is to say, there exists a projector
P_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{3,d}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{3,d-n}
given by

\mathbf{z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}\mapsto P_{n}(\mathbf{z}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-n}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d-n},

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{d-n});

iii)

In particular,
P_{d}
acts as a projector of
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
onto
\mathbb{C}
, given by
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}\mapsto P_{d}(\mathbf{z}):=[\mathbf{z}]_{0}=z_{\boldsymbol{0}}:=z_{(0,\cdots,0)}.

Proof.

The fact that
I_{n}
is nilpotent is straightforward. For the projection part, we observe that by (
2.12
) the product of an arbitrary
\mathbf{z}
by
I_{n}
kills off all terms containing
\mathbf{e}_{d-n+1},\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{d},
that is, given

\mathbf{z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-n}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{d-n}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}},

we have

\mathbf{z}I_{n}=(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}})I_{n}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-n}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}I_{n},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{d-n}).

Hence, we identify the projection of
\mathbf{z}
into
\mathcal{G}_{3,d-n}
with
P_{n}(\mathbf{z}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d-n}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}.
The third proposition is now immediate.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Based on the last proposition, the non-scalar part of an element
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is obtained by

(1-P_{d})\mathbf{z}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\mu}\not=\boldsymbol{0}}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}.

Hence,

\mathbf{z}=P_{d}\mathbf{z}+(1-P_{d})\mathbf{z}.

Henceforward we shall use the notations
z_{\boldsymbol{0}}:=P_{d}\mathbf{z}
for its scalar part (also, body of
\mathbf{z}
) and
\mathbf{z}_{r}:=(1-P_{d})\mathbf{z}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\mu}\not=\boldsymbol{0}}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
for its remainder (also, soul of
\mathbf{z}
).

Lemma 2.13
.

An arbitrary element
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is invertible if and only if its scalar part
[\mathbf{z}]_{0}=z_{\boldsymbol{0}}
is non-zero.

Proof.

We begin our proof by showing that if the scalar part of an element
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is zero then this element cannot be invertible. Indeed, if
[\mathbf{z}]_{0}=0
then
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{{\nu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}\atop{\nu\not=\mathbf{0}}}z_{\nu}\mathbf{e}^{\nu}
and by Lemma
2.6
we get
[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}]_{0}=0
for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}.
Hence,
\mathbf{z}
is not invertible.

Now we consider
[\mathbf{z}]_{0}\not=0.
First, we observe that there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{z}_{r}^{m}=0.
Take
m(\mathbf{z}):=\min\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbf{z}_{r}^{m}=0\}.
Whenever
z_{\mathbf{0}}\not=0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{n}=z_{\mathbf{0}}^{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}}\mathbf{z}_{r}\right)^{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, for
n=m(\mathbf{z}_{r})-1
we obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}}\mathbf{z}_{r}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}}\mathbf{z}_{r}+\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}^{2}}\mathbf{z}_{r}^{2}+\cdots+(-1)^{m(\mathbf{z})-1}\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}^{m(\mathbf{z})-1}}\mathbf{z}_{r}^{m(\mathbf{z})-1}\right)=1,

so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{-1}=\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}}-\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}^{2}}\mathbf{z}_{r}+\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}^{3}}\mathbf{z}_{r}^{2}+\cdots+(-1)^{m(\mathbf{z})-1}\frac{1}{z_{\mathbf{0}}^{m(\mathbf{z})}}\mathbf{z}_{r}^{m(\mathbf{z})-1},

is the right inverse of
\mathbf{z}.
The same construction holds for the left inverse which proves the unicity of
\mathbf{z}^{-1}
.
∎

2.2.
A conjugation in
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}

We present a morphism acting on the finite generalised ternary algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}.

Definition 2.14

(Pseudo-conjugation)
.

The pseudo-conjugation in the ternary Grassmann algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
is defined as the morphism
\overline{\cdot}:\mathcal{G}_{3,d}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{3,d},
with
\mathbf{z}\mapsto\overline{\mathbf{z}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\overline{z}}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}~\overline{\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}},

where
\overline{z}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
denotes the standard complex conjugation, while its action on the basis elements
\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d},
is given by,

(2.20)

\overline{1}=1,\quad\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2},\quad j=1,\cdots,d,

and satisfying to

(2.21)

\overline{\mathbf{ab}+\mathbf{c}}=\overline{\mathbf{b}}~\overline{\mathbf{a}}+\overline{\mathbf{c}},\quad for~all~\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}.

Remark 2.15
.

As a consequence, we get
\overline{\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2}}=\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2}=0.
Hence,
\overline{\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\nu}\not\in\{0,1\}^{d}.
Furthermore this morphism is not onto and it is not an involution since
\overline{(\overline{\mathbf{e}}_{j})}=0
.

Lemma 2.16
.

For all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
it holds:

i)

\overline{(\overline{\mathbf{z}})}=z_{\mathbf{0}};

ii)

[\mathbf{z}\overline{\mathbf{z}}]_{0}=[\overline{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{z}]_{0}=|z_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2},

where we recall,
z_{\mathbf{0}}
denotes the scalar part of
\mathbf{z}.

Proof.

The first proposition is obvious since the action of the pseudo-conjugation on the basis elements is given by

\overline{\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=\mathbf{e}_{d}^{2\nu_{d}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2\nu_{2}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2\nu_{1}},\qquad\mbox{\rm whenever }\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\{0,1\}^{d},

and zero otherwise.
Furthermore, as
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}=\omega^{2}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{2},~i<j,
we obtain

\overline{\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\mathbf{e}^{2\boldsymbol{\nu}}:=\omega^{2(\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\sum_{s=j+1}^{d}\nu_{j}\nu_{s})}\mathbf{e}^{2\boldsymbol{\nu}},\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\{0,1\}^{d}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\overline{(\overline{\mathbf{z}})}=\overline{\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\overline{z}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}_{d}^{2\nu_{d}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2\nu_{2}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2\nu_{1}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\overline{\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2\nu_{1}}}~\overline{\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2\nu_{2}}}\cdots\overline{\mathbf{e}_{d}^{2\nu_{d}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\mathbf{e}_{1}^{4\nu_{1}}}~{\mathbf{e}_{2}^{4\nu_{2}}}\cdots{\mathbf{e}_{d}^{4\nu_{d}}}=z_{\mathbf{0}}.

For the second proposition we have

\displaystyle[\mathbf{z}\overline{\mathbf{z}}]_{0}=\left[\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right)\left(\overline{\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}z_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\right)\right]_{0}

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}_{d}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\overline{z}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{{\nu}_{1}}\mathbf{e}_{2}^{{\nu}_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{d}^{{\nu}_{d}}\mathbf{e}_{d}^{2\mu_{d}}\cdots\mathbf{e}_{2}^{2\mu_{2}}\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2\mu_{1}}\right]_{0}=z_{\boldsymbol{0}}\overline{z}_{\boldsymbol{0}}=|z_{\boldsymbol{0}}|^{2}.

The same holds for
[\overline{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{z}]_{0},
which completes our proof.
∎

3.
Completions of Grassmann Algebras

We now consider the ternary Grassmann algebra generated by taking the formal limit
d\rightarrow\infty
of
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}.
We denote the correspondent algebra by
\mathcal{G}_{3},
associated to the countable set
\mathfrak{G}=\{\mathbf{e}_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}\}.
Similar to the case of the infinite dimensional Grassmann algebra
\Lambda_{\infty}
the resulting ternary Grassmann algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3}
is an associative but not commutative algebra over
\mathbb{C}.

We denote its elements
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{G}_{3}
as
ternary supernumbers
where
\mathcal{I}=\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the set of indexes, and we endow the ternary Grassmann algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3}
with a
p
-norm. Remark that, since the pseudo-conjugation is not an isomorphism it does not induce a norm. Hence, we will use the
\ell^{p}
-norm where
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is to be identified with
(z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\in\ell^{p}(\mathbb{C}).

The conjugation and product in
\mathcal{G}_{3}
(an infinite dimensional algebra) are well defined provide that
\boldsymbol{\nu}
satisfy
\#\boldsymbol{\nu}<\infty,
where
\#\boldsymbol{\nu}
denotes the number of non-zero entries in the sequence
\boldsymbol{\nu}
(equal to the number of
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\prime}s
present in the basis element
\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
). For example, for
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(1,0,2,1,0,0,\ldots)
we get
\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=3
corresponding to
\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\mathbf{e}_{1}\mathbf{e}_{3}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{4}.

Hence, we have for the conjugation

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\quad\mapsto\quad\overline{\mathbf{w}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu})~\overline{w}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{2\boldsymbol{\mu}},

and for the product between
\mathbf{z}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\mathbf{w}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathcal{G}_{3}
we get

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})~z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}},

under the restriction
\#\boldsymbol{\nu},\#\boldsymbol{\mu}
are finite, and where
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})
is defined as in (
2.15
). We stress that only under particular conditions we have
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})=\overline{\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})}.

Henceforth we assume all sequences to have a finite number of non-zero entries.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}.
We define the
p
-norm of
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{G}_{3}
is defined as

(3.3)

\|\mathbf{z}\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|^{p}\right)^{1/p},

where
|\cdot|
is the usual modulus of a complex number.

We remark that Definition
3.1
holds also for any real
p\geq 1.

Restricted to the finite dimensional sub-algebra
\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
the
p
-norm satisfy the following properties:

Theorem 3.2
.

For all
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{3,d}
it holds

i)

(3.4)

\|\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{z}\|_{1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1},

ii)

and for
p=2,3,\ldots

(3.5)

\|\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|\mathbf{z}\|_{1}^{p}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2^{p-1}}\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2^{k}},\quad\|\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|\mathbf{w}\|_{1}^{p}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2^{p-1}}\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2^{k}}.

The proof of (
3.4
) is straightforward. Moreover, the proof of (
3.5
) follows in the same as the one presented in
[
6
]
, where the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is used repeated times and having in mind that
|\omega|=1
.

In order to study analytic properties of stochastic processes taking values in this algebra one needs to consider its completion with respect to the
\ell^{2}-
norm. In the next section we study the completion of
\mathcal{G}_{3}
with respect to the
p
-norm, which we denote by
\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{3}^{(p)}.
This closure is widely studied in the literature in the classical case of Grassmann algebras (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
33
,
35
]
). Also, remark that by (
3.4
) we have that
\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{3}^{(1)}
has a Banach algebra structure. The study of completion of the
p-
norm has the purpose of establish a ternary Fock space based on the
\ell^{2}
-inner product between two supernumbers
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{G}_{3}
given by

(3.6)

\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}z_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\overline{w_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}.

3.1.
Topological Algebra Associated with
\mathcal{G}_{3}

In order to establish an analysis and stochastic process theory in the framework of ternary Grassmann algebras we need to establish an equivalent to the classic Gel’fand triple
(\mathcal{S},\mathbf{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R},dx),\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
where
\mathcal{S}
is the space of test functions and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
denotes its dual (see for instance
[
36
]
). The commutative setting was first introduced by Kondratiev and adapted to the framework of Hida’s white noise space theory and commutative Fock space (see
[
23
,
29
]
). More, the commutative case is associated with bosons and applied to study solutions of stochastic differential equations and to model stochastic processes and their derivatives. The construction of the noncommutative counterpart of this theory in the classic setting (see e.g.
[
5
,
9
]
) was motivated by fermionic formulation. In our case of topological hypersymmetry we need to establish the corresponding noncommutative counterpart in terms of our algebra and to construct a Gel’fand triple in this ternary Grassmann setting.

Gel’fand triples allow to define other products (on itself not necessarily laws of composition) different from the usual inner product in the Hilbert space. One such example is the Wick product in the white noise space which is not a law of composition. By embedding the white noise space into an analogous space of stochastic distributions the Wick product becomes a law of composition by strict inclusion. Another important reason such a construction of the space of stochastic distributions is the fact that such spaces are necessary for a future study of their derivatives (see
[
5
]
).

Hence, we now recall a few facts from the classical case and from the theory of perfect spaces and strong algebras. For more details on these spaces we refer the reader to
[
20
,
19
]
.

Starting from a decreasing family of Hilbert spaces
(\mathcal{H}_{p},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{p}})_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}
, with increasing norms,

\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{-1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{-2}\ldots

it is known that the intersection
\mathcal{F}=\cap_{p=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{p}
is a Fréchet space. If, furthermore,
\mathcal{F}
is perfect then compactness is equivalent to being compact and bounded. In particular, this is ensured when for each
p
there exists
q>p
such that the injection map from
{\mathcal{H}_{q}}
into
{\mathcal{H}_{p}}
is compact. In the usual way we identify
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}^{\prime}
with
{\mathcal{H}}_{-p}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
together with its the dual
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:=\cup_{p=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{H}}_{-p}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}
forms a Gel’fand triple
(\mathcal{F},\Gamma(\mathcal{H}_{0}),\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
. Indeed, the dual
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
endowed with the strong topology defined in terms of the bounded sets of
\mathcal{F}
is then locally convex. Furthermore, the strong topology coincides with the inductive limit topology. See
[
11
, Section 3]
for a discussion. Thus, the space of distributions
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is the dual of a Fréchet nuclear space.

We recall here two statements about compactness and convergence of sequences in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

[
20
]

A set is (weakly or strongly) compact in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
if and only if it is compact in one of the spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{-p}
in the corresponding norm.

Proposition 3.4
.

[
20
]

Assume
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
perfect. Then, weak and strong convergence of sequences are equivalent, and a sequence converges (weakly or strongly) if and only if it converges in one of the spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{-p}
in the corresponding norm.

Now, we are going to show that
\mathcal{F}
can be made a topological algebra denoted
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
where the product satisfies the so-called Våge inequality. This ensures that we can consider
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
as an inductive can be done as limit of Hilbert spaces. This is used in the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

A topological algebra is assumed to be separately continuous in each variable. It is not immediate, but true that a strong algebra is jointly continuous in the two variables (see
[
16
, IV.26, Theorem 2]
and also the discussion in
[
12
, pp. 215-216]
).

In our case, we define

(3.1)

\mathcal{H}_{p}(\mathbf{c})=\left\{f=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{3}^{(2)}\;|\;\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{2p}<\infty\right\},

with
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. The coefficients give rise a sequence
\mathbf{c}=(c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}
of positive real numbers such that

(3.2)

c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\leq c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\quad\mbox{for all }\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}\mbox{ such that }\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I},

and where

(3.3)

\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2d}<\infty,\quad\mbox{for }d=1,2,3,\ldots

By construction we have

\mathcal{H}_{-q}(\mathbf{c})\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{-p}(\mathbf{c}),

if
p\geq q
.

From here on we abbreviate
\mathcal{H}_{-p}(\mathbf{c})
by
\mathcal{H}_{-p}
.

Definition 3.5
.

The norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}
in
\mathcal{H}_{-p}
is defined as

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2p}.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}
, then
c_{\mathbf{0}}=1
.

Proof.

If so, then

c_{\mathbf{0}}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=c_{\mathbf{0}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}=c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}

so that
c_{\mathbf{0}}=1.

∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{c}=(c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}
be such that
c_{\mathbf{0}}=1
and
c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}>1,
for all
\boldsymbol{\nu}\not=\mathbf{0}.
Then,

\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}=|f_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2},\quad\mbox{for all }f\in\mathcal{H}_{-p}.

Proof.

Since
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2p}=0
for every
\boldsymbol{\nu}\not=\mathbf{0}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|^{2}\left(\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|f_{\mathbf{0}}|^{2}.

∎

Definition 3.8
.

We consider the space

(3.4)

\mathfrak{S}_{1}=\cap_{p\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{p}

and its topological dual

(3.5)

\mathfrak{S}_{-1}=\cup_{p\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{-p},

which can be considered as analogues of the spaces
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, respectively, in our setting.

The next theorem introduces a Våge-like inequality
[
37
]
which permits the analysis of stochastic processes to be done locally in a Hilbert space.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
f\in\mathcal{H}_{-q}
and
g\in\mathcal{H}_{-p}
, with
p>q
, then

(3.6)

\left\|fg\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}\leq C_{p-q}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-q}}\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}},\quad\left\|gf\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}\leq C_{p-q}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-q}}\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}},

with
C_{p-q}>0
being a constant.

Proof.

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{H}_{-q}
and
g\in\mathcal{H}_{-p}
. Applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

\displaystyle\left\|fg\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}}|(fg)_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-2p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}}\left|\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-2p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}||g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}||f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}||g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}|\right)c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-2p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-p}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{-p}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}^{-p}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}^{-p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-p}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}^{-p}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}:\exists\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{-p}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}^{-p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-p}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}^{-p}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}:\exists\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{-2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathcal{I}:\exists\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}^{-2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-p}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\prime}}^{-p}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{-2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}|g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}|^{2}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}}^{-2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-p}\right)^{2}\|g\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}|f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}|c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-q}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-(p-q)}\right)^{2}\|g\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2(p-q)}\right)\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-q}}^{2}\|g\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}.

It remains to prove that there exists a sequence
\mathbf{c}=(c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}})
such that
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2d}<\infty
for all
d=1,2,\ldots
. We assume this sequence to be given by

c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=e^{\sum_{k}\varphi(3^{k-1}\nu_{k})}=e^{\varphi(\nu_{1})+\varphi(3\nu_{2})+\varphi(3^{2}\nu_{3})+\cdots},

where
\nu_{k}\in\{0,1,2\}.
We now look into the properties of such a function
\varphi.

Bering in mind that
c_{\mathbf{0}}=1
and
c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}
for all
\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I},
we obtain

i)

c_{\mathbf{0}}=e^{\sum_{k}\varphi(0)},
leading to
\varphi(0)=0;

ii)

since
c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}
if
\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{I},
we have that for a given position
k\in\mathbb{N},

\nu_{k}+\mu_{k}\not=3,4;

iii)

c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}
implies

e^{\sum_{k}\varphi(3^{k-1}\nu_{k})}e^{\sum_{j}\varphi(3^{j-1}\mu_{j})}=e^{\sum_{k}\varphi\big(3^{k-1}(\nu_{k}+\mu_{k})\big)};

iv)

furthermore, for integers
k>j
and
\nu_{k},\mu_{j}\in\{1,2\}
we have
3^{k-1}\nu_{k}>3^{j-1}\mu_{j}
so that
\varphi
should be an increasing function, satisfying to
\varphi(a)+\varphi(b)=\varphi(a+b),
for
a,b>0.

For example, consider
\varphi(x)=x,~x>0.
Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{|\boldsymbol{\nu}\not=\mathbf{0}}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|\not=\mathbf{0}}}e^{-2d\sum_{k}3^{k-1}\nu_{k}}

Now, we split this sum in terms of the number
\#\boldsymbol{\nu}
of non-zero entries in the sequence
\boldsymbol{\nu}.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=m}}e^{-2d\sum_{k}3^{k-1}\nu_{k}}.

We observe that, for
m=1
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=1}}e^{-2d\sum_{k}3^{k-1}\nu_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-2d3^{k-1}\nu_{k}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-2d3^{k-1}}\qquad(\mbox{recall: }\nu_{k}=1,2)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-2dk}=e^{-2d}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2d}}<\infty.

Furthermore, remark that
0<e^{-2d}<1,~d=1,2,\ldots
so that
\frac{1}{1-e^{-2d}}>1.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle e^{-2d}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2d}}<1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 2e^{-2d}<1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle-2d<-\ln 2

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle d>\ln\sqrt{2}.

Now, for
\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=m>1
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=m}}e^{-2d\sum_{k}3^{k-1}\nu_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=m}}e^{-2d\nu_{1}}e^{-2d3\nu_{2}}\cdots e^{-2d3^{m-1}\nu_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-2dk}\right)^{m}=\left(\frac{e^{-2d}}{1-e^{-2d}}\right)^{m},\quad m=1,2,3,\ldots

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{-2d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{I}}\atop{\#\boldsymbol{\nu}=m}}e^{-2d\sum_{k}3^{k-1}\nu_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-2d}}{1-e^{-2d}}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-e^{-2d}}{1-2e^{-2d}}.

Finally, we remark that although none of the Banach algebras
\mathcal{H}_{-p}
is commutative the second inequality in (
3.6
) holds with the same value of constant
C_{p-q}
. Indeed, due to the multiplication rules (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) we have that
f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\mu})\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}=g_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\mathbf{e}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\nu}},
so that
\left\|fg\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}=\left\|gf\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{-p}}.
∎

There is more than one possible such Gel’fand triple. One particularly convenient space of stochastic distributions has been introduced by Yuri Kondratiev, and has a special algebraic structure. It is a strong algebra, as defined above, and in fact provided to the authors of
