CoTBal: Comprehensive Task Balancing for Multi-Task Visual Instruction Tuning
Yanqi Dai, Zebin You, Dong Jing, Yutian Luo, Nanyi Fei, Guoxing Yang, Zhiwu Lu

Abstract
Visual instruction tuning is an important training stage for large multimodal models. Nevertheless, when learning multiple visual tasks simultaneously, this approach may lead to suboptimal and imbalanced overall performance due to latent knowledge conflicts across tasks. To mitigate this issue, we introduce a novel Co mprehensive T ask Bal ancing ( CoTBal ) algorithm tailored for multi-task visual instruction tuning. To our knowledge, this is the first work to explore multi-task optimization in visual instruction tuning. Specifically, we consider two critical dimensions for task balancing: (1) Inter-Task Contribution , which represents the phenomenon where learning one task could enhance the performance on others owing to the overlapping knowledge domains across tasks, and (2) Intra-Task Difficulty , which indicates the inherent learning difficulty of a single task. Furthermore, by quantifying these with performance-based metrics, comprehensive task balancing is thus achieved by assigning greater weight to tasks that offer substantial contributions to others, receive minimal contributions from others, and present high learning difficulties. Extensive experiments on three benchmarks demonstrate that our CoTBal algorithm results in superior and more balanced overall performance in multi-task visual instruction tuning.

Inter-Task Contribution:

The extent of these overlaps varies across tasks, resulting in differing degrees of inter-task contributions.

As illustrated in Figure

2.

3.

3
Methodology

In this section, we first introduce the Visual Instruction Task Weighting (VITW) paradigm tailored for multi-task visual instruction tuning.
Building upon this paradigm, we elaborate on three task weighting strategies based on two crucial dimensions: inter-task contribution and intra-task difficulty.
Finally, all three strategies are integrated into our Comprehensive Task Balancing (CoTBal) algorithm.

3.1
Visual Instruction Task Weighting

Typically, data from various instruction-following tasks are mixed indiscriminately for visual instruction tuning.
In this process, the training loss is computed as the average cross-entropy loss across all valid tokens, as expressed in the following equation:

L=\frac{\sum^{N}_{i=1}\sum^{S_{i}}_{j=1}\sum^{T_{ij}}_{k=1}-\log(p(t_{ijk}))}{\sum^{N}_{i=1}\sum^{S_{i}}_{j=1}T_{ij}},

(1)

where
N
denotes the task number,
S_{i}
indicates the sample number of Task
i
,
T_{ij}
represents the valid token number in the
j
-th sample for Task
i
, and
t_{ijk}
signifies the
k
-th valid token in the
j
-th sample for Task
i
.
However, the traditional task weighting paradigm in MTL is not directly applicable in this context, which computes the total loss as a weighted average of all single-task losses.
Due to variations in sample size and sequence length across tasks, the valid token number for each task within a single batch is different.
Therefore, computing each single-task loss and averaging across all tasks may introduce implicit weight to the losses of valid tokens, leading to biased learning across tasks.

To address this issue, we propose the Visual Instruction Task Weighting (VITW) paradigm, which is specifically designed for multi-task visual instruction tuning.
The training loss of VITW is calculated as:

L_{\text{VITW}}=\frac{\sum^{N}_{i=1}\sum^{S_{i}}_{j=1}\sum^{T_{ij}}_{k=1}-\lambda_{i}\log(p(t_{ijk}))}{\sum^{N}_{i=1}\sum^{S_{i}}_{j=1}\lambda_{i}T_{ij}},

(2)

where
\lambda_{i}
signifies the weight of Task
i
.
The losses of valid tokens are assigned task-specific weight and averaged at the token level, rather than at the task level, to ensure an equitable consideration for each valid token.
This paradigm is a solid foundation for both our algorithm and future research in multi-task visual instruction tuning.

3.2
Inter-Task Contribution Balancing

Although the focal points of different tasks may vary in visual instruction tuning, a central shared objective exists: to improve the capability of visual information understanding and reasoning.
As depicted in Figure
1(a)
, the detailed image captioning data from ShareGPT-4V
[
9
]
and the visual question answering data from VQAv2
[
18
]
contain overlapping information within the same image, such as color, quantity, and category, exemplifying the overlapping knowledge domains among tasks.
Therefore, it is reasonable to argue that learning one task could potentially enhance the performance on others, a phenomenon termed inter-task contribution, as supported by the experimental results in Section
4
.
The extent of inter-task contributions varies depending on the degree of overlap in the knowledge domains among tasks.

In practice, the inter-task contribution of Task
i
to Task
j
can be quantified as the normalized validation performance on Task
j
of a model trained on Task
i
.
This relationship is formally expressed in the following equation:

C_{ij}=\frac{V_{j}(i+\text{mini})-V_{j}(\text{mini})}{V_{j}(j+\text{mini})-V_{j}(\text{mini})},

(3)

where
V_{j}(i+\text{mini})
denotes the performance on Task
j
of a model trained on the entire dataset or a large enough subset of Task
i
and mini subsets of all other tasks, and
V_{j}(\text{mini})
indicates the performance on Task
j
of a model trained on mini subsets of all tasks.
The large enough subsets are randomly sampled from the entire datasets, while the mini subsets are randomly sampled from the large enough subsets.
To ensure fairness across tasks, each of these two sampling rates remains consistent across all tasks and is independent of the other.
Notably, these mini subsets are added to the training data to ensure the model understands the instruction demands of all tasks.
In the formula,
V_{j}(\text{mini})
is subtracted from both the numerator and the denominator, which mitigates the influence of incorporating mini subsets into the training set on the validation performance on Task
j
.

Based on the precise quantification of the inter-task contribution, we propose two types of task weighting strategies for inter-task contribution balancing:

1.

Task-Outward Contribution Balancing
:
We define the task-outward contribution,
C_{\text{out}}
, as the average inter-task contribution of a single task to all other tasks.
This concept signifies the extent to which a given task contributes to the performance of all other tasks.
Therefore, we propose assigning greater weight to tasks with higher
C_{\text{out}}
.
In this way, the model prioritizes tasks that are more beneficial for overall multi-task training, leading to superior overall performance.
Specifically, the task weight,
\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{out}}}
, for task-outward contribution balancing is computed as:

C_{\text{out},i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}C_{ij},

(4)

\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{out}}}=N\times\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\bm{C_{\textbf{out}}}}{T}\right),

(5)

where
C_{\text{out},i}
represents the task-outward contribution of Task
i
,
\bm{C_{\textbf{out}}}
denotes the task-outward contribution vector of all tasks, and
T
is the temperature hyperparameter adjusting the smoothness of the weight distribution.

2.

Task-Inward Contribution Balancing
:
Conversely, we characterize the task-inward contribution,
C_{\text{in}}
, as the average inter-task contribution from all other tasks to a single task.
This concept represents the degree to which the performance of a given task benefits from all other tasks.
Tasks that receive minimal contributions from others are prone to exhibiting lower performance compared to tasks that benefit from more collaborative training.
Therefore, we suggest assigning greater weight to tasks with lower
C_{\text{in}}
to mitigate performance imbalance.
Specifically, the task weight,
\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{in}}}
, for task-inward contribution balancing is calculated as:

C_{\text{in},i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}C_{ji},

(6)

\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{in}}}=N\times\text{softmax}\left(-\frac{\bm{C_{\textbf{in}}}}{T}\right),

(7)

where
C_{\text{in},i}
denotes the task-inward contribution of Task
i
,
\bm{C_{\textbf{in}}}
signifies the task-inward contribution vector of all tasks, and
T
is the same temperature hyperparameter utilized in
Equation

5
.

Subsequently, we integrate the two aforementioned task weighting strategies. The specific task weight,
\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{C}}}
, for inter-task contribution balancing is determined as:

\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{C}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{out}}}+\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{in}}}).

(8)

3.3
Intra-Task Difficulty Balancing

Moreover, the inherent learning difficulty, termed intra-task difficulty, varies across different tasks in visual instruction tuning.
As illustrated in Figure
1(b)
, various tasks exhibit distinct patterns of performance improvement w.r.t. increasing training data amount.
Tasks quickly achieving near-optimal performance with a limited training dataset are considered to have lower intra-task difficulties, whereas those requiring the entire dataset to reach optimal performance are regarded as having higher intra-task difficulties.
For example, in order of increasing intra-task difficulties, the order of the tasks shown in Figure
1(b)
is as follows:
visual question answering on VQAv2
[
18
]
, detailed image captioning on ShareGPT-4V
[
9
]
, and visual grounding on RefCOCO
[
22
,
42
]
.

In practice, the intra-task difficulty of Task
i
can be quantified as the normalized validation performance gap on Task
i
between a model trained on the entire dataset or a large enough subset and a model trained on a mini subset of Task
i
.
Furthermore, we repurpose the additional models trained for inter-task contribution balancing to reduce the time cost.
Consequently, the intra-task difficulty of Task
i
can be calculated as follows:

D_{i}=1-\frac{V_{i}(\text{mini})}{V_{i}(i+\text{mini})},

(9)

where
V_{i}(i+\text{mini})
denotes the performance on Task
i
of a model trained on the entire dataset or a large enough subset of Task
i
and mini subsets of all other tasks, and
V_{i}(\text{mini})
indicates the performance on Task
i
of a model trained on mini subsets of all tasks.
Importantly, compared to the contribution of Task
i
to Task
i
itself, the inter-task contributions of other tasks to Task
i
are minimal.
Accordingly, the impact of mini subsets from other tasks on the Task
i
validation performance is negligible, so that the additional time cost is markedly reduced with minimal error in computing intra-task difficulties through model repurposing.

Based on the measurement of the intra-task difficulty, we introduce a task weighting strategy for intra-task difficulty balancing.
Specifically, due to the various degrees of intra-task difficulties across tasks, training all tasks equally may result in underfitting in more challenging tasks, despite simpler ones being adequately trained or overfitted.
Therefore, we recommend assigning greater weight to tasks that have higher intra-task difficulties.
The task weight,
\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{D}}}
, for intra-task difficulty balancing is computed as:

\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{D}}}=N\times\text{softmax}(\frac{\bm{D}}{T}),

(10)

where
\bm{D}
denotes the intra-task difficulty vector of all tasks, and
T
is the same temperature hyperparameter utilized in
Equation

5
and
Equation

7
.

3.4
Comprehensive Task Balancing

Finally, these task weighting strategies for inter-task contribution and intra-task difficulty balancing are integrated into our CoTBal algorithm.
The specific task weight,
\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{CoTBal}}}
, for comprehensive task balancing is computed as:

\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{CoTBal}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{C}}}+\bm{\lambda_{\textbf{D}}}).

(11)

In summary, tasks that offer substantial inter-task contributions to others and present high intra-task difficulties are assigned greater weight to achieve superior overall performance, while tasks that receive minimal inter-task contributions from others are assigned greater weight to mitigate performance imbalance.
Additionally, the task weights are determined before training the final model and remain unchanged during training.

4.1.3
Evaluation Metrics

We first report the common evaluation metrics for each visual task, in which Ref-test and Ref-testB indicate the two test sets of
Kazemzadeh et al. 2014
, and Refg-test signifies the test set of
Mao et al. 2016
.
Furthermore, inspired by the traditional MTL evaluation, we introduce two overall metrics:

\bm{\Delta I}
%
, average per-task improvement, and
\bm{\Delta E}
%
, average per-task error, in test performance on fine-tuned tasks compared to the STL baseline, which are calculated as:

I_{i}=\frac{1}{K_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{i}}(-1)^{\delta_{ij}}\frac{M_{\text{e},ij}-M_{\text{b},ij}}{M_{\text{b},ij}},

(12)

\Delta I\%=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{i},

(13)

\Delta E\%=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max(0,-I_{i}),

(14)

where
N
represents the task number,
K_{i}
signifies the metric number for Task
i
, and
I_{i}
denotes the test performance improvement on Task
i
.

M_{\text{e},ij}
and
M_{\text{b},ij}
are the values on the
j
-th metric for Task
i
of the model trained by the evaluated method and the STL baseline.

\delta_{ij}
is an indicator function, where
\delta_{ij}=0
if a higher value is better on the
j
-th metric for Task
i
, and
\delta_{ij}=1
otherwise.

\Delta I\%
reflects the extent of overall performance improvement, while
\Delta E\%
signifies the degree of overall performance imbalance.

Moreover, to assess the generalizability of methods, we introduce
\bm{\Delta I_{\text{zero}}}
%
, average per-task improvement in test performance on zero-shot tasks compared to EW.
The calculation of
\Delta I_{\text{zero}}\%
is similar to that of
\Delta I\%
, except the STL baseline is replaced with the EW method.

4.1.4
Implementation Details

In the main experiments, we fine-tune the pretrained model of LLaVA-v1.5-7B on 8
\times
A100 GPUs using the same settings as
Liu et al. 2024a
.
In the Academic Benchmark, CoTBal performs task balancing across all visual tasks, whereas using a weight of
1.0
(the average weight value for all visual tasks) for ShareGPT.
In the Large Benchmark, we consider each task group as a whole, and perform balancing at the group level.
Besides, the temperature hyperparameter
T
is set to
0.5
in the Academic Benchmark and the Chat Benchmark, while it is set to
1.0
in the Large Benchmark.

To compute inter-task contributions and intra-task difficulties, the entire datasets and
1/32
nd mini subsets of tasks are utilized in the Academic Benchmark, which is guided by the training loss decline pattern, as detailed in Appendix
C
.
In the Chat Benchmark, there are no specific constraints on the output format.
Consequently, only the entire datasets of tasks are required, without the need for mini subsets, simplifying the form of CoTBal, as shown in Appendix
F
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we propose a
Comprehensive Task Balancing (CoTBal)
algorithm, the first work to explore multi-task optimization in visual instruction tuning of LMMs.
Specifically, we initially design a Visual Instruction Task Weighting (VITW) paradigm, which averages weighted losses at the token level.
Building upon this paradigm, we introduce three task weighting strategies based on inter-task contribution and intra-task difficulty.
Experiments show that CoTBal outperforms existing methods, significantly improving overall performance while ensuring task balance.

Appendix C
The Sampling Rate for Mini Subsets in the Academic Benchmark

As discussed in Section
3.2
, mini subsets enable the model to understand the instruction demands of all tasks.
Due to the remarkable few-shot learning capabilities of large models, incorporating a small amount of training data can significantly enhance the model’s adherence to the task instructions.
In our experiments on the Academic Benchmark, the mini subset from each task is obtained by randomly sampling
1/32
nd of the entire dataset from that task.
As shown in Figure
3
, this sampling ratio is informed by the decline pattern of training loss, which exhibits a rapid decrease during the initial 100+ steps, followed by a gradual reduction until reaching the final 3,700+ steps.
This pattern suggests that the model effectively grasps the instruction demands in the initial phase, with the subsequent phase primarily focused on acquiring the knowledge embedded within the data.
Practically, we recommend ensuring that the mini subset from each task contains at least 1,000 data points.

Appendix F
A Simpler Form of CoTBal in the Chat Benchmark

In the Chat Benchmark, there are no specific constraints on the output format.
Consequently, only the entire datasets of tasks are required, without the need for mini subsets, simplifying the form of CoTBal.
Specifically, the inter-task contribution of Task
i
to Task
j
can be calculated as:

C_{ij}=\frac{V_{j}(i)-V_{j}(\text{base})}{V_{j}(j)-V_{j}(\text{base})},

(15)

where
V_{j}(i)
represents the validation performance on Task
j
of a model trained on Task
i
,
V_{j}(j)
denotes the validation performance on Task
j
of a model trained on Task
j
itself, and
V_{j}(\text{base})
signifies the validation performance on Task
j
of a pretrained base model.
Additionally, the intra-task difficulty of Task
i
can be computed as:

D_{i}=1-\frac{V_{i}(\text{mini}_{i})}{V_{i}(i)},

(16)

where
V_{i}(\text{mini}_{i})
representes the validation performance on Task
i
of a model trained on the mini subset of Task
i
, and
V_{i}(i)
denotes the validation performance on Task
i
of a model trained on Task
i
itself.

Appendix G
The Precise Calculation Approach for Intra-Task Difficulty

In the precise calculation approach for intra-task difficulty, the intra-task difficulty of Task
i
can be computed as:

D_{i}=1-\frac{V_{i}(\text{mini}_{i})}{V_{i}(i)},

(17)

where
V_{i}(\text{mini}_{i})
representes the validation performance on Task
i
of a model trained on the mini subset of Task
i
, and
V_{i}(i)
denotes the validation performance on Task
i
of a model trained on Task
i
itself.
