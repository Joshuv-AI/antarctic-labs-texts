Weighted and Near Weighted Potential Games with Application to Game Theoretic Control footnoteinfo
Daizhan Cheng, Zhengping Ji

Abstract
An algorithm is proposed to verify whether a finite game is a weighted potential game (WPG) without pre-knowledge on its weights. Then the algorithm is also applied to find the closest WPG for a given finite game. The concept and criterion of near weighted potential games (NWPGs) are given, indicating the evolutionary equivalence between a finite game and its closest WPG. Based on this, a method is proposed for replacing PGs by NWPGs in game theoretic control, which improved the applicability of PG-based optimization.



keywords

near weighted potential game, game theoretic control, semi-tensor product of matrices.

Introduction

In 1973, Rosenthal proposed a new type of games called potential games (PGs) in
[
26
]
. The paper also revealed that some practically important games such as congestion games are potential. Since then many follow up investigations have been done
[
16
,
1
,
22
,
23
]
. A systematic research on fundamental properties of PGs was presented in
[
24
]
. The theory of PGs has also been used to many engineering problems, including distributed power control and scheduling
[
17
]
, road pricing
[
29
]
, consensus of multi-agent systems
[
20
]
, etc. Particularly, the fact that PGs have pure Nash equilibria makes the optimization via game-based approach technically possible, therefore it has become the kernel of game theoretic control
[
13
,
14
]
.

Unfortunately, the set of finite PGs forms a lower dimensional subspace in the whole space of finite games
[
2
,
9
]
. This essential weakness impedes its applications to many game-based systems. There are two ways to improve the applicability of PGs: (i) weighted potential game (WPG) approach
[
24
]
; (ii) near potential game (NPG) approach
[
3
]
.

Consider the WPG approach. Since a WPG has exactly the same advantages as a PG, and the set of WPGs is much larger than that of PGs, it is a reasonable idea to replace the former by the weighed ones to improve its applicability. However, there have been almost no applications of WPGs reported in literature, the obstacle for which lies in that there is no efficient way to determine a set of proper weights. As for a pre-assigned set of weights, the game is obviously equivalent to a PG; to the authors’ best knowledge, there is no efficient way to verify if a finite game is a WPG, unless the weights are pre-assigned.

Next, consider the NPG approach. As is described in
[
3
]
, an NPG comes from a PG with certain small perturbations on its payoff functions. This kind of NPGs are of limited usages, because for a given game it is difficult to see whether there is a PG “nearby”. A more challenging problem is, it is very likely that there exists a WPG, which is closer than any other PG to the given game, but we were not able to find it up to now.

The first aim of this paper is to provide an algorithm to verify whether a finite game is a WPG, without assigning its weights in advance. Secondly, for a given finite game, the algorithm can provide the closest WPG to approximate it. Meanwhile, if the weights of a WPG are unknown, the algorithm can be used to reveal them. Hence, this method makes it possible to take WPG as a substitute for PG, paving a way for applying them to practical problems. For instance, the game theoretic control problem
[
13
]
is considered, where a PG is the kernel for the control design. With the aid of evolutionary equivalence, a technique is proposed to replace PGs by near weighted potential games (NWPGs), which provides much more freedom for designing game-based optimization.

Our main tool is the semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices, which is a generalization of conventional matrix product
[
5
]
.
It has been successfully applied to the analysis and control of Boolean networks
[
4
,
6
,
10
,
18
]
, graph theory
[
30
]
, etc. Recently, it has also been used to investigate the problems of (networked) evolutionary games
[
8
,
15
]
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce some preliminaries, including (i) a brief review of STP; (ii) the definition of PGs; (iii) the algebraic expression of finite games. Section 3 considers the weighted potential equation (WPE). Moreover, the formula for calculating potential functions by solving WPEs is presented. Some algebraic properties of WPEs, including its subspaces, dimension, and basis are revealed. Finally, an algorithm is obtained to calculate the weights of a WPG. Section 4 introduces the concept of NWPGs and an algorithm to check the evolutionary equivalence of a game with its closest WPG. In Section 5, a technique is provided to find the closest WPG for a given finite game while its potential function is pre-assigned. This technique makes it possible to substitute PGs with NWPGs in game theoretic control. Section 6 is a conclusion.

Semi-tensor Product of Matrices

This subsection provides a brief survey on semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices. We refer to
[
5
,
6
]
for more details.

Definition
\thethm

[
4
,
5
]
:
Let
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
,
N\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
, and
t=\lcm\{n,p\}
be the least common multiple of
n
and
p
.
The STP of
M
and
N
, denoted by
M\ltimes N
, is defined as

\displaystyle M\ltimes N:=\left(M\otimes I_{t/n}\right)\left(N\otimes I_{t/p}\right)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{mt/n\times qt/p},

(1)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product.

We briefly review some basic properties:

Proposition
\thethm

1.

(Associative Law)
\forall F\in{\mathcal{M}}_{m\times n}
,
G\in{\mathcal{M}}_{p\times q}
,
H\in{\mathcal{M}}_{r\times s}
,

\displaystyle(F\ltimes G)\ltimes H=F\ltimes(G\ltimes H).

(2)

2.

(Distributive Law)
\forall a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}F\ltimes(aG\pm bH)=aF\ltimes G\pm bF\ltimes H,\\
(aF\pm bG)\ltimes H=aF\ltimes H\pm bG\ltimes H.\end{cases}

(3)

As for the transpose and inverse, we have

Proposition
\thethm

\displaystyle(A\ltimes B)^{\mathrm{T}}=B^{\mathrm{T}}\ltimes A^{\mathrm{T}}.

(4)

Proposition
\thethm

Assume
A
and
B
are invertible, then

\displaystyle(A\ltimes B)^{-1}=B^{-1}\ltimes A^{-1}.

(5)

Remark 1
.

Note that when
n=p
,
M\ltimes N=MN
. That is to say, the STP is a generalization of conventional matrix product. Moreover, it keeps almost all the properties of conventional matrix product
[
5
]
. Hence throughout this paper the matrix product is assumed to be the STP and the symbol
\ltimes
is mostly omitted.

The following property is for STP only.

Proposition 2

Let
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a column vector and
M
a matrix. Then

\displaystyle X\ltimes M=\left(I_{m}\otimes M\right)X.

(6)

[
4
,
5
]
A matrix
W_{[m,n]}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{mn\times mn}
, defined by

\displaystyle W_{[m,n]}=\left[I_{n}\otimes\delta_{m}^{1},I_{n}\otimes\delta_{m}^{2},\cdots,I_{n}\otimes\delta_{m}^{m}\right].

(7)

is called the
(m,n)
-th swap matrix.

The basic function of the swap matrix is to “swap” two vectors. That is,

Proposition 4

Let
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be two columns. Then

\displaystyle W_{[m,n]}\ltimes X\ltimes Y=Y\ltimes X.

(8)

2.2
From Finite Game to Finite Potential Game

Definition 5

[
12
]
A finite (non-cooperative normal) game is a triple
G=(N,S,C)
, where

(i)

N=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
is the set of players;

(ii)

S=\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
is called the profile, where
S_{i}=\{1,2,\cdots,k_{i}\}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
is called the set of strategies of player
i
.

(iii)

C=(c_{1},\cdots,c_{n}):S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where each component
c_{i}:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is called the payoff (or utility) function of player
i
.

Denote by
{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
the set of finite games with
n
players and
k_{i}
strategies for player
i
,
i=1,\cdots,n
. Denote
\kappa:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
.

Identify the strategy
j\in S_{i}
with
\delta_{k_{i}}^{j}
called the vector form of strategy
j
. Then
c_{i}
can be expressed algebraically as

\displaystyle c_{i}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=V_{i}\ltimes_{j=1}^{n}x_{j},

(9)

where
V_{i}
is called the structure vector of payoff function
c_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
.

Definition 6

[
24
]
A finite game
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n,k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
is said to be a WPG, if there exists a function
P:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, called the potential function, and
n
constants
w_{i}>0
,
i=1,\cdots,n
called weights, such that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}c_{i}(x_{i},s_{-i})-c_{i}(y_{i},s_{-i})=w_{i}\left(P(x_{i},s_{-i})-P(y_{i},s_{-i})\right),\\
\forall x_{i},y_{i}\in S_{i},\;\forall s_{-i}\in S_{-i}:=\prod_{j\neq i}S_{j},~i=1,\cdots,n,\end{array}

If
w_{i}=1
,
i=1,\cdots,n
,
G
is called a (pure) PG.

A repeated game is called an evolutionary game. If the strategies of player
i
at time
t+1
, denoted by
x_{i}(t+1)
, depend only on the strategies
x_{j}(t)
and payoffs
c_{j}(t)
of players
j=1,\cdots,n
, it is called a Markoven type evolutionary game.

Proposition 7

[
8
]
The strategy profile dynamics (SPD) of an Markoven evolutionary game can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x_{1}(t+1)=f_{1}(x_{1}(t),\cdots,x_{n}(t))\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}(t+1)=f_{n}(x_{1}(t),\cdots,x_{n}(t)),\\
\end{cases}

(12)

where
f_{i}:\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Delta_{k_{j}}\rightarrow\Delta_{k_{i}}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
are determined by the strategy updating rule.

Again using the vector expression of strategies (identify
x_{i}=j\in S_{i}
with
\delta_{k_{i}}^{j}
), the evolutionary dynamic equation (
12
) can be expressed in its algebraic form as

\displaystyle x(t+1)=Lx(t),

(13)

where
x(t)=\ltimes_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}(t)
,
L\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa\times\kappa}
.

The SPD of a dynamic game is determined by the strategy updating rule. To simplify the statement, in this paper we assume the strategy updating rule is myopic best response arrangement (MBRA), that is,

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+1)=\underset{s_{i}\in S_{i}}{\mathrm{\argmax}}~c_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i}(t)).

(14)

3
Weighted Potential Games

In this section we first review some results about finite PGs. Extensions are done to make them applicable to WPGs. Finally, an algorithm is proposed for verifying WPGs and calculating their weights.

3.1
Equation for a Weighted Potential Game

The following proposition is based on results in
[
7
]
. The extension has also been presented in
[
9
]
.

Proposition 8

Assume
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
.

(i)

G
is a WPG with weights
w_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, if and only if, the following potential equation (
15
) has solution:

\displaystyle E_{w}\xi=B,

(15)

where

\displaystyle E_{w}=\begin{bmatrix}-w_{2}E_{1}&w_{1}E_{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
-w_{3}E_{1}&0&w_{1}E_{3}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&~&~&\vdots\\
-w_{n}E_{1}&0&0&\cdots&w_{1}E_{n}\end{bmatrix},

(16)

\displaystyle B=\begin{bmatrix}\left(w_{1}V_{2}-w_{2}V_{1}\right)^{T}\\
\left(w_{1}V_{3}-w_{3}V_{1}\right)^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\left(w_{1}V_{n}-w_{n}V_{1}\right)^{T}\\
\end{bmatrix};

(17)

and

\displaystyle E_{i}=I_{\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}k_{j}}\otimes{\bf 1}_{k_{i}}\otimes I_{\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}k_{j}},\quad i=1,\cdots,n.

(18)

(ii)

Let
\xi=(\xi_{1}^{T},\xi_{2}^{T},\cdots,\xi_{n}^{T})^{T}
be a solution of (
15
), where
\xi^{1}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa/k_{i}}
. Then the game has its potential function as
P=V^{P}\ltimes_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}
, where

\displaystyle V^{P}=\frac{1}{w_{i}}\left[V_{i}-\xi_{i}^{T}E_{i}^{T}\right],\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant n.

(19)

Remark 9
.

(i)

In equation (
18
) we assume
I_{\emptyset}:=1
. That is,

I_{\prod_{j=1}^{0}k_{j}}:=1;\quad I_{\prod_{j=n+1}^{n}k_{j}}:=1,

which means that such factors do not exist.

(ii)

(
19
) can be used to calculate
V^{P}
by choosing an arbitrary
i
from
\{1,\cdots,n\}
.

3.2
Basis of Weighted Potential Games

According to Proposition
8
, it is obvious that
G
is a WPG with
\{w_{i}>0\;|\;1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}
, if and only if,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}V_{2}^{T}-w_{2}V_{1}^{T}\\
w_{1}V_{3}^{T}-w_{3}V_{1}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
w_{1}V_{n}^{T}-w_{n}V_{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}=E_{w}\xi\in\Span(E_{w}).

(20)

Define
E^{e}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}I_{\kappa}&0\\
0&E_{w}\end{bmatrix}
, then (
20
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}V_{1}^{T}\\
w_{1}V_{2}^{T}-w_{2}V_{1}^{T}\\
w_{1}V_{3}^{T}-w_{3}V_{1}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
w_{1}V_{n}^{T}-w_{n}V_{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}=E^{e}\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{T}\\
\xi\end{bmatrix}\in\Span(E^{e}).

(21)

The left hand side of (
21
) can further be expressed as

C\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}^{T}\\
V_{2}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
V_{n}^{T}\end{pmatrix},

where

C=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}I_{\kappa}&0&\cdots&0\\
-w_{2}I_{\kappa}&w_{1}I_{\kappa}&\cdots&0\\
~&~&\ddots&~\\
-w_{n}I_{\kappa}&0&\cdots&w_{1}I_{\kappa}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

It is easy to calculate that

C^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{w_{1}}I_{\kappa}&0&\cdots&0\\
\frac{w_{2}}{w_{1}^{2}}I_{\kappa}&\frac{1}{w_{1}}I_{\kappa}&\cdots&0\\
~&~&\ddots&~\\
\frac{w_{n}}{w_{1}^{2}}I_{\kappa}&0&\cdots&\frac{1}{w_{1}}I_{\kappa}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Next, we define

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccl}E^{P}_{w}&:=&w_{1}C^{-1}E^{e}\\
&=&\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}I_{\kappa}&0\\
\begin{array}[]{c}w_{2}I_{\kappa}\\
w_{3}I_{\kappa}\\
\vdots\\
w_{n}I_{\kappa}\end{array}&E_{w}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=&\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}I_{\kappa}&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
w_{2}I_{\kappa}&-w_{2}E_{1}&w_{1}E_{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
w_{3}I_{\kappa}&-w_{3}E_{1}&0&w_{1}E_{3}&\cdots&~\\
~&~&~&~&\ddots&0\\
w_{n}I_{\kappa}&-w_{n}E_{1}&0&0&\cdots&w_{1}E_{n}\\
\end{bmatrix}\\
&\in&{\mathcal{M}}_{n\kappa\times s},\end{array}

where

\displaystyle s=\kappa+\frac{\kappa}{k_{1}}+\frac{\kappa}{k_{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\kappa}{k_{n}}.

(26)

Then we have

\displaystyle V^{T}_{G}:=\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}^{T}\\
V_{2}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
V_{n}^{T}\end{pmatrix}=C^{-1}E^{e}\begin{bmatrix}V^{T}_{1}\\
\xi\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{w_{1}}E^{P}_{w}\begin{bmatrix}V^{T}_{1}\\
\xi\end{bmatrix}.

(27)

Remark 10
.

It is worth noting that even if
\xi
has already been known we are still not able to determine
V_{G}
completely by (
27
). In fact, the condition for
G
to be a WPG depends on only the differences
V_{i}-V_{1}
,
i=2,\cdots,n
. Hence
V_{1}
is completely free. To determine
V_{G}
by
\xi
we can simply set
V_{1}=0
(or any constant vector). This is important in numerical calculations.

Denote the set of weighted potential games
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
by

G\in{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
. Then the above argument yields the following result:

Proposition 11

Given
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
, then
G\in{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}

with
\{w_{i}|1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}
, if and only if,

\displaystyle V^{T}_{G}\in\Span(E^{P}_{w}).

(28)

Precisely speaking, there exists
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s-\kappa}
such that (
27
) holds.

In the light of the argument in
[
7
]
, one sees easily that
E^{P}_{w}
has codimension
1
with respect to columns. Particularly, deleting the last column of
E^{P}_{w}
, denote what remains by

\tilde{E}^{P}_{w}=E^{P}_{w}\backslash\{\Col_{s}(E^{P}_{w})\},

(29)

then
\tilde{E}^{P}_{w}
is of full column rank.

We also have the following

Proposition 12

{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}

is a vector subspace of
{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{n\kappa}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\dim\left({\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}\right)=\kappa+\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\kappa}{k_{i}}-1.

(30)

Moreover,
\Col(\tilde{E}^{P}_{w})
is its basis.

Using least square approach, the following conclusion is obvious.

Corollary 13
.

Assume
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
with its structure vector
V_{G}
, and a set of weights
\{w_{i}\;|\;i=1,\cdots,n\}
are given. The closest weighted potential game of
G
having
\{w_{i}\}
as its weights, denoted by
G_{w}
, has its structure vector as

\displaystyle V_{G_{w}}^{T}=\tilde{E}^{P}_{w}x,

(31)

where
\tilde{E}^{P}_{w}
is defined in (
29
), and

\displaystyle x=\left[(\tilde{E}^{P}_{w})^{T}\tilde{E}^{P}_{w}\right]^{-1}(\tilde{E}^{P}_{w})^{T}V_{G}^{T}

(32)

Moreover, since
{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{n\kappa}
, the Euclidean distance between
G
and
G_{w}
is

\displaystyle d(G,G_{w})=\big\|V_{G}^{T}-V_{G_{w}}^{T}\big\|_{2}=\sqrt{(V_{G}-V_{G_{w}})(V_{G}^{T}-V_{G_{w}}^{T})}.

(33)

.

•

Step 1

–

1-a: Assume

,

. Using (

\xi^{1}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\xi^{1}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\kappa_{0}},

where

4
Near Weighted Potential Games

Since
{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
is not dense in
{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
, one cannot always find a WPG close to a given finite game within an arbitrary distance; but still, we can consider the resemblance of their dynamics when two games are both repeated, which motivates the investigation of near weighted potential games (NWPGs).

4.1
Verification of NWPGs

Near potential games were firstly proposed by
[
3
]
. Its basic idea is: if a finite game is close to a PG, then the original game may have the same dynamic behavior as the potential one. Intuitively, a near potential game (NPG) comes from a PG with certain mild perturbations on its payoff functions. Based on the orthogonal decomposition of finite games, we propose an alternative way to construct near potential games: starting from an arbitrary finite game
G
, find its closest PG, denoted by
G_{P}
. Then verify whether
G
and
G_{P}
have the same strategy profile dynamics. If “yes”,
G
is called an NPG.

Note that when a set of weights is fixed, corresponding to this set we have also a similar orthogonal decomposition as follows
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}=\hbox to0.0pt{$\underbrace{\phantom{\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{w}^{P_{0}}\quad\oplus\quad{\mathcal{G}}^{N}}}_{{\mathcal{G}}_{w}^{P}}$\hss}\quad{{\mathcal{G}}^{P_{0}}_{w}}\quad\oplus\quad\overbrace{{\mathcal{G}}^{N}\quad\oplus\quad{\mathcal{G}}_{w}^{H_{0}}}^{{\mathcal{G}}_{w}^{H}},

(42)

From now on we consider the WPG-based NWPGs. First we give a rigorous definition of it.

Definition 17

Given two finite evolutionary game
G
,
G^{\prime}
,

(i)

G
and
G^{\prime}
are said to be evolutionary equivalent, if their strategy profile dynamics are identical.

(ii)

If there exists an evolutionary (weighted) potential game
G^{P}
(
G^{P}_{w}
) such that

G
and
G^{P}
(
G^{P}_{w}
) are evolutionary equivalent, then
G
is called an NPG (NWPG).

In the following we give an algorithm to verify if a finite game is an NWPG.

•

1
to find the closest WPG, which has weights

and least square solution

5
Application of NWPG to Game Theoretic Control

The game theoretic control (GTC) is described in Figure
1
according to
[
13
]
.

Figure 1:
Hourglass Architecture of Game Theoretical Control

Roughly speaking, the optimization via game theoretic control can be described as follows: Consider a networked multi-agent system with
n
agents. Assume there is a global objective function
J(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
, where
x_{i}
is the action (or strategy) of the
i
-th agent. As is shown in Fig.
1
, GTC approach consists of two major steps: (i) Design utility (or payoff) functions for each agents such that the overall system becomes a PG with
J
as its potential function. (ii) Design a strategy updating rule (precisely, learning algorithm) such that when each agents are optimizing their own utility functions the system can converge to a Nash equilibrium, which is an optimal value of
J
(a local one it might be). Note that, in general, since each agent can only obtain its neighbors’ strategies, the learning algorithm must be based on local information.

As is discussed before, the application of PGs is very limited. In this section we provide a new method for game theoretic control by replacing PGs by NWPGs. Let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
and the overall performance criterion
J(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be given. If there exists
G^{P}_{w}\in{\mathcal{G}}^{w}_{[n;k_{1},\cdots,k_{n}]}
with potential function
J
, which is evolutionary equivalent to
G
, then the GTC approach can be used for
G
. In other words, we consider structures of the top two parts of Figure
1
and substitute them by Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
NWPG-based Game Theoretic Control

As for learning design, it is out of the scope of this paper. But we should emphasize that all the algorithms designed to realize the optimization for PG case are also applicable for NWPG case. We refer to

6
Conclusion

The WPGs and NWPGs are investigated. First, an algorithm is provided to verify if a finite game is a WPG, and further determine the unknown weights. Meanwhile, when a game is not weighted potential, the algorithm can find its closest WPG. A finite game evolutionary equivalent to its closest WPG is called a NWPG, for which all main properties of a PG remain true. A criterion for NWPGs is given. In the end we proposed a method to find the closest WPG
G^{P}_{w}
of a finite game
G
, which has a pre-assigned potential function; this result makes NWPGs applicable to game theoretic control when
G
and
G^{P}_{w}
are evolutionary equivalent.
