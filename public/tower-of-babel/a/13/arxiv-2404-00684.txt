Generative Retrieval as Multi-Vector Dense Retrieval
Shiguang Wu, Wenda Wei, Mengqi Zhang, Zhumin Chen, Jun Ma, Zhaochun Ren, Maarten de Rijke, Pengjie Ren

Abstract
. GR generates identifiers of relevant documents in an end-to-end manner using a sequence-to-sequence architecture for a given query. The relation between GR and other retrieval methods, especially those based on matching within dense retrieval models, is not yet fully comprehended. Prior work has demonstrated that GR with atomic identifiers is equivalent to single-vector dense retrieval. Accordingly, GR exhibits behavior analogous to hierarchical search within a tree index in dense retrieval when using hierarchical semantic identifiers. However, prior work focuses solely on the retrieval stage without considering the deep interactions within the decoder of GR . In this paper, we fill this gap by demonstrating that GR and MVDR share the same framework for measuring the relevance to a query of a document. Specifically, we examine the attention layer and prediction head of GR , revealing that GR can be understood as a special case of MVDR . Both methods compute relevance as a sum of products of query and document vectors and an alignment matrix. We then explore how GR applies this framework, employing distinct strategies for computing document token vectors and the alignment matrix. We have conducted experiments to verify our conclusions and show that both paradigms exhibit commonalities of term matching in their alignment matrix. Our finding applies to many GR identifier designs and provides possible explanations on how GR can express query-document relevance. As MVDR is the state-of-the-art dense retrieval method currently, understanding the connection between GR and MVDR is crucial for shedding light on the underlying mechanisms of GR and for developing, and understanding the potential of, new retrieval models.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, the advent of pre-trained language models has catalyzed the popularity of neural-based retrieval models within the information retrieval community
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
;
Ni et al., 2022b
;
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Qian et al., 2022
;
Tay et al., 2022
)
.

Neural-based retrieval models.

DR
(
DR
), as one of the effective neural-based retrieval methods, has achieved the state-of-the-art ranking performance on multiple benchmarks
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
;
Ni et al., 2022b
;
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
.
Several approaches have been proposed to use multiple vectors to represent documents or queries, a.k.a.,
MVDR

(
MVDR
)
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Qian et al., 2022
;
Zhou and Devlin, 2021
)
.

Recently,
GR

(
GR
) has emerged as a new paradigm in information retrieval.
It aims to generate identifiers of relevant documents for a given query directly and parametrizes the indexing, retrieval, and ranking process in dense retrieval systems into a single model.

GR
(
GR
) adopts a sequence-to-sequence architecture model and is trained to directly map queries to their relevant document identifiers.

GR
vs.
DR
.

Dense retrieval models typically employ encoders, e.g., BERT
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
, for encoding both queries and documents, while the
GR
model adopts an encoder for query encoding and a decoder for identifier generation.
Despite their superficial differences,
DR
and
GR
share key characteristics in their query-document relevance computations.
When the two methods use document identifiers such as sub-strings, titles, or semantic IDs as representations for documents, relevance to a query of a document is computed as the dot product of two vectors in both methods.
Dense retrieval involves using the direct product of the query vectors and document vectors as the relevance, while
GR
leverages the product of the last latent state from the decoder at each position with the prediction head, a.k.a., the word embedding lookup table.
Consequently, a natural question that arises in this context:

How is
GR
related to
DR
?

Although
GR
has shown promising results in various benchmarks as a new end-to-end retrieval paradigm
(
Li et al., 2023c
;
Wang et al., 2022
;
Sun et al., 2023
;
Yang et al., 2023
;
Lee et al., 2023a
)
, relatively few publications have closely examined how
GR
models work.

Nguyen and Yates (2023)
have shown that
GR
using atomic identifiers can be viewed as a variant of bi-encoders for dense retrieval because the word embedding lookup table in
GR
works exactly the same as the flat index in dense retrieval.
Thus, we can partially respond to the above question that
GR
with atomic identifiers
is
single-vector dense retrieval.
Although atomic identifiers are considered non-mainstream in
GR
, it offers an insightful perspective on the matter.

Nguyen and Yates (2023)
also discuss that
GR
with hierarchical semantic identifiers exhibits behavior similar to hierarchical search within a tree index in dense retrieval.
However, their discussion focuses only on the retrieval stage without rigorously considering deep interactions within the decoder.

GR
as
MVDR
.

In this work, we connect

to a state-of-the-art dense retrieval method,
MVDR
, in a rigorous way.
We illustrate that these two methods exhibit

.
We first examine the attention layer and the prediction head of

.
This corresponds to the unified

.
In Section

.

(i)

its simple document encoding and how

)
and
NP

), and

(ii)

the distinct alignment strategy employed by

).

Our discovery provides reliable explanations of how
GR
can express query-document relevance.
By explaining how the
GR

GR
is fundamentally different from dense retrieval methods and adds to the spectrum of neural-based retrieval models.
The connection we present provides the variants of
GR

Contributions.

Our main contributions in this paper are:

(1)

We offer new insights into

(2)

2.
Related Work

MVDR
(
MVDR
)
can be seen as a generalization of single-vector dual encoder models
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
;
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
.
Instead of encoding the complete content of both query and documents into a single low-dimensional vector,
MVDR
uses fine-grained token-level modeling for scoring.
Existing
MVDR
models such as ColBERT
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
compute query-document relevance by selecting the highest-scoring document token for each query token and aggregating the scores.
The postponed token-level interactions allow us to efficiently apply the model for retrieval, benefiting the effectiveness of modeling fine-grained interactions.

MVDR
overcomes the limited expressivity of single-vector retrieval and achieves significantly better results across various benchmarks
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
;
Ni et al., 2022b
;
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Qian et al., 2022
;
Lee et al., 2023b
;
Li et al., 2023a
)
.
However, due to the cost of storing vectors for each document token, it is challenging to scale the approach to large collections
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Formal et al., 2021a
;
Qian et al., 2022
;
Lee et al., 2023b
)
.

GR
(
GR
)
is an emerging paradigm in information retrieval
(
Tay et al., 2022
;
Zeng et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2022
;
Lee et al., 2023a
;
Pradeep et al., 2023
;
Rajput et al., 2023
)
.
It leverages generative models to directly generate identifiers of relevant documents.
This approach originated with
(
Cao et al., 2021
;
Tay et al., 2022
)
and has garnered considerable attention
(
Tang et al., 2023a
, see, e.g.,)
.
Currently, all implementations of the generative retrieval paradigm adhere to an encoder-decoder transformer architecture, e.g., T5
(
Raffel et al., 2020
)
and BART
(
Lewis et al., 2020
)
.
In this method, documents are initially associated with a concise token sequence that serves as an identifier.
The model is then trained to predict this token sequence autoregressively, using conventional cross-entropy loss.

One notable advantage of the generative retrieval model is its streamlined end-to-end architecture, which requires significantly less disk storage space compared to other retrieval methods.
However, it is important to note that due to the limited supervision of each token, the generative retrieval may not achieve comparable performance when compared to dense retrieval
(
Pradeep et al., 2023
;
Yuan et al., 2024
)
.

Connecting dense retrieval and generative retrieval.

Nguyen and Yates (2023)
show that
GR
with atomic identifiers is equivalent to single-vector dense retrieval.
They compare the inferential processes of
DR
with a tree index and
GR
with hierarchical identifiers.
However, the former is just an optimized version of the original
DR
without changing the semantic matching method, while the latter also considers the predicted IDs and the query in each generation step, which greatly affects how
GR
would express the relevance, but this is ignored in
(
Nguyen and Yates, 2023
)
.

Yuan et al. (2024)
empirically analyze the error rate at each generation step of
GR
and identify the problem of poor memory accuracy for fine-grained features compared with
DR
.
They straightforwardly integrated
GR
and
DR
into a new coarse-to-fine retrieval paradigm, combining their respective strengths, but circumvented an in-depth discussion of the connection between them.

In this work, we address the limitations listed above by showing that
GR
expresses query-document relevance in the same way as
MVDR
.
This connection is rigorously derived from the decoder of
GR
and can be applied to many identifiers.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we formulate our task and introduce key notation and the mainstream framework of
MVDR
models.

Task definition.

We formulate the retrieval task as ranking by relevance score.
Given a query
q
, we aim to retrieve relevant documents
d
in
\mathcal{D}
by ranking them by their relevance
\operatorname{rel}(d,q)
to
q
.

Notations.

Table
1
lists the main notation used in the paper.
We denote the word embedding lookup table in the decoder as
\mathbold{E}
and the vocabulary set as
\mathcal{V}
.
Each document
d
comprises
M
tokens. To ensure uniform length, padding tokens are added or excess tokens are truncated from each document.
The word embedding matrix of
d
is denoted as
\mathbold{E}_{d}\coloneqq[\mathbold{e}_{d_{1}},\ldots,\mathbold{e}_{d_{M}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
, and the latent token vector matrix after encoding is
\mathbold{D}\coloneqq[\mathbold{d}_{1},\ldots,\mathbold{d}_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
.
Each query
q
with
N
tokens has token vectors
\mathbold{Q}\coloneqq[\mathbold{q}_{1},\ldots,\mathbold{q}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
after encoding, similar to the documents.

Framework for
MVDR
.

Following
(
Qian et al., 2022
;
Lee et al., 2023b
)
,
MVDR
methods can be represented as a unified framework, in which the relevance between query
q
and document
d
is given by:

(1)

\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\frac{1}{Z}\operatorname{sum}(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A})=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}A_{ij},

where
\mathbold{A}
is the alignment matrix that controls whether a document and query token pair can be matched and contribute to the relevance, and
Z=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}
is used for normalization and is dropped in many
MVDR
methods.

Alignment strategy.
Different
MVDR
models adopt different alignment strategies, and, thus, a different alignment matrix
\mathbold{A}
.
It is often computed using heuristic algorithms, such as lexical exact match
(
Gao et al., 2021
)
, top-
1
relevant token match
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
, single-vector alignment
(
Karpukhin et al., 2020
;
Luan et al., 2021
)
, or sparse unary salience
(
Qian et al., 2022
)
.

Contrastive loss used in
MVDR
.

MVDR
methods usually use contrastive loss as the training target, where negative documents are used.
For a query
q
and target document
d
, the loss is computed as

(2)

\mathcal{L}(d,q)=-\log\frac{\exp{\operatorname{rel}(d,q)}}{\sum_{d^{-}\in\mathcal{D}^{-}}\exp{\operatorname{rel}(d^{-},q)}},

where
\mathcal{D}^{-}
is the collected negative set.

In-Depth Analysis of Generative Retrieval

To address the question posed in Section
1
,
this section conducts a detailed analysis of GR.
Specifically, we first illustrate the model architecture and training loss of the GR (Section
4.1
).
Subsequently, we derive that the training target of
GR
falls into the framework of
MVDR
(Section
4.2
):

(3)

\mathcal{L}(d,q)\propto\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}\right),

where
\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}
,
\mathbold{Q}
and
\mathbold{A}
correspond to
\mathbold{D}
,
\mathbold{Q}
and
\mathbold{A}
in Eq. (
1
).

4.1.
Model architecture and training loss

Model architecture.

We focus on the transformer sequence-to-sequence architecture utilized in GR, more precisely, the encoder-decoder structure.
Within this framework, the encoder primarily targets processing the input query, while the decoder is tasked with predicting document identifiers.

The decoder component consists of stacks of self-attention, cross-attention, and feed-forward layers.
We particularly underscore the significance of the cross-attention layers, as they facilitate interaction between query tokens and document tokens.

To predict the document token
d_{i}
at the
i
-th position, we compute the cross attention weights between query token vectors
\mathbold{Q}
and
\hat{\mathbold{d}}_{i-1}
from the previous attention layers at position
(i-1)
as follows:

(4)

\mathbold{\alpha}_{i}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbold{Q}^{\top}\mathbold{W}\hat{\mathbold{d}}_{i-1}),

where
\operatorname{softmax}(\cdot)
denotes the column-wise softmax function,
\mathbold{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the product of the attention matrices
\mathbold{W}_{K}
and
\mathbold{W}_{Q}
, i.e.,
\mathbold{W}=\mathbold{W}_{K}^{\top}\mathbold{W}_{Q}
, and
\mathbold{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Consequently, the output of the cross-attention layer is

(5)

\mathbold{h}_{i}=\mathbold{W}_{V}\mathbold{Q}\mathbold{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For simplicity, we ignore the non-linear activation functions, and the linear maps in the feedforward layers can be absorbed in attention weights, e.g.,
\mathbold{W}_{V}
.
Therefore,
\mathbold{h}_{i}
serves as the prediction head for generating the next token.

Training loss.

The loss function to minimize at position
i
is formulated as:

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(d,q)

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\frac{\exp{\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}}{\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\exp{\mathbold{e}_{v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=-\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+\log\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\exp{\mathbold{e}_{v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}.

4.2.
GR
has the same framework as
MVDR

Next, we demonstrate that GR shares a similar framework with MVDR, namely, that the logits within the loss function can be reformulated as a product of document word embeddings, query token vectors, and attention matrix. This formulation corresponds to Eq. (
1
).

In particular, as we employ teacher-forcing supervision, ground-truth document identifiers are directly fed into the decoder, and token vectors at all positions are computed simultaneously.
Based on this configuration, the overall loss is given by:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(d,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[M]}\log p(d_{i}\mid d_{i-1},\ldots,d_{0},q)

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[M]}\mathcal{L}_{i}(d,q)

(10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[M]}\left(-\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+\log\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\exp{\mathbold{e}_{v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}\right),

where
d_{0}
could be some special token such as
[BOS]
or the
[CLS]
token vector from the query.

When utilizing the sampled softmax loss, which involves employing several negative tokens instead of the entire set of tokens in the lookup embedding table, the loss exhibits a similar structure to the contrastive loss used in
DR
and
MVDR
.
Consequently, we treat the dot product of embedding
\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}
and token vector
\mathbold{h}_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}
, as the final relevance score at position
i
.
Further insights into the reason are elaborated in Appendix
A
.
We plug in the dot product with the computing of
\mathbold{h}_{i}
from Eq. (
5
) and obtain:

(11)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[M]}\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}

(12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[M]}\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{W}_{V}\mathbold{Q}\mathbold{\alpha}_{i}

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[M]}\sum_{j\in[N]}\mathbold{\tilde{e}}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}\alpha_{ij}

(14)

\displaystyle=\,\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}\right),

where
\mathbold{\tilde{e}}_{d_{i}}^{\top}=\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{W}_{V}
,

\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}=\mathbold{E}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{W}_{V}
,

\mathbold{A}=[\mathbold{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\mathbold{\alpha}_{M}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
,
and
\odot
is the element-wise matrix product operation.

Further, we have a more detailed computation

(15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbold{\hat{D}}_{-1}^{\top}\mathbold{W}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\right)\right),

where
\mathbold{\hat{D}}_{-1}=\left[\mathbold{\hat{d}}_{0},\mathbold{\hat{d}}_{1},\ldots,\mathbold{\hat{d}}_{M-1}\right]
is the output from the previous layer with the right-shifted document tokens as model input.

In conclusion, we observe a similar framework from
GR
to
MVDR
,
\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\operatorname{sum}(\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A})
, where the relevance is represented by an interaction of multiple “token vectors,” i.e.,
\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}
and
\mathbold{Q}
, from both query and document and aligned by a matrix
\mathbold{A}
.

.

PAWA

(
Wang et al., 2022
)
aims to improve the embedding modeling for distinguishing different semantics of a token ID at different positions.
Unlike the standard transformer, which uses a static embedding lookup table for every position,
PAWA
generates different embedding tables at each generation step.

PAWA
consists of a transformer decoder and an adaptive projection layer
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times|\mathcal{V}|\times d\times d}
.
The projection matrix of token
v
at the
i
-th position is

\mathbold{E}_{i,v}=\mathbf{E}[i,v,:,:]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Here,
\mathbf{E}
can be seen as a generalized version of the embedding lookup table that uses a matrix
\mathbold{E}_{i,v}
to represent each token
v
.
To get the generated embedding vector for token
v
at the
i
-th position,
PAWA
decoder first uses the transformer decoder to process the document into a set of latent vectors
\mathbold{D}^{\prime}=[\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
.
Then it multiplies the projection matrix
\mathbold{E}_{i,v}
with the latent vector
\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{i}
and gets the final embedding vector
\mathbold{e}_{i,v}=\mathbold{E}_{i,v}\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{i}
.

Therefore, we have the logit
\mathbold{e}_{v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}
in loss Eq. (
10
) replaced by
\mathbold{e}_{i,v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}=\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\prime\top}\mathbold{E}_{i,v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}

(17)

\mathcal{L}(d,q)=\sum_{i\in[M]}\left[-\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\prime\top}\mathbold{E}_{i,d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+\log\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\exp{\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\prime\top}\mathbold{E}_{i,v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}\right].

4.2
, the relevance can be established as

(18)

\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{\tilde{D}}^{\prime\top}\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}\right),

where

(19)

\mathbold{\tilde{D}}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{1}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbold{d}^{\prime}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d\times M)\times M},

and

\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}=\mathbold{W}_{V}^{\top}[\mathbold{E}_{1,d_{1}},\ldots,\mathbold{E}_{M,d_{M}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d\times M)}
.

As we can see,
PAWA
multiplies the term
\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}
with contextualized document token vectors
\mathbold{\tilde{D}}^{\prime}
, which greatly improves the expressivity of document encoding.

NP
-decoding directly replaces
\mathbold{\tilde{E}}_{d}
with
\mathbold{D}
.

Lee et al. (2023c)
employ an approach akin to contextualized sparse retrieval methods, leveraging token vectors encoded by the Contextualized Embedding Encoder
(
Lee et al., 2023c
, CE Encoder,)
, referred to as contextualized token embeddings.
This set of vectors, denoted as
\mathbold{D}
in our notation, serves as the embedding table for the decoder.
Both the
Base
and
Async
nonparametric decoding methods in
(
Lee et al., 2023c
)
can be reformulated within our framework as:

(20)

\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\operatorname{sum}(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}),

where
\mathbold{D}
is the token vectors of documents either pre-computed (as done by the pre-trained T5 model and frozen in the
Base
method) or gradually updated (by the encoder of the
GR
model every
N
epochs in the
Async
method) during the training of the
GR
model.

While the
NP
-decoding method shares the same document encoding with
MVDR
, two significant differences exist:

(1)

In
NP
-decoding,
\mathbold{D}
is mostly frozen and detached from training, causing training imbalance compared to
MVDR
.

\mathbold{Q}
and
\mathbold{A}
in
NP
-decoding are fully trained, while
\mathbold{D}
remains frozen.

(2)

Due to
GR
computing logits for the entire vocabulary in each generation step, there’s a need to reduce the large storage footprint of
\mathbold{D}
to save computation.

NP
-decoding methods address this by using clustering to compress token vectors.

MVDR
, on the other side, achieves lower inference time through a sparse alignment strategy.

5.2.
Alignment strategy

In addition to document encoding, the alignment matrix represents a crucial distinction between
GR
and
MVDR
methods.
This matrix plays a decisive role in shaping the divergent inference procedures employed in retrieval.
In this section, we analyze the alignment matrix, denoted as
\mathbold{A}
within our unified framework, in terms of sparsity, alignment direction, and some common properties.

5.2.1.
The concept of “alignment” in both methods

The concept of “alignment” has garnered significant attention in
MVDR

(
Qian et al., 2022
;
Lee et al., 2023b
;
Li et al., 2023b
;
Fan et al., 2023
)
.
We will briefly introduce the alignment problem in
MVDR
models, and claim that the alignment matrix of the
GR
method, as asserted in our framework Eq. (
16
), indeed exhibits similar alignment functionality to
MVDR
models.

Token alignment involves determining whether tokens from the query and document should be matched lexically or semantically.
It essentially represents another formulation of the “term mismatch problem”
(
Qian et al., 2022
;
Zhao, 2012
;
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Formal et al., 2021a
)
.
The prevailing strategy considered optimal at present is the all-to-all soft alignment strategy in
MVDR
models
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Qian et al., 2022
)
, which eliminates the lexical form match restriction.

GR
methods leverage the transformer architecture that originated in NLP, and the concept of “alignment” has been extensively discussed in the domain of neural machine translation
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Li, 2022
;
Li et al., 2022
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
, focusing on the alignment between tokens in source and target sentences.
The attention mechanism, as a core component, computes the alignment matrix and proves highly effective in capturing alignment between source and target sentences
(
Chen et al., 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Jiang et al., 2020
;
Yu et al., 2021
)
.
Theoretical works
(
Elhage et al., 2021
;
Olsson et al., 2022
)
have further validated the phenomenon of copying behavior, forming a foundational basis for the alignment ability.

We conclude that, in
GR
methods, the attention matrix is able to capture the alignment between the query and the document, akin to the alignment matrix observed in
MVDR
methods.

5.2.2.
Different sparsity and learnability: sparse vs. dense and learned alignment matrices

The alignment matrices of
MVDR
and
GR
differ in sparsity and learnability.

MVDR
typically employs a sparse alignment matrix for maximum efficiency during inference.
In contrast,
GR
utilizes a dense and fully learnable alignment matrix derived from the computationally intensive attention mechanism.

For
MVDR
methods
, the sparse alignment matrix is often computed using heuristic algorithms
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
;
Gao et al., 2021
;
Qian et al., 2022
)
.
Taking ColBERT
(
Khattab and Zaharia, 2020
)
as an example, it selects the most relevant document token for each query token.
The relevance score between document
d
and query
q
is computed as

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}\right)=\sum_{i,j}\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}A_{ij}

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[N]}\max_{i\in[M]}\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}=\sum_{j\in[N]}\operatorname{rel}(d,q_{j}),

where
\mathbold{A}
is a sparse alignment matrix with only one non-zero element for each column (
A_{ij}=1
if
\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}=\max_{i\in[M]}\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}
;
A_{ij}=0
otherwise).
The sum-max operation is highly parallelizable, ensuring efficiency during inference.

For
GR
methods
, the alignment matrix is computed through the attention mechanism, considering all possible pairs of query and document tokens, as shown in Eq. (
16
).

The dense alignment matrix is highly expressive and trainable.
While not suitable for exact relevance score computation in inference for each query-document pair, efficient approximate algorithms such as greedy search or beam search can be used to retrieve the top-
k
documents.
These decoding algorithms rely on the following decomposition:

(22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\sum_{i\in[M]}\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{\tilde{e}}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\mathbold{\alpha}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i\in[M]}\operatorname{rel}(d_{i},q),

where
\operatorname{rel}(d_{i},q)
is conditioned on
d_{0},\ldots,d_{i-1}
, approximating the search for the most relevant document
d
to finding the most relevant token
d_{i}
at each position
i
.

5.2.3.
Different alignment directions: query-to-document vs. document-to-query alignment

Beyond differences in the sparsity and learnability of the alignment matrix,
MVDR
and
GR
exhibit distinctions in their alignment directions.

Eq. (
21
) reveals that MVDR’s relevance score can be decomposed into the sum of relevance scores for each query token and its aligned document token.
In this context, we consider the alignment matrix in
MVDR
as
query-to-document
alignment.
Each query token individually aligns to a document token, seeking the optimal match during retrieval.
Mathematically, the alignment matrix is computed
column-wise
and represents a one-hot vector for each column.

Conversely, the relevance score of
GR
, as depicted in Eq. (
22
), is the sum of relevance scores for each document token and its softly aligned query token.
Here, we categorize the alignment matrix in
GR
as
document-to-query
alignment.
Each document token is considered individually to focus attention on the most relevant query token.
The alignment matrix is computed
row-wise
with a
\operatorname{softmax}(\cdot)
operation to normalize attention weights in each row.

Document-to-query alignment may seem counter-intuitive for a retrieval task, as we do not know the target documents while predicting.
As a solution,
GR
pre-computes the alignment strategy for the document token
d_{i}
(to be predicted) using
previous
document tokens
d_{0},\ldots,d_{i-1}
and thus can retrieve the next token that best aligns with the desired
next
alignment strategy.

5.2.4.
Low-rank nature of both alignment matrices

In analyzing the shared characteristics of the two alignment matrices, it is demonstrated that both matrices exhibit a low-rank property.

MVDR
model, e.g.,
AligneR

(
Qian et al., 2022
)
, integrates the pairwise alignment matrix with unary salience, given by

(23)

\mathbold{A}=\mathbold{\tilde{A}}\odot\mathbold{u}_{d}\mathbold{u}_{q}^{\top}.

Here,
\mathbold{\tilde{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
signifies the pairwise alignment matrix, determining the alignment of query and document tokens.
The sparse token weights,
\mathbold{u}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\mathbold{u}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, decide whether a token requires alignment.
Notably, the alignment matrix
\mathbold{A}
contains a low-rank component
\mathbold{u}_{d}\mathbold{u}_{q}^{\top}
that influences the alignment strategy.

In the case of
GR
methods, the alignment matrix is computed using an attention mechanism, which inherently results in a low-rank matrix.
A lemma provides evidence of this low-rank property and is presented briefly here, with a detailed proof in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 5.1.

For a matrix
\mathbold{A}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{Q}\right)
, there exists a rank-one matrix
\mathbold{R}
such that

(24)

\|\mathbold{A}-\mathbold{R}\|\leq 4\gamma\|\mathbold{W}\|,

where the term
\gamma
depends on the matrix entries.

From this lemma, we can conclude that both
MVDR
and
GR
methods reveal a rank-one component in their alignment matrices.

5.2.5.
Decomposition of both relevance scores

In this subsection, we show that the relevance score computation in both
MVDR
and
GR
models can be decomposed into query and document components.

The
MVDR
method employs a bi-encoder architecture, wherein query and document tokens are modeled separately.
This architecture can easily be regarded as a decomposition of the relevance score between the query and document:

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\operatorname{sum}\left(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A}\right)=\operatorname{sum}\left(\operatorname{top-1}(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q})\right),

where
\operatorname{top-1}(\cdot)
is the operator that selects the maximum value in each column of the matrix.

In the subsequent lemma, we establish that the relevance score of
GR
cannot only be decomposed but also be kernelized, implying the existence of a kernel function capable of processing both query vectors and document vectors to compute the score (further details can be found in Appendix
C
):

Lemma 5.2.

For simplicity, let
\operatorname{rel}(d,q)=\operatorname{sum}(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\odot\mathbold{A})
, where
\mathbold{A}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{Q})
.
It can be kernelized as

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}A_{ij}=\sum_{i,j}\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}\operatorname{softmax}\left(\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{Q}\right)_{j}

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{p_{ij}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{d}_{i})^{\top}\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{q}_{j})\right),

where
\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{x})=\mathbold{x}\mathbold{\phi}(\mathbold{x})^{\top}
, and
p_{ij}
is a term that depends on
\mathbold{d}_{i}
and
\mathbold{q}_{j}
.
We choose
\operatorname{elu}(\cdot)
as the kernel function
\mathbold{\phi}(\cdot)
.

Furthermore, by applying the trace inequality, we can approximately decompose the relevance score as

\operatorname{rel}(d,q)\leq\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{i}\hat{p}_{j}}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{d}_{i})^{\top}\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{d}_{i})\right)}\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{q}_{j})^{\top}\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{q}_{j})\right)}.

From this lemma, we can conclude that both relevance scores in
MVDR
and
GR
methods can be decomposed.
The decomposition of
MVDR
is more straightforward, and the kernelization of
GR
is more complicated.
Both kernelizations would provide possibilities for new retrieval strategies.

5.3.
Upshot

In summary, our findings indicate that certain studies enhance the modeling capacity of
GR
by employing more expressive document encoding, akin to
MVDR
.
Furthermore,
GR
employs a distinct alignment direction, but it also exhibits similar low-rank and decomposition properties with
MVDR
.

7.2.
Term matching in alignment

As we have discussed in Section
5.2.1
, alignment is essentially a term-matching problem.
In this section, we design an experiment to observe the extent of term matching in the two methods, and we find both methods exhibit a preference for exact term matching in their alignment.

Exact match of
MVDR
in query-to-document direction.

We calculate the exact matching rate between document token IDs and each query token ID during the alignment process, which we refer to as the “hard exact match rate.”
We also define a “soft exact match rate” which is the alignment score corresponding to the exact match query-document token pairs.
The alignment score is defined as the element in the alignment matrix.
As
MVDR
uses a sparse alignment matrix, we apply column-wise
\operatorname{softmax(\cdot)}
to
\mathbold{A}
and use the element as the alignment score.
We average the rate over candidate documents for each query token and categorize the query tokens according to their IDF.
We assume that IDF approximates the term importance as is done in
(
Formal et al., 2021b
)
.
From the result in Figure
2
, we can see that
MVDR
chooses exactly matched document tokens in 11.4% on average.
Also, we notice that rare query tokens have not received much attention during alignment.
This observation suggests that
MVDR
may prioritize commonly occurring query tokens in its alignment process, potentially overlooking or underemphasizing the importance of rare query tokens.

Exact match of
MVDR
and
GR
in document-to-query direction.

We have devised an experiment to investigate the alignment in the opposite direction, i.e., document-to-query, in Figure
3
.
As
GR
is not trained with hard alignment, we only examine the soft exact match rate of both methods.
The computation of the exact match rate is similar except that it is computed for each document token.
From the results, we have discerned a consistent trend: as the importance of tokens increases, the rate of exact matches also tends to rise.
We think this is because it is hard for the rare query token to match among many common tokens since the document is much longer than the query.
When we look at each document token, it will be easier to match among fewer query tokens.
We also conduct experiments on MS MARCO and have similar results.

8.
Limitations

We have examined the training target of
GR
and have connected it with
MVDR
, but we have not discussed whether relevance computing can be generalized to the generative style inference.
We have not considered the multi-layer interactions in the cross-attention between query and document for simplicity.

Our framework does not discuss how query-generation augmentation reduces the discrepancy
(
Zhuang et al., 2022
)
.
We aim to study how different architectures and identifier designs will affect the alignment and generalization during inference in future works.

9.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have offered new insights into
GR
from the perspective of
MVDR
that both paradigms share the same frameworks for measuring the relevance between a query and a document.
Both paradigms compute relevance as a sum of products of query and document vectors and an alignment matrix.
We have explored how
GR
applies this framework and differs from
MVDR
.
We have shown that
GR
has simpler document encoding and an alignment strategy with different sparsity and direction.
They also share a low-rank property and can be decomposed into query and document components.
We have conducted extensive experiments to verify our conclusions and found that both methods have commonalities of term matching in the alignment.
We also found that query-to-document alignment direction has better performance than document-to-query.

Based on our findings, practitioners in the field may consider leveraging the shared frameworks highlighted in this study to understand and develop new
GR
methods, and pay more attention to the classic term matching problem underlying
GR
models.

As to future work, we will continue to study how multi-layer attention may affect the framework.
The difference in the generalization properties for new documents between
DR
and
GR

(
Nguyen and Yates, 2023
;
Mehta et al., 2023
;
Zhou et al., 2023
;
Chen et al., 2023
)
base on our framework is also an important aspect deserving further investigation.
We will continue to discover new relations in the
GR
paradigm and provide more insights into the methodology.

Appendix A
Further explanation of the relevance score in
GR

Further insights into the reason for using
\sum_{i\in[M]}\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}
as the relevance can be elaborated as follows.
Suppose we treat all other token embeddings
\mathbold{e}_{k}
, where
k\neq d_{i}
, as fixed with respect to
\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}
, the loss can be expressed as:

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(d,q)

\displaystyle=-\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+\log\left(\exp\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+\sum_{v\neq d_{i}}\exp\mathbold{e}_{v}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=-\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+\log\left(\exp\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}+C\right).

Given that
C\gg\exp{\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}
at the early stage of the training, we can consider the last term
\log(\exp{\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}}+C)
as a constant w.r.t.
\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}
. Hence,

(28)

\log p(d_{i}\mid d_{i-1},\ldots,d_{0},q)=\mathcal{L}_{i}(d,q)\propto\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i},

and further we have

(29)

\log p(d\mid q)=\mathcal{L}(d,q)=\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}(d,q)\propto\sum_{i\in[M]}\mathbold{e}_{d_{i}}^{\top}\mathbold{h}_{i}.

In summary, the dot-product can effectively indicate the relevance between document
d
and query
q
.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Proof.

We define
\mathbold{R}_{d}=\mathbold{D}-\mathbold{1}\mathbold{d}^{\top}
, where
\mathbold{d}=\arg\min\|\mathbold{D}-\mathbold{1}\mathbold{d}^{\top}\|
.

\mathbold{R}_{q}
is defined similarly for
\mathbold{Q}
.
Then we have

\mathbold{D}^{\top}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{Q}=(\mathbold{d}^{\top}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{q}+\mathbold{R}_{d}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{q})\mathbold{1}^{\top}+(\mathbold{1}\mathbold{d}^{\top}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{R}_{q}^{\top}+\mathbold{R}_{d}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{R}_{q}^{\top}),

therefore,
\mathbold{A}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbold{R}_{d}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{R}_{q}^{\top}+\mathbold{1}\mathbold{r}^{\top})
.

Let
\mathbold{R}=\mathbold{1}\mathbold{r}^{\top}
, according to Lemma A.3 in
(
Dong et al., 2021
)
, we have
\|\mathbold{A}-\mathbold{R}\|_{1,\infty}\leq 4\gamma^{2}\|\mathbold{R}_{d}\mathbold{W}\mathbold{R}_{q}^{\top}\|_{1}\leq 4\gamma C\|\mathbold{W}\|_{1}
,
where
C
depends on
\mathbold{R}_{d}
and
\mathbold{R}_{q}
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
5.2

Proof.

Given that
\mathbold{A}_{ij}=\exp(\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j})/p_{ij}
, where
p_{ij}
is the normalization, we can kernelize
\exp(\cdot)
term as
\phi(\mathbold{d}_{i})^{\top}\phi(\mathbold{q}_{j})
, and

\displaystyle\operatorname{rel}(d,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbold{d}_{i}^{\top}\mathbold{q}_{j}\phi(\mathbold{d}_{i})^{\top}\phi(\mathbold{q}_{j}))/p_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{p_{ij}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{d}_{i})^{\top}\mathbold{F}(\mathbold{q}_{j})\right).\qed

Appendix D
T5 variants of ColBERT and SEAL

The official ColBERTv1 uses pair-wise contrastive loss for training, which is inefficient.
Therefore, we use in-batch negatives and compute all negative samples simultaneously in each iteration.
We set the batch size to 256 and train 5 epochs.
Using in-batch negatives will greatly save training time without decreasing the performance much.
We summarize our comparison results in Table
3
.
