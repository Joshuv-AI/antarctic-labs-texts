Measure estimation on manifolds: an optimal transport approach
Vincent Divol

Abstract
. Assume that we observe i.i.d. points lying close to some unknown d d -dimensional 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} submanifold M M in a possibly high-dimensional space. We study the problem of reconstructing the probability distribution generating the sample. After remarking that this problem is degenerate for a large class of standard losses ( L p L_{p} , Hellinger, total variation, etc.), we focus on the Wasserstein loss, for which we build an estimator, based on kernel density estimation, whose rate of convergence depends on d d and the regularity s ≤ k − 1 s\leq k-1 of the underlying density, but not on the ambient dimension. In particular, we show that the estimator is minimax and matches previous rates in the literature in the case where the manifold M M is a d d -dimensional cube. The related problem of the estimation of the volume measure of M M for the Wasserstein loss is also considered, for which a minimax estimator is exhibited.



1.
Introduction

Density estimation is one of the most fundamental tasks in non-parametric statistics. If efficient methods (from both a theoretical and a practical point of view) exist when the ambient space is of low dimension, minimax rates of estimation become increasingly slow as the dimension increases. To overcome this so-called
curse of dimensionality
, some structural assumptions on the underlying probability are to be made in moderate to high dimensions, which may take different forms, including e.g. the existence of a parametric component
[
LLW07
]
, the single-index model
[
LZZL13
]
, sparsity assumptions
[
Tib96
]
, or constraints on the shape of the support. We focus in this work on the latter, namely on the case where the probability distribution
\mu
generating the observations is assumed to be concentrated around a submanifold
M
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, of dimension
d
smaller than
D
. This assumption, known as the manifold assumption, has been fruitfully studied, with an emphasis put on reconstructing different geometric quantities related to the manifold, such as
M
itself
[
GPPIW12
,
AL18
,
AL19
,
Div21a
]
, its homology groups
[
NSW08
,
BRS+12
]
, its dimension
[
HA05
,
LJM09
,
KRW19
]
or its reach
[
AKC+19
,
BHHS21
]
. The topic of density estimation in the manifold setting has itself been studied for over thirty years, with the emphasis initially being put on reconstructing the density in the case where the manifold
M
is given—think for instance of datasets lying on the space of orthogonal matrices—notable works including
[
Hen90
,
HJR93
,
Pel05
,
CGK+20
]
. Less attention has been dedicated to the more general setting where the manifold
M
is unknown and acts as a nuisance parameter. Kernel density estimators on manifolds are designed in
[
BS17
,
WW20
]
, where rates are exhibited, respectively in the case where the manifold has a boundary and in the case where the density is Hölder continuous. In
[
BH19
]
, kernel density estimators are shown to be minimax, and an adaptive procedure is designed, based on Lepski’s method, to estimate the unknown density in a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
which is known to belong to the unknown (and possibly nonsmooth) manifold
M
.

To go beyond the pointwise estimation of
\mu
, even the choice of a relevant loss is nontrivial. Indeed, most standard losses between probability measures (e.g. the
L_{p}
distance, the Hellinger distance or the Kullback-Leibler divergence) are degenerate when comparing mutually singular measures, which will typically be the case for measures on two distinct manifolds, even if they are very close to each other with respect to the Hausdorff distance. This implies that the estimation problem is degenerate from a minimax perspective when choosing such losses (see Theorem
2.11
). On the contrary, the Wasserstein distances
W_{p}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
are particularly adapted to this problem, as they are by design robust to small metric perturbations of the support of a measure.

Apart from this first motivation, the use of Wasserstein distances, and more generally of the theory of optimal transport, has shown to be an efficient tool in widely different recent problems of machine learning, with fast implementations and sound theoretical results (see e.g.
[
PC19
]
for a survey). From a statistical perspective, most of the attention has been dedicated to studying rates of convergence between a probability distribution
\mu
and its empirical counterpart
\mu_{n}

[
Dud69
,
DSS13
,
FG15
,
SP18
,
WB19a
,
Lei20
]
. Unsurprisingly, if more regularity is assumed on
\mu
, then it is possible to build estimators with smaller risks than the empirical measure
\mu_{n}
. Assume for instance that
\mu
is a probability distribution on the cube
[-1,1]^{D}
, with density
f
of regularity
s
(measured through the Besov scale
B^{s}_{p,q}
). In this setting, it has been shown in
[
WB19b
]
that, given
n
i.i.d. points of law
\mu
, the minimax rate (up to logarithmic factors) for the estimation of
\mu
with respect to the Wasserstein distance
W_{p}
is of order

(1.1)

\begin{cases}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+D}}&\text{ if }D\geq 3\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log n&\text{ if }D=2\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }D=1,\end{cases}

and that this rate is attained by a modified linear wavelet density estimator.
Our main contribution consists in extending the results of
[
WB19b
]
by allowing the support of the probability to be any
d
-dimensional compact
\mathcal{C}^{k}
submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
for
k\geq 2
. More precisely, assume that some probability
\mu
on
M
has a lower and upper bounded density
f
which belongs to the Besov space
B^{s}_{p,q}(M)
for some
0<s\leq k-1
,
1\leq p<\infty
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
(see Section
2
for details). We first show (Theorem
3.1
) that some weighted kernel density estimator that we integrate against the volume measure
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
on
M
attains, for the
W_{p}
distance, the rate of estimation

(1.2)

\begin{cases}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+d}}&\text{ if }d\geq 3\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\log n\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d=2\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d=1.\end{cases}

In the case where the manifold
M
is unknown, we do not have access to the volume measure
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, so that the latter estimator is not computable. We therefore propose to estimate the volume measure
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
in a preliminary step. Such an estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
is defined by using local polynomial estimation techniques from
[
AL19
]
. We show that this estimator is a minimax estimator of the volume measure up to logarithmic factors (Theorem
3.7
), with a risk of order
\left(\log n/n\right)^{k/d}
. We then show (Theorem
3.8
) that a weighted kernel density estimator integrated against
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
attains the rate (
1.2
). Those rates are significantly faster than the rates of (
1.1
) if
d\ll D
and are shown to be minimax up to logarithmic factors.

Being able to estimate accurately the volume measure
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
has also other useful implications, e.g. we provide an algorithm to sample points uniformly on a (possibly unknown) manifold, and we leverage results from
[
TGHS20
]
to provide precise estimates of the eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
(see Section
5
).

In Section
2
, we define our statistical model and give some preliminary results on Wasserstein distances. In Section
3
, we define kernel density estimators on a manifold
M
, and state our main results.
Proofs of the main theorems are then given in Section
4
. Section
5
discusses the implementation of our estimators, in particular proving that the local polynomial estimators of Aamari & Levrard
[
AL19
]
can be efficiently computed. Additional proofs are found in the Appendix.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Regularity of manifolds

For any
d>0
, we write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the dot product and
|v|
for the norm of a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The open ball centered at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
h>0
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(x,h)
. For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a set and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
d(x,\Omega)\vcentcolon=\inf\{|x-y|,\ y\in\Omega\}
be the distance from
x
to
\Omega
and we write
\mathcal{B}_{\Omega}(x,h)
for
\mathcal{B}(x,h)\cap\Omega
. Also, we let
\Omega^{h}\vcentcolon=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ d(x,\Omega)<h\}
be the
h
-tubular neighborhood of
\Omega
. Given a tensor
A:(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})^{i}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
of order
i\geq 0
, the operator norm
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is defined as
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vcentcolon=\max\{|A[v_{1},\dots,v_{i}]|,\ |v_{1}|,\dots,|v_{i}|\leq 1\}
. Also, we let
A^{\top}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
denote the adjoint of the operator
A:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. If
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
function defined on an open set
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
, we let
\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}(\Omega)}\vcentcolon=\max_{0\leq i\leq l}\sup\{\left\|d^{i}f(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}},\ x\in\Omega\}
, where
d^{i}f(x)
is the
i
th differential of
f
at
x
.

Let
D>0
and let
\mathcal{M}_{d}
be the set of all smooth
d
-dimensional connected submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
without boundary, endowed with the metric induced by the standard metric on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. We denote by
d_{g}
the geodesic distance on
M
. The tangent space at a point
x\in M
is denoted by
T_{x}M
. It is identified with a
d
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and the orthogonal projection on
T_{x}M
is denoted by
\pi_{x}
. We also let
\tilde{\pi}_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to T_{x}M
be defined by
\tilde{\pi}_{x}(y)=\pi_{x}(y-x)
. We denote by
T_{x}M^{\bot}
the normal space at
x\in M
.
The key quantity used to describe the regularity of a manifold
M
is its reach
\tau(M)
. It is defined as the distance between
M
and its medial axis, that is the set of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
for which there are at least two points of
M
which attain the distance from
x
to
M
. In particular, the projection
\pi_{M}
on the manifold
M
is defined on
M^{\tau(M)}
. Originally introduced in
[
Fed59
]
, the reach
\tau(M)
measures both the local regularity of
M
(namely its curvature) and its global regularity, see e.g.
[
AKC+19
,
BHHS21
]
or
[
DZ01
, Section 6.6]
for precise results on the relationships between the reach of a manifold and its geometry. We then measure the regularity of
M
through the regularity of local parametrizations of
M
(see
[
AL19
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, and
\tau_{\min},L>0
,
k\geq 2
. Let
r_{0}=(\tau_{\min}\wedge L)/4
. We say that
M
is in
\mathcal{M}_{d,\tau_{\min},L}^{k}
if
M
is closed, of reach larger than
\tau_{\min}
and if, for all
x\in M
, the projection
\tilde{\pi}_{x}:M\to T_{x}M
is a local diffeomorphism in
x
, with inverse
\Psi_{x}
defined on
\mathcal{B}_{T_{x}M}(0,r_{0})
, satisfying
\|\Psi_{x}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{B}_{T_{x}M}(0,r_{0}))}\leq L
.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

Remark that we only consider manifolds
M
that are smooth in the above definition, with a controlled
\mathcal{C}^{k}
norm. As the set of smooth submanifolds is dense in the set of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
submanifolds (for an appropriate topology), this is not a strong assumption. Dealing with smooth submanifolds is more convenient for us, as we do not have to deal with tricky existence issues for defining different functional spaces on manifolds.

(ii)

For the sake of convenience, we use a definition slightly different from the definition of
[
AL19
]
, where authors assume the existence of local parametrizations
\tilde{\Psi}_{x}
having controlled
\mathcal{C}^{k}
norms, with
\tilde{\Psi}_{x}
not necessarily equal to the inverse
\Psi_{x}
of the orthogonal projection. However, our definition is not restrictive. Indeed, on can write
\Psi_{x}=\tilde{\Psi}_{x}\circ(\tilde{\pi}_{x}\circ\tilde{\Psi}_{x})^{-1}
, where the
\mathcal{C}^{k}
norm of
(\tilde{\pi}_{x}\circ\tilde{\Psi}_{x})^{-1}
is controlled by the inverse function theorem. Therefore, the
\mathcal{C}^{k}
norm of
\Psi_{x}
can always be controlled by the
\mathcal{C}^{k}
norms of other parametrizations
\tilde{\Psi}_{x}
. Both definitions can also be proven to be equivalent to assuming that the function
d^{2}(\cdot,M)
has a controlled
\mathcal{C}^{k}
norm on
M^{\tau(M)}
, see e.g.
[
PR84
]
.

(iii)

The value of the scale parameter
r_{0}
is used for convenience. Other small scales could be used, or the radius
r_{0}
could also be added as another parameter of the model, without any substantial gain in doing so.

If
M_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{1}}
,
M_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{2}}
,
x\in M_{1}
and
f:M_{1}\to M_{2}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function, then we let
df(x):T_{x}M_{1}\to T_{f(x)}M_{2}
be the differential of
f
at
x
. For
0\leq l\leq k
, if
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{l}
, we let
\left\|d^{i}f(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\vcentcolon=\max_{0\leq i\leq l}\left\|d^{i}(f\circ\Psi_{x})(0)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and

\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{l}(M_{1})}\vcentcolon=\sup_{x\in M_{1}}\left\|d^{i}f(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}.
If
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
, then we define the Jacobian of
f
at
x\in M_{1}
as
Jf(x)=\sqrt{\det(df(x)^{\top}df(x))}
. We let
\mathcal{C}^{l}(M)
be the space of all
\mathcal{C}^{l}
functions
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
(with possibly
l=\infty
) and for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(M)
, we let
\nabla f
denote the gradient of
f
. We also denote by
\nabla\cdot
the divergence operator on
M
.

Let
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
be the volume measure associated with the Riemannian metric on
M
. We will denote the integration with respect to
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}(x)
by
\mathrm{d}x
when the context is clear.
For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we let
L_{p}(M)
be the set of measurable functions
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
with finite
p
-norm
\|f\|_{L_{p}(M)}\vcentcolon=\left(\int f\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}\right)^{1/p}
(and usual modification if
p=\infty
). We say that a locally integrable function
f
is weakly differentiable if there exists a measurable section
\nabla f
of the tangent bundle
TM
(uniquely defined almost everywhere) such that for all smooth vector fields
w
on
M
with compact support, we have

\int f(\nabla\cdot w)\ \mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}=-\int(\nabla f)\cdot w\ \mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}.

Furthermore, we will denote by
p^{*}\in[1,\infty]
the number satisfying
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{*}}=1
.

2.2.
Besov spaces on manifolds

Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d,\tau_{\min},L}^{k}
for some
k\geq 2
,
\tau_{\min},L>0
. As stated in the introduction, minimax rates for the estimation of a given probability will depend crucially on the regularity of its density
f
, which is assumed to belong to some Besov space
B^{s}_{p,q}(M)
. We first introduce Sobolev spaces
H^{l}_{p}(M)
on
M
for
l\leq k
an integer, and Besov spaces on
M
are then defined by real interpolation.

Definition 2.3

(Sobolev space on a manifold)
.

Let
0\leq l\leq k
,
1\leq p<\infty
and let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
function. We let

(2.1)

\begin{split}\|f\|_{H^{l}_{p}(M)}&\vcentcolon=\max_{0\leq i\leq l}\left(\int\left\|d^{i}f(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{p}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}(x)\right)^{1/p}.\end{split}

The space
H^{l}_{p}(M)
is the completion of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
for the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{l}_{p}(M)}
.

Remark 2.4

(On the case
p=\infty
)
.

The previous definition cannot be extended to the case
p=\infty
. Indeed, the completion of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
for the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{l}_{\infty}(M)}
is equal to
\mathcal{C}^{l}(M)
, whereas for instance
H^{0}_{\infty}(M)
should be equal to
L_{\infty}(M)
. For
l=1
, the space
H^{1}_{p}(M)
can equivalently be defined as the space of weakly differentiable functions
f
with
\|f\|_{H^{1}_{p}(M)}<\infty
, while this definition can be easily extended to the case
p=\infty
. In particular, if
f\in H^{1}_{\infty}(M)
, then one can verify that
f\circ\Psi_{x}\in H^{1}_{\infty}(\mathcal{B}_{T_{x}M}(0,r_{0}))
for any
x\in M
. It follows from standard results on Sobolev spaces on domains that
f\circ\Psi_{x}
is Lipschitz continuous (see e.g.
[
Bre10
, Proposition 9.3]
). Hence,
f
is also locally Lipschitz continuous. By Rademacher theorem,
f
is therefore almost everywhere differentiable, and its differential coincides with the weak differential. As a consequence, a function
f\in H^{1}_{\infty}(M)
is Lipschitz continuous, with Lipschitz constant for the distance
d_{g}
equal to
\|f\|_{H^{1}_{\infty}(M)}
.

For
1\leq p<\infty
, we introduce the negative homogeneous Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}
, defined, for
f\in L_{p}(M)
with
\int f\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}=0
, by

(2.2)

\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}\vcentcolon=\sup\left\{\int fg\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M},\ \|\nabla g\|_{L_{p^{*}}(M)}\leq 1\right\},

where the supremum is taken over all functions
g\in H^{1}_{p^{*}}(M)
. For
f\in L_{p}(M)
, the negative Sobolev norm is defined by

(2.3)

\|f\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\vcentcolon=\sup\left\{\int fg\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M},\ \|g\|_{H^{1}_{p^{*}}(M)}\leq 1\right\},

and the corresponding Banach space is denoted by
H_{p}^{-1}(M)
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
f\in H^{-1}_{p}(M)
with
\int f\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}=0
.

(i)

We have
C_{d,\tau_{\min}}|\mathrm{vol}_{M}|^{\frac{d-1}{p}-d}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}\leq\|f\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\leq\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}
for some positive constant
C_{d,\tau_{\min}}
depending on
d
and
\tau_{\min}
.

(ii)

We have
\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}=\inf\{\|w\|_{L_{p}(M)},\ \nabla\cdot w=f\},

where the infimum is taken over all measurable vector fields
w
on
M
with finite
p
-norm, and where
\nabla\cdot w=f
means that
\int fg\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}=-\int w\cdot\nabla g\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}
for all
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
.

Following
[
Tri92
]
, Besov spaces on a manifold
M
are defined as real interpolation of Sobolev spaces. We refer to
[
Lun18
]
for definition of real interpolations of Banach spaces.

Definition 2.6

(Besov space on a manifold)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
0<s<k
.
The Besov space
B^{s}_{p,q}(M)
is defined as
B^{s}_{p,q}(M)\vcentcolon=(L_{p}(M),H^{k}_{p}(M))_{s/k,q},
the real interpolation space between
L_{p}(M)
and
H^{k}_{p}(M)
of parameters
s/k
and
q
.

Basic results from interpolation theory then imply that
\|\cdot\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M)}\leq\|\cdot\|_{B^{s^{\prime}}_{p,q}(M)}
if
0<s\leq s^{\prime}<k
.

2.3.
Wasserstein distances and negative Sobolev distances

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of finite Borel measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, with
|\mu|
the total mass of
\mu
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}
be the set of measures in
\mathcal{P}
with
|\mu|=1
. For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, let
\mathcal{P}^{p}
be the set of measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
satifying
\left(\int|x|^{p}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{1/p}<\infty
(with usual modification for
p=\infty
) and let
\mathcal{P}^{p}_{1}=\mathcal{P}^{p}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}
. The pushforward of a measure
\mu
by a measurable application
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is defined by

(2.4)

\phi_{\#}\mu(A)\vcentcolon=\mu(\phi^{-1}(A))

for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to[0,\infty)
a measurable function, we denote by
\rho\cdot\mu
the measure having density
\rho
with respect to
\mu
.

Definition 2.7

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}^{p}
with the same total mass. Let
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be the set of transport plans between
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e. measures on
\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}
with first marginal
\pi^{1}
(resp. second marginal
\pi^{2}
) equal to
\mu
(resp.
\nu
). The cost
C_{p}(\pi)
of
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is defined as
\int|x-y|^{p}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)
. The
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

(2.5)

W_{p}(\mu,\nu)\vcentcolon=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}C_{p}(\pi)^{1/p},

with usual modification for
p=\infty
.

A crucial point in the study conducted in the following is the relation between Wasserstein distances and negative Sobolev norms.

Proposition 2.8

(Wasserstein distances and negative Sobolev norms)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
be a manifold with reach
\tau(M)\geq\tau_{\min}
, and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{p}
be two probability measures supported on
M
, absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, with densities
f
and
g
. Assume that
f,g\geq f_{\min}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{M}
for some
f_{\min}>0
. Then, we have

(2.6)

\begin{split}W_{p}(\mu,\nu)&\leq p^{-1/p}f_{\min}^{1/p-1}\|f-g\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}\\
&\leq p^{-1/p}C_{d,\tau_{\min},f_{\min}}\|f-g\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)},\end{split}

for some constant
C_{d,\tau_{\min},f_{\min}}
depending on
d
,
\tau_{\min}
and
f_{\min}
.

In particular, if
p=1
, then the first inequality in (
2.6
) is actually an equality by the Kantorovitch duality formula
[
Vil08
, Particular Case 5.16]
.
This inequality appears in
[
Pey18
]
for
p=2
and in
[
San15
, Section 5.5.1]
for measures having density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We carefully adapt their proofs in Appendix
B
.

2.4.
Statistical models

We consider the two following models, where points are sampled on a manifold, with possibly tubular noise. We fix in the following some parameters
\tau_{\min},L_{s},L_{k}>0
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
and
0<f_{\min}<f_{\max}<\infty
. We also write
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{k}
instead of
\mathcal{M}_{d,\tau_{\min},L_{k}}^{k}
.

Definition 2.9

(Noise-free model)
.

Let
d\leq D
be integers,
k\geq 2
,
0\leq s<k
and
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{k}
. For
s=0
, the set
\mathcal{Q}^{0}(M)
is the set of probability distributions
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
absolutely continuous with respect to the volume measure
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, with a density
f
satisfying
f_{\min}\leq f\leq f_{\max}
almost everywhere. For
s>0
, the set
\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
is the set of distributions
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{0}(M)
, with density
f\in B^{s}_{p,q}(M)
satisfying
\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M)}\leq L_{s}
. The model
\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}
is equal to the union of the sets
\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
for
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{k}
.

Remark 2.10
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
, then, as
\mu\geq f_{\min}\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, one has
\mathrm{vol}_{M}(M)\leq f_{\min}^{-1}
. One can then use standard packing arguments to show that this implies that
\diam(M)\leq C_{d}/(f_{\min}\tau_{\min}^{d-1})
for some constant
C_{d}
depending only on
d
. In particular, the manifold
M
is automatically compact.

Given a set of observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
sampled according to
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}
, the goal of statistical inference is to reconstruct some quantity
\vartheta(\mu)
related to
\mu
. If
\mathcal{L}
is a loss function defined on the set of outputs of
\vartheta
, we define the minimax risk for this problem as

(2.7)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\vartheta,\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d},\mathcal{L})\vcentcolon=\inf_{\hat{\vartheta}}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{L}(\hat{\vartheta},\vartheta),

where
\hat{\vartheta}=\hat{\vartheta}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
is an i.i.d. sample with law
\mu
. We will focus here on reconstructing (i) the measure
\vartheta(\mu)=\mu
, and (ii) the uniform measure
\vartheta(\mu)=U_{M}\vcentcolon=\mathrm{vol}_{M}/|\mathrm{vol}_{M}|
, where
M
is the support of
\mu
. We first show that task (i) is impossible if the loss function
\mathcal{L}
is larger than the total variation distance
\TV
, which is defined by
\TV(\mu,\nu)\vcentcolon=\sup_{A}|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
, where the supremum is taken over all measurable sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
d\leq D
be integers,
k\geq 2
,
0\leq s<k
,
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\to[0,\infty]
be a measurable map with respect to the Borel
\sigma
-algebra associated with the total variation distance on
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}
. Assume that
\mathcal{L}(\mu,\nu)\geq g(\TV(\mu,\nu))
for a convex nondecreasing function
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
with
g(0)=0
. Then, for any
\tau_{\min}>0
, if
f_{\min}
is small enough and
L_{k},L_{s},f_{\max}
are large enough, we have

(2.8)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mu,\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d},\mathcal{L})\geq g(c_{d}),

for some constant
c_{d}>0
.

Examples of such losses include the total variation distance, the Hellinger distance (with
g(x)=x
), the Kullback-Leibler divergence (with
g(x)=x^{2}/2
), and the
L_{p}
distance with respect to some dominating measure (with
g(x)=x^{p}
).
We give a proof of Theorem
2.11
, based on Assouad’s lemma, in Appendix
G
. A simple example of loss
\mathcal{L}
which is not degenerate for mutually singular measures is given by the
W_{p}
distance. As stated in the introduction, we will therefore choose this loss, and study
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mu,\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d},W_{p})
, the minimax rate of estimation for
\mu
with respect to
W_{p}
.

We will also study this problem in the presence of tubular noise.

Definition 2.12

(Tubular noise model)
.

Let
d\leq D
be integers,
k\geq 2
,
0\leq s<k
,
1\leq p<\infty
and
\gamma\geq 0
. The set
\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}(\gamma)
is the set of laws
\nu
of random variables
Y+Z
where
Y\sim\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}
and
Z\in\mathcal{B}(0,\gamma)
is such that
Z\in T_{Y}M^{\bot}
.

Note that the tubular noise model is not identifiable, in the sense that there are several admissible couples
(Y,Z)
(with possibly different supports) such that
X=Y+Z
follows the same distribution
\nu
. For each
\nu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}(\gamma)
, we will make an arbitrary choice among the admissible couples, while this will not have any impact on the following study (all the admissible couples have their first marginal at most
2\gamma
apart for the Wasserstein distance).

Remark 2.13
.

For ease of notation, we will write in the following
a\lesssim b
to indicate that there exists a constant
C
depending on the parameters
p,k,\tau_{\min},L_{s},L_{k},f_{\min},f_{\max}
,
but not on
D
, such that
a\leq Cb
, and write
a\asymp b
to indicate that
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
. Also, we will write
c_{\alpha}
to indicate that a constant
c
depends on some parameter
\alpha
.

Note that in particular, the risk of the estimators proposed in the next section will not depend on the ambient dimension
D
, but only on intrinsic parameters such as the regularity of
\nu
and its effective dimension
d
.

3.
Kernel density estimation on an unknown manifold

Before building an estimator in the model
\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}(\gamma)
, let us consider the easier problem of the estimation of
\mu
in the case where
\gamma=0
(noise free model) and the support
M
is known. Let
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be a
n
-sample of law
\mu
. Let
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
be the empirical measure of the sample. Identify
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}^{D-d}
and consider a kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions:

—

Condition
A
:
The kernel
K
is a smooth radial function with support
\mathcal{B}(0,1)
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K=1
.

—

Condition
B(m)
:
The kernel
K
is of order
m\geq 0
in the following sense. Let
|\alpha|\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}
be the length of a multiindex
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
. Then, for all multiindexes
\alpha^{0}
,
\alpha^{1}
with
0\leq|\alpha^{0}|<m
,
0\leq|\alpha^{1}|<m+|\alpha_{0}|
, and with
|\alpha^{1}|>0
if
\alpha^{0}=0
, we have

(3.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha^{0}}K(v)v^{\alpha^{1}}\mathrm{d}v=0,

where
v^{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}v_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
and
\partial^{\alpha}K
is the partial derivative of
K
in the direction
\alpha
.

—

Condition
C(\beta)
:
The negative part
K_{-}\vcentcolon=-\min(0,K)
of
K
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K_{-}\leq\beta
.

We show in Appendix
H
that for every integer
m\geq 0
and real number
\beta>0
, there exists a kernel
K
satisfying conditions
A
,
B(m)
and
C(\beta)
. Define the convolution of
K
with a measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}
as

(3.2)

K*\rho(x)\vcentcolon=\int K(x-y)\mathrm{d}\rho(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

and, for
h>0
, let
K_{h}\vcentcolon=h^{-d}K(\cdot/h)
. Let
\rho_{h}\vcentcolon=K_{h}*\mathrm{vol}_{M}
and let
\mu_{n,h}
be the measure with density
f_{n,h}\vcentcolon=K_{h}*(\mu_{n}/\rho_{h})
with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
. Dividing by
\rho_{h}
ensures that
\mu_{n,h}
is a measure of mass
1
. Remark that the computation of
\mu_{n,h}
requires to have access to
M
, that is
\mu_{n,h}
is an estimator on
\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
but not on
\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}
. By linearity, the expectation of
\mu_{n,h}
is given by
\mu_{h}
, the measure having for density
f_{h}\vcentcolon=K_{h}*(\mu/\rho_{h})
on
M
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d\leq D
be integers,
0<s\leq k-1
with
k\geq 2
and
1\leq p<\infty
. Let
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{k}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
with density
f
. Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be a
n
-sample of law
\mu
. There exists a constant
\beta
depending on the parameters of the model such that, if
K
is a kernel satisfying conditions
A
,
B(k)
and
C(\beta)
, then the measure
\mu_{n,h}
satisfies the following:

(i)

If
(\log n/n)^{1/d}\lesssim h\lesssim 1
, then, with probability larger than
1-cn^{-k/d}
, the density
f_{n,h}
of
\mu_{n,h}
is larger than
f_{\min}/2
and smaller than
2f_{\max}
everywhere on
M
.

(ii)

If
n^{-1/d}\lesssim h\lesssim 1
, then we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|f-f_{n,h}\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}

\displaystyle\leq\|f-f_{h}\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}+\mathbb{E}\|f_{n,h}-f_{h}\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{s+1}+\frac{h^{1-d/2}I_{d}(h)}{\sqrt{n}},

where
I_{d}(h)=1
if
d\geq 3
,
(-\log(h))^{1/2}
if
d=2
and
h^{-1/2}
if
d=1
.

(iii)

Let
h\asymp n^{-1/(2s+d)}
if
d\geq 3
,
h\asymp(\log n/n)^{1/d}
if
d\leq 2
. Define
\mu^{0}_{n,h}=\mu_{n,h}
if
\mu_{n,h}
is a nonnegative measure and
\mu_{n,h}^{0}=\delta_{X_{1}}
otherwise. Then,

(3.5)

\mathbb{E}W_{p}(\mu^{0}_{n,h},\mu)\lesssim\begin{cases}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+d}}&\text{ if }d\geq 3,\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\log n\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d=2,\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d=1.\end{cases}

(iv)

Furthermore, for any
0\leq s<k
and
\tau_{\min}>0
, if
f_{\min}
is small enough and if
f_{\max}
and
L_{s}
are large enough, then there exists a manifold
M\in\mathcal{M}^{k}_{d}
such that

(3.6)

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mu,W_{p},\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M))\gtrsim\begin{cases}n^{-\frac{s+1}{2s+d}}&\text{ if }d\geq 3,\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d\leq 2.\end{cases}

Remark 3.2
.

The condition
C(\beta)
on the kernel is only used to ensure that the measure
\mu_{n,h}
has a lower and upper bounded density on
M
. An alternative possibility to ensure this property is to assume that the density of
\mu
is Hölder continuous of exponent
\delta
for some
\delta>0
. Techniques from
[
BH19
]
then imply that
\|\mu_{n,h}-\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}\lesssim h^{\delta}+n^{-1/2}h^{-d/2}\ll 1
with high probability, ensuring in particular that the density is lowerbounded. If
sp>d
, then every element of
B^{s}_{p,q}(M)
is Hölder continuous
[
Tri92
, Theorem 7.4.2]
, and condition
C(\beta)
is no longer required. However, Theorem
3.1
also holds for non-continuous densities.

Remark 3.3
.

Let
K
be a nonnegative kernel satisfying conditions
A
,
B(0)
and
C(\beta)
. It is straightforward to check that
W_{p}(\mu_{n},\mu_{n,h})\lesssim h
. Therefore, Theorem
3.1
(ii)
and Proposition
2.8
imply in particular that
W_{p}(\mu_{n},\mu)\lesssim h+\frac{h^{1-d/2}I_{d}(h)}{\sqrt{n}}
. By choosing
h
of the order
n^{-1/d}
, we obtain that

(3.7)

W_{p}(\mu_{n},\mu)\lesssim\begin{cases}n^{-\frac{1}{d}}&\text{ if }d\geq 3\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d=2\\
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ if }d=1.\end{cases}

Such a result was already shown for
p=\infty

[
TGHS20
]
with additional logarithmic factors, with a proof very different than ours. See also
[
Div21b
]
for a short proof of this result when
M
is the flat torus.

Remark 3.4
.

There is a logarithmic gap between the minimax lower bound and the upper risk of the proposed estimator for
d=2
in Theorem
3.1
. When
\mu
is the uniform measure on the square
[0,1]^{2}
, it is known that the empirical measure
\mu_{n}
attains exactly the rate
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{\frac{1}{2}}

[
Tal14
, Section 6.4]
, suggesting that the factor
(\log n)^{1/2}
is not a proof artifact. It is however not clear how one can transform Talagrand’s tree construction used to lower bound
\mathbb{E}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\mu)
into a more general minimax lower bound on
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mu,W_{p},\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M))
, so that the (non-)existence of estimators attaining a rate of
n^{-1/2}
for
d=2
is still an open problem.

In (
3.4
), a classical bias-variance trade-off appears. Namely, the bias of the estimator is of order
h^{s+1}
, whereas its fluctuations are of order
h^{1-d/2}/\sqrt{n}
(at least for
d\geq 3
). This decomposition can be compared to the classical bias-variance decomposition for a kernel density estimator of bandwidth
h
, say for the pointwise estimation of a function of class
\mathcal{C}^{s}
on the cube
[0,1]^{d}
. It is then well-known (see e.g.
[
Tsy08
, Chapter 1]
) that the bias of the estimator is of order
h^{s}
whereas its variance is of order
h^{-d/2}/\sqrt{n}
. The supplementary factor
h
appearing both in the bias and fluctuation terms can be explained by the fact that we are using a norm
H^{-1}_{p}(M)
instead of a pointwise norm to quantify the risk of the estimator: in some sense, we are estimating the antiderivative of the density rather than the density itself. This is particularly striking when
d=1
and
p=1
, the Wasserstein distance between two measures being then given by the
L_{1}
distance between the cumulative distribution functions of the two measures
[
San15
, Proposition 2.17]
.

Before giving a proof of Theorem
3.1
, let us explain how to extend it to the case where the manifold
M
is unknown and in the presence of tubular noise. The measure
\mu_{n,h}
is the measure having density
K_{h}*(\mu_{n}/\rho_{h})
with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
. Of course, if
M
is unknown, then so is
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, and we therefore propose the following estimation procedure of
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, using local polynomial estimation techniques from
[
AL19
]
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a
n
-sample in the model with tubular noise
\mathcal{Q}^{s,k}_{d}(\gamma)
, with
X_{i}=Y_{i}+Z_{i}
,
Y_{i}
of law
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
and
Z_{i}\in T_{Y_{i}}M^{\bot}
with
|Z_{i}|\leq\gamma
. Let
\nu_{n}^{(i)}
be the empirical measure
\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{X_{j}-X_{i}}
. For two positive parameters
\ell
,
\varepsilon
, the local polynomial estimator
(\hat{\pi}_{i},\hat{V}_{2,i},\dots,\hat{V}_{m-1,i})
of order
m
at
X_{j}
is defined as an element of

(3.8)

\argmin_{\pi,\sup_{2\leq j\leq m-1}\left\|V_{j}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\ell}\mu_{n}^{(i)}\left(\left|x-\pi(x)-\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}V_{j}[\pi(x)^{\otimes j}]\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}\{x\in\mathcal{B}(0,\varepsilon)\}\right),

where the argmin is taken over all orthogonal projectors
\pi
of rank
d
and symmetric tensors
V_{j}:(\mathbb{R}^{D})^{j}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
of order
j
.
Let
\hat{T}_{i}
be the image of
\hat{\pi}_{i}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{i}:v\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\mapsto X_{i}+v+\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}\hat{V}_{j,i}[v^{\otimes j}]
. Let
\angle(T_{1},T_{2})
denote the angle between two
d
-dimensional subspaces, defined by
\left\|\pi_{T_{1}}-\pi_{T_{2}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, where
\pi_{T_{i}}
is the orthogonal projection on
T_{i}
for
i=1,2
. We summarize the results of
[
AL19
]
in the following proposition (see Appendix
A
for details).

Proposition 3.5
.

With probability at least
1-cn^{-k/d}
, if
m\leq k
,
\left(\log n/n\right)^{1/d}\lesssim\varepsilon\lesssim 1
,
\gamma\lesssim\varepsilon
and
1\lesssim\ell\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
, then,

(3.9)

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\angle(T_{Y_{i}}M,\hat{T}_{i})\lesssim\varepsilon^{m-1}+\gamma\varepsilon^{-1}

and, for all
1\leq i\leq n
, if
v\in\hat{T}_{i}
with
|v|\leq 3\varepsilon
, we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle|\hat{\Psi}_{i}(v)-\Psi_{Y_{i}}\circ\pi_{Y_{i}}(v)|\lesssim\varepsilon^{m}+\gamma

(3.11)

\displaystyle\left\|d\hat{\Psi}_{i}(v)-d(\Psi_{Y_{i}}\circ\pi_{Y_{i}})(v)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{m-1}+\gamma\varepsilon^{-1}.

Hence, if
\gamma
is of order at most
\varepsilon^{k}
, then it is possible to approximate the tangent space at
Y_{i}
with precision
\varepsilon^{k-1}
and the local parametrization with precision
\varepsilon^{k}
. In particular, authors in
[
AL19
]
show that, with high probability,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{B}_{\hat{\Psi}_{i}(\hat{T}_{i})}(X_{i},\varepsilon)
is at Hausdorff distance of order at most
\varepsilon^{k}+\gamma
from
M
. We now define an estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
of
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
by using an appropriate partition of unity
(\chi_{j})_{j}
, which is built thanks to the next lemma. For
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, introduce the asymmetric Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(A|B)\vcentcolon=\sup_{x\in A}d(x,B)
and the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}(A,B)\vcentcolon=d_{H}(A|B)\vee d_{H}(B|A)
. We say that a set
S
is
\delta
-sparse if
|x-y|\geq\delta
for all distinct points
x,y\in S
.

Lemma 3.6

(Construction of partitions of unity)
.

Let
\delta\lesssim 1
. Let
S\subset M^{\delta}
be a set which is
\frac{7}{3}\delta
-sparse, with
d_{H}(M^{\delta}|S)\leq 4\delta
. Let
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to[0,1]
be a smooth radial function supported on
\mathcal{B}(0,1)
, which is equal to
1
on
\mathcal{B}(0,1/2)
. Define, for
y\in M^{\delta}
and
x\in S
,

(3.12)

\chi_{x}(y)=\frac{\theta\left(\frac{y-x}{8\delta}\right)}{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in S}\theta\left(\frac{y-x^{\prime}}{8\delta}\right)}.

Then, the sequence of functions
\chi_{x}:M^{\delta}\to[0,1]
for
x\in S
, satisfies (i)
\sum_{x\in S}\chi_{x}\equiv 1
, with at most
c_{d}
non zero terms in the sum at any given point of
M^{\delta}
, (ii)
\|\chi_{x}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{l}(M^{\delta})}\leq C_{l,d}\delta^{-l}
for any
l\geq 0
and, (iii)
\chi_{x}
is supported on
\mathcal{B}_{M^{\delta}}(x,8\delta)
.

A proof of Lemma
3.6
is given in Appendix
A
. Lemma
3.6
requires the set
S
to be
7\delta/3
-sparse. Actually, given a set
S_{0}\subset M^{\delta}
with
d_{H}(M^{\delta}|S_{0})\leq 5\delta/3
, there always exist a subset
S\subset S_{0}
that satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
3.6
. Such a subset
S
can be computed using the farthest point sampling algorithm (see e.g.
[
AL18
, Section 3.3]
): initialize
S
with an arbitrary point of
S_{0}
, and, at each step, add the farthest point
s\in S_{0}
from
S
(that is the point that maximizes
d(s,S_{0})
). We stop the algorithm when every point of
s\in S_{0}
satisfies
d(s,S)\leq 7\delta/3
. By construction, the set
S
is
7\delta/3
-sparse and satisfies
d_{H}(S,S_{0})\leq 7\delta/3
. We then have
d_{H}(M^{\delta}|S)\leq 5\delta/3+7\delta/3=4\delta
, so that indeed one can construct a partition of unity using
S
.

The next proposition describes how we may define a minimax estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
of the volume measure on
M
(up to logarithmic factors) using such a partition of unity.

Theorem 3.7

(Minimax estimation of the volume measure on
M
)
.

Let
d\leq D
be integers and
k\geq 2
. Let
\nu\in\mathcal{Q}^{0,k}_{d}(\gamma)
and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a
n
-sample of law
\nu
. Let
\left(\log n/n\right)^{1/d}\lesssim\varepsilon\lesssim 1
,
\gamma\lesssim\varepsilon
,
1\lesssim\ell\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
.

(i)

Let
\{X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{J}}\}
be the output of the farthest point sampling algorithm with parameter
7\varepsilon/24
and input
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
. With probability larger than
1-cn^{-k/d}
, there exists a sequence of smooth nonnegative functions
\chi_{j}:M^{\varepsilon/8}\to[0,1]
for
1\leq j\leq J
, such that
\chi_{j}
is supported on
\mathcal{B}_{M^{\varepsilon/8}}(X_{i_{j}},\varepsilon)
,
\|\chi_{j}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1}(M^{\varepsilon/8})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{J}\chi_{j}(z)=1
for
z\in M^{\varepsilon/8}
, with at most
c_{d}
non-zero terms in the sum.

(ii)

Let
\hat{\Psi}_{i}
be the local polynomial estimator of order
m\leq k
with parameter
\varepsilon
and
\ell
, and
\hat{T}_{i}
the associated tangent space. Let
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
be the measure defined by, for all continuous bounded functions
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(3.13)

\int\phi(x)\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\hat{\Psi}_{i_{j}}(\hat{T}_{i_{j}})}\phi(x)\chi_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where the integration is taken against the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\hat{\Psi}_{i_{j}}(\hat{T}_{i_{j}})
. Then, for
1\leq r\leq\infty
, with probability larger than
1-cn^{-k/d}
, we have

(3.14)

\begin{split}&W_{r}\left(\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}}{|\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}|},\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{M}}{|\mathrm{vol}_{M}|}\right)\lesssim(\gamma+\varepsilon^{m})(1+\gamma\varepsilon^{-2}).\end{split}

(iii)

In particular, if
m=k
,
\varepsilon\asymp(\log n/n)^{1/d}
and
\gamma\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
, we obtain that

(3.15)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}W_{r}\left(\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}}{|\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}|},\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{M}}{|\mathrm{vol}_{M}|}\right)\lesssim\gamma+\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{k}{d}}.\end{split}

4.
Proofs of the main theorems

The proof of Theorem
3.1
relies on the classical bias variance decomposition displayed in (
3.3
), together with the linearization inequality given in Proposition
2.8
. We first bound the bias of the estimator, which can be expressed in term of a convolution operator on
M
.

4.1.
Bias of the kernel density estimator

The bias of the estimator is given by the distance
\|\cdot\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}
between
f
the density of
\mu
and
f_{h}\vcentcolon=K_{h}*(\mu/\rho_{h})
. For
\phi\in L_{1}(M)
, we write
\tilde{\phi}
for
\phi/\rho_{h}
. Introduce the operator
A_{h}:B^{s}_{p,q}(M)\to H^{-1}_{p}(M)
defined for
\phi\in L_{1}(M)
by

(4.1)

A_{h}\phi\vcentcolon=K_{h}*\left(\frac{\phi}{\rho_{h}}\right)-\phi=\int_{M}K_{h}(\cdot-y)\left(\tilde{\phi}(y)-\tilde{\phi}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}(y).

Then,

(4.2)

\begin{split}\|f_{h}-f\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}=\|A_{h}f\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}&\leq\|A_{h}\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M),H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M)}\\
&\leq\|A_{h}\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M),H^{-1}_{p}(M)}L_{s}.\end{split}

Therefore, it suffices to control the operator norm of
A_{h}
to bound the bias.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
0<s\leq k-1
and
1\leq p<\infty
. Assume that the kernel
K
is of order
k
. Then, if
h\lesssim 1
,

(4.3)

\|A_{h}\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M),H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\lesssim h^{s+1}.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
consists in considering the Taylor expansion of a function
\phi\in B^{s}_{p,q}(M)
. In this Taylor expansion, all polynomial terms of low order disappear when integrated against
K
, as the kernel
K
is of sufficiently large order. Namely, we have the following property, whose proof is given in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that the kernel
K
is of order
k
and let
B:(\mathbb{R}^{D})^{j}\to\mathbb{R}
be a tensor of order
1\leq j<k
. Then,
for all
x\in M
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{M}K_{h}(x-y)B[(x-y)^{\otimes j}]\mathrm{d}y\right|\lesssim\left\|B\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}h^{k}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\left|\rho_{h}(x)-1\right|\lesssim h^{k-1}\ \text{ and }\ \|\rho_{h}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{j}(M)}\lesssim h^{k-1-j}

Let us now give a sketch of proof of Proposition
4.1
in the case
0<s\leq 1
. The
H^{-1}_{p}(M)
-norm of
A_{h}\phi
is by definition equal to

\|A_{h}\phi\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}=\sup\left\{\int(A_{h}\phi)g\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M},\ \|g\|_{H^{1}_{p^{*}}(M)}\leq 1\right\}.

Let
g\in H^{1}_{p^{*}}(M)
with
\|g\|_{H^{1}_{p^{*}}(M)}\leq 1
. We use the following symmetrization trick:

\displaystyle\int(A_{h}\phi)(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x=\iint K_{h}(x-y)(\tilde{\phi}(y)-\tilde{\phi}(x))g(x)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\iint K_{h}(y-x)(\tilde{\phi}(x)-\tilde{\phi}(y))g(y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x\text{ (by swapping the indexes $x$ and $y$)}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\iint K_{h}(x-y)(\tilde{\phi}(y)-\tilde{\phi}(x))(g(x)-g(y))\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x

where, at the last line, we averaged the two previous lines and used that
K
is an even function. Informally, as
K_{h}(x-y)=0
if
|x-y|\geq h
, and as
\rho_{h}
is roughly constant, we expect
|\tilde{\phi}(y)-\tilde{\phi}(x)|
to be of order
h^{s}
and
|g(x)-g(y)|
to be of order
h

\int(A_{h}\phi)(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x
of order
h^{s+1}
. Such a trick can be generalized to a function
\phi
of regularity
s>1
by writing a Taylor expansion of
\phi
and using that all the polynomial terms
(x-y)^{\alpha}
vanish when integrated against
K_{h}(x-y)
according to Lemma
4.2
. More precisely, we prove in Appendix
C
the following higher order analogue of the symmetrization trick.

Lemma 4.3

(Symmetrization trick)
.

There exists
h_{0}\lesssim 1
such that the following holds. Let
l
be an even number between
0
and
k-1
and let
K^{(l)}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}K_{\lambda}(x)\frac{(1-\lambda)^{l-1}\lambda^{-l}}{(l-1)!}\mathrm{d}\lambda
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Fix
x_{0}\in M
and let
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
be a function supported in
\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})
. Define
\tilde{\phi}_{l}\vcentcolon=d^{l}(\tilde{\phi}\circ\Psi_{x_{0}})\circ\tilde{\pi}_{x_{0}}
. Let
g\in L_{p^{*}}(M)
with
\|g\|_{L_{p^{*}}(M)}\leq 1
.

Then, for
h\lesssim 1
,
\int(A_{h}\phi)(x)g(x)\mathrm{d}x
is equal to

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})^{2}}K^{(l)}_{h}(x-y)(\tilde{\phi}_{l}(y)-\tilde{\phi}_{l}(x))\left[\pi_{x_{0}}(x-y)\right]^{\otimes l}\left(g(x)-g(y)\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x+R,

where

There are two restrictions in the above lemma: (i) the function

0
and

. Let us first see how one can use Lemma

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)

(4.8)

\begin{split}&\left(h^{-d}\iint_{\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})^{2}}\mathbf{1}\{|x-y|\leq h\}\frac{\left\|\eta_{l}(x)-\eta_{l}(y)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{p}}{|x-y|^{p}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/p}\\
&\qquad\qquad\lesssim\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})}\left\|\eta_{l+1}(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}\lesssim\|\eta\|_{H^{l+1}_{p}(M)}.\end{split}

Let
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)

Case

Case

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})^{2}}|K_{\lambda h}(x-y)|\left\|\tilde{\phi}_{l}(y)-\tilde{\phi}_{l}(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}|g(x)-g(y)||x-y|^{l}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {}\lesssim(\lambda h)^{l+2}\hskip-2.84544pt\left((\lambda h)^{-d}\iint_{\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})^{2}}\mathbf{1}\{|x-y|\leq\lambda h\}\left\|\tilde{\phi}_{l}(y)-\tilde{\phi}_{l}(x)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{p}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\right)^{\hskip-2.84544pt1/p}\\

So far, we have proven that

(4.11)

\|A_{h}\phi\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\lesssim h^{s+1}\|\phi\|_{H^{s}_{p}(M)}

for all integers

and
\phi
a smooth function supported on
\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})

To obtain the result when
\phi
is not supported on some ball
\mathcal{B}_{M}(x_{0},h_{0})
, we use an appropriate partition of unity. Indeed, for
\delta=h_{0}/8
, standard packing arguments show the existence of a set
S_{0}
of cardinality
N\leq c_{d}|\mathrm{vol}_{M}|\delta^{-d}
with

. By the remark following Lemma
3.6
, the output

3.6
, and is of cardinality smaller than

. We consider such a covering
(\mathcal{B}_{M}(x,h_{0}))_{x\in S}
, with associated partition of unity
(\chi_{x})_{x\in S}
given by Lemma

. Then,
\|A_{h}\phi\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S}\|A_{h}(\chi_{x}\phi)\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\lesssim h^{s+1}\sum_{x\in S}\|\chi_{x}\phi\|_{H^{s}_{p}(M)}\lesssim h^{s+1}\sum_{x\in S}\|\chi_{x}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{s}(M)}\|\phi\|_{H^{s}_{p}(M)}\lesssim h^{s+1}\|\phi\|_{H^{s}_{p}(M)},

where the second to last inequality follows from Leibniz rule for the derivative of a product. Also, the last inequality follows from the fact that
(\chi_{x})_{|M}=\chi_{x}\circ i_{M}
, where

is the inclusion, which is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}

-norm. Hence,

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
is dense in

, this gives the desired bound on the operator norm of

for
0\leq s\leq k-1
an integer. To obtain the conclusion for Besov spaces

, we use an interpolation inequality
[
Lun18
, Theorem 1.1.6]
. By the reiteration theorem
[
Lun18
, Theorem 1.3.5]
, for

,

, with an equivalent norm. Hence, we have, for
0<s<k-1
, letting

\displaystyle\|A_{h}\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M),H^{-1}_{p}(M)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|A_{h}\|_{L_{p}(M),H^{-1}_{p}(M)}^{1-\theta}\|A_{h}\|_{H^{k-1}_{p}(M),H^{-1}_{p}(M)}^{\theta}

\displaystyle\lesssim h^{1-\frac{s}{k-1}}h^{k\frac{s}{k-1}}\lesssim h^{s+1},

so that Proposition
4.1
is proven for

. It remains to prove the inequality in the case
s=k-1
. By Fatou’s lemma and the definition of interpolation spaces
[
Lun18
, Definition 1.1.2]
, we have, for some constant
C
not depending on

,

\displaystyle\|A_{h}f\|_{B^{k-1}_{p,q}(M)}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\to k-1\\
s<k-1\end{subarray}}\|A_{h}f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M)}\leq\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\to k-1\\
s<k-1\end{subarray}}\left(Ch^{s+1}\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M)}\right)\leq Ch^{k}\|f\|_{B^{k-1}_{p,q}(M)},

where we used that
\|f\|_{B^{s}_{p,q}(M)}\leq\|f\|_{B^{k-1}_{p,q}(M)}
. This concludes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

4.2.
Fluctuations of the kernel density estimator

The second step in bounding the Sobolev risk
\mathbb{E}\|f_{n,h}-f\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}
is to control the fluctuation term
\mathbb{E}\|f_{n,h}-f_{h}\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}
. If we were considering a classical
L_{p}
-norm instead of a negative Sobolev norm, then we could simply express
f_{n,h}-f_{h}
as a sum of the i.i.d. terms to obtain the right order. The key idea to bound the fluctuation term is to show that
\|f_{n,h}-f\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}
is smaller than the
L_{p}
-norm of a similar sum of i.i.d. functions that can be expressed in term of the Green’s function on
M
. We obtain the following control.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
with
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
a
n
-sample of law
\mu
. Assume that
h\lesssim 1
and that
nh^{d}\gtrsim 1
. Then,

(4.12)

\mathbb{E}\|f_{n,h}-f_{h}\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\lesssim n^{-1/2}h^{1-d/2}I_{d}(h),

where
I_{d}(h)
is defined in Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\Delta
be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
and
G:\mathcal{D}_{M}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Green’s function, defined on
\mathcal{D}_{M}\vcentcolon=\{(x,y)\in M\times M,\ x\neq y\}
(see
[
Aub82
, Chapter 4]
). By definition, if
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
, then the function
G\phi:x\in M\mapsto\int G(x,y)\phi(y)\mathrm{d}y
is a smooth function satisfying
\Delta G\phi=\phi
, with
\nabla G\phi(x)=\int\nabla_{x}G(x,y)\phi(y)\mathrm{d}y
for
x\in M
.
Hence, if
w=\nabla G\phi
, then
\nabla\cdot w=\phi
, so that, Proposition
2.5
yields

\|\phi\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\leq\|\phi\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}_{p}(M)}\leq\|\nabla G\phi\|_{L_{p}(M)}.

By linearity, we have

(4.13)

\begin{split}&\|f_{n,h}-f_{h}\|_{H^{-1}_{p}(M)}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla G\left(K_{h}*\left(\frac{\delta_{Y_{i}}}{\rho_{h}(Y_{i})}\right)\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla G\left(K_{h}*\left(\frac{\delta_{Y_{i}}}{\rho_{h}(Y_{i})}\right)\right)\right]\right\|_{L_{p}(M)}.\end{split}

The expectation of the
L_{p}
-norm of the sum of i.i.d. centered functions is controlled using Rosenthal inequality.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be i.i.d. functions on
L_{p}(M)
. Then,
\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(U_{i}-\mathbb{E}U_{i})\right\|_{L_{p}(M)}^{p}
is smaller than

(4.14)

\begin{cases}n^{-p/2}\int\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|U_{1}(z)|^{2}\right]\right)^{p/2}\mathrm{d}z&\text{ if $p\leq 2$},\\
C_{p}n^{-p/2}\int\left(\mathbb{E}|U_{1}(z)|^{2}\right)^{p/2}\mathrm{d}z+C_{p}n^{1-p}\int_{M}\mathbb{E}\left[|U_{1}(z)|^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}z&\text{ if $p>2$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If

5.1.
Computation of the local polynomial estimator

A crucial step in the implementation of the estimators
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
and
\hat{\nu}_{n,h}
is the minimization procedure described in (
3.8
). Assume for the sake of simplicity that no noise is present in the dataset. Further assume without loss of generality that
0\in M
, and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be
N
points sampled according to some distribution
\tilde{\mu}
supported on
\mathcal{B}_{M}(0,\varepsilon)
, with density lower bounded by
f_{\min}
(in the setting of Section
3
,
\tilde{\mu}
is the conditional probability of
X\sim\mu
given that
X\in\mathcal{B}_{M}(0,\varepsilon)
, where
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{s}(M)
). Our goal is to find a minimizer of the functional

(5.1)

\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|X_{i}-\pi(X_{i})-\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}V_{j}[\pi(X_{i})^{\otimes j}]\right|^{2},

where
\pi
is an orthogonal projector of rank
d
and each
V_{j}
is a
j
-tensor with operator norm smaller than
\ell
. We propose here a fast procedure with theoretical guarantees to solve this problem, answering a question raised in
[
AL19
]
. There are two issues that make this optimization problem not trivial. First, the objective functional is defined on a manifold, and second, it is not globally geodesically convex. We will actually show that the functional is
\lambda
-strongly geodesically convex and
\beta
-smooth on a small neighborhood of size
\varepsilon
around its minimizer, for both
\lambda
and
\beta
of order
\varepsilon^{2}
. Furthermore, we show that it is possible to find a point in this neighborhood, by letting
V_{j}=0
, and
\pi
being given by a PCA on the dataset
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
. Any standard optimization algorithm on Riemannian manifolds will then converge with such an initialization. As an example, we show that a classical gradient descent converges linearly, although it is expected that more refined algorithms such as a Riemannian SVRG will also converge, with possibly a better behavior in practice
[
SKM19
]
. Before going further, we give some background on the Grassmannian manifold that will allow us to rewrite the objective functional in a more practical way. We refer to
[
EAS98
]
for a more detailed introduction on the geometry of the Grassmannian. We also provide a short review of convex optimization on Riemannian manifolds in Appendix
I
.

Let
\mathcal{O}(D)
be the manifold of orthogonal matrices on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, that we endow with the Riemannian structure induced by the inclusion in
\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. For
Q\in\mathcal{O}(D)
, the tangent space of
\mathcal{O}(D)
at
Q
is given by
T_{Q}\mathcal{O}(D)=\{QA:\ A\text{ skew-symmetric}\}
, whereas the exponential map is given by
\exp_{Q}(QA)=Q\exp(A)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}(d,D)
be the Grassmannian manifold. It can be defined as the quotient
\mathcal{O}(D)/(\mathcal{O}(d)\times\mathcal{O}(D-d))
: we identify a
d
-dimensional subspace
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with the set of matrices

(5.2)

\left\{Q\begin{pmatrix}Q_{1}&0\\
0&Q_{2}\end{pmatrix}:\ Q_{1}\in\mathcal{O}(d),Q_{2}\in\mathcal{O}(D-d)\right\},

where
Q
is any orthogonal matrix such that the vector space
\Pi(Q)
spanned by its first
d
-columns is equal to
U
. An orthogonal projector of rank
d
is then identified with
\pi_{U}
, the projection on the subspace
U
. The manifold
\mathcal{G}(D,d)
is endowed with the Riemannian structure given by the quotient map. Fix an element
U_{*}\in\mathcal{G}(d,D)
, with an arbitrary representant
Q_{*}
. Then, the tangent space
T_{U_{*}}\mathcal{G}(d,D)
at

is given by the set of matrices

(5.3)

\Delta=Q_{*}\begin{pmatrix}0&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{pmatrix},

where

is any
(D-d)\times d
matrix. The exponential map on the Grassmannian is given by the quotient projection of the exponential on the orthogonal group, that is
\exp_{U_{*}}(\Delta)=\Pi\circ\exp_{Q_{*}}(\Delta)
. The exponential map being surjective, any element of the Grassmannian can be written as
\Pi\circ\exp_{Q_{*}}(\Delta)
for some
\Delta\in T_{U_{*}}\mathcal{G}(d,D)
. Furthermore, the quotient map
\Pi:\exp_{Q_{*}}(T_{U_{*}}\mathcal{G}(d,D))\to\mathcal{G}(d,D)
is a local isometry. As it will be convenient for us to work with deformations of a fixed orthogonal basis
Q_{*}
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we will consider the
d(D-d)
-dimensional submanifold
\mathcal{O}_{*}(d,D)\vcentcolon=\exp_{Q_{*}}(T_{U_{0}}\mathcal{G}(d,D))
of
\mathcal{O}(D)
instead of
\mathcal{G}(d,D)
. A small neighborhood of
U_{*}
in
\mathcal{G}(d,D)
is isometric to a neighborhood of
Q_{*}
in
\mathcal{O}_{*}(d,D)
, so that both points of view are equivalent. Given

, we write

, where

is a

is a

Given

-tensors from

5.1
) is defined on
\mathcal{G}(d,D)\times\prod_{j=2}^{m-1}\mathcal{S}^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
, that is a manifold of possibly very large dimension (of order
D^{m}
). However, given a subspace
U\in\mathcal{G}(d,D)
, one can always decrease the loss function by replacing a tensor
V_{j}
by the tensor
\pi_{U}^{\bot}\circ V_{j}\circ\pi_{U}
. That is, we may assume that
V_{j}\in\mathcal{S}^{j}(U,U^{\bot})
. For
Q=(e_{1},\dots,e_{D})\in\mathcal{O}_{*}(d,D)
and
V_{j}=(V_{j,k})_{d+1\leq k\leq D}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{D-d})
, we let
\iota_{j}(Q,V)
be the
j
-tensor in
\mathcal{S}_{j}(\Pi(Q),\Pi(Q)^{\bot})
defined by

\iota_{j}(Q,V)[x_{1},\dots,x_{j}]=\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}e_{k}V_{j,k}[Q_{[d]}^{\top}(x_{1}\cdots x_{j})]

for
x_{1},\dots,x_{j}\in\Pi(Q)^{j}
. Introduce the functional

(5.4)

G_{m,x}(Q,V)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}\left|x-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle x,e_{k}\rangle e_{k}-\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}\iota_{j}(Q,V)[x^{\otimes j}]\right|^{2}.

The function
G_{m}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G_{m,X_{i}}
is defined on the manifold

\mathcal{M}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{O}_{*}(d,D)\times\prod_{j=2}^{m-1}\mathcal{S}_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{D-d}).

Note that
\mathcal{M}
is a manifold of dimension
(D-d)\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\binom{d+j-1}{j}
, that is of order
Dd^{m-1}\ll D^{m}
. We endow
\mathcal{M}
with the Riemannian metric
g_{\mathcal{M}}
given by

(5.5)

(g_{\mathcal{M}})_{(Q,V)}((\Delta_{1},W_{1}),(\Delta_{2},W_{2}))=\langle\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\rangle+\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}\varepsilon^{2(j-1)}\langle W_{1,j},W_{2,j}\rangle,

for
(Q,V)\in\mathcal{M}
and
(\Delta_{1},W_{1}),(\Delta_{2},W_{2})\in T_{(Q,V)}\mathcal{M}
. We also denote by
d_{\mathcal{M}}
the geodesic distance on
\mathcal{M}
. Consider the submanifold
\mathcal{M}_{0}
of
\mathcal{M}
, where we impose that each
j
-tensor
V_{j}
has operator norm smaller than
\ell
. Then, solving (
5.1
) is equivalent to minimizing
G_{m}
on
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Let us choose
(Q_{*},V_{*})
that minimizes
G_{m}
on
\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Consider the (geodesically convex) neighborhood
\Omega
of
(Q_{*},0)
in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
given by matrices
Q\in\mathcal{O}_{*}(d,D)
that are
r
close from
Q_{*}
for the geodesic distance.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\lesssim 1
,
\delta>0
, and let
r\leq\delta\varepsilon
. If
1\lesssim\ell\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}
is small enough with respect to
\delta
, then, with probability
1-e^{-cN}
, the functional
G_{m}
is geodesically
\lambda
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth on
\Omega
with
\varepsilon^{2}\lesssim\lambda\leq\beta\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
, where the constant
c
depends on
d
,
m
,
\tau_{\min}
and
f_{\min}
.

This implies in particular that a gradient descent with step
\alpha
of order
\varepsilon^{-2}
will converge at linear rate towards the minimizer of
G_{m}
, when initialized in
\Omega
(see Appendix
I
for details). A number of steps of order
|\log\varepsilon|\simeq\log n
is needed to attain a point at distance
\varepsilon^{m-1}
from the minimizer. Such a point
(Q,V)
will then satisfy the same inequalities than the minimizer
(\hat{\pi},\hat{V})
from Proposition
3.5
. Proposition
3.5
with
m=2
implies that a local PCA yields a subspace that is
\delta\varepsilon
-close from
\Pi(Q^{*})
for some
\delta>0
. Therefore, choosing
\ell
small enough, Proposition
5.1
implies that one can initialize the gradient descent at the local PCA, with linear convergence of the gradient descent.

Let us end this section with a word about the computational complexity of gradient descent. Evaluating the gradient of
G_{m}
requires
O(NDd^{m-1})
operations. Also, one need to compute the exponential map on
\mathcal{M}
at each step of the gradient descent. This boils down to computing a SVD, which can be made in
O(Dd^{2})
time. In total, each step of the gradient descent takes
O(NDd^{m-1})
time. The dependence in
N
is not an issue as
N
is of order
\log n
in our setting (that is the expected number of points of a
n
-sample in a ball of radius
\varepsilon\simeq(\log n/n)^{1/d}
). Still, one could use a stochastic gradient descent algorithm to remove the
N
factor if needed.

5.2.
Sampling from the estimators

The issue of evaluating integrals and sampling from distributions supported defined on manifolds has been addressed in several works using Monte Carlo methods: e.g. authors in
[
DHS13
]
propose rejection sampling and Gibbs sampling methods in the case where the manifold can be covered by a single known chart (except on a set of null measure), while authors in
[
ZHCG18
]
design a MCMC in the case where the manifolds is defined through equality constraints.
We here address the problem of sampling from two estimators: the estimated uniform measure
\hat{U}_{M}\vcentcolon=\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}/|\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}|
and the measure
\hat{\mu}_{n,h}
. Our goal is not to propose state-of-the-art procedures but to show that basic sampling algorithms already have good theoretical behaviors.

Let

(5.6)

q_{j}\vcentcolon=\hat{U}_{M}(\hat{\Psi}_{i_{j}}(\hat{T}_{j}))=\int_{\hat{T}_{j}}\chi_{j}(\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u))J\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u)\mathrm{d}u

and
\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}=q_{j}^{-1}\cdot(\hat{U}_{M})_{|\hat{\Psi}_{j}(\hat{T}_{j})}
, the measure
\hat{U}_{M}
conditioned on being in
\hat{\Psi}_{j}(\hat{T}_{j})
. Each
q_{j}
can be estimated by a Monte Carlo method, for instance by sampling uniform points on
\mathcal{B}_{\hat{T}_{j}}(0,\varepsilon)
. As the integrand in (
5.6
) is bounded by
2
(for
\varepsilon
small enough), the variance of the estimator of the integral can be bounded, and
\delta^{-2}
samples are necessary to obtain a precision
\delta
. The volume
|\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}|
can then be approximated by the sum of the
q_{j}
s.
A
N
-sample of law
\hat{U}_{M}
is built in two steps. First, by sampling
(N_{1},\dots,N_{J})
that follows a multinomial distribution of parameters
n
and
(q_{1},\dots,q_{J})
. Second, by sampling
N_{j}
points from
\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}
for every
j
.
We propose the following method to produce a sample with approximate distribution
\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}
. Let
\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}
be the pushforward measure of the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{B}_{\hat{T}_{j}}(0,\varepsilon)
by
\hat{\Psi}_{j}
.
It is immediate to simulate from
\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}
, while
\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}\ll\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}
, with density
\frac{d\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}}{d\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}}
being equal to, for
u\in\hat{T}_{j}
,

(5.7)

\frac{d\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}}{d\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}}(\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u))=\frac{\omega_{d}\varepsilon^{d}}{q_{j}}\chi_{j}(\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u))J\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u).

Rejection sampling with proposal distribution
\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}
then allows one to create a sample from law
\hat{U}_{M}^{(j)}
. One can check that the density in (
5.7
) is upper bounded by some constant depending only on
d
(for
\varepsilon
small enough), so that the acceptance ratio of the procedure is bounded away from zero.

Another quantity of interest is the number
N
of samples that are needed to obtain a good approximation of
\hat{U}_{M}
based on the empirical distribution
(\hat{U}_{M})_{N}
of a
N
-sample from law
\hat{U}_{M}
. It is known
[
TGHS20
]
that if a measure is supported on a
d
-dimensional manifold, then the
W_{\infty}
-distance between a measure and a
N
-sample is of order
(\log N/N)^{1/d}
(for
d\geq 3
). However,
\hat{U}_{M}
is not supported on a manifold, but on a union of overlapping polynomial patches. We however show that the expected rate of convergence still holds in this case. A proof is provided in Appendix
F
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\hat{U}_{M}
be the estimator of the uniform measure built on
n
points sampled from
\mu\in\mathcal{Q}^{0,k}_{d}
. Let
W_{1},\dots,W_{N}
be a
N
-sample of law
\hat{U}_{M}
, with associated empirical measure
(\hat{U}_{M})_{N}
. Then,

(5.8)

\mathbb{E}[W_{\infty}((\hat{U}_{M})_{N},U_{M})]\lesssim\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{k/d}+\begin{cases}\frac{(\log N)^{3/4}}{N^{1/2}}&\text{ if }d=2,\\
\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)^{1/d}&\text{ if }d\geq 3.\end{cases}

Remark 5.3

(Spectral estimation of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
)
.

García Trillos & al.
[
TGHS20
]
study the problem of estimating the spectral properties of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
on
M
. They show that the eigenvalues of a properly tuned graph Laplacian built on top of a uniform sample of points
W_{1},\dots,W_{n}
on
M
will converge at rate
(\log n/n)^{1/(2d)}
towards the eigenvalues of
\Delta
. We may build upon their results using the estimated volume measure
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
. Let
W_{1},\dots,W_{N}
be a
N
-sample of law
\hat{U}_{M}
. Then, Theorem 4 in
[
TGHS20
]
together with Proposition
5.2
yield that the eigenvalues of an appropriate graph Laplacian built on top of the sample
W_{1},\dots,W_{N}
will approximate the eigenvalues of
\Delta
at rate (for
d\geq 3
)

\sqrt{\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{k/d}+\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right)^{1/d}}.

In particular, the faster rate of convergence
(\log n/n)^{k/(2d)}
is attained by such an estimator, at the price of of being able to sample
N=n^{k}
points according to
\hat{U}_{M}
. This tradeoff between statistical accuracy and computational efficiency is a common phenomenon, discussed for instance in
[
WB19b
, Section 6]
in the setting of Wasserstein density estimation. Another advantage of our procedure is that, unlike results from
[
TGHS20
]
, it allows one to recover the eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta
even in the case where one has access to a sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of non-uniform points on
M
.

We now turn to the problem of sampling from
\hat{\nu}_{n,h}
. Computing the density of
K_{h}*(\nu_{n}/\hat{\rho}_{h})
of
\hat{\nu}_{n,h}
requires first the computation of

(5.9)

\hat{\rho}_{h}(X_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\hat{T}_{j}}K_{h}(\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u)-X_{i})\chi_{j}(\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u))J\hat{\Psi}_{j}(u)\mathrm{d}u

for every point
X_{i}
of the sample. Note that there are only a small number of non-zero terms in this sum. Still, the previous basic Monte Carlo method using uniform samples will not perform well in this case, as the integrand in (
5.9
) has
\infty
-norm of order
h^{-d}\gg 1
. A slight modification of the estimator
\hat{\nu}_{n,h}
is however possible: consider the measure
\tilde{\nu}_{n,h}
with density proportional to
K_{h}*\nu_{n}
with respect to
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
. Then, Lemma
4.9
yields that
\tilde{\nu}_{n,h}
is at distance
\varepsilon^{k}
from the measure with density
cK_{h}*\nu_{n}
with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{M}
, where
c=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int K_{h}(x-X_{i})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{M}(x)
is a normalizing constant. Furthermore, Lemma
4.2
implies that both
c
and
\hat{\rho}_{h}(X_{i})
deviates from
1
with an error at most of order
h^{k-1}
. This implies, from the linearization inequality (
2.6
) that the distance
W_{p}(\tilde{\nu}_{n,h},\hat{\nu}_{n,h})
is also of order
h^{k-1}
. In particular, the risk of the estimator
\tilde{\nu}_{n,h}
is the same as long as
s\leq k-2
(with a deterioration of the rate for
k-2\leq s\leq k-1
), while sampling from
\tilde{\nu}_{n,h}
does not require computing any integrals. Indeed, the measure
\tilde{\nu}_{n,h}
has a density
\alpha
with respect to the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\hat{\Psi}_{j}(\hat{T}_{j})
equal to, at the point
x
,

(5.10)

\alpha(x)\propto\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathbf{1}\{x\in\hat{\Psi}_{j}(\hat{T}_{j})\}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{h}(x-X_{i})\chi_{j}(x).

A Metropolis-Hasting scheme with proposal distribution
\sum_{j=1}^{J}q_{j}\tilde{U}_{M}^{(j)}
is then implementable. Furthermore, adapting the proof of Theorem
3.1
(i)
, one can check that
K_{h}*\nu_{n}(x)
is upper bounded by (say)
3f_{\max}
and lower bounded by
f_{\min}/3
for
x
in the support of
\widehat{\mathrm{vol}}_{M}
. This implies that the acceptance ratio of the Metropolis-Hasting scheme is lower bounded by a positive constant depending only on
f_{\min}
,
f_{\max}
and
d
(for
\varepsilon
small enough). The behavior in practice of those different estimators remains to be investigated and is left for future work.

Proof.

The second inequality in

on the Ricci curvature of

. This is done in the following lemma.

Appendix H
Existence of kernels satisfying conditions
A
,
B(m)
and
C(\beta)

The goal of the section is to prove the existence of a kernel
K
satisfying the conditions
A
,
B(m)
and
C(\beta)
stated at the beginning of Section
3
.

If
K
is a radial kernel, we have by integration by parts, as
K
is smooth with compact support,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha_{0}}K(v)v^{\alpha_{1}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(v)v^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{0}}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=C^{\prime}_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)r^{d+|\alpha_{0}|+|\alpha_{1}|-1}\mathrm{d}r.

Hence, to show the existence of such a kernel, it suffices to find, for every
m\geq 0
and every positive constant
\kappa
, a smooth even function
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
supported on
[-1,1]
satisfying

—

Condition
A^{\prime}
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\kappa
,

—

Condition
B^{\prime}(m)
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)r^{d+i-1}\mathrm{d}r=0
for
i=1,\dots,m
,

—

Condition
C^{\prime}(\beta)
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)^{-}r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r\leq\beta
.

We show by recursion on
m
that for any
\beta>0
, there exists a such a kernel. For
m=0
, let
K_{0}
be any smooth even nonnegative function supported on
[-1,1]
. Then, letting
K=\kappa K_{0}/\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{0}
, we obtain a kernel
K
satisfying the desired conditions for any
\beta>0
. Consider now the case
m>0
. Let
\beta>0
.

—

If
m+d
is even, then any
K
satisfying conditions
A^{\prime}
,
B^{\prime}(m-1)
and
C^{\prime}(\beta)
will also satisfy
B^{\prime}(m)
. Indeed, as
K
is even, we have
\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)r^{m+d-1}\mathrm{d}r=0
, so that the induction step is proven.

—

If
m+d
is odd, let
K
be a kernel satisfying conditions
A^{\prime}
,
B^{\prime}(m-1)
and
C^{\prime}(\beta/2)
. We use the following lemma.

Lemma H.1
.

For
i\geq 0
, let
e_{i}:x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto x^{i+d-1}
and fix an integer
m>0
. For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, let
F_{a}
be the set of smooth functions

with compact support satisfying
\int fe_{i}=0\text{ for }0\leq i<m\text{ and }\int fe_{m}=a
. Then,

(H.1)

\inf\left\{\int|f(r)|r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r,\ f\in F_{a}\right\}=0.

Assume first that the lemma is true. Let
a=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)r^{m+d-1}
and

. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\int(K(r)+f(|r|))r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\kappa+\int f(|r|)r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\kappa\\
\int(K(r)+f(|r|))r^{i+d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\int f(|r|)r^{i+d-1}\mathrm{d}r=0\text{ for }0<i<m\\
\int(K(r)+f(|r|))r^{m+d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\int K(r)r^{m+d-1}\mathrm{d}r+2\int_{1}^{\infty}f(r)r^{m+d-1}\mathrm{d}r=0.\end{cases}

Hence, the kernel
K+f(|\cdot|)
satisfies the conditions
A
and
B^{\prime}(m)
. Also, we have, as

if
|r|\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(K(r)+f(|r|))_{-}r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(r)_{-}\mathrm{d}r+2\int_{1}^{\infty}f(r)_{-}r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\beta/2+\int_{1}^{\infty}|f(r)|r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r,

where we used at the last line that
\int_{1}^{\infty}f(r)_{-}r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\int_{1}^{\infty}f(r)_{+}r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}|f(r)|r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r
.
Lemma
H.1
asserts the existence of

with
\int|f(r)|r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r\leq\beta/2
. For such a choice of

, the kernel

has a compact support, included in

A
,
B(m)
and

.

I.1.
Optimization of convex functions on Riemannian manifolds

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a complete Riemannian manifold endowed with a metric
g_{\mathcal{M}}
. We write
\mathrm{Exp}_{x}
for the exponential map at
x\in\mathcal{M}
. The geodesic distance is written as
d_{\mathcal{M}}
. We say that a set
\Omega\subset\mathcal{M}
is geodesically convex if every geodesic joining two points of
\Omega
is included in
\Omega
. We will assume that
\Omega
is small enough so that the logarithmic map
\mathrm{Exp}_{x}^{-1}
is defined on
\Omega
for every
x\in\Omega
. We say that a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function
G:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is
\lambda
-strongly geodesically convex and
\beta
-smooth if
G\circ\gamma
is
\lambda
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth for every unit-speed geodesic
\gamma
in
\Omega
. In particular, this implies

(I.1)

\begin{split}&G(y)\geq G(x)+\langle\nabla G(x),\mathrm{Exp}_{x}^{-1}(y)\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)^{2}\\
&G(y)\leq G(x)+\langle\nabla G(x),\mathrm{Exp}_{x}^{-1}(y)\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)^{2}.\end{split}

A fundamental result of convex optimization
[
Udr13
]
states that a
\beta
-smooth and
\lambda
-strongly geodesically convex function can be optimized efficiently through a gradient descent.

Proposition I.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}
has nonnegative curvature. Let
G:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be
\beta
-smooth and
\lambda
-strongly geodesically convex, with minimizer
x^{*}
. Assume that
\Omega
contains a geodesic ball centered at
x^{*}
of radius
r_{0}
. Fix
x^{0}
a point in this geodesic ball and let
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta^{-1}
.

(1)

The sequence of iterates
x^{a+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{x^{a}}(-\alpha\nabla G(x^{a}))
is well-defined for any
a\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

The sequence of iterates satisfies

(I.2)

d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{a},x^{*})^{2}\leq(1-\lambda\alpha)^{t}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{0},x^{*})^{2}.

Such a result is standard, although we could not find it in this form in the literature. We provide a short proof here.

Proof.

The fact that the sequence of iterates is well-defined follows from
\mathcal{M}
being complete, inequality (
I.2
), and the fact that
\Omega
contains a geodesic ball centered at
x^{*}
. It therefore suffices to show (
I.2
). As the manifold
\mathcal{M}
has nonnegative curvature, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{a+1},x^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{a},x^{*})^{2}+d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{a},x^{a+1})^{2}-2\langle\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}_{x^{a}}(x^{a+1}),\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}_{x^{a}}(x^{*})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{a},x^{*})^{2}+\alpha^{2}|\nabla G(x^{a})|^{2}+2\alpha\langle\nabla G(x^{a}),\mathrm{Exp}^{-1}_{x^{a}}(x^{*})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda\alpha)d_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{a},x^{*})^{2}+\alpha^{2}|\nabla G(x^{a})|^{2}+2\alpha(G(x^{*})-G(x^{a})),

where we used (
I.1
) at the last line. Also, we have by (
I.1
)

\displaystyle G(x^{*})-G(x^{a})

\displaystyle\leq G(\mathrm{Exp}_{x^{a}}(-\beta^{-1}\nabla G(x^{a})))-G(x^{a})

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla G(x^{a}),-\beta^{-1}\nabla G(x^{a})\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}|\nabla G(x^{a})|^{2}\leq-\frac{\beta}{2}|\nabla G(x^{a})|^{2},

concluding the proof.

B&0\end{pmatrix}=(Q_{[d,D]}B\ |-Q_{[d]}B^{\top})\\
&\ddot{Q}=(\ddot{e}_{1}\cdots\ddot{e}_{D})=Q\begin{pmatrix}0&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{pmatrix}^{2}=-(Q_{[d]}B^{\top}B\ |\ Q_{[d,D]}BB^{\top}).\end{split}

Note that (

(I.7)

\begin{cases}\langle\dot{e}_{k},e_{l}\rangle=-\langle e_{k},\dot{e}_{l}\rangle\text{ and }\langle\ddot{e}_{k},e_{l}\rangle=\langle e_{k},\ddot{e}_{l}\rangle,\\
\text{for $k\leq d$, }\dot{e}_{k}\in U^{\bot}\text{ and }\ddot{e}_{k}\in U,\\
\text{for $k>d$, }\dot{e}_{k}\in U\text{ and }\ddot{e}_{k}\in U^{\bot}.\end{cases}

We let
\tilde{x}=Q_{[d]}^{\top}x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Also, we insist on the distinction between the tensor

(that is a tensor from
\mathbb{R}^{D}
to

(that is a tensor from

. We will also write
\mathbb{W}_{j}
for the tensor given by
\mathbb{W}_{j}=\iota_{j}(Q,W_{j})
and let

for the SVD of

(resp.

\Sigma

Lemma I.2
.

Let
a=(a_{d+1},\dots,a_{D})\in\mathbb{R}^{D-d}
. Then,

(I.8)

|\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}\dot{e}_{k}a_{k}|\leq|a||B|.

Proof.

We have
\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}\dot{e}_{k}a_{k}=\dot{Q}_{[d,D]}a=Q_{[d]}v\Sigma u^{\top}a
. As
Q_{[d]}
and
v
have orthogonal columns, the squared norm of this vector is equal to the squared norm of
\Sigma u^{\top}a
, that is equal to

\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}\sigma_{k}^{2}\langle u_{k},a\rangle^{2}\leq|a|^{2}|B|^{2},

as each
u_{k}
is of norm
1
.
∎

Step 1

We first give bounds on
-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\langle\ddot{e}_{k},x\rangle\langle e_{k},x\rangle+\langle\dot{e}_{k},x\rangle^{2}\right)

|\langle\dot{e}_{k},x\rangle|
is negligible.

Lemma I.3
.

For
1\leq k\leq d

|\langle\dot{e}_{k},x\rangle|\leq 2|\dot{e}_{k}|\varepsilon^{2}\delta

Proof.

Let

with
|x|\leq\varepsilon
. Recall that
U
is the vector space spanned by
Q_{[d]}
. It holds that

\displaystyle|\langle\dot{e}_{k},x\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle\dot{e}_{k},\pi_{U}^{\bot}(x)\rangle|\leq|\dot{e}_{k}|(|(\pi_{U}^{\bot}-\pi_{T_{0}M}^{\bot})(x)|+|\pi_{T_{0}M}^{\bot}(x)|)

\displaystyle\leq|\dot{e}_{k}|(r\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{2}/(2\tau_{\min})).

The fact that
\delta\geq 1/(2\tau_{\min})
and that
r=\delta\varepsilon
gives the conclusion.
∎

Lemma
I.3
implies that
\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle\dot{e}_{k},x\rangle^{2}\leq 4\delta^{2}|B|^{2}\varepsilon^{4}\leq 4\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{4}

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\langle\ddot{e}_{k},x\rangle\langle e_{k},x\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}x^{\top}\ddot{e}_{k}e_{k}^{\top}x=-x^{\top}\ddot{Q}_{[d]}Q_{[d]}^{\top}x

\displaystyle=x^{\top}Q_{[d]}B^{\top}BQ_{[d]}^{\top}x=|B\tilde{x}|^{2}.

Therefore, we may lower bound the first term in the expression of

(I.9)

\begin{split}&-\mathbb{E}_{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\langle\ddot{e}_{k},X\rangle\langle e_{k},X\rangle+\langle\dot{e}_{k},X\rangle^{2}\right)\geq\mathbb{E}_{N}|B\tilde{X}|^{2}-4\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{4}\end{split}

Also, as

(I.10)

\displaystyle\qquad+e_{k}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{j}\leq d}V_{j,k}^{i_{1},\dots,i_{j}}\sum_{a=1}^{j}e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\dot{e}_{i_{a}}\cdots\otimes e_{i_{j}}\Bigg)

and

for

\displaystyle|\dot{\mathbb{V}}(x)|^{2}

\begin{split}\left(\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}|A_{2,k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}&=\left(\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{j}\leq d}V_{j,k}^{i_{1},\dots,i_{j}}\sum_{a=1}^{j}\langle e_{i_{a}},z\rangle\prod_{c\neq a}\langle e_{i_{c}},x\rangle^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}\\

Also, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}|A_{1,k}||A_{2,k}|\leq C_{d,m}\left(\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}\varepsilon^{j}|W_{j,k}|\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}|A_{2,k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

where we used at the last line that

\displaystyle|\sum_{k=d+1}^{D}\dot{e}_{k}V_{j,k}[\tilde{x}^{\otimes j}]|^{2}=|Q_{[d]}B^{\top}V_{j}[\tilde{x}^{\otimes j}]|^{2}=|Q_{[d]}v\Sigma u^{\top}V_{j}[\tilde{x}^{\otimes j}]|^{2}

Lemma I.4
.

Let
S_{j}
be a
j
-tensor from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
for each
j=1,\dots,m-1
. Then,

(I.17)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}S_{j}[Y^{\otimes j}]\right)^{2}\right]\geq f_{\min}c_{d,m}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}|S_{j}|^{2}.

Proof.

The random variable
\tilde{X}
has entries
\langle e_{k},X\rangle
for
1\leq k\leq d
. As
(e_{1},\dots,e_{d})
is an orthonormal basis of
U
that is
\delta\varepsilon
-close from
T_{0}M
, the random variable
\tilde{X}
has a density lower bounded by
f_{\min}/2
on its support, and this support contains
\mathcal{B}(0,\varepsilon/2)
. Therefore, the expectation with respect to
Y
is larger than

f_{\min}\int_{\mathcal{B}(0,1/2)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}S_{j}[y^{\otimes j}]\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

We may also write
\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}S_{j}[y^{\otimes j}]
as the dot product
\langle\mathbf{S},\mathbf{y}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{y}
are vectors indexed by
\sigma\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{m-1}\{1,\dots,d\}^{j}
, with the entries corresponding to
\sigma=(i_{1},\dots,i_{j})
given by
\mathbf{S}_{\sigma}=S_{j}^{i_{1},\dots,i_{j}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\sigma}=\prod_{a=1}^{j}y_{i_{a}}
. Therefore, this integral is exactly equal to
\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{S}
, where
\mathbf{C}
is the matrix with entries
\mathbf{C}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}(0,1/2)}\mathbf{y}_{\sigma}\mathbf{y}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}y
. To conclude, we need to show that this matrix is positive definite. This follows from
\mathbf{C}
being a Gram matrix for the
L_{2}
dot product on
\mathcal{B}(0,1/2)
associated with the
L_{2}
functions
y\mapsto\mathbf{y}_{\sigma}
that are linearly independent. Therefore, we have
\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{S}\geq c_{d,m}|\mathbf{S}|^{2}=c_{d,m}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}|S_{j}|^{2}
.
