Abstract
. We implement a version of conformal field theory in a doubly connected domain to connect it to the theory of annulus SLE of various types, including the standard annulus SLE, the reversible annulus SLE, and the annulus SLE with several force points. This implementation considers the statistical fields generated under the OPE multiplication by the Gaussian free field and its central/background charge modifications with a weighted combination of Dirichlet and excursion-reflected boundary conditions. We derive the Eguchi-Ooguri version of Ward’s equations and Belavin–Polyakov–Zamolodchikov equations for those statistical fields and use them to show that the correlations of fields in the OPE family under the insertion of the one-leg operators are martingale-observables for variants of annulus SLEs. We find Coulomb gas (Dotsenko-Fateev integral) solutions to the parabolic partial differential equations for partition functions of conformal field theory for the reversible annulus SLE.

1.
Introduction

Since Schramm introduced Schramm-Loewner evolution (SLE) in a simply connected domain
[
58
]
, SLE theory has successfully produced rigorous proofs of several remarkable conjectures in statistical physics over the past two decades.
Examples include the proofs of conformal invariance of scaling limits of some important critical lattice models by employing SLE.
Such results have been obtained by the fundamental fact that certain discrete observables converge to conformally covariant martingale processes.
For instance, in
[
62
]
, Smirnov used Cardy’s observables to prove conformal invariance of scaling limit of critical site percolation in a hexagonal lattice.
Schramm and Sheffield made use of a certain bosonic observable to prove that the level lines of discrete Gaussian free field (GFF) with specific height gaps converge to SLE(4), see
[
60
]
.
Chelkak, Duminil-Copin, Hongler, Kemppaninen, and Smirnov proved the existence of scaling limit of FK Ising model interfaces and its conformal invariance utilizing certain parafermionic observables, see
[
9
]
and references therein.
In addition, some geometric properties of SLE curves have been obtained through
SLE martingale-observables
.
Examples include the study of Hausdorff dimensions (Beffara’s observables)
[
5
]
, and left passage probabilities (Schramm’s observables)
[
59
]
of SLE.

Beyond the SLE theory in a simply connected domain, there have been several developments of the theory in a multiply connected domain
[
45
]
that contains richer geometric structures.
In a doubly connected domain, Zhan has made significant results on basic properties of SLE, see e.g.,
[
66
,
67
,
72
]
.
In particular, in
[
72
]
, he considered the annulus
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
with two marked points and introduced specific parabolic PDE (known as the
null-vector equation
in the physics literature) of
Z
that provides a necessary condition for which the associated SLE trace is reversible, cf.
[
50
,
69
]
.
Here
\Lambda:=\kappa(\log Z)^{\prime}
is the drift function of the driving process of SLE, where the function
Z
is called
annulus SLE partition function
.
We remark that such an equation for some special cases with
\kappa=3,6
also appeared in the literature
[
15
,
31
]
in connection with the study of discrete lattice models.

In the physics literature, in particular, in the context of conformal field theory (CFT)
[
13
]
, it is well known that under the insertion of the ratio of the
one-leg operator

\Psi
to its correlation function
\mathbf{E}\Psi
, the correlators in a particular class of fields are martingale-observables for SLEs.
In a doubly connected domain, Hagendorf, Bernard, and Bauer used a certain bosonic observable to propose the partition functions for annulus SLE with
\kappa=4
associated with GFFs, see
[
28
]
.
Hagendorf presented physical arguments on producing martingale-observables for the Dirichlet boundary case in
[
27
]
.

In this paper, we use the framework built in
[
35
,
36
,
1
,
34
]
to construct a version of CFT in a doubly connected domain with a plan to serve as a solid cornerstone to develop a theory in a general multiply connected domain.
The scope of this paper includes Coulomb gas formalism and derivations of some important equations in CFT, such as the Eguchi-Ooguri version of Ward’s equations and Belavin-Polyakov-Zamolodchikov (BPZ) equations.

The simplest fields we consider in this paper form a one-parameter family of GFFs with a
weighted combination
of Dirichlet and excursion reflected (ER) boundary conditions, see Figure
1
.
In the analysis literature, the Green’s function with ER boundary condition is often called the
modified Green’s function

[
39
]
.
In probability theory, the associated stochastic process, ER Brownian motion, was introduced by Lawler and Drenning
[
14
,
44
]
.
As a characteristic feature of the ER Green’s function, its normal derivatives on the inner boundary component have a vanishing mean, see e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
.
By combining this property with zero Dirichlet boundary condition under the (rational) weight, we introduce a one-parameter family of the Green’s functions interpolating Dirichlet Green’s function with ER Green’s function and consider the associated GFFs.

One of the primary motivations for introducing such general boundary conditions is that this interpolation gives rise to infinitely many linearly independent solutions to BPZ equation.
Our observation affirms the prediction that contrary to a simply connected domain where BPZ equation has a finite-dimensional solution space
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
, the solution space is no longer finite-dimensional in a doubly connected domain.
We also refer to
[
42
,
54
,
41
]
for the analysis of solutions to various equations arising from CFT with a concrete understanding of the underlying algebraic structure.

The statistical fields satisfying BPZ equations, thus producing SLE martingale-observables form the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{(b),\boldsymbol{\beta}}
generated by central/background charge modifications
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\Phi_{(b),\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of the GFFs under OPE multiplication.
Here,
b
is a real parameter related to the central charge
c
as
c=1-12b^{2}
and the SLE parameter
\kappa
as
b=\sqrt{\kappa/8}-\sqrt{2/\kappa}
.
We require that for a divisor
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum\beta_{j}\cdot q_{j}
called a background charge, the total sum of the charges should vanish, i.e.,
\sum\beta_{j}=0
.
On a complex torus (of genus one), this requirement is known as the
neutrality condition
(
\mathrm{NC}_{0}
) in the physics literature, see e.g.,
[
13
]
.
To derive BPZ equations, we need two main ingredients, the first is
Ward’s equation
and the second is the construction of the one-leg operator
\Psi
satisfying
level two degeneracy equation
.

Whereas Ward’s equations in a simply connected domain describe the Virasoro fields in terms of Lie derivative operators within correlations, the statement of those in a doubly connected domain contains the derivative of correlation functions with respect to the modular (Teichmüller) parameter as well.
These forms of Ward’s equations are well known in the physics literature, e.g., Ward’s equations in a doubly connected domain
[
28
]
and Eguchi-Ooguri’s version of Ward’s equations on a complex torus of genus one
[
20
]
.
We derive them not from the path integral formalism but the functional equations of classical special functions such as pseudo-addition theorem of Weierstrass zeta function.
See
[
36
]
for a mathematical derivation of Eguchi-Ooguri’s version of Ward’s equations.

For given another divisor
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\sum_{j}\tau_{j}\cdot\xi_{j}
satisfying
a+\int\boldsymbol{\tau}=0
, we construct the one-leg operator
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})
from the GFF as the modified multi-vertex field (or the OPE exponential) with the charge
a=\sqrt{2/\kappa}
at given marked point
p
.
With this specific choice of the charge at
p
, the one-leg operator
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
satisfies the desired level two degeneracy equation.
The correlation function
\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of the one-leg operator (boundary condition changing operator) is called the
partition function
in CFT (up to a multiplicative constant depending only on a domain), see e.g.,
[
13
, Section 11.3]
. The partition functions have different descriptions in other contexts, such as random matrix theory, statistical mechanics, and SLE theory. For example, Dubédat introduced the partition functions of SLE and the partition functions of the free field with associated boundary data and then showed that these two definitions coincide, see
[
19
, Theorem 5.3]
. Unless stated otherwise, the partition function means the correlation function of the one-leg operator throughout the paper.
In some typical examples, we relate the partition function in CFT with the partition of the associated lattice model.

We first study the annulus
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
with
\Lambda\equiv\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})=\kappa\,\partial_{\xi}|_{\xi=p}\log\mathbf{E}\,\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, see
[
34
]
for this type of SLE in a simply connected domain from a viewpoint of Radon-Nikodym derivative (or coordinate change
[
61
]
) between the laws of SLEs.
Here, the marked boundary points
p,\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{\xi}
play different roles: the point
p
corresponds to the starting point of
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
, whereas
\boldsymbol{\xi}
(resp.,
\boldsymbol{q}
) are force points having non-trivial (resp., trivial) conformal dimensions.
We consider a collection of all correlation functions of fields in the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
under the insertion of the properly normalized one-leg operator.
Then employing BPZ-Cardy equations, we show that this collection forms martingale-observables for
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
.
In the case of Dirichlet boundary condition, this statement generalizes a result of Izyurov and Kytölä in
[
33
]
, where they showed that 1-and 2-point functions of GFF with piecewise Dirichlet boundary condition are martingale-observables for associated
\mathrm{SLE}(4,\Lambda)
.
Here, the neutrality condition coincides with the requirement that jumps of the 1-point function on the boundary should add up to zero.

However, the correlation function
Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\mathbf{E}\,\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of this type of
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
with one force point does not satisfies the null-vector equation (see (
2.5
) in Subsection
2.3
) for the reversible annulus SLE partition function (introduced in
[
72
]
) unless
\kappa=4
.
To find explicit solutions to the null-vector equation of this type for generic
\kappa>0
and construct a class of
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
martingale-observables associated with these solutions, we apply the
method of screening
(also known as the
Coulomb gas
or
Dotsenko-Fateev
integrals).
If
\kappa>4
, we can deform a Pochhammer contour in this methodology so that the solution can be represented in terms of the Euler type integral.
In the case
\kappa\leq 4
, we perform proper analysis on an analytic continuation (with respect to
\kappa
) of the Pochhammer contour integral to obtain a non-trivial real-valued solution.

Beyond the method of screening, we present further constructions of SLE martingale-observables, which include the implementations of non-atomic background charges and the use of periodization (or chiral bosonization) of conformal fields.
These methods allow us to construct martingale-observables for continuum interface curves in statistical physics such as discrete GFF, loop-erased random walk, and critical Ising interfaces.
Moreover, we derive some geometric properties such as hitting/left passage probabilities of certain annulus SLEs.

Main results

2.1.
Notation

Throughout this paper, we use the following symbols and notations: let
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
; for
r>0
let
\mathbb{A}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:e^{-r}<|z|<1\}
,
\mathbb{T}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=e^{-r}\}
,
\mathbb{S}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\mathrm{Im}\,z<r\}
, and
\mathbb{R}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Im}\,z=r\}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{r}
the cylinder
\mathbb{S}_{r}/\langle z\mapsto z+2\pi\rangle
, and by
D_{r}
a doubly connected domain with modulus
r
.
A subset
K
of a doubly connected domain
D
is called a hull in
D
if
D\setminus K
is a doubly connected domain and
K
has positive distance from one boundary component of
D
.
For any hull
K
of
D
, let us define
\text{cap}_{D}(K):=\text{mod}(D)-\text{mod}(D\setminus K)
as the capacity of
K
in
D
, where
\text{mod}(\cdot)
denotes the modulus of a doubly connected domain.
Following
[
67
,
72
]
, we write
\Theta(r,z):=\theta(\frac{z}{2\pi},\frac{ir}{\pi})
, where
\theta
is a Jacobi theta function, see e.g.,
[
8
, p.63]
.
In Subsection
3.1
we recall the definition of
\Theta
and its basic properties.
It is convenient to introduce

\Theta_{\chi}(r,z):=\Theta(r,z)\,\exp\Big(\frac{z^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big),\qquad\chi\in[0,\infty]

to describe a one-parameter family of GFFs with a
weighted combination
of Dirichlet and excursion reflected (ER) boundary conditions.
We write
\zeta_{r}
for the Weierstrass zeta function with basic periods
(2\pi,2ir)
.
In the sequel we sometimes omit the modular parameter
r
and write for instance
\Theta_{\chi}(\cdot)\equiv\Theta_{\chi}(r,\cdot)
.

2.2.
Basic setup

Let
\Phi_{(0)}
be the GFF in
D_{r}
with a weighted combination of Dirichlet and ER boundary conditions, see Subsection
3.2
for details. Its 2-point function is given by

\mathbf{E}\,\Phi_{(0)}(\zeta)\Phi_{(0)}(z)=2\log\Big|\dfrac{\Theta_{\chi}(r,\zeta-\bar{z})}{\Theta_{\chi}(r,\zeta-z)}\Big|

in the
\mathcal{C}_{r}
-uniformization.
For a real parameter
b
, we define the
central charge modification

\Phi_{(b)}
of GFF by adding a non-random pre-pre-Schwarzian (PPS) form as follows:

\Phi_{(b)}:=\Phi_{(0)}-2b\arg w^{\prime},

where
w
is a conformal map from
D_{r}
onto
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
See Subsection
3.3
for the definition of PPS forms.
We remark that
\arg w^{\prime}
does not depend on the choice of a conformal map.

Given marked points
\boldsymbol{q}=\{q_{k}\}
, we call a divisor
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\cdot q_{k}
a
background charge
if it satisfies the neutrality condition
\int\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
.
We define the
background charge modification

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}
associated with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
as

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\Phi_{(b)}+\sum\beta_{k}\arg\Big\{\Theta_{\chi}(w_{k}-w_{z})\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{k}-w_{z})\Big\},

where
w_{z}=w(z)
and
w_{k}=w(q_{k})
.
Let us denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}
the OPE family of the
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, the algebra (over
\mathbb{C}
) spanned by the generators
1
, mixed derivatives of
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, those of OPE exponentials
e^{\ast\alpha\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
(
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
), and their holomorphic/anti-holomorphic/mixed parts (with certain neutrality conditions) under the OPE multiplication
\ast
.
We present a more precise definition for OPE exponentials in the following subsection.

We now recall the (covering) annulus SLE with
N
-force points
q_{1},\cdots q_{N}
.
Like the SLE in a simply connected domain, the annulus
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
can be described by the associated Loewner’s differential equation
[
67
]
.
Let
\xi
be a (real-valued) continuous function defined on
[0,T)
for some
0<T<r
.
By definition, the Loewner kernel
S(r,z)
on
\mathbb{A}_{r}=\{z:e^{-r}<|z|<1\}
is given by

S(r,z):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\frac{e^{2nr}+z}{e^{2nr}-z}.

For each
z\in\mathbb{A}_{r}
, let
g_{t}(z)
be the solution (which exists up to the first time
\tau_{z}\in(0,T]
that
g_{t}(z)
hits
\xi_{t}
) of the annulus Loewner equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{t}(z)=g_{t}(z)S(r-t,g_{t}(z)e^{-i\xi_{t}}),\qquad g_{0}(z)=z.

For each time
t\in[0,T)
, let
K_{t}:=\{z\in\mathbb{A}_{r}:\tau_{z}\leq t\}
.
Then
K_{t}
is a hull in
\mathbb{A}_{r}
with capacity
t
.
Moreover,
g_{t}
is a conformal map from
(\mathbb{A}_{r}\setminus K_{t},\mathbb{T}_{r})
onto
(\mathbb{A}_{r-t},\mathbb{T}_{r-t})
.

It is convenient to describe the annulus SLE in the covering space
\mathbb{S}_{r}
, where the Loewner kernel
H
is expressed in terms of
\Theta
as

H(r,z):=-i\,S(r,e^{iz})=2\,\partial_{z}\log\Theta(r,z).

For each
z\in\mathbb{S}_{r}
, we denote by
\tilde{g}_{t}(z)
the solution of the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{g}_{t}(z)=H(r-t,\tilde{g}_{t}(z)-\xi_{t}),\qquad\tilde{g}_{0}(z)=z,

and set
\tilde{K}_{t}:=\{z\in\mathbb{S}_{r}:\tau_{z}\leq t\}
.
Then
\tilde{g}_{t}
is a conformal map from
(\mathbb{S}_{r}\setminus\tilde{K}_{t};\mathbb{R}_{r})
onto
(\mathbb{S}_{r-t};\mathbb{R}_{r-t})
.
Moreover for each
z\in\mathbb{S}_{r}\setminus\tilde{K}_{t}
,
e^{i\tilde{g}_{t}(z)}=g_{t}(e^{iz})
, and
K_{t}=\{e^{iz}\in\mathbb{A}_{r}:z\in\tilde{K}_{t}\}
.
In particular,
\tilde{K}_{t}
is
2\pi
-periodic and for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\tilde{g}_{t}(z+2n\pi)=\tilde{g}_{t}(z)+2n\pi
.
We call that
\xi
generates a trace
\gamma
if

\gamma_{t}=\gamma(t):=\lim_{z\to\xi_{t}}\tilde{g}_{t}^{-1}(z)
.
It was proved by Zhan in
[
67
]
that
\xi_{t}:=\sqrt{\kappa}\,B_{t}
(
t<r
) a.s. generates continuous trace, where
B_{t}
is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion.

Suppose
\Lambda
is a real-valued
C^{0,1}
function on
(0,\infty)\times(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}+2ir\mathbb{Z}\})^{N}
. Let
\xi_{t}
be the solution, (which exists up to the first time
T
such that
\xi_{t}-\tilde{g}_{t}(q_{j})\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
,) of the SDE

d\xi_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dB_{t}+\Lambda(r-t,\xi_{t}-\tilde{g}_{t}(q_{1}),\cdots,\xi_{t}-\tilde{g}_{t}(q_{N}))\,dt,\qquad\xi_{0}=p.

Applying the Girsanov theorem, it can be shown that up to time
T
,
\xi_{t}
a.s. generates continuous trace, see
[
72
, Section 3.2]
for further details.
We call the trace generated by
\xi_{t}
the (covering) annulus
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
trace started from
p
with force points
q_{1}.\cdots,q_{N}
.

Now we recall the notion of SLE martingale-observables.
A non-random (conformal) field
f
in a planar domain
D
(or on a Riemann surface in general) is an assignment of smooth function

(f\|\phi):\phi\,U\to\mathbb{C}

for each local chart
\phi
.
By definition, a non-random field
M
is a
martingale-observable
for annulus
\text{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
if for any
z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}
, the process

M_{t}(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}):=(M\|\tilde{g}_{t}^{-1})(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}),\qquad(t<T)

(stopped when any
z_{j}
or any
q_{k}
is swallowed by the Loewner hull
\tilde{K}_{t}
) is a local martingale on the annulus SLE probability space.

2.3.
Statement of main results

For a smooth vector field
v
, let us denote by
\mathcal{L}^{+}_{v}
(resp.,
\mathcal{L}^{-}_{v}
) the
\mathbb{C}
-linear (resp., anti
\mathbb{C}
-linear) part of Lie derivative operator
\mathcal{L}_{v}
with respect to
v
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{v}^{\pm}:=(\mathcal{L}_{v}\mp i\mathcal{L}_{iv})/2
.
We define a
stress tensor

A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv A_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}
in terms of the current fields
J_{(0)}:=\partial\Phi_{(0)}
and
J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\partial\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
by

A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=-\frac{1}{2}J_{(0)}\odot J_{(0)}+\Big(ib\,\partial-\mathbf{E}J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Big)J_{(0)},

where
\odot
denotes the Wick’s product.
The Virasoro field
T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv T_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}
is given by

T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=-\frac{1}{2}J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+ib\,\partial J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

See Subsection
3.7
for more details.

We derive the following form of Ward’s equation.

Theorem A
.

For any string
\mathcal{X}
of fields in the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we have

2\,\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\mathcal{X}=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{\zeta}}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{\zeta}}}^{-}\Big)\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}+\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X},

where all fields are evaluated in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
and the Loewner vector field
v_{\zeta}
is given by

(v_{\zeta}\|\mathrm{id}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{r}})(z):=H(r,\zeta-z).

Example

(Hadamard’s variational formula)
.

As a simplest non-trivial example of Theorem
A
, let us consider the case
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\mathcal{X}=\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})
. Then Ward’s equation is equivalent to the following functional relation of the Green’s function
G
:

(2.1)

4\,\partial_{\zeta}G(\zeta,z_{1})\partial_{\zeta}G(\zeta,z_{2})+\Big(v_{\zeta}(z_{1})\partial_{z_{1}}+v_{\zeta}(z_{2})\partial_{z_{2}}+v_{\zeta}(\bar{z}_{1}){\bar{\partial}}_{z_{1}}+v_{\zeta}(\bar{z}_{2}){\bar{\partial}}_{z_{2}}+\partial_{r}\Big)G(z_{1},z_{2})=0.

Letting
\zeta\to p\in\mathbb{R}
, this equation gives rise to Hadamard’s variational formula for annulus Loewner chains:

\frac{d}{dt}G_{\Omega_{t}}(z_{1},z_{2})\Big|_{t=0}=-P(z_{1})P(z_{2}),\qquad P(z):=\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\Big|_{\zeta=p}G(\zeta,z),

where
\Omega_{t}:=\mathcal{C}_{r}\backslash\tilde{K}_{t}
.
Such a variational formula was studied by Izyurov and Kytölä
[
33
]
.
We also refer to
[
29
,
40
]
for variants of Hadamard’s formula in a planar domain.

We define the one-leg operator
\Psi
in terms of a modified multi-vertex field (OPE exponential) below. For this purpose, it is convenient to introduce the formal (1-point) bosonic fields
\Phi_{(0)}^{\pm}
in
D_{r}
.
Although they are not Fock space fields, they can be interpreted as the “holomorphic part” and the “anti-holomorphic part” of the GFF
\Phi_{(0)}
in the following sense:

\Phi_{(0)}=\Phi^{+}_{(0)}+\Phi^{-}_{(0)}
,
\Phi^{-}_{(0)}=\overline{\Phi^{+}_{(0)}}
.
Given divisors
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}\cdot z_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{*j}\cdot z_{j}
,
we set

\Phi_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\sigma_{j}\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{j})-\sigma_{*j}\Phi^{-}_{(0)}(z_{j})\Big).

Then
\Phi_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]
is a well-defined Fock space field if and only if the following neutrality condition
(\mathrm{NC}_{0})
holds:

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{*j})=0
.

Given a double divisor
(\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}})
for
z_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
satisfying the neutrality condition
(\mathrm{NC}_{0})
, we define the
modified multi-vertex field (OPE exponentials)

\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
by

\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}:=\frac{C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}+\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}{C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}\,e^{\odot i\Phi_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}.

(The reason for the terminology “OPE exponentials” will become clear due to Proposition
3.6
.)
Here the Coulomb gas correlation function
C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
is a differential of conformal dimensions
(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{*j})=(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{j}^{2}-b\,\sigma_{j}\,,\,\tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{*j}^{2}-b\,\sigma_{*j})
at each
z_{j}
and its evaluation in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
is given by

\displaystyle C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}=

\displaystyle\,\Theta^{\prime}(0)^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}(\sigma_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{*j}^{2})}\exp\Big(-\frac{(\sum\sigma_{j}z_{j}+\sigma_{*j}\bar{z}_{j})^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)\prod_{j}\Theta(z_{j}-\bar{z}_{j})^{\sigma_{j}\sigma_{*j}}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod_{j<k}\Theta(z_{j}-z_{k})^{\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}}\Theta(\bar{z}_{j}-z_{k})^{\sigma_{*j}\sigma_{k}}\Theta(z_{j}-\bar{z}_{k})^{\sigma_{j}\sigma_{*k}}\Theta(\bar{z}_{j}-\bar{z}_{k})^{\sigma_{*j}\sigma_{*k}}.

Given a divisor
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\sum\tau_{j}\cdot\xi_{j}
satisfying
a+\sum\tau_{j}=0
, we define the
one-leg operator

\Psi
by

\Psi(z)\big(\equiv\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})\equiv\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot z+\boldsymbol{\tau}]\,\big):=\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot z+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,,\,\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\tau}].

Then the following form of BPZ equation holds.

Theorem B
.

Suppose that
2a(a+b)=1
. Then for any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we have

(2.2)

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{z}\mathbf{E}\Psi(z)\mathcal{X}=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{z}}}^{-}+\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)\Big)\mathbf{E}\Psi(z)\mathcal{X},

where all fields are evaluated in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
Here, Lie derivative operators do not apply to
z
and
h_{1,2}=a^{2}/2-ab
.

To connect CFT with SLE theory, we choose real parameters
a
and
b
in terms of SLE parameter
\kappa
as

a=\sqrt{2/\kappa},\qquad b=\sqrt{\kappa/8}-\sqrt{2/\kappa}.

Now we consider
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
, where
\Lambda
is defined by

\Lambda(r,p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\kappa\frac{\partial_{\xi}|_{\xi=p}\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})},\qquad Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

The insertion of the one-leg operator produces an operator that changes the values of Fock space fields.
See
[
35
, Section 2.3]
for its probabilistic counterpart: insertion of Wick’s exponential
e^{\odot\Phi(f)}
of the GFF in application to a test function
f
results in the change of the law of
\Phi(f)
in terms of the Green potential.
We now consider the following insertion procedure:

\widehat{\mathbf{E}}[\mathcal{X}]:=\frac{\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})}.

By the BPZ-Cardy type equations (Proposition
5.7
), we construct a family of SLE martingale-observables.

Theorem C
.

For any string
\mathcal{X}
of fields in the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, a non-random field

M=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}[\mathcal{X}]
is a martingale-observable for
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
.

Example
.

(
Bosonic observables
)
Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
and
M(z):=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}\Phi(z)
. Then
M
is evaluated as

(2.3)

M(z)=-2b\arg w^{\prime}+2a\arg\Theta_{\chi}(r,w_{z})+\sum\tau_{k}\arg\Big\{\Theta_{\chi}(r,w_{z}-w_{k})\Theta_{\chi}(w_{z}-\bar{w}_{k})\Big\}.

In particular when
\chi=0
, the harmonic function
M
satisfies piecewise Dirichlet boundary conditions having additional jump of
2\pi a
at
p
and
2\pi\tau_{k}
at
\xi_{k}
’s.

For
b=0
(thus when
\kappa=4
) the associated bounded martingale plays an important role in the study of scaling limits of discrete GFF interface
[
60
]
and GFF/SLE couplings
[
33
,
19
]
.
Further applications of such observables are presented in Propositions
5.8
and
7.4
in the context of left passage probability and hitting probability of SLE traces.

Example
.

(
Vertex observables
) Let
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\tau}}=\sum\tilde{\tau}_{j}\cdot\tilde{\xi}_{j}
be a divisor satisfying
a+\int\boldsymbol{\tilde{\tau}}=0
.
Consider the vertex observable
M
given by

M=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\boldsymbol{\tau}]=\frac{\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tilde{\tau}}]}{\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tau}]}.

This martingale-observable provides the Radon-Nikodym derivative between the law of two annulus SLEs associated with partition functions
\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tilde{\tau}}]
and
\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tau}]
.
Similar martingale-observables also appear in the study of restriction properties, see e.g.,
[
43
,
16
]
.

We emphasize that except for the ER case when
\chi=\infty
, the drift function
\Lambda
is not
2\pi
-periodic with respect to space variables.
To construct martingale-observables for annulus SLE with
2\pi
-periodic drift function, we use the weighted summation of the chiral fields, see
[
64
]
for a similar idea on compact Riemann surfaces. (cf. see also
[
72
]
for implementation of this idea to the annulus SLE partition functions.)
To be more precise, for a weight function
\omega:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, set

\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\omega(n)\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p+2n\pi,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Here we assume that
\omega
is suitably chosen so that the summation above converges within correlations.
For instance, when
\chi<\infty
, we allow any weight function
\omega
of polynomial type.
We then define

Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}),\qquad\Lambda^{\omega}(r,p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\kappa\,\partial_{p}\log Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Extending Theorem
C
, we obtain the following.

Theorem D
.

For any string
\mathcal{X}
of fields in the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, a non-random field

(2.4)

M=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}[\mathcal{X}]:=\frac{\mathbf{E}\Psi^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})}

is a martingale-observable for
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda^{\omega})
.

Example
.

Let us consider the case that
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}=-a\cdot q
for
q\in\mathbb{R}_{r}
and
\omega(n)\equiv 1
.
Then up to a multiplicative constant, the partition functions
Z\equiv Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
Z^{\omega}\equiv Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})
are given by (
x=p-\mathrm{Re}\,q
)

Z(x)=\Theta_{I}(r,x)^{\frac{2}{\kappa}}\exp\Big(\frac{x^{2}}{2\kappa(r+\chi)}\Big),\qquad Z^{\omega}(x)=\Theta_{I}(r,x)^{\frac{2}{\kappa}}\Theta_{I}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(r+\chi),x+\pi\Big),

where

\Theta_{I}(r,z)=i\,\Theta(r,z-ir)\exp\Big(-\frac{r}{4}-\frac{iz}{2}\Big).

This leads to

\Lambda(x)=H_{I}(r,x)+\frac{x}{r+\chi},\qquad\Lambda^{\omega}(x)=H_{I}(r,x)+\frac{\kappa}{2}H_{I}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(r+\chi),x+\pi\Big),

where
H_{I}(r,z):=2\,\partial_{z}\log\Theta_{I}(r,z)
.
Notice here that
\Lambda^{\omega}
is
2\pi
-periodic, whereas
\Lambda
is not.

In particular when
\chi=0
, it can be shown that the associated SLE
(\kappa,\Lambda^{\omega})
trace ends at
\{q+2n\pi:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
Then by virtue of vertex observables in the previous example, one can see that for each
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,

M_{m}(x):=\frac{Z(x-2m\pi)}{Z^{\omega}(x)}=\exp\Big(\frac{(x-2m\pi)^{2}}{2\kappa\,r}\Big)\Theta_{I}\Big(\frac{\kappa\,r}{2},x+\pi\Big)^{-1}

gives rise to the probability that SLE
(\kappa,\Lambda^{\omega})
trace ends at
q+2m\pi
.

We now focus on the
chordal type
annulus
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda)
with one force point
q
starting from
p
, where both of the marked points lie on the same boundary component.
It was shown in
[
72
]
that in order for the SLE process to be reversible, the associated partition function

should satisfy the null-vector equation

(2.5)

\partial_{r}Z=\frac{\kappa}{2}Z^{\prime\prime}+H\,Z^{\prime}+h_{1,2}(\kappa)H^{\prime}\,Z+C(r)Z,\qquad h_{1,2}(\kappa):=\frac{6-\kappa}{2\kappa},

where
C(r)
is a constant depending only on the modular parameter
r
.

To construct CFT for such chordal type annulus SLE, we use the method of
screening
.
Due to the local commutations of annulus SLE
(\kappa,\Lambda)
, the partition function should have the same conformal dimensions at
p
and
q
.
In the case of chordal SLE in the upper-half plane, it can be achieved by the
effective one-leg operator
with the total charge
2b-a
(including the background charge
2b
) placed at
q
and with the charge
a
placed at
p
.
For more details we refer the reader to
[
34
]
.
However, this choice of partition function cannot be realized in the annulus because the neutrality conditions depend on the connectivity.
See
[
36
]
for the neutrality conditions for background charges on a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
.
Instead we consider

\Psi_{\beta}(p,q):=C(\kappa)\oint_{\mathcal{P}(p,q)}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+a\cdot q+s\cdot\zeta,\,\boldsymbol{0}]\,d\zeta,\qquad(s=-2a),

where
\mathcal{P}(p,q)
is the Pochhammer contour entwining
p
and
q
, see Figure
2
.
Here
C(\kappa)
is a universal constant depending only on
\kappa
and the background charge is given as
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=\beta\cdot q_{1}-\beta\cdot q_{2}
(
q_{2}-q_{1}=2\pi
) for some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Figure 2
.
Pochhammer contour

With this choice of
s=-2a
, the modified multi-vertex field
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+a\cdot z+s\cdot\zeta,\,\boldsymbol{0}]
is a
1
-differential with respect to the “screening” variable

and it satisfies the neutrality condition
(\mathrm{NC}_{0})
.
Clearly,
\Psi_{\beta}
has the same conformal dimensions both at
p
and
q
.
For each
\kappa>0
, we have the following expression (up to a multiplicative constant) of

in terms of Jacobi’s theta function:

(2.6)

Z_{\beta}=\Theta(p-q)^{\frac{2}{\kappa}}\oint_{\mathcal{P}(p,q)}\Theta(p-\zeta)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\Theta(\zeta-q)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(\Sigma-2\beta\pi)^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)\,d\zeta,

where
\Sigma=a\,(p+q-2\zeta)
.
We remark that for
\kappa>4
, this Pochhammer contour integral simplifies to the integration over the interval between
p
and
q
.
It can be shown by the contour deformation method as we explain in Subsection

.
With this choice of the one-leg operator
\Psi_{\beta}(p,q)
, we construct a family of martingale-observables for chordal type
\mathrm{SLE}(\kappa,\Lambda_{\beta})
, where
\Lambda_{\beta}=\kappa\,\partial_{p}\log Z_{\beta}
.

Theorem E
.

For each
\kappa>0
, the partition function
Z_{\beta}:=\mathbf{E}\,\Psi_{\beta}(\cdot,q)
is a non-trivial (real-valued) solution of the null-vector equation (
2.5
).
Moreover, for any string
\mathcal{X}
of fields in the OPE family

Organization of the paper and further results

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.

In Section

we review some standard notions in CFT and describe the conformal Fock space fields generated by central charge modifications of GFF.
For the convenience of the reader we borrow the relevant material from
[
35
]
without proofs, thus making our exposition self-contained.

Section

In Section

.

In Section

and

.
Moreover, the implementation of non-atomic background charge is explained in Subsection

In Section

In Appendix

3.
Conformal Fock space fields

We introduce a class of random fields in a doubly connected domain as correlation functional valued maps.
All fields that we consider in this paper are constructed from the (modifications of) GFF and their derivatives by means of Wick’s calculus and named after Fock space.
We consider Dirichlet and ER boundary conditions for the GFF and their interpolations, see Subsection
3.2
for more details.
This subsection also recalls some basic concepts and properties concerning the Fock space fields and OPE products.
In Subsection
3.3
we revise the definition of Fock space fields so that their values depend on local coordinates.
In Subsection
3.4
we introduce the central charge modifications of GFF and present the associated Virasoro field.
Based on the Coulomb gas formalism introduced in the following subsections, in Subsection
3.7
, we present a more general theory of background charge modifications of GFF.

3.1.
Special functions

In this subsection we compile basic properties of some special functions used to represent the Loewner vector field and the correlation functions of Fock space fields.

For each
r>0
, the Loewner kernel
H(r,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{S}_{r}
is given by

H(r,z):=-i\,\textrm{P.V.}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{n\in 2\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e^{nr}+e^{iz}}{e^{nr}-e^{iz}}.

We also write

H_{I}(r,z):=H(r,z+ir)+i.

We refer to
[
72
]
for fundamental properties of the Loewner kernel
H(r,\cdot)
.
For reader’s convenience, we list some of them as follows:

•

a meromorphic function
H(r,\cdot)
on
\mathbb{C}
has simple poles at
2\pi m+2irn,(m,n\in\mathbb{Z})
with residue 2;

•

H(r,z+2\pi)=H(r,z)
and
H(r,z+2ir)=H(r,z)-2i
;

•

H(r,\cdot)
is an odd function;

•

H(r,\cdot)
takes real values on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{2\pi n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and
\mathrm{Im}\,H(r,\cdot)\equiv-1
on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
;

•

H(r,\pi)=0
,
H(r,ir)=-i
, and
H(r,\pi+ir)=-i
.

A holomorphic function

\Theta(r,z):=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e^{-r(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}e^{(n+\frac{1}{2})iz}

vanishes only on
2\pi\mathbb{Z}+2ir\mathbb{Z}
and

\Theta_{I}(r,z):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e^{-rn^{2}}e^{niz}

is related to
\Theta
as

(3.1)

\Theta_{I}(r,z)=i\,\Theta(r,z-ir)\exp\Big(-\frac{r}{4}-\frac{iz}{2}\Big).

Notice that
\Theta(r,\cdot)
is odd, whereas
\Theta_{I}(r,\cdot)
is even.
The functions
H
and
H_{I}
are represented in terms of
\Theta
and
\Theta_{I}
as

(3.2)

H(r,z)=2\frac{\Theta^{\prime}(r,z)}{\Theta(r,z)},\qquad H_{I}(r,z)=2\frac{\Theta^{\prime}_{I}(r,z)}{\Theta_{I}(r,z)},

where derivatives are taken with respect to the
z
-variable.
We remark that
\Theta
and
\Theta_{I}
are related to the classical Jacobi theta functions

\theta(z,\tau)=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e^{\pi i\tau(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}e^{(2n+1)\pi iz},\qquad\theta_{2}(z,\tau)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}e^{\pi i\tau n^{2}}e^{2n\pi iz}

as

\Theta(r,z)=\theta\Big(\frac{z}{2\pi},\frac{ir}{\pi}\Big),\qquad\Theta_{I}(r,z)=\theta_{2}\Big(\frac{z}{2\pi},\frac{ir}{\pi}\Big).

It is well known that
\Theta
and
\Theta_{I}
solve the heat equations

(3.3)

\partial_{r}\Theta=\Theta^{\prime\prime},\qquad\partial_{r}\Theta_{I}=\Theta_{I}^{\prime\prime}.

Moreover, they are represented in terms of fundamental solutions of the heat equation as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Theta(r,z)&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{r}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}\exp\Big(-\frac{(z-\pi+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big),\\
\Theta_{I}(r,z)&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{r}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(z-\pi+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big),\end{split}

see e.g.,
[
53
, Eq.(20.2.13),(20.13.4)]
.

To describe correlation functions with weighted boundary conditions indexed by
\chi\in[0,\infty]
, it is convenient to introduce the following theta function

(3.5)

\Theta_{\chi}(r,z):=\Theta(r,z)\exp\Big(\frac{z^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big).

In the sequel, we also write

(3.6)

H_{\chi}(r,z):=2\log\Theta^{\prime}_{\chi}(r,z)=H(r,z)+\frac{z}{r+\chi}.

In particular, we denote
\tilde{\Theta}:=\Theta_{0}
and
\tilde{H}:=H_{0}
.
It is worth pointing out that
\tilde{\Theta}(r,z)\in i\mathbb{R}_{+}
if
z\in\mathbb{R}_{r}
.
Note also that the function
H_{\chi}(r,\cdot)
is real-valued on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
if and only if
\chi=0
.
Throughout this paper, we will frequently use the following (quasi) periodicities of theta functions

(3.7)

\displaystyle\Theta(r,z+2\pi)=-\Theta(r,z),\qquad\Theta(r,z+2ir)=-\Theta(r,z)\exp(r-iz),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Theta_{I}(r,z+2\pi)=\Theta_{I}(r,z),\qquad\Theta_{I}(r,z+2ir)=-\Theta_{I}(r,z)\exp(r-iz),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Theta}(r,z+2\pi)=-\tilde{\Theta}(r,z)\exp(\pi(z+\pi)/r),\qquad\tilde{\Theta}(r,z+2ir)=-\tilde{\Theta}(r,z).

The Weierstrass zeta function
\zeta_{r}
with basic periods
(2\pi,2ir)

(3.10)

\zeta_{r}(z):=\frac{1}{z}+{\sum_{\eta}}^{*}\Big(\frac{1}{z-\eta}+\frac{1}{\eta}+\frac{z}{\eta^{2}}\Big)

is holomorphic except for the points
z\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}+2ir\mathbb{Z}
, where it has simple poles with residue 1.
Here, the sum
\sum_{\eta}^{*}
in (
3.10
) is taken over all
\eta
of the form
2\pi m+2irn
for
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
.
It is well known that
\zeta_{r}(\pi)/\pi
is represented as

(3.11)

\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}=-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\Theta^{\prime\prime\prime}(r,0)}{\Theta^{\prime}(r,0)}=-\frac{1}{3}\partial_{r}\log\Theta^{\prime}(r,0)=\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sinh^{-2}(kr).

This value will appear in the formula of the correlation function of the Virasoro field (Proposition
3.2
).

The Loewner kernel
H
is represented in terms of the Weierstrass zeta function:

(3.12)

H(r,z)=2\,\zeta_{r}(z)-\frac{2}{\pi}\zeta_{r}(\pi)\,z.

In particular, we have the following asymptotic behavior of
H
:

(3.13)

H(r,z)=\frac{2}{z}-\frac{2\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}z+O(z^{3}),\qquad\textrm{as }z\to 0.

The following pseudo-addition formula (see
[
8
, p.57]
) for zeta function is a crucial ingredient to derive a version of Eguchi-Ooguri equations (Proposition
4.2
).

Proposition 3.1
.

If
x+y+z=0
, then

(3.14)

(\zeta_{r}(x)+\zeta_{r}(y)+\zeta_{r}(z))^{2}+\zeta_{r}^{\prime}(x)+\zeta_{r}^{\prime}(y)+\zeta_{r}^{\prime}(z)=0.

By (
3.2
) and (
3.12
), one can rewrite (
3.14
) as follows:

(3.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(z-w)(H(z)-H(w))&=\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(z-w)+\frac{1}{2}(H(z)-H(w))^{2}\\
&+H^{\prime}(z-w)+H^{\prime}(z)+H^{\prime}(w)+\frac{6}{\pi}\zeta_{r}(\pi).\end{split}

3.2.
Correlation functions of Fock space fields

In this subsection we introduce a one-parameter family of GFFs with a weighted combination of Dirichlet and ER boundary conditions in a doubly connected domain and recall some basic concepts in CFT such as Fock space fields and OPE products.
As an expansion of the tensor product of two Fock space fields near diagonal, operator product expansion (OPE) gives rise to important binary operations, namely OPE multiplications or OPE products on Fock space fields.

3.2.1.
One-parameter family of Gaussian free fields

An excursion reflected Brownian motion (ERBM)
B_{D_{r}}^{ER}
in a doubly connected domain
D_{r}
is a strong Markov process stopped at the outer boundary of
D_{r}
that acts like a Brownian motion when it is away from
\partial D_{r}
.
Also it reflects on the inner boundary of
D_{r}
at a random point chosen according to the harmonic measure from
\infty
whenever it hits the inner boundary.
It is well known that ERBM is conformally invariant and so is the Green’s function for ERBM.
See
[
45
,
14
]
for more details.
We also remark that ERBM is a special case of a more general process called Brownian motion with darning
[
10
]
, which can be constructed using Dirichlet form theory.
See also
[
6
]
for another generalization of ERBM, called obliquely reflected Brownian motion.

The Green’s function
G_{D_{r}}^{Diri}
in a doubly connected domain
D_{r}
with zero Dirichlet boundary condition (b.c.) vanishes on
\partial D_{r}
.
In the identity chart of the annulus
\mathbb{A}_{r}
we have

(3.16)

G_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}^{Diri}(\zeta,z)=-\frac{\log|\zeta|\log|z|}{r}-\log\Big|z\frac{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e^{-(k^{2}-k)r}(\zeta/z)^{k}}{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e^{-(k^{2}-k)r}(\zeta\bar{z})^{k}}\Big|,

see e.g.,
[
30
, p.262]
.
The Green’s function
G_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}^{ER}
for ERBM in the annulus
\mathbb{A}_{r}
is characterized by

\mathbb{E}^{\zeta}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{E}(B_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}^{ER})(t)\,dt\Big]=\int_{E}G_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}^{ER}(\zeta,z)\,dz

for any Borel subset
E\subset\mathbb{A}_{r}
, where
\tau_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}:=\inf\{t:B_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}^{ER}(t)\in\mathbb{T}\}
.
Then by
[
14
, Proposition 5.2 and Lemma 5.5]
, we have

G_{\mathbb{A}_{r}}^{ER}(\zeta,z)=-\log\Big|z\,\frac{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e^{-(k^{2}-k)r}(\zeta/z)^{k}}{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}e^{-(k^{2}-k)r}(\zeta\bar{z})^{k}}\Big|.

In the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, such Green’s functions
G_{r}\equiv G_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
are represented in terms of Jacobi theta function as

(3.17)

G_{r}(\zeta,z)=\begin{cases}\log\Big|\dfrac{\Theta(r,\zeta-\bar{z})}{\Theta(r,\zeta-z)}\Big|&\text{for ER b.c.}\\
\displaystyle\log\Big|\dfrac{\Theta(r,\zeta-\bar{z})}{\Theta(r,\zeta-z)}\Big|-\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta\,\mathrm{Im}\,z}{r}&\text{for Dirichlet b.c.}\end{cases}

From the analytic point of view, the Green’s function
G_{r}
with ER b.c. satisfies the
zero period condition
on the inner boundary component
\gamma
. Therefore we have that for
z\in\gamma
,

(3.18)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\oint_{\gamma}\frac{\partial G_{r}}{\partial n_{z}}\,ds_{z}=0&\text{for ER b.c.}\\
\hskip 11.99998ptG_{r}(\zeta,z)=0&\text{for Dirichlet b.c.}\end{cases}

Here
\partial n_{z}
denotes the normal derivative and
ds_{z}
is the arc length measure.
Extending (
3.18
), we introduce the weighted combination of Dirichlet and ER boundary conditions: for
\chi\in[0,\infty]

(3.19)

\frac{1}{1+\chi}G_{r}(\zeta,z)+\frac{\chi}{1+\chi}\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\frac{\partial G_{r}}{\partial n_{z}}\,ds_{z}\Big)=0,

where
z\in\gamma
.
One may realize (
3.19
) as an analog of the well-known Robin boundary condition, in which the Neumann condition is replaced by the ER one.
It is then straightforward to see that the Green’s function

(3.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{r}^{\chi}(\zeta,z):=\log\Big|\dfrac{\Theta_{\chi}(r,\zeta-\bar{z})}{\Theta_{\chi}(r,\zeta-z)}\Big|=\log\Big|\dfrac{\Theta(r,\zeta-\bar{z})}{\Theta(r,\zeta-z)}\Big|-\frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta\,\mathrm{Im}\,z}{r+\chi}\end{split}

satisfies the boundary condition (
3.19
).

We denote by
\Phi
the GFF with weighted boundary condition (
3.19
), see Figure
1
. By definition, the
2
-point correlation function of GFF
\Phi
in
D_{r}
is given by

(3.21)

\mathbf{E}[\Phi(\zeta)\Phi(z)]:=2\,G^{\chi}_{D_{r}}(\zeta,z)=2\log\Big|\frac{\Theta_{\chi}(r,w(\zeta)-\overline{w(z)})}{\Theta_{\chi}(r,w(\zeta)-w(z))}\Big|,

where
w
is a conformal map from
D_{r}
onto
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. In general, the
n
-point correlation function of
\Phi
is given by

(3.22)

\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{n})]=\sum\prod_{k}2G^{\chi}_{D_{r}}(z_{i_{k}},z_{j_{k}}),

where the sum is taken over all partitions of the set
\{1,\cdots,n\}
into disjoint pairs
\{i_{k},j_{k}\}
.
The
current field

J=\partial\Phi
, its conjugate
\bar{J}={\bar{\partial}}\Phi
, and higher order derivatives of
\Phi
are distributional fields (random generalized functions) and their correlators are computed by differentiating those of GFF.
For example, for
\zeta\neq z
, in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, we have

(3.23)

\mathbf{E}[J(\zeta)\Phi(z)]=\dfrac{1}{2}(H_{\chi}(r,\zeta-\bar{z})-H_{\chi}(r,\zeta-z)),\qquad\mathbf{E}[J(\zeta)J(z)]=\frac{1}{2}\,H^{\prime}_{\chi}(r,\zeta-z).

3.2.2.
Fock space fields

We treat the GFF as a Gaussian (correlation) functional-valued function and view its correlation function as the kernel representing the expectation of the value of GFF on test functions.
We also treat the derivatives of GFF as centered (mean zero) Gaussian generalized functions and include Wick’s product of derivatives of GFF in a collection of fields we consider.

By definition, a basic Fock space correlation functional is a (formal form of) Wick’s product
\mathcal{X}=X_{1}(z_{1})\odot\cdots\odot X_{n}(z_{n})
of derivatives
X_{j}
of the GFF, and it has zero correlation, i.e.,

.
Here, points

of

3.2.3.
OPE calculations

The
operator product expansion
(OPE) of two Fock space fields
X
and
Y
is an asymptotic expansion of
X(\zeta)Y(z)
near diagonal.
In particular, if a field
X
is holomorphic (i.e.,
{\bar{\partial}}X=0
within correlation), the OPE is defined as a formal Laurent series expansion

X(\zeta)Y(z)=\sum C_{n}(z)(\zeta-z)^{n},\qquad\text{as }\zeta\to z.

More precisely, it means that
\mathbf{E}X(\zeta)Y(z)\mathcal{X}=\mathbf{E}\sum C_{n}(z)(\zeta-z)^{n}\mathcal{X}
for each Fock space correlation functional
\mathcal{X}
for
\zeta,z\notin S_{\mathcal{X}}
.
In this case,
*_{n}
product is defined as
X*_{n}Y:=C_{n}
, the
n
-th OPE coefficient.
In particular, we write
*
for
*_{0}
and call
X*Y
the
OPE multiplication
, or the
OPE product
of
X
and
Y
.
We remark that the
\ast_{n}
product is neither associative nor commutative. On the other hand, it satisfies Leibniz’s rule,
\partial(X*_{n}Y)=\partial X*_{n}Y+X*_{n}\partial Y
.

We now present some examples of OPEs. As
\zeta\to z,(\zeta\neq z)
,

(3.25)

\Phi(\zeta)\Phi(z)=\log\frac{1}{|\zeta-z|^{2}}+2c(z)+\Phi^{\odot 2}(z)+o(1),

where
c(z):=u(z,z)
and
u(\zeta,z):=G_{D_{r}}^{\chi}(\zeta,z)+\log|\zeta-z|
.
By definition, for
z\in D_{r}
,
\Phi*\Phi(z)=2c(z)+\Phi^{\odot 2}(z)
, where
c(z)
is evaluated as

(3.26)

c(z)=\log\Big|\frac{\Theta_{\chi}(w(z)-\overline{w(z)})}{w^{\prime}(z)\Theta^{\prime}_{\chi}(0)}\Big|.

We remark that the function
c(z)
is called the
conformal radius
for the ER b.c., whereas it is called the
domain constant
for the Dirichlet b.c., see e.g.,
[
4
, Section 4]
.

Differentiating (
3.25
), we have

(3.27)

\displaystyle J(\zeta)\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{\zeta-z}+(J\odot\Phi)(z)+\dfrac{w^{\prime}(z)}{2}H_{\chi}(w(z)-\overline{w(z)})-\frac{w^{\prime\prime}(z)}{2w^{\prime}(z)}+o(1),

(3.28)

\displaystyle J(\zeta)J(z)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+(J\odot J)(z)-\frac{1}{6}S_{w}(z)-w^{\prime}(z)^{2}\Big(\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}-\frac{1}{2(r+\chi)}\Big)+o(1).

Here,
S_{h}
is the Schwarzian derivative
S_{h}
of a conformal map
h
, i.e.,

S_{h}:=N^{\prime}_{h}-\frac{1}{2}N_{h}^{2},\qquad N_{h}=\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{h^{\prime}}.

We sometimes use the notation
\sim
for the singular part of the OPE. For instance, we have

(3.29)

J(\zeta)\Phi(z)\sim-\frac{1}{\zeta-z},\qquad J(\zeta)J(z)\sim-\frac{1}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}.

3.3.
Conformal Fock space fields

We treat a stress tensor as a Lie derivative operator to state Ward’s identities.
For this purpose, it is needed to consider Fock space fields in a planar domain (e.g., a doubly connected domain) as defined on a Riemann surface.
The correlation functions of conformal Fock space fields depend on the choice of local charts at their nodes.

By definition, a non-random conformal field
f
on a Riemann surface is a smooth function
(f\|\phi):\phi U\to\mathbb{C}
for each local chart
\phi
.
We define a general conformal Fock space field as
X=\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}
, where
f_{\alpha}
’s are non-random conformal fields and basic fields
X_{\alpha}
are Wick’s products of derivatives of GFF.

We now list some basic non-random fields
f
with specific transformation laws between
f=(f\|\phi)
and
\tilde{f}=(f\|\tilde{\phi})
for any two overlapping charts
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
.
A non-random field
f
is called a
differential
of conformal dimension
(\lambda,\lambda_{*})
if

f=(h^{\prime})^{\lambda}(\overline{h^{\prime}})^{\lambda_{*}}\tilde{f}\circ h,

where
h
is the transition map between
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
, i.e.,

h=\tilde{\phi}\circ\phi^{-1}:\phi(U\cap\tilde{U})\to\tilde{\phi}(U\cap\tilde{U})
.
By definition, pre-pre-Schwarzian forms (PPS-forms), pre-Schwarzian forms (PS-forms) and Schwazian forms (S-forms) of order
\mu
are conformal Fock space fields with transformation laws

f=\tilde{f}\circ h+\mu\log h^{\prime},\qquad f=h^{\prime}\tilde{f}\circ h+\mu N_{h},\qquad f=(h^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{f}\circ h+\mu S_{h}

respectively.
In general, PPS-forms of order
(\mu,\nu)
satisfy

f=\tilde{f}\circ h+\mu\log h^{\prime}+\nu\log\overline{h^{\prime}}.

3.3.1.
Lie derivative of conformal Fock space field

Suppose a non-random smooth vector field
v
is holomorphic in some open set
U\subset M
.
For a conformal Fock space field
X
, the Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{v}X
in
U
is defined as

(\mathcal{L}_{v}X\|\phi)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}(X\|\phi\circ\psi_{-t}),

where
\psi_{t}
is a local flow of
v
, and
\phi
is a given chart.
For example, we have the followings:

•

\mathcal{L}_{v}X=(v\partial+\bar{v}{\bar{\partial}}+\lambda v^{\prime}+\lambda_{*}\overline{v^{\prime}})X
for a
(\lambda,\lambda_{*})
-differential
X
;

•

\mathcal{L}_{v}X=(v\partial+v^{\prime})X+\mu v^{\prime\prime}
for a PS-form
X
of order
\mu
;

•

\mathcal{L}_{v}X=(v\partial+2v^{\prime})X+\mu v^{\prime\prime\prime}
for an S-form
X
of order
\mu
,

see
[
35
, Proposition 4.1]
.
For reader’s convenience, we list some basic properties of the Lie derivatives:

•

\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{L}_{v}X]=\mathcal{L}_{v}\mathbf{E}[X]
;

•

\mathcal{L}_{v}(\bar{X})=\overline{(\mathcal{L}_{v}X)}
;

•

\mathcal{L}_{v}(\partial X)=\partial(\mathcal{L}_{v}X)
;

•

Leibniz’s rule holds for tensor, Wick’s, and OPE products.

We define the
\mathbb{C}
-linear part
\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}
and the anti
\mathbb{C}
-linear part
\mathcal{L}_{v}^{-}
of
\mathcal{L}_{v}
by

\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}:=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{v}-i\mathcal{L}_{iv}}{2},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{v}^{-}:=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{v}+i\mathcal{L}_{iv}}{2}

so that
\mathcal{L}_{v}
is decomposed as
\mathcal{L}_{v}=\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v}^{-}
.
Note that while
\mathcal{L}_{v}
depends
\mathbb{R}
-linearly on
v
,
\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}
(resp.,
\mathcal{L}_{v}^{-}
) depends (resp., anti-)
\mathbb{C}
-linearly on
v
.

3.3.2.
Stress energy tensor

Here we recall the definition of a
stress tensor
and review its basic properties. See
[
35
, Sections 5.2 5.3, Lecture 7, and Appendix 11]
for more details.

A stress tensor for a family of conformal Fock space fields is defined as a pair of holomorphic and anti-holomorphic quadratic differentials which represent the Lie derivative operators within correlations of fields in this family.
In the physics literature, special forms of formulas are known as (conformal) Ward’s identities.
The existence of a stress tensor is a remarkable property of certain families of conformal Fock space fields.
For instance, the OPE family of GFFs has a stress tensor in common.

A Fock space field
X
in
D_{r}
is said to have a stress tensor
(A,\bar{A})
(or
X\in\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A}))
if
A
is a holomorphic quadratic differential and the residue form of Ward’s identity

(3.30)

\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}X(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(z)}vAX(z),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{v}^{-}X(z)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(z)}\bar{v}\bar{A}X(z)

holds on
D_{\textrm{hol}}(v)\cap U
for all smooth vector field
v
, where
D_{\textrm{hol}}(v)
is the the maximal open set where
v
is holomorphic.
By
[
35
, Proposition 5.8]
,
\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
is closed under
\ast_{n}
product, in particular, under differentiations.
If
X
is a differential or a form, the residue form of Ward’s identity holds for
X
if and only if Ward’s OPE for
X
holds in some or every chart.
For example, a
(\lambda,\lambda_{*})
-differential
X
is in
\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
if and only if the following singular OPEs hold in every/some chart:

(3.31)

A(\zeta)X(z)\sim\frac{\lambda X(z)}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\frac{\partial X(z)}{\zeta-z};\qquad A(\zeta)\bar{X}(z)\sim\frac{\overline{\lambda}_{*}\bar{X}(z)}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\frac{\partial\bar{X}(z)}{\zeta-z}.

For any form
X
of order
\mu
belongs to
\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
if and only if the following singular OPE holds in every/some chart:

(3.32)

\displaystyle A(\zeta)X(z)

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{\mu}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial X(z)}{\zeta-z}

\displaystyle\textrm{for a PPS-form }X;

(3.33)

\displaystyle A(\zeta)X(z)

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{2\mu}{(\zeta-z)^{3}}+\dfrac{X(z)}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial X(z)}{\zeta-z}

\displaystyle\textrm{for a PS-form }X;

(3.34)

\displaystyle A(\zeta)X(z)

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{6\mu}{(\zeta-z)^{4}}+\dfrac{2X(z)}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial X(z)}{\zeta-z}

\displaystyle\textrm{for an S-form }X.

3.4.
Central charge modification

In this subsection we introduce the central charge modification
\Phi_{(b)}
of GFF, where
b
is the real parameter related to the central charge
c
as
c=1-12b^{2}
.
In the chordal/radial CFT in a simply connected domain with a marked boundary/interior point
q
, two neutrality conditions are imposed in the construction of OPE exponentials.
They have Wick’s part and the correlation part; Wick’s part is Wick’s exponential of the linear combination of bosonic fields, and the correlation part can be expressed in terms of Coulomb gas correlation functions.
We require that this linear combination of bosonic fields should be a well-defined Fock space field.
This requirement is called a neutrality condition on the coefficients or charges of linear combination.
At the same time, conformal invariance of Coulomb gas correlation functions in a simply connected domain gives rise to the other neutrality condition.
Because the neutrality conditions are different, we need to place the background charge at
q
to reconcile these two neutrality conditions.
In a doubly connected domain (or on a compact Riemann surface of genus one), no background charge is placed for
\Phi_{(b)}
. It is consistent with the neutrality condition (
\mathrm{NC}_{0}
) that the sum of background charges should vanish due to a version of Gauss-Bonnet theorem. In Subsection
3.7
we introduce the background charge modification
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
of GFF for a background charge
\boldsymbol{\beta}
with
\mathrm{NC}_{0}
.

We express a stress tensor and the Virasoro field of
\Phi_{(b)}
in terms of the current field
J_{(b)}:=\partial\Phi_{(b)}
.
From now on, the subscript
(0)
is added to the notation of fields in the OPE family of the GFF, the algebra over
\mathbb{C}
spanned by
1
and the derivatives of GFF under OPE multiplication.
For instance,
\Phi_{(0)}
is the new notation for the GFF and
J_{(0)}=\partial\Phi_{(0)}
.
First, we recall the definition of the Virasoro field.

Definition
.

A Fock space field
T
is said to be
Virasoro field
for
\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
if

•

T\in\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
, and

•

T-A
is a non-random meromorphic Schwarzian form.

The order of Schwarzian form
T-A
is denoted by
\frac{1}{12}c
, where
c
is called the
central charge
.

For each real parameter
b
, we define

(3.35)

\Phi\equiv\Phi_{(b)}:=\Phi_{(0)}-2b\arg w^{\prime},\qquad J\equiv J_{(b)}:=J_{(0)}+ib\dfrac{w^{\prime\prime}}{w^{\prime}},

where
w
is a conformal map from
D_{r}
onto
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The field
\Phi_{(b)}
has a stress tensor

A_{(b)}:=-\frac{1}{2}J_{(0)}\odot J_{(0)}+(ib\partial-\jmath_{(b)})J_{(0)},\qquad\jmath_{(b)}:=\mathbf{E}[J_{(b)}]=ib\dfrac{w^{\prime\prime}}{w^{\prime}},

and its Virasoro field is given by

\displaystyle T_{(b)}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}J_{(b)}\ast J_{(b)}+ib\partial J_{(b)}=A_{(b)}+\dfrac{c}{12}S_{w}+(w^{\prime})^{2}\Big(\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}\Big),

where
c=1-12b^{2}
.

Proof.

First, we prove the case
b=0
.
It is obvious that
A_{(0)}:=-\frac{1}{2}J_{(0)}\odot J_{(0)}
is holomorphic quadratic differential.
We claim that
W=(A_{(0)},\bar{A}_{(0)})
is a stress tensor for
\Phi_{(0)}
.
Since
\Phi_{(0)}
is a scalar field or a
(0,0)
-differential, by (
3.31
), all we need to verify is the following (Ward’s) OPE in the identity chart of cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}:

(3.36)

A_{(0)}(\zeta)\Phi_{(0)}(z)\sim\dfrac{J_{(0)}(z)}{\zeta-z}.

This is clear from (
3.27
) and Wick’s calculus.

We now define the Virasoro field
T_{(0)}
of
\Phi_{(0)}
by
T_{(0)}:=-\frac{1}{2}J_{(0)}*J_{(0)}
, equivalently by the following OPE:

(3.37)

J_{(0)}(\zeta)J_{(0)}(z)=-\dfrac{1}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}-2\,T_{(0)}(z)+o(1).

Then it follows from (
3.28
) that

T_{(0)}=A_{(0)}+\dfrac{1}{12}S_{w}+(w^{\prime})^{2}\Big(\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}\Big).

Next, let us prove the general case
b\neq 0
. It is easy to see that
ib\partial J_{(0)}
and
\jmath_{(b)}J_{(0)}
satisfy the following transformation rules:

\displaystyle ib\partial J_{(0)}=ibh^{\prime\prime}\tilde{J}_{(0)}\circ h+ib(h^{\prime})^{2}\partial\tilde{J}_{(0)}\circ h;\qquad\jmath_{(b)}J_{(0)}=ib\Big(\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\Big)h^{\prime}\tilde{J}_{(0)}\circ h+(h^{\prime})^{2}(\tilde{\jmath}_{(b)}\tilde{J}_{(0)})\circ h.

Thus we readily see that
A_{(b)}
is a holomorphic quadratic differential.

Again, we claim that
W=(A_{(b)},\bar{A}_{(b)})
is a stress tensor for
\Phi_{(b)}
. Since
\Phi_{(b)}
is the real part of a PPS-form, by (
3.32
), it suffices to show the following (Ward’s) OPE in the identity chart of cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}:

A_{(b)}(\zeta)\Phi_{(b)}(z)=\Big(A_{(0)}(\zeta)+(ib\partial-\jmath_{(b)})J_{(0)}(\zeta)\Big)\Phi_{(b)}(z)\sim\dfrac{ib}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\dfrac{J_{(b)}(z)}{\zeta-z}.

The above OPE follows from (
3.27
), (
3.28
) and (
3.36
). Let

T_{(b)}=-\frac{1}{2}J_{(b)}\ast J_{(b)}+ib\partial J_{(b)}.

Then by (
3.37
), we obtain

\displaystyle T_{(b)}

\displaystyle=A_{(b)}+\dfrac{1}{12}S_{w}+(w^{\prime})^{2}\Big(\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)-\dfrac{1}{2}\jmath_{(b)}^{2}+ib\partial\jmath_{(b)}

\displaystyle=A_{(b)}+\dfrac{1-12b^{2}}{12}S_{w}+(w^{\prime})^{2}\Big(\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}\Big),

which completes the proof.
∎

3.5.
Formal bosonic fields and neutrality condition

In this subsection we introduce formal fields and their formal correlation functions.
We use them to describe modified multi-vertex fields (OPE exponentials) in the next subsection.
We first define the
bi-variant
field
\Phi^{+}\equiv\Phi^{+}_{(b)}
by

\Phi^{+}_{(b)}(z,z_{0}):=\Big\{\Phi_{(b)}^{+}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}J_{(b)}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\Big\}=\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z,z_{0})+ib\log\dfrac{w^{\prime}(z)}{w^{\prime}(z_{0})},

where
\gamma
is a curve from
z_{0}
to
z
and
w:D_{r}\to\mathcal{C}_{r}
is a conformal map.
Note that the values of
\Phi^{+}
are multivalued functionals.

Define the complex Green’s function in
D_{r}
as

G^{\chi+}_{D_{r}}(z,z_{1}):=\dfrac{1}{2}\log\Big(\dfrac{\Theta_{\chi}(r,w(z)-\overline{w(z_{1})})}{\Theta_{\chi}(r,w(z)-w(z_{1}))}\Big).

We remark that for the ER case when
\chi=\infty
, the complex Green’s function plays an important role in the theory of conformal mappings between canonical multiply connected domains, see e.g.,
[
11
]
.
Integrating (
3.23
) over a curve from
z_{0}
to
z
, we obtain

\mathbf{E}[\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z,z_{0})\Phi_{(0)}(z_{1})]=2(G^{\chi+}_{D_{r}}(z,z_{1})-G^{\chi+}_{D_{r}}(z_{0},z_{1})).

Now we introduce
formal (1-point) fields

\Phi_{(0)}^{\pm}
in
D_{r}
as centered Gaussian formal fields with (formal) 2-point correlations:

(3.38)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{1})\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{2})]

\displaystyle=-\log\Theta_{\chi}(r,w(z_{1})-w(z_{2}));

(3.39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{1})\Phi^{-}_{(0)}(z_{2})]

\displaystyle=+\log\Theta_{\chi}(r,w(z_{1})-\overline{w(z_{2})}).

Formal fields
\Phi_{(0)}^{\pm}
satisfy
\Phi_{(0)}=\Phi_{(0)}^{+}+\Phi_{(0)}^{-}
within correlations, e.g.,

\mathbf{E}\,\Phi_{(0)}(z_{1})\Phi_{(0)}(z_{2})=\mathbf{E}(\Phi_{(0)}^{+}(z_{1})+\Phi_{(0)}^{-}(z_{1}))(\Phi_{(0)}^{+}(z_{2})+\Phi_{(0)}^{-}(z_{2})),

where
\mathbf{E}
in the left-hand side stands for correlation while we use formal correlations in the right-hand side. We define
\Phi_{(b)}^{\pm}
by

(3.40)

\Phi^{+}_{(b)}=\Phi^{+}_{(0)}+ib\log w^{\prime},\qquad\Phi^{-}_{(b)}=\Phi^{-}_{(0)}-ib\log\overline{w^{\prime}}.

By definition, the following formal rule holds:

\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z,z_{0})=\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z)-\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{0}).

The formal
dual boson

\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}
is defined by

(3.41)

\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}=-i(\Phi^{+}_{(0)}-\Phi^{-}_{(0)}),\qquad\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(z,z_{0})=\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(z)-\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(z_{0}).

Note that we have the following relations

2\,\Phi^{+}_{(0)}=\Phi_{(0)}+i\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)},\qquad 2\,\Phi^{-}_{(0)}=\Phi_{(0)}-i\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}.

For a finite set of distinct points
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
in
\bar{D}_{r}
, let us denote
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum\sigma_{j}\cdot z_{j}
, where
\sigma_{j}
is a “charge” at each
z_{j}
.
We also consider
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as a divisor, i.e., a function
\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\bar{D}_{r}\to\mathbb{R}
which takes the value
0
at all points except for finitely many points, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(z_{j})=\sigma_{j}
.
Sometimes it is convenient to consider
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
as an atomic measure
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum\sigma_{j}\cdot\delta_{z_{j}}
.
For example,
\int\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum\sigma_{j}
.

For a double divisor
(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}})
, we define the formal bosonic field
\Phi\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]
by

(3.42)

\Phi\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]:=\sum\sigma_{j}\Phi^{+}(z_{j})-\sigma_{*j}\Phi^{-}(z_{j}),\qquad\Phi^{\pm}\equiv\Phi^{\pm}_{(0)}.

In general, it is not a well-defined Fock space field.
The proposition below shows that the neutrality condition

(3.43)

\int\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}=0

guarantees well-definedness of
\Phi[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]
as a Fock space field.
For example, bi-variant fields satisfy the neutrality condition.

Proposition 3.3
.

If a double divisor
(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}})
satisfies (
3.43
), then the formal bosonic field
\Phi[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]
can be represented as a linear combination of well-defined Fock space fields.

Proof.

Let us choose any point
z_{0}\in D_{r}
. Then

\Phi[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]=\Phi^{+}(z_{0})\int\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\Phi^{-}(z_{0})\int\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}+\sum\sigma_{j}\Phi^{+}(z_{j},z_{0})-\sigma_{*j}\Phi^{-}(z_{j},z_{0}).

Under the neutrality condition, the first two terms on the right-hand side become the Fock space correlation functional:

\Phi^{+}(z_{0})\int\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\Phi^{-}(z_{0})\int\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}=\Phi(z_{0})\int\boldsymbol{\sigma}.

∎

3.6.
Coulomb gas correlation function

In this subsection we construct modified multi-vertex fields and compute their correlation functions by the
Coulomb gas formalism
.

Given divisors
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sum\sigma_{j}\cdot z_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{{\tau}}=\sum\tau_{k}\cdot\zeta_{k}
for
z_{j},\zeta_{k}\in D_{r}
, set

\Phi[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]:=\sum\sigma_{j}\,\Phi(z_{j}),\qquad\Phi[\boldsymbol{\tau}]:=\sum\tau_{k}\,\Phi(\zeta_{k}),

where
\Phi\equiv\Phi_{(0)}
.
Define

\mathbf{E}\Phi[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]*\Phi[\boldsymbol{\tau}]:=\sum_{z_{j}\neq\zeta_{k}}\sigma_{j}\tau_{k}\,\mathbf{E}\Phi(z_{j})\Phi(\zeta_{k})+\sum_{z_{j}=\zeta_{k}}\sigma_{j}\tau_{k}\,\mathbf{E}\Phi*\Phi(z_{j}).

In particular, set

c[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]:=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{E}\Phi[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]*\Phi[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]

and define the (non-chiral)
Coulomb gas correlation function

C_{(b)}
as

C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]:=e^{-c[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}\prod(w^{\prime}_{j})^{-b\sigma_{j}}(\overline{w^{\prime}_{j}})^{b\sigma_{j}},

where
w_{j}:=w(z_{j})
. By (
3.21
) and (
3.26
), if all
z_{j}
’s are in the bulk,
C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]
is
(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}-b\sigma_{j},\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}+b\sigma_{j})
-differential at
z_{j}
, and its evaluation in the identity chart of cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}
is given as

\displaystyle C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\Theta^{\prime}_{\chi}(0)^{\sum\sigma_{j}^{2}}\prod\Theta_{\chi}(z_{j}-\bar{z}_{j})^{-\sigma_{j}^{2}}\prod_{j<k}\Big|\frac{\Theta_{\chi}(z_{j}-z_{k})}{\Theta_{\chi}(z_{j}-\bar{z}_{k})}\Big|^{2\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}}.

We remark that the terminology “Coulomb gas” comes from the resemblance of the 2-point function
\mathbf{E}\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})
with the electric potential energy between two unit charges in a planar domain (or on a compact Riemann surface).
For the genus one case we refer to
[
37
,
26
]
and references therein.

By definition, a non-chiral vertex field
\mathcal{V}_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]
is given by the OPE exponential

\mathcal{V}_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]:=e^{*i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{n}\Phi^{\ast n}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}{n!}.

Then it is easy to show that

\mathcal{V}_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]=C_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]\mathcal{V}^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}],\qquad\mathcal{V}^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]=e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}.

Let us denote by
(\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}})=(\sum\sigma_{j}\cdot z_{j},\sum\sigma_{*j}\cdot z_{j})
a double divisor satisfying the neutrality condition
(\mathrm{NC}_{0})
. We define (chiral) Coulomb gas correlation function
C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
as

(3.44)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}:&=\Theta^{\prime}_{\chi}(0)^{\sum\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{*j}^{2})}\prod\Theta_{\chi}(w_{j}-\bar{w}_{j})^{\sigma_{j}\sigma_{*j}}\prod(w^{\prime}_{j})^{\lambda_{j}}(\overline{w^{\prime}_{j}})^{\lambda_{*j}}\\
&\times\prod_{j<k}\Theta_{\chi}(w_{j}-w_{k})^{\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}}\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{j}-w_{k})^{\sigma_{*j}\sigma_{k}}\Theta_{\chi}(w_{j}-\bar{w}_{k})^{\sigma_{j}\sigma_{*k}}\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{j}-\bar{w}_{k})^{\sigma_{*j}\sigma_{*k}},\end{split}

where the conformal dimensions are given as

(3.45)

(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{*j})=(\lambda(\sigma_{j}),\lambda(\sigma_{*j})),\qquad\lambda(x):=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}-b\,x.

Notice here that the non-chiral Coulomb gas correlation function is related to chiral one as

C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]=C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},-\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}
.

By definition, the
multi-vertex field

\mathcal{O}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
is given by

(3.46)

\mathcal{O}_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}:=C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}],\qquad V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}]=e^{\odot i\Phi_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]}.

Then the field
\mathcal{O}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
is a differential with conformal dimension

(h_{j},h_{*j})=(\lambda(\sigma_{j}),\lambda(\sigma_{*j}))
at
z_{j}
.
In particular, we have

(3.47)

\mathcal{O}_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}:=\mathcal{O}_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},-\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}=C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]e^{\odot i\Phi_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}=e^{\ast i\Phi_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}.

Now we show that
\mathcal{O}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
satisfies Ward’s OPE.

Proposition 3.4
.

Under the neutrality condition,
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{z}})\equiv\mathcal{O}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}(\boldsymbol{{z}})
satisfies Ward’s OPE: as
\zeta\to z_{j}\in D_{r}
,

T(\zeta)\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{z}})\sim h_{j}\frac{\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{j}\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\zeta-z_{j}},\qquad T(\zeta)\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{z})\sim\bar{h}_{*j}\frac{\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{j}\overline{\mathcal{O}}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\zeta-z_{j}}.

Proof.

Since vertex fields are differentials, it is enough to prove the proposition in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. Recall that in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
,

J_{(b)}=J_{(0)},\qquad T_{(b)}=-\dfrac{1}{2}J_{(0)}\odot J_{(0)}+ib\,\partial J_{(0)}+\Big(\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}\Big).

By Wick’s calculus, we have

\partial_{j}\mathcal{O}=\dfrac{\partial_{j}C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}{C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}\mathcal{O}+i\sigma_{j}J_{(b)}(z_{j})\odot\mathcal{O}.

First, let us show Ward’s OPE in the case
b=0
. Let us denote

\displaystyle F(\zeta,\boldsymbol{z}):

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}J_{(0)}(\zeta)\sum\Big(i\sigma_{j}\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{j})-i\sigma_{*j}\Phi^{-}_{(0)}(z_{j})\Big)=-\dfrac{i}{2}\sum\Big(\sigma_{j}H_{\chi}(r,\zeta-z_{j})+\sigma_{*j}H_{\chi}(r,\zeta-\bar{z}_{j})\Big).

Then by (
3.13
),(
3.44
) and (
3.45
) as
\zeta\to z_{j}
, we have

F(\zeta,\boldsymbol{z})\sim-\dfrac{i\sigma_{j}}{\zeta-z_{j}},\qquad F(\zeta,\boldsymbol{z})^{2}\sim-\frac{2h_{j}}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}}-\frac{\partial_{j}C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}{C_{(b)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}\frac{2}{\zeta-z_{j}}.

On the other hand, by Wick’s formula, we have

T_{(0)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})=T_{(0)}(\zeta)\odot\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})-F(\zeta,\boldsymbol{z})J_{(0)}(\zeta)\odot\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})-\tfrac{1}{2}F(\zeta,\boldsymbol{z})^{2}\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z}).

Combining all the above equations, our assertion in the case
b=0
follows. For general
b\in\mathbb{R}
, we only need to check

ib\partial J_{(0)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})\sim-\sigma_{j}b\frac{\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{z})}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}},\qquad\textrm{as }\zeta\to z_{j}.

Again, this easily follows from Wick’s calculus. We leave it to the reader to verify the proposition for
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
.
∎

3.7.
Background charge modifications

Given marked points
q_{k}
, we call a divisor
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\cdot q_{k}
satisfying the neutrality condition
\int\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
a
background charge
.
By definition, a
background charge modification

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}
of the GFF
\Phi\equiv\Phi_{(b)}
is given as

(3.48)

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}:=\Phi_{(0)}+\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\qquad\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z):=-2b\arg w^{\prime}_{z}+\sum\beta_{k}\arg\Big\{\Theta_{\chi}(w_{k}-w_{z})\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{k}-w_{z})\Big\}.

Here
w_{z}=w(z)
and
w_{k}=w(q_{k})
. By Wick’s calculus, the non-random harmonic function
\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
can also be recognized as

(3.49)

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)=\mathbf{E}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)=\mathbf{E}[e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}\Phi(z)].

The current field
J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is defined in a natural way as

(3.50)

J_{\boldsymbol{\beta},(b)}\equiv J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\partial\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=J_{(0)}+\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},

where

(3.51)

\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\partial\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=ib\dfrac{w^{\prime\prime}_{z}}{w^{\prime}_{z}}+\dfrac{iw^{\prime}_{z}}{4}\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H_{\chi}(w_{k}-w_{z})+H_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{k}-w_{z})\Big\}.

Notice that for the trivial divisor
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
, we have

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{0}}=\Phi_{(b)}
and
J_{\boldsymbol{0}}=J_{(b)}
.

We now generalize Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The field
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
has a stress tensor

A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=A_{(0)}+\Big(ib\partial-\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Big)J_{(0)},

and its Virasoro field is given as

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle:=-\dfrac{1}{2}J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+ib\,\partial J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

In particular,
\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)
is evaluated in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)&=\dfrac{1}{32}\Big[\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H_{\chi}(r,q_{k}-z)+H_{\chi}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-z)\Big\}\Big]^{2}\\
&+\dfrac{b}{4}\,\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H^{\prime}_{\chi}(r,q_{k}-z)+H^{\prime}_{\chi}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-z)\Big\}+\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}.\end{split}

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we denote by
\phi
the local chart and
h=\tilde{\phi}\circ\phi^{-1}
the transition map between
\phi
and a chart
\tilde{\phi}
overlapping with
\phi
. We also use the tilde notation for conformal fields evaluated to the chart
\tilde{\phi}
, e.g.,
\tilde{J}_{(0)}=(J_{(0)}\|\tilde{\phi})
.

We claim that
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is a
(2,0)
-differential and Ward’s OPE

(3.52)

A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)\sim\frac{ib}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\frac{J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)}{\zeta-z}

holds. Then the first part of the proposition immediately follows from (
3.32
).

Recall that

\partial J_{(0)}=h^{\prime\prime}\tilde{J}_{(0)}\circ h+(h^{\prime})^{2}\partial\tilde{J}_{(0)}\circ h
. Also by (
3.51
),

(3.53)

\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=h^{\prime}\,\tilde{\jmath}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\circ h+ib\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{h^{\prime}}.

Combining the above equations, one can observe that
(ib\partial-\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})J_{(0)}
is a
(2,0)
-differential.
Note that

A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)\sim A_{(0)}(\zeta)\Phi_{(0)}(z)+\Big(ib\partial-\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Big)J_{(0)}(\zeta)\Phi_{(0)}(z).

Then (
3.52
) follows from (
3.29
) and Proposition
3.2
.

Next, we prove the second part of the proposition. By Proposition
3.2
, we have

(3.54)

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\mathbf{E}\,T_{(0)}-\tfrac{1}{2}\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}+ib\partial\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},

and
\mathbf{E}\,T_{(0)}
is an S-form of order
1/12
. Moreover, by (
3.53
), the term
-\frac{1}{2}\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}+ib\partial\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is an S-form of order
-b^{2}
, which completes the proof.
∎

We define holomorhpic (resp., anti-holomorphic) function
\varphi^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
(resp.,
\varphi^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
) as

(3.55)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&:=+ib\log w^{\prime}_{z}-\dfrac{i}{2}\sum\beta_{k}\log\Big\{\Theta_{\chi}(w_{k}-w_{z})\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{k}-w_{z})\Big\},\\
\varphi^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&:=-ib\log\overline{w_{z}^{\prime}}+\dfrac{i}{2}\sum\beta_{k}\log\Big\{\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{k}-\bar{w}_{z})\Theta_{\chi}(w_{k}-\bar{w}_{z})\Big\}.\end{split}

The chiral and anti-chiral parts of
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
are defined by

(3.56)

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}:=\Phi_{(0)}^{+}+\varphi^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\qquad\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}:=\Phi_{(0)}^{-}+\varphi^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Notice that by definition,
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}+\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}
.

For a double divisor
(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}})=(\sum\sigma_{j}\cdot z_{j},\sum\sigma_{*j}\cdot z_{j})
satisfying
\int(\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}})=0
, we write

(3.57)

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]:=\sum\sigma_{j}\,\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}-\sigma_{*j}\,\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}.

We define the formal bosonic field

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]
associated with the background charge
\boldsymbol{\beta}
as

(3.58)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]:=\sum\sigma_{j}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{+}(z_{j})-\sigma_{*j}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}(z_{j})=\Phi_{(0)}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]+\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}].\end{split}

The modified multi-vertex field
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]
is defined by

(3.59)

\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}:=\frac{C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}+\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}}{C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}}V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}].

In particular, if
\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}=-\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, we simply omit
\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}
in the notations, e.g.,

(3.60)

\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}]:=\sum\sigma_{j}\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\qquad\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}}]:=\sum\sigma_{j}\,\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z_{j}).

The modified multi-vertex fields are called the OPE exponentials of GFF.
The reason for this terminology is clear due to the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

We have

\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}=e^{\ast i\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}
.

Proof.

By
[
36
, Lemma 5.2]
and (
3.47
),

\displaystyle e^{\ast i\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}=e^{i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}e^{\ast i\Phi_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}=C_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]e^{i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}e^{\odot i\Phi_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]}.

Therefore all we need to show is

(3.61)

C_{(0)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]e^{i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}=\frac{C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}+\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}}{C_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}}.

Observe that
e^{i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]}
is a scalar field with respect to each
q_{k}
and is a differential with dimension
(-b\sigma_{j},-b\sigma_{*j})
at each
z_{j}
.
Therefore by (
3.45
), conformal dimensions of both sides of (
3.61
) are identical. Thus it is enough to show (
3.61
) in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
By construction,

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{i\varphi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]}&=\prod_{j<k}\Big(\Theta_{\chi}(r,q_{k}-z_{j})\Theta_{\chi}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-z_{j})\Big)^{\frac{\sigma_{j}\beta_{k}}{2}}\Big(\Theta_{\chi}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-\bar{z}_{j})\Theta_{\chi}(r,q_{k}-\bar{z}_{j})\Big)^{\frac{\sigma_{*j}\beta_{k}}{2}}.\end{split}

Now proposition follows from (
3.44
).
∎

We now generalize Proposition
3.4
for a non-trivial background charge
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The vertex field
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
satisfies Ward’s OPE

T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}\sim\lambda_{j}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}}{\zeta-z_{j}},\qquad\text{as }\zeta\to z_{j},

and similar OPE holds for
\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
.

Proof.

We write
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
and
\mathcal{O}_{(b)}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{(b)}{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}
throughout the proof.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is a differential, it suffices to show the proposition in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. By combining Propositions
3.2
and
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=T_{(b)}

\displaystyle-(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\jmath_{(b)})J_{(0)}-\frac{1}{2}(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}-\jmath_{(b)}^{2})+ib\partial(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\jmath_{(b)}).

Then it follows from Proposition
3.4
and
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)})\mathcal{O}_{(b)}
that

\displaystyle T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)}}T_{(b)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{(b)}-(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\jmath_{(b)})J_{(0)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\lambda_{j}\,\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}}+\frac{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)}}\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{(b)}}{\zeta-z_{j}}-(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\jmath_{(b)})J_{(0)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\lambda_{j}\,\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{(\zeta-z_{j})^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\zeta-z_{j}}-\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}(\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)})}{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)}}\frac{\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\zeta-z_{j}}-(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\jmath_{(b)})J_{(0)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

On the other hand, by Wick’s calculus together with (
3.38
),(
3.39
) and (
3.13
), we have

(3.62)

\displaystyle J_{(0)}(\zeta)\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\,\mathbf{E}J_{(0)}(\zeta)\sum(i\sigma_{j}\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z_{j})-i\sigma_{*j}\Phi^{-}_{(0)}(z_{j}))\sim-i\sigma_{j}\,\frac{\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\zeta-z_{j}}.

Moreover by (
3.44
) and (
3.51
), we have

\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-\frac{\partial_{z_{j}}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)}}{\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}_{(b)}}=i\sigma_{j}(\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\jmath_{(b)}).

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Eguchi-Ooguri and Ward’s equations

In
[
20
]
, Eguchi and Ooguri stated Ward’s equation for tensor product
\mathcal{X}
of primary fields (differentials in the OPE family) on a complex torus of genus one in terms of linear partial differential operators with respect to the nodes of fields and the modular parameter
\tau
.
They explained the so-called Teichmüller term
\partial_{\tau}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}
by means of the Virasoro generator
L_{0}
, the zeroth mode of the Virasoro field.
In this section we derive Ward’s equation in a doubly connected domain not from Eguchi and Ooguri’s path integral formalism but some basic identities in special function theory together with Wick’s calculus.

4.1.
Ward’s functional and Ward’s identities

We first introduce global Ward’s functional and present the relation between a stress tensor and Ward’s functional.

Let
W=(A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{A}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
be a stress tensor for
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.
By Proposition
3.5
, it is easy to see that
A=A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is continuous and real on the boundary.
For a given open set
U\subset D_{r}
and a smooth vector field
v
in
\overline{U}
continuous up to the boundary, Ward’s functionals
W^{\pm}(v;U)
are defined by

W^{+}(v;U)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial U}vA-\frac{1}{\pi}\int\!\!\int_{U}({\bar{\partial}}v)A,\qquad\qquad W^{-}(v;U)=\overline{W^{+}(v;U)}.

We also write
W(v;U):=2\,\mathrm{Re}\,W^{+}(v;U)
. Then for any Fock space functional
\mathcal{X}
with nodes in
D_{r}
,
\mathbf{E}[W^{+}(v;U)\mathcal{X}]
is a well-defined correlation function in
D_{\textrm{hol}}(v)
.

We will frequently use the following proposition.
See
[
35
, Propositions 5.3 and 5.10]
for more details.
The last equations in the following proposition are known as Ward’s identities.

Proposition 4.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

•

Ward’s OPE holds for a Fock space field
X;

•

The residue form of Ward’s identity for
X

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{v}X(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(z)}vAX(z)-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(z)}\overline{v}\bar{A}X(z)

holds on
D_{\textrm{hol}}(v)\cap U
for all smooth vector field
v;

•

For all
z\in D_{\textrm{hol}}(v)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{L}(v,U)X(z)]=\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{Y}W(v,U)X(z)]

holds for all Fock space functionals
\mathcal{Y}
with nodes outside supp(
v
).

The definition of Ward’s functionals can be extended to meromorphic vector fields. For a meromorphic vector field
v
with poles
\{p_{k}\}
in
D_{r}
,
we define

W^{+}(v)=W^{+}(v;D_{r}):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}W^{+}(v;D_{r}^{\varepsilon}),

where
D_{r}^{\varepsilon}=D_{r}\setminus\bigcup_{k}B(p_{k},\varepsilon)
.
Thus, we have

W^{+}(v)=W^{+}(v;D_{r})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{r}}vA-\frac{1}{\pi}\int\!\!\int_{D_{r}}({\bar{\partial}}v)A,

with the interpretation of
{\bar{\partial}}v
in the sense of distributions.

Proposition 4.2
.

In the identity chart
\mathrm{id}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{r}}
, we have

2A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)=W^{+}(v_{\zeta})+W^{-}(v_{\bar{\zeta}})+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\,d\xi,

where
(v_{\zeta}\|\mathrm{id}_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{r}})(z)=H(r,\zeta-z)
.

Proof.

Recall that a stress tensor
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

is a holomorphic quadratic differential in
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, and real on the boundary. For
\zeta\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
, the reflected vector field
v_{\zeta}^{\sharp}
is defined by

v_{\zeta}^{\sharp}(z):=\overline{v_{\zeta}(\bar{z})}=v_{\bar{\zeta}}(z).

Since
v_{\zeta}
is meromorphic on
\mathcal{C}_{r}
with poles at
\{\zeta+2\pi m+2irn:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, the reflected vector field
v_{\zeta}^{\sharp}
is holomorphic on
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. Then by definition,

W^{+}(v_{\zeta})=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}({\bar{\partial}}v_{\zeta})A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{r}}v_{\zeta}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\qquad W^{+}(v_{\zeta}^{\sharp})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{r}}v_{\zeta}^{\sharp}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Also, since
A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bar{A}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
on
\partial\mathcal{C}_{r}
, we have

W^{-}(v_{\bar{\zeta}})=\overline{W^{+}(v_{\zeta}^{\sharp})}=\overline{\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{r}}v_{\bar{\zeta}}(\xi)A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\,d\xi}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{r}}v_{\zeta}(\bar{\xi})A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Then it follows from
{\bar{\partial}}v_{\zeta}=-2\pi\delta_{\zeta}
, and
H(r,z+2ir)=H(r,z)-2i
that

\displaystyle W^{+}(v_{\zeta})+W^{-}(v_{\bar{\zeta}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}({\bar{\partial}}v_{\zeta})A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\,d\xi=2A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\,d\xi.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Eguchi-Ooguri equations

We now state and prove a version of Eguchi-Ooguri equations.

First let us consider the case
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{F}_{(b)}=\mathcal{F}_{(b),\boldsymbol{0}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{(b),\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is the OPE family generated by
\Phi_{(b),\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{(b)}
, in the
\mathcal{C}_{r}
-uniformization,

(4.1)

\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}\mathbf{E}A(\zeta)\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{W}
the family of Fock space fields satisfying (
4.1
).
We show
\mathcal{F}_{(b)}\subset\mathcal{W}
through the following steps.

Step 1.
A tensor product of two GFFs is in
\mathcal{W}
.

Step 2.
All tensor products of GFFs and their derivatives are in
\mathcal{W}
.

Step 3.
The family
\mathcal{W}
is closed under linear combinations over
\mathbb{C}
and OPE products
\ast_{n}
.

Proof of Step 1.

Let us denote
A\equiv A_{(b)}
,
\Phi\equiv\Phi_{(b)}
. Recall that in the identity chart
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}_{r}}
,
A=-\frac{1}{2}J_{(0)}\odot J_{(0)}+ib\partial J_{(0)}
, and
\Phi=\Phi_{(0)}
. Since
\partial J
is exact, by the fundamental theorem of calculus, and the fact that all correlation functions lifted to the covering space of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
are 2
\pi
periodic, it suffices to show the case
b=0
.

First, we show this step for the ER boundary condition (i.e.,
\chi=\infty
).
For
\mathcal{X}=\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})
, by (
3.21
) and the fact

\partial_{r}\log\Theta=H^{\prime}/2+H^{2}/4
(which follows from (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})]=-\textup{I}(z_{1},z_{2})+\textup{I}(\bar{z}_{1},z_{2})+\textup{I}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{2})-\textup{I}(\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2}),

where
\textup{I}(z,w):=H^{\prime}(z-w)/2+H^{2}(z-w)/4
.
On the other hand, by Wick’s calculus and (
3.23
), we have

\mathbf{E}[A(\zeta)\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})]=-\textup{II}_{z_{1},z_{2}}(\zeta)+\textup{II}_{\bar{z}_{1},z_{2}}(\zeta)+\textup{II}_{z_{1},\bar{z}_{2}}(\zeta)-\textup{II}_{\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2}}(\zeta),

where

\textup{II}_{z,w}(\zeta):=H(\zeta-z)H(\zeta-w)/4
. Note that by (
3.15
),
\textup{II}_{z,w}(\zeta)
can be rewritten as

(4.2)

\displaystyle\textup{II}_{z,w}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\textup{I}(z,w)+\frac{1}{4}\textup{III}_{z,w}(\zeta)+\frac{1}{8}H^{2}(\zeta-z)+\frac{1}{8}H^{2}(\zeta-w)+\frac{3\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi},

where

\textup{III}_{z,w}(\zeta):=H^{\prime}(\zeta-z)+H^{\prime}(\zeta-w)+(H(\zeta-w)-H(\zeta-z))H(w-z).

Combining all of the above, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[A(\zeta)\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})]-\frac{1}{4}\Big(\textup{III}_{z_{1},z_{2}}(\zeta)-\textup{III}_{\bar{z}_{1},z_{2}}(\zeta)-\textup{III}_{z_{1},\bar{z}_{2}}(\zeta)+\textup{III}_{\bar{z}_{1},\bar{z}_{2}}(\zeta)\Big).

Finally, observe that by (
3.2
) and (
3.7
), we have

\oint_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}\textup{III}_{z,w}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\equiv 0
,
which leads to (
4.1
) for
\mathcal{X}=\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})
with ER boundary condition.

We now show this step for the weighted boundary conditions with any
\chi\in[0,\infty)
. For notational convenience, we add the superscript
``ER"
for the field with ER boundary condition, e.g.,
\Phi^{ER},A^{ER}
etc.
Then by (
3.20
) and the periodicity of
H
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\pi}\oint\Big(\mathbf{E}[A(\zeta)\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})]-\mathbf{E}[A^{ER}(\zeta)\Phi^{ER}(z_{1})\Phi^{ER}(z_{2})]\Big)\,d\zeta=-\frac{(z_{1}-\bar{z}_{1})(z_{2}-\bar{z}_{2})}{2\,(r+\chi)^{2}}.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{r}\Big(\mathbf{E}\Phi(z_{1})\Phi(z_{2})-\mathbf{E}\Phi^{ER}(z_{1})\Phi^{ER}(z_{2})\Big)=\partial_{r}\Big(\frac{1}{r+\chi}\Big)\frac{(z_{1}-\bar{z}_{1})(z_{2}-\bar{z}_{2})}{2}.\end{split}

Combining all of the above identities, the claim follows.

Proof of Step 2.

There is nothing to prove for
\mathcal{X}=\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n-1})
. Therefore it is enough to show (
4.1
) for
\mathcal{X}=\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n})
.
For
j\neq k
, let us denote

\mathcal{X}_{jk}=\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{k-1})\Phi(z_{k+1})\cdots\Phi(z_{j-1})\Phi(z_{j+1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n}).

Then by Wick’s formula and an induction argument, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int\mathbf{E}[A(\zeta)\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n})]\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\sum_{j\neq k}\mathbf{E}[A(\zeta)\Phi(z_{j})\Phi(z_{k})]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{X}_{jk}]\,d\zeta=\sum_{j\neq k}\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{j})\Phi(z_{k})]\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{X}_{jk}].

On the other hand, by (
3.22
) and Leibniz’s rule, we express the left-hand side of (
4.1
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n})]

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\sum\prod_{k}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{i_{k}})\Phi(z_{j_{k}})]

\displaystyle=\sum\Big(\prod_{k}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{i_{k}})\Phi(z_{j_{k}})]\Big)\Big(\sum_{k}\frac{\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{i_{k}})\Phi(z_{j_{k}})]}{\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{i_{k}})\Phi(z_{j_{k}})]}\Big),

where the outer sum is taken over all partitions of the set
\{1,\cdots,2n\}
into disjoint pairs
\{i_{l},j_{l}\}
.
Rearranging the terms, we rewrite the above identity as

\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n})]=\sum_{j\neq k}\Big(\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{j})\Phi(z_{k})]\Big)\Big(\sum\prod_{l}\mathbf{E}[\Phi(z_{i_{l}})\Phi(z_{j_{l}})]\Big),

where the sum is over all partitions of the set
\{1,\cdots,2n\}\setminus\{j,k\}
into disjoint pairs
\{i_{k},j_{k}\}
. Therefore again, by (
3.22
), we have (
4.1
) for
\mathcal{X}=\Phi(z_{1})\cdots\Phi(z_{2n})
. Differentiating term by term, we complete the proof of Step 2.

Proof of Step 3.
The first part of this step is obvious. For the second part, we consider the case that the field
X
is holomorphic. The other cases can be treated in a similar way. Suppose that we have the following OPE

X(\zeta)Y(z)=\sum C_{n}(z)(\zeta-z)^{n},\qquad\zeta\to z.

If both
X
and
Y
are in
\mathcal{W}
, then the term by term integration gives

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}\mathbf{E}[A(\xi)X(\zeta)Y(z)]\,d\xi=\sum_{n}\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}\mathbf{E}[A(\xi)C_{n}(z)]\,d\xi\Big)(\zeta-z)^{n}

as
\zeta\to z
. On the other hand, differentiating term by term, we have

\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[X(\zeta)Y(z)]=\sum\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}[C_{n}(z)](\zeta-z)^{n}.

Therefore, by comparing the coefficients of the two expansions above, we conclude
C_{n}\in\mathcal{W}
.
∎

Remark
.

Let us consider the GFF
\Phi
with 2-point correlation function given by a probabilistic interpolation (via Bernoulli distribution with probability
p\equiv p(r)
) of Green’s functions with ER and Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Phi(\zeta)\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=2\Big(p(r)G^{Diri}_{r}(\zeta,z)+(1-p(r))G^{ER}_{r}(\zeta,z)\Big)=2\log\Big|\frac{\Theta(r,\zeta-\bar{z})}{\Theta(r,\zeta-z)}\Big|-\frac{2\,p(r)}{r}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta\,\mathrm{Im}\,z.

Then the conclusion of Lemma
4.3
can be drawn if and only if
\partial_{r}(r/p(r))=1
. In other words, to derive Eguchi-Ooguri type equation of the form (
4.1
), it is required that

(4.3)

p(r)=r/(r+\chi),\qquad(\chi\geq 0).

This corresponds to the rational interpolation (
3.20
).

Let us emphasize that Lemma
4.3
holds only for the non-chiral field
\mathcal{X}
.
To our purpose of implementing Ward’s equation for the OPE family of GFFs associated with the general background charge, we now extend Eguchi-Ooguri equations to a certain family of chiral fields.
We write
(\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}})=(\sum\sigma_{j}\cdot z_{j},\sum\sigma_{*j}\cdot z_{j})
for a double divisor satisfying
(\mathrm{NC}_{0})
and denote by

the associated multi-vertex field.
Due to the following proposition, we can revise the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{(b)}
so that it includes the fields of the form
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\gamma=[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]
. Then for any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{(b)}
,

(4.4)

\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}A(\zeta)\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\,\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X},

where all fields are evaluated in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}

Proof.

We present the proof for ER boundary conditions and leave it to the reader for the general case.

Again it suffices to show the case
b=0
. In the sequel, we omit the subscript
(0)
and write for instance

\textup{I}(z,w),\textup{II}_{z,w}(\zeta)
and
\textup{III}_{z,w}(\zeta)

4.3
.

By definition of the multi-vertex fields, (
4.4
) is equivalent to

(4.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}A(\zeta)V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}]\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\frac{\partial_{r}C{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}}{C{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}}\mathbf{E}V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}]\mathcal{X}+\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}]\mathcal{X},\end{split}

where
V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}]=e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]}

4.3
, it suffices to show the identity (
4.5
) for tensor products of GFFs.

Let us first show that the identity (
4.5
) holds for trivial string
\mathcal{X}=1
.
Combining (
3.44
) with (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.11
), we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{r}\log C{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}&=-\frac{3\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}\sum(\sigma_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{*j}^{2})+\sum\sigma_{j}\sigma_{*j}\textup{I}(z_{j},\bar{z}_{j})\\
&+\sum_{j<k}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}\textup{I}(z_{j},z_{k})+\sigma_{*j}\sigma_{k}\textup{I}(\bar{z}_{j},z_{k})+\sigma_{j}\sigma_{*k}\textup{I}(z_{j},\bar{z}_{k})+\sigma_{*j}\sigma_{*k}\textup{I}(\bar{z}_{j},\bar{z}_{k}).\end{split}

Differentiating (
3.38
) and (
3.39
), we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}J(\zeta)\Phi\,[\boldsymbol{{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{{\sigma_{*}}}]=-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum\Big(\sigma_{j}H(\zeta-z_{j})+\sigma_{*j}H(\zeta-\bar{z}_{j})\Big).

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{E}A(\zeta)V^{\odot}[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{*}]-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\log C{[\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma_{*}}]}

Now claim follows from the fact that

.
By Wick’s formula, we have

On the other hand, by (

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{4}\sum\sigma_{j}\Big(\textup{III}_{z_{j},z}(\zeta)-\textup{III}_{z_{j},\bar{z}}(\zeta)\Big)+\sigma_{*j}\Big(\textup{III}_{\bar{z}_{j},z}(\zeta)-\textup{III}_{\bar{z}_{j},\bar{z}}(\zeta)\Big).

) for

,

,

By Proposition
4.4
, we obtain following corollary.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\gamma=[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]
. Then for any

(4.14)

\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}T(\zeta)\mathcal{O}^{\textup{eff}}\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}^{\textup{eff}}\mathcal{X},

Proof.

By Proposition
4.4
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}T(\zeta)\mathcal{O}^{\textrm{eff}}\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}T(\zeta)\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta\Big)Z_{r}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}A(\zeta)\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta\Big)Z_{r}+\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}T(\zeta)\,d\zeta\Big)\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}Z_{r}=\Big(\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}\Big)Z_{r}+2\mathbf{E}T\,\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}Z_{r}.

Note that by Proposition
3.2
, (
3.11
) and (
4.13
), we have

(4.15)

\mathbf{E}T=-\frac{1}{6}\frac{\Theta^{\prime\prime\prime}(r,0)}{\Theta^{\prime}(r,0)}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{r}\log Z_{r}.

Therefore we conclude that

\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{E}T(\zeta)\mathcal{O}^{\textrm{eff}}\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\Big(\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}\Big)Z_{r}+\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{X}\partial_{r}Z_{r}=\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{O}^{\textup{eff}}\mathcal{X},

which completes the proof.
∎

By (
5.6
), one can see that the background charge modifications can be treated as an insertion of a multi-vertex field. (See Subsection
5.1
for further details.)
Therefore, as a consequence of Proposition
4.4
, we extend Lemma
4.3
to the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with non-trivial background charges
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

For any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, in the
\mathcal{C}_{r}
-uniformization,

(4.16)

\frac{1}{\pi}\oint_{[-\pi+ir,\pi+ir]}\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\mathcal{X}\,d\zeta=\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}.

4.3.
Ward’s equations

In this subsection we derive Ward’s equations (Theorem
A
) in terms of a stress tensor, Lie derivative operator and the modular parameter. Later, we combine these equations with the level two degeneracy equations for the one-leg operator to derive BPZ equations.

We first prove Theorem
A
in the case trivial background charge
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Proof of Theorem
A
in the case
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Let us choose

v(z)=v_{\zeta}(z)=H(r,\zeta-z)
with
\zeta\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
By Proposition
4.2
, Eguchi-Ooguri equations (Corollary
4.6
), and Ward’s identities (see Proposition
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}W^{+}(v_{\zeta})\mathcal{X}+\mathbf{E}W^{-}(v_{\bar{\zeta}})\mathcal{X}+\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{\zeta}}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{\zeta}}}^{-}\Big)\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}+\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X},

which completes the proof. ∎

For the general background charge
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, it is required to generalize the residue form of Ward’s identity at the nodes of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, see Lemma
5.5
.
Since this lemma can be more easily obtained by employing the insertion procedure, we defer its proof to Subsection
5.1
.
Then the proof of Theorem
A
for the non-trivial background charge can be obtained along the same lines for the trivial background charge with slight modifications.

As a corollary of Theorem
A
, we derive the following form of Ward’s equations.

Corollary 4.7
.

For a holomorphic differential
Y\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with conformal dimension
h
, and
X=X_{1}\cdots X_{n}
,
(X_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
), we have

2\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast Y(z)X=\mathbf{E}Y(z)\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}^{+}X+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{z}}}^{-}\mathbf{E}Y(z)X+\Big(2h\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+\partial_{r}\Big)\mathbf{E}Y(z)X,

where all fields are evaluated in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.

Proof.

By Ward’s OPE, we have the following singular part of the operator product expansion:

\Sing_{\zeta\to z}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)Y(z)X=\frac{h}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}Y(z)X+\frac{\partial_{z}}{\zeta-z}Y(z)X.

On the other hand, by Theorem
A
, we have

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)Y(z)X=(hv^{\prime}_{\zeta}(z)+v_{\zeta}(z)\partial_{z}+\partial_{r})\mathbf{E}Y(z)X+\mathbf{E}Y(z)\mathcal{L}_{v_{\zeta}}^{+}X+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{\zeta}}}^{-}\mathbf{E}Y(z)X.

Subtracting the singular part from the both sides of the above and then taking the limit as
\zeta\to z
, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast Y(z)X

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}Y(z)\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}^{+}X+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{z}}}^{-}\mathbf{E}Y(z)X+\partial_{r}\mathbf{E}Y(z)X

\displaystyle+\lim_{\zeta\to z}\Big\{hv_{\zeta}^{\prime}(z)-\frac{2h}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}+\Big(v_{\zeta}(z)-\frac{2}{\zeta-z}\Big)\partial_{z}\Big\}\mathbf{E}Y(z)X.

Corollary now follows from the asymptotic behavior (
3.13
) of the Loewner vector field
v_{\zeta}(z)=H(r,\zeta-z)
.
∎

5.
Annulus SLE martingale-observables

In this section we present the precise relation between CFT and SLE in a doubly connected domain.
In the first subsection we define the one-leg operator
\Psi
and explain its implementation as a boundary condition changing operator.
Subsection
5.2
is devoted to the study of general null-vector equations.
In Subsection
5.3
, we show a version of BPZ-Cardy type equation and prove Theorem
C
.

5.1.
One-leg operator and BPZ equations

We introduce the
one-leg operator

\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, a specific form of the modified multi-vertex field set to satisfy level two degeneracy equation. Throughout this subsection we denote by
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\cdot q_{k}
a given background charge.

By definition, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, Virasoro and current generators
L_{n}
,
J_{n}
are given as

L_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(z)}(\zeta-z)^{n+1}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\,d\zeta,\qquad J_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(z)}(\zeta-z)^{n}J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\zeta)\,d\zeta,

respectively.
Let us recall the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1

(Cf. Proposition 7.5
[
35
]
)
.

Let
X
be a Fock space field. Then any two of the following assertions imply the third one:

•

X
belongs to
\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
;

•

X
is a
[\lambda,\lambda_{*}]
-differential;

•

L_{n}X=0
for all
n\geq 1
,
L_{0}X=\lambda X,L_{-1}X=\partial X
, and similar equations hold for
\bar{X}
.

Any field satisfying all conditions in Proposition
5.1
is called a
(Virasoro) primary
field in
\mathcal{F}(A,\bar{A})
.
Furthermore, a Virasoro primary field
X
is called
current primary
if
J_{n}X=J_{n}\bar{X}=0
for all
n\geq 1
and

J_{0}X=-iqX
,
J_{0}\bar{X}=i\overline{q}_{*}\bar{X}
for some numbers
q
and
q_{*}
, which are called charges. One of the main ingredients to connect CFT with SLE theory is the following
level two degeneracy equation
.

Proposition 5.2

(Cf. Proposition 11.2
[
35
]
)
.

For a current primary field
V
with charges
q
,
q_{*}
in
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we have

\displaystyle L_{-2}V=\frac{1}{2q^{2}}L^{2}_{-1}V

provided
2q(b+q)=1
.

Suppose that the real parameters
a
and
b
are related to the SLE parameter
\kappa
as

a=\sqrt{2/\kappa},\qquad b=a(\kappa/4-1).

In the sequel, we sometimes use the following Kac labeling of the conformal weights or dimensions:

h_{r,s}=\frac{(r\kappa-4s)^{2}-(\kappa-4)^{2}}{16\kappa}.

For a divisor
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{j}\cdot z_{j}
satisfying the neutrality condition

(5.1)

a+\int\boldsymbol{\tau}=0,

we define the one-leg operator

(5.2)

\Psi(z)\big(\equiv\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})\equiv\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot z+\boldsymbol{\tau}]\,\big):=\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot z+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,,\,\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\tau}].

Notice that
\Psi
is a scalar field with respect to the each node
q_{j}
of the background charge
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. On the other hand, by (
3.45
), the conformal dimensions of
\Psi
with respect to
z
and
z_{j}
are given by

(5.3)

h_{z}=\tfrac{6-\kappa}{2\kappa}=h_{1,2},\qquad h_{j}=h_{*j}=\tfrac{1}{8}\tau_{j}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau_{j}b.

It follows from (
3.59
) and (
5.2
) that

(5.4)

\mathbf{E}\Psi(z)=\frac{C_{(b)}[a\cdot z+\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{\beta})\,,\,\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{\beta})]}{C_{(b)}{[\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}\,,\,\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}]}}.

From now on, we call

Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
the
partition function
following the terminology in CFT literature, see e.g.,
[
13
, Section 11.3]
.
Set

\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\equiv\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}):=\kappa\,\partial_{z}\log Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

By (
5.4
) and (
3.44
), the field
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is evaluated in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
as

(5.5)

\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\kappa}{2}}\Big[\sum\dfrac{\beta_{j}}{2}\Big(H_{\chi}(r,z-z_{j})+H_{\chi}(r,z-\bar{z}_{j})\Big)+\sum\dfrac{\tau_{j}}{2}\Big(H_{\chi}(r,z-q_{j})+H_{\chi}(r,z-\bar{q}_{j})\Big)\Big].

Now we derive the level two degeneracy equation for
\Psi
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The one-leg operator
\Psi
is a current primary field with charges
q=a,q_{*}=0
at
z
, i.e.,

J_{0}\Psi=-ia\Psi,\;\;\;J_{0}\overline{\Psi}=0,\;\;\;J_{n}\Psi=J_{n}\overline{\Psi}=0\qquad(n\geq 1).

Proof.

To show the above equations, it is enough to show that as
\zeta\to z
,

J_{(0)}(\zeta)\Psi(z)\sim-ia\frac{\Psi(z)}{\zeta-z},\qquad J_{(0)}(\zeta)\overline{\Psi}(z)\sim 0

hold in the identity chart. By Wick’s formula, we have

\displaystyle J_{(0)}(\zeta)\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=J_{(0)}(\zeta)\odot\Psi(z)+ia\mathbf{E}[J_{(0)}(\zeta)\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(z)]\Psi(z)

\displaystyle-\sum\frac{\tau_{j}}{2}\mathbf{E}[J_{(0)}(\zeta)\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(z_{j})]\Psi(z)-\sum\frac{\beta_{j}}{2}\mathbf{E}[J_{(0)}(\zeta)\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(q_{j})]\Psi(z),

where
\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}
is the dual boson in (
3.41
). It is also easy to see that a similar expression holds for
\overline{\Psi}
.
Now lemma follows from (
3.38
) and (
3.13
).
∎

By Proposition
5.2
, we immediately obtain the following level two degeneracy equation of
\Psi
.

Proposition 5.4
.

If
2a(a+b)=1
, then

T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast_{z}\Psi=\frac{1}{2a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{z}\Psi(z).

We are now ready to show BPZ equation, Theorem
B
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

By Proposition
5.4
, we have

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{z}\mathbf{E}\Psi(z)\mathcal{X}=2\mathbf{E}(T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast_{z}\Psi)\mathcal{X}.

Then it follows from Corollary
4.7
that

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{E}(T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast_{z}\Psi)\mathcal{X}=2\mathbf{E}(A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ast_{z}\Psi)\mathcal{X}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)\,\mathbf{E}\Psi(z)\mathcal{X},

which completes the proof.
∎

We now discuss how the one-leg operator
\Psi
acts on Fock space fields in
D_{r}
as a boundary condition changing operator. Given marked boundary points
p\in\mathbb{R}
and
\{q_{j}\},\{\xi_{j}\}\in\partial\mathcal{C}_{r}
, set
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum\beta_{j}\cdot q_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\sum\tau_{j}\cdot\xi_{j}
. By abuse of notation, for
z_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
, we sometimes use the same symbol
\boldsymbol{\tau}
for
\sum\tau_{j}\cdot z_{j}
.
From now on, we write
w_{z}=w(z)
,
w_{p}:=w(p)
,
w_{j}:=w(\xi_{j})
and
\tilde{w}_{j}:=w(q_{j})
, where
w:D_{r}\to\mathcal{C}_{r}
is a conformal transformation.
The insertion of field
\Psi/\mathbf{E}\Psi
produces an operator
\mathcal{X}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
on Fock space fields. It is given by

\partial\mathcal{X}\mapsto\partial\widehat{\mathcal{X}},\qquad\bar{\partial}\mathcal{X}\mapsto\bar{\partial}\widehat{\mathcal{X}},\qquad\alpha\mathcal{X}+\beta\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\alpha\widehat{\mathcal{X}}+\beta\widehat{\mathcal{Y}},\qquad\mathcal{X}\odot\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\odot\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}

and by the formula:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)=\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)+2a\arg\Theta_{\chi}(w_{p}-w_{z})

\displaystyle+\sum\tau_{j}\arg\Big\{\Theta_{\chi}(w_{j}-w_{z})\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{w}_{j}-w_{z})\Big\}.

When
\kappa=4
, this insertion procedure produces specific height gap of GFF, which gives rise to the coupling with SLE(
4,\Lambda
). For instance, in
[
28
]
, Hagendorf, Bernard and Bauer studied coupling of GFF with Dirichlet boundary condition and SLE(
4,\Lambda
) with one force point
q=\xi_{1}
using the one-leg operator
\Psi=\mathcal{O}[a\cdot p-a\cdot q,\boldsymbol{0}]
.
In the case of multiple force points, the (multivalued) harmonic function
\widehat{\varphi}:=\mathbf{E}\widehat{\Phi}
in
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{r}\setminus\{p,\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N}\}
has piecewise Dirichlet boundary conditions having additional jump of
2\pi a
at
p
and
2\pi\tau_{j}
at
\xi_{j}
’s. Due to the neutrality condition, we require that all jumps should add up to
0
. This type of boundary condition of GFF was studied by Izyurov and Kytölä
[
33
]
.

As in Subsection
2.3
, we let

\widehat{\mathbf{E}}[\mathcal{X}]=\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{\odot ia\Phi_{(0)}^{+}(p)-\sum\frac{\tau_{j}}{2}\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(\xi_{j})}\mathcal{X}].

Then it is easy to check that
\widehat{\mathbf{E}}[\mathcal{X}]=\mathbf{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{X}}]
for all
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.

The operator
\mathcal{X}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
acting on
\mathcal{F}_{(b)}
plays a role in imposing background charge
a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tau}
.
To be precise, by letting
\boldsymbol{\beta}=a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tau},
we have

(5.6)

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\qquad\text{if }\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{(b)}.

For a deeper discussion of the relation between the insertion procedure and the background charge modifications, we refer the reader to
[
34
]
.

Examples
.

.

The Virasoro field

is an S-form of order

We have

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\widehat{J}\ast\widehat{J}+ib\partial\widehat{J}=T-(\widehat{\jmath}-\jmath)J_{(0)}-\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{\jmath}^{2}-\jmath^{2})+ib\partial(\widehat{\jmath}-\jmath)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j,k}(\Theta_{\chi}(\xi_{j}-z_{k})\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{\xi}_{j}-z_{k}))^{\tau_{j}\sigma_{k}/2}(\Theta_{\chi}(\xi_{j}-\bar{z}_{k})\Theta_{\chi}(\bar{\xi}_{j}-\bar{z}_{k}))^{\tau_{j}\sigma_{*k}/2}

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
with nodes outside
\textup{supp }\boldsymbol{\beta}=\{q_{k}\}
and a vector field
v
holomorphic in a neighborhood of each of
q_{k}
, we have

(5.7)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(q_{k})}v\,\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbf{E}\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}(q_{k})\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is a scalar with respect to
q_{k}
, we have

\mathbf{E}\mathcal{L}_{v}^{+}(q_{k})\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=v(q_{k})\partial_{q_{k}}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Moreover, it follows from
\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbf{E}[e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}]
that

(5.8)

\partial_{q_{k}}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\frac{i\beta_{k}}{2}\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}J(q_{k})\odot e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}.

On the other hand, by the residue calculus using (

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{(q_{k})}v\,\mathbf{E}A_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\frac{i\beta_{k}}{2}\,v(q_{k})\mathbf{E}J(q_{k})\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Thus all we need to show is

(5.9)

\mathbf{E}J(q_{k})\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}J(q_{k})\odot e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}.

By Wick’s calculus and (

), we have that for

J(q_{k}^{\prime})\odot e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}=J(q_{k}^{\prime})e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}+(\jmath(q_{k}^{\prime})-\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(q_{k}^{\prime}))e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}.

Therefore by (

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}J(q_{k}^{\prime})\odot e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}J(q_{k}^{\prime})e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}+(\jmath(q_{k}^{\prime})-\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(q_{k}^{\prime}))\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{0}}e^{\odot i\Phi[\boldsymbol{\beta}/2,\boldsymbol{\beta}/2]}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}J_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(q_{k}^{\prime})-\jmath_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(q_{k}^{\prime})\mathbf{E}\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbf{E}J(q_{k}^{\prime})\mathcal{X}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Now (
5.9
) follows by taking the limit
q_{k}^{\prime}\to q_{k}.

5.2.
Null-vector equations

Throughout this subsection, until further notice, we consider the case that all marked points are on the outer boundary component.
This allows us to simply consider the one-leg operator of the form
\Psi(z)=\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tau}\,,\,\boldsymbol{0}]
.
By (
5.4
), the associated partition function

Z(p)\big(\equiv Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})\equiv Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+\boldsymbol{\tau}]\big):=\mathbf{E}\Psi(p)

is evaluated in the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}
as

(5.10)

Z=\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\prod_{j}\Theta_{\chi}(r,p-\xi_{j})^{a\tau_{j}}\prod_{j}\Theta_{\chi}(r,p-q_{j})^{a\beta_{j}}\prod_{j<k}\Theta_{\chi}(r,\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})^{\tau_{j}\tau_{k}}\prod_{j,k}\Theta_{\chi}(r,\xi_{j}-q_{k})^{\tau_{j}\beta_{k}},

where
C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\Theta^{\prime}(r,0)^{\frac{a^{2}}{2}+\sum\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}}{2}}
. It also follows from the neutrality condition that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z&=\displaystyle C_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(ap+\int z\,d\boldsymbol{\tau}+\int z\,d\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}-(\int z\,d\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)\\
&\times\prod_{j}\Theta(r,p-\xi_{j})^{a\tau_{j}}\prod_{j}\Theta(r,p-q_{j})^{a\beta_{j}}\prod_{j<k}\Theta(r,\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})^{\tau_{j}\tau_{k}}\prod_{j,k}\Theta(r,\xi_{j}-q_{k})^{\tau_{j}\beta_{k}}.\end{split}

We write
\lambda_{p}
,
\lambda_{j}
for the (holomorphic) conformal dimension of
\Psi
with respect to
p
and
\xi_{j}
. Then by (
3.45
), we have

(5.12)

\lambda_{p}=\frac{6-\kappa}{2\kappa},\qquad\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}-\beta_{j}b.

It is worth to pointing out that by the neutrality condition, the sum of all the exponents (of theta functions) in the expression (
5.11
) vanishes, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}}{2}+\sum_{j}\frac{\tau_{j}^{2}}{2}+\sum_{j}a\tau_{j}+\sum_{k}a\beta_{k}+\sum_{j<k}\tau_{j}\tau_{k}+\sum_{j,k}\tau_{j}\beta_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(a+\sum_{j}\tau_{j}+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\Big)^{2}-\Big(\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\Big)^{2}=0.

Therefore it follows from asymptotic behaviors of theta function (see e.g.,
[
8
, pp.65-69]
)

(5.13)

\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}e^{r/4}\Theta(r,x)=2\sin\Big(\frac{x}{2}\Big),\qquad\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}e^{r/4}\Theta^{\prime}(r,0)=1

that the partition function
Z
has a non-trivial limit as
r\to\infty
. Indeed, the partition functions degenerate to (up to a multiplicative constant)

(5.14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\infty}:=\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}Z&=\prod_{j}\sin^{a\tau_{j}}\Big(\frac{p-\xi_{j}}{2}\Big)\prod_{j}\sin^{a\beta_{j}}\Big(\frac{p-q_{j}}{2}\Big)\\
&\times\prod_{j<k}\sin^{\tau_{j}\tau_{k}}\Big(\frac{\xi_{j}-\xi_{k}}{2}\Big)\prod_{j,k}\sin^{\tau_{j}\beta_{k}}\Big(\frac{\xi_{j}-q_{k}}{2}\Big).\end{split}

As a consequence of BPZ equation (Theorem
B
), we obtain a general form of the null-vector equation for the partition function

Z
. Here and subsequently,
\partial
and
′
stand for the differentiation with respect to
p
.

Corollary 5.6
.

The partition function
Z
satisfies

(5.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{r}Z=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial^{2}Z&+\sum_{j}\Big(\lambda_{j}H^{\prime}(r,p-\xi_{j})-H(r,p-\xi_{j})\partial_{\xi_{j}}\Big)Z-\sum_{j}H(r,p-q_{j})\partial_{q_{j}}Z+C(r,\boldsymbol{q})Z,\end{split}

where

(5.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C(r,\boldsymbol{q})&=-\dfrac{1}{16}\Big[\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H_{\chi}(r,q_{k}-p)+H_{\chi}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-p)\Big\}\Big]^{2}\\
&-\dfrac{b}{2}\,\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H^{\prime}_{\chi}(r,q_{k}-p)+H^{\prime}_{\chi}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-p)\Big\}-\frac{6}{\kappa}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2(r+\chi)}.\end{split}

Proof.

By applying trivial string
\mathcal{X}\equiv 1
to Theorem
B
, we have

\partial_{r}Z=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial^{2}Z-\mathcal{L}_{v_{p}}^{+}Z+C(r,\boldsymbol{q})Z,\qquad C(r,\boldsymbol{q})=-2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}-2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Now corollary follows from Proposition
3.5
and the fact that

\mathcal{L}_{v_{p}}^{+}Z=\sum\Big(v_{p}(\xi_{j})\partial_{\xi_{j}}+\lambda_{j}v^{\prime}_{p}(\xi_{j})\Big)Z+\sum v_{p}(q_{j})\partial_{q_{j}}Z.

∎

5.3.
BPZ-Cardy equations

In this subsection we prove BPZ-Cardy type equation, a key ingredient to connect CFT with SLE theory, and complete the proof of Theorem
C
.

Fix a marked point
p=\xi
on the outer boundary. Recall the definitions

Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}):=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}),\qquad\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}):=\kappa\,\partial_{z}\log Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}).

For a Fock space functional
\mathcal{X}
whose nodes do not intersect marked boundary points, set

R_{z}:=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{z}[\mathcal{X}]:=\frac{\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})\mathcal{X}]}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})},\qquad R_{\xi}:=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\xi}[\mathcal{X}]=\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Big[e^{\odot ia\Phi^{+}_{(0)}(\xi)-\sum\frac{\tau_{j}}{2}\tilde{\Phi}_{(0)}(\xi_{j})}\mathcal{X}\Big].

Let us denote

\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v_{z}}:=\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}(\bar{D}_{r}\setminus(\{z\}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}))
, i.e.,
\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v_{z}}\Psi Y(z)X:=\Psi Y(z)\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v_{z}}X
if the nodes of
\mathcal{X}
do not intersect
z
and
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
.

Now we prove the following BPZ-Cardy equation.

Proposition 5.7
.

For any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, in the
\mathcal{C}_{r}
-uniformization, we have

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\xi}R_{\xi}+\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\partial_{\xi}R_{\xi}=\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v_{\xi}}R_{\xi}+\Big(\sum v_{\xi}(\xi_{j})\partial_{\xi_{j}}+\bar{v}_{\xi}(\xi_{j})\bar{\partial}_{\xi_{j}}\Big)R_{\xi}+\partial_{r}R_{\xi}.

Here
\partial_{\xi}=\partial+\bar{\partial}
is the operator of differentiation with respect to the real variable
\xi
whereas
\partial_{\xi_{j}}
denotes the one with respect to the complex variable
\xi_{j}
.

Proof.

Let us write
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=S_{\mathcal{X}}
. Since
\Psi(z)
is a holomorphic differential with respect to
z
, we rewrite Theorem
B
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{z}\Big(R_{z}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{z}}}^{-}\Big)(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{q})R_{z}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})+\Big(\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)\Big)R_{z}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}).

By applying the trivial string
\mathcal{X}\equiv 1
to the above, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{z}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{z}}}^{-}\Big)(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{q})Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})+\Big(\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z)\Big)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z}).

Subtracting the above two equations, we obtain

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{z}R_{z}+\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(z,\boldsymbol{z})\partial_{z}R_{z}=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{z}}^{+}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\bar{z}}}^{-}\Big)(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{q})R_{z}+\partial_{r}R_{z}.

We now take the limit
z\to\xi
and
z_{j}\to\xi_{j}
. Since
\partial_{\xi}=\partial+\bar{\partial}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\xi}R_{\xi}+\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\partial_{\xi}R_{\xi}=\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{q})R_{\xi}+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})R_{\xi}+\partial_{r}R_{\xi}.

Now proposition follows from the fact
R_{\xi}
is a scalar field with respect to each
\xi_{j}
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
C
.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

Let us denote
R_{\xi}=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\xi}[\mathcal{X}]
. Then

M_{t}=m\Big(r-t,\xi_{t},\tilde{g}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\Big),\qquad m(r,\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi},t)=\Big(R(r,\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\|\tilde{g}_{t}^{-1}\Big).

Hence, by Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle dM_{t}=-\partial_{r}m\,dt+\partial_{\xi}m\,d\xi_{t}+\partial^{2}_{\xi}m\frac{d\langle\xi\rangle_{t}}{2}+(L_{t}R\|\tilde{g_{t}}^{-1})\,dt+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big[\partial_{\xi_{j}}m\,d\xi_{j}(t)+\bar{\partial}_{\xi_{j}}m\,d\overline{\xi_{j}(t)}\Big],

where

L_{t}:=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\Big(R(r-t,\xi_{t},\tilde{g}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\xi}))\|\tilde{g}_{t+s}^{-1}\Big).

Let
f_{s,t}=\tilde{g}_{t+s}^{-1}\circ\tilde{g}_{t}^{-1}
. Then since the time-dependent flow
f_{s,t}
satisfies the ODE

\frac{d}{ds}f_{s,t}=-v_{\xi_{t+s}}f_{s,t},\qquad v_{\xi}(z)=H(r,\xi-z),

we obtain

f_{s,t}=\text{id}-s\,v_{\xi_{t}}+o(s).

From the fact that the fields in
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
depends smoothly on local charts, it follows

L_{t}=-\Big(\check{\mathcal{L}}_{v_{\xi_{t}}}R_{\xi}\|\tilde{g}_{t}^{-1}\Big).

By the Loewner’s equation
d\xi_{j}(t)=-v_{\xi_{t}}(\xi_{j}(t))
, it follows from Proposition
5.7
that the drift term of
dM_{t}
vanishes. This completes the proof of theorem.
∎

6.
Further generalizations

In this section we discuss several methods to extend the class of SLE martingale-observables based on some intrinsic ideas from CFT.

6.1.
Non-atomic background charges

In
[
56
]
, Powell and Wu studied GFF with general Dirichlet boundary conditions and its coupling with SLE in a simply connected domain, see also a recent work
[
47
]
.
Intending to account for bosonic fields with such boundary conditions from the viewpoint of CFT, we implement general measure-valued background charges beyond the linear combination of the atomic masses.

Let
\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
be a (signed) measure on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
with total mass
-2b
and set

\boldsymbol{\beta}:=2b\cdot q+\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
where
q\in\mathbb{R}
.
We write
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}
for its discrete counterpart given by

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}:=2b\cdot q+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\beta_{k}}{n}\cdot q_{k},\qquad(\sum\beta_{k}=-2b).

Set
\boldsymbol{\tau}=-a\cdot q
and define the one-leg operator
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
as the continuum limit of its discrete counterpart, i.e.,

\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p-a\cdot q]:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}[a\cdot p-a\cdot q].

Let us also write

Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p-a\cdot q],\qquad Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}:=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}[a\cdot p-a\cdot q].

Denoting
q_{k}=s_{k}+ir
, by (
6.4
) below, we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&=\Theta^{\prime}(0)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Theta(p-q)^{1-\frac{6}{\kappa}}\exp\Big(a\int\log\frac{\Theta_{I}(p-s)}{\Theta_{I}(q-s)}\,d\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(s)\Big)\\
&\times\exp\Big[\Big(1-\frac{6}{k}\Big)\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}+a\frac{p-q}{4(r+\chi)}\int(p+q-2s)\,\,d\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(s)\Big]\end{split}

in the identity chart of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
. In the sequel, without loss of generality, let
p+q=0
.

We now derive the null-vector equation for
Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

The partition function
Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
satisfies

(6.2)

\partial_{r}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial_{p}^{2}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+H(p-q)\partial_{p}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+h_{1,2}H^{\prime}(p-q)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},

where

(6.3)

F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=-2\Big(\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p)+h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}\Big)-\frac{a}{2}\int\Big(H(p-q)-H_{I}(p-s)\Big)\Big(H_{I}(p-s)-H_{I}(q-s)+\frac{p-q}{r+\chi}\Big)\,d\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(s).

Here

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p)&=\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}+b^{2}\Big[H^{\prime}(p-q)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(H(p-q)+\frac{p-q}{r+\chi}\Big)^{2}\Big]\\
&+\dfrac{1}{8}\Big[\int\Big(H_{I}(p-s)+\frac{p-s}{r+\chi}\Big)\,d\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(s)\Big]^{2}+\dfrac{b}{2}\,\int H^{\prime}_{I}(p-s)\,d\check{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(s).\end{split}

Proof.

By (
5.11
), we have (up to a multiplicative constant)

(6.4)

Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}=\Theta(p-q)^{1-\frac{6}{\kappa}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{\Theta_{I}(p-s_{k})}{\Theta_{I}(q-s_{k})}\Big)^{\frac{a\beta_{k}}{n}}\exp\Big[\Big(1-\frac{6}{k}\Big)\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}+a\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\beta_{k}}{n}\frac{(p-q)(p+q-2s_{k})}{4(r+\chi)}\Big].

By Theorem
B
, the partition function
Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}
satisfies the following form of the null-vector equation

\displaystyle\partial_{r}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial_{p}^{2}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}-H(p-q)\partial_{q}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+h_{1,2}H^{\prime}(p-q)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}

\displaystyle-2\Big(\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}(p)+h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}\Big)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}-\sum H_{I}(p-s_{k})\partial_{s_{k}}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+i(\partial_{q_{k}}-{\bar{\partial}}_{q_{k}})Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}.

It follows from the translation invariance

(\partial_{p}+\partial_{q}+\sum\partial_{s_{k}})Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}=0

that this equation is rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{r}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial_{p}^{2}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+H(p-q)\partial_{p}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+h_{1,2}H^{\prime}(p-q)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}

\displaystyle-2\Big(\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}(p)+h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}\Big)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+\sum\Big(H(p-q)-H_{I}(p-s_{k})\Big)\partial_{s_{k}}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+i(\partial_{q_{k}}-{\bar{\partial}}_{q_{k}})Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}.

Note that by (

\frac{\partial_{q_{k}}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}}=-\frac{a}{2}\frac{\beta_{k}}{n}\Big(H_{I}(p-s_{k})-H_{I}(q-s_{k})+\frac{p-q}{r+\chi}\Big).

This gives rise to

\partial_{r}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial_{p}^{2}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+H(p-q)\partial_{p}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+h_{1,2}H^{\prime}(p-q)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}+F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}},

where

Furthermore, by Proposition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{n}}(p)&=\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}+b^{2}\Big[H^{\prime}(p-q)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(H(p-q)+\frac{p-q}{r+\chi}\Big)^{2}\Big]\\
&+\dfrac{1}{8}\Big[\sum\frac{\beta_{k}}{n}\Big(H_{I}(p-s_{k})+\frac{p-s_{k}}{r+\chi}\Big)\Big]^{2}+\dfrac{b}{2}\,\sum\frac{\beta_{k}}{n}H^{\prime}_{I}(p-s_{k}).\end{split}

Now proposition follows by letting

∎

at

For a general background charge

, we have (up to a multiplicative constant)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,q)&=\Theta(p-q)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(p-q+\sqrt{2}\int s\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s))^{2}}{8r}\Big)\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int\log\Big(\frac{\Theta_{I}(p-s)}{\Theta_{I}(q-s)}\Big)\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s)\Big).\end{split}

By Proposition

, the null-vector equation for

\partial_{r}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=2\,\partial_{p}^{2}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+H(p-q)\partial_{p}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\frac{1}{4}H^{\prime}(p-q)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2r}-\frac{1}{4}\Big[\int\Big(H_{I}(p-s)+\frac{p-s}{r}\Big)\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s)\Big]^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\int H_{I}(p-s)\Big(H_{I}(p-s)-H_{I}(q-s)+\frac{p-q}{r}\Big)\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s).\end{split}

Moreover, by (

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+\frac{1}{2r}-\frac{1}{4}\Big[\int\Big(H_{I}(p-s)+\frac{p-s}{r}\Big)\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s)\Big]^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\int\Big(H_{I}^{\prime}-\frac{H_{I}^{2}}{2}\Big)(p-s)+\Big(H_{I}^{\prime}+\frac{H_{I}^{2}}{2}\Big)(q-s)\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{p-q}{r}\int H_{I}(p-s)\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}(s).\end{split}

Remark

Note that if

6.2.
Periodization

In this section we present the implementation of weighted summation of the chiral conformal fields.
We refer to
[
64
]
for a similar idea (under the name of
chiral bosonization
) on compact Riemann surfaces.
This allows us to construct some important examples of SLE martingale-observables in the next section.

For a suitable weight function
\omega:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, let us define

\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\omega(n)\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p+2n\pi,\boldsymbol{\xi})

and write

Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}),\qquad\Lambda^{\omega}(r,p,\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\kappa\,\partial_{p}\log Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

We remark that in
[

]
, Zhan used the weight function

\omega(n):=e^{-\frac{2\pi s_{0}}{\kappa}n},\qquad(s_{0}\in\mathbb{R})

to construct partition functions having rotational periodicity.

Example
.

Let us consider the case that all
\xi_{j}
’s and
q_{k}
’s are on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
. Then up to a multiplicative constant,

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})&=\exp\Big(-\frac{(ap+\sum_{j}\tau_{j}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\xi_{j}+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\,\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k})^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)\\
&\times\prod_{j}\Theta_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,\xi_{j})^{a\tau_{j}}\prod_{j}\Theta_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,q_{j})^{a\beta_{j}}\prod_{j<k}\Theta(r,\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})^{\tau_{j}\tau_{k}}\prod_{j,k}\Theta(r,\xi_{j}-q_{k})^{\tau_{j}\beta_{k}}.\end{split}

Note that for
\chi<\infty
, the partition function
Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})
is not
2\pi
-periodic with respect to the space variable
p
.
Set
\omega(n)\equiv 1
. By (

), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})&=\Theta_{I}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(r+\chi),p+\sum_{j}\frac{\tau_{j}}{a}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\xi_{j}+\sum_{k}\frac{\beta_{k}}{a}\,\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k}+\pi\Big)\\
&\times\prod_{j}\Theta_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,\xi_{j})^{a\tau_{j}}\prod_{k}\Theta_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k})^{a\beta_{k}}\prod_{j<k}\Theta(r,\xi_{j}-\xi_{k})^{\tau_{j}\tau_{k}}\prod_{j,k}\Theta(r,\xi_{j}-q_{k})^{\tau_{j}\beta_{k}}\end{split}

and

(6.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(p,\boldsymbol{\xi})&=\frac{\kappa}{2}H_{I}\Big(\frac{\kappa}{2}(r+\chi),p+\sum_{j}\frac{\tau_{j}}{a}\,\mathrm{Re}\,\xi_{j}+\sum_{k}\frac{\beta_{k}}{a}\,\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k}+\pi\Big)\\
&+\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\Big[\sum_{j}\tau_{j}H_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,\xi_{j})+\sum_{k}\beta_{k}H_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k})\Big].\end{split}

Then it follows from the periodicity of

that

is well-defined in the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.

We now prove Theorem
D
.

Proof of Theorem
D
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a string of fields in the OPE family
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and set

R_{\xi}=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}\mathcal{X}:=\frac{\mathbf{E}\Psi^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}}{\mathbf{E}\Psi^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})}.

By the argument presented in the proof of Theorem
C
, it suffices to show the following form of BPZ-Cardy equation

(6.11)

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial_{\xi}^{2}R_{\xi}+\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}\partial_{\xi}R_{\xi}=\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}R_{\xi}+\partial_{r}R_{\xi}.

By Theorem
B
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\xi}\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}+\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\Big)\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}.

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\xi}\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}&=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}+\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}\Big)\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\omega}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}+2\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi+2n\pi)\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi+2n\pi,\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{X}.\end{split}

By Proposition
3.5
and the neutrality condition
\int\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
,

(6.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)&=\dfrac{1}{32}\Big[\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H(r,q_{k}-\xi)+H(r,\bar{q}_{k}-\xi)+2\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k}}{r+\chi}\Big\}\Big]^{2}\\
&+\dfrac{b}{4}\,\sum\beta_{k}\Big\{H^{\prime}(r,q_{k}-\xi)+H^{\prime}(r,\bar{q}_{k}-\xi)\Big\}+\dfrac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{2\pi}-\frac{1}{4(r+\chi)}.\end{split}

Thus we have for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(6.13)

\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi+2n\pi)=\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi),

which leads to

(6.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\xi}R_{\xi}Z^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}+\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\Big)R_{\xi}Z^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

By applying the trivial string
\mathcal{X}\equiv 1
to the above, we have the null-vector equation

(6.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial^{2}_{\xi}Z^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}+\partial_{r}+2h_{1,2}\frac{\zeta_{r}(\pi)}{\pi}+2\mathbf{E}T_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi)\Big)Z^{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\xi,\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Subtracting the above two equations, we obtain (
6.11
), which completes the proof.

∎

6.3.
Screening

From now on, we focus on an SLE
(\kappa,\Lambda)
starting from
p
with one force point
q
.
In this subsection we discuss the screening method to construct family of solutions to the null-vector equation

(6.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}Z=\frac{\kappa}{2}Z^{\prime\prime}+HZ^{\prime}+\Big(\frac{3}{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)H^{\prime}Z+C(r)Z

for the chordal type annulus partition function and prove Theorem
E
. The screening is a well-known method in CFT to find solutions of the Knizhnik-Zamolodchikov type equations, see e.g.,
[
63
,
21
]
.

In general, it has been turned out that the screening method is useful particularly in the theory of multiple SLEs. For instance, in
[
17
]
, Dubédat found Euler integral representations for solutions to a system of PDEs characterizing commuting SLEs in
\mathbb{H}
by means of screening. We refer the reader to
[
41
,
55
,
46
,
24
]
and references therein for recent studies on solutions to such PDE system and the geometric properties of associated multiple SLEs.

For
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
, we set

(6.17)

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\beta\cdot q_{1}-\beta\cdot q_{2}.

Here we assume that
q_{1},q_{2}
are two different fibers of a marked point in annulus satisfying
q_{2}=q_{1}+2\pi
.
For
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, write

(6.18)

\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(p,q,\zeta):=\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[a\cdot p+a\cdot q-2a\cdot\zeta,\,\boldsymbol{0}],\qquad Z_{\beta}^{\sharp}:=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}.

Then the conformal dimension of
\Psi^{\sharp}
at
p,q
and
\zeta
are given by

(6.19)

\lambda_{p}=\lambda_{q}=\frac{6-\kappa}{2\kappa},\qquad\lambda_{\zeta}=1.

By (

), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z^{\sharp}_{\beta}(p,q,\zeta)&=\Theta^{\prime}(0)^{\frac{6}{\kappa}}\Theta_{\chi}(p-q)^{\frac{2}{\kappa}}\Theta_{\chi}(p-\zeta)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\Theta_{\chi}(q-\zeta)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\Big|\frac{\Theta_{\chi}(p-z_{0})}{\Theta_{\chi}(p-z_{1})}\frac{\Theta_{\chi}(q-z_{0})}{\Theta_{\chi}(q-z_{1})}\frac{\Theta_{\chi}(\zeta-z_{1})^{2}}{\Theta_{\chi}(\zeta-z_{0})^{2}}\Big|^{a\beta}.\end{split}

In particular since
q_{2}-q_{1}=2\pi
and (

), this expression reduces to
(up to a multiplicative constant)

(6.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z^{\sharp}_{\beta}(p,q,\zeta)&=\Theta^{\prime}(0)^{\frac{6}{\kappa}}\exp\Big(\frac{\beta^{2}\pi^{2}}{r+\chi}\Big)\Theta(p-q)^{\frac{2}{\kappa}}\Theta(p-\zeta)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\Theta(\zeta-q)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(\Sigma-2\beta\pi)^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big),\end{split}

where
\Sigma=a(p+q-2\zeta)
.
For generic
\kappa>0
, we need to specify a principal branch (as a function of
\zeta
) in the expression (
6.20
). In what follows, we use the branch cut
[-\infty,q]\cup[p,\infty]
.

For a closed contour
\gamma
, set

\Psi_{\beta}\equiv\Psi_{\beta}(p,q):=C(\kappa)\oint_{\gamma}\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(p,q,\zeta)\,d\zeta,

where the normalization constant

is given by

(6.22)

C(\kappa)=\sin^{-2}\Big(\frac{4\pi}{\kappa}\Big)\,\frac{1}{4\,\Gamma(1-4/\kappa)}.

Here

is the Gamma function. It is easy to see that

has a simple pole at
\kappa
if and only if

is a positive integer. By (

), the partition function

Z_{\beta}\equiv Z_{\beta}(r,p-q):=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\beta}=C(\kappa)\oint_{\gamma}Z_{\beta}^{\sharp}(p,q,\zeta)\,d\zeta

is evaluated as (up to a multiplicative constant)

(6.24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{\beta}&=\Theta(p-q)^{\frac{2}{\kappa}}\oint_{\gamma}\Theta(p-\zeta)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\Theta(q-\zeta)^{-\frac{4}{\kappa}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(\Sigma-2\beta\pi)^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)\,d\zeta.\end{split}

Remark
.

In general, partition functions
Z_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
do not have periodic property with respect to
2\pi
except the case
\chi=\infty
(i.e., ER b.c.). On the other hand, for
\chi=0
(i.e., Dirichlet b.c.)

has following rotational periodicity with respect to

(6.25)

Z_{\beta}(r,x+2ir)=\exp\Big(a(2\beta-a)\pi i\Big)Z_{\beta}(r,x).

We denote by

the fundamental group of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{p,q\}
with base point
\zeta_{0}
.
If

is not a positive integer, in order to make the partition function

single-valued, the homotopy class

should reside in the commutator subgroup of the

. Moreover, this specific choice (

) of

makes the expression (

) non-trivial for all values of

after removing possible singularities. We will explain this in Proposition

below.

The simplest example of such

is the Pochhammer contour
\mathcal{P}(p,q)
entwining

and

See Figure

, where we indicate the branch cut as a dotted line.
It is an elementary but remarkable property that the winding number of

with respect to each point

as a single-valued analytic function on the contour.

Indeed, the Pochhammer contour is the typical example of path contained in the class of

or

, see

For any
\kappa>0

where

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle\partial_{r}Z_{\beta}^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial^{2}Z_{\beta}^{\sharp}+\Big[\Big(\frac{3}{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)H^{\prime}+H^{\prime}(p-\zeta)\Big]Z^{\sharp}_{\beta}-\Big(H\partial_{q}Z^{\sharp}_{\beta}+H(p-\zeta)\partial_{\zeta}Z^{\sharp}_{\beta}\Big)+C(r)Z^{\sharp}_{\beta}.

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial_{\xi}^{2}R_{\xi}+\Lambda\partial_{\xi}R_{\xi}=\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}R_{\xi}+\partial_{r}R_{\xi}.

By Theorem
B
, we have

(6.36)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(\xi,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}]&=\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}(q,S_{\mathcal{X}})\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(\xi,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}]+\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(\xi,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}]\\
&+(\partial_{r}-C(r))\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(\xi,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}],\end{split}

where
C(r)
is given as (
6.28
). Note that since
\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}
is a
(1,0)
-differential with respect to
\zeta
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}\Big[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(\xi,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}\Big]=\partial_{\zeta}\Big[v_{\xi}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(\xi,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}]\Big].

Therefore by (
6.26
),

\oint_{\mathcal{P}(p,q)}\mathcal{L}_{v_{p}}(\zeta)\mathbf{E}[\Psi_{\beta}^{\sharp}(p,q,\zeta)\mathcal{X}]\,d\zeta=0.

Integrating (
6.36
) with respect to
\zeta
along
\mathcal{P}(p,q)
, we obtain

(6.37)

\frac{1}{a^{2}}\partial_{\xi}^{2}\Big(Z_{\beta}R_{\xi}\Big)=\mathcal{L}_{v_{\xi}}\Big(Z_{\beta}R_{\xi}\Big)+(\partial_{r}-C(r))\Big(Z_{\beta}R_{\xi}\Big).

Combining (
6.37
) with Proposition
6.2
we conclude (
6.35
), which completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

For
\kappa=4
, let
\gamma
be a contour around
q
not encircling
p
and write

\Psi=\mathcal{O}[a\cdot p-a\cdot q],\qquad\Psi_{\gamma}=\int_{\gamma}\mathcal{O}[a\cdot p+a\cdot q-2a\cdot\zeta]\,d\zeta.

Then one can easily see that
\mathbf{E}\Psi=\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\gamma}=\Theta_{\chi}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

Thus the insertion of such one-leg operators produces martingale-observables for same SLE

.
However, we emphasize that as operators
\mathcal{X}\to\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
, they are not the same in general.

For instance, let us assume that
\gamma
encircles the node

. Then for ER boundary condition, we have

\frac{\mathbf{E}J(z)\Psi(p,q)}{\mathbf{E}\Psi(p,q)}=X(z):=w_{z}^{\prime}\Big(H(w_{p}-w_{z})-H(w_{q}-w_{z})\Big),

whereas

\frac{\mathbf{E}J(z)\Psi_{\gamma}(p,q)}{\mathbf{E}\Psi_{\gamma}(p,q)}=X(z)+Y(z),\qquad Y(z):=\frac{w_{z}^{\prime}\,\Theta^{\prime}(0)\Theta(w_{p}-w_{q})}{\Theta(w_{p}-w_{z})\Theta(w_{q}-w_{z})}.

Combining Theorems
C
and

7.
Examples of martingale-observables

This section is devoted to applying the theory in previous sections to construct martingale-observables associated with the scaling limits of lattice models. As a consequence, some examples are indicated as well.

7.1.
Bosonic observables

Since Schramm and Sheffield discovered the relation between level lines of discrete GFF and SLE
(4)
type curves
[

[

A key ingredient for the coupling of GFF and SLE in the continumm level is the associated bosonic observables. In this spirit, Izyurov and Kytölä proposed a general framework to develop Schramm-Sheffield’s coupling relations in various conformal setups including doubly connected domains with Dirichlet or Dirichlet-Neumann mixed boundary conditions with several force points
[

[

and references therein for further examples of GFF/SLE couplings in different conformal setups.

For
\kappa=4

Remark
.

For the Dirichlet boundary condition (i.e.,

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\eta

Proof.

By definition and (

\xi_{t}
of
\mathrm{SLE}(4,\Lambda)

d\xi_{t}=2\,dB_{t}-\Big(H(r-t,\xi_{t}-q_{t})+\frac{\xi_{t}-q_{t}-\mu}{r+\chi-t}\Big)\,dt,\qquad q_{t}:=\tilde{g}_{t}(q).

We denote by

up to time

On the other hand, due to Loewner’s equation,

dX_{t}:=2\,dB_{t}-\frac{X_{t}-\mu}{r+\chi-t}\,dt,\qquad X_{0}=x.

Therefore the process

is identified to a Brownian bridge starting from

[

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\chi}^{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\Pr\Big\{0<\min_{0\leq s\leq r}B(s)<\max_{0\leq s\leq r}B(s)<\pi\Big|B(0)=\frac{x}{2},B(r+\chi)=\frac{\mu}{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi(r+\chi)}\exp\Big(\frac{(\mu/2-x/2)^{2}}{2(r+\chi)}\Big)\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\chi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(z-\mu/2)^{2}}{2\chi}\Big)\,d\sigma(z),

\displaystyle d\sigma(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi r}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big[\exp\Big(-\frac{(x-2z+4n\pi)^{2}}{2r}\Big)-\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+2z+4n\pi)^{2}}{2r}\Big)\Big]\,dz.

Now proposition follows from (
3.4
).
∎

7.2.
LERW observables

In this subsection we present a way of constructing martingale-observables for variants of continuum loop-erased random walk (LERW). In the physics literature, a version of CFT constructed from sympletic fermions has been proposed for LERW, see e.g.,
[
48
,
2
,
7
]
and references therein.

In a doubly connected domain, one may consider variants of LERW by imposing boundary conditions on the inner boundary components.
For instance, let us consider a simple random walk on the lattice approximation of the annulus started from an interior vertex near the initial boundary point conditioned to hit a boundary vertex near the target point.
We assume that the random walk continues when it hits the inner circle, and stops when it visits the outer one.
Then after the loop-erasing procedure, the resulting curve is the LERW connecting two marked points on the outer boundary component, obeying the Neumann condition on the inner one.

For generic LERW, it is well known that the associated partition function
Z
is expressed as

(7.7)

Z(p,q)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial n_{\zeta}\partial n_{z}}\Big|_{\zeta=p,z=q}G(\zeta,z),

see e.g.,
[
66
, Section 4.5]
,
[
18
, Section 9]
and
[
49
]
. Here the Green’s function
G
obeys the boundary condition of LERW.

We now present a way of constructing LERW partition functions from the methods introduced in the previous sections. Then by Theorems
D
and
E
, the way of constructing the associated martingale-observables also follows.

Recall that for
4/\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, one can choose the integration contour
\gamma
as a circle encircles
p
or/and
q
in the expression of
Z^{\omega}_{\beta}
.
With such a choice of
\gamma
, by residue calculus, we obtain following two-parameter family of solutions
Z_{\beta}(r,x)\equiv Z_{\beta,\chi}(r,x)
to null-vector equation (
2.5
) for
\kappa=2
:

(7.8)

Z_{\beta}(r,x)=\Theta(r,x)^{-1}\exp\Big(-\frac{(x-2\beta\pi)^{2}}{4(r+\chi)}\Big)\Big(H(r,x)+\dfrac{x-2\beta\pi}{r+\chi}\Big).

Using the weight functions
\omega(n)=1,(-1)^{n}
and (
3.4
), we obtain following family of solutions having
2\pi
-(anti) periodicity:

(7.9)

Z_{\beta}^{\omega}(r,x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\Theta(r+\chi,x+\pi-2\beta\pi)}{\Theta(r,x)}\Big(H(r,x)-H(r+\chi,x+\pi-2\beta\pi)\Big),&\text{if}\quad\omega(n)=1,\\
\dfrac{\Theta_{I}(r+\chi,x+\pi-2\beta\pi)}{\Theta(r,x)}\Big(H(r,x)-H_{I}(r+\chi,x+\pi-2\beta\pi)\Big)&\text{if}\quad\omega(n)=(-1)^{n}.\end{cases}

We emphasize here that the
2\pi
-periodic partition functions
Z_{\beta}^{\omega}
are positive for all
\beta
. Let us also point out that such partition functions for
\beta\in\{0,b\}
with
\chi=0
correspond to those found by Zhan in
[
72
, Section 8.2]
.

7.2.1.
LERW partition functions

Let us now focus on the case with
\chi=0
. By (
6.25
), such
2\pi
-periodic partition functions further satisfy rotational periodicities with respect to
2ir
;

(7.10)

Z^{\omega}_{\beta}(r,x+2ir)=\exp\Big((2\beta-1)\pi i\Big)Z^{\omega}_{\beta}(r,x).

\bullet

LERW with Dirichlet/Riemann-Hilbert mixed boundary conditions.

Let
\omega(n)=1
. Then by (
7.9
), we have

(7.11)

Z_{\beta}^{\omega}(r,x)=\dfrac{\Theta(r,x+\pi-2\beta\pi)}{\Theta(r,x)}\Big(H(r,x)-H(r,x+\pi-2\beta\pi)\Big).

The partition function (
7.11
) describes the continuous LERW with
Riemann-Hilbert
boundary condition, i.e., the requirement that the derivative along the oblique direction (indexed by
\beta
) vanishes. This follows from (
7.7
) and the representation of the Green’s function satisfying Riemann-Hilbert boundary condition on the inner boundary component, see Appendix
A
.

It is well known that Riemann-Hilbert b.c. interpolates Neumann and ER conditions, see e.g.,
[
6
]
.
For
\beta=0
, we have an alternative expression

(7.12)

Z(r,x)=H^{\prime}(2r,x)-H_{I}^{\prime}(2r,x).

On the other hand, when
\beta\to b
, after normalization, we have

(7.13)

Z(r,x)=H^{\prime}(r,x).

These partition functions (
7.12
) and (
7.13
) correspond to the partition functions of LERW with Neumann and ER b.c., respectively.

\bullet

LERW with Dirichlet/Dirichlet boundary condition.

Let
\beta=b
and
\omega(n)=n
, i.e.,

(7.14)

\Psi_{\beta}^{\omega}(x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\Psi_{b}(x+2n\pi).

Then it is straightforward to check that

(7.15)

Z_{\beta}^{\omega}(r,x)=\tilde{H}^{\prime}(r,x)=H^{\prime}(r,x)+\frac{1}{r}.

This corresponds to the LERW partition function with Dirichlet boundary condition, which appears in
[
70
]
.

Let us pause here to briefly explain the derivation of (
7.15
).
Note that by (
7.8
),

\mathbf{E}\Psi_{b}(r,x)=\Theta(r,x)^{-1}\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big)\Big(H(r,x)+\dfrac{x+\pi}{r}\Big).

Therefore we have

\displaystyle Z_{\beta}^{\omega}(r,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{H(r,x)}{\Theta(r,x)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}\,n\,\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+\pi+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\Theta(r,x)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}\,n\,\Big(\frac{x+\pi+2n\pi}{r}\Big)\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+\pi+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big).

It follows from (
3.4
) that

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}\,n\,\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+\pi+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big)=\frac{r}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{r}{\pi}}\Big(\Theta^{\prime}(r,x)+\frac{x+\pi}{2r}\Theta(r,x)\Big).

Combining this identity with (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we obtain (up to a multiplicative constant)

\displaystyle Z_{\beta}^{\omega}(r,x)

\displaystyle=H(r,x)\Big(\frac{\Theta^{\prime}(r,x)}{\Theta(r,x)}+\frac{x+\pi}{2r}\Big)-\Big(2\frac{\Theta^{\prime\prime}(r,x)}{\Theta(r,x)}+\frac{1}{r}+\frac{x+\pi}{r}\frac{\Theta^{\prime}(r,x)}{\Theta(r,x)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(r,x)+\frac{x+\pi}{2r}H(r,x)\Big)-\Big(H^{\prime}(r,x)+\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(r,x)+\frac{1}{r}+\frac{x+\pi}{2r}H(r,x)\Big),

which leads to (
7.15
).

Remark
.

In parallel, one can construct such partition functions for the crossing case that
p\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}_{r}
. In particular, for
\beta=b,\chi=0
with the choice of weight function
\omega(n)=n

which corresponds to Zhan’s Feynmann-Kac solution, see
[

.

\bullet

LERW aiming at a side arc.
Due to the special feature

at the “target point”

For a subset

.

In particular, when

7.2.2.

Let
\Psi

\bullet

Generalized Poisson kernel.
For simplicity we only consider the case that target set is a boundary point

\mathcal{O}:=\mathcal{O}[a\cdot z-a\cdot q]

Let us define

For the Dirichlet boundary condition, Zhan considered the following bounded martingale (cf. (

[

]
.

\bullet

Set
I=[q_{1}+ir,q_{2}+ir]\subset\mathbb{R}_{r}

\Psi
for the one-leg operator associated with the standard SLE(2).
Set

\mathcal{O}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{I}\mathcal{O}[a\cdot z-a\cdot q]\,dq.

M(z)=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}\mathcal{O}=\frac{\mathbf{E}\Psi\mathcal{O}}{\mathbf{E}\Psi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{q_{1}}^{q_{2}}\Big(rH_{I}^{\prime}(r,p-q)+1\Big)\,dq

, see

.

7.3.

In this subsection we focus on the case

The null-vector equation for

This corresponds to the partition function for the critical Ising interface studied by Izyurov
[

. Therefore the insertion of the field
\Psi

Since the interface curve satisfies reversibility, it follows from Izyurov’s convergence result

2.5
) with
\kappa=3
.
Indeed this also follows from the martingale property of parafermionic observable in the continuum limit.
See

Here we present a field theoretical method to show that
Z
satisfies (
2.5
).

Proposition 7.2
.

The partition function
Z
in (
7.23
) satisfies the null-vector equation (
2.5
) with
\kappa=3
.

Proof.

By (
7.20
), all we need to show is

(7.24)

\frac{1}{Z(r,x)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}F_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(x+2n\pi)Z_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(r,x+2n\pi)

is a constant depending only on the modular parameter
r
.
By differentiating (
3.4
), we have

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{x+2n\pi}{2r}\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big)=-\sqrt{\frac{r}{\pi}}\Theta_{I}^{\prime}(r,x+\pi)

and

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{(x+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r^{2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(x+2n\pi)^{2}}{4r}\Big)=\sqrt{\frac{r}{\pi}}\Big(\Theta_{I}^{\prime\prime}(r,x+\pi)+\frac{1}{2r}\Theta_{I}(r,x+\pi)\Big).

Using these identities, we obtain that (

\frac{1}{2}H(r,x)H_{I}(r,x+\pi)-\Big(\frac{H_{I}^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{H_{I}^{2}}{4}\Big)(r,x+\pi)-\Big(\frac{H_{I}^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{H_{I}^{2}}{4}\Big)(r,x+\pi)-\frac{1}{2r}.

Now the proposition follows from (

∎

7.4.
Annulus SLE with one marked point

One can use the implementation of the measure-valued charges to construct martingale-observables for annulus SLE with one marked point. A typical example of a such process is called the standard annulus SLE(
\kappa
)
[
66
]
with driving process
\xi_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,B_{t}
.

For
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
q_{k}:=q-\pi+2\pi\,\frac{k}{n}
, where
q\in\mathbb{R}_{r}
. We then consider

(7.26)

\Psi_{n}(p)=\mathcal{O}\Big[a\cdot p-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a}{2n}\cdot q_{k},-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a}{2n}\cdot q_{k}\Big].

By (
5.5
), it follows that the associated drift function
\Lambda_{n}
is given by

(7.27)

\Lambda_{n}(p)=-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{\chi}(r,p-q_{k})+H_{\chi}(p-\bar{q}_{k})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{I}(r,p-\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k})+\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,q_{k}-p}{r+\chi}.

We denote

(7.28)

\Psi(p):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Psi_{n}(p)=\mathcal{O}\Big[a\cdot p-\frac{a}{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{q},-\frac{a}{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}\Big],\qquad\Lambda:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,q-p}{r+\chi},

where
\boldsymbol{q}
is the uniform measure on
[q-\pi,q+\pi]
for
q\in\mathbb{R}_{r}
. Here we use the
2\pi
-periodicity of
H_{I}(r,\cdot)
.
By Theorem
C
, the insertion of the one-leg operator
\Psi(p)
produces martingale-observables for annulus SLE driven by

(7.29)

d\xi_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dB_{t}+\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,q-\xi_{t}}{r+\chi-t}\,dt,\qquad\xi_{0}=p.

Notice that this corresponds to the law of the Brownian bridge.
In particular, for the ER b.c. case when
\chi=\infty
(thus
\Lambda\equiv 0
), it corresponds to the standard SLE
(\kappa)
. On the other hand, for the Dirichlet b.c. case when
\chi=0
, the SLE trace ends at the point
q
.

Example

and

where

Appendix A
Representation of Green’s function

In this appendix we present an analytic representation of Green’s function in a doubly connected domain with various boundary conditions.

For a parameter
\beta\in(-1/2,1/2)
, we say a function
F
defined in a domain
\bar{D}
satisfies RH
β
boundary condition (b.c.) on
l\subset\partial D
if

(A.1)

\Big[\cos(\beta\pi)\partial_{n}-\sin(\beta\pi)\partial_{\tau}\Big]F(\cdot)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\hskip 5.0ptl\subset\partial D.

Here
\partial_{n}
is the (inwards) normal derivative and
\partial_{\tau}
is the tangential one. In particular, the case
\beta=0
corresponds to the Neumann b.c.

Let us define

(A.2)

f_{\beta}(r,z):=\frac{\Theta^{\prime}(r,0)}{\Theta(r,\pi-2\beta\pi)}\frac{\Theta(r,z+\pi-2\beta\pi)}{\Theta(r,z)}.

By the quasi-periodicities (
3.7
) of the theta function, we have

(A.3)

f_{\beta}(r,z+2\pi)=f_{\beta}(r,z),\qquad f_{\beta}(r,z+2ir)=e^{i(2\beta-1)\pi}f_{\beta}(r,z).

Since
\Theta(r,\cdot)
is an odd function,

(A.4)

f_{\beta}(r,z)=-f_{-\beta}(r,-z).

On the other hand,
f_{\beta}
satisfies

(A.5)

f_{\beta}(r,z)=\frac{1}{z}+\frac{H(r,\pi-2\beta\pi)}{2}+O(z),\qquad\text{as }z\to 0.

Thus one can observe that
f_{0}
has an alternative expression

(A.6)

f_{0}(r,z)=\frac{1}{2}(H(2r,z)-H_{I}(2r,z)).

It is also easy to show that
f_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
is a conformal map from the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{r}
to the upper half-plane minus a slit whose argument is
(1/2-\beta)\pi
.

For given
w\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
, a meromorphic function

(A.7)

g_{\beta}(z):=f_{\beta}(r,z-\bar{w})-f_{\beta}(r,z-w)

satisfies following properties:

•

g_{\beta}(z+2\pi)=g_{\beta}(z)
,
g_{\beta}(r,z+2ir)=e^{i(2\beta-1)\pi}g_{\beta}(r,z)
;

•

g_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
has simple poles at
w
(resp.,
\bar{w}
) with residue
-1
(resp.,
1
);

•

g_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
maps
\mathbb{R}
to
i\mathbb{R}
;

•

g_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
maps
\mathbb{R}_{r}
to a line passing through 0 with angle
\beta
with
\mathbb{R}
.

All of these properties are immediate consequences of (
A.3
), (
A.4
) and (
A.5
). For instance, the last property follows from that for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle g_{\beta}(x+ir)

\displaystyle=\overline{f_{\beta}(r,x-ir-w)}-f_{\beta}(r,x+ir-w)

\displaystyle=e^{i(2\beta-1)\pi}\overline{f_{\beta}(r,x+ir-w)}-f_{\beta}(r,x+ir-w)

\displaystyle=-e^{i\beta\pi}\Big(e^{i\beta\pi}\overline{f_{\beta}(r,x+ir-w)}+e^{-i\beta\pi}f_{\beta}(r,x+ir-w)\Big)\in e^{i\beta\pi}\mathbb{R}.

We denote by
F_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
a primitive of
f_{\beta}(r,\cdot)
, i.e.,

\partial_{z}F_{\beta}(r,z)=f_{\beta}(r,z)
.
To our knowledge, for general
\beta
, there is no known expression for
F_{\beta}(r,z)
in terms of well-known special functions. On the other hand, by (
A.6
), we have

(A.8)

F_{0}(r,z)=\log\Big(\frac{\Theta(2r,z)}{\Theta_{I}(2r,z)}\Big)

up to an additive constant.

We now present Green’s function
G_{\beta}
in
\mathcal{C}_{r}
with zero Dirichlet b.c. on
\mathbb{R}
which satisfies RH
β
condition on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
. The associated (non-symmetric) stochastic process is called
obliquely reflected Brownian motion
(ORBM), see
[
6
]
and references therein. We remark that ORBM gives a geometric interpolation between reflected Brownian motion (
\beta=0
) and ERBM (
\beta\to\pm 1/2
).

We claim that Green’s function
G_{\beta}
in
\mathcal{C}_{r}
with zero Dirichlet b.c. on
\mathbb{R}
which satisfies RH
β
condition on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
is expressed as

(A.9)

G_{\beta}(z_{1},z_{2})=\mathrm{Re}\,[F_{\beta}(r,z_{1}-\bar{z}_{2})-F_{\beta}(r,z_{1}-z_{2})].

In particular, by (
A.8
), Green’s function
G_{0}
with Dirichlet-Neumann mixed boundary condition is given by

(A.10)

G_{0}(z_{1},z_{2})=\log\Big|\frac{\Theta/\Theta_{I}(2r,z_{1}-\bar{z}_{2})}{\Theta/\Theta_{I}(2r,z_{1}-z_{2})}\Big|.

To show (
A.9
), it suffices to check the followings:

•

G_{\beta}(z_{1},z_{2})+\log|z_{1}-z_{2}|
is harmonic function in both variables;

•

G_{\beta}(z_{1},z_{2})=0
if
z_{1}
or
z_{2}
is on
\mathbb{R}
;

•

G_{\beta}(\cdot,z_{2})
satisfies RH
β
condition on
\mathbb{R}_{r}
.

All of these requirements easily follow from the properties presented above. We remark that
G_{\beta}
satisfies the asymmetric relation
G_{\beta}(z_{1},z_{2})=G_{-\beta}(z_{2},z_{1})
.
