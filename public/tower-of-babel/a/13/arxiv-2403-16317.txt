Optimization on a Finer Scale: Bounded Local Subgradient Variation Perspective
Jelena Diakonikolas, Cristóbal Guzmán

Abstract
We initiate the study of nonsmooth optimization problems under bounded local subgradient variation, which postulates bounded difference between (sub)gradients in small local regions around points, in either average or maximum sense. The resulting class of objective functions encapsulates the classes of objective functions traditionally studied in the optimization literature, which are defined based on either Lipschitz continuity of the objective or Hölder/Lipschitz continuity of the function’s gradient. Further, the defined class is richer in the sense that it contains functions that are neither Lipschitz continuous nor have a Hölder continuous gradient. Finally, when restricted to the aforementioned traditional classes of optimization problems, the constants defining the studied classes lead to more fine-grained oracle complexity bounds. Some highlights of our results are that: (i) it is possible to obtain complexity results for both convex and nonconvex optimization problems with (local or global) Lipschitz constant being replaced by a constant of local subgradient variation, corresponding to small local regions and (ii) complexity of the subgradient set around the set of optima – measured by its mean width in a local region around optima – plays a role in the complexity of nonsmooth optimization, particularly in parallel optimization settings. A consequence of (ii) is that for any error parameter ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , parallel oracle complexity of nonsmooth Lipschitz convex optimization is lower than its sequential oracle complexity by a factor Ω ~ ​ ( 1 ϵ ) \tilde{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big) whenever the objective function is piecewise-linear with the number of pieces polynomial in the dimension and 1 / ϵ . 1/\epsilon. This is particularly surprising considering that existing parallel complexity lower bounds are based on such classes of functions. The seeming contradiction is resolved by considering the region in which the algorithm is allowed to query the objective.

Nonsmooth optimization problems pose some of the most intricate challenges within the realm of continuous optimization.
As a result, they have been intensively studied from the algorithm design perspective since at least the 1960s

1.2
Main Results

Our main findings are summarized as follows.

Local subgradient variation as a measure of complexity

The main contribution of this work is initiating the study of new classes of objectives in the context of (convex and nonconvex) nonsmooth and (weakly) smooth optimization. These classes of objectives are called
bounded maximum local variation of subgradients
(or BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
, for short) and
bounded mean local oscillation of subgradients
(or BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
, for short), and are introduced in Section
3
. BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
is the weaker of these two properties, in the sense that it is implied by BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
, while the converse does not hold in general. We provide different characterizations of structural properties of functions under these two notions of local subgradient variation and systematically investigate the complexity of classes of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
problems, with the focus on demonstrating that these weak regularity properties suffice for obtaining a more fine-grained characterization of oracle complexity. Notably, because BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
functions are not necessarily (globally) Lipschitz continuous (but all Lipschitz-continuous functions are both BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
with a constant at most 2 times larger and possibly much smaller), on a conceptual level, our results demonstrate that weaker properties than Lipschitz continuity suffice for tractability of optimization.

Deterministic convex optimization under BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}

We begin our discussion of oracle upper bounds by treating BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
functions as approximately smooth functions. While this idea is not new and was used in
[
17
,
50
]
as a means of handling weakly smooth functions in a universal manner, the prior work has only considered the settings in which such properties hold in a
global
sense, between any pair of points. By contrast, we demonstrate that only a
local
such property, assumed to hold only between points at distance at most
r>0
, suffices. As a result, we obtain results that are similar to those in
[
17
,
50
]
, but with a parameter that is potentially much lower than the Lipschitz/weak smoothness constants from prior work, thus providing a more fine-grained characterization of oracle complexity.

Randomized and possibly parallel convex optimization under BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}

1.3
Related Work

The complexity of continuous optimization is an actively investigated problem since the 1970s
[
47
]
. One of the main achievements of this theory is the precise quantification of the minimax optimal rates of convergence for smooth and nonsmooth convex optimization. However, as argued earlier, this coarse parameterization based on the maximum Lipschitz constant (either for the objective or its gradient) misses much of the information that determines the difficulty of performing optimization. The goal of our work is to bridge this gap. Below we review various threads of research that are related to our work.

Smoothing approaches

Both randomized and deterministic smoothing approaches have been widely used in nonlinear optimization for a long time and in different contexts
[

,
4
,
53
,

,

,

,

6

,

,
49
,
51
]
. The idea of smoothing is a natural one: approximate a nonsmooth function by a smooth one and then apply methods for smooth minimization to the smoothed function. Some of the most basic examples are the use of Moreau envelope for deterministic methods (see, e.g.,
[
43
,
57
]
), which requires access to a proximal point oracle, and local randomized smoothing using a Gaussian kernel or uniform distribution on a ball or a sphere
[
60
,
47
,
21
,
51
,
20
]
(all these kernels lead to very similar results, due to concentration properties of these distributions in high dimensions; see, e.g.,
[
5
, Chapter 2]
).
We note that the goal of our work is not to devise new smoothing approaches (in fact, we rely on a very simple randomized smoothing over a Euclidean ball), but to demonstrate usefulness of the introduced BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
concept in proving oracle complexity upper bounds.

Local Lipschitzness and Relaxations

Much of the recent literature on nonsmooth optimization (e.g.,
[
16
,
15
,
31
]
) replaces the global Lipschitz condition by a local one, which posits Lipschitz continuity on compact sets and implies bounded subgradients on those sets. This is based on the insight that many optimization algorithms ensure that their iterates remain on a compact set around optima, thus how a function behaves outside this set is irrelevant for optimization. An alternative definition is that of Lipschitz continuity in local neighborhoods of points
[
13
, Chapter 1]
; however, this definition seems to have been primarily used to define and study generalized notions of derivatives rather than oracle complexity of optimization.

We further highlight the following works which addressed nonsmooth problems that are not necessarily Lipschitz-continuous. In
[
25
]
, a variant of projected subgradient method with normalized subgradients was analyzed, motivated by insights from
[
58
]
. This work shows that the rate of convergence
1/\sqrt{k}
can be established assuming that there is a nondecreasing nonnegative mapping
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that
f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\mathcal{D}(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2})
where
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is a fixed minimizer of
f
and the complexity results are expressed in terms of this mapping. Such an assumption removes the requirement for regularity such as Lipschitz continuity to hold on a compact set between
any
pair of points, but still requires at least some bounded growth condition to hold on the entire feasible set or on a sufficiently large ball around
\mathbf{x}_{*}
. Another line of work
[
56
,
24
]
develops a generic transformation from nonsmooth non-Lipschitz convex problems to convex Lipschitz problems and an algorithmic framework to address them at the cost of a simple line search (but removing projections in constrained settings) and with an error guarantee of the form
\frac{f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})}{f(\mathbf{x}_{0})+c-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})}\leq\epsilon,
where
c>0
is a parameter of the algorithm. This corresponds to a multiplicative error guarantee for the shifted function
f(\cdot)-f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-c,
but on the original problem the error is additive and equal to
(f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})+c)\epsilon.
The resulting complexity bound replaces the usual dependence on the Lipschitz constant in traditional oracle complexity bounds by the inverse of a parameter defined by
R=\sup\{r\in\mathbb{R}:f(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})+c\text{ for all }\mathbf{x}\text{ with }\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\leq r\}
. Given a fixed
c,
for Lipschitz functions this constant is bounded by
c/M
, where
M
is the Lipschitz constant of
f
on the sublevel set
\{\mathbf{x}:f(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})+c\}.
More generally, for the result to be informative, one needs to bound the growth of
f
on a sufficiently large neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
intersected with the sublevel set
\{\mathbf{x}:f(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})+c\}.
In summary, all the results using notions of local Lipschitzness (and related concepts) that we are aware of require bounding the
growth
of the function in a possibly small region. By contrast, our results rely on bounding the
subgradient variation
in local regions.

Stronger oracles

Improved complexity results for nonsmooth optimization are possible if access to additional oracles or structure of the problem is accessible to the algorithm. For example, there is vast literature on methods utilizing the proximal point oracle (see, e.g.,

and

Another example in the recent literature is the ball optimization oracle
[

\min_{\mathbf{x}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\leq r}f(\mathbf{x})
for any
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and some
r>0.
Further, there are multiple results assuming that the objective function can be expressed as a structured maximization problem (e.g., arising from the convex conjugate of a function

composed with a linear map
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}

[
49
,
45
,

]
or the objective simply being a maximum of
N
smooth or nonsmooth functions
[

) and where one is given oracle access to components of the said maximization problem (such as proximal point oracle access to the convex conjugate of
F

or first-order oracle access to component functions in the case where

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{*}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2MD_{h}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})}}{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|}

Finally, our work leverages existing algorithms and techniques in convex optimization

Bounded Local Variation of the Subgradient

In this section, we provide local regularity assumptions for the subgradient that define the complexity class that we study in this work. The two notions are: (i) bounded maximum local variation and (ii) bounded mean oscillation. We later argue that these two (local) assumptions are sufficient for obtaining upper complexity bounds. Both properties are defined for a fixed radius
r.
We later discuss how this radius can be chosen or estimated.
The motivation for the considered notions of local variability is illustrated in examples provided in Figure
1
, as discussed in the introduction.

3.1
Bounded Maximum Local Variation

Bounded maximum local variation requires that the subgradient of a function does not change much over small regions, although it is possible for each of the subgradients to have a large norm. Formally,

Definition 3.1

(BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
)
.

Given
r>0,
we say that a locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
has bounded maximum local variation of the subgradient in norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
(is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}

\widehat{L}_{r}<\infty
such that

(\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})(\forall\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{B}):\;\;\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq\widehat{L}_{r}.

Observe that if
f
were (globally)
L
-Lipschitz continuous, then
\widehat{L}_{r}\leq 2L.
However, it is possible for
\widehat{L}_{r}
to be much smaller than

For example, a univariate function
f

\frac{1}{2}
for
|x|<1
and equal to
\frac{1}{2}x^{2}

(Observe that the “local” Lipschitz constant as in

thus it is clearly nonsmooth.

Then,
f
is said to be a BMO function if

\|f\|_{\rm BMO}:=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},r>0}\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}}|f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}<\infty,

(3.1)

where
\operatorname{vol}
denotes the volume and

f_{r}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}}f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}.

(3.2)

The (semi-)norm
\|\cdot\|_{\rm BMO}
is referred to as the BMO norm.

Note that under minimal assumptions (e.g., local Lipschitzness),
f_{r}
is differentiable.
The integral
\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}}|f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}
is known as the mean oscillation of
f
over
\mathcal{B}_{r}
.
The above definition is often stated for the unit ball w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty},
i.e., by defining BMO functions as functions with bounded oscillations
\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{r}}|f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x})|\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}
over hypercubes. However, using the definition as ours is not uncommon in the literature and the definitions using different norms are all equivalent (though the value of the resulting BMO norms may differ)
[
63
,
59
]
. In this work, we focus on the Euclidean case, where
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{2}.
All bounded functions are BMO.

Gradient BMO Functions

The definition of BMO functions is not directly useful in our setting, for two reasons: (1) the bounded oscillation is defined with respect to the function value, whereas in our case it is the slope (or the subgradient) whose changes with respect to small perturbations determine how close a function is to its smoothed approximation (see Figure
1
); and (2) BMO is a global property of functions, whereas we are interested in small, local changes in the slope (or the subgradient). Accounting for these two issues, we introduce the following definition of (sub)gradient
r
-BMO functions.

Definition 3.3

(BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function. We say that
f
is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
if there exist
r>0
and
L_{r}<\infty
such that

\|\nabla f\|_{{\rm BMO},r}:=\sup_{0<\rho\leq r}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{S}_{\rho})}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}\leq L_{r},

(3.3)

where
f_{r}
is defined by (
3.2
) and
\mathcal{S}_{\rho}=\{\mathbf{x}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\rho\}
.
In this case, we also say that
f
is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
with constant
L_{r}.

BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
property plays a role in local smoothing of a function. Intuitively, functions with lower BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
constants (for the same
r
) are closer to their smooth approximations obtained using local smoothing (such as randomized smoothing over small balls used in this work). Our main insight is that this property, together with the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
from Definition
3.1
, allows us to characterize complexity of nonsmooth optimization problem classes at a finer scale. For illustration, recall the two functions shown in Fig.
1
. Both these functions are nonsmooth (in fact, both are piecewise-linear) with the same Lipschitz constant. However, the right function has a smaller BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
constant
L_{r}
for a sufficiently small radius indicated on the right subfigure. Even though both functions belong to the same class of nonsmooth Lipschitz functions, visually, the right functions is “closer to being smooth,” as the transitions between the linear pieces have less dramatic changes in the slope.

For our results, we impose a bound on the oscillation at multiple scales, and that naturally introduces the supremum over
\rho
in the range
(0,r]
. This requirement comes from the need to simultaneously bound
\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{S}_{r})}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{r}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}
(in e.g., Lemma
3.8
) and
\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{B}_{\rho})}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\rho}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}
for
\rho\in(0,r]
(in e.g., Lemma
3.5
). Both these quantities can be bounded by
\sup_{0<\rho\leq r}\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{S}_{\rho})}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}
as in (
3.3
).

Randomized Local Smoothing

Observe that, given
r>0
, if we consider the uniform distribution
\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})
on the centered Euclidean ball of radius
r,

\mathcal{B}_{r}=r\mathcal{B},
then we can equivalently define the smoothed function
f_{r}
from (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle f_{r}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}[f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})].

(3.4)

This observation gives rise to the use of randomized smoothing, where we can obtain an unbiased estimate of the (sub)gradient of
f_{r}
using one of the two following ideas. The first is simply using
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})
, where
\mathbf{u}
is drawn uniformly at random from
\mathcal{B}_{r}
: this results in an unbiased estimate by the dominated convergence theorem. The second is
f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u},
where
\mathbf{u}
is drawn uniformly at random from the sphere of radius one. This is a valid unbiased estimate as a consequence of Stokes theorem, summarized in the lemma below. The proof of the lemma can be found in
[
47
, Chapter 9]
and in
[
21
]
, and is thus omitted for brevity.

Lemma 3.4
.

Given
r>0,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}[f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}]=\frac{r}{d}\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}).

(3.5)

We first show that for BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
convex functions with small constant
L_{r}
, the smoothed function
f_{r}
is close to the original function
f
, which aligns well with our intuition from Fig.
1
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function that is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
with constant
L_{r}
and let
f_{r}
be defined by (
3.4
). Then, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\leq L_{r}r.

Additionally, if
f
is convex, then
f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

The second claim (for convex functions) follows from Jensen’s inequality. For the first claim, we start by using the definition of
f_{r}
and the fundamental theorem of calculus (see Theorem
2.2
) applied to
f
to conclude that

\displaystyle f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+tr\mathbf{u}),r\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg].

Because
\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})
is centrally symmetric, we have that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\left\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle]=0
for any fixed vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\in\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\left\langle\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle]=0
and thus we can write

\displaystyle|f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle=r\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+tr\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}r\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+tr\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}r\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+tr\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{=}}r\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\big[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+tr\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iv)}}{{\leq}}rL_{r},

where (
i
) is by Jensen’s inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz, (
ii
) is by
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{B}
, so
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq 1,
(
iii
) is by Fubini’s theorem,
and (
iv
) follows from Definition
3.3
.
∎

It is possible to obtain a tighter bound on the distance between
f
and
f_{r}
under an additional assumption about the subgradients of
f.
This result is summarized in the following lemma and it will be particularly useful for obtaining near-dimension-independent convergence results in the parallel optimization setting.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f
be a locally Lipschitz function. Then for almost all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\leq r\,w(\partial_{r}f(\mathbf{x})),

where
w(K)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}{({\cal S})}}[\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in K}\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\rangle]
denotes the mean width of a set
K
.

Proof.

For a generic
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that
f
is differentiable at
\mathbf{x}
. Hence, using the first theorem of calculus and the central symmetry of
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, we have

\displaystyle f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}[f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{u})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg].

Thus, letting
K=\partial_{r}f(\mathbf{x})
we can further conclude that

\displaystyle f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{r})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2}\in K}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{1}-\mathbf{g}_{2},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle=r\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\Big[\sup_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2}\in K}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{1}-\mathbf{g}_{2},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=rd\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}({\cal S})}\Big[\sup_{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2}\in K}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{1}-\mathbf{g}_{2},\tau\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\Big]\tau^{d-1}\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.6)

\displaystyle=rw(K)d\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{d}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d+1}rw(K),

where in (
3.6
) we used integration by polar coordinates.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

For discussions on the mean width, and the closely related Gaussian width, we refer the interested reader to
[
62
, Section 7.5]
. We provide some useful examples of mean width bounds from this reference:

(i)

Euclidean ball:
w(\mathcal{B})=1
.

(ii)

Cube:
w([-1,+1]^{d})=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
.

(iii)

Polytopes: If
K
is a polytope with
k
vertices, then
w(K)=O\Big(\mbox{diam}(K)\sqrt{\frac{\log(k)}{d}}\Big)
.

The last example is particularly important. Many problems of interest in convex optimization can be formulated as (or approximated by) the maximum of finitely-many affine functions. In that case, the
1/\sqrt{d}
factor in the mean width bound provides a much more benign approximation than the worst-case bound for Lipschitz functions, corresponding to example (i).

We now argue about the smoothness of the smoothed function
f_{r}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
function with constant
\widehat{L}_{r}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
with constant
L_{r},
where both constants are defined w.r.t. the same fixed radius
r>0.
Let
f_{r}
be defined by (
3.2
). Then, for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\|\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\leq\min\Big\{\frac{L_{r}d}{r},\,\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}\sqrt{d}}{r}\Big\}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}.

Proof.

For the first bound in the min, we use Lemma
3.4
, as follows.

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{r}\big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\big[\big(f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{y}+r\mathbf{u})\big)\mathbf{u}\big]\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{r}\bigg\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\mathbf{u}\bigg]\bigg\|_{2},

where we have used the first theorem of calculus. Further,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}[c\mathbf{u}]=\textbf{0},
for any constant
c,
as
\mathbf{u}
is centrally symmetric. Hence, we can further write

\displaystyle\;\|\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\bigg\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\mathbf{u}\bigg]\bigg\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\bigg\|\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\mathbf{u}\bigg\|_{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}|\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle|\mathrm{d}t\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))\|_{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})))\|_{2}\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{L_{r}d\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}{r},

where we have used Jensen’s inequality (twice),
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1
for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{S}
, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and Definition
3.3
.

For the second bound, we use the following sequence of inequalities with
\mathbf{z}:=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\Big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}[(f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{y}+r\mathbf{u}))\mathbf{u}]\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}[(f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{y}+r\mathbf{u}))\mathbf{u}]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{z}),\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\mathbf{u}\bigg]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{z})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{z})),\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\mathbf{u}\bigg]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\bigg[\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{z})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{z})),\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\big[|\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle|\big]\sup_{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}\leq 1}\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{w}+t\mathbf{z})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{z})),\mathbf{z}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}d}{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\big[|\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle|\big],

where we have used Definition
3.1
in the last line. To complete the proof, it remains to use

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\big[|\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle|\big]=\;

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\leq 1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[|\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\rangle|>t]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\exp(-dt^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2d}},

where we have used
\Pr[\left\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\right\rangle\geq c]\leq e^{-\frac{dc^{2}}{2}}
, which holds for all
c\in[0,1),
by the concentration of measure on a (unit) sphere; see, e.g.,
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
. ∎

3.3
A Discussion of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
Classes

We now provide some examples that illustrate how classes of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
functions compare to each other and to classical classes of objective functions studied in the optimization literature. First, based on the definition of constants
L_{r}
and
\widehat{L}_{r}
defining the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
classes, it is immediate that

L_{r}\leq\widehat{L}_{2r}.

(3.7)

As it turns out, it is possible for
L_{r}
to be much smaller than
\widehat{L}_{r},
as illustrated below.

Example 3.9
.

Consider the following function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for
K=\left\lceil\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\ln(2d)}}\right\rceil:

f(\mathbf{x})=\max_{0\leq i\leq K}\Big\{\frac{i}{K}x_{1}-\frac{(i-1)i}{K^{2}}\Big\}.

This is a piecewise-linear function dependent only on
x_{1},
with subgradient being the zero vector for
x_{1}\leq 0,
and then gradually increasing as
\frac{i}{K}\mathbf{e}_{1}
as
x_{1}
is increased between 0 and 1 in
1/K
increments. As a consequence,
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{i}{K}\mathds{1}\{x_{1}\in(\frac{i-1}{K},\frac{i}{K}]\}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathds{1}\{x_{1}\geq 1\}\mathbf{e}_{1}
is a subgradient of
f
at
\mathbf{x}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
where
\mathds{1}\{\cdot\}
is one if its argument is true and zero otherwise, while
\mathbf{e}_{1}
denotes the first standard basis vector. Taking
r=1,
it is immediate that
\widehat{L}_{r}=1.
To bound
L_{r}
above, it is evident that only vectors with
x_{1}\in[0,1]
can attain the supremum that defines
L_{r}
. Suppose that
\frac{i-1}{K}<x_{1}\leq\frac{i}{K}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Let
\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S}_{\rho})
for
0<\rho\leq 1
.
Using concentration of measure on the sphere, we have that with probability at least
1-1/d
,
|u_{1}|\leq 1/K
. As a consequence, under the same event,
x_{1}+u_{1}
can only reach up to two (neighboring) linear pieces defining
f(\mathbf{x})
. Without loss of generality, in the worst case,

\frac{i-2}{K}<x_{1}+u_{1}<\frac{i+1}{K}
. This implies that
\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\in[\frac{i-2}{K},\frac{i+1}{K}]
, and that with probability
1-1/d
,
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})\in\{\frac{i-1}{K},\frac{i}{K},\frac{i+1}{K}\}
, thus

\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{S}_{\rho})}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}\leq\frac{1}{d}+O\Big(\frac{1}{K}\Big)=O\Big(\frac{1}{K}\Big)=O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\ln d}{d}}\Big).

Taking
d\to+\infty
, we conclude that the ratio between
\hat{L}_{r}
and
L_{r}
can be arbitrarily large.

Additionally, if we look at
local
values of
L_{r}
and
\widehat{L}_{r}
(in a ball of radius
r
around a point
\mathbf{x}
that we fix)—denoted by
\widehat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{x})
and
L_{r}(\mathbf{x})
to disambiguate from their worst-case values defined earlier in this section—it is possible for
L_{r}(\mathbf{x})
to be even exponentially smaller than
\widehat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{x}),
in terms of the dimension. This is illustrated in Example
3.10
below. The provided observation makes the possibility of developing algorithms that are adaptive to local values of
L_{r}
particularly appealing as a direction for future research.

Example 3.10
.

Consider the following function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for
c\in[1,\sqrt{d-1})
:

f(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }|x_{1}|\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\\
|x_{1}|-\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}},&\text{ if }|x_{1}|>\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\end{cases}.

(3.8)

This function is convex and 1-Lipschitz continuous. It is differentiable everywhere except at the set
\{|x_{1}|=\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\}
, with its subdifferential given by

\partial f(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\{\textbf{0}\},&\text{ if }|x_{1}|<\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\\
\{\mathrm{sign}(x_{1})\mathbf{e}_{1}\},&\text{ if }|x_{1}|>\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\\
\mathrm{conv}(\textbf{0},\mathrm{sign}(x_{1})\mathbf{e}_{1}),&\text{ if }|x_{1}|=\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\end{cases},

(3.9)

where
\mathrm{sign}(x_{1})
is equal to one if
x_{1}\geq 0
and is equal to
-1
otherwise.
For
r\geq 2\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}
and
\mathbf{x}=\textbf{0},
assuming
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\arg\sup_{\mathbf{g}\in\partial f(\mathbf{x})}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2},
we have that
\widehat{L}_{r}(\textbf{0})=2
, which immediately follows from (
3.9
), since
\|\gamma_{f}((r/2)\mathbf{e}_{1})-\gamma_{f}(-(r/2)\mathbf{e}_{1})\|_{2}=2
. On the other hand,
L_{r}(\textbf{0})
is determined by the average subgradient variation around
\mathbf{x}=\textbf{0},
where, by symmetry,
\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})=0.
Assuming
\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and taking
r=1,
we thus get that

L_{r}(\textbf{0})=\sup_{0<\rho\leq 1}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{u})\|_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\{|u_{1}|>\frac{c}{\sqrt{d-1}}\}}1\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}.

Using an adaption of
[
5
, Theorem 2.7]
from the unit ball to the unit sphere, it is possible to show that the above integral is bounded by
\frac{2}{c}e^{-c^{2}/2}.
Thus, taking, e.g.,
c=\frac{\sqrt{d-1}}{2}
and increasing the dimension
d,
we can make
L_{r}(\textbf{0})\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{d-1}}e^{-\frac{d-1}{8}}
arbitrarily small.

Because
M
-Lipschitz continuous functions can be equivalently defined as functions whose subgradient is uniformly bounded by
M,
it is immediate that
L_{r}\leq\widehat{L}_{r}\leq 2M
for any
r>0.
The latter inequality is tight in general, as is apparent by considering the univariate function
f(x)=M|x|.

Classes of
(M,\kappa)
-weakly smooth functions for
\kappa\in[0,1]
are also captured by classes of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
functions. In particular,
(M,\kappa)
-weakly smooth functions are defined as continuously differentiable functions with Hölder-continuous gradient, satisfying

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\leq M\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{\kappa},\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.10)

It is immediate from this definition that
L_{r}\leq\widehat{L}_{r}\leq Mr^{\kappa},

\forall r>0.

It is possible that a function is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
(and thus also BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
) but neither Lipschitz-continuous nor weakly smooth, for any finite
M
and
\kappa\in[0,1].
This is illustrated by the following example, which extends the univariate example from Section
3.1
.

Example 3.11
.

Consider the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq 1,\\
\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2},&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

(3.11)

This is a continuous function whose gradient is discontinuous on the sphere
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1.
In more detail, within the unit ball
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2},

\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=0,
while outside the unit ball
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}.
Thus, for
r\in(0,1],

\widehat{L}_{r}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{B}}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}=1.
On the other hand, this function is neither weakly smooth (as its gradient is not continuous) nor globally Lipschitz continuous (as for
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}>1,
we have
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2},
which is unbounded).

Another interesting consequence of Example
3.11
is that, unlike Lipschitz continuity, BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
does not preclude quadratic growth of a function. This property appears particularly useful for the study of complexity of nonsmooth optimization under local error bound conditions
[
54
]
,
[
13
, Chapter 8]
, which can enable linear convergence of algorithms; see
[
16
]
for one such example.

3.4
Structural Results

In this subsection, we derive structural results that are particularly useful for the analysis of standard first-order methods. We first show that BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
functions can be characterized as being “approximately smooth,” in the sense that they behave as smooth functions between points further apart than
r.
Precise statements formalizing this geometric intuition are provided in the following two lemmas.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
at some radius
r_{0}>0.
Then for all
r\in(0,r_{0}]
, for BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
parameter
\widehat{L}_{r}
associated with
r,
the following holds:

f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\leq\begin{cases}\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{2r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2},\text{ if }\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}>r,\\
\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2},\text{ if }\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq r.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is based on interpreting the subgradient of
f
as an inexact oracle for a smooth function. While this is a known idea in convex optimization
[
50
,
17
]
, we proceed differently from previous works when the points of interest lie further apart. Here we partition the line segment joining these two points and apply the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
property in each of these intervals (see Case 2 below). Aggregating these bounds then provides a sharper quadratic upper bound.

The definition of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
functions requires that the function is locally Lipschitz, thus FTC (Theorem
2.2
) applies, by which

f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

(3.12)

It is immediate from the definition of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
that if a function is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
at radius
r_{0},
then it is BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
at any radius
r\in(0,r_{0}],
possibly with a smaller value of the associated parameter
\widehat{L}_{r}.
Fix any
r\in(0,r_{0}]
and consider the following two possible cases.

Case 1:
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq r
.
Then, by the definition of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
functions, it must be
\|\gamma(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq\widehat{L}_{r}
for any
t\in[0,1].
Thus, bounding the inner product on the right-hand side of (
3.12
) using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
\|\gamma(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq\widehat{L}_{r}
, and integrating, we get that

f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\leq\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

(3.13)

Case 2:
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}>r
.
Let
m=\lfloor\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}{r}\rfloor+1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

We now bound each of the integrals as follows:

\displaystyle\;\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+(i-1)/m(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+j/m(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}))-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{j}+(j-1)/m(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\;\leq\frac{1}{m}\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\frac{i-1}{m}\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\frac{i}{m}\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

Now summing over
i
and plugging back into (
3.12
), we finally get

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{i}{m}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{L}_{r}\frac{m-1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{2r}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2},

(3.14)

where in the last inequality we used
m-1=\lfloor\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}{r}\rfloor\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}}{r},
by our choice of
m.

To complete the proof, it remains to combine (
3.13
) and (
3.14
).
∎

Another useful inequality that is a consequence of Lemma
3.12
is akin to interpolation inequalities, which are used to characterize the class of smooth convex functions; see, for example
[
61
]
. It is provided in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
at some radius
r_{0}>0.
Then for all
r\in(0,r_{0}]
, for BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
parameter
\widehat{L}_{r}
associated with
r,
the following holds: for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{y})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}>\widehat{L}_{r},

\frac{r}{2\widehat{L}_{r}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{y})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}\leq f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rangle.

Proof.

Following a standard approach for proving interpolation inequalities of this type (see, e.g.,
[
61
]
), fix
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider the function
h_{\mathbf{x}}
defined by
h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y}):=f(\mathbf{y})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}\right\rangle.
It is not hard to verify that this function is convex, BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
for all
r\in(0,r_{0}],
and minimized by
\mathbf{x}.
Fix any
r\in(0,r_{0}].
Suppose that
\|\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}=\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{y})-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}>\widehat{L}_{r}.
Then
\|\mathbf{y}-(\mathbf{y}-\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y}))\|_{2}>r
. Thus, using
h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})\leq h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}),
for all
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
and applying Lemma
3.12
, we get

\displaystyle h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x})-h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq h_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y}-\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y}))-h(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}\left\langle\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y}),\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y})\right\rangle+\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{2r}\Big\|\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y})\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{r}{2\widehat{L}_{r}}\|\gamma_{h_{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}^{2}.

To complete the proof, it remains to plug the definition of
h_{\mathbf{x}}
into the above inequality and simplify.

∎

Finally, to make use of approaches based on randomized smoothing, it is important to bound the variance of stochastic gradient estimates
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}),

\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}),
of
\nabla f_{r},
where we recall the smoothed function
f_{r}
was defined in (
3.2
), with an equivalent definition using expectations provided in (
3.4
).

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
at some radius
r>0
with parameter
L_{r}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
at radius
2r
with parameter
\widehat{L}_{2r}
. Then, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\big[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq L_{r}\widehat{L}_{2r}.

Proof.

First, observe that for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{B},

\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq\widehat{L}_{2r},
as a consequence of the definition of
f_{r}
, Jensen’s inequality, and the definition of
\widehat{L}_{2r}.
Thus,

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\big[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{2r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}\big[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{2r}d\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}({\cal S})}\big[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\tau r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\big]\tau^{d-1}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{2r}d\int_{0}^{1}L_{r}\tau^{d-1}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\widehat{L}_{2r}\frac{d}{d+1}L_{r}\leq\widehat{L}_{2r}L_{r},

where in the second inequality we used integration over polar coordinates and in the third we used
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}({\cal S})}\big[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\tau r\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq L_{r},
which holds for all
\tau\in[0,1],
by the definition of
L_{r}.

∎

4
Optimization under Bounded Local Variation of the Subgradient

In this section, we discuss how to optimize functions with bounded local variation of the subgradient. We first provide bounds for convex optimization obtained using randomized smoothing and stochastic optimization methods applied to the smoothed function
f_{r}.
We then provide alternative bounds based on Goldstein’s method.

Throughout this section, we assume that there exists some finite radius
r_{0}
for which the function
f
is a BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
function (and thus is also a BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
function). It is immediate by our definitions of BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
properties that both hold for any
r\in(0,r_{0}],
possibly with smaller constants. For simplicity and as is standard, we assume that
f
is minimized by some
\mathbf{x}_{*}
in convex settings and bounded below by some
f_{*}
in nonconvex settings.

4.1
Convex Optimization

We begin this section by reviewing algorithm AGD+
[
12
]
(see also the “method of similar triangles” in
[
22
]
), which we subsequently use to obtain convergence bounds under the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
property. For completeness, we provide full details of the analysis of AGD+, stated in slightly different terms than the original analysis from
[
12
]
to make the application of those results more direct and suitable to our setting.

4.1.1
AGD+ and its Analysis

Iterates of AGD+ applied to Euclidean, projection-based settings and for an arbitrary estimate
\mathbf{g}_{k}
of
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}\mathbf{y}_{k-1}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k-1})

(AGD+)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\mathbf{g}_{k}

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}\mathbf{y}_{k-1}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k}).

We recall that
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
is arbitrary and at initialization
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{0}),

\mathbf{z}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}-a_{0}\mathbf{g}_{0}
. Recall also that
A_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i},
where
a_{i}
,
i\geq 0,
are positive step sizes. Denote
\mathbf{v}_{i}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{i}).

The analysis that we use here is slightly different than those in
[
19
,
12
]
, in that we make the “sources of error” that constrain the convergence rate of the algorithm more explicit and suitable to our discussion in Section
4
.
It is based on the approximate gap technique
[
19
]
, which bounds a gap estimate
G_{k}(\mathbf{w})\geq f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{w})
, for
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X}
. In particular, the argument constructs a lower bound on
f(\mathbf{w})
,
L_{k}(\mathbf{w})\leq f(\mathbf{w})
, and then bounds “error terms”
E_{k},
which satisfy
E_{0}\geq A_{0}G_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
and
E_{k}\geq A_{k}G_{k}-A_{k-1}G_{k-1}.
Then the optimality gap is simply bounded using

f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{w})\leq G_{k}(\mathbf{w})\leq\frac{(1/2)\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}E_{i}}{A_{k}}

(4.1)

and we draw inferences about the convergence by choosing the sequence
A_{k}
to ensure the right-hand side of (
4.1
) decays as fast as possible with
k.

Throughout this section, we let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X}
be arbitrary but fixed. In particular,
\mathbf{w}
can be chosen as a minimizer of
f
, in which case (
4.1
) bounds the optimality gap. For notational simplicity, we write
G_{k},L_{k}
to mean
G_{k}(\mathbf{w}),

L_{k}(\mathbf{w}),
as the context is clear.

We now define the “error terms”
E_{k},

k\geq 0,
that appear in the analysis. There are three main sources of error: (i)
E_{k}^{s}
corresponding to smoothness of
f
(less than or equal to zero if
f
is smooth and step sizes are appropriately chosen), (ii)
E_{k}^{b},
corresponding to the bias of gradient estimates
\mathbf{g}_{k},
and (iii)
E_{k}^{v}
related to the variance of gradient estimates
\mathbf{g}_{k}.
We define them as follows and note that they are directly obtained from the subsequent analysis.

\displaystyle E_{k}^{s}

\displaystyle:=A_{k}\Big(f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{A_{k}}{2{a_{k}}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)

(4.2)

\displaystyle E_{k}^{b}

\displaystyle=E_{k}^{b}(\mathbf{w}):=a_{k}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{k}-\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle E_{k}^{v}

\displaystyle:=a_{k}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathbf{g}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle E_{k}

\displaystyle:=E_{k}^{s}+E_{k}^{b}+E_{k}^{v}.

We begin by constructing the gap estimates
G_{k},
in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a proper convex continuous function and let
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i}
for
i\geq 0
be the iterates of (
AGD+
). Let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X}
be arbitrary.
Then for all
k\geq 0,

f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{w})\leq G_{k},
where

\displaystyle G_{k}:=

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle\big)+\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}E_{i}^{b}

(4.3)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2A_{k}}\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2A_{k}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

Proof.

Define
G_{k}:=f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-L_{k},
where
L_{k}\leq f(\mathbf{w}).
To carry out the proof, we then just need to construct
L_{k}
that bounds
f(\mathbf{w})
below and agrees with the expression from the statement of the proposition.
First, by convexity of
f
and
A_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i},
we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{w})\geq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle\big)+\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A_{k}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2A_{k}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle\big)+\frac{1}{A_{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2A_{k}}\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2A_{k}}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=:L_{k},

where in the last inequality we used the definition of
\mathbf{v}_{k}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})
and
\mathbf{z}_{k}=\mathbf{x}_{0}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}
(by its definition). It remains to use the definition of
E_{i}^{b}
and
G_{k}=f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-L_{k}.

∎

We now formally prove that the error sequences defined in (
4.2
) satisfy the requirement that
E_{0}\geq A_{0}G_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
and
E_{k}\geq A_{k}G_{k}-A_{k-1}G_{k-1},
which immediately implies (
4.1
). Obtaining a convergence bound for (
AGD+
) then reduces to bounding the individual error terms in (
4.2
) using assumptions about
f
and the gradient estimates
\mathbf{g}_{k}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider iterates of (
AGD+
) for
k\geq 0
and let
G_{k}
be defined via (
4.3
) and
E_{k}
be defined via (
4.2
). Then for any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X},

\displaystyle A_{0}G_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+E_{0},

\displaystyle A_{k}G_{k}-A_{k-1}G_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq E_{k},\;\text{ for }\;k\geq 1.

As a consequence, for all
k\geq 0,

f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{w})\leq\frac{(1/2)\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}E_{i}}{A_{k}}.

Proof.

We start with bounding
A_{0}G_{0}

a_{0}=A_{0}.
We have

\displaystyle A_{0}G_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=\;

\displaystyle A_{0}(f(\mathbf{y}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\right\rangle)-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+E_{0}^{b}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle A_{0}\Big(f(\mathbf{y}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0}),\mathbf{y}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\right\rangle-\frac{A_{0}}{2{a_{0}}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+a_{0}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{g}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\right\rangle+E_{0}^{b}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle E_{0},

where we have used the definitions of error terms from (

),
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{y}_{0}
, and
a_{0}=A_{0}.

For the second inequality in the statement of the theorem, define

m_{k}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

Then
A_{k}G_{k}-A_{k-1}G_{k-1}
can be written as

\displaystyle A_{k}G_{k}-A_{k-1}G_{k-1}=

\displaystyle A_{k}f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-A_{k-1}f(\mathbf{y}_{k-1})-a_{k}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

(4.4)

\displaystyle-m_{k}(\mathbf{v}_{k})+m_{k-1}(\mathbf{v}_{k-1})+E_{k}^{b}.

Observe that
m_{k}
is 1-strongly convex and minimized by
\mathbf{v}_{k},
by the definition of
\mathbf{v}_{k}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k}).
Thus, using its definition, we have

\displaystyle m_{k}(\mathbf{v}_{k})-m_{k-1}(\mathbf{v}_{k-1})

\displaystyle=a_{k}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle+m_{k-1}(\mathbf{v}_{k})-m_{k-1}(\mathbf{v}_{k-1})

\displaystyle\geq a_{k}\left\langle\mathbf{g}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{k}\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}-E_{k}^{v},

(4.5)

where the inequality holds because
m_{k-1}
is 1-strongly convex and minimized by
\mathbf{v}_{k-1}
and the last line is by the definition of
E_{k}^{v}
.
On the other hand, by convexity of
f,
we have

f(\mathbf{y}_{k-1})\geq f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k-1}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle.

(4.6)

Finally, plugging (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) back into (

) and using that

can be expressed as
\mathbf{y}_{k}=\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}\mathbf{y}_{k-1}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}\mathbf{v}_{k}=\mathbf{x}_{k}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}(\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{k-1})
(from (

\displaystyle\;A_{k}G_{k}-A_{k-1}G_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle E_{k}^{b}+E_{k}^{v}+A_{k}(f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle)-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{v}_{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle E_{k}^{b}+E_{k}^{v}+A_{k}\Big(f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{A_{k}}{{a_{k}}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle E_{k}.

The last inequality in the theorem statement follows immediately from

) and the first two inequalities in the theorem statement.
∎

4.1.2
Approximately Smooth Minimization

We now show how to directly apply AGD+ to
f,
with
\mathbf{g}_{k}=\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. In this case, clearly,
E_{k}^{b}=E_{k}^{v}=0,

\forall k\geq 0,
so to use the result from Theorem
4.2
to get concrete complexity bounds, we need to bound
E_{k}^{s},
for
k\geq 0.
We do so using the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
property, based on the upper quadratic function approximation from Lemma
3.12
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
at radius
r_{0}>0.
For
r\in(0,r_{0}],
let
\widehat{L}_{r}
be the constant of maximum local variation of the subgradient of
f.
Consider applying (
AGD+
) to
f,
initialized at an arbitrary
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}.
If for all
k\geq 0,

\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}},
then
E_{k}^{s}\leq\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}{2},

\forall k\geq 0.

Proof.

Fix any
k\geq 0
and recall that, by definition,

E_{k}^{s}=A_{k}\Big(f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{A_{k}}{2{a_{k}}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

If
\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}\leq r,
then by Lemma
3.12
,
f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle\leq\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2},
and we can conclude that

\displaystyle E_{k}^{s}\leq\;

\displaystyle A_{k}\Big(\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}-\frac{A_{k}}{2{a_{k}}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\leq\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}{2},

by Young’s inequality. Thus the lemma claim holds in this case.

Now consider the remaining case that
\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}>r.
Then, by Lemma
3.12
,
f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rangle\leq\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{2r}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2},
and we conclude that

\displaystyle E_{k}^{s}\leq\;

\displaystyle A_{k}\Big(\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{2r}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{A_{k}}{2{a_{k}}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\leq 0,

as
\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}},
by the lemma assumption.

∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
function at radius
r_{0}>0
and assume
f
is minimized by some
\mathbf{x}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
For
r\in(0,r_{0}],
let
\widehat{L}_{r}
be the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
of
f.
Consider applying (
AGD+
) to
f,
initialized at an arbitrary
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}.
If for all
k\geq 0,

\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}},
then
\forall k\geq 0,

f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}{a_{i}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}{2A_{k}}.

In particular, for any
\epsilon>0,
any
r\in(0,r_{0}]
and the corresponding constant
\widehat{L}_{r},
there exists a choice of the step sizes
a_{k}\geq 0
such that
f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon
after

k=O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{r\epsilon}}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}+\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigg)

(4.7)

iterations. Furthermore, these step sizes can be chosen adaptively w.r.t.
r
and
\widehat{L}_{r}
, with at most an additive logarithmic cost in the complexity.

Proof.

The first inequality in the statement follows directly from Lemma
4.3
and Theorem
4.2
. Observe that the condition
\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}
for
k\geq 0

a_{0}=A_{0}\leq\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}
and
a_{k}\leq\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4A_{k-1}\widehat{L}_{r}/r}}{2}
for
k\geq 1.
By convention, let
A_{-1}=0
and define
a_{k}
via

a_{k}=\min\Big\{\frac{\epsilon}{\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}},\,\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4A_{k-1}\widehat{L}_{r}/r}}{2}\Big\}.

(4.8)

Because
a_{k}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}
for all
k\geq 0,
we have that
\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{k}{a_{i}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}{2A_{k}}\leq\frac{\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{k}{a_{i}}}{2A_{k}}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}.
Further, by the choice of
a_{k}
from (
4.8
), we have that
A_{k}\geq\min\{(k+1)\frac{\epsilon}{\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}},\frac{r}{\widehat{L}_{r}}(k+1)^{2}\}.
As a consequence,

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2A_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\Big\{\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2(k+1){\epsilon}/{\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}},\,\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2({r}/{\widehat{L}_{r}})(k+1)^{2}}\Big\}.

Thus,
\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2A_{k}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
(which immediately leads to
f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon
) for
k\geq\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}}{r\epsilon}}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}-1,
completing the proof for the claimed number of iterations in (
4.7
).

Finally, to obtain the claimed bounds all that was needed was that

Since

is computable based on the iterates of the algorithm and step sizes set by the algorithm, this is a computable condition that can be checked. If the condition does not hold for the current choice of the step size

in iteration

the step size can be halved. Since we have already argued in (

) a lower bound on the step size that suffices for the claimed iteration complexity, we get that the step size can be determined with at most a logarithmic cost using standard arguments based on the backtracking line search as in e.g.,
[

.
∎

4.1.3
Randomized Smoothing

We now discuss how to obtain complexity bounds that potentially depend on the weaker BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
property, using a randomized smoothing approach. We further show how the mean width of the subdifferential set around optima (see Lemma
3.6
) affects the oracle complexity, leading to the first positive result on parallelizing convex optimization for a nontrivial class of problems (e.g., piecewise-linear functions with polynomially many pieces). . The idea is to apply AGD+ to
f_{r}
defined by (
3.2
), using gradient estimates
\mathbf{g}_{k}=\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k}).
To do so, we need to show that
E_{k}^{b}
and
E_{k}^{v}
can be bounded in expectation, while
E_{k}^{s}
will be at most zero under the appropriate step size choice, as
f_{r}
is smooth (recall the results from Lemma
3.8
).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
r>0.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
and BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
function with constants
\widehat{L}_{2r}
and
L_{r}
, respectively. Let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{X}
be arbitrary but fixed. Let
f_{r}
be defined by (
3.2
). Let
\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{z}_{k}
be the iterates of (
AGD+
). Then:

\mathbb{E}[E_{k}^{b}+E_{k}^{v}(\mathbf{w})]\leq{a_{k}}^{2}L_{r}\widehat{L}_{2r}.

Proof.

By the definition of
f_{r},

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})]=\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k});
hence, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{v}\right\rangle]=0
for any fixed
\mathbf{v}
, and, thus,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}[E_{k}^{b}]=0
.

Let
\mathbf{v}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k})).
Recalling that
\mathbf{z}_{k}=\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k}),
we have

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{w}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\right\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\right\rangle]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|_{2}\|\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\;a_{k}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\;a_{k}\widehat{L}_{2r}L_{r},

where (
i
) is by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
ii
) is by nonexpansiveness of the projection operator and
\mathbf{z}_{k}=\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})
, and (
iii
) is by Lemma
3.14
. As a consequence,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}}[E_{k}^{v}(\mathbf{w})]\leq{a_{k}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{2r}L_{r}.

To complete the proof, it remains to take the expectation w.r.t.
\mathbf{u}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k-1}
on both sides of the last inequality.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Observe that in proving Proposition
4.5
, we showed that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\left\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{w}-\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\right\rangle]

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a_{k}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})}[\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|_{2}^{2}].

This quantity is clearly bounded by
a_{k}\widehat{L}_{2r}^{2}
(which is generally a looser bound than what is provided in Proposition
4.5
; recall Example
3.10
), but can also be bounded by defining a slightly stronger Lipschitz condition than BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize avg}}
, which is more “variance-like:”

\sup_{0<\rho\leq r}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{S}_{\rho})}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\rho}}\|\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})-\nabla f_{r}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}\leq\tilde{L}_{r}<\infty.

The main usefulness of (
4.10
) is that in parallel optimization settings this quantity can be reduced by taking more samples: by standard properties of the variance, the empirical average of
m
samples
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u})
for
\mathbf{u}
’s drawn i.i.d. from
\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})
would reduce the right-hand side of (
4.10
) by a factor
\frac{1}{m}.
Thus, for any
\epsilon>0
,
m=\lceil\frac{\tilde{L}^{2}}{\epsilon}\rceil
samples suffice to makes this quantity at most
\epsilon.
We further note that without considerations related to reducing the variance via minibatching, it is possible to obtain a slightly tighter bound scaling with
{a_{k}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}{L}_{r}
instead of
{a_{k}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{2r}{L}_{r}
by choosing
\mathbf{v}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{z}_{k-1}-a_{k}\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
and following the same line of argument in the proof of Proposition
4.5
.

Choosing the smoothing radius
r

Our standing assumption is that there exists a radius
r_{0}
for which
\widehat{L}_{r_{0}},L_{r_{0}}
are bounded on the feasible set
\mathcal{X}
. This is clearly true for Lipschitz continuous functions, but, as we have discussed before, can hold more generally. Observe that if this assumption holds for some
r_{0},
then for any
\epsilon_{0}>0,
we can choose
r>0
such that
r\widehat{L}_{r}\leq\epsilon_{0}
and
rL_{r}\leq\epsilon_{0}
, as for
r\in(0,r_{0}]
, we have
\widehat{L}_{r}\leq\widehat{L}_{r_{0}}
and
L_{r}\leq L_{r_{0}}
.

Our “ideal” choice of a smoothing radius is the largest radius
r>0
such that
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon/2,
where
\epsilon>0
is the target error and
\mathbf{x}_{*}
a minimizer of
f
. Using Lemma
3.5
, to have
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon/2,
it suffices that
rL_{r}\leq\epsilon/2.
Alternatively, based on Lemma
3.6
an Remark
3.7
, if the Goldstein
r
-subdifferential at
\mathbf{x}_{*}
,
\partial_{r}f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
, is contained in a polytope
K_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
of Euclidean diameter
D_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
,
then
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})=O\big(rD_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}\sqrt{\frac{\ln|{\operatorname{vert}}(K_{\mathbf{x}_{*}})|}{d}}\big).

Observe that it suffices that such a condition holds only for the Goldstein
r
-subdifferential at
\mathbf{x}_{*}
. The reason for considering this condition is that it allows choosing a potentially much larger smoothing radius
r.
In particular, due to the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
property, it is possible to choose an enclosing polytope
K_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
to be of diameter
C\widehat{L}_{r},
for any
C>1.
Here, the tradeoff in choosing
C
is that we want the polytope to have as few vertices as possible while keeping
C
as an absolute constant. In particular, if
K_{\mathbf{x}_{*}}
has
\mathrm{poly}(d)
vertices, then we can ensure
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon/2
with
r\widehat{L}_{r}=O\big(\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{d}{\ln(d)}}\big)
. We summarize the resulting complexity bounds as follows.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function minimized by some
\mathbf{x}_{*}\in\mathcal{X}
on a closed convex set
\mathcal{X}.
Suppose that there exists a radius
r_{0}>0
such that
f
is a BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
function for
r_{0}
. Given a target error
\epsilon>0
, suppose the radius
r>0
is chosen so that
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon/2
, where
f_{r}
is defined by (
3.2
). Let
\lambda_{r}
denote the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f_{r}.
Consider applying (
AGD+
) to
f_{r}(\mathbf{x})
, using stochastic gradient oracle
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}),

\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}),
for an arbitrary initial point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}},

\forall k\geq 0.
Then,
\forall k\geq 0,

\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})]\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}{a_{i}}^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}L_{r}}{A_{k}}.

In particular, there exist step sizes
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
such that
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})]\leq\epsilon
after at most

k=O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{r}}{\epsilon}}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}+\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}L_{r}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigg)

(4.11)

iterations. In the above bound,
\lambda_{r}
satisfies the following

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}=O\bigg(\min\bigg\{\frac{{L_{r}}^{2}d}{\epsilon},\,\frac{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon},\,\frac{{\widehat{L}_{r}}^{2}\sqrt{\ln(|\operatorname{vert}(K_{\mathbf{x}_{*}})|)}}{\epsilon}\bigg\}\bigg).

Before proving the corollary, a few remarks are in order. Observe first that the second term in (
4.11
) can be replaced by a term absorbed by the first one by taking multiple samples
\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{k}+r\mathbf{u}_{k})
in parallel and choosing
\mathbf{g}_{k}
as their average (see Remark
4.6
). This means that it is possible to parallelize the method using
\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon,\widehat{L}_{r},\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2})
oracle queries per round and have the first term in (
4.11
) determine the number of parallel rounds. See, e.g.,
[
6
,
20
]
for similar ideas used in nonsmooth Lipschitz continuous optimization.

Second, similar to the result from Corollary
4.4
, the value of the Lipschitz constant of
f
plays no role in the oracle complexity bound in Corollary
4.7
. It is possible that a function is not Lipschitz continuous at all (recall the examples from Section
3
), yet we get complexity bounds that are similar to the complexity of nonsmooth Lipschitz convex optimization, at least in some regimes of the problem parameters.

Because
L_{r}
can generally be much smaller than
\widehat{L}_{r}
, it is not clear a priori which term in the minimum determines the value of
\lambda_{r}
(and thus the oracle complexity in (
4.11
)). When
L_{r}=\Omega(\widehat{L}_{r}/\sqrt{d}),
then we have
\lambda_{r}=O(\min\{\frac{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}\sqrt{d}}{\epsilon},\,\frac{{\widehat{L}_{r}}^{2}\sqrt{\ln(|\operatorname{vert}(K_{\mathbf{x}_{*}})|)}}{\epsilon}\})
. In particular, when the Goldstein subdifferential
\partial_{r}f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
is contained in a polytope of diameter
O(\widehat{L}_{r})
with
\mathrm{poly}(d)
vertices,
\lambda_{r}
is nearly independent of the dimension (the dependence on the dimension becomes
\sqrt{\ln(d)}
).

A surprising aspect of this result is that not only do we get complexity that depends on
\widehat{L}_{r}
, which can be much smaller than the objective’s Lipschitz constant, but in this case it is also possible to obtain a parallel algorithm that makes
\mathrm{poly}(\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}/\epsilon)
queries per round and has depth (number of parallel rounds) that scales with
O(\frac{\widehat{L}_{r}(\ln(d))^{1/4}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}}{\epsilon}).
As a consequence, we get
the first example of a class of nonsmooth optimization problems for which parallelization leads to improved depth of the algorithm that is essentially dimension-independent
. As a specific example, nonsmooth
M
-Lipschitz-continuous functions that can be expressed as or closely approximated by a maximum of polynomially many in
d
and
1/\epsilon
linear functions have parallel complexity at most
O(\frac{M(\ln(d/\epsilon))^{1/4}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}}{\epsilon})
– significantly lower than the sequential complexity
O(\frac{M^{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}})
for any
d
that is polynomial in
1/\epsilon.

This last statement seems at odds with parallel oracle complexity lower bounds for standard Euclidean settings
[
18
,
6
,
64
,
2
]
, which are all based on a max-of-linear hard probabilistic instance with
\mathrm{poly}(d,1/\epsilon)
components originally introduced by Nemirovski
[
44
]
. The apparent contradiction is resolved by observing that all these existing lower bounds become informative for
d\gg 1/\epsilon^{2}
and crucially rely on the
informative
queries being confined to the unit Euclidean ball. By contrast, our randomized smoothing approach in this case relies on queries to
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}+r\mathbf{u}),

\mathbf{u}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B})
, with
r\approx\sqrt{d}\epsilon
, meaning that all queries fall well
outside
the unit ball with high probability and thus the existing lower bounds do not apply.

Proof of Corollary
4.7
.

First, because
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon/2
and
f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})\geq f(\mathbf{y}_{k})
(due to convexity of
f,
by Lemma

), we have

\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})]\leq\epsilon/2+\mathbb{E}[f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})],

(4.12)

so we only need to focus on bounding
\mathbb{E}[f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]
, which we do using Theorem
4.2
and Proposition

. In particular, because
f_{r}
is
\lambda_{r}
-smooth, we have that

E_{k}^{s}\leq A_{k}\Big(\frac{\lambda_{r}}{2}-\frac{A_{k}}{2{a_{k}}^{2}}\Big)\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 0,

as
\frac{{a_{k}}^{2}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}},
by assumption. Thus, applying Theorem
4.2
and Proposition
4.5
, we have

\mathbb{E}[f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}{a_{i}}^{2}L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}}{A_{k}},

(4.13)

which, combined with (

) leads to the first inequality in Corollary
4.7
.

The bound on
\lambda_{r}
follows from Lemma
3.8
and upper bounds on
f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
in Lemmas
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
, by setting those upper bounds to
\epsilon/2
and solving for
r
.

Finally, it remains to argue that there is a choice of step sizes
a_{i}
such that
f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\epsilon/2
in the number of iterations stated in (
4.11
). This is done using similar ideas as in
[
23
]
. In particular, define
a_{i}
’s via
\frac{{a_{i}}^{2}}{A_{i}}=\beta
(this enforces
a_{0}=\beta
and for
i\geq 1
is a quadratic equality with a unique solution, using
A_{i}=A_{i-1}+a_{i}
) for
\beta>0
to be specified shortly. It is well-known that in this case for
i\geq 1,

a_{i}=\Theta(\beta i)
and
A_{i}=\Theta(\beta i^{2}).
Thus the bound on
f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
becomes

\mathbb{E}[f_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{k})-f_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{*})]=O\Big(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\beta k^{2}}+k\beta L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}\Big).

(4.14)

In particular,
\beta=\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}}k^{3/2}}
balances the terms on the right-hand side of (
4.14
), but we also need
\beta\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}}
to satisfy the assumption that
\frac{{a_{i}}^{2}}{A_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}}.
Hence, we choose
\beta=\min\{\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}},\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}}k^{3/2}}\}
. Since
\beta\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}}k^{3/2}},
it follows that
k\beta L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}\leq\epsilon/4
for
k=O(\frac{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}).
On the other hand, by the choice of
\beta,
we have
\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\beta k^{2}}\leq\epsilon/4
for
k=O(\max\{\frac{L_{r}\widehat{L}_{r}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}},\,\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{r}}{\epsilon}}\|\mathbf{x}_{*}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\}),
hence the claimed bound (
4.11
) follows.
∎

4.2
Goldstein’s Method and Nonconvex Optimization

Interestingly, our framework also proves to be useful in the nonconvex setting. In particular, in this section we provide refined complexity results for the Goldstein method for approximating stationary points in locally Lipschitz (nonconvex) optimization. To do so, we adapt the results from
[
15
]
– which pertain to Lipschitz objectives – to the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
class of functions studied in this paper.

In what follows, we consider a function
f
with (local) Lipschitz constant
M
and local variation of subgradients bounded by
\widehat{L}_{r}
for some
r>0
. For any vector
\mathbf{g}
, we let
\hat{\mathbf{g}}:=\frac{\mathbf{g}}{\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}}
. The following lemma can be seen as an extension of
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
to this setting.
In what follows, we will make the particular choice
r=2\delta
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
M
-locally Lipschitz function. Let
\mathbf{g}\in\partial_{\delta}f(\mathbf{x})
be such that
\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}>\epsilon
and

f(\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{g}})-f(\mathbf{x})\geq-\frac{\delta}{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}.

(4.15)

Let
p\geq 1
be an integer,

\mathbf{h}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{B}_{2^{-p}}(\mathbf{g}))
, and
\mathbf{u}=\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{y})
, where
\mathbf{y}\sim\operatorname{Unif}([\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{h}}])
. Then

\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{g}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}+2M\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}2^{-p}

In particular, if
p\geq\log_{2}\big(\frac{12M}{\epsilon}\big)
, there exists
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that if
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{g}+\lambda(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{g})
, then

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{4}}{9\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}},&\mbox{ if }\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3\widehat{L}_{r}\\
\frac{2}{3}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2},&\mbox{ if }\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}>3\widehat{L}_{r}\end{array}\right.,

where
r=2\delta.

Proof.

Since
\hat{\mathbf{h}}
is generic,
f
is differentiable in almost every point on the interval
[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{h}}]
. Hence, by inequality (
4.15
) and the first theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{g}})}{\delta}=\frac{f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{h}})}{\delta}+\frac{f(\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{h}})-f(\mathbf{x}-\delta\hat{\mathbf{g}})}{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\langle\gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}-\tau\hat{\mathbf{h}}),\hat{\mathbf{h}}\rangle d\tau-M\|\hat{\mathbf{h}}-\hat{\mathbf{g}}\|

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbf{u},\hat{\mathbf{g}}\rangle-2M\|\hat{\mathbf{h}}-\hat{\mathbf{g}}\|_{2}.

In particular,

\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{g}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}+2M\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}2^{-p}.

Notice that if
p\geq\log_{2}\big(\frac{12M}{\epsilon}\big)
, then

\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{g}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}+2M\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}2^{-p}\leq\frac{2}{3}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}.

For the rest of the proof we impose this assumption.
Now, we consider the random variable
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{g}+\lambda(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{g})
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}+2\lambda\mathbb{E}\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{g}\rangle+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\big(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}\big)\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{2}\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}.

Here we have two choices. First, if
\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}<3\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}
, then we can set
\lambda=\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}/[3\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}]
, leading to

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}\big(1-\frac{\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}}{9\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}\big).

Otherwise, set
\lambda=1
, which leads to

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{3}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2}+\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{3}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{2}^{2},

completing the proof.
∎

With this technical lemma, the algorithm and its analysis follow naturally. The algorithm performs gradient descent-style steps using a vector from the Goldstein subdifferential chosen at random. If the subgradient has norm smaller than
\epsilon
, then the algorithm stops and outputs the current iterate; alternatively, if the subgradient provides sufficient decrease, we update the vector taking a normalized step of length
\delta
in this direction; finally, if neither of the above holds, the algorithm enters a loop where – due to Lemma
4.8
– we can find elements in the Goldstein subdifferential which decrease the subgradient norm multiplicatively. In particular, either this loop leads to a “sufficient decrease” step, or we obtain a subgradient with norm less than
\epsilon
. The convergence analysis of the algorithm follows from a combination of the sufficient decrease steps and a bound on the length of each internal loop.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
M

for

Proof.

Due to our assumption on the suboptimality of
\mathbf{x}_{0}

\frac{K\epsilon\delta}{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}[f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})]=f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}_{K})\leq\Delta.

On the other hand, the length of the innermost while loop can be bounded using Lemma

in iteration

\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3\widehat{L}_{r}

\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\Big(1-\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}}{9\widehat{L}_{r}^{2}}\Big)

A few remarks are in order here. First, same as in the settings of convex optimization considered earlier in this section, the resulting oracle complexity upper bound is
independent
of the (local) Lipschitz constant of
f.
However, here we crucially rely on the assumption that the (local) Lipschitz constant
M
of
f
is finite to ensure that vectors
\mathbf{h}
utilized by the algorithm are random. We note that the constant
M
need not be known to the algorithm; instead, it can be adaptively estimated with only a logarithmic overhead in the complexity, by simply choosing
p
growing within the innermost while loop. On the other hand, making the algorithm independent of
\widehat{L}_{r}
(and thus fully parameter-free) appears to be more challenging and is an interesting question for future research. We note that obtaining a parameter-free version of the Goldstein method with provable convergence guarantees is open even in the case of Lipschitz nonsmooth nonconvex optimization studied in
[
15
,
66
]
.

Finally, because
\widehat{L}_{r}\leq 2L,

In this case, the function is differentiable and its gradient is Hölder-continuous. From the definition of
\partial_{r}f(\mathbf{x})
we can further deduce that the output point
\mathbf{x}_{K}
of Algorithm

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{K})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon+Mr^{\kappa},

In particular, if

we have
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{K})\|_{2}\leq 2\epsilon.
The total number of oracle queries in this case is

For the case of smooth functions (

result, stated in Theorem

though we conjecture it is.

5
Conclusion

We introduced new classes of nonsmooth optimization problems based on local (maximum or average) variation of the function’s subgradient and showed that this perspective generalizes classical results in optimization based on Lipschitz continuity and weak smoothness, leading to more fine-grained oracle complexity bounds. On a conceptual level, one bottom line of our work is that it is not the growth of the function that determines complexity, but how its slope changes over small regions. Another is that complexity of parallel convex optimization depends on the complexity of the subdifferential set around optima.

As a byproduct of our results, we showed that – contrary to prior belief based on lower bounds
[
44
,
18
,
2
,
64
,
6
]
– the complexity of parallel optimization can, in fact, be improved even in high-dimensional settings under fairly mild assumptions about the complexity of the subdifferential set around optima. All that is needed is that the algorithm is given slightly more power: to be able to query points outside the unit ball. As a specific example, functions that can be expressed as or closely approximated by piecewise linear functions with polynomially many pieces in the dimension
d
and the inverse accuracy
1/\epsilon
can benefit from parallelization in terms of sequential oracle complexity (parallel “depth”) by a factor
\tilde{\Omega}(1/\epsilon)
so long as we are allowed to query them at points at distance
O(\epsilon\sqrt{d})
from the feasible set. Despite the seemingly specific nature of this example, minimizing a maximum of linear functions has been a key focus of research in nonsmooth convex optimization, and some of the most important developments in this area were inspired by this example
[
48
,
45
]
. Our results not only provide an alternative view of this setting, but also a broader perspective on functional classes that are amenable to parallelization by randomization.

Some interesting questions that merit further investigation remain. For example, can recent techniques on adaptive, parameter-free optimization
[
41
,
39
]
be generalized to our BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
class and lead to algorithms that are both universal and parameter free but avoid the line search used in our result? Can parallel optimization methods based on randomized smoothing be made parameter-free while maintaining the oracle complexity benefits described in our work? Is it possible for the Goldstein’s method we analyzed on the BVG
{}_{\mbox{\footnotesize max}}
class in Section
4.2
to be made completely parameter-free? Finally, while we did not pursue this direction, there are more sophisticated algorithms for parallel convex optimization based on randomized smoothing and higher-order optimization
[
6
,
10
]
. It seems plausible that the use of such techniques could further reduce parallel complexity of nonsmooth optimization for piecewise linear functions. It would be interesting to formally establish such a result.
