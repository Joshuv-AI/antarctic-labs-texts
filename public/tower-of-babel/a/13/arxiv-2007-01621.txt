Hydrodynamic limit for a boundary driven super-diffusive symmetric exclusion
Cédric Bernardin, Pedro Cardoso, Patrícia Gonçalves, Stefano Scotta

Abstract
. We study the hydrodynamic limit for symmetric exclusion processes with heavy-tailed long jumps and in contact with infinitely extended reservoirs. We show how the corresponding hydrodynamic equations are affected by the parameters defining the model. The hydrodynamic equations are characterized by a class of super-diffusive operators that are given by the regional fractional Laplacian with some additional reaction terms and various boundary conditions. Here we answer to all the questions left open in [ 3 ] and we prove a conjecture stated in that same article.

1.
Introduction

The understanding of the macroscopic behavior of a physical system, from the microscopic description of its molecules, is the main goal of statistical mechanics. Stochastic interacting particle systems (IPS), introduced in the mathematics community by
[
31
]
, have been intensively used to obtain, rigorously, the macroscopic laws which rule the space-time evolution of the conserved quantities of a system. These macroscopic laws take the form of partial differential equations (PDEs) whose nature depends on some features of the underlying IPS. The PDEs associated with an IPS are derived thanks to a scaling parameter
N\to\infty
, which connects the macroscopic space, where the solutions of the PDE are defined, with the microscopic space, a discrete space where the particles of the IPS evolve. The exclusion process is the most studied IPS in the mathematical literature. Its dynamics consists of a large system of particles evolving on the lattice
\mdmathbb Z
like independent continuous-time random walks, but with the rule that any jump, which would result in the occupation of a site by more than one particle, is suppressed. This “exclusion” constraint characterizes the interactions between particles and gives its name to this Markov process. The number of particles is preserved and the goal is then to understand how does the particles’ density evolves in space and time. The transition probability of the underlying random walks is denoted by
p:\mdmathbb Z\times\mdmathbb Z\to[0,1]
.

When the transition probability
p
is translation invariant, i.e.
p(x,y)=p(y-x)
, with zero mean and finite variance, the density is governed by the heat equation with a constant diffusion coefficient equal to the variance of
p
. Different boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Robin, Neumann) can be imposed by putting the system in contact with some stochastic reservoirs. The nature of the boundary conditions depends on the reservoirs’ dynamics and their intensity via some scaling parameter. If
p
has heavy tails, in particular, if the variance is infinite, then the density evolves super-diffusively according to a fractional diffusion equation
[
21
]
, which is a non-local PDE. Putting the system in contact with reservoirs gives then rise to a variety of boundary conditions which are much more difficult to describe than in the diffusive case. This is due to the fact that a boundary condition usually has a local nature while the PDE, in this case, does not have.

The exclusion process we analyze in this work was first introduced in
[
2
,
3
,
5
]
. We recall here how it is defined. Let
N>1
and call “bulk” the lattice
\Lambda_{N}=\{1,\dots,N-1\}
. To the right and to the left of the bulk we add infinitely many stochastic reservoirs, which means that, according to some rules that we will explain in detail later, the sets
\{x\leq 0\;;\;x\in{\mdmathbb Z}\}
and
\{x\geq N\;;\;x\in{\mdmathbb Z}\}
interact with the particles in the bulk, creating or annihilating them. Particles in the bulk move according to an exclusion process with long jumps: this means that particles can perform jumps of any length but they can not jump to a site where there is already a particle (exclusion rule). This kind of dynamics is determined by a symmetric transition probability

(1.1)

p:z\in{\mdmathbb Z}\to p(z)=\frac{c}{|z|^{\gamma+1}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{z\neq 0\}},

depending only on the length of the jump and on a parameter

. Hence the constant

(1.2)

Associated with this transition probability we denote its second moment by

2.1.
The model

The Markov process we consider is analogous to the one introduced in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
, so we will use the same notation.

The dynamics of the process we are interested in is determined by the interactions between particles inside the set, defined for
N\geq 2
by
\Lambda_{N}:=\{1,\cdots,N-1\}
called “bulk”. The evolution of the configuration of particles is described by a function of time
\eta_{\cdot}
, taking values in the space
{}_{N}:=\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{N}}
of functions which associate to any point of
\Lambda_{N}
either the value
1
or
0
. Thus, if we fix some horizon time
T>0
, at time
t\in[0,T]
, the configuration
\eta_{t}
is fully described by the values of
\eta_{t}(x)
for
x\in\Lambda_{N}
: if
\eta_{t}(x)=1
it means that there is a particle at site
x
at time
t
, otherwise if
\eta_{t}(x)=0
it means that the site
x
is empty at time
t
.

The dynamics of this process is explained in detail in
[
2
,
3
]
.
We recall it here briefly. It is easier to understand it separating bulk and reservoirs dynamics:

—

bulk dynamics:
each pair of sites
(x,y)\in\Lambda_{N}\times\Lambda_{N}
carries a Poisson process of intensity one. When there is an occurrence in this process we exchange the value of
\eta(x)
and
\eta(y)
with probability
p(y-x)/2
;

—

reservoirs dynamics
: let us start describing the dynamics associated to the left reservoirs. Every pair of points
(x,y)\in\{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}:x\leq 0\}\times\Lambda_{N}
carries a Poisson process of intensity one. When there is an occurrence in this process we exchange
\eta(y)
with
1-\eta(y)
with rate
\alpha\tfrac{\kappa}{N^{\theta}}p(y-x)
if
\eta(y)=0
and with rate
(1-\alpha)\tfrac{\kappa}{N^{\theta}}p(x,y)
if
\eta(y)=1
. The right reservoirs act in an analogous way: every pair of points
(x,y)\in\{x\in{\mdmathbb Z}:x\geq N\}\times\Lambda_{N}
carries a Poisson process of intensity one. When there is an occurrence in this process we exchange
\eta(y)
with
1-\eta(y)
with rate
\beta\tfrac{\kappa}{N^{\theta}}p(y-x)
if
\eta(y)=0
and with rate
(1-\beta)\tfrac{\kappa}{N^{\theta}}p(y-x)
if
\eta(y)=1
.

The infinitesimal generator of the process considered, which depends on some parameter
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
,
\kappa>0
and
\theta\in\mdmathbb R
, is defined as

(2.1)

L_{N}=L_{N}^{0}+\kappa N^{-\theta}L_{N}^{l}+\kappa N^{-\theta}L_{N}^{r},

and acts on functions
f:{}_{N}\to{\mdmathbb R}
as

(2.2)

\begin{split}&(L^{0}_{N}f)(\eta)=\cfrac{1}{2}\,\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{N}}p(x-y)[f(\sigma^{x,y}\eta)-f(\eta)],\\
&(L_{N}^{l}f)(\eta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
y\leq 0\end{subarray}}p(x-y)c_{x}(\eta;\alpha)[f(\sigma^{x}\eta)-f(\eta)],\\
&(L_{N}^{r}f)(\eta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
y\geq N\end{subarray}}p(x-y)c_{x}(\eta;\beta)[f(\sigma^{x}\eta)-f(\eta)]\end{split}

where

(\sigma^{x,y}\eta)(z)=\begin{cases}\eta(z),&\textrm{if}\;\;z\neq x,y,\\
\eta(y),&\textrm{if}\;\;z=x,\\
\eta(x),&\textrm{if}\;\;z=y\end{cases},\quad(\sigma^{x}\eta)(z)=\begin{cases}\;\;\eta(z),&\textrm{if}\;\;z\neq x,\\
1-\eta(x),&\textrm{if}\;\;z=x,\end{cases}

and for
\delta\in(0,1)
and
x\in\Lambda_{N}
, we define

(2.3)

c_{x}(\eta;\delta):=\left[\eta(x)\left(1-\delta\right)+(1-\eta(x))\delta\right].

We will consider the Markov process in the time scale
t\Theta(N)
, where
\Theta(N)
is defined in (
2.26
). In order to have a lighter notation we will denote
\eta_{t}^{N}:=\eta_{t\Theta(N)}
, so that the process
\eta_{\cdot}^{N}
has as infinitesimal generator
\Theta(N)L_{N}
.

by suppressing its jumps from

to its complement. It is proved that for

\gamma>1

Definition 2.5
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}:=\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}([0,1])
the Sobolev space containing all the functions

such that
\|g\|_{\gamma/2}<\infty
. This is a Hilbert space endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}
defined by

\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}}^{2}:=\|g\|^{2}+\|g\|_{\gamma/2}^{2}.

If
\gamma\in(1,2)
its elements coincide a.e. with continuous functions. The space
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}:={{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}([0,1])
is defined as the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,1))
for the norm just introduced. If
\gamma\in(1,2)
, its elements coincide a.e. with continuous functions vanishing at

and

and as pointed out in
[

]
, on
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}
, the norms

and
\|\cdot\|_{\gamma/2}
are equivalent. If
\gamma\in(0,1]
,

(see Theorem 3, Section 2.4 of

).

Remark 2.6
.

The main difference between the case
\gamma\in(0,1]
and the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
is that the trace on
\{0,1\}
of a function

exists if
\gamma>1
while it does not exist (a priori) if
\gamma\leq 1
.

The weak solutions of the hydrodynamic equations that we will define belong to the space
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
which is the set of measurable functions
f:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}
such that

\int^{T}_{0}\|f_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}}dt<\infty.

The spaces
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\gamma/2})
and
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{h}([0,1]))
are defined in an analogous way, using the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\gamma/2}
and
L^{2}_{h}([0,1])
instead of
\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}
.

The regional fractional Laplacian can be extended to the space
\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}
. Indeed for any

we can define the distribution
\mdmathbb{L}\rho
on
(0,1)
by its action on functions

:

\langle{\mdmathbb L}\rho,f\rangle=\langle\rho,{\mdmathbb L}f\rangle.

The proof that
\mdmathbb{L}\rho
is indeed a well defined distribution can be found in
[
3
]

Definition 2.7

(2.14)

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(u)={\mdmathbb L}\rho_{t}(u),\quad(t,u)\in[0,T]\times(0,1),\\

&{\rho}_{0}(u)=g(u),\quad u\in(0,1),\end{cases}

if :

Let

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.18

3.1.
Heuristics for the hydrodynamic equations

In this section we give the main ideas of the proof of the derivation of the weak solution of the hydrodynamic equation for each regime of
\theta
and
\gamma
. Let us, by now, assume that the probability measure
\mdmathbb Q^{N}
on
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{M}^{+})
defined above, converges to a measure
\mdmathbb Q
on the same space, concentrated on a trajectory of measures
\pi_{t}(du)
, which is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure with a density
\rho(t,u)
. Moreover, we will assume Theorem
3.2
, which will be proved later. This means that we can assume that
\rho\in L^{2}(0,T;{\mathcal{H}}^{\gamma/2})
and that
\rho_{s}(0)
is well-defined for almost every
s\in[0,T]
. We will prove the aforementioned convergence, up to a subsequence, in the next subsections.

For simplicity we will forget the time dependence on the test functions, which would only make the notation heavier without bringing additional difficulties. Therefore, consider a test function
G:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mdmathbb R}
for which
\mdmathbb{L}G
is well defined. From Dynkin’s formula (Lemma A.5 of
[
23
]
) we have that

(3.1)

M_{t}^{N}(G)=\langle\pi_{t}^{N},G\rangle-\langle\pi_{0}^{N},G\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\Theta(N)L_{N}\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G\rangle ds,

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}^{N}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, where
\mathcal{F}^{N}_{t}:=\sigma(\{\eta^{N}_{s}\}_{s\leq t})
for all
t\in[0,T]
. In the previous display we denoted by
\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G\rangle
the integration of the function
G
on
[0,1]
with respect to the empirical measure
\pi^{N}_{s}
, i.e.

\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G\rangle=\dfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right)\eta^{N}_{s}(x).

Now, we compute the explicit value of the integrand function in (
3.1
), which is given by

(3.2)

\begin{split}\Theta(N)&L_{N}\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G\rangle=\cfrac{\Theta(N)}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}({\mathcal{L}}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})\eta^{N}_{s}(x)\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\cfrac{\kappa\Theta(N)}{(N-1)N^{\theta}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\left(r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\alpha-\eta^{N}_{s}(x))+r_{N}^{+}(\tfrac{x}{N}){(\beta-\eta^{N}_{s}(x))}\right),\end{split}

where,
{\mathcal{L}}_{N}G
is defined on test functions
G
by

(3.3)

({{\mathcal{L}}}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})=\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{N}}p(y-x)\left[G(\tfrac{y}{N})-G(\tfrac{x}{N})\right],

for all
x\in\Lambda_{N}
with
({\mathcal{L}}_{N}G)(0)=({\mathcal{L}}_{N}G)(1)=0
and
r^{\pm}(x/N)
are defined as

(3.4)

r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})=\sum_{y\geq x}p(y)\quad\text{ and }\quad r^{+}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})=\sum_{y\leq N-x}p(y).

We have to study the various terms in (
3.2
) in the different regimes of
\gamma\in(0,2)
and
\theta\in\mdmathbb{R}
. Recall that the cases
\theta\leq 0
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
were already treated in
[
3
]
and, for that reason, they will not be considered in this work.

3.1.1.
Case
\theta<0
and
\gamma\in(0,1]

In this regime the time scale is given by
\Theta(N)=N^{\gamma+\theta}
and the test functions are in
C_{c}^{\infty}([0,1])
. Let us first analyze the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
). Recalling (
3.3
) and performing a Taylor expansion of
G
around
x/N
, we can bound from above this term by a constant times

(3.5)

\cfrac{N^{\gamma+\theta}}{N^{2}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
y\neq x\end{subarray}}\cfrac{1}{|y-x|}

plus lower order terms with respect to
N
, since
G^{\prime}
is uniformly bounded and
|\eta^{N}_{s}(x)|\leq 1
. Then, changing variables, that term can be bounded from above by a constant times

(3.6)

\cfrac{N^{\gamma+\theta}}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\cfrac{1}{N}\sum_{z=1}^{x}\cfrac{1}{z}\lesssim N^{\theta-1}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\int_{\tfrac{1}{N}}^{\tfrac{x}{N}}u^{-\gamma}du\lesssim N^{\theta-1}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}(\tfrac{x}{N})^{-\gamma+1}\lesssim N^{\theta},

assuming
\gamma\in(0,1)
. If
\gamma=1
, the left-hand side of (
3.6
) is

(3.7)

N^{\theta}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\cfrac{1}{N}\sum_{z=1}^{x}\cfrac{1}{z^{-1}}\lesssim N^{\theta}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\cfrac{1+\log(x)}{N}\lesssim N^{\theta}[1+\log(N)].

So, since
\theta<0
, the term in the last display vanishes in (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), as
N
goes to infinity.

Let us now analyze the second terms on the right-hand side of (
3.2
). We treat in details just the term involving
r^{-}_{N}
since the other one is completely analogous. Since
G
has compact support, that term can be rewritten as

(3.8)

\begin{split}&\cfrac{\kappa}{N-1}\sum_{x=aN}^{N-1}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\left(Nr_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})-r^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})\right)(\alpha-\eta^{N}_{s}(x))\\
&+\cfrac{\kappa}{N-1}\sum_{x=aN}^{N-1}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\alpha-\eta^{N}_{s}(x)),\end{split}

for some
a\in(0,1)
. We will show now that the term on the left-hand side in the previous display vanishes as
N
goes to infinity. In order to do that, we use the estimate

|Nr^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{z}{N})-r^{-}(\tfrac{z}{N})|\leq cN^{-1}(\tfrac{z}{N})^{-\gamma-1}

when
z/N>a
for some fixed
a\in(0,1)
, proved in the Appendix of
[
5
]
. Using this estimate, the fact that
(\alpha-\eta^{N}_{s}(x))
is uniformly bounded and
G
has compact support, we get that the term on the left-hand side of (
3.8
) is bounded from above by a constant times

(3.9)

\cfrac{\kappa}{N^{2}}\sum_{x=aN}^{N-1}(\tfrac{x}{N})^{-\gamma-1}\lesssim\cfrac{1}{N}\int_{a}^{1}u^{-\gamma-1}du\lesssim N^{-1},

which vanishes as
N
goes to infinity. Finally, we prove that the second term of (
3.8
) converges to

(3.10)

\kappa\int_{0}^{1}\alpha G(u)r^{-}(u)du-\kappa\int_{0}^{1}G(u)r^{-}(u)\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(u)du,

provided that
\pi_{s}^{N}
converges to an absolutely continuous measure, with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, with density
\rho_{s}^{\kappa}(\cdot)
. In order to do that let us denote
H:=Gr^{-}
which is a function in
C_{c}^{\infty}([0,1])
. Then, the last term in (
3.2
) is equal to

(3.11)

\frac{\kappa}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}H(\tfrac{x}{N})(\alpha-\eta_{s}^{N}(x)),

plus terms vanishing as
N
goes to infinity.
Which, under the hypothesis of the convergence of
\pi^{N}
that we just did, is exactly the Riemann series converging to (
3.10
).
Repeating the same proof for the term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) involving
r^{+}_{N}
, we get that the whole term converges to

(3.12)

\kappa\int_{0}^{1}G(u)V_{0}(u)du-\kappa\int_{0}^{1}G(u)V_{1}(u)\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(u)du,

as
N
goes to infinity, which is the term involved in the definition of the weak solution for this regime of
\theta
and
\gamma
.

3.1.2.
Case
\theta=0
and
\gamma\in(0,1]

In this regime the time scale is
\Theta(N)=N
and the test functions are again in
C_{c}^{\infty}([0,1])
. Then, the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) can be written as

(3.13)

\cfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\big(N({\mathcal{L}}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-\mdmathbb{L}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\big)\eta^{N}_{s}(x)+\cfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\mdmathbb{L}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\eta^{N}_{s}(x).

The first term on the left-hand side of the previous display vanishes as
N
goes to infinity, as a consequence of the fact that
|\eta^{N}_{s}(x)|\leq 1
and Lemma
5.1
. Now, using an argument similar to the one used in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, it is possible to approximate the operator
\mdmathbb{L}
by an operator
\mdmathbb{L}_{\epsilon}
in such a way that
\mdmathbb{L}_{\epsilon}G
is a continuous function on
(0,1)
and
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}||\mdmathbb{L}_{\epsilon}G-\mdmathbb{L}G||_{{L^{1}(0,1)}}=0
.

Therefore we can rewrite the term on the right-hand side of (
3.13
) as

\cfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\big[\mdmathbb{L}G(\tfrac{x}{N})-\mdmathbb{L}_{\epsilon}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\big]\eta^{N}_{s}(x)+\cfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\mdmathbb{L}_{\epsilon}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\eta^{N}_{s}(x).

Then, reasoning as we did in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, it is possible to show that the first term of the previous display vanishes as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and the second one converges, as
N\rightarrow\infty
and
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, to

(3.14)

\int_{0}^{1}\mdmathbb{L}G(u)\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(u)du.

The second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) can be treated exactly as in the previous case, where we showed that it converges to (
3.10
)
as
N
goes to infinity. Note that (
3.14
) and (
3.10
) are exactly the terms involved in the definition of weak solution in this regime of
\theta
and
\gamma
.

3.1.3.
Case
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
\theta>0
or
\gamma\in(1,2)
and
\theta>\gamma-1

In these regimes the time scale is again
\Theta(N)=N
and the test functions are in
C^{\infty}([0,1])
. In both cases, the analysis of the first term in
(
3.2
)
is completely analogous to the one that we did in the previous subsection. Indeed, using Lemma
5.1
it is possible to show, with the assumption on the convergence of
\pi^{N}
, that this term converges to (
3.14
) with
G\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
.
This is the only term involved in the definition of weak solution in these regimes. Indeed, we show now that the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity. Since
G(\tfrac{x}{N})
and
\alpha-\eta^{N}_{s}(x)
are bounded, we can bound from above the part involving
r^{-}_{N}
on the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) as a constant times

(3.15)

N^{\gamma-\theta-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N}).

Now, observe that:

—

in the case
\gamma\in(0,1]
the sum in (
3.15
) is diverging so we have to bound it in some way. Observing that
y^{-\gamma-1}
is a decreasing function we can perform the following bound

(3.16)

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}&r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})=c\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\sum_{y\geq x}y^{-\gamma-1}\lesssim r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{1}{N})+\sum_{x=2}^{N-1}\sum_{y\geq x}y^{-\gamma-1}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{x=2}^{N-1}\int_{x-1}^{+\infty}y^{-\gamma-1}dy\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{x=1}^{N-2}x^{-\gamma},\end{split}

since
r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{1}{N})=\frac{1}{2}
. We observe that the sum in the last display can be bounded by
N^{-\gamma+1}
if
\gamma\in(0,1)
and by
\log(N)
if
\gamma=1
. So, we can bound (
3.15
) by

\frac{N^{\gamma-\theta-1}}{2}+\frac{N^{-\theta}}{\gamma}\lesssim N^{-\theta}

if
\gamma\in(0,1)
and by

\frac{N^{-\theta}}{2}+\frac{\log(N)}{\gamma}\lesssim N^{-\theta}[1+\log(N)]

if
\gamma=1
and both vanish, as
N
goes to infinity;

—

in the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
the sum in (
3.15
) is converging
since, in this regime,
\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})
converges to
m
(see
[
3
]
)
. So the order of the whole term is
N^{\gamma-\theta-1}
, therefore it vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity, since
\theta>\gamma-1
.

The term involving
r^{+}_{N}
in (
3.2
) is treated in the same way. Notice that the integration by parts formula (Proposition
2.1
) plus the boundary conditions of Definition
2.7
implies (
2.15
) (under the assumption of convergence of
\pi^{N}
).

3.1.4.
Case
\gamma\in(1,2)
and
0<\theta<\gamma-1

In this regime the time scale is
\Theta(N)=N
and the test functions are in
C^{\infty}_{c}([0,1])
. The analysis of the first term at the right-hand side of (
3.2
) is analogous to the one of the previous case.

Let us then show how we treat the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
). We will use the fact that the test function
G
satisfies
G(x)=0
for
x\in\{0,1\}
. Observe indeed that the term in (
3.2
) involving
r^{-}_{N}
can be rewritten as a constant times

N^{\gamma-\theta-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}x^{-\gamma}G(\tfrac{x}{N}),

since the order of
r^{-}_{N}(x/N)
is
x^{-\gamma}
when we are considering
{x}>aN
for some fixed
a\in(0,1)
(see Lemma 3.3 of
[
5
]
) and this is true whenever the test function has compact support.
Then, performing a Taylor expansion on
G
around
0
we can bound it from above by a constant times

N^{\gamma-\theta-2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}x^{-\gamma+1},

plus lower order terms in
N
.
Therefore, since the sum in the last display is of order
N^{-\gamma+2}
, the order of the whole term in the last display is
N^{-\theta}
, and it vanishes as
N
goes to infinity. The same analysis can be done for the term involving
r^{+}_{N}
in (
3.2
).

The item (iii) of Definition
2.10
also holds, indeed
the fact that
\rho_{t}(0)=\alpha
and
\rho_{t}(1)=\beta
follows by standard arguments thanks to Proposition
5.3
(see Appendix A.4 of
[
13
]
for details).

3.1.5.
Case
\gamma\in(1,2)
and
\theta=\gamma-1

In this regime the time scale is
\Theta(N)=N
and the test functions are in
C^{\infty}([0,1])
. The analysis of the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) is analogous to the previous cases and is done using Lemma
5.1
.

The analysis of the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) is a bit different from the previous cases.

Consider first the part involving
r^{-}_{N}
. For any
\epsilon>0
, we can rewrite it as

\begin{split}&\cfrac{\kappa N}{(N-1)N^{\gamma-1}}(\alpha-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0))\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})\\
&+\cfrac{\kappa N}{(N-1)N^{\gamma-1}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0)-\eta^{N}_{s}(x)),\end{split}

where
\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0)
is defined in (
5.28
).
On the other hand, the term on the right-hand side of the previous display vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity and then as
\epsilon
goes to
0
, thanks to Remark
5.10
.

The term on the left-hand side of the last display, performing a Taylor expansion on
G
, can be written as

(3.17)

\cfrac{\kappa N}{(N-1)N^{\gamma-1}}G(0)(\alpha-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0))\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})+\cfrac{\kappa}{N-1}G^{\prime}(0)(\alpha-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0))\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}xr_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})

plus lower order terms in
N
. Observe that the term on the right-hand side of the previous display can be bounded by a constant times

\cfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}x^{-\gamma+1}=O(N^{-\gamma+1}),

which goes to
0
as
N
goes to infinity, since
\gamma>1
. The remaining term in (
3.17
) converges in some sense to
G(0)(\alpha-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(0))m
, as
N
goes to infinity, since
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})=m
(see, for example,
[
3
]
, for details about this computation) and since, in some sense, well explained in
[
13
]
,
\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0)\rightarrow\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(0)
. Then, reasoning in a similar way as before, we can conclude that, for
N
going to infinity, the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) converges to

mG(0)(\alpha-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(0))+mG(1)(\beta-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(1)).

This is exactly the term involved in (
2.21
) and also that gives us, using the integration by parts formula (see Proposition
2.1
), the boundary conditions in the Definition
2.11
.

3.2.
Tightness

In this subsection we prove that the sequence
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is tight. Despite our approach being standard (see
[
2
]
), we decided to write all the arguments for the convenience of the reader. To prove tightness, we use Proposition 4.1.6 in
[
23
]
, from where it is enough to show that, for all
\epsilon>0

(3.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T},\bar{\tau}\leq\delta}{\mdmathbb{P}}_{\mu_{N}}\Big[\eta_{\cdot}^{N}\in{\mathcal{D}}([0,T],{}_{N}):\left|\langle\pi^{N}_{\tau+\bar{\tau}},G\rangle-\langle\pi^{N}_{\tau},G\rangle\right|>\epsilon\Big]=0,

for any function
G
belonging to
C^{0}([0,1])
. Above
\mathcal{T}_{T}
is the set of stopping times bounded by
T
and we implicitly assume that all the stopping times are bounded by
T
, thus,
\tau+\bar{\tau}
should be read as
(\tau+\bar{\tau})\wedge T
. Indeed, we prove below that (
3.18
) is true for any function in
C_{c}^{2}([0,1])
, and then, by using an
L^{1}
approximation procedure (as it is done in
[
2
]
), we can extend the result to functions in
C^{0}([0,1])
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The sequence of measures
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is tight with respect to the Skorohod topology of
\mathcal{D}([0,T],{\mathcal{M^{+}}})
.

Proof.

The proof is almost analogous to the one presented in
[
3
]
so we will omit most of the details.
We are going to prove (
3.18
) for functions
G
in
C_{c}^{2}([0,1])
. Recall now (
3.1
). Then, in order to prove (
3.18
), it is enough to show that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T},\bar{\tau}\leq\delta}\mathcal{\mdmathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}\left[\Bigg|\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\bar{\tau}}\Theta(N)L_{N}\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G\rangle ds\Bigg|\right]=0

and

(3.20)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T},\bar{\tau}\leq\delta}\mathcal{\mdmathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}\left[\left(M_{\tau}^{N}(G)-M_{\tau+\bar{\tau}}^{N}(G)\right)^{2}\right]=0.

We start with (
3.19
). Observe that we know the explicit form of the term inside the integral in (
3.19
) which is given by (
3.2
). Then,
using Lemma (
5.1
) and the fact that
G\in C_{c}^{2}((0,1))
we can bound the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) by a constant, since the regional fractional Laplacian is well defined on functions with compact support. For the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) we can proceed as follows. Using the fact that
|\eta_{s}^{N}(x)|\leq 1
and a Taylor expansion on
G
around
0
we get that it can be bounded by a constant times

(3.21)

\Theta(N)N^{-\theta-2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}x(r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})+r_{N}^{+}(\tfrac{x}{N}))

plus lower order terms. Since
r^{-}_{N}(x/N)
and
r^{+}_{N}(x/N)
are both of order
x^{-\gamma}
, the sum is diverging and can be bounded by something of order
N^{-\gamma+2}
. This means that the order of the whole previous display is
\Theta(N)N^{-\gamma-\theta}
. So, for every value of
\theta
and
\Theta(N)
this term is of order at most
1
.
Then we have just finished to show that

(3.22)

|\Theta(N)L_{N}(\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle)|\lesssim 1

for any
s\leq T
, which trivially implies (
3.19
).

Now we prove (
3.20
).
By Dynkin’s formula (see Appendix 1 in
[
23
]
) we know that

\displaystyle\left(M^{N}_{t}(G)\right)^{2}-\int^{t}_{0}\Theta(N)\left[L_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle^{2}-2\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle L_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle\right]ds,

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Proceeding as in
[
3
]
it is possible to prove that the previous display is bounded from above by a constant times

(3.23)

\begin{split}&\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{(N-1)^{4}}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{N}}(x-y)^{2}p(x-y)+\dfrac{\kappa\Theta(N)}{(N-1)^{2}N^{\theta}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\left(G\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right)\right)^{2}\left(r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})+r_{N}^{+}(\tfrac{x}{N})\right).\\
\end{split}

Then, we can bound from above the leftmost term in the previous display as

\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{(N-1)^{4}}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{N}}(x-y)^{2}p(x-y)\lesssim\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{(N-1)^{4}}\sum_{x\neq y\in\Lambda_{N}}|x-y|^{1-\gamma}\lesssim\frac{\Theta(N)}{N}N^{-\gamma}\leq N^{-1},

if
\gamma\in(0,1]
. In the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
, the same procedure of
[
3
]
leads to an upper bound of order
N^{\gamma-2}
. The remaining term in (
3.23
) is of order less than
\mathcal{O}(1)
, since performing a Taylor expansion on
G
around
0
we can rewrite it as a constant times

\dfrac{\kappa\Theta(N)}{(N-1)^{2}N^{2+\theta}}(G^{\prime}(0))^{2}\sum_{x=aN}^{N-1}x^{-\gamma+2}

plus lower order terms in
N
, for some
a\in(0,1)
. Then, since the sum is of order
N^{3-\gamma}
, this term is at most of order
\Theta(N)N^{-\gamma-\theta-1}
, which, for any value of
\Theta(N)
given by (
2.26
), is bounded by
N^{-1}
and vanishes as
N
goes to infinity.

Thus, since
\tau
is a bounded stopping time we have that

\begin{split}&\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T},\bar{\tau}\leq\delta}\mathcal{\mdmathbb{E}}_{\mu^{N}}\left[\left(M_{\tau}^{N,G}-M_{\tau+\bar{\tau}}^{N,G}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{T},\bar{\tau}\leq\delta}\mathcal{\mdmathbb{E}}_{\mu^{N}}\left[\int^{\tau+\bar{\tau}}_{\tau}\Theta(N)\left[L_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle^{2}-2\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle L_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle\right]ds\right]\\
&=0.\end{split}

Therefore, we have proved (
3.18
) for functions
G
in
C_{c}^{2}([0,1])
and, as we have recalled in the beginning of the proof, this is enough to conclude tightness.
∎

3.3.
Energy estimates

We prove in this subsection that any limit point
\mdmathbb{Q}
of the sequence
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is concentrated on trajectories
\{\pi_{t}(u)du\}_{t\in[0,T]}
with finite energy, i.e.,
\pi
belongs to the space
L^{2}(0,T;{\mathcal{H}}^{\gamma/2})
. The latter is the content of Theorem
3.2
stated below. Despite our approach being standard (see
[
3
]
), we decided to write the arguments for the convenience of the reader. Fix a limit point
\mdmathbb{Q}
of the sequence
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
and assume, without loss of generality, that the whole sequence
\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}
converges to
\mdmathbb{Q}
, as
N
goes to infinity.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
\theta\in\mdmathbb{R}
and
\gamma\in(0,2)
, the measure
\mdmathbb{Q}
is concentrated on trajectories of measures of the form
\{\pi_{t}(u)du\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, such that for any interval
I\subset[0,T]
the density
\pi
satisfies
\mdmathbb{Q}
-a.s.

i)

\int_{I}\|\pi_{t}\|_{\gamma/2}^{2}dt\lesssim|I|,\;\text{if}\;\theta\geq 0.

ii)

\int_{I}\int_{0}^{1}\Bigg\{\frac{[\alpha-\pi_{t}(u)]^{2}}{u}+\frac{[\beta-\pi_{t}(u)]^{2}}{(1-u)}\Bigg\}dudt\lesssim|I|,\;\text{if}\;\theta\leq 0.

Before we prove Theorem
3.2
, we establish some estimates on the Dirichlet form which are needed for the proof. Let

\rho:[0,1]\rightarrow[\alpha,\beta]
. Let
\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}
be the inhomogeneous Bernoulli product measure on

N
with marginals given by

(3.24)

\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}\{\eta:\eta(x)=1\}=\rho\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right).

We denote by
H_{N}(\mu|\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})
the relative entropy of a probability measure
\mu
on

N
with respect to the probability measure
\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}
. For exclusion processes on a finite domain and for any initial measure
\mu_{N}
, it is easy to prove the existence of a constant
C_{0}
, such that

(3.25)

H(\mu_{N}|\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})\leq C_{0}N,

see for example
[
2
]
for the proof. We remark here that the restriction
\alpha,\beta\notin{\{0,1\}}
comes from last estimate since the constant
C_{0}
above is given by
C_{0}=-\log(\alpha\wedge(1-\beta))
, when
\alpha<\beta
. On the other hand, for a probability measure
\mu
on
N
and a density function
f:{}_{N}\to[0,\infty)
with respect to
\mu
we introduce

(3.26)

D_{N}^{0}(\sqrt{f},\mu):=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{N}}p(y-x)\,I_{x,y}(\sqrt{f},\mu),

(3.27)

D_{N}^{l}(\sqrt{f},\mu):=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\sum_{y\leq 0}p(y-x)\,I^{\alpha}_{x}(\sqrt{f},\mu)=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r_{N}^{-}(\tfrac{x}{N})I^{\alpha}_{x}\,(\sqrt{f},\mu),

(3.28)

D_{N}^{r}(\sqrt{f},\mu):=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\sum_{y\geq N}p(y-x)\,I^{\beta}_{x}(\sqrt{f},\mu)=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r_{N}^{+}(\tfrac{x}{N})I^{\beta}_{x}\,(\sqrt{f},\mu).

Above, we used the following notation

(3.29)

\begin{split}I_{x,y}(\sqrt{f},\mu):=&\int\left(\sqrt{f(\sigma^{x,y}\eta)}-\sqrt{f(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\mu,\\
I_{x}^{\delta}(\sqrt{f},\mu):=&\int c_{x}(\eta;\delta)\left(\sqrt{f(\sigma^{x}\eta)}-\sqrt{f(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\mu,\quad\delta\in\{\alpha,\beta\}.\end{split}

Our goal is to express, for the measure
\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}
, a relation between the Dirichlet form defined by
\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}}
and the quantity

(3.30)

D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}):=(D_{N}^{0}+\kappa N^{-\theta}D_{N}^{l}+\kappa N^{-\theta}D_{N}^{r})(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}).

That relation is given by the following Lemma, already proved in Section 3.3.1 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for any positive constant
B
and any density function
f
with respect to
\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}
, we have that

(3.31)

\begin{split}&\frac{\Theta(N)}{NB}\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}}\\
&\leq-\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{4NB}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})+\dfrac{C\Theta(N)}{NB}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{N}}p(y-x)\Big(\rho(\tfrac{x}{N})-\rho(\tfrac{y}{N})\Big)^{2}\\
&+\dfrac{C\kappa\Theta(N)}{N^{\theta+1}B}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\left\{\Big(\rho(\tfrac{x}{N})-\alpha\Big)^{2}r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})+\Big(\rho(\tfrac{x}{N})-\beta\Big)^{2}r^{+}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})\right\}.\end{split}

Remark 3.4
.

Note that, as a consequence of the previous lemma,
for a Lipschitz function
\rho(\cdot)
such that
\alpha\leq\rho(u)\leq\beta
and
\rho(0)=\alpha
and
\rho(1)=\beta
, we have that

(3.32)

\begin{split}\frac{\Theta(N)}{NB}\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}}&\leq-\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{4NB}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})+\frac{C\Theta(N)(\kappa+N^{\theta})}{BN^{\gamma+\theta}}.\end{split}

Remark 3.5
.

Recasting the bound found in Lemma
3.3
when
\rho(\cdot)
is equal to a constant in
(0,1)
we get that:

a)

if
\gamma\in(1,2)
, then

(3.33)

\begin{split}\frac{\Theta(N)}{NB}\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}}&\leq-\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{4NB}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})+\frac{C\kappa\Theta(N)}{BN^{\theta+1}}\end{split}

b)

if
\gamma=1
, then

(3.34)

\begin{split}\frac{\Theta(N)}{NB}\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}}&\leq-\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{4NB}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})+\frac{C\kappa\Theta(N)\log(N)}{BN^{\theta+1}},\end{split}

c)

if
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then

(3.35)

\begin{split}\frac{\Theta(N)}{NB}\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}}&\leq-\dfrac{\Theta(N)}{4NB}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N})+\frac{C\kappa\Theta(N)}{BN^{\gamma+\theta}}.\end{split}

The difference between the previous bounds is a consequence of the behavior of the sum
\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}r_{N}^{\pm}(\tfrac{x}{N})
according to the value of
\gamma
.

Remark 3.6
.

Now we can establish the properties that we need to impose on the profile
\rho(\cdot)
in order to have a good approximation of
\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)}}
via
D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)})
. This is equivalent to find the condition on
\rho(\cdot)
such that the last two terms on the right-hand side of (
3.31
) vanish when first
N\to+\infty
and then
B\to+\infty
. Thanks to the previous remarks we make the choice:

—

\rho(\cdot)=\rho\in(0,1)
constant if
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\theta\geq 0
or if
\gamma=1
and
\theta>0
or if
\gamma\in(1,2)
and
\theta\geq\gamma-1
;

—

\rho(\cdot)
Lipschitz such that
\rho(0)=\alpha
and
\rho(1)=\beta
in all the other regimes.

3.3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.2

The proof of the second item is exactly the proof of item ii) of Theorem 3.2 in
[
3
]
and will be omitted. The proof of the first item is almost analogous to the proof of item i) of Theorem 3.2 in
[
3
]
(where the authors proved it for the regime
\gamma\in(1,2)
and
\theta\leq 0
). We write here the main steps.
We want to show that
\pi_{\cdot}\in L^{2}(0,T;{\mathcal{H}}^{\gamma/2})

\mdmathbb{Q}
-almost surely. In order to do that we will show the next bound

(3.36)

\mdmathbb{E}_{\mdmathbb{Q}}\Bigg[\int_{I}\|\pi_{t}\|^{2}_{\gamma/2}dt\Bigg]\lesssim|I|,

which implies the result in the statement above.

Recall that the system is speeded up on the time scale
\Theta(N)
, defined by (
2.26
). Let
\rho(\cdot)
be a macroscopic profile chosen according to Remark
3.6
. Let
\epsilon>0
be a small real number. Let
F\in C_{c}^{0,\infty}(I\times[0,1]^{2})
, where
I
is a subinterval of
[0,T]
. By the entropy and Jensen’s inequality and Feynman-Kac’s formula (see Lemma A.7.2 in
[
23
]
), we have that

(3.37)

\begin{split}&{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\int_{I}\;\Theta(N)N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}F_{t}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})p(y-x)(\eta^{N}_{t}(y)-\eta^{N}_{t}(x))dt\Bigg]\\
&\leq C_{0}+\int_{I}\sup_{f}\Bigg\{\Theta(N)N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\varepsilon N\end{subarray}}F_{t}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})p(y-x)\int(\eta(y)-\eta(x))f(\eta)d\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\Theta(N)N^{-1}\left\langle L_{N}{\sqrt{f}},{\sqrt{f}}\right\rangle_{\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)}}\Bigg\}dt\end{split}

where the supremum is taken over all densities
f
on
N
with respect to
\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}
. Now, denoting the antisymmetric part of
F_{t}
by
F_{t}^{a}
with

F_{t}^{a}(u,v)=\cfrac{1}{2}\Big[F_{t}(u,v)-F_{t}(v,u)\Big]

for all
t\in I
and
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
, we can rewrite the first term inside the supremum in (
3.37
) as

(3.38)

\begin{split}\Theta(N)&N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}F_{t}^{a}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})p(y-x)\int(\eta(y)-\eta(x))f(\eta)d\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)}\\
=\,&\Theta(N)N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}F_{t}^{a}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})p(y-x)\int\eta(y)\left(f(\eta)-f(\sigma^{x,y}\eta)\right)d\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)}\\
+\,&\Theta(N)N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}F_{t}^{a}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})p(y-x)\int\eta(x)f(\eta)\left(\theta^{x,y}(\eta)-1\right)d\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)},\end{split}

where the equality follows from the change of variables
\eta\mapsto\sigma^{x,y}\eta
and

\theta^{x,y}(\eta)=\frac{d\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}(\sigma^{x,y}\eta)}{d\nu_{\rho(\cdot)}^{N}(\eta)}.

Now, we have to split the proof in two cases: the case in which
\rho(\cdot)=\rho
is constant (corresponding to the regimes
(\theta,\gamma)\in[0,\infty)\times(0,1)
,
(\theta,\gamma)\in(0,\infty)\times\{1\}
and
(\theta,\gamma)\in[\gamma-1,\infty)\times(1,2)
) and the case in which
\rho(\cdot)
is a Lipschitz profile (corresponding to the regime
(\theta,\gamma)\in(0,\gamma-1)\times(1,2)
).

In the case in which
\rho(\cdot)=\rho
is constant we have that
\theta^{x,y}(\eta)-1=0
. Therefore, by Young’s inequality, the fact that
f
is a density and
|\eta(y)|\leq 1
, we have that, for any
A>0
, (
3.38
) is bounded from above by a constant times

{cA\Theta(N)N^{-2-\gamma}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}\cfrac{\Big(F_{t}^{a}\Big(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N}\Big)\Big)^{2}}{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\tfrac{y}{N}|^{1+\gamma}}\;+\;\dfrac{2\Theta(N)N^{-1}}{A}D^{0}_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu^{N}_{\rho(\cdot)}).

Then, using (
3.33
), (
3.34
) and (
3.35
), we can bound
(
3.37
) in the following way:

(3.39)

\begin{split}&{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\int_{I}\Theta(N)N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\varepsilon N\end{subarray}}F^{a}_{t}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})p(y-x)(\eta_{t}^{N}(y)-\eta_{t}^{N}(x))\,dt\bigg]\\
&={\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{N}}\left[\int_{I}-2c\langle\pi^{N}_{t},g^{N}_{t}\rangle\,dt\right]\lesssim\int_{I}N^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda_{N}\\
|x-y|\geq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}\cfrac{c\left(F_{t}^{a}(\tfrac{x}{N},\tfrac{y}{N})\right)^{2}}{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\tfrac{y}{N}|^{1+\gamma}}dt+|I|(\kappa+1),\end{split}

where the function
g^{N}
is defined on
I\times[0,1]
by

g_{t}^{N}(u)=\cfrac{1}{N-1}\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{N}}\,\textbf{1}_{\big|\tfrac{y}{N}-u\big|\geq\epsilon}\cfrac{F_{t}^{a}\big(u,\tfrac{y}{N}\big)}{|u-\tfrac{y}{N}|^{1+\gamma}}

and it is a discretization of the smooth function
g
defined on
(t,u)\in I\times[0,1]
by

\quad g_{t}(u)=\int_{0}^{1}\textbf{1}_{\{|v-u|\geq\epsilon\}}\cfrac{F_{t}^{a}(u,v)}{|u-v|^{1+\gamma}}\,dv.

Now, we can proceed exactly in the same way as in the proof of Theorem 3.2 of
[
3
]
to conclude that

(3.40)

\begin{split}&{{\mdmathbb E}}_{\mdmathbb{Q}}\left[\sup_{F}\left\{\int_{I}\iint_{Q_{\epsilon}}\cfrac{(\pi_{t}(v)-\pi_{t}(u))F_{t}(u,v)}{|u-v|^{1+\gamma}}\;-C\cfrac{\big(F_{t}(u,v)\big)^{2}}{|u-v|^{1+\gamma}}\;dudvdt\right\}\right]\\
&\lesssim|I|(\kappa+1),\end{split}

where
Q_{\epsilon}=\{(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}\;;\;|u-v|\geq\epsilon\}
. That bound, passing to the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, by the monotone convergence Theorem, implies the statement (
3.36
).

The case in which
\rho(\cdot):[0,1]\to[\alpha,\beta]
is Lipschitz continuous and such that
\rho(0)=\alpha
and
\rho(1)=\beta
can be analyzed exactly in the same way as it was proved in Theorem 3.2 of
[
3
]
therefore we will omit this part of the proof.

3.4.
Characterization of the limit points

In this subsection we characterize all limit points
\mdmathbb{Q}
of the sequence
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
, which we know that exist from Proposition
3.1
. Despite our approach being standard (see
[
2
,
3
]
), we decided to write the arguments for the convenience of the reader. Let us assume without loss of generality, that
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
converges to
\mdmathbb{Q}
, as
N
goes to infinity. Since there is at most one particle per site, it is easy to show that
\mdmathbb{Q}
is concentrated on trajectories of measures absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, i.e.
\pi_{t}^{\kappa}(du)=\rho_{t}^{\kappa}(u)du
(for details see
[
23
]
). In Proposition
3.7
below we prove, for each range of
\theta
and
\gamma
, that
\mdmathbb{Q}
is concentrated on trajectories of measures whose density
\rho_{t}^{k}

satisfies a weak form of the corresponding hydrodynamic equation. Moreover, we have seen in Theorem
3.2
that
\mdmathbb{Q}
is concentrated on trajectories of measures whose density satisfies the energy estimate, i.e.
\rho^{\kappa}\in L^{2}(0,T;{\mathcal{H}}^{\gamma/2})
when
\theta\geq 0
and
\gamma\in(0,2)
.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
{\mdmathbb Q}
is a limit point of
\{{\mdmathbb Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
then

\displaystyle{\mdmathbb Q}\left(\pi_{\cdot}\in{\mathcal{D}}([0,T],{{\mathcal{M}}^{+}}):F_{\theta,\gamma}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,g)=0,\forall t\in[0,T],\for]\forall G\in C_{\theta,\gamma}\right)=1,

for each
\theta
and each
\gamma
, where
F_{\theta,\gamma}
is defined by

(3.41)

\begin{split}&F_{\theta,\gamma}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,g)\\
&\quad:=\begin{cases}F_{Reac}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,g)&\text{ if }\theta<0,\gamma\in(0,1];\\
F_{RD}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,g)&\text{ if }\theta=0,\gamma\in(0,1];\\
F_{Neu}(t,\rho^{0},G,g)&\text{ if }\theta>0,\gamma\in(0,1]\text{ or }\theta>\gamma-1,\gamma\in(1,2);\\
F_{Rob}(t,\rho,G,g)&\text{ if }\theta=\gamma-1,\gamma\in(1,2);\\
F_{Dir}(t,\rho,G,g)&\text{ if }\theta\in(0,\gamma-1),\gamma\in(1,2).\end{cases}\end{split}

where
C_{\theta,\gamma}=C^{1,2}([0,T]\times[0,1])
if
\theta>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
or if
\theta\geq\gamma-1
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
, while in all the other regimes it is
C_{\theta,\gamma}=C_{c}^{1,2}([0,T]\times[0,1])
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the one in
[
3
]
and for that reason some details are omitted.
Note that in order to prove the proposition, it is enough to verify, for
\delta>0
and
G
in the corresponding space of test functions, that

(3.42)

{\mdmathbb Q}\left(\pi_{\cdot}\in{\mathcal{D}}([0,T],{{\mathcal{M}}^{+}}):\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|F_{\theta,\gamma}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,g)\right|>\delta\right)=0,

for each
\theta
and each
\gamma
in the respective domains.

We have that

(3.43)

\begin{split}&F_{\theta,\gamma}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,g)\\
&=\left\langle\rho^{\kappa}_{t},G_{t}\right\rangle-\left\langle g,G_{0}\right\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\rho^{\kappa}_{s},\Big(\partial_{s}+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\geq 0\}}{\mdmathbb L}\Big)G_{s}\right\rangle ds\\
&+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\leq 0\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(0,1]\}}\kappa\int^{t}_{0}\left\langle\rho_{s}^{\kappa},G_{s}\right\rangle_{V_{1}}ds-\int^{t}_{0}\left\langle G_{s},V_{0}\right\rangle ds\\
&-\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta=\gamma-1\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(1,2)\}}\kappa\int_{0}^{t}mG_{s}(0)(\alpha-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(0))+mG_{s}(1)(\beta-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(1))ds.\end{split}

From here on, in order to simplify notation, we will erase
\pi_{\cdot}
from the sets that we have to look at.
By (
3.43
) we can bound from above (
3.42
) by the sum of

(3.44)

{\mdmathbb Q}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|F_{\theta,\gamma}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,\rho_{0})\right|>\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right)

and

(3.45)

{\mdmathbb Q}\left(\left|\left\langle\rho_{0}-g,G_{0}\right\rangle\right|>\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right).

We note that (
3.45
) is equal to zero since
\mdmathbb Q
is a limit point of
\{\mdmathbb Q^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
and
\mdmathbb Q^{N}
is induced by
\mu_{N}
which is associated to
g(\cdot)
. Now we deal with (
3.44
).
Note that by Proposition A.3 of
[
10
]
, the set inside the probability in (
3.44
) is an open set in the Skorohod topology. Therefore, from Portmanteau’s Theorem we bound (
3.44
) from above by

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\,{\mdmathbb Q}^{N}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|F_{\theta,\gamma}(t,\rho^{\kappa},G,\rho_{0})\right|>\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right).

Summing and subtracting
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\Theta(N)L_{N}\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G_{s}\rangle ds
to the term inside the previous absolute value, recalling (
3.1
), (
3.43
) and the definition of
\mdmathbb Q^{N}
, we can bound the previous probability from above by the sum of

{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{N}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|M_{t}^{N}(G)\right|>\dfrac{\delta}{4}\right)

and

(3.46)

\begin{split}&{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\Theta(N)L_{N}\langle\pi_{s}^{N},G_{s}\rangle ds-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\rho^{\kappa}_{s},\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\geq 0\}}{\mdmathbb L}G_{s}\right\rangle ds\\
&+\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\leq 0\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(0,1]\}}\kappa\int^{t}_{0}\left\langle\rho_{s}^{\kappa},G_{s}\right\rangle_{V_{1}}ds-\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\leq 0\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(0,1]\}}\kappa\int^{t}_{0}\left\langle G_{s},V_{0}\right\rangle ds\\
-&\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta=\gamma-1\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(1,2)\}}\kappa\int_{0}^{t}mG_{s}(0)(\alpha-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(0))+mG_{s}(1)(\beta-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(1))ds\Big|>\dfrac{\delta}{4}\bigg).\end{split}

By Doob’s inequality we have that

\begin{split}&{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{N}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|M_{t}^{N}(G)\right|>\dfrac{\delta}{4}\right)\\
&\lesssim\dfrac{1}{\delta^{2}}{\mdmathbb E}_{\mu_{N}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\Theta(N)\left[L_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle^{2}-2\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle L_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},G\rangle\right]ds\right].\end{split}

In the proof of Proposition
3.1
we have proved that the term inside the time integral in the previous expression vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity. It remains to prove that (
3.46
) vanishes as well when we send
N
to infinity. Recalling (
3.2
), we can bound (
3.46
) from above by the sum of the following terms

(3.47)

{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{N}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\cfrac{\Theta(N)}{N-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\mathcal{L}_{N}G_{s}(\tfrac{x}{N})\eta_{s}^{N}(x)ds-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\rho^{\kappa}_{s},\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\geq 0\}}{\mdmathbb L}G_{s}\right\rangle ds\right|>\dfrac{\delta}{2^{4}}\right),

(3.48)

{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg\{\dfrac{\kappa\Theta(N)}{N^{\theta}(N-1)}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}(G_{s}r_{N}^{-})(\tfrac{x}{N}){(\alpha-\eta_{s}^{N}(x))}\\
-\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\leq 0\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(0,1]\}}\kappa\int_{0}^{1}(G_{s}r^{-})(u)(\alpha-\rho_{s}^{\kappa}(u))du\\
-\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta=\gamma-1\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(1,2)\}}\kappa mG_{s}(0)(\alpha-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(0))\bigg\}ds\bigg|>\dfrac{\delta}{2^{4}}\Bigg).

and

(3.49)

{\mdmathbb P}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg\{\dfrac{\kappa\Theta(N)}{N^{\theta}(N-1)}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}(G_{s}r_{N}^{+})(\tfrac{x}{N}){(\beta-\eta_{s}^{N}(x))}\\
-\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta\leq 0\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(0,1]\}}\kappa\int_{0}^{1}(G_{s}r^{+})(u)(\beta-\rho_{s}^{\kappa}(u))du\\
-\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\theta=\gamma-1\}}\mdmathbb{1}_{\{\gamma\in(1,2)\}}\kappa mG_{s}(1)(\beta-\rho^{\kappa}_{s}(1))\bigg\}ds\bigg|>\dfrac{\delta}{2^{4}}\Bigg).

For
\theta\geq 0
, since
\Theta(N)=N
and Lemma
5.1
holds for any
G
at least
C^{2}
in the space variable, applying Markov’s inequality we have that (
3.47
) goes to
0
, as
N
goes to infinity. For
\theta<0
and
\gamma\in(0,1]

we also have that (
3.47
) vanishes as
N
goes to infinity thanks to Markov’s inequality and the same analysis that we did in Subsection
3.1.1
.
The boundary terms (
3.48
) and (
3.49
) can be treated in a similar way, therefore we explain in details why they vanish just referring to (
3.48
). In the case
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
\theta\leq 0
, it is enough to use Markov’s inequality and what we did in Subsection
3.1.1
to show that (
3.48
) vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity. In the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
and
\theta=\gamma-1
we again use Markov’s inequality and then we repeat the computations we did in Subsection
3.1.5
to see that also in this case (
3.48
) vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity. In all the other regimes that we are considering the only term we have to estimate using Markov’s inequality is

(3.50)

\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg\{\dfrac{\kappa N}{N^{\theta}(N-1)}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}(G_{s}r_{N}^{-})(\tfrac{x}{N}){(\beta-\eta_{s}^{N}(x))}ds\bigg|\Bigg]

and it vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity, thanks to the analysis that we did in Section
3.1
relative to these regimes.
This finishes the proof of Proposition
3.7
.
∎

3.5.
Conclusion

Note that Proposition
3.1
, Theorem
3.2
and Proposition
3.7
just proved, imply that the sequence
\{\mdmathbb{Q}^{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
converges up to subsequences to a measure
\mdmathbb{Q}
concentrated on an absolutely continuous measure with respect to the Lebesgue measure of density
\rho^{\kappa}
, where
\rho^{\kappa}
is the weak solution of the hydrodynamic equation chosen accordingly to the values of the parameters of the model (as stated in Theorem
2.18
). Moreover, thanks to Proposition
2.16
which grants the uniqueness of the weak solutions, we can conclude that this limiting measure is unique and this proves completely Theorem
2.18
.

4.
Uniqueness of weak solutions

In this section we prove uniqueness of the weak solutions we defined in Subsection
2.3
. Since the PDEs are linear, the strategy is standard. We consider two weak solutions starting from the same initial condition, take their difference and write down the integral formulation for the difference of the two solutions. Then, chose a proper test function, plug it into the integral formulation and from there obtain that the difference is
0
in some topology, which implies the uniqueness result we need.

4.1.
Uniqueness of weak solutions of (
2.14
)

Fix
T>0
and
\gamma\in(0,2)
. Consider
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
two weak solutions of (
2.14
) starting from the same initial condition and denote by
\rho
their difference:
\rho=\rho^{1}-\rho^{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
. The set
C^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1))
is dense in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
. Therefore we can consider a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}\subset C^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1))
converging to
\rho
with respect to the norm of
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
. Now, we consider a special set of test functions, defined as
H_{n}(t,u):=\int_{t}^{T}G_{n}(s,u)ds
, for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
u\in[0,1]
. It is easy to check that
H_{n}
are suitable test functions. Now we state the Lemma 6.1 of
[
3
]
adapting its proof to our case.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
defined as above. Then we have

(1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\rho_{s},\partial_{s}H_{n}(s,\cdot)\rangle ds=-\int_{0}^{T}\|\rho_{s}\|^{2}ds
;

(2)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\rho_{s},\mdmathbb LH_{n}(s,\cdot)\rangle ds=-\cfrac{1}{2}\Big|\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{s}ds\Big|\Big|_{\gamma/2}^{2}
;

Proof.

The proof of item (1) is completely analogous to the one which can be found in
[
3
]
. Let us now prove item (2). Since
H_{n}(s,\cdot)
is at least
C^{2}([0,1])
and
\rho(s,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}([0,1])\cap\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2}
for almost every
s\in[0,T]
, we can apply Proposition
2.1
and get that

(4.1)

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\rho_{s},\mdmathbb{L}H_{n}(s,\cdot)\rangle ds=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\rho_{s},H_{n}(s,\cdot)\rangle_{\gamma/2}ds.

The rest of the proof of item (2) is then analogous to the one given in
[
3
]
.
∎

Thanks to this Lemma, we just have to plug in (
2.19
) the test function
H_{n}
, and then pass to the limit when
n
goes to infinity, as done in
[
3
]
, to get

\int_{0}^{T}||\rho_{s}||^{2}ds+\cfrac{1}{2}\Big|\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{s}ds\Big|\Big|_{\gamma/2}^{2}=0.

This implies that for almost every time
s\in[0,T]
the function
\rho_{s}=0
and this means that
\rho^{1}
coincides with
\rho^{2}
almost at every time. So, the weak solution of (
2.14
) is unique.

4.2.
Uniqueness of weak solutions of (
2.16
) for
\gamma\neq 1
.

The uniqueness of the solution to (
2.16
) in the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
has been proved in
[
2
]
. Hence we assume now
\gamma\in(0,1)
and prove the uniqueness of the weak solution to (
2.16
). The proof relies on the following Hardy’s inequality proved in Corollary 2.4 in
[
8
]
:

(4.2)

\forall H\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,1)),\quad\|H\|_{V_{1}}\lesssim\|H\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}.

Since
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,1))
is dense in
L^{2}_{V_{1}}([0,1])
and in
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}
, and since for
\gamma<1
we have that
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}={{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}
, then (
4.2
) holds for any
H\in L^{2}_{V_{1}}([0,1])\cap{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}
. Once this observed, the proof follows the same arguments as in
[
2
]
for the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
.

Remark 4.2
.

We observe that the crucial difference between the case
\gamma\in(1,2)
and the case
\gamma\in(0,1)
is that in the former case, the weak solutions are assumed to satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions so that the difference of two weak solutions belongs to
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}
and Hardy’s inequality can be used for the difference. For
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we do not know if weak solutions satisfy, in the strong sense, Dirichlet boundary conditions. Hence the difference of two weak solutions does not necessarily vanishes at the boundary (we do not even know if it has a continuous representative). Fortunately if
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}^{\gamma/2}={{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}
, so that it is not a real problem.

Remark 4.3
.

We observe that the uniqueness in the case
\gamma=1
is still open. The crucial point in the proof presented above is the fractional Hardy’s inequality (
4.2
), but we did not find in the literature a result indicating that (
4.2
) holds for
\gamma=1
.

4.3.
Uniqueness of weak solutions of (
2.18
)

We have
\gamma\in(1,2)
. Fix
T>0
. Consider
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
two weak solutions of (
2.18
) starting from the same initial condition and denote by
\rho
their difference:
\rho=\rho^{1}-\rho^{2}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
. Since the continuous representatives of
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
satisfy Dirichlet boundary conditions, we have in fact that
\rho\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\gamma/2})
. The set
C_{c}^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1))
is dense in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\gamma/2})
. Therefore we can consider a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}
of of functions in
C_{c}^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1))
converging to
\rho
with respect to the norm of
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\gamma/2})
. Now, we consider a special set of test functions, defined as
H_{n}(t,u):=\int_{t}^{T}G_{n}(s,u)ds
, for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
u\in[0,1]
. It is easy to check that
H_{n}
are suitable test functions that can be used in (
2.19
). Lemma
4.1
holds and we get then

\int_{0}^{T}||\rho_{s}||^{2}ds+\cfrac{1}{2}\,\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\rho_{s}ds\right\|_{\gamma/2}^{2}=0

which implies that
\rho=0
and shows uniqueness.

4.4.
Uniqueness of weak solutions of (
2.20
)

We have
\gamma\in(1,2)
. Fix
T>0
. Consider two weak solutions of (
2.20
) denoted by
\rho^{\hat{\kappa},1}
and
\rho^{\hat{\kappa},2}
, starting from the same initial condition. Let the difference be
\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}=\rho^{\hat{\kappa},1}-\rho^{\hat{\kappa},2}
. We have that
\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
. Since
\gamma>1
we may consider a continuous representative (for a.e. time) of
\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}
. The set
C^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1))
is dense in
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
. Therefore, we can consider a sequence
\{G^{\hat{\kappa}}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mdmathbb N}}\subset C^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1))
converging to
\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}
with respect to the norm of
L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{\gamma/2})
, and we use as test functions
H^{\hat{\kappa}}_{n}(t,u):=\int_{t}^{T}G^{\hat{\kappa}}_{n}(s,u)ds
, for any
t\in[0,T]
and any
u\in[0,1]
. Observe that Lemma
4.1
holds for these test functions. Plugging the test functions
H^{\hat{\kappa}}_{n}
in (
2.21
) and passing to the limit as
n
goes to infinity we get

(4.3)

\int_{0}^{T}||\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}||^{2}ds+\cfrac{1}{2}\Big|\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}ds\Big|\Big|_{\gamma/2}^{2}+\cfrac{\hat{\kappa}m}{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}(0)ds\Big|^{2}+\cfrac{\hat{\kappa}m}{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}(1)ds\Big|^{2}=0,

if we can prove that

(4.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}H_{n}^{\hat{\kappa}}(s,0)\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}(0)ds=\cfrac{1}{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}(0)ds\Big|^{2}

and similarly with the point
0
replaced by the point
1
. This will imply the uniqueness of weak solutions for (
2.20
).

To prove (
4.4
) we observe, by using Fubini’s Theorem, that

\int_{0}^{T}H_{n}^{\hat{\kappa}}(s,0)\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}(0)ds=\iint_{0\leq s\leq r\leq T}{\hat{G}}_{n}^{\hat{\kappa}}(r,0){\rho}^{\hat{\kappa}}_{s}(0)drds.

To conclude it is sufficient to prove that
G_{n}^{\hat{\kappa}}(\cdot,0)
converges to
\rho_{\cdot}^{\hat{\kappa}}(0)
in the space
L^{2}([0,T],dt)
. Let us denote
f_{n}=G_{n}^{\hat{\kappa}}-\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}
. We know that
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mdmathbb N}
converges to
0
in the space
L^{2}([0,T];{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2})
, as
n
goes to infinity. By Theorem 8.2 in
[
9
]
we have that for any
s\in[0,T]

\sup_{u,v\in[0,1]^{2}}|f_{n}(s,u)-f_{n}(s,v)|\;\lesssim\;|u-v|^{\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}}\,\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}\lesssim\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}.

By the triangular inequality, averaging and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get that for any
s\in[0,T]
and any
0<R<1

\begin{split}|f_{n}(s,0)|&\lesssim\cfrac{1}{R}\int_{0}^{R}|f_{n}(s,v)|dv\,+\,\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}\\
&\lesssim\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\,\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|\,+\,\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}\,\lesssim\,\cfrac{1}{\sqrt{R}}\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}.\end{split}

It follows that

\int_{0}^{T}|f_{n}(s,0)|^{2}ds\lesssim\cfrac{1}{R}\int_{0}^{T}\|f_{n}(s,\cdot)\|_{{{\mathcal{H}}}^{\gamma/2}}^{2}\,ds.

The right-hand side of the previous inequality goes to
0
, as
n
goes to infinity, so that the same holds for the left-hand side. This completes the proof.

4.5.
Uniqueness of weak solutions of (
2.22
)

Consider two weak solutions
\rho^{\hat{\kappa},1}
and
\rho^{\hat{\kappa},2}
, and note that their difference
\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}:=\rho^{\hat{\kappa},1}-\rho^{\hat{\kappa},2}
belongs to
L^{2}(0,T;V_{1})
. Observe that

\displaystyle\forall H\in C_{c}^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times(0,1)),\forall s\in[0,T],\quad\|H(s,\cdot)\|\lesssim\|H(s,\cdot)\|_{V_{1}}.

Since
C_{c}^{1,\infty}\big([0,T]\times(0,1)\big)
is dense in
L^{2}(0,T;V_{1})
, it is enough to approximate
\rho^{\hat{\kappa}}
in
L^{2}(0,T;V_{1})
by a sequence
(H_{n})_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}}
in

. Repeating the procedure analogous to the one in
[
3
]
for the proof of uniqueness of (

) in the regime

, we get the desired result.

5.
Technical lemmas

In this section we state and prove the main lemmas we used in this work.

5.1.
Convergence of a discrete operator to the regional fractional Laplacian

The first lemma we prove in this section establishes the convergence in
L^{1}
of the discrete operator
N\mathcal{L}_{N}
defined by (
3.3
) to the regional fractional Laplacian
\mdmathbb L
when applied to smooth test functions.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
G\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
and
\gamma\in(0,2)
, the following convergence holds

(5.1)

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|=0.

Proof.

Fix a small
\epsilon>0
, then the expression inside the limit in (
5.1
) is equal to

(5.2)

\begin{split}N^{-1}&\Bigg\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq x\leq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\\
&+\sum_{\epsilon N<x\leq({N-1})/{2}}|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\\
&+\sum_{(N-1)/2<x<N-1-\epsilon N}|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\\
&+\sum_{N-1-\epsilon N\leq x\leq N-1}|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\Bigg\}.\end{split}

We will analyze the four terms above when
N\rightarrow\infty
and afterwards
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. From the triangular inequality, the first one is bounded from above by

(5.3)

N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|+N^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq x\leq\epsilon N\end{subarray}}|(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|.

Now, notice that in this case (
1\leq x\leq\epsilon N
) we can write

(5.4)

(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})=\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}p(y)\theta_{\tfrac{x}{N}}(\tfrac{y}{N})+\sum_{y=x}^{N-1-x}p(y)(G(\tfrac{x+y}{N})-G(\tfrac{x}{N})),

where we introduced

(5.5)

\theta_{\tfrac{x}{N}}(z):=G(\tfrac{x}{N}+z)+G(\tfrac{x}{N}-z)-2G(\tfrac{x}{N}).

Therefore, the term on the left-hand side of (
5.3
) is bounded from above by

(5.6)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}\Big|N\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}p(y)\theta_{\tfrac{x}{N}}(\tfrac{y}{N})\Big|+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}\Big|N\sum_{y=x}^{N-1-x}p(y)(G(\tfrac{x+y}{N})-G(\tfrac{x}{N}))\Big|.

Performing a second order Taylor expansion in
\theta_{\tfrac{x}{N}}
, we can bound from above the term on the left-hand side of the previous display in the following way:

(5.7)

\begin{split}N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}\Big|N\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}p(y)\theta_{\tfrac{x}{N}}(\tfrac{y}{N})\Big|&\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}\,N^{\gamma-2}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}y^{1-\gamma}\\
&\lesssim N^{\gamma-3}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}x^{2-\gamma}\lesssim\epsilon^{3-\gamma}\end{split}

which goes to
0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
In order to prove that the first term of (
5.3
) goes to
0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
and then
\epsilon\rightarrow\infty
, we still have to treat the term on the right-hand side of (
5.6
). Performing a Taylor expansion on
G
, we get

(5.8)

\begin{split}&N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}\Big|N\sum_{y=x}^{N-1-x}p(y)(G(\tfrac{x+y}{N})-G(\tfrac{x}{N}))\Big|\\
&\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}N^{\gamma-1}\sum_{y=x}^{N-1-x}{y}^{-\gamma}\\
&\lesssim N^{\gamma-2}\sum_{y=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq x\leq\epsilon N\\
x\leq\inf\{y,N-1-y\}\end{subarray}}y^{-\gamma}\\
&\lesssim N^{\gamma-2}\Big[\sum_{y\leq\epsilon N}y^{-\gamma+1}+\sum_{y>\epsilon N}\epsilon Ny^{-\gamma}\Big]\lesssim\epsilon^{-\gamma+2}\end{split}

and so it goes to
0
for
\gamma<2
when we pass to the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Now, we have to estimate the term on the right-hand side of (
5.3
). In order to do that, we first get an upper bound for
\big|\mdmathbb{L}G(\tfrac{x}{N})\big|
for
1\leq x\leq\epsilon N
. By definition of the operator
\mdmathbb{L}
, we have that

(5.9)

\mdmathbb{L}G(\tfrac{x}{N}):=c\lim_{\epsilon^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{1}\mdmathbb{1}_{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|>\epsilon^{\prime}}\frac{G(\nu)-G(\tfrac{x}{N})}{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|^{\gamma+1}}d\nu.

We use a first order Taylor expansion to write

(5.10)

\int_{0}^{1}\mdmathbb{1}_{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|>\epsilon^{\prime}}\frac{G(\nu)-G(\tfrac{x}{N})}{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|^{\gamma+1}}d\nu=G^{\prime}\big(\tfrac{x}{N}\big)\int_{0}^{1}\mdmathbb{1}_{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|>\epsilon^{\prime}}\frac{\nu-\tfrac{x}{N}}{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|^{\gamma+1}}d\nu\;+\;R_{N,x,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},G},

where the remainder term can be bounded as follows (since
\gamma<2
)

|R_{N,x,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},G}|\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\mdmathbb{1}_{|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|>\epsilon^{\prime}}|\tfrac{x}{N}-\nu|^{1-\gamma}d\nu\lesssim 1.

Moreover, by changing variables and taking
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq 1/N\leq\epsilon
, we can write the integral on the right-hand side of (
5.10
) as

(5.11)

\begin{split}\int_{-\tfrac{x}{N}}^{1-\tfrac{x}{N}}&\mdmathbb{1}_{\{|z|>\epsilon^{\prime}\}}\,z|z|^{-\gamma-1}dz=\int_{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{1-\tfrac{x}{N}}\,zz^{-\gamma-1}dz+\int_{-\tfrac{x}{N}}^{-\epsilon^{\prime}}\,z(-z)^{-\gamma-1}dz\\
&=\int_{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{1-\tfrac{x}{N}}z^{-\gamma}dz-\int_{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{\tfrac{x}{N}}z^{-\gamma}dz=\frac{(1-\tfrac{x}{N})^{-\gamma+1}-(\tfrac{x}{N})^{-\gamma+1}}{-\gamma+1},\end{split}

assuming
\gamma\neq 1
. Therefore, since
G^{\prime}
is uniformly bounded, if
\gamma\in(1,2)
we have the following bound:

(5.12)

N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}|(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}\Big[(\tfrac{x}{N})^{1-\gamma}+1\Big]\lesssim\epsilon^{2-\gamma}

which goes to
0
if we send
\epsilon
to
0
.
Otherwise, if
\gamma\in(0,1)
we have

(5.13)

N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}|(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}1=\epsilon

which also goes to
0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. It remains to consider the case
\gamma=1
, in which the integral on the right-hand side of (
5.10
) is

\displaystyle\int_{-\tfrac{x}{N}}^{1-\tfrac{x}{N}}

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{1}_{\{|z|>\epsilon^{\prime}\}}\,z|z|^{-\gamma-1}dz=\int_{\tfrac{x}{N}}^{1-\tfrac{x}{N}}z^{-1}dz=\log\Big(\frac{N-x}{x}\Big),

which leads to

\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}|(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{1\leq x\leq\epsilon N}[\log(N-x)-\log(x)+1]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ N^{-1}\Big(\int_{N-\epsilon N}^{N-2}\log(v)dv-\int_{2}^{\epsilon N}\log(v)dv\Big)+\epsilon=\epsilon[1-\log(\epsilon)]-(1-\epsilon)\log(1-\epsilon),

which goes to zero as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. This concludes the proof of the fact that the first term of (
5.2
) vanishes as
N\rightarrow\infty
and then
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Let us now focus on the second term of (
5.2
), namely the one involving the sites
\epsilon N<x<(N-1)/2
. We can bound this term by

(5.14)

\begin{split}N^{-1}\sum_{\epsilon N<x<(N-1)/2}&|N(\mathcal{L}_{N}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-(\mdmathbb{L}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})|\\

since

(5.18)

\begin{split}&N^{-1}\sum_{\epsilon N<x<(N-1)/2}|(\mdmathbb{L}_{\epsilon}G)(\tfrac{x}{N})-\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon^{\prime}\rightarrow 0\\

, we get

y=y^{\prime}+\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}+j^{\prime},\quad\tfrac{\ell_{i-1}}{2}+j=\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}-j^{\prime}.

Since
1\leq y\leq\ell_{i-1}
,
1\leq y^{\prime}\leq\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}
,
1\leq j\leq\ell_{i-1}/2
and
1\leq j^{\prime}\leq\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}/2
we have that

(5.73)

\begin{split}&1\leq y\leq\ell_{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad 2+\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}\leq y\leq 2\ell_{i^{\prime}-1},\\
&1+\tfrac{\ell_{i-1}}{2}\leq\tfrac{\ell_{i-1}}{2}+j\leq\ell_{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\tfrac{\ell_{i-1}}{2}+j\leq\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}-1.\end{split}

If
i\leq i^{\prime}-1
then
\ell_{i-1}\leq\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}<2+\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}
and there is a contradiction with the first line of (
5.73
). If
i^{\prime}\leq i
then
\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}\leq{\ell_{i-1}}
, hence
\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}-1<1+\tfrac{\ell_{i-1}}{2}
, which is in contradiction with the second line of (
5.73
). Hence this case is not possible and we are done.

—

k=2
and
k^{\prime}=1
: by symmetry this case is equivalent to the previous one.

—

k^{\prime}=2
and
k=2
: then
z_{1,j}^{i}(y)=z_{1,j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})
and
z_{2,j}^{i}(y)=z_{2,j^{\prime}}^{i^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})
imply that

\tfrac{\ell_{i-1}}{2}+j+y=\tfrac{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}{2}+j^{\prime}+y^{\prime},\quad{\ell_{i-1}}+y={\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}+y^{\prime}.

The second equality and the fact that
1\leq y\leq\ell_{i-1}
, resp.
1\leq y^{\prime}\leq\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}
implies that

(5.74)

1+\ell_{i-1}\leq y+\ell_{i-1}\leq 2\ell_{i-1}\quad\text{and}\quad 1+\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}\leq y+\ell_{i-1}\leq 2\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}.

If
i\leq i^{\prime}-1
then
2\ell_{i-1}\leq{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}<{\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}}+1
which is in contradiction with (
5.74
). Similarly if
i^{\prime}\leq i-1
then
2\ell_{i^{\prime}-1}\leq{\ell_{i-1}}<{\ell_{i-1}}+1
is in contradiction with (
5.74
). Hence
i=i^{\prime}
and consequently we deduce that
y=y^{\prime}
and
j=j^{\prime}
.

This concludes the proof of the lemma.

∎

5.4.
Auxiliary result

The next proposition is used in order to define a notion of weak solution in the case
\theta=\gamma-1
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
. The strategy of the proof is similar to the one used in the previous lemmas, so we will just sketch its proof.

Lemma 5.9
.

Fix
\theta=\gamma-1
,
\gamma\in(1,2)
. For any
T>0
, for any
G\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
it holds

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\ell_{0}}(0))ds\Big|\Bigg]=0,

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{+}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overleftarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\ell_{0}}(N))ds\Big|\Bigg]=0,

where
\ell_{0}=\epsilon N^{\gamma-1}
.

Proof.

We prove only the part involving
r^{-}_{N}
, since the other one can be proved analogously. By the triangular inequality we can bound the expectation in the statement by the sum of

(5.75)

\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\ell_{0}}(0))ds\Big|\Bigg]

and

(5.76)

\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x>\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\ell_{0}}(0))ds\Big|\Bigg].

The last display can be bounded from above by a constant times

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x>\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}|G(\tfrac{x}{N})||r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})|\leq||G||_{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x>\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}|r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})|.

Since
r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})\lesssim x^{-\gamma}
the right-hand side of the last display can be bounded from above, using Riemann integral approximation, by a constant times

(\ell_{0})^{-\gamma+1}=\epsilon^{-\gamma+1}(N^{\gamma-1})^{-\gamma+1}=\epsilon^{-\gamma+1}N^{-(\gamma-1)^{2}},

and it vanishes in the limit
N\to\infty,\epsilon\to 0
. This means that in order to prove the statement of the lemma we only need to show that the limit (
5.75
) is equal to zero.

We proceed as we did in the proof of Lemma
5.4
. Let
\rho\in(0,1)
be a constant. By Jensen’s and entropy inequalities plus Feynman-Kac’s formula, we are reduced to show that

(5.77)

\frac{C_{0}}{B}+\sup_{f}\Bigg\{\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta(x)-\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\ell_{0}}(0))f(\eta)d\nu_{\rho}^{N}+\dfrac{N^{\gamma-1}}{B}\langle L_{N}\sqrt{f},\sqrt{f}\rangle_{\nu^{N}_{\rho}}\Bigg\}

vanishes in the limit
N\to\infty
,
\epsilon\to 0
and then
B\to\infty
. As usual
C_{0}
is a constant independent from
N
and the supremum is carried over all the densities with respect to the Bernoulli product measure
\nu^{N}_{\rho}
. From (
3.33
) and recalling that
\theta=\gamma-1
, the term on the right-hand side of last display becomes bounded from above by

(5.78)

-\dfrac{N}{4NB}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu^{N}_{\rho})+\frac{C\kappa}{B}

where
C
is a constant independent of
N
,
B
(and
\epsilon
). The first term inside the supremum of (
5.77
) can be written as

\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})\Big[\sum_{y=1}^{x}\sum_{z=y}^{x-1}(\eta(z+1)-\eta(z))+\sum_{y=x}^{\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x}^{y-1}(\eta(z)-\eta(z+1))\Big]f(\eta)d\nu_{\rho}^{N}.

Now, since the profile in the product measure
\nu_{\rho}^{N}
is a constant we can rewrite the last term, by a change of variable as

(5.79)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2\ell_{0}}\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})&\Big[\sum_{y=1}^{x}\sum_{z=y}^{x-1}(\eta(z+1)-\eta(z))(f(\eta)-f(\sigma^{z,z+1}\eta))\\
&+\sum_{y=x}^{\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x}^{y-1}(\eta(z)-\eta(z+1))(f(\eta)-f(\sigma^{z,z+1}\eta))\Big]d\nu_{\rho}^{N}.\end{split}

Hence, using Young’s inequality on both the terms in the last display with the same constant
A>0
we can bound from above the whole term by

(5.80)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{4\ell_{0}}\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}\left|G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})\right|&\Big[A\sum_{y=1}^{x}\sum_{z=y}^{x-1}(\eta(z+1)-\eta(z))^{2}(\sqrt{f}(\eta)+\sqrt{f}(\sigma^{z,z+1}\eta))^{2}\\
&+A\sum_{y=x}^{\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x}^{y-1}(\eta(z)-\eta(z+1))^{2}(\sqrt{f}(\eta)+\sqrt{f}(\sigma^{z,z+1}\eta))^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{A}\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=1}^{\ell_{0}}(\sqrt{f}(\eta)-\sqrt{f}(\sigma^{z,z+1}\eta))^{2}\Big]d\nu_{\rho}^{N}.\end{split}

Since
f
is a density and
|\eta(z)|\leq 1
for any
z
, we can bound from above the whole last term by (recall the definition (
3.29
))

(5.81)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{4\ell_{0}}&\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}\left|G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})\right|\left[A\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=1}^{\ell_{0}}2+\frac{1}{A}\sum_{y=1}^{\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=1}^{\ell_{0}}I_{z,z+1}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho}^{N})\right]\\
&\leq\frac{C^{\prime}A\ell_{0}}{2}+\frac{1}{4A}D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu^{N}_{\rho}),\end{split}

where
C^{\prime}
is a constant independent of
N,\epsilon,B
(this because
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Lambda_{N}\\
x\leq\ell_{0}\end{subarray}}|G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})|\lesssim 1
and
\sum_{z=1}^{\ell_{0}}I_{z,z+1}(\sqrt{f},\nu_{\rho}^{N})\leq D_{N}(\sqrt{f},\nu^{N}_{\rho})
).

Then, taking
A=BN^{1-\gamma}
the second term on the right-hand side of (
5.81
) cancels with the first of (
5.78
). Summarising we bounded (
5.77
) and hence the expectation in (
5.75
) by

(5.82)

\frac{C\kappa}{B}+C^{\prime}\frac{BN^{1-\gamma}\ell_{0}}{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{B}+B\epsilon.

So, sending
\epsilon
to
0
and then
B
to infinity, the proof is concluded.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

Observe that if we apply Lemma
5.9
and then Lemma
5.5
it is easy to show that

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0))ds\Big|\Bigg]=0.

Indeed the expectation above can be bounded from above by

(5.83)

\begin{split}&\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\ell_{0}}(0))ds\Big|\Bigg]\\
&+\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\overrightarrow{\eta}^{\ell_{0}}_{s}(0)-\overrightarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(0))ds\Big|\Bigg].\end{split}

The first expectation above vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity and
\epsilon
goes to
0
, thanks to Lemma
5.9
. Now, call
C_{N}:=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{-}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})
, which is of order
1
since the sum is convergent. Then, the second expectation above vanishes, as
N
goes to infinity and
\epsilon
goes to
0
, thanks to Lemma
5.5
.
Moreover, note that by the symmetry property of this model, the results that we used works also when we study the right boundary (see Remark
5.7
). So, it is possible to show in the same way as above that

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mdmathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G(\tfrac{x}{N})r^{+}_{N}(\tfrac{x}{N})(\eta^{N}_{s}(x)-\overleftarrow{\eta}_{s}^{\epsilon N}(N))ds\Big|\Bigg]=0.
