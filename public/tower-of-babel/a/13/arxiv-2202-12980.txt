Simulability of high-dimensional quantum measurements
Marie Ioannou, Pavel Sekatski, Sébastien Designolle, Benjamin D.M. Jones, Roope Uola, Nicolas Brunner

Abstract
We investigate the compression of quantum information with respect to a given set ℳ \mathcal{M} of high-dimensional measurements. This leads to a notion of simulability, where we demand that the statistics obtained from ℳ \mathcal{M} and an arbitrary quantum state ρ \rho are recovered exactly by first compressing ρ \rho into a lower dimensional space, followed by some quantum measurements. A full quantum compression is possible, i.e., leaving only classical information, if and only if the set ℳ \mathcal{M} is jointly measurable. Our notion of simulability can thus be seen as a quantification of measurement incompatibility in terms of dimension. After defining these concepts, we provide an illustrative examples involving mutually unbiased basis, and develop a method based on semi-definite programming for constructing simulation models. In turn we analytically construct optimal simulation models for all projective measurements subjected to white noise or losses. Finally, we discuss how our approach connects with other concepts introduced in the context of quantum channels and quantum correlations.



Introduction.—

Quantum measurements play a fundamental role in quantum theory and its applications, notably in quantum information processing and metrology. Indeed measurements represent the bridge between a quantum system and an external observer, hence essentially any quantum experiment relies on a quantum measurement process. More recently, the role of quantum measurements as a resource was clarified in the context of quantum information processing tasks, see
JMinvitation
;
JMreview
for a review. At the formal level, the concept of joint measurability

provides a framework for characterisation and quantification of the incompatibility of quantum measurements, which can be connected to their usefulness in, e.g., state-discrimination problems
carmeli19a
;
skrzypczyk19
;
oszmaniec19
;
uola19b
;
uola19c
;
Ducuara2020
and quantum steering

;
uola14
;
uola15
;
kiukas17
.

A natural question is whether quantum measurement incompatibility can also be quantified in terms of dimension. Intuitively, a set of quantum measurement defined on a high-dimensional Hilbert space may feature a stronger form of incompatibility than what is possible for lower dimensions.

In this work we address this question and propose a notion of dimensionality for measurement incompatibility. This notion can be understood, and naturally motivated, in a scenario involving the compression of quantum information. Loosely speaking, we ask whether the statistics of a set of
d
-dimensional positive operator-valued measures (POVMs)
\mathcal{M}
(considering any possible quantum state
\rho
) can be exactly recovered from first projecting
\rho
onto a
n
-dimensional space (with
1\leqslant n<d
) and then performing POVMs in this lower-dimensional space. If such a compression is possible, we say the set
\mathcal{M}
is
n
-simulable. Note that the case
n=1
exactly corresponds to the notion of joint measurability; indeed, in this case, the full quantum information can be compressed to classical information, see also
heinosaari2017
;
guerini2019distributed
. However, as we will see below, there exist sets of POVMs
\mathcal{M}
that are incompatible, but yet simulable with
n
-dimensional measurements with
1<n<d
. The notion of
n
-simulability can thus be seen as an extension of the concept of joint measurability, providing a quantification of the incompatibility of quantum measurements in terms of dimension.

After introducing these ideas more formally, we provide illustrative examples based on sets of mutually unbiased measurements. Then, we present a method based on semi-definite programming (SDP) to show that a set of measurements is
n
-simulable. In turn we consider the continuous sets of all projective measurements subjected to noise or losses, and construct optimal
n
-simulation models. Finally, we establish a link to partially entanglement-breaking channels
chruscinski2006partially
, and discuss connections to other concepts as well as open questions.

Scenario and definition.—
Consider the following task: a sender (Alice) is located on the moon and wants to transmit an (arbitrary)
d
-dimensional quantum state
\rho

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{a|x}\}_{a,x}

\{M_{a|x}\}_{a}

M_{a|x}\geqslant 0

\sum_{a}M_{a|x}=1

\forall a,x

p(a|x,\rho)=\Tr(M_{a|x}\rho)

So far, we assumed that the channel between Alice and Bob is ideal, i.e., a

We now ask if the target data could be reproduced by using a lower-dimensional quantum channel. That is, we look for a quantum instrument
\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
, where each
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}:B(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to B(\mathbb{C}^{n})

\sum_{\lambda}\Tr[N_{a|x,\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(\rho)]=\Tr(M_{a|x}\rho)\quad\forall\rho\,,

(1)

or equivalently if

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{*}

is

, the instrument corresponds to a POVM and the measurements afterwards form classical post-processings of the classical output. This coincides with the concept of joint measurability:
M_{a|x}=\sum_{\lambda}G_{\lambda}p(a|x,\lambda)

represents the joint (or parent) POVM

;

SDP method.—

In order to explore more general schemes, involving arbitrary bases for the compression, we now present a numerical approach based on semi-definite programming (SDP). For the example of MUBs, this shows that better schemes are indeed possible.

Consider a set of measurements
M_{a|x}
, to which we add noise. For clarity we focus here on the case of white noise, but the technique applies in general. Formally, we consider sets of POVMs of the form:

M_{a|x}^{\eta}=\eta M_{a|x}+(1-\eta)\Tr[M_{a|x}]\frac{\mathds{1}_{d}}{d}\,.

Our goal now is derive a lower bound on
\eta
for which the set becomes
n
-simulable. In particular, if the lower bound is found to be trivial, i.e.,
\eta\geqslant 1
, we conclude that the original measurements
M_{a|x}
are
n
-simulable.

We first choose a compression map, consisting of a set of
|\mu|
instruments
\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda|\mu}\}
. We can now find the maximal value of
\eta
(i.e., minimise the noise) while ensuring
n
-simulability, optimising over Bob’s final measurements, via the following SDP:

\displaystyle\max_{\eta,\{\tilde{N}_{a|x,\lambda}\},\alpha_{\lambda}}\;\;

\displaystyle\eta

(7)

\displaystyle s.t.\;

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda=0}^{{d\choose n}-1}\sum_{\mu=0}^{|\mu|-1}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda|\mu}\tilde{N}_{a|x,\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda|\mu}^{\dagger}=M_{a|x}^{\eta}\qquad\forall a,x

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\tilde{N}_{a|x,\lambda,\mu}=\alpha_{\lambda,\mu}\mathds{1}_{n}\;\;\;\forall x,\lambda,\mu

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{a|x,\lambda,\mu}\geqslant 0,\;{\tilde{N}_{a|x,\lambda,\mu}}^{\dagger}=\tilde{N}_{a|x,\lambda,\mu}\;\;\;\forall a,x,\lambda,\mu

To illustrate the relevance of this method, let us consider again the case of a pair of MUBs in dimension
d=3
, i.e., setting
M_{a|x}=\outerproduct{\varphi_a^x}{\varphi_a^x}
, and compressing to

) and perform a projection onto the

-dimensional subspaces, denoted by projectors

. Hence we set

The case of all projective measurements.—

So far we analysed sets
\mathcal{M}
with finitely many measurements. Now we turn our attention to continuous sets of measurements. Precisely, we consider assemblages
\mathcal{M}_{PVM}^{\eta}
made of all rank-1 projective measurements subjected to white noise. Note that this automatically extends to all projective measurements which can be obtained from rank-1 projective measurements by post-processing.
Assuming an isotropic noise,

An equivalent definition.—

The notion of joint measurability has a direct connection to entanglement-breaking properties of quantum channels, cf.
kiukas2017continuous
and
Kogias15
;
Moroder16
. As a final point we now discuss how this connection naturally extends to
n
-simulability.

A quantum channel
\Lambda
is said to be
n
-partially entanglement breaking
(
n
-PEB) if, for all states
\rho
, the Schmidt number of the state
(\Lambda\otimes\mathds{1})[\rho]

is less than or equal to
n

chruscinski2006partially
. In the finite-dimensional setting,
chruscinski2006partially
this is equivalent to the existence of Kraus operators
\{K_{\lambda}\}
of
\Lambda
each with
\text{rank}(K_{\lambda})\leqslant n
. This notion provides an alternative way to define

-simulability:

Claim 5
.

A measurement assemblage
M_{a|x}
with finitely many inputs is
n
-simulable if and only if there exists a quantum channel
\Lambda
that is
n
-PEB and a measurement assemblage
N_{a|x}
such that

M_{a|x}=\Lambda^{*}\big(N_{a|x}\big).

(17)

For the if direction one uses the singular value decomposition of the rank-

Kraus operators

and

to define the instrument

with
n
-dimensional output and the subsequent measurement

. For the only if direction one can simply view any compression instrument
\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\}
as a channel

that outputs a quantum system of dimension

a related notion of “
n
-compressibility” of a set of quantum measurements has been proposed. Similarly to our definition
a set of measurements is said to be
n
-compressible if
M_{a|x}=\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{*}(N_{a|x,\lambda})
1
1

1

Notably the authors of
bluhm2018quantum
also consider the possibility to bound the amount of classical communication via the number of different instrument outcomes
\lambda
(the dimension of the classical register output by the instrument).
, but in sharp contrast the compressed measurement has to take the form

N_{a|x,\lambda}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{*}_{|\lambda}(M_{a|x})
.
Here,
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{|\lambda}
is a set of CPTP maps decompressing the system back into dimension
d
, and the same measurement
M_{a|x}
has to be used after the decompression. Clearly, a set of
n
-compressible measurements is n-simulable by construction, but not the other way around. In particular, it is known that single measurements can have a larger compression dimension than one
bluhm2018quantum
.

Other works have investigated measurement (in)compatibility in subspaces
LN21
;
UKD+21
, while Ref.
Carmeli16
defined a concept of
n
-compatibility considering a scenario where a set of measurements is performed on
n
copies of a state. As far as we can say, these concepts are unrelated to
n
-simulability.

Finally, it is also worth pointing the approach of “projective simulability” discussed in Refs
OGW17+
;
GBT+17
(see also
Hirsch17
), where one asks whether a set of POVMs can be simulated from projective measurements (of the same dimension) only.

Conclusion.—

We have introduced the concept of
n
-simulability of a set of measurements, motivated by a scenario of compression of quantum information. When full compression is possible (i.e.
n
=1), our notion corresponds to joint measurability. Hence our approach can be used as a quantification of measurement incompatibility in terms of dimension. We discussed a number of examples, providing analytical constructions as well as numerical methods.

More generally, the concept of
n
-simulability turns out to be connected to several other relevant notions of quantum information theory. First, as we showed above,
n
-simulability relates to partially entanglement-breaking channels. Second, there is a direct connection between
n
-simulability and the notion of genuine high-dimensional steering
designolle2021genuine
, which will be presented in detail in a companion article
Paper2
. These links generalise the well-known connection between joint measurability, steering and quantum channels
uola14
;
quintino14
;
uola15
. They also open new questions, for example, whether the dimensionality of a quantum channel could be tested in a partially device-independent manner.

Beyond quantum compression, our approach also has implications for device-independent quantum information processing. For example, it is clear that a set of measurements that is
n
-simulable is of limited use for randomness certification, as it can lead to (at most)
2\log(n)
bits of randomness in a black-box setting.

Finally, an intriguing question is whether our approach could be generalised to the case of continuous variable measurements. It turns out that, in the infinite-dimensional case, the Kraus operators’ ranks and entanglement breaking properties of a channel are not anymore tied together
holevo2005separability
;
shirokov2011schmidt
, opening different possibilities to extend our concept.

Appendix A: Proof of claim 2

Here we prove that for a measurement assemblage satisfying
V^{\dagger}M_{a|U}V=M_{a|UV}
, we can take our Kraus operators to be of the form
K_{V}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\Pi_{n}V
, where
\Pi_{n}
projects onto the first
n
basis states, and the normalisation condition reads
\int dVK_{V}^{\dagger}K_{V}=\mathbbm{1}
with
dV
the Haar measure. We prove this explicitly for measurements
M_{a|U}
of the above form for clarity of presentation, but the argument holds for any invariant assemblage
\mathcal{M}
.

Suppose we have

M_{a|U}=\sum_{\lambda}K_{\lambda}^{\dagger}~N_{a|U,\lambda}\quad K_{\lambda},

(18)

for some Kraus operators
K_{\lambda}
of rank at most
n
and measurements
N_{a|U,\lambda}
. We take
\lambda
as belonging to a countable outcome space, but our proof would extend straightforwardly to the case of continuous instruments (we comment on some subtleties below).

Each Kraus operator admits a singular value decomposition
K_{\lambda}=U_{\lambda}D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}
, but by redefining the measurements as
N_{a|U,\lambda}\mapsto U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}N_{a|U,\lambda}U_{\lambda}
we can without loss of generality take each Kraus operator to be of the form
K_{\lambda}=D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}
. We can also write this as
K_{\lambda}=\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}\Pi_{n}V_{\lambda}
, where
\Pi_{n}
is a
n\times d
matrix projecting onto the first
n
basis states, and
\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}=\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
is an
n\times n
matrix containing the singular values along the diagonal.

Now for each permutation
\pi
on
n
elements let
U_{\pi}=\sum_{i}\outerproduct{\pi(i)}{i}
be the
n\times n
unitary matrix permuting the computational basis states. We can thus further write each Kraus operator as follows

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}U_{\pi}^{\dagger}U_{\pi}\Pi_{n}V_{\lambda}

(19)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}U_{\pi}^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}\overline{U}_{\pi}V_{\lambda},

(20)

where
\overline{U}_{\pi}=U_{\pi}\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{d-n}
is the
d\times d
unitary matrix with
U_{\pi}
acting on the first
n
basis vectors and the identity on the rest.

In addition, by exploiting the property
V^{\dagger}M_{a|U}V=M_{a|UV}
for all
d\times d
unitaries
V
we can write

M_{a|U}=\int dV\sum_{\lambda}V^{\dagger}K_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\quad N_{a|UV^{\dagger},\lambda}\quad K_{\lambda}V,

(21)

where
dV=d\mu(V)
is the Haar measure. Through the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, we can exchange the sum and integral (we make the natural assumption that
\lambda
belongs to a
\sigma
-finite measure space).
Then by inserting Eq. (
20
) and changing variables
\overline{U}_{\pi}V_{\lambda}V\mapsto V
(using Haar invariance), we obtain the following expression for
M_{a|U}
:

\sum_{\lambda}\int dV\quad V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}^{\dagger}U_{\pi}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\quad N_{a|UV^{\dagger}\overline{U}_{\pi}V_{\lambda},\lambda}\quad\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}U_{\pi}^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V.

(22)

We can now average over all permutations
\pi
to obtain

\displaystyle M_{a|U}=\int dV~V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\lambda,\pi}U_{\pi}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle N_{a|UV^{\dagger}\overline{U}_{\pi}V_{\lambda},\lambda}\quad\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}U_{\pi}^{\dagger}\bigg]\Pi_{n}V.

(23)

Finally, we can take
K_{V}:=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\Pi_{n}V
as Kraus operators satisfying

\int dVK_{V}^{\dagger}K_{V}=\frac{d}{n}\int dV~V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V=\mathbbm{1},

(24)

and define

N_{a|U,V}:=\frac{n}{d\times n!}\sum_{\lambda,\pi}U_{\pi}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\quad N_{a|UV^{\dagger}\overline{U}_{\pi}V_{\lambda},\lambda}\quad\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}U_{\pi}^{\dagger}.

(25)

This defines a valid POVM by virtue of

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\lambda,\pi}U_{\pi}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}U_{\pi}^{\dagger}=\frac{d}{n}\mathbbm{1},

(26)

where the identity is on
\mathbbm{C}^{n}
, and we have used the fact that
\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}
is diagonal and
\text{Tr}(\sum_{\lambda}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\widetilde{D}_{\lambda})=d
.

We have shown that for arbitrary Kraus operators
K_{\lambda}
that demonstrate the
n
-simulability of
M_{a|U}
, there always exist measurements
N_{a|U,V}
such that
K_{V}
as defined above also demonstrates the
n
-simulability. Crucially, each
K_{V}
has rank
n
, the same as the original Kraus operators
K_{\lambda}
. This completes the proof. ∎

Appendix B: Proof of claim 3

In this section we show that the measurement
N_{a|V}
defined in Eq. (
11
) in the main text is optimal for the compression of all noisy projective measurements. The claim 3 then follows straightforwardly.

By claim 2 we know that without loss of generality one can consider compression by means of the continuous instrument
\mathcal{E}_{V}
with Kraus operator density

K_{V}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\Pi_{n}V.

(27)

Furthermore, it also tells us that it is enough to study
N_{a|V}
achieving the maximal
\eta
for which the POVM
M_{a}^{\eta}
can be compressed, as it would imply the compression of all measurements
M_{a|U}^{\eta}
by symmetry. Hence, we now can focus on finding
N_{a|V}
such that

M^{\prime}_{a}=\int\differential V\mathcal{E}_{V}^{*}(N_{a|V})

(28)

equals
M_{a}^{\eta}
for the largest value

possible, i.e. for the lowest amount of white noise.
Precisely, we now show that the measurement

N_{a|V}=\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\
\,\,\textrm{on}\,\mathds{C}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\textrm{argmax}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\tilde{N}_{a}V\ket{a},

(29)

defined in the main text is optimal for the purpose. An attentive reader notes that the right hand side of this equation is not necessary always well defined. For some choices of
V
there might be several POVMs
\tilde{N}_{a}
that attain the maximum. In these case, we tell the argmax to select such a

at random, defining a valid average POVM. This solves possible issues with argmax and does not affect the following arguments.

The main challenge in proving the optimality of

in Eq. (

M_{a}^{\prime}=\eta\outerproduct{a}{a}+(1-\eta)\frac{\mathds{1}}{d}

V_{\bm{\theta}}=Ve^{\mathrm{i}\,\text{Diag}(\bm{\theta})}

(30)

with
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})
,

(31)

permuting the elements of the computational basis. Both
V_{\bm{\theta}}
and

are also unitary operators. Furthermore, they satisfy the following properties. For
V_{\bm{\theta}}

\begin{split}N_{a|V_{\bm{\theta}}}&=\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\

&=N_{a|V}\end{split}

since
V_{\bm{\theta}}\ket{a}=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{a}}V\ket{a}

one finds

\begin{split}N_{a|V_{\pi}}&=\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\

\,\,\textrm{on}\,\mathds{C}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\textrm{argmax}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{\pi(a)}V^{\dagger}\tilde{N}_{a}V\ket{\pi(a)}\\

.

\differential\bm{\theta}

Appendix C: Upper bound on n-simulability threshold of all noisy PVMs

In this section we derive a upper bound on the white noise
n
-simulability threshold of all projective measurements
\eta_{d\mapsto n}
. The starting point is to using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to upper bound
x
in Eq. (13) of the main text. Precisely, for two vectors of operators
\bm{A}=(\dots,A_{i},\dots)
and
\bm{B}
one may defines an inner product
(\bm{A},\bm{B})=\sum_{i}\tr A_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}
. Then Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then implies
(\bm{A},\bm{B})\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{i}\tr A_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}\sum_{j}\tr B_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}}
. In our case,

\begin{split}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{a}V^{\dagger}N_{a}V\ket{a}&=\sum_{a=1}^{d}\tr\left(\Pi_{n}V\outerproduct{a}{a}V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}~N_{a}\right)\\
&\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{a=1}^{d}\tr(\Pi_n V \ketbra{a}{a} V^\dag\Pi_n)^{2}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\tr N_{a}^{2}}\\
&\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{a=1}^{d}|\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V\ket{a}|^{2}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\tr N_{a}}\\
&=\sqrt{\sum_{a=1}^{d}|\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V\ket{a}|^{2}\,n},\end{split}

where we used
N_{a}^{2}\leqslant N_{a}
, enforced by
0\leqslant N_{a}\leqslant\mathds{1}
, and
\sum_{a}N_{a}=\mathds{1}_{n}\oplus 0_{d-n}
.
Plugging in the definition of
x
one obtains

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int\differential V\!\!\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\
\,\,\textrm{on}\,\mathds{C}^{n}\end{subarray}}{\textrm{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{a}V^{\dagger}N_{a}V\ket{a}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}\int\differential V\sqrt{\sum_{a=1}^{d}|\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V\ket{a}|^{2}}

(47)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\int\differential V\sum_{a=1}^{d}|\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V\ket{a}|^{2}},

(48)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\int\differential V|\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\Pi_{n}V\ket{a}|^{2}},

(49)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}\int\differential\Psi|\bra{\Psi}\Pi_{n}\ket{\Psi}|^{2}},

(50)

where the last inequality follows by Jensen’s inequality and the concavity of the square root function. Notably both upper bounds (
47
) and (
50
) do not contain an optimisation.

To compute the last integral let us define

\displaystyle\Theta(d,n)

\displaystyle=\int\differential\Psi\absolutevalue{\bra{\Psi}\Pi_n\ket{\Psi}}^{2}

(51)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\bigg(\int\differential\Psi~\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}\otimes\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}~\Pi_{n}\otimes\Pi_{n}\bigg).

(52)

Now recall that
\int\differential\Psi~\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}\otimes\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}
denotes the normalised projector onto the symmetric subspace
\frac{2}{d(d+1)}\Pi_{\text{sym}}=\frac{(\mathbbm{1}+S)}{d(d+1)}
, with
S
the swap operator
harrow2013church
. Hence we can write

\displaystyle\Theta(d,n)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\bigg(\frac{(\mathbbm{1}+S)}{d(d+1)}~\Pi_{n}\otimes\Pi_{n}\bigg)

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\bigg[\text{Tr}(\Pi_{n})^{2}+\text{Tr}(\Pi_{n}^{2})\bigg]

(54)

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n+1)}{d(d+1)},

(55)

where in the second line we used the fact that
\text{Tr}(A\otimes BS)=\text{Tr}(AB)
.

By virtue of Eq. (
50
) we thus find
x\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{d+1}}
, and

\eta_{d\mapsto n}\leqslant\frac{d\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{d+1}}-1}{d-1}.

(56)

n=d-1
, a lower bound

We here focus on the
d-1
-simulability of all noisy projective measurement. As shown in the main text, finding the white noise threshold
\eta
boils down to solving the integral of Eq. (13). In particular one has to solve the maximisation

X(V)=\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\
\,\,\textrm{on}\,\mathds{C}^{d-1}\end{subarray}}{\textrm{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{a}V^{\dagger}\tilde{N}_{a}V\ket{a}.

(57)

Here we change the notation slightly by considering the subspace
\mathds{C}^{d-1}=\text{span}\{\ket{2},\dots{\ket{d}}\}
, so that the POVM elements sum up to
\sum_{a=1}^{d}\tilde{N}=\Pi_{d-1}
identity on this subspace and fulfill
\tilde{N}_{a}\ket{1}=0
.

It is now convenient to use the composite parametrization of the unitary group introduced in
spengler2012composite

V^{\dagger}=\left[\prod_{m=1}^{d-1}\left(\prod_{k=m+1}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,m}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{m,k}Y_{m,k}}\right)\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{\ell,\ell}P_{\ell}}\right]

(58)

with the hermitian opertators

Y_{m,k}=\mathrm{i}(\outerproduct{k}{m}-\outerproduct{m}{k})\qquad P_{\ell}=\outerproduct{\ell}{\ell},

(59)

d^{2}
real parameters
\lambda_{m,k}\in[0,2\pi]
for
m\geqslant k
and
\lambda_{m,k}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
for
m<k
, integer indices
m,k=1,\dots,d
, and all terms appearing in the products from left to right for increasing index. In
spengler2012composite
the expression of the Haar measure for the above parametrization is also derived, it reads

\differential V=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\prod_{m-1}^{d-1}\prod_{k=m+1}^{d}\sin(\lambda_{m,k})\cos^{2(k-m)-1}(\lambda_{m,k})\prod_{\ell,j}\differential\lambda_{\ell,j}

(60)

with the normalization constant
N_{d}
that we are not going to need.

This parametrization of Eq. (
58
) is convenient because it allows us to decompose

V^{\dagger}=\left(\prod_{k=2}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,1}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,k}Y_{1,k}}\right)\overline{W}^{(d-1)}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,1}P_{1}}

(61)

with

\overline{W}^{(d-1)}=\left[\prod_{m=2}^{d-1}\left(\prod_{k=m+1}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,m}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{m,k}Y_{m,k}}\right)\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{\ell=2}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{\ell,\ell}P_{\ell}}\right]

which acts trivially on
\ket{1}
.

Denoting
U=\left(\prod_{k=2}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,1}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,k}Y_{1,k}}\right)
we can now rewrite the maximization Eq (
57
) with the
V
expressed in terms of the parameters
\lambda_{m,k}
. Noting that
e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,1}P_{1}}\tilde{N}_{a}e^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,1}P_{1}}=\tilde{N}_{a}
we obtain

\begin{split}X(V)&=\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\
\,\,\textrm{on}\,\mathds{C}^{d-1}\end{subarray}}{\textrm{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{a}U\overline{W}^{(d-1)}\tilde{N}_{a}\overline{W}^{(d-1){\dagger}}U^{\dagger}\ket{a}\\
&=\underset{\tilde{N}_{a}:\begin{subarray}{c}\,\text{POVM}\\
\,\,\textrm{on}\,\mathds{C}^{d-1}\end{subarray}}{\textrm{max}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}\bra{a}U\tilde{N}_{a}U^{\dagger}\ket{a}\end{split}

(62)

because the transformation
\tilde{N}_{a}\to\overline{W}^{(d-1){\dagger}}\tilde{N}_{a}\overline{W}^{(d-1)}
does not affect the optimization.

Lower bound–

Next, to obtain a lower bound on
x
we relax the maximisation by fixing

\tilde{N}_{a}=\begin{cases}0&a=1\\
\mathinner{|{a}\rangle}\!\!\mathinner{\langle{a}|}&2\leqslant a\leqslant d\end{cases}

(63)

in the last equation. This choice implies

X(V)\geqslant X^{\prime}(V)=\sum_{a=2}^{d}|\bra{a}U\ket{a}|^{2}.

(64)

Let us now compute

|\bra{a}U\ket{a}|=\left|\bra{a}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{d}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,1}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,k}Y_{1,k}}\right)\ket{a}\right|

(65)

for
a\geqslant 2
. Using

\begin{split}\bra{a}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{a-1}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,1}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,k}Y_{1,k}}\right)&=\bra{a}\\
\left(\prod_{k=a+1}^{d-1}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k,1}P_{k}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,k}Y_{1,k}}\right)\ket{a}&=\ket{a},\end{split}

(66)

one obtains

\begin{split}|\bra{a}U\ket{a}|&=\left|\bra{a}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{a,1}P_{a}}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,a}Y_{1,a}}\ket{a}\right|\\
&=\left|\bra{a}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{1,a}Y_{1,a}}\ket{a}\right|\\
&=|\cos(\lambda_{1,a} )|,\end{split}

(67)

and

X^{\prime}(V)=\sum_{k=2}^{d}\cos^{2}(\lambda_{1,k}).

(68)

To express the lower bound

x=\frac{1}{d-1}\int\differential VX(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{d-1}\int\differential VX^{\prime}(V)

(69)

it thus remains to compute the integrals

\begin{split}I_{k}&=\int\differential V\cos^{2}(\lambda_{1,k})\\
&=\frac{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin(\lambda_{1,k})\cos^{2k-1}(\lambda_{1,k})\differential\lambda_{1,k}}{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin(\lambda_{1,k})\cos^{2k-3}(\lambda_{1,k})\differential\lambda_{1,k}}\\
&=1-\frac{1}{k}\end{split}

(70)

where we used the expression of the Haar measure in Eq. (
60
).
Taking the sum over
k
one obtains

x\geqslant\frac{\sum_{k=2}^{d}I_{k}}{d-1}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{d}I_{k}}{d-1}=\frac{d-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{k}}{d-1}.

(71)

With the claim 3 we finally gets to the lower bound reported in the main text

\eta_{d\mapsto d-1}\geqslant\frac{d^{2}-d(1+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1}{k})+1}{(d-1)^{2}}.

(72)

Appendix E: Proof of claim 5

Below we prove the equivalence of the claim 5, which proposes that the two following definitions of n-simulability are equivalent for a finite
d
:

\begin{split}&(1)\qquad M_{a|x}=\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{*}(N_{a|x,\lambda})\\
&(2)\qquad M_{a|x}=\Lambda^{*}(N^{\prime}_{a|x})\end{split}

(73)

In the former case
(1)
,
\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\}
made of CP-maps
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}:B(\mathds{C}^{d})\to B(\mathds{C}^{n})
and
N_{a|x,\lambda}
is a POVM on
B(\mathds{C}^{n})
. In the latter (2),
\Lambda
is a
n
-partially entanglement breaking channel, for which the dimension of the output quantum system is not a priori specified, and
N^{\prime}_{a|x}
is a subsequent POVM. As mentioned in the main text the
n
-PEB map
\Lambda
has Kraus operators or rank-
n
at most.

We start by showing that
(1)\implies(2)
, to do so let us introduce a classical register
C
that encodes the classical output of the instrument
\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\}
. We can then associate the instrument with a channel

\Lambda:\rho\mapsto\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}[\rho]_{Q}\otimes\mathinner{|{\lambda}\rangle}\!\!\mathinner{\langle{\lambda}|}_{C},

(74)

that outputs a quantum-classical state with a quantum system of dimension
n
and a classical register
\lambda
. By construction
\Lambda
is
n
-PEB. In turn, the measurement
N_{a|x,\lambda}
conditioned on the the classical label
\lambda
, can be viewed as a POVM
N^{\prime}_{a|x}
on the quantum-classical system
QC
. Hence, an
n
-simulable assemblage accordingly to Def. (1) is also
n
-simulable accordingly to Def. (2).

To show
(2)\implies(1)
consider a
n
-PEB channel
\Lambda
and a measurement assemblage
M_{a|x}
. The channel
\Lambda
admits a Kraus representation {
K_{\lambda}\}
with Kraus operators of rank
n
. Each Kraus operator then admits the singular value decomposition

K_{\lambda}=U_{\lambda}D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger},

(75)

where
U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}U_{\lambda}=\mathds{1}
and
V_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\mathds{1}
, and
D_{\lambda}:\mathds{C}^{n}\to\mathds{C}^{n}

with
\mathds{1}\geqslant D_{\lambda}\geqslant 0
is diagonal in the preferred basis. The Kraus operators satisfy
\mathds{1}=\sum_{\lambda}K^{\dagger}_{\lambda}K_{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}D_{\lambda}^{2}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
.

Let us now define a quantum instrument
\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}\}
as

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}[\rho]=D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\,\rho\,(D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}.

(76)

It is a valid instrument
\sum_{\lambda}(D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}(D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger})=\mathds{1}
and manifestly outputs a quantum system of dimension
n
. Furthermore, with the measurement
N^{\prime}_{a|x}
let us define

N_{a|x,\lambda}=U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}N^{\prime}_{a|x}U_{\lambda},

(77)

which also acts on the
n
-dimensional system. It is straightforward to verify that

\begin{split}\Lambda^{*}\Big(N^{\prime}_{a|x}\Big)&=\sum_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}D_{\lambda}U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}N^{\prime}_{a|x}U_{\lambda}D_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\\
&=\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}^{*}\Big(N^{\prime}_{a|x,\lambda}\Big).\end{split}

(78)

This completes the proof.
