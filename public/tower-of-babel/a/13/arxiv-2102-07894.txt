Abstract
. We study two simplicial complexes arising from a directed graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) with two chosen vertices s s and t t : the path-free complex , consisting of all subsets F ⊆ E F\subseteq E that contain no path from s s to t t , and the path-missing complex , its Alexander dual. Using discrete Morse theory, we prove that both complexes have well-behaved homotopy types – either contractible or homotopy-equivalent to spheres.

Introduction

In this paper, we introduce two simplicial complexes associated to a directed graph with specified source and sink nodes, which we call the
path-free
and
path-missing
complex of the graph.
These complexes, whose definitions were inspired in a natural way by the theory of network flows and cuts, are not constructed
a priori
to be well behaved.
Our main result will show that, nevertheless, they have a very nice topological/homotopical structure.
The two complexes add to a long history of graph-related complexes, which are often defined in terms of some form of connectivity or independence (cf.
[
Bay+25
]
,
[
For02
, Section 5]
,
[
Jon08
]
,
[
Koz08
]
,
[
EH06
]
, or the recently proved Kalai–Meshulam conjecture
[
Chu+20
]
,
[
Kim22
]
).
The path-free complex can be viewed as a “local” version of the “complex of non-connected graphs” from
[
For02
, Section 5]
, in which the connectivity of the entire graph is replaced by the existence of paths between two given vertices. Our graphs here will be directed; this adds another twist on the existing theory, which is usually concerned with undirected graphs.

The rest of this introduction is devoted to the precise definitions of the path-free and path-missing complexes, and the statements of our main results.

1.1.
General definitions

In the following, all graphs are directed multigraphs, and self-loops are allowed.
Let
G=\left(V,E,s,t\right)
be a directed graph

We recall that a
directed graph
consists of a set
V
of vertices and a set
E
of arcs. Each arc has a
source
and a
target
; if these are equal, the arc is called a
self-loop
. An arc can carry additional information beyond its source and target; thus, parallel arcs are allowed.

with vertex set
V
, arc set
E
, and two distinguished vertices
s,t\in V
.
A
walk
(of
G
) means a sequence
\left(v_{0},e_{1},v_{1},e_{2},v_{2},\ldots,e_{n},v_{n}\right)
, where
n
is a nonnegative integer and where
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in V
and
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\in E
have the property that each
e_{i}
is an arc from
v_{i-1}
to
v_{i}
.
This walk is said to have the vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
and the arcs
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}
, and it is furthermore said to be a walk from
v_{0}
to
v_{n}
.
A
path
means a walk that does not visit any vertex more than once.
If
u,v\in V
, then a
u-v
-walk
means a walk from
u
to
v
.
A
u-v
-path
means a
u-v
-walk that is a path.

We shall use the language of (abstract) simplicial complexes.
For an introduction to this language, see

We recall the most fundamental notions of this language:
A
simplicial complex
means a pair
\left(W,\Delta\right)
consisting of a finite set
W

\Delta
of the powerset
2^{W}
of
W
such that the following axiom holds:

2

In terms of posets, this axiom says that
\Delta
is an order ideal (i.e., a down-set) of the Boolean lattice
2^{W}
(ordered by inclusion).

If
A\in\Delta
and
B\subseteq A
, then
B\in\Delta
.

(Thus,
\varnothing\in\Delta

\Delta

\Delta

Unlike various authors, we do not require
\left\{w\right\}\in\Delta

We shall often denote the simplicial complex
\left(W,\Delta\right)

\Delta

W

We will refer to the set
W

\left(W,\Delta\right)

\left(W,\Delta\right)
is a complex

W

The elements of
\Delta
(which are, themselves, subsets of
W

faces
of the complex
\Delta

Given a graph
G
, we say that a subset
F\subseteq E

contains
a path
p

F
contains each arc of
p
. Let us now define the two simplicial complexes we are going to study.

Definition 1.1
.

The
path-free complex

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and the
path-missing complex

\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
are the following simplicial complexes on the ground set
E
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)

\displaystyle:=\left\{F\subseteq E\,\colon\,F\text{ contains no $s-t$-path}\right\};

\displaystyle\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)

\displaystyle:=\left\{F\subseteq E\,\colon\,E\setminus F\text{ contains an $s-t$-path}\right\}.

1.2.1.
The Euler characteristic

The

1.2.3.
Homotopy types of
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)

The

of a simplicial complex is defined to be its equivalence class with respect to homotopy-equivalence.
Our next goal is to describe the homotopy types of the complexes
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
in terms of the structure of
G
.

First we consider
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
.

Theorem 1.9
.

The path-missing complex
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
has one of the following two homotopy types:

(a)

If
G
contains a useless arc or a cycle, then
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
is contractible.

(b)

Otherwise,
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
is homotopy-equivalent to a sphere of dimension
\#E-\#V^{\prime}-1
.

Here and in the following, we say that a simplicial complex
\left(W,\Delta\right)
is “homotopy-equivalent to a sphere of dimension
-1

\Delta=\left\{\varnothing\right\}
. (Such a complex is often called an

, and its geometric realization is the empty space.)
Moreover, we agree to consider the empty complex

to be contractible.

Now we move on to
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
. If
s=t
, then
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)=\varnothing
, since any subset of

contains the trivial

Theorem 1.10
.

Assume that
E\neq\varnothing
. Then, the path-free complex
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
has one of the following two homotopy types:

(a)

If
G
contains a useless arc or a cycle, then
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
is contractible.

(b)

Otherwise,
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
is homotopy-equivalent to a sphere of dimension
\#V^{\prime}-2
.

Both Theorems

and
1.10

The proofs will rely on

2.
Simple properties

Before we get to the proofs of the above theorems, let us explore some of the most elementary properties of the complexes
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
.

If
\Delta
is a simplicial complex on the ground set
W
, then the
Alexander dual

\Delta^{\vee}
of
\Delta
is defined by

\Delta^{\vee}=\left\{F\subseteq W\,\colon\,W\setminus F\notin\Delta\right\}.

This is again a simplicial complex on the ground set
W
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The simplicial complexes
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
are Alexander duals of each other.

Proof.

This is immediate from the definitions.
∎

Given a simplicial complex
\Delta
with ground set
W
and an element
w\in W
, we say that
\Delta
is a
cone with apex
w
if it has the following property:

For any face
I
of
\Delta
, we have
I\cup\left\{w\right\}\in\Delta
.

Note that the “dual” property (i.e., that
I\setminus\left\{w\right\}\in\Delta
for any
I\in\Delta
) automatically holds for any simplicial complex
\Delta
.
Thus,
\Delta
is a cone with apex
w
if and only if adding
w
to any subset of
W
does not change whether this subset is a face of
\Delta
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
G
has a useless arc
e
.
Then, both
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
are cones with apex
e
.

Proof.

The arc
e
is useless, and thus is not contained in any
s-t
-path.
Hence, if
I
is a subset of
E
, then any
s-t
-path contained in

I\cup\left\{e\right\}
must also be contained in
I
.
Therefore, if a subset
I
of
E
contains no
s-t
-path, then the
subset
I\cup\left\{e\right\}
contains no
s-t
-path either.
In other words, for any
I\in\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
it also holds that
I\cup\left\{e\right\}\in\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
.
Thus,
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
is a cone with apex
e
.
A similar argument applies to
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
.
(Alternatively, using Lemma
2.1
, we can derive this from the following general fact:
If a simplicial complex
\Delta
is a cone with apex
w
, then
\Delta^{\vee}
is a cone with apex
w
as well.)
∎

Let us observe a few more properties of
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
, which will not be used in the sequel.
If
\Delta
is a simplicial complex on ground set
W
, then the subsets of
W
that do not belong to
\Delta
are called the
nonfaces
of
\Delta
.
A
minimal nonface
of
\Delta
means a nonface of
\Delta
that contains no smaller nonfaces of
\Delta
as subsets.
The
codimension
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
on ground set
W
is defined to be
\#W-\max_{F\in\Delta}\#F
.

Proposition 2.3
.

(a)

The minimal nonfaces of
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
are the
s-t
-paths of
G
.

(b)

The minimal nonfaces of
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
are the minimal
s-t
-cut-sets of
G
, i.e., the minimal subsets of
E
that intersect every
s-t
-path nontrivially.

(c)

The codimension of
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
is the size of a smallest
s-t
-cut-set of
G
.

(d)

The codimension of
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
is the length of a shortest
s-t
-path.

Proof.

Parts (a) and (b) are immediate from the definitions.
For parts (c) and (d), recall that the facets (i.e., maximal faces) of a complex are the complements of the minimal nonfaces of its Alexander dual.
Hence the codimension of a complex is the minimum size of a minimal nonface of its Alexander dual.
∎

Proposition
2.3
(b) has the consequence that the minimum size of a nonface of
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
is the minimum size of an
s-t
-cut-set of
G
. This situates the complex

in the context of combinatorial optimization, where minimum cuts have been a long-time topic of research

]
,
[

]
.
It is well-known (and easy to prove) that any minimal

\left\{e\in E:e\text{ has source in }S\text{ and target in }V\setminus S\right\}

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)

We will then connect the former to the latter (Subsection

3.1.
Deletions and links

Given any simplicial complex

•

If

If

\lk_{\Delta}(w)
on the ground set

defined by

\displaystyle\lk_{\Delta}(w)

\displaystyle:=\left\{F\subseteq W\setminus\left\{w\right\}\,\colon\,F\cup\left\{w\right\}\in\Delta\right\}.

As an exercise in working with the definitions, the simplicially innocent reader may prove the following fundamental fact.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

be a simplicial complex on ground set
W

Let

(a)

We have
\dl_{\Delta}(w)=\left\{F\in\Delta\,\colon\,w\notin F\right\}
.

(b)

We have

\displaystyle\lk_{\Delta}(w)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\in\dl_{\Delta}(w)\,\colon\,F\cup\left\{w\right\}\in\Delta\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{I\setminus\left\{w\right\}\,\colon\,I\in\Delta\text{ and }w\in I\right\}.

(c)

The map
\left\{I\in\Delta\,\colon\,w\in I\right\}\to\lk_{\Delta}(w),\ I\mapsto I\setminus\left\{w\right\}
is a bijection.

We also invite the reader to verify the following easy fact, which says that
taking the Alexander dual of a simplicial complex turns deletions into links
and vice versa:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on ground set

.
Let

(a)

We have

(b)

We have
\left(\lk_{\Delta}\left(w\right)\right)^{\vee}=\dl_{\Delta^{\vee}}\left(w\right)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let

[

. (Note that

]

\Delta_{W\setminus\left\{w\right\}}^{\varnothing,\left\{w\right\}}
and
\Delta_{W\setminus\left\{w\right\}}^{\left\{w\right\},\varnothing}

[
Fl03
, Lemma 1.1]

yields Proposition

(a) by setting

and

∎

e
are arcs of
G/e
), and so we are done.
If, however,
p
does contain
e
, then we can obtain an
s-t
-walk of
G/e

by removing
e
from
p
,
and this
s-t
-walk is clearly contained in
I\setminus\left\{e\right\}
.
This proves the “
\Longrightarrow
” direction of Lemma
3.3
.

\Longleftarrow:

Assume that
I\setminus\left\{e\right\}
contains an
s-t
-path
q
of
G/e
.
We must prove that
I
contains an
s-t
-path of
G
.

Let
s^{\prime}
be the target of
e
. Note that the contraction of
e
identifies the two vertices
s
and
s^{\prime}
.
Thus, the arcs of
G/e
starting at
s
are (1) the arcs of
G
starting at
s
except for the arc
e
, and (2) the arcs of
G
starting at
s^{\prime}
(if
s^{\prime}\neq s
).

Now, let us regard the arcs on the
s-t
-path
q
as arcs of the original graph
G
.
In this way, it is no longer guaranteed that they still form an
s-t
-path,
since
s
and
s^{\prime}
are not necessarily identical in
G
;
however, they still form either an
s-t
-path or an
s^{\prime}-t
-path (because
s
and
s^{\prime}
are the only two vertices that get identified in
G/e
, and the
s-t
-path
q
never revisits its starting vertex
s
after leaving it).
If they form an
s-t
-path, then we conclude that
I
contains an
s-t
-path of
G
(namely, the path that they form), and thus we are done.
If they don’t, then they form an
s^{\prime}-t
-path
q^{\prime}
that does not contain the vertex
s
, and we can again conclude that
I
contains an
s-t
-path of
G
(namely, the path obtained by prepending
e
to
q^{\prime}
).
In either case, we have shown that
I
contains an
s-t
-path of
G
.
This proves the “
\Longleftarrow
” direction of Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
.
Let
F
be a subset of
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
.
Then,
E\setminus F
contains an
s-t
-path of
G
if and only if
\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F
contains an
s-t
-path of
G/e
.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
3.3
(applied to

), because
e\in E\setminus F
and
\left(E\setminus F\right)\setminus\left\{e\right\}=\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F
.
∎

3.3.
Links and deletions of
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)

The link and the deletion of an arc
e
in
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
can be described in terms of smaller graphs at least when the source of
e
is
s
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
e\in E
. Then:

(a)

We always have
\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)
.

(b)

We have
\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)
if the source of
e
is
s
.

Proof.

(a) An
s-t
-path of
G\backslash e
is the same thing as an
s-t
-path of
G
that does not contain
e
.
Hence, for any subset
I
of
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
, we have the equivalence

(3)

\left(I\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G$}\right)\Longleftrightarrow\left(I\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G\backslash e$}\right)

(since an
s-t
-path of
G
contained in
I
cannot use the arc
e
anyway, and thus must be an
s-t
-path of
G\backslash e
).

From the definition of
\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)
, we have

\displaystyle\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\cup\left\{e\right\}\in\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,E\setminus\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\right)\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)$}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{since $E\setminus\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\right)=\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F$ for any set $F$}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G\backslash e$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the equivalence \eqref{pf.lem:delcon.1}, applied to $I=\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F$}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)$}\right).

This proves Lemma
3.5
(a).

(b) Assume that the source of
e
is
s
.
From the definition of
\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)
, we have

\displaystyle\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\in\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,E\setminus F\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)$}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus F\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G/e$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem.G/e.stpath-comp}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)$}\right).

This proves Lemma
3.5
(b).
∎

The following is the
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
-analogue of Lemma
3.5
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
e\in E
. Then:

(a)

We always have
\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)
.

(b)

We have
\lk_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PF}\left(G/e\right)
if the source of
e
is
s
.

First proof of Lemma
3.6
.

(a) For any subset
I
of
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
, we have the equivalence

(4)

\left(I\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G$}\right)\Longleftrightarrow\left(I\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G\backslash e$}\right).

(Indeed, this follows from the equivalence (
3
) by negating both sides.)

From the definition of
\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)
, we have

\displaystyle\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\in\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)$}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G\backslash e$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the equivalence \eqref{pf.lem:delcon_pf.1}, applied to $I=F$}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)$}\right).

This proves Lemma
3.6
(a).

(b) Assume that the source of
e
is
s
.
Let
F
be a subset of
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
. Hence,

F\cup\left\{e\right\}
is a subset of
E
that contains
e
.
Thus, Lemma
3.3
(applied to
I=F\cup\left\{e\right\}
)
shows that
F\cup\left\{e\right\}
contains an
s-t
-path of
G
if and
only if
\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus\left\{e\right\}
contains an
s-t
-path of
G/e
.
Thus, we have the equivalence

\displaystyle\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G$}\right)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left(\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus\left\{e\right\}\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G/e$}\right)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left(F\text{ contains an $s-t$-path of $G/e$}\right)

(since
\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\right)\setminus\left\{e\right\}=F
).
Negating both sides of this, we obtain the equivalence

\displaystyle\left(F\cup\left\{e\right\}\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G$}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left(F\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G/e$}\right).

Forget that we fixed
F
.
We thus have proved the equivalence (
5
)
for any subset
F
of
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
.

From the definition of
\lk_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)
, we have

\displaystyle\lk_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\cup\left\{e\right\}\in\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\cup\left\{e\right\}\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)$}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\subseteq E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\,\colon\,F\text{ contains no $s-t$-path of $G/e$}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left(\text{by the equivalence \eqref{pf.lem:delcon_pf.2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{PF}\left(G/e\right)\qquad\left(\text{by the definition of $\mathcal{PF}\left(G/e\right)$}\right).

This proves Lemma
3.6
(b).
∎

Second proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Lemma
3.6
can also
be derived from Lemma
3.5
using Lemma
2.1

and Proposition
3.2
. For example, let us prove part (a).
Lemma
2.1
yields
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)=\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)^{\vee}
. Thus,

(6)

\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\dl_{\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)^{\vee}}(e)=\left(\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(e\right)\right)^{\vee},

since Proposition
3.2
(b) yields

\left(\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(e\right)\right)^{\vee}=\dl_{\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)^{\vee}}(e)
.
But Lemma
3.5
(a) yields
\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)
.
Hence, (
6
) rewrites as

\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)\right)^{\vee}=\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)

(by Lemma
2.1
, applied to
G\backslash e
instead of
G
). Thus,
Lemma
3.6
(a) is proved. Similarly, we obtain part (b) from
Lemma
3.5
(b) using Proposition
3.2
(a).
∎

3.4.
Graph-theoretical properties of deletion and contraction

The next lemmas discuss how deletion and contraction of a single arc affect various properties of our graph, such as the existence of useless arcs and cycles and the number of nonsinks.

Lemma 3.7
.

Any arc of
G
that has target
s
is useless.

Proof.

An arc with target
s
cannot appear in an
s-t
-path.
Thus, it must be useless.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
.
Let
s^{\prime}
be the target of
e
.
Assume that
e
is not the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.
Then,
G/e
has a useless arc.

Proof.

We assumed that
e
is not the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.
Thus, there is another arc
f\neq e
with target
s^{\prime}
.
In the graph
G/e
, this arc
f
has target
s
(since its target
s^{\prime}
is identified with
s
in
G/e
) and thus is useless (by Lemma
3.7
, applied to
G/e
instead of
G
).
This proves Lemma
3.8
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
.
Assume that
e
is not useless.
Let
s^{\prime}
be the target of
e
.
Assume further that
s^{\prime}\neq t
, and that
e
is the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.
Then,
G\backslash e
has a useless arc.

Proof.

The arc
e
is not useless; thus, there exists an
s-t
-path that contains
e
.
This
s-t
-path cannot end at
s^{\prime}
(since
s^{\prime}\neq t
), and thus its last arc cannot be
e
.
Hence, there exists an arc that follows
e
in this
s-t
-path.
Let
e^{\prime}
be this arc. Clearly,
e^{\prime}
has source
s^{\prime}
.

We have assumed that
e
is the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.
Hence, there is no arc of
G\backslash e
that has target
s^{\prime}
.

We shall show that
e^{\prime}
is useless in
G\backslash e
.
Indeed, assume the contrary. Thus, there exists some
s-t
-path of
G\backslash e
that uses this arc
e^{\prime}
.
This path must pass through the vertex
s^{\prime}
(since the arc
e^{\prime}
has source
s^{\prime}
),
and thus must start at
s^{\prime}

(since there is no arc of
G\backslash e
that has target
s^{\prime}
).
Since it is an
s-t
-path, this means that
s^{\prime}=s
.
Hence, the arc
e
has target
s
(since it has target
s^{\prime}
),
and thus is useless in
G
(by Lemma
3.7
).
But this contradicts the fact that
e
is not useless in
G
.
This contradiction shows that our assumption was false.
Hence,
G\backslash e
has a useless arc (namely,
e^{\prime}
).
This proves Lemma
3.9
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
.
Assume that
e
is not useless.
Let
s^{\prime}
be the target of
e
.
Assume further that
\#E>1
, and that
e
is the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.
Then,
G\backslash e
has a useless arc.

Proof.

If
s^{\prime}\neq t
, then this follows from
Lemma
3.9
.
Thus, for the rest of this proof, we WLOG assume that
s^{\prime}=t
.

The arc
e
is not useless; thus, it belongs to an
s-t
-path.
This
s-t
-path thus contains at least one arc.
Therefore,
s\neq t
.

But recall that
e
is the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.
In other words,
e
is the only arc with target
t

(since
s^{\prime}=t
).
Hence, the graph
G\backslash e
has no arc with target
t
.
Therefore, the graph
G\backslash e
has no
s-t
-path
(because
s\neq t
, so any
s-t
-path would have to end with an arc with target
t
).
Therefore, any arc of
G\backslash e
is useless.

We have
\#E>1
; hence, the set
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
is nonempty.
In other words,
G\backslash e
has an arc (since

E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
is the arc set of
G\backslash e
).
Hence,
G\backslash e
has a useless arc
(since any arc of
G\backslash e
is useless).
Lemma
3.10
is thus proved.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
.
Let
s^{\prime}
be the target of
e
.
Assume that
G
has no useless arcs.
Then:

(a)

The graph
G
has no cycle containing
s
.

(b)

We have
s^{\prime}\neq s
.

(c)

If
s^{\prime}\neq t
, then there exists an arc
of
G\backslash e
whose source is
s^{\prime}
.

(d)

If
s^{\prime}\neq t
, then the graph
G/e

has exactly one fewer nonsink than
G
.

(e)

If
e
is the only arc of
G
with target
s^{\prime}
,
and if
\#E>1
, then
s^{\prime}\neq t
.

Proof.

(a) Any cycle containing
s
would contain an arc with target
s
. By Lemma
3.7
, such an arc would be useless.
This contradicts the assumption that
G
has no useless arcs. Thus,
G
has no cycle containing
s
. This proves Lemma
3.11
(a).

(b) If we had
s^{\prime}=s
, then the arc
e
would be a self-loop and therefore useless (since every self-loop is useless). But this is impossible (since
G
has no useless arcs).
Hence, Lemma
3.11
(b) is proven.

(c) Assume that
s^{\prime}\neq t
.
The arc
e
of
G
is not useless (since
G
has no useless arcs),
and thus is contained in an
s-t
-path of
G
.
This path cannot end with
e
(since
s^{\prime}\neq t
);
thus, there exists an arc that follows
e
in this path.
Let
f
be this arc.
Then, the source of
f
is
s^{\prime}
,
which is distinct from
s
(since Lemma
3.11
(b)
yields
s^{\prime}\neq s
).
Hence,
f\neq e
(since the source of
e
is
s
).
Thus,
f
is also an arc of
G\backslash e
.
Hence, there exists an arc
of
G\backslash e
whose source is
s^{\prime}
(namely,
f
).
This proves Lemma
3.11
(c).

(d) Assume that
s^{\prime}\neq t
.
Thus, Lemma
3.11
(c) shows that
there exists an arc of
G\backslash e
whose source is
s^{\prime}
.
Let
f
be this arc.
Then,
f
is an arc of
G\backslash e
, thus also an
arc of
G/e
and of
G
.
Hence,
G
has an arc with source
s^{\prime}
(namely,
f
).
In other words,
s^{\prime}
is a nonsink of
G
.
Also,
s
is a nonsink of
G
(since the arc

has source
s
).
The two nonsinks

and
s^{\prime}
are distinct (by
Lemma
3.11
(b)).
In the graph
G/e
, these two nonsinks become identical, but still
remain a nonsink of
G/e
(since the arc

has source
s^{\prime}
and
is an arc of
G/e
).
For any vertex

, it is clear that

is a nonsink of
G/e
if and only if

is a nonsink of
G

(since the arcs of
G/e
with source

are exactly the arcs of
G

with source

).
Thus, when passing from

to

, we only lose one nonsink (since the

and
s^{\prime}
become identical).
This proves Lemma
3.11
(d).

(e) Assume that
e
is the only arc of
G
with target
s^{\prime}
,
and that
\#E>1
. We must prove that
s^{\prime}\neq t
.

Assume the contrary. Thus,
s^{\prime}=t
.
Hence,
e
is the only arc of
G
with target
t

(since
e
is the only arc of
G
with target
s^{\prime}
).

Since
\#E>1
, there exists at least one arc
f\in E

distinct from
e
. This arc
f
cannot be useless (since
G

has no useless arcs).
In other words,
f
is contained in an
s-t
-path
p
of
G
.
Consider this path
p
.

This path
p
has at least one arc (since it contains
f
).
Thus, it has a last arc. This last arc of

must have

(since
p
is an
s-t
-path), and thus must be

(since

is the only arc of

with target

).
Therefore, the second-to-last vertex of

is the source of

.
In other words, the second-to-last vertex of

is

(since the source of

is
s
).
Of course, the first vertex of

is

, too (since

is an
s-t
-path).

We know that both

and

are arcs of

.
Since

is distinct from

, this entails that

has at
least two arcs, thus at least three vertices.
Hence, the first vertex and the second-to-last vertex of

are distinct
(since the vertices of a path are distinct).
But this contradicts the fact that both of these vertices are

.
This contradiction shows that our assumption was false.
Lemma
3.11
(e) is proved.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
.
Assume that

has a cycle but no useless arcs.
Then, both graphs

and

have cycles.

We have assumed that

has a cycle.
This cycle cannot contain

(by Lemma

(a)), and thus cannot contain the arc

.
Hence, this cycle is a cycle of

and also a nontrivial closed walk of

.
Thus, both graphs

and
G/e
have nontrivial closed walks,
and therefore have cycles.
This proves Lemma
3.12
.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let

be an arc whose source is

.
Let

be the target of

.
Assume that the graph

-path.

.

.

.
In other words,

-path

.

.

But

is useless as an arc of

.

belongs to no

-path of

.
Note that

is distinct from

, since

is an arc of

.

The

uses the arc

, and thus also uses the

(since otherwise,

would be an

s-t
-path of

-path of

).

We assumed that

-path.
Let us denote this path by

.

to be the polynomial

c_{G}\left(x,y\right):=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }G\text{ has a useless arc or a cycle};\\
x^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

in
\mathbb{Z}\left[x,y\right]
. (This polynomial is well-defined, since
Lemma
5.1
shows that both exponents
\#V^{\prime}-1
and

\#E-\#V^{\prime}
are nonnegative integers.)

Note that
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)
depends not only on the underlying
digraph
\left(V,E\right)
but also on the vertices
s
and
t
.

5.2.
The recursion for c-polynomials

The most useful feature of the c-polynomial (to us) will be the following
recursive formula.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
G
has no useless arcs, and that
\#E>1
.
Let
e\in E
be an arc whose source is
s
. Then,

c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=xc_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)+yc_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right).

Proof.

From
\#E>1
, we obtain
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\neq\varnothing
.
Hence,
c_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)
and
c_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)
are defined.

If
G
has a cycle, then so do
G/e
and
G\backslash e
(by Lemma

3.12
), and thus all three polynomials
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)

and
c_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)
and
c_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)

equal
0
(by Definition
5.2
). Hence, in this case, the claim
of Lemma
5.3
boils down to
0=x0+y0
, which is obvious.

Thus, we WLOG assume that
G
has no cycles. Hence,
G
has no useless arcs
and no cycles. Definition
5.2
thus yields

(12)

c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=x^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}}.

Let
s^{\prime}
be the target of the arc
e
. We are in one of the following
two cases:

Case 1:
The arc
e
is not the only arc of
G
with target

s^{\prime}
.

Case 2:
The arc
e
is the only arc of
G
with target
s^{\prime}
.

Let us first consider Case 1. In this case, the arc
e
is not the only arc
of
G
with target
s^{\prime}
. Hence, Lemma
3.8
shows
that
G/e
has a useless arc. Hence, Definition
5.2
yields

(13)

\displaystyle c_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)=0.

Furthermore, the graph
G\backslash e
is a subgraph of
G
, and thus has no
cycles (since
G
has no cycles). If
G\backslash e
had a useless arc, then
Lemma
3.14
would yield that the graph
G/e
has no cycles and no
useless arcs; but this would contradict the fact that
G/e
has a useless
arc. Thus,
G\backslash e
has no useless arcs.

Therefore, Lemma
3.15
shows that the graph
G\backslash e

has the same nonsinks as
G
. In other words, the set of nonsinks of

G\backslash e
is
V^{\prime}
(since the set of nonsinks of
G
is

V^{\prime}
).

We now know that the graph
G\backslash e
has no useless arcs and no cycles,
and its set of nonsinks is
V^{\prime}
, whereas its arc set is

E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
(by its definition). Thus, Definition

5.2
yields

\displaystyle c_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)

\displaystyle=x^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)-\#V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=x^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-1-\#V^{\prime}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }\#\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)=\#E-1\right).

Using this equality and using (
13
), we have

\displaystyle xc_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)+yc_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)

\displaystyle=x0+yx^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-1-\#V^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=yx^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-1-\#V^{\prime}}=x^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}}.

Comparing this with (
12
), we obtain
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=xc_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)+yc_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)
. Hence, Lemma
5.3
is proved in Case 1.

Let us now consider Case 2. In this case, the arc
e
is the only arc of
G

with target
s^{\prime}
. Moreover, the arc
e
of
G
is not useless (since

G
has no useless arcs). Hence, Lemma
3.10
yields
that
G\backslash e
has a useless arc. Therefore,
Definition
5.2
yields

(14)

\displaystyle c_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)=0.

Furthermore, Lemma
3.14
shows that the graph
G/e
has no cycles
and no useless arcs.

Let
V^{\prime\prime}
be the set of all nonsinks of
G/e
. Lemma

3.11
(e) yields that
s^{\prime}\neq t
. Thus, Lemma

3.11
(d) shows that the graph
G/e
has exactly one fewer
nonsink than
G
. In other words,
\#V^{\prime\prime}=\#V^{\prime}-1
.
Next, recall that
G/e
has no cycles
and no useless arcs, and the set of all nonsinks of
G/e
is
V^{\prime\prime}
, whereas the arc set of
G/e
is
E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
.
Thus, Definition
5.2
yields

\displaystyle c_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)

\displaystyle=x^{\#V^{\prime\prime}-1}y^{\#\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)-\#V^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=x^{\left(\#V^{\prime}-1\right)-1}y^{\left(\#E-1\right)-\left(\#V^{\prime}-1\right)}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since }\#V^{\prime\prime}=\#V^{\prime}-1\\
\text{and }\#\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)=\#E-1\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=x^{\#V^{\prime}-2}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}}.

Using this equality and using (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle xc_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)+yc_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)

\displaystyle=xx^{\#V^{\prime}-2}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}}+y0

\displaystyle=xx^{\#V^{\prime}-2}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}}=x^{\#V^{\prime}-1}y^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}}.

Comparing this with (
12
), we obtain
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=xc_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)+yc_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right)
. Hence, Lemma
5.3
is proved in Case 2.

We have now proved Lemma
5.3
in all cases.
∎

6.
Proof of the Euler characteristic

We shall now work towards computing the Euler characteristics of our complexes.

6.1.
General facts about Euler characteristics

We begin with some general properties of reduced Euler characteristics.
Recall that these were defined in Subsection
1.2.1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on the ground set
W
. Let
w\in W
. Then,

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right)=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\dl_{\Delta}(w)\right)-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\lk_{\Delta}(w)\right).

Proof.

Lemma
4.1
yields

f_{\Delta}\left(x\right)=f_{\dl_{\Delta}(w)}\left(x\right)+xf_{\lk_{\Delta}(w)}\left(x\right)
.
Substituting
-1
for
x
on both sides of this equality,
we find

f_{\Delta}\left(-1\right)=f_{\dl_{\Delta}(w)}\left(-1\right)-f_{\lk_{\Delta}(w)}\left(-1\right)
.
In view of (
2
), this rewrites as

-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right)=-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\dl_{\Delta}(w)\right)-\left(-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\lk_{\Delta}(w)\right)\right)
.
Multiplying both sides of this equality by
-1
, we obtain precisely
the claim of Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on a nonempty ground set
W
. Then,

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta^{\vee}\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\#W-1}\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right).

Proof.

By definition, the faces of
\Delta^{\vee}

are the complements (in
W
) of the subsets of
W
that do not belong to
\Delta
.
Hence, the map
2^{W}\setminus\Delta\to\Delta^{\vee},\ I\mapsto W\setminus I

is a bijection.
Thus,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta^{\vee}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in\Delta^{\vee}}\left(-1\right)^{\#I-1}=\sum_{I\in 2^{W}\setminus\Delta}\left(-1\right)^{\#\left(W\setminus I\right)-1}

(15)

\displaystyle=\left(-1\right)^{\#W-1}\sum_{I\in 2^{W}\setminus\Delta}\left(-1\right)^{\#I}.

Since
W
is nonempty, we have
\sum_{I\in 2^{W}}\left(-1\right)^{\#I}=0
, and therefore

\sum_{I\in 2^{W}\setminus\Delta}\left(-1\right)^{\#I}=-\sum_{I\in\Delta}\left(-1\right)^{\#I}=\sum_{I\in\Delta}\left(-1\right)^{\#I-1}=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right).

Substituting this into (
15
), we obtain Lemma
6.2
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on a ground set
W
. Let
w\in W
. Assume that
\Delta
is a cone with apex
w
.
Then,
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right)=0
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.2
(b) yields

f_{\Delta}\left(x\right)=\left(1+x\right)f_{\lk_{\Delta}(w)}\left(x\right)
.
Substituting
-1
for
x
on both sides of this equality, we obtain

f_{\Delta}\left(-1\right)=\underbrace{\left(1+\left(-1\right)\right)}_{=0}f_{\lk_{\Delta}(w)}\left(-1\right)=0
.
In view of (
2
), this rewrites as

-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right)=0
. Thus,

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\Delta\right)=0
.
This proves Lemma
6.3
.
∎

6.2.
The Euler characteristics of
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)

Recall the c-polynomial
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)
introduced in Definition

5.2
. We shall now restate Theorem
1.3
in the
form that is most convenient for our proof.

Lemma 6.4
.

Assume that
E\neq\varnothing
. Then,

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right).

This lemma will quickly yield the original form of Theorem
1.3

(once we compute
c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)
and handle the
E=\varnothing

case by hand).

Proof of Lemma
6.4
.

We proceed in two steps:

Step 1:
We claim that Lemma
6.4
is true when
G

has a useless arc.

Indeed, assume that
G
has a useless arc
e
. Then, Lemma
2.2

shows that
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
is a cone with apex
e
, and therefore
Lemma
6.3
(applied to
W=E
,
\Delta=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and

w=e
) shows that
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=0
.
Meanwhile, Definition
5.2
yields
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=0

(since
G
has a useless arc) and therefore
c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)=0
.
Therefore,
-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)=-0=0
. Comparing this with

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=0
, we see that

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)
. Hence, we have proved Lemma
6.4
in the case
when
G
has a useless arc.

Step 2:
Let us now prove Lemma
6.4
in general.

We proceed by induction on the positive integer
\#E
(this is a positive
integer, since
E\neq\varnothing
).

Base case:
We must show that Lemma
6.4
holds when

\#E=1
.

Indeed, assume that
\#E=1
. Thus,
E=\left\{e\right\}
for some arc
e

of
G
. Consider this arc
e
. If
G
has a useless arc, then we already
know (from Step 1) that Lemma
6.4
is true. Thus, we WLOG
assume that
G
has no useless arcs. Hence, the arc
e
is not useless. In
other words,
e
is contained in an
s-t
-path of
G
. This
s-t
-path cannot
have any other arcs beyond
e
(since
E=\left\{e\right\}
), and thus
must consist of the arc
e
alone. Thus, the source of
e
is
s
, whereas
the target of
e
is
t
. Moreover, the arc
e
cannot be a self-loop (since
it is contained in a path), and thus we have
s\neq t
. Thus, the digraph
G

consists of the single arc
s\overset{e}{\longrightarrow}t
and a (possibly
empty) set of other vertices but no other arcs (since
E=\left\{e\right\}
). Thus, we can describe
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
explicitly: The
only subsets of
E
are
\varnothing
and
\left\{e\right\}
(since

E=\left\{e\right\}
), and we have
\varnothing\in\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)

(since
E\setminus\varnothing=E
contains an
s-t
-path) but

\left\{e\right\}\notin\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
(since

E\setminus\left\{e\right\}=\varnothing
contains no
s-t
-path (because

s\neq t
)). Hence,
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)=\left\{\varnothing\right\}
, so that
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\left\{\varnothing\right\}\right)=-1
.
On the other hand, our description of
G
shows that
G
has no useless arcs
and no cycles, and has exactly
1
arc (namely,
e
) and exactly
1
nonsink
(namely,
s
). Definition
5.2
thus shows that
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=x^{1-1}y^{1-1}=x^{0}y^{0}=1
. Hence,
c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)=1
, so that
-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)=-1
. Comparing this with

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=-1
, we
obtain
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)
. This shows that Lemma
6.4

holds for our
G
. Thus, Lemma
6.4
is proved when
\#E=1
.
This completes the base case.

Induction step:
We fix a directed graph
G=\left(V,E,s,t\right)

with
\#E>1
, and we assume (as induction hypothesis) that Lemma

6.4
is true for all graphs with exactly
\#E-1
arcs.
We must now prove Lemma
6.4
for our graph
G
.

If
G
has a useless arc, then we already know (from Step 1) that Lemma

6.4
is true. Thus, we WLOG assume that
G
has no useless
arcs. However,
E\neq\varnothing
. Thus, there exists some
f\in E
. Consider
this
f
. The arc
f
cannot be useless (since
G
has no useless arcs), and
thus is contained in an
s-t
-path of
G
. This path has at least one arc
(since it contains
f
), and thus has a first arc. Let
e
be this first
arc. Then, the arc
e\in E
has source
s
(since it is the first arc of an

s-t
-path). Lemma
5.3
thus yields

c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=xc_{G/e}\left(x,y\right)+yc_{G\backslash e}\left(x,y\right).

Substituting
1
and
-1
for
x
and
y
in this equality, we find

\displaystyle c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)

\displaystyle=1c_{G/e}\left(1,-1\right)+\left(-1\right)c_{G\backslash e}\left(1,-1\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=c_{G/e}\left(1,-1\right)-c_{G\backslash e}\left(1,-1\right).

However,
\#E>1
entails
E\not\subseteq\left\{e\right\}
, thus

E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\neq\varnothing
. The graph
G/e
has arc set

E\setminus\left\{e\right\}
, whose size is
\#\left(E\setminus\left\{e\right\}\right)=\#E-1
. Thus, by our induction hypothesis, Lemma

6.4
is true for
G/e
instead of
G
. In other words, we
have

(17)

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)\right)=-c_{G/e}\left(1,-1\right).

The same argument (applied to
G\backslash e
instead of
G/e
) shows that

(18)

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)\right)=-c_{G\backslash e}\left(1,-1\right).

But Lemma
6.1
(applied to
W=E
,
\Delta=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)

and
w=e
) yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(e\right)\right)-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(e\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)\right)-\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)\right)

(since
\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(e\right)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)

by Lemma
3.5
(b), and since

\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(e\right)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)
by
Lemma
3.5
(a)). In view of (
17
) and
(
18
), we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-c_{G/e}\left(1,-1\right)\right)-\left(-c_{G\backslash e}\left(1,-1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{\left(c_{G/e}\left(1,-1\right)-c_{G\backslash e}\left(1,-1\right)\right)}_{=c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)\hskip 8.19447pt\text{(by
\eqref{pf.lem.PM.chitil-c.c-rec})}}=-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right).

This shows that Lemma
6.4
holds for our
G
. This completes
the induction step. Thus, Lemma
6.4
is proved by induction.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.3
in its original form:

Theorem
1.3
:

(a)

If
G
contains a useless arc or a cycle or satisfies
\left(E=\varnothing\text{ and }s\neq t\right)
, then
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=0
.

(b)

Otherwise,
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}+1}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We are in one of the following four cases:

Case 1:
We have
E=\varnothing
and
s=t
.

Case 2:
We have
E=\varnothing
and
s\neq t
.

Case 3:
We have
E\neq\varnothing
, and the graph
G
has a useless
arc or a cycle.

Case 4:
We have
E\neq\varnothing
, and the graph
G
has no useless
arcs and no cycles.

Let us first consider Case 1. In this case, we have
E=\varnothing
and
s=t
.
Thus, the graph
G
has no arcs (since
E=\varnothing
). Moreover,
s=t

shows that the set
\varnothing\setminus\varnothing
contains an
s-t
-path
(namely, the trivial path, with no arcs at all). Hence,
\varnothing\in\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
, so that
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)=\left\{\varnothing\right\}
. Therefore,
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\left\{\varnothing\right\}\right)=-1
. On the other hand,
G
has no nonsinks
(since
G
has no arcs); thus,

and therefore

. Combined with
\#E=0
(since

), this yields

\left(-1\right)^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}+1}=\left(-1\right)^{0-0+1}=-1
.
Comparing this with
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=-1
, we obtain
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\#E-\#V^{\prime}+1}
, which is

(b) predicts for

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)
. Thus,

1.3
is proved in Case 1.

Let us next consider Case 2. In this case, we have

and

. Thus, the graph
G
has no arcs (since

). Moreover,
s\neq t
shows that any

-path must contain at least one arc. Hence,
G
has no

s-t
-path (since
G
has no arcs). Therefore,
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)=\varnothing
, so that
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=\widetilde{\chi}\left(\varnothing\right)=0
. But this is
precisely the value that Theorem
1.3
(a) predicts for

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)
(since
E=\varnothing
and

).
Thus, Theorem
1.3
is proved in Case 2.

Next, let us consider Case 3. In this case, we have
E\neq\varnothing
, and
the graph
G
has a useless arc or a cycle. Hence, Definition

5.2
yields
c_{G}\left(x,y\right)=0
. Substituting
1
and

-1
for
x
and

in this equality, we find

.
However, Lemma
6.4
yields

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)=-c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)=0\qquad\left(\text{since }c_{G}\left(1,-1\right)=0\right).

But this is precisely the value that Theorem

(a)
predicts for
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)
. Thus, Theorem

1.3
is proved in Case 3.

Finally, let us consider Case 4. In this case, we have

, and

yields

. Substituting

and

But this is precisely the value that Theorem

(b)
predicts for
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)
. Thus, Theorem

1.3
is proved in Case 4.

We have now proved Theorem

in all four cases.
∎

It is even easier to prove Theorem

:

Theorem

:

If

, then:

(a)

If

\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)\right)=0

(b)

Otherwise,
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)\right)=\left(-1\right)^{\#V^{\prime}}

Proof of Theorem

.

Lemma

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)=\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)\right)^{\vee}

7.
Discrete Morse theory

7.1.
Definitions and topological meaning

In this section, we shall recall the basics of Forman’s
discrete Morse
theory
(foreshadowed by Brown’s
[
Bro92
]
). We refer to

[
For02
]
and
[
Koz20
]
for deeper-going expositions of this
subject. Here we shall only recall the basics that we need. We will follow the
modern terminology of “acyclic matchings”,
as in Kozlov’s
[
Koz20
]
.

First, we introduce a basic set-theoretic notation.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two sets. Then, we write
A\prec B
if
there exists some
b\in B\setminus A
such that
B=A\cup\left\{b\right\}
.
Equivalently, we write
B\succ A
in this case.

Clearly, if
A
and
B
are two finite sets, then we have the equivalences

\displaystyle\left(A\prec B\right)\

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \left(B\succ A\right)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \left(A\subseteq B\text{ and }\#\left(B\setminus A\right)=1\right)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \left(A\subseteq B\text{ and }\#B=\#A+1\right).

For instance,
\left\{2,5\right\}\prec\left\{2,3,5\right\}
but not

\left\{2,5\right\}\prec\left\{2,3,4,5\right\}
. The binary relations

\prec
and
\succ
are called “is covered
by” and “covers”.

Next, we define the notion of a matching (following
[
Koz20
, Definition
10.6]
6
6

6

In
[
Koz20
, Definition 10.6]
, Kozlov works in
a more general setting, replacing a simplicial complex
\Delta
by an
arbitrary poset. We do not need this generality here; the only posets we will
be using are simplicial complexes
\Delta
, ordered by inclusion (so that the
relation
\prec
introduced in Definition
7.1
is precisely the
covering relation of this poset).
).

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex with ground set
W
. A

partial matching
(or
matching
for short) on
\Delta
shall
mean a pair
\left(M,\mu\right)
, where
M
is a subset of
\Delta
(that
is, a set of faces of
\Delta
), and where
\mu:M\rightarrow M
is an
involution (that is, a map satisfying
\mu\circ\mu=\operatorname*{id}
) with
the property that each
A\in M
satisfies

\text{either }\mu\left(A\right)\prec A\text{ or }\mu\left(A\right)\succ A.

Note that
M
is uniquely determined by
\mu
(namely, as the domain of
\mu
), so that we will refer to
\mu
alone as a matching.

Given a matching
\left(M,\mu\right)
, we shall refer to the faces
A\in M
as the
matched
faces of this matching, while the faces
A\in\Delta\setminus M
will be called the
unmatched
faces of this matching.

Example 7.3
.

Let
W=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
. Let
\Delta
be the
simplicial complex with ground set

that contains all

subsets of
W
as
faces. Consider the matching
\left(M,\mu\right)
given by

and

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left\{1\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{1,2\right\},\qquad\mu\left(\left\{2\right\}\right)=\left\{2,3\right\},\qquad\mu\left(\left\{3\right\}\right)=\left\{3,1\right\},

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left\{1,2\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{1\right\},\qquad\mu\left(\left\{2,3\right\}\right)=\left\{2\right\},\qquad\mu\left(\left\{3,1\right\}\right)=\left\{3\right\}.

The unmatched faces of this matching are
\varnothing
and
W
.

Example 7.4
.

Let
n
and
k
be two nonnegative integers. Let
W
be an

n
-element set. Let
\Delta
be the simplicial complex consisting of all
subsets
A
of

having size

. (This is called the
\left(k-1\right)
-skeleton
of the simplex on

. Let

be the set of all subsets

of

that satisfy

\#\left(A\setminus\left\{w\right\}\right)<k
(or, equivalently, that
satisfy
\#A<k
or
\left(\#A=k\text{ and }w\in A\right)
). We can then
define a map
\mu:M\rightarrow M
by setting

\mu\left(A\right)=\begin{cases}A\cup\left\{w\right\},&\text{if }w\notin A;\\
A\setminus\left\{w\right\},&\text{if }w\in A\end{cases}\qquad\text{for each }A\in M.

(That is, the map
\mu
inserts the element

into any face that does not

, and removes it from any face that does.) It is easy to see that
this map

is well-defined

7

7

Indeed, each

\left(A\cup\left\{w\right\}\right)\setminus\left\{w\right\}=A\setminus\left\{w\right\}
, so that

and

(or, more precisely, the pair

The unmatched faces of this matching are precisely the
k

. Their number is

.

Discrete Morse theory is interested in matchings with a special property,
defined in terms of cycles (
[

),
that we describe now.

Definition 7.5
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex with ground set
W
. Let
\left(M,\mu\right)
be a matching on
\Delta
.

(a)

A

of
\mu
means an
n

of distinct faces in

such that

F_{1}\succ\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\prec F_{2}\succ\mu\left(F_{2}\right)\prec F_{3}\succ\cdots\prec F_{n}\succ\mu\left(F_{n}\right)\prec F_{1}

(that is, such that each

satisfies

F_{i}\succ\mu\left(F_{i}\right)\prec F_{i+1}
, where
F_{n+1}:=F_{1}
).

(b)

The matching
\mu
is said to be

if it has no cycle.

Example 7.6
.

(a)

The matching
\mu
constructed in Example
7.3
is not
acyclic. Indeed, the

\left(\left\{1,2\right\},\ \left\{3,1\right\},\ \left\{2,3\right\}\right)
is a cycle of
\mu
, since

\left\{1,2\right\}\succ\underbrace{\left\{1\right\}}_{=\mu\left(\left\{1,2\right\}\right)}\prec\left\{3,1\right\}\succ\underbrace{\left\{3\right\}}_{=\mu\left(\left\{3,1\right\}\right)}\prec\left\{2,3\right\}\succ\underbrace{\left\{2\right\}}_{=\mu\left(\left\{2,3\right\}\right)}\prec\left\{1,2\right\}.

(b)

The matching
\mu
in Example

is acyclic.
Indeed, the faces
F\in M
satisfying
F\succ\mu\left(F\right)
are
precisely the faces
F\in M
that contain

; therefore, a cycle
\left(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}\right)
of
\mu
would have to satisfy
w\in F_{1}

and

, which would easily yield
F_{1}=\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\cup\left\{w\right\}=F_{2}
, contradicting the distinctness of

F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}
.

Acyclic matchings are the main objects of discrete Morse theory, although
different texts give different definitions whose equivalence is not always
immediate

8

8

The closest notion in Forman’s original work is that of a
gradient vector field in
[

conceived discrete Morse theory in 1995, but is nowadays considered as a

We note that unmatched faces of a partial matching are called
“critical simplices” in
[
For02
]
.
There is a subtle but confusing notational disagreement in
the literature. Our conventions follow
[

]
. Meanwhile, more

. They give highly useful information on the homotopy type of a complex, as

Let

and

7.2.
The unmatched f-polynomial

First, we shall show some basic properties of acyclic matchings. We will use
the following polynomial fingerprint of matchings.

Definition 7.8
.

Let
\left(M,\mu\right)
be a matching on a simplicial complex
\Delta
.
Then, we define the
unmatched f-polynomial
of
\mu
to be the
polynomial

u_{\mu}\left(x\right):=\sum_{I\in\Delta\setminus M}x^{\#I}\in\mathbb{Z}\left[x\right].

Note that the sum here ranges over all unmatched faces of
\mu
.

For instance, the matching
\mu
in Example
7.3
has unmatched
f-polynomial
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=x^{0}+x^{3}
, whereas the one in
Example
7.4
has unmatched f-polynomial
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=\dbinom{n-1}{k}x^{k}
. A trivial example is the empty matching

\left(\varnothing,\varnothing\right)
, which exists for every simplicial
complex
\Delta
, and whose unmatched f-polynomial
u_{\varnothing}\left(x\right)
is just the usual f-polynomial
f_{\Delta}\left(x\right)

(since
\Delta\setminus\varnothing=\Delta
).

A matching
\mu
whose unmatched f-polynomial
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)
is a
single monomial
x^{m}
is one that has only one unmatched face (which has
size
m
, that is, dimension
m-1
). For such matchings, Theorem

7.7
has the following consequence.

Corollary 7.9
.

Let
\mu
be an acyclic matching on a simplicial complex

\Delta
.

(a)

If
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=0
, then
\Delta
is contractible.

(b)

If
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=x^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
then
\Delta
is homotopy-equivalent to a sphere of dimension
m-1
.

We note that the condition
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=0
in Corollary

7.9
(a) means that all faces of
\Delta
are matched; a
simplicial complex
\Delta
with such a matching
\mu
is said to be

collapsible
(see
[
For02
, Theorem 6.4]
).

9.
Further directions

9.1.
Combinatorial grapes

Marietti and Testa, in
[
MT08
, Definition 3.2]
, introduced a certain well-behaved class of simplicial complexes: the
combinatorial grapes
.
We recall their definition (translated into our language) next.

Definition 9.1
.

The
combinatorial grapes
are a class of simplicial complexes defined recursively:

•

Any simplicial complex
\left(W,\Delta\right)
with
\#W\leq 1
is a combinatorial grape.

•

Let
\left(W,\Delta\right)
be a simplicial complex. If there exists an
a\in W
such that both
\lk_{\Delta}(a)
and
\dl_{\Delta}(a)
are combinatorial grapes, and such that there is a cone
\left(W\setminus\left\{a\right\},\Gamma\right)
satisfying
\lk_{\Delta}(a)\subseteq\Gamma\subseteq\dl_{\Delta}(a)
, then
\left(W,\Delta\right)
is a combinatorial grape.
10
10

10

Here, the statement
\lk_{\Delta}(a)\subseteq\Gamma\subseteq\dl_{\Delta}(a)
means that each face of
\lk_{\Delta}(a)
is a face of
\Gamma
, and that each face of
\Gamma
is a face of
\dl_{\Delta}(a)
.

It is shown in
[
MT08
, Proposition 3.3]
that combinatorial grapes have a rather simple homotopy type (viz., they are disjoint unions of points or wedges of spheres).

An even more restrictive notion is that of a
strong grape
, which we define as follows.

Definition 9.2
.

The
strong grapes
are a class of simplicial complexes defined recursively:

•

Any simplicial complex
\left(W,\Delta\right)
with
\#W\leq 1
is a strong grape.

•

Let
\left(W,\Delta\right)
be a simplicial complex. If there exists an
a\in W
such that both
\lk_{\Delta}(a)
and
\dl_{\Delta}(a)
are strong grapes, and such that at least one of
\lk_{\Delta}(a)
and
\dl_{\Delta}(a)
is a cone, then
\left(W,\Delta\right)
is a strong grape.

Clearly, every strong grape is a combinatorial grape.
We shall now show that both complexes
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
for a graph
G
are strong grapes.

Proposition 9.3
.

Both
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
are strong grapes.

Proof.

We proceed by strong induction on
\#E
.
Thus, we assume (as induction hypothesis) that both
\mathcal{PF}\left(H\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(H\right)
are strong grapes whenever
H
is a graph with fewer arcs than
G
.
We must now show that both
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
are strong grapes.
This is obvious if
\#E\leq 1
, so we WLOG assume that
\#E>1
.

The case when
s=t
is easy
11
11

11

Indeed, in this case, it is clear that any subset of
E

contains an
s-t
-path, and thus we have

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)=2^{E}
.
Thus, all that needs to be proved is that both
\left(E,2^{E}\right)

and
\left(E,\varnothing\right)
are strong grapes.
But this is straightforward to do by induction on
\#E

(taking any
a\in E
in the induction step).
.
Thus, we WLOG assume that
s\neq t
.

The case when
G
has no
s-t
-path is easy
12
12

12

Indeed, in this case, it can easily be checked that

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)=2^{E}
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)=\varnothing
.
Thus, all that needs to be proved is that both
\left(E,2^{E}\right)

and
\left(E,\varnothing\right)
are strong grapes.
But this was done in the previous footnote.
.
Thus, we WLOG assume that
G
has at least one
s-t
-path.
This
s-t
-path has at least one arc (since
s\neq t
).
Thus, its first arc is well-defined.
This first arc must have source
s
and is not useless (since it
belongs to an
s-t
-path).
Hence, there exists an arc
e
with source
s
that is not useless.
Consider this arc
e
.
Let
s^{\prime}
be its target.

The graph
G/e
has fewer arcs than
G
.
Hence, by the induction hypothesis, both

\mathcal{PF}\left(G/e\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)
are strong grapes.
Likewise, both

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)
are strong grapes.

Lemma
3.6
(a) yields
that
\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)
,
whereas
Lemma
3.6
(b) yields
that
\lk_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PF}\left(G/e\right)
.
Likewise, Lemma
3.5
(a) yields
that
\lk_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G\backslash e\right)
,
whereas
Lemma
3.5
(b) yields
that
\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PM}\left(G/e\right)
.

Recall that
\mathcal{PF}\left(G/e\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)
are
strong grapes.
In other words,
\lk_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)
and
\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)

are strong grapes
(since
\dl_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)=\mathcal{PF}\left(G\backslash e\right)

Thus, if we can show that at least one of

Likewise, if we can show that at least one of

\dl_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}(e)

then we can conclude that
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
is a strong grape
(since

We are in one of the following two cases:

Case 1:
The arc

is not the only arc with target

.

Case 2:
The arc

is the only arc with target
s^{\prime}
.

In this case, the arc

is not the only arc with target

.
Hence, Lemma

shows that

Thus, Lemma

In other words,

\lk_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}(e)

).

We thus have shown that

.

Remark 9.4
.

It is easy to see (recursively, using Lemma
7.11
)
that any strong grape has an acyclic matching
\left(M,\mu\right)
satisfying

u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=0
or
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=x^{m}
for some

m\in\mathbb{N}
.
However, it does not seem easy to describe which of
these two alternatives holds, nor what
m
is.
Thus, this does not yield alternative proofs of
Theorems
8.1
and
8.2
.

9.2.
Open questions

While the homotopy types of
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
answer
many natural questions about these complexes, some further
questions remain to be studied.

Question 9.5
.

Let
k
be a nonnegative integer, and assume that
G
has at least
k
disjoint quasi-cycles.
What can we say about the remainders of the polynomials
f_{\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)}\left(x\right)
and
f_{\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)}\left(x\right)
modulo
\left(1+x\right)^{k+1}
?

Question 9.6
.

We proved our claims about
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
by induction.
Can they be proved more directly? In particular, can the
acyclic matchings we recursively constructed on
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and

\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
be described directly?

Question 9.7
.

We can generalize
\mathcal{PM}\left(G\right)
and
\mathcal{PF}\left(G\right)
as follows:
Fix a positive integer
r
.
Define two complexes

\displaystyle\mathcal{PF}\left(G,r\right)

\displaystyle:=\left\{F\subseteq E\,\colon\,F\text{ contains no $r$ arc-disjoint $s-t$-paths}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{PM}\left(G,r\right)

\displaystyle:=\left\{F\subseteq E\,\colon\,E\setminus F\text{ contains $r$ arc-disjoint $s-t$-paths}\right\}

on the ground set
E
.
What can we say about these two complexes?
The Euler characteristics no longer restrict themselves to the values
0,1,-1

(for example, if
G
consists of two vertices
s
and
t
and
k
arcs from
s
to
t
,
then
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PF}\left(G,r\right)\right)=\left(-1\right)^{r}\dbinom{k-1}{r-1}

and
\widetilde{\chi}\left(\mathcal{PM}\left(G,r\right)\right)=\left(-1\right)^{k+r-1}\dbinom{k-1}{r-1}
).
Still, can anything be said about these complexes? E.g., are they bouquets of spheres? combinatorial grapes?

The latter question is of particular interest in that
any interesting homotopical properties of the complexes

\mathcal{PF}\left(G,r\right)
and
\mathcal{PM}\left(G,r\right)
would suggest a topological
undercurrent in the theory of network flows.
(Indeed, the existence of
r
arc-disjoint
s-t
-paths
is equivalent to the existence of an
s-t
-flow of
value
r
; see
[
FF62
, Theorem 4.2 and paragraph thereafter]
.)

Appendix A
Proofs of Morse theory basics

In this appendix, we shall give proofs for two lemmas left unproved
in Section
7
.

Proof of Lemma
7.10
.

Let
W
be the ground set of
\Delta
. Let
w\in W
be such that
\Delta
is a
cone with apex
w
. (Such a
w
exists by assumption.)

For each subset
A
of
W
that contains
w
, we set
A^{-}:=A\setminus\left\{w\right\}
. If
A\in\Delta
contains
w
, then
A^{-}\in\Delta

(since
A^{-}=A\setminus\left\{w\right\}\subseteq A
) and
w\notin A^{-}

(by definition).

For each subset
A
of
W
that does not contain
w
, we set
A^{+}:=A\cup\left\{w\right\}
. If
A\in\Delta
does not contain
w
, then

A^{+}\in\Delta
(since
\Delta
is a cone with apex
w
, so that
A\in\Delta

entails
A\cup\left\{w\right\}\in\Delta
) and
w\in A^{+}
(by definition).

Define a map
\mu:\Delta\rightarrow\Delta
by setting

\mu\left(A\right)=\begin{cases}A^{+},&\text{if }w\notin A;\\
A^{-},&\text{if }w\in A\end{cases}\qquad\text{for each }A\in\Delta.

This map
\mu
is well-defined (by the previous two paragraphs) and is an
involution (since each subset
A
of
W
satisfies
\left(A^{-}\right)^{+}=A
if it contains
w
, and
\left(A^{+}\right)^{-}=A
if it does
not). Moreover, each
A\in\Delta
satisfies either
\mu\left(A\right)\prec A
or
\mu\left(A\right)\succ A
(indeed, if
w\in A
, then
\mu\left(A\right)=A^{-}=A\setminus\left\{w\right\}\prec A
, whereas otherwise we
have
\mu\left(A\right)=A^{+}=A\cup\left\{w\right\}\succ A
). Thus,

\left(\Delta,\mu\right)
is a matching on
\Delta
. It has no unmatched
faces. Thus, by the definition of
u_{\mu}\left(x\right)
, we have

u_{\mu}\left(x\right)=\sum_{I\in\Delta\setminus\Delta}x^{\#I}=\left(\text{empty sum}\right)=0.

It remains to show that this matching
\left(\Delta,\mu\right)
is acyclic.

To show this, we let
\left(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}\right)
be a cycle of

\mu
. Thus,
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}
are distinct faces of
\Delta
,
satisfying
n\geq 2
and

F_{1}\succ\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\prec F_{2}\succ\mu\left(F_{2}\right)\prec F_{3}\succ\cdots\prec F_{n}\succ\mu\left(F_{n}\right)\prec F_{1}.

Our matching
\mu
has the property that the only faces
A\in\Delta
that
satisfy
A\succ\mu\left(A\right)
are the faces that contain
w
. Thus,
from
F_{1}\succ\mu\left(F_{1}\right)
, we conclude that
F_{1}
contains

w
. Similarly,
F_{2}
contains
w
. The definition of
\mu
yields

\mu\left(F_{1}\right)=F_{1}^{-}
(since
F_{1}
contains
w
), so that

\mu\left(F_{1}\right)
does not contain
w
.

However,
\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\prec F_{2}
, so that the set difference

F_{2}\setminus\mu\left(F_{1}\right)
has exactly
1
element. This
element must be
w
(since
F_{2}
contains
w
, but
\mu\left(F_{1}\right)
does not). Therefore,
F_{2}\setminus\mu\left(F_{1}\right)=\left\{w\right\}
. Since
\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\subseteq F_{2}
(because

\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\prec F_{2}
), this entails
F_{2}=\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\cup\left\{w\right\}
. On the other hand,
F_{1}=\mu\left(F_{1}\right)\cup\left\{w\right\}
(since
\mu\left(F_{1}\right)=F_{1}^{-}=F_{1}\setminus\left\{w\right\}
). Comparing these two
equalities, we obtain
F_{1}=F_{2}
. This contradicts the fact that the faces

F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}
are distinct.

Forget that we fixed
\left(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}\right)
. We thus have
found a contradiction for every cycle
\left(F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{n}\right)
of
\mu
. Hence, the matching
\mu
has no cycle, i.e., is
acyclic. This completes the proof of Lemma
7.10
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
7.11
.

For each subset
A
of
W
