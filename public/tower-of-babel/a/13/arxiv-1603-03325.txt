Global smooth solutions for the inviscid SQG equation
Angel Castro, Diego Córdoba, Javier Gómez-Serrano

Abstract
In this paper, we show the existence of the first non trivial family of classical global solutions of the inviscid surface quasi-geostrophic equation. Keywords: global existence, surface quasi-geostrophic, incompressible, computer-assisted



1
Introduction

We consider the initial value problem for the inviscid surface quasi-geostrophic equation (SQG):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta(x,t)+u(x,t)\cdot\nabla\theta(x,t)

\displaystyle=0,\ \ (x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}

(1.1)

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=(-R_{2}(\theta),R_{1}(\theta))(x,t)

\displaystyle\theta(x,0)

\displaystyle=\theta_{0}(x),

where
R_{j}
is the
j
-th Riesz transform:

\displaystyle R_{j}(\theta)(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{(x_{j}-y_{j})}{|x-y|^{3}}\theta(y)dy.

This equation is derived considering small Rossby and Ekman numbers and constant potential vorticity. It models the evolution of the temperature from a general quasi-geostrophic system for atmospheric and oceanic flows (see
[
15
,
33
,
49
,
44
]
for more details). The numerical and analytical study of the equation was started by Constantin, Majda and Tabak in
[
15
]
, since the SQG system presents an analogy with the 3D Euler equations.

The aim of this paper is to address the main problem of whether its classical solution corresponding to given initial data
\theta(x,0)=\theta_{0}(x)
with finite energy exists for all time or not. We remark that both the
L^{p}
norms of theta
(1\leq p\leq\infty)
and the
L^{2}
norm of
u
(the energy of the system) are conserved quantities. Moreover, the
L^{p}
norms of
u
obey the following bounds:

||u(\cdot,t)||_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p}||\theta_{0}||_{L^{p}}\quad 1<p<\infty.

Local existence of solutions for (
1.1
) was first shown in
[
15
]
in Sobolev spaces. By using different functional frameworks local existence has been also addressed in several papers, see for example
[
9
,
43
,
56
,
57
]
.

Resnick, in his thesis
[
50
]
, showed global existence of weak solutions in
L^{2}
using an extra cancellation due to the oddness of the Riesz transform. Marchand
[
46
]
extended Resnick’s result to the class of initial data belonging to
L^{p}
with
p>4/3
. The question of non-uniqueness for weak solutions in
L^{2}
is still a challenging open problem (see
[
37
,
1
,
52
]
and references therein).

The problem of whether the SQG system presents finite time singularities or there is global existence is open for the smooth case. Kiselev and Nazarov
[
39
]
constructed solutions that started arbitrarily small but grew arbitrarily big in finite time, and Friedlander and Shvydkoy
[
28
]
showed the existence of unstable eigenvalues of the spectrum. Castro and Córdoba constructed singular solutions with infinite energy in
[
5
]
and Dritschel
[
25
]
constructed global solutions that have
C^{1/2}
regularity.

The numerical simulations in
[
15
]
indicated a possible singularity in the form of a hyperbolic saddle closing in finite time. Ohkitani and Yamada
[
48
]
and Constantin et al
[
16
]
suggested that the growth was double exponential. The question was settled by Cordoba
[
17
]
who bounded the growth by a quadruple exponential and further improved by Cordoba and Fefferman
[
18
]
to a double exponential (see also
[
24
]
). The same scenario was recomputed almost 20 years with bigger computational power and improved algorithms by Constantin et al.
[
14
]
, yielding no evidence of blowup and the depletion of the hyperbolic saddle past the previously computed times. In
[
45
]
, Majda and Tabak compared simulations for the SQG scenario with the Euler case. Scott, in
[
53
]
, starting from elliptical configurations, proposed a candidate that develops filamentation and after a few cascades, blowup of
\nabla\theta
.

Several criteria of blowup have been found and blowup can only occur through the blowup of either some geometric quantities or certain space-time norms. For more details see
[
13
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
19
,
36
,
38
,
4
]
.

A different approach for the study of the formation of singularities for SQG comes from the patch-problem, i.e., “sharp fronts” . In this problem one considers that the scalar
\theta(x,t)
is the characteristic function of some compact and simple connected domain which depends on time and with smooth boundary. Local existence for the patch was proven by Rodrigo
[
51
]
for a
C^{\infty}
boundary and by Gancedo
[
29
]
in Sobolev spaces. Córdoba et al. found, in
[
21
]
, strong numerical evidences of the formation of a singularity in the boundary of the patch. For further numerical simulations addressing formation of singularities see
[
54
]
and
[
53
]
. The possibility of a splash singularity scenario (i.e. when the interface touches itself on a point but the curve does not lose regularity) was ruled out by Gancedo and Strain
[
30
]
.

Through a different motivation, Cordoba et al.
[
20
]
, Fefferman and Rodrigo
[
27
]
and Fefferman et al.
[
26
]
studied the existence of a special type of solutions that are known as “almost sharp fronts” for SQG. These solutions can be thought of as a regularization of a front, with a small strip around the front in which the solution changes (reasonably) from one value of the front to the other. These are strong solutions of the equation with large gradient (
\sim\text{(Width of the strip)}^{-1}
).

The main purpose of the paper is to show the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1

There is a nontrivial global smooth solution for the SQG equations that has finite energy.

It is well known that radial functions are stationary solutions for (
1.1
) due to the structure of the nonlinear term. The solutions that will be constructed in this paper are a smooth perturbation in a suitable direction of a specific radial function. The smooth profile we will perturb satisfies (in polar coordinates)

\displaystyle\theta(r)\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\text{for $0\leq r\leq 1-a$}\\
\text{smooth and decreasing}&\quad\text{for $1-a<r<1$}\\
0&\text{for $1\leq r<\infty$}\end{array}\right.,

where
a
is a small number (below we will impose some more constraints in this profile). In addition the dynamics of these solutions consist of global rotating level sets with constant angular velocity. These level sets are a perturbation of the circle. The limit case
a=0
gives rise to the well known V-state solution for SQG, i.e., a global rotating patch which solves weakly (
1.1
). The existence of V-states with
C^{\infty}
boundary for SQG was proven in
[
6
]
. It was shown in
[
7
]
that the boundary of these solutions is actually analytic.

The proofs of these results are motivated by the ones for 2D incompressible Euler in the simply connected case; Burbea in
[
3
]
proved the existence of V-states for Euler and
C^{\infty}
-regularity for its boundary was proved by Hmidi at al. in
[
34
]
(see also
[
32
]
).

The paper is organized as follows: section
2
is devoted to the reformulation of the equations (
1.1
) in new variables. In section
3
we state the main theorem and present the Crandall-Rabinowitz (C-R) theorem which will be the main tool in our proof. In section
4
we check that our equation satisfies the hypotheses of the C-R theorem. This will be the main part of our work.

In particular section
4.3
is different from previous analysis. We stress the following main differences:

•

The study of the linear problem is now reduced to a functional equation, as opposed to a scalar equation (which was in the patch case). Even the existence of nontrivial elements in the kernel of the linear part is not evident a priori.

•

There is no algebraic formula for neither the eigenvalue nor the eigenvector, not even in an implicit way (such as in
[
7
]
). This makes the proof of the dimensionality of the kernel much harder since one needs to show that the eigenvalue is simple and have some control of the rest of the eigenvalues.

Remark 1.2

In a forthcoming paper
[
8
]
, by using the same techniques, we are able to extend our construction to the 2D Incompressible Euler equations.

Finally, in Appendix
A
, we compute the asymptotics and bounds on the error terms of some of the elliptic integrals that appear and Appendix
B
is devoted to discuss the details of the computer-assisted code and its implementation, as well as to show the rigorous numerical bounds used in the theorems. Appendix
C

A major theme of our work is the interplay between rigorous computer calculations and traditional mathematics. We use interval arithmetics as part of a proof whenever they are needed.

Advances in computing power have made rigorous computer-assisted proofs realizable. Naturally, floating-point operations can result in numerical errors. In order to overcome these, we will employ interval arithmetics to deal with this issue. The main paradigm is the following: instead of working with arbitrary real numbers, we perform computations over intervals which have representable numbers by the computer as endpoints in order to guarantee that the true result at any point belongs to the interval by which is represented. On these objects, an arithmetic is defined in such a way that we are guaranteed that for every
x\in X,y\in Y

\displaystyle x\star y\in X\star Y,

for any operation
\star
. For example,

\displaystyle[\underline{x},\overline{x}]+[\underline{y},\overline{y}]

\displaystyle=[\underline{x}+\underline{y},\overline{x}+\overline{y}]

\displaystyle[\underline{x},\overline{x}]\times[\underline{y},\overline{y}]

\displaystyle=[\min\{\underline{x}\underline{y},\underline{x}\overline{y},\overline{x}\underline{y},\overline{x}\overline{y}\},\max\{\underline{x}\underline{y},\underline{x}\overline{y},\overline{x}\underline{y},\overline{x}\overline{y}\}].

We can also define the interval version of a function
f(X)
as an interval
I
that satisfies that for every
x\in X
,
f(x)\in I
. Rigorous computation of integrals has been theoretically developed since the seminal works of Moore and many others (see
[
2
,
22
,
40
,
41
,
47
,
55
]
for just a small sample). In our computations, all arithmetic will be double precision (64 bits).

2
The equations

In this section we describe the equation that a global rotating solution with constant angular velocity of SQG must satisfy. We will look to the level sets of this solution rather than the solution itself. Let’s assume that
\theta(x,t)
is a smooth solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
\theta_{0}(x)
. On
\theta_{0}(x)
we will assume that its level sets can be parameterized by
z_{0}(\alpha,\rho)
in such a way that

\theta_{0}(z_{0}(\alpha,\rho))=f(\rho)

for some smooth and even
function
f\,:\,\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. The application
z_{0}(\alpha,\rho)
satisfies:

1.

It is one to one from
\{(\alpha,\rho)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,-\pi\leq\alpha<\pi,\,0<\rho<\infty\}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

2.

For all
\alpha
,
z_{0}(\alpha,0)=(0,0)
.

3.

For fixed
\rho>0
,
z^{i}_{0}(\alpha,\rho)
, with
i=1,2
, are
2\pi
-periodic and
z_{0}(\alpha,\rho)
, with
-\pi\leq\alpha<\pi
, is a closed and
C^{1}
curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying the chord-arc condition. Also we parametrize this curve counterclockwise with
\alpha
.

4.

It is differentiable with respect to
\alpha
and
\rho
in
\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty)
,
|z_{0\,\rho}(\alpha,\rho)|>c>0
in
\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty)
,
|z_{0\,\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)|>0
in
\mathbb{T}\times(0,\infty)
and
z_{0\,\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)\cdot z_{0\,\rho}(\alpha,\rho)<0\quad\text{for $-\pi\leq\alpha<\pi$ and $0<\rho<\infty$}.

Because of the transport character of the equation (
1.1
) and by continuity we can assume that the level sets of the solution
\theta(x,t)
at time
t
can be parameterized by an application
z(\alpha,\rho,t)
such that

\displaystyle\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)=f(\rho),

(2.1)

and it satisfies properties 1, 3 and 4.

Property 2 changes to

z(\alpha,0,t)=c(t)\quad\text{for all $\alpha\in\mathbb{T}$},

where, here,
c(t)
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Differentiating (
2.1
) with respect to
\alpha
and with respect to
\rho
we have that

\displaystyle z_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho,t)\cdot\nabla\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)=0

\displaystyle z_{\rho}(\alpha,\rho,t)\cdot\nabla\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)=f_{\rho}(\rho).

Therefore

\displaystyle\nabla\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t))=\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho)}{z_{\alpha}^{\perp}\cdot z_{\rho}(\alpha,\rho,t)}z_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho,t),

(2.2)

Taking a time derivative in (
2.1
) and using (
1.1
) and (
2.2
) yields

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)=\partial_{t}\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)+z_{t}(\alpha,\rho,t)\cdot\nabla\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)+z_{t}(\alpha,\rho,t))\cdot\nabla\theta(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)+z_{t}(\alpha,\rho,t)\right)\cdot z_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho,t)\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho)}{z_{\alpha}^{\perp}\cdot z_{\rho}(\alpha,\rho,t)}.

This last expression is the equation that the level sets
z(\alpha,\rho,t)
of the solutions
\theta(x,t)
satisfy.

Notice that since
u(x,t)=R^{\perp}\theta(x,t)
we can also write
u(x,t)=-\Lambda^{-1}\nabla^{\perp}\theta(x,t)
and therefore

\displaystyle u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|z(\alpha,\rho,t)-y|}\nabla^{\perp}\theta(y,t)dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1}{|z(\alpha,\rho,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},t)|}f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime}.

where we just did the change of variables
y=z(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},t)
and used (
2.2
).

Conversely, if
z(\alpha,\rho,t)
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\left(-u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)+z_{t}(\alpha,\rho,t)\right)\cdot z_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho,t)\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho)}{z_{\alpha}^{\perp}\cdot z_{\rho}(\alpha,\rho,t)}=0

(2.3)

\displaystyle z(\alpha,\rho,0)=z_{0}(\alpha,\rho)

with

\displaystyle u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1}{|z(\alpha,\rho,t)-z(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},t)|}f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})z_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime},

(2.4)

we can prove that the function
\theta\,:\,\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by (
2.1
)
is a solution of the equation (
1.1
).

Let us assume now that
{\rm supp}\thinspace(f_{\rho})\subset[1-a,1]
, where
0<a<1
will be chosen later. Then, in order to find a solution for SQG, we can solve the equation

\displaystyle\left(z_{t}(\alpha,\rho,t)-u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)\right)\cdot z_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho,t)

\displaystyle=0,

with
u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)
given by (
2.4
), in the domain
\rho\in(1-a,1)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
. After that we extend the solution
z(\alpha,\rho,t)
to the domain
0\leq\rho<\infty
,
-\pi\leq\alpha<\pi
in a smooth way and finally define
\theta(x,t)
through equation (
2.1
).

In addition, we will assume that our solution rotates with angular velocity
\lambda
, counterclockwise. Hence

\displaystyle z(\alpha,\rho,t)=\mathcal{O}(t)x(\alpha,\rho),\quad\mathcal{O}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos(\lambda t)&-\sin(\lambda t)\\
\sin(\lambda t)&\cos(\lambda t)\end{array}\right)

This implies on one hand

\displaystyle z_{t}(\alpha,\rho,t)=\mathcal{O}_{t}(t)x(\alpha,\rho)=\lambda\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\sin(\lambda t)&-\cos(\lambda t)\\
\cos(\lambda t)&-\sin(\lambda t)\end{array}\right)x(\alpha,\rho),\quad z_{\alpha}(\rho,\alpha,t)=\mathcal{O}(t)x_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho),

and

\displaystyle z_{t}\cdot z_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho,t)=\langle\mathcal{O}_{t}(t)x(\alpha,\rho),(\mathcal{O}(t)x_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho))^{\perp}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{O}_{t}(t)x(\alpha,\rho),\mathcal{O}(t)x_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{O}^{T}\mathcal{O}_{t}(t)x(\alpha,\rho),x_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)\rangle=-\lambda\langle x(\alpha,\rho),x_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)\rangle.

On the other hand from (
2.4
) we see that

\displaystyle u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t)=-\mathcal{O}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{1}{|x(\alpha,\rho)-x(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})|}f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})x_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{O}\overline{u}(x(\alpha,\rho)).

Thus

\displaystyle\langle u(z(\alpha,\rho,t),t),z_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho,t)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{O}(t)\overline{u}(x(\alpha,\rho)),\mathcal{O}(t)x_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)\rangle=\langle\overline{u}(x(\alpha,\rho)),x_{\alpha}^{\perp}\rangle,

which yields having to solve, for the pair
(x(\alpha,\rho),\lambda)
,

\displaystyle-\lambda x(\alpha,\rho)\cdot x_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)+\frac{1}{2\pi}x_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})}{|x(\alpha,\rho)-x(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})|}x_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime}=0.

(2.5)

We now write
x(\alpha,\rho)
in polar coordinates

\displaystyle x(\alpha,\rho)

\displaystyle=r(\alpha,\rho)(\cos(\alpha),\sin(\alpha)).

(2.6)

This choice restricts the class of functions we are considering. However this restriction will not be strong enough and we will be able to find a solution.

We have the following relations:

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)

\displaystyle=r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)(\cos(\alpha),\sin(\alpha))+r(\alpha,\rho)(-\sin(\alpha),\cos(\alpha))=r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)n(\alpha)+r(\alpha,\rho)t(\alpha)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)

\displaystyle=r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)n^{\perp}(\alpha)+r(\alpha,\rho)t^{\perp}(\alpha)=r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)t(\alpha)-r(\alpha,\rho)n(\alpha)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\cdot x_{\alpha}^{\perp}(\alpha,\rho)

\displaystyle=r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)r_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})n(\alpha^{\prime})\cdot t(\alpha)-r_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})r(\alpha,\rho)n(\alpha)\cdot n(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle+r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)r(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})t(\alpha^{\prime})\cdot t(\alpha)-r(\alpha,\rho)r(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})n(\alpha)\cdot t(\alpha^{\prime}),

where

\displaystyle n(\alpha)\cdot n(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\cos(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle t(\alpha)\cdot t(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\cos(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle n(\alpha^{\prime})\cdot t(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\sin(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle n(\alpha)\cdot t(\alpha^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sin(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}).

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle x(\alpha,\rho)\cdot x_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)=r(\alpha,\rho)n(\alpha)\cdot(r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)n(\alpha)+r(\alpha,\rho)t(\alpha))=r(\alpha,\rho)r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho).

Therefore, equation (
2.5
) reads

\displaystyle F[r,\,\lambda]=0\quad\text{in $\rho\in(1-a,1)$, $\alpha\in\mathbb{T}$},

(2.7)

with

\displaystyle F[r,\,\lambda]

(2.8)

\displaystyle\equiv\lambda r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})}{|x(\alpha,\rho)-x(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})|}\cos(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})(r_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})-r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho))d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)}{2\pi r(\alpha,\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})}{|x(\alpha,\rho)-x(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})|}\cos(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})(r(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})-r(\alpha,\rho))d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi r(\alpha,\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})}{|x(\alpha,\rho)-x(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})|}\sin(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})(r(\alpha,\rho)r(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})+r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)r_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}))d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime},

where
x(\alpha,\rho)
is given by (
2.6
) and we have added and subtracted

\frac{r_{\alpha}(\alpha,\rho)}{2\pi r(\alpha,\rho)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{f_{\rho}(\rho^{\prime})}{|x(\alpha,\rho)-x(\alpha^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})|}\cos(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})r(\alpha,\rho)d\alpha^{\prime}d\rho^{\prime}

for cosmetic reasons.

The rest of the paper consists in finding a nontrivial solution
(r(\alpha,\rho),\lambda)
of (
2.7
) and with

3
Main theorem and Crandall-Rabinowitz (C-R) theorem

This section is devoted to state the main theorem of the paper. But firstly, we will fix the function
f(\rho)
. The derivative of this function will be given by the expression

\displaystyle\frac{a}{2}f_{\rho}\left(\rho\right)

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\left\{\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{1}{2\beta^{9}}(126\beta^{4}-420\beta^{3}(1+\tilde{\rho})+540\beta^{2}(1+\tilde{\rho})^{2}-315\beta(1+\tilde{\rho})^{3}+70(1+\tilde{\rho})^{4})(1+\tilde{\rho})^{5}&\text{ if }-1\leq\tilde{\rho}\leq-1+\beta\\

Theorem 3.4

(Crandall-Rabinowitz)

Let
X
,
Y

V

X

\displaystyle F\,:\,

\displaystyle V\times(-1,\,1)\rightarrow Y

\displaystyle\,\qquad(r,\,\mu)\,\,\,\,\rightarrow F[r,\,\mu]

\displaystyle\emph{$F[0,\,\mu]=0$forany$|\mu|<1$.}

(3.5)

Lemma 4.1

Let
\Omega\in[0,1]\times[-\pi,\pi]
and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\in H^{4,3}(\Omega)
. Then:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)}

Proof.

If

, then:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{4}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{3}\partial_{x}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{x}^{3}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}<C

On one hand,
f_{\alpha}\in H^{3}(\Omega)
, since

\displaystyle\|f_{\alpha}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}^{3}f_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{3}f_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{4}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}^{3}\partial_{\alpha}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\partial_{\alpha}^{4}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)}

This implies that
f_{\alpha}\in C^{1+\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})
, yielding
\|f_{\alpha\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C
and
\|f_{\alpha x}\|_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C
for some constant
C
.

On the other hand, if we define

\displaystyle g(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)dx,

we claim that

. In order to see this, we can compute

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])}^{2}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(\int\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)dx\right)^{2}d\alpha\leq\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)|^{2}dxd\alpha\leq\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)}.

In addition

\displaystyle\|g_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])}^{2}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(\int\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{\alpha}f(x,\alpha)dx\right)^{2}d\alpha\leq\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{\alpha}f(x,\alpha)|^{2}dxd\alpha\leq\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|g\|_{L^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi])}\leq\|g\|_{H^{1}([-\pi,\pi])}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)}.

We have that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)=\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x^{\prime},\alpha)dx^{\prime}+g(\alpha),

which we can bound in the following way:

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}^{2}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)-\partial_{x}^{2}f(x^{\prime},\alpha)dx^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

In addition:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,\alpha)-\partial_{x}^{2}f(x^{\prime},\alpha)dx^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x^{\prime}}^{x}\partial_{x}^{3}f(x^{\prime\prime},\alpha)dx^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}=h(\alpha,x)

We now fix
x
and we show that

is uniformly bounded. We achieve that by using the following estimate:

\displaystyle\|h(\alpha,x)\|_{L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x^{\prime}}^{x}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{x}^{3}f(x^{\prime\prime},\alpha)|^{2}d\alpha\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}dx^{\prime\prime}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|x-x^{\prime}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{x^{\prime}}^{x}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{x}^{3}f(x^{\prime\prime},\alpha)|^{2}\text{sign}(x-x^{\prime})d\alpha dx^{\prime\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|\partial_{x}^{3}f(x^{\prime\prime},\alpha)|^{2}d\alpha dx^{\prime\prime}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}dx^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)},

where

is independent of

. We can do the same procedure with

, getting

\displaystyle\|h(\alpha,x)\|_{L^{\infty}([-\pi,\pi])}\leq\|h(\alpha,x)\|_{H^{1}([-\pi,\pi])}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{4,3}(\Omega)}

Taking the supremum over

yields the desired result.

Lemma 4.11

Let

be defined as:

\displaystyle T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(mx)}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)^{2}-2\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)\cos(x)}}dx

Then, we have that, for every
(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}

(a)

(b)

T^{m+1}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)<T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)
.

Let
r=\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}
. We have that

\displaystyle T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(mx)}{(1+r^{2}-2r\cos(x))^{\frac{1}{2}}}dx

We do first the
r<1
case. The
r>1

\displaystyle T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)=\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)^{3}T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+r}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(mx)}{\left(1-\frac{4r}{(1+r)^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{2}{1+r}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(2mx)}{\left(1-\frac{4r}{(1+r)^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(x\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{4}{1+r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos(2mx)\left(\frac{4r}{(1+r)^{2}}\right)^{k}\cos^{2k}\left(x\right)\frac{1}{k!}\left(1/2\right)_{k}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{4}{1+r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{4r}{(1+r)^{2}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k!}\left(1/2\right)_{k}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos(2mx)\cos^{2k}\left(x\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{4}{1+r}\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\left(\frac{4r}{(1+r)^{2}}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k!}\left(1/2\right)_{k}\frac{\pi}{2^{2k+1}}\frac{\Gamma(2k+1)}{\Gamma(1+k+m)\Gamma(1+k-m)}

A_{k}>0
for every

r<1

\displaystyle T^{m}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)-T^{m+1}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{m}}{\Gamma(1+2m)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}A_{k}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+k+m)\Gamma(1+k-m)}-\frac{1}{\Gamma(2+k+m)\Gamma(k-m)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{m}}{\Gamma(1+2m)}+\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{k}}{\Gamma(1+k+m)\Gamma(1+k-m)}\left(\frac{1+2m}{1+k+m}\right)>0.

This completes the proof of the lemma.

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{||-\Theta^{3}_{A}B_{sj}+e||_{L^{2}}}{(c^{*}-\lambda_{0})\|B_{sj}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}\right)^{2}\left|\langle e,B_{sj}\rangle\right|\geq-\left(\frac{0.1534}{0.4409}\right)^{2}0.0101\geq-0.002

Adding all the numbers we get the desired positivity result. This finishes the proof.

∎

Appendix A
Asymptotics

Part of the computer-assisted proof involves having to compute the kernels
T^{1},T^{3}
and
T^{6}
, which are given by elliptic integrals. As far as we know, we are not aware of any rigorous implementation of them in any library. One possibility could be to leave the (singular) integrals as they are and integrate over a domain of one more dimension. This would be very time consuming in terms of the computer performance. Instead, we do the laborious work of deriving explicit approximations (to order 0) of the kernels by hand, with computable error bounds of order greater than 1. Once we do this, whenever we have to code either
T^{1},T^{3}
or
T^{6}
, we substitute it by the explicit expression found here.

We start with the elliptic integral

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(x)}{\sqrt{1+r^{2}-2r\cos(x)}}dx.

Taking
\displaystyle u=\frac{1-r}{1+r}
:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+r}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(x)dx}{\sqrt{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+u^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}}=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(2y)dy}{\sqrt{\sin^{2}(y)+u^{2}\cos^{2}(y)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1-2\sin^{2}(y)}{\sqrt{\sin^{2}(y)+u^{2}\cos^{2}(y)}}dy=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}z&=&\tan(y)\\
\frac{dz}{1+z^{2}}&=&dy\end{array}\right\}=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1+z^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{2}+u^{2}}}.

We remark that
u
will be close to zero. We need to derive the asymptotics in powers of
u
as
u\rightarrow 0
of

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1+z^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{2}+u^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{|u|}\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1+z^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{2}+u^{2}}}+\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{|u|}^{1}\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1+z^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{2}+u^{2}}}+\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1-z^{2}}{(1+z^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{2}+u^{2}}}

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

We start with
I_{1}
:

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-u^{2}w^{2}}{(1+u^{2}w^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}.

We expand
(1+u^{2}w^{2})^{-3/2}
as a power series around
w=0
:

\displaystyle(1+u^{2}w^{2})^{-3/2}=1-\frac{3}{2}u^{2}w^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{15}{4}(u^{2}w^{2})^{2}E^{1}_{2},

where
(1+u^{2})^{-7/2}\leq E^{1}_{2}\leq 1
. A naive integration and bounding then yields:

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-u^{2}w^{2}}{\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}dw-\frac{3}{2}u^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-u^{2}w^{2}}{\sqrt{1+w^{2}}}w^{2}dw+\frac{15}{8}u^{4}\tilde{E}^{1}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\arcsinh(1)+u^{2}\left(\frac{5}{4}(-\sqrt{2}+\arcsinh(1))\right)+u^{4}\left(-\frac{3}{16}\left(\sqrt{2}-3\arcsinh(1)\right)+\frac{15}{8}\tilde{E}^{1}_{2}\right)\right),

where

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1-u^{2}}{(1+u^{2})^{7/2}}\leq\tilde{E}^{1}_{2}\leq 1.

Next, we proceed with
I_{3}
. We can write it as:

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}-1}{(w^{2}+1)^{3/2}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1+w^{2}u^{2}}}.

Expanding in series
(1+w^{2}u^{2})^{-1/2}
:

\displaystyle(1+u^{2}w^{2})^{-1/2}=1-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}w^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{3}{4}(u^{2}w^{2})^{2}E^{2}_{2},

where
\frac{1}{(1+u^{2})^{5/2}}\leq E^{2}_{2}\leq 1
. A naive integration and bounding then yields:

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w^{2}-1}{(w^{2}+1)^{3/2}}dw-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}\frac{w^{2}-1}{(w^{2}+1)^{3/2}}dw+\frac{3}{8}u^{4}\tilde{E}^{2}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\arcsinh(1)-\sqrt{2}+\frac{1}{4}u^{2}\left(-3\sqrt{2}+5\arcsinh(1)\right)+\frac{3}{8}u^{4}\tilde{E}^{2}_{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle-1\leq\tilde{E}^{2}_{2}\leq 0.

Finally, we do the asymptotics for
I_{2}
. This is the most careful part. We start by writing

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{1}{2}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}\frac{1}{w}\frac{1-w}{(1+w)^{3/2}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1+\frac{u^{2}}{w}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{1}{2}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}\frac{1}{w}\frac{1}{(1+w)^{3/2}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1+\frac{u^{2}}{w}}}-\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{1}{2}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}\frac{1}{w}\frac{w}{(1+w)^{3/2}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1+\frac{u^{2}}{w}}}

\displaystyle=I_{21}+I_{22}.

Next, we expand the term
\left(1+\frac{u^{2}}{w}\right)^{-1/2}
, but we do not truncate. Hence

\displaystyle I_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1+r}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}{-1/2\choose k}\underbrace{u^{2k}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}\frac{1}{w^{1+k}}\frac{dw}{(1+w)^{3/2}}}_{F_{k}}\right).

Using the following integration by parts formula for
k>0
:

\displaystyle\int_{u^{2}}^{1}w^{-k}(1+w)^{-3/2}dw

\displaystyle=\left.(-2)(1+w)^{-1/2}w^{-k}\right|_{w=u^{2}}^{w=1}-2k\int_{u^{2}}^{1}w^{-k-1}(1+w)^{-1/2}dw

\displaystyle=\left.(-2)(1+w)^{-1/2}w^{-k}\right|_{w=u^{2}}^{w=1}-2k\int_{u^{2}}^{1}w^{-k-1}(1+w)^{-3/2}dw

\displaystyle-2k\int_{u^{2}}^{1}w^{-k}(1+w)^{-3/2}dw,

which implies:

\displaystyle\int_{u^{2}}^{1}w^{-k-1}(1+w)^{-3/2}dw

\displaystyle=\left.-\frac{1}{k}(1+w)^{-1/2}w^{-k}\right|_{w=u^{2}}^{w=1}-\frac{2k+1}{2k}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}w^{-k}(1+w)^{-3/2}dw,

yielding

\displaystyle F_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-\frac{u^{2k}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-u^{2}\left(\frac{2k+1}{2k}\right)F_{k-1}.

We now get back to
I_{21}
. We have to compute

\displaystyle I_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1+r}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}{-1/2\choose k}F_{k}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{1+r}\left(F_{0}+\sum_{k\geq 1}{-1/2\choose k}\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-\frac{u^{2k}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-u^{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}{-1/2\choose k}\left(\frac{2k+1}{2k}\right)F_{k-1}\right).

We calculate the following explicit numbers:

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-2\arcsinh(1)+2\arcsinh\left(\frac{1}{|u|}\right).

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}{-1/2\choose k}\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-\frac{u^{2k}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-2\arcsinh(1)+\log(4)}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}+\sqrt{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+u^{2}})\right).

We treat the rightmost sum as an error. Using the fact that the terms are alternating (since
\displaystyle{-1/2\choose k}
is alternating and the other factors are positive), and that, for
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle F_{k-1}=\frac{1}{u^{2}}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}\left(\frac{u^{2}}{w}\right)^{k}\frac{dw}{(1+w)^{3/2}}\geq\frac{1}{u^{2}}\int_{u^{2}}^{1}\left(\frac{u^{2}}{w}\right)^{k+1}\frac{dw}{(1+w)^{3/2}}=F_{k}\quad\forall\quad 0<u^{2}<1,

\displaystyle\left|{-1/2\choose k}\right|\frac{2k+1}{2k}=\frac{2(1+k)^{2}}{k(3+2k)}\left|{-1/2\choose k+1}\right|\frac{2(k+1)+1}{2(k+1)}\geq\left|{-1/2\choose k+1}\right|\frac{2(k+1)+1}{2(k+1)}

we can bound the absolute value of the sum by the absolute value of its first term, yielding:

\displaystyle\left|u^{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}{-1/2\choose k}\left(\frac{2k+1}{2k}\right)F_{k-1}\right|\leq\frac{3}{4}u^{2}|F_{0}|.

We move on to
I_{22}
. We can write it as:

\displaystyle I_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1+r}\left(-u^{2}F_{-1}-u^{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}{-1/2\choose k}F_{k-1}\right).

Together with the explicit calculation

\displaystyle-u^{2}F_{-1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}},

and the bound on the last term by the same reason as above:

\displaystyle\left|u^{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}{-1/2\choose k}F_{k-1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}u^{2}|F_{0}|

we can conclude, after gathering all the contributions, that
I
splits in the following way:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=I_{ho,ns}+I_{ho,s}+I_{e,s}+I_{e,ns},

where

\displaystyle I_{ho,ns}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\arcsinh(1)+u^{2}\left(\frac{5}{4}(-\sqrt{2}+\arcsinh(1))\right)+u^{4}\left(-\frac{3}{16}\left(\sqrt{2}-3\arcsinh(1)\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\arcsinh(1)-\sqrt{2}+\frac{1}{4}u^{2}\left(-3\sqrt{2}+5\arcsinh(1)\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{1+r}\left(\sqrt{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-2\arcsinh(1)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{1+r}\left(\frac{-2\arcsinh(1)+\log(4)}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}+\sqrt{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+u^{2}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{1+r}\left(\sqrt{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+u^{2}})\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}\left(-2+\left(-1+\sqrt{1+u^{2}}\right)\arcsinh(1)+\log(2)\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{1+r}\left(u^{2}\left(-2\sqrt{2}+\frac{5}{2}\arcsinh(1)\right)-\frac{3}{16}u^{4}(\sqrt{2}-3\arcsinh(1))\right)

\displaystyle I_{ho,s}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\arcsinh\left(\frac{1}{|u|}\right)

and

\displaystyle|I_{e,ns}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{15}{8}u^{4}+\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{3}{8}u^{4}+\frac{2}{1+r}\frac{5}{4}u^{2}\left|\sqrt{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-2\arcsinh(1)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{9}{4}u^{4}+\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{5}{8}u^{2}\left|\sqrt{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}-2\arcsinh(1)\right|

\displaystyle|I_{e,s}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{1+r}\frac{5}{4}u^{2}\arcsinh\left(\frac{1}{|u|}\right).

Note that in the computer implementation any error term
I_{e}
will be implemented as the interval
[-M,M]
whenever
|I_{e}|\leq M
.

We do the same for the other elliptic integral:

Taking
\displaystyle u=\frac{1-r}{1+r}
:

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+r}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\cos(3x)dx}{\sqrt{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+u^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}}=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\cos(6y)dy}{\sqrt{\sin^{2}(y)+u^{2}\cos^{2}(y)}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}z&=&\tan(y)\\
\frac{dz}{1+z^{2}}&=&dy\end{array}\right\}=\frac{4}{1+r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-15z^{2}+15z^{4}-z^{6}}{(1+z^{2})^{7/2}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{2}+u^{2}}}.

We repeat the splitting that we did for

Appendix B
Implementation of the computer-assisted part and rigorous numerical results

In this section we will discuss the technical details about the implementation of the different integrals that appear in the proofs. We remark that we are computing explicit (but complicated) functions over a one dimensional domain. In order to perform the rigorous computations we used the C-XSC library
[
35
]
. The code can be found in the supplementary material.

The implementation is split into several files, and many of the headers of the functions (such as the integration methods) contain pointers to functions (the integrands) so that they can be reused for an arbitrary number of integrals with minimal changes and easy and safe debugging. For the sake of clarity, and at the cost of numerical performance and duplicity in the code, we decided to treat many simple integrals instead of a single big one.

We will only integrate in
\rho^{\prime}
(or more specifically, in

In order to minimize the impact of

[-1,1]
.

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi\rho}\int_{1-a}^{1}f^{\prime}(\rho^{\prime})K^{1}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)d\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi\left(\frac{a}{2}(\tilde{\rho}-1)+1\right)}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{a}{2}f_{\rho}\left(\frac{a}{2}(\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}-1)+1\right)K^{1}\left(\frac{(\tilde{\rho}-1)+\frac{2}{a}}{(\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}-1)+\frac{2}{a}}\right)d\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}

We now explain how the integrals are calculated. By technical issues explained below, we will split the integration region
[-1,1]
into smaller pieces and sum the contributions over each piece. Regardless of the domain, the integration is done in an adaptive way unless specified. We keep track of the regions over which we need to integrate in a Standard Template Library

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx\in\frac{b-a}{2}\left(f\left(\frac{b-a}{2}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}+\frac{b+a}{2}\right)+f\left(-\frac{b-a}{2}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}+\frac{b+a}{2}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{4320}(b-a)^{5}f^{4}([a,b]).

where we have used that the operator is symmetric and the Generalized Young inequality. We computed the following bound:

and
[1-\beta,1]
for
