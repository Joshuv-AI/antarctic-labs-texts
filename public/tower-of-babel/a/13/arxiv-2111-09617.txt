Self-adjointness of the 2D Dirac operator with singular interactions supported on star-graphs Thanks: * Corresponding Author
Dale Frymark*, Vladimir Lotoreichik

Abstract
. We consider the two-dimensional Dirac operator with Lorentz-scalar δ \delta -shell interactions on each edge of a star-graph. An orthogonal decomposition is performed which shows such an operator is unitarily equivalent to an orthogonal sum of half-line Dirac operators with off-diagonal Coulomb potentials. This decomposition reduces the computation of the deficiency indices to determining the number of eigenvalues of a one-dimensional spin-orbit operator in the interval ( − 1 / 2 , 1 / 2 ) (-1/2,1/2) . If the number of edges of the star graph is two or three, these deficiency indices can then be analytically determined for a range of parameters. For higher numbers of edges, it is possible to numerically calculate the deficiency indices. Among others, examples are given where the strength of the Lorentz-scalar interactions directly change the deficiency indices while other parameters are all fixed and where the deficiency indices are ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) , neither of which have been observed in the literature to the best knowledge of the authors. For those Dirac operators which are not already self-adjoint and do not have 0 0 in the spectrum of the associated spin-orbit operator, the distinguished self-adjoint extension is also characterized.

1.
Introduction

Dirac operators with singular interactions were first studied in the 1980’s by Gesztesy and Šeba in one dimension and later by Dittrich, Exner and Šeba
[
18
,
22
]
in three dimensions. After that, the subject fell into relative obscurity for 25 years until a revival took place in 2014 due to a series of papers by Arrizabalaga, Mas and Vega
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Since then, Dirac operators with various types of singular interactions have garnered much interest and been studied in many different settings, see e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
25
]
. This renewed interest is due in part to their applications in physics to the confinement of quarks
[
26
]
and to the mathematical model of graphene
[
8
]
, and in part because they serve as an idealized model for the Dirac operator with a regular potential localized in the vicinity of a surface
[
12
,
28
]
.

In the present paper, we study the two-dimensional Dirac operator with a singular interaction supported on a star-graph with finitely many edges. This star-graph separates the Euclidean plane into finitely many sectors
with corners at the origin and thus can be viewed as a special corner domain.

Many results have been obtained in the past for polygons. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the number of non-convex corners of
the polygon
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
; angles with magnitude larger than
\pi
. For instance, Birman and Skvortsov
[
9
]
(see also
[
19
, Section 10]
and
[
31
]
) analyzed the Dirichlet Laplacian acting in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega)
as

H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\ni u\mapsto-\Delta u,

and found the deficiency indices to be
(n,n)
. A detailed analysis of this and related models in a slightly different terminology can be found in the monographs
[
15
,
23
,
24
]
.

A direct counterpart of this result for the two-dimensional Dirac operator on a (curvilinear) polygon with the infinite mass boundary condition is obtained by Le Treust and Ourmières-Bonafos in
[
27
]
and by Pizzichillo and Van Den Bosch in
[
30
]
; the deficiency indices again given by the number of non-convex corners. The manuscript
[
30
]
also obtains a generalization for Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interactions supported on the boundary of a polygon, with the deficiency indices given simply by the number of corners.

Behind all these considerations of Dirac operators on polygons there is an explicit analysis of the Dirac operator on an infinite sector combined with a localization technique. This model of an infinite sector, however, admits further generalizations. One important case is considered by Cassano and the second author
[
11
]
for infinite mass boundary conditions: where the orientation of the normal vector is opposite on the two edges of the sector. In that case, the normal vector enters the boundary condition and gives rise to a slightly different model where the deficiency indices are unequal.

To the best knowledge of the authors, no previous investigation of two-dimensional Dirac operators on sectors with singular interactions
have revealed deficiency indices higher than
(1,1)
. Moreover, there are no known cases where varying the singular interaction strength and fixing all geometric parameters results in the deficiency indices changing. It is therefore natural to wonder whether two-dimensional Dirac operators with singular interactions on star-graphs, a further generalization of the infinite sector model discussed above, also adhere to these trends – these lines of inquiry are the main motivations of the manuscript. The answers, determined in Subsections
3.2
and
3.4
, are negative; there are star-graphs where the deficiency indices of the respective Dirac operator vary with the strengths of the singular interactions and where they are
(2,2)
.

In order to discuss the self-adjointness of such Dirac operators and other results of the manuscript, it is first necessary to introduce some terminology. Recall that the
2\times 2
Hermitian
Pauli matrices

\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
, and
\sigma_{3}
are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\hskip 15.00002pt\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\hskip 5.0pt\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

These matrices satisfy the anti-commutation relation
\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=2\delta_{ij}
for
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta. It is convenient to define
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
so that for
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{1}\sigma_{1}+x_{2}\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{1}-ix_{2}\\
x_{1}+ix_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The first-order Dirac differential expression in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-i(\sigma\cdot\nabla)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i(\partial_{1}-i\partial_{2})\\
-i(\partial_{1}+i\partial_{2})&0\end{pmatrix}.

A mass term is not included in the expression as this is crucial only for spectral considerations which fall outside the scope of the current manuscript. Let
\Gamma=\cup_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma_{j}
be a star-graph, where
\{\Gamma_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are infinite rays emerging from the origin with normal vectors all oriented clockwise and enumerated counter-clockwise; thereby separating
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into
N
sectors. Let
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote the strengths of Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interactions on each respective edge of the star-graph
\Gamma
.
We consider the Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
, which is associated to the formal differential expression

(1.2)

-i(\sigma\cdot\nabla)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tau_{j}\sigma_{3}\delta_{\Gamma_{j}},

where
\delta_{\Gamma_{j}}
is the Dirac
\delta
-function supported on
\Gamma_{j}
.
The operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
is rigorously defined
in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})

acting via the differential expression
\mathcal{D}
on the domain consisting of two-component
H^{1}
-functions on each sector subject to special boundary conditions on each edge
\Gamma_{j}
corresponding to
\tau_{j}

[
6
,
12
]
(see Section
3
for details).
Throughout the paper we exclude the case of confinement and assume that
\tau_{j}\neq\pm 2
for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. It should be noted that formally equation (
1.2
) anticommutes with the antiunitary involution given by the composition of componentwise complex conjugation and multiplication by
\sigma_{1}
.

The operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
is shown to be closed, densely defined and symmetric, so it remains only to identify whether it has non-trivial deficiency indices or is self-adjoint. Our method of analysis relies on separation of variables in polar coordinates, with the angular component naturally giving rise to a one-dimensional spin-orbit operator
J_{N}
acting in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
with
N
special point interactions. This spin-orbit operator is shown to be self-adjoint by identifying it with a momentum-type operator on a certain graph
[
21
]
.

Spectral analysis of the spin-orbit operator
J_{N}
allows us to obtain a decomposition of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
into an orthogonal sum of half-line Dirac operators with off-diagonal Coloumb potentials, expressed via the eigenvalues of
J_{N}
. In particular, we find that the multiplicity of the eigenvalues of
J_{N}
is at most two; double eigenvalues should be treated with special care in the decomposition of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
. Further subtlety is required to handle a potential double eigenvalue at
0
.

Fortunately, the half-line Dirac operators arising in the decomposition of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
have appeared many times in the literature and with the help of, i.e.
[
13
]
, it is shown that the deficiency indices
(n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{N}),n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{N}))
of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are precisely equal to half of the number of the eigenvalues of
J_{N}
in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
, with multiplicities taken into account.
Thus, determining the deficiency indices of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
reduces to the spectral analysis of
J_{N}
. In the most general case it is only possible to prove an upper bound using perturbation theory:

(1.3)

n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})\leq N.

However, in many special cases it is possible to conduct an explicit analysis of the spectrum of
J_{N}
. The most notable being when
N=2
, i.e. the singular interactions are supported on a broken line, see Section
2
for full details. Several conditions are given in Proposition
2.12
that help classify when eigenvalues are simple or have multiplicity two.
If the interaction strengths are equal on each edge, so
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
, then
n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{2})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{2})=1
unless
\Gamma
is a straight line, where the respective Dirac operator is self-adjoint. Note this setup is essentially covered by the analysis in
[
30
]
. If the interaction strengths are opposite on each edge, so
\tau_{1}=-\tau_{2}
, we also obtain that
n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{2})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{2})=1
(even in the case
\Gamma
is a straight line). The additional degenerate case where the interaction supported on one of the rays has strength
0
, and therefore the geometric situation collapses to the consideration of an interaction supported on one ray, also has
n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{2})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{2})=1
. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that a Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interaction supported on a non-closed curve is analyzed.

Explicit analysis is also viable when
\Gamma
is a symmetric graph with
N=3
edges and
\tau=\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\tau_{3}\neq\pm 2
. Critically, it is observed in Corollary
3.13
that the respective Dirac operator transitions from being self-adjoint to having deficiency indices
(1,1)
when the absolute value of the interaction strength
\tau
exceeds
2\sqrt{3}
.

Eigenvalues of
J_{N}
for
N=2,3
were, in essence, established by taking the determinant of a
2N\times 2N
matrix and setting it equal to zero. The resulting equation involves the angles and singular interaction strengths of each edge, as well as the spectral parameter. For star-graphs with a higher number of edges, it is therefore not feasible to obtain and then extract spectral information from such a formula. Thankfully, it is possible to establish an alternative spectral condition, at least for symmetric star-graphs, thanks to the identification of
J_{N}
with a momentum operator on a certain graph. In particular, the number of eigenvalues of
J_{N}
in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
with multiplicities taken into account is found to be equal to the number of eigenvalues lying on the arc
\{z\in\mathbb{T}\colon 0<{\rm arg}(z)<2\pi/N\}
of the unit circle, with multiplicities, of an explicitly given unitary matrix. This method is applied to the symmetric star-graph with
N=6
edges and coupling constants
\tau_{1}=\tau_{3}=\tau_{4}=\tau_{6}=1
,
\tau_{2}=\tau_{5}=-1
in order to show that the respective Dirac operator possesses deficiency indices
(2,2)
. Further numerical tests using the alternative condition have not identified any examples with deficiency indices higher than
(1,1)
when
N\leq 5
, indicating that the bound in equation (
1.3
) can probably be improved. We also expect that by taking star-graphs with large number of edges and properly adjusted interaction strengths one can construct examples with arbitrarily large equal deficiency indices.

It is also possible to consider electrostatic
\delta
-shell interactions on each lead of the
N
star-graph by adding extra contributions to equation (
1.2
), as the associated Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
is still densely defined and symmetric. Indeed, such interactions are initially considered in Section
2
but the subsequent analysis immediately breaks down because the respective spin-orbit operator is not self-adjoint. Computation of the deficiency indices for such interactions remains an open question.

Finally, when the deficiency indices of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are non-trivial we characterize self-adjoint extensions of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
using the classical von Neumann extension theory. If
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
, then it is possible to identify the unique “distinguished” self-adjoint extension; the extension whose domain is contained in the Sobolev space
H^{1/2}
on each sector. In fact, the regularity of this extension is slightly better, in terms of the scale of Sobolev spaces, and can be computed in terms of the spectral gap for
J_{N}
. The special case
0\in\sigma(J_{N})
, on the other hand, does not have a self-adjoint extension whose domain in contained in
H^{1/2}
and there is no clear way to fix the distinguished self-adjoint extension.

Structure of the paper

Section
2
presents a detailed analysis of the case where the star-graph has
N=2
edges – where the star-graph is simply a broken line. This analysis provides both a base case for the more general star-graphs of Section
3
as well as many extra details that are too difficult to calculate in full generality. Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
determine the number of eigenvalues of the spin-orbit operator in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
when the strengths of the Lorentz-scalar interactions are equal or opposite on either edge. In Subsection
2.3
we perform the same analysis for the Lorentz-scalar interaction supported on a ray. Subsection
2.4
then details an orthogonal decomposition which shows that the Dirac operator is unitarily equivalent to half-line Dirac operators with off-diagonal Coulomb potentials which depend on the studied eigenvalues, see Theorem
2.14
.

Section
3
proves that the orthogonal decomposition of the Dirac operator with
N=2
edges from Subsection
2.4
holds in general for
N\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the proof that the spin-orbit operator is self-adjoint is extended and relies on an important identification with a momentum operator on a certain graph, see Proposition
3.2
. Subsection
3.1
gives an explicit formula for the eigenvalues of the spin-orbit operator when the star graph consists of
N=3
edges. Subsection
3.2
then uses this formula to analyze the special case where the three edges are equally spaced and the Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interactions on each lead have the same strength. In the special case where the sectors of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by the star-graph each have the same opening angle, Subsection
3.3
exploits the connection to momentum operators used in some of the previous proofs to reduce the problem of finding eigenvalues of the spin-orbit operator in an interval to finding eigenvalues of a unitary matrix on an arc of the unit circle. An example using this method is computed in Subsection
3.4
where
N=6
and the respective Dirac operator is shown to have deficiency indices
(2,2)
.

Dirac operators that are not already self-adjoint then have their self-adjoint extensions characterized by the classical von Neumann theory in Section
4
. When
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
, the unique distinguished self-adjoint extension is also constructed. Appendix
A
includes a technical lemma which is used in the proof of the orthogonal decomposition.

2.
Singular interaction supported on a broken line

We first consider the special case where the star-graph
\Gamma
has only two edges and therefore splits the plane into two sectors. Adhering to the nomenclature of existing literature, such a star-graph is viewed as a broken line. While a similar situation is considered in
[
30
]
, where the Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interaction is supported on the boundary of a curvilinear polygon, the analysis here differs significantly. In particular, we assume that the Lorentz-scalar interactions can be of different strengths on each edge and include a partial analysis of the case where both electrostatic and Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interactions are allowed. Also note that the notation of this section differs from that of Section
3
, as it is more suitable for this simple geometric configuration.

Define the wedge

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{(r\cos{\theta},r\sin{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon r>0,~{\theta}\in\mathbb{I}_{+}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\mathbb{I}_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(-\omega,\omega)
with
\omega\in(0,\pi)
. The wedge has an opening angle of
2\omega
and divides the plane into two domains; the complementary sector is then denoted by

\Omega_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{+}=\left\{(r\cos{\theta},r\sin{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon r>0,~{\theta}\in\mathbb{I}_{-}\right\},

where
\mathbb{I}_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\omega,2\pi-\omega)
.
A function (spinor)
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
can be decomposed into two parts corresponding to these domains

\displaystyle u=u_{+}\oplus u_{-}\in L^{2}(\Omega_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus L^{2}(\Omega_{-},\mathbb{C}^{2}),~~\text{ where }~~u_{\pm}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=u|_{\Omega_{\pm}}.

The two sides of the wedge can then be denoted by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{l}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{(r\cos\omega,r\sin\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon r>0\right\},~\text{and}

\displaystyle\Gamma^{r}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{(r\cos\omega,-r\sin\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon r>0\right\}.

The dependence on
\omega
is suppressed for the sake of notational accessibility and the choice
\omega=\pi/2
, of course, refers to the straight line. The Dirac operator of interest may possess two different types of singular interactions on each edge: electrostatic and Lorentz-scalar
\delta
-shell interactions. The strengths of these interactions will be interpreted by the real parameters
\eta_{l}
,
\eta_{r}
and
\tau_{l}
,
\tau_{r}
, respectively. Letting
\sigma_{0}
denote the identity matrix of rank two, such a Dirac operator corresponds to the formal differential expression:

(2.1)

\displaystyle-i(\sigma\cdot\nabla)+(\eta_{l}\sigma_{0}+\tau_{l}\sigma_{3})\delta_{\Gamma^{l}}+(\eta_{r}\sigma_{0}+\tau_{r}\sigma_{3})\delta_{\Gamma^{r}}.

The interactions can be translated into boundary conditions on each edge of the wedge, and were first rigorously described in
[
6
]
. Denote the normal vectors on
\Gamma^{l}
and
\Gamma^{r}
, which are chosen to be pointing outward from
\Omega_{+}
, as
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{l}=(\nu_{l,1},\nu_{l,2})=(-\sin\omega,\cos\omega)
and
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{r}=(\nu_{r,1},\nu_{r,2})=(-\sin\omega,-\cos\omega)
, respectively. Recall that the free massless Dirac differential expression is denoted by
\mathcal{D}
and given in equation (
1.1
). The operator associated to the formal differential expression (
2.1
) in the current context is rigorously given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}u

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\!(\mathcal{D}u_{+})\oplus(\mathcal{D}u_{-}),

\displaystyle\dom\mathfrak{D}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\!\Big\{u=u_{+}\oplus u_{-}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus H^{1}(\Omega_{-},\mathbb{C}^{2})\colon

\displaystyle-i(\sigma_{1}\nu_{j,1}+\sigma_{2}\nu_{j,2})\left(u_{+}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\!-\!u_{-}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\right)\!=\!\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{j}\sigma_{0}\!+\!\tau_{j}\sigma_{3})\left(u_{+}|_{\Gamma^{j}}+u_{-}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\right),j\!=\!l,r\Big\}.

The trace operator implicitly denoted by the “restriction” in equation (
2.2
) is bounded as an operator from
H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})
into
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})
via
[
29
, Theorem 3.38]
, the proof of which still applies in this case because the regions
\Omega_{\pm}
are Lipschitz hypographs.

While it is possible to consider the operator
\mathfrak{D}
for all real interaction strengths, we make the additional assumption that neither edge is in the case of confinement: when
\eta_{l}^{2}-\tau_{l}^{2}=-4
or
\eta_{r}^{2}-\tau_{r}^{2}=-4
. If both of these conditions are met, it is possible to decouple the operator into orthogonal sum of operators with respect to decomposition
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})=L^{2}(\Omega_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus L^{2}(\Omega_{-},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, see
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
. In other words, the boundary conditions can be rewritten as pertaining to each sector individually. Therefore, we prefer to work in the non-confining regime. The intermediate case of one confining edge and one non-confining requires an additional consideration that goes beyond the present paper.

The values
\eta_{l}^{2}-\tau_{l}^{2}
and
\eta_{r}^{2}-\tau_{r}^{2}
will consistently play a role in calculations, and so will be denoted by
\varepsilon_{l}
and
\varepsilon_{r}
, respectively. Furthermore, it is convenient to label

(2.3)

\displaystyle p_{l}=1+\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{l}\hskip 5.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.0ptm_{l}=1-\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{l},

with
p_{r}
and
m_{r}
being similarly defined.

It is also necessary to introduce the operator using standard polar coordinates. Let
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})
so that
x_{1}=r\cos{\theta}
and
x_{2}=r\sin{\theta}
and define

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm rad}}({\theta})=\frac{\partial\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}}{\partial r}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\theta}\\
\sin{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\hskip 5.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 5.0pt\overrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm ang}}({\theta})=\frac{\partial\overrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm rad}}}{\partial{\theta}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin{\theta}\\
\cos{\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

The Hilbert spaces
L^{2}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{I}_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2};rdrd{\theta})
can then be viewed as the tensor products
L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{I}_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, with
L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};rdr)
. The unitary transformation

\displaystyle V_{\pm}\mathchar 58\relax L^{2}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2}),\qquad(V_{\pm}u)(r,\theta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=u(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta),

is responsible for changing coordinate systems and yields the polar Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle H_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}^{1}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V_{\pm}(H^{1}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2}))=\left\{u\colon u,\partial_{r}u,r^{-1}(\partial_{{\theta}}u)\in L^{2}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{\pm},\mathbb{C}^{2})\right\}.

The Dirac operator acting on
L^{2}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus L^{2}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{-},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is therefore given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V\mathfrak{D}V^{-1},\qquad\dom\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V(\dom\mathfrak{D}),

where
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V_{+}\oplus V_{-}
.
The corresponding Dirac differential expression in polar coordinates is now

(2.4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}u=-i(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm rad}})\left(\partial_{r}u+\frac{u}{2r}-\frac{\left(-i\sigma_{3}\partial_{{\theta}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)u}{r}\right).

In analogy to equation (
2.2
), the Dirac operator of interest is thus

(2.5)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}u

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\!(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{+})\oplus(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{-}),

\displaystyle\dom\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\!\big\{u=u_{+}\oplus u_{-}\in H^{1}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus H^{1}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm pol}}(\Omega_{-},\mathbb{C}^{2})~\mathchar 58\relax~

\displaystyle-i(\sigma_{1}\nu_{j,1}+\sigma_{2}\nu_{j,2})\left(u_{+}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\!-\!u_{-}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{j}\sigma_{0}+\tau_{j}\sigma_{3})\left(u_{+}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\!+\!u_{-}|_{\Gamma^{j}}\right),j\!=\!l,r\big\}.

A more convenient form of the boundary conditions imposed on both
\dom\mathfrak{D}
and
\dom\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}
can be immediately obtained by using
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If the non-confining case where
\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{r}\neq-4
holds, then
u=u_{+}\oplus u_{-}\in H^{1}_{\rm pol}(\Omega_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\oplus H^{1}_{\rm pol}(\Omega_{-},\mathbb{C}^{2})

belongs to
\dom\mathfrak{D}
if and only if

(2.6)

\displaystyle u_{-}|_{\Gamma^{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{l}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{l}&-e^{-i\omega}(\eta_{l}-\tau_{l})\\
e^{i\omega}(\eta_{l}+\tau_{l})&m_{l}\end{pmatrix}u_{+}|_{\Gamma^{l}},

\displaystyle u_{-}|_{\Gamma^{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{r}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{r}&e^{i\omega}(\eta_{r}-\tau_{r})\\
-e^{-i\omega}(\eta_{r}+\tau_{r})&m_{r}\end{pmatrix}u_{+}|_{\Gamma^{r}}.

The matrices from the above lemma that give the relation between the boundary values will be denoted by
M_{l}
and
M_{r}
, respectively. These matrices do not possess many nice properties, like in the cases of infinite-mass boundary conditions or only Lorentz-scalar interactions, see e.g.
[
30
, Proposition 2.2]
. This is the effect of having both
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{3}
in the boundary conditions, which commute and anti-commute with
\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}
, respectively. However, some quick calculations show that

(2.7)

\displaystyle(M_{l})^{*}(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{l})(M_{l})

\displaystyle=(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{l}),

\displaystyle(M_{r})^{*}(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{r})(M_{r})

\displaystyle=(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{r}).

Proposition 2.2

The operator

Proof.

\displaystyle\langle\mathfrak{D}u,v\rangle-\langle u,\mathfrak{D}v\rangle=-i\left[\int_{\Gamma^{l}\cup\Gamma^{r}}((\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}})u_{+})\cdot\overline{v_{+}}ds-\int_{\Gamma^{l}\cup\Gamma^{r}}((\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}})u_{-})\cdot\overline{v_{-}}ds\right],

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma^{l}\cup\Gamma^{r}}((\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}})u_{-})\cdot\overline{v_{-}}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma^{l}}(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{l})(M_{l}u_{+})\cdot\overline{(M_{l}v_{+})}ds+\int_{\Gamma^{r}}(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\nu}}_{r})(M_{r}u_{+})\cdot\overline{(M_{r}v_{+})}ds

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that
\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{r}\neq\pm 4

\eta_{l}=\eta_{r}=0

Proof.

The operator

\displaystyle\psi_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm out}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\psi_{1}(-\omega),\psi_{2}(\omega),\psi_{3}(\omega),\psi_{4}(2\pi-\omega)),

\displaystyle\psi_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm in}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\psi_{1}(\omega),\psi_{2}(-\omega),\psi_{3}(2\pi-\omega),\psi_{4}(\omega)).

\displaystyle\left[\frac{(\eta_{j}\pm\tau_{j})}{m_{j}}\right]^{2}+\left[\frac{p_{j}}{m_{j}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=1,

(2.10)

\displaystyle(\eta_{j}\pm\tau_{j})+(\eta_{j}\mp\tau_{j})

\displaystyle=0.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}1&0&-\frac{m_{l}}{p_{l}}&-\frac{\tau_{l}e^{-i\omega(2{\lambda}+1)}}{p_{l}}\\

2.1.
Example: equal interaction strengths on each edge

The assumption that the interaction strengths are related via
\tau_{r}=a\tau_{l}
, for some

which offer further simplifications. In this subsection, we assume that the interactions on both edges are equal, i.e.
\tau_{l}=\tau_{r}=\tau
so that

, and try to determine how many eigenvalues of

lie in
(-1,0)
without considering the multiplicities. The analysis of multiplicities is subtle and therefore postponed to Subsection
2.4

This scenario is also discussed in
[
30

for
C^{2}
domains with a single straight-edged corner; see, in particular, Proposition 3.3 therein where a similar conclusion is reached.
Subsection
2.2
will discuss the case where
a=-1
. Recall that we tacitly assume
\eta_{l}=\eta_{r}=0
and
\tau\neq\pm 2
.

After some rearranging, equation (
2.13
) becomes

(2.14)

\displaystyle(4-\tau^{2})^{2}-(4+\tau^{2})^{2}\cos(2\pi\la)-16\tau^{2}\cos[2(\pi{\lambda}-2\omega{\lambda}-\omega)]=0.

The left-hand side of this equation can be viewed as function of
{\lambda}
with other parameters fixed, and its roots analyzed to determine how many eigenvalues lie in the interval
(-1,0)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Given the above assumptions, for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,0,2\}
and
\omega\in(0,\pi)
such that
\omega\neq\pi/2
there exists one
{\lambda}_{1}\in(-1,-1/2)
and one
{\lambda}_{2}\in(-1/2,0)
that satisfy equation (
2.14

{\lambda}=-1/2
does not satisfy (
2.14
). For
\omega=\pi/2
, there are no solutions of (
2.14
) in the interval
(-1,0)
.

Proof.

First, consider the case
\omega=\pi/2
. Equation (
2.14
) reduces to
\cos(2\pi\la)=1
and it is obvious that there are no solutions in the interval
(-1,0)
. Then, in light of Remark
2.5
, it is sufficient to show the result for
\omega\in(0,\pi/2)
.
Consider the function

F_{\tau,\omega}(\lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(4-\tau^{2})^{2}-(4+\tau^{2})^{2}\cos(2\pi\lambda)-16\tau^{2}\cos(2\pi\lambda-4\omega\lambda-2\omega),

and notice the condition (
2.14
) is equivalent to

. It is also easy to see that

F_{\tau,\omega}(-1-\lambda)=F_{\tau,\omega}(\lambda),

and hence
F_{\tau,\omega}(\lambda)
is symmetric with respect to the point

.
Furthermore,

F_{\tau,\omega}(-1)=F_{\tau,\omega}(0)=(4-\tau^{2})^{2}-(4+\tau^{2})^{2}-16\tau^{2}\cos(2\omega)=-16\tau^{2}(1+\cos(2\omega))<0.

This means the number of zeros of the function

on the interval

is odd. But symmetry and Proposition
3.9
imply that
F_{\tau,\omega}
has a maximum of two zeros in the interval
(-1/2,0)

F_{\tau,\omega}
has exactly one zero in
(-1/2,0)
.
∎

2.2.
Example: interaction strengths

.

Given the above assumptions, for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,0,2\}
and

Assume without loss of generality, by Remark

2.4.
Orthogonal decomposition for the broken line

In this subsection, we decompose the Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}
with Lorentz-scalar
\delta

. For equal interaction strengths on the edges and interaction strengths of opposite signs on the edges, as in Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
respectively, the deficiency indices are given explicitly. Computation of the deficiency indices crucially requires knowledge of how many eigenvalues of the operator
J
, counting multiplicities, lie in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
.

The normalized eigenfunctions for
J
form an orthonormal basis for the space

and Corollary
2.6
says that the spectrum of
J
is symmetric about the origin. Eigenspaces of
J
will be denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{{\lambda}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ker(J-\widetilde{{\lambda}}),

where
\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{{\lambda}}}=\{0\}
if
\widetilde{{\lambda}}\notin\sigma(J)

An orthogonal decomposition will require knowledge of how the subspace
\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{{\lambda}}}
behaves when the unitary operator
\mathcal{S}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})

\mathcal{S}\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm rad}})\varphi
is applied, due to the polar decomposition in equation (
2.5
). The statement of the following proposition is necessarily slightly stronger than other similar results in the literature only because the eigenfunctions are not explicitly determined. See, e.g.
[
30

where Lorentz-scalar interactions on
C^{2}
domains with finitely many corners are considered, for a comparison.

Proposition 2.11
.

The unitary operator
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{F}_{-\widetilde{{\lambda}}}

Set
{\lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widetilde{{\lambda}}-1/2

Let

\varphi({\theta})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}(c_{+,1}e^{i{\lambda}{\theta}},c_{+,2}e^{-i{\lambda}{\theta}})^{\top},\qquad{\theta}\in(-\omega,\omega),\\
(c_{-,1}e^{i{\lambda}{\theta}},c_{-,2}e^{-i{\lambda}{\theta}})^{\top},\qquad{\theta}\in(\omega,2\pi-\omega),\\
\end{cases}

with certain constants
c_{\pm,j}\in\mathbb{C}
,

Begin by defining

\displaystyle\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{S}{\varphi}=(\sigma\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathbf{e}}_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm rad}}){\varphi}.

In order to prove that
\mathcal{S}
maps
\mathcal{F}_{\widetilde{{\lambda}}}
onto
\mathcal{F}_{-\widetilde{{\lambda}}}
we start by showing
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{-\widetilde{{\lambda}}}

We can now consider a generalization of the former model to the singular interaction supported on a star-graph in

Proposition 3.2

Assume that

Proof.

The operator

2.3

2N

\psi_{j}

N

-\omega_{1},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N-1}

\omega_{0}=\omega_{N}=-\omega_{1}

\omega_{j-1}

\omega_{j}

\omega_{j}
to
\omega_{j-1}

\psi_{2k-1}

\psi_{2k}

{\varphi}_{k}
. The Hamiltonian acting on these vectors is thus

and the effect of
\sigma_{3}
in

\displaystyle\psi_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm out}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\psi_{1}(-\omega_{1}),\psi_{2}(\omega_{1}),\psi_{3}(\omega_{1}),\psi_{4}(\omega_{2}),\psi_{5}(\omega_{2}),\psi_{6}(\omega_{3}),\dots,\psi_{2N-1}(\omega_{N-1}),\psi_{2N}(2\pi-\omega_{1}))

\displaystyle\psi_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm in}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\psi_{1}(\omega_{1}),\psi_{2}(-\omega_{1}),\psi_{3}(\omega_{2}),\psi_{4}(\omega_{1}),\psi_{5}(\omega_{3}),\psi_{6}(\omega_{2}),\dots,\psi_{2N-1}(2\pi-\omega_{1}),\psi_{2N}(\omega_{N-1})),

which list the terminals and origins of the vectors
\psi_{j}

is identical for the angles
-\omega_{1}

-\omega_{1}
and

-\omega_{1}

Self-adjoint realizations of this Hamiltonian are in one-to-one correspondence with
2N\times 2N
unitary matrices

which realize

here. Hence, omitting the dependence on

\displaystyle\psi_{2j-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\omega_{j-1}}\tau_{j}}{m_{j}}\psi_{2j-3}+\frac{p_{j}}{m_{j}}\psi_{2j}

\displaystyle\psi_{2j-1}

(3.4)

\displaystyle({\mathcal{U}}_{N})_{(2j-2,2j-3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\omega_{j-1}}\tau_{j}}{m_{j}},\qquad

\displaystyle({\mathcal{U}}_{N})_{(2j-1,2j-3)}

\displaystyle({\mathcal{U}}_{N})_{(2j-1,2j)}

W_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}

to the half-line Dirac operator
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, while the operator
d_{0}
is unitarily equivalent via
W_{0}
to
\boldsymbol{d}_{0}
.
In particular, the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{N}\simeq\begin{cases}\bigoplus_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\infty)}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}},&0\notin\sigma(J_{N}),\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{0}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\infty)}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\right),&0\in\sigma(J_{N}),\end{cases}

holds, the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
is closed and its deficiency indices can be computed as

n_{\pm}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=\begin{cases}\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\infty)}n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\cdot n_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}),&0\notin\sigma(J_{N}),\\
n_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{d}_{0})+\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\infty)}n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\cdot n_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}),&0\in\sigma(J_{N}).\end{cases}

The deficiency indices of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
can now be characterized by arguing analogously to Proposition
2.15
.

Proposition 3.8
.

The deficiency indices
n_{\pm}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})
of the Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are both equal to half of the number of eigenvalues of the spin-orbit operator
J_{N}
lying in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
with multiplicities taken into account.

In the most general case, the deficiency indices of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
can be estimated from above by using perturbation theory.

Proposition 3.9
.

For any vector of angles
\{\omega_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}
satisfying the above assumptions and for
\tau_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,2\}
for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, the operator
J_{N}
has at most
2N
eigenvalues in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
, with multiplicities taken into account. In particular, the deficiency indices of the Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
satisfy
n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})\leq N
.

Proof.

Consider the symmetric operator

A\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-i\sigma_{3}\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{\varphi}{2},\qquad\dom A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{I}_{j},\mathbb{C}^{2}),

which is closed and densely defined in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
. The operator
H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{I}_{j})\ni\varphi\mapsto-i\varphi^{\prime}
acting in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{I}_{j})
has deficiency indices
(1,1)
, so the deficiency indices of the operator
A
can be identified as
(2N,2N)
.

Consider the self-adjoint extension of the symmetric operator
A
given by

B\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-i\sigma_{3}\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{\varphi}{2},\qquad\dom B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2}).

The spectrum of
B
can be explicitly computed as
\sigma(B)=\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
and, in particular,
\sigma(B)\cap(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})=\varnothing
. The spin-orbit operator
J_{N}
is also a self-adjoint extension of
A
though, so we conclude from
[
33
, Satz 10.18]
that
the number of eigenvalues of
J_{N}
in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
, with multiplicities taken into account, is at most
2N
. Finally, the upper bound on the deficiency indices for
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
follows by applying Proposition
3.8
.
∎

3.1.
Example: star-graphs with three edges

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}1&0&0&0&-\frac{m_{1}e^{i2\pi{\lambda}}}{p_{1}}&\frac{\tau_{1}e^{i[\omega(2{\lambda}+1)-2\pi{\lambda}]}}{p_{1}}\\

\displaystyle(4+\tau^{2})^{3}\cos(2\pi\la)-(4-\tau^{2})^{3}=48(4+\tau^{2})\tau^{2}\cos\left[\frac{\pi}{3}(2{\lambda}+1)\right].

Proposition 3.11

Let the above assumptions hold and

Proof.

Analysis of equation (

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\lambda}=\begin{cases}3k+1/2\\

Proposition 3.12

Let the above assumptions hold and

The proof is analogous to that of Proposition

\displaystyle f(\tau,{\lambda})

3.3.
Alternative spectral condition

As shown in Proposition
3.8
, computing the deficiency indices of the Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
corresponding to the star-graph with
N
leads boils down to determining how many eigenvalues of the operator
J_{N}
, counting multiplicities, are in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
. Instead of determining a system of equations that yields conditions on generic eigenfunctions of
J_{N}
and finding when this system has a non-trivial solution, as elsewhere in the manuscript, we can further analyze the momentum operator
J_{N}-1/2

on the graph discussed in Proposition
3.2
. Recall that the respective graph has
2N
directed edges and
N
vertices (given by
\omega_{j-1}
for
j=1,\dots,N
) and
J_{N}-1/2
operates as the differential expression
-i\frac{d}{dx}
on each edge. Each vertex has two incoming and two outgoing edges, one to each of the neighboring vertices. See the proof of Proposition
3.2
for more details on this setup.

The key object from this construction is the unitary matrix
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
which realizes
the boundary condition
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}\psi_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm in}}=\psi_{\scriptstyle\text{\rm out}}
and whose entries are given explicitly in equation (
3.4
). In the special case where the angles
\omega_{j}
are chosen so that
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is split into equal sectors, the spectral analysis of
J_{N}-1/2
can be reduced to finding the eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
. In this symmetric case, the operator
J_{N}-1/2
can be identified with the following operator acting in the Hilbert space
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2N}L^{2}(0,2\pi/N)
via

(3.16)

\left\{\psi=\oplus_{j=1}^{2N}\psi_{j}\in\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2N}H^{1}(0,2\pi/N)\colon{\mathcal{U}}_{N}\psi(2\pi/N)=\psi(0)\right\}\mapsto\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2N}(-i\psi_{j}^{\prime}).

This interpretation is inspired by
[
21
]
, and the proof of the following Proposition is a slight generalization of the first step of the proof
[
21
, Theorem 5.4]
, as it takes multiplicities into account.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\omega_{j}-\omega_{j-1}=2\pi/N
for all
j=1,\dots,N
. Then
{\lambda}
is an eigenvalue of
J_{N}-1/2
if and only if
e^{-2i\pi{\lambda}/N}
is an eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}

and the multiplicities of these eigenvalues coincide.

Proof.

We claim that the mapping

\mathcal{R}\colon\mathbb{C}^{2N}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{n=1}^{2N}L^{2}(0,2\pi/N),\qquad\mathcal{R}{\bf c}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2N}c_{j}e^{i{\lambda}{\theta}},\qquad{\bf c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{2N})^{\top}.

is a bijection between
\ker({\mathcal{U}}_{N}-e^{-2i\pi{\lambda}/N})
and
\ker(J_{N}-1/2-{\lambda})
, where the operator
J_{N}-1/2
is interpreted as in (
3.16
).

Let
{\bf c}\in\ker({\mathcal{U}}_{N}-e^{-2i\pi{\lambda}/N})
. Then,
-i(\mathcal{R}{\bf c})^{\prime}={\lambda}\mathcal{R}{\bf c}
and the boundary condition

{\mathcal{U}}_{N}((\mathcal{R}{\bf c})(2\pi/N))=e^{i2\pi{\lambda}/N}{\mathcal{U}}_{N}((\mathcal{R}{\bf c})(0))=\mathcal{R}{\bf c}(0)

holds. Hence, we conclude that
\mathcal{R}{\bf c}\in\ker(J_{N}-1/2-{\lambda})
. Since
\ker\mathcal{R}=\{0\}
, the mapping
\mathcal{R}
is injective and it only remains to check that
\mathcal{R}
maps
\ker({\mathcal{U}}_{N}-e^{-2i\pi{\lambda}/N})
onto
\ker(J_{N}-1/2-{\lambda})
. Let
\psi\in\ker(J_{N}-1/2-{\lambda})
be arbitrary. Then,
\psi(0)=e^{-i2\pi{\lambda}/N}\psi(2\pi{\lambda}/N)
and the boundary condition
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}\psi(0)=\psi(2\pi{\lambda}/N)
imply that
\psi(0)\in\ker({\mathcal{U}}_{N}-e^{-2i\pi{\lambda}/N})
. Finally, this means that
\mathcal{R}(\psi(0))=\psi
and
\mathcal{R}
is surjective.
∎

Proposition
3.14
says that when the star-graph is symmetric instead of searching for those
{\lambda}\in(-1,0)
that are eigenvalues of
J_{N}-1/2
, we can search for eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
that fall on the arc
\mathcal{A}_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{z\in\mathbb{T}\colon 0<{\rm arg}\,(z)<2\pi/N\}
of the unit circle. The deficiency indices of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are then equal to half of the number of eigenvalues of the matrix
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
on the arc
\mathcal{A}_{N}
with multiplicities taken into account.
In practice, the spectrum of
{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
is much easier to determine than by using the general methods of Section
3
and allows for more sophisticated examples to be analyzed.

Thanks to the alternative spectral condition given in Proposition

Let

into

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=\tau_{3}=\tau_{4}=\tau_{6}=1\text{ and }\tau_{2}=\tau_{5}=-1

and

It can be inferred that alternating the interaction strengths on each edge between positive and negative values may be the crucial element which gives rise to these multiple eigenvalues and increased deficiency indices. After all, the explicit analyses for

4.
Characterization of self-adjoint extensions

The Dirac operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
presented in Section
3
has equal deficiency indices by Proposition
3.8
, so it possesses a family of self-adjoint extensions. These extensions can be parametrized with the help of the orthogonal decomposition from Theorem
3.7
and classical von Neumann extension theory; see e.g.
[
32
, §13.2]
and
[
20
, §6.1]
. In applications, it is important to fix a specific self-adjoint extension to work with and there are many ways of doing this depending on the context. One common way to choose such an extension from the parametrized family is to find the most regular operator domain, in terms of the scale of Sobolev spaces. In the literature, such an extension is usually referred to as the ‘distinguished’ self-adjoint extension. Under the assumption
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
, it is possible for us to identify a unique distinguished self-adjoint extension by requiring that its operator domain is contained in the Sobolev space
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}H^{1/2}(\Omega_{j},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.

The first step to achieving a parametrization of all self-adjoint extensions is to explicitly find defect elements for the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
. Theorem
3.7
shows that deficiency indices of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
come solely from the operators
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
in its orthogonal decomposition related to the eigenvalues
\widetilde{\lambda}
of
J_{N}
lying in the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
. All other operators in the orthogonal decomposition of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are self-adjoint.

The next lemma characterizes the defect subspaces of
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
for
\widetilde{\lambda}\in[0,1/2)
. This analysis is partially carried out in
[
13
, Equation (1.25)]
and
[
27

but we provide some details here for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let the symmetric operator
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
for
\widetilde{\lambda}\in[0,1/2)
, acting in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
be as in equation (
2.21
).
Then the defect subspaces are characterized by
\Ker(\boldsymbol{d}^{*}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\pm i)={\rm span}\,\{f_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{\pm}\}
where

\displaystyle f_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}r^{1/2}K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{{\lambda}}-1/2}}(r)\\
-ir^{1/2}K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{{\lambda}}+1/2}}(r)\end{pmatrix},\hskip 20.00003ptf_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{pmatrix}r^{1/2}K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{{\lambda}}-1/2}}(r)\\
ir^{1/2}K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{{\lambda}}+1/2}}(r)\end{pmatrix},

and
K_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind and order
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We will first characterize
\ker(\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{*}+i)
. Simplification gives

(4.1)

\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\Ker(\boldsymbol{d}^{*}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}+i)\quad\iff\quad\begin{cases}if_{1}+\left(-\dfrac{d}{dr}-\dfrac{\widetilde{{\lambda}}}{r}\right)f_{2}=0,\\
\left(\dfrac{d}{dr}-\dfrac{\widetilde{{\lambda}}}{r}\right)f_{1}+if_{2}=0.\end{cases}

Solving the second equation for
f_{2}
and substituting back into the first equation yields the differential equation

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime\prime}+\left(-1-\dfrac{\widetilde{{\lambda}}^{2}-\widetilde{{\lambda}}}{r^{2}}\right)f_{1}=0.

The square-integrable solution is given up to multiplication by a constant by
[
14
, Equation 10.13.1]
and rewritten by
[
14
, Equation 10.27.8]
as

\displaystyle f_{1}=r^{1/2}K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{{\lambda}}-1/2}}(r).

Plugging this back into the system of differential equations in (
4.1
) and using
[
14
, Equation 10.29.2]
yields

\displaystyle f_{2}=-ir^{1/2}K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{{\lambda}}+1/2}}(r).

Characterization of
\ker(\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{*}-i)
proceeds similarly, with the same differential equation providing for
f_{1}
, but with an additional factor of
-1
present in
f_{2}
.
∎

Lemma
4.1
immediately allows us to describe all possible self-adjoint extensions of the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
. To this end, let
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{\pm}=\ker(\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{*}\mp i)
denote the positive and negative defect spaces of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
. Also recall that
{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}
,
1\leq j\leq n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}

is the orthonormal basis of
\ker(J_{N}-\widetilde{\lambda})
for
\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\infty)
of multiplicity
n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\in\{1,2\}
. The orthonormal basis of
\ker J_{N}
is
\{{\varphi}_{0}^{1},{\varphi}_{0}^{2}\}
and is constructed as in Remark
3.5
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let the angles
\{\omega_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}
and the interaction strengths
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be chosen as in Section
3
so that the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
has deficiency indices
n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=n\geq 1
. All self-adjoint extensions of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are in one-to-one correspondence with unitary
n\times n
matrices
{\mathcal{U}}=\{u_{m,k}\}_{k,m=1}^{n,n}
.
If
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
, the self-adjoint extension
\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{\mathcal{U}}
of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
corresponding to the matrix
{\mathcal{U}}
is characterized by

\displaystyle\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{{\mathcal{U}}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{w=u+v_{+}+v_{-}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})\colon u\in\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N},

\displaystyle v_{+}=\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}c_{k(\widetilde{\lambda})+j}\big[K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})+K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\big]

\displaystyle v_{-}=\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}({\mathcal{U}}{\bf c})_{k(\widetilde{\lambda})+j}\big[K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})-K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\big]\bigg\}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{U}}_{N}w

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{{\mathcal{U}}}(u+v_{+}+v_{-})=\mathfrak{D}_{N}u+iv_{+}-iv_{-},

where
{\bf c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is an arbitrary vector with complex entries
and
k(\widetilde{\lambda})
is the number of eigenvalues of
J_{N}
in the interval
(0,\widetilde{\lambda})
counted with multiplicities.
If
0\in\sigma(J_{N})
, the self-adjoint extension
\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{\mathcal{U}}
of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
corresponding to the matrix
{\mathcal{U}}
is characterized by

\displaystyle\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{{\mathcal{U}}}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{w=u+v_{+}+v_{-}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})\colon u\in\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N},

\displaystyle v_{+}=c_{1}\big[K_{1/2}(r){\varphi}_{0}^{1}({\theta})+K_{1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{0}^{1}({\theta})\big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}c_{k(\widetilde{\lambda})+j+1}\big[K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})+K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\big]

\displaystyle v_{-}=({\mathcal{U}}{\bf c})_{1}\big[K_{1/2}(r){\varphi}_{0}^{1}({\theta})+K_{1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{0}^{1}({\theta})\big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}({\mathcal{U}}{\bf c})_{k(\widetilde{\lambda})+j+1}\big[K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})-K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\big]\bigg\}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{U}}_{N}w

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{{\mathcal{U}}}(u+v_{+}+v_{-})=\mathfrak{D}_{N}u+iv_{+}-iv_{-},

where the vector
{\bf c}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

and the function
k(\widetilde{\lambda})
are as before.

Proof.

Assume
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
. The case
0\in\sigma(J_{N})
will make use of the identity
K_{\nu}(z)=K_{-\nu}(z)
, but can otherwise be treated analogously. The defect elements of
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
for
\widetilde{\lambda}\in[0,1/2)
, stated in Lemma
4.1
, can be mapped via the inverses of the unitary operators in (
3.8
) that yield the orthogonal decomposition:

\displaystyle[(W_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j})^{-1}f^{+}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}](r,{\theta})

\displaystyle=K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}}(r){\varphi}^{j}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}({\theta})+K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}^{j}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}({\theta})\in\mathcal{D}_{N}^{+},

\displaystyle[(W_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j})^{-1}f^{-}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}](r,{\theta})

\displaystyle=K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}}(r){\varphi}^{j}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}({\theta})-K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}^{j}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}({\theta})\in\mathcal{D}_{N}^{-}.

These pre-images form orthogonal bases for
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{+}
and
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{-}
.
The matrix
{\mathcal{U}}
defines a unitary mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N}^{+}

\displaystyle\ni\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}c_{k(\widetilde{\lambda})+j}\big[K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})+K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\big]

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}({\mathcal{U}}{\bf c})_{k(\widetilde{\lambda})+j}\big[K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})-K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)\mathcal{S}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\big]\in\mathcal{D}_{N}^{-}.

All self-adjoint extensions of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
are thus in one-to-one correspondence with unitary
n\times n
matrices
{\mathcal{U}}
by the von Neumann decomposition, see e.g.
[
20
, Equation (6.1.7)]
and also
[
32
, Theorem 13.10]
. This decomposition also yields the characterization of the action and operator domain for
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{U}}_{N}
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

As in
[
27

, the functions
K_{\nu}(r)
,
\nu\in(0,1)
,
in the characterization of the operator domain of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{\mathcal{U}}
can be replaced by
r^{-\nu}\chi(r)
where
\chi\colon{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a
C^{\infty}
-smooth function that equals
1
in a neighbourhood of

0

The decomposition of general functions in the domain
\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{\mathcal{U}}
given in Theorem
4.2
provides a tool to classify the regularity of functions in the domain. When
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
, it is possible to single out a unique distinguished self-adjoint extension whose operator domain is the most regular in the scale of Sobolev spaces. A similar statement is made for Dirac operators with infinite mass boundary conditions in sectors in
[
27
, Theorem 1.2(ii)]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let the angles
\{\omega_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}
and the interaction strengths
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be chosen as in Section
3
so that the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
has deficiency indices
n_{+}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=n_{-}(\mathfrak{D}_{N})=n\geq 1
and
0\notin\sigma(J_{N})
. The so called ‘distinguished’ self-adjoint extension of
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
, the only extension such that

\displaystyle\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{{\mathcal{U}}}\subset\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}H^{1/2}(\Omega_{j},\mathbb{C}^{2}),

is the extension corresponding to
{\mathcal{U}}=\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the
n\times n
identity matrix. In fact, the following slightly stronger statement holds

\displaystyle\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{\mathcal{I}_{n}}\subset\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}H^{s}(\Omega_{j},\mathbb{C}^{2}),

for all
s<1/2+\min\{\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,\infty)\}
.

Before beginning the proof, we recall a few facts about modified Bessel functions, which will play a key role.
In particular, the Bessel function
K_{\nu}(z)
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
,
K_{\nu}(z)=K_{-\nu}(z)
(see
[
14
, Equation (10.27.3)]
) and let
\Gamma(\cdot)
denote Euler’s Gamma function. Then, the following asymptotic behaviour holds
for all
\nu>0

(4.2)

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(z)\sim_{z\to 0^{+}}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu)}{2}\left(\dfrac{z}{2}\right)^{-\nu}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{\nu}(z)\sim_{z\to\infty}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2z}\right)^{1/2}e^{-z},

by
[
14
, Equation (10.30.2) and (10.30.3)]
and
[
14
, Equation (10.25.3)]
, respectively.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
\widetilde{\lambda}\in\sigma(J_{N})\cap(0,1/2)
.
We claim that

(4.3)

K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\notin H^{1/2}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2}),\qquad k\in\{1,2,\dots,N\},

where, with a slight abuse of notation,
{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}
is understood to be its restriction to the interval
\mathbb{I}_{k}
.
The space
H^{1/2}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is embedded into
L^{4}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
by
[
16
, Corollary 4.53]
. However, the asymptotics of
K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}(r)
as
r\rightarrow 0^{+}
for
\nu=\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2
imply that
K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}\notin L^{4}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
and hence
K_{\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}\notin H^{1/2}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, proving the claim.

Furthermore, we claim

(4.4)

K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})\in H^{s}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2}),\qquad k\in\{1,2,\dots,N\},~\text{for all}\,s<1/2+\widetilde{\lambda}.

The Sobolev space
H^{1}_{\frac{2}{2-s}}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})=\{u\colon u,|\nabla u|\in L^{\frac{2}{2-s}}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})\}

is embedded into
H^{s}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
though (see e.g.
[
23
, Equation (1.4.4.5)]
), so it remains to verify that
K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r){\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}\in H^{1}_{\frac{2}{2-s}}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
The asymptotics of
K_{\nu}
in (
4.2
) yields that

K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}\in L^{\frac{2}{2-s}}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
Combining the representation

\big|\nabla(K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j})\big|^{2}(r,{\theta})=|K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}^{\prime}(r)|^{2}|{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j}({\theta})|^{2}+\frac{K^{2}_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r)}{r^{2}}|({\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j})^{\prime}({\theta})|^{2},

with the identity (cf.
[
14
, Equation (10.29.2)]
)

K^{\prime}_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r)=-K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-3/2}(r)-\frac{(\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2)K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}(r)}{r},

and the asymptotics in (
4.2
), we see that

|\nabla(K_{\widetilde{\lambda}-1/2}{\varphi}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}^{j})|\in L^{\frac{2}{2-s}}(\Omega_{k},\mathbb{C}^{2})
. The claim in equation (
4.4
) follows.

The decomposition from Theorem
4.2
then says that the theorem holds if and only if the functions in
\dom\mathfrak{D}_{N}^{{\mathcal{U}}}
have no terms with the factor
K_{{}_{\scriptstyle\widetilde{\lambda}+1/2}}(r)
present. Clearly, this can happen only if
{\mathcal{U}}=\mathcal{I}_{n}
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

If
0\in\sigma(J_{N})
, the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{N}
has no self-adjoint extension whose domain is contained in

\oplus_{j=1}^{N}H^{1/2}(\Omega_{j},\mathbb{C}^{2})
. In this case, the construction in the proof of Theorem
4.4
can be repeated to observe that, for
\varepsilon>0
, a self-adjoint extension exists with domain contained in
\oplus_{j=1}^{N}H^{1/2-\varepsilon}(\Omega_{j},\mathbb{C}^{2})
. However, this inclusion does not allow for a unique distinguished self-adjoint extension to be fixed.

\diamondsuit

Appendix A
Closedness of the operator
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}

Lemma A.1
.

The closure of the restriction
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\upharpoonright C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
with domain given by (
2.21
). In particular,
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
with domain given by (
2.21
) is closed.

Proof.

We split the analysis into consideration of the two cases
\widetilde{\lambda}\neq 1/2
and
\widetilde{\lambda}=1/2
.

Let
\widetilde{\lambda}\neq 1/2
.
For any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, observe that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\psi

\displaystyle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}\!=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left|\psi_{2}^{\prime}+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}\psi_{2}}{r}\right|^{2}dr+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left|\psi_{1}^{\prime}-\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}\psi_{1}}{r}\right|^{2}dr

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|\psi_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{2\widetilde{\lambda}\real(\psi_{2}^{\prime}\overline{\psi_{2}})}{r}+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}|\psi_{2}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)dr\!+\!\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|\psi_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-\frac{2\widetilde{\lambda}\real(\psi_{1}^{\prime}\overline{\psi_{1}})}{r}+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}|\psi_{1}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)dr

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|\psi_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}(|\psi_{2}|^{2})^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}|\psi_{2}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)dr\!+\!\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|\psi_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}-\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}(|\psi_{1}|^{2})^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}|\psi_{1}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)dr

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|\psi_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}+\widetilde{\lambda})|\psi_{2}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)dr+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(|\psi_{1}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{(\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}-\widetilde{\lambda})|\psi_{1}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)dr,

where in the last step we performed integration by parts.
Combining the above formula with the one-dimensional Hardy inequality

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|\psi^{\prime}|^{2}dr\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{|\psi|^{2}}{4r^{2}}dr,\qquad\forall\,\psi\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

we conclude that the graph-norm induced by the operator
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
on the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is equivalent to the
H^{1}
-norm.
Indeed, we get the double sided estimate

\min\{1,(1-2\widetilde{\lambda})^{2}\}\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}\leq\|\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}+\|\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}\leq\left(1+2\widetilde{\lambda}\right)^{2}\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}

for any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})
. Hence, we have
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}=\overline{\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}\upharpoonright C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})}
and thus the operator
\boldsymbol{d}_{\widetilde{\lambda}}
is closed in this case.

Let
\widetilde{\lambda}=1/2
. Using
[
17
, Proposition 40]
(see also
[
10
, Proposition 3.1]
) one can check that the domain of
\boldsymbol{d}_{1/2}
can be alternatively characterized as

\dom\boldsymbol{d}_{1/2}=\left\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{C}^{2})\colon\psi_{1}^{\prime}-\frac{\psi_{1}}{2r},\psi_{2}^{\prime}+\frac{\psi_{2}}{2r}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\right\}.

Closedness of
\boldsymbol{d}_{1/2}
then follows from
[
13
, Equation (1.10) and Theorem 1.1 (ii)]
.
∎
