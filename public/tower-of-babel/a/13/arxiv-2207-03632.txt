Introduction

In the last 15 years there have been several papers looking at the ‘reconfiguration’ and ‘mixing’ variations of the classical graph colouring, and of the more general graph homomorphism, or
H
-colouring, problem. See
[

These variations of the

A

While the well known

Problem

(

)
.

Instance: A graph

Decision: Is

The

-recolouring

Problem

Homomorphisms, the Hom-graph, and the above problems can be defined for all relational structures, not just graphs, and it is well known (

In

The expectation is that with graphs

2.
Outline of Proof and of Main Construction

One of the essential features in many papers on recolouring and homomorphism reconfiguration is the simple fact that when mapping a cycle
B
to another smaller cycle
C
of girth at least
4
, (or
5
if
B
is not reflexive) the number of times that
B
winds around
C
is invariant under reconfiguration. In
[
12
]
, Wrochna, to great effect, stated this in terms of homotopy, and showed that the homotopy, appropriately defined, of cycles in
G
when mapped to
H
, is invariant under reconfiguration.
This idea was adapted to reflexive graphs in
[
9
]
, and digraphs in
[
10
]
.
In this paper we use the sightly coarser, but computationally simpler topological invariant of homology. We define homology properly in Section
3
, but for the purposes of this outline, one unfamiliar with the concept benefits by thinking of
H
as a cycle, and the ‘homology’ of another cycle
C
mapping to
H
as being the number of times it winds around
H
; having trivial homology means it does not wind.

As
H
is reflexive, there are always constant maps in
\Hom(G,H)
, and as
H
is connected, it is easy to see that these are always in the same component of
\Hom(G,H)
. So to decide if
\Hom(G,H)
is connected, one must decide if every
H
-colouring of
G
reconfigures to a constant map; this follows the basic approach used in
[
2
]
. In
[
9
]
it was shown that an
H
-colouring of
G
reconfigures to a constant map if and only if it has trivial homotopy (see Corollary
4.4
below), and trivial homotopy is implied by trivial homology (see Lemma
4.5
). So deciding if all
H
-colourings of
G
have trivial homology is a sub-problem of
\Mix(H)
. Calling an
H
-colouring of
G

flat
if it has trivial homology, we reduce our task to showing
NP
-completeness of the problem
\NF(H)
to deciding if there is a non-flat
H
-colouring of an instance
G
(see Lemma
4.7
).

To prove this, we will reduce
3
-colouring to
\NF(H)
via a gadget construction. Given an instance
G
of
3
-colouring, we construct an instance
G_{*}
of
\NF(H)
such that
G
has a
3
-colouring if and only if
G_{*}
has a non-flat homomorphism to
H
. Now, we give an overview of the construction.

The (categorical) product
A\times B
of two graphs
A
and
B
is the graph with vertex set
V(A)\times V(B)
, in which
(a,b)\sim(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
if
a\sim a^{\prime}
in
A
and
b\sim b^{\prime}
in
B
.
For a vertex
a
of
A
,
a\times B
is the subgraph induced on the vertices
\{(a,b)\mid b\in B\}
. If
A
has a loop, then this is an isomorphic copy of
B
.

Where
P_{\ell}
is the reflexive path on vertices
{0,\dots,\ell}
, it is well known, and easy to show, that the existence of a path
P_{\ell}
from
\phi
to
\psi
in
\Hom(G,C)
is equivalent to the existence of a homomorphism from
P_{\ell}\times G
to
C
that restricts on
0\times G
to
\phi
and on
\ell\times G
to
\psi
. For a cycle
C
, we refer to
P_{\ell}\times C
as a
path-of-cycles
and to each copy
i\times C
of
C
a
slice
of the path.

In Fact
3.4
, we observe the basic fact that under a homomorphism of a path-of-cycles to
H
, all slices have the same homology. This property of paths-of-cycles is the main building block in our construction of
G_{*}
. Let
Z
be a smallest cycle in
H
. The graph
G_{*}
will contain a special copy
Z_{*}
of
Z
, and for every vertex
v
of
G
, three more copies
Z_{0}^{v},Z_{1}^{v}
and
Z_{2}^{v}
of
Z
.

Using a gadget
S_{3}
made of paths-of-cycles we will ensure that under a homomorphism
\phi:G_{*}\to H
, if
Z_{*}
has nontrivial homology, then exactly one of the
Z_{i}^{v}
for each
v
have the same homology as
Z_{*}
, and the other two have trivial homology. For every edge
uv
of
G
we use another path-of-cycles gadget
S_{2}
to ensure that not both
Z_{i}^{v}
and
Z_{i}^{u}
get nontrivial homology. From this it will not be hard to show that if there is a non-flat homomorphism
\phi:G_{*}\to H
; one such that
Z_{*}
has nontrivial homology, then we get a
3
-colouring of
G
by mapping
v
to the
i
such that
Z_{i}^{v}
has nontrivial homology.

The construction as described above is not so difficult. The gadgets are constructed in Section
5
and the rest of the construction is done with Construction
6.1
of Section
6
. However, we also have to show that if there is no
3
-colouring of
G
, then all homomorphism of
G_{*}
to
H
are flat. It is not difficult to show that if there is no
3
-colouring of
G
, then any homomorphism of
G_{*}
to
H
has trivial homology on the slices of our path-of-cycles gadgets. This does not yet ensure that all
H
-colourings of
G_{*}
are flat. There may be a lot of other ‘non-gadget’ cycles in
G_{*}
that also must have trivial homology.

To deal with this we define a subgraph
T_{*}
of
G_{*}
that contains a basis, in the cycle space, of all of these non gadget-cycles. For each cycle in
T_{*}
, we ‘plug’ it, by triangulating it to a new central vertex. So that this does not break the necessary properties of
G_{*}
, we must build
G_{*}
and the gadgets so that we know the possible colourings on the vertices of
T_{*}
. Because of this, our gadget construction mentions a subgraph

the copies of which will become part of
T_{*}
in
G_{*}
. The proof that the cycles in
T_{*}
that we plug in this way are all the cycles that have to be plugged takes considerable work, using a lot of topological machinery.

We try, as much as possible, to remove these topological proofs from the main flow of the proof. They take considerable patience for a reader unfamiliar with them. However, we also found that they provide a nice intuitive motivation for such basic results of a first algebraic topology class as the Künneth formula, excision, and Mayer-Vietoris sequences.

In Section
3
we recall the basic definitions of homology that are necessary throughout the paper. In Section
4
we reduce the mixing problem to the non-flat problem and state our main theorem about the non-flat problem. In Sections
5
and
6
we give our construction and prove the main theorem modulo a result about the basis of its cycle space that we prove in Section
7

3.
The homology of a graph

We recall the basic definitions of simplicial homology with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. This is basically Section 2.1 of Hatcher
[
7
]
applied to the clique-complex of a graph
G

In the
clique-complex
\mathbf{G}
of a graph
G
, an
n
-simplex
is an ordered
(n+1)
-clique of
G
. As
G
is reflexive, we consider multisets
[v,v,v]
, and
[u,v,u]
when
u\sim v
, to be
3
-cliques.

The
chain group

C_{n}(\mathbf{G})
of
G
is the group generated as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module over the set of
n
-simplices of
\mathbf{G}
;
C_{-1}(\mathbf{G})
is taken to be
0
. The elements of the groups
C_{n}(\mathbf{G})
are
n
-chains– they can be view as multisets of the of
n
-simplices of
\mathbf{G}
.
Between these groups we have the boundary maps
\delta_{n}:C_{n}(\mathbf{G})\to C_{n-1}(\mathbf{G})
defined on
n
-simplices by

\delta_{n}([v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}])=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}[v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}],

and extended to the whole group additively. For example, assume that
G
has two
3
-cliques,
\{a,b,c\}
and
\{a,d,b\}
. The sum,
[a,b,c]+[a,d,b]
say, is a
2
-chain. Its boundary is

\displaystyle\delta([a,b,c]+[a,d,b])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[b,c]-[a,c]+[a,b]+[d,b]-[a,b]+[a,d]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[a,d]+[d,b]+[b,c]-[a,c].

Homology groups are defined using the boundary maps as follows.
The co-kernel

B_{i}(\mathbf{G}):=\delta_{i+1}(C_{i+1}(\mathbf{G}))\leq C_{i}(\mathbf{G})

of
\delta_{i+1}
is the
i^{th}
boundary group
; its elements are
i
-boundaries.
The kernel
Z_{i}(\mathbf{G})=\ker(\delta_{i})\leq C_{i}(\mathbf{G})
is the

i^{th}
cycle group
; its elements are
i
-cycles
.
It is easy to check that
\delta_{i}\circ\delta_{i+1}=0
and so
B_{i}(\mathbf{G})\leq Z_{i}(\mathbf{G})
.
The
i^{th}
homology group
is
H_{i}(\mathbf{G})=Z_{i}(\mathbf{G})/B_{i}(\mathbf{G})
; its elements are classes of
homologically equivalent
cycles, though we often refer to these classes simply as cycles.

We start with some standard easy calculations.

Example 3.1
.

For any connected graph
G
,
H_{0}(\mathbf{G})={\mathbb{Z}}
.
Indeed,
C_{0}(\mathbf{G})=\langle[v]\mid v\in V(G)\rangle
and as
\delta_{0}
is the
0
map,

Z_{0}(\mathbf{G})=\ker{\delta_{0}}=C_{0}(\mathbf{G})
. On the other hand,
C_{1}(\mathbf{G})=\langle[u,v]\mid uv\in E(G)\rangle
, so
B_{0}(\mathbf{G})=\delta_{1}(C_{1}(\mathbf{G}))=\langle[u]-[v]\mid uv\in E(G)\rangle
.
Thus
H_{0}(\mathbf{G})=Z_{0}(\mathbf{G})/B_{0}(\mathbf{G})={\mathbb{Z}}
.

\triangle

Example 3.2
.

The following are all true for any triangle-free reflexive
G
.

(1)

C_{2}(\mathbf{G})
is the free group generated by the set

\{[v,v,v]\mid v\in V(G)\}\cup\{[u,u,v],[u,v,u],[v,u,u]\mid uv\in E(G)\}

(2)

As
\delta([v,v,v])=[v,v]
and
\delta([u,v,u])=[v,u]-[u,u]+[u,v]
, one finds that
B_{1}(\mathbf{G})=\delta_{2}(C_{2})
is free generated by

\{[v,v]\mid v\in V(G)\}\cup\{[u,v]+[v,u]\mid uv\in E(G)\}.

It follows that
[v,u]=-[u,v]
in
H_{1}(\mathbf{G})
for every edge
uv
of
G
.

(3)

Z_{1}(\mathbf{G})
is free generated by cycles (including loops and
2
-cycles) in
G
.

(4)

H_{1}(\mathbf{G})=Z_{1}(\mathbf{G})/B_{1}(\mathbf{G})
is free generated by the undirected edge-sets of cycles (not-including loops and
2
-cycles) in
G
.

(5)

H_{n}(\mathbf{G})
is
trivial
; that is, has only the
0
element; for
n\geq 2
.

\triangle

In light of (3), we will often talk of a cycle
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell},x_{0})
of
G
as being in
Z_{1}(\mathbf{G})
. This really means the
1
-cycle

[x_{0},x_{1}]+[x_{1},x_{2}]+\dots+[x_{\ell},x_{0}].

Example 3.3
.

If
G
is not triangle free, then a triangle such as
(u,v,w,u)
, which lives
in
Z_{1}(\mathbf{G})
as
\sigma=[u,v]+[v,w]+[w,u]
is a boundary in
B_{1}(\mathbf{G})
:

\delta_{2}([u,v,w])=[v,w]-[u,w]+[u,v]=[u,v]+[v,w]+[w,u],

so
[\sigma]=0
in
H_{1}(\mathbf{G})
. Thus we still have that
H_{1}(\mathbf{G})
is generated by undirected edges-sets of cycles in
G
, but it may no longer be free, and there can be more complicated boundaries as well. The groups
H_{n}
for
n\geq 2
are also not generally trivial, if
G
is not triangle-free.

\triangle

The group
Z_{1}(\mathbf{G})
of
1
-cycles is what is often called the integral cycle space of
G
; as opposed to the cycle space, which we get with the same development using
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
in place of
{\mathbb{Z}}
. A cycle
C
in

is
trivial
if
[C]=0
in

, where
0
represents the class containing the empty cycle. If
H_{1}(\mathbf{G})=\{0\}
then we say

.

The following is the basic statement that all slices of a path of cycle get the same homology under any homomorphism.

Fact 3.4
.

Let
\phi:P_{\ell}\times C\to H
be a homomorphism. For all
j,k\in\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
we have
[\phi(j\times C)]=[\phi(k\times C)]
in

.

Proof.

Clearly it is enough to prove this for

and
k=1
.
Observe that the boundary
\delta_{2}(c_{i})
of the
2
-chain

c_{i}:=[(0,i),(0,i+1),(1,i)]+[(1,i),(0,i+1),(1,i+1)]

is the square

[(0,i),(0,i+1)]-[(0,i),(1,i)]-[(1,i),(1,i+1)]+[(0,i+1),(1,i+1)].

So the boundary of the
2
-chain
\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}c_{i}
is

(0\times C)-(1\times C)
, and so its image
\phi(0\times C)-\phi(1\times C)

is also a boundary.
Thus
[\phi(0\times C)]=[\phi(1\times C)]
in

, as needed.

4.

In this section we reduce our main result to a problem called

Definition 4.1
.

A homomorphism
\phi:G\to H
is

if for all cycles

in
G

[\phi(C)]=0
in
H_{1}(\mathbf{H})
. Equivalently
\phi

[\phi(C)]=0
for all cycles

in a basis of

.

Consider the following problem.

Problem

(
\NF(H)
)
.

Instance: A graph
G
.

Decision: Is there a non-flat homomorphism of

to
H
?

We will prove the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
H
be a reflexive graph such that
H_{1}(\mathbf{H})
is free and nontrivial.
The problem
\NF(H)
is
\NP
-hard.

The proof of this takes up most of the paper, but for the rest of this section, we show that it implies Theorem

. We need a result from
[
9
]
where homotopy classes of cycles in a graph are defined.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\Pi(H;x_{0})
be the graph whose vertices are cycles in

, and in which a cycle

(P1)

, or

Lemma 4.5

Let

Proof.

Our proof is by induction on the length of

Let

, for

As a cycle in

If the negation of some element in the chain appears later in the chain:

for some

, then these disappear in

, and we get in

that

with indices modulo

So we may assume that the

is free and

is as given in Example

.
∎

\displaystyle H_{1}(\mathbf{P}_{\ell}\times\mathbf{C})

\displaystyle(H_{1}(\mathbf{P}_{\ell})\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}H_{0}(\mathbf{C}))\oplus(H_{0}(\mathbf{P}_{\ell})\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}H_{1}(\mathbf{C}))

\displaystyle(0\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus({\mathbb{Z}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Z}})

Our main tools are excision for dealing with quotients, and Mayer-Vietoris sequences for patching together gadgets.

The following is a special case of the process known as excision. It tells us that when contracting an edge not in a induced

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

, and let

Thus, as simplicial complexes,

Take an epsilon neighbourhood in

\displaystyle\dots\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\delta_{n+1}}}

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mathbf{C})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\Phi_{n}}}H_{n}(\mathbf{A})\oplus H_{n}(\mathbf{B})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\Psi_{n}}}H_{n}(\mathbf{X})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\delta_{n}}}H_{n-1}(\mathbf{C})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\Phi_{n-1}}}
