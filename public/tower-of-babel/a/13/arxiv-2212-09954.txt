Singularities of Fitzpatrick and convex functions
Dmitry Kramkov, Mihai Sîrbu

Abstract
In a pseudo-Euclidean space with scalar product S ⁡ ( ⋅ , ⋅ ) S(\cdot,\cdot) , we show that the singularities of projections on S S -monotone sets and of the associated Fitzpatrick functions are covered by countable c − c c-c surfaces having positive normal vectors with respect to the S S -product. By Zajíček 1979 , the singularities of a convex function f f can be covered by a countable collection of c − c c-c surfaces. We show that the normal vectors to these surfaces are restricted to the cone generated by F − F F-F , where F := cl range ∇ f F:=\cl\range\nabla f , the closure of the range of the gradient of f f .

convexity, subdifferential, Fitzpatrick function,
projection, pseudo-Euclidean space, normal vector, singularity.

Introduction

A locally Lipschitz function
f=f(x)
on
\real{d}
is differentiable
almost everywhere, according to the Rademacher’s Theorem. The set of
its singularities

\Sigma(f):=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;f\text{ is not differentiable at
}x\right\}

can be quite irregular. For instance, for
d=1
,
Zahorski 1950

(see
Fowler and Preiss 2009
for a simple proof) shows that
any

G_{\delta\sigma}
set (countable union of countable intersections of
open sets) of Lebesgue measure zero is the singular set of
some

Lipschitz function.

By
Zajíček 1979
, the
\Sigma(f)
of a convex function
f=f(x)

on
\real{d}
has
c-c
structure: it can be covered by a countable
collection of the graphs of the differences of two convex functions of
dimension
d-1
. Short proofs of the
Zajíček 1979
theorem
can be found in
Benyamini and Lindenstrauss 2000
,

Thibault 2023
,
and
Hajłasz 2022
.
Alberti 1994
shows that, except for sets
with zero
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
measure, the Hausdorff measure of
dimension
d-1
, the covering can be achieved with smooth
surfaces. These results yield sharp conditions for the existence and
uniqueness of optimal maps in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
optimal transport,
see
Brenier 1991
and
Ambrosio and Gigli 2013
.

Let
E
be a closed subset of
\real{d}
and

d_{E}(x):=\inf_{y\in E}|x-y|,\quad P_{E}(x):=\left\{{y\in E}|\;|x-y|=d_{E}(x)\right\},

be the Euclidean distance to
E
and the projection on
E
,
respectively.
Erdös 1946
proves that the singular set

\Sigma(P_{E}):=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;P_{E}(x)\text{ contains at least two points}\right\}

can be covered by countable sets with finite
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}

measure.
Hajłasz 2022
uses
[
24
]
and the
observation of
Asplund 1969
that the function

\psi_{E}(x):=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}d_{E}^{2}(x),\quad x\in\real{d},

is convex, to conclude that
\Sigma(P_{E})
has the
c-c
structure.

Albano and Cannarsa 1999
obtain a lower bound on the size of the set

\Sigma(d_{E})
, where
d_{E}
is not differentiable.

Let
S
be a
d\times d
invertible symmetric matrix with

m\in\left\{{0,1,\dots,d}\right\}
positive eigenvalues. Motivated by
applications to backward martingale transport in
[
17
]
,

[
15
]
, and
[
16
]
, we investigate in this
paper the singularities of projections on monotone sets in the
pseudo-Euclidean space with the scalar product

S(x,y):=\left\langle x,Sy\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}x^{i}S^{ij}y^{j},\quad x,y\in\real{d}.

Let
G\subset\real{d}
be an
S
-monotone or
S
-positive set:

S(x-y,x-y)\geq 0,\quad x,y\in G.

For every
x\in\real{d}
, the scalar square to
G
and the projection
on
G
are given by

\displaystyle\phi_{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{y\in G}S(x-y,x-y),

\displaystyle P_{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{y\in G}S(x-y,x-y).

Note that
S
-monotonicity is equivalent to

x\in G\implies x\in P_{G}(x)
and that

\psi_{G}(x):=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)-\frac{1}{2}\phi_{G}(x),\quad x\in\real{d},

is the Fitzpatrick function studied in
[
10
]
,

[
21
]
, and
[
18
]
.

The singularities of the projection
P_{G}
can be classified as

\displaystyle\Sigma(P_{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;P_{G}(x)\text{ contains at
least two points}\right\}

\displaystyle\;={\Sigma}_{0}(P_{G})\cup\Sigma_{1}(P_{G}),

\displaystyle{\Sigma}_{0}(P_{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{x\in\Sigma(P_{G})}|\;S(y_{1}-y_{2},y_{1}-y_{2})=0\text{~for \emph{all}~}y_{1},y_{2}\in P_{G}(x)\right\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}(P_{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{{x\in\Sigma(P_{G})}|\;S(y_{1}-y_{2},y_{1}-y_{2})>0\text{~for \emph{some}~}y_{1},y_{2}\in P_{G}(x)\right\}.

By Theorem
4.6
,
\Sigma_{1}(P_{G})
is contained in a countable
union of
c-c
surfaces having strictly positive normal vectors in the

S
-space. The structure of the zero-order singularities is described
in Theorems
4.8
and
4.7
. If
m=1
, then

{\Sigma}_{0}(P_{G})
is covered by a countable number of hyperplanes
having isotropic normal vectors in the
S
-space. If
m\geq 2
, then

{\Sigma}_{0}(P_{G})
is covered by a countable family of
c-c
surfaces
whose normal vectors are positive and almost isotropic in the

S
-space. These results yield sharp conditions for the existence and
uniqueness of backward martingale maps in
[
16
]
.

Using similar tools, in Theorem
3.1
, we improve the
c-c

description of singularities of general convex functions
f=f(x)
from

Zajíček 1979
by showing that the covering surfaces have normal
vectors belonging to the cone generated by
F-F
, where

F:=\cl\range\nabla f
, the closure of the range of the gradient
of
f
.

2
Parametrization of singularities

We say that a function
g=g(x)
on
\real{d}
has
linear
growth
if there is a constant
K=K(g)>0
such that

\left\lvert g(x)\right\rvert\leq K(1+\left\lvert x\right\rvert),\quad x\in\real{d}.

We write
\dom{\nabla g}
for the set of points where
g
is
differentiable.

Let
j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{j}
the
collection of compact sets
C
in
\real{d}
such that

y^{j}=1,\quad y\in C.

Any compact set
C\subset\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;x^{j}>0\right\}
can be rescaled as

\theta^{j}(C):=\left\{{\frac{y}{y^{j}}}|\;y\in C\right\}\in\mathcal{C}^{j}.

For
x\in\real{d}
, we denote by
x^{-j}
its sub-vector without the

j
th coordinate:

x^{-j}:=\left({x^{1},\dots,x^{j-1},x^{j+1},\dots,x^{d}}\right)\in\real{d-1}.

For
C\in\mathcal{C}^{j}
, we write
\mathcal{H}^{j}_{C}
for the
family of functions
h=h(x)
on
\real{d}
having the decomposition:

h(x)=x^{j}+g_{1}(x^{-j})-g_{2}(x^{-j}),\quad x\in\real{d},

(1)

where the functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
on
\real{d-1}
are convex, have
linear growth, and

\nabla h(x)\in C,\quad x^{-j}\in\dom{\nabla g_{1}}\cap\dom{\nabla g_{2}}.

(2)

The latter property has a clear geometric interpretation. Let
H
be a
closed set in
\real{d}
and
x\in H
. Following
[
20
, Definition 6.3
on page 199]
, we call a vector
w\in\real{d}

regular normal to
H
at
x
if

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\ni y\to x\\
y\not=x\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\langle w,y-x\right\rangle}{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}\leq 0.

A vector
w\in\real{d}
is called
normal to
H
at
x
if
there exist
x_{n}\in H
and a regular normal vector
w_{n}
to
H
at

x_{n}
such that
x_{n}\rightarrow x
and
w_{n}\rightarrow w
.

For a set
B
in
\real{d}
, we denote by
\conv{B}
its convex hull.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}
,
C\in\mathcal{C}^{j}
,
h
be given
by (
1
) for convex functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
with linear
growth, and
H
be the zero-level set of
h
:

H:=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;h(x)=0\right\}.

Then
h\in\mathcal{H}^{j}_{C}
, that is, (
2
) holds, if and
only if for every
x\in H
, there exists a normal vector
w\in C
to

H
at
x
.

Proof.

We can assume that
j=1
. Denote
f:=g_{1}-g_{2}
, so that

h(x)=x^{1}+f(x^{-1})
. We have that

\dom{\nabla{h}}=\real{}\times\dom{\nabla{f}}
,

\nabla h(x)=\left({1,\nabla f(x^{-1})}\right)
, and

H=\left\{{(-f(u),u)}|\;u\in\real{d-1}\right\}.

Denote also

U:=\dom{\nabla g_{1}}\cap\dom{\nabla g_{2}}\subset\dom{\nabla{f}}
.

\implies
: Clearly, the gradient
\nabla h(x)
is a regular normal
vector to
H
at
x\in\dom{\nabla h}\cap H
. As
U
is dense in

\real{d-1}
, the result holds by standard compactness arguments.

\impliedby
: Let
u\in U
,
v\in\real{d-1}
, and
w=(1,v)\in C
be
a normal vector to
H
at
x=(-f(u),u)
. Take a sequence

(x_{n},w_{n})
,
n\geq 1
, that converges to
(x,w)
and where
w_{n}
is
a regular normal vector to
H
at
x_{n}\in H
with
w_{n}^{1}=1
. Such a
sequence exists by the definition of a normal vector. We can
represent
x_{n}=(-f(u_{n}),u_{n})
for
u_{n}\in\real{d-1}
and

w_{n}=(1,v_{n})
for
v_{n}\in\real{d-1}
.

We claim that
v_{n}
belongs to the Clarke gradient of
f
at
u_{n}
:

v_{n}\in\overline{\partial}f(u_{n}):=\conv\left\{{\lim_{m}\nabla f(r_{m})}|\;\dom\nabla f\ni r_{m}\to u_{n}\right\}.

The continuity of
\nabla f
at
u\in U
then yields that

v_{n}\to\nabla f(u)
. Hence,
\nabla f(u)=v
, implying that

\nabla h(x)=(1,\nabla f(u))=w\in C
.

In order to prove the claim, we write the definition of

w_{n}=(1,v_{n})
being regular normal to
H
at
x_{n}=(-f(u_{n}),u_{n})
as

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\rightarrow u_{n}\\
r\not=u_{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{-\left({f(r)-f(u_{n})}\right)+\left\langle v_{n},r-u_{n}\right\rangle}{|f(r)-f(u_{n})|+|r-u_{n}|}\leq 0.

Using the Lipschitz property of
f
, we obtain that

\left\langle v_{n},s\right\rangle\leq\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{f(u_{n}+\delta s)-f(u_{n})}{\delta},\quad s\in\real{d-1}.

By
[
7
, Corollary 1.10]
, we have that

v_{n}\in\overline{\partial}f(u_{n})
, as claimed.
∎

We recall that the subdifferential

\partial f:\;\real{d}\rightrightarrows\real{d}
of a closed convex function

f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
is defined as

\partial f(x):=\left\{{y\in\real d}|\;\left\langle z,y\right\rangle\leq f(x+z)-f(x),\,z\in\real{d}\right\}.

Clearly,

\dom\partial f:=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;\partial f(x)\not=\emptyset\right\}\subset\dom f:=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;f(x)<\infty\right\}
.

The following theorem is our main technical tool for the study of
singularities of convex and Fitzpatrick functions in
Sections
3
and
4
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function,

j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}
, and
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be compact sets in

\real{d}
such that

y^{j}>0,\quad y\in C_{2}-C_{1}.

There exists a function
h\in\mathcal{H}^{j}_{\theta^{j}(C_{2}-C_{1})}

such that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{C_{1},C_{2}}(\partial f):=

\displaystyle\left\{{x\in\dom{\partial f(x)}}|\;\partial f(x)\cap C_{i}\not=\emptyset,\,i=1,2\right\}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;h(x)=0\right\}.

The proof of the theorem relies on Lemma
2.3
. For a closed
set
A\subset\real{d}
, we denote

f_{A}(x):=\sup_{y\in A}\left({\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right),\quad x\in\real{d},

(3)

where
f^{*}
is the convex conjugate of
f
:

f^{*}(y):=\sup_{x\in\real{d}}\left({\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f(x)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\},\quad y\in\real{d}.

We have that
f_{A}
is a closed convex function taking values in

\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
if and only if

A\cap\dom{f^{*}}=\left\{{x\in A}|\;f^{*}(x)<\infty\right\}\not=\emptyset;

otherwise,
f_{A}=-\infty
. We recall that

f(x)=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)\iff y\in\partial f(x)\iff x\in\partial f^{*}(y).

(4)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function and

C
be a compact set in
\real{d}
such that

C\cap\dom{f^{*}}\not=\emptyset
. Then
f_{C}
has linear growth and
for every
x\in\real{d}
,

\displaystyle\partial f_{C}(x)\cap C=\Arg_{C}(x):=\argmax_{y\in C}\left({\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right)\not=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\partial f_{C}(x)=\conv{\left({\partial f_{C}(x)\cap C}\right)},

\displaystyle\partial f(x)\cap C\not=\emptyset\iff f(x)=f_{C}(x)\iff\partial f(x)\cap C=\partial f_{C}(x)\cap C.

In particular,
f_{C}
is differentiable at
x
if and only if

\partial f_{C}(x)\cap C
is a singleton, in which case

\partial f_{C}(x)=\left\{{\nabla f_{C}(x)}\right\}\in C.

Proof.

Since
C
is a compact set,
f^{*}
is a closed convex function, and

C\cap\dom{f^{*}}\not=\emptyset
, we have that

\sup_{y\in C}\left\lvert y\right\rvert<\infty,\quad\inf_{x\in C}f^{*}(x)>-\infty,

and that
\Arg_{C}(x)
is a non-empty compact. Let

y_{0}\in C\cap\dom{f^{*}}
. From the definition of
f_{C}
we deduce
that

-\left\lvert x\right\rvert\left\lvert y_{0}\right\rvert-f^{*}(y_{0})\leq f_{C}(x)\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert\sup_{y\in C}\left\lvert y\right\rvert-\inf_{y\in C}f^{*}(y),\quad x\in\real{d}.

It follows that
f_{C}
has linear growth.

The function

h(x,y):=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)\in\real{}\cup\left\{{-\infty}\right\},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

is linear in
x
and concave and upper semi-continuous in
y
. Fix

x\in\real{d}
. We can choose
K
large enough such that for

E:=\left\{{z\in C}|\;f^{*}(z)\leq K\right\},

we have that
f_{C}=f_{E}
in a neighborhood of
x
and

\Arg_{C}(x)=\Arg_{E}(x):=\argmax_{y\in E}\left({\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right).

Since
E
is compact and the function
h(\cdot,y)
is finite for

y\in E
, the classical envelope theorem
[
14
, Theorem 4.4.2,
p. 189]
yields that

\displaystyle\partial f_{C}(x)\

\displaystyle=\partial f_{E}(x)=\partial\max_{y\in E}h(x,y)=\conv\bigcup_{y\in\Arg_{E}(x)}\partial_{x}h(x,y)=\conv\Arg_{E}(x)

\displaystyle=\conv{\Arg_{C}(x)}.

From the concavity of
h(x,\cdot)
we deduce that

\partial f_{C}(x)\cap C=\left({\conv{\Arg_{C}(x)}}\right)\cap C=\Arg_{C}(x).

Clearly,
f_{C}\leq f
. If
y\in\partial f(x)\cap C
, then

f(x)=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)\leq f_{C}(x)
. Hence,
f_{C}(x)=f(x)
and

y\in\Arg_{C}(x)\subset\partial f_{C}(x)
.

Conversely, let
f_{C}(x)=f(x)
. For every

y\in\partial f_{C}(x)\cap C
, we have that

f(x)=f_{C}(x)=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)
and then that

y\in\partial f(x)\cap C
.

Finally, being a convex function,
f_{C}
is differentiable at
x
if
and and only if
\partial f_{C}(x)
is a singleton. In this case,

\partial f_{C}(x)=\left\{{\nabla f_{C}(x)}\right\}
.
∎

3
Singular points of convex functions

For a multi-function
\Psi:\;\real{d}\rightrightarrows\real{d}
taking values
in closed subsets of
\real{d}
, we denote its domain by

\dom\Psi:=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;\Psi(x)\not=\emptyset\right\}.

Given an index
j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}
and a closed set
A
in

\real{d}
, the
singular
set of
\Psi
is defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{j}_{A}(\Psi):=\left\{{x\in\dom\Psi}|\;\exists y_{1},y_{2}\in\Psi(x)\cap A\text{ with }y_{1}^{j}\not=y_{2}^{j}\right\}.

We also write
\Sigma^{j}(\Psi)=\Sigma^{j}_{\real{d}}(\Psi)
and

\Sigma(\Psi)=\cup_{j=1,\dots,d}\Sigma^{j}(\Psi)
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function such
that its domain has a non-empty interior:

D:=\interior\dom f\not=\emptyset.

It is well-known that

\dom\nabla f:=\left\{{x\in D}|\;\nabla f(x)\text{~exists}\right\}
is
dense in
D
and

Recalling that

\displaystyle\dom{\overline{\partial}f}

(7)

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}f(x)

(8)

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function with

. Let

j\in\left\{{1,\dots,d}\right\}
and

be a closed set in
\real{d}

containing
\range\nabla f
. Then

\displaystyle\Sigma^{j}(\partial f)\cap D=\Sigma^{j}(\overline{\partial}f)\cap D=\Sigma^{j}_{A}(\partial f)\cap D,

(10)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{j}(\overline{\partial}f)\subset\Sigma^{j}_{A}(\partial f).

(11)

If
y^{j}=z^{j}
for all

, then, clearly,

\Sigma_{A}^{j}(\partial f)=\emptyset
. Otherwise, for every

there exist a compact set

with

,

(12)

For any

Taking the unions over

\Sigma(\overline{\partial}f)

The proof of Theorem
3.1
relies on some lemmas. We start with a
simple fact from convex analysis.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function
attaining a strict minimum at a point
x_{0}
:

f(x_{0})<f(x),\quad x\in\real{d},\,x\not=x_{0}.

Then

f(x_{0})<\inf_{\left\lvert x-x_{0}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}f(x),\quad\epsilon>0.

Proof.

If the conclusion is not true, then there exist
\epsilon>0
and a
sequence
(x_{n})
with
|x_{n}-x_{0}|\geq\epsilon
such that

f(x_{n})\rightarrow f(x_{0})
. As

z_{n}:=x_{0}+\frac{\epsilon}{\left\lvert x_{n}-x_{0}\right\rvert}(x_{n}-x_{0})

is a convex combination of
x_{0}
and
x_{n}
, we deduce that

f(z_{n})\leq\max(f(x_{0}),f(x_{n}))=f(x_{n})\to f(x_{0}).

By compactness,
z_{n}\rightarrow z_{0}
over a subsequence. Clearly,

\left\lvert z_{0}-x_{0}\right\rvert=\epsilon
, while by the lower semi-continuity,

f(z_{0})\leq\liminf f(z_{n})\leq f(x_{0})
. We have arrived to a
contradiction.
∎

The following result explains the special role played by

\cl\range\nabla f
. Recall the notation
f_{A}
from (
3
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function with

D:=\interior\dom f\not=\emptyset
and
A
be a closed set in

\real{d}
. Then

f_{A}(x)=f(x),\,x\in\cl D\iff\range\nabla f\subset A.

In other words,
F:=\cl\range\nabla f
is the minimal closed set
such that
f_{F}=f
on
\cl D
.

Proof.

\impliedby
: If
x\in\dom\nabla f
, then

y:=\nabla f(x)\in A
and (
4
) yields that

f(x)=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)=f_{A}(x).

Since
\dom{\nabla f}
is dense in
D
, the closed convex functions

f_{A}
and
f
coincide on
\cl D
.

\implies
: We fix
x_{0}\in\dom\nabla f
and set

y_{0}:=\nabla f(x_{0})
. By the assumption,
f_{A}(x_{0})=f(x_{0})
. If

y_{0}\notin A
, then the distance between
y_{0}
and
A
is at least

\epsilon>0
. According to (
4
), the concave upper
semi-continuous function

y\rightarrow\left\langle x_{0},y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)

attains a strict global maximum at
y_{0}
and has the maximum value

f(x_{0})
. By Lemma
3.2
,

\sup_{\left\lvert y-y_{0}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}\left({\left\langle x_{0},y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right)<f(x_{0}).

As
A\subset\left\{{y\in\real{d}}|\;|y-y_{0}|\geq\epsilon\right\}
, we arrive
to a contradiction:

f_{A}(x_{0})=\sup_{y\in A}\left({\left\langle x_{0},y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right)\leq\sup_{\left\lvert y-y_{0}\right\rvert\geq\epsilon}\left({\left\langle x_{0},y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right)<f(x_{0}).

Hence,
y_{0}\in A
, as required.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function with

D:=\interior\dom f\not=\emptyset
. Let
A
be a closed set in

\real{d}
such that
f=f_{A}
on
\cl D
. Then

\displaystyle\partial f(x)\cap A=\Arg_{A}(x):=\argmax_{y\in A}\left({\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y)}\right),\quad x\in\cl D,

\displaystyle\dom\Arg_{A}\cap\cl D=\dom\partial f=\dom\overline{\partial}f,

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}f(x)=\partial f(x)=\conv{\left({\partial f(x)\cap A}\right)},\quad x\in D,

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}f(x)\subset\cl\conv\left({\partial f(x)\cap A}\right),\quad x\in\dom\overline{\partial}f.

Proof.

If
x\in\dom\Arg_{A}\cap\cl D
and
y\in\Arg_{A}(x)
, then

f(x)=f_{A}(x)=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-f^{*}(y).

(13)

Hence,
y\in\partial f(x)\cap A
, by the properties of
subdifferentials in (
4
).

Conversely, let
x\in\dom\partial f
. Lemma
3.3
shows
that
F:=\cl\range\nabla f\subset A
. Accounting
for (
7
) and (
8
) and the definition of

\overline{\partial}f(x)
, we obtain that

\partial f(x)\cap A\supset\partial f(x)\cap F\supset\overline{\partial}f(x)\cap F\not=\emptyset.

Let
y\in\partial f(x)\cap A
. From (
4
) we
deduce (
13
) and then that
y\in\Arg_{A}(x)
. We have proved
the first two assertions of the lemma.

The fact that
\overline{\partial}f=\partial f
on
D
has been already
stated in (
9
). To prove the second equality in the third
assertion, we fix
x_{0}\in D
and choose
\epsilon>0
such that

B(x_{0},\epsilon):=\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;\left\lvert x-x_{0}\right\rvert\leq\epsilon\right\}\subset D.

The uniform boundedness of
\partial f
on compacts in
D
implies
the existence of a constant
K>0
such that

\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq K<\infty,\quad y\in\partial f(x),\,x\in B(x_{0},\epsilon).

Denote by
A_{K}:=A\cap\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;\left\lvert x\right\rvert\leq K\right\}
.
As
\range\nabla f\subset A
, we deduce from (
4
) that

f(x)=f_{A_{K}}(x),\quad x\in B(x_{0},\epsilon)\cap\dom\nabla f,

and then, by the density of
\dom\nabla f
in
D
, that
f=f_{A_{K}}

on
B(x_{0},\epsilon)
. Finally, Lemma
2.3
shows that

\partial f(x_{0})=\partial f_{A_{K}}(x_{0})=\conv\left({\partial f_{A_{K}}(x_{0})\cap A_{K}}\right)=\conv\left({\partial f(x_{0})\cap A}\right).

If
\dom\nabla f\ni x_{n}\to x
and
\nabla f(x_{n})\to y
, then,
clearly,
y\in F\subset A
. Moreover,
y\in\partial f(x)
, by the
continuity of subdifferentials. The last assertion of the lemma
readily follows.
∎

We are ready to finish the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma
3.3
shows that
f=f_{A}
on
\cl D
. Then, by
Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}f(x)=\partial f(x)=\conv{\left({\partial f(x)\cap A}\right)},\quad x\in D,

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}f(x)\subset\cl\conv\left({\partial f(x)\cap A}\right),\quad x\in\dom\overline{\partial}f.

Recalling that
\dom{\partial f}=\dom{\overline{\partial}f}
, we
deduce (
10
) and (
11
).

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Let
(x_{n})
be a dense sequence in
A
and

(r_{n})
be an enumeration of all positive rationals. Denote by

\alpha:=(m,n,k,l)
the indexes for which the compacts

\diam\theta^{j}(C^{\alpha}_{2}-C^{\alpha}_{1})<\epsilon\text{~and~}x^{j}>0,\;x\in C_{2}^{\alpha}-C_{1}^{\alpha}.

We have that

yields a function

\Sigma_{C^{\alpha}_{1},C^{\alpha}_{2}}(\partial f)\subset\left\{{x\in\real{d}}|\;h(x)=0\right\}.

∎

We conclude the section with the geometric interpretation of

\range\nabla f
. For a closed convex function

f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
, we denote by
\epi f
its epigraph:

\epi f:=\left\{{(x,q)\in\real{d}\times\real{}}|\;f(x)\leq q\right\}.

Let
E
be a closed convex set in
\real{d}
. A point
x_{0}\in E
is
called
exposed
if there is a hyperplane intersecting
E
only
at
x_{0}
. In other words, there is
y_{0}\in\real{d}
such that

\left\langle x-x_{0},y_{0}\right\rangle>0,\quad x\in E\setminus\left\{{x_{0}}\right\}.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f:\;\real{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\}
be a closed convex function with

\interior{\dom f}\not=\emptyset
. Then

\left\{{\left({y,f^{*}(y)}\right)}|\;y\in\range\nabla f\right\}=\text{
exposed points of }\epi f^{*}.

Proof.

By definition,
(y_{0},r_{0})
is an exposed point of
\epi f^{*}
if it
belongs to
\epi f^{*}
and

\left\langle y-y_{0},x_{0}\right\rangle+(r-r_{0})q_{0}>0,\quad(y,r)\in\epi f^{*}\setminus\left\{{(y_{0},r_{0})}\right\},

for some
(x_{0},q_{0})\in\real{d}\times\real{}
. The definition of

\epi f^{*}
ensures that
q_{0}>0
and
r_{0}=f^{*}(y_{0})
. Rescaling

(x_{0},q_{0})
so that
q_{0}=1
, we deduce that the function

{y}\to{\left\langle x_{0},y\right\rangle+f^{*}(y)}

has the unique minimizer
y_{0}
. This is equivalent to
y_{0}
being
the only element of
\partial f(-x_{0})
, which in turn is equivalent
to
-x_{0}\in\dom\nabla f
and
y_{0}=\nabla f(-x_{0})
.
∎

4
Singular points of projections in
S
-spaces

We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
the family of symmetric

d\times d
-matrices of full rank with
m\in\left\{{0,1,\dots,d}\right\}

positive eigenvalues. For
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
, the bilinear form

S(x,y):=\left\langle x,Sy\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}x^{i}S_{ij}y^{j},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

defines the scalar product on a pseudo-Euclidean space
\real{d}_{m}

with dimension
d
and index
m
, which we call the
S
-space. The
quadratic form
S(x,x)
is the
scalar square
on the
S
-space;
its value may be negative.

For a closed set
G\subset\real{d}
, we define the

Fitzpatrick-type
function

\psi_{G}(x):=\sup_{y\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(y,y)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{+\infty}\right\},\quad x\in\real{d},

and the projection multi-function

\displaystyle P_{G}(x):=

\displaystyle\argmin_{y\in G}S(x-y,x-y)=\argmax_{y\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(y,y)}\right),\quad x\in\real{d}.

Clearly,
\psi_{G}
is a closed convex function and
P_{G}
takes values
in the closed (possibly empty) subsets of
G
.

A closed set
G\subset\real{d}
is called
S
-
monotone
or

S
-
positive
if

S(x-y,x-y)\geq 0,\quad x,y\in G,

or, equivalently, if its projection multi-function has the natural
fixed-point property:

x\in P_{G}(x),\quad x\in G.

We denote by
\mathcal{M}(S)
the family of closed non-empty

S
-monotone sets in
\real{d}
. We refer to
Fitzpatrick 1988
,

Simons 2007
, and
Penot 2009
for the results on Fitzpatrick
functions
\psi_{G}
associated with
G\in\mathcal{M}(S)
.

Example 4.1

(Standard form)
.

If
d=2m
and

S(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left({x^{i}y^{m+i}+x^{m+i}y^{i}}\right),\quad x,y\in\real{2m},

then
S\in\mathcal{S}^{2m}_{m}
and the
S
-monotonicity means the standard
monotonicity in
\real{2m}=\real{m}\times\real{m}
. For a

maximal
monotone set
G
, the function
\psi_{G}
becomes the
classical Fitzpatrick function from
[
10
]
.

Example 4.2

(Canonical form)
.

If
\Lambda
is the
canonical
quadratic form in
\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
:

\Lambda(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x^{i}y^{i}-\sum_{i=m+1}^{d}x^{i}y^{i},\quad x,y\in\real{d},

then a closed set
G
is
\Lambda
-monotone if and only if

G=\graph{f}:=\left\{{(u,f(u))}|\;u\in D\right\},

where
D
is a closed set in
\real{m}
and
f:\;D\rightarrow\real{d-m}

is a
1
-Lipschitz function:

\left\lvert f(u)-f(v)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert u-v\right\rvert,\quad u,v\in D.

In view of the Kirszbraun Theorem,
[
9
, 2.10.43]
,
G
is
maximal
\Lambda
-monotone if and only if
D=\real{m}
.

While working on the
S
-space, it is convenient to use appropriate
versions of subdifferential and Clark-type subdifferential for a
convex function
f
. For
x\in\cl{\dom{f}}
, they are defined as

\displaystyle\partial^{S}f(x):=

\displaystyle\left\{{y\in\real d}|\;S(z,y)\leq f(x+z)-f(x),\,z\in\real{d}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{{S^{-1}y\in\real d}|\;\left\langle z,y\right\rangle\leq f(x+z)-f(x),\,z\in\real{d}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;S^{-1}\partial f(x),

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}^{S}f(x):=

\displaystyle S^{-1}\overline{\partial}f(x).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}^{d}_{m}
,
G\in\mathcal{M}(S)
, and assume that

D:=\interior\dom{\psi_{G}}\not=\emptyset
. Then

\displaystyle\range{S^{-1}\nabla\psi_{G}}\subset G,

\displaystyle\dom{P_{G}}=\dom{\partial^{S}\psi_{G}}=\dom{\overline{\partial}^{S}\psi_{G}},

and

\displaystyle\psi_{G}(x)=\psi_{G}^{*}(Sx)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x),\quad x\in G,

\displaystyle P_{G}(x)=\partial^{S}\psi_{G}(x)\cap G,\quad x\in\real{d}.

Proof.

We write
\psi_{G}
as

\psi_{G}(x)=g^{*}(x):=\sup_{y\in\real{d}}(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle-g(y)),\quad x\in\real{d},

where
g(y)=\frac{1}{2}S^{-1}(y,y)
for
y\in SG
and

g(y)=+\infty
for
y\not\in SG
. From the definition of

\psi_{G}
and the
S
-monotonicity of
G
we deduce that

\psi_{G}(x)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)-\frac{1}{2}\inf_{y\in G}S(x-y,x-y)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x),\quad x\in G,

and then that

\psi_{G}(S^{-1}x)\leq g(x),\quad x\in\real{d}.

As
\psi^{*}_{G}=g^{**}
and
g^{**}
is the largest closed convex
function less than
g
, we have that

\psi_{G}(S^{-1}x)\leq\psi^{*}_{G}(x)\leq g(x),\quad x\in\real{d}.

Putting together the relations above, we obtain that

\frac{1}{2}S(x,x)=\psi_{G}(x)\leq\psi^{*}_{G}(Sx)\leq g(Sx)=\frac{1}{2}S(x,x),\quad x\in G.

For every
x\in\real{d}
, the values of the Fitzpatrick function

\psi_{G}
and of the projection multi-function
P_{G}
can now be
written as

\displaystyle\psi_{G}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\psi_{G}^{*}(Sy)}\right)=\sup_{z\in SG}\left({\left\langle x,z\right\rangle-\psi_{G}^{*}(z)}\right),

\displaystyle P_{G}(x)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{y\in G}\left({S(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}S(y,y)}\right)=S^{-1}\argmax_{z\in SG}\left({\left\langle x,z\right\rangle-\psi^{*}_{G}(z)}\right).

The stated relations between
P_{G}
and
\partial^{S}\psi_{G}
follow
from Lemma
3.4
as soon as we observe that

P_{G}(x)=\partial^{S}\psi_{G}(x)=\emptyset
for
x\notin\cl D
. The
equality of the domains of
\partial^{S}\psi_{G}
and

\overline{\partial}^{S}\psi_{G}
is just a restatement of (
7
).
Finally, Lemma
3.3
yields the inclusion of

\range\nabla\psi_{G}
into
SG
.
∎

To facilitate geometric interpretations, we also adapt the concept of
a normal vector to the product structure of the
S
-space. Let
H
be
a closed set in
\real{d}
. A vector

is called

if

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\ni y\to x\\

y\not=x\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\langle Sw,y-x\right\rangle}{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}\leq 0.

A vector
w\in\real{d}
is called
S
-
normal to
H
at
x
if
there exist
x_{n}\in H
and an
S
-regular normal vector
w_{n}
to
H

at
x_{n}
, such that
x_{n}\rightarrow x
and
w_{n}\rightarrow w
. In
other words,
w
is
S
-(regular) normal to
H
at
x\in H
if
Sw
is
(regular) normal to
H
at
x
in the classical Euclidean sense. It
is easy to see that if
x\in P_{H}(z)
, then the vector
