Simultaneous Momentum and Position Measurement and the Instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg Group
Christopher S. Jackson, Carlton M. Caves

Abstract
The canonical commutation relation, [ Q , P ] = i ​ ℏ [Q,P]=i\hbar , stands at the foundation of quantum theory and the original Hilbert space. The interpretation of P P and Q Q as observables has always relied on the analogies that exist between the unitary transformations of Hilbert space and the canonical (a.k.a. contact) transformations of classical phase space. Now that the theory of quantum measurement is essentially complete (this took a while), it is possible to revisit the canonical commutation relation in a way that sets the foundation of quantum theory not on unitary transformations, but on positive transformations. This paper shows how the concept of simultaneous measurement leads to a fundamental differential geometric problem whose solution shows us the following: The simultaneous P & Q P\&Q measurement (SPQM) defines a universal measuring instrument, which takes the shape of a 7-dimensional manifold, a universal covering group we call the Instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg Group, IWH \mathrm{IWH} . The group IWH \mathrm{IWH} connects the identity to classical phase space in unexpected ways that are significant enough that the positive-operator-valued measure (POVM) offers a complete alternative to energy quantization. Five of the dimensions define processes that can be easily recognized and understood. The other two dimensions, the normalization and phase in the center of IWH \mathrm{IWH} , are less familiar. The normalization, in particular, requires special handling in order to describe and understand the SPQM instrument.

Kraus operators can be interpreted as an
indirect measurement

vonNeumann1932a
;
Arthurs1965a
;
Kraus1983a
;
Nielsen2000a
;
CSJackson2023a
, where in the differential case a meter with initial meter wavefunction,

\sqrt{dq}\,\langle q|0\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{dq}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-q^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}\,,

(35)

is displaced by the system according to the interaction

-\frac{i}{\hbar}Hdt=\sqrt{\kappa\,dt}\,X\otimes\!\left({-}2\sigma\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\right)\,,

(36)

which in turn registers some “position”
q
, fixing

dW=\sqrt{dt}\,\frac{q}{\sigma}\,,

(37)

so that

\sqrt{d\mu(dW)}\,L_{X}(dW)=\sqrt{dq}\,\langle q|e^{-iH\,dt/\hbar}|0\rangle

(38)

(for further details, see
CSJackson2023a
).

An irresistible sidenote, developed more generally and in more detail in
CSJackson2023a
, is that the stochastic unitary transformations of equation
27
follow from the same meter interaction
36
, but with registration of the meter momentum
p
, instead of its position
q
. As such, the stochastic unitary transformations have a total operation identical to equation
32
,

\int d\mu(dW)\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\!\left(e^{-iX\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW}\right)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa\,dt\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}}\,,

(39)

This alternative unraveling of the total operation corresponds to a symmetry of the general Lindbladian,

\mathcal{L}(A)=A\odot A^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big({A^{\dagger}A\odot 1+1\odot A^{\dagger}A}\Big)\,,

(40)

which is

\mathcal{L}(-iX)=\mathcal{L}(X)\,,

(41)

so that
-iX
is an alternative Lindblad operator of the total operation.

II.2
SPQM and the Group IWH

The subject of this paper is the continual (or continuous) simultaneous observation of the canonical observables
P
and
Q
, defined by the canonical commutation relations,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt[Q,P]=i\Omega\hskip 15.0pt\text{and}\hskip 15.0pt[\Omega,Q]=[\Omega,P]=0\,.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(42)

As a Lie algebra of infinitesimal generators, these observables are usually considered to generate unitary displacement operators,

D_{\alpha}=e^{iQ\alpha_{2}-iP\alpha_{1}}\,,\hskip 20.0pt\text{where}\hskip 20.0pt\alpha=\frac{\alpha_{1}+i\alpha_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,

(43)

which together define the 3-dimensional
Unitary Weyl-Heisenberg Group
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptK=\mathrm{UWH}\equiv\left\{D_{\alpha}\,e^{i\Omega\phi}\,:\,\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\;\phi\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(44)

If the generators operate irreducibly on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then by Shur’s lemma
\Omega=\hbar 1_{\mathcal{H}}
for some
\hbar\in\mathbb{R}
(because
g\Omega g^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=\Omega
for all
g\in\mathrm{UWH}
.)
Assuming that
\hbar
is finite, that is
\hbar\neq 0
, all such representations are essentially equivalent because the observables can always be rescaled so as to make the choice
\hbar=1
. Therefore, it is usually assumed that

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\Omega=1_{\mathcal{H}}\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(45)

We shall now assume
\hbar=1
, but we will continue nonetheless to use
\Omega
for the infinitesimal generator to emphasize its existence is not defined by the Hilbert space, but instead by the canonical commutation relations.
In particular, what is defined by the Hilbert space is the relation
\Omega^{2}=\Omega
, which is associative algebraic and not Lie algebraic.

For quantization, the Unitary Weyl-Heisenberg Group is supplemented with the unitary group generated by

H_{\textrm{o}}\equiv\frac{P^{2}+Q^{2}}{2}\,,

(46)

defining the 4-dimensional
Dynamical Weyl-Heisenberg Group
,

\mathrm{DWH}\equiv\left\{e^{-iH_{\textrm{o}}s}D_{\alpha}\,e^{i\Omega\phi}\,:\,s\in\mathbb{R},\;\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\;\phi\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\,.

(47)

Here, the use of “dynamical” refers to the analogies between
H_{\textrm{o}}
and the classical Hamiltonian of a simple harmonic oscillator, which quantum mechanics was originally founded upon.

The observables
P
and
Q
can be measured simultaneously in the sense that the positive transformations they generate commute infinitesimally (to order
dt
),

\displaystyle L(dw)

\displaystyle\equiv L_{Q}\left(dW^{q}\right)L_{P}\left(dW^{p}\right)

(48)

\displaystyle=e^{-Q^{2}\kappa dt+Q\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{q}}e^{-P^{2}\kappa dt+P\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{p}}

(49)

\displaystyle=e^{-(Q^{2}+P^{2})\kappa dt+Q\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{q}+P\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{p}+\frac{1}{2}[Q,P]\kappa\,dW^{q}dW^{p}}

(50)

\displaystyle=e^{-(Q^{2}+P^{2})\kappa dt+Q\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{q}+P\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{p}}

(51)

\displaystyle=L_{P}\!\left(dW^{p}\right)L_{Q}\!\left(dW^{q}\right)\,,

(52)

so long as the Wiener outcome increments,
dW^{q}
and
dW^{p}
, are independent, their joint Wiener measure being

d\mu(dw)\equiv d\mu(dW^{q})d\mu(dW^{p})=\frac{d^{2}(dw)}{\pi dt}e^{-dw^{*}dw/dt}\,.

(53)

Here we switch to using a complex Wiener increment

dw\equiv\frac{dW^{q}+idW^{p}}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(54)

Continually repeating this simultaneous measurement for a finite amount of time,
T
, defines the overall Kraus operators,

L\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]=\mathcal{T}\!\!\prod_{k=0}^{T/dt-1}L(dw_{kdt})\,,

(55)

where
\mathcal{T}\prod
denotes a time-ordered product.
It is important to appreciate that while the Kraus operators commute infinitesimally, they do not commute over finite amounts of time.
Finally, these Kraus operators are accompanied by the Wiener path measure,

\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]=\prod_{k=0}^{T/dt-1}\!d\mu(dw_{kdt})=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{T/dt-1}\!d^{\,2}\!\big(dw_{kdt}\big)\right)\!\!\left(\frac{1}{\pi dt}\right)^{T/dt}\exp\!\left(-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\frac{|dw_{t}|^{2}}{dt}\,\right)\,,

(56)

which is written here in terms of the complex Wiener increments.
In summary, we have defined a time-dependent instrument with Kraus operators

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]}\,L\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]}\mathcal{T}\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa dt+Q\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{q}+P\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{p}\right)\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(57)

where
\mathcal{T}\exp\int
is the time-ordered exponential.
This is the
Simultaneous P&Q Measurement
(SPQM.)
It is worth repeating that
H_{\textrm{o}}
appears here due to the form of the differential positive transformations of equation
28
and in this context is not a Hamiltonian because it is not generating unitary transformations.

While SPQM has been considered as far back as
Barchielli1982a
, it has not been fully solved before.
There are two other ways of measuring
P
and
Q
simultaneously that are important to distinguish from SPQM: the Arthurs-Kelly measurement
Arthurs1965a
;
Braunstein1991a
and the Goetsch-Graham-Wiseman (GGW) model of heterodyne detection
Barchielli1991a
;
Goetsch1994a
;
Wiseman1994b
;
Wiseman1996a
;
CSJackson2022a
.
The Arthurs-Kelly measurement has the same system-meter interaction as the SPQM process, but is different in that Arthurs and Kelly imagine continually interacting the same two meters with the system until the measurement is terminated, whereas in the SPQM process the system interacts with many pairs of meters successively, registering the complex Wiener path
dw_{[0,T)}
.
The GGW model of heterodyne detection has the same many-meter model as the SPQM process, but each system-meter interaction is energy conserving (the so-called leaky cavity), whereas the system-meter interaction of the SPQM process is the meter displacement of equation
36
.

The total operation of the SPQM process is a Wiener-like path integral,

\mathcal{Z}_{T}\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\Big(L\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\!\Big)\,,

(58)

which is absolutely trivial to solve,

\mathcal{Z}_{T}=\left(\int d\mu(dw)\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\Big(e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa dt+Q\sqrt{\kappa}dW_{t}^{q}+P\sqrt{\kappa}dW_{t}^{p}}\Big)\right)^{\circ T/dt}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa T(\mathrm{ad}_{Q}^{2}+\mathrm{ad}_{P}^{2})}\,.

(59)

The interest of this article, however is entirely in the manifold diffusion process defined by SPQM, where equation
57
is understood to define sample-paths in a finite-dimensional manifold.
The infinitesimal generators of these sample-paths are
Q
,
P
, and
H_{\textrm{o}}
.
By simply considering their Lie brackets to first order,

\displaystyle[Q,P]

\displaystyle=i\Omega\,,

(60)

\displaystyle[H_{\textrm{o}},Q]

\displaystyle=-iP\,,

(61)

\displaystyle[H_{\textrm{o}},P]

\displaystyle=iQ\,,

(62)

and second order,

\displaystyle\big[[H_{\textrm{o}},Q],Q\big]

\displaystyle=-\Omega\,,

(63)

\displaystyle\big[[H_{\textrm{o}},P],P\big]

\displaystyle=-\Omega\,,

(64)

\displaystyle\big[H_{\textrm{o}},[H_{\textrm{o}},Q]\big]

\displaystyle=Q\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\big[H_{\textrm{o}},[H_{\textrm{o}},P]\big]

\displaystyle=P\,,

(66)

we can see that SPQM defines a 7-dimensional manifold, a representation of what we will call the
Instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg Group
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptG=\mathrm{IWH}\equiv\left\{e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r}e^{Qq}e^{Pp}D_{\alpha}\,e^{i\Omega\phi}e^{-\Omega\ell}\,:\,r\in\mathbb{R},\;q,p\in\mathbb{R},\;\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\;\phi,\ell\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(67)

There is a fourth and final Weyl-Heisenberg group worth defining, the 6-dimensional
Complex Weyl-Heisenberg Group
,

\mathbb{C}\mathrm{WH}\equiv\left\{e^{Qq}e^{Pp}e^{-\Omega\ell}D_{\alpha}\,e^{i\Omega\phi}\,:\,q,p\in\mathbb{R},\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\phi,\ell\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

(68)

which is a maximal normal subgroup of
\mathrm{IWH}
, called the derived subgroup.
In Lie-group terminology,
\mathrm{IWH}
is said to be solvable while
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{WH}
is said to be nilpotent or unipotent
Barut1980a
;
Knapp2002a
.
In particular, the derived series of
G
is

G=\mathrm{IWH}\vartriangleright\mathbb{C}\mathrm{WH}\vartriangleright Z\vartriangleright 1\,,

(69)

where defined is the
center
of
G
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptZ\equiv\left\{e^{i\Omega\phi}e^{-\Omega\ell}\,:\,\phi,\ell\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(70)

Before proceeding, it is useful to introduce the complex-amplitude (a.k.a. annihilation) operator

\displaystyle a\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(Q+iP)\,,

(71)

which has Lie bracket

\displaystyle[a,a^{\dagger}]=\Omega\,.

(72)

We have

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{o}}={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}(aa^{\dagger}+a^{\dagger}a)=a^{\dagger}a+{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\Omega\,.

(73)

Note also that the displacement operator,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}=e^{iQ\alpha_{2}-iP\alpha_{1}}=e^{a^{\dagger}\alpha-a\alpha^{*}}\,,

(74)

has the ordered forms,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}i\Omega\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}e^{iQ\alpha_{2}}e^{-iP\alpha_{1}}=e^{\frac{1}{2}i\Omega\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}e^{-iP\alpha_{1}}e^{iQ\alpha_{2}}

(75)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}\Omega}e^{a^{\dagger}\alpha}e^{-a\alpha^{*}}=e^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}\Omega}e^{-a\alpha^{*}}e^{a^{\dagger}\alpha}\,,

(76)

which will prove useful in relating coördinate systems and in evaluating right-invariant derivatives. The first form in equation
76
is usually called normal ordering, and the second form is called antinormal ordering.

II.3
Haar Measure, Dirac Delta, and Kraus-Operator Distribution Function

Many readers will interpret the groups defined in the previous section as matrix groups, where the observables are quantized in the usual way.
The exponentials can be understood more abstractly, however, as generating path-connections, and this way of thinking gives rise to what is called the
universal covering group

Poincare2010a
;
Weyl1913a
;
Bourbaki1989a
;
Pontrjagin1946a
;
Knapp2002a
;
CSJackson2023a
.
We will now start to consider more seriously the points of
\mathrm{IWH}
in this universal fashion and think of the instrument of SPQM as a representation of
G=\mathrm{IWH}
.
The map
L:\mathbb{C}^{T/dt}\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
from the set of paths,
\mathbb{C}^{T/dt}
, to the operator space,
\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
, can be factored into two maps,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptL=R\circ\gamma\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(77)

where
\gamma:\mathbb{C}^{T/dt}\longrightarrow G
maps Wiener paths to the universal covering group and
R:G\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
is the representation, mapping the universal cover to the space of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}

Knapp2002a
. We have denoted a sample-path by
dw_{[0,T)}
, and we now start denoting elements of the instrumental group by
x
. To drive the notation home, we note that the instrumental group element and Kraus operator associated with a sample path are denoted by

\displaystyle x_{T}=\gamma[dw_{[0,T)}]\qquad\quad\textrm{and}\qquad\quad L[dw_{[0,T)}]=R\big(x_{T}\big)\,.

(78)

The distinction between
L
and
\gamma
emphasizes that the time-ordered exponential of equation
57
actually defines a diffusion problem on the instrumental group that is independent of the spectral information inherent in the definition of a linear operator.
In particular, this means the entire analysis of this article is independent of whether or how
H_{\textrm{o}}
is quantized.
(Remember,
H_{\textrm{o}}
is quantized in the usual way the moment
\mathcal{H}
is assumed to be irreducible and to have a ground state.)

As does every finite-dimensional Lie group,
G=\mathrm{IWH}
has a right-invariant
Haar measure

Haar1933a
;
vonNeumann1999a
;
Nachbin1965a
;
Bourbaki2004a
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptd\mu(xg)=d\mu(x)\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(79)

As is
not
always the case for (solvable) Lie groups
Barut1980a
, it turns out that this right-invariant measure is also equal to the left-invariant measure,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptd\mu(x)=d\mu(gx)\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(80)

and this left invariance will turn out to be important for the interpretation of the SPQM process as a diffusion, as will be seen in the very next subsection.
Comments about the existence and uniqueness of the Haar measure will be given in appendices
D
and
E
, but first it is worth taking it for granted and appreciating what can be done with it.

With the Haar measure, we can introduce the accompanying singular distributions or “
Dirac deltas
,” defined by the property (sometimes called reproduction
Brif1999a
;
STAli1999a
) that for any function
f
of
G=\mathrm{IWH}
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\int_{G}d\mu(x)\,\delta(y,x)f(x)=f(y)\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(81)

From the invariance properties of the Haar distribution, the Dirac delta distributions inherit the corresponding covariance properties,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\delta(xg,yg)=\delta(x,y)=\delta(gx,gy)\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(82)

With the Haar measure and the Dirac delta, we can define a universal instrument by adding up all of the Wiener paths that end at the same Kraus operator, starting from the origin.
This becomes visible by considering the total operation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{T}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\Big(L\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\!\Big)

(83)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\Big(R\big(\gamma\big[dw_{[0,T)}\big]\big)\Big)

(84)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\Big(R\big(\gamma\big[dw_{[0,T)}\big]\big)\Big)\int_{G}d\mu(x)\,\delta\Big(x,\gamma\big[dw_{[0,T)}\big]\Big)

(85)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}d\mu(x)\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\big(R(x)\big)\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\delta\Big(x,\gamma\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\Big)\,.

(86)

In summary, the SPQM process defines a
universal instrument
, which unravels the total operation,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathcal{Z}_{T}=\int_{G}d\mu(x)\,D_{T}(x)\;{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\big(R(x)\big)\,,\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(87)

according to a Haar-based
Kraus-operator distribution function
(KOD),

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptD_{T}(x)\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\delta\Big(x,\gamma\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\Big)\,,\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(88)

which is defined by a Wiener path integral
Wiener1921b
;
Wiener1921a
;
Wiener1924a
;
Kac1947a
;
Kac1959a
;
Chaichian2001a
. The total operation is a completely positive, trace-preserving superoperator. The trace-preserving property is equivalent to saying that the POVM elements,
d\mu(x)\,D_{T}(x)\,R(x)^{\dagger}R(x)
, satisfy a completeness relation,

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{G}\!d\mu(x)\,D_{T}(x)\,R(x)^{\dagger}R(x)\,.

(89)

The term “universal” refers to the fact that this description of the instrument is common to every representation and comes from the concepts of universal covering group and universal enveloping algebra
Bourbaki1989a
;
Dixmier1996a
;
CSJackson2023a
.
It is important to understand that the universal covering group,
\mathrm{IWH}
, is defined purely by the local structure (that is, the Lie algebra) of the observables and the quadratic term, here
H_{\textrm{o}}
, which accompanies the observables due to the nature of differential positive transformations.
In particular, this means
G=\mathrm{IWH}
is not defined by the Hilbert space of states.
This ability to describe the measuring instrument without reference to a Hilbert space is so striking that we give it a name:
universal instrument autonomy

CSJackson2022a
;
CSJackson2023a
. SPQM is in a very special class of universal instruments for which the universal covering group is finite dimensional; we dub such instruments
principal instruments

CSJackson2023a
.

II.4
Diffusion Equation in Terms of Right-Invariant Derivatives

The definition of the Kraus-operator distribution function
88
can be thought of as a Feynman-Kac formula
Kac1947a
;
Kac1959a
;
Chaichian2001a
for the solution of a Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) diffusion equation
Gardiner2009a
;
Gardiner2021a
.
This FPK diffusion equation can be obtained easily with the help of the so-called right-invariant derivatives
Knapp1988a
;
Frankel2012a
;
Kitaev2018a
;
CSJackson2021a
;
CSJackson2023a
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\underleftarrow{X}[f](x)\equiv\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(e^{Xh}x)-f(x)}{h}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(90)

which can be seen to have commutators
CSJackson2023a

\underleftarrow{X}\underleftarrow{Y}-\underleftarrow{Y}\underleftarrow{X}=-\underleftarrow{[X,Y]}\,.

(91)

This negative sign is why left-invariant derivatives are usually considered instead of right-invariant ones.
Nevertheless, because the convention is to consider operators as acting to the right we are more-or-less stuck with having to consider a right-invariant basis of local transformations.

With the right-invariant derivatives,
D_{t+dt}
can then be expanded about
t
in the standard way. We start with

\displaystyle D_{t+dt}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,t+dt)}\right]\delta\Big(x,\gamma\!\left[dw_{[0,t+dt)}\right]\Big)

(92)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dw_{t})\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,t)}\right]\delta\Big(x,\gamma(dw_{t})\gamma\!\left[dw_{[0,t)}\right]\Big)\,,

(93)

where

\displaystyle\gamma(dw_{t})=e^{\updelta_{t}}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\updelta_{t}\equiv-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa\,dt+Q\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{q}+P\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW^{p}=-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa\,dt+a\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}^{*}+a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}

(94)

is the purely group-theoretic version of
L(dw_{t})
of equation
48
, that is,
L(dw_{t})=R\big(\gamma[dw_{t}]\big)
. Here we also define the
forward generator

\updelta_{t}
:
\gamma(dw_{t})=e^{\updelta_{t}}
is the fundamental differential positive operator for SPQM, and the forward generator
\updelta_{t}
is thus the core mathematical object for the theory of the SPQM instrument.
Continuing with equation
93
, we have

\displaystyle D_{t+dt}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dw_{t})\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,t)}\right]\delta\Big(\gamma(dw_{t})^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x,\gamma\!\left[dw_{[0,t)}\right]\Big)

(95)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dw_{t})\,D_{t}\big(\gamma(dw_{t})^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}x\big)

(96)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dw_{t})\,D_{t}\big(e^{-\updelta_{t}}x\big)

(97)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dw_{t})\,e^{-\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}}[D_{t}]\,\big(x\big)

(98)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dw_{t})\,\left(D_{t}(x)-\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}[D_{t}](x)+\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big[\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}[D_{t}]\big](x)\right)

(99)

\displaystyle=D_{t}(x)+\kappa\,dt\,\Delta\big[D_{t}](x)\,,

(100)

where

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\Delta\equiv 2\underleftarrow{H_{\textrm{o}}}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\underleftarrow{Q}\,\underleftarrow{Q}+\underleftarrow{P}\,\underleftarrow{P}\big)\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(101)

is the
FPK forward generator
.
Equation
95
is where the left invariance of the Haar measure is used.
Equation
96
is the analog of a Chapman-Kolmogorov equation for the distribution function
Gardiner2009a
;
Gardiner2021a
.
Equation
98
moves
e^{\updelta_{t}}
outside the argument of the KOD to become an exponential of the right-invariant derivative

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}=-\underleftarrow{H_{\textrm{o}}}2\kappa\,dt+\underleftarrow{Q}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{q}+\underleftarrow{P}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{p}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(102)

which we call the
vector-valued SPQM increment
.
Equation
99
Taylor expands the distribution function to second order, that is, to order
dt
, as required by the Itô rule for the Wiener outcome increments. The remaining step to the FPK forward generator
\Delta
involves averaging over the Wiener distribution
d\mu(dw_{t})
; in this averaging the deterministic term
-2\kappa\,dt\underleftarrow{H_{\textrm{o}}}
in
\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}
contributes a first-derivative term to the FPK forward generator, whereas the Wiener outcome increment terms in
\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}
contribute second-derivative diffusion terms.

In summary, the KOD of SPQM evolves according to the FPK diffusion equation,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}(x)=\Delta[D_{t}](x)\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(103)

with initial condition

D_{0}(x)=\delta(x,1)\,,

(104)

where defined is the group identity

1\equiv e^{0}\,,

(105)

which is the origin of
\mathrm{IWH}
.
Equation
103
will be (mostly) solved in section
III.5
after having established two coördinate systems. The subtlest thing about equation
103
is remembering that the Lie algebra of the three directions apparent in the equation means that the motion beyond first order is actually 7-dimensional.

Before proceeding to SDEs, we draw attention to an important property of right-invariant derivatives. The reader might already be thinking about this property by wondering why we didn’t write
\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}
and
\Delta

and

\mathbb{R}
-linear and not
\mathbb{C}
-linear

. This means that
\underleftarrow{Q}
,
\underleftarrow{P}
,
\underleftarrow{iQ}
, and
-\underleftarrow{iP}
are
\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent, with
\underleftarrow{Q}
and
\underleftarrow{P}
displacing Kraus operators in positive directions on IWH and
\underleftarrow{iQ}
and

\underleftarrow{a^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}=-\underleftarrow{H_{\textrm{o}}}2\kappa\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}\big(\underleftarrow{a}+\underleftarrow{a^{\dagger}}+i\,\underleftarrow{ia}-i\,\underleftarrow{ia^{\dagger}}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}^{*}\big(\underleftarrow{a}+\underleftarrow{a^{\dagger}}-i\,\underleftarrow{ia}+i\,\underleftarrow{ia^{\dagger}}\big)

(106)

This means, in particular, that

\displaystyle\Delta\neq 2\underleftarrow{H_{\textrm{o}}}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\underleftarrow{a}\,\underleftarrow{a^{\dagger}}+\underleftarrow{a^{\dagger}}\underleftarrow{a}\big)\,.

II.5
Sample-Path SDEs in Terms of Right-Invariant One-Forms

As has been mentioned, the time-ordered exponential of the SPQM process (equation
57
) can be interpreted as defining sample-paths in the 7-dimensional manifold
G=\mathrm{IWH}
.
Sample-paths are usually described by stochastic-differential equations (SDEs). We finish this section by explaining how such SDEs can be expressed in terms of the right-invariant structure.

The basis of right-invariant derivatives,

\Big\{\underleftarrow{e_{\nu}}\Big\}\equiv\Big\{-\underleftarrow{H_{\textrm{o}}},\underleftarrow{Q},\underleftarrow{P},-\underleftarrow{\,iP},\underleftarrow{\,iQ},-\underleftarrow{\Omega},\underleftarrow{\,i\Omega}\Big\}\,,

(108)

defines a dual basis of right-invariant one-forms,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\theta^{\mu}\!\Big(\underleftarrow{e_{\nu}}\Big)\equiv\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(109)

In terms of the right-invariant one-forms, the Haar measure has a simple expression in terms of wedge products,

d\mu(x)=\theta^{-H_{\textrm{o}}}\wedge\theta^{Q}\wedge\theta^{P}\wedge\theta^{iQ}\wedge\theta^{-iP}\wedge\theta^{-\Omega}\wedge\theta^{i\Omega}.

(110)

Also in terms of the right-invariant one-forms, the SDEs equivalent to equation
57
are given by reading off the coefficient conjugate to the corresponding generator in the vector-valued SPQM increment
102
,

\displaystyle\theta^{i\Omega}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(111)

\displaystyle\theta^{-\Omega}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(112)

\displaystyle\theta^{-iP}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(113)

\displaystyle\theta^{iQ}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(114)

\displaystyle\theta^{Q}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{q}\,,

(115)

\displaystyle\theta^{P}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{p}\,,

(116)

\displaystyle\theta^{-H_{\textrm{o}}}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)

\displaystyle=2\kappa\,dt\,.

(117)

These SDEs can be broken into two types:
the first-order SDEs,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\theta^{-H_{\textrm{o}}}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=2\kappa\,dt\,,\hskip 15.0pt\theta^{Q}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{q}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\theta^{P}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dW_{t}^{p}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(118)

and the Pfaffians,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\theta^{-iP}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=\theta^{iQ}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=\theta^{-\Omega}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=\theta^{i\Omega}\big(\underleftarrow{\updelta_{t}}\big)=0\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(119)

These equations will be solved in section
III.4
after having established a coördinate system.

The stochastic equations
118
and
119
are almost obvious by definition, but there is a subtlety that requires attention. The right-invariant derivatives and one-forms live in the tangent and cotangent spaces to the group manifold
G
and thus are based on linear transformations that use the chain rule of ordinary calculus. Hence the stochastic equations that come from the right-invariant one-forms are Stratonovich-form SDEs
Gardiner2009a
;
Gardiner2021a
—this means mid-point evaluation of coefficients of stochastic increments—and should be converted to the Itô-form SDEs in which coefficients are evaluated at the beginning of the increment. In the context of IWH, the only place this subtlety makes a difference is in the SDEs that come from
\theta^{i\Omega}
and
\theta^{-\Omega}
. Jackson and Caves
CSJackson2021a
;
CSJackson2023a
introduced the
modified Maurer-Cartan stochastic differential
(MMCSD) as a way to get to the Itô-form equations directly. The MMCSD is an example of the Itô correction in SDEs
Gardiner2009a
;
Gardiner2021a
, specifically the Itô correction that arises when one transforms between a stochastic variable and the exponential function of that variable, as occurs in the forward generator
94
. In this paper we get directly to Itô-form SDEs in a different way, when we consider the Harish-Chandra decomposition in section
III.2
.

A further subtlety about the right-invariant one-forms is that they have “curl” in the same sense as Gibbs would have defined.
In the standard language of forms, this is because the exterior algebra of forms is equivalent to the Lie algebra of derivatives
Knapp1988a
,

[\underleftarrow{e_{\mu}},\underleftarrow{e_{\nu}}]=-{c_{\mu\nu}}^{\lambda}\underleftarrow{e_{\lambda}}\hskip 20.0pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 20.0ptd\theta^{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}{c_{\mu\nu}}^{\lambda}\,\theta^{\mu}\!\wedge\theta^{\nu}\,.

(120)

This equivalence is standard in modern differential topology, but an introduction is included in appendix
A
; though no use will be made of the “curls”
d\theta^{\lambda}
in this article, they are given for completeness in equations
360
–
366
.

III.1
Usual Elements of Semisimple Lie Group Theory

As introduced in the previous section, the Lie group of interest is the so-called Instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg Group

G=\mathrm{IWH}\equiv\left\{e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r}e^{Qq}e^{Pp}D_{\alpha}\,e^{i\Omega\phi}e^{-\Omega\ell}\,:\,r\in\mathbb{R},\;q,p\in\mathbb{R},\;\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\;\phi,\ell\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\,.

(121)

Although
G
is literally solvable, with derived series,

G=\mathrm{IWH}\vartriangleright\mathbb{C}\mathrm{WH}\vartriangleright Z\vartriangleright 1\,,

(122)

and center,

Z\equiv\left\{e^{i\Omega\phi}e^{-\Omega\ell}\,:\,\phi,\ell\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

(123)

it can be navigated in much the same way as a semisimple group.
While a bit of the terminology
Knapp2002a
will be used here, the theory of semisimple groups will be more-or-less glossed over.
The purpose of this subsection is basically to label the various subgroups that will prove to be both meaningful and useful for navigating
\mathrm{IWH}
and therefore understanding
SPQM
.
The significance of these subgroups should become apparent as they are applied.

The map from the SPQM instrument to the SPQM POVM,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\pi(x)=x^{\dagger}x\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(124)

defines the SPQM POVM as similar to a symmetric space, albeit a nonRiemannian one, with
Cartan group involution

x^{\iota}\equiv x^{-{\dagger}}=(x^{{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}})^{\dagger}=(x^{{\dagger}})^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\,.

(125)

The subgroup of transformations that are even under the Cartan involution is the usual Unitary Weyl-Heisenberg Group of equation
44
:

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptK\equiv\Big\{x\in G\,:\,x^{\iota}=x\Big\}=\pi^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}(1)=\mathrm{UWH}\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(126)

On the other hand, the remainder of
G
displaces from the origin of the
symmetric space
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathcal{E}\equiv\pi(G)\cong K\backslash G\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(127)

Considering the conjugation action of
K
on
\mathcal{E}
, almost all of the
K
-conjugacy classes can be parameterized by the
Cartan subgroup
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptA\equiv\left\{e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r}e^{-\Omega\ell}\,:\,r,\ell\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(128)

and regular POVM elements are invariant under the
commutant
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptM\equiv\left\{k\in K\,:\,\forall a\in A,kak^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=a\right\}=\left\{e^{i\Omega\phi}\,:\,\phi\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(129)

Thus the
K
-conjugacy classes have the topology of the familiar phase space,

K/M\cong\mathbb{C}\,.

(130)

Indeed, it is the “almost all” feature where
G
and
\mathcal{E}
depart from semisimple groups and Riemannian symmetric spaces, since positive transformations of the form
e^{Qq+Pp}
are characteristically not in the conjugacy classes of
A
(see appendix
B
for additional perspective).
Finally, important also are the
maximal nilpotent (or unipotent) subgroup
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptN\equiv\left\{e^{a\mu^{*}}\,:\,\mu\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(131)

and perhaps the most important, the
Borel subgroup
,

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptB\equiv A\ltimes N=\left\{e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r}e^{-\Omega\ell}e^{a\mu^{*}}\,:\,r,\ell\in\mathbb{R},\;\mu\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(132)

This group-theoretic context now in hand, we stress that the most important groups for what follows are
G=\textrm{IWH}
itself, the center
Z
of
G
, and the quotient group
G/Z=\mathrm{IWH}/Z
. The center
Z
contains a phase, which is of no importance, and a normalization, which is the main source of difficulty in analyzing SPQM. The cosets

are parametrized by what we call the ruler,
r
, and by two complex phase-space parameters. One of these complex phase-space parameters is associated with the POVM, and the other parametrizes a post-measurement displacement operator. We call

the

(RIWH). It is isomorphic to the adjoint group of

as a central extension of
G/Z

III.2
Harish-Chandra Decomposition and SDEs as a Proof by Transfinite Induction

The 7-dimensional Instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg Group
G
affords a
Harish-Chandra decomposition
,
G=N^{\dagger}\!MAN
, where every element can be decomposed into the form

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptx=e^{a^{\dagger}\nu}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r+\Omega z}e^{a\mu^{*}}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(133)

defining seven
Harish-Chandra coördinates

(\nu,r,z,\mu)\in\mathbb{C}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{C}\!\times\!\mathbb{C}

We often break the complex coördinates into real and imaginary parts,

\nu=\frac{\nu_{1}+i\nu_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\hskip 20.0pt\mu=\frac{\mu_{1}+i\mu_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\hskip 20.0ptz=-s+i\psi\,.

(134)

The coördinate
r
we call the ruler,
\nu
and
\mu
are the postmeasurement and POVM phase-plane coördinates, and
z
is the IWH center coördinate.

A proof that this decomposition exists for every element of the SPQM process (equation
57
) is not difficult if we allow ourselves to apply transfinite induction:
At time
t=dt
(the first infinitesimal increment), it is easy to see that the decomposition exists because (see equation
94
)

\displaystyle x_{dt}=e^{\updelta}

\displaystyle=e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa\,dt+a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}+a\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}^{*}}

(135)

\displaystyle=e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa dt}e^{a^{\dagger}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}+a\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}^{*}}

(136)

\displaystyle=e^{a^{\dagger}\!\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa dt+\Omega\frac{1}{2}\kappa|dw_{0}|^{2}}e^{a\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}^{*}}.

(137)

Trivially, this also means that the decomposition exists for any finite integer
n
and infinitesimal time
t=ndt
simply because the one-parameter subgroups commute to infinitesimal order, so long as the Wiener increments are independent. Now for the transfinite step.
If we assume that the decomposition holds for a finite time
t
,

x_{t}=e^{a^{\dagger}\nu_{t}}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r_{t}+\Omega z_{t}}e^{a\mu_{t}^{*}}\,,

(138)

then an increment later in the SPQM process we have

\displaystyle x_{t+dt}

\displaystyle=e^{\updelta_{t}}x_{t}

(139)

\displaystyle=e^{a^{\dagger}\!\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa dt+\Omega\frac{1}{2}\kappa|dw_{t}|^{2}}e^{a\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}^{*}}\;e^{a^{\dagger}\nu_{t}}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r_{t}+\Omega z_{t}}e^{a\mu_{t}^{*}}

(140)

\displaystyle=e^{a^{\dagger}\!\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2\kappa dt}\;e^{a^{\dagger}\nu_{t}}e^{a\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}^{*}}\;e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r_{t}+\Omega(z_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa|dw_{t}|^{2}+\nu_{t}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}^{*})}e^{a\mu_{t}^{*}}

(141)

\displaystyle=e^{a^{\dagger}(e^{-2\kappa dt}\nu_{t}+\!\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t})}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}(r+2\kappa dt)+\Omega(z+\frac{1}{2}\kappa|dw_{t}|^{2}+\nu_{t}\sqrt{\kappa}dw_{t}^{*})}e^{a(\mu_{t}+e^{-r_{t}}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t})^{*}}\,.

(142)

This concludes the proof of the Harish-Chandra decomposition for the SPQM process and
G=\mathrm{IWH}
.

A consequence of the proof is that the SPQM process in Harish-Chandra coördinates is equivalent to the Itô-form SDEs

;
Ito1996a
;
Chirikjian2009a
;

\displaystyle dr_{t}

\displaystyle=2\kappa\,dt\,,

\displaystyle d\nu_{t}

\displaystyle=-2\kappa\,dt\,\nu_{t}+\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}\,,

(144)

\displaystyle d\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{-r_{t}}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}\,,

(145)

\displaystyle-ds_{t}+id\psi_{t}=dz_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\kappa|dw_{t}|^{2}+\nu_{t}\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{t}^{*}\,.

(146)

in the SDE for the center coördinate

III.3
Cartan Decomposition and Various Transformations

As far as what the SPQM process is ultimately doing in time, section
III.2
in many ways says it all—except for that elephant in the room. We now turn our attention to a detailed understanding of the measuring process at finite times, which requires addressing the elephant.
Key to this is the realization that the Harish-Chandra decomposition is not well suited to telling us the behavior of the
POVM
.
Rather, a Cartan decomposition of the Instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg Group,
G=KAK
, works best for this purpose.
A straightforward calculation (left to appendix

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptx=\left(D_{\beta}\,e^{i\Omega\phi}\right)e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r-\Omega\ell}D_{\alpha}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(156)

and therefore the POVM elements decompose as

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptx^{\dagger}x=D_{\alpha}\,e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2r-\Omega 2\ell}D_{\alpha}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(157)

where the

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{r}\nu+\mu}{2\sinh r}\,,

(158)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{r}\mu+\nu}{2\sinh r}\,,

(159)

\displaystyle\ell

\displaystyle=s-f\,,

(160)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\psi-\xi\,.

(161)

Here

\xi

is quantized in the standard way, we have at late times,

\displaystyle D_{\beta}\,e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r}D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\big|_{T\gg 1/\kappa}\sim e^{-\kappa T}D_{\beta_{T}}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|D_{\alpha_{T}}^{\dagger}=e^{-\kappa T}|\beta_{T}\rangle\langle\alpha_{T}|\,.

(230)

This makes clear that

is the elephant in the room hinted at in section

: it is the phase-space weighting function that is crucial for the POVM completeness relation, which takes the form

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{G}\!d^{7}\!\mu(x)\,D_{T}(x)\,x^{\dagger}x=\int_{{\raisebox{-1.5pt}{$\scriptstyle G/Z$}}}\!\!\!\!\!d^{5}\!\mu(Zx)\,C_{T}(Zx)\,D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}2r}D_{\alpha}\,.

(231)

As an elephant, however,
C_{t}(Zx)
is not normalized to unity—indeed, its normalization is ill-defined. Moreover,

is not the weight function whose moments are those of the Cartan phase-point variables
\beta

\alpha

over the center

(232)

This distribution is normalized and has finite moments, those coming from the SDE solutions

cannot possibly give rise to a POVM completeness relation; it will not be seen again in this paper.

To derive an evolution equation for

of equation

of equation

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}C_{t}(Zx)=\Big({-}2\partial_{r}-2\coth r+\nabla^{*}\nabla\Big)[C_{t}](Zx)\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(233)

where recall that

. This PDE is ballistic in the ruler
r
—solution proportional to
\delta(r-2\kappa t)
—and Gaussian preserving in the phase-space variables, but as a consequence of the

term, the PDE does not preserve normalization

;
Kac1959a
;
Chaichian2001a
.

To solve for

requires knowing

, which is, when

\delta

(234)

The distinctive feature of this distribution is the wide, normalized Gaussian in
\beta+\alpha

\beta+\alpha

\phi=\ell=r=0
and
\beta=\alpha
, with
\beta+\alpha
free to take on any complex value. With this expression, it is easy to see that

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptC_{dt}(Zx)=\int_{Z}\!d\phi\,d\ell\,D_{dt}(x)e^{-2\ell}=\frac{2}{r}\delta(r-2\kappa dt)\,\pi\delta^{2}(\beta-\alpha)\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

The integral of this distribution has a zero from the

behavior multiplying the

and an infinity from the uniformity in

The initial condition

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0ptC_{T}(Zx)=\frac{1}{\sinh r}\delta(r-2\kappa T)\,2\pi\frac{1}{\pi\Sigma_{T}}e^{-|\beta-\alpha|^{2}/\Sigma_{T}}\,.\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pt\mathcal{Z}_{T}=\int d^{5}\!\mu(Zx)\,C_{T}(Zx)\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\!\left(D_{\beta}\,e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}r}D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\right)\,,\hskip 15.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

There are four notable features in the temporal behavior of the reduced SPQM instrument:

1.

The ruler
r
(or purity parameter) is ballistic, which means that

collapses exponentially to
e^{-\kappa T}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
in the standard quantization. More generally,
D_{\beta}\,e^{-rH_{\textrm{o}}}D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}
collapses exponentially at late times to an outer product of coherent states,
e^{-\kappa T}|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|
.

2.

The dependence on the future and past phase-space parameters,
\beta
and
\alpha
, is only in their difference.

3.

The distribution of the difference spreads out very slowly for small times, as
\Sigma_{T}\propto T^{3}
, and then for long times becomes normal diffusion, with
\Sigma_{T}\propto T
.

4.

There is a center normalization,
2\sinh 2\kappa T
, that increases over time.

This center normalization is the focus of the next section, which finds that the elephant provides an alternative perspective on the quantum.

III.6
POVM as an Alternative Perspective on the Quantum

The center normalization just mentioned is remarkable in that it is equivalent to traditional energy quantization.
Specifically, the completeness relation
231
for the SPQM process is

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{H}}=2\sinh 2\kappa T\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}D_{\alpha}\,e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\int\frac{d^{2}\beta}{\pi\Sigma_{T}}e^{-|\beta-\alpha|^{2}/\Sigma_{T}}=2\sinh 2\kappa T\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}D_{\alpha}\,e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\,.

(243)

It is important to appreciate that for late times
T\gg 1/\kappa
, when
e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}
collapses to
e^{-2\kappa T}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
in the standard quantization, this completeness relation becomes the coherent-state resolution of the identity of equation
155
:

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{H}}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}|\alpha\rangle\!\langle\alpha|\,.

(244)

The completeness relation says much more, however, when considered for arbitrary times
T
. If
\mathcal{H}
is an irreducible representation, Schur’s lemma says that

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{\pi}D_{\alpha}\,e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}D_{\alpha}^{\dagger}=1_{\mathcal{H}}\tr\!\big(e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}\big)\,.

(245)

The trace, which one recognizes as a partition function, is defined within the representation and is evaluated using traditional energy quantization as

\displaystyle\tr\big(e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}\big)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-(n+\frac{1}{2})4\kappa T}=\frac{1}{2\sinh 2\kappa T}\,.

(246)

The center normalization in the POVM completeness relation thus evaluates the partition function of
e^{-H_{\textrm{o}}4\kappa T}
without using traditional energy quantization. This is to be expected in view of the Stone-von-Neumann theorem, but expected though it is, please appreciate how different the setting of this paper is from the original ideas of energy quantization and thermal equilibrium. Remember that here the operator
H_{\textrm{o}}
comes from the trace-preserving character of the instrument. It is not the energy of the system; indeed we have explicitly eschewed any notion of system energy or Hamiltonian. The operator
H_{\textrm{o}}
plays the role of a “dissipator,” specifically a dissipator that damps the POVM to the coherent states, but there is no notion of energy associated with this dissipation. The coherent states and
H_{\textrm{o}}
arise within a group structure constructed solely from the measured observables,
Q
and
P
. Moreover, the parameter conjugate to
H_{\textrm{o}}
, the ruler
r
, is quite literally time, rather than an inverse temperature. It seems remarkable that this result holds, from the completeness of the SPQM POVM, without any assumption of a Hamiltonian, a ground state, or thermal equilibrium.

IV
Reduced Distribution Functions and Feynman-Kac Path Integrals

This section further considers reduced distribution functions, their path-integral expressions and diffusion equations, and their relation to SDEs.
The path-integral expression,

D_{T}(x)=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]\delta(x,\gamma[dw_{[0,T)}])\,,

(247)

is generally considered to be a Feynman-Kac formula

;
Kac1959a
;
Chaichian2001a

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{T}=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\Big(R\big(\gamma\big[dw_{[0,T)}\big]\big)\Big)\,.

Equation

and a point in the group manifold IWH,
\gamma\big[dw_{[0,T)}\big]
and in turn the overall Kraus operator

Equation
247
defines the Kraus-operator distribution function (KOD) as the amalgamation of all paths that lead to the same Kraus operator.
The KOD inherits the path-integral expression from equation
248

The reason the path integral is called a Feynman-Kac formula is that everybody after Kac thinks about going in the opposite direction, starting with the diffusion equation and formulating an equivalent path integral.

Section

reviews the Cartan-section reduced distribution

of Eq. (

Section

Section
IV.3
formulates the diffusion equations for

and

, and section

solves the path integral for

using the stochastic integrals for the Harish-Chandra phase-space coördinates.

IV.2
Normalized Harish-Chandra Reduced Distribution Function and Modified Path-Integration Measure

The Feynman-Kac formula
260
for
B_{T}(Zx)
suggests that we combine
e^{-2s[dw_{[0,T)}]}
with the Weiner measure
56
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]e^{-2s[dw_{[0,T)}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{T/dt-1}\!d^{\,2}\!\big(dw_{kdt}\big)\right)\!\!\left(\frac{1}{\pi dt}\right)^{T/dt}\exp\!\left({-}\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\frac{|dw_{t}|^{2}}{dt}+\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\!\!\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\!\!\kappa\,dw_{t}^{*}\,dw_{s}\,e^{-2\kappa|t-s|}\right)\,.

(265)

The quadratic functional in the exponential can be written as

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\frac{|dw_{t}|^{2}}{dt}+\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\!\!\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\!\!\kappa\,dw_{t}^{*}\,dw_{s}\,e^{-2\kappa|t-s|}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{dt}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}dw_{t}^{*}\,dw_{s}\Big(\delta_{ts}-\kappa\,dt\,e^{-2\kappa|t-s|}\Big)

(266)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{dt}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}dw_{k}^{*}\,dw_{l}\Big(\delta_{kl}-\kappa\,dt\,e^{-2\kappa dt\,|k-l|}\Big)\,.

(267)

When converting between stochastic integrals and sums, we use
t_{k}=k\,dt
(
t_{N}=T=N\,dt
) and
dw_{k}=dw_{kdt}=dw_{t_{k}}
. Now define the real, symmetric, and positive
N\times N
matrix
M_{T}
, whose matrix elements are

\displaystyle M_{kl}=\delta_{kl}-\kappa\,dt\,e^{-2\kappa\,dt\,|k-l|}\,,

(268)

It is elegant for various formal expressions to introduce a continuous version of
M_{T}
, but we do not bother with that here, since we work with the sums that the stochastic integrals represent. Notice that
M_{T}
is a Toeplitz matrix, that is,
M_{k+j,l+j}=M_{kl}
. Putting this together, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]e^{-2s[dw_{[0,T)}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\!d^{\,2}\!\big(dw_{kdt}\big)\right)\!\!\left(\frac{1}{\pi dt}\right)^{N}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{dt}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}dw_{k}^{*}\,M_{kl}\,dw_{l}\right)\,,

(269)

which integrates over the Wiener outcome paths to

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]e^{-2s[dw_{[0,T)}]}=\left(\frac{1}{\pi dt}\right)^{N}\frac{\pi^{N}}{\det(M_{T}/dt)}=\frac{1}{\det M_{T}}\,.

(270)

This prompts us to define a new (normalized, zero-mean) Gaussian measure on the Wiener outcome paths,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\mu_{M}\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\det M_{T}\,\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]e^{-2s[dw_{[0,T)}]}

(271)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}d^{\,2}\!\big(dw_{k}\big)\right)\left(\frac{\det M_{T}}{(\pi dt)^{N}}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{dt}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}dw_{k}^{*}\,M_{kl}\,dw_{l}\right)\,.

(272)

Relative to this measure, the outcome increments are correlated,

\displaystyle\langle dw_{k}^{*}\,dw_{l}\rangle_{M}=dt\,(M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})_{kl}\,.

(273)

Notice that the increment correlation
\langle dw_{k}^{*}\,dw_{l}\rangle_{M}
, with
k
and

held fixed, changes as the total time

changes. The inverse matrix
M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}
matrix is real, symmetric, and positive, all properties inherited from
M_{T}
. The inverse does not inherit the Toeplitz property of
M_{T}
. That
M_{T}
is Toeplitz implies that it is persymmetric, that is, symmetric across the anti-diagonal;
M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}
does inherit the persymmetry, which turns out to have an important consequence in section

.

Returning to the Harish-Chandra reduced distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\int d^{5}\!\mu(Zx)\,B_{T}(Zx)

(274)

\displaystyle=\int d^{5}\!\mu(Zx)\,C_{T}(Zx)e^{-2f(Zx)}

(275)

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\!d^{7}\!\mu(x)\,D_{T}(x)e^{-2s}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]e^{-2s[dw_{[0,T)}]}=\frac{1}{\det M_{T}}\,.

(277)

The normalized version of the Harish-Chandra reduced distribution is

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{T}(Zx)\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{T}}B_{T}(Zx)=\frac{e^{-2f(Zx)}}{\mathcal{N}_{T}}C_{T}(Zx)\,.

(278)

Section
IV.3
finds the diffusion equations satisfied by the reduced Harish-Chandra distributions, and section

(279)

to evaluate

V
Concluding Remarks. The Stochastic Trinity

We set out on the project of analyzing simultaneous measurements of noncommuting observables
CSJackson2021a
;
CSJackson2023a
with the goal of showing that such measurements end up with a POVM in the overcomplete coherent-state basis. We now think that we have uncovered something more ambitious, a distinctive new window into the space of quantum dynamics. The formulation of the problem of continual, differential measurements invites one—compels one, really—to think in terms of the paths of Wiener outcome increments. These outcome paths, as sample-paths drawn from the Wiener measure, know nothing about a space in which they are wandering. When they are instantiated in the exponents of Kraus operators, however, the time-ordered products of the differential Kraus operators generate a (complex) Lie-group manifold, the instrumental Lie group, in which the Kraus operators are, in the way of groups, both the transformations and the moving points. The motion in the instrumental Lie-group manifold is described by Kraus-operator SDEs or by the diffusion of the KOD, as embodied in a FPK diffusion equation. The continuous, but not differentiable paths are handled effortlessly by the Itô calculus of the outcome increments, with its terms of order
\sqrt{dt}
and
dt
. This requires getting just beyond the linear structure of vector fields (right-invariant derivatives) and one-forms (right-invariant one-forms) on the Lie-group manifold, as is evident from our discussion of Stratonovich vs. Itô SDEs and in the derivation of the FPK diffusion equation, where right-invariant derivatives end up as diffusive second derivatives. We end up in a very comfortable place, working in all three corners of the stochastic trinity: path integrals, FPK diffusion equations, and SDEs, all three describing motion, equivalently, on the instrumental Lie-group manifold.

Why don’t others find the same comfort in all three faces of the trinity? Field theorists, interested in the propagator of closed-system dynamics, have a different way of handling the continuous, but not differentiable paths, coming from a Stratonovich-calculus way of dealing with the temporal derivatives in the kinetic terms in a Lagrangian. While they usually have an equivalent Schrödinger-like equation for the propagator, they do not have the analog of SDEs, even though the problem often undergoes a Wick rotation into “Euclidean spacetime.”

Open-systems theorists, both in condensed-matter physics and in quantum optics, generally work with master equations or stochastic master equations for the evolution of quantum states and sometimes with diffusion equations for a probability distribution associated with the states. Those who start with diffusion equations can avail themselves of a Feynman-Kac formula for a path integral, but the connection to SDEs has generally not been made. The reason for not using all three faces of the stochastic trinity is, we think, a failure to identify the appropriate Lie-group manifold; this failure is connected to the emphasis on the evolution of quantum states, which obscures nearly entirely the Lie-group manifold on which the open-system dynamics is occurring.

Suffice it to say that we think we have found something: the home of quantum dynamics, the Lie-group manifold that supports all three faces of the trinity.
The exhausted reader who has survived to read this concluding sentence of a very long paper might be pleased—or so we hope—to learn that our ambition is larger than was evident at the beginning.

In this appendix we find the Cartan form of the
\delta
-function

in two ways. The first uses that

\displaystyle\qquad\mu_{dt}=d\mu_{0}=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dw_{0}\,,

Appendix G

In this appendix we derive th Ricatti equations

,

, and

, from their expressions in equations

,
319
, and

\mathcal{D}\mu_{M}\!\left[dw_{[0,T)}\right]
of equation

within the interval
[0,T)
, depends on the overall time

and to increment the overall time from

It is quite convenient to introduce a bra-ket notation and to define vectors

\displaystyle|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{dT}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,(N-k)}|k\rangle\,,

(501)

\displaystyle|{\mathord{-}}_{T}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{dT}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,k}|k\rangle\,,

(502)

where the kets
|k\rangle
are orthonormal. The matrix elements of
M_{T}
are

\displaystyle(M_{T})_{kl}=\langle k|M_{T}|l\rangle=\delta_{kl}-\kappa\,dT\,e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,|k-l|}\,,\quad k,l=0,\ldots,N-1.

(503)

The utility of the bra-ket notation becomes apparent when we write the three moments as matrix elements of
M_{T}
,

\displaystyle n_{T}

\displaystyle=\kappa{}\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|M_{T}^{-1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\,,

(504)

\displaystyle m_{T}

\displaystyle=\kappa\langle{\mathord{-}}_{T}|M_{T}^{-1}|{\mathord{-}}_{T}\rangle\,,

(505)

\displaystyle q_{T}

\displaystyle=\kappa\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|M_{T}^{-1}|{\mathord{-}}_{T}\rangle=\kappa\langle{\mathord{-}}_{T}|M_{T}^{-1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\,.

(506)

Replacing
M_{T}
and
M_{t}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}
with the unit matrix gives the moments with respect to the original Wiener measure; these moments are the inner products of the kets
|\pm_{T}\rangle
.

Now increment
M_{T}
forward one step in time, from
T
to

. What happens is really nothing,

\displaystyle\langle k|M_{T+dT}|l\rangle=\delta_{kl}-\kappa\,dT\,e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,|k-l|}\,,\quad k,l=0,\ldots,N,

(507)

except that an outside row and column, labeled by the index
N
, are added,

\displaystyle\vphantom{\Big(}k,l=0,\ldots,N-1:\quad

\displaystyle\langle k|M_{T+dT}|l\rangle=\langle k|M_{T}|l\rangle=\delta_{kl}-\kappa\,dT\,e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,|k-l|}\,,

(508)

\displaystyle\vphantom{\Big(}k=0,\ldots,N-1:\quad

\displaystyle\langle k|M_{T+dT}|N\rangle=-\kappa\,dT\,e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,(N-k)}=-\kappa\sqrt{dT}\,\langle k|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\,,

(509)

\displaystyle\vphantom{\Big(}l=0,\ldots,N-1:\quad

\displaystyle\langle N|M_{T+dT}|l\rangle=-\kappa\,dT\,e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,(N-k)}=-\kappa\sqrt{dT}\,\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|l\rangle\,,

(510)

\displaystyle\langle N|M_{T+dT}|N\rangle=1-\kappa\,dT\,.

(511)

The incremented matrix can then be written in block form relative to the outer row and column,

\displaystyle M_{T+dT}=M_{T}-\kappa\sqrt{dT}\big(|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\langle N|+|N\rangle\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|\big)+(1-\kappa\,dT)|N\rangle\langle N|\,.

(512)

We also need to increment the kets

\displaystyle|{\mathord{+}}_{T+dT}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{dT}\,\sum_{k=0}^{N}e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,(N+1-k)}|k\rangle=e^{-2\kappa\,dT}\big(|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle+\sqrt{dT}\,|N\rangle\big)\,,

(513)

\displaystyle|{\mathord{-}}_{T+dT}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{dT}\,\sum_{k=0}^{N}e^{-2\kappa\,dT\,k}|k\rangle=|{\mathord{-}}_{T}\rangle+\sqrt{dT}\,e^{-2\kappa T}|N\rangle\,.

(514)

The harder task, incrementing

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\,,

(515)

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
CA^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&M/A\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&A^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}B\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\,,

(516)

where

\displaystyle M/A\equiv D-CA^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}B

(517)

\displaystyle\det M=\det A\,\det(M/A)\,,

(518)

and the second thing is that the inverse of

\displaystyle M^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=\begin{pmatrix}\vphantom{\Big(}A^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}+A^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}B(M/A)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}CA^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}&\quad&-A^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}B(M/A)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\\
\vphantom{\Big(}-(M/A)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}CA^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}&\quad&(M/A)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(519)

In the case at hand, the block form

\displaystyle=M_{T}\,,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=-\kappa\sqrt{dT}\,|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\langle N|\,,

(521)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=-\kappa\sqrt{dT}\,|N\rangle\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|\,,

(522)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa\,dT)|N\rangle\langle N|\,.

(523)

The Schur complement is the
1\times 1
matrix

\displaystyle M_{T+dT}/M_{T}=\big(1-\kappa\,dT-\kappa^{2}\,dT\,\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|M_{T}^{-1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\big)|N\rangle\langle N|=\big[1-\kappa\,dT(1+n_{T})\big]|N\rangle\langle N|\,.

(524)

One has immediately how the determinant advances by one increment,

\displaystyle\det M_{T+dT}=\det M_{T}\big(1-\kappa\,dT(1+n_{T})\big)\,,

(525)

and thus the ODE

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{d\ln\det M_{T}}{dT}=-(1+n_{T})\,,

(526)

which agrees with the differential equation
304
for the normalization factor
\mathcal{N}_{T}=1/\det M_{T}
. Further, one can work out from equation
519
how the inverse increments,

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{T+dT}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}&=M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}+\kappa^{2}\,dT\,M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\\
&\qquad+\kappa\sqrt{dT}\,\big(M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\langle N|+|N\rangle\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\big)\\
&\qquad+\big[1+\kappa\,dT\big(1+\kappa\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T}|M_{T}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T}\rangle\big)\big]|N\rangle\langle N|\,.\end{split}

(527)

where we keep only the leading-order term in
dT
in each block.

Everything is set now to determine how the three moments increment,

\displaystyle n_{T+dT}

\displaystyle=\kappa\langle{\mathord{+}}_{T+dT}|M_{T+dT}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T+dT}\rangle=n_{T}+\kappa\,dT\,(1-n_{T})^{2}\,,

(528)

\displaystyle m_{T+dT}

\displaystyle=\kappa\langle{\mathord{-}}_{T+dT}|M_{T+dT}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|{\mathord{-}}_{T+dT}\rangle=m_{T}+\kappa\,dT\,\big(q_{T}+e^{-2\kappa T}\big)^{2}\,,

(529)

\displaystyle q_{T+dT}

\displaystyle=\kappa\langle{\mathord{-}}_{T+dT}|M_{T+dT}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|{\mathord{+}}_{T+dT}\rangle=q_{T}+\kappa\,dT\,\big({-}q_{T}(1-n_{T})+e^{-2\kappa T}(1+n_{T})\big)\,,

(530)

(532)
