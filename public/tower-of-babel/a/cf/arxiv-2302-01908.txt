Linear Absorption Spectrum of the Spin-Boson Model Studied by Extended Hierarchical Equations of Motion
Qianlong Wang, Jianlan Wu

Abstract
With a decomposition scheme for the bath correlation function, the hierarchical equations of motion (HEOM) are extended to the zero-temperature sub-Ohmic and Ohmic spin-boson model. We investigate the linear absorption spectrum of the sub-Ohmic and Ohmic spin-boson model at zero temperature. By applying the extended HEOM, the equilibrium spin dynamics are obtained approximately. Then the linear response function is calculated according to the Kubo formula rewritten in Liouville space. To explore the essence of phase transition, we compute the linear absorption spectrum defined by the linear response function of a dipole moment. By analyzing the peak structure of the linear response spectrum, we get the dependence of linear absorption spectrum with Kondo parameter and different bath exponents. The spin relaxation dynamics are also calculated to explore the coherent-incoherent dynamic transition (CI) and delocalized-localized phase transition (DL). The corresponding phase diagram of DL and CI transition are also obtained. We pose a energy level picture to understand the different mechanism of DL phase transition between the deep sub-Ohmic and Ohmic spin-boson model.



I
Introduction

As one of the most generally studied models in open quantum system, the spin-boson model (SBM) has
attracted significantly attention because it can capture the essential physics
of quantum decoherence and quantum phase transition
Leggett1987:RMP
.
Different environment can induce abundant physical phenomenons.
Optical spectroscopy is a powerful tool to understand the abundant phenomenons
behind their electronic structure and dynamics.
It has been widely used to investigate the excitonic dynamics, such as
biological light harvesting systems
renger2001:PR
;
grondelle2006:PCCP
;
cheng2009:PRC
;
Herman2018:JPB
.

Physically, the linear absorption spectrum can describe the single-photon
absorption process, which determines how the energy level is affected by the weak external perturbation.
The investigation of the linear absorption spectrum relies on the equilibrium state of total system.
For the open quantum system interacting with an environment bath, it is still
challenging to calculate the equilibrium state, mostly due to the huge number of
the bath degrees of freedom.
In order to deal with this challenge, numerous methods has been developed
Berkelbach2017:JCP
.
One common strategy is to apply the rigorous variational principle.
For example, the variational polaron transformation and its extensions

Silbey1984:JCP
;
Zheng2007:PRB
;
Zheng2009:PRB
;
Zheng2009:PRE
;
Zheng2013:JCP
;
Plenio2011:PRL
;
Zhao2016:JCP
;
Chen2018:PRB

that use a self-consistent way to determine a renormalized tunneling amplitude and the equilibrium state,
the (multilayer) multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree (MCTDH/ML-MCTDH)
method
Cederbaum1990:CPL
;
Wang2001:hybridP1
;
Wang2001:hybridP2
;
Wang2003:MLMCTDH
;
Wang2008:NJP

obtained via the Dirac-Frenkel variational principle,
and the time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) for variational matrix product states (VMPS)
Chin2016:PRB
.
Meanwhile, many other kinetic methods based on the evolution of wave function or
density matrix can also be used to calculate the equilibrium properties.
For example, the Hilbert space can be efficient compressed in the methods of
the numerical renormalization group (NRG)
Schiller2005:PRL
;
Vojta2007:PRL
;
Freyn2009:PRL
;
Costi1996:PRL
;
Anders2007:PRL
;
Bulla2007:PRB
,
the sparse polynomial space representation (SPSR)
Fehske2009:PRL
,
the time-dependent density matrix renormalization group (t-DMRG)
Plenio2010:PRL
, etc.
Additionally,
the bosonic Hilbert space can be alternatively sampled by stochastic trajectories in the methods of
the quantum Monte Carlo (QMC)
Mak1994:PRB
;
Bulla2009:PRL
,
the path-integral Monte Carlo (PIMC)
Escher2004:JCP
;
Ankerhold2013:PRL
;
Muelken2013:arXiv
,
the stochastic Liouville-von Neumann equation (SLN)
Stockburger2004:CP

the stochastic path integral (SPI)
Moix2012:JCP
;
Moix2012:PRB
, etc.
Due to the limitation of space, a huge number of other methods are not able to be discussed here
Berkelbach2017:JCP
.

In this paper, we applied the HEOM formalism to calculate the linear absorption spectrum based on the
linear response function.
As a numerical exact kinetic method, HEOM has been widely used in open quantum physics
Yan2009:JCP
;
Yan2011:arXiv
;
Shi2013:JCP
;
Tanimura2020:JCP
.
With the assistance of the auxiliary density operator, all the bath effects exert
on the reduced system dynamics are contained.
The HEOM formalism was firstly proposed by Tanimura and Kubo in the case of Drude
spectral density and high temperature
Tanimura1989:JPSJ
.
In order to overcome the limitation of original HEOM in exponential, many
modifications have been proposed to improve the application width of HEOM.
To improve the calculation efficiently of high truncation order case, Q. Shi
et al.

rescaled the HEOM equation and proposed a filtering algorithm which can significantly
reduce the number of auxiliary density operators
Shi2009:JCP
.
To deal with any complex form of the bath time correlation function, we proposed
an extended form of HEOM. By decomposing the bath time correlation function into
a series of basis function, we can deal with arbitrary bath time correlation function
in principle
Wu2015:eHEOM
;
Duan2017:PRB
;
Duan2017:JCP
;
Wang2019:JCP
.
In addition, recently, some new bath correlation decomposition schemes have been proposed to improve
the original exponential HEOM scheme, such as the Chebyshev
hierarchical equations of motion (C-HEOM)
Rahman2019:JCP
.
With the assistance of HEOM method,
Tanimura and coworkers
Kato2004:JCP
;
Tanimura2006:JPSJ
;
Ishizaki2006:JPSJ
;
Ishizaki2007:JPC

have applied it to calculate the two-dimensional infrared spectra.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, the Hamiltonian of the spin-boson model is introduced, followed by the description

interval, the bath time correlation is fitted well with a series of base functions.
The Kubo formula in Liouville space is introduced to
calculate the linear response function and the linear absorption spectrum.
In Sec.
III
, we introduce how to use the extended HEOM to calculate the

In Sec.

the extended HEOM at zero temperature both for sub-Ohmic and Ohmic SBM.
Through an analysis of the average magnetic moment and its rate kernel,
we obtain the DL phase transition and the CI dynamic transition.
Meanwhile, different behaviors of the linear absorption spectrum between the deep sub-Ohmic and
Ohmic SBM are presented.
A summary of this work is given in Sec.

II
Linear Absorption Spectrum of the Spin-Boson Model

II.1
Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian of the spin-boson model is written as
Leggett1987:RMP

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{S}}+H_{\mathrm{B}}+H_{\mathrm{SB}}

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left(p_{j}^{2}+\omega_{j}^{2}q_{j}^{2}\right)+\sigma_{z}\sum_{j}c_{j}q_{j}.

The system Hamiltonian,
H_{\mathrm{S}}=\Delta\sigma_{x}
, describes the tunneling between two degenerate spin states
(
|+\rangle
and
|-\rangle
), where
\Delta
is the tunneling amplitude and
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}
is the set of the Pauli matrices. The bath Hamiltonian,
H_{\mathrm{B}}=(1/2)\sum_{j}(p_{j}^{2}+\omega_{j}^{2}q_{j}^{2})
, is introduced for an ensemble of harmonic oscillators, where
q_{j}
,
p_{j}
and

\omega_{j}
are the coordinate, momentum and frequency of the
j
-th oscillator. The system-bath interaction,

H_{\mathrm{SB}}=\sigma_{z}F_{\mathrm{B}}=\sigma_{z}\sum_{j}c_{j}q_{j}
, follows a bilinear form, where
c_{j}
is
the amplitude between the spin and the
j
-th oscillator. The mass of each oscillator is set to be unity
(
m_{j}=1
) and the same for the reduced Planck constant (
\hbar\!=\!1
).

(a) The spectral densities
J(\omega)

s\!=\!0.1

The influence of the bosonic bath onto the spin system is characterized by a spectral density,

J(\omega)=(\pi/2)\sum_{j}(c_{j}^{2}/\omega_{j})\delta(\omega-\omega_{j})
. Based on its low-frequency feature,
the spectral density can be expressed as

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha\omega^{s}\omega_{c}^{1-s}f\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}\right),

(2)

where the Kondo parameter
\alpha
represents the average system-bath coupling strength.
The bosonic bath is categorized by the exponent
s
: Ohmic (
s=1
), sub-Ohmic (
0<s<1
)
and super-Ohmic (
s>1
)
Leggett1987:RMP
. The cutoff function
f(\omega/\omega_{c})

is assigned for the high frequency feature of the spectral density where
\omega_{c}
is
a cutoff frequency. In this paper, we focus on a rational fraction form
Shao2008:JCP
;
Wang2019:JCP
,

\displaystyle f\left(x=\frac{\omega}{\omega_{c}}\right)=\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})^{2}},

(3)

while the other forms of
f(x)
can be studied similarly
Duan2017:JCP
;
Wang2019:JCP
.

With respect to a Gaussian distribution
\rho_{\mathrm{B}}\!\propto\!\exp(-\beta H_{\mathrm{B}})
,
the influence of the bosonic bath is alternatively described by the time correlation function,

C(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\{F_{\mathrm{B}}(t)F_{\mathrm{B}}\rho_{\mathrm{B}}\}
. The parameter
\beta\!=\!1/{k_{B}T}
is the inverse product of the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
and temperature
T
.
With the substitution of the spectral density, the bath correlation function is explicitly given by

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{R}}(t)+iC_{\mathrm{I}}(t)

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\left[\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\cos\omega t-i\sin\omega t\right],

where
C_{{\mathrm{R}}}(t)
and
C_{{\mathrm{I}}}(t)
are the real and imaginary parts, respectively.
In this paper, we focus on zero temperature (
T\!=\!0
) where the Bose factor is
\coth(\beta\omega/2)\!=\!1
.
In Fig.
1
(a), we present the curves of
J(\omega)
with
s\!=\!0.1,0.5
and
1
as a demonstration.
The corresponding time correlation functions are shown in Figs.
1
(b)-
1
(d).
For each exponent, the real part
C_{{\mathrm{R}}}(t)
experiences the change from a positive to negative value
with the increase of the time while the imaginary part
C_{\mathrm{I}}(t)
is always negative. For the sub-Ohmic
case (e.g.,
s=0.1
and 0.5), both
C_{\mathrm{R}}(t)
and
C_{\mathrm{I}}(t)
behaves asymptotically as

C_{{\mathrm{R}}/{\mathrm{I}}}(t\rightarrow\infty)\sim-(\omega_{c}t)^{-(1+s)}
in the long time limit.
For the Ohmic case (
s=1
), the asymptotic behavior of the real part is the same,

C_{{\mathrm{R}}}(t\rightarrow\infty)\sim-(\omega_{c}t)^{-2}
while the imaginary part is
analytically given by
C_{\mathrm{I}}(t)=-(1/8)\pi\alpha\omega_{c}^{3}te^{-\omega_{c}t}
.

II.2
Linear Response Function and Linear Absorption Spectrum

The physical properties of the spin-boson model are determined by the eigenstructure
of the Hamiltonian
H
in Eq. (
1
).
One approach of calculating the eigenstates is to diagonalize
H

by either a deterministic solver or random sampling. Alternatively, we can introduce
an external field and inspect the excited states through the linear response of
the spin system.

With respect to a time-dependent electric field
\vec{E}(t)
,
the total Hamiltonian is approximated as

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=H-\sigma_{z}\mu_{E}E(t),

(5)

where
\mu_{E}
is the component of the dipole moment
\vec{\mu}
along the direction of
\vec{E}(t)
.
For convenience,
\mu_{E}
is set to be the unity (
\mu_{E}=1
) throughout this paper.
The linear response function
\chi(t)
is then derived as
Mukamel1995

\displaystyle\chi\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=i\Uptheta(t)\mathrm{Tr}\{\left[\sigma_{z}(t),\sigma_{z}\right]\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}\},

(6)

where
\Uptheta(t)
is the Heaviside step function,
\mathrm{Tr}\!=\!\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}

denotes a trace over the degrees of freedom of both the system and the bath,

\sigma_{z}(t)\!=\!\exp(iHt)\sigma_{z}\exp(-iHt)
is the time-dependent Pauli-
Z

matrix, and
\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}\!\propto\!\exp(-\beta H)
is the equilibrium density
matrix of the spin-boson model without the influence of
\vec{E}(t)
.
To be consistent with our HEOM calculation, Eq. (
6
) is re-formulated
in the Liouville space, given by

\displaystyle\chi\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\Uptheta(t)\mathrm{Tr}\{\sigma_{z}\exp(-i\mathcal{L}t)\mathcal{L}_{z}\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}\}

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\Uptheta(t)\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\{\sigma_{z}\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)\}.

For a Liouville superoperator
\mathcal{L}=[H,\cdots]
, its time evolution
superoperator is given by
\exp(-i\mathcal{L}t)=\exp(-iHt)\cdots\exp(iHt)
.
Based on an initial value
\varrho(0)=\mathcal{L}_{z}\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}=[\sigma_{z},\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}]
, we introduce a
quasi reduced density matrix (RDM) as

\displaystyle\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\{\exp(-i\mathcal{L}t)\varrho(0)\}.

(8)

As a result, one procedure of calculating the linear response function is described as

\displaystyle\rho(0)

\displaystyle\overset{\lim_{t^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}\exp\left(-i\mathcal{L}t^{\prime}\right)\rho(0)}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 85.35826pt}}\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}\overset{\mathcal{L}_{z}\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\varrho(0)

\displaystyle~\overset{\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\{\exp(-i\mathcal{L}t)\varrho(0)\}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 85.35826pt}}\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)\overset{\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\{\sigma_{z}\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)\}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 56.9055pt}}\chi(t).

(1) The spin-boson model is propagated from an arbitrary initial state
\rho(0)
(e.g., a product state)
to the equilibrium state
\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}=\lim_{t^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}\exp(-i\mathcal{L}t^{\prime})\rho(0)
.
(2) The equilibrium state is taken the action of the Pauli-
Z
matrix to form
a non-normalized state
\varrho(0)=\mathcal{L}_{z}\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
(3) The quasi RDM
\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)
is obtained through the time evolution in Eq. (
8
).
(4) The linear response function
\chi(t)
is estimated by Eq. (
7
).
The real-time HEOM will be utilized in steps (1) and (3).
Furthermore, we further interested in the linear absorption spectrum,

\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
, which can be viewed as an experimental measurement.
Based on its definition, the linear absorption spectrum is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)=\operatorname{Im}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi(t)e^{i\omega t}\,dt,

(9)

where
\mathrm{Re}
and
\mathrm{Im}
denote the real and imaginary parts of a complex
variable, respectively.

III
An Extended HEOM

In this section, we provide a brief description of an extended
HEOM
Wu2015:eHEOM
;
Duan2017:PRB
;
Duan2017:JCP
for the calculation of the
linear response function and the linear absorption spectrum. Based on the
decomposition of the bath correlation function
C(t)
, the HEOM builds a
linearized array of time differential equations of the auxiliary density
operators (ADOs), which gives rise to the RDM at the zeroth order. Our
decomposition is performed onto
C(t)
directly rather than the Bose factor and
the spectral density so that the HEOM can be easily extended to the zero
temperature (
T=0
). More importantly, the HEOM is founded on a complete dynamic
set of ADOs after tracing the bath degrees of freedom so that this method can be
applied to the numerical calculation of dynamic quantities beyond the RDM. Many
efforts have been attributed to the development of systematic and exact
theoretical frameworks
Tanimura2012:JCP
;
Tanimura2015:JCP
;
Wang2013:PRB
.

III.1
Decomposition of the Bath Correlation Function

To treat a general bath at an arbitrary temperature,
an efficient and reliable decomposition of the bath correlation function
is a key technical step in the application of our extended HEOM.
In principle,
C(t)
can be decomposed exactly over a complete
set of orthonormal basis functions, which is however inefficient in the scenario of
a slowly varying function. On the other hand,
the correlation functions of both the sub-Ohmic and Ohmic baths
exhibit a similar behavior: a non-monotonic variation in the short time regime while an asymptotic power-law
decay in the long time regime. Accordingly, we assume the following function,

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{fit}}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{R}}}a_{{\mathrm{R}};n}\varphi_{{\mathrm{R}};n}(t)+i\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathrm{I}}}a_{{\mathrm{I}};m}\varphi_{{\mathrm{I}};m}(t),

(10)

to fit the bath correlation function over a large but finite time interval (
0\leqslant t\leqslant t_{\max}
).
At the maximum time
t_{\max}
, the spin system is assumed to be close to its final equilibrium state.
Here
\{\varphi_{{\mathrm{R}};n}(t),a_{{\mathrm{R}};n}\}
and
\{\varphi_{{\mathrm{I}};m}(t),a_{{\mathrm{I}};m}\}
are two sets of basis functions
and their coefficients for the real and imaginary parts, respectively. In practice, we choose oscillatory and
nonoscillatory exponentially decaying functions
Duan2017:PRB
;
Duan2017:JCP
;
Wang2019:JCP
,

\displaystyle\{\varphi_{\mathrm{X};n}(t)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{X};1}t)e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{X};1}t},\sin(\omega_{\mathrm{X};1}t)e^{-\gamma_{\mathrm{X};1}t},\cdots,e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{X};1}t},e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{X};2}t},\dots\},

(11)

with
\mathrm{X}\!=\!{\mathrm{R}}
and
{\mathrm{I}}
. The number of the basis functions,
N_{\mathrm{R}}
and
N_{\mathrm{I}}
,
are allowed to be different.
In the case of
C_{\mathrm{I}}(t;s=1)
, two basis functions,
\{t\exp(-\omega_{c}t),\exp(-\omega_{c}t)\}
,
are considered based on its analytical form. Due to the nature of a high-dimensional fitting
problem, an appropriate choice is required for the initial trial values of

\{\omega_{\mathrm{X};n},\gamma_{\mathrm{X};n},\Gamma_{\mathrm{X};n}\}

Duan2017:PRB
;
Duan2017:JCP
;
Wang2019:JCP
.
In short, the non-monotonic variation of
C_{X}(t)
in the short time
can be fitted by oscillatory or nonoscillatory terms with similar decay rates,
while the power-law decay in the long time is effectively fitted by
a series of exponential decays over different time scales.

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\\

\displaystyle~~~\times\cdots\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{k}\varphi_{{\mathrm{R}};n_{k}}(t-\tau_{k})[-i\mathcal{L}_{z}(\tau_{k})]\times\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{1}^{\prime}\varphi_{{\mathrm{I}};m_{1}}(t-\tau_{1}^{\prime})\mathcal{S}_{z}(\tau_{1}^{\prime})

This ADO is characterized by the two sequences,
\{n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\}
and
\{m_{1},\cdots,m_{l}\}
,
where each index refers to a specific basis function, i.e.,
n_{i}\rightarrow\varphi_{{\mathrm{R}};n_{i}}(\tau_{i})
and
m_{j}\rightarrow\varphi_{{\mathrm{I}};m_{j}}(\tau_{j})
.
The hierarchical expansion order is given by the total number of basis functions, satisfying
h\!=\!k+l
.
The zeroth order ADO recovers the RDM of the spin system, giving
\sigma_{0}(t)\!=\!\rho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)
.
In Eq. (
12
),
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{S}}(t)\!=\!\exp{(-i\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}t)}
is the time propagator of the system
according to the commutator of the system Hamiltonian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}\!=\![H_{\mathrm{S}},\dots]
.
With respect to the Pauli-
Z
matrix, we introduce its commutator
\mathcal{L}_{z}(t)\!=\![\sigma_{z}(t),\cdots]

and its anti-commutator
\mathcal{S}_{z}(t)\!=\![\sigma_{z}(t),\cdots]_{+}
with

\sigma_{z}(t)=\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{S}}^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{z}
.
With the consideration of the forward time ordering operator
\mathcal{T}_{+}\{\cdots\}
, we introduce an abbreviation
for a time ordering expansion,
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{RDM}}(t)\!=\!\mathcal{T}_{+}\exp{[-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{W}(\tau)\,d\tau]}
, which can be viewed
as a formal expression of the time propagator of the RDM. Here the transition rate kernel reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\left[\mathcal{L}_{z}(t)C_{\mathrm{R}}(t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{z}(\tau)+i\mathcal{L}_{z}(t)C_{\mathrm{I}}(t-\tau)\mathcal{S}_{z}(\tau)\right].

(13)

Next the time evolution of the
h
-th order ADO

\sigma_{h}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\\
m_{1},\cdots,m_{l}\end{subarray}\right)}(t)
is determined by the time
differentials over various
t
-dependent terms in Eq. (
12
). The time derivative of each basis
function in the decomposition of
C_{\mathrm{X}={\mathrm{R}},{\mathrm{I}}}(t)
is in a closed form, i.e.,

\dot{\varphi}_{\mathrm{X};n}(t)=\sum_{n^{\prime}}\eta_{\mathrm{X};n,n^{\prime}}\varphi_{\mathrm{X};n^{\prime}}(t)
with

\{\eta_{\mathrm{X};n,n^{\prime}}\}
the time-independent coefficients. Without further details, we summarize the final
time evolutions equation as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma_{h}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\\
m_{1},\cdots,m_{l}\end{subarray}\right)}(t)=-i\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}\sigma_{h}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\cdots,n_{k}\\
m_{1},\cdots,m_{l}\end{subarray}\right)}(t)

\displaystyle~~~+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{R}}}\eta_{{\mathrm{R}};jj^{\prime}}\sigma_{h}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\cdots,n_{j-1},n_{j^{\prime}},n_{j+1},\cdots\\
\cdots\end{subarray}\right)}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{I}}}\eta_{{\mathrm{I}};jj^{\prime}}\sigma_{h}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\cdots\\
\cdots,m_{j-1},m_{j^{\prime}},m_{j+1},\cdots\end{subarray}\right)}(t)

\displaystyle~~~-i\mathcal{L}_{z}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varphi_{{\mathrm{R}};n_{j}}(0)\sigma_{h-1}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\cdots,n_{j-1},n_{j+1},\cdots\\
\cdots\end{subarray}\right)}(t)+\mathcal{S}_{z}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\varphi_{{\mathrm{I}};m_{j}}(0)\sigma_{h-1}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\cdots\\
\cdots,m_{j-1},m_{j+1},\cdots\end{subarray}\right)}(t)

\displaystyle~~~-i\mathcal{L}_{z}\sum_{n_{k+1}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{R}}}a_{{\mathrm{R}};n_{k+1}}\sigma_{h+1}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\cdots,n_{k},n_{k+1}\\
\cdots\end{subarray}\right)}(t)-i\mathcal{L}_{z}\sum_{m_{l+1}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{I}}}a_{{\mathrm{I}};m_{l+1}}\sigma_{h+1}^{\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\cdots\\
\cdots,m_{l},m_{l+1}\end{subarray}\right)}(t),

(14)

which is an extension of the original HEOM
Wu2015:eHEOM
;
Duan2017:PRB
;
Duan2017:JCP
.
For convenience, we introduce a matrix form to rewritten Eq. (
14
) into

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\sigma}}(t)\!=\!-\bm{\mathcal{W}}\bm{\sigma}(t),

(15)

where the vector
\bm{\sigma}(t)
is the set of ADOs,

\bm{\sigma}(t)\!=\!\{\sigma_{0}(t)=\rho_{\mathrm{S}}(t),\sigma_{1}(t),\sigma_{2}(t),\cdots\}
,
and the transition rate matrix
\bm{\mathcal{W}}
follows a block tri-diagonal form,

\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{h,h^{\prime}}\!=\!\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{h,h}\delta_{h^{\prime},h}+\bm{\mathcal{W}}_{h,h\pm 1}\delta_{h^{\prime},h\pm 1}
.
Due to the assumptions of the system-bath factorized initial state, the initial values of the ADOs
are set to be
\bm{\sigma}(0)\!=\!\{\sigma_{0}(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{S}}(0),\sigma_{1}(0)=0,\cdots\}
.
In practice, Eq. (
15
) is truncated at an expansion order
H
for the numerical convergence.

III.3
Application to the Linear Absorption Spectrum

Although the ADOs in Eq. (
12
) are obtained under the assumption of the initial system-bath factorized
state, the HEOM in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) can be applied to a much more general scenario. The
set of the ADOs
\bm{\sigma}(t)=\{\sigma_{0}(t),\sigma_{1}(t),\cdots\}
can be viewed as a complete dynamic basis
set with respect to the partial trace
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\{\cdots\rho_{\mathrm{B}}\}
. A one-to-one mapping can be built to
represent a reduced dynamic variable of the system in the framework of the ADOs, e.g.,
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{B}}\{\cdots e^{-i\mathcal{L}t}\cdots e^{-i\mathcal{L}t^{\prime}}\rho(0)\}\Leftrightarrow\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{S}}\cdots e^{-\bm{\mathcal{W}}t}\cdots e^{-\bm{\mathcal{W}}t^{\prime}}\bm{\sigma}(0)
. Here we introduce a projection matrix onto the subspace of the
system RDM, given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{4}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

The block matrix in Eq. (
15
) is expanded over the set of the ADOs and
\mathcal{I}_{4}
is
a
4\times 4
identity matrix regarding the Liouville space of the spin system.
Without further details, the linear response function in Eq. (
7
) can be re-expressed in terms of the
HEOM matrices as

\displaystyle\chi(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{t^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}i\Uptheta(t)\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\{\sigma_{z}\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{S}}e^{-\bm{\mathcal{W}}t}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{z}e^{-\bm{\mathcal{W}}t^{\prime}}\bm{\sigma}(0)\},

(17)

where the Liouville superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{z}
is expanded in the dynamic space of the ADOs, given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{L}_{z}&0&\cdots\\
0&\mathcal{L}_{z}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

Following the same strategy in Sec.
II.2
, the calculation of
\chi(t)
is divided into four steps.
(1) With respect to a system-bath factorized initial state
\bm{\sigma}(0)
, the ADOs are propagated
by the HEOM in Eq. (
15
) up to a long time (
t^{\prime}\Delta\gg 1
), approaching to an approximate
equilibrium state,
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eq}}\approx\lim_{t^{\prime}\Delta\gg 1}\exp(-\bm{\mathcal{W}}t^{\prime})\bm{\sigma}(0)
.
In the delocalized phase with a single ground state (equivalent to the equilibrium state at
T=0
),

\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eq}}
is independent of the initial ADOs
\bm{\sigma}(0)
.
(2) The commutator of the Pauli-
Z
matrix is applied onto
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eq}}
, leading to a set of
the initial quasi-ADOs,
\bm{\varsigma}(0)=\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{z}\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
(3) The quasi-RDM
\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)
is obtained by the projection of the quasi-ADOs evolved by the HEOM,
given by
\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathrm{S}}e^{-\bm{\mathcal{W}}t}\bm{\varsigma}(0)
.
(4) The linear response function is estimated by
\chi(t)=i\Uptheta(t)\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{S}}\{\sigma_{z}\varrho_{\mathrm{S}}(t)\}
. The linear absorption spectrum is extracted from the
imaginary part of the Fourier transform of
\chi(t)
by Eq. (
9
).

V
Conclusion and Disscussion

In this paper, we apply an extended HEOM method to explore the zero-temperature spin dynamics and
linear absorption spectrum of spin-boson model from deep sub-Ohmic bath to Ohmic

In the process of approaching to DL phase transition, we have observed the
significant difference between deep sub-Ohmic and Ohmic SBM.
Here we propose an energy level picture to explain their different behaviors.
The DL phase transition arises from the degenerate ground state.
From the view of linear absorption spectrum, in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\alpha_{c}
,
the low-frequency absorption peak of
\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
approaches a
\delta(\omega)

which is the indication of DL phase transition for Ohmic SBM.

The deep sub-Ohmic corresponds to the three-level picture in Fig.

In the Ohmic regime, it corresponds to the two-level picture, so there is
only one absorption peak. When the DL phase transition
occurs, the two energy level becomes degenerate and the renormalized tunneling amplitude vanishes.
