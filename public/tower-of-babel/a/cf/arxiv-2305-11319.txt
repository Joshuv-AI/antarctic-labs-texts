Risk Budgeting Allocation for Dynamic Risk Measures First version: May 18, 2023.
Silvana M. Pesenti, Sebastian Jaimungal, Yuri F. Saporito, Rodrigo S. Targino

Abstract
We define and develop an approach for risk budgeting allocation – a risk diversification portfolio strategy – where risk is measured using a dynamic time-consistent risk measure. For this, we introduce a notion of dynamic risk contributions that generalise the classical Euler contributions and which allow us to obtain dynamic risk contributions in a recursive manner. We prove that, for the class of coherent dynamic distortion risk measures, the risk allocation problem may be recast as a sequence of strictly convex optimisation problems. Moreover, we show that self-financing dynamic risk budgeting strategies with initial wealth of 1 1 are scaled versions of the solution of the sequence of convex optimisation problems. Furthermore, we develop an actor-critic approach, leveraging the elicitability of dynamic risk measures, to solve for risk budgeting strategies using deep learning.



keywords
Dynamic Risk Measures, Portfolio Allocation, Risk Parity, Elicitability, Deep Learning

Introduction

The “risk parity” portfolio has been pioneered by Bridgewater Associates, when in 1996 it launched the
All Weather
asset allocation strategy – a portfolio strategy withstanding all weathers – although the term risk parity was only coined in 2005 in the white paper by
Qian 2005
. Risk parity originated from the desire of a diversified portfolio and the realisation that an equally weighted portfolio is diversified in asset allocation but not in the extent in which each asset contributes to the overall portfolio risk
(
Qian 2011
)
. Emphasised by the 2008 financial crisis, the call for “maximally” diversifying a portfolio’s risk was born, see e.g.
Choueifaty and Coignard 2008
. Risk parity enjoys widespread popularity in industry as numerous portfolio (performance) comparison studies illustrate, see, e.g.,
Chaves et al. 2011
,
Lee 2011
, and
Asness et al. 2012
. An early mathematical formalisation of risk parity strategies can be found in
Maillard et al. 2010
and
Roncalli 2013
.

Risk parity strategies and more broader risk budgeting strategies are portfolio allocations where the contribution of each asset to the overall portfolio risk is prespecified, e.g. for risk parity each assets contributes equally to the portfolio risk. Thus, central to risk budgeting is the way the risk of a portfolio is quantified. While most of the extant literature measures risk using the portfolio variance and further restrict to assets that follow multivariate Gaussian distributions, recent works relax these assumptions.
Bruder et al. 2016
and
Jurczenko and Teiletche 2019
study the Expected Shortfall (ES; also called Conditional Value-at-Risk) risk measure under the assumption that assets are multivariate Gaussian distributed, resulting in explicit formulae for risk contributions. Further works on risk budgeting include
Ji and Lejeune 2018
who utilise the downside risk measure,

Bellini et al. 2021
who consider expectile risk measures,
Anis and Kwon 2022
who incorporate asset selection, and
da Costa et al. 2023
who propose algorithms based on the cutting planes methodology to calculate risk budgeting strategies for coherent risk measures.
Haugh et al. 2017
combine risk budgeting of (overlapping) groups of asset with simultaneously maximising return and minimising risk.
Variations of risk budgeting portfolio strategies are considered in
Bai et al. 2016
who propose alternative optimisation problems to solve for risk parity portfolios.
Meucci et al. 2015
and
Roncalli and Weisang 2016
construct risk factor budgeting portfolios, that are portfolios where each (uncorrelated) factor, rather than asset, contributes equally to the portfolio variance.
Lassance et al. 2022
continues this line of work by including independent component analysis.

None of these works, however, addresses the dynamic nature of investments, i.e., that portfolio strategies are typically holistically considered over a time horizon larger than one period; we henceforth refer to the one period setting as the ‘‘static’’ setting. In this paper, we develop a dynamic setting in which an investor trades over a finite time horizon using a self-financing risk budgeting strategy. Specifically, the investor aims to create a portfolio strategy, such that at every decision point each asset contributes a prespecified percentage to the future risk of the portfolio. This means that the investor’s problem is a multi-period decision problem. Whenever decisions occur over multiple periods, the investor’s ‘‘optimal’’ choices should be coherent over time. This can be achieved, for example, by optimising a time-consistent criterion.
1
1

1

There are a number of alternative approaches to time-inconsistencies, see, e.g.,
Björk et al. 2021
, we however, opt to use time-consistent criteria.
Indeed, when decisions are stemming from a time-inconsistent objective (e.g. a static risk measure), the “optimal” decision at a future point in time and state may not be optimal when one arrives at that future point in time in that very state
(
Bielecki et al. 2018
)
. This is in contrast to time-consistent objectives (e.g., dynamic time-consistent risk measures), which result in decisions that are coherent across time and state. Thus, we consider dynamic time-consistent risk measures to evaluate the risk of a portfolio strategy.

Dynamic time-consistent risk measures have been extensively studied to evaluate the risk of a sequence of random costs, such as the profit and loss (P&L) of a portfolio strategy; indicatively see
Cheridito et al. 2006
,
Ruszczyński 2010
,
Bielecki et al. 2022
,
Coache et al. 2023
,
Bielecki et al. 2023
. For dynamic risk budgeting, however, we further require the allocations of the dynamic risk to each asset and each time point, a topic whose literature is sparse. An early work for allocations of coherent dynamic risk measures is
Cherny 2009
and for
BSDE-based dynamic time-consistent risk measures we refer to
Kromer and Overbeck 2014
,
Kromer and Overbeck 2017
, and
Mastrogiacomo and Rosazza-Gianin 2022
. Related but conceptually different is the work of
Schilling et al. 2020
who axiomatically study how to decompose a risk dynamically. While working in a dynamic setting, their risk is the portfolio loss itself and not a dynamic risk measure applied to it.

In this work, we consider the class of dynamic time-consistent risk measures that arise from conditional one-step distortion risk measures. A case in point is the ES whose security level may depend on the investor’s wealth or asset price. For this class, we define their dynamic risk contributions via Gâteaux derivatives and derive explicit formulae. While most of our results hold for conditional distortion risk measures, we focus on the subset of conditional coherent distortion risk measures, as defining risk allocations for non-coherent risk measures provide an “incentive for infinite fragmentation of portfolios”
(
Tsanakas 2009
)
. In the static setting, Gâteaux derivatives enjoy a long history as risk contributions, also in connection to cooperative game theory. We provide a detailed literature review in Section
3
. With this definition of dynamic risk contributions at hand, we define a dynamic risk budgeting portfolio as a strategy whose risk contributions at each point in time are a predefined percentage of the future risk of the strategy. We prove, under mild conditions, that a self-financing dynamic risk budgeting strategy with initial wealth of
1
is a scaled version of the solution of a sequence of strictly convex optimisation problems. Furthermore, we develop an actor-critic approach to solve the sequence of optimisation problems using deep learning techniques and provide illustrative examples.

This manuscript is organised as follows. Section
2
introduces dynamic time-consistent risk measures and in Section
2.2
, we apply a dynamic time-consistent risk measure to a self-financing strategy and derive a recursive representation. In Section
3
we define dynamic risk contributions via the Gâteaux derivative and derive explicit formulae for the class of dynamic distortion risk measures. Section
4
is devoted to dynamic risk budgeting portfolio strategies and we show in Theorem
4.4
that a self-financing dynamic risk budgeting strategy with initial wealth of
1
can be obtained as a scaled version of the solution to a collection of strictly convex optimisation problems. Section
5
discusses how to solve this family of optimisation problems using neural networks leveraging elicitability of conditional risk measures (Subsection
5.2
). Illustrations of risk budgeting strategies are provided in Section
6
. Delegated to the appendix are auxiliary definitions and results (
B
), additional technical proofs (
C
), elaborations on conditional elicitability (
D
), and details on the numerical implementation (
E
).

Dynamic Risk Assessment

We work on a filtered and completed probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}},{\mathbb{P}})
, where
\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}:=\{0,1,\dots,T+1\}
, and
T\in\mathds{N}
is a known and finite time horizon. The information available to the investor is encapsulated in the filtration
({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}}
, and we assume that
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}=(\emptyset,\Omega)
is the trivial
\sigma
-algebra, and simply write
{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot]:={\mathbb{E}}[\cdot~|{\mathcal{F}}_{0}]
. We further denote the spaces of square-integrable random variables (rvs) and sequences by
{\mathcal{Z}}:=\{Z\in{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathbb{E}}[Z^{2}]<+\infty\}
,
{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}:=\{Z_{t}\in{\mathcal{Z}}:Z_{t}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
, and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}:=\{(Z_{t},Z_{t+1},\ldots,Z_{T+1})\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}\times{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{Z}}_{T+1}\}
, for all
t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}
. Similarly, we define the spaces of
n
-dimensional random vectors and sequences by
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{Z}}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}):Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}\,,\;\forall i=1,\ldots,n\}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t}:=\{{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}:{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t:T+1}:=\{({\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t+1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{T+1})\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t}\times{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t+1}\times\cdots\times{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{T+1}\}
, for all
t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}
. We further define
{\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}:={\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}:={\mathcal{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}}_{t},{\mathbb{P}})
for all
t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}
. Unless otherwise stated, all (in)equalities of random vectors are to be understood component-wise and in a
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost sure (a.s.) sense.

2.1
Dynamic Risk Measures

The agent assesses the risk associated with a trading strategy by a dynamic time-consistent risk measure, which are families of conditional risk measures that satisfy the property of strong time-consistency; see Definition
2.2
below.
We adopt the setting of
Cheridito et al. 2006
and
Ruszczyński 2010
for dynamic risk measures and refer the interested reader to those works and reference therein.

Definition 2.1

(Dynamic Risk Measures)

A dynamic risk measure on
\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}
is a family
\{\rho_{t,T+1}\}_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}}
, where for each
t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}
, the conditional risk measure is a mapping
\rho_{t,T+1}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
.
We say that a dynamic risk measure possesses one of the following properties, if for all
t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}
:

i)

Normalisation:

\rho_{t,T+1}(0,\ldots,0)=0
.

ii)

Monotonicity:

\rho_{t,T+1}(Z_{t:T+1})\leq\rho_{t,T+1}(Y_{t:T+1})
, for all
Z_{t:T+1},Y_{t:T+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}
with
Z_{t:T+1}\leq Y_{t:T+1}
.

iii)

Translation invariance:

\rho_{t,T+1}(Z_{t:T+1})=Z_{t}+\rho_{t,T+1}(0,Z_{t+1},\ldots,Z_{T+1})
, for all
Z_{t:T+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}
.

iv)

Convexity:

\rho_{t,T+1}(\lambda\,Z_{t:T+1}+(1-\lambda)\,Y_{t:T+1})\leq\lambda\,\rho_{t,T+1}(Z_{t:T+1})+(1-\lambda)\,\rho_{t,T+1}(Y_{t:T+1})
, for all
\lambda\in{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
with
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and
Y_{t:T+1},Z_{t:T+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}
.

v)

Positive homogeneity:

\rho_{t,T+1}(\lambda\,Z_{t:T+1})=\lambda\,\rho_{t,T+1}(Z_{t:T+1})
, for all
\lambda\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t}^{\infty}
with
\lambda>0
and
Z_{t:T+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}
.

vi)

Coherency:

\rho_{t,T+1}
is monotone, translation invariant, convex, and positive homogeneous.

The mapping
\rho_{t,T+1}
thus assesses the risk of the sequence
Z_{t:T+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}
viewed from time
t
, by mapping it to an
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-measureable rv. The investor may view this as the
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-measurable quantity they are willing to exchange in place of the sequence of future risks.

Next, we recall the notion of strong time-consistency, which, for simplicity, we refer to as time-consistency. A dynamic risk measure is time-consistent if it compares risks coherently over time. Specifically, if the time-
s
risk of one stochastic process is larger than another, then the former should also be riskier at an earlier time
t<s
, if the processes are a.s. equal at all times
u
satisfying
t\leq u<s
. Thus,
time-consistency is a property that results in optimal sequential decisions that are coherent when optimised at different points in time, and that further leads to a dynamic programming principle for optimising dynamic risk measures.

Definition 2.2

(Strong time-consistency –
Cheridito et al. 2006
)

A dynamic risk measure
\{\rho_{t,T+1}\}_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}}
is (strong) time-consistent if for all
Z_{t:T+1},Y_{t:T+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t:T+1}
that satisfy for some
s\in\{t,\ldots,T+1\}

Z_{t:s}=Y_{t:s}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{s,T+1}(Z_{s:T+1})\leq\rho_{s,T+1}(Y_{s:T+1})

it holds that

\rho_{t,T+1}(Z_{t:T+1})\leq\rho_{t,T+1}(Y_{t:T+1})\,,

and where
Z_{t:s}:=(Z_{t},\ldots,Z_{s},0,\ldots,0)
is understood as the projection of
Z_{t:T+1}
onto
{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{Z}}_{s}
.

While not apparent at first, the theorem below shows that time-consistency creates a connection between dynamic risk measures and so-called one-step risk measures. In particular, the theorem states that a dynamic time-consistent risk measure induces a family of one-step risk measures and conversely, any family of one-step risk measures defines a dynamic time-consistent risk measure. The following theorem is due to
Cheridito et al. 2006
and
Ruszczyński 2010
.

Theorem 2.3

(Recursive Relation)

Let
\{\rho_{t,T+1}\}_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}}
be a dynamic risk measure which is monotone, normalised, and translation invariant.
Then
\{\rho_{t,T+1}\}_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}}
is time-consistent if and only if the following recursive representation holds:

\rho_{t,T+1}(Z_{t},\ldots,Z_{T+1})=Z_{t}+\rho_{t}\Bigg(Z_{t+1}+\rho_{t+1}\bigg(Z_{t+2}+\cdots+\rho_{T-1}\Big(Z_{T}+\rho_{T}\big(Z_{T+1}\big)\Big)\cdots\bigg)\Bigg)\,,

(1)

where the
one-step risk measures
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
are mappings
\rho_{t}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
, defined by
\rho_{t}(Z_{t+1}):=\rho_{t,T+1}(0,Z_{t+1},0,\ldots,0)
, for
Z_{t+1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
. Moreover the one-step risk measures are monotone, normalised, and translation invariant.

By Theorem
2.3
, any family of mappings
\rho_{t}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
that are monotone, normalised, and translation invariant, for all
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
,
{\mathcal{T}}:=\{0,1,\dots,T\}
, gives rise to a dynamic time-consistent risk measure and vice-versa. Thus, without loss of generalisation, we make a slight abuse of terminology and call
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
a dynamic time-consistent risk measure (DRM) with representation (
1
).

For defining risk budgeting strategies we further require the DRM to be positive homogeneous and convex; thus, coherent. Throughout the remainder of the exposition we focus on coherent distortion DRMs, which are a generalisation of the class of distortion risk measures to the dynamic setting. Coherent distortion risk measures span the subclass of law-invariant coherent risk measures that are comonotonic additive
(
Kusuoka 2001
)
. Dynamic distortion risk measures with deterministic distortion function have been considered in
Bielecki et al. 2023
who focus on coherent dynamic acceptability indices generated by families of distortion functions. Here, we allow the distortion function to be both time and state dependent, which differs from earlier works. For this we first define for each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
the regular cumulative distribution function (cdf) of
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
conditional on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
as
F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(z):={\mathbb{P}}(Z\leq z~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
, and the regular (left-) quantile function of
Z
conditional on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
as
F_{Z|F_{t}}^{-1}(u):=\inf\{y\in{\mathds{R}}~|~F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(u)\geq y\}
. Moreover, define
U_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}:=F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(Z)
,
which is
{\mathcal{F}}_{t+1}
-measurable and, conditional on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, uniform distributed on
(0,1)
.
2
2

2

If
F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(\cdot)
is continuous, then
U_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}
is, conditionally on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, a uniform rv. If
F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(\cdot)
is discontinuous, define
\tilde{F}_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(z,\lambda):={\mathbb{P}}\left(Z<z~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right)+\lambda\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z=z~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right)
. Next, let
V
be a uniform
{\mathcal{F}}_{t+1}
-measurable rv that is, conditional on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, independent of
Z
. Then
\tilde{U}_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}:=\tilde{F}_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(Z,V)
is, conditional on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, a uniform rv, see
(
Rüschendorf 2013
, Def. 1.2.)
. For simplicity, we use the notation
U_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}:=\tilde{U}_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}
.

We refer the reader to Appendix
B
, Definition
B.1
for further discussion of one-step distortion risk measures and their representation via Choquet integrals.

Definition 2.4

(Coherent One-step Distortion Risk Measures)

For each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, let
\gamma_{t}:[0,1]\times\Omega\to{\mathds{R}}_{+}
be a (state dependent) distortion weight function. This means that
\int_{0}^{1}\gamma_{t}(u,\omega)\,du=1
and
\gamma_{t}(\cdot,\omega)
is non-decreasing for all
\omega\in\Omega
, and that the rv
\gamma_{t}(u,\cdot):\Omega\to{\mathds{R}}_{+}
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-measurable for every
u\in[0,1]
and for all
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Then, the coherent distortion dynamic risk measure with weight functions
\{\gamma_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
is the family
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
, where for each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
, the one-step risk measure
\rho_{t}
is defined as

\rho_{t}(Z):=\int_{0}^{1}F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}^{-1}(u)\;\gamma_{t}(u)\,du={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\;Z\,\gamma_{t}\left(F_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(Z)\right)\;\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\;\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\;Z\,\gamma_{t}\left(U_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}\right)\;\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big]\,,

(2)

where, as usual, we suppress the dependence of
\gamma_{t}
on its second argument.

Allowing for a time- and state-dependent distortion weight function includes, e.g., Expected Shortfall at level
\alpha_{t}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
,
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1)
, in which case
\gamma_{t}(u)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{t}}{\mathds{1}}_{u\geq\alpha_{t}}
. The level
\alpha_{t}
may, e.g., decrease as wealth decreases to express the fact that the investor becomes more risk averse if their wealth drops.

One-step distortion risk measures that are coherent give raise to coherent DRM via representation (
2.3
). Throughout, we work with coherent dynamic DRM.

{assumption}

We assume that
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
is a coherent distortion DRM with representation (
2
) that satisfy
\int_{0}^{1}\gamma_{t}(u)^{2}\,du<C
a.s., for some
C<+\infty
, for all
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
.

The integrability condition on
\{\gamma_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
guarantees by Lemma
B.2
, that any coherent one-step distortion risk measure is a mapping
\rho_{t}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
,
t\in{\mathcal{T}}/\{0\}
and
\rho_{0}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\to{\mathds{R}}
.

2.2
Risk-to-go of a Strategy

We denote by
{\boldsymbol{X}}=({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})_{t\in\overline{{\mathcal{T}}}}\,\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{0:T+1}
the (strictly) positive a.s.
n
-dimensional price process of the universe of assets and consider an investor who invokes a long-only self-financing trading strategy and invests in all assets. We also denote by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}\,\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{0:T}
a (not necessarily self-financing) strategy, where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}=(\theta_{t,1},\ldots,\theta_{t,n})
is an
n
-dimensional, positive a.s. random vector representing the amount of shares invested in each asset at time
t
. In the sequel, we often use the “slice notation”
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}:=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{1}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{1}+1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{2}})
for
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
.

A strategy
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
induces a self-financing strategy
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}=({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
– referred to as the
induced self-financing strategy
– as follows

{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{0}:={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t}:=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t-1}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}}{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}}\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\,,\quad\forall\;t\in{\mathcal{T}}/\{0\}.

Recall that the investor invokes a long-only strategy and invests in all assets, thus
\theta_{t,i}>0
, a.s., for all
i\in{\mathcal{N}}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and hence
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{0:T}
is well-defined. The strategy
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
is self-financing, i.e. it satisfies
({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t-1})^{\intercal}\,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}=0
, for all
t\in{\mathcal{T}}/\{0\}
. To simplify notation, we define the weight process
\boldsymbol{{w}}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\boldsymbol{{w}}_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}})_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
:

\displaystyle w^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}:=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}_{t}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}_{t+1}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}\,,\quad\forall\;t\in{\mathcal{T}}\,,

and notice that,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}_{t}=\left(\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}w^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{s}\right)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\,,\quad\forall\;t\in{\mathcal{T}}/\{0\}.

For each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
,
w^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{t}
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{t+1}
-measurable and if the original strategy
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}
is already self-financing, then
w_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=1
.

We denote the (negative) price increment by
{\Delta\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}:=-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}-{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})
. As the investor aims to invest in a risk budgeting portfolio, they only consider strategies that are long-only and satisfy the following integrability conditions.

Definition 2.5

For any
c\geq 0
define the set of admissible strategies by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}_{c}}:=\bigg\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{0:T}~

\displaystyle|~{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}>c\;\;\;a.s.\;,\quad w_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}^{\infty}\,,\;\forall t\in{\mathcal{T}}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\big({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\Delta\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\big)^{2}\right]<+\infty\,\bigg\}\,.

For any
c\geq 0
, by Lemma
B.4
,

{\mathcal{A}_{c}}
is a a convex set and any induced self-financing strategy
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
belongs to
{\mathcal{A}_{0}}
. We further use the notation
{\mathcal{A}_{c,t}}
,
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, to denote the sliced set of admissible strategies at time
t
, that is
{\mathcal{A}_{c,t}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}~|~{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}\in{\mathcal{A}_{c}}\}
for each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
. We use the terminology that the strategy
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is admissible, if there exists a
c\geq 0
such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{A}_{c}}
.

The investor assesses the risk of an admissible strategy
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}\in{\mathcal{A}_{c}}
at time
t=0
with a coherent distortion DRM
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
by

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{R}}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}]

We next discuss how to allocate the risk-to-go onto its components at each point in time.

3
Dynamic Risk Contributions

The literature on risk contribution – also called capital (cost) allocation – in the static setting is extensive. Approaches ranging from performance measurement
(
Tasche 1999
)
, cooperative game theory including Aumann-Shapley allocation (see e.g.,
Mirman and Tauman 1982
and
Billera and Heath 1982
for early works on cost allocation and
Denault 2001
in a risk management setting) and allocation in the fuzzy core (
Tsanakas and Barnett 2003
), as well as Gâteaux derivatives and Euler allocations
(
Kalkbrener 2005
)
. Using an axiomatic approach,
Kalkbrener 2005
showed that for any positive homogeneous and sub-additive static risk measure, the only linear and diversifying
capital allocation rule is the Gâteaux derivative.

Here, we proceed inline with
Kalkbrener 2005
by defining risk contributions as a sub-differential, specifically through the Gâteaux derivative. We note that in case of coherent risk measures, the allocation defined via the Gâteaux derivatives is the same as the Aumann-Shapley allocation
(
Tsanakas 2009
)
.
An advantage of defining risk contributions through Gâteaux derivatives of a distortion risk measure is that they satisfy
full allocation
, the property that the sum of the risk contributions adds up to the total risk. In the sequel, we show that our dynamic risk contributions also satisfies a dynamic version of the full allocation property. When defining dynamic risk budgeting strategies, the full allocation property is imperative as it allows to allocate the entire risk to its components.

As we work in a dynamic setting, at each time
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, the investor faces the future risk of the induced self-financing strategy and aims to allocate the risk-to-go to each asset
i
. Thus for each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, we define the risk contribution of asset
i
as the one-sided Gâteaux derivative of the risk-to-go processes
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
in direction
\theta_{t,i}
. This allows to measure the degree to which the risk-to-go is impacted by the investor’s position in the
i^{\text{th}}
asset at time
t
. Moreover, as we show in Corollary
3.5
, this approach allows for full allocation.

We start by recalling the definition of one-sided Gâteaux derivative of a functional.

Definition 3.1

For a functional
F_{t}:{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t:T}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
,
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, we denote by
{\mathcal{D}}_{i}^{{\zeta}}\,F_{t}
its one-sided Gâteaux derivative of
F_{t}
to the
i^{\text{th}}
component in direction
{\zeta}\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
. That is, for
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t:T}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t:T}

{\mathcal{D}}_{i}^{{\zeta}}\,F_{t}[{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t:T}]:=\lim\limits_{{\varepsilon}\downarrow 0}\;\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}\Big(F_{t}[{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t:T}+{\varepsilon}\,{\boldsymbol{1}}_{t,i}{\zeta}]-F_{t}[{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{t:T}]\Big)\,,

where
({\boldsymbol{1}}_{t,i})_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
is the stochastic process taking value
1
in component
i
at time
t
, and
0
otherwise.

We consider the one-sided Gâteaux derivative as we work with long-only portfolios. Thus, when taking directional derivatives with respect to
\theta_{t,i}
, its perturbed values should also be positive, which is guaranteed with non-negative
{\varepsilon}
in Definition
3.1
.

Definition 3.2

For each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, we define the risk contribution of the risk-to-go to the
i^{\text{th}}
investment as

RC_{t,i}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]:={\mathcal{D}}_{i}^{\theta_{t,i}}\,{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]\,.

Note that the risk contributions
RC_{t,i}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
are
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-measurable rvs.

The next result shows that also the risk contribution are positive homogeneous.

Proof 3.6

Proof:

Using the dynamic risk contributions defined in the last section, we now define a dynamic risk budgeting strategy.

Definition 4.1

Let

Proof:

5
Approximation of Risk Budgeting Strategies

While Theorems
4.4
and
4.6
provide a characterisation of risk budgeting strategies as solutions to a sequence of convex optimisation problems, they do not provide a methodology for finding them. Thus, we develop a deep learning approach that leverages the flexibility of neural networks (NNs) to approximate high dimensional functions (see e.g.,
Goodfellow et al. 2016
), together with new techniques that have been developed for optimising convex DRMs (
Coache and Jaimungal 2023
and
Coache et al. 2023
). The latter works solve portfolio allocation and algorithmic trading problems by making explicit use of the dual representation of convex risk measures.
In contrast, our proposed approach relies on the analytical results developed for the class of coherent distortion DRM and in particular their risk contributions given in Theorem
3.7
.

To optimise the performance criterion
L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
(given in Equation (
12
)) of (

P

) we make use of gradient descent methods. The Gâteaux derivative is related to the risk contributions (see Theorem
3.7
) and requires evaluating the risk-to-go
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
and the rv
U_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
. Therefore, we develop algorithms for estimating and sampling from
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
and
U_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
, and for estimating the Gâteaux derivative of
L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
.

The general strategy behind our actor-critic algorithm is:

1.)

Parameterise the strategy
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}
by a NN with parameters
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.

2.)

For estimating
U_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
, we approximate the conditional cdf of
g_{t}:={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}_{t}\Delta{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}+w_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\mathfrak{R}}_{t+1}
given
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, i.e.
F_{g_{t}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(z):=\mathbb{P}(g_{t}\leq z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
, by a NN with parameters
{\mathfrak{f}}
.

3.)

For estimating
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
, we approximate the risk-to-go by a NN with parameters
{\mathfrak{r}}
.

To implement points 2) and 3), we use the notion of elicitability – see Section
5.2
–, which provides a numerically efficient alternative to nested simulations when calculating conditional risk measures and cdfs
(
Coache et al. 2023
)
. Specifically, we use Proposition
D.6
for approximating
F_{g_{t}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}(\cdot)
and Proposition
D.4
for approximating
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
.

The parameterised strategies
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}
are the so-called “actors”, as they are at the investor’s discretion. The approximation of the conditional cdf and the risk-to-go are the “critics”, as they evaluate the effectiveness of a strategy.
In the following subsections, we provide details on the algorithm and the gradients for training the NNs (Section
5.1
), how conditional elicitability is leveraged in the algorithm (Section
5.2
), and the specific NN architectures used (Section
5.3
).

({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t})_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
, where for all
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
, we have
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{B}}
,
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathds{R}}^{m}
. We write, with slight abuse of notation,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}(X_{0},\dots,X_{t};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n\times t}\times{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathds{R}}
. We call
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0:T}
a policy, as it parametrises the investor’s strategy. Next, for
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
we view the criterion in Equation (
12
) as a function of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}
, and aim to minimise it over these parameters. To this end, we write the time
t
loss function as

\begin{split}L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t};\,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1:T}^{*}]:=&\,{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}\big[({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}^{*}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}^{*}})\big]-\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\,b_{t,i}\,\log\theta_{t,i}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}}\,\Bigg]\,,\end{split}

where
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1:T}^{*}
are the optimal policies from time
t+1
onwards.
We adopt for the methodology of deterministic policy gradient and, starting from the current estimate of the parameters, update the parameters using the gradient step rule

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}\;\leftarrow\;{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}-\eta\,\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\beta}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1:T}^{*}]\Big|_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}={\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t}}\,,

where
0<\eta\ll 1
is a learning rate. Hence, we require an efficient way to estimate the gradient of
L_{t}
. By the chain rule, (as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{\beta_{k}}L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\beta}};{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1:T}^{*}]=\;&{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg[\partial_{\beta_{k}}\theta_{t,i}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\Bigg\{\left({\Delta{X}}_{t,i}+\frac{X_{t+1,i}}{\big({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}^{*}}\big)^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}{\mathfrak{R}}_{t+1}\Big[\big({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}^{*}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}^{*}}\big)\Big]\right)\\
&\hskip 50.00008pt\times\gamma_{t}\Big(U_{t}[({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}^{*}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}^{*}})]\Big)-\frac{b_{t,i}}{\theta_{t,i}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}\,\Bigg\}\Bigg]\,.\end{split}

(16)

Given a policy
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, we estimate the expectation in (
16
) using its sample average, which however, requires samples of
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t+1}\Big[\big({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}^{*}}\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}^{*}}\big)\Big]
and
U_{t}[({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}^{*}}\dots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{T}^{*}})]
. Thus, we next elaborate on how we leverage the notion of conditional elicitability to simulate from these rvs.

5.2
Conditional Elicitability

As the risk-to-go
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]=\rho_{t}\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\intercal}{\Delta\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}+w^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\;{\mathfrak{R}}_{t+1}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1:T}]\right)
consists of one-step (conditional) risk measures, both the risk-to-go and the conditional cdf could be estimated via nested simulations, which, however is numerically expensive. To overcome this, we use the notion of elicitable functionals which circumvents the need for nested simulations. The key concept is that a functional is elicitable, e.g., mean,
\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha}
,
\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}
, if it is the minimiser of the expected value of a scoring function. Thus, an elicitable functional may be estimated by solving a convex optimisation problem. Furthermore, conditional elicitable functionals can be estimated by minimising the expected score over arbitrary functions of the conditioning variables (see e.g., Proposition
5.1
). For completeness, in Appendix
D
we collect known results on elicitability and conditional elicitability that are relevant to the exposition, as well as new results pertaining to the specific risk measures considered here.

A large class of one-step distortion risk measures are elicitable – in particular those with piecewise constant weight function – see
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
for the static and
Coache et al. 2023
for the dynamic setting. In the numerical examples we consider a subclass of coherent distortion DRM given by the weighted average of the ES and expected value, but our approach can be generalised to other elicitable coherent distortion DRMs.
Specifically, we consider the family of coherent distortion DRMs
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
parametrised by
p\in[0,1]

\rho_{t}(Z):=p\;\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}(Z\,|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t})+(1-p)\;{\mathbb{E}}[\,Z\,|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]\,,\quad Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}

(17)

which for each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
is a one-step distortion risk measure with weight function
\gamma_{t}(u)=p\,\frac{1}{1-\alpha}{\mathds{1}}_{u\geq\alpha}+(1-p)
. For each
p\in[0,1]
,
\rho_{t}(\cdot)
is coherent since the distortion weight function
\gamma_{t}(\cdot)
is increasing. If
p=0
, then
\rho_{t}(\cdot)={\mathbb{E}}[\cdot|\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\;]
and if
p=1
, then
\rho_{t}(\cdot)=\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}(\cdot|\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\;)
.

For the remainder of this subsection, let
({\boldsymbol{X}},Y)
be a
{\mathcal{F}}
-measurable random vector with joint cdf
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y}
, where
Y
is a univariate rv with cdf
F_{Y}
and
{\boldsymbol{X}}
an
n
-dimensional random vector with cdf
F_{\boldsymbol{X}}
. The next result explains how the one-step risk measures in (
17
) can be estimated using conditional elicitability.

Proposition 5.1

(Mean-ES risk measure)

Let
\rho_{t}
be given in (
17
) with
p\in(0,1)
. Denote by
\mathcal{G}:=\{g~|~g\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}\}
.
Then it holds

\big(\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(Y|{\boldsymbol{X}})\,,\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}(Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}),\,\rho_{t}(Y|{\boldsymbol{X}})\,\big)\;=\argmin_{(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})\in\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}}\;{\mathbb{E}}\big[S_{\rho}(g_{1}({\boldsymbol{X}}),g_{2}({\boldsymbol{X}}),g_{3}({\boldsymbol{X}}),Y)\big]\,,

where, for
0<D<+\infty
,
S_{\rho}
is the strictly consistent scoring function given by

\displaystyle S_{\rho}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},y):

\displaystyle=\log\Big(\frac{z_{2}+D}{y+D}\Big)-\frac{z_{2}}{z_{2}+D}+\frac{\big({\mathds{1}}_{\{y\leq z_{1}\}}-\alpha\big)z_{1}+{\mathds{1}}_{\{y>z_{1}\}}\,y}{(z_{2}+D)(1-\alpha)}+\Big(\frac{z_{3}-p\,z_{2}}{1-p}-y\Big)^{2}\,.

(18)

Thus, to estimate
\rho_{t}(Y|{\boldsymbol{X}})
we proceed similarly to
Fissler and Pesenti 2023
and
Coache et al. 2023
and parameterise each function
g_{i}
with a NN
{\mathfrak{g}}^{i}
with parameters
\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
. Then, the NN parameters are estimated via

\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}}_{1},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}}_{2},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}}_{3}\big)=\argmin_{(\boldsymbol{\nu_{1}},\,\boldsymbol{\nu_{2}},\,\boldsymbol{\nu_{3}})}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{\rho}\Big({\mathfrak{g}}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\nu_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)}),\,{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\nu_{2}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)})\,,{\mathfrak{g}}^{3}_{\boldsymbol{\nu_{3}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)})\,,y^{(k)}\Big)\,,

over independent simulated mini-batches
({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)},y^{(k)})_{k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}
of
({\boldsymbol{X}},Y)
. As
\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}
is always larger than

{\mathfrak{g}}^{4}_{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{2}}
is a NN with parameters
\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{2}
and a softplus output layer, so that
{\mathfrak{g}}^{4}_{\boldsymbol{{\nu}}_{2}}(\cdot)\geq 0
.

Elicitability of cdfs was shown in
Gneiting and Raftery 2007

D.6
in the appendix.

Proposition 5.2

(Conditional Distribution Function)

Denote by
\mathcal{H}:=\{h~|~h\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\times{\mathds{R}}\to[0,1],\;h(\cdot,z)\text{ increasing in $z$}\}
.
Then it holds that

F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}}(\cdot)=\argmin_{F\in\mathcal{H}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[S_{cdf}(F({\boldsymbol{X}},\cdot),Y)\big]\,,\qquad\text{where}\quad S_{cdf}({\boldsymbol{x}},y):=\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(F({\boldsymbol{x}},z)-{\mathds{1}}_{z\geq y}\right)^{2}\,dz\,.

(19)

As above, we parameterise the functions
F\in\mathcal{H}
by NNs
{\mathfrak{F}}
with parameters
{\mathfrak{f}}
and estimate the parameters by

\hat{{\mathfrak{f}}}=\argmin_{{\mathfrak{f}}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Big({\mathfrak{F}}_{\mathfrak{f}}\big({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(k)},z_{l}\big)-{\mathds{1}}_{z_{l}\geq y^{(k)}}\Big)^{2}\,\Delta z,

over independent simulated mini-batches

({\boldsymbol{X}},Y)

7
Conclusion

In this work, we show how an investor can allocate investments in risky assets to attain a predefined risk budget in a dynamic setting. To do so, we first propose a notion of risk contributions for coherent distortion DRM and demonstrate that they satisfy the full allocation property. For the class of coherent distortion DRM, we derive explicit formulae for risk contributions and prove that strategies that attain a particular risk budget are specified by the solution to a collection of convex optimisation problems. Leveraging elicitability of coherent distortion DRM, we further provide a deep learning approach for solving those optimisation problems. Finally, we demonstrate the stability of the numerical scheme through several examples using a stochastic volatility market model.

{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-measurable for every
x\in[0,1]
and for all
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
. Then, the one-step (conditional) distortion risk measure with distortion functions
\{g_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
is the family
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
, where for each
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}

\rho_{t}
is defined as

\rho_{t}(Z):=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\Big[1-g_{t}\big(1-F_{Z|F_{t}}(x)\big)\Big]\,dx+\int_{0}^{+\infty}g_{t}\big(1-F_{Z|F_{t}}(x)\big)\,dx\,.

If
g_{t}(\cdot,\omega)
is absolutely continuous for all
\omega\in\Omega
, then the one-step risk measure admits representation (
2
),
where the distortion weight function
\gamma_{t}:(0,1)\times\Omega\to{\mathds{R}}_{+}
is given by
\gamma_{t}(u,\omega):=\frac{\partial^{-}}{\partial x}g(x,\omega)|_{x=1-u}
, where
\frac{\partial^{-}}{\partial x}
is the left derivative with respect to
x
, for all
u\in(0,1)
and
\omega\in\Omega
, see e.g.,
Dhaene et al. 2012
for a proof in the static setting.

Lemma B.2

Under Assumption
2.1
, any coherent one-step distortion risk measure
\rho_{t}
is a mapping
\rho_{t}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
,
t\in{\mathcal{T}}/\{0\}
and
\rho_{0}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\to{\mathds{R}}
.

Proof B.3

Proof:
Take
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
, then we have by (
2
) that
\rho_{t}(Z)
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-measurable, and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|\rho_{t}(Z)|]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z\,\gamma_{t}(U_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[Z^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[(\gamma_{t}(U_{Z|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}})^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\leq C^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<+\infty,

\square

Lemma B.4

For any
c\geq 0
, the set of admissible strategies
{\mathcal{A}_{c}}
is a convex set.

Proof B.5

Proof:
Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}
be two admissible strategies and define
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(a)}:=a\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}+(1-a)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}
for
a\in[0,1]
. First note that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(a)}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{0:T}

Part 1:

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0:T}^{(a)}
is lower bounded by
c
. Indeed for
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{(a)}=a\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}_{t}+(1-a)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}_{t}>ac+(1-a)c=c
.

Part 2:

it holds that
w^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(a)}}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}^{\infty}
.
For this note that

\displaystyle w^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(a)}}_{t}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(a)\intercal}_{t}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(a)\intercal}_{t+1}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=a\,\frac{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)\intercal}_{t}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}{\left(a\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}_{t+1}+(1-a)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}_{t+1}\right)^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}+(1-a)\frac{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)\intercal}_{t}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}{\left(a\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}_{t+1}+(1-a)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}_{t+1}\right)^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}}

where the first inequality follows as
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)\intercal}_{t}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\geq 0
and the last by admissibility of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}
.

Part 3:

For every

, it holds that

\displaystyle\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\big({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{(a)\intercal}\Delta{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\big)^{2}\,\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\Big(\big(a\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)\,}_{t}+(1-a)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}_{t}\big)^{\intercal}\Delta{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\Big)^{2}\,\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left({\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big(a\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)\,\intercal}_{t}\Delta{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\Big)^{2}\,\bigg]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left({\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big((1-a)\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)\,\intercal}_{t}\Delta{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\Big)^{2}\,\bigg]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

where the inequality is the triangle inequality and the strict inequality by admissibility of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(0)}
and

Proposition B.10

(Lipschitz continuity of One-step Coherent Distortion Risk Measures)

Let
\rho_{t}
be a one-step coherent distortion risk measure with distortion weight function
\gamma_{t}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Then,
\rho_{t}
is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t the conditional
L^{2}
norm, i.e. for any
X,Y\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
, it holds that

\big|\rho_{t}(X)-\rho_{t}(Y)\big|\leq C^{\frac{1}{2}}\;\|X-Y\|_{t}\quad a.s.\,,

where the conditional norm
\|\cdot\|_{t}
is defined for all
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
by
\|Z\|_{t}:=\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[Z^{2}\big|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
\forall\;t\in{\mathcal{T}}/\{0\}
. Furthermore,
\rho_{0}\colon{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\to{\mathds{R}}
.

Proof B.11

Proof
This is an adaption of Lemma 2.1 in
Inoue 2003
to one-step distortion risk measures. By sub-additivity of the conditional distortion risk measure we have a.s.

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(X)-\rho_{t}(Y)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{t}\big(X-Y\big)={\mathbb{E}}\Big[(X-Y)\gamma_{t}(U_{X-Y|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}})\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[(X-Y)^{2}\big|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\;\left({\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big(\gamma_{t}(U_{X-Y|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}})\big)^{2}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{2}}\;||X-Y||_{t}\,.

where in the second inequality we use the conditional Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the last inequality follows by assumption on
\gamma_{t}
. Interchanging
X
and
Y
concludes the proof.

The fact that
\rho_{0}\colon Z_{1}\to{\mathds{R}}
follows by e.g., Def. 6.37 in
Shapiro et al. 2021
.

\square

Proposition B.12

(Interchanging Expectation and Limits)

Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1:T}^{*})

\lim_{{\varepsilon}\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}\left(L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+{\varepsilon}{\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}}]-L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}]\right)=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{N}}}\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\;{\mathcal{D}}_{i}^{{{\delta\theta}_{i}}}\,{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1:T}^{*})]-b_{t,i}\;\frac{{{\delta\theta}_{i}}}{\theta_{t,i}}\;\right].

For any
{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\varepsilon}:={\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\varepsilon}\,{\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(1-{\varepsilon})\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\varepsilon}\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
B.4
,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\varepsilon}
is admissible. Then, by definition, we have that

\displaystyle\hskip-30.00005pt\lim_{{\varepsilon}\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}\left(L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+{\varepsilon}{\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}}]-L_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}]\right)

\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}|A_{t}|\leq{\mathfrak{A}}_{t}

{\mathfrak{A}}_{t}
is independent of
{\varepsilon}
and

, and similarly for
\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}|B_{t}|\leq{\mathfrak{B}}_{t}

is independent of
{\varepsilon}
and
{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathfrak{B}}_{t}\,\big]<+\infty

To this end, by Proposition

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}\big|A_{t}\big|\;

is independent of
{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle C^{-\frac{1}{2}}\;{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{t}\,]

\displaystyle\leq\,\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left({\delta\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}{\Delta\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\right)^{2}\,|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]\,\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\qquad\text{\tiny(by def. $\|\cdot\|_{t}$ and Jensen) }

Proposition B.14

Let
\rho_{t}
be a one-step coherent distortion risk measures with distortion weight function
\gamma_{t}
satisfying Assumption
2.1
. Let
t\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
Y,W\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}

{\varepsilon}\to F^{-1}_{Y+{\varepsilon}W}(u)
is differentiable in a neighbourhood around

with bounded derivative, for all

Proof B.15

Proof:
First we define the conditional cdfs

and

and their corresponding densities by

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}F(y,{\varepsilon})

F_{Y|W}
and

conditional on
W
.
Plugging (
24
) and (
25

23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{\rho_{t}\left(Y+{\varepsilon}\,W\right)-\rho_{t}(Y)}{{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left[W\,f_{Y|W}(y)\right]}{f(y)}\Big|_{y=F^{-1}(U)}\;\gamma_{t}(U)\;\right|\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.{\mathbb{E}}\left[W\;|\;Y=y\right]\Big|_{y=F^{-1}(U)}\;\gamma_{t}(U)\;\right|\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.{\mathbb{E}}\left[W\;|\;F(Y)=U\right]\;\gamma_{t}(U)\;\right|\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[W\;\gamma_{t}(U_{Y|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}})\;|\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\right]\,.

\square

Appendix D
Elicitability

We first recall the classical definition of elicitability and scoring functions, see e.g.,
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
. For this first define the set of cdfs
{\mathcal{M}}:=\{F_{Y}~|~Y\in{\mathcal{F}}_{T+1}\}
.

Definition D.1

(Elicitability)

A functional
{\mathfrak{T}}\colon{\mathcal{M}}\to A
,
A\subset{\mathds{R}}^{k}
, is called
k
-elicitable on
\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
, if there exists a measurable function
S\colon A\times{\mathds{R}}\to[0,\infty]
– called a strictly consistent scoring function – if for all
F\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{M}}}
and for all
z\in A

\int S\big({\mathfrak{T}}(F),y\big)\,dF(y)\leq\int S(z,y)\,dF(y)\,,

(30)

and equality in (
30
) holds only if
z={\mathfrak{T}}(F)
.

In the numerical examples, we consider the family of coherent distortion DRM
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathcal{T}}}
, a convex combination of the mean and ES, given in (
17
). We first illustrate that its static version

\rho(Z):=p\,\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}(Z)+(1-p)\,{\mathbb{E}}[X]\,,\qquad Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{T+1}\,,

(31)

is elicitable.
While the mean is well-known to be 1-elicitable, the
\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}
is only jointly elicitable together with
\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha}
at the same
\alpha
-level. We recall these well-known results.

Proposition D.2

(Mean –
Gneiting 2011
)

Let
\phi\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
be strictly convex with subgradient
\phi^{\prime}
and denote by
{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}\subset{\mathcal{M}}
be the class of cdfs with finite mean such that
\int|\phi(y)|\,\mathrm{d}F(y)<+\infty
for all
F\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}
. Then

S_{{\mathbb{E}}}(z,y):=\phi^{\prime}(z)(z-y)-\phi(z)+\phi(y)\,,\qquad z,y\in{\mathds{R}}\,,

is strictly
{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}
-consistent for the mean.

Proposition D.3

(
(\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES})
–
Acerbi and Szekely 2014
,
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
)

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
A:=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathds{R}}^{2}\,:\,z_{1}\geq z_{2}\}
and define the scoring functions
S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES}}:A\times{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
by

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES}}(z_{1},z_{2},y)

\displaystyle=\big({\mathds{1}}_{\{y\leq z_{1}\}}-\alpha\big)\big(g(z_{1})-g(y)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\Phi^{\prime}(z_{2})\Big(z_{2}-\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}S^{+}_{\alpha}(z_{1},y)\Big)-\Phi(z_{2})+\Phi(y)\,,

where
S^{+}_{\alpha}(z_{1},y)=({\mathds{1}}_{\{y\leq z_{1}\}}-\alpha)z_{1}-{\mathds{1}}_{\{y\leq z_{1}\}}y+y
,

\Phi\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
is strictly convex with subgradient
\Phi^{\prime}
and
g\colon{\mathds{R}}\to{\mathds{R}}
is such that for all
z_{2}\in{\mathds{R}}

\displaystyle z_{1}\mapsto g(z_{1})-z_{1}\Phi^{\prime}(z_{2})/(1-\alpha)

is strictly increasing.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}^{\ddagger}\subset{\mathcal{M}}
be the cdfs with unique
\alpha
-quantile, finite mean, and such that
\int|g(y)|\,dF(y)<+\infty
and
\int|\Phi(y)|\,dF(y)<+\infty
for all
F\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\ddagger}
. Then
S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES}}
is strictly
{\mathcal{M}}^{\ddagger}
-consistent for the couple
(\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha},\textrm{ES}_{\alpha})
.

Next, we show that for
p\in(0,1)
, the risk measure
\rho
given in (
31
) is 3-elicitable, that is jointly elicitable together with
\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha}
and
\textrm{ES}_{\alpha}
. The following proposition is different to Corollary 5.4 in
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
, which states that
\rho
is 4-elicitable, specifically they show that
(\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha},\textrm{ES}_{\alpha},{\mathbb{E}},\rho)

Proposition D.4

(Mean-ES risk measure)

For
p\in(0,1)
, let
\rho
be given in (
31
) and let the assumptions of Propositions
D.2
and
D.3
be enforced. Then the function
S_{\rho}\colon A\times{\mathds{R}}^{2}\to[0,\infty]
given by

\displaystyle S_{\rho}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},y):

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES}}(z_{1},z_{2},y)+S_{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(\frac{z_{3}-p\,z_{2}}{1-p},\;y\right)\,,

(32)

is a strictly
{\mathcal{M}}^{\ddagger}
-consistent scoring function for the triplet

Proof D.5

Proof:
From Propositions
D.2
and
D.3
, the functionals
(\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha},\textrm{ES}_{\alpha})
and
{\mathbb{E}}
are elicitable. Using Lemma 2.6 in
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
, we obtain that
(\textrm{VaR}_{\alpha},\textrm{ES}_{\alpha},{\mathbb{E}})
is elicitable with consistent scoring function given by

S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES},{\mathbb{E}}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},y)=S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES}}(z_{1},z_{2},y)+S_{{\mathbb{E}}}(z_{3},y)\,.

Next, we apply Osband’s revelation principle, see e.g., Theorem 4 in
Gneiting 2011
. First, define the bijective function

g(\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES},{\mathbb{E}})=(\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES},\rho)^{\intercal}
is elicitable with scoring function

S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES},\rho}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},y)=S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES},{\mathbb{E}}}\left(g^{-1}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})^{\intercal},y\right)=S_{\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES}}(z_{1},z_{2},y)+S_{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(\frac{z_{3}-p\,z_{2}}{1-p},\,y\right)\,.

are strictly consistent for
(\textrm{VaR},\textrm{ES})
and
{\mathbb{E}}
, respectively, then

.

\square

In our implementations, we make the specific choices of
\phi(z)=z^{2}
,

and
\Phi(z)=-\log(z+C)
, where
C>0
.

Finally, we need to elicit the conditional cdf
F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to[0,1]
, defined by
F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}}(y):={\mathbb{P}}(Y\leq y\>|\>{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}})
, for any
Y\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{t+1}
,
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}}_{t}
, and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathds{R}}^{n}
.
Cdfs are known to be elicitable with the
continuous ranked probability score
, see e.g. Equation (20) in
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
. Here we recall the key result.

Proposition D.6

(Distribution Function –
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
)

Let
{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}
be the set of cdfs with finite mean. Then the scoring function
S_{cdf}\colon{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}\times{\mathds{R}}\to[0,\infty]
given by

S_{cdf}(F,y):=\int_{\mathds{R}}\left(F(z)-{\mathds{1}}_{z\geq y}\right)^{2}\,dz\,,

(33)

is a strictly consistent scoring function for
F_{Y}
. In particular, it holds that

\argmin_{F\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}}{\mathbb{E}}[\;S_{cdf}(F,Y)\;]\,,

is attained by the cdf
F_{Y}\colon{\mathds{R}}\to[0,1]
.

Next, we consider the concept of conditional elicitability.
Let
({\boldsymbol{X}},Y)
be a random vector with joint cdf
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y}
, where
Y
is a univariate rv with cdf
F_{Y}
and
{\boldsymbol{X}}
an
n
-dimensional random vector with cdf
F_{\boldsymbol{X}}
. Further, let
S\colon A\times{\mathds{R}}\to[0,\infty]
be a strictly consistent scoring function that elicits the functional
{\mathfrak{T}}:{\mathcal{M}}\to A
. Next define the expected score
{\mathfrak{S}}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n+1}\to[0,\infty]
by

{\mathfrak{S}}(F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y},g):=\int S\big(g({\boldsymbol{x}}),y\big)\,F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y}(d{\boldsymbol{x}},dy)=\int\left(\int S\big(g({\boldsymbol{x}}),y\big)\,F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}}}(dy)\right)F_{\boldsymbol{X}}(d{\boldsymbol{x}})\,,

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{n+1}
is the space of cdfs of
(n+1)
–dimensional random vectors, and
\mathcal{G}:=\{g~|~g\colon{\mathds{R}}^{n}\to{\mathds{R}}\,,\text{ with }{\mathfrak{S}}(F,g)<+\infty\}
. Then the expected score satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{S}}(F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y},g)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\int S\big(g({\boldsymbol{x}}),y\big)\,F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}}}(dy)\right)F_{\boldsymbol{X}}(d{\boldsymbol{x}})

(by def.
D.1
)

\displaystyle\geq\int\left(\int S\big({\mathfrak{T}}(F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}}}\big)\,,\,y)\,F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}}}(dy)\right)F_{\boldsymbol{X}}(d{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\int S\big({\mathfrak{T}}(F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}}})\,,\,y\big)\;F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y}(d{\boldsymbol{x}},dy)={\mathfrak{S}}\big(F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y}\,,\,{\mathfrak{T}}(F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}})\big)\,.

Therefore,
{\mathfrak{T}}(F_{Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}})
minimises the expected score
{\mathfrak{S}}(F,g)
over all functions
g
. Note, we may also write
{\mathfrak{S}}(F,g):={\mathbb{E}}[S(g({\boldsymbol{X}}),Y)]
with the expectation taken over a probability measure where
({\boldsymbol{X}},Y)
has cdf
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Y}
. We can approximate the minimiser of this expected score by seeking over a rich parameterised class of functions (such as NNs) and estimate the expectation using the empirical mean from simulations. This is what we use when eliciting approximations of
{\mathfrak{R}}_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
and
U_{t}[{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t:T}]
.
