Covariance Operator Estimation: Sparsity, Lengthscale, and Ensemble Kalman Filters
O. Al-Ghattas, J. Chen, D. Sanz-Alonso, N. Waniorek

Abstract
This paper investigates covariance operator estimation via thresholding. For Gaussian random fields with approximately sparse covariance operators, we establish non-asymptotic bounds on the estimation error in terms of the sparsity level of the covariance and the expected supremum of the field. We prove that thresholded estimators enjoy an exponential improvement in sample complexity compared with the standard sample covariance estimator if the field has a small correlation lengthscale. As an application of the theory, we study thresholded estimation of covariance operators within ensemble Kalman filters.

Introduction

This paper studies thresholded estimation of the covariance operator of a Gaussian random field. Under a sparsity assumption on the covariance model, we bound the estimation error in terms of the sparsity level and the expected supremum of the field. Using this bound, we then analyze covariance operator estimation in the interesting regime where the correlation lengthscale is small, and show that the thresholded covariance estimator achieves an exponential improvement in sample complexity compared with the standard sample covariance estimator. As an application of the theory, we demonstrate the advantage of using thresholded covariance estimators within ensemble Kalman filters.

The first contribution of this paper is to lift the theory of covariance estimation from finite to infinite dimension.
In the finite-dimensional setting, a rich body of work
[
62
,
6
,
24
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
19
,
59
,
3
]
shows that, exploiting various forms of sparsity, it is possible to consistently estimate the covariance matrix
of a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
with
N\sim\log(d_{u})
samples. The sparsity of the covariance matrix
—along with the use of thresholded, tapered, or banded estimators that exploit this structure— facilitates an
exponential improvement in sample complexity relative to the unstructured case, where
N\sim d_{u}
samples are needed
[
4
,
32
,
57
]
.
In this work we investigate the setting
in which
u
is an infinite-dimensional random field with an approximately sparse covariance model. Specifically,
we generalize notions of approximate sparsity often employed in the finite-dimensional covariance estimation literature
[
7
,
15
]
.
We show that the statistical error of thresholded estimators can be bounded in terms of the expected supremum of the field and the sparsity level, the latter of which quantifies the rate of spatial decay of correlations of the random field.
Our analysis not only lifts existing theory from finite to infinite dimension, but also provides non-asymptotic
moment bounds not yet available in finite dimension.

The second contribution of this paper is to showcase the benefit of thresholding in the challenging regime where the correlation lengthscale of the field is small relative to the size of the physical domain. While a vast literature in nonparametric statistics
[
30
]
and approximation theory
[
60
]
highlights the key role of smoothness in determining optimal convergence rates for many nonparametric estimation tasks, our non-asymptotic theory emphasizes that the lengthscale rather than the smoothness of the covariance function drives the difficulty of the estimation problem and the advantage of thresholded estimators. Fields with small correlation lengthscale are ubiquitous in applications. For instance, they arise naturally in climate science and numerical weather forecasting, where global forecasts need to account for the effect of local processes with a small correlation lengthscale, such as cloud formation or propagation of gravitational waves. We show that thresholded estimators achieve an exponential improvement in
sample complexity: For a field with lengthscale
\lambda
in
d
-dimensional physical space, the standard sample covariance requires
N\sim\lambda^{-d}
samples, while thresholded estimators only require
N\sim\log(\lambda^{-d})
. Therefore, our theory suggests that the parameter
\lambda^{-d}
plays the same role in infinite dimension as
d_{u}
in the classical finite-dimensional setting.
To analyze thresholded estimators in the small lengthscale regime, we use our general non-asymptotic moment bounds and the sharp scaling of sparsity level and expected supremum with lengthscale.

The third contribution of this paper is to demonstrate the advantage of using thresholded covariance estimators within ensemble Kalman filters

Perhaps partly for this reason, the empirical success of localization techniques in ensemble Kalman methods is poorly understood, with few exceptions that study localization in finite dimension
[
54

. The work

In this paper, we use our novel non-asymptotic covariance estimation theory to obtain a sufficient sample size to approximate an idealized mean-field ensemble Kalman filter using a localized ensemble Kalman update. In finite dimension,

2.1
Thresholded Estimation of Covariance Operators

Let
u,u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{N}
be
i.i.d.
centered almost surely continuous Gaussian random functions on
D=[0,1]^{d}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
with covariance function (kernel)
k:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
and covariance operator
\mathcal{C}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D),
so that, for
x,x^{\prime}\in D
and
\psi\in L^{2}(D),

k(x,x^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[u(x)u(x^{\prime})\Bigr],\qquad(\mathcal{C}\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}.

The sample covariance function
\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})
and sample covariance operator
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
are defined analogously by

\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_{n}(x)u_{n}(x^{\prime}),\qquad(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\,\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}\widehat{k}(\cdot,x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}.

We introduce the thresholded sample covariance estimators with thresholding parameter
\rho_{N}

\widehat{k}_{\rho_{N}}(x,x^{\prime}):=\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\rho_{N}\}}(x,x^{\prime}),\qquad(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}\,\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}\widehat{k}_{\rho_{N}}(\cdot,x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime},

where
\mathbf{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator function of the set
A
.
Our first main result, Theorem
2.2
below, relies on the following general assumption:

Assumption 2.1
.

u,u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{N}
are
i.i.d.
centered almost surely continuous Gaussian random functions on
D=[0,1]^{d}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
with covariance function
k
. Moreover, the following holds:

(i)

\sup_{x\in D}\mathbb{E}\big[u(x)^{2}\big]=1
.

(ii)

For some
q\in(0,1)
and
R_{q}>0
,
\sup_{x\in D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{D}|k(x,x^{\prime})|^{q}\,dx^{\prime}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq R_{q}
. ∎

We assume fully observed functional data and defer extensions to partially observed data
[
37
,
38
,
63
,
64
,
46
,
26
]
to future work.
Assumption
2.1
(i) normalizes the fields to have unit maximum marginal variance over
D.
Assumption
2.1
(ii) generalizes standard notions of sparsity in finite dimension to our infinite-dimensional setting —refer e.g. to
[
7
,
15
,
59
]
, which study estimation of a covariance matrix
\Sigma=(\sigma_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{u}}
under the row-wise approximate sparsity assumption that
\max_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{u}}|\sigma_{ij}|^{q}\leq\widetilde{R}_{q}^{q}.

Our first main result establishes moment bounds on the
deviation of the thresholded covariance estimator from its target in terms of the approximate sparsity level
R_{q}
and the expected supremum of the field, the latter of which determines the scaling of
\rho_{N}
. We prove Theorem
2.2
and several auxiliary results of independent interest in Section
3
.

Theorem 2.2.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
1\leq c_{0}\leq\sqrt{N}
and set

\displaystyle\rho_{N}

\displaystyle:=c_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}\lor\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big]\lor\frac{1}{N}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big]\Big)^{2}}}\right],

(2.1)

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{N}

\displaystyle:=c_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}\lor\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big)\lor\frac{1}{N}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big)^{2}}}\right].

(2.2)

Then, for any
p\geq 1
,

\big[\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|^{p}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{p}R_{q}^{q}\rho_{N}^{1-q}+\rho_{N}e^{-\frac{c}{p}N\big(\,\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2}\big)},

(2.3)

where
c
is a universal constant.

An appealing feature of Theorem
2.2
is that it holds for
any
sample size
N\geq 1.
The following immediate corollary provides a simplified statement which holds for sufficiently large sample size.

Corollary 2.3.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds and that
\sqrt{N}\geq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big]\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.
Set

\displaystyle\rho_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big],\qquad\widehat{\rho}_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big).

Then, for any
p\geq 1
,

\big[\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|^{p}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{p}R_{q}^{q}\rho_{N}^{1-q}+\rho_{N}e^{-\frac{c}{p}N\rho_{N}^{2}},

where
c
is a universal constant.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
are the first results in the literature to consider covariance operator estimation under the natural sparsity Assumption
2.1
(ii).
As will be discussed next, the first of the two terms in the right-hand side of (
2.3
) is reminiscent of existing results for covariance matrix estimation. The second term in (
2.3
) depends only on the expected supremum of the field, and, as we will show in Subsection
2.2
, it is negligible in the small lengthscale regime.

For covariance matrix estimation under
\ell_{q}
-sparsity,
[
59
, Theorem 6.27]
proves that if the sample covariance matrix satisfies
|\widehat{\Sigma}_{ij}-\Sigma_{ij}|\lesssim\widetilde{\rho}_{N}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq d_{u},
then the error of an estimator with thresholding parameter
\widetilde{\rho}_{N}
can be bounded by
\widetilde{R}_{q}^{q}\widetilde{\rho}_{N}^{1-q},
where
\widetilde{R}_{q}
is a quantity analogous to our
R_{q}
that controls the row-wise
\ell_{q}
-sparsity of
\Sigma
. This explains the choice of thresholding parameter

\widetilde{\rho}_{N}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\log d_{u}}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq d_{u}}u_{i}}}\right]

in finite dimension, which ensures an entry-wise control on the sample covariance matrix with high probability. Analogously, our infinite-dimensional theory relies on sup-norm bounds for the sample covariance function
\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime});
we obtain these bounds in Subsection
3.1
using tools from empirical process theory. For instance, Proposition
3.3
shows that with our choice of thresholding parameter
\rho_{N}
, we have
\sup_{x\in D}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\rho_{N}
with high probability.
Therefore, Theorem
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
reveal that the expected supremum is the key dimension-free quantity that determines the choice of thresholding parameter and the error of estimation in both finite and infinite-dimensional settings. Since in practice the expected supremum of the field (and hence
\rho_{N}
) is unknown, we replace it with
\widehat{\rho}_{N}
to define a computable thresholded estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}
. The concentration of
\widehat{\rho}_{N}
around
\rho_{N}
is established in Lemma
3.4
.

Remark 2.4
.

In contrast to existing results in the finite-dimensional setting (see e.g.
[
7
,
15
,
59
]
) that provide in-probability bounds or moment bounds of order up to
p=2
, Theorem
2.2
provides moment bounds for all
p\geq 1
. For example,
[
59
, Theorem 6.27]
shows a high-probability statement where
\widetilde{\rho}_{N}
necessarily depends on the desired confidence level. Consequently,
[
59
, Theorem 6.27]
cannot be used to derive moment bounds of arbitrary order. In contrast, Theorem
2.2
shows that the tuning parameter of the covariance operator estimator need not be tied to the confidence level. The proof technique therefore contributes to the literature on confidence parameter independent estimators; see e.g.
[
5
]
for an analogous finding that, contrary to standard practice
[
8
]
, the Lasso tuning parameter need not depend on the confidence level.

Remark 2.6
.

As in the finite-dimensional setting
[
15
,
24
]
, our thresholded estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}
is positive semi-definite with high probability, but it is not guaranteed to be positive semi-definite. Fortunately, a simple modification ensures positive semi-definiteness while maintaining the same order of estimation error achieved by the original estimator. Notice that
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}
is a self-adjoint and Hilbert-Schmidt operator since
\int_{D\times D}\big|\widehat{k}_{\rho_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})\big|^{2}dxdx^{\prime}<\infty
, see
[
36
, Example 9.23]
. Therefore, there is an orthonormal basis
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of
L^{2}(D)
consisting of eigenfunctions of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}
such that
\widehat{k}_{\rho_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\varphi_{i}(x)\varphi_{i}(x^{\prime}),
where
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
is the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}
. Let
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}^{+}=\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\lor 0
be the positive part of
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
and define

\widehat{k}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}(x,x^{\prime}):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}^{+}\varphi_{i}(x)\varphi_{i}(x^{\prime}),\qquad(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}\,\psi)(\cdot):=\int_{D}\widehat{k}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}(\cdot,x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}.

Then,
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}
is positive semi-definite and further

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}-\mathcal{C}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}-\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}\|+\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|\leq\max_{i:\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\leq 0}|\widehat{\lambda}_{i}|+\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i:\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\leq 0}|\widehat{\lambda}_{i}-\lambda_{i}|+\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|\leq 2\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|,

where
\lambda_{i}
is the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\mathcal{C}
. Thus,
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}^{+}
is positive semi-definite and attains the same estimation error as the original thresholded estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}
. In light of this fact, we will henceforth assume that
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}
is positive semi-definite wherever needed. ∎

2.2
Small Lengthscale Regime

Our second main result, Theorem
2.8
, shows that in the small lengthscale regime thresholded estimators enjoy an exponential improvement in sample complexity relative to the sample covariance estimator. To formalize this regime, we introduce the following additional assumption:

Assumption 2.7
.

The following holds:

(i)

The covariance function
k
is isotropic and positive, so that
k(x,x^{\prime})=k(|x-x^{\prime}|)>0
. Moreover,
k(r)
is differentiable, strictly decreasing on

[0,\infty),
and satisfies
k(r)\to 0
as
r\to\infty.

(ii)

The kernel
k=k_{\lambda}
depends on a correlation lengthscale parameter
\lambda>0
such that
k_{\lambda}(\alpha r)=k_{\lambda\alpha^{-1}}(r)
for any
\alpha>0
, and
k_{\lambda}(0)=k(0)
is independent of
\lambda.
∎

Assumption
2.7
(i) requires isotropy of the covariance kernel on
D;
this assumption, while restrictive, is often invoked in applications
[
61
,
50
]
. Assumption
2.7
(ii) makes explicit the dependence of the kernel on the correlation lengthscale parameter
\lambda.
As discussed later,
the nonparametric Assumption
2.7
is satisfied by important parametric covariance functions, such as squared exponential and Matérn models.
The
small lengthscale regime
holds whenever the underlying covariance function satisfies Assumption
2.7
and
\lambda
is sufficiently small. In the scientific applications that motivate our work, the dimension of the physical space is small (
d=1,2,3
). Hence, we will treat
d
as a constant in our analysis of the small lenghtscale regime. Theorem
2.8
compares the errors of sample and thresholded covariance estimators.
The proof can be found in Section
4
.

Theorem 2.8.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.7
hold. Let
c_{0}\gtrsim 1
be an absolute constant and set

\widehat{\rho}_{N}:=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big).

There is a universal constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
and
N\gtrsim\log(\lambda^{-d})
, the sample covariance estimator and the thresholded covariance estimator satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}

\displaystyle\asymp\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N}}\lor\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N}\bigg),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\,c(q)\bigg(\frac{\log(\lambda^{-d})}{N}\bigg)^{\frac{1-q}{2}},

(2.5)

where
c(q)
is a constant that depends only on
q
.

Remark 2.9
.

The term
c(q)
in (
2.5
) admits a form

c(q)\asymp\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)^{q}r^{d-1}dr}{\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)r^{d-1}dr},

where
k_{1}(r)
is the kernel function with correlation lengthscale parameter
\lambda=1
in Assumption
2.7
. As an explicit example, for the squared exponential kernel defined in (
2.7
), we have
k_{1}(r)=e^{-r^{2}/2}
and a straightforward calculation shows that
c(q)\asymp q^{-d/2}
.
∎

Theorem
2.8
shows that, for sufficiently small
\lambda,
we need
N\gtrsim\lambda^{-d}
samples to control the relative error of the sample covariance estimator, while
N\gtrsim\log(\lambda^{-d})
samples suffice to control the relative error of the thresholded estimator. The error bound in (
2.5
) is reminiscent of the convergence rate
s_{0}\big(\frac{\log d_{u}}{N}\big)^{(1-q)/2}
of thresholded estimators for
\ell_{q}
-sparse matrices
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{u}}
with sparsity level
s_{0}

[
7
,
15
]
. Therefore, Theorem
2.8
indicates that, in our infinite-dimensional setting, the parameter
\lambda^{-d}
plays an analogous role to
d_{u}
and
c(q)
plays an analogous role to
s_{0}
. However, we remark that the estimation error in Theorem
2.8
is
relative error
, whereas in the finite-dimensional covariance matrix estimation literature
[
7
,
15
,
12
]
, the estimation error is often
absolute error
. While in the finite-dimensional setting the sparsity parameter
s_{0}
may increase with
d_{u},
the constant
c(q)
in our bound (
2.5
) is independent of the lengthscale parameter
\lambda
. Moreover, inspired by the minimax optimality of thresholded estimators for
\ell_{q}
-sparse covariance matrix estimation
[
15
]
, we conjecture that the convergence rate (
2.5
) is also minimax optimal, and we intend to investigate this question in future work.

The bound (
2.4
) for the sample covariance estimator relies on the seminal work
[
41
]
, which shows that, for any sample size
N,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}-\mathcal{C}\|}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{r(\mathcal{C})}{N}}\lor\frac{r(\mathcal{C})}{N},\qquad r(\mathcal{C}):=\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathcal{C})}{\|\mathcal{C}\|}.

(2.6)

Consequently, (
2.4
) follows by a sharp characterization of the operator norm and the trace of
\mathcal{C}
in terms of
\lambda
. In contrast, the bound (
2.5
) for the thresholded estimator relies on our new Theorem
2.2
, and requires an analogous characterization of the thresholding parameter
\rho_{N}
and approximate sparsity level
R_{q}
in terms of
\lambda
.

In the remainder of this subsection, we illustrate Theorem

with a simple numerical experiment where we consider the estimation of covariance operators for squared exponential (SE) and Matérn (Ma) models in dimensions

and

We emphasize that our theory is developed under mild nonparametric assumptions on the covariance kernel
as outlined in Assumption

;
however, for simplicity here we focus on two important parametric models.
For

, define the corresponding covariance functions

\displaystyle k^{\mathrm{SE}}_{\lambda}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}}}\right),

(2.7)

\displaystyle k^{\mathrm{Ma}}_{\lambda}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}}{\lambda}|x-x^{\prime}|}}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}}{\lambda}|x-x^{\prime}|}}\right),

where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function and

denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind. In both cases, the parameter
\lambda
is interpreted as the correlation lengthscale of the field and Assumption

is satisfied. Moreover, Assumption

is satisfied by the squared exponential model, and it is satisfied by the Matérn model provided that the smoothness parameter

\nu>(\frac{d-1}{2}\lor\frac{1}{2})
. We refer to
[
49
, Lemma 4.2]

. For the Matérn model, we take the smoothness parameter to be

We will report results in physical dimension

and

To respectively resolve small lengthscales up to order
\lambda\asymp 10^{-3}
and
\lambda\asymp 10^{-2},
we discretize the domain

with a mesh of
L=1250

In the
d=1

30

to

, and in the
d=2

2.3
Application in Ensemble Kalman Filters

Nonlinear filtering is concerned with online estimation of the state of a dynamical system from partial and noisy observations. Filtering algorithms blend the dynamics and observations by sequentially solving inverse problems of the form

y=\mathcal{A}u+\eta,

(2.9)

where
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
denotes the observation,
u\in L^{2}(D)
denotes the state,
\mathcal{A}:L^{2}(D)\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is a linear observation operator, and
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma)
is the observation error with positive definite covariance matrix
\Gamma
. In Bayesian filtering
[
47
]
, the model dynamics define a prior or
forecast
distribution on the state, which is combined with the data likelihood implied by the observation model (
2.9
) to obtain a posterior or
analysis
distribution. In most applications, the update from forecast to analysis distribution must be implemented through an approximate filtering algorithm. For instance, in operational numerical weather forecasting where the state may represent a temperature field along the surface of the Earth, discretizations of size
10^{9}
are routinely used to capture small lengthscales on the order of kilometers. In this setting, computing exactly the Kalman formulas that define the forecast-to-analysis update would be unfeasible.

Ensemble Kalman filters (EnKFs) are a rich family of algorithms scalable to highly complex data assimilation tasks
[
25
]
, including operational numerical weather forecasting
[
35
]
. The key idea behind these methods is to represent forecast and analysis distributions using an ensemble of
N
particles, so that the computational cost is controlled by the number of particles, which is typically small, rather than by the level of discretization. For instance, in operational weather forecasting
N\sim 10^{2}\ll 10^{9};
we refer to
[
53
]
for a summary of the computational and memory costs of different EnKFs in terms of the discretization level and the number of particles. Taking as input a forecast ensemble
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{C})

and observed data
y
generated according to (
2.9
), EnKFs produce an analysis ensemble
\{\upsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.
Each analysis particle
\upsilon_{n}
is obtained by nudging a forecast particle
u_{n}
towards the observed data
y.
The amount of nudging is controlled by a
Kalman gain
operator to be estimated using the first two moments of the forecast ensemble.
Vanilla implementations of EnKFs rely on the sample covariance, see e.g.
[
47
, Algorithm 10.2]
. However, some form of covariance localization is required for EnKFs to scale to operational settings
[
34
]
. While the use of localization within EnKFs is standard, few works have demonstrated its statistical benefit
[
54
,
3
]
, and none in the functional setting that is most relevant in applications. In this subsection we show that thresholded covariance operator estimators within the EnKF analysis step can dramatically reduce the ensemble size required to approximate an idealized, non-implementable,
mean-field
EnKF that uses the population moments of the forecast distribution. Consequently, we identify an ensemble size which suffices for each EnKF particle to be updated similarly as in the limit of infinite number of particles. We refer to
[
33
,
16
]
for recent works that study the behavior of EnKFs in the mean-field limit.

Define the mean-field EnKF analysis update by

\displaystyle\upsilon_{n}^{\star}

\displaystyle:=u_{n}+\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C})\bigl(y-\mathcal{A}u_{n}-\eta_{n}\bigr),\qquad 1\leq n\leq N,

(2.10)

where
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma)
and

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}(\mathcal{C}):=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{A}^{*}+\Gamma)^{-1}

(2.11)

denotes the Kalman gain. Practical algorithms do not have access to the forecast distribution, and rely instead on the forecast ensemble to estimate both
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathscr{K}
.
We will investigate two popular analysis steps, given by

\displaystyle\upsilon_{n}

\displaystyle:=u_{n}+\mathscr{K}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}})\bigl(y-\mathcal{A}u_{n}-\eta_{n}\bigr),\qquad

\displaystyle 1\leq n\leq N,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{n}^{\,\rho}

\displaystyle:=u_{n}+\mathscr{K}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}})\bigl(y-\mathcal{A}u_{n}-\eta_{n}\bigr),\ \qquad

\displaystyle 1\leq n\leq N.

(2.13)

The analysis step in (
2.12
) is known as the
perturbed observation
or
stochastic
EnKF
[
11
]
. For simplicity of exposition, we will assume here that when updating
u_{n},
this particle is not included in the sample covariance
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
used to define the Kalman gain. This slight modification of the sample covariance will facilitate a cleaner statement and proof of our main result, Theorem
2.10
, without altering the qualitative behavior of the algorithm. The analysis step in (
2.13
) is based on a thresholded covariance operator estimator. Again, we assume that the thresholded estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho_{N}}
is defined without using the particle
u_{n}.

The following result is a direct consequence of our theory on covariance operator estimation in the small lengthscale regime. The proof can be found in Section
5
.

Theorem 2.10 (Approximation of Mean-Field EnKF).

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.7
hold. Let
y
be generated according to (
2.9
) with bounded observation operator
\mathcal{A}:L^{2}(D)\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
. Let
\upsilon_{n}^{\star}
be the mean-field EnKF update in (
2.10
), and let
\upsilon_{n}
and
\upsilon_{n}^{\,\rho}
be the EnKF and localized EnKF updates in (
2.12
) and (
2.13
). Let
c_{0}\gtrsim 1
be an absolute constant and set

\displaystyle\rho_{N}\asymp\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sup_{x\in D}u_{n}(x)\Big).

Then, there is a universal constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
and
N\gtrsim\log(\lambda^{-d})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|\upsilon_{n}-\upsilon_{n}^{\star}|\mid u_{n},\eta_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim c\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N}}\lor\frac{\lambda^{-d}}{N}\bigg),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[|\upsilon_{n}^{\,\rho}-\upsilon_{n}^{\star}|\mid u_{n},\eta_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim c\Bigg[c(q)\bigg(\frac{\log(\lambda^{-d})}{N}\bigg)^{\frac{1-q}{2}}\Bigg],

where
c=\|\mathcal{A}\|\|\Gamma^{-1}\|\|\mathcal{C}\||y-\mathcal{A}u_{n}-\eta_{n}|
.

3
Thresholded Estimation of Covariance Operators

This section studies thresholded estimation of covariance operators in the general setting of Assumption
2.1
.
In Subsection
3.1

. These results are used in Subsection

3.1
Covariance Function Estimation

In this subsection we establish uniform error bounds on the sample covariance function estimator. These bounds will play a central role in our analysis of thresholded estimation of covariance operators developed in the next subsection. We first establish a high-probability bound, which is uniform over both arguments of the covariance function.

Proposition 3.1.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exist positive absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that, for all
t\geq 1
, it holds with probability at least
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}t}
that

\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|\lesssim\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{N}\vee\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big]}}\right]\vee\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]}}\right)^{2}}{N}.

We will apply the product empirical process bound in
[
43
, Theorem 1.13]
. To that end, define the evaluation functional at
x\in D
by

\ell_{x}:u\longmapsto\ell_{x}(u)=u(x)

and write

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_{n}(x)u_{n}(x^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u(x)u(x^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{x}(u_{n})\ell_{x^{\prime}}(u_{n})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell_{x}(u)\ell_{x^{\prime}}(u)}}\right]}}\right|,

so that

\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|=\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal{F}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(u_{n})g(u_{n})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(u)g(u)}}\right]}}\right|,

where
\mathcal{F}:=\{\ell_{x}\}_{x\in D}
denotes the family of evaluation functionals. Note that
\{\ell_{x}\}_{x\in D}
are continuous linear functionals on
C(D),
the space of continuous functions on
D
endowed with its usual topology. We can then apply
[
43
, Theorem 1.13]
(see also
[
3
, Theorem B.11]
) which implies that, with probability
1-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}t}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|\lesssim\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{N}\vee\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{\psi_{2}}\gamma_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right]\vee\frac{\gamma_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2}}}\right)}{N},

(3.1)

where here and henceforth
\gamma_{2}
denotes Talagrand’s generic complexity
[
52
, Definition 2.7.3]
and
\psi_{2}
denotes the Orlicz norm with Orlicz function
\psi(x)=e^{x^{2}}-1,
see e.g.
[
58
, Definition 2.5.6]
. Since
u
is Gaussian, the
\psi_{2}
-norm of linear functionals is equivalent to the
L^{2}
-norm. Hence,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f^{2}(u)}}\right]}=\sup_{x\in D}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u^{2}(x)}}\right]}=\sup_{x\in D}\sqrt{k(x,x)}=1,

(3.2)

where we used Assumption
2.1

1
in the last step.
Next, to control the complexity
\gamma_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2}}}\right),
let

\mathsf{d}(x,x^{\prime}):=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(u(x)-u(x^{\prime}))^{2}}}\right]}=\|\ell_{x}(\cdot)-\ell_{x^{\prime}}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(P)},\quad x,x^{\prime}\in D,

where
P
is the distribution of the random function
u
. Then,

\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F},\psi_{2})\overset{(\text{i})}{\lesssim}\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F},L^{2})=\gamma_{2}(D,\mathsf{d})\overset{(\text{ii})}{\asymp}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right],

(3.3)

where (i) follows by the equivalence of
\psi_{2}
and
L^{2}
norms for linear functionals and (ii) follows by Talagrand’s majorizing-measure theorem
[
52
, Theorem 2.10.1]
. Combining the inequalities (
3.1
), (
3.2
), and (
3.3
) gives the desired result.
∎

Corollary 3.2.

Under Assumption
2.1
,
it holds that, for any
p\geq 1
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in D}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|^{p}}}\right]}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{p}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]}{\sqrt{N}}\vee\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]}}\right)^{2}}{N}.

The result follows by integrating the tail bound in Proposition
3.1
.
∎

In contrast to Proposition
3.1
, the following result provides uniform control over the error when holding fixed one of the two covariance function inputs.
For this easier estimation task, we obtain an improved exponential tail bound that we will use in the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Proposition 3.3.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.
Let
1\leq c_{0}\leq N
and set

\rho_{N}:=c_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{1}{N}\lor\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big]\lor\frac{1}{N}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)\Big]\Big)^{2}}}\right].

Then, for every
x^{\prime}\in D
, it holds with probability at least
1-4e^{-c_{1}N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})}
that

\sup_{x\in D}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|\lesssim\rho_{N}.

We will apply the multiplier empirical process bound in
[
43
, Theorem 4.4]
. To that end, we write

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_{n}(x)u_{n}(x^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u(x)u(x^{\prime})}}\right]}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{x}(u_{n})\ell_{x^{\prime}}(u_{n})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell_{x}(u)\ell_{x^{\prime}}(u)}}\right]}}\right|,

so that for the class
\mathcal{F}:=\{\ell_{x}\}_{x\in D}
of evaluation functionals and for a fixed
g\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in D}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(u_{n})g(u_{n})-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(u)g(u)}}\right]}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(f(u_{n})\xi_{n}-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(u)\xi}}\right]\big)}}\right|,

where
\xi_{n}:=g(u_{n}).
Note that
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}
are i.i.d. copies of
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,k(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\bigr),
where
x^{\prime}\in D
is the point indexed by
g
. By
[
43
, Theorem 4.4]
we have that for any
s,t\geq 1,
it holds with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{1}s^{2}(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}}-2e^{-c_{1}Nt^{2}}
that

\sup_{x\in D}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{st\|\xi\|_{\psi_{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}}\leq\frac{st\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}},

(3.4)

where the last inequality follows by the fact that
\|\xi\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sqrt{k(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})}\leq\sup_{x\in D}\sqrt{k(x,x)}=1.
We consider three cases:

Case 1:

If
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]<\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
, then
\rho_{N}=\frac{c_{0}}{N}<1
. We take

s=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}>1,\qquad t=1,

and then (
3.4
) implies that it holds with probability at least
1-2e^{-c_{1}c_{0}^{2}/N}-2e^{-c_{1}N}\overset{\text{(i)}}{\geq}1-4e^{-c_{1}c_{0}^{2}/N}=1-4e^{-c_{1}N\rho_{N}^{2}}
that

\sup_{x\in D}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{st\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}}=\frac{c_{0}}{N}=\rho_{N},

where (i) follows since
c_{0}<N
by assumption.

Case 2:
If
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\leq\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]\leq\sqrt{N}
, then
\rho_{N}=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]
. In this case, if
\rho_{N}=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]>1
, we take

s=\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}\sqrt{N}}{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}}\geq 1,\qquad t=\sqrt{\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}>1,

and then (
3.4
) implies that it holds with probability at least
1-4e^{-c_{1}c_{0}\sqrt{N}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}=1-4e^{-c_{1}N\rho_{N}}
that

\sup_{x\in D}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{st\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}}=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]=\rho_{N}.

If
\rho_{N}=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]\leq 1
, then we take
s=c_{0}\geq 1
and
t=1
, and (
3.4
) implies that, with probability at least

1-2e^{-c_{1}c_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}}-2e^{-c_{1}N}\geq 1-4e^{-c_{1}c_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}}=1-4e^{-c_{1}N\rho_{N}^{2}},

it holds that

\sup_{x\in D}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{st\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}}=\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]=\rho_{N}.

Case 3:
If
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]>\sqrt{N}
, then
\rho_{N}=\frac{c_{0}}{N}(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}>1.
We take

s=\sqrt{c_{0}}\geq 1,\qquad t=\sqrt{c_{0}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}}>1,

and (
3.4
) implies that it holds with probability at least
1-4e^{-c_{1}c_{0}(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}}=1-4e^{-c_{1}N\rho_{N}}
that

\sup_{x\in D}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\lesssim\frac{st\,\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]}{\sqrt{N}}=\frac{c_{0}}{N}(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}=\rho_{N}.

Combining the three cases above gives the desired result.
∎

3.2
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Before proving Theorem
2.2
, the next result establishes moment and concentration bounds for the estimator
\widehat{\rho}_{N}
of the thresholding parameter
\rho_{N}.

Lemma 3.4.

Under the setting of Theorem
2.2
, it holds that

(A)

For any
p\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}\big[\,\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,p}\big]\lesssim_{p}\rho_{N}^{p}
.

(B)

For any
t\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\widehat{\rho}_{N}<t\rho_{N}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{t})^{2}N(\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)])^{2}}\mathbf{1}\big\{\mathbb{E}[\sup_{x\in D}u(x)]\geq 1/\sqrt{N}\big\}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{t})^{2}N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})}.

(3.6)

The proof of Lemma
3.4
can be found in Appendix
A
.

As shown in Lemma
B.1
in Appendix
B
, the operator norm can be upper bounded as

\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|\leq\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}\,\big|\widehat{k}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})\big|\,dx^{\prime}.

Let
\Omega_{x}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x^{\prime}\in D:|k(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\widehat{\rho}_{N}}}\right\}
and let
\Omega^{c}_{x}
be its complement. Then, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}-\mathcal{C}\|^{p}\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}\ \big|\widehat{k}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})\big|\ dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\
&\leq 2^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega_{x}}\ \big|\widehat{k}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})\big|\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]+2^{p-1}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ \big|\widehat{k}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})\big|\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\
&\lesssim_{p}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega_{x}}\ \big|\widehat{k}_{\widehat{\rho}_{N}}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})\big|\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ |k(x,x^{\prime})|\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|<\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big\}\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ |\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big\}\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|<4|k(x,x^{\prime})|\big\}\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ |\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big\}\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\geq 4|k(x,x^{\prime})|\big\}\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\
&=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4},\end{split}

(3.7)

where in the second inequality we used that

which follows directly from the convexity of

for

We next bound the four terms

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big(R^{q}_{q}\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-q}\land 1\big)^{p}(\widehat{\rho}_{N}+\|\widehat{k}-k\|_{\max})^{p}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(R^{q}_{q}\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-q}\land 1\big)^{2p}\Big]\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\widehat{\rho}_{N}+\|\widehat{k}-k\|_{\max}\big)^{2p}\Big]}.

(3.8)

3.4

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\widehat{\rho}_{N}+\|\widehat{k}-k\|_{\max}\big)^{2p}\Big]\lesssim_{p}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big)^{2p}\big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\widehat{k}-k\|_{\max}^{2p}\Big]\lesssim_{p}\rho_{N}^{2p}.

(3.9)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(R^{q}_{q}\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-q}\land 1\big)^{2p}\Big]

\displaystyle=R_{q}^{2pq}\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-2pq}\land R_{q}^{-2pq}}}\right]=R_{q}^{2pq}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[\big(\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-2pq}\land R_{q}^{-2pq}\big)>t\Big]dt

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(R^{q}_{q}\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-q}\land 1\big)^{2p}\Big]&\leq 2pqR_{q}^{2pq}\int_{\rho_{N}}^{\infty}t^{-2pq-1}dt=R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}.\end{split}

(3.10)

If
R_{q}<\rho_{N}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(R^{q}_{q}\widehat{\rho}_{N}^{\,-q}\land 1\big)^{2p}\Big]=2pqR_{q}^{2pq}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\rho_{N}}^{\infty}+\int_{R_{q}}^{\rho_{N}}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\widehat{\rho}_{N}<t}}\right]\ t^{-2pq-1}dt

\displaystyle=R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}+2pqR_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}\int_{R_{q}\rho_{N}^{-1}}^{1}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\widehat{\rho}_{N}<t\rho_{N}}}\right]\ t^{-2pq-1}dt

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(i)}}{\leq}R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}+2pqR_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}\int_{R_{q}\rho_{N}^{-1}}^{1}2\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{t})^{2}N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})\Big)t^{-2pq-1}dt

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(ii)}}{=}R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}\Bigg[1+8pq\int_{0}^{\sqrt{N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})}(1-\sqrt{R_{q}\rho_{N}^{-1}})}\frac{\big(N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})\big)^{2pq}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}t^{2})}{\big(\sqrt{N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})}-t\big)^{4pq+1}}\ dt\Bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(iii)}}{\lesssim}R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}+R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}\cdot 8pq\bigg(\frac{2R_{q}^{-2pq}\rho_{N}^{2pq}}{4pq}e^{-\frac{1}{8}N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})\big(1-\sqrt{R_{q}\rho_{N}^{-1}}\big)^{2}}+\frac{2^{4pq}}{4pq}\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(iv)}}{\lesssim}_{p}R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}+e^{-cN(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})},

(3.11)

where (i) follows from Lemma
3.4
, (ii) follows by a change of variable, and (iii) follows by applying Lemma

in Appendix

. To prove (iv), notice that if

\big|1-\sqrt{R_{q}\rho_{N}^{-1}}\big|>\frac{1}{2}
and (iv) holds; if

e^{-\frac{1}{8}N(\rho_{N}\land\,\rho_{N}^{2})\big(1-\sqrt{R_{q}\rho_{N}^{-1}}\big)^{2}}\leq 1<16^{p}R_{q}^{2p}\rho_{N}^{-2p}\leq 16^{p}R_{q}^{2pq}\rho_{N}^{-2pq}.

Combining the inequalities (

), and (

For

\begin{split}I_{2}+I_{3}&=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|k(x,x^{\prime})}}\right|\,\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|<\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big\}\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\
&\ +\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ |\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\,\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big\}\,\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|<4|k(x,x^{\prime})|\big\}\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]\\

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\bigg(\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\Omega^{c}_{x}}\ |\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\widehat{\rho}_{N}\big\}\mathbf{1}\big\{|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\geq 4|k(x,x^{\prime})|\big\}\,dx^{\prime}\bigg)^{p}}}\right]

|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})-k(x,x^{\prime})|\geq|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|-|k(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\frac{2}{3}|\widehat{k}(x,x^{\prime})|\geq\frac{2}{3}\widehat{\rho}_{N}.

\displaystyle I_{4}

4
Small Lengthscale Regime

This section studies thresholded estimation of covariance operators under the small lengthscale regime formalized in Assumption
2.7
. We first present three lemmas which establish the sharp scaling of the
L^{q}
-sparsity level, the operator norm of the covariance operator, and the suprema of Gaussian fields in the small lengthscale regime.
Combining these lemmas and Theorem
2.2
, we then prove Theorem
2.8
. Throughout this section, we use the notation “
(\mathcal{B}),\,\lambda\to 0
” to indicate that there is a universal constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that if
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
, the conclusion
(\mathcal{B})
holds.

The following result establishes the scaling of the
L^{q}
-sparsity level in the small lengthscale regime.

Lemma 4.1.

Under Assumption
2.7
, it holds that

\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}|k(x,x^{\prime})|^{q}dx^{\prime}\asymp\lambda^{d}A(d)\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)^{q}r^{d-1}dr,\quad\lambda\to 0,

where
A(d)
denotes the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Using
k_{\lambda}(r)=k_{1}(\lambda^{-1}r)
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}

\displaystyle|k(x,x^{\prime})|^{q}dx^{\prime}\geq\int_{D\times D}k(x,x^{\prime})^{q}dxdx^{\prime}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}}k_{\lambda}(|x-x^{\prime}|)^{q}\,dxdx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}}k_{1}(\lambda^{-1}|x-x^{\prime}|)^{q}\,dxdx^{\prime}=\lambda^{2d}\int_{[0,\lambda^{-1}]^{d}\times[0,\lambda^{-1}]^{d}}k_{1}(|x-x^{\prime}|)^{q}\,dxdx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{i})}{=}\lambda^{2d}\int_{[-\lambda^{-1},\lambda^{-1}]^{d}}k_{1}(|w|)^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\lambda^{-1}-|w_{j}|)\,dw

\displaystyle=\lambda^{d}\int_{[-\lambda^{-1},\lambda^{-1}]^{d}}k_{1}(|w|)^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-\lambda|w_{j}|)\,dw

\displaystyle\overset{(\text{ii})}{\asymp}\lambda^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k_{1}(|w|)^{q}\,dw\overset{(\text{iii})}{=}\lambda^{d}A(d)\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)^{q}r^{d-1}\,dr,\quad\lambda\to 0,

(4.1)

where (i) follows by a change of variables
w=x-x^{\prime},z=x+x^{\prime}
and integrating
z
, (ii) follows by dominated convergence as
\lambda\to 0
, and (iii) follows from the polar coordinate transform in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. On the other hand,

\begin{split}\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}|k(x,x^{\prime})|^{q}dx^{\prime}&\leq\sup_{x\in D}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(|x-x^{\prime}|)^{q}dx^{\prime}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(|x^{\prime}|)^{q}\,dx^{\prime}=\lambda^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k_{1}(|x^{\prime}|)^{q}\,dx^{\prime}=\lambda^{d}A(d)\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)^{q}r^{d-1}\,dr,\end{split}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we establish the scaling of the operator norm of the covariance operator.

Lemma 4.2.

Under Assumption
2.7
, it holds that

\|\mathcal{C}\|\asymp\lambda^{d}A(d)\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)r^{d-1}dr,\quad\lambda\to 0,

where
A(d)
denotes the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

First, the operator norm can be upper bounded by

\|\mathcal{C}\|\leq\sup_{x\in D}\int_{D}|k(x,x^{\prime})|dx^{\prime}\asymp\lambda^{d}A(d)\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)r^{d-1}dr,\quad\lambda\to 0,

where the last step follows by Lemma
4.1
.

For the lower bound, taking the test function
\psi(x)\equiv 1
yields that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}=1}\Big(\int_{D}\Big(\int_{D}k(x,x^{\prime})\psi(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\Big)^{2}dx\Big)^{1/2}\geq\Big(\int_{D}\Big(\int_{D}k(x,x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\Big)^{2}dx\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(i)}}{\geq}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Vol}(D)}}\int_{D\times D}k(x,x^{\prime})dxdx^{\prime}\overset{\text{(ii)}}{=}\int_{D\times D}k(x,x^{\prime})dxdx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(iii)}}{\asymp}\lambda^{d}A(d)\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{1}(r)r^{d-1}\,dr,\quad\lambda\to 0,

where (i) follows by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (ii) follows since
\mathrm{Vol}(D)=1
for
D=[0,1]^{d}
, and (iii) follows from (
4
) with
q=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we establish the scaling of the suprema of Gaussian fields in the small lengthscale regime.

Lemma 4.3.

Under Assumption
2.7

1
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]\asymp\sqrt{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{k(0)-k(c\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}})}\ dt,

where
c
is an absolute constant. Furthermore, if Assumption
2.7

2
also holds, then

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]\asymp\sqrt{k(0)d\log\Big(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{s\lambda}\Big)}\,\,,\quad\lambda\to 0,

where
s>0
is the unique solution of
k_{1}(s)=\frac{1}{2}k(0),
which is independent of
\lambda
.

By Fernique’s theorem
[
28
]
and the discussion in
[
56
, Theorem 6.19]
, for the stationary Gaussian random field
u,
it holds that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{M}(D,\mathsf{d},\varepsilon)}\ d\varepsilon,

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{M}(D,\mathsf{d},\varepsilon)
denotes the smallest cardinality of an
\varepsilon
-net of
D
in the canonical metric
\mathsf{d}
given by

\mathsf{d}(x,x^{\prime}):=\big(\mathbb{E}[(u(x)-u(x^{\prime}))^{2}]\big)^{1/2}=\sqrt{2k(0)-2k(|x-x^{\prime}|)}<\sqrt{2k(0)},\quad x,x^{\prime}\in D.

Since the Gaussian field is assumed to be isotropic under Assumption
2.7

1
, the field is necessarily stationary. Consequently, Fernique’s bound implies that
\mathcal{M}(D,\mathsf{d},\varepsilon)=1
for
\varepsilon\geq\sqrt{2k(0)},
and hence we can assume without loss of generality that
\varepsilon<\sqrt{2k(0)}
in the rest of the proof. Next, notice that

\mathsf{d}(x,x^{\prime})=\sqrt{2k(0)-2k(|x-x^{\prime}|)}\leq\varepsilon\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|x-x^{\prime}|\leq k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2),

where
k^{-1}
is the inverse function of
k
.
By the standard volume argument
[
58
, Proposition 4.2.12]
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}(D,\mathsf{d},\varepsilon)&=\mathcal{M}\bigl(D,|\cdot|,k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)\bigr)\\
&\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)}}}\right)^{d}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(D)}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{2}^{d})}\geq\frac{1}{c_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)}}}\right)^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{2\pi e}}}\right)^{d/2},\end{split}

where we used that

\begin{split}&\mathcal{M}(D,\mathsf{d},\varepsilon)=\mathcal{M}\bigl(D,|\cdot|,k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)\bigr)\\
&\leq\mathcal{M}\bigl(B_{2}^{d},|\cdot|,d^{-1/2}k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)\bigr)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3}{k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)}}}\right)^{d}d^{\,d/2}}}\right]\lor 1,\quad\varepsilon<\sqrt{2k(0)}.\end{split}

Therefore, (

imply that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]&\asymp\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2k(0)}}\sqrt{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)}}}\right)^{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d}{2\pi e}}}\right)^{d/2}}}\right)\vee 0}\ d\varepsilon\\
&\asymp\sqrt{d}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2k(0)}}\sqrt{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c\sqrt{d}}{k^{-1}(k(0)-\varepsilon^{2}/2)}}}\right)\vee 0}\ d\varepsilon.\end{split}

By a change of variable

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\sup_{x\in D}u(x)}}\right]&\asymp\sqrt{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}-t\,\frac{d}{dt}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{k(0)-k(c\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}})}}}\right)\ dt\\
&=\sqrt{d}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t\sqrt{k(0)-k(c\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}})}\,\Big|_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{k(0)-k(c\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}})}\ dt}}\right)\\
&=\sqrt{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{k(0)-k(c\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}})}\ dt,\end{split}

where in the last equality we used that

as

since

is assumed to be differentiable at

Furthermore, if Assumption

2

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d}\int_{t>\sqrt{\log\big(\frac{c\sqrt{d}}{s\lambda}\big)}}\sqrt{k(0)-k_{1}(c\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}})}\ dt\leq\sqrt{d}\int_{t>\sqrt{\log\big(\frac{c\sqrt{d}}{s\lambda}\big)}}\sqrt{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{d}e^{-t^{2}}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ dt

Remark 4.4

6
Conclusions, Discussion, and Future Directions

This paper has studied thresholded estimation of sparse covariance operators, lifting the theory of sparse covariance matrix estimation from finite to infinite dimension. We have established non-asymptotic bounds on the estimation error in terms of the sparsity
level of the covariance and the expected supremum of the field. In the challenging regime where the correlation lengthscale is small, we have shown that estimation via thresholding achieves an exponential improvement in sample complexity over the standard sample covariance estimator. As an application of the theory, we have demonstrated the advantage of using thresholded covariance estimators within ensemble Kalman filters. While our focus has been on studying the statistical benefit of estimation via thresholding, sparsifying the covariance estimator can also lead to significant computational speed-up in downstream tasks
[
29
,
21
,
18
]
.

As mentioned in the discussion of Theorem
2.8
, a natural question is whether the convergence rate of our thresholded estimator is minimax optimal. For
\ell_{q}
-sparse covariance matrix estimation,
[
15
]
established the minimax optimality of thresholded estimators. Inspired by the correspondence between our error bound (
2.5
) and their optimal rate, we conjecture that our thresholded estimator is also minimax optimal in the infinite-dimensional setting.

Another interesting future direction is to relax the assumption of stationarity in our analysis of the small lengthscale regime. In finite dimension,
[
12
]
proposed adaptive thresholding estimators for sparse covariance matrix estimation that account for variability across individual entries and designed a data-driven choice of the prefactor
c_{0}
through cross-validation. Other interesting extensions include covariance operator estimation for heavy-tailed distributions
[
1
]
and robust covariance operator estimation
[
31
,
23
]
. Finally, connections with the thriving topics of infinite-dimensional regression
[
44
]
and operator learning
[
22
,
39
]
will be explored in future work.
