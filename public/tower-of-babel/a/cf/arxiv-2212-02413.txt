23, 381, 6242, 103268, 1743183, …: Hilbert series for CP-violating operators in SMEFT The title sequence is the total number of independent CP-violating operators, as defined in this paper, in the SMEFT with one fermion generation for mass dimensions 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, … .
Dan Kondo, Hitoshi Murayama, Risshin Okabe

Abstract
We introduce a systematic method to classify the Standard Model Effective Field Theory (SMEFT) operators based on their CP properties with the Hilbert series techniques. Our method makes it possible to enumerate operators violating CP symmetry in a few seconds. We present the complete classification of dimension eight operators under CP transformation, and the number of CP-odd or CP-violating operators are listed up to dimension 14. We also provide a companion code in Form that allows anybody to reproduce our results.

2
Two types of charge conjugation

We can think of charge conjugation as an automorphism that interchanges a representation with its complex conjugate representation.
1
1

1

The outer automorphisms for
\mathrm{SO}(4k)
interchange one spinor with another spinor representation, but are not complex conjugation. The case of
\mathrm{SO}(8)
allows for three such possibilities due to its triality. We will not discuss these cases since they are not relevant to the SMEFT.

From this viewpoint, previous papers
[
20
,
21
]
have suggested that there are two inequivalent definitions of charge conjugation for the fundamental representation of
\mathrm{SU}(N)
with even
N
.
However, we found that one of the two charge conjugations fails to be consistent with Hermitian conjugation on field operators.
In this section, it is shown that we can still define two types of charge conjugation on a general compact Lie group by weakening requirements in the previous studies.

2.1
Requirements

Let us consider the charge conjugation
\mathcal{C}
which operates on some unitary representation of a compact Lie group
G
.
We suppose that charge conjugation
\mathcal{C}
interchanges fundamental and anti-fundamental representations.
We impose five requirements on
\mathcal{C}
to deduce its properties:

1.

linearity,

2.

unitarity,

3.

\mathcal{C}^{2}=e^{i\theta}\mathds{1}
with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

4.

compatibility with
G
,

5.

consistency with Hermitian conjugation on field operators.

We will see what each requirement means and discuss properties of
\mathcal{C}
derived from them in the following sections.

2.2
Charge conjugation for fundamental representations

First, we consider the charge conjugation on fundamental representations.
Let us denote the
N
-dimensional fundamental representation of a group
G
by
{\bf N}

and its complex conjugate representation by
\overline{\bf N}
.
The operation of charge conjugation
\mathcal{C}
is not closed within one representation itself because
{\bf N}
and
\overline{\bf N}
interchange among each other under charge conjugation.
Taking this into consideration,
charge conjugation works only on the direct sum
{\bf N}\oplus\overline{\bf N}
.
For simplicity, we discuss a scalar field
\Phi=(\phi_{1},\ \cdots,\ \phi_{N})^{T}
in the representation
{\bf N}
.
In this case, its Hermitian conjugate
\Phi^{\dagger}=(\phi^{\dagger}_{1},\ \cdots,\ \phi^{\dagger}_{N})^{T}
is in the representation
\overline{\bf N}
. Generalizations to fermion fields are straightforward.

The first requirement, linearity, restricts the form of
\mathcal{C}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi\\
\Phi^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C_{-}\\
C_{+}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi\\
\Phi^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)\,.

Here,
C_{\pm}
are
N\times N
matrices.
Note that the symbol
\mathcal{C}
is used for both the group element of charge conjugation and its representation matrix.

Next, we will use the fifth requirement for the consistency of Hermitian conjugation on field operators. This requirement was missing in Refs.
[
20
,
21
]
.
From
section

2.2
, the transformation laws under charge conjugation are

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }C_{-}\Phi^{\dagger}\,,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }C_{+}\Phi\,.

(2.8)

By taking Hermitian conjugation of
eq.

2.7
, we get another expression for transformation of
\Phi^{\dagger}
:

\Phi^{\dagger}\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }C^{*}_{-}\Phi\,.

(2.9)

It should be noted that we considered Hermitian conjugation for quantum operators but not for matrices, which means the Hermitian conjugate of
C_{-}
is
C_{-}^{*}
.

Equations

2.8
and
2.9
implies

C_{-}=C^{*}_{+}.

(2.10)

Therefore, we will use the notation
C_{+}=C
and
C_{-}=C^{*}
.

The second requirement, unitarity, leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{C}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C^{\dagger}\\
C^{T}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C^{*}\\
C&0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C^{\dagger}C&0\\
0&C^{T}C^{*}\end{array}\right)=\mathds{1}\,,

and hence

\displaystyle C^{\dagger}C=CC^{\dagger}=\mathds{1}\,,

(2.17)

which means that the matrix
C
is unitary.

Let us use the third requirement;
\mathcal{C}^{2}
is a phase transformation of fields,
2
2

2

One might think that we need to interchange the first and second columns for the second
\mathcal{C}
operation because
\mathcal{C}
interchanges
{\bf N}
and
\overline{\bf N}
. However, once the representation space is fixed, the representation matrix
\mathcal{C}
should not change its form according to what it operates on. Therefore, we must use the same representation matrices for two
\mathcal{C}
s.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C^{*}\\
C&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C^{*}\\
C&0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C^{*}C&0\\
0&CC^{*}\end{array}\right)=e^{i\theta}\mathds{1}\,.

By comparing each block, we see that

\displaystyle C^{*}C=CC^{*}=e^{i\theta}\mathds{1}\,,

(2.24)

and hence,

\displaystyle C=e^{i\theta}(C^{*})^{-1}=e^{i\theta}C^{T}\,.

(2.25)

Taking transpose, we get

\displaystyle C^{T}=e^{i\theta}\,C\,.

(2.26)

From
eqs.

2.25
and
2.26
, we find

\displaystyle C=e^{i\theta}\,C^{T}=(e^{i\theta})^{2}\,C\,,

(2.27)

which means
e^{i\theta}=\pm 1
.
Furthermore, by taking determinant on the left and right-hand sides of
eq.

2.25
, we find

\displaystyle\det C=(e^{i\theta})^{N}\det C^{T}=(e^{i\theta})^{N}\det C\,,

(2.28)

and hence
(e^{i\theta})^{N}=1
.
For odd
N
, only
e^{i\theta}=1
is allowed, while for even
N
, we have two possibilities
e^{i\theta}=\pm 1
.
Let us use
\mathcal{C}_{S}
for charge conjugation satisfying
\mathcal{C}_{S}^{2}=+1
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
for
\mathcal{C}_{A}^{2}=-1
.
The subscripts
S
and
A
indicate the symmetry of the matrix
C
:
C_{S}=C_{S}^{T}
(symmetric) and
C_{A}=-C_{A}^{T}
(anti-symmetric).

Finally, we will use the fourth requirement, the compatibility with
G
.
To see what this requirement means, we define two discrete groups corresponding to
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}_{S}}

\displaystyle\equiv\{1,\ \mathcal{C}_{S}\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\,,

(2.29)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}_{A}}

\displaystyle\equiv\{1,\ \mathcal{C}_{A},\ -1,-\mathcal{C}_{A}\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{4}\,.

(2.30)

The discrete group
\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
extends
G
to the disconnected group
\widetilde{G}
:

\widetilde{G}\equiv G\rtimes\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}\equiv\{g\gamma\ |\ g\in G,\ \gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{C}}\}\,.

(2.31)

In order for
\widetilde{G}
to be well-defined as a group, the product of two elements
g\gamma,g^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}\in\widetilde{G}
has to be in the form of
g^{\prime\prime}\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in\widetilde{G}
.
Thus, we have

g\gamma\cdot g^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}=(g\gamma g^{\prime}\gamma^{-1})(\gamma\gamma^{\prime})\equiv g^{\prime\prime}\gamma^{\prime\prime}\,.

(2.32)

Therefore,
\gamma g^{\prime}\gamma^{-1}
needs to be an element of
G
.
3
3

3

In other words,
\gamma
is an automorphism of the group
\gamma:G\rightarrow G
which preserves the multiplication rules (endomorphism). An inner automorphism is a conjugation by an element of
\gamma\in G
and hence does not extend the group. What is interesting is an outer automorphism that does not belong to
G
.

This gives our fourth requirement;
\mathcal{C}g\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}
is an element of
G
for all
g\in G
.
Any element of
G
can be represented on
{\bf N}\oplus\overline{\bf N}
as

\displaystyle U(g)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi\\
\Phi^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}&0\\
0&V^{\dagger}e^{-iT^{aT}\omega^{a}}V\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi\\
\Phi^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)\,.

Here
\{T^{a}\}
are the unitary
{\bf N}
representation of Lie algebra of
G

and
\omega^{a}\in\mathbb{R}
are real parameters.
Its complex conjugate representation is equivalent to
\left(e^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}\right)^{*}=e^{-iT^{aT}\omega^{a}}
up to a unitary transformation
V
.
Without loss of generality, we can set
V=1
by changing the basis for the
\overline{\bf N}
representation. Then the element
\mathcal{C}g\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}
is represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}U(g)\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&e^{-i\theta}C^{\dagger}\\
C&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}&0\\
0&e^{-iT^{aT}\omega^{a}}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C^{\dagger}\\
e^{i\theta}C&0\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C^{\dagger}e^{-iT^{aT}\omega^{a}}C&0\\
0&Ce^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}C^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)\,.

At the same time, this is also an element of
G
,
this can be written with other real parameters
\eta^{a}
as

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C^{\dagger}e^{-iT^{aT}\omega^{a}}C&0\\
0&Ce^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}C^{\dagger}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{iT^{a}\eta^{a}}&0\\
0&e^{-iT^{aT}\eta^{a}}\end{array}\right)\,.

Therefore, the lower right block of the right-hand side has to be a complex conjugate of its upper left block:

Ce^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}C^{\dagger}=(C^{\dagger}e^{-iT^{aT}\omega^{a}}C)^{*}\,,

(2.51)

and hence

C^{*}Ce^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}=e^{iT^{a}\omega^{a}}C^{*}C\,.

(2.52)

That is,
C^{*}C
commutes with any element of
G
.
By Schur’s lemma,
C^{*}C
is proportional to an identity matrix

\displaystyle C^{*}C=\alpha\,\mathds{1}\,.

(2.53)

This is exactly the same form as

2.24
. Therefore we find

\alpha=e^{i\theta}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &+1&&\text{for odd $N$}\\
&\pm 1&&\text{for even $N$}\end{aligned}\right.\,.

(2.54)

To summarize,

•

for odd

, charge conjugation has to be symmetric and involutive:

C_{S}^{T}=C_{S}\text{\ \ and \ }\mathcal{C}_{S}^{2}=+\mathds{1}.

•

for even

, there are two possibilities.

–

symmetric:
C_{S}^{T}=C_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{S}^{2}=+\mathds{1}
,

–

C_{A}^{T}=-C_{A}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}^{2}=-\mathds{1}
.

Let us revisit the transformation laws of
\Phi

:

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }C^{*}\Phi^{\dagger}\,,

(2.55)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }C\Phi\,.

(2.56)

For

This phase is sometimes called “charge-conjugation parity” and assigned the symbol

Equations

Previous papers
[

21

require charge conjugation
\mathcal{C}
to be (1) linear, (2) unitary, (3) involutive (

In a similar way we did, they prove that there are two versions of charge conjugation for even

) and anti-symmetric (

However, when we additionally impose the fifth requirement in

, which is necessary in the context of QFT,
\mathcal{C}_{A}
fails to satisfy their third requirement,
\mathcal{C}_{A}^{2}=+1
.
However, we can define

consistently by weakening their third requirement;
\mathcal{C}^{2}
is not necessarily

In this section, we have discussed charge conjugation only for fundamental representations.
If we want to know the symmetry properties of other general representations, we need to consider the action of charge conjugation onto the tensor products of fundamental and anti-fundamental representations.
For example, charge conjugation of the adjoint representation can be understood by the transformation laws of fundamental and anti-fundamental representations.
Let us use the upper indices for fundamental representations and the lower indices for anti-fundamental representations as the tensor notation.
The transformation of the adjoint representation can be written as

{A^{i}}_{j}\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }\eta_{C}(C^{*})^{ik}C_{jl}{A^{l}}_{k}\,,

(2.57)

which is equivalent to

A\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }\eta_{C}C^{*}A^{T}C^{T}=\eta_{C}C^{\dagger}A^{T}C\,.

(2.58)

Since the adjoint representation is real, the overall phase factor
\eta_{C}
is limited to
\pm 1
.
Furthermore, when
A
is a gauge field as
A=A^{a}T^{a}
,

\eta_{C}
must be
-1
to maintain the Lie algebra.

If we require
-C^{\dagger}H^{aT}C=-H^{a}
for the Cartan subalgebra
H^{a}
as in
[
23
]
, the charge conjugation matrix is restricted to the symmetric one
C_{S}
.
This requirement comes from a viewpoint that charge conjugation should reverse all quantum numbers or, in other words, the diagonal components of the Cartan subalgebra.
However, we do not require this condition because such quantum numbers are not directly related to the “charges” of particles in general cases. In addition,
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is much more natural for some cases from the point of view of the Hilbert series as we will see later.
Therefore, we will consider both
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
henceforth.

2.3
Charge conjugation for (pseudo)real representations

For a (pseudo)real representation of Lie algebra
\{T^{a}\}
,
there is a unitary matrix
R
such that

R^{\dagger}(-{T^{a}}^{T})R=T^{a}\,,

(2.59)

where the matrix
R
is symmetric for real representations and anti-symmetric for pseudoreal representations.
In this case, the charge conjugation is an inner automorphism

[
24
]
.

One can define charge conjugation for (pseudo)real representations as a transformation from a representation to the same representation.
This definition is accomplished by setting the matrix
C
, which is defined in the previous section, to be equal to
R
.
In this case, fields in real representations are transformed by
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and those in pseudoreal representations are transformed by
\mathcal{C}_{A}
.
For example, the fundamental representation
{\bf N}
of
\mathrm{SO}(N)
is real. Therefore, as long as the charge conjugation
\mathcal{C}
is defined for the irreducible representation
{\bf N}
, charge conjugation for
{\bf N}
is limited to
\mathcal{C}_{S}
.

4

In contrast, since the fundamental representation of

However, for a pseudoreal representation, if we allow

to transform

Note that, although this is possible, it would require an artificial differentiation of

Let us see an example of the Higgs doublet

.
This is a fundamental representation of

There are two definitions of charge conjugation

When we write the representation of

2.4
Symmetric and anti-symmetric charge conjugations

For even-dimensional representations, we can define charge conjugation in two ways,
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
.
These symmetry properties are independent of the choice of basis.
When we change the basis of
{\bf N}
-representation field
\Phi
by a unitary transformation
\Phi\to U\Phi
, the charge conjugation matrix
\mathcal{C}
changes as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}U&0\\
0&U^{*}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&C^{*}\\
C&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}U^{\dagger}&0\\
0&U^{T}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&UC^{*}U^{T}\\
U^{*}CU^{\dagger}&0\end{array}\right).

The sign of the square of charge conjugation does not change by this transformation:

{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}^{2}=\mathcal{C}^{2}.

(2.70)

Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
cannot be mixed together by the change of the basis.

Similarly,
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
are gauge inequivalent. This is shown by replacing

in

with a group element

.
However, this gauge inequivalence does not mean that the extended groups by

and

Let us consider the case

If we take the choice

(2.71)

there is a group element

, which satisfies

\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0 & C_S^*\\ C_S & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix-J & 0 \\ 0 & -J^*\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0 & C_A^* \\ C_A & 0\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

Therefore, the extended groups are equivalent:

In such a case, charge conjugation

CP violation in the SMEFT

3.1
CP violation at dimension 4 or less

The SM has two CP-violating sources: the phase
\delta_{\text{CKM}}
in the Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa (CKM) matrix and the strong CP phase
\theta_{\text{QCD}}
.
We will briefly review the origins of the two phases and list all U(1) transformations that keep these phases unchanged.
Here we assume that neutrinos are massless up to mass dimension 4 (namely the absence of right-handed neutrinos) and the number of the fermion generations is three.

Let us begin with the
\mathrm{SU}(2)_{W}
doublets and singlets in the quark sector

Q_{i}^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{iL}^{0}\\
d_{iL}^{0}\end{pmatrix},\ u_{iR}^{0},\ d_{iR}^{0}\quad(i=1,2,3)\,.

(3.1)

The upper script 0 means that they are weak eigenstates.
In terms of them, we can write the Yukawa interaction terms

\mathcal{L}_{Y}=-(f_{d})_{ij}\overline{Q_{i}^{0}}Hd_{jR}^{0}-(f_{u})_{ij}\overline{Q_{i}^{0}}\tilde{H}u_{jR}^{0}+\text{h.c.}\,.

(3.2)

Here,
H
is the Higgs doublet and
\tilde{H}=i\sigma^{2}H^{*}
where
\sigma^{I}

The matrices

3.2
Definition of “CP violation by a single operator”

In this paper, we use three different terms for operators:
CP-even
,
CP-odd
, and
CP-violating
operators.
The first two operators are defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

An operator that is invariant under a CP transformation is a CP-even operator.
An operator whose sign is reversed by a CP transformation is a CP-odd operator.

A general operator
\mathcal{O}
is either CP-even, CP-odd, or a linear combination of them.
All operators in the Lagrangian are gauge singlets.
Hence, they are transformed by
\mathcal{C}_{S}\mathcal{P}
under CP transformation.
Since
(\mathcal{C}_{S}\mathcal{P})^{2}=1
, the CP transformation law of operators is classified into three ways:
8
8

8

Here we assume that the operator
\mathcal{O}
consists only of field operators and does not include its coefficient. Therefore,
\mathcal{O}
does not have to be Hermitian by itself.

Type I:
Not self-conjugate

\mathcal{O}\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{CP}\ \ }\mathcal{O}^{CP}\neq\pm\mathcal{O},

Type I​I:
Self-conjugate

\mathcal{O}\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{CP}\ \ }\mathcal{O},

Type I​I​I:
Self-conjugate with a sign flip

\mathcal{O}\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{CP}\ \ }-\mathcal{O}.

From this classification, we find two types of CP-even operators:
the sum of a type-I operator and its conjugate,
\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{O}^{CP}
, and the type-I​I operators themselves.
Similarly, there are two types of CP-odd operators:
the difference between a type-I operator and its conjugate,
\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{O}^{CP}
, and the type-I​I​I operators themselves.

Not all CP-odd operators cause CP violation because the above definition ignores the coefficients of operators.
For example, a type-I operator
\mathcal{O}
has its complex coefficient
\lambda
in the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
.
Because the Lagrangian has to be Hermitian,
\mathcal{L}
has another term
\lambda^{*}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}
.
Because
\mathcal{O}^{CP}
should be proportional to
\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}
, we expect that
\mathcal{O}^{CP}=e^{i\phi}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}
.
In this case, these terms in
\mathcal{L}
are written as

\displaystyle\lambda\mathcal{O}+\lambda^{*}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathcal{O}+\lambda^{*}e^{-i\phi}\mathcal{O}^{CP}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\phi/2}\quantity(\lambda e^{i\phi/2}\mathcal{O} + \lambda^*e^{-i\phi/2} \mathcal{O}^{CP})

\displaystyle=e^{-i\phi/2}\quantity[\Re(\lambda e^{i\phi/2})\qty(\mathcal{O}+\mathcal{O}^{CP}) +i\Im(\lambda e^{i\phi/2})\qty(\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{O}^{CP})]\,.

(3.11)

The second term seems to violate CP. However, sometimes the rephasing of fermions in
\mathcal{O}
can remove the phase of
\lambda e^{i\phi/2}
, which is called the CP phase.
In such a case, we can take
\imaginary(\lambda e^{i\phi/2})=0
, and there is no CP violation by the CP-odd operator
\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{O}^{CP}
.
Therefore, we provide another definition for operators that cause CP violations.

Definition 2
.

If a CP phase of an operator
\mathcal{O}
cannot be removed by rephasing performed while preserving the CP phases in the SM Lagrangian, the CP-odd operator corresponding to
\mathcal{O}
is a CP-violating operator.

In the following part of this paper, we ignore
\mathrm{SU}(2)_{W}
instanton effect.
In this case, as we discussed in
section

3.1
, there are four U(1) symmetries that do not change
\delta_{\text{CKM}}
and
\theta_{\text{QCD}}
.
If a CP-odd operator is not invariant under at least one of four U(1) transformations, its CP phase can be removed by the U(1) transformation.
Therefore, CP-violating operators have to be invariant under all four U(1) transformations.

Note that CP-violating operators are defined as operators that violate CP when
only one
of them is added to the SM Lagrangian.
In general, however, when two or more CP-odd but not CP-violating operators are added to the SM Lagrangian, they can cause CP violation.
This is because rephasing does not necessarily remove all of their CP phases simultaneously.
For example, suppose that there are
N
CP-violating operators and
M
CP-odd but not CP-violating operators, and that the latter contain at least one operator that is not invariant under each of the four U(1) transformations.
Then, we have
N+M-4
independent CP phases. Since we can perform this counting once we have a complete list of CP-odd and CP-violating operators, we do not discuss the case with multiple operators any further in this paper.

\mathrm{tr}(g_{-}^{n})(p,x_{1},y_{c,1},y_{W})\to-\mathrm{tr}(g_{-}^{n})(p,x_{1},y_{c,1},y_{W}^{\prime})
.
Therefore, the same result can be obtained whichever you choose
\mathcal{C}_{S}
or
\mathcal{C}_{A}
.
This result is expected from
eq.

2.73
.
Yet it is clear that the choice of
\mathcal{C}_{A}
is more “natural” in this formulation.

CP-violating operators defined in
section

3.2
are obtained by a simple modification; just add four U(1) symmetries,
\mathrm{U}(1)_{B}
and
\mathrm{U}(1)_{L_{i}}
(
i=1,2,3
) to the Hilbert series
H_{\pm}
.
All you need is two procedures.
First, assign these U(1) charges to the character of each building block.
Second, integrate with four U(1) Haar measures.

5
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we have shown how to classify the SMEFT operators based on CP property by means of the Hilbert series techniques.
We successfully reproduced the same enumerations as those by
[
17
,
19
]
for dimension-six operators and pointed out a misidentification by
[
18
]
for dimension-eight operators.
Our
Form
code can output these results in a few seconds and can list higher-dimensional operators quickly.
Our method can be easily applied to other EFT theories besides the SMEFT, such as QCD EFT or the SMEFT with gravity.

As is mentioned at the end of
section

3.2
, our method correctly lists operators that violate CP that cannot be removed by rephasing when only one of them is added to SMEFT. In the presence of multiple operators, CP-conserving operators on our list can conspire together to violate CP as there is less freedom for rephasing. The classification of such a possibility is beyond the scope of this paper.

Note Added

While we were finalizing this paper, the paper
[
26
]
appeared. They consider Higgs Effective Field Theory (HEFT), not SMEFT, with CP transformation. They introduce right-handed neutrinos and another
\mathrm{SU}(2)_{R}
gauge to allow for separate C and P transformations, which we do not need in our formulation. In addition, they do not consider removing CP-violating phases by rephasing either. Some details of the implementations of CP also are different, and the consistency between theirs and ours will be studied elsewhere, given that SMEFT and HEFT operators cannot be compared directly.

Appendix A
Unitarity of charge conjugation

It is sometimes misunderstood that the charge conjugation is anti-unitary given that it involves a complex conjugation. In this section, we clarify why the apparent complex conjugation is consistent with charge conjugation being unitary. We demonstrate how to define unitary charge conjugation
\mathcal{C}
that is consistent with
eqs.

2.55
and
2.56
. Anti-unitary operations appear only when they involve time reversal.

As in
section

2.2
, let
\Phi=(\phi_{1},\ \cdots,\ \phi_{N})^{T}
be a scalar quantum field operator in the representation
{\bf N}
.
We write them and their Hermitian conjugates in terms of the annihilation operators
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
as

\displaystyle\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\phi_1(x) \\ \vdots\\ \phi_N(x)\endlx@physics@matrix)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}2E}\left[\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a_1({\bf p}) \\ \vdots\\ a_N({\bf p})\endlx@physics@matrix)e^{-ip\cdot x}+\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix b_1^\dag({\bf p}) \\ \vdots\\ b_N^\dag({\bf p})\endlx@physics@matrix)e^{+ip\cdot x}\right]\,,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\phi_1^*(x) \\ \vdots\\ \phi_N^*(x)\endlx@physics@matrix)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}2E}\left[\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a_1^\dag({\bf p}) \\ \vdots\\ a_N^\dag({\bf p})\endlx@physics@matrix)e^{+ip\cdot x}+\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix b_1({\bf p}) \\ \vdots\\ b_N({\bf p})\endlx@physics@matrix)e^{-ip\cdot x}\right]\,.

(A.2)

Here, we assumed that
\Phi
is a scalar field for simplicity, but
the following discussion can be applied to a general field.

By comparing the coefficients of
e^{\pm ip\cdot x}
in
eqs.

2.55
and
2.56
, we find the transformation laws of creation and annihilation operators:

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a_1 \\ \vdots\\ a_N\endlx@physics@matrix)\,

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }\,C\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix b_1 \\ \vdots\\ b_N\endlx@physics@matrix)\,,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix b_1^\dag\\ \vdots\\ b_N^\dag\endlx@physics@matrix)\,

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \ \mathcal{C}\ \ }\,C\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a_1^\dag\\ \vdots\\ a_N^\dag\endlx@physics@matrix)\,.

(A.4)

These give the definition of the unitary charge conjugation operator.
Conversely,
eqs.

A.3
and
A.4
cannot be consistent if
\mathcal{C}
is antiunitary because antiunitary
\mathcal{C}
would transform
e^{\pm ip\cdot x}
to
e^{\mp ip\cdot x}
.
