Weakly concave operators
Sameer Chavan, Jan Stochel

Abstract
. We study a class of left-invertible operators which we call weakly concave operators. It includes the class of concave operators and some subclasses of expansive strict m m -isometries with m > 2 m>2 . We prove a Wold-type decomposition for weakly concave operators. We also obtain a Berger-Shaw-type theorem for analytic finitely cyclic weakly concave operators. The proofs of these results rely heavily on a spectral dichotomy for left-invertible operators. It provides a fairly close relationship, written in terms of the reciprocal automorphism of the Riemann sphere, between the spectra of a left-invertible operator and any of its left inverses. We further place the class of weakly concave operators, as the term 𝒜 1 \mathcal{A}_{1} , in the chain 𝒜 0 ⊆ 𝒜 1 ⊆ … ⊆ 𝒜 ∞ \mathcal{A}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{1}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\infty} of collections of left-invertible operators. We show that most of the aforementioned results can be proved for members of these classes. Subtleties arise depending on whether the index k k of the class 𝒜 k \mathcal{A}_{k} is finite or not. In particular, a Berger-Shaw-type theorem fails to be true for members of 𝒜 ∞ \mathcal{A}_{\infty} . This discrepancy is better revealed in the context of C ∗ C^{*} - and W ∗ W^{*} -algebras.

2.
An invitation to weakly concave operators

It is well known that any concave operator
admits a Wold-type decomposition (see

[
35
, Theorem 3.6]
). Since concave

m
-isometries are necessarily
2
-isometries
(see
[
14
, Proposition 4.9]
), the above
result is insufficient to obtain a Wold-type
decomposition for concave strict
m
-isometries
if
m>2.
As a first step towards obtaining
the Wold-type decomposition for expansive

m
-isometries, we consider a class of operators
containing concave operators and some strict

m
-isometries with
m>2.
Furthermore, in view
of the fact that the Cauchy dual of an expansive

m
-isometry is not necessarily hyponormal (in
contrast to the case of concave operators, see

[
36
, p. 294]
or

[
12
, Theorem 2.9]
), it is natural to
search for a class of left-invertible operators
satisfying a hyponormality-like condition. This
motivates us to define the following notion,
which is central to the present article.

Definition 2.1
.

A left-invertible operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
weakly concave
if it
satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\sigma_{ap}(T)

\displaystyle\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|x\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|Tx\|,\quad x\in\mathcal{H},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|T^{*}x\|^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|Tx\|^{\prime},\quad x\in\mathcal{H},

(2.3)

where
\|\cdot\|^{\prime}
is the norm on
\mathcal{H}
given by

\|x\|^{\prime}:=\|T^{\prime}x\|
for
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
T^{\prime}

denotes the Cauchy dual of
T
.

Remark 2.2
.

In view of
[
26
, Lemma 3.1]
and the inclusion
\partial\sigma(T)\subseteq\sigma_{ap}(T)
(see

[
17
, Proposition VII.6.7]
), it is easy to see that,
under the assumption (
2.2
), the condition (
2.1
)
is equivalent to
\sigma(T)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}

(and to
r(T)\leqslant 1
).

\diamondsuit

According to
[
31
, Lemma 1]
and Remark
2.2
,
concave operators satisfy the conditions (
2.1
) and
(
2.2
). Hence, trying to generalize the concept of
concavity, we included them in Definition
2.1
.
Moreover, since the concavity is antithetical to the
hyponormality (see
[
12
, p. 646]
), it is reasonable to
expect that the third condition (
2.3
), resembling the
hyponormality, may yield some kind of concavity. Although at
first glance it is unclear why we use the term “weakly
concave”, the following theorem sheds more light on this by
connecting, via Cauchy dual, the theory of weakly concave
operators with the theory of hyponormal operators.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a left-invertible operator.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

T
satisfies (
2.3
),

(ii)

T(T^{*}T)^{-1}T^{*}\leqslant T^{*}(T^{*}T)^{-1}T,

(iii)

P_{{\mathcal{R}}(T)}\leqslant T^{*}(T^{*}T)^{-1}T,

(iv)

the restriction of
T^{\prime}
to its range is hyponormal.

In particular, any concave operator is weakly concave.

Proof.

That the conditions (i)-(iii) are equivalent is immediate
from the following identities (see Proposition
1.1
):

\displaystyle T^{\prime*}T^{\prime}=(T^{*}T)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad T^{\prime}T^{*}=P_{{\mathcal{R}}(T)}.

(2.4)

To see that the conditions (i) and (iv) are
equivalent, note that
T^{\prime}|_{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})}
is
hyponormal if and only if

\displaystyle\|(T^{\prime}|_{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})})^{*}T^{\prime}x\|\leqslant\|T^{\prime 2}x\|,\quad x\in\mathcal{H}.

Using (
2.4
) and the equalities

(T^{\prime}|_{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})})^{*}=P_{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})}T^{\prime*}|_{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})}

and
{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})={\mathcal{R}}(T)
, we conclude that the
operator
T^{\prime}|_{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime})}
is hyponormal if and
only if

\displaystyle\|T^{\prime}T^{*}x\|\leqslant\|T^{\prime}Tx\|,\quad x\in\mathcal{H}.

The above inequality coincides with (
2.3
).
This shows that the conditions (i)-(iv) are
equivalent.

To show the remaining part, recall that any concave operator
satisfies (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). Since the Cauchy dual
of a concave operator is hyponormal (see
[
36
, p. 294]
) and the restriction of a hyponormal operator to
its closed invariant subspace is hyponormal (see

[
18
, Proposition II.4.4]
), an application of the
implication (iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) completes the proof.
∎

Our next goal is to show that the only
invertible weakly concave operators are unitary
operators (cf. Corollary
2.5
). In
fact, this is a consequence of a much more
general fact proved below.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfy (
2.1
)
and let
T^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{H}_{u}^{\prime}}
be hyponormal, where

\mathcal{H}_{u}^{\prime}:=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{R}}(T^{\prime n})
. Then the following statements are
valid:

(i)

T
is unitary if
T
is invertible,

(ii)

T
is completely
non-normal if
T
is analytic.

Proof.

(i) Assume that
T
is invertible. Since

\partial\sigma(T)\subseteq\sigma_{ap}(T)
, we
infer from (
2.1
) that
\sigma(T)\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D}
. Hence

\displaystyle\sigma(T^{*})=\sigma(T)^{*}\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D}.

(2.5)

Noting that the invertibility of
T
implies the
invertibility of
T^{\prime}
, we deduce that
\mathcal{H}_{u}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}
. From the
assumption that
T^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{H}_{u}^{\prime}}
is hyponormal, we see that

T^{\prime}
is hyponormal. However, by invertibility of
T
,

T^{\prime}=T^{*-1},
which by
[
18
, Proposition II.4.4]
implies
that
T^{*}
is hyponormal. Hence, by (
2.5
) and the
second corollary on
[
40
, Pg 473]
,
T
is unitary.

(ii) Assume now that
T
is analytic. Suppose to the contrary
that
T
has a non-zero normal direct summand, say
N
. Then
by (
2.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sigma(N)=\sigma_{ap}(N)\subseteq\sigma_{ap}(T)\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D}.

Hence,
N
is unitary. This contradicts the fact that every
analytic operator is completely non-unitary.
∎

The following corollary is immediate from
Propositions
2.3
and

2.4
and

[
18
, Proposition II.4.4]
.

Corollary 2.5
.

If
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is weakly concave,
then the following statements are valid:

(i)

T
is unitary if
T
is invertible,

(ii)

T
is completely
non-normal if
T
is analytic.

3.
Examples

In this section, we show that there are weakly concave
operators which are not concave (see Example

below). This is done by means of unilateral weighted shifts.
Recall that for a bounded sequence
\{w_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

of positive real numbers, there exists a unique operator

(called a

\displaystyle We_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=w_{n}e_{n+1},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

the Hilbert space of square

\mathbb{Z}_{+}.
Note that
W

is always analytic. Before providing the aforementioned
example, we need some auxiliary results. We begin by
characterizing those unilateral weighted shifts whose given
power is left-invertible and satisfies the
condition (
2.3
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
W
be a unilateral weighted shift with
weight sequence

and let

(i)

4.
A spectral dichotomy

Recall the well-known fact that the spectra of
an invertible operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

and its inverse
T^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are
related to each other and the correspondence is
given by

\displaystyle\sigma(T^{-1})=\phi(\sigma(T)),

(4.1)

where
\phi(\lambda)=1/\lambda
is an automorphism of the
punctured plane
\mathbb{C}_{\bullet}:=\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
(see
[
39
, Theorem 2.3.2]
for the general version
of the spectral mapping theorem). A question worth
considering in the context of the present paper is to find
the counterpart of this simple fact for left-invertible
operators
T
. More specifically, if
L
is a left-inverse
of
T
, then what is the relationship between the spectra of

T
and
L
. The purpose of this section is largely to answer
this question. It is worth noting that among the left
inverses of a left-invertible operator
T
there exist such
as
T^{\prime*}
, where
T^{\prime}
is the Cauchy dual of

T
(see
[
30
, Proposition 5.1]
for concrete
examples).

Before stating the main result of this section,
observe that if
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

is left-invertible and
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a left-inverse of

T
, then

0\in\sigma(T)
if and only if
0\in\sigma(L)
.

(4.2)

Theorem 4.1

(Spectral Dichotomy)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a left-invertible
operator with a left-inverse
L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Consider the automorphism
\phi
of the
punctured plane
\mathbb{C}_{\bullet}
given by

\phi(z)=1/{z}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{\bullet},
and extend it to the Riemann
sphere
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}

and
\phi(\infty)=0.
Then

either

or

\phi(\sigma(L))=\sigma(T)

(4.3)

according as

or
0\notin\sigma(T)

(

any case,

and

\displaystyle\phi(\partial\sigma(L))\subseteq\sigma_{r}(T).

(4.4)

Proof.

We begin by noting that

\displaystyle L-\lambda I=L(I-\lambda T)=-\lambda L(T-\lambda^{-1}I),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\bullet}.

(4.5)

Second, there is no loss of generality in

L
is

\displaystyle 0\in\sigma_{r}(T)\setminus\sigma_{l}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad 0\in\sigma_{l}(L)\setminus\sigma_{r}(L).

(4.6)

We claim that

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbb{C}_{\bullet}\setminus\sigma_{l}(L))\subseteq\sigma_{r}(T)\setminus\sigma_{l}(T).

(4.7)

For, assume that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{\bullet}\setminus\sigma_{l}(L)

such that

\displaystyle L_{1}(L-\lambda I)=I.

(4.8)

Hence, by (

is

\lambda^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma_{l}(T)
. Thus, to get

Suppose, to

Right-multiplying (
4.5

\displaystyle(L-\lambda I)(T-\lambda^{-1}I)^{-1}=-\lambda L.

Now left-multiplying (

leads

\displaystyle(T-\lambda^{-1}I)^{-1}\overset{\eqref{inv-ab}}{=}L_{1}(L-\lambda I)(T-\lambda^{-1}I)^{-1}=-\lambda L_{1}L.

(4.10)

Finally, left-multiplying (

\displaystyle I=\big(-\lambda(T-\lambda^{-1}I)L_{1}\big)L.

This means that

Combined with (

To prove (

\displaystyle 0\in\partial\sigma(L)\Longleftrightarrow 0\in\partial\sigma(L^{*}).

(4.11)

Next, since

Combined with (

This completes the proof.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let

be a left-invertible

be the Cauchy dual of

\displaystyle\sigma_{l}(L)=\sigma_{l}(T^{\prime*})=\sigma_{r}(T^{\prime})^{*}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

t\in\mathcal{C}
be an expansion.
Define the sequences
\{p_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty},

\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
and

\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{C}
by

p_{k}=t^{\prime k}c_{k}^{-1}t^{\prime*k},

u_{k}=t^{\prime(k+1)}c_{k}^{-1}t^{\prime*(k+1)}
and
v_{k}=t^{\prime k}c_{k}^{-1}c_{k+1}c_{k}^{-1}t^{\prime*k}.

(8.2)

Then the following statements hold for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
:

(i)

p_{k}=p_{k}^{*}p_{k},

p_{k}p_{k+1}=p_{k+1}
and
0\leqslant p_{k+1}\leqslant p_{k}\leqslant e,

(ii)

0\leqslant c_{k+1}\leqslant c_{k}\leqslant e
and
c_{k}^{-1/2}c_{k+1}c_{k}^{-1/2}\leqslant e,

(iii)

u_{k}=t^{\prime}p_{k}t^{\prime*},

0\leqslant t^{\prime(k+1)}t^{\prime*(k+1)}\leqslant u_{k}\leqslant p_{k+1}\leqslant e
and
u_{k+1}\leqslant u_{k},

(iv)

v_{k+1}=p_{k+1}v_{k}p_{k+1},

v_{k}=p_{k}t^{\prime*}t^{\prime}p_{k}
and
0\leqslant v_{k}\leqslant p_{k}\leqslant e.

Moreover,
t
is an expansive element of
C^{*}(t)
and the
elements
t^{\prime}
,
c_{k}
,
c_{k}^{-1}
,
p_{k}
,
u_{k}
and

v_{k}
belong to
C^{*}(t).

Proof.

(i) By definition,
p_{k}=p_{k}^{*}
and

\displaystyle p_{k}^{2}=t^{\prime k}c_{k}^{-1}(t^{\prime*k}t^{\prime k})c_{k}^{-1}t^{\prime*k}=p_{k},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(8.3)

so
p_{k}=p_{k}^{*}p_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.
Since

\displaystyle p_{k}p_{k+1}=t^{\prime k}c_{k}^{-1}(t^{\prime*k}t^{\prime k})t^{\prime}c_{k+1}^{-1}t^{\prime*(k+1)}=p_{k+1},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(8.4)

and thus
p_{k}p_{k+1}=p_{k+1}p_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},
we deduce that

\displaystyle(p_{k}-p_{k+1})^{2}\overset{\eqref{pikay}}{=}p_{k}-2p_{k}p_{k+1}+p_{k+1}\overset{\eqref{pikaypi}}{=}p_{k}-p_{k+1},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Hence,
p_{k+1}\leqslant p_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Since
e-p_{k}
is a selfadjoint idempotent, we
see that
p_{k}\leqslant e
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.
This
proves (i).

(ii) We can argue as in the second paragraph of
the proof of Corollary
7.5
(the
arguments given there are purely

C^{*}
-algebraic).

(iii) The identity
u_{k}=t^{\prime}p_{k}t^{\prime*}
is
obvious. It follows from (ii) that
c_{k}^{-1}\geqslant e
and

c_{k}^{-1}\leqslant c_{k+1}^{-1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, so

\displaystyle 0\leqslant t^{\prime(k+1)}t^{\prime*(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\overset{u_{k}}{\overbrace{t^{\prime(k+1)}c_{k}^{-1}t^{\prime*(k+1)}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant t^{\prime(k+1)}c_{k+1}^{-1}t^{\prime*(k+1)}=p_{k+1}\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{\leqslant}e,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

which gives the second part of (iii). The third
part of (iii) follows from

\displaystyle u_{k+1}=t^{\prime}p_{k+1}t^{\prime*}\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{\leqslant}t^{\prime}p_{k}t^{\prime*}=u_{k},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(iv) It is easy to see that the identity
v_{k}=p_{k}t^{\prime*}t^{\prime}p_{k}
is valid. Hence,
by (i) and (ii), we have

\displaystyle 0\leqslant v_{k}=p_{k}c_{1}p_{k}\leqslant p_{k}^{2}=p_{k}\leqslant e,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

which proves the third part of (iv). Finally, we
come to the conclusion that

\displaystyle v_{k+1}=p_{k+1}(t^{\prime*}t^{\prime})p_{k+1}\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{=}p_{k+1}(p_{k}(t^{\prime*}t^{\prime})p_{k})p_{k+1}=p_{k+1}v_{k}p_{k+1},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

which gives the first identity in (iv).

The proof of the “moreover” part is as
follows. That
t
is an expansive element of

C^{*}(t)
and
t^{\prime},c_{k},c_{k}^{-1}\in C^{*}(t)
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
follows from
(
8.1
) and the fact that

\sigma_{C^{*}(t)}(s)=\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}(s)
for
every
s\in C^{*}(t)
(see

[
17
, Proposition VIII.1.14]
; see also

[
17
, Theorem VIII.3.6]
). As a consequence,
each of the elements
p_{k}
,
u_{k}
and
v_{k}

belongs to
C^{*}(t).
This completes the proof.
∎
