Ehrenfest’s theorem beyond the Ehrenfest time
Felipe Hernández, Daniel Ranard, C. Jess Riedel

Abstract
In closed quantum systems, wavepackets can spread exponentially in time due to chaos, forming long-range superpositions in just seconds for ordinary macroscopic systems. A weakly coupled environment is conjectured to decohere the system and restore the quantum-classical correspondence while necessarily introducing diffusive noise—but at what coupling strength, and under which conditions? For Markovian open systems with Hamiltonians of the form H ^ = p ^ 2 / 2 ​ m + V ⁡ ( x ^ ) \hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V(\hat{x}) and Hermitian linear Lindblad operators, we prove the quantum and classical evolutions are close whenever the strength of the environment-induced diffusion satisfies D ≫ ( ℏ / s H ) 4 / 3 ​ D H D\gg(\hbar/s_{H})^{4/3}D_{H} , where s H s_{H} and D H D_{H} are characteristic action and diffusion scales that we define precisely using the classical Hamiltonian H H . The bound applies for all observables and for times exponentially longer than the Ehrenfest timescale, which is when the correspondence can break down in closed systems. The strength of the diffusive noise can vanish in the classical limit to give the appearance of reversible dynamics. The 4 / 3 4/3 exponent may be optimal, suggested by heuristic arguments and prior numerical evidence. Based on our bound, we give an efficient classical algorithm for simulating quantum Lindblad dynamics, which becomes provably accurate when the strength of environmental coupling exceeds the above threshold.

We study the macroscopic emergence of classical mechanics from quantum mechanics in the limit when
\hbar
is small compared to the characteristic features of the system. This is a well-studied topic in the case of
closed
quantum systems – that is to say, in Hamiltonian systems isolated from any influence from an external environment – where classical and quantum observables are known
[
egorov1969canonical
,
hagedorn2000exponentially
,
silvestrov2002ehrenfest
]

by Egorov’s theorem
[
egorov1969canonical
]
to match closely for times up to the Ehrenfest time
\tau_{\mathrm{E}}\sim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\log(s_{H}/\hbar)
. This timescale is governed by the dominant Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
and the characteristic action scale
s_{H}
of the classical dynamics, and it quantifies the time for a minimal uncertainty wavepacket to spread significantly due to chaos. The above correspondence arises essentially because of Ehrenfest’s theorem
[
ehrenfest1927bemerkung
]
, which implies that a localized
wavepacket will approximately follow a classical equation of motion as long as it remains well-localized.

For real macroscopic systems, the Ehrenfest time can be quite short—even seconds or minutes—because the dependence on
\hbar^{-1}
is only logarithmic
[
berman1978condition
,
berry1979quantum
,
chirikov1988quantum
]
.
In closed systems beyond the Ehrenfest time, even as
\hbar\to 0
, the correspondence between the classical and quantum evolution breaks down. In particular,

(1)

superpositions over macroscopic distances are generated, detectable through delicate interference experiments
[
haake1987classical
,
zurek1994decoherence
,
zurek1998decoherence
,
habib1998decoherence
,
berry2001chaos
,
silvestrov2002ehrenfest
]
(cf.
[
casati1995comment
,
wiebe2005quantum
,
schlosshauer2008classicality
]
);
relatedly, the Wigner function develops negativity
[
berry1979quantum
,
korsch1981evolution
,
zurek2001subplanck
]
(cf.
[
tomsovic1991semiclassical
]
);
see Fig.
1
.

(2)

large differences possibly arise between quantum expectation values and the corresponding classical predictions even for smooth observables like
\hat{x}^{2}
, according to some numerical studies
[
karkuszewski2002breakdown
,
carvalho2004environmental
]
(cf.
[
emerson2002quantum
]
).

In other words, the
\hbar\to 0
and
t\to\infty
limits do not commute in closed systems: if one fixes a time duration
t>0
and takes
\hbar\to 0
, the quantum state trajectory approaches the classical state trajectory, but if one fixes arbitrarily small
\hbar>0
and takes
t\to\infty
, then the quantum trajectory may develop superpositions over macroscopic distances. The limit is
singular
in the sense of Berry
[
berry1995asymptotics
,
berry2001chaos
]
.

Despite this theoretical breakdown,
macroscopic systems appear to obey the laws of classical mechanics for much longer times. To theoretically justify a quantum-classical correspondence beyond the Ehrenfest time, one longstanding suggestion is to consider decoherence effects from the environment, acknowledging that macroscopic systems are rarely well-isolated
[
zeh1973quantum
,
zurek1981pointer
,
zurek1994decoherence
,
shiokawa1995decoherence
,
zurek1998decoherence
]

(cf.
[
ballentine1994inadequacy
,
fox1994chaos1
,
schlautmann1995measurement
,
casati1995comment
,
emerson2002quantum
,
wiebe2005quantum
,
kofler2007classical
]
).
Numerical simulations and analytical arguments suggest that decoherence successfully restores the quantitative agreement between the quantum and classical evolution of many specific systems
[
spiller1994emergence
,
habib1998decoherence
,
bhattacharya2000continuous
,
bhattacharya2003continuous
,
toscano2005decoherence
]
.
In particular, it was conjectured that there is a regime where the system-bath coupling is (i) large enough for decoherence to ensure classicality by inhibiting long-range coherence in phase space,
but still (ii) small enough that the noise introduced by the bath does not significantly alter the classical dynamics on fixed time scales, so the system
appears
well-isolated
[
bhattacharya2000continuous
,
bhattacharya2003continuous
,
toscano2005decoherence
]
.

In this work we prove several aspects of the quantum-classical correspondence suggested above using decoherence modeled by the Lindblad equation. Our basic strategy is to generalize the Glauber-Sudarshan P function
[
glauber1963coherent
,
sudarshan1963equivalence
]

to include
variously
but
boundedly
squeezed Gaussian pure states and then evolve these states with the local harmonic approximation to the dynamics (i.e., an open-system generalization of Heller’s thawed-Gaussian approximation

[
heller1975time
]
).

The results presented here apply to Hamiltonians of the common form
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V(\hat{x})
with Hermitian linear Lindblad operators (and hence no friction).
(The case of general Hamiltonians
\hat{H}(\hat{x},\hat{p})
and Lindblad operators
\hat{L}_{k}(\hat{x},\hat{p})
is presented in companion work
[
hernandez2023decoherence2
]
.)
Such a system may represent multiple particles in multiple spatial dimensions, with general position-dependent interactions. (The kinetic term could be replaced with a general quadratic function of momenta, allowing particles with distinct masses, but we omit this variation for simplicity.) With
N
particles in
n
spatial dimensions, we have
d=Nn
degrees of freedom.

The Hermitian linear Lindblad operators used here may be seen as the first-order approximation of a general environmental interaction, and their effect is to introduce both decoherence and noise in phase space.
The momentum variance
\langle\hat{p}^{2}\rangle
of a Gaussian wavepacket grows like the momentum diffusion rate,
\dot{\sigma}^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}\sim D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}
, while the coherence between wavepackets separated by distance
\Delta x
decays at the “localization rate”
\Lambda_{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\Delta x^{2}
for
\Lambda_{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}=\hbar^{-2}D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}

[
joos1985emergence
,
zurek1991decoherence
]
. (The same holds under exchange
x\leftrightarrow p
.) Because of the factor of
\hbar^{-2}
, we can simultaneously have
\Lambda_{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\to\infty
and
D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}\to 0
in the classical limit
\hbar\to 0
, giving the appearance of reversible noiseless dynamics while keeping macroscopic superpositions suppressed.

We prove that given sufficient diffusion, for times
t\ll\hbar^{-1/2},

the quantum evolution under the Lindblad equation is well-approximated by the above classical evolution for all possible observables, in a sense we will make precise.
This timescale is exponentially longer than the Ehrenfest time (as defined via local Lyapunov exponent; see Section
2
).
Indeed, for a macroscopic system with characteristic action scale

s_{H}\sim 1\,\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{m}^{2}/\mathrm{s}
and Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\sim 1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
, the Ehrenfest timescale is
\tau_{\mathrm{E}}\sim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\log(s_{H}/\hbar)\sim 1
minute, while the bound presented below extends to the timescale
\tau\sim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\sqrt{s_{H}/\hbar}\sim 10^{17}\,\mathrm{s}\sim 1
billion years.

We find the above quantum-classical correspondence holds when the environment-induced diffusion satisfies
D\gg\hbar^{4/3}
. Remarkably, evidence from Toscano et al.
[
toscano2005decoherence
,
wisniacki2009scaling
]

suggests this condition is actually necessary, in contrast to the weaker condition
D\gg\hbar^{2}
often suggested
[
kolovsky1994remark
,
zurek2003decoherence
,
pattanayak2003parameter
]
;
then
D\sim\hbar^{4/3}
may be a genuine threshold for the correspondence. We offer a heuristic argument later with Eq. (
4.1
).

1
Preliminaries

For a quantum system of finite
d
degrees of freedom,
we consider the quantum evolution
\rho(t)
under the Lindblad equation
\partial_{t}\rho(t)=\hat{\mathcal{L}}[\rho(t)]
with Lindbladian

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\frac{-i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\rho]+\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{k}(\hat{L}_{k}\rho\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k},\rho\}).

(1.1)

We treat the case of Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V(\hat{x})
with Hermitian linear Lindblad operators
\hat{L}_{k}=\ell_{k}\cdot(\hat{x},\hat{p})=\ell_{k,\mathrm{x}}\cdot\hat{x}+\ell_{k,\mathrm{p}}\cdot\hat{p}
for
\ell_{k}=(\ell_{k,\mathrm{x}},\ell_{k,\mathrm{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The corresponding classical dynamics are given by a frictionless Fokker-Planck equation
\partial_{t}f=\mathcal{L}[f]
using the Liouvillian

[
risken1984fokkerplanck
]

\mathcal{L}[f]=-\omega^{ab}(\partial_{a}f)(\partial_{b}H)+\frac{1}{2}D^{ab}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}f,

(1.2)

where the first term above produces classical Hamiltonian flow using
H=p^{2}/2m+V(x)
, while the second term above produces diffusion described by the positive semidefinite matrix
D^{ab}=\hbar\omega^{ac}\omega^{bd}\sum_{k}\Real\ell_{k,a}\ell_{k,b}^{*}
. Here,
\omega=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\mathds{1}_{d}\\
-\mathds{1}_{d}&0\end{smallmatrix}\big)
is the (antisymmetric) symplectic form and
\partial_{a}
denotes the partial derivative in phase space, where
the indices
a,b\in\{\mathrm{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{x}_{d},\mathrm{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{p}_{d}\}
range over the
2d
directions in phase space and
repeated indices are summed.

Just as the diffusion equation arises from a random walk, the Fokker-Planck equation in (
1.2
) could also be interpreted as arising from a Langevin equation
[
kerr2000generalized
]
, describing noisy trajectories on phase space. Thus (
1.2
) describes an ensemble of noisy classical trajectories, each roughly following the classical equation of motion when
D
is small.

We identify some key scales in any Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V(\hat{x})
. At each position
x
there is a local harmonic approximation to the dynamics depending on the Hessian
\nabla^{2}V(x)
of the potential.
The
harmonic time

t_{H}:=\sqrt{m/\|\nabla^{2}V\|}\lesssim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}
is the shortest timescale associated with such local dynamics,
where

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{j}V\|:=\sup_{x}\sup_{\|w_{i}\|=1}\left|\left[\prod_{i=1}^{j}(w_{i}\cdot\nabla)\right]V(x)\right|

(1.3)

is the maximum over space of
j
directional derivatives of the potential. Similarly, the
harmonic aspect parameter

\eta_{H}:=\sqrt{m\|\nabla^{2}V\|}=m/t_{H}=\|\nabla^{2}V\|t_{H}
(with units of [momentum/length]
\sim
kg/s) picks out a preferred “aspect ratio” in phase space. The
anharmonic action

s_{H}:=\eta_{H}^{3}/(t_{H}\|\nabla^{3}V\|)^{2}=m^{1/2}\|\nabla^{2}V\|^{5/2}\|\nabla^{3}V\|^{-2}
is the action scale above which anharmonicities of the potential (quantified by the max
third
derivative
\|\nabla^{3}V\|
) are important.
Finally,

\displaystyle D_{H}:=\frac{s_{H}}{t_{H}}\left(\begin{matrix}\eta^{-1}_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&\eta_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}\end{matrix}\right)

is the
anharmonic diffusion matrix
. In making use of the above scales, we assume
\|\nabla^{2}V\|,\|\nabla^{3}V\|
are finite.

Let
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}
and
\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}
denote the normalized Gaussian quantum state (a density matrix) and classical state (a distribution)
with mean
\alpha=(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}},\alpha^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
in phase
space and with covariance matrix
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
The above parameters naturally define a preferred covariance matrix

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}:=\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\begin{matrix}\eta^{-1}_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&\eta_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}\end{matrix}\right)=\frac{\hbar t_{H}}{2s_{H}}D_{H}

and the corresponding (unsqueezed)
coherent states

\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma_{*}}
, which are pure state that saturate the uncertainty principle.
Our result utilizes a special subset of all normalized pure Gaussian quantum states that we call
not too squeezed
(NTS):
the states are squeezed relative to the coherent states by no more than a factor

z_{\textsc{nts}}:=\max\{\frac{\hbar/s_{H}}{D_{0}},1\}\geq 1
,
where the minimum eigenvalue

\displaystyle D_{0}:=\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(D_{H}^{-1/2}DD_{H}^{-1/2}\right)

(1.4)

is a dimensionless measure of the diffusion strength relative to
D_{H}
. In other words, NTS states
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}
are pure and satisfy

\sigma\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}
where

\displaystyle\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}:=z_{\textsc{nts}}\,\sigma_{*}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\max\left\{\frac{\hbar/s_{H}}{D_{0}},1\right\}\left(\begin{matrix}\eta^{-1}_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&\eta_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}\end{matrix}\right).

(1.5)

\displaystyle\rho(t\mathord{=}0):=

\displaystyle\int_{\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t\mathord{=}0)\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}

(1.6)

where
p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t\mathord{=}0)\geq
is a probability distribution supported only on

.
We will compare it to the analogously initialized classical system,

\displaystyle f(t\mathord{=}0):=

\displaystyle\int_{\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t\mathord{=}0)\tau_{\alpha,\sigma},

2
Main result and implications

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 1

(Main result)
.

Suppose
\rho(t)
solves the Lindblad equation (
1.1
) with initial condition satisfying (
1.6
), e.g.,
\rho(0)=\hat{\tau}_{\sigma_{*},\alpha_{0}}
, and let
f(t)
be the unique classical trajectory solving the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation (
1.1
) with initial condition (
1.7
).
Then there exists a time-dependent extension
p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\geq 0
of the initial probability distribution for all
t\geq 0
that defines both of the approximating trajectories

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\rho}(t):=&\int_{\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}\\
\tilde{f}(t):=&\int_{\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}\end{split}

(2.1)

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\,\tilde{\rho}(t)-\rho(t)\|_{\mathrm{Tr}\,}&\leq rt\\
\|\tilde{f}(t)-f(t)\|_{L^{1}}&\leq rt\end{split}

(2.2)

with error rate

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=d^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{1}{t_{H}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{s_{H}}}\max\left\{\frac{\hbar/s_{H}}{D_{0}},1\right\}^{\frac{3}{2}}.

(2.3)

Note that the classical approximate trajectory
\tilde{f}
is simply the
Wigner function
of the quantum approximate trajectory
\tilde{\rho}
, that is
\tilde{f}(t)=\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]
, because
\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}=\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]
(see Methods Section
B
).

A proof of Theorem
1
is sketched in Section
5
and given in detail in the Methods Section
D
. We consider classical simulation, asymptotic optimality, and physical examples in Sections
3
,
4
, and
6
. Before turning to these, let us interpret the result.

Recall that
d
is the number of degrees of freedom, and that
t_{H}
,
s_{H}
, and
D_{H}
above are just characteristic scales defined with the parameters
m
,
\|\nabla^{2}V\|
, and
\|\nabla^{3}V\|
of the classical Hamiltonian
H=p^{2}/2m+V(x)
.
In (
2.2
),
\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{Tr}}:=\Tr[(\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{A})^{1/2}]
is the trace norm on quantum operators and
\|A\|_{L^{1}}:=\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha|A(\alpha)|
is the analogous
L^{1}
norm on classical phase-space functions. As recalled in Methods Section
A
, they constrain the probability of a discrepancy being revealed by the measurement of any quantum observable
\hat{A}
or classical observable (function on phase space)
A
.
We thus have the following corollary.

Corollary 2

(Indistinguishable observables)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1
, the expectation values of any quantum observable
\hat{A}
and its Wigner-Weyl-transformed classical counterpart
A
(a function on phase space) obey

\displaystyle\left|\Tr[\hat{A}\rho(t)]-\int\!A(\alpha)f(t,\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha\right|\leq(\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|A\|_{L^{\infty}})rt

where
r
is given by (
2.3
).

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}}
denote the operator norm and the essential supremum norm.
This corollary follows from the Weyl trace formula
\Tr[\hat{A}\tilde{\rho}(t)]=\int\!A(\alpha)\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\tilde{\rho}](t)(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha
.

How much environment-induced diffusion is necessary for Theorem
1
to ensure a close quantum-classical correspondence? First note from (
2.3
) that regardless of the strength of diffusion, our construction can never bound the
rate
at which the
quantum-classical error accumulates to be lower than

\bar{r}:=d^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{1}{t_{H}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{s_{H}}}.

(2.4)

Then, given a desired error rate
r\geq\bar{r}
, we can see the required diffusion strength is

D\geq\left(\frac{r}{d^{\frac{3}{2}}t_{H}^{-1}}\right)^{-\frac{2}{3}}\left(\frac{\hbar}{s_{H}}\right)^{\frac{4}{3}}D_{H}.

(2.5)

See Fig.
2
for a heuristic argument suggesting the adequacy of condition (
2.5
).

Alternatively, if
D\gtrsim(\hbar/s_{H})^{\frac{4}{3}-p}D_{H}
for some power
p>0
, we find that the total error
\epsilon=rt
in the correspondence (
2.2
) is small for times
t\lesssim t_{H}(s_{H}/\hbar)^{q}
, for power
q=\min\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3p}{2}\}
. This time range is exponentially longer than the Ehrenfest time. Here we refer to the Ehrenfest time as
\tau_{\mathrm{E}}\sim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\log(\hbar^{-1})
where
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
is the maximum
local
or
instantaneous
Lyapunov exponent given by
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\sim t_{H}^{-1}=\sqrt{\|\nabla^{2}V\|/m}
, the maximum nonlinearity of the classical flow.
This quantity upper bounds the
global
Lyapunov exponent, defined by an infinite-time limit.

Figure 4:

Consider the error rate incurred by evolving a pure Gaussian state (ellipse) using the local Harmonic approximation to the dynamics.
The discrepancy is dominated by the leading-order anharmonicity
\nabla^{3}V
, which is strongest (relative to the center) at the tips of the ellipse lying on either end of the long axis of length
v\sim\hbar/w
. As the third-order term in the Taylor approximation, this changes the speed of the local flow by
s\lesssim v^{2}\|\nabla^{3}V\|
. So the discrepancy (red shaded area) between the distribution under the exact dynamics (curved boomerang) and the area of the ellipse grows at rate
\lesssim sv
. Compared to the ellipse’s area
\hbar
, this gives an error rate
r\sim sv/\hbar\lesssim\hbar^{-1}v^{3}\|\nabla^{3}V\|
.
Because the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix

\sigma\sim\textrm{diag}(w^{2},v^{2})

are just the squares of the semimajor axes, the error rate is bounded as
r\lesssim\hbar^{-1}\|\sigma\|^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\nabla^{3}V\|
, as proved in Lemma
5
.
As explained in Fig.
2
, the width
w
of the narrow axis is lower bounded by the diffusion strength:
w\gtrsim(D/\lambda_{\mathrm{L}})^{1/2}
. So we can also write
r\lesssim(\hbar^{4/3}/D)^{3/2}\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{3/2}\|\nabla^{3}V\|
, which is small when
D\gg\hbar^{4/3}
.

We emphasize that
D
may be chosen vanishingly small as
\hbar\to 0
, while still yielding small quantum-classical error. Thus the correspondence holds for long times, even while the system appears isolated during any fixed time interval. (That is, for any fixed timescale, the effect of the noise on the trajectory vanishes as
\hbar\to 0
.)
Nonetheless, for any fixed nonzero
D
, at sufficiently large time, the effect of
D
on the classical solution to
(
1.2
)
becomes large. For chaotic evolution, this effect already occurs after time
t\sim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\log(D^{-1})
, or equivalently the Ehrenfest time if
D\sim\hbar
, because the diffusive noise is exponentially amplified.

3
Classical algorithm for simulation

Building on Theorem
1
, we offer a classical algorithm to simulate the quantum Lindblad evolution, in the sense of computing observables
\Tr[\hat{A}\rho(t)]
. For
d
continuous degrees of freedom, a naive classical algorithm to simulate a closed quantum system (say via exact diagonalization, or Schrodinger evolution of the discretized wavefunction) would require time and space complexity
\textrm{poly}(\hbar^{-d})
, due to effective Hilbert space dimension
(S/\hbar)^{d}
for a region of phase space of area
S^{d}
. However, for an open system with sufficient decoherence, Corollary
2
enables one to estimate
\Tr[\hat{A}\rho(t)]
by simulating the classical Fokker-Planck equation rather than the quantum Lindblad equation. Then the computational complexity becomes

\textrm{poly}(D_{0}^{-1},d)
,
now depending on the diffusion strength
D_{0}
but
not
on
\hbar
, with only
polynomial
dependence on the
d
degrees of freedom.

To formalize this result about classical simulation, we consider a slightly modified class of Hamiltonians. This modification guarantees the system is confined in phase space, avoiding arbitrarily large flows that become problematic for simulation techniques.

Theorem 3

(Lindblad simulation (simplified))
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
1
but with
\hat{H}=Y(\hat{p})+V(\hat{x})
where the first three derivatives of
Y
and
V
are bounded.
Let
T>0
be a time and
\epsilon\geq 12Tr
a desired error, with
r
given by (
2.3
) for this Hamiltonian.
Then there exists a (randomized) classical algorithm to compute the expectation value
\Tr[\rho(t)\hat{A}]
of any quantum observable
\hat{A}
satisfying
\|A\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
within error
\epsilon
(with probability at least
99\%
) and time-complexity

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\textup{poly}(d,\epsilon^{-1},T/t_{H},D_{0}^{-1})\right)

(3.1)

independent of
\hbar
.

The constant hidden by
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
includes polynomial dependence on
V
and
Y
and their derivatives up to third order and also on
D_{\infty}:=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(D_{H}^{-1/2}DD_{H}^{-1/2})
, because large forces or diffusion require small time-steps. A stronger version of this theorem is proven in Methods Section
F
, with more explicit parameter dependence than (
3.1
).

This result may be seen as a continuous-variable analog to discrete-variable results in noisy random circuit sampling
[
aharonov2023polynomial
]
,
or as an analog to results about noisy boson sampling
[
qi2020regimes
]
(but without the restriction to linear dynamics on phase space),
both cases where noiseless closed quantum evolution is infeasible to classically simulate while the noisy version is tractable.

4
Asymptotic optimality

Theorem
1
show that the
D\gtrsim\hbar^{4/3}
threshold is
sufficient
to guarantee quantum-classical correspondence beyond the Ehrenfest time. We now present a heuristic argument that it is
necessary
, i.e., that our bound is optimal and cannot be asymptotically improved without further assumptions about specific systems. Denoting the Wigner function as
W(t)=\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\rho(t)]
, it evolves according to the Moyal bracket, expanded as

\begin{split}\partial_{t}W(t)&=\left\{\!\!\left\{H,W\right\}\!\!\right\}_{\text{MB}}\\
&=(\partial_{a}H)(\partial^{a}W)-\hbar^{2}(\partial^{3}H)(\partial^{3}W)+\ldots\end{split}

(4.1)

where
\partial^{3}
schematically denotes a sum of third partial derivatives. The ellipses indicate further terms of the form
(\partial^{2n+1}H)(\partial^{2n+1}W)
. If
W
has a minimum length
w
on which it varies (e.g. if the distribution has tendrils of minimum width
w
), then
\partial^{3}f\lesssim w^{-3}f
, and the leading
\hbar
-dependent term in the above expansion has magnitude
\hbar^{2}w^{-3}f
. Meanwhile, the
\partial^{n}H
terms are independent of
\hbar
, so the error between the classical flow of
f
under
H
and the corresponding evolution by the Moyal bracket is

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{t}f-(\partial_{a}H)(\partial^{a}f)\right\rVert_{L_{1}}\lesssim\hbar^{2}w^{-3}.

(4.2)

In a closed chaotic system, the minimum width of variation of
f
can decrease like
w\sim e^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}t}
for Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
, due to exponential squeezing and stretching, leading to large error within the Ehrenfest time. Meanwhile, for an open system, the squeezing is balanced by diffusion, leading to a minimum width
w\sim(D/\lambda_{\mathrm{L}})^{1/2}
(Fig.
2
). Then the error in Eq. (
4.2
) scales as
\hbar^{2}D^{-3/2}
, which is large unless
D\gg\hbar^{-4/3}
, suggesting this threshold is necessary for the classical approximation.

5
Proof sketch

The proof requires three steps: defining
p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)
using the harmonic approximation to the exact dynamics;
applying the Duhamel formula to bound the harmonic approximation error as a sum (integral) of errors accumulated at each time step; and bounding the error at each time step.

Step 1. Constructing the distribution
p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)
:

If
\tilde{\rho}(t)
is to approximate
\rho(t)
, which satisfies
the Lindblad equation (
1.1
),
then we would like to find

p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)
such that

\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(t)\approx\hat{\mathcal{L}}[\tilde{\rho}(t)]=\iint\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\sigma\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\hat{\mathcal{L}}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]
.
To accomplish this, we will use a
harmonic approximation

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}
to
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
near the point
\alpha
in phase space. Harmonic dynamics are characterized by a quadratic Hamiltonians and linear Lindblad operators, and they exactly preserve the set of Gaussian states

[
isar1994open
]
.
In the case of linear Lindblad operators
and Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V(\hat{x})
, the harmonic approximation

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}
to
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
at
\alpha
is obtained simply by replacing the potential
V
with its local quadratic approximation about
x=\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}
. (For the harmonic approximation to more general Lindbladians, see the companion work
[
hernandez2023decoherence2
]
.)
This is an open-system generalization of Heller’s thawed-Gaussian approximation

[
heller1975time
]
.
We will find a function
p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)
that exactly solves

\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(t)=\int_{\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}].

(5.1)

In Eq. (
5.1
) we evolve each Gaussian state
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}
in the mixture
\tilde{\rho}(t)
according to the harmonic approximation to the dynamics centered at
\alpha
. The form of Eq. (
2.1
) and (
5.1
) requires that we can continually express
\tilde{\rho}(t)
as a mixture of NTS states. For arbitrary Lindblad dynamics, we could not write
\tilde{\rho}
as such a mixture. For instance, in the limiting case of a closed system (
D=0
), the Gaussian states could become arbitrarily squeezed over time, and these squeezed states could not be rewritten as mixtures of NTS states. We must therefore take advantage of the environment-induced diffusion
D
present in the open system, which diffusively broadens each Gaussian state in the mixture, causing it to become mixed, which can then be decomposed into less squeezed Gaussian states satisfying

\sigma\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}
.
This essential mechanism is illustrated in Figure
2
(b).

Step 2. Duhamel bound:

Having defined our approximating trajectory
\tilde{\rho}
, we can
compare it to the exact evolution
\rho(t)
using
the Duhamel formula:

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(t)-\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\,e^{(t-s)\hat{\mathcal{L}}}\left(\partial_{s}-\hat{\mathcal{L}}\right)[\tilde{\rho}(s)].

(5.2)

This allows us to express the error at time
t
as an accumulation of errors at previous times, yielding

\displaystyle\left\lVert\tilde{\rho}(t)-\rho(t)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}\leq t\sup_{\sigma\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\sup_{\alpha}\left\lVert(\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}-\hat{\mathcal{L}})[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}.

(5.3)

The corresponding classical expression is similar:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}(t)-f(t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq t\sup_{\sigma\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}}\sup_{\alpha}\left\lVert(\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}-\mathcal{L})[\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{L^{1}}.

(5.4)

Now we only need to bound the errors appearing above, involving the difference between the exact evolutions
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{L}
and the corresponding harmonic approximations
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}
and
\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}
.

Step 3. Harmonic approximation error:

The error from the harmonic approximation will be small when the spatial size of the Gaussian state is small compared to the anharmonicity of the potential
V
. Indeed, the only modification necessary to obtain the harmonic approximation to the dynamics involves replacing the potential
V
with its second-order Taylor approximation at
x=\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}
, so the error is proportional to the max leading-order (i.e., third-order) correction
\Delta x^{3}\|\nabla^{3}V\|
at the characteristic spatial width
\Delta x\sim\|\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
of the wavepacket with covariance matrix
\sigma
. (The
\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}
superscript is used to denote the upper left block of
\sigma
.) More precisely, in Methods Section
E
we prove the bounds

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}-\hat{\mathcal{L}})[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

\displaystyle\leq r_{\sigma},\quad\left\lVert(\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}-\hat{\mathcal{L}})[\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq r_{\sigma}

(5.5)

where

\displaystyle r_{\sigma}=\sqrt{3}\,d^{\frac{3}{2}}\hbar^{-1}\|\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\nabla^{3}V\|.

(5.6)

See Figure
4
for a visual derivation of this harmonic error term.
In the quantum case, the
\hbar^{-1}
factor above can be seen to arise from the
\hbar^{-1}
in the Schrödinger equation; in the classical case, it is related to the fact that the coherent state has area
\hbar
in phase space.

The harmonic errors appearing in the quantum error (
5.3
) and classical error (
5.4
) are taken only over covariance matrices satisfying
\sigma\leq\sigma_{\textsc{nts}}=z_{\textsc{nts}}\sigma_{*}
. Because
\|\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\|\leq z_{\textsc{nts}}\|\sigma_{*}^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\|=z_{\textsc{nts}}\hbar/2\eta_{H}
, we can combine
(
5.3
–
5.6
)
to get the error rate bound (
2.3
).

6
Physical examples

To compare with previous studies, let us estimate the strength of the environment-induced diffusion for real physical systems: a grain of dust being decohered by the cosmic microwave background, a large molecule being decohered by sunlight, and so on
[
joos2013decoherence
]
. Many of the strongest sources of decoherence act much more strongly on position than momentum

[
paz1993reduction
]
,
i.e., the diffusion matrix is approximately degenerate:
D\approx\diag(0,D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}})
. This makes our error bound (
2.2
) weak, per (
2.3
) and (
1.4
). However, because the error rate (
5.6
) depends only on the position block
\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}

of the covariance matrix of the Gaussian states used in the construction of
\tilde{\rho}
, we can strengthen the theorem by allowing any pure Gaussian state with covariance matrix satisfying
\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\leq(\hbar^{2}/2D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}t_{H})\mathds{1}_{d}
or, equivalently,
\sigma^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}\geq(D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}t_{H}/2)\mathds{1}_{d}
. We can call these states
not too thin
(NTT), since they are a superset of the NTS state where the squeezing constraint is enforced along only the position directions of phase space. The strengthened theorem then bounds the quantum-classical error rate (
2.2
) as

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=d^{\frac{3}{2}}\hbar^{2}m^{-\frac{3}{4}}\|\nabla^{2}V\|^{\frac{3}{4}}\|\nabla^{3}V\|(D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}})^{-\frac{3}{2}}.

(6.1)

when the initial state is NTT.
(Although in principle the NTT constraint is compatible with the state
\tilde{\rho}(t)
accumulating coherence between arbitrarily different amounts of momentum, it’s previously been observed

[
paz1993reduction
]

that the
\hat{p}^{2}/2m
part of the Hamiltonian smears that momentum coherence into the position direction where it is then decohered.)

In other words, it will not be feasible to distinguish the quantum and classical dynamics observables by measuring any observable for a time

\displaystyle T\sim r^{-1}=\frac{x_{H}t_{H}^{\frac{7}{2}}(D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}})^{\frac{3}{2}}}{d^{\frac{3}{2}}\hbar^{2}m}

(6.2)

where
t_{H}=\sqrt{m/\|\nabla^{2}V\|}
and
x_{H}=\|\nabla^{2}V\|/\|\nabla^{3}V\|
are the characteristic time and length scales of the dust particle’s motion.

For instance, a dust particle with diameter
10\,\mathrm{\mu m}
and mass

m\sim 10^{-11}
kg decohering
in sunlight (
T\sim 5800
K) has a momentum diffusion rate
D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}=\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\sim 10^{-43}\mathrm{kg}^{2}\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{s}^{-3}

(Table 3.1 of Ref.
[
joos2013decoherence
]
).
If it moves through a macroscopic potential varying on the length scale of
x_{H}\sim 1\,\mathrm{m}
at characteristic speed
x_{H}/t_{H}\sim 1\mathrm{m/s}
, the errors
\left\lVert\rho(t)-\tilde{\rho}(t)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}
and
\|\tilde{f}(t)-f(t)\|_{L^{1}}
are small for

T\sim 2
million years, in comparison to the Ehrenfest time
\tau_{\mathrm{E}}\sim\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}\log(s_{H}/\hbar)\sim 1
minute. The timescale needed for the sunlight to appreciably change the energy of the dust particle, which would otherwise be conserved, is

(mx_{H}/t_{H})^{2}/D^{\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}}\sim 30
trillion years,
so its classical motion appears noiseless and reversible.

Appendix A
Norms

For a function
f(\alpha)
over phase space variable
\alpha=(x,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, the
(Lebesgue)
L^{q}
norm
is

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{q}}:=\left(\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\left|f(\alpha)\right|^{q}\right)^{1/q}.

(A.1)

Our classical error bound on the difference in two distributions
f
and
g
is stated with
L^{1}
norm:
\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{L^{1}}:=\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha|f(\alpha)-g(\alpha)|
. The analogous norm on the quantum side is the
trace norm

\|\hat{A}\|_{\mathrm{Tr}}=\Tr[(\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{A})^{1/2}]=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(\hat{A})
of an operator
\hat{A}
, i.e., the sum of the singular values
\sigma_{i}(\hat{A})
.

The importance of these two norms follows from their standard variational characterization. For quantum states
\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}
, the respective probabilities
q_{\mathrm{q}}=\Tr[\hat{Q}\rho(t)]
and
\tilde{q}_{\mathrm{q}}=\Tr[\hat{Q}\tilde{\rho}]
for the measurement outcome associated with a projector
\hat{Q}\leq\hat{I}
are constrained by

\displaystyle\begin{split}|q_{\mathrm{q}}-\tilde{q}_{\mathrm{q}}|&=|\Tr[Q(\rho-\tilde{\rho})]|\\
&\leq\|\hat{Q}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\lVert\rho-\tilde{\rho}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}\\
&\leq\left\lVert\rho-\tilde{\rho}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}\end{split}

(A.2)

Likewise, for classical states
f
and
\tilde{f}
the respective probabilities
q_{\mathrm{c}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,Q(\alpha)f(\alpha)
and
\tilde{q}_{\mathrm{c}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,Q(\alpha)\tilde{f}(\alpha)
for the measurement of any classical indicator variable
Q(\alpha)\leq 1
are constrained by

\displaystyle\begin{split}|q_{\mathrm{c}}-\tilde{q}_{\mathrm{c}}|&=\left|\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,Q(\alpha)(f(\alpha)-\tilde{f}(\alpha))\right|\\
&\leq\|Q\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{L^{1}}\\
&\leq\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{L^{1}}\end{split}

(A.3)

Thus, two classical states cannot be readily distinguished when they are close in
L^{1}
norm, and two quantum states cannot be readily distinguished when they are close in trace norm, no matter what measurement is performed.

Appendix B
Wigner function

The Weyl representation and the closely related Wigner function are not necessary for stating our main result, Theorem
1
, but in its proof and in Corollary
2
we make use of these three facts:

1.

When
\rho
is a Gaussian state, its Wigner function
\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\rho]
is a Gaussian probability distribution on phase space with the same mean and covariance:
\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]=\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}
.

2.

When
\rho
is a mixture of Gaussian states,
\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\rho]
is a mixture of the respective Gaussian distributions, because
\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}[\cdot]
is a linear map.

3.

The Weyl trace formula in the special case of the expectation value of a function
V
of position for a Gaussian state is

\displaystyle\Tr[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}V(\hat{x})]=\int\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\,\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}(\alpha)V(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}).

(B.1)

The companion paper, Ref.
[
hernandez2023decoherence2
]
, more thoroughly discusses the Weyl representation in the context of these and related results. For more about the Wigner function in general, see e.g. the review in
[
curtright2014concise
]
.

Appendix C
Symplectic and Hamiltonian matrices

In this section we recall basic facts about symplectic and Hamiltonian matrices. Our starting point is the
symplectic form

\displaystyle\omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathds{1}_{d}\\
-\mathds{1}_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}

(C.1)

associated with the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
for
d
classical degrees of freedom.
A
symplectic
matrix
A
satisfies

A^{\top}\omega A=\omega.

(C.2)

Note that the symplectic matrices
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})
form a group which is a subgroup of the special linear
group
\mathrm{SL}(2d,\mathbb{R})
.

For a one-parameter family
A=A(t)
of symplectic matrices,

\begin{split}0&=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(A^{\top}\omega A)\\
&=\dot{A}^{\top}\omega A+A^{\top}\omega\dot{A}.\end{split}

(C.3)

Taking
A(t)=\exp(tF)
so
\dot{A}=FA=AF
, we see that the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})
is generated by the Lie algebra
\mathrm{sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})
, i.e., the set of Hamiltonian matrices
F
defined by the constraint

F^{\top}\omega+\omega F=0.

(C.4)

[Hamiltonian
matrices
should not to be confused with the classical Hamiltonian variable (the energy) or the corresponding quantum Hamiltonian operator.]
Using
\omega^{2}=\mathds{1}_{2d}
and
\omega^{\top}=-\omega
, we can rearrange this as

F^{\top}=\omega F\omega=-\omega^{\top}F\omega.

(C.5)

Using the identities
\omega=A^{\top}\omega A=A^{-\top}\omega A^{-1}

we can see that if
F
is Hamiltonian and
A
is symplectic, then

A^{-1}FA
is also symplectic.

We can also rearrange (
C.3
)
by left-multiplying by
A^{-\top}
and right-multiplying
by
A^{-1}
:

A^{-\top}\dot{A}^{\top}\omega+\omega\dot{A}A^{-1}=0.

(C.6)

Thus
\dot{A}A^{-1}
is Hamiltonian when
A=A(t)
is symplectic.
Conjugating by
A
we can see that
A^{-1}\dot{A}
is also Hamiltonian
in this case.

When
A
is additionally symmetric,
it follows that
\dot{A}
is also symmetric so we can write

\dot{A}=FA+AF^{\top}

(C.7)

for some Hamiltonian matrix
F
.

In order that
\sigma
is the covariance matrices of a pure Gaussian state, it must be positive definite (
\sigma>0
), and
\sigma/(\hbar/2)
must be symplectic (
\sigma^{\top}\omega\sigma=(\hbar/2)^{2}\omega
)
[
simon1994quantumnoise
,
weedbrook2012gaussian
]
. As can be checked, this is true for

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}:=\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\begin{matrix}\eta_{H}^{-1}\mathds{1}_{d}&0\\
0&\eta_{H}\mathds{1}_{d}\end{matrix}\right).

(C.8)

In the proof of Theorem
1
we work with such covariance matrices
\sigma
that additionally satisfy
the matrix inequality

\displaystyle\sigma\leq z_{\textsc{nts}}\sigma_{*}

(C.9)

for a particular
z_{\textsc{nts}}\geq 1
. Inverting this (and using the positivity of
\sigma
and
\sigma_{*}
) we obtain the inequality

\sigma^{-1}\geq z_{\textsc{nts}}^{-1}\sigma_{*}^{-1}.

(C.10)

Now rewriting the symplectic conditions on
\sigma/(\hbar/2)
and
\sigma_{*}/(\hbar/2)
as

\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}

\displaystyle\,=(\hbar/2)^{-2}\omega^{\top}\sigma\omega,

(C.11)

\displaystyle\sigma_{*}^{-1}

\displaystyle\,=(\hbar/2)^{-2}\omega^{\top}\sigma_{*}\omega,

(C.12)

we derive from (
C.10
) the inequality

\omega^{\top}\sigma\omega\geq z_{\textsc{nts}}^{-1}\omega^{\top}\sigma_{*}\omega.

(C.13)

De-conjugating by the symplectic form
\omega
gives our desired equivalent NTS condition:

\sigma\geq z_{\textsc{nts}}^{-1}\sigma_{*}
. That is, we conclude

\sigma\geq z_{\textsc{nts}}^{-1}\sigma_{*}\iff\sigma\leq z_{\textsc{nts}}\sigma_{*}

(C.14)

for covariance matrices of pure Gaussian states.
More generally, for a pure Gaussian state, the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
\sigma
come in pairs
\lambda
and
(\hbar/2)^{2}\lambda^{-1}

[
simon1994quantumnoise
,
weedbrook2012gaussian
]
.

Proof of Theorem

[
hernandez2023decoherence2

addresses a broader class of dynamics and includes technical discussion about the trace- and positivity-preservation of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
. See Ref.
[
li2024long
]
for trace-norm bounds directly between
\rho(t)
and
\mathcal{W}_{\hbar}^{-1}[f(t)]

Proof of Theorem

.

We will build a trajectory
\tilde{\rho}(t)
that approximates the true evolution
\rho(t)
by using a mixture of Gaussian states
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}
, each centered at a point
\alpha=(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}},\alpha^{\mathrm{p}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
in phase

\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(t)=\iint\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\sigma\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma},

where

only supports covariance matrices

We assume the true initial state

is a (possibly trivial) mixture of such states, so that the true trajectory and our approximation initially coincide:

We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 4. Constructing the distribution
p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)
:

We use

to denote the harmonic approximation to
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
with respect to the point
\alpha
in phase space. Then
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}
is obtained from

simply by replacing the potential with its local quadratic approximation about

, i.e., by
replacing

with

is the second-order Taylor approximation about
\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}
.
Harmonic dynamics (i.e., quadratic Hamiltonian and linear Lindblad operators) exactly preserve the set of Gaussian states

brodier2004symplectic
,
graefe2018lindblad
]

We will find a function

(D.2)

Before we proceed to analyze the error between

\tilde{\rho}(t)
and
\rho(t)
, we first show that

Under the harmonic approximation about

brodier2004symplectic

graefe2018lindblad
]

\displaystyle U(\alpha):=

-\nabla V(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}})\end{matrix}\right)

\mathrm{d}\alpha(t)/\mathrm{d}t=U(\alpha)

\displaystyle S(\alpha,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=F(\alpha)\sigma+\sigma F^{\top}(\alpha)+D,

\displaystyle F(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&-\mathds{1}_{d}/m\\
\nabla^{2}V(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}})&0\end{matrix}\right),

(D.5)

and where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]

With (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\hskip-10.00002pt\iint\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\sigma\,\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\\
&=\iint\!\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\sigma\,p_{\alpha,\sigma}(t)\left[U(\alpha)\cdot\partial_{\alpha}+S(\alpha,\sigma)\cdot\partial_{\sigma}\right]\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}.\end{split}

We could integrate the right-hand side of (

For any choice of decomposition

Plugging this into (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathchoice{\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 0.80002pt$\hss\resizebox{327682}{254863}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{(}}\hss$\crcr\cr$\hss\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\hss$\crcr}}}\limits}{\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 0.80002pt$\hss\resizebox{327682}{254863}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{(}}\hss$\crcr\cr$\hss\textstyle\mathcal{L}\hss$\crcr}}}\limits}{\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 0.80002pt$\hss\resizebox{259232}{204801}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{(}}\hss$\crcr\cr$\hss\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}\hss$\crcr}}}\limits}{\mathop{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\kern 0.80002pt$\hss\resizebox{217728}{177495}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{(}}\hss$\crcr\cr$\hss\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{L}\hss$\crcr}}}\limits}^{(\alpha)}[p_{\alpha,\sigma}]:=&\,\Big[-\partial_{\alpha}\cdot U(\alpha)-\partial_{\sigma}\cdot S_{0}(\alpha,\sigma)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\cdot S_{\mathrm{D}}(\alpha,\sigma)\Big]p_{\alpha,\sigma}\end{split}

It is useful to work with the rescaled matrices

\displaystyle:=[{\tilde{F}}(\alpha)-g({\bar{\sigma}})]{\bar{\sigma}}+{\bar{\sigma}}[{\tilde{F}}^{\top}(\alpha)-g({\bar{\sigma}})],

(D.9)

\displaystyle:={\bar{D}}+[g({\bar{\sigma}}){\bar{\sigma}}+{\bar{\sigma}}g({\bar{\sigma}})],

Then the lower-bound condition above will be preserved if

\begin{split}\langle v|{\bar{S}}_{0}(\alpha,\sigma)|v\rangle&\geq-2\|{\tilde{F}}(\alpha)\|_{\mathrm{op}}z_{\textsc{nts}}^{-1}+2D_{0}s_{H}t_{H}^{-1}\geq 0\end{split}

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\left\lVert\tilde{\rho}(t)-\rho(t)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

Lemma 5

(Harmonic approximation error)
.

For a Lindblad equation (
1.1
) with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V(\hat{x})
and linear lindblad operators
\hat{L}_{k}
, the error
\delta\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}:=\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}
for the local harmonic approximation
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}
to the quantum dynamics at
\alpha
acting on the pure Gaussian quantum state
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{5d^{3}}{3}}\|\nabla^{3}V\|\frac{\|\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\hbar}

(E.1)

Likewise for a Fokker-Planck equation (
1.2
), the error
\delta\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}:=\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}
for the local harmonic approximation
\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}
to the classical dynamics at
\alpha
acting on the Gaussian classical state
\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha)}[\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3d^{3}}\|\nabla^{3}V\|\frac{\|\sigma^{\mathrm{x}\mathrm{x}}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{\frac{2}{3}}}{\hbar}

(E.2)

Proof.

We first bound the quantum error

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(\hat{\mathcal{L}}-\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)})[\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

(E.3)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\delta\hat{H}^{[\alpha,2]},\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

(E.4)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\hbar}\left\lVert\delta\hat{H}^{[\alpha,2]}\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}

(E.5)

for the Gaussian quantum state
\hat{\tau}_{\alpha,\sigma}=|\alpha,\sigma\rangle\langle\alpha,\sigma|
with covariance matrix
\sigma
and mean
\alpha
. Here,
\hat{I}
denotes the identity operator and
\delta\hat{H}^{[\alpha,2]}=\hat{H}-\hat{H}^{[\alpha,2]}=\delta V^{[\alpha^{\mathrm{x}},2]}(\hat{x})
is the operator error from the harmonic approximation. By Taylor’s theorem we have the classical remainder from the quadratic approximation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta V^{[\alpha^{\mathrm{x}},2]}(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}+\Delta x)&=V(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}+\Delta x)-V^{[\alpha^{\mathrm{x}},2]}(\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}+\Delta x)\\
&=\frac{1}{3!}\left[(\Delta x\cdot\nabla)^{3}V\right](\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}+\xi\Delta x)\end{split}

(E.6)

for some choice of
\xi\in[0,1]
(depending on
\alpha^{\mathrm{x}}
and
\Delta x
).

Recalling that
\left\lVert\mathinner{|{\psi}\rangle}\mathinner{\langle{\phi}|}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{Tr}}^{2}=\|\psi\|^{2}\|\phi\|^{2}
, we have

Appendix F
Classically simulating the Lindblad equation

In this section we discuss analytical bounds on the computational complexity of simulating the quantum Lindblad evolution using a classical computer.
While we expect this task to be difficult for closed quantum systems in general, we will see that the simulation becomes tractable when the desired Lindblad equation includes sufficient diffusion.

F.1
Summary

Our setup is similar to that Theorem
4
, the extension of Theorem
1
to “separable” Hamiltonians. Specifically, we consider Hermitian linear Lindblad functions
L_{k}
,
a Hamiltonian of the form
\hat{H}=Y(\hat{p})+V(\hat{x})
where the second and third derivatives of
V
and
Y
are bounded, and an initial state
\rho(t\mathord{=}0)
that is a mixture of NTS states as in (
1.6
).

To guarantee a well-behaved numerical simulation, we will additionally demand that the

derivatives of

and
Y
are bounded. This ensures that the forces experienced by the system do not become arbitrarily large, which would be problematic for both quantum and classical simulation techniques (e.g., necessitating arbitrarily small Runge-Kutta time steps). Note that, strictly speaking, the usual kinetic energy

is therefore forbidden, but one could take

and

Under these assumptions, we ask how the computational complexity of the simulation depends on
\hbar

(see Eq. (

We will focus on the asymptotic behavior with respect to these parameters and will not keep track of other constants.
The main result is that, as long as the diffusion is strong enough, there is an efficient

III.2
Gaussian integrals

Here we recall the evaluation of some Gaussian integrals, as can be done with Wick’s theorem. We define the shorthand:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle(\beta^{\top}A\beta)\rangle_{\sigma}:=&\int\mathrm{d}\beta\tau_{0,\sigma}(\beta)(\beta^{\top}A\beta)\\
=&\int\mathrm{d}\beta\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}(\alpha+\beta)(\beta^{\top}A\beta)\\
=&A_{ab}\int\mathrm{d}\beta\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}(\alpha+\beta)\beta^{a}\beta^{b}\\
=&A_{ab}\sigma^{ab}\\
=&\Tr[\sigma A].\end{split}

(III.11)

for any positive semidefinite matrix
A
. (The covariance matrix
\sigma
is also positive semidefinite, of course.) Likewise, for
B
and
C
also positive semidefinite, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle(\beta^{\top}A\beta)(\beta^{\top}B\beta)\rangle_{\sigma}:=&\int\mathrm{d}\beta\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}(\alpha+\beta)(\beta^{\top}A\beta)(\beta^{\top}B\beta)\\
=&A_{ab}B_{cd}\left[\sigma^{ab}\sigma^{cd}+2\sigma^{ad}\sigma^{bc}\right]\\
=&\Tr[\sigma A]\Tr[\sigma B]+2\Tr[\sigma A\sigma B]\end{split}

(III.12)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle(\beta^{\top}&A\beta)(\beta^{\top}B\beta)(\beta^{\top}C\beta)\rangle_{\sigma}\\
:=&\int\mathrm{d}\beta\tau_{\alpha,\sigma}(\alpha+\beta)(\beta^{\top}A\beta)(\beta^{\top}B\beta)(\beta^{\top}C\beta)\\
=&A_{ab}B_{cd}C_{ef}\Big[\sigma^{ab}\sigma^{cd}\sigma^{ef}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\big(\sigma^{ab}\sigma^{cf}\sigma^{de}+\sigma^{af}\sigma^{cd}\sigma^{be}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\sigma^{ad}\sigma^{bc}\sigma^{ef}\big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+4\left(\sigma^{ad}\sigma^{be}\sigma^{cf}+\sigma^{af}\sigma^{bc}\sigma^{de}\right)\Big]\\
=&\Tr[\sigma A]\Tr[\sigma B]\Tr[\sigma C]+2\Tr[\sigma A]\Tr[\sigma B\sigma C]\\
&\qquad+2\Tr[\sigma B]\Tr[\sigma C\sigma A]+2\Tr[\sigma C]\Tr[\sigma B\sigma A]\\
&\qquad+8\Tr[\sigma A\sigma B\sigma C].\end{split}
