Bootstrap-based tests for the total time on test order, the NBUE property, and the excess wealth order Journal: Elsevier
Tommaso Lando, Sirio Legramanti

Abstract
Given a pair of non-negative random variables X X and Y Y , we introduce a class of nonparametric tests for the null hypothesis that X X dominates Y Y in the total time on test order. Critical values are determined using bootstrap-based inference, and the tests are shown to be consistent. The same approach is used to construct tests for the excess wealth order. As a byproduct, we also obtain a class of goodness-of-fit tests for the NBUE family of distributions.



1
Introduction

Let
H
be a cumulative distribution function (CDF) defined on the nonnegative half-line, with finite mean
\mu_{H}
and quantile function
H^{-1}(p)=\inf\{u:H(u)\geq p\}
. In this paper, we will consider the following transforms:

T_{H}(p)=\int_{0}^{H^{-1}(p)}(1-H(x))dx,\qquad W_{H}(p)=\int^{1}_{H^{-1}(p)}(1-H(x))dx=\mu_{H}-T_{H}(p).

T_{H}
is referred to as the
total time on test
(TTT) transform of
H
, and is a primary tool in reliability and survival analysis
(
Marshall and Olkin 2007
)
. In particular, when testing the lifetime of a population with distribution
H
,
T_{H}(p)
gives the expected lifetime of the items in the population not exceeding the
p
-th quantile. In fact, if
Z\sim H
, the TTT transform may be expressed as
T_{H}(p)=\mathbb{E}(\min(Z,H^{-1}(p)))
. Differently,
W_{H}
is known as the
excess wealth
transform or
expected shortfall
at level
p
. In particular, for an income distribution
H
,
W_{H}(p)
can be interpreted as the expected additional wealth of the individuals whose wealth exceeds the
p
-th quantile. More formally,
W_{H}(p)=\mathbb{E}(\max(Z,H^{-1}(p)))
. This transform is commonly employed in insurance mathematics
(
Dhaene et al. 2006
)
.

Given a pair of non-negative random variables,
X
and
Y
, with CDFs
F
and
G
, respectively, TTT and excess wealth transforms may be used to define two complementary stochastic orders, recalled in the following definition.

Definition 1
.

1.

X
dominates
Y
in the TTT order, abbreviated as
X\geq_{ttt}Y
, if
T_{F}(p)\geq T_{G}(p),\forall p\in[0,1]
.

2.

X
dominates
Y
in the excess wealth order, abbreviated as
X\geq_{ew}Y
, if
W_{F}(p)\geq W_{G}(p),\forall p\in[0,1].

The TTT order has been studied, for instance, by
Kochar et al. 2002
,
Li and Shaked 2007
,
Belzunce et al. 2014
and
Belzunce and Martínez-Riquelme 2015
. It compares random variables in terms of size and dispersion. The TTT order is weaker than the usual stochastic order, but it implies the well-known
increasing concave order
, also known as
second-order stochastic dominance

(
Shaked and Shantikumar 2007
)
. One of the main applications of the TTT order is comparing lifetime distributions, thanks to the key role of the TTT transform in life testing problems. In particular, if
X
and
Y
represent the random lifetime in two populations,
X\geq_{ttt}Y
means that, for every proportion
p\in[0,1]
, the expected lifetime of the items in
X
, up to its
p
-th quantile
F^{-1}(p)
, is greater than or equal to the expected lifetime in
Y
, up to the corresponding quantile
G^{-1}(p)
. Differently, the excess wealth order has been mostly applied in insurance mathematics, where the main focus is comparing risks
(
Denuit and Vermandele 1999
;
Sordo 2009
)
.

The objective of this paper is to test the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{ttt}_{0}:X\geq_{ttt}Y
versus the alternative
\mathcal{H}^{ttt}_{1}:X\not\geq_{ttt}Y
. Given the relation between the TTT and the excess wealth transforms, the same approach may be used to test
\mathcal{H}^{ew}_{0}:X\geq_{ew}Y
versus
\mathcal{H}^{ew}_{1}:X\not\geq_{ew}Y
.
Belzunce et al. 2005
proposed nonparametric tests for the TTT order, considering the null hypothesis of equality in distribution versus the alternative of strict dominance. Tests of this kind have the disadvantage that we don’t know how they behave under
X\not\geq_{ttt}Y
. On the other hand, setting
X\geq_{ttt}Y
as the null hypothesis, it is difficult to determine the distribution of the test statistic under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
, as this contains infinitely many pairs of distributions. Using a similar approach as in
Barrett et al. 2014
(see also
Barrett and Donald 2003
), we propose a family of conservative tests for the TTT order, in which critical values are approximated using bootstrap procedures. The same approach can be used to construct tests for the excess wealth order. Consistency of these tests is established under relatively mild distributional assumptions, which allow for both dependent and independent sampling schemes.

The TTT order can be used to define the
new better than used in expectation
(NBUE) family of distributions. The NBUE condition has applications in survival analysis and reliability. For example, it can be used in age replacement policies
(
Barlow and Proschan 1975
)
, applied to shock models
(
Block and Savits 1978
)
, or used to derive bounds for the survival function
(
Cheng and Lam 2002
;
Brown 2013
)
. This condition expresses the notion of positive ageing – meaning that ageing has an adverse effect on lifetime – in a weaker sense compared to popular other properties, such as the
increasing hazard rate
(IHR), the
increasing hazard rate on average
(IHRA) and the
decreasing mean residual life
(DMRL) ones
(
Marshall and Olkin 2007
)
. In particular, a lifetime random variable
X
is said to be NBUE if
X/\mu_{F}\geq_{ttt}E
, where
E
is a unit exponential random variable. By exploiting this relation, the approach considered in this paper can also be used to define a class of bootstrap-based tests for the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{0}:X/\mu_{F}\geq_{ttt}E
versus the alternative
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{1}:X/\mu_{F}\not\geq_{ttt}E
. This class of tests may be used as a reliable alternative to other popular tests (see for instance
Hollander and Proschan 1975
;
Anis and Mitra 2011
;
Anis and Basu 2014
), which consider the null hypothesis of exponentiality, namely,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{E}:X/\mu_{f}=_{d}E
, versus the alternative that
F
is strictly NBUE, namely,
X/\mu_{F}>_{ttt}E
. Such tests are not exhaustive, in other words, they are not supposed to work under
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{1}
.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce a class of test statistics that measure departures from the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
. In Section
3
, we establish the consistency properties of our class of tests. In Section
4
, we obtain tests for the NBUE null hypothesis using the relation between the TTT order and the NBUE class, while in Section
5
we extend our approach to the excess wealth order. Finite sample properties of our tests are investigated by simulation studies in Section
6
. All proofs are reported in the Appendix.

2
Construction of the tests

Throughout this paper, let
C[0,1]
denote the space of bounded continuous functions over
[0,1]
. Given some
v\in C[0,1]
and
p\geq 1
,
||v||_{p}
denotes the
L^{p}
norm of
v
, that is, for
p
finite,
||v||_{p}=(\int_{0}^{1}|v(t)|^{p}dt)^{1/p},
while
||v||_{\infty}=\sup_{[0,1]}|v|
is the uniform norm.

By Definition
1
,
\mathcal{H}^{ttt}_{0}
is false if and only if the difference
\delta(u)=T_{G}(p)-T_{F}(p)
is positive at some point
p\in(0,1]
. Therefore, we can measure the positive part of
\delta
, namely,
\delta_{+}=\max(0,\delta)
with the following family of functionals

\Phi_{r}(\delta)=||\delta_{+}||_{r},

for
r\geq 1
, including
r=\infty.
The following properties hold (for the proof see
Lando and Legramanti 2023
).

Proposition 1
.

For every
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in C[0,1]
and for every
r\geq 1
,
\Phi_{r}
satisfies the following properties.

1.

If
\delta_{1}(p)=0,\forall p\in[0,1]
, then
\Phi_{r}(\delta_{1})=0
;

2.

if
\delta_{1}(p)\leq 0,\forall p\in[0,1]
, then
\Phi_{r}(\delta_{2})\leq\Phi_{r}(\delta_{2}-\delta_{1})
for every
\delta_{2}
;

3.

if
\delta_{1}(p)>0
for some
p\in[0,1]
, then
\Phi_{r}(\delta_{1})>0
;

4.

|\Phi_{r}(\delta_{1})-\Phi_{r}(\delta_{2})|\leq||\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}||_{\infty}
;

5.

for any positive constant
c>0
,
c\Phi_{r}(\delta_{1})=\Phi_{r}(c\delta_{1})
;

6.

\Phi_{r}
is convex.

7.

For any
1\leq r\leq s
,
\Phi_{r}(\delta_{1})\leq\Phi_{s}(\delta_{1})

Note that Proposition
1
generalises Lemma 2 of
Barrett et al. 2014
, who proved it for the special cases of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{\infty}
.

As established in part 3 of Proposition
1
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
is false if and only if
\Phi_{r}(\delta)>0
, therefore we reject the null hypothesis if an empirical version of
\Phi_{r}(\delta)
, namely
\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}),
is significantly large. We will now discuss how to estimate
\delta
.

2.1
Sampling assumptions

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},...,X_{n}\}
be an i.i.d. random sample from
F
, and
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{1},...,Y_{m}\}
an i.i.d. random sample from
G
. In this paper, we deal with two different sampling schemes: independent sampling and matched pairs. Under independent sampling, the samples
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are independent of each other and the sample sizes
n
and
m
may differ. Differently, in the matched-pairs scheme,
n=m
and we have
n
i.i.d. pairs
\{(X_{1},Y_{1}),...,(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
drawn from a bivariate distribution with marginal CDFs
F
and
G
, respectively. For our asymptotic analysis, we also assume that, as
n\to\infty
, also
m\to\infty
, and that
\lim_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}nm/(n+m)=\infty
, and
\lim_{n,m\rightarrow\infty}n/(n+m)=\lambda\in[0,1]
.

2.2
Empirical TTT transform

The random samples
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
defined earlier give the empirical CDFs

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(X_{i}\leq x),\qquad G_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}\leq x).

We will denote the order statistics of rank
k
from
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
with
X_{(k)}
and
Y_{(k)}
, and the sample means by
\overline{X}_{n}
and
\overline{Y}_{m}
, respectively. The empirical counterpart of
T_{F}
, denoted by as
T_{F_{n}}
, is defined as the piecewise linear function which interpolates the jump points of the step function
\int_{0}^{F_{n}^{-1}(p)}(1-F_{n}(x))dx
. In particular, for
k=0,...,n
, we have that

T_{F_{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\int_{0}^{X_{(k)}}(1-F_{n}(t))dt=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n-i+1)(X_{(i)}-X_{(i-1)})=\frac{n-k}{n}X_{(k)}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{(i)},

where
X_{(0)}:=0
. The sample mean is obtained as
T_{F_{n}}(1)=1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{(i)}=\overline{X}_{n}
. Note that, when testing the lifetime of
n
items,
nT_{F_{n}}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)
gives the total time on test up to the
k
-th observation.
T_{G_{m}}
is defined similarly. Now, an empirical version of
\delta
is just
\widehat{\delta}=T_{G_{m}}-T_{F_{n}}
. It can be shown that, as
n
and
m
tend to infinity,
T_{F_{n}}
and
T_{G_{m}}
converge strongly and uniformly to
T_{F}
and
T_{G}
, respectively, in
[0,1]

(
Barlow and Van Zwet 1969
, Theorem 2.1)
. This implies that
\widehat{\delta}
also converges strongly and uniformly to
\delta
in
[0,1]
, under our asymptotic regime.

3
Consistency

3.1
Asymptotic properties of the test statistic

Let us define the empirical TTT processes associated with
F
and
G
as

t^{F}_{n}=\sqrt{n}(T_{F_{n}}-T_{F}),\qquad t^{G}_{m}=\sqrt{m}(T_{G_{m}}-T_{G}),

respectively. Note that such processes have trajectories in
C[0,1]
. Correspondingly, consider the process

\Delta_{n,m}=\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\left(\widehat{\delta}-\delta\right)=\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{n+m}}\ t^{G}_{m}-\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{n+m}}\ t^{F}_{n},

where
r_{n,m}=nm/(n+m)
. Recall that, under our assumptions,
r_{nm}\to\infty
as
n
and
m
diverge. To establish the asymptotic properties of our class of tests, we determine the limit behaviour of
\Delta_{n,m}
via the functional delta method. However, this requires the derivation of the Hadamard derivative of the map
H\rightarrow T_{H}
, which can be achieved under the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

Both
F
and
G
are continuously differentiable with strictly positive densities
f
and
g
, respectively, and have a finite moment of order
2+\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
. Moreover,
F(0)=G(0)=0
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a centered Gaussian element of
C[0,1]\times C[0,1]
, with covariance function

Cov(\mathcal{B}(x_{1},y_{1}),\mathcal{B}(x_{2},y_{2}))=C(x_{1}\land x_{2},y_{1}\land y_{2})-C(x_{1},y_{1})C(x_{2},y_{2}).

Under the independent-sampling scheme,
C(x_{1},y_{1})=x_{1}y_{1}
is the product copula, whereas, under the matched-pairs scheme,
C
is the copula associated with the pair
(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
i=1,...,n
. Accordingly,
\mathcal{B}_{1}(x_{1})=\mathcal{B}(x_{1},1)
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}(x_{2})=\mathcal{B}(1,x_{2})
are independent Brownian bridge processes under independent-sampling, but they may be dependent under paired sampling.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, for the sampling schemes discussed in Section
2.1
we have

\sqrt{r_{n,m}}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)\rightsquigarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}=\sqrt{\lambda}\mathcal{T}_{G}-\sqrt{1-\lambda}\mathcal{T}_{F}\quad\text{ in }C[0,1],

where

\mathcal{T}_{F}(p)=\frac{(1-p)\mathcal{B}_{1}(p)}{f\circ F^{-1}(p)}+\int_{0}^{p}\mathcal{B}_{1}(y)dF^{-1}(y),\quad\mathcal{T}_{G}(p)=\frac{(1-p)\mathcal{B}_{2}(p)}{g\circ G^{-1}(p)}+\int_{0}^{p}\mathcal{B}_{2}(y)dG^{-1}(y)

We measure departures from the null hypothesis with the test statistic
\widehat{\Phi}_{r}=\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}),

in particular, “large" values of
\widehat{\Phi}_{r}
lead to rejection of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
. In fact, under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}:\delta\leq 0,
it is clear from property 2 in Proposition
1
that
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})\leq\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta).
Moreover, taking into account properties 4 and 5 in Proposition
1
, the continuous mapping theorem implies that
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)\rightsquigarrow\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}).
In other words, the test statistic
\widehat{\Phi}_{r}
is dominated under the null hypothesis by a statistic that has the same asymptotic behaviour as
\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
. The same approach was used by
Barrett et al. 2014
in testing Lorenz dominance.

Now, a critical value
c_{\alpha}
is determined as the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of
\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
, that is,
P(\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})>c_{\alpha})=\alpha
. For any
\alpha<1/2
, such a quantile is positive, finite, and unique because
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
is a mean-zero Gaussian process. Since, under
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{ttt}:X\not\geq_{ttt}Y,
the statistic
\widehat{\Phi}_{r}/\sqrt{r_{n,m}}=\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})
converges in probability to the strictly positive number
\Phi_{r}(\delta)
, then it is clear that
\widehat{\Phi}_{r}
will diverge and exceed the threshold value
c_{\alpha}
. These properties are summarised in the following lemma. The proof is omitted as it can be obtained using similar arguments as in Lemma 4 of
Barrett et al. 2014
.

Lemma 1
.

1.

Under
\mathcal{H}^{ttt}_{0}
,
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})\leq\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)\rightsquigarrow\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
. Moreover, for any
\alpha<1/2
, the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of the distribution of
\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
is positive, finite, and unique.

2.

Under
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{ttt}
,
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\widehat{\Phi}_{r}\rightarrow_{p}\infty
.

Now, critical values may be approximated by simulating the distribution of
\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
via bootstrap procedures.

3.2
Bootstrap-based decision rule

Let us denote the bootstrap estimators of the empirical CDFs
F_{n}
and
G_{m}
as
F^{*}_{n}
and
G^{*}_{m}
, respectively. These may be written as

F^{*}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}^{(1)}\mathbbm{1}(x\leq X_{i}),\qquad G^{*}_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}M_{i}^{(2)}\mathbbm{1}(x\leq Y_{i}),

where
M^{(1)}=(M_{1}^{(1)},...,M^{(1)}_{n})
and
M^{(2)}=(M_{1}^{(2)},...,M^{(2)}_{m})
are independent of the data and are drawn from a multinomial distribution according to the chosen sampling scheme. In particular, under the independent-sampling scheme,
M^{(1)}
and
M^{(2)}
are independently drawn from multinomial distributions with uniform probabilities over
n
and
m
trials, respectively. Under the matched-pairs scheme, we have
M^{(1)}=M^{(2)}
, drawn from the multinomial distribution with uniform probabilities over
n=m
trials, which means that we sample (with replacement) pairs of data, from the
n
pairs
\{(X_{1},Y_{1}),...,(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
.
Correspondingly, the bootstrap estimators of
T_{F_{n}}
and
T_{G_{m}}
are given, respectively, by
T_{F^{*}_{n}}
and
T_{G^{*}_{m}}
. Accordingly, we have

\widehat{\delta}^{*}=T_{G^{*}_{n}}-T_{F^{*}_{n}}.

By applying the functional delta method for the bootstrap (see Section 3.9.3 of
Van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
), one may show that
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}^{*}-\widehat{\delta})
has the same limiting distribution as
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
. This allows the approximation of the
p
-values
p=P\{\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}^{*}-\widehat{\delta})>\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})\},
using
K
bootstrap replications, namely,

p\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}(\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}_{k}^{*}-\widehat{\delta})>\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})),

where
\widehat{\delta}_{k}^{*}
is the
k
-th resampled realisation of
\widehat{\delta}^{*}
. We reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
whenever
p<\alpha
.

As proved in the following proposition, the bootstrap approximation of the critical values is consistent. This allows us to exploit the results of Lemma
1
and establish the asymptotic behaviour of our class of tests.

Proposition 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and the sampling schemes in Section 3.1,

1.

If
\mathcal{H}^{ttt}_{0}
is true,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\{\text{reject }\mathcal{H}_{0}\}\leq\alpha
;

2.

If
\mathcal{H}^{ttt}_{1}
is true,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\{\text{reject }\mathcal{H}_{0}\}=1.

4
Goodness-of-fit tests for the NBUE family

The classic way of expressing the NBUE condition is assuming that, for every
t>0
,
\mathbb{E}(X-t|X>t)\leq\mathbb{E}(X).
This means that an item of any age
t
has a mean residual life smaller than or equal to the expected lifetime of a new item in the same population. Besides being weaker than the IHR, IHRA and DMRL ageing conditions, the NBUE property has the advantage that it does not require the existence of all moments, except for the first one, extending the scope of applicability of the positive ageing notion.

It is important to remark that, generally, ageing properties can also be defined in terms of a suitable stochastic order between the random variable of interest
X
and the unit exponential
E
, which indeed expresses the concept of “no ageing" thanks to its memoryless property. In this regard, as discussed in Section
1
,
X
is NBUE if
X/\mu_{F}\geq_{ttt}E
. This stochastic inequality boils down to
S_{F}(p)\geq p,\forall p\in[0,1]
, where
S_{F}=T_{F}/\mu_{F}
is the scaled TTT transform of
F
, while it is easy to see that the TTT transform of
E
is just the identity function.

We now introduce a family of tests for
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{0}:X/\mu_{F}\geq_{ttt}E
versus
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{1}:X/\mu_{F}\not\geq_{ttt}E
based on a random sample of size
n
from
F
. Goodness-of-fit tests for ageing properties of this type are especially relevant in life testing problems and in shape-constrained statistical inference. Examples are tests for the IHR and the IHRA families
(
Hall and Van Keilegom 2005
;
Groeneboom and Jongbloed 2012
;
Lando et al. 2023
;
Lando 2023
)
. Compared to tests for the null hypothesis of exponentiality versus strictly NBUE alternatives, the approach considered in this paper has the advantage of being exhaustive, since
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{1}
determine two complementary families. On the other hand, our null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{0}
is nonparametric. This requires a distribution-free approach in which critical values are computed via bootstrap.

Let
S_{F_{n}}=T_{F_{n}}/\overline{X}
be the empirical version of
S_{F}
. We can measure deviations from the null hypothesis by measuring the positive part of the function
\widehat{\delta}(p):=p-S_{F_{n}}(p)
, which is the empirical counterpart of
\delta(p)=p-S_{F}(p)
. Under
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{0}
,
p-S_{F}(p)\leq 0,\forall p\in[0,1]
, therefore
\widehat{\delta}(p)\leq S_{F}(p)-S_{F_{n}}(p),\forall p\in[0,1]
. The following result, based on Theorem
1
, establishes the weak convergence of the process
s^{F}_{n}=\sqrt{n}(S_{F_{n}}-S_{F})
.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, for
n\to\infty
,
s_{n}^{F}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{S}_{F}
in
C[0,1]
,
where

\mathcal{S}_{F}(p)=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{F}(p)}{\mu_{F}}-\frac{T_{F}(p)\mathcal{T}_{F}(1)}{\mu_{F}^{2}}.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{F}
is a centered Gaussian process, we can establish that, under
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{0}
,

\sqrt{n}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})\leq\sqrt{n}\Phi_{r}(S_{F_{n}}-S_{F})\rightsquigarrow\Phi_{r}(\mathcal{S}_{F}),

where, for any
\alpha<1/2
, the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of
\Phi_{r}(\mathcal{S}_{F})
is positive, finite, and unique. Differently, under
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{1}
, the test statistic
\sqrt{n}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})
diverges in probability. Now, we may approximate the distribution of
\sqrt{n}\Phi_{r}(S_{F_{n}}-S_{F})
using the same bootstrap procedure discussed earlier. In particular, let
S_{F^{*}_{n}}
be the bootstrap version of
S_{F_{n}}
. The
p
-values may be approximated as

p\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}(\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}((S_{F_{n}}-S_{F_{n}^{*}})_{k})>\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta})),

where
(S_{F_{n}}-S_{F_{n}^{*}})_{k}
is the
k
-th realisation of the simulated process
S_{F_{n}}-S_{F_{n}^{*}}
. Accordingly, we may establish the following asymptotic properties.

Proposition 3
.

Under Assumption
1
,

1.

If
\mathcal{H}^{NUBE}_{0}
is true,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\{\text{reject }\mathcal{H}_{0}\}\leq\alpha
;

2.

If
\mathcal{H}^{NBUE}_{1}
is true,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\{\text{reject }\mathcal{H}_{0}\}=1
.

5
Tests for the excess wealth order

Similarly to the case of the TTT order,
\mathcal{H}^{ew}_{0}
is false if and only if the difference
\delta(p)=W_{G}(p)-W_{F}(p)
is positive at some point
p\in(0,1]
. Letting
{W}_{F_{n}}(p)=\overline{X}_{n}-T_{F_{n}}
and
{W}_{G_{m}}(p)=\overline{Y}_{m}-T_{G_{n}}
, we reject the null hypothesis if
\Phi_{r}(W_{G_{m}}-W_{F_{n}})
is large enough. As
n
and
m
tend to infinity,
\widehat{\delta}:=W_{G_{m}}-W_{F_{n}}
converges strongly and uniformly to
\delta
in
[0,1]
.
The empirical excess wealth processes associated with
F
and
G
may be defined as

w^{F}_{n}=\sqrt{n}(W_{F_{n}}-W_{F})\qquad w^{G}_{m}=\sqrt{m}(W_{G_{m}}-W_{G}),

respectively. These processes yield

\Delta_{n,m}:=\sqrt{r_{n,m}}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)=\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{n+m}}w^{G}_{m}-\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{n+m}}w^{F}_{n},

where
r_{n,m}=nm/(n+m)
. Weak convergence of
\Delta_{n,m}
may be established using the same arguments of Theorem
1
.

Corollary 2
.

Under Assumption
1
, for the sampling schemes discussed in Section
2.1
we have

\sqrt{r_{n,m}}(\widehat{\delta}-\delta)\rightsquigarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}=\sqrt{\lambda}\mathcal{W}_{G}-\sqrt{1-\lambda}\mathcal{W}_{F}\quad\text{ in }C[0,1],

where

\mathcal{W}_{F}(p)=-\frac{(1-p)\mathcal{B}_{1}(p)}{f\circ F^{-1}(p)}+\int_{p}^{1}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{1}(y)}{f\circ F^{-1}(y)}dy,\qquad\mathcal{W}_{G}(p)=-\frac{(1-p)\mathcal{B}_{2}(p)}{g\circ G^{-1}(p)}+\int_{p}^{1}\frac{\mathcal{B}_{2}(y)}{g\circ G^{-1}(y)}dy.

Using the same arguments of Section
3
, we may establish an equivalent result to Lemma
1
. Denoting the bootstrap estimators of
T_{F_{n}}
and
W_{G_{m}}
as
W_{F^{*}_{n}}
and
T_{G^{*}_{m}}
, respectively, and letting
\widehat{\delta}^{*}=W_{G^{*}_{n}}-W_{F^{*}_{n}}
, the functional delta method for the bootstrap implies that
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}^{*}-\widehat{\delta})
has the same limiting distribution of
\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}})
. This allows the approximation of
p
-values using bootstrap replications as in Section
3.2
, accordingly, by the same arguments used in the proof of Proposition
2
, we show consistency of the corresponding bootstrap-based tests.

Under Assumptions

and the sampling schemes in Section 3.1,

6
Simulations

In this section, we perform some numerical analyses to investigate our tests’ finite sample properties. We focus on tests for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{NBUE}
.
We will use the following distributions on the nonnegative half-line,

F_{\text{W}}(x;a,b)=1-\exp(-(x/b)^{a}),
i.e., a Weibull distribution
W(a,b)
with shape parameter
a>0
and scale parameter
b>0
; and
F_{\text{SM}}(x;a,b,c)=1-\left((x/c)^{b}+1\right)^{-a}
, i.e., a Singh-Maddala distribution
\text{SM}(a,b,c)
with shape parameters
a>0,b>0
and scale parameter
c>0
. The TTT transform of
W(a,b)
is

T_{F_{\text{W}}}(p)=\frac{b}{a}\left(\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{a},0\right)-\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{a},-\log(1-p)\right)\right),

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function, while that of
\text{SM}(a,b,c)
exists just for
ab>1
and has the following expression

T_{F_{\text{SM}}}(p)=c\left((1-p)^{-1/a}-1\right)^{1/b}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(a,\frac{1}{b};1+\frac{1}{b};1-(1-x)^{-1/a}\right),

where
\,{}_{2}F_{1}
is the hypergeometric function.

We simulate different scenarios from the distributions above under the independent sampling framework, for
n=50,100,200,500,
to investigate finite sample properties of the tests based on
\Phi_{\infty}
and
\Phi_{1}
. For each scenario, we run 500 simulation experiments and 500 bootstrap replications. The significance level of the test is set to
\alpha=0.1
. To simplify computations, we use the step function
\widetilde{T}_{F_{n}}(p)=\int_{0}^{F_{n}^{-1}(p)}(1-F_{n}(x))dx
instead of
T_{F_{n}}
, since the difference between these is asymptotically negligible (recall that
T_{F_{n}}
interpolates the jumps points of
\widetilde{T}_{F_{n}}
).

6.1
Tests for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}

We first let
X\sim\text{W}(a,1)
and
Y\sim\text{W}(2,1)
, in this case, we have
X\geq_{ttt}Y
when
a\geq 2
, and the dominance relation becomes more evident for larger values of
a
. Both tests in these cases provide rejection rates that are always smaller than the nominal level
\alpha
and get closer to 0 when the sample size grows, for this reason, we do not report these results here. Differently, letting
X\sim\text{W}(2,1)
and
Y\sim\text{W}(1,1)
we clearly have
X\not\geq_{ttt}Y
for
a>1
. In this case, it is not difficult to detect deviations from the null hypothesis since
T_{G}
is always above
T_{F}
. This is confirmed by the rejection rates in Figure
1
. In this case,
\Phi_{1}
tends to deliver larger power compared to
\Phi_{\infty}
. However, it may be harder to reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{ttt}
when
T_{G}
and
T_{F}
cross. We consider the case in which
X\sim\text{W}(a,1)
and
Y\sim\text{W}(1,1.2)
, for
a\geq 1
, and also the reverse case, namely,
X\sim\text{W}(1,1.2)
and
Y\sim\text{W}(a,1)
. The latter case is the most critical to detect, in particular for smaller values of
a
. Our results show that
\Phi_{\infty}
often provides larger simulated power compared to
\Phi_{1}
in these cases. For
X\sim\text{W}(1,1.2)
and
Y\sim\text{W}(1.25,1)
both tests (especially
\Phi_{1}
) struggle to reject the null hypothesis, at least for the sample sizes considered (we obtain larger power increasing
n
to 2000). However, even in this critical case, the
p
-values tend to decrease as the sample size grows, coherently with the established consistency property. Overall, our results suggest that
\Phi_{\infty}
may be more reliable than
\Phi_{1}
, especially when the TTT transforms cross.

First, recall that ageing properties are scale-independent, so there is no loss of generality in setting scale parameters equal to 1. The Weibull distribution
\text{W}(a,b)

a\geq 1

a<1

new worse than used in expectation

E\geq_{ttt}X/\mu_{F}
. Letting
X\sim\text{W}(a,1),
where
a
varies within
[0.8,1.1]
, we may observe the performance of the tests under the null and the alternative hypotheses. The results, reported in Figure
3

\Phi_{1}
provides larger power under the alternative compared to
\Phi_{\infty}
. The latter seems to be more conservative, as it appeares also by observing the behaviour of the tests under the null hypothesis. For both tests, the rejection rates tend to 1 under the alternative and tend to 0 under the null, confirming the asymptotic consistency properties. Moreover, the empirical power is always above the nominal level 0.1 when
a<1
, showing an unbiased behaviour against NBUE alternatives.

Appendix: Proofs

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be the space of maps
z:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}z(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}z(x)=1
, and the norm
||z||_{\mathbb{L}}=\max\{||z||_{\infty},||1-z||_{1}\}
. Moreover, let
\mathbb{B}
be the Banach space of measurable functions
z
from
(0,1)
to
\mathbb{R}
equipped with the norm
||z||_{\mathbb{B}}=\int_{0}^{1}|z(p)|dp
. As shown by
Kaji 2018
, under Assumption
1
, the map
\theta(F)=F^{-1}
, from CDFs to quantile functions, is Hadamard differentiable at
F
, tangentially to the set
\mathbb{L}_{0}
of continuous functions in
\mathbb{L}
, with derivative map
\theta^{\prime}_{F}:\mathbb{L}_{0}\to\mathbb{B}
given by
\theta_{F}^{\prime}(z)=-(z\circ F^{-1})(F^{-1})^{\prime}.

Now, we need to establish the Hadamard differentiability of the map
\phi:F^{-1}\rightarrow T_{F}
as a map from
\mathbb{B}
to
C[0,1]
. By the change of variable
y=F(x)
, we can write

T_{F}(p)=\int_{0}^{F^{-1}(p)}(1-F(x))dx=\int_{0}^{p}(1-y)dF^{-1}(y).

By linearity of the operator
\phi(F^{-1})=\int_{0}^{.}(1-y)dF^{-1}(y)
, it is easy to see that the Hadamard derivative of
\phi
at
F^{-1}
in the direction
\alpha
is still
\phi
, namely
\phi^{\prime}_{F^{-1}}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{.}(1-y)d\alpha(y)
. That is, letting
F^{-1}_{t}=F^{-1}+t\alpha_{t}
, where
\alpha_{t}\rightarrow\alpha
and
t\rightarrow 0
,

\frac{\phi(F_{t}^{-1})-\phi(F^{-1})}{t}-\phi^{\prime}_{F^{-1}}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{.}(1-y)d(\alpha_{t}-\alpha)(y)\to 0.

By the chain rule for Hadamard differentiability
(
Van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
, Lemma 3.9.3)
, we have that the map
\phi\circ\theta:F\rightarrow T_{F}
is Hadamard differentiable at
F
, tangentially to
\mathbb{L}_{0}
, with derivative

(\phi\circ\theta)^{\prime}_{F}(z)=\phi^{\prime}_{\theta(F)}\circ\theta^{\prime}_{F}(z)=\int_{0}^{.}(1-y)d\theta^{\prime}_{F}(z(y)).

Integrating by parts,

(\phi\circ\theta)^{\prime}_{F}(z(p))=(1-p)\theta_{F}(z(p))+\int_{0}^{p}\theta_{F}(z(y))dy=-(1-p)(z\circ F^{-1}(p))(F^{-1}(p))^{\prime}-\int_{0}^{p}(z\circ F^{-1}(y))dF^{-1}(y).

Now, observe that

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{n}(F_{n}-F)\\
\sqrt{m}(G_{m}-G)\end{pmatrix}\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{B}_{1}\circ F\\
\mathcal{B}_{2}\circ G\end{pmatrix}\text{ in }\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L},

as shown in Lemma 5.1 of
Sun and Beare 2021
. Then, the functional delta method
(
Van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
, Theorem 3.9.4)
implies the joint weak convergence

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{n}(T_{F_{n}}(p)-T_{F}(p))\\
\sqrt{m}T_{G_{m}}(p)-T_{G}(p))\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{n}(\phi\circ\theta({F_{n}})(p)-\phi\circ\theta({F})(p))\\
\sqrt{m}(\phi\circ\theta({G_{m}})(p)-\phi\circ\theta({G})(p))\end{pmatrix}\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}(\phi\circ\theta)^{\prime}_{F}(\mathcal{B}_{1}\circ F)(p)\\
(\phi\circ\theta)^{\prime}_{G}(\mathcal{B}_{2}\circ G)(p)\end{pmatrix}\\
=\begin{pmatrix}-(1-p)\mathcal{B}_{1}(p)(F^{-1})^{\prime}(p)-\int_{0}^{p}\mathcal{B}_{1}(y)dF^{-1}(y)\\
-(1-p)\mathcal{B}_{2}(p)(G^{-1})^{\prime}(p)-\int_{0}^{p}\mathcal{B}_{2}(y)dG^{-1}(y)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathcal{T}_{F}(p)\\
-\mathcal{T}_{G}(p)\end{pmatrix}=_{d}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{T}_{F}(p)\\
\mathcal{T}_{G}(p)\end{pmatrix}\text{ in }C[0,1]\times C[0,1].

(1)

The last relation (
=_{d}
) is because
\mathcal{T}_{F}
and
\mathcal{T}_{G}
are centered Gaussian processes.
Then the results follow from the continuous mapping theorem, as in the proof of Lemma 2.1 of
Sun and Beare 2021
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2
.

.
As proved in Lemma 5.2 of
Sun and Beare 2021
,

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{n}({F^{*}_{n}}-{F_{n}})\\
\sqrt{m}({G^{*}_{m}}-{G_{m}})\end{pmatrix}\overset{as*}{\underset{M}{\rightsquigarrow}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{B}_{1}\circ F\\
\mathcal{B}_{2}\circ G\end{pmatrix}\text{ in }\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L},

where
\overset{as*}{\underset{M}{\rightsquigarrow}}
denotes weak convergence conditional on the data almost surely, see
Kosorok 2008
.
The proof of Theorem
1
establishes the Hadamard differentiability of the map
\phi\circ\theta:H\rightarrow T_{H}
, so that the functional delta method for the bootstrap implies

\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\begin{pmatrix}T_{F^{*}_{n}}-T_{F_{n}}\\
T_{G^{*}_{n}}-T_{G_{m}}\end{pmatrix}\overset{P}{\underset{M}{\rightsquigarrow}}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\mathcal{T}_{F}\\
(1-\lambda)\mathcal{T}_{G}\end{pmatrix}\text{ in }C[0,1]\times C[0,1],

where
\overset{P}{\underset{M}{\rightsquigarrow}}
denotes weak convergence conditional on the data in probability
(see
Kosorok 2008
, p.20)
.
This entails that
\Phi_{r}(\sqrt{r_{n,m}}(\widehat{\delta}_{k}^{*}-\widehat{\delta}))\overset{P}{\underset{M}{\rightsquigarrow}}\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})
by the continuous mapping theorem. The test rejects the null hypothesis if the test statistic exceeds the bootstrap threshold value
c^{*}_{n}(\alpha)=\inf\{y:P(\sqrt{r_{n,m}}\ \Phi_{r}(\widehat{\delta}^{*}-\widehat{\delta})>y|{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}})\leq\alpha\}
. However, the weak convergence result implies
c_{n}^{*}(\alpha)\rightarrow c(\alpha)=\inf\{y:P(\Phi_{r}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})>y)\leq\alpha\}
in probability, so Lemma
1
yields the result.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

Using the same notations for
\theta
and
\phi
as in the proof of Theorem
1
, we now focus on the map
\eta\circ\phi\circ\theta:F\to S_{F}
, where
\eta:C[0,1]\to C[0,1]
is defined as
\eta(T_{F})=T_{F}(\cdot)/T_{F}(1)
. The Hadamard derivative of
\eta
at
T_{F}
in the direction
u\in C[0,1]
is given by

\eta^{\prime}_{T_{F}}(u)=\frac{u(\cdot)}{T_{F}(1)}-\frac{u(1)T_{F}(\cdot)}{T_{F}(1)^{2}},

see the proof of Lemma 2.1 in
Sun and Beare 2021
. Therefore, the result follows from the chain rule and the functional delta method.
∎

Proof of Corollary

As in Theorem
1
, we need to establish the Hadamard differentiability of the map
\psi:F^{-1}\rightarrow W_{F}^{-1}
as a map from
\mathbb{B}
to

. Writing
W_{F}(p)=\mu_{F}-\int_{0}^{p}(1-y)dF^{-1}(y)=\int_{p}^{1}(1-y)dF^{-1}(y),

F^{-1}
