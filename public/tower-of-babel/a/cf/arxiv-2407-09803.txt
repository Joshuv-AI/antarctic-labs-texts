Introduction: Codes in Graphs

Traditionally error-correcting codes are modelled as subsets
{\mathcal{C}}
of vectors in a finite vector space
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
over a field of order
q
, where the vectors are represented as
n
-dimensional row vectors with entries from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and distance between two vectors is the number of entries where they differ.
The
minimum distance

\delta
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is the smallest distance between two codewords, and the code is called
perfect
if the balls of radius
\lfloor(\delta-1)/2\rfloor
partition the space
V
. Much work was devoted by many researchers to understanding and finding new perfect codes, and eventually, Tietäväinen
[
89
]
, and Zinoviev and Leontiev
[
92
]
, independently showed that the only non-trivial perfect codes over finite fields are ‘Hamming-like’ codes (perfect single-error correcting codes), and the famous Golay codes in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{23}
and
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{11}
, (see for example, the 1975 survey on perfect codes by Van Lint
[
90
]
). Larger families of codes with desirable properties were sought.
For example, in 1971, Semakov, Zinoviev, and Zaitsev
[
81
]
introduced a family of codes, properly containing the perfect codes, which they called uniformly packed codes, and which retained strong regularity properties for ‘packing’ codewords in the vector space. Then independently, around 1973, Norman Biggs
[
13
,
14
]
and Philippe Delsarte
[
28
,
29
]
suggested a complete change of focus.
Biggs introduced the concept of a perfect code in a graph, while Delsarte developed a general theory of association schemes in coding theory. This led to the notion of
codes in graphs
, which is the theme of this chapter.

The chapter will provide an overview of recent work on codes in graphs which are neighbour-transitive, or have stronger symmetry properties. Most of the graphs considered are distance-regular, and our particular focus is codes in Hamming graphs since the current work on neighbour-transitive codes in Johnson graphs (a particular study advocated by Delsarte) has been recently covered in the 2021 survey by the second author
[
74
]
1

1

This was the Clay Lecture at the British Combinatorial Conference 2021, and a version of the survey may be downloaded from
https://www.claymath.org/events/british-combinatorial-conference-2021/
. In Section
3
we give a brief historical account of completely transitive codes in Hamming graphs and Johnson graphs which motivated the more general developments on neighbour-transitivity, particularly in Hamming graphs, surveyed in Section
5
. We also summarise very recent work on codes in other graphs, such as Kneser graphs and the incidence graphs of generalised quadrangles, as well as state some results for Grassmann graphs and bilinear forms graphs (the
q
-analogues of the Johnson and Hamming graphs).

1
Codes in graphs: neighbour-transitivity and complete transitivity

A
graph

\Gamma=(V,E)
consists of a set
V
of
vertices
and a set
E
of
edges
(unordered pairs of vertices), and
a
code

{\mathcal{C}}
in
\Gamma
is defined as a subset of
V
.
Distance
between distinct
codewords
(elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
) is given by the length of a shortest path between them in the graph
\Gamma
, and the
minimum distance

\delta({\mathcal{C}})
is the minimum distance between distinct codewords. This viewpoint also suggests a natural measure for the symmetry of a code
{\mathcal{C}}
in
\Gamma
.
Symmetry of the graph
\Gamma
is measured by its
automorphism group

{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
, namely the subgroup of permutations of
V
which leave invariant the edge-set
E
. We take as the
automorphism group

{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
of the code
{\mathcal{C}}
the subgroup of
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
consisting of all elements that leave
{\mathcal{C}}
invariant (setwise).
Delsarte suggested choosing
\Gamma
to be a
distance-regular graph
: that is to say,
for any (possibly equal) vertices
v
and
w
, the number of vertices at distance
j
from
v
and at distance
k
from
w
depends only upon
j,k
, and the distance between
v
and
w
.
He defined a special type of code, now
called a completely-regular code, ‘which enjoys combinatorial (and often
algebraic) symmetry akin to that observed for perfect codes’ (see
[
68
, page 1]
). Again, disappointingly, not many examples of completely regular codes
were found with good error-correcting properties – that is to say, with large distance between distinct codewords (see the comments and references in
[
74
, Section 1.1]
).

A useful notion for exploring a code
{\mathcal{C}}
in a graph
\Gamma
is the
distance partition
of
{\mathcal{C}}
. This is the partition:

\{{\mathcal{C}}_{0},{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho}\}
of
V
such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}={\mathcal{C}}
and, for
i\geq 1
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is the set of all vertices
\gamma
such that the minimum distance between
\gamma
and a codeword is equal to
i
. The largest integer
i
such that
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\neq\emptyset
is called the
covering radius
of
{\mathcal{C}}
, and is denoted
\rho
and sometimes
\rho({\mathcal{C}})
.

(1)

Since graph automorphisms preserve distance, the automorphism group
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
fixes each of the subsets
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
setwise, and so each
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is a union of
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
-orbits. A natural, but very strong, symmetry condition to place on a code is to require that each
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is a single orbit of
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
. Codes with this property are called
completely transitive
, and this family of codes was among the first family of codes to be studied in various families of graphs. We discuss some of the results about completely transitive codes in Section
3
.

Most recent studies of codes in graphs have focused on strictly larger families than the completely regular codes or the completely transitive codes, where their strict regularity or symmetry conditions have been replaced by more ‘local’ conditions. This new approach perhaps dates back to a discussion between the second author and (Bob) Liebler in 2005 (leading to
[
66
]
). In the context of codes in Johnson graphs, Bob suggested that the stringent regularity conditions imposed for complete regularity, could be replaced by a ‘local transitivity’ property. This led to the notion of a

neighbour-transitive code
in an arbitrary graph
\Gamma
, that is to say, a code
{\mathcal{C}}
such that

{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
is transitive both on
{\mathcal{C}}
and on the set
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
of the distance partition. The vertices in
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
are called
code-neighbours
(the non-codewords that are adjacent in
\Gamma
to some codeword). More recently, for any positive integer
s\leq\rho({\mathcal{C}})
, a code
{\mathcal{C}}
in
\Gamma
is called
s
-neighbour-transitive
if each of
{\mathcal{C}}_{0},{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{C}}_{s}
is an
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
-orbit. Thus the neighour-transitive codes are
1
-neighbour-transitive, and the completely transitive codes are
\rho({\mathcal{C}})
-neighbour-transitive.

2
Fundamental concepts: neighbour-transitive codes

In this section we present some general concepts and results regarding codes in graphs. We begin with a discussion in arbitrary graphs before specialising to the Hamming graphs and other specific graph families.

1
Parameters and regularity properties for codes in graphs

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph. Note that we will always assume that
\Gamma
is simple, finite, undirected and connected. Let
\alpha,\beta\in V
. Denote by
d(\alpha,\beta)
the
distance
in
\Gamma
between
\alpha
and
\beta
, that is, the length of the shortest path between
\alpha
and
\beta
. Define
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)=\{\gamma\in V\mid d(\alpha,\gamma)=i\}
. Furthermore, the
ball

B_{i}(\alpha)
of radius
i
centered at
\alpha
is defined to be
\bigcup_{j=0}^{i}\Gamma_{j}(\alpha)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a code in a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
. We refer to elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
as
codewords
. A code
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
|{\mathcal{C}}|\leq 1
or
{\mathcal{C}}=V(\Gamma)
is called
trivial
, and we will often be assuming without statement that
{\mathcal{C}}
is non-trivial. If
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
is a subset of
{\mathcal{C}}
then we say that
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
is a
subcode
of
{\mathcal{C}}
. The
minimum distance

\delta
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is the smallest distance between a pair of distinct elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
. The
error-correction capacity

e
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is defined to be the largest value of
i
for which, given distinct
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
, the balls
B_{i}(\alpha)
and
B_{i}(\beta)
are disjoint. These two parameters are related by
e=\lfloor(\delta-1)/2\rfloor
. The
covering radius

\rho
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is the smallest value of
i
for which
\bigcup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}}B_{i}(\alpha)=V
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a code with error-correction capacity
e
in a graph
\Gamma
and let
i\leq e
. Then the following hold.

(1)

For each
\gamma\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
there exists a unique
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\gamma\in\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
.

(2)

{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is the disjoint union
\bigcup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}}\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
.

(3)

\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)\neq\emptyset
for all
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proof.

All the assertions hold for a trivial code
{\mathcal{C}}
, with
i=e=0
, so we may assume that
{\mathcal{C}}
is non-trivial. Then its minimum distance
\delta=\delta({\mathcal{C}})
is a positive integer.
Since every element of
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is at distance
i
from some element of
{\mathcal{C}}
we have
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\subseteq\bigcup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}}\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
. Let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
. Consider a codeword
\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\alpha\neq\beta
. If
d(\beta,\gamma)\leq i
, then
\gamma\in B_{i}(\alpha)\cap B_{i}(\beta)
and hence
d(\alpha,\beta)\leq 2i\leq 2e<\delta
, which is a contradiction. Thus
d(\beta,\gamma)>i
. In particular,
\alpha
is the unique codeword in
{\mathcal{C}}
at distance
i
from
\gamma
, and part (1) holds. This implies moreover that
\gamma\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
. Thus
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
for each
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and hence
\bigcup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}}\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)={\mathcal{C}}_{i}
. Now, if
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are distinct codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}
, then the assumption
i\leq e
implies that
B_{i}(\alpha)\cap B_{i}(\beta)=\emptyset
so that
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)\cap\Gamma_{i}(\beta)=\emptyset
. Thus the union is disjoint, and part (2) is proved.

Consider distinct codewords
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and let
(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r})
be a path in
\Gamma
of length
r=d(\alpha,\beta)
from
\alpha=\gamma_{0}
to
\gamma_{r}=\beta
. It follows from the minimality in the definition of
d(\alpha,\beta)
that
d(\alpha,\gamma_{j})=j
and
d(\beta,\gamma_{r-j})=j
whenever
0\leq j\leq r
. If
r\leq i
, then taking
j=r
we have
\beta\in B_{i}(\alpha)\cap B_{i}(\beta)
, which is a contradiction, since
i\leq e
. Hence
r>i
and
\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
, proving part (3).
∎

Definition 2.2
.

For a code
{\mathcal{C}}
in a graph
\Gamma
and a non-negative integer
s
at most the covering radius
\rho
,
{\mathcal{C}}
is said to be
s
-regular
if for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,s\}
there exist non-negative integers
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
(if
s<\rho
), and
c_{i}
(if
i>0
), such that for each vertex
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
there are precisely:

(1)

a_{i}
vertices in
\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
,

(2)

b_{i}
vertices in
\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{i+1}
, and,

(3)

c_{i}
vertices in
\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{i-1}
,

and
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
depend only on
i
, and not on the particular choice of
\alpha
. If
s=\rho
then
{\mathcal{C}}
is said to be
completely regular
.

It is worth mentioning that a graph
\Gamma
is distance-regular, according to the usual definition, if and only if, for every vertex
\alpha
of
\Gamma
, the singleton set
\{\alpha\}
is a completely regular code in
\Gamma
.

2
Symmetry of codes in graphs

The
symmetric group

{\rm Sym}(V)
is the group of all permutations of
V
, and, for
\alpha\in V
and
g\in G
, we write
\alpha^{g}
for the image of
\alpha
under
g
.
Each element
g\in{\rm Sym}(V)
permutes subsets of
V
in a natural way, namely for
U\subseteq V
, the image
U^{g}
of
U
under
g
is the set
\{\alpha^{g}\mid\alpha\in U\}
of images for the elements of
U
.
The
setwise stabiliser
of
U
is the set
{\rm Sym}(V)_{U}:=\{g\in{\rm Sym}(V)\mid U^{g}=U\}
, and this is a subgroup of
{\rm Sym}(V)
. In particular, for each
i<|V|
,
g
permutes the
i
-element subsets of
V
among themselves and, for a set
E
of
i
-element subsets, we say that
g

leaves
E
invariant
if
U^{g}\in E
for all
U\in E
.
We often deal with
permutation groups on
V
, that is, subgroups
G\leq{\rm Sym}(V)
. A permutation group is
transitive
on
V
if, for all
u,v\in V
, there exists
g\in G
such that
u^{g}=v
. For
1<i<v
, we say that
G
is
i
-transitive
on
V
if
G
is transitive on
V
and, for
v\in V
, the stabiliser
G_{v}
is
(i-1)
-transitive on
V\setminus\{v\}
. Also
G
is said to be
i
-homogeneous
on
V
if
G
is transitive on the set of
i
-element subsets of
V
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph. Then an
automorphism
of
\Gamma
is a permutation
g\in{\rm Sym}(V)
such that
g
leaves the edge set
E
invariant. The set of all automorphisms of
\Gamma
forms a subgroup
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
called the
automorphism group
of
\Gamma
.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is a code in
\Gamma
then, as introduced in Section
1
, the
automorphism group
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is the setwise stabiliser
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
of
{\mathcal{C}}
in
{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
, that is to say,
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})={\rm Sym}(V)_{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
.

(2)

Two codes are
equivalent
if there exists an automorphism of
\Gamma
mapping one to the other. Note that equivalent codes have many of the same properties, for instance, the same minimum distance, the same covering radius and isomorphic automorphism groups. Hence, we will often be interested in codes only up to equivalence.

The following concepts are the main focus of this chapter and may be viewed as algebraic analogues of Definition
2.2
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a code with covering radius
\rho
in a graph
\Gamma
, let
G\leq{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
, and let
s\in\{1,\ldots,\rho\}
. Then we make the following definitions.

(1)

{\mathcal{C}}
is
(G,s)
-neighbour-transitive
if
G
acts transitively on each of the sets
{\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{s}
.

(2)

{\mathcal{C}}
is
G
-neighbour-transitive
if
{\mathcal{C}}
is
(G,1)
-neighbour-transitive.

(3)

{\mathcal{C}}
is
G
-completely transitive
if
{\mathcal{C}}
is
(G,\rho)
-neighbour-transitive.

Moreover, we say that
{\mathcal{C}}
is
neighbour-transitive
,
s
-neighbour-transitive
, or
completely transitive
, respectively, if
{\mathcal{C}}
is
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
-neighbour-transitive,
({\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}}),s)
-neighbour-transitive, or
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
-completely transitive, respectively.

It turns out that many famous codes have these symmetry properties. We mention several of them in Example
2.4
. We also give in Example
2.5
a simple example of an explicit infinite family of completely transitive codes.

.

-dimensional vector-subspace of

Example 2.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be the graph with vertex set
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2n}
and vertices
i,j
defined to be adjacent if
i-j=\pm 1
(
i.e.
,
\Gamma
is a cycle of length
2n
) and let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{0,n\}
. Then
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
is generated by the rotation
i\mapsto i+n
(for
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2n}
), and the reflection
i\mapsto-i
(for
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2n}
). Hence
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})\cong C_{2}^{2}
and
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}
. We also have
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}=\{\pm i,n\pm i\}
for each
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq n/2
. Note that when
1\leq i<n/2
the cardinality
|{\mathcal{C}}_{i}|=4
, but when
n
is even we have
|{\mathcal{C}}_{n/2}|=2
. In all cases
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
for each
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq n/2
, and thus
{\mathcal{C}}
is completely transitive.

Lemma
2.6
, below, gives two additional equivalent conditions for
(G,s)
-neighbour-transitivity for integers
s
at most the error-correction capacity. It is most useful for
s
-neighbour-transitive codes with ‘large’ minimum distance
\delta
, namely
\delta\geq 2s+1
. Applications of this result are two-fold. Firstly, it is often simpler to prove
s
-neighbour-transitivity of a code in terms of the ‘local action’ of the stabiliser of a codeword
\alpha
on the ball
B_{s}(\alpha)
(Lemma
2.6
(2)). Secondly, Lemma
2.6
often allows, in the case of a specific graph, for us to prove structural results. We will see examples of such structural results in later sections.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a code in a graph
\Gamma
such that
{\mathcal{C}}
has error-correction capacity
e\geq 1
, let
G\leq{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
, let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and let
s
be an integer such that
1\leq s\leq e
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

{\mathcal{C}}
is
(G,s)
-neighbour-transitive.

(2)

G
acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}
and, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, the stabiliser
G_{\alpha}
is transitive on
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
.

(3)

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
,
G
acts transitively on the set
\{(\beta,\gamma)\mid\beta\in{\mathcal{C}},\gamma\in\Gamma_{i}(\beta)\}
.

Proof.

Suppose that part (1) holds. Then, by Definition
2.3
,
G
acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}
and transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
. Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
. Then, by Lemma
2.1
(2),
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
and so there exists
g\in G
such that
\gamma_{1}^{g}=\gamma_{2}
. By Lemma
2.1
(1),
\alpha
is the unique element of
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
d(\alpha,\gamma_{1})=d(\alpha,\gamma_{2})=i
, and hence
\alpha^{g}=\alpha
, that is,
g\in G_{\alpha}
. Thus
G_{\alpha}
acts transitively on
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
and so part (1) implies part (2).

Suppose that part (2) holds. Let
1\leq i\leq s
and let
\beta\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{i}(\beta)
. Since
G
acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}
, there exists
g_{1}\in G
such that
(\beta,\gamma)^{g_{1}}=(\alpha,\gamma^{\prime})
for some
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
. Furthermore,
G_{\alpha}
acts transitively on
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
, and hence there exists
g_{2}\in G_{\alpha}
such that
(\gamma^{\prime})^{g_{2}}=\gamma_{1}
. Thus,
(\beta,\gamma)^{g_{1}g_{2}}=(\alpha,\gamma_{1})
and so part (2) implies part (3).

Finally, suppose that part (3) holds. Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
and, as in Lemma
2.1
, let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
be the unique elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
d(\beta_{1},\nu_{1})=d(\beta_{2},\nu_{2})=i
. Then
(\beta_{j},\nu_{j})
(for
j=1,2
) lies in the set of pairs in part (3), and so there exists an element
h\in G
such that
(\beta_{1},\nu_{1})^{h}=(\beta_{2},\nu_{2})
. Thus
\nu_{1}^{h}=\nu_{2}
and
G
acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
. Also by Lemma
2.1
part (3), for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, there exist
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
such that, for each
j=1,2
,
\nu_{j}\in\Gamma_{i}(\beta_{j})
so
(\beta_{j},\nu_{j})

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a non-trivial code with covering radius
\rho
and minimum distance

in a connected graph
\Gamma
. Suppose that

such that

{\mathcal{C}}

G_{\alpha}
acts transitively on each of
\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)
and
\Gamma_{2}(\alpha)
. Then one of the following holds:

(1)

\rho\geq 2
;

(2)

\rho=1
,
\delta=3

is a perfect code.

(3)

\rho=1
,

, and either

(a)

\Gamma

(b)

for every pair

.

Proof.

If

is a trivial code, but since this is not the case we have

. If

. In particular

. If

and

and

, and any pair of balls of radius

centered at distinct codewords is disjoint. Moreover, since

partitions

. Thus

Thus we may assume that
\delta=2

{\mathcal{C}}
and

acts transitively on
\Gamma_{2}(\alpha)
, it follows, for each codeword

, that

is contained in

. If there are no edges between distinct vertices of

and a vertex of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}

Elusive codes

In this subsection we make a short commentary on the concept of neighbour-transitivity. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a code in a connected graph
\Gamma
such that the minimum distance
\delta({\mathcal{C}})\geq 3
so, by Lemma
2.1
, the set of code neighbours is the disjoint union
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}=\cup_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{C}}}\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)
. It turns out that

determines the code

if
\delta({\mathcal{C}})
is large enough and if the graph

is
reduced
, that is, if:

\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)=\Gamma_{1}(\alpha^{\prime})
if and only if
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
.

(3)

We note that all the graphs we consider in the chapter are reduced in this sense.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
be the set of code-neighbours of a non-trivial code in a connected regular reduced graph

. If

then
{\mathcal{C}}=\{\alpha\in V(\Gamma)\mid\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\}
, and hence
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
determines

.

Proof.

Let
X:=\{\alpha\in V(\Gamma)\mid\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\}
. Since
\delta({\mathcal{C}})\geq 5
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
contains
\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)
for each codeword

and hence

. We claim that equality holds. Suppose to the contrary that
\alpha\in X\setminus{\mathcal{C}}
, and let
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
be distinct vertices in
\Gamma_{1}(\alpha)\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
. By the definition of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
there are codewords
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that

. Since the minimum distance

, it follows that

. For fixed

.
∎

It follows from Lemma

that, under the conditions of that lemma, the setwise stabilisers in

and of

4

-Neighbour-transitive codes and

-distance-transitive graphs

There is a beautiful link between
(G,s)
-neighbour-transitive codes and
(G,s)
-distance-transitive graphs
, namely connected graphs
\Gamma=(V,E)
for which
G\leq{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
is transitive on the distance sets
\Gamma_{i}:=\{(\alpha,\beta)\mid d(\alpha,\beta)=i\}
, for

. The link involves a
quotient graph

\Gamma_{N}
modulo a normal subgroup
N\unlhd G
: the quotient
\Gamma_{N}
is the graph with vertices the
N
-orbits in
V
such that distinct
N
-orbits
U,U^{\prime}
form an edge of
\Gamma_{N}
provided there exist
\alpha\in U
and
\beta\in U^{\prime}
such that
\{\alpha,\beta\}\in E
. For a vertex
\alpha\in V
, we denote the
N
-orbit containing
\alpha
by
\alpha^{N}:=\{\alpha^{x}\mid x\in N\}
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a graph and
G\leq{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
such that
G
acts transitively on
V
, and let
N\lhd G
be intransitive on
V
. Suppose further that, for some
\alpha\in V

{\mathcal{C}}=\alpha^{N}
is a
(G_{{\mathcal{C}}},s)
-neighbour-transitive code in
\Gamma
. Then the quotient graph
\Gamma_{N}
is
(G/N,s)
-distance-transitive.

Proof.

Since
N\lhd G
and
G
is transitive on
V
, it follows that
G
acts transitively on the set of
N
-orbits in
V
, that is to say, the vertex set
V(\Gamma_{N})
of
\Gamma_{N}
, see
[
75
, Lemma 2.20]

N
leaves each of its orbits invariant,

is contained in the kernel of this
G

acts vertex-transitively on
\Gamma_{N}
. Recall that
{\mathcal{C}}=\alpha^{N}
is one of the
N
-orbits, and as a code in
\Gamma
, it is
(G_{{\mathcal{C}}},s)
-neighbour-transitive, by assumption.

Let
U,U^{\prime}
be adjacent vertices of
\Gamma_{N}
, and let
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}\in E
such that
\alpha_{1}\in U
and
\alpha_{2}\in U^{\prime}
. Since
N
acts transitively on
U
and on
U^{\prime}
, it follows that every vertex of

is adjacent in
\Gamma
to some vertex in
U^{\prime}
, and every vertex of
U^{\prime}
is adjacent in
\Gamma
to some vertex in
U
.

Let
X,Y
be vertices of
\Gamma_{N}
at distance
i
in
\Gamma_{N}
from
{\mathcal{C}}
, where
1\leq i\leq s
. Then, arguing as in the preceding paragraph, there exist vertices
\beta\in X
and
\gamma\in Y
such that there are paths of length
i
in
\Gamma
from
\alpha
to each of
\beta
and
\gamma
. Suppose that there exists a codeword
\alpha^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that there is a path of length
j
from
\alpha^{\prime}
to
\beta
with
0<j<i
. Then
{\mathcal{C}}
and
X
would be at distance less than
i
in
\Gamma_{N}
, which is not the case. Hence each codeword of
{\mathcal{C}}
has distance at least
i
from
\beta
, and it follows that

. By an identical argument

. Since
{\mathcal{C}}
is
(G_{{\mathcal{C}}},s)
-neighbour-transitive, there exists an element

such that
\beta^{g}=\gamma
. Since
G
permutes the
N
-orbits among themselves, this means that

maps the
N
-orbit

containing
\beta
to the
N
-orbit

containing
\gamma
. Thus

induces a transitive action on the vertices of
\Gamma_{N}
at distance

from

. Since we have already shown that
\Gamma_{N}
is vertex-transitive, and since

is in the kernel of the

\Gamma_{N}
, it follows that

acts transitively on the set of ordered pairs of

-vertices at distance

. Finally since this holds for all

, the result follows.
∎

Further techniques for analysing codes

We now define a concept that has proved useful for keeping track of code invariants, especially for analysing
s
-neighbour-transitive codes.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a set
\Omega
and let
\iota:\Omega\rightarrow S
, for some set
S
. If for all
\alpha\in\Omega
and

the equality
\iota(\alpha)=\iota(\alpha^{g})

\iota
is called
G
-invariant
. The elements of
S
are called
types
, namely if
\alpha\in\Omega
, then we say that
\alpha
has
type

\iota(\alpha)
.

The archetypal example of a
G
-invariant map
\iota:\Omega\rightarrow S
is to take
S
as the set of
G
-orbits in
\Omega
and define
\iota(\alpha)=\alpha^{G}
, for each
\alpha\in\Omega
. We consider another example below for the Johnson graphs which are defined in Definition
4.5
. Note that this example will be typical for us, in that
\Omega
will often be the vertex set of a graph containing a code of interest.

Example 2.17
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a
G
-neighbour-transitive code in the Johnson graph

(see Section
2
) with underlying set
{\mathcal{V}}

fixes setwise some subset

with
0<|U|<v
. For a vertex
\alpha
of

define
\iota(\alpha)=|\alpha\cap U|
. Let

. Then, since

, it follows that
\iota(\alpha^{g})=|\alpha^{g}\cap U|=|(\alpha\cap U)^{g}|=\iota(\alpha)
. Hence
\iota
is
G
-invariant.

In Example
2.17

G
acts transitively on

has the same type. The next result expands on this observation.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}

(2)

Proof.

3
Commentary on the origins of completely-transitive codes

In a 1987 preprint
[
83
, Section 7]
, Patrick Solé introduced the term ‘completely transitive code’ in the context of binary linear codes. His concept is different in a number of respects from that introduced in
Definition
2.3
(3), and we comment more below.
To the best of our knowledge, the notion of a ‘completely-transitive code in a graph’ dates back to 1988, when Chris Godsil suggested to the second author the study of completely-transitive codes in Johnson graphs (see Definition
4.5
). Since a subset of vertices in a Johnson graph
J(v,k)
is a set of
k
-subsets of the underlying
v
-set
{\mathcal{V}}
, each code
{\mathcal{C}}
in
J(v,k)
has a natural interpretation as a ‘design’ with point-set
{\mathcal{V}}
and with constant block size
k
. Thus completely-transitive codes in Johnson graphs were first called
completely-transitive designs
. In 1988, Bill Martin was a PhD student of Chris Godsil studying completely regular designs (completely regular codes in
J(v,k)
) and Chris’s hope was that by studying the more restricted family of completely transitive designs we might discover new examples, and characterisations. An account of this investigation, dating from 1997, can be found in
[
48
]
. It was never published as a journal article, but was posted on the arXiv in 2014 because of repeated requests for copies.

When regarded as codes in the Hamming graph
H(n,2)
(Definition
4.1
), the ‘completely-transitive codes’ defined by Solé
[
83
, Section 7]
, or see
[
84
]
for the 1990 published version, are precisely those binary linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that are
G
-completely-transitive in the sense of Definition
2.3
(3) for the subgroup
G=T\rtimes G_{0}
of affine transformations, where
T
is the group of translations of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
by codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}
, and
G_{0}
is the subgroup of permutation matrices which leave
{\mathcal{C}}
invariant.
Note that
G_{0}
is the group traditionally regarded as the automorphism group of a linear code. In
[
84
, Section 7]
, Solé gave examples of such codes, and also a necessary condition, and a sufficient condition (separate conditions), for complete transitivity in terms of the natural action of
G_{0}
as a permutation group on a set of
n
points. Shortly afterwards, in 1991, Rifà and Pujol
[
76
]
(or see
[
18
, Proposition 13]
) showed that, for a binary linear completely transitive code
{\mathcal{C}}
of length
n
, the natural coset graph on the set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/{\mathcal{C}}
of additive cosets of
{\mathcal{C}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is distance-transitive. Solé’s concept of complete transitivity extends naturally to linear codes in
H(n,q)
for arbitrary prime powers
q
, as does the link with distance transitivity of the quotient graph
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}/{\mathcal{C}}
(see Proposition
2.12
with
s=\rho({\mathcal{C}})
). For this reason, in order to distinguish Solé’s completely transitive linear codes from the more general family of completely transitive codes in Hamming graphs, Solé’s notion is sometimes called

coset-complete-transitivity
, see
[
45
,
46
]
.
For linear codes in
H(n,q)
, where
q
is a prime power, the concepts of complete transitivity and coset-complete transitivity are equivalent if
q\leq 3
and definitely not equivalent if
q=7
or
q\geq 9
, see
[
46
, Theorem 1.3 and Example 3.1]
.

Giudici, in his masters thesis
[
45
]
and a joint paper with the second author
[
46
]
, introduced the notion given in Definition
2.3
of a
G
-completely-transitive code in a Hamming graph
H(n,q)
for arbitrary
n
and
q
, and made a general study of the structure of such graphs. He gave families of examples, and significant structural descriptions, which pointed to the importance of those codes in
H(n,q)
for which the automorphism group
G
induces a transitive action on the set of
n
entries of codewords, see
[
46
, Sections 4, 5 and 6]
. A recent survey article of Borges, Rifa and Zinoviev contains an overview of this and more recent work, see in particular
[
18
, Section 3]
on coset-completely transitive codes.

The error-correcting capacity
e
of a binary coset-completely transitive code was shown by Borges, Rifà and Zinoviev to be no larger than three,
[
16
,
17
]
or see
[
18
, Theorem 12]
,
but there are families of examples with unbounded covering radius (with
e=1
)
[
78
]
. We will see that similar statements can be made about the more general family of
s
-neighbour-transitive codes. In particular we will prove the following result in Section
4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{C}}
is an
s
-neighbour-transitive code in the Hamming graph
H(n,q)
such that
{\mathcal{C}}
has error-correcting capacity
e
with
\min\{e,s\}\geq 4
. Then
n\geq 9
,
q=2
,
e=\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
,
s=\lceil\frac{n-1}{2}\rceil
, and
{\mathcal{C}}
is equivalent to the binary repetition code
{\rm Rep}_{n}(2)
(see Definition
\ref{def:productandrepetition}(3)
).

Borges, Rifà and Zinoviev showed further that coset-completely transitive codes may be obtained via some innovative combinatorial methods, such as a Kronecker product construction (
[
79
]
or see
[
18
, Section 5.5]
), or a lifting procedure (
[
80
]
or see
[
18
, Section 5.2]
), while Gill, Gillespie and Semeraro
[
37
, Theorem C]
constructed new families from the incidence matrices of certain designs. Also, sometimes a well-known family of codes may contain only a few codes which are completely transitive. For example, a Preparata code of length
n
, or its extension, is completely transitive if and only if
n=15
, (
[
43
]
or see
[
18
, Theorem 15]
); these exceptional completely transitive codes are the Nordstrom–Robinson codes.

In this chapter we will see that a similar situation arises for the larger families of neighbour-transitive, and 2-neighbour-transitive codes, where substructures in graphs, and in finite geometries such as generalised quadrangles, have been used in constructions, and where group theory, sometimes relying on the finite simple group classification, has been applied to achieve classifications.

4
Graphs of interest

In this section we introduce several families of graphs. For each family we provide at least one example of a code arising from an interesting combinatorial or algebraic structure. Most of these families are analysed further in later sections. We have already informally met the first family of graphs, the Hamming graphs. They will also receive the most attention throughout the chapter.

Definition 4.1
.

(1)

(2)

Definition 4.5

Definition 4.6

Let

Example 4.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a set of size
v
, let
k
satisfy
2\leq k\leq(v-1)/2
, and let

and

be the Johnson and Kneser graphs, respectively, with underlying set

.
Since both graphs have the same vertex set, namely
{{\mathcal{V}}\choose k}
, a code in one is also a code in the other, generally with different parameters. For instance:

(a)

Let
U\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
such that
k<|U|<v
and let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{\alpha\in{{\mathcal{V}}\choose k}\mid\alpha\subset U\}
. By Theorem

(1), as a code in the Johnson graph,
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum distance
1
and is neighbour-transitive. We now consider
{\mathcal{C}}
to be a code in the Kneser graph. In this case, since there exists a pair of disjoint
k
-subsets of
U
if and only if
|U|\geq 2k
, it follows that
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum distance
1
in the Kneser graph if and only if
|U|\geq 2k
. Moreover, since there always exists a pair of
k
-subsets of
U
at distance
2
in
K(v,k)
(in particular,
|U|\geq k+1
implies there exists such a pair intersecting in a
(k-1)
-subset of

), we deduce that
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum distance

in the Kneser graph when
|U|<2k
. Moreover, it turns out that

is neighbour-transitive in the Kneser graph if and only if

(see Theorem
6.4
(1) and Example
6.3
).

(b)

Let
U\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
such that
0<|U|<k
and let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{\alpha\in{{\mathcal{V}}\choose k}\mid U\subset\alpha\}
. Theorem
2.19
(2) again tells us that, as a code in the Johnson graph,

has minimum distance
1
and is neighbour-transitive. However, since every codeword contains

, no pair of elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
are disjoint. Hence

has minimum distance at least

in the Kneser graph, and it is not difficult to see that the minimum distance equals

is always neighbour-transitive in the Kneser graph (see Theorem
6.4
(3) and Example
6.3
).

Roughly speaking, if a combinatorial definition is phrased in terms of sets then, for a prime power

Definition 4.8
.

Let

and

with

. The
bilinear forms graph

is the graph with vertex set the vector space of all linear maps
\alpha:X\rightarrow Y
where two vertices
\alpha
and
\beta
are adjacent if
{\rm rank}(\alpha-\beta)=1
.

Example 4.9
.

Delsarte
[
30
]
and Gabidulin
[
36
]
independently introduced the following class of codes. Let
n,k,s
be positive integers such that
\gcd(n,s)=1
. Identify
X
and
Y
with
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}
, considered as an
n
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-vector space, and note that each of the polynomials

defines an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear map

.

Define
{\mathcal{C}}

to be the code in
\Gamma=H_{q}(n,n)
given by the polynomials in the
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}
-vector space

{\mathcal{C}}=\left\langle x,x^{q^{s}},\ldots,x^{q^{s(k-1)}}\right\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}}.

Note that each polynomial in

is a

polynomial (see
[
65
, Section 3.4]
) and thus defines an
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-linear map from

to

. Also,
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum distance

. Hence, as long as

, the set
\Gamma_{1}(0)
comprises all rank
1
linear maps. The trace function
{\rm tr}(x)=x^{q^{n-1}}+\cdots+x^{q}+x
is one such rank

contains all translations by elements of

contains a subgroup isomorphic to

acts transitively on

and the stabiliser in

.

Let

Let

Two graphs related to incidence structures

An
incidence structure
(see
[
32
]
) is a triple

, where
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}
are disjoint non-empty sets of elements called

and

, respectively, and

is a point-line incidence relation. If

then we say that
p

lies on

\ell
,
p
is
incident
with
\ell
, or
\ell
is
incident
with
p
. The elements of
{\rm I}
are called
flags
and if
p\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}
and
(p,\ell)\notin{\rm I}
then
(p,\ell)
is called an
antiflag
. If there exists an
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that
(p_{1},\ell),(p_{2},\ell)\in{\rm I}
then
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are
collinear
. The
dual
incidence structure
{\mathcal{S}}^{D}=({\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{P}},{\rm I}^{D})
is obtained from

by interchanging the sets of points and lines, with incidence
{\rm I}^{D}
defined by
(\ell,p)\in{\rm I}^{D}
if

.

Definition 4.13
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}=({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{L}},{\rm I})
be an incidence structure. Then the

of
{\mathcal{S}}
is the graph with vertex set
{\mathcal{P}}\cup{\mathcal{L}}
and an edge

whenever
(p,\ell)\in{\rm I}
(and no further edges). The
collinearity graph
of

is the graph with vertex set
{\mathcal{P}}
and an edge
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
whenever
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are collinear.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\Gamma
be the incidence graph of an incidence structure
{\mathcal{S}}=({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{L}},{\rm I})
and let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an
s
-neighbour-transitive code in

. Then
{\mathcal{C}}
is an

-neighbour-transitive code in the collinearity graph of

.

Proof.

This follows from applying Lemma
2.6
to

as a vertex subset of both the incidence graph of
{\mathcal{S}}
and the collinearity graph of
{\mathcal{S}}
.
∎

The next three propositions are applications of Lemma
2.6

s
-neighbour-transitive codes in Hamming graphs. We include here a proof of the first in order to illustrate the general idea. Recall the notions of
i

-transitive group actions from Section

Proposition 5.1
.

[
38
, Proposition 2.5]

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a
(G,s)
-neighbour-transitive code, with error-correction capacity

, in
\Gamma=H(n,q)=H({\mathcal{N}},q)
and let

. Then, for each

,
G_{\alpha}
acts
i
-homogeneously on
{\mathcal{N}}
.

Proof.

Since the automorphism group of the Hamming graph is vertex-transitive, we may assume (replacing
{\mathcal{C}}
if necessary by
{\mathcal{C}}^{g}
, for some
g\in{\rm Aut}(H(n,q))={\rm Sym}(q)\wr{\rm Sym}(n)

\alpha=(0,\ldots,0)\in{\mathcal{C}}
for some distinguished element
0\in{\mathcal{Q}}
. By Lemma
2.6
, the stabiliser

acts transitively on
\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
for each

\Gamma_{i}(\alpha)
is the set of all weight
i

and

{\rm supp}(\beta_{1})=I
and
{\rm supp}(\beta_{2})=J
. Then there exists
g\in G
such that

. Since

, the codeword

is the only vertex of
{\mathcal{C}}
that has distance

from
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
, and thus

. This means that

is of the form

, where

,
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})\in B

Alphabet-affine codes in Hamming graphs

In this section we consider codes that are alphabet-affine (Definition
5.4
), that is, codes
H(n,q)
having an automorphism group not acting faithfully on entries, and giving rise to a
2
-transitive affine group in the action induced on the alphabet. A natural family of examples of such codes are the cyclic codes: a code
{\mathcal{C}}
in
H({\mathcal{N}},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})

is called
cyclic
if
{\mathcal{C}}
is linear and there exists an
n
-cycle
\sigma\in L\cap{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
.

(7)

In particular each cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
is alphabet-affine since
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
contains the subgroup of translations by elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
(acting transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}
). Moreover, a cyclic code is neighbour-transitive since
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
also contains both the subgroup of scalars and an
n
-cycle from the top group
L
.
On the other hand, if
x=h\sigma
with
h\in B
and
\sigma\in L
, then
x\in{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
does not necessarily imply that
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
. That is to say, a linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
may not be cyclic even if the induced group
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})^{\mathcal{N}}
on entries contains an
n
-cycle. We exhibit a small non-cyclic linear code with this property in Example
5.18
. In fact, as we see below, the class of codes that are alphabet-affine and neighbour-transitive is strictly larger than the class of cyclic codes.

Proposition 5.17
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a linear code with minimum distance
\delta\geq 3
in
H({\mathcal{N}},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
. Then
{\mathcal{C}}
is neighbour-transitive if and only if
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})^{{\mathcal{N}}}
is transitive. In particular, if
{\mathcal{C}}
is a cyclic code then
{\mathcal{C}}
is neighbour-transitive.

Proof.

If
{\mathcal{C}}
is neighbour-transitive then, by Proposition
5.1
,
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})^{\mathcal{N}}
is transitive. Suppose now that
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})^{\mathcal{N}}
is transitive. Since
{\mathcal{C}}
is linear,
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})
contains
T_{{\mathcal{C}}}
, which acts transitively on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Moreover, this implies that
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})=T_{{\mathcal{C}}}.{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})_{{\mathbf{0}}}
. In particular,
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})_{{\mathbf{0}}}^{{\mathcal{N}}}
is transitive. For each
i\in{\mathcal{N}}
, let
e_{i}:{\mathcal{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
such that
e_{i}:i\to 1
and
e_{i}:k\to 0
if
k\neq i
. Then
\Gamma_{1}({\mathbf{0}})=\{ae_{i}\mid a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\}
. Let
i,j\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
a_{i},a_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
so that
a_{i}e_{i},a_{j}e_{j}\in\Gamma_{1}({\mathbf{0}})
. Then, since
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})_{{\mathbf{0}}}
induces a transitive action on
{\mathcal{N}}
, there exists an
x\in{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})_{{\mathbf{0}}}
such that
(a_{i}e_{i})^{x}=be_{j}
, for some
b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
. Using the linearity of
{\mathcal{C}}
again, scalar multiplication by
a_{j}b^{-1}
gives an element of
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})_{{\mathbf{0}}}
, and hence we can map
a_{i}e_{i}
to
a_{j}e_{j}
. It then follows from Lemma
2.6
that
{\mathcal{C}}
is neighbour-transitive. The last sentence holds since
{\mathcal{C}}
being cyclic implies that
{\rm Aut}({\mathcal{C}})^{\mathcal{N}}
is transitive.
∎

5
Codes in Hamming graphs from permutation modules

In this section, we present examples of linear codes that are
s
-neighbour-transitive, for
s\geq 2
, arising from permutation modules. We construct these modules via polynomial algebras. Historically, polynomial algebras have been used to construct many interesting examples of codes, such as the generalised Reed–Muller codes and the projective Reed–Muller codes; see Definitions
5.27
and
5.28
below. We present a richer family of examples that may eventually lead to a classification of alphabet-affine,
2
-neighbour-transitive codes in
H(n,q)
with minimum distance at least five: such a classification would be a huge strengthening of Theorem
3.1
.

Throughout this section let
R={\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}]
, the ring of polynomials with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}
. Each element of
R
may be viewed as a function
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{t}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and conversely, by Lagrange interpolation (see
[
65
, Theorem 1.7.1]
), every such function may be represented (in at least one way) by an element of
R
. A monomial
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{t}^{a_{t}}
is said to have
degree

a_{1}+\cdots+a_{t}
and the degree of a polynomial is the maximum value of the degrees of its constituent monomials. The generalised Reed–Muller codes arise as follows as subspaces of
R
; see
[
31
]
.

Definition 5.27
.

Let
k
be an integer with
0\leq k\leq t(q-1)
. The
k
-th order
q
-ary generalised Reed–Muller code

{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{M}}_{q}(k,t)
in
H({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{t},{\mathbb{F}}_{q})
is the subspace of
R
consisting of all polynomials of degree at most
k
. When
q=2
these are simply called
Reed–Muller
codes.

The parameters of the generalised Reed–Muller codes are given in
[
5
, Theorem 5.4.1 and Corollary 5.5.4]
.
Let
d\in\{1,2,\ldots,q-1\}
and let
R[d]
be the subspace of
R
consisting of all polynomials
f
such that
f(ax_{1},\ldots,ax_{t})=a^{d}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
, for each
a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. In other words,
R[d]
consists of all polynomials
f
of
R
such that each monomial of
f
has non-zero degree equivalent to
d
modulo
q-1
. This leads to a similar construction to that in the previous definition, but now involving
R[d]
. See
[
85
]
for more details. Note that we are taking
{\mathcal{N}}
to be a set of representatives for the
1
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{t}
and then regarding the elements of
R[d]
as functions
{\mathcal{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.

Definition 5.28
.

Let
k
be an integer with

, let
{\mathcal{N}}
be a fixed set of representatives for the
1
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{t}
and let

. The

-th order

A

(MDS) code is a code

in the Hamming graph

[
87

[

, a linear MDS code

is “equivalent” to an
n

in the projective space
{\rm PG}_{k-1}(q)

{\mathcal{C}}
are representatives for a set of
n
points in
{\rm PG}_{k-1}(q)

k
points is contained in a hyperplane. The archetypal example of an MDS code is a Reed–Solomon code
{\mathcal{C}}
in

(see
[
87
, Section 2]
). In fact, such a Reed–Solomon code is equivalent to the projective Reed–Muller code

and, by Proposition

with automorphism group

]
