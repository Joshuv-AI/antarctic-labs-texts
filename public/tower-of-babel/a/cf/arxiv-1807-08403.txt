Bipolar thermoelectric effects in semiconducting carbon nanotubes: Description in terms of one-dimensional Dirac electrons
Takahiro Yamamoto 1 and Hidetoshi Fukuyama 2

Abstract
The thermoelectric effects in semiconducting single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) are investigated based on the linear response theory combined with the thermal Green’s function method. It is shown that the electronic states near the lowest conduction band minimum and the highest valence band maximum can be effectively described in terms of one-dimensional (1D) Dirac electrons to which a theoretical scheme is developed to describe the thermoelectric responses making it possible to study the effects of inter-band impurity scattering and in-gap states. Using the proposed scheme, the bipolar thermoelectric effects ( i.e. , the sign inversion of the Seebeck coefficient) in semiconducting SWCNTs observed in recent experiments are explained. Moreover, the temperature dependence of the Seebeck coefficient of semiconducting SWCNTs at low temperature is clarified.

2
Theory of Thermoelectric Effects of Dirac Electrons in One-Dimensional Solids

2.1
Dirac electrons in one-dimensional solids with disorder

This section gives a brief review of 1D Dirac electrons in solids with disorder.
The Hamiltonian of 1D Dirac electrons in an impurity disorder potential is given by

\displaystyle{\mathscr{H}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dx~\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)H(x)\Psi(x,t)

(1)

with the local Hamiltonian matrix

\displaystyle H(x)=-i\hbar v\sigma_{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\Delta\sigma_{z}+U(x),

(2)

where
\hbar
is the Dirac constant,
\Delta
is one half of the band gap,
v
is the velocity of a Dirac electron
in the high-energy region of
|E|\gg\Delta
,
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{z}
are the
x
and
z
components of Pauli matrices,

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{matrix}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad\sigma_{z}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&-1\\
\end{matrix}\right),

(3)

respectively, and
U(x)
is an impurity potential.

In Eq. (
1
), the field operators
\Psi(x,t)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)
are defined as
the following column and row vectors,

\displaystyle\Psi(x,t)\equiv\left(\begin{matrix}\psi_{1}(x,t)\\
\psi_{2}(x,t)\\
\end{matrix}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)\equiv(\psi_{1}^{\dagger}(x,t),\psi_{2}^{\dagger}(x,t)),

(4)

respectively. Here,
\psi_{n}^{\dagger}(x,t)
and
\psi_{n}(x,t)
are the fermionic creation and annihilation field operators in the
Heisenberg picture and satisfy the Heisenberg equations
i\hbar\frac{d\psi_{n}(x,t)}{dt}=[\psi_{n}(x,t),{\mathscr{H}}]
and

i\hbar\frac{d\psi_{n}^{\dagger}(x,t)}{dt}=[\psi_{n}^{\dagger}(x,t),{\mathscr{H}}]
, respectively. Thus, it can be easily proven that

\Psi(x,t)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)
satisfy the Shrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{d\Psi(x,t)}{dt}=H(x)\Psi(x,t)

(5)

and its Hermitian conjugate

\displaystyle-i\hbar\frac{d\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)}{dt}=\left(H(x)\Psi(x,t)\right)^{\dagger}.

(6)

Performing the Fourier transform of
\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)
as

\displaystyle\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{k}e^{-ikx}\Phi_{k}^{\dagger}(t)

(7)

with
\Phi_{k}^{\dagger}(t)\equiv(c_{1k}^{\dagger}(t),c_{2k}^{\dagger}(t))
and using

\displaystyle U(x)=\sum_{q}e^{iqx}U(q),

(8)

the 1D Dirac Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\mathscr{H}}=\sum_{k}\Phi^{\dagger}_{k}H_{0}(k)\Phi_{k}+\sum_{k,q}\Phi^{\dagger}_{k+q}U(q)\Phi_{k},

(9)

where
H_{0}(k)
is the Hamiltonian density of a 1D free Dirac electron in
k
space, which is given by

\displaystyle H_{0}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar vk\sigma_{x}+\Delta\sigma_{z}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\Delta&\hbar vk\\
\hbar vk&-\Delta\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(11)

The eigenvalues of
H_{0}(k)
in Eq. (
11
) can be easily obtained as

\displaystyle E_{\pm}(k)=\pm\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(\hbar vk)^{2}},

(12)

where
\pm
corresponds the conduction (
+
) and valence (
-
) bands, respectively. Thus, the band gap
is given by
E_{\rm g}\equiv E_{+}(0)-E_{-}(0)=2\Delta
.

2.2
General theory of thermoelectric responses

The thermoelectric effect is typically characterized by the Seebeck coefficient,
S
, which is defined as the voltage induced by
a finite temperature gradient along a given direction (herein the
x
-direction) under the condition that there is no electrical current
(
i.e.
,
J=0
) along that direction. This can be written as

\displaystyle S\equiv-\left(\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T}\right)_{J=0},

(13)

where
\Delta V
is the induced voltage and
\Delta T
is the temperature difference between the two ends of the material.

In the presence of both an electric field
\mathcal{E}
and a temperature gradient
dT/dx
along the
x
-direction, the current density

J
is generally given by

\displaystyle J=L_{11}\mathcal{E}-\frac{L_{12}}{T}\frac{dT}{dx}

(14)

within the linear response regime with respect to
\mathcal{E}
and
dT/dx
. Here,
L_{11}
and
L_{12}
are
the electrical conductivity and the thermoelectrical conductivity, respectively.
The zero-current condition (
J=0
) leads to

L_{11}\mathcal{E}=\frac{L_{12}}{T}\frac{dT}{dx}
. Because the electric field and the temperature gradient can be written as

\mathcal{E}=-\Delta V/L
and
dT/dx=\Delta T/L
, respectively, for a spatially uniform system with length
L
(assumed here),

S
as defined in Eq. (
13
) can be expressed in terms of the response functions
L_{11}
and
L_{12}
as

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{T}\frac{L_{12}}{L_{11}}.

(15)

One of the figures of merit for thermoelectric materials is the power factor,
PF
, defined as

\displaystyle PF\equiv\sigma S^{2}=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\frac{L_{12}^{2}}{L_{11}}.

(16)

It should be noted that the basic quantities used for the thermoelectric responses of materials are
L_{11}
and
L_{12}
rather than
S
and
PF
.

2.3
Microscopic representation of
L_{11}
and
L_{12}

L_{11}
and
L_{12}
are expressed in terms of the
J_{\rm e}
-
J_{\rm e}
correlation function and
the
J_{\rm e}
-
J_{\rm Q}
correlation function, respectively, where
J_{\rm e}
and
J_{\rm Q}
are the electronic current and
the thermal current, respectively
[
26
,
27
]
. Microscopic justification for calculating
L_{11}
based on the
J_{\rm e}
-
J_{\rm e}
correlation function
and
L_{12}
based on the
J_{\rm e}
-
J_{\rm Q}
correlation function was first given by Kubo in 1957
[
28
]
and Luttinger in 1964
[
29
]
, respectively.
This subsection presents the explicit expressions of
L_{11}
and
L_{12}
for 1D Dirac electrons with an impurity potential
based on the thermal Green’s function formalism.

According to the linear response theory, the thermoelectrical conductivity
L_{12}
can be obtained as

\displaystyle L_{12}=-\lim_{\omega\to{0}}\frac{\chi^{\rm R}_{12}(\omega)-\chi^{\rm R}_{12}(0)}{i\omega},

(17)

\displaystyle\chi_{12}^{\rm R}(\omega)=\chi_{12}(i\omega_{\lambda})\Big|_{i\omega_{\lambda}\to\hbar\omega+i\delta},

(18)

where
\chi_{12}(i\omega_{\lambda})
is the correlation function between the electrical current
J_{\rm e}
and
the thermal current
J_{\rm Q}
, expressed as

\displaystyle\chi_{12}(i\omega_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{V}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!d\tau\left\langle T_{\tau}\{J_{\rm e}(\tau)J_{\rm Q}(0)\}\right\rangle e^{i\omega_{\lambda}\tau},

(19)

where
\beta\equiv 1/(k_{\rm B}T)
is the inverse temperature,
T_{\tau}
is the imaginary-time-ordering operator,

\langle\cdots\rangle
denotes the thermal average, and
V
is the volume of a system.

Now,
J_{\rm e}(\tau)
and
J_{\rm Q}(\tau)
for 1D Dirac electrons
with a disorder potential are needed. The electric current carried by 1D Dirac electrons is given by

\displaystyle J_{\rm e}(t)=-e\sum_{k}\Phi_{k}^{\dagger}(t)v(k)\Phi_{k}(t),

(20)

where
e(>0)
is the elementary charge and
v(k)
is a
2\times 2
velocity matrix, which is given by

\displaystyle v(k)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\partial H_{0}(k)}{\partial k}=v\sigma_{x}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&v\\
v&0\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(21)

The thermal current
J_{\rm Q}(t)
is expressed as
J_{\rm Q}(t)=J_{\rm E}(t)+\frac{\mu}{e}J_{\rm e}(t)
, where
the energy current
J_{\rm E}(t)
is defined as

\displaystyle J_{\rm E}(t)=\frac{d{\mathscr{A}}(t)}{dt}

(22)

with energy polarization

\displaystyle{\mathscr{A}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dx~\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)\left(\frac{\sin Qx}{Q}H(x)+H(x)\frac{\sin Qx}{Q}\right)\Psi(x,t).

(23)

Here,
Q
is a parameter used to control the effects of the unbounded variable
x
and should be set to zero in the final step of a calculation
[
30
]
.
Using Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), Eq. (
22
) is calculated as

\displaystyle J_{\rm E}(t)=\frac{v}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dx~\Psi^{\dagger}(x,t)\left\{\sigma_{x}H(x)+H(x)\sigma_{x}\right\}\Psi(x,t).

(24)

The derivation of Eq. (
24
) is given in Appendix
B
.
Substituting Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) into Eq. (
24
) yields

\displaystyle J_{\rm E}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\left[\Phi^{\dagger}_{k}(t)\left\{v(k)H_{0}(k)+H_{0}(k)v(k)\right\}\Phi_{k}(t)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{q}\left.\Phi^{\dagger}_{k+q}(t)\left\{v(k+q)U(q)+U(q)v(k)\right\}\Phi_{k}(t)\right],

(25)

where
H_{0}(k)
is the Hamiltonian density of 1D free Dirac electrons in Eq. (
11
).

In the imaginary-time Heisenberg picture (
t\to-i\tau
), the electric current is expressed as

\displaystyle J_{\rm e}(\tau)=-e\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dx~{\bar{\Phi}}_{k}(\tau)v(k)\Phi_{k}(\tau),

(26)

where
{\bar{\Phi}}_{k}(\tau)\equiv({\bar{c}}_{1k}(\tau),{\bar{c}}_{2k}(\tau))=(e^{\tau{H}}c_{1k}^{\dagger}{e}^{-\tau{H}},e^{\tau{H}}c_{2k}^{\dagger}{e}^{-\tau{H}})
.
Similarly, the energy current is given by

\displaystyle J_{\rm E}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2e}\sum_{k}\left[\left\{{\bar{\Phi}}_{k}(\tau)H_{0}(k)+\sum_{q}{\bar{\Phi}}_{k+q}(\tau)U(q)\right\}J_{\rm e}\Phi_{k}(\tau)\right.

(27)

\displaystyle+\left.{\bar{\Phi}}_{k}(\tau)J_{\rm e}\left\{H_{0}(k)\Phi_{k}(\tau)+\sum_{q}U(q)\Phi_{k-q}(\tau)\right\}\right],

where the current matrix
J_{\rm e}=-ev\sigma_{x}
. As first noted by Johnson and Mahan
[
31
]
, Eq. (
27
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle J_{\rm E}(\tau)=-\frac{1}{2e}\sum_{k}\left[\frac{d{\bar{\Phi}}_{k}(\tau)}{d\tau}J_{\rm e}\Phi_{k}(\tau)-{\bar{\Phi}_{k}}(\tau)J_{\rm e}\frac{d\Phi_{k}(\tau)}{d\tau}\right]

(28)

by using the relations

\displaystyle\frac{d\Phi_{k}(\tau)}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-H_{0}(k)\Phi_{k}(\tau)-\sum_{q}U(q)\Phi_{k-q}(\tau),

(29)

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bar{\Phi}}_{nk}(\tau)}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bar{\Phi}}_{k}(\tau)H_{0}(k)+\sum_{q}{\bar{\Phi}}_{k+q}(\tau)U(q),

(30)

which are derived from
dc_{nk}(\tau)/d\tau=[H,c_{nk}(\tau)]
and
d{\bar{c}}_{nk}(\tau)/d\tau=[H,{\bar{c}}_{nk}(\tau)]
.
Using Eq. (
28
), the correlation function
\chi_{12}(i\omega_{\lambda})
is
expressed in terms of the impurity-averaged thermal Green’s function
{\mathscr{G}}(k,i\epsilon_{n})\equiv\langle{\mathscr{G}}(k,k^{\prime},i\epsilon_{n})\rangle_{\rm imp}
as

\displaystyle\chi_{12}(i\omega_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ev^{2}}{V\beta}\sum_{n}\left(\frac{i\epsilon_{n}+i\epsilon_{n+}}{2}-\mu\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k}{\rm Tr}\left[\sigma_{x}{\mathscr{G}}(k,i\epsilon_{n}){\tilde{\sigma}}_{x}(k){\mathscr{G}}(k,i\epsilon_{n+})\right]

with
\epsilon_{n+}\equiv\epsilon_{n}+\omega_{\lambda}
and
{\tilde{\sigma}}_{x}(k)
is given as
{\tilde{\sigma}}_{x}(k)=\sigma_{x}+\sigma^{\prime}_{x}(k)
with

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{x}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime\prime}}\left\langle U(k-k^{\prime}){\mathscr{G}}(k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime},i\epsilon_{n}){\tilde{\sigma}}_{x}(k^{\prime\prime})\right.

(32)

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\times~{\mathscr{G}}(k^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime\prime},i\epsilon_{n})U(k^{\prime\prime\prime}-k)\right\rangle_{\rm imp}

that gives the vertex correction for the current operator. In the following discussion,
we assume that the vertex correction is neglected,
i.e.
,
{\tilde{\sigma}}_{x}\to\sigma_{x}
.
In Eq. (
31
),
{\mathscr{G}}(k,i\epsilon_{n})
is given by a 2
\times
2 matrix as

\displaystyle{\mathscr{G}}(k,i\epsilon_{n})=\left(\begin{matrix}{\mathscr{G}}_{11}(k,i\epsilon_{n})&{\mathscr{G}}_{12}(k,i\epsilon_{n})\\
{\mathscr{G}}_{21}(k,i\epsilon_{n})&{\mathscr{G}}_{22}(k,i\epsilon_{n})\\
\end{matrix}\right),

(33)

where
{\mathscr{G}}(k,i\epsilon_{n})
is defined by the Fourier transform of
{\mathscr{G}}_{nm}(k,\tau)\equiv-\left\langle{T_{\tau}\{c_{nk}(\tau){\bar{c}}_{mk}(0)}\}\right\rangle
,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{G}}_{nm}(k,i\epsilon_{n})=\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!d\tau~{\mathscr{G}}_{nm}(k,\tau)e^{i\epsilon_{n}\tau}.

(34)

The summation over
n
in Eq. (
31
) can be transformed into a contour integral in the complex energy space. Taking the limit of
\omega\to{0}
after the analytic continuation
i\omega_{\lambda}\to\hbar\omega+i\delta
yields
the expression of
L_{12}
as

\displaystyle L_{12}=-\frac{1}{e}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dE\left(-\frac{\partial f(E-\mu)}{\partial E}\right)(E-\mu)\alpha(E),

(35)

where
f(E)=[1+\exp\{(E-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T\}]^{-1}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function and

\alpha(E)
is often called the spectral conductivity, which is expressed as

\displaystyle\alpha(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar e^{2}v^{2}}{2\pi V}\sum_{k}{\rm Tr}\left[\sigma_{x}G^{\rm A}(k,E){\sigma}_{x}G^{\rm R}(k,E)\right.

(36)

\displaystyle\left.-{\rm Re}\left\{\sigma_{x}G^{\rm R}(k,E){\sigma}_{x}G^{\rm R}(k,E)\right\}\right]

by use of the retarded/advanced Green’s function,

\displaystyle G^{\rm R/A}(E,k)=\left\{EI-H_{0}(k)-\Sigma^{\rm R/A}(E,k)\right\}^{-1},

(37)

where
I
is the 2
\times
2 identity matrix. The expression of
L_{12}
in Eq. (
35
)
was first proposed for general cases by Sommerfeld and Bethe
[
32
]
in 1933, and subsequently by
Mott and Jones
[
33
]
and by Wilson
[
34
]
based on Boltzmann transport theory.
Recently, Ogata and Fukuyama clarified the range of validity of the Sommerfeld and Bethe relation expressed as Eq. (
35
)
for single-band systems with a disorder potential, electron-phonon coupling, and electron correlations as well as for
multi-band disorder systems based on the Lüttinger-Kohn representation
[
30
]
.

Using the expression of
\alpha(E)
in Eq. (
36
), the electrical conductivity
L_{11}
of 1D Dirac electrons in a disorder potential
is given by

\displaystyle L_{11}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dE\left(-\frac{\partial f(E-\mu)}{\partial E}\right)\alpha(E).

(38)

3
Electronic States and Thermoelectric Responses of 1D Dirac Electrons

In this section, the thermoelectric effects of 1D Dirac electrons in a disorder potential are studied based on the thermal Green’s function formalism
using the self-energy corrections of Green’s functions. The following subsections present two methods for treating self-energy
correction: the constant-
\tau
approximation and the self-consistent Born approximation.

3.1
Constant-
\tau
approximation

As the simplest treatment of self-energy correction for a disorder potential, the constant-
\tau
approximation (
i.e.
,

\Sigma^{{\rm R}/{\rm A}}=\mp{i}(\hbar/2\tau)I
) is employed here and vertex correction is neglected.
Here,
\tau
is the relaxation time of a Dirac electron scattered by a disorder potential, which is assumed to be independent of the energy
E
and the wavenumber
k
.
Although this constant-
\tau
approximation is simple, the approximation is very useful for obtaining an overview of the thermoelectric response of
1D Dirac electrons in a disorder potential.

3.1.1
Density of states and spectral conductivity

In the constant-
\tau
approximation, the retarded and advanced Green’s functions
for a 1D Dirac electron are given by

\displaystyle G^{{\rm R}/{\rm A}}(E,k)=\left\{\left(E\pm i\frac{\hbar}{2\tau}\right)I-(\hbar vk\sigma_{x}+\Delta\sigma_{z})\right\}^{-1},

(39)

which leads to the following expressions for the density of states (DOS)

per unit cell of the system and

\displaystyle\rho(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\pi N}{\rm Tr}\sum_{k}{\rm Im}~G^{{\rm R}}(k,E)

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{\pi(\hbar v)^{2}}{\rm Im}\left(\frac{\gamma+iE}{k_{+}}\right)

(41)

and

\displaystyle\alpha(E)=i\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\frac{1}{\pi A}\frac{1}{k_{+}-k_{-}}\left\{1-\frac{1}{k_{+}k_{-}}\frac{E^{2}+\gamma^{2}-\Delta^{2}}{(\hbar v)^{2}}\right\},

(42)

with

. Here,

is the total number of unit cells,

is the length of a unit cell,

is the cross-sectional area of the system,
and

\displaystyle k_{\pm}^{2}\equiv\frac{(E\pm i\gamma)^{2}-\Delta^{2}}{(\hbar v)^{2}},\quad{\rm Im}~k_{\pm}>0.

(43)

For an SWCNT,

,

3.2
Self-consistent Born approximation

In this subsection, another refined approximation,
i.e.
, the self-consistent Born approximation (SCBA), is adopted for self-energy corrections
due to a disorder potential. For the disorder potential, a short-range random impurity potential is considered. It
includes the following two types of scatterers,

\displaystyle U(x)=U_{1}a\sum_{\langle{j}\rangle}^{N_{1}}\delta(x-x_{j})+U_{2}a\sum_{\langle{l}\rangle}^{N_{2}}\delta(x-x_{l}),

(44)

where
x_{j}
and
x_{l}
represent the locations of different types of impurity, such as n-type and p-type impurities,
and
\langle{j}\rangle
and
\langle{l}\rangle
denote the sums of different impurity sites (
i.e.
,
j\neq l
).
The potential strengths
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle U_{1}\equiv\left(\begin{matrix}u_{11}&0\\
0&0\\
\end{matrix}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad U_{2}\equiv\left(\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&u_{22}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

(45)

When the potential is expressed as Eq. (
45
), the vertex correction vanishes, as shown in Appendix
C
.
In this case, the impurity potential in the momentum space is given by

\displaystyle U(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dx~e^{-iqx}U(x)

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{U_{1}}{N}\sum_{\langle{j}\rangle}e^{-iqx_{j}}+\frac{U_{2}}{N}\sum_{\langle{l}\rangle}e^{-iqx_{l}},

(47)

where
N
is the total number of unit cells in the system.

Figure 6:
Self-consistent Born approximation for the retarded self-energy
\Sigma^{\rm R}_{ii}(E)
(
i=1,2

an arrow denote the impurity sites, impurity potential, and

In the SCBA for the short-range potential in Eq. (
47
)
with Eq. (
45
), the retarded self-energy matrix is given in a diagonal and
k
-independent form as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}(E)=\left(\begin{matrix}\Sigma^{\rm R}_{11}(E)&0\\
0&\Sigma^{\rm R}_{22}(E)\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad{\rm Im}\Sigma^{\rm R}_{jj}(E)<0

(48)

and the self-consistency equations are

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{11}(E)=c_{1}u_{11}+c_{1}u_{11}^{2}X_{11}(E)

(49)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{22}(E)=c_{2}u_{22}+c_{2}u_{22}^{2}X_{22}(E)

(50)

with
X_{jj}(E)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}G_{jj}^{\rm R}(k,E)
(
j=1
,
2
). In Eqs. (
49
) and
(
50
),
c_{1}\equiv N_{1}/N
and
c_{2}\equiv N_{2}/N
are the concentrations of impurities (
i.e.
, the impurity
density per unit cell) with potential strengths
u_{11}
and
u_{22}
, respectively (see Fig.
6
).
Moreover,
X_{11}(E)
and
X_{22}(E)
can be analytically calculated for the 1D Dirac electrons as

\displaystyle X_{11}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\frac{a}{2\hbar v}\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}},

(51)

\displaystyle X_{22}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\frac{a}{2\hbar v}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}}

(52)

with
\kappa_{1}\equiv(E-\Delta-\Sigma_{11}^{\rm R}(E))/\hbar v
,
\kappa_{2}\equiv(E+\Delta-\Sigma_{22}^{\rm R}(E))/\hbar v
,
and
{\rm Im}\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}>0
. Thus, the self-consistent equations for 1D Dirac electrons are given by

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{11}(E)-c_{1}u_{11}=-i\frac{\hbar}{2\tau_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}},

(53)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{22}(E)-c_{2}u_{22}=-i\frac{\hbar}{2\tau_{2}}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}},

(54)

where
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are the relaxation times related to
u_{11}
and
u_{22}
in the limit
|E|\to\infty
,
which are defined as
\tau_{1}\equiv\frac{\hbar^{2}v}{c_{1}u_{11}^{2}a}
and
\tau_{2}\equiv\frac{\hbar^{2}v}{c_{2}u_{22}^{2}a}
,
respectively.

3.3
Low-temperature behavior of Seebeck coefficient

In this section, we discuss the low-temperature behavior of
S
for SWCNTs within SCBA.
Since a clear band gap exists, as shown in Fig.
9
, the spectral conductivity
\alpha(E)
can be divided into two parts as

\displaystyle\alpha(E)=\alpha_{\rm e}(E)\theta(E-E_{\rm c})+\alpha_{\rm h}(E)\theta(E-E_{\rm v}),

(60)

where
\alpha_{\rm e(h)}(E)
is the conduction-electron (valence-hole) spectral conductivity and
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function.
For this case, the Seebeck coefficient
S
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle S(E)=\frac{1}{T}\frac{L_{12}^{\rm e}+L_{12}^{\rm h}}{L_{11}^{\rm e}+L_{11}^{\rm h}}=\frac{L_{11}^{\rm e}S^{\rm e}+L_{11}^{\rm h}S^{\rm h}}{L_{11}^{\rm e}+L_{11}^{\rm h}}

(61)

with

\displaystyle L_{11}^{\rm e}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{E_{\rm c}}^{\infty}\!\!\!dE\left(-\frac{\partial f(E-\mu)}{\partial E}\right)\alpha_{\rm e}(E)

(62)

\displaystyle L_{11}^{\rm h}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{E_{\rm v}}\!\!\!dE\left(-\frac{\partial f(E-\mu)}{\partial E}\right)\alpha_{\rm h}(E)

(63)

\displaystyle L_{12}^{\rm e}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{e}\int_{E_{\rm c}}^{\infty}\!\!\!dE\left(-\frac{\partial f(E-\mu)}{\partial E}\right)(E-\mu)\alpha_{\rm e}(E)

(64)

\displaystyle L_{12}^{\rm h}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{e}\int_{-\infty}^{E_{\rm v}}\!\!\!dE\left(-\frac{\partial f(E-\mu)}{\partial E}\right)(E-\mu)\alpha_{\rm h}(E)

(65)

and
S^{\rm e(h)}\equiv L_{12}^{\rm e(h)}/TL_{11}^{\rm e(h)}
. Here, the superscripts e and h represent electrons and holes, respectively.
Note that although Eq. (
61
) is formally the same as
the TIB model for the Seebeck coefficient based on the Boltzmann transport theory, Eqs. (
63
)-(
65
) include the effects of
inter-band scattering between the conduction and valence bands, which is not taken into account in TIB model.

The temperature dependence of the Seebeck coefficient for a symmetric case satisfying
E_{\rm v}=-E_{\rm c}=\Delta
is now discussed.
For the case of
\mu>E_{\rm c}
and
2\Delta\gg k_{\rm B}T
,
L_{11}^{\rm h}
and
L_{12}^{\rm h}
can be neglected and

S\approx S^{\rm e}
.
For this case,
S^{\rm e}
in the low-temperature limit can be easily obtained as

\displaystyle S^{\rm e}\approx-\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\rm B}^{2}T}{3e}\left(\frac{d\ln\alpha_{\rm e}(E)}{dE}\right)_{E=\mu},\quad(\mu>E_{\rm c})

(66)

by performing the Sommerfeld expansion of Eqs. (
63
)-(
65
).
The
T
-linear behavior of
S
, known as Mott’s formula
[
39
]
, can be seen for the (10,0) SWCNTs
when
\mu
is larger than
E_{\rm c}=0.30
eV, as shown in Fig.
13
. The
T
-linear region becomes smaller as
\mu

approaches the mobility edge
E_{\rm c}
.

(Color online) Temperature dependence of the Seebeck coefficient of (10,0) SWCNTs with
\tau=10
fs for

\mu=0.28
eV (black curve),

5
Conclusion

The present study based on 1D Dirac electrons has developed a theoretical framework of bipolar thermoelectric effects in SWCNT described as 1d Dirac electrons under disorder.
Based on the thermal Green’s functions, effects of disorder have been treated within self-consistent Born approximation (SCBA), which is the simplest
version of coherent potential approximation (CPA). The results has led to prediction of characteristic behaviors of Seebeck coefficient and power factor
of semiconducting SWCNT, including the sign change of Seebeck coefficient as a function of chemical potential (gate voltage) as observed in recent experiments
[
12
,
13
]
. It is to be noted that the effects of Anderson localization, which will play important roles at low temperatures, are totally ignored in the present study since our interest here in mainly at elevated temperatures,
e.g.
, room temperature.
We have also studied the crossover from
T
-linear to
T
-inverse behavior of the Seebeck coefficient
of semiconducting SWCNTs at low
T
when the chemical potential changes from
\mu>E_{\rm c}
to
\mu<E_{\rm c}
.
The

behavior for
\mu<E_{\rm c}
, which is commonly seen in text books
[
40
,
41
]
, should be taken with care
for the temperature range of its observability because the present linear response theory breaks down in the limit of
T\to{0}

[
42
]
,
which needs separate and detailed studies.
