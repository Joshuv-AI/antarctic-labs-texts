Interpolation theorems for conjugations and applications
Zouheir Amara

Abstract
. Let ℋ \mathcal{H} be a separable complex Hilbert space. A conjugate-linear map C : ℋ → ℋ C:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H} is called a conjugation if it is an involutive isometry. In this paper, we focus on the following interpolation problems: Let { x i } i ∈ I \{x_{i}\}_{i\in I} and { y i } i ∈ I \{y_{i}\}_{i\in I} be orthonormal sets of vectors in ℋ \mathcal{H} , and let { N k } k ∈ K \{N_{k}\}_{k\in K} be a set of mutually commuting normal operators. We seek to determine under which conditions there exists a conjugation C C on ℋ \mathcal{H} such that (a) C ​ x i = y i Cx_{i}=y_{i} and C ​ N k ​ C = N k ∗ CN_{k}C=N_{k}^{*} for all i ∈ I i\in I and k ∈ K k\in K ; or (b) C ​ x i = y i Cx_{i}=y_{i} and C ​ N k ​ C = − N k ∗ CN_{k}C=-N_{k}^{*} for all i ∈ I i\in I and k ∈ K k\in K . We provide complete answers to problems (a) and (b) using the spectral projections of normal operators. Our results are then applied to the study of complex symmetric and skew symmetric operators, as well as to the characterization of hyperinvariant subspaces of normal operators through conjugations.

1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
are separable complex Hilbert spaces. We use the notation
\langle.,.\rangle
for the inner product of both
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
. The algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
, and the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
I_{\mathcal{H}}
. The range and the null space of an operator
T
are denoted by
{\rm ran}(T)
and
{\rm ker}(T)
, respectively. All direct sums in this paper are orthogonal. The sets of non-negative integers and positive integers are denoted, as customary, by
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, respectively. Throughout the paper,
\mathcal{N}=\{N_{k}\}_{k\in K}
and
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{k}\}_{k\in K}
are two sets of normal operators on
\mathcal{H}
such that each two operators in their union are commuting, and
X=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
Y=\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
will always denote two orthonormal sets of non-zero vectors in
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A
conjugation

C
on
\mathcal{H}
is a conjugate-linear map
C:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
that satisfies

(i)

C^{2}=I_{\mathcal{H}}
;

(ii)

\langle Ch,Ck\rangle=\langle k,h\rangle
for all
h,k\in\mathcal{H}
.

The simplest example of a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
is the map given by

C\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}e_{i}\right)=\overline{\lambda_{i}}e_{i}

where
\{e_{i}\}_{i}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. In fact, according to
[
14
, Lemma 1]
, any conjugation can be represented in this manner with respect to some orthonormal basis.

Conjugations have been the subject of intensive study in recent years
[
6
,
7
,
12
,
13
,
21
,
22
]
, with origins in physics. The composition
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
, of the
parity
operator
[\mathcal{P}f](x)=f(-x)
and the
time-reversal
operator
[\mathcal{T}f](x)=\overline{f(x)}
defines a conjugation on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which holds significant importance in the theory of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetric quantum theory (see, for instance,
[
3
,
4
]
). For more historical comments about conjugations, we refer the interested reader to
[
6
,
27
]
and references therein. Recent investigations into conjugations have largely stemmed from their connections to complex-symmetric and skew-symmetric operators.

An operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
is called
complex-symmetric
(resp.
skew-symmetric
) if there exists a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CTC=T^{*}
(resp.
CTC=-T^{*}
); in this case, we say more precisely that
T
is
C
-symmetric
(resp.
C
-skew symmetric
). An equivalent definition is that
T
has a symmetric (resp. skew-symmetric) matrix
M
with respect to some orthonormal basis; i.e.,
M^{\rm tr}=M
(resp.
M^{\rm tr}=-M
) where
{\rm tr}
stands for the transpose (
[
15
,
18
]
). Denote by
{\rm CSO}
and
{\rm SSO}
the classes of complex symmetric and skew symmetric operators on
\mathcal{H}
, respectively. These classes of linear operators have received significant attention in the last two decades. Applications of complex symmetric operators and skew-symmetric matrices are found in various areas (see, for instance,
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
25
]
and
[
8
,
23
,
24
]
).

Given two vectors
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
, we denote by
x\otimes y
the operator defined by
(x\otimes y)h=\langle h,y\rangle x
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
. It is easy to see that if
C
is a conjugation, then

(1.1)

C(x\otimes y)C=(Cx)\otimes(Cy)\quad\mbox{for all }x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

In
[
19
,
20
,
27
,
30
]
, the authors investigated various interpolation problems for conjugations. For instance, in
[
19
]
, Liu et al. established that for two orthogonal projections
P
and
Q
in
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
, a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
exists with
CPC=Q
if and only if
{\rm ran}(P)
and
{\rm ran}(Q)
are in symmetric position; i.e.,

\dim\big({\rm ran}(P)^{\perp}\cap{\rm ran}(Q)\big)=\dim\big({\rm ran}(P)\cap{\rm ran}(Q)^{\perp}\big).

The main motivation for our results in Section
2
stems from the study of complex symmetric operators. It should be mentioned that the class
{\rm CSO}
is not stable under the addition. Indeed, it is well-known that every normal operator
N\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
is complex symmetric. However, even its sum with the simplest complex symmetric operator may fail to be in
{\rm CSO}
(see
[
1
, Theorem 2.4]
). The main results in Section
2
can be used to study complex symmetry of
N+T
for a dense class of operators
T
in
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
. To elaborate, Zhu and Li proved in
[
30
]
that operators
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
of the form

(1.2)

T=\sum_{j\in J}r_{j}e_{j}\otimes f_{j},

where
\{r_{j}\}_{j\in J}
are distinct positive scalars and
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}
and
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in J}
are orthonormal subsets of
\mathcal{H}
, form a dense class in
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
. Moreover, they showed that such an operator is complex symmetric if and only if there exist unimodular scalars
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
j\in J
, such that

(1.3)

\lambda_{i}\langle e_{i},f_{j}\rangle=\lambda_{j}\langle e_{j},f_{i}\rangle\quad\mbox{for all }i,j\in J.

Corollary
2.7
provides geometric conditions for the existence of a conjugation
C
such that
N_{k}+\lambda T
is
C
-symmetric for all
k\in K
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. When
N_{k}=0
for every
k\in K
, we recover the result in (
1.3
) with ease. Furthermore, our results extend a recent finding by Mashreghi et al.
[
21
]
on hyperinvariant subspaces of unitary operators (see Proposition
2.10
).

In Section
3
, we establish necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a conjugation
C
such that
CN_{k}C=-N_{k}^{*}
and
Cx_{i}=y_{i}
for all
k\in K
and
i\in I
. These results are used to characterize skew symmetry of operators of the form

(1.4)

N+\sum_{j\in J}r_{j}\left(e_{j}\otimes e_{j}-f_{j}\otimes f_{j}\right),

where
N\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
is normal,
\{r_{j}\}_{j\in J}
are distinct positive scalars and
\{e_{j},f_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is an orthonormal subset. Notably, skew symmetric operators of the form (
1.4
) are dense in
{\rm SSO}
(see Section
3
).

To prove the main results of Section
3
, we will uncover certain facts about skew symmetric normal operators. We will show that
\{N_{k}\}_{k\in K}
are simultaneously skew-symmetric (i.e., there exists a conjugation
C
such that
CN_{k}C=-N_{k}^{*}
for every
k\in K
) if and only if there exists a unitary operator
U\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
such that
UN_{k}U^{*}=-N_{k}
for every
k\in K
. Furthermore, for a normal operator
N\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
, we provide a condition on the multiplicity of
N
under which skew symmetry of
N\oplus T
implies that one of
T
, where
T
is an arbitrary operator.

2.
Interpolation theorems for conjugations :
C
-symmetric versions

Given a subset
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{k}\}_{k\in K}\subset{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
of mutually commuting normal operators and a subset of vectors
Z\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, we denote

L_{\mathcal{A},Z}=\bigvee\big\{\prod_{k\in K_{0}}A_{k}^{n_{k}}A_{k}^{m_{k}*}h:K_{0}\subseteq K\mbox{ is finite, }n_{k},m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\mbox{ and }h\in Z\big\}

where
\vee
denotes the closed linear span. By the Fuglede Theorem, we also have
A_{k}A_{l}^{*}=A_{l}^{*}A_{k}
for any
k,l\in K
. Hence, one can see that
L_{\mathcal{A},Z}
is a reducing subspace of each
A_{k}
.

For a normal operator
N
, we denote by
E_{N}
the associated spectral measure defined on Borel subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. The following theorem is central to proving our main results.

2.1.
Main results

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that
\mathcal{H}=L_{\mathcal{N}\cup\mathcal{M},X\cup Y}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CN_{k}C=M_{k}^{*}
and
Cx_{i}=y_{i}
for all
k\in K
and
i\in I
.

(ii)

For all finite subsets
K_{0},K_{1}\subseteq K
, Borel subsets
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k\in K_{0}}
and
\{D_{k}\}_{k\in K_{1}}
of
\mathbb{C}
, and
i,j\in I
, we have

\left\langle\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{N_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)x_{i},\left(\prod_{l\in K_{1}}E_{M_{l}}(D_{l})\right)x_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(\prod_{l\in K_{1}}E_{N_{l}}(D_{l})\right)y_{j},\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{M_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)y_{i}\right\rangle

and

\left\langle\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{N_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)x_{i},\left(\prod_{l\in K_{1}}E_{M_{l}}(D_{l})\right)y_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(\prod_{l\in K_{1}}E_{N_{l}}(D_{l})\right)x_{j},\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{M_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)y_{i}\right\rangle.

Remark 2.2
.

Recall that an
anti-automorphism

\rho:{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})\to{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
is a vector space automorphism satisfying
\rho(T^{*})=\rho(T)^{*}
and
\rho(TS)=\rho(S)\rho(T)
for all
T,S\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
. It is well-known that each anti-automorphism
\rho:{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})\to{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
, of order two (i.e.,
\rho^{2}=I_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})}
), has the form
\rho(T)=CT^{*}C
for every
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
, where
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
. In the light of this fact, it is not hard to see that Theorem
2.1
can be used to obtain conditions under which there exists an anti-automorphism
\rho:{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})\to{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
of order two such that

\rho(T)=T\quad\mbox{and}\quad\rho(N_{k})=M_{k}\;\mbox{for every }k\in K,

where
T
is an operator of the form (
1.2
).

For a subspace
M
of
\mathcal{H}
, denote by
P_{M}\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto
M
. The following corollary is a direct consequence of Theorem
2.1
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Assume that
\mathcal{H}=\vee\{x_{i},y_{i}:i\in I\}
, and let
M
and
N
be subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
such that
P_{M}
and
P_{N}
commute. The following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CM=N
and
Cx_{i}=y_{i}
for every
i\in I
.

(ii)

For all
i,j\in I
and
r,s\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\left\langle P_{N}^{r}P_{M}^{s}x_{i},x_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{N}^{s}P_{M}^{r}y_{j},y_{i}\right\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left\langle P_{N}^{r}P_{M}^{s}x_{i},y_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{N}^{s}P_{M}^{r}x_{j},y_{i}\right\rangle.

Recall that a linear (or conjugate-linear) operator

on
\mathcal{H}
is called

if the restriction of

is an isometry.

If
N_{k}=M_{k}
for every

becomes:

Theorem 2.4
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a conjugation

CN_{k}C=N_{k}^{*}

Corollary 2.7

Let

(i)

\mathcal{H}

(ii)

\lambda_{i}\left\langle\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{N_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)x_{i},x_{j}\right\rangle=\lambda_{j}\left\langle\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{N_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)y_{j},y_{i}\right\rangle

\lambda_{i}\left\langle\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{N_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)x_{i},y_{j}\right\rangle=\lambda_{j}\left\langle\left(\prod_{k\in K_{0}}E_{N_{k}}(\Delta_{k})\right)x_{j},y_{i}\right\rangle

Proof.

(ii)
\implies
(i). For every

. Moreover, since

, and consequently,

.

(i)
\implies

Let

\mathcal{H}

(i)

is hyperinvariant for

(ii)

(i)
\implies

, and let

.

(ii)
\implies
(iii) If

\mathcal{H}
such that

, and since

, it follows that

, which leads to the desired containment.

(iii)
\implies
(i). We need to show that for each

, the commutant of
N
,

. Additionally, since

is a von Neumann algebra, it is spanned by its unitary operators (

Given a conjugation

Another consequence of Theorem

is the following result, which characterizes normal operators in terms of complex symmetric and conjugate-normal ones.

Let

(ii)

Proof.

The implication (i)
\implies

(ii)

(iii)

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\{N_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\subset{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
and
\{M_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
be two finite sets of mutually commuting normal operators, and let
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
and
h,k\in\mathcal{K}
be vectors such that

\left\langle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle=\left\langle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle\quad\mbox{for every Borel subset $\Delta_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$}.

Then, for every
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\left\langle p(N_{1},N_{1}^{*},\dots,N_{n},N_{n}^{*})x,y\right\rangle=\left\langle p(M_{1},M_{1}^{*},\dots,M_{n},M_{n}^{*})h,k\right\rangle.

Proof.

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2n}(\mathbb{C})
. By the commutativity assumption, we may suppose that
p(\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\lambda_{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{r_{i}}\lambda_{i}^{s_{i}}
where
r_{i},s_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, put
f_{i}(z)=z^{r_{i}}\overline{z}^{s_{i}}\chi_{K_{i}}(z)
where
K_{i}=\sigma(N_{i})\cup\sigma(M_{i})
and
\chi_{K_{i}}
is the characteristic function of
K_{i}
. Then, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
f_{i}
is a bounded Borel function on
\mathbb{C}
. Additionally,

(2.1)

f_{i}(N_{i})=N_{i}^{r_{i}}N_{i}^{*s_{i}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad f_{i}(M_{i})=M_{i}^{r_{i}}M_{i}^{*s_{i}}\quad\mbox{for every }i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Fix Borel subsets
\{\Delta_{i}\}_{2\leq i\leq n}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and denote by
\mu_{1}
and
\nu_{1}
the complex-valued measures on Borel subsets of
\mathbb{C}
given by

\mu_{1}(\Delta)=\left\langle E_{N_{1}}(\Delta)\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nu_{1}(\Delta)=\left\langle E_{M_{1}}(\Delta)\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle.

Then,

\left\langle N_{1}^{r_{1}}N_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle=\left\langle f_{1}(N_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle=\int f_{1}{\,\rm d}\mu_{1}

and

\left\langle M_{1}^{r_{1}}M_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle=\left\langle f_{1}(M_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle=\int f_{1}{\,\rm d}\nu_{1}.

As the measures
\mu_{1}
and
\nu_{1}
are presumed equal by hypothesis, we deduce

\left\langle N_{1}^{r_{1}}N_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle=\left\langle M_{1}^{r_{1}}M_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle.

By the Fuglede Theorem, we obtain
\mu_{2}(\Delta_{2})=\nu_{2}(\Delta_{2})
where

\mu_{2}(\Delta_{2}):=\left\langle E_{N_{2}}(\Delta_{2})N_{1}^{r_{1}}N_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle

and

\nu_{2}(\Delta_{2}):=\left\langle E_{M_{2}}(\Delta_{2})M_{1}^{r_{1}}M_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle.

Since
\Delta_{2}
was arbitrary, the complex-valued measures
\mu_{2}
and
\nu_{2}
are equal. Integrating
f_{2}
with respect to these measures and taking into account (
2.1
), we obtain

\left\langle N_{2}^{r_{2}}N_{2}^{*s_{2}}N_{1}^{r_{1}}N_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\left(E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)x,y\right\rangle=\left\langle M_{2}^{r_{2}}M_{2}^{*s_{2}}M_{1}^{r_{1}}M_{1}^{*s_{1}}\prod_{i=3}^{n}\left(E_{M_{i}}(\Delta_{i})\right)h,k\right\rangle.

The desired equality will follow by continuing the previous process.
∎

The following lemma will be very useful throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
N\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
M\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
be normal operators, and let
T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
be a bounded linear (or conjugate-linear) operator. The following statements hold:

(i)

If
T
is linear (resp. conjugate-linear) and
TN=MT
(resp.
TN=M^{*}T
), then

TE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{M}(\Delta)T\quad\mbox{for every Borel subset }\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

(ii)

If
T
is linear (resp. conjugate-linear) and
TN=-MT
(resp.
TN=-M^{*}T
), then

TE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{M}(-\Delta)T\quad\mbox{for every Borel subset }\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

(i) Suppose first that
T
is linear and
TN=MT
, and put

L=\begin{bmatrix}N&0\\
0&M\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{H}\\
\mathcal{K}\end{array}\quad\mbox{and}\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
T&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{H}\\
\mathcal{K}\end{array}.

Then,
L
and
A
are bounded linear operators acting on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{K}
. Furthermore, one can verify that
AL=LA
. Since
L
is normal, by the Fuglede Theorem,
AE_{L}(\Delta)=E_{L}(\Delta)A
for every Borel subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
.

Fix a Borel subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, and note that

E_{L}(\Delta)=\begin{bmatrix}E_{N}(\Delta)&0\\
0&E_{M}(\Delta)\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{H}\\
\mathcal{K}\end{array}.

Therefore, the matrix equality
AE_{L}(\Delta)=E_{L}(\Delta)A
implies
TE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{M}(\Delta)T
.

Assume now that
T
is a conjugate-linear operator such that
TN=M^{*}T
, and let
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a Borel subset. Since
M
is normal, there exists a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{K}
such that
CMC=M^{*}
. Hence,
TN=M^{*}T=(CMC)T
, and since
C^{-1}=C
, we obtain

(2.3)

(CT)N=M(CT).

Clearly,
CT:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
is a bounded linear operator. By our previous result, we have

(2.4)

CTE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{M}(\Delta)CT.

By
[
9
, p. 287, Lemma 8.7]
,
E_{M}(\Delta)=\chi_{\Delta}(M)\in W^{*}(M)
, the von Neumann algebra generated by
M
. Hence, there exists a net
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
p_{i}(M,M^{*})
converges to
E_{M}(\Delta)
in the weak operator topology. One can easily check that
Cp_{i}(M,M^{*})C=p_{i}(M,M^{*})^{*}
for any
i
. Consequently, taking the limit in the weak operator topology, we get

CE_{M}(\Delta)C=E_{M}(\Delta)^{*}=E_{M}(\Delta).

Therefore, from (
2.4
), we obtain

TE_{N}(\Delta)=C^{2}TE_{N}(\Delta)=CE_{M}(\Delta)CT=E_{M}(\Delta)T,

the desired equality.

(ii) Suppose that
TN=-MT
if
T
is linear and that
TN=-M^{*}T
if
T
is conjugate-linear. Applying the first part of lemma to
N
and
-M
, we get
TE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{-M}(\Delta)T
for every Borel subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Since
E_{-M}(\Delta)=\chi_{\Delta}(-M)
and
\chi_{\Delta}(-z)=\chi_{-\Delta}(z)
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

TE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{-M}(\Delta)T=\chi_{\Delta}(-M)T=\chi_{-\Delta}(M)T=E_{M}(-\Delta)T.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Interpolation theorems for conjugations : C-skew symmetric versions

A conjugate-linear operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
is said to be

if it is a surjective isometry. Clearly, every conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
is anti-unitary.

For two operators

and

on Hilbert spaces, we denote

if there exists an isometric isomorphism

between the underlying Hilbert spaces such that
UAU^{-1}=B
; or equivalently,

. It is worth mentioning that both complex symmetry and skew symmetry are preserved under the equivalence relation

For

, where

, let

denote the direct sum of
\mathcal{H}
with itself

the direct sum of

.

3.1.
Main results

Theorem 3.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

on
\mathcal{H}

Before stating the second main result of this section, we recall some facts about normal operators on separable complex Hilbert spaces. Given a finite measure with compact support
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
, denote by
M_{\mu}
the operator defined on

by
[M_{\mu}f](z)=zf(z)
for all

. According to spectral multiplicity theory (see, for instance,
[
9
, p. 298, Theorem 10.16]
), each normal operator

acting on

is unitarily equivalent to

(3.1)

M_{\mu_{\infty}}^{(\infty)}\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}

where

,

(some of which may be zero), are mutually singular finite measures on Borel subsets of

, each with compact support. Moreover, the previous decomposition is unique in the sense that if

is another normal operator with corresponding measures

,

, then

if and only if, for every

,

(3.2)

\mu_{i}(\Delta)=0\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\nu_{i}(\Delta)=0\quad\mbox{for every Borel subset }\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

In

18

, Li et al. proved that

is skew-symmetric if and only if the sequence

can be chosen so that, for every

,

(3.3)

\mu_{i}(\Delta)=\mu_{i}(-\Delta)\quad\mbox{for every Borel subset }\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

Given a normal operator

, we write

if the measure

in (

) is zero; in other words,

can be omitted from the decomposition (

). This definition makes sense due to the uniqueness property described in (

).

and

are
C
-skew symmetric self-adjoint operators. Using
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
, we obtain a sequence of self-adjoint diagonalizable
C
-skew symmetric operators
A_{n}
that converges to
A
, with
\dim{\rm ker}(A_{n}-rI_{\mathcal{H}})=1
whenever
r
is an eigenvalue of
A_{n}
. Now, by skew-symmetry of
A_{n}
, we have
\dim{\rm ker}(A_{n}-rI_{\mathcal{H}})=\dim{\rm ker}(A_{n}+rI_{\mathcal{H}})
for all real scalars
r
. Consequently,
T
can be approximated by operators of the form (
3.5
).

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be an operator of the form (
3.5
) and denote by
B_{1}
the restriction of
B
to
\left(L_{\{B\},\{e_{j},f_{j}:j\in J\}}\right)^{\perp}
. Then,
T
is skew-symmetric if and only if
B_{1}\cong-B_{1}
and there exists unimodular scalars
\lambda_{j}
, for
j\in J
, such that

(3.6)

\lambda_{i}\left\langle E_{B}(\Delta)e_{i},e_{j}\right\rangle=\lambda_{j}\left\langle E_{B}(-\Delta)f_{j},f_{i}\right\rangle\;\mbox{and}\;\lambda_{i}\left\langle E_{B}(\Delta)e_{i},f_{j}\right\rangle=\lambda_{j}\left\langle E_{B}(-\Delta)e_{j},f_{i}\right\rangle

for every Borel subset
\Delta
of
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Assume that (
3.6
) holds for every Borel subset
\Delta
of
\mathbb{C}
. Since
B_{1}\cong-B_{1}
, Lemma
3.7
implies that
VB_{1}V^{-1}=-B_{1}^{*}
for some anti-unitary operator
V
. Then, applying Theorem
3.1
to the self-adjoint operator
B
and the orthonormal subsets
\{\lambda_{j}e_{j}\}_{j\in J}
and
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in J}
, we obtain a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CBC=-B
and
C(\lambda e_{j})=f_{j}
for every
j\in J
. One can check that
C(e_{j}\otimes e_{j}-f_{j}\otimes f_{j})C=-(e_{j}\otimes e_{j}-f_{j}\otimes f_{j})=-(e_{j}\otimes e_{j}-f_{j}\otimes f_{j})^{*}
for every
j\in J
. Thus,
T
is
C
-skew symmetric as the sum of two
C
-skew symmetric ones. This proves the sufficiency.

Suppose that
T
is
C
-skew symmetric for some conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
, and denote
A=\sum_{j\in J}r_{j}(e_{j}\otimes e_{j}-f_{j}\otimes f_{j})
. Then,
A
and
B
are both
C
-skew symmetric. Moreover, for every
j\in J
,
{\rm ker}(A-r_{j}I_{\mathcal{H}})=\vee\{e_{j}\}
and
{\rm ker}(A+r_{j}I_{\mathcal{H}})=\vee\{f_{i}\}
. As in (
3.4
), we have that
Ce_{j}\in{\rm span}\{f_{j}\}
for every
j\in J
. Hence, there exist unimodular scalars
\lambda_{j}
, for
j\in J
, so that
C(\lambda_{j}e_{j})=f_{j}
for every
j\in J
. The necessity follows now by combining Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.7
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

(i)

If the operator

(ii)

If

3.2
, one can make minor changes in the proof of Corollary
3.5
to show that the condition “

” can be replaced with the simpler one “

”.

.

3.2.
Proof of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2

The following lemma provides conditions under which a family of mutually commuting normal operators have a common orthonormal basis in which their corresponding matrices are skew-symmetric. This result is inspired by
[
18
, Theorem 1.10]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\{N_{k}\}_{k\in K}\subset{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be mutually commuting normal operators. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
such that

CN_{k}C=-N_{k}^{*}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$}.

(ii)

There exists an anti-unitary operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
such that

VN_{k}V^{-1}=-N_{k}^{*}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$}.

(iii)

There exists a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
such that

UN_{k}U^{*}=-N_{k}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$}.

Proof.

The implication (i)
\implies
(ii) is obvious.

(ii)
\implies
(iii). Let
V
be an anti-unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
such that
VN_{k}V^{-1}=-N_{k}^{*}
for every
k\in K
. As in (
2.10
), there exists a conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies
JN_{k}J=N_{k}^{*}
for every
k\in K
. Hence, we have
VN_{k}V^{-1}=-JN_{k}J
, and so, since
J^{-1}=J
, we get

(3.7)

(JV)N_{k}(V^{-1}J)=-J^{2}N_{k}J^{2}=-N_{k}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$}.

Put
U=JV
; then
U\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
is unitary and
U^{*}=U^{-1}=(JV)^{-1}=V^{-1}J^{-1}=V^{-1}J
. Therefore, for any
k\in K
,
UN_{k}U^{*}=-N_{k}
.

(iii)
\implies
(i). It is easy to see that we may assume without loss of generality that
K
is infinite. Let
U\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be a unitary operator satisfying
UN_{k}=-N_{k}U
for every
k\in K
. First, let us reduce the problem to the case where
\bigcap_{k\in K}{\rm ker}(N_{k})=\{0\}
. Denote this subspace by
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. One can easily see that
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
reduces
U
, and that each
N_{k}
has the form
N_{k}=M_{k}\oplus 0
with respect to the decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{\perp}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. Clearly,
\bigcap_{k\in K}{\rm ker}(M_{k})=\{0\}
; hence, if a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{\perp}
exists such that
CM_{k}C=-M_{k}^{*}
for each
k\in K
, then one can see that the map
C\oplus C_{\infty}
, where
C_{\infty}
is an arbitrary conjugation on
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
, is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies all the desired equalities.

Note that we can further assume that there exists a countable subset
\{N_{k_{p}}\}_{p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
of
\{N_{k}\}_{k\in K}
such that
\bigcap_{p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}{\rm ker}(N_{k_{p}})=\{0\}
. In fact, since
\mathcal{H}=\bigvee\left(\bigcup_{k\in K}{\rm ker}(N_{k})^{\perp}\right)
, one can use the separability of
\mathcal{H}
to show that there exists a countable subset
\{k_{p}\}_{p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\subseteq K
so that
\mathcal{H}=\bigvee\left(\bigcup_{p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}{\rm ker}(N_{k_{p}})^{\perp}\right)
.

Fix a Borel subset
\Delta_{0}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{0}=-\Delta_{0}\cup\{0\}
. By Lemma
2.15
, we have

UE_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0})=E_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0})U

and

UE_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0})=E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0})U.

Consequently,

(3.8)

U{\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0}))={\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0}))\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;U{\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0}))={\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0})).

For convenience, put
\mathcal{H}_{+}={\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0}))
,
\mathcal{H}_{-}={\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0}))
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}={\rm ran}(E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\{0\}))={\rm ker}(N_{k_{1}})
. Given that
\mathbb{C}=\Delta_{0}\cup(-\Delta_{0})\cup\{0\}
is a partition, it follows from (
3.8
) that
U
has the form

U=\begin{bmatrix}0&*&0\\
W&0&0\\
0&0&U_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{H}_{+}\\
\mathcal{H}_{-}\\
\mathcal{L}_{1}\end{array}

where
W
and
U_{1}
are isometric isomorphisms between Hilbert spaces. Additionally, since for every
k\in K
,

N_{k}E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0})=E_{N_{k_{1}}}(\Delta_{0})N_{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad N_{k}E_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0})=E_{N_{k_{1}}}(-\Delta_{0})N_{k},

we can also write

N_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}N_{k,+}&0&0\\
0&N_{k,-}&0\\
0&0&R_{k,1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{H}_{+}\\
\mathcal{H}_{-}\\
\mathcal{L}_{1}\end{array}

where
N_{k,+}
,
N_{k,-}
and
R_{k,1}
,
k\in K
, are normal operators such that the
R_{k,1}
’s are mutually commuting.

Since
UN_{k}=-N_{k}U
, simple matrix calculations show that

(3.9)

WN_{k,+}=-N_{k,-}W\quad\mbox{and}\quad U_{1}R_{k,1}=-R_{k,1}U_{1}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$}.

Put
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{+}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-}
and let
V:\mathcal{M}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{-}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-}
be the unitary operator given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}V:\mathcal{H}_{+}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-}&\to&\mathcal{H}_{-}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-}\\
(x,y)&\mapsto&(Wx,y).\end{array}

Then, it is easy to see that
V{N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{1}}V^{-1}=(-N_{k,-})\oplus N_{k,-}
for every
k\in K
. Since the normal operators
\{N_{k,-}\}_{k\in K}
are mutually commuting, as in (
2.10
), there exists a conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}_{-}
such that
JN_{k,-}J=\left(N_{k,-}\right)^{*}
for every
k\in K
. Put

J_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
J&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{H}_{-}\\
\mathcal{H}_{-}\end{array}.

It is easy to see that
J_{1}
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}_{-}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{-}
satisfying

J_{1}\left((-N_{k,-})\oplus N_{k,-}\right)J_{1}=-\left((-N_{k,-})\oplus N_{k,-}\right)^{*}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$}.

Hence,
C_{1}:=V^{-1}J_{1}V
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{M}_{1}
that satisfies
C_{1}{N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{1}}C_{1}=-\left({N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{1}}\right)^{*}
for every
k\in K
.

Since
\{R_{k,1}\}_{k\in K}
are mutually commuting and
U_{1}R_{k,1}U_{1}^{*}=-R_{k,1}
for every
k\in K
, by applying the preceding argument to
R_{k_{2},1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{L}_{1})
, we obtain subspaces
\mathcal{M}_{2}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
, with
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{M}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{2}
, a conjugation
C_{2}
on
\mathcal{M}_{2}
such that

C_{2}{N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{2}}C_{2}=C_{2}{R_{k,1}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{2}}C_{2}=-\left({R_{k,1}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{2}}\right)^{*}=-\left({N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{2}}\right)^{*}\quad\mbox{for every $k\in K$},

a unitary operator
U_{2}
on
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, and a set of mutually commuting normal operators
\{R_{k,2}\}_{k\in K}
on
\mathcal{L}_{2}
such that
{N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{L}_{2}}=R_{k,2}
and
U_{2}R_{k,2}U_{2}^{*}=-\left(R_{k,2}\right)^{*}
. This process produces a sequence
\mathcal{M}_{i}
,
i\geq 1
, of reducing subspaces of
N_{k}
, for
k\in K
, and conjugations
C_{i}
on
\mathcal{M}_{i}
such that
{N_{k}}_{|\mathcal{M}_{i}}
is
C_{i}
-skew symmetric for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
k\in K
.

For each
i\geq 1
, it is easy to see that

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathcal{M}_{j}\right)^{\perp}=\mathcal{L}_{i}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{i}{\rm ker}(N_{k_{j}}).

Hence,

\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{i}\right)^{\perp}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathcal{M}_{j}\right)^{\perp}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}{\rm ker}(N_{k_{i}})=\{0\}.

Consequently,
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{i}
; moreover, one can check that
C:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}C_{i}
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying
CN_{k}C=-N_{k}^{*}
for every
k\in K
.
∎

Note that every bounded linear operator
T
acting on a Hilbert space can be written as
T=N\oplus A
where
N
is normal and
A
is an operator that has no reducing subspace for which the restriction of
A
is normal (of course, either
N
or
A
could be absent). In fact,

N
is the restriction of
T
to the reducing subspace

(3.10)

\mathcal{H}_{\rm nor}=\bigcap_{1\leq n,m<\infty}{\rm ker}({T^{*}}^{n}T^{m}-T^{m}{T^{*}}^{n}).

The operator
N
is called the
normal part
of
T
, while
A
is called the
abnormal part
of
T
. In this case, we denote
T_{\rm nor}=N
and
T_{\rm abnor}=A
.

Using (
3.10
), one can easily see that if
M
is a normal operator, then

(3.11)

(M\oplus T)_{\rm nor}=M\oplus T_{\rm nor}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(M\oplus T)_{\rm abnor}=T_{\rm abnor}.

The following lemma is an immediate consequence of
[
29
, Proposition 3.10]
and (
3.11
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
M\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be normal and
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{K})
such that
M\oplus T
is skew-symmetric. Then,
M\oplus T_{\rm nor}
and
T_{\rm abnor}
are skew-symmetric.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
N\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be a skew-symmetric normal operator and
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
a Borel subset such
\Delta=-\Delta
. Then, the restriction of
N
to the reducing subspace
{\rm ran}(E_{N}(\Delta))
is skew-symmetric.

Proof.

Let
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CNC=-N^{*}
. By Lemma
2.15
, we have
CE_{N}(\Delta)=E_{N}(-\Delta)C=E_{N}(\Delta)C
; hence,
C{\rm ran}(E_{N}(\Delta))={\rm ran}(E_{N}(\Delta))
. Let
C_{0}
and
N_{0}
denote the restrictions of
C
and
N
to
{\rm ran}(E_{N}(\Delta))
, respectively. Then,
C_{0}
is a conjugation on
E_{N}(\Delta)
. Moreover, it follows immediately from the equality
CNC=-N^{*}
that
C_{0}N_{0}C_{0}=-N_{0}^{*}
.
∎

In
[
17
, Lemma 3.2]
and
[
2
, Corollary 3.4]
, the authors proved that if
N
is normal, then a direct sum
N\oplus T
is complex symmetric (resp. conjugate-normal) if and only if
T
is complex-symmetric (resp. conjugate-normal). However, this property does not hold for skew-symmetry even if
N
is skew-symmetric. The next result provides a complete description of normal operators
N
that satisfy this property.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
N\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be normal. The followings statements are equivalent:

(i)

For every separable complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and every
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
,

N\oplus T\mbox{ is skew-symmetric}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\mbox{ is skew-symmetric.}

(ii)

N
is skew-symmetric and
m(N_{|{\rm ker}(N)^{\perp}})<\infty
.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii). Suppose statement (i) holds. Taking
T=0
, the zero operator on any Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, we obtain that
N\oplus 0
is skew-symmetric. By
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
, it follows that
N_{|({\rm ker}N)^{\perp}}
is skew-symmetric. Therefore,
N=N_{|({\rm ker}N)^{\perp}}\oplus 0
is skew-symmetric.

For convenience, put
N_{1}=N_{|{\rm ker}(N)^{\perp}}
, and let
N_{1}\cong M_{\mu_{\infty}}^{(\infty)}\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}
be the canonical decomposition in (
3.1
) of
N_{1}
.

We need to show that
\mu_{\infty}=0
. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
\mu_{\infty}\neq 0
. Hence,
M_{\mu_{\infty}}
is not absent and is not zero due to the injectivity of
N_{1}
. Since
\sigma(M_{\mu_{\infty}})\neq\{0\}
, using the spectral theorem, we can write
M_{\mu_{\infty}}=M\oplus L
, were
L
is a normal operator with
\sigma(L)\neq-\sigma(L)
; in particular,
L\ncong-L
and thus not skew-symmetric by Lemma
3.7
.

Let us verify that
N\oplus L
is skew symmetric. We have

\displaystyle N_{1}\oplus L\cong M_{\mu_{\infty}}^{(\infty)}\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}\oplus L

\displaystyle\cong(M\oplus L)^{(\infty)}\oplus L\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\cong M^{(\infty)}\oplus L^{(\infty)}\oplus L\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\cong M^{(\infty)}\oplus L^{(\infty)}\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\cong N_{1}.

Thus,
N\oplus L\cong N
; in particular,
N\oplus L
is skew-symmetric; a contradiction. Therefore,
m(N_{1})<\infty
.

(ii)
\implies
(i). Since
N
is skew-symmetric, the reverse implication in (i) is always true. Fix a bounded linear operator
T
, assume that
N\oplus T
is skew-symmetric, and let us show that
T
is skew-symmetric.

In view of Lemma
3.8
and
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
, we can assume without loss of generality that
T
is normal and
N=N_{|{\rm ker}(N)^{\perp}}
. Moreover, by (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), we may directly assume that

N=\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{1\leq i<\infty}L^{2}(\mu_{i})^{(i)}

where the
\mu_{i}
’s are mutually singular and satisfy
\mu_{i}(\Delta)=\mu_{i}(-\Delta)
for every Borel subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Since the measures
\mu_{i}
’s are mutually singular and satisfy (
3.3
), it is elementary to see that there exist mutually disjoint Borel subsets
\{\Delta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
of
\mathbb{C}
such that for all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
i\neq j
,

(3.12)

\Delta_{i}=-\Delta_{i},\quad\mu_{i}(\Delta)=\mu_{i}(\Delta\cap\Delta_{i}),\quad\mu_{i}(\Delta_{j})=0\;\;\mbox{ for every Borel }\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

Let
\Delta_{0}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\cup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\Delta_{i}
; clearly,
\Delta_{0}=-\Delta_{0}
. For convenience, put
N_{i}=M_{\mu_{i}}^{(i)}
,
\mathcal{H}_{i}=L^{2}(\mu_{i})^{(i)}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and let
T=T_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}T_{i}
be the decomposition of
T
with respect to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{K}={\rm ran}(E_{T}(\Delta_{0}))\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}{\rm ran}(E_{T}(\Delta_{i}))
. For
0\leq i<\infty
, put
\mathcal{K}_{i}={\rm ran}(E_{T}(\Delta_{i}))
.

Since by (
3.12
),
\mu_{i}(\Delta_{0})=\mu_{i}(\Delta_{j})=0
all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
i\neq j
, it follows that
E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{0})=E_{N_{i}}(\Delta_{j})=0
. Therefore,

{\rm ran}(E_{N\oplus T}(\Delta_{0}))=\mathcal{K}_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\rm ran}(E_{N\oplus T}(\Delta_{i}))=\mathcal{H}_{i}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{i}\;\;\mbox{for every }i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

It follows that

(N\oplus T)_{|{\rm ran}(E_{N\oplus T}(\Delta_{0}))}=T_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(N\oplus T)_{|{\rm ran}(E_{N\oplus T}(\Delta_{i}))}=N_{i}\oplus T_{i}\;\;\mbox{for every }i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

Consequently, by Lemma
3.9
,
T_{0}
and
N_{i}\oplus T_{i}
, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, are skew-symmetric operators. In particular, by Lemma
3.7
, we have

(3.13)

N_{i}\oplus T_{i}\cong-N_{i}\oplus-T_{i}\quad\mbox{for every }i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

Fix
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and let us prove that
T_{i}
is skew-symmetric. Given
\mu_{i}(\Delta)=\mu_{i}(-\Delta)
for every Borel subset
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{C}
,
[
18
, Theorem 1.11]
implies that
N_{i}
is skew-symmetric; hence,
N_{i}\cong-N_{i}
by Lemma
3.7
. Combining this with (
3.13
), we obtain
N_{i}\oplus T_{i}\cong N_{i}\oplus-T_{i}
. Since
M_{\mu_{i}}
is a star-cyclic normal operator (
[
9
, p. 269, Theorem 3.4]
), applying
[
9
, p. 295, Proposition 10.6]

i
times to
M_{\mu_{i}}
, we conclude that
T_{i}\cong-T_{i}
, and so
T_{i}
is skew-symmetric by Lemma
3.7
.

Thus,
T=T_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}T_{i}
is skew-symmetric as a direct sum of skew-symmetric operators.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
N\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be normal, and suppose that there exist reducing subspaces
\{\mathcal{H}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq r}
,
r<\infty
, of
N
such that
\mathcal{H}=\vee(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{i})
and
N_{|\mathcal{H}_{i}}
is star-cyclic for each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,r\}
. Then, for every reducing subspace
L
of
N
, we have
m(N_{|L})<\infty
.

Proof.

First, let us show that
N
is a finite direct sum of star-cyclic normal operators.
We will proceed by induction on
r
. The result is clear when
r=1
. Assume the lemma holds for some integer
r\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\dots\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
be
r+1
reducing subspaces of
N
such that
\mathcal{H}=\vee(\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}\mathcal{H}_{i})
and
N_{|\mathcal{H}_{i}}
is star cyclic for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,r+1\}
. Let
\mathcal{K}=\vee(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{H}_{i})
and
P\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H})
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{K}
. Then,
\mathcal{K}
reduces
N
; moreover, by the induction hypothesis,
N_{|\mathcal{K}}
is a finite direct sum of star-cyclic operators.

By star-cyclicity of
N_{|\mathcal{H}_{r+1}}
, there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}=L_{\{N\},\{h\}}
. To complete the proof, it is clear that it suffices to show that
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}\oplus L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}
. Note that
\mathcal{K}
and
L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}
are orthogonal. In fact, since
\mathcal{K}
reduces
N
, its orthogonal projection
P
, and hence
(I-P)
, commutes with
N
and
N^{*}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}

\displaystyle=\bigvee\big\{p(N,N^{*})(I-P)h:p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\big\}

\displaystyle=(I-P)\bigvee\big\{p(N,N^{*})h:p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\big\}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\perp}.

It remains to prove that
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}+L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}
; we need only to show that
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}+L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}
. For
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

p(N,N^{*})h=p(N,N^{*})Ph+p(N,N^{*})(I-P)h.

Since
Ph\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}
reduces
p(N,N^{*})
, we have
p(N,N^{*})Ph\in\mathcal{K}
. Hence,
p(N,N^{*})h\in\mathcal{K}+L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}
, and thus
\mathcal{H}_{r+1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}+L_{\{N\},\{(I-P)h\}}
.

Now, let
L
be a reducing subspace of
N
and assume, for contradiction, that
m(N_{|L})\nless\infty
. According to (
3.1
),
N_{|L}
has a reducing subspace for which its restriction is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\mu}^{(\infty)}
for some finite non-zero compactly supported measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
. Thus, we can write
N\cong N_{0}\oplus M_{\mu}^{(\infty)}
for some normal operator
N_{0}
. This implies

N\oplus M_{\mu}^{(2)}\cong N_{0}\oplus M_{\mu}^{(\infty)}\oplus M_{\mu}^{(2)}\cong N_{0}\oplus M_{\mu}^{(\infty)}\oplus M_{\mu}\cong N\oplus M_{\mu}.

Since
N
is a direct sum of
n
star-cyclic normal operators
M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{n}
with
n<\infty
, applying
[
9
, p. 295, Proposition 10.6]
to each
M_{i}
, we conclude
M_{\mu}\cong M_{\mu}^{(2)}
; which contradicts (
3.2
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
.
