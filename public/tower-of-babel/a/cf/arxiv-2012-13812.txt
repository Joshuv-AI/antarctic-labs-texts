Abstract
. An isometric operator V V in a Pontryagin space ℌ {{\mathfrak{H}}} is called standard, if its domain and the range are nondegenerate subspaces in ℌ {{\mathfrak{H}}} . A description of coresolvents for standard isometric operators is known and basic underlying concepts that appear in the literature are unitary colligations and characteristic functions. In the present paper generalized coresolvents of non-standard Pontryagin space isometric operators are described. The methods used in this paper rely on a new general notion of boundary pairs introduced for isometric operators in a Pontryagin space setting. Even in the Hilbert space case this notion generalizes the earlier concept of boundary triples for isometric operators and offers an alternative approach to study operator valued Schur functions without any additional invertibility requirements appearing in the ordinary boundary triple approach.

Introduction

Extension theory for standard symmetric and isometric operators in Pontryagin spaces was first developed by I.S. Iokhvidov and M.G. Kreĭn in
[
30
]
, generalized
resolvents of such operators were described by M.G. Kreĭn and H. Langer in
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Following
[
28
]
we will use the notion
standard
for an isometric operator
V
in a Pontryagin space
{{\mathfrak{H}}}
, if its domain
{\rm dom\,}V
and the
range
{\rm ran\,}V
are nondegenerate subspaces in
{{\mathfrak{H}}}
. In this case every unitary extension of
V
can be obtained in pretty much the same way as in the case of Hilbert space isometric operator.
Similarly, the extension theory and the theory of generalized coresolvents of standard isometric operators in Kreĭn spaces was built by A. Dijksma, H. Langer and H. de Snoo in
[
28
]
. For a nonstandard isometric operator in a Pontryagin space, description of its regular (resp. nonregular) generalized coresolvents in Pontryagin spaces without growth (resp. with growth) of negative index was given by P. Sorjonen
[
46
]
(resp. by O. Nitz
[
43
]
,
[
44
]
). However, the proof in
[
44
]
is not so convincing, as it becomes quite complicated and contains some gaps.

Another approach to the extension theory of symmetric operators in Hilbert spaces is based on the concept of
abstract boundary value
introduced by J. Calkin
[
13
]
and later formalized in the notion of boundary value space in
[
35
]
,
[
33
]
(or ordinary
boundary triple
in
[
26
]
). In
[
25
]
with each boundary triple there was associated an analytic object –
abstract Weyl function
which allows to carry out spectral analysis of extensions of symmetric operators. In the case of a Hilbert space isometric operator (and more generally for a dual pair of operators) the notions of a boundary triple and a corresponding Weyl function were introduced in
[
41
,
42
]
. These notions, when generalized to the indefinite case in
[
5
]
, proved to be an adequate language in the extension theory of nonstandard isometric operator
V
in a Pontryagin space, since they allowed to give full description of generalized coresolvents of
V
.
However, the method proposed in
[
5
]
is restricted to the case of regular generalized coresolvents, which have minimal realizations in Pontryagin spaces
{{\widetilde{\mathfrak{H}}}}
with the same negative index as
{\mathfrak{H}}
, and does not work for generalized coresolvents of
V
which have minimal realizations in Pontryagin spaces
{{\widetilde{\mathfrak{H}}}}
with bigger negative indices.

This difficulty can be prevented by using an appropriate notion of
boundary pairs
, which extend the concept of ordinary boundary triples.
In the case of symmetric operators in Hilbert spaces an extension of ordinary boundary triples, a so-called
generalized boundary triple
, was introduced and studied in
[
26
]
. This notion was further generalized in
[
19
]
to the notion of a
unitary boundary pair
(called therein as a
boundary relation
), which can be applied to study generalized resolvents of symmetric operators
[
20
,
21
]
and various general classes of boundary value problems for ordinary and partial differential operators, see
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
In particular, in
[
19
]
it was shown that every Nevanlinna pair (or Nevanlinna family of holomorphic relations) can be realized as the Weyl family of some unitary boundary pair, and in
[
18
,
20
]
this notion was used to get a new proof of M.G. Kreĭn formula for generalized resolvents of symmetric operators via the coupling method developed therein.
In
[
8
]
the notion of unitary boundary pair was introduced for symmetric operators in Pontryagin spaces and it was shown that every generalized Nevanlinna pair, allowing a finite negative index for the associated Nevanlinna kernel, can be realized as the Weyl family of such
a unitary boundary pair.

In this paper a new notion of a unitary boundary pair with an associated Weyl function is introduced and studied in the setting of isometric operators in Pontryagin spaces. In particular, it is shown in Section
3
how a certain subclass of unitary boundary pairs is connected to unitary colligations (see
[
1
]
) and, moreover, that the Weyl functions associated with that subclass of unitary boundary pairs actually coincide with characteristic functions of the corresponding unitary colligations; see Theorems
3.10
,
3.11
. Furthermore, using some transformations results, being motivated by
[
1
]
, it is also shown that every operator valued generalized Schur function (not necessarily holomorphic at the origin) can still be realized as the Weyl function of some unitary boundary pair for an isometric operator
V
in a Pontryagin space; see Theorems
3.15
,
3.17
. These two theorems show that the present notion of a unitary boundary pair for isometric operators in a Pontryagin (as well as in the classical Hilbert) space setting is a natural object to realize and study generalized (or standard) Schur functions as their Weyl functions. In particular, these new notions complement and extend the approach, which relies on characteristic functions of unitary colligations being associated with the special case stated in Theorem
3.11
.

After these characteristic results on unitary boundary pairs and their Weyl function for isometric operators we study in Section
4
some spectral properties of proper extensions of
V
and find a formula for their canonical coresolvents, see Theorem
4.2
, and then with these preparations prove an analog of M.G. Kreĭn formula for the generalized coresolvents of the isometric operator
V
. This latter problem is solved via the coupling method, where we consider a coupled unitary boundary pair as a direct sum of an ordinary boundary triple and a unitary boundary pair and then derive the formula for generalized coresolvents from the formula for canonical coresolvents associated with the coupled boundary pair.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Indefinite inner product spaces

A linear space
{\mathfrak{H}}
endowed with an inner product
[\cdot,\cdot]_{{\mathfrak{H}}}
is called an inner product space, see
[
11
]
,
[
4
]
.
A vector
f\in{\mathfrak{H}}
is called positive (resp. negative or neutral), if
[f,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}}>0
(resp.
[f,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}}<0
or
[f,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}}=0
).
A subspace
{{\mathfrak{L}}}\subset{\mathfrak{H}}
is called positive (resp. negative or neutral), if every vector
f\in{\mathfrak{L}}\setminus\{0\}
is positive (resp. negative or neutral). The orthogonal complement of a subspace
{{\mathfrak{L}}}
is denoted by
{{\mathfrak{L}}}^{[\perp]}
.

An inner product space
{\mathfrak{H}}
is called a
Kreĭn space
, if it admits a fundamental decomposition

(2.1)

{\mathfrak{H}}={\mathfrak{H}}_{+}[+]{\mathfrak{H}}_{-}

as an orthogonal sum of a positive subspace
{\mathfrak{H}}_{+}
and a negative subspace
{\mathfrak{H}}_{-}
. The operator
J=P_{+}-P_{-}
, where
P_{\pm}
are orthogonal projections in
{\mathfrak{H}}
onto
{\mathfrak{H}}_{\pm}
, is called
the fundamental symmetry
of
{\mathfrak{H}}
. We will use the notation
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
for the Kreĭn space
{\mathfrak{H}}
with the fundamental symmetry
J
. A Kreĭn space
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
with a finite negative index
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}):={\rm dim\,}{\mathfrak{H}}_{-}
is called a
Pontryagin space
.

Every closed subspace
{{\mathfrak{D}}}
of a Pontryagin space
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
admits
the following decomposition

(2.2)

{{\mathfrak{D}}}={{\mathfrak{D}}}_{0}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{-},

where
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{0}={{\mathfrak{D}}}\cap{{\mathfrak{D}}}^{[\perp]}
is a neutral subspace (the
isotropic part of
{{\mathfrak{D}}}
) and
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+}
and
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{-}
are closed
(uniformly) positive and negative subspaces of
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
; see e.g.

[
11
, Theorems IX.2.5]
. We will need the following slightly
modified version of this statement.

Lemma 2.1
.

Every linear subspace
{\mathfrak{T}}
of a Pontryagin space
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
admits
the following decomposition

(2.3)

{\mathfrak{T}}={\mathfrak{T}}_{+}\dot{+}{\mathfrak{T}}_{1},

where
{\mathfrak{T}}_{+}
is a positive subspace of
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
, such
that
\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}_{+}}
is a maximal positive subspace of

\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}}
, and
{{\mathfrak{T}}_{1}}
is a
k
-dimensional subspace of

({\mathfrak{H}},J)
, where
k={\rm dim\,}\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}}/\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}_{+}}
.

Proof.

Let
{{\mathfrak{D}}}
be the closure of
{\mathfrak{T}}
in
{\mathfrak{H}}
and decompose
{\mathfrak{D}}
as
in (
2.2
),

{{\mathfrak{D}}}={{\mathfrak{D}}}_{0}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{-}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+},

where
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{0}={{\mathfrak{D}}}\cap{{\mathfrak{D}}}^{[\perp]}
,
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{-}
, and
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+}

are closed neutral, negative, and positive
subspaces of
({\mathfrak{H}},J)
, respectively. Since
{\mathfrak{T}}
is a dense subset
of
{{\mathfrak{D}}}
and the subspaces
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{0}
and
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{-}
are finite
dimensional,
{\mathfrak{T}}
has a dense intersection with
{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+}
,

(2.4)

\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}\cap{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+}}={{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+};

see e.g.
[
32
, Lemma 2.1]
. Denote
{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}:={\mathfrak{T}}\cap{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{+}

and let
k={\rm dim\,}({{\mathfrak{D}}}_{0}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{D}}}_{-})
. Since
\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}}={\mathfrak{D}}

one concludes that there exists a
k
-dimensional subspace

{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}\subset{\mathfrak{T}}\setminus{\mathfrak{D}}_{+}
. The closed subspace

{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}\subset{\mathfrak{T}}
decomposes
{\mathfrak{T}}
and (
2.4
) together
with a dimension argument leads to

\overline{{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}}=\overline{{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}={\mathfrak{D}}_{+}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}={\mathfrak{D}}=\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}}.

The equality
\overline{{\mathfrak{T}}}={{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}\dot{+}\overline{{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}}

combined with (
2.4
) yields the decomposition (
2.3
)
for
{\mathfrak{T}}
.
∎

2.2.
Linear relations in Kreĭn spaces

Let
({\mathfrak{H}}_{1},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}})
and
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
be two Kreĭn spaces.
A linear relation
T
from
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
is a linear subspace
of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}\times{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
, see e.g.
[
2
]
.
Often a linear operator
T:{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}\to{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
will be identified with its graph

\textup{gr }T:=\{\,\{f,Tf\}:\,f\in{\rm dom\,}T\,\}.

For a linear relation
T
from
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
the symbols
{\rm dom\,}T
,
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T
,
{\rm ran\,}T
, and
{\rm mul\,}T
stand for the domain, kernel, range,
and multivalued part, respectively. The inverse
T^{-1}
is a
relation from
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
defined by

\{\,\{f^{\prime},f\}:\,\{f,f^{\prime}\}\in T\,\}
.
Denote by
\rho(T)
the resolvent set of
T
,
by
\sigma(T)
the spectrum of
T
and by
\sigma_{p}(T)
(resp.
\sigma_{c}(T)
,
\sigma_{r}(T)
) the point (resp. continuous, residual) spectrum of
T
.
The adjoint
T^{[*]}
is the closed linear relation from
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
to

{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
defined by (see
[
9
]
)

(2.5)

T^{[*]}=\{\,\{h,k\}\in{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}\times{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}:\,[k,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}=[h,g]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}},\,\{f,g\}\in T\,\}.

The following equalities are obvious from (
2.5
)

(2.6)

({\rm dom\,}T)^{[\perp]}={\rm mul\,}T^{[*]},\quad({\rm ran\,}T)^{[\perp]}={{\rm ker\,}\,}T^{[*]}.

A linear relation
T
from
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
is called

isometric
(resp.
contractive
or
expanding
), if for every

\{f,g\}\in T
one has

(2.7)

[g,g]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}=[f,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}\quad\left(\textup{ resp. }\,[g,g]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}\leq[f,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}\textup{ or }\,[g,g]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}\geq[f,f]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}\right).

It follows from (
2.5
) and (
2.7
) that
T
is
isometric, if
T^{-1}\subseteq T^{[*]}
. A linear relation
T
from

{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
is called
unitary
, if
T^{-1}=T^{[*]}
,
[
45
]
. Moreover,
T
is said to be a
standard
unitary operator
if
{\rm dom\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
and
{\rm ran\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
. For an
isometric linear relation
T
one obtains from
T^{-1}\subseteq T^{[*]}
and the identities (
2.6
) that

(2.8)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}T\subseteq({\rm dom\,}T)^{[\perp]},\quad{\rm mul\,}T\subseteq({\rm ran\,}T)^{[\perp]}.

For a unitary linear relation the following statements hold, see
[
45
, Theorem 2]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
T
be a unitary relation from the Kreĭn space

({\mathfrak{H}}_{1},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}})
to the Kreĭn space
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
.
Then:

(i)

{\rm dom\,}T
is closed if and only if
{\rm ran\,}T
is closed;

(ii)

the following equalities hold:

(2.9)

{{\rm ker\,}\,}T=({\rm dom\,}T)^{[\perp]},\quad{\rm mul\,}T=({\rm ran\,}T)^{[\perp]}.

Denote

(2.10)

{\mathbb{D}}=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,|\lambda|<1\},\quad{\mathbb{D}}_{e}=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,|\lambda|>1\},\quad{\mathbb{T}}=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,|\lambda|=1\}.

If
V
is a single-valued closed isometric operator in a Pontryagin space
{\mathfrak{H}}

then the subspaces
{\rm ran\,}(V-\lambda I)
are closed for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}\cup{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
, see e.g.

[
40
, Section 1.3]
or Lemma
2.4
below,
and each of the sets
\sigma_{p}(V)\cap{\mathbb{D}}
and
\sigma_{p}(V)\cap{\mathbb{D}}_{e}
consist of at most
\kappa=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}})
eigenvalues,
see e.g.
[
31
, p.49 Corollary 2]
.
Denote by
{\mathfrak{N}}_{\lambda}
the
defect subspace
of
V
:

{\mathfrak{N}}_{\lambda}:={\mathfrak{H}}[-]{\rm ran\,}(I-\overline{\lambda}V),\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}\cup{\mathbb{D}}_{e}.

Then

{\mathfrak{N}}_{\lambda}={{\rm ker\,}\,}(V^{-[*]}-\lambda I)=\{f_{\lambda}:(f_{\lambda},\lambda f_{\lambda})^{T}\in V^{-{[*]}}\}.

As is known, see
[
31
, Theorem 6.1]
, the numbers
{\rm dim\,}{\mathfrak{N}}_{\lambda}
take a constant value
n_{+}(V)
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus{\overline{\sigma_{p}(V)}}^{-1}
, and
n_{-}(V)
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}_{e}\setminus{\overline{\sigma_{p}(V)}}^{-1}
.
The numbers
n_{\pm}(V)
are called the
defect numbers
of
V
.

Definition 2.3
.

The isometric operator
V
in
{{\mathfrak{H}}}
is called
simple
, if
\sigma_{p}(V)\setminus{\mathbb{T}}=\emptyset
and

\overline{{\rm span\,}}\{{\mathfrak{N}}_{\lambda}:\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}\cup{\mathbb{D}}_{e}\}={{\mathfrak{H}}}.

In the case of Pontryagin spaces some further results on isometric
and unitary relations can be established. For any isometric relation

T
between two Kreĭn spaces it is clear that
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T
and

{\rm mul\,}T
are neutral subspaces. Therefore, in a Pontryagin space

{{\rm ker\,}\,}T
and
{\rm mul\,}T
are necessarily finite dimensional. If
T
is
closed then
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T
and
{\rm mul\,}T
are also closed. In Pontryagin
spaces the following stronger result is true.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
T
be a closed isometric relation from the Pontryagin space

({\mathfrak{H}}_{1},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}})
to the Pontryagin space
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
.
Then the domain and the range of
T
are closed.

Proof.

The isometry of
T
means that
T^{-1}\subseteq T^{[*]}
. Taking
inverses one gets
T=(T^{-1})^{-1}\subseteq T^{-[*]}
, i.e.,
T
and

T^{-1}
are simultaneously isometric. Therefore, to prove the
statement it suffices to prove that the range of
T
is a closed
subspace in
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
, since
T
is closed precisely when its inverse

T^{-1}
is closed and clearly
{\rm dom\,}T={\rm ran\,}T^{-1}
.

Now let
{\rm dom\,}T
be decomposed as in Lemma
2.1
,

(2.11)

{\rm dom\,}T={{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}\dot{+}{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1},

so that
{\mathfrak{D}}_{+}:=\overline{{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}}
is a maximal uniformly positive
subspace of
{\mathfrak{D}}:={\rm\overline{dom}\,}T
.
Next introduce the restriction of (the graph of)
T
by setting

T_{+}:=T\cap\left({\mathfrak{D}}_{+}\times{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}\right).

Then
T_{+}
is closed and as a restriction of
T
it is also an
isometric relation from
({\mathfrak{H}}_{1},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}})
to
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
.
Moreover,
{\rm dom\,}T_{+}={{\mathfrak{T}}}_{+}\subseteq{\mathfrak{D}}_{+}
is a uniformly positive
subspace. This implies that for all
\{f,f^{\prime}\}\in T_{+}
and some

\delta>0
,

[f^{\prime},f^{\prime}]_{2}=[f,f]_{1}\geq\delta\|f\|_{1}^{2},

which shows that
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T_{+}=\{0\}
, so that
(T_{+})^{-1}
is an
isometric operator, and, moreover,

\|(T_{+})^{-1}f^{\prime}\|_{1}^{2}=\|f\|_{1}^{2}\leq\delta^{-1}[f,f]_{1}\leq\delta^{-1}\|f\|_{1}^{2}.

Therefore, the closed isometric operator
(T_{+})^{-1}
is also
bounded. Consequently,
{\rm ran\,}T_{+}={\rm dom\,}(T_{+})^{-1}
is a closed
subspace in
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
. On the other hand, since
{\rm dom\,}T
admits the
decomposition (
2.11
), where
{{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1}
is finite dimensional and

{\rm mul\,}T={\rm mul\,}T_{+}
(also finite dimensional), one concludes that

{\rm ran\,}T=T({{\mathfrak{T}}}_{1})+{\rm ran\,}T_{+}

as a finite dimensional extension of the closed subspace
{\rm ran\,}T_{+}

is a closed subspace of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
2.4
can be seen as an extension of

[
11
, Theorem IX.3.1]
. It is known e.g. from

[
11
, Theorem IX.3.2]
and
[
31
, Theorems 6.2, 6.3]
)
that if
T
is an isometric operator in a Pontryagin space such that

{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T
(resp.
{\rm\overline{dom}\,}T
) is a nondegenerate subspace, then
T

(resp.
T^{-1}
) is continuous. The next lemma contains main
properties of isometric relations acting between two Pontryagin
spaces.

Lemma 2.5
.

For an isometric relation
T
from the Pontryagin space

({\mathfrak{H}}_{1},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}})
to the Pontryagin space
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
the
following statements hold:

(i)

If
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T
(resp.
{\rm\overline{dom}\,}T
) is a nondegenerate subspace of
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
, then
T

(resp.
T^{-1}
) is a continuous operator.

(ii)

If
T
is densely defined then
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})\leq\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
and if, in addition,

\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
, then
{\rm ran\,}\overline{T}
is a
closed nondegenerate subspace of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
and, moreover,
T
and

T^{-1}
are continuous operators.

(iii)

If
{\rm ran\,}T
is dense in
{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
then
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})\geq\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
and if, in addition,

\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
, then
{\rm dom\,}\overline{T}
is a
closed nondegenerate subspace of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
and, moreover,
T
and

T^{-1}
are continuous operators.

(iv)

If the relation
T
is unitary and
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T={\rm mul\,}T=\{0\}
, then

\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
and
T
is a standard unitary
operator.

(v)

If the relation
T
is unitary and
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
, then

(2.12)

{\rm mul\,}T=\{0\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{{\rm ker\,}\,}T=\{0\}.

In particular, if
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
then a unitary

T
is an operator if and only if it is a standard unitary

Proof.

(i) By assumption
T\subseteq T^{-[*]}
and
\overline{T}\subseteq T^{-[*]}
, i.e.,
\overline{T}
is also isometric. Hence, if
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T
is nondegenerate,
(
2.8
) implies

{\rm mul\,}\overline{T}\subset{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T\cap({\rm\overline{ran}\,}T)^{[\perp]}=\{0\}.

Thus
T
is a closable operator, which by Lemma
2.4
and
the closed graph theorem implies that
\overline{T}
and, therefore,
also
T
is continuous. Similarly it is seen that

{{\rm ker\,}\,}\overline{T}\subseteq{\rm\overline{dom}\,}T\cap({\rm\overline{dom}\,}T)^{[\perp]}=\{0\},

if
{\rm\overline{dom}\,}T
is nondegenerate, and then
\overline{T}^{-1}
and

T^{-1}
are continuous.

(ii) Since
{\rm\overline{dom}\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
is nondegenerate,
T^{-1}
is a
continuous operator by item (i). On the other hand
{\rm dom\,}T
, as a
dense subspace of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
, contains a negative subspace

{\mathcal{D}}_{-}\subset{\rm dom\,}T
of dimension
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})
; see

[
11
, Theorem IX.1.4]
. Then also
{\rm ran\,}T
contains a
negative subspace of the same dimension and hence

\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})\geq\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})
.

Now assume that
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
. Then
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T={\rm\overline{ran}\,}\overline{T}
is necessarily a nondegenerate subspace of the
Pontryagin space
({\mathfrak{H}}_{2},J_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}})
, see e.g.

[
11
, Lemma II.10.5]
, and (i) shows that
T
is a continuous
operator.

(iii) This follows by applying (ii) to
T^{-1}
, which is also an
isometric relation.

(iv) If
T
is unitary then the conditions
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T={\rm mul\,}T=\{0\}
are
equivalent to
{\rm\overline{dom}\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
and
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
; see (
2.9
)
in Proposition
2.2
. Now the assertions follow from (ii) and
(iii).

(v) Let
\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{1})=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}_{2})
and let
T
be unitary. By
symmetry it suffices to prove one implication in (
2.12
),
say, “
\Rightarrow
”. The condition
{\rm mul\,}T=\{0\}
is equivalent
to
{\rm\overline{ran}\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
; see (
2.9
). Now item (iii) shows that
T
and

T^{-1}
are continuous operators. Thus, in particular,
{{\rm ker\,}\,}T=\{0\}
and (
2.9
) together with Lemma
2.4
shows
that
{\rm dom\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{1}
and
{\rm ran\,}T={\mathfrak{H}}_{2}
.
∎

2.3.
Operator colligations

Let
{\mathfrak{H}}
be a Pontryagin space,
{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces.
The set of bounded everywhere defined operators from
{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
to
{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
is denoted by
\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
,
\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1}):=\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{1})
.
Let
U
be a bounded operator
from
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
to
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
represented in the block form

U=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}T&F\\
G&H\\
\end{array}\right):\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathfrak{H}}}\\
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathfrak{H}}}\\
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}

The quadruple
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},U)
is called a
colligation
,
{\mathfrak{H}}
is the state space,
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
and
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
are the incoming and the outgoing spaces,
T
is the main operator and
U
is called the connecting operator of the colligation. The colligation
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},U)
is called
unitary
, if
U
is a unitary operator from
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
to
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
. The colligation
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},U^{[*]})
is called adjoint to the colligation
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},U)
; cf.
[
12
,
1
]
.

Components of a
unitary colligation
satisfy the following identities

(2.13)

\begin{split}&T^{[*]}T+G^{[*]}G=I_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}},\ F^{[*]}F+H^{*}H=I_{{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}},\ T^{[*]}F+G^{[*]}H=0,\\
&TT^{[*]}+FF^{[*]}=I_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}},\ GG^{[*]}+HH^{*}=I_{{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}},\ TG^{[*]}+FH^{*}=0,\end{split}

which are equivalent to the identities

U^{[*]}U=I_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}},\quad UU^{[*]}=I_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}}.

A colligation
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},U)
is said to be

closely connected
, if

(2.14)

{\mathfrak{H}}=\overline{\textup{span}}\,\{{\rm ran\,}(T^{m}F),{\rm ran\,}((T^{[*]})^{n}G^{[*]}):\,m,n\geq 0\}.

A unitary colligation is closely connected if and only if the operator
U
has no nontrivial reducing subspaces.
The operator valued function

\Theta_{\Delta}(\lambda):=H+\lambda G(I-\lambda T)^{-1}F,\quad 1/\lambda\in\rho(T),

is called the characteristic function of the unitary colligation
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},U)
.

Recall, see e.g.
[
1
]
, that a
\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
-valued function
\Theta(\lambda)
is said to belong
to the
generalized Schur class

{\mathcal{S}}_{\kappa}^{0}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
if it is holomorphic in a neighborhood
\Omega
of
0
and the
kernel

(2.15)

{\mathsf{K}}_{\omega}^{\Theta}(\lambda)=\frac{I-\Theta(\lambda)\Theta(\omega)^{*}}{1-\lambda\overline{\omega}}

has
\kappa
negative squares in
\Omega\times\Omega
, i.e. for any finite set of points
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
in
\Omega
and vectors
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
in
{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
, the
Hermitian matrix

(2.16)

\left(({\mathsf{K}}_{\omega_{i}}^{\Theta}(\omega_{j})f_{j},f_{i})_{{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}

has at most
\kappa
negative eigenvalues, and for some choice of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
in
\Omega
and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
in
{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
the matrix (
2.16
) has exactly
\kappa

negative eigenvalues.

As is known, see
[
1
]
, the characteristic function of a closely connected unitary colligation belongs
to the generalized Schur class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\kappa}^{0}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
, where
\kappa=\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}})
. Moreover, the converse is also true; see e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 2.3.1]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces and let
S(z)
belong to the generalized Schur class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\kappa}^{0}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
. Then there exists a closely connected unitary colligation
({{\mathfrak{H}}},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},U)
, such that the corresponding characteristic function
\Theta_{\Delta}(z)
coincides with
S(z)
in a neighborhood
\Omega
of
0
.

In what follows a
\mathbf{B}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
-valued
function
\Theta(\cdot)
holomorphic in some open subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{D}}
is said to belong to the generalized Schur class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\kappa}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
,
if the kernel (
2.15
) has
\kappa
negative squares in
\Omega\times\Omega
. In particular, we do not require that
0\in\Omega
, which implies
that characteristic functions of unitary colligations used in Theorem
2.6
are not sufficient to give a realization for all functions
\Theta(\cdot)
from the class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\kappa}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
.
In the next section we introduce the notions of a unitary boundary pair for an isometric operator and an associated Weyl function as a replacement for unitary colligations and their characteristic functions. These new notions allow to realize an arbitrary operator function from the class
{\mathcal{S}}_{\kappa}({\mathfrak{L}}_{1},{\mathfrak{L}}_{2})
as the Weyl function of a Pontryagin space isometric operator, corresponding to some unitary boundary pair.

3.
Unitary boundary pairs for isometric operators

3.1.
Unitary boundary pairs and the main transform

Let
{{\mathfrak{H}}}
be a Pontryagin space with the negative index
\kappa
and the fundamental symmetry
J_{\mathfrak{H}}

and let
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
and
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces. In this section we introduce the notion of

a unitary boundary pair for an isometric operator

V
:
here, and in what follows,
V
is assumed to be closed.

For this purpose equip the Hilbert spaces

{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}
and
{{\mathfrak{L}}}={{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
with the indefinite inner
products by the formulas

[{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{g}}}]_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}}=(J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}}}{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{g}}})_{{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}},\quad[{{\widehat{u}}},{{\widehat{v}}}]_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}}=\left(J_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}}{{\widehat{u}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\right)_{{\mathfrak{L}}},

where

J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}}&0\\
0&-J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}}\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad J_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}}&0\\
0&-I_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right),

and
{{\widehat{f}}}=\{f,f^{\prime}\},{{\widehat{g}}}=\{g,g^{\prime}\}\in{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}
,
{{\widehat{u}}}=\{u_{1},u_{2}\},{{\widehat{v}}}=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\in{\mathfrak{L}}
.
Then
({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}})
and

({{\mathfrak{L}}},J_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}})
are Kreĭn spaces.
In particular, for
{\mathfrak{V}}\subset{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}
the linear set
{\mathfrak{V}}^{[\perp]}

in the Kreĭn space
({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}})

can be characterized as follows

(3.1)

{{\widehat{g}}}\in{\mathfrak{V}}^{[\perp]}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{g}}}]_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}}=0\text{ for all }{{\widehat{f}}}\in{\mathfrak{V}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{{\widehat{g}}}\in{\mathfrak{V}}^{-[*]}.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
and
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces, let
V
be a closed
isometric operator (or isometric relation) in
{{\mathfrak{H}}}
and let
\Gamma
be a linear relation

{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}\rightarrow{{\mathfrak{L}}}
, where
{{\mathfrak{L}}}:={{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
.

The pair
({{\mathfrak{L}}},\Gamma)
will be called a unitary boundary pair for
V
if:

(1)

V={{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma
and for all
\{{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{u}}}\},\{{{\widehat{g}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\}\in\Gamma

the following identity holds

(3.2)

[f,g]_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}}-[f^{\prime},g^{\prime}]_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}}=(u_{1},v_{1})_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}}-(u_{2},v_{2})_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}};

(2)

\Gamma
is maximal in the sense that if
\{{{\widehat{g}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\}\in{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}
satisfies (
3.2
) for all
\{{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{u}}}\}\in\Gamma
, then

\{{{\widehat{g}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\}\in\Gamma
.
Here

\{{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{u}}}\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\},\quad\{{{\widehat{g}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}g\\
g^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}.

Item (1) of Definition
3.1
means that
\Gamma
is an isometric
linear relation from the Kreĭn space
({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}})
to the Kreĭn space
({{\mathfrak{L}}},J_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}})
, while items (1) and (2) together mean that
\Gamma
is unitary.

Application of Proposition
2.2
to a unitary boundary pair
leads to the following statement.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
({{\mathfrak{L}}},\Gamma)
be a unitary boundary pair for
V
. Then:

(i)

V_{*}:={\rm dom\,}\Gamma
is dense in
V^{-[*]}
;

(ii)

{\rm ran\,}\Gamma
is dense in
{{\mathfrak{L}}}
if and only if
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma=\{0\}
;

(iii)

{\rm ran\,}\Gamma={{\mathfrak{L}}}
if and only if
{\rm dom\,}\Gamma=V^{-[*]}
and
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma=\{0\}
.

Define the components
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
of
\Gamma
by

(3.3)

\Gamma_{1}:=\left\{\{\widehat{f},u_{1}\}:\left\{\widehat{f},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in\Gamma\quad\text{for some}\quad u_{2}\in{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}\right\};

(3.4)

\Gamma_{2}:=\left\{\{\widehat{f},u_{2}\}:\left\{\widehat{f},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in\Gamma\quad\text{for some}\quad u_{1}\in{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\right\}.

In the case that
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are single-valued, i.e.
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{1}={\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{2}=0
and
{\rm dom\,}\Gamma=V^{-{[*]}}
and
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma={{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
the collection

\{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2},\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\}
is called an
ordinary boundary triple
for the isometric operator
V
.
For a Hilbert space isometric operator the corresponding notion was introduced and studied in
[
41
,
42
]

as a boundary triple for the dual pair
(V,V^{-1})
.

For ordinary boundary triples an application of the closed graph theorem
shows that the component mappings
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are bounded.
However, for a general unitary boundary pair
({{\mathfrak{L}}},\Gamma)
the mappings
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}

need not be bounded or single-valued. With
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
one associates the extensions
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
of
V

by the equalities

(3.5)

V_{1}:={{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{1},\quad V_{2}:={{\rm ker\,}\,}\Gamma_{2}.

It follows from the identity (
3.2
) that
V_{1}
is an
expanding linear relation

and
V_{2}
is a
contractive linear relation
in the Pontryagin space
{\mathfrak{H}}
.
Moreover, it is clear from (
3.2
) that the sets
{{\rm ker\,}\,}V_{1}\setminus{{\rm ker\,}\,}V
and
{\rm mul\,}V_{2}\,(={\rm mul\,}V_{2}\setminus{\rm mul\,}V)
consist of negative vectors in
{\mathfrak{H}}
while the sets

{{\rm ker\,}\,}V_{2}\setminus{{\rm ker\,}\,}V
and
{\rm mul\,}V_{1}\,(={\rm mul\,}V_{1}\setminus{\rm mul\,}V)
consist
of positive vectors of
{\mathfrak{H}}
.

Let
{\mathfrak{H}}_{j}:={{\mathfrak{H}}}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{j}

(j=1,2)
be a Pontryagin space with the inner product

\left[\begin{pmatrix}f\\
u\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}f\\
u\end{pmatrix}\right]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{j}}=[f,f]_{\mathfrak{H}}+\|u\|_{{\mathfrak{L}}_{j}}^{2},\quad f\in{\mathfrak{H}},\,u\in{\mathfrak{L}}_{j},\,j=1,2.

In establishing some properties of unitary boundary pairs it is useful to connect the unitary relation
\Gamma
which acts
between two Kreĭn spaces to another unitary relation that acts between two Pontryagin spaces, since
unitary relations between Pontryagin spaces have simpler structure. For this purpose
we introduce the following transform from the Kreĭn space
\left({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}\oplus{{\mathfrak{L}}},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}}\oplus(-J_{\mathfrak{L}})\right)
to
the Kreĭn space
\left({{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}\oplus{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}},(J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}})\oplus(-J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}})\right)
by

{\mathcal{J}}:\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\mapsto\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}\\
u_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right\},\quad f,f^{\prime}\in{{\mathfrak{H}}}\text{ and }u_{1}\in{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1},\,u_{2}\in{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}

It establishes a one-to-one correspondence between
(closed) linear relations
\Gamma
from
({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}})
to
({\mathfrak{L}},J_{\mathfrak{L}})

and (closed) linear relations
{\mathcal{U}}
from
{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}
to
{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}
via

(3.6)

\Gamma\mapsto{\mathcal{U}}:={\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma)=\left\{\,\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}\\
u_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right\}:\,\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in\Gamma\,\right\}.

The linear relation
{\mathcal{U}}
will be called the
main transform
of
\Gamma
; cf.
[
19
]
for the case of symmetric operators.
In the following lemma, which is an analog of
[
19
, Proposition 2.10]
, some basic properties of the transform
{\mathcal{J}}
are given.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let the linear relation
\Gamma
from
({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}})
to
({{\mathfrak{L}}},J_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}})
and the linear relation
{\mathcal{U}}
from

{{\mathfrak{H}}}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
to
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
be connected by
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma)
. Then

(3.7)

{\mathcal{U}}^{-[*]}={\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma^{-[*]}).

Moreover, the transform
{\mathcal{J}}
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between isometric (unitary, contractive, expanding) relations
\Gamma
from
({{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2},J_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}^{2}})
to
({{\mathfrak{L}}},J_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}})
and isometric (unitary, contractive, expanding) relations
{\mathcal{U}}
from
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}
to
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\times{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that for all elements of the form

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\},\left\{\begin{pmatrix}g\\
g^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathfrak{H}}}\\
{{\mathfrak{H}}}\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}\\
{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

the following identity is satisfied:

(3.8)

\left[\begin{pmatrix}f\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}g\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{2}}-\left[\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}\\
u_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}g^{\prime}\\
v_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{{\mathfrak{H}}_{1}}=\left[\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}g\\
g^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}}-\left[\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right]_{\mathfrak{L}}.

In view of (
3.1
) this identity implies the equivalence

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}\\
u_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{-[*]}\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\in\Gamma^{-[*]}

which leads to identity (
3.7
). It follows from (
3.7
) that

{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}^{[*]}\Longleftrightarrow\Gamma^{-1}\subseteq\Gamma^{[*]},\quad{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}={\mathcal{U}}^{[*]}\Longleftrightarrow\Gamma^{-1}=\Gamma^{[*]},

i.e.,
{\mathcal{U}}
is isometric (unitary) precisely when
\Gamma
is isometric (resp. unitary).
The connection between contractive (expanding) relations
\Gamma
and
{\mathcal{U}}
is clear from (
3.8
).
∎

The next proposition contains the basic properties of
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
and
V_{1}
,
V_{2}

for a unitary boundary pair
({{\mathfrak{L}}},\Gamma)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
({{\mathfrak{L}}},\Gamma)
be a unitary boundary pair for
V
, let
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
and
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
be defined by (
3.3
), (
3.3
) and (
3.5
), and let
{\mathcal{U}}
be the main transform of
\Gamma
.
Then:

(i)

\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are closed;

(ii)

{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}={\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
and
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{2}={\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
;

(iii)

{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{j}=P_{{\mathfrak{L}}_{j}}({\rm mul\,}\Gamma)
,
j=1,2
, and the following equivalences hold:

{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{1}=\{0\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{2}=\{0\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\rm mul\,}\Gamma=\{0\};

(iv)

the extensions
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
of
V
are closed,
V_{1}\subseteq V_{2}^{-[*]}
and
V_{2}\subseteq V_{1}^{-[*]}
;

(v)

the following equivalences hold:

{\rm mul\,}V_{2}=\{0\}\Longleftrightarrow{\rm mul\,}{\mathcal{U}}=\{0\};

{{\rm ker\,}\,}V_{1}=\{0\}\Longleftrightarrow{{\rm ker\,}\,}{\mathcal{U}}=\{0\};

{\rm mul\,}V_{2}=\{0\}\Longleftrightarrow{{\rm ker\,}\,}V_{1}=\{0\}.

If one of the sets appearing in the above equivalences is trivial, then the main transform
{\mathcal{U}}
from
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
to
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}

of
\Gamma
is a standard unitary operator.

Proof.

(i) To prove that
\Gamma_{1}
is closed assume that
\{{{\widehat{f}}}_{n},{{\widehat{u}}}_{n}\}\in\Gamma
such that

{{\widehat{f}}}_{n}\to{{\widehat{f}}}\in{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}
and
u_{1,n}\to u_{1}\in{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
. Then (
3.2
) gives

[f_{n}-f_{m},f_{n}-f_{m}]_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}}-[f_{n}^{\prime}-f_{m}^{\prime},f_{n}^{\prime}-f_{m}^{\prime}]_{{{\mathfrak{H}}}}=\|u_{1,n}-u_{1,m}\|^{2}_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{1}}-\|u_{2,n}-u_{2,m}\|^{2}_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}}

and letting
n,m\to\infty
one concludes that
\|u_{2,n}-u_{2,m}\|_{{{\mathfrak{L}}}_{2}}\to 0
. As a Cauchy sequence
(u_{2,n})

converges to some element
u_{2}
in
{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
. This means that
\{{{\widehat{f}}}_{n},{{\widehat{u}}}_{n}\}\to\{{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{u}}}\}
and, since
\Gamma
is closed
as a unitary relation,
\{{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{u}}}\}\in\Gamma
and thus
\{{{\widehat{f}}},u_{1}\}\in\Gamma_{1}
.
This proves that
\Gamma_{1}
is closed. Similarly one proves that
\Gamma_{2}
is closed.

(ii) First it is shown that
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}
is a closed subspace of
{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
. For this consider
the main transform
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{J}}(\Gamma)
. By Lemma
3.3

{\mathcal{U}}
is a unitary relation between the Pontryagin spaces

{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
and
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
. Moreover, by Lemma
2.4

{\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}
is closed and Proposition
2.2

shows that
{\rm mul\,}{\mathcal{U}}
is the isotropic part of
{\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}
. Therefore
{\rm mul\,}{\mathcal{U}}
is a closed finite dimensional
subspace of
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
and thus also the co-dimension
k
of
{\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}
is finite (
k\leq\kappa_{-}({\mathfrak{H}}))
.
Let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be any
k
-dimensional subspace such that
{\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}\dot{+}{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
and let
P_{1}
be the orthogonal projection
from
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
onto
{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
. Then

{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}=P_{1}({\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}\dot{+}{\mathfrak{M}})=P_{1}{\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}+P_{1}{\mathfrak{M}},

and here
{\rm dim\,}P_{1}{\mathfrak{M}}\leq k
, which implies that
P_{1}{\rm ran\,}{\mathcal{U}}={\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}
is closed.

To see that
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}={\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
it suffices to prove that
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}
is dense in
{\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
. For this assume that
v_{1}\perp{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}
.
Let
{{\widehat{g}}}=\{0,0\}\in{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}
and
{{\widehat{v}}}=\{v_{1},0\}\in{\mathfrak{L}}
. Then
\{{{\widehat{g}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\}
satisfies the identity (
3.2
)
for all
\{{{\widehat{f}}},{{\widehat{u}}}\}\in\Gamma
, and hence assumption (2) in Definition
3.1
implies that
\{{{\widehat{g}}},{{\widehat{v}}}\}\in\Gamma
.
This means that
{{\widehat{v}}}\in{\rm mul\,}\Gamma
and then, in particular,
v_{1}\in{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{1}\subseteq{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}
. Thus,
v_{1}=0
and this proves
that
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{1}={\mathfrak{L}}_{1}
. The equality
{\rm ran\,}\Gamma_{2}={\mathfrak{L}}_{2}
is then clear by symmetry.

(iii) The identities
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{j}=P_{{\mathfrak{L}}_{j}}({\rm mul\,}\Gamma)
,
j=1,2
, are clear from the definition of
\Gamma
.
Hence,
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma=\{0\}
implies that
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{1}={\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{2}=\{0\}
.
Conversely, assume that e.g.
{\rm mul\,}\Gamma_{1}=\{0\}
and that

Lemma 3.6

(cf.
[
16

Let

{{\mathcal{H}}}

Then the Gram matrix of the functions

(j=1,\dots,n)

in the space
L_{2}({{\mathcal{H}}})
over the unit circle

(i)

is equal to

G=\left(\frac{(u_{j},u_{k})_{{{\mathcal{H}}}}}{1-\lambda_{j}\overline{\lambda}_{k}}\right)_{j,k=1}^{n}

(j,k=1,\dots,n)

(ii)

(j,k=1,\dots,n)

Proof.

To determine the Gram matrix consider the inner product of the

-valued functions

and
f_{k}(\lambda)
with

(i) If

then in view of the equalities

\begin{split}\left(f_{j},f_{k}\right)_{L_{2}({{\mathcal{H}}})}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{dt}{(1-\lambda_{j}e^{it})(1-\overline{\lambda}_{k}e^{-it})}(u_{j},u_{k})_{{{\mathcal{H}}}}\\

(j=1,\dots,n)

in the space

on the unit circle.

(j=1,\dots,n)

that the matrix
