Qualitative properties for the 1 − D 1-D impulsive wave equation: controllability and observability
Akram BEN AISSA ∗, Walid Zouhair ∗∗

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish some important results for the impulsive wave equation. We begin by proving the existence of a solution. Then, we study the impulse approximate controllability where the control function acts on a subdomain ω \omega and at one instant of time τ ∈ ( 0 , T ) \tau\in(0,T) . Afterward, we study impulse observability.

1.
Introduction

Impulsive dynamic systems are a type of hybrid systems for which the trajectory admits discontinuities at certain instants due to sudden jumps of the state called pulses ( see more in
[
1
]
). Dynamical behavior of many systems in real life can be characterized by abrupt changes that appear suddenly, such as heartbeats, drug flows, the value of stocks, impulse vaccination, and bonds on the stock market. This class of hybrid systems is presented as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\xi^{\prime}(t)&=&f(t,\xi(t)),\quad t\in(0,T],\,\,t\neq t_{k},\\[8.53581pt]
\bigtriangleup\xi(t_{k})&=&\nu_{k}(\xi(t_{k})),\quad k\in\theta_{m}^{n},\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
\theta_{m}^{n}=\{m,m+1,...,n\},

\bigtriangleup\xi(t_{k})=\xi(t_{k}^{+})-\xi(t_{k}^{-}),
with
\xi(t_{k}^{+})

(\text{respectively}\,\xi(t_{k}^{-}))
denotes the limit to the right (respectively to the left) of
t
and
\xi^{\prime}(t)=f(t,\xi(t)),
is a differential equation.

The theory of impulsive differential equations was initiated by V. D. Mil’man and A. Mishkis in 1960
[
16
]
. Afterward, many scientists contributed to the enrichment of this theory, they launched different studies on this discipline and a large number of results were established, we invite the interested reader to see
[
14

21
]
.

Unlike the interior controllability for impulsive systems that have been extensively studied in the literature, see for instance
[
8
,
4
,
9
,
3
,
11
]
and the references therein, the problem of controllability with impulse controls has attracted less attention and not as many works are available in this area. We mention A. Khapalov
[
10
]
who proved the exact controllability of a class of second-order hyperbolic boundary problems with impulse controls using the Huygens’ principle. S. Qin and G. Wang
[
20
]
presented a necessary and sufficient condition for the approximate controllability in finite dimension, which is exactly Kalman’s controllability rank condition. Recently K. D. Phung, G. Wang, and Y. Xu
[
19
]
considered the following impulsive functional differential systems

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\psi-\Delta\psi=0,&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)\backslash\{\tau\}\\
\psi=0,&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\\
\psi(\cdot,0)=\psi^{0},&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\psi(\cdot,\tau)=\psi\left(\cdot,\tau^{-}\right)+\mathbb{1}_{\omega}h,&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

They proved that the above system is null approximate impulse controllable at any time
T>0
.

Motivated by the above works, we study in this paper the following
1-D
impulsive wave equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(x,t)}{\partial{t}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(x,t)}{\partial{x}^{2}}=0,&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega\,\times(0,T)\backslash\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in\theta_{1}^{n}},&\\
\kappa(x,t_{k}^{+})-\kappa(x,t_{k}^{-})=0,&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,\,\,\,\,k\in\theta_{1}^{n},&\\
\frac{\partial\kappa(x,t_{k}^{+})}{\partial{t}}-\frac{\partial\kappa(x,t_{k}^{-})}{\partial{t}}=\Upsilon_{k}(x),&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,\,\,\,\,k\in\theta_{1}^{n},&\\
\kappa(0,t)=\kappa(1,t)=0,&\text{ on }\,\,\,\,(0,T),&\\
\kappa(x,0)=\kappa^{0}(x),\frac{\partial\kappa(x,0)}{\partial{t}}=\kappa^{1}(x),&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,&\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

where
\Omega=(0,1),

\displaystyle\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\theta_{1}^{n}}\,
are instantaneous pulses such that
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}<T
and
\Upsilon_{k}
is an impulse control that satisfies a suitable assumptions,
(\kappa^{0},\kappa^{1})
are the initial conditions in an appropriate Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In section 2, we briefly present the problem formulation and the well-posedness result. In section 3 we discuss the impulse approximate controllability and we present some related definitions and propositions. The last section is devoted to studying impulse observability.

2.
Well-posedness

In order to use the theory of semigroup to establish the well-posedness of problem (
1.3
), we start by putting the previous equation to the first order in time
\psi=\left(\kappa,\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial{t}}\right)^{\top}
. Then, the problem (
1.3
) turns into a Cauchy problem as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial\Psi(.,t)}{\partial{t}}=\mathcal{A}\Psi(.,t)&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,(0,T)\backslash\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in\theta_{1}^{n}},&\\
\Psi(t_{k}^{+})=\Psi(t_{k}^{-})+\Upsilon_{k}(.)B&k\in\theta_{1}^{n},&\\
\Psi(0)=\Psi_{0}.\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

First we introduce the Hilbert space as follows:

\mathcal{H}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega),

endowed with the following inner product

\left(\left(u,v\right)^{\top},\,\left(\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\right)^{\top}\right)_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\frac{\overline{\tilde{u}}}{\partial x}dx+\int_{\Omega}v\overline{\tilde{v}}dx.

(2.5)

Then, we define
B
and the operator
\mathcal{A}
on
\mathcal{H}
by

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\left(u,v\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(v,\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}\right)^{\top},\forall(u,\,v)^{\top}\in D(\mathcal{A})

(2.6)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0,1\right)^{\top}.

One can easily check that
\mathcal{A}
is an unbounded skew-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
, then it generates a group of isometries
(S(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\,
( Stone’s theorem
[
18
]
).

For further purposes and with the notations
\mathcal{I}:=[0,T],
and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}:=[0,T]\backslash\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in\theta_{1}^{n}},
we set the following Banach space which is natural framework space for evolution equations with pulses:

\displaystyle\mathcal{PC}(\mathcal{I};\mathcal{H})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}:y\in C(\mathcal{I}^{\prime};\mathcal{H}):\forall k\quad y\left(t_{k}^{-}\right),y\left(t_{k}^{+}\right)\text{exists}\\
\mbox{ and }y\left(t_{k}\right)=y\left(t_{k}^{-}\right)\end{array}\right\},

(2.7)

endowed with the norm

\|y\|=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|y(t)\|.

(2.8)

We define the classical solution for the impulsive wave equation (
2.4
) as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

Classical solution
\Psi
for (
2.4
)is an absolutely continuous piecewise continuous mapping with discontinuities at points
t=t_{k}
which, for almost all
t
satisfies the system (
2.4
), and for
t=t_{k}
satisfies the jump conditions. In other words, a classical solution for (
2.4
) is a function

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Psi\in\mathcal{P}\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathcal{H})\,\cap\,C^{1}\left((0,T)\backslash\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in\theta_{1}^{n}},\mathcal{H}\right),\\
\Psi(t)\in D(A),\text{ for }t\in(0,T)\backslash\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in\theta_{1}^{n}}\end{array}\right.

such that
\Psi
satisfies (
2.4
) in
[0,T).

Proposition 2.1

(Well-posedness)
.

Assume that
\Psi_{0}=(\kappa^{0},\kappa^{1})\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\,,
and
\Upsilon_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

k\in\theta_{1}^{n}.
Then, the impulsive system (
2.4
) has a unique classical solution
\Psi
which, for
t\in[0,T),
satisfies

\Psi(t)=S(t)\Psi_{0}+\displaystyle{\sum_{0<t_{k}<t}}S(t-t_{k})\Upsilon_{k}B.

Proof.

First, in the interval
J_{1}=[0,t_{1}),
we consider the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial\Psi(.,t)}{\partial{t}}=\mathcal{A}\Psi(.,t),\quad\,0<t<t_{1},\\[5.69054pt]
\Psi(0)=\Psi_{0}.\\
\end{array}\right.

Note that the classical solutions for evolution equations without pulses are defined in an obvious way, see Pazy
[
18
]
. Then it is clear that the unique classical solution of this system is given by

\Psi_{1}(t)=S(t)\Psi_{0}.

Next, we define

\Psi_{1}(t_{1})=S(t_{1})\Psi_{0},

furthermore, we can check that
\Psi_{1}(.)
is left continuous at
t_{1},
and
\Psi_{1}(t_{1})\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}).

On the other hand in
J_{2}=[t_{1},t_{2})
, we consider the following equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial\Psi(x,t)}{\partial{t}}=\mathcal{A}\Psi(x,t),\quad\,t_{1}<t<t_{2},\\[5.69054pt]
\Psi(t_{1})=S(t_{1})\Psi_{0}+\Upsilon_{1}B.\\
\end{array}\right.

Since
S(t_{1})\Psi_{0}+\Upsilon_{1}B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}),
Once again the unique classical solution of the previous equation is given by

\begin{array}[]{lll}\Psi_{2}(t)=S(t)\Psi_{0}+S(t-t_{1})\Upsilon_{1}B,\end{array}

furthermore, we have
\Psi_{2}(.)
is left continuous at
t_{2}
and
\Psi_{2}(t_{2})\in D(\mathcal{A}).
If we continue in the same manner then at the k-th step, in the interval
J_{k}=[t_{k-1},t_{k}),

k\in\theta_{1}^{n+1}
we get the following unique classical solution

\Psi_{k}(t)=S(t)\Psi_{0}+\displaystyle{\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}}S(t-t_{m})\Upsilon_{m}B.

Now, we define the function
\Psi
as follows:

\Psi(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Psi_{1}(t)&t_{0}<t<t_{1},&\\[5.69054pt]
&\vdots&\\
\Psi_{k}(t)&t_{k-1}<t<t_{k},&\\
&\vdots&\\
\Psi_{n+1}&t_{n}<t<T,&\\
\end{array}\right.

it is clear that
\Psi(.)
is the unique classical solution of (
2.4
).
∎

Note that if
\Psi_{0}\in\mathcal{H},
the existence of a solution for the system (
2.4
) can be proved in a similar way by assuming that
\Upsilon_{k}\in L^{2}(\Omega).

3.
Impulse Controllability of the System (
3.9
)

Control theory deals with how an arbitrary initial state can be directed exactly or approximately close to a given final state using a set of admissible controls. In this section, we study the impulse approximate controllability for the system below with one pulse
\tau\in(0,T)
. Here, the control function acts on a subdomain
\omega
and at one point of time
\tau\in(0,T)
(see more in
[
17
]
).

We consider the following wave equation with one pulse:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(x,t)}{\partial{t}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(x,t)}{\partial{x}^{2}}=0,&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega\,\times(0,T)\backslash\left\{\tau\right\},&\\
\kappa(x,\tau^{+})=\kappa(x,\tau^{-}),&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,&\\
\frac{\partial\kappa(x,\tau^{+})}{\partial{t}}-\frac{\partial\kappa(x,\tau^{-})}{\partial{t}}=\mathbf{1}_{\omega}\Upsilon_{\tau}(x),&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,&\\
\kappa(0,t)=\kappa(1,t)=0,&\text{ on }\,\,\,\,(0,T),&\\
\kappa(x,0)=\kappa^{0}(x),\frac{\partial\kappa(x,0)}{\partial{t}}=\kappa^{1}(x),&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega.&\\
\end{array}\right.

(3.9)

Let us first remark that (
3.9
) is equivalent to the following system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(x,t)}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\kappa(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}=\mathbf{1}_{\omega}\Upsilon_{\tau}\left(x\right)\delta_{\tau}(t),&\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\kappa(0,t)=\kappa(1,t)=0,&\text{ on }(0,T),\\
\kappa(x,0)=\kappa^{0}(x),\frac{\partial\kappa(x,0)}{\partial t}=\kappa^{1}(x),&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(3.10)

where
\delta_{\tau}
is the Dirac measure at time
t=\tau
, which in turn is equivalent to the following abstract control problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\psi^{{}^{\prime}}(t)=\mathcal{A}\psi(t)+B_{\omega}\Upsilon_{\tau}\delta_{\tau}(t),&\text{ on }\,\,\,\,(0,T),&\\[5.69054pt]
\psi(0)=\psi_{0},\\
\end{array}\right.

(3.11)

where

\psi(0)=\left(\kappa^{0},\kappa^{1}\right)^{\top},\;B_{\omega}=\left(0,\mathbf{1}_{\omega}\right)^{\top}
and

\mathcal{A}
is an unbounded linear operator defined on
\mathcal{H}
as in (
2.6
).

Next, we introduce a lemma that will be frequently used in what follows:

Lemma 3.1
.

[
22
]

The eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}
are given by

\Phi^{n}=\left(\sin(n\pi x),\lambda_{n}\sin(n\pi x)\right)^{\top},\quad\lambda_{n}=in\pi,\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{*},

they forms an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, the group
(S(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
and its generator can be represented as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}\langle x,\phi_{n}\rangle\phi_{n},\;x\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle S(t)x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-n^{2}t}\langle x,\phi_{n}\rangle\phi_{n},\;x\in\mathcal{H}.

Definition 3.1

(Impulse approximate Controllability)
.

System (
3.11
) is said to be impulse approximately controllable on
[0,T],
if for all desired state
\psi^{1}\in\mathcal{H},
and initial condition
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{H},
and for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\Upsilon_{\tau}\in L^{2}(\omega)
such that the mild solution
\psi(t,\Upsilon_{\tau},\psi_{0})
of (
3.11
) verifies:

\left\|\psi(T,\Upsilon_{\tau},\psi_{0})-\psi^{1}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\varepsilon.

The following lemma will be used to establish the main result of this section. We consider
G:W\longrightarrow Z
a linear bounded operator between Hilbert spaces
W
and
Z
.

Lemma 3.2
.

(see
[
6
,
7
,
12
]
)
The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\quad\overline{\operatorname{Rang}\left(G\right)}=Z,

(2)

\quad\ker\left(G^{*}\right)=\{0\}

(3)

\left\langle GG^{*}v,v\right\rangle>0,\quad v\neq 0\quad\text{in}\,\,Z,

(4)

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\alpha\left(\alpha I+GG^{*}\right)^{-1}v=0.

\forall v\in Z.

where
G^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
G
. Now, we are in a position to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\omega=\Omega
, the system (
3.9
) is approximate impulse controllable at any time
T>0.

If
\omega\varsubsetneq\Omega
and
\{\Phi^{1},\Phi^{2},\ldots,\Phi^{N}\},\,N\in\rm I\hskip-2.0ptN,
forms a finite basis of
\mathcal{H}
, the system (
3.9
) is approximate impulse controllable at any time
T>0
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.2
, see also
[
5
, Theorem 2.43, p.56]
, the approximate impulse controllability of the system (
3.11
) at
T>\tau,
returns to the following uniqueness result for the adjoint problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vartheta_{t}=-\mathcal{A}\vartheta\\
B_{\omega}^{*}\vartheta(x,\tau)=0\quad\text{ a.e}\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega\end{array}\right\}\Longrightarrow\vartheta\equiv 0.

(3.12)

Indeed, we shall distinguish two cases:
\omega=\Omega;

\omega\varsubsetneq\Omega.

\blacktriangleright
Case 1:
For
\omega=\Omega,

the solution of the adjoint problem with the following initial condition

\vartheta_{0}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}a_{k}\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}(\Omega),

is given by

\vartheta(x,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z^{*}}}a_{k}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k}(x).

We assume that

B_{\omega}^{*}\vartheta(x,\tau)=0,\quad\text{ a.e}\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega

that is

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z^{*}}}a_{k}e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k}(x)=0,\quad\text{a.e}\quad\text{in}\quad\omega.

(3.13)

then

\left\|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z^{*}}}a_{k}e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k}(.)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}(\Omega)}=0,

since
(\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k})_{k\in{\bf Z}^{*}}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}(\Omega)
, we get

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z^{*}}}\left|a_{k}\right|^{2}\left|e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\right|^{2}=0,

thus

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z^{*}}}\left|a_{k}\right|^{2}=0,

therefore, we obtain
a_{k}=0
, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z^{*}},

which prove that

\vartheta\equiv 0.

\blacktriangleright
Case 2:
For
\omega\varsubsetneq\Omega,
and
\{\Phi^{1},\Phi^{2},\ldots,\Phi^{N}\}
, form a finite basis of
H
, the solution of the adjoint problem with the following initial condition:

\vartheta_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k},

is given by

\vartheta(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}\lambda_{k}\Phi^{k}(x).

We assume that

\displaystyle B_{\omega}^{*}\,\vartheta(x,\tau)=0\quad\text{a.e}\quad\Omega,

that is

\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}\,e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\sin(k\pi x)=0\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega.

(3.14)

We recall that the classic Chebychev polynomials of second species are given by the following relation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}U_{0}=1,\;U_{1}=2X,\\
\\
U_{n+1}=2XU_{n}-U_{n-1},\quad\forall n\geq 1.\\
\end{array}\right.

Polynomials
(U_{n})
can be defined alternatively by trigonometric forms of their associated polynomial functions on
\omega.
Indeed, for every
n\in\rm I\hskip-2.0ptN^{*}
we have

\sin(n\pi x)=\sin(\pi x)U_{n-1}(\cos(\pi x)),\quad\forall x\in\omega.

Therefore the assumption
(
3.14
)
, becomes

\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}\,e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\sin(\pi x)U_{k-1}(\cos(\pi x))=0,\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega.

(3.15)

Hence, it is enough to prove the following implication:

\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\sin(\pi x)U_{k-1}(\cos(\pi x))=0\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega\Longrightarrow a_{k}=0\quad k=1,2,...,N.

(3.16)

For
N=1
the implication (
3.16
) is clearly verified. Now, we assume that it is true for
n<N,
and we prove it for
n+1
. By taking

\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\sin(\pi x)U_{k-1}(\cos(\pi x))=0\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega.

(3.17)

since
\sin(\pi x)\neq 0
, for all
x\in\omega
, we obtain that

a_{1}\lambda_{1}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{1}\tau}+...+a_{n+1}\lambda_{n+1}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{n+1}\tau}U_{n}(\cos(\pi x))=0,\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega.

(3.18)

we compute the derivative of (
3.18
), we obtain that

a_{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}\tau}\pi U_{1}^{(1)}(\cos(\pi x))+\ldots+a_{n+1}\lambda_{n+1}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{n+1}\tau}\pi U_{n}^{(1)}(\cos(\pi x))=0,\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega,

(3.19)

again, if we compute the derivative of the previous formula (
3.19
), we obtain that

a_{3}\lambda_{3}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{3}\tau}\pi^{2}U_{2}^{(2)}(\cos(\pi x))+...+a_{n+1}\lambda_{n+1}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{n+1}\tau}\pi^{2}U_{n}^{(1)}(\cos(\pi x))=0\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega,

we continue in the same manner. Finally, at the n-
\mathrm{th}
step, we get

a_{n+1}\lambda_{n+1}^{2}e^{-\lambda_{n+1}\tau}\pi^{n}U_{n}^{(n)}(\cos(\pi x))=0\quad\text{a.e}\quad\omega,

since
U_{n}^{(n)}(\cos(\pi x))\in\mathbb{R}^{*},
then
a_{n+1}=0.

This ends to the desired implication.
∎

4.
Impulse Observability Inequality for (
3.9
)

In this section we make use of the strategy presented by Lions
[
13
]
to obtain an observation estimate at one instant of time
\tau=2,
(
0<\tau<T)
for the impulsive wave equation (
3.9
).

For
(\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1})\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\times C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),
the homogeneous problem associated to (
3.9
) is as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(x,t)}{\partial{t}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi(x,t)}{\partial{x}^{2}}=0,&\text{in}\,\,\,\,\Omega\,\times(0,T),\\
\Phi(0,t)=\Phi(1,t)=0,&\text{on}\,\,\,(0,T),\\
\Phi(x,0)=\Phi^{0}(x),\frac{\partial\Phi(x,0)}{\partial{t}}=\Phi^{1}(x),&\text{in}\,\,\,\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(4.20)

System (
4.20
) admits a unique solution expressed in Fourier series as

\Phi(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_{n}\cos(n\pi t)+\frac{b_{n}}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi t)\right)\sin(n\pi x),

where

\Phi^{0}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\sin(n\pi x)\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{1}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\sin(n\pi x),

(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
are the coefficients of Fourier in the orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)

\theta_{n}(x)=\sin(n\pi x),\quad n=1,2,\ldots.

Next, we consider the following backward problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(x,t)}{\partial{t}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(x,t)}{\partial{x}^{2}}=0,&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega\,\times(0,T)\backslash\left\{\tau\right\},\\
\Psi(x,\tau^{-})=\Psi(x,\tau^{+}),&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,&\\
\frac{\partial\Psi(x,\tau^{+})}{\partial{t}}-\frac{\partial\Psi(x,\tau^{-})}{\partial{t}}=-\mathbf{1}_{\omega}\Phi_{t}(x,\tau)\delta_{\tau},&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega,\\
\Psi(0,t)=\Psi(1,t)=0,&\text{ on }\,\,\,\,(0,T),\\
\Psi(T)=\Psi^{{}^{\prime}}(T)=0,&\text{ in }\,\,\,\,\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(4.21)

We can easily check that this system is well-posed, by a simple calculation we obtain

\int\int_{Q}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(x,t)}{\partial{t}^{2}}\Phi(x,t)dxdt-\int\int_{Q}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(x,t)}{\partial{x}^{2}}\Phi(x,t)dxdt=-\int\int_{Q}\mathbf{1}_{\omega}\Phi_{t}^{2}(x,\tau)dxdt,

with
Q=\Omega\times(0.T).

A double integration by parts yields

T\int_{\omega}\Phi_{t}^{2}(x,\tau)dx=\int_{\Omega}\Phi^{0}\Psi^{{}^{\prime}}(0)-\Psi(0)\Phi^{1}dx,

we define the operator
\Lambda
as follows:

\Lambda(\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1})=(\Psi^{{}^{\prime}}(0),-\Psi(0))\quad\forall(\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1})\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\times C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),

then,

T\int_{\omega}(\Phi_{t}(x,\tau)^{2}dx=<\Lambda(\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1}),(\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1})>_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}.

For
(\Phi^{0},\Phi^{1})\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\times C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),
we define the semi-norm

(4.22)

Indeed, we shall distinguish two cases:
\omega=\Omega;

\omega\varsubsetneq\Omega.

\blacktriangleright

For

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mid\Phi_{t}(x,\tau)\mid^{2}dx&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(n\pi\,a_{n}\cos(n\pi\tau)+b_{n}\sin(n\pi\tau)\right)^{2}\\[14.22636pt]

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\phi_{1}\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_{n}^{2}+\frac{b_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}\pi^{2}}\right).\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle|\sin(n\pi\tau+y_{n})|\geq c\quad\forall n\in\rm I\hskip-2.0ptN^{*},\end{array}

(4.23)

since
\tau=2,

4.23

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle|\sin(y_{n})|\geq c\quad\forall n\in\rm I\hskip-2.0ptN^{*},\end{array}

(4.24)

this inequality is false for all
y_{n}.

y_{n},

5.
Conclusion and Open problems

Impulse control is a very weak control function, it acts only at one instant of time, and the problem of the wave equation with impulse control on a subdomain
\omega
, without any geometrical conditions on the space
\Omega
is very challenging. In this paper, we made an analysis of this problem from different angles and we obtained some interesting results. But there are still several open questions that we have not been able to answer like the problem of controllability of the system (
3.9
), in a case
\omega\varsubsetneq\Omega,
in infinite dimension. The novelty in this work is that the literature on impulse control systems is very small, there are very few numbers of works on systems with impulse control, to our knowledge wave equation with impulse control have not been studied yet.
