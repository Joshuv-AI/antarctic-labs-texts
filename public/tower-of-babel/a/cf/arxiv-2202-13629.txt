1.
Introduction

The singularities of the viscosity solutions of Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(HJ
s
)

H(x,Du(x),u(x))=0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

characterize the focus and crossing of associated characteristics. The occurrence of singularities of the viscosity solutions of (
HJs
) provide useful information of the complicated dynamical behavior of the underlying Hamiltonian systems as well as the invertibility of the associated Lax-Oleinik evolution from the level of functional analysis. This is closely related to many problems from mathematical physics. The readers can refer to
[
5
]
to a survey of this topic.

This paper is devoted to some finer analysis of the propagation of singularities involving the notion of singular characteristics. If the Hamiltonian
H(x,p,u)
is convex in
p
-variable, the semiconcavity is the so called
maximal regularity
for the viscosity solutions of (
HJs
). Throughout this paper, we suppose
H(x,p,u):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
C^{1}
and satisfies

(H1)

H(x,\cdot,u)
is strictly convex for all
(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
.

(H2)

\Omega
is a connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally semiconcave (viscosity) solution of (
HJs
).

The notion of singular characteristics for Hamilton-Jacobi equations comes from early work by Albano and Cannarsa (
[
1
]
). The name
generalized characteristics
was coined in the paper
[
1
]
after similar notion introduced by Dafermos
[
11
]
in hyberbolic conservation law. Recall that a Lipschitz arc
\mathbf{x}:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
generalized characteristic
starting from
x
for the pair
(H,u)
if it satisfies the following:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{x}}(s)\in\mathrm{co}\,H_{p}\big(\mathbf{x}(s),D^{+}u(\mathbf{x}(s)),u(\mathbf{x}(s))\big)&\quad\text{a.e.}\;s\in[0,\tau],\\
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(0)=x.&\end{cases}

The local structure of singular (generalized) characteristics was further investigated by Cannarsa and Yu in
[
9
]
, where
singular characteristics
were proved more regular near the starting point than the arcs constructed in
[
1
]
. For any weak KAM solution
u
of (
HJs
), the class of
intrinsic singular characteristics
was constructed in
[
3
]
by Cannarsa and the first author of this paper, using the positive type Lax-Oleinik semi-group.

However, due to convex hull in the right side of (
1.1
), there is no uniqueness result for the singular characteristics except for the well known systems of generalized gradients (
[
2
]
). Recent significant progress in the attempt to develop a more restrictive notion of singular characteristics is due to Khanin and Sobolevski (
[
12
]
). In this paper, we will call such curves
strict singular characteristic
but in the literature they are also refereed to as
broken characteristics
, see
[
13
,
14
]
. We now proceed to recall their definition: given a semiconcave solution
u
of (
HJs
), a Lipschitz singular curve
\mathbf{x}:[0,T]\to\Omega
is called a strict singular characteristic from
x\in\mbox{\rm Sing}\,(u)
if there exists a measurable selection
p(t)\in D^{+}u(\mathbf{x}(t))
such that

(1.2)

\begin{split}\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=H_{p}(\mathbf{x}(t),p(t),u(\mathbf{x}(t)))&a.e.\ t\in[0,T],\\
\mathbf{x}(0)=x.&\end{cases}\end{split}

As proved in
[
12
]
, there exists a solution of (
1.2
) (for a time dependent Hamiltonian
H(t,x,p)
). Moreover, some additional regularity properties of such curves were established, including

–

the right-differentiability of
\mathbf{x}
for every
t
,

–

the right-continuity of
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
,

–

the fact that
p(\cdot):[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
p(t)=\arg\min_{p\in D^{+}_{x}u(t,\mathbf{x}(t))}H(t,\mathbf{x}(t),p)
.

First general result on the uniqueness of the strict singular characteristics was obtained in
[
4
]
for the classical Tonelli-type Hamiltonian
H(x,p)
and semiconcave solution of (
HJs
) in two dimensional case. More precisely, if the initial point
x\in\mbox{\rm Sing}\,(u)
is not a critical point, i.e.,
0\not\in H_{p}(x,D^{+}u(x))
, then

–

any two singular characteristics are identical up to a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism,

–

there exists a unique strict singular characteristic from
x
.

However, known proofs of the existence of strict singular characteristics still look rather mysterious (see
[
12
]
and the appendix of
[
4
]
). In our previous paper
[
10
]
, we considered the problem in a more intuitive way. For a Cauchy problem

(HJ
e
)

\left\{\begin{split}D_{t}u(t,x)+H(t,x,D_{x}u(t,x),u(t,x))=&\,0\\
u(0,x)=&\,u_{0}(x)\end{split}\right.\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t>0,

with
u_{0}\in C^{2}
, we proved the local existence of smooth strict singular characteristics from non-conjugate singular point of the solution
u(t,x)
of (
HJe
), when
n=1
or
n>1
with some degenerate initial condition. Recall that any viscosity solution of (
HJs
) or (
HJe
) can be regarded as the value function of a Herglotz-type variational problem introduced in
[
7
]
and
[
6
]
(see also
[
15
]
for a representation formula by an implicit variational principle). So, one can define the conjugate point when the initial data are of class
C^{2}
(see
[
10
]
), which does not work for general initial data. In this paper, we will deal with the general case mainly on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for the stationary equation (
HJs
).

Since condition (H1), given
x\in\Omega
, there exists a unique
p^{\#}(x)\in D^{+}u(x)
such that

\displaystyle H(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))\leqslant H(x,p,u(x)),\qquad\forall p\in D^{+}u(x).

We denote
v^{\#}(x)=H_{p}(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))
.

Now, we can formulate our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
n=2
, then for any
x_{0}\in\Sigma(u)
, there exists
t_{0}>0
and a Lipschitz curve
\gamma:[0,t_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\gamma(0)=x_{0}
such that

(1)

\dot{\gamma}^{+}(t)=v^{\#}(\gamma(t)),\ \forall t\in[0,t_{0})
.

(2)

\lim_{s\to t^{+}}v^{\#}(\gamma(s))=v^{\#}(\gamma(t)),\ \forall t\in[0,t_{0})
.

(3)

\gamma(t)\in\Sigma(u),\ \forall t\in[0,t_{0}]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we introduce necessary material on Hamilton-Jacobi equations, viscosity solutions and semiconcavity. In section 3, we proved the main result of this paper. There is an appendix which is composed of some technical materials on the reparameterization of Lipschitz curves.

2.
Semiconcave function and viscosity solution

2.1.
Semiconcave function

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set. We recall that a function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
semiconcave
(with linear modulus) if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(2.1)

\lambda u(x)+(1-\lambda)u(y)-u(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leqslant\frac{C}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)|x-y|^{2}

for any
x,y\in\Omega
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Any constant
C
that satisfies the above inequality is called a
semiconcavity constant
for
u
in
\Omega
. A function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
locally semiconcave
if for each
x\in\Omega
there exists an open ball
B(x,r)\subset\Omega
such that
u
is a semiconcave function on
B(x,r)
.

For any continuous function
u:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and for any
x\in\Omega
, the closed convex sets

\displaystyle D^{-}u(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\liminf_{y\to x}\frac{u(y)-u(x)-\langle p,y-x\rangle}{|y-x|}\geqslant 0\right\},

\displaystyle D^{+}u(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{u(y)-u(x)-\langle p,y-x\rangle}{|y-x|}\leqslant 0\right\}.

are called the
subdifferential
and
superdifferential
of
u
at
x
, respectively. If
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz, a vector
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a
reachable
(or
limiting
)
gradient
of
u
at
x
if there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subset\Omega\setminus\{x\}
, converging to
x
, such that
u
is differentiable at
x_{k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

\lim_{k\to\infty}Du(x_{k})=p.

The set of all reachable gradients of
u
at
x
is denoted by
D^{\ast}u(x)
.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
u:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a semiconcave function and let
x\in\Omega
. Then the following properties hold.

(a)

D^{+}u(x)
is a nonempty compact convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
D^{\ast}u(x)\subset\partial D^{+}u(x)
, where
\partial D^{+}u(x)
denotes the topological boundary of
D^{+}u(x)
.

(b)

The set-valued function
x\rightsquigarrow D^{+}u(x)
is upper semicontinuous.

(c)

If
D^{+}u(x)
is a singleton, then
u
is differentiable at
x
. Moreover, if
D^{+}u(x)
is a singleton for every point in
\Omega
, then
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
.

(d)

D^{+}u(x)=\mathrm{co}\,D^{\ast}u(x)
.

We call
\Sigma(u)
the
singular set
of a semiconcave function
u
, that is
x\in\Sigma(u)
if
D^{+}u(x)
is not a singleton.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. If there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for any
x\in\Omega
, there exists
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(2.2)

u(y)\leqslant u(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+\frac{C}{2}|y-x|^{2},\quad\forall y\in\Omega,

then
u
is semiconcave with constant
C
and
p\in D^{+}u(x)
.
Conversely,
if
u
is semiconcave in
\Omega
with constant
C
, then (
2.2
) holds for any
x\in\Omega
and
p\in D^{+}u(x)
.

2.2.
Viscosity solution and its superdiffferential

Since our convexity assumption (H1), instead of the standard definition, we say
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
viscosity solution
of (
HJs
) if
u
is locally semiconcave and satisfies (
HJs
) almost everywhere. Comparing to item (d) of Proposition
2.1
, we have the following structural result of
D^{+}u(x)
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
8
]
)
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm ext}\,(D^{+}u(x))=D^{*}u(x)=\{p\in D^{+}u(x):H(x,p,u(x))=0\},

where
\mbox{\rm ext}\,C
stands for the set of extremal points with respect to the convex set
C
.

Let us recall some facts from convex analysis. For any non-empty convex compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)=\{x\in K:\langle x,\theta\rangle\leqslant\langle y,\theta\rangle,\ \forall y\in K\}.

Obviously,
\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,0)=K
, and we have
\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)=\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\lambda\theta)
when
\lambda>0
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a non-empty convex compact set and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then we have

(1)

\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
is non-empty, convex and compact.

(2)

If
x\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
,
x_{1},x_{2}\in K
,
x\in(x_{1},x_{2})
, then
[x_{1},x_{2}]\subset\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
.

(3)

If
\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)=x
is a singleton, then
x\in\mbox{\rm ext}\,(K)
.

Proof.

The proof of (1) is trivial. Since
x\in(x_{1},x_{2})
, there exists
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
x=\lambda x_{1}+(1-\lambda)x_{2}
, that is,
\lambda(x-x_{1})=(1-\lambda)(x_{2}-x)
. Recall
x\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
implies
\langle x-x_{1},\theta\rangle\leqslant 0
. On the other hand,
\langle x-x_{1},\theta\rangle=\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}\langle x_{2}-x,\theta\rangle\geqslant 0
. Thus, we have
\langle x-x_{1},\theta\rangle=0
, which implies
x_{1}\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
. Similarly, we also have
x_{2}\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
. Notice that
\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
is convex, by (1). We conclude that
[x_{1},x_{2}]\subset\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,\theta)
. This completes of proof of (2). Item (3) follows directly from (2).
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a non-empty convex compact set, and
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
is strictly convex. Then

(1)

There exists a unique
x\in K
such that
f(x)\leqslant f(y)
for all
y\in K
, and we have
x\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,Df(x))
.

(2)

If
x\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(K,Df(x))
, then
f(x)\leqslant f(y)
for all
y\in K
.

The following Lemma is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.5
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(1)

There exists a unique
p^{\#}(x)\in D^{+}u(x)
such that

\displaystyle H(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))\leqslant H(x,p,u(x)),\qquad\forall p\in D^{+}u(x).

(2)

Denote
v^{\#}(x)=H_{p}(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))
, then
p^{\#}(x)\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(D^{+}u(x),v^{\#}(x))
.

(3)

If
p\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(D^{+}u(x),H_{p}(x,p,u(x)))
, then
p=p^{\#}(x)
.

(4)

If
x\in\Sigma(u)
, then
H(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))<0
and
p^{\#}(x)\in D^{+}u(x)\setminus D^{*}u(x)
.

3.
Proof of the main result

3.1.
A bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
, and let

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(x,p,w)

\displaystyle=H(x,p+Df(x),w+f(x)),\qquad(x,p,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle w(x)

\displaystyle=u(x)-f(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then we have

(1)

\tilde{H}
is of class
C^{1}
and satisfies
(H1).
w
is locally semiconcave on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and we have

\displaystyle D^{+}w(x)=D^{+}u(x)-Df(x),\ D^{*}w(x)=D^{*}u(x)-Df(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Moreover,
w
is a viscosity solution of

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(x,Dw(x),w(x))=0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2)

\Sigma(w)=\Sigma(u)
.

(3)

Let
\tilde{p},\tilde{v}
be the vectors corresponding to
p^{\#},v^{\#}
in Lemma
2.6
for
\tilde{H}
and
w
respectively, then

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(x)=p^{\#}(x)-Df(x),\ \tilde{v}(x)=v^{\#}(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

(1) and (2) follows from the
C^{2}
regularity of
f
. We only need to prove (3). For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\in D^{+}w(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(x,p^{\#}(x)-Df(x),w(x))=H(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))\leqslant H(x,p+Df(x),u(x))=\tilde{H}(x,p,w(x)).

This implies
\tilde{p}(x)=p^{\#}(x)-Df(x)
. Notice that

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{p}(x,p,w)=H_{p}(x,p+Df(x),w+f(x)),\qquad\forall(x,p,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(x)=\tilde{H}_{p}(x,\tilde{p}(x),w(x))=H_{p}(x,\tilde{p}(x)+Df(x),w(x)+f(x))=H_{p}(x,p^{\#}(x),u(x))=v^{\#}(x).

This completes the proof.
∎

Notice that the conclusion in Theorem
1.1
is local. We only need to make our proof for any bounded open convex set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x_{0}\in U
. In the following, we suppose
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded, open and convex, and
u
is linearly semiconcave on
U
with constant
C_{0}
. Let
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}(C_{0}+1)|x|^{2}
in Lemma
3.1
, then
w(x)=u(x)-\frac{1}{2}(C_{0}+1)|x|^{2}
is uniformly concave with constant
-1
. By Lemma
3.1
(2) (3), Theorem
1.1
holds for
H
and
u
if and only if it holds for
\tilde{H}
and
w
. Lemma
3.1
(1) shows
\tilde{H}
and
w
satisfy the same condition as
H
and
u
. Therefore, with out loss of generality, we always suppose that
u
is uniformly concave on
U
with constant
-1
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
8
]
)
.

(1)

For any
x_{1},x_{2}\in U
,
p_{i}\in D^{+}u(x_{i}),\ i=1,2
, we have

\displaystyle\langle p_{2}-p_{1},x_{2}-x_{1}\rangle\leqslant-|x_{2}-x_{1}|^{2}.

(2)

Set
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. If
\{x_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}
and
p_{i}\in D^{+}u(x_{i}),\ i\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}x_{i}=x,\ \lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{x_{i}-x}{|x_{i}-x|}=\theta,\ \lim_{i\to\infty}p_{i}=p\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

then
p\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(D^{+}u(x),\theta)
.

We define the map

\displaystyle\varphi:\mbox{\rm graph}\,(D^{+}u\Big|_{U})\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad(x,p)\mapsto p.

Obviously,
\varphi((x,D^{+}u(x)))=D^{+}u(x)
for all

Lemma 3.3
.

(1)

\varphi
is injective.

(2)

\varphi
is Lipschitz with constant
1
.

(3)

For any
x\in U
, there exists
\delta_{x}>0

D^{+}u(x)+B_{\delta_{x}}\subset\mbox{\rm Im}\,(\varphi)

Proof.

If
\varphi(x_{i},p)=p,\ i=1,2

. By Lemma

(1), we have

\displaystyle 0=\langle p-p,x_{2}-x_{1}\rangle\leqslant-|x_{2}-x_{1}|^{2}.

This implies
x_{1}=x_{2}
. Therefore,
\varphi
is injective. This leads to (1). Item (2) follows directly from the definition of
\varphi
.

Now, turn to prove (3). For any
x\in U
, there exists
\varepsilon_{x}>0
such that
\bar{B}_{\varepsilon_{x}}(x)\subset U
. Let
\delta_{x}=\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{x}
. For any

\displaystyle\phi_{p}(y)=u(y)-\langle p,y\rangle,\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and denote

to be the projection of

, that is,
p^{\prime}=\operatorname*{arg\ min}_{q\in D^{+}u(x)}|q-p|
. Then we have

\displaystyle\phi_{p}(y)-\phi_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=u(y)-\langle p,y\rangle-u(x)+\langle p,x\rangle

\displaystyle=u(y)-u(x)-\langle p^{\prime},y-x\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|y-x|^{2}+\langle p^{\prime}-p,y-x\rangle-\frac{1}{2}|y-x|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle p^{\prime}-p,y-x\rangle-\frac{1}{2}|y-x|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant|y-x|(|p^{\prime}-p|-\frac{1}{2}|y-x|)

\displaystyle\leqslant|y-x|(\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_{x}-\frac{1}{2}|y-x|),\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

When

. Therefore,

.
∎

.

(1)

By Lemma

\mbox{\rm dist}\,\{[p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t)],p^{\#}(x_{0})\}\leqslant\varepsilon_{2}
. Using (
3.6
), there exists a unique
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,
|t^{\prime}|\leqslant\frac{2\varepsilon_{2}}{|v^{\#}(x_{0})|}
such that
\eta(t^{\prime})
is on the line containing
[p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t)]
. Notice also that

(3.9)

|p_{i}(t)-p_{i}|<\varepsilon_{2}\leqslant\varepsilon_{1},\ |\eta(t^{\prime})-p^{\#}(x_{0})|=|t^{\prime}|\leqslant\frac{2\varepsilon_{2}}{|v^{\#}(x_{0})|}\leqslant\varepsilon_{1}.

Combing (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we obtain
\langle p_{1}(t)-\eta(t^{\prime}),p_{2}(t)-\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2}<0
. So we have
\eta(t^{\prime})\in(p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t))
. Finally, because
D^{+}u(\gamma_{1}(t))
is convex and
\tau_{2}(t)<t_{4}
, there holds
\tau_{2}(t)=\max\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}:\eta(\tau)\in D^{+}u(\gamma_{1}(t))\}
. If
t^{\prime}<\tau_{2}(t)
, then

\displaystyle\langle\eta(\tau_{2}(t))-\eta(t^{\prime}),v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(t))\rangle=(\tau_{2}(t)-t^{\prime})\left\langle-\frac{v^{\#}(x_{0})}{|v^{\#}(x_{0})|},v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(t))\right\rangle\leqslant-\frac{1}{2}(\tau_{2}(t)-t^{\prime})|v^{\#}(x_{0})|<0.

This leads to a contradiction with
\eta(t^{\prime})\in[p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t)]=\mbox{\rm epf}\,(D^{+}u(\gamma_{1}(t)),v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(t)))
. Therefore, we conclude that
t^{\prime}=\tau_{2}(t)
, and as a result,
\eta(\tau_{2}(t))\in(p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t))
.

Step 3.
Since
p^{\#}(x_{0})\in\mbox{\rm epf}\,(D^{+}u(x_{0}),v^{\#}(x_{0}))
, we obtain that
\gamma_{1}(t)\neq x_{0}
for all
t>0
. Thus, we have
l(0,t_{4})\geqslant\gamma_{1}(t_{4})-x_{0}>0
, where
l(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in Appendix with respect to
\gamma_{1}:[0,t_{4}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Now, we define the curve
\gamma_{2}:[0,l(0,t_{4})]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\gamma_{2}(s)=\gamma_{1}(t)
, where
l(0,t)=s
. Obviously,
\gamma_{2}(0)=\gamma_{1}(0)=x_{0}
,
\gamma_{2}(s)=\gamma_{1}(t)\in\Sigma(u)\cap U
for all
s\in[0,l(0,t_{4})]
, and by Lemma
A.2
,
\gamma_{2}
is Lipschitz. For any
t\in[0,t_{4}]
,
\tau_{2}(t)<t_{4}
, using (
3.6
), (
3.7
) and Lemma
3.7
(1) (3), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\tau_{2}(t)^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{1}(r)-\gamma_{1}(t)}{|\gamma_{1}(r)-\gamma_{1}(t)|}=\frac{v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(t))}{|v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(t))|},\qquad\lim_{r\to\tau_{2}(t)^{+}}v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(r))=v^{\#}(\gamma_{1}(t)).

Therefore, due to Lemma
A.3
,
\gamma_{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{+}(s)=\frac{v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(s))}{|v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(s))|},\qquad\lim_{r\to s^{+}}v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(r))=v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(s)),\qquad\forall s\in[0,l(0,t_{4})).

Since
v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\cdot))
is right-continuous, there exists
0<a<l(0,t_{4})
and
0<b_{1}\leqslant b_{2}<+\infty
such that

\displaystyle b_{1}\leqslant|v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(s))|\leqslant b_{2},\qquad\forall s\in[0,a].

Now, by Lemma
A.4
, there exists a Lipschitz function
\tau:[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\to[0,a]
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{\tau}^{+}(s)=|v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(s)))|,\qquad s\in[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}),\\
\tau(0)=0.\end{cases}

Step 4.
Finally, let
\gamma:[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\gamma(s)=\gamma_{2}(\tau(s))
. Obviously,
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{2}(0)=x_{0}
,
\gamma(s)\in\Sigma(u)\cap U
for all
s\in[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]
, and
\gamma=\gamma_{2}\circ\tau
is a Lipschitz curve. For any
s\in[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}})
, noticing that
b_{1}\leqslant\dot{\tau}^{+}(s)\leqslant b_{2}
and
v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\cdot))
is right-continuous, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}^{+}(s)=\dot{\gamma}_{2}^{+}(\tau(s))\cdot\dot{\tau}^{+}(s)=\frac{v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(s)))}{|v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(s)))|}\cdot|v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(s)))|=v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(s)))=v^{\#}(\gamma(s)),

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to s^{+}}v^{\#}(\gamma(r))=\lim_{r\to s^{+}}v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(r)))=v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\lim_{r\to s^{+}}\tau(r)))=v^{\#}(\gamma_{2}(\tau(s)))=v^{\#}(\gamma(s)).

Therefore,
\gamma:[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfies all the required properties. This completes our proof.
∎

Appendix A
rescaling of a lipschitz curve

In this section, we suppose
\tau_{0}>0
,
\gamma:[0,\tau_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Lipschitz curve with Lipschitz constant
\kappa
. For
t\in[0,\tau_{0}]
, we define

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\tau\in[0,t]:\gamma(r)=\gamma(t),\ \forall r\in[\tau,t]\},

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\tau\in[t,\tau_{0}]:\gamma(r)=\gamma(t),\ \forall r\in[t,\tau]\}.

Obviously, we have
0\leqslant\tau_{1}(t)\leqslant t\leqslant\tau_{2}(t)\leqslant\tau_{0}
. For
0\leqslant t_{1}\leqslant t_{2}\leqslant\tau_{0}
, we define the length of
\gamma\Big|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
as follows

\displaystyle l(t_{1},t_{2})=\sup\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\gamma(r_{i})-\gamma(r_{i-1})|:k\in\mathbb{N},\ t_{1}=r_{0}\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant r_{k}=t_{2}\}.

We can easily obtain
0\leqslant l(t_{1},t_{2})\leqslant\kappa(t_{2}-t_{1})
.

Lemma A.1
.

(1)

For any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,\tau_{0}]
, we have

\displaystyle[\tau_{1}(t_{1}),\tau_{2}(t_{1})]\cap[\tau_{1}(t_{2}),\tau_{2}(t_{2})]\neq\emptyset\Longleftrightarrow[\tau_{1}(t_{1}),\tau_{2}(t_{1})]=[\tau_{1}(t_{2}),\tau_{2}(t_{2})]

(2)

l(t_{1},t_{3})=l(t_{1},t_{2})+l(t_{2},t_{3})
,
\forall 0\leqslant t_{1}\leqslant t_{2}\leqslant t_{3}\leqslant\tau_{0}
.

(3)

For any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,\tau_{0}]
, we have

\displaystyle l(0,t_{1})=l(0,t_{2})\Longleftrightarrow[\tau_{1}(t_{1}),\tau_{2}(t_{1})]=[\tau_{1}(t_{2}),\tau_{2}(t_{2})].

(4)

If
t,t_{i}\in[0,\tau_{0}]
satisfies
l(0,t_{i})>l(0,t)
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim_{i\to\infty}l(0,t_{i})=l(0,t)
, then

\displaystyle t_{i}>\tau_{2}(t),\ \forall i\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\lim_{i\to\infty}t_{i}=\tau_{2}(t).

Proof.

The proofs of (1) and (2) are immediate. To prove (3), without loss of generality, we assume
t_{1}\leqslant t_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle l(0,t_{1})=l(0,t_{2})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow l(t_{1},t_{2})=0\Longleftrightarrow\gamma(t)=\gamma(t_{1}),\ \forall t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow t_{2}\in[\tau_{1}(t_{1}),\tau_{2}(t_{1})]\Longleftrightarrow[\tau_{1}(t_{1}),\tau_{2}(t_{1})]=[\tau_{1}(t_{2}),\tau_{2}(t_{2})],

where the first equivalence is from (2) and the last is from (1).

Finally, it follows from (3) that
l(0,\tau_{2}(t))=l(0,t)<l(0,t_{i})
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies
t_{i}>\tau_{2}(t)
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
. If there exists
\tilde{t}>\tau_{2}(t)
and a subsequence
\{t_{i_{k}}\}
of
\{t_{i}\}
such that
t_{i_{k}}\geqslant\tilde{t}
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
, then by (3) we have
l(t,\tilde{t})=l(0,\tilde{t})-l(0,t)>0
and

\displaystyle l(0,t_{i_{k}})\geqslant l(0,\tilde{t})=l(0,t)+l(t,\tilde{t}),\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},

which leads to a contradiction to
\lim_{i\to\infty}l(0,t_{i})=l(0,t)
. Therefore, we have
\lim_{i\to\infty}t_{i}=\tau_{2}(t)
. This completes the proof of (4).
∎

Now, we define a curve
\gamma_{l}:[0,l(0,\tau_{0})]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\gamma_{l}(s)=\gamma(t)
, where
t\in[0,\tau_{0}]
satisfies
l(0,t)=s
. Due to Lemma
A.1
(3), the curve
\gamma_{l}
is well defined.

Lemma A.2
.

\gamma_{l}
is a Lipschitz curve with constant 1 and satisfies

(A.1)

|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|=1,\qquad a.e.\ s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0})].

Proof.

For any
0\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant s_{2}\leqslant l(0,\tau_{0})
, there holds

\displaystyle|\gamma_{l}(s_{2})-\gamma_{l}(s_{1})|=|\gamma(t_{2})-\gamma(t_{1})|\leqslant l(t_{1},t_{2})=l(0,t_{2})-l(0,t_{1})=s_{2}-s_{1},

where
t_{i}\in[0,\tau_{0}]
satisfies
l(0,t_{i})=s_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Thus,
\gamma_{l}
is a Lipschitz curve with constant 1 and

(A.2)

|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|\leqslant 1,\qquad a.e.\ s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0})].

On the other hand, for any
0=t_{0}\leqslant t_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant t_{k}=\tau_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\gamma(t_{i})-\gamma(t_{i-1})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\gamma_{l}(l(0,t_{i}))-\gamma_{l}(l(0,t_{i-1}))|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\int_{l(0,t_{i-1})}^{l(0,t_{i})}\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)\ ds|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{l(0,t_{i-1})}^{l(0,t_{i})}|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|\ ds=\int_{0}^{l(0,\tau_{0})}|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|\ ds.

It follows that
l(0,\tau_{0})\leqslant\int_{0}^{l(0,\tau_{0})}|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|\ ds
, that is,
\int_{0}^{l(0,\tau_{0})}1-|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|\ ds\leqslant 0
. Combing the inequality with (
A.2
), (
A.1
) follows.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose
\gamma:[0,\tau_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Lipschitz curve,
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a map.

(1)

If for any
t\in[0,\tau_{0}]
with
\tau_{2}(t)<\tau_{0}
, there holds
\lim_{r\to\tau_{2}(t)^{+}}f(\gamma(r))=f(\gamma(t))
, then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to s+}f(\gamma_{l}(r))=f(\gamma_{l}(s)),\qquad\forall s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0})).

(2)

Under the assumption of
(1), if for any
t\in[0,\tau_{0}]
with
\tau_{2}(t)<\tau_{0}
, there holds

\lim_{r\to\tau_{2}(t)^{+}}\frac{\gamma(r)-\gamma(t)}{|\gamma(r)-\gamma(t)|}=f(\gamma(t)),

we have

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{l}^{+}(s)=f(\gamma_{l}(s)),\qquad\forall s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0})).

Proof.

Set
s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0}))
. For any
r_{i}\to s^{+}
,
i\to\infty
, choose
t,t_{i}\in[0,\tau_{0}]
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
l(0,t)=s
,
l(0,t_{i})=r_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Lemma
A.1
(4) implies
t_{i}\to\tau_{2}(t)^{+}
,
i\to\infty
. Thus,

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}f(\gamma_{l}(r_{i}))=\lim_{i\to\infty}f(\gamma(t_{i}))=f(\gamma(t))=f(\gamma_{l}(s)).

It follows that
\lim_{r\to s+}f(\gamma_{l}(r))=f(\gamma_{l}(s))
, and (1) holds.

Now, we turn to the proof of (2). First, for almost all
s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0}))
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)=\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{\gamma_{l}(r)-\gamma_{l}(s)}{r-s}=\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{\gamma(t_{r})-\gamma(t)}{|\gamma(t_{r})-\gamma(t)|}\cdot\frac{|\gamma_{l}(r)-\gamma_{l}(s)|}{r-s},

where
t_{r}
and
t
satisfy
l(0,t_{r})=r
and
l(0,t)=s
. Lemma
A.2
implies

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{|\gamma_{l}(r)-\gamma_{l}(s)|}{r-s}=|\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)|=1.

It follows from Lemma
A.1
(4) that

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{\gamma(t_{r})-\gamma(t)}{|\gamma(t_{r})-\gamma(t)|}=\lim_{r\to\tau_{2}(t)^{+}}\frac{\gamma(r)-\gamma(t)}{|\gamma(r)-\gamma(t)|}=f(\gamma(t))=f(\gamma_{l}(s)).

Therefore,
\dot{\gamma}_{l}(s)=f(\gamma_{l}(s))
. Now, for any
s\in[0,l(0,\tau_{0}))
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}^{+}_{l}(s)=\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{\gamma_{l}(r)-\gamma_{l}(s)}{r-s}=\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{1}{r-s}\int_{s}^{r}\dot{\gamma}_{l}(\tau)\ d\tau=\lim_{r\to s+}\frac{1}{r-s}\int_{s}^{r}f(\gamma_{l}(\tau))\ d\tau=f(\gamma_{l}(s)),

where the last equality is from (1).
∎

We say a function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
b
-increasing
with
b>0
if

\displaystyle g(t_{2})\geqslant g(t_{1})+b(t_{2}-t_{1}),\qquad\forall t_{1}\leqslant t_{2}.

Lemma A.4
.

Suppose
a>0
,
0<b_{1}\leqslant b_{2}<+\infty
,
f:[0,a]\to[b_{1},b_{2}]
is right-continuous. Then there exists a Lipschitz function
x:[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\to\mathbb{R}
such that it is a solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}^{+}(t)=f(x(t)),\qquad t\in[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}})\\
x(0)=0\end{cases}

Proof.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
x_{m}:[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle x_{m}(t)=\begin{cases}f(0)t,\qquad t\in[0,\frac{1}{m}\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\\
x_{m}(\frac{i-1}{m}\frac{a}{b_{2}})+f(x_{m}(\frac{i-1}{m}\frac{a}{b_{2}}))(t-\frac{i-1}{m}\frac{a}{b_{2}}),\qquad t\in[\frac{i-1}{m}\frac{a}{b_{2}},\frac{i}{m}\frac{a}{b_{2}}],2\leqslant i\leqslant m.\end{cases}

It is easy to verify that
x_{m}
are all
b_{1}
-increasing,
b_{2}
-Lipschitz functions on
[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]
, and
x_{m}(0)=0
. By Alzela-Ascoli theorem, there exists a subsequence
\{x_{m_{k}}\}
and an
x:[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
x_{m_{k}}
converges uniformly to
x
on
[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]
.
x
is also
b_{1}
-increasing,
b_{2}
-Lipschitz on
[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}]
, and
x(0)=0
. So we have

\displaystyle 0\leqslant x(t)<a,\qquad\forall t\in[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}}).

Fix any
t\in[0,\frac{a}{b_{2}})
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, by the right-continuity of
f
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(x(t))|<\varepsilon,\qquad\forall x(t)\leqslant y\leqslant x(t)+\delta.

Since
x_{m_{k}}
converges uniformly to
x
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|x_{m_{k}}-x\|<\frac{1}{2}\delta
for all
k\geqslant n_{0}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle x_{m_{k}}(s)

\displaystyle<x(s)+\frac{1}{2}\delta\leqslant x(t)+(s-t)b_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta\leqslant x(t)+\frac{\delta}{2b_{2}}b_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta

\displaystyle=x(t)+\delta,\qquad\forall k\geqslant n_{0},t\leqslant s\leqslant t+\frac{\delta}{2b_{2}}.

Furthermore, choosing any
t<\tilde{t}<s\leqslant t+\frac{\delta}{2b_{2}}
, by the uniform convergence of
x_{m_{k}}
, there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|x_{m_{k}}-x\|<\frac{b_{1}}{2}(\tilde{t}-t)
for all
k\geqslant n_{1}
. Therefore, when
k\geqslant\max\{n_{0},n_{1}\}
,
\frac{t+\tilde{t}}{2}\leqslant\tau\leqslant s
, we have

\displaystyle x_{m_{k}}(\tau)\geqslant x_{m_{k}}\left(\frac{t+\tilde{t}}{2}\right)>x\left(\frac{t+\tilde{t}}{2}\right)-\frac{b_{1}}{2}(\tilde{t}-t)\geqslant x(t)+\frac{b_{1}}{2}(\tilde{t}-t)-\frac{b_{1}}{2}(\tilde{t}-t)=x(t),

and
x_{m_{k}}(\tau)\leqslant x(t)+\delta
. This implies
|f(x_{m_{k}}(\tau))-f(x(t))|<\varepsilon
. Combing this with the definition of
x_{m}
, it is not hard to obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{x(s)-x(\tilde{t})}{s-\tilde{t}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{x_{m_{k}}(s)-x_{m_{k}}(\tilde{t})}{s-\tilde{t}}\in(f(x(t))-\varepsilon,f(x(t))+\varepsilon).

Since
\tilde{t}
is arbitrary, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{x(s)-x(t)}{s-t}=\lim_{\tilde{t}\to t^{+}}\frac{x(s)-x(\tilde{t})}{s-\tilde{t}}\in(f(x(t))-\varepsilon,f(x(t))+\varepsilon).

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{+}(t)=\lim_{s\to t^{+}}\frac{x(s)-x(t)}{s-t}=f(x(t)).

This completes the proof.
∎
