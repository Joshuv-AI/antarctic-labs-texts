Abstract
. We discuss how to resolve generic skew-symmetric and generic symmetric determinantal singularities. The key ingredients are (skew-) symmetry preserving matrix operations in order to deduce an inductive argument.

1.
Introduction

The class of determinantal singularities generalizes the class of complete intersections and carries more structure than arbitrary singularities because of the underlying matrix structure.
For references providing more on the background of determinantal singularities, we refer to
the survey of Frühbis-Krüger and Zach
[
FKZ
]
, the book of Bruns and Vetter
[
BV
]
, or the book of Harris

[
Ha
, Lecture 9]
.
More specialized approaches are the studies of symmetric determinantal singularities, as in the articles of Gaffney and Molino
[
GM1
]
and
[
GM2
]
or the studies of skew-symmetric determinantal singularities by Bruce, Goryunov and Haslinger
[
BGH
]
,
for example.

In this article, we focus on resolution of generic skew-symmetric resp. generic symmetric determinantal singularities.
A local variant of resolution via blowing ups of regular centers of more general determinantal singularities, namely such determinantal singularities for which there exists a procedure which simultaneous locally monomializes the initial entries (e.g. binomial entries), will be discussed in
[
Gau2
]
, see also
[
Gau1
]
.

We fix positive integers
m,r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
with
r\leq m
.
Let
R_{0}
be a commutative regular ring
(e.g.,
R_{0}={\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{F}}_{q},{\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{Z}}[T]_{\langle 2,T\rangle},\ldots
)
and let

R:=R_{0}[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\leq m]

be the polynomial ring with
m^{2}
independent variables and coefficients in
R_{0}
.
Consider a generic square matrix
(In fact, more generally, one may take matrices into account that are not necessarily square matrices.
Since the (skew-) symmetric setting is guiding us to consider square matrices, we neglect the mentioned more general situation here.)

M_{m}:=(x_{i,j})_{i,j}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,2}&\cdots&x_{1,m}\\
x_{2,1}&x_{2,2}&\cdots&x_{2,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{m,1}&x_{m,2}&\cdots&x_{m,m}\end{pmatrix}.

In
[
Sch
, Section 5]
, the second named author discussed how to resolve the singularities of the
generic determinantal singularity
X_{m,r}
that is defined by the vanishing of all
r
-minors of
M_{m}
,
i.e.,
X_{m,r}:=\operatorname{Spec}(R/{\mathcal{J}}_{m,r})
for

{\mathcal{J}}_{m,r}:=\langle f_{I,J}\mid I,J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}:\#I=\#J=r\rangle\subset R,

where

f_{I,J}:=\det(M_{I,J}),
for
I,J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\},\#I=\#J=r
and where
M_{I,J}:=(x_{i,j})_{i\in I,j\in J}
denotes the
r\times r
submatrix of
M_{m}
determined by
I
and
J
.
This generalized a result by Vainsencher
[
Vai
]
who provided a desingularization for generic determinantal singularities if
R_{0}
contains an algebraic closed field.

It is a natural question to ask whether a desingularization exists for non-generic determinantal singularities which are equipped with some additional structure, i.e., where the entries
x_{i,j}
are replaced by more general elements in
R
.
Furthermore, it is an interesting problem to investigate to what extent the determinantal structure resp. the geometry of the entries contribute to the singularity and its resolution.
If
R_{0}
contains a field of characteristic zero, there exists a resolution by Hironaka
[
Hi
]
.
First constructive proofs have been given by Bierstone and Milman
[
BM
]
and by Villamayor
[
V1
,
V2
]
;
we also refer to
[
Cut
,
EV
,
Kol
]
, for example.
We allow
R_{0}
to be of positive and mixed characteristics.
Hence, the question on the existence of a desingularization is neither known nor clear, in general.

In the present article, we provide an affirmative answer to this in the generic symmetric and generic skew-symmetric setup, where we consider the generic matrix with additional relations
x_{i,j}=x_{j,i}
resp.
x_{i,j}=-x_{j,i}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
A different approach for not necessarily generic entries in the matrix will appear in
[
Gau2
]
, where the determinantal structure takes an essential role, see also
[
Gau1
]
.

Let us fix some notation in order to formulate our main result in the skew-symmetric case.
First, assume
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})\neq 2
. Then
x_{i,j}=-x_{j,i}
implies for
i=j
that
x_{i,i}=0
.
Hence we introduce

I_{\rm skew}:=\langle x_{i,j}+x_{j,i},\,x_{k,k}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq m,1\leq k\leq m\rangle

and define

S_{\rm skew}:=R/I_{\rm skew},\hskip 10.0ptZ_{\rm skew}:=\operatorname{Spec}(S_{\rm skew}),\hskip 10.0pt{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm skew}_{m,r}:={\mathcal{J}}_{m,r}+I_{\rm skew},

Y^{\rm skew}_{m,r}:=\operatorname{Spec}(R/{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm skew}_{m,r})\subseteq Z_{\rm skew}.

In other words,
if
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})\neq 2
,
then

Y^{\rm skew}_{m,r}
is defined by the vanishing locus of the
r
-minors of the skew-symmetric matrix

(1.1)

A_{m}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{1,2}&\cdots&x_{1,m}\\
-x_{1,2}&0&&x_{2,m}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
-x_{1,m}&-x_{2,m}&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}.

Recall the following facts:

(F1)

Since
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})\neq 2
, it is easy to show that the determinant of every skew-symmetric matrix of odd size is identically zero.
In particular,
Y^{\rm skew}_{2\ell-1,2\ell-1}=Z_{\rm skew}
is regular for every
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

(F2)

By
[
KLS
, Theorem 3.1.(1)]
,
\sqrt{\langle 2\ell\text{-minors of }A_{m}\rangle}=\sqrt{\langle(2\ell-1)\text{-minors of }A_{m}\rangle},
where
\sqrt{I}
denotes the radical of an ideal
I
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

(F3)

The equality

\det(A)=(\operatorname{pf}(A))^{2}
holds for any skew-symmetric square matrix
A
,
where
\operatorname{pf}(A)
denotes the pfaffian of
A
.

Recall that a principal minor of a matrix is a minor for which the index set of the corresponding columns and rows coincide.
Its follows from
[
KLS
, Theorem 3.12(2) and (3)]
that we have

\sqrt{\langle 2\ell\text{-minors of }A_{m}\rangle}=\sqrt{\langle\text{principal-}2\ell\text{-minors of }A_{m}\rangle}=\sqrt{\langle\text{pfaffian of principal-}2\ell\text{-minors of }A_{m}\rangle}
.

By (F2), it is sufficient to consider only the vanishing loci determined by the
2\ell
-minors in the skew-symmetric setting.
Writing
X_{\rm red}
for the reduction of
X
,
for every
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, we have

(1.2)

(Y^{\rm skew}_{m,2\ell})_{\rm red}\cong(Y^{\rm skew}_{m,2\ell-1})_{\rm red}.

Using (F3), we could further reduce this to the vanishing loci of the pfaffian of the principal-
2\ell
-minors of
A_{m}
.
Since we will mainly work with the transform of the matrix
A_{m}
along a blowing up, we stick to the
2\ell
-minors.
For more details on pfaffians and the ideals generated by them, we refer to
[
KLS
]
.

An
embedded resolution of singularities
of a reduced scheme
Y\subset Z
with
Z
regular, is a proper, birational morphism
\pi:Z^{\prime}\to Z
, for some regular scheme
Z^{\prime}
,
such that

(a)

the strict transform
Y^{\prime}
of
Y
in
Z^{\prime}
is regular,

(b)

\pi
is an isomorphism outside of the singular locus of
Y
,
i.e.,
\pi^{-1}(Z\setminus\operatorname{Sing}(Y))\cong Z\setminus\operatorname{Sing}(Y)
,

(c)

\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{Sing}(Y))
is a simple normal crossings divisor
(i.e., all irreducible components are regular and they intersect transversally)
which intersects
Y^{\prime}
transversally.

One way to achieve this is to construct a finite sequence of blowing ups in regular centers contained in the singular locus of the strict transform of
Y
.

It is necessary to be careful and to work in the reduced setting.
For example,
Y^{\rm skew}_{2,2}
is defined by the vanishing of

\det\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{1,2}\\
-x_{1,2}&0\end{pmatrix}=x_{1,2}^{2}
.
Hence,
Y^{\rm skew}_{2,2}
is
non reduced, which implies that no blowing up in a regular center improves the singularities, while on the other hand its reduction is regular.

Theorem A
.

Let
m,\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
be positive integers with
2\ell\leq m
,
let
R_{0}
be a commutative regular ring with
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})\neq 2
.
The following sequence of blowing ups is an embedded resolution of singularities for the reduction of the generic skew-symmetric determinantal singularity
Y^{\rm skew}_{m,2\ell}\subset Z_{\rm skew}
,

Z_{\rm skew}=:Z_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{1}}}{{\longleftarrow}}Z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\pi_{2}}}}{{\longleftarrow}}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{{\ell-1}}}}{{\longleftarrow}}Z_{\ell-1},

where
\pi_{\alpha}\colon Z_{\alpha}\to Z_{\alpha-1}
is the blowing up with center the strict transform of
{(Y^{\rm skew}_{m,2\alpha})_{\rm red}}\cong{(Y^{\rm skew}_{m,2\alpha-1})_{\rm red}}
in
Z_{\alpha-1}
,
for
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
.

The situation changes slightly if we move on to
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})=2
.
The relation
x_{i,j}=-x_{i,j}
becomes
x_{i,j}=x_{j,i}
.
In particular, for
i=j
the relation
x_{i,j}=-x_{j,i}
does not imply
x_{i,i}=0
.
So if
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})=2
, the skew-symmetric generic case naturally leads us to the generic symmetric case.

Let us introduce the symmetric setup.
Let
R_{0}
be a regular ring of arbitrary characteristic.
We define

I_{\rm sym}:=\langle x_{i,j}-x_{j,i}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq m\rangle,

S_{\rm sym}:=R/I_{\rm sym},\hskip 10.0ptZ_{\rm sym}:=\operatorname{Spec}(S_{\rm sym}),\hskip 10.0pt{\mathcal{I}}_{m,r}^{\rm sym}:={\mathcal{J}}_{m,r}+I_{\rm sym},

Y_{m,r}^{\rm sym}:=\operatorname{Spec}(R/{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm sym}_{m,r})\subset Z_{\rm sym}.

Therefore,
Y_{m,r}^{\rm sym}
is
given as the locus where all
r
-minors of the following matrix
B_{m}
vanish,

(1.3)

B_{m}:=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,2}&\cdots&x_{1,m}\\
x_{1,2}&x_{2,2}&&x_{2,m}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{1,m}&x_{2,m}&\cdots&x_{m,m}\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem B
.

Let
m,r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
be positive integers with
r\leq m
,
let
R_{0}
be a commutative regular ring. The following sequence of blowing ups is an embedded resolution of singularities for the generic symmetric determinantal singularity
Y_{m,r}^{\rm sym}\subseteq Z_{\rm sym}
,

Z_{\rm sym}=:Z_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{1}}}{{\longleftarrow}}Z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{2}}}{{\longleftarrow}}\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{r-1}}}{{\longleftarrow}}Z_{r-1}

where
\pi_{\alpha}\colon Z_{\alpha}\to Z_{\alpha-1}
is the blowing up with center the strict transform of
Y_{m,\alpha}^{\rm sym}
in
Z_{\alpha-1}
, for
\alpha\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}
.

The proofs of Theorems
A
and
B
are done by induction on the size
m\geq 1
of the matrix defining the determinantal singularity.
If
m=1
, there is nothing to show and the case
m=2
is easy (Example
2.2
).
For
m>2
, the key ingredient is to apply suitable row and column operations to the strict transform of
A_{m}
resp.
B_{m}
which preserve the (skew-) symmetric structure in each chart of a blowing up.
Eventually, the determinantal structure allows to make a reduction to cases, where the respective integer
m
decreased.

Based on Theorems
A
and
B
,
the first named author studied aspects of an implementation as well as the complexity of the constructed desingularizations.
In particular, see

[
Gau1
, Section A.5.1 and Section B.2]

and
[
Gau1
, Chapter 8]
, where algorithmic aspects for more general determinantal singularities are considered.

Let us briefly summarize the content of the paper: In Section
2
,
we discuss examples for small values of
m
to visualize the ideas
and to provide the basis for our induction.
In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
A
and in Section
4
, we show Theorem
B
.

All rings considered in this article are assumed to be commutative.

2.
First Examples

First, we fix some notation and illustrate the ideas and appearing phenomena for some examples.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
R_{0}
be a regular ring and
Z=\operatorname{Spec}(R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}])\cong\mathbb{A}^{N}_{R_{0}}
.
Consider the blowing up
\pi\colon{B\ell}_{D}(Z)\to Z

of
Z
with center
D=\operatorname{Spec}(R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}]/\langle t_{i}\mid i\in I\rangle)
,
for some
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Set
d:=\#I
.
Globally,
{B\ell}_{D}(Z)
is described by the
\operatorname{Proj}
-construction as

{B\ell}_{D}(Z)=\operatorname{Proj}(R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}][T_{i}\mid i\in I]/\langle t_{i}T_{j}-t_{j}T_{i}\mid i,j\in I\rangle)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{N}_{R_{0}}\times\mathbb{P}^{d-1}_{R_{0}},

where
(T_{i}\mid i\in I)
are projective coordinates,
and
\pi
is induced by the projection on the first factor
\mathbb{A}^{N}_{R_{0}}\times\mathbb{P}^{d-1}_{R_{0}}\to\mathbb{A}^{N}_{R_{0}}
.
In particular,
{B\ell}_{D}(Z)
is covered by the affine charts
D_{+}(T_{i})

with
i\in I
,
where
D_{+}(T_{i})
denotes the standard open set of points where
T_{i}
is invertible.
We call
D_{+}(T_{i})
also the
T_{i}
-chart
of the blowing up.

Fix
i\in I
.
In the
T_{i}
-chart, the relation
t_{i}T_{j}-t_{j}T_{i}=0
can be rewritten as

t_{j}=t_{i}\frac{T_{j}}{T_{i}},

for

j\in I\setminus\{i\}
.
This provides that
{B\ell}_{D}(Z)\cap D_{+}(T_{i})
is isomorphic to

\operatorname{Spec}(R_{0}[t_{k},t_{i},\frac{T_{j}}{T_{i}}\mid k\notin I,j\in I\setminus\{i\}])\cong\mathbb{A}_{R_{0}}^{N}.

Often the abbreviation
t_{j}^{\prime}:=\frac{T_{j}}{T_{j}}
is used and by setting
t_{i}^{\prime}:=t_{i}
and
t_{k}^{\prime}:=t_{k}
for
k\notin I
,
it is said that the variables of the
T_{i}
-chart are
(t_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,t_{N}^{\prime})
,
where the relation to the variables
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
before the blowing up is described by

(2.1)

t_{j}=\begin{cases}t_{i}^{\prime}t_{j}^{\prime},&\mbox{if }j\in I\setminus\{i\},\\
t_{j}^{\prime},&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that the exceptional divisor of the blowing up
\pi
,
i.e., the preimage of
D
along
\pi

(the locus where
\pi
is not an isomorphism),
is given by the divisor
\operatorname{div}(t_{i}^{\prime})
in the
T_{i}
-chart.

Finally, recall that for an element
f\in R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}]
, its
strict transform
in the
T_{i}
-chart is the element
f^{\prime}\in R_{0}[t_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,t_{N}^{\prime}]

which we obtain by applying the transformation rule (
2.1
) to
f
and then factoring the exceptional variable
t_{i}^{\prime}
as much as possible.
On the other hand, given
X\subseteq Z
its
strict transform
X^{\prime}\subseteq Z^{\prime}
is the closure of
\pi^{-1}(X\setminus D)
in
Z^{\prime}
.

In our setting, we have
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})=(x_{i,j}\mid i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\})
and we speak of the
X_{1,2}
-chart, or similar expressions.
Besides reflecting the origin of the variables
(x_{i,j})
coming from a matrix structure, the index set
\{1,\ldots,m\}^{2}
has no impact or deeper meaning.

Example 2.2

(Theorems
A
and
B
for
m=2
)
.

Fix a regular basis ring
R_{0}
.
Let us consider
Y_{2,r}^{\rm skew}
and
Y_{2,r}^{\rm sym}
for
r\in\{1,2\}
.

(1)

It is clear that both are regular for
r=1
since we assume
R_{0}
to be regular.

(2)

Suppose that
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})\neq 2
.
We have already seen in the introduction
that the reduction of
Y_{2,2}^{\rm skew}
is regular and hence no further blowing ups are required.

(3)

In the symmetric case,
Y_{2,2}^{\rm sym}
is a singular hypersurface given by the equation

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,2}\\
x_{1,2}&x_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}=x_{1,1}x_{2,2}-x_{1,2}^{2}=0.

The singular locus is equal to
Y_{2,1}^{\rm sym}
and by blowing up the center
D_{1}=Y_{2,1}^{\rm sym}=V(x_{1,1},x_{1,2,},x_{2,2})
,
we obtain an embedded desingularization.

Therefore, we have verified Theorems
A
and
B
for the special case
m=2
.

In general,
Y_{m,r}^{\rm skew}
and
Y_{m,r}^{\rm sym}
are not hypersurfaces if
r<m
.
In order to be able to control them after a blowing up, we need the following result.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
R_{0}
be a regular ring and

X=\operatorname{Spec}(R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}]/I)=Z\cong\mathbb{A}^{N}_{R_{0}}
,
for some non-zero ideal
I\subset R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}]
.
Consider the blowing up
\pi\colon Z^{\prime}\to Z
with center
D=\operatorname{Spec}(R_{0}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}]/\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\rangle)
.
If there exists a system of generators
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
for
I
such that each
f_{j}
is homogeneous in the variables
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
,
then the ideal of the strict transform of
X
in the
T_{i}
-chart is generated by the strict transforms of
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
.

In general, the hypothesis to have homogeneous generators is necessary.
For example, consider

R=K[x,y,z]
,
P=\langle x,y,z\rangle\subset R
,
and the ideal

I=\langle x^{2}-y^{3},x^{2}-z^{5}\rangle\subset R
, where
K
is any field.
Clearly,
(g_{1},g_{2}):=(x^{2}-y^{3},x^{2}-z^{5})
are not homogeneous with respect to
P
.
Set
h:=y^{3}-z^{5}=g_{2}-g_{1}
.
If we blow up the origin
D=\operatorname{Spec}(R/P)
and consider the
Z
-chart, we get the strict transforms

g_{1}^{\prime}=x^{\prime 2}-y^{\prime 3}z^{\prime}
,

g_{2}^{\prime}=x^{\prime 2}-z^{\prime 3}
,

h^{\prime}=y^{\prime 3}-z^{\prime 2}
.
Note that
g_{2}^{\prime}-g_{1}^{\prime}=z^{\prime}(y^{\prime 3}-z^{\prime 2})
.
Let
I^{\prime}
be the ideal of the strict transform
X^{\prime}
in the given chart.
We see that
h^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
,
but
h^{\prime}\notin\langle g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}^{\prime}\rangle
.
Hence,
I^{\prime}\neq\langle g_{1}^{\prime},g_{2}^{\prime}\rangle
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Suppose the conclusion is false.
Then, there exists
h\in I=\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
such that its strict transform
h^{\prime}
is not contained in
\langle f_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,f_{m}^{\prime}\rangle
.
Set
P:=\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\rangle
and

r_{i}:=\operatorname{ord}_{P}(f_{i}):=\sup\{k\geq 0\mid f_{i}\in P^{k}\}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

We expand
h
as finite sum
h=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{A\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{N}}\lambda_{i,A}t^{A}f_{i}
with coefficients
\lambda_{i,A}\in R_{0}
.
We have
\operatorname{ord}_{P}(h)\geq\min\{\operatorname{ord}_{P}(t^{A}f_{i})\mid\lambda_{i,A}\neq 0\}=:\mu
and if equality holds, then it is easy to verify that
h^{\prime}\in\langle f_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,f_{m}^{\prime}\rangle
by applying the rule of transformation (
2.1
).
Thus, this would lead to a contradiction.

On the other hand,
\operatorname{ord}_{P}(h)>\mu
implies that there is cancellation within the part of the expansion which is homogeneous of degreee
\mu
in
(t)
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{A:|A|+r_{i}=\mu}\lambda_{i,A}t^{A}f_{i}\equiv 0
,
where
|A|:=A_{1}+\ldots+A_{N}
for
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{N})
.
Notice that the hypothesis that all
f_{i}
are homogeneous is used here, as the degree
\mu
-part does not lie in the ideal
\langle f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\rangle
, in general.
But now, we may omit the degree
\mu
-part of the expansion, as it is obsolete, and repeat our argument for the new expansion.
Since
h
has to be non-zero and the expansion is finite, we eventually end up in the first case, which provides a contradiction, as desired.
∎

In order to discuss the relevant examples for the lemma in our context and for the later use, we introduce the following notation.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
r,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
with
r\leq m
.
Let
R
be a regular ring and let
M=(m_{i,j})
be an
m\times m
matrix with entries in
R
.
We define
{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(M)\subseteq R
to be the ideal generated by the
r
-minors of
M
,
i.e.,

{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(M)=\langle\det(M_{I,J})\mid I,J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}:\#I=\#J=r\rangle,

where
M_{I,J}
denotes the
r\times r
submatrix of
M
given by
(m_{i,j})_{i\in I,j\in J}.

We fix a regular ring
R_{0}
for the remainder of the section.

Example 2.5
.

Let
r,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
with
r\leq m
and consider the matrices
A_{m}
and
B_{m}
introduced in (
1.1
) and (
1.3
), respectively.
Using the notation of the introduction, we have

{\mathcal{I}}_{m,r}^{\rm skew}={\mathcal{I}}_{r}(A_{m})\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt{\mathcal{I}}_{m,r}^{\rm sym}={\mathcal{I}}_{r}(B_{m})

in
R_{0}[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq m]
(with
\operatorname{char}(R_{0})\neq 2
), resp.
R_{0}[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\leq m]
.

Let
I,J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
be subsets of cardinality
r
.
Set
f_{I,J}^{{\rm skew}}:=\det((A_{m})_{I,J})
and
f_{I,J}^{{\rm sym}}:=\det((B_{m})_{I,J})
.
Clearly,
f_{I,J}^{{\rm skew}}
and
f_{I,J}^{{\rm sym}}
are homogeneous of degree
r
in the variables
(x_{i,j})
.
Therefore, these elements provide systems of generators for the ideal

In the next example, we illustrate how we preserve the symmetry by appropriate row and column operations.

Example 2.6

(Theorem
B
for
m=3
)
.

Consider

, which is determined by the vanishing of the determinant of

,

B_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,2}&x_{1,3}\\

By Theorem
B
,
the first center to blow up

Our strategy for the next step is to check the following two things after the first blowing up:

(1)

All singularities of the strict transform of

lie on the

-charts,

;

(2)

The strict transform of

Consider the

-chart,
i.e., as described in Remark
2.1
,

x_{1,1}=x_{1,1}^{\prime}
and

at position

y_{2,3}&y_{3,3}\end{pmatrix},

y_{2,3}:=x_{2,3}^{\prime}-x_{1,2}^{\prime}x_{1,3}^{\prime}

y_{3,3}:=x_{3,3}^{\prime}-x_{1,3}^{\prime 2}

\operatorname{div}(x_{1,1}^{\prime})
and

is transveral to
y_{2,2},y_{2,3},y_{3,3}

We have reduced the problem to

.

(3).

is given by a sheaf of ideals which in the

-chart is given by

:
We observe that
y_{i,j}
is the strict transform of the
2
-minor of

that contains

and
x_{i,j}
,

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,j}\\

The strict transform of every
2
-minor of
B_{3}
that does not contain

is already contained in
I
:
For

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,\ell}^{\prime}&x_{i,\ell}^{\prime}\\

Further, we get

\det\begin{pmatrix}x_{2,2}^{\prime}&x_{2,3}^{\prime}\\

defined by the
2
-minors of
B_{3}
as suggested by Theorem
B
.

- and the

-chart.

Hence, the transformation of the variables is

and

for

The strict transform of

Example 2.7

•

In the given chart, the strict transform

of

-x_{j,i(\kappa+1)}^{\prime}\\
\vdots\\
-x_{j,i(r)}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}_{j\in J}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{i(1),j}+x^{\prime}_{2,j}x^{\prime}_{1,i(1)}-x^{\prime}_{1,j}x^{\prime}_{2,i(1)}\\
\vdots\\
y_{i(\kappa),j}+x^{\prime}_{2,j}x^{\prime}_{1,i(\kappa)}-x^{\prime}_{1,j}x^{\prime}_{2,i(\kappa)}\\
-y_{j,i(\kappa+1)}-x^{\prime}_{2,i(\kappa+1)}x^{\prime}_{1,j}+x^{\prime}_{1,i(\kappa+1)}x^{\prime}_{2,j}\\
\vdots\\
-y_{j,i(r)}-x^{\prime}_{2,i(r)}x^{\prime}_{1,j}+x^{\prime}_{1,i(r)}x^{\prime}_{2,j}\end{pmatrix}_{j\in J}.

Notice that, if
i(\kappa)=j
, then the entry at position
(i(k),j)
is zero.
Further, if
\kappa(j)=0
, then
j
-th column begins with
-x_{j,i(1)}^{\prime}
.

Set
E_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
and

\mathcal{B}:=\begin{pmatrix}E_{2}&0\\
{\mathcal{A}}&{\mathcal{M}}_{I,J}\end{pmatrix}
,
where
{\mathcal{A}}
is an arbitrary
r\times 2
matrix and
0
stands for the matrix of size
2\times r
with all entries zero.
We have

\det(\mathcal{B})=\det({\mathcal{M}}_{I,J})=g_{I,J}.

Let us consider the matrix
{\mathcal{A}}
given by

{\mathcal{A}}:=\begin{pmatrix}-x^{\prime}_{1,i(1)}&-x^{\prime}_{2,i(1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
-x^{\prime}_{1,i(r)}&-x^{\prime}_{2,i(r)}\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that the entries of
{\mathcal{A}}
are coming from the first two columns of
A_{m}^{\prime}
.

In the next step, we use the entries of
\mathcal{A}

to eliminate the terms
x^{\prime}_{2,j}x^{\prime}_{1,i}-x^{\prime}_{1,j}x^{\prime}_{2,i}
in the columns of
\mathcal{B}
corresponding to
{\mathcal{M}}_{I,J}
(whose index ranges in
J
).
More precisely, we apply the following elementary column operations to
\mathcal{B}
for every
j\in J
:

•

Add
x_{2,j}^{\prime}
-times the first column of
\mathcal{B}
to the column with index
j
;

•

add
(-x_{1,j}^{\prime})
-times the second column of
\mathcal{B}
to the column with index
j
.

This leads to

g_{I,J}=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\\
-x^{\prime}_{1,i(1)}&-x^{\prime}_{2,i(1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
-x^{\prime}_{1,i(\kappa)}&-x^{\prime}_{2,i(\kappa)}\\
-x^{\prime}_{1,i(\kappa+1)}&-x^{\prime}_{2,i(\kappa+1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
-x^{\prime}_{1,i(r)}&-x^{\prime}_{2,i(r)}\end{array}\begin{pmatrix}x_{2,j}^{\prime}\\
-x_{1,j}^{\prime}\\
y_{i(1),j}\\
\vdots\\
y_{i(\kappa),j}\\
-y_{j,i(\kappa+1)}\\
\vdots\\
-y_{j,i(r)}\end{pmatrix}_{j\in J}\right).

We expand the determinant with respect to the second column so that it becomes a sum of determinants of matrices of size
(r+1)\times(r+1)
.
(Of course, there are certain coefficients appearing in the sum, but these will not be relevant, so we neglect them here).
We continue and expand each of these matrices with respect to their respective first column so that
g_{I,J}
is expressed as a sum of determinants of
r\times r
matrices.
Observe that the latter matrices fulfill the property that if we neglect the first and second row then all entries are of the form
y_{i,j}
or
-y_{i,j}
for some
i\in I
and
j\in J
.
We proceed and expand the determinants of the appearing
r\times r
matrices with respect to the second row
and after that we expand the resulting determinants of
(r-1)\times(r-1)
matrices with respect to the first row.
In total, due to the described property of the
r\times r
matrices,
we have written
g_{I,J}
as a sum of
(r-2)
-minors of the matrix
\widetilde{A}_{m-2}
,
i.e.,
g_{I,J}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{r-2}(\widetilde{A}_{m-2})
,
as claimed.

It is not hard to adapt the argument for the remaining

r
-minors
g_{I,J}
of
A_{m}^{\prime}
with
\{1,2\}\not\subset I
or
\{1,2\}\not\subset J
:
First, one has to enlarge the matrix
{\mathcal{M}}_{I,J}
determining
g_{I,J}

by the needed entries of the first and second column of
A_{m}^{\prime}
which are not already appearing in
{\mathcal{M}}_{I,J}
.
Note that if
\{1,2\}\subset J
, then no enlargement is required.
On the other hand, observe, if
1\in I
(resp.
2\in I
), then the corresponding row for
{\mathcal{A}}
is induced by
(0\ 1)
(resp.
(-1\ 0)
).
After that, we substitute
x_{i,j}^{\prime}
using (
3.1
), for
3\leq i<j\leq m
, and apply elementary column operations in order to reach that these entries are of the form
\pm y_{i,j}
.
If
1\in I
(or
2\in I
), then these column operations eliminate all entries of the first (resp. second) row of the
{\mathcal{M}}_{I,J}
-part of the analogue of
\mathcal{B}
.
Finally, by a suitable expansion of the determinant, one achieves the desired statement

g_{I,J}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{r-2}(\widetilde{A}_{m-2})
.

In conclusion, we proved (
3.2
), which ends the proof.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
B

Let us turn to the generic symmetric case.
We fix the notation:

•

R_{0}
is a regular ring.

•

S:=S_{\rm sym}\cong R_{0}[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\leq m]

and
Z:=Z_{\rm sym}=\operatorname{Spec}(S)
.

•

B_{m}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,2}&\cdots&x_{1,m}\\
x_{1,2}&x_{2,2}&\cdots&x_{2,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{1,m}&x_{2,m}&\cdots&x_{m,m}\end{pmatrix}

and

Y_{m,r}:=Y_{m,r}^{\rm sym}=\operatorname{Spec}(S/{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(B_{m}))
.

The goal of this section is to show that the sequence of blowing ups described in Theorem
B
is an embedded resolution of singularities for
Y_{m,r}^{\rm sym}
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

As in the proof of Theorem
A
, we perform an induction on the size
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
of the matrix
B_{m}
.
If
m=1
, there is nothing to prove.
In Examples
2.2
and
2.6
, we treated the cases
m\in\{2,3\}
.
Thus, let
m\geq 4
.

Analogous to before, we study
Y_{m,m}
and handle the cases
Y_{m,r}
with
r<m
along the way.
Clearly,
Y_{m,1}
is always regular, since
Y_{m,1}\cong\operatorname{Spec}(R_{0})
and
R_{0}
is assumed to be regular.
Following Theorem
B
, we blow up the center
D_{1}:=Y_{m,1}
.

We begin with the
X_{1,1}
-chart as representative of the
X_{i,i}
-charts for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}

(since by suitably interchanging columns and rows followed by renaming variables, we reach the
X_{1,1}
-chart for the other
X_{i,i}
-charts).
The strict transform
f^{\prime}
of
f:=\det(B_{m})
is given by the determinant of the matrix

B_{m}^{\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&x^{\prime}_{1,2}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{1,m}\\
x^{\prime}_{1,2}&x^{\prime}_{2,2}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{2,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x^{\prime}_{1,m}&x^{\prime}_{2,m}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{m,m}\end{pmatrix}.

We eliminate the entries in the first column by subtracting
x_{1,j}
-times the first row from the
j
-th row for
j\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
.
We get

f^{\prime}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&x^{\prime}_{1,2}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{1,m}\\
0&x^{\prime}_{2,2}-x^{\prime 2}_{1,2}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{2,m}-x^{\prime}_{1,m}x^{\prime}_{1,2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&x^{\prime}_{2,m}-x^{\prime}_{1,2}x^{\prime}_{1,m}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{m,m}-x^{\prime 2}_{1,m}\end{pmatrix}=\det\begin{pmatrix}y_{2,2}&\cdots&y_{2,m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
y_{2,m}&\cdots&y_{m,m}\end{pmatrix},

where we expand the determinant with respect to the first column in the last equality and introduce

y_{i,j}:=x^{\prime}_{i,j}-x^{\prime}_{1,i}x_{1,j}^{\prime}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ for }i,j\in\{2,\ldots,m\}.

These
(y_{i,j})_{i,j}
are independent variables which are transversal to the exceptional variable
x^{\prime}_{1,1}
.
This shows that
f^{\prime}
is defined by the determinant of the generic symmetric matrix of size
m-1
.
In other words, in the given chart,
the strict transform
Y_{m,m}^{\prime}
of
Y_{m,m}
is isomorphic to
Y_{m-1,m-1}
.
By induction, the remaining resolution process is determined.

Let
\widetilde{B}_{m-1}
be the symmetric matrix with entries
y_{i,j}
.
Analogous to the proof of Theorem
A
, it remains to prove that the ideal generated by the
(r-1)
-minors of
\widetilde{B}_{m-1}
coincides with the strict transform of the
r
-minors of
B_{m}
,

{\mathcal{I}}_{r-1}(\widetilde{B}_{m-1})={\mathcal{I}}_{r}(B_{m}^{\prime}),

for
r\geq 2
.
We already proved the equality for
r=m
.
Hence, let
1<r<m
.
The inclusion
``\subseteq"
is clear since the
(r-1)
-minors of
\widetilde{B}_{m-1}
correspond to the
r
-minors of
B_{m}^{\prime}
involving the entry of
B_{m}^{\prime}
at position
(1,1)
.
For the reverse inclusion
``\supseteq"
, we apply the analogous arguments as in the proof of Theorem
A
:
We consider an
r
-minor of
B_{m}^{\prime}
, apply the definition of
y_{i,j}
to substitute the corresponding
x_{i,j}^{\prime}
, if necessary we enlarge the matrix by the respective entries of the first column of
B_{m}^{\prime}

to perform suitably column operations and expand the determinant to reach the desired inclusion.

Next let us have a look at the
X_{1,2}
-chart, which is a representative of the
X_{i,j}
-charts for
1\leq i<j\leq m
.
The strict transform
f^{\prime}
of
f=\det(B_{m})
is equal to

f^{\prime}=\det(B_{m}^{\prime}),\hskip 19.91684pt\mbox{ for }B_{m}^{\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}_{1,1}&1&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{1,m}\\
1&x^{\prime}_{2,2}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{2,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x^{\prime}_{1,m}&x^{\prime}_{2,m}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{m,m}\end{pmatrix}.

We eliminate all entries in the first row and second column by subtracting
x^{\prime}_{2,j}
-times the first row from the
j
-th row for
j\in\{2,\ldots,m\}

and then cleaning up the first row analogous to before.
We obtain

f^{\prime}=\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,2}&0&x^{\prime}_{2,3}-x^{\prime}_{2,2}x^{\prime}_{1,3}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{2,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,2}x^{\prime}_{1,m}\par\\
x^{\prime}_{1,3}-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,3}&0&x^{\prime}_{3,3}-x^{\prime}_{2,3}x^{\prime}_{1,3}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{3,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,3}x^{\prime}_{1,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x^{\prime}_{1,m}-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,m}&0&x^{\prime}_{3,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,m}x^{\prime}_{1,3}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{m,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,m}x^{\prime}_{1,m}\end{pmatrix}.

Since we already discussed the
X_{i,i}
-charts, we may neglect the part of the given
X_{1,2}
-chart which is also contained in some
X_{i,i}
-chart, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
In particular, we may assume that
x_{1,1}^{\prime}=\frac{X_{1,1}}{X_{1,2}}
is not a unit
(using the notation of Remark
2.1
),
cf. Example
2.6
.
Hence, may assume that

\varepsilon:=1-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,2}
is invertible in present chart.

In order to regain the symmetry, we multiply the first column by
\varepsilon^{-1}

and multiply the
j
-th row by
\varepsilon
, for
j\in\{3,\ldots,m\}
.
This provides

f^{\prime}=\phi\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&0&x^{\prime}_{2,3}-x^{\prime}_{2,2}x^{\prime}_{1,3}&\cdots&x^{\prime}_{2,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,2}x^{\prime}_{1,m}\\
x^{\prime}_{1,3}-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,3}&0&\varepsilon(x^{\prime}_{3,3}-x^{\prime}_{2,3}x^{\prime}_{1,3})&\cdots&\varepsilon(x^{\prime}_{3,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,3}x^{\prime}_{1,m})\par\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x^{\prime}_{1,m}-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,m}&0&\varepsilon(x^{\prime}_{3,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,m}x^{\prime}_{1,3})&\cdots&\varepsilon(x^{\prime}_{m,m}-x^{\prime}_{2,m}x^{\prime}_{1,m})\end{pmatrix},

for
\phi
invertible.
Hence,
\phi
may be neglected when considering the vanishing locus.

In the next step, we use the
1
at position
(2,1)
to eliminate all other entries of the first column.
More precisely, for
i\in\{3,\ldots,m\}
,
we subtract

(x^{\prime}_{1,i}-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,i})
-times the second row
from the
i
-th row.
Afterwards, we expand the determinant with respect to the first two columns.
Altogether, this leads to

f^{\prime}=(-1)\cdot\phi\cdot\det(\widetilde{B}_{m-2}),

where

\widetilde{B}_{m-2}:=\begin{pmatrix}y_{3,3}&\cdots&y_{3,m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
y_{3,m}&\cdots&y_{m,m}\end{pmatrix}

and

y_{i,j}:=\varepsilon(x^{\prime}_{i,j}-x^{\prime}_{2,i}x^{\prime}_{1,j})-(x^{\prime}_{1,i}-x^{\prime}_{1,1}x^{\prime}_{2,i})(x^{\prime}_{2,j}-x^{\prime}_{2,2}x^{\prime}_{1,j}),

for
i,j\in\{3,\ldots,m\}
.
Note that this definition is well-defined
since
\varepsilon
is a unit
and
(y_{i,j})_{i,j}
are independent variables which are transversal to the exceptional variable
x^{\prime}_{1,2}
.

In conclusion, the strict transform
Y_{m,m}^{\prime}
of
Y_{m,m}
in the present chart is isomorphic to
Y_{m-2,m-2}
.
By induction, Theorem
B
holds for
Y_{m-2,m-2}
and the remaining embedded resolution process is known.
Analogous to before, it remains to prove

{\mathcal{I}}_{r-2}(\widetilde{B}_{m-2})={\mathcal{I}}_{r}(B_{m}^{\prime})
,
for
r\in\{3,\ldots,m-1\}
.
Since similar arguments as before apply,
we leave the details to the reader as an exercise.

Notice that in the present chart,
the vanishing locus of the
2
-minors of
B_{m}^{\prime}
is empty, since we assume that
\varepsilon
is invertible.
Therefore, from a global perspective,
we do not see the second center of the resolution procedure described in the statement of Theorem
B
here.

In conclusion, we deduced that the local computations in each chart coincide with the global sequence of blowing ups described in Theorem
B
.
Furthermore, an inductive argument shows that this is indeed an embedded resolution of singularities.
∎
