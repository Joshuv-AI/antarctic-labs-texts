The difference variational bicomplex and multisymplectic systems
Linyu Peng and Peter E. Hydon 1. Department of Mechanical Engineering, Keio University,Yokohama 223-8522, Japan2. School of Mathematics, Statistics and Actuarial Science,University of Kent, Canterbury CT2 7FS, UK

Abstract
The difference variational bicomplex, which is the natural setting for systems of difference equations, is constructed and used to examine the geometric and algebraic properties of various systems. Exactness of the bicomplex gives a coordinate-free setting for finite difference variational problems, Euler–Lagrange equations and Noether’s theorem. We also examine the connection between the condition for existence of a Hamiltonian and the multisymplecticity of systems of partial difference equations. Furthermore, we define difference multimomentum maps of multisymplectic systems, which yield their conservation laws. To conclude, we demonstrate how multisymplectic integrators can be comprehended even on non-uniform meshes through a generalized difference variational bicomplex. Keywords: difference variational bicomplex; multisymplectic system; multisymplectic integrator; conservation law; multimomentum map

The (differential) variational bicomplex

The variational bicomplex is a double complex of differential forms that arises by regarding independent variables as coordinates on a base space, with dependent variables and their derivatives coordinatizing fibres over each point of the base space. The geometric setting is the infinite prolongation bundle. We review the differential variational bicomplex (following Anderson’s presentation
[
1
,
2
]
, see also
[
42
,
23
,
44
]
), and show that this is a natural setting for multisymplectic systems of PDEs.

2.1

An overview of the variational bicomplex

For differential equations with independent variables
\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},x^{2},\ldots,x^{p})\in X\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and dependent variables
\mathbf{u}=(u^{1},u^{2},\ldots,u^{q})\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
, a natural geometric structure is the trivial fibred manifold

\displaystyle\pi:X\times U

\displaystyle\rightarrow X,

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbf{x}.

A solution
\mathbf{u}=f(\mathbf{x})
can be regarded as a local section,
s(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}))
. Restricting attention to neighbourhoods in which
f
is smooth, a section can be prolonged to the infinite jet bundle
J^{\infty}(X\times U)
whose coordinates represent derivatives:

\displaystyle\pi^{\infty}:J^{\infty}(X\times U)

\displaystyle\rightarrow X,

(2.2)

\displaystyle(x^{i},u^{\alpha},u_{\mathbf{1}_{i}}^{\alpha},\ldots,u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}},\ldots)

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathbf{x}.

The prolonged section
s
has coordinates

x^{i},\quad u^{\alpha}=f^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}),\quad u_{\mathbf{1}_{i}}^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial f^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x^{i}}\,,\quad\ldots,\quad u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}=\frac{\partial^{|\mathbf{J}|}f^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{(\partial x^{1})^{j^{1}}(\partial x^{2})^{j^{2}}\cdots(\partial x^{p})^{j^{p}}}\,,\quad\ldots,

(2.3)

where
\mathbf{1}_{i}
is the
p
-tuple whose only nonzero entry is
1
in its
i
th place,
\mathbf{J}=(j^{1},j^{2},\ldots,j^{p})
with all entries being non-negative integers, and
|\mathbf{J}|=j^{1}+j^{2}+\cdots+j^{p}
. In this setting, a differential equation defines a variety on the jet bundle.

The exterior derivative on
J^{\infty}(X\times U)
can be written in terms of these local coordinates. Let
[\mathbf{u}]
denote
\mathbf{u}
and finitely many of its partial derivatives; then the exterior derivative of a locally smooth function
f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])
is

\operatorname{d}\!f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])=\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])}{\partial x^{i}}\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}+\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}}\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}\,.

(2.4)

The Einstein summation convention is used from (
2.4
) on. It is natural to split the exterior derivative using the contact forms
\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}-u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}
, because the pullback of each contact form by any section
s
is zero. This splitting gives

\operatorname{d}\!f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])=\left(D_{i}f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])\right)\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}+\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}}\left(\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}-u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}\right),

(2.5)

where

D_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}+u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{1}_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\alpha}}+\cdots+u_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\mathbf{J}}^{\alpha}}+\cdots

(2.6)

is the total derivative with respect to
x^{i}
. This splitting gives a basis for the set of differential one-forms:

\operatorname{d}\!x^{i},\qquad\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}-u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}\operatorname{d}\!x^{i},

(2.7)

which is extended to a basis for the set
\Omega
of all differential forms by using the wedge product.
The exterior derivative splits into the horizontal derivative
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}
and the vertical derivative
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
, as follows:

\operatorname{d}=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}+\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}},

(2.8)

where

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}=\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}\wedge D_{i}\,,\quad\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}=\left(\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}-u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}\!\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}\right)\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}}\,.

(2.9)

Direct calculation yields the identities

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{2}=0,\qquad\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}},\qquad\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}^{2}=0.

(2.10)

The contact forms
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}=\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}-u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}\!\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}
form a basis for the set of vertical differential one-forms; the set of horizontal differential one-forms has a basis
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!x^{i}=\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}
. Furthermore,

\mathbf{u}

\mathbf{v}

(k+l)

\mathcal{I}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{l}\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha}\wedge(-D)_{\mathbf{J}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\mathbf{J}}^{\alpha}}\,\,\,\,\begin{picture}(5.0,5.0)\put(0.0,4.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(5.0,4.0){\line{0}{1}{8.0}}\end{picture}\,\,\sigma\right),\quad\sigma\in\Omega^{p,l},

which gives the same outcome (up to a divergence) as integration by parts. The rows containing

(for fixed

\text{ker}(\mathcal{I})=\text{im}(\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}})
. Moreover, the interior Euler operator is a projection (so
\mathcal{I}^{2}=\mathcal{I}

\sigma\in\Omega^{p,l},~l\geq 1
, there exists
\tau\in\Omega^{p-1,l}
such that

\sigma=\mathcal{I}(\sigma)-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\tau.

(2.17)

The

\mathcal{E}:\Omega^{p,0}\rightarrow\mathscr{F}^{1}
is defined by
\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{I}\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
. Given a Lagrangian form,
\mathscr{L}[\mathbf{u}]=L(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])\text{vol}

\text{vol}=\operatorname{d}\!x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\operatorname{d}\!x^{p}
is the volume form,

\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{L})=(-D)_{\mathbf{J}}\left(\frac{\partial L(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}}\right)\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha}\wedge\text{vol},

(2.18)

so the Euler–Lagrange equations are the coefficients of
\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{L})=0
.

Bearing in mind that
\mathscr{F}^{l}\subset\Omega^{p,l}
, the operators

give higher variations; in particular,
\delta_{\operatorname{v}}:\mathscr{F}^{1}\rightarrow\mathscr{F}^{2}
gives the Helmholtz conditions for the inverse problem of variational calculus.
The variational complex is the edge of the augmented variational bicomplex, consisting of the bottom row, the Euler–Lagrange operator, and the column containing the spaces
\mathscr{F}^{l}
. The variational complex is exact, so

\text{ker}(\mathcal{E})=\text{im}(\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}),\qquad\text{im}(\mathcal{E})=\text{ker}(\delta_{\operatorname{v}}),

and the column containing
\mathscr{F}^{l}
is exact.

2.2

Multisymplectic systems via the variational bicomplex

The (augmented) variational bicomplex provides a natural framework for studying multisymplectic PDEs; this framework was introduced in
[
7
]
for first-order quasilinear systems on Riemannian manifolds, but it applies equally to other types of multisymplectic systems. For simplicity, we restrict attention to the base manifold
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, but the results are local and can be adapted to other base manifolds.

The starting-point is Zuckerman’s discovery in
[
47
]
of a ‘universal conserved current’ for any given Lagrangian form
\mathscr{L}\in\Omega^{p,0}
. This is a vertically closed differential form
\omega\in\Omega^{p-1,2}
that is conserved on solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equations. It is instructive to revisit Zuckerman’s proof using the augmented variational bicomplex. From (
2.17
), there exists
\eta\in\Omega^{p-1,1}
such that

\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{L})=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\mathscr{L}+\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\eta.

(2.19)

The
(p-1,2)
-form
\omega=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\eta
satisfies

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\omega=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\eta=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{L}).

(2.20)

Consequently
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\omega=0
on the solution submanifold of the Euler–Lagrange equations.

Independently, Gotay
[
14
]
developed a covariant Hamiltonian formalism for field theories of arbitrary order, using a generalized Legendre transformation to identify additional phase space variables
p_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{J}}
from the Lagrangian. A key ingredient is the Poincaré–Cartan form
\Theta
, which is a Lepagean equivalent of the Lagrangian form. In the above notation,

\Theta=\mathscr{L}+\eta,

(2.21)

which is a
p
-form of mixed type (from the viewpoint of the bicomplex). From (
2.19
), we have

\operatorname{d}\!\Theta=\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{L})+\omega,

(2.22)

and (
2.20
) arises from
\operatorname{d}^{2}\!\Theta=0
.

Up to inessential terms, Gotay’s covariant Hamiltonian formalism yields the quasilinear first-order system of Euler–Lagrange equations for the modified Lagrangian

\widehat{L}=L(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{u}])+p_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}(D_{i}u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}-u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}).

(2.23)

Here
u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}},p_{\alpha}^{\mathbf{J}}
are distinct dependent variables on the phase space, with the latter variables playing the role of Lagrange multipliers. So nothing is lost by restricting attention to first-order quasilinear systems, in which case
[\mathbf{u}]
fully coordinatizes the phase space. This approach was introduced by Bridges
[
5
]
.

One can partially reverse the above derivation, using the fact that the vertical cohomology groups are trivial, even for the restricted bicomplex. A system of PDEs is
multisymplectic
if there exists a vertically closed
(p-1,2)
-form
\omega
such that
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\omega
is zero on the solution submanifold (but not identically zero). So every system of Euler–Lagrange equations is multisymplectic.
In coordinates, write

\omega=\kappa^{i}\wedge(D_{i}\,\,\,\,\begin{picture}(5.0,5.0)\put(0.0,4.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(5.0,4.0){\line{0}{1}{8.0}}\end{picture}\,\,\text{vol}),

(2.24)

where each
\kappa^{i}
is a vertically closed
(0,2)
-form. Then on solutions of the system,
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\omega=0
amounts to the form-valued conservation law

D_{i}\kappa^{i}=0.

As
\omega
is vertically closed, exactness of the vertical columns implies the existence of
\eta\in\Omega^{p-1,1}
such that
\omega=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\eta
. Moreover, on the solution submanifold,

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\eta=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\eta=0,

so there exists
\mathscr{L}\in\Omega^{p,0}
(restricted to this submanifold) such that

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\eta+\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\mathscr{L}=0.

Khavkine
[
20
]
used this observation to prove the existence of a Lagrangian
(p,0)
-form on the full jet space, whose Euler–Lagrange equations are solved by the given multisymplectic system of PDEs. (However, these equations may be weaker than the given system and so admit other solutions.)

It is convenient to restrict attention to multisymplectic systems that are first-order and quasilinear, so that both
\omega
and
\eta
are defined over each point
\mathbf{x}
in terms of the phase space variables and their vertical derivatives. For difference equations, however, this turns out not to be possible, as differences are not defined pointwise, nor are they derivations. Nevertheless, we now show that there is a difference analogue of the variational bicomplex which has very similar features to the differential case, and this gives rise to a standard form for multisymplectic difference equations.

Construction of the difference variational bicomplex

The building-blocks for the difference variational bicomplex are difference prolongation spaces
[
31
]
, difference forms
[
18
,
30
]
and the difference variational complex
[
18
]
over the base space
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
.

Consider a P
\Delta
E with
p
independent variables,
n^{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
q
dependent variables,
u^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
. These variables can be regarded as coordinates on the
total space
,
\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
: the discrete base space
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
and the connected fibres
\mathbb{R}^{q}
are coordinatized respectively by
\mathbf{n}=(n^{1},n^{2},\ldots,n^{p})
and
\mathbf{u}=(u^{1},u^{2},\dots,u^{q})
. For simplicity, we shall assume that this coordinate system applies everywhere, though the results below can be adapted if more than one coordinate patch is needed for a particular P
\Delta
E.
The fibres are mapped to one another by the horizontal translations

\displaystyle\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{J}}:\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}

(3.1)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{J},\mathbf{u}).

Note that
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{J}}\circ\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{K}}
for all
\mathbf{J},\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. (See
[
16
]
for other transformations of total space.)

As the total space is disconnected, it is helpful to construct a connected representation of this space over each base point. To do this, each fibre is prolonged to include the values of the coordinates on all other fibres as coordinates in a Cartesian product, using the pullback of each
u^{\alpha}
with respect to every
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{J}}
. The (connected) total prolongation space over an arbitrary base point, denoted
P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
(or
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
if the base point,
\mathbf{n}
, is specified), has coordinates
(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}})
, where

u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{J}}^{*}\!u^{\alpha}.

In particular,
u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{0}}=u^{\alpha}
. The total prolongation space provides a convenient setting for the study of geometric properties of difference equations.

The composition rule for horizontal translations gives the identities

u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{K}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}^{*}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}.

More generally, let
f
be a function on
\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
and denote its restriction to each total prolongation space
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
by
f_{\mathbf{n}}((u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))=f(\mathbf{n},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))
; for simplicity, we assume that every
f_{\mathbf{n}}
is smooth. Then the pullback of
f_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K}}((u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))
with respect to
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}
is the function
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}^{*}\!f_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K}}
on
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
whose values are
f(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{K}}))
. So the action of each horizontal translation
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}
on the space of smooth functions on
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
can be represented by the
shift operator

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}:C^{\infty}(P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q}))

\displaystyle\rightarrow C^{\infty}(P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q}))

(3.2)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{n},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))

\displaystyle\mapsto f(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{K}})),

so that
\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\!f_{\mathbf{n}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}^{*}\!f_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K}}
. Similarly, let
\sigma
be a differential form on
\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
whose restriction to each
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
is
\sigma_{\mathbf{n}}
. Then the action of
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}
on
\sigma_{\mathbf{n}}
is represented by the shift

\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\!\sigma_{\mathbf{n}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}^{*}\!\sigma_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K}}.

(3.3)

By the standard properties of the pullback,
\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}
commutes with the wedge product and with the exterior derivative on the fibre
P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
, which we denote by
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
(as it acts on dependent variables only):

\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}(\sigma_{1}\wedge\sigma_{2})=(\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\!\sigma_{1})\wedge(\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\!\sigma_{2}),\qquad\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}(\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\sigma)=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}(\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\!\sigma).

(3.4)

The difference structure is a consequence of the ordering of each independent variable. For any multi-index
\mathbf{J}=(j^{1},j^{2},\dots,j^{p})=j^{i}\mathbf{1}_{i}
, the corresponding shift operator is

\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{J}}=\operatorname{S}_{1}^{j^{1}}\operatorname{S}_{2}^{j^{2}}\cdots\operatorname{S}_{p}^{j^{p}}
, where
\operatorname{S}_{i}:=\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{1}_{i}}
denotes the forward shift with respect to
n^{i}
. Then the forward difference in the
n^{i}
-direction is represented on each
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
by the operator

\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}=\operatorname{S}_{i}-\operatorname{id},

(3.5)

where
\operatorname{id}
is the identity mapping. In
[
18
]
, Hydon & Mansfield introduced difference forms on
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. These have the same algebraic properties as differential forms on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with the exterior algebra on
p
symbols,
\Delta^{1},\Delta^{2},\dots,\Delta^{p}
, replacing the exterior algebra on
\operatorname{d}\!x^{1},\operatorname{d}\!x^{2},\dots,\operatorname{d}\!x^{p}
. The symbols
\Delta^{i}
at any two different points are related by (horizontal) translation, so that

\Delta^{i}\big|_{\mathbf{n}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{K}}^{*}(\Delta^{i}\big|_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{K}})=:\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}(\Delta^{i}\big|_{\mathbf{n}}).

(3.6)

A difference
k
-form
\sigma
on
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
assigns a
k
-form,

\sigma_{\mathbf{n}}=f_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}(\mathbf{n})\,\Delta^{i_{1}}\big|_{\mathbf{n}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{i_{k}}\big|_{\mathbf{n}},

to each
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. In view of the invariance of
\Delta^{i}
under horizontal translations, we write

\sigma=f_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}(\mathbf{n})\,\Delta^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{i_{k}}.

(3.7)

The exterior difference operator
\bm{\Delta}
maps difference
k
-forms to difference
(k+1)
-forms as follows:

\bm{\Delta}\sigma=\Delta^{i}\wedge\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}\sigma.

(3.8)

Unlike the exterior derivative
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
, the exterior difference
\bm{\Delta}
is not a derivation; however, like
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
, it satisfies the important identity
\bm{\Delta}^{2}=0
. Note also that
\bm{\Delta}n^{i}=\Delta^{i}
. The exterior difference acts pointwise on difference forms over
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
and extends immediately to difference forms over
\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
,

\sigma=f_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}(\mathbf{n},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))\,\Delta^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{i_{k}}.

In particular, the exterior difference of a difference
(p-1)
-form,

\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(-1)^{i-1}F^{i}(\mathbf{n},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))\,\Delta^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\Delta^{i}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{p}

where
\widehat{\Delta^{i}}
denotes the absence of
\Delta^{i}
, is

\bm{\Delta}\sigma=\text{Div}\,\mathbf{F}\,\Delta^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{p},\qquad\text{where}\quad\text{Div}\,\mathbf{F}:=\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}\!\left\{F^{i}(\mathbf{n},(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}))\right\}.

Any function of the form
\text{Div}\,\mathbf{F}
, as defined above, is called a (difference)
divergence
.

To obtain the difference variational bicomplex, we combine the above exterior difference and differential structures, using the wedge product. From here on, we consider only forms whose restriction to any particular
P_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
have coefficients depending only on
\mathbf{n}
and a finite subset, denoted
[\mathbf{u}]
, of the coordinates
(u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}})
. Under this condition, a
(k,l)
-
form
on

\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
is a
(k+l)
-form
\sigma
that can be written (without redundancies) as

\sigma=f^{\mathbf{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{J}_{l}}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}}(\mathbf{n},[\mathbf{u}])\,\Delta^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{i_{k}}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathbf{J}_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha_{l}}_{\mathbf{J}_{l}};

(3.9)

we denote the set of all such forms by
\Omega^{k,l}
. The exterior derivative is the mapping
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}:\Omega^{k,l}\rightarrow\Omega^{k,l+1}
whose action on (
3.9
) gives

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!\sigma=\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}}\left\{f^{\mathbf{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{J}_{l}}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}}(\mathbf{n},[\mathbf{u}])\right\}\,\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}}\wedge\Delta^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{i_{k}}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathbf{J}_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha_{l}}_{\mathbf{J}_{l}}.

(3.10)

Shifts of (
3.9
) are given by

\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\;\!\!\sigma=\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\left\{f^{\mathbf{J}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{J}_{l}}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l}}(\mathbf{n},[\mathbf{u}])\right\}\,\Delta^{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Delta^{i_{k}}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{K}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha_{l}}_{\mathbf{J}_{l}+\mathbf{K}},

(3.11)

because (

) implies that
\operatorname{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\!\Delta^{j}=\Delta^{j}
.
Bearing this in mind, the exterior difference is the mapping

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}:\Omega^{k,l}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Omega^{k+1,l}

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\mapsto\Delta^{i}\wedge\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}\sigma.

Remark 3.1
.

1. The operator
\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}
is the
Lie difference
with respect to the horizontal translation
\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{1}_{i}}
, because

(\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}\sigma)|_{\mathbf{n}}=\operatorname{T}_{\mathbf{1}_{i}}^{*}(\sigma_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{1}_{i}})-\sigma_{\mathbf{n}},

the right-hand side of this expression being the standard definition of the Lie difference
[
11
]
.

2. We use
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}
instead of
\bm{\Delta}
(which was designed for pure difference forms), as it is helpful to mirror the standard notation used for the differential variational bicomplex. Both
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}
and
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
are invariant under all allowable changes of the coordinates used to describe their respective spaces, namely
GL(p,\mathbb{Z})
transformations of the base space
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
and diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
(prolonged to
\mathbb{Z}^{p}\times P(\mathbb{R}^{q})
).

Lemma 3.1
.

The operators
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}
and
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
satisfy the identity

\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}.

(3.13)

Consequently, the operator
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}:=\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}+\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
satisfies
(\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle})^{2}=0
.

Proof.

To prove (
3.13
), apply
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
to an arbitrary
(k,l)
-form
\sigma
, then use the identities (
3.11
) and
\operatorname{S}_{i}(\partial f/\partial u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}})=\partial(\operatorname{S}_{i}\!f)/\partial(\operatorname{S}_{i}\!u^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{J}})
. The identity for
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}
follows from
(\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}})^{2}=0
and
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}^{2}=0
.
∎

Consider the following

This gives each fibre in the total space the structure of a symplectic manifold. Suppose that
the horizontal translation map

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!p_{1}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!q_{1}-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!p_{0}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!q_{0}=0.

As the augmented difference variational bicomplex is exact, there exists a Hamiltonian function

(p_{1}-p_{0})\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!q_{0}-(q_{1}-q_{0})\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!p_{1}=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!H.

q_{1}-q_{0}=\frac{\partial H(n,q_{0},p_{1})}{\partial p_{1}},\qquad p_{1}-p_{0}=-\,\frac{\partial H(n,q_{0},p_{1})}{\partial q_{0}}.

(4.3)

With the step-length incorporated into

Hamiltonian system; see
[
27

Reversing the above argument (with

defined on the total space and apply the map

q_{1}-q_{0}=\frac{\partial H(n,q_{0},p_{0})}{\partial p_{0}},\qquad p_{0}-p_{-1}=-\,\frac{\partial H(n,q_{0},p_{0})}{\partial q_{0}}.

(p_{0}-p_{-1})\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!q_{0}-(q_{1}-q_{0})\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!p_{0}=-\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!H,

\omega=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!p_{-1}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!q_{0}

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-(\operatorname{id}-\operatorname{S}^{-1})\\
\operatorname{S}-\operatorname{id}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q_{0}\\
p_{0}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial H(n,q_{0},p_{0})/\partial q_{0}\\
\partial H(n,q_{0},p_{0})/\partial p_{0}\end{array}\right).

The system (

(1,0)

So

For symplectic difference maps in general, at most one of the Hamiltonian function and the symplectic form is defined on the total space, so it is essential to work in an appropriate prolongation space.

So far, we have worked mainly in terms of the given coordinates. For an entirely coordinate-free formulation, the exterior difference operator

Let
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{u}
be multi-dimensional continuous independent and dependent variables, respectively. A system of PDEs is multisymplectic if (but not only if) it can be
represented as a variational problem with a Lagrangian

L[\mathbf{u}]=L_{\alpha}^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})D_{i}u^{\alpha}-H(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}).

(5.1)

Hence the Euler–Lagrange equations are

K_{\alpha\beta}^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})D_{i}u^{\beta}-\frac{\partial L_{\alpha}^{i}}{\partial x^{i}}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial u^{\alpha}}=0,

(5.2)

where

K^{i}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})=\frac{\partial L_{\beta}^{i}}{\partial u^{\alpha}}-\frac{\partial L_{\alpha}^{i}}{\partial u^{\beta}}.

(5.3)

Bridges
[
5
]
showed that closed symplectic two-forms can then be defined as

\kappa^{i}=\sum_{\alpha<\beta}K_{\alpha\beta}^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\operatorname{d}\!u^{\alpha}\wedge\operatorname{d}\!u^{\beta},

(5.4)

such that the structural conservation law
D_{i}\kappa^{i}=0
holds on solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equations.
Bridges
et al
.
[
7
]
generalized this approach by using the
differential variational bicomplex; see also
[
6
]
. Instead of closed symplectic
forms, one has vertically closed symplectic two-forms:

\kappa^{i}=\sum_{\alpha<\beta}K_{\alpha\beta}^{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\alpha}\wedge\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u^{\beta};

(5.5)

the structural conservation law
D_{i}\kappa^{i}
again vanishes on solutions of the Euler–Lagrange equations.

Similarly, for a system of P
\Delta
Es, a prerequisite of

standard

(0,2)

Multisymplectic integrator via the generalized difference variational bicomplex on non-uniform meshes

In this section, we generalize the difference variational bicomplex
on
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
to non-uniform meshes. Multisymplectic integrators
are re-investigated using the generalized bicomplex structure.

For the difference variational bicomplex proposed above, we
considered only uniform discrete independent variables, i.e.,

\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. However, with practical problems such as
discretization or numerical methods for a differential
system, we frequently encounter spaces of independent variables that
are not
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. Suppose the difference system is built on a
space coordinatized by
(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}},\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{n}})
where
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}}
and
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{n}}
are the
independent and dependent variables. The dimensions of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}}
and
\mathbf{n}

are both
p
, while we set the dimension of
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{n}}
to be
q
. Denote
the step size or the distance of two mesh points in each direction as

\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}:=d(\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{1}_{i}},\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}}),~~i=1,2,\ldots,p,

(6.1)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the distance of two points with respect to a given
metric.

Remark 6.1
.

If the points
\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{n}}\}
are located in the Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{p}
, then the distance is
\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}=x_{\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{1}_{i}}-x_{\mathbf{n}}
.
Otherwise, it depends on the specific geometric structure of the space. For example, if the
points are on a Riemannian manifold, then obviously the distance
connecting two points will be the length of the local geodesic
connecting them.

Analogously, the
exterior difference-derivative

\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}
from the mesh viewpoint is defined as a
summation of a horizontal operator
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}
, i.e., the exterior difference, and a
vertical operator
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
, i.e., the exterior derivative, as
follows:

\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}+\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}},

(6.2)

where

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{i}_{\mathbf{n}}\wedge\frac{\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}},\quad\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}:=\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}\!u_{\mathbf{J}}^{\alpha}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\mathbf{J}}^{\alpha}}.

(6.3)

Here the difference one-forms are

\Delta_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}=\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}\Delta^{i}
. The exterior derivative
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{v}}
is exactly the one given by (
3.10
).

Remark 6.2
.

When the limit
\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}\rightarrow 0
is taken,
\Delta_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}
tends to

\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}
and

\frac{\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}}
tends to the total
derivative
D_{i}
. Namely, the horizontal
operator
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}
is
an approximation of the horizontal derivative

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}\!=\operatorname{d}\!x^{i}\wedge D_{i}
(see (
2.9
)).

Rewrite the horizontal operator as follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Delta^{i}_{\mathbf{n}}\wedge\frac{\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}\Delta^{i}\wedge\frac{\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}^{i}}=\sum_{i}\Delta^{i}\wedge\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}.

(6.4)

This is exactly the same as the exterior difference

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{h}}^{\vartriangle}
we introduced
for the uniform lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
. Therefore, the two
exterior difference operators
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle_{\mathbf{n}}}

and
\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}
are consistent for any step size

\epsilon_{\mathbf{n}}
. Those properties of the exterior difference-derivative we studied above in
the uniform case still hold in the non-uniform case. In the following we study several numerical examples using the non-uniform difference variational bicomplex.

7

Conclusions

We established the theory of difference variational bicomplex, standing as
a geometric framework for the study of finite difference equations, particularly of
variational problems, and their symmetries, conservation laws, etc. Using the bicomplex, we re-examined the equations of motion arising from discrete mechanics, and in particular found an equivalent condition for the existence of Hamiltonians for such equations. It is also a natural structure for
investigating multisymplectic systems of P
\Delta
Es and serves as a proper theoretic foundation for multisymplectic integrators. Finite difference conservation laws of discrete multisymplectic systems could be obtained either through Noether’s first theorem, which was proved in a coordinate-free manner on the bicomplex, or by using difference multimomentum maps corresponding to groups of vertical transformations.
Furthermore, we were able to analyze multisymplectic integrators by generalizing the difference variational bicomplex from uniform lattices to non-uniform meshes, for instance, their discrete multisymplectic structures and variational structures. Various illustrative examples are provided.

following property is needed that for any
(p,m)
-form
\sigma

\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}(\sigma)=\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}\mathcal{I}^{\vartriangle}(\sigma),

\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}

\sigma=\mathcal{I}^{\vartriangle}(\sigma)+\operatorname{d}^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{h}}\!\tau
,
for some
\tau\in\Omega^{p-1,m}

\sigma\in\mathscr{F}^{l},l\geq 2

is a
(p,m)
-form with

A.19

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}\circ h_{\operatorname{v}}^{p,l}(\sigma)+\mathcal{I}^{\vartriangle}\circ h_{\operatorname{v}}^{p,l+1}\circ\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}(\sigma)

(A.22)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}\circ\mathcal{I}^{\vartriangle}\circ h_{\operatorname{v}}^{p,l}(\sigma)+\mathcal{I}^{\vartriangle}\circ h_{\operatorname{v}}^{p,l+1}\circ\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}\left(\mathcal{H}^{l}(\sigma)\right)+\mathcal{H}^{l+1}\left(\delta^{\vartriangle}_{\operatorname{v}}(\sigma)\right),

where the homotopy operators

\mathcal{H}^{l}:\mathscr{F}^{l}\rightarrow\mathscr{F}^{l-1},l\geq 2

\mathcal{H}^{l}=\mathcal{I}^{\vartriangle}\circ h_{\operatorname{v}}^{p,l}
