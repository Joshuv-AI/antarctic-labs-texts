Spectacularly large expansion coefficients in Müntz’s theorem
Lloyd N. Trefethen

Abstract
Müntz’s theorem asserts, for example, that the even powers 1 , x 2 , x 4 , … 1,x^{2},x^{4},\dots are dense in C ⁡ ( [ 0 , 1 ] ) C([\kern 0.3pt0,1]) . We show that the associated expansions are so inefficient as to have no conceivable relevance to any actual computation. For example, approximating f ⁡ ( x ) = x f(x)=x to accuracy ε = 10 − 6 \varepsilon=10^{-6} in this basis requires powers larger than x 280,000 x^{280{,}000} and coefficients larger than 10 107,000 10^{107{,}000} . We present a theorem establishing exponential growth of coefficients with respect to 1 / ε 1/\varepsilon .

Introduction

The Müntz approximation theorem, conjectured by Bernstein in
1912
b12
and proved by Müntz in 1914
muentz
, is a beautiful result.
Suppose we are interested in continuous functions on
[\kern 0.3pt0,1]
,
i.e.,
f\in C([\kern 0.3pt0,1])
, and we want to approximate them as linear
combinations of the monomials

x^{a_{1}},x^{a_{2}},\dots,

where
\{a_{k}\}
is a set of exponents (not necessarily
integers) satisfying

0=a_{0}<a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots.

Certainly this is possible if
a_{k}=k
for each
k
, by the
Weierstrass approximation theorem
atap
, but what
if the set of powers is sparser? For example, are the even
powers

1,x^{2},x^{4},\dots

(1)

dense in
C([\kern 0.3pt0,1])
? The theorem characterizes all suitable sets of exponents:

Theorem 1

(Müntz approximation theorem)
.

The family
\{x^{a_{k}}\}
is dense in
C([0,1])
if and only
if

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{1\over a_{k}}=\infty.

(2)

Thus the set (
1
) easily qualifies, as do many other
collections of exponents, such as the primes:

1,x^{2},x^{3},\dots,x^{89},x^{97},\dots.

(3)

For discussions
of the theorem with proofs, see
almira
,
be
, and
lz
.
After 1914, Müntz’s theorem was generalized by
Szász and others.

As a numerical analyst, I work with algorithms based on
expanding functions in nonorthogonal bases, a powerful technique in
certain contexts
fna1
;
rect
.
This led me to consider Müntz’s theorem from a computational
angle, and what emerged is startling. To make the point, it
is enough to consider a particular case of
what might be regarded as the most basic
nontrivial Müntz approximation. The name “E” alludes to the use
of even powers.

Problem E. Given
\varepsilon>0
, find an integer
n\geq 0
and coefficients

c_{0},\dots,c_{n}
such that

\Bigl|\kern 1.0ptx-\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}x^{2k}\Bigr|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad x\in[\kern 0.3pt0,1].

(4)

We shall prove:

Theorem 2
.

If
\varepsilon<1/2
, then any solution of Problem E has

n>{1\over 20\kern 0.5pt\varepsilon}

(5)

and

\max_{k}|c_{k}|>0.75\kern 0.7pt\varepsilon\kern 0.7pt2^{\kern 0.8pt1/(40\kern 0.4pt\varepsilon)}.

(6)

Actually, I believe the following sharper bounds hold:

n>{1\over 8\varepsilon},

(7)

\max_{k}|c_{k}|>{(1+\sqrt{2}\kern 1.0pt)^{2\kern 0.4ptn}\over 16\kern 0.5ptn^{1.5}}.

(8)

For accuracy
\varepsilon=10^{-6}
, my estimate is that
one needs
n>140{,}000
and
\max_{k}|c_{k}|>10^{107{,}000}
.
In such an expansion, the enormous
coefficients have oscillating signs, so that they cancel
almost exactly (namely to one part in
10^{107{,}000}
).
On a computer in floating-point arithmetic, all information
will be lost unless one works in a precision of more than
107{,}000
digits.
(The usual precision is 16 digits.)

Proof

Problem E is
equivalent to the more familiar problem of approximation of
|x|
on
[-1,1]
:

\Bigl||x|-\sum_{k=0}^{2\kern 0.3ptn}c_{k}x^{2k}\Bigr|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad x\in[-1,1].

(9)

Since Lebesgue first used approximations of
|x|
for a proof of
the Weierstrass approximation theorem at age 23 in 1898, a great deal has
been learned about this problem, as recounted in
chapter 25 of
atap
. In particular, Bernstein’s 1914 paper
bernstein

was a landmark contribution. Among many other things, Bernstein
proved that
\varepsilon
satisfies

\varepsilon>{1\over 4(1+\sqrt{2}\kern 1.0pt)(2\kern 0.3ptn-1)}

(10)

for any
n\geq 1
, which implies, since
4(1+\sqrt{2}\kern 1.0pt)\approx 9.66
,

n>{1\over 20\kern 0.5pt\varepsilon}.

(11)

Since
\varepsilon<1/2
in (
9
) implies
n\geq 1
, this
establishes condition (
5
) of Theorem
2
.

To establish condition (
6
), we make use of (
11
).
Given
\varepsilon
, let

n
and
\{c_{k}\}
define a solution (
4
) of Problem E. If we
split the series into roughly the first quarter and the last three-quarters,

\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor 1/(80\kern 0.3pt\varepsilon)\rfloor}c_{k}x^{2k}+\sum_{k=1+\lfloor 1/(80\kern 0.3pt\varepsilon)\rfloor}^{n}c_{k}x^{2k},

(12)

then by (
11
), the first part can approximate
|x|
no more
closely than
4\kern 0.5pt\varepsilon
. More to our purpose, by a linear scaling,
it can approximate
|x|
over the subinterval
[-1/2,1/2]
no more
closely than
2\kern 0.5pt\varepsilon
. Therefore, since the sum of
the two series in (
12
) has accuracy better than
\varepsilon
,
the second series must have

\varepsilon
over
[-1/2,1/2]

|x^{2k}|\leq 2^{-2k}

for

huge coefficients. Specifically, summing a power

4

\sum_{k=1+\lfloor 1/(80\kern 0.3pt\varepsilon)\rfloor}^{n}|c_{k}x^{2k}|\leq{4\over 3}\kern 1.0pt\max_{k}|c_{k}|\kern 1.0pt2^{-2(\lfloor 1/(80\kern 0.3pt\varepsilon)\rfloor+1)}.

(13)

Therefore we must have

{4\over 3}\kern 1.0pt\max_{k}|c_{k}|\kern 1.0pt2^{-2(\lfloor 1/(80\kern 0.3pt\varepsilon)\rfloor+1)}>\varepsilon,

(14)

that is,

\max_{k}|c_{k}|>0.75\kern 0.7pt\varepsilon\kern 0.8pt2^{2(\lfloor 1/(80\kern 0.3pt\varepsilon)\rfloor+1)}.

(15)

This implies (
6

Numerical estimates

The theorem and proof just given were all about lower bounds, but now let us look
at more accurate (though unrigorous) estimates. Bernstein

also proved
that the best degree
2\kern 0.4ptn
maximum-norm

\varepsilon\sim{\beta\over 2\kern 0.4ptn},\quad n\to\infty

\beta\approx 0.28016949902386913303643649\dots.

A remark about mathematics

Theorem
2
is startling and interesting. From the usual
mathematical point of view, however, it is
not much more than that. After all, Müntz’s theorem remains
valid and beautiful. From the usual mathematical perspective,
Müntz’s theorem expresses a fundamental truth, and Theorem
2
,
however interesting, is an engineering footnote.

As I have discussed in the context of other problems
yogi
;
apology
,
I believe this usual perspective is too comfortable.
Theorem
2
implies that typical
sets of powers deemed useful by Müntz’s theorem would
in fact be useless in any actual application. If it was not the business
of mathematicians to notice and analyze this effect in the past 110 years,
then whose business was it?
