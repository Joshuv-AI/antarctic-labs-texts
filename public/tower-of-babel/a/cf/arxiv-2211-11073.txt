Line Defects in Fermionic CFTs


Abstract
We study line defects in the fermionic CFTs in the Gross-Neveu-Yukawa universality class in dimensions 2 < d < 4 2<d<4 . These CFTs may be described as the IR fixed points of the Gross-Neveu-Yukawa (GNY) model in d = 4 − ϵ d=4-\epsilon , or as the UV fixed points of the Gross-Neveu (GN) model, which can be studied using the large N N expansion in 2 < d < 4 2<d<4 . These models admit natural line defects obtained by integrating over a line either the scalar field in the GNY description, or the fermion bilinear operator in the GN description. We compute the beta function for the defect RG flow using both the epsilon expansion and the large N N approach, and find IR stable fixed points for the defect coupling, thus providing evidence for a non-trivial IR DCFT. We also compute some of the DCFT observables at the fixed point, and check that the g g -function associated with the circular defect is consistent with the g g -theorem for the defect RG flow.

Line defect in the Gross-Neveu-Yukawa description

Let us start by discussing the line defect in the Gross-Neveu-Yukawa model

S=\int d^{d}x\left(\frac{(\partial_{\mu}s)^{2}}{2}-\left(\bar{\Psi}_{i}\gamma\cdot\partial\Psi^{i}+g_{1,0}s\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}\right)+\frac{g_{2,0}}{24}s^{4}\right)+h_{0}\int d\tau s(\tau,\mathbf{0}).

(2.1)

\begin{split}(g_{1}^{*})^{2}&=\frac{(4\pi)^{2}}{N+6}\epsilon\xrightarrow{\textrm{Large N}}\frac{(4\pi)^{2}}{N}\epsilon\\
g_{2}^{*}&=\frac{(4\pi)^{2}\left(-N+6+\sqrt{N^{2}+132N+36}\right)}{6(N+6)}\epsilon\xrightarrow{\textrm{Large N}}\frac{12(4\pi)^{2}}{N}\epsilon.\end{split}

(2.3)

Similarly for the defect coupling, we may express the bare coupling in terms of the renormalized coupling as

h_{0}=M^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\left(h+\frac{\delta h}{\epsilon}+\dots\right).

(2.4)

by requiring that the counterterms cancel the divergences appearing in the one-point function
\langle s(x)\rangle
. At leading order in the bulk coupling

DCFT data

2.1.1
Defect operator dimensions: mapping to
H^{2}\times S^{d-2}

We now study the spectrum of defect primaries induced by the bulk scalar and fermions. The operators induced by the bulk fermions are in the fundamental representation of
U(N)
while the operators induced by the scalar
s
are
U(N)
singlets. There is a tower of scalars and fermions on the defect, and it is easiest to see this by performing a Weyl transformation to
H^{2}\times S^{d-2}
. Such an approach to obtain DCFT data has been used before in

Recall that the metric of flat space is related to the metric of
H^{2}\times S^{d-2}
by a Weyl transformation

ds^{2}=\rho^{2}\left(\frac{d\rho^{2}+d\tau^{2}}{\rho^{2}}+ds^{2}_{S^{d-2}}\right)=\rho^{2}ds^{2}_{H^{2}\times S^{d-2}}.

(2.16)

In this picture, the defect is located at the boundary of
H^{2}
. The action of the GNY model on this space is

\begin{split}S&=\int d^{d}x\sqrt{g}\left(\frac{(\nabla_{\mu}s)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(d-2)(d-4)}{8}s^{2}-\left(\bar{\Psi}_{i}\gamma\cdot\nabla\Psi^{i}+g_{1,0}s\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}\right)+\frac{g_{2,0}}{24}s^{4}\right)\\
&+h_{0}\int d\tau s(\tau,\mathbf{0}).\end{split}

(2.17)

We then perform a KK reduction on
S^{d-2}
to obtain a theory on
H^{2}
. To do that, we split the gamma matrices into blocks as
[
33
,
34
,
35
]

\gamma^{1}=\sigma^{1}\otimes I,\hskip 14.22636pt\gamma^{2}=\sigma^{2}\otimes I,\hskip 14.22636pt\gamma^{i}=\sigma^{3}\otimes\Gamma^{i}

(2.18)

where
I
is the
c_{d-2}

\Gamma^{i}
are gamma matrices in
d-2

\sigma^{i}
are Pauli matrices. The scalar Laplacian and Dirac operator split as

(\nabla^{2})_{H^{2}\times S^{d-2}}=\nabla^{2}_{H^{2}}+\nabla^{2}_{S^{d-2}},\hskip 28.45274pt\left(\not{\nabla}\right)_{H^{2}\times S^{d-2}}=\not{\nabla}_{H^{2}}\otimes I+\sigma^{3}\otimes\not{\nabla}_{S^{d-2}}.

(2.19)

We then expand the scalar and the fermion into the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian and the Dirac operator on
S^{d-2}

\begin{split}s&=\sum_{l,m}t_{lm}(\rho,\tau)Y_{lm},\hskip 28.45274pt\left(\nabla^{2}\right)_{S^{d-2}}Y_{lm}=-l(l+d-3)Y_{lm}\\
\Psi&=\sum_{l,m}\left(\psi^{+}_{lm}\otimes\chi^{+}_{lm}+\psi^{-}_{lm}\otimes\chi^{-}_{lm}\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\left(\not{\nabla}\right)_{S^{d-2}}\chi^{\pm}_{lm}=\pm i\left(l+\frac{d}{2}-1\right)\chi^{\pm}_{lm}.\end{split}

(2.20)

The sum over
l
runs from
0
to infinity, while the sum over
m
runs over the degeneracy of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian and Dirac operator, respectively. With this reduction, we obtain an effective action on
H^{2}
. The quadratic terms of this effective action are as follows

\begin{split}S_{2}&=\int\frac{d\tau d\rho}{\rho^{2}}\sum_{l,m}\bigg[\frac{\nabla_{\mu}t^{*}_{l,m}\nabla^{\mu}t_{l,m}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(l(l+d-3)+\frac{(d-2)(d-4)}{4}\right)t^{*}_{l,m}t_{l,m}\\
&-\sum_{\pm}\left(\bar{\psi}_{lm}^{\pm}\not{\nabla}_{H^{2}}\psi_{lm}^{\pm}\pm i\left(l+\frac{d}{2}-1\right)(\bar{\psi}_{lm}^{\pm})^{\dagger}\sigma^{3}\psi^{\pm}_{lm}\right)\bigg].\end{split}

(2.21)

We are assuming the following normalizations for the eigenfunctions

\int_{S^{2}}Y^{*}_{l,m}Y_{l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}},\hskip 14.22636pt\int_{S^{2}}{\chi^{\pm}}_{lm}\chi_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

(2.22)

In the action above, we have fermions with chiral mass but we can perform a rotation
\psi\rightarrow e^{i\alpha\sigma^{3}}\psi

\alpha

\alpha=\pi/4

-i\bar{\psi}\sigma^{3}\psi\rightarrow\bar{\psi}\psi

\hat{\Delta}_{l}^{s}=\frac{d}{2}-1+l,\hskip 28.45274pt\hat{\Delta}_{l}^{f}=\frac{d-1}{2}+l

(2.23)

for scalars and fermions respectively.

Recall that the two-point function of the bulk scalar with the transverse spin

\langle s(x)\hat{s}_{l}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{w})\rangle=\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})^{l}}{|\mathbf{x}|^{\Delta_{s}-\hat{\Delta}^{s}_{l}+l}(\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2})^{\hat{\Delta}^{s}_{l}}}

where

\frac{\nabla^{2}\langle s(x)\hat{s}_{l}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{w})\rangle}{\langle s(x)\hat{s}_{l}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{w})\rangle}=\left[\frac{2\hat{\Delta}^{s}_{l}\left(2\Delta_{s}-d+2\right)}{\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2}}-\frac{\left(\Delta_{s}-\hat{\Delta}^{s}_{l}+l\right)\left(d-3+l-\Delta_{s}+\hat{\Delta}^{s}_{l}\right)}{\mathbf{x}^{2}}\right].

(2.25)

We then use the fact that the bulk scalar satisfies an equation of motion, which implies the following equation for this two-point function, to leading order in
\epsilon

\nabla^{2}\langle s(x)\hat{s}_{l}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{w})\rangle=\frac{g_{2}}{2}\langle s^{2}(x)\rangle\langle s(x)\hat{s}_{l}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{w})\rangle-g_{1}^{2}\int d^{d}x_{1}\langle\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}(x)\bar{\Psi}_{j}\Psi^{j}(x_{1})\rangle\langle s(x_{1})\hat{s}_{l}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{w})\rangle.

(2.26)

Since there are explicit factors of the coupling constant on the R.H.S., we may plug in the correlators in the free theory on R.H.S. and we will get the L.H.S. correct to order
\epsilon
. This tells us that the anomalous dimension of the defect operators must satisfy

\begin{split}&\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})^{l}}{(\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2})^{1+l}}\left[\frac{2\gamma_{s}(2+2l)}{\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2}}+\frac{\left(\hat{\gamma}^{s}_{l}-\gamma_{s}\right)(1+2l)}{\mathbf{x}^{2}}\right]\\
&=\frac{g_{2}h^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})^{l}}{(\mathbf{x}^{2})(\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2})^{1+l}}-\frac{g_{1}^{2}N\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{2}}{4\pi^{d}}\int d^{d}x_{1}\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})^{l}}{\left((x-x_{1})^{2}\right)^{d-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{2}+(\tau_{1}-\tau^{\prime})^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}-1+l}}\\
&=\frac{g_{2}h^{2}}{32\pi^{2}}\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})^{l}}{(\mathbf{x}^{2})(\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2})^{1+l}}+\frac{g_{1}^{2}N(1+l)}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})^{l}}{(\mathbf{x}^{2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2})^{2+l}}\end{split}

(2.27)

where we used that
\mathbf{w}^{2}=0
. This reproduces the correct anomalous dimension of the bulk scalar
\gamma_{s}=Ng_{1}^{2}/(32\pi^{2})
(see
[
6
]
, and also the
Z_{s}
factor in (
2.9
)), and tells us that the defect operator of spin
l
has dimensions

\hat{\gamma}^{s}_{l}=\gamma_{s}+\frac{9\epsilon}{(N+6)(1+2l)}\implies\hat{\Delta}^{s}_{l}=\Delta_{s}+l+\frac{9\epsilon}{(N+6)(1+2l)}=1+l+\frac{6(1-l)}{(N+6)(1+2l)}\epsilon.

(2.28)

where we used the known results for the fixed point value of the bulk coupling constants
[
6
]
. In the limit
l\rightarrow\infty
, the anomalous dimension of the defect primaries is the same as the anomalous dimension of bulk operator, which is consistent with the results of
[
41
]
. The
l=1
operator should be identified with the displacement and should have protected dimension equal to
2
to all orders in perturbation theory. This follows from the equation that defines the displacement

\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu i}(x)=D^{i}(\tau)\delta^{d-1}(\mathbf{x})

(2.29)

where
i
here represents transverse directions.

This technique of using bulk equations of motion to obtain the anomalous dimensions of defect primaries can also be used in the
O(N)
model with a localized magnetic field line defect. We perform this calculation in Appendix
C
and show its consistency with the computation in
[
18
]
of the scaling dimensions of
l=0
and
l=1
defect primaries.

For the leading
l=0
operator (let us denote it by
\hat{s}
), there is another way to calculate its scaling dimensions by adapting to the case of a line defect the standard argument that relates the scaling dimension of the perturbing operator to the derivative of the beta function (see for instance
[
42
]
). To obtain such a relation, note that away from the fixed point, there is a defect “stress-tensor”
T_{D}
localized on the defect
[
21
]
, which in this case is proportional to the beta function of the coupling
h

T_{D}(\tau)=\beta_{h}\hat{s}(\tau).

(2.30)

This defect stress-tensor must have fixed dimension equal to one, hence when we differentiate the above equation with respect to the renormalization scale
M
, we get

\Delta_{\hat{s}}=1+\frac{\partial\beta_{h}}{\partial h}.

(2.31)

In the UV DCFT, i.e.
h=0
, the defect is trivial, so
\Delta_{\hat{s}}
is the same as the dimension
\Delta_{s}
of the bulk operator
s
(note that this also implies that in the beta function, the term linear in
h
must be equal to
\Delta(s)-1
, which is true). In the IR DCFT, we have

\Delta_{\hat{s}}=1+\frac{\partial\beta_{h}}{\partial h}\bigg|_{h=h_{*}}=1+\frac{6\epsilon}{N+6}.

(2.32)

Note that this agrees with the result (
2.28
) obtained above from the equation of motion method, for
l=0
. One can see that the operator
\hat{s}
is irrelevant in the IR DCFT. Since there is no other candidate for a relevant operator on the defect, this implies that the IR DCFT is stable.

Next, we make a comment on the anomalous dimension of the defect fermions. We could proceed as we did above for the scalar. However, this involves working out the tensor structures involved in bulk-defect two point function of a fermion. We leave this for a future work, and here just point out how this may be done by the usual Feynman diagram approach. We need to compute the two-point function of the fermion localized on the defect. To leading order in perturbation theory, we get

\begin{split}\langle\bar{\Psi}_{i}(\tau_{1})\Psi^{j}(\tau_{2})\rangle&=\delta_{i}^{j}\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\gamma^{0}(\tau_{12})}{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}|\tau_{12}|^{d}}-\frac{hg_{1}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{3}}{8(d-2)\pi^{\frac{3d}{2}}}\int d^{d}xd\tau\frac{\gamma\cdot(x-\tau_{1})\gamma\cdot(x-\tau_{2})}{|x-\tau_{1}|^{d}|x-\tau_{2}|^{d}|x-\tau|^{d-2}}\right]\\
&=\delta_{i}^{j}\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\gamma^{0}(\tau_{12})}{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}|\tau_{12}|^{d}}-\frac{hg_{1}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-3}{2}\right)}{16\pi^{\frac{3d-1}{2}}}\int d^{d}x\frac{\gamma\cdot(x-\tau_{1})\gamma\cdot(x-\tau_{2})}{|x-\tau_{1}|^{d}|x-\tau_{2}|^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}^{2}\right)^{\frac{d-3}{2}}}\right]\\
&=\delta_{i}^{j}\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\gamma^{0}(\tau_{12})}{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}|\tau_{12}|^{d}}-\frac{hg_{1}(4-d)\Gamma\left(d-\frac{5}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{5-d}{2}\right)}{2^{7-d}\pi^{d-1}(d-3)\Gamma\left(3-\frac{d}{2}\right)(\tau_{12}^{2})^{d-\frac{5}{2}}}\right]\end{split}

(2.33)

To go from the second line to the third line, we first did the integral over
x^{0}
by introducing a Feynman parameter, then performed the integral over
\mathbf{x}
by introducing another Feynman parameter, and finally performed the integral over the two Feynman parameters. Note that to obtain the correct spinor structure, we need to project this two-point function onto the defect fermion representation. But it is already clear that the order
g_{1}
term actually vanishes in
d=4
and does not contribute to the anomalous dimension of defect fermions. So the anomalous dimension must start at order
g_{1}^{2}
. The fact that this diagram should not contribute to anomalous dimensions is also clear from a symmetry argument: there is a parity symmetry in the original theory which is broken by the defect. It may be restored if we demand that
h\rightarrow-h
under the symmetry. So the anomalous dimensions must be even functions of
h
.

2.1.2
g-function

In this subsection, we calculate the defect
g
-function defined as the normalized partition function in the presence of a circular defect of radius
R

\log g=\log\left(Z^{\textrm{bulk $+$ defect}}/Z^{\textrm{bulk}}\right).

(2.34)

It was shown in
[
21
]
that the defect entropy defined by

s=\left(1-R\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\right)\log g

(2.35)

decreases monotonically along the defect RG flows. Using the fact the
\log g
satisfies the Callan-Symanzik equation

\left(R\frac{\partial}{\partial R}+\beta_{h}\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\right)\log g=0

(2.36)

we see that at the fixed point,
\log g
and
s
must be equal.

For the trivial defect,
\log g=0
and since
h
generates an RG flow, we know that there must be a non-trivial IR DCFT with
\log g<0
. In what follows, we will calculate this quantity at the IR fixed point. Let’s place the defect on a unit circle around the origin parametrized by
\tau
,
x^{\mu}(\tau)=\left(\cos\tau,\sin\tau,0,\dots\right)
. There are three diagrams that we need, to calculate this to leading order in
\epsilon

\log g=\underset{\hbox{$(a)$}}{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{logg_a.pdf}}}}\ \ +\ \ \underset{\hbox{$(b)$}}{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{logg_b.pdf}}}}\ \ +\ \ \underset{\hbox{$(c)$}}{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{logg_c.pdf}}}}.

(2.37)

The diagrams
(a)
and
(b)
were already calculated in
[
18
]
. In
d=4-\epsilon
, their results are

\begin{split}(a)&=\frac{h_{0}^{2}}{2}\int d\tau_{1}^{\prime}d\tau_{2}^{\prime}\langle s(x(\tau_{1}^{\prime}))s(x(\tau_{2}^{\prime}))\rangle=-\frac{\epsilon}{8}h_{0}^{2}+O(\epsilon^{2}),\\
(b)&=\frac{h_{0}^{4}g_{2,0}}{24}\int d^{d}x_{1}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\left(\int d\tau_{i}^{\prime}\langle s(x(\tau_{i}^{\prime}))s(x_{1})\rangle\right)=\frac{h_{0}^{4}g_{2,0}}{384\pi^{2}}+O(\epsilon).\end{split}

(2.38)

Here we calculate the last one involving a fermion loop

\begin{split}(c)&=\frac{h_{0}^{2}g_{1,0}^{2}N_{f}c_{d}(d-2)^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)^{4}}{2(4\pi^{d/2})^{4}}\int\frac{d^{d}x_{1}d^{d}x_{2}d\tau_{1}^{\prime}d\tau_{2}^{\prime}}{\left(x_{12}^{2}\right)^{d-1}\left(\left(x(\tau_{1}^{\prime})-x_{1}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(\left(x(\tau_{2}^{\prime})-x_{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\\
&=-\frac{h_{0}^{2}g_{1,0}^{2}N\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)^{2}}{128\pi^{d}(d-3)(4-d)}\int\frac{d\tau d\tau^{\prime}}{\left(\left(x(\tau)-x(\tau^{\prime})\right)^{2}\right)^{d-3}}\\
&=-\frac{h_{0}^{2}g_{1,0}^{2}N\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{7-2d}{2}\right)}{2^{2d-1}\pi^{d-\frac{3}{2}}(d-3)\Gamma\left(5-d\right)}.\end{split}

(2.39)

In
d=4-\epsilon
, this is

(c)=\frac{h_{0}^{2}g_{1,0}^{2}N}{64\pi^{2}}+O(\epsilon).

(2.40)

Combining all three terms and plugging in the bare couplings in terms of renormalized ones, we get

\log g=-\frac{\epsilon}{8}h^{2}+\frac{h^{4}g_{2}}{768\pi^{2}}+\frac{h^{2}g_{1}^{2}N}{128\pi^{2}}+O(g_{2}^{2},g_{1}^{4}).

(2.41)

It may be checked that its derivative with respect to
h
is equal to
\beta_{h}/2
. The fact that
\partial\log g/\partial h\propto\beta_{h}
is expected on general grounds (see for instance
[
22
,
18
]
for related discussions).

At the fixed point, we finally find

\log g\bigg|_{h=h_{*}}=-\frac{81\epsilon}{2(N+6)\left(6-N+\sqrt{N^{2}+132N+36}\right)}.

(2.42)

This is negative, consistent with the
g
-theorem.

3
Line defect at large
N

In this section, we study the line defect in the large
N
description given by the action in (
1.4
). As is well known, the
1/N
perturbation theory in this model may be developed by using the usual propagator for
\Psi
and the following resummed propagator for
\sigma
(see for instance
[
7
]
)

\langle\sigma_{0}(x)\sigma_{0}(0)\rangle=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}{|x|^{2+\delta}},\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}=-\frac{2^{d}\sin\left(\frac{\pi d}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)}

(3.1)

where we introduced a regulator
\delta
which we will set to zero at the end. This makes the dimension of the bare field
1+\delta/2
at large
N
, so the bare coupling
h_{0}
also acquires a dimension
-\delta/2
. We also need to introduce a coupling
\tilde{g}
in the bulk with dimension
-\delta/2

S=\int d^{d}x\left(\bar{\Psi}_{i}\gamma\cdot\partial\Psi^{i}+\frac{\tilde{g}_{0}}{\sqrt{N}}\sigma_{0}\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}\right)+h_{0}\int d\tau\sigma_{0}(\tau,\mathbf{0}).

(3.2)

Note that this coupling
\tilde{g}
is really just a rescaling of the
\sigma
field and in particular, the
\beta
-function of
g
must vanish at
\delta=0

\tilde{g}_{0}=M^{-\delta/2}\tilde{g},\hskip 28.45274pt\beta_{\tilde{g}}=\frac{\delta}{2}\tilde{g}.

(3.3)

We will set the renormalized
\tilde{g}=1
at the end. As before, we express the defect bare coupling in terms of the renormalized coupling as

h_{0}=M^{-\delta/2}\left(h+\frac{\delta_{1}h}{\delta}+\dots\right).

(3.4)

Following the same procedure as in the GNY case, we calculate the one-point function of
\sigma
and hence calculate the counterterm
\delta_{1}h
. The one-point function gets contributions from the following diagrams

\langle\sigma_{0}(x)\rangle=\underset{\hbox{$(a)$}}{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{GNLargeNOnePoint_a.pdf}}}}\ \ +\ \ \underset{\hbox{$(b)$}}{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{GNLargeNOnePoint_b.pdf}}}}\ \ +\ \ \underset{\hbox{$(c)$}}{\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{GNLargeNOnePoint_c.pdf}}}}.

(3.5)

The first diagram is just the tree level piece

(a)=-h_{0}\int d\tau^{\prime}\langle\sigma_{0}(x)\sigma_{0}(\tau^{\prime},\mathbf{0})\rangle=-\frac{h_{0}\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}\pi}{|\mathbf{x}|}.

(3.6)

For the other two loop-diagrams, we will only extract the
1/\delta
poles. The divergent piece of the second diagram has the structure

(b)=\frac{h_{0}\tilde{g}^{4}b}{N|\mathbf{x}|\delta}.

(3.7)

Notice that this diagram is similar to the one used to calculate the
O(1/N)
contribution to the
\sigma
anomalous dimension in the usual theory without defect, except that one of its points is integrated over a line. So we should be able to relate the coefficient
b
to the bulk anomalous dimension of
\sigma
. To do that, note that if we were to calculate the anomalous dimension of the field
\sigma
by calculating the divergent piece of the two-point function, we would instead get

Z_{\sigma}^{2}\langle\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\rangle=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{\tilde{g}^{4}b}{N\pi|x|^{2}\delta}.

(3.8)

Thus
Z_{\sigma}=1-\frac{\tilde{g}^{4}b}{2N\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}\delta}
and
\gamma_{\sigma}=\beta_{\tilde{g}}(\partial Z_{\sigma}/\partial\tilde{g})
. So we can write
b
in terms of the anomalous dimension of
\sigma

b=-N\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}\gamma_{\sigma},\hskip 14.22636pt\gamma_{\sigma}=\frac{2^{d+1}(d-1)\sin\left(\frac{\pi d}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{Nd(d-2)\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)}

(3.9)

where we used
\gamma_{\sigma}
from
[
6
]
.

Finally, the last diagram involves the following integral

\begin{split}(c)&=-\frac{\tilde{g}_{0}^{4}h_{0}^{3}\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}^{4}M^{-4\delta}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{4}}{16N\pi^{2d}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{3}d^{d}x_{i}d\tau^{\prime}_{i}\frac{1}{|x_{i}-x(\tau^{\prime}_{i})|^{2+\delta}}\right)\int d^{d}x_{4}\frac{1}{|x-x_{4}|^{2+\delta}}\\
&\times\frac{(x_{14}\cdot x_{12})(x_{23}\cdot x_{34})-(x_{14}\cdot x_{23})(x_{12}\cdot x_{34})+(x_{14}\cdot x_{34})(x_{23}\cdot x_{12})}{|x_{12}|^{d}|x_{23}|^{d}|x_{34}|^{d}|x_{14}|^{d}}.\end{split}

(3.10)

This integral looks very hard to calculate, but we know that the result is supposed to take the form

(c)=\frac{\tilde{g}_{0}^{4}h_{0}^{3}C(\delta)M^{-4\delta}}{|\mathbf{x}|^{1+4\delta}}\approx\frac{h_{0}^{3}\tilde{g}_{0}^{4}}{|\mathbf{x}|}\left(\frac{c}{N\delta}+\textrm{finite terms as $\delta\rightarrow 0$}\right).

(3.11)

We are only interested in the constant
c
for the purposes of calculating the
\beta
-function. We calculate this constant in Appendix
B
and here we just report the result

c=-\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}^{4}\pi^{4}\Gamma\left(\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}\left[(d-3)\left(\psi\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)-\psi(d-3)\right)-1\right]}{12(d-3)\Gamma(d-2)\sin\left(\frac{d\pi}{2}\right)}.

(3.12)

To cancel the
1/\delta
divergences, the bare coupling should then be

h_{0}=M^{-\delta/2}\left(h+\frac{h\tilde{g}^{4}b}{2N\delta\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}+\frac{h^{3}\tilde{g}^{4}c}{N\delta\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}+\dots\right).

(3.13)

Setting
\tilde{g}=1
, this gives the
\beta
-function for
h
and the fixed point at leading order in
N

\begin{split}\beta_{h}&=-\frac{hb}{N\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}-\frac{3h^{3}c}{N\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}\\
h_{*}^{2}&=\frac{N\gamma_{\sigma}\pi\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}{3c}=-\frac{2^{d+5}\pi^{\frac{3}{2}(d-3)}(d-3)(d-2)\left(1-\cos(\pi d)\right)\Gamma(\tfrac{d-1}{2})\Gamma(d)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{d}{2}-1\right)^{3}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)^{3}d\left((d-3)H_{\frac{d}{2}-2}+(3-d)H_{d-4}-1\right)}\end{split}

(3.14)

where
H_{a}
is the harmonic number and
b
and
c
are given by Eqs. (
3.9
) and (
3.12
), respectively. This immediately gives us the scaling dimension of the defect operator
\hat{\sigma}
as

\Delta(\hat{\sigma})=1+\frac{\partial\beta_{h}}{\partial h}\bigg|_{h=h_{*}}=1-\frac{2^{d+2}(d-1)\sin\left(\frac{\pi d}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}{Nd(d-2)\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)}.

(3.15)

In
d=4-\epsilon
, this is

\Delta(\hat{\sigma})\bigg|_{d=4-\epsilon}=1+\frac{6\epsilon}{N}+O(1/N^{2}).

(3.16)

This is consistent with what we found above using
\epsilon
expansion techniques for the lowest dimension scalar on the defect, (
2.32
). We may also calculate the regularized one-point function of
\sigma
at leading order in
N

\langle\sigma(x)\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}a_{\sigma}}{|\mathbf{x}|^{1}}=-\frac{h_{*}\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}\pi}{|\mathbf{x}|}\implies a_{\sigma}^{2}=-\frac{(d-3)(d-1)}{(d-2)d\left((d-3)H_{\frac{d}{2}-2}+(3-d)H_{d-4}-1\right)}.

(3.17)

To compare with GNY results, the
\sigma
operator in the large
N
formalism must be identified with
s
in the GNY formalism, up to normalization. In
d=4-\epsilon
, the above result for the normalized one-point function of
\sigma
is equal to
3/8+O(\epsilon)
, which is consistent with (
2.15
).

Let us now discuss the spectrum of defect operators at large
N
. By the same arguments we gave above in
2.1.1
, the spectrum of defect fermions at leading order in large
N
is still the same as described by (
2.23
). However, the
\sigma
operator is no longer a free field even at infinite
N
, so the spectrum of defect scalars differs from (
2.23
). To obtain this spectrum, note that at leading order in large
N
, the two-point function of
\sigma
is given by

\langle\sigma(x_{1})\sigma(x_{2})\rangle=\frac{h_{*}^{2}\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}^{2}\pi^{2}}{|\mathbf{x}_{1}||\mathbf{x}_{2}|}+\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}}{x_{12}^{2}}.

(3.18)

To obtain the leading large
N
defect spectrum, we should decompose the above two-point function into defect channel conformal blocks (
[
40
,
43
]
). The first term above is the contribution of the identity operator on the defect, while the second term comes from an infinite tower of defect operators with transverse spin
l
and dimensions
1+l+2m
, as was shown in
[
43
]

\frac{1}{x_{12}^{2}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}_{1}||\mathbf{x}_{2}|}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}b^{2}_{m,l}\hat{f}_{1+l+2m,l}

(3.19)

where
\hat{f}_{1+l+2m,l}
is the defect channel conformal block for the scalar two-point function. We are suppressing the dependence of the conformal block on the cross-ratios. The coefficient
b^{2}_{m,l}
was computed in
[
43
]
and is given by

b^{2}_{m,l}=\frac{\Gamma\left(2-\frac{d}{2}+m\right)\Gamma\left(1+l+2m\right)\Gamma\left(l+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+l+m\right)}{m!\ l!\ \Gamma\left(2-\frac{d}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(l+\frac{d-1}{2}+m\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+l+2m\right)}.

(3.20)

In
d=4
, the above coefficient is only non-zero for
m=0
, so only a single operator of dimension
1+l
for each transverse spin
l
survives, which is consistent with what we obtained in (
2.28
).

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we defined and studied a line defect in the Gross-Neveu-Yukawa universality class using
\epsilon
-expansion and large
N
techniques. We provided evidence that there is a non-trivial IR DCFT that this line defect flows to. It should of course be possible to obtain more precise estimates of the DCFT data by going to higher orders in
\epsilon
expansion in
d=4-\epsilon
dimensions. Another immediate future direction is to develop an
\epsilon
-expansion in
d=2+\epsilon
dimensions using the action in (
1.1
). In that case, the line defect must be defined by integrating the fermion bilinear along the line. We make some remarks on this below in Appendix
A
. It would be interesting to develop this expansion for arbitrary
N
and compare with our large
N
results.

Symmetry breaking defects, such as the one we considered in this paper, have recently proved useful in Monte-Carlo simulations near the critical point
[
12
,
44
]
. Typically, near the phase transition, the value of the order parameter is rather small, and therefore in the usual approach of working with the two-point function of the order parameter, one has to deal with a quantity that is quadratically small. However, in the presence of symmetry breaking defects, the one-point function is also non-zero and hence may be used to infer bulk critical exponents. In
[
44
]
, this approach was also used to determine scaling dimensions of the defect operators for the pinning field defect in the Ising CFT in
d=3
. It would be very interesting to do such a study for the line defect we studied in the Gross-Neveu CFT and verify our predictions using Monte-Carlo.

Appendix A
Line defect in free fermion theory

In this appendix we discuss the line defect in the free fermion theory defined by

S=\int d^{d}x\bar{\Psi}\gamma\cdot\partial\Psi+h_{0}\int d\tau\bar{\Psi}\Psi(\tau).

(A.1)

Let’s calculate the one-point function of
\langle\bar{\Psi}\Psi\rangle
. Already at this order, we need to sum up infinite number of diagrams. The first few of them look like

\begin{split}\langle\bar{\Psi}\Psi(x)\rangle=&\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{GN2dFree_a.pdf}}}+\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{GN2dFree_b.pdf}}}+\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[scale]{GN2dFree_c.pdf}}}+\dots\\
=&-\frac{h_{0}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{d-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(d-1)(\mathbf{x}^{2})^{\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right)}}-\frac{h_{0}^{3}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{4}}{16\pi^{2d}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\left(\int d\tau_{i}^{\prime}\right)\frac{(x-\tau_{1}^{\prime})\cdot(x-\tau_{2}^{\prime})\tau_{13}^{\prime}\tau_{32}^{\prime}}{(|x-\tau_{1}^{\prime}||x-\tau_{2}^{\prime}||\tau_{13}^{\prime}||\tau_{32}^{\prime}|)^{d}}\\
&-\frac{h_{0}^{5}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{6}}{64\pi^{3d}}\prod_{i=1}^{5}\left(\int d\tau_{i}^{\prime}\right)\frac{(x-\tau_{1}^{\prime})\cdot(x-\tau_{2}^{\prime})\tau_{13}^{\prime}\tau_{34}^{\prime}\tau_{45}^{\prime}\tau_{52}^{\prime}}{(|x-\tau_{1}^{\prime}||x-\tau_{2}^{\prime}||\tau_{13}^{\prime}||\tau_{34}^{\prime}||\tau_{45}^{\prime}||\tau_{52}^{\prime}|)^{d}}+\dots\end{split}

(A.2)

So there are diagrams with increasing powers of
h
and number of propagators. It is easy to organize them as a geometric progression if we go to momentum space on the line defect (as was done for instance in
[
45
]
)

\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\frac{\gamma^{0}\tau_{12}}{|\tau_{12}|^{d}}=\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{i\gamma^{0}k\Gamma\left(\frac{3-d}{2}\right)e^{-ik\tau_{12}}}{2^{d-1}\pi^{\frac{d-1}{2}}|k|^{3-d}}.

(A.3)

We may then resum the propagator localized on the defect as follows

\begin{split}&\langle\bar{\Psi}(\tau_{1})\Psi(\tau_{2})\rangle\\

\begin{split}\langle\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}(x)\rangle&=-\frac{h_{0}N\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right)}{4\pi^{d-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(d-1)(\mathbf{x}^{2})^{\left(d-\frac{3}{2}\right)}}+\frac{h_{0}^{3}N\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{3-d}{2}\right)^{2}}{2^{2d}\pi^{2d-1}}\int d\tau_{1}d\tau_{2}\frac{(x-\tau_{1})\cdot(x-\tau_{2})}{(|x-\tau_{1}||x-\tau_{2}|)^{d}}\\

(A.5)

In
d=2

\langle\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}(x)\rangle=-\frac{h_{0}N}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}|\left(1+\frac{h_{0}^{2}}{4}\right)}.

(A.6)

In the interacting GN model, the line defect can be described as

S=-\int d^{d}x\left(\bar{\Psi}_{i}\gamma\cdot\partial\Psi^{i}+\frac{g}{2}\left(\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}\right)^{2}\right)+h_{0}\int d\tau\bar{\Psi}_{i}\Psi^{i}(\tau).

(A.7)

At the fixed point in

\sigma
