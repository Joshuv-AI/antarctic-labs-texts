Linear Regression for Power Law Distribution Fitting
Samuel Forbes

Abstract
We fit the exponent of the Pareto distribution, that is equivalent or can approximate the continuous power law distribution given a cutoff point, using linear regression (LR). We use LR on the logged variables of the empirical tail (one minus the empirical cumulative distribution function). We find the distribution of the consistent LR estimator and an approximate sigmoid relationship of the mean that underestimates the exponent. By factoring out a sigmoid function used to approximate the mean we transform the LR estimator so it is approximately unbiased with variance comparable to the minimum variance unbiased transformed MLE estimator.

Introduction

Power laws in the probability distribution appear across a wide number of disciplines
[
3
]
. We shall focus in this paper on
continuous power law distributions
that approximate variables such as income, wealth and stock returns
[
5
]
. Several definitions of a power law distribution appear in the literature. We focus on the piecewise power law distribution and the regularly varying distribution which we define in the next section. The power law part of the former distribution can be exactly viewed as a Pareto distribution whereas the power law part of the latter can only be approximated as a Pareto distribution. We fit only the exponent of the distribution and not the cutoff point which can be estimated using a Monte Carlo method found in Clauset et al.
[
3
]
. There are several methods of fitting the exponent of a Pareto distribution that are consistent (the estimator tends to the true parameter as the sample size tends to infinity) including the method of moments, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), quantile methods and linear regression (LR)
[
11
]
. Many have pointed out, for example
[
3
]
, that LR is inferior to MLE but to the author’s knowledge it has not been analysed in detail why this is the case.
We focus in this paper on fitting the exponent with LR on the empirical tail (one minus the empirical cumulative distribution funciton). We give a theoretical result on the distribution of the LR estimator as well as approximations on the mean and variance via simulation
1
1

1

Simulations are found at the author’s github
[
4
]
. We find evidence that the LR estimator is biased with a mean that underestimates the true exponent in a sigmoidal fashion depending on the sample size. Factoring out a sigmoid function we find this non-linear transformation of the LR estimator is roughly unbiased and comparable though greater in variance to a non-linear transformation of the MLE estimator which is unbiased of minimum variance
[
13
]
. We emphasise that the MLE estimator is superior however we present these results on the LR estimator as they may be of interest.

2
Definitions of a Power Law Distribution

As in Clauset et al.
[
3
]
we define the power law in two ways: one as a piecewise distribution and the other asymptotically as a regularly varying distribution. The piecewise power law distribution approximates the regularly varying distribution after a cutoff point which we assume is known. In this paper we will fit only the exponent of the piecewise power law distribution assuming the cutoff. We show below how this is identical to fitting the exponent of the Pareto distribution.

2.1
Piecewise Distribution

Let
X
be a continuous random variable.
We define the
power law distribution
as one that has a power law tail
2
2

2

We note Clauset et al.
[
3
]
defines the power law such that
\mathbb{P}(X>x)=\alpha/x^{\beta-1}
so the density is
f(x)=c/x^{\beta}
for an appropriate constant
c\,.

\mathbb{P}(X>x)=\alpha/x^{\beta}\,,\quad x>x_{m}\,

(1)

with
\alpha,\,\beta,\,x_{m}>0\,.
We call
\beta
the
power law exponent
and
x_{m}
the
cutoff
. Before
x_{m}
we assume the tail of
X
is defined by another function so that
X
is a piecewise distribution.
By taking the negative of the derivative of (
1
) we have that the density of
X
is

f(x)=\beta\alpha/x^{\beta+1}\,,\quad x>x_{m}\,.

(2)

Let us define the truncated distribution:

X_{\text{P}}=\{X|X>x_{m}\}\,,

(3)

then one can integrate over the entire domain,
[x_{m},\infty)\,,
to find that
X_{\text{P}}
is a
Pareto distribution
with tail (
1
) and density (
2
) such that
\alpha=x_{m}^{\beta}\,.
Now, for all
x>x_{m}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X>x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>x,X>x_{m})=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})\mathbb{P}(X>x|X>x_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})\mathbb{P}(X_{\text{P}}>x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})\left(\frac{x_{m}}{x}\right)^{\beta}\,.

(4)

Thus in general
\alpha=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})x_{m}^{\beta}\,.

We shall assume
the cutoff

x_{m}

is known (or has been fitted)
and we are concerned only with
fitting the exponent

\beta\,.
We refer the reader to Clauset et al.
[
3
]
for a Monte-Carlo method using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic to fit
x_{m}\,.

2.2
Regularly Varying Distribution

We shall briefly discuss a more general asymptotic representation of the power law distribution, the regularly varying distribution, and show how the piecewise distribution above approximates this distribution for large enough
x_{m}\,.
We mention here that the method for fitting
x_{m}
in Clauset et al.
[
3
]
has useful statistical properties such as consistency when applied to regularly varying distributions, see
[
2
]
.

Definition 1

(Regularly Varying Distribution, see e.g.
[
7
]
)
.

A continuous random variable
X
has a regularly varying distribution if
for all
\lambda>0
there exists a
\beta>0
such that the tail distribution is a regularly varying function:

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(X>\lambda x)}{\mathbb{P}(X>x)}=\lambda^{-\beta}\,.

We see simply that the piecewise distribution (
1
) is a regularly varying distribution.

Definition 2

(Slowly Varying Function, see e.g.
[
7
,
15
]
)
.

A function
l
is slowly varying if for all
\lambda>0

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{l(\lambda x)}{l(x)}=1\,.

One can show (p2 of
[
15
]
) that a regularly varying distribution can be written as

\mathbb{P}(X>x)=l(x)x^{-\beta}

where
l(x)
is a slowly varying function. We have the following properties
(p18 of
[
15
]
) of slowly varying functions: for all
\varepsilon>0

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}l(x)x^{-\varepsilon}=0\,,\quad\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}l(x)x^{\varepsilon}=\infty\,.

(5)

We now show that a regularly varying distribution can be approximated by the piecewise power law distribution (
1
).

Proposition 1
.

If
X
is a regularly varying distribution then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
x_{m}>0
such that with
\alpha=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})x_{m}^{\beta}
and
x>x_{m}

\alpha x^{-\beta-\varepsilon}<\mathbb{P}(X>x)<\alpha x^{-\beta+\varepsilon}\,.

Proof.

Suppose for any
\varepsilon>0\,,
that there exists an
x_{m}>0
such that for
x>x_{m}
and
\alpha=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})x_{m}^{\beta}
that

\displaystyle\alpha x^{-\beta-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle<\mathbb{P}(X>x)<\alpha x^{-\beta+\varepsilon}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha x^{-\beta-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle<l(x)x^{-\beta}<\alpha x^{-\beta+\varepsilon}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha x^{-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle<l(x)<\alpha x^{\varepsilon}

which is true by the definition of the above limits of slowly varying functions (
5
).
∎

Thus Proposition
1
shows that for
\varepsilon\gtrapprox 0
there exists an
x_{m}
such that for
x>x_{m}
we have
\mathbb{P}(X>x)\approx\alpha x^{-\beta}
with
\alpha=\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})x_{m}^{\beta}\,.

Examples

We now give an example of each of the two types of power law defined above. First a power law distribution that is piecewise (and regularly varying) that has an exponential then power law shape:

Example 1

(Piecewise power law, similar to (3.10) in
[

)
.

For
x_{m}\,,\,\beta>0
define the continuous random variable
X

\mathbb{P}(X>x)=\begin{cases}e^{-\beta\frac{x}{x_{m}}}\,,&0<x\leq x_{m}\\
e^{-\beta}\left(\dfrac{x_{m}}{x}\right)^{\beta}\,,&x>x_{m}\end{cases}

(6)

Second a power law distribution that is not piecewise but is regularly varying:

Example 2

(Lomax
[
9

or equivalently Pareto Type II
[

)
.

For
\lambda\,,\,\beta>0
define the continuous random variable
X
by the regularly varying tail

\mathbb{P}(X>x)=(1+x/\lambda)^{-\beta}\,,\quad x>0\,.

(7)

We now sample once from each of the distributions in the above examples and plot the empirical tail, see the appendix. We fit with the unbiased minimum variance MLE and roughly unbiased LR power law exponent estimators found in Sections
3.1
and

. For the piecewise distribution the power law part can be viewed exactly as points from a Pareto distribution. For the Lomax distribution a cutoff point

must be chosen so that beyond

[

Thus we urge caution when using these methods for fitting the exponent of a regularly varying distribution (we find evidence from simulation not presented here that in this case a larger initial sample with a subsequently larger
x_{m}
is required to get a closer estimate consistently).

3
Fitting the Power Law Exponent

We consider an i.i.d. sample
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
from a random variable
X
that is from the power law tail part of the distribution (
1
). We assume
x_{m}
and
\mathbb{P}(X>x_{m})
are known or have been fitted and emphasise
x_{m}
is not from the sample but is the cutoff in (
1
) for which
x_{i}>x_{m}
for all
x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}\,
. We note from (
4
) we only have to fit the Pareto distribution and thus take
\mathbf{x}
from the Pareto distribution
X_{\text{P}}
(
3
).

3.1
Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Suppose
X
follows a power law distribution defined by (
1
). We saw that

X_{\text{P}}
follows a Pareto distribution with density

f(x|\beta)=\beta x_{m}^{\beta}x^{-(\beta+1)}\,.
Assume
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
is an i.i.d. sample from
X_{\text{P}}
then the log-likelihood is defined

\log L(\mathbf{x}|\beta)=\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}|\beta)\right)\,.

By maximising the log-likelihood (respectively the likelihood) with respect to
\beta
we find that the MLE estimator for
\beta
given
x_{m}
is

\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{1}}=\frac{1}{1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i}/x_{m})}\,.

(8)

The MLE estimator (
8
) for the Pareto distribution has been known for a long time with Clauset et al.
[
3
]
referencing Muniruzzaman, 1957
[
10
]
as an early paper containing this result. We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for a summary of useful statistical properties of this estimator which includes consistency.
We note that (
8
) is exactly the Hill’s estimator for the Pareto distribution
X_{\text{P}}

[
6
]
. In particular it can be shown, see Rytgaard
[
13
]
, that

\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{1}}
has an inverse Gamma distribution with mean and variance

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{1}}]=n\beta/(n-1)\,,\quad\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{1}})=n^{2}\beta^{2}/((n-1)^{2}(n-2))\,.

(9)

This MLE estimator (
8
), as noted in
[
13
]
, can be corrected for bias. From (
9
) we see the
unbiased MLE estimator
is (for
n>1
)

\tilde{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{2}}=\frac{n-1}{n}\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{2}}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i}/x_{m})}

(10)

with mean and variance (for
n>2
)

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{2}}]=\beta\,,\quad\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{2}})=\frac{\beta^{2}}{n-2}<\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{MLE}_{1}})\,.

(11)

It is shown in
[
13
]
that the unbiased MLE estimator (
10
) has
minimum variance
and is
asymptotically normally distributed
with mean
\beta
and variance
\beta^{2}/n
and is a
consistent estimator
(the same for the untransformed MLE estimator (
8
)
[
3
]
).

Now approximating either MLE estimator with the asymptotic normal we have that
\approx 68
% of values of the estimator lie within one standard deviation i.e. lie within the interval

(\beta-\beta/\sqrt{n},\beta+\beta/\sqrt{n})\,.

(12)

Thus for the size of this interval to be less than
\varepsilon>0
we want

\frac{2\beta}{\sqrt{n}}<\varepsilon\Leftrightarrow n>4\beta^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}\,.

Therefore for a majority of the values of the MLE estimator to be within for example
0.1
of either side of
\beta
we would want a sample of size at least
400\beta^{2}\,.
Now for many real-world situations
0.5<\beta<3\,,
see e.g. Table 6.1 in
[
3
]
, thus the sample is required to be quite large before one is reasonably confident that the minimum variance MLE estimator is close to
\beta\,.

3.2
Linear Regression

We shall now apply linear regression to fit the power law exponent to the power law model (
1
) using the empirical tail (one minus the empirical distribution function, see the appendix). Let us again assume
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
is an i.i.d. sample from
X_{\text{P}}
defined above. Then for any
x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}\,
the empirical tail is

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X_{\text{P}}\geq x_{i})=(x_{m}/x_{i})^{\beta}+\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}}\,

(13)

where the error
\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}}
has the following properties (see the appendix):

\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}}\sim(1/n)\text{Binom}_{k\geq 1}(n,(x_{m}/x_{i})^{\beta})-(x_{m}/x_{i})^{\beta}\,,

(14)

where
\text{Binom}_{k\geq 1}(.)
is the truncated Binomial distribution that restricts the support to
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, for large
n

\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}}\approx\mathcal{N}(0,(1/n)(x_{m}/x_{i})^{\beta}(1-(x_{m}/x_{i})^{\beta}))\,

and

\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{almost surely as }n\rightarrow\infty\,.

(15)

Applying log’s to (
13
) we have

\displaystyle\log\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X_{\text{P}}\geq x_{i})

\displaystyle=\beta\log x_{m}-\beta\log x_{i}+\log(1+(x_{m}/x_{i})^{-\beta}\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}})

\displaystyle=-\beta\log(x_{i}/x_{m})+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}

(16)

Where the new error is

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}=\log(1+(x_{m}/x_{i})^{-\beta}\varepsilon_{n,x_{i}})\,.

(17)

We note from (
14
) that

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}\sim\log B_{n,x_{i}}

(18)

where
B_{n,x_{i}}\sim(1/n)(x_{m}/x_{i})^{-\beta}\text{Binom}_{k\geq 1}(n,(x_{m}/x_{i})^{\beta})\,.

Applying
ordinary least squares
, see e.g. Section 3.1 of
[
14
]
, to (
16
) the
LR estimator
for
\beta
can be found to be

\hat{\beta}^{\text{OLS}_{1}}_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i}/x_{m})\log(1/\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X_{\text{P}}\geq x_{i}))}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\log(x_{i}/x_{m}))^{2}}\,.

(19)

We note that if
a\,,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
then by the change of base formula for logs that

\log_{a}x=\log_{b}x/\log_{b}a

and so due to cancellation it does not matter which base we take for the log in (
19
). As the
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}
are a function of the independent
x_{i}
then they are independent. However as we will see from simulation, the mean of (
19
) is biased, indicating that the errors
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}
do not satisfy the properties of zero mean and constant variance to satisfy the further Gauss-Markov conditions, see e.g. Theorem 3.2 of
[
14
]
. Due to the relatively complicated distribution of
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}
(
18
) it is however hard to prove these properties formally.
However as
n\rightarrow\infty
we have by (
15
) and
(
17
) that

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n,x_{i}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{almost surely as }n\rightarrow\infty\,,

thus the estimator will tend almost surely to
\beta
as
n\rightarrow\infty
and so the
LR estimator (
19
) is a
consistent estimator
as noted by
Quandt
[
11
]
.

Now let
\mathbf{x}_{\text{o}}=\{x_{(1)},x_{(2)},\dots,x_{(n)}\}=\mathbf{x}
be the ordered sample of
\mathbf{x}
thus

x_{(1)}<x_{(2)}<\dots<x_{(n)}\,.
We have that the empirical tail, see the appendix, of the ordered sample is

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x_{(i)})=\frac{n-i+1}{n}\,,\quad i=1,2,\dots,n\,.

(20)

As
x_{i}
is from a Pareto distribution with parameters
x_{m}
and
\beta
then
k_{i}=\log(x_{i}/x_{m})
(specifically for the
natural logarithm
which we use from now on) is exponentially distributed with parameter
\beta\,
. Now the result by Rényi, see
[
12
]
, on the distribution of the order statistics for an exponential distribution gives

k_{(i)}=\log(x_{(i)}/x_{m})\sim\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{Z_{j}}{n-j+1}\,,\quad i=1,2,\dots,n\,

(21)

where
Z_{j}\sim\text{Exp}(1)\,
(exponentially distributed with parameter
1\,
).
Substitution of (
20
) and (
21
) into the the OLS estimator (
19
) leads to

\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{1}}\sim\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{Z_{j}}{n-j+1}\log\left(\frac{n}{n-i+1}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{Z_{j}}{n-j+1}\right)^{2}}\right)\beta\,.

(22)

Thus we have a representation of the distribution of the LR estimator (
19
). It is a rather complicated formula which is beyond the scope of this paper to analyse fully so we make do with analysing the LR estimator via simulation. We note though that the term in the brackets of (
22
) does not depend on
\beta\,,
thus the distribution of the LR estimator is a random factor multiplied by
\beta\,.

From simulations, see Figure
2
, we hypothesise two results:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{1}}]

\displaystyle\approx\log\left(e-(\log n)^{\gamma}/n\right)\beta\,,\quad\gamma\approx 1.6

(23)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{1}})

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}(1/n)

(24)

where
e=2.718\dots
is Euler’s number. The free parameter
\gamma
in (
23
) was approximated using non-linear least squares. Let

r_{n}=\log\left(e-(\log n)^{\gamma}/n\right)\,,\quad\gamma\approx 1.6\,.

This function we propose is an approximation to the mean of the random factor in the brackets in (
22
).
We see
r_{n}<1
for finite
n
and
r_{n}\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
so that
(
23
) underestimates
\beta
but tends to
\beta
as
n\rightarrow\infty\,.
We note that the mean approximation (
23
) is
sigmoidal
but imperfect especially for small
n
and further simulation would be needed to check if it is still a good approximation above
n=1000\,,
see Figure
2
. It would be future work to determine more about the form of (
22
) and whether it has an analytic mean or variance. If an analytic mean exists then a strictly unbiased LR estimator can be found.

We can though still approximately correct for the bias to obtain the approximately unbiased, consistent LR estimator

\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{2}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{1}}}{r_{n}}

(25)

with

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{2}}]\approx\beta\,,\quad\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{2}})\approx\mathcal{O}(1/n)\,.

(26)

We note though that this transformation is greater in terms of variance:

\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{2}})=\frac{\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{1}})}{r_{n}^{2}}>\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{n}^{\text{OLS}_{1}})\,.

However we find through simulation, see Figure
2
and Figure
3
in the next section, that the variance of both LR estimators are not too much larger than the minimum variance of the unbiased MLE estimator.

4
Conclusion

We use linear regression on the empirical tail to fit the exponent of the Pareto distribution that approximates or is identical to a continuous power law distribution given a cutoff value. We give an analytical representation of the distribution of the LR estimator and show evidence it’s mean underestimates the true exponent in a sigmoidal fashion. By factoring out a sigmoid function of the LR estimator we find comparable results to the transformed MLE estimator. Although the minimum variance unbiased transformed MLE estimator is superior we nonetheless present these novel results for the LR estimator.

Appendix: the Empirical Tail

Suppose
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
is an i.i.d. random sample from any random variable
X
.
The
empirical cumulative distribution function
is defined, see e.g. Section 19.1 of
[
16
]

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X<x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}<x}

where
\mathbf{1}_{A}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{ if }x\in A\\
0\quad\text{ if }x\notin A\end{cases}
is the indicator function. We define the
empirical tail
as

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)=1-\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X<x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}\geq x}\,.

(27)

which takes values
0,1/n,2/n,\dots,1\,.

We also introduce the error of the empirical tail
\varepsilon_{n}

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)=\mathbb{P}(X>x)+\varepsilon_{n}\,.

We now state some results which follow from known results on the empirical cumulative distribution function, see e.g. Section 19.1 of
[
16
]
. We have

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)\sim(1/n)\text{Binom}(n,\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))\,.

with mean and variance

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)]=\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\,,\quad\text{Var}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x))=(1/n)\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\mathbb{P}(X<x)\,.

By the law of large numbers

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)\rightarrow\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\quad\text{almost surely as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty\,.

By the central limit theorem

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)-\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\mathbb{P}(X<x))\,

in distribution.

From the above we have the following properties for
\varepsilon_{n}\,:

\varepsilon_{n}\sim(1/n)\text{Binom}(n,\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))-\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)

with mean and variance

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{n}]=0\,,\quad\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{n})=(1/n)\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\mathbb{P}(X<x)\,,

\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{almost surely as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty

and for large
n
we can approximate the error

\varepsilon_{n}\approx\mathcal{N}(0,(1/n)\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\mathbb{P}(X<x))\,.

Let
m=\min(\mathbf{x})
and
M=\max(\mathbf{x})
be respectively the min and max of the sample
\mathbf{x}\,.
Also let
\mathbf{x}_{\text{o}}=\{x_{(1)},x_{(2)},\dots,x_{(n)}\}=\mathbf{x}
be the ordered sample of
\mathbf{x}
so that

m=x_{(1)}<x_{(2)}<\dots<x_{(n)}=M\,.
Then we have that

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x_{(i)})=\frac{n-i+1}{n}\in[1/n,1]\,,\quad i=1,2,\dots,n\,.

and in particular for

x\in[m,M]

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)\in[1/n,1]\,,\quad i=1,2,\dots,n\,.

Thus for this case the empirical tail with
x\in[m,M]
will not be zero and so

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(X\geq x)\sim(1/n)\text{Binom}_{k\geq 1}(n,\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))

where
\text{Binom}_{k\geq 1}(n,\mathbb{P}(X\geq x))
is the truncated Binomial distribution, see e.g. Section 3.11 of
[
8
]
, excluding values that take
0
and thus with support
\{1,2,\dots,n\}\,.
