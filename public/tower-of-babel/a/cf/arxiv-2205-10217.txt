Memorization and Optimization in Deep Neural Networks with Minimum Over-parameterization
Simone Bombari, Mohammad Hossein Amani, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) has emerged as a powerful tool to provide memorization, optimization and generalization guarantees in deep neural networks. A line of work has studied the NTK spectrum for two-layer and deep networks with at least a layer with Ω ⁡ ( N ) \Omega(N) neurons, N N being the number of training samples. Furthermore, there is increasing evidence suggesting that deep networks with sub-linear layer widths are powerful memorizers and optimizers, as long as the number of parameters exceeds the number of samples. Thus, a natural open question is whether the NTK is well conditioned in such a challenging sub-linear setup. In this paper, we answer this question in the affirmative. Our key technical contribution is a lower bound on the smallest NTK eigenvalue for deep networks with the minimum possible over-parameterization : up to logarithmic factors, the number of parameters is Ω ⁡ ( N ) \Omega(N) and, hence, the number of neurons is as little as Ω ⁡ ( N ) \Omega(\sqrt{N}) . To showcase the applicability of our NTK bounds, we provide two results concerning memorization capacity and optimization guarantees for gradient descent training.



1
Introduction

Training a neural network is a non-convex problem that exhibits disconnected local minima
[
8
,
57
,
70
]
. Yet, in practice gradient descent (GD) and its variants routinely find solutions with zero training loss
[
72
]
. A framework to understand this phenomenon comes from the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK). This quantity was introduced in
[
33
]
, where it was proved that, during GD training, the network follows the kernel gradient of the functional cost with respect to the NTK. Furthermore, as the layer widths go large, the NTK converges to a deterministic limit which stays constant during training. Hence, in this infinite-width limit, it suffices that the smallest eigenvalue of the NTK is bounded away from
0
for gradient descent to reach zero loss. Going to finite widths, a recipe to prove GD convergence can be summarized as follows: show that
(i)
the NTK is well conditioned at initialization, and
(ii)
the NTK has not changed significantly by the time GD has reached zero loss (see e.g.
[
49
,
17
,
11
]
). A number of papers have exploited this recipe for networks with progressively smaller over-parameterization: two-layer networks
[
22
,
50
,
63
,
69
,
62
]
, deep networks with polynomially wide layers

[
5
,
21
,
73
,
74
]
, and deep networks with a single wide layer
[
47
,
43
]
. Besides optimization, showing that the NTK is well conditioned directly implies a result on memorization capacity
[
42
,
48
]
, and the smallest eigenvalue of the NTK has also been related to generalization
[
6
]
.

In
[
48
]
, it is shown that, given
N
training samples, a single layer with
\Omega(N)
neurons suffices for the NTK to be well conditioned in networks of arbitrary depth. However, there is increasing evidence that, in the challenging setup in which the layer widths are
sub-linear
in
N
, neural networks still memorize the training data
[
14
,
71
,
66
]
, reach zero loss under GD training in the two-layer setting
[
61
,
50
,
42
]
, and in the deep case, GD explores a nicely behaved region of the loss landscape
[
44
]
.
This is in agreement with simple back-of-the-envelope calculations on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet: CIFAR-10 has
N=50000
images and roughly
10^{6}
parameters suffice to fit random labels
[
72
]
; furthermore, in order to fit random labels to a subset of
1.2\cdot 10^{6}
ImageNet data points,
2.4\cdot 10^{7}
parameters are enough
[
72
]
. These numbers suggest that having a number of parameters of the same order as the dataset size is much closer to practice than having a number of neurons of that order.

We also note that, by counting degrees of freedom or bounding the VC dimension
[
10
]
,
\Omega(N)
parameters (and, therefore,
\Omega(\sqrt{N})
neurons) are in general necessary to fit
N
data points. This naturally brings forward the following open question:

Is the NTK well conditioned for deep networks with the minimum possible over-parameterization (i.e., containing
\Omega(N)
parameters corresponding to
\Omega(\sqrt{N})
neurons)?

Main contributions.

In this paper,
we settle this open question
for a large class of deep networks. We consider
(i)
a smooth activation function,
(ii)
i.i.d. data satisfying Lipschitz concentration (e.g., data with a Gaussian distribution, uniform on the sphere/hypercube, or obtained via a Generative Adversarial Network),
(iii)
a standard initialization of the weights (e.g., He’s or LeCun’s initialization), and
(iv)
a loose pyramidal topology in which the layer widths can increase by at most a multiplicative constant as the network gets deep. Then, in Theorem
1
we show that the NTK is well conditioned under the
minimum possible over-parameterization
requirement:
the number of parameters between the last two layers has to be
\tilde{\Omega}(N)
or, equivalently, the number of neurons has to be
\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{N})
, where
\tilde{\Omega}
includes extra logarithmic factors
. We achieve this goal by giving a lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of the NTK. This lower bound is tight when all the layer widths are of the same order.

Our NTK bounds open the way towards understanding the behavior of deep networks with minimum over-parameterization. In particular, an immediate consequence of the fact that the NTK is well conditioned is a result on the memorization capacity (Corollary
4.1
). Furthermore, by suitably choosing the initialization, we provide convergence guarantees for gradient descent training (Theorem
4
). Finally, we highlight that, in order to obtain our bounds on the smallest NTK eigenvalue, we give a number of tight estimates on the
\ell_{2}
norms of feature vectors and of their centered counterparts, which may be of independent interest.

Proof ideas.

To prove Theorem
1
, we restrict to the kernel
K_{L-2}=J_{L-2}J_{L-2}^{\top}
, where
J_{L-2}
is the Jacobian of the output w.r.t. the parameters between the last two hidden layers. This suffices as
K_{L-2}
is a lower bound on the NTK in the positive semi-definite (PSD) sense. We note that the
i
-th row (
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
) of
J_{L-2}
is given by the Kronecker product
\otimes
between the feature vector at layer
L-2
and the backpropagation term from the same layer. One key technical hurdle is to center
J_{L-2}
, so that its rows have the form
u\otimes v-\mathbb{E}\left[u\otimes v\right]
, where
\mathbb{E}[u]=\mathbb{E}[v]=0
, and all expectations are taken with respect to the (random) training data. To do so, we perform three steps of centering:
(i)
we center the feature vectors (corresponding to
u
),
(ii)
we center the backpropagation terms (corresponding to
v
), and
(iii)
we center again the whole row (corresponding to
u\otimes v
). These centering steps are approximate in the sense that the centered matrix is
not necessarily
a lower bound (in the PSD sense) on the original one. However, we are able to control the operator norm of the difference, and show that it scales slower than the smallest eigenvalue of the centered kernel.
At this point, we leverage the structure of the rows of the centered Jacobian to bound their sub-exponential norm via a version of the Hanson-Wright inequality for (weakly) correlated random vectors
[
1
]
. Finally, after providing also a tight estimate on the
\ell_{2}
norms of such rows, we can exploit a result from
[
2
]
to lower bound the smallest singular value of a matrix whose rows are independent random vectors with well controlled sub-exponential and
\ell_{2}
norms.

Existing work bounds the smallest NTK eigenvalue for networks with two layers
[
61
,
42
]
, or deep networks with a layer containing
\Omega(N)

neurons

[
48
]
. In particular,
[
61
]
also exploits the results
[
1
,
2
]
. However, the centering of the Jacobian is achieved via a combination of whitening and dropping rows, which appears to be difficult to generalize to deep networks. In contrast, the 3-step centering we described above applies to networks of arbitrary depth
L
. A different approach is put forward in
[
42
]
, and it is based on a decomposition of the kernel via spherical harmonics. This technique allows to obtain the exact limit of the smallest NTK eigenvalue, it has been used to analyze random feature models
[
26
,
41
,
25
,
40
]
and to obtain generalization bounds for two-layer networks (see again
[
42
]
). However, understanding how to carry out such a decomposition in the multi-layer setup is an open problem. Finally,
[
48
]
considers the deep case, and it relates the smallest eigenvalue of the NTK to the smallest singular value of a feature matrix. As feature matrices are full rank only when the number of neurons is
\Omega(N)
, this approach is inherently limited to networks with a linear-width layer.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
discusses the problem setup and our model assumptions; Section
3
presents our main result on the smallest NTK eigenvalue and gives a roadmap of the argument; Section
4
provides two applications of our NTK bounds: memorization capacity and gradient descent training; Section
5
discusses additional related work, and Section
6
provides some concluding remarks. The details of the proofs are deferred to the appendices.

2
Preliminaries

Neural network setup.

We consider an
L
-layer neural network with feature maps
f_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
defined for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle f_{l}(x)=\begin{cases}x,&l=0,\\
\phi(W_{l}^{\top}f_{l-1}),&l\in[L-1],\\
W_{L}^{\top}f_{L-1},&l=L.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Here,
W_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l-1}\times n_{l}}
is the weight matrix at layer
l
,
\phi
is the activation function and, given an integer
n
, we use the shorthand
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
We assume that the network has a single output,
i.e.
n_{L}=1
and
W_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-1}}
, and for consistency we have
n_{0}=d
. Let
g_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
be the pre-activation feature map so that
f_{l}(x)=\phi(g_{l}(x))
for
l\in[L]
. We define
g_{0}(x)=f_{0}(x)=x
.
Let
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
be the data matrix containing
N
samples in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\theta=[\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{L})]
be the vector of the parameters of the network,
and
F_{L}(\theta)=[f_{L}(x_{1}),\ldots,f_{L}(x_{N})]^{\top}
be the network output. We denote by
J
the Jacobian of
F_{L}
with respect to all the parameters of the network:

J=\left[\frac{\partial F_{L}}{\partial\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{1})},\ldots,\frac{\partial F_{L}}{\partial\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{L})}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\sum_{l=1}^{L}n_{l-1}n_{l}}.

(2.2)

Our key object of interest is the
empirical Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix
, denoted by
K\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and defined as:

K=JJ^{T}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left[\frac{\partial F_{L}}{\partial\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{l})}\right]\left[\frac{\partial F_{L}}{\partial\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{l})}\right]^{\top}.

(2.3)

In
[
33
]
, it is shown that, as
n_{l}\to\infty
for all
l\in[L-1]
,
K
converges to a deterministic
limit
, which stays constant during gradient descent training. The focus of this paper is on the
finite-width
behavior of the empirical NTK (
2.3
). Quantitative bounds for the NTK convergence rate can be obtained from
[
7
,
15
]
. However, these bounds lead to a significant over-parameterization requirement, see the discussion at the end of Section 3 in
[
48
]
. Here, our main result consists in showing that the NTK is well conditioned for a class of neural networks with the
minimum possible over-parameterization
, i.e.,
\Omega(N)
parameter or, equivalently,
\Omega(\sqrt{N})
neurons.

Weight and data distribution.

We consider the following initialization of the weight matrices:
(W_{l})_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{l}^{2}/n_{l-1})
for
l\in[L-1],i\in[n_{l-1}],j\in[n_{l}]
, where
\beta_{l}
is a numerical constant independent of the layer widths. This covers the popular cases of He’s and LeCun’s initialization
[
27
,
30
,
36
]
. For the last layer, we assume that
(W_{L})_{i}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{L}^{2})
for
i\in[n_{L-1}]
. Throughout the paper, we let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
be
N
i.i.d. samples from the data distribution
P_{X}
, which satisfies the conditions below.

Assumption 1

(Data scaling)
.

The data distribution
P_{X}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

\int\left\|x\right\|_{2}dP_{X}(x)=\Theta(\sqrt{d}).

2.

\int\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}dP_{X}(x)=\Theta(d).

3.

\int\left\|x-\int x^{\prime}\,dP_{X}(x^{\prime})\right\|_{2}^{2}dP_{X}(x)=\Omega\left(d\right).

Assumption 2

(Lipschitz concentration)
.

The data distribution
P_{X}
satisfies the
Lipschitz concentration property
. Namely, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every Lipschitz continuous function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)|<+\infty
, and for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\varphi(x)-\int\varphi(x^{\prime})\,dP_{X}(x^{\prime})\right|>t\right)\leq 2e^{-ct^{2}/\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}}.

Assumption
1
is simply a scaling of the training data points and their centered counterparts. Assumption
2
covers a number of important cases, e.g., standard Gaussian distribution
[
65
]
, uniform distributions on the sphere and on the unit (binary or continuous) hypercube
[
65
]
,
data produced via a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
1
1

1

By applying a Lipschitz map to a standard Gaussian distribution, the map output satisfies Assumption
2
.

[
59
]
, and more generally any distribution satisfying the log-Sobolev inequality with a dimension-independent constant. We also remark that Assumption
2
is rather common in the related literature
[
48
]
, or it is even replaced by a stronger requirement (e.g., Gaussian distribution or uniform on the sphere)
[
42
]
.

Assumption 3

(Activation function)
.

The activation function
\phi
satisfies the following properties:

1.

\phi
is a non-linear (and therefore also non-constant)
M
-Lipschitz function;

2.

its derivative
\phi^{\prime}
is a
M^{\prime}
-Lipschitz function;

These requirements are satisfied by common activations, e.g. smoothed ReLU, sigmoid, or
\tanh
.

Assumption 4

(Network topology)
.

The network satisfies a loose pyramidal topology condition, i.e.
n_{l}=\mathcal{O}\left(n_{l-1}\right)
, for all
l\in[L-1]
.

A
strict
pyramidal topology (namely, non-increasing layer widths) has been considered in prior work concerning the loss landscape
[
45
,
46
]
and gradient descent training
[
47
]
. Our Assumption
4
requires a
loose
pyramidal topology in the sense that, as we go deep, the layer widths are allowed to increase by a constant multiplicative factor. We also note that the widths of neural networks used in practice are often large in the first layers and then start decreasing
[
28
,
60
]
.

Assumption 5

(Over-parameterization)
.

We have that

N\cdot\log^{8}N=o(n_{L-2}n_{L-1}).

(2.4)

Furthermore, there exists
\gamma>0
such that

N^{\gamma}=\mathcal{O}\left(n_{L-1}\right).

(2.5)

Condition (
2.4
) requires the number of parameters between the last two hidden layers to be linear in the number of samples
, up to logarithmic factors
. This represents our
key over-parameterization condition
.
When all the widths have the same scaling, (
2.4
) reduces to
N=\tilde{o}(d^{2})
. This is satisfied by several “standard” datasets, such as MNIST (
N=60000
,
d=784
), CIFAR-10 (
N=5\cdot 10^{4}
,
d=3\cdot 32^{2}
), and ImageNet (
N=1.4\cdot 10^{7}
,
d=2\cdot 10^{5}
). We also note that, if
N\gg d^{2}
, the NTK is low-rank and
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=0
. Furthermore, Corollary
4.1
and Theorem
4
cannot generally hold when
N\gg d^{2}
, as there are more data points to fit than parameters to help with the fitting. A milder requirement is possible for classification tasks, see the discussion at the end of Section
5
.

The second condition (
2.5
) is rather mild, as
\gamma
can be taken to be arbitrarily small, and it avoids an exponential bottleneck in the last hidden layer.

l
is

F_{l}=[f_{l}(x_{1}),\ldots,f_{l}(x_{N})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{l}}
, and
\Sigma_{l}(x)=\operatorname{\mathop{diag}}([\phi^{\prime}(g_{l,j}(x))]_{j=1}^{n_{l}})

,

, let
\left\|v\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
and
\left\|v\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
denote its sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential norm, respectively (see also Appendix

for a detailed definition). Given a matrix

let
\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
be its operator norm,
\left\|A\right\|_{F}
its Frobenius norm,
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(A\right)

\sigma_{\rm min}\left(A\right)

We denote by
\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}

\varphi
.
All the complexity notations

3
NTK Bounds with Minimum Over-parameterization

Our main technical contribution on the smallest eigenvalue of the empirical NTK (
2.3
) is stated below.

Theorem 1

(Smallest NTK eigenvalue under minimum over-parameterization)
.

Consider an
L
-layer neural network (
2.1
), where the activation function satisfies Assumption
3
and the layer widths satisfy Assumptions
4
and
5
.
Let
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}P_{X}
,
where
P_{X}
satisfies the Assumptions
1
-
2
,
and let
K
be the empirical NTK Gram matrix (
2.3
).
Assume that the weights of the network are initialized as

(W_{l})_{i,j}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{l}^{2}/n_{l-1})
for
l\in[L-1]
,
i\in[n_{l-1}]
,
j\in[n_{l}]
and
(W_{L})_{i}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{L}^{2})
for
i\in[n_{L-1}]
. Then, we have

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(n_{L-2}n_{L-1}),

(3.1)

with probability at least

1-C\,Ne^{-c\log^{2}n_{L-1}}-Ce^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
, where
c
and
C
are numerical constants.
Moreover, we have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(dn_{L-1}\right),

(3.2)

with probability at least

1-Ce^{-cn_{L-1}}
, over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
.

Figure 1:
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)
as a function of
d^{2}
in a 3-layer neural network, with sigmoid activation and
d=n_{1}=n_{2}
.

This result implies that the NTK is well conditioned for a class of networks in which the number of parameters
n_{L-2}n_{L-1}
between the last two hidden layers
is linear in the number of data samples
N
, up to logarithmic factors.
This means that the total number of neurons of the network can be as little as
\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{N})
, which meets the
minimum possible amount of over-parameterization
.
The lower bound (
3.1
) and the upper bound (
3.2
) match when all the widths (up to layer
L-2
) have the same scaling, i.e.,
d=\Theta(n_{L-2})
. Throughout the paper, we do not explicitly track the dependence of our bounds on
L
, in the sense that the numerical constants
c,C
may depend on
L
.

In Figure
1
, we consider a 3-layer neural network with
d=n_{1}=n_{2}
, and we plot
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)
as a function of
d^{2}
, for three different values of
N
. The inputs are sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution, the activation function is the sigmoid
\sigma(x)=(1+e^{-x})^{-1}
, and we set
\beta_{l}=1
for all
l\in[L]
. We repeat the experiment 10 times, and report average and confidence interval at 1
standard deviation. The linear scaling of
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)
in
d^{2}
is in agreement with the result of Theorem
1
. The code used to obtain the results of Figure
1
(and Figure
2
as well) is available at
https://github.com/simone-bombari/smallest-eigenvalue-NTK/
.

3.1
Roadmap of the proof of Theorem
1

After an application of the chain rule and some standard manipulations, we have

JJ^{\top}=\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}K_{k},\qquad\mbox{ with }K_{k}=F_{k}F_{k}^{\top}\circ B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

(3.3)

where
B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{k}}
is a matrix whose
i
-th row is given by

(B_{k})_{i:}=\begin{cases}\Sigma_{k}(x_{i})\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{L-1}W_{l}\Sigma_{l}(x_{i})\right)W_{L},&k\in[L-2],\\
\Sigma_{L-1}(x_{i})W_{L},&k=L-1,\\
1,&k=L.\end{cases}

(3.4)

From the decomposition (
3.3
), one readily obtains that
K\succeq K_{L-2}
, since
K_{k}
is PSD for all
k\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}
. Hence, it suffices to prove a lower bound on
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K_{L-2}\right)
.
We denote by
J_{L-2}
the Jacobian obtained by computing the gradient only over the parameters between layer
L-2
and layer
L-1
. Then,
K_{L-2}=J_{L-2}J_{L-2}^{\top}
and, by using (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), the
i
-th row of
J_{L-2}
can be expressed as

(J_{L-2})_{i:}=f_{L-2}(x_{i})\otimes(D_{L}\phi^{\prime}(W^{\top}_{L-1}f_{L-2}(x_{i}))),\qquad i\in[N],\vskip 5.0pt

(3.5)

where
D_{L}
is a diagonal matrix with the entries of
W_{L}
on its diagonal. Note that, if we fix the weight matrices
(W_{l})_{l=1}^{L}
, the rows
((J_{L-2})_{i:})_{i=1}^{N}
are i.i.d., as the training data
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are i.i.d. too.

The proof consists of two main parts. First, we construct the centered Jacobian
\tilde{J}_{L-2}
, which is obtained from
J_{L-2}
by iteratively removing expectations with respect to
X
to its parts, and we show that its smallest singular value is
close
to the smallest singular value of
J_{L-2}
. The details of this part are contained in Appendix
D
. Second, we bound the smallest eigenvalue of the kernel obtained from the centered Jacobian
\tilde{J}_{L-2}
by providing an accurate estimate of the
\ell_{2}
and sub-exponential norms of its rows. The details of this part are contained in Appendix
E
. In order to carry out this program, we exploit a number of concentration results on the
\ell_{2}
norms of feature and backpropagation vectors, and on the
\ell_{2}
norms of their centered counterparts. These results are contained in Appendix
C
, and they could be of independent interest. In particular, we provide tight high-probability estimates on
(i)

\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|_{2}
,
(ii)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}]
,
(iii)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|_{2}]
,
(iv)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[\left\|f_{l}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}[f_{l}(x)]\right\|_{2}^{2}]
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\left\|f_{l}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}[f_{l}(x)]\right\|_{2}]
, and
\left\|f_{l}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}[f_{l}(x)]\right\|_{2}
, and
(v)

\left\|D_{L}\phi^{\prime}(g_{L-1}(x))-\mathbb{E}_{x}[D_{L}\phi^{\prime}(g_{L-1}(x))]\right\|_{2}
. Some preliminary calculations are also contained in Appendix
B
.

Part 1: Centering.

We consider the centered Jacobian

4
Two Applications: Memorization and Optimization

Memorization capacity.

The fact that the NTK Gram matrix (
2.3
) is well conditioned readily implies a result on memorization capacity. This was already observed in
[
42
]
for two-layer networks and in
[
48
]
for deep networks with a layer containing
\Omega(N)
neurons. Here, by using Theorem
1
, we show that
\Omega(N)
parameters between the last pair of hidden layers are enough for the network to fit
N
real-valued labels up to an arbitrarily small error. The result is stated below and, for completeness, we give the proof in Appendix
G
.

Corollary 4.1

(Memorization)
.

Consider an
L
-layer neural network (
2.1
), where the activation function satisfies Assumption
3
and the layer widths satisfy Assumptions
4
and
5
.
Let
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}P_{X}
,
where
P_{X}
satisfies the Assumptions
1
-
2
. Then, it holds that for every
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set of parameters
\theta
such that

\left\|F_{L}(\theta)-Y\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon,

(4.1)

with probability at least

1-C\,Ne^{-c\log^{2}n_{L-1}}-Ce^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, where
c
and
C
are numerical constants.

Gradient descent training.

Theorem
1
has implications on the convergence of gradient descent algorithms. In particular, by choosing carefully the initialization, we show that gradient descent trained on the
N
samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with labels
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})
converges to zero loss. This is – at the best of our knowledge – the
first result of this kind for deep networks with minimum over-parameterization
.

To define our initialization, let us assume that the
(L-1)
-th layer has an even number of neurons. With a slight abuse of notation, we indicate its width as
2n_{L-1}
. Thus,
W_{L-1}
can be written in the form
\left[W_{L-1}^{(1)},W_{L-1}^{(2)}\right]
, where
W_{L-1}^{(1)},W_{L-1}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-2}\times n_{L-1}}
. Similarly,
W_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{L-1}}
is the concatenation of
W_{L}^{(1)},W_{L}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L-1}}
. Then, we define the initialization
\theta_{0}=[\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{1,0}),\ldots,\operatorname{\mathop{vec}}(W_{L,0})]
as follows:

\begin{split}(W_{l,0})_{i,j}&\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{l}^{2}/n_{l-1}),\,\,l\in[L-2],\,i\in[n_{l-1}],\,j\in[n_{l}],\\[4.0pt]
(W_{L-1,0}^{(1)})_{i,j}&\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{L-1}^{2}/n_{L-2}),\,\,i\in[n_{L-2}],\,j\in[n_{L-1}],\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,W_{L-1,0}^{(2)}=W_{L-1,0}^{(1)},\\[4.0pt]
(W_{L,0}^{(1)})_{i}&\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta_{L}^{2}\gamma),\,\,i\in[n_{L-1}],\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,W_{L,0}^{(2)}=-W_{L,0}^{(1)}.\end{split}

(4.2)

As usual,
(\beta_{l})_{l=1}^{L}
are numerical constants independent of the layer widths. In contrast, the quantity
\gamma
will be set to a suitably large value depending on the number of training samples and on the layer widths. In words, the initialization is standard for
W_{1,0},\ldots,W_{L-2,0}
and
W_{L-1,0}^{(1)}
; the variance of
W_{L,0}^{(1)}
is boosted up by a factor
\gamma
; and we duplicate the neurons of layer
L-1
so that
W_{L-1,0}^{(2)}=W_{L-1,0}^{(1)}
and
W_{L,0}^{(2)}=-W_{L,0}^{(1)}
. At this point, we are ready to state our optimization result.

Theorem 4

(Optimization via gradient descent)
.

Consider an
L
-layer neural network (
2.1
), where the activation function satisfies Assumption
3
and the layer widths satisfy Assumptions
4
and
5
.
Consider training data
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}P_{X}
,
where
P_{X}
satisfies the Assumptions
1
-
2
, with labels
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\left\|Y\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{N})
. We consider solving the least-squares optimization problem

\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\left\|F_{L}(\theta)-Y\right\|_{2}^{2},

(4.3)

by running gradient descent updates of the form

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})
,
where the initialization
\theta_{0}
is defined in (
4.2
) with
\gamma=d^{3}N^{2}
and
\eta\leq C(\gamma Ndn_{L-1})^{-1}
. Then, for all
t\geq 1
,

\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\leq\left(1-c\eta\gamma n_{L-2}n_{L-1}\right)^{t}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0}),

(4.4)

with probability at least

1-C\,Ne^{-c\log^{2}n_{L-1}}-Ce^{-c\log^{2}N}

over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and the initialization
\theta_{0}
, where
c
and
C
are numerical constants.

In words, (
4.4
) guarantees that gradient descent
linearly
converges to a point with zero loss. We also note that (our upper bound on) the convergence rate can be expressed as a simple function of the learning rate
\eta
, the number of training samples
N
, and the layer widths
\{n_{i}\}_{i=0}^{L-1}
.

We now provide a sketch of the argument of Theorem
4
, deferring the detailed proof to Appendix
H
. As a consequence of Theorem
1
, the NTK is well conditioned at the initialization
\theta_{0}
, which implies that gradient descent makes progress at the beginning. The idea is that the NTK remains sufficiently well conditioned also during training and, hence, the loss vanishes with the linear rate in (
4.4
), since the trained weights remain confined in a ball of sufficiently small radius centered at
\theta_{0}
. More specifically, we exploit the optimization framework developed in
[
49
]
. There, a convergence result is proved if the following condition holds: there exists
\alpha,\beta
such that

\begin{split}&\alpha\leq\sigma_{\textup{min}}(J(\theta))\leq\left\lVert J(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\beta,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\left\lVert J(\theta_{1})-J(\theta_{2})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta},\end{split}

(4.5)

for all
\theta,\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}(\theta_{0},R)
, where
\mathcal{D}
denotes a ball centered at
\theta_{0}
of radius
R:=4\|F_{L}(\theta_{0})-Y\|_{2}/\alpha
(cf. Proposition
H.1
). In order to control the radius
R
, we take advantage of the form (
4.2
) of the initialization
\theta_{0}
. In particular, the spectrum of the Jacobian
J
(and, hence,
\alpha
) scales linearly in
\sqrt{\gamma}
, and the network output at initialization
F_{L}(\theta_{0})
is equal to
0
(cf. Lemma
H.2
). Thus, as
\left\|Y\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{N})
, we have that
R=\Theta(\sqrt{N}/\alpha)
, which can be controlled by choosing suitably
\gamma
. At this point, the argument consists in providing a number of estimates on feature vectors, backpropagation terms, and finally the Jacobian
J(\theta)
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{D}
(cf. Lemmas
H.3
-
H.9
). This allows us to find
\alpha,\beta
such that (
4.5
) holds and conclude.

In Figure
2
, we give an illustrative example that 4-layer networks achieve
0
loss when the number of parameters is at least linear in the number of training samples, i.e., under minimum over-parameterization. To ease the experimental setup, we use a ReLU activation, with Adam optimizer. We initialize the network as in the setting of Theorem
1
, picking
\beta_{l}=1
for all
l\in[L]
. The inputs, as well as the targets, are sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution. The plot is averaged over
10
independent trials. As predicted by Theorem
4
, the loss experiences a phase transition and it goes from
1
to
0
when the layer widths are of order
\sqrt{N}
.

Related Work and Discussion

Random matrices in deep learning.

The limiting spectra of several random matrices related to neural networks have been the subject of a recent line of work. In particular, the
Conjugate Kernel (CK) – namely, the Gram matrix of the features from the last hidden layer – has been studied for models with two layers
[
54
,
37
,
39
,
51
]
, with a bias term
[
3
,
56
]
, and with multiple layers
[
13
]
. The Hessian matrix has been considered in
[
52
]
, and the closely related Fisher information matrix in
[
55
]
. Using tools from free probability, the input-output Jacobian (as opposed to the parameter-output Jacobian considered in this work) of deep networks is studied in
[
53
]
and the NTK of a two-layer model in
[
4
]
. In
[
23
]
, the spectrum of NTK and CK for deep networks is characterized via an iterated Marchenko-Pastur map. The generalization error has also been studied via the spectrum of suitable random matrices, see
[
29
,
41
,
38
,
42
]
and Section 6 of the review
[
11
]
. Most of the existing results focus on the linear-width asymptotic regime,
where the widths of the various layers are linearly proportional. An exception is
[
68
]
, which focuses on the ultra-wide two-layer case (
n_{1}\gg N
).

Smallest eigenvalue of empirical kernels.

In the line of work mentioned above, the limiting spectrum of the random matrix is characterized in terms of weak convergence, which does not lead to a direct implication on the behavior of its smallest eigenvalue. In fact, lower bounding the smallest eigenvalue often requires understanding the speed of convergence to the limit spectrum. Quantitative bounds for random Fourier features are obtained in
[
9
]
. In the two-layer setting, the smallest NTK eigenvalue is lower bounded in
[
61
,
42
,
68
]
, and concentration bounds can also be obtained from
[
4
,
63
,
31
]
. In particular,
[
42
]
establishes a tight bound for two-layer networks with roughly as many parameters as training samples (hence, under minimum over-parameterization), and for a wide class of activations. However, this result still requires that
n_{1}=\Omega(d)
.
For deep networks, a convergence rate on the NTK can be obtained from
[
7
]
and potentially from
[
15
]
, however these results require all the layers to be wide. In
[
48
]
, it is proved that a single layer of linear width suffices for the NTK to be well conditioned. Here, Theorem
1
provides the first result for deep networks under minimum over-parameterization, therefore allowing for sub-linear layer widths.

For binary classification, the memorization capacity of neural networks was first studied by Cover

Recently, tight results for two-layer networks in the regression setting have been provided
[

In particular,

generalizes the construction of
[

]
, while the memorization result of
[

comes from a bound on the smallest NTK eigenvalue. This same route is also followed to analyze deep networks in
[

]
and in this work. Results for multiple layers have been provided in
[

71

66
,
24

. As concerns efficient algorithms achieving memorization,
[

20

study classification with two-layer networks. A popular line of research has exploited the NTK view to give optimization guarantees on gradient descent. Existing work has focused on the two-layer case
[
61
,
22
,
50
,
63
,
69
,
62

, and optimal results in terms of over-parameterization have been obtained
[

. For deep networks,
[
21
]
assumes a lower bound on the smallest NTK eigenvalue,
[
5
,
73
,
74
]
require all the layer widths to be (rather large) polynomials in the number of samples, and
[
47
,
43

reduce the over-parameterization to a single layer of linear width. In particular, in these last two papers, the analysis of the NTK is reduced to that of a certain feature matrix (the first one in
[

.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we show that the NTK is well conditioned for deep networks with sub-linear layer widths. As consequences of our NTK bounds, we also prove results on memorization and optimization for such a class of networks. Our approach requires smooth activations and layer widths which do not significantly grow in size with the depth. We believe such assumptions to be purely technical, and we leave as an open question their removal. Our novel NTK analysis could potentially be applied beyond standard gradient descent, e.g., for optimization with momentum
[
67
]
, or federated learning
[
32
]
.

Appendix A
Additional Notations

Given a sub-exponential random variable
X
, let
\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}=\inf\{t>0\,\,:\,\,\mathbb{E}[\exp(|X|/t)]\leq 2\}
.
Similarly, for a sub-Gaussian random variable, let
\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\{t>0\,\,:\,\,\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{2}/t^{2})]\leq 2\}
.

We use the analogous definitions for vectors. In particular, let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector, then
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
and
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
.

We indicate with
C
and
c
absolute, strictly positive, numerical constants, that do not depend on the layer widths of the network
\{n_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L-1}
or the number of training samples
N
. Their value may change from line to line.

B.4

N\cdot\log^{2}n_{L-1}\cdot\log^{4}N=\mathcal{O}\left(N\cdot\log^{6}n_{L-1}\right)=o(n_{L-1}n_{L-2}),

(B.7)

where in the first passage we use that

(from the second requirement of Assumption
5

B.1
).

Lemma B.2

(Lipschitz constant of function of the features)
.

For all
l\in[L-1]
, and for every Lipschitz function
\varphi
, we have

\left\|\varphi(g_{l}(x))\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(B.8)

with probability at least

1-2l\exp{(-n_{L-1})},

(B.9)

over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l}
. We recall that
\varphi
is applied component-wise to
g_{l}(x)
, and
\varphi(g_{l}(x)):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}
is intended as a function of
x
.

Proof.

Note that
\varphi(g_{l})
is a composition of Lipschitz functions. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi(g_{l})\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left\|g_{l}\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left\lVert W_{l}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\prod_{k=1}^{l-1}\left(\left\lVert W_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\right)

(B.10)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left\lVert W_{l}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}M^{l-1}\prod_{k=1}^{l-1}\left\lVert W_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}},

where the last step is justified by Assumption
3
.

Recall that, by the assumption on the initialization of the weights,
(W_{k})_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2}_{k}/n_{k-1})
, for some constant
\beta_{k}
which does not depend on the layer widths. Then, by Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
65
]
, we have that, for any
k\in[l]
,

\left\lVert W_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq C\frac{\beta_{k}}{\sqrt{n_{k-1}}}(\sqrt{n_{k-1}}+2\sqrt{n_{k}}),

(B.11)

with probability at least
1-2\exp{(-n_{k})}
,
C
being a numerical constant. By Assumption
4
on the topology of the network, we can rewrite this result as

\left\lVert W_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(B.12)

with probability at least
1-2\exp{(-n_{L-1})}
. To conclude, using a union bound over the layers up to layer
l
, we have that

\left\|\varphi(g_{l})\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(B.13)

with probability at least
1-2l\exp{(-n_{L-1})}
over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l}
.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

We have that

\left\lVert D_{L}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\log{n_{L-1}},

(B.14)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c\log^{2}n_{L-1})
over
W_{L}
, where
c
is a numerical constant.

Proof.

Recall that
D_{L}=\text{diag}(W_{L})
contains on the diagonal
n_{L-1}
independent Gaussian random variables

. Thus, for any

,

\mathbb{P}(|(D_{L})_{ii}|>\log n_{L-1})<2\exp(-\log^{2}n_{L-1}/(2\beta^{2}_{L})),

(B.15)

which gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert D_{L}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}>\log n_{L-1})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\in[n_{L-1}]}|(D_{L})_{ii}|>\log n_{L-1})

(B.16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n_{L-1}\mathbb{P}(|(D_{L})_{11}|>\log n_{L-1})

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2\exp(\log n_{L-1}-\log^{2}n_{L-1}/(2\beta^{2}_{L}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2\exp(-c\log^{2}n_{L-1}),

where the second step is a union bound on the entries of

Lemma B.5

(Exponential tails of quadratic forms)
.

Let
x\sim P_{X}
. Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
and
v:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
be mean-0 Lipschitz functions with respect to
x
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[u(x)]=0
,
\mathbb{E}_{x}[v(x)]=0
,
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=c_{1}
and
\left\|v\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=c_{2}
. Let
U
be a
d_{u}\times d_{v}
matrix, and

\Gamma(x)=u(x)^{\top}Uv(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[u(x)^{\top}Uv(x)\right].

(B.19)

Then,

\left\|\Gamma\right\|_{\psi_{1}}<CK^{2}\left\|U\right\|_{F}.

(B.20)

where
K=\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}}
, and
C
is a numerical constant.

Proof.

Consider the function
z(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}+d_{v}}
obtained by concatenating the vectors
u
and
v
, i.e.,

z(x):=[u(x),v(x)]^{\top}.

(B.21)

One can readily verify that
\left\|z\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}}:=K
. Let us set

M=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}0&U\\
\hline\cr U^{\top}&0\end{array}\right).

(B.22)

Then, we have that

\Gamma=z^{\top}Mz-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[z^{\top}Mz\right].

(B.23)

Since
x
satisfies Assumption
2
and
z(x)
is Lipschitz, in order to obtain tail bounds on
\Gamma
, we can apply the version of the Hanson-Wright inequality given by Theorem 2.3 in
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\Gamma|>t)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|z^{\top}Mz-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[z^{\top}Mz\right]|>t)

(B.24)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{C_{1}}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{4}\left\|M\right\|^{2}_{F}},\frac{t}{K^{2}\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{1}{C_{1}}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{4}\left\|U\right\|^{2}_{F}},\frac{t}{K^{2}\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}\right)\right),

where
C_{1}
is a numerical constant, and in the last step we use that
\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert U\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
and
\left\|M\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|U\right\|_{F}^{2}/2\leq\left\|U\right\|_{F}^{2}
.
Thus, by Lemma 5.5 of
[
61
]
, we conclude that

\left\|\Gamma\right\|_{\psi_{1}}<C_{2}K^{2}\left\|U\right\|_{F},

(B.25)

for some numerical constant
C_{2}
, which gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma B.6
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
be two mean-0 sub-Gaussian vectors such that
\left\|u\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=c_{1}
and
\left\|v\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=c_{2}
. Set
A_{uv}=\mathbb{E}\left[uv^{\top}\right]
. Then,

\left\lVert A_{uv}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq C(c_{1}+c_{2})^{2},

(B.26)

where
C
is a numerical constant.

Proof.

Consider the vector

z:=[u,v]^{\top}.

(B.27)

Then,
z
is sub-Gaussian and, by triangle inequality on the vectors
[u,0]
and
[0,v]
, we have that
\left\|z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq c_{1}+c_{2}
. Since
u
and
v
are mean-0, then
z
is also mean-0 and its covariance matrix can be written as
A_{z}:=\mathbb{E}\left[zz^{\top}\right]
. Furthermore, we can show that

\left\lVert A_{z}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq C(c_{1}+c_{2})^{2}.

(B.28)

In fact, let
w
be the unitary eigenvector associated to the maximum eigenvalue of
A_{z}
. Then,

\left\lVert A_{z}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=w^{\top}A_{z}w=\mathbb{E}\left[w^{\top}zz^{\top}w\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[(w^{\top}z)^{2}\right].

(B.29)

Furthermore, we have that

\left\|z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{w^{\prime}\text{s.t.}\left\|w^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|(w^{\prime})^{\top}z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\left\|w^{\top}z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\geq\frac{1}{C}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[(w^{\top}z)^{2}\right]},

(B.30)

where
C
is a numerical constant, and the last inequality comes from Eq. (2.15) of
[
65
]
. By combining (
B.29
) and (
B.30
) with
\left\|z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq c_{1}+c_{2}
, (
B.28
) readily follows.

Finally, we have that

A_{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}A_{u}&A_{uv}\\
\hline\cr A_{uv}^{\top}&A_{v}\end{array}\right),

(B.31)

where
A_{u}:=\mathbb{E}\left[uu^{\top}\right]
and
A_{v}:=\mathbb{E}\left[vv^{\top}\right]
. As
A_{u}
and
A_{v}
are PSD, we have that

\left\lVert A_{uv}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert A_{z}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}.

(B.32)

Hence, the desired result follows from (
B.28
) and (
B.32
).
∎

Lemma B.7
.

Let
A
be an
N\times n
matrix whose rows
A_{i}
are i.i.d. mean-0 sub-Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
K=\left\|A_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
the sub-Gaussian norm of each row. Then, we have

\left\lVert AA^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=K^{2}\mathcal{O}\left(N+n\right),

(B.33)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-cn)
, for some numerical constant
c
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
K=1
to simplify the proof. Let
\Sigma
be the second moment matrix of each of the rows of
A
. Then,
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}A_{i}^{\top}\right]
, since the rows are mean-0. Note that, as the rows of
A
are i.i.d.,
\Sigma
is independent of
i
. Furthermore, Lemma
B.6
implies that the covariance matrix
\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}A_{i}^{\top}\right]
has operator norm bounded by a constant, since the sub-Gaussian norm of the rows is 1. Then, by using Remark 5.40 in
[
64
]
, we have that

\left\lVert\frac{A^{\top}A}{N}-\Sigma\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits(\delta,\delta^{2}),\qquad\mbox{ where }\delta=C\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}+\frac{t}{\sqrt{N}},

(B.34)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-ct^{2})
, where
c
and
C
are numerical constants. Setting
t=\sqrt{n}
and using a triangular inequality gives that, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-ct^{2})
,

\left\lVert AA^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert A^{\top}A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq N\left\lVert\Sigma\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits(C\sqrt{nN}+\sqrt{nN},(C\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{n})^{2})=\mathcal{O}\left(N+n\right),

(B.35)

which implies the desired result (after re-scaling by
K
).
∎

Lemma B.10
.

Let
\rho
be a standard Gaussian random variable, then we have that

\varphi_{1}(c):=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\phi(c\rho)\right],

(B.46)

and

\varphi_{2}(c):=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\phi^{2}(c\rho)\right],

(B.47)

are continuous functions in
c
. Furthermore,
\varphi_{1}(c)
is Lipschitz in
c
, and

\left|\varphi_{2}(c_{1})-\varphi_{2}(c_{2})\right|\leq C_{1}\left|c_{1}-c_{2}\right|+C_{2}|c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}|,

(B.48)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are numerical constants (independent of
c_{1},c_{2}
).

Let
p(\rho)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\rho^{2}/2}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{1}(c+\varepsilon)-\varphi_{1}(c)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int p(\rho)\left|\phi((c+\varepsilon)\rho)-\phi(c\rho)\right|d\rho

(B.49)

\displaystyle\leq\int p(\rho)\left|M\varepsilon\rho\right|d\rho

\displaystyle=M\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[|\rho|\right]

\displaystyle=C\varepsilon,

where in the second line we use that
\phi
is
M
-Lipschitz by Assumption
3
.

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{2}(c+\varepsilon)-\varphi_{2}(c)\right|

\displaystyle=\int p(\rho)\left|\phi((c+\varepsilon)\rho)-\phi(c\rho)\right|\left|\phi((c+\varepsilon)\rho)+\phi(c\rho)\right|d\rho

\displaystyle\leq\int p(\rho)\left|M\varepsilon\rho\right|\left(2\left|\phi(0)\right|+M(|c+\varepsilon|+|\varepsilon|)|\rho|\right)d\rho

\displaystyle=C_{1}\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[|\rho|\right]+C_{2}\varepsilon|c|\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho^{2}\right]+C_{3}\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\rho^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=C_{4}\varepsilon+C_{2}|c|\varepsilon+C_{3}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\varepsilon+C_{6}\left|(c+\varepsilon)^{2}-c^{2}\right|.

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\tilde{c}_{l}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{c}_{l}(x)\right]\right|>t\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|\right]\right|>\sqrt{n_{l}}t/\beta_{l}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}2\exp\left(-cn_{l}t^{2}/\left\|f_{l}\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle=C\frac{\left\|f_{l}\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}}{\sqrt{n_{l}}},

where
c
and
C
are numerical constants, and the third line is justified by Assumption
2
.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\tilde{c}_{l}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{c}_{l}(x)\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\tilde{c}_{l}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{c}_{l}(x)\right]\right)^{2}>t\right)dt

(B.63)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|\right]\right|>\sqrt{n_{l}t}/\beta_{l}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}2\exp\left(-cn_{l}t/\left\|f_{l}\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle=C\frac{\left\|f_{l}\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}{n_{l}},

where, again,
c
and
C
are numerical constants and the third line is justified by Assumption
2
.
∎

Lemma B.15
.

Let
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
be two standard Gaussian random variables, possibly correlated. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\phi(\rho_{1}x_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})-\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}y_{2})\right]\right|\leq

(B.64)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|+C_{2}\left|x_{2}\right|\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|+C_{3}\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|+C_{4}\left|y_{1}\right|\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|,

where
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
are numerical constants (which do not depend on
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
). Furthermore, the same result holds with
\phi^{\prime}
instead of
\phi
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\phi(\rho_{1}x_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})-\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}y_{2})\right]\right|

(B.65)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\phi(\rho_{1}x_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})-\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})-\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\phi(\rho_{2}y_{2})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\left|\phi(\rho_{1}x_{1})-\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\right|\left|\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\left|\phi(\rho_{2}x_{2})-\phi(\rho_{2}y_{2})\right|\left|\phi(\rho_{1}y_{1})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\left|M\rho_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})\right|\left(\left|\phi(0)\right|+M\left|\rho_{2}x_{2}\right|\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}\rho_{2}}\left[\left|M\rho_{2}(x_{2}-y_{2})\right|\left(\left|\phi(0)\right|+M\left|\rho_{1}y_{1}\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{1}|\right]+C_{2}\left|x_{2}\right|\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{1}||\rho_{2}|\right]

\displaystyle+C_{3}\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{2}|\right]+C_{4}\left|y_{1}\right|\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{1}||\rho_{2}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|+C_{2}\left|x_{2}\right|\left|x_{1}-y_{1}\right|+C_{3}\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|+C_{4}\left|y_{1}\right|\left|x_{2}-y_{2}\right|,

where in third inequality we use that
\phi
is
M
-Lipschitz, and in the last inequality we use that the quantities
\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{1}|\right]
,
\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{2}|\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[|\rho_{1}||\rho_{2}|\right]
are all smaller than
1
(regardless of the correlation between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
). Since we only used the fact that
\phi
is
M
-Lipschitz, the same result holds with
\phi^{\prime}
in place of
\phi
.

∎

Lemma C.1

(
\ell_{2}
norm of features)
.

Let
x\sim P_{X}
.
Then, for every
0\leq l\leq L-1
,

\left\|f_{l}(x))\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{l}}),

(C.1)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
x
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l}
. As usual,
\phi
is applied component-wise on
g_{l}(x)
, and
c
and
C
are numerical constants.

Proof.

We prove this by induction over
l
, and we start with the base case (
l=0
). Recall that we have defined
f_{0}(x):=x
. As the
\ell_{2}
norm is a 1-Lipschitz function, by Assumption
2
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\|x\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\left\|x\right\|_{2}]\right|>t\right)\leq 2e^{-ct^{2}}.

(C.2)

Furthermore, Assumption
1
implies that
\mathbb{E}[\left\|x\right\|_{2}]=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
, hence setting
t=\mathbb{E}[\left\|x\right\|_{2}]/2
in (
C.2
) proves the desired result for the base case (recalling that
n_{L-1}=\mathcal{O}\left(d\right)
by Assumption
4
).

By inductive hypothesis, we have

\left\|f_{l-1}(x))\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{l-1}}),

(C.3)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
.

Define
\tilde{c}:=\beta_{l}\left\|f_{l-1}(x)\right\|_{2}/\sqrt{n_{l-1}}
. From now on, we condition on a realization of
x
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
such that
\tilde{c}=\Theta(1)
. By (
C.3
), this happens with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
.

To ease the notation, we use the shorthands
f:=f_{l-1}(x)
and
W:=W_{l}
. Then, we can write

\displaystyle\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\phi(W^{\top}f)\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{n_{l}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}\phi^{2}((W^{\top})_{i:}f)}.

(C.4)

Recall that
(W_{l})_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2}_{l}/n_{l-1})
and that the Gaussian distribution is rotationally invariant. Thus, the RHS of (
C.4
) has the same distribution as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n_{l}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)}=\sqrt{n_{l}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{1}\right)\right]+\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}Z_{i}},

(C.5)

where
(\rho_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{l}}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and also independent of
f
, and we have defined the independent, mean-0 random variables

Z_{i}=\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{1}\right)\right].

(C.6)

Note that, in the definition of
Z_{i}
, the randomness comes only from
\rho_{i}
, since we are conditioning on
\tilde{c}
.

We have that

\left\|\phi\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\left\|\phi\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\phi\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\phi\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}+C_{2}=C_{3},

(C.7)

where the first term is bounded by a constant by Theorem 5.2.2 in
[
65
]
, and the bound on the second term follows from Lemma
B.11
.
As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\left\|Z_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}=

\displaystyle\left\|\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{1}}

(C.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{4}\left\|\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)\right\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{4}\left\|\phi\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{i}\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{5},

where the inequality in the second line follows from Exercise 2.7.10 of
[
65
]
, the equality in the third line follows from Lemma 2.7.6 of
[
65
]
, and the inequality in the last line follows from (
C.7
). Hence, the
Z_{i}
-s are i.i.d. sub-exponential random variables, with sub-exponential norm bounded by a numerical constant. An application of Bernstein inequality (cf. Corollary 2.8.3. in
[
65
]
) gives that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}Z_{i}\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{t^{2}}{C_{6}^{2}},\frac{t}{C_{6}}\right)n_{l}\right),

(C.9)

where
c,C_{6}
are numerical constants. Furthermore, by Lemma
B.12
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{1}\right)\right]=\Theta(1).

(C.10)

By setting
t=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}\rho_{1}\right)\right]/2
into (
C.9
) and using (
C.5
) and (
C.10
), we conclude that

\left\|\phi(W^{\top}f)\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{l}}),

(C.11)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})-2\exp(-cn_{l})\geq 1-C_{1}\exp(-cn_{L-1})
, for some numerical constant
c
and
C_{1}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.2

(Expected squared
\ell_{2}
norm of features)
.

Let
x\sim P_{X}
.
Then, for every
0\leq l\leq L-1
,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|f_{l}(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\Theta(n_{l}),

(C.12)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l}
. As usual,
c
and
C
are numerical constants.

Proof.

The argument is by induction over
l
. The base case is a direct consequence of Assumption
1
, since
f_{0}(x)=x
.

By inductive hypothesis, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|f_{l-1}(x))\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]=\Theta(n_{l-1}),

(C.13)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
. Define
\tilde{c}(x):=\beta_{l}\left\|f_{l-1}(x)\right\|_{2}/\sqrt{n_{l-1}}
. From now on, we condition on a realization of
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{c}^{2}(x)\right]=\Theta(1)
. By (
C.13
), this happens with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
.

To ease the notation, we use the shorthands
f:=f_{l-1}(x)
,
W:=W_{l}
and
w_{i}=W_{:i}
. Then, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|f_{l}(x))\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|\phi(W^{\top}f)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]

(C.14)

\displaystyle=n_{l}\left(\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{2}((W^{\top})_{i:}f)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=n_{l}\left(\mathbb{E}_{w_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{2}(w_{1}^{\top}f)\right]+\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}Z_{i}\right),

where we use that the
w_{i}
-s are equally distributed and we have defined the independent, mean-0 random variables

Z_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{2}\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{w_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\phi^{2}\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right].

(C.15)

Note that, in the definition of
Z_{i}
, the randomness comes only from
w_{i}
, since we are conditioning on
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
.

We have that

\displaystyle\left\|Z_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|\phi^{2}\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)-\mathbb{E}_{w_{i}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\right]

(C.16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[C_{1}\left\|\phi^{2}\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{1}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|\phi\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right],

where the first line follows from Jensen’s inequality as
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
is convex, the inequality in the second line follows from Exercise 2.7.10 of
[
65
]
, and the equality in the third line follows from Lemma 2.7.6 of
[
65
]
.

Recall that
(W_{l})_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2}_{l}/n_{l-1})
and that the Gaussian distribution is rotationally invariant. Thus,
\phi\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)
has the same distribution as
\phi\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{i}\right)
, where
(\rho_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{l}}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and also independent of
\tilde{c}(x)
.
We now condition on a realization of
x
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
and provide an upper bound on the sub-Gaussian norm
\left\|\phi\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
, where the only randomness comes again from
w_{i}
(and, hence, from
\rho_{i}
). We have that

\displaystyle\left\|\phi\left(w_{i}^{\top}f(x)\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\phi\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{i}\right)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

(C.17)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\phi\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\phi\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{i}\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{i}}\left[\phi\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{i}\right)\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\tilde{c}(x)+C_{2}\tilde{c}(x)+C_{3}=C_{4}(\tilde{c}(x)+1).

where the first term in the RHS in the second line is bounded by
C_{1}\tilde{c}(x)
by Theorem 5.2.2 in
[
65
]
, and the second term is bounded by
C_{2}\tilde{c}(x)+C_{3}
by following the same proof of Lemma
B.11
as
\phi
is Lipschitz. By combining (
C.16
) and (
C.17
), we get

\left\|Z_{i}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq C_{4}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[(\tilde{c}(x)+1)^{2}\right]\leq C_{5},

(C.18)

where we use that
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{c}^{2}(x)\right]=\Theta(1)
.

Hence, the
Z_{i}
-s are i.i.d. sub-exponential random variables, with sub-exponential norm bounded by a numerical constant. An application of Bernstein inequality (cf. Corollary 2.8.3. in
[
65
]
) gives that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{l}}Z_{i}\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{t^{2}}{C_{5}^{2}},\frac{t}{C_{5}}\right)n_{l}\right),

(C.19)

where
c,C_{5}
are numerical constants.

Let us consider the first term in (
C.14
):

\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{w_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}(w_{1}^{\top}f)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{1}\right)\right],

(C.20)

where the equality comes again from the rotational invariance of the Gaussian distribution of
w_{1}
. We will show that

\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{1}\right)\right]=\Theta(1),

(C.21)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
.

The upper bound in (
C.21
) follows from the same passages in (
B.56
), as
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tilde{c}^{2}(x)]=\Theta(1)
. We now prove the lower bound.
By Lemma
C.1
, we have that there exist numerical constants
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
such that
\tilde{c}(x)\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
x
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
. Hence, with probability at least
1-2C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tilde{c}(x)\in[c_{1},c_{2}])\geq 1/2,

(C.22)

where we use the symbol
\mathbb{P}_{x}
to highlight that this last probability is taken over
x
. Let us condition on a realization of
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{l-1}
s.t. (
C.22
) holds. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{1}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\inf_{c\in[c_{1},c_{2}]}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(c\rho_{1}\right)\right].

(C.23)

By Lemma
B.10
, we have that
\varphi(c)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(c\rho_{1}\right)\right]
is continuous in
c
. Therefore, by Weierstrass theorem, there exists a strictly positive
c^{*}\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
such that
\inf_{c\in[c_{1},c_{2}]}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(c\rho_{1}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(c^{*}\rho_{1}\right)\right]
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\tilde{c}(x)\rho_{1}\right)\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(c^{*}\rho_{1}\right)\right]=\Theta(1),

(C.24)

where the last equality is a consequence of Lemma
B.12
. This concludes the proof of the lower bound in (
C.21
).

By setting
t=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(c^{*}\rho_{1}\right)\right]/4
into (
C.19
) and using (
C.21
) and (
C.14
), we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|f_{l}(x))\right\|^{2}_{2}\right]=\Theta(n_{l}),

(C.25)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
, for some numerical constants
C
and
c
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.4

(
\ell_{2}
norms of centered features)
.

Let
x\sim P_{X}
. Then, for every
0\leq l\leq L-1
, the following results hold.

1.

With probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over

\Sigma_{k}(x_{1})=\operatorname{\mathop{diag}}([\phi^{\prime}(g_{k,j}(x_{1}))]_{j=1}^{n_{k}})
, by Assumption
3
, we have that

\left\lVert\Sigma_{k}(x_{1})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq M.

(F.5)

Let us now condition on the following two events:
(i)

\left\lVert W_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\Theta(1)
, for all
k\in[L-1]
(this happens with probability at least
1-C^{\prime}\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{L-1}
, see (
B.12
) in the proof of Lemma
B.2
), and
(ii)

\left\|W_{L}\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{n_{L-1}})
(this happens with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
W_{L}
, by Theorem 3.1.1 in
[
65
]
). Then, we readily get

\left\|(B_{l})_{1:}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(n_{L-1}\right).

(F.6)

Taking the intersection of all the events over which we conditioned, we finally obtain

\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\left\|(F_{l})_{1:}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|(B_{l+1})_{1:}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(n_{L-1}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}n_{l}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(dn_{L-1}\right),

(F.7)

with probability at least
1-(1+C^{\prime}+C^{\prime\prime})\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
and
x_{1}
, where in the last step we have used Assumption
4
. By combining (
F.2
) and (
F.7

Proof of Corollary

Proof of Corollary
4.1
.

By Theorem
1
, we have that the smallest eigenvalue of
JJ^{\top}
is bounded away from zero with probability at least
1-p
over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
(W_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
, where

(G.1)

Hence, with probability at least
1-p
over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, there exists a set of parameters
\theta_{0}
such that
J(\theta_{0})
has a right inverse. Thus, for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, there exists
\theta^{\prime}
such that

J(\theta_{0})\theta^{\prime}=\frac{\partial F_{L}(\theta)}{\partial\theta}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\theta^{\prime}=Y.

(G.2)

This can also be written, for all
i\in[N]
, as

y_{i}=\frac{\partial f_{L}(\theta,x_{i})}{\partial\theta}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}^{\top}\theta^{\prime}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f_{L}(\theta_{0}+h\theta^{\prime},x_{i})-f_{L}(\theta_{0},x_{i})}{h}.

Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

Proof of Theorem

Notation for this appendix.

In this appendix, we use

to denote the Jacobian of the network output
F_{L}
, evaluated in
\theta
. We recall that

is a matrix with
N

\sum_{l=0}^{L-3}n_{l}n_{l+1}+2n_{L-2}n_{L-1}+2n_{L-1}

(L-1)
-th layer has an even number of neurons and denote its width as
2n_{L-1}
). Let

be the associated empirical NTK Gram matrix, and let
\theta_{0}
be the initialization defined in (
4.2
). We also make the dependence on
\theta
explicit for feature vectors and backpropagation terms: the feature vector at the
l
-th layer with input
x_{i}
and network parameter
\theta
is denoted by
f_{l}(\theta,x_{i})
, and the corresponding backpropagation term is denoted by
b_{l}(\theta,x_{i})
, where

b_{l}(\theta,x_{i})=(B_{l}(\theta))_{i:}
. Finally, we use
W_{l}(\theta)
to denote the weights of the
l
-th layer evaluated at the parameter
\theta
.

A straightforward application of Theorem 2.1 in
[
49
]
gives the following proposition.

Proposition H.1
.

Consider solving the least-squares optimization problem

\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{\theta}\left\|F_{L}(\theta)-Y\right\|_{2}^{2},

(H.1)

by running gradient descent updates of the form

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})
,
with some initialization
\tilde{\theta}_{0}
. Assume there exists
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, such that, if we define
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\theta}_{0},R)
as the
\ell_{2}
ball centered in
\tilde{\theta}_{0}
with radius
R
, with

R:=\frac{4\left\|F_{L}(\tilde{\theta}_{0})-Y\right\|_{2}}{\alpha},

(H.2)

the following holds

\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}:\;\alpha\leq\sigma_{\textup{min}}(J(\theta))\leq\left\lVert J(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\beta,

(H.3)

\forall\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}:\;\left\lVert J(\theta_{1})-J(\theta_{2})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\beta}.

(H.4)

Then, by setting
\eta\leq 1/(2\beta^{2})
, we have that, for all
t\geq 1
,

\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\leq\left(1-\frac{\eta\alpha^{2}}{2}\right)^{t}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\theta}_{0}).

(H.5)

In order to apply this proposition with initialization
\tilde{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}
, we need to prove that the necessary assumptions hold. We will do so by showing the following intermediate results:

•

Lemma
H.2
shows that, at the initial point
\theta_{0}
, the network output is
0
and the smallest NTK eigenvalue is lower bounded.

•

Lemma
H.3
gives a tight estimate on the operator norm of the weights inside a ball
\mathcal{D}
centered at
\theta_{0}
and with radius

.

•

Lemma
H.4
gives an upper bound on the distance between a feature vector in
\mathcal{D}
and the feature vector at
\theta_{0}
.

•

Lemma
H.5
gives upper bounds on the
\ell_{2}
norm and the
\ell_{2}
distance between feature vectors in
\mathcal{D}
.

•

Lemmas
H.6
and
H.7
give upper bounds on the
\ell_{2}
norm and the
\ell_{2}
distance of backpropagation terms in
\mathcal{D}
, respectively.

•

Lemma
H.8
gives an upper bound on the difference in operator norm between Jacobians in
\mathcal{D}
.

•

Finally, Lemma
H.9
gives upper and lower bounds on the NTK spectrum in
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma H.2

(Network output and smallest NTK eigenvalue at initialization)
.

Let
\theta_{0}
be defined in (
4.2
). Then, we have that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f_{L}(x,\theta_{0})=0.

(H.6)

Furthermore, we have that

\sigma_{\textup{min}}(J(\theta_{0}))\geq c_{1}\sqrt{\gamma n_{L-2}n_{L-1}}),

(H.7)

with probability at least
1-C\,Ne^{-c\log^{2}n_{L-1}}-Ce^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\sim_{\rm i.i.d.}P_{X}
and
\theta_{0}
, where
c,c_{1}
and
C
are numerical constants.

Proof.

By definition (
4.2
) of the initialization
\theta_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle f_{L}(x,\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\phi((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x))

(H.8)

\displaystyle+(W_{L}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\phi((W_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x))

\displaystyle=(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\phi((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x))

\displaystyle+(-W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\phi((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x))

\displaystyle=0,

where in the second equality we use that
W_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})=W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
and that
W_{L}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})=-W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
. This proves (
H.6
).

Let us now compute the Jacobian at initialization
J(\theta_{0})
. For
l\in[L-2]
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{L}(x)}{\partial(W_{l})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle=(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\hskip-3.99994pt\left(\phi^{\prime}\left((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x)\right)\left((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-2}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{l})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)\right)

(H.9)

\displaystyle+(W_{L}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left((W_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x)\right)\left((W_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-2}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{l})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\hskip-3.99994pt\left(\phi^{\prime}\left((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x)\right)\left((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-2}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{l})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta_{0},x)\right)\left((W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-2}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{l})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

where in the second equality we use again that
W_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})=W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
and that
W_{L}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})=-W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
.

Let us define
f_{L-1}^{(k)}(\theta,x):=\phi((W_{L-1}^{(k)}(\theta))^{\top}f_{L-2}(\theta,x))
for
k\in\{1,2\}
. Then, for the
(L-1)
-th layer, by isolating the computation over
W_{L-1}^{(1)}
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{L}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{L-1}^{(1)})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle=(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{L-1}^{(1)})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}+(W_{L}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{L-1}^{(1)})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}

(H.10)

\displaystyle=(W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}\frac{\partial f_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{L-1}^{(1)})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle=:J^{(1)}(\theta_{0}),

where we use that
f_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta,x)
does not depend on the parameters
W_{L-1}^{(1)}
.
Proceeding in the same way and using that
W_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})=W_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
and
W_{L}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})=-W_{L}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
, we also obtain that

\frac{\partial f_{L}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{L-1}^{(2)})_{ij}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}=-J^{(1)}(\theta_{0}).

(H.11)

Finally, by observing that
f_{L}(\theta,x)=(W_{L}^{(1)})^{\top}f_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta,x)+(W_{L}^{(2)})^{\top}f_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta,x)
, we deduce

\frac{\partial f_{L}(\theta,x)}{\partial(W_{L}^{(k)})_{i}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta=\theta_{0}}=\left(f_{L-1}^{(k)}(\theta_{0},x)\right)_{i},\quad\mbox{for }k\in\{1,2\}.

(H.12)

Hence, the NTK at initialization
K(\theta_{0})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle K(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=J^{(1)}(\theta_{0})(J^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}+J^{(1)}(\theta_{0})(J^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}

(H.13)

\displaystyle+F_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0})(F_{L-1}^{(1)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}+F_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0})(F_{L-1}^{(2)}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}.

Note that, by construction,
J^{(1)}(\theta_{0})
has the same distribution of
J_{L-2}
, whose rows are given by (
3.5
). Therefore, by combining the results from Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
, we conclude that

\sigma_{\text{min}}(J(\theta_{0}))\geq 2\sqrt{\gamma}\sigma_{\text{min}}(J_{L-2})\geq c_{1}\sqrt{\gamma n_{L-2}n_{L-1}},

(H.14)

with probability at least

1-C\,Ne^{-c\log^{2}n_{L-1}}-Ce^{-c\log^{2}N}
over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
\theta_{0}
, where
c_{1},C
are numerical constants. This proves (
H.7
), and concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma H.3

(Operator norm of weights in
\mathcal{D}
)
.

Let
\theta_{0}
be defined in (
4.2
), let
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}(\theta_{0},R)
and assume that
R=o(1)
. Then, for any
l\in[L-1]
,

\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(H.15)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
W_{l}(\theta_{0})
.

Proof.

By Weyl’s theorem, we have that, for all
l\in[L-2]
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta_{0})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta)-W_{l}(\theta_{0})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(H.16)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta_{0})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|W_{l}(\theta)-W_{l}(\theta_{0})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta_{0})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta_{0})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

where in the fourth line we use that
R=o(1)
, and the result of the last line holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
W_{l}(\theta_{0})
by Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
65
]
. By following the same argument, we have that, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
W_{L-1}(\theta_{0})
,

\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{L-1}^{(k)}(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),\quad\mbox{for }k\in\{1,2\},

(H.17)

which readily implies that
\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{L-1}(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
and concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma H.4

(Distance of features in
\mathcal{D}
from initialization)
.

Let
\theta_{0}
be defined in (
4.2
),
x\sim P_{X}
,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}(\theta_{0},R)

over

and
x
, where

For the induction case, let

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|f_{l}(\theta,x)-f_{l}(\theta_{0},x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\phi\left((W_{l}(\theta))^{\top}f_{l-1}(\theta,x)\right)-\phi\left((W_{l}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{l-1}(\theta_{0},x)\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle+M\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|(W_{l}(\theta))^{\top}f_{l-1}(\theta_{0},x)-(W_{l}(\theta_{0}))^{\top}f_{l-1}(\theta_{0},x)\right\|_{2}

with probability at least

over
W_{l}(\theta_{0})

gives that

(H.23)

with probability at least

(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=1}^{l-1}
and
x

with probability at least

over
(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=1}^{l}

x

\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{l}(\theta_{1})-W_{l}(\theta_{2})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|W_{l}(\theta_{1})-W_{l}(\theta_{2})\right\|_{F}\leq\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|\leq R.

(H.31)

Furthermore, by using (
H.25
), we have that

\sup_{\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|f_{l-1}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}\leq C\,R\,\sqrt{d},

(H.32)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=1}^{l-1}
and
x
. By combining (
H.28
), (
H.29
), (
H.30
), (
H.31
) and (
H.32
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|f_{l}(\theta_{1},x)-f_{l}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\,R\sqrt{d},

with probability at least

Lemma H.6

(
\ell_{2}
norm of backpropagation in
\mathcal{D}
)
.

Let
\theta_{0}
be defined in (
4.2
),
x\sim P_{X}
, and
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}(\theta_{0},R)
. Assume that
R=o(1)
and that
\gamma>1
. Then, for any
l\in[L]
, we have

\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|b_{l}(\theta,x)\right\|_{2}\leq C\sqrt{\gamma\cdot n_{L-1}},

(H.34)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=l+1}^{L}
, where
c,C
are numerical constants.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
l\in\{L,L-1,\ldots,1\}
. For the base case, we have that
\left\|b_{L}(\theta,x)\right\|_{2}=1
, hence (
H.34
) clearly holds.

For the induction case, pick
l\in[L-1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|b_{l}(\theta,x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\left(\prod_{k=l}^{L-2}\Sigma_{k}(\theta,x)W_{k+1}(\theta)\right)\Sigma_{L-1}(\theta,x)W_{L}(\theta)\right\|_{2}

(H.35)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\left(\prod_{k=l}^{L-2}\Sigma_{k}(\theta,x)W_{k+1}(\theta)\right)\Sigma_{L-1}(\theta,x)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|W_{L}(\theta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{k=l}^{L-2}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\Sigma_{k}(\theta,x)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{k+1}(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right)\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\Sigma_{L-1}(\theta,x)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|W_{L}(\theta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq M^{L-l}\left(\prod_{k=l+1}^{L-1}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{k}(\theta)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right)\left(\left\|W_{L}(\theta_{0})\right\|_{2}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|W_{L}(\theta)-W_{L}(\theta_{0})\right\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\,M^{L-l}(\left\|W_{L}(\theta_{0})\right\|_{2}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|_{2})

\displaystyle\leq C\,M^{L-l}(\sqrt{\gamma n_{L-1}}+\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\theta-\theta_{0}\right\|_{2})

\displaystyle=C\sqrt{\gamma n_{L-1}}.

Here, the fourth line follows from Assumption
3
, which gives
\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\Sigma_{k}(\theta,x)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq M
; the fifth line holds with probability
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=l+1}^{L-1}
by Lemma
H.3
; the sixth line holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
W_{L}(\theta_{0})
by Theorem 3.1.1 in
[
65
]
; and the last line follows from
R=o(1)
. Taking the intersection of these events gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma H.7

(

)
.

(H.36)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=l+1}^{L}
and
x
, where

are numerical constants.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
l\in\{L,L-1,\ldots,1\}
. For the base case,

\theta

) clearly holds.
For the induction case, pick
l\in[L-1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\left\|b_{l}(\theta_{1},x)-b_{l}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}

(H.37)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\Sigma_{l}(\theta_{1},x)W_{l+1}(\theta_{1})b_{l+1}(\theta_{1},x)-\Sigma_{l}(\theta_{2},x)W_{l+1}(\theta_{2})b_{l+1}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta_{1}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\Sigma_{l}(\theta_{1},x)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta_{1}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert W_{l+1}(\theta_{1})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|b_{l+1}(\theta_{1},x)-b_{l+1}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\left(\Sigma_{l}(\theta_{1},x)-\Sigma_{l}(\theta_{2},x)\right)W_{l+1}(\theta_{2})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|b_{l+1}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle+\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\Sigma_{l}(\theta_{2},x)\left(W_{l+1}(\theta_{1})-W_{l+1}(\theta_{2})\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|b_{l+1}(\theta_{2},x)\right\|_{2}.

3

H.3

with probability

over
W_{l+1}(\theta_{0})
;

1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})

(W_{k}(\theta_{0}))_{k=l+2}^{L}
and
x
;

By the same argument of the second statement in Lemma
H.5

H.3
,

Lemma H.8

(Difference of Jacobians in
\mathcal{D}
)
.

Let
\theta_{0}
be defined in (
4.2
),
x\sim P_{X}
, and
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{B}(\theta_{0},R)
. Assume that
R=o(1)

. Then, we have

\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert J(\theta_{1})-J(\theta_{2})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq C\,R\,d\sqrt{\gamma n_{L-1}N},

over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
\theta_{0}
, where

Proof.

Pick

. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\left\|(J(\theta_{1}))_{i:}-(J(\theta_{2}))_{i:}\right\|^{2}_{2}

(H.40)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|(F_{l}(\theta_{1}))_{:i}\otimes(B_{l+1}(\theta_{1}))_{:i}-(F_{l}(\theta_{2}))_{:i}\otimes(B_{l+1}(\theta_{2}))_{:i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|\left(f_{l}(\theta_{1},x_{i})-f_{l}(\theta_{2},x_{i})\right)\otimes b_{l+1}(\theta_{1},x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|f_{l}(\theta_{2},x_{i})\otimes\left(b_{l+1}(\theta_{1},x_{i})-b_{l+1}(\theta_{1},x_{i})\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|f_{l}(\theta_{1},x_{i})-f_{l}(\theta_{2},x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}\sup_{\theta_{1}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|b_{l+1}(\theta_{1},x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}

H.4
,
H.5
,
H.6
and
H.7

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma R^{2}d^{2}n_{L-1},

(H.41)

with probability at least
1-C\exp(-cn_{L-1})
over
x_{i}
and
\theta_{0}
.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert J(\theta_{1})-J(\theta_{2})\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(H.42)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{D}}\left\|(J(\theta_{1}))_{i:}-(J(\theta_{2}))_{i:}\right\|^{2}_{2}}

over
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{N}

\theta_{0}
