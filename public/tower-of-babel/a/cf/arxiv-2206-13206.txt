Geometric Characterization of the Eyring-Kramers Formula
Benny Avelin, Vesa Julin, Lauri Viitasaari

Abstract
. In this paper we consider the mean transition time of an over-damped Brownian particle between local minima of a smooth potential. When the minima and saddles are non-degenerate this is in the low noise regime exactly characterized by the so called Eyring-Kramers law and gives the mean transition time as a quantity depending on the curvature of the minima and the saddle. In this paper we find an extension of the Eyring-Kramers law giving an upper bound on the mean transition time when both the minima/saddles are degenerate (flat) while at the same time covering multiple saddles at the same height. Our main contribution is a new sharp characterization of the capacity of two local minimas as a ratio of two geometric quantities, i.e., the smallest separating surface and the geodesic distance.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we investigate the so called metastable exit times for the stochastic differential equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle dX_{t}=-\nabla F(X_{t})dt+\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}dB_{t},

where
F
is a smooth potential with many local minimas and
\varepsilon
is a small number.

The main question of metastability is to determine how long time does the process
Eq.

1.1
take from going from one local minima to another one. We call these the metastable exit times. This question has a rich history and in the double well case with non-degenerate minimas and a saddle point this is characterized by a formula called Eyring-Kramers law
[
11
,
17
]
which can be stated as follows: Assume that
x
and
y
are quadratic local minimas of
F
, separated by a unique saddle
z
which is such that the Hessian has a single negative eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(z)
. Then the expected transition time from
x
to
y
satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}[\tau]\simeq\frac{2\pi}{|\lambda_{1}(z)|}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}F(z))}{\det(\nabla^{2}F(x))}}e^{(F(z)-F(x))/\varepsilon},

where
\simeq
denotes that the comparison constant tends to
1
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The validity of the above formula has been studied, from a qualitative perspective, quite extensively, starting from the work of Freidlin and Wentzell. For more information, see the book
[
12
]
. Roughly 15 years ago, Bovier et. al. produced a series of papers
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
(see also
[
5
]
) which provided the first proof of
Eq.

1.2
in the general setting of Morse functions. Specifically, they showed that the comparison function is like
1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1/2}|\log\varepsilon|^{3/2})
. In these papers, they utilized the connection to classical potential theory in order to reduce the problem of estimating metastable exit times to the problem of estimating certain capacities sharply. This approach was later used in
[
4
]
to generalize
Eq.

1.2
to general polynomial type of degeneracies.

In this paper we are interested in estimating the metastable exit times in the case of general type of degenerate critical points. This requires new techniques and effective notation from geometric function theory which we will describe below. Our motivation comes from the field of non-convex optimization where we cannot expect the minimas/saddles to be quadratic or even to have polynomial growth in any direction. In particular, such situations are well known in the context of neural networks, where the minimas and saddles may be completely flat in some directions
[
15
]
. Furthermore, it seems that they are preferrable, see
[
19
]
for a discussion, see also
[
3
]
for an explicit example.

The main goal is to estimate the dependency of the metastable exit times with respect to the geometry of the potential
F
. In the proof of
Eq.

1.2
in
[
8
]
this is reduced to estimating the ratio of the
L^{1}
norm of the hitting probability and the capacity. Thus in order to estimate the metastable exit times, one needs to produce

(1)

Estimates of the integral of the hitting probability, i.e. the integral of capacitary potentials with respect to the Gibbs measure.

(2)

Estimates of the capacity itself, i.e. estimates of the energy of the capacitary potentials.

The interesting point is that the influence of
F
on
Items

1
and
2
is in a sense dual. Specifically, the shape of minimas of
F
influence
Item

1
while the shape of saddles between minimas influence
Item

2
. As is well known, the main difficulty is to estimate
Item

2
, which is an interesting topic of its own.

Our main contribution is a sharp capacity estimate for a very general class of degenerate saddle points. In order to achieve this, we phrase the problem in the language of geometric function theory, where the capacity estimates are central topic
[
13
,
20
]
. We introduce two geometric quantities which allow us to estimate the capacity in a sharp and natural way. As a byproduct, we see that in the case of several saddle points at the same height, the topology dictates how the local capacities add up. Specifically, we consider two cases, which we call the parallel and the serial case, and it turns out that the formulas for the total capacity have natural counterparts in electrical networks of capacitors, see
Theorem

1
. Even in the context of non-degenerate saddles, our formulas provide a generalization of the result of
[
8
]
where the authors consider only the parallel case. As we mentioned, we allow the saddle points to be degenerate but we have to assume that saddles are non-branching, see
Eq.

1.6
.

1.1.
Assumptions and statement of the main results

In order to state our main results we first need to introduce our assumptions on the potential
F
. We also need to introduce notation from geometric function theory
which might seem rather heavy at first, but it turns out to be robust enough for us to treat the potentials with possible degenerate critical points.

Let us first introduce some general terminology. We say that a critical point
z
of a function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a local minimum (maximum) of
f
if
f(x)\geq f(z)
(
f(x)\leq f(z)
) in a neighborhood of
z
. If
f
is not locally constant at a critical point
z
, then
z
is a saddle point if it is not a local minimum / maximum.
For technical reasons we also allow saddle points to include points
z
where
f
is locally constant. We say that a local minimum at
z
is
proper
if there exists a
\hat{\delta}>0
such that for every
0<\delta<\hat{\delta}
there exists a
\rho
such that

\displaystyle f(x)\geq f(z)+\delta\quad\text{for all }\,x\in\partial B_{\rho}(z),

where
B_{\rho}(z)
denotes open ball with radius
\rho
centered at
z
. When the center is at the origin we use the short notation
B_{\rho}
.

Let us then proceed to our assumptions on the potential
F
. Throughout the paper we assume that
F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and satisfies the following quadratic growth condition

(1.3)

F(x)\geq\frac{|x|^{2}}{C_{0}}-C_{0}

for a constant
C_{0}\geq 1
. We assume that every local minimum point
z
of
F
is proper, as described above, and that there is a convex function
G_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which has a proper minimum at
0
with
G(0)=0
such that

(1.4)

\big|F(x+z)-F(z)-G_{z}(x)\big|\leq\omega\big(G_{z}(x)\big),

where
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous and increasing function with

(1.5)

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\omega(s)}{s}=0.

We denote by
\delta_{0}
the largest number for which
\omega(\delta)\leq\frac{\delta}{8}
for all
\delta\leq 4\delta_{0}
. We define a neighborhood of the local minimum point
z
and
\delta<\delta_{0}
as

O_{z,\delta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:G_{z}(x)<\delta\}+\{z\}.

For the saddles, we assume that for every saddle point
z
of
F
there are convex functions
g_{z}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
G_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
which have proper minimum at
0
with
g_{z}(0)=G_{z}(0)=0
, and that there exists an isometry
T_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, denoting
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, it holds

(1.6)

\big|(F\circ T_{z})(x)-F(z)+g_{z}(x_{1})-G_{z}(x^{\prime}))\big|\leq\omega(g_{z}(x_{1}))+\omega(G_{z}(x^{\prime})),

where
\omega:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is as in
Eq.

1.5
. The assumption
Eq.

1.6
allows the saddle point to be degenerate, but we do not allow them to have many branches, i.e., the sets
\{F<F(z)\}\cap B_{\rho}(z)
cannot have more than two components. Note that the convex functions
g_{z},G_{z}
and the isometry
T_{z}
depend on
z
, while the function
\omega
is the same for all saddle points. We define a neighborhood of the saddle point
z
and
\delta<\delta_{0}
as

(1.7)

O_{z,\delta}:=T_{z}^{-1}\left(\{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}:g_{z}(x_{1})<\delta\}\times\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:G_{z}(x^{\prime})<\delta\}\right),

where
T_{z}
is the isometry in
Eq.

1.4
. Note that, since the saddle may be flat, we should talk about sets rather than points. However, we adopt the convention that we always choose a representative point from each saddle (set) and thus we may label the saddles by points
z_{1},z_{2},\dots
. Moreover, we assume that there is a
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{0}
such that for
\delta<\delta_{1}
we have that if
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are two different saddle points, then their neighborhoods
O_{z_{1},3\delta}
and
O_{z_{2},3\delta}
defined in
Eq.

1.7
are disjoint. We assume the same for local minimas (or more precisely, the representative points of sets of local minimas).

Let us then introduce the notation related to geometric function theory, see
[
13
,
20
]
. Let us fix two disjoint sets
A
and
B
in a domain
\Omega
(open and connected set). We say that a smooth path
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

connects
A
and
B
in the domain
\Omega
if

\displaystyle\gamma(0)\in A,\quad\gamma(1)\in B\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma([0,1])\subset\Omega.

In this case we denote
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(A,B;\Omega)
. We follow the standard notation from geometric function theory and define a dual object to this by saying that a smooth hypersurface
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(possibly with boundary)
separates
A
from
B
in
\Omega
if every path

\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(A,B;\Omega)
intersects
S
. In this case we denote
S\in\mathcal{S}(A,B;\Omega)
. We define the geodesic distance between
A
and
B
in

as

(1.8)

d_{\varepsilon}(A,B;\Omega):=\inf\left(\int_{\gamma}|\gamma^{\prime}|e^{\frac{F(\gamma)}{\varepsilon}}\,dt:\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(A,B;\Omega)\right)

and its dual by

(1.9)

V_{\varepsilon}(A,B;\Omega):=\inf\left(\int_{S}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x):S\in\mathcal{S}(A,B;\Omega)\right).

Here

denotes the

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(A,B)=\operatorname{cap}(A,B;\mathbb{R}^{n})

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
F
satisfies the structural assumptions above. Let
x_{a}
and
x_{b}
be two local minimum points of
F
and let
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
be the set of saddle points which charge capacity as defined above, and let
0<\delta\leq\delta_{1}
be fixed. There exists an
0<\varepsilon_{0}\leq\delta
such that if
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
the following holds:

If the points in
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
are parallel, then, using the notation
U_{z_{i},\delta}
from
Eq.

1.10
, it holds

(1.17)

\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))\simeq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});U_{z_{i},\delta}).

Moreover for all
i=1,\dots,N
we have the estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});U_{z_{i},\delta})\simeq\varepsilon\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});U_{z_{i},\delta})}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});U_{z_{i},\delta})}e^{\frac{F(z_{i})}{\varepsilon}},

where
d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});U_{z_{i},\delta})
and
V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});U_{z_{i},\delta})
are defined in
Eq.

1.8
and
Eq.

1.9
.

If the points in
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}
are in series, then, using the ordering
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
from
Eq.

1.11
for the points in
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}
and the points
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
defined in
Eq.

1.12
, it holds

(1.18)

\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))}\simeq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))},

where we have the estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))\simeq\varepsilon\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i}))}e^{\frac{F(z_{i})}{\varepsilon}}

for all
i=1,\dots,N
.

Let us make a few remarks on the statement of the above theorem. First, in the case of a single saddle
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}=\{z\}
the above capacity estimate reduces to

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))\simeq\varepsilon\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))}e^{\frac{F(z)}{\varepsilon}},

where
d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))
is the geodesic distance between
B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a})
and
B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),
and
V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))
is the area of the ’smallest cross section’. This is in accordance with the classical result on parallel plate capacitors, where the capacity depends linearly on the area and is inversely proportional to their distance.

The statement
Eq.

1.17
, when the saddle points are parallel, means that each saddle point
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
charges capacity and the total capacity is their sum. Again the situation is the same as in the case of parallel plate capacitors with capacity
C_{1},\dots,C_{N}
, where the total capacity is the sum

\displaystyle C=C_{1}+\dots+C_{N}.

On the other hand, if the plate capacitors are in series their total capacity satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}=\frac{1}{C_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{C_{N}}

which is precisely the statement in
Eq.

1.18
.

Using the assumption
Eq.

1.6
we calculate in
Proposition

4.1
and in
Proposition

4.2
more explicit, but less geometric, formulas for the single saddle case in a domain
\Omega
. Namely, we have

\displaystyle d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)\simeq e^{\frac{F(z)}{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{g_{z}(x_{1})}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}

and

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)\simeq e^{-\frac{F(z)}{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-\frac{G_{z}(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}\,dx^{\prime},

and thus we recover the result in
[
4
]
. In particular, if the saddle point is non-degenerate, i.e.,
g_{z}
and
G_{z}
are second order polynomials and the negative eigenvalue of
\nabla^{2}F(z)
is
-\lambda_{1}
, we may estimate

\displaystyle d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{\lambda_{1}}}\,e^{\frac{F(z)}{\varepsilon}}

and

\displaystyle V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}))\simeq(2\pi\varepsilon)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\,e^{-\frac{F(z)}{\varepsilon}}}{\sqrt{\det(\nabla^{2}F(z))}}.

In particular, we recover the classical formula
Eq.

1.2
.

Our second main theorem is an estimate on the so called metastable exit times. However, in order to state it we need some further definitions. Assume that the local minimas of
F
are labelled
x_{i}
and ordered such that
F(x_{i})\leq F(x_{j})
if
i\leq j
. We will group the minimas at the same level using the sets
G_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, i.e.
x_{i},x_{j}\in G_{k}
if
F(x_{i})=F(x_{j})
, and
x\in G_{i}
and
y\in G_{j}
, then
F(x)<F(y)
for
i<j
. We also write
F(G_{i}):=F(x)
with
x\in G_{i}
. Furthermore, we will denote
S_{k}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
.
We will also consider
G_{k}^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{x\in G_{k}}B_{\varepsilon}(x)
and
S_{k}^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}^{\varepsilon}
.

In addition to the previous structural assumptions we assume further that for
\delta_{2}\leq\delta_{1}
small enough, it holds

(1.19)

F(G_{k+1})-F(G_{k})\geq\delta_{2}

for all
k=1,\ldots,K
. In our second theorem we give an upper bound on the exit time for the process defined by
Eq.

1.1
to go from local minimum point in
G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon}
to a lower one in
S_{k}
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
F
satisfies the structural assumptions above, and let
\Omega
be a domain that contains
S_{k+1}^{\epsilon}
. There exists an
0<\varepsilon_{0}\leq\delta_{2}
such that if
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the following holds:

For
x\in G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}[\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}<\tau_{\Omega^{c}}}]\leq\frac{Ce^{-F(G_{k+1})/\varepsilon}\sum_{x\in G_{k+1}}|O_{x,\varepsilon}|}{\max_{x\in G_{k},y\in G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon}}\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x),B_{\varepsilon}(y);\Omega)}+C\varepsilon^{\alpha/2}.

Let
x_{a}\in G_{k}
,
x_{b}\in G_{k+1}
be a pair that maximizes the pairwise capacity. Then, with the notation of
Theorem

1
, we get in the parallel case

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}[\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}<\tau_{\Omega^{c}}}]\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\frac{Ce^{-F(G_{k+1})/\varepsilon}\sum_{x\in G_{k+1}}|O_{x,\varepsilon}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-F(z_{i})/\epsilon}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{G_{z_{i}}<\epsilon\})}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{g_{z_{i}}<\epsilon\})}}+C\varepsilon^{\alpha/2},

and in the series case we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}[\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}<\tau_{\Omega^{c}}}]\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\sum_{x\in G_{k+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{(F(z_{i})-F(G_{k+1}))/\varepsilon}\frac{|O_{x,\varepsilon}|\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{g_{z_{i}}<\epsilon\})}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{G_{z_{i}}<\epsilon\})}+C\varepsilon^{\alpha/2}.

If both the minimas and saddles are non-degenerate points, and there is only one saddle connecting
x_{a},x_{b}
(with
F(x_{a})<F(x_{b})
), where
x_{a},x_{b}
are the only minimas of
F
, the above estimate coincides with Eyring-Kramers formula (up to a constant)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{x}[\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}<\tau_{\Omega^{c}}}]\leq C\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}F(z))}{\det(\nabla^{2}F(x_{a}))}}e^{(F(z)-F(x_{a}))/\epsilon}.

Here
\lambda_{1}
is the first eigenvalue of the Hessian of
F
at the saddle
z
, and the additive error in
Theorem

2
can be removed for small
\epsilon
as the right hand side of the above tends to
\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

2.
Preliminaries

The generator of the process
Eq.

1.1
is the following elliptic operator

(2.1)

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon\Delta+\nabla F\cdot\nabla.

In this section we study the potential and regularity theory associated with the operator
Eq.

2.1
.
We provide the identities and pointwise estimates that we will need in the course of the proofs. Most of these are standard, but we provide them adapted to our situation for the reader’s convenience. We note that in this section we only require that the potential
F
is of class
C^{2}
and satisfy the quadratic growth condition
Eq.

1.3
.

2.1.
Potential theory

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a regular domain and let
G_{\Omega}(x,y)
be the Green’s function for
\Omega
, i.e., for every
f\in C(\Omega)
the function

\displaystyle u_{f}=\int_{\Omega}G_{\Omega}(x,y)f(y)dy

is the solution of the Poisson equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}L_{\varepsilon}u_{f}=f&\text{in }\,\Omega\\
u_{f}=0&\text{on }\,\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The natural associated measures are the Gibbs measure
d\mu_{\varepsilon}=e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx
and the Gibbs surface measure
d\sigma_{\varepsilon}=e^{-F/\varepsilon}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the Green’s function is symmetric w.r.t. the Gibbs measure, i.e.

\displaystyle G(x,y)e^{-F(x)/\varepsilon}=G(y,x)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}.

We also have the fundamental Green’s identities. Here we assume that
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain and denote the inner normal by
n
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain,
\psi,\phi
be in
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
, and
G_{\Omega}
be the Green’s function for
\Omega
. Then the following Green’s identities holds
(Green’s first identity)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi L_{\varepsilon}\phi-\varepsilon\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\phi d\mu_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\nabla\phi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon},

and
(Green’s second identity)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi L_{\varepsilon}\phi-\phi L_{\varepsilon}\psi d\mu_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\nabla\phi\cdot n-\phi\nabla\psi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

Furthermore, the following (balayage) representation formula holds: for every
g\in C(\partial\Omega)
the function

(2.4)

\displaystyle u(x)=\varepsilon e^{F(x)/\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega}g\nabla_{y}G_{\Omega}(y,x)\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)

is the solution of the Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}L_{\varepsilon}u=0&\text{in }\,\Omega\\
u=g&\text{on }\,\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

Integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi L_{\varepsilon}\phi d\mu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\psi(-\varepsilon\Delta\phi+\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi)d\mu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\phi-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\psi\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi+\psi\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi)d\mu_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\nabla\phi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\phi d\mu_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\nabla\phi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

The second Green’s identity follows from the first by applying it twice

\int_{\Omega}\psi L_{\varepsilon}\phi-\varepsilon\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\phi d\mu_{\varepsilon}-\int_{\Omega}\phi L_{\varepsilon}\psi+\varepsilon\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\phi d\mu_{\varepsilon}\\
=\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\nabla\phi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi\nabla\psi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

We may now obtain the representation formula for the Dirichlet problem. We choose
\phi(x)=G_{\Omega}(x,y)
and obtain by Green’s second identity that

\displaystyle\psi(x)e^{-F(x)/\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi\nabla\phi\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}\phi L_{\varepsilon}\psi d\mu_{\varepsilon}.

Now relabeling
x\to y
, we get the representation formula
Eq.

2.4
.
∎

Recall the definition of capacity (variational): for
A,B\subset\Omega
two disjoint compact sets

(2.5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(A,B;\Omega):=\inf\left(\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla h|^{2}e^{-\frac{F}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\,:\,\,h\geq 1\,\,\text{in }A,u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega\setminus B)\right).

The extension of capacity to open sets follows in the classical way

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(U,B;\Omega):=\sup\{\operatorname{cap}(A,B;\Omega)\,:\,\text{$A$ compact and $A\subset U$}\}.

It is well known that for bounded sets with regular boundary the continuity of the capacity implies that
\operatorname{cap}(U,B;\Omega)=\operatorname{cap}(\overline{U},B;\Omega)
. The extension w.r.t the second entry follows similarly.
The variational definition of the capacity has many equivalent forms, one that we will need is the one below:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A,B\subset\Omega
be two disjoint compact sets. Then the variational formulation of capacity coincides with the balayage definition, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(A,B;\Omega)=\sup\left\{\int_{A}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu(y):\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset A,\int_{\Omega}G_{\Omega\setminus B}(x,y)d\mu(y)\leq 1\right\}.

The unique measure which maximizes the above, i.e., satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{A}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{A,B}(y)=\operatorname{cap}(A,B;\Omega),\quad\int_{\Omega}G_{\Omega\setminus B}(x,y)d\mu(y)\leq 1,

is called the equilibrium measure
\mu_{A,B}
. The corresponding equilibrium potential is defined as
h_{A,B}=\int_{\Omega}G_{\Omega\setminus B}(x,y)d\mu_{A,B}(y)
and is the minimizer of
Eq.

2.5
.

If in addition
A,B
are smooth, then we have

(2.6)

\begin{split}\operatorname{cap}(A,B;\Omega)&=\int_{A}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{A,B}(y)=\varepsilon\int|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}d\mu_{\varepsilon}\\
&=-\varepsilon\int_{\partial A}\nabla h_{A,B}\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.\end{split}

Proof.

The claim follows from the symmetry of the Green’s function,
Remark

2.2
, and the strong maximum principle that
h_{A,B}=1
in
A
, see
[
1
,
10
]
.
From
Eq.

2.2
we get that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus B}h_{A,B}L_{\varepsilon}h_{A,B}-\varepsilon|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}d\mu_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\int_{\partial(\Omega\setminus B)}h_{A,B}\nabla h_{A,B}\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

Using
h_{A,B}=0
on
\partial(\Omega\setminus B)
we see that the right hand side of the above is zero. Moreover, from
L_{\epsilon}h_{A,B}=\mu_{A,B}
and from the definition of
d\mu_{\epsilon}
we get

(2.7)

\displaystyle\int_{A}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{A,B}(y)=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}d\mu_{\varepsilon}.

Note that since
h_{A,B}=1
in
A
and
0
on
\partial(\Omega\setminus B)
, and since
L_{\epsilon}h_{A,B}=0
in
\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)
, we have by the uniqueness of the solution to the Dirichlet problem that
h_{A,B}
coincides with the variational minimizer of
Eq.

2.5
. This establishes the first two equalities of
Eq.

2.6
.

To prove the last equality in
Eq.

2.6
we insert
h_{A,B}=\phi=\psi
into
Eq.

2.2
(Green’s first identity) and get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}h_{A,B}L_{\varepsilon}h_{A,B}-\varepsilon|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}d\mu_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\int_{\partial(\Omega\setminus(A\cup B))}h_{A,B}\nabla h_{A,B}\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

Since
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{A,B}\subset A
, and
h_{A,B}=0
on
B
and
1
on
A
, we get

(2.8)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}d\mu_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\int_{\partial A}\nabla h_{A,B}\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon},

where
n
is the outward unit normal of
A
.
The result now follows from
Eqs.

2.7
and
2.8
.
∎

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain and
A\subset\Omega
. Then we define the potential of the equilibrium potential as

\displaystyle w_{A,\Omega}(x)=\int_{\Omega}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(x,y)h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(y)dy.

The definition of the potential of the equilibrium potential might seem technical at first. However,
w_{A,\Omega}
has a clear probabilistic interpretation as the expected hitting time of hitting
A
of a process killed at
\partial\Omega
. Indeed, the probabilistic interpretation of
h_{A,\Omega^{c}}
is
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{A}<\tau_{\Omega^{c}})
i.e. the probability of hitting
A
before
{\Omega^{c}}
.
By Dynkin’s formula we see that then

\displaystyle w_{A,{\Omega}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}[w_{A,{\Omega}}(X_{\tau_{A\cup{\Omega^{c}}}})]-\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{A\cup{\Omega^{c}}}}L_{\varepsilon}w_{A,{\Omega}}(X_{t})dt\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\mathbb{I}_{t\leq\tau_{A\cup{\Omega^{c}}}}\mathbb{E}^{X_{t}}[\mathbb{I}_{A}(X_{\tau_{A\cup{\Omega^{c}}}})]\right]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}^{x}\left[\mathbb{I}_{t\leq\tau_{A\cup{\Omega^{c}}}}\mathbb{I}_{A}(X_{\tau_{A\cup{\Omega^{c}}}})\right]dt

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}[\tau_{A}\mathbb{I}_{\tau_{A}<\tau_{\Omega^{c}}}].

We also have the following integration by parts formula for the potential of the equilibrium potential:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain, let
A\subset\Omega
be a smooth set, and assume that
B_{2\rho}(x)\subset\Omega\setminus A
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}w_{A,\Omega}(y)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)=\int_{\Omega\setminus A}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)h_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(z)d\mu_{\varepsilon}.

The above statement looks more familiar if we write it in the formal way as

\displaystyle\int w_{A,\Omega}e^{-F/\varepsilon}L_{\varepsilon}h_{B_{\rho},A}=\int L_{\varepsilon}w_{A,\Omega}e^{-F/\varepsilon}h_{B_{\rho},A}dz.

Proof.

Using the definition of
w_{A,\Omega}
and Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}

\displaystyle w_{A,\Omega}(y)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus A}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(y,z)h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)dz\right)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus A}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(y,z)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)dz.

Using the symmetry of the Green’s function,
Remark

2.2
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus A}

\displaystyle h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(y,z)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\setminus A}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)e^{-F(z)/\varepsilon}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(z,y)d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)dz.

Note that
\operatorname{supp}\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}\subset\partial B_{\rho}(x)
and as such

\displaystyle u(z)=\int_{\Omega}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(z,y)d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)=\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(z,y)d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)

solves the equation
L_{\epsilon}u=\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}
in
\Omega
and
u=0
on
\partial{\Omega\setminus A}
. Consequently, by the uniqueness result to the Dirichlet-Poisson problem, we get
u(z)=h_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(z)
. Hence

\int_{\Omega\setminus A}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)e^{-F(z)/\varepsilon}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}G_{\Omega\setminus A}(z,y)d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)dz\\
=\int_{\Omega\setminus A}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)h_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(z)d\mu_{\varepsilon}(z).

Combining the equalities above yields the result.
∎

2.2.
Classical pointwise estimates

In this section we recall classical pointwise estimates for functions which satisfy

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}u=f

in a domain
\Omega
, where the operator
L_{\varepsilon}
is defined in
Eq.

2.1
. First, since we assume that
F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then for all Hölder continuous
f
the solutions of the above equation are
C^{2,\alpha}
-regular, see
[
14
]
. However, these regularity estimates depend on
\varepsilon
and blow up as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The point is that we may obtain regularity estimates for constants independent of
\varepsilon
if we restrict ourselves on small enough scales. To this aim, for a given domain
\Omega
we choose a positive number
\nu
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla F\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}{\varepsilon}\leq\nu.

We have the following two theorems from
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be a non-negative function satisfying
L_{\varepsilon}u=0
. Then for any
B_{3R}(x)\subset\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}(x)}u\leq C\inf_{B_{R}(x)}u

for a constant
C=C(n,\nu R)
. In particular, if
\|\nabla F\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq L
, then for
R\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{L}
the constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, for
p\in(1,\infty)
and any number
k
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}(x)}|u-k|\leq C_{p}\left(\strokedint_{B_{2R}(x)}|u-k|^{p}dx\right)^{1/p}

and

\displaystyle\left(\strokedint_{B_{2R}(x)}|u|^{p}dx\right)^{1/p}\leq C_{p}\inf_{B_{R}(x)}u,

where the symbol
\strokedint
denotes the average integral, and the constant
C_{p}
in addition to above depends also on
p
.

In the non-homogeneous case
L_{\varepsilon}u=f
we have the following generalization of Harnack’s inequality.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be a non-negative function satisfying
L_{\varepsilon}u=f
. Then for any
B_{3R}(x)\subset\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}(x)}u\leq C\left(\inf_{B_{R}(x)}u+\frac{R}{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{n}(B_{2R}(x))}\right)

for a constant
C=C(n,\nu R)
. In particular, if
\|\nabla F\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq L
, then for
R\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{L}
the constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
and we have

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}(x)}u\leq C\left(\inf_{B_{R}(x)}u+\|f\|_{L^{n}(B_{2R}(x))}\right).

The Harnack inequality in
Lemma

2.7
holds also in the case of the punctured ball.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(B_{3R}(x)\setminus\{x\})
be a non-negative function satisfying
L_{\varepsilon}u=0
in
B_{3R}(x)\setminus\{x\}
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\partial B_{R}(x)}u\leq C\inf_{\partial B_{R}(x)}u

for a constant
C=C(n,\nu R)
.

Proof.

By translating the coordinates we may assume that
x=0
. Let
x_{0},y_{0}\in\partial B_{R}
be such that
\sup_{\partial B_{R}(x)}u=u(x_{0})
and
\inf_{\partial B_{R}(x)}u=u(y_{0})
. We choose points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N-1},x_{N}\in\partial B_{R}
such that
|x_{i}-x_{i-1}|\leq R/4
and
x_{N}=y_{0}
. Note that the number
N
is bounded. Now we may use Harnack’s inequality
Lemma

2.7
in balls
B_{R/4}(x_{i})
to get

\displaystyle u(x_{i-1})\leq Cu(x_{i}).

We obtain the claim by summing over
i=1,\dots,N
.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and let
u,h\in C^{2}(\Omega)
be non-negative functions such that
L_{\varepsilon}u=h
and
h
satisfies Harnack’s inequality with constant
c_{0}
. Then the function
v=u+h
satisfies Harnack’s inequality, i.e., for all
B_{3R}(x)\subset\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}(x)}v\leq C\inf_{B_{R}(x)}v

for a constant
C=C(n,\nu R,R^{2}/\varepsilon,c_{0})
. In particular, if
\|\nabla F\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq L
, then for
R\leq\min\{\varepsilon/L,\sqrt{\varepsilon}\}
the constant
C
is independent of
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Again we may assume that
x=0
. Using
Lemma

2.8
and Harnack’s inequality for
h
yields

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}}u

\displaystyle\leq C\inf_{B_{R}}u+\frac{CR}{\varepsilon}\|h\|_{L^{n}(B_{2R})}

\displaystyle\leq C\inf_{B_{R}}u+\frac{CR}{\varepsilon}|B_{R}|^{1/n}\inf_{B_{R}}h

\displaystyle\leq C\inf_{B_{R}}u+\frac{CR^{2}}{\varepsilon}\inf_{B_{R}}h.

Now, using Harnack’s inequality for
h
again, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{R}}v

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{B_{R}}u+\sup_{B_{R}}h\leq C\inf_{B_{R}}u+C\inf_{B_{R}}h\leq C\inf_{B_{R}}v

for a constant
C
as in the statement. This proves the claim.
∎

The Harnack’s inequality in
Lemma

2.7
implies Hölder continuity for solutions of
L_{\varepsilon}u=0
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(B_{3R}(x))
be a function such that for any constant
c
, for which
v=u+c
is non-negative, the function
v
satisfies Harnack’s inequality with constant
C_{0}
, independent of
c
. Then there exists
C=C(C_{0})>1
and
\alpha=\alpha(C_{0})\in(0,1)
such that, for all
\rho\leq R
, it holds that

\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}(x)}u\leq C\left(\frac{\rho}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\operatorname{osc}_{B_{R}(x)}u.

In particular, if
u,h\in C^{2}(\Omega)
are non-negative functions such that
L_{\varepsilon}u=h
and
h
satisfies Harnack’s inequality with constant
C_{0}
, then
u+h
satisfies the estimate above.

Proof.

The proof follows verbatim from the classical proof of Moser, see
[
14
, Theorem 8.22]
.
∎

3.
Technical lemmas

In this section we provide some preliminary results for the proofs of the main theorems. We recall that we assume that the potential
F
satisfies the structural assumptions from

Section

1.1
, and that from this moment on our constants are allowed to depend on the data, see paragraph after
Eq.

1.16
.

3.1.
Rough estimates for potentials

In this subsection we provide estimates for the capacitary potential
h_{A,B}
, when
A
and
B
are two disjoint closed sets.
The first estimate is the so called renewal estimate of
[
8
]
. In order to trace dependencies of constants, we provide a proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain, let
A,B\subset\Omega
be disjoint smooth sets, and consider
h_{A,B}
as the capacitary potential in
\Omega
. Assume that
B_{4\varrho}(x)\subset(\Omega\setminus(A\cup B))
, and that
r\leq\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{\|\nabla F\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{2\varrho}(x))}},\varrho\right\}
.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(n,\nu)>1
such that

\displaystyle h_{A,B}(x)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\operatorname{cap}(B_{r}(x),A;\Omega)}{\operatorname{cap}(B_{r}(x),B;\Omega)}.

Proof.

Again, without loss of generality, we may assume that
x=0
. Since
h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}=h_{A,B}
on
\partial(\Omega\setminus(A\cup B))
, we can use
Eq.

2.4
to represent
h_{A,B}
as follows

(3.1)

\displaystyle h_{A,B}(z)=\varepsilon e^{F(z)/\varepsilon}\int_{\partial(\Omega\setminus(A\cup B))}h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}\nabla G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y).

Now by Green’s second identity
Eq.

2.3
in
\Omega\setminus(A\cup B\cup B_{r})
and
Eq.

3.1
we see that, for
z\in\Omega
,

(3.2)

h_{A,B}(z)=h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}(z)\\
-\varepsilon e^{F(z)/\varepsilon}\int_{\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)\nabla h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}(y)\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y),

where
n
is the inward unit normal of
B_{r}
.
First note that by
Eq.

2.3
we can identify the equilibrium measure as

\displaystyle\mu_{B\cup B_{r},A}=-\varepsilon\nabla h_{B\cup B_{r},A}\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\nabla h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}\cdot nd\sigma_{\varepsilon}.

Using that
h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}=1-h_{B\cup B_{r},A}
, together with the above and
Eq.

3.2
, we get for
z\in\overline{B_{r}}
(since
h_{A,B\cup B_{r}}(z)=0
) that

(3.3)

\displaystyle h_{A,B}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)e^{(F(z)-F(y))/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B\cup B_{r},A}(y).

First note that
\mu_{B_{r}\cup B,A}\lvert_{\partial B_{r}}
is an admissible measure for
\operatorname{cap}(B_{r},A;\Omega)
, which follows from the fact that by the comparison principle, the potentials for ordered measures are ordered and the support of
\mu_{B_{r}\cup B,A}\lvert_{\partial B_{r}}
is in
\overline{B_{r}}
.
To bound
h_{A,B}
from above, note that by the balayage representation of capacity (see
Lemma

2.4
) and the above, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B\cup B_{r},A}(y)\leq\operatorname{cap}(B_{r},A;\Omega).

Applying the above to
Eq.

3.3
gives, for
z\in B_{r}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle h_{A,B}(z)\leq\sup_{y\in\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)e^{F(z)/\varepsilon}\operatorname{cap}(B_{r},A;\Omega).

It remains to bound the Green’s function.
For
z\in\overline{B_{r}}
we have by
Eqs.

2.4
,
2.2
and
2.6
that

(3.5)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=h_{B_{r},A\cup B}(z)=\int_{\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(z,y)d\mu_{B_{r},A\cup B}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)e^{(F(z)-F(y))/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{r},A\cup B}(y)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)e^{F(z)/\varepsilon}\operatorname{cap}(B_{r},A\cup B;\Omega)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\partial B_{r}}G_{\Omega\setminus(A\cup B)}(y,z)e^{F(z)/\varepsilon}\operatorname{cap}(B_{r},B;\Omega).

Now putting together
Eqs.

3.4
,
3.5
and
2.9
we are done.
∎

The result below is a version of the rough capacity bound of
[
8
]
, but we give a simplified proof. We will later use a similar argument in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
D\subset B_{R}
be a smooth closed set. Let
x\in B_{R}\setminus D
be such that
B_{4\rho}(x)\subset B_{R}\setminus D
, for
\rho\leq\varepsilon
. Then there exists constants
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
C>1
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\varepsilon^{q_{1}}\rho^{n-1}e^{-F(x;D)/\varepsilon}\leq\operatorname{cap}(B_{\rho}(x),D)\leq C\varepsilon\rho^{q_{2}}e^{-F(x;D)/\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
F(B_{\rho};D)=0
, since the quantities can always be scaled back.
Consider
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(B_{\rho}(x),D;B_{R})
(i.e. a curve connecting
B_{\rho}(x)
and
D
inside
\Omega
) such that
\sup_{t}F(\gamma(t))\leq C\varepsilon
and let
u(z)=h_{D,B_{\rho}(x)}(z)
. We first note by
Lemma

2.4
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{cap}(B_{\rho}(x),D)=\varepsilon\int|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dy.

Fix an
n-1
dimensional disk
D_{\rho}
of radius
\rho
. Then by Cauchy-Schwarz

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dy\geq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{D_{\rho}}\left|\left\langle\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{|\dot{\gamma}|},\nabla u(\gamma(t)+z)\right\rangle\right|^{2}|\dot{\gamma}|d\sigma_{\varepsilon}(z)dt.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus and Cauchy-Schwarz, we have for a fixed point
z\in D_{\rho}
that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=u(\gamma(1))-u(\gamma(0))=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}u(\gamma(t)+z)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}u(\gamma(t)+z)\frac{\sqrt{|\dot{\gamma}|}}{\sqrt{|\dot{\gamma}|}}e^{-F(\gamma(t)+z)/(2\varepsilon)}e^{F(\gamma(t)+z)/(2\varepsilon)}dt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\frac{d}{dt}u(\gamma(t)+z)|^{2}\frac{1}{|\dot{\gamma}|}e^{-F(\gamma(t)+z)/\varepsilon}dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int|\dot{\gamma}|e^{F(\gamma(t)+z)/\varepsilon}dt\right)^{1/2}.

From the above we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dy

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{D_{\rho}}\left|\left\langle\frac{\dot{\gamma}}{|\dot{\gamma}|},\nabla u(\gamma(t)+z)\right\rangle\right|^{2}|\dot{\gamma}|d\sigma_{\varepsilon}(z)dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{D_{\rho}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}|e^{F(\gamma(t)+z)/\varepsilon}dt\right)^{-1}d\sigma_{\varepsilon}(z).

Now since
F
is Lipschitz in
B_{R}
and
F(B_{\rho};D)=0,
we know that there exists a constant
C(\gamma)
such that, for
z\in D_{\rho}
and
\rho<2\varepsilon
,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}|e^{F(\gamma(t)+z)/\varepsilon}dt\leq C(\gamma).

In the above the constant
C
depends on the length of
\gamma
, which can be assumed to be bounded. To see this, take an
\epsilon
neighborhood of
\gamma
,
E_{\varepsilon}
and consider a set of balls
\bigcup_{i}B_{\varepsilon}(y_{i})\supset E_{\varepsilon}
such that
\bigcup_{i}B_{\varepsilon}(y_{i})\subset E_{C_{1}\varepsilon}
(for some large
C_{1}
), the maximal number of such balls needed is
C_{1}R^{n}/\epsilon^{n}
. If we construct a piecewise linear curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
connecting the center of each ball in the covering, this curve will be inside
E_{C_{1}\varepsilon}
and its length will be bounded by
2C_{1}R^{n}/\varepsilon^{n-1}
.
This newly constructed curve can be mollified to achieve a smooth curve without increasing the length by more than a factor. From the above and the Lipschitz continuity of
F
it is clear that
\sup_{t}F(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))\leq C\varepsilon
, and as such we can replace
\gamma
with
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
in the above and get from
Eq.

3.6
that there is a constant
C>1
depending only on the data such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}|e^{F(\gamma(t)+z)/\varepsilon}dt\leq\varepsilon^{1-n}C.

This implies that for a new constant
C
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dy\geq C\varepsilon^{n-1}\rho^{n-1},

which completes the proof of the lower bound after rescaling our potential
F
.

To prove the upper bound we have two possible cases:
In the case when
F(x;D)=F(x)
we can take a cutoff function
\chi_{B_{\rho}(x)}\leq\phi\leq\chi_{B_{2\rho}(x)}
where
|\nabla\phi|\leq C/\rho
as a competitor in the variational formulation of capacity
Eq.

2.5
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dx=\int_{B_{2\rho}(x)}|\nabla\phi|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dx\leq C\rho^{n-2}.

In the case where
F(x;D)>F(x)
, consider the set
\hat{D}=\{z\in B_{R}:F(z)\leq F(x;D)\}
and let
\hat{D}_{1}
be the component that intersects
D
. We set
\widetilde{D}=(\hat{D}_{1}\cup D)\setminus B_{4\rho}(x)
. By the Lipschitz continuity, we know that
\inf_{\widetilde{D}}F>-C\rho
. We take
\chi_{\widetilde{D}+B_{\rho}}\leq\phi\leq\chi_{\widetilde{D}+B_{2\rho}}
, where
|\nabla\phi|\leq C/\rho
, and get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{(\widetilde{D}+B_{2\rho})\setminus\widetilde{D}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}dx

\displaystyle\leq C|(\widetilde{D}+B_{2\rho})\setminus\widetilde{D}|.

Again, the upper bound follows from rescaling the potential
F
as in the case of the lower bound. This completes the whole proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A,B\subset B_{R}
be smooth disjoint sets, and let
x\in B_{R}
be such that
B_{\varepsilon}(x)\subset B_{R}\setminus(A\cup B)
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, if
F(x;B)\leq F(x;A)
, there exists constants
q
and
C
such that

\displaystyle h_{A,B}(x)\leq C\varepsilon^{q}e^{-(F(x;A)-F(x;B))/\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Let
L:=\|\nabla F\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}
. By combining
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
with
R=\varepsilon
,
r=\min\{\varepsilon/L,\varepsilon\}
yields the result.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

By relabeling
A,B
to
B,A
and using the fact that
h_{A,B}=1-h_{B,A}
, we get that if the reverse inequality holds, i.e.
F(x;B)>F(x;A)
, then

\displaystyle 1-h_{A,B}(x)\leq C\varepsilon^{q}e^{-(F(x;B)-F(x;A))/\varepsilon}.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain and let
x_{a},x_{b}\in\Omega\subset B_{R}
be two local minimum points of
F
. Fix
0<\delta<\delta_{1}
and assume that
U_{-\delta/3}=\{x:F(x)<F(x_{a};x_{b})-\delta/3\}\subset\Omega
.
Then there exists an
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
and a constant
C=C>1
such that, for any
0\leq\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for which
B_{3\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{3\varepsilon}(x_{b})\subset U_{-\delta/3}
, the following holds:
If
U_{i}
is a component of
U_{-\delta/3}
, then

\displaystyle\underset{U_{i}}{\operatorname{osc}\,}h_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b})}\leq C\varepsilon.

Proof.

Consider any component
U_{i}
of
U_{-\delta/3}
. We note that we can take
\varepsilon
small enough depending on the Lipschitz constant of
F
in
B_{R}
and
\delta
such that there exists a Lipschitz domain
D_{i}
satisfying

\displaystyle U_{i}+B_{\varepsilon}\subset D_{i}\subset U_{-\delta/4}.

For simplicity, denote
u:=h_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b})}
. Since
D_{i}
is Lipschitz we may use the Poincaré inequality to get

\displaystyle\int_{D_{i}}|u-u_{D_{i}}|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{D_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx.

Using that
D_{i}\subset U_{-\delta/4}
together with
Lemma

2.4

\displaystyle\int_{D_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq e^{\sup_{D_{i}}F/\varepsilon}\int_{D_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-F(x)/\varepsilon}dx

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-1}e^{\sup_{D_{i}}F/\varepsilon}\operatorname{cap}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega).

Using the definition of
U_{-\delta/4}
and
Lemma

3.2
, we get

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{i}}|u-u_{D_{i}}|^{2}dx\leq C\varepsilon^{q_{1}}e^{-\delta/4\varepsilon}

for some constant
q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. Now, for any
x_{0}\in U_{i}
we have by
Lemma

2.7
that

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|u-u_{D_{i}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\strokedint_{B_{2\varepsilon}}|u-u_{D_{i}}|^{2}dx\right)

which together with
Eq.

3.7
gives

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}|u-u_{D_{i}}|^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{q_{1}-n}e^{-\delta/4\varepsilon}.

Since
x_{0}
was an arbitrary point in
U_{i}
we conclude that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
depending only on the data such that if
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the claim holds.
∎

We conclude this subsection with an estimate relating the value of the potential of the equilibrium potential to the ratio of the
L^{1}
norm of the equilibrium potential and the capacity.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain and let
A\Subset\Omega
be a smooth open set and consider
w_{A,\Omega}
as the potential of the equilibrium potential in
\Omega
(see
Definition

2.5
). Let
x\in\Omega
be a critical point of
F
such that
B_{3\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(x)\subset\Omega
. Then there exists a constant
C>1
such that for
\rho<\sqrt{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle w_{A,\Omega}(x)-C\bigg(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\bigg)^{\alpha}(w_{A,\Omega}(x)+1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\displaystyle\int}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}h_{B_{\rho}(x),A}d\mu_{\varepsilon}}{\operatorname{cap}(B_{\rho}(x),A;\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq w_{A,\Omega}(x)+C\bigg(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\bigg)^{\alpha}(w_{A,\Omega}(x)+1).

Proof.

From
Lemma

2.6
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}w_{A,\Omega}(y)e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)=\int_{(A\cup B)^{c}}h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(z)h_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(z)d\mu_{\varepsilon}.

We can estimate the left hand side as

\displaystyle w_{A,\Omega}(x)-\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}}w_{A,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{B_{\rho}(x)}w_{A,\Omega}\leq\frac{\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}w_{A,\Omega}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)}{\int_{\partial B_{\rho}(x)}e^{-F(y)/\varepsilon}d\mu_{B_{\rho}(x),A}(y)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{B_{\rho}(x)}w_{A,\Omega}\leq w_{A,\Omega}(x)+\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}}w_{A,\Omega}.

We want to estimate the oscillation of
w_{A,\Omega}
which we do by considering

\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}w_{A,\Omega}=\operatorname{osc}(w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}}-h_{A,\Omega^{c}})\leq\operatorname{osc}(w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}})+\operatorname{osc}(h_{A,\Omega^{c}}).

Now, the oscillation of
w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}}
and
h_{A,\Omega^{c}}
can estimated by
Lemma

2.11
for
\rho\leq\frac{1}{C}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. That is,

\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}}(w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}})+\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}}(h_{A,\Omega^{c}})\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\alpha}\sup_{B_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}(w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}})

\displaystyle+C\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\alpha}\sup_{B_{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}}(h_{A,\Omega^{c}}).

We apply
Lemma

2.7
to replace the supremums on the right hand side with the value at
x
as both
w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}}
and
h_{A,\Omega^{c}}
satisfies the Harnack inequality (see
Lemma

2.10
). That is,

\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}}(w_{A,\Omega}+h_{A,\Omega^{c}})+\operatorname{osc}_{B_{\rho}}(h_{A,\Omega^{c}})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\alpha}(w_{A,\Omega}(x)+h_{A,\Omega^{c}}(x))

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\alpha}(w_{A,\Omega}(x)+1).

It is easily seen that the above can be extended to
\rho\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}
by applying
Lemma

2.10
again and by enlarging the constant
C
. The proof is completed by using
Eq.

2.6
and collecting the estimates above.
∎

3.2.
Laplace asymptotics for log-concave functions

The assumptions
Eq.

1.4
and
Eq.

1.6
ensure that near critical points the potential
F
is well approximated by convex functions. Therefore we will need basic estimates for log-concave functions, which rather surprisingly we did not find in the literature.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a convex function which has a proper minimum at the origin and
G(0)=0
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(n)>1
such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\leq C|\{G<\varepsilon\}|.

Moreover, there is a constant
C=C(n)
such that for all
\Lambda>1
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\{G<\Lambda\varepsilon\}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\geq(1-\eta(C\Lambda^{-1}))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx,

with
\eta
as in
Eq.

1.15
.

Proof.

By approximation we may assume that
G
is smooth. The lower bound in
Eq.

3.8
follows immediately from

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\geq\int_{\{G<\varepsilon\}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\geq e^{-1}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|.

To prove the upper bound in
Eq.

3.8
we first show that, for all
t>0
, it holds

(3.10)

\displaystyle|\{G<2t\}|\leq 2^{n}|\{G<t\}|.

In order to prove
Eq.

3.10
it is enough to consider only the case
t=1
(the general case follows by considering
\widetilde{G}=G/t
). Denote
E_{1}=\{G<1\}
and
E_{2}=\{G<2\}
. Hence our goal is to show

\displaystyle E_{2}\subset 2E_{1}=\{2x:x\in E_{1}\}.

Fix
\hat{x}\in\partial E_{1}
and define
g(t)=G(t\hat{x})
for
t\geq 0
. By our assumptions,
g(t)
is a smooth convex function satisfying
g(0)=0
and
g(1)=1
. As such, both
g
,
g^{\prime}
are increasing functions from which we can conclude that
g^{\prime}(1)\geq 1
.
Now, by the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle g(2)-g(1)=\int_{1}^{2}g^{\prime}(t)\,dt\geq 1

which gives
g(2)\geq 2
. This means that for all
\hat{x}\in\partial E_{1}
we have
G(2\hat{x})\geq 2
. That is, we have
E_{2}\subset 2E_{1}
. Thus

\displaystyle|E_{2}|\leq|2E_{1}|\leq 2^{n}|E_{1}|

and
Eq.

3.10
follows. Iterating
Eq.

3.10
gives

\displaystyle|\{G<2^{j}\varepsilon\}|\leq 2^{jn}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|

and hence

(3.11)

\displaystyle|\{G<\varrho\varepsilon\}|\leq(2\varrho)^{n}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|

for all
\varrho\geq 1
.
We conclude the proof of the upper bound in
Eq.

3.8
by using
Eq.

3.11
as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{\{j\varepsilon\leq G<(j+1)\varepsilon\}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\{j\varepsilon\leq G<(j+1)\varepsilon\}|e^{-j}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-j}(j+1)^{n}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|\leq C(n)|\{G<\varepsilon\}|.

It remains to prove
Eq.

3.9
. Fix
\Lambda>1
. Then, for every
x\in\{G\geq\Lambda\varepsilon\}
, it holds

(3.12)

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{G(x)}{\varepsilon}}=e^{-\frac{\Lambda G(x)}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}=\left(e^{-\frac{G(x)}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}\right)^{\Lambda}=\left(e^{-\frac{G(x)}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}\right)^{\Lambda-1}e^{-\frac{G(x)}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}\leq e^{-\Lambda+1}e^{-\frac{G(x)}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}.

Therefore we have, by
Eqs.

3.12
,
3.8
and
3.11
,

(3.13)

\begin{split}\int_{\{G\geq\Lambda\varepsilon\}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx&\leq e^{-\Lambda+1}\int_{\{G\geq\Lambda\varepsilon\}}e^{-\frac{G}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}\,dx\leq e^{-\Lambda+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\Lambda\varepsilon}}\,dx\\
&\leq Ce^{-\Lambda}|\{G<\Lambda\varepsilon\}|\\
&\leq Ce^{-\Lambda}\Lambda^{n}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|\\
&\leq Ce^{-\Lambda}\Lambda^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\end{split}

and the inequality
Eq.

3.9
follows by using
Eq.

1.15
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function which has a proper global minimum at the origin and
G(0)=0
. Furthermore, assume there is a constant
C_{0}
such that, for all
a>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

(3.14)

\displaystyle\int_{G>a}e^{-G/\varepsilon}dx<C_{0}e^{-a/\varepsilon}.

If there is a level
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
G
is convex on the component of
\{G(x)<\varepsilon_{0}\}
that contains
0
, then there is an
\varepsilon_{1}(n,|\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}|)<\varepsilon_{0}
and a constant
C=C(C_{0},n)>1
such that, for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle C^{-1}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\leq C|\{G<\varepsilon\}|.

Proof.

Since
G
is convex in the level set
\{G<\varepsilon_{0}\}
, we know that the level set
\{G\leq\varepsilon_{0}/2\}
is convex and as such we can extend the function
G
outside that level set to a globally convex function. This allows us to apply
Lemma

3.7
and obtain

(3.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|\leq\int_{\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}}e^{-\frac{G}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\leq C|\{G<\varepsilon\}|.

Now, split the integral as

\displaystyle\int e^{-G/\varepsilon}dx=\int_{G\leq\varepsilon_{0}/2}e^{-G/\varepsilon}dx+\int_{G>\varepsilon_{0}/2}e^{-G/\varepsilon}dx.

From
Eq.

3.15
it follows that it suffices to bound the second integral on the right hand side.
Using
Eq.

3.14
for
a=\varepsilon_{0}/2
we get

\displaystyle\int_{G>\varepsilon_{0}/2}e^{-G/\varepsilon}dx\leq C_{0}e^{-(\varepsilon_{0}/2)/\varepsilon}.

Since
G
is convex in the level set
\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}
, which is again convex, it follows that we can construct a conical function
\widetilde{G}
as follows: For any
\hat{x}\in\partial\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}
define
\widetilde{G}(t\hat{x}/\|\hat{x}\|)=t\varepsilon_{0}/2
. The level sets of
\widetilde{G}
satisfy, for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}/2
,

\displaystyle|\{\widetilde{G}<\varepsilon\}|\leq|\{G<\varepsilon\}|.

However,

\displaystyle|\{\widetilde{G}<\epsilon\}|=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}/2}\right)^{n}|\{\widetilde{G}<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}|=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}/2}\right)^{n}|\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}|.

Now, we can choose
\varepsilon_{1}(n,C_{0},|\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}|)<\varepsilon_{0}/2
such that for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
we have

\displaystyle e^{-(\varepsilon_{0}/2)/\varepsilon}\leq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{n}|\{G<\varepsilon_{0}/2\}|.

This means that for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
we also have

\displaystyle\int_{G>\varepsilon_{0}/2}e^{-G/\varepsilon}dx\leq C_{0}|\{G<\varepsilon\}|

which together with
Eq.

3.15
completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this section with the following technical lemma which is useful when we study the potential near critical points.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume

(4.14)

\begin{split}\int_{\hat{O}}|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx&\geq\frac{(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)}\int_{\{G<\delta/100\}}e^{-\frac{G(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}e^{-\frac{\omega(G(x^{\prime}))}{\varepsilon}}\,dx^{\prime}\\
&\geq\frac{(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-\frac{G(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}\,dx^{\prime}\\
&\geq(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)}.\end{split}

Therefore, by repeating the argument for every saddle
z_{i}\in Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}
and using the fact that the bridges
O_{z_{i},\delta}
are disjoint, we obtain after scaling back the potential

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\geq(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)}{d_{\varepsilon}(B_{\varepsilon}(x_{a}),B_{\varepsilon}(x_{b});\Omega)}e^{\frac{F(z_{i})}{\varepsilon}}.\end{split}

This yields the lower bound when the saddle points are parallel.

For the upper bound, we only give a sketch of the argument as it is fairly straightforward. The idea is to contruct a competitor
h
in the variational characterization of the capacity, see
Eq.

2.5
. Let us first define
h
in the set
U_{\delta/3}=\{F<F(x_{a};x_{b})+\delta/3\}
.
Since the saddle points
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
are parallel, it follows that the points
x_{a}
and
x_{b}
lie in different components of the set

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}=U_{\delta/3}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}O_{z_{i},\delta},

where
O_{z_{i},\delta}
is defined in
Eq.

1.8
. Denote the components of
\widetilde{U}
containing
x_{a}
and
x_{b}
by
\widetilde{U}_{a}
and
\widetilde{U}_{b}
, respectively. We define first

\displaystyle h=1\,\text{ in }\,\widetilde{U}_{a}\quad\text{and}\quad h=0\,\text{ in }\,\widetilde{U}_{b}.

Let us next fix a saddle point
z_{i}\in Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}
. As before, we may again assume that

\displaystyle z_{i}=0,\quad F(0)=0

and

\displaystyle O_{\delta}:=O_{0,\delta}=\{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}:g(x_{1})<\delta\}\times\{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:G(x^{\prime})<\delta\}.

Moreover, we may assume that

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{a}\cap\partial O_{\delta}\subset\{x_{1}>0\}\quad\text{ and}\quad\widetilde{U}_{b}\cap\partial O_{\delta}\subset\{x_{1}<0\}.

Let
c_{-}<0<c_{+}
be numbers such that
g(c_{-})=g(c_{+})=\delta/100
. We define
h(x)=\varphi(x_{1})
in
O_{\delta}
such that the function
\varphi:[c_{-},c_{+}]\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution of the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(\varphi^{\prime}(s)e^{\frac{g(s)}{\varepsilon}}\right)=0\quad\text{in }\,(c_{-},c_{+})

with boundary values
\varphi(c_{-})=0
and
\varphi(c_{+})=1
. We extend
\varphi
into
\mathbb{R}
by setting
\varphi(s)=0
for
s\leq c_{-}
and
\varphi(s)=1
for
s\geq c_{+}
. It follows that for the function
h
we have, by construction,
Lemma

3.9
, and an argument similar to the one leading to
Eq.

4.12
, that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{O_{\delta}}&|\nabla h|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\\
&\leq\int_{\{g<\delta\}}|\varphi^{\prime}(x_{1})|^{2}e^{\frac{g(x_{1})}{\varepsilon}}e^{\frac{\omega(g(x_{1}))}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}\int_{\{G<\delta\}}e^{-\frac{G(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}e^{\frac{\omega(G(x^{\prime}))}{\varepsilon}}\,dx^{\prime}\\
&\leq(1+\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{g(x_{1})}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}\right)^{-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-\frac{G(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}\,dx^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

By repeating the construction for every saddle point
z_{i}\in Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}
, we obtain a function which is defined in
U_{\delta/3}
. We denote this function by
h:U_{\delta/3}\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that now for
h
the estimate
Eq.

4.14
is optimal. Moreover,
h
is Lipschitz continuous. We extend
h
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
without increasing the Lipschitz constant

, e.g., by defining

\displaystyle h(x)=\sup_{y\in U_{\delta/3}}\big(h(y)-L|x-y|\big)\quad\text{for }\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus U_{\delta/3}.

This finally leads to the upper bound completing the proof of the parallel case, while we leave the final details on the upper bound for the reader.

Series case:

Assume that the saddle points
Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
are in series, see
Fig.

2
. We use the ordering as in
Eq.

1.11
and denote the points
x_{i}
as in
Eq.

1.12
. We also fix the islands,
U_{x_{i-1}}
and
U_{x_{i}}
(components of
\{F<F(x_{a};x_{b})-\delta/3\}
), which are connected by the bridge
O_{z_{i},\delta}
. Again we may assume that

z_{i}=0
,
F(0)=0
and that

\displaystyle O_{\delta}=O_{z_{i},\delta}=\{x_{1}:g(x_{1})<\delta\}\times\{x^{\prime}:G(x_{1})<\delta\}.

By
Lemma

3.5
we have

\text{osc}_{U_{x_{i-1}}}(h_{A,B})+\text{osc}_{U_{x_{i}}}(h_{A,B})\leq C\varepsilon
. Therefore there are numbers
c_{i-1},c_{i}
such that

\displaystyle h_{A,B}\geq c_{i-1}-C\varepsilon\,\,\text{in }\,U_{x_{i-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{A,B}\leq c_{i}+C\varepsilon\,\,\text{in }\,U_{x_{i}}.

Then, using the fundamental theorem of calculus as in
Eq.

4.12
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}|c_{i-1}-c_{i}|-2C\varepsilon\leq\left(\int_{\{g<\delta\}}|\nabla h_{A,B}(x)|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\{g<\delta\}}e^{\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{split}

for

\displaystyle(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in\{g<\delta\}\times\{G<\delta/100\}.

Moreover, arguing as in
Eq.

4.13
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\{g<\delta\}}e^{\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}\leq(1+\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))e^{\frac{G(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}e^{\frac{\omega(G(x^{\prime}))}{\varepsilon}}d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i}).

These together imply

\displaystyle\int_{\{g<\delta\}}|\nabla h_{A,B}(x)|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx_{1}\geq\frac{(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))(c_{i-1}-c_{i})^{2}}{d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}e^{-\frac{G(x^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}}e^{-\frac{\omega(G(x^{\prime}))}{\varepsilon}}.

By integrating over
x^{\prime}\in\{G<\delta/100\}
we have, by Fubini’s theorem,
Lemma

3.9
, and
Proposition

4.2
, that

\displaystyle\int_{O_{\delta}}|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\geq(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))(c_{i-1}-c_{i})^{2}\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}{d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}.

By repeating the argument for every saddle
z_{i}\in Z_{x_{a},x_{b}}
and using the fact that the sets
O_{z_{i},\delta}
are disjoint we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla h_{A,B}|^{2}e^{-\frac{F(x)}{\varepsilon}}\,dx\geq(1-\hat{\eta}(C\varepsilon))\sum_{i=1}^{N}(c_{i-1}-c_{i})^{2}\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}{d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}.

Recall that the numbers
c_{i}
are the approximate values of
h_{A,B}
in the components
U_{x_{i}}
. Therefore we may choose them such that
1=c_{0}
and
c_{N}=0
.
By denoting
y_{i}=c_{i-1}-c_{i}
and
a_{i}=\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}{d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}
we may write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}(c_{i-1}-c_{i})^{2}\frac{V_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}{d_{\varepsilon}(x_{i-1},x_{i})}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}y_{i}^{2},

where we have a constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}=1
. By a standard optimization argument (using Lagrange multipliers) we get that under such a constraint it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}y_{i}^{2}\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{a_{i}}\right)^{-1}.

This yields the lower bound in the case when the saddle points are in series. The upper bound on the other hand follows from a similar argument than in the parallel case, and we leave the details for the reader. This completes the proof in the series case, and hence the whole proof.

∎

Proof of

First we will prove a localization estimate for exponential integrals. Consider a set

\displaystyle\int_{O}e^{-f(x)/\varepsilon}dx\leq Ce^{-l/\varepsilon}|\{f<\varepsilon\}\cap O|.

We will first prove

4.3

In order to prove

4.16
, we begin by rescaling such that
l=0
. Then we extend
f

+\infty

\displaystyle\int_{\{\hat{f}>a\}}e^{-\hat{f}(x)/\varepsilon}dx\leq ce^{-a/\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\int_{\{f>a\}\cap O}e^{-f(x)/\varepsilon}dx\leq ce^{-a/\varepsilon}.

This now follows from
Lemma

3.8
by using
|O|<\infty
and observing that
\hat{f}
satisfies the assumptions of
Lemma

3.8
. Hence we observe
Eq.

4.16
.

Consider now the set

\displaystyle U_{-\delta_{2}/3}\equiv\{y:F(y)\leq F(G_{k+1};S_{k})-\delta_{2}/3\}

and let
U_{i}
be the component of
U_{-\delta_{2}/3}
containing
x_{i}
. We split

\displaystyle\int h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx=\int_{U_{-\delta_{2}/3}^{c}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx+\int_{U_{-\delta_{2}/3}\setminus S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx,

where complement is understood with respect to the domain
\Omega

By assumptions (
1.4
) and (
1.6
) on

(4.17)

F(G_{k+1};S_{k})\geq F(G_{k+1})+\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{2}.

Also by the quadratic growth (

\displaystyle\int_{U_{-\delta_{2}/3}^{c}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx\leq Ce^{-(F(G_{k+1};S_{k})-\delta_{2}/3)/\varepsilon}\leq Ce^{-\frac{\delta_{2}}{3\varepsilon}}e^{-F(G_{k+1})/\varepsilon},

which shows that the first integral is neglible in the final estimate. For the second integral we further split

\displaystyle\int_{U_{-\delta_{2}/3}\setminus S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx=\sum_{i}\int_{U_{i}\setminus S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx.

We will now consider all the different components
U_{i}
depending on what minimas they contain. We start with the components
U_{i}
that do not intersect

. Then all local minimas in
U_{i}
are larger than

, and hence from

4.16

such that

\displaystyle\int_{U_{i}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx\leq Ce^{-F(G_{k+2})/\varepsilon}\leq Ce^{-\delta_{2}/\varepsilon}e^{-F(G_{k+1})/\varepsilon},

where the last inequality follows from (
1.19
). This shows that also this term is neglible. Consider next the component
U_{i}
that intersects

but do not intersect

. In this case, by

4.16
and

3.7
and
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\sum_{x\in G_{k+1}\cap U_{i}}|O_{x,\varepsilon}|\leq e^{\frac{F(G_{k+1})}{\varepsilon}}\int_{U_{i}}h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}e^{-F/\varepsilon}dx\leq C\sum_{x\in G_{k+1}\cap U_{i}}|O_{x,\varepsilon}|

U_{i}
such that

\displaystyle h_{G_{k+1}^{\varepsilon},S_{k}^{\varepsilon}}\leq C\varepsilon^{q}e^{-(F(y;G_{k+1})-F(y;S_{k}))/\varepsilon}.
