. 1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the class of functions
f
which are holomorphic in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\;\mbox{for}\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

Then
\mathcal{H}
is a locally convex topological vector space endowed with the topology of uniform convergence over compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the class of function
f\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)=1
. That is, the function
f
takes the following form

(1.2)

\displaystyle f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\;\mbox{for}\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the subclass of all functions in
\mathcal{A}
which are univalent. For a general theory of univalent functions, we refer the classical books
[
8
,
11
]
.

Let

(1.3)

\displaystyle F_{f}(z):=\log\dfrac{f(z)}{z}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}(f)z^{n},\;\;z\in\mathbb{D},\;\;\log 1:=0,

be a logarithmic function associated with
f\in\mathcal{S}
. The numbers
\gamma_{n}:=\gamma_{n}(f)
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) are called the logarithmic coefficients of
f
. Although the logarithmic coefficients
\gamma_{n}
play a critical role in the theory of univalent functions, it appears that only a limited number of sharp bounds have been established for them. As is well known, the logarithmic coefficients play a crucial role in Milin’s conjecture (
[
18
]
, see also
[
8
, p.155]
).
Milin conjectured that for
f\in\mathcal{S}
and
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(k|\gamma_{k}|^{2}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\leq 0

where the equality holds if, and only if,
f
is a rotation of the Koebe function. De Branges
[
6
]
proved Milin conjecture which confirmed the famous Bieberbach conjecture. On the other hand, one of reasons for more attention has been given to the logarithmic coefficients is that the sharp bound for the class
\mathcal{S}
is known only for
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, namely

\displaystyle|\gamma_{1}|\leq 1,\;\;|\gamma_{2}|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{e}=0.635\ldots

It is known that for the Koebe function
f(z)=z/(1-z)^{2}
, the logarithmic coefficients are
\gamma_{n}=1/n
, for each positive integer
n
. Since the Koebe function appears as an extremal function in many problems of geometric theory of analytic functions, one could expect that
\gamma_{n}=1/n
holds for functions in
\mathcal{S}
. But this is not true in general, even in order of magnitude. Finding the sharp bounds of
\gamma_{n}
, with
n\geq 3
, for the class
\mathcal{S}
remains an unsolved problem. Estimating the modulus of logarithmic coefficients for
f\in\mathcal{S}
and various sub-classes has been considered recently by several authors. For in-depth information on the topic, we suggest referring to the articles
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
25
,
20
,
22
,
10
]
, and exploring the references provided therein.

A function
f
is said to be starlike in
\mathbb{C}
if it maps the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
conformally onto a region that is starlike with respect to the origin. The class of functions that are starlike with respect to symmetric points, which is denoted as
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
, was introduced by Sakaguchi in
1959

[
23
]
. A function
f
that belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
is characterized by the following conditions:

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)-f(-z)}\right)>0,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

A function
f
is said to be convex in
\mathbb{C}
if it maps the open unit disk
|z|<1
conformally onto a region that is convex. We consider another class, which is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{S}
, that is, a function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be convex with respect to symmetric points if, and only if,

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{(zf^{\prime}(z))^{\prime}}{(f(z)-f(-z))^{\prime}}\right)>0,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

The functions that are members of
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
are identified as close-to-convex, and consequently, they are univalent. The class of functions that are starlike with respect to symmetric points also includes the classes of convex functions and odd starlike functions with respect to the origin.

The study of coefficients problems in geometric function theory has a rich history. The challenging and interesting task lies in determining sharp bounds for various inequalities related to functions within different classes of univalent functions. Over the past several years, a significant amount of research in geometric function theory has been devoted to establishing sharp bounds of Hankel or Toeplitz determinants for different classes of functions. However, upon in-depth analysis of these findings, it becomes evident that there is relatively less study focused on exploring the aforementioned determinants not only for logarithmic coefficients but also for the inverse functions of these classes. This study is dedicated to providing the sharp bound for the second Hankel and Toeplitz determinants, whose entries are the logarithmic coefficients of inverse functions.

Let
F
be the inverse function of
f\in\mathcal{S}
defined in a neighborhood of the origin with the Taylor series expansion

(1.4)

\displaystyle F(w):=f^{-1}(w)=w+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}A_{n}w^{n},

where we may choose
|w|<1/4
, as we know that the famous Koebe’s
1/4
-theorem ensures that, for each univalent function
f
defined in
\mathbb{D}
, it inverse
f^{-1}
exists at least on a disc of radius
1/4
. Using a variational method, L
\ddot{\mbox{o}}
wner
[
17
]
obtained the sharp estimate
|A_{n}|\leq K_{n}\;\mbox{for each}\;n,
where
K_{n}=(2n)!/(n!(n+1)!)
and
K(w)=w+K_{2}w^{2}+K_{3}w^{3}+\cdots
is the inverse of the Köebe function. There has been a good deal of interest in determining the behavior of the inverse coefficients of
f
given in (
1.2
) when the corresponding function
f
is restricted to some proper geometric subclasses of
\mathcal{S}
.

Let
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
be a function in class
\mathcal{S}
. Science
f(f^{-1}(w))=w
and using (
1.4
) we obtain

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}A_{2}=-a_{2},\\
A_{3}=-a_{3}+2a^{2}_{2},\\
A_{4}=-a_{4}+5a_{2}a_{3}-5a^{3}_{2},\\
A_{5}=-a_{5}+6a_{4}a_{2}-21a_{3}a^{2}_{2}+3a^{2}_{3}+14a^{4}_{2}.\end{cases}

The notation of the logarithmic coefficient of inverse of
f
was introduced by Ponnusamy
et al.
(see
[
19
]
). As with
f
, the logarithmic inverse coefficients
\Gamma_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of
F
are defined by the equation

(1.6)

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{F(w)}{w}\right)=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{n}(F)w^{n}\;\mbox{for}\;|w|<1/4.

The author’s obtained the sharp bound for the logarithmic inverse coefficients for the class
\mathcal{S}
. In fact, Ponnusamy
et al.

[
19
]
have established for
f\in\mathcal{S}
that

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{n}(F)|\leq\frac{1}{2n}\binom{2n}{n}

and showed that the equality holds only for the Köebe function or its rotations. Differentiating (
1.6
) and using (
1.5
), we obtain

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Gamma_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}a_{2},\\
\Gamma_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{3}-\frac{3}{2}a^{2}_{2}\right),\\
\Gamma_{3}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{4}-4a_{2}a_{3}+\frac{10}{3}a^{3}_{2}\right),\\
\Gamma_{4}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{5}-5a_{4}a_{2}+15a_{3}a^{2}_{2}-\frac{5}{2}a^{2}_{3}-\frac{35}{4}a^{4}_{2}\right).\end{cases}

In
2016
, Ye and Lim
[
27
]
proved that any
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{C}
generically can be written as the product of some Toeplitz matrices or Hankel matrices. Hankel matrices and determinants are essential components in various mathematical disciplines, offering a wide range of applications
[
27
]
. Likewise, in both pure and applied mathematics, Toeplitz matrices and Toeplitz determinants play a role
[
26
]
. They occur in analysis, integral equations, image processing, signal processing, quantum mechanics and among other areas. For more applications, we refer to the survey article
[
27
]
and references therein. Toeplitz matrices contain same entries along their diagonals. This study is dedicated to providing the sharp bound for the second Hankel and Toeplitz determinants, whose entries are the logarithmic coefficients.

The Hankel and Toeplitz determinants
H_{q,n}(f)
and
T_{q,n}(f)
of Taylor’s coefficients of functions
f\in\mathcal{A}
represented by (
1.1
) is defined for
q,n\in\mathbb{N}
as follows:

\displaystyle H_{q,n}(f):=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\cdots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n+2}&\cdots&a_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q}&\cdots&a_{n+2(q-1)}\end{vmatrix},\;T_{q,n}(f):=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\cdots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n}&\cdots&a_{n+q-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q-2}&\cdots&a_{n}\end{vmatrix}.

Recently, Kowalczyk and Lecko
[
12
]
proposed a Hankel determinant whose elements are the logarithmic coefficients of
f\in\mathcal{S}
, realizing the extensive use of these coefficients. Inspired by these ideas, we introduce the investigation of the Hankel determinant
H_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
and Toeplitz determinant
T_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
, wherein the elements are logarithmic coefficients of inverse functions of
f^{-1}\in\mathcal{S}
. The determinant
H_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle H_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)=\begin{vmatrix}\Gamma_{n}&\Gamma_{n+1}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+q-1}\\
\Gamma_{n+1}&\Gamma_{n+2}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\Gamma_{n+q-1}&\Gamma_{n+q}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+2(q-1)}\end{vmatrix},

and, the determinant
T_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle T_{q,n}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)=\begin{vmatrix}\Gamma_{n}&\Gamma_{n+1}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+q-1}\\
\Gamma_{n+1}&\Gamma_{n}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n+q-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\Gamma_{n+q-1}&\Gamma_{n+q-2}&\cdots&\Gamma_{n}\end{vmatrix}.

Some progress has been made in this direction, especially concerning Toeplitz determinants for univalent functions with specific symmetries or in particular special domains. In 2021, Zaprawa

obtained the sharp bounds of the initial logarithmic coefficients

However, the primary aim of this paper is to find the sharp bound of

(1.8)

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{3}-\Gamma^{2}_{2}=\frac{1}{48}\left(13a^{4}_{2}-12a^{2}_{2}a_{3}-12a^{2}_{3}+12a_{2}a_{4}\right),

2.
Main results for classes
\mathcal{K}_{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
, remarks and examples

Suppose
\mathcal{B}_{0}
be the class of Schwarz function
i.e
analytic function
w:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that
w(0)=0
. A function
w\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
can be written as a power series

\displaystyle w(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}.

For two functions
f
and
g
which are analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, we say that the function
f
is subordinate to
g
in
\mathbb{D}
and written as

f(z)\prec g(z)
there exists a Schwarz function
w\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
such that
f(z)=g(w(z))
,
z\in\mathbb{D}
. To prove our results, the following lemma for Schwarz functions will play a key role.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
9
]

Let
w(z)=c_{1}z+c_{2}z^{2}+c_{3}z^{3}+\ldots
be a Schwarz function. Then

\displaystyle|c_{1}|\leq 1,\;\;|c_{2}|\leq 1-|c_{1}|^{2},\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;|c_{3}|\leq 1-|c_{1}|^{2}-\dfrac{|c_{2}|^{2}}{1+|c_{1}|}.

2.1.
Sharp bound of
|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|
for
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
:

We obtain the following sharp bound for
H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
for functions in the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
. Then

\displaystyle|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\dfrac{1}{4}.

The inequality is sharp with the extremal function

\displaystyle h_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z^{2}}=z+z^{3}+z^{5}+\cdots,\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

An example is presented below to demonstrate that the strict inequality in Theorem
2.1
remains valid.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the function

\displaystyle h_{2}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}=z+z^{2}+z^{3}+\cdots,\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{zh^{\prime}_{2}(z)}{h_{2}(z)-h_{2}(-z)}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{1+z}{1-z}\right)>0.

Hence, the function
h_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
. We can easily compute and find that

\displaystyle|H_{2,1}(F_{h^{-1}_{2}}/2)|=\frac{1}{12}<\frac{1}{4}.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
be of the form
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
,
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Then by the definition of subordination there exits a Schwarz function
w(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\dfrac{2zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)-f(-z)}=\dfrac{1+w(z)}{1-w(z)}.

By equating the coefficients on each side of (
2.1
), we get

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{2}=c_{1},\\
a_{3}=c_{2}+c^{2}_{1},\vskip 4.2679pt\\
a_{4}=\dfrac{1}{2}(c_{3}+3c_{1}c_{2}+2c^{3}_{1}).\end{cases}

In view of (
1.8
) and (
2.2
), a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{48}\left(13a^{4}_{2}-12a^{2}_{2}a_{3}-12a^{2}_{3}+12a_{2}a_{4}\right)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{48}\left(c^{4}_{1}-18c^{2}_{1}c_{2}-12c^{2}_{2}+6c_{1}c_{3}\right).

Using the Lemma
2.1
into (
2.1
), we obtain

(2.4)

\displaystyle 48|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq|c_{1}|^{4}+18|c_{1}|^{2}|c_{2}|+12|c_{2}|^{2}+6|c_{1}|\left(1-|c_{1}|^{2}-\dfrac{|c_{2}|^{2}}{1+|c_{1}|}\right).

Suppose that
x=|c_{1}|
and
y=|c_{2}|
. Then it follows from (
2.4
) that

(2.5)

\displaystyle 48|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq M(x,y),

where

\displaystyle M(x,y)

\displaystyle:=x^{4}+18x^{2}y+12y^{2}+6x\left(1-x^{2}-\dfrac{y^{2}}{1+x}\right).

In view of Lemma
2.1
, the region of variability of a pair
(x,y)
coincides with the set

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Omega=\{(x,y):0\leq x\leq 1,0\leq y\leq 1-x^{2}\}.

The goal is to establish the maximum value of
M(x,y)
in the region
\Omega
.
Therefore, the critical point of
M(x,y)
satisfies the conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M}{\partial x}=4x^{3}-18x^{2}+36xy+6-\frac{6y^{2}}{(1+x)}+\frac{6xy^{2}}{(1+x)^{2}}=0

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=18x^{2}+24y-\frac{12xy}{(1+x)}=0.

There are no solutions of
M(x,y)
inside the interior of
\Omega
, hence it is not possible for the function to attain a maximum value within this region. Since
M(x,y)
is a continuous function on a compact set
\Omega
, its maximum value must occur at some point on the boundary of
\Omega
. On the boundary of
\Omega
, a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle M(x,0)=6x-6x^{3}+x^{4}\leq 2.437828...\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq x\leq 1,

\displaystyle M(0,y)=12y^{2}\leq 12\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq y\leq 1

and

\displaystyle M(x,1-x^{2})=12-11x^{4}\leq 12\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq x\leq 1.

Therefore, we see that
\max_{(x,y)\in\Omega}M(x,y)=12
and it fllows from (
2.5
) that

(2.7)

\displaystyle|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\frac{1}{4}.

To prove the equality in (
2.7
) sharp, we consider the function

\displaystyle h_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1-z^{2}}=z+z^{3}+z^{5}+\cdots,\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

It is easy to see that
h_{1}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
and
|H_{2,1}(F_{h^{-1}_{1}}/2)|=1/4
. Hence equality holds in (
2.7
). This completes the proof
∎

2.2.
Sharp bound of
|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|
for

By considering functions from the class

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

\displaystyle|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\dfrac{1}{36}.

The extremal function creates a precise inequality

\displaystyle h_{3}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)=z+\frac{z^{3}}{3}+\frac{z^{5}}{5}\cdots,\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

An example of a function in the class

Example 2.2

Consider the function

\displaystyle h_{4}(z)=-\log(1-z)=z+\frac{1}{2}z^{2}+\frac{1}{3}z^{3}+\frac{1}{4}z^{4}+\cdots,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{(zh^{\prime}_{4}(z))^{\prime}}{(h_{4}(z)-h_{4}(-z))^{\prime}}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{1+z}{1-z}\right)>0.

\displaystyle|H_{2,1}(F_{h^{-1}_{4}}/2)|=\dfrac{11}{576}<\dfrac{1}{36}.

2.2

f\in\mathcal{K}_{S}
be of the form

,

Comparing the coefficients on both side of (

), we obtain

(2.9)

\displaystyle a_{2}=\frac{1}{2}c_{1},a_{3}=\frac{1}{3}(c_{2}+c^{2}_{1}),\;\mbox{and}\;a_{4}=\frac{1}{8}(c_{3}+3c_{1}c_{2}+2c^{3}_{1}).

By utilizing (
1.8
) and (

\displaystyle H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{48}\left(13a^{4}_{2}-12a^{2}_{2}a_{3}-12a^{2}_{3}+12a_{2}a_{4}\right)

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2304}\left(-c^{4}_{1}-68c^{2}_{1}c_{2}-64c^{2}_{2}+36c_{1}c_{3}\right).

Applying the triangle inequality to (
2.2
) and using Lemma

(2.11)

\displaystyle 2304|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|

\displaystyle\leq|c_{1}|^{4}+68|c_{1}|^{2}|c_{2}|+64|c_{2}|^{2}+36|c_{1}|\left(1-|c_{1}|^{2}-\dfrac{|c_{2}|^{2}}{1+|c_{1}|}\right).

Suppose that
x=|c_{1}|
and
y=|c_{2}|
. Then it follows from (
2.11
) that

(2.12)

\displaystyle 2304|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq N(x,y),

where

\displaystyle N(x,y):=x^{4}+68x^{2}y+64y^{2}+36x\left(1-x^{2}-\dfrac{y^{2}}{1+x}\right).

In view of Lemma
2.1
, the region of variability of a pair
(x,y)
coincides with the set
\Omega
as defined in (

). Given this information, we proceed to find the maximum value of

within the region

. Therefore, the critical point of

satisfies the conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}=4x^{3}-108x^{2}+136xy+36-\frac{36y^{2}}{(1+x)}+\frac{36xy^{2}}{(1+x)^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial N}{\partial y}=68x^{2}+128y-\frac{72xy}{(1+x)}.

By applying the analogous reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we establish that the maximum of
N(x,y)
is obtained on the boundary of
\Omega
. Specifically, on the boundary, it can be seen that

\displaystyle N(x,0)=36x-36x^{3}+x^{4}\leq 13.969963...\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq x\leq 1,

\displaystyle N(0,y)=64y^{2}\leq 64\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq y\leq 1

and

\displaystyle N(x,1-x^{2})=64-24x^{2}-39x^{4}\leq 64\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq x\leq 1.

Therefore, it is clear that
\displaystyle\max_{(x,y)\in\Omega}N(x,y)=64
and from (
2.12
), we easily obtain

(2.13)

\displaystyle|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\frac{1}{36}.

In order to show the equality in (
2.13
) sharp, we consider the function

\displaystyle h_{3}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)=z+\frac{z^{3}}{3}+\frac{z^{5}}{5}\cdots,\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

which belongs to the class
\mathcal{K}_{S}
. A simple computation shows that
|H_{2,1}(F_{h^{-1}_{3}}/2)|=1/36
. This shows that the equality holds in (
2.12
). This completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Sharp bound of
|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|
for
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
:

We derive the subsequent sharp bound of
T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
concerning functions within the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
. Then

\displaystyle|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\dfrac{5}{16}.

The inequality is sharp with the extremal function

\displaystyle h_{5}(z)=\dfrac{z}{1-iz}=z+iz^{2}-z^{3}-iz^{4}+z^{5}+\ldots,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

We present a function as an illustrative example to bolster the claim of the strict inequality established in Theorem
2.3
.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the function

\displaystyle h_{6}(z)=\dfrac{z}{1-z}=z+z^{2}+z^{3}+z^{4}+\ldots,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}

which belongs to the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
. A simple computation shows that

\displaystyle|T_{2,1}(F_{h^{-1}_{6}}/2)|=\dfrac{3}{16}<\dfrac{5}{16}.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
be of the form
f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
,
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Then by the definition of subordination there exits a Schwarz function
w(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}
such that

(2.14)

\displaystyle\dfrac{2zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)-f(-z)}=\dfrac{1+w(z)}{1-w(z)}.

Now, comparing the coefficients on both side of (
2.14
), we see that

(2.15)

\displaystyle a_{2}=c_{1}\;\mbox{and}\;a_{3}=c_{2}+c^{2}_{1}.

In view of (
1.9
) and (
2.15
), a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}(-9a^{4}_{2}+4a^{2}_{2}-4a^{2}_{3}+12a^{2}_{2}a_{3})

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}(-c^{4}_{1}+4c^{2}_{1}-4c^{2}_{2}+4c^{2}_{1}c_{2}).

In view of the triangle inequality, the equation (
2.3
) can be written as

(2.17)

\displaystyle 16|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq|c_{1}|^{4}+4|c_{1}|^{2}+4|c_{2}|^{2}+4|c_{1}|^{2}|c_{2}|.

Suppose that
x=|c_{1}|
and
y=|c_{2}|
. Then it follows from (
2.17
) that

(2.18)

\displaystyle 16|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq P(x,y),

where

\displaystyle P(x,y)=x^{4}+4x^{2}+4y^{2}+4x^{2}y.

In view of Lemma
2.1
, the region of variability of a pair
(x,y)
coincides with the set
\Omega
as defined in (
2.6
).
Taking this information into account, we proceed to compute the maximum value of
P(x,y)
within the bounded area
\Omega
. The critical point of
P(x,y)
satisfies the conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}=4x^{3}+8x+8xy,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=8y+4x^{2}.

The function
P(x,y)
cannot have a maximum in the interior of
\Omega
, as it has no solution in the interior of
\Omega
. In fact,
P(x,y)
is continuous on a compact set
\Omega
, the maximum of
P(x,y)
attains boundary of
\Omega
. On the boundary, an easy computation yields that

\displaystyle P(x,0)=x^{4}+4x^{2}\leq 5\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq x\leq 1,

\displaystyle P(0,y)=4y^{2}\leq 4\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq y\leq 1

and

\displaystyle P(x,1-x^{2})=x^{4}+4\leq 5\;\;\mbox{for}\;0\leq x\leq 1.

Therefore, we see that
\displaystyle\max_{(x,y)\in\Omega}P(x,y)=5
and from (
2.18
) we obtain

(2.19)

\displaystyle|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq\dfrac{5}{16}.

To prove the equality in (
2.19
), we consider the function

\displaystyle h_{5}(z)=\dfrac{z}{1-iz}=z+iz^{2}-z^{3}-iz^{4}+z^{5}+\ldots,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}\left(\dfrac{zh_{5}^{\prime}(z)}{h_{5}(z)-h_{5}(-z)}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\mbox{Re}\left(\frac{1+iz}{1-iz}\right)>0,\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

and hence the function
h_{1}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}_{S}
. Moreover, by a simple computation, it can be shown that
|T_{2,1}(F_{h^{-1}_{5}}/2)|=5/16
and hence equality hods in (
2.19
). This completes the proof.
∎

.

An example of a function in the class

3.
Concluding remarks

This article delves into the investigation of the second Hankel and Toeplitz determinants, denoted as
H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
and
T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)
, which are defined in terms of the logarithmic coefficients of inverse functions belonging to the class
\mathcal{S}
of normalized univalent functions. The specific focus lies on the subclasses of starlike functions and convex functions with respect to symmetric points.

Through rigorous analysis, we establish significant results in the form of sharp inequalities for these determinants. Notably, we prove that
|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|
and
|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|
are bounded above by specific constants. Specifically, we demonstrate that
|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq 1/4
,
|H_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq 1/36
,
|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq 5/16
, and
|T_{2,1}(F_{f^{-1}}/2)|\leq 145/2304
. These inequalities provide valuable insights into the behavior of the logarithmic coefficients of inverse functions in the considered classes. Moreover, the strength of our conclusions is enhanced through the use of illustrative examples, which showcase the validity of the strict inequalities established in this study. The incorporation of these examples reinforces the reliability and significance of our findings, supporting the broader implications of our research.

Overall, this study contributes to the understanding of univalent functions and their inverse functions, shedding light on the interplay between their logarithmic coefficients and the associated determinants. The obtained results extend the existing knowledge in the field and may serve as a valuable reference for further investigations in complex analysis and related areas.

It seems that by employing similar methods and using the tools described in the paper, we can investigate the sharp bounds of larger size determinants
