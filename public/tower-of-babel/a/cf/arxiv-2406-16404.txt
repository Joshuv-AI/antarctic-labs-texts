Bijections between Variants of Dyck Paths and Integer Compositions
Manosij Ghosh Dastidar, Michael Wallner

Abstract
We give bijective results between several variants of lattice paths of length 2 ​ n 2n (or 2 ​ n − 2 2n-2 ) and integer compositions of n n , all enumerated by the seemingly innocuous formula 4 n − 1 4^{n-1} . These associations lead us to make new connections between these objects, such as congruence results. Keywords: Integer compositions, lattice paths, Dyck paths, bijections

We explore several links between different variants of integer compositions and generalizations of Dyck paths.
Let us first introduce these objects.
First, an
integer composition
of a nonnegative integer
n

(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})

n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}
.
Note that

n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\dots\geq n_{k}

Second, a

-axis, and never crosses the

All classes of paths we consider will start at the origin and consist of steps
\mathtt{u}
and
\mathtt{d}

Natural classes are
Dyck walks
that have no constraints, i.e., they may end anywhere and go below the
x
-axis,
and
Dyck bridges
(also known as
grand Dyck paths
) that have to end on the
x
-axis but may go below it.

Bijections involving integer compositions

We start with the simple initial bijection, connecting pairs of compositions and Dyck walks.

Proposition 2.1
.

There exists a natural bijection between pairs of compositions of
n
and Dyck walks of length
2n-2
.

Proof.

Let a pair
(A,B)
of two compositions of
n
be given.
First, we convert each composition to a binary sequence:
for each element
k
in a composition, append
k-1
zeros followed by a
1
.
By construction, both of these sequences have to end in
1
.
So we remove these ones and then concatenate the binary sequences, with
A
’s sequence coming first.
Finally, after replacing each
0
by an up step
\mathtt{u}
and each
1
by a down step
\mathtt{d}
the claim follows.
For the reverse direction cut the walk in the middle into two parts, and re-add the ones.
∎

Proposition 2.2

There exists a natural bijection between

Proof.

Now, we describe a map from

First, we create two identical copies.
In the first copy, we remove the labels

If the next part has also a label

Similarly, in the second copy, we remove the labels

Again, if the next part has also a label

Observe that the size of both compositions has not changed.

To prove that this map is in fact a bijection, let us consider an arbitrary pair

of compositions of
n

The key statistic to consider is the

Let

A run is a sequence of maximal length such that

If

0

For the inverse map, we will describe a recursive algorithm, which reduces the sizes of

and

We start with an empty

Depending on the first parts of

Bijections involving Dyck paths

Let us now consider more complicated classes of Dyck paths.
All of them use the concept of a
peak
, which is a consecutive pattern
\mathtt{u}\mathtt{d}
.
The first class we consider are
Dyck paths with a marked peak

Therefore, the number of these paths is equal to the number of peaks in all Dyck paths, whose enumeration is well-known; see, e.g.,
[
4

Theorem 3.1

There is an explicit bijection between Dyck paths with a marked peak of height
h
and Dyck bridges starting with a
\mathtt{d}

Therefore, the number of peaks in all Dyck paths of length

Let

Using this peak, we decompose the path

\mathtt{u}

\displaystyle D_{i}=\mathtt{u}L_{i}\mathtt{d}R_{i}.

Proposition 3.2
.

There is an explicit bijection between
2

Proof.

Recall the classical notion of a Dyck meander, which is defined as the prefix of a Dyck path, i.e., a path that starts at
0

We will use repeatedly that there is an explicit bijection between Dyck bridges

We distinguish four cases.
First, the first and second bridges are non-empty.
The idea is that the change in color corresponds to the last crossing of the
x

For this purpose we transform the second bridge into a meander or negative meander and
attach it to the first bridge such that the attached meander continues on the other side of the
x

We can easily reverse this procedure by cutting at the last crossing of the
x
-axis.
All the other cases will have no crossings. Second, if the first bridge is non-empty and the second one is empty, we transform the first bridge into a meander.
Third, if the first bridge is empty and the second on is non-empty, we transform the second bridge into a negative meander.
Finally, if both bridges are empty, we map them to the empty walk.
∎

4
Conclusion and Outlook

Having established links between the internal structures of Dyck paths and integer compositions, it is only natural to ask whether important theorems from one subject can be transported to the other. When we talk about integer compositions or partitions we are keen to see arithmetic properties in those structures.
In the long version of this work
[
5
]
, we give further bijective links and we show that such arithmetic results also exist in lattice paths.
In particular, we are pleased to note that:

Theorem 4.1

(
[
5
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

Let
D_{r}(n)
be the number of Dyck paths with semi-length
n
and with
exactly

r
peaks for every reached height.
Then
D_{r}(n)\equiv 0\pmod{r+1}
for
n>r
.

In the opposite direction we also want to see if important theorems in integer compositions and partitions can “generate” theorems in the world of lattice paths. In 2020, Kim, Kim, and Lovejoy
[
6
]
observed the phenomenon of
parity bias
in partitions, where they showed that:
if
p_{o}(n)
denotes the number of partitions of
n
with more odd parts than even parts and if
p_{e}(n)
denotes the number of partitions of
n
with more even parts than odd parts, then
p_{o}(n)>p_{e}(n)
.

In a subsequent article we will show that the analogous theorem is true even for integer compositions.
Furthermore, we will demonstrate that even for Dyck paths a similar result holds, when segregating paths with respect to whether they have more peaks at odd or even heights.
