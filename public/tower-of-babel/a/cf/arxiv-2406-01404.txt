Non-existence of a holomorphic embedding of the Sobolev loop space into the projective Hilbert space Thanks: * The second author was partially supported by the R-CDP-24-004-C2EMPI project.
Anakkar M., Ivashkovych S*

Abstract
. The goal of this paper is to understand the properties of meromorphic mappings with values in two model complex Hibert manifolds: projective Hilbert space ℙ ⁡ ( l 2 ) {\mathbb{P}}(l^{2}) and Sobolev loop space of the Riemann sphere L ​ ℙ 1 L{\mathbb{P}}^{1} . It occurs that these properties are quite different. Based on our study we obtain as a corollary that L ​ ℙ 1 L{\mathbb{P}}^{1} is not biholomorphic to a submanifold of ℙ ⁡ ( l 2 ) {\mathbb{P}}(l^{2}) . In other words L ​ ℙ 1 L{\mathbb{P}}^{1} is not a Hilbert projective variety despite of the fact that it is Kähler and meromorphic functions separate points on it. Moreover, we prove that L ​ ℙ 1 L{\mathbb{P}}^{1} doesn’t admit even a non-degenerate meromorphic map to ℙ ⁡ ( l 2 ) {\mathbb{P}}(l^{2}) .

1.1. On the embedding of a loop space into a projective space

By a complex Hilbert manifold we understand a Hausdorff topological space
{\mathcal{X}}

locally homeomorphic to open subsets in a separable Hilbert space
L
,

{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

or
\it{l}^{2}

are holomorphic. The notation

standard Hermitian scalar product
(z,w)=\sum_{k}z_{k}\bar{w}_{k}
and the standard basis

\{e_{1},e_{2},...\}
. By

we denote the ball of radius

centred at
z_{0}
in
L
, by
B(r)
the ball centred at
the origin and by

the unit ball. If we want to underline that we are speaking about
unit ball in
\it{l}^{2}
we write

. We say that meromorphic functions separate
points on the complex Hilbert manifold

if for every pair

of points
of
{\mathcal{X}}
there exists a meromorphic function

on
{\mathcal{X}}
such that

to the complex projective space

.
By a meromorphic injection we mean a meromorphic mapping

This turns out to not be the case in infinite dimensions. Namely, despite

of the Riemann sphere possesses the property of

, it cannot be meromorphically
injected into the projective Hilbert space
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
. Even more, the following is true.

Theorem 1
.

There doesn’t exist a meromorphic map

such that the

of
{\mathsf{Ker}}\,dg_{q}
is
\geqslant 2
at some point

where

is holomorphic.

Mappings

with
{\mathsf{codim}}\,{\mathsf{Ker}}\,dg_{z}\leqslant 1
for all

where

is holomorphic, are
mappings to curves and such obviously do exist.
The non-existence of an
equivariant holomorphic embedding
with respect to the
natural group action of
\Omega\left(PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\right)
and (any) its representation
in
U(l^{2})
was proved in
[
Z
]
responding (partially and negatively) to the statement
from
[
MZ
]
. The proof in
[

]
is based on the comparison of Picard groups and

and

and doesn’t requires

to be equivariant in any sense.

1.2. Projective Hilbert space

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be complex Hilbert manifolds. Intuitively a meromorphic mapping

{\mathcal{X}}\dashedrightarrow{\mathcal{Y}}
should be defined by a holomorphic map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
I
is a Hilbert analytic set in
{\mathcal{X}}
of codimension
\geqslant 2
such
that the graph
\Gamma_{f}
of
f
“extends” in some sense to
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
. And
this “extended object” is what we call
a meromorphic
graph
(or, a
a meromorphic map
) and denote it still as
\Gamma_{f}
(or, as
f
).
The indeterminacy set of a meromorphic mapping
f
is the smallest closed subset
I
of

{\mathcal{X}}
such that
f
is holomorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I
. It will be denoted as
I_{f}
.
We do not claim that
I_{f}
is Hilbert analytic in general, but this will be so in both
model examples of Hilbert manifolds mentioned above. Furthermore we shall investigate
in what sense the aforementioned extension is realized in the examples of
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and

{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
. They are quite different in nature but have one common feature, namely the
notion of meromorphicity for them is somehow “predefined”, as it will be
explained below.

We start with the case when
{\mathcal{Y}}
is the projective Hilbert space

{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}(l^{2}\setminus\{0\})/w\sim\lambda w
,
\lambda\not=0
, where

w=(w_{0},w_{1},...)\in\it{l}^{2}
. By
z^{j}_{k}=w_{j}/w_{k}
we denote the affine coordinates in
the affine chart
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}=\{[w]:w_{k}\not=0\}={\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})\setminus{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
, where

{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
is the hyperplane

{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}\{[w]\in{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2}):w_{k}=0\}.

(1.1)

Let a holomorphic mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
be given. Assuming that
f({\mathcal{X}}\setminus I)\not\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
set
A_{0}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}f^{-1}({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
.
Observe that
f|_{{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}}
is a holomorphic map with values in
{\mathcal{U}}_{0}\equiv\it{l}^{2}
and therefore writes as
f=(f_{1},f_{2},...)
. Here

are holomorphic functions on

{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}
and such that
\left\|{f(z)}\right\|_{\it{l}^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{k}|f_{k}(z)|^{2}
is locally
bounded, see subsection
2.2. Holomorphic functions and mappings
. We prove the following statement.

Theorem 2
.

The closure
\bar{A}_{0}
of the divisor
A_{0}
is a divisor in
{\mathcal{X}}
and all
f_{k}
extend
meromorphically to the whole of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Moreover, the orders of the poles of these extensions
are locally uniformly bounded along
\bar{A}_{0}
. In addition the following is true.

i)
For every point
a\in{\mathcal{X}}
there exists a neighbourhood
{\mathcal{V}}\ni a
and holomorphic
in
{\mathcal{V}}

functions
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...
without common factors such that

a)
\left\|{\varphi(z)}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k}|\varphi_{k}(z)|^{2}
is locally bounded and
f_{k}=\varphi_{k}/\varphi_{0}
on
{\mathcal{V}}
;

b)
{\mathcal{V}}\cap I\supset\{z\in{\mathcal{V}}:\varphi_{k}(z)=0\text{ for all }k\}
.

ii)
The indeterminacy set
I_{f}
of the meromorphic map
f
thus obtained is locally

defined by the equation
\varphi(z)=0
,
i.e.,
I_{f}\cap{\mathcal{V}}=\{z\in{\mathcal{V}}:\varphi_{0}(z)=\varphi_{1}(z)=...0\}
.

Now it is clear that by a meromorphic mapping in the case of
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
we should understand
the object obtained by the extension from
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I
to
{\mathcal{X}}
of a holomorphic map
f

as in this theorem. It is worth to note that on
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus I_{f}
our map is
defined as

f(z)=[\varphi_{0}(z):\varphi_{1}(z):...]

(1.2)

for an appropriate holomorphic map
\varphi=(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...)
with values in
\it{l}^{2}
.
Therefore it is natural to give the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A meromorphic mapping from a Hilbert manifold
{\mathcal{X}}
to the complex projective Hilbert
space
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
is defined by a sequence
f_{1},f_{2},...
of meromorphic functions on

{\mathcal{X}}
such that locally there exist holomorphic functions
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...
with

\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\it{l}^{2}}
bounded and
f_{k}=\frac{\varphi_{k}}{\varphi_{0}}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Notice that such mapping
f
is locally given by (
1.2
) outside of
the set of common zeroes of
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...
. We prove Thullen, Levi and a Hartogs
type extension theorems for meromorphic mappings with values in
{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
, see Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
as well as Corollary
3.1
. In this Introduction
we shall need the following corollary from the Hartogs type extension.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
-smooth closed curve in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and let

{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

This corollary follows from the Hartogs type extension theorem by placing a Hartogs
figure

near a given point

polydisk

1.3. Case of loop spaces.

Our second model example when the meromorphicity is ”predefined” is the Sobolev loop
space of the Riemann sphere
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, i.e.
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}=W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
, where the
latter denotes the space of maps
u:{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
which are of Sobolev class

W^{1,2}
. The natural complex structure on
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
was introduced
by Lempert, see
[
L3
]
, and will be recalled in section
4.
Loop space of the Riemann sphere
. For the moment,
it will be sufficient to say that a holomorphic map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
from a complex
Hilbert manifold
{\mathcal{X}}
to
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
can be seen as a
mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
such that:

•

for every
s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
the map
f(\cdot,s):{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is holomorphic,

•

for every
z\in{\mathcal{X}}
one has
f(z,\cdot)\in W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
and the
correspondence

{\mathcal{X}}\ni z\mapsto f(z,\cdot)\in W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})

is continuous with respect to the standard topology on
{\mathcal{X}}
and the Sobolev
topology on
W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
.

Such description will be called the
representation
of
f
. We shall prove
the following

Theorem 3
.

Any holomorphic mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, where
{\mathcal{X}}
is a Hilbert manifold
and
I
a Hilbert analytic set in
{\mathcal{X}}
of codimension
\geqslant 2
possesses the following properties:

i)
for every
s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
the map
f(\cdot,s)
is a meromorphic function which meromorphically

extends to the whole of
{\mathcal{X}}
;

ii)
the family of indeterminacy sets
\{I_{f(\cdot,s)}:s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\}
is locally finite
in
{\mathcal{X}}
;

iii)
mapping
f
holomorphically extends to
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}
, and therefore
I_{f}=\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}

is the indeterminacy set of the meromorphic mapping thus obtained.

Notice that since every
I_{f(\cdot,s)}
is an analytic set of pure codimension two such is
their locally finite union
I_{f}
. These items force us to give the following definition.

Definition 2
.

A meromorphic mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\dashedrightarrow L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
from a Hilbert manifold
{\mathcal{X}}
to the Sobolev
loop space
W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
is defined by a family of meromorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}
functions

\{f(\cdot,s):s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\}
such that:

i)
the family of their indeterminacy sets
\{I_{f(\cdot,s)}:s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\}
is locally
finite in
{\mathcal{X}}
;

ii)

f
is holomorphic when restricted to
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}
.

Extension properties of meromorphic mappings with values in
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
occur to be not
as good as for those with values in
{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
. In Example
4.1
we construct
a holomorphic mapping
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma\to W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
, where
\Gamma=\{\gamma(s)=(\gamma_{1}(s),\gamma_{2}(s))\}
is a loop in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
of class
W^{1,2}\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{1,\frac{1}{2}}
, such that for every
s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
the point
\gamma(s)
is an
essential singularity of
f
. That is
f
doesn’t extend to any neighbourhood of

\gamma(s)
even meromorphically. This example shows that neither Thullen no Hartogs
type extension theorems are valid for meromorphic mappings with values in

2.1. Hilbert manifolds and analytic sets

We shall consecutively use results about Hilbert analytic sets from

[

be a Hilbert manifold.

Definition 2.1
.

By a Hilbert analytic set in

we understand a subset

.
If

.

We shall use under several occasions the Weierstrass preparation theorem in
the Hilbert settings. The proof is the same as in the finite dimensional case. Let
f

be a holomorphic function in open neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of

such that

f(a)=0

in

Theorem 2.1
.

There exists a neighbourhood

of the origin such that for all

f(z+a)=\left(\lambda^{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}(z^{\prime})\lambda^{k-j}\right)\varphi(z)

. Moreover, for all

.

Proof.
By taking

one can consider only the case where

and by continuity of

one can found a direction

, a ball

centred at the origin and a small disk

and for all

k=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Delta}\frac{\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(\xi v)}{g(\xi v)}\,d\xi

Let us denote the zeros of

\lambda_{1}(z^{\prime}),\dots,\lambda_{k}(z^{\prime})
. Then we
obtain Newton polynomials evaluated on the zeros by

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}(z^{\prime})^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Delta}\xi^{\mu}\frac{\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}(z^{\prime}+\xi v)}{g(z^{\prime}+\xi v)}\,d\xi

From algebra one knows that elementary symmetric polynomials can be expressed as polynomials
from Newton polynomials. Therefore the polynomial

\square

Choose a direction

2.2. Holomorphic functions and mappings

An
\it{l}^{2}
-valued holomorphic mapping from an open subset
{\mathcal{X}}
of
\it{l}^{2}
is a mapping

f:{\mathcal{X}}\to\it{l}^{2}
which is Fréchet differentiable at all points of
{\mathcal{X}}
. It is
well known that

is holomorphic if and only if it is
G
-holomorphic,

i.e.,
Gâteaux differentiable, and
\left\|{f(z)}\right\|
is locally bounded, see Proposition 8.6
and Theorem 8.7 in
[
Mu
]
. This implies that if
f
is written in coordinates as

f(z)=(f_{1}(z),f_{2}(z),...)

(2.4)

then
f
is holomorphic if and only if all
f_{k}
are holomorphic functions
and
\left\|{f(z)}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|f_{k}(z)\right|^{2}
is locally bounded. Indeed,
since we need to prove the Gâteaux differentiability
only we can suppose that
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and then Cauchy formula and local boundedness of
\left\|{f(z)}\right\|

gave us the local boundedness of
\left\|{f^{\prime}(z)}\right\|
where
f^{\prime}(z)=(f_{1}^{\prime}(z),f_{2}^{\prime}(z),\dots)
. Indeed

\left\|{f^{\prime}(z)}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{k}\left|\int\limits_{|\zeta-z|=\varepsilon}\frac{f_{k}(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^{2}}d\zeta\right|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}\sum_{k}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\left|f_{k}(\zeta)\right|\varepsilon d\theta\right)^{2}\leqslant

(2.5)

\leqslant\frac{1}{(2\pi\varepsilon)^{2}}\sum_{k}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\left|f_{k}(\zeta)\right|^{2}d\theta\cdot 2\pi\leqslant\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon^{2}}{\mathsf{sup}}\,_{|\zeta-z|=\varepsilon}\left\|{f(\zeta)}\right\|^{2}.

In the same fashion one proves the local boundedness of
\left\|{f^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(z)}\right\|
and hence the continuity
of
f^{\prime}(z)
. And this in its turn implies Gâteaux differentiability:

\left\|{f(z+h)-f(z)-f^{\prime}(z)h}\right\|=\left\|{\int\limits_{0}^{1}\frac{df(z+th)}{dt}dt-f^{\prime}(z)h}\right\|=

=\left\|{\int\limits_{0}^{1}(f^{\prime}(z+th)-f^{\prime}(z))dt\cdot h}\right\|=o(h)

by the continuity of
f^{\prime}(z)
.

2.3. Meromorphic functions

The field of quotients of

. A meromorphic function on a Hilbert manifold

is

of

, where

are connected, and meromorphic fractions

and

. Another open covering

and

define the same meromorphic function if

on

If the covering

.
By

we denote the field of meromorphic functions on
{\mathcal{X}}
. The
following lemma is well known in finite dimensions and its proof in Hilbert settings
is similar.

Let

and

By translation one can suppose

Let

and

do not vanish identically on

and

.
Since

f(z,w)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(w)(z-z_{0})^{k}=0
,
where
f_{k}
are
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
-valued holomorphic functions in a neighbourhood of
w_{0}
.
Notice that all
f_{k}(w_{0})=0
and therefore in a neighbourhood of
w_{0}
we have

I=\{f_{1}(w)=f_{2}(w)=\dots=0\}
. Extension of
f

to
\Delta\times\left(B^{\infty}\setminus I\right)
can be achieved as in the proof of
Theorem
2.2
using Theorem
2.5
. Now
f
is meromorphic on
(\Delta\times B^{\infty})\setminus(\Delta\times I)
and meromorphic on
A_{1-r,1}\times B^{\infty}
. So we
are under assumptions of the Thullen-type theorem
2.2
and conclude that
f
extends
to a meromorphic functions on
\Delta\times B^{\infty}
.

\square

3.
Meromorphic mappings to the projective Hilbert space

3.1. Holomorphic mappings to the projective Hilbert space

In the definition of the projective Hilbert space over
\it{l}^{2}
one requires homogeneous
coordinates
[w]=[w_{0}:w_{1}:...]
to be such that
w=(w_{0},w_{1},...)\in\it{l}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
. Notice that
the affine coordinate
(z^{j}_{1},z^{j}_{2},...)
in the chart
{\mathcal{U}}_{j}
belong to
\it{l}^{2}
as well. Denote by

h_{j}:{\mathcal{U}}_{j}\to\it{l}^{2}
the corresponding coordinate map, namely
h_{j}([w])=\{\frac{w_{k}}{w_{j}}\}_{k\neq j}
.
Transition maps

h_{j}\circ h_{i}^{-1}:\it{l}^{2}\setminus\{z^{i}_{j}=0\}\to\it{l}^{2}\setminus\{z^{j}_{i}=0\}

are given by

h_{j}\circ h_{i}^{-1}:(z^{i}_{0},z^{i}_{1},...)\mapsto(z^{i}_{0}/z^{i}_{j},z^{i}_{1}/z^{i}_{j},...)=(z^{j}_{0},z^{j}_{1},...).

(3.1)

They are obviously biholomorphic and therefore
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
is a complex Hilbert manifold
modelled over
\it{l}^{2}
. Closed submanifolds of
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
will be called projective Hilbert
or simply projective manifolds. I.e, a projective Hilbert manifold is a closed subset

{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
such that for every point
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
there exists a neighbourhood

{\mathcal{U}}\ni m
and a holomorphic map
h:{\mathcal{U}}\to L
to a Hilbert space
L
with surjective
differential such that
{\mathcal{M}}=\{z\in{\mathcal{U}}:h(z)=0\}
. Their intersections with

{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})\setminus\{w_{0}=0\}
will be called affine. As well as closed
submanifolds of
{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})\setminus\{w_{0}=0\}
will be called Stein.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a complex Hilbert manifold and let
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})

be a holomorphic mapping. Without loss of generality we assume that the image of

f
is not contained in any hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}_{j}
, see (
1.1
). Otherwise we would get

f:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\cong{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
and so on. Taking into account the fact that

{\mathbb{P}}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}\bigcap\limits_{{\mathcal{H}}_{j}\supset f({\mathcal{X}})}{\mathcal{H}}_{j}

is a closed linear subspace of
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
isomorphic either to
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
or to
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}

for some
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we find ourselves under our assumption with
{\mathbb{P}}
as a target space of
f
.
Set
A_{j}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}f^{-1}({\mathcal{H}}_{j})
. These are hypersurfaces (some of them may be empty) in
{\mathcal{X}}
.
For the restriction
f|_{{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}}:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}\to{\mathcal{U}}_{0}
the composition

h_{0}\circ f
is a holomorphic
\it{l}^{2}
-valued mapping. In affine coordinates
z^{0}_{1},...,z^{0}_{k},...

it writes as

(h_{0}\circ f)(z)=(f^{0}_{1}(z),f^{0}_{2}(z),...),

(3.2)

where
f^{0}_{k}
are holomorphic and
\left\|{(h_{0}\circ f)(z)}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k}|f^{0}_{k}(z)|^{2}

is locally
bounded in
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The functions
\{f_{k}^{0}\}
, holomorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}
, are in fact meromorphic on

{\mathcal{X}}
. Moreover, their orders of poles along
A_{0}
are locally bounded and consequently
our mapping in a neighbourhood of any point
a\in{\mathcal{X}}
writes as

f(z)=[\varphi_{0}(z):\varphi_{1}(z):...],

(3.3)

where
\varphi_{k}
are holomorphic, have no common zeros and, in addition,
\left\|{\varphi(z)}\right\|^{2}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}\sum_{k}|\varphi_{k}(z)|^{2}
is locally bounded.

Proof.
Take a point
a\in A_{0}
such that
a\notin A_{1}

i.e.,
f(a)\notin{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
.
Then the holomorphic map
h_{1}\circ f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{1}\to U_{1}
writes as
h_{1}\circ f=(f^{1}_{0},f^{1}_{2},...)
with
f^{1}_{0}=1/f^{0}_{1},f^{1}_{2}=f^{0}_{2}/f^{0}_{1},...,f^{1}_{k}=f^{0}_{k}/f^{0}_{1},...

holomorphic in a neighbourhood of
a
and
\left\|{(f^{1}_{0}(z),f^{1}_{2}(z),...)}\right\|
locally bounded there.
This proves that
f^{0}_{k}
are meromorphic in a neighbourhood of
a
and therefore on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus(A_{0}\cap A_{1})
. The same argument can be repeated for any
A_{j}
and since

\bigcap A_{j}=\varnothing
we see that
f^{0}_{k}
are meromorphic on the whole of
{\mathcal{X}}
.

To prove the second assertion fix again a point
a\in A_{0}
and let
h=0
be the
primitive equation of
A_{0}
in a neighbourhood
U
of
a
. Existence of such
h
follows from
the fact that the ring
{\mathcal{O}}_{a}
is factorial. We can choose

U
in such a way that
U=\Delta\times B
, where
B
is the unit ball and
A_{0}\cap(\partial\Delta\times B)=\varnothing
. Such
U
will be called
adapted

to
A_{0}
.

Claim 1.

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists
N_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}\{0\}\cup{\mathbb{N}}
such that

f^{0}_{k}=\frac{\psi_{k}}{h^{N_{k}}}\quad\text{ with }\psi_{k}\text{ holomorphic and not divisible by }h\text{ in }U.

(3.4)

To prove this claim suppose first that
A_{0}\cap U
is irreducible. Let
P(z_{1},z^{\prime})
be
the Weierstrass polynomial for
h
,
i.e.,
h(z_{1},z^{\prime})=z_{1}^{p}+a_{1}(z^{\prime})z_{1}^{p-1}+...+a_{p}(z^{\prime})
with

a_{j}\in{\mathcal{O}}(B),a_{j}(0^{\prime})=0
and
h=P\varphi
, where
\varphi
is holomorphic in
U
and doesn’t
vanish. Since
f^{0}_{k}
has poles at most on
A_{0}\cap U
we can multiply it by
P^{N_{k}}
, where

N_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
is the order of the pole of
f^{0}_{k}
along
A_{0}\cap U
to get a holomorphic
function
\psi_{k}
in
U
which is obviously not divisible by
h
. If
A_{0}\cap U
is reducible
represent it as a union of a finite number of irreducible components
A_{0}\cap U=\bigcup_{l}A_{0}^{l}

with primitive equations
h^{l}=0
and repeat the argument as above to each
A_{0}^{l}
. The rest is
obvious.

Claim 2.

Next we claim that the sequence
\{N_{k}\}
in

3.4
is bounded.
If not one can find a subsequence
\{k_{n}\}
such that
N_{k_{n}}\nearrow+\infty
and

f^{0}_{k_{n}}=\frac{\psi_{k_{n}}}{h^{N_{k_{n}}}}\quad\text{ with }\psi_{k_{n}}\text{ holomorphic an not divisible by }h\text{ in }U.

(3.5)

Since
\bigcap A_{j}=\varnothing
there exists
m
such that
a\in A_{0}\setminus A_{m}
. Then

f^{m}_{j}=\frac{f^{0}_{j}}{f^{0}_{m}}=\frac{\psi_{j}}{\psi_{m}}\cdot h^{N_{m}-N_{j}}

should be holomorphic in a neighbourhood of
a
for all
j
. But this is certainly not true
for
j=k_{n}
with
n
big enough, contradiction.

Let
N={\mathsf{max}}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}{N_{k}}
and set
\varphi_{0}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}h^{N}
,
\varphi_{k}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}f^{0}_{k}h^{N}
for

k=1,2,...
. Consider the map

\varphi\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...):U\to\it{l}^{2}.

(3.6)

We need to prove that
\varphi
indeed takes its values in
\it{l}^{2}
and is locally bounded on
U
.
Remark that
\left\|{\varphi(z)}\right\|=|h(z)|^{N}\left\|{\left(1,(h_{0}\circ f)(z)\right)}\right\|
for
z
in a neighbourhood
of
\partial\Delta\times B
and therefore is
locally
bounded there. Using compactness of

\partial\Delta
we deduce that
\left\|{\varphi}\right\|
is bounded on
\partial\Delta\times B_{\delta}
, say by
M
, for

\delta>0
small enough. Each cut
\sum_{k=0}^{N}|\varphi_{k}|^{2}
is plurisubharmonic on
U
and
therefore is bounded by
M
on
\Delta\times B_{\delta}
. Tending
N\to\infty
we get the boundedness
of
\left\|{\varphi}\right\|
on
\Delta\times B_{\delta}
.

All what left to prove is that
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...
can be taken without common zeroes.
Set
I=\{z\in U:\varphi_{0}(z)=\varphi_{1}(z)=...=0\}
. If
\varphi_{j}
-s have common divisor in
{}_{L}{\mathcal{O}}_{a}
we can
divide them by this divisor and this will not change our map
f
. Therefore
{\mathsf{codim}}\,I\geqslant 2
. Suppose
it is non-empty an choose some
b\in I
. Our map on
U\setminus I
is given by

f(z)=[\varphi_{0}(z):\varphi_{1}(z):...].

Since
f
is holomorphic in a neighbourhood of
b
there exists
j
such that
f(b)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{j}
.
Therefore
\varphi_{j}(b)\not=0
. Contradiction.

\square

Remark 3.1
.

1. The affine coordinate chart
{\mathcal{U}}_{0}
will be considered as distinguished and
f^{0}_{k}
-s will
be denoted simply as
f_{k}
-s. We proved that our holomorphic map
f
is represented by a sequence
of meromorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}
functions
(f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{k},..)

\left\|{f}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\left\|{f_{k}}\right\|}^{2}
is locally bounded

{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A_{0}
.

there exits a neighbourhood

{\mathcal{V}}\ni a
, holomorphic in

functions
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...
without common zeroes such that

\varphi=(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...)
is locally bounded as a mapping
{\mathcal{V}}\to\it{l}^{2}
and such that our

is
represented on
{\mathcal{V}}
as in (
3.3
),
i.e.,

, where

is the canonical projection.

3. Remark that if a holomorphic mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
is given on
an open
{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}

) then

.

.

3.2. Meromorphic mappings to
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
.

Recall that a meromorphic mapping between finite dimensional complex manifolds
X
and
Y
is a
holomorphic mapping
f:X\setminus I\to Y
where
I
is analytic set with
{\mathsf{codim}}\,I\geqslant 2
,
such that the closure
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
of its graph
\Gamma_{f}
is an analytic set in
X\times Y

and the restriction of the natural projection
\pi_{1}:X\times Y\to X
to
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
is proper.
With some ambiguity this meromorphic mapping is denoted still as
f
, instead of
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}

one writes simply
\Gamma_{f}
and calls it the graph of
f
.

Therefore an object which pretends to be a meromorphic map in Hilbert case
should be at least holomorphic outside of an analytic set of codimension two.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a complex Hilbert manifold,
I\subset{\mathcal{X}}
an analytic subset
of codimension
\geqslant 2
and let
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
be a holomorphic mapping.
Then for every
a\in I
there exists a neighbourhood
a\in{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
and
functions
\{\varphi_{k}\}
holomorphic in
{\mathcal{U}}
such that

i)

\varphi=(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...)
is a holomorphic mapping from
{\mathcal{U}}
to
\it{l}^{2}
;

ii)

{\mathcal{U}}\cap I\supset\{z\in{\mathcal{U}}:\varphi_{k}(z)=0\text{ for all }k\}
;

iii)

(\pi\circ\varphi)(z)=f(z)
for
z\in{\mathcal{U}}\setminus I
, where
\pi:\it{l}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
is the canonical projection.

Proof.
As we saw in Proposition
3.1
mapping
f
is defined by a sequence

\{f_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of meromorphic functions on
{\mathcal{X}}\backslash I
which are holomorphic
on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus(I\cup A_{0})
where
A_{0}=f^{-1}({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
. We can extend
f_{k}
meromorphically
to
{\mathcal{X}}
by Lemma
2.6
, denote by
\tilde{f}_{k}
these extensions, and by
Proposition
2.1
we can extend
A_{0}
to an analytic set
\tilde{A}_{0}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
. Given

a\in I
we need to examine
f
in a neighbourhood of
a
. If
a\in I\setminus\tilde{A}_{0}

all
f_{k}
extend holomorphically to a neighbourhood of
a
by Lemma
2.2
,

i.e.,
our mapping
f
extends holomorphically to a neighbourhood of such
a
. Suppose now that

a\in I\cap\tilde{A}_{0}
. Meromorphic functions
\tilde{f}_{k}
have poles at most on
\tilde{A}_{0}

and we need to prove that their orders stay bounded,
i.e.,
that there exists
N_{k}
such that
h^{N_{k}}\tilde{f}_{k}
is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of
a
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
N={\mathsf{sup}}\,_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}N_{k}<\infty
. Here
h
is a minimal defining function of
\tilde{A}_{0}
at
a
. We assume that
that
N_{k}
are minimal that do the job. Suppose this is not true,
i.e.,
there exists a subsequence

N_{k_{j}}\to\infty
such that
h^{N_{k_{j}}}\tilde{f}_{k_{j}}
are holomorphic but

h^{N_{k_{j}}-1}\tilde{f}_{k_{j}}
are not. But
h=0
is an equation for
\tilde{A}_{0}
in a
neighbourhood of
a
and we get in this case that
h^{N_{k_{j}}-1}\tilde{f}_{k_{j}}
are not
holomorphic in this neighbourhood. This contradicts to Proposition
3.1
.
Now set
\varphi_{0}=h^{N}
and
\varphi_{k}=h^{N}\tilde{f}_{k}
. Then
ii)
and
iii)
are satisfied. The
same consideration as in Proposition
3.1
gives us

.

\square

The discussion above leads us to the following

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a complex Hilbert manifold. A meromorphic map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\dashedrightarrow\mathbb{P}(l^{2})

is given by a holomorphic map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\backslash I\to\mathbb{P}(l^{2})
, where

I\subset{\mathcal{X}}
in an analytic subset of codimension
\geqslant 2
, such that for every

a\in I
there exists a neighbourhood
{\mathcal{V}}
of
a
and a
holomorphic map
\varphi=\{\varphi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}:{\mathcal{V}}\to l^{2}
such that
f=\pi\circ\varphi
,

i.e.,
f(z)=[\varphi_{0}(z):\varphi_{1}(z):\dots]
for
z\in{\mathcal{V}}\backslash I
.

The minimal such
I
is called the
indeterminacy set of
f
and is denoted as
I_{f}
. We can interpret Proposition
3.2

as follows : if
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
is a holomorphic map and
{\mathsf{codim}}\,I\geqslant 2
then
f
extends meromorphically to
{\mathcal{X}}
. One more remark: every meromorphic
map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\dashedrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
can be represented by globally defined meromorphic functions

\{f_{k}\}
such that the orders of their denominators are locally uniformly bounded in the
following sense. Let
A_{0}=f^{-1}({\mathcal{H}}_{0})
be the preimage of the hyperplane
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}=\{z_{0}=0\}
under the holomorphic map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I_{f}\to{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
. Then
A_{0}
extends
to an analytic hypersurface in the whole of
{\mathcal{X}}
, we denote it still as
A_{0}
. And now the
poles of all
f_{k}
are on
A_{0}
and their orders are uniformly bounded in a neighbourhood
of every point of
a
.

The indeterminacy set
I
of a meromorphic mapping between Hilbert manifolds might look not as
an analytic set in finite dimensions. And, moreover, the graph of
f
might be not a direct
analytic subset of
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
in the sense that it cannot be locally represented as
a finite analytic cover.

3.3. Example

Consider the following mapping
f:\it{l}^{2}\dashedrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})

f:z=\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\mapsto\left[\{z_{k}(z_{k}-1/k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\right],

(3.7)

or, in more details

\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\mapsto\left[z_{1}(z_{1}-1):...:z_{k}(z_{k}-1/k):...\right].

The indeterminacy set
I
of
f
(
i.e.,
all
z
such that all components in (
3.7
)
vanish) is of Cantor type, see pp. 33-34 in
[
Ra
]
. In particular it is totally
disconnected and contains the sequence
\{e_{1},...,\frac{1}{k}e_{k},...\}
converging to zero.

Fix
z=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\in l^{2}
and fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
. Consider
y_{n,k}\in l^{2}
defined
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
by

\displaystyle y_{n,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{kz_{k}}{n^{2}}\right)e_{k}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq k}^{n}\frac{-jz_{j}}{n^{2}}e_{j}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-z_{1}}{n^{2}},\frac{-2z_{2}}{n^{2}},\dots,\frac{-(k-1)z_{k-1}}{n^{2}},\frac{1}{k}+\frac{kz_{k}}{n^{2}},\frac{-(k+1)z_{k+1}}{n^{2}},\dots,\frac{-nz_{n}}{n^{2}},0,\dots\right).

Notice that
y_{n,k}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{k}e_{k}
.
For
n
big enough we have

\displaystyle f(y_{n,k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{-z_{1}}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{-z_{1}}{n^{2}}-1\right):\frac{-2z_{2}}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{-2z_{2}}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right):\dots:\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{kz_{k}}{n^{2}}\right)\frac{kz_{k}}{n^{2}}:\dots\right]

(3.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-z_{1}\left(\frac{-z_{1}}{n^{2}}-1\right):-2z_{2}\left(\frac{-2z_{2}}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\right):\dots:\left(\frac{1}{k}+\frac{kz_{k}}{n^{2}}\right)kz_{k}:\dots\right].

(3.9)

Therefore
f(y_{n,k})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\left[z_{1}:z_{2}:\dots:z_{k}:\dots\right]
and consequently
(y_{n,k},f(y_{n,k}))\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}(\frac{1}{k}e_{k},[z])
. This means that
\pi_{1}^{-1}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{k}e_{k}\right\}\right)={\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, here
\pi_{1}
is the restriction of the
natural projection
l^{2}\times{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})\to l^{2}
to the graph of
f
. We conclude that
the set
\pi_{1}^{-1}(I)\supset\{\frac{1}{k}e_{k}\}\times{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
is not direct.

3.4. Restrictions to complex curves

Let us prove that any meromorphic mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\dashedrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
restricted to a complex
curve, which is not contained in the indeterminacy set of
f
, extends holomorphically to this
curve. Since
f
is meromorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}
there exists an analytic subset
I\subset{\mathcal{X}}
of
codimension
\geqslant 2
such that
f
is holomorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I
. Let
\varphi:\Delta\to{\mathcal{X}}
be a complex curve which is not contained in
I
. Then
\varphi
is holomorphic on
\Delta\setminus\varphi^{-1}(I)
. Moreover the subset
\varphi^{-1}(I)
is discrete.

Let
\lambda_{0}\in\varphi^{<-1>}(I)
. Consider a ball
B\subset{\mathcal{X}}
centered on
\varphi(\lambda_{0})

such that there exists
F\in{\mathcal{O}}(B,l^{2})
with
f|_{B\setminus I}=\pi\circ F
. Then there exists
0<r<1
such that
\partial\Delta(\lambda_{0},r)\cap I=\varnothing
and
\varphi(\Delta(\lambda_{0},r))\subset B
.

For every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have
F_{j}\circ\varphi(\lambda_{0})=0
. So there exists
k_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

F_{j}\circ\varphi(\lambda)=(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{k_{j}}G_{j}(\lambda)

with
G_{j}\in{\mathcal{O}}(B)
and
G_{j}
does not vanish on
\Delta(\lambda_{0},r)
. Let
k={\mathsf{min}}\{k_{j}\ |\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
.
Therefore

f\circ\varphi(\lambda)=\left[F_{0}\circ\varphi(\lambda):F_{1}\circ\varphi(\lambda):\dots\right]=\left[(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{k_{0}-k}G_{0}(\lambda):(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{k_{1}-k}G_{1}(\lambda):\dots\right]

with

(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{2k}\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}|(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{k_{j}-k}G_{j}(\lambda)|^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\left|F_{j}\circ\varphi(\lambda)\right|^{2}=\left\|{F\circ\varphi(\lambda)}\right\|^{2}<\infty

and then
f\circ\varphi
is holomorphic on
\lambda_{0}
.

3.5. Extension properties of meromorphic maps to
{\mathbb{P}}(\it{l}^{2})
.

Now we shall prove the following generalization of the classical Levi, Thullen and
Hartogs type theorems. We start with Levi-Thullen.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a complex Hilbert manifold and let
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus A\to{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
be
a meromorphic mapping where
A
is analytic and

This

is projective. Take the inverse map to
\tilde{f}^{-1}
,
denote it as
\tilde{f}:X\to\tilde{Y}
. This map doesn’t contract
divisors in
X
(because its inverse is regular) and is bimeromorphic.

Suppose its differential vanishes at some point
x\in X\setminus I_{\tilde{f}}
. If
x
is taken generic then
\tilde{f}
is a ramified
cover in a neighbourhood of
x
. But this contradicts to the fact that

\tilde{f}
is bimeromorphic. Therefore
\tilde{f}
is an embedding on

X\setminus I_{\tilde{f}}
.

\square

4.2. Meromorphic maps to the loop space

From now on we assume that
X={\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
k=1
and we are going to explain that the Definition

2
from the Introduction gives the natural notion of a meromorphic mapping
in the case of
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}=W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
as a target manifold. Let a meromorphic
(in any sense) mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be given. Then the least thing we know is
that there should exist
an analytic of codimension
\geqslant 2
set
I
in
{\mathcal{X}}
such that the restriction
of
f
to
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I
is holomorphic. Denote this restriction still as
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
as well as its representation
f:({\mathcal{X}}\setminus I)\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Notice that the latter satisfies items (
i)
and (
ii)
of Proposition

4.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let a holomorphic mapping
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be given, where
I
is analytic
in
{\mathcal{X}}
of codimension
\geqslant 2
. Then:

i)
for every
s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
mapping
f(\cdot,s)
is a meromorphic function which extends
mero-

morphically on the whole of
{\mathcal{X}}
and its indeterminacy set
I_{f(\cdot,s)}

is contained in
I
;

ii)
the family of the indeterminacy sets
\{I_{f(\cdot,s)}:s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\}
is locally
finite in
{\mathcal{X}}
and

I_{f}=\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}
is an analytic set in
{\mathcal{X}}
of pure
codimension two;

iii)
mapping
f
holomorphically extends to
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I_{f}
.

Proof.
The proof will be achieved in several steps.

Step 1.

Meromorphicity of
f(\cdot,s)
.
Notice that
for every
s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
mapping
f(\cdot,s):{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is in fact a
meromorphic function without indeterminacy points and it extends to a meromorphic
function on the whole of
{\mathcal{X}}
by Lemma
2.6
. Denote by
I_{f(\cdot,s)}

the indeterminacy set of the extended
f(\cdot,s)
. We have that
I_{f(\cdot,s)}\subset I

because
f
is holomorphic on
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I
. Since
I_{f(\cdot,s)}
is the
indeterminacy set of a meromorphic function and, therefore is locally an intersection of
two relatively prime divisors it is of pure codimension two.
Take some point
z_{0}\in\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}\subset I^{2}_{z_{0}}
, where

I^{2}_{z_{0}}
is the (necessarily finite) union of codimension two components of
I
at

z_{0}
as in (
2.1
). Due to the description of the structure of Hilbert analytic
sets of finite definition in
[
Ra
]
one can find a neighbourhood
{\mathcal{U}}=B^{\infty}\times\Delta^{2}
of
z_{0}
such that
I^{2}_{z_{0}}\cap{\mathcal{U}}
is a finite cover of
B^{\infty}
in these
coordinates. Therefore
\left(\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}\right)\cap{\mathcal{U}}
can
have only finitely many components. We obtain as a result that the union
\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}
is an analytic of pure codimension two subset of
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Items (
i)
and (
ii)
are proved and we turn to item (
iii)
. Take some
z_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}
and let
{\mathcal{U}}
be a neighbourhood
of
z_{0}
not intersecting
\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}
. Then
f
extends to a
mapping
f:{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. We need to prove its holomorphicity. Condition (
i)
of Proposition
4.1
for our
f
is obviously satisfied. Therefore all we need
to prove is the condition (
ii)
,
i.e.,
continuity of the mapping
z\to f(z,\cdot)
in
Sobolev topology.

Step 2.

Continuity in classical topology.
We shall first prove
that
f:{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is continuous in the classical sense. We need to prove this at the
points
(z_{0},s_{0})\in{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
with
z_{0}\in I\setminus I_{f}
only. Fix such point
z_{0}

and take an open neighbourhood
{\mathcal{U}}
of
z_{0}
biholomorphic to
B^{\infty}\times\Delta^{2}
, where
bidisk
\{0\}\times\Delta^{2}
intersects
I
at
z_{0}
transversely,
i.e.,
z_{0}
is an isolated point
of
(\{0\}\times\Delta^{2})\cap I
. Natural coordinates in
B^{\infty}\times\Delta^{2}
centered at

z_{0}=(0,0)
denote as
z=(w,t)
. Note that
f
is holomorphic on
B^{\infty}\times\partial\Delta^{2}
,
provided
B^{\infty}
was taken sufficiently small, and that
f(w,\cdot,s_{0})|_{\Delta^{2}}
is
holomorphic over the whole of
\{w\}\times\Delta^{2}
for every fixed
w\in B^{\infty}
. Due to
Lemma 3.1 from
[
AZ
]
to prove the continuity of
f
at
(z_{0},s_{0})
it is sufficient to prove
that for any sequences
s_{n}\to s_{0}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
,
w_{n}\to 0
in
B^{\infty}
and any neighbourhood

{\mathcal{V}}
of the graph
\Gamma_{0}
of
f(0,\cdot,s_{0})|_{\Delta^{2}}
in
{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
the
graphs
\Gamma_{n}
of
f(w_{n},\cdot,s_{n})|_{\Delta^{2}}
are contained in
{\mathcal{V}}
for
n
big
enough. By the main result of
[
AZ
]
,
i.e.,
Theorem 2.1 there, we can assume that
{\mathcal{V}}
is

1
-complete. That is it admits a strictly plurisubharmonic exhausting function
\rho
. Now it
is easy to conclude from Sobolev embedding (
4.1
) that for
w_{n}
and
s_{n}
close enough
to
0
and
s_{0}
respectively
f(w_{n},\cdot,s_{n})|_{\partial\Delta^{2}}
is close to

f(0,\cdot,s_{0})|_{\partial\Delta^{2}}
. And than by maximum principle for
\rho
to deduce that

\Gamma_{n}
stays in
{\mathcal{V}}
. The step is proved.

Step 3.

Continuity in Sobolev topology.
Now we need to prove
that a continuous in classical sense mapping
f:{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is continuous also
in Sobolev topology. Using compactness of
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
cover it by arcs
S_{j}(\varepsilon)=]s_{j}-\varepsilon,s_{j}+\varepsilon[,j=1,...,N
centered at
s_{j}
and shrink a neighbourhood
{\mathcal{U}}
of
z_{0}
so that

f({\mathcal{U}}\times S_{j})
is contained in a coordinate chart
({\mathcal{V}}_{j},\alpha_{j})
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Make
sure that
f\left(\overline{{\mathcal{U}}\times S_{j}(\varepsilon_{1})}\right)\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{j}
still holds true
for some
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon
. Consider the mapping
f_{j}\mathrel{\raise 0.3pt\hbox{\rm:}{=}}\rho_{j}\alpha_{j}\circ f|_{{\mathcal{U}}\times S_{j}(\varepsilon_{1})}
, where
\rho_{j}
is a cut-off function supported in
S_{j}(\varepsilon_{1})
and equal to

{\mathsf{1}}
on
S_{j}(\varepsilon)
. Take
{\mathcal{U}}
this time in the form
\Delta\times B^{\infty}
and find
a Hartogs figure
H^{\infty}_{1}(r)=\Delta\times B^{\infty}(r)\cup A_{1-r,1}\times B^{\infty}\subset\Delta\times B^{\infty}
not intersecting
I
. Let
(z,t)
be the natural coordinates
in
\Delta\times B^{\infty}
.

Note that:

a) for every
(z,t)\in H^{\infty}_{1}(r)
mapping
f_{j}
is in
W^{1,2}(S_{j}(\varepsilon_{1}),{\mathbb{C}})

and has compact support.

b) Mapping
(z,t)\to f_{j}(z,t,\cdot)\in W^{1,2}(S_{j}(\varepsilon_{1}),{\mathbb{C}})
is continuous in Sobolev
topology.

c) Moreover, for every
s\in S_{j}(\varepsilon_{1})
mapping
f_{j}(\cdot,s)
is holomorphic on

\hat{H}^{\infty}_{1}(r)=\Delta\times B^{\infty}
.

The latter is because the multiplication by a smooth cutoff function preserves the
Sobolev class and continuity in Sobolev topology and since it depends only on the space variable

s
it doesn’t spoils the holomorphicity in
(z,t)
. Therefore
f_{j}
is a holomorphic mapping
from
H^{\infty}_{1}(r)
to a complex Hilbert space. Consequently it extends holomorphically to

\Delta\times B^{\infty}
. In particular we obtain that
f|_{{\mathcal{U}}\times S_{j}(\varepsilon)}
is
continuous in Sobolev topology. By the definition of the Sobolev topology, see (
4.2
)
we obtain the desired continuity of
f
. Lemma is proved.

\square

Remark 4.2
.

As the result we reach the understanding that the Definition
2
from the Introduction
is the natural definition of meromorphicity for mappings with values in
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Indeed, what
we proved in Proposition
4.1
is that a holomorphic map
f:{\mathcal{X}}\setminus I\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
where
I
is Hilbert analytic of codimension
\geqslant 2
, extends to a meromorphic map from

{\mathcal{X}}
to
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
in the sense of Definition
2
.

4.3. Non-existence of an embedding of the loop space to the projective space

We start this subsection with the following example.

Example 4.1
.

Let
\gamma(s)=(\gamma_{1}(s),\gamma_{2}(s))
be a loop in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
of class
W^{1,2}
. Denote
by
\Gamma
the image of
\gamma
. Consider the mapping
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma\to W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
defined as follows

f(z_{1},z_{2},s)=[z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s):z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)].

(4.10)

For every fixed
s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
mapping
f(\cdot,s):{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is
holomorphic. Moreover, for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
we have that
f(z,\cdot)\in W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
and it continuously depends on
z
. This means that
f
is a holomorphic
mapping from
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
to
W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
. Furthermore mapping

f(\cdot,s)
is in fact holomorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\{\gamma(s)\}
, having
\gamma(s)
as its
only indeterminacy point. Therefore
\bigcup_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}}I_{f(\cdot,s)}=\Gamma
,
i.e.,
the family
\{I_{f(\cdot,s)}\}
is not locally finite and henceforth is not an analytic set in

{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. We see that
\Gamma
is an
essential singularity
of
f
in the sense that
f

does not extend meromorphically to a neighbourhood of any point of
\Gamma
. Indeed, if
f

extends meromorphically to a neighbourhood
V
of some
\gamma(s_{0})\in\Gamma
it should be
holomorphic on
V
minus a discrete set. But
f
is obviously not holomorphic at any point
of
\Gamma\cap V
.

Remark 4.3
.

This example shows that the Hartogs type extension theorem is not valid for
meromorphic values in
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Taking
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{C}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
we
obtain a counter example also for the Thullen type extension.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Suppose to the contrary that
g:L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\dashedrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(l^{2})
is such a map and let
\gamma
be such
as in Example
4.1
. Take
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
such that it is of class

W^{2,2}\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{1,\frac{1}{2}}
, both components are an immersed curves and in addition
that its image
\Gamma
is polynomially convex. In particular
it is
not contained
in any complex curve in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Consider the holomorphic map

f:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
defined in (
4.10
). Using the homogeneity of

L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
compose
f
with an automorphism of
L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
in order to map some point
p\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
by a resulting map to a point
q\in L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
of
g
such
that
{\mathsf{codim}}\,{\mathsf{Ker}}\,dg_{q}\geqslant 2
. Denote this resulting map still as
f
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The differential of mapping
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\setminus\Gamma\to L{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is everywhere injective.

Proof.
We need to prove this for the original
f
as in (
4.10
) since
a composition with an automorphism doesn’t affect the injectivity. Take
z^{0}=(z_{1}^{0},z_{2}^{0})\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma
such that
z_{1}^{0}-\gamma_{1}(s)
never vanishes. Then

f(z,s)=[z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s):z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)]

takes its values in
U_{0}=\{[w]=[w_{0}:w_{1}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}:w_{0}\not=0\}
for
z
in some neighbourhood

U\ni z^{0}
. I.e.,

f(z,s)=\frac{z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)}{z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)}

represents a holomorphic map
f:U\to W^{1,2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1},{\mathbb{C}})
. Now for
z\in U
and
{\mathrm{v}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

one has

df_{z}[{\mathrm{v}}]=\frac{\partial f(z,s)}{\partial z_{1}}{\mathrm{v}}_{1}+\frac{\partial f(z,s)}{\partial z_{2}}{\mathrm{v}}_{2}=\left(s,\frac{z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)}{[z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)]^{2}}{\mathrm{v}}_{1}+\frac{{\mathrm{v}}_{2}}{z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)}\right).

(4.11)

Here the right hand side of (
4.11
) should be understood as a section of the pulled-back
tangent bundle
f^{*}(z,\cdot)T{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\equiv{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{C}}
. To prove the injectivity of
df_{z}

assume that
df_{z}[{\mathrm{v}}]=0
for some
{\mathrm{v}}\not=0
. Then

\frac{z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)}{[z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)]^{2}}{\mathrm{v}}_{1}+\frac{{\mathrm{v}}_{2}}{z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)}=0

for all
s
. And this means that

\frac{z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)}{z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)}{\mathrm{v}}_{1}+{\mathrm{v}}_{2}=0.

Since
{\mathrm{v}}\not=0
this implies that
\frac{z_{2}-\gamma_{2}(s)}{z_{1}-\gamma_{1}(s)}={\mathsf{const}}
and
therefore
\gamma_{2}(s)-{\mathsf{c}}_{1}\gamma_{1}(s)={\mathsf{c}}_{2}
for some constants
{\mathsf{c}}_{1},{\mathsf{c}}_{2}
and all

.
I.e.,
\Gamma
is contained in the complex line
C=\{z_{2}{\mathsf{c}}_{1}-z_{1}={\mathsf{c}}_{2}\}
and henceforth
cannot be polynomially convex. Contradiction. Analogously one proves that
df_{z}
is injective
in an neighbourhood of any point
z^{0}=(z_{1}^{0},z_{2}^{0})
such that
z_{2}^{0}-\gamma_{2}(s)
never vanishes.

We proved that
df_{z}
can degenerate for
z
in a complex curve
C\subset\left(\gamma_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})\times\gamma_{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})\right)\setminus\Gamma
at most. But

\gamma_{1}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})\times\gamma_{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{1})
is totally real and therefore
C=\varnothing
.

\square

Lemma 4.3
.

After an appropriate perturbation
\tilde{\gamma}
of

\tilde{f}

df_{p}[{\mathbb{C}}^{2}]\pitchfork{\mathsf{Ker}}\,dg_{q}

Proof.
Injectivity of

(4.12)

are linearly independent and generate
