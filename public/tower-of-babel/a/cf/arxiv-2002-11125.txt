Modular Exercises for Four-Point Blocks – I
Miranda C.N. CHENG a, Terry GANNON b and Guglielmo LOCKHART cd

Abstract
The well-known modular property of the torus characters and torus partition functions of (rational) vertex operator algebras (VOAs) and 2d conformal field theories (CFTs) has been an invaluable tool for studying this class of theories. In this work we prove that sphere four-point chiral blocks of rational VOAs are vector-valued modular forms for the groups Γ ⁡ ( 2 ) \Gamma(2) , Γ 0 ​ ( 2 ) \Gamma_{0}(2) , or SL 2 ​ ( ℤ ) \mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) . Moreover, we prove that the four-point correlators, combining the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic chiral blocks, are modular invariant. In particular, in this language the crossing symmetries are simply modular symmetries. This gives the possibility of exploiting the available techniques and knowledge about modular forms to determine or constrain the physically interesting quantities such as chiral blocks and fusion coefficients, which we illustrate with a few examples. We also highlight the existence of a sphere-torus correspondence equating the sphere quantities of certain theories 𝒯 s {\mathcal{T}}_{s} with the torus quantities of another family of theories 𝒯 t {\mathcal{T}}_{t} . A companion paper will delve into more examples and explore more systematically this sphere-torus duality.

Introduction

Modular forms have been useful since they were known to humankind. Specifically, in the study of two-dimensional conformal field theories (CFTs) and closely related vertex operator algebras (VOAs, often referred to as chiral algebras), modular forms have played an important role. On the VOA side, the torus characters of
\mathcal{V}
-modules are well known to transform as components of a vector-valued modular form (vvmf), when the VOA is rational and satisfies the so-called
C_{2}
co-finite condition.
Moreover, the
S
-matrix of the corresponding modular group representation determines certain crucial data of the VOA – the fusion coefficients – via the celebrated Verlinde formula (
4.2
).
When combining the chiral and anti-chiral theories into a physical CFT, the torus partition function is famously modular invariant.
The role of modular forms in this case can be understood through the role of
the modular group
{\rm SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})

as the mapping class group of the torus with one puncture. The modularity poses stringent constraints on the spectrum of the theories and has been very valuable in exploring the existence of potential theories
[
1
,
8
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
33
,
37
,
41
]
.

On the other hand, four-point conformal blocks (or
chiral blocks
more generally) are key quantities of a VOA, as the braiding and fusing matrices associated to them essentially determine the representation theory (i.e., the modular tensor category) of the VOA. For physical CFTs, the crossing symmetry of the four-point correlation functions together with the modular invariance on the torus guarantees the consistency of the theory on arbitrary Riemann surfaces. Again, crossing symmetries pose valuable constraints on the theory and form the cornerstone of the conformal bootstrap method.

In this work, we first focus on the chiral blocks and make explicit their relation to modular forms. Namely, we prove that the corresponding
core blocks
, which we define as the part of the chiral blocks that is not
dictated by conformal symmetry and that is invariant under the Dehn twists about punctures, form vector-valued modular forms.
They are modular forms for the modular group
{\rm SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
when at least three of the external insertions are identical; when two of the external insertions are identical, they are modular forms for its subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(2)
, and when none are, they are modular forms for its subgroup
\Gamma(2)
. To be more precise, for a given set of operators
\phi_{1}\in M_{1}
,
\phi_{2}\in M_{2}
,
\phi_{3}\in M_{3}
,
\phi_{4}\in M_{4}
, inserted at the four punctures, we obtain a vector-valued modular form, with each component labelled by a module
P
(the
“internal channel”
) – cf. Figure
1
.

Note that we consider not just the cases of Virasoro blocks but also general chiral blocks when the chiral algebra is an extension of the Virasoro algebra, as is almost always the case. In the case of Virasoro blocks, the relation between four-point conformal blocks and modular forms is already implicit in the work by Zamolodchikov
[
77
]
. For generic CFTs, each chiral block will involve the contributions from infinitely many Virasoro families (Virasoro primaries and their Virasoro descendents) which are related through the action of the chiral algebra. Here, the appearance of the modular group can again be understood via its relation with the relevant mapping class group (cf. Section
3
), or alternatively via the geometric picture of a torus as a double cover of a sphere with four punctures (cf. Section
6
).
Subsequently, we consider the four-point correlation function by assembling the chiral and anti-chiral sides and appropriately summing over internal channels. Using the language of modular forms, what we do is to combine holomorphic and anti-holomorphic vector-valued modular forms into a modular invariant, non-holomorphic function. In other words, in the language of modular forms the crossing symmetry is nothing but the modular symmetry of the correlation function (with a universal factor removed). This is of course extremely reminiscent of the familiar relation between 2d CFT and modular forms when torus blocks and torus partition functions are considered.

In fact, in some instances the analogy to torus modularity runs deeper. Namely, we find that for certain VOAs
\mathcal{V}_{s}
the four-point core blocks associated to a specific choice of external operators
M_{*}
coincide with the characters of a different VOA
\mathcal{V}_{t}
. Moreover, one can combine a chiral- and anti-chiral copy of the VOAs
{\mathcal{V}}_{s}
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{t}
into full CFTs
{\mathcal{T}}_{s}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{t}
, and arrive at a CFT version of the correspondence that is also outlined in Table
1
, where we write
\ast:=\bigl(M_{\ast},\widetilde{M_{\ast}}\bigr)
. We stress that for the same
\mathcal{V}_{s}
the core blocks for different choices of “twist field”
\mathcal{M}_{*}
may be in correspondence with the characters of different VOAs
\mathcal{V}_{t}
. This is the case for the Ising model, for which the four-point core block associated to the
h=\tfrac{1}{2}
operator
\epsilon
coincides with the characters of the
E_{8,1}
Kac–Moody algebra, while the four-point blocks associated to the
h=\tfrac{1}{16}
operator
\sigma
coincide with the characters of the
A_{1,1}
Kac–Moody algebra.

Clearly, the versatile and powerful arsenal of modular forms should find applications in the computation of sphere four-point chiral blocks and the four-point correlators, just like it has been applied to the torus characters/partition functions to draw conclusions on a wide range of properties of 2d VOAs and CFTs. Indeed, while different techniques such as Zamolodchikov’s recursion relation and the BPZ/KZ equations can be employed to determine the conformal blocks for different classes of theories, one of the major advantages of our approach is that it applies uniformly to Virasoro minimal models as well as to any rational conformal field theory with extended chiral algebras, and even to non-rational examples such as Liouville theory with one degenerate external operator, as we will see in Section
8

The plan of the paper is as follows.
In Section
2
, we list the various notations and symbols used in the paper.
In Section

, we introduce the various discrete groups related to the mapping class group of spheres with four punctures and clarify the relation among them.
In Section

In Section

In particular, in Theorem

In Section

In Section

In Section
8
, we provide some examples illustrating the relevance of modular forms in four-point chiral blocks and correlators.
In Section
9

We also include three appendices.
In Appendix
A
, the relation between modular differential equations and BPZ equations is exemplified by a detailed treatment in the case of the second-order BPZ equation.
In Appendix
B
, we collect several facts about the braiding and fusing operators that we make use of in the main text. In Appendix
C

While we were preparing this manuscript we learned that
[
56
]
has also developed an approach that exploits modularity to constrain the correlators of Virasoro minimal models (the same approach has recently also been extended to WZW models
[
55
]
). We thank the authors of
[
56
]
for considering to coordinate the arXiv submission. While the basic idea of exploiting the relation of the mapping class group to modular groups is the same as for us, the approach and scope of these papers differs significantly. On the one hand, in this paper we develop an approach to determine (holomorphic) conformal blocks of arbitrary rational vertex operator algebras by exploiting their transformations as vector-valued modular forms; physical CFT correlators are then constructed from bilinear combinations of conformal blocks by imposing modular invariance on them. On the other hand,
[
55
,
56
]
work directly at the level of the full CFT, and determine candidate, crossing symmetry invariant, correlation functions by taking a specific choice of conformal blocks as input data and averaging over its images under
{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
The focus there is on determining structure constants consistent with crossing symmetry. We comment further on the overlap between these approaches in Section
8
when we consider specific examples.

The above presentation of the full mapping class group

The subgroup

is generated by the

This subgroup corresponds

arbitrarily:
\overline{\sigma}_{1}

In terms of the cross ratio, the above generators act as

{\overline{\sigma}_{T}\colon\ w\mapsto{w\over w-1},\qquad\overline{\sigma}_{S}\colon\ w\mapsto 1-w}.

Of course, more important is the fact that

Note that

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}_{S}:=\overline{\sigma}_{3}\overline{\sigma}_{2}\overline{\sigma}_{3}=\tilde{\sigma}_{3}\tilde{\sigma}_{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{3}.

The subgroup
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

An element

In CFT, a fundamental operation is sewing together surfaces at the punctures. This requires us to specify additional data. This can be done in many ways, but most convenient for the VOA language is Vafa’s picture

\displaystyle f_{i}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a^{(i)}_{n}(z-z_{i})^{n}\qquad\text{when $z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}$ and}

\displaystyle f_{i}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a^{(i)}_{n}z^{-n}\qquad\text{when $z_{i}=\infty$},

Conformal equivalences between these extended surfaces (i.e., surfaces with local coordinates at punctures) can be defined in the obvious way, giving an extended moduli space

To any chiral datum

A standard way to determine the space

Let

restricts to a representation of the Virasoro algebra contained in

As mentioned earlier, the full mapping class group permutes the punctures. So if for example, the modules

We will shortly see several examples of how chiral blocks are built from the intertwiners. Here we recall some of their basic properties.
Being (components of) quantum fields, they are operator-valued distributions on the sphere, and as such cannot be evaluated at a point.

Now, we can relate

Torus one-point functions.
The most familiar example of chiral blocks are the 1-point functions on the torus, i.e., the case
{\mathcal{D}}=(1,1;{\mathcal{V}})
. The surface
\Sigma
here is the torus
\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\tau\mathbb{Z})
. We can take the puncture
z_{1}
to be at

f_{1}(z)=[z]\in\Sigma

0\in\mathbb{C}
to a small patch about
[0]\in\Sigma

In this case, the dimension of the space of the chiral blocks is given by (

\sum_{M\in\Upsilon}1=\|\Upsilon\|

This suggests that a basis of the space

for each
{M\in\Upsilon}

\chi_{M}(\phi,\tau)=\operatorname{e}(kz)\operatorname{tr}_{M}Y^{M}(\phi,\operatorname{e}(z)-1)q^{L_{0}-c/24}=q^{h_{M}-c/24}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{tr}_{M(n)}o(\phi)q^{n}.

Here and elsewhere, we write
\operatorname{e}(x):={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}x}
for

. Moreover,
q=\operatorname{e}(\tau)
where
\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau)
is our torus,

is the central charge of
{\mathcal{V}}
, and

is the conformal weight as before.

is the vertex operator.
Implicit in (

, which is the source of the pervasive
-c/24

denote the new Virasoro operators, whose corresponding conformal vector is proportional to
\omega-c/24

The appearance of

They are the components of a

\chi_{M}

\displaystyle{\underline{\chi}(\phi,\tau)=\tau^{-k}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\underline{\chi}\bigl(\phi,-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\bigr),}

\mathbf{T}=R_{(1,1;{\mathcal{V}})}\bigl(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\

The origin of the aforementioned modular property can be understood in the following way.
The extended mapping class group

5.1
Definition and basic properties

Given a chiral block, we take its corresponding
core block
to be the part capturing the non-trivial information that only depends on the conformal equivalence classes of Riemann surfaces, and is independent of the Dehn twists about the punctures. In particular, a core block will be a multilinear map
\varphi\colon M_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}M_{n}\rightarrow\Psi_{\mathcal{D}}
to the space of meromorphic sections of
E_{\mathcal{D}}
. In analogy to the chiral blocks, for a choice of states
\phi_{i}\in M_{i}
, we denote the space of core blocks at
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})
by
{{\mathfrak{F}}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})
. The core blocks are sensitive only to the most trivial choices of local coordinates
f_{i}(z)
. In particular, they transform with respect to the mapping class group, and do not see the extra parts of the extended mapping class group corresponding to the Dehn twists.

As we have seen in Section
4
, the action of Dehn twists is trivial on the torus one-point functions, and therefore the definition of core and chiral blocks is identical in that case. The same is true for the sphere one-point functions. We therefore focus on the cases
(g,n)=(0,n)
with
{n\geq 2}
. The effect on the chiral blocks of the Dehn twist about the
i
-th puncture will be multiplication by
\operatorname{e}(h_{M_{i}})
, where
h_{M_{i}}
is the conformal weight (the minimal
L_{0}
-eigenvalue) of
M_{i}
. These give rise to the factor
\prod_{i}{\rm d}z_{i}^{h_{M_{i}}}
in the chiral blocks, which we will drop when considering the core blocks. The
(0,n)
-chiral blocks moreover depend on the local coordinates
z_{i}
at the punctures through factors of the form
(z_{i}-z_{j})^{d_{ij}}
for quantities
d_{ij}
depending on the
h_{M_{i}}
; dropping those factors as well gives the core blocks. We will describe this more explicitly and illustrate it with a few examples.

First of all, in the case
n=2
, we have seen in Section
4
that the space of chiral blocks
\mathfrak{F}_{(M_{1},M_{2})}(\phi,\psi^{*})
has dimension 1 only if
M_{1}^{*}=M_{2}=M
,
\phi\in M(k)
and
\psi^{*}\in M^{*}(k)
, in which case the chiral block is given by equation (
4.10
), i.e.,

\displaystyle z_{21}^{-2h_{M}-2k}\psi^{*}(\phi)\,{\rm d}z_{1}^{\prime\,\bar{h}}{\rm d}z_{2}^{\prime\,\bar{h}}.

Otherwise, the space of chiral blocks has dimension zero. The Dehn twist-independent part, which we take as our definition of core block, is then simply

\displaystyle\varphi_{\phi,\psi^{\ast}}:=\psi^{*}(\phi).

Next, consider the case
n=3
. Here, the only terms in the chiral block that do not depend on Dehn twists are the structure constants (equation (
4.12
)), which we take as our definition of the core blocks

\displaystyle{\varphi}^{\Phi}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}}:=c_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}}^{\Phi}={z_{2}^{\prime\bar{h}_{2}+\bar{h}_{3}-\bar{h}_{1}}}\bigl\langle\phi_{1}\big|\Phi\bigl(\phi_{2},z_{2}^{\prime}\bigr)\phi_{3}\bigr\rangle.

We can combine these into a vector

of three-point core blocks.

Finally, let us turn to the case of greatest interest, namely the four-point core blocks. Fixing Virasoro-primaries

\displaystyle\qquad\underline{\varphi}{}_{C}(\widetilde{w})={\mathcal{S}}^{-1}_{C}\cdot\underline{\varphi}{}_{C}(\bar{\sigma}_{S}\widetilde{w}),

where the matrices
{\mathcal{T}}_{B}
,
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{C}
,
{\mathcal{S}}_{C}
are defined in Appendix
C
.

In case
(\text{B})
, the group
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
acts on the vector-valued function
\underline{\varphi}(\widetilde{w})=\underline{\varphi}{}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{3}}(\widetilde{w})
by
\underline{\varphi}(\widetilde{w})={\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\varphi}{}(\bar{\sigma}_{T}\widetilde{w})
and
\underline{\varphi}{}(\widetilde{w})={\mathcal{R}}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\varphi}{}(\bar{\sigma}_{R}\widetilde{w})
. In case
(\text{C})
, the group
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
acts on the vector-valued function
\underline{\varphi}(\widetilde{w})=\underline{\varphi}{}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(\widetilde{w})
by
\underline{\varphi}(\widetilde{w})=\bigl({\mathcal{T}}^{2}\bigr){}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\varphi}{}(\bar{\sigma}_{T}\bar{\sigma}_{T}\widetilde{w})
and
\underline{\varphi}{}(\widetilde{w})={\mathcal{U}}^{-1}\cdot\underline{\varphi}{}(\bar{\sigma}_{U}\widetilde{w})
.

In cases (A) and (B), one verifies straightforwardly that these generators satisfy the
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
relations

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{2}=({\mathcal{T}}\cdot{\mathcal{S}})^{3}=1,\qquad{\mathcal{R}}^{2}=1

(the generators
{\mathcal{T}}^{2}
and
{\mathcal{U}}
for

), which follow from equations (

)–(

). That
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{B}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{C}
,

satisfy the

.

6
Modular forms

In this section, we review the basic properties of the relevant modular objects. Subsequently, we discuss the properties of core blocks as modular forms and how such properties can be employed to determine the core blocks.

6.1
Vector-valued modular forms

From Section
5.3
, it follows that the type of modular object that is most relevant to us is the vector-valued modular forms. In this subsection, we include some general comments on vvmf’s that will be useful later.

6.1.1
Modular forms for
\boldsymbol{{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

The modular group
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, or its quotient
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})={\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/\{\pm\}
which is what we will use, is genus zero. We say that a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
of
{\rm SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
is
genus zero
if the quotient
\Gamma\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
is a Riemann surface of genus zero minus finitely many points. This means that the (meromorphic) modular functions form the field of rational functions
\mathbb{C}(j(\tau))
in the
j
-function, with expansion

j(\tau)=q^{-1}+744+196884q+21493760q^{2}+\cdots,

(6.1)

in terms of the local parameter
q=\mathrm{e}(\tau)
near the cusp
\tau={\rm i}\infty
.
By a
weakly holomorphic
modular form or function,
we mean one which is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}
but with poles allowed at the cusps. The weakly holomorphic modular functions form the ring
\mathbb{C}[j(\tau)]
of polynomials in
j(\tau)
.

The (holomorphic) modular forms form the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[E_{4}(\tau),E_{6}(\tau)]
,
where

\displaystyle E_{n}(\tau)=1-\frac{4n}{B_{2n}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2n-1}q^{k}}{1-q^{k}}

is the weight-
n
Eisenstein series satisfying (for
n>2
)

\displaystyle E_{n}(\tau)=E_{n}(\tau+1),\qquad E_{n}(\tau)=\tau^{-n}E_{n}\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\bigr).

An important modular form is the discriminant form

\displaystyle\Delta(\tau)=\eta(\tau)^{24}=\frac{E_{4}(\tau)^{3}-E_{6}(\tau)^{2}}{1728},

where

\displaystyle\eta(\tau)=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})

is the Dedekind eta function, which transforms under
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
as

\eta(\tau)=\mathrm{e}\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{24}\bigr)\eta(\tau+1),\qquad\eta(\tau)=(-{\rm i}\tau)^{-{1\over 2}}\eta\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\bigr).

(6.2)

The importance of
\Delta(\tau)
lies in the fact that it has no zeroes in
\mathbb{H}
.

The modular transformations of
E_{2}(\tau)
are a little more complicated – it is a
quasi-modular
form. It appears in the differential modular (Serre) derivative

D_{(k)}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}-\frac{k}{12}E_{2}(\tau),

(6.3)

which takes weight-
k
(vector valued) modular forms to weight-
(k+2)
vvmf’s. In several equations below we employ the differential operator

D_{(0)}^{\ell}:=D_{(2\ell-2)}\circ\cdots\circ D_{(2)}\circ D_{(0)},

(6.4)

which takes a weight-0 vvmf to a weight-
2\ell
vvmf.

The Dedekind eta function is a modular form for
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

(
of weight
\frac{1}{2}
)
, but with nontrivial multiplier (a projective representation of
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with values in 24th roots of 1). More generally, we can speak of a
vector-valued modular form

\underline{f}(\tau)
for
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of weight
k
and multiplier
\rho
: here,
\rho
is a representation of
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
(merely projective, if
k\not\in 2\mathbb{Z}
) by
d\times d
matrices for some
d
called the rank,
\underline{f}(\tau)
takes values in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and they obey

\displaystyle\underline{f}(\tau)=(c\tau+d)^{-k}\rho\bigl(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)^{-1}\underline{f}\biggl(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\biggr).

We call
\underline{f}(\tau)

weakly holomorphic
if each component
f_{i}(\tau)
is weakly holomorphic.

Corresponding to a representation
\rho
of
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we can define a space of weight-zero weakly holomorphic vvmf’s, which we will denote by
\mathcal{M}^{!}_{0}(\rho)
.
Furthermore, for any
d
-tuple
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}(\rho;\lambda)\subset{\mathcal{M}}^{!}_{0}(\rho)
the subspace

{\mathcal{M}}_{0}(\rho;\lambda):=\bigl\{\underline{f}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{!}_{0}(\rho)\mid q^{-\lambda_{i}}f_{i}(\tau)={\mathcal{O}}(1)\text{ for all }i,\text{ as }\tau\to{\rm i}\infty\bigr\},

(6.5)

where
T=\rho\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
is assumed to be diagonal.
Note that there is a natural filtration among these spaces:
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}(\rho;\lambda^{\prime})\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{0}(\rho;\lambda)
whenever
\lambda^{\prime}\geq\lambda
component-wise.

The properties of vvmf’s can then be employed to help to compute the precise form of the core blocks. In the remainder of this paper we will describe this application in some detail. The scalar case
\dim(\rho)=1
is particularly simple: any element of
\mathcal{M}^{!}_{0}(\rho)
can be expressed as a polynomial in the
j
-function (
6.1
) multiplying one of the following functions
[
10
]
:

1,\qquad\frac{E_{4}(\tau)}{\eta(\tau)^{8}},\qquad\frac{E_{6}(\tau)}{\eta(\tau)^{12}},\qquad\frac{E_{8}(\tau)}{\eta(\tau)^{16}},\qquad\frac{E_{10}(\tau)}{\eta(\tau)^{20}},\qquad\frac{E_{14}(\tau)}{\eta(\tau)^{28}}.

(6.6)

Because
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is generated by
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\hphantom{-}0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
and
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, the multiplier
\rho
is uniquely determined by the values
{\mathcal{S}}:=\rho\bigl(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\hphantom{-}0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)
and

{\mathcal{T}}:=\rho\bigl(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)
. We are primarily interested in the cases where the corresponding
{\mathcal{T}}
matrices are diagonal. Then the condition of weakly holomorphic reduces to requiring that there is a diagonal matrix
\lambda
such that
{\mathcal{T}}=\operatorname{e}(\lambda)
and

\displaystyle\underline{f}(\tau)=q^{\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\underline{f}[n]q^{n}.

The coefficients
\underline{f}[n]\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
will in general not be integral – integrality is expected (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
) only when the kernel of
\rho
contains a congruence subgroup

\Gamma(N):=\bigl\{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\mid\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\mod N\bigr\}.

(6.7)

6.1.2
Modular forms for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma(2)}

The congruence group
\Gamma(2)
(recall (
6.7
)), as well as its quotient
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)=\Gamma(2)/\{\pm 1\}
, are also genus-0. The role of
j(\tau)
is played in this case by

\lambda(\tau)=\frac{\theta_{2}(\tau)^{4}}{\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}}=16q^{1/2}-128q+704q^{3/2}-3072q^{2}+11488q^{5/2}-38400q^{3}+\cdots\,,

(6.8)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{\frac{1}{2}(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}},\qquad\theta_{3}(\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}},\qquad\theta_{4}(\tau)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}q^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}},

transform under
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
as a weight
\frac{1}{2}
vvmf:

\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{2}\\
\theta_{3}\\
\theta_{4}\end{pmatrix}(\tau)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}\bigl(-\frac{1}{8}\bigr)\theta_{2}\\
\theta_{4}\\
\theta_{3}\end{pmatrix}(\tau+1),\qquad\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{2}\\
\theta_{3}\\
\theta_{4}\end{pmatrix}(\tau)=(-{\rm i}\tau)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{4}\\
\theta_{3}\\
\theta_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\bigr).

(6.9)

Recall that
\theta_{2}
,
\theta_{3}
,
\theta_{4}
do not vanish in
\mathbb{H}
, and (holomorphic) modular forms for
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
form the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}\bigl[\theta_{3}^{4},\theta_{4}^{4}\bigr]
. A useful identity among these functions is
\theta_{2}^{4}=\theta_{3}^{4}-\theta_{4}^{4}
.

The group
\Gamma(2)
is index 6 in
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
:
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma(2)\cong\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/({\rm P}\Gamma(2))\cong{\rm Sym}(3)
. The function
\lambda(\tau)
is related to the
j
-function
as follows:

j(\tau)=256\frac{\bigl(\lambda(\tau)^{2}-\lambda(\tau)+1\bigr)^{3}}{\lambda(\tau)^{2}(1-\lambda(\tau))^{2}}.

(6.10)

The group
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
has 3 cusps, namely 0, 1 and
{\rm i}\infty
, and it is necessary to have control on how the modular forms behave at all 3 cusps. One way to do this is to use
\tau\mapsto-1/\tau
and
{\tau\to-1/(\tau-1)}
to map the other cusps to
{\rm i}\infty
. For example, applying these to
\lambda
shows that
{\lambda=1}
at
\tau=0
and has a pole at
\tau=1
. For
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
, weakly holomorphic functions are defined as before: they are holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}
and have at worse poles at the 3 cusps. For example, the weakly holomorphic modular functions form the ring
\mathbb{C}\bigl[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},1/(1-\lambda)\bigr]
. Note that
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
, like any subgroup of
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, inherits the same modular derivative
D_{(k)}
.

Next we discuss the multiplier for a
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
vector-valued modular form.
The multiplier
\rho
is a representation.
Recall that
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
is freely generated by
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
and
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\hphantom{-}1&0\\
-2&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
.
As a result,
\rho
is uniquely determined by its values
\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)
and
\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\hphantom{-}1&0\\
-2&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)
. In the one-dimensional case,
\rho=\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
is specified by a pair
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, where

\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)=\alpha
and
{\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\hphantom{-}1&0\\
-2&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)=\beta}
. Writing
{\alpha=\operatorname{e}(a)}
and
\beta=\operatorname{e}(b)
, we see that the space of weakly holomorphic vvmf of weight 0 for
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
takes the form
\lambda^{a}(1-\lambda)^{b}\mathbb{C}\bigl[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},1/(1-\lambda)\bigr]
. To understand this, note that
\theta_{2}(\tau)
,
\theta_{3}(\tau)
,
\theta_{4}(\tau)
have no zeros or poles in the simply-connected region
\mathbb{H}
, and hence they have holomorphic logarithms there. Being the composition of holomorphic functions, functions such as
\lambda(\tau)^{a}=\exp(4a\log(\theta_{2}(\tau))-4a\log(\theta_{3}(\tau)))
are themselves holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}
.
Finally, in Appendix
C
we explain how a vvmf for
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
can induce up to one for
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

6.1.3
Modular forms for
\boldsymbol{\Gamma_{0}(2)}

The group
\Gamma_{0}(2)=\bigl\{\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\mid c\in 2\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}
is index 3 in
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and is generated by
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
and an order two element
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
2&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
. As before, we are more interested in
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
. It is genus zero, with the role of
j(\tau)
being played by

\displaystyle\kappa(\tau)=\frac{\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}\theta_{4}(\tau)^{4}}{\theta_{2}(\tau)^{8}}=\frac{1}{256}q^{-1}-\frac{3}{32}+\frac{69}{64}q-128q^{2}+\frac{5601}{128}q^{3}-\cdots.

Holomorphic modular forms for
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
form the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}\bigl[E_{4},\theta_{3}^{4}+\theta_{4}^{4}\bigr]
.

The group
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
has 2 cusps, namely at 0 and
{\rm i}\infty
. Note that
\kappa=0
at
\tau=0
, and hence the weakly holomorphic modular functions form the ring
\mathbb{C}\bigl[\kappa,\kappa^{-1}\bigr]
. The space of holomorphic vvmf forms a module over that ring.
The group
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
is generated by
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
and
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
2&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, where the latter has order 2, so the multiplier
\rho
of any vvmf (of even weight) is uniquely determined by the matrices
\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)
and
\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
2&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)
(the latter squaring to
I
). In Appendix
C
we explain how a vvmf for
{\rm P}\Gamma_{0}(2)
induces up to one for
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

The one-dimensional representations are
\rho_{\alpha,\sigma}
where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
and
\sigma=\pm 1
:
\rho_{\alpha,\sigma}\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)=\alpha
and
\rho\bigl(\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
2&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\bigr)=\sigma
. Writing

\alpha=\operatorname{e}(a)
and
\sigma=(-1)^{s}
for
s\in\{0,1\}
, we find that the space of weakly holomorphic vvmf of weight 0 for
\rho_{\alpha,\sigma}
are
\kappa^{-a}\bigl(1+\frac{1}{4\kappa}\bigr)^{s/2}\mathbb{C}\bigl[\kappa,\kappa^{-1}\bigr]
. To see this, note that
\kappa
can be raised to arbitrary powers by the same argument as at the end of the last subsection; the minus sign in the factor
\kappa^{-a}
arises because
\kappa
has a pole and not a zero at
\tau={\rm i}\infty
. The elliptic element
\pm\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
2&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
fixes
\tau=\frac{1+{\rm i}}{2}
and
\kappa\bigl(\frac{1+{\rm i}}{2}\bigr)=-\frac{1}{4}
, so
\kappa+\frac{1}{4}
must have a double-zero at
\tau=\frac{1+{\rm i}}{2}
. Being a uniformizing function for
\mathbb{H}/\Gamma_{0}(2)
, the only zeros and poles of
\kappa+\frac{1}{4}
can be at the obvious spots, namely the orbits of
\frac{1+{\rm i}}{2}
and
{\rm i}\infty
. Thus
\sqrt{\kappa+\smash{\frac{1}{4}}\vphantom{A^{1}}}
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{H}
.

6.2
Core blocks as vector-valued modular forms

As we have seen in the previous section, the core blocks transform in
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
representations which are completely determined provided that the braiding and fusing matrices of the RCFT are given. This
{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
action is displayed very naturally by passing to the double-cover of the four-punctured sphere, which is a torus. By a change of variable, one can express the chiral blocks in terms of the torus complex structure
\tau=\widetilde{w}

[
77
]
, which is related to the cross-ratio as follows:

w=\lambda(\tau).

(6.11)

The function
w=\lambda(\tau)
is the Hauptmodul identifying
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}

(
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with three points removed
)
with
\Gamma(2)\backslash\mathbb{H}
, which maps the three cusps at
\tau={\rm i}\infty,0,1
to the three points
w=0,1,\infty
, respectively.

It is easy to see that the identification (
6.11
) is consistent with the action of
\Gamma^{0,4}\cong{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
via
(
3.7
), when the action of
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
on
{\mathbb{H}}
is taken to be the usual
\tau\mapsto\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}
. To see this, note that
\bar{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
sends
z_{12}\mapsto\operatorname{e}(1)z_{12}
while leaving the other
z_{ij}
invariant, and similarly for
\bar{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
and
z_{41}
.

As alluded to above, the map (
6.11
) has a simple geometric origin in terms of the torus that is the double cover of the four puncture sphere. Namely, the hyper-elliptic equation
{y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-\lambda)}
defines an isomorphism class of an elliptic curve together with a basis of the group of its 2-torsion points, and hence an orbit
[\tau]\in\Gamma(2)\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
.
Conversely, given two periods
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
with
\tau=\omega_{1}/\omega_{2}
, the points
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\in{\mathbb{C}}
given by

\displaystyle e_{1}=\wp\biggl(\frac{\omega_{2}}{2}\biggr),\qquad e_{2}=\wp\biggl(\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{2}\biggr),\qquad e_{3}=\wp\biggl(\frac{\omega_{1}}{2}\biggr),

together with the point at infinity, have cross-ratio

\displaystyle\lambda(\tau)={e_{1}-e_{2}\over e_{1}-e_{3}}.

A more direct way to motivate the identification (
6.11
) of
\widetilde{w}
with
\tau
, is the realization that the moduli space of 4-punctured spheres is the 3-punctured sphere (namely the location of the 4-th point, or the cross-ratio, after the first 3 are moved to
0
,
1
,
\infty
). The 3-punctured sphere has universal cover
\mathbb{H}
and mapping class group
F_{2}\cong{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
:
{\rm P}\Gamma(2)
is genus zero with 3 cusps and no elliptic points. Then Theorem
5.3
becomes:

Theorem 6.1
.

The core blocks
\underline{\varphi}{}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}
are single-valued functions on
\mathbb{H}
via the identification (
6.11
). Moreover, they are weight zero vector-valued modular forms for multipliers given in Theorem
5.3
.

After establishing the single-valuedness as above, we will often write

\displaystyle\underline{\varphi}{}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(\widetilde{w})=\underline{\varphi}{}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(\tau)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varphi^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(\widetilde{w})=\varphi^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(\tau)

to denote the (vector of) core blocks considered as (vector-valued) functions on
{\mathbb{H}}
.

6.3
Methods for constructing vector-valued modular forms

For vvmf’s of general dimensions, three methods for constructing vector-valued modular forms have appeared in the literature which we review next. In Section
6.3.4
, we present a new hybrid method which combines two of the existing techniques and is more effective in more sophisticated examples. For concreteness, we will restrict our discussion to
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, although all the approaches we discuss should generalize. We also mention that for
d\leq 5
the (equivalence classes of) irreducible representations are uniquely determined by their
T
eigenvalues
[
71
]
, which leads to useful simplifications in explicit calculations.

6.3.1
Rademacher sum

The simplest method is the Rademacher sum of
[
25
,
57
]
(related are the Poincaré series of
[
48
]
).
Let
\rho
be a representation for
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of dimension
d
, with diagonal
\rho(T)
with
entries
\rho(T)_{j,j}=\mathrm{e}(\lambda_{j})
for
0\leq\lambda_{j}<1
.
Suppose

\displaystyle X_{j}(\tau)=q^{\lambda_{j}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}X_{j}[n]q^{n},\qquad 1\leq j\leq d

are the components of a weakly holomorphic vector-valued modular form of weight 0 with multiplier
\rho
, with
each
X_{j}[n]\in\mathbb{C}
and
X_{j}[n]\neq 0
for all but finitely many
n<0
.
For any pair of coprime integers
(c,d)
, there will be infinitely many
\gamma\in{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

with bottom row
(c\;d)
; let
{\gamma_{cd}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)}
be any one of these. Define the
principal part
of
X_{j}(\tau)
to be

{\mathcal{P}}[X_{j}(\tau)]=q^{\lambda_{j}}\sum_{n+\lambda_{j}<0}X_{j}[n]q^{n}.

(6.12)

Given the principal part of
X_{j}(\tau)
, one has
[
48
,
69
,
74
]

\displaystyle X_{j}[n]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in{\mathbb{Z}},\\
m+\lambda_{i}<0\end{subarray}}-(m+\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\hphantom{X_{j}[n]=}{}\times\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{c^{2}}\mathrm{Kl}(n,j,m,i;c)X_{i}[m]{I}\biggl(-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{c^{2}}{(m+\lambda_{i})(n+\lambda_{j})}\biggr),

(6.13)

where
I
is given in terms of the Bessel function as

\displaystyle{I}(x)=\sqrt{x}^{-1}I_{1}\bigl(2\sqrt{x}\bigr)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k!(k+1)!}=1+\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x^{2}}{12}+\frac{x^{3}}{144}+\cdots

and where the generalised Kloosterman sum Kl is given by

6.3.3
Modular linear differential equation

The oldest available method (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
58
,
60
,
62
]
) is the method of Wronskians, now more commonly known as the method of modular linear differential equations (MLDE). The idea is that, if one has a
d
-dimensional vector-valued modular form
X(\tau)
(which for the purposes of our paper we take to be weakly holomorphic and of weight 0), then the vanishing of

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}\begin{pmatrix}f&f^{\prime}&f^{\prime\prime}&\cdots&f^{(d)}\cr X_{1}&X_{1}^{\prime}&X_{1}^{\prime\prime}&\cdots&X_{1}^{(d)}\cr X_{2}&X_{2}^{\prime}&X_{2}^{\prime\prime}&\cdots&X_{2}^{(d)}\cr\vdots&&&&\vdots\cr X_{d}&X_{d}^{\prime}&X_{d}^{\prime\prime}&\cdots&X_{d}^{(d)}\end{pmatrix}

gives an order-
d
differential equation
a_{d}f^{(d)}+a_{d-1}f^{(d-1)}+\cdots+a_{1}f^{\prime}+a_{0}f=0
satisfied by the
d
components
X_{j}
. The coefficients
a_{j}(\tau)
are quasi-modular (i.e., they also involves powers of the second Eisenstein series
E_{2}(\tau)
), but we get something simpler by replacing
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}
and its powers by the modular derivative (
6.3
)
and its powers. Replacing the
\frac{{\rm d}^{l}}{{\rm d}\tau^{l}}
in the Wronskian with
D_{(0)}^{l}
only changes it by a factor of
(2\pi{\rm i})^{d}
, so the expansion

\displaystyle b_{d}D_{(0)}^{d}f+b_{d-1}D_{(0)}^{d-1}f+\cdots+b_{1}D_{(0)}f+b_{0}f=0,

where we have used the notation (
6.4
),
is also satisfied by all components, but now the coefficients
b_{k}(\tau)
will be weakly holomorphic modular forms of weight
d(d+1)-2k
with multiplier
{\rm det}(\rho)
, with principal parts controlled by the
X_{j}
. The spaces of such modular forms are finite-dimensional, so partial information on the
X_{j}
(for instance the first few terms of their
q
-expansions) will suffice to determine the coefficients
b_{j}(\tau)
exactly. We work out a nontrivial example of this method in the next subsection on our hybrid method.

The strength of this method is its simplicity: any vvmf
X(\tau)
is associated to a differential equation with scalar modular forms as coefficients, and the solution space is the span over
\mathbb{C}
of the components
X_{j}(\tau)
. Furthermore, the representation
\rho
need not be unitary, and this method can be generalized to any Fuchsian group.
A drawback of this method is that, at best, this method determines vector-valued modular forms which transform with some multiplier equivalent to
\rho
; moreover it is unclear in general how one can obtain enough partial information of the
X_{j}
to pin down the coefficients
b_{j}(\tau)
.

We end this section by commenting on the role of modular differential equations in the context of core blocks.
It is very well known that conformal blocks involving primaries which have a null state among their descendants at level-
n
is are solutions to an order-
n
differential equation (the most famous examples being the BPZ equation
[
14
]
for Virasoro conformal blocks, and the KZ equation in WZW models
[
47
]
). On the other hand, as we just reviewed,
\ell
-dimensional vector-valued modular forms are solutions to an MLDE of order
\ell
; one therefore expects that differential equations following from the presence of null states can be rephrased as MLDEs for the core blocks. In Appendix
A
, we analyse explicitly this relation in the case of Virasoro blocks, and for a null state at level 2 we rewrite the BPZ equation for the chiral blocks as a second-order MLDE.

Physical correlators

We now turn our attention to the correlation functions of the rational CFT, built out of the physical operators which involve both chiral and anti-chiral degrees of freedom. Correlation functions can always be expressed as sesquilinear combinations of chiral and anti-chiral blocks. In a rational CFT, the physical Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle{{\mathcal{H}}={\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\Upsilon\\
\tilde{M}\in\tilde{\Upsilon}\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{Z}}_{M,\tilde{M}}M\otimes\tilde{M}}},

where the sum is over the finitely many irreducible modules of the left- and right-moving chiral algebras,
{\mathcal{V}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
, and

are non-negative integers corresponding to the multiplicities. Here and everywhere else we use the tilde notation to indicate quantities associated to the right chiral algebra.
The multiplicities determine the modular invariant partition function of the RCFT:

\displaystyle Z_{T^{2}}(\tau,\bar{\tau})={\sum_{M,\tilde{M}}\mathcal{Z}_{M,\tilde{M}}\chi_{M}(\tau)\overline{\chi_{\tilde{M}}(\tau)}},

where
\chi_{M}(\tau)
are the graded dimensions defined in (
4.6

The modular invariance of

(under
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
) requires the matrix
{\mathcal{Z}}_{M,\tilde{M}}
to satisfy

{{\mathcal{Z}}_{M,\tilde{M}}=\bigl(\mathbf{S}\mathcal{Z}\mathbf{\widetilde{S}}^{\dagger}\bigr)_{M,\tilde{M}}=\bigl(\mathbf{T}\mathcal{Z}\mathbf{\widetilde{T}}^{\dagger}\bigr)_{M,\tilde{M}}},

(7.1)

where
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T}

(
resp.
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}
)
are the
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
modular transformation matrices of the chiral (anti-chiral) sectors of the CFT. In particular, for the case
{\mathcal{V}}\cong\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
, the unitary of the

{\mathcal{Z}}
commutes with
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{T}

It was shown in

]
that if

and

are the maximally-extended chiral algebras, then there exists an isomorphism
\Omega

{\mathcal{Z}}_{M,\tilde{M}}=\delta_{M,\Omega(\tilde{M})}.

(7.2)

Without loss of generality, in the following we assume that
{\mathcal{V}}
and

are maximally-extended chiral algebras, which lead
to simpler expressions.

In this paper, we will mainly focus on

8
Examples

In this section, we present a few examples to illustrate the theorems in the main text. While we organize the examples into various classes, we stress that our techniques apply uniformly to all examples, regardless of the class of theories they belong to. For all examples we choose, the conformal blocks will turn out to be elements of a one-dimensional space of vvmf’s, and therefore we will be able to determine them with the braiding matrices of the CFT as the sole input. More general examples and more detailed analysis will be presented in the companion paper
[
3
]
.

8.1
Virasoro minimal models

Our first class of examples arises in Virasoro minimal models. It should be emphasized that these are misleadingly simple: there is no difference between Virasoro-primary and VOA-primaries, the spaces of VOA-primaries are all 1-dimensional, and all fusion coefficients are either
0
or
1
; it is not necessary for these properties to hold in order for our methods to be applicable.

Recall that Virasoro minimal models are parametrized by two positive integers
p>p^{\prime}
. The left and right chiral algebras are just two copies of the Virasoro algebra at central charge

\displaystyle c=1-6\frac{(p-p^{\prime})^{2}}{pp^{\prime}},

possibly extended. More precisely, the left and right chiral algebras are isomorphic to the canonical VOA structure on the irreducible Virasoro module with
h=0
and the given
c
. We denote this chiral algebra as
\mathcal{M}(p,p^{\prime})
. A minimal model is unitary when
p=p^{\prime}+1
.

The minimal model
\mathcal{M}(p,p^{\prime})
possesses the following collection of irreducible modules:

M_{r,s}\qquad\text{with}\quad 1\leq r<p^{\prime},\ 1\leq s<p,

(8.1)

subject to the identification
M_{r,s}=M_{p^{\prime}-r,p-s}
. The conformal weight of
M_{r,s}
is given by

\displaystyle h_{r,s}=\frac{(pr-p^{\prime}s)^{2}-(p-p^{\prime})^{2}}{4p^{\prime}p}.

Furthermore one has
M_{r,s}=M_{r,s}^{*}
,
that is, the charge conjugation matrix
\mathbf{C}
is the identity matrix. We denote the corresponding Virasoro primaries by
\phi_{r,s}=\phi_{p^{\prime}-r,p-s}
.

The fusion rules for the
{\mathcal{M}}(p,p^{\prime})
minimal model are given as follows:

M_{r,s}\otimes M_{m,n}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1+|r-m|\\
k+r+m=1\text{ mod }2\end{subarray}}^{k_{\max}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1+|s-n|\\
l+s+n=1\text{ mod }2\end{subarray}}^{l_{\max}}M_{k,l},

(8.2)

where

\displaystyle k_{\max}=\min(r+m-1,2p^{\prime}-1-r-m),

\displaystyle l_{\max}=\min(s+n-1,2p-1-s-n).

In particular, all fusion coefficients are either
0
or
1
.

Explicit expressions are known for the braiding and fusing matrices of the minimal models
[
27
,
28
]
. In the following examples, we employed the
Mathematica
code provided in
[
30
]
to compute the braiding matrices required to determine the
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
matrices
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}
.

For the
{\mathcal{M}}(p,p^{\prime})
, clearly chiral blocks and conformal blocks coincide. It hence suffices to consider here the insertions to be
\phi_{i}=\phi_{r_{i},s_{i}}
.
Virasoro conformal blocks have been determined by a variety of techniques, including by making explicit use of the Virasoro algebra, by exploiting the Zamolodchikov recursion relations
[
76
]
, or for minimal models by solving the BPZ equation (see Appendix
A
). More recently, Perlmutter has found an explicit solution to Zamolodchikov’s recursion relations
[
68
]
which provides expressions at arbitrary orders in the perturbation series of the Virasoro conformal blocks.
For the discussion that follows, it will be sufficient to know the leading order term in the
q
-expansion of the core blocks. From (

) and (

) we immediately see that

\varphi^{N}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(w)=Cq^{-\frac{h}{6}+\frac{h_{N}}{2}}(1+{\mathcal{O}}(q)),

(8.3)

where
C=c_{M_{1},M_{2},N}c_{N^{\ast},M_{3},M_{4}}2^{8(-\frac{h}{6}+\frac{h_{N}}{2})}
, and

M_{i}=M_{r_{i},s_{i}}
and

.

The examples below demonstrate how the technology developed in the previous sections efficiently determines the Virasoro blocks in terms of vector-valued modular forms, often leading to exact, analytic expressions.

allowing us to organize them into a three-dimensional representation of
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, in complete analogy with the sphere core blocks.

8.3
WZW models

We illustrate our techniques by applying them to the
G={\rm SU}(2)
WZW model at level 1. This model admits a free-field realization in terms of a compact chiral boson
\phi(x)
. The chiral algebra is generated by the operators
H={\rm i}\partial\phi
and
E^{\pm}={:}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{2}\phi}{:}
, which have the following non-vanishing OPE’s:

\displaystyle H(z)H(w)\sim\frac{1}{(z-w)^{2}},\qquad H(z)E^{\pm}(w)\sim\frac{\pm\sqrt{2}E^{\pm}(w)}{z-w},

\displaystyle E^{+}(z)E^{-}(w)\sim\frac{1}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}H(w)}{z-w}.

For a generic group
G
, the chiral algebra primaries are labeled by highest weights
\omega
of the Lie algebra of
G
. The conformal weight of a primary is given by

\displaystyle h_{\omega}=\frac{C_{2}(\omega)}{h^{\vee}_{G}+k},

where
C_{2}(\omega)
is the Casimir invariant of the representation of highest weight
\omega
. For a chiral block to be nonzero, the tensor product of the corresponding representations must contain a singlet.

For
G={\rm SU}(2)
, at level 1 the only primaries are the vacuum and the WZW-primary
{\xi_{\omega_{1}}\equiv{:}{\rm e}^{\frac{{\rm i}}{\sqrt{2}}\phi}{:}}
corresponding to the highest weight
\omega_{1}
of the fundamental representation of
{\rm SU}(2)
.
The core block corresponding to the chiral datum
{\mathcal{D}}=(\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}})
would be a (scalar)
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
modular form with
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{D}}=\bigl(\mathrm{e}\bigl(-{1\over 6}\bigr)\bigr)
, since the leading order
q
-power in the corresponding core blocks is
q^{-{1\over 6}}
; however, no such modular form exists (see the discussion around equation (
6.6
)), and therefore the conformal block vanishes. In order to obtain non-vanishing blocks, we must take some of the external operators to be the operator
\xi_{-\omega_{1}}=E^{-}\xi_{\omega_{1}}
, which is a Virasoro-primary but not a WZW-primary.

The core block
{\mathcal{D}}=(\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}})
still vanishes for the same reason; on the other hand, for
{\mathcal{D}}=(\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}})
one obtains the three-dimensional
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
representation

\displaystyle\underline{\varphi}(\tau)=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi^{1}_{\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}}}\\[4.2679pt]
\varphi^{1}_{\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}}}\\[4.2679pt]
\varphi^{1}_{\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}}}\end{pmatrix}{(\tau)}

(alternatively, each component of
\underline{\varphi}(\tau)
can be viewed as scalar-valued modular form for
\Gamma(2)
).
Determining the modular transformation properties requires some care. The OPE between
\xi_{\omega_{1}}
and
\xi_{-\omega_{1}}
contains the identity operator, and as a result the leading order
q
-power in the core blocks
{\varphi}^{1}_{\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}}}(\tau)
is
q^{-\frac{1}{6}(4h_{\omega_{1}})}=q^{-\frac{1}{6}}
. On the other hand, the OPE of
\xi_{\pm\omega_{1}}
with itself contains descendants of the identity but not the identity itself:

\displaystyle\xi_{\pm\omega_{1}}(z)\xi_{\pm\omega_{1}}(w)=E^{\pm}(z)(z-w)^{\frac{1}{2}}+{{\mathcal{O}}}\bigl((z-w)^{\frac{3}{2}}\bigr).

As a consequence, for the core block
{\varphi}^{1}_{\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}},\xi_{-\omega_{1}}}(\tau)
the operator of lowest dimension appearing in the vacuum channel is the operator
E^{+}
which has conformal weight 1. With this information, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}\bigl(\frac{1}{3}\bigr)&0&0\\
0&0&\mathrm{e}\bigl(-\frac{1}{6}\bigr)\\
0&\mathrm{e}\bigl(-\frac{1}{6}\bigr)&0\end{pmatrix}.

As the dimension of the representation is less than
6
, there is a unique
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}
compatible with
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{D}}
. This is easily found to be

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

The relevant space of vvmf’s is again one-dimensional, as can be checked by applying equation (
6.15
) (after applying the appropriate conjugation to
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}
to make
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{D}}
diagonal). It is easy to recognize that the conformal blocks must be given by

\displaystyle\underline{\varphi}(\tau)=\frac{2^{-\frac{4}{3}}}{\eta(\tau)^{4}}\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{2}(\tau)^{4}\\
\theta_{4}(\tau)^{4}\\
\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}\end{pmatrix},

which, once expressed in terms of the cross ratio
w
, is in agreement with the expressions for the conformal blocks obtained by Knizhnik and Zamolodchikov
[
47
]
.

8.4
Liouville theory

In our last example, we show how our approach also extends to the case of non-rational conformal field theories which contain null states. In particular we apply our method to the Liouville theory with central charge

\displaystyle c=1+6Q^{2},\qquad\text{where}\quad Q=b+\frac{1}{b},\ b\in\mathbb{R}.

As is well known, the spectrum of Liouville theory primaries consists of a continuum of states
\phi_{\mathfrak{p}}
(see

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathfrak{p}}=\frac{b}{2}+\frac{1}{2b}+\mathfrak{p},\qquad\mathfrak{p}\in{\rm i}\mathbb{R}

of momentum

\displaystyle\alpha_{m,n}=-\frac{b}{2}m-\frac{1}{2b}n,\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

The conformal weight of an operator is given in terms of its momentum

as

\displaystyle h=\alpha(Q-\alpha).

For definiteness, let us consider a core block involving one degenerate operator of momentum
\alpha_{1,0}=-{b\over 2}
and three non-degenerate operators of momentum

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}=(\phi_{1,0},\phi_{\mathfrak{p}},\phi_{\mathfrak{p}},\phi_{\mathfrak{p}}).

with primary

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}^{4}=1,\qquad{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}=({\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}\cdot{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathcal{D}})^{3}

and by working with a basis of conformal blocks such that
{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{D}}
is symmetric (cf. equation (
A.3

\displaystyle\varphi^{M_{\mathfrak{p}\mp\frac{b}{2}}}_{\phi_{1,0},\phi_{\mathfrak{p}},\phi_{\mathfrak{p}},\phi_{\mathfrak{p}}}(\tau)

A sphere-torus correspondence

In the previous section, we have encountered a number of examples in which the core blocks coincide with the characters of different VOAs. Note that this relation bears some similarity to another better-known relation between a theory
{\mathcal{T}}_{t}
on the torus and the corresponding symmetric product theory
{\rm Sym}^{2}{\mathcal{T}}_{t}=({\mathcal{T}}_{t}\times{\mathcal{T}}_{t})/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on the sphere
[
40
,
54
,
75
]
. We now briefly review the main ingredients of this correspondence. On one side, one considers the partition function of a torus theory
{\mathcal{T}}_{t}
of central charges

(
which for simplicity we take to be diagonal theory with chiral algebras
{\mathcal{V}}_{t}=\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}

in the symmetric product theory

of central charges

6.2

{\mathcal{T}}_{t}
, one for each of the two sheets. The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
orbifold reflects the fact that in circling one of the branch points one moves between the two sheets, thus exchanging the two theories. This is implemented on the sphere side by inserting at every branch point a twist operator
{\mathcal{O}}(z,\overline{z})=O(z)\otimes\tilde{O}(\overline{z})
, which is the twisted sector ground state of
{\rm Sym}^{2}{\mathcal{T}}_{t}
of conformal weights
\bigl(h_{\mathcal{O}},\tilde{h}_{\mathcal{O}}\bigr)=\bigl(\frac{c}{16},\frac{c}{16}\bigr)
.

is unique as long as the CFT

It can be shown that the OPE of

{\mathcal{T}}_{t}

\displaystyle Z_{{\mathcal{T}}_{t}}(\tau,\overline{\tau})=2^{\frac{2c}{3}}\,z_{\rm 4pt}\,\langle{\mathcal{O}}(z_{1},\overline{z}_{1}),\dots,{\mathcal{O}}(z_{4},\overline{z}_{4})\rangle_{{\rm Sym}^{2}{\mathcal{T}}_{t}}=2^{\frac{2c}{3}}F_{{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{O}}}(\tau,\overline{\tau})_{{\rm Sym}^{2}{\mathcal{T}}_{t}},

In Section

, we have argued that the core blocks are components of a weight-zero weakly holomorphic vector-valued modular form; in particular, they must be solutions of a MLDE. On the other hand, four-point chiral blocks of Virasoro minimal models are known to be solutions to the BPZ differential equation
[
14
]
, due to the presence of null states. In what follows, we verify explicitly that the BPZ equation satisfied by the chiral block (in the simplest case where the null descendant occurs at level 2) can be turned into the order-
2
MLDE for the core block.

Let us recall briefly how the BPZ equation arises. Consider a chiral block between Virasoro primaries
\phi_{1}(z_{1}),\phi_{2}(z_{2}),\dots
of conformal weight
h_{1},h_{2},\dots
, and assume
\phi_{1}(z_{1})
has a null Virasoro descendant at level
n
. A generic level-
n
descendant of a Virasoro primary operator
\phi_{1}(z_{1})
can written in the form

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\\
|Y|=n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{Y}L_{-Y}\phi(z)

for
\alpha_{Y}\in\mathbb{C}
; here,

\displaystyle Y=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\},\qquad 1\leq r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\dots\leq r_{k}

is a Young diagram of size
n
, and
L_{-Y}=L_{-r_{1}}L_{-r_{2}}\dots L_{-r_{k}}
. When such a descendant is a null state, any physical correlator involving it vanishes:

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\bigl(\phi^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1})\phi^{2}(z_{2},\bar{z}_{2})\dots\bigr\rangle=0,

where
\phi^{i}(z,\bar{z})=\phi_{i}(z)\times\tilde{\phi}_{i}(\bar{z})
. This implies that physical correlators involving the primary field
\phi^{1}(z,\bar{z})
satisfy a holomorphic order
n
differential equation:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\\
|Y|=n\end{subarray}}\alpha_{Y}\mathcal{L}_{-Y}(z_{1})\langle\phi(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1})\phi_{1}(z_{2},\bar{z}_{2})\dots\rangle=0,

(A.4)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{-Y}(z_{1})=\mathcal{L}_{-r_{1}}(z_{1})\mathcal{L}_{-r_{2}}(z_{1})\dots\mathcal{L}_{-r_{k}}(z_{1})

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{-r}(z_{1})=\sum_{i\geq 2}\biggl(\frac{(r-1)h_{i}}{z_{i1}^{r}}-\frac{1}{z_{i1}^{r-1}}\partial_{z_{i}}\biggr).

Let us from now on focus on four-point blocks. The BPZ equation (
A.4
) then translates to the following differential equation for the chiral blocks:

\Biggl[\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}-t\sum_{i=2}^{4}\biggl(\frac{h_{i}}{z_{i1}^{2}}-\frac{1}{z_{i1}}\partial_{z_{i}}\biggr)\Biggr]\mathcal{F}^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})=0,

(A.5)

where
P
is an internal channel. We claim that the BPZ equation lifts to a modular differential equation for the core of the conformal block. Let us explicitly verify this in the case where the null state is at level
2
. Such a null state is given by
\phi_{1}^{\prime}(z_{1})=\bigl(L_{-1}^{2}-tL_{-2}\bigr)\phi(z_{1})
, where

\displaystyle t=\frac{4}{3}h_{1}+\frac{2}{3}.

We now express the holomorphic cross ratio
w
as
w=\lambda(\tau)
in terms of the modular parameter on the torus. The relation between conformal and core block is given by

\mathcal{F}^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})=\varphi^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(w)\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq 4}z_{jk}^{\mu_{jk}},

(A.6)

where
\mu_{jk}
is defined in equation (
4.16
). To obtain the differential equation for the core block, we express the differential operator appearing in equation (
A.5
) in terms of derivatives with respect to the cross ratio
w={z_{12}z_{34}\over z_{13}z_{24}}
. Thus, for example

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{1}}=\partial_{z_{1}}(w)\partial_{w}+\frac{\mu_{12}}{{z_{12}}}+\frac{\mu_{13}}{{z_{13}}}+\frac{\mu_{14}}{{z_{14}}},

where

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{1}}(w)=\frac{z_{23}z_{34}}{z_{13}^{2}z_{24}},

and similarly for
\partial_{z_{2}}(w)
,
\partial_{z_{3}}(w)
,
\partial_{z_{4}}(w)
. Likewise, one finds

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle{}=-\biggl(\frac{\mu_{12}}{z_{12}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{13}}{z_{13}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{14}}{z_{14}^{2}}\biggr)+\biggl(\frac{\mu_{12}}{z_{12}}+\frac{\mu_{13}}{z_{13}}+\frac{\mu_{14}}{z_{14}}\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{}+2\biggl(\frac{\mu_{12}}{z_{12}}+\frac{\mu_{13}}{z_{13}}+\frac{\mu_{14}}{z_{14}}\biggr)\partial_{z_{1}}(w)\partial_{w}+\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}(w)\partial_{w}+\partial_{z_{1}}(w)^{2}\partial_{w}^{2}.

Taking the limit
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})\to(\infty,1,w,0)
and using relation (
A.6
), we obtain the following differential equation for the core block:

\displaystyle\biggl\{{{\rm d}^{2}\over{\rm d}w^{2}}+\biggl[2\biggl(\frac{\mu_{12}}{w}+\frac{\mu_{14}}{w-1}\biggr)+t\frac{2w-1}{w(w-1)}\biggr]{{\rm d}\over{\rm d}w}+\biggl[\biggl(\frac{\mu_{12}}{w}+\frac{\mu_{14}}{w-1}\biggr)^{2}-\frac{\mu_{12}}{{w}^{2}}-\frac{\mu_{14}}{(w-1)^{2}}\biggr]

\displaystyle\qquad{}+t\biggl(\frac{\mu_{12}-h_{2}}{{w}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{14}-h_{4}}{(w-1)^{2}}-\frac{\mu_{24}}{w({w}-1)}\biggr)\biggr\}\varphi^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(w)=0.

(A.7)

Next, we use
w=\lambda(\tau)=\bigl(\frac{\theta_{2}(\tau)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)}\bigr)^{4}
to rewrite equation (
A.7
) in terms of the variable
q=\mathrm{e}(\tau)
. In particular, we find

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\over{\rm d}w}=\frac{2}{\lambda(\tau)(1-\lambda(\tau))\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}}D,

and

\displaystyle{{\rm d}^{2}\over{\rm d}w^{2}}=\frac{4}{\lambda(\tau)^{2}(1-\lambda(\tau))^{2}\theta_{3}(\tau)^{8}}\biggl\{D^{2}+\biggl(\frac{2\lambda(\tau)-1}{2}\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}-\frac{D\bigl(\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}\bigr)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}}\biggr)D\biggr\}.

Making also use of the identities

\displaystyle\bigl(\lambda(\tau)^{2}-\lambda(\tau)+1\bigr)\theta_{3}(\tau)^{8}=E_{4}(\tau),

\displaystyle\frac{2\lambda(\tau)-1}{6}\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}-\frac{D\bigl(\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}\bigr)}{\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}}=-\frac{1}{6}E_{2}(\tau),

we can rewrite equation (
A.7
) as

\displaystyle\Biggl\{D^{2}+\biggl[-\frac{E_{2}(\tau)}{6}+\biggl(\theta_{2}(\tau)^{4}\frac{h_{4}-h_{3}}{3}+\theta_{3}(\tau)^{4}\frac{h_{4}-h_{2}}{3}+\theta_{4}(\tau)^{4}\frac{h_{3}-h_{2}}{3}\biggr)\biggr]D

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\frac{1}{18}E_{4}(\tau)(2h_{1}(h-1)+h)-\frac{1}{36}\sum_{i=2}^{4}\Lambda_{i}\theta_{i}(\tau)^{8}\Biggr\}\varphi^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}}(\lambda(\tau))=0,

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{i}=(4h_{1}+1)(3\mu_{1i}+2h_{1})+\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=2\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{4}(3\mu_{1j}+2h_{1}).

Let us now further specialize to the case
\phi_{2}=\phi_{3}=\phi_{4}
with
h_{2}=h_{3}=h_{4}=h_{\ast}
, corresponding to
\operatorname{{\rm PSL}}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
symmetry. Note that in this case
\Lambda_{i}=0
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\biggl\{D^{2}-\frac{E_{2}(\tau)}{6}D-\frac{\mu(h_{1},h_{*})}{4}E_{4}(\tau)\biggr\}\varphi^{P}_{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{2},\phi_{2}}(\lambda(\tau))=0,

which takes precisely the form of the modular differential equation (
A.1
), with

\displaystyle\mu(h_{1},h_{*})=\frac{2}{9}\bigl(2h_{1}^{2}+6h_{1}h_{*}+3h_{*}-h_{1}\bigr).

This establishes that the second-order BPZ equations for four-point chiral blocks are equivalent to modular linear differential equations satisfied by the core blocks.
It should be straightforward to establish analogous relations for the higher-order cases.

One can further show that braiding and fusing are related as follows:

\displaystyle B_{PQ}\begin{bmatrix}N_{2}&N_{3}\\
N_{1}&N_{4}\end{bmatrix}^{a,b}_{c,d}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{e,f}\bigl(\xi_{N_{3},N_{4}}^{P}\bigr)^{b}_{e}\bigl(\xi_{Q,N_{3}}^{N_{1}}\bigr)^{f}_{c}\,\mathrm{e}\biggl(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(h_{P}+h_{Q}-h_{N_{1}}-h_{N_{4}})\biggr)F_{PQ}\begin{bmatrix}N_{2}&N_{4}\\
N_{1}&N_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{a,e}_{f,d}.

(B.5)

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\sum_{Q}\sum_{c,d}B_{PQ}\begin{bmatrix}N_{2}&N_{3}\\
N_{1}&N_{4}\end{bmatrix}^{a,b}_{c,d}(\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{e}\biggl(\varepsilon\biggl(h_{N_{1}}+h_{N_{4}}-\frac{h_{P}}{2}-h_{Q}-\frac{h_{R}}{2}\biggr)\biggr)B_{QR}\begin{bmatrix}N_{3}&N_{2}\\
N_{1}&N_{4}\end{bmatrix}^{c,d}_{e,f}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad{}={\delta_{P,R}}\delta^{a}_{e}\delta^{b}_{f}

(B.6)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{Q}{\sum_{b,c,d,e,h,i}}F_{PQ}\begin{bmatrix}N_{2}&N_{3}\\
N_{1}&N_{4}\end{bmatrix}^{a,b}_{c,d}F_{QR}\begin{bmatrix}N_{3}&N_{4}\\
N_{1}&N_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{h,i}_{e,f}\bigl(\xi_{N_{4},N_{3}}^{P}\bigr)^{g}_{b}\bigl(\xi_{Q,N_{4}}^{N_{1}}\bigr)^{c}_{h}\bigl(\xi_{N_{4},N_{2}}^{Q}\bigr)^{d}_{i}\bigl(\xi_{R,N_{4}}^{N_{1}}\bigr)^{e}_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad{}={\delta_{P,R}}\delta^{a}_{j}\delta^{g}_{f}.
