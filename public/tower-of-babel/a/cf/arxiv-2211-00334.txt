Central extensions of axial algebras
Ivan Kaygorodov, Cándido Martín González, Pilar Páez-Guillán

Abstract
. In this article, we develop a further adaptation of the method of Skjelbred-Sund to construct central extensions of axial algebras. We use our method to prove that all axial central extensions (with respect to a maximal set of axes) of complex simple finite-dimensional Jordan algebras are split, and that all non-split axial central extensions of dimension n ≤ 4 n\leq 4 over an algebraically closed field of characteristic not 2 2 are Jordan. Also, we give a classification of 2 2 -dimensional axial algebras and describe some important properties of these algebras.

Introduction

Axial algebras are a recent class of non-associative commutative algebras introduced by Hall,
Rehren and Shpectorov
[
18
]
in 2015. They can be seen as a certain generalisation of commutative, associative algebras, and as a common frame for Majorana algebras
[
18
,
39
]
, Jordan algebras
[
19
,
20
,
28
]
and other types of algebras appearing in mathematical physics. They are also related to code algebras
[
7
]
.

The relevance of Majorana and axial algebras lies on the fact that they provide an axiomatic approach to some properties of vertex operator algebras (VOAs), complex algebraic structures rooted in theoretical physics. In mathematics, the best-known VOA is the moonshine
V^{\#}
, constructed by Frenkel, Lepowsky and
Meurman in
[
14
]
, and whose automorphism
group is the Monster
M
, the largest sporadic finite simple group. This object shows a link to the theory of modular functions, and was key in the proof of Borcherds
[
4
]
of the monstrous moonshine conjecture on the connection between the Monster and modular functions. The rigorous development of the theory of VOAs, an important tool for the proof, is also due to Borcherds
[
3
]
.

After the cited paper of Hall, Rehren and Shpectorov
[
18
]
, it began a systematic study of axial algebras.
An interesting and active direction in this study is the description of
n
-generated axial algebras of a certain type.
So, two-generated primitive axial algebras of Jordan type
\eta
over fields of characteristic different from
2
were classified in
[
19
]
by Hall, Rehren and Shpectorov.
Rehren proved in
[
31
,
32
]
that the dimension of two-generated primitive axial algebras of Monster type
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
does not exceed
8
if the characteristic of the ground field is not
2
and
\alpha\notin\left\{2\beta,4\beta\right\}
.
Later, Franchi, Mainardis and Shpectorov constructed an infinite dimensional two-generated primitive axial algebra of Monster type
\left(2,\frac{1}{2}\right),
today known as
Highwater algebra

[
11
]
,
and they classified all two-generated primitive axial algebras of Monster type
\left(2\beta,\beta\right)
over a field of characteristic not
2
in
[
12
]
. Also, a classification of primitive symmetric two-generated axial algebras of Monster type was given between Yabe’s
[
40
]
, Franchi and Mainardis’
[
13
]
and Franchi, Mainardis and McInroy’s
[
10
]
.
On the other hand, Gorshkov and Staroletov
showed that a three-generated primitive axial algebra of Jordan type has dimension at most
9

[
16
]
; Khasraw, McInroy and Shpectorov enumerated all the three-generated primitive axial algebras of Monster type
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
of a certain subclass, the so-called
4
-algebras
[
26
]
.

We cite some other directions in the research on axial algebras. Khasraw, McInroy and Shpectorov described the structure of axial algebras
[
25
]
.
De Medts and Van Couwenberghe introduced axial representations of groups and modules over axial algebras as new tools to study axial algebras
[
8
]
.
Axial algebras have been also studied from a computational approach in McInroy and Shpectorov’s
[
27
]
(see also
[
37
]
), and from a categorical point of view in De Medts, Peacock, Shpectorov and Van Couwenberghe’s
[
9
]
. Also, a non-commutative version of axial algebras was considered by
Rowen and Segev
[
35
,
34
,
36
]
.

On the other hand,
the study of algebras generated by idempotents has a proper interest.
Rowen and Segev described all associative and Jordan algebras generated by two idempotents
[
33
]
;
Brešar proved that a finite-dimensional (unital) algebra is zero product determined if and only
if it is generated by idempotents
[
5
]
, and so on.

The paper is organised as follows. The introductory Section
1
provides some basic definitions about axial algebras. We also give a classification of complex two-dimensional axial algebras and describe some of the main properties of these algebras.
Section
2
is devoted to a detailed explanation of an adaptation of the Skjelbred-Sund method
[
38
]
for constructing central extensions of axial algebras:
we describe the conditions that ensure that a given central extension of an axial algebra will also be axial (Theorem
2.12
) and prove that
an axial algebra with non-zero annihilator is a central extension of an axial algebra of smaller dimension
(Theorem
2.15
).
In Section
3
, we apply the methods developed in Section
2
to prove
that a complex finite-dimensional simple Jordan algebra does not have non-split axial central extensions with respect to a maximal set of axes, and also that all the axial central extensions of Jordan algebras of dimension
n\leq 4
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic not
2
are again Jordan algebras.

Although all the examples throughout the paper are already known in the literature, it is our hope that this technique will allow us to find new examples in the near future.

Unless otherwise stated, all algebras and vector spaces throughout the paper are assumed to be of arbitrary dimension and over an arbitrary field
F
. The generating sets for the algebras are assumed to be finite. Also, we will denote the direct sum of vector spaces by
\dot{+}
, while
\oplus
will be reserved to denote the direct sum of algebras. Finally, we will employ
\subseteq
for denoting non-strict inclusions, and
\subset
for the strict ones.

1.
Preliminaries on axial algebras

Let
F
be a field,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq F
a subset, and
\star\colon\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\to 2^{\mathcal{F}}
a symmetric binary operation. The pair
(\mathcal{F},\star)
is called a fusion law over
F
, and will be denoted simply by
\mathcal{F}
whenever there is not possibility of confusion. We say that the fusion law
(\mathcal{F},\star)
is contained in the fusion law
(\mathcal{G},\odot)
if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and, for every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{F}
, it holds that
\lambda\star\mu\subseteq\lambda\odot\mu
. Also, if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{G}
, we will denote by
(\mathcal{G},\star)
the fusion law resulting from setting
\lambda\star\mu=\emptyset
for every
\lambda\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{F}
and every
\mu\in\mathcal{G}
.

The values of any fusion law
(\mathcal{F},\star)
can be displayed in a symmetric square table. This is the most common way to represent them; we employ it in Tables
14
and
17
. Following the conventions in the literature (e.g.
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
), we will abuse notation in the tables by not writing the set symbols in unitary sets, or using a blank entry to mean the empty set. On other occasions, we will limit to write explicitly the relevant products of the fusion laws, as in Tables
5
and
11
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative algebra. For any element
x\in\mathbf{A}
, we denote by
\Spec(x)
the spectrum of the endomorphism
L_{x}\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}
,
y\mapsto xy
,
and by
\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{x}
the eigenspace associated with an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\Spec(x)
. If
\mu\notin\Spec(x)
, we assume
\mathbf{A}_{\mu}^{x}=0
. Also, given a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Spec(x)
, we denote
\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{S}}^{x}=\dot{+}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{x}
and
\mathbf{A}_{\emptyset}^{x}=\{0\}
.

Let
(\mathcal{F},\star)
be a fusion law over
F
. An element
a\in\mathbf{A}
is called an
\mathcal{F}
-axis if the following conditions hold:

(1)

a
is idempotent;

(2)

a
is semisimple;

(3)

\Spec(a)\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and
\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{a}\mathbf{A}_{\mu}^{a}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\lambda\star\mu}^{a}
, for all
\lambda,\mu\in\Spec(a)
.

Recall that, if
\mathbf{A}
has finite dimension, by
a
being semisimple we just mean that
L_{a}
is diagonalisable. In the infinite-dimensional case,
a
must satisfy the next two conditions:

(i)

For every
x\in\mathbf{A}
, there exists a finite-dimensional subspace
W_{x}\subset\mathbf{A}
stable by
L_{a}
such that
x\in W_{x}
.

(ii)

For every subspace
W\subset\mathbf{A}
of finite dimension stable by
L_{a}
, the restriction
L_{a}|_{W}
is diagonalisable.

An
\mathcal{F}
-axial algebra over
F
is a pair
(\mathbf{A},X)
, where
\mathbf{A}
is a commutative algebra over
F
and
X
is a finite set of
\mathcal{F}
-axes that generate
\mathbf{A}
.
If the fusion law is clear, we will simply refer to axes and axial algebras.

We will now recall some basic definitions regarding axial algebras. For more information, see for instance
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
25
,
29
]
.

Note that, from conditions
(1)
and
(3)
above, any axis
a\in\mathbf{A}
satisfies
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{a}\neq 0
. An axis
a\in\mathbf{A}
is called primitive if
\dim\mathbf{A}_{1}^{a}=1
. If an axial algebra
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
is generated by primitive axes, then it is called primitive. In this case,
\mathbf{A}_{1}^{a}\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{a}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{a}
, for all
\lambda\in\Spec(a)
.

A two-generated axial algebra
(\mathbf{A},\{a_{1},a_{2}\})
is called symmetric if it admits a flip, i.e. if there exists an automorphism switching the generating axes
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.

An axial algebra
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
is said to be
m
-closed if
\mathbf{A}
is spanned by products of axes of
{\rm X}
of length at most
m
.

Also, we say that an axial algebra
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
admits a Frobenius form if there exists a (non-zero) bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to F
which associates with the product of
\mathbf{A}
, i.e.

(x,yz)=(xy,z)

for all
x,y,z\in\mathbf{A}
. Note that this form is necessarily symmetric
[
18
, Proposition 3.5]
.

The radical
R(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
of a primitive axial algebra
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
is the unique largest ideal of
\mathbf{A}
containing no axes from
{\rm X}
. If
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
admits a Frobenius form, the radical of the form and
R(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
are closely related (see
[
25
]
).

Sometimes, the fusion law
({\mathcal{F}},\star)
is graded by a finite abelian group
T
, in the sense that there exists a partition
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\mid t\in T\}
such that for all
s,t\in T
,

{\mathcal{F}}_{s}\star{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}_{st}.

In these cases, it is induced a
T
-grading on
\mathbf{A}
for each axis
a
, namely
\mathbf{A}=\dot{+}_{t\in T}\mathbf{A}^{a}_{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}
.

Let
T^{*}
be the group of linear characters of
T
. For each axis
a
, there exists a group homomorphism,
\tau_{a}\colon T^{*}\to\Aut(\mathbf{A})
, where
\tau_{a}(\chi)\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}
, with
\chi\in T^{*}
, is defined by the linear extension of

\tau_{a}(\chi)\colon\mathbf{A}

\to

\mathbf{A}

u

\longmapsto

\chi(t)u,

for
u\in\mathbf{A}_{t}^{a}
.

The automorphisms of the type
\tau_{a}(\chi)
are called Miyamoto automorphisms, and the image
T_{a}\coloneqq\textup{Im}\>\tau_{a}
is called the axial subgroup of
\Aut(\mathbf{A})
corresponding to
a
. If
{\mathrm{Z}}
is a set of axes of
\mathbf{A}
, the subgroup

G({\mathrm{Z}})\coloneqq\langle T_{a}\mid a\in{\mathrm{Z}}\rangle\subseteq\Aut(\mathbf{A})

is known as the Miyamoto group of
\mathbf{A}
with respect to
{\mathrm{Z}}
.

In this paper, we will restrict to dealing with gradings by the cyclic group
C_{2}
of order two. To avoid pathological situations, on many occasions we will assume that
\charac F\neq 2
. Note that, in this setting,
T_{a}\subseteq\Aut(\mathbf{A})
has order two for every axis
a
. Let us write
T_{a}=\{\id_{\mathbf{A}},\phi_{a}\}
.

A set of axes
{\mathrm{Z}}
is called closed if
\phi({\mathrm{Z}})={\mathrm{Z}}
for any
\phi\in G({\mathrm{Z}})
. The minimal closed set of axes containing
{\mathrm{Z}}
exists and it is called the closure of
{\mathrm{Z}}
,
\overline{{\mathrm{Z}}}
.

2.
Central extensions of axial algebras

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algebra and
{\rm V}
a vector space, and recall that a central extension of
\mathbf{A}
by
\rm V
is a short exact sequence
0\to{\rm V}\to\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{A}\to 0
such that the image of
\rm V
is contained in the annihilator
\Ann(\mathbf{B})
; we say that the dimension of the extension is
\dim{\rm V}
. It is customary to identify the central extension with
\mathbf{B}
. Let also
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
be a bilinear map, and define
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}=\mathbf{A}\dot{+}{\rm V}
, which can be given structure of algebra with the product
[x+v,y+w]_{\theta}=xy+\theta(x,y)
. It is immediate to check that
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
is a central extension of
\mathbf{A}
with respect to
{\rm V}
. Also, it can be seen that every central extension of
\mathbf{A}
of arbitrary dimension is isomorphic to
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
for some
{\rm V}
and
\theta
in the above conditions. Then, we will identify any central extension of
\mathbf{A}
with a certain
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
for some
{\rm V}
and
\theta
in the above conditions.

In the following paragraphs, we present some basic features of this approach to central extensions which will be needed later (for more details about the Skjelbred-Sund method, see, for example,
[
21
,
23
]
and references therein).

Let
f\colon\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
be a linear map and define the bilinear map
\delta f\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
by
\delta f(x,y)=f(xy)
. The set
\{\delta f\mid f\colon\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}\text{ is linear}\}
is a linear subspace of the bilinear maps from
\mathbf{A}
to
{\rm V}
, so we can consider the quotient space. Note that if
\theta^{\prime}=\theta+\delta f
for some
f\colon\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
, then the map
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\to\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}
defined by
\varphi(x+v)=x+v+f(x)
is an isomorphism. Therefore, the isomorphy class of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
does not depend on the representatives
\theta
of the equivalence class
[\theta]
. Also, there is a natural (right) action of
\Aut(\mathbf{A})
on this quotient space, induced by
\phi\theta(x,y)=\theta(\phi(x),\phi(y))
for
\phi\in\Aut(\mathbf{A})
. The isomorphy class of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
does not depend either on the particular point in the orbit of
[\theta]
under this action, as the map
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\to\mathbf{A}_{\phi\theta}
given by
\varphi(x+v)=\phi^{-1}(x)+v
is an isomorphism.

Given a bilinear map
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
and a basis
\{e_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
of
{\rm V}
, there exist
\lvert\Gamma\rvert
unique bilinear maps
\theta_{\gamma}\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to F
such that
\theta(x,y)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\theta_{\gamma}(x,y)e_{\gamma}
.
A central extension
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
is said to have an annihilator component if there exist an ideal
I
and a non-zero subalgebra of
\Ann\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
,
J
, such that
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}=I\oplus J
. The central extensions with annihilator component are called split; without annihilator component, non-split. If the dimensions of
\mathbf{A}
and
{\rm V}
are finite, for a non-split central extension
A_{\theta}
, it holds that the set
\{[\theta_{\gamma}]\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
is linearly independent; the converse is also true under the hypothesis
\Ann\mathbf{A}_{\theta}={\rm V}
.

In the subsequent, we study when a central extension of an axial algebra is axial in terms of the bilinear map
\theta
. Let us fix the following notation. We will denote by
L_{x}
and
L_{x+v}^{\theta}
the operators of left multiplication in
\mathbf{A}
and in
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
, respectively. The spectrum of
L_{x+v}^{\theta}
will be denoted by
\Spec_{\theta}(x+v)
. Also, we write
\theta_{x}^{\perp}=\{y\in\mathbf{A}\mid\theta(x,y)=0\}
, and denote by
P\colon\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\to\mathbf{A}
the natural projection onto
\mathbf{A}
.

The following results are easy consequences of the definitions:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a bilinear map. Then,
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
is commutative if and only if
\theta
is symmetric.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\rm V}
be a vector space and
\mathbf{A}
an algebra
with an element
x\in\mathbf{A}
such that
L_{x}\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}
is diagonalisable. Choose a symmetric bilinear map
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
such that
\ker(L_{x})\subseteq\theta_{x}^{\perp}
. Then if
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
is a basis of
\mathbf{A}
diagonalising
L_{x}
and

L_{x}(e_{\alpha})=\lambda_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
,
\lambda_{\alpha}\in F
, for any
\alpha
, we may construct a basis of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}

given by

B:=\{e_{\alpha}+\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}\theta(x,e_{\alpha})\colon\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0\}\sqcup\{e_{\alpha}\colon\lambda_{\alpha}=0\}\sqcup B_{\rm V}

where
B_{\rm V}
is any basis of
{\rm V}
. The basis
B
diagonalises
L_{x+v}
for any
v\in{\rm V}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a bilinear map. Then,
\Spec_{\theta}(x+v)=\Spec(x)\cup\{0\}
for all semisimple
x\in\mathbf{A}
and all
v\in{\rm V}
, and the eigenspaces of
L_{x+v}^{\theta}
are

(\mathbf{A}_{\theta})_{\lambda}^{x+v}=\{y+\lambda^{-1}\theta(x,y)\mid y\in\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{x}\},

for
\lambda\in\Spec(x)
,
\lambda\neq 0
, and

(\mathbf{A}_{\theta})_{0}^{x+v}=\{y+w\in\mathbf{A}_{\theta}\mid y\in\mathbf{A}_{0}^{x}\cap\theta_{x}^{\perp}\},

recalling that we mean
\mathbf{A}_{0}^{x}=0
if
0\notin\Spec(x)
.

Furthermore we have:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
B=\{e_{\alpha}+v_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
be a basis of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
diagonalising
L_{x+v}
. Then

\ker(L_{x})\subseteq\theta_{x}^{\perp}
and
L_{x}
is diagonalisable.

Proof.

We have
L_{x+v}(e_{\alpha}+v_{\alpha})=\lambda_{\alpha}(e_{\alpha}+v_{\alpha})
, which implies
xe_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
and
\theta(x,e_{\alpha})=\lambda_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Note that when
\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0
we have
v_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}\theta(x,e_{\alpha})
. Define
S\coloneqq\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\colon\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
and
T\coloneqq\mathcal{A}\setminus S
. So
B=\{e_{\alpha}+\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}\theta(x,e_{\alpha})\colon\alpha\in S\}\sqcup\{e_{\alpha}+v_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in T\}
. The set
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
is a system of linear generators of
\mathbf{A}
and so a suitable subset
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
is a basis of
\mathbf{A}
. In this basis we can distinguish those
e_{\alpha}
’s whose
\lambda_{\alpha}
is non-zero and those whose
\lambda_{\alpha}=0
. So we have a basis of
\mathbf{A}
of the form
B^{\prime}=\{e_{\alpha}\colon\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\sqcup\{e_{\alpha}\colon\lambda_{\alpha}=0\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
. Take
z\in\ker(L_{x})
and write
z=\sum_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
with
k_{\alpha}\in F
relative to the basis
B^{\prime}
. Then

0=xz=\sum_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}xe_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}

where the
\lambda_{\alpha}
’s in the last sum are those which are non-zero. Consequently
k_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}=0
, that is,
k_{\alpha}=0
. Thus
z=\sum_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
where the sum is extended to those indices
\alpha
for which
\lambda_{\alpha}=0
. So
\theta(x,z)=\sum_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}\theta(x,e_{\alpha})=\sum_{\alpha}k_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}=0

and
z\in\theta_{x}^{\perp}
. The fact that
L_{x}
is diagonalisable follows now from the fact that
xe_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
∎

Fix a fusion law
(\mathcal{F},\star)
. Unless otherwise stated, all axial algebras will be assumed to be axial with respect to
(\mathcal{F},\star)
.

Let
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
an axial algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a symmetric bilinear map such that
\{[\theta_{\gamma}]\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
are linearly independent. Let
\{{\rm X}^{i}\}_{i\in I}
be the family of minimal sets of axes that generate

, being
{\rm X}^{i}=\{a_{1}^{i},\ldots,a_{r^{i}}^{i}\}
. In particular, each
{\rm X}^{i}
is linearly independent and can be extended to a basis

of
\mathbf{A}
.

Set
\omega^{i}_{j}=\theta(a^{i}_{j},a^{i}_{j})
and define
f^{i}_{j}\colon\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
by
f^{i}_{j}(a^{i}_{k})=\omega^{i}_{j}\delta_{jk}
, for

and

. Then, consider

\theta^{i}=\theta-\sum_{j=1}^{r^{i}}\delta f_{j}^{i}.

It is clear that
[\theta]=[\theta^{i}]
; moreover, it holds that
\theta^{i}(a^{i}_{k},a^{i}_{k})=0
for all
k=1,\dots,r^{i}
:

(2.1)

\theta^{i}(a^{i}_{k},a^{i}_{k})=\theta(a^{i}_{k},a^{i}_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{r^{i}}\delta f_{j}(a^{i}_{k},a^{i}_{k})=\omega^{i}_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{r^{i}}f_{j}(a^{i}_{k})=\omega^{i}_{k}-\omega^{i}_{k}=0.

For the sake of simplicity, we will drop the superscript

whenever
{\rm X}
is assumed to be a minimal set of axes generating
\mathbf{A}
. Also, when
{\rm X}
is linearly independent, we can assume without loss of generality that
\theta(a_{j},a_{j})=0
for all
j=1,\dots,r
.

Let us establish another piece of notation. Let
a\in{\rm X}
and
\lambda,\mu\in\Spec(a)
. For

and

, write

xy=\sum_{0\neq\nu\in\lambda\star\mu}z_{\nu}+z_{0},

where
z_{\nu}\in\mathbf{A}_{\nu}^{a}
and
z_{0}\in\mathbf{A}_{0}^{a}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
be an axial algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a symmetric bilinear map. Let
x\in\mathbf{A}
be semisimple and take
v\in{\rm V}
. Then,
x+v\in\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
is semisimple if and only if

(2.2)

\ker L_{x}\subseteq\theta_{x}^{\perp}.

Furthermore, if an axis
a\in{\rm X}
satisfies condition (
2.2
), the eigenspace decomposition of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
according to
a+v
follows the fusion law
(\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\},\star)

if and only if for every
\lambda,\mu\in\Spec(a)
such that
0\notin\lambda\star\mu
, it holds that

(2.3)

\theta(x,y)=\sum_{\nu\in\lambda\star\mu}\nu^{-1}\theta(a,z_{\nu})

for all
x\in\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{a}
,
y\in\mathbf{A}_{\mu}^{a}
.

Proof.

The first part of the proposition follows trivially from Lemmas
2.2
and
2.4
.

Take
a\in{\rm X}
satisfying condition (
2.2
) and
v\in{\rm V}
. By Lemma
2.3
, the eigenspace decomposition of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
according to
a+v
follows the fusion law
(\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\},\odot)
for some symmetric binary operation
\odot
.

Throughout the rest of the proof, we will denote
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
for the sake of simplicity. Lemma
2.3
gives the description of
\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}^{a+v}
for all
\lambda\in\Spec_{\theta}(a+v)
; moreover, under the present hypotheses, we can particularise

\mathbf{B}_{0}^{a+v}=\{x+v\in\mathbf{B}\mid x\in\mathbf{A}_{0}^{a}\}.

Take
\lambda,\mu\in\Spec(a)
,
x+u\in\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}^{a+v}
and
y+w\in\mathbf{B}_{\mu}^{a+v}
. Then,

\displaystyle[x+u,y+w]_{\theta}=xy+\theta(x,y)=

\displaystyle\sum_{0\neq\nu\in\lambda\star\mu}(z_{\nu}+\nu^{-1}\theta(a,z_{\nu}))+\left(z_{0}+\left(\theta(x,y)-\sum_{0\neq\nu\in\lambda\star\mu}\nu^{-1}\theta(a,z_{\nu})\right)\right)\in\sum_{0\neq\nu\in\lambda\star\mu}\mathbf{B}_{\nu}^{a+v}\oplus\mathbf{B}_{0}^{a+v}.

It is clear that
\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}^{a+v}\mathbf{B}_{\mu}^{a+v}\subseteq\mathbf{B}_{\lambda\star\mu}^{a+v}
if and only if
0\in\lambda\star\mu
or condition (
2.3
) holds for every
x\in\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{a}
and every

Also, if

,
\mathbf{B}_{0}^{a+v}\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}^{a+v}=\mathbf{B}_{0}^{a+v}\mathbf{B}_{0}^{a+v}=\{0\}
for all

∎

Note that conditions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) do not depend on the representative of
[\theta]
. Set

for some linear map

and

. Given

, and therefore

does. Also, given

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
be a two-dimensional axial algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a non-zero symmetric bilinear map.
Take an axis

. Then, condition (
2.2
) is satisfied if and only if
0\notin\Spec(a)
.

Proof.

Assume that

and that
\{a,b\}
is a minimal set of axes, with
\theta(a,a)=\theta(b,b)=0
, and note that
\{a,b\}
is also a basis of
\mathbf{A}
. Note that
a\in\theta_{a}^{\perp}
but

, as otherwise

would be the zero map. Then,
\theta_{a}^{\perp}=\langle a\rangle
. By hypothesis,

is non-zero and

We introduce now a notion of cocycles for axial algebras. Note that we do not intend to relate them to any theory of cohomology for axial algebras; instead, the choice of the term “cocycle” is motivated because they will help to describe the extensions of axial algebras.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
be an
(\mathcal{F},\star)
-axial algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a symmetric bilinear map. We say that
\theta
is a cocycle relative to a subset
{\rm X}^{\prime}\subseteq{\rm X}
if condition (
2.2
) is satisfied for all
a\in{\rm X}^{\prime}
and if, for every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{F}
such that

, condition (
2.3
) holds for all
a\in{\rm X}^{\prime}
such that
\lambda,\mu\in\Spec(a)
, all
x\in\mathbf{A}_{\lambda}^{a}
and all
y\in\mathbf{A}_{\mu}^{a}
. The vector space formed by them will be denoted by
{\mathrm{Z}}(\mathbf{A},{\rm V};{\rm X}^{\prime})
.

The next technical lemma will be needed for the main results of this section.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(\mathbf{A},{\rm X})
be an axial algebra,
{\rm V}
a vector space and
\theta\colon\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
a symmetric bilinear map such that
\{[\theta_{\gamma}]\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
are linearly independent. Assume that
{\rm X}=\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
is a minimal set of axes generating
\mathbf{A}
. Then,
{\rm X}
is a minimal generating set for
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\langle{\rm X}\rangle
the subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}
generated by
{\rm X}
, by
\langle{\rm X}\rangle_{\theta}
the subalgebra of
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
generated by
{\rm X}
.
Once we prove that
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\langle{\rm X}\rangle_{\theta}
, we will know that
\langle{\rm X}\rangle_{\theta}=\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
by the linear independence of
\{[\theta_{\gamma}]\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
.

Let
B=\{a_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be a basis of
\mathbf{A}
extending
{\rm X}
, and denote
J_{r}=J\setminus\{1,\dots,r\}
. Since
\mathbf{A}=\langle{\rm X}\rangle
, we can express each
a_{j}
, for
j\in J_{r}
, as a finite sum
a_{j}=\sum_{l=1}^{m_{j}}\alpha_{j,l}\prod{\rm X}_{j,l}
, where
\alpha_{j,l}\in F
and
\prod{\rm X}_{j,l}
denotes a product of elements of
{\rm X}
with a certain arrangement of brackets.
Set
\prod{\rm X}_{j,l}=(\prod{\rm X}^{1}_{j,l})(\prod{\rm X}^{2}_{j,l})
, where
\prod{\rm X}^{1}_{j,l}
and
\prod{\rm X}^{2}_{j,l}
are products of elements of
\rm X
with strictly smaller length than
\prod{\rm X}_{j,l}
. Set also
\omega_{j}=\sum_{l=1}^{m_{j}}\alpha_{j,l}\theta(\prod{\rm X}^{1}_{j,l},\prod{\rm X}^{2}_{j,l})
, and define the homomorphism
f\colon\mathbf{A}\to{\rm V}
by
f(a_{j})=0
for
j=1,\dots,r
, and
f(a_{j})=\omega_{j}
, for
j\in J_{r}
. Consider
\theta^{\prime}=\theta-\delta f
. Since
\mathbf{A}_{\theta}
and
\mathbf{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}
are isomorphic and
{\rm X}
is preserved by the isomorphism, it is enough to show that
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\langle{\rm X}\rangle_{\theta^{\prime}}
. We proceed by induction in the largest length
L
of the products
\prod{\rm X}_{j,l}
for
l=1,\dots,m_{j}
. If
L=1
, it is trivial that
a_{k}\in\langle{\rm X}\rangle_{\theta^{\prime}}
. In the general case,

\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m_{k}}

\alpha_{k,l}

\left(\left[\prod{\rm X}^{1}_{k,l},\prod{\rm X}^{2}_{k,l}\right]_{\theta^{\prime}}\right)

{\rm V}

\{[\theta_{\gamma}]\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
are linearly independent. Furthermore, assume that
({\mathcal{F}},\star)

on
{\mathrm{Z}}(\mathbf{A},{\rm V};{\rm X})
.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in{\mathrm{Z}}(\mathbf{A},{\rm V};{\rm X})
and
\phi\in G({\rm X})
; we are going to prove that
\phi\theta\in{\mathrm{Z}}(\mathbf{A},{\rm V};{\rm X})
. Take

. If

, clearly

. By Proposition
2.13
,
\phi\theta(a,x)=\theta(\phi(a),\phi(x))=0
and condition (

) is satisfied. Now take

and

for some
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that

. A straightforward computation shows that
\mathbf{A}_{\nu}^{\phi(a)}=\phi(\mathbf{A}_{\nu}^{a})
for all

, so
\phi(x)\in\mathbf{A}^{\phi(a)}_{\lambda}
and
\phi(y)\in\mathbf{A}^{\phi(a)}_{\mu}

ensures that

\phi\theta(x,y)=\theta(\phi(x),\phi(y))=\sum_{\nu\in\lambda\star\mu}\nu^{-1}\theta(\phi(a),z_{\nu}^{\phi}),

and

\mathbf{A}
with the product

denotes the product in
\mathbf{B}

is a homomorphism of algebras: indeed, for all

{\rm X}=P_{\mathbf{A}}({\rm Y})
. Since
{\rm Y}
is a generating set for
\mathbf{B}
,
{\rm X}
generates
P_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{A}
. Take
a\in{\rm X}
such that

for a certain

. Then

and

is idempotent.

Write

for the left multiplication by
b

\mathbf{B}
, and
\Spec_{\mathbf{B}}(b)
for its spectrum. We reserve the notations

and
\Spec(a)
for their correspondences in
\mathbf{A}
. It is clear that

and
\Ann(\mathbf{B})\subseteq\mathbf{B}_{0}^{b}
. Choose a basis
\{z_{\beta}^{b}\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}
of
