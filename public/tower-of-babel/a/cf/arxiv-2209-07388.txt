Sharpness of saturated fusion systems on a Sylow p p -subgroup of G 2 ​ ( p ) {\rm G}_{2}(p)
Valentina Grazian, Ettore Marmo

Abstract
. We prove that the Díaz-Park’s sharpness conjecture holds for saturated fusion systems defined on a Sylow p p -subgroup of the group G 2 ​ ( p ) {\rm G}_{2}(p) , for p ≥ 5 p\geq 5 .



1.
Introduction

Given a finite group
G
, a homology decomposition of the classifying space
BG
for
G
is a process of glueing together classifying spaces of a suitable collection of subgroups of
G
in order to obtain a space that is isomorphic to
BG
in mod-
p
homology. In
[
Dwy98
]
Dwyer proved that the collection of
p
-centric subgroups of
G
gives rise to a
sharp
homology decomposition, yielding a particularly simple formula for the
cohomology of the classifying space
BG
in terms of the cohomology of the spaces involved in it:

Theorem

(Subgroup sharpness for
p
-centric subgroups)
.

Let
p
be a prime, let
G
be a finite group and let
\mathcal{O}_{p}^{c}(G)
denote the
p
-centric orbit category of
G
. Then
H^{j}(-;\mathbb{F}_{p})
is acyclic as a controvariant functor over
\mathcal{O}_{p}^{c}(G)
:

{\lim\limits_{\longleftarrow}}_{\mathcal{O}_{p}^{c}(G)}^{i}H^{j}(-;\mathbb{F}_{p})=0

for all
i\geq 1,j\geq 0.

Such a context can be extended to saturated fusion systems
\mathcal{F}
, thanks to the homotopy theory of fusion systems introduced in
[
BLO03
]
and the existence of a unique object that plays the role of the classifying space
B\mathcal{F}
for
\mathcal{F}
(
[
Che13
]
). In this view, in
[
DP15
]
Díaz and Park stated the following sharpness conjecture for fusion systems, not only passing from finite groups to saturated fusion systems, but also replacing the cohomology functor
H^{j}(-;\mathbb{F}_{p})
with a more general Mackey functor
M
(See Definition
2.3
):

Conjecture

(Sharpness for fusion systems)
.

Let
p
be a prime, let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on a finite
p
-group
S
, let
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})
denote the centric orbit category of
\mathcal{F}
and let
M=(M^{*},M_{*}):\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}

be a Mackey functor over the full orbit category
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{F}
.
Then the contravariant part
M^{*}
over
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})
is acyclic:

{\lim\limits_{\longleftarrow}}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})}^{i}M^{*}|_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})}=0

for all
i>0
.

In particular, the sharpness conjecture for fusion systems implies the acyclicity of the cohomology functor
H^{j}(-;\mathbb{F}_{p})\colon\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
for any
j\geq 0
, which is one of the open problems listed in
[
AKO11
, III.7]
.

As a direct consequence of Dwyer’s subgroup sharpness theorem, the conjecture holds for the cohomology functor
H^{j}(-;\mathbb{F}_{p})\colon\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
and any fusion system
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{S}(G)
that is realized by a finite group
G
containing
S
as a Sylow
p
-subgroup. In
[
DP15
]
the authors showed that in fact all fusion systems realized by finite groups satisfy the sharpness conjecture for any Mackey functor
M
. Therefore it remains to test the conjecture on the so called exotic fusion systems, that is, saturated fusion systems not realizable by finite groups. Using the information on saturated fusion systems defined on a
p
-group having a maximal subgroup that is abelian uncovered by Oliver(
[
Oli14
]
), Díaz and Park proved that the sharpness conjecture also holds in this scenario (see
[
DP15
, Theorem C]
).
In this paper, we apply Díaz and Park’s methodology to saturated fusion systems defined on a Sylow
p
-subgroup of the group
{\rm G}_{2}(p)
, for
p\geq 5
, to prove that the sharpness conjecture holds for this family of saturated fusion systems as well.

Theorem A
.

Let
p\geq 5
be a prime, let
S
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of the group
{\rm G}_{2}(p)
, let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on
S
and let

M=(M^{*},M_{*}):\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}

be a Mackey functor over
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
.
Then

{\lim\limits_{\longleftarrow}}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})}^{i}M^{*}|_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})}=0

for all
i>0
.

Indeed, Theorem
A
is a consequence of the following stronger result involving simple Mackey functors
S_{Q,V}
(Definition
2.7
):

Theorem B
.

Let
p\geq 5
be a prime, let
S
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of the group
{\rm G}_{2}(p)
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on
S
. Suppose
Q\leq S
is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric and
V
is a simple
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module. Then for all
\mathcal{F}
-centric subgroups

such that their intersection

is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric the composition

(1)

S_{Q,V}(P)\xrightarrow{{\rm Res}_{T}^{P}}S_{Q,V}(T)\xrightarrow{{\rm Ind}_{T}^{R}}S_{Q,V}(R)

is zero.

One of the main ingredients used in the proof of Theorem

is the knowledge of the structure of the
\mathcal{F}
-essential subgroups of

, first described in
[
PS18
]
and then in more generality in

]
, while characterizing saturated fusion systems defined on a

We remark that in a recent paper
[
HLL22
]
, Henke, Libman and Lynd proved that the families of fusion systems affording the structure of punctured groups, such as the Benson-Solomon system

[
AKO11
, III.6.3, Theorem 6.7]
, the Parker-Stroth systems

]

in which all essential subgroups are abelian, satisfy the sharpness conjecture (although they gave an explicit proof only for the cohomology functor and not for a generic Mackey functor).
The sharpness conjecture for fusion systems is still open for other families of exotic fusion systems.

Organization of the paper. In Section 2 we recall the definitions of orbit category, Mackey functors and simple Mackey functors for fusion systems and we prove some basic properties. In Section 3 we work under the assumption that
\mathcal{F}

-group

and

and

and

2.
Preliminaries

We refer to
[
AKO11
]
for an introduction to saturated fusion systems.
Here we recall the definitions of orbit categories and Mackey functors adapted to the setting of fusion systems, following the approach of
[
DP15
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fusion system on a
p
-group
S
. The
orbit category
of
\mathcal{F}
is a category
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
whose objects are the subgroups of
S
and whose morphisms are given by the left quotient:

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})}(P,Q)=\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\backslash\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{F}}(P,Q).

If
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{F}}(P,Q)
for some
P,Q\leq S
, we denote by
[\varphi]
the corresponding morphism in
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})}(P,Q)
.

Remark 2.2
.

Any morphism
\alpha:Q^{\prime}\to P
in the orbit category is injective and can be described as a composition of the equivalence class of an inclusion with an isomorphism, i.e.

\alpha=[\iota_{Q}^{P}][\varphi]

where
\iota_{Q}^{P}
is the inclusion map
Q\hookrightarrow P
of a subgroup
Q
of
P
and
\varphi:Q^{\prime}\to Q
is an
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphism.

If
\mathcal{X}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-overconjugacy closed collection of subgroups of
S
(that is, if
P\in\mathcal{X}
then all overgroups of
P
in
S
and all
\mathcal{F}
-conjugates of
P
are contained in
\mathcal{X}
as well), then we have an analogous definition for
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})
in which the objects are exactly the subgroups in
\mathcal{X}
. An important example is given by the
centric-orbit category

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})
where
\mathcal{F}^{c}
is the collection of
\mathcal{F}
-centric subgroups of
S
, that is, all subgroups
P
of
S
such that
C_{S}(Q)\leq Q
for every subgroup
Q
of
S
that is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
P
.

We will now recall the definition of
\mathcal{X}
-restricted Mackey functor for
\mathcal{F}
as introduced in
[
DP15
, Defintion 2.1]
, specializing it for the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on a
p
-group
S
and let
\mathcal{X}
be an
\mathcal{F}
-overconjugacy closed collection of subgroups of
S
. A
\mathcal{X}
-restricted Mackey functor for
\mathcal{F}
is given by a pair of functors

M=(M^{*},M_{*}):\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}

satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

(Bivariance) The functors
M^{*}
and
M_{*}
are respectively covariant and contravariant and take the same values on all objects of
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})
. For all
P\leq S
we denote
M^{*}(P)=M_{*}(P)
by
M(P)
.

(2)

(Isomorphism) For all isomorphisms
\alpha
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})
we have
M^{*}(\alpha)=M_{*}(\alpha^{-1})
.

(3)

(
\mathcal{X}
-truncated Mackey decomposition) If
Q,R\leq P\leq S
with
Q,R,P\in\mathcal{X}
we have

M^{*}([\iota_{R}^{P}])\circ M_{*}([\iota_{Q}^{P}])=\sum\limits_{x\in[R\backslash P/Q]_{\mathcal{X}}}M_{*}([\iota_{R\cap\prescript{x}{}{Q}}^{R}])\circ M^{*}([\iota_{R\cap\prescript{x}{}{Q}}^{\prescript{x}{}{Q}}])\circ M_{*}([c_{x}|_{Q}]).

where
[R\backslash P/Q]_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes a set of representatives of double cosets of
P
of the form
RxQ
with
Q\cap\prescript{x}{}{R}\in\mathcal{X}

When
\mathcal{X}
consists of all subgroups of
S
, then
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
and we simply say that
M
is a Mackey functor for
\mathcal{F}
.

If the functor
M
is clear from the context we will adopt the following notation: for subgroups
Q\leq P\leq S
and an
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphism
\varphi
let

•

{\rm Ind}_{Q}^{P}:=M_{*}([\iota_{Q}^{P}]):M(Q)\to M(P)
;

•

{\rm Res}_{Q}^{P}:=M^{*}([\iota_{Q}^{P}]):M(P)\to M(Q)
;

•

\mathrm{Iso}(\varphi):=M_{*}([\varphi])
.

Remark 2.4
.

For any Mackey functor
M:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
, the image
M(P)
of
P\leq S
always admits a structure of
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(P)
-module.

We will be interested in restricting the domain of a Mackey functor
M
to the centric-orbit category
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})
. It is however not always the case that such a restriction will satisfy the
\mathcal{F}^{c}
-truncated Mackey formula.

In other words, using the notation of Definition
2.3
, for a Mackey functor

M=(M^{*},M_{*}):\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}

the restriction
M|_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})}=(M^{*}|_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})},M_{*}|_{\mathcal{O}(F^{c})})
is not necessarily an
\mathcal{F}^{c}
-restricted Mackey functor. A sufficient condition is given by the following criterion.

Proposition 2.5
.

[
DP15
, Proposition 4.4]

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on
S
and
M
a Mackey functor for
\mathcal{F}
over a field of characteristic
p
. Then the restriction
M|_{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{c})}
is an
\mathcal{F}^{c}
-truncated Mackey functor for
\mathcal{F}
if the composite

M(P)\xrightarrow{{\rm Res}_{P\cap R}^{P}}M(P\cap R)\xrightarrow{{\rm Ind}_{P\cap R}^{R}}M(R)

is zero whenever
P,R\leq S
are
\mathcal{F}
-centric and
P\cap R
is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric.

We are now going to introduce the important family
S_{Q,V}
of Mackey functors, parametrized by a subgroup
Q
of
S
and a left
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module
V
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fusion system on a
p
-group
S
and let
P,R\leq S
be such that
\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(P,R)\neq\emptyset
. If
\alpha\in\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(P,R)
and
V
is a
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(P)
-module, then we denote by
\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
the structure of
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(R)
-module on
V
induced by the following action

[\gamma]\cdot v:=[\alpha^{-1}\gamma\alpha]v

for all
[\gamma]\in\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(R)
and
v\in V
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on a
p
-group
S
. Consider a subgroup
Q\leq S
and a left
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module
V
. We are going to define a Mackey functor
S_{Q,V}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
.

(a)

For any subgroup
L\leq S
which is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
, fix an
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphism
\alpha:Q\to L
and define
S_{Q,V}(L):=\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
.

(b)

For
P\leq S
we define

S_{Q,V}(P):=\bigoplus_{\alpha:Q\cong L\leq_{P}P}{\rm tr}_{L}^{N_{P}(L)}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}),

where the sum is indexed over the
P
-conjugacy classes of subgroups
L
that are
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphic to
Q
. We will sometimes denote the direct summands of
S_{Q,V}(P)
by
V_{\alpha,L}(P)
.

(c)

Consider an isomorphism
[\varphi]:L_{1}\to L_{2}
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
. Suppose we fixed
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphisms
\alpha_{i}:Q\to L_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Then we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{Iso}([\varphi]):={S_{Q,V}}_{*}([\varphi]):\prescript{\alpha_{1}}{}{V}

\displaystyle\to\prescript{\alpha_{2}}{}{V}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto[\alpha_{2}^{-1}\varphi\alpha_{1}]\cdot v,

Finally we define
{S_{Q,V}}^{*}([\varphi]):=\mathrm{Iso}([\varphi^{-1}])
.

(d)

Consider an isomorphism
[\varphi]:P\to R
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
. We define
{\rm iso}([\varphi]):={S_{Q,V}}_{*}([\varphi])
on each summand
V_{\alpha,L}(P)
using point (c):

\mathrm{Iso}([\varphi])|_{V_{\alpha,L}(P)}:=\mathrm{Iso}([\varphi|_{L}]).

Finally we define
{S_{Q,V}}^{*}([\varphi]):=\mathrm{Iso}([\varphi^{-1}])
.

(e)

Consider an inclusion of subgroups
\iota_{P}^{R}:P\hookrightarrow R
of
S
and the corresponding morphism
[\iota_{P}^{R}]
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
. We define
{\rm Ind}_{P}^{R}:={S_{Q,V}}_{*}([\iota_{P}^{R}]):S_{Q,V}(P)\to S_{Q,V}(R)
on each direct summand as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm Ind}_{P}^{R}|_{V_{\alpha,L}(P)}:V_{\alpha,L}(P)

\displaystyle\to V_{\alpha,L}(R)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto{\rm tr}_{N_{P}(L)}^{N_{R}(L)}(v).

(f)

The contravariant image,
{\rm Res}_{P}^{R}:={S_{Q,V}}^{*}([\iota_{P}^{R}]):S_{Q,V}(R)\to S_{Q,V}(P)
, is defined as follows. Consider a direct summand
V_{\alpha,L}(R)
, if for all
r\in R
the conjugate
\prescript{r}{}{L}\nleq P
then
V_{\alpha,L}(R)
is mapped to zero. Suppose otherwise that there are
R
-conjugates of
L
in
P
, fix the representatives for their
P
-conjugacy classes
L_{i}=\prescript{r_{i}}{}{L}
and the
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphisms
\beta_{i}=c_{r_{i}}\circ\alpha
. Then
V_{\alpha,L}(R)
is mapped to
V_{\beta_{1},L_{1}}(P)\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\beta_{k},L_{k}}(P)
via the diagonal map:

v\mapsto v+\cdots+v.

Remark 2.8
.

Given two Mackey functors
M,N:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
we say that
N
is a subfunctor of
M
if for all
P\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
we have
N(P)\leq M(P)
. Accordingly, a Mackey functor is
simple
if it admits no subfunctor than itself and the zero functor. The work of
[
Web93
]
, generalized to the context of fusion systems by Díaz and Park
[
DP15
]
, shows that
S_{Q,V}
(as defined in Definition
2.7
) is in fact a simple Mackey functor as soon as
V
is a simple
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module and that all simple Mackey functors are of this form.

We now state and prove a property first uncovered by Webb
[
Web93
, Proposition 2.8]
, in the language used in
[
DP15
]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on a
p
-group
S
and let
S_{Q,V}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
be the simple Mackey functor associated to the subgroup
Q\leq S
and the left
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module
V
.
Let
L\leq P\leq S
be such that
L\in Q^{\mathcal{F}}
.
If
\alpha\in\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q,L)
and
V_{\alpha,L}(P)={\rm tr}_{L}^{N_{P}(L)}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})\neq 0
, then
C_{P}(L)\leq L
.

Proof.

Suppose
C_{P}(L)\nleq L
, then
L<LC_{P}(L)\leq N_{P}(L)
and

V_{\alpha,L}(P)={\rm tr}_{L}^{N_{P}(L)}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})={\rm tr}_{LC_{P}(L)}^{N_{P}(L)}{\rm tr}_{L}^{LC_{P}(L)}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}).

The action of
N_{P}(L)
on the
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(L)
-module
\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
is given by the composition of maps
N_{P}(L)\to\mathrm{Aut}_{P}(L)\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}}(L)\to\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(L)
. Both
L
and
C_{P}(L)
get mapped to the trivial subgroup under this composition, hence their action on
\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
is trivial and so
{\rm tr}_{L}^{LC_{P}(L)}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})=0
which implies
V_{\alpha,L}(P)=0
, a contradiction.
∎

We now set a notation for the subgroups of
P
that are
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
and self-centralizing in
P
, whose importance is highlighted by Lemma
2.9
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on a
p
-group
S
. For
P,Q\leq S
, we define the set

\textbf{cc}(P,Q)=\{L\leq P\ |\ \mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q,L)\neq\emptyset{\rm\ and\ }C_{P}(L)\leq L\}.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a saturated fusion system on a
p
-group
S
and let
S_{Q,V}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})\to\mathbb{F}_{p}{\operatorname{\mathbf{-mod}}}
be the simple Mackey functor associated to the subgroup
Q\leq S
and the left
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module
V
.
Let
P\leq S
.

(i)

If
S_{Q,V}(P)\neq 0
, then
\textbf{cc}(P,Q)\neq\emptyset
;

(ii)

If
L\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
and
x\in P
then
\prescript{x}{}{L}\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
.

(iii)

If
L\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
and
L\leq T\leq P
then
L\in\textbf{cc}(T,Q)
.

Proof.

Suppose
S_{Q,V}(P)\neq 0
. By Definition
2.7
(a)(b) there exists a subgroup
L
of
P
such that
L\in Q^{\mathcal{F}}
and
V_{\alpha,L}(P)\neq\emptyset
. Part (i) now follows from Lemma
2.9
.

Consider now
L\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
and
\alpha\in\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q,L)
. For all
x\in P
the composition
c_{x}\circ\alpha:Q\to\prescript{x}{}{L}
is still an isomorphism in
\mathcal{F}
. Also, since
C_{P}(L)\leq L
we have
C_{P}(\prescript{x}{}{L})=\prescript{x}{}{C_{P}(L)}\leq\prescript{x}{}{L}
proving
\prescript{x}{}{L}\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
. Thus part (ii) holds.

Finally, if
L\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
and
L\leq T\leq P
, then
C_{T}(L)\leq C_{P}(L)\leq L
which implies
L\in\textbf{cc}(T,Q)
and proves part (iii).

∎

3.
First results

In this section we work under the following:

Hypothesis 3.1
.

Suppose
p
is an odd prime,
S
is a finite
p
-group and
\mathcal{F}
is a saturated fusion system on
S
. Fix a subgroup
Q\leq S
that is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric and a simple
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.1
holds. If
K\leq S
is abelian and
S_{Q,V}(K)\neq 0
, then
K
is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
. In particular if
\alpha\in\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q,K)
then
S_{Q,V}(K)=\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.11
the assumption
S_{Q,V}(K)\neq 0
implies that there exists a subgroup
L\leq K
which is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
and such that

. Since
K
is abelian, we obtain
K=C_{K}(L)\leq L
and so
K=L
is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
. ∎

and

of

Suppose Hypothesis

\mathcal{F}
-centric subgroups of

such that

\mathcal{F}
-centric and

It is clear that under the assumptions of Hypothesis
3.3

and

P
and
R
are proper subgroups of
S
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.3
holds. Then

(i)

S_{Q,V}(P)
,
S_{Q,V}(R)
and
S_{Q,V}(T)
are all non-zero;

(ii)

T<P<S
and
T<R<S
;

(iii)

Z(S)\leq T
; and

(iv)

P
and
R
are not abelian.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from the fact that the composition (
1
) is non-zero by assumption. Part (ii) is a direct consequence of the fact that
P
and
R
are
\mathcal{F}
-centric while
T
is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric. Since
P
and
R
are
\mathcal{F}
-centric, we also get
Z(S)\leq C_{S}(P)\cap\mathrm{C}_{S}(R)\leq P\cap R=T
, proving (iii). Finally, aiming for a contradiction, suppose
P
is abelian. Then
P
is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
by Lemma
3.2
, contradicting the fact that
P
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric while
Q
is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric. The same reasoning works for
R
. Therefore
P
and
R
are not abelian and this is (iv). ∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.3
holds. Then the following holds:

(i)

C_{S}(P)\leq T
;

(ii)

C_{S}(R)\leq T
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
(i) we get that
S_{Q,V}(P)
and
S_{Q,V}(T)
are nonzero. Hence Lemma
2.11
(i) implies that both
\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
and
\textbf{cc}(T,Q)
are nonempty.
Aiming for a contradiction, suppose that for all
L\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
we have
L\nleq T
. By Lemma
2.9
and Definition
2.7
(f) we conclude that
{\rm Res}_{T}^{P}
is the zero map, contradicting Hypothesis
3.3
. So there exists
L\in\textbf{cc}(P,Q)
such that
L\leq T
. Since
P
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric, we get

C_{S}(P)=Z(P)\leq C_{P}(L)\leq L\leq T.

So
C_{S}(P)\leq T
.

To prove part (ii) note that by Lemma
2.11
a necessary condition for
{\rm Ind}_{T}^{R}
to be nonzero is that there must exist
L\in\textbf{cc}(T,Q)
such that
L\in\textbf{cc}(R,Q)
. For such subgroup
L
we then have
\mathrm{Z}(R)\leq C_{R}(L)\leq L\leq T
and so
Z(R)\leq T
. But
R
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric, so we conclude
C_{S}(R)=Z(R)\leq T
as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.3
holds and
T
is abelian. Then

(i)

T
is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to
Q
, so there exists
\alpha\in\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q,T)
such that
S_{Q,V}(T)=\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
; and

(ii)

if
T<C_{K}(T)\leq K\leq S
then
{\rm tr}_{T}^{K}(S_{Q,V}(T))=0
.

Proof.

Since
T
is abelian and
S_{Q,V}(T)\neq 0
by Lemma
3.4
, part (i) follows from Lemma
3.2
. In particular there exists
\alpha\in\mathrm{Iso}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q,T)
and
S_{Q,V}(T)=\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
.

If
T<C_{K}(T)\leq K
, then

{\rm tr}_{T}^{K}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})={\rm tr}_{C_{K}(T)}^{K}{\rm tr}_{T}^{C_{K}(T)}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}).

Since the action of
C_{K}(T)
on
\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
(a
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(T)
-module) is trivial, by definition of relative trace we obtain

{\rm tr}_{T}^{C_{K}(T)}(v)=[C_{K}(T):T]v,

for all
v\in\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
. As
C_{K}(T)
and
T
are
p
-groups and we are working in characteristic
p
, the map
{\rm tr}_{T}^{C_{K}(T)}
is identically zero. Hence
{\rm tr}_{T}^{K}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})=0
, proving (ii).
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.3
holds and
T
is abelian.

(i)

If
T
is normal in
U\in\{P,R\}
then
T=C_{U}(T)
.

(ii)

If
T
is normal in both
P
and
R
then
\mathrm{Out}_{P}(T)\neq\mathrm{Out}_{R}(T)
.

Proof.

As
T
is abelian, Lemma
3.6
(i) tells us that there exists an
\mathcal{F}
-isomorphism
\alpha:Q\cong T
and that
S_{Q,V}(T)=\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
. Let
U\in\{P,R\}
. As
T\mathrel{\unlhd}U
, we have

V_{\alpha,T}(U)={\rm tr}_{T}^{U}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}).

Applying the definition of simple Mackey functors we see that if
U=P
then the restriction
{\rm Res}_{T}^{P}
is defined as the zero map on those summands
V_{\beta,L}(P)
with
L\neq T
and as the inclusion
{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})\hookrightarrow\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
for
L=T
. So the first part of the composition of maps (
1
) reduces to
{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})\hookrightarrow\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
, that is therefore non-zero by assumption. Similarly, if
U=R
the induction
{\rm Ind}_{T}^{R}
coincides with the relative trace map
\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}\to{\rm tr}_{T}^{R}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})
, which is again non-zero.

Now, if
T<C_{U}(T)
for
U\in\{P,R\}
then by Lemma
3.6
(ii) we have
{\rm tr}_{T}^{U}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})=0
, contradicting what we just proved. Hence (i) holds.

To prove (ii), suppose
\mathrm{Out}_{P}(T)=\mathrm{Out}_{R}(T)
. Note that, as
T
is abelian and normal in both
P
and
R
, we have
\mathrm{Out}_{P}(T)\cong P/T
and
\mathrm{Out}_{R}(T)\cong R/T
. Moreover, by what we showed above, the non-zero composition of maps (
1
) is reduced to

{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})\hookrightarrow\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}\to{\rm tr}_{T}^{R}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})

Now the relative trace of an element
v\in\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
with respect to the action of
R
is

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{T}^{R}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[R/T]}[c_{x}|_{T}]\cdot v

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{x}|_{T}\in\mathrm{Out}_{R}(T)}[c_{x}|_{T}]\cdot v

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{y}|_{T}\in\mathrm{Out}_{P}(T)}[c_{y}|_{T}]\cdot v

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in[P/T]}[c_{y}|_{T}]\cdot v

\displaystyle={\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v).

Hence the maps
v\mapsto{\rm tr}_{T}^{R}({\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v))
and
v\mapsto{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}({\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v))
coincide. This second map however is the zero map as elements of
{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(\prescript{\alpha}{}{V})
are invariant under the action of
\mathrm{Out}_{T}(P)
, hence

{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}({\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v))=\sum_{x\in[P/T]}[c_{x}|_{T}]\cdot{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v)=\sum_{x\in[P/T]}{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v)=[P:T]{\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v)=0

where the last equality is justified by the fact that
[P:T]
is a power of
p
and
\prescript{\alpha}{}{V}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space. This proves
v\mapsto{\rm tr}_{T}^{R}({\rm tr}_{T}^{P}(v))
is the zero map thus composition (
1
) is also zero, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.3
holds. Then
Z(S)<T
and
T
has order at least
p^{2}
.

Proof.

Note that
Z(S)
is an abelian normal subgroup of
S
centralized by
S
. Hence
Z(S)\neq T
by Lemma
3.7
(i) and we conclude using Lemma
3.4
(iii).
∎

4.
The maximal class case

For
p\geq 5
, the Sylow
p
-subgroups of
{\rm G}_{2}(p)
are
p
-groups of maximal nilpotency class. For this reason, we now focus our attention on this class of
p
-groups.

Suppose
S
is a
p
-group of order
p^{n}
having maximal nilpotency class. Recall that the terms of the lower central series for
S
are defined as

\gamma_{2}(S):=[S,S];\quad\gamma_{i}(S):=[\gamma_{i-1},S]\quad\text{ for all }3\leq i\leq n.

Also, the terms of the upper central series of
S
are

\mathrm{Z}_{1}(S)=\mathrm{Z}(S);\quad\mathrm{Z}_{i}(S)/\mathrm{Z}_{i-1}(S)=\mathrm{Z}(S/Z_{i-1}(S))\quad\text{ for all }2\leq i\leq n-1.

If
|S|\geq p^{4}
, we set

\gamma_{1}(S):=\mathrm{C}_{S}(\gamma_{2}(S)/\gamma_{4}(S))

and, following the terminology introduced in
[
GP22
]
, we say that
S
is exceptional if
\gamma_{1}(S)\neq\mathrm{C}_{S}(\mathrm{Z}_{2}(S))
. Note that both
\gamma_{1}(S)
and
\mathrm{C}_{S}(\mathrm{Z}_{2}(S))
are maximal subgroups of
S
. We refer to
[
GP22
]
for a detailed analysis of the properties of these groups.

If
p\geq 5
and
S
is a Sylow
p
-subgroups of
{\rm G}_{2}(p)
, then
|S|=p^{6}
,
\gamma_{1}(S)
is extraspecial and
\mathrm{Z}(\gamma_{1}(S))=\mathrm{Z}(S)
. In particular
S
is exceptional.

In this section, we assume the following:

Hypothesis 4.1
.

Suppose Hypothesis
3.3
holds and
S
is a
p
-group of maximal nilpotency class.

A crucial role is played by the characterization of the essential subgroups of saturated fusion systems on maximal calss
p
-groups:

Theorem 4.2
.

[
GP22
, Theorem D]

Suppose that
p
is a prime,
S
is a
p
-group of maximal class and order at least
p^{4}
and
\mathcal{F}
is a saturated fusion system on
S
. If
E
is an
\mathcal{F}
-essential subgroup, then either
|E|\leq p^{3}
, or
E=\gamma_{1}(S)
or
E=\mathrm{C}_{S}(\mathrm{Z}_{2}(S))
. Furthermore,
if
S
is exceptional, then
|E|\neq p^{3}
.

Thanks to the work of Díaz and Park
[
DP15
]
, we can exclude the case in which the group
\gamma_{1}(S)
is abelian:

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose Hypothesis
4.1
holds. Then
\gamma_{1}(S)
is not abelian.

Proof.

The proof of
[
DP15
, Theorem C]
implies that the composition

S_{Q,V}(P)\xrightarrow{{\rm Res}_{T}^{P}}S_{Q,V}(T)\xrightarrow{{\rm Ind}_{T}^{R}}S_{Q,V}(R)

is zero, contradicting our assumptions.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose Hypothesis
4.1
holds. If
T\mathrel{\unlhd}S
then

(i)

T=\gamma_{i}(S)
for some
i\geq 2
;

(ii)

\gamma_{i-1}(S)\leq C_{S}(T)
is abelian (so in particular
T
is abelian); and

(iii)

C_{S}(T)
is not a maximal subgroup of
S
.

In particular
T\neq Z_{2}(S)
and
T\neq\gamma_{2}(S)
.

Proof.

First note that the assumption
T\mathrel{\unlhd}S
implies that the group
T
is not
S
-centric. Indeed
T
is fully centralized in
\mathcal{F}

[
RS09
, Theorem 5.2]
and if it were
S
-centric then
C_{S}(T)=Z(T)
and, for any
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate
T^{\prime}
of
T
, we would have
|C_{S}(T)|\geq|C_{S}(T^{\prime})|\geq|Z(T^{\prime})|=|Z(T)|=|C_{S}(T)|
, and so
C_{S}(T^{\prime})=Z(T^{\prime})\leq T^{\prime}
, implying that
T
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric, a contradiction.

Since
S
has maximal nilpotency class and
T
is normal in
S
but not a maximal subgroup of
S
by Lemma
3.4
(ii), we deduce that
T=\gamma_{i}(S)
for some
i\geq 2
, and this is statement (i). Note that
C_{S}(\gamma_{i}(S))=\gamma_{j}(S)
for some
j\geq 1
.
Since
T
is not
S
-centric, we get
\gamma_{i-1}(S)\leq C_{S}(T)
and so
\gamma_{i-1}(S)
is abelian, proving (ii).
Now, aiming for a contradiction, suppose
C_{S}(T)
is a maximal subgroup of
S
. Thus
|\mathrm{Aut}_{S}(T)|=[S\colon C_{S}(T)]=p
. Since
T
is abelian and normal in
S
, by Lemma
3.7
(i) we get
T=C_{P}(T)=C_{R}(T)
. Thus
\mathrm{Aut}_{P}(T)\neq 1\neq\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(T)
, giving

\mathrm{Aut}_{P}(T)=\mathrm{Aut}_{S}(T)=\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(T).

This implies
\mathrm{Out}_{P}(T)=\mathrm{Out}_{R}(T)
contradicting Lemma
3.7
(ii). Hence (iii) holds.

For the chaser of the Lemma, note that
C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
is a maximal subgroup of
S
(contrary to the statement of part (iii)) and if
T=\gamma_{2}(S)
then by part (ii) the group
\gamma_{1}(S)
should be abelian, contradicting Lemma
4.3
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose Hypothesis
4.1
holds. Then either
T\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
or
T\leq C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
.

Proof.

Aiming for a contradiction, suppose
T\nleq\gamma_{1}(S)
and
T\nleq C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
. By Lemma
3.8

we have
Z(S)<T
and
|T|\geq p^{2}
. So
P
and
R
are subgroups of
S
of order at least
p^{3}
not contained in
\gamma_{1}(S)
. By
[
Gra18
, Lemma 2.12]
we deduce that
Z_{2}(S)\leq P\cap R=T
. Note that
Z_{2}(S)\neq T
by Lemma
4.4
, so
|T|\geq p^{3}
. In particular, Theorem
4.2
and the fact that
T
is not
\mathcal{F}
-centric imply that
T
is not contained in any
\mathcal{F}
-essential subgroup of
S
and so, by the Alperin-Goldschmidt’s fusion theorem (
[
AKO11
, Theorem I.3.5]
), all
\mathcal{F}
-conjugates of
T
can be found applying automorphisms of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{F}}(S)
.

Let
t\in T\setminus(\gamma_{1}(S)\cup\mathrm{C}_{S}(Z_{2}(S)))
. By
[
GP22
, Lemma 3.4]
we have
C_{S}(t)=\langle Z(S),t\rangle
and so by Lemma
3.4
(iii) we get

C_{S}(T)\leq C_{S}(t)=\langle Z(S),t\rangle\leq T.

Thus
T
is
S
-centric. Since
Z(S)
,
\gamma_{1}(S)
and
C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
are characteristic subgroups of
S
we deduce that all
\mathcal{F}
-conjugates of
T
contain
Z(S)
and are not contained in
\gamma_{1}(S)
or
C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
. The same reasoning used for
T
then shows that all its
\mathcal{F}
-conjugates are
S
-centric, impying that
T
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric, a contradiction. This proves the statement.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose Hypothesis
4.1
holds and
\gamma_{1}(S)
is extraspecial. Then the following holds

(1)

either
T\mathrel{\unlhd}S
or
T\leq\gamma_{1}(S)=\mathrm{N}_{S}(T)
; and

(2)

T
is not
S
-centric.

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.8
and
4.5

we have
Z(S)<T\subseteq\gamma_{1}(S)\cup C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
. Thus
[
GP22
, Lemma 3.7]
implies that either
\mathrm{N}_{S}(T)\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
or
T\mathrel{\unlhd}S
. If
T
is not normal in
S
, then
T\leq N_{S}(T)\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
and since
\gamma_{1}(S)
is extraspecial and
[\gamma_{1}(S),\gamma_{1}(S)]=Z(S)\leq T
we deduce that
\gamma_{1}(S)=\mathrm{N}_{S}(T)
.

As for the second statement, aiming for a contradiction, suppose
T
is
S
-centric, that is,
C_{S}(T)\leq T
. As
\mathcal{F}
is saturated, if
T
is fully normalized in
\mathcal{F}
then it is
\mathcal{F}
-centric, contradicting Hypothesis
4.1
.
Hence
T
is
\mathcal{F}
-conjugate to a subgroup
T^{\prime}
of
S
with
|N_{S}(T^{\prime})|>|N_{S}(T)|
. Using Lemma
3.8
and the fact that
\gamma_{1}(S)
is extraspecial, we get
[\gamma_{1}(S),\gamma_{1}(S)]=Z(S)<T
and so
T\mathrel{\unlhd}\gamma_{1}(S)
. Thus
N_{S}(T)=\gamma_{1}(S)
and
T^{\prime}\mathrel{\unlhd}S
.
Also,
|T|=|T^{\prime}|
, so
T^{\prime}
is not a maximal subgroup of
S
and we deduce that
T^{\prime}\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
. As
\gamma_{1}(S)
is extraspecial and
T
and
T^{\prime}
are normal subgroups of
\gamma_{1}(S)
having the same order, we deduce that

|C_{S}(T^{\prime})|=|C_{S}(T)|.

Therefore

|C_{S}(T^{\prime})|=|C_{S}(T)|\leq|T|=|T^{\prime}|.

Now, since
T^{\prime}
is normal in
S
, which has maximal nilpotency class, we have
T^{\prime}=\gamma_{i}(S)
and
C_{S}(T^{\prime})=\gamma_{j}(S)
for some
i,j\geq 1
. Thus
C_{S}(T^{\prime})\leq T^{\prime}
. Now,
T^{\prime}
is
S
-centric and fully normalized in
\mathcal{F}
, and so
T^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric. However, this implies that
T
is
\mathcal{F}
-centric as well, a contradiction.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorems
A
and
B

We now focus on a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
{\rm G}_{2}(p)
, for
p\geq 5
, obtaining the final ingredients to prove Theorems
A
and
B
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Aiming for a contradiction, suppose Theorem
B
is false. Hence there are subgroups
Q,P,R\leq S
and a simple
\mathbb{F}_{p}\mathrm{Out}_{\mathcal{F}}(Q)
-module
V
such that
P
and
R
are
\mathcal{F}
-centric,
Q
and
T:=P\cap R
are not
\mathcal{F}
-centric and the composition

S_{Q,V}(P)\xrightarrow{{\rm Res}_{T}^{P}}S_{Q,V}(T)\xrightarrow{{\rm Ind}_{T}^{R}}S_{Q,V}(R)

is non-zero. Hence Hypothesis
4.1
holds.

We first show that
\mathrm{N}_{S}(T)=\gamma_{1}(S)
. By Lemma
4.6
either
\mathrm{N}_{S}(T)=\gamma_{1}(S)
or
T\mathrel{\unlhd}S
. Aiming for a contradiction, suppose the latter holds.
By Lemma
4.4
we get
T=\gamma_{i}(S)
for some
i\geq 2
such that
\gamma_{i-1}(S)
is an abelian subgroup of
\gamma_{1}(S)
. Since
|S|=p^{6}
and
\gamma_{1}(S)
is extraspecial, we deduce that either
T=Z(S)
or
T=Z_{2}(S)
, contradicting Lemmas
3.8
and
4.4
. Hence
T
is not normal in
S
and we conclude that
\mathrm{N}_{S}(T)=\gamma_{1}(S)
.

In particular
|T|\leq p^{4}
. Also, by Lemma
3.8
the group
T
has order at least
p^{2}
. If
|T|=p^{4}
then
P
and
R
are maximal subgroups of
S
. Since
S
has maximal nilpotency class, the intersection of any pair of distinct maximal subgroups of
S
coincides with the group
\gamma_{2}(S)
. Hence
T=\gamma_{2}(S)
, contradicting the fact that
T
is not normal in
S
. Thus
p^{2}\leq|T|\leq p^{3}
.
By Lemma
4.6
, the group
T
is not
S
-centric, so if
|T|=p^{3}
then it is not abelian.

Let’s now consider the groups
P
and
R
. Recall that
P
and
R
are distinct
\mathcal{F}
-centric subgroups of
S
that are non abelian by Lemma
3.4
(iv).
By Lemma
3.5
we have
Z(P)\leq T
and
Z(R)\leq T
. Since
Z_{2}(S)\neq T
and if
|T|=p^{3}
then
T
is not abelian, we deduce that
P,R\nleq C_{S}(Z_{2}(S))
.

Let
U\in\{P,R\}
.

Claim 1:

If
U\nleq\gamma_{1}(S)
then
\gamma_{3}(S)\leq U\cap\gamma_{1}(S)<U
and
|U|\geq p^{4}
.

Proof:
Set
U_{0}=U\cap\gamma_{1}(S)
. By
[
Gra18
, Lemma 2.12]
we have
U_{0}=\gamma_{i}(S)
for some
i\geq 2
and
[U\colon U_{0}]=p
. Since
|U|\geq p^{3}
, we deduce that
Z_{2}(S)=\gamma_{4}(S)\leq U_{0}
, and so
TZ_{2}(S)\leq U_{0}
. This proves
|U_{0}|\geq p^{3}
, and so
\gamma_{3}(S)\leq U_{0}<U
and
|U|\geq p^{4}
.

Claim 2:

If
U\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
then either
U=\gamma_{1}(S)
or
|U|=p^{4}
and
T=Z(U)
has order
p^{2}
.

Proof:
Since
U
is
S
-centric nonabelian, we deduce that either
U=\gamma_{1}(S)
or
|U|=p^{4}
and
|Z(U)|=p^{2}
. In the latter case, since
Z(U)\leq T
and
|T|\leq p^{3}
, we deduce that
T
is abelian and so
|T|=p^{2}
, that is,
T=Z(U)
.

Claim 3:

If
U\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
then
U=\gamma_{1}(S)
.

Proof:
Aiming for a contradiction, suppose
U<\gamma_{1}(S)
. Hence by Claim 2
|U|=p^{4}
and
T=Z(U)
. In particular,
T\mathrel{\unlhd}U
and
T<C_{U}(T)=U
, contradicting Lemma
3.7
(i).

Suppose first that both
P
and
R
are not contained in
\gamma_{1}(S)
. Then Claim 1 implies that
\gamma_{3}(S)\leq P\cap R=T
and so
T=\gamma_{3}(S)
, contradicting the fact that
T
is not normal in
S
. Thus, without loss of generality, we can suppose
P\leq\gamma_{1}(S)
. Hence by Claim 3 we have
P=\gamma_{1}(S)
and
R\nleq\gamma_{1}(S)
. Again by Claim 1,
\gamma_{3}(S)<R
. therefore
\gamma_{3}(S)\leq P\cap R=T
, a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A
.
