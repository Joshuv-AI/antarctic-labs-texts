Fizay-Noah Lee

Abstract
. The Nernst-Planck-Stokes (NPS) system models electroconvection of ions in a fluid. We consider the system, for two oppositely charged ionic species, on three dimensional bounded domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions for the ionic concentrations (modelling ion selectivity), Dirichlet boundary conditions for the electrical potential (modelling an applied potential), and no-slip boundary conditions for the fluid velocity. In this paper, we obtain quantitative bounds on solutions of the NPS system in the long time limit, which we use to prove 1) the existence of a compact global attractor with finite fractal (box-counting) dimension and 2) space-time averaged electroneutrality ρ ≈ 0 \rho\approx 0 in the singular limit of Debye length going to zero, ϵ → 0 \epsilon\to 0 .



1.
Introduction

We consider the Nernst-Planck-Stokes (NPS) system on an open connected bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary. This system models electroconvection of ions in a fluid in the presence of boundaries. In this paper we focus on the case of two oppositely charged ionic species (valences
\pm 1
). The full system is then given by the Nernst-Planck equations

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{1}+u\cdot\nabla c_{1}

\displaystyle=D_{1}{\mbox{div}\,}(\nabla c_{1}+c_{1}\nabla\Phi)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{2}+u\cdot\nabla c_{2}

\displaystyle=D_{2}{\mbox{div}\,}(\nabla c_{2}-c_{2}\nabla\Phi)

coupled to the Poisson equation

(2)

-\epsilon\Delta\Phi=c_{1}-c_{2}=\rho

and to the time dependent Stokes system

(3)

\partial_{t}u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p=-K\rho\nabla\Phi,\quad{\mbox{div}\,}u=0.

Above,
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the local ionic concentrations of the cation and anion, respectively,
\rho
is a rescaled local charge density,
u
is the fluid velocity, and
\Phi
is a rescaled electrical potential. The positive constant
K
is a coupling constant given by the product of Boltzmann’s constant and the absolute temperature. The positive constants
D_{i}
are the ionic diffusitives, and
\epsilon
is a rescaled dielectric permittivity of the solvent and is proportional to the square of the Debye length. The Debye length in typical electrolytes (e.g. water) is very small, on the order of a few nanometers. Lastly,
\nu>0
is the kinematic viscoscity of the fluid. The dimensional counterparts of
\Phi
and
\rho
are given by
(K/e)\Phi
and
e\rho
, respectively, where
e
is elementary charge.

According to (
1
), ionic concentrations are transported by the fluid, diffuse under their own concentration gradients, and are transported by the electrical field, which in turn, by (
2
), is generated by the charge density. The fluid itself is also forced by the electrical field, as indicated by the nonlinear forcing term in (
3
).

Electroconvective systems like NPS have widespread applications in many fields of biology and chemistry and in engineering sciences (e.g. semiconductors, desalination processes, electrodialysis, etc.). We refer the reader to the books
[
18
,
20
,
21
]
for discussions on the applications of ionic electrodiffusion and electroconvection and for the physical derivation of the equations (
1
)-(
3
).

For boundary conditions, we distinguish, as in
[
12
]
, between equilibrium and nonequilibrium boundary conditions.
Equilibrium boundary conditions
are boundary conditions on
c_{i}
and
\Phi
that admit steady state NPS solutions with
constant
electrochemical potentials, defined by

(4)

\mu_{1}=\log c_{1}+\Phi,\quad\mu_{2}=\log c_{2}-\Phi.

In this case, these steady state solutions are also unique. Equilibrium boundary conditions include the case where
c_{i}
obey
blocking (no-flux) boundary conditions

(5)

{\partial_{n}\mu_{i}}_{|\partial\Omega}=0,\quad i=1,2

and
\Phi
obey inhomogeneous Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin boundary conditions. Also included is the case where
\Phi
obeys Dirichlet boundary conditions and
c_{i}
obey blocking boundary conditions on boundary portions
S_{i}\subset\partial\Omega
while satisfying Dirichlet boundary conditions on the complements
\partial\Omega\setminus S_{i}
in such a way that

(6)

{\mu_{1}}_{|\partial\Omega\setminus S_{1}}=\text{constant},\quad{\mu_{2}}_{|\partial\Omega\setminus S_{2}}=\text{constant}.

We remark that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
need not be identical, and we allow for
S_{i}\in\{\emptyset,\partial\Omega\}.
In all the cases mentioned above, we take no-slip boundary conditions for the fluid velocity
u_{|\partial\Omega}=0
.

In the mathematics literature, many authors have considered various Nernst-Planck models in the context of equilibrium boundary conditions. For the uncoupled Nernst-Planck equations, global existence of strong solutions is proved in
[
3
,
5
]
for various equilibrium boundary conditions with blocking boundary conditions for
c_{i}
. Global existence of strong solutions of the two dimensional Nernst-Planck-Navier-Stokes (NPNS) system with various equilibrium boundary conditions is proved in
[
4
,
8
,
26
]
. A common feature in analyzing Nernst-Planck/NPNS with equilibrium boundary conditions is the existence of a natural dissipative energy inequality, which gives a priori bounds, from which higher regularity bounds are obtained through boostrapping. This same inequality is also used in
[
10
,
25
]
and
[
17
]
to obtain small data global regularity for three dimensional NPNS and large data global regularity for three dimensional NPS, respectively.

For equilibrium boundary conditions, the corresponding unique steady state solution (
Boltzmann state
solution) is characterized by zero fluid flow
u^{*}\equiv 0
and concentrations
c_{i}^{*}
, related to
\Phi^{*}
by

(7)

c_{1}^{*}=Z_{1}^{-1}e^{-\Phi^{*}},\quad c_{2}^{*}=Z_{2}^{-1}e^{\Phi^{*}}

with
Z_{i}>0
appropriate constants, determined by the (equilibrium) boundary conditions and/or initial conditions
[
8
,
10
]
. Here,
\Phi^{*}
is the unique solution to a nonlinear Poisson equation, called the Poisson-Boltzmann equation

(8)

-\epsilon\Delta\Phi^{*}=Z_{1}^{-1}e^{-\Phi^{*}}-Z_{2}^{-1}e^{\Phi^{*}}

with the relevant boundary conditions. In two dimensions, these Boltzmann states are globally asymptotically stable - that is, starting from arbitrarily large initial conditions, solutions converge towards Boltzmann states. In three dimensions, Boltzmann states are at least locally asymptotically stable - solutions converge towards the relevant Botlzmann state, provided they are initially sufficiently close to them. These facts are established for NPNS in
[
8
]
(2D) and
[
10
]
(3D).

In full generality, one may define nonequilibrium boundary conditions in the negation - that is,
nonequilibrium boundary conditions
are boundary conditions that do
not
admit steady state NPS solutions with constant electrochemical potentials. A concrete example is given by
c_{1}
and
\Phi
satisfying Dirichlet boundary conditions on a nonempty boundary portion
S\subset\partial\Omega
in such a way that
(\log c_{1}+\Phi)_{|S}
is not constant.

Relative to the equilibrium case, there are much fewer rigorous mathematical results for nonequilibrium boundary conditions. On the other hand, physically, nonequilibrium boundary conditions are linked to various interesting phenomena. For example, both numerical
[
14
,
19
]
and experimental
[
16
,
22
]
evidence exists for the development of instabilities to NPNS/NPS systems associated with nonequilibrium boundary conditions, whereby for boundary conditions sufficiently far from equilibrium, vortical and even chaotic flow patterns are observed adjacent to the fluid/boundary interface. Such observations are reminiscent of the pattern formation and thermal turbulence associated with Rayleigh-Bénard convection. Despite the prevalence and widespread application of this so-called
electrokinetic instability
, our theoretical understanding of this phenomenon is far from complete. On one hand, the question of the exact mechanism behind the development of these instabilities is yet to be fully resolved (c.f. classical works
[
24
,
27
]
and also the works
[
19
,
23
]
). On the other, a mathematically rigorous qualitative picture of the long time dynamics of solutions of NPNS/NPS for nonequililbrium boundary conditions
after
the onset of instability is also, to this date, unavailable.

In this paper, our focus is on the long time regime, long after the onset of electrokinetic instability. For nonequilibrium boundary conditions, however, even the question of global existence of strong solutions of NPNS/NPS is not resolved in full generality. The works
[
8
]
(2D NPNS) and
[
11
,
17
]
(3D NPS) establish global regularity for large classes of nonequilibrium boundary conditions. In particular, in
[
11
]
, the authors consider nonequilibrium boundary conditions whereby both the concentrations
c_{i}
and
\Phi
satisfy
arbitrary
Dirichlet boundary conditions, together with no-slip for
u
:

(9)

\displaystyle{c_{i}}_{|\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i}>0,\quad i=1,2

(10)

\displaystyle\Phi_{|\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=W

(11)

\displaystyle u_{|\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0.

We observe that other than for special choices of
\gamma_{i}
and
W
, the relation
\log\gamma_{1}+\Phi=
constant,
\log\gamma_{2}-\Phi=
constant does not hold, implying that, generally, boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
) are nonequilibrium.

In this paper, we consider the same class of boundary conditions as in
[
11
]
, given by (
9
)-(
11
). Also, for simplicity, we assume
\gamma_{i}
and
W
are smooth. Beyond the aforementioned global regularity however, there are few rigorous results on the long time dynamics of solutions of NPS or NPNS with nonequilibrium boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
); the goal of this paper is to study this long time behavior.

In
[
2
]
, the authors establish the existence of a finite dimensional (i.e. finite fractal (box-counting) dimension) global attractor for NPNS on the two dimensional torus, in the presence of a time independent external force acting on the fluid velocity
u
(in addition to the time dependent nonlinear electrical forcing term). A global attractor is a compact subset of phase space that is invariant under the solution map and attracting in the sense that all time dependent solutions are drawn arbitrarily close to it (in the appropriate phase space topology) in the long time limit. However, insofar as it is our goal to analyze the long time behavior of NPS solutions beyond the onset of electrokinetic instability, it is important to account for the presence of boundaries as this is a phenomenon that takes place, empirically, in a boundary layer. This motivates our first main result, in Section
3
, which establishes the existence of a finite dimensional global attractor for NPS on an arbitrary open, connected, bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary, with boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
). Our results (Theorems
2
and
3
) are the first such results for the NPS system on a domain with boundary. The existence of a finite dimensional global attractor, in particular, implies that the long time trajectories of NPS solutions are limited to a finite dimensional manifold,
independent
of initial conditions, thus simplifying, at least on a theoretical level, the long time behavior of electroconvective flow, even in the emergence of the turbulent behavior observed experimentally/numerically.

It is worth noting that, at this stage, it is an open problem as to whether the NPS system has a global attractor for
arbitrary
(but sufficiently regular) nonequiliubrium boundary conditions. For example, while there are other classes of nonequilibrium boundary conditions for which unique, global strong solutions are known to exist
[
8
,
17
]
, the analysis of this paper does not naturally extend to these other cases (these are cases that may be described as having
mixed
boundary conditions; say, the ionic concentrations satisfying a mixture of Dirichlet and blocking boundary conditions). For our boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
), the starting point of our proof of the existence of a global attractor is the energy inequality given in Lemma
1
, which gives absorbing ball, and in particular
uniform in time
, bounds on solutions of NPS (this lemma is taken from
[
11
]
, where it is used to prove global regularity of the NPS system). This situation is in contrast to the cases in
[
8
,
17
]
where the corresponding bounds are at least
exponential in time
. Determining whether or not a global attractor exists in these other cases, then, critically depends on whether or not these exponential/superexponential in time bounds are sharp or can be improved. This remains an open task.

We also remark that an energy inequality similar to that of Lemma
1
is also used in
[
2
]
to prove the existence of a global attractor for NPNS on the two dimensional torus. One critical difference, however, is that the energy functional of Lemma
1
is a linear combination, with precisely chosen coefficients (c.f. (
19
)), of various norms, including the
L^{2}
norms of the ionic concentrations
c_{i}
, the kinetic energy
\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
and the
H^{-1}
norm of the charge density
\rho
. It it the presence of boundaries that necessitates the use of this specific energy functional, a need that does not arise in the absence of boundaries.

Section
4
of this paper is devoted to a feature of charged fluids called electroneutrality, which refers to the fact that at distances larger than the Debye length from charged boundaries, roughly neutral charge density is maintained
|\rho|\ll 1
. Formally, electroneutrality is also a natural expectation given that
\epsilon
is a small parameter (c.f. (
2
)). This leads to the study of the NPS system in the limit of
\epsilon\to 0
. The mathematical challenge posed by this limit is the fact that
\epsilon
is a coefficient of a second order operator, thus making the limit singular.

In the case of equilibrium boundary conditions (heuristically, when no electric current is flowing through the fluid), electroneutrality is verified rigorously in
[
9
,
15
]
. The mathematical statement of electroneutrality for equilibrium boundary conditions is that

(12)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{x\in K}|\rho_{\epsilon}(t,x)|=0.

Above the
\epsilon
subscript indicates the dependence of
\rho
on the value of
\epsilon
, and
K\subset\Omega
is any compact subset. Thus, to be more precise, (
12
) establishes uniform pointwise electroneutrality, away from boundaries, in the long time limit. The proof of (
12
) in
[
9
]
follows a two-step analysis. First, in equilibrium, the existence of a dissipative energy inequality implies that the global attractor of
c_{i,\epsilon}(t,x)
is a singleton (c.f. (
7
)), and thus
\rho_{\epsilon}(t,x)\to_{t\to\infty}\rho_{\epsilon}^{*}(x)
, where
\rho_{\epsilon}^{*}
is determined uniquely by the prescribed data. The second step is to then analyze the steady state charge density
\rho_{\epsilon}^{*}
and to establish
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{x\in K}|\rho_{\epsilon}^{*}(x)|=0
on compact subsets
K\subset\Omega
.

Now the question we consider is whether electroneutrality is just an equilibrium feature. For general Dirichlet boundary conditions on
c_{i}
that are nonequilibrium, it is not known and not expected that the global attractor is a singleton. So, it is not possible to identically replicate the two-step analysis of
[
9
]
to prove an electroneutrality statement. Nonetheless, the existence of a compact global attractor allows us to replace the analysis on a fixed steady state with the analysis of general elements of the global attractor obtained in Section
3
. Due to the added level of generality, we do not expect a result as strong as (
12
) to hold. However, we prove the following weaker statement, which establishes electroneutrality in a space-time averaged sense,

(13)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}^{2}(t,x)\,dx\right)\,dt=0.

We emphasize that the above result (see Theorem
4
for the precise statement) holds for arbitrary Dirichlet data on
c_{i}
, which in particular includes cases where, based on experiments and numerical simulations, the time dependent solutions are expected to exhibit unstable and even chaotic behavior. While in spirit, the proof of this space-time averaged electroneutrality is a consequence of the absorbing ball properties established in Section
3
, the actual proof uses a different absorbing ball estimate, with bounds in
L^{\infty}
. Namely, we use a result proved in
[
12
]
, which gives long time upper and lower pointwise bounds on the ionic concentrations
c_{i}
in terms of only the boundary data (Theorem
5
). In partciular, these bounds are independent of
\epsilon
. In contrast, the bounds obtained in Section
3
are in
L^{2}
based Sobolev spaces and depend on
\epsilon
in such a way that they become unbounded in the limit of
\epsilon\to 0
. To the best of our knowledge, no prior mathematical work exists on electroneutrality under nonequilibrium conditions.

We note that our focus on the NPS system, as opposed to the NPNS system, is physically relevant in the sense that the typical length scale of electroconvective systems is small enough to justify the use of the Stokes equations. However, this choice is also due to our choice of spatial dimension (i.e. three) and the fact that we have global regularity in the case of three dimensional NPS for our boundary conditions (see Theorem
1
below). This is not the case for three dimensional NPNS, due to coupling with the Navier-Stokes equations for which global regularity in three dimensions, as in well known, is an open problem. The results of this paper, then, hold for NPNS in two dimensions, where global regularity is known and for which adequate quantitative bounds on the velocity
u
are available. The proof of the corresponding results in this case follow from the computations of this paper, with straightforward modifications having to do with the added presence of the nonlinear term in the Navier-Stokes equations; however, we shall not pursue this extension here.

This paper is structured is follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the relevant functional setting along with various notation. In Section
3
, we prove the existence of a finite dimensional global attractor to the NPS system. In Section
4
we show that electroneutrality holds for NPS in a space-time averaged sense.

the

H_{0}^{1}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

H^{1}(\Omega)

We remark that for

(f,g)_{H_{0}^{1}}=(f,-\Delta g)_{L^{2}}=(g,-\Delta f)_{L^{2}}.

We define
H
and

to be the closures of
\{f\in(C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega))^{3}\,|\,{\mbox{div}\,}f=0\}
in

and
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{3}
, respectively. We endow
H
with the
L^{2}
inner product and
V
with the Dirichlet inner product,
(f,g)_{V}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla f:\nabla g\,dx.

To avoid dealing directly with the pressure
p

3
) projected onto the space of divergence free functions. We denote by
\mathbb{P}:(L^{2}(\Omega))^{3}\to H
the Leray projection which is the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
onto
H
(see
[
7
]
for more details). Then, applying
\mathbb{P}
to (

), we obtain

(14)

\partial_{t}u+\nu Au=-K\mathbb{P}(\rho\nabla\Phi)

where
A=\mathbb{P}(-\Delta):\mathcal{D}(A)=(H^{2})^{3}\cap V\to H
is the Stokes operator whose inverse

is a self-adjoint, nonnegative compact operator on

. From elliptic regularity estimates for

(see e.g.
[

]
), we also have that

. We remark that for
f,g\in\mathcal{D}(A)
, we have that
(f,g)_{V}=(f,Ag)_{H}=(g,Af)_{H}
. This follows from the self-adjointness of
\mathbb{P}
and the fact that for
f,g\in\mathcal{D}(A)
, we have
\mathbb{P}f=f
and
\mathbb{P}g=g
.

We define
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}\times L^{2}\times H
,
\mathcal{V}=H^{1}\times H^{1}\times V
, and
\mathcal{H}^{2}=H^{2}\times H^{2}\times\mathcal{D}(A)
and endow them with the norms

\displaystyle\|(f,g,h)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|(f,g,h)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}^{2}=

\mathcal{V}_{0}=H_{0}^{1}\times H_{0}^{1}\times V

(15)

\|(f,g,h)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}^{2}=\|f\|_{H_{0}^{1}}^{2}+\|g\|_{H_{0}^{1}}^{2}+\|h\|_{V}^{2},

\mathcal{V}^{+}=\{(f,g,h)\in\mathcal{V}\,|\,f,g\geq 0\}\subset\mathcal{V}

3.
Existence and Finite Dimensionality of Global Attractor

The global existence and regularity of solutions of the NPS system (
1
)-(
3
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
) is established in
[
11
]
. In particular, the following theorem is proved.

Theorem 1
.

Let
T>0
be arbitrary. Let initial conditions
c_{i}(\cdot,0)\geq 0
,
c_{i}(\cdot,0)\in H^{1}
and
u_{0}\in V
, and smooth boundary conditions
{c_{i}}_{|\partial\Omega}=\gamma_{i}>0
and
\Phi_{|\partial\Omega}=W
be given. Then the NPS system (
1
)-(
3
) has a unique global strong solution on
[0,T]
. That is, there exist unique
0\leq c_{i}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}),u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;V)\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{D}(A))
that solve (
1
)-(
3
) in the sense of distributions and satisfy the boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
) in the sense of traces.

Thus, for fixed, smooth boundary conditions
{c_{i}}_{|\partial\Omega}=\gamma_{i}>0
and
\Phi_{|\partial\Omega}=W
, we define the
solution map

S(\cdot,\cdot)
, seen as a map from
[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{V}^{+}
to
\mathcal{V}^{+}
, such that
(t,x)\mapsto S(t,w_{0})(x)
is the unique solution to the initial-boundary value problem (
1
)-(
3
) with initial conditions
w_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
and satisfying the fixed boundary conditions. By some abuse of notation, we also denote
S(t)
to be the map from
\mathcal{V}^{+}
to itself that maps
w_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
to
S(t,w_{0})\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
. We note that due to the uniqueness of strong solutions to the NPS system, the map
S
satisfies the semigroup property

(16)

S(t+s)=S(t)S(s)=S(s)S(t),\quad t,s\geq 0.

The goal of this section is to show the existence and finite dimensionality of a global attractor associated with the NPS system.

Definition 1
.

We say that
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{V}^{+}
is a global attractor of the NPS system (
1
)-(
3
) with boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
) if
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the following:

(1)

\mathcal{A}
is compact in
\mathcal{V}

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is invariant under
S
i.e.
S(t)\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}
for all
t\geq 0

(3)

\mathcal{A}
is maximal in the sense that if
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{V}^{+}
is bounded in
\mathcal{V}
and satisfies
S(t)\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}
for all
t\geq 0
, then
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}

(4)

For all
v\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}d_{\mathcal{V}}(S(t)v,\mathcal{A})=0
, where
d_{\mathcal{V}}(S(t)v,\mathcal{A}):=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{A}}\|S(t)v-x\|_{\mathcal{V}}

(5)

\mathcal{A}
is connected in
\mathcal{V}.

We establish the existence and finite dimensionality of a global attractor of the NPS system in two separate theorems.

Theorem 2
.

The NPS system (
1
)-(
3
) with boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
) possesses a global attractor
\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 3
.

The global attractor
\mathcal{A}
has finite fractal (box-counting) dimension (c.f. (
96
)) in
\mathcal{V}
:

(17)

d_{f}(\mathcal{A})\leq D<\infty

where
D
depends only on parameters and boundary conditions.

From here on, for initial conditions
w_{0}=(c_{1}(0),c_{2}(0),u(0))\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
, we denote

(18)

w(t,x)=(c_{1}(t,x),c_{2}(t,x),u(t,x))=S(t,w_{0})(x).

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2
.

To prove Theorem
2
, we first establish some properties of the solution map
S
.

Proposition 1
.

The solution map
S
satisfies

(I)

(absorbing ball) for initial conditions
w_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
there exist
T_{0}=T_{0}(\|w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{H}})>0
and
R>0
independent of
w_{0}
such that for all
t\geq T_{0}
,
S(t,w_{0})\in B^{2}_{R}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}^{+}\,|\,\|v\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\leq R\}

(II)

(continuity) for all
t\geq 0
,
S(t):\mathcal{V}^{+}\to\mathcal{V}^{+}
is continuous in the
\mathcal{V}
topology

(III)

(injectivity) for all
t\geq 0
,
S(t):\mathcal{V}^{+}\to\mathcal{V}^{+}
is injective.

To simplify the proof of the proposition, it is helpful to invoke the following result, the first half of which is found in
[
11
]
:

Lemma 1
.

For the NPS system (
1
)-(
3
) with boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
), there exist constants
\delta,C_{j}>0
,
j=1,2,3,4

S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})\underline{w}_{0}

\tilde{w}(t)=S(t)(w_{0}+\underline{w}_{0})-S(t)w_{0}
. Below, in Proposition
2

Proposition 2
.

Let
w_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
and
0<r\leq 1
. Then there exists a constant
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}
(independent of
r
) such that for every
\bar{w}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{\gamma}^{+}=\{(f,g,h)\in\mathcal{V}^{+}\,|\,f_{|\partial\Omega}=\gamma_{1},g_{|\partial\Omega}=\gamma_{2}\}

0<\|\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}\leq r

(103)

\|S(t)\bar{w}_{0}-S(t)w_{0}-S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})(\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0})\|_{\mathcal{V}}\leq e^{\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(1+t)}\|\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}.

Here, the constant
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}
depends on parameters, boundary conditions and on the diameter of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{V}
(since
\mathcal{A}\subset B_{R}^{2}
, we may view
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}
as a function, say, of
R
). In particular, we have for each
w_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
and
t>0

(104)

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{w}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{\gamma}^{+}\\
0<\|\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}\leq r\end{subarray}}\frac{\|S(t)\bar{w}_{0}-S(t)w_{0}-S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})(\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0})\|_{\mathcal{V}}}{\|\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}}=0.

Proof.

We fix
w_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\bar{w}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{\gamma}^{+}
as in the hypothesis. We denote

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{0}=

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{0}-w_{0}

\displaystyle S(t)w_{0}=

\displaystyle w(t)=(c_{1}(t),c_{2}(t),u(t))

\displaystyle S(t)\bar{w}_{0}=

\displaystyle\bar{w}(t)=(\bar{c}_{1}(t),\bar{c}_{2}(t),\bar{u}(t))

\displaystyle S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})\tilde{w}_{0}=

\displaystyle\underline{w}(t)=(\underline{c}_{1},\underline{c}_{2},\underline{u})

\displaystyle\xi_{i}=

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{i}-c_{i}-\underline{c}_{i}

\displaystyle\xi_{\rho}=

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}-\rho-\underline{\rho}

\displaystyle\xi_{\Phi}=

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}-\Phi-\underline{\Phi}

\displaystyle\xi_{u}=

\displaystyle\bar{u}-u-\underline{u}

\displaystyle\tilde{w}=

\displaystyle(\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c}_{2},\tilde{u})=(\bar{c}_{1}-c_{1},\bar{c}_{2}-c_{2},\bar{u}-u)

where
\underline{\rho}=\underline{c}_{1}-\underline{c}_{2}
and
\underline{\Phi}=(-\epsilon\Delta_{D})^{-1}\underline{\rho}
. For each
t>0
, the function
S(t)\bar{w}_{0}-S(t)w_{0}-S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})\tilde{w}_{0}
has zero trace, so by Poincaré’s inequality, we have

(105)

\displaystyle N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{u}:=

\displaystyle\|\nabla\xi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\xi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\xi_{u}\|_{V}^{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\|S(t)\bar{w}_{0}-S(t)w_{0}-S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})\tilde{w}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}.

So, it suffices to control the growth of
N_{1},N_{2},N_{u}
. We achieve this by computing the time derivatives of
N_{1},N_{2},N_{u}
. We start with
N_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

We observe that
\xi_{i}
satisfies the equation

(106)

\partial_{t}\xi_{i}-D_{i}\Delta\xi_{i}=D_{i}z_{i}\nabla\cdot(\xi_{i}\nabla\Phi+c_{i}\nabla\xi_{\Phi})-\xi_{u}\cdot\nabla c_{i}-u\cdot\nabla\xi_{i}+D_{i}z_{i}\nabla\cdot(\tilde{c}_{i}\nabla\tilde{\Phi})-\tilde{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{c}_{i}.

Multiplying the above equation by
-\Delta\xi_{i}
, integrating by parts, and using Hölder and Young’s inequalities together with elliptic estimates, we obtain

(107)

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{i}}{dt}+D_{i}\|\Delta\xi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\nabla\xi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\xi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\nabla\xi_{\Phi}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|c_{i}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|\xi_{\rho}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\xi_{u}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\nabla\xi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\Phi}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\tilde{c}_{i}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|\tilde{\rho}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}+\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\Xi_{i}(t)(N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{u})+\left(\sum_{j}\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|A\tilde{u}\|_{H}^{2}\right)\sum_{j}\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

where

(108)

\Xi_{i}(t)=\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|c_{i}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}+\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}.

Similarly,
\xi_{u}
satisfies the equation

(109)

\partial_{t}\xi_{u}+\nu A\xi_{u}=-K\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\rho}\nabla\Phi+\rho\nabla\xi_{\Phi})-K\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\rho}\nabla\tilde{\Phi})

so taking the inner product with
A\xi_{u}
, integrating by parts, and using Hölder and Young’s inequalities together with elliptic estimates, we obtain

(110)

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{u}}{dt}+\nu\|A\xi_{u}\|_{H}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\xi_{\rho}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\rho\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\nabla\xi_{\Phi}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\tilde{\rho}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\nabla\tilde{\Phi}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\Xi_{u}(t)(N_{1}+N_{2})+\left(\sum_{j}\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{2}

where

(111)

\Xi_{u}(t)=\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\rho\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus, adding (
107
) and (
110
) and applying Grönwall’s inequality, we obtain

(112)

\displaystyle N_{1}(t)+N_{2}(t)+N_{u}(t)\leq

\displaystyle C\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sum_{i}\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{i}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{i}\|\nabla\tilde{c}_{i}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|A\tilde{u}(s)\|_{H}^{2}\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i}\Xi_{i}(s)+\Xi_{u}(s)\,ds\right)

where we have used the fact that
N_{1}(0)=N_{2}(0)=N_{u}(t)=0
. Then using (
68
), we find that

(113)

N_{1}(t)+N_{2}(t)+N_{u}(t)\leq\|\tilde{w}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{4}\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i}(\Xi_{i}(s)+G_{i}(s))+\Xi_{u}(s)+G_{u}(s)\,ds\right).

Now, we recall the definitions of
G_{i},G_{u},\Xi_{i},\Xi_{u}
(c.f. (
63
),(
66
),(
108
),(
111
)). By Lemma
2
and using Sobolev embeddings, we have

(114)

N_{1}(t)+N_{2}(t)+N_{u}(t)\leq\|\tilde{w}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{4}e^{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{A}}(1+t)}

where
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{A}}
depends on the norms
\|w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}},\|\bar{w}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}
. However, these norms are bounded by
R
and
R+1
, respectively, by hypothesis. Thus we have shown (
103
) and the proof is complete.
∎

Motivated by the preceding proposition, we analyze the linear operator

in the proposition below.

Proposition 3

Let

(ii)

(iii)

\mathcal{V}_{0}
into
\mathcal{V}_{0}\cap\mathcal{H}^{2}

\mathcal{V}_{0}

Proof.

The proof of the proposition follows the same steps as in the proof of Proposition
1
, and so we just highlight the main steps. We first prove (
ii
). Multiplying (
99
) by
-\Delta\underline{c}_{i}
and integrating by parts, we obtain

(116)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\nabla\underline{c}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+D_{i}\|\Delta\underline{c}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle A_{i}(t)\left(\sum_{j}\|\nabla\underline{c}_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\underline{u}\|_{V}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{i}\|\nabla\underline{c}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\underline{u}\|_{V}^{2}\right)+\sum_{i}D_{i}\|\Delta\underline{c}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu\|A\underline{u}\|_{H}^{2}

Equipped with the

In addition to the preceding propositions, a crucial ingredient in proving the finite dimensionality of the global attractor is showing that sufficiently high (but finite) dimensional volume elements of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
decay exponentially in time when transported by the linear flow
S^{\prime}(t;w_{0})
, for any
w_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
. We make this statement more precise. We denote by
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}=\bigwedge^{N}\mathcal{V}_{0}
the
N
-th exterior product of
\mathcal{V}_{0}
with inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}

(122)

(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{N},w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{N})_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}=\det\{(v_{i},w_{i})_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\}_{ij}

where
\{(v_{i},w_{i})_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\}_{ij}
is the
N\times N

(v_{i},w_{i})_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}
.

Now, fix initial conditions
w_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
and a set of small initial perturbations
\tilde{w}_{1,0},...,\tilde{w}_{N,0}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
. Then, consider the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by
\tilde{w}_{1,0},...,\tilde{w}_{N,0}

V_{N}(0)=\|\tilde{w}_{1,0}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N,0}\|_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}.

We track the time evolution of the above volume element as the perturbations
\tilde{w}_{i,0}
evolve under the flow of
S^{\prime}(t,w_{0})
, according to the linear PDE

(124)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{w}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(-\mathbb{A}+\mathcal{L}(t))\tilde{w}_{i}(t),\quad t>0

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}(t)_{|\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,\quad t>0

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=\tilde{w}_{i,0}

where above, we denote (c.f. (
99

))

(125)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}=

\displaystyle(-D_{1}\Delta,-D_{2}\Delta,\nu A)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t;w_{0})=(\mathcal{L}_{1}(t;w_{0}),\mathcal{L}_{2}(t;w_{0}),\mathcal{L}_{u}(t;w_{0})).

We note that
\mathbb{A}^{-1}
(with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions) is a compact, positive, self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{V}_{0}
. It follows from (

V_{N}^{2}(t)=\|\tilde{w}_{1}(t)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}

(126)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}V_{N}^{2}(t)=((-\mathbb{A}_{N}+\mathcal{L}_{N})(\tilde{w}_{1}(t)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(t)),\tilde{w}_{1}(t)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(t))_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}

where
\mathcal{L}_{N}:\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}\to\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}
is the extension of
\mathcal{L}
, defined component-wise (below, we omit the dependencies on time)

(127)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}(\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N})=

\displaystyle((\mathcal{L}_{1}\tilde{c}_{1,1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\tilde{c}_{2,1},\mathcal{L}_{u}\tilde{u}_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge(\tilde{c}_{1,N},\tilde{c}_{2,N},\tilde{u}_{N}))+

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle+((\tilde{c}_{1,1},\tilde{c}_{2,1},\tilde{u}_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge(\mathcal{L}_{1}\tilde{c}_{1,N},\mathcal{L}_{2}\tilde{c}_{2,N},\mathcal{L}_{u}\tilde{u}_{N}))

and similarly for
\mathbb{A}_{N}
.

We now prove the following, which establishes exponential in time decay of volume elements:

Proposition 4
.

For initial conditions
w_{0}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}
, there exists
N^{*}
depending on parameters and boundary conditions such that for every
N\geq N^{*}
and every set of
N
initial perturbations
\tilde{w}_{i,0}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
,
i=1,...,N
, the corresponding volume element
V_{N}(t)
decays exponentially:

(128)

V_{N}(t)\leq V_{N}(0)e^{-Nt},\quad t\geq\bar{t}

where
\bar{t}
depends on parameters, boundary conditions, and
\|w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

We invoke the following formula, proved in
[
6
]
: for any linear operator
T:\mathcal{D}(T)\subset\mathcal{V}_{0}\to\mathcal{V}_{0}
, we have

(129)

(T_{N}(\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}),\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\tilde{w}_{N})_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}=\|\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}\|_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}^{2}\text{Tr}(TP(\tilde{w}_{1},...,\tilde{w}_{N}))

where
P(\tilde{w}_{1},...,\tilde{w}_{N})
is defined to be the orthogonal projection from
\mathcal{V}_{0}
onto the subspace spanned by
\tilde{w}_{1},...,\tilde{w}_{N}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
. It follows from the above formula and (

V_{N}^{2}(t)=\|\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}\|_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}^{2}

(130)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}V_{N}^{2}(t)=V_{N}^{2}(t)\text{Tr}((-\mathbb{A}+\mathcal{L}(t))P(\tilde{w}_{1}(t),...,\tilde{w}_{N}(t)))

so that

(131)

V_{N}^{2}(t)=V_{N}^{2}(0)\exp\left(2\int_{0}^{t}\text{Tr}((-\mathbb{A}+\mathcal{L}(s))P(\tilde{w}_{1}(s),...,\tilde{w}_{N}(s)))\,ds\right).

Therefore,
V_{N}(t)
is either identically zero for all
t\geq 0
or strictly positive for all
t\geq 0
. Thus, without loss of generality, we assume that
V_{N}(0)>0
so that
V_{N}(t)>0
(and
\tilde{w}_{1}(t)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(t)\neq 0)

To obtain the decay, it suffices to show that for all sufficiently large

, depending on parameters and boundary conditions, we have

(132)

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{Tr}(-\mathbb{A}P(\tilde{w}_{1}(s),...,\tilde{w}_{N}(s)))\,ds+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\text{Tr}(\mathcal{L}(s)P(\tilde{w}_{1}(s),...,\tilde{w}_{N}(s)))\,ds=:a_{N}(t)+l_{N}(t)\leq-N

t

\tilde{w}_{1,0},...,\tilde{w}_{N,0}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
.

To estimate

, we again use the formula (

-a_{N}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(\mathbb{A}_{N}(\tilde{w}_{1}(s)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(s)),\tilde{w}_{1}(s)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(s))_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}}{\|\tilde{w}_{1}(s)\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}(s)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}^{2}}\,ds\geq\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{N}

where we have denoted the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}
by

and used the fact that the smallest eigenvalue of

, then, is
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}

Next, to estimate

, for each

we fix an orthonormal family
{\mathring{w}}_{j}(t)=({\mathring{c}}_{1,j}(t),{\mathring{c}}_{2,j}(t),{\mathring{u}}_{j}(t))

(134)

l_{N}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathcal{L}(s){\mathring{w}}_{j}(s),{\mathring{w}}_{j}(s))_{\mathcal{V}_{0}}\,ds

we focus on estimating the integrand in the integral above. We estimate the inner product, component by component, omitting below the dependence on time,

(135)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathcal{L}_{i}{\mathring{c}}_{i,j},\Delta{\mathring{c}}_{i,j})_{L^{2}}\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\Delta{\mathring{c}}_{i,j}\|_{L^{2}}\times(\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla{\mathring{\Phi}}_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|c_{i}\|_{L^{6}}\|{\mathring{\rho}}_{j}\|_{L^{3}}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla{\mathring{c}}_{i,j}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|{\mathring{c}}_{i,j}\|_{L^{6}}\|\rho\|_{L^{3}}+\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla{\mathring{c}}_{i,j}\|_{L^{2}}+\|{\mathring{u}}_{j}\|_{L^{6}}\|\nabla c_{i}\|_{L^{3}})

\displaystyle\frac{D_{i}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\Delta{\mathring{c}}_{i,j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\|\nabla{\mathring{c}}_{k,j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|{\mathring{u}}_{j}\|_{V}^{2}\right)b_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D_{i}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\Delta{\mathring{c}}_{i,j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+CNb_{i}(t)

where
in the last line we used the fact that
{\mathring{c}}_{k,j}
and
{\mathring{u}}_{j}

(136)

We also point out that the final constant

Similarly, we estimate

(137)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathcal{L}_{u}{\mathring{u}}_{j},A{\mathring{u}}_{j})_{H}\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|A{\mathring{u}}_{j}\|_{H}\left(\|\rho\|_{L^{3}}\|\nabla{\mathring{\Phi}}_{j}\|_{L^{6}}+\|{\mathring{\rho}}_{j}\|_{L^{3}}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{6}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|A{\mathring{u}}_{j}\|_{H}^{2}+C\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\|\nabla{\mathring{c}}_{k,j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)b_{u}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|A{\mathring{u}}_{j}\|_{H}^{2}+CNb_{u}(t)

where

(138)

b_{u}(t)=\|\rho\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{6}}^{2}.

Thus we have that

(139)

\displaystyle l_{N}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{1}}{2}\|\Delta{\mathring{c}}_{1,j}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{D_{2}}{2}\|\Delta{\mathring{c}}_{2,j}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|A{\mathring{u}}_{j}(s)\|_{H}^{2}\right)\,ds

\displaystyle+CN\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}b_{1}(s)+b_{2}(s)+b_{u}(s)\,ds.

At this point, let us note that due the formula (
129

(140)

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbb{A}_{N}(\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}),\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N})_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}}{\|\tilde{w}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\tilde{w}_{N}\|_{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{N}}^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{N})+CN\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}b_{1}(s)+b_{2}(s)+b_{u}(s)\,ds.

4.
Space-time Averaged Electroneutrality

In this section, we investigate the long time behavior of the local charge density
\rho
in the singular limit of
\epsilon\to 0
. We establish electroneutrality
|\rho|\ll 1
in a space-time averaged sense, for large times and small
\epsilon
.

Theorem 4
.

For any global smooth solution of (
1
)-(
3
) with boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
), there exist a time
T^{\epsilon}
(depending on parameters and boundary and initial data) and a constant
B_{1}
(depending only on boundary data and parameters but not on initial data or
\epsilon
) such that for any
\tau\geq\epsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}
and any

, we have

(153)

\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{T}^{T+\tau}\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}^{2}(s,x)\,dx\right)\,ds\leq B_{1}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.

In particular, we have

(154)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\Omega}\rho_{\epsilon}^{2}(s,x)\,dx\right)\,ds\leq B_{1}\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.

Above, the subscript
\epsilon
is denoted to emphasize the dependence of solutions on
\epsilon
. The proof of the theorem makes essential use of the following
L^{\infty}
absorbing ball property proved in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose
(c_{1},c_{2},u)
is a global smooth solution of (
1
)-(
3
) with boundary conditions (
9
)-(
11
). Then, for all
\delta>0
, there exists
T_{\delta}
depending on
\delta
, initial and boundary data, and parameters such that for all

we have

(155)

\underline{\gamma}-\delta\leq\underline{M}(t)\leq\overline{M}(t)\leq\overline{\gamma}+\delta

where
\underline{\gamma}=\min_{i}\inf_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{i}
and
\overline{\gamma}=\max_{i}\sup_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{i}
, and

(156)

\displaystyle\overline{M}(t)=\max_{i}\sup_{x\in\Omega}c_{i}(t,x),\quad\underline{M}(t)=\min_{i}\inf_{x\in\Omega}c_{i}(t,x).

In particular, the theorem implies that for large times,
c_{i}
obeys pointwise lower and upper bounds independent of
\epsilon
and of initial data. One immediate consequence of this theorem, together with the Poisson equation (
2
) and elliptic regularity, is the fact that, for all large times,

(157)

\|\nabla\Phi(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}.

Here, and for the remainder of the section,

denotes a constant that does not depend on initial conditions
nor
on
\epsilon
. This constant may differ from line to line.

Below, we show that by exploiting the dissipative structure of the NPS system, it is possible to improve on the

Proposition 5
.

For any global smooth solution of (

)-(
3
) with boundary conditions (
9
)-(

depending on parameters and initial and boundary data such that for some

(158)

\|\nabla\Phi(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{B_{2}}{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

Proof.

We wish to analyze the long time behavior of the quantity

To do so, we consider the time evolution of the energy

(159)

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}^{*}\psi\left(\frac{c_{i}}{c_{i}^{*}}\right)\,dx+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\nabla(\Phi-\Phi^{*})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2K}\|u\|_{H}^{2}

(160)

\displaystyle{\mbox{div}\,}(\nabla c_{i}^{*}+z_{i}c_{i}^{*}\nabla\Phi^{*})=

\displaystyle 0,\quad i=1,2

(161)

\displaystyle-\epsilon\Delta\Phi^{*}=\rho^{*}=

\displaystyle c_{1}^{*}-c_{2}^{*}

(162)

\displaystyle{c_{i}^{*}}_{|\partial\Omega}=

\displaystyle\gamma_{i},\quad i=1,2

(163)

\displaystyle{\Phi^{*}}_{|\partial\Omega}=

\displaystyle W.

In
[
12
]
, the existence of steady state solutions to the Nernst-Planck-Navier-Stokes equations with boundary conditions (
9
)-(

) is shown. A streamlined version of the proof then establishes the existence of steady state solutions of the above uncoupled Nernst-Planck system (
160
)-(
163
). In general, it is not known if such steady states are unique, and for our purpose, we simply fix one steady state solution. All we make use of in the proceeding computations is the fact that
c_{i}^{*},\Phi^{*}
satisfy (
160
)-(

Lemma 3

Suppose

(1)

\inf_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{i}e^{z_{i}W}=\lambda_{i}\leq c_{i}^{*}e^{z_{i}\Phi^{*}}\leq\Lambda_{i}=\sup_{\partial\Omega}\gamma_{i}e^{z_{i}W}
on

(2)

The proof of this lemma may be found in

Proceeding with the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle\left(-u\cdot\nabla c_{1}+D_{1}{\mbox{div}\,}(c_{1}\nabla(\mu_{1}-\mu_{1}^{*})+(c_{1}-c_{1}^{*})\nabla\mu_{1}^{*}),\mu_{1}-\mu_{1}^{*}\right)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle-\left(u\cdot\nabla c_{1},\mu_{1}-\mu_{1}^{*}\right)_{L^{2}}-\frac{D_{1}}{2}\int_{\Omega}c_{1}|\nabla(\mu_{1}-\mu_{1}^{*})|^{2}\,dx+C\int_{\Omega}c_{1}^{*}|\nabla\mu_{1}^{*}|^{2}\,dx.

\underline{\gamma},\overline{\gamma}

(184)

\Delta\tilde{\Phi}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad\tilde{\Phi}=W\text{ on }\partial\Omega

we multiply (
161
) by
\Phi^{*}-\tilde{\Phi}
and integrate by parts to find,

(185)

\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Phi^{*}|^{2}\,dx=\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Phi^{*}\cdot\nabla\tilde{\Phi}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\rho^{*}(\Phi^{*}-\tilde{\Phi})\,dx.

From Lemma

and we conclude that

(187)

for some

independent of
\epsilon
. Combining this last estimate with (

).

We now prove the main result of this section, Theorem
154
:

Proof.

We define
\Gamma_{i}
, the harmonic extension of
\gamma_{i}
, satisfying

(189)

\Delta\Gamma_{i}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad\Gamma_{i}=\gamma_{i}\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

We restrict ourselves to
t\geq T^{\epsilon}
, where
T^{\epsilon}
is determined by Proposition
5
. We recall that in particular
t\geq T^{\epsilon}
implies
\frac{\underline{\gamma}}{2}\leq c_{i}(t)\leq\frac{3\overline{\gamma}}{2}
(c.f. paragraph below (
169
)) and
\|\nabla\Phi(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{B_{2}}{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}
(Proposition
5
).

We multiply (
1
) by
\frac{1}{D_{i}}\log\frac{c_{i}}{\Gamma_{i}}
and integrate by parts to obtain

(190)

\frac{1}{D_{i}}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma_{i}\psi\left(\frac{c_{i}}{\Gamma_{i}}\right)\,dx=I_{1}^{i}+I_{2}^{i}+I_{3}^{i}

where

(191)

\displaystyle I_{1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Delta c_{i}\log\frac{c_{i}}{\Gamma_{i}}\,dx

(192)

\displaystyle I_{2}^{i}

\displaystyle=-D_{i}^{-1}\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla c_{i}\log\frac{c_{i}}{\Gamma_{i}}\,dx

(193)

\displaystyle I_{3}^{i}

\displaystyle=z_{i}\int_{\Omega}{\mbox{div}\,}(c_{i}\nabla\Phi)\log\frac{c_{i}}{\Gamma_{i}}\,dx.

We estimate
I_{1}^{i}
by integrating by parts and using the bound
c_{i}\leq\frac{3\overline{\gamma}}{2}
and a Young’s inequality,

(194)

I_{1}^{i}=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla c_{i}|^{2}}{c_{i}}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla c_{i}}{c_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\cdot(\nabla\log\Gamma_{i})c_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dx\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla c_{i}|^{2}}{c_{i}}\,dx+C.

To estimate
I_{2}^{i}
, we first note that

(195)

\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla c_{i}\log c_{i}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla(c_{i}\log c_{i}-c_{i})\,dx=0

so that, using
c_{i}\leq\frac{3\overline{\gamma}}{2}
and Proposition

,

(196)

I_{2}^{i}\leq D_{i}^{-1}\left|\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla c_{i}\log\Gamma_{i}\,dx\right|=D_{i}^{-1}\left|\int_{\Omega}uc_{i}\cdot\nabla\log\Gamma_{i}\,dx\right|\leq C\|u\|_{H}\leq C.

Lastly we estimate
I_{3}^{i}
. Integrating by parts, we have

(197)

\displaystyle I_{3}^{i}

\displaystyle=-z_{i}\int_{\Omega}\nabla c_{i}\cdot\nabla\Phi\,dx+z_{i}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla\log\Gamma_{i}\,dx

\displaystyle=J_{1}^{i}+J_{2}^{i}.

First, using
c_{i}\leq\frac{3\overline{\gamma}}{2}
, we have, by Proposition
5

(198)

J_{2}^{i}\leq C\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

To estimate
J_{1}^{i}
, we construct another extension of the boundary data
\gamma_{i}
to
\Omega
in the following way. We fix a family of smooth, nonnegative cutoff functions
\chi^{\epsilon}:\bar{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
so that
0\leq\chi^{\epsilon}\leq 1,

{\chi^{\epsilon}}_{|\partial\Omega}=1
and
\chi^{\epsilon}(x)=0
for all
x\in\Omega
such that
d(x,\partial\Omega)=\inf_{y\in\partial\Omega}|x-y|\geq\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.
We further require that
|\nabla\chi^{\epsilon}|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}}
, where the constant
C
depends on the domain but not on
\epsilon.
Then, the function
\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}
defined by
\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}=\chi^{\epsilon}\Gamma_{i}
is an extension of
\gamma_{i}
that vanishes away from the boundary
\partial\Omega
at distances larger than
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.
Furthermore, we have
|\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}|\leq\overline{\gamma}
and
|\nabla\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}|\leq|\Gamma_{i}\nabla\chi^{\epsilon}|+|\chi^{\epsilon}\nabla\Gamma_{i}|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}}.
It follows from the latter bound and the fact that
\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}
is supported on a set of measure of order

that

(199)

\|\nabla\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\epsilon^{-\frac{2}{3}})^{\frac{1}{2}}=C\epsilon^{-\frac{1}{6}}.

Now we estimate

(200)

\displaystyle J_{1}^{i}

\displaystyle=-z_{i}\int_{\Omega}\nabla c_{i}\cdot\nabla\Phi\,dx

\displaystyle=-z_{i}\int_{\Omega}\nabla(c_{i}-\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon})\cdot\nabla\Phi\,dx-z_{i}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\Phi\,dx

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{z_{i}}{\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}\rho\,dx+\frac{z_{i}}{\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}\rho\,dx+\|\nabla\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{z_{i}}{\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}\rho\,dx+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{2}\,dx+\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}}

where in the fourth line, we used Proposition
5
and (
199

\Gamma_{i}^{\epsilon}
is supported on a set of measure of order
\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.

and sum in

\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{D_{i}}\int_{\Omega}\Gamma_{i}\psi\left(\frac{c_{i}}{\Gamma_{i}}\right)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla c_{i}|^{2}}{c_{i}}\,dx+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}}+C\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}}.

Then, for any

Since for times larger than or equal to

, we obtain from (

(203)

\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{T}^{T+\tau}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{2}\,dx\,ds\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{\tau}+C\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq C\epsilon^{\frac{1}{3}},\quad\tau\geq\epsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}.

Thus we have shown (
153
). To show (
