Channel linear Weingarten surfaces in space forms
Udo Hertrich-Jeromin, Mason Pember, Denis Polly

Abstract
. Channel linear Weingarten surfaces in space forms are investigated in a Lie sphere geometric setting, which allows for a uniform treatment of different ambient geometries. We show that any channel linear Weingarten surface in a space form is isothermic and, in particular, a surface of revolution in its ambient space form. We obtain explicit parametrisations for channel surfaces of constant Gauss curvature in space forms, and thereby for a large class of linear Weingarten surfaces up to parallel transformation.

1.
Introduction

Two different ways to define linear Weingarten surfaces can be found
in the literature, either by requiring an affine relationship between the
principal curvatures or one between the mean and the Gauss curvature. In
these notes, we adopt the second definition:
a surface in a space form is called linear Weingarten if, for some
non-trivial triple
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, its Gauss and mean curvatures

K
and
H
satisfy

\displaystyle aK+2bH+c=0.

This includes surfaces of constant Gauss or mean curvature (CGC or CMC,
respectively) as well as their parallel surfaces.

We investigate linear Weingarten surfaces that are additionally channel
surfaces, that is, envelop a
1
-parameter family of spheres.
An example is provided by surfaces that are invariant under a
1
-parameter
subgroup of rotations in the given space form.
The study of such rotational linear Weingarten surfaces goes back
to Delaunay’s investigations of CMC surfaces of revolution in Euclidean
space in
[
12
]
,
but have again sparked interest in recent years from various points
of view, see

[
3
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
25
]
or most recently

[
2
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
27
]
and references
therein. Since the considered surfaces arise via the action of isometries
on a planar profile curve, all curvature notions only depend on the
profile curve. This fact has been used to prove various classification results
for these curves and, subsequently, rotational linear Weingarten surfaces.
However, explicit parametrisation formulas for these surfaces seem only
to be available in special cases.

Channel linear Weingarten surfaces in Euclidean space are either tubular
or surfaces of revolution, and are parallel to either the catenoid
or a CGC surface, as was shown in
[
20
]

by giving explicit parametrisations in terms of Jacobi’s elliptic
functions (
[
1
, Chap. 16]
).
In this way, the authors obtained a complete and transparent
classification of channel linear Weingarten surfaces in Euclidean space.

In this present text, we aim to complement the existing results by
explicit parametrisations for rotational CGC surfaces, in particular,
in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and the
3
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and,
in consequence, for any linear Weingarten surface that is parallel
to such a CGC surface
—
the clear advantage being that many results about such surfaces can
then be derived or verified by mere computation.
Particular attention is paid to a choice of parametrisations that
are well-behaved across singularities of the surfaces,
that necessarily occur in various cases according to Hilbert’s theorem.
Our parametrisations provide a complete classification result for
non-tubular channel linear Weingarten surfaces in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
,
where every such surface is parallel to a CGC surface
(cf
[
3
]
),
and encompass the large class of channel linear Weingarten surfaces
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
that have a rotational CGC surface in their parallel
family (cf
[
16
]
,
[
24
]
or
[
27
]
).

Considering the ambient space form geometries as subgeometries
of Lie sphere geometry will allow for a unified treatment
of the various cases that occur:
note that channel surfaces naturally belong to the realm of sphere geometries,
see
[
28
]
, for example.
Further, in
[
6
]
, the authors have shown that
linear Weingarten surfaces appear as special
\Omega
-surfaces in Lie
sphere geometry.
Section

2
will serve the reader as a brief
introduction into the projective model of this geometry and explain how
space form geometries may be viewed as subgeometries.
In this way, we may conveniently investigate surfaces in different space forms
in a unified manner.
A Bonnet type theorem (
2.8
) will demonstrate the key role
of CGC surfaces within the class of linear Weingarten
surfaces and will provide for a simple generalisation of our parametrisations
to rotational linear Weingarten surfaces.

As another instance of the unifying sphere geometric treatment,
rotational surfaces will be considered in
Section

3
, where
we express the Gauss curvature of a rotational
surface in terms of one parameter. This expression will be used to
classify rotational CGC surfaces.

In
Section

4
we provide a Lie geometric version of Vessiot’s theorem

[
19
, Theorem 3.7.5]
:
as sphere geometries provide a natural ambient setting for channel
surfaces, Möbius geometry provides for a natural ambient geometry
for isothermic surfaces, that is, surfaces that admit conformal
curvature line parameters.
Vessiot’s theorem states that any channel
isothermic surface is, upon a suitable stereographic projection
into Euclidean space, a surface of revolution or has straight
curvature lines.
Similarly, we shall discover that
\Omega
-surfaces,
a Lie sphere geometric generalisation of isothermic surfaces,
that are additionally channel surfaces are isothermic upon a suitable choice of a Möbius (sub-)geometry
of Lie sphere geometry (see
4.3
).

Motivated by
2.8
and
4.7
, we will investigate
rotational CGC surfaces in
Section

5
: elliptic differential
equations are obtained and used to provide constructions for families of
such surfaces. The principal aim of this section is to provide general
strategies to solve the occurring differential equations and to discuss
reality of the produced solutions.
Our case
analysis, depending on the relation between the constant Gauss
curvature and the curvature of the ambient space form, is yet another
instance of a splitting that is frequently observed when constructing
immersions between space forms
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
,

[
25
]
.

Specific parametrisations, and the classification results they imply,
are then stated in
Section

6

Weingarten surfaces in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
are parametrised by the functions given
in Table

The corresponding classification in

richer, partly due to the appearance of various types of

2.1.
Lie sphere geometry and its subgeometries

Consider
\mathbb{R}^{4,2}
, a
6
-dimensional real vector space with inner
product
(.,.)
of signature
{(-++++-)}
. We call a vector
\mathfrak{v}

timelike
,
spacelike
or
lightlike
depending on
whether
(\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{v})
is negative, positive or vanishes.

We call the
projective light cone

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:=\mathbb{P}L^{5}=\mathbb{P}\{\mathfrak{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{4,2}\,|\,(\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{v})=0\}=\{\langle\mathfrak{v}\rangle\subset\mathbb{R}^{4,2}|\mathfrak{v}\neq 0\penalty\ \textrm{is lightlike}\},

the
Lie quadric
, where
\langle\mathfrak{v}\rangle
denotes the linear span of

\mathfrak{v}
. Points in the Lie quadric represent oriented
2
-spheres in

3
-dimensional space forms (here, points are spheres with vanishing
radius).

Lie sphere transformations are given by the action of orthogonal
transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{4,2}
on the Lie quadric. For
A\in O(4,2)

we have
A\cdot\langle\mathfrak{v}\rangle=\langle A\mathfrak{v}\rangle
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{4,2}
be a unit timelike vector, that is
(\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p})=-1
.
The projective sub-quadric

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}}:=\{x\in\mathcal{L}|x\perp\mathfrak{p}\},

is a model space of
3
-dimensional Möbius geometry and

Möbius transformations
are induced by orthogonal
transformations that fix
\mathfrak{p}
. We call
\mathfrak{p}
a
point sphere
complex
, and elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}}

point spheres
.
More generally, every
\mathfrak{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{4,2}\setminus\{0\}
spans a

linear sphere complex
, consisting of all spheres which are
represented by null lines perpendicular to it. A sphere
\langle\mathfrak{s}\rangle\in\mathcal{L}

contains a point
\langle\mathfrak{x}\rangle\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}}
if and only if

(\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{s})=0
.

If we orthogonally project a sphere
s=\langle\mathfrak{s}\rangle\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{\mathfrak{p}}
onto
\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle^{\perp}

we obtain a vector
\sigma\in\langle\mathfrak{p}\rangle^{\perp}\cong\mathbb{R}^{4,1}
with

\displaystyle(\sigma,\sigma)=(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{p})^{2}>0,

i.e., the representative of a sphere in the projective model of
Möbius geometry as described in
[
19
]
.
We call
\sigma
a
Möbius representative of
s
.

Let
\mathfrak{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{4,2}\setminus\{0\}
be perpendicular to
\mathfrak{p}
and denote

\kappa:=-(\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q})
. Define the affine sub-quadric

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}:=\{\mathfrak{x}\in L^{5}|(\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{p})=0,\penalty\ (\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{q})=-1\}.

Then,
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
has constant curvature
\kappa
, hence each connected component of
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}

yields a model for a space form geometry. The isometry group of the space form is then
denoted as
Iso_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}(3)
and consists of all orthogonal
transformations that fix
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
. We call
\mathfrak{q}
the

space form vector
.
Spheres represented by null lines orthogonal to
\mathfrak{q}
represent
planes
;
hence the set of planes in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
is identified with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}:=\{\mathfrak{x}\in L^{5}|(\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{p})=-1,\penalty\ (\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{q})=0\}.

2.2.
Surfaces

Let
\mathfrak{f}:\Sigma^{2}\to\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
parametrise a surface in a space
form. Its tangent plane congruence, denoted by
\mathfrak{t}:\Sigma^{2}\to\mathfrak{T}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
, is
of the form
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{p}+\mathfrak{n}
, where
\mathfrak{n}
denotes the
usual Gauss map of
\mathfrak{f}
when
(\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q})\neq 0
.

The line congruence
\Lambda=\langle\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{t}\rangle
is called the
Legendre
lift of
\mathfrak{f}
.
\Lambda
is a
Legendre immersion
, that is, for
any two sections
\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{s}_{2}\in\Gamma\Lambda
we have

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{s}_{2})=0\penalty\ \textrm{and}\penalty\ (d\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{s}_{2})=0,

and for all
p\in\Sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\mathfrak{s}\in\Gamma\Lambda\colon d_{p}\mathfrak{s}(X)\in\Lambda(p)\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ X=0.

We say that
\Lambda

envelops
a sphere congruence
\langle\mathfrak{s}\rangle:\Sigma^{2}\to\mathcal{L}

if
\mathfrak{s}(p)\in\Lambda(p)
for all
p\in\Sigma^{2}
.

Lie sphere transformations naturally act on Legendre immersions and
Legendre lifts of surfaces in space forms: for

A\in O(4,2)
,

A\cdot\Lambda:=A\cdot\langle\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{t}\rangle=\langle A\mathfrak{f},A\mathfrak{t}\rangle,

which induces a map on surfaces by mapping
\mathfrak{f}
to the point sphere
congruence enveloped by
A\cdot\Lambda
. The following lemma, a
proof of which can be found in
[
10
, Sect 2.5]
, shows
that this map on surfaces is well-defined.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given a point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
and a Legendre immersion

\Lambda
, there is precisely one point sphere congruence
\mathfrak{f}

enveloped by
\Lambda
.

Apart from the point sphere and tangent plane congruences, Legendre
lifts also envelop their
curvature sphere congruences

s_{1},s_{2}
:
away from umbilic points, let
(u,v)
denote curvature line coordinates. Then, the curvature sphere congruences are characterised by

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{s}_{1})_{u},(\mathfrak{s}_{2})_{v}\in\Gamma\Lambda,

for any lifts
\mathfrak{s}_{i}
of
s_{i}
. Further, we have
\Lambda=\langle\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{s}_{2}\rangle
.

A sphere congruence
s:\Sigma^{2}\to\mathcal{L}
is
isothermic

if it allows for a
Moutard lift
\mathfrak{s}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}_{uv}||\mathfrak{s},

for parameters
(u,v)
, which are then curvature line parameters.
Equivalently, any lift
\mathfrak{s}
of
s
satisfies a
Laplace equation

0=\mathfrak{s}_{uv}+a\mathfrak{s}_{u}+b\mathfrak{s}_{v}+c\mathfrak{s}
with equal
Laplace invariants
,

a_{u}+ab-c=b_{v}+ab-c
(
[
11
, Chap II]
,
[
15
]
).

Definition 2.2
.

A surface
\mathfrak{f}
in a space form is called an
\Omega
-surface
,
if its Legendre lift
\Lambda
envelops a (possibly complex conjugate)
pair of isothermic sphere congruences
s^{\pm}
that separate the
curvature spheres harmonically.
We will also call
\Lambda
itself an
\Omega
-surface.

Remark 2.3
.

For real isothermic sphere congruences, this is the definition of
Demoulin in
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
.
However, we will also consider
\Omega
-surfaces enveloping complex isothermic
sphere congruences (also characterised by the existence of Moutard lifts).
If the two isothermic sphere congruences coincide
(with one of the curvature sphere congruences),
the surface
\mathfrak{f}
is called an
\Omega_{0}
-surface
.

In our considerations, we will use a characterisation of

\Omega
-surfaces in terms of special lifts of their curvature
sphere congruences: while one can always choose lifts

\mathfrak{s}_{1}\in\Gamma s_{1}
and
\mathfrak{s}_{2}\in\Gamma s_{2}
of the curvature
sphere congruences of a Legendre lift such that

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{s}_{1})_{u}\in\Gamma s_{2},\penalty\ \textrm{and}\penalty\ (\mathfrak{s}_{2})_{v}\in\Gamma s_{1},

for
\Omega
-surfaces, even more can be achieved (
[
7
]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

A surface
\mathfrak{f}:\Sigma^{2}\to\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
with curvature sphere
congruences
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
is an
\Omega
-surface, if and only if
there exists a function
\phi
and lifts
\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{s}_{2}
of
s_{1},s_{2}

such that

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathfrak{s}_{1})_{u}&=\phi_{u}\mathfrak{s}_{2},\\
(\mathfrak{s}_{2})_{v}&=\varepsilon^{2}\phi_{v}\mathfrak{s}_{1},\end{split}

where
\varepsilon\in\{1,i\}
.

Remark 2.5
.

For
\Omega_{0}
-surfaces, (
1
) holds with

\varepsilon=0
.

Proof.

A proof for this can be found in
[
29
, Sect 4.3]
. We
summarise the argument given there briefly:

Let
\Lambda
be an
\Omega
-surface with Moutard lifts
\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}
of
the enveloped isothermic sphere congruences. Since they separate the
curvature sphere congruences
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
harmonically, there are lifts

\mathfrak{s}_{1}\in\Gamma s_{1}
and
\mathfrak{s}_{2}\in\Gamma s_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}=\mathfrak{s}_{1}\pm\varepsilon\mathfrak{s}_{2},

and functions
\alpha,\overline{\alpha},\beta,\overline{\beta}

such that

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{s}_{1})_{u}=\overline{\alpha}\mathfrak{s}_{1}+\alpha\mathfrak{s}_{2}

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{s}_{2})_{v}=\beta\mathfrak{s}_{1}+\overline{\beta}\mathfrak{s}_{2}.

The Moutard condition
\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}_{uv}||\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}\in\Lambda
yields

\overline{\alpha}=\overline{\beta}=0
as well as

\alpha_{v}=\varepsilon^{2}\beta_{u}
, which is the integrability
condition of

\displaystyle\phi_{u}

\displaystyle=\alpha

\displaystyle\phi_{v}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}\beta.

Hence (
1
) is satisfied with any
solution of that system.

Conversely, given lifts satisfying (
1
),
the two sphere congruences given by

{s^{\pm}=\langle\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}\rangle}
with
{\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}:=\mathfrak{s}_{1}\pm\varepsilon\mathfrak{s}_{2}}

obviously separate the curvature spheres harmonically. They are also
isothermic, as

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{s}^{\pm})_{uv}=\varepsilon^{2}\left(\phi_{u}\phi_{v}\pm\varepsilon\phi_{uv}\right)\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}

demonstrates that
\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}
are Moutard lifts.

2.3.
Curvature

The principal curvatures of
\mathfrak{f}
can be expressed by

(2)

\displaystyle k_{i}=\frac{(\mathfrak{s}_{i},\mathfrak{q})}{(\mathfrak{s}_{i},\mathfrak{p})},

which is lift-invariant. Denote by
K=k_{1}k_{2}
and

H=\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}
the (extrinsic) Gauss and mean curvatures of

\mathfrak{f}
. We call
\mathfrak{f}
a
linear Weingarten surface
if there is a
non-trivial triple of constants
a,b,c
such that the linear
Weingarten condition

\displaystyle aK+2bH+c=0,

holds. A linear Weingarten surface is called
tubular
if

ac-b^{2}=0
, that is, if one of the principal curvatures
is constant.
In what follows we will generally assume that
\mathfrak{f}
is non-tubular.

We restate a version of the linear Weingarten condition given in

[
7
]
. Since the principal curvatures of a linear
Weingarten surface
\mathfrak{f}
can be written in terms of arbitrary lifts

\mathfrak{s}_{i}
of the curvature sphere congruences as in
(
2
), we may write the linear Weingarten
condition as

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{q}),(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\mathfrak{p})\end{pmatrix}W\begin{pmatrix}(\mathfrak{s}_{2},\mathfrak{q})\cr(\mathfrak{s}_{2},\mathfrak{p})\end{pmatrix}=0,\penalty\ \textrm{with}\penalty\ W=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\cr b&c\end{pmatrix}.

Then,
W
induces a non-degenerate bilinear form on
\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\rangle
,
and the linear Weingarten condition can be seen as an orthogonality condition
for two vectors with respect to that form. A change of basis in
\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\rangle
,
given by a
\operatorname{GL}(2)
matrix
B
as
(\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{p})\mapsto(\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{p})B^{-1}
,
changes the linear Weingarten condition by

(4)

\displaystyle W\mapsto BWB^{t}.

In
[
7
]
this is used to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.6
.

Non-tubular linear Weingarten surfaces in space forms are those

\Omega
-surfaces
\Lambda=\langle\mathfrak{s}^{+},\mathfrak{s}^{-}\rangle
that envelop a (possibly
complex conjugate) pair of isothermic sphere congruences

s^{\pm}=\langle\mathfrak{s}^{\pm}\rangle
, each of which takes values in a linear sphere
complex
\mathfrak{p}^{\pm}
. The plane
\langle\mathfrak{p}^{+},\mathfrak{p}^{-}\rangle
is the plane spanned
by the point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
and the space form vector
\mathfrak{q}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The linear sphere complexes
\mathfrak{p}^{\pm}
are constant conserved quantities
for the isothermic sphere congruences of the enveloped pair in the sense
of
[
9
, Def 2.1]
, for details see

[
5
]
.

This characterisation is useful to investigate parallel families of
linear Weingarten surfaces: Given a space form
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
, a

parallel transformation

P
is a Lie sphere transformation
that acts solely on
\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\rangle
. It is well known that parallel
transformations preserve linear Weingarten surfaces
(see, for instance,
[
26
, Sect 2.7]
),
a fact that follows by straightforward computations in a space form,
or from
2.6
:
P\cdot\Lambda=\langle P\mathfrak{s}^{+},P\mathfrak{s}^{-}\rangle

is still spanned by isothermic sphere congruences taking values in
fixed linear sphere complexes
P\mathfrak{p}^{\pm}
. The fact that
P\mathfrak{p}^{\pm}
span

\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\rangle
makes
P\Lambda
a linear Weingarten surface in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}

again.

Let
\mathfrak{f}
be a linear Weingarten surface in
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
satisfying
(
3
). If we interpret the action
of
P
in
\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\rangle
as a change of basis in that plane, we learn
that the parallel surface
\tilde{\mathfrak{f}}\in\Gamma P\Lambda
satisfies the linear
Weingarten equation

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{1},\mathfrak{q}),(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{1},\mathfrak{p})\end{pmatrix}\tilde{W}\begin{pmatrix}(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{2},\mathfrak{q})\\
(\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}_{2},\mathfrak{p})\end{pmatrix}=0,

with a matrix

\tilde{W}=PWP^{t}

of the form given in (
4
).
In
[
8
]
, the authors investigate parallel families
of discrete linear Weingarten surfaces in this manner. For our
purpose of classifying channel linear Weingarten surfaces, we formulate
the following proposition, the proof of which is analogous to that of the
discrete version, see
[
8
, Sect 4.6]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{f}:\Sigma\to\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
be a non-tubular linear Weingarten
surface satisfying (
3
) in a space
form of curvature
\kappa=-(\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q})\in\{-1,0,+1\}
.

(1)

\kappa=1
: the parallel family of
\mathfrak{f}
contains two
antipodal pairs of CGC surfaces.

(2)

\kappa=0
: if
c\neq 0
,
\mathfrak{f}
is parallel to a CGC surface with
K\neq 0
.

(3)

\kappa=-1
: if
\left|\frac{a+c}{2}\right|>|b|
,
\mathfrak{f}

is parallel to precisely one CGC surface with

K\neq 0
.

Remark 2.9
.

The parallel CGC surfaces satisfy
K\neq 0
, because surfaces
with
K=0
are tubular and tubularity is preserved under parallel
transformation.
Similarly, parallel transformations in a space form
with
\kappa\neq 0
preserve flatness, that is, vanishing of the intrinsic
Gauss curvature
K+\kappa
.
For
\kappa=-1
, flat surfaces satisfy
\tfrac{a+c}{2}=b=0
,
hence
\mathfrak{f}
is parallel to a non-flat surface if

\left|\tfrac{a+c}{2}\right|>|b|
.

Remark 2.10
.

In the remaining cases,

\kappa=0
and
c=0
or

\kappa=-1
and
\left|\frac{a+c}{2}\right|\leq|b|\neq 0
,
that are not stated in the proposition,
the parallel family does not contain a CGC surface.

In the
Euclidean case, parallel families like this consist of a
minimal surface and its parallel surfaces, all of which have
constant harmonic mean curvature, that is, constant ratio of
Gauss and mean curvature. It was mentioned in

[
20
]
that the only minimal channel surface in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the catenoid.

For
\kappa=-1
, each parallel family
not containing a CGC surface contains either one CMC surface or
one surface of constant harmonic mean curvature. Classification of these
surfaces will be the subject of a future publication.

3.
Rotational surfaces

The goal of this section is to obtain formulas for the Gauss curvature
of rotational surfaces. We aim to achieve this in a symmetric way, so
that our formulas are as independent as possible of the type of space form and
rotation.

The isometry group of a space form
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}
is the subgroup

\operatorname{Iso}_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}}(3)\subset O(4,2)
of orthogonal
transformations that fix the point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
and the space
form vector
\mathfrak{q}
. For a
2
-plane
\Pi\perp\langle\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}\rangle
, we call a

1
-parameter subgroup of isometries
\rho_{1}
a
subgroup of
rotations (in
\Pi
)
, if it acts as the identity on
\Pi^{\perp}
.
Denote by
\operatorname{sgn}{\Pi}
the signature of the induced metric on
\Pi
.
We call a subgroup of rotations

•

elliptic
if
\operatorname{sgn}{\Pi}=(++)
,

•

parabolic
if
\operatorname{sgn}{\Pi}=(+0)
, or

•

hyperbolic
if
\operatorname{sgn}{\Pi}=(+-)
.

Note that these are all possible signatures because
\Pi
is
perpendicular to the timelike point sphere complex
\mathfrak{p}
. The causal
character of the space form vector
\mathfrak{q}
further restricts the
possible signatures: for instance, there are no parabolic subgroups
of rotations in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
(
\mathfrak{q}
timelike) but they act as translations
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

A.1.
Elliptic functions

The
Jacobi elliptic functions of pole type
n
may be given for
a
modulus

p\in[0,1]
by their characterising elliptic
differential equations

\displaystyle y^{\prime 2}(s)=(1-y^{2}(s))(1-p^{2}y^{2}(s))\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ y(s)=\operatorname{sn}_{p}(s),

\displaystyle y^{\prime 2}(s)=(1-y^{2}(s))(q^{2}+p^{2}y^{2}(s))\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ y(s)=\operatorname{cn}_{p}(s),

\displaystyle y^{\prime 2}(s)=(1-y^{2}(s))(-q^{2}+y^{2}(s))\penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ y(s)=\operatorname{dn}_{p}(s),

where
q=\sqrt{1-p^{2}}
is called the
complementary modulus
.
The
Jacobi amplitude function

\operatorname{am}_{p}
may be defined via

\operatorname{am}_{p}(s)=\operatorname{arcsin}\operatorname{sn}_{p}(s)=\operatorname{arccos}\operatorname{cn}_{p}(s)
, then the characterising
differential equations imply
\operatorname{am}_{p}^{\prime}=\operatorname{dn}_{p}
. Further,
we obtain the
Pythagorean laws

\displaystyle\operatorname{sn}_{p}^{2}+\operatorname{cn}_{p}^{2}=\operatorname{dn}_{p}^{2}+p^{2}\operatorname{sn}_{p}^{2}=1.

A wider class of Jacobi elliptic functions may be defined via algebraic
combinations of the three functions of pole type
n
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{ef}_{p}(s):=\tfrac{\operatorname{en}_{p}(s)}{\operatorname{fn}_{p}(s)}\penalty\ \textrm{and}\penalty\ \operatorname{ne}_{p}(s)=\tfrac{1}{\operatorname{en}_{p}(s)},\penalty\ \textrm{for}\penalty\ e,f\in\{c,d,s\}.

The Jacobi elliptic functions take complex arguments: purely imaginary
arguments are evaluated using
Jacobi’s imaginary transformations

\displaystyle\operatorname{sn}_{p}(is)=i\operatorname{sc}_{q}(s),\quad\operatorname{cn}_{p}(is)=\operatorname{nc}_{q}(s),\quad\operatorname{dn}_{p}(is)=\operatorname{dc}_{q}(s).

Note that
\operatorname{cn}
and
\operatorname{dn}
are real functions of imaginary
arguments, whereas
\operatorname{sn}
becomes imaginary.

The restriction
p\in[0,1]
on the modulus
p
can be lifted
by means of
Jacobi’s real transformations
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{sn}_{\tfrac{1}{p}}(s)=p\operatorname{sn}_{p}\left(\tfrac{s}{p}\right),\quad\operatorname{cn}_{\tfrac{1}{p}}(s)=\operatorname{dn}_{p}\left(\tfrac{s}{p}\right),\quad\operatorname{dn}_{\tfrac{1}{p}}(s)=\operatorname{cn}_{p}\left(\tfrac{s}{p}\right),

allow evaluation for
p\geq 1
, and by definition, all Jacobi elliptic
functions are even with respect to their modulus.
Also, with respect to an imaginary modulus
ip\in i\mathbb{R}
,
the Jacobi elliptic functions take real values for a real
argument: the corresponding transformations read

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\operatorname{sn}_{ip}(s)=q^{\prime}\operatorname{sd}_{p^{\prime}}\left(s\sqrt{1+p^{2}}\right),\\
\operatorname{cn}_{ip}(s)=\operatorname{cd}_{p^{\prime}}\left(s\sqrt{1+p^{2}}\right)\vskip 5.0pt,\\
\operatorname{dn}_{ip}(s)=\operatorname{nd}_{p^{\prime}}\left(s\sqrt{1+p^{2}}\right).\end{array}\penalty\ \textrm{with}\penalty\ p^{\prime}=\tfrac{p}{\sqrt{1+p^{2}}},
