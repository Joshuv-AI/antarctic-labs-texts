Abstract
We present a novel approach to causal measurement for advertising, namely to use exogenous variation in advertising exposure (RCTs) for a subset of ad campaigns to build a model that can predict the causal effect of ad campaigns that were run without RCTs. This approach—Predictive Incrementality by Experimentation (PIE)—frames the task of estimating the causal effect of an ad campaign as a prediction problem, with the unit of observation being an RCT itself. In contrast, traditional causal inference approaches with observational data seek to adjust covariate imbalance at the user level. A key insight is to use post-campaign features, such as last-click conversion counts, that do not require an RCT, as features in our predictive model. We find that our PIE model recovers RCT-derived incremental conversions per dollar (ICPD) much better than the program evaluation approaches analyzed in lift2 . The prediction errors from the best PIE model are 48%, 42%, and 62% of the RCT-based average ICPD for upper-, mid-, and lower funnel conversion outcomes, respectively. In contrast, across the same data, the average prediction error of stratified propensity score matching exceeds 491%, and that of double/debiased machine learning exceeds 2,904%. Using a decision-making framework inspired by industry, we show that PIE leads to different decisions compared to RCTs for only 6% of upper funnel, 7% of mid-funnel, and 13% of lower funnel outcomes. We conclude that PIE could enable advertising platforms to scale causal ad measurement by extrapolating from a limited number of RCTs to a large set of non-experimental ad campaigns. Keywords: Digital Advertising, Advertising Effects, Advertising Measurement, Field Experiments, Causal Inference, Attribution Metrics, Proxy Metrics.

Introduction

Marketers face pressure from their organizations to justify their digital advertising spend by showing that their advertising campaigns yield positive returns. The key ingredient for assessing a campaign’s return is the causal effect, or “incrementality,” of the ad campaign on a desired conversion outcome, for example, sales. This incrementality is the difference between the outcome under the ad campaign (the factual outcome) and the outcome had the ad campaign not been run (the counterfactual outcome). The core of the advertising measurement problem is that the counterfactual outcome is not directly observable by the advertiser.

Methods to assess the performance of digital ad campaigns rely on different data and assumptions to approximate this counterfactual outcome, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. A common approach is the use of ‘‘proxy metrics’’ (such as last-click conversion counts) because they are easy to calculate and understand. However, proxy metrics can easily over or understate the incremental effect of ads.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—the gold standard for causal inference—are a popular way to ensure exogenous variation in ad exposure. However, RCTs carry significant opportunity costs and are technically challenging to implement in auction systems, limiting their widespread adoption

Since the need to measure advertising effects remains, even if there are significant limitations of all approaches, what should advertisers do? In this paper, we propose a new methodology to measure the causal effect of ad campaigns that are not implemented as RCTs. The idea is to pool exogenous variation in advertising exposure across campaigns to build scalable models for ad measurement. Our goal is to produce estimates of causal advertising effects that come closer to those that would be obtained through RCTs but at lower opportunity costs to the advertiser and advertising platform. Specifically, we propose implementing RCTs for a
subset
of campaigns and then modeling the relationship between the RCT-derived causal effect of advertising and a set of easily observed proxy metrics that are available regardless of whether a campaign was implemented as an RCT. This approach frames the task of estimating the causal effect of an ad campaign as a prediction problem, with the unit of observation being an RCT itself. This contrasts with traditional causal inference approaches which attempt to handle covariate imbalance through user-level observables with non-experimental data
(
ImbensRubin2015
)
.

We name this approach ‘‘Predictive Incrementality by Experimentation’’ (or PIE for short).

PIE combines the best elements of prior approaches: PIE uses ground-truth measurements (from RCTs) to estimate scalable models (from machine learning), which enable measurement even when campaigns are not run as RCTs (as when relying on proxy metrics). The goal of PIE is to model the relationship between the RCT results and two classes of features. The first class are pre-determined features
known ahead
of running the campaign at the advertiser level (e.g., industry vertical) or advertiser-campaign level (e.g., target segment, budget). The second class are post-determined features that are measured
after
running the campaign. These features include proxy metrics, such as the last-click (LC) conversions we use in our primary analysis, which consist of the number of users who convert within a time window after clicking on the last ad they saw before converting. These features can be extended to include other metrics that span outcomes across the conversion funnel. For example, LC conversions and product page landings can both be used to predict incremental purchases. Our approach’s basic idea is that, so long as these post-determined features correlate with the ad campaign’s RCT-derived incremental outcome, we can use a predictive model to estimate said causal effect.

We focus on predicting an expenditure-normalized version of the causal effect of advertising, namely incremental conversions per dollar (ICPD). This normalization is common in the advertising industry and allows firms to compare the performance of campaigns with different budgets. We measure out-of-sample prediction error as the weighted root mean square error (WRMSE). This yields an intuitive interpretation of the prediction error: A percent WRMSE of 0.5 or 50% means that the predicted ICPD is, on average, 50% of the average ICPD.

We explore PIE models with different levels of complexity. At the simple end, we predict ICPD using univariate regressions on the expenditure-normalized version of the LC proxy metrics, last-click conversions per dollar (LCPD). This simple PIE model yields a “calibration factor” to convert LC metrics to RCT-derived incremental conversion counts. For example, if an advertiser observes 0.1 LCPD in a 7-day window, but the RCT shows 0.05 ICPD, the advertiser can “calibrate” the LC metric by multiplying it by a scalar of 0.5 to obtain an estimate of
incremental
conversions. We can interpret our simple PIE estimates as a calibration factor because we estimate our univariate regression without an intercept. This allows us to speak to a frequently asked question in the industry, namely whether such a calibration factor can be applied to proxy metrics, such as LC metrics.

This is because our results so far report whether PIE can produce estimates of advertising’s causal effects that come close to the estimates obtained using RCTs. However, in most cases, the end objective is not to report a causal estimate but to reach a judgment or make a decision based on this estimate. A manager could make the same decision regardless of whether they used an RCT for a specific campaign or created a prediction using PIE. If so, PIE’s remaining inaccuracy would not be that relevant.

To create a decision-making framework, we adopt a simple perspective drawn from industry practice, in which marketers consider a campaign successful if it achieves an ICPD that exceeds some threshold. Otherwise, it is viewed as a failure, and the marketer would have preferred to use the campaign budget for another business activity with a greater return. The marketing manager can choose this threshold based on the expected profit they would observe for every incremental conversion. From a decision-making standpoint, the critical question is whether the marketer would reach the same conclusion had they used an RCT or PIE. We quantify this assessment as the disagreement probability between the RCT and PIE, which is related to the probability of false positives (type I) and false negatives (type II). We find that the best PIE model leads to disagreements of only 6% for upper funnel, 7% for mid-funnel, and 13% for lower funnel outcomes.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we are the first to propose the approach of predicting incrementality by experimentation (PIE). The core idea is to inject randomness to obtain “ground truth” estimates that allow us to estimate predictive models using simple-to-obtain proxy metrics to predict causal advertising effects. This enables us to predict the incremental effects of campaigns that were
not
run as RCTs. We show that this approach is much more accurate than what we could achieve with traditional causal inference approaches using non-experimental data. Nonetheless, because PIE requires RCTs only for model building, it is potentially very scalable in practice. Second, we propose a novel way to evaluate non-experimental approaches’ performance by focusing on the mistakes managers would make if they used these approaches. Third, we speak to whether a simple calibration factor could be applied to last-click attribution to infer the correct causal effect of an ad campaign. We conclude that---at least for the display ads shown on Facebook---calculating such a calibration factor may be possible with a sufficient number of RCTs. Although the notion of calibrating proxy metrics based on the occasional RCT is common in the ad industry, we are unaware of any prior academic work that evaluates the efficacy of this practice.

A common element in all the work above is that the advertising effect estimation problem is framed as a causal inference problem

Our key contribution is that, rather than casting the problem as one of causal inference at the user level, we frame the problem as one of prediction at the campaign (experiment) level. The primary benefit of this new framing is that an advertising platform can predict the incrementality of a campaign that was not implemented as an RCT, using not only data that was known before the campaign but also data that was
collected during the campaign

Our approach is also related to, but distinct from, recent work on surrogate models
(

;

)

W\rightarrow Y
. However, measuring this causal effect with an experiment is infeasible for various reasons. Instead, the researcher can use an experiment to measure the effect of treatment on a surrogate outcome,
W\rightarrow S
. Next, the researcher constructs a predictive model from historical data of the relationship
S\rightarrow Y
. Combining these two pieces allows the firm to evaluate the effects of the treatment on the outcome through the surrogate.

In our setting, the surrogates take the form of our proxy metrics, such as last-click attributed conversions. We can
always

sometimes

W\rightarrow Y
using an RCT. Our approach uses a predictive model between

based on the RCTs we observe to generate out-of-sample predictions of
W\rightarrow Y
.

Our methodology rests on the ability of researchers to identify adequate proxy metrics that are easy to record for any campaign and that are predictive of the target RCT outcome. Finding proxy variables that correlate closely with a preferred outcome of interest is not new

By using flexible models from machine learning, we can incorporate many (potentially) weak proxy metrics that collectively achieve sufficient predictive accuracy.

Assessing digital ad performance

Digital display advertising is commonplace for companies and makes up a significant percentage of advertisers’ spending
(
emarketer2023
)
. Marketers want to measure the efficacy of their ad campaigns. Fundamentally, the efficacy of an ad campaign is the causal effect or “incrementality” of the ad campaign on a desired conversion outcome, for example, a product purchase. As discussed in the introduction, the core of the advertising measurement problem is that the counterfactual outcome is not directly observable by the advertiser. Methods to assess the performance of digital ad campaigns rely on different data and assumptions to approximate this unobserved counterfactual outcome. We review some common approaches below with the goal of helping to frame the benefits and limitations of our PIE approach.

Proxy Metrics

A common proxy metric is last-click conversion counts. In this approach, an advertiser counts a conversion outcome as being associated with an ad campaign if certain conditions are satisfied. First, last-click defines an ‘‘attribution window,’’ such as 7 days or 14 days. If a consumer clicks on only one ad during that attribution window, the conversion outcome is attributed to that ad campaign. If a consumer clicks on multiple ads during the attribution window, the conversion outcomes is attributed to the last ad that was clicked. If no ad is clicked, then no attributed conversion occurs---the conversion was not a consequence of any marketing activity. The end-result of last-click is that some number conversions are attributed

8

8

A critical distinction is around the use of the term “attributed” in digital advertising. In the context of a single ad platform measuring outcomes, “attribution” generally refers only to what is observed on the ad platform and ignores ad spending on other platforms. This can lead to confusion given that the term “attribution” in the ad industry can also refer to attributing whether a conversion outcome was from one platform versus another. As many advertisers run ads across many ad platforms, this question of cross-channel attribution is important. However, for the scope of this paper, when we use the term “attribution,” we are restricting ourselves to the scope of a single ad platform. In this sense, some platforms also refer to this window as a “conversion window” or “lookback window.”
to an ad campaign and others are not. While these metrics are not inherently measuring causal effects, advertisers often mesh these interpretations together which is why we refer to them as proxy metrics—they are commonly used as a proxy for incremental conversions.

In practice, to interpret last-click metrics as causal/incremental requires the assumption that the user would not have converted in the absence of the ad. This assumption might be valid for some users, but opposing forces introduce error into last-click metrics relative to the true number of incremental conversions. First, last-click is likely to underestimate the true number of incremental conversions by ignoring two cases: (a) users who clicked but converted outside the specific window and (b) users who saw the ad and converted but who never clicked (often called “view-through” conversions). Second, last-click will overestimate the correct number of incremental conversions by attributing incrementality to some users who clicked on the ad and converted within the window, even though these users would have converted in the absence of the ad. Quantifying the relative magnitudes of these sources of bias is difficult, making it impossible to consistently sign the bias.

Last-click metrics are both useful and practical because their data requirements are low. They do not rely on any other user-level data beyond the timing of exposures and conversions, plus the conversion outcomes themselves, among exposed users. Advertisers may be able to calculate last-click metrics based on their weblogs and transaction database. Also, last-click metrics are easy to explain: there is no need to discuss concepts such as “counterfactuals” to stakeholders and last-click metrics do not require complex tooling, unlike experiments. As the advertising ecosystem continues to evolve and advertisers lose measurement signals
(
wernerfelt_2022
)
, methods for measuring effectiveness that rely strictly on weblogs and other first-party advertiser data may be increasingly useful.

Observational Models

Because the shortcomings of last-click are widely known, advertisers, advertising platforms (e.g., Google, Meta), measurement providers (e.g., Comscore), and academic researchers have tried to come up with measurement methods that come closer to measuring the causal effect of ads. Specifically, motivated by the rapid advance of causal inference models over the last 20 years, industry practitioners and academics (including us) have made numerous attempts to model the counterfactual using observational advertising data.

lift1
;
lift2
investigated whether the non-experimental campaign data logged at Facebook are sufficient to “undo” the selection effect induced by the ranking and delivery systems when ads are served to consumers. Building on the more limited data in
lift1
,
lift2
used an extensive set of campaign- and user-level data for over 600 ad experiments to assess the ability of double/debiased machine learning (DML) and stratified propensity score matching (SPSM) to estimate the causal advertising effects.

Regrettably, the available data combined with these causal inference methods failed to sufficiently control for the selection effects induced by the advertising platform’s ranking and delivery system, rendering these observational approaches inadequate to estimate the causal effect of advertising. This conclusion comes despite the fact that the models and data are better than what advertisers are typically able to use, and are close to the best of what third-party measurement partners and large advertising platforms use.

Recently, others have introduced more promising observational approaches, which rely on very granular, impression-level data. Using machine learning models, these models produce impression-level incrementality estimates, albeit with very demanding data requirements
(
du_et_al_2019
;
tunuguntla_2021
)
.

Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)

The limitations of last-click and causal inference approaches based on observational data are one reason why some advertising platforms allow advertisers to run RCTs for ad measurement: RCTs enable advertisers to get unbiased estimates of the impact of their ad campaigns. To run an RCT on the advertiser’s behalf, most advertising experimentation platforms randomly assign users from the advertiser’s selected target audience to a test or control group according to some ratio. Users in the test group are eligible to be exposed to an ad from the advertiser and users in the control group will not be exposed. Since users are randomly assigned to these two groups, they are probabilistically equivalent except for the focal advertiser’s ad exposure. This equivalence allows us to easily compare both groups to get estimates of a campaign’s performance in terms of the number of “incremental conversions,” i.e., conversions that only occurred due to the ad campaign.

One might conclude that the solution to advertising measurement is to run well-powered RCTs for every campaign. However, there are several reasons an advertiser and an advertising platform may not wish to use RCTs. By randomly assigning users to a no-ad control group, an advertiser is intentionally withholding potentially effective ads from being shown to their audience. RCTs also require pre-planning before they are run and cannot be implemented after the fact. Also, RCTs impose opportunity costs for advertising platforms since common approaches such as “Ghost Ads”
ghostads
require punting the highest-ranked ad for the control group and replacing it with the second-highest ad. This decreases the price an advertising platform can charge for ad impressions in the control group.

Our Approach: Predicted Incrementality by Experimentation

We introduce a method that combines RCTs, proxy metrics, and predictive modeling to help advertisers estimate the causal impact of their campaigns—even when the campaign is not implemented as an RCT. Specifically, our idea is to use a
sample
of RCTs to build an econometric model that predicts the relevant RCT outcome as a function of proxy metrics (e.g., last-click conversions) and other campaign-level features. Our approach tries to balance the pros and cons of RCTs with the wide availability of proxy metrics to provide scalable and actionable estimates of causal effects.

Given that last-click conversions are likely biased, why might these metrics still useful from a predictive standpoint? The underlying intuition for our approach is that incremental conversions (IC) and last-click (LC) conversions are correlated due to the following relationship:

IC

\displaystyle=\text{LC}_{w}+\text{LC}_{-w}+\text{Conversions}_{\text{no click}}-\text{Conversions}_{\text{organic}}\ .

(1)

The expression above captures the fact that the number of incremental conversions is the sum of (a) conversions associated with a LC event within window
w
, (b) conversions associated with a LC event that fall outside window
w
, (c) conversions associated with an ad impression but no click (view-through conversions) and (d) subtracting the set of conversions that would have occurred organically, without any ad impressions. We only observe IC and
\text{LC}_{w}
, with the remaining terms being unobserved.

Our model seeks to predict
IC
, or rather functions of it, using
\text{LC}_{w}
and other features. Thus, the strength of the relationship between
IC
and
\text{LC}_{w}
will ultimately determine the predictive accuracy of the model. This correlation depends on the relative magnitude of each component above in determining
IC
. For example, higher values of
w
will strictly increase
\text{LC}_{w}
, decrease
\text{LC}_{-w}
, and have no effect on
\text{Conversions}_{\text{no click}}
. However, a longer window implies that more of the conversions counted through
\text{LC}_{w}
are likely to be non-incremental, i.e., they would have happened even without the ad impression and click. This implies that
\text{Conversions}_{\text{organic}}
rises to offset the increase in
\text{LC}_{w}
.

Since we only observe
\text{LC}_{w}
on the right-hand side of equation (
1
), the remaining terms effectively become the error term. To the extent that the relative influence of these unobserved components is stable, pooling the data across RCTs will help us control for the influence of these unobserved components through the inclusion of
\text{LC}_{w}
metrics with varying windows and various advertiser-campaign level features.

Generally, firms do not share their ad campaigns’ performance with other firms, especially with competitors. We envision two ways in which this exercise could be feasible. First, an advertising platform with RCT capabilities could make such a model available to advertisers as a tool to assist their marketing decisions. Second, a third-party firm, such as a market research company or a data co-op, could help aggregate campaign performance metrics and share the learnings with advertisers.

To understand if these experiments were delivered in line with how they were set up, we conducted a randomization check to verify that each experiment observed a test/control split that matched what was selected during the experimental design. We calculate the percentage of users in the test and control groups for each experiment, then compare this with the planned split using an exact binomial test. The distribution of p-values from this test should be approximately distributed
\textrm{Uniform}(0,1)
if we consistently fail to reject the null hypothesis that the realized test/control split differs from the planned split. Overall, we observe that 6% of p-values are below 0.05, 25% are below 0.25, and 76% are below 0.75, indicating that the experiments were randomized correctly into their expected test/control splits.

3.2
Incremental Outcomes and RCT Features

To derive incremental conversion outcomes for a particular RCT, let
Z_{i}=\{0,1\}
indicate user
i
’s random assignment to the control or test groups and let ad exposure be given by
W_{i}(Z_{i})=\{0,1\}
. Users assigned to the control condition are never exposed to any ads from the campaign,
W_{i}(Z_{i}=0)=0
. However, assignment to the test group only ensures eligibility, not exposure, such that
W_{i}(Z_{i}=1)=\{0,1\}
(i.e., there is one-sided non-compliance). Potential outcomes for user
i
are
Y_{i}(Z_{i},W_{i}(Z_{i}))
. Define the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) as:

\displaystyle\tau\equiv\text{ATT}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Y(1,W(1))-Y(0,W(0))|W(1)=1\right]\ .

(2)

Conveniently, we can estimate
\tau
and its variance,
\sigma^{2}
, using two-staged least squares with
Z
as an instrument for endogenous exposure
W
. This relies on several well-known assumptions, such as Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA), monotonicity, and an exclusion restriction
(
ImbensAngrist94
)
, that are likely satisfied in our experimental setting.

For any given RCT, we can calculate the number of incremental conversions (IC) by multiplying the ATT and the number of exposed users:

IC

\displaystyle=\tau\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}W_{i}(1)\ ,

(3)

where
N_{t}
is the number of users assigned to the test group. Although the number of incremental conversions is a critical outcome from any RCT, interpreting this value directly can be difficult because it varies based on the number of users reached and the amount of money spent on the campaign. For these reasons, advertisers often prefer to normalize the number of incremental conversions by the cost of the campaign, which yields the incremental conversions per dollar (ICPD),

ICPD

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{IC}}{\text{Cost}}\ .

(4)

In the next section, we use ICPD as the primary outcome variable for our PIE model.

Figure
2
presents the distribution of ICPD across purchase funnel outcomes based on the RCTs in our sample. To preserve confidentiality, we normalize all ICPD by the median ICPD value in the lower funnel. Therefore, the normalized median ICPD for the lower funnel is 1. As expected, one dollar of ad spend yields fewer lower funnel incremental outcomes than mid and upper funnel incremental outcomes. We find that the normalized median mid-funnel ICPD is 2.6 and the normalized median upper funnel ICPD is 11.69 times higher than the lower funnel median ICPD. Although we cannot report the unnormalized ICPD values, we find they are broadly comparable to figures reported elsewhere.

Finally, we observe a set of advertiser- and campaign-level features for each experiment. These include (1) features that describe the targeting settings for the campaign (i.e., what types of users to include), (2) features that pertain to the advertiser’s bidding strategy, (3) features that describe the optimization settings of the campaign, and (4) certain characteristics about the advertisers and their experience on the ad platform. We also observe the advertiser’s vertical and the actual amount of money spent on the experiment. Along with an RCT’s funnel level, we refer to these features collectively as “campaign characteristics”.

4
Predictive Incrementality by Experimentation (PIE)

We frame our task of calculating an ad campaign’s causal effect as a prediction problem. In this approach, the unit of observation is an RCT. We observe a set of
R=
2,246 RCTs, indexed by
r
. Our approach pools data across all advertisers and experiments to model the relationship between
\text{ICPD}_{r}
and two classes of features:

\displaystyle\text{ICPD}_{r}

\displaystyle=f\left(X_{r}^{\text{pre}},X_{r}^{\text{post}};\theta\right)+\varepsilon_{r}\ ,

(6)

where
\theta
is a parameter vector to be estimated and
\varepsilon_{r}
is an econometric error term. Our results in the next section consider two models for
f(\cdot)
, linear regression and random forests, but researchers could employ any predictive model.

The first class of features,
X_{r}^{\text{pre}}
, are measured at the advertiser-level (e.g., vertical) or advertiser-campaign level (e.g., target audience, bidding strategy) and are known
before
running the campaign. These pre-determined features include time-invariant characteristics of the advertiser or the product being advertised, whereas others represent specific choices pertaining to the campaign. Given their nature, these features should be readily accessible by the ad platform and advertiser. In our most complex model formulation, we take
X_{r}^{\text{pre}}
to be the full set of campaign characteristics discussed in the previous section.

The second class of features,
X_{r}^{\text{post}}
, are post-determined variables that are measured
after
the campaign concludes. These variables could be proxy metrics, such as last-click conversions, that advertisers use to assess the performance of their campaigns. These metrics are aggregated up from user-level data and often reported by default on most advertising platforms. The variables could also be aggregates that help describe other features of consumer behavior in the campaign. For example, if the researcher is studying lower-funnel purchase outcomes, they could use mid- and upper-funnel outcomes such as (normalized) add-to-carts and page views, since these are likely correlated with incremental purchase outcomes through the consumer conversion funnel
(
JohnsonLewisNubbemeyer2017
)
.

Critically, neither
X_{r}^{\text{pre}}
nor
X_{r}^{\text{post}}
require the campaign to have been implemented as an RCT. This is important because our goal is to use the estimated model to generate out-of-sample predictions of ICPD, or any other causal metric the researcher desires, from campaigns that were not implemented as RCTs. Thus, any feature that depends on the existence of a randomized control group would not exist in a non-experimental campaign.

We rely on three key assumptions to use the model to predict out-of-sample campaign performance. First, the underlying campaigns must have been implemented as proper RCTs that provide an unbiased estimate of the causal effect. Second, we assume that any covariate shift between the distribution of
\left(X_{r}^{\text{pre}},X_{r}^{\text{post}}\right)
in our data and the corresponding features in the out-of-sample ad campaigns is sufficiently captured in the model
f(\cdot)
. Third, we assume there is no concept shift, i.e., the relationship we estimate in equation (
6
) is stable.

We evaluate each model using an out-of-sample estimate of its predictive performance. This entails generating a predicted outcome,
\widehat{\text{ICPD}}_{r}
, for each RCT in the data. With these predictions in hand, we calculate the weighted root mean squared error (WRMSE) for the model:

WRMSE

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\sum_{r}\omega_{r}\cdot\left(\text{ICPD}_{r}-\widehat{\text{ICPD}}_{r}\right)^{2}\right)}

where
\omega_{r}
is the weight associated with RCT
r
, which sum to one.

We follow a “leave-one-RCT-out” strategy to generate these predictions. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set of all RCTs across all advertisers. For each RCT
r\in\mathcal{R}
, we follow these steps:

1.

Set the training data to be
\mathcal{R}_{{\scalebox{0.65}[0.80]{$-$}r}}
, the set of RCTs that
excludes

r
.

2.

Using
\mathcal{R}_{{\scalebox{0.65}[0.80]{$-$}r}}
, select hyper-parameters of the model (if they exist) using ten-fold cross-validation, evaluating a potential hyper-parameter value using the
WRMSE
from the validation fold.

3.

Given the hyper-parameters above, train the predictive model using all observations in
\mathcal{R}_{{\scalebox{0.65}[0.80]{$-$}r}}
.

4.

Apply this model to the holdout RCT
r
to generate a predicted
\widehat{\text{ICPD}}_{r}
.

After repeating these steps for all RCTs, we can calculate the
WRMSE
across all the RCTs in the data. We can also generate the
WRMSE
for subsets of the data, e.g., for all lower funnel RCTs, by renormalizing the weights to sum to one within that subset.

Implicit in the approach above are three choices that deserve comment. First, we chose ICPD as the outcome in the model above, which itself represents a transformation of
\tau
, IC, and the experiment’s cost. We think using ICPD is preferred to using
\tau
or IC directly because the transformation helps normalize the causal effect across RCTs and advertisers. ICPD is a relevant metric for many advertisers because it helps them compare campaign performance under different budgets. Normalizing the LC metrics by the campaign cost, to generate the LCPD, places the proxy metrics on the same relative scale as the outcome variable. Data normalizations such as this are thought to improve the performance and numerical stability of various machine learning models.
16
16

16

Another metric closely related to ICPD is its inverse, the cost per incremental conversion (CPIC). The CPIC is often used when discussing advertising prices because it helps the advertiser interpret incremental outcomes through the lens of profitability. The advertiser likely has (private) information on the profit they obtain from a conversion, and so the CPIC can help them determine whether the campaign produced a positive return on ad spend (ROAS). The downside of using CPIC as an outcome is that it could be negative if IC happens to be negative.

The second issue concerns our choice of WRMSE as a model fit metric. An advertising platform (or advertiser) might reasonably prefer alternative metrics, such as the (weighted) mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). One benefit of the MAPE is that it operates in terms of percentages, which can be appealing to managers. Advertisers themselves might be more concerned with generating predictions that are close on a relative basis, if not in absolute terms.
17
17

17

Suppose campaign A has a true treatment effect of 1 and a PIE estimate of 2, whereas campaign B has a true treatment effect of 10 and a PIE estimate of 20. In terms of relative accuracy, each observation would contribute equally to MAPE. However, if RMSE is used, campaign B would receive more weight.
A downside of MAPE is that errors are not symmetric: under-predictions are given less weight than over-predictions, which implies the predictive model will be biased towards lower predictions
(
green_tashman_2009
)
. Thus, we view choosing the right evaluation metric as a design choice by the advertising platform: does it wish to be close in absolute terms or in relative terms? The platform could train different models according to different evaluation metrics.

The third issue is that our outcome variables are themselves noisy estimates of their (unknown) true values. This has two important implications. First, the variance in RCT outcomes implies that any prediction model based on noisy estimates of the truth will never be able to fit perfectly---even if we could estimate the correct prediction model using the true values. This means there is some lower bound that can be attained by model fits metrics such as WRMSE.
18
18

18

Thank you to Randall Lewis for making this point to us.

Second, we use weights,
\omega_{r}
, when calculating the RMSE to account for the uncertainty in our outcomes. These weights are used both in model training, as part of the K-fold cross-validation process, and in generating the summary out-of-sample model fit metrics. A typical approach is to set the weights equal to the inverse of the sample variance for each RCT’s outcome,
\omega_{r}=1/\hat{\sigma}_{r}^{2}
, and then normalizing the weights to sum to one. These weights are inversely proportional to the size of an RCT’s user population, such that they help correct for within-RCT finite-sample measurement error. RCTs outcomes that are more precisely estimated get greater weight in the predictive model, which is important considering that 30.5% of the 2,246 RCTs in our sample are statistically insignificant.

However, one complication of using the sample variance is that it, too, represents an estimate, ignoring all the variation that exists between RCTs in terms of advertiser identity, targeting rules, bidding strategies, and so on. One campaign might have a small variance estimate due to narrow targeting rules, which would result in the campaign getting higher weight even though the extent to which this matters for generalizability—and out-of-sample prediction—is unclear.

To address these concerns, we draw on the meta-analysis literature to generate weights that account for within- and between-experiment variance. We follow the common approach from
DL1986
that assumes outcomes follow a random effects model with a hierarchical normal-normal distribution, with a within-RCT variance of
\sigma_{r}^{2}
and a between-RCT variance of
\gamma^{2}
. This yields weights of
\omega_{r}=1/(\sigma_{r}^{2}+\gamma^{2})
. Although this technique makes parametric assumptions on the distribution of effects, no distributional assumptions are made regarding
\gamma^{2}
. We only rely on this method to generate modified RCT weights
\omega_{r}
for model evaluation purposes.
19
19

19

See
Veroniki_et_al_2016
for a review of methods to estimate uncertainty in meta-analysis.
Given the normalization to sum to one, the addition of
\gamma^{2}
acts akin to a shrinkage estimator, reducing the influence of extreme RCTs when training the predictive model.

Much like our discussion of the evaluation metric, an advertising platform might reasonably prefer different weights depending on which RCTs they feel are most important. For example, an advertising platform could weight RCTs by the cost of the campaign, such that the predictive model would give more weight to more expensive campaigns. Since more expensive campaigns tend to reach larger populations of users, we would expect similar results to our present approach. However, some campaigns are expensive because they target a narrow subset of users. The platform might prefer to handle such campaigns separately since sample-size related weights may not favor the predictive performance among these targeted campaigns. Thus, both the model fit metric and weights could be parameters tuned across different models by an ad platform that seeks to address different advertiser needs.

5
Results

We now answer the question whether the information contained in a set of (potentially biased) proxy metrics can produce reasonable RCT ICPD predictions. If so, this would permit an advertiser with access to proxy metrics to estimate the ICPD of a campaign, even if the advertiser did not implement the campaign as an RCT.

5.1
Simple PIE models (Calibration Factors)

We begin with a simple exploration of how LC conversions are correlated with
\text{ICPD}_{r}
(i.e., incremental conversions as determined by our RCT). To analyze, we regress
\text{ICPD}_{r}
for each funnel position (lower, mid, and upper funnel) on each of the four last-click metrics (
\text{LCPD}_{\text{1h}}
,
\text{LCPD}_{\text{1d}}
,
\text{LCPD}_{\text{7d}}
,
\text{LCPD}_{\text{28d}}

\displaystyle\text{ICPD}_{r}

\displaystyle=\theta\cdot\text{LCPD}_{r,w}+\varepsilon_{r}

(7)

Note that this regression purposefully omits an intercept so that we can interpret the coefficient
\theta
as a simple calibration factor in the sense commonly seen in the ad measurement industry. There is an intense interest in the industry as to whether such a calibration factor could be applied to last-click metrics to obtain the causal effect of an ad campaign. Part of this motivation stems from the fact that running RCTs is challenging for many advertisers. It would be helpful if an advertiser knew that it could occasionally run an RCT to establish a calibration factor between the causal effect and proxy metric, and then to use this calibration factor to adjust future non-causal campaign effects.

5.2
More complex PIE models

So far, we have only considered individual LC metrics to predict the causal effect of ads. Next, we follow the more general approach in equation (
6
), namely to model the relationship between the outcome of interest, the RCT ICPD, and the proxy metrics and other campaign features.

We consider two groupings of features. Our first specification considers the joint performance of all four LC metrics in
X_{r}^{\text{post}}
without any additional features,
X_{r}^{\text{pre}}=\emptyset
. The second specification adds in the full set of campaign characteristics as features in
X_{r}^{\text{pre}}
. This includes conversion funnel, vertical, characteristics of the target user segment, the degree to which the campaign targets new customers or existing customers, the number of users in the test group, the total budget, the length of the campaign (in days), the stated objective of the overall campaign (e.g., driving sales, brand awareness), and others. All the features included in
X_{r}^{\text{pre}}
are known to an advertiser, even if they do not run an experiment.

We present results using two PIE models, linear regression and random forests.

A few additional insights emerge from this analysis. The richest and most flexible model, “rf-m2,” does best across all funnel outcomes. However, for mid and lower funnel outcomes, the raw
\text{LCPD}_{\text{1h}}
predicts RCT incremental conversions nearly as well. This—perhaps surprising—finding suggests that
\text{LCPD}_{\text{1h}}
suffers from little to no selection bias as a proxy for RCT incremental conversions. However, this is too simple an interpretation for two reasons. First, as discussed in section
3.2
, opposing forces introduce error into the LC metrics relative to the RCT incremental conversions. LC metrics will underestimate the true number of RCT incremental conversions for users who clicked but converted outside the specific window and users who saw the ad and converted but who never clicked. LC metrics will overestimate the correct number of incremental conversions by attributing incrementality to some users who clicked on the ad and converted within the window, even though these users would have converted even in the absence of the ad. This latter effect is the selection bias we just mentioned. Therefore, the finding that the raw
\text{LCPD}_{\text{1h}}
predicts RCT incremental conversions well does not indicate no selection bias. Instead, it suggests that any overestimation induced by selection bias balances out any underestimation induced by not counting users who converted without clicking or outside the 1-hour attribution window.

The second reason one should be cautious in declaring
\text{LCPD}_{\text{1h}}
to be a universally valid proxy for incremental conversions is that display advertising on Facebook is likely to induce less selection bias than contextually targeted display advertising or search advertising. Facebook advertising is targeted based on how advertisers define their audiences (e.g., “women 18-49 who like cooking”).

6
Decision Making

So far, we have focused on whether the ad effects estimates obtained from non-experimental methods come close to those from RCTs. In practice, however, the estimates are more likely a means to an end, which is to inform a managerial decision. What if a manager would make the same campaign decision regardless of whether they used a non-experimental method or an RCT? If this is the case, the practical implications of non-experimental methods would be lessened despite their potential inaccuracy.

Our goal in this section is to investigate whether non-experimental methods would lead to different decisions than RCTs. We begin by formalizing a manager’s decision problem using incremental conversions per dollar, a standard metric used by advertisers. In the context of this decision problem, we then explain how an advertiser might reach different conclusions about a campaign’s success (or failure) depending on which measurement methodology the advertiser used. We then show our results by quantifying advertisers’ conclusions as a disagreement probability between decision-making using an RCT vs. a non-experimental method. We present results for all key approaches used in the prior two sections.

6.1
Incremental Conversion Per Dollar and Disagreement Probabilities

How should we think about managerial decisions related to advertising campaigns? We adopt a simple perspective drawn from industry practice, wherein marketers evaluate campaign performance based on incremental conversions per dollar (ICPD).

We assume that a manager considers a campaign successful if it achieves an ICPD that falls above some threshold
\text{ICPD}^{*}
. Otherwise, it is viewed as a failure, and the manager would have preferred to use the campaign budget for another business activity. The advertising manager can choose this threshold based on the expected profit they would observe for every incremental conversion. From a decision-making standpoint, the critical question is whether the manager would reach the same conclusion had they used an RCT or a predictive approach.
23
23

23

The ICPD is simply the inverse of a “Cost per X” type metric. A significant proportion of marketers evaluate campaign success using metrics such as Cost per Conversion (CPC), Cost per Acquisition (CPA), Cost per Completed View (CPCV), Cost per Engagement (CPE) and others
(
xaxis_2018
)
. All of these metric relate some type of outcome to campaign costs. Marketers typically want to achieve a sufficiently low Cost per “conversion” outcome in comparison to some type of benchmark, either internal or from their industry. For example, see
https://growhackscale.com/glossary/cost-per-conversion-metric
, accessed on February 28, 2023. However, these metrics are normally interpreted as if they measure the cost per
incremental
outcome, even though the advertiser is likely relying on proxy metrics that do not necessarily yield causal estimates.

For illustration, consider a simple example where a manager sets an advertising budget of $1,000 and an ICPD threshold of
\text{ICPD}^{*}=0.1
. Assuming the entire budget is spent, the manager hopes to achieve at least one hundred incremental conversions. Suppose the manager implements the campaign as an RCT and obtains only fifty incremental conversions. This outcome implies that
\text{ICPD}_{r}=\dfrac{50}{\$1,000}=0.05
, leading the manager to declare the campaign a failure because
\text{ICPD}^{*}>\text{ICPD}_{r}
. Instead, suppose that the manager had used a predictive model that estimated the campaign would deliver 200 incremental conversions, or
\widehat{\text{ICPD}}_{r}=0.2
. The manager would have judged the campaign a success using the predictive approach. Here the two methods
disagree
, because the manager would have reached a different conclusion had they implemented an RCT compared to the predictive approach. Notice that this example represents a false positive: the predictive approach yields a positive campaign evaluation even though the RCT would have indicated otherwise. If the estimates of incremental conversions from the RCT and the non-experimental method had been reversed, the methods would still disagree, representing a false negative. To conclude the example, if the estimates of both methods imply ICPD that are below or above the threshold, we say that the methods
agree
.

We estimate the disagreement probability between the RCT and the four raw LC metrics and three PIE models,
(\text{cf-lc1h},\text{lm-m2},\text{rf-m2})
, as a function of
\text{ICPD}^{*}
. In this way, we do not take a stand on what might constitute a reasonable
\text{ICPD}^{*}
for any given RCT since we lack information on the value of advertisers’ outside options. For each purchase funnel position
p
, the disagreement probability given threshold
\text{ICPD}^{*}
is taken as the following mean across campaigns within that funnel position:

Note that a manager may have different costs for committing either type of error. A false positive—incorrectly concluding that the campaign was a success—might lead the manager to implement future ad campaigns that are similarly unsuccessful, potentially wasting a significant amount of funds over time. A false negative might lead to the opposite outcome, causing an advertiser to hold back on further advertising spending even though it would have been the right decision. In this paper, we cannot assess the relative magnitudes of these costs. However, this information could be incorporated into the decision problem if an advertiser can.
