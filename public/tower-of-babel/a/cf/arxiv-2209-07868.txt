On Stochastic Orders and Total Positivity
Lutz Dümbgen, Alexandre Mösching

Abstract
The usual stochastic order and the likelihood ratio order between probability distributions on the real line are reviewed in full generality. In addition, for the distribution of a random pair ( X , Y ) (X,Y) , it is shown that the conditional distributions of Y Y , given X = x X=x , are increasing in x x with respect to the likelihood ratio order if and only if the joint distribution of ( X , Y ) (X,Y) is totally positive of order two (TP2) in a certain sense. It is also shown that these three types of constraints are stable under weak convergence, and that weak convergence of TP2 distributions implies convergence of the conditional distributions just mentioned.



Keywords:

Conditional distribution, likelihood ratio order, order constraint, weak convergence.

AMS 2000 subject classifications:

60E15, 62E10, 62H05.

1
Introduction

In nonparametric statistics, shape-constraints on distributions offer an interesting alternative to more traditional smoothness constraints. Specifically, consider a univariate regression setting with a generic observation
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. In many applications it is a natural constraint that the conditional distribution
Q(\cdot\,|\,x):=\mathcal{L}(Y\,|\,X=x)
is increasing in
x\in\mathbb{R}
with respect to some order on the set of probability distributions on the real line.
Mösching and Dümbgen 2020
analyze estimators of
Q(\cdot\,|\,x)
under the sole assumption that it is increasing in
x
with respect to the usual stochastic order, and
Mösching and Dümbgen 2022
investigate the same estimation problem under the stronger constraint that
Q(\cdot\,|\,x)
is increasing in
x
with respect to the likelihood ratio order.

The likelihood ratio order has also received attention in nonparametric methods for two- or
k
-sample problems, for instance,
Dardanoni and Forcina 1998
,
Westling et al. 2022
and
Hu et al. 2022
.

One goal of this paper is to review stochastic order, likelihood ratio order and their properties and relations in full generality, extending previous results presented by
Shaked and Shanthikumar 2007
. Here are precise definitions of these orders, where
Q_{1},Q_{2}
denote arbitrary probability distributions on the real line:

Stochastic order:

Q_{1}\leq_{\rm st}Q_{2}
if
Q_{1}((y,\infty))\leq Q_{2}((y,\infty))
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Likelihood ratio order:

Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
if
Q_{1},Q_{2}
admit densities
g_{1},g_{2}
with respect to some dominating measure such that
g_{2}/g_{1}
is increasing on
\{g_{1}+g_{2}>0\}
.

It is known and will be explained later that
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
implies that
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm st}Q_{2}
. Note that likelihood ratio ordering is familiar from discriminant analysis and mathematical statistics, see
Karlin and Rubin 1956
and
Lehmann and Romano 2005
. Suppose that
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
. If we observe
(Y,C)\in\mathbb{R}\times\{1,2\}
, where
\mathcal{L}(Y\,|\,C=c)=Q_{c}
, then the posterior probability
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(C=2\,|\,Y)
is isotonic in
Y
. If we observe
Y\sim Q_{\theta}
with an unknown parameter
\theta\in\{1,2\}
, then an optimal test of the null hypothesis that
\theta=1
versus the alternative hypothesis that
\theta=2
rejects the former for large values of
Y
. Furthermore, likelihood ratio ordering is a frequent assumption or implication of models in mathematical finance, see
Beare and Moon 2015
and
Jewitt 1991
.

The notion of likelihood ratio order and its relation to stochastic order is reviewed thoroughly in Section
2
, showing that it defines a partial order on the set of all probability measures on the real line which is preserved under weak convergence. No restrictions on the distributions are needed. It is also explained that two distributions are likelihood ratio ordered if and only if the corresponding receiving operator characteristic (ROC) is a concave curve, and this is related to convexity of their ordinal dominance curve. The latter results generalize previous work, e.g. by
Westling et al. 2022
.

Another goal of this paper is to fully understand the relationship between the distribution of a random pair
(X,Y)
and order constraints on the conditional distributions
Q(\cdot\,|\,x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In Section
3
it is shown how to characterize
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
such that
Q(\cdot\,|\,x)
is increasing in
x
with respect to stochastic order or likelihood ratio order. In particular, we explain the connection between likelihood ratio ordering and total positivity of order two (TP2) of bivariate distributions. Finally, it is shown that these properties of bivariate distributions are preserved under weak convergence, and that this implies convergence of the conditional distributions. To the best of our knowledge, most of the material in Section
3
is completely new.

All proofs are deferred to Section
4
.

2
Likelihood ratio order

Let
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}
. Our first result provides various equivalent conditions for
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
, the first of which is the definition given in the introduction.

Theorem 1
.

The following four conditions on
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are equivalent:

(i)
There exist a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
and densities
g_{j}=\mathrm{d}Q_{j}/\mathrm{d}\mu
(
j=1,2
) such that

g_{2}/g_{1}\ \ \text{is isotonic on}\ \ \{g_{1}+g_{2}>0\}.

(ii)
There exist a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
and densities
g_{j}=\mathrm{d}Q_{j}/\mathrm{d}\mu
(
j=1,2
) such that

g_{1}(y)g_{2}(x)\ \leq\ g_{1}(x)g_{2}(y)\quad\text{whenever}\ x<y.

(iii)
For arbitrary sets
A,B\in\mathcal{B}
such that
A<B
(element-wise),

Q_{1}(B)Q_{2}(A)\ \leq\ Q_{1}(A)Q_{2}(B).

(iv)
There exists a dense subset
D
of
\mathbb{R}
such that for all intervals
A=(x,y]
and
B=(y,z]
with endpoints
x<y<z
in
D
,

Q_{1}(B)Q_{2}(A)\ \leq\ Q_{1}(A)Q_{2}(B).

Remark 2
.

Shaked and Shanthikumar 2007
restrict their attention to probability measures which are either discrete or dominated by Lebesgue measure. Their definition of likelihood ratio order corresponds to properties (i–ii) with
\mu
being counting measure or Lebesgue measure on the real line.

Remark 3

(Weak convergence).

An important aspect of Theorem
1
is that conditions (iii-iv) do not involve an explicit dominating measure or explicit densities of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
. In particular, if
(Q_{n1})_{n}
and
(Q_{n2})_{n}
are sequences of distributions on
\mathbb{R}
converging weakly to
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, respectively, and if
Q_{n1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{n2}
for all
n
, then
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
as well. This follows from (iv) with
D
being the set of all
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
Q_{1}(\{x\})=0=Q_{2}(\{x\})
.

Remark 4

(Stochastic order).

The likelihood ratio order is stronger than the stochastic order in the sense that
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm st}Q_{2}
if
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
. This follows immediately from condition (iii) applied to
A:=(-\infty,y]
and
B:=(y,\infty)
for arbitrary
y\in\mathbb{R}
, because
Q_{1}(B)Q_{2}(A)=Q_{1}(B)-Q_{1}(B)Q_{2}(B)
and
Q_{1}(A)Q_{2}(B)=Q_{2}(B)-Q_{1}(B)Q_{2}(B)
.

The reverse statement is false in general, but the likelihood ratio order of two distributions is tightly connected to stochastic order of domain-conditional distributions.

Lemma 5
.

The following four properties of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are equivalent:

(i)

Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
.

(ii)

Q_{1}(\cdot\,|\,C)\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}(\cdot\,|\,C)
for all sets
C\in\mathcal{B}
such that
Q_{1}(C),Q_{2}(C)>0
.

(iii)

Q_{1}(\cdot\,|\,C)\leq_{\rm st}Q_{2}(\cdot\,|\,C)
for all sets
C\in\mathcal{B}
such that
Q_{1}(C),Q_{2}(C)>0
.

(iv)
There exists a dense subset
D
of
\mathbb{R}
such that
Q_{1}(\cdot\,|\,(x,y])\leq_{\rm st}Q_{2}(\cdot\,|\,(x,y])
for arbitrary points
x<y
in
D
such that
Q_{1}((x,y]),Q_{2}((x,y])>0
.

The next result shows that the relation
\leq_{\rm lr}
defines indeed a partial order on the space of arbitrary probability measures on the real line.

Lemma 6
.

For arbitrary probability measures
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
on
\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{1}

\displaystyle\text{(reflexivity)};

\displaystyle Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}\ \ \text{and}\ \ Q_{2}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{1}\ \ \text{implies that}\ \ Q_{1}=Q_{2}

\displaystyle\text{(antisymmetry)};

\displaystyle Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}\ \ \text{and}\ \ Q_{2}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{3}\ \ \text{implies that}\ \ Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{3}

\displaystyle\text{(transitivity)}.

Another interesting aspect of the likelihood ratio order is its connection with the
receiver operating characteristic (ROC)
of a pair
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
, that means, the set

\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})\ :=\ \bigl\{\bigl(Q_{1}(H),Q_{2}(H)\bigr):H\in\mathcal{H}\bigr\}

with
\mathcal{H}
denoting the set of left-bounded half-lines, augmented by
\emptyset
and
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{H}\ :=\ \bigl\{(y,\infty):y\in\mathbb{R}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{[y,\infty):y\in\mathbb{R}\bigr\}\cup\{\emptyset,\mathbb{R}\}.

Note that the family
\mathcal{H}
is totally ordered by inclusion. With the distribution function
G_{j}
of
Q_{j}
(
j=1,2
) one may also write

\displaystyle\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})

\displaystyle\ =\ \bigl\{\bigl(1-G_{1}(y),1-G_{2}(y)\bigr):y\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{\bigl(1-G_{1}(y\,-),1-G_{2}(y\,-)\bigr):y\in\mathbb{R}\bigr\},

where
\bar{\mathbb{R}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
and
G_{j}(-\infty):=0
,
G_{j}(\infty):=1
. Thus we take the freedom to refer to
\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
as the ROC
curve
of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, imagining a (possibly non-continuous) curve within the unit square
[0,1]\times[0,1]
, connecting the points
(1,1)
and
(0,0)
.

Obviously, the ROC curve is isotonic in the sense that if
(a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
, then
(a_{1},a_{2})\leq(b_{1},b_{2})
or
(a_{1},a_{2})\geq(b_{1},b_{2})
component-wise. By means of Theorem
1
, one can easily show that likelihood ratio order is equivalent to a concavity property of the ROC curve.

Corollary 7
.

Two distributions
Q_{1},Q_{2}
satisfy
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
if and only if
\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
is concave in the following sense: If
(a_{1},a_{2})
,
(b_{1},b_{2})
and
(c_{1},c_{2})
are three different points in
\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
with
a_{1}\leq b_{1}\leq c_{1}
and
a_{2}\leq b_{2}\leq c_{2}
, then

\frac{b_{2}-a_{2}}{b_{1}-a_{1}}\ \geq\ \frac{c_{2}-b_{2}}{c_{1}-b_{1}}.

An object related to the ROC curve of
Q_{1},Q_{2}
is the
ordinal dominance curve

H_{G_{1},G_{2}}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
,

H_{G_{1},G_{2}}(\alpha)\ :=\ G_{2}\bigl(G_{1}^{-1}(\alpha)\bigr),

where
G_{j}^{-1}(\alpha):=\min\{y\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}:G_{j}(y)\geq\alpha\}
. The next result has been shown by
Lehmann and Rojo 1992
in the special case of
Q_{2}\ll Q_{1}
and
G_{1},G_{2}
being continuous and strictly increasing, where
Q_{2}\ll Q_{1}
means that
Q_{2}
admits a density with respect to
Q_{1}
.
Westling et al. 2022
proved the same result under the assumption that
Q_{2}\ll Q_{1}
with a density
\mathrm{d}Q_{2}/\mathrm{d}Q_{1}
which is continuous on the support of
Q_{1}
.

Corollary 8
.

Suppose that
Q_{2}\ll Q_{1}
. Then
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
if and only if the ordinal dominance curve
H_{G_{1},G_{2}}
is convex on the image of
G_{1}
, i.e. the set
G_{1}(\bar{\mathbb{R}})=\{G_{1}(y):y\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}\}
.

Remark 9
.

The assumption that
Q_{2}\ll Q_{1}
is essential in Corollary
8
. For instance, if
Q_{1}=(\delta_{0}+\delta_{1})/2
with
\delta_{y}
denoting Dirac measure at
y
and
Q_{2}=\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, then
H_{G_{1},G_{2}}(0)=H_{G_{1},G_{2}}(1/2)=0
and
H_{G_{1},G_{2}}(1)=1
. In consequence, the ordinal dominance curve restricted to
G_{1}(\bar{\mathbb{R}})=\{0,1/2,1\}
is convex, but
Q_{1}
is not smaller than
Q_{2}
with respect to likelihood ratio order since
\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})=\{(0,0),(1,1)\}\cup\{(1/2,u):0\leq u\leq 1\}
is not concave.

Example 10
.

Let
Q_{1}=\mathcal{N}(1,1)
and
Q_{2}=\mathcal{N}(3,6)
. Here
\log dQ_{2}/dQ_{1}(y)
is strictly convex in
y\in\mathbb{R}
with limit
\infty
as
|y|\to\infty
, whence neither
Q_{1}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{2}
nor
Q_{2}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{1}
. The left panel of Figure
1
shows the corresponding ROC curve
\mathrm{ROC}(Q_{1},Q_{2})
which is obviously not concave. Now let us replace these Gaussian distributions with gamma distributions,
Q_{1}=\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
and
Q_{2}=\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
, where
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
denotes the gamma distribution with shape parameter
\alpha>0
and scale parameter
\beta>0
. One can easily show that
\log dQ_{2}/dQ_{1}(y)
is strictly increasing in
y>0
if
\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}
,
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
and
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})\neq(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
. The right panel of Figure
1
shows the concave ROC curve for
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})=(1,1)
and
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})=(1.5,2)
, i.e. the first and second moments of
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
coincide with those of the Gaussian distributions considered before.

ROC curves for two pairs
(Q_{1},Q_{2})
.

3
Bivariate distributions

Throughout this section we consider a pair
(X,Y)
of real-valued random variables with distribution
R=\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
and marginal distributions
P=\mathcal{L}(X)
and
Q=\mathcal{L}(Y)
. We investigate order constraints on the conditional distributions of
Y
, given that
X
lies in certain regions. The
range
of the random variable
X
is defined as the set

\mathcal{X}\ :=\ \bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X\leq x),\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq x)>0\bigr\}.

One can easily verify that
\mathcal{X}
is the smallest real interval such that
P(\mathcal{X})=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X\in\mathcal{X})=1
.

3.1
Conditional distributions and order constraints

It is well-known from measure theory that the conditional distribution of
Y
, given
X
, may be described by a stochastic kernel
K:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{B}\to[0,1]
. That is,

•

for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
K(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}
;

•

for any fixed
B\in\mathcal{B}
,
K(\cdot,B)
is measurable on
\mathbb{R}
;

•

for arbitrary
A,B\in\mathcal{B}
,

R(A\times B)\ =\ \int_{A}K(x,B)\,P(\mathrm{d}x).

The following two theorems clarify under which conditions on the distribution
R
of
(X,Y)
, the conditional distribution
K(x,\cdot)
is isotonic in
x\in\mathcal{X}
with respect to stochastic order or likelihood ratio order. Interestingly, the proof is constructive, showing that

K(x,(y,\infty))\ =\ \sup_{a<x\colon P((a,x])>0}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(Y>y\,|\,a<X\leq x)

for
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 11

(Stochastic order).

The following three conditions are equivalent:

(i)

For arbitrary sets
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
with
A_{1}<A_{2}
and real numbers
y
,

(1)

R(A_{1}\times(y,\infty))P(A_{2})\ \leq\ P(A_{1})R(A_{2}\times(y,\infty)).

(ii)

There exists a dense subset
D
of
\mathbb{R}
such that for arbitrary numbers
x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}
and
y
in
D
, (
1
) holds true with
A_{1}=(x_{0},x_{1}]
and
A_{2}=(x_{1},x_{2}]
.

(iii)

The stochastic kernel
K
may be constructed such that for arbitrary
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
,

K(x_{1},\cdot)\ \leq_{\rm st}\ K(x_{2},\cdot).

Remark 12
.

Note that
P(A_{j})=R(A_{j}\times(-\infty,y])+R(A_{j}\times(y,\infty))
, so inequality (
1
) is equivalent to

(2)

R(A_{1}\times(y,\infty))R(A_{2}\times(-\infty,y])\ \leq\ R(A_{1}\times(-\infty,y])R(A_{2}\times(y,\infty)).

This underlines the connection to the next theorem about likelihood ratio order.

Theorem 13

(Likelihood ratio order).

The following three conditions are equivalent:

(i)

For arbitrary Borel sets
A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2}
with
A_{1}<A_{2}
and
B_{1}<B_{2}
,

(3)

R(A_{1}\times B_{2})R(A_{2}\times B_{1})\ \leq\ R(A_{1}\times B_{1})R(A_{2}\times B_{2}).

(ii)

There exists a dense subset
D
of
\mathbb{R}
such that for arbitrary numbers
x_{0}<x_{1}<x_{2}
and
y_{0}<y_{1}<y_{2}
in
D
, (
3
) holds true with
A_{1}=(x_{0},x_{1}]
,
A_{2}=(x_{1},x_{2}]
and
B_{1}=(y_{0},y_{1}]
,
B_{2}=(y_{1},y_{2}]
.

(iii)

The stochastic kernel
K
may be constructed such that for arbitrary
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
,

K(x_{1},\cdot)\ \leq_{\rm lr}\ K(x_{2},\cdot).

Remark 14
.

Condition (i) in Theorems
11
and
13
is equivalent to the following statement about conditional distributions: Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be arbitrary Borel sets such that
A_{1}<A_{2}
and
P(A_{1})
,
P(A_{2})
are strictly positive. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Y\,|\,X\in A_{1})\

\displaystyle\leq_{\rm st}\ \mathcal{L}(Y\,|\,X\in A_{2})\quad\text{(Theorem~\ref{Thm:Kernel.st})},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Y\,|\,X\in A_{1})\

\displaystyle\leq_{\rm lr}\ \mathcal{L}(Y\,|\,X\in A_{2})\quad\text{(Theorem~\ref{Thm:Kernel.lr})}.

Condition (ii) in Theorems
11
and
13
may be re-interpreted analogously.

3.2
Total positivity of order two

Theorem
13
hints at a connection between likelihood ratio ordering and total positivity. A good starting point for the latter concept is the monograph of
Karlin 1968
. Recall that a function
h:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
totally positive of order two (TP2)
if for arbitrary real numbers
x_{1}<x_{2}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}
,

(4)

h(x_{2},y_{1})h(x_{1},y_{2})\ \leq\ h(x_{1},y_{1})h(x_{2},y_{2}).

Thus, conditions (i) and (ii) of Theorem
13
may be interpreted as a distributional notion of total positivity of order two.

Definition 15

(Total positivity of order two of bivariate distributions).

A probability distribution
R
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
is called
totally positive of order two (TP2)
if for arbitrary Borel sets
A_{1}<A_{2}
and
B_{1}<B_{2}
,

R(A_{2}\times B_{1})R(A_{1}\times B_{2})\ \leq\ R(A_{1}\times B_{1})R(A_{2}\times B_{2}).

Remark 16

(TP2 of distributions and densities).

Suppose that the distribution of
(X,Y)
has a density
h
with respect to a product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
\mu
and
\nu
are
\sigma
-finite measures on
\mathbb{R}
. If the function
h
is TP2, then the distribution of
(X,Y)
is TP2. To verify this, one has to express the probabilities in conditions (i) or (ii) of Theorem
13
as integrals of
h
with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
.

When looking at the previous remark, one might think at first glance that a reverse statement is true with, say,
\mu=P
and
\nu=Q
. But this is false. For instance, let
X
be uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, and let the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
be given by the kernel
K
with

K(x,\cdot)\ :=\ \begin{cases}\mathrm{Unif}(0,1/3)&\text{if}\ x\leq 1/3,\\
\delta_{x}&\text{if}\ 1/3<x<2/3,\\
\mathrm{Unif}(2/3,1)&\text{if}\ x\geq 2/3.\end{cases}

Then
Y
follows the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
too, and condition (iii) of Theorem
13
is clearly satisfied, but the distribution of
(X,Y)
has no density with respect to any product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
. Theorem
18
below provides a general statement, but let us start with a special case with a clean, positive answer which follows immediately from Theorem
13
:

Corollary 17
.

Suppose that
(X,Y)
has a discrete distribution with probability mass function
h
, i.e.
h(x,y)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X=x,Y=y)
. Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

For arbitrary real numbers
x_{1}<x_{2}
with
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{1}),\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X=x_{2})>0
,

K(x_{1},\cdot)\ \leq_{\rm lr}\ K(x_{2},\cdot).

(ii)

The function
h
is TP2.

To formulate our general result about total positivity and likelihood ratio order, we introduce a northwest and a southeast boundary for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle S^{\rm nw}(x)\ :=\

\displaystyle\min\bigl\{y\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}:\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X\leq x,Y>y)=0\bigr\},

\displaystyle S^{\rm se}(x)\ :=\

\displaystyle\max\bigl\{y\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}:\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq x,Y<y)=0\bigr\}.

One can easily verify that this defines two isotonic functions
S^{\rm nw},S^{\rm se}:\mathbb{R}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, on
\mathcal{X}
,
S^{\rm nw}>-\infty
and
S^{\rm se}<\infty
.

Theorem 18
.

Suppose that the distribution of
(X,Y)
is TP2. Set

\mathcal{X}_{o}\ :=\ \bigl\{x\in\mathcal{X}:S^{\rm nw}(x)\leq S^{\rm se}(x)\bigr\}.

Then the kernel
K
can be constructed such that it satisfies condition (iii) in Theorem
13
and has the following properties:

(a)

If
x\in\mathcal{X}_{o}
, then
K(x,\cdot)=\delta_{S(x)}
, where
S:\mathcal{X}_{o}\to\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary isotonic function such that
S^{\rm nw}(x)\leq S(x)\leq S^{\rm se}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{o}
.

(b)

If
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{o}
, then
K(x,\cdot)
has a bounded density
h(x,\cdot)
with respect to
Q
such that
h(x,y)=0
if
y<S^{\rm se}(x)
or
y>S^{\rm nw}(x)
. Moreover,
h
is TP2 on the set

, that means, (
4
) holds true for arbitrary numbers
x_{1}<x_{2}
in
\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{o}
and real numbers
y_{1}<y_{2}
.

3.3
Weak convergence

Our starting point is the following basic result.

Lemma 19
.

Consider a sequence
(R_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of distributions on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
converging weakly to a distribution
R
. Suppose that each
R_{n}
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
11
or even of Theorem
13
. Then the limit
R
satisfies the same conditions too.

This lemma follows from condition (ii) in both theorems, if we chose
D
to be the set of all
y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
P(\{y\})=Q(\{y\})=0
. The question is whether and in what sense the corresponding stochastic kernel
K_{n}
for
R_{n}
described in condition (iii) converges to the stochastic kernel
K
for
R
.

Specifically, we consider conditional quantiles of
K_{n}(x,\cdot)
. For
\beta\in(0,1)
, let
q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x)
be some real number
y
such that

K_{n}(x,(-\infty,y))\ \leq\ \beta\ \leq\ K_{n}(x,(-\infty,y]).

Since the kernel
K(x,\cdot)
of the limiting distribution
R
is not necessarily unique for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we consider the extremal kernels
K^{\rm w}
and
K^{\rm e}
introduced in the proof of Theorem
18
and the corresponding minimal and maximal
\beta
-quantiles, respectively, that is,

\displaystyle q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)\

\displaystyle:=\ \min\bigl\{y\in\mathbb{R}\colon K^{\rm w}(x,(-\infty,y])\geq\beta\bigr\},

\displaystyle q^{\rm e}(\beta\,|\,x)\

\displaystyle:=\ \max\bigl\{y\in\mathbb{R}\colon K^{\rm e}(x,(-\infty,y))\leq\beta\bigr\}.

Theorem 20
.

Suppose that all
R_{n}
(and thus
R
) satisfy the conditions of Theorem
11
. For any
\beta\in(0,1)
and arbitrary interior points
x_{1}<x_{2}
of
\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x_{2})\

\displaystyle\geq\ q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x_{1}),

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x_{1})\

\displaystyle\leq\ q^{\rm e}(\beta\,|\,x_{2}).

Corollary 21
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
20
, suppose that
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)=q^{\rm e}(\beta\,|\,x)
for some fixed
\beta\in(0,1)
and all
x
in an interval
(a,b)\subset\mathcal{X}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\sup_{x\in[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}\bigl|q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x)-q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)\bigr|\ =\ 0

for arbitrary
a<a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}<b
.

The previous findings lead naturally to consistent estimators of a TP2 distribution
R
in principle. Suppose that
(\hat{R}_{n})_{n}
is a (random) sequence of distributions which converges to
R
(almost surely) with respect to the bivariate Kuiper norm
\|\hat{R}_{n}-R\|_{\rm K}
, say, where

\|\sigma\|_{\rm K}\ :=\ \sup_{a_{1}<a_{2},b_{1}<b_{2}}\,\bigl|\sigma\bigl((a_{1},a_{2}]\times(b_{1},b_{2}]\bigr)\bigr|

for a finite signed measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. For instance,
\hat{R}_{n}
could be the empirical distribution of independent random variables
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
with distribution
R
. If
\check{R}_{n}
is a TP2 distribution such that

\|\check{R}_{n}-\hat{R}_{n}\|_{\rm K}\ =\ \min_{\tilde{R}\ \text{a TP2 distribution}}\|\tilde{R}-\hat{R}_{n}\|_{\rm K},

then
\|\check{R}_{n}-R\|_{\rm K}
is bounded by
\|\check{R}_{n}-\hat{R}_{n}\|_{\rm K}+\|R-\hat{R}_{n}\|_{\rm K}\leq 2\|R-\hat{R}_{n}\|_{\rm K}
. In particular, the sequence
(\check{R}_{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
R
(almost surely). The existence of a TP2 approximation
\check{R}_{n}
of
\hat{R}_{n}
is not obvious for arbitrary distributions
\hat{R}_{n}
, but at least in case of
\hat{R}_{n}
having finite support this is true.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\hat{R}
be a distribution with finite support. Then there exists a TP2 distribution
\check{R}
minimising
\|\tilde{R}-\hat{R}\|_{\rm K}
over all TP2 distributions
\tilde{R}
.

The proof of this lemma reveals that the best approximating distribution
\check{R}
may have finite support too, but it is not unique. Its explicit computation is yet an open problem.

4.1
Isotonic densities

For later purposes, we need a constructive version of the Radon-Nikodym theorem in a special case.

Lemma 23
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be finite measures on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\nu\leq\mu
. Further, suppose that

(5)

\mu((y,z])\nu((x,y])\ \leq\ \mu((x,y])\nu((y,z])\quad\text{whenever}\ x<y<z.

Then

f(x)\ :=\ \sup_{a<x}\,\frac{\nu((a,x])}{\mu((a,x])}\ \in\ [0,1]\quad(\text{with}\ 0/0:=0)

defines an isotonic density of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
. Moreover, if
\mu((x^{\prime},x])=0
for numbers
x^{\prime}<x
, then
f(x^{\prime})=f(x)
. Further, if
x\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\mu((a,x])>0
for all
a<x
, then

f(x)\ =\ \lim_{a\to x\,-}\,\frac{\nu((a,x])}{\mu((a,x])}.

In particular,
f(x)=\nu(\{x\})/\mu(\{x\})
in case of
\mu(\{x\})>0
.

Remark 24
.

The density
f
constructed in Lemma
23
is minimal in the sense that any isotonic density
\tilde{f}
of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
satisfies
\tilde{f}\geq f
pointwise. For if
f(x)>0
, then for all
a<x
with
\mu((a,x])>0
,

\mu((a,x])^{-1}\nu((a,x])\ =\ \mu((a,x])^{-1}\int_{(a,x]}\tilde{f}(w)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}w)\ \leq\ \tilde{f}(x).

A maximal isotonic density could be constructed analogously: First note that with a simple approximation argument one can show that condition (
5
) is equivalent to the same condition with
[x_{0},x_{1})
and
[x_{1},x_{2})
in place of
(x_{0},x_{1}]
and
(x_{1},x_{2}]
, respectively. Then one could define the density at
x
by

\inf_{b>x}\,\frac{\nu([x,b))}{\mu([x,b))}\ \in\ [0,1]\quad(\text{with}\ 0/0:=1).

Proof of Lemma
23
.

Let
\gamma:=\nu/\mu
with
0/0:=0
. Then
f(x)=\sup_{a<x}\gamma((a,x])
. Condition (
5
) is easily verified to be equivalent to

(6)

\gamma((x,y])\ \leq\ \gamma((y,z])\quad\text{whenever}\ x<y<z\ \text{and}\ \mu((x,z])>0.

As to isotonicity of
f
, let
x<y
. If
\mu((x,y])=0
, then
\nu((x,y])=0
, too, whence
\gamma((a,y])=0
for
x\leq a<y
and
\gamma((a,y])=\gamma((a,x])
for
a<x
. Consequently,

f(y)\ =\ \sup_{a<x}\,\gamma((a,y])\ =\ \sup_{a<x}\,\gamma((a,x])\ =\ f(x).

On the other hand, if
\mu((x,y])>0
, then (
6
) implies that
\gamma((a,x])\leq\gamma((x,y])\leq f(y)
for
a<x
, whence
f(x)\leq\gamma((x,y])\leq f(y)
. This implies that

(7)

f(x)\mu((x,y])\ \leq\ \nu((x,y])\ \leq\ f(y)\mu((x,y])\quad\text{whenever}\ x<y.

If
x\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\mu((a,x])>0
for all
a<x
, then it follows from (
6
) that for
a<a^{\prime}<x
,

\gamma((a,x])\ =\ \frac{\mu((a,a^{\prime}])}{\mu((a,x])}\,\gamma((a,a^{\prime}])+\frac{\mu((a^{\prime},x])}{\mu((a,x])}\,\gamma((a^{\prime},x])\ \leq\ \gamma((a^{\prime},x]),

because
\gamma((a,a^{\prime}])\leq\gamma((a^{\prime},x])
. Hence,
\gamma((a,x])
is isotonic in
a<x
, and this implies the representation of
f(x)
as
\lim_{a\to x\,-}\gamma((a,x])
. In particular,

(8)

\nu(\{x\})\ =\ f(x)\mu(\{x\})\quad\text{for all}\ x\in\mathbb{R},

because this equation is trivial in case of
\mu(\{x\})=0
.

Now the previous inequalities (
7
) and (
8
) are generalized as follows: For any bounded interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,

(9)

\inf_{z\in I}\,f(z)\,\mu(I)\ \leq\ \nu(I)\ \leq\ \sup_{z\in I}\,f(z)\mu(I).

In case of
I=(x,y]
, this follows from (
7
) applied to
(x^{\prime},y]
with
x^{\prime}\to x\,+
. In case of
I=(x,y)
, this follows from (
9
) applied to
(x,y^{\prime}]
with
y^{\prime}\to y\,-
. In case of
I=[x,y]
or
I=[x,y)
, we may deduce from (
8
) that

\nu(I)\ =\ \nu(\{x\})+\nu(I\setminus\{x\})\ =\ f(x)\mu(\{x\})+\nu(I\setminus\{x\}),

and then the assertion follows from applying the available inequalities to
I\setminus\{x\}
instead of
I
.

It remains to be shown that
f
is a density of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
. That means,
\nu(I)=\int_{I}f\,d\mu
for any bounded interval
I
. To this end we fix an arbitrary integer
k>1
and split
I
into the disjoint intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
where
I_{1}:=I\cap\{f\leq 1/k\}
and
I_{j}:=I\cap\{(j-1)/k<f\leq j/k\}
for
2\leq j\leq k
. Then it follows from (
9
) that

\nu(I)\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu(I_{j})\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\leq\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}(j/k)\mu(I_{j})\ \leq\int_{I}(f+1/k)\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle\geq\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}((j-1)/k)\mu(I_{j})\ \geq\int_{I}(f-1/k)\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\end{cases}

and letting
k\to\infty
yields the asserted equation.
∎

Lemma 25

Let

Proof of Theorem

.

We prove the result via a chain of implications, introducing two additional equivalent conditions:

(v)
There exists an isotonic densitiy
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
of
Q_{2}

Q_{1}+Q_{2}

(vi)
Suppose that
\mu

\sigma
-finite measure dominating

and

Step 1.

\displaystyle Q_{1}(B)Q_{2}(A)\

\displaystyle=\ \int 1_{A}(x)1_{B}(y)g_{1}(y)g_{2}(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq\ \int 1_{A}(x)1_{B}(y)g_{1}(x)g_{2}(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)

\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}=1
. Then

g(y)\ :=\ \lim_{x\to y\,-}\,\frac{Q_{*}((x,y])}{Q((x,y])}

with
0/0:=0
exists and defines a bounded density of
Q_{*}
with respect to
Q
.

Proof of Lemma
29
.

By assumption, for
j=0,1
, there exists a density
\rho_{j}
of
Q_{*}
with respect to
Q_{j}+Q_{*}
with values in
[0,1]
, where
\rho_{0}
is isotonic and
\rho_{1}
is antitonic. Note that
1-\rho_{j}
is automatically a version of
dQ_{j}/d(Q_{j}+Q_{*})
. An important fact is that
\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}
is bounded away from
2
, i.e.
\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}\leq 2-\delta
for some
\delta>0
. To verify this, suppose the contrary. Then there exists a sequence
(x_{k})_{k}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\rho_{j}(x_{k})=1
for
j=0,1
. Without loss of generality let
(x_{k})_{k}
converge to a point
x\in[-\infty,\infty]
. If
x<\infty
, then
\rho_{0}\equiv 1
on
(x,\infty)
, whence
Q_{0}((x,\infty))=0
. Likewise, if
x>-\infty
, then
\rho_{1}\equiv 1
on
(-\infty,x)
, whence
Q_{1}((-\infty,x))=0
. In particular,
x
has to be a real number, but then our assumption on
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
is obviously violated.

Note first that
A_{0}:=\{\rho_{0}=0\}<\{\rho_{0}>0\}
satisfies
Q_{*}(A_{0})=0
, and then
Q_{*}\leq_{\rm lr}Q_{1}
implies that
Q_{1}(A_{0})=0
. Similarly,
A_{1}:=\{\rho_{1}=0\}>\{\rho_{1}>0\}
satisfies
Q_{*}(A_{1})=0=Q_{0}(A_{1})
. Thus the real line may be partitioned into the sets
A_{0}<A_{*}:=\{\rho_{0}\rho_{1}>0\}<A_{1}
, and
Q_{*}(A_{*})=1
. For measurable functions
h\geq 0
on
A_{*}
and
j=0,1
,

\int_{A_{*}}h\,dQ_{*}\ =\ \int h\rho_{j}\,d(Q_{*}+Q_{j}),

so

\int_{A_{*}}h(1-\rho_{j})\,dQ_{*}\ =\ \int h\rho_{j}\,dQ_{j},

and replacing
h
with
h/\rho_{j}
shows that

\int_{A_{*}}h(\rho_{j}^{-1}-1)\,dQ_{*}\ =\ \int h\,dQ_{j}.

Consequently,

\int_{A_{*}}h\bigl(\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}^{-1}-1)\bigr)\,dQ_{*}\ =\ \int_{A_{*}}h\,dQ.

Note that
\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}^{-1}-1)
is not smaller than
\lambda_{0}(1-\rho_{0})+\lambda_{1}(1-\rho_{1})\geq\min(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})\delta>0
. Therefore, we may replace
h
in the previously displayed integrals with
h\big/\bigl(\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}^{-1}-1)\bigr)
and conclude that for measurable functions
h\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
,

\int h\,dQ_{*}\ =\ \int h\tilde{g}\,dQ

with

\tilde{g}\ :=\ \begin{cases}\bigl(\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}^{-1}-1)\bigr)^{-1}&\text{on}\ A_{*},\\
0&\text{on}\ \mathbb{R}\setminus A_{*}.\end{cases}

In other words,
dQ_{*}/dQ=\tilde{g}
, and
\tilde{g}\leq\min(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})^{-1}\delta^{-1}
.

It remains to be shown that
\tilde{g}(y)
may be replaced with the stated limit
g(y)
. Note first that by Lemma
28
, the set
\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathrm{supp}_{\rm left}(Q_{*})
has probability
0
under
Q_{*}
, and
g(y)=0
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathrm{supp}_{\rm left}(Q_{*})
, because
Q_{*}((x,y])=0
if
x<y
is sufficiently close to
y
. It suffices to show that
g(y)=\tilde{g}(y)
for any
y\in\mathrm{supp}_{\rm left}(Q_{*})\subset\mathrm{supp}_{\rm left}(Q)
. If
\rho_{0}
and
1-\rho_{1}
are constructed as in the proof of Theorem
1
by means of Lemma
23
, then for
j=0,1
,

\rho_{j}(x,y):=\frac{Q_{*}((x,y])}{(Q_{j}+Q_{*})((x,y])}\ \to\ \rho_{j}(y)\quad\text{as}\ x\to y\,-,

and

Q_{j}((x,y])\ =\ (\rho_{j}(x,y)^{-1}-1)Q_{*}((x,y]).

Consequently,
Q((x,y])=\bigl(\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}(x,y)^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}(x,y)^{-1}-1)\bigr)Q_{*}((x,y])
, and this leads to

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{*}((x,y])}{Q((x,y])}\

\displaystyle=\ \bigl(\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}(x,y)^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}(x,y)^{-1}-1)\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle\to\ \begin{cases}0&\text{if}\ y\not\in A_{*}\\
\bigl(\lambda_{0}(\rho_{0}(y)^{-1}-1)+\lambda_{1}(\rho_{1}(y)^{-1}-1)\bigr)^{-1}&\text{if}\ y\in A_{*}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\ \tilde{g}(y)

as
x\to y\,-
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
20
.

Note first that by the continuous mapping theorem, the marginal distributions
P_{n}=R_{n}(\cdot\times\mathbb{R})
converge weakly to
P
. Consequently, the Portmanteau theorem implies that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}P_{n}(U)\geq P(U)
for any open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}
, while
\limsup_{n\to\infty}R_{n}(A)\leq R(A)
for any closed set
A\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. Specifically, taking
U=(-\infty,x_{1})
and
U=(x_{2},\infty)
shows that
x_{1}<x_{2}
are interior points of
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon P_{n}((-\infty,x]),P([x,\infty))>0\bigr\}
for sufficiently large
n
.

For symmetry reasons, it suffices to show that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x_{2})\geq y
for any fixed
y<q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x_{1})
. The definition of
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x_{1})
and the construction of
K^{\rm w}
imply that

\beta\ >\ K^{\rm w}(x_{1},(-\infty,y])\ =\ \lim_{a\to a_{o}-}\frac{R\bigl((a,x_{1}]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)}{P((a,x_{1}])}

with
a_{o}:=\min\{a\leq x_{1}:P((a,x_{1}])=0\}
. Hence, there exists a number
a<a_{o}
such that
P(\{a\})=0
,
P((a,x_{1}])>0
and
R\bigl((a,x_{1}]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)/P((a,x_{1}])<\beta
. Consequently, for any fixed
b\in(x_{1},x_{2})
and sufficiently large
n
, the stochastic ordering of
K_{n}(x,\cdot)
implies that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}K_{n}(x_{2},(-\infty,y])\

\displaystyle\leq\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_{n}\bigl((a,b]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)}{P_{n}((a,b])}

\displaystyle\leq\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_{n}\bigl([a,b]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)}{P_{n}((a,b))}

\displaystyle\leq\ \frac{R\bigl([a,b]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)}{P((a,b))}\ =\ \frac{R\bigl((a,b]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)}{P(a,b)}.

But the right-hand side converges to
R\bigl((a,x_{1}]\times(-\infty,y]\bigr)/P((a,x_{1}])<\beta
as
b\downarrow x_{1}
, and this implies that
q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x_{1})>y
for sufficiently large
n
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
21
.

Note first that in general,
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
and
q^{\rm e}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
are left- and right-continuous on
\mathcal{X}
, respectively. For symmetry reasons, it suffices to verify this for
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
. Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
x>\inf(\mathcal{X})
. Since
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
is increasing on
\mathcal{X}
, it suffices to show that
\liminf_{a\to x-}q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,a)\geq q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)
. If
P((a,x])=0
for some
a<x
, then
K^{\rm w}(a^{\prime},\cdot)
and thus
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,a^{\prime})
are constant in
a^{\prime}\in[a,x)
. If
P((a,x])>0
for all
a<x
, then for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

K^{\rm w}(x,(-\infty,y])\ =\ \lim_{a\to x-}P((a,x])^{-1}\int_{(a,x]}K^{\rm w}(t,(-\infty,y])\,P(\mathrm{d}t),

and since
K^{\rm w}(t,(-\infty,y])
is decreasing in
t\in\mathcal{X}
, the right hand side equals

\lim_{a\to x-}K^{\rm w}(a,(-\infty,y]).

For any
y<q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)
, this shows that
K^{\rm w}(a,(-\infty,y])<\beta
if
a<x
is sufficiently close to
x
, whence
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,a)>y
. This implies that
\liminf_{a\to x-}q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,a)=q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)
.

If
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)=q^{\rm e}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
on
(a,b)\subset\mathcal{X}
, then
q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
is continuous on
(a,b)
. But then one can easily deduce from Theorem
20
that
\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}(\beta\,|\,x)=q^{\rm w}(\beta\,|\,x)
for any
x\in(a,b)
. But for any fixed interval
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\subset(a,b)
and sufficiently large
n
, the functions
q_{n}(\beta\,|\,\cdot)
are increasing on
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
, and it is well-known that the pointwise convergence of monotone functions on a compact interval to a continuous function implies uniform convergence.
∎

Proof of Lemma
22
.

Let
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{\ell}
and
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{m}
be real numbers such that the support of
\hat{R}
is contained in
\{(x_{j},y_{k})\colon 1\leq j\leq\ell,1\leq k\leq m\}
. With
x_{0}:=y_{0}:=-\infty
and
x_{\ell+1}:=y_{m+1}:=\infty
, one can easily show that with the family
\mathcal{S}:=\bigl\{(a_{1},a_{2}]\times(b_{1},b_{2}]:a_{1}<a_{2},b_{1}<b_{2}\bigr\}
, for an arbitrary distribution
\tilde{R}
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\,(\hat{R}-\tilde{R})(S)\

\displaystyle=\ \max_{S\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}}\,(\hat{R}-\tilde{R})(S),

and

\displaystyle\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\,(\tilde{R}-\hat{R})(S)\

\displaystyle=\ \max_{S\in\underline{\mathcal{S}}}\,(\tilde{R}-\hat{R})(S),

where

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{S}}\

\displaystyle:=\ \bigl\{[x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}}]\times[y_{k_{1}},y_{k_{2}}]:1\leq j_{1}\leq j_{2}\leq\ell,1\leq k_{1}\leq k_{2}\leq m\bigr\},

\displaystyle\underline{\mathcal{S}}\

\displaystyle:=\ \bigl\{(x_{j_{1}},x_{j_{2}})\times(y_{k_{1}},y_{k_{2}}):0\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\ell+1,0\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq m+1\bigr\}.

Now choose real numbers
x_{1}^{\prime}<x_{2}^{\prime}<\cdots<x_{2\ell+1}^{\prime}
and
y_{1}^{\prime}<y_{2}^{\prime}<\cdots<y_{2m+1}^{\prime}
with
x_{2j}^{\prime}=x_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq\ell
and
y_{2k}^{\prime}=y_{k}
for
1\leq k\leq m
. Then
\|\tilde{R}-\hat{R}\|_{\rm K}
does not change if we replace
\tilde{R}
with the discrete distribution

\sum_{j=1}^{2\ell+1}\sum_{k=1}^{2m+1}p_{jk}\delta_{(x_{j}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime})},

where

\displaystyle p_{2j,2k}\

\displaystyle:=\ \tilde{R}\bigl(\{x_{j}\}\times\{y_{k}\}\bigr)\quad\text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq\ell,1\leq k\leq m,

\displaystyle p_{2j-1,2k}\

\displaystyle:=\ \tilde{R}\bigl((x_{j-1},x_{j})\times\{y_{k}\}\bigr)\quad\text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq\ell+1,1\leq k\leq m,

\displaystyle p_{2j,2k-1}\

\displaystyle:=\ \tilde{R}\bigl(\{x_{j}\}\times(y_{k-1},y_{k})\bigr)\quad\text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq\ell,1\leq k\leq m+1,

\displaystyle p_{2j-1,2k-1}\

\displaystyle:=\ \tilde{R}\bigl((x_{j-1},x_{j})\times(y_{k-1},y_{k})\bigr)\quad\text{for}\ 1\leq j\leq\ell+1,1\leq k\leq m+1.

Note that the values
\tilde{R}(S)
,
S\in\overline{\mathcal{S}}\cup\underline{\mathcal{S}}
, do not change when we perform this discretization of
\tilde{R}
. Moreover, if
\tilde{R}
is TP2, then this discretized version is TP2 as well. Consequently, to minimize
\|\tilde{R}-\hat{R}\|_{\rm K}
over all TP2 distributions
\tilde{R}
, we may restrict our attention to all TP2 distributions on the finite grid
\{x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{2\ell+1}^{\prime}\}\times\{y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,y_{2m+1}^{\prime}\}
. Then
\|\tilde{R}-\hat{R}_{\rm emp}\|_{\rm K}
is a continuous function of the corresponding matrix
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{jk})_{j,k}\in[0,1]^{(2\ell+1)\times(2m+1)}
, and the constraints on
\tilde{R}
define a compact subset of
[0,1]^{(2\ell+1)\times(2m+1)}
. Consequently, a minimizer does exist.
∎
