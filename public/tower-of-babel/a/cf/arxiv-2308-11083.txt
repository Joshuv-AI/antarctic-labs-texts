An Improved Drift Theorem for Balanced Allocations Some of the results of this paper were presented at SPAA 2022 [ 26 ] .
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald

Abstract
In the balanced allocations framework, there are m m jobs (balls) to be allocated to n n servers (bins). The goal is to minimize the gap , the difference between the maximum and the average load. Peres, Talwar and Wieder (RSA 2015) used the hyperbolic cosine potential function to analyze a large family of allocation processes including the ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) -process and graphical balanced allocations. The key ingredient was to prove that the potential drops in every step, i.e., a drift inequality . In this work we improve the drift inequality so that ( i ) (i) it is asymptotically tighter, ( i ​ i ) (ii) it assumes weaker preconditions, ( i ​ i ​ i ) (iii) it applies not only to processes allocating to more than one bin in a single step and ( i ​ v ) (iv) to processes allocating a varying number of balls depending on the sampled bin. Our applications include the processes of (RSA 2015), but also several new processes, and we believe that our techniques may lead to further results in future work. Keywords— Balls-into-bins, balanced allocations, potential functions, drift theorem, heavily loaded, gap bounds, maximum load, memory, two-choices, weighted balls. Notation, Settings and Processes

2.1
Notation

We consider processes that allocate balls into
n
bins, labeled
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The
load vector
at step
t
, that is, after
t
allocations, is
x^{t}=(x_{1}^{t},x_{2}^{t},\ldots,x_{n}^{t})
starting with
x_{i}^{0}=0
for all
i\in[n]
.
Also
y^{t}=(y_{1}^{t},y_{2}^{t},\ldots,y_{n}^{t})
will be the permuted load vector, sorted non-increasingly in load. In this load vector, each bin has a
rank

\Rank^{t}(i)
, where
\Rank^{t}
forms a permutation of
[n]
and satisfies

y_{\Rank^{t}(i)}^{t}=x_{i}^{t}.

Following previous work, we analyze allocation processes in terms of the

\Gap(t):=\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}-\frac{t}{n}=y_{1}^{t},

i.e., the difference between maximum and average load at time
t\geqslant 0
. In all of our bounds, we also bound the difference between the maximum and minimum load. We also define the
height
of a ball as
h\geqslant 1
if it is the
h
-th ball added to the bin. Next we define
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
, the
filtration
corresponding to the first
t
allocations of the process (so in particular,
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
reveals
x^{t}
). A
probability vector

p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any vector satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1
and
p_{i}\in[0,1]
for all
i\in[n]
. Any
allocation process
is defined by a
probability allocation vector

p^{t}:=p^{t}(\mathfrak{F}^{t})
, which is the probability vector where
p_{i}^{t}
is probability of allocating a ball to the
i
-th heaviest bin in step
t
. For some of our analysis, it will be convenient to merge multiple steps into
rounds
.

For two probability allocation vectors
p
and
q
(or analogously, for two sorted load vectors), we say that
p

majorizes

q
if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i},
for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. We denote this by
p\succeq q
.

Theorem 2.1

(Majorization, cf.
[
8
, Theorem 3.5]
)
.

Consider any two processes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
with probability allocation vectors
p^{t}
and
q^{t}
respectively, such that
p^{t}\succeq q^{t}
for every step
t\geqslant 0
. Then,
y_{\mathcal{P}}^{t}\succeq y_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t}
and in particular
(y_{\mathcal{P}}^{t})_{1}\geqslant(y_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{1}
and
(y_{\mathcal{P}}^{t})_{n}\leqslant(y_{\mathcal{Q}}^{t})_{n}
.

Many statements in this work hold only for sufficiently large
n
, and several constants are chosen generously with the intention of making it easier to verify some technical inequalities.

2.2
Settings

The
Weighted
setting.

We will now extend the definitions to
weighted balls
into bins. To this end, let
w^{t}\geqslant 0
be the weight of the
t
-th ball to be allocated (
t\geqslant 1
). By
W^{t}
we denote the total weights of all balls allocated after the first
t\geqslant 0

W^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}w^{s}
. The
normalized loads
are
\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t}:=x_{i}^{t}-\frac{W^{t}}{n}
, and with
y_{i}^{t}
being again the decreasingly sorted, normalized load vector, we have
\Gap(t)=y_{1}^{t}
.

In the
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(\zeta)
setting, the weight of each ball will be drawn independently from a fixed distribution
\mathcal{W}
over
[0,\infty)
. Following
[
35
]
, we assume that the distribution
\mathcal{W}
satisfies:

•

\mathbf{E}[\,\mathcal{W}\,]=1
.

•

\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,]=\Theta(1)
for
\zeta:=\zeta(n)\in(0,1]
.

Specific examples of distributions satisfying above conditions (after scaling) are the geometric, exponential, binomial and Poisson distributions.

Similar to the arguments in
[
35
]
, the above two assumptions imply that:

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider any
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(\zeta)
distribution for any
\zeta\in(0,1]
. Then, there exists
S:=S(\zeta)\geqslant 1/\zeta
, such that for any
\gamma\in(0,\zeta/2]
and any
\ell\in[-1,1]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\gamma\cdot\ell\cdot\mathcal{W}}\,\right]\leqslant 1+\ell\cdot\gamma+S\cdot\ell^{2}\cdot\gamma^{2}.

As this parameter
S
is used in most of the upper bounds involving the
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(\zeta)
weights, we often refer to the setting as
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(S)
.

Proof.

This proof closely follows the argument in
[
35
, Lemma 2.1]
. Let
M(z)=\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{z\mathcal{W}}\,\right]
, then using Taylor’s Theorem (mean value form remainder), for any
z\in[-\gamma,\gamma]
there exists
\xi\in[-\gamma,\gamma]
such that

M(z)=M(0)+M^{\prime}(0)\cdot z+M^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot z^{2}=1+z+M^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot z^{2}.

By the assumptions on
\gamma
and
\zeta
,

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\,\mathcal{W}^{2}e^{\xi\mathcal{W}}\,]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[\,\mathcal{W}^{4}\,]\cdot\mathbf{E}[\,e^{2\xi\mathcal{W}}\,]}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}[\,\mathcal{W}^{4}\,]+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{2\xi\mathcal{W}}\,]\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{8}{\zeta}\cdot\log\left(\frac{8}{\zeta}\right)\right)^{4}+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,]+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{2\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,]\right),

where
(a)
uses the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality
|\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\cdot Y\,\right]|\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{2}\,\right]\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y^{2}\,\right]}
for random variables
X
and
Y
,
(b)
uses the AM-GM inequality, and
(c)
uses
A.2
. Now defining

S:=\max\left\{\left(\frac{8}{\zeta}\cdot\log\left(\frac{8}{\zeta}\right)\right)^{4},\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,]+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{2\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,]\right\}\geqslant 1,

using that
\zeta\leqslant 1
and choosing
z:=\ell\cdot\gamma
,
the lemma follows.

In this part, we include the formal description of all allocation processes considered in this work. Recall that
w^{t+1}

One-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
At step
t\geqslant 0
, sample one bin
i\in[n]
independently and uniformly at random. Then, update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+w^{t+1}.

We continue with a formal description of the

Two-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
At step
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1},i_{2}\in[n]
, independently and uniformly at random. Let
i\in\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
be such that
x_{i}^{t}=\min\{x_{i_{1}}^{t},x_{i_{2}}^{t}\}
, favoring bins with a higher index. Then, update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+w^{t+1}.

It is immediate that the probability allocation vector of
Two-Choice
is

p_{i}=\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

Following
[
31
]

(

i_{1},i_{2}\in[n]

\begin{cases}x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+w^{t+1}&\mbox{with probability $\beta$},\\
x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+w^{t+1}&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The probability allocation vector of the
(1+\beta)
-process is given by:

p_{i}=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

Claim 4.1
.

Consider the probability vector
r
as defined in
Eq.

4.1
. For any bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}\geqslant 0
, we have that

\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}\leqslant-\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+\frac{2\gamma\varepsilon}{n},

and for any bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}<0
, we have that

\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}\leqslant-\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+\frac{2\gamma\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}.

Proof.

For any bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}\geqslant 0
, we have that

\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}\leqslant\max\left\{+\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{n},-\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}\right\}=-\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma}{n}\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}+\varepsilon\right)\leqslant-\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{2\gamma\varepsilon}{n},

using that
\delta\leqslant 1
.

Similarly, for any bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}<0
, we have that

\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}\leqslant\max\left\{+\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n},-\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{n}\right\}=-\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma}{n}\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}\right)\leqslant-\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\gamma\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{2\gamma\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n},

using that
\varepsilon\delta\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{1-\delta}=\widetilde{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Proof of
3.3
.

Fix a labeling of the bins so that they are sorted non-increasingly according to their load in
x

are at least as large for
r
than for
p
.
Hence, from now on, we will be working with

Recall that we partition overloaded bins
i

Lemma 5.5

Consider any allocation process and let

.

\Phi:=\Phi(\gamma)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

In this section, we prove an upper bound on the difference between the maximum and minimum load for a variety of processes. We primarily make use of

6.1
The
(1+\beta)
-process

In this section, we improve the upper bound on the gap for the
(1+\beta)
-process from previous work for very small
\beta
in the unit weights setting. In
[
35

, it was shown that this gap is
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta+\log(1/\beta)/\beta)

\beta=n^{-\omega(1)}
, the second term dominates. We improve this gap bound to
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta)
, which by
[
35
, Section 4]
is tight for any
\beta
being bounded away from
1
(
6.2
).

Theorem 6.1
.

Consider the
(1+\beta)
-process for any
\beta\in(0,1]
in the
Weighted
setting with weights from a
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(S)
distribution with
S\geqslant 1
. Then, there exists a constant
\kappa>0
, such that for any step
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}\left|y_{i}^{m}\right|\leqslant\kappa\cdot S\cdot\frac{\log n}{\beta}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

By
2.4
, the
(1+\beta)
-process satisfies conditions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for
\varepsilon:=\frac{\beta}{2}
and
\delta:=\frac{1}{4}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
for
C:=2
. Hence, by
5.2
, there exists a constant
c:=c(\delta)>0
, such that for
\Gamma:=\Gamma(\gamma)
with
\gamma:=\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{16CS}=\frac{\beta}{256S}
, for any step
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n.

Hence, using Markov’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n^{3}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

When the event
\big\{\Gamma^{m}\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n^{3}\big\}
holds, we deduce the desired bound on the gap

\Gap(m)\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot\left(\log\left(\frac{8c}{\delta}\right)+3\cdot\log n\right)\leqslant\frac{4}{\gamma}\cdot\log n=4\cdot\frac{256S}{\beta}\cdot\log n=\mathcal{O}\left(S\cdot\frac{\log n}{\beta}\right).\qed

Remark 6.2
.

For the unit weights setting, this upper bound is tight up to multiplicative constants for any
\beta\leqslant 1-\xi
(for any constant
\xi\in(0,1)
), due to a lower bound of
\Omega(\log n/\beta)
shown in
[
35
, Section 4]
which holds with probability at least

.

Remark 6.3
.

More generally, the fact that
\Gamma:=\Gamma(\gamma)
with
\gamma=\Theta(\beta)
is

in expectation, allows us to immediately deduce that with constant probability the number of bins with normalized load at least

(or load at most

[

]
and by

and

, there is also a matching lower bound of

for sufficiently large

.

\delta:=1/2
,
\varepsilon:=\phi
and condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
with
C:=2
.

The proof of this lemma closely follows

, Proof of Theorem 3.2]

Proof.

Fix any load vector
x^{t}
in step
t
. Consider any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n/2
. Let
S_{k}
be the set of the
k
bins with the largest load. Hence in order to allocate a ball into
S_{k}
, both endpoints of the sampled edge must be in
S_{k}
, and so

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot|E(S_{k},S_{k})|}{2\cdot|E|}\leqslant\frac{d\cdot k-\phi\cdot d\cdot k}{d\cdot n}=(1-\phi)\cdot\frac{k}{n},

where the inequality used that
d\cdot|S_{k}|=|E(S_{k},V\setminus S_{k})|+2\cdot|E(S_{k},S_{k})|
and the definition of the conductance
\phi
.
Now, we will consider the suffix sums for
n/2+1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. We start by upper bounding the prefix sum up to
k-1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{d\cdot(k-1)-\phi\cdot d\cdot(n-k+1)}{d\cdot n}=\frac{(k-1)-\phi\cdot(n-k+1)}{n},

where we proceeded as in the bound above and used the definition of
\varphi
.
Therefore, we lower bound the suffix sum by

\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}^{t}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1-\frac{(k-1)-\phi\cdot(n-k+1)}{n}=(1+\phi)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}.

Hence,
p^{t}
satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
\varepsilon:=\phi
.
Finally, we also know that
p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{d}{d\cdot n/2}=\frac{2}{n},

for any bin
i\in[n]
, since in the worst-case we allocate a ball to bin

whenever one of its

incident edges are chosen.
∎

\gamma:=\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8K}=\frac{\varepsilon\delta n}{40C^{2}b}
, we get w.h.p. the
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\log n\big)
bound on the difference between maximum and minimum load.
∎

7
Lower Bounds

In this section, we prove a general lower bound that applies to any process that allocates at least one ball (of unit weight) with uniform probability in each round (which could consist of multiple steps). This implies an
\Omega(\log n)
bound for the gap of
\textsc{Quantile-Twinning}(\delta)
,
\textsc{Quantile-With-Penalty}(\delta)
and
Reset-Memory
for any
\delta=\Theta(1)
.

In the following lemma, we prove a lower bound for general allocation processes which allocate a constant number of balls in each round and at least one ball with uniform probability across the bins.

Lemma 7.1
.

Consider an allocation process
for which there are constants
c>0
and
c_{max}>0
such that in each round
t\geqslant 0
, the process allocates at least one ball with uniform probability
q\geqslant c/n
over all bins, and at most
c_{\max}
balls in total. Then, there exists a round
m:=m(c,c_{\max})
and constant
\kappa:=\kappa(c,c_{\max})>0
such that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\kappa\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

Proof.

Let
m:=Cn\log n
, where
C:=\frac{c}{400\cdot e\cdot c_{\max}^{2}}
. By a Chernoff bound (
A.5
), w.h.p. in these
m
steps at least
\frac{c}{e}\cdot m
balls are being allocated using a
One-Choice
process.

By, e.g.
[
35
, Section 4]
, when
rn\log n
balls are allocated using
One-Choice
, for any constant
r>0
, then with probability at least
1-n^{-2}
, the maximum load is at least
(r+\frac{1}{10}\sqrt{r})\log n
. Using this with
r:=\frac{c^{2}}{400\cdot e^{2}\cdot c_{\max}^{2}}
and applying the union bound, we conclude that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{m}\geqslant\left(\frac{c^{2}}{400\cdot e^{2}\cdot c_{\max}^{2}}+\frac{1}{10}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{400\cdot e^{2}\cdot c_{\max}^{2}}}\right)\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

Further, at step
m
the average load can be bounded by

\frac{W^{m}}{n}\leqslant\frac{m\cdot c_{\max}}{n}=C\cdot c_{\max}\cdot\log n=\frac{c}{400\cdot e\cdot c_{\max}}\cdot\log n=\frac{1}{20}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{400\cdot e^{2}\cdot c_{\max}^{2}}}\cdot\log n.

Therefore,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant\frac{c}{400\cdot e\cdot c_{\max}}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.\qed

The
\textsc{Quantile-Twinning}(\delta)
process has a uniform probability of
\delta/n
to allocate (at least) one ball over all bins and allocates at most two balls per step, so by

, the fact that the hyperbolic cosine potential is

in expectation gives an
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot e^{-\Omega(z)})
bound on the number of bins with normalized load at least
+z
. In the following, we show that these bounds are asymptotically tight for all values of
z\in(0,\mathcal{O}(\log n))
for the
(1+\beta)
-process with constant
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
with constant
\delta\in(0,1)

Lemma 7.7
.

Consider any allocation process
for which there are constants
c\in(0,1]
and
c_{max}\geqslant 1
such that in each round
t\geqslant 0
, the process allocates at least one ball with uniform probability
q\geqslant c/n
over all bins, and at most
c_{\max}
balls in total. Then, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any
z\in(0,c_{1}\log n)
and
m:=n\cdot z
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Bigl|i\in[n]:y_{i}^{m}\geqslant z\Bigr|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}n\cdot e^{-c_{2}z}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-1/8}.

Proof.

We define a coupling between the allocations of the process and a
One-Choice
process. In each step
t\geqslant 0
:

•

With probability
c
, we allocate following the
One-Choice
process (i.e., we allocate to a bin chosen uniformly at random).

•

Otherwise, we allocate to a bin
i\in[n]
chosen with probability
\frac{p_{i}^{t}-c/n}{1-c}
.

We will show that just the
One-Choice
allocations are enough to produce sufficiently many bins with load at least
c_{\max}\cdot\frac{m}{n}+z
and this will imply the statement as the average can change by at most
c_{\max}/n
in each step.

We will use the Poisson Approximation
[
34
, Chapter 5]
. To this end, let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}
be
n
independent Poisson random variables with parameter
\lambda=\frac{m^{\prime}}{n}=\frac{c}{2}\cdot\frac{m}{n}=\frac{c}{2}z
. We will now use the following lower bound for each Poisson random variable
Z_{i}\sim\mathsf{Poi}(\lambda)
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z_{i}\geqslant\lambda+u\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z_{i}=\lambda+u\,\right]=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\cdot\lambda^{\lambda+u}}{(\lambda+u)!}\geqslant\frac{e^{-\lambda}\cdot\lambda^{\lambda+u}}{e\cdot(\lambda+u)\cdot\big(\frac{\lambda+u}{e}\big)^{\lambda+u}},

using that
v!\leqslant e\cdot v\cdot\big(\frac{v}{e}\big)^{v}
. We choose
u
such that

\lambda+u=c_{\max}\cdot\frac{m}{n}+z\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad u=\left(c_{\max}+1-\frac{c}{2}\right)\cdot z.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z_{i}\geqslant\lambda+u\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\left(-\lambda+(\lambda+u)\cdot\left(1+\log\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+u}\right)-1-\log(\lambda+u)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geqslant}}\exp\left(-\lambda+(\lambda+u)\cdot\left(1-\frac{u}{\lambda}\right)-1-\log(\lambda+u)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{\lambda}-1-\log(\lambda+u)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geqslant}}\exp(-c_{2}z),

where in
(a)
we used the Taylor estimate
\log\frac{1}{1+v}\geqslant-v
(since
e^{v}\geqslant 1+v
), and
(b)
holds for some constant
c_{2}>0
, using that
z\geqslant c_{3}
for some sufficiently large constant
c_{3}>0
. For
z\in(0,c_{3})
, this follows trivially by considering the bins with normalized load at least
c_{3}
(and a smaller
c_{2}
).

Therefore, by applying a standard Chernoff bound with
\delta:=\frac{1}{2}
, we have that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Bigl|i\in[n]:Z_{i}\geqslant c_{\max}\cdot\frac{m}{n}+z\Bigr|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}n\cdot e^{-c_{2}z}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\frac{1}{8}\cdot n\cdot e^{-c_{2}z}}\geqslant 1-o(1),

using that
z<c_{1}\log n
for sufficiently small constant
c_{1}>0
. Let
M
be the number of balls allocated with
One-Choice
, then by the previous inequality we obtain that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\Bigl|i\in[n]:y_{i}^{m}\geqslant z\Bigr|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}n\cdot e^{-c_{2}z}\,\right|\,M\geqslant m^{\prime}\,\right]\geqslant 1-o(1),

By a Chernoff bound with
\delta:=\frac{1}{2}
we have that the number of balls
M
allocated using
One-Choice
is

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,M\geqslant m^{\prime}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,M\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot c\cdot m\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-1/8}.

By combining the last two inequalities, we get the conclusion.
∎

Similarly, we prove tight bounds for the number of bins with an underload of at least
-z
for
z\in(0,\mathcal{O}(\log n))
.

Lemma 7.8
.

Consider any allocation process which in each round allocates to a subset of a constant number of
d
bins sampled uniformly at random and allocates on aggregate at least one unit weight. Then, for any
z\in(0,\frac{1}{8d}\log n)
and
m:=n\cdot z
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Bigl|i\in[n]:y_{i}^{m}\leqslant-z\Bigr|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}n\cdot e^{-2dz}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-1}.

Proof.

Consider any fixed bin
i\in[n]
. If this bin is not sampled in any of the first
m
steps, then we have that

y_{i}^{m}\leqslant-\frac{m}{n}=-z,

since in each step we are allocating at least one unit weight ball. This occurs with probability

p:=\left(1-\frac{d}{n}\right)^{m}\geqslant\left(e^{-\frac{d}{n}-\frac{d^{2}}{n^{2}}}\right)^{m}\geqslant\left(e^{-2\frac{d}{n}}\right)^{m}=e^{-2d\cdot\frac{m}{n}},

using that
1-x\geqslant e^{-x-x^{2}}
for any
x\in(0,0.5)
.
Let
f:=f(Z_{11},\ldots,Z_{1d},\ldots,Z_{m1},\ldots,Z_{md})
be the number of bins that were not sampled in the first
m
steps. So,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,f\,\right]=n\cdot p\geqslant n\cdot e^{-2d\cdot\frac{m}{n}}.

Then, changing any one of the
md
samples can change
f
by at most one, so for any
(i,j)\in[m]\times[d]
,

\max_{z_{1,1},\ldots,z_{m,d}\in[n]}\max_{z_{i,j},z_{i,j}^{\prime}\in[n]}\left|f(Z_{1,1},\ldots,z_{i,j},\ldots,Z_{m,d})-f(Z_{1,1},\ldots,z_{i,j}^{\prime},\ldots,Z_{m,d})\right|\leqslant 1.

Therefore, applying the method of bounded differences (
A.6
) gives

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Bigl|i\in[n]:y_{i}^{m}\leqslant-z\Bigr|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}n\cdot e^{-2dz}\,\right]\geqslant\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}\cdot e^{-4dz}}{2n}\right)\geqslant 1-n^{-1},

using that
z<\frac{1}{8d}\log n
.
∎

8
Conclusions

In this work we improved the drift theorem for balanced allocation processes from
[
35
]
. This allowed us to obtain new bounds for a wider variety of processes and improve bounds on existing processes.

We believe this drift theorem will be helpful in analyzing other allocation processes and possibly even more general settings that go beyond balls-and-bins. One specific open problem is to establish tight bounds for
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
with non-constant
\delta\in(0,1)
. In particular, we think there exist choices of
\delta
for which the gap would match the general lower bound of
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
, which holds for any
Two-Thinning
process. Finally, it is also open to establish tight bounds for the
k
-Quantile
process
[
27
]
with
k>1
and
d\textsc{-Thinning}
with
d>2
. Another avenue for future work is to study allocation processes where balls can be removed or the weights of the balls are chosen by an adversary (instead of being sampled randomly).

A.1
Auxiliary Probabilistic Claims

For convenience, we add the following well-known inequality for a sequence of random variables, whose expectations are related through a recurrence inequality.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider any sequence of random variables
(X^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
for which there exist
a\in(0,1)
and
b>0
, such that every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant X^{i-1}\cdot a+b.

Then, if
X^{0}\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}
holds, then for every
i\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}.

Proof.

We will prove this claim by induction. Then, assuming that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}
holds for
i\geqslant 0
, we have for
i+1

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{i}\,\right]\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\,\right]\cdot a+b\leqslant\frac{b}{1-a}\cdot a+b=\frac{b}{1-a}.\qed

We give a proof for the well-known fact that when
\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,\right]=\Theta(1)
then
\mathbf{E}[\,\mathcal{W}^{4}\,]
is also bounded.

Lemma A.2
.

Consider any non-negative random variable
\mathcal{W}
with
\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,\right]<\infty
for some
\zeta>0
. then

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathcal{W}^{4}\,\right]<\left(\left(\frac{8}{\zeta}\right)\cdot\log\left(\frac{8}{\zeta}\right)\right)^{4}+\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,\right].

Proof.

Let
\kappa:=(8/\zeta)\cdot\log(8/\zeta)
. Consider any
x\geqslant\max(0,\kappa)=:\kappa^{*}
. Then

e^{\zeta x/4}=e^{\zeta x/8}\cdot e^{\zeta x/8}\geqslant e^{\log(8/\zeta)}\cdot e^{\zeta x/8}\geqslant\frac{8}{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\zeta x}{8}=x,

using that
e^{v}\geqslant v
for any
v
. Hence,

e^{\zeta x}=(e^{\zeta x/4})^{4}\geqslant x^{4}.

Hence, if
p_{x}
is the pdf of
\mathcal{W}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathcal{W}^{4}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}x^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx=\int_{x=0}^{\kappa^{*}}x^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx+\int_{x=\kappa^{*}}^{\infty}x^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{x=0}^{\kappa^{*}}\kappa^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx+\int_{x=\kappa^{*}}^{\infty}e^{\zeta x}\cdot p_{x}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa^{4}\cdot\int_{x=0}^{\infty}p_{x}dx+\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\zeta x}\cdot p_{x}dx=\kappa^{4}+\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,\right].\qed
