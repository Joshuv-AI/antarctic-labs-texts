Unification of Random Dynamical Decoupling and the Quantum Zeno Effect
Alexander Hahn Daniel Burgarth Kazuya Yuasa

Abstract
Periodic deterministic bang-bang dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect are known to emerge from the same physical mechanism. Both concepts are based on cycles of strong and frequent kicks provoking a subdivision of the Hilbert space into independent subspaces. However, previous unification results do not capture the case of random bang-bang dynamical decoupling, which can be advantageous to the deterministic case but has an inherently acyclic structure. Here, we establish a correspondence between random dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect by investigating the average over random decoupling evolutions. This protocol is a manifestation of the quantum Zeno dynamics and leads to a unitary bath evolution. By providing a framework that we call equitability of system and bath , we show that the system dynamics under random dynamical decoupling converges to a unitary with a decoupling error that characteristically depends on the convergence speed of the Zeno limit. This reveals a unification of the random dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect.



1
Introduction

The inherent interaction of a quantum system with a surrounding environment causes loss of its initial coherences over time
[
1
]
. In particular, in the contemporary NISQ era
[
2
]
, such environmental noise is a major obstacle in engineering quantum devices. A prosperous technique to overcome this hurdle is to remove undesired couplings by applying strong controls to the system. This procedure falls under the name of
dynamical decoupling
(see e.g. Refs.
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
) and historically originates from the field of NMR
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Its intuitive idea is to perform cycles of frequent and strong unitary kicks, e.g. laser pulses, on the system with a high repetition rate. If the time intervals between the kicks are shorter than the system-bath interaction time, transitions among the system and environmental subspaces are suppressed due to a rotational average effect
[
11
]
. In turn, one obtains a decoupled evolution generated by an effective projected (Zeno) Hamiltonian
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Likewise, the very same idea underlies the
quantum Zeno effect

[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, where projective measurements are performed on the system frequently. This leads to an effective Zeno dynamics, which takes place independently in the subspaces specified by the applied projections
[
21
]
.

The apparent connection between the dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect suggests the existence of a framework unifying both concepts. Indeed, such a consolidation has been initiated in Ref.
[
12
]
via von Neumann’s ergodic theorem
[
22
, Theorem II.11]
. This result has been completed and generalized more recently in Ref.
[
14
]
through an adiabatic theorem
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
, where it is shown that a quantum Zeno dynamics is obtained by repeatedly kicking the system with a cycle of general quantum operations. In the context of dynamical decoupling, this situation is described by
periodic deterministic decoupling
(PDD). See Refs.
[
27
,
28
]
for an overview of different decoupling schemes. However, interestingly, for larger system dimensions or longer evolution times, randomized decoupling schemes seem to be more efficient in suppressing errors than their deterministic counterparts
[
29
,
30
,
27
,
28
]
. In its most elementary form, random dynamical decoupling is achieved by drawing the unitary kicks at random from some group at each time step. We make this notion of so-called
naive random decoupling
(NRD)
[
29
,
30
,
27
,
28
]
rigorous later. For now, we emphasize that such a scheme is intrinsically acyclic
[
29
]
. Therefore, the previous unifications of dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect
[
12
,
14
]
do not directly apply to the NRD case. This raises the question whether a similar consideration is still possible for random dynamical decoupling. The goal of this paper is to answer this question by using a symmetry between the system and environmental dynamics, that we call
equitability of system and bath
and that provides a framework for unifying random dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect.

The idea is to study the
average evolution
under the random dynamical decoupling, which can be described by the Zeno dynamics, where one frequently projects the system onto the maximally mixed state. Unfortunately, by this procedure one loses all information about the initial system state making it rather unsuitable for quantum control purposes. Rather, the goal of dynamical decoupling is to maintain the initial state. However, one can still learn something about random dynamical decoupling when investigating the Zeno dynamics induced by its average evolution. Namely, by removing the interaction Hamiltonian, the latter leads to an environmental unitary dynamics.
As a consequence, the environmental evolution under non-averaged random dynamical decoupling has to be close to the unitary with a high probability. This statement can be lifted to the system evolution by a Schmidt-decomposition of the Choi-Jamipłkowski state of the total evolution. By this virtue, explicit quantitative error bounds for the random dynamical decoupling are directly obtained from bounding the Zeno error. This shows that the decoupling efficiency of the random dynamical decoupling can be written in terms of the Zeno convergence of its average evolution, which manifests an interesting connection between the random dynamical decoupling and the quantum Zeno effect that we want to explore here in detail.

. It includes a recap on completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps in Sec.

as well as on randomized dynamical decoupling in Sec.

. Section

4.1
. We then apply this bound to the case of the average dynamical decoupling protocol to bound the convergence of its environmental evolution in Sec.
4.2
. As we wish to shift our discourse from CPTP maps to Choi-Jamiołkowski states, we add a discussion about reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski states in Sec.
5
. Finally, in Sec.
6
, we prove the main theorems presented in Sec.
3
, and conclude this paper in Sec.
7
. Appendix
A
contains some basic lemmas. The models we use in numerical simulations are specified in Appendix
B

2
Mathematical Definitions and Notation

In this section, we introduce the notation we will use throughout the paper.
We also define what we mean by random dynamical decoupling both in the trajectory and in the average picture.
Let us start with some preliminaries on
completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps
.

2.1
Preliminaries on CPTP Maps

Dynamical decoupling protocols are described by CPTP maps, also known as
quantum channels

[
31
,
32
,
33
]
.
For completeness and notation, we start by recapitulating some basic properties of CPTP maps.
This subsection can be skipped by experienced readers.

Recall that a CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
sends density operators to density operators.
Its action on an input state
\rho
can be written in the
Kraus representation

\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\sum_{k}E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger},

(2.1)

where the Kraus operators
E_{k}
satisfy
\sum_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}=\mathbb{1}
.
Now, in a fixed basis
\{|{e_{i}}\rangle\}_{i=1,\ldots,d}
of a
d
-dimensional system, a density operator
\rho
has a matrix representation

\rho=\sum_{i,j}r_{ij}|{e_{i}}\rangle\langle{e_{j}}|.

(2.2)

By concatenating each row of this matrix, we construct an equivalent vector
|{\rho})
in a
d^{2}
-dimensional vector space as

|{\rho})=\sum_{i,j}r_{ij}|{e_{i}}\rangle\otimes|{e_{j}}\rangle.

(2.3)

This procedure falls under the name of
row-vectorization

[
34
,
35
,
33
]
and is a basis-dependent linear isomorphism
\rho\mapsto|{\rho})
.
It is closely related to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of operators as

(A|B)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{\dagger}B).

(2.4)

The row-vectorization
A\mapsto|{A})
can also be done by

|{A})=(A\otimes\mathbb{1})|\mathbb{1}),

(2.5)

where
|\mathbb{1})=\sum_{i}|{e_{i}}\rangle\otimes|{e_{i}}\rangle
is a maximally entangled state up to normalization, defined on the given basis
\{|{e_{i}}\rangle\}
.
In this framework, a CPTP map
\mathcal{T}:\rho\mapsto\rho^{\prime}
is a
d^{2}\times d^{2}
matrix
\hat{\mathcal{T}}:|{\rho})\mapsto|{\rho^{\prime}})
, acting on vectorized density matrices.
The action of CPTP maps then just becomes matrix multiplication. This matrix
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
is a
matrix representation
of the CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
.
In the following, we denote the matrix representation corresponding to a CPTP map with a hat.
In order to handle vectorized operators, there is a useful theorem called Roth’s lemma
[
36
]
: for
d\times d
matrices
A
,
B
, and
C
, we have

|{ABC})=(A\otimes C^{\intercal})|{B}),

(2.6)

where
⊺
denotes the transposition of a matrix with respect to the given basis.
Using Roth’s lemma and the linearity of the vectorization, we get a matrix representation
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
of the CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
in terms of the Kraus operators,

\hat{\mathcal{T}}=\sum_{k}E_{k}\otimes\overline{E_{k}},

(2.7)

where the bar denotes the entry-wise complex conjugation in the given basis.
If
\mathcal{T}
is unitary, the matrix representation (
2.7
) reduces to

\hat{\mathcal{T}}=U\otimes\overline{U},

(2.8)

with a unitary
U
.

There is another useful representation of CPTP maps, namely the
Choi-Jamiołkowski state

\Lambda
defined by

\Lambda=(\mathcal{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})\!\left(\frac{1}{d}|\mathbb{1})(\mathbb{1}|\right),

(2.9)

where
\mathbb{I}
represents the identity map.
It is normalized as
\operatorname{tr}\Lambda=1
for a TP map
\mathcal{T}
, and the map
\mathcal{T}
is CP iff
\Lambda\geq 0
.
Let us consider the spectral decomposition of the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda
of a CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
,

\Lambda=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}|v_{k})(v_{k}|.

(2.10)

We have
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
,
\forall k
, and
\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}=1
.
The eigenvectors
|v_{k})
are orthonormalized as
(v_{k}|v_{\ell})=\delta_{k\ell}
, and provide the Kraus operators

E_{k}=\sqrt{d\lambda_{k}}\,v_{k}

(2.11)

of a Kraus representation (
2.1
) of the given CPTP map
\mathcal{T}

[
33
, Proposition 2.20]
.
While the Kraus representation is not unique and the set of Kraus operators given by (
2.11
) is not the only choice, this will be a convenient pick for our analysis in the following.
If only one of the
d^{2}
eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
of
\Lambda
is nonvanishing, namely, if the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda
is a pure state, the map
\mathcal{T}
is unitary.

We will be interested in comparing different CPTP maps.
For instance, we will wish to estimate the distance of a CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
from a unitary evolution in the Zeno limit.
In order to be able to talk about the distance between CPTP maps, we will need some distance measures, which can be defined via norms of maps.
We will use Schatten
p
-norms defined for matrices
A
by

\|A\|_{p}=\left(\operatorname{tr}[(A^{\dagger}A)^{\frac{p}{2}}]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

(2.12)

where
\dagger
denotes the adjoint with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product.
A useful norm for our purposes will be the Frobenius norm

\|{A}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^{\dagger}A)}.

(2.13)

We will also use the operator norm
\|A\|_{\infty}
,
which gives the largest singular value of
A
.
For density operators, the trace norm

\|A\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}=\operatorname{tr}|A|

(2.14)

is useful.
Note that in finite-dimensional vector spaces all norms are equivalent
[
37
]
.
In particular, we will use the following norm equivalences
[
33
, Eqs. (1.168) and (1.169)]
for the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda
, which is a
d^{2}\times d^{2}
matrix for a CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
acting on a
d
-dimensional system,

\displaystyle\|{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\Lambda}\|_{2}\leq d\|{\Lambda}\|_{\infty},

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Lambda\|_{1}\leq d^{2}\|\Lambda\|_{\infty},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|\Lambda\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Lambda\|_{1}\leq d\|\Lambda\|_{2}.

(2.17)

The operator norm
\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\infty}
of a matrix representation
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
of a map
\mathcal{T}
can also be induced by the Frobenius norm on the vector space
\mathscr{A}
of matrices on which the map
\mathcal{T}
acts,

\|{\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\|_{\infty}=\|\mathcal{T}\|_{2\to 2},\qquad\|\mathcal{T}\|_{p\to q}=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}\frac{\|\mathcal{T}(A)\|_{q}}{\|A\|_{p}}.

(2.18)

To bound the convergence to a unitary via dynamical decoupling, the Frobenius norm
\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}\|_{2}
will be a natural one, since it is easily related to the purity
\mathtt{P}(\Lambda)=\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda^{2})
of the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda
, which is a measure of the unitarity of the corresponding map
\mathcal{T}
.
Indeed, the Frobenius norm of the Kraus representation (
2.7
) yields

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{k}E_{k}\otimes\overline{E_{k}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\!\left[\left(\sum_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}\otimes\overline{E_{k}}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell}E_{\ell}\otimes\overline{E_{\ell}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}|(E_{k}|E_{\ell})|^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\sum_{k,\ell}\lambda_{k}\lambda_{\ell}|(v_{k}|v_{\ell})|^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\mathtt{P}(\Lambda),

(2.19)

where we have used the Kraus operators (
2.11
) and the orthonormality
(v_{k}|v_{\ell})=\delta_{k\ell}
of the eigenvectors
|v_{k})
of
\Lambda
in (
2.10
).
To bound the Zeno limit, the operator norm
\|{\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\|_{\infty}
will prove useful, as we will have to deal with Hermitian projection operators, whose operator norms are simply
1
. Even though this property also holds for the
1\rightarrow 1
norm
\|{\mathcal{T}}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}
, which is induced by the trace norm, the operator norm has the advantage that it directly relates to the Frobenius norm via (
2.18
). This makes it more attractive for a Zeno bound in achieving the goal of unifying the Zeno dynamics with random dynamical decoupling.
In the literature, a commonly used norm of maps is the the diamond norm
[
33
, Sec. 3.3]

\|{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{\|A\|_{1}=1}\|(\mathcal{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})(A)\|_{1},

(2.20)

which is stable under tensoring additional subsystems. We will also convert our error bounds to this norm using a norm equivalence established in Lemma
26
in Appendix
A
.

2.2
Random Dynamical Decoupling

In this subsection, we define the protocols of dynamical decoupling introduced in Sec.
1
.

Consider a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2}
with
d_{1}=\dim\mathscr{H}_{1}<\infty
and
d_{2}=\dim\mathscr{H}_{2}<\infty
. We model an open quantum system with the Hamiltonian

H=H_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2}+\mathbb{1}_{1}\otimes H_{2}+H_{12},

(2.21)

where
H_{1}
is the Hamiltonian of system 1 (“system”),
H_{2}
describes system 2 (“environment” or “bath”),
and
H_{12}=\sum_{i}w_{i}h_{1}^{(i)}\otimes h_{2}^{(i)}
is the interaction, i.e.,
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
,
h_{1}^{(i)}
, and
h_{2}^{(i)}
are Hermitian operators, and
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Without loss of generality, we neglect global phases and furthermore set
\operatorname{tr}H_{1}=\operatorname{tr}H_{2}=\operatorname{tr}h_{1}^{(i)}=\operatorname{tr}h_{2}^{(i)}=0
,
\forall i
.
Using an operator version of the Schmidt decomposition
[
38
]
, such decomposition is always possible for finite-dimensional systems, and the operators involved in the decomposition are orthogonal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
The total evolution under this Hamiltonian
H
is then given by a unitary
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\mathcal{H}}
with
\mathcal{H}=[H,{}\bullet{}]
.
In the following, we will use calligraphic symbols to denote maps acting on operators on the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
.

The goal of a dynamical decoupling protocol is to quickly rotate
\mathscr{H}_{1}
in order to average out the system-bath interaction
H_{12}
. In the standard periodic deterministic dynamical decoupling protocol
[
11
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
, we apply unitaries on system 1 in a fixed order. The unitaries are taken from an irreducible representation
\mathscr{V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{|\mathscr{V}|}\}
of a finite group, and the total evolution is given by

\mathcal{E}_{m}(t)=\left((\mathcal{V}_{|\mathscr{V}|}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{m|\mathscr{V}|}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{|\mathscr{V}|})\cdots(\mathcal{V}_{2}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{m|\mathscr{V}|}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{2})(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{m|\mathscr{V}|}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{1})\right)^{m},\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

(2.22)

where
\mathcal{V}_{i}=(V_{i}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2}){}\bullet{}(V_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2})
with
\mathbb{1}_{2}
the identity operator on
\mathscr{H}_{2}
. Observe that this equation is written in terms of quantum channels, which will provide a unified picture with random dynamical decoupling.

In the random dynamical decoupling protocol, on the other hand, we choose a unitary
V_{i}^{(j)}
randomly (i.i.d.) from
\mathscr{V}
for each step and proceed as

\mathcal{E}^{(j)}_{n}(t)=\mathcal{V}_{n+1}^{(j)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(j)}\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{2}^{(j)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{(j)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

(2.23)

where
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{(j)}=(V_{i}^{(j)}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2}){}\bullet{}(V_{i}^{(j){\dagger}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2})
.
A sequence of
(n+1)
randomly sampled unitaries

\{V_{1}^{(j)},\ldots,V_{n+1}^{(j)}\}

characterizes a “trajectory,” and there are
|\mathscr{V}|^{n+1}
different trajectories, which are labeled by
j=1,\ldots,|\mathscr{V}|^{n+1}
.
Each of the
|\mathscr{V}|^{n+1}
trajectories is realized by the sequence of random i.i.d. samplings.

If we do not know which random unitaries
V_{i}^{(j)}
are drawn from the unitary group
\mathscr{V}
, a good description of the protocol is provided by the average picture.
It is obtained by averaging the trajectory protocols
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
over all possible trajectory realizations.
The channel representing the average picture is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathscr{V}|^{n+1}}\sum_{V_{n+1}^{(j)}\in\mathscr{V}}\sum_{V_{n}^{(j)}\in\mathscr{V}}\cdots\sum_{V_{1}^{(j)}\in\mathscr{V}}\mathcal{V}_{n+1}^{(j)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{n}^{(j)}\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{V}_{1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{D}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{D})^{n},

(2.24)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the group average

\mathcal{D}(A)=\frac{1}{|\mathscr{V}|}\sum_{V\in\mathscr{V}}(V\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2})A(V^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2})=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\mathbb{1}_{1}\otimes(\operatorname{tr}_{1}A)

(2.25)

over system 1. By irreducibility of
\mathscr{V}
, it projects
system 1 onto the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{1}_{1}/d_{1}.

Note that
\mathcal{D}
is CPTP
[
32
]
and, furthermore, a Hermitian projection, as is clear from its matrix representation

\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}|{\mathbb{1}_{1}})({\mathbb{1}_{1}}|\otimes\hat{\mathbb{I}}_{22^{\prime}},

(2.26)

where
|\mathbb{1}_{1})
is the row-vectorization of the identity operator
\mathbb{1}_{1}
on
\mathscr{H}_{1}
to an extended Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{1^{\prime}}
of system 1, while
\hat{\mathbb{I}}_{22^{\prime}}
is the identity on another extended Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{2}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2^{\prime}}
of system 2.
The average channel
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)
in (
2.24
) is formally equivalent to the standard quantum Zeno dynamics by frequent projective measurements
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
That is why we will estimate an error bound on the quantum Zeno limit, which immediately gives a bound on the convergence of
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This bound can then be used to bound the convergence of the trajectory protocol
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
in the decoupling limit
n\to\infty
.

3
Main Results

Our main objective is to establish an explicit connection between the quantum Zeno dynamics and the decoupled dynamics by a random dynamical decoupling sequence. As we have seen above, the average dynamical decoupling protocol is a manifestation of the quantum Zeno procedure. We will show that the decoupling efficiency of the random dynamical decoupling protocol
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
is determined by the convergence speed of the average protocol
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)
, which is ruled by the Zeno limit.
We hence first provide an explicit bound on the quantum Zeno limit via frequent projective measurements.
The Zeno limit of the average protocol
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)
yields quantum Zeno dynamics, in which system 2 evolves unitarily.
The fact that the evolution of system 2 converges to a unitary on average implies that almost all trajectory evolutions of system 2 in the trajectory picture
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
also converge to the unitary in the decoupling limit.
Since the total evolution
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
in the trajectory picture is unitary, this further implies that the evolution of system 1 in the trajectory picture also converges to a unitary, and the evolutions of systems 1 and 2 are decoupled.

To prove these statements in mathematically rigorous ways, we look at the Choi-Jamiołkowski states of the evolutions.
In this section, we present and discuss main theorems, but defer their proofs to later sections.
Let
\Lambda^{(j)}(n)
be the Choi-Jamiołkowski state of the random trajectory dynamical decoupling protocol
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
defined in (
2.23
), i.e.,

\Lambda^{(j)}(n)=[\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)\otimes\mathbb{I}_{1^{\prime}2^{\prime}}]\!\left(\frac{1}{d}|\mathbb{1}_{12})(\mathbb{1}_{12}|\right),

(3.1)

where
d=d_{1}d_{2}
is the dimension of the total system.
By linearity, the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}(n)
of the random dynamical decoupling protocol in the average picture
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)
is obtained by taking the average of the Choi-Jamiołkowski states
\Lambda^{(j)}(n)
of the trajectory picture over all trajectories,

\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}(n)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\Lambda^{(j)}(n)].

(3.2)

Taking the trace over the doubled Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{1^{\prime}}
of system 1, we get a reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state for system 2,

\Lambda_{2}^{\mathrm{av}}(n)=\operatorname{tr}_{11^{\prime}}\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}(n)=\Lambda_{2,\mathbb{1}_{1}/d_{1}}^{\mathrm{av}}(n).

(3.3)

This is the Choi-Jamiołkowski state of the reduced dynamics
\mathcal{E}_{2,\mathbb{1}_{1}/d_{1}}^{\mathrm{av}}(n)
of
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathrm{av}}(t)
for system 2 when the initial state of system 1 is the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{1}_{1}/d_{1}
(see Sec.
5
).

3.1
Convergence of the Average Evolution of System 2

We first show that the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda_{2}^{\mathrm{av}}(n)
converges to a pure state, i.e., the corresponding reduced dynamics of system 2 converges to a unitary in the decoupling limit
n\to\infty
.
To bound the convergence, we prove the following theorem on the standard Zeno limit via frequent projective measurements.

Theorem 1

(Explicit error bound on the quantum Zeno limit)
.

Let
H=H^{\dagger}
be a Hermitian operator and let
P=P^{2}=P^{\dagger}
be a Hermitian projection.
Then, for any
t\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\|(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{t}{n}\|H\|_{\infty}+\frac{t^{2}}{n}\|H\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(3.4)

Proof.

This is proved in Sec.
4.1
.
∎

Notice that the bound in Theorem
1
shows an
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
behavior for
n\rightarrow\infty
, which is consistent with numerical simulations. See Fig.
1
. It takes a particularly simple form and therefore has a few advantages over previously known error bounds on the quantum Zeno effect. For instance, in comparison to Ref.
[
42
]
, this bound looks much simpler. The bound in Ref.
[
43
, Theorem 1]
only shows an
\mathcal{O}(n^{-2/3})
scaling. Reference
[
44
, Lemma 4]
studies an error bound in a more general setting, which still depends on an undetermined constant. This result has recently been improved in Ref.
[
45
, Theorem 3.1]

In summary, all decoupling bounds presented here are ruled by the Zeno bound derived in Theorem
1
. This shows a deep connection between the quantum Zeno effect and the random dynamical decoupling through a phenomenon that we call
equitability of system and bath
. This is, by using the fact that the bath evolution (system 2) is close to unitary, we infer that the system evolution (system 1) is so as well. Hence, there is no preferred subsystem for quantum control and both
\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2}
can be treated equitable. Physically, this idea is based on the fact that we only need to remove the interaction term in the Hamiltonian to achieve decoupling. If that happens
both
subsystems evolve unitarily. Mathematically, the approach is justified by the fact that the total evolution
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
is unitary even in the presence of controls. Thus, its Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda^{(j)}(n)
is pure. Due to the Schmidt-decomposition, the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski states then have the same purity. As a consequence, it suffices to enhance the purity of one subsystem to obtain a higher purity on
both
subsystems. On one hand, performing projective measurements on system 1 leads to a unitary Zeno dynamics on system 2. On the other hand, random dynamical decoupling gives rise to a unitary evolution on system 1. However, the idea of equitability of system and bath shows that these two are essentially the same.

The mathematical structure of this approach to the random dynamical decoupling might remind some readers of the procedure of randomized benchmarking. Therefore, it is worth briefly discussing similarities and differences between the two. On one hand, the projection
\mathcal{D}
that specifies the average random dynamical decoupling evolution in the Zeno limit projects
\mathscr{H}_{1}
to the group average of the decoupling group
\mathscr{V}
. Due to the irreducibility assumption,
\mathcal{D}
is a
\mathscr{V}
-twirl over a unitary quantum
1
-design on
\mathscr{H}_{1}
, which will average away (switch off) the noise induced by the bath. On the other hand, in randomized benchmarking, one applies random Clifford operations to a quantum system in the presence of noise, i.e., one implements a twirl over a unitary quantum
2
-design. By this procedure, one averages over different noise sources to make the noise more isotropic or less correlated. Whilst there is this apparent mathematical similarity between random dynamical decoupling and randomized benchmarking of twirling the noise over a unitary quantum
t
-design, these two methods are different from a physical perspective. The goal of randomized benchmarking is to characterize the noise whereas random dynamical decoupling aims to remove it. Furthermore, the description of random dynamical decoupling relies on the full model of a quantum system together with a quantum bath. In contrast, in randomized benchmarking, the bath is not specified and only a noisy quantum system is considered. A study of explicit error bounds for the average gate fidelities in the context of randomized benchmarking can be found in Ref.
[
48
]
.

4
Convergence of the Quantum Zeno Limit and the Average Dynamical Decoupling

Ultimately, our goal is to show that the decoupling error of random dynamical decoupling is essentially determined by the convergence of the quantum Zeno limit, and hence to prove Theorems
9
and
10
. The route we take is to infer information from the average evolution. This section is dedicated to the average protocol, hence we prove the statements of Sec.
3.1
. The evolution under the average protocol can be understood as a special case of quantum Zeno dynamics. A very general treatment of the quantum Zeno dynamics by arbitrary quantum operations has been developed in Ref.
[
14
]
. Here, we only focus on the standard procedure with repeated projective measurements. We first discuss the convergence of this scheme and prove a bound, which scales as
\mathcal{O}({1/n})
(Theorem
1
). Afterwards, we show how this relates to the average dynamical decoupling protocol. This discussion directly gives us a convergence bound on the average evolution under the random dynamical decoupling (Propositions
2
and
4
).

4.1
Convergence of the Quantum Zeno Limit

In this subsection, we discuss the convergence of the quantum Zeno limit and prove Theorem
1
. We will extensively make use of the fact that unitaries as well as Hermitian projection operators have norm
1
in the operator norm, e.g.
\|{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH}}\|_{\infty}=\|{P}\|_{\infty}=\|{\mathbb{1}-P}\|_{\infty}=1
. Let us start by recalling an auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 14
.

For any Hermitian operator
X=X^{\dagger}
and for any
t\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tX}-\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq t\|X\|_{\infty},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tX}-\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{i}tX\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\|X\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(4.2)

Proof.

Observe first that

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tX}-\mathbb{1}=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}sX}=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,X\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}sX},

(4.3)

which yields the first bound (
4.1
).
Continuing (
4.3
) by integration by parts,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tX}-\mathbb{1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}(t-s)X\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}sX}\biggr|_{s=0}^{s=t}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,(t-s)X^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}sX}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}tX-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,(t-s)X^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}sX},

(4.4)

this gives the second bound (
4.2
).
∎

We apply Lemma
14
to prove a bound on the following quantum Zeno limit.

Proposition 15
.

Let
H=H^{\dagger}
be a Hermitian operator and let
P=P^{2}=P^{\dagger}
be a Hermitian projection.
Then, for any
t\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\|(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{n}\|H\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(4.5)

Proof.

First, observe that

\displaystyle\|P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}PHP}P\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|P\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}-\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H\right)P-\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}PHP}-\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}PHP\right)P\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}-\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}PHP}-\mathbb{1}+\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}PHP\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2n^{2}}\|H\|_{\infty}^{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{2n^{2}}\|PHP\|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}}\|H\|_{\infty}^{2},

(4.6)

where we have used Lemma
14
for the second inequality.
We then use a standard telescope sum trick

A^{n}-B^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}(A-B)B^{n-1-k},

(4.7)

for any operators
A
and
B
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This is proved in Lemma
22
in Appendix
A
.
By applying (
4.7
) to
A=P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P

and
B=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}PHP}P
and using (
4.6
), we get

\displaystyle\|(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P\|_{\infty}^{k}\|P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}^{n-1-k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{n}\|{H}\|_{\infty}^{2},

(4.8)

which proves the proposition.
∎

To prove Theorem
1
presented in Sec.
3.1
, we need to adjust the end of the sequence of repeated projections with the help of the following lemma.

Lemma 16
.

Let
H=H^{\dagger}
be a Hermitian operator and let
P=P^{2}=P^{\dagger}
be a Hermitianity projection.
Then, for any
t\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\|(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{t}{n}\|H\|_{\infty}.

(4.9)

Proof.

We first split as

\displaystyle(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}

\displaystyle=P(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}+(\mathbb{1}-P)(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}

\displaystyle=(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}+(\mathbb{1}-P)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}+(\mathbb{1}-P)(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}-\mathbb{1})P(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n-1}.

(4.10)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbb{1}-P)(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}-\mathbb{1})P(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n-1}\|_{\infty}\vphantom{\frac{t}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}-\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}\vphantom{\frac{t}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{n}\|H\|_{\infty},

(4.11)

where we have used Lemma
14
.
∎

Theorem
1
in Sec.
3.1
then follows by combining Proposition
15
and Lemma
16
through the triangle inequality.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Using Proposition
15
and Lemma
16

\displaystyle\|(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}\|_{\infty}+\|(P\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{t}{n}H}P)^{n}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tPHP}P\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{n}\|H\|_{\infty}+\frac{t^{2}}{n}\|H\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(4.12)

∎

Remark 17
.

The dynamics considered here starts with a projective measurement followed by a free evolution for a short time

(4.13)

To see this, we only need to change Lemma
16

5
Reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski States

For the sake of clarity, we introduce some notation first. Let
\mathcal{T}:\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}

\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2}

\mathcal{T}_{1,\sigma_{2}}:\mathscr{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2,\sigma_{1}}:\mathscr{H}_{2}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1,\sigma_{2}}(\rho_{1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{2}[\mathcal{T}(\rho_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})],

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{1}[\mathcal{T}(\sigma_{1}\otimes\rho_{2})],

(5.2)

and let
\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}
and
\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}
denote their corresponding Choi-Jamiołkowski states. On the other hand, the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda
of
\mathcal{T}
lives on an enlarged Hilbert space
(\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{1^{\prime}})\otimes(\mathscr{H}_{2}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{22^{\prime}}\Lambda,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}_{11^{\prime}}\Lambda.

(5.4)

These reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski states
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
are related to the Choi-Jamiołkowski states

Lemma 18
.

For the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski states of a CPTP map
\mathcal{T}:\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\sigma_{2}=\frac{1}{d_{2}}\mathbb{1}_{2},

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad\sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\mathbb{1}_{1}.

(5.6)

Proof.

Let
\{|{i}\rangle\}_{i=1,\ldots,d_{1}}
and
\{|{i^{\prime}}\rangle\}_{i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,d_{1}}
be orthonormal bases of
\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathscr{H}_{1^{\prime}}
, respectively. Analogously,
\{|{j}\rangle\}_{j=1,\ldots,d_{2}}
and
\{|{j^{\prime}}\rangle\}_{j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,d_{2}}
for
\mathscr{H}_{2}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2^{\prime}}
, respectively.
Let
\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\sum_{k}E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{\dagger}
be a Kraus representation of the CPTP map
\mathcal{T}
.
Then, on one hand, the reduced CPTP map
\mathcal{T}_{1,\sigma_{2}}
, which only acts on
\mathscr{H}_{1}
, is given by

\mathcal{T}_{1,\sigma_{2}}(\rho_{1})=\sum_{k}\operatorname{tr}_{2}[E_{k}(\rho_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})E_{k}^{\dagger}],

(5.7)

and its Choi-Jamiołkowski state reads

\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}}\sum_{k}\operatorname{tr}_{2}[E_{k}(|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|\otimes\sigma_{2})E_{k}^{\dagger}]\otimes|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|.

(5.8)

On the other hand, the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda_{1}
takes the form

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{k}\operatorname{tr}_{22^{\prime}}[E_{k}(|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|\otimes|{j}\rangle\langle{j^{\prime}}|)E_{k}^{\dagger}\otimes(|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|\otimes|{j}\rangle\langle{j^{\prime}}|)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime},j}\sum_{k}\operatorname{tr}_{2}[E_{k}(|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|\otimes|{j}\rangle\langle{j}|)E_{k}^{\dagger}]\otimes|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}}\sum_{k}\operatorname{tr}_{2}\!\left[E_{k}\left(|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|\otimes\frac{1}{d_{2}}\mathbb{1}_{2}\right)E_{k}^{\dagger}\right]\otimes|{i}\rangle\langle{i^{\prime}}|.

(5.9)

Comparing (
5.8
) and (
5.9

5.5

The relation (

∎

Lemma

In order to bridge between

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mathscr{H}
be a Hilbert space of dimension
d
and let
\sigma
be a density operator on
\mathscr{H}
with spectral decomposition
\sigma=\sum_{i}s_{i}|{i}\rangle\langle{i}|
. Then, the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{1}/d
on

can be decomposed as

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}=\frac{1}{d\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\sigma+\left(1-\frac{1}{d\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\right)\omega,

(5.10)

with another density operator

\omega=\frac{1}{d-1/\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\sum_{i}\left(1-\frac{s_{i}}{\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\right)|{i}\rangle\langle{i}|.

(5.11)

Proof.

The orthonormalized vectors
\{|{i}\rangle\}_{i=1,\ldots,d}
in the spectral decomposition of
\sigma

form a basis of
\mathscr{H}
.
The maximally mixed state

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}|{i}\rangle\langle{i}|.

(5.12)

In order to find a convex decomposition of the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{1}/d
into the state
\sigma
and another state
\omega
, let us rewrite the expansion of the maximally mixed state

as

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}=p\sigma+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1}{d}-ps_{i}\right)|{i}\rangle\langle{i}|.

(5.13)

In order for this to be a convex decomposition, we should have

,
\forall i
.
That is, we need

p\leq\frac{1}{ds_{i}},\quad\forall i.

(5.14)

Therefore, let us choose

(5.15)

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\sigma+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}\left(1-\frac{s_{i}}{\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\right)|{i}\rangle\langle{i}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\sigma+\left(1-\frac{1}{d\|\sigma\|_{\infty}}\right)\omega,

(5.16)

Lemma 20
.

Let
\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathscr{H}_{1^{\prime}}
be Hilbert spaces of dimension

, and let
\mathscr{H}_{2}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2^{\prime}}
be Hilbert spaces of dimension

|w)
a normalized vector in

\displaystyle(v|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}|v)

\displaystyle\geq 1-d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}[1-(v|\Lambda_{1}|v)],

(5.17)

\displaystyle(w|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}|w)

\displaystyle\geq 1-d_{1}\|\sigma_{1}\|_{\infty}[1-(w|\Lambda_{2}|w)],

(5.20)

Proof.

We focus on the proof for

on system 1.
The relations for

By using the convex decomposition of the maximally mixed state from Lemma

\frac{1}{d_{2}}\mathbb{1}_{2}=\frac{1}{d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}}\sigma_{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}}\right)\omega_{2},

\displaystyle(v|\Lambda_{1}|v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}}(v|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}|v)+\left(1-\frac{1}{d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}}\right)(v|\Lambda_{1,\omega_{2}}|v)

and hence,

for any normalized vector
|v)

By choosing

\displaystyle\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq(v_{\mathrm{max}}|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}|v_{\mathrm{max}})

\displaystyle\geq 1-d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}[1-(v_{\mathrm{max}}|\Lambda_{1}|v_{\mathrm{max}})]

∎

6.1
Convergence of the Trajectory Evolution of System 2

Theorem
5
gives a bound on the expected purity, the expected operator norm, and the expected fidelity to the unitary
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}
of the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state of the trajectory evolution on system 2.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

First, notice that according to Lemma
25
in Appendix
A
we have

\sqrt{\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)}\geq\|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}).

(6.1)

By Jensen’s inequality
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[f({}\cdots{})]\leq f(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{}\cdots{}])
for concave functions
f(x)
, we can bound the expected purity, the expected operator norm, and the expected fidelity of the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state of the trajectory evolution on system 2 as

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sqrt{\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d_{1}}\,\|\sigma_{1}\|_{2}T^{2},

(6.2)

where we have used the linearity of the expectation value for the equality and the last inequality follows from Proposition
4
.
∎

Theorem
5
can be used to bound the expected distance of the Choi-Jamiołkowski state of system 2 to the Zeno unitary, stated in Theorem
6
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

First, observe that for each trajectory
j
we have by definition

\left\|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)-\frac{1}{d_{2}}|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)-\frac{2}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})+1.

(6.3)

Now, consider the expected distance to the Zeno unitary. By Jensen’s inequality for convex functions, we get

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)-\frac{1}{d_{2}}|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\right\|_{2}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)-\frac{1}{d_{2}}|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)\right]-\frac{2}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})+1

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1-\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sqrt{d_{1}}\,\|\sigma_{1}\|_{2}T^{2},

(6.4)

where we have bounded the purity of the Choi-Jamiołkowski state by 1 and used Proposition
4
.
This proves the bound (
3.10
) on the distance in terms of the Choi-Jamiołkowski state.
The other bound (
3.11
) on the diamond distance is then obtained by using Lemma

in Appendix
A

These proofs are based on the fact that the average dynamical decoupling evolution on system 2 is an arithmetic mean of the trajectory dynamical decoupling evolutions on system 2, and hence, the Choi-Jamiołkowski state of the average evolution is given by a
convex sum
of the Choi-Jamiołkowski states of the trajectory evolutions, i.e.,

\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)=\sum_{j}q_{j}\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n),

(6.5)

with
q_{j}
being the probability of realizing trajectory
j
(which is actually uniformly distributed).
As proved in Propositions
2
and
4
, the average dynamical decoupling evolution on system 2 converges to the Zeno unitary in the decoupling limit
n\rightarrow\infty
. This leads to the results proved in Theorems
5
and
6
, which show that the trajectory dynamical decoupling evolutions on system 2 converge to the Zeno unitary
on average
. This further ensures that
almost all
trajectory dynamical decoupling evolutions on system 2 converge to the same Zeno unitary, due to the extremality of the pure Choi-Jamiołkowski state of a unitary. This is made explicit in a quantitative way by Proposition
7
.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

Consider the fidelity of the reduced Choi-Jamiolłkowski state of the average dynamical decoupling evolution on system 2 to the unitary
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}
. It is expressed, using the convex sum (
6.5
), as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}q_{j}\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}).

(6.6)

Let
r_{j}=\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda_{2,\sigma_{1}}^{(j)}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})
be the fidelity of trajectory
j
and fix
r\in[0,1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}q_{j}r_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r_{j}>r}q_{j}r_{j}+\sum_{r_{j}\leq r}q_{j}r_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r_{j}>r}q_{j}+\sum_{r_{j}\leq r}q_{j}r

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\leq r)+\mathbb{P}(\leq r)r,

(6.7)

where
\mathbb{P}(\leq r)=\sum_{r_{j}\leq r}q_{j}
is the probability of finding
r_{j}\leq r
. Notice that we have used
\sum_{r_{j}>r}q_{j}=1-\mathbb{P}(\leq r)
. Rearranging this inequality and using Proposition
4
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\leq r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-r}\left(1-\frac{1}{d_{2}}(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}}|\Lambda^{\mathrm{av}}_{2,\sigma_{1}}(n)|\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-r}\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{d_{1}}\,\|\sigma_{1}\|_{2}T^{2},

(6.8)

and thereby the statement of the proposition is proved.
∎

Now that we have proved the convergence bounds for the trajectory dynamical decoupling evolutions on system 2, we move on to proving the convergence of the trajectory dynamical decoupling evolutions on system 1.

6.2
Convergence of the Trajectory Evolution of System 1

Recall that for each trajectory
j
the evolution of the total system
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{(j)}(t)
is unitary. Therefore, its Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda^{(j)}(n)
is pure. As a consequence, the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski states
\Lambda_{1}^{(j)}(n)
and
\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}(n)
share the same spectrum given by the Schmidt coefficients of the full state
\Lambda^{(j)}(n)
. This makes it possible to shift the error bounds on system 2 to system 1. By doing so, we prove Theorem
9
, which gives a bound on the expected purity and the expected operator norm of the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state of system 1, as well as Theorem
10
, which provides a bound on the expected distance of the reduced Choi-Jamiołkowski state of the trajectory evolution of system 1 to a pure state.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

First, notice that for each trajectory
j
we can apply Lemma
20
.
In addition, we use
\|\Lambda_{1}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}=\|\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}
[see (
3.20
)], and get

\displaystyle\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq 1-d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}(1-\|\Lambda_{1}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle=1-d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}(1-\|\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}).

(6.9)

Now, consider the square root of the expected purity of the Choi-Jamiołkowski state
\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)
of the trajectory evolution of system 1, and use Jensen’s inequality for the square root (which is concave) to obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sqrt{\mathtt{P}\bigl(\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)\bigr)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\geq 1-d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\|\Lambda_{2}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}\Bigr]\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{n}d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}T^{2}.

(6.10)

The second inequality follows from Lemma
25
in Appendix
A
, the third inequality is due to (
6.9
) with the linearity of the expectation value, and the last bound is a consequence of Theorem
5
for
\sigma_{1}=\mathbb{1}/d_{1}
(noting Lemma
18
), for which we have
\|\sigma_{1}\|_{2}=1/\sqrt{d_{1}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
10
.

Without loss of generality, let us label the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}
of
\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)
in a descending order as
\lambda_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d_{1}^{2}-1}
. Then,
|v_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n))
is the normalized eigenvector of
\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)
belonging to the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
, and we have

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)-|v_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n))(v_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)|\right\|_{\infty}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\max(1-\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1})].

(6.11)

However, since
\lambda_{1}\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}=1-\lambda_{0}=1-\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)-|v_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n))(v_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)|\right\|_{\infty}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\|\Lambda_{1,\sigma_{2}}^{(j)}(n)\|_{\infty}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}d_{2}\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}T^{2},

(6.12)

where the last inequality is due to Theorem
9
.
∎

):
Using the triangle inequality,

\|{\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\|_{2}\leq\|{\hat{\mathcal{T}}-E_{0}\otimes\overline{E_{0}}}\|_{2}+\|{E_{0}\otimes\overline{E_{0}}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\|_{2}.

(6.13)

The first term can be estimated in the following way.

\displaystyle\|{\hat{\mathcal{T}}-E_{0}\otimes\overline{E_{0}}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{k\geq 1}E_{k}\otimes\overline{E_{k}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(6.14)

\displaystyle=d^{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}^{2}

(6.15)

\displaystyle=d^{2}(\mathtt{P}-\lambda_{0}^{2})\vphantom{\sum_{k\geq 1}}

(6.16)

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}(\mathtt{P}-\mathtt{P}^{2}),

(6.17)

where we have followed the same steps as (
2.19
) and have used Lemma
25
in Appendix
A
.
For the second term, we make use of the polar decomposition of
E_{0}=U|E_{0}|

[
37
, Sec. 7.3]
, where
U
is the closest unitary from
E_{0}

[
49
, Theorem IX.7.2]
.
Exploiting the fact that
\|{}\bullet{}\|_{2}
is unitarily invariant, and recalling the Kraus condition
\sum_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}=\mathbb{1}
, which implies
|E_{0}|^{2}\leq|E_{0}|\leq\mathbb{1}
, we can bound the second term as

\displaystyle\|{E_{0}\otimes\overline{E_{0}}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|{E_{0}\otimes\overline{E_{0}}-U\otimes\overline{U}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}-|E_{0}|\otimes\overline{|E_{0}|}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}-2|E_{0}|\otimes\overline{|E_{0}|}+|E_{0}|^{2}\otimes\overline{|E_{0}|^{2}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{tr}\Bigl(\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}-|E_{0}|^{2}\otimes\overline{|E_{0}|^{2}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=d^{2}(1-\lambda_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}(1-\mathtt{P}^{2}),

(6.18)

where we have used
\operatorname{tr}(|E_{0}|^{2})=\operatorname{tr}(E_{0}^{\dagger}E_{0})=d\lambda_{0}
for the second last line, and Lemma
25
in Appendix
A
for the last inequality.
Combining (
6.17
) and (
6.18
), the upper bound of the proposition is proved.

•

Lower bound in (
3.27
):
This follows by direct calculation.

\displaystyle\|{\hat{\mathcal{T}}-\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|{U\otimes\overline{U}}\|_{2}-\left\|\sum_{k}E_{k}\otimes\overline{E_{k}}\right\|_{2}

(6.19)

\displaystyle=d-\sqrt{d^{2}\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}}

(6.20)

\displaystyle=d(1-\sqrt{\mathtt{P}}).

(6.21)

•

Bound (
3.28
):
By the triangle inequality, we can write

\|\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{U}\|_{\diamond}\leq\|\mathcal{T}-E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}+\|E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}-U\bullet U^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}.

(6.22)

Let us bound both terms in (
6.22
) individually, starting with the first one.
By using Lemma
26
and (
2.5
),

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}-E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq d\left\|\Lambda-\frac{1}{d}|E_{0})(E_{0}|\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=d\,\Bigl\|\Lambda-\lambda_{0}|v_{0})(v_{0}|\Bigr\|_{1}

\displaystyle=d\left\|\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}|v_{k})(v_{k}|\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=d\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=d(1-\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=d(1-\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}),

(6.23)

where from the third to the fourth line we used the fact that for a positive matrix
A
we have
\|A\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}A
.
For the second term in (
6.22
), we first recall the definition of the diamond norm

\|E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}-U\bullet U^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{\|A\|_{1}=1}\|(E_{0}\otimes\mathbb{1})A(E_{0}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1})-(U\otimes\mathbb{1})A(U^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1})\|_{1},

(6.24)

where
\mathbb{1}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Then, using the triangle inequality and the unitary invariance of the trace norm,

\|E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}-U\bullet U^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}\leq\sup_{\|A\|_{1}=1}\left(\Bigl\|[(E_{0}-U)\otimes\mathbb{1}]A(E_{0}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1})\Bigr\|_{1}+\Bigl\|A[(E_{0}^{\dagger}-U^{\dagger})\otimes\mathbb{1}]\Bigr\|_{1}\right).

(6.25)

By using Hölder’s inequality twice,

\displaystyle\|E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}-U\bullet U^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|A\|_{1}=1}\left(\|(E_{0}-U)\otimes\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}\|A\|_{1}\|E_{0}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}+\|A\|_{1}\|(E_{0}^{\dagger}-U^{\dagger})\otimes\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle=\|E_{0}-U\|_{\infty}(1+\|E_{0}\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{1}-|E_{0}|\|_{\infty}(1+\|E_{0}\|_{\infty}),

(6.26)

where in the last step we wrote
E_{0}
in its polar decomposition
E_{0}=U|E_{0}|

[
37
, Sec. 7.3]
with
U
the closest unitary to
E_{0}

[
49
, Theorem. IX.7.2]
and
|E_{0}|=\sqrt{E_{0}^{\dagger}E_{0}}
. Also, we used the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm. Rewriting this expression while recalling the Kraus condition
\sum_{k}E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}=\mathbb{1}
, which implies
|E_{0}|^{2}\leq|E_{0}|\leq\mathbb{1}
, yields

\displaystyle\|E_{0}\bullet E_{0}^{\dagger}-U\bullet U^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{1}-|E_{0}|^{2}\|_{\infty}(1+\|\mathbb{1}\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle=2\|\mathbb{1}-E_{0}^{\dagger}E_{0}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbb{1}-E_{0}^{\dagger}E_{0}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=2\left\|\sum_{k\geq 1}E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{tr}\sum_{k\geq 1}E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\geq 1}(E_{k}|E_{k})

\displaystyle=2d\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=2d(1-\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=2d(1-\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}),

(6.27)

which proves the statement. Again, in the step from the fourth to the fifth line, we used the fact that for a positive matrix
A
we have
\|A\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}A
.

∎

7
Conclusion

In summary, we have provided a framework to unify the random dynamical decoupling with the quantum Zeno effect through the average of dynamical decoupling evolution and a phenomenon we call equitability of system and bath. By this approach, we infer explicit error bounds for the random dynamical decoupling only from an error bound on the quantum Zeno limit.

Our framework makes use of the fact that the average random dynamical decoupling evolution is a manifestation of the quantum Zeno dynamics. In the Zeno limit, we obtain a unitary evolution on the bath through this average protocol. In turn, with a high probability, trajectory random dynamical decoupling evolutions converge to a unitary bath evolution in the decoupling limit as well. This observation can be transferred to the system by studying the Schmidt decomposition of the Choi-Jamiołkowsi state of the full evolution: As it is a pure state for each trajectory evolution, its reduced states for the two systems have the same spectrum. Our discussion shows that the system and the bath can get assigned the same role in the context of quantum control and therefore can be treated equitable: There is no dedicated subsystem for control, i.e., if one subsystem evolves unitarily, the other subsystem will do so as well.

Appendix A
Basic Lemmas

In this appendix, we prove five basic lemmas. The first one (Lemma
22
) is a standard telescope sum identity, which we use to prove the bound on the quantum Zeno limit, i.e. in the proof of Theorem
1
. The second one (Lemma
23
) and the third one (Lemma
24
) are used in the discussion about the average dynamical decoupling. In particular, we need them in order to prove Proposition
2
. They are used to convert the norm of a reduced map to the the norm of the matrix representation of the full map. The fourth one (Lemma
25
) is an elementary statement, which relates the purity of a Choi-Jamiołkowski state with its operator norm. The last lemma (Lemma
26
) gives a norm equivalence between the diamond norm distance between two CPTP maps and the trace distance of their respective Choi-Jamiołkowski states.

Lemma 22
.

For any

n\in\mathbb{N}
and for any operators
A
and
B
, we have

A^{n}-B^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}(A-B)B^{n-1-k}.

(A.1)

Proof.

This lemma can be proved by performing an index shift,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}(A-B)B^{n-1-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k+1}B^{n-1-k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k}B^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{k+1}B^{n-1-k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}A^{k+1}B^{n-k-1}-B^{n}

\displaystyle=A^{n}-B^{n}.\vphantom{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}}

(A.2)

∎

Lemma 23
.

Let
A
be an operator acting on a bipartite Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2}
, and
|{u}\rangle_{1},|{v}\rangle_{1}\in\mathscr{H}_{1}
.
Then,

\|{}_{1}\langle{u}|A|{v}\rangle_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|A\|_{\infty}\||{u}\rangle_{1}\|\||{v}\rangle_{1}\|,

(A.3)

where
\|\bullet\|
for vectors denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathscr{H}_{1}
.

Proof.

Note that

A_{2}={}_{1}\langle{u}|A|{v}\rangle_{1}

(A.4)

acts on
\mathscr{H}_{2}
.
Then, there exists a normalized
|{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}\rangle_{2}\in\mathscr{H}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\|A_{2}\|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle={}_{2}\langle{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}|A_{2}^{\dagger}A_{2}|{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl({}_{1}\langle{v}|\otimes{}_{2}\langle{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}|\Bigr)\,A^{\dagger}\,\Bigl(|{u}\rangle_{1}\langle{u}|\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2}\Bigr)\,A\,\Bigl(|{v}\rangle_{1}\otimes|{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}\rangle_{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\||{u}\rangle_{1}\|^{2}\Bigl({}_{1}\langle{v}|\otimes{}_{2}\langle{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}|\Bigr)\,A^{\dagger}A\,\Bigl(|{v}\rangle_{1}\otimes|{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}}\rangle_{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\||{u}\rangle_{1}\|^{2}\||{v}\rangle_{1}\|^{2}\|A\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(A.5)

∎

Lemma 24
.

Let
\sigma_{1}
be an operator acting on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{1}
of dimension
d_{1}
and let
A_{2}
be an operator acting on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{2}
. Let
\mathcal{T}
denote a linear map on operators acting on
\mathscr{H}_{1}\otimes\mathscr{H}_{2}
(not necessarily CPTP). Then,

\sup_{\|A_{2}\|_{2}=1}\|{\operatorname{tr}_{1}}[\mathcal{T}(\sigma_{1}\otimes A_{2})]\|_{2}=\|(\mathbb{1}_{1}|\hat{\mathcal{T}}|\sigma_{1})\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{d_{1}}\,\|\sigma_{1}\|_{2}\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\infty}.

(A.6)

Proof.

The Frobenius norm of the image of an operator under a linear map can be related to the operator norm of the matrix representation of the map according to (
2.18
). The partial trace on the level of matrix representations of linear maps is discussed in Ref.
[
35
, Sec. VD]
. Combining these two facts gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\|A_{2}\|_{2}=1}\|{\operatorname{tr}_{1}}[\mathcal{T}(\sigma_{1}\otimes A_{2})]\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathbb{1}_{1}|\hat{\mathcal{T}}|\sigma_{1})\|_{\infty},

(A.7)

which can be bounded by Lemma
23
as

\displaystyle\|(\mathbb{1}_{1}|\hat{\mathcal{T}}|\sigma_{1})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(\mathbb{1}_{1}|\mathbb{1}_{1})}\sqrt{(\sigma_{1}|\sigma_{1})}\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d_{1}}\,\|\sigma_{1}\|_{2}\|\hat{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\infty}.

(A.8)

∎

Lemma 25
.

Let
\Lambda
be a density operator with spectral decomposition
\Lambda=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}|v_{k})(v_{k}|
and purity
\mathtt{P}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}
. Then,

\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\mathtt{P}\leq\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}.

(A.9)

Proof.

The statement follows from the definition of
\mathtt{P}
and
\|{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}
as well as the fact that
0\leq\lambda_{k}\leq 1
,
\forall k
, and
\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}=1
. Denoting by
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
the largest eigenvalue among
\{\lambda_{k}\}
,

\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}^{2}=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{2}=\mathtt{P}\leq\sum_{k}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\lambda_{k}=\|\Lambda\|_{\infty}.

(A.10)

∎

Lemma 26
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be linear maps acting on a
d
-dimensional quantum system. Let
\Lambda(\mathcal{S})
and
\Lambda(\mathcal{T})
be their Choi-Jamiołkowski states. Then,

\frac{1}{d}\|\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}\|_{\diamond}\leq\|\Lambda(\mathcal{S})-\Lambda(\mathcal{T})\|_{1}\leq\|\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}\|_{\diamond}.

(A.11)

Proof.

•

Upper bound:
By linearity,

\displaystyle\|\Lambda(\mathcal{S})-\Lambda(\mathcal{T})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|[(\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T})\otimes\mathbb{I}]\!\left(\frac{1}{d}|\mathbb{1})(\mathbb{1}|\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|A\|_{1}=1}\|[(\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T})\otimes\mathbb{I}](A)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}\|_{\diamond}.

(A.12)

•

Lower bound:
By Ref.
[
33
, Proposition 3.38]
, the supremum in the diamond norm distance is reached by a rank 1 operator, say
A=|X)(Y|
with
\|{X}\|_{2}=1=\|{Y}\|_{2}
, i.e.,

\|\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}\|_{\diamond}=\left\|[(\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T})\otimes\mathbb{I}]\bigl(|X)(Y|\bigr)\right\|_{1}.

(A.13)

Using Roth’s lemma (
2.6
), we can write
|X)=|\mathbb{1}X)=(\mathbb{1}\otimes X^{\intercal})|\mathbb{1})
(and analogously for
Y
), and hence

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle=d\left\|[(\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T})\otimes\mathbb{I}]\left((\mathbb{1}\otimes X^{\intercal})\frac{1}{d}|\mathbb{1})(\mathbb{1}|(\mathbb{1}\otimes Y^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=d\|(\mathbb{1}\otimes X^{\intercal})[\Lambda(\mathcal{S})-\Lambda(\mathcal{T})](\mathbb{1}\otimes Y^{\intercal})^{\dagger}\|_{1}.

(A.14)

By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq d\|\mathbb{1}\otimes X^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbb{1}\otimes\overline{Y}\|_{\infty}\|\Lambda(\mathcal{S})-\Lambda(\mathcal{T})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq d\|X^{\intercal}\|_{2}\|\overline{Y}\|_{2}\|\Lambda(\mathcal{S})-\Lambda(\mathcal{T})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=d\|\Lambda(\mathcal{S})-\Lambda(\mathcal{T})\|_{1}.

(A.15)

∎
