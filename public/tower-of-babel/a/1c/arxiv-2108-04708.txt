Quantum graphs: self-adjoint, and yet exhibiting a nontrivial 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetry Journal: Physics Letters A
Pavel Exner, Miloš Tater

Abstract
We demonstrate that a quantum graph exhibits a 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetry provided the coefficients in the condition describing the wave function matching at the vertices are circulant matrices; this symmetry is nontrivial if they are not invariant with respect to transposition. We also illustrate how the transport properties of such graphs are significantly influenced by the presence or absence of the non-Robin component of the coupling.

Having two research areas, each based on a strong concept, one is naturally inspired to look for connecting links between them. This applies, in particular, to the concepts of quantum graphs

Speaking of

This motivated the use of complex potentials having in mind that Schrödinger operators with real ones are naturally time-reversal invariant. Sometimes, of course, a real potential emerges in this context, as is the case of the negative quartic one, but even then the ways in which it is treated may be very different

Various ways of introducing

The main observation in this letter is that self-adjoint quantum

circulant matrices

As usual the state Hilbert of a quantum graph is the direct sum,
\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j}^{\oplus}L^{2}(e_{j})
, over the graph edges and the Hamiltonian acts on the
j
th edge as
\psi_{j}\mapsto-\psi_{j}^{\prime\prime}
. Without loss of generality we may discuss a star graph,
N
halflines meeting at a single vertex, because (a) the vertex coupling is local, and (b) more complicated graphs can be replaced by a graph with one vertex only, joining a certain number of halflines and loops, and the boundary conditions with matrix coefficients having a block structure reflecting the topology of the original graph. We use the symbols
\Psi=(\psi_{1}(0),\ldots,\psi_{n}(0))^{T}
and
\Psi^{\prime}=(\psi_{1}^{\prime}(0),\ldots,\psi_{n}^{\prime}(0))^{T}
, respectively, for the vectors of boundary values of the wave function components and their derivatives, conventionally taken in the outward direction, at the vertex. The boundary conditions
A\Psi+B\psi^{\prime}=0
ensure the self-adjointness provided the matrix
(A|B)
has maximum rank and
B^{*}A
is Hermitean
[
15
]
; alternatively we can write them in the unique form,

(U-I)\Psi+i\ell(U+I)\Psi^{\prime}=0,

(1)

where
U
is an
N\times N
unitary matrix
[
12
,
13
]
. The parameter
\ell>0
fixes the length scale; keeping it fixed, we denote the Hamiltonian determined by the condition (
1
) by
H_{U}
.

As usual in quantum mechanics, a symmetry is described by an operator
\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
with respect to which the Hamiltonian is invariant. Since the symbol of our operator is real and invariant with respect to the orientation of the edges, the nontrivial part of the task concerns the condition (
1
): a particular symmetry is associated with an invertible map in the space of the boundary values,
\Theta:\,\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
, such that
(U-I)\Theta\Psi(0)+i(U+I)\Theta\Psi^{\prime}(0)=0
holds for all
\Psi
satisfying (
1
), which is equivalent to the fact that
U
obeys the identity

\Theta^{-1}U\Theta=U.

(2)

The question is now which operators can be associated with the parity and time reversal transformations.

The latter is simpler. The operator
\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}
is antilinear and idempotent, and since the particles we consider have no internal degrees of freedom such as spin, it is just the complex conjugation. Using the unitarity,
U^{T}\bar{U}=\bar{U}U^{T}=I
we find easily that
\bar{\Psi}
satisfies the condition (
1
) with the transposed matrix, that is,

\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}U\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}=\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}U\Theta_{\mathcal{T}}=U^{T},

(3)

and consequently, the
H_{U}
is
\mathcal{T}
-invariant if and only the matrix defining the coupling is transposal-symmetric,
U=U^{T}
. This also immediately implies that a (self-adjoint) quantum graph
is
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric if and only if the mirror transformation acts in the same way,

\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}U\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}=\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}U\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}=U^{T}.

(4)

Asking under which circumstances can the condition (
4

per se
does not need an ambient space – investigation of spatial reflections forces us to think of the graph as embedded in the Euclidean space. For the sake of simplicity we regard our star graph as planar, but the conclusion will certainly extend to more general situations.

Let us first indicate which operators cannot serve as
\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}
. One might be tempted to reverse the edge orientation leading to flipping the sign of
\Psi^{\prime}

\delta

[
11
]
), but the corresponding candidate for the role of
\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}
, the matrix with one on the main antidiagonal and zeros elsewhere, leads to a double transpose of
U
, both with respect to the diagonal and antidiagonal, meaning just renaming the edges.

To see which linear operator can facilitate the similarity between
U
and
U^{T}

such that
VUV^{*}
is diagonal, and as such equal to its transpose. It follows that the matrix

satisfying
\Theta U\Theta=U^{T}
is of the form
\Theta=V^{T}V
. We know, however, how

looks like: the

coincides with

\phi_{j}
is the

th normalized eigenvector of

\Theta_{ij}=(\bar{\phi}_{i},\phi_{j}),\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n\,;

(5)

note the complex conjugation in the left entry which makes these expressions nontrivial. Denoting by
\{\nu_{j}\}
the basis in the boundary value space referring to the direct sum form of

\nu_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
etc., we see that
\Theta

\nu_{j}
to
((\bar{\phi}_{1},\phi_{j}),\ldots(\bar{\phi}_{n},\phi_{j}))^{T}

The situation changes, however, when we restrict our attention to vertex couplings referring to the subset of

U=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-1}&c_{n}\\
c_{n}&c_{1}&c_{2}&&c_{n-1}\\
\vdots&c_{n}&c_{1}&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{3}&&\ddots&\ddots&c_{2}\\
c_{2}&c_{3}&\cdots&c_{n}&c_{1}\end{array}\right)\,;

where

\phi_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1,\omega^{j},\omega^{2j},\ldots,\omega^{(n-1)j}\right)^{T},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Furthermore, the eigenvalues can be written in terms of the matrix entries as

V^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&1&1&1&\ldots&1\\
1&\omega&\omega^{2}&\omega^{3}&\ldots&\omega^{(n-1)}\\
1&\omega^{2}&\omega^{4}&\omega^{6}&\ldots&\omega^{2(n-1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&\omega^{n-1}&\omega^{2(n-1)}&\omega^{3(n-1)}&\ldots&\omega^{(n-1)^{2}}\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

Using relations (
5

\Theta_{\mathcal{P}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}1&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&0&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&1&0\\
\vdots&&&\iddots&&&\vdots\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0&0&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&0&0\end{array}\right)\,;

(9)

Now we may add the subscript
\mathcal{P}
to

, as well as

One may ask what is the meaning of the vertex couplings characterized by matrices of the form (
6

is invariant with respect to a cyclic renumbering of the edges meeting at the vertex. This becomes illustrative if we think of our graph as embedded in the Euclidean space, for simplicity again as a planar star. If the angles between the edges are the same, each such renumbering is equivalent to a discrete rotations; in this sense we can regard such a system as

In this way we have identified a class of vertex couplings for which the graph exhibits a

The former contains some well-known coupling such as

0&0&0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&1\\

which is obviously circulant and transposition-asymmetric. The same is true for a one-parameter class of couplings introduced in

which interpolates between the

Spectral properties of a self-adjoint star graph do not depend on the particular form of the matrix

, only on its spectrum. The essential spectrum of

is always the halfline

\kappa_{j}=\frac{1}{\ell}\,\tan\frac{\gamma_{j}}{2},\quad\gamma_{j}\in(0,\pi).

(11)

Likewise, the eigenvalues of

On the other hand, transport properties of such a star graph depend on the form of

This example illustrates the importance of the decomposition of the vertex coupling into the Dirichlet (the eigenspace referring to eigenvalue -1), Neumann (the eigenvalue 1 eigenspace) and Robin (the rest) parts
[

\eta(1-\eta)&-\eta&\eta-1\\
\eta-1&\eta(1-\eta)&-\eta\end{pmatrix}.

This time the Dirichlet component is present but the Neumann one is not, and consequently, we have
\lim_{k\to 0}S(k)=I
, while the transport at high energies is nontrivial,

\lim_{k\to\infty}S(k)=\frac{1}{3}\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&-2&-2\\
-2&1&-2\\
-2&-2&1\end{array}\right),

(14)

On the other hand, the vertex coupling can have a purely Robin character. To illustrate this claim, we replace the matrix (
