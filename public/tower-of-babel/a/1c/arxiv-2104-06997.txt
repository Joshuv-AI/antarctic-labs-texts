A Multifractal Decomposition for Self-similar Measures with Exact Overlaps Thanks: The author was supported by EPSRC Grant EP/V520123/1. Thanks: This paper is in final form and no version of it will be submitted for publication elsewhere.
Alex Rutar

Abstract
. We study self-similar measures in ℝ \R satisfying the weak separation condition along with weak technical assumptions which are satisfied in all known examples. For such a measure μ \mu , we show that there is a finite set of concave functions { τ 1 , … , τ m } \{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\} such that the L q L^{q} -spectrum of μ \mu is given by min ⁡ { τ 1 , … , τ m } \min\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\} and the multifractal spectrum of μ \mu is given by max ⁡ { τ 1 ∗ , … , τ m ∗ } \max\{\tau_{1}^{*},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{*}\} , where τ i ∗ \tau_{i}^{*} denotes the concave conjugate of τ i \tau_{i} . In particular, the measure μ \mu satisfies the multifractal formalism if and only if its multifractal spectrum is a concave function. This implies that μ \mu satisfies the multifractal formalism at values corresponding to points of differentiability of the L q L^{q} -spectrum. We also verify existence of the limit for the L q L^{q} -spectra of such measures for every q ∈ ℝ q\in\R . As a direct application, we obtain many new results and simple proofs of well-known results in the multifractal analysis of self-similar measures satisfying the weak separation condition.

1.
Introduction

Given a finite Borel measure
\mu
, a standard way to quantify the density of
\mu
at a given point
x
in its support is through the
local dimension
, which is the quantity

\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\log B(x,r)}{\log r}

when the limit exists.
A natural question to ask is the following: what is the structure of the set of points which have a prescribed local dimension
\alpha
?
In many interesting cases, these level sets of local dimensions are uncountable and dense in
\supp\mu
, but have
\mu
-measure zero for most values of
\alpha
.
We will focus on the Hausdorff dimensions of these level sets of local dimensions, which we denote by

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\dim_{H}\{x\in\supp\mu:\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha\}.

The function
f_{\mu}
is commonly known as the
(fine Hausdorff) multifractal spectrum
of
\mu
.

Related to the multifractal spectrum is the
L^{q}
-spectrum
of the measure
\mu
, which is given by

\tau_{\mu}(q)=\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\sup\sum_{i}\mu(B(x_{i},r))^{q}}{\log r}

where the supremum is taken over all disjoint families of balls
\{B(x_{i},r)\}_{i}
with
x_{i}\in\supp\mu
.
A standard application of Hölder’s inequality shows that
\tau_{\mu}
is a concave function of
q
.
The
L^{q}
-spectrum is related to the multifractal spectrum through a heuristic relationship known as the
multifractal formalism
.
It states that if the measure
\mu
is “sufficiently nice”, then the multifractal spectrum is the concave function given by

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=\inf\{\alpha q-\tau_{\mu}(q):q\in\R\}.

One can think of the
L^{q}
-spectrum as a sort of box-counting dimension, whereas the multifractal spectrum is a generalization of the Hausdorff dimension.
Of course, the multifractal formalism does not hold in general: for example, in the presence of non-conformality,
f_{\mu}
and
\tau_{\mu}
can both be concave conjugate functions but
f_{\mu}(\alpha)<\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)
for all
\alpha

[
23
]
.
In some sense, this is a consequence of the fact that the box and Hausdorff dimensions of non-conformal sets are, in general, not the same.
However, even when a measure is “locally nice”, the multifractal formalism can fail: if
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are probability measures with disjoint supports each satisfying the multifractal formalism and
\nu=(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})/2
, a straightforward exercise from the definitions shows that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\tau_{\nu}(q)

\displaystyle=\min\{\tau_{\mu_{1}}(q),\tau_{\mu_{2}}(q)\}

\displaystyle f_{\nu}(q)

\displaystyle=\max\{f_{\mu_{1}}(q),f_{\mu_{2}}(q)\}.

In particular,
\nu
satisfies the multifractal formalism if and only if
\tau_{\mu_{1}}(q)\leq\tau_{\mu_{2}}(q)
or
\tau_{\mu_{2}}(q)\leq\tau_{\mu_{1}}(q)
.
Our main result states, for a certain class of conformal measures, that this phenomenon is the only way in which the multifractal formalism can fail.

We will focus on the multifractal analysis of self-similar measures in
\R
, which are defined as follows.
Given a finite set of maps
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
where each
S_{i}:\R\to\R
is given by
S_{i}(x)=r_{i}x+d_{i}
where
0<|r_{i}|<1
and probabilities
(p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
with
p_{i}>0
and
\sum p_{i}=1
, the self-similar measure
\mu
is uniquely defined by

\mu=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{i}\cdot S_{i}\mu

where
S_{i}\mu
is the pushforward of
\mu
by
S_{i}
.

Self-similar measures are “locally nice” by nature of their construction (indeed, they have equal box and Hausdorff dimensions
[
4
]
), so one might be more optimistic for nice multifractal properties.
For example, self-similar measures are exact-dimensional
[
10
]
, which means that there is precisely one value
\alpha
for which the level set
\{x\in\supp\mu:\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha\}
has full
\mu
-measure.
If there is an open set
U
satisfying
\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}S_{i}(U)\subseteq U
where the union is disjoint, we say that
\mu
satisfies the
open set condition

[
22
]
.
For such measures, the
L^{q}
-spectrum is the unique smooth function satisfying
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{i}^{q}r_{i}^{-\tau_{\mu}(q)}=1
, and the multifractal formalism holds
[
2
,
30
]
.

However, for self-similar measures with overlaps, the multifractal formalism can fail.
One of the earliest known examples of this fact is due to Hu and Lau
[
21
]
, where they show that the three-fold convolution of the Cantor measure has an isolated point in its set of local dimensions, and therefore fails the multifractal formalism.
This measure, and generalizations, have been studied in
[
12
,
16
,
27
,
35
]
among other papers.
Another class of well-studied measures are the Bernoulli convolutions, which is the law of the random variable
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pm\lambda^{n}
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
where the
+
and
-
signs are chosen with equal probabilities.
In this case, for any parameter
\lambda\in(1/\phi,1)
where
\phi
is the Golden mean, the set of local dimensions has an isolated point
[
15
, Prop. 2.2]
, and
\phi
is maximal with this property.
Testud
[
36
]
constructed self-similar measures associated with digit-like sets for which the multifractal spectrum is non-concave and the maximum of two non-trivial concave functions.
Thus behaviour similar to
Eq.

1.1
can occur for self-similar measures with overlaps.

On the other hand, for Bernoulli convolutions with contraction ratio the reciprocal of a simple Pisot number (the unique positive root of a polynomial
x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1
for some
k\geq 2
), the multifractal formalism is known to hold
[
6
]
.
It is also shown in
[
33
]
that any self-similar measure associated with the IFS
\{\lambda_{1}x,\lambda_{2}x+\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2}x+(1-\lambda_{2})\}
for
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
and
\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\leq 1
satisfies the multifractal formalism.
The
L^{q}
-spectra of self-similar measures also have a certain amount of regularity: the limit defining
\tau_{\mu}(q)
is known to exist for any
q\geq 0

[
31
]
.

We see that, even for self-similar measures, a wide variety of behaviour is possible.
Determining precisely when the multifractal formalism is satisfied, and more generally understanding properties of the
L^{q}
-spectrum and multifractal spectrum when it is not, is a very challenging question and little is known.

In this paper, we develop a general theory in an attempt to remedy this.
We will show for an important class of self-similar measures that the varied multifractal behaviour observed above follows from a decomposition similar in form to
Eq.

1.1
.
More precisely, we show that the
L^{q}
-spectrum of
\mu
is given by the minimum of a finite set of concave functions, and the multifractal spectrum of
\mu
is given by the maximum of their concave conjugates.
These concave functions can be loosely interpreted as the
L^{q}
-spectra of a decomposition of
\mu
as a sum of subadditive set functions, each satisfying a multifractal formalism.
By standard arguments involving concave functions, this shows that the multifractal formalism holds for
\mu
in the following generic sense:
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism if and only if
f_{\mu}
is a concave function.
This is in stark contrast to measures associated with iterated function systems of non-conformal maps as discussed above.

1.1.
The weak separation condition and finite type conditions

Many of the examples mentioned in the preceding section satisfy various closely-related finite type conditions
[
5
,
17
,
25
,
28
]
.
Heuristically, these finite type conditions require that there are only “finitely many overlaps”
These separation conditions are all special cases of the
weak separation condition
of Lau and Ngai
[
24
]
, which states that there is a uniform bound on the number of simultaneous “distinct overlaps” (see
Eq.

3.9
for a precise statement).
Note that the weak separation condition is strictly weaker than the open set condition.
When the invariant set
\supp\mu
is a closed interval, the generalized finite type condition coincides with the weak separation condition
[
9
,
17
]
.
It is an open question to determine, outside certain degenerate situations, if these two separation conditions are equivalent in general.

The multifractal analysis of such measures have been extensively studied since.
Such measures have enough structure to allow strong results, yet the class contains many interesting examples and exceptional behaviour.
The most significant general result to date, due to Feng and Lau
[
11
]
, states that for self-similar measures satisfying the weak separation condition, the multifractal formalism holds for any
q\geq 0
, and for
q<0
there is an open set
U_{0}
on which
\mu
is sufficiently regular so that the
L^{q}
- and multifractal spectra restricted to
U_{0}
satisfy the multifractal formalism.
However, the relatively open set
U_{0}\cap K
is almost always a proper subset of
K
, so this result only gives a (somewhat coarse) lower bound for
f_{\mu}
.
The case for
q<0
is more challenging to establish in general: indeed, we already saw for such self-similar measures that the multifractal formalism need not hold.

For measures satisfying the weak separation condition in
\R
, the author recently established general conditions based on connectivity properties of an associated graph for which the regularity on the set
U_{0}
can be extended to the entire set
K

[
33
]
.
This can be applied to verify the multifractal formalism for all
q\in\R
for certain examples such as those discussed in
[
26
, Prop. 4.3]
or
[
3
, Ex. 8.5]
.

Our work here vastly extends these results under a slightly more specialized hypothesis (detailed in
Definition

3.14
).
We will discuss our technical conditions and results in detail in the following section.
We are not aware of any IFS satisfying the weak separation condition for which the technical conditions do not hold.

Main results and outline of the paper

1.2.1.
Symbolic encoding and the transition graph

In
Section

3
, we define a generalized version of the constructions in
[
5
,
19
,
33
]
which provides a more cohesive perspective on the “net interval” constructions defined therein and simplifies the study of certain examples.
The construction is based on the idea of an
iteration rule

\Phi
(see
Definition

3.3
), which describes how to define inductively a nested heirarchy of partitions
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in a way which depends only on the local geometry of
K
(see
Proposition

3.5
).
The end result is to construct a rooted directed graph
\mathcal{G}
, which we call the
transition graph
.
The edges of the graph
\mathcal{G}
are equipped with matrices
T(e)
, such that norms of products of matrices corresponding to finite paths beginning at the root vertex encode the measure
\mu
on a rich family of subsets (this result is given in
Proposition

3.12
).
When the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is finite, we say that the IFS satisfies the
finite neighbour condition with respect to
\Phi
, or the
\Phi
-FNC for short (see
Definition

3.14
).
For the remainder of this paper, we will assume that this condition is satisfied.

We denote by
\Omega^{\infty}
the set of infinite paths in
\mathcal{G}
originating at the root vertex, which is equipped with an “almost injective” Lipschitz projection
\pi:\Omega^{\infty}\to K
.
The set
\Omega^{\infty}
can be thought of as “symbolic” analogue of
K
, where the weights
W(e)
encode the metric structure of
K
and the matrices
T(e)
encode the self-similar measure
\mu
.
The overarching approach in this paper is to establish results in the space
\Omega^{\infty}
, and then using the projection
\pi
obtain corresponding results about the multifractal analysis of the self-similar measure
\mu
.
The main technical challenge is that the map
\pi
is not, in general, bi-Lipschitz.

The graph
\mathcal{G}
need not be strongly connected.
We call the non-trivial connected components of
\mathcal{G}

loop classes
, which we define fully in
Section

4.1
.
Since the tail of any infinite path is an infinite path in a loop class, we obtain a decomposition

\Omega^{\infty}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}

for appropriate sets
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
, where the union is disjoint.
This decomposition of
\mathcal{G}
will correspond directly (outside certain degenerate situations) with the decomposition given in
B
.

for

1.2.2.
Loop classes and the upper bounds

There can be components
\mathcal{L}_{i}
where the corresponding sets
\pi(\Omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}})\subseteq K
have measure 0 (in
Fig.

1
, this is
\mathcal{L}_{1}
,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{3}
).
However, even though the measure
\mu
cannot be restricted to
\pi(\Omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}})
in a sensible way, the corresponding symbolic measure (which we denote by
\rho
) does restrict properly.
In
Section

5.1
, we define symbolic analogues
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
of the
L^{q}
-spectrum and
f_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
of the multifractal spectrum for the loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{i}
.
These functions can be interpreted as
L^{q}
-spectra and multifractal spectra of some appropriate subadditive set functions defined on
\pi(\Omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}})
.

In
Lemma

6.14
and
Theorem

6.6
, we establish the following general upper bounds.

Theorem A
.

Suppose
\mu
is a self-similar measure satisfying the
\Phi
-FNC with loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
and corresponding symbolic
L^{q}
-spectra
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}
.
Then

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\tau_{1}^{*}(\alpha),\ldots,\tau_{m}^{*}(\alpha)\}

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}(q)\leq\min\{\tau_{1}(q),\ldots,\tau_{m}(q)\}.

Unlike the general upper bound
f_{\mu}\leq\tau_{\mu}^{*}

[
24
, Thm. 4.1]
, this upper bound for
f_{\mu}
follows by an argument which depends sensitively on the existence of the local dimension in the definition of
f_{\mu}(\alpha)
.
The precise ideas here can be found in
Lemma

6.4
and the surrounding discussion.
Note that upper bound given in
A
is already a non-trivial improvement on the general bound
\tau_{\mu}^{*}
when
\max\{\tau_{1}^{*},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{*}\}
is not a concave function.
Indeed, since
\tau_{\mu}(q)\leq\tau_{i}(q)
, we have
\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)\geq\tau_{i}^{*}(\alpha)
so that

\tau_{\mu}^{*}\geq\max\{\tau_{1}^{*},\ldots,\tau_{m}^{*}\},

but
\tau_{\mu}^{*}
is necessarily concave.

1.2.3.
Irreducibility, decomposability, and the lower bounds

In order to establish the lower bounds, we require two main assumptions.
The first, which we call
irreducibility
, can be interpreted as an internal connectivity property for the loop classes, and depends only on properties of the paths and transition matrices internal to some loop class
\mathcal{L}_{i}
(see
Section

4.2
).
This property was introduced and studied in
[
7
]
; as with that paper, this result is essential for establishing the symbolic multifractal formalism in
Theorem

5.8
.
The irreducibility assumption is also important to resolve the fact that the projection
\pi
is not, in general, bi-Lipschitz.
This technical result is given in
Theorem

5.12
.
While irreducibility formally depends on the choice of probabilities, in practice, every example of which the author is aware can be verified by the slightly stronger hypothesis of
Lemma

4.5
, which does not depend on the choice of probabilities.

The second main assumption, which we call
decomposability
, is a statement about the finite paths which do not have any edges in loop classes (see
Section

4.3
).
This property is closely related to the positive transition matrix assumption in
[
18
]
, and our proof of
Theorem

6.15
largely follows the ideas in that document.
This assumption allows a product-like decomposition of
\Omega^{\infty}
as
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}^{\infty}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}^{\infty}
in a way which preserves the norms of matrices.
See
Eq.

6.3
for the precise statement and application of this idea.

We will also assume a simple non-degeneracy property (given in
Definition

6.12
).
Similar statements can be made assuming some loop classes are degenerate, but we omit this discussion for simplicity.
We then have the following result, proven in
Corollary

6.13
and
Theorem

6.15
.

Theorem B
.

Suppose
\mu
is a self-similar measure satisfying the
\Phi
-FNC with loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
and corresponding symbolic
L^{q}
-spectra
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}
.
Suppose each loop class is non-degenerate.
Then:

(1)

If the irreducibility assumption is satisfied,

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\max\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}^{*}(\alpha),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}^{*}(\alpha)\}.

(2)

If the decomposability assumption is satisfied, the limit defining
\tau_{\mu}(q)
exists for every
q\in\R
.
Moreover,

\tau_{\mu}(q)=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}.

Outside the open set condition
[
1
]
and the case
q\geq 0

[
31
]
, there does not appear to be any general existence results for the limit
\tau_{\mu}(q)
when
\mu
is a self-similar measure.
Moreover, the author is not aware of any self-similar measure satisfying the weak separation condition which does not satisfy all the hypotheses in
B
.
To provide evidence for this claim, we observe that the hypotheses are satisfied for a number of examples (see
Section

7
).
However, verifying these conditions in general seems to be a very challenging question.

We can now use
B
to describe precisely when the multifractal formalism holds.
We say that
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism at
\alpha
if
f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)
.
Recall that the subdifferential
\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
is the interval from the right derivative to the left derivative of
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
at
q
.
The following result is proven in
Corollary

7.1
.

Corollary C
.

Let
\mu
satisfy the same hypotheses as
B
, along with the irreducibility and decomposability assumptions.
Then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism at
\alpha
if and only if
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
for some
1\leq i\leq m
and
q\in\R
with
\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
.
In particular, if the derivative
\alpha=\tau_{\mu}^{\prime}(q)
exists, then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism at
\alpha
.

In other words, the multifractal formalism fails precisely on phase transitions (values of
\alpha
corresponding to points of non-differentiability of the
L^{q}
-spectrum) caused by transitions in
\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}
from some
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
to
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{j}}(q)
for
i\neq j
.
This corollary is highlighted in

\mathcal{L}_{1}

and

For values of
\alpha

\tau_{\mu}^{*}

Corollary D
.

Let
\mu
satisfy the same hypotheses as

\mu

1.2.4.
Applications and analysis of examples

The hypotheses in
D
are satisfied in many well-known examples.
Here, we list some IFSs for which
D
applies, so that any associated self-similar measure satisfies the multifractal formalism if and only if the set of local dimensions is a closed interval:

—

the family
\{\frac{x}{d}+\frac{j}{md}(d-1):j=0,1,\ldots,m\}
with
m\geq d-1\geq 1
integers, which includes the 3-fold convolution of the Cantor measure
[
21
]
, and is discussed in detail in
[
16
, Sec. 5]
.

—

Bernoulli convolutions with parameters that are reciprocals of simple Pisot numbers
[
6
]
, or reciprocals of the Pisot roots of the polynomials
x^{3}-2x^{2}+x-1
,
x^{4}-x^{3}-2x^{2}+1
, and
x^{4}-2x^{3}+x-1
(see
Section

7.3
).

—

the IFS
\{\rho x,\rho^{2}x+\rho-\rho^{2},\rho^{2}x+1-\rho^{2}\}
where
1/\rho
is the Golden mean, considered in
[
20
, Sec. 5.3.3]
.

By combining our results with the detailed study of sets of local dimensions contained in the references cited above, we obtain a number of new examples of measures satisfying the multifractal formalism which were not previously known in the literature.
Such results about the validity of the multifractal formalism were previously only known for Bernoulli convolutions associated with simple Pisot numbers
[
6
]
.
We refer the reader to
[
20
]
for details related to the computation of sets of local dimensions under similar assumptions to this paper.

To conclude this paper, we will provide a detailed study of some examples in
Section

7
to illustrate more concretely how our results may be applied in specific situations.
Our selection of examples does not attempt to be exhaustive, and the examples are primarily chosen to illustrate how our results explain the different multifractal phenomena exhibited by self-similar measures satisfying the weak separation condition.

In
Section

7.2
, we study a family of self-similar measures associated with an IFS with maps of the form
x\mapsto x/\ell+i/\ell
or
x\mapsto-x/\ell+(i+1)/\ell
where
\ell\geq 2
is an integer and
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
.
Such measures were first studied by Testud
[
36
]
, where he provided some of the first known examples of self-similar measures which exhibit non-trivial non-concave spectra.
Our results extend and contextualize his results, since we do not require any assumptions on the digit sets.
This also extends results obtained by Olsen and Snigireva
[
29
]
for such measures.

In
Section

7.3
, we provide a simple (given our general results) verification of the multifractal formalism for Bernoulli convolutions with parameters that are reciprocals of simple Pisot numbers.
This fact was first observed by Feng
[
6
]
.
Our technique is more general and depends only on establishing certain structural properties of the transition graph.
Our results also apply, for example, to the polynomials
x^{3}-2x^{2}+x-1
,
x^{4}-x^{3}-2x^{2}+1
, and
x^{4}-2x^{3}+x-1
.

Finally, in
Section

7.4
, we verify the multifractal formalism for any self-similar measure associated with a class of IFS generalizing an example of Lau and Wang
[
26
]
, which is the IFS
\{\lambda_{1}x,\lambda_{2}x+\lambda_{1}(1-\lambda_{2}),\lambda_{2}x+(1-\lambda_{2})\}
.
The multifractal formalism for the self-similar measure studied by Lau and Wang was first verified by the author in a recent paper
[
33
]
.
We provide a simplified proof of this fact, which generalizes naturally to a family of related examples (which also includes
[
3
, Ex. 8.5]
).

1.2.5.
Questions

We conclude this section with three natural questions.

(1)

Are the hypotheses in
B
satisfied for every measure
\mu
satisfying the weak separation condition?
Both a counterexample or a proof of non-existence here would be very interesting.

(2)

In what generality does a version of
B
hold?
Is the multifractal spectrum of any self-similar measure always the maximum of a finite set of concave functions?

(3)

If
\mu
is a self-similar (or self-conformal) measure and
f_{\mu}
is a concave function, is it necessarily true that
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism?

.

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C|x-y|

bi-Lipschitz if

2.
Some brief preliminaries

2.1.
Weighted iterated function systems

In our setting, a
weighted iterated function system
(WIFS) is a tuple
(S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
where

(2.1)

S_{i}(x)=r_{i}x+d_{i}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ for each }i\in\mathcal{I}

with
0<\left|r_{i}\right|<1
, so that each
S_{i}
is a contracting similaritity in
\R
, and the
p_{i}
satisfy
p_{i}>0
and
\sum p_{i}=1
.
We refer to the tuple
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
simply as an
iterated function system
(IFS).

There are two important invariant objects associated with a WIFS, both of which can be realized as the unique fixed point of a contraction mapping on an appropriate metric space.
The first is a non-empty, compact set
K
satisfying

K=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}S_{i}(K),

known as the
self-similar set
associated with the WIFS.
The second is a Borel probability measure
\mu
satisfying

(2.2)

\mu(E)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\cdot S_{i}\mu(E)

for any Borel set
E\subseteq K
, where
S_{i}\mu(E)=\mu(S_{i}^{-1}(E))
is the pushforward of
\mu
by
S_{i}
.
We say that
\mu
is the
self-similar measure
associated with the WIFS.
We refer the reader to the book of Falconer
[
4
]
for details concerning the existence and uniqueness of these objects.

Note that
\supp\mu=K
.
Throughout this document, we will assume that
K
is not a singleton, so that
\mu
is a non-atomic measure.
By conjugating the maps as necessary (which amounts to an appropriate translation of the
d_{i}
), we may assume that the convex hull of
K
is
[0,1]
.

Let
\mathcal{I}^{*}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}^{n}
denote the set of all finite tuples on
\mathcal{I}
.
Given
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})\in\mathcal{I}^{n}
, we write

S_{\sigma}=S_{\sigma_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{\sigma_{n}},\qquad r_{\sigma}=r_{\sigma_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ r_{\sigma_{n}},

and

p_{\sigma}=p_{\sigma_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ p_{\sigma_{n}}.

Abusing notation slightly, we denote the empty word (the unique word of length zero) by
\emptyset
and write
S_{\emptyset}=\id
,
p_{\emptyset}=1
, and
r_{\emptyset}=1
.
Given another word
\tau=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m})
, the
concatenation

\sigma\tau
is the word
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n},\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m})
.
We say that a word
\sigma
is a
prefix
of
\tau
if there exists some
\omega
such that
\tau=\sigma\omega
.

2.2.
Concave functions

Let
f:\R\to\R\cup\{-\infty\}
be a concave function.
The
subdifferential
of
f
at
x
is given by

\partial f(x)=\{\alpha:\alpha(y-x)+f(x)\geq f(y)\text{ for any }y\in\R\}.

Of course, if
f
is differentiable at
x
, then
\partial f(x)=\{f^{\prime}(x)\}
.
The
concave conjugate
of
f
is the function

f^{*}(\alpha):=\inf\{\alpha x-f(x):x\in\R\}.

Naturally, the infimum may be attained at
-\infty
.
Note that
f^{*}
is always concave, and concave convolution is involutive (i.e.
f^{**}=f
when
f
is a concave function).
We will use the fact that
f^{*}(\alpha)+f(x)=\alpha x
whenever
\alpha\in\partial f(x)
.

We refer the reader to
[
32
]
for more detail and proofs of these facts.

2.3.
Local dimensions and multifractal analysis

Let
\mu
be a finite Borel measure in
\R
with compact support.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
x\in\supp\mu
be arbitrary.
Then the
lower local dimension of
\mu
at
x
is given by

\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,t))}{\log t}

and the
upper local dimension

\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)
is given similarly with the limit inferior replaced by the limit superior.
When the values of the upper and lower local dimension agree, we call the shared value the
local dimension of
\mu
at
x
, denoted
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
.

We are primarily interested in understanding geometric properties of the level sets of local dimensions.
Define

E_{\mu}(\alpha)=\bigl\{x\in\supp\mu:\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha\bigr\}.

We will focus on the
(fine Hausdorff) multifractal spectrum
of
\mu
, which is the function
f_{\mu}:\R\to\R\cup\{-\infty\}
given by

f_{\mu}(\alpha):=\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\alpha)

where, by convention, we write
\dim_{H}\emptyset=-\infty
.

A different (but related) way to quantify the density of
\mu
is through the
L^{q}
-spectrum of the measure.

Definition 2.2
.

The
L^{q}
-spectrum
of
\mu
is given by

\tau_{\mu}(q)=\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sup\sum_{i}\mu(B(x_{i},t))^{q}}{\log t}

where the supremum is over families of disjoint balls
\{B(x_{i},t)\}_{i=1}^{m}
centred at
x_{i}\in K
.

Standard arguments show that the function
\tau_{\mu}
is an increasing concave function of
q
.
Set

\displaystyle\alpha_{\min}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{\mu}(q)}{q}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\max}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\to-\infty}\frac{\tau_{\mu}(q)}{q}.

When
\mu
is a self-similar measure, it is known that
\alpha_{\min}
and
\alpha_{\max}
are finite real numbers (see, for example,
[
11
, Cor. 3.2]
).

The multifractal formalism is a heuristic relationship introduced in
[
14
]
which relates the
L^{q}
-spectrum and the multifractal spectrum of
\mu
under certain conditions.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
\alpha\in\R
, we say that the measure
\mu
satisfies the
multifractal formalism at
\alpha
if

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)

where
\tau_{\mu}^{*}
is the concave conjugate of
\tau_{\mu}
.
We say that
\mu
satisfies the
(complete) multifractal formalism
if
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism at every
\alpha\in\R
.

In particular,
f_{\mu}(\alpha)
is a concave function which takes finite values precisely on the interval
[\alpha_{\min}(\mu),\alpha_{\max}(\mu)]
.

It always holds that
f_{\mu}(\alpha)\leq\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)
(see, for example,
[
24
, Thm. 4.1]
) and if
E_{\mu}(\alpha)
is non-empty, then
\alpha_{\min}\leq\alpha\leq\alpha_{\max}

[
11
, Cor. 3.2]
.
However, as discussed in the introduction, the set of
\alpha
where
E_{\mu}(\alpha)\neq\emptyset
need not be a closed interval and, even if it is, the multifractal formalism need not hold
[
36
]
.

3.
A generalized transition graph construction

Self-similar measures have a natural encoding as a projection of self-similar measures in sequence space.
Let
\mathcal{I}^{\infty}
denote the set of all infinite sequences on the alphabet
\mathcal{I}
equipped with the natural product metric.
Given a sequence
(i_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{I}^{\infty}
, define the projection
\pi_{0}:\mathcal{I}^{\infty}\to K
by the rule

\pi_{0}((i_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty})=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{i_{n}}(0).

When the compact sets
S_{i}(K)
are disjoint for distinct
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the map
\pi_{0}
is bi-Lipschitz.
In this case, the value of the measure
\mu
has a simple formula for a rich family of subsets of
K
, namely

(3.1)

\mu\bigl(S_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{i_{n}}(K)\bigr)=p_{i_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{n}}.

However, when the measure
\mu
has overlaps, such a simple formula no longer holds since the projection
\pi_{0}
fails (in some situations quite badly) to be bi-Lipschitz.

A technique to overcome this limitation was first introduced by Feng
[
5
]
and extended in
[
19
,
33
]
.
In the subsequent sections, we will introduce a convenient framework which generalizes the prior net interval constructions; this will allow the simplification of analysis of examples in
Section

7
.
As this construction underlies all the results in this paper, we informally summarize the main ideas here.

Recall that the convex hull of
K
is
[0,1]
.
In
Section

3.1
, we inductively construct a nested sequence of partitions of
K
with mesh size tending to 0, which we will denote by
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
.
Here, a partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a finite collection of closed intervals
\{\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{\ell}\}
where
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}\cap\Delta_{j}^{\circ}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
,
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}\cap K\neq\emptyset
, and
K\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\Delta_{i}
.
We set
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{[0,1]\}
.
We will associate to each
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
a
neighbour set

\mathcal{V}(\Delta)
(an ordered tuple of similarity maps from
\R
to
\R
) such that each similarity map is a normalized version of some word
S_{\sigma}
with
S_{\sigma}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}
.
In the sense of
Lemma

3.4
, we also require that
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)
does not contain repetitions and satisfies a sort of maximality.
For a given
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we want that the
children

\{\Delta^{\prime}:\Delta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1},\Delta^{\prime}\subset\Delta\}
depend uniquely on
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)
, as made precise in
Proposition

3.5
.
The
(basic) iteration rule
given in
Definition

3.1
and
Definition

3.3
underpins this inductive construction (we think of the domain of an iteration rule as the set of all possible neighbour sets of net intervals), and the technical hypotheses ensure that the various properties listed above are satisfied.
Now, in
Section

3.2
, we construct a directed
transition graph

\mathcal{G}
with root vertex
v_{\operatorname{root}}
such that the finite paths in
\mathcal{G}
of length
n
are in bijection with the partitions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
(see
Lemma

3.9
).
We associate to the edges in
\mathcal{G}

transition matrices
such that the
\mu
-measure of a net interval is the norm of the corresponding products of matrices (see
Proposition

3.12
).

Our main assumption from this point on will be that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is finite; more details on this assumption are given in
Section

3.4
.
While
\mathcal{G}
is not, in general, strongly connected, we can enumerate the non-trivial maximal strongly connected components as
\{\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}\}
(we refer to these as
loop classes
, as defined in
Definition

4.1
).
Denote the set of infinite paths in
\mathcal{G}
beginning at
v_{\operatorname{root}}
by
\Omega^{\infty}
.
Given an infinite path
(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
\Omega^{\infty}
, there is a unique loop class
\mathcal{L}_{i}
such that for all
n
sufficiently large,
e_{n}
is an edge in some loop class
\mathcal{L}_{i}
.
The bijections from finite paths of length
n
to
\mathcal{P}_{n}
induce a Lipschitz surjection
\pi:\Omega^{\infty}\to K
(note that the metric structure on
\Omega^{\infty}
is defined in
Section

5.1
, and depends on the edge weights).
The space
\Omega^{\infty}
equipped with the projection
\pi
is analgous to the space
\mathcal{I}^{\infty}
along with the projection
\pi_{0}
.
Moreover, since the
\mathcal{P}_{n}
are partitions of
K
,
\pi
is nearly a bijection (it is injective on all but countably many points), and while
\pi
need not be bi-Lipschitz, it is close to being so in a heuristic sense.
The main cost is that we must replace the products of scalars in
Eq.

3.1
with norms of products of matrices.
This introduces additional technical challenges, which necessitate the assumptions of irreducibility and decomposability, which are discussed in more detail in
Section

4.1
.

3.1.

We write by

a\geq 1
.
Thus, (i) implies that the diameters of net intervals also tend uniformly to zero.
In fact, since
K\subseteq\bigcup_{\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\Delta
and the endpoints of each
\Delta
are elements of
K
,

\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\Delta=K.

We now have the following basic lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix some pair
(\Delta,v)\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
.
Then for each
f\in v
,
f(K)\cap(0,1)\neq\emptyset
, and

(3.4)

\Delta^{\circ}\cap K=\Delta^{\circ}\cap\bigcup_{f\in v}T_{\Delta}\circ f(K).

Moreover, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
is any word satisfying
S_{\sigma}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
, there is a unique word
\tau
such that
T_{\Delta}^{-1}\circ S_{\tau}\in v
and either
\tau
is a prefix of
\sigma
or
\sigma
is a prefix of
\tau
.

Proof.

We prove
Eq.

3.4
by induction on
n
.
The case
n=0
is immediate, so now let
(\Delta,v)\in\mathcal{V}_{n}
have parent
(\Delta^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}_{n-1}
.
Write
v^{\prime}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
and
\Phi(v^{\prime})=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{m})
.
Note that by definition of
\Phi
, for each
i
,

K=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}S_{\sigma}(K).

Thus by the inductive hypothesis,

(\Delta^{\prime})^{\circ}\cap K=(\Delta^{\prime})^{\circ}\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}T_{\Delta^{\prime}}\circ f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}(K).

But by construction, if
T_{\Delta^{\prime}}\circ f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
, then
T_{\Delta}^{-1}\circ T_{\Delta^{\prime}}\circ f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}\in v
, so the result follows.

For the second part, the existence of the word
\tau
follows by construction, and uniqueness follows from (ii) in
Definition

3.3
.
∎

We now have the following fundamental result, the proof of which is similar to
[
33
, Thm. 2.8]
.
We include the main details and leave additional verification to the reader.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(f_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be an IFS with basic iteration rule
\Phi
.
Then for any
\Delta^{(1)}\in\mathcal{P}_{n_{1}}
with children
(\Delta_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\Delta_{m_{1}}^{(1)})
, if
\Delta^{(2)}\in\mathcal{P}_{n_{2}}
where
\mathcal{V}(\Delta^{(1)})=\mathcal{V}(\Delta^{(2)})
has children
(\Delta_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,\Delta_{m_{2}}^{(2)})
, then
m_{1}=m_{2}
and for each
1\leq i\leq m
,

(1)

\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{i}^{(1)})=\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{i}^{(2)})
,

(2)

q(\Delta,\Delta_{i})=q(\Delta^{(1)},\Delta_{i}^{(2)})
, and

(3)

\diam(\Delta)/\diam(\Delta_{i})=\diam(\Delta^{\prime})/\diam(\Delta_{i}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

For each
j=1,2
, let
\mathcal{Y}_{j},Y_{j}
correspond to the set
\Delta^{(j)}
as in
Eq.

3.2
in the definition of children.
Then with
\psi=T_{\Delta^{(1)}}\circ T_{\Delta^{(2)}}^{-1}
, we have
\mathcal{Y}_{1}=\{\psi\circ g:g\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}\}
and
\psi(Y_{2})=Y_{1}
.
Thus with the elements of
Y_{1}
in order as
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{k+1}
, the elements of
Y_{2}
are given in order as
\psi(y_{1})<\cdots<\psi(y_{k+1})
.

Now since

(\Delta^{(j)})^{\circ}\cap K=(\Delta^{(j)})^{\circ}\cap\bigcup_{f\in v}T_{\Delta^{(j)}}\circ f(K)

from
Lemma

3.4
, it follows that
\psi:\Delta^{(2)}\cap K\to\Delta^{(1)}\cap K
is a surjection so that
(y_{i},y_{i+1})\cap K\neq\emptyset
if and only if
(\psi(y_{i}),\psi(y_{i+1}))\cap K\neq\emptyset
.
Thus
m_{1}=m_{2}
.

From here, (i), (ii), and (iii) follow by direct computation.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Sometimes, it can hold that
(\Delta,v)
has a unique child
(\Delta^{\prime},v^{\prime})
where
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
.
For technical purposes, in order to avoid this degenerate situation, it is convenient to redefine the iteration rule
\Phi
as follows.
Write
v=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
,
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{k})
, and suppose
\Phi(v)=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{m})
and
\Phi(v^{\prime})=(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\prime})
.
Since
\Delta=\Delta^{\prime}
, for each
f_{i}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
, either
f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}=\emptyset
or
f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}=g_{j}
for some
j
.
Now for each
1\leq i\leq m
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
, set

\mathcal{U}_{i,\sigma}=\begin{cases}\{\emptyset\}&:f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}=\emptyset\\
\mathcal{C}_{j}&:f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}=g_{j}\end{cases}

and define

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{i}=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\{\sigma\tau:\tau\in\mathcal{U}_{i,\sigma}\}.

Then define
\widetilde{\Phi}
by
\widetilde{\Phi}(v)=(\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m})
, and
\widetilde{\Phi}=\Phi
otherwise.
It is straightforward to verify that
\widetilde{\Phi}
is an iteration rule, and with this definition, the children of
(\Delta,v)
with respect to
\widetilde{\Phi}
are precisely the children of
(\Delta^{\prime},v^{\prime})
with respect to
\Phi
.

Note that an infinite sequence of children where all the net intervals are identical is disallowed by (i) in
Definition

3.3
.
Repeating this construction, we may thus assume that each net interval
\Delta
has at least two distinct children, and for any
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}
, there is a unique
n
such that
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

We conclude with two examples explaining the relationship with our general net interval construction and earlier net interval constructions.
In practice, all iteration rules the author has used fall into these two classes.

Example 3.7
.

As discussed, the rule

\Phi(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})=(\mathcal{I},\ldots,\mathcal{I})

always defines an iteration rule.
Here, the neighbour sets and net intervals can be described in a slightly different way.
Enumerate the points

(3.5)

\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}

(3.6)

\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=\{T_{\Delta}^{-1}\circ S_{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{n},S_{\sigma}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}\neq\emptyset\}.

Let us prove that this holds by induction.
When

Eq.

3.5
and

both hold trivially.
Thus suppose

From the definition in

3.2

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}

and therefore

Thus the children of

3.5
, and if
\Delta_{i}

, from the definition

3.3
it has neighbour set

\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{i})=\{T_{\Delta_{i}}^{-1}\circ g:g\in\mathcal{Y},g(K)\cap\Delta_{i}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset\}=\{T_{\Delta_{i}}^{-1}\circ S_{\tau}:\tau\in\mathcal{I}^{n+1},S_{\tau}(K)\cap\Delta_{i}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset\}

and

holds for

.
Since any net interval

, every net interval in

Thus

3.5
and

hold for

.

If each

for some fixed

]
, and our definition of a neighbour set is closely related to the characteristic vector defined in that paper.

for more details on this relationship.

Example 3.8
.

Given a tuple of similarities

with each

, let

.

.

3.2.
The transition graph and symbolic representations

We begin by introducing some useful terminology from graph theory.
By a rooted graph
\mathcal{G}
, we mean a directed graph (possibly with loops and multiple edges) consisting of a set
V(\mathcal{G})
of vertices with a distinguished vertex
v_{\operatorname{root}}\in V(\mathcal{G})
, and a set
E(\mathcal{G})
of edges.
By an
edge

e
, we mean a triple
e=(v_{1},v_{2},q)
where
v_{1}\in V(\mathcal{G})
is the
source
,
v_{2}\in V(\mathcal{G})
is the
target
, and
q
is the
label
of the edge
e
.
The point of the label is to distinguish multiple edges, but it is safe to imagine that the graph does not have multiple edges.

A
finite path
in
\mathcal{G}
is a sequence
\eta=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
of edges in
\mathcal{G}
such that the target of each
e_{i}
is the source of
e_{i+1}
.
We say that the
length
of
\eta
is
n
, and denote this by
|\eta|
.
A finite path is a
cycle
if, in addition, the source of
e_{1}
is the target of
e_{n}
.
A (one-way) infinite path is a sequence
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
where the target of each
e_{i}
is the source of
e_{i+1}
for
i\in\N
.
Given paths
\eta_{1}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
and
\eta_{2}=(e_{n+1},\ldots,e_{n+m})
, if the target of
e_{n}
is the source of
e_{n+1}
, the
concatenation

\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
is the path
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+m})
.
When it is convenient, we will abuse notation and treat edges as paths of length
1
.

We say that a (finite or infinite) path is
rooted
if it begins at the root vertex
v_{\operatorname{root}}
, and we denote by
\Omega^{\infty}
(resp.
\Omega^{*}
) the set of all infinite (resp. finite) rooted paths.
For any
n\in\N\cup\{0\}
,
\Omega^{n}
denotes the set of all rooted paths of length
n
.
We say that
\eta_{1}
is a
prefix
of
\eta
in
\Omega^{*}
(resp.
\Omega^{\infty}
) if
\eta=\eta_{1}\eta^{\prime}
for some finite (resp. infinite) path
\eta^{\prime}
.
Given a path
\gamma=(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega^{\infty}
, we denote the unique prefix of
\gamma
in
\Omega^{n}
by
\gamma|n=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
.

We now define the main object in consideration in this document.
Fix a WIFS
(S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
along with an iteration rule
\Phi
.
Then the
transition graph

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}\bigl((S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\Phi\bigr)
, is a rooted graph defined as follows.
The vertex set of
\mathcal{G}
is the set of all neighbour sets
\{v:{(\Delta,v)\in\mathcal{V}}\}
with root vertex
v_{\operatorname{root}}=\{\id\}
corresponding to the net interval
[0,1]
.
Now whenever
(\Delta,v)
has child
(\Delta^{\prime},v^{\prime})
, we introduce an edge
(v,v^{\prime},q(\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}))
, where the position index
q(\Delta,\Delta^{\prime})
is the label distinguishing multiple edges between the vertices
v
and
v^{\prime}
.
This construction is well-defined by
Proposition

3.5
.
Given a vertex
v\in V(\mathcal{G})
, which is a neighbour set
v=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
, we write
d(v)=m
.
For the remainder of this document, the set
\Omega^{\infty}
(and
\Omega^{*}
,
\Omega^{n}
) will always be associated with the transition graph
\mathcal{G}

Now given a path
\eta=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\Omega^{n}
, there is a unique sequence of net intervals
(\Delta_{i},v_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
with
\Delta_{0}=[0,1]
where each
(\Delta_{i},v_{i})\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
,
\Delta_{i+1}
is the child of
\Delta_{i}
, and

e_{i}=\bigl(v_{i},v_{i+1},q(\Delta_{i},\Delta_{i+1})\bigr).

This follows directly by construction of the edge set of the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.
Since net intervals either coincide or overlap only on endpoints, this path is uniquely determined by the last net interval in the sequence.
Thus we may define a map
\pi:\Omega^{n}\to\mathcal{P}_{n}
by
\pi(\eta)=\Delta_{n}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

The map
\pi:\Omega^{n}\to\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a well-defined bijection for each
n\in\N\cup\{0\}
.

Given a net interval
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the
symbolic representation
of
\Delta
is the path
\pi^{-1}(\Delta)\in\Omega^{n}
.

Now given an infinite path
\gamma=(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega^{\infty}
, there corresponds a sequence of net intervals
(\Delta_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
with
\Delta_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
where
\Delta_{0}=[0,1]
and
\Delta_{i+1}
is a the child of
\Delta_{i}
corresponding to the edge
e_{i+1}
.
Of course,
\Delta_{n}=\pi(\gamma|n)
.
Since
\lim_{i\to\infty}\diam(\Delta_{i})=0
, there exists a unique point in
K
, which we call
\pi(\gamma)
, satisfying

\{\pi(\gamma)\}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{i}.

In analogy to the net interval case, we refer to a path
\gamma\in\pi^{-1}(x)
as a
symbolic representation
of
x
.
It is clear by construction of net intervals that the map
\pi:\Omega^{\infty}\to K
is surjective.
Note that
\pi
need not be injective, but if
x\in K
has fibre
\pi^{-1}(x)
with cardinality greater than 1, then
x
must be an endpoint of some net interval
\Delta
.
In this situation,
\pi^{-1}(x)
contains two paths.
Since there are only countably many net intervals,
\pi
is injective on all but at most countably many paths.
We say that
x
is an
interior point
of
K
if
\pi^{-1}(x)
has cardinality 1.

3.3.
Edge weights and transition matrices

For our purposes, perhaps the two most important attributes of a net interval
\Delta
are its diameter
\diam(\Delta)
and measure
\mu(\Delta)
.
Moreover, recall that we have a correspondence
\pi:\Omega^{n}\to\mathcal{P}_{n}
taking rooted paths in the transition graph to net intervals in
\R
.
Through this correspondence, we get the corresponding “symbolic diameter”
\diam\circ\pi
and “symbolic measure”
\mu\circ\pi
defined on the set of rooted finite paths
\Omega^{*}
.
In this section, we will define two natural objects which takes values on
E(\mathcal{G})
which will allow us to encode the functions
\diam\circ\pi
and
\mu\circ\pi
respectively in a way intrinsic to the transition graph.

We first describe
\diam\circ\pi
as a product of weights on edges.

Definition 3.10
.

The
edge weight function
for
\mathcal{G}
is the map
W:E(\mathcal{G})\to(0,1)
such that if the edge
e
corresponds to the child
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta
, then
W(e)=\diam(\Delta^{\prime})/\diam(\Delta)
.
Given a path
\eta=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
, we write
W(\eta)=W(e_{1})\cdots W(e_{n})
.

Note that the edge weight is well-defined by
Proposition

3.5
(see also
Remark

3.6
).
Of course, when
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
has symbolic representation
\eta=(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,

\diam(\Delta)=\diam(\pi(\eta))=W(e_{1})\cdots W(e_{n})=W(\eta),

so that
\diam\circ\pi=W
.

We now describe
\mu\circ\pi
as the norm of products of matrices associated with edges.
Let
e\in E(\mathcal{G})
be an edge corresponding to
(\Delta_{1},(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}))
the parent of
(\Delta_{2},(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}))
, and let
\Phi(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})=(\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{m})
where
\Phi
is the iteration rule.
For each
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\times\{1,\ldots,n\}
, set

\mathcal{E}_{i,j}=\{\omega\in\mathcal{C}_{i}:T_{\Delta_{1}}\circ f_{i}\circ S_{\omega}=T_{\Delta_{2}}\circ g_{j}\}.

Then the
transition matrix
is the
n\times m
matrix
T(e)
given by

(3.7)

T(e)_{i,j}=\frac{f_{i}\mu((0,1))}{g_{j}\mu((0,1))}\cdot\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{E}_{i,j}}p_{\omega}

where we recall that
f\mu
is the pushforward of
\mu
by the function
f
.
It is clear that the transition matrix depends only on the edge
e
.
We note the following important observations.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
e\in E(\mathcal{G})
is any edge, then
T(e)
has a positive entry in each column.
Moreover, for any
v\in V(\mathcal{G})
and
1\leq i\leq d(v)
, there is an edge
e
with source
v
such that
T(e)
has a positive entry in row
i
.

Proof.

Since each neighbour
g_{j}
is of the form
T_{\Delta_{2}}\circ T_{\Delta_{1}}^{-1}\circ f_{i}\circ S_{\sigma}
for some
i
and (possibly empty) word
\sigma
, each column of
T(e)
has a non-negative entry.

To see the second part, let
\Delta
be a net interval with
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v
.
Since
T_{\Delta}((0,1))\cap f_{i}(K)\neq\emptyset
for each
1\leq i\leq m
, there is some child
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta
such that
T_{\Delta^{\prime}}((0,1))\cap f_{i}(K)\neq\emptyset
.
Then if
e
is the edge corresponding to
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta
,
T(e)
has a positive entry in row
i
by
Lemma

3.4
and the definition of the transition matrix.
∎

Given a path
\eta=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
, we write
T(\eta)=T(e_{1})\cdots T(e_{n})
.
We write
\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert=\sum_{i,j}T(\eta)_{i,j}
to denote the matrix
1
-norm.

Now fix a pair
(\Delta,(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}))\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
, let
Q(\Delta)=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m})
where

(3.8)

q_{i}=f_{i}\mu((0,1))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{*}\\
S_{\sigma}=T_{\Delta}\circ f_{i}\end{subarray}}p_{\sigma}.

Using the self-similarity relation of
\mu
, the definition of the iteration rule
\Phi
, and condition (ii) in
Definition

3.3
, one can verify that

\mu(\Delta)=\left\lVert Q(\Delta)\right\rVert.

Now a similar argument as the proof of
[
33
, Thm. 2.12]
gives the following result:

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
have associated self-similar measure
\mu
and fix an iteration rule
\Phi
.
Then
Q\circ\pi=T
for every
n\in\N
, so if
\eta\in\Omega^{n}
,

\mu(\pi(\eta))=\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert.

Proof.

Let
\Delta=\pi(\eta)
and let
\eta
end at the vertex
v
.
Given
(\Delta,v)\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
, there exists a unique sequence
(\Delta_{i},v_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
where
\Delta_{i}\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
,
\Delta_{i+1}
is a child of
\Delta_{i}
, and
\Delta_{n}=\Delta
.
Now for
f\in v
, let
T_{\Delta}\circ f=S_{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
.
Then one can write
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
if and only if
\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{j(i)}
where
j(i)
satisfies
T_{\Delta_{i}}^{-1}\circ f^{(i)}_{j(i)}=S_{\sigma_{i}}
with
v_{i}=(f^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,f^{(i)}_{m_{i}})
.
Thus the entry of
T(\eta)
corresponding to the index
f
is the sum of
p_{\sigma}
over all
\sigma
satisfying
T_{\Delta}\circ f=S_{\sigma}
.
∎

We observe that the transition matrices are analgous to the role of the probabilities
(p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
described in
Eq.

3.1
.

We conclude by mentioning the following straightforward but important property of transition matrices.

Lemma 3.13
.

If
\eta=\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\in\Omega^{*}
with
\eta_{1}\in\Omega^{n}
, then
\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert\asymp_{n}\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.11
, every transition matrix has a non-zero entry in each column, so a straightforward calculation shows that there exists some constant
a=a(\eta_{1})
such that
\left\lVert T(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})\right\rVert\geq a(\eta_{1})\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert
.
On the other hand,
\left\lVert T(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert
by submultiplicativity of the matrix norm.
But there are only finitely many paths in
\Omega^{n}
, giving the result.
∎

3.4.
The finite neighbour condition

Throughout this section, we have made no assumptions about the IFS
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
or the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.
We now introduce the main restriction of this paper.

The finite neighbour condition was introduced in
[
17
]
as a variation of the generalized finite type condition introduced by Lau and Ngai
[
25
]
.
In general, such “finite type” conditions attempt to capture the idea that an IFS only has finitely many possible overlaps.
It is known that the finite neighbour condition is equivalent to the generalized finite type condition holding with respect to the interval
(0,1)

[
17
]
.
We introduce the following definition, which is a natural generalization of the usual finite neighbour condition with respect to our more general transition graph construction.

Definition 3.14
.

We say that the IFS
(S_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
satisfies the
finite neighbour condition with respect to the iteration rule
\Phi
, or the
\Phi
-FNC for short, if the corresponding transition graph is a finite graph.

Closely related to this finite neighbour condition is the
weak separation condition
.
This separation condition is satisfied if

(3.9)

\sup_{x\in K,r>0}\#\{S_{\sigma}:r\cdot r_{\min}<|r_{\sigma}|\leq r,S_{\sigma}(K)\cap(x-r,x+r)\neq\emptyset\}<\infty.

The weak separation condition was introduced by Lau and Ngai in
[
24
]
; this definition is not the original but equivalent by
[
37
, Thm. 1]
.
Standard arguments show that any IFS satisfying the
\Phi
-FNC necessarily satisfies the weak separation condition (see, for example,
[
17
,
25
]
).
Moreover, when
K
is a convex set, the weak separation condition implies that the
\Phi
-FNC holds with respect to the iteration rule
\Phi
from
Example

3.8

[
17
]
.

4.
Loop classes, irreducibility, and decomposability

In this section, we introduce the notion of a loop class of the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
, and other related definitions.
These definitions are required to state the main technical assumptions (irreducibility and decomposability) which underpin the main results presented later in this paper.
We also discuss certain general situations in which the technical assumptions are satisfied.

For the remainder of the paper (including this section), we will assume that
(S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
satisfies the
\Phi
-FNC with finite transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.
Note that many concepts in this section hold more generally for an arbitrary transition graph
\mathcal{G}
, but we do not distinguish this during the subsequent discussions for sake of simplicity.

4.1.
Loop classes

Let
G
be a directed multigraph.
Recall that a graph
H
is an
induced subgraph
of
G
if
H
is the graph consisting of the vertices
V(H)
and any edge
e\in E(G)
such that
e
connects two vertices in
H
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
H
be an induced subgraph of
G
.
We say that
H
is
strongly connected
if for any vertices
v,w\in V(H)
, there is a directed path from
v
to
w
.
Then
H
is a
loop class
(in
G
) if it is strongly connected, contains at least one edge, and is maximal with these properties.

Now if
H
is a loop class, we say that
H
is
simple
if each vertex in
H
has exactly one outgoing edge (in
H
).
We say that
H
is
essential
if for any
v\in V(H)
and
w\in V(G)
, if there is a directed path from
v
to
w
, then
w\in V(H)
as well.

Of course, any essential loop class is necessarily not simple.
Note that distinct loop classes have disjoint vertex and edge sets, but there may be vertices which do not belong to any loop class.

Remark 4.2
.

Previous authors (e.g.
[
16
]
) distinguished between loop classes and maximal loop classes.
In this document, our loop classes are always maximal.

Example 4.3
.

In
Fig.

1
, the loop classes are given by
\{\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{3},\mathcal{L}_{4}\}
.
The loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
are simple, while
\mathcal{L}_{3}
is not; and
\mathcal{L}_{4}
, being an essential loop class, is not simple.

Since the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is a finite graph, there are only finitely many loop classes.
Given any path
\gamma=(e_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega^{\infty}
, there is a unique loop class
\mathcal{L}
such that there is some
N
such that for all
k\geq N
,
e_{k}
is an edge in
\mathcal{L}
.
We say that
\gamma
is
eventually in
\mathcal{L}
and denote the set of all such
\gamma
by
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}
.

We may now set

\displaystyle K_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\{x\in K:\pi^{-1}(x)\cap\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\{x\in K:\pi^{-1}(x)\subseteq\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}\}.

Of course, for each
x\in K
there is at least one loop class
\mathcal{L}
such that
x\in K_{\mathcal{L}}
, and at most two such sets.
Note that
K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}
is the topological interior of
K_{\mathcal{L}}
(relative to
K
) if and only if
\mathcal{L}
is an essential loop class.
If
x\in K
is an interior point, then
x\in K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}
for a unique loop class
\mathcal{L}
.

Our analysis is focused on two technical assumptions, which we call irreducibility and decomposability.
We discuss these assumptions in the following two sections.

4.2.
Irreducibility

Irreducibility can be loosely interpreted as a type of “measure connectivity” within the loop class.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a loop class.
We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
irreducible
if there exists a finite set of paths
\mathcal{H}
such that for any paths
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
in
\mathcal{L}
, there is some
\gamma\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\eta_{1}\gamma\eta_{2}
is a path and

\left\lVert T(\eta_{1}\gamma\eta_{2})\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert.

We say that the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is
irreducible
if every loop class is irreducible.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is a finite graph, by submultiplicativity of the matrix norm, one can always guarantee that

\left\lVert T(\eta_{1}\gamma\eta_{2})\right\rVert\preccurlyeq\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert.

On the other hand, establishing the lower inequality is more challenging.
This notion of irreducibility is motivated by various hypotheses studied by past authors
[
7
,
20
]
.
We are not aware of any loop class of any IFS satisfying the finite neighbour condition that does not satisfy this irreducibility hypothesis.

In the following lemmas, we observe that this technical hypothesis is satisfied in a number of general cases.

Enumerate
V(\mathcal{L})=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
.
For each
1\leq i,j\leq k
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\{e\in E(G):e\text{ is an edge from }v_{i}\text{ to }v_{j}\}

\displaystyle M_{i,j}=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{A}_{i,j}}T(e)

and define the block matrix

M=M(\mathcal{L}):=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1,1}&\cdots&M_{1,k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{k,1}&\cdots&M_{k,k}\end{pmatrix}

The following proof is straightforward and included in, say,
[
20
]
.
Recall the matrix
M
is irreducible if for each
i,j
, there exists some
n=n(i,j)
such that
(M^{n})_{i,j}>0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose the matrix
M
is irreducible.
Then
\mathcal{L}
is an irreducible loop class.

Proof.

It suffices to show for any vertices
v,w\in V(\mathcal{L})
,
1\leq i\leq d(v)
, and
1\leq j\leq d(w)
, there exists some path
\gamma
from
v
to
w
such that
T(\gamma)_{i,j}>0
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the finite set of paths in
Definition

4.4
and let
A
be the smallest strictly positive entry for any
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
and let
d_{\max}=\max\{d(v):v\in V(\mathcal{L})\}
(which is also the maximum number of rows or columns of any transition matrix
T(e)
where
e\in E(\mathcal{L})
).
Let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
be any finite paths in
\mathcal{L}
.
By the pidgeonhole principle, there exists some
k,i,j,\ell
such that
T(\eta_{1})_{k,i}\geq d_{\max}^{-2}\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert
and
T(\eta_{2})_{j,\ell}\geq d_{\max}^{-2}\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert
.
Let
\phi\in\mathcal{H}
be a path from the target vertex of
\eta_{1}
to the source vertex of
\eta_{2}
such that
T(\phi)_{i,j}\geq A>0
.
Then

\left\lVert T(\eta_{1}\phi\eta_{2})\right\rVert\geq T(\eta_{1})_{k,i}T(\phi)_{i,j}T(\eta_{2})_{j,\ell}\geq Ad_{\max}^{-4}\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert.

The upper bound follows since

\left\lVert T(\eta_{1}\phi\eta_{2})\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert\max\{\left\lVert T(\phi)\right\rVert:\phi\in\mathcal{H}\}

by submultiplicativity of the norm.
∎

We next observe that an essential loop class is always irreducible.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an essential loop class of
\mathcal{G}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}
is irreducible.

Proof.

In fact, we will show for any
v,w\in V(\mathcal{L})
and
1\leq i\leq d(v)
, there exists some path
\gamma
from
v
to
w
such that row
i
of
T(\gamma)
is strictly positive.
The required result will then follow by
Lemma

4.5
.

Let
\Delta
be any net interval with
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v
and neighbour set
v=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d(v)})
.
Since
T_{\Delta}\circ f_{i}=S_{\sigma_{0}}
for some
\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
with
S_{\sigma_{0}}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
, there exists some word
\sigma
with prefix
\sigma_{0}
such that
S_{\sigma}\subseteq\Delta
.
Let
U=(x-r,x+r)
attain the supremum in
Eq.

3.9
with words
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{\ell}
satisfying
r\cdot r_{\min}<|r_{\tau_{k}}|\leq r
and
S_{\tau_{k}}(K)\cap U\neq\emptyset
.
Observe that
S_{\sigma}(U)
also attains the supremum in
Eq.

3.9
with words
\sigma\tau_{1},\ldots,\sigma\tau_{\ell}
.
By condition (i) in
Definition

3.3
and since
\mathcal{L}
is an essential loop class, there exists some net interval
\Delta_{1}\subseteq S_{\sigma}(U)
with
\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{1})=w
such that if
g
is any neighbour of
\Delta_{1}
, then the contraction ratio
T_{\Delta_{1}}\circ g
is less than
|r_{\sigma}|r
.
Let
\gamma
be the path corresponding to
\Delta_{1}\subseteq\Delta
, which is necessarily a path from
v
to
w
in
\mathcal{L}
.

It remains to show that row
i
of
T(\gamma)
is strictly positive.
Let
g\in\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{1})
be arbitrary and let
S_{\omega}=T_{\Delta_{1}}\circ g
; by choice of
\Delta_{1}
, we have
|r_{\omega}|\leq|r_{\sigma}|r
.
Since
S_{\omega}(K)\cap\Delta^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
, we have
S_{\omega}(K)\cap S_{\sigma}(U)\neq\emptyset
.
Let
\xi
be the unique prefix of
\omega
with minimal length satisfying
|r_{\xi}|\leq|r_{\sigma}|r
.
In particular,
|r_{\xi}|>|r_{\sigma}|r\cdot r_{\min}
and
S_{\xi}(K)\cap S_{\sigma}(U)\neq\emptyset
, forcing
S_{\xi}=S_{\sigma\tau_{j}}
for some

by maximality of

.
Unpacking definitions, this means that there is some word
\phi
such that

T_{\Delta_{1}}\circ g=S_{\sigma_{0}}\circ S_{\phi}=T_{\Delta}\circ f_{i}\circ S_{\phi}.

In other words, the entry in
T(\gamma)
corresponding to the neighbours

of

and
g
of

is strictly positive.
Since

was an arbitrary neighbour of
\Delta_{1}
, the result follows.
∎

4.3.
Decomposability

Unlike irreducibility which, up to a fixed constant multiple, states that one can join paths within a loop class without changing the norm of the corresponding transition matrix, decomposability states that for a given path passing through multiple loop classes, the norm is comparable to the norms of the components of the path within each loop class it passes through.

We begin by defining the notion of an
initial path
and a
transition path
in the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
as follows.
Let
\mathcal{G}
have loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
and root vertex
v_{\operatorname{root}}
.
Let
\psi=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
be a path in
\mathcal{G}
connecting vertices
(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
.
We say a path
\psi
is a
transition path
if

(1)

v_{0}
is a vertex in
V(\mathcal{L}_{j})
for some
j
,

(2)

v_{n}
is a vertex in
V(\mathcal{L}_{k})
for some
k\neq j
, and

(3)

each
v_{i}
with
0<i<n
is not a vertex in any loop class.

Similarly, we say that
\psi
is an
initial path
if we replace condition (1) with

(1’)

v_{0}=v_{\operatorname{root}}

There are only finitely many initial paths and transition paths since they cannot repeat vertices.

By definition of the loop class, we can sort the loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
in a (not necessarily unique) order such that if
\psi
is any transition path joining loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{i}
and
\mathcal{L}_{j}
, then
i<j
.
Now suppose
\eta=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\Omega^{*}
is any finite rooted path.
Then we can uniquely write

\eta=\phi\lambda_{1}\psi_{1}\ldots\psi_{m-1}\lambda_{m}

for possibly empty paths
\phi,\psi_{i},\lambda_{i}
, where
\phi
is an initial path, each
\lambda_{i}
is a path in
\mathcal{L}_{i}
, and each
\psi_{i}
is a transition path.
We call the tuple
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
the
decomposition
of the path
\eta
.

Example 4.7
.

In
Fig.

1
, an example of a valid order is
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{3},\mathcal{L}_{4}
.
Note that any decomposition can contain a maximum of
3
non-empty paths
\lambda_{i}
(corresponding to the loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{3},\mathcal{L}_{4}
).

By convention, if
\lambda_{i}
is an empty path, we write
\left\lVert T(\lambda_{i})\right\rVert=1
.

Definition 4.8
.

We say that the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is
decomposable
if for any path
\eta\in\Omega^{*}
with decomposition
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
, we have

\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert T(\lambda_{1})\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert T(\lambda_{m})\right\rVert

with constants depending only on the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.

We now discuss a few examples in which the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is decomposable.

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose every transition path
\eta
has that
T(\eta)
is a strictly positive matrix.
Then
\mathcal{G}
is decomposable.

Proof.

Since there are only finitely many transition paths, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any transition path
\psi
and valid indices
i,j
,
T(\eta)_{i,j}\geq C
.
But now if
\eta\in\Omega^{*}
has decomposition
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
, we can uniquely write
\eta=\phi\lambda_{1}\psi_{1}\ldots\psi_{m-1}\lambda_{m}
where each
\psi_{i}
is a transition path.
Then by
Lemma

3.13
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert T(\phi\lambda_{1}\psi_{1}\ldots\psi_{m-1}\lambda_{m})\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert T(\lambda_{1}\psi_{1}\ldots\psi_{m-1}\lambda_{m})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq C^{m-1}\left\lVert T(\lambda_{1})\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert T(\lambda_{m})\right\rVert.

Of course,
C
and
m
depend only on
\mathcal{G}
, so the lower bound holds.
The upper bound always follows by submultiplicativity of the matrix norm, since there are only finitely many choices for the paths
\phi
,
\psi_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose that every vertex in a non-essential loop class is a neighbour set of size one.
Then
\mathcal{G}
is decomposable.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.9
, it suffices to show for any transition path
\eta
that
T(\eta)
is a strictly positive matrix.
By definition of an essential loop class, if
\eta
is a transition path from loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{i}
to
\mathcal{L}_{j}
, then
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is non-essential.
Thus by assumption,
\eta
is a path beginning at a vertex with neighbour set consisting of a single neighbour, so that
T(\eta)
is a matrix with 1 row.
Since every transition matrix has a positive entry in every column by
Lemma

3.11
,
T(\eta)
is a strictly positive matrix, so the result follows by
Lemma

4.9

∎

We now establish an irreducibility type condition to guarantee that the transition graph is decomposable when all non-essential loop classes are simple.

We begin with some general observations about non-negative irreducible matrices.
Let
M
be an irreducible matrix with spectral radius
r
.
It is known that if
M
is a strictly positive matrix, then the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}M^{k}/r^{k}
exists
[
34
]
.
While this limit need not exist in general if
M
is irreducible, using similar arguments, one can show that there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all
n
sufficiently large, either
M^{n}_{i,j}=0
or

c_{1}r^{n}\leq M^{n}_{i,j}\leq c_{2}r^{n}.

In particular, suppose
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}
are irreducible matrices and
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k+1}
are such that
A=A_{1}M_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots A_{k}M_{k}^{n_{k}}A_{k+1}\neq 0
.
Then for all
n_{i}
sufficiently large, if
M_{i}
has spectral radius
r_{i}
, either
A_{k,\ell}=0
or

(4.1)

A_{j,\ell}\asymp_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k+1}}r_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots r_{k}^{n_{k}}.

This observation is the main idea in the following result.

Lemma 4.11
.

Suppose every non-essential loop class is simple.
For each simple loop class
\mathcal{L}
, suppose there is a path
\theta
in
\mathcal{L}
beginning and ending at the same vertex such that
T(\theta)
is an irreducible matrix.
Then
\mathcal{G}
is decomposable.

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume there is a unique essental class; the proof in the general case follows similarly.
Denote the simple loop classes by
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
, and for each
1\leq i\leq k
, let
\theta_{i}
be a cycle in
V(\mathcal{L}_{i})
such that
T(\theta_{i})
is an irreducible matrix.
Let
T(\theta_{i})
have spectral radius
r_{i}
.

If
\eta\in\Omega^{*}
is an arbitrary path, it has decomposition of the form
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},\xi)
where
\lambda_{i}=\gamma_{i}^{(1)}\theta_{i}^{n_{i}}\gamma_{i}^{(2)}
with
n_{i}
maximal and
\xi
is a path in the essential loop class.
Since
n_{i}
is maximal and
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is simple, the paths
\gamma_{i}^{(1)}
and
\gamma_{i}^{(2)}
have length at most the length of
\theta
, so there are only finitely many possible paths
\gamma_{i}^{(j)}
.
Thus by
Eq.

4.1
,

\left\lVert T(\lambda_{i})\right\rVert\asymp r_{i}^{n_{i}}.

Now, we may write

\eta=\phi\theta_{1}^{n_{1}}\psi_{1}\theta_{2}^{n_{2}}\ldots\theta_{k}^{n_{k}}\psi_{k}\xi

where
\phi=\phi^{\prime}\gamma_{1}^{(1)}
with
\phi^{\prime}
an initial path, and each
\psi_{i}
is of the form
\gamma_{i}^{(2)}\psi_{i}^{\prime}\gamma_{i+1}^{(1)}
for
i<k
or
\gamma_{k}^{(2)}\psi_{k}^{\prime}
, and the paths
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
are transition paths.
Of course, some of the paths
\gamma_{i}^{(j)}
or
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
may be the empty path.
The point here is that there are only finitely many possible choices for the paths
\phi,\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}
, independent of the choice of
\eta
.

It always holds that
\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert\preccurlyeq\left\lVert T(\lambda_{1})\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert T(\lambda_{k})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\xi)\right\rVert
.
Thus it suffices to show that

\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert\succcurlyeq r_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots r_{k}^{n_{k}}\left\lVert T(\xi)\right\rVert.

Let
M=T(\phi\theta_{1}^{n_{1}}\psi_{1}\theta_{2}^{n_{2}}\ldots\theta_{k}^{n_{k}}\psi_{k})
.
By
Eq.

4.1
, for each index
j,\ell
, either
M_{j,\ell}=0
or

M_{j,\ell}\asymp r_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots r_{k}^{n_{k}}.

But now if
T(\xi)
has maximal entry
T(\xi)_{p,q}
, we have
\left\lVert T(\xi)\right\rVert\succcurlyeq T(\xi)_{p,q}
.
Since column
p
of the matrix
M
has a non-negative entry by
Lemma

3.11
, get
p^{\prime}
such that
M_{p^{\prime},p}\succcurlyeq r_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots r_{k}^{n_{k}}
and

\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert=\left\lVert M\cdot T(\xi)\right\rVert\geq M_{p^{\prime},p}T(\xi)_{p,q}\succcurlyeq r_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots r_{k}^{n_{k}}\left\lVert T(\xi)\right\rVert

as required.
∎

5.
Loop class spectra and a multifractal formalism

5.1.
Measures and metric structure on paths in the transition graph

The set
\Omega^{\infty}
of infinite rooted paths has a natural metric space structure given by the weights.
Given paths
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, define

d(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\inf\{W(\eta):\eta\text{ a prefix of }\gamma_{1}\text{ and }\gamma_{2}\}.

The topology is generated by the closed and open cylinders

[\eta]:=\{\gamma\in\Omega^{*}:\eta\text{ a prefix of }\gamma\}.

It is easy to see that this space is compact and totally disconnected.
Of course,
\pi([\eta])=\pi(\eta)\cap K
where we recall that
\pi(\eta)
is the net interval with symbolic representation
\eta
.
It is productive to interpret the space
\Omega^{\infty}
with the above metric as a “separated” version of the set
K
.

We have the following straightforward result:

Lemma 5.1
.

The map
\pi:\Omega^{\infty}\to K
is Lipschitz with constant
1
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be two distinct paths in
\Omega^{\infty}
with maximal common prefix
\eta\in\Omega^{n}
, so that
d(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=W(\eta)
.
Let
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
have symbolic representation
\eta
.
By definition,
W(\eta)=\diam(\Delta)
.
But then
\pi(\gamma_{1}),\pi(\gamma_{2})\in\Delta
so

|\pi(\gamma_{1})-\pi(\gamma_{2})|\leq\diam(\Delta)=W(\eta)=d(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

as required.
∎

Our general philosophy is to establish multifractal properties of the space
\Omega^{\infty}
(in terms of the corresponding subspaces
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
defined below), and then translate these results to the self-similar measure
\mu
.
However, the main difficulty in establishing corresponding multifractal results is that, in general, the map
\pi
is not in general bi-Lipschitz (even when restricted to the maximal domain on which it is injective).
Many of the technical results in the following sections are established to overcome this.

Since the graph
\mathcal{G}
is not in general strongly connected, we will study subspaces of
\Omega^{\infty}
corresponding to the loop classes.
Fix a loop class
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
be a fixed path which ends at a vertex
v
in
\mathcal{L}
.
Recall that
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}
is the set of infinite paths eventually in the loop class
\mathcal{L}
.
Now, define the set

\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}:=\{\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}:\zeta\text{ is a prefix of }\gamma\}

This is a compact subspace of
\Omega^{\infty}
(note that the sets
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}
need not be compact).
We also define the analgous sets

\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}:=\{\eta\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L}}:\zeta\text{ is a prefix of }\eta\}

consisting of finite, rather than infinite, paths.
Often, given
\eta\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
, we will abuse notation and write
[\eta]
to denote the cylinder
[\eta]\cap\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}\subseteq\Omega^{\infty}
.

Suppose
\Delta=\pi(\zeta)
is the net interval with symblic representation
\zeta
.
Then one can verify that

\pi\bigl(\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}\bigr)=K_{\mathcal{L}}\cap\Delta.

We now turn our attention to the measure
\mu
.
Since distinct net intervals in the same level overlap only on endpoints and the self-similar measure
\mu
is non-atomic, one can verify that the rule

\mu(\pi(\eta))=\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert

for paths
\eta\in\Omega^{*}
extends to a unique Borel measure on
\Omega^{\infty}
.
We would like to restrict this measure
\mu\circ\pi
to the subsets
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
in a meaningful way.
However, these sets can have measure
0
in
\Omega^{\infty}
(in fact, they have non-zero measure if and only if
\mathcal{L}
is an essential loop class).

Regardless, it is convenient to simply consider the measure
\mu\circ\pi
as being defined on finite rooted paths (or the corresponding cylinders).
With this in mind, we define a function
\rho:\Omega^{*}\to[0,1]
by the rule

\rho(\eta)=\mu\circ\pi(\eta).

Now
\rho
restricts naturally to a function
\rho:\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}\to[0,1]
, though this restriction is not in general additive.

5.1.1.
Loop class
L^{q}
-spectra

We now use the function
\rho
to define an analogue of the
L^{q}
-spectrum of measures for loop classes.

To motivate this, we first state an equivalent formulation of the
L^{q}
-spectrum of
\mu
using the function
\rho
.
Set

\mathcal{F}(t)=\bigl\{\eta=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\Omega^{*}:W(e_{1}\ldots e_{n})\leq t<W(e_{1}\ldots e_{n-1})\bigr\}.

One can think of the sets
\mathcal{F}(t)
as a “scale-uniform” analogue in
\Omega^{\infty}
of the partitions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
(which may contaimay contain intervals with vastly different diameters).
We then have the following standard result, which is a weighted version of
[
18
, Prop. 4.3]
or
[
5
, Prop. 5.6]
.
We include the main details for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mu
be a self-similar measure satisfying the finite neighbour condition.
Then

\tau_{\mu}(q)=\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t}

Proof.

First suppose
x\in K
and
t>0
is arbitrary, and let
\{B(x_{i},t)\}_{i}
be any centred packing of
K
.
If
\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)
has
x\in\pi(\eta)
, we always have
\pi(\eta)\subseteq B(x,t)
, so for
q<0

\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}\geq\sum_{i}\mu(B(x_{i},t))^{q}.

On the other hand, for
q\geq 0
, since there are only finitely many edge weights, there is some
N
such that
\#\{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t):\pi(\eta)\cap B(x,t)\neq\emptyset\}\leq N
.
Since a given net interval
\pi(\eta)
overlaps with at most 2 distinct balls in
\{B(x_{i},t)\}_{i}
, for
q\geq 0
by Jensen’s inequality

\sum_{i}\mu(B(x_{i},t))^{q}\leq\sum_{i}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)\\
\pi(\eta)\cap B(x_{i},t)\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(\eta)\Bigr)^{q}\preccurlyeq_{q}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}.

Thus

\tau_{\mu}(q)\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t}.

Conversely, suppose
\Delta=\pi(\eta)
is some net interval, where
\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)
.
If
f
is any neighbour of
\Delta
, since
f(K)\cap(0,1)\neq\emptyset
, there exists some word some
\epsilon>0
and
\tau\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
depending only on
f
such that
f\circ S_{\tau}(K)\subseteq(\epsilon,1-\epsilon)
and
0<r_{\tau}<\epsilon
.
Since there are only finitely many neighbour sets, and hence only finitely many neighbours, we may assume
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{0}>0
and
p_{\tau}\geq p_{0}>0
for some fixed
\epsilon_{0},p_{0}
.

Now by
Proposition

3.12
along with
Eq.

3.8
, there is some
M>0
fixed such that there is
f\in\mathcal{V}(\Delta)
satisfying

\rho(\eta)=\mu(\Delta)\leq M\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{*}\\
S_{\sigma}=T_{\Delta}\circ f\end{subarray}}p_{\sigma}.

But then by choice of
\tau
, with
x_{\eta}=T_{\Delta}\circ f\circ S_{\tau}(0)\in K
and
r_{\eta}=\epsilon|r_{\tau}|\diam(\Delta)
, we have
B(x_{\eta},r_{\eta})\subseteq\Delta
and

\rho(\eta)\geq\mu(B(x_{\eta},r_{\eta}))\geq\mu(T_{\Delta}\circ f\circ S_{\tau}(K))\succcurlyeq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{*}\\
S_{\sigma}=T_{\Delta}\circ f\end{subarray}}p_{\sigma}p_{\tau}\succcurlyeq\rho(\eta).

Thus the centred packing
\{B(x_{\eta},r_{\eta})\}_{\eta}
satisfies

\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\mu(B(x_{\eta},r_{\eta}))^{q}\asymp_{q}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}.

But
r_{\eta}\asymp t
, so for any
q\in\R
,

\tau_{\mu}(q)\leq\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t}.

This gives the desired result.
∎

When
q\geq 0
, for any “sufficiently uniform” (e.g. dyadic) partition of
K
, one may always define the
L^{q}
-spectrum of any finite measure with respect to such a partition (see, for example,
[
24
, Prop. 3.1]
).
When
q<0
, such an operation is more delicate since the intervals in a partition can intersect
K
on sets of disproportionately small
\mu
-measure.

Proposition

5.2
essentially states that the partitions
\{\pi(\eta):\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)\}
of
K
for
t>0
avoid this issue.

Now for
t>0
, and
\zeta
and
\mathcal{L}
defined as above, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(t)\cap\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}.

We then define

\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t}.

We have the following basic lemma.
The argument giving existence of the limit is similar to
[
7
, Lem. 2.2]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The function
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)
is a concave function of
q
, and the limit exists for any
q\in\R
.
Moreover, if
\zeta^{\prime}
is any other path ending in
\mathcal{L}
, then
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta^{\prime}}=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
.

Proof.

Concavity is a standard application of Hölder’s inequality.
We now see existence of the limit.
Write
A_{q}(t)=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}
.
All implicit constants below may depend on the choice of
\zeta
.

First suppose
q\geq 0
and set
r_{0}=W_{\min}\cdot\min\{W(\zeta\gamma_{w}):w\in V(\mathcal{L})\}
where
W_{\min}=\min\{W(e):e\in E(\mathcal{L})\}
.
Suppose
\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(r_{0}t_{1}t_{2})
, so we may write
\eta=\eta_{1}\phi
where
\eta_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t_{2})
.
If
\phi
begins at the vertex
w
, by choice of
r_{0}
write
\phi=\psi\phi_{0}
such that with
\eta_{2}=\zeta\gamma_{w}\psi
,
\eta_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t_{2})
.
Observe that
W(\phi_{0})\asymp r_{0}
so there are only finitely possible values of
\left\lVert T(\phi_{0})\right\rVert
.
Thus by
Lemma

3.13
we have
\left\lVert T(\psi)\right\rVert\asymp\rho(\eta_{2})
so that

\rho(\eta)\leq\rho(\eta_{1})\left\lVert T(\psi)\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\phi_{0})\right\rVert\preccurlyeq\rho(\eta_{1})\rho(\eta_{2}).

Thus
A_{q}(r_{0}t_{1}t_{2})\preccurlyeq_{q}A_{q}(t_{1})A_{q}(t_{2})
so the limit exists for
q\geq 0
by submultiplicativity.

Now suppose
q<0
.
Let
\zeta
end at the vertex
v\in V(\mathcal{L})
, and for each
w\in V(\mathcal{L})
, let
\gamma_{w}
be a path in
\mathcal{L}
from
v
to
w
.
Given
\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t_{i})
for
i=1,2
and
t_{i}
sufficiently small, write
\eta_{2}=\zeta\phi_{2}
so that
W(\phi_{2})\asymp t_{2}
and
\left\lVert T(\phi_{2})\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert T(\eta_{2})\right\rVert
by
Lemma

3.13
.
Then if the path
\eta_{1}
ends at the vertex
w
,
\eta_{1}\gamma_{w}\phi_{2}
is an admissible path with
W(\eta_{1}\gamma_{w}\phi_{2})\asymp t_{1}t_{2}
, so there exists some fixed
r_{0}>0
such that
W(\eta_{1}\gamma_{w}\phi_{2})\geq r_{0}t_{1}t_{2}
.
Thus get a path
\psi
with
W(\psi)\asymp r_{0}
such that
\eta_{1}\gamma_{w}\phi_{2}\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(r_{0}t_{1}t_{2})
, and by
Lemma

3.13

\rho(\eta_{1}\gamma_{w}\phi_{2}\psi)^{q}\succcurlyeq_{q}\bigl(\left\lVert T(\gamma_{w})\right\rVert\left\lVert T(\psi)\right\rVert\bigr)^{q}\cdot\rho(\eta_{1})^{q}\rho(\eta_{2})^{q}.

But
\mathcal{L}
is a finite graph (so there are only finitely many paths
\gamma_{w}
) and
W(\psi)\asymp r_{0}
(so there are only finitely many paths
\psi
).
Thus
A_{q}(t_{1})A_{q}(t_{2})\preccurlyeq_{q}A_{q}(r_{0}t_{1}t_{2})
and the limit exists for
q<0
by supermultiplicativity.

To see the final claim, suppose
\zeta
ends at the vertex
v
and
\zeta^{\prime}
ends at the vertex
v^{\prime}
where
v,v^{\prime}
are both vertices in
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\phi
be any path in
\mathcal{L}
from
v
to
v^{\prime}
.
Let
\Psi:\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta^{\prime})\to\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
be given by
\Psi(\zeta^{\prime}\eta)=\zeta\phi\eta
, and note that

(5.1)

\Psi(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta^{\prime}}(W(\zeta^{\prime})t))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(W(\zeta\phi)t).

Now if
\zeta^{\prime}\eta\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
, by
Lemma

3.13
,

\rho(\Psi(\zeta^{\prime}\eta))=\left\lVert T(\zeta\phi\eta)\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert\asymp\left\lVert T(\zeta^{\prime}\eta)\right\rVert=\rho(\zeta^{\prime}\eta)

and combining this with
Eq.

5.1
yields

\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta^{\prime}}(W(\zeta^{\prime})t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}\succcurlyeq_{q}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(W(\zeta\phi)t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}.

Since
\zeta
,
\zeta^{\prime}
, and
\phi
are fixed , it follows that
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)\geq\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta^{\prime}}(q)
.
The reverse inequality follow by the same argument with the roles of
\zeta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
swapped.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is an essential loop class of
\mathcal{G}
.
Then if
\Delta
is any net interval with neighbour set
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)\in V(\mathcal{L})
, with
\nu=\mu|_{\Delta}
, we have

\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\nu}(q).

In particular,
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mu}(q)
for any
q\geq 0
.

Proof.

This follows by the same argument as
Proposition

5.2
, observing that if
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
is a path ending in an essential loop class
\mathcal{L}
, then
\eta\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
if and only if
\eta\in\Omega^{*}
and
\zeta
is a prefix of
\eta
.

That
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mu}(q)
for

follows by standard arguments (see, for example,
[
11
, Prop. 3.1]
).
∎

Remark 5.5
.

In fact, using arguments similar to the proof of

, Thm. 4.5]
, one can show that if

and

.
In practice, with the standard choices of iteration rules given in

5.1.2.
Loop class local dimensions

Given an infinite path
\gamma=(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega^{\infty}
, recall that
\gamma|n=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\Omega^{n}
.
We then define

\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|n)}{\log W(\gamma|n)}

with similar definitions for the (upper) local dimension.
With this, we define

E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)=\{\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}:\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\alpha\}.

Now let
f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}:\R\to\R\cup\{-\infty\}
be given by

f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha):=\dim_{H}E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha).

Note that
E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)
may be the empty set; by convention, we write
\dim_{H}\emptyset=-\infty
.
Following the theme for
L^{q}
-spectra, we have the following easy result.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
\mathcal{L}
is a loop class and
\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}\in\Omega^{*}
both end in
\mathcal{L}
, then

f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)=f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta^{\prime}}(\alpha).

Proof.

Let
\zeta
end at the vertex
v
and
\zeta^{\prime}
end at the vertex
v^{\prime}
, and let
\phi
be a path in
\mathcal{L}
from
v
to
v^{\prime}
.
Now if
\zeta^{\prime}\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}(\zeta^{\prime})
, then
\zeta\phi\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
and by
Lemma

3.13
,

\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\zeta^{\prime}\gamma)=\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\zeta\phi\gamma).

Moreover, it is straightforward to verify that the map
\zeta^{\prime}\gamma\mapsto\zeta\phi\gamma
is bi-Lipschitz on its image, so that
f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)\geq f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta^{\prime}}(\alpha)
for any
\alpha
.
The same argument yields the converse inequality, as required.
∎

5.2.
Multifractal formalism for irreducible loop classes

We maintain notation from the previous section, recalling that
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
is a path ending at a vertex in the loop class
\mathcal{L}
.

In light of the results in the previous section, the following notions are well-defined.

Definition 5.7
.

We define the
loop class
L^{q}
-spectrum
, denoted by
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)
, as
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)
.
Similarly, we define the
loop class multifractal spectrum
by
f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)=f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)
.

For convenience, we write

(5.2)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)}{q}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\to-\infty}\frac{\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)}{q}.

The limits necessarily exist by concavity of
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)
, and a straightforward argument shows that they are finite.

Our main result in this section is the following multifractal formalism, which relates the multifractal spectrum with the
L^{q}
-spectrum on the loop class.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an irreducible loop class in
\mathcal{G}
.
Then
f_{\mathcal{L}}=\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
.

In particular,
f_{\mathcal{L}}
is a concave function taking finite values precisely on the interval
[\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L}),\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})]
.

By definition, it suffices to show
f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{*}
for a path
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
ending at a vertex in
\mathcal{L}
.
There are many ways to prove this result.
One could use a weighted version of the arguments in
[
7
]
, which are proven in a similar irreducible matrix-product setting.
Another option is to follow the arguments in
[
11
]
.

We find it most efficient to use the following result, which is a simplified “symbolic” version of
[
8
, Thm. 2.2]
.

Proposition 5.9

(
[
8
]
)
.

Suppose for any
q\in\R
such that the derivative
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{\prime}(q)=\alpha
exists, there exist numbers
b(q,k)
and
c(q,k)
such that the following properties hold:

(1)

We have
\lim_{k\to\infty}b(q,k)=0
.

(2)

Suppose
n\in\N
and
\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(2^{-n})
.
Then for any
m\geq c(q,k)
, there are distinct paths
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(2^{-n-m})
such that
\eta
is a prefix of each
\eta_{i}
,

N\geq 2^{m(\tau^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)-b(q,k))},

and

2^{-m(\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{\prime}(q)+1/k)}\leq\frac{\rho(\eta_{i})}{\rho(\eta)}\leq 2^{-m(\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{\prime}(q)-1/k)}.

Then
f_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{*}(\alpha)
for each
\alpha\in\R
.

We first observe the following standard counting lemma, which is similar to
[
11
, Prop. 3.3]
, but the proof is easier.

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is any loop class (not necessarily irreducible) and the derivative
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{\prime}(q)=\alpha
exists.
Then for any
\delta>0
, there is
t_{0}=t_{0}(\delta,q)
such that for all
0<t<t_{0}
, there is
F^{*}(t)\subset\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)
such that

(1)

\#F^{*}(t)\geq t^{-\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{*}(\alpha)+\delta(|q|+1)}
and

(2)

t^{\alpha+\delta}\leq\rho(\eta)\leq t^{\alpha-\delta}
for each
\eta\in F^{*}(t)
.

Proof.

Write
A_{q}(t)=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}
.
Since
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{\prime}(q)
exists, get
\epsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle(\alpha-\delta/2)\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq|\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q\pm\epsilon)-\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)|\leq(\alpha+\delta/2)\epsilon.

Let
0<\gamma<\min\{\epsilon\delta/6,\delta/2,1\}
and observe that
\gamma
depends only on
\delta
and
q
.
Then since the limit defining
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
exists by
Lemma

5.3
, get
t_{0}
depending on
\gamma
and
q
such that for all
0<t<t_{0}
,

t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q+z)+\gamma}\leq A_{q+z}(t)\leq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q+z)-\gamma}

for each
z\in\{0,-\epsilon,\epsilon\}
.
Next, write
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)=F^{-}(t)\cup F^{*}(t)\cup F^{+}(t)
where

\displaystyle F^{-}(t)

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t):\rho(\eta)\leq t^{\alpha+\delta}\}

\displaystyle F^{+}(t)

\displaystyle=\{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t):\rho(\eta)\geq t^{\alpha-\delta}\}

and
F^{*}(t)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)\setminus(F^{-}(t)\cup F^{+}(t))
.
By definition, (ii) holds for
\eta\in F^{*}(t)
.

Combining the above inequalities gives that

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in F^{-}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq A_{q+\epsilon}(t)t^{-\epsilon(\alpha-\delta)}\leq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)+\epsilon\delta/2-\gamma}

with the analgous inequality for
F^{+}(t)
.
Then since
\gamma,t\in(0,1)
and
\gamma<\epsilon\delta/6
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in F^{*}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}

\displaystyle\geq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)+\gamma}-2t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)+\epsilon\delta/2-\gamma}\geq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)+2\gamma}(t^{-\gamma}-2)\geq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)+2\gamma}.

But now for each
\eta\in F^{*}(t)
, we have
\rho(\eta)^{q}\leq\max\{t^{(\alpha+\delta)q},t^{(\alpha-\delta)q}\}=t^{\alpha q-\delta|q|}
so that

\#F^{*}(t)\geq t^{-\alpha q+\delta|q|}\sum_{\eta\in F^{*}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}\geq t^{-\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{*}(\alpha)+\delta|q|+2\gamma}\geq t^{-\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)+\delta(|q|+1)}

since
\gamma<\delta/2
, giving (i).
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.8
.

Let
q\in\R
with
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}^{\prime}(q)=\alpha

\rho(\Psi(\phi))\asymp\rho(\eta_{0})
.

Now by
Lemma

5.10
with constant

such that for all

there are paths

Now with

\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(2^{-n-m})
and

N\geq 2^{m_{0}(\tau_{\mathcal{L},\phi}^{*}(\alpha)-(|q|+1)/2k}\geq 2^{m(\tau_{\mathcal{L},\phi}^{*}(\alpha)-(|q|+1)/k)}

and

\frac{\rho(\eta_{i})}{\rho(\eta)}\leq C\rho(\phi_{i})\leq C2^{-m_{0}(\alpha-1/2k)}\leq 2^{-m(\alpha-1/k)}

with a similar lower bound, for all
m\geq c_{0}(q,k)+m^{\prime}
sufficiently large depending only on fixed quantities.
Thus the conditions for
Proposition

5.9
are satisfied, giving the desired result.
∎

5.3.
Regular points in level sets of local dimensions

As before, we fix a path
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
ending at a vertex in the loop class
\mathcal{L}
.

Recall that

E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)=\{\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}:\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\alpha\}.

We wish to show that the set
E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)
can be approximated (in the sense of dimensions) by sets of points which have particularly nice properties.

Definition 5.11
.

Let
\xi
be a finite path (not necessarily rooted) in
\mathcal{G}
.
We say that a path
\gamma=(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega^{\infty}
is
\xi
-regular
if there exists a monotonically increasing sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\N
such that
\xi
is a prefix of
(e_{n_{j}},e_{n_{j}+1},\ldots)
for each
j
and

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_{j}}=1.

This will be of key importance in
Section

6
.
The proof of the following result is very similar to
[
7
, Prop. 3.2]
, so we are somewhat terse with details.
The irreducibility hypothesis is critical in order to obtain this result.

Theorem 5.12
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is an irreducible loop class and
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
a path ending at a vertex
v
in
\mathcal{L}
.
Then for any
\alpha\in[\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L}),\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})]
and finite path
\xi
contained in
\mathcal{L}
beginning at the vertex
v
, there exists
\emptyset\neq\Gamma=\Gamma(\xi)\subset E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)
such that

\dim_{H}\Gamma=\dim_{H}E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)=f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)

and
\Gamma
is composed only of
\xi
-regular points.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{L}
is simple, this result is immediate.

Otherwise we assume
\mathcal{L}
is not simple.
All cylinders in the proof are taken relative to
\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
.
Set

\displaystyle F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t):t^{\alpha+\epsilon}\leq\rho(\eta)\leq t^{\alpha-\epsilon}\bigr\}

\displaystyle G(\alpha;s,\epsilon)

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{0<t\leq s}\bigcup_{\eta\in F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)}[\eta].

Of course, if
\gamma\in E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)
, for any
\epsilon>0
,
\gamma\in G(\alpha;t,\epsilon)
for all
t
sufficiently small (depending on
\epsilon
) and thus

E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)\subseteq\bigcup_{s>0}G(\alpha;s,\epsilon).

Since each cylinder
[\eta]
where
\eta\in F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)
has diameter
W(\eta)\asymp t
, for any
s>0
and
\epsilon>0
,

\dim_{H}G(\alpha;s,\epsilon)\leq\underline{\dim}_{B}G(\alpha;s,\epsilon)\leq\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\#F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)}{-\log t}.

This holds for any
\epsilon>0
.
Thus by countable stability of the Hausdorff dimension,

(5.4)

\dim_{H}E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)\leq\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\#F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)}{-\log t}.

We now turn to the construction of the set
\Gamma
.
For the remainder of this proof, unless otherwise stated, all implicit constants may depend on the (fixed) paths
\zeta
and
\xi
.
Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary.
By
Eq.

5.4
, there are strictly monotonic sequences
(t_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset(0,1)
and
(\epsilon_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
both tending to 0 such that

(5.5)

\frac{\log\#F(\alpha;t_{j},\epsilon_{j})}{-\log t_{j}}>\dim_{H}E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)-\delta

for each
j\in\N
.
Define a sequence
\{t_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
by

\underbrace{t_{1},\ldots,t_{1}}_{N_{1}},\underbrace{t_{2},\ldots,t_{2}}_{N_{2}},\ldots,\underbrace{t_{i},\ldots,t_{i}}_{N_{i}},\ldots

where
N_{j}
is defined recursively by
N_{1}=1
and, for
j\geq 2
,

N_{j}=2^{-\log t_{j+1}+N_{j-1}}.

For each
i
, let
A_{i}
denote the set of indices
j\in\N
where
t_{j}^{*}=t_{i}
.
Set
\epsilon_{j}^{*}=\epsilon_{i}
when
t_{j}^{*}=t_{i}
.

Since there are only finitely many vertices in
\mathcal{L}
and finitely many possible dimensions of transition matrices, by the pidgeonhole principle, for each
j
, there exists an index
(m_{j},n_{j})
and a subset
G_{j}^{*}\subset F(\alpha;t_{j}^{*},\epsilon_{j}^{*})
such that each path in
G_{j}
begins and ends at the same vertex and
\#G_{j}^{*}\succcurlyeq\#F(\alpha;t_{j}^{*},\epsilon_{j}^{*})
.
Let
\eta_{j}^{*}=\eta_{i}
when
t_{j}^{*}=t_{i}
.

Recall that the path
\xi
begins at vertex
v
.
There exist constants
C,D>0
such that by repeatedly applying irreducibility of
\mathcal{L}
, for each path
\eta_{j}^{*}\in G_{j}^{*}
, there exist paths
\phi(\eta_{j}^{*}),\psi(\eta_{j}^{*})\in\mathcal{H}
such that the following two conditions hold:

(1)

the path
\theta(\eta_{j}^{*}):=\xi\phi(\eta_{j}^{*})\eta_{j}^{*}\psi(\eta_{j}^{*})
is a cycle beginning and ending at vertex
v
, and

(2)

for any
\gamma=\theta(\eta_{1}^{*})\ldots\theta(\eta_{k}^{*})\eta^{\prime}
where
\eta^{\prime}
is a prefix of
\theta(\eta_{k+1}^{*})
,

D^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left\lVert T(\eta_{i}^{*})\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert T(\gamma)\right\rVert\geq C^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}\left\lVert T(\eta_{i}^{*})\right\rVert.

Then let for
n\in\N

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\bigl\{[\zeta\theta(\eta_{1}^{*})\ldots\theta(\eta_{n}^{*})]:(\eta^{*}_{1},\ldots,\eta^{*}_{n})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}^{*}\bigr\}

which is a nested sequence of families of cylinders, and set

\Gamma_{\delta}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{I\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}I.

A direct computation shows that
\Gamma_{\delta}\subset\Sigma(\alpha)
.

We now show that
\dim_{H}\Gamma_{\delta}\geq\Lambda(\alpha)-\delta
.
By
[
13
, Prop. 3.1]
(the technical assumptions are immediate to verify),
\dim_{H}\Gamma_{\delta}=\liminf_{k\to\infty}a_{k}
where
a_{k}
satisfies

\sum_{(\eta_{1}^{*},\ldots,\eta_{k}^{*})\in G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{k}}W(\eta_{1}^{*}\ldots\eta_{k}^{*})^{a_{k}}=1.

Let
1\leq j\leq k
and choose
i
such that
j\in A_{i}
.
As
\xi
is fixed,
W(\theta(\eta_{j}^{*}))\asymp W(\eta_{j}^{*})\asymp t_{i}
and
\eta_{j}^{*}\in F(\alpha;t_{j}^{*},\epsilon_{j}^{*})
so
\eta_{j}^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}^{*}}(\Delta)=\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}(\Delta)
.
Let
r>0
be such that
W(\eta_{j}^{*})\geq rt_{j}^{*}
.
Thus since
(t_{j}^{*})\to 0
and
\#F(\alpha;t_{j}^{*},\epsilon_{j}^{*})\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\dim_{H}\Gamma_{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\prod_{j=1}^{k}\#G_{j}^{*}}{-\log\prod_{j=1}^{k}(rt_{j}^{*})}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{-k+\log\prod_{j=1}^{k}\#F(\alpha;t_{j}^{*},\epsilon_{j}^{*})}{k-\log\prod_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}^{*}}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\prod_{j=1}^{k}\#F(\alpha;t_{j}^{*},\epsilon_{j}^{*})}{-\log\prod_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}^{*}}.

Now by definition of the
N_{j}
and
Eq.

5.5
, it follows that

\displaystyle\dim_{H}\Gamma_{\delta}

\displaystyle\geq\dim_{H}E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)-\delta

as claimed.
Take
\Gamma=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{2^{-n}}
, and the result follows.
∎

6.
Multifractal analysis of self-similar measures

We continue to use the notation of the previous section.
We fix a WIFS
(S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
with self-similar measure
\mu
.
In particular, we assume that
\Phi
is an iteration rule with corresponding finite transition graph
\mathcal{G}
, as described in
Section

3.4
.

6.1.
Local dimensions and regular points

Intuitively, the multifractal analysis of self-similar sets satisfying the finite neighbour condition is related to the multifractal analysis results for loop classes from the preceding section.
However, the exact relationship is somewhat more complicated to establish: while the local dimension of
\rho
at a path
\gamma
depends only on the single sequence of edges determining
\gamma
, the local dimension of
\mu
at a point
x\in K
can also depend on net intervals which are adjacent to net intervals containing
x
.
This happens when
x
is the shared boundary point of two distinct net intervals, but it can also happen when
x
is an interior point approximated very well by boundary points (so that balls
B(x,r)
overlap significantly with neighbouring net intervals, for infinitely many values of
r
).

In order to better understand this adjacency structure, we introduce the notion of the approximation sequence of an interior point, as well as the set of regular points
K_{R}\subseteq K
.
Let
x\in K
be an interior point, which we recall means that
\pi^{-1}(x)=\{\gamma\}
is a single (infinite) path.
Let
(\Delta_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
with
\Delta_{0}=[0,1]
and each
\Delta_{i+1}
a child of
\Delta_{i}
denote the sequence of net intervals corresponding to
\gamma
.
Of course,
\Delta_{n}=\pi(\gamma|n)
.
Given some
i
and
[a,b]=\Delta_{i+1}\subseteq\Delta_{i}=[c,d]
, by the reductions described in
Remark

3.6
, exactly one of
c=a<b<d
,
c<a<b=d
, or
c<a<b<d
must hold.
Moreover, since
x
is an interior point, it cannot hold that all
\Delta_{k}
share a common left (resp. right) endpoint for all sufficiently large
k
where the left (resp. right) endpoint is also the right (resp. left) endpoint of some adjacent net interval.
In particular, there exists a monotonically increasing infinite sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that there exists a neighbourhood of
\Delta_{n_{j}+2}
in
K
which is contained entirely in
\Delta_{n_{j}}
.

We now make the following definition:

Definition 6.1
.

Given an interior point
x\in K
, we call the sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
described above the
approximation sequence
of
x
.
We then say that
x
is
regular
if its approximation sequence satisfies

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{n_{j+1}}{n_{j}}=1.

We denote the set of regular points in
K
by
K_{R}
.

The intuition is that interior points in
K
which are approximated very well by boundary points are contained in long sequences of net intervals which share left endpoints or right endpoints, so that regular points are those which are poorly approximated by boundary points.

The main point of the approximation sequence is that
x
is bounded uniformly away from the neighbouring net intervals of
\Delta_{n_{j}}=\pi(\gamma|n_{j})
for each
j\in\N
.
To be precise, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
x
be an interior point with approximation sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
There exists some
R>0
depending only on the IFS and
m=m(R)\in\N
such that, for any
j\in\N
,

\Delta_{n_{j}+m}\subseteq B(x,R\cdot\diam(\Delta_{n_{j}}))\cap K\subseteq\Delta_{n_{j}}.

Proof.

Since there is a neighbourhood of
\Delta_{n_{j}+2}
in
K
contained entirely in
\Delta_{n_{j}}
, either
\Delta_{n_{j}+2}\subseteq\Delta_{n_{j}}^{\circ}
or
\Delta_{n_{j}+2}
shares an endpoint with
\Delta_{n_{j}}
but there is no other adjacent net interval in
\mathcal{P}_{n_{j}}
.
We only treat the first case; the second follows by similar arguments.
We recall by
Proposition

3.5
that the position index
q(\Delta_{i+1},\Delta_{i})
depends only on the neighbour set of
\Delta_{i}
.
Thus if we write
\Delta_{n_{j}+2}=[a,b]
and
\Delta_{n_{j}}=[c,d]
where
a<c<d<b
, there are only finitely many positive values for
(c-a)/(d-c)
and
(b-d)/(d-c)
.
The existence of
R
follows.

Moreover, recall that
W(e)=\diam(\Delta_{i})/\diam(\Delta_{i+1})
when
\Delta_{i+1}
is the child of
\Delta_{i}
corresponding to the edge
e
.
Therefore, with
W_{\min}=\min\{W(e):e\in E(\mathcal{G})\}
, it suffices to take
m
such that
W_{\min}^{m-2}\leq R
.
∎

Using the approximation sequence, we can establish some basic relationships between local dimensions and their loop class analogues.
A similar version of the following result was first proven in
[
20
]
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose
x
is an interior point with unique symbolic representation
\gamma
.

(1)

We always have

\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)\leq\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)\leq\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)\leq\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma).

(2)

If
\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
exists, then
\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
.

(3)

If
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
exists, then
\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
.

Proof.

It suffices to show (i), since it is clear that (ii) and (iii) follow directly.

For each
t>0
let
n(t)
be maximal such that
W(\gamma|n(t))\leq t
.
Then if
\Delta_{t}=\pi(\gamma|n(t))
, we have
\Delta_{t}\subseteq B(x,t)
so that

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,t))}{\log t}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|n(t))}{\log t}

\displaystyle=\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma).

Replacing the limit superior with the limit inferior, we also have that
\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)\leq\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
.

To get the remaining bound, let
x
have approximation sequence
(n_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
and let
R,m
be as in
Lemma

6.2
.
We then have, since
W(\gamma|(n_{k}+m))\asymp\rho W(\gamma|n_{k})
,

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{\loc}(\mu,x)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,t))}{\log t}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,R\cdot W(\gamma|n_{k})))}{\log R\cdot W(\gamma|n_{k})}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|(n_{k}+m))}{\log W(\gamma|(n_{k}+m))}

\displaystyle\geq\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma).

as required.
∎

When the local dimension exists, the content of the following lemma states that we can extend the nice properties along the approximation sequence to net intervals in similar levels.
A similar statement holds when the loop class local dimension exists.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose
x
is an interior point with symbolic representation
\gamma
.
Let
x
have approximation sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
and let
(k_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\N
satisfy
\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{k_{j}}{n_{j}}=0
.

(1)

Suppose
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
exists.
Then

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|n_{j}-k_{j})}{\log\rho(\gamma|n_{j})}=1.

(2)

Suppose
\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
exists.
Then with
m
from
Lemma

6.2
,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu\bigl(B(x,W(\gamma|n_{j}+m))\bigr)}{\log\mu\bigl(B(x,W(\gamma|n_{j}+m+k_{j}))\bigr)}=1

Proof.

We first see (i).
For each
i
let
\Delta_{i}
have symbolic representation
\gamma|i
, set
t_{i}=\diam(\Delta_{n_{i}})
, and let
\alpha=\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
.
By
Lemma

6.2
, there exists some
R>0
and
m\in\N
such that

(6.1)

B(x,Rt_{j})\subseteq\Delta_{n_{j}}\subseteq\Delta_{n_{j}-k_{j}}\subseteq B(x,c_{j}t_{j})

where
c_{j}=W_{\min}^{-k_{j}-1}
.
But then since
\log c_{j}t_{j}\asymp n_{j}-k_{j}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{(k_{j}+1)\log W_{\min}}{\log c_{j}t_{j}}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{k_{j}}{n_{j}-k_{j}}=0

so that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,c_{j}t_{j}))}{\log t_{j}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,c_{j}t_{j}))}{(k_{j}+1)\log W_{\min}+\log c_{j}t_{j}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,c_{j}t_{j}))}{\log c_{j}t_{j}}=\alpha

Arguing similarly, we also have
\alpha=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,Rt_{j}))}{\log t_{j}}
.
Thus

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,c_{j}t_{j}))}{\log\mu(B(x,Rt_{j}))}=1

and the result follows from
Eq.

6.1
.

The proof of (ii) follows similarly after observing that

\displaystyle\Delta_{n_{j}}

\displaystyle\supseteq B(x,R\cdot W(\gamma|n_{j}))\supseteq B(x,W(\gamma|n_{j}+m))

\displaystyle\supseteq B(x,W(\gamma|n_{j}+m+k_{j}))\supseteq\Delta_{n_{j}+m+k_{j}}.

∎

Finally, the situation is nicest when
x\in K_{R}
is a regular point.
Note that this strengthens the usual observations in
Proposition

6.3
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Suppose
x\in K_{R}
is a regular point with unique symbolic representation
\gamma
eventually in the loop class
\mathcal{L}
.
If either
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
exists or
\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
exists, then they both exist and are equal.

Proof.

We see this when
\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\alpha
exists; the proof when
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
exists is analgous.
Set
k_{j}=n_{j+1}-n_{j}
.
But then for all
i
sufficiently large,
n_{j}+m\leq i\leq n_{j}+m+k_{j}
for some
j
.
Then since
x
is a regular point,
Lemma

6.4
applies with
(k_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
and

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu\bigl(B(x,W(\gamma|i))\bigr)}{\log W(\gamma|i)}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu\Bigl(B\bigl(x,W(\gamma|(n_{j}+m))\bigr)\Bigr)}{\log W(\gamma|(n_{j}+m))}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|n_{j})}{\log W(\gamma|n_{j})}=\alpha.

The lower bound follows similarly, so that
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha
.
∎

6.2.
The upper bound for the multifractal spectrum

Set

E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})=\{x\in K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}:\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha\}=K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}\cap E_{\mu}(\alpha).

Given a path
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
ending at a vertex in
\mathcal{L}
, one can think of
E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})\cap\pi(\zeta)
as an analogue of the set
E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(\alpha)
from
Section

5.1
.
In
Theorem

5.8
the upper bound
f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)
always holds, with no assumptions on
\mathcal{L}
.
Here, we show that
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)
is also an upper bound for the Hausdorff dimension of the level sets
E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})
.

Compare part (i) in
Theorem

6.6
with
[
20
, Prop. 4.4]
.
Note that our definition of
\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L})
and
\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})
(as defined in
Eq.

5.2
) is formally different from that paper.
Regardless, one can shown that they coincide when
\mathcal{L}
is an irreducible loop class.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
(S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a WIFS satisfying the finite neighbour condition with associated self-similar measure
\mu
.
Let
\mathcal{L}
be a loop class.
Then

(1)

\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)\in[\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L}),\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})]
for any
x\in K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}
for which the local dimension exists, and

(2)

\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)
for any
\alpha\in\R
.

We first recall some notation from
Section

5.2
.
Fix some
\Delta_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{0})\in V(\mathcal{L})
.
Let
\Delta_{0}
have symbolic representation
\zeta_{0}
and set

A_{q}(t)=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{0}}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}.

so that

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{0}}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\log A_{q}(t)}{\log t}.

The projection
\pi
taking paths in
\Omega^{*}
to net intervals in
\mathcal{P}
restricts to the map

\pi:\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{0}}\to\{\Delta\in\mathcal{P}:\Delta\subseteq\Delta_{0},\mathcal{V}(\Delta)\in V(\mathcal{L})\}.

We recall that
\rho=\mu\circ\pi
.
As defined in
Theorem

5.12
, we also set

F(\alpha;t,\epsilon):=\bigl\{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{0}}(t):t^{\alpha+\epsilon}\leq\rho(\eta)\leq t^{\alpha-\epsilon}\bigr\}.

We first prove the following standard counting result on the size of the sets
F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)
.
This is essentially the same as, for example,
[
24
, Lem. 4.1]
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
be arbitrary and
q\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)
.
Then there exists some
r>0
such that for all
0<t<r
,

\#F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)\leq t^{-\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)-(1+|q|)\epsilon}.

Proof.

We prove this for
q<0
, but the case
q\geq 0
follows identically.
To do this, we bound
A_{q}(t)
in two ways for
t
sufficiently small.
On one hand,

A_{q}(t)\geq\sum_{\eta\in F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)}\rho(\eta)^{q}\geq t^{q(\alpha-\epsilon)}\#F(\alpha;t,\epsilon).

On the other hand, for
t
sufficiently small (depending on
\epsilon
and
\Delta_{0}
),
A_{q}(t)\leq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)-\epsilon}
.
Combining these observations, we have

\#F(\alpha;t,\epsilon)\leq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)-\epsilon}t^{-q(\alpha-\epsilon)}=t^{-\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)-(1-q)\epsilon}

since
q\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)
so that
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)=\alpha q-\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)
.
∎

We now begin the main proof.

Proof of
Theorem

6.6
.

To see (i), suppose
x\in K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}
is arbitrary with unique symbolic representation
\gamma=(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
Let
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
be a prefix of
\gamma
ending in
\mathcal{L}
.

By
Proposition

6.3
,
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
, so there exists an increasing sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that

\underline{\dim}_{\loc}(\rho,x)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|n_{j})}{\log W(\gamma|n_{j})}.

With
t_{j}=W(\gamma|n_{j})
, since
\gamma|n_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}}
, we have for
j
sufficiently large that
\gamma|n_{j}\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
so that

\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t_{j})}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t_{j}}\leq q\frac{\log\rho(\gamma|n_{j})}{\log t_{j}}.

Taking the limit infimum as
j
goes to infinity yields

\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)\leq q\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)

where
q\in\R
is arbitrary.
It follows that
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)\in[\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L}),\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})]
.

We now see (ii).
Since

K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}=\bigcup_{\{\Delta\in\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{V}(\Delta)\in V(\mathcal{L})\}}\Delta\cap K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}},

it suffices to show that
\dim_{H}E_{0}\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)
where

E_{0}:=E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})\cap\Delta_{0}

and
\Delta_{0}=\pi(\zeta_{0})
is a fixed net interval with neighbour set in
\mathcal{L}
.
We fix notation as above; in particular, we recall that
\zeta_{0}
is the symbolic representation of
\Delta_{0}
.

Again we assume
q<0
; the case
q\geq 0
follows similarly.
Fix
\epsilon>0
and set

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{\pi(\eta):\eta\in F(\alpha;2^{-n},\epsilon)\}

where
\pi(\eta)
is the net interval with symbolic representation
\eta
.
By
Lemma

6.7
, there exists
N=N(\epsilon)
such that for all
n\geq N
,

\#\mathcal{G}_{n}=\#F(\alpha;2^{-n},\epsilon)\leq 2^{n(\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)+(1-q)\epsilon)}.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\bigcup_{n=N(\epsilon)}^{\infty}G_{n}
.

We first see that
\mathcal{G}
is a Vitali cover for
E_{0}
.
Let
x\in E_{0}
be arbitrary.
Since
x=\pi(\gamma)
is an interior point, it has an approximation sequence
(n_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
Let
m
be such that any path
\eta
in
\mathcal{G}
of length at least
m
has
W(\eta)\leq 1/3
.
Such a constant exists since there are only finitely many possible edge weights
W(e)\in(0,1)
.
The choice of
m
ensures that there exists some
m_{j}\in\N
such that
W(\gamma|n_{j})\leq 2^{-m_{j}}\leq W(\gamma|n_{j}-m)
.
Since
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)
exists and
R\cdot W(\gamma|n_{j})\asymp 2^{-m_{j}}
where
R>0
is a fixed constant,

(6.2)

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,R\cdot W(\gamma|n_{j})))}{\log\mu(B(x,2^{-m_{j}}))}=1.

Now, by
Lemma

6.2
and
Lemma

6.4
applied to the constant sequence
k_{j}=m
, we have for
j
sufficiently large and
0\leq i\leq m
arbitrary

\mu(B(x,R\cdot W(\gamma|n_{j})))\leq\rho(\gamma|n_{j})\leq\rho(\gamma|n_{j}-i)\leq\rho(\gamma|n_{j})^{1-\epsilon}.

Moreover, we always have

B(x,2^{-m_{j}})\supseteq B(x,W(\gamma|n_{j}))\supseteq\pi(\gamma|n_{j})

so that
\rho(\gamma|n_{j})\leq\mu(B(x,2^{-m_{j}}))
.
Thus applying
Eq.

6.2
, for all
j
sufficiently small,

\mu(B(x,2^{-m_{j}}))^{1+\epsilon}\leq\rho(\gamma|n_{j}-i)\leq\rho(\gamma|n_{j})^{1-\epsilon}\leq\mu(B(x,2^{-m_{j}}))^{1-\epsilon}.

Finally, since
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha
, for all
j
sufficiently small and
0\leq i\leq m
with
\gamma|(n_{j}-i)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{0}}(2^{-m_{j}})
,

(2^{-m_{j}})^{\alpha+\epsilon}\leq\rho(\gamma|n_{j}-i)\leq(2^{-m_{j}})^{\alpha-\epsilon}.

Thus
\pi(\gamma|(n_{j}-i))\in\mathcal{G}
.
Since this is true for all
j
sufficiently large, we may take
\diam(\gamma|(n_{j}-i))
arbitrarily small, so
\mathcal{G}
is indeed a Vitali cover for
E_{0}
.

Now suppose
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is any disjoint subcollection of
\mathcal{G}
: then for
s=\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)+2(1-q)\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\diam(E_{i})^{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=N(\epsilon)}^{\infty}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{G}_{n}}\diam(\Delta)^{s}\leq\sum_{n=N(\epsilon)}^{\infty}2^{-ns}\#\mathcal{G}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=N(\epsilon)}^{\infty}\bigl(2^{-\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)-2(1-q)\epsilon)}2^{\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)+(1-q)\epsilon}\bigr)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=N(\epsilon)}^{\infty}(2^{-(1-q)\epsilon})^{n}<\infty.

Thus by the Vitali covering theorem for Hausdorff measure, we must have

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E_{0})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\diam(E_{i})^{s}<\infty

so that
\dim_{H}E_{0}\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\alpha)+2(1-q)\epsilon
.
Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

6.3.
Irreducibility and the lower bound for the multifractal spectrum

We recall that the notion of irreducibility was introduced in
Section

4.2
.
Moreover, recall that a point
x\in K_{\mathcal{L}}
is said to be an interior point of
K_{\mathcal{L}}
if it only has symbolic representations that are eventually in
\mathcal{L}
, and the set of such points is denoted by
K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}
.

We now introduce the notion of an interior path, and use this to relate the notions of
\xi
-regularity in
\Omega^{\infty}
(introduced in
Definition

5.11
) with regular points in
K
(as defined in
Definition

6.1
).

Definition 6.8
.

We say
\xi
is an
interior path
if whenever
(\Delta_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
is a sequence of net interals where
\Delta_{i+1}
is a child of
\Delta_{i}
corresponding to
\xi
, there is a neighbourhood of
\Delta_{m}
in
K
which is contained entirely in
\Delta_{0}
.

Recall that
\Omega^{\infty}
is the set of rooted infinite paths in
\mathcal{G}
and
K_{R}
is the set of regular points.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
\xi
be an interior path in a loop class
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}
be
\xi
-regular.
Then for any path
\eta
such that
\eta\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}
,
\pi(\eta\gamma)\in K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}\cap K_{R}
.

Proof.

This is a direct application of the definitions, noting that if
\gamma=(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
\eta
has length
m_{1}
,
\xi
has length
m_{2}
, and
\xi
appears at some position
n
, then some
j
with
n+m_{1}\leq j\leq n+m_{1}+m_{2}
is a point in the approximation sequence of
\pi(\eta\gamma)
.
∎

Recall that

E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})=\{x\in K^{\operatorname{int}}_{\mathcal{L}}:\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\alpha\}.

We will also need the following result, which follows by a similar argument to
[
33
, Prop. 3.15]
or (in a somewhat more specialized case)
[
16
, Prop. 2.7]
.

Lemma 6.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is a simple loop class and
x\in K_{\mathcal{L}}
.
If
x
is an interior point with
\pi^{-1}(x)=\{\gamma\}
, then

\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma).

Otherwise
x\in K
is a boundary point with
\pi^{-1}(x)=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}
, and

\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\min\{\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma_{1}),\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma_{2})\}.

We now show here that the regular points in a non-simple
K_{\mathcal{L}}
are abundant.

Theorem 6.11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an irreducible loop class which is not simple, or simple and contains an interior point.
Then
E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)\geq 0
if and only if
\alpha\in[\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L}),\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})]
.
Moreover,

\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L})\cap K_{R}\geq f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)

for all
\alpha
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{L}
is simple, since
\mathcal{L}
contains interior points, the result follows directly from
Lemma

6.10
.

Otherwise,
\mathcal{L}
is not simple, so there exists some vertex
v\in V(\mathcal{L})
and an interior path
\xi\in\Omega^{*}(\mathcal{L},v)
.
Let
\zeta_{1}\in\Omega^{*}
be any path ending at a vertex in
\mathcal{L}
.
By
Theorem

5.12
, get
\Gamma\subseteq E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{1}}(\alpha)
such that
\dim_{H}\Gamma\geq\dim_{H}E_{\mathcal{L},\zeta_{1}}(\alpha)
and each
\gamma\in\Gamma
is
\xi
-regular with
\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)=\alpha
.

By
Lemma

6.9
and
Corollary

6.5
,
\pi(\Gamma)\subseteq E(\mathcal{L},\alpha)\cap K_{R}
.
In particular, this proves
E_{\mu}(\mathcal{L};\alpha)\cap K_{R}
is non-empty whenever
f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)\geq 0
, and

\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\mathcal{L};\alpha)\cap K_{R}\geq\dim_{H}\pi(\Gamma).

We also know by
Theorem

5.8
that
\alpha\in[\alpha_{\min}(\mathcal{L}),\alpha_{\max}(\mathcal{L})]
if and only if
f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)\geq 0
.
The remaining implication follows from
Theorem

6.6
.

It remains to prove that
\dim_{H}\pi(\Gamma)=\dim_{H}(\Gamma)
.
We recall from
Lemma

5.1
that
\pi
is Lipschitz, so
\dim_{H}\pi(\Gamma)\leq\dim_{H}\Gamma
.
Conversely, let
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}
be the net interval with symbolic representation
\zeta_{1}
and let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be some
\epsilon
-cover of
\pi(\Gamma)\subseteq\Delta
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
U_{i}\subseteq\Delta
for each
i\in\N
.
Let
t_{i}=\diam U_{i}<\epsilon
and let
b_{i}
denote the maximal number of net intervals of generation
t_{i}
which intersect
U_{i}
.
Note that
b_{i}\leq 1/[a]+1
where the diameter of any generation
t
net interval is at least
at
.
These net intervals have symbolic representations
\{\zeta_{1}\eta_{ij}:1\leq j\leq b_{i}\}
, and the corresponding cylinders
\mathcal{C}=\{[\eta_{ij}]:i\in\N,1\leq j\leq b_{i}\}
cover
\Gamma
and have diameter
W(\eta_{ij})\asymp t_{i}
.
Thus there exists some
A>0
such that
\mathcal{C}
forms an
A\epsilon
-cover of
\Gamma
.

It follows that for a suitable constant
c
,

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{i}}\left(\diam([\eta_{ij}])\right)^{s}\leq cA^{s}\sum_{i}(\diam(U_{i}))^{s}

and therefore for each
\epsilon>0
,

H_{\epsilon A}^{s}(\Gamma)\leq cA^{s}H_{\epsilon}^{s}(\pi(\Gamma)).

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we deduce that
H^{s}(\pi(\Gamma))\geq(cA^{s})^{-1}H^{s}(\Gamma)
.
This implies
\dim_{H}\pi(\Gamma)\geq\dim_{H}(\Gamma)
, so that
\dim_{H}\pi(\Gamma)=\dim_{H}\Gamma
.
∎

If
\mathcal{L}
is an irreducible non-simple loop class, then necessarily
\mathcal{L}
contains an interior path.
The only additional case occurs when
\mathcal{L}
is a simple loop class without an interior path.
In this case, it may hold that every
x\in K_{\mathcal{L}}
has two symbolic representations, and the local dimension is always given by the symbolic representation of the adjacent path not eventually in
\mathcal{L}
.
This motivates the following definition.

Definition 6.12
.

We say that a loop class
\mathcal{L}
is
non-degenerate
if
\mathcal{L}
is not simple, or if
\mathcal{L}
is simple and there exists some
x\in K
such that

\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)

for some
\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}
.
We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
degenerate
otherwise.

Corollary 6.13
.

Suppose every loop class in
\mathcal{G}
is irreducible, with non-degenerate loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
.
Then the multifractal spectrum of
\mu
is given by

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\max\{f_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\alpha),\ldots,f_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(\alpha)\}

for each
\alpha\in\R
.

Proof.

Combining the general upper bound from
Theorem

6.6
and the lower bound
Theorem

6.11
using irreducibility, it follows for each
1\leq i\leq m
that

\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L}_{i})=f_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\alpha).

Of course,

\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}E_{\mu}(\alpha;\mathcal{L}_{i})\supseteq E_{\mu}(\alpha)\cap K^{\operatorname{int}}.

Moreover, if
x\notin K^{\operatorname{int}}
, by
Lemma

6.10
, then
\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x)=\dim_{\loc}(\rho,\gamma)
for some infinite path
\gamma\in\Omega^{\infty}_{\mathcal{L}}
.
Then this
\mathcal{L}
is non-degenerate, and
K\setminus K^{\operatorname{int}}
is countable and hence has Hausdorff dimension 0.
Thus

f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\alpha)=\dim_{H}E_{\mu}(\alpha)\cap K^{\operatorname{int}}

as required.
∎

6.4.
Decomposability and bounds for the
L^{q}
-spectrum

Recall that the notion of decomposability was introduced in
Section

4.3
.

Similarly to how we bounded the multifractal formalism
f_{\mu}
in terms of the functions
f_{\mathcal{L}}
for loop classes
\mathcal{L}
, in this section, we establish bounds for the
L^{q}
-spectrum
\tau_{\mu}
in terms of the functions
\tau_{\mathcal{L}}
.
We first note the following general upper bound.

Lemma 6.14
.

Let
\mu
be a self-similar measure satisfying the
\Phi
-FNC, with loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
.
Then

\tau_{\mu}(q)\leq\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sup\sum_{i}\mu(B(x_{i},t))^{q}}{\log t}\leq\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}

where the supremum is taken over all centred packings
\{B(x_{i},t)\}_{i}
of
K=\supp\mu
.

Proof.

The first inequality follows by definition.

To see the second inequality, let
\mathcal{L}
be an arbitrary loop class.
Let
\zeta\in\Omega^{*}
be a path ending at a vertex in
\mathcal{L}
.
Then by definition

\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}\geq\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}.

Now the same proof as
Proposition

5.2
shows that

\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sup\sum_{i}\mu(B(x_{i},t))^{q}}{\log t}=\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t}\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}(q)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)

by existence of the limit defining
\tau_{\mathcal{L},\zeta}
given in
Lemma

5.3
.
But
\mathcal{L}
was arbitrary, so the result follows.
∎

We now have the following result establishing our lower bound as well.
Note the similarity of this result and proof to
[
18
, Thm. 5.2]
.

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
\mu
be a self-similar satisfying the
\Phi
-FNC with decomposable transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}
have loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
.
Then

\tau_{\mu}(q)=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}.

for any
q\in\R
.
Moreover, the limit defining
\tau_{\mu}(q)
exists for any
q\in\R
.

Proof.

For each loop class
\mathcal{L}_{i}
, fix a path
\zeta_{i}\in\Omega^{*}
ending at a vertex
v_{i}\in V(\mathcal{L}_{i})
.
Now for each vertex
w\in V(\mathcal{L}_{i})
, let
\gamma_{i,w}
be a path in
\mathcal{L}_{i}
from
v_{i}
to
w
.
Let
s_{0}>0
be such that

s_{0}^{1/m}\leq\min_{i}\min_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}W(\zeta_{i}\gamma_{i,w}).

Similarly, since there are only finitely many initial and transition paths, there is
s_{1}>0
such that if
\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)
has decomposition
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, then

s_{1}t\geq W(\lambda_{1})\cdots W(\lambda_{n}).

Next, define sets of path weights

\displaystyle\Lambda_{i}

\displaystyle:=\{W(\eta):\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i},\zeta_{i}}\}

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)

\displaystyle:=\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\Lambda_{1}\times\cdots\times\Lambda_{m}:s_{1}t\geq t_{1}\cdots t_{m}\geq s_{0}t\}.

Since there are only finitely many edge weights
W(e)
for
e\in E(\mathcal{G})
, it follows that there is some
k\in\N
such that
\#\Lambda(t)\leq(-\log t)^{k}
for all
t
sufficiently small.

We now construct a function

(6.3)

\Psi:\mathcal{F}(t)\to\bigcup_{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\Lambda(t)}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{1},\zeta_{1}}(t_{1})\times\cdots\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{m},\zeta_{m}}(t_{m})

as follows.
Suppose the path
\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)
has decomposition
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
.
Then if the path
\lambda_{i}
begins at vertex
w_{i}\in V(\mathcal{L}_{i})
, we set

\Psi(\eta)=(\zeta_{1}\gamma_{1,w_{1}}\lambda_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m}\gamma_{m,w_{m}}\lambda_{m}).

Note that
\Psi
is well-defined by choice of
s_{0}
and the definition of
\Lambda(t)
.

Since there are only finitely many transition paths, there is a uniform bound on the number of paths with the same decomposition.
Moreover, since there are only finitely many paths
\gamma_{i,w_{i}}
, for a fixed path
\eta
, the number of distinct decompositions of paths
\eta^{\prime}
with
\Psi(\eta)=\Psi(\eta^{\prime})
is also uniformly bounded.
Thus, even though
\Psi
need not be injective, there is some constant
N\in\N
(independent of
t
) such that each fibre of
\Psi
has cardinality at most
N
.

Fix

\theta(q):=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}.

By
Lemma

5.3
, for any
\epsilon>0
and all
t
sufficiently small,

\sum_{\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i},\zeta_{i}}(t)}\left\lVert T(\eta_{i})\right\rVert^{q}\leq t^{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)-\epsilon}\leq t^{\theta(q)-\epsilon}.

Moreover, by the decomposability assumption and
Lemma

3.13
, it follows that if
\Psi(\eta)=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m})
, then

\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert^{q}\preccurlyeq_{q}\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert^{q}\cdots\left\lVert T(\eta_{m})\right\rVert^{q}.

Thus for all
t
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq_{q}\sum_{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\Lambda(t)}\left(\sum_{\eta_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{1},\zeta_{1}}(t_{1})}\cdots\sum_{\eta_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{m},\zeta_{m}}(t_{m})}\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert^{q}\cdots\left\lVert T(\eta_{m})\right\rVert^{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\Lambda(t)}\left(\sum_{\eta_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{1},\zeta_{1}}(t_{1})}\left\lVert T(\eta_{1})\right\rVert^{q}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{\eta_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{m},\zeta_{m}}(t_{m})}\left\lVert T(\eta_{m})\right\rVert^{q}\right)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq_{q}\sum_{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\Lambda(t)}t_{1}^{\theta(q)-\epsilon}\cdots t_{m}^{\theta(q)-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq_{q}\#\Lambda(t)t^{\theta(q)-\epsilon}.

Since
\#\Lambda(t)
grows polynomially in
\log t
, it follows by
Proposition

5.2
that

\tau_{\mu}(q)\geq\liminf_{t\to 0}\frac{\log\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(t)}\rho(\eta)^{q}}{\log t}\geq\theta(q)-\epsilon.

But
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, and combining this with
Lemma

6.14
yields the desired result.
∎

Remark 6.16
.

In fact, since for any path
\eta
with decomposition
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
, we have

\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert\preccurlyeq\left\lVert T(\lambda_{1})\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert T(\lambda_{m})\right\rVert

with no assumptions on the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
, the same proof as above shows that

\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mu}(q)=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}

by
Proposition

5.4
for
q\geq 0
without the decomposability assumption.

Remark 6.17
.

Unlike the results for the multifractal formalism in
Corollary

6.13
, we note that
Lemma

6.14
and
Theorem

6.15
write the
L^{q}
-spectrum in terms of
all
loop classes, and not just the non-degenerate loop classes.

7.
Applications and examples

Throughout this section, naturally,
(S_{i},p_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is a WIFS satisfying the finite neighbour condition with respect to the iteration rule
\Phi
, and has transition graph
\mathcal{G}
and associated self-similar measure
\mu
.

7.1.
Consequences of the main results

Our first application, which follows essentially from the bounds in the previous section along with standard properties of concave functions, describes precisely when the multifractal formalism holds.

Corollary 7.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is irreducible and decomposable, and suppose the maximal loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
are non-degenerate.
Then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism at
\alpha
if and only if
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
for some
1\leq i\leq m
and
q\in\R
with
\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
.
In particular, if the derivative
\alpha=\tau_{\mu}^{\prime}(q)
exists at some
q\in\R
, then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism at
\alpha
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is decomposable,
\tau_{\mu}=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}\}
by
Theorem

6.15
.

First suppose
f_{\mu}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)
, so there is some
\mathcal{L}_{i}
such that

\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=f_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}^{*}(\alpha)

by
Corollary

6.13
and
Theorem

5.8
.
Since
\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}^{*}(\alpha)
, there are
q_{1},q_{2}\in\R
such that
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{1})\cap\partial\tau_{\mu}(q_{2})
: therefore,
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{1})-\tau_{\mu}(q_{2})=\alpha(q_{1}-q_{2})
.
Without loss of generality, suppose
q_{1}<q_{2}
.
Since
\tau_{\mu}(q_{1})\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{1})
and
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{1})\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{2})-(q_{2}-q_{1})\alpha
by concavity, this can only happen when
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{1})=\tau_{\mu}(q_{1})
and
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q_{1})
, as required.

Conversely, suppose
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
where
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)=\tau_{\mu}(q)
.
Since
\tau_{\mu}\leq\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
, it follows that
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mu}(q)
so that
\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}^{*}(\alpha)
.
But
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)\leq\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}
by assumption so

\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}^{*}(\alpha)=\max\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}^{*}(\alpha),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}^{*}(\alpha)\}=f_{\mu}(\alpha)

by
Corollary

6.13
.

If
\tau_{\mu}^{\prime}(q)
exists, it follows immediately that
\alpha\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)
for any
i
such that
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(q)=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q),\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(q)\}
.
∎

Our next result was obtained in
[
33
]
under the weak separation condition, but we obtain it here (in a slightly more specialized case) as a direct corollary of the prior results.

Corollary 7.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
has exactly one loop class
\mathcal{L}
.
Then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{L}
is the only loop class, it must be essential, so it is irreducible by
Lemma

4.6
.
Since there is only one loop class and therefore no transition paths, the decomposability condition holds vacuously, and by
Theorem

6.15
,
\tau_{\mu}=\tau_{\mathcal{L}}
.
Thus the result follows from the multifractal formalism for irreducible graph-directed systems proven in
Theorem

5.8
.
∎

We now prove the following result, which completely characterizes the validity of the multifractal formalism in terms of a qualitative property of the multifractal spectrum.

Corollary 7.3
.

Suppose the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is irreducible and decomposable, and every loop class is non-degenerate.
Then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism if and only if
f_{\mu}
is a concave function.

Proof.

If
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism, then
f_{\mu}=\tau_{\mu}^{*}
where
\tau_{\mu}^{*}
is a concave function.

Conversely, suppose
f_{\mu}
is a concave function.
We have by
Theorem

6.15
and
Corollary

6.13
that

(7.1)

f_{\mu}=\max\{f_{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\ldots,f_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}\}\text{ and }\tau_{\mu}=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}\}.

where
\mathcal{G}
has loop classes
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}
.

Now let
\alpha_{0}\in\R
be arbitrary.
Let
q
be the unique value such that
\alpha_{0}\in\partial\tau_{\mu}(q)=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
.
If
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
,
\tau_{\mu}
is differentiable at
q
and we are done by
Corollary

7.1
.
Otherwise, by
Eq.

7.1
, there exist two loop classes, say
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, such that
\alpha_{1}\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q)
,
\alpha_{2}\in\partial\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(q)
and
\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(q)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(q)=\tau_{\mu}(q)
.
Observe that
\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=\alpha q-\tau_{\mu}(q)
for any
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
.
Moreover, since concave conjugation is order reversing, by
Eq.

7.1
,
f_{\mu}(\alpha_{1})=f_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\alpha_{1})=\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha_{1})
and
f_{\mu}(\alpha_{2})=f_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(\alpha_{2})=\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha_{2})
.
But
f_{\mu}(\alpha_{0})\leq\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha_{0})
and
f_{\mu}
is concave by assumption, forcing
\tau_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha_{0})=f_{\mu}(\alpha_{0})
as required.
∎

Remark 7.4
.

For IFS of the form
(\lambda x+d_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
satisfying the finite type condition, the following version of the reverse implication was first observed in
[
7
, Rem. 5.3]
: if
\tau_{\mu}=\tau_{\mathcal{L}}
for an essential loop class
\mathcal{L}
, then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism.
This result follows for any IFS satisfying the
\Phi
-FNC by combining
Proposition

5.4
, the fact that the essential loop class is always irreducible, and the general upper bound
f_{\mu}\leq\tau_{\mu}^{*}
,

A sufficient condition for the measure
\mu
to fail the multifractal formalism is for the set of attainable local dimensions of
\mu
to not be a closed interval.
In general, this condition is not necessary.
However, in certain situations, we can determine that it is necessary and sufficient.

Corollary 7.5
.

Suppose the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is decomposable.
Suppose in addition that every non-essential loop class is simple and non-degenerate.
Then
\mu
satisfies the multifractal formalism if and only if the set of local dimensions

\{\dim_{\loc}(\mu,x):x\in K\}

is a closed interval.

Proof.

The forward direction is immediate.

Conversely, denote the loop classes by
\{\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{m}\}
.
If
\mathcal{L}
is any simple loop class, then
f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)=0
for precisely one value of
\alpha
, and is
-\infty
otherwise.
Since the essential loop class and any simple loop class is irreducible, by
Remark

5.5
, we have

f_{\mathcal{L}}(\alpha)=\max\{f_{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(\alpha),\ldots,f_{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(\alpha)\}=f_{\mu}(\alpha).

Thus the result follows by
Corollary

7.3
.
∎

7.2.
A family of examples of Testud

Let
\ell\geq 2
be a positive integer.
Let
P,N\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
where
\{0,\ell-1\}\subseteq P\cup N
.
Let
\mathcal{I}=P\times\{1\}\cup N\times\{-1\}
and for
(i,\pm 1)\in\mathcal{I}
, define

\displaystyle S_{(i,1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{\ell}+\frac{i}{\ell}

\displaystyle S_{(i,-1)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{x}{\ell}+\frac{i+1}{\ell}.

In this subsection, we study the multifractal theory of the IFS
\{S_{\underline{i}}\}_{\underline{i}\in\mathcal{I}}
.
This family of IFS was studied in
[
36
]
and
[
29
]
under the assumption that
P=\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
.
We do not require this assumption in our analysis.

Fix the iteration rule
\Phi
from
Example

3.7
.
Write
V=\{v_{1},v_{-1},v_{\pm 1}\}
where
v_{1}=\{x\mapsto x\}
,
v_{-1}=\{x\mapsto-x+1\}
and
v_{\pm 1}=v_{1}\cup v_{-1}
.
Since the images
S_{(i,\pm 1)}((0,1))
are either disjoint or coincide exactly,

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{S_{\sigma}([0,1]):\sigma\in\mathcal{I}^{n}\}.

In particular, if
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}
is any net interval, then
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)\in V
.
Thus
(S_{\underline{i}})_{\underline{i}\in\mathcal{I}}
satisfies the
\Phi
-FNC.
Note that
v_{1}=v_{\operatorname{root}}\in V(\mathcal{G})
.

If
P\cap N=\emptyset
, then the IFS satisfies the open set condition with respect to the open interval
(0,1)
.
Otherwise, there exists some index
i
such that
(i,1)
and
(i,-1)
are both in
\mathcal{I}
, so that
v_{\pm 1}
is a neighbour set in
V
.
For the remainder of this section, we will assume that this is the case.
The open set condition may hold even when
P\cap N\neq\emptyset
with respect to an open set that is not an interval (take, for example,
\ell=4
,
P=\{0,1,3\}
, and
N=\{1\}
), but for simplicity we omit this discussion.

7.2.1.
Properties of the transition graph

We begin with a description of the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.

Proposition 7.6
.

Suppose
P\cap N\neq\emptyset
.
There is a unique essential loop class
\mathcal{G}_{\ess}
, and
v_{\pm 1}\in V(\mathcal{G}_{\ess})
.
Moreover, exactly one of the following holds:

(1)

We have
P=N
.
Then
\mathcal{G}_{\ess}
is the only loop class and
V(\mathcal{G}_{\ess})=\{v_{\pm 1}\}
.

(2)

There is some
i
such that
i\in P\setminus N
and
\ell-1-i\notin P
or
i\in N\setminus P
and
i,\ell-1-i\notin N
.
Then
\mathcal{G}_{\ess}
is the only loop class and
V(\mathcal{G}_{\ess})=V(\mathcal{G})=\{v_{1},v_{-1},v_{\pm 1}\}
.

(3)

Otherwise, there is exactly one non-essential loop class
\mathcal{L}
.
In this case, if
P\setminus N\neq\emptyset
, then
v_{1}\in V(\mathcal{L})
, and if
N\setminus P\neq\emptyset
, then
v_{-1}\in V(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

If
i\in P\cap N
, then
\{(i,1),(i,-1)\}\subset\mathcal{I}
so that
S_{(i,1)}([0,1])=S_{(i,-1)}([0,1])=\Delta
is a net interval with neighbour set
v_{\pm 1}
.
This neighbour set is essential since if
\Delta=S_{\sigma}([0,1])
is any net interval, then
S_{\sigma(i,1)}([0,1])
is a net interval with neighbour set
v_{\pm 1}
.

It is clear that exactly one of the conditions must hold.
We verify corresponding properties of the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
.

(1)

If
P=N
, then for any net interval in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, we see that
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v_{\pm 1}
.
Thus every outgoing edge from
v_{\operatorname{root}}
ends at the vertex
v_{\pm 1}
.

(2)

Suppose there is some
i\in P\setminus N
with
\ell-1-i\notin N
.
Let
\Delta=S_{\sigma}([0,1])\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
have
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v_{\pm 1}
.
Then
S_{\sigma(i,1)}([0,1])
is a net interval with
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v_{1}
and
S_{\sigma(\ell-1-i,-1)}([0,1])
is a net interval with
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v_{-1}
.

The other case follows similarly.

(3)

Finally, suppose (i) and (ii) do not hold.
Let
S_{\sigma}([0,1])=\Delta
be a net interval with
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v_{\pm 1}
, and suppose
r_{\sigma}>0
, and
\tau
has
r_{\tau}<0
and
S_{\tau}([0,1])=\Delta
as well.
Suppose
i\in P
so that
\Delta^{\prime}=S_{\sigma(i,1)}([0,1])
is a child of
\Delta
.
Then negating the condition (ii), we either have
i\in N
(and
\Delta^{\prime}
has neighbours generated by
\sigma(i,1)
and
\sigma(i,-1)
) or
\ell-1-i\in P
(and
\Delta^{\prime}
has neighbours generated by
\sigma(i,1)
and
\tau(i,1)
), so
\Delta^{\prime}
has neighbour set
v_{\pm 1}
.
The other case
i\in N
, or the cases where
\Delta^{\prime}=S_{\tau(i,\pm 1)}([0,1])
, follow similarly.
Thus
V(\mathcal{G}_{\ess})=\{v_{\pm 1}\}
.

Since
P\neq N
, if
P\setminus N\neq\emptyset
, there is an edge from
v_{\operatorname{root}}=v_{1}
to
v_{1}
and if
N\setminus P\neq\emptyset
, there are edges from
v_{1}
to
v_{-1}
and
v_{-1}
to
v_{1}
.
Thus the claim follows.

∎

We can now observe the following result.

Lemma 7.7
.

With any choice of probabilities, the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is irreducible and decomposable.

Proof.

The essential loop class
\mathcal{G}_{\ess}
is always irreducible by
Lemma

4.6
.
If there is a loop class
\mathcal{L}
, we observe that either
V(\mathcal{L})
consists of a single vertex or
V(\mathcal{L})=\{v_{1},v_{-1}\}
and there are edges joining
v_{1}
and
v_{-1}
and
v_{-1}
and
v_{1}
.

Since the neighbour sets
v_{1}
and
v_{-1}
have cardinality one, irreducibility follows by
Lemma

4.5
.
Decomposability follows directly from
Lemma

4.10
.
∎

7.2.2.
Multifractal properties of associated measures

We can compute formulas for the loop class
L^{q}
-spectra.

Proposition 7.8
.

(1)

Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is a non-essential loop class.
Let
\mathcal{J}=(P\setminus N)\cup(N\setminus P)
, and for
j\in\mathcal{J}
, write
p_{j}=p_{(j,1)}
if
j\in P\setminus N
, and
p_{j}=p_{(j,-1)}
if
j\in N\setminus P
.
Then

\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\frac{\log\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}p_{j}^{q}}{-\log\ell}.

(2)

Let
T(x)=1-x
.
Then with
\nu=\mu+\mu\circ T
,

\tau_{\mathcal{G}_{\ess}}(q)=\tau_{\nu}(q).

Proof.

(1)

Observe that there is a bijection between paths in
\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}
and words in
\mathcal{J}^{n}
.
Moreover, if
\eta\in\Omega^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}
has corresponding sequence
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
, a direct computation gives that
\rho(\eta)=\left\lVert T(\eta)\right\rVert=p_{j_{1}}\cdots p_{j_{n}}
.
Thus since
v_{\operatorname{root}}=v_{1}\in V(\mathcal{L})
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q)=\tau_{\mathcal{L},\emptyset}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n})\in\mathcal{J}^{n}}p_{j_{1}}\cdots p_{j_{n}}}{-n\log\ell}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}p_{j}\right)^{n}}{-n\log\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}p_{j}^{q}}{-\log\ell}

as claimed.

(2)

Let
\Delta\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
have
\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=v_{\pm 1}
.
By
Proposition

5.4
,
\tau_{\mathcal{G}_{\ess}}(q)=\tau_{\mu|_{\Delta}}(q)
.
But for any Borel set

, we have by
Eq.

3.8

\mathcal{V}(\Delta)=\{\id,T\}

\displaystyle\mu(E)

\displaystyle=\mu(E)p_{(i,1)}+\mu\circ T(E)p_{(i,-1)}\asymp\nu(E).

Thus
\tau_{\mathcal{G}_{\ess}}(q)=\tau_{\nu}(q)
for any

.

∎

\displaystyle=\min\{\tau_{\mathcal{L}}(q),\tau_{\mathcal{G}_{\ess}}(q)\}

This follows directly from

, and the multifractal analysis of this measure was studied extensively in
[
3
,
33
]
.

The IFS in
Eq.

7.3
is a special case of the following general construction.
Fix parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
and some
k\in\N
, and for
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
let
\beta_{j}=\lambda_{1}\cdot(\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1})^{j}
.
Then consider the IFS given by the
k+2
maps

(7.4)

\displaystyle S_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}x

\displaystyle S_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}x+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\beta_{j-1}(1-\beta_{j})\text{ for each }i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

\displaystyle S_{k+1}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}x+(1-\lambda_{2})

under the constraint
S_{k}(1)+\lambda_{2}\leq 1
.
This IFS coincides with
Eq.

7.3
when
k=1
, and coincides with
[
3
, Ex. 8.5]
when
k=2
.

The author proved in
[
33
, Thm. 5.7]
that any self-similar measure associated with the IFS
Eq.

7.3
satisfies the multifractal formalism.
However, the proof in that paper is complicated by the use of the iteration rule given in
Example

3.8
.
If we instead take the iteration rule from
Example

3.7
with corresponding transition graph
\mathcal{G}
, the situation is much more straightforward, even with our general setup.

Proposition 7.13
.

The transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is strongly connected.

Proof.

The definition of the IFS
(S_{i})_{i=0}^{k+1}
ensures for each
i=1,\ldots,k
that

(7.5)

S_{i-1}\circ S_{k+1}=S_{0}\circ S_{i}\text{ and }S_{i-1}(0)<S_{i}(0)<S_{i-1}(1)<S_{i}(1),

and by assumption
S_{k}(1)\leq S_{k+1}(0)
.
Thus the net intervals in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
are the intervals

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=[0,S_{1}(0)]

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=[S_{k-1}(1),S_{k}(1)]

\displaystyle\Delta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=S_{k+1}([0,1])

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle=[S_{i}(1)\cap S_{i+1}(0)]\text{ for }i=0,1,\ldots,k-1

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle=[S_{i}(1),S_{i+1}(0)]\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,k-1

which are ordered from left to write as
(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{0,1},\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{k-1,k},\Delta_{k},\Delta_{k+1})
.
Note that
v_{\operatorname{root}}=\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{k+1})
, and set
v_{i}=\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{i})
for
i=0,\ldots,k
and
v_{i,i+1}=\mathcal{V}(\Delta_{i,i+1})
for
i=0,\ldots,k-1
.
Set
V=\{v_{\operatorname{root}}\}\cup\{v_{i}:i=0,\ldots,k\}\cup\{v_{i,i+1}:i=0,\ldots,k-1\}
.

It follows from
Eq.

7.5
that the net intervals in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
contained in
S_{i}([0,1])
for all
0\leq i\leq k+1
are just the intervals
S_{i}(\Delta_{j})
and
S_{i}(\Delta_{j,j+1})
with
\mathcal{V}(S_{i}(\Delta_{j}))=v_{j}
and
\mathcal{V}(S_{i}(\Delta_{j,j+1}))=v_{j,j+1}
for all valid
j
.
Tracking inclusion of these net intervals in the net intervals in
\mathcal{P}_{1}
yields the graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
with (unlabelled) edges given by

—

(v_{\operatorname{root}},v)\text{ for all }v\in V
.

—

(v_{0},v)\text{ for all }v\in V\setminus\{v_{\operatorname{root}}\}
.

—

(v_{k},v)\text{ for all }v\in V\setminus\{v_{0},v_{0,1}\}
.

—

(v_{i},v)\text{ for all }v\in V\setminus\{v_{0},v_{0,1},v_{\operatorname{root}}\}\text{ and }i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
.

—

(v_{i-1,i},v)\text{ for all }v\in\{v_{0},v_{0,1}\}\text{ and }i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

In particular, we observe that
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is strongly connected.
Note that, for certain choices of
k,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, the list
V
of neighbour sets given above may include repetitions.
In any case, the transition graph
\mathcal{G}
is given by identifying vertices in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
corresponding to the same neighbour set, so
\mathcal{G}
is strongly connected.
∎

Proof.

This is immediate from
