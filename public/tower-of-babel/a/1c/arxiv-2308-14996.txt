The projected dynamic linear model for time series on the sphere
John Zito, Daniel R. Kowal

Abstract
Time series on the unit n n -sphere arise in directional statistics, compositional data analysis, and many scientific fields. There are few models for such data, and the ones that exist suffer from several limitations: they are often computationally challenging to fit, many of them apply only to the circular case of n = 2 n=2 , and they are usually based on families of distributions that are not flexible enough to capture the complexities observed in real data. Furthermore, there is little work on Bayesian methods for spherical time series. To address these shortcomings, we propose a state space model based on the projected normal distribution that can be applied to spherical time series of arbitrary dimension. We describe how to perform fully Bayesian offline inference for this model using a simple and efficient Gibbs sampling algorithm, and we develop a Rao-Blackwellized particle filter to perform online inference for streaming data. In analyses of wind direction and energy market time series, we show that the proposed model outperforms competitors in terms of point, set, and density forecasting.

Introduction

Time series that take values on the unit
n
-sphere
S^{n-1}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1\}
arise in many fields. The field of directional statistics studies measurements about the orientation or direction-of-travel of an object, with data recorded as points on
S^{1}
or
S^{2}

(
Pewsey and García-Portugués 2021
)
. Weather stations across the globe, for example, collect high frequency time series on the direction-of-arrival of the wind (Figure
1
), which is important for monitoring the spread of wildfires or air pollution
(
García-Portugués et al. 2013
;
García-Portugués et al. 2014

. Alternatively, compositional data analysis studies measurements on the standard simplex
C^{n-1}=\{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1,\,p_{i}\geq 0\}
, which can represent proportions of a total such as the market shares of firms in an industry or the fraction of total electricity produced by different energy sources (Figure
5
). The simplex-structure of these data poses many challenges, but one promising approach is to apply a square root transformation to compositional data, thus turning it into unit vector data that can be analyzed using spherical methods
(
Scealy and Welsh 2011
)
.

Because
S^{n-1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a non-Euclidean manifold, naively applying familiar linear, Gaussian methods to spherical time series can generate distorted and misleading results. To see this, consider the simplest instance of spherical time series: the circular case with
n=2
. In this case,
S^{1}
is the unit circle in the plane, so a unit vector
\mathbf{u}_{t}\in S^{1}
can be represented as an angle
a_{t}\in[0,\,2\pi)
, and the correspondence between the two is given by
\mathbf{u}_{t}=[\cos a_{t}\,\sin a_{t}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
and
a_{t}=\text{atan2}(\mathbf{u}_{t})\text{ mod }2\pi
. Figure
1
displays a classic time series from
Fisher 1993
of the hourly direction-of-arrival of the wind at Black Mountain in Australia. The wind in this example is generally blowing from a south easterly direction, and occasionally passes counterclockwise across the
0/2\pi

If we naively apply a standard method like a local-level dynamic linear model to these data, we obtain a (smoothed) trend estimate (blue) that overfits to the spikes and overstates the uncertainty because the linear, Gaussian model does not understand the “wrapped” structure of the circle. By comparison, applying the proposed projected dynamic linear model (Section
2
) that
does
understand this structure, we obtain more satisfactory trend estimates and uncertainty bands (red).

1.1
Previous work

We prioritize the following properties for modeling spherical time series:

•

data coherence
: naive linear, Gaussian methods generate distorted results for spherical data (Figure
1
). It is necessary to apply methods that take into account the special structure of the sphere by, for instance, making use of probability distributions supported on the sphere;

•

generalize to the hyperspherical case
: circular data (
n=2
) is an important special case, but models should also generalize to the
spherical (
n=3
) and hyperspherical (
n>3
) cases;

•

generate full predictive distributions
: we prioritize flexible predictive distributions, rather than just point forecasts, that acknowledge as many sources of uncertainty as possible. This is most commonly achieved using Bayesian inference via the posterior predictive distribution;

•

sequential inference for streaming data
: in the time series setting, observations are often streaming by in real-time and potentially at high frequency. We wish to recursively update our inferences and predictions in an efficient way that adapts to incoming data;

•

exact inference
: statistical computations should be “exact” in the sense that estimates, predictions, or posterior distributions can be approximated arbitrarily well in the limit of some algorithm setting that the user controls, such as the number of Monte Carlo samples;

•

jointly estimate static and dynamic unobservables
: for state space models, there are often unknown static parameters. These should be estimated jointly with the dynamic latent states.

Naive linear, Gaussian methods like the autoregression (AR) or the dynamic linear model (DLM) are simple and convenient to implement and understand, but their lack of data coherence makes them fundamentally inappropriate. The first data coherent time series models were developed by
Craig 1988
,
Breckling 1989
, and
Fisher and Lee 1994
with the aim of extending the ARMA framework to circular data. This work generated the wrapped autoregression (WAR) and the linked autoregression (LAR). Recently these models have been extended by
Holzmann et al. 2006
,
Ailliot et al. 2015
,
Harvey and Palumbo 2023
, and
Harvey et al. 2024
to allow regime-switching and hidden Markov structure. These models focus solely on the case of circular (
n=2
) time series, with no straightforward avenue toward generalizing them to the hyperspherical case. The models are all based on either the von Mises (vM) distribution or the wrapped normal distribution, which are families of distributions on the unit circle whose densities are restricted to being symmetric and unimodal. Finally, these models are developed from a frequentist point of view, and estimation can be computationally challenging, with no standard software implementations.

In parallel to these developments, various state space models have been proposed, which are summarized in
Kurz et al. 2016a
. These state space models have a certain recursive Bayesian interpretation, but they do not admit analytically tractable recursions for filtering or smoothing, so inference is approximated using unscented transformations and deterministic sampling. As with the classical models, the state space models are limited to the circular case, and they only make use of the vM or wrapped normal distribution. This seriously limits the flexibility of the (approximate) forecasting distributions that the models generate. Furthermore, there is little or no practical guidance on how to estimate the static parameters that govern these models. There is software for these models in the form of the
Matlab
toolbox
libDirectional

(
Kurz et al. 2019
)
.

Beyond this modest literature on time series analysis for circular data, there is much less work on the general spherical case (
n\geq 3)
.
Zhu and Müller 2024
recently defined a general spherical autoregressive model. They derive Yule-Walker-type estimators for the model’s parameters, derive their asymptotic properties, and apply the model to compositional data problems. However, this approach only provides point predictions and not full distributional forecasts.
Kurz et al. 2016b
generalized
Kurz et al. 2016a
by using the von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution to define the measurement and state transition distributions of a state space model, thereby allowing the model to scale to arbitrary values of
n
. However, this model retains all of the limitations of the circular ones on which it is based: the vMF distribution is not very flexible, inference in the model is only approximate, and the static parameters are not estimated.

1.2
The projected normal distribution

A recurring and critical shortcoming of existing work is the reliance on the vM(F) and wrapped normal distributions, which have restricted shapes and may not be flexible enough for the demands of real data. This has motivated a surge of recent interest in the projected normal distribution for non-dynamic data
(
Presnell et al. 1998
;
Nuñez-Antonio and Gutiérrez-Peña 2005
;
Wang and Gelfand 2013
;
Hernandez-Stumpfhauser et al. 2017
)
. If
\mathbf{x}\sim\textrm{N}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, then
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}/||\mathbf{x}||_{2}\in S^{n-1}
has the
projected normal distribution
, denoted
\mathbf{u}\sim\textrm{PN}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
. One of the advantages of this parametric family is that it encompasses much more flexible distributional shapes than its competitors. The formula for the projected normal density on the unit circle is given in
Wang and Gelfand 2013
, and Figure
2
displays examples of the shapes it can exhibit. Whereas the vM(F) and wrapped normals densities are only ever symmetric and unimodal, the projected normal density can be asymmetric, bimodal, and antipodal. This means that a fully Bayesian model based on the projected normal distribution has the potential to generate much more flexible predictive distributions.

Figure 2:
The projected normal density can exhibit asymmetry, bimodality, and antipodality.

Another advantage of this class of distributions is that the connection to the normal distribution enables convenient statistical computation. Considering the static case for a moment, if we observe
\mathbf{u}\sim\textrm{PN}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, we can think of this as a partially observed Gaussian random vector
\mathbf{x}=r\mathbf{u}\sim\textrm{N}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where the length
r>0
is an unobserved latent variable. This representation enables classical inference for the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
via an EM algorithm
(
Presnell et al. 1998
)
or Bayesian inference via a Gibbs sampler that alternates between sampling the parameters and sampling the length
(
Nuñez-Antonio and Gutiérrez-Peña 2005
)
. In this way, the introduction of the latent length functions as a data augmentation in the same spirit as
Albert and Chib 1993
for Bayesian probit regression or
Diebolt and Robert 1994
for Gaussian mixture models.

1.3
Our contribution

In this paper, we combine the computational tractability and distributional flexibility of the projected normal distribution with the tools of dynamic linear models to introduce a state space model that can be applied to spherical time series of arbitrary dimension (Section
2
). The model can be represented as a partially-observed linear, Gaussian state space model, which allows us to perform fully Bayesian inference for all of the model’s unobservables (both dynamic latent states and static parameters) using a simple and efficient Gibbs sampler based on the familiar Kalman simulation smoother. Leveraging this unique model tractability, we introduce a Rao-Blackwellized particle filter to perform online inference for streaming data (Section
3
). As such, our model is the first with the potential to realize all of the desired properties that previous models have been unable to balance (Table
1
). To put this model to its fullest use, we provide the first full enumeration of how to produce and evaluate point, set, and density predictions on the hypersphere (Section
4
). These tools are applicable beyond the proposed model and apply for any Bayesian model that generates a predictive distribution on the hypersphere. In applications to wind direction and energy market composition data, we find that our model outperforms others in terms of real-time, out-of-sample probabilistic forecasting (Section
6
). Supplementary material includes additional computing details (Appendix
A
), validation of the sampling algorithm (Appendix
B
), and verification of parameter recovery for simulated data (Appendix
C
).

This is a state space model where the observed unit vectors
\mathbf{u}_{t}\in S^{n-1}
depend on the latent time series

which we must estimate. The observed and unobserved time series are linked by the measurement distribution in (

n\times p

p=n

1
). If we take
p=nm
and set

Presnell et al. 1998

Nuñez-Antonio et al. 2011

\mathbf{s}_{t}

The model is governed by the static parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\,\mathbf{G},\,\mathbf{W})

\mathbf{s}_{t}
evolves according to the VAR(1) given in (
2

\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{W}
determine these dynamics. In some cases we may choose to fix these hyperparameters (like setting
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
for random walk state evolution), but in general we can estimate them. For convenience we choose a (conditionally) conjugate matrix normal inverse Wishart prior:

\mathbf{G},\,\mathbf{W}\sim\textrm{MNIW}_{p,\,p}\big(\nu_{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0},\,\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}_{0}\big).

(4)

We fix the prior hyperparameters to impose the desired amount of shrinkage. Typically, we truncate the prior on
\mathbf{G}
so that it always has eigenvalues inside the unit circle, which enforces stationarity of
\mathbf{s}_{t}
and thus
\mathbf{u}_{t}
. The parameter
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
influences the shape of the measurement distribution, and in general it is not identified because
\textrm{PN}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
\textrm{PN}_{n}(c\boldsymbol{\mu},\,c^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
are the same distribution for any

Hernandez-Stumpfhauser et al. 2017

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}&\boldsymbol{\gamma}\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\sim\textrm{IW}_{n-1}(d_{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{0})\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim\textrm{N}_{n-1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{0}).\end{matrix}

(5)

\displaystyle r_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}\mathbf{s}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\textrm{N}_{n}\left(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right).

3
Sequential inference

Algorithm
1
describes how to perform “batch” or “offline” inference for the PDLM. In this environment, all of the historical data
\mathbf{u}_{1:T}
that we wish to analyze are available at once, and we can use our Gibbs sampler to generate full information estimates of all of the PDLM unobservables: both static parameters and dynamic latent states. But because we are modeling time series data, we must also consider the “streaming” or “online” environment where the observations
\mathbf{u}_{t}
are arriving one-after-another in real-time, and we wish to recursively update our posterior approximation to keep up with the new information. Our Gibbs sampler can be used for this, but it will be computationally inefficient. When generating a sample from the time
t
posterior, naive MCMC does not provide a means of recycling the old sample from the time
t-1
posterior and adjusting it to reflect the influence of the new observation. This ignores the fact that time-adjacent posteriors will typically be quite similar, since they differ by only one observation in their conditioning set. Furthermore, if the data are streaming by at high frequency, there may not be enough time between observations to run the MCMC algorithm long enough.

To overcome these issues and perform online inference for streaming data, we design a particle filter for the PDLM. This algorithm uses importance sampling to recursively construct a weighted Monte Carlo sample from the sequence of data-tempered posteriors. Unlike a naive MCMC approach, this algorithm
does
recycle and adjust the draws from the previous period in order to avoid reprocessing the entire history of data from scratch. This makes the algorithm much more efficient in terms of clock time per-period than the MCMC algorithm when faced with streaming data. Ideally, our online algorithm would recursively traverse the sequence of full posteriors
p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t},\,r_{1:t},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
just as our MCMC algorithm does in the offline setting. Unfortunately, online inference for both the states and the parameters of a state space model is notoriously difficult in general
(
Kantas et al. 2015
)
, and we will not confront this issue here. In what follows we will assume that the static parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are fixed and known, and we henceforth drop them from the notation. Furthermore, while in theory our algorithm targets the sequence of full smoothing distributions
p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t},\,r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
, we only apply it to the filtering problem of recursively approximating the marginal posterior
p(\mathbf{s}_{t},\,r_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
. Because of the Markovian structure of the model, this is sufficient for accessing the model’s forecast distribution in real-time.

As with the Gibbs sampler, the inclusion of
r_{t}
makes the filtering problem challenging, but because of the model’s unique tractability, we will not have to sample it jointly with
\mathbf{s}_{t}
in the usual way. To see this, consider this marginal-conditional decomposition of the full posterior:

p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t},\,r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})=p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t}\,|\,r_{1:t},\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})p(r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t}).

(7)

p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t},\,r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
is not tractable, but as we discussed in Section
2
, we can access
p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t}\,|\,r_{1:t},\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
exactly via the Kalman filter and smoother. The well-known filtering recursions are restated in Table

for convenience. In order, then, to access
p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t},\,r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})

p(r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})

\{r_{1:t}^{(m)}\}\sim p(r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
, we can perfectly simulate
\mathbf{s}_{0:t}^{(m)}\sim p(\mathbf{s}_{0:t}\,|\,r_{1:t}^{(m)},\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
to produce draws from the full posterior. This sampling scheme amounts to a Rao-Blackwellization that can improve the Monte Carlo efficiency of the posterior approximation by significantly reducing the dimension of the problem
(
Robert and Roberts 2021

. Instead of applying Monte Carlo methods to the
(p+1)

jointly, we need only apply them to the one dimensional problem of sampling
r_{t}
. The rest of the posterior is handled exactly, with no added Monte Carlo error.

We will use a particle filter to sequentially traverse the sequence of posteriors
p(r_{1:t}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})

\mathbf{u}_{t}

(8)

and in general we know that

(9)

From Table

(10)

If we apply the change of variables

from Euclidean to polar coordinates, we get

p(r_{k},\,\mathbf{u}_{k}\,|\,r_{1:k-1},\,\mathbf{u}_{1:k-1})=r_{k}^{n-1}\textrm{N}_{n}(r_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k};\,\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{k|k-1},\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{k|k-1})q(\mathbf{u}_{k}),

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{k(r_{1:t})}{g(r_{1:t})}=\frac{k(r_{1:t-1})}{k(r_{1:t-1})}\frac{k(r_{1:t})}{g(r_{1:t})}=\frac{k(r_{1:t-1})}{g(r_{1:t-1})}\frac{k(r_{1:t})}{k(r_{1:t-1})g_{t}(r_{t})}=W_{t-1}\frac{k(r_{1:t})}{k(r_{1:t-1})g_{t}(r_{t})}.

With these preliminaries sorted, we give the RBPF in Algorithm
3

\mathbf{K}_{t}

\text{LogNormal}(\ln r_{t-1},\,\sigma^{2}_{g})

\tau

Resampling helps prevent degeneracy in the weights, but at the cost of additional Monte Carlo noise, since the resulting swarm will now contain duplicate values and be less diverse. To combat this, the mutation phase replenishes the particles by “jittering” them according to an MCMC algorithm that targets
p(r_{t}\,|\,r_{1:t-1},\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})

4
Probabilistic forecasting with spherical data

In this work we are primarily interested in probabilistic forecasting for spherical time series. That is, we are interested in using data
\mathbf{u}_{1:t}\subseteq S^{n-1}
to produce and evaluate a full forecast distribution
f_{t+1|t}(\mathbf{u})
on the sphere that incorporates as many sources of uncertainty as possible about the future observation
\mathbf{u}_{t+1}
. Once we have a forecast distribution
f_{t+1|t}
, we can also extract from it point forecasts
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}\in S^{n-1}
and forecast sets
C_{t+1|t}\subseteq S^{n-1}
. To operationalize this, we consider the case where we have Monte Carlo output
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{J}\sim f_{t+1|t}(\mathbf{u})
from the forecast distribution. In this setting we seek to use these draws to approximate and evaluate point, set, and density predictions on the sphere. Although
Wang and Gelfand 2014
consider spatial point and density (but not set) prediction for circular data (
n=2
), methods for point, set, and density prediction in the general (hyper)spherical case have yet to be enumerated in the literature.

For our purposes specifically, we operate in a Bayesian framework where we take as our forecast distribution
f_{t+1|t}(\mathbf{u})
the posterior predictive distribution:

p(\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})=\int p(\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t},\,\boldsymbol{\phi})p(\boldsymbol{\phi}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})\,\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}.

For the PDLM, Algorithm
1
(or
3
) produces a sample
\{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}\sim p(\boldsymbol{\phi}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
from the full posterior distribution of the dynamic and static unobservables
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\{\mathbf{s}_{1:t+1},\,r_{1:t},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}
. For each draw
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(j)}
, we simulate forward the model dynamics in (
1
) to produce a Monte Carlo sample
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{J}\sim p(\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})
from the predictive distribution. So in practice, we apply the methods described below to the specific task of summarizing posterior predictive simulations. But it is important to bear in mind that these techniques are completely generic, and they would apply to Monte Carlo output
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{J}\sim f_{t+1|t}(\mathbf{u})
coming from any predictive distribution
f_{t+1|t}(\mathbf{u})
produced by any method. Furthermore, we describe everything in terms of one-period-ahead forecasting for simplicity, but the extension to
h
-periods-ahead is straightforward.

4.1
Point forecasting

To generate a point forecast, we can calculate a summary statistic of the draws
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, such as the sample mean or median direction. We opt for the latter
(
Fisher 1985
)
, which is given by

\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}=\underset{\mathbf{m}\in S^{n-1}}{\arg\min}\,\frac{1}{J}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\cos^{-1}\big(\mathbf{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\big),

(16)

where
d(\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{v})=\cos^{-1}(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v})
is the geodesic distance on the hypersphere. We can also use this metric to measure the error between a forecast
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
and a realization
\mathbf{u}
. So given a sequence of point forecasts
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}
and subsequent realizations
\mathbf{u}_{t+1}
, the mean spherical forecast error of a method is

\textrm{MSpFE}=\frac{1}{T-t_{0}-1}\sum\limits_{t=t_{0}}^{T-1}\cos^{-1}\big(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\big).

(17)

This measures the average quality of the one-step-ahead point forecasts (lower is better).

4.2
Set forecasting

To generate a set forecast, we consider posterior predictive credible sets
\mathcal{C}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}\subseteq S^{n-1}
satisfying
P(\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\in\mathcal{C}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:t})=1-\alpha
. To approximate such a set using our draws
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}\}
, we calculate the upper quantile cap
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}
of
Ley et al. 2014
, which satisfies
J^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}1\big(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}\big)\approx 1-\alpha
. In the original context, the quantile cap is used as a descriptive statistic for spherical data. Here, we use it as a Monte Carlo estimate of a posterior summary (the credible set). This calculation proceeds in four steps, which we visualize in Figure
4
for the case of the unit circle
S^{1}

1.

We start with the sample median direction
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}
as in (
16
), and construct a credible set centered around it. In this way,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}
is a (hyper)spherical analog to a prediction interval of the form
\hat{y}\pm\varepsilon
;

2.

we project each draw
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}
onto the median direction, which produces the univariate sample
\{\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}\}\subseteq[-1,\,1]
;

3.

we compute the ordinary sample quantile of the scalar projections, which
Ley et al. 2014
call the
projection quantile

\hat{c}^{(\alpha)}_{t+1|t}=\underset{-1\leq c\leq 1}{\arg\min}\frac{1}{J}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\rho_{\alpha}\left(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}-c\right),\quad\rho_{\alpha}(y)=y(\alpha-1_{y\leq 0}).

This defines a hyperplane
H^{(\alpha)}
that is orthogonal to
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}
and intersects it at the point
\hat{c}^{(\alpha)}_{t+1|t}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}

H^{(\alpha)}
bisects
S^{n-1}
into two upper and lower

;

4.

we take for our forecast set the upper quantile cap:

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in S^{n-1}:\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\geq\hat{c}^{(\alpha)}_{t+1|t}\right\}.

By construction,
100\times(1-\alpha)\%
of the posterior predictive draws
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}
have
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)}\geq\hat{c}^{(\alpha)}_{t+1|t}

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{t+1|t}^{(\alpha)}

4.3
Density forecasting

The sample
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{J}
represents a discrete approximation of the forecast distribution, so to score the distribution forecast as a whole, we use a proper kernel scoring rule like in
Steinwart and Ziegel 2021
. If
f_{t+1|t}(\mathbf{u})
is a forecast distribution on
S^{n-1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{t+1}
the subsequent realization, then

S\left(f_{t+1|t},\,\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}E\left[g(\tilde{\mathbf{u}},\,\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})\right]-E\left[g(\tilde{\mathbf{u}},\,\mathbf{u}_{t+1})\right],\quad\tilde{\mathbf{u}},\,\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}f_{t+1|t}

(18)

is a proper scoring rule so long as the kernel function
g:S^{n-1}\times S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
is symmetric positive definite.
Gneiting 2013
studied several such functions on the sphere, and in this work we take the powered exponential kernel
g(\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{v})=\exp(-\cos^{-1}(\mathbf{u}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v}))
. In this case where
f_{t+1|t}
is represented by the Monte Carlo sample
\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, we approximate the kernel score in (
18
) with

\hat{S}\left(\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\},\,\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{J^{2}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{J}g(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)},\,\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(k)})-\frac{1}{J}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}g(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1}^{(j)},\,\mathbf{u}_{t+1}).

(19)

To score the entire sequence of forecast distributions, we calculate each method’s mean kernel score over time:

\textrm{MKS}=\frac{1}{T-1-t_{0}}\sum\limits_{t=t_{0}}^{T-1}\hat{S}\big(\{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(j)}_{t+1}\},\,\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\big).

(20)

The smaller the MKS is, the better the forecast distributions are on average over time.

5
Competing models

In the forecasting comparisons presented in Section
6
, we compare our PDLM to a basket of alternatives. The first is a naive local-level dynamic linear model (DLM) like we applied in Figure
1
:

\displaystyle a_{t}

\displaystyle=s_{t}+\varepsilon_{t},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\textrm{N}(0,\,v)

(21)

\displaystyle s_{t}

\displaystyle=s_{t-1}+\eta_{t},

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\textrm{N}(0,\,w)

(22)

with independent normal and inverse gamma priors on
s_{0}
,
v
, and
w
. This is fit using using a Gibbs sampler supplied in the
dlm
package in
R

(
Petris 2010
)
. For circular data, we fit this model to an observed time series of angles
a_{t}\in[0,\,2\pi)
, but because the model does not incorporate the circular structure of the data, it produces posterior predictive distributions
p(a_{t+1}\,|\,a_{1:t})
mistakenly supported on the entire real line. To correct for this, we define the posterior predictive functional
\mathbf{u}_{t+1}=[\cos a_{t+1}\,\sin a_{t+1}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in S^{1}
and use
p(\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\,|\,a_{1:t})
to generate forecasts. So as a post-processing step, we forcibly project the ill-supported posterior predictive output to the correct space. As we will see, this naive correction is largely cosmetic, and does not repair the underlying issues with applying a naive linear, Gaussian model to these data.

Next, we consider the local-level state space model proposed by
Kurz et al. 2016b
, which we abbreviate vMF-SSM:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{s}_{t}

\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{indep}}{\sim}\textrm{vMF}_{n}(\mathbf{s}_{t},\,\kappa)

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{s}_{t-1}

\displaystyle\sim\textrm{vMF}_{n}(\mathbf{s}_{t-1},\,\eta).

(24)

\textrm{vMF}_{n}(...)
denotes the von Mises-Fisher distribution. Unlike the DLM, this is a proper, data-coherent time series model for spherical data of arbitrary dimension, and in principle it will produce posterior predictive distributions with the correct support. Unfortunately, it does not admit closed-form recursions for filtering and smoothing, and so these operations must be approximated using unscented transformations and deterministic sampling. This is implemented in the
Matlab
toolbox
libDirectional

(
Kurz et al. 2019
)
. The literature does not provide guidance on how to jointly estimate the static parameters
\kappa
and
\eta
, so we fix
\eta=1
and estimate
\kappa
from a pre-sample using the method in
Sra 2012
.

Lastly, we compare to the spherical autoregression (SAR) of
Zhu and Müller 2024
, which is another data-coherent method that is properly supported on the unit sphere of arbitrary dimension. The SAR assumes
\mathbf{u}_{t}
is stationary, and models it in terms of its “spherical difference”
R_{t}=\mathbf{u}_{t}\ominus\boldsymbol{\mu}
from a time-invariant Fréchet mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
:

\displaystyle R_{t}-\mu_{R}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}(R_{t-1}-\mu_{R})+\cdots+\alpha_{p}(R_{t-p}-\mu_{R})+\varepsilon_{t}.

(25)

Here,
\alpha_{l}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu_{R}=E[R_{t}]
, and
\varepsilon_{t}
are iid mean zero.
Zhu and Müller 2024
also discuss a differenced version of the model (DSAR) where the autoregression applies to the first spherical difference of the data
R_{t}=\mathbf{u}_{t+1}\ominus\mathbf{u}_{t}
, which allows their framework to be applied to certain kinds of nonstationary spherical time series. Yule-Walker type estimators are used for
\alpha_{i}
and the lag order
p
is selected using time series cross-validation. Given a fitted model, this method produces point predictions
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t+1|t}
, but the authors do not provide guidance about generating prediction sets or full predictive distributions, so we cannot produce and evaluate these in our comparisons.

7
Conclusion

There is clear empirical evidence (Figure
1
, Table
3
, etc.) that naive application of ordinary linear, Gaussian methods for spherical time series is strongly inadvisable. We can greatly improve predictive accuracy by using models that are tailored to data on
S^{n-1}
. There have been several such models proposed for the special case of
n=2
, but very few for the more general case of
n>2
, and little of this work has been from the Bayesian point of view. In this paper we proposed a state space model based on the projected normal distribution that can model spherical time series of arbitrary dimension. We described how to perform fully Bayesian inference in this model using an MCMC algorithm for offline inference and an RBPF for online inference. We also enumerated how to produce and evaluate the full range of probabilistic forecasts on a hypersphere of arbitrary dimension, which to the best of our knowledge is a first in this literature. In applications to forecasting wind direction and energy market composition, we showed that the proposed model outperforms its competitors in terms of point, set, and density prediction.

There are numerous promising avenues for future work. First, we focused on the “local level” (
\mathbf{F}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
) variant of our model. Future work could explore other model variants, such as the dynamic regression model (
\mathbf{F}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
). Second, the proposed approach for set prediction applies broadly for predictive models on the
n
-sphere, including spatial models and regression models, and beyond the circular case of
n=2
. Thus, our methods may help construct interpretable summaries of these predictive models. Finally, with a few exceptions, spherical time series methods are notably deficient in software availability. Future work will build upon our replication files (
johnczito/ProjectedDLM
) to provide user-friendly and freely-available statistical software for Bayesian time series on the
n
-sphere.

Appendix B
Simulation: Algorithm
1
“gets it right”

In order to provide evidence that Algorithm
1
has the correct invariant distribution and has been implemented correctly in the computer, we apply the check from
Geweke 2004
. The main idea behind
Geweke 2004
is that our Bayesian model specifies a joint distribution for both observables and unobservables:

p(\mathbf{u}_{1:T},\,r_{1:T},\,\mathbf{s}_{0:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})=\underbrace{p(\mathbf{u}_{1:T},\,r_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{s}_{0:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{(\ref{eq:measurement2})}\underbrace{p(\mathbf{s}_{0:T}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{(\ref{eq:transition},\,\ref{eq:initialization1})}\underbrace{p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{(\ref{eq:gw},\,\ref{eq:sig})}.

(26)

This joint distribution can be simulated in two ways:

•

(
marginal-conditional sampler
) first sample
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
from (
4
,
5
), then
p(\mathbf{s}_{0:T}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
from (
2
,
3
), then
p(\mathbf{u}_{1:T},\,r_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{s}_{0:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
from (
6
). This is a direct sampler that generates iid draws from the joint distribution;

•

(
successive-conditional sampler
) alternate between sampling the conditional distributions
p(r_{1:T},\,\mathbf{s}_{0:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{u}_{1:T})
and
p(\mathbf{u}_{1:T}\,|\,r_{1:T},\,\mathbf{s}_{0:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
. This is a Gibbs sampler that simulates a Markov chain that has (
26
) as its stationary distribution. The first conditional is the usual posterior distribution that is targeted by our MCMC algorithm. The second conditional is the model likelihood (which is implicit in our case).

The marginal-conditional sampler is straightforward to implement using standard software. The successive-conditional sampler depends on the implementation of our MCMC algorithm. If everything (including the MCMC algorithm) has been implemented correctly, then the two samplers should generate draws from the same distribution, and we can verify this using probability plots or a statistical test. Furthermore, if we can conclude that Algorithm
1
has been implemented correctly, we can directly compare its output to that of Algorithm
3
(as we did in Figure
3
), which provides evidence that our particle filter has also been implemented correctly.

Implementing this check requires that we sample from
p(\mathbf{u}_{1:T}\,|\,r_{1:T},\,\mathbf{s}_{0:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which is difficult in general. However, in the special case with
n=2
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
, it is possible. First, we reparametrize the unit vector observation
\mathbf{u}_{t}
as
\mathbf{u}_{t}=[\cos a_{t}\,\sin a_{t}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
for some
a_{t}\in[0,\,2\pi)
, and we set
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=\mathbf{F}_{t}\mathbf{s}_{t}
. We see in (
6

r_{t}\mathbf{u}_{t}=r_{t}\begin{bmatrix}\cos a_{t}\\
\sin a_{t}\end{bmatrix}\sim\textrm{N}_{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{t}^{(1)}\\
\mu_{t}^{(2)}\end{bmatrix},\,\mathbf{I}_{2}\right).

Applying a change-of-variables to polar coordinates, we can rewrite the measurement density as
