Abstract
The semisimple bismash product Hopf algebra
J_{n}=k^{S_{n-1}}\#kC_{n}
for an algebraically closed field
k
is constructed using the matched pair actions of
C_{n}
and
S_{n-1}
on each other. In this work, we reinterpret these actions and use an understanding of the involutions of
S_{n-1}
to derive a new Froebnius-Schur indicator formula for irreps of
J_{n}
and show that for
n
odd, all indicators of
J_{n}
are nonnegative. We also derive a variety of counting formulas including
Theorem

6.2.2
which fully describes the indicators of all
2
-dimensional irreps of
J_{n}
and
Theorem

6.1.2
which fully describes the indicators of all odd-dimensional irreps of
J_{n}
and use these formulas to show that nonzero indicators become rare for large
n
.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the indicators of the simple modules (irreps) of a particular semisimple Hopf algebra, specifically the bismash product
J_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{S_{n-1}}\#\mathbb{C}C_{n}
where
S_{n}
is the symmetric group. We rely especially on the works of
[
12
]
which served as a general resource on Hopf Algebras as well as
[
5
]
which derived an indicator formula for irreps of bismash product Hopf algebras. Additionally, a key result of
[
7
]
(
Theorem

3.2
) showed that there is a one-to-one correspondence between irreducible representations (irreps) of
J_{n}
and group representations of
F_{x}\subset C_{n}
where
F_{x}
is the stabilizer of a permutation
x\in S_{n-1}
under the matched pair action of
C_{n}
on
S_{n-1}
(see
Section

2
). Using this correspondence, we adapt the formulas of
[
5
]
and find new ways to explicitly describe and count irreps of
J_{n}
with certain indicators. Finally, we use code developed in Python to provide insight into negative indicators.

The idea that indicators give insight into the structure of representations started with groups. Given an irreducible character
\chi
of a finite group
G
, averaging the values outputted by the character evaluated at the square of each group element is called the Frobenius-Schur
[
4
]
indicator

\nu(\chi)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g^{2}).

First discovered by its namesakes in 1906, the magic of the Frobenius-Schur indicator is that it always takes the value
0
,
1,
or
-1
. These three values determine whether the associated representation–thought of as
kG
-module–admits a nondegenerate
G
-invariant bilinear form (
\nu(\chi)\not=0
) and if so, if that form is symmetric or skew-symmetric (
\nu(\chi)=1
or
-1
respectively).

Although
[
3
]
extended the indicator to Kac algebras in 1999, explicitly constructing an analogue of the Frobenius-Schur indicator for semisimple Hopf algebras was first done by Linchenko and Montgomery in 2000
[
10
]
. The key idea of
[
10
]
came from the fact that a semisimple Hopf algebra
H
over an algebraically closed field admits a unique two-sided integral element
\Lambda
satisfying
h\Lambda=\varepsilon(h)\Lambda=\Lambda h
for all
h\in H.
It was then found that by computing the (irreducible) character of the second Sweedler power of this integral

\nu(\chi)=\chi((m\circ\Delta)(\Lambda))=\chi\left(\sum\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}\right),

that the resulting sum would also only return
0,1,
or
-1
. Here
m
and
\Delta
are the multiplication and comultiplication on the Hopf algebra. Furthermore, these three values given by
\nu(\chi)
determine whether the associated
H
-module admits a nondegenerate
H
-invariant bilinear form (
\nu(\chi)\not=0
) and if so, if that form is symmetric or skew-symmetric (
\nu(\chi)=1
or
-1
respectively). It is a perfect analogue of its group-theoretic counterpart for semisimple Hopf algebras.

Frobenius-Schur indicators on Hopf algebras are of interest for a variety of reasons, three of which stand out. First, and most obviously, they determine if the associated
H
-module (representation) has an
H
-invariant, bilinear form and further if it is symmetric or skew-symmetric. In fact, it was shown by Linchenko and Montgomery
[
10
]
that
\nu(\chi)\not=0
if and only if the associated simple module (with character
\chi
) is self dual.

Secondly, the indicator has been found to aid in the classification of finite dimensional Hopf algebras. This problem of describing all Hopf algebras of a certain dimension is in general very difficult and tedious. For example, in 2000, Kashina
[
6
]
classified all semisimple Hopf alebgras of dimension
16
and then in 2001, Kashina, Mason, and Montgomery
[
7
]
showed that the computations of all Hopf Algebras of dimension 16 could be improved using the (second) indicator
\nu

[
7
]
. Also
[
8
]
used the indicator to put restrictions on the dimension of a Hopf algebra based on the dimension of its simple modules. For example, they showed that if
H
has an even dimensional simple module, then the dimension of
H
itself must be even
[
8
]
.

Thirdly, and most generally, Frobenius-Schur indicators can be adapted to have broader applications to category theory. If
H
is a semisimple Hopf algebra over
\mathbb{C}
, then we can define Rep
(H)
, which is the category of finite dimensional representation of
H
. In fact, Rep
(H)
is a pivotal fusion tensor category meaning that it is a category with finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects (fusion) with a notion of dualization (pivotal) and tensor product (tensor). It was shown by Mason and Ng in 2005
[
13
]
that the Frobenius-Schur indicator is a categorical invariant. That is, if
H
and
H^{\prime}
are two semisimple Hopf algebras over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
such that Rep
(H)
is equivalent to Rep
(H^{\prime})
as
k
-linear tensor categories, then
\{\nu(\chi)\}_{\chi\in Irr(H)}
and
\{\nu(\chi^{\prime})\}_{\chi\in Irr(H^{\prime})}
are identical.

Although we do not discuss them in this paper, there are abundant applications if one were to expand out to higher indicators. That is, by computing the
n^{\text{th}}
-Sweedler power of
\Lambda
(as described above). For example, in the same paper where the idea is introduced,
[
9
]
prove a version of Cauchy’s Theorem for semisimple Hopf alebras using the regular representation.

In this paper, we focus on the semisimple Hopf algebra
J_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{S_{n-1}}\#\mathbb{C}C_{n}
which is a bismash product of the two groups
S_{n-1}
and
C_{n}
. Matched pairs of groups have been known to group theorists as factorizable groups since the 1950s, but the construction of Hopf algebras from a mathced pair of groups was first proposed by Takeuchi
[
17
]
in 1981. In fact, it was shown by Masuoka
[
11
]
that the Hopf algebra of interest in this paper,

, is one of only two Hopf algebras that arise from the decomposition of

as a matched pair of
S_{n-1}
and
C_{n}
. The other Hopf alebgra being
H_{n}=(J_{n})^{*}
the dual of

for which
[
5
]
have already shown that all indicators of simple modules are
+1
.

Similar work has been done by Timmer
[
18
]
in 2015 using different factorizations of the symmetric group
S_{n}=S_{n-r}G
and considering
H=k^{G}\#kS_{n-r}
. Timmer
[

The goal of this work was to fully describe all irreps of

and state their second indicators

, there exists representations of

with negative indicator which has complicated the problem of classifcation. The organization of the paper is as follows.

In this section, we describe the underlying group actions which make up the structure of the bismash product Hopf algebra

Consider a (finite) group

and

which intersect trivially and whose product is the entire group

Definition 2.1
.

A group
L
is factorizable if it has two subgroups
F
and
G
such that
F\cap G=1
and
L=GF=FG
.

Being able to write that
L=GF
and knowing that
F\cap G=1
implies that
L=FG
and it also implies that every element

can be written uniquely as a product of something in
F
and something in
G
.

Note we cannot say that
L
is a direct (or even semi-direct) product of
F
and
G
because nowhere are we assuming normality of either subgroup.

Definition 2.2

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
F
and
G
be groups. Let
\lhd:G\times F\to G
and
\rhd:G\times F\to F
be group actions. Then
(F,G,\rhd,\lhd)
is a matched pair if for all
x,y\in G
and all
a,b,\in F
,

\displaystyle x\rhd ab

\displaystyle=(x\rhd a)((x\lhd a)\rhd c)

\displaystyle xy\lhd a

\displaystyle=(x\lhd(y\rhd a))(y\lhd a)

While this definition turns out to be equivalent to that of a factorizable group,
[
17
]
did not know that when he coined this term in the ’80s.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
L=GF
be a factorizable group. Then
(F,G,\rhd,\lhd)
is a matched pair where for
x\in G
and
b\in F
, the decomposition below is unique:

xb=(x\rhd b)(x\lhd b).

Conversely, if
(F,G,\rhd,\lhd)
is a matched pair, then
L
which has underlying set
G\times F
and multiplication defined by

(a,x)(b,y)=(a(x\rhd b),(x\lhd b)y)

is a factorizable groups

We use the same definitions as from
[
5
]
. Many of the results given next proved in
[
5
]
or follow immediately from the definitions.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

be a factorizable group where the actions

and

(2)

(3)

Explicitly Counting

Of course, the goal for the project is to try and count explicitly the number of irreps of
J_{n}
which have indicator
+1,-1
, and
0
for a given dimension. This result is very difficult to obtain directly since there are
(n-1)!
permutations and we must check the stabilizer of each permutation in order to determine how many associated representations are induced from
x
(see
Theorem

3.2
). However, using our new understanding of the group actions which describe the structure of
J_{n}
and our new reduced formulas from the previous section, we are able to remove a lot of extraneous computation.

In this section, we show explicitly how to describe all irreps of
J_{n}
of dimension
t
if
t
is odd. Not only do we present formulas for counting the total number of such irreps, but we describe explicitly how to construct the set of permutations
x
which we will later show produce nonzero indicators and what these irreps look like.

Throughout this section, all arithmetic will be done modulo
n
unless otherwise specified. For example, when we write
x(2t)=2x(t)
we mean
x(2t\mod n)=2x(t)\mod n.

For us, an involution is any permutation
x\in S_{n-1}
which is its own inverse (including the identity).

Additionally, we will frequently invoke a result from elementary number theory: The equation
ax=b\mod n
has either no solutions, or gcd
(a,n)
solutions. Specifically, the equation
ax=0\mod n
always has gcd
(a,n)
solutions.

Structure of Permutations with Given Stabilizer

As we have seen from the previous sections, if
t
is odd, then all
t
-dimensional irreps of
J_{n}
have nonnegative indicators (
Proposition

4.3.5
). Furthermore, the only way an (odd)
t
-dimensional irrep of
J_{n}
can have an indicator of
+1
is if the permutation
x
which induces that irrep shares its orbit with an involution.

Therefore, describing the odd dimensional irreps as having indicator either
+1
or
0
is equivalent to understanding and counting the orbits under the matched pair action which contain involutions.

Proposition 5.1.1
.

Let
M_{n/t}
be the set of
x\in S_{n-1}
such that
F_{x}=\langle a^{t}\rangle
. Let
\varphi(n)
be the Euler Totient Function which is the number of integers coprime to

. Then

\displaystyle|M_{n/1}|

\displaystyle=\varphi(n)

(4)

\displaystyle|M_{n/t}|

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)^{t-1}(t-1)!-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s|t\\
s\not=t\end{subarray}}|M_{n/s}|

(5)

Proof.

Let
\varphi(n)=\{1\leq j\leq n\,|\,j\text{ coprime to }n\}
be the Euler Totient function.

The proof is simple counting.
Corollary

4.2.4
tells us that
F_{x}=F
if and only if
x(u)=ux(1)
,
Lemma

4.2.1
says that
x(1)=j
for some

coprime to
n
, and

4.2.3
states that
x
is determined by where it sends
1
. Thus, the number of permutations
x\in S_{n-1}
with full stabilizer is given by the number of choices of
x(1)
, which is the number of integers coprime to
n.
Therefore,
|M_{n/1}|=\varphi(n)
.

Now, let
t>1
be a divisor of
n.
Again,
Corollary

4.2.3
tell us that if we want to count all permutations
x\in S_{n-1}
with stabilizer
F_{x}=\langle a^{t}\rangle
, then to fully determine
x
, we need only understand where it sends
1,2,3,...,t
. Since
x(t)=jt
where

is coprime to
\frac{n}{t}
by

4.2.1
, we know there are
\varphi(\frac{n}{t})
choices for where
x
can send
t.

Now, where can
x
send

? Well it certainly cannot send it to any multiple of
t
since then

would no longer be a bijection. Thus, it can send
1
to any number

where

and

is not a multiple of
t
. There are
n-\frac{n}{t}=(t-1)\frac{n}{t}
such options.

Recall that once we know

, we also know

,
x(1+2t)
,…,
x(1+(\frac{n}{t}-1)t)
. Namely, once we choose

we have fixed
\frac{n}{t}
outputs.

Similarly,

can send
2
to any available value. Since all multiples of
t
and now all values of the form

for

are taken. So there are

possible options for

.

Inductively we obtain:

\underbrace{\varphi\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)}_{\text{choices for }x(t)}\cdot\underbrace{(t-1)\frac{n}{t}}_{\text{choices for }x(1)}\cdot\underbrace{(t-2)\frac{n}{t}}_{\text{choices for }x(2)}\cdots\underbrace{\frac{n}{t}}_{\text{choices for }x(t-1)}.

Now, define

and because this is a necessary condition we have that
a^{t}\in F_{x}
by
Corollary

4.2.3
.

However, we will have over counted here. Because this now counts all

for which
a^{t}\in F_{x}
, and it is not necessarily true that
F_{x}=\langle a^{t}\rangle
for each of these permutations. Namely, we must now remove all

for which

where

. That is, we have over counted by exactly

for each

.

Finally, this gives the desired result,

\displaystyle|M_{n/t}|

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)(t-1)\frac{n}{t}(t-2)\frac{n}{t}\cdots\frac{n}{t}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s|t\\
s\not=t\end{subarray}}M_{n/s}

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)^{t-1}(t-1)!-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s|t\\
s\not=t\end{subarray}}|M_{n/s}|\qed

.

For example, when

Lemma 5.1.3
.

Let
x\in S_{n-1}
with
F_{x}=\langle a^{t}\rangle
. Then there exists an integer

which is coprime to
\frac{n}{t}
(and so therefore invertible modulo

), a permutation

and integers

such that

\displaystyle x(i)

\displaystyle=u_{i}t+\sigma_{x}(i)\mod n\qquad\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq t-1

\displaystyle x(qt)

\displaystyle=qjt\mod n\qquad\text{ for all }1\leq q\leq\frac{n}{t}

\displaystyle x(qt+i)

\displaystyle=qx(t)+x(i)\mod n

Furthermore,

\displaystyle x^{-1}(i)

\displaystyle=-ju_{\sigma_{x}^{-1}(i)}t+\sigma_{x}^{-1}(i)\mod n\qquad\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq t-1

\displaystyle x^{-1}(t)

\displaystyle=j^{-1}t\mod n

\displaystyle x^{-1}(qt+i)

\displaystyle=qx^{-1}(t)+x^{-1}(i)\mod n

a remainder permutation for

.

Proof.
Let
x\in S_{n-1}
with stabilizer
F_{x}=\langle a^{t}\rangle
. We have already established in

4.2.3
that
x
is determined by where it sends

. We now claim that
x
defines a permutation modulo
t
. From,
Lemma

4.2.1
, there exists a
j
coprime to
\frac{n}{t}
(and so invertible modulo
\frac{n}{t}
) with

Now, if

and
x(i)=x(i^{\prime})\mod t
, then
x(i)-x(i^{\prime})
is a multiple of
t
, say
x(i)-x(i^{\prime})=qt
for some value
q
. Then

x(j^{-1}qt+i^{\prime})=x(j^{-1}qt)+x(i^{\prime})=jj^{-1}qt+x(i^{\prime})=qt+x(i^{\prime})=x(i)-x(i^{\prime})+x(i^{\prime})=x(i).

Therefore, because
x
is a bijection,
j^{-1}qt+i^{\prime}=i\mod n
and so

is a multiple of
t
. However, we assumed that both

and

were positive integers smaller than

so their difference is only a multiple of

if their difference is

. Thus,

actually defines a permutation in

.

Specifically, for each

\sigma_{x}\in S_{t-1}
defined by
\sigma_{x}(i):=x(i)\mod t

and a value

.

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=x(x^{-1}(i))=x(tl_{i}+\sigma_{x^{-1}}(i))

Limiting Behavior

Theorem

suggests that the conditions that a permutation

must meet in order to induce an irrep of
J_{n}
with indicator
+1
are very restrictive. This intuition turns out to be correct and can be formalized in the following theorem. However, to prove it, we must give a few bounding relationships.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
E_{n/t}
, where
\frac{n}{t}\geq 2
, be as from (1) in
1
(
Table 1

(Big Table of Commonly Cited Values and Sets)
.

\varphi(m)
be the Euler totient function which is the number of integers smaller than

which are coprime to
m

\omega(m)
is the number of distinct prime divisors of

m\geq 3

(1)

|E_{n/t}|\leq 2^{\omega(n/t)+2}

(2)

\omega(m)\leq 1.3841\frac{\log m}{\log(\log m)}

(3)

\varphi(m)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{m}

(4)

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{2^{1.3841\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\log(m))}}}{\sqrt{m}}=0

Proof.

(1)

This is a well known result which comes from the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Note the special case if
\frac{n}{t}=2
then
E_{n/t}=\{1\}
and
\omega(n/t)=\omega(2)=1
so the bound provided holds.

(2)

Robin proves this in
[
16
]
.

(3)

Let
m=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots p_{u}^{\alpha_{u}}
. Then since
\alpha_{i}\geq 1

\frac{\varphi(m)^{2}}{m}=\prod_{i=1}^{u}\frac{(p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}(p_{i}-1))^{2}}{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}=\prod_{i=1}^{u}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-2}(p_{i}-1)^{2}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{u}p_{i}^{-1}(p_{i}-1)^{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{u}\frac{(p_{i}-1)^{2}}{p_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Note the last step is because if
p_{i}>2
, then
(p_{i}-1)^{2}=p_{i}^{2}-2p_{i}+1=p_{i}(p_{i}-2)+1\geq p_{i}+1\geq p_{i}
and so
\frac{(p_{i}-1)^{2}}{p_{i}}\geq 1
. Of course, if
p_{i}=2
then
\frac{(p_{i}-1)^{2}}{p_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Finally, we get that

\frac{\varphi(m)^{2}}{m}\geq\frac{1}{2}\implies\varphi(m)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{m}.

(4)

This is elementary calculus. Let
L=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{2^{1.3841\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\log(m))}}}{\sqrt{m}}=0
. Then a standard logarithm trick gives

\displaystyle\log L

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1.3841\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\log(m))}\log 2-\log\left(\sqrt{m}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}-\frac{1}{2}\log(m)=-\infty

and so
L=0.
∎

Theorem 7.4
.

by

J_{n}
of dimension
t=[F:F_{x}]

Thus, there are
\frac{n}{t}|M_{n/t}|

of dimension

From

,

is the number of irreps of

of dimension

+1
and so the ratio is given by

To show convegence to

and then show that this ratio converges to zero. We do this in two cases, first for

(1)

First, an upper bound on

\displaystyle I_{n/t}^{(+1)}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{j\in E_{n/t}}\sum_{r=1}^{t}\alpha_{j,n/t}O_{n/t,r,j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{t}2^{\omega(n/t)+2}\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)^{\frac{t+r}{2}}\frac{t!}{r!2^{\frac{t-r}{2}}\left(\frac{t-r}{2}\right)!}\qquad\text{ \hyperref@@ii[lem:some bounds (1)]{\lx@cref{creftype}{lem:some bounds}\penalty\ \lx@cref{refnum}{*}{lem:some bounds}\penalty\ \ref*{lem:some bounds (1)}}
}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{t}2^{1.3841\frac{\log(n/t)}{\log(\log(n/t))}+2}\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)^{\frac{t+r}{2}}\frac{t!}{r!2^{\frac{t-r}{2}}\left(\frac{t-r}{2}\right)!}\qquad\text{ \hyperref@@ii[lem:some bounds (2)]{\lx@cref{creftype}{lem:some bounds}\penalty\ \lx@cref{refnum}{*}{lem:some bounds}\penalty\ \ref*{lem:some bounds (2)}}

\frac{n}{t}|M_{n/t}|\geq\frac{n}{t}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{n}{t}}\left(\frac{n}{t}\right)^{t-1}(t-1)!-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s|t\\

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{t}2^{1.3841\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\log(m))}+2}\left(m\right)^{\frac{t+r}{2}}\frac{t!}{r!2^{\frac{t-r}{2}}\left(\frac{t-r}{2}\right)!}}{m\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{m}\left(m\right)^{t-1}(t-1)!-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s|t\\

tells us that

-dimensional irreps of

We summarize the results of these limit results as follows.

Corollary 7.5
.

Fix
t
and let
n=mt
. Then for large
m
(and so large

(1)

i_{n-1}
is much less than
(n-1)!

(2)

|M_{n/t}|

(3)
