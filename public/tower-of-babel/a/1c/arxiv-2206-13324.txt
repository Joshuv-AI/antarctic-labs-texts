Quantum theory, thermal gradients and the curved Euclidean space
S. Ganesh

Abstract
The Euclidean space, obtained by the analytical continuation of time, to an imaginary time, is used to model thermal systems. In this work, it is taken a step further to systems with spatial thermal variation, by developing an equivalence between the spatial variation of temperature in a thermal bath and the curvature of the Euclidean space. The variation in temperature is recast as a variation in the metric, leading to a curved Euclidean space. The equivalence is substantiated by analyzing the Polyakov loop, the partition function and the periodicity of the correlation function. The bulk thermodynamic properties like the energy, entropy and the Helmholtz free energy are calculated from the partition function, for small metric perturbations, for a neutral scalar field. The Dirac equation for an external Dirac spinor, traversing in a thermal bath with spatial thermal gradients, is solved in the curved Euclidean space. The fundamental behavior exhibited by the Dirac spinor eigenstate, may provide a possible mechanism to validate the theory, at a more basal level, than examining only bulk thermodynamic properties. Furthermore, in order to verify the equivalence at the level of classical mechanics, the geodesic equation is analyzed in a classical backdrop. The mathematical apparatus is borrowed from the physics of quantum theory in a gravity-induced space-time curvature. As spatial thermal variations are obtainable at QCD or QED energies, it may be feasible for the proposed formulation to be validated experimentally. Keywords : Quantum theory, Curved Euclidean space, Dirac Equation, Thermal gradient, Equivalence, Scalar Field PACS numbers : 04.62.+v, 11.10.Wx, 03.65.-w

\times\Big[i\pi_{j}({\bf{x}})\Big\{\phi_{j+1}({\bf{x}})-\phi_{j}({\bf{x}})\Big\}-s({\bf{x}})\mathcal{H}_{j}\Delta\beta\Big]\Bigg\}

(17)

=\lim_{\Delta\beta\rightarrow 0}\int\left(\prod\limits_{j}d\phi_{j}\frac{d\pi_{j}}{2\pi}\right)\exp\Bigg\{\sum_{l}\Big(\Delta\beta\int d^{3}x\\
\times s({\bf{x}})\left[i\pi_{l}({\bf{x}})\frac{\left\{\phi_{l+1}({\bf{x}})-\phi_{l}({\bf{x}})\right\}}{s({\bf{x}})\Delta\beta}-\mathcal{H}_{l}\right]\Big)\Bigg\}.

(18)

Let us recollect that,
s({\bf{x}})=\sqrt{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}
. Moreover, for the metric
G^{Euc}=(s({\bf{x}})^{2},1,1,1)
, we have,
\sqrt{|G^{Euc}|}=s({\bf{x}})
. Denoting the determinant of the Euclidean metric,
|G^{EUC}|
, by
g_{e}
, we obtain in the continuum limit:

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},\phi,\beta_{0})=\int D\phi\int\frac{D\pi}{2\pi}\exp\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}

\displaystyle\times\left[i\pi({\bf{x}},\tau)\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}}D_{0}\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)-\mathcal{H}(\pi({\bf{x}},\tau),\phi({\bf{x}},\tau))\right]\Bigg\},

where,
D_{0}
is the covariant derivative w.r.t.
\tau
. For a scalar
\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)
,
D_{0}\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)=\frac{\partial\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)}{\partial\tau}
.
The exponent in Eq.
III.1
, is consistent with the action in a curved space if
\pi({\bf{x}},\tau)
is identified as the proper
\pi
, i.e.,
\pi=\sqrt{h_{00}}\pi^{0}=i\sqrt{h_{00}}\frac{\delta L}{\delta\partial_{0}\phi}
.
The form of Eq.
III.1
, incorporates the case where the 3-D space is also curved, i.e.,
\int d^{3}x\rightarrow\sqrt{|G^{Euc}_{ij}|}d^{3}x,\forall i,j=1.2.3
.
In this case,
\sqrt{g_{e}}d\tau d^{3}x=s(x)\sqrt{|G^{Euc}_{ij}|}d\tau d^{3}x
.
For this work, however, we continue to consider,
G^{Euc}_{ij}=I_{3}
.
Compactly,

\displaystyle K(\phi_{f},\phi,\beta_{0})=\int D\phi\int\frac{D\pi}{2\pi}e^{S_{E}(\beta_{0})},

(20)

where,
S_{E}
is the Euclidean action in the curved Euclidean space, and

S_{E}(\beta_{0})=\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}\\
\times\left[i\pi({\bf{x}},\tau)\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}}D_{0}\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)-\mathcal{H}(\pi({\bf{x}},\tau),\phi({\bf{x}},\tau))\right].

(21)

The partition function is then:

Z(\beta_{0})=tr\left[K(\phi_{f},\phi_{0},\beta_{0})\right]\\
=tr\left[\int D\phi\int\frac{D\pi}{2\pi}e^{S_{E}(\beta_{0})}\right]\\
=\int_{periodic}D\phi\int\frac{D\pi}{2\pi}e^{S_{E}(\beta_{0})}.

(22)

The trace operation implies,
\phi_{f}=\phi_{0}\Rightarrow\phi({\bf{x}},\beta_{0})=\phi({\bf{x}},0)
.
This leads to periodicity being introduced in the curved Euclidean space, just like in the flat Euclidean space. We shall look at the periodicity in more detail in Sec.
IV
.
Equation
III.1
, plays a pivotal role in establishing the concept of a curved Euclidean space, as an elegant formalism to model spatial thermal gradients.
For a small temperature variation, i.e., for small values of
s({\bf{x}})-1
, the partition function,
Z(\beta_{0})
, and subsequently, the energy expectation value and entropy, have been calculated for a neutral scalar field in Sec.
V
.

In the current proposal, with the interpretation of the variation of temperature as a variation in the metric, we have,

\beta({\bf{x}})=\sqrt{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}\beta_{0}.

(23)

This is reminiscent of the Ehrenfest and Tolman relation
ET1
;
ET2
.
The Ehrenfest and Tolman relation for a diagonal metric tensor
diag(g_{00}({\bf{x}}),1,1,1)
, gives,

\beta({\bf{x}})=\sqrt{-g_{00}({\bf{x}})}\beta_{0}.

(24)

Apart from the replacement of
-g_{00}({\bf{x}})
with
h_{00}({\bf{x}})
, the two relations, given by Eqs.
23
and
24
, are similar, mathematically.
The physics, however, is significantly different. Equation
24
is interpreted as the appearance of thermal gradients, in a system under global equilibrium, in the presence of a gravitational field. The Lorentzian space-time is curved due to gravity.
On the other hand, Eq.
23
is interpreted as the curvature of the Euclidean space (not Lorentzian) due to thermal gradients. The thermal gradients arise due to heat sources and sinks (Ref. Eqs.
6
and
7
).
Gravitational field is absent, and the Lorentzian space continues to be flat.
The thermal system is under local thermal equilibrium, and not global equilibrium.
The similarities and differences lead to physical consequences.
Figures
5
and
8
, highlight the similarities and differences between the effect of a curved Euclidean and a curved Lorentzian space, on a Dirac fermion eigenstate. This is discussed further in Sec.
VII.6
.

III.2
Generic Hamiltonian

Let
H(t)
be the form of the Hamiltonian, and
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\pi}({\bf{x}},t),\hat{\phi}({\bf{x}},t),t)\equiv\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},t)
, be the corresponding Hamiltonian density.
The analytic continuation,
t\rightarrow i\tau
, gives:

\displaystyle U(\mathcal{H},\beta_{0})=\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\beta_{0}}\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},\tau)d\tau d^{3}x\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int\Big\{F({\bf{x}},s({\bf{x}})\beta_{0})-F({\bf{x}},0)\Big\}d^{3}x\right),

(25)

where
F({\bf{x}},\tau)=\int\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},\tau)d\tau
.
In the case of uniform temperature, the result can be written as:

\displaystyle U(\mathcal{H},\tau_{0})=\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},\tau)d\tau d^{3}x\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\int\Big\{F({\bf{x}},\tau_{0})-F({\bf{x}},0)\Big\}d^{3}x\right).

(26)

Comparing Eqs.
III.2
and
III.2
, the final result in Eq.
III.2
is obtained by a transformation,
\tau_{0}\rightarrow s({\bf{x}})\beta_{0}
, where
\tau_{0}
itself is given by
i\tau_{0}\leftarrow t
. The metric,
ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}
, would then transform to:

ds^{2}=s({\bf{x}})^{2}d\beta^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}.

(27)

Note:
{\bf{x}}\equiv(x,y,z)
.
Again, this is a curved space with
h_{00}({\bf{x}})=s({\bf{x}})^{2}
, indicating a curved Euclideanized space.

The results of Sec.
III.1
and Sec.
III.2

indicate, that the evaluation of the partition function in the context of a spatial thermal variation, seamlessly leads to the re-interpretation of the spatial thermal variation as a curved Euclidean space.

III.3
Other Thermodynamic Quantities

With the re-interpretation of the temperature gradient as a system with uniform temperature,
\beta_{0}
, in a curved Euclidean space, the other thermodynamic quantities can be determined from the partition function
Z(\beta_{0})
.
The Helmholtz free energy,
A
, can be defined as:

A(\beta_{0})=-\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}\ln(Z(\beta_{0})).

(28)

Since
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\forall i,j=1,2,3
, the volume,
\mathbb{V}
, remains the same as a flat Euclidean space.
The entropy,
S(\beta_{0})
, becomes:

S(\beta_{0})=-\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\Theta_{0}}\right)_{\mathbb{V}},

(29)

where
\Theta_{0}=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}
.

IV
Periodicity of the Correlation Function

For the proposed equivalence between the thermal gradients and the curved Euclidean space to be valid, the periodicity of the correlation function, in a curved Euclidean space, needs to be established.
We had touched upon the periodicity of the curved Euclidean space in Eq.
22
.
This section explores the periodicity of the correlation function in greater detail.

IV.1
Time invariant Hamiltonian

The case where
\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}})
is time invariant, is first considered.
From Eq.
III.1
, we get,

\displaystyle U(\mathcal{H},\beta_{0})=\exp\left(-\left[\int s({\bf{x}})\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}})d^{3}x\right]\beta_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-H^{\prime}\beta_{0}\right).

(30)

The periodicity of the correlation function is now manifest. An Heisenberg operator,
A
, would evolve as
e^{-H^{\prime}\beta_{0}}A(t,i0)e^{H^{\prime}\beta_{0}}=A(t,i\beta_{0})
. For any two Heisenberg operators,
A
and
B
, and partition function,
Z(=Z(\beta_{0}))
, we proceed on lines similar to the standard (KMS) approach
martin
;
kubo
;
Ashok
,

\displaystyle\langle A_{h}(t,i0)B_{h}(t^{\prime},i0)\rangle=Z^{-1}Tr\left[e^{-\beta_{0}H^{\prime}}A(t,i0)B(t^{\prime},i0)\right]

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}Tr\left[e^{-\beta_{0}H^{\prime}}A(t,i0)e^{\beta_{0}H^{\prime}}e^{-\beta_{0}H^{\prime}}B(t^{\prime},i0)\right]

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}Tr\left[e^{-\beta_{0}H^{\prime}}B(t^{\prime},i0)A(t,i\beta_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\langle B(t^{\prime},i0)A(t,i\beta_{0})\rangle~~~~~.

(31)

This result reinforces the interpretation, that, in the curved Euclidean space, the topology is that of a cylinder of uniform radius
\frac{\beta_{0}}{2\pi}
.
The value of the ”constant”
\beta
, in the R.H.S. of Eq.
5
, would then be
\beta_{0}
.

IV.2
Generic Hamiltonian

We now look at a generic Hamiltonian,
H(t)=H_{0}+H_{I}(t)
, where
H_{0}
is the free Hamiltonian, and
H_{I}(t)
is the interaction part.
H_{0}
continues to be time invariant, and the time dependence comes from
H_{I}(t)
.
Let
\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},t)
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}({\bf{x}})
and
\mathcal{H}_{I}({\bf{x}},t)
be the corresponding Hamiltonian densities.
In the curved Euclidean space, the effective free Hamiltonian becomes,

H_{0}^{\prime}=\int s({\bf{x}})\mathcal{H}_{0}({\bf{x}})d^{3}x
. Then, the Interaction picture is given by (as
H_{0}^{\prime}
part of the Hamiltonian is time invariant),

\phi_{I}(\tau_{0})=\exp(\tau_{0}H_{0}^{\prime})\,\phi(0)\,\exp(-\tau_{0}H_{0}^{\prime}).

(32)

The Heisenberg operator,
\phi_{H}(\tau_{0})
, is given by:

\displaystyle\phi_{H}(\tau_{0})=\exp\left(\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}d\tau d^{3}x\right)\phi(0)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}d\tau d^{3}x\right).

(33)

The relation between the Heisenberg and Interaction picture is then:

\displaystyle\phi_{H}(\tau_{0})=\exp\left(\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}d\tau d^{3}x\right)\exp(-\tau_{0}H_{0}^{\prime})\phi_{I}(\tau_{0})

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\tau_{0}H_{0}^{\prime}\right)\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}d\tau d^{3}x\right)

\displaystyle=S^{-1}(\tau_{0})\phi_{I}(\tau_{0})S(\tau_{0}),

(34)

where,

S(\tau_{0})=\exp\left(\tau_{0}H_{0}^{\prime}\right)\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}d\tau d^{3}x\right).

For convenience, let us define
R(\tau_{0})
as:

\displaystyle R(\tau_{0})=\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{s({\bf{x}})\tau_{0}}\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},\tau)d\tau d^{3}x\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\tau_{0}H_{0}^{\prime})S(\tau_{0}).

(35)

The periodicity of the correlation function is now analyzed for
0<\tau_{0}<\beta_{0}
.

G_{\beta 0}(0,\tau_{0})=\langle T\left[\phi_{H}(0)\phi_{H}^{\dagger}(\tau_{0})\right]\rangle\\
=Z^{-1}(\beta_{0})Tr\left\{R(\beta_{0})T\left[\phi_{H}(0)\phi_{H}^{\dagger}(\tau_{0})\right]\right\}\\
=Z^{-1}(\beta_{0})Tr\left\{\pm R(\beta_{0})\phi_{H}^{\dagger}(\tau_{0})\phi_{H}(0)\right\}\\
=\pm Z^{-1}(\beta_{0})Tr\left\{R(\beta_{0})R^{-1}(\beta_{0})\phi_{H}(0)R(\beta_{0})\phi_{H}^{\dagger}(\tau_{0})\right\}.

(36)

It is easily seen from Eq.
IV.2
, that,

\displaystyle R^{-1}(\beta_{0})\phi_{H}(0)R(\beta_{0})=\phi_{H}(\beta_{0}).

(37)

This gives,

\displaystyle G_{\beta 0}(0,\tau_{0})=\pm Z^{-1}(\beta_{0})Tr\left\{R(\beta_{0})\phi_{H}(\beta_{0})\phi_{H}^{\dagger}(\tau_{0})\right\}

\displaystyle=\pm G_{\beta 0}(\beta_{0},\tau_{0}),

(38)

which manifests the periodicity of the correlation function.
The
\pm
sign depends on whether the particle is a fermion or a boson.
The results of Eqs.
IV.2
and
IV.2

are similar in form, to the results of the uniform temperature case (flat Euclidean space)
Ashok
.
The evaluation of the Green’s function, however, is a lot more involved as compared to the uniform temperature case.

The question that now arises is, what happens to periodicity, if we had taken
s({\bf{x}}_{i})=1
instead of
s(0)=1
? Let
s_{i}({\bf{x}}_{i})=1
and
\beta({\bf{x}})=s_{i}({\bf{x}}_{i})\beta_{i}
. Let’s define

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(\beta_{i})=\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{s_{i}({\bf{x}})\beta_{i}}\mathcal{H}({\bf{x}},\tau)d\tau d^{3}x\right).

(39)

Equation
IV.2
would now read as,

\displaystyle G_{\beta i}(0,\tau_{0})=\pm G_{\beta i}(\beta_{i},\tau_{0}).

(40)

This however does not mean that the periodicity has changed from
\beta_{0}
to
\beta_{i}
, by merely redefining
s(x)
.
The function
R^{\prime}(\beta_{i})
is exactly identical to
R(\beta_{0})
since
s_{i}({\bf{x}})\beta_{i}=s({\bf{x}})\beta_{0}
.
The correlation function,
G_{\beta i}(0,\tau_{0})
, is identical to
G_{\beta 0}(0,\tau_{0})
.
The topological cylinder is non-uniform as shown in Fig.
1
, and hence possesses a non-uniform periodicity.
Only the reference point related to the non uniform periodicity, has changed from
\beta_{0}
to
\beta_{i}
.

In summary, it is seen that the periodicity of the correlation function, continues to be maintained in a curved Euclidean space.

V
Neutral Scalar Field

In this section, Eq.
22
is used to determine the partition function for a neutral scalar field, for the case where the temperature variations are small. This requirement translates to
s({\bf{x}})-1~(=\eta({\bf{x}}))
being small.
Subsequently, the energy, the Helmholtz free energy and entropy are calculated.

From Eq.
20
, the transition amplitude for a scalar,
\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)
, would be given by:

K(\phi_{f},\phi,\beta_{0})=\int D\phi\int\frac{D\pi}{2\pi}\exp\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}\\
\times\left[i\pi({\bf{x}},\tau)\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}}\frac{\partial\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)}{\partial\tau}-\mathcal{H}\left(\pi({\bf{x}},\tau),\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)\right)\right]\Bigg\}.

(41)

The Lagrangian density for a neutral scalar field reads:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi-m^{2}\phi^{2}\right),

(42)

and the proper conjugate momenta is,
\pi=\frac{-i}{\sqrt{h_{00}}}\partial_{0}\phi
.
Consequently, the Hamiltonian density is:

\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\pi^{2}+(\nabla\phi)^{2}+m^{2}\phi^{2}\right).

(43)

The partition function then reads,

\displaystyle Z=trK(\phi_{f},\phi,\beta_{0})=\int_{periodic}D\phi\int\frac{D\pi}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\times\exp\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}\Big[i\pi({\bf{x}},\tau)\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}}\frac{\partial\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)}{\partial\tau}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\pi^{2}+(\nabla\phi({\bf{x}},\tau))^{2}+m^{2}\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)^{2}\right)\Big]\Bigg\}.

To perform the
D\pi
integrals, we switch to the discretized version and divide the space into
M^{3}
cubes of length
\Delta a
each.
Completing the square and integrating out
\pi({\bf{x}})
,
we get,

\displaystyle Z=\Bigg[\prod_{N}\prod_{l=1}^{M^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\Delta\tau\Delta a^{3}\sqrt{g_{e}({\bf{x}}_{l})}}\right)^{1/2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\times\int_{periodic}D\phi\exp\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\int d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}

\displaystyle\times\left[-\frac{1}{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}-(\nabla\phi)^{2}-m^{2}\phi^{2}\right]\Bigg\}.

(45)

For the sake of convenience, the continuum domain has been retained for the
\int D\phi
integrals.
Now,
\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)
can be decomposed as:

\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathbb{V}}\Theta_{0}}}\sum_{i}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{ih_{00}p^{0}\tau\right\}e^{i{\bf{p}}_{i}.{\bf{x}}}\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}},\\

(46)

with,
p^{0}=h^{00}C_{n}
, being the discrete Matsubara frequency, and

C_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{\beta_{0}}
for integer
n
.
Also,
\Theta_{0}=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}}
, is the ”constant” temperature in the curved Euclidean space, and
{\mathbb{V}}
is the volume.
This, finally, provides the Fourier decomposition as:

\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{0}}{{\mathbb{V}}}}\sum_{i}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iC_{n}\tau}e^{i{\bf{p}}_{i}.{\bf{x}}}\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{i}}.

(47)

The Matsubara frequency has been discretized due to the periodicity condition imposed on
\phi
.
The above form of the Matsubara frequency, keeps the phase factor,
\exp(iC_{n}\tau)
, independent of
{\bf{x}}
,
and in Sec.
VII
, can be seen to also solve the Dirac equation for a spinor.
A more detailed explanation of the Matsubara frequency, in the context of a curved Euclidean space, can be found in Sec.
VII.2
.

Substituting
\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)
from Eq.
47
, the exponent in Eq.
V
can be written as

\int d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}\left[-\frac{1}{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}-(\nabla\phi)^{2}-m^{2}\phi^{2}\right]\\
=-\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{0}^{2}}{{\mathbb{V}}}\int d^{3}x\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\Big(\frac{C_{n}^{2}}{s({\bf{x}})}+s({\bf{x}}){\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}\\
+s({\bf{x}})m^{2}\Big)e^{i({\bf{p}}_{i}-{\bf{p}}_{j}).{\bf{x}}}\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{j}}^{*}\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{i}}.

(48)

Let
s({\bf{x}})=1+\eta({\bf{x}})
. If
\eta({\bf{x}})
is small, then
\frac{1}{s({\bf{x}})}\approx 1-\eta({\bf{x}})
.
Expand
\eta({\bf{x}})
in terms of its Fourier components,
\eta({\bf{x}})=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{V}}}\sum_{q}\eta({\bf{q}})\exp(i{\bf{q}}.{\bf{x}})
. After carrying out the
\int d^{3}x
integral, and summing over
{\bf{q}}
to eliminate the resulting
\delta
function, one obtains,

\int d\tau\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}\left[-\frac{1}{h_{00}({\bf{x}})}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\tau}-(\nabla\phi)^{2}-m^{2}\phi^{2}\right]\\
=-\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{0}^{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{j}}^{*}\Bigg[(C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\delta_{ij}\\
+\frac{1}{{\mathbb{V}}}\eta_{f}({\bf{p}}_{j}-{\bf{p}}_{i})(-C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\Bigg]\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{i}},

(49)

where, the
\delta
function, in the discretized space, is defined as:

\delta_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&if~{\bf{p}}_{i}={\bf{p}}_{j},\\
0&\it otherwise.\end{cases}

Let
J
be the Jacobian for converting the integral measure,
\int D\phi({\bf{x}},\tau)
, to the measure
\int D\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}}
.
The Jacobian,
J
, is lumped along with the factor
\prod_{N}\prod_{l}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta\tau\Delta a^{3}}\right)^{1/2}
, into an overall factor,
\mathcal{N}
. The factor,
\mathcal{N}
, does not affect the dynamics of the system
kapusta
;
Nbeta
. The new factor,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{e}({\bf{x}}_{l})}}
, is independent of
\beta_{0}
and
{\mathbb{V}}
. Consequently, it is irrelevant to the thermodynamics, and is clubbed into
\mathcal{N}
.

The volume,
{\mathbb{V}}
, can be represented in the discrete momentum domain as,
{\mathbb{V}}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{\Delta p}\right)^{3}
.
Substituting the expression in Eq.
49
, inside the exponent of Eq.
V
, we get,

\displaystyle Z=\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{NM^{3}}{2}}\int D\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}}\exp\Bigg\{\frac{-1}{2}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}

\displaystyle\times\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{j}}^{*}\Bigg[\beta_{0}^{2}(C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle\times\Big\{I_{ij}+\frac{\delta_{ij}}{C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2}}\eta_{f}({\bf{p}}_{j}-{\bf{p}}_{i})

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Delta p^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(-C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\Big\}\Bigg]\phi_{n,{\bf{p}}_{i}}\Bigg\}.

(50)

Note that the matrix,
\frac{\delta_{ij}}{C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2}}
, is the inverse of the diagonal matrix,
(C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\delta_{ij}
, and there is no implied sum over repeated indices.
Finally, evaluating the
\int D\phi_{{\bf{p}}}
Gaussian integrals,

\displaystyle Z=\mathcal{N}\prod_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigg\{\det\Bigg[\beta_{0}^{2}(C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle\times\Big\{I_{ij}+\frac{\delta_{ij}}{C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2}}\eta_{f}({\bf{p}}_{j}-{\bf{p}}_{i})

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Delta p^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(-C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\Big\}\Bigg]\Bigg\}^{-1/2}.

(51)

To calculate
\ln(Z)
,
we make use of the identities,
\det(M)=\exp(tr\ln(M))
, and
\ln(1+X)=\sum_{r}\frac{-1^{r-1}}{r}X^{r}
. After dropping the irrelevant factor,
\mathcal{N}
, we get,

\ln(Z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{i}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\beta_{0}^{2}(C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}^{2}+m^{2})\right)\Big]\\
-\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}tr\sum_{r}\frac{-1^{r-1}}{r}\Big\{\frac{\delta_{ij}}{C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2}}\eta_{f}({\bf{p}}_{j}-{\bf{p}}_{i})\\
\times\frac{\Delta p^{3}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(-C_{n}^{2}+{\bf{p}}_{i}{\bf{p}}_{j}+m^{2})\Big\}^{r}.

(52)

The trace operation is on the indices,
i,j
.
We substitute
C_{n}=\frac{2\pi n}{\beta_{0}}
,
Approximating the power series in
r
to the first order, and differentiating w.r.t.
\beta_{0}
,
and finally, going over to the continuum limit in the momentum space,

VI
Classical system and the Geodesic Equation

This section explores the applicability of the equivalence between spatial thermal gradients, and curved Euclidean space, in a classical scenario.
We determine the equations describing the kinetics of a massive test particle in thermal medium with spatial thermal gradients, and compare it with the geodesic equations of the curved Euclidean space.
Besides, this provides an interpretation of the geodesics in a curved Euclidean space.

VI.1
The Geodesic Equation

Let us consider a candidate metric, where the temperature varies across the two dimensional
x-z
plane.

ds^{2}=h_{00}(x,z)dt^{2}-dx^{2}-dy^{2}-dz^{2},

(61)

with
t=i\beta
.
The temperature variation is given by
\Theta(x,z)=\frac{1}{\beta_{0}\sqrt{h_{00}(x,z)}}
.

Two of the geodesic equations for the above metric include,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial h_{00}(x,z)}{\partial x}-2\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\lambda^{2}}=0,

\displaystyle 2\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial\lambda^{2}}-\left(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial h_{00}(x,z)}{\partial z}=0,

(62)

where,
\lambda
is an affine parameter.
Eliminating
\left(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\lambda}\right)^{2}

between the set of equations in Eq.
VI.1
, we obtain,

\frac{\partial h_{00}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial\lambda^{2}}=\frac{\partial h_{00}}{\partial z}\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\lambda^{2}}.

(63)

Equation
63
indicates that, for any non-zero
\frac{\partial h_{00}}{\partial x}
and
\frac{\partial h_{00}}{\partial z}
, the particle will bend, and the geodesic is not straight.
For photons, the above may be seen equivalent to bending of light in a medium of varying refractive index.
Since no quantum condition has been imposed in arriving at the geodesic equation, it is expected to be valid in the classical limit, where the deBroglie wavelength of the particle is much smaller than the space-time curvature.
This section makes use of the geodesic equation to provide a physical validation of the theory, albeit, at a classical level.

VI.2
Interpretation at a classical level

We now look at the possible origins of the geodesic curve, given by Eq.
63
, from a kinetic theory point of view, for a massive particle.
We study the propagation of a massive test particle in a plasma.
Let
\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle
be the expected r.m.s. velocity of a constituent particle of the medium at a spatial location,
(x,z)
.
Classically,

\frac{1}{2}m_{p}\langle v^{2}\rangle=\frac{n_{p}}{2}k\Theta,

where
n_{p}
and
m_{p}
are the degrees of freedom and the effective mass of the constituent plasma particle, and
\Theta
is the temperature.
This means,

\sqrt{\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle}=\sqrt{\frac{n_{p}k}{m_{p}}}\sqrt{\Theta}=k_{0}\sqrt{\Theta}.

Rewriting
\Theta
in terms of the metric
h_{00}(x,z)
,

\sqrt{\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle}=\frac{k^{\prime}}{h_{00}(x,z)^{1/4}},

(64)

with
k^{\prime}=\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{\beta_{0}}}
.
The gradient in the velocity along the
x
-direction, would then become,

\partial_{x}\sqrt{\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle}=\frac{k^{\prime\prime}}{h_{00}(x,z)^{5/4}}h_{00x},

(65)

where,
h_{00x}=\frac{\partial h_{00}(x,z)}{\partial x}
.
The variation in r.m.s. velocity of the plasma constituents will bring about a change in momentum of the test particle in the plasma.
The momentum change of the test particle over a unit distance
\Delta x=\Delta l
, is given by:

\Delta p_{x}\propto\partial_{x}\sqrt{\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle}\Delta l.

(66)

Acceleration of the test particle would be proportional to the rate of change of momentum. This gives,
a_{x}\propto\frac{\Delta p_{x}}{\Delta t}
. Under conditions of local thermodynamic equilibrium and stationarity,
\partial_{x}\sqrt{\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle}
is static in time.
Furthermore, the time variable,
t
, is analytically continued and replaced by the affine parameter
\lambda
(i.e.
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\propto i\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}
). This finally gives,

a_{x}\propto i\partial_{x}\sqrt{\langle v^{2}(x,z)\rangle}\frac{\Delta l}{\Delta\lambda}=\frac{ik^{\prime\prime}h_{00x}}{h_{00}(x,z)^{5/4}}\frac{\Delta l}{\Delta\lambda}.

(67)

In the
z
-direction, we again calculate the momentum change,
\Delta p_{z}
, over the same (in magnitude) unit distance,
\Delta z=\Delta l
. This gives,

a_{z}\propto\frac{ik^{\prime\prime}h_{00z}}{h_{00}(x,z)^{5/4}}\frac{\Delta l}{\Delta\lambda}.

(68)

Rewriting,
a_{x}=\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\lambda^{2}}
and
a_{z}=\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial\lambda^{2}}
, and taking the ratio of Eqs.
67
and
68
, we get,

\frac{1}{h_{00x}}\frac{\partial^{2}x}{\partial\lambda^{2}}=\frac{1}{h_{00z}}\frac{\partial^{2}z}{\partial\lambda^{2}},

(69)

which is nothing but the geodesic equation, Eq.
63
.

What has been achieved here, is that the curved path taken by the test particle, determined by the kinetic considerations after an analytic continuation (
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rightarrow i\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}
), is seen to be the geodesic in the curved Euclidean space.
This result plays an important role, and is a step towards establishing the physical validity of the theory.

A note: Equation
61
begins with the signature, (+,-,-,-), and after the analytical continuation,
t\rightarrow i\beta
, it becomes (-,-,-,-).
One could begin with (-,+,+,+), leading to a more pleasing, (+,+,+,+), after the analytic continuation. The geodesic (Eq.
VI.1
) remains the same in either case.
The former signature (+,-,-,-) has been chosen to be consistent with the convention used in particle physics, in order to facilitate solving the Dirac equation in Sec.
VII
.

VII
Dirac Spinor Eigenstate Transfiguration

Having justified the concept of a curved Euclidean space in a generic field theoretic and a classical setup, in the earlier sections, we now attempt to understand the important properties of the solutions of the relativistic Dirac equation.
This section models an external Dirac spinor, with energy,
E
, and mass,
m_{g}
, propagating through a thermal medium with spatial thermal gradients.
The external Dirac spinor is modeled via the Dirac equation.
The above setup helps to elucidate the basic quirk(s) of the theory, and provides a mechanism for the physical validation of the mathematical framework of the curved Euclidean space.
These properties should carry over to the field theoretic aspects as well.
To elaborate, let the solutions of the Dirac equation,
\psi_{n}(\beta,z)
, constitute the complete set of eigenfunctions (or eigenstates). Consequently,
\psi_{n}(\beta,z)
forms a basis set, and any solution can then be written as
\psi(\beta,z)=\sum_{n}a_{n}\psi_{n}(\beta,z)
, with
a_{n}
being arbitrary constants. For a field theoretic approach, we promote
a_{n}
to an operator, satisfying the standard anti-commutation relations. Since
\psi_{n}(\beta,z)
forms the complete solution set, we can express the field theoretic solution,
\Psi(\beta,z)
, as
\Psi(\beta,z)=\sum_{n}a_{n}\psi_{n}(\beta,z)
, with
a^{\dagger}_{n}
and
a_{n}
, now becoming the creation and annihilation operators.
As a side note, the antiparticle wave-functions have been omitted for both
\psi(\beta,x)
and
\Psi(\beta,x)
, which is but a matter of detail.
Any fundamental property or quirks, that
\psi_{n}(z)
exhibits, would be carried over to the field theoretic solutions as well. Therefore, investigating the properties of the basis function,
\psi_{n}(z)
, in a given curved space, is fundamental to understanding the physics of the quantum theory in that curved space.

Let us then state the small gradient approximation as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\frac{C_{n}}{\sqrt{V(z)}})\sim 0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left\{-{(-k_{1}k_{2}+sk_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}-m_{g}^{2})-h^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(z)}\right\}\sim 0.

(81)

\displaystyle q(z)^{2}=-{(-k_{1}k_{2}+sk_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}-m_{g}^{2})-h^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(z)}

(82)

\displaystyle\approx\left\{-(E^{2}-m_{g}^{2})+\frac{C_{n}^{2}}{V(z)}-\frac{i2EC_{n}}{\sqrt{V(z)}}\right\}.

(83)

The imaginary part of q(z), i.e. Imag(q(z)) gives the 3-momentum of the Dirac fermion, while the

Imag(q(z))

This could then be interpreted as the ”instantaneous” 3-momentum. Alternatively,

The change in the 3-momentum leads to a time lag as the fermion passes through the thermal bath, which can then be measured.
We can then write the solution as:

where

a scale invariant factor,

b)

It can also be inferred that, if the temperature gradient and the initial momentum of the fermion are in perpendicular directions, then the change in the momentum along the direction of the temperature gradient would lead to curving of the fermion path.

The curving of the fermion path is reminiscent of the geodesic equations in Sec.

VIII
Interpretation of the Matsubara Eigenstates, and other considerations

VIII.1
Interpretation of the Matsubara Eigenstates and energy E

This section outlines a physical interpretation of the subtle aspects of the formalism developed so far.
Section
V
looks at the bulk thermodynamic properties, while Sec.
VII
analyzes the properties of a single eigenstate of an external particle, i.e., a particle which is not part of the thermal medium. Both the scenarios are now compared.

For the purpose of the physical interpretation of aspects like the Matsubara frequency, it is not a necessity to consider a curved space, and hence we revert to the uniform temperature case, i.e.,
h_{00}=1
.
In the original Matsubara formalism, the imaginary time theory does not introduce the particle energy,
E
, in the Lagrangian.
The energy,
E
, is the energy of the particle when it emanates from the source. Clearly,
E
is an intrinsic property of the particle, and not the result of interaction with the thermal medium. It does not obey Fermi-Dirac or Bose-Einstein distribution.
The introduction of
E
has been enabled as a result of modeling the 8-D space,
\beta^{\mu}\times x^{\nu}
, since time axis is treated as a separate dimension from temperature.
The viewpoint of 8-D space
\beta^{\mu}\times x^{\nu}
, is a subtle difference having profound implications.
In Eq.
VII.3
, if we remove the contribution of
E
, by substituting
E=0
, we get,

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\prime\prime}+(-\frac{C_{n}^{2}}{{V_{0}}}-m_{g}^{2})u_{1}=0,

\displaystyle u_{2}^{\prime\prime}+(-\frac{C_{n}^{2}}{{V_{0}}}-m_{g}^{2})u_{2}=0.

The solution is straightforward, namely,
u_{i}=e^{\pm\sqrt{\frac{C_{n}^{2}}{{V_{0}}}+m_{g}^{2}}z}
, where
i=1,2
. This is a pure decay (enhancement) function, and the decay rate (enhancement rate) increases with mass.
There is no real valued momentum observable (wave-function of the form
\exp(ipz)
), and the lack of the momentum observable is independent of the particle energy. This seems unrealistic.
It is evident that at sufficiently high energy, a particle will have some momentum inside a plasma, and not just decay.

Let us take the example of bottom quark in a QGP. Taking
m_{g}\approx 4.5GeV
, and radius of QGP
\approx 5fm\approx 25GeV^{-1}
, we get
e^{-m_{g}z}\approx 2.9\times 10^{-50}
. If this were correct, there would not be any bottom quark production at all in a QGP.
However, there is sufficient production of these particles in QGP
hq1
;
hq2
;
hq3
;
hq4
.
While, the interaction Lagrangian, in the imaginary time formalism, leads to reasonable results for the bulk properties like entropy etc., the Euler-Lagrange equations of motion arising from the free field Lagrangian, do not lead to meaningful results.
The drawback can be root caused to the space
(i\beta,x,y,z)
. This space does not lend itself to a Lorentz invariant measure.
However, the 2-norm in the
\beta^{\mu}\times x^{\nu}
space is Lorentz invariant, as shown in Eq.
1
. In a 8-D space, the consequence of the presence of the time axis is the introduction of particle energy,
E
, via the operator
\frac{i\partial}{\partial t}
.
The presence of
E
leads to the availability of a real valued momentum observable, as detailed in Sec.
VII

To summarize, the introduction of
E
leads to the following, highly desirable properties:

1.

Enables modeling of the particle energy when it emanates from the source. This particle energy is distinct from the kinetics of the thermal medium.

2.

Introduction of a momentum observable in the Euler Lagrange equations of motion.

3.

In the limit of zero temperature (
\beta\rightarrow\infty
), one gets back the zero temperature Dirac equation.

The Matsubara frequencies are viewed upon as the poles of the Bose-Einstein or Fermi-Dirac distribution. Consequently, the Matsubara frequency can be viewed as imaginary energy,
iE^{c}
(=
iC_{n}/\sqrt{(}V)
), and is unrelated to the particle energy
E
. Thereupon, the energy,
E^{c}
, would be the energy of interaction of the particle with the thermal medium.
The periodicity of the Euclidean space, effects the interaction energy,
E^{c}
, to be quantized. The interaction, then, leads to the decay of the wave-function, as depicted in Eq.
VII.3
. This seems to be a reasonable outcome, as interaction may lead to a part of the particle wave-function merging with the thermal medium.
The various Matsubara frequency eigenstates, would then imply, that the particle is in a superposition of Matsubara frequency eigenstates, wherein, each eigenstate determines the degree (or energy) of interaction with the thermal medium.
Ergo, the spatial variation of temperature, would naturally lead to varying degrees of interaction of the particle with the thermal medium, giving way to the spatial variation of the Matsubara frequency.

The standard imaginary time formalism can be seen to be a limiting case when
E\rightarrow 0
. In Sec.
VII
, an external particle of energy,
E
, has been analyzed. This particle had not equilibrated and was distinct from the thermal system.
If, however, the particle ”dissolves” or ”equilibrates” and becomes a part of the thermal system, then, the only energy it would posses, would be the thermal interaction energy,
E^{c}
. It would no longer posses its own energy,
E
, which is distinct from the thermal system.
The momentum of a single particle becomes meaningless, as only the ensemble average carries physical significance. The single particle Dirac equation seems to become redundant.
Thus, the classical imaginary time formalism can be used to model the bulk properties of an ensemble of particles which have been equilibrated and have become part of the thermal system.
The concept of curved Euclidean space, obtained by recasting the temperature variation as a variation in the metric, is applicable in either case.
As a case in point, Sec.
V
explores the equivalence in the context of bulk thermodynamic properties in a conventional 4-D Euclidean space, while, Sec.
VII
explores the external single particle behavior and includes
E
in the modeling.

The application of the Dirac’s theory, rather than the Field theory, leads to approximations, because the contributions due to particle creation and annihilation are ignored. However, this approximation should be much smaller for QED, relative to QCD, due to the much smaller QED coupling constant.
The approximation may also work well for heavy quarks, such as the bottom or top quark, where the corrections due to the creation and annihilation of particles may be small.

VIII.2
The Grand Canonical Ensemble

The equivalence between the curved Euclidean space and spatial thermal variations can be extended to the grand canonical ensemble. This allows one to explore the effect of the chemical potential on the Matsubara frequency.
In the case of uniform temperature,
\beta
, the partition function is,
Z=Tr\exp[\beta(H-\mu Q)]
, where,
\mu
is the chemical potential,
H
is the Hamiltonian and
Q
is the conserved charge with
Q=\int j^{0}d^{3}x

In the case of a thermal system with spatial thermal variations, the partition function with a time independent chemical potential,
\mu

\displaystyle Z=Tr\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{\beta({\bf{x}})}\left(\mathcal{H}-\mu j^{0}\right)d\tau d^{3}x\right)

\displaystyle=Tr\exp\left(-\int\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}s({\bf{x}})\left(\mathcal{H}-\mu j^{0}\right)d\tau d^{3}x\right),

(96)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hamiltonian density.
This suggests, that, like the case of the canonical ensemble, the grand canonical ensemble may also be recast in the framework of the curved Euclidean space, with the metric,
diag[s({\bf{x}})^{2},1,1,1]
.
Let us consider the following Lagrangian density for a fermion in the curved Euclidean space given by the metric
diag[s({\bf{x}})^{2},1,1,1]

\mathcal{L}=\bar{\psi}\left(ie_{a}^{0}\gamma^{a}D_{i\tau}+i\bf{\gamma.\nabla}-m\right)\psi,

where,

is the vierbein, and the transformation,
t\rightarrow i\tau
is incorporated in the spinor covariant derivative,
D_{i\tau}

The conserved charge density for the above Lagrangian density is

j^{0}=\bar{\psi}(e_{a}^{0}\gamma^{a})\psi,

(98)

With all this, the partition function can be written as:

\displaystyle Z=\int D\psi^{\dagger}D\psi\exp\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\int d^{3}x\sqrt{g_{e}}

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}\left(ie^{0}_{a}\gamma^{a}D_{i\tau}+i\bf{\gamma.\nabla}-m+\mu e^{0}_{a}\gamma^{a}\right)\psi\Bigg],

(99)

where,
\sqrt{g_{e}}=s({\bf{x}})
as before.
If we decompose,
\psi({\bf{x}},\tau)\rightarrow\sum_{n}\exp(-iC_{n}\tau)\psi_{n}({\bf{x}})
,
the term,
(ie^{0}_{a}\gamma^{a}D_{i\tau}+\mu e^{0}_{a}\gamma^{a})\psi({\bf{x}},\tau)\rightarrow\left[e^{0}_{a}\gamma^{a}(-iC_{n}+\mu)\psi_{n}({\bf{x}})+{\it spin~connection~terms}\right]
. The shift in the Matsubara frequency, along the imaginary axis, due to the chemical potential, is akin to the uniform temperature case.

In Sec.
VII
, the eigenstate transfiguration of a Dirac particle is modeled. In case the chemical potential of the thermal medium, were to affect the Dirac spinor, the results of Sec.
VII
, would be an accurate representation, predominantly for those thermal systems, where chemical potential is close to zero.

It is planned to probe the grand canonical ensemble case, in more detail, in future research.

VIII.3
The 8-D space

Another significant outcome of the 8-D point of view, is the doubling of the degrees of freedom.
The expansion to 8-D space from 4-D space involves the introduction of additional variables, namely, the 4-D conjugate momenta,
\omega_{\mu}
in the
\beta^{\mu}
subspace. In thermo field dynamics
tft1
;
tft2

The product space,

|\tilde{m}\rangle\otimes|m\rangle
, in thermo field dynamics
tft1
;
tft2
, in the sense of the degrees of freedom being doubled.
It remains to be seen, as to the extent the correspondence can be developed further.

Introduction of the 8-D,
\beta^{\mu}\times x^{\nu}
space, leads to a field theory in a eight dimensional space.
There has been research in the renormalizability of
\phi^{4}
theory in higher dimensional space (for example, Ref.
phi4

However, Eqs.
1
and
2

\phi^{4}
theory in dimensions lesser than 8-D.
These issues are hoped to be addressed in future research.

IX
Conclusion

Starting with the help of a Polyakov loop correlator, we have propounded a mathematical equivalence between the temperature gradient and the curvature of the Euclidean space. The variation in the temperature is recast as a variation in the metric.
Bulk thermodynamic parameters, like the energy expectation value, Helmholtz free energy and entropy is calculated for a neutral scalar field in a thermal bath with spatial thermal gradients, using the concept of a curved Euclidean space.
Subsequently, the Dirac equation for an external massive fermion is solved in the curved Euclidean space.
This analyzes the behavior of an individual eigenstate, instead of examining only bulk thermodynamic properties.
The recovery of the fermion wave-function in the regions of negative thermal gradient is unexpected, and thus, can be a key indicator of the physical validity of the curved Euclidean space model.
In case the recovery does not happen, it may likely require further research into discovering a valid model for systems with thermal gradients.
The properties of the Dirac function, highlighted in this paper, is expected to carry over to a field theoretic approach as well, as the basis functions can remain the same.

If the Euclidean space model, indeed models the quantum behavior in a thermal medium, then, it may have other implications.
The similarity of the particle behavior in the Euclidean and Lorentzian spaces, may lead to better understanding of particle behavior in a curved Lorentzian space-time.
As a case in point, for a scalar field,
\phi
, the curved Euclidean space experiments may help establish, whether,
R\phi^{2}
is a required term in the Lagrangian.

It would be desirable to generalize the proposed formalism to a covariant framework. A fully covariant field theoretic approach can help analyze the vacuum structure.
