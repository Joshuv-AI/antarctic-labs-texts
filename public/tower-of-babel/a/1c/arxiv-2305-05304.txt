The structure and density of k k -product-free sets in the free semigroup
Freddie Illingworth, Lukas Michel, Alex Scott

Abstract
The free semigroup ℱ \mathcal{F} over a finite alphabet 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is the set of all finite words with letters from 𝒜 \mathcal{A} equipped with the operation of concatenation. A subset S S of ℱ \mathcal{F} is k k -product-free if no element of S S can be obtained by concatenating k k words from S S , and strongly k k -product-free if no element of S S is a (non-trivial) concatenation of at most k k words from S S . We prove that a k k -product-free subset of ℱ \mathcal{F} has upper Banach density at most 1 / ρ ⁡ ( k ) 1/\rho(k) , where ρ ⁡ ( k ) = min ⁡ { ℓ : ℓ ∤ k − 1 } \rho(k)=\min\{\ell\colon\ell\nmid k-1\} . We also determine the structure of the extremal k k -product-free subsets for all k ∉ { 3 , 5 , 7 , 13 } k\notin\{3,5,7,13\} ; a special case of this proves a conjecture of Leader, Letzter, Narayanan, and Walters. We further determine the structure of all strongly k k -product-free sets with maximum density. Finally, we prove that k k -product-free subsets of the free group have upper Banach density at most 1 / ρ ⁡ ( k ) 1/\rho(k) , which confirms a conjecture of Ortega, Rué, and Serra.

2
Density

Throughout this paper
\mathcal{F}
will be the free semigroup on a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. To motivate and provide intuition for the notation we view things from the perspective of a randomly generated word. Let
\mathbf{W}=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dotsb
be a random infinite word where each
\alpha_{i}
is an independent uniformly random letter in
\mathcal{A}
. Taking
\mathbf{W}_{n}=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dotsb\alpha_{n}
, we may view
(\mathbf{W}_{n})
as a random walk on the infinite
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
-ary tree. We say
\mathbf{W}

hits
a set
B\subset\mathcal{F}
if the random walks hits
B
(equivalently if
\mathbf{W}
has a prefix in
B
) and
\mathbf{W}

avoids

B
otherwise. We equip
\mathcal{F}
with a measure
\mu
satisfying, for every word
w\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mu(w)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{W}\text{ hits }w)=\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert w\rvert}.

Note that, for
B\subset\mathcal{F}
,
\mu(B)=\sum_{w\in B}\mu(w)
is the expected number of times that
\mathbf{W}
hits
B
. This has a useful corollary. A set
C\subset\mathcal{F}
is
prefix-free
if there are not distinct words
a,b\in C
where
a
is a prefix of
b
.
\mathbf{W}
can hit a prefix-free set at most once.

Observation 2.1
.

If
C\subset\mathcal{F}
is prefix-free, then
\mu(C)\leqslant 1
.

For a positive integer
n
and a set
B\subset\mathcal{F}
the
length
n
layer of
B
is

B(n)\coloneqq\{w\in B\colon\lvert w\rvert=n\},

while, for an interval
I\subset\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

B(I)\coloneqq\{w\in B\colon\lvert w\rvert\in I\}.

Note that the measure
\mu
is defined so that
\mu(\mathcal{F}(n))=1
. The density of
B
on layer
n
is
\lvert B(n)\rvert/\lvert\mathcal{F}(n)\rvert=\mu(B(n))
, which is the probability that
\mathbf{W}_{n}
is in
B
. The
density of
B
on interval
I
is

d^{I}(B)\coloneqq\frac{\mu(B(I))}{\mu(\mathcal{F}(I))}=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)).

With these definitions in place, we may give standard notions of density. The
upper asymptotic density
of
B
is

\bar{d}(B)\coloneqq\limsup_{m\to\infty}d^{\{1,2,\dotsc,m\}}(B)=\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\mu(B(n))/m.

The
upper Banach density
of
B
is

d^{\ast}(B)\coloneqq\limsup_{I\to\infty}d^{I}(B)=\limsup_{I\to\infty}\ \lvert I\rvert^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)),

where
I
is an interval and the notation
I\to\infty
denotes that both
\lvert I\rvert
and
\min I
tend to infinity
2
2

2

The condition
\min I\to\infty
is often omitted from the definition. However, some simple analysis shows that, whether or not this condition is included, the resulting density is the same.
. Now
d^{\ast}(B)\geqslant\bar{d}(B)
for any set
B
and so all of our results also hold for asymptotic density.

It should be noted that limit superiors are only subadditive (and not additive). In particular, for disjoint sets
A,B\subset\mathcal{F}
we have
d^{\ast}(A\cup B)\leqslant d^{\ast}(A)+d^{\ast}(B)
and equality may not hold. As an example, the sets

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\mathcal{F}(\{(2n-1)!+1,(2n-1)!+2,\dotsc,(2n)!\}),

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}\mathcal{F}(\{(2n)!+1,(2n)!+2,\dotsc,(2n+1)!\})

are disjoint and both have density 1.

Despite this, in the group of non-negative integers
\mathcal{F}=\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
d^{\ast}(B)
satisfies some useful properties. For example, it holds that
\lvert d^{I}(x+B)-d^{I}(B)\rvert\leqslant x/\lvert I\rvert
. This implies that
d^{\ast}(x+B)=d^{\ast}(B)
. Even more importantly, if
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
are such that
x_{1}+B,\dots,x_{n}+B
are disjoint, then
d^{I}(x_{1}+B)+\dots+d^{I}(x_{n}+B)\leqslant 1
, implying that

n\cdot d^{I}(B)\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(d^{I}(x_{i}+B)+\frac{x_{i}}{\lvert I\rvert}\right)\leqslant 1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{\lvert I\rvert}

and so
n\cdot d^{\ast}(B)\leqslant 1
. Not only can this provide upper bounds on the density of
B
, but if we knew that
d^{\ast}(B)>1/n
, we could conclude that the sets
x_{1}+B,\dots,x_{n}+B
cannot all be disjoint and thereby deduce some structural information about
B
. Such arguments were used by Łuczak and Schoen
[
Łuc95
,
ŁS97
]
for their results about sum-free subsets of the non-negative integers.

If
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert>1
, these arguments no longer work. For example, if
w\in\mathcal{F}
, it is easy to see that
d^{\ast}(wB)=\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert w\rvert}\cdot d^{\ast}(B)
where
wB\coloneqq\{wb\colon b\in B\}
. Also, the fact that
w_{1}B,\dots,w_{n}B
are disjoint gives no general upper bound on the density of
B
. Even if we consider nested sets
B,wB,\dots,w^{n}B
, taking
B\coloneqq\mathcal{F}\setminus(w\mathcal{F})
provides an example where these sets are pairwise disjoint, but
d^{\ast}(B)=1-\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert w\rvert}
which can be arbitrarily close to
1
.

We address these issues in the next section. By modifying the density that we consider, we can ensure that the density is additive. Importantly, the density of the set
S\subset\mathcal{F}
whose upper Banach density we want to bound will not change. Moreover, in certain situations, we prove that
n
disjoint nested copies of
B
imply that the density of
B
is at most
1/n
. This will be crucial for proving our structural results.

3
Diagonalisation and relative density

Throughout the paper
S\subset\mathcal{F}
will be a fixed set whose upper Banach density we wish to bound (for example,
S
might be
k
-product-free). There is a sequence of intervals
(I_{j})
such that
I_{j}\to\infty
and

d^{I_{j}}(S)\to d^{\ast}(S),\quad\text{as }j\to\infty.

Let
B\subset\mathcal{F}
be another set. The sequence
(d^{I_{j}}(B))
is bounded (all terms are in
[0,1]
) and so, by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, has a convergent subsequence. In particular, by passing to a subsequence of
(I_{j})
, we may assume that
d^{I_{j}}(S)\to d^{\ast}(S)
and
(d^{I_{j}}(B))
converges to some limit that we will call
d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
. Given a countable collection of subsets of
\mathcal{F}
, we may, by a diagonalisation argument, assume there is a subsequence
(I_{j})
such that
d^{I_{j}}(B)\to d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
for every
B
in the collection where
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d^{\ast}(S)
. Throughout this paper we will only ever consider countably many sequences and so this convergence occurs for all sets we consider. These limits, unlike the corresponding upper Banach densities, are additive. Indeed, if sets
A
and
B
are disjoint, then
d^{I_{j}}(A\cup B)=d^{I_{j}}(A)+d^{I_{j}}(B)
and so
d^{I_{\infty}}(A\cup B)=d^{I_{\infty}}(A)+d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
. It should be noted that while
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d^{\ast}(S)
, we only have
d^{I_{\infty}}(B)\leqslant d^{\ast}(B)
for the other sets that we consider.

For our structural proofs we will need not only to bound the density of a product-free set
S
but also to bound the density of
S
on subtrees. We now begin to define this.

The product
AB
of two sets
A,B\subset\mathcal{F}
is

AB\coloneqq\{ab\colon a\in A,b\in B\}

and the set
B^{k}
is the product of
k
copies of
B
. Note that
B
is
k
-product-free exactly if
B\cap B^{k}=\varnothing
. A particular important example of a product is
w\mathcal{F}
for a word
w\in\mathcal{F}
: this is exactly the subtree of
\mathcal{F}
consisting of all words starting with
w
. Similarly
B\mathcal{F}
is exactly the set of words that have a prefix in
B
.

For a finite set
B\subset\mathcal{F}
we write
\min B
and
\max B
for the length of the shortest and longest words in
B
, respectively. Note that if
B
is finite, then for
n\geqslant\max B
the random infinite word
\mathbf{W}
hits
(B\mathcal{F})(n)
if and only if it hits
B
.

Observation 3.1
.

If
n\geqslant\lvert w\rvert
, then
\mu((w\mathcal{F})(n))=\mu(w)
. If
C\subset\mathcal{F}
is prefix-free and finite, then
\mu((C\mathcal{F})(n))=\mu(C)
for
n\geqslant\max C
.

Definition 3.2

(relative density)
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{F}
and
B\subset\mathcal{F}
. For
n\geqslant\lvert w\rvert
, the relative density of
B
in
w\mathcal{F}
on layer
n
is

\frac{\lvert B(n)\cap w\mathcal{F}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{F}(n)\cap w\mathcal{F}\rvert}=\frac{\mu(B(n)\cap w\mathcal{F})}{\mu(\mathcal{F}(n)\cap w\mathcal{F})}=\frac{\mu(B(n)\cap w\mathcal{F})}{\mu(w)}

which is the probability that
\mathbf{W}_{n}
is in
B
conditioned on the event that
\mathbf{W}
hits
w
. If
n<\lvert w\rvert
, then we will take the relative density to be
0
by convention.

Furthermore, if
I
is an interval with
\min I\geqslant\lvert w\rvert
, then the
relative density of
B
in
w\mathcal{F}
on interval
I
is

d_{w\mathcal{F}}^{I}(B)\coloneqq\frac{\mu(B(I)\cap w\mathcal{F})}{\mu(\mathcal{F}(I)\cap w\mathcal{F})}=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(w)^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)\cap w\mathcal{F})=\mu(w)^{-1}\cdot d^{I}(B\cap w\mathcal{F}).

If
\min I<\lvert w\rvert
, then we will take the relative density to be
0
by convention.

Note that if
w
is the empty word then this relative density is just
d^{I}(B)
.

Consider the sequence of intervals
(I_{j})
given above where
d^{I_{j}}(B)\to d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
for every set
B
in a countable collection. For each word
w\in\mathcal{F}
and each set in the collection, the sequence
(d_{w\mathcal{F}}^{I_{j}}(B))
is bounded (all terms are in
[0,1]
) and so, by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, has a convergent subsequence. Since
\mathcal{F}
is countable (it consists of only finite words) we may, via a diagonalisation argument, pass to a subsequence
(I_{j})
such that, for every
w\in\mathcal{F}
and every
B
in the countable collection,
(d_{w\mathcal{F}}^{I_{j}}(B))
converges to some limit
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)
. In conclusion, we may assume throughout the paper that for any set
B
we encounter and for all
w\in\mathcal{F}
we have

d_{w\mathcal{F}}^{I_{j}}(B)\to d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B),

where
d^{I_{\infty}}(B)\leqslant d^{\ast}(B)
and
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d^{\ast}(S)
for one fixed set
S
. As before, these limits are additive. They satisfy the useful property that we may strip away prefixes.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
w,v\in\mathcal{F}
, then
d^{I_{\infty}}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(wB)=d^{I_{\infty}}_{v\mathcal{F}}(B)
.

Proof.

Let
I
be any interval with
\min I>\lvert wv\rvert
. Now

d^{I}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(wB)=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(wv)^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu((wB)(n)\cap wv\mathcal{F}).

Removing the leading
w
from each word in
(wB)(n)\cap wv\mathcal{F}
shows that
\mu((wB)(n)\cap wv\mathcal{F})=\mu(w)\cdot\mu(B(n-\lvert w\rvert)\cap v\mathcal{F})
. Also
\mu(wv)=\mu(w)\mu(v)
and so

d^{I}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(wB)=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(v)^{-1}\sum_{n\in I-\lvert w\rvert}\mu(B(n)\cap v\mathcal{F}),

where
I-\lvert w\rvert
is the interval obtained by subtracting
\lvert w\rvert
from each element of
I
. Thus

\lvert d^{I}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(wB)-d^{I}_{v\mathcal{F}}(B)\rvert=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(v)^{-1}\cdot\bigg\lvert\sum_{n\in I-\lvert w\rvert}\mu(B(n)\cap v\mathcal{F})-\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)\cap v\mathcal{F})\bigg\rvert

But, for each integer
n
,
\mu(B(n)\cap v\mathcal{F})\in[0,1]
and so

\lvert d^{I}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(wB)-d^{I}_{v\mathcal{F}}(B)\rvert\leqslant\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(v)^{-1}\cdot\lvert w\rvert

Setting
I=I_{j}
and taking
j
to infinity gives the required result.
∎

We are now ready to make an important definition that captures the densest that a set
B
can be down a subtree.

Definition 3.4

(sup density)
.

For a set
B
in the countable collection, the
sup density of
B
is

d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)\coloneqq\sup_{w\in\mathcal{F}}d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B).

Of course, the sup density satisfies
d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
(note that the empty word is in
\mathcal{F}
) and so
d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)\geqslant d^{\ast}(S)
.

We will prove the following strengthening of
Theorems

1.3
and
1.6
in
Section

7
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer,
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set, and
\mathcal{F}
be the free semigroup with alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.

(a)

If
S\subset\mathcal{F}
is strongly
k
-product-free, then
d^{\ast}(S)\leqslant 1/k
. Moreover, if
d^{\ast}(S)=1/k
, then
d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/k
.

(b)

If
S\subset\mathcal{F}
is
k
-product-free, then
d^{\ast}(S)\leqslant 1/\rho(k)
. Moreover, if
d^{\ast}(S)=1/\rho(k)
, then
d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/\rho(k)
.

This strengthening is needed for our structural results,
Theorems

1.4
and
1.7
. For example, if
S\subset\mathcal{F}
is strongly
k
-product-free with
d^{\ast}(S)=1/k
, then by
(a)
,
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d^{\ast}(S)=d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)
. This suggests that
S
is uniformly distributed down subtrees which is made precise by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
d^{I_{\infty}}(B)=d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)
, then
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)=d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
for every word
w\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be a non-negative integer and let
I
be an interval with
\min I>\ell
. Every word of length greater than
\ell
is in exactly one
w\mathcal{F}
(where
w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)
). Hence,

d^{I}(B)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)}d^{I}(B\cap w\mathcal{F})=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)}\mu(w)\cdot d^{I}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B).

Setting
I=I_{j}
and taking
j
to infinity gives

d^{I_{\infty}}(B)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)}\mu(w)\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B).

Now
\sum_{w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)}\mu(w)=\mu(\mathcal{F}(\ell))=1
and every
w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)
satisfies
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)\leqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)=d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
. Hence we must have
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)=d^{I_{\infty}}(B)
for every
w\in\mathcal{F}(\ell)
. The integer
\ell
was arbitrary and so we have the required result.
∎

The next two lemmas are the key technical results for our structural proofs. We remark that for the non-negative integers (that is, when
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert=1
) they are much more obvious.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
S\subset\mathcal{F}
be such that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)>1/n
. Then, for any
w_{1},\dotsc,w_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
, the sets

w_{1}S,\quad w_{1}w_{2}S,\quad\dotsc,\quad w_{1}w_{2}\dotsb w_{n-1}S,\quad w_{1}w_{2}\dotsb w_{n}S

cannot be pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

Assume that these sets are pairwise disjoint. Then, for any word
w\in\mathcal{F}
,

d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(w_{1}S)+\dots+d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(w_{1}\dotsb w_{n}S)=d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}((w_{1}S)\cup\dots\cup(w_{1}\dotsb w_{n}S))\leqslant 1.

Choose
w=w_{1}\dotsb w_{n}
. Applying
Lemma

3.3
to each term gives

d^{I_{\infty}}_{w_{2}\dotsb w_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+\dotsb+d^{I_{\infty}}_{w_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(S)\leqslant 1.

By
Lemma

3.6
, each term is
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
which contradicts
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)>1/n
, as required.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
S\subset\mathcal{F}
be such that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)>2/(2n-1)
. Then, for any
w_{1},\dotsc,w_{n},v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
and
C\subset S
, either the sets

w_{1}S,\quad w_{1}w_{2}S,\quad\dotsc,\quad w_{1}\dotsb w_{n-1}S,\quad w_{1}\dotsb w_{n}C

or the sets

v_{1}S,\quad v_{1}v_{2}S,\quad\dotsc,\quad v_{1}\dotsb v_{n-1}S,\quad v_{1}\dotsb v_{n}(S\setminus C)

are not pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

Assume that both collections of sets are pairwise disjoint. Then, as in the proof of
Lemma

3.7
,

d^{I_{\infty}}_{w_{2}\dotsb w_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+\dots+d^{I_{\infty}}_{w_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(C)\leqslant 1

and

d^{I_{\infty}}_{v_{2}\dotsb v_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+\dots+d^{I_{\infty}}_{v_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(S\setminus C)\leqslant 1.

Note that
d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(S\setminus C)=d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(S)-d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(C)
. Applying this and adding the two inequalities, we get

\displaystyle d^{I_{\infty}}_{w_{2}\dotsb w_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+\dots+d^{I_{\infty}}_{w_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d^{I_{\infty}}_{v_{2}\dotsb v_{n}\mathcal{F}}(S)+\dots+d^{I_{\infty}}_{\mathcal{F}}(S)\leqslant 2.

However, by
Lemma

3.6
, each term is
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
which contradicts
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)>2/(2n-1)
, as required.
∎

4
Structure of strongly
k
-product-free sets

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.4
assuming
Theorem

3.5
. Therefore, let
S\subset\mathcal{F}
be strongly
k
-product-free satisfying
d^{\ast}(S)=1/k
. Note, by
Theorem

3.5
, that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/k=d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)
and so we may and will frequently apply
Lemmas

3.7
and
3.8
with
n=k+1
.

We want to show that we can label each letter of
\mathcal{A}
with a label in
\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
such that
S
is a subset of

T\coloneqq\{a\in\mathcal{F}\colon\text{the sum of the labels of letters in $a$ is }1\bmod{k}\}.

Assume that each
a\in\mathcal{F}
is labelled with this sum. To deduce the structure of
S
, we would like to identify these labels for all words
a\in\mathcal{F}
. Clearly, everything in
S
should be labelled
1
. For any other
a\in\mathcal{F}
, appending a word from
S
should increase the label by
1
. So, if
a
has label
\ell
and we append
i=-\ell\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
words from
S
to
a
, we should get the label
0
, and appending one more word from
S
should give the label
1
, which might itself be a word from
S
. On the other hand, for any other
j\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
, appending
j+1
words from
S
to
a
should give a label different from
1
and should therefore never yield a word from
S
.

Based on this intuition, for
i=0,1,\dotsc,k-1
define

T_{i}\coloneqq\{a\in\mathcal{F}\colon S\cap aS^{i+1}\neq\varnothing\}.

Then, everything in
T_{i}
should have the label
-i\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
. So, we expect that
S\subset T_{k-1}
and that
T_{i}T_{j}\subset T_{i+j}
. This is exactly what we will show and which allows us to deduce the structure of
T_{k-1}
, which will be the set
T
from above.

Remark 4.1
.

Throughout we will view the indices of the
T_{i}
as elements of
\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
and, in particular, all addition of indices is modulo
k
.

Note that our definition of
T_{i}
is slightly arbitrary. Whether we append or prepend words from
S
to some
a\in\mathcal{F}
, the change in the label of
a
should always be the same. So, we could also have defined
T_{i}
as the set
\{a\in\mathcal{F}\colon S\cap S^{i+1}a\neq\varnothing\}
. Fortunately, the following result tells us that these definitions are equivalent.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any positive integer
r
and any
a\in\mathcal{F}
,

S\cap S^{r}a\neq\varnothing\Leftrightarrow S\cap S^{r-1}aS\neq\varnothing\Leftrightarrow\dotsb\Leftrightarrow S\cap SaS^{r-1}\neq\varnothing\Leftrightarrow S\cap aS^{r}\neq\varnothing.

Proof.

We first prove the case
r=1
. Suppose that
S\cap Sa\neq\varnothing
. Then there is some
x
such that
x,xa\in S
. Consider the sets
S,xS,x^{2}S,\dotsc,x^{k-1}S,x^{k-1}aS=x^{k-2}(xa)S
. By
Lemma

3.7
, these cannot all be pairwise disjoint. Since
S
is strongly
k
-product-free and
x\in S
, the sets
S,xS,\dotsc,x^{k-1}S
are pairwise disjoint. Since
S
is strongly
k
-product-free and
xa\in S
, the sets
S,xS,\dotsc,x^{k-2}S,x^{k-2}(xa)S
are pairwise disjoint. Thus
x^{k-1}S
and
x^{k-1}aS
are not disjoint and so
S\cap aS\neq\varnothing
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}
be the
reverse map
that reverses each word of
\mathcal{F}
(that is, reads them from right to left). The function
f
is a measure-preserving involution. Let
\overline{S}=f(S)
. Now
\overline{S}
is a strongly
k
-product-free subset of
\mathcal{F}
with
d^{\ast}(\overline{S})=d^{\ast}(S)=1/k
. In particular, the previous paragraph shows that
\overline{S}\cap\overline{S}a\neq\varnothing\Rightarrow\overline{S}\cap a\overline{S}\neq\varnothing
. Now,
\overline{S}\cap\overline{S}a=f(S\cap aS)
and
\overline{S}\cap a\overline{S}=f(S\cap Sa)
and so
S\cap aS\neq\varnothing\Rightarrow S\cap Sa\neq\varnothing
concluding the case
r=1
.

For the general case it suffices to prove that for all non-negative integers
i,j
:
S\cap S^{i+1}aS^{j}\neq\varnothing\Leftrightarrow S\cap S^{i}aS^{j+1}\neq\varnothing
. Suppose that
S\cap S^{i+1}aS^{j}\neq\varnothing
. Then there is
x_{i}\in S^{i}
and
x_{j}\in S^{j}
such that
S\cap Sx_{i}ax_{j}\neq\varnothing
. Applying the
r=1
case to the word
x_{i}ax_{j}
shows that
S\cap x_{i}ax_{j}S\neq\varnothing
and so
S\cap S^{i}aS^{j+1}\neq\varnothing
. The other direction is analogous.
∎

If the sets
T_{1},\dots,T_{k-1}
are supposed to correctly identify the labels of all words
a\in\mathcal{F}
, then every
a
should be in exactly one of these sets, and
S
should satisfy
S\subset T_{k-1}
. This is proved by the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

The sets
T_{0}
,
T_{1}
, …,
T_{k-1}
partition
\mathcal{F}
and
S\subset T_{k-1}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathcal{F}
and
x\in S
. Consider the sets
S
,
aS
,
axS
,
ax^{2}S
, …,
ax^{k-1}S
. By
Lemma

3.7
, these cannot all be pairwise disjoint. Since
S
is strongly
k
-product-free and
x\in S
, the sets
aS
,
axS
, …,
ax^{k-1}S
are pairwise disjoint. Hence there is some
r\in\{0,1,\dotsc,k-1\}
such that
S\cap ax^{r}S\neq\varnothing
and so
S\cap aS^{r+1}\neq\varnothing
. That is,
\cup_{r=0}^{k-1}T_{r}=\mathcal{F}
.

We next show that the
T_{i}
are pairwise disjoint (and so partition
\mathcal{F}
). Suppose that
a\in T_{i}\cap T_{j}
where
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant k-1
. Since
a\in T_{i}
,
S\cap SaS^{i}\neq\varnothing
and so there is
x\in S
and
y\in S^{i}
such that
xay\in S
. Let

C\coloneqq\{s\in S\colon ays\in S\}\subset S.

Consider the
k+1
sets

S,\quad xS,\quad x^{2}S,\quad\dotsc,\quad x^{k-1}S,\quad x^{k-1}ay(S\setminus C).

As
S
is strongly
k
-product-free and
x\in S
, the first
k
of these sets are pairwise disjoint. Similarly, noting that
x^{k-1}ay=x^{k-2}(xay)
and
xay\in S
, we have that the last set is disjoint from each of the first
k-1
. Finally, the last two sets are disjoint by the definition of
C
. Hence, all
k+1
sets are pairwise disjoint.

Since
a\in T_{j}
there are
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{j+1}\in S
such that
z_{1}\dotsb z_{j+1}a\in S
. Consider the
k+1
sets

S,\quad z_{1}S,\quad z_{1}^{2}S,\quad\dotsc,\quad z_{1}^{k-j}S,\quad z_{1}^{k-j}z_{2}S,\quad\dotsc,\quad z_{1}^{k-j}z_{2}\dotsb z_{j}S,\quad z_{1}^{k-j}z_{2}\dotsb z_{j+1}ayC.

The first
k
of these sets are pairwise disjoint as
S
is strongly
k
-product-free. Similarly, noting that
z_{1}z_{2}\dotsb z_{j+1}a\in S
, the last set is disjoint from each of
S
,
z_{1}S
, …,
z_{1}^{k-j-1}S
. Now, by the definition of
C
,
ayC\subset S
. Using this and product-freeness shows that the last set is disjoint from each of
z_{1}^{k-j}S
,
z_{1}^{k-j}z_{2}S
, …,
z_{1}^{k-j}z_{2}\dotsb z_{j}S
. Hence, all
k+1
sets are pairwise disjoint. This contradicts
Lemma

3.8
and so the
T_{i}
do partition
\mathcal{F}
.

It remains to show that
S\subset T_{k-1}
. Since
S
is strongly
k
-product-free, for any
x\in S
, the set
S
is disjoint from each of
xS
,
xS^{2}
, …,
xS^{k-1}
and so
x\notin T_{0}\cup\dotsb\cup T_{k-2}
. Since the
T_{i}
partition
\mathcal{F}
, we must have
x\in T_{k-1}
, as required.
∎

Given these two results, we already know that
a\in T_{i}
should be labelled by
-i\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
. Next, we want to show that the label of a product
ab
should be the sum of the labels of
a
and
b
. We begin by proving that this is true whenever we append a word from
S
.

Proposition 4.4
.

The following hold for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
.

(a)

If
ax\in T_{j}
and
x\in S
, then
a\in T_{j+1}
.

(b)

T_{j+1}S\subset T_{j}
.

Proof.

We first prove
(a)
. Suppose that
0\leqslant j\leqslant k-2
. We have
S\cap axS^{j+1}\neq\varnothing
and
x\in S
, so
S\cap aS^{j+2}\neq\varnothing
and so
a\in T_{j+1}
.

Now suppose that
j=k-1
. Consider the sets
S
,
aS
,
axS
,
ax^{2}S
, …,
ax^{k-1}S
. By
Lemma

3.7
, these cannot all be pairwise disjoint. Since
S
is strongly
k
-product-free and
x\in S
, the sets
aS
,
axS
, …,
ax^{k-1}S
are pairwise disjoint. Also, as
ax\in T_{k-1}
(and so
ax
is not in
T_{0}\cup T_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup T_{k-2}
by
Proposition

4.3
),
S
is disjoint from each of
axS
,
ax^{2}S
, …,
ax^{k-1}S
. Thus
S
and
aS
are not disjoint and so
a\in T_{0}
, as required.

We now prove
(b)
. Let
a\in T_{j+1}
and
x\in S
. Suppose that
ax\in T_{i}
(such an
i
exists by
Proposition

4.3
). By
(a)
,
i+1=j+1\bmod{k}
and so
i=j\bmod{k}
, as required.
∎

It is now an easy consequence that the labels of all
T_{i}
are very well-behaved with respect to products.

Proposition 4.5
.

For all
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
,
T_{i}T_{j}\subset T_{i+j}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in T_{i}
and
b\in T_{j}
. As
b\in T_{j}
there are
x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc,x_{j+1}\in S
such that
bx_{1}x_{2}\dotsb x_{j+1}\in S
. By
Proposition

4.4
(b)
,

abx_{1}\dotsb x_{j+1}=a(bx_{1}\dotsb x_{j+1})\in T_{i-1}.

Applying
Proposition

4.4
(a)

j+1
times, once to remove each
x_{\ell}
, gives
ab\in T_{i-1+(j+1)}=T_{i+j}
, as required.
∎

Finally, this allows us to complete the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

For each letter
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, there is, by
Proposition

4.3
, a unique
i\in\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
such that
\alpha\in T_{i}
. Label
\alpha
with
i
. By
Proposition

4.5
, for each
i
,

T_{i}=\{w\in\mathcal{F}\colon\text{the sum of the labels of letters in $w$ is }i\bmod{k}\}.

In particular, by
Proposition

4.3
,

S\subset T_{k-1}=\{w\in\mathcal{F}\colon\text{the sum of the labels of letters in $w$ is }-1\bmod{k}\}.

Note that
T_{k-1}
is strongly
k
-product-free: if
w
is the concatenation of
\ell
words from
T_{k-1}
, then the sum of the labels of letters in
w
is
-\ell\bmod{k}
.

To obtain the result given in the statement of
Theorem

1.4
(i.e. with
1\bmod{k}
instead of
-1\bmod{k}
) simply multiply the label of each letter by
-1
.
∎

5
Structure of
k
-product-free sets

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.7
assuming
Theorem

3.5
. Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer with
k\notin\{3,5,7,13\}
, let
\rho=\rho(k)
, and let
S\subset\mathcal{F}
be
k
-product-free satisfying
d^{\ast}(S)=1/\rho
. Note, by
Theorem

3.5
, that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/\rho=d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)
and so we may and will frequently apply
Lemma

3.7
with
n=\rho+1
.

We will show that, in fact,
S
is strongly
\rho
-product-free and so the result follows from
Theorem

1.4
. To this end we make the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

For a set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the set
S_{A}

\subset\mathcal{F}
is

S_{A}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in A}S^{i},

where we will omit set parentheses so, for example,
S_{1}=S
and
S_{1,3}=S\cap S^{3}
.

Since
S
is
k
-product-free,
S_{1,k}=\varnothing
. It is enough for us to show that
S_{1,2}=S_{1,3}=\dotsb=S_{1,\rho}=\varnothing
as then
S
is strongly
\rho
-product-free. Note that the case
k=2
is immediate and so we assume that
k\geqslant 3
from now on.

We need a quick technical lemma about the size of
\rho
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 3
be an integer with
k\notin\{3,5,7,13\}
and let
\rho=\rho(k)
. Then

k-1\geqslant\max\{(\rho-t)t(t+1)\colon t\in\{1,2,\dotsc,\rho-1\}\}.

(1)

Proof.

By the arithmetic mean-geometric mean inequality, for any
t\in[0,\rho]
,

(\rho-t)t(t+1)=4(\rho-t)\tfrac{t}{2}\tfrac{t+1}{2}\leqslant 4\bigl(\tfrac{\rho+1/2}{3}\bigr)^{3}=4/27\cdot(\rho+1/2)^{3}.

On the other hand, Lev
[
Lev03
, Lem. 18]
proved that, for all positive integers
k\geqslant 2
,

\rho(k)\leqslant 2\log_{2}k+2.

Now, for all
k\geqslant 2400
,

k-1\geqslant 4/27\cdot(2\log_{2}k+5/2)^{3},

and so (
1
) holds. Now, if
\rho\geqslant 10
, then
k-1\geqslant 5\times 7\times 8\times 9=2520
and so (
1
) holds. We are left to check the remaining cases.

•

If
\rho=2
, then the right-hand side of (
1
) is 2. The smallest
k\geqslant 3
with
\rho=2
is 4.

•

If
\rho=3
, then the right-hand side of (
1
) is 6. The only
k\leqslant 6
with
\rho=3
are 3 and 5.

•

If
\rho=4
, then the right-hand side of (
1
) is 12. The only
k\leqslant 12
with
\rho=4
is 7.

•

If
\rho=5
, then the right-hand side of (
1
) is 24. The only
k\leqslant 24
with
\rho=5
is 13.

•

\rho
is always the power of a prime so there are no
k
with
\rho=6
.

•

If
\rho=7
, then the right-hand side of (
1

6
Steeplechases

In this section, we develop some results which will be used in the next section to bound the density of a (strongly)
k
-product-free set
S
and so prove
Theorem

3.5
. To motivate our approach, assume that
S
is strongly 3-product-free. To bound the density of
S
, we might hope that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d^{I_{\infty}}(S^{2})=d^{I_{\infty}}(S^{3})
. Because all of these sets are disjoint, this would imply that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2}\cup S^{3})=3\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
and so
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)\leqslant 1/3
, as required.

If
S
is evenly distributed, such an argument works. Indeed, note that for all
w\in S
we have
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(S^{2})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(wS)=d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
, so the relative density of
S^{2}
in
S\mathcal{F}
is at least
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
. If
S\mathcal{F}
covers all of
\mathcal{F}
, this implies that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S^{2})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
, and so
d^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant 2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
. To include
S^{3}
in the union, we can just repeat the argument. For
w\in S
we have
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(w(S\cup S^{2}))=d^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant 2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
, giving
d^{I_{\infty}}(S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant 2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
and thus
d^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant 3\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
, as required.

If
S
is not evenly distributed, we want to ignore the part of
\mathcal{F}
where
S
has a very low density. In the rest, the density of
S
should be at least
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
and
S
should be somewhat evenly distributed. Within this part, we then want to show that
S\cup S^{2}
has density
2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
and
S\cup S^{2}\cup S^{3}
has density
3\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
to again obtain the sought result.

While the density of
S\cup S^{2}
could be computed as before, this no longer works for
S\cup S^{2}\cup S^{3}
. We only know that
S\cup S^{2}
has a high density within a part of
\mathcal{F}
, for example
d^{I_{\infty}}_{v\mathcal{F}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant 2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
for some
v\in\mathcal{F}
. This does not suffice to get a lower bound on
d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(S^{2}\cup S^{3})
in the calculation above.

Instead, note that
d^{I_{\infty}}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{wv\mathcal{F}}(w(S\cup S^{2}))=d^{I_{\infty}}_{v\mathcal{F}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant 2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
which tells us that the relative density of
S^{2}\cup S^{3}
in
Sv\mathcal{F}
is at least
2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
. If we could now show that
Sv\mathcal{F}
covers essentially all of
S\mathcal{F}
, this would imply that
S^{2}\cup S^{3}
has density at least
2\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S)
in
S\mathcal{F}
which in turn would suffice to show that
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)\leqslant 1/3
.

The technical arguments in this section are mostly devoted to showing that this is true, at least up to some small error. The idea is that we partition
S
into prefix-free sets
(C_{k})
such that
C_{k+1}\subset C_{k}\mathcal{F}
. At some point, the measure of
C_{k}
will no longer drop. This means that
C_{k+1}\mathcal{F}
covers almost all subtrees of
C_{k}\mathcal{F}
.

Now,
C_{k}v\mathcal{F}
will cover a fraction of size
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert v\rvert}
of
C_{k}\mathcal{F}
. We also know that all uncovered subtrees are covered by
C_{k+1}\mathcal{F}
. So,
C_{k+1}v\mathcal{F}
will cover a fraction of size
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert v\rvert}
of the still uncovered subtrees of
C_{k}\mathcal{F}
, and the remaining subtrees are covered by
C_{k+2}\mathcal{F}
. By repeating this argument with
C_{k+2}v\mathcal{F},C_{k+3}v\mathcal{F},\dots
, we can eventually cover almost all of
C_{k}\mathcal{F}
with
\bigcup_{\ell\geqslant k}C_{\ell}v\mathcal{F}
. By deleting the first few layers of our partition of
S
, we therefore get that
S\mathcal{F}
is covered by
Sv\mathcal{F}
, which is what we need.

This motivate the following definition.

Definition 6.1

(steeplechase)
.

An infinite sequence
(C_{k})
of subsets of
\mathcal{F}
is a
steeplechase
if, for each positive integer
k
,

•

each
C_{k}
is prefix-free and finite,

•

every word in
C_{k+1}
has a proper prefix in
C_{k}
(in particular,
C_{k+1}\mathcal{F}\subset C_{k}\mathcal{F}
).

Steeplechase
(C_{k})
is
spread
if
\max C_{k}<\min C_{k+1}
for all
k
and is
\varepsilon
-tight
if, for all
m,n
,
\lvert\mu(C_{m})-\mu(C_{n})\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon
.

Every steeplechase contains a spread steeplechase. Indeed, note that
\min C_{k}\geqslant k
, since every word in
C_{k}
has a proper prefix in
C_{k-1}
. Let
\ell_{1}=\max C_{1}
. Then
\min C_{\ell_{1}+1}>\ell_{1}=\max C_{1}
. Let
\ell_{2}=\max C_{\ell_{1}+1}
. Then
\min C_{\ell_{2}+1}>\max C_{\ell_{1}+1}
. Iteratively doing this gives a spread steeplechase
C_{1},C_{\ell_{1}+1},C_{\ell_{2}+1},\dotsc
.

Since
C_{k}
is prefix-free,
\mu(C_{k})\in[0,1]
. Also, for each
k
,
C_{k+1}\mathcal{F}\subset C_{k}\mathcal{F}
and so the sequence
(\mu(C_{k}))
is non-increasing. In particular, this sequence tends to a limit. Hence the sequence is Cauchy: for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
K
such that, for all
\ell,k\geqslant K
,
\lvert\mu(C_{k})-\mu(C_{\ell})\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon
. Thus, ignoring the first few
C_{k}
gives an
\varepsilon
-tight steeplechase.

In particular, given any steeplechase
(C_{k})
we may, by passing to a subsequence, assume that
(C_{k})
is both spread and
\varepsilon
-tight.

The following lemma shows that, for any set
B\subset\mathcal{F}
, there is a steeplechase that captures almost all of
B
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
B\subset\mathcal{F}
. There is an
\varepsilon
-tight spread steeplechase
(C_{k})
such that

•

,

•

for all
k
and all large

(

)

Proof.

For
x\in B
, let the

For each positive integer

for all

Let

.

Let

.

3.

Remove
(D_{k}\setminus C_{k})\mathcal{F}
from

(including from all later

Let

be the set remaining at the end of this procedure. Note that in step 3 the headcounts of words either remain the same or those words are removed from

has a proper prefix in

. Also, by construction,

is a finite subset of

Fix

Finally, this implies that

Hence

is a steeplechase satisfying all three conditions. As noted above, we may, by passing to a subsequence, assume that

is spread and
\varepsilon
-tight. Passing to a subsequence does not affect the three conditions.
∎

We call the steeplechase

\varepsilon
-capturing steeplechase for

, then
\mathbf{W}
must fail each of these spelling checks and so the probability that
\mathbf{W}
hits

is at most

(1-\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert w\rvert})^{K+1}.

By taking
N
(and so
K
) sufficiently large in terms of
\lvert w\rvert
and
\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert
we may ensure this is at most
\varepsilon
, as required.
∎

Before proving our key technical result for our density proofs (
Lemma

6.5
) we will need to define the relative density of
B
on
C\mathcal{F}
. If
C\subset\mathcal{F}
is finite and interval
I
satisfies
\min I\geqslant\max C
, then the
relative density of
B
in
C\mathcal{F}
on interval
I
is

d^{I}_{C\mathcal{F}}(B)\coloneqq\frac{\mu(B(I)\cap C\mathcal{F})}{\mu(\mathcal{F}(I)\cap C\mathcal{F})}.

Suppose
C
is also prefix-free. Then, by
3.1
,
\mu(\mathcal{F}(I)\cap C\mathcal{F})=\lvert I\rvert\mu(C)
. Also
(c\mathcal{F}\colon c\in C)
partition
C\mathcal{F}
. In particular,

\displaystyle d^{I}_{C\mathcal{F}}(B)

\displaystyle=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(C)^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)\cap C\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in C}\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(C)^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)\cap c\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in C}\frac{\mu(c)}{\mu(C)}\cdot d^{I}_{c\mathcal{F}}(B)

\displaystyle=\mu(C)^{-1}\sum_{c\in C}d^{I}(B\cap c\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\mu(C)^{-1}\cdot d^{I}(B\cap C\mathcal{F}).

For every set
B
that we consider in this paper and every word
c
, the sequence
d^{I_{j}}_{c\mathcal{F}}(B)
converges (to
d^{I_{\infty}}_{c\mathcal{F}}(B)
). Thus the sequence
d^{I_{j}}_{C\mathcal{F}}(B)
converges to a limit
d^{I_{\infty}}_{C\mathcal{F}}(B)
. Again, these limits are additive.

Observation 6.4
.

Let
C\subset\mathcal{F}
be finite and prefix-free. Then

d^{I_{\infty}}_{C\mathcal{F}}(B)=\sum_{c\in C}\frac{\mu(c)}{\mu(C)}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{c\mathcal{F}}(B)=\mu(C)^{-1}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap C\mathcal{F}).

Now for the key technical lemma for our density results.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
A,B\subset\mathcal{F}
. If
(C_{k})
is an
\varepsilon
-capturing steeplechase for
A
with
\mu(C_{1})\geqslant 2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1/3}
, then

d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(AB)\geqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)-3\varepsilon^{1/3}.

Proof.

Let
w\in\mathcal{F}
be such that

d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)\geqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)-\varepsilon^{1/3}.

Apply
Lemma

6.3
to
(C_{k})
and
w
to give an
N
such that letting
C=C_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup C_{N}
, if
n\geqslant\max C_{N}+\lvert w\rvert
, then

\mu((C_{1}\mathcal{F}\setminus Cw\mathcal{F})(n))\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

We may greedily choose
\widetilde{C}\subset C
(starting with shorter words first) such that
\widetilde{C}w
is prefix-free and
\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F}=Cw\mathcal{F}
. Note that

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu((C_{1}\mathcal{F}\setminus\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F})(n))\geqslant\mu((C_{1}\mathcal{F})(n))-\mu((\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F})(n))

\displaystyle=\mu(C_{1})-\mu(\widetilde{C}w)\geqslant 2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1/3}-\mu(\widetilde{C}w)

and so
\mu(\widetilde{C}w)\geqslant\varepsilon^{1/3}
.

Let
I
be an interval with
\min I\geqslant\max C_{N}+\lvert w\rvert
and let
X\subset\mathcal{F}
. Note that
\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F}\subset C_{1}\mathcal{F}
and so

\displaystyle d^{I}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(X)

\displaystyle=\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(C_{1})^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(X(n)\cap C_{1}\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\geqslant\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\mu(C_{1})^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(X(n)\cap\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\geqslant\lvert I\rvert^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\frac{\mu(X(n)\cap\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F})}{\mu(\widetilde{C}w)+2\varepsilon}.

Using the fact that
x/(y+2\varepsilon)\geqslant x/y-2\varepsilon x/y^{2}\geqslant x/y-2\varepsilon^{1/3}
for
\varepsilon>0
,
x\in[0,1]
, and
y\geqslant\varepsilon^{1/3}
, we have

d^{I}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(X)\geqslant d^{I}_{\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F}}(X)-2\varepsilon^{1/3}.

Setting
X=AB
,
I=I_{j}
, and taking
j
to infinity gives

d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(AB)\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F}}(AB)-2\varepsilon^{1/3}.

(2)

Now,

\displaystyle d^{I_{\infty}}_{\widetilde{C}w\mathcal{F}}(AB)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\widetilde{C}}\frac{\mu(c)}{\mu(\widetilde{C})}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{cw\mathcal{F}}(AB)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{c\in\widetilde{C}}\frac{\mu(c)}{\mu(\widetilde{C})}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{cw\mathcal{F}}(cB)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\widetilde{C}}\frac{\mu(c)}{\mu(\widetilde{C})}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)

\displaystyle=d^{I_{\infty}}_{w\mathcal{F}}(B)\geqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(B)-\varepsilon^{1/3},

where the first equality used
6.4
, the first inequality used the fact that
c\in\widetilde{C}\subset C\subset A
, the second equality used
Lemma

3.3
, and the second inequality is due to the choice of
w
. Combining this with (
2
) gives the required result.
∎

In this section we prove

,

Proof of
Theorem

3.5
(a)
.

Let

be strongly
k
-product-free and let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small. Let
(C_{k})
be an
\varepsilon
-capturing steeplechase for

, as given by
Lemma

6.2
. If
\mu(C_{1})<2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1/3}
, then
d^{\ast}(S)=d^{I_{\infty}}(S)\leqslant\mu(C_{1})+\varepsilon<3\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1/3}
which is less than

. Otherwise, by
Lemma

6.5
,
d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S^{2})\geqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)-3\varepsilon^{1/3}
. Since

is strongly
k
-product-free,
S
and
S^{2}
are disjoint and so

d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)-3\varepsilon^{1/3}.

Now, by
6.4
,

d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S\cup S^{2})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)-3\varepsilon^{1/3},

and so, by
Lemma

6.5
,

d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2})-3\varepsilon^{1/3}\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)-6\varepsilon^{1/3}.

Hence,

d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S\cup S^{2}\cup S^{3})\geqslant 2d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)-6\varepsilon^{1/3}.

Iterating this argument gives

1\geqslant d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S\cup S^{2}\cup\dotsb\cup S^{k})\geqslant(k-1)d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)-3(k-1)\varepsilon^{1/3}.

But, since
(C_{k})
is
\varepsilon
-capturing for
S
,
d^{I_{\infty}}_{C_{1}\mathcal{F}}(S)=\mu(C_{1})^{-1}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}(S\cap C_{1}\mathcal{F})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}(S\cap C_{1}\mathcal{F})\geqslant d^{I_{\infty}}(S)-\varepsilon
. Hence,
(k-1)d^{I_{\infty}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)\leqslant 1+(k-1)\varepsilon+3(k-1)\varepsilon^{1/3}
. As

is arbitrarily small,

1\geqslant(k-1)d^{I_{\infty}}(S)+d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S).

But

and so
d^{\ast}(S)=d^{I_{\infty}}(S)\leqslant 1/k
. Furthermore, if
d^{\ast}(S)=1/k
, then
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/k
and so
d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}(S)\leqslant 1/k
, as required.
∎

8
Product-free sets in the free group

We now adapt our methods to the free group and prove
Theorem

1.8
. Throughout,
F
denotes the free group on a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, and
S\subset F
is a
k
-product-free set whose density we want to bound. We always assume that all words are in reduced form. Moreover,
AB
denotes the product of two sets
A,B\subset F
without cancellation, that is

AB\coloneqq\{w\in F:\text{ there is a substring decomposition }w=ab\text{ with }a\in A\text{ and }b\in B\}.

In particular,
CF
consists of all words with a prefix in
C
. We equip
F
with the measure
\mu
defined as
\mu(w)=1/\lvert F(\lvert w\rvert)\rvert
. If
\mathbf{W}=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dotsb
is a random infinite word where each
\alpha_{i+1}
is an independent uniformly random letter other than
\alpha_{i}^{-1}
, then

\mu(w)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{W}\text{ hits }w)=\begin{cases}(2\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert)^{-1}(2\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert-1)^{-(\lvert w\rvert-1)}&\text{if $w$ is not the empty word},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

As before, for
B\subset F
,
\mu(B)=\sum_{w\in B}\mu(w)
is the expected number of times that
\mathbf{W}
hits
B
. So, we can make the following observations corresponding to
2.1
and
3.1
.

Observation 8.1
.

If
C\subset F
is prefix-free, then
\mu(C)\leqslant 1
,
\mu(CF(n))\leqslant\mu(C)
for all
n
, and
\mu(CF(n))=\mu(C)
if
C
is finite and
n\geqslant\max C
.

We now define the relative density of subsets of
F
as follows.

Definition 8.2

(relative density)
.

Let
B\subset F
, and
G
be a subsemigroup of
F
with
G(n)\neq\varnothing
for all sufficiently large
n
. If
I
is an interval, then the
relative density of
B
in
G
on interval
I
is

d_{G}^{I}(B)\coloneqq\frac{\mu(B(I)\cap G)}{\mu(G(I))}=\mu(G(I))^{-1}\sum_{n\in I}\mu(B(n)\cap G).

If
G(I)=\varnothing
, we will take the relative density to be
0
by convention, and
d^{I}(B)\coloneqq d_{F}^{I}(B)
.

The upper Banach density of
B
is then
d^{\ast}(B)=\limsup_{I\to\infty}d^{I}(B)
. At this point, we can again diagonalise to obtain a sequence
(I_{j})
is such that, for every
G
and
B
that we consider in our proofs,
(d_{G}^{I_{j}}(B))
converges to some limit
d^{I_{\infty}}_{G}(B)
, and
d^{I_{\infty}}(S)=d^{\ast}(S)
. These limits are again additive. Also note that
d_{G}^{I}(B)=d^{I}(B\cap G)/d^{I}(G)
and so
d^{I_{\infty}}_{G}(B)=d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap G)/d^{I_{\infty}}(G)
if
d^{I_{\infty}}(G)>0
. We define sup density as follows.

Definition 8.3

(sup density)
.

For a set
B
, the
sup density of
B
in
G
is

d_{\sup G}^{I_{\infty}}(B)\coloneqq\sup_{w\in G}d^{I_{\infty}}_{wF\cap G}(B).

From now on, let
G=F^{\alpha\beta}\subset F
be the subsemigroup of
F
consisting of all words starting with
\alpha
and ending in
\beta
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{A}^{-1}
and
\alpha\neq\beta^{-1}
. A random sequence argument shows the following.

Observation 8.4
.

Let
C\subset G
be finite and prefix-free. Then, for all
n\geqslant\max C+2
,

\mu((CF\cap G)(n))\geqslant\frac{\mu(C)}{(2\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert-1)^{2}}.

In particular,
d^{I_{\infty}}(wF\cap G)>0
for all
w\in G
. As in
Lemma

3.3
, subtree densities of

satisfy the property that we may strip away prefixes.

Lemma 8.5
.

If
w,v\in G
, then
d^{I_{\infty}}_{wvF\cap G}(wB)=d^{I_{\infty}}_{vF\cap G}(B)
.

Proof.

For
u\in G
, note that
\mu(wu)=a\cdot\mu(u)
where
a=(2\lvert A\rvert-1)^{-\lvert w\rvert}
. So, if
X\subset G
is finite, then
\mu(wX)=a\cdot\mu(X)
. Let
I
be any interval with
\min I>\lvert wv\rvert
. Then

d^{I}_{wvF\cap G}(wB)=\frac{\mu((wB)(I)\cap wvF)}{\mu((wvF\cap G)(I))}=\frac{\mu(B(I-\lvert w\rvert)\cap vF)}{\mu((vF\cap G)(I-\lvert w\rvert))}.

where we used that
wvF\cap G=w(vF\cap G)
. For any
X\subset G
, the fact that
\mu(X(n))\in[0,1]
implies that

\lvert\mu(X(I))-\mu(X(I-\lvert w\rvert))\rvert=\bigg\lvert\sum_{n\in I}\mu(X(n))-\sum_{n\in I-\lvert w\rvert}\mu(X(n))\bigg\rvert\leqslant\lvert w\rvert.

Therefore,

\frac{\mu(B(I)\cap vF)-\lvert w\rvert}{\mu((vF\cap G)(I))+\lvert w\rvert}\leqslant d^{I}_{wvF\cap G}(wB)\leqslant\frac{\mu(B(I)\cap vF)+\lvert w\rvert}{\mu((vF\cap G)(I))-\lvert w\rvert}.

Set
I=I_{j}
and take

to infinity. From
d^{I_{\infty}}(vF\cap G)>0
it follows
\mu((vF\cap G)(I_{j}))\to\infty
. Hence, both bounds above tend to

and so
d^{I_{\infty}}_{wvF\cap G}(wB)=d^{I_{\infty}}_{vF\cap G}(B)
.
∎

We can also obtain the following analogue to

.

Observation 8.6
.

Let

be finite and prefix-free. Then

d^{I_{\infty}}_{CF\cap G}(B)=\sum_{c\in C}\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(cF\cap G)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(CF\cap G)}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{cF\cap G}(B)=\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap CF)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(CF\cap G)}.

Because

for all

d^{I_{\infty}}_{cF\cap G}(B)=\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap cF)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(cF\cap G)}\quad\text{ and }\quad d^{I_{\infty}}_{CF\cap G}(B)=\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap CF)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(CF\cap G)}.

Since

is additive, this implies that

d^{I_{\infty}}_{CF\cap G}(B)=\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap CF)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(CF\cap G)}=\sum_{c\in C}\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(B\cap cF)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(CF\cap G)}=\sum_{c\in C}\frac{d^{I_{\infty}}(cF\cap G)}{d^{I_{\infty}}(CF\cap G)}\cdot d^{I_{\infty}}_{cF\cap G}(B).\qed

Steeplechases in the free group can be defined exactly as for the free semigroup.

Definition 8.7

.

An infinite sequence

. For each positive integer

\mu(C_{i})
by
d^{I_{\infty}}(C_{i}F\cap G)
,
d_{\sup}^{I_{\infty}}
by
d_{\sup G}^{I_{\infty}}
, and all references by references to the corresponding results in this section to prove the following analogue of
Theorem

3.5
.

Theorem 8.11
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer,
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set,
F
be the free group with alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, and
G=F^{\alpha\beta}
be the subsemigroup of
F
consisting of all words starting with
\alpha
and ending with
\beta
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{A}^{-1}
and
\alpha\neq\beta^{-1}
.

(a)

If
S\subset G
is strongly
k
-product-free, then
d^{I_{\infty}}_{G}(S)\leqslant 1/k
. Moreover, if
d^{I_{\infty}}_{G}(S)=1/k
, then
d_{\sup G}^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/k
.

(b)

If
S\subset G
is
k
-product-free, then
d^{I_{\infty}}_{G}(S)\leqslant 1/\rho(k)
. Moreover, if
d^{I_{\infty}}_{G}(S)=1/\rho(k)
, then
d_{\sup G}^{I_{\infty}}(S)=1/\rho(k)
.

The arguments from Ortega, Rué, and Serra
[
ORS23
]
show that a density bound on (strongly)
k
-product-free sets in
F^{\alpha\beta}
immediately translates to a density bound in
F
. Therefore,
Theorems

1.3
and
1.8
are immediate corollaries of
Theorem

8.11
.

9
Open problems

A first natural problem left open is to determine the structure of the extremal
k
-product-free sets for
k\in\{3,5,7,13\}
. For
k=5,7,13
, we conjecture that the extremal sets are exactly as in
Theorem

1.7
. The extremal sets for
k=3
will be slightly more complicated. Indeed, while
1+3\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
2+3\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
are both maximal 3-sum-free subsets of the non-negative integers of density
1/3
, so are both
\{1,2\}+6\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\{5,6\}+6\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
(Łuczak and Schoen
[
ŁS97
]
showed that there are no others). We conjecture the corresponding result holds for the free semigroup.

Conjecture 9.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set and
\mathcal{F}
be the free semigroup with alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. If
S\subset\mathcal{F}
is 3-product-free and
d^{\ast}(S)=1/3
, then one of the following hold. Either it is possible to label each letter of
\mathcal{A}
with a label in
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
such that
S
is a subset of

\{w\in\mathcal{F}\colon\text{the sum of the labels of letters in $w$ is $1\bmod{3}$}\},

or it is possible to label each letter of
\mathcal{A}
with a label in
\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
such that
S
is a subset of

\{w\in\mathcal{F}\colon\text{the sum of the labels of letters in $w$ is $1,2\bmod{6}$}\}.

Łuczak
[
Łuc95
]
proved that every sum-free subset of the non-negative integers with density greater than
2/5
is a subset of the odd integers (Łuczak and Schoen proved similar results for (strongly)
k
-sum-free sets). Such strengthenings for subsets of the free semigroup are false as the constants
1/k
in
Theorem

1.4
and
1/\rho(k)
in
Theorem

1.7
cannot be replaced by anything smaller. For example, let
k=2
,
T
be the set of words of odd length, and
x
be any word of even length. Let

\begin{split}T^{\prime}\coloneqq&\{w\in T\colon\text{neither $x$ nor $w$ is a prefix or suffix of the other}\}\\
&\cup\{xwx\colon\text{$xwx$ has length $1\bmod{3}$}\}.\end{split}

Then
T^{\prime}
is product-free, has density at least
1/2-2\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert^{-\lvert x\rvert}
, and is not a subset of an odd-occurrence set (in fact, a set of positive density would need to be removed before this happens). Nonetheless,
T^{\prime}
is a small perturbation from the odd-occurrence set
T
. Hence, it is natural to ask whether there is some form of stability.

Conjecture 9.2
.

For each
\delta>0
, is there some
\varepsilon>0
such that if
S\subset\mathcal{F}
is product-free and
d^{\ast}(S)>1/2-\varepsilon
, then there exists an odd-occurrence set
\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma}
such that
d^{\ast}(S\setminus\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma})<\delta
?

Theorems

1.4
and
1.7
give the structure of extremal (strongly)
k
-product-free sets in the free semigroup. The free group case remains. The simplest open case is the following

Conjecture 9.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set and
F
be the free group with alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. If
S\subset F
is product-free and
d^{\ast}(S)=1/2
, then the following holds. It is possible to label each letter of
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{A}^{-1}
with a label in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
such that the label of
\alpha^{-1}
is the negation of the label of
\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
S
is a subset of

T\coloneqq\{w\in F\colon\text{the sum of the labels of letters in $w$ is $1\bmod{2}$}\}.

For strongly
k
-product-free we expect the above conjecture to hold with 2 replaced by
k
. For
k
-product-free we expect the behaviour to be the same as for the free semigroup.

We remark that our methods do give some structure. Similar arguments to
Section

4
show there is a labelling of all words in the subsemigroup
F^{\alpha\beta}
(defined in
Section

8
) such that the label of a concatenation is the sum of the individual labels and all words in
S\cap F^{\alpha\beta}
have label 1. What is missing is an understanding of how the labellings interact when letters cancel during concatenation.
