The Stochastic-Quantum Theorem
Jacob A. Barandes

Abstract
This paper introduces several new classes of mathematical structures that have close connections with physics and with the theory of dynamical systems. The most general of these structures, called generalized stochastic systems, collectively encompass many important kinds of stochastic processes, including Markov chains and random dynamical systems. This paper then states and proves a new theorem that establishes a precise correspondence between any generalized stochastic system and a unitarily evolving quantum system. This theorem therefore leads to a new formulation of quantum theory, alongside the Hilbert-space, path-integral, and quasiprobability formulations. The theorem also provides a first-principles explanation for why quantum systems are based on the complex numbers, Hilbert spaces, linear-unitary time evolution, and the Born rule. In addition, the theorem suggests that by selecting a suitable Hilbert space, together with an appropriate choice of unitary evolution, one can simulate any generalized stochastic system on a quantum computer, thereby potentially opening up an extensive set of novel applications for quantum computing.



I
Introduction

In the development of physical theories, it sometimes turns out that
existing definitions are too conceptually limiting, and that more
flexible definitions are needed. Working with more flexible definitions
at a higher level of abstraction or generality may make it easier
to discover new connections or prove new theorems, which would then
also apply down at the lower level of the original definitions.

This paper will argue that by appropriately generalizing standard
definitions of dynamical systems, one can obtain novel classes of
mathematical structures that encompass an extensive array of physically
important models. As with a traditionally defined dynamical system,
each such mathematical structure describes a physical system moving
deterministically or stochastically along some trajectory in a configuration
space, albeit with a more general set of laws than according to standard
definitions. (For pedagogical treatments of the standard theory of
dynamical systems, see, for instance,
(
Devaney:1989aitcds
;
Strogatz:1994ndac
;
KatokHasselblatt:1995ittmtods
)
.)

This paper also states and proves a new theorem showing that despite
being based on trajectories in configuration spaces, the newly introduced
class of
generalized stochastic systems
actually includes
all quantum systems. As a consequence, this
stochastic-quantum theorem

offers a more conceptually transparent way to understand quantum
systems, with superpositions no longer regarded as literal blends
of physical states. The theorem also provides a first-principles explanation
for features of quantum theory that are usually taken to be axiomatic,
including Hilbert spaces over the complex numbers, linear-unitary
evolution, and the Born rule.

Seen from another point of view, this
stochastic-quantum correspondence

yields an alternative way to formulate quantum theory, in the language
of trajectories unfolding stochastically in configuration spaces.
This alternative formulation is distinct from the traditional Hilbert-space
formulation
(
Dirac:1930pofm
;
vonNeumann:1932mgdq
)
, the path-integral
formulation
(
Dirac:1933tliqm
;
Feynman:1942tpolaiqm
;
Feynman:1948statnrqm
)
,
and the quasi-probability formulation
(
Wigner:1932otqcfte
;
Moyal:1949qmaast
)
.

From a more practical perspective, turning this stochastic-quantum
correspondence around suggests that unitarily evolving quantum systems
can be put to work simulating a very broad class of non-Markovian
stochastic processes, thereby potentially opening up an extensive
suite of new applications for quantum computers.

Section
II
begins by defining deterministic
generalizations of dynamical systems, followed by the introduction
of important distinctions between
indivisible
,
divisible
,
and
Markovian
dynamics. Section
III

provides a generalized definition of a system with stochastic laws,
shows how to represent such a generalized stochastic system in the
formalism of linear algebra, describes connections between this work
and the existing research literature, defines the relationship between
a composite system and its subsystems, and introduces the crucial
notion of a
unistochastic system
. Section
IV

states the stochastic-quantum theorem, whose proof is this paper’s
primary goal, and then discusses some important corollaries and provides
a simple example of the theorem in practice. Section
V

lays out the theorem’s proof, which entails explicitly constructing
the claimed correspondence between stochastic systems and quantum
systems along the way. Section
VI

concludes the paper with a brief discussion of future work.

II
Deterministic Systems

II.1
Generalized Dynamical Systems

Dynamical systems are abstract mathematical structures that usefully
model many deterministic physical processes. According to the standard
definition
(
Devaney:1989aitcds
;
Strogatz:1994ndac
;
KatokHasselblatt:1995ittmtods
)
,
a dynamical system consists of a map representing some kind of evolution
law that can be repeatedly applied to the elements of some set of
states. A dynamical system is usually assumed to be
divisible
,
in the sense that one can ‘divide up’
its evolution law over any time duration into well-defined evolution
laws that describe intermediate time durations. The more general case
would be an
indivisible
dynamical system that might lack this
feature.

The terms ‘divisible’ and ‘indivisible’
for dynamical laws are remarkably new. The terminology appears to
be due to Wolf and Cirac, who introduced it in a 2008 paper
(
WolfCirac:2008dqc
)

on quantum channels.
1
1

1

Note that this terminology is unrelated to the much older concept
of
infinite divisibility
, which refers to a probability distribution
that can be expressed as the probability distribution of a sum of
any integer number of independent and identically distributed random
variables.

To accommodate the eventual possibility of indivisible evolution,
this paper will define a
generalized dynamical system
to
mean a tuple of the form

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)

(1)

that consists of the following data.

•

The symbol
\mathcal{X}
denotes a set that will be called the generalized
dynamical system’s
state space
(or
phase space
), and
whose individual elements
i\in\mathcal{X}
denote the system’s
(allowed) states
.

•

Note that
\mathcal{X}
may or may not be a finite set, and it may
or may not involve additional structure in its definition, such as
measure-theoretic structure or a vector-space structure. For the purposes
of this paper, no such additional structure will be specified or assumed.
More broadly, for reasons of brevity and simplicity, this paper will
entirely set aside measure-theoretic considerations that arise for
the case of uncountable sets.

•

The symbol
\mathcal{T}
denotes a
set of times

t\in\mathcal{T}
,
where
\mathcal{T}
may or may not be isomorphic to a subset of the
real line
\mathbb{R}
under addition.

•

The symbol
f
denotes a map

f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}

(2)

that will be called the system’s
dynamical map
. This dynamical
map
f
takes as inputs any state
i
and any time
t
, and outputs
a state
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\in\mathcal{X}
:

i,t\mapsto f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(3)

•

Fixing the time
t
turns
f
into a
time-dependent dynamical map

f_{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}

(4)

defined by

i\mapsto f_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)\equiv f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X}}}\right].

(5)

•

Without any important loss of generality, the set of times
\mathcal{T}

will be assumed to include an element denoted by
0
and called the

initial time
. It will be further assumed that at the initial
time
0
, the time-dependent dynamical map
f_{t}
trivializes to
the identity map
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
:

f_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}},\quad\textrm{or}\quad f_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=i\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X}}}\right].

(6)

•

One can regard the argument
i
appearing in the expression
f_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)

as an initial state of the system at the initial time
0
, with the
time-dependent dynamical map
f_{t}
then describing the evolution
of that state
i
from the initial time
0
to the time
t
.

•

Given a fixed state
i
, the set of states

\{f_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)\,|\,t\in\mathcal{T}\}\subset\mathcal{X}

(7)

describes the
orbit
, or
trajectory
, of the initial
state
i
through the system’s state space
\mathcal{X}
according
to the dynamical map
f
.

In many applications, one takes the set of times
\mathcal{T}
to
be a
semigroup
, meaning that the definition of
\mathcal{T}

includes an associative binary operation
\star
(which is often
denoted instead by
+
in the commutative case):

t,t^{\prime}\mapsto t\star t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(8)

(t\star t^{\prime})\star t^{\prime\prime}=t\star(t^{\prime}\star t^{\prime\prime})\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(9)

One usually also takes the initial time
0
to be the identity element
under this binary operation,

0\star t=t\star 0=t\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(10)

in which case
\mathcal{T}
becomes a
monoid
, meaning a
semigroup with an identity element. If, furthermore, every time
t

has an inverse
t^{\prime}
such that
t\star t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}\star t=0
,
then
\mathcal{T}
becomes a
group
.

II.2
Divisible Dynamical Systems

In the most general case, a generalized dynamical system will not
provide a way to evolve a system from a
non-initial
time
t^{\prime}\neq 0

to another time
t
. A generalized dynamical system will also generically
lack any means of ‘dividing up’ the
evolution from
0
to
t\neq 0
into well-defined forms of evolution
over intermediate time durations between
0
and
t
(even assuming
that the set of times
\mathcal{T}
has a notion of ordering). To
make contact with the kinds of dynamical systems considered more widely
in the research literature, it will therefore be necessary to introduce
a somewhat less general class of mathematical structures.

This paper will define a
divisible dynamical system
to be
a tuple of the form

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},g}}\right).

(11)

•

Here
\mathcal{X}
is a state space and
\mathcal{T}
is a set of
times forming a monoid, whose identity element
0
plays the role
of an initial time, as usual.

•

The symbol
g
denotes a map

g:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}^{2}\to\mathcal{X}

(12)

that will be called the divisible dynamical system’s
transition map
.
This transition map
g
takes as inputs any state
i
and any pair
of times
t,t^{\prime}
, and outputs a state
g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,t^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)\in\mathcal{X}
:

\displaystyle i,t,t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mapsto g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,t^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)\in\mathcal{X}

(13)

\displaystyle\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X},\,t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

The transition map
g
here should be understood as describing the
evolution or transition of the state
i
at the time
t^{\prime}

to the state
g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,t^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)
at the time

t
.

•

Fixing two times
t,t^{\prime}
turns
g
into a
time-dependent transition map

g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}

(14)

defined by

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)\equiv g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t,t^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X}}}\right].

This time-dependent transition map will be required to trivialize
to the identity map
\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}

when
t^{\prime}=t
,

\displaystyle g_{t\leftarrow t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\textrm{or}\quad g_{t\leftarrow t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)=i\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

as well as satisfy the
divisibility condition

\displaystyle g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}\circ g_{t^{\prime}\leftarrow t^{\prime\prime}}

(17)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

where
\circ
denotes function composition. The divisibility
condition means that the time-dependent transition map
g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime\prime}}

factorizes or ‘divides up’ into a part

g_{t^{\prime}\leftarrow t^{\prime\prime}}
that carries out the evolution
from
t^{\prime\prime}
to
t^{\prime}
, followed by a part
g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}

that carries out the evolution from
t^{\prime}
to
t
.

•

Choosing the time
t^{\prime}
in the time-dependent transition map

g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}
to be the initial time
0
naturally defines
a time-dependent dynamical map (
4
),

f_{t}\equiv g_{t\leftarrow 0}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(18)

which then also defines an overall dynamical map
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}

according to
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\equiv f_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)
. The trivialization
condition
g_{0\leftarrow 0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}
from (
16
)
ensures that
f_{t}
satisfies the corresponding trivialization condition

f_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}
in (
6
).
It follows that every divisible dynamical system is, in particular,
a generalized dynamical system, one that includes the additional structure
that corresponds to having a transition map
g
.

•

Meanwhile, setting
t^{\prime\prime}=0
in the divisibility condition
(
17
) on the transition
map
g
yields the subsidiary divisibility condition

f_{t}=g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}\circ f_{t^{\prime}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(19)

which means that the time-dependent dynamical map
f_{t}
‘divides
up’ into a part
f_{t^{\prime}}
that carries out
the evolution from the initial time
0
to
t^{\prime}
, followed
by a part
g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}
that carries out the evolution
from
t^{\prime}
to
t
.

•

It also follows from the subsidiary divisibility condition (
19
),
together with the trivialization condition
f_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}

in (
6
),
that the time-dependent dynamical map
f_{t}
has a well-defined
inverse
f_{t}^{-1}
given by

f_{t}^{-1}=g_{0\leftarrow t}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(20)

That is, a divisible dynamical system’s time-dependent dynamical maps

f_{t}
are invertible.
2
2

2

If the set of times
\mathcal{T}
has a well-defined ordering relation,
and if one modifies the definition of a divisible dynamical system

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},g}}\right)
by restricting the transition
map (
12
) so that
g_{t\leftarrow t^{\prime}}

is only defined when
t^{\prime}
comes before
t
according to
that ordering relation, then the arguments leading to (
20
)
will break down. In that case, the time-dependent dynamical maps
f_{t}

will not necessarily have inverses.

II.3
Markovian Dynamical Systems

A divisible dynamical system will be called
memoryless
, or

Markovian
, if it has the special property

g_{t\star t^{\prime}\leftarrow t^{\prime}}=g_{t\leftarrow 0}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(21)

Equivalently, using
f_{t}\equiv g_{t\leftarrow 0}
from (
18
),
one can write this special property as

g_{t\star t^{\prime}\leftarrow t^{\prime}}=f_{t}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(22)

It then follows immediately from the subsidiary divisibility condition
(
19
)
that the dynamical map
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}

gives a semigroup action of the set of times
\mathcal{T}
on the
state space
\mathcal{X}
, in the sense that the semigroup operation

\star
on
\mathcal{T}
is mapped to function composition:

f_{t\star t^{\prime}}=f_{t}\circ f_{t^{\prime}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(23)

This equation is an example of
Markovianity
, or a
Markov property
.
Roughly speaking, a Markov property implies that time evolution depends
only on duration, and not on history.

Notice that the Markov property (
23
)
is phrased directly in terms of a dynamical map
f
, so it can be
imposed on a generalized dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)
,
without any need to invoke a transition map
g
. In that case, it
also follows that there is no longer a meaningful distinction between
a generalized dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)

and a divisible dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},g}}\right)
.
As such, if the Markov property (
23
)
is imposed, then the resulting mathematical structure will simply
be called a
Markovian dynamical system
.

What most references call a ‘dynamical system’
corresponds to what this paper would call a Markovian dynamical system.
That is, a dynamical system, without any further qualifiers, is a
tuple
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)
for which
\mathcal{X}

is a state space,
\mathcal{T}
is a set of times forming a monoid,
and
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}

is a dynamical map satisfying the Markov property (
23
).
It follows that a generalized dynamical system (
1
),
as befits its name, really is a generalization of the kinds of dynamical
systems that are usually considered in textbooks, in the research
literature, and in many applications.

In the most general cases, however, generalized dynamical systems
will not satisfy the Markov property (
23
).
Nor will it necessarily be possible to obtain a given generalized
dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)
by starting
with a divisible dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},g}}\right)

and then defining a dynamical map
f
according to
f_{t}\equiv g_{t\leftarrow 0}

for all times
t
, as in (
18
).
Indeed, in light of the inversion formula (
20
),
the absence of any inverse time-dependent dynamical maps
f_{t}^{-1}

would present an immediate obstruction to deriving a dynamical map

f
from a transition map
g
.

A generalized dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)

that cannot be derived from a divisible dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},g}}\right)

will lack the structure needed to divide up its time evolution into
intermediate durations, and will therefore be called an
indivisible dynamical system
.

III
Stochastic Systems

III.1
Generalized Stochastic Systems

This paper will be primarily concerned with a mathematical structure
that replaces the deterministic behavior of a generalized dynamical
system (
1
) with probabilistic,
or stochastic, behavior. A
generalized stochastic system

will be defined to mean a tuple of the form

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},\Gamma,p,\mathcal{A}}}\right)

(24)

that consists of the following data.

•

The symbol
\mathcal{C}
will denote a set called the system’s
configuration space
,
and the elements of
\mathcal{C}
will be called the system’s
(allowed) configurations
.
Configurations and configuration spaces will play an analogous role
for generalized stochastic systems that states and state spaces play
for generalized dynamical systems.

•

The reason for switching the terminology from ‘states’
to ‘configurations’ is conceptual. In
applications of the theory of dynamical systems to physical situations,
such as in classical Hamiltonian mechanics, the notion of a ‘state’
is often taken to include rates of change or momenta in addition to
configurations, because defining a ‘state’
in that way can make it possible to obtain deterministic laws in the
form of first-order differential equations. For a generalized stochastic
system, by contrast, the system’s probabilistic laws may mean that
rates of change and momenta are not well-defined in the absence of
specifying a trajectory. In that case, the only available notion of
a ‘state’ is limited to the more rudimentary
notion of a ‘configuration.’

•

For the purposes of this paper,
\mathcal{C}
will be assumed to
be a finite set, with some (possibly very large) number
N
of elements
denoted by
1,2,3,\dots,N
, and labeled abstractly by Latin letters

i,j,\dots
. By an abuse of notation, the symbol
\mathcal{C}

will sometimes be used to refer to the generalized stochastic system
as a whole.

•

The symbol
\mathcal{T}
will again denote a
set of times
,
including a time
0
that will be called the system’s
initial time
.

•

The symbol
\Gamma
will denote a map

\Gamma:\mathcal{C}^{2}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}

(25)

that will be called the system’s
stochastic map
, where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

denotes the closed unit interval
0\leq x\leq 1
. Each value of this
stochastic map will be labeled as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right),t}}\right)

(26)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

and will be called the
conditional probability
or
transition probability

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)
for the system to be in its
i
th
configuration at the time
t
, given that the system is in its
j
th
configuration at the initial time
0
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

(27)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

•

The stochastic map
\Gamma
will be required to satisfy
the standard normalization condition

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)=1

(28)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

as well as the initial condition

\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)\equiv\delta_{ij}\equiv\begin{cases}1&\textrm{for }i=j,\\
0&\textrm{for }i\neq j,\end{cases}

(29)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the usual Kronecker delta.

•

The symbol
p
will denote a map

p:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\subset\mathbb{R}

(30)

that will be called the system’s
probability distribution
.
Each value of this map will be labeled as

\displaystyle p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

(31)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

and will be called the
standalone probability
for the system
to be in its
i
th configuration at the time
t
.

•

Only the standalone probabilities
p_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right),\dots,p_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

at the initial time
0
will be taken to be freely adjustable, subject
to the standard normalization condition

\sum_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=1.

(32)

The standalone probabilities at every other time
t
will be assumed
to be defined by the following
Bayesian marginalization condition
:

\displaystyle p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)p_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)p_{j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

The normalization condition (
28
)
on the stochastic map
\Gamma
and the normalization condition
(
32
) on the standalone
probabilities at the initial time
0
then together ensure that the
probability distribution
p
satisfies the standard normalization
condition more generally:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=1\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(34)

•

Note that the definition of the stochastic map
\Gamma

is independent of the choice of standalone probabilities
p_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right),\dots,p_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

at the initial time
0
. That is,
\Gamma
can be freely
adjusted independently of those initial standalone probabilities.
Importantly, the Bayesian marginalization condition (
33
)
therefore defines a
linear relationship
between the standalone
probabilities
p_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right),\dots,p_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)
at
the initial time
0
and the standalone probabilities
p_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right),\dots,p_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

at any other time
t
. In the work ahead, it will be argued that
this linear relationship is ultimately responsible for the linearity
of time evolution in quantum theory.

•

The stochastic map
\Gamma
will
not
be assumed
to have anything like a Markov or divisibility property. More precisely,
no assumption will be made that given any pair of times
t,t^{\prime}
,
there will exist a time
t^{\prime\prime}
such that the following
Markov property holds:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma_{ik}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{\prime\prime}}}\right)\Gamma_{kj}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{\prime}}}\right)

(35)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C}}}\right].

Nor will it be assumed that
\Gamma
satisfies even the

weaker
divisibility condition that for any pair of times
t,t^{\prime}
,
there exists a set of real-valued, non-negative quantities
X_{ik}\geq 0

for which
3
3

3

Note that if the quantities
\Gamma_{kj}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{\prime}}}\right)

are regarded as forming an
N\times N
stochastic matrix, a term
to be defined shortly, then the inverse matrix, if it exists, will
always have negative entries unless both matrices are permutation
matrices. It follows that one cannot safely define the non-negative
quantities
X_{ij}
by invoking inverse matrices.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{ij}

\displaystyle=1\textrm{\quad and}\quad\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{ik}\Gamma_{kj}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{\prime}}}\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C}}}\right].

That is, a generalized stochastic system will generically be
non-Markovian

and
indivisible
, in a sense that parallels the notion of indivisibility
for a generalized dynamical system, as introduced earlier in this
paper.

•

This notion of non-Markovianity represents a distinct way to generalize
the Markovian case, as compared with most forms of non-Markovianity
described in textbooks and in the research literature. According to
those more traditional forms of non-Markovianity, one assumes that
the set of times
\mathcal{T}
has an ordering relation, and one
further assumes the existence of higher-order conditional probabilities

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1};j_{2},t_{2};\dots}}\right)
that are
conditioned on arbitrarily many times
t_{1},t_{2},\dots
. From that
more traditional standpoint, the system is Markovian if and only if
the latest conditioning time
t_{1}
always screens off all the earlier
conditioning times
t_{2},\dots
, so that
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1};j_{2},t_{2};\dots}}\right)=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j_{1},t_{1}}}\right)
.
The definition of a generalized stochastic system presented in this
paper does not assume the existence of such higher-order conditional
probabilities in the first place.

•

The symbol
\mathcal{A}
will denote a commutative algebra of maps
of the form

A:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}

(37)

under the usual rules of function arithmetic, and will be called
the system’s
algebra of random variables
. The individual
values of each
random variable

A\in\mathcal{A}
will be labeled
as

a_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv A\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(38)

Each such value
a_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
will be called the
magnitude

of the random variable
A
when the system is in its
i
th configuration
at the time
t
. No assumption will be made here that these magnitudes
are all distinct, even at any fixed time
t
.

•

For the purposes of this paper, the algebra of random variables
\mathcal{A}

will always be taken to be maximal, in the sense of containing every
well-defined map of the form (
37
).

•

Fixing the time
t
, the
expectation value

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle A\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right\rangle

of a random variable
A
will denote its statistical average according
to the probability distribution
p
at the time
t
:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle A\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right\rangle\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right).

(39)

III.2
Ingredients from Linear Algebra

Given a generalized stochastic system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},\Gamma,p,\mathcal{A}}}\right)
,
with
\mathcal{C}
a configuration space of finite integer size

N
, it will be convenient to introduce some formalism from linear
algebra.

•

Fixing the time
t
, let
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
, called the system’s

(time-dependent) probability vector
, denote the
N\times 1

column vector whose
i
th entry is the
i
th standalone probability

p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
:

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\\
\vdots\\
p_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

•

Again fixing the time
t
, let
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
,
called the system’s
(time-dependent) transition matrix
, denote
the
N\times N
matrix for which the entry in the
i
th row,
j
th
column is the transition probability
\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)
:

\displaystyle\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{11}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)&\Gamma_{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\\
\Gamma_{21}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)&\ddots\\
&&\Gamma_{NN}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,t|1,0}}\right)&p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,t|2,0}}\right)\\
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2,t|1,0}}\right)&\ddots\\
&&p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N,t|N,0}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

It follows that for any time
t
, the transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

is a
(column) stochastic matrix
in the mathematical sense,
meaning that its entries are all non-negative real numbers,

\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\geq 0\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(42)

and that its columns each sum to
1
, as required in (
28
).
The initial condition (
29
) on
the stochastic map
\Gamma
then becomes the statement
that the transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)
at the
initial time
0
is just the
N\times N
identity matrix
\mymathbb{1}
:

\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\mymathbb{1}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\ddots\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}.

(43)

Observe that the Bayesian marginalization condition (
33
)
naturally takes the form of matrix multiplication:

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(44)

III.3
Connections with Other Constructions

There is a definite sense in which a generalized stochastic system

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},\Gamma,p,\mathcal{A}}}\right)
,
as introduced in (
24
), provides
a probabilistic extension of a generalized dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)
,
as introduced in (
1
). Indeed,
if the stochastic map
\Gamma:\mathcal{C}^{2}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

from (
25
) outputs only the trivial probabilities

1
and
0
, then
\Gamma
is effectively deterministic.
In that case, one can naturally set
\mathcal{X}\equiv\mathcal{C}

and define a dynamical map
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}

according to

\displaystyle f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j,t}}\right)

\displaystyle=i\quad\textrm{if and only if}\quad\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,j}}\right),t}}\right)=1

(45)

\displaystyle\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{X},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

A generalized stochastic system also naturally generalizes a mathematical
structure called a
stochastic dynamical system
, or
random dynamical system

(
Honerkamp:1996sdscnmda
;
Arnold:1998rds
)
.

•

The definition of a random dynamical system starts with what this
paper would call a generalized dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)
,
with
\mathcal{T}
a monoid, and replaces the single dynamical map

f
with a probabilistic family or ensemble of dynamical maps
\{f_{\omega}\,|\,\omega\in\Omega\}

that are indexed by a point
\omega
in some sample space
\Omega
.
That is, a randomly sampled point
\omega
in
\Omega
picks
out an entire dynamical map
f_{\omega}
, as a whole.

•

For any randomly sampled point
\omega
and any fixed time
t
,
one can define a map

f_{\omega,t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}

(46)

by

i\mapsto f_{\omega,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i}}\right)\equiv f_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{X}}}\right].

(47)

A random dynamical system is then assumed to satisfy an initial condition
that generalizes (
6
),

f_{\omega,0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }\omega\in\Omega}}\right],

(48)

as well as a Markov property that generalizes (
23
),

\displaystyle f_{\omega^{\prime},t\star t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega^{\prime},t^{\prime}}}\right),t}\circ f_{\omega^{\prime},t^{\prime}}

(49)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega,\,t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

Here
\theta:\Omega\times\mathcal{T}\to\Omega

is a map that is part of the definition of the random dynamical system,
and specifies a deterministic rule for updating the sample point
\omega^{\prime}

and the dynamical map
f_{\omega^{\prime}}
to accommodate replacing
the original initial time
0
with the effectively new initial time

t^{\prime}
. That is,
\theta
is necessary to account for any
(purportedly deterministic) evolution in the underlying source of
randomness itself.

•

It follows from the foregoing definitions that the conditional probability

p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)
for the system to be in its
i
th
state at the time
t
, given that the system is in its
j
th state
at the initial time
0
, is obtained by adding up the probabilities
for all the points
\omega
in the sample space
\Omega
whose
corresponding dynamical maps
f_{\omega}
take the state
j
at
the initial time
0
and yield the state
i
at the final time
t
:

\displaystyle p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,t|j,0}}\right)

\displaystyle=\textrm{probability}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{\omega\in\Omega\,|\,f_{\omega,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)=i\}}}\right)

(50)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{X},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

•

Notice that a random dynamical system assigns a well-defined probability
to any set of dynamical maps
\{f_{\omega}\,|\,\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}\}

that corresponds to a measurable subset
\Omega^{\prime}
of
the sample space
\Omega
. Thus, after fixing a state
j

at the initial time
0
, a random dynamical system assigns a well-defined
probability to any set of trajectories of the form
\{\{f_{\omega,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j}}\right)\,|\,t\in\mathcal{T}\}\,|\,\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}\}
.
(The nested subset notation here is just intended to make clear that
this expression refers to an indexed set of indexed sets.) In particular,
by generalizing the right-hand side of (
50
)
to impose more conditions on the system’s dynamical maps, one can
define conditional probabilities
p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i_{1},t_{1};i_{2},t_{2};\dots|j,0}}\right)

that involve arbitrarily many times. Random dynamical systems therefore
contain far more information than the generalized stochastic systems
defined in this paper, meaning that a generalized stochastic system
indeed represents a less constrained, more general kind of mathematical
structure.

In a sense, the absolutely
most

general
kind of stochastic
mathematical structure is a
stochastic process
, which essentially
requires only the specification of a state space
\mathcal{X}
, a
set of times
\mathcal{T}
, an initial probability distribution
p
,
and a set of one or more time-dependent random variables
\mathcal{A}
.
(See
(
Rosenblatt:1962rp
;
Parzen:1962sp
;
Doob:1990sp
;
Ross:1995sp
)

for textbook treatments.) Importantly, the definition of stochastic
process lacks the specification of a
dynamical law
. By requiring
a dynamical law in the form of a stochastic map
\Gamma
,
a generalized stochastic system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},\Gamma,p,\mathcal{A}}}\right)

is not quite as general as a stochastic process, but will still be
general enough to encompass a large class of physical and mathematical
models.

In particular, one can regard any
Markov chain
as a special
case of a generalized stochastic system. The starting place is to
assume that the set of times
\mathcal{T}
is isomorphic to the integers,

\mathcal{T}\cong\mathbb{Z}
, with each time
t=n\,\delta t
an
integer number
n\in\mathbb{Z}
of steps of some fixed, elementary
time scale
\delta t
. One then further assumes that for each integer

n
, the time-dependent transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right)

originally defined in (
41
) can be expressed
as the
n
th power of the transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta t}}\right)

that implements the evolution for just the first time step
\delta t
:

\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta t}}\right)}}\right]^{n}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}}}\right].

(51)

More broadly, a generalized stochastic system can therefore be understood
as a kind of non-Markovian generalization of a Markov chain.

As this paper will also show, the class of generalized stochastic
systems essentially includes all quantum systems as well.

III.4
Composite Systems and Subsystems

Introducing a notation of primes and tildes now to distinguish between
specific generalized stochastic systems, a generalized stochastic
system
\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\Gamma},\tilde{p},\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\end{pmatrix}

will be called a
composite system
if its configuration space

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
is naturally expressible as a nontrivial Cartesian
product of two sets
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
:

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.

(52)

A composite system has the following salient features.

•

Letting
N
denote the size of
\mathcal{C}
, and letting
N^{\prime}

denote the size of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, the composite system’s
configuration space
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
has size
\tilde{N}\equiv NN^{\prime}
.

•

Labeling the elements of
\mathcal{C}
by unprimed Latin letters

i,j,\dots
, and labeling the elements of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

by primed Latin letters
i^{\prime},j^{\prime},\dots
, the composite
system’s stochastic map
\tilde{\Gamma}:\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{2}\times\tilde{\mathcal{T}}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

has individual values that will be denoted by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{ii^{\prime},jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{\Gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,i^{\prime}}}\right),\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j,j^{\prime}}}\right)}}\right),t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,i^{\prime}}}\right),t|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(j,j^{\prime}}}\right),0}}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C},\,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\,t\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}].

The normalization condition (
28
)
on the stochastic map
\tilde{\Gamma}
takes the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{ii^{\prime},jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=1

(54)

\displaystyle\quad[\textrm{for all }j\in\mathcal{C},\,j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\,t\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}].

•

The composite system’s probability distribution
\tilde{p}:\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\times\tilde{\mathcal{T}}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

has individual values at any fixed time
t
that will be denoted
by

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{ii^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i,i^{\prime}}}\right),t}}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\negthickspace\negthickspace\negthickspace[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\,t\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}].

The normalization condition (
34
)
on the probability distribution
\tilde{p}
takes the form

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{p}_{ii^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=1\quad[\textrm{for all }t\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}],

(56)

and the Bayesian marginalization condition (
33
)
becomes the definition

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{ii^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{ii^{\prime},jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\tilde{p}_{jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle\negmedspace\negmedspace\negmedspace[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\,t\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}}].

Given a composite system
(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\Gamma},\tilde{p},\tilde{\mathcal{A}})

with configuration space
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
,
as in (
52
), the composite
system’s set of times
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
trivially defines two
sets of times
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
according
to

\mathcal{T}\equiv\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\equiv\tilde{\mathcal{T}}.

(58)

Meanwhile, the composite system’s probability distribution
\tilde{p}

defines two probability distributions
p:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

and
p^{\prime}:\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

according to the respective marginalization formulas

\displaystyle p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{p}_{ii^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\quad[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}],

(59)

\displaystyle p_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{p}_{ii^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\quad[\textrm{for all }i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\,t\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}].

(60)

These two probability distributions
p
and
p^{\prime}
do not
necessarily turn
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
into
generalized stochastic systems of their own, in the sense of (
24
),
due to the generic lack of well-defined stochastic maps
\Gamma:\mathcal{C}^{2}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]

and
\Gamma^{\prime}:\mathcal{C}^{\prime 2}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
.
Indeed, in place of the Bayesian marginalization condition (
33
),
one instead has the relations

\displaystyle p_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{ii^{\prime},jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\tilde{p}_{jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}],

\displaystyle p_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{ii^{\prime},jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\tilde{p}_{jj^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)

(62)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad[\textrm{for all }i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\,t\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}].

The two sets
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
will be
called the configuration spaces of
subsystems
of the composite
system
(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\Gamma},\tilde{p},\tilde{\mathcal{A}})
.

III.5
Unistochastic Systems

Recall that an
N\times N
matrix
U
with complex-valued entries
is called
unitary
if it satisfies

U^{\dagger}U=UU^{\dagger}=\mymathbb{1}.

(63)

Here
\dagger
denotes the adjoint operation, meaning complex conjugation
combined with the transpose operation.

A stochastic matrix
X
is called
unistochastic
if each of
its entries
X_{ij}
is the modulus-squared
|U_{ij}|^{2}

of the corresponding entry
U_{ij}
of a unitary matrix
U
:

X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{11}&X_{12}\\
X_{21}&\ddots\\
&&X_{NN}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}|U_{11}|^{2}&|U_{12}|^{2}\\
|U_{21}|^{2}&\ddots\\
&&|U_{NN}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(64)

The study of unistochastic matrices was initiated in a 1954 paper
(
Horn:1954dsmatdoarm
)

by Horn, who originally called them ‘ortho-stochastic
matrices.’ Unistochastic matrices were given their
modern name in 1989 by Thompson
(
Thompson:1989uln
;
NylenTamUhlig:1993oteopsonhasm
)
,
and today
orthostochastic matrices
refer to the special case
in which
U
can be taken to be a real orthogonal matrix, meaning
a unitary matrix with entries that are all real-valued.

Every unistochastic matrix is
doubly stochastic
, or
bistochastic
,
meaning that its columns
and
rows each sum to
1
.
4
4

4

Proof:
\sum_{i}|V_{ij}|^{2}=\sum_{i}\overline{V_{ij}}V_{ij}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V^{\dagger}V}}\right]_{jj}=1

and
\sum_{j}|V_{ij}|^{2}=\sum_{j}V_{ij}\overline{V_{ij}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[VV^{\dagger}}}\right]_{ii}=1
,
where bars denote complex conjugation. QED
All
2\times 2
doubly stochastic matrices are unistochastic,
5
5

5

Proof: Every
2\times 2
doubly stochastic matrix is of the form
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x&1-x\\
1-x&x\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
, where
0\leq x\leq 1
, and the matrix
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\sqrt{x}&-\sqrt{1-x}\\
\sqrt{1-x}&\sqrt{x}\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
is easily seen to be unitary. QED
but this equivalence does not extend beyond the
2\times 2
case.
6
6

6

For example, the
3\times 3
doubly stochastic matrix
\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&0&1\\
1&1&0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
is provably not unistochastic.
Again beyond the
2\times 2
case, not every unistochastic matrix
is orthostochastic.
7
7

7

For example, the
3\times 3
matrix
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&z&z^{2}\\
1&z^{2}&z\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)

A generalized stochastic system

systems.

IV
The Stochastic-Quantum Theorem

IV.1
Statement of the Theorem

With all these preliminaries now in place, this paper’s next major
goal will be to prove the following theorem, which is a new result.

\boxed{\begin{subarray}{c}{\displaystyle\textbf{The Stochastic-Quantum Theorem}}\\
\ \\
{\displaystyle\textrm{Every generalized stochastic system}}\\
{\displaystyle\textrm{can be regarded as a subsystem of a}}\\
{\displaystyle\textrm{unistochastic system.}}\end{subarray}}

(65)

Remarkably, to study the class of generalized stochastic systems,
this theorem implies that it suffices to restrict one’s attention
to the subclass of unistochastic systems.

IV.2

The proof of the stochastic-quantum theorem (

IV.3
A Simple Example

The notion of embedding a generalized stochastic system into a unistochastic
system does not always require an elaborate construction. As a simple
example, consider a
discrete
Markovian dynamical system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T},f}}\right)

whose state space
\mathcal{X}
has some finite size
N
, whose
set of times
\mathcal{T}
is isomorphic to the integers
\mathbb{Z}

under addition, and whose dynamical map
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{X}

satisfies the appropriate version of the Markov property (
23

f_{t+t^{\prime}}=f_{t}\circ f_{t^{\prime}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}\cong\mathbb{Z}}}\right].

(66)

Based on these definitions and assumptions, it follows from the
Markov property and the trivialization condition
f_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{X}}

from (

that the time-dependent dynamical map
f_{t}
is invertible for any
time
t\in\mathcal{T}
, with inverse given by

f_{t}^{-1}=f_{-t}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }t\in\mathcal{T}\cong\mathbb{Z}}}\right].

(67)

Markovian dynamical systems of this kind provide a discretization
of many of the kinds of deterministic, time-reversible physical systems
that show up in classical physics.

Letting
\delta t
be the elementary duration of a single time step,
and letting
n\in\mathbb{Z}
denote an integer number of time steps,
the system’s specific state

at the time

be identified as an
N\times 1
column vector with a

in its

th
entry and
0

dynamical map

can be expressed as the

of a fixed
permutation matrix

\Sigma
, meaning
a matrix consisting of only
1
s and
0

in
each row and in each column.

Every permutation matrix is unitary, and is also unistochastic, because
computing the modulus-squares of
1
s and
0
s gives back
1
s
and
0
s. Hence, a discrete Markovian dynamical system defined in
this way is already a unistochastic system, and therefore trivially
satisfies the stochastic-quantum theorem (
65
).

Interestingly, there exists an analytic interpolation of this
discrete-time

unistochastic system that yields a corresponding
continuous-time

unistochastic system, at the cost of introducing nontrivial stochasticity
into the intervals between the discrete time steps. This new unistochastic
system is based on a time-dependent unistochastic matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

whose entries are the modulus-squares of the corresponding entries
of the
N\times N
unitary time-evolution operator defined by
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv\Sigma^{t/\delta t}
,
and therefore satisfies
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right)=\Sigma^{n}

for any integer
n
. One can even go on to define an
N\times N

self-adjoint
Hamiltonian

H
as the infinitesimal generator
of
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
, so there is ultimately a
Schrödinger equation

underlying this system.
9
9

9

To define the unitary matrix
\Sigma^{t/\delta t}
, one
starts by using the fact that every permutation matrix is also unitary
to write the permutation matrix
\Sigma
as
V^{\dagger}DV

for some unitary matrix
V
and some diagonal matrix
D
, where
the entries of
D
are the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
.
Because
\Sigma
is unitary, its eigenvalues are all
phase factors
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\theta_{m}}}\right)
, where
m=1,\dots,N
,
and where each phase
\theta_{m}
is a real number. Setting all the
eigenvalues to the power

, so that they each take the
new form
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i\theta_{m}t/\delta t}}\right)

the matrix
\Sigma^{t/\delta t}\equiv V^{\dagger}D^{t/\delta t}V
,
which is still unitary and now depends analytically on the time parameter

t
. The quantum-theoretic Hamiltonian matrix
H
for this system
then has energy eigenvalues defined by
E_{m}\equiv-\hbar\theta_{m}/\delta t

for
m=1,\dots,N
, and is diagonalized by the same unitary matrix

that diagonalizes
\Sigma

V
The Hilbert-Space Representation

V.1
The Time-Evolution Operator

To commence the proof of the stochastic-quantum theorem (
65
),
one starts with a given generalized stochastic system
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{T},\Gamma,p,\mathcal{A}}}\right)
.
The non-negativity (
42
)
of the system’s transition probabilities,
\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\geq 0,

means that each transition probability can be written as the modulus-squared
of a (non-unique) complex number
\Theta_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
:

\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\Theta_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right|^{2}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

(68)

For each fixed time

, the complex numbers
\Theta_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

collectively form their own
N\times N
matrix, which will be called
the system’s
time-evolution operator

\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\Theta_{11}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)&\Theta_{12}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\\
\Theta_{21}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)&\ddots\\
&&\Theta_{NN}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(69)

(As a concession to terminological conventions, the terms ‘matrix’
and ‘operator’ will be used more-or-less
interchangeably in what follows.)

The normalization condition (
28
)
on the system’s transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

then becomes the summation condition

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\Theta_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)}}\right|^{2}=1\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right],

(70)

which can roughly be regarded as a generalization of a unitarity
constraint. In keeping with the initial condition
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)=\mymathbb{1}

from (
43
), the time-evolution
operator
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)
at the initial time
0
will be
taken to be the
N\times N
identity matrix
\mymathbb{1}
:

\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)\equiv\mymathbb{1}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\ddots\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}.

(71)

V.2
The Hilbert Space

The time-evolution operator
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
acts on an
N

Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
defined as the space
\mathbb{C}^{N}

of
N\times 1
column vectors with complex-valued entries, together
with the standard inner product

for all
v,w\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
:

\mathcal{H}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{N}.

(72)

The standard orthonormal basis

is defined by

e_{1}\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right),\quad\dots,\quad e_{N}\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}}}\right).

(73)

These vectors are labeled as

, where each value of

denotes a configuration in the system’s configuration space

,
so this basis will be called the system’s

.

There exists an associated set of rank-one projectors
P_{1},\dots,P_{N}

defined by

P_{i}\equiv e_{i}e_{i}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{diag}(0,\dots,0,\underset{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}\uparrow\\
i\textrm{th entry}\end{subarray}}}{1},0,\dots,0)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i\in\mathcal{C}}}\right].

(74)

These

form a

;
Mackey:1957qmahs
)
,
meaning that they satisfy the conditions of

P_{i}P_{j}=\delta_{ij}P_{i}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C}}}\right],

(75)

with
\delta_{ij}
again the usual Kronecker delta, and
completeness
,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{i}=\mymathbb{1},

(76)

with
\mymathbb{1}
again the
N\times N
identity matrix.

V.3
The Dictionary

It follows from a short calculation that the relationship
\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=|\Theta_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)|^{2}

in (
68

in terms of a matrix trace as

\displaystyle\boxed{\Gamma_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)=\mathrm{tr}\big(\Theta^{\dagger}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)P_{i}\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)P_{j}\big)}

(77)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textrm{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{C},\,t\in\mathcal{T}}}\right].

This ‘dictionary’ essentially translates
between the theory of generalized stochastic systems, as expressed
by the left-hand side, and a corresponding Hilbert-space representation,
as expressed by the right-hand side. This equation will turn out to
form the core of a
stochastic-quantum correspondence
that
will play a crucial role in the proof of the stochastic-quantum theorem.

V.4
The Density Matrix

Inserting the dictionary (

For any fixed time

V.5
An Aside on ‘Classical Wave Functions’

Pausing for a moment, recall the smooth unistochastic interpolation
of a discrete Markovian dynamical system described in Subsection
IV.3
.
For that system, the unitary time-evolution operator
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)\equiv\Sigma^{t/\delta t}

trivializes to a permutation matrix
\Sigma^{n}
at every
integer time step

. It follows that the system’s density
matrix
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
, as defined in terms of its

from (

reduces to a diagonal matrix
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right)

at each integer time step. Taking the square root of each of its diagonal
entries

and allowing for arbitrary phase factors, one obtains a ‘classical

\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right),\dots,\Psi_{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right)

satisfying

and capturing precisely the same information as the diagonal density
matrix
\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\,\delta t}}\right)
.

This classical wave function is the starting place for a representation
of classical deterministic physics known popularly as the
‘Koopman-von Neumann’ formulation
,
due to its superficial resemblance to work by Koopman
(
Koopman:1931hsatihs
)

and von Neumann
(
vonNeumann:1932zoidkm
;
vonNeumann:1932zzazo
)

in the 1930s. However, as pointed out explicitly in
(
JordanSudarshan:1961dmdoqm
)
,
Koopman and von Neumann were actually using Hilbert spaces to represent
observables, rather than to represent probability distributions. The
formulation of classical physics in terms of ‘classical
wave functions’ is more properly due to Schönberg
(
Schoenberg:1953aosqmttcsmii
)
,
Loinger
(
Loinger:1962ggale
)
, Della Riccia and Wiener
(
DellaRicciaWiener:1966wmicpsbmaqt
)
,
and Sudarshan
(
Sudarshan:1976ibcaqsatmoqo
)
.

V.9
The Corresponding Quantum System

Corresponding to the dilated unistochastic system
(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\Gamma},\tilde{p},\tilde{\mathcal{A}})

is a quantum system based on a Hilbert space
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}

of dimension
\tilde{N}\leq N^{3}
, with linear-unitary time evolution
encoded in the unitary time-evolution operator
\tilde{U}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
.

Unistochastic matrices are not generally orthostochastic, meaning
that they are not guaranteed to be expressible in terms of real orthogonal
matrices. As a consequence, if one is given a generalized stochastic
system whose
N\times N
transition matrix
\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

is
already
unistochastic, then there is no guarantee that the
corresponding
N\times N
unitary time-evolution operator
U\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

can be assumed to be a real orthogonal matrix. The stochastic-quantum
correspondence therefore implies that in order to provide Hilbert-space
representations for the most general kinds of generalized stochastic
systems, the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
will be an important feature
of quantum theory.

Whether in that case or more generally, of course, one is always free
to start with an
N\times N
time-evolution operator
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

in (
68
) whose individual
entries are all real. With that choice, the
\tilde{N}\times\tilde{N}

unitary time-evolution operator
\tilde{U}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

obtained from the Stinespring dilation theorem will likewise be real,
and will therefore be an orthogonal matrix.

However, it is important to keep in mind that from the point of view
of the stochastic-quantum correspondence, a generalized stochastic
system’s Hilbert-space representations are convenient fictions, and
so one is entirely free to assume that they involve the complex numbers
anyway. Assuming that a given choice of Hilbert-space representation
is defined over the complex numbers, rather than merely over the real
numbers, allows one to take advantage of the spectral theorem, eigenvectors
of the time-evolution operator
\tilde{U}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
,
and anti-unitary operators. Assuming appropriate smoothness conditions
in time, one can further make use of a self-adjoint Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)

with real-valued energy eigenvalues, as well as the Schrödinger equation.
To the extent that these mathematical constructs are often taken by
textbooks to be indisputable features of quantum systems, the complex
numbers become an avoidable part of quantum theory.
11
11

11

Even if one assumes that a given choice of Hilbert-space representation
is defined over the complex numbers, one can always double the dimension
of the Hilbert space from
N
to
2N
and represent the imaginary
unit
i\equiv\sqrt{-1}
by the
2\times 2
real matrix
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
, in which case all
N\times N
unitary matrices become
2N\times 2N

real orthogonal matrices. Interestingly, one can then also represent
the complex-conjugation operation
K
needed for anti-unitary operators
as a
2\times 2
real matrix
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}}}\right)
. However, this approach still ultimately preserves the algebraic
structure of the complex numbers in the Hilbert space, in the sense
that they will correspond to a subalgebra of matrices on the overall
Hilbert space that commute with the Hamiltonian and all the system’s
observables. Hence, this approach does not truly eliminate the complex
numbers from the Hilbert-space formalism of quantum theory.

VI
Discussion and Future Work

The unitarily evolving quantum system that lies on the other side
of the stochastic-quantum correspondence is not limited to a
commutative

algebra of observables represented by operators that are diagonal
in the configuration basis.

Indeed, as explained in other work
(
Barandes:2023tsqc
)
, one
can model the quantum measurement process of an observable represented
by an
arbitrary
self-adjoint operator in terms of a unistochastic
system that contains a subject system, a measuring device, and an
environment, all as explicitly defined subsystems. By taking the measuring
device’s allowed configurations to correspond to definite readings
of outcomes, and by taking the overall unistochastic system’s transition
matrix to be based on precisely the type of unitary time-evolution
operator employed in standard textbook treatments of the measurement
process, one inevitably finds that the measuring device ends up in
one of its measurement-reading configurations with a stochastic probability
given by the general form of the
Born rule
. Hence, in principle,
one has access to a quantum system’s entire
noncommutative

algebra of observables.

With the stochastic-quantum correspondence in hand, one can refer
other exotic features of quantum systems back to their associated
generalized stochastic systems to give those features a more physically
transparent interpretation. For example, as shown in
(
Barandes:2023tsqc
)
,

interference
and
entanglement
can be understood as
artifacts of the generic indivisibility of a generalized stochastic
system’s dynamics. (It is also shown in
(
Barandes:2023tsqc
)

how this interpretative framework navigates the various
no-go theorems

that have appeared in the research literature over the past century.)

This overall approach to quantum foundations therefore sheds new light
on some of the strangest features of quantum theory, in addition to
suggesting novel applications of quantum computers. This approach
might even provide a helpful stepping stone for the development of
self-consistent generalizations of quantum theory itself.
