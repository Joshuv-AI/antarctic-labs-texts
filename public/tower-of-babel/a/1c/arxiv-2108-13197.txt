Fast Hyperbolic Wavelet Regression meets ANOVA
Laura Lippert, Daniel Potts, Tino Ullrich

Abstract
We use hyperbolic wavelet regression for the fast reconstruction of high-dimensional functions having only low dimensional variable interactions. Compactly supported periodic Chui-Wang wavelets are used for the tensorized hyperbolic wavelet basis. In a first step we give a self-contained characterization of tensor product Sobolev-Besov spaces on the d d -torus with arbitrary smoothness in terms of the decay of such wavelet coefficients. In the second part we perform and analyze scattered-data approximation using a hyperbolic cross type truncation of the basis expansion for the associated least squares method. The corresponding system matrix is sparse due to the compact support of the wavelets, which leads to a significant acceleration of the matrix vector multiplication. In case of i.i.d. samples we can even bound the approximation error with high probability by loosing only log \log -terms that do not depend on d d compared to the best approximation. In addition, if the function has low effective dimension (i.e. only interactions of few variables), we qualitatively determine the variable interactions and omit ANOVA terms with low variance in a second step in order to increase the accuracy. This allows us to suggest an adapted model for the approximation. Numerical results show the efficiency of the proposed method.



Keywords:
Least squares approximation, random sampling, wavelets, ANOVA decomposition

see also

Note that, in contrast to

with a constant

Our further strategy, finding the unimportant dimension interactions and then building an adapted model,
allows an interpretation of the data. This strategy is based on sensitivity

A similar algorithm for the approximation with Fourier methods is described in
[

Note that in this case one has to use a full

for the
same approximation error, for details see Remark

In this paper we consider multivariate periodic functions

,

, where we identify the torus
\mathbb{T}

. The complement of those subsets is always with respect to

, i.e.,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}=[d]\backslash\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}

Since we will often use vector-notation, the relations

The ANOVA decomposition

The aim of sensitivity analysis is to describe the structure of multivariate periodic functions
f
and analyze the influence of each
variable. A traditional approach is to study the variance, defined in (
2.3
), of
f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})
for
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subseteq[d]
,
to find out which terms contribute how much to the total variance of
f
. A concept used frequently, see for instance
[
6
,
23
,
28
]
, is the following.

Definition 2.1
.

The
ANOVA decomposition
(Analysis of variance) of a function
{f\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}}
is given by

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=f_{\varnothing}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{\{i\}}(x_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j=1}^{d}f_{\{i,j\}}(x_{i},x_{j})+\cdots+f_{[d]}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{P}([d])}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}).

(2.1)

For
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}=\varnothing
the function
f_{\varnothing}
is a constant that is equal to the grand mean

f_{\varnothing}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}.

The one-dimensional terms for
j=1,\ldots,d
, the so-called main effects, can be estimated by the integral

f_{\{j\}}(x_{j})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-1}}(f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})-f_{\varnothing})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j^{c}},

which only depends on
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}
through
x_{j}
, all other components
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j^{c}}
have been integrated out.
The corresponding two-factor interactions are

f_{\{i,j\}}(x_{j},x_{i})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-2}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\{j,i\}^{c}}-f_{\{j\}}(x_{j})-f_{\{i\}}(x_{i})-f_{\varnothing}.

In general, we define the following.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f
be in
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. For a subset
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subseteq[d]
we define the
ANOVA terms
by

f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}-\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subset\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}).

(2.2)

We do not want to attribute anything to
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
that can be explained by
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}
for
strict subsets
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subset\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
, so we subtract the corresponding
f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})
.
By averaging over all other variables not in
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
, we receive functions that depend only on
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
. The definition of
f_{[d]}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})

ensures that the functions defined in Definition
2.2
satisfy (
2.1
).
There are many ways to make a decomposition of the form (
2.1
). Indeed, an
arbitrary choice of
f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
for all
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|<d
can be accommodated by taking
f_{[d]}
to be
f

minus all the other terms. The terms in Definition
2.2
are the unique decomposition (
2.1
),
such that they have additionally mean zero.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\varnothing
the decomposition in Definition
2.2
fulfills

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}=0.

Proof.

The result follows by induction over
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|
by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}-\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}|}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}

\displaystyle=f_{\varnothing}-f_{\varnothing}=0.\qed

In order to show orthogonality of the ANOVA terms, we use the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\varnothing
. Then for the ANOVA terms from Definition
2.2
we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=0

for
j\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|
. For
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}=\{j\}
this follows from Lemma
2.3
.
Now suppose that
\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=0
for
j\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}
whenever
1\leq|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}|\leq r<d
.
Choose
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
with
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|=r+1
and pick
j\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
. To complete the induction, we calculate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}-\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}-\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}

\displaystyle=f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\backslash j}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\backslash j})+\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subset\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{{j\notin\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})-\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{{j\notin\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})=0.\qed

This lemma establishes
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-orthogonality of the ANOVA-terms:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Then the ANOVA-terms from Definition
2.2
are orthogonal, i.e. for
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subseteq[d]

\langle f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\overline{f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}=0.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}
, there either exists
j\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
with
j\notin\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}
, or
j\in v
with
j\notin u
.
Without loss of generality suppose that
j\in u
and
j\notin v
. We integrate
x_{j}
out of
f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\overline{f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\overline{f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j^{c}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}\overline{f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j^{c}}=0,

using Lemma
2.4
for the inner integral.
∎

In order to get a notion of the importance of single terms compared to the entire function, we define the
variance
of a function by

\sigma^{2}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}-f_{\varnothing}^{2}.

(2.3)

The idea of the ANOVA decomposition is to analyze which combinations of the input variables
x_{j}
play a role for
the approximation of
f
, i.e. which ANOVA terms are necessary to approximate the function
f
and which terms can be omitted. The variances
of the ANOVA terms indicate their importance, i.e. if an ANOVA-term has high variance, this term contributes
much to the variance of
f
. For that reason we do the following.
For subsets
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subseteq[d]
with
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\varnothing
the
global sensitivity indices
(gsi)

[
37
]
are then defined as

\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},f):=\frac{\sigma^{2}(f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})}{\sigma^{2}(f)}\in[0,1],

(2.4)

where the variance of the ANOVA term
f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
is

\sigma^{2}(f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}|f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},

since the mean of the ANOVA terms is zero.
The
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-orthogonality of the ANOVA terms implies that the variance of
f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})
for
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-functions
f
can be decomposed as

\sigma^{2}(f)=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subseteq[d]}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\varnothing}}}\sigma^{2}(f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}).

This implies

\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subseteq[d]}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\varnothing}}}\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},f)=1.

The global sensitivity index
\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},f)
represents the proportion of variance of
f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})
explained by the interaction between
the variables indexed by
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
. The knowledge of the indices is very helpful for understanding the influence
of the inputs, but the computation of these relies on the
computation of the integrals of equation (
2.2
). Following
[
16
]
we want to perform
the sensitivity analysis on a function
g
, which approximates
f
.

Remark 2.6
.

Instead of using the conventional Lebesgue measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we can instead use another measure, which
leads to a possible different ANVOVA-decomposition. But
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-orthogonality of the ANOVA terms still
holds. Using for instance the Dirac measure located at a point
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}
, which is a simple evaluation of
f
at
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}
,
yields to the
anchored ANOVA decomposition
, see
[
27
]
.

We want to approximate a function

If the wavelets do not induce an orthonormal basis, but a Riesz-basis for every index

\psi_{j,k}^{*}

\langle\psi_{j,k},\psi_{i,\ell}^{*}\rangle=\delta_{i,j}\delta_{k,\ell}.

f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle f,\psi_{-1,k}^{*}\rangle\psi_{-1,k}+\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle f,\psi_{j,k}^{*}\rangle\psi_{j,k}.

If
\{\psi_{j,k}(x)\}_{k}
is an orthonormal basis for

\psi_{j,k}=\psi_{j,k}^{*}
.
The periodized version of (

L_{2}(\mathbb{T})=V_{0}^{\mathrm{per}}\oplus W_{0}^{\mathrm{per}}\oplus W_{1}^{\mathrm{per}}\oplus\cdots.

Hence, we can decompose every function

f=\langle f,\psi_{-1,0}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle\psi_{-1,0}^{\mathrm{per}}+\sum_{j\geq 0}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}\langle f,\psi^{\mathrm{per}*}_{j,k}\rangle\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{j,k},

(3.4)

since the periodization of
\psi_{j,k}
reduces the parameter
k
to the range
\{0,\ldots 2^{j}-1\}
and the

The periodization inherits indeed the orthogonality in (

and
\tilde{\ell}=\ell-\ell^{\prime}
in

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}},\psi_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle

\displaystyle=2^{\tfrac{j+j^{\prime}}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi(2^{j}x+2^{j}\ell-k)\psi(2^{j^{\prime}}x+2^{j}\ell^{\prime}-k^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2^{\tfrac{j+j^{\prime}}{2}}\sum_{\tilde{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-\tfrac{1}{2}+\ell^{\prime}}^{\tfrac{1}{2}+\ell^{\prime}}\psi_{2^{j}y+2^{j}\ell^{\prime\prime}-k}\psi_{2^{j^{\prime}}y-k^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tilde{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}}2^{\tfrac{j+j^{\prime}}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(2^{j}y+2^{j}\tilde{\ell}-k)\psi(2^{j^{\prime}}y-k^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tilde{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle\psi_{j,-2^{j}\tilde{\ell}+k},\psi_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\rangle=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }j\neq j^{\prime}\\

3.2
Boundedness of wavelet coefficients for mixed regularity

The following results are essentially known and appear in several papers
[
13
,
22
,
36
]

in various different settings. We decided to give a rather simple and elementary proof of the one-sided sharp wavelet
characterization in our periodic setting. We would like to point out that
the vanishing moments of order
m
of these wavelets play a crucial role for the partial characterization which we have in mind.
Our proof can be easily extended to
1<p<\infty
. Note, that the analysis in
[
22
]
relies on proper
Jackson and Bernstein inequalities.

The relevant function spaces are defined in the appendix. In order to analyze a best-approximation error of the function space
V_{j}
, we characterize the
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
-norm of a
function by a sequence-norm of the wavelet-coefficients.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T})
and
\psi
a wavelet, which is compactly supported, see (
P1
), and has vanishing moments of order
m
, see (
P2
). Then there exists a constant
C
, which depends on
m
, such that

\sup_{j\geq-1}2^{jm}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f,\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{m}(\mathbb{T})}.

Proof.

We define the function

\Psi_{m}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{\psi(t)(x-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Note that this function is defined using the non-periodic wavelet function
\psi
, which has compact
support on
[0,S]
. Because of the moment condition (
P2
) and the fact
that
(x-t)^{m-1}
is a polynomial of degree at most
m-1
, we have
\Psi_{m}(x)\rightarrow 0
for
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
.
Hence,
\Psi_{m}(x)
has also support
[0,S]
.
Furthermore,
m
-times differentiation yields

\mathrm{D}^{m}\Psi_{m}(x)=\psi(x).

The periodization of
\Psi_{m}
is

\Psi_{m}^{\mathrm{per}}(x)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\Psi_{m}(x+\ell)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{x+\ell}\frac{\psi(t)(x+\ell-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\Psi_{m}
has compact support, the summation over
\ell
is finite and we can interchange differentiation and summation, which yields

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\,\mathrm{d}x^{m}}\Psi_{m}^{\mathrm{per}}(x)=\psi^{\mathrm{per}}(x).

Analogously, we confirm that

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\,\mathrm{d}x^{m}}\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{x+\ell}\frac{\psi_{j,k}(t)(x+\ell-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)=\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}(x).

(3.7)

Now we calculate the inner products by using variable substitutions

\displaystyle|\langle f,\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{f(x)}\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{f(x)}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\,\mathrm{d}x^{m}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{x+\ell}\frac{\psi_{j,k}(t)(x+\ell-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=2^{j/2}\,2^{-j}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{f^{(m)}(x)}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{2^{j}x+2^{j}\ell-k}\frac{\psi(t^{\prime})(x+\ell-\tfrac{t^{\prime}+k}{2^{j}})^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=2^{j/2}\,2^{-j}\,2^{-j(m-1)}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{f^{(m)}(x)}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{2^{j}x+2^{j}\ell-k}\frac{\psi(t^{\prime})(2^{j}x+2^{j}\ell-k-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=2^{j/2}\,2^{-jm}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{f^{(m)}(x)}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\Psi_{m}(2^{j}x+2^{j}\ell-k)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{j/2}\,2^{-jm}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{I_{\ell,j,k}}\left|\overline{f^{(m)}(x)}\Psi_{m}(2^{j}x+2^{j}\ell-k)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3.8)

where
I_{\ell,j,k}=\supp V(2^{j}\cdot+2^{j}\ell-k)
. Since
\Psi_{m}
is supported on
[0,S]
, the interval

I_{\ell,j,k}
is
[2^{-j}k-\ell,2^{-j}(S+k)-\ell]\cap[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. We denote the union
\cup_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{\ell,j,k}

by
I_{j,k}
. Note that in this proof we put points that are in multiple
I_{\ell,j,k}
,
multiple times in
I_{j,k}
. This is the case if
S>2^{j}
, hence
|I_{j,k}|=S\,2^{-j}
, which can be greater than
1
.

\displaystyle|\langle f,\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{j/2}\,2^{-jm}\int_{I_{j,k}}\left|\overline{f^{(m)}(x)}\Psi_{m}(2^{j}x-k)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq 2^{j/2}\,2^{-jm}\,\left(\int_{I_{j,k}}\left|f^{(m)}(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{I_{j,k}}\left|\Psi_{m}(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{j/2}\,2^{-jm}\,\left(\int_{I_{j,k}}\left|f^{(m)}(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Psi_{m}(x)\,|I_{j,k}|^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-jm}\,S^{1/2}\,\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Psi_{m}(x)\left\|f^{(m)}\right\|_{L_{2}(I_{j,k})}.

(3.9)

Summation over
k
means to unite
I_{j,k}
for all
k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
. The intervals
I_{j,k}
for fixed
j

overlap for different
k
, but at most
\left\lceil S\right\rceil
-times. Hence for
I_{j}=\displaystyle\cup_{k=0}^{2j-1}I_{j,k}
, we have

\int_{I_{j}}|g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq S\int_{\mathbb{T}}|g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
.
All together, this yields

2^{jm}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}|\langle f,\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C\left\lVert\smash{f^{(m)}}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

(3.10)

For the scaling function we have

\displaystyle|\langle f,1_{\mathbb{T}}\rangle|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

(3.11)

Now the assertion follows from (
3.10
) and (
3.11
).
∎

We use this one-dimensional Lemma to give a characterization for multi-dimensional functions.
Instead of the function space
H^{m}(\mathbb{T})
we have to use functions from
H^{m}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\in H^{m}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}
be the periodized, dilated and translated versions
of a wavelet
\psi
, which is compactly supported, see (
P1
), and has vanishing moments of order
m
, see (
P2
).
Then there exists a constant
C
, which depends on
m
and
d
, such that

\sup_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}m}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}|\langle f,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\leq C\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{m}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

where we define the index norm
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}=\sum_{i,j_{i}\geq 0}j_{i}
.

Proof.

We use a multi-variate version of the function
\Psi_{m}
of the proof of Lemma
3.4
, which we get by tensorizing
the one-dimensional functions,
\Psi_{m}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\Psi_{m}(x_{i})
. This function is supported on
[0,S]^{d}
. Furthermore, we have

\mathrm{D}^{(m\cdot\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}})}\Psi_{m}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})=\psi(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}),

where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}
is the
d
-dimensional vector of ones. We also derive multi-dimensional identities like in (
3.7
),
where we tensorize the functions and differentiate in every dimension
m
times.
Let the multi-index
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}
be fixed, with
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}=\{i\in[d]\mid j_{i}\geq 0\}
.
We apply the partial integration in the dimensions
i
, where
j_{i}\geq 0
, i.e. in the dimensions
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
. In these dimensions
we use (
3.7
) with
j=j_{i}
and
x=x_{i}
. Therefore we get

\displaystyle|\langle f,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\overline{f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})}\,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}|}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\overline{f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})}\,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}|}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\overline{f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})}\,\prod_{i=1}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{u_{i}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{u_{i}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(x_{u_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}/2}2^{-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}m}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}|}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\int_{I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}}|(D^{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}_{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})}\,m)}\overline{f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})})\,\Psi_{m}(2^{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}+2^{j_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{l}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})|\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}},

where the vector
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}_{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})}
is the vector which is
1
at the indices
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
and all other entries are zero.
The intervals for integration are given by the support of
\Psi_{m}
, i.e.

I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\supp\Psi_{m}(2^{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}+2^{j_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{l}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})=[2^{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{l}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},2^{-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(S+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}]\subset\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|},

where expressions over multi-indices are always meant component-wise. That means
(2^{-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}})_{i}=2^{-{j_{i}}}k_{i}
.
We denote the union
\cup_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}
by
I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.
Note that in this proof we put points that are in multiple
I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}
, multiple

I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}
. This is the case if

for some

|I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}|=S\,2^{-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}}
, which can be greater than

. Like in (

we use Cauchy-Schwarz-inequality and get

\displaystyle|\langle f,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|\leq C2^{-|j_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}m}\left\lVert\smash{D^{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}_{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})}\,m)}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\otimes\mathbb{T}^{d-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|})},

where the constant depends on
m
and
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|
.
Summation over
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}
means to unite
I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}
for all
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in I_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}

The intervals

\displaystyle 2^{2|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}m}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}|\langle f,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)\leq C\left\lVert\smash{D^{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}_{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})}\,m)}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\leq C\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{m}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2},

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

,
0<s<m
and
\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{j,k}
an

where

Proof.

The first summand for

We will use the equivalent norm given in (

(3.12)

The decomposition of
f

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f,\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f_{j+\ell},\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Let now

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f_{j+\ell},\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\leq\delta_{m}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

(3.13)

Note that at this point we need the property that

form a Riesz basis for fixed

P3
), and every Riesz basis is a frame with the same constants.
Using the Riesz-basis property (

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell>0}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2js}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f_{j+\ell},\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{m}\sum_{\ell>0}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2(j+\ell)s}\,2^{-2\ell s}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{m}\sum_{\ell>0}2^{-\ell s}\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}=\delta_{m}\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-1}\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}.

(3.14)

For the remaining case
\ell\leq 0
we use Lemma
3.4
and (
3.12
), i.e we have

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f_{j+\ell},\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\leq C2^{-2jm}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{m}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C2^{-2jm}2^{2(j+\ell)m}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}=C2^{2\ell m}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})},

(3.15)

where the constant is from Lemma
3.4
and depends on
m
.
This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\leq 0}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2js}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|\langle f_{j+\ell},\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell\leq 0}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2(j+\ell)s}\,2^{-2\ell s}2^{2\ell m}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\leq 0}2^{\ell(m-s)}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{(j+\ell)s}\left\lVert\smash{f_{j+\ell}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\leq 0}2^{\ell(m-s)}\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}=\frac{1}{2^{(m-s)}-1}\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}.

(3.16)

The assertion follows from (
3.2
) and (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.11
.

Let

be the number of parameters, that we need to describe the space

N=2^{n}\left(\frac{n^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}+\mathcal{O}(n^{d-2})\right)=\mathcal{O}(2^{n}\,n^{d-1}).

Proof.

The number of parameters is

N=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}|\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}|=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}}=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{P}([d])}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}}{{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}\leq n}}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}},

(3.23)

where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
always denotes the index set
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}=\{i\in[d]\mid\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{i}\geq 0\}

We consider each summand seperately. Therefore we consider the case where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}}{{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}\leq n}}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=d}^{d+n}2^{i-d}\sum_{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}=i}1=\sum_{i=d}^{d+n}2^{i-d}\binom{i-1}{d-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^{i}\binom{i+d-1}{d-1},

\sum_{i=0}^{n}2^{i}\binom{i+d-1}{d-1}=2^{n}\left(\frac{n^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}+\mathcal{O}(n^{d-2})\right).

Summing over all

gives us

N=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d}{k}2^{n}\left(\frac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}+\mathcal{O}(n^{k-2})\right)=\mathcal{O}(2^{n}\,n^{d-1}).\qed

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}}{{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}>n}}}2^{-2|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}m}

The estimation regarding the number

3.4
Tools from probability theory

In this subsection we collect some basic tools from probability theory, which we will apply later to our concrete settings.
Concentration inequalities describe how much a random variable spreads around the expectation value.
One basic result about the spectral norm of sums of complex rank-
1
-matrices from
[
40
, Theorem 1.1]
is the following.

Theorem 3.16

(Matrix Chernoff)
.

Consider a finite sequence
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
of independent, random, self-adjoint, positive definite matrices,
where the eigenvalues satisfy
\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i})\leq R
almost surely. Define

\mu:=\mu_{\min}\left(\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i})\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\tilde{\mu}:=\mu_{\max}\left(\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i})\right),

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{\min}\left(\sum_{i}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i}\right)\leq(1-\delta)\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\mu/R},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{\max}\left(\sum_{i}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i}\right)\geq(1+\delta)\tilde{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{\tilde{\mu}/R}.

We will use this theorem in Theorem
3.19
to prepare error estimates for the recovery of individual functions.

Another basic inequality which we will use later is the Bernstein inequality, see
[
38
, Theorem 6.12]
.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
B>0
and
\sigma>0
be real numbers and
M\geq 1
be an integer. Furthermore, let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{M}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be independent random variables satisfying
\mathbb{E}\,\xi_{i}=0
,
\left\lVert\smash{\xi_{i}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq B
and
\mathbb{E}\,\xi_{i}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
for all
i=1,\ldots,M
. Then we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\xi_{i}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}\tau}{M}}+\frac{2B\tau}{3M}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\tau},\quad\quad\quad\tau>0.

3.5
Hyperbolic wavelet regression

So far we have bounded the error between a function and the approximation
operator
P_{n}f
, defined in (
3.20
), in Corollary
3.10
. Now we want to consider the case
where we have random sample points
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
with cardinality
|\mathcal{X}|=M
together
with the function values
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}=(f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}))_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}
. In the sequel we always consider the case where the samples
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
are drawn i.i.d. at random according to the uniform Lebesgue measure. For that reason we introduce the
d

\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\otimes_{i=1}^{d}\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}
.
In this scenario we do not have the
wavelet coefficients
\langle f,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per},*}\rangle
at hand.
However, we study least squares solutions of the overdetermined system

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}},

(3.25)

where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}=(\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}))_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}

is the
hyperbolic wavelet matrix
with
M>N
. At some point we will reduce the number of columns of the hyperbolic
wavelet matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
. For that reason we will always denote the number of
parameters, i.e. the number of columns of our wavelet matrix, by
N
.
In order to also minimize the
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-error, we minimize the residual
\left\lVert\smash{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}}\right\rVert_{2}
.
Multiplying the system (
3.25
)
with
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
gives

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}.

If the hyperbolic wavelet matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
has full rank, the unique solution of the least squares problem is

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}=\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}=:\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{+}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}.

Computing these coefficients
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}
gives us the wavelet coefficients of an approximation
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f
to
f
, i.e

S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f:=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}.

(3.26)

To compute this approximant
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f
numerically, we have to ensure that the condition number of the
matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
is bounded away from zero.
In order to apply Theorem
3.16
to our purposes, we use for
i=1,\ldots M
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
the matrices

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\left(\left(\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\right)_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}}}\right)\left(\left(\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\right)_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}}}\right)^{\top}.

Hence, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
. Additionally, these matrices fulfill the conditions
in Theorem
3.16
. Since we will often consider the mass matrix

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}:=\frac{1}{M}\,\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}),

(3.27)

we have a closer look to its structure. In fact, the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
has entries

\langle\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}},\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{i}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle
, that are zero for
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\neq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{i}}

because of the orthogonality of the one-dimensional wavelets for different scales
j
and
i
, see (
3.1
). We denote the entries of the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
for
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}
by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}:

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\langle\psi_{j_{i},0}^{\mathrm{per}},\psi_{j_{i},k_{i}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{j_{i},0}(x_{i})\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{j_{i},k_{i}}(x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Having a closer look at these entries, we see that there are only at most
\left\lceil 2S-1\right\rceil
ones of the
\lambda_{j,k}
non-zero
for every one-dimensional index
j
. Additionally, these non-zero entries are the same for every index
j
. Furthermore, the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
is symmetric.
Since the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
has the entries
\langle\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}},\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{i}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle
,
it has a block structure and every block is dedicated to one index
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}
. Therefore we introduce the partial matrices

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}:=\left(\lambda_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\right)_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{d}\cir\left(\left(\lambda_{j_{i},k_{i}}\right)_{k_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{j_{i}}}\right),

(3.28)

where the circulant matrices
\cir\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
are defined by
(\cir\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}})_{i,j}=y_{i-j+1\mod r}
and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product of matrices.

Having these only few different entries of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
in mind, we can bound the lowest eigenvalue of this matrix away from zero.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
be defined like in (
3.27
). Then we can bound the eigenvalues of this matrix by

\displaystyle\mu_{\min}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{m}^{d},

(3.29)

\displaystyle\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}})

\displaystyle\leq\max\{1,\delta_{m}^{d}\},

(3.30)

where the constants
\gamma_{m}
and
\delta_{m}
are the Riesz bounds from (
P3
).

Proof.

As usual, we begin with the one-dimensional case. As mentioned before this lemma, this matrix has only few non-zero entries. In fact, for
d=1

we have a block-diagonal matrix with blocks belonging to every
j
, see also
3.4
. To be precise, the blocks are circulant matrices

\cir\left((\lambda_{j,k})_{k\in I_{j}}\right)=\cir(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}).

For the case
j=-1
this is only
1
, which is also the eigenvalue. For
j\geq 0
we use
[
32
, Theorem 3.31]
and we write a circulant matrix as

\cir(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j})=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}^{-1}\diag(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}))\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}},

where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}=\left(w_{2^{j}}^{k\ell}\right)_{k,\ell=0}^{2^{j}}
is the Fourier matrix of dimension
2^{j}
with the
primitive
2^{j}
-th roots of unity
w_{2^{j}}:=\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}2^{-j}}
. In order to bound the
eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
, we have to determine the infimum and supremum of all eigenvalues of all blocks
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{j}
.
Since the Fourier matrices are orthogonal, we have

\mu_{\min}(\cir(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{j}))=\min|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j})|,

and analog for the maximum. To bound this term we calculate the Fourier coefficients (see (
1.1
)) of the wavelets
\psi_{j,k}(x)
using
substitution in the integral by

\displaystyle c_{\ell}(\psi_{j,k})

\displaystyle=2^{-\tfrac{j}{2}}w_{2^{j}}^{\ell k}c_{\tfrac{\ell}{2^{j}}}(\psi).

We begin with the case where
j
is big enough, such that
2^{j}>S
, so that
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}=\left(\langle\psi_{j,0},\psi_{j,k}\rangle\right)_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}
. Therefore we get, using Parsevals’ equality,

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{j,0},\psi_{j,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{\ell}(\psi_{j,0})c_{-\ell}(\psi_{j,k})=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}w_{2^{j}}^{-\ell k}2^{-j}c_{\tfrac{\ell}{2^{j}}}(\psi)c_{\tfrac{-\ell}{2^{j}}}(\psi)=\sum_{r=0}^{2^{j}-1}w_{2^{j}}^{-rk}2^{-j}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|c_{\tfrac{r}{2^{j}}+s}(\psi)\right|^{2}.

We denote

E(w^{r}_{2^{j}})=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|c_{\tfrac{r}{2^{j}}+s}(\psi)\right|^{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j}))_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}w_{2^{j}}^{ik}\langle\psi_{j,0},\psi_{j,k}\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{2^{j}-1}w_{2^{j}}^{ik}w_{2^{j}}^{-rk}2^{-j}E(w^{r}_{2^{j}})=E(w^{i}_{2^{j}}).

For the other case, where
2^{j}\leq S
, we get the same estimates in a similar way.
Therefore the eigenvalues of our block of the desired matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
are bounded by

\min_{r}E(w^{r}_{2^{j}})\leq\mu_{\min}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{j})\leq\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{j})\leq\sup_{r}E(w^{r}_{2^{j}}).

These extreme values coincide with the Riesz constants, which can be seen as follows

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{2^{j}-1}d_{j,k}\psi_{j,k}\right\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{2^{j}-1}d_{j,k_{1}}\,d_{j,k_{2}}c_{\ell}(\psi_{j,k_{1}})c_{-\ell}(\psi_{j,k_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{2^{j}-1}d_{j,k_{1}}\,d_{j,k_{2}}w^{\ell(k_{1}-k_{2})}2^{-j}|c_{\tfrac{\ell}{2^{j}}}(\psi)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{2^{j}-1}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=0}^{2^{j}-1}d_{j,k_{1}}\,d_{j,k_{2}}w^{r(k_{1}-k_{2})}2^{-j}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}|c_{\tfrac{r}{2^{j}}+s}(\psi)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{2^{j}-1}\left|\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{d}}}_{r}\right|^{2}\,2^{-j}\,E(w^{r}_{2^{j}}),

where
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{d}}}_{r}
is the
r
-th component of
\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{d}}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{d}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{F}}_{2^{j}}(d_{j,k})_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}.
Taking into account that
\left\lVert\smash{\hat{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{d}}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=2^{j}\left\lVert\smash{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{d}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, the one-dimensional assertion follows.

To generalize this to the multi-dimensional case, we have a closer look at the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
for
d>1
.
Again we have a block diagonal matrix, because of the orthogonality of the wavelets
\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}
for
different scales
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}
. So according to every
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}
, we have a block in the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
.
Because of the tensor product form of our wavelet functions
\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}
, we order the functions

\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}
in the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
such that the block belonging to
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}
is equal to the Kronecker product

\otimes_{i=1}^{d}(\cir\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{j_{i}}).

Since the eigenvalues of the Kronecker product of a matrix are the products of the eigenvalues of the matrices,
we can bound the smallest eigenvalue of every block matrix by
\gamma_{m}^{d}
and the largest eigenvalues by
\delta_{m}^{d}
.
The eigenvalue
\mu=1
is explained by the first block for
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}=-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}
, which is basically
1
.
∎

in the previous proof is in this case the Euler-Frobenius polynomial
\Psi_{2m}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}}\sup_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}|\psi(2^{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})|^{2}

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
drawn i.i.d. and uniformly at random, the wavelet function
\psi
having vanishing moments of order
m
,

r>1

\gamma_{m}

Then the matrix
\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
, where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
is the hyperbolic wavelet matrix from (

only has eigenvalues greater than
\tfrac{\gamma_{m}^{d}}{2}
with probability at least

(3.34)

Especially, we have for the operator norm

\left\lVert\smash{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{+}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{M\,\gamma_{m}^{d}}}.

(3.35)

Proof.

We have that

\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{i})=\frac{1}{M}\left\|\left(\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})\right)_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{R(n)}{M},\quad\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,M.

Hence, we use
R=\frac{R(n)}{M}
,
\mu_{\min}=\gamma_{m}^{d}
and
\delta=\tfrac{1}{2}
in Theorem
3.16
.
Using the bound in (
3.33
) and
N\leq M
, we confirm

\displaystyle R(n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma_{m}^{d}\,M\log\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathrm{e}}{2}}\right)}{(r+1)\log M}=c_{m,d}\,\frac{M}{(r+1)\log M}

\displaystyle R(n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-\gamma_{m}^{d}\,M\log\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}}}\right)}{r\log M+\log N}

\displaystyle\log N+\frac{\gamma_{m}^{d}M}{R(n)}\log\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\mathrm{e}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-r\log M

\displaystyle N\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma_{m}^{d}M}{R(n)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M^{r}}.

Therefore, it follows that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{\min}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\leq\frac{M\,\gamma_{m}^{d}}{2}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{M^{r}}
.
Hence,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
has singular values at least
\sqrt{\frac{2\,\gamma_{m}^{d}}{M}}
with high probability. This yields an upper bound for

by using Proposition 3.1 in

3.35

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
M
be the number of samples satisfying (
3.36
),
(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})_{i=1}^{M}
drawn i.i.d. and uniformly at random,
r>1
and
f\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
a continuous function. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\smash{f-S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\leq e_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{2}{\gamma_{m}^{d}}\,\left(e_{2}^{2}+e_{2}e_{\infty}\sqrt{\tfrac{r\,\log M}{M}}+e_{\infty}^{2}\tfrac{r\,\log M}{M}\right)\right)\geq 1-2\,M^{-r},

where we define
e_{2}:=\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
and
e_{\infty}:=\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
.
That means, the
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-error of our approximation can be bounded with high probability by rates of the
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-and the
L_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-error of the projection
P_{n}
.

Proof.

Using the orthogonality of
\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}
and
\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{i}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{\mathrm{per}}
for
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\neq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{i}}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\smash{f-S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle=e_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\smash{P_{n}f-S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=e_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\smash{S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}(P_{n}f-f)}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\smash{S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert\smash{P_{n}f-f}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}.

(3.37)

We apply Theorem
3.19
to bound the operator norm
\left\lVert\smash{S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{2}
.
For the
\ell_{2}
-norm we give a bound with high probability by using Bernstein inequality. Therefore we introduce the random variables

\xi_{i}=|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{2}-e_{2}^{2}=\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\eta_{i}),

where
\eta_{i}=|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{2}
. These random variables are centered, i.e.
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{i})=0
. The variances of these random
variables can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\xi_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\eta_{i}^{2})-\mathbb{E}(\eta_{i})^{2}=\int|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\left(\int|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\int|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\left(\int|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}-\int|f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\right)\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=e_{2}^{2}\,e_{\infty}^{2}.

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\smash{\xi_{i}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert\smash{\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{i})}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sup_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left||f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}-\left\lVert\smash{f-P_{n}f}\right\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right|\leq e_{\infty}^{2}.

For the last estimation we used that for positive
y_{1},y_{2}
it holds
|y_{1}-y_{2}|\leq\max\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
.

Now we are in the position to merge all inequalities in order to apply Bernstein’s inequality from Theorem
3.17
. Using
\tau=r\log M
, this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\xi_{i}\geq\sqrt{\tfrac{2\,e_{\infty}^{2}e_{2}^{2}r\log M}{M}}+\tfrac{2\,e_{\infty}^{2}r\log M}{3M}\right)

\displaystyle\leq M^{-r}.

Because of our choice for the random variables
\xi_{i}
, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{M}\xi_{i}+e_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\eta_{i}=\left\lVert\smash{f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}.

Hence, we add the mean
e_{2}^{2}
and get

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\smash{P_{n}f-f}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\leq M\left(e_{2}^{2}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2\,e_{\infty}^{2}e_{2}^{2}r\log M}{M}}+\tfrac{2\,e_{\infty}^{2}r\log M}{3M}\right)\right)\geq 1-M^{-r}.

(3.38)

The terms in (
3.5
) are bounded with high probability. Let us define the events

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathcal{X}\left|\left\lVert\smash{f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-P_{n}f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}(\mathcal{X})}^{2}\leq M\left(e_{2}^{2}+\sqrt{\tfrac{2\,e_{\infty}^{2}e_{2}^{2}r\log M}{M}}+\tfrac{2\,e_{\infty}^{2}r\log M}{3M}\right)\right.\right\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathcal{X}\left|\left\lVert\smash{S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{M\gamma_{m}^{d}}}\right.\right\}.

Due to (
3.38
) and Theorem
3.19
we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A)>1-M^{-r}\text{ and }\mathbb{P}(B)>1-M^{-r},

which implies that

\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}(A^{c})-\mathbb{P}(B^{c})\geq 1-2M^{-r}.

This gives us the assertion.
∎

Theorem 3.21
.

Let
M
be the number of samples satisfying (
3.36

(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})_{i=1}^{M}

This proof is similar to the proof of the previous theorem. Triangle inequality gives

We denote the function
g=P_{n}f-S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}\langle g,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per},*}\rangle\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}

\displaystyle|g(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}|\langle g,\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per},*}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}|\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{m}^{d/2}\left\lVert\smash{P_{n}f-S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\sqrt{R(n)}.

The condition (
3.33

and the estimation of

follows the same lines as the proof of Theorem
3.20

4
Computing the ANOVA decomposition

The hyperbolic wavelet regression in Algorithm
1
already reduces the curse of dimensionality because of the
hyperbolic structure of our index set. This is reflected in the number of parameters of the wavelet spaces
N=\mathcal{O}(2^{n}n^{d})
.
But we still have the dimension
d
in the exponent, which grows fast for high dimensions
d
. Therefore we want to
reduce the number of necessary parameters further by taking into account which variable interactions play a role for describing the function.

In this section we calculate the global sensitivity indices
\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f)
defined in (
2.4
) for the approximated functions

S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f
, which we introduced in the last section. Knowing these indices
\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f)
, we can reduce the number of parameters by
omitting the ANOVA terms which do not play a role in describing the variance of a function
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Solving the least-squares problem

\min_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\left\lVert\smash{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{y}}}\right\rVert_{2},

where the hyperbolic wavelet matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
has the form

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}=(\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}))_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}}}
,
leads to a coefficient vector
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}})_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}
, which describes the approximant by

g=S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}.

We calculate the global sensitivity indices
\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},g)

in terms of the coefficient vector
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be a function in the periodic wavelet space written as

g(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}).

(4.1)

For a function
f\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
let
g
be the projection
g=P_{n}f
defined in (
3.20
)
or the approximation
g=S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f
from (
3.26
). In these cases the sum for the index
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}
reduces to
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
.
Furthermore, let
g
be decomposed in ANOVA terms
g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
as in Definition
2.2
. For these terms yields

g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}),

where we define the notion for up-sampling
\uparrow\colon\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given by

(\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})_{i}=\begin{cases}j_{i}&\text{ if }i\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},\\
-1&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then the variances
\sigma^{2}(g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})
of these ANOVA terms for
\varnothing\neq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subseteq[d]
are given by

\sigma^{2}(g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}},

(4.2)

where the matrices
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}
are defined in (
3.28
) and we denote the vectors

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}=\left(a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right)_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}
.

Proof.

We use Definition
2.2
to determine the ANOVA terms. We take into account that the wavelets have the property that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi_{j,k}^{\mathrm{per}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{j,-1}.

We use induction over
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|
and begin with

\displaystyle g_{\varnothing}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}=a_{-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}.

The induction step reads

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d-|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}a_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}-\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}\subset\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}(x_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})-\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{v}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}).

For determining the variances of these terms, we use the fact that
\langle\psi_{j_{1},k_{1}}^{\mathrm{per}},\psi_{j_{2},k_{2}}^{\mathrm{per}}\rangle=0,\text{ if }j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, see (
3.1
).
Hence,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}x_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\left(\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}}{{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}\leq n}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}a_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}}\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\geq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{0}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}.\qed

This theorem tells us that the description of a function
g\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
in terms of wavelets like in (
4.1
) inherits the ANOVA structure of the function, since we have for every subset
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{P}([d])
,

\langle g(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}),\psi_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per},*}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\rangle=\langle g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}),\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\mathrm{per},*}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\rangle.

Note that there are only few different matrix entries in the matrices
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}
.
Hence, these few entries only have to be precomputed and with (
4.2
) the global sensitivity indices
\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f)
can be computed in a fast way.

4.1
Truncating the ANOVA decomposition

So far we used in Algorithm
1
all ANOVA terms to approximate a function
f\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. The number of ANOVA terms of a
function is equal to
2^{d}
and therefore grows exponentially in the dimension
d
. This reflects the curse of dimensionality
in a certain way and poses a problem for the approximation of a function, even while we use a wavelet decomposition,
which decreases the number of used parameters in comparison to using a full grid approximation. For that reason, we want to truncate
the ANOVA decomposition, i.e., removing certain terms
f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
, and creating certain form of sparsity.

To this end we introduce the notion of effective dimension, see
[
6
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

For
0<\epsilon_{s}\leq 1
the
effective dimension
of
f
, in the
superposition sense
, is the smallest integer
\nu\leq d
, such that

\sum_{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|\leq\nu}\sigma^{2}(f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}})\geq\epsilon_{\nu}\sigma^{2}(f).

This means, we can describe a function with low effective dimension in the superposition sense by only using a low dimensional approximant very well.
For that reason, we introduce the set
U_{\nu}
, where we use all ANOVA terms up to the superposition dimension
\nu
, i.e.

U_{\nu}:=\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in[d]\mid|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|\leq\nu\}.

(4.3)

Furthermore, in
[
34
]
was shown that functions of dominating mixed smoothness have low effective dimension.
There they bounded the truncation error
\left\lVert\smash{\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\notin U_{\nu}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

which we accept in the following by supposing that a function has only dimension interactions up to order
\nu
.

To gain from a low effective dimension, we introduce the following ANOVA inspired Sobolev spaces of dominating mixed derivatives with superposition dimension
\nu

\displaystyle H^{s,\nu}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

\displaystyle=\{f\in H_{\mathrm{mix}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\mid f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}=0\text{ for all }\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\notin U_{\nu}\},

(4.4)

\displaystyle H^{s,U}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

\displaystyle=\{f\in H_{\mathrm{mix}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\mid f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}=0\text{ for all }\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\notin U\}.

(4.5)

For functions in these subspaces of
H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we adapt our algorithm to benefit from the structure of
f
. The first spaces were already introduced in
[
15
]
in terms of Fourier coefficients. But there they used trigonometric polynomials for approximation.

Theorem
4.1
tells us, which coefficients of the wavelets representation of a function in
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

coincide to which ANOVA terms. We truncate the operator
P_{n}
, defined in (
3.20
) to a set

\varnothing\in U\subseteq\mathcal{P}([d])
by

P_{n,U}f:=\langle f,1_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rangle\,1_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}+\sum_{\varnothing\neq\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in U}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\}}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}}}\langle f,\psi_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}*}\rangle\psi_{\uparrow\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}},

where we define analog to (
3.21
) the index-sets

\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\}}:=\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\mid\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\geq-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}^{c}}=-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{1}},|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|_{1}\leq n\}

and
\mathcal{J}_{n}^{U}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in U}\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\}}
.
Note that for the whole power set
U=\mathcal{P}([d])
we obtain the untruncated projection
P_{n}f
from (
3.20
).
In order to truncate the ANOVA decomposition of
f
, we have to know which terms we can omit. If the function
f
has
low superposition dimension
\nu
, we use
U_{\nu}
as truncation index set, where we have to know
\nu
in advance or
we have to make a suitable guess. It turns out that in many real world problems the superposition dimension is low,
see
[
6
,
14
,
27
,
42
,
35
]
.
Therefore we have to determine in a first step the variances of the ANOVA terms. Then we omit in the second step these ANOVA terms for which

\rho(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}},S_{n}^{\mathcal{X}}f)<\varepsilon
for some threshold-parameter
\varepsilon
.

Algorithm
1
allows us to restrict our approximation to some index-set
U\subset\mathcal{P}([d])
,
while using the decomposition in Theorem
4.1
. This coincides with deleting columns in the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
,
which belong to ANOVA terms
g_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}
with
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\notin U
. Instead of the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
of the first step we use the reduced matrix

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{U}=(\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}))_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X},\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}^{U}}}{{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}}},

which allows us to increase the maximal level
n
. The whole algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
2
.
One remaining question is, how much samples do we need for the approximation if we only use some columns
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{U}
of
the hyperbolic wavelet matrix. Again, we denote by
N
the number of parameters, i.e.

N=\text{dim }\lin\{\psi_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\mathrm{per}}\mid\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}\}
.
If we do require nothing to the index set
\mathcal{J}
, we have

\sup_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}}\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}\left|\psi^{\mathrm{per}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}})\right|^{2}\lesssim\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}}=N,

which follows by the same arguments as in (
3.5
).
This shows that our theory in Section
3.5
also applies if we choose other index sets than

\mathcal{J}_{n}
as for the hyperbolic wavelet regression. Especially we can also apply Theorem
3.19
.
If we use a number of
N
wavelets for the approximation, we have to use
M\approx N\log N
samples.
Especially, if we choose an index set
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}_{n}^{U_{\nu}}
, this has cardinality
\binom{d}{\nu}2^{n}n^{\nu-1}
. Therefore, we do in a first step the hyperbolic wavelet approximation with the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{U_{\nu}}
. Then we calculate the global sensitivity indices of the resulting approximant. In the second step we omit ANOVA terms with low variances. This reduction allows us to increase the accuracy, i.e. to increase
n
. Algorithm
2
summarizes that approach.

The theory in Section
3.5
suffers from the truncation to low dimensional terms. All proofs can be done in the same way, but
instead of the
d
-dependence we receive the a
\nu
-dependance. The number
N
of necessary
parameters reduces in this case to
N=\mathcal{O}\left(\binom{d}{\nu}2^{n}n^{\nu-1}\right)
. The number of samples has to fulfill

M\geq\frac{c_{\psi}^{\nu}(r+1)}{\gamma_{m}^{\nu}\log((e/2)^{(1/2)})}N\log N=\mathcal{O}\left(\binom{d}{\nu}2^{n}n^{\nu}\right).

Similarly to Corollary
3.22
we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\smash{f-S_{n}^{\mathcal{X},U_{\nu}}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim(1+\tfrac{2}{\gamma_{m}^{\nu}}(r+\sqrt{r}+1))\,2^{-2ns}\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\geq 1-2\,M^{-r},

where we eliminate the
d
-dependance. The approximation operator
S_{n}^{\mathcal{X},U_{\nu}}
is defined in Algorithm
2
. Analog estimates can be done for the
L_{\infty}
-error as well as for the spaces
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}_{2,\infty}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Numerical results

After deriving our theoretical statements in the previous chapters, in this section we now underpin our
findings by several numerical results. We use the Chui-Wang wavelets from Example
3.2
,
which fulfill the properties (
P1
), (
P2
) and (
P3
).
First we give estimates about the computational cost. We begin with a kink
function to illustrate our theorems from Section
3
by using Algorithm
1
.
Our second example shows the benefit of using wavelets with vanishing moments of higher order for function with higher regularity. The example in Section
5.3
of
a high-dimensional function shows that Algorithm
2

5.1
Computational cost

In order to determine the complexity of our algorithms we first calculate the complexity of one matrix multiplication with the hyperbolic wavelet matrix.
For fixed
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
there are at most
\lceil 2m-1\rceil^{d}
non-zero entries in every block of columns in every row. Since

\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\in U}\sum_{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|\leq n}1=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\mathcal{O}(n^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}),

every matrix multiplication with the hyperbolic wavelet matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}_{U}
has complexity

\mathcal{O}(M\,\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\mathcal{O}(n^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}))=\mathcal{O}(2^{n}|U|n^{\max|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|})=\mathcal{O}(M(\log M)^{\max|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}).

The cases where we choose
U=\mathcal{P}([d])
in Algorithm
1
and
U=U_{\nu}
in Algorithm
2
are included in this consideration.
Note that in contrast to the Fourier setting, see
[
34
]
, our index set has a lower cardinality
N
, which gives a better complexity of the algorithm.

The second factor which plays a role is the number of iterations
r^{*}
. The whole algorithm has a complexity of

\mathcal{O}(r^{*}\,M(\log M)^{\max|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}|}).

Theorem
3.19
gives us an estimation about the minimal eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
.
In order to bound the maximal eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
, we can use the same argumentation as in the
proof of Theorem
3.19
together with the bound for the maximal eigenvalues in Theorem
3.16
. This gives us for
r>1

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{\max}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\geq\tfrac{3\,M}{2}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{M^{r}},

if we are in the setting of logarithmic oversampling. Hence, with high probability we can bound the condition number of the matrix
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}
by

\kappa(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}):=\frac{\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})}{\mu_{\min}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{A}})}\leq\frac{3}{\gamma_{m}^{d}}.

Following
[
1
, Example 13.1]
the maximal number of iteration
r^{*}
, to achieve an accuracy of
\varepsilon
, can be bounded by

\displaystyle r^{*}\leq\log\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)\,\left(\log\left(\frac{1+(\gamma_{m}^{d}/3)^{1/4}}{1-(\gamma_{m}^{d}/3)^{1/4}}\right)\right)^{-1}.

As an example, if we aim an accuracy of
\varepsilon=10^{-4}
and we choose as parameters the order of vanishing moments
m=3
and the dimension
d=2
, this bound gives us
r^{*}\leq 85
with high probability.

The calculation of the global sensitivity indices in step
4
of Algorithm
2
does not play role compared to the LSQR-algorithm, since this
can be done in a fast way. There are only few different matrix entries in the matrices
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}
in (
4.2
), so these
few entries can be precomputed. Also, these matrices are sparse circulant matrices of size smaller than
2^{n\nu}
.

5.2
Kink test function

We start with an example of an
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-normalized kink function,

f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sqrt{\frac{98415}{32}}\max\left(\frac{1}{9}-x^{2},0\right)\right)\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{3/2}_{2,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

(5.1)

Appendix A
Besov-Nikolskij-Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness on the
d
-torus

Here we summarize some relevant results from
[
17
, Chapt. 3]
. In particular we give the standard definition of the used function spaces. Let us first define Besov-Nikolskij spaces of mixed smoothness. We
will use the classical definition via mixed moduli of smoothness. Let us
first recall the basic concepts. For univariate functions
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
the

m
-th difference operator
\Delta_{h}^{m}
is defined by

\Delta_{h}^{m}(f,x):=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}f(x+jh)\quad,\quad x\in\mathbb{T},h\in[0,1]\,.

Let
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}
be any subset of
\{1,...,d\}
. For multivariate functions
f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{h}}\in[0,1]^{d}
the mixed
(m,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})
-th difference operator
\Delta_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{h}}}^{m,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}

is defined by

\Delta_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{h}}}^{m,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}:=\ \prod_{i\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\Delta_{h_{i},i}^{m}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Delta_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{h}}}^{m,\varnothing}=\operatorname{Id},

where
\operatorname{Id}f=f
and
\Delta_{h_{i},i}^{m}
is the univariate
operator applied to the
i
-th coordinate of
f
with the other variables kept
fixed.

Definition A.1
.

Let
s>0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Fixing an integer
m>s
, we define the space
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{s}_{p,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
as the set of all
f\in L_{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that for any
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subset\{1,...,d\}

\big\|\Delta_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{h}}}^{m,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(f,\cdot)\big\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\ \leq\ C\,\prod_{i\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|h_{i}|^{s}

for some positive constant
C
and introduce the norm in this space

\|\,f\,\|_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{s}_{p,\infty}}:=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}\subset\{1,...,d\}}\,|\,f\,|_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{s}_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})},

where

|\,f\,|_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{s}_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}})}:=\sup_{0<|h_{i}|\leq 1,\ i\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,\left(\prod_{i\in\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}|h_{i}|^{-s}\right)\,\big\|\,\Delta_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{h}}}^{m,\mathbf{\boldsymbol{u}}}(f,\cdot)\,\big\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\,.

Let us proceed to the related (univariate) Sobolev spaces with smoothness
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

H^{s}_{p}(\mathbb{T}):=\left\{f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{p}(\mathbb{T})}<\infty\right\},

where the norm is defined by

\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}=\sum_{0\leq k\leq s}\left\lVert\smash{\mathrm{D}^{k}f}\right\rVert_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p},

with
\mathrm{D}^{k}f=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\,\mathrm{d}x^{k}}f
.
An equivalent norm for this function space is

\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}=\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}+\left\lVert\smash{\mathrm{D}^{s}f}\right\rVert_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{p}.

In this paper we are mainly interested in the case
p=2
but higher dimensions
d
. We introduce the
so-called Sobolev spaces with dominating mixed derivatives
H^{m}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
in the usual way:

H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=\left\{f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}<\infty\right\},

(A.1)

where the norm is defined by

\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\sum_{0\leq\left\lVert\smash{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq s}\left\lVert\smash{\mathrm{D}^{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}f}\right\rVert_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

(A.2)

with the partial derivatives
\mathrm{D}^{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}f=\tfrac{\partial^{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{d}}}{\partial x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{k_{d}}}
.

It clearly holds for
d=1

H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T})=H^{s}_{2}(\mathbb{T})=:H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\,.

The case
p=2
allows for a straight-forward extension to fractional smoothness parameters.

Definition A.2
.

Let
s>0
. Then we define

H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=\left\{f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}<\infty\right\},

where the norm is defined by

\left\lVert\smash{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|c_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}(f)|^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1+|k_{i}|^{2})^{s},

This norm is equivalent to the norm in (
A.2
) for
s\in\mathbb{N}
, see
[
26
]
. We will consider the case where
s>\tfrac{1}{2}
, since in this case we have that
H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, which is necessary to sample the function.

There is a further useful equivalent norm which is based on a decomposition of
f
in dyadic blocks. We introduce the dyadic blocks

J_{j}=\begin{cases}\{k\in\mathbb{Z}\mid 2^{j-1}\leq|k|<2^{j}\}&\text{ if }j\geq 1,\\
\{0\}&\text{ if }j=0.\end{cases}

For
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we define

J_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}:=J_{j_{1}}\times...\times J_{j_{d}}\,.

if all components belong to
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Using these dyadic blocks, we decompose the Fourier series of the function
f
into

f=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{d}}f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}({\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}})\text{ with }f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}({\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}\in J_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}}c_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\langle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{k}},{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle}

in case that
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}:=0
otherwise. This immediately gives

\|f\|_{H^{s}_{\mathrm{mix}}}^{2}\asymp\sum\limits_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}2^{2|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}s}\|f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

(A.3)

Interestingly, there is also a Fourier analytic characterization of the above defined Besov-Nikolskij spaces
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{s}_{p,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
which even works for
1<p<\infty
. Instead of taking the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the weighted sequence
(2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}s}\|f_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})})_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
we take the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm. We have

\|f\|_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{B}}^{s}_{p,\infty}}\asymp\sup\limits_{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}2^{|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{j}}|_{1}s}\|f_{j}\|_{L_{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\,.
