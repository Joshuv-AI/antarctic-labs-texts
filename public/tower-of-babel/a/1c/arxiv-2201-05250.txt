Consistent Approximations in Composite Optimization
Johannes O. Royset

Abstract




Abstract
.  Approximations of optimization problems arise in computational procedures and sensitivity analysis. The resulting effect on solutions can be significant, with even small approximations of components of a problem translating into large errors in the solutions. We specify conditions under which approximations are well behaved in the sense of minimizers, stationary points, and level-sets and this leads to a framework of consistent approximations. The framework is developed for a broad class of composite problems, which are neither convex nor smooth. We demonstrate the framework using examples from stochastic optimization, neural-network based machine learning, distributionally robust optimization, penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods, interior-point methods, homotopy methods, smoothing methods, extended nonlinear programming, difference-of-convex programming, and multi-objective optimization. An enhanced proximal method illustrates the algorithmic possibilities. A quantitative analysis supplements the development by furnishing rates of convergence.

Keywords
: set-convergence, epi-convergence, graphical convergence, approximation theory.

Introduction

A fundamental approach to optimization is to replace an actual problem by an approximating one, which is then solved using an existing algorithm. In sensitivity analysis, the actual problem of interest is also replaced by approximating ones for the purpose of identifying the effect of perturbations. These situations raise the questions: If some portion of an optimization problem is changed and the resulting problem is solved, would the obtained solution be a reasonable approximation of a solution of the actual problem? How large would the solution error be relative to the magnitude of the initial change? These questions are further complicated by the need for considering both optimal and stationary points in the nonconvex setting. Thus, we aspire to construct approximations that have minimizers
and
stationary points near the corresponding points for the actual problem. We may even seek approximating objective functions and feasible sets that are near those of the actual problem in a broad sense.

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive framework for constructing and analyzing approximations of optimization problems with a composite structure. We provide sufficient conditions for
consistent approximations
, which guarantee that the approximating problems eventually become accurate relative to the actual problem in the sense of minimizers, stationary points, and level-sets. Examples from stochastic optimization, neural-network based machine learning, distributionally robust optimization, penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods, interior-point methods, homotopy methods, smoothing methods, extended nonlinear programming, difference-of-convex programming, and multi-objective optimization demonstrate the framework. The algorithmic possibilities are illustrated by a proximal composite method for solving an array of nonconvex composite problems via a sequence of convex problems. A quantitative analysis supplements the development by furnishing rates of convergence.

We consider the broad class of composite optimization problems in the form

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}~\varphi(x)=\iota_{X}(x)+h\big(F(x)\big),

(1.1)

where the
objective function

\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
is defined in terms of a nonempty closed set

that models

being the

. The actual problem (

Nevertheless, the actual problem may not be solvable directly and often needs approximations. The mapping

may be approximated by a smooth or even an affine mapping. It might be specified by integrals, suprema, or other expressions that necessitate imprecise, numerical evaluation. The function

Our approach is traced back to 1902 when P. Painlevé defined set-convergence. It is now well-known that set-convergence of epigraphs (i.e., epi-convergence) of approximating functions and set-convergence of graphs (i.e., graphical convergence) of approximating set-valued mappings furnish the desired convergence guarantees about the solutions of the corresponding minimization problems and generalized equations; see
[
50

consistent approximations
of the actual problem (
1.1

In the convex setting, epi-convergence of approximating functions

In the nonconvex case, one explicitly needs to impose conditions related to the optimality conditions to guarantee convergence of stationary points and this is recognized by the added requirement about graphical convergence of set-valued mappings in the definition of consistent approximations. If the approximating functions

Consistent Approximations

Parallel to the actual problem (
1.1
), we define the
approximating problems

\bigg\{\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}~\varphi^{\nu}(x)=\iota_{X^{\nu}}(x)+h^{\nu}\big(F^{\nu}(x)\big),~~~~\nu\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\}

We seek to confirm that small discrepancies between the respective components of the approximating and actual problems indeed translate into small errors in the solutions of the approximating problems relative to those of the actual problem. It is clear that pointwise convergence
\varphi^{\nu}(x)\to\varphi(x)
for all

[

We make the following

X,X^{\nu}\mbox{ are nonempty and closed};~~~h,h^{\nu}\mbox{ are proper, lsc, and convex};~~~F,F^{\nu}\mbox{ are lLc.}

is epi-regular at

and

is epi-regular at

for all

. A closer examination shows that (

because

is Clarke regular at

is epi-regular at

which implies the assertion.

In the absence of regularity of the kind invoked in the second part of the proposition, the optimality condition

could be strictly stronger than

. For example, let

,

, and

for

and

Generally, the advantage of the optimality condition

is its explicit form in terms of the “primitives” of the actual problem (

). It is therefore much more computationally accessible than

, which can be checked numerically only in special cases.

Proposition

likewise confirms that

is a necessary optimality condition for an approximating problem under a qualification parallel to (

). We are now in a position to state what it means for the approximating problems, paired with their optimality conditions, to be well-justified surrogates of the actual problem and its optimality condition.

2.2 Definition

(consistent approximations).

The pairs
\{(\varphi^{\nu},S^{\nu}),\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
are
consistent approximations
of
(\varphi,S)
when

\varphi^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pt\varphi~~~~\mbox{ and }~~~~S^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0ptS.

If the graphical convergence is relaxed to merely
\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\subset\gph S
, then
\{(\varphi^{\nu},S^{\nu}),\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
are
weakly consistent approximations
of
(\varphi,S)
.

Under consistency, even in its weak form, we are on solid ground because the approximating problems indeed produce approximating solutions of the actual problem as formalized next using the following notation. For
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\;{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-12.0pt\hbox{\raise 2.5pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\;\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
set of
\delta
-solutions
to the generalized equation
0\in T(x)
is defined as

T^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta)\big)=\bigcup_{v\in\mathbb{B}(0,\delta)}T^{-1}(v)=\bigcup_{v\in\mathbb{B}(0,\delta)}\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~\big|~v\in T(x)\big\}.

Thus, a
\delta
-solution
\hat{x}\in T^{-1}(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta))
“almost” satisfies
0\in T(x)
in the sense that
v\in T(\hat{x})
for some
v
with
\|v\|\leq\delta
. We observe that such near-solutions may depend on the choice of norm
\|\cdot\|
.

2.3 Proposition

(consequences of consistency).
Suppose that
\{(\varphi^{\nu},S^{\nu}),\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
are weakly consistent approximations of
(\varphi,S)
, the tolerances
\varepsilon^{\nu}
and
\delta^{\nu}
vanish, and
\alpha<\beta
. Then, the following hold:

(a)

\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits\big(\varepsilon^{\nu}\mbox{-}\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits\varphi^{\nu}\big)\subset\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits\varphi
, provided that
\dom\varphi\neq\emptyset
.

(b)

\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits\{\varphi^{\nu}\leq\alpha\}\subset\{\varphi\leq\alpha\}\subset\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits\{\varphi^{\nu}\leq\beta\}
.

(c)

\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big)\subset S^{-1}(0)
.

Moreover, if the approximations are consistent (and not merely weakly consistent), then there are
\gamma^{\nu}\to 0
such that
\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(S^{\nu})^{-1}(\mathbb{B}(0,\gamma^{\nu}))\supset S^{-1}(0)
.

Proof.
This is a compilation of well-known facts; see
[
50
, Thm. 7.31]
for (a);
[
50
, Prop. 7.7]
for (b); and
[
50
, Thm. 5.37]
for (c) and the final statement.

Consistency indeed guarantees a comprehensive sense of approximation tailored to the need of minimization problems. From (a) in the proposition, we see that every cluster point of a sequence of near-minimizers of the approximating problems is a minimizer of the actual problem provided that the tolerances vanish. That is, if
x^{\nu}\in\varepsilon^{\nu}\mbox{-}\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits\varphi^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{x}
along a subsequence
N\in{\cal N}_{\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}
, then
\bar{x}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits\varphi
. This is a valuable property, but remains somewhat conceptual in nonconvex settings because there might be no practical algorithm for computing near-minimizers of the approximating problems. Item (b) shows that the lower level-sets of
\varphi^{\nu}
eventually become essentially indistinguishable from those of
\varphi
. For example, if
\varphi^{\nu}(x^{\nu})\leq\alpha
and
x^{\nu}\to\bar{x}
, then
\varphi(\bar{x})\leq\alpha
by the first inclusion in (b). The second inclusion in (b) establishes that any
\bar{x}
with
\varphi(\bar{x})\leq\alpha
can be approached by points
x^{\nu}
with
\varphi^{\nu}(x^{\nu})\leq\beta
. Thus,
\varphi^{\nu}
accurately “predicts” the values of
\varphi
as
\nu\to\infty
.

Item (c) confirms that any cluster point
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})
of a sequence
\{(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu}),\,\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
, with
0\in S^{\nu}(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu})
, satisfies
0\in S(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})
. This holds even if the points
(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu})
are computed with tolerances
\delta^{\nu}
as long as they vanish. The approximating problems and their optimality conditions are often constructed such that computing nearly stationary points is indeed possible in finite time; see Section 3 for examples. Combining the facts from (b) and (c), we summarize: If
N\in{\cal N}_{\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}
and
(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu})\in(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big)\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})
, then

(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})\in S^{-1}(0)~~\mbox{ and }~~\varphi(\bar{x})\leq\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits_{\nu\in N}\varphi^{\nu}(x^{\nu}).

Thus,
\bar{x}
is stationary for the actual problem, with an objective function value at least as good as predicted by the approximations.

These consequences occur under
weak
consistency. Passing to consistency, we achieve enhanced robustness in the sense that every stationary point of the actual problem can be approached by nearly stationary points of the approximating problems. In fact, the proposition guarantees that for some tolerances
\gamma^{\nu}\to 0
, one has
(S^{\nu})^{-1}(\mathbb{B}(0,\gamma^{\nu}))\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle s$}}\hskip 7.0ptS^{-1}(0)
.

The proposition justifies the following broad algorithmic framework for solving the actual problem.

Consistent Approximation Algorithm.

Data.

\delta^{\nu}\geq 0
, with
\delta^{\nu}\to 0
.

Step 0.

Set
\nu=1
.

Step 1.

Minimize

over

until one obtains

Step 2.

Replace

and go to Step 1.

\delta^{\nu}

u^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{u}
,
v^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{v}
, and
w^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{w}
with

(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu},u^{\nu},v^{\nu},w^{\nu})\in\gph S^{\nu}
. Consequently,

u^{\nu}=F^{\nu}(x^{\nu})-z^{\nu},~~~v^{\nu}\in\partial h^{\nu}(z^{\nu})-y^{\nu},~~~w^{\nu}\in\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}^{\nu}\con\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(x^{\nu})+N_{X^{\nu}}(x^{\nu}).

The existence of
w^{\nu}
implies that

because otherwise

would have been an empty set. Since

. Thus,

and we conclude that

, Thm. 12.35]
states that

\partial h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0pt\partial h
. Consequently,

.

The inclusion for
w^{\nu}
implies that there exist

a_{i}^{\nu}\in\con\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(x^{\nu}),~i=1,\dots,m,~~~\mbox{ such that }~~~w^{\nu}-\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}^{\nu}a_{i}^{\nu}\in N_{X^{\nu}}(x^{\nu}).

(2.4)

By Caratheodory’s theorem, there are
\lambda_{ij}^{\nu}\geq 0
and

,
j=0,1,\dots,n
, such that
\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{ij}^{\nu}=1
and
a_{i}^{\nu}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{ij}^{\nu}b_{ij}^{\nu}
. The sequence

is contained in a compact set and thus has a convergent subsequence. By assumption (
2.3
),
\{b_{ij}^{\nu},\nu\in N\}
is bounded with all its cluster points in
\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})
. Consequently, there exist
\bar{\lambda}_{i}=(\bar{\lambda}_{i0},\dots,\bar{\lambda}_{in})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
,
\bar{b}_{i0},\dots,\bar{b}_{in}
, and a subsequence of

, which we also denote by

(\lambda_{i0}^{\nu},\dots,\lambda_{in}^{\nu})\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{\lambda}_{i}\geq 0,~\mbox{ with }~\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{j=0}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{ij}=1,~~~\mbox{ and }~~~b_{ij}^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{b}_{ij}\in\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x}).

This means that

a_{i}^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{a}_{i}=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{j=0}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{ij}\bar{b}_{ij}\in\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x}).

Via Attouch’s theorem
[
50
, Thm. 12.35]
,
\iota_{X^{\nu}}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pt\iota_{X}
implies
N_{X^{\nu}}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0ptN_{X}
because

and

are convex. Using this fact as well as the observations that
y_{i}^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{y}_{i}
and
w^{\nu}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}^{\nu}a_{i}^{\nu}\,{\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-13.0pt\hbox{\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle N$}}\hskip 7.0pt\bar{w}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}\bar{a}_{i}

2.4
) that
\bar{w}-\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}\bar{a}_{i}\in N_{X}(\bar{x})
. Thus, because
\bar{a}_{i}\in\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})
, one has

\bar{w}\in\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})+N_{X}(\bar{x})

and we conclude that
(\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in S(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})
. We have established that
\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\subset\gph S
and this holds without leveraging any of the conditions (a)-(e).

Under the modified assumption that
X^{\nu}=X
, but not necessarily convex, we still have
N_{X^{\nu}}=N_{X}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0ptN_{X}
because
N_{X}
is outer semicontinuous; see
[
50
, Prop. 6.6]
. Thus, the above argument carries over.

If
f_{i}^{\nu}=f_{i}
, then (
2.3
) holds automatically because

is lLc and
\partial f_{i}
is outer semicontinuous; see
[
50
, Prop. 8.7]
.

If

and (c) holds, then
\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits f_{i}^{\nu}(x^{\nu})\leq\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits f_{i}(x^{\nu})=f_{i}(x)
, with the last equality following by continuity of
f_{i}
.

If
f_{i}^{\nu},f_{i}
are convex and
f_{i}^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptf
, then
[

, Prop. 4.18]
establishes that
f_{i}^{\nu}(x^{\nu})\to f_{i}(\bar{x})
if

. Cluster points of the sequence
\{v^{\nu}\in\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(x^{\nu}),\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
must be in
\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})
by Attouch’s theorem; see
[
50
, Thm. 12.35]
. It only remains to show that the sequence is bounded. We recall that

\partial f(x)=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{v}\big\{f^{*}(v)-\langle v,x\rangle\big\}~~~\mbox{ and }~~~\partial f^{\nu}(x)=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{v}\big\{f^{\nu*}(v)-\langle v,x\rangle\big\}

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
; see
[
50
, Prop. 11.3]
. Here,
f^{*}
and
f^{\nu*}
are the conjugates of
f
and
f^{\nu}
, respectively, which are also proper, lsc, and convex by the Fenchel-Moreau theorem 5.23 in
[
56
]
. By Wijsman’s theorem
[
50
, Thm. 11.34]
,
f^{\nu*}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptf^{*}
and then we also have
g^{\nu}=f^{\nu*}-\langle\cdot\,,x^{\nu}\rangle\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptg=f^{*}-\langle\cdot\,,\bar{x}\rangle
as seen from
[
56
, Prop. 4.19(a)]
. Since
\partial f(\bar{x})
is compact by
[
56
, Prop. 2.54]
, there is
\rho\in(0,\infty)
such that
\partial f(\bar{x})=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits g\subset\mathbb{B}(0,\rho/2)
. Let
B=\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)
. Set
\eta=\inf_{\bdry B}g
. If
\eta<\infty
, then
g(v)<\infty
for some
v\in\bdry B
so
[
56
, Thm. 4.9]
applies,
\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\bdry B}g\neq\emptyset
, and
\eta=g(v^{\star})
for all
v^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\bdry B}g
. Since
\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits g\cap\bdry B=\emptyset
,
g(v^{\star})>\inf g
for
v^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\bdry B}g
. Thus, regardless of
\eta
being finite or not, there is
\delta\in(0,\infty)
such that

g(v)\geq\inf g+\delta~~~\forall v\in\bdry B.

(2.5)

Since
\partial f(\bar{x})
is nonempty by
[
56
, Prop. 2.25]
, there is
\bar{v}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits g
. From the definition of epi-convergence,
g^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptg
implies that there are points
w^{\nu}\to\bar{v}
such that
\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})\leq g(\bar{v})
. Thus, there exists
\nu_{0}
such that
g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})\leq\inf g+{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\frac{1}{3}$}}\delta
and
w^{\nu}\in B
for all
\nu\geq\nu_{0}
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that there are
N\in{\cal N}_{\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}
such that
\{v^{\nu}\not\in B,\nu\in N\}
. Since
v^{\nu}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits g^{\nu}
, one has
g^{\nu}(v^{\nu})\leq g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})
for all
\nu\in N
. For
\nu\in N
with
\nu\geq\nu_{0}
, let
z^{\nu}
be the unique point in
\bdry B
on the line segment between
v^{\nu}
and
w^{\nu}
, i.e.,
z^{\nu}=(1-\lambda^{\nu})v^{\nu}+\lambda^{\nu}w^{\nu}
for some
\lambda^{\nu}\in[0,1]
. The convexity inequality (
[
56
, Prop. 1.10]
) applied to

implies that

\displaystyle g^{\nu}(z^{\nu})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda^{\nu})g^{\nu}(v^{\nu})+\lambda^{\nu}g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda^{\nu})g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})+\lambda^{\nu}g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})=g^{\nu}(w^{\nu})\leq\inf g+{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\frac{1}{3}$}}\delta

(2.6)

for
\nu\in N
with
\nu\geq\nu_{0}
. Since
\{z^{\nu},\nu\in N,\nu\geq\nu_{0}\}
is contained in the compact set
\bdry B
, it has a cluster point
\bar{z}\in\bdry B
. After passing to the corresponding subsequence, which we also denote by

, we find that
\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits_{\nu\in N}g^{\nu}(z^{\nu})\geq g(\bar{z})\geq\inf g+\delta
, where the first inequality follows from the fact that
g^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0ptg
and the second one by (
2.5
). Thus, for sufficiently large

,
g^{\nu}(z^{\nu})\geq\inf g+{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\frac{2}{3}$}}\delta
. However, this contradicts (
2
) and we conclude that
v^{\nu}\in B
for all but a finite number of
\nu
.

Under the assumption that
X^{\nu}=X
and
f_{i}^{\nu}=f_{i}
, we show that
\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\supset\gph S
. Let
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in\gph S
. This means that

\bar{u}=F(\bar{x})-\bar{z},~~~\bar{v}\in\partial h(\bar{z})-\bar{y},~~~\bar{w}\in\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})+N_{X}(\bar{x}).

Set
x^{\nu}=\bar{x}
,
y^{\nu}=\bar{y}
, and
w^{\nu}=\bar{w}
. Since
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
, it follows by Attouch’s theorem

[
50
, Thm. 12.35]
that
\partial h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0pt\partial h
and there are
v^{\nu}\to\bar{v}
and
z^{\nu}\to\bar{z}
such that
v^{\nu}+\bar{y}\in\partial h^{\nu}(z^{\nu})
. Let
u^{\nu}=F(\bar{x})-z^{\nu}
. This means that
(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu},u^{\nu},v^{\nu},w^{\nu})\in\gph S^{\nu}\to(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})
. Consequently,
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})
and
\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\supset\gph S
holds. In view of the
earlier results,
S^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0ptS
and we conclude that the approximations are consistent.

For the case with smooth
f_{i}^{\nu},f_{i}
, the claim about (
2.3
) is trivial and it only remains to show
\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\supset\gph S
. Now, we obtain the simplification

\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\con\partial f_{i}(x)=\Big\{\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\nabla f_{i}(x)\Big\}=\big\{\nabla F(x)^{\top}y\big\},

with a similar expression for the approximating functions. Let
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in\gph S
. This means that
\bar{u}=F(\bar{x})-\bar{z}
,
\bar{v}\in\partial h(\bar{z})-\bar{y}
, and
\bar{w}\in\nabla F(\bar{x})^{\top}\bar{y}+N_{X}(\bar{x})
.
In particular,
\bar{x}\in X
. Set
y^{\nu}=\bar{y}
. Since
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
, it follows by Attouch’s theorem
[
50
, Thm. 12.35]
that
\partial h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0pt\partial h
and there are
v^{\nu}\to\bar{v}
and
z^{\nu}\to\bar{z}
such that
v^{\nu}+\bar{y}\in\partial h^{\nu}(z^{\nu})
.

Since
N_{X^{\nu}}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.5pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle g$}}\hskip 7.0ptN_{X}
, as argued above, there’s
(x^{\nu},t^{\nu})\in\gph N_{X^{\nu}}
with
x^{\nu}\in X^{\nu}\to\bar{x}
and
t^{\nu}\to\bar{w}-\nabla F(\bar{x})^{\top}\bar{y}
. Construct
w^{\nu}=t^{\nu}+\nabla F^{\nu}(x^{\nu})^{\top}\bar{y}
. We then have
w^{\nu}\to\bar{w}
and
w^{\nu}-\nabla F^{\nu}(x^{\nu})^{\top}\bar{y}\in N_{X^{\nu}}(x^{\nu})
. Also, construct
u^{\nu}=F^{\nu}(x^{\nu})-z^{\nu}
, which converges to
\bar{u}
.
In summary, we have constructed
(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu},u^{\nu},v^{\nu},w^{\nu})\in\gph S^{\nu}\to(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})
. This means that
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})
and, thus,
\mathop{\rm LimInn}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\supset\gph S
holds.

While we believe weak consistency is the natural goal in most applications, in some cases one might be willing to sacrifice some assurances with the benefit of relaxed assumptions.

2.5 Corollary

(approximations without full epi-convergence).

Suppose that
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
,
X^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle s$}}\hskip 7.0ptX
with these sets being convex, and (
2.3
) holds for
i=1,\dots,m
. Then, for
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and vanishing
\delta^{\nu}
, one has

\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits\{\varphi^{\nu}\leq\alpha\}\subset\{\varphi\leq\alpha\}~~~~~\mbox{ and }~~~~~\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big)\subset S^{-1}(0).

If
X^{\nu}=X
, then the assertions hold without the convexity assumption on
X
.

Proof.
Following the proof of Theorem
2.4
, we see that the present assumption suffices for
\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits\varphi^{\nu}(x^{\nu})

\geq

\varphi(x)
to hold whenever
x^{\nu}\to x
. By
[
50
, Prop. 7.7]
, the assertion about level-sets follows. Again following the proof, we deduce that
\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})\subset\gph S
holds without the conditions (a)-(e) in the theorem and we invoke
[
50
, Thm. 5.37]
to reach the conclusion.

The assumptions of the corollary permit the approximating
\varphi^{\nu}
to be arbitrarily “high” relative to
\varphi
, i.e.,
\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(\epi\varphi^{\nu})
could be a strict subset of
\epi\varphi
. This is a main reason why we avoid calling the approximations consistent in this case. Still, the mild assumptions make the corollary widely applicable.

3
Examples

An array of examples illustrate the breadth of the framework, but numerous possibilities are omitted including those involving polyhedral approximations of
X
as in
[
6
]
. We recall that
X,X^{\nu}
and
f_{i},f_{i}^{\nu}
are nonconvex unless specified otherwise.

3.1 Example

(goal optimization).

For parameters
\alpha_{i}\in[0,\infty)
and
\tau_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in X}\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\max\big\{0,~f_{i}(x)-\tau_{i}\big\},

which aims to lower each
f_{i}(x)
down to the goal of
\tau_{i}
, with
\alpha_{i}
being the per-unit penalty for failing to do so. The problem is of the form (
1.1
) with
h(z)=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\max\{0,z_{i}-\tau_{i}\}
. A possible approximation is to set
X^{\nu}=X
and
F^{\nu}=F
, but replace
h
by

h^{\nu}(z)=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\psi^{\nu}(z_{i}-\tau_{i}),~\mbox{ where }\psi^{\nu}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{\theta^{\nu}}\ln\big(1+\exp(\theta^{\nu}\gamma)\big),~~~\theta^{\nu}\in(0,\infty).

Since
h^{\nu}
is convex and differentiable any number of times, it offers computational benefits over
h
. The resulting approximating problems and their optimality conditions are consistent approximations provided that
\theta^{\nu}\to\infty
. We refer to
[
14
,
12
]
for more general smoothing schemes.

Detail.
By Theorem
2.4
, condition (a), and the refinements, we only need to show
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
. Since
\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{\nu}(\gamma)-\max\{0,\gamma\}|\leq(\ln 2)/\theta^{\nu}
as seen from
[
43
]
, (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) hold.

For an illustration of the optimality conditions, suppose that
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are smooth.
Then,
0\in S^{\nu}(x,y,z)
simplifies to
F(x)=z
,
y=\nabla h^{\nu}(z)
,
-\nabla F(x)^{\top}y\in N_{X}(x)
. In fact, this can be written as
-\nabla F(x)^{\top}\nabla h^{\nu}(F(x))\in N_{X}(x)
, with

\nabla h^{\nu}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\nabla\psi^{\nu}(z_{1}-\tau_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{m}\nabla\psi^{\nu}(z_{m}-\tau_{m})\end{bmatrix}~~~\mbox{ and }~~~~\nabla\psi^{\nu}(\gamma)=\frac{\exp(\theta^{\nu}\gamma)}{1+\exp(\theta^{\nu}\gamma)}.

Similarly, the optimality condition
0\in S(x,y,z)
specializes to
F(x)=z
,
y\in\partial h(z)
,
-\nabla F(x)^{\top}y\in N_{X}(x)
. Since
\partial h(z)=C_{1}\times\cdots\times C_{m}
, where
C_{i}=\{0\}
if
z_{i}<\tau_{i}
,
C_{i}=[0,\alpha_{i}]
if
z_{i}=\tau_{i}
, and
C_{i}=\{\alpha_{i}\}
otherwise, the optimality condition simplifies further to
-\nabla F(x)^{\top}y\in N_{X}(x)
with
y_{i}=0
if
f_{i}(x)<\tau_{i}
,
y_{i}\in[0,\alpha_{i}]
if
f_{i}(x)=\tau_{i}
, and
y_{i}=\alpha_{i}
otherwise. These conditions are necessary for optimality in the actual and approximating problems by Proposition
2.1
; the qualification (
2.2
) holds in each case because
\dom h=\dom h^{\nu}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

3.2 Example

(stochastic optimization).

For probabilities
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\in P
, where
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the set of nonnegative vectors with components summing to one, consider the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in X}\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}f_{i}(x),

which appears in stochastic optimization and machine learning. The problem is of the form (
1.1
) with
h(z)=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}z_{i}
. In practice, the probabilities might not be fully known and this leads to approximating problems with
p
replaced by
p^{\nu}\in P
, i.e.,
h^{\nu}(z)=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{\nu}z_{i}
in (
2.1
). The change may also be part of a sensitivity analysis such as when developing influence functions
[
33
,
4
]
. The approximations are consistent provided that
p^{\nu}\to p
.

Detail.
Since
h^{\nu}(z^{\nu})\to h(z)
whenever
z^{\nu}\to z
, we obtain
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
; see (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Thus, the consistency follows via condition (a) of Theorem
2.4
. The optimality condition
0\in S(x,y,z)
is necessary by Proposition
2.1
because the qualification (
2.2
) holds. In fact, the optimality condition simplifies to
0\in\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}\con\partial f_{i}(x)+N_{X}(x)
because
y=\nabla h(z)=p
.

3.3 Example

(distributionally robust optimization).

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be as in the previous example and let
A
and
A^{\nu}
be nonempty closed subsets of
P
. We consider the problem

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in X}\,\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A}\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}f_{i}(x)

and its approximation obtained by replacing
A
by
A^{\nu}
. The set
A
might consist of a single, true probability vector and be approximated by a set
A^{\nu}
“centered” on a current best estimate of the true probability vector with some “radius” reflecting the uncertainty about this estimate. The resulting problems fit the forms of (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) with
h(z)=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A}\langle p,z\rangle
and
h^{\nu}(z)=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A^{\nu}}\langle p,z\rangle

Detail.
Let
\hat{A}(z)=\mathop{\rm argmax}\nolimits_{p\in A}\langle p,z\rangle
and
\hat{A}^{\nu}(z)=\mathop{\rm argmax}\nolimits_{p\in A^{\nu}}\langle p,z\rangle
. The functions
h
and
h^{\nu}
are convex. They are also real-valued, and thus continuous, because

and
A^{\nu}
are compact. To establish
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
, we argue using (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Suppose that
z^{\nu}\to z
. Let
\bar{p}\in\hat{A}(z)
. Since
A^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle s$}}\hskip 7.0ptA
, there exists
p^{\nu}\in A^{\nu}\to\bar{p}
. This implies that

\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A^{\nu}}\langle p,z^{\nu}\rangle\big)\geq\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits\langle p^{\nu},z^{\nu}\rangle=\langle\bar{p},z\rangle=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A}\langle p,z\rangle.

Thus, (
1.2
) holds for
h
and
h^{\nu}
. For fixed
z
, let
p^{\nu}\in\hat{A}^{\nu}(z)
. Then, for any
\{\bar{p}^{\nu}\in A,\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}

\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A^{\nu}}\langle p,z\rangle=\langle p^{\nu},z\rangle=\langle p^{\nu}-\bar{p}^{\nu},z\rangle+\langle\bar{p}^{\nu},z\rangle\leq\|p^{\nu}-\bar{p}^{\nu}\|_{2}\|z\|_{2}+\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{p\in A}\langle p,z\rangle.

Since there are
\bar{p}^{\nu}\in A
such that
\|p^{\nu}-\bar{p}^{\nu}\|_{2}\to 0
because
A^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle s$}}\hskip 7.0ptA
,
\mathop{\rm limsup}\nolimits h^{\nu}(z)\leq h(z)
. Consequently,
h^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle e$}}\hskip 7.0pth
and we again invoke condition (a) of Theorem
2.4
and the refinements.

3.4 Example

(augmented Lagrangian methods).

With
h(z)=z_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\iota_{\{0\}}(z_{i})

Detail.
The function
h
is not real-valued and we turn to condition (b) of Theorem
2.4
. For
z\in

\dom h

=

\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}^{m-1}
, one has

and the condition holds. This also confirms the limsup-condition (
1.3
) for
h^{\nu}
and
h

\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits h^{\nu}(z^{\nu})\geq z_{1}+\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=2}^{m}\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits(y_{i}^{\nu}z_{i}^{\nu})+{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=2}^{m}\mathop{\rm liminf}\nolimits\big(\theta^{\nu}(z_{i}^{\nu})^{2}\big).

3.5 Example

(min-functions).

For smooth

,

5

Detail.

(3.1)

Moreover, we need to confirm (

3.8 Example

(expectation functions).

Expectation functions arise in stochastic optimization and machine learning and then the component functions

may take the form

Detail.
If there is a set

and

is smooth for all

, then one can attempt to repeat the above arguments with

replaced by the partial derivatives of

and conclude that

whenever

. This would allow us to satisfy the requirement (

). For further details and refinements about approximations of expectation functions and their subgradients, we refer to

]
.

(oracle functions).

Suppose that
h
is real-valued, but not available in an explicit form. If for each

and a subgradient

By

3.10 Example

(homotopy method).

For proper, lsc, and convex
\hat{h}:\mathbb{R}^{m-1}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}

4.1 Theorem

(enhanced proximal composite algorithm).

Suppose that
X^{\nu}\,{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-10.0pt\hbox{\raise 4.0pt\hbox{$\,\scriptstyle s$}}\hskip 7.0ptX
with these sets being convex,

Proof.
The two first claims follow by Corollary
2.5

for all
\nu

for each problem. The outer loop indexed by

in Step 4. The best way to compute such

and

. In Step 5, there exists likewise

by (

(\bar{u}^{k+1},0,\bar{w}^{k+1})\in S^{\nu}(\bar{x}^{k+1},\bar{y}^{k+1},\bar{z}^{k+1})

), say
\bar{x}

5
Inverse Problems in Machine Learning

An inverse problem in machine learning is that of determining an input to a collection of neural networks such that their outputs best match a given quantity
[
28
,
30
]
. Specifically, we are given
s
neural networks represented by the mappings
F_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
,
i=1,\dots,s
. Each network associates an input vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
with an output vector
F_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
. The goal is to determine an input vector
x
such that the output vectors
F_{1}(x),\dots,F_{s}(x)
are optimized in the sense of a convex function
\hat{h}:\mathbb{R}^{sn_{q}}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x\in\hat{X}}~\hat{h}\big(F_{1}(x),\dots,F_{s}(x)\big),

(5.1)

where
\hat{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
is a nonempty closed set that could impose restrictions on the choice of input vector. For some target output vector
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{q}}
and nonnegative weights
p_{1},\dots,p_{s}
, we may simply have

\hat{h}\big(F_{1}(x),\dots,F_{s}(x)\big)=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}\big\|t-F_{i}(x)\big\|_{2}^{2}.

Suppose that the neural networks are of the feed-forward kind producing their output from passing through
q
affine mappings composed with an activation function. For each layer
k=1,\dots,q
, there is a given
n_{k}\times n_{k-1}
-matrix
A_{i,k}
, a given vector

, and

with

where
G_{i,k}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
has
G_{i,k}(y)=(g_{i,1,k}(y_{1}),\dots,g_{i,n_{k},k}(y_{n_{k}}))
for
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n_{k}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
and
g_{i,j,k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are given epi-regular lLc functions. For example, the ReLU function
g_{i,j,k}(\gamma)=\max\{0,\gamma\}
is a common such activation function; it is epi-regular and lLc. Then,
F_{i}=F_{i,q}\circ\dots\circ F_{i,1}
.

It is convenient to express (
5.1
) using additional variables as follows: We think of

as the output of the
k
th layer for neural network
i

,
X=\hat{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{sr}

and

F(x)=\big(x_{1,1,q},\dots,x_{1,s,q},H_{1}(x),\dots,H_{s}(x)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{sn_{q}+sr}~~~\mbox{ and }~~~h(z)=\hat{h}(z_{0})+\iota_{\{0\}^{sr}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{s}),

where, for

and

,

, and

H_{i}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}G_{i,1}\big(A_{i,1}\,x_{0}+b_{i,1}\big)-x_{1,i,1}\\
G_{i,2}\big(A_{i,2}\,x_{1,i,1}+b_{i,2}\big)-x_{1,i,2}\\
\vdots\\
G_{i,q}\big(A_{i,q}\,x_{1,i,q-1}+b_{i,q}\big)-x_{1,i,q}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, the inverse problem (
5.1

1.1
) with

and

is proper, lsc, and convex and

For theoretical and computational reasons, several approximations may arise. Suppose that

,

, convex

,

5.1 Proposition

(weak consistency in inverse machine learning).

In the notation of this section, suppose that for each
(i,j,k)

\begin{cases}\gamma^{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}\to\gamma\\
\alpha^{\nu}\in\partial g_{i,j,k}^{\nu}(\gamma^{\nu})\end{cases}~\Longrightarrow~\begin{cases}g_{i,j,k}^{\nu}(\gamma^{\nu})\to g_{i,j,k}(\gamma)\\

(5.2)

If

,

Next, we turn to the optimality conditions. Suppose that
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})
\in
\mathop{\rm Lim\hskip-0.28436ptOut}\nolimits(\gph S^{\nu})

N\in{\cal N}_{\infty}^{\scriptscriptstyle\#}
and
(x^{\nu},y^{\nu},z^{\nu},u^{\nu},v^{\nu},w^{\nu})

\rightarrow

with

Let
A_{i,j,k}
be the

Rates and Error Estimates

Consistency furnishes guarantees about the limiting behavior of approximations, but it also can be beneficial to quantify the rate of convergence. In this section, we refine results from
[
53
]
and estimate the discrepancy between near-solutions of the optimality condition
0\in S^{\nu}(x,y,z)
and those of
0\in S(x,y,z)
. Chapter 8 of
[
16
]
addresses similar issues using different techniques, especially for problems with affine structure. For error estimates of minimizers, minima, and level-sets, we refer to
[
52
,
53
]
.

The
point-to-set distance
between
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
under norm
\|\cdot\|
is denoted by

\dist(\bar{x},C)=\mathop{\rm inf}\nolimits_{x\in C}\|x-\bar{x}\|~\mbox{ when }~C\neq\emptyset~~\mbox{ and }~\dist(\bar{x},\emptyset)=\infty.

The
excess
of
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
over
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

\exs(C;D)=\begin{cases}\mathop{\rm sup}\nolimits_{x\in C}\dist(x,D)&\mbox{ if }C\neq\emptyset,D\neq\emptyset\\
\infty&\mbox{ if }C\neq\emptyset,D=\emptyset\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We concentrate on a truncated version given by

\exs_{\rho}(C;D)=\exs\big(C\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\rho);D\big)~~\mbox{ for }\rho\in[0,\infty).

All these concepts rely on the choice of norm. The Euclidean norm remains the default, but in the context of
S,S^{\nu}:\mathbb{R}^{n+2m}\;{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\kern-12.0pt\hbox{\raise 2.5pt\hbox{$\rightarrow$}}\;\mathbb{R}^{2m+n}
we adopt the norms given by

\|(x,y,z)\|_{\rm in}=\max\big\{\|x\|_{2},\|y\|_{2},\|z\|_{2}\big\}~~\mbox{ and }~~\|(u,v,w)\|_{\rm out}=\max\big\{\|u\|_{2},\|v\|_{2},\|w\|_{2}\big\}

(6.1)

for the argument and value spaces, respectively, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The graphs of
S,S^{\nu}
are subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n+2m}\times\mathbb{R}^{2m+n}
, for which we adopt the norm given by

\max\big\{\|(x,y,z)\|_{\rm in},\|(u,v,w)\|_{\rm out}\big\}.

(6.2)

Thus,
S^{-1}(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon))
is the set of near-solutions of
0\in S(x,y,z)
with the tolerance now being specified by
\|\cdot\|_{\rm out}
, i.e.,
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})\in S^{-1}(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon))
if and only if
(\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in S(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})
and
\|(\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\|_{\rm out}\leq\varepsilon
.

6.1 Proposition

(solution error in optimality conditions).
Suppose that
0\leq\delta^{\nu}\leq\rho<\infty
and
\varepsilon\geq\delta^{\nu}+\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};~\gph S)
. Then, under the norms (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), one has

\exs_{\rho}\Big(\,(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big);\,~S^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon)\big)\,\Big)\leq\exs_{\rho}\big(\gph S^{\nu};~\gph S\big).

Proof.
A slight modification of the proof of Theorem 5.1 in
[
53
]
yields this fact.

The proposition ensures that if
(\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{z})\in\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)
nearly satisfies the optimality condition
0\in S^{\nu}(x,y,z)
with tolerance
\delta^{\nu}\in[0,\infty)
, i.e.,
\dist(0,S^{\nu}(\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{z}))\leq\delta^{\nu}
, then
(\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{z})
is no further away than
\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};~\gph S)
from a point that nearly satisfies the optimality condition
0\in S(x,y,z)
with tolerance
\varepsilon
. Thus, the rate of convergence of near-solutions of the approximating problems is governed by the rate of decay of
\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};~\gph S)
as
\nu\to\infty
.

In the context of the consistent approximation algorithm, Proposition
6.1
asserts that the solution tolerance
\varepsilon
(for the actual problem) is bounded by the sum of
\delta^{\nu}
(the tolerance adopted when solving the approximating problem) and the error in the approximation as expressed by
\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};\gph S)
. Thus, it would be “optimal” to make
\delta^{\nu}
in the consistent approximation algorithm vanish at the same rate as
\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};\gph S)
tends to zero. Concrete illustrations follow in the examples below.

6.2 Theorem

(estimate of excess).

Suppose that
\rho\in[0,\infty)
and
X=X^{\nu}
. Let

\displaystyle\eta_{0}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\big\|F^{\nu}(x)-F(x)\big\|_{2}~\Big|~x\in X\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)\Big\}

\displaystyle\eta^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\exs\big(\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(x);\con\partial f_{i}(x)\big)~\Big|~x\in X\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\rho),~i=1,\dots,m\Big\}.

If the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2m}\times\mathbb{R}^{2m+n}
is (
6.2
) and the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
\max\{\|z\|_{2},\|v\|_{2}\}
, then one has

\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};~\gph S)\leq\max\big\{\sqrt{m}\rho\eta^{\nu},~\eta^{\nu}_{0}+\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};~\gph\partial h)\big\}.

Proof.
Suppose that
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in\gph S^{\nu}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)
, where the ball is specified by (
6.2
). Then,

\bar{u}=F^{\nu}(\bar{x})-\bar{z},~~~~\bar{v}+\bar{y}\in\partial h^{\nu}(\bar{z}),~~~~\bar{w}\in\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}\con\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(\bar{x})+N_{X}(\bar{x}).

We construct a point
(\bar{x},\bar{y},z,u,v,w)\in\gph S
that is near
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})
in the norm (
6.2
).

Since
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})\in\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)
, one has
\max\{\|\bar{z}\|_{2},\|\bar{v}+\bar{y}\|_{2}\}\leq 2\rho
. Moreover,
\gph\partial h
is nonempty, which follows largely from the definition of subgradients; cf.
[
50
, Corollary 8.10]
. These facts ensure that there are
z,v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
(z,v+\bar{y})\in\gph\partial h
and

\max\Big\{\|z-\bar{z}\|_{2},\big\|(v+\bar{y})-(\bar{v}+\bar{y})\big\|_{2}\Big\}\leq\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};~\gph\partial h).

There are
\bar{a}_{i}\in\con\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(\bar{x})
such that
\bar{w}-\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}\bar{a}_{i}\in N_{X}(\bar{x})
. By Caratheodory’s theorem, there exist
\lambda_{i}^{k}\geq 0
and
a_{i}^{k}\in\partial f_{i}^{\nu}(\bar{x})
,
k=0,1,\dots,n
, such that
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{k}=1
and
\bar{a}_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{k}a_{i}^{k}
. By assumption, there exists
b_{i}^{k}\in\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})
such that
\|a_{i}^{k}-b_{i}^{k}\|_{2}\leq\eta^{\nu}
and this holds for each
i
and
k
. Let
\bar{b}_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{k}b_{i}^{k}
, which then also is in
\con\partial f_{i}(\bar{x})
. We construct

w=\bar{w}+\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{y}_{i}(\bar{b}_{i}-\bar{a}_{i})~~~\mbox{ and }~~~u=F(\bar{x})-z.

It now follows that
(\bar{x},\bar{y},z,u,v,w)\in\gph S
and it remains to compute the distance between this point and
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{w})
. We already have
\max\{\|z-\bar{z}\|_{2},\|v-\bar{v}\|_{2}\}\leq\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};~\gph\partial h)
. Moreover,

\|u-\bar{u}\|_{2}\leq\big\|F^{\nu}(\bar{x})-F(\bar{x})\big\|_{2}+\|z-\bar{z}\|_{2}\leq\eta_{0}^{\nu}+\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};~\gph\partial h).

Finally, one has
\|w-\bar{w}\|_{2}\leq\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}|\bar{y}_{i}|\|\bar{b}_{i}-\bar{a}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}\rho\eta^{\nu}
.

The theorem allows us to bound the solution error associated with a particular approximation in terms of the error in the individual components
h^{\nu}
and
F^{\nu}
. (We refer to
[
53
]
for error estimates involving
X^{\nu}\neq X
in the simplified setting with smooth
F
.) Thus, the focus turns to estimating the component errors. For that purpose, the following fact, which is a direct application of Theorem 5.2 in
[
1
]
, is useful. It leverages the
truncated Hausdorff distance
between the sets
C
and
D
:

d\hat{\kern-1.49994ptl}_{\rho}(C,D)=\max\big\{\exs_{\rho}(C;D);\,\exs_{\rho}(D;C)\big\}.

6.3 Proposition

(approximation of subgradients).

Suppose that the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
is
\max\{\|z\|_{2},|\alpha|\}
, the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
\max\{\|z\|_{2},\|v\|_{2}\}
, and
\rho>\max\{\dist(0,\epi h^{\nu}),\dist(0,\epi h)\}
. Then, there are
\alpha,\bar{\rho}\in[0,\infty)
, dependent on
\rho
, such that

\exs_{\rho}\big(\gph\partial h^{\nu};\,\gph\partial h\big)\leq d\hat{\kern-1.49994ptl}_{\rho}\big(\gph\partial h^{\nu},\,\gph\partial h\big)\leq\alpha\sqrt{d\hat{\kern-1.49994ptl}_{\bar{\rho}}(\epi h^{\nu},\epi h)}\leq\alpha\sqrt{\mathop{\rm sup}\nolimits_{z\in\mathbb{B}(0,\bar{\rho})}\big|h(z)-h^{\nu}(z)\big|}.

The truncated Hausdorff distance between epigraphs can be computed using results from
[
53
]
, with the sup-expression given above being among the crudest possibilities.

6.4 Example

(goal optimization; cont.).

In Example
3.1
, smoothing of
h^{\nu}
causes a solution error

\exs_{\rho}\Big(\,(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big);\,~S^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon)\big)\,\Big)\leq\beta/\sqrt{\theta^{\nu}}

for a constant
\beta
when
0\leq\delta^{\nu}\leq\rho<\infty
,
\varepsilon\geq\delta^{\nu}+\beta/\sqrt{\theta^{\nu}}
, and
\rho>{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\frac{1}{2}$}}\max\{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min\{0,\tau_{i}\}^{2}},\bar{\alpha}/\theta^{\nu}\}
, where
\bar{\alpha}=(\ln 2)(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i})
.

Detail.
Since
\mathop{\rm sup}\nolimits_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}|h^{\nu}(z)-h(z)|\leq\bar{\alpha}/\theta^{\nu}
by Example
3.1
, we obtain via Proposition
6.3
that

\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};\,\gph\partial h)\leq\alpha\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}/\theta^{\nu}}

for some
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
when
\rho
is sufficiently large as indicated. The claim then follows by Theorem
6.2
and Proposition
6.1
.

6.5 Example

(distributionally robust optimization; cont.).

For Example
3.3
, there is a constant
\beta\in[0,\infty)
such that

\exs_{\rho}\Big(\,(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big);\,~S^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon)\big)\,\Big)\leq\beta\sqrt{\alpha^{\nu}}

as long as
0\leq\delta^{\nu}\leq\rho<\infty
and
\varepsilon\geq\delta^{\nu}+\beta\sqrt{\alpha^{\nu}}
, where
\alpha^{\nu}=\max\{\exs(A;A^{\nu}),\exs(A^{\nu};A)\}
.

Detail.
Since
\dist(0,\epi h^{\nu})=\dist(0,\epi h)=0
, it follows by Proposition
6.3
that

d\hat{\kern-1.49994ptl}_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu},\gph\partial h)\leq\alpha\sqrt{\mathop{\rm sup}\nolimits_{z\in\mathbb{B}(0,\bar{\rho})}\big|h(z)-h^{\nu}(z)\big|}

(6.3)

for some
\alpha,\bar{\rho}\in[0,\infty)
. Fix
z\in\mathbb{B}(0,\bar{\rho})
and let
p^{\nu}\in\hat{A}^{\nu}(z)
; see Example
3.3
for notation. Then, there exists
\bar{p}\in A
such that
\|p^{\nu}-\bar{p}\|_{2}\leq\alpha^{\nu}
. Thus,
h^{\nu}(z)-h(z)\leq\langle p^{\nu}-\bar{p},z\rangle\leq\|p^{\nu}-\bar{p}\|_{2}\|z\|_{2}\leq\bar{\rho}\alpha^{\nu}
. We repeat this argument with the roles of
h
and
h^{\nu}
reversed and conclude that
|h(z)-h^{\nu}(z)|\leq\bar{\rho}\alpha^{\nu}
. In combination with (
6.3
), Theorem
6.2
, and Proposition
6.1
, the claim follows.

6.6 Example

(augmented Lagrangian methods; cont.).

For Example
3.4
, we obtain that

\exs_{\rho}\Big(\,(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big);\,~S^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon)\big)\,\Big)\leq\beta^{\nu}/\theta^{\nu},~\mbox{ where }\beta^{\nu}=\big(2\rho+\|y^{\nu}\|_{\infty}\big)\sqrt{m-1},

provided that
0\leq\delta^{\nu}\leq\rho<\infty
and
\varepsilon\geq\delta^{\nu}+\beta^{\nu}/\theta^{\nu}
.

Detail.
In this case,
\gph\partial h=(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}^{m-1})\times(\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-1})
and

\gph\partial h^{\nu}=\big\{(z,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}~\big|~v=(1,y_{2}^{\nu}+\theta^{\nu}z_{2},\dots,y_{m}^{\nu}+\theta^{\nu}z_{m})\big\}.

In view Proposition
6.1
and Theorem
6.2
, it suffices to bound
\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};\gph\partial h)
. Let
(z^{\nu},v^{\nu})\in\gph\partial h^{\nu}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,2\rho)
. We construct
(\bar{z},\bar{v})\in\gph\partial h
by setting
\bar{v}_{1}=1
,
\bar{z}_{1}=z_{1}^{\nu}
,
\bar{z}_{i}=0
,
\bar{v}_{i}=y_{i}^{\nu}+\theta^{\nu}z_{i}^{\nu}
for
i=2,\dots,m
. Since
\|v^{\nu}\|_{2}\leq 2\rho
, one has
|v_{i}^{\nu}|\leq 2\rho
and
|y_{i}^{\nu}+\theta^{\nu}z_{i}^{\nu}|\leq 2\rho
for
i=2,\dots,m
. Moreover,
\|v^{\nu}-\bar{v}\|_{2}^{2}=0
and

\|z^{\nu}-\bar{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=2}^{m}(z_{i}^{\nu}-\bar{z}_{i})^{2}=\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=2}^{m}(z_{i}^{\nu})^{2}\leq\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=2}^{m}\Big(\frac{2\rho+|y_{i}^{\nu}|}{\theta^{\nu}}\Big)^{2}\leq(\beta^{\nu}/\theta^{\nu})^{2}.

Thus, the distance (in the appropriate norm) between
(z^{\nu},v^{\nu})
and
(\bar{z},\bar{v})
is at most
\beta^{\nu}/\theta^{\nu}
.

6.7 Example

(exact penalty methods).

We next approach the problem in Example
3.4
using an exact penalty method. Thus,
h(z)=z_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\iota_{\{0\}}(z_{i})
as before, but for
\theta^{\nu}\in[0,\infty)
we set

h^{\nu}(z)=z_{1}+\mathop{\sum}\nolimits_{i=2}^{m}\theta^{\nu}|z_{i}|.

If
\theta^{\nu}\to\infty
, then the resulting approximations are consistent; the details are omitted. Interestingly,

(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big)\cap\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)\subset S^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon)\big)

provided that
0\leq\delta^{\nu}\leq\rho<\infty
,
\varepsilon\geq\delta^{\nu}
, and
\theta^{\nu}\geq 2\rho
. This means that the approximating problem has a certain “exactness” property: for sufficiently large
\theta^{\nu}
, satisfaction of the optimality condition for the approximation problem locally in
\mathbb{B}(0,\rho)
implies satisfaction of the condition for the actual problem.

Detail.
Example
6.6
furnishes
\gph\partial h
. Moreover,
\partial h^{\nu}(z)=\{1\}\times C_{2}\times\cdots\times C_{m}
, where
C_{i}=\{\theta^{\nu}\}
if
z_{i}>0
,
C_{i}=[-\theta^{\nu},\theta^{\nu}]
if
z_{i}=0
, and
C_{i}=\{-\theta^{\nu}\}
otherwise.

Suppose that
\theta^{\nu}\geq 2\rho
. Let
(z^{\nu},v^{\nu})\in\gph\partial h^{\nu}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,2\rho)
. Then,
v_{1}^{\nu}=1
and
v_{i}^{\nu}\in C_{i}
for
i=2,\dots,m
. Since
\|v^{\nu}\|_{2}\leq 2\rho
, one has
|v_{i}^{\nu}|<2\rho\leq\theta^{\nu}
for
i=2,\dots,m
. This means that
z_{i}^{\nu}=0
for such
i
in view of the definition of
C_{i}
. We conclude that
(z^{\nu},v^{\nu})\in\gph\partial h
and
\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};\,\gph\partial h)=0
. This in turn implies that
\exs_{\rho}(\gph S^{\nu};\,\gph S)=0
by Theorem
6.2
. The claim follows from Proposition
6.1
.

6.8 Example

(homotopy method; cont.).

In Example
3.10
, we obtain for
\lambda^{\nu}\in(0,1)
that

\exs_{\rho}\Big(\,(S^{\nu})^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\delta^{\nu})\big);\,~S^{-1}\big(\mathbb{B}(0,\varepsilon)\big)\,\Big)\leq\beta^{\nu}\lambda^{\nu},\mbox{ where }\beta^{\nu}=\sqrt{1+\frac{4\rho^{2}-(\lambda^{\nu})^{2}}{(1-\lambda^{\nu})^{2}}},

provided that
0\leq\delta^{\nu}\leq\rho<\infty
,
\varepsilon\geq\delta^{\nu}+\beta^{\nu}\lambda^{\nu}
, and
\rho\geq\lambda^{\nu}/2
.

Detail.
In this case,
\partial h(z)=\partial\hat{h}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m-1})\times\{0\}
and
\partial h^{\nu}(z)=(1-\lambda^{\nu})\partial\hat{h}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m-1})\times\{\lambda^{\nu}\}
. To bound
\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};\,\gph\partial h)
, let
(z^{\nu},v^{\nu})\in\gph\partial h^{\nu}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,2\rho)
. Then
v^{\nu}=((1-\lambda^{\nu})\hat{y}^{\nu},\lambda^{\nu})
with
\hat{y}^{\nu}\in\partial\hat{h}(z_{1}^{\nu},\dots,z_{m-1}^{\nu})
. We construct
(\bar{z},\bar{v})\in\gph\partial h
by setting
\bar{v}=(\hat{y}^{\nu},0)
and
\bar{z}=z^{\nu}
. Then, one has
\|z^{\nu}-\bar{z}\|_{2}=0
and
\|v^{\nu}-\bar{v}\|_{2}^{2}=(1+\|\hat{y}^{\nu}\|_{2}^{2})(\lambda^{\nu})^{2}
. Since
\|v^{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}=(1-\lambda^{\nu})^{2}\|\hat{y}^{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}+(\lambda^{\nu})^{2}\leq 4\rho^{2}
, we obtain that
1+\|\hat{y}^{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(\beta^{\nu})^{2}
. From this we conclude that
\exs_{2\rho}(\gph\partial h^{\nu};\,\gph\partial h)\leq\beta^{\nu}\lambda^{\nu}
. The result then follows by Theorem
6.2
and Proposition
6.1
.
