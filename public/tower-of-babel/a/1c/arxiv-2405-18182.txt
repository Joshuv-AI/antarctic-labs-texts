Drawing with Distance
Bart Jacobs ORCID:0000-0002-0740-0336

Abstract
. Drawing (a multiset of) coloured balls from an urn is one of the most basic models in discrete probability theory. Three modes of drawing are commonly distinguished: multinomial (draw-replace), hypergeometric (draw-delete), and Pólya (draw-add). These drawing operations are represented as maps from urns to distributions over multisets of draws. The set of urns is a metric space via the Kantorovich distance. The set of distributions over draws is also a metric space, using Kantorovich-over-Kantorovich. It is shown that these three draw operations are all isometries, that is, they exactly preserve the Kantorovich distances. Further, drawing is studied in the limit, both for large urns and for large draws. First it is shown that, as the urn size increases, the Kantorovich distances go to zero between hypergeometric and multinomial draws, and also between Pólya and multinomial draws. Second, it is shown that, as the drawsize increases, the Kantorovich distance goes to zero (in probability) between an urn and (normalised) multinomial draws from the urn. These results are known, but here, they are formulated in a novel metric manner as limits of Kantorovich distances. We call these two limit results the law of large urns and the law of large draws.

This paper adds two more results about drawing and distance, to which
we refer as the law of large urns and the law of large draws. Recall
that multinomial and Pólya drawing involves removal and addition of
the drawn balls from / to the urn. The effect of such removal /
addition is neglible when the urn is very large, in comparison to the
drawsize, so that one may expect that there is no difference with
multinomial drawing (where the urn remains unchanged). The law of
large urns make these intuitions precise in terms of limits of
distances going to zero:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\lim\limits_{\upsilon\rightarrow\infty}d\Big(\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\upsilon\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)\big)\Big)&=&0&=&\lim\limits_{\upsilon\rightarrow\infty}d\Big(\mathsl{pl}[K]\big(\upsilon\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)\big)\Big).\end{array}

The first equation expresses that the Kantorovich distances
between hypergeometric and multinomial distributions goes to zero as
the urns beccome large. The multinomial distribution acts on urns as
distributions, so that the normalisation operation
\mathsl{Flrn}
must be
inserted, see below for details. Similarly, the second equation makes
precise that Pólya draws from large urns are close to multinomial
draws.

The second law in this paper is what we call the law of large draws.
It can be seen as a variation on the law of large numbers, in terms of
Kantorovich distances. This law takes the form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\omega,\mathsl{Flrn}(-)\big)&\,=&0.\end{array}

Informally it says that (normalisations of) large
multinomial draws are close to the urn
\omega
. This closeness holds
“in probability” as expressed by the validity sign
\models
in the
above formulation. Details can be found in Theorem
23

below.

2.
Preliminaries on multisets and distributions

A
multiset
over a set
X
is a finite formal sum of the form

\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, for elements
x_{i}\in X
and natural
numbers
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
describing the multiplicities of these elements

x_{i}
. We shall write
\mathcal{M}(X)
for the set of such multisets over

X
. A multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
may equivalently be described in
functional form, as a function
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with
finite support:
\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{x\in X\;|\;\varphi(x)\neq 0\}
. Such a function
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
can be
written in ket form as
\sum_{x\in X}\varphi(x)|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. We switch
back-and-forth between the ket and functional form and use the
formulation that best suits a particular situation.

For a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
we write
\|\varphi\|\in\mathbb{N}
for
the
size
of the multiset. It is the total number of elements,
including multiplicities:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\|\varphi\|&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)}\varphi(x)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\varphi(x).\end{array}

For a number
K\in\mathbb{N}
we write
\mathcal{M}[K](X)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X)
for the subset of multisets of size
K
. When the set
X
has

n\geq 1
elements, the number of multisets of size
K
in the set

\mathcal{M}[K](X)
is given by the
multichoose
coefficient:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\displaystyle\left({\kern-2.15277pt}\binom{n}{K}{\kern-2.15277pt}\right)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\binom{n+K-1}{K}&=&\displaystyle\frac{(n+K-1)!}{K!\cdot(n-1)!}.\end{array}

We refer to
[
Jac22c
,
Jac25
]
for details, but we
do recall the analogy that the number of subsets of size
K
of an

n
-element set is given by the ordinary binomial coefficient

\binom{n}{K}=\frac{n!}{K!\cdot(n-K)!}
.

For each set
X
and number
K
there is an ‘accumulation’ map
\mathsl{acc}\colon X^{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}[K](X)
that turns lists into multisets

\mathsl{acc}\big(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\big)\coloneqq 1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{1}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{K}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle

\mathsl{acc}\big(c,b,a,c,a,c)=2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. A standard result (see
[
Jac25
]
) is that
for a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
there are
({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\coloneqq\frac{K!}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width=7.17496pt]{exclamation.png}}{\kern-1.63885pt}}

\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width=8.99994pt]{exclamation.png}}{\kern-1.99997pt}=\prod_{x}\varphi(x)!

all transposition maps
X^{K}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{1.50694pt}{$\scriptstyle\cong$}}}{{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\longrightarrow$}}}}}X^{K}

Multisets

in which

. In writing

Each multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
of non-zero size can be turned into
a distribution via normalisation. This operation is called frequentist
learning, since it involves learning a distribution from a multiset of
data, via counting. Explicitly:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi\|}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

For instance, if we learn from an urn with three red, two
green and five blue balls, we get the probability distribution for
drawing a ball of a particular colour from the urn:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}\Big(3|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)&=&\frac{3}{10}|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{5}|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle.\end{array}

This map

is a natural transformation (but not a map
of monads).

Given two distributions
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and

, we can form their parallel product

, given in functional form as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big(\omega\otimes\rho\big)(x,y)&\coloneqq&\omega(x)\cdot\rho(y).\end{array}

The tensors of multisets and of distributions are related
via frequentist learning:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}\big(\varphi\otimes\psi\big)&=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)\otimes\mathsl{Flrn}(\psi).\end{array}

(7)

Like for multisets, we call a joint distribution

a

of

and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
when

\omega,\rho
are the two marginals of
\tau
, that is, when

\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1})(\tau)=\omega
and
\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})=\rho
. We can
express this also via a decouple map
\mathsl{dcpl}=\langle\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1}),\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})\rangle
as in (
3
).
The tensor
\omega\otimes\rho
is an obvious coupling of the
distributions
\omega
and
\rho
.

An

on a set

is a function of the form

. Such a map

the distribution is often left implicit. The map

will be called a

if it restricts to
non-negative reals
X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Each element

\mathbf{1}_{x}\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, with

\mathbf{1}_{x}(x^{\prime})=1
if
x=x^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{1}_{x}(x^{\prime})=0
if
x\neq x^{\prime}
.

For a distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and an observation

on the same set

we write
\omega\models p
for the
validity (expected value) of

\omega

\sum_{x\in X}\omega(x)\cdot p(x)

and an observation

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega)\models q&\,=&\omega\models(q\mathrel{\circ}f).\end{array}

(8)

This equation is sometimes called ‘the law of the
unconscious statistician’, see

e.g.

[
CB02
, §2.2]
. We shall write
\mathsl{Obs}(X)=\mathbb{R}^{X}
and
\mathsl{Fact}(X)=(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{X}
for the sets of observations and

.

3.
Preliminaries on metric spaces

A metric space will be written as a pair
(X,d_{X})
, where
X
is a
set and
d_{X}\colon X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a distance
function, also called metric. This metric satisfies:

•

separation:
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=0
iff
x=x^{\prime}
;

•

symmetry:
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=d_{X}(x^{\prime},x)
;

•

triangular inequality:
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{X}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
.

Often, we drop the subscript
X
in
d_{X}
if it is clear from
the context. We use the standard distance
d(x,y)=|x-y|
on real
and natural numbers.

{defi}

Let
(X,d_{X})
,
(Y,d_{Y})
be two metric spaces.

(1)

A function
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
is called
short

(or also
non-expansive
) if:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{Y}\big(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\big)&\leq&d_{X}\big(x,x^{\prime}\big),\qquad\mbox{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in X.\end{array}

Such a map is called an
isometry
or an

isometric embedding
if the above inequality
\leq
is an actual
equality
=
. This implies that the function
f
is injective, and
thus an ‘embedding’.

We write
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{S}}
for the category of metric spaces with short
maps between them.

(2)

A function
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
is
Lipschitz
or

M
-
Lipschitz
, if there is a number
M\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{Y}\big(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\big)&\leq&M\cdot d_{X}\big(x,x^{\prime}\big),\qquad\mbox{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in X.\end{array}

The number
M
is sometimes called the
Lipschitz
constant
. Thus, a short function is Lipschitz, with constant
1
.
We write
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{L}}
for the category of metric spaces with Lipschitz
maps between them (with arbitrary Lipschitz constants).

It is easy to see that if
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
is
M
-Lipschitz
and
g\colon Y\rightarrow Z
is
L
-Lipschitz, then the composite
g\mathrel{\circ}f\colon X\rightarrow Z
is Lipschitz with constant
M\cdot L
.

Lemma 1
.

For two metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2})
we equip the
cartesian product
X_{1}\times X_{2}
of sets with the sum of the two
metrics:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\Big((x_{1},x_{2}),(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})\Big)&\coloneqq&d_{1}(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})+d_{2}(x_{2},x^{\prime}_{2}).\end{array}

(9)

We shall extend these product distances to
K
-ary form

X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{K}
and
X^{K}=X\times\cdots\times X
.

(1)

With the usual projections and tuples this forms a product in
the category
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{L}}
. The definition yields a
monoidal

product (tensor) in the category
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{S}}
since the diagonal map
X\rightarrow X\times X
is 2-Lipschitz and not short.

(2)

If
f_{i}\colon X_{i}\rightarrow Y_{i}
is
M_{i}
-Lipschitz,
then
f_{1}\times f_{2}\colon X_{1}\times X_{2}\rightarrow Y_{1}\times Y_{2}

is
\max(M_{1},M_{2})
-Lipschitz.

Products of metric spaces involve some subtleties, which are not
relevant for the main line of the paper, but which we briefly make
explicit as background information.

Remark 2
.

In the above description (
9
) we have used the sum
of the distances in the components. As stated, this formulation yields
a categorical product in the category of metric spaces with Lipschitz
maps, but not with short maps.

Instead of the sum
+
in (
9
) one can use the
maximum. This yields a categorical product in the both the categories

\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{L}}
and
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{S}}
of metric spaces with Lipschitz and with short
maps. The maximum also works when the metric is 1-bounded, that is, of
the form
d\colon X\times X\rightarrow[0,1]
, restricted to the unit
interval
[0,1]
. This restriction is common in program semantics, see
e.g.
[
BV96
,
Bre01
]
. This 1-bounded case has
advantages since it admits infinite products and also coproducts of
metric spaces. We do not need such structure and we will work with
general
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
-valued metrics.

Thus, in the category
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{L}}
with Lipschitz maps one can
equivalently use the maximum or the sum of distances
in (
9
). The resulting products are isomorphic in

\mathbf{Met}_{\mathsl{L}}
. This works since for
r,s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
one has
\max(r,s)\leq r+s
and
r+s\leq 2\cdot\max(r,s)
. Here we use the sum of distances
in the product, since it is used for certain results, like
Lemma
4
(
9
). A concrete example
where the sum of distances is used is for shortness of the addition
function
+\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, in:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{\mathbb{R}}\big(r+s,r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}\big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\big|\,(r+s)-(r^{\prime}+s^{\prime})\,\big|&=&\big|\,(r-r^{\prime})+(s-s^{\prime})\,\big|\\
&\leq&\big|r-r^{\prime}\big|+\big|s-s^{\prime}\big|\\
&=&d_{\mathbb{R}}\big(r,r^{\prime}\big)+d_{\mathbb{R}}\big(s,s^{\prime}\big)\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ProductMetricEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0ptd_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\big((r,s),(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\big).\end{array}

Below we shall use the familiar zip function. It is in fact an
isometry.

Lemma 3
.

For metric spaces
X,Y
and for
K\in\mathbb{N}
consider the zip function

\mathsl{zip}\colon X^{K}\times Y^{K}\rightarrow(X\times Y)^{K}
given
by
\mathsl{zip}\big(\vec{x},\vec{y})=((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{K},y_{K}))
.
This
\mathsl{zip}
function is a bijective function and an isometry.

Similarly, concatenation
\mathbin{+{\kern-2.15277pt}+}\colon X^{K}\times X^{L}\rightarrow X^{K+L}
is a bijection and an isometry.

Proof 3.1
.

We only do the zip case. The zip function is obviously a
bijection. For
\vec{x},\vec{x^{\prime}}\in X^{K}
and
\vec{y},\vec{y^{\prime}}\in Y^{K}
one has via repeated use of (
9
),

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle d_{(X\times Y)^{K}}\Big(\mathsl{zip}(\vec{x},\vec{y}),\mathsl{zip}(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y^{\prime}})\Big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&=&d_{X\times Y}\Big((x_{1},y_{1}),(x^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{1})\Big)+\cdots+d_{X\times Y}\Big((x_{K},y_{K}),(x^{\prime}_{K},y^{\prime}_{K})\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\Big(d_{X}(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})+d_{Y}(y_{1},y^{\prime}_{1})\Big)+\cdots+\Big(d_{X}(x_{K},x^{\prime}_{K})+d_{Y}(y_{K},y^{\prime}_{K})\Big)\\[3.99994pt]
&=&\Big(d_{X}(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})+\cdots+d_{X}(x_{K},x^{\prime}_{K})\Big)+\Big(d_{Y}(y_{1},y^{\prime}_{1})+\cdots+d_{Y}(y_{K},y^{\prime}_{K})\Big)\\[3.99994pt]
&=&d_{X^{K}}\big(\vec{x},\vec{x^{\prime}}\big)+d_{Y^{K}}\big(\vec{y},\vec{y^{\prime}}\big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptd_{X^{K}\times Y^{K}}\big((\vec{x},\vec{y}),(\vec{x^{\prime}},\vec{y^{\prime}})\big).\end{array}

∎

4.
The Kantorovich distance between distributions

This section introduces the Kantorovich distance between probability
distributions and recalls some basic results. There are several
equivalent formulations for this distance. We express them in terms
of validity and couplings, see also
e.g.
[
Bre01
,
Bre18
,
DD09
,
FP19
,
DGJP04
]
.

{defi}

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space. The
Kantorovich
metric
d=d_{\mathcal{D}(X)}\colon\mathcal{D}(X)\times\mathcal{D}(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on
distributions over
X
is defined by any of the three equivalent
formulas:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big)&\,\coloneqq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,\,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X),\,p\oplus p^{\prime}\,\leq\,d_{X}}\,\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\bigvee_{q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|.\end{array}

(10)

This turns
\mathcal{D}(X)
into a metric space. The operation

\oplus
in the second formulation is defined as
(p\oplus p^{\prime})(x,x^{\prime})=p(x)+p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
. The set
\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)\subseteq\mathsl{Fact}(X)
in the third
formulation is the subset of short factors
X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. To be
precise, we should write
\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X,d_{X})
since the distance
d_{X}

on
X
is a parameter, but we leave it implicit for convenience. The
meet
\bigwedge
and joins
\bigvee
in (
10
) are
actually reached, by what are called the
optimal
coupling and
the
optimal
observations / factor.

The equivalence of the first and second formulation
in (
10
) is an instance of strong duality in linear
programming, which can be obtained via Farkas’ Lemma, see
e.g.
[
MG06
]
. The second formulation is commonly
associated with Monge. The single factor
q
in the third formulation
can be obtained from the two observations
p,p^{\prime}
in the second
formulation, and vice-versa. In
[
DGJP04
]
it is shown
that instead of using all short factors
q
in the third formulation
one can restrict to those factors that arise as interpretation of
formulas in a logic — since these interpretations are dense. What
we call the Kantorovich distance is also called the
Wasserstein-Kantorovich, or Monge-Kantorovich distance.

A proof of the equivalence of the three formulations for the
Kantorovich distance
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
between two distributions

\omega,\omega^{\prime}
in (
10
) is given in the
appendix. These three formulations do not immediately suggest how to
calculate distances. What helps is that the minimum and maxima are
actually reached and can be computed. This is done via linear
programming, originally introduced by Kantorovich,
see
[
MG06
,
Vil09
,
DD09
]
. In the sequel, we shall see
several examples of distances between distributions. They are obtained
via our (adapted) Python implementation of the linear optimisation,
which also produces an optimal coupling, observations or factor. This
implementation is used only for illustrations.

{exa}

Consider the set
X
containing the first eight natural numbers, so
X=\{0,1,\ldots,7\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, with the usual distance, written
as
d_{X}
, between natural numbers:
d_{X}(n,m)=|n-m|
. We look at
the following two distributions on
X
.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\omega&=&\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}4{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle&&\omega^{\prime}&=&\frac{1}{8}|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{8}|{\kern 1.00006pt}3{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{8}|{\kern 1.00006pt}6{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{5}{8}|{\kern 1.00006pt}7{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle.\end{array}

We claim that the Kantorovich distance
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

is
\frac{15}{4}
. This will be illustrated for each of the three
formulations in Definition
4
.

•

An optimal coupling
\tau\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau&=&\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,2{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,3{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,6{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,7{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}4,7{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

It is not hard to see that
\tau
’s first marginal is
\omega
,
and its second marginal is
\omega^{\prime}
. We compute the distances as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\tau\models d_{X}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(0,2)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(0,3)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(0,6)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(0,7)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot d_{X}(4,7)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\frac{2}{8}+\frac{3}{8}+\frac{6}{8}+\frac{7}{8}+\frac{3}{2}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{18}{8}+\frac{3}{2}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{9}{4}+\frac{6}{4}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{15}{4}.\end{array}

•

There are the following two optimal observations
p,p^{\prime}\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, described as sums of weighted point predicates:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}p&=&-1\cdot\mathbf{1}_{1}-2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{2}-3\cdot\mathbf{1}_{3}-4\cdot\mathbf{1}_{4}-5\cdot\mathbf{1}_{5}-6\cdot\mathbf{1}_{6}-7\cdot\mathbf{1}_{7}\\
p^{\prime}&=&1\cdot\mathbf{1}_{1}+2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{2}+3\cdot\mathbf{1}_{3}+4\cdot\mathbf{1}_{4}+5\cdot\mathbf{1}_{5}+6\cdot\mathbf{1}_{6}+7\cdot\mathbf{1}_{7}.\end{array}

It is not hard to see that
(p\oplus p^{\prime})(i,j)\coloneqq p(i)+p^{\prime}(j)\leq d_{X}(i,j)
holds for all
i,j\in X
. Using the second
formulation in (
10
) we get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big(\omega\models p\big)+\big(\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}\big)&=&\frac{1}{2}\cdot p(0)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot p(4)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot p^{\prime}(2)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot p^{\prime}(3)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot p^{\prime}(6)+\frac{5}{8}\cdot p^{\prime}(7)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\frac{-4}{2}+\frac{2}{8}+\frac{3}{8}+\frac{6}{8}+\frac{35}{8}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt-2+\frac{46}{8}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{30}{8}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{15}{4}.\end{array}

•

Finally, there is a (single) short factor
q\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
given by:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}q&=&7\cdot\mathbf{1}_{0}+6\cdot\mathbf{1}_{1}+5\cdot\mathbf{1}_{2}+4\cdot\mathbf{1}_{3}+3\cdot\mathbf{1}_{4}+2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{5}+1\cdot\mathbf{1}_{6}.\end{array}

Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big(\omega\models q\big)-\big(\omega^{\prime}\models q\big)&=&\frac{1}{2}\cdot q(0)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot q(4)-\Big(\frac{1}{8}\cdot q(2)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot q(3)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot q(6)+\frac{5}{8}\cdot q(7)\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\frac{7}{2}+\frac{3}{2}-\Big(\frac{5}{8}+\frac{4}{8}+\frac{1}{8}\Big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{10}{2}-\frac{10}{8}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{20}{4}-\frac{5}{4}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{15}{4}.\end{array}

From the fact that the coupling
\tau
, the two observations

p,p^{\prime}
, and the single factor
q
produce the same distance one can
deduce that they are optimal, using the formula (
10
).

distance on distributions.

For distributions

one has:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\Big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega_{i},\,\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega^{\prime}_{i}\Big)&\leq&\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot d\big(\omega_{i},\omega^{\prime}_{i}\big).\end{array}

(9)

The permutation channel
\mathsl{prm}\colon X^{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(X^{K})

Proof 4.1
.

(1)

Let
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
be
M
-Lipschitz. If

\tau\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
is a coupling of

\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
, then
\mathcal{D}(f\times f)(\tau)\in\mathcal{D}(Y\times Y)
is a coupling of
\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega),\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
. Thus:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{\mathcal{D}(Y)}\Big(\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega),\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime})\Big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DstMetricEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega),\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime}))}\sigma\models d_{Y}\\[11.99998pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\mathcal{D}(f\times f)(\tau)\models d_{Y}\\[11.99998pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{TransformationValidityEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{Y}\mathrel{\circ}(f\times f)\\[11.99998pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models M\cdot d_{X}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle M\cdot\left(\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}\right)\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DstMetricEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0ptM\cdot d_{\mathcal{D}(X)}\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

(2)

Let
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
be an isometry. Then it is

1
-Lipschitz, so
\mathcal{D}(f)
is as well, by the previous item. It thus
suffices to prove:
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\leq d\big(\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega),\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime})\big)
, for

\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
. Let
p\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be short.
We turn it into a short factor
q\colon Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
via the
definition:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}q(y)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{x\in X}\,p(x)+d_{Y}(f(x),y)&\qquad\mbox{satisfying, by construction,}&q\mathrel{\circ}f&=&p.\end{array}

This map
q
is short, since for arbitrary
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big|\,q(y)-q(y^{\prime})\,\big|&=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\,\big|\,\big(p(x)+d_{Y}(f(x),y)\big)-\big(p(x^{\prime})+d_{Y}(f(x^{\prime}),y^{\prime})\big)\,\big|\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{x\in X}\,\big|\,p(x)+d_{Y}(f(x),y)-p(x)-d_{Y}(f(x),y^{\prime})\big)\,\big|\\
&=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{x\in X}\,\big|\,d_{Y}(f(x),y)-d_{Y}(f(x),y^{\prime})\big)\,\big|\hskip 2.0pt\leq\hskip 2.0ptd_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}).\end{array}

The latter inequality — sometimes called the reverse triangle
inequality — follows easily from the triangular inequality.

Using
q\mathrel{\circ}f=p
we get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big|\,\omega\models p-\omega^{\prime}\models p\,\big|&=&\big|\,\omega\models(q\mathrel{\circ}f)-\omega^{\prime}\models(q\mathrel{\circ}f)\,\big|\\[3.99994pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{TransformationValidityEqn}}}{{=}}}&\big|\,\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega)\models q-\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime})\models q\,\big|\hskip 2.0pt\leq\hskip 2.0ptd\big(\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega),\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime})\big).\end{array}

Since this holds for all short factors
p
we obtain, as
required:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})&=&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models p\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models p\,\big|&\leq&d\big(\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega),\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega^{\prime})\big).\end{array}

(3)

Let
q\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be
M
-Lipschitz, then

\frac{1}{M}\cdot q\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is short. The
function
(-)\models q\colon\mathcal{D}(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is then
also
M
-Lipschitz, since for
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big|\,\omega\models q-\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|&=&M\cdot\big|\,\omega\models\frac{1}{M}\cdot q-\omega^{\prime}\models\frac{1}{M}\cdot q\,\big|\\[1.99997pt]
&\leq&M\cdot\displaystyle\bigvee_{p\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models p-\omega^{\prime}\models p\,\big|\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptM\cdot d\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

(4)

The only coupling of
1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
is

1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\otimes\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
. Hence:

\begin{array}[]{rcccccl}d\big(1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,\omega\big)&=&1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\otimes\omega\models d_{X}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\,\omega(x^{\prime})\cdot d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})&=&\omega\models d_{X}(x,-).\end{array}

(5)

We first note that for a distribution of distributions

\Omega\in\mathcal{D}^{2}(X)
and a short factor
p\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
the validity in
\Omega
of the short validity factor

(-)\models p\colon\mathcal{D}(X)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
from
item (

) satisfies:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega\models\big((-)\models p\big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)}\,\Omega(\omega)\cdot\big(\omega\models p\big)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)}\,\sum_{x\in X}\,\Omega(\omega)\cdot\omega(x)\cdot p(x)\\[11.99998pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DstMonadEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\mathsl{flat}(\Omega)(x)\cdot p(x)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\mathsl{flat}(\Omega)\models p.\end{array}

Thus for
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}^{2}(X)
,

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{D}(X)}\Big(\mathsl{flat}(\Omega),\mathsl{flat}(\Omega^{\prime})\Big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\,\big|\,\mathsl{flat}(\Omega)\models p\,-\,\mathsl{flat}(\Omega^{\prime})\models p\,\big|\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\,\big|\,\Omega\models\big((-)\models p\big)\,-\,\Omega^{\prime}\models\big((-)\models p\big)\,\big|&\mbox{as just shown}\\[11.99998pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\bigvee_{Q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(\mathcal{D}(X))}\,\big|\,\Omega\models Q\,-\,\Omega^{\prime}\models Q\,\big|&\mbox{by item~\eqref{DstMetricLemVal}}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&d_{\mathcal{D}^{2}(X)}\big(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

(6)

Directly by points (
1
)
and (
5
).

(7)

The channel composite
d\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}c=\mathsl{flat}\mathrel{\circ}\mathcal{D}(d)\mathrel{\circ}c
consists of a functional composite of
M
-Lipschitz,

L
-Lipschitz, and

-Lipschitz maps, and is thus
(M\cdot L\cdot 1)
-Lipschitz. This uses items (
1
)
and (
5
).

(8)

If we have couplings
\tau_{i}
for
\omega_{i},\omega^{\prime}_{i}
,
then
\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\tau_{i}
is a coupling of

\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega_{i}
and
\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega^{\prime}_{i}
.
Moreover:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}d\Big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega_{i},\,\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega^{\prime}_{i}\Big)&\leq&\Big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\tau_{i}\Big)\models d_{X}&=&\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\Big(\tau_{i}\models d_{X}\Big).\end{array}

Since this holds for all

, we get:

d\big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega_{i},\,\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot\omega^{\prime}_{i}\big)\leq\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot d\big(\omega_{i},\omega^{\prime}_{i}\big)
.

(9)

We unfold the definition of the
\mathsl{prm}
map
from (
6
) and use the previous item in the first

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}d_{\mathcal{D}(X^{K})}\big(\mathsl{prm}(\vec{x}),\mathsl{prm}(\vec{y})\big)&\leq&\displaystyle\sum_{t\colon\!K\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\rightarrow}}\,K}\frac{1}{K!}\cdot d_{\mathcal{D}(X^{K})}\big(1\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\underline{t}(\vec{x}){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle,1\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\underline{t}(\vec{y}){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\big)\\[10.00002pt]

∎

Later on we need the following facts about tensors of distributions.

Proposition 5
.

Let
X,Y
be metric spaces, and
K
be a positive natural number.

(1)

The tensor map
\otimes\colon\mathcal{D}(X)\times\mathcal{D}(Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)
is an isometry.

(2)

The
K
-fold tensor map
\mathsl{iid}[K]\colon\mathcal{D}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(X^{K})
, given by
\mathsl{iid}[K](\omega)\coloneqq\omega^{K}=\omega\otimes\cdots\otimes\omega
, is

K

d\big(\mathsl{iid}[K](\omega),\mathsl{iid}[K](\rho)\big)=K\cdot d(\omega,\rho)
.

Proof 4.2
.

(1)

Let distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and

\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
be given. For the inequality

d_{\mathcal{D}(X)\times\mathcal{D}(Y)}\big((\omega,\rho),(\omega^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\big)\leq d_{\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)}\big(\omega\otimes\rho,\omega^{\prime}\otimes\rho^{\prime}\big)
one uses that a coupling
\tau\in\mathcal{D}\big((X\times Y)\times(X\times Y)\big)
of
\omega\otimes\rho,\omega^{\prime}\otimes\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)
can be turned into two

\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
and of

\rho,\rho^{\prime}
, namely as
\tau_{i}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}\big(\pi_{i}\times\pi_{i}\big)(\tau)
. For the reverse
inequality one turns two couplings
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
of

\omega,\omega^{\prime}
and
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
into a coupling
\tau
of

\omega\otimes\rho,\omega^{\prime}\otimes\rho^{\prime}
via
\tau\coloneqq\mathcal{D}\big(\langle\pi_{1}\times\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\times\pi_{2}\rangle\big)\big(\tau_{1}\otimes\tau_{2}\big)
.

(2)

For
\omega,\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
, using the

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}d_{\mathcal{D}(X^{K})}\big(\omega^{K},\rho^{K}\big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{DstMetricTensorPropTwo}}}{{=}}}&d_{\mathcal{D}(X)^{K}}\Big((\omega,\ldots,\omega),(\rho,\ldots,\rho)\Big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ProductMetricEqn}}}{{=}}}&K\cdot d_{\mathcal{D}(X)}\big(\omega,\rho\big).\end{array}

∎

For the next result we use for an arbitrary set
Y
and natural number

K>0
the set
\mathcal{D}[K](Y)
of ‘fractional distributions’ obtained
from multisets of size
K
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{D}[K](Y)&\coloneqq&\{\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)\;|\;\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](Y)\}.\end{array}

Fact 6
.

For fractional distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}[K](X)
over a
metric space
X
there is an optimal coupling
\tau\in\mathcal{D}[K](X\times X)
with
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\tau\models d_{X}
.

The fact that this coupling
\tau
is also fractional, for the same
number
K
, can be seen by inspecting the simplex algorithm that is
standardly used in linear programming, see
e.g.
[
MG06
]
. The details of this go beyond the
scope of this paper. Since this fact is given without proof we shall
make explicit which results depend on it.

We illustrate that these matters are subtle.

{exa}

Consider the set
X=\{1,2,3\}
with the following two distributions

\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}[4](X)
, namely:
\omega=\frac{3}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
and
\omega^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}3{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. We consider the following
two couplings of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau&=&\frac{1}{2}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,2{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}2,3{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[1.99997pt]
\sigma&=&\frac{1}{2}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,2{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,3{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}2,2{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}2,3{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\end{array}

The first coupling
\tau
is in
\mathcal{D}[4](X\times X)
and
forms an optimal coupling, giving rise to
d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}
. Interestingly,
\sigma
is also an optimal coupling of

\omega,\omega^{\prime}
giving rise to the same distance, but inhabits the
set
\mathcal{D}[8](X\times X)
. Explicitly:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau\models d_{X}&=&\frac{1}{4}\cdot d_{X}(1,2)+\frac{1}{4}\cdot d_{X}(2,3)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{1}{4}\cdot 1+\frac{1}{4}\cdot 1\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{1}{2}.\\[1.99997pt]
\sigma\models d_{X}&=&\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(1,2)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(1,3)+\frac{1}{8}\cdot d_{X}(2,3)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{1}{8}\cdot 1+\frac{1}{8}\cdot 2+\frac{1}{8}\cdot 1\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}

The conclusion is that, in general, if
\tau\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
is an optimal coupling of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}[K](X)
, then

K\cdot\tau
need not be a multiset of (size
K
).
Fact
6
says that there is at least one optimal
coupling
\tau
such that
K\cdot\tau
does form a multiset.

5.
Kantorovich distance and total variation distance

In this section we relate the so-called total variation distance
between distributions to the Kantorovich distance, in two different
ways.

•

The total variation distance is an instance of the Kantorovich
distance, for discrete metric spaces, see
Proposition
8
.

•

The total variation distance forms a (scaled) upperbound for the
Kantorovich distance, see
Proposition
9
.

Both facts are familiar from the literature, but proofs are
scattered around or left implicit for the discrete case. We include
the details for the record and for convenience of the reader. The
second point will be useful to prove further (limit) properties of the
Kantorovich distance, via the total variation distance. The latter
distance is easier to calculate and reason about.

We first recall the
total variation distance

\mathsl{tvd}
for
distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
, on a set
X
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})&\coloneqq&\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\big|\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)\,\big|.\end{array}

(11)

Since
\big|\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)\,\big|\leq\big|\omega(x)\big|+\big|\omega^{\prime}(x)\big|=\omega(x)+\omega^{\prime}(x)
this
distance is 1-bounded, with values in the unit interval
[0,1]
. This
total variation distance is quite common, see

e.g.

[
JW20
]
and the references given there.

We first introduce a reformulation that is convenient to reason about
the total variation distance.

Lemma 7
.

Fix two distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and write:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}X_{>}&\coloneqq&\{x\in X\;|\;\omega(x)>\omega^{\prime}(x)\}\\
X_{=}&\coloneqq&\{x\in X\;|\;\omega(x)=\omega^{\prime}(x)\}\\
X_{<}&\coloneqq&\{x\in X\;|\;\omega(x)<\omega^{\prime}(x)\}\end{array}\right.\qquad\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcccl}X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)&\geq&0\\[11.99998pt]
X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{<}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)-\omega(x)&\geq&0.\end{array}\right.

Then:
X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow=X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow=\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
.

The sets
X_{>},X_{=},X_{<}
and the numbers
X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow,X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow

depend on the distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
but this is left implicit
for simplicity.

Proof 5.1
.

We have both:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}1&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega(x)+\sum_{x\in X_{=}}\,\omega(x)+\sum_{x\in X_{<}}\,\omega(x)\\
1&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)+\sum_{x\in X_{=}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)+\sum_{x\in X_{<}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x).\end{array}

Subtraction yields
0=X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow-X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow
, so that

X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow=X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow
. Further:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\big|\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)\,\big|&=&\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)+\sum_{x\in X_{<}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)-\omega(x)\right)\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\frac{1}{2}\Big(X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow+X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow\!\Big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\frac{1}{2}\Big(X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow+X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow\!\Big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptX{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptX{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow.\end{array}

∎

Each set
X
forms a ‘discrete’ metric with distance
d_{=}\colon X\times X\rightarrow[0,1]\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
given by:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{=}(x,x^{\prime})&\coloneqq&\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if }x=x^{\prime}\\
1&\mbox{if }x\neq x^{\prime}.\end{cases}\end{array}

(12)

Proposition 8
.

For each set
X
with distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
one has:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})&\;=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\,\tau\models d_{=}&\;=&d(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).\end{array}

(13)

The latter distance
d
is the Kantorovich distance for
distributions on the discrete metric space
(X,d_{=})
.

Proof 5.2
.

Let
\tau
be an arbitrary coupling of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega(x)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\,\tau(x,y)&=&\displaystyle\tau(x,x)+\sum_{y\neq x}\,\tau(x,y)\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\sum_{z\in X}\,\tau(z,x)+\sum_{y\neq x}\,\tau(x,y)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}(x)+\sum_{y\neq x}\,\tau(x,y).\end{array}

Thus, using Lemma
7
we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptX{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)&\leq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\sum_{y\neq x}\,\tau(x,y)\\[11.99998pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\sum_{y\in X}\,\tau(x,y)\cdot d_{=}(x,y)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\tau\models d_{=}.\end{array}

Since this holds for all couplings
\tau
we get the inequality

(\leq)
in (
13
).

For the inequality
(\geq)
we may assume
\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}
and so

\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\neq 0
. One uses the function
\rho\colon X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\rho(x,y)&\coloneqq&\begin{cases}\min\big(\omega(x),\omega^{\prime}(x)\big)&\mbox{if }x=y\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(\omega(x)\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\omega^{\prime}(x))\cdot(\omega^{\prime}(y)\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\omega(y))}{\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{cases}\end{array}

(14)

where
a\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}b\coloneqq\max(a-b,0)
, for

a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. This formula occurs for instance in
[
Vil09
, proof of
Thm. 6.15]
(for the continuous case).

We first check that this
\rho
is a coupling of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
.
Let
x\in X_{>}
so that
\omega(x)>\omega^{\prime}(x)
; then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\,\rho(x,y)&=&\min\big(\omega(x),\omega^{\prime}(x)\big)+(\omega(x)\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\omega^{\prime}(x))\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{y\neq x}\frac{\omega^{\prime}(y)\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}\omega(y)}{\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\omega^{\prime}(x)+(\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x))\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{y\in X_{<}}\omega^{\prime}(y)-\omega(y)}{\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\omega^{\prime}(x)+(\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x))\cdot\displaystyle\frac{X{\kern-2.58334pt}\downarrow}{\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\\[6.00006pt]
&=&\omega^{\prime}(x)+(\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x))\cdot 1\qquad\qquad\mbox{see Lemma~\ref{TvdLem}}\\
&=&\omega(x).\end{array}

If
x\not\in X_{>}
, so that
\omega(x)\leq\omega^{\prime}(x)
,
then it is obvious that
\sum_{y}\rho(x,y)=\min\big(\omega(x),\omega^{\prime}(x)\big)+0=\omega(x)
. This shows that the first marginal

\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1})(\rho)
is
\omega
. In a similar way one obtains

\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})(\rho)=\omega^{\prime}
. In particular, we can conclude that

\rho
is a distribution.

Finally,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}1-\big(\rho\models d_{=}\big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}\rho(x,y)-\sum_{x\neq y}\rho(x,y)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\rho(x,x)\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\min\big(\omega(x),\omega^{\prime}(x)\big)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)\;+\;\sum_{x\not\in X_{>}}\omega(x)\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega^{\prime}(x)+1-\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega(x)\\
&=&\displaystyle 1-\sum_{x\in X_{>}}\,\omega(x)-\omega^{\prime}(x)\\
&=&1-X{\kern-2.58334pt}\uparrow\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt1-\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}).\end{array}

Hence
\mathsl{tvd}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,=\,\rho\models d_{=}\,\geq\,\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\,\tau\models d_{=}
. In particular
\rho
in (
14
) is an
optimal coupling.

∎

For the next result we need the following notion. The diameter

\mathsl{diam}(U)
of a subset
U\subseteq X
of a metric space
X
, if it
exists, is the supremum:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{diam}(U)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\bigvee_{x,x^{\prime}\in U}d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}).\end{array}

(15)

The subset
U
is called bounded if this join exists. This
is the case when
U
is finite, as in the proposition below. This
diameter is used to show that the total variation distance forms a
scaled upperbound for the Kantorovich distance. The continuous version
of this result occurs in
[
Vil09
, 6.16]
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
X
be a metric space, with distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
.
The Kantorovich distance
d
and the total variation distance
\mathsl{tvd}

are related via the following inequality.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big)&\leq&D\cdot\mathsl{tvd}\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big),\end{array}

(16)

where:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D&\coloneqq&\max\{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\;|\;x\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega),x^{\prime}\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega^{\prime})\}\end{array}

Proof 5.3
.

We use the coupling
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}

from (
14
). It satisfies, as we have
seen:

\displaystyle d\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big)\leq\rho\models d_{X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\,\rho(x,x^{\prime})\cdot d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\neq x^{\prime}}\,\rho(x,x^{\prime})\cdot D

since
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathsl{supp}(\rho)
implies

x\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)
and
x^{\prime}\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\big(\rho\models d_{=}\big)\cdot D

\displaystyle=D\cdot\mathsl{tvd}\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big).

6.
The Kantorovich distance between multisets

There is also a Kantorovich distance between multisets of the same
size. This section recalls the definition and the main results.

{defi}

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
K\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
a positive natural
number. We can turn the metric
d_{X}\colon X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
into the
Kantorovich
metric
d\colon\mathcal{M}[K](X)\times\mathcal{M}[K](X)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on multisets (of the same size
K
), via:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\big)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})}\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\vec{x}\in\mathsl{acc}^{-1}(\varphi),\,\vec{y}\in\mathsl{acc}^{-1}(\varphi^{\prime})}\textstyle\frac{1}{K}\cdot d_{X^{K}}\big(\vec{x},\vec{y}\big)\\[13.99995pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ProductMetricEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\vec{x}\in\mathsl{acc}^{-1}(\varphi),\,\vec{y}\in\mathsl{acc}^{-1}(\varphi^{\prime})}\,\sum_{1\leq i\leq K}\textstyle\frac{1}{K}\cdot d_{X}(x_{i},y_{i}).\end{array}

(17)

This distance is defined for
K>0
. We can extend it
trivially to
K=0

Proof 6.1
.

(1)

Via naturality of frequentist learning
\mathsl{Flrn}\colon\mathcal{M}[K]\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}
we obtain that if the multiset

\tau\in\mathcal{M}[K](X\times X)
is a coupling of

\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
, then the distribution

\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
is a coupling of
\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}(X)
. This gives:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime})\big)&=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime}))}\,\sigma\models d_{X}&\,\leq&\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}.\end{array}

Since this holds for all
\tau
we get shortness of
\mathsl{Flrn}
in:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime})\big)&\leq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})}\,\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}&\,=&d\big(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

For the reverse inequality
(\geq)
, let
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
be
an optimal coupling of
\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}[K](X)
.
By Fact
6
we may assume
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}[K](X\times X)
,
so that
K\cdot\sigma\in\mathcal{M}[K](X\times X)
This is then a coupling
of
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
. We thus get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime})\big)\hskip 2.0pt\,=\,\hskip 2.0pt\sigma\models d_{X}&\,=&\mathsl{Flrn}(K\cdot\sigma)\models d_{X}\\
&\geq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})}\,\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}\hskip 2.0pt\,=\,\hskip 2.0ptd\big(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

(2)

If
\tau\in\mathcal{M}[K](X\times X)
is a coupling of

\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
, then

is obviously a coupling of
n\cdot\varphi,n\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[n\cdot K](X)
. This gives:

\qquad\begin{array}[]{rcccccl}d(n\cdot\varphi,n\cdot\varphi^{\prime})&=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(n\cdot\varphi,n\cdot\varphi^{\prime})}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\sigma)\models d_{X}&\,\leq&\mathsl{Flrn}(n\cdot\tau)\models d_{X}&\,=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}.\end{array}

Since this holds for all
\tau
we get
d(n\cdot\varphi,n\cdot\varphi^{\prime})\leq d(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
.

For the reverse inequality
(\geq)
, let multisets

\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
be given. Using that
\mathsl{Flrn}
is an
isometry, via Fact
6
, we get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d_{\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\big(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\big)&=&d_{\mathcal{D}(X)}\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi^{\prime})\big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&d_{\mathcal{D}(X)}\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(n\cdot\varphi),\mathsl{Flrn}(n\cdot\varphi^{\prime})\big)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptd_{\mathcal{M}[n\cdot K](X)}\big(n\cdot\varphi,n\cdot\varphi^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

(3)

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}[K](X\times X)
and
\tau\in\mathcal{M}[L](X\times X)
be couplings of multisets
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
and

\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}[L](X)
. The multiset sum

\sigma+\tau\in\mathcal{M}[K+L](X\times X)
is then a coupling of

\varphi+\varphi^{\prime}
and
\psi+\psi^{\prime}
. Hence:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\Big(\varphi+\psi,\,\varphi^{\prime}+\psi^{\prime}\Big)&\leq&\mathsl{Flrn}(\sigma+\tau)\models d_{X}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\Big(\frac{K}{K+L}\cdot\mathsl{Flrn}(\sigma)+\frac{L}{K+L}\cdot\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\Big)\models d_{X}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\frac{K}{K+L}\cdot\Big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sigma)\models d_{X}\Big)+\frac{L}{K+L}\cdot\Big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&\leq&\mathsl{Flrn}(\sigma)\models d_{X}\,+\,\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}.\end{array}

Since this holds for each coupling
\sigma
and
\tau
we
get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\Big(\varphi+\psi,\,\varphi^{\prime}+\psi^{\prime}\Big)&\leq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\sigma\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})}\mathsl{Flrn}(\sigma)\models d_{X}\;+\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\psi,\psi^{\prime})}\mathsl{Flrn}(\tau)\models d_{X}\\[13.99995pt]

&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ProductMetricEqn}}}{{=}}}&d\Big((\varphi,\psi),(\varphi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})\Big).\end{array}

(4)

Let

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{acc}\big(f^{K}(\vec{x})\big)&=&\mathcal{M}(f)\big(\mathsl{acc}(\vec{x})\big)&=&\mathcal{M}(f)\big(\varphi\big).\end{array}

This means that

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}d\Big(\mathcal{M}(f)\big(\varphi\big),\mathcal{M}(f)\big(\varphi^{\prime}\big)\Big)&=&\frac{1}{K}\cdot\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\vec{y}\in\mathsl{acc}^{-1}(\mathcal{M}(f)(\varphi)),\,\vec{y^{\prime}}\in\mathsl{acc}^{-1}((\mathcal{M}(f)(\varphi^{\prime}))}d_{Y^{K}}\big(\vec{y},\vec{y^{\prime}}\big)\\[13.99995pt]

(5)

The map

7.
Multinomial drawing is isometric

At this stage we have prepared the ground so that we are able to show
that the three draw channels — multionomial
\mathsl{mn}[K]

hypergeometric
\mathsl{hg}[K]
and Pólya
\mathsl{pl}[K]

involved in an isometry will be split in two inequalities, where one
is for shortness. The other inequality, for each of the drawing modes,
will be proven in basically the same manner. Each of the drawing

\begin{array}[]{rclcrclcrcl}{\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega))&=&\omega&&{\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon))&=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)&&{\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{pl}[K](\upsilon))&=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon).\end{array}

(20)

With these equations we can already prove, without having
seen the details of
\mathsl{mn}[K]
,
\mathsl{hg}[K]
or

\mathsl{pl}[K]
, via Lemma

(
1

including Fact

Lemma

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{FlrnDrawEqn}}}{{=}}}&d\big({\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)),{\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega^{\prime}))\big)\\[1.99997pt]
&\leq&d\big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega),\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega^{\prime})\big)\\[1.99997pt]
d\big(\upsilon,\upsilon^{\prime}\big)&=&d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon),\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon^{\prime})\big)\\[1.99997pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{FlrnDrawEqn}}}{{=}}}&d\big({\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon)),{\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon^{\prime}))\big)\\[1.99997pt]

Hypergeometric drawing uses the draw-delete mode, where a drawn ball
is removed from the urn. The urn thus changes with every draw. It will
be presented as a multiset, say initially of size
L
. The size
K
of
draws (multisets) must thus be restricted as
K\leq L
, since one
cannot draw more balls than the urn contains — unless one uses
negative probabilities, see
[

require that drawn multisets
\varphi
are sub-multisets of the urn,
that is
\varphi\leq\upsilon
, if
\upsilon
is the urn. This
requirement ensures that the number of drawn balls for each color is
below the number of balls of that colour in the urn.

The hypergeometric channel thus takes the form
\mathsl{hg}[K]\colon\mathcal{M}[L](X)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)

see
[
Jac21

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon)&\coloneqq&\big(\underbrace{\mathsl{DD}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\cdots\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{DD}}_{L-K\text{ times}}\big)(\upsilon)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X),\,\varphi\leq\upsilon}\frac{\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}}{\binom{L}{K}}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle,\end{array}

9.
Pólya drawing is isometric

Hypergeometric distributions use the draw-delete mode: a drawn ball is
removed from the urn. The less well-known Pólya draws
[
Hop84
]

use the draw-add mode. This means that a drawn ball is returned to the
urn, together with another ball of the same colour (as the drawn
ball). Thus, with hypergeometric draws the urn decreases in size, so
that only finitely many draws are possible, whereas with Pólya draws
the urn grows in size, and the drawing may be repeated arbitrarily
many times. As a result, for Pólya distributions we do not need to
impose restrictions on the size
K
of draws. We do have to restrict
draws from urn
\upsilon
to multisets
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
with

\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon)
since we can only draw
balls of colours that are in the urn. Following
[
Jac22c
]
,
Pólya distributions are formulated in terms of multichoose binomials

\left({\kern-2.15277pt}\binom{n}{m}{\kern-2.15277pt}\right)\coloneqq\binom{n+m-1}{m}=\frac{(n+m-1)!}{m!\cdot(n-1)!}
, for
n>0
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{pl}[K](\upsilon)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X),\,\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon)}\frac{\big(\!\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}\!\big)}{\big(\!\binom{L}{K}\!\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle,\end{array}

(25)

where
\big(\!\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}\!\big)\coloneqq{\displaystyle\prod_{x\in\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon)}}\big(\!\binom{\upsilon(x)}{\varphi(x)}\!\big)
.

The following result was already announced in the beginning of
Section
7
and is used for part of the isometry proof.

Proposition 16
.

For a non-empty urn
\upsilon
and drawsize
K>0
one has

{\mathsl{Flrn}}_{*}(\mathsl{pl}[K](\upsilon))=\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)
.

Proof 9.1
.

We use:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon))}\mathsl{pl}[K](\upsilon)(\varphi)\cdot\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)(y)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{PolyaEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon))}\frac{\big(\!\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}\!\big)\cdot\varphi(y)}{\big(\!\binom{L}{K}\!\big)\cdot K}\\[11.99998pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon))}\,\frac{\upsilon(y)\cdot\big(\!\binom{\upsilon+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle}{\varphi-1|{\kern 0.81949pt}y{\kern 0.81949pt}\rangle}\!\big)}{L\cdot\big(\!\binom{L+1}{K-1}\!\big)}\\[11.99998pt]

The marked equation

uses

\begin{array}[]{rcccccl}\big(\!\binom{L}{K}\!\big)\cdot K&=&\displaystyle\frac{(L+K-1)!}{(L-1)!\cdot K!}\cdot K&=&\displaystyle L\cdot\frac{(L+K-1)!}{L!\cdot(K-1)!}&=&L\cdot\big(\!\binom{L+1}{K-1}\!\big).\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big(\mathguilsinglleft\mathsl{acc}\mathguilsinglright\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{spol}[0]\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{arr}\big)(\upsilon)&=&\mathcal{D}(\mathsl{acc})\Big(\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})\big(1\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\vec{x},\langle\rangle{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\big)\Big)\\

The latter equation holds because the only possible draw of

The induction step is more work.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\big(\mathguilsinglleft\mathsl{acc}\mathguilsinglright\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{spol}[K+1]\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{arr}\big)(\upsilon)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]

&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{PolyaEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon))}\,\sum_{z\in\mathsl{supp}(\upsilon)}\,\frac{\upsilon(z)+\varphi(z)}{L+K}\cdot\frac{\big(\!\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}\!\big)}{\big(\!\binom{L}{K}\!\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}z{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\

&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{PolyaEqn}}}{{=}}}&\mathsl{pl}[K+1](\upsilon).\end{array}

The marked equation

10.
The law of large urns

Hypergeometric and Pólya draws involve removals from, and additions
to, the urn. When the urn
\upsilon
is very large, in comparison to
the drawsize, the effects of such removals and additions are
relatively small. Thus, hypergeometric and Pólya draws from large
urns look very much like multinomial draws — which do not change the
urn. For the corresponding multinomial draws one uses the distribution

\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)
as urn, the normalised version of the original urn

\upsilon
. This statement that hypergeometric and Pólya draws from
large urns are like multinomial draws is intuitively clear and
well-known. We make it mathematically precise in terms of limits of
Kantorovich-over-Kantorovich distances going to zero.

The starting point is the following observation about binomial and
multichoose coeffients.

Lemma 19
.

Fix a number
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\binom{n}{m}}{n^{m}}&\,=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}&\,=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\left({\kern-2.15277pt}\binom{n}{m}{\kern-2.15277pt}\right)}{n^{m}}.\end{array}

We shall use these equations in the following alternative form.

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n!}{(n-m)!\cdot n^{m}}&\,=&1&\,=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!\cdot n^{m}}.\end{array}

(27)

Proof 10.1
.

We may assume
n\geq m
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\binom{n}{m}}{n^{m}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\cdot\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n!}{(n-m)!\cdot n^{m}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\cdot\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n}{n}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{n-m+1}{n}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\cdot\left(\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n}{n}\right)\cdot\left(\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n-1}{n}\right)\cdot\ldots\cdot\left(\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n-m+1}{n}\right)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}.\end{array}

Similarly, for the multichoose coefficient:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\left({\kern-2.15277pt}\binom{n}{m}{\kern-2.15277pt}\right)}{n^{m}}&=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{(n+m-1)!}{m!\cdot(n-1)!\cdot n^{m}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\cdot\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n}{n}\cdot\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{n+m-1}{n}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}.\end{array}

∎

Proposition 20
.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
be a finite set with
N\geq 2

elements and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
be a draw of size

K\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.

(1)

The hypergeometric probability of
drawing
\varphi
from an urn becomes equal to the multinomial
probability of drawing
\varphi
from the frequentist learning of
the urn, when the number of balls in the urn for each color
x_{i}

goes to infinity:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle)\big)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)}&=&1.\end{array}

(2)

Similarly, Pólya draw probabilities
from a large urn are like multinomial probabilities:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle)\big)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{pl}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)}&=&1.\end{array}

Proof 10.2
.

(1)

By unpacking the relevant definitions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle)\big)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
&=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{K!}{\prod_{i}\varphi(x_{i})!}\cdot\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\Big(\frac{n_{i}}{\sum_{j}n_{j}}\Big)^{\varphi(x_{i})}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\binom{\sum_{j}n_{j}}{K}}{\prod_{i}\binom{n_{i}}{\varphi(x_{i})}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{(\sum_{j}n_{j})!}{((\sum_{j}n_{j})-K)!\cdot(\sum_{j}n_{j})^{K}}\cdot\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\displaystyle\frac{(n_{i}-\varphi(x_{i}))!\cdot n_{i}^{\varphi(x_{i})}}{n_{i}!}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{n!}{(n-K)!\cdot n^{K}}\right)\cdot\left(\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{i}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{(n_{i}-\varphi(x_{i}))!\cdot n_{i}^{\varphi(x_{i})}}{n_{i}!}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{CoefficientLimitEqn}}}{{=}}}&1.\end{array}

(2)

Similarly:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle)\big)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{pl}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
&=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{K!}{\prod_{i}\varphi(x_{i})!}\cdot\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\Big(\frac{n_{i}}{\sum_{j}n_{j}}\Big)^{\varphi(x_{i})}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\big(\!\binom{\sum_{j}n_{j}}{K}\!\big)}{\prod_{i}\big(\!\binom{n_{i}}{\varphi(x_{i})}\!\big)}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{((\sum_{j}n_{j})+K-1)!}{((\sum_{j}n_{j})-1)!\cdot(\sum_{j}n_{j})^{K}}\cdot\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\displaystyle\frac{(n_{i}-1)!\cdot n_{i}^{\varphi(x_{i})}}{(n_{i}+\varphi(x_{i})-1)!}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{(n+K-1)!}{(n-1)!\cdot n^{K}}\right)\cdot\left(\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n_{i}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{(n_{i}-1)!\cdot n_{i}^{\varphi(x_{i})}}{(n_{i}+\varphi(x_{i})-1)!}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{CoefficientLimitEqn}}}{{=}}}&1.\end{array}

∎

We translate these results into limits of Kantorovich distances going
to zero.

Theorem 21
.

Let
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subseteq X
be a finite set of
colors, in a metric space
X
, of size
N\geq 2
, and let
K\in\mathbb{N}

be a fixed drawsize.

(1)

When the urn size increases, the Kantorovich distance
between hypergeometric and multinomial distributions goes to zero,
as in:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,d\Big(\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle)\big)\Big)&=&0.\end{array}

(2)

Similarly, for Pólya distributions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,d\Big(\mathsl{pl}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle)\big)\Big)&=&0.\end{array}

Proof 10.3
.

By Proposition
9
it suffices to
prove the result for the total variation distance, where we take as
constant
D=\mathsl{diam}\big(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\big)
. We prove
the first point only, since the proof of the second one works
similarly. We use
Proposition
20
(
1
) in:

\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\textstyle\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{tvd}\Big(\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big)\Big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[6.00006pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{TvdEqn}}}{{=}}}&\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\left|\,\textstyle\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)\,-\,\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big)(\varphi)\,\right|\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\textstyle\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)\cdot\displaystyle\left|\,\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)}\;-\;1\,\right|\\[11.99998pt]
&\leq&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\displaystyle\left|\,\lim\limits_{n_{1},\ldots n_{N}\rightarrow\infty}\,\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(\varphi)}\;-\;1\,\right|\\[11.99998pt]
&=&0.\end{array}

∎

We may reformulate the limit results in

as ‘urn limits’ of the form:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\lim\limits_{\upsilon\rightarrow\infty}d\Big(\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\upsilon\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)\big)\Big)&=&0&=&\lim\limits_{\upsilon\rightarrow\infty}d\Big(\mathsl{pl}[K]\big(\upsilon\big),\;\mathsl{mn}[K]\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)\big)\Big).\end{array}

The meaning of
\upsilon\rightarrow\infty
for

\upsilon\in\mathcal{M}(X)
is that each color multiplicity
\upsilon(x)

goes to infinity, for each element
x
of the finite set of colours

X
. We refer to the above equations as the law(s) of large urns.

11.
The law of large draws

This section contains another limit property, not for large urns but
for large draws. It may be seen as a metric reformulation of the law
of large numbers.

But first some preliminaries. For a distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)

and an observation
p\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the variance

\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
describes how much the observable is
spread-out, in relation to the validity, or expected value,

\omega\models p
. Explicitly:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)&\,\coloneqq&\omega\models\big(p-(\omega\models p)\cdot\mathbf{1}\big)^{2}.\end{array}

(28)

We recall a number of basic facts about this variance.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and
p\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given.

(1)

The variance (
28
) can also be described as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)&\,=&\big(\omega\models p^{2}\big)-\big(\omega\models p\big)^{2}.\end{array}

(2)

As a result,
\big(\omega\models p^{2}\big)\geq\big(\omega\models p\big)^{2}
.

(3)

There is also an inequality:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega\models\big|\,p-(\omega\models p)\cdot\mathbf{1}\,\big|&\,\leq&\sqrt{\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)}.\end{array}

Proof 11.1
.

(1)

Via a standard argument:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{VarEqn}}}{{=}}}&\omega\models\big(p-(\omega\models p)\cdot\mathbf{1}\big)^{2}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot\big(p(x)-(\omega\models p)\big)^{2}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot\Big(p(x)^{2}-2(\omega\models p)\cdot p(x)+(\omega\models p)^{2}\Big)\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot p^{2}(x)\right)-2(\omega\models p)\cdot\left(\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot p(x)\right)+\left(\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot(\omega\models p)^{2}\right)\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\big(\omega\models p^{2}\big)-2\big(\omega\models p)\cdot(\omega\models p)+(\omega\models p)^{2}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\big(\omega\models p^{2}\big)-(\omega\models p)^{2}.\end{array}

(2)

Obviously, by the previous point, since
\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)\geq 0
.

(3)

We abbreviate
q\coloneqq\big|\,p-(\omega\models p)\cdot\mathbf{1}\,\big|
, so that
q(x)=\big|\,p(x)-(\omega\models p)\,\big|
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)&\,=&\omega\models q^{2}&\,\geq&\big(\omega\models q\big)^{2},\qquad\mbox{by the previous point.}\end{array}

As a result,

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\omega\models\big|\,p-(\omega\models p)\cdot\mathbf{1}\,\big|&\,=&\omega\models q&\,\leq&\sqrt{\mathsl{Var}(\omega,p)}.\end{array}

∎

Informally, the next result expresses that large multinomial draws,
when normalised, are close to the urn distribution. This is
intuitively clear: when we draw very many balls (in draw-replace
mode), then the frequencies of the colours in the draw resemble the
urn. The more formal aspect of this statement is below. It expresses
that this closeness works “in probability”, that is, the
probabilities of the draws must be taken into account. Indeed, a very
large draw may differ significantly from the urn, for instance when
all drawn balls have the same colour, but the likelihood of such draws
is low.

Theorem 23
.

For each distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
on a metric space
X
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\omega,\mathsl{Flrn}(-)\big)&\,=&0.\end{array}

Proof 11.2
.

Since Proposition
9
applies, with
D=\mathsl{diam}\big(\mathsl{supp}(\omega)\big)
, it suffices to prove the limit
equation for the total variation distance
\mathsl{tvd}
.

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\mathsl{tvd}\big(\omega,\mathsl{Flrn}(-)\big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[6.00006pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{TvdEqn}}}{{=}}}&\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)}\big|\,\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)(y)-\omega(y)\,\big|\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\big|\,\mathsl{Flrn}(-)(y)-\big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\mathsl{Flrn}(-)(y)\big)\,\big|\\
&&\qquad\mbox{by Proposition~\ref{FlrnMulnomProp}~\eqref{FlrnMulnomPropElt1}}\\
&\leq&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\!\sqrt{\mathsl{Var}\big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega),\mathsl{Flrn}(-)(y)\big)}\qquad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{VarLem}~\eqref{VarLemAbs}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\!\displaystyle\sqrt{\Big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\mathsl{Flrn}(-)(y)^{2}\Big)-\Big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\mathsl{Flrn}(-)(y)\Big)^{2}}\\
&&\qquad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{VarLem}~\eqref{VarLemVar}}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\!\sqrt{\frac{(K-1)\cdot\omega(y)^{2}+\omega(y)}{K}-\omega(y)^{2}}\qquad\mbox{by Proposition~\ref{FlrnMulnomProp}~\eqref{FlrnMulnomPropFlrn2},
\eqref{FlrnMulnomPropFlrn1}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\frac{1}{2}\!\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathsl{supp}(\omega)}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sqrt{\omega(y)\cdot(1-\omega(y))}}{\sqrt{K}}\\[6.00006pt]
&=&0.\end{array}

∎

Corollary 24
.

For two distributions
\omega,\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X)
on a metric space
X
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\rho\big)&\,=&d\big(\omega,\rho\big).\end{array}

Proof 11.3
.

We use the triangular inequality, both for
(\leq)
and
(\geq)
.
First, for each multiset
\varphi
we have
d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\rho\big)\leq d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\omega\big)+d\big(\omega,\rho\big)
. Hence:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\rho\big)&\leq&\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\Big(d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\omega\big)+d\big(\omega,\rho\big)\Big)\\[6.00006pt]
&=&\Big(\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\omega\big)\Big)+d\big(\omega,\rho\big)\\[6.00006pt]
&=&d\big(\omega,\rho\big).\end{array}

In the other direction, we use
d\big(\omega,\rho\big)\leq d\big(\omega,\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)\big)+d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi),\rho\big)
. Hence:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}d\big(\omega,\rho\big)&=&\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\omega,\rho\big)\\[1.99997pt]
&\leq&\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models\Big(d\big(\omega,\mathsl{Flrn}(-)\big)+d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\rho\big)\Big)\\[6.00006pt]
&=&\Big(\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\omega,\mathsl{Flrn}(-)\big)\Big)+\Big(\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\rho\big)\Big)\\[3.99994pt]
&=&\lim\limits_{K\rightarrow\infty}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\models d\big(\mathsl{Flrn}(-),\rho\big).\end{array}

∎

12.
Concluding remarks

Category theory provides a fresh look at the area of probability
theory, see e.g.
[
Fri20
]
or
[
Jac25
]
for an
overview. This paper demonstrates that draw operations, viewed as
(Kleisli) maps, are remarkably well-behaved: they exactly preserve
Kantorovich distances. Such distances on urns filled with coloured
balls are relatively simple, starting from a ‘ground’ metric on the
set of colours. But on draw distributions, the distances involve
Kantorovich-over-Kantorovich. In addition, well-known limit
behaviour, for large urns and large draws, is formulated in terms of
Kantorovich distances.

This paper arose from a fresh categorical perspective on classical urn
models, in line with earlier work
[
Jac21
,
Jac22b
,
Jac22c
]

of the author. Is category theory necessary to obtain (or prove) these
results? No, one can strip all categorical language from what has been
described above and formulate and prove the isometry results in
traditional probabilistic language. But it is a fact that these
results have not appeared within the traditional, non-categorical
setting. We do not argue that category theory is necessary, but we do
believe that its new, fresh perspective is helpful and leads to new
ideas and results. More generally, category theory often helps in a
particular mathematical setting to organise the material in a
structured manner and to ask relevant questions: does this category
have limits or colimits? Is this operation functorial, and if so, what
does it preserve, does it have adjoints, etc. This paper uses
category theory in a light-weight manner and is not a hard-core paper
in categorical probability theory, using for instance Markov
categories
[
Fri20
]
. Hopefully it works as invitation to invest
efforts to learn the new categorically-inspired approach and what it
brings.

This paper concentrates on drawing from an urn using finite discrete
probability distributions. A natural question is whether other
operations, especially involving infinite discrete and continuous
probability, also preserve distance. This question is solved
in
[
AG21
, Prop. 3.2]
for parameterised distributions

\omega[\theta]
on (subsets of) the reals: the Kantorovich distance
equals the (real-number) distance between the expected values:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\omega[\theta_{1}],\omega[\theta_{2}]\big)&=&\big|\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(\omega[\theta_{1}])-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}(\omega[\theta_{2}])\,\big|.\end{array}

(31)

As special cases, one has, for instance for the Kantorovich
distances between Poisson and exponential distributions (on
\mathbb{N}
and

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
):

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}d\big(\mathsl{Pois}[\lambda_{1}],\mathsl{Pois}[\lambda_{2}]\big)&=&\big|\,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\,\big|&&d\big(\mathsl{Exp}[\lambda_{1}],\mathsl{Exp}[\lambda_{2}]\big)&=&\big|\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\,\big|.\end{array}

This equation (
31
) is very powerful.
However, the isometry results for draw distributions in this paper are
not instances of (
31
) since these draw distributions
are not distributions on real numbers, but on multisets.

Another question that may be asked is if the (isometry) results for
draw distributions, with respect to the Kantorovich distance, also
hold for Kullback-Leibler divergence (see
[
CT06
]
for
extensive information). This is largely an open question. What we
can offer is the following result for multinomial distributions.
We write
\mathsl{D}_{\mathsl{KL}}
for this Kullback-Leibler divergence and use
its definition implicitly in the proof below.

Proposition 26
.

Let distributions
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
be given with a
draw-size number
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{D}_{\mathsl{KL}}\Big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega),\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega^{\prime})\Big)&=&K\cdot\mathsl{D}_{\mathsl{KL}}\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big).\end{array}

Proof 12.1
.

We may assume
\mathsl{supp}(\omega)\subseteq\mathsl{supp}(\omega^{\prime})
and write

\mathsl{supp}(\omega^{\prime})=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subseteq X
.

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathsl{D}_{\mathsl{KL}}\Big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega),\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega^{\prime})\Big)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)}{\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega^{\prime})(\varphi)}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\ln\left(\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{i}\omega(x_{i})^{\varphi(x_{i})}}{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{i}\omega^{\prime}(x_{i})^{\varphi(x_{i})}}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\ln\left({\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,}\left(\frac{\omega(x_{i})}{\omega^{\prime}(x_{i})}\right)^{\varphi(x_{i})}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot{\textstyle{\displaystyle\sum}_{i}\,}\varphi(x_{i})\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\omega(x_{i})}{\omega^{\prime}(x_{i})}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle{\textstyle{\displaystyle\sum}_{i}\,}\left(\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\varphi(x_{i})\right)\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\omega(x_{i})}{\omega^{\prime}(x_{i})}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle{\textstyle{\displaystyle\sum}_{i}\,}K\cdot\omega(x_{i})\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\omega(x_{i})}{\omega^{\prime}(x_{i})}\right)\qquad\mbox{by Proposition~\ref{FlrnMulnomProp}~\eqref{FlrnMulnomPropElt1}}\\[6.00006pt]
&=&K\cdot\mathsl{D}_{\mathsl{KL}}\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big).\hskip 0.0pt\parbox[t]{0.0pt}{\qed}\end{array}

Appendix A

Proof of the equations in (
10
)

We recall the three formulations (
10
) of the
Kantorovich distance.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big)\hskip 2.0pt\coloneqq\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,\,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X),\,p\oplus p^{\prime}\,\leq\,d_{X}}\,\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}\\[13.99995pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\bigvee_{q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|.\end{array}

(32)

The observation
p\oplus p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X\times X)
is defined
as
(p\oplus p^{\prime})(x,x^{\prime})=p(x)+p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
. The minimum given by the meet

\bigwedge
exists since there is at least one coupling of

\omega,\omega^{\prime}
, namely the product
\omega\otimes\omega^{\prime}
. The two
joins
\bigvee
are also taken over non-empty domains since one can
instantiate with the zero observation / factor
\mathbf{0}
.

We first concentrate on equation
(*)

in (
32
). The inequality
(\geq)
is easy: let

p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)
satisfy
p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}
. Then for each
coupling
\tau\in\mathcal{D}(X\times X)
of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
one has:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tau\models d_{X}\hskip 2.0pt\geq\hskip 2.0pt\tau\models p\oplus p^{\prime}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\,\tau(x,x^{\prime})\cdot(p\oplus p^{\prime})(x,x^{\prime})\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\,\tau(x,x^{\prime})\cdot\big(p(x)+p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\big)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\,\tau(x,x^{\prime})\cdot p(x)+\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\,\tau(x,x^{\prime})\cdot p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\left(\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\,\tau(x,x^{\prime})\right)\cdot p(x)+\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\,\left(\sum_{x\in X}\,\tau(x,x^{\prime})\right)\cdot p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot p(x)+\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\,\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\cdot p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\\
&=&\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}.\end{array}

As a result, since this inequality holds for all couplings

\tau
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}&\,\geq&\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}.\end{array}

And since the latter holds for all
p,p^{\prime}
we get the

(\geq)

For the

Lemma 27

(Farkas Lemma)
.

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and a vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
there
are equivalences of the following form.

(1)

\exists v\in\big(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\big)^{n}.\,A\cdot v=b\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\neg\exists u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.\,u^{T}\cdot A\geq\mathbf{0}\mbox{ and }u^{T}\cdot b<0
.

(2)

\exists v\in\big(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\big)^{n}.\,A\cdot v\leq b\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\neg\exists u\in\big(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\big)^{m}.\,u^{T}\cdot A\geq\mathbf{0}\mbox{ and }u^{T}\cdot b<0
.

Proof A.1
.

A (short) proof of the first item may be found
in
[
Kag24
]
. Next,
[
MG06
, Prop. 6.4.3]
contains a
derivation of the second item from the first. ∎

We are going to apply this lemma in the Kantorovich context, in order
to prove the
(\leq)
part of
\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}}

in (
32
). Let
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
be

X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. We take

in

with vector

given by the

\begin{array}[]{rcl}b&\coloneqq&\big(\omega(x_{1}),\ldots,\omega(x_{n}),\omega^{\prime}(x_{1}),\ldots,\omega^{\prime}(x_{n})\big).\end{array}

For convenience we write
t
for the minimum validity

\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}
.

We use the matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n^{2}}
that captures
marginalisation, when applied to distributions, giving a mapping

\mathcal{D}(X\times X)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(X)\times\mathcal{D}(X)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A&\coloneqq&\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccccc}1&1&\cdots&1&0&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0\\[-5.0pt]

0&1&\cdots&0&0&1&\cdots&0&\cdots\cdots&0&1&\cdots&0\\[-5.0pt]
&&\vdots&&&&\vdots&&&&&\vdots&\\[-5.0pt]
0&0&\cdots&1&0&0&\cdots&1&\cdots\cdots&0&0&\cdots&1\end{array}\right)}_{\text{$n^{2}$ columns}}\begin{array}[]{c}{\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{}\\[15.00002pt]
\mbox{}\end{array}\right\}\text{\scriptsize$n$ rows}}\\
{\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\mbox{}\\[15.00002pt]
\mbox{}\end{array}\right\}\text{\scriptsize$n$ rows}}\end{array}\end{array}

We turn the matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n^{2}}
into a matrix

A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{(4n+1)\times n^{2}}
in the following manner.

•

On top of
A
we add the row
d_{X}(x_{1},x_{1}),d(x_{1},x_{2}),\ldots,d_{X}(x_{n},x_{n})
containing the
n^{2}
distance
values.

•

Below
A
we add the matrix
-A
, with all numbers in
A

negated.

For each
\varepsilon\!>\!0
we form a similar sequence

b_{\varepsilon}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{4n+1}
of the form
(t-\varepsilon,b_{1},...\ ,b_{2n},-b_{1},...\ ,
-b_{2n})
. In this way the
following statement holds.

\neg\exists v\in\big(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\big)^{n^{2}}.\,A^{\prime}\cdot v\leq b_{\varepsilon}.

Indeed,
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n^{2}})
with
A^{\prime}\cdot v\leq b_{\varepsilon}
would give a multiset
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(X\times X)
,
namely
\sigma=\sum_{i,j}v_{(i-1)*n+j}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i},x_{j}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. It is a
coupling of
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
since
A\cdot v=b=(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

via the inequalities
A\cdot v\leq b
and
-A\cdot v\leq-b
. Therefore,
\sigma
is a distribution. Moreover, it satisfies

\sigma\models d_{X}\leq t-\varepsilon
. The latter contradicts the
minimality of
t
.

By applying item (
2
) of Farkas
Lemma
27
we get a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{4n+1}
with
u\geq\mathbf{0}
satisfying:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle u_{0}\cdot d_{X}+(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2n})\cdot A-(u_{2n+1},\ldots,u_{4n})\cdot A&\geq&\mathbf{0}\\[1.99997pt]
\displaystyle u_{0}\cdot(t-\varepsilon)+(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2n})\cdot b-(u_{2n+1},\ldots,u_{4n})\cdot b&<&0.\end{array}

(33)

We first cover the case
u_{0}=0
. The vector
u
is then
a solution in (
33
) for
\varepsilon=t
, so that
applying the direction
(\Leftarrow)
in
Lemma
27
(
2
) gives a coupling

\sigma
with
\sigma\models d_{X}=0
. This gives
t=0
.
But then we are done, since trivially:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)\text{ with }p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}&\geq&\omega\models\mathbf{0}\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models\mathbf{0}\\[-5.0pt]
&=&0\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptt\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}.\end{array}

We may now assume
u_{0}>0
, so we can form two observations

p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)
via the definitions:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}p(x_{i})&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{u_{2n+i}-u_{i}}{u_{0}}&\qquad\mbox{and}&p^{\prime}(x_{i})&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{u_{3n+i}-u_{n+i}}{u_{0}}.\end{array}

The first of the inequalities (
33
)
translates to
p(x_{i})+p^{\prime}(x_{j})\leq d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})
for each

i,j
, that is, to
p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}
. The second inequality
gives us
\omega\models p+\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}>t-\varepsilon
. We thus get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)\text{ with }p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}&>&t-\varepsilon.\end{array}

Since this holds for each
\varepsilon>0
we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)\text{ with }p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}&\geq&t&=&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\tau\in\mathsl{dcpl}^{-1}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})}\tau\models d_{X}.\end{array}

This proves the
(\leq)
part of
\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}}

in (
32
).

We now turn to the second equation
\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{=}}}

in (
32
), again starting from two distributions

\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
. Its proof is more elementary. For

(\geq)
, let
q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)
be given. Without loss of generality we
assume
\omega\models q\,\leq\,\omega^{\prime}\models q
. We take as
observations
p=-q
and
p^{\prime}=q
. Then, because
q
is short:

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccl}(p\oplus p^{\prime})(x,x^{\prime})&=&p(x)+p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})&=&q(x^{\prime})-q(x)&\leq&\big|\,q(x^{\prime})-q(x)\,\big|&\leq&d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}).\end{array}

Moreover,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|&=&\omega^{\prime}\models q-\omega\models q\\
&=&\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)\text{ with }p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}.\end{array}

Since this holds for all
q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)
we get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\bigvee_{q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|&\,\leq&\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)\text{ with }p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}.\end{array}

For
(\leq)
let
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)
be given with
p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}
. We form
q\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}q(x)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{y\in X}\,d_{X}(x,y)-p(y)\end{array}

We make a few points explicit.

•

By using
x
in place of
y
in the range of the above meet

\bigwedge
we get
q(x)\leq-p(x)
. Further, since
p(x)+p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
we have
p^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-p(x)\leq q(x^{\prime})
. Thus,
p\leq-q
and
p^{\prime}\leq q
.

•

Next,
q
is short. Indeed, for
x,x^{\prime}\in X
, and
arbitrary
y\in X
we have, by the triangular inequality,

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}q(x)&\leq&d_{X}(x,y)-p(y)&\leq&d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{X}(x^{\prime},y)-p(y).\end{array}

Since this holds for each
y
we get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}q(x)&\leq&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{y\in X}\,d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{X}(x^{\prime},y)-p(y)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+\displaystyle\bigwedge_{y\in X}\,d_{X}(x^{\prime},y)-p(y)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0ptd_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+q(x^{\prime}).\end{array}

Thus:
q(x)-q(x^{\prime})\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
. By symmetry also:

q(x^{\prime})-q(x)\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
, and thus
\big|\,q(x)-q(x^{\prime})\,\big|=\max\big(q(x)-q(x^{\prime}),q(x^{\prime})-q(x)\big)\leq d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
.

•

Let
M
be the minimum value of
q
, which exists, since we
assume that
X
is a finite set. We take
q^{\prime}=q+M\cdot\mathbf{1}
. This is a factor, that is, a function
X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

taking non-negative values. Moreover,
q^{\prime}
is short since
q
is
short.

We can now put things together.

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}&\leq&\omega\models-q\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models q&\mbox{since $p\leq-q$ and $p^{\prime}\leq q$}\\
&\leq&\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\big|\,\omega\models q^{\prime}\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q^{\prime}\,\big|&\mbox{since }q^{\prime}=q+M\cdot\mathbf{1}\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\bigvee_{q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|.\end{array}

Because this holds for all
p,p^{\prime}
we get, as required,
the inequality
(\leq)
for
\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(**)}}{{=}}}

in (
32
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\bigvee_{p,p^{\prime}\in\mathsl{Obs}(X)\text{ with }p\oplus p^{\prime}\leq d_{X}}\omega\models p\,+\,\omega^{\prime}\models p^{\prime}&\,\leq&\displaystyle\bigvee_{q\in\mathsl{Fact}_{\mathsl{S}}(X)}\big|\,\omega\models q\,-\,\omega^{\prime}\models q\,\big|.\end{array}
