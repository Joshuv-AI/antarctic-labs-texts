Monopoles And Landau-Ginzburg Models I
Donghao Wang

Abstract
. The endpoint of this series of papers is to construct the monopole Floer homology for any pair ( Y , ω ) (Y,\omega) , where Y Y is a compact oriented 3-manifold with toroidal boundary and ω \omega is a suitable closed 2-form. In the first paper, we exploit the framework of the gauged Landau-Ginzburg models to address two model problems for the (perturbed) Seiberg-Witten moduli spaces on either ℂ × Σ \mathbb{C}\times\Sigma or ℍ + 2 × Σ \mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma , where Σ \Sigma is any compact Riemann surface of genus ≥ 1 \geq 1 . Our first result states that finite energy solutions to the perturbed equations on ℂ × Σ \mathbb{C}\times\Sigma are necessarily trivial. The second states that small energy solutions on ℍ + 2 × Σ \mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma necessarily have energy decaying exponentially in the spatial direction. These results will lead eventually to the compactness theorem in the second paper [ Wan20a ] .



Part I
Introduction

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivations in Floer Homology

The Seiberg-Witten Floer homology of closed oriented 3-manifolds as introduced by Kronheimer-Mrowka
[
KM07
]
has greatly influenced the study of 3-manifold topology since its inception. The underlying idea is an infinite-dimensional Morse theory: solutions to the Seiberg-Witten equations on a closed 3-manifold
Z
are critical points of the Chern-Simons-Dirac functional
\mathcal{L}
, while solutions on the 4-manifold
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times Z
are downward gradient flowlines of
\mathcal{L}
.

The purpose of this series of papers is to generalize their construction and define the Seiberg-Witten Floer homology for any pair
(Y,\omega)
, where
Y
is a compact oriented 3-manifold with toroidal boundary and
\omega
is a suitable closed 2-form. This analytic construction will recover the monopole link Floer homology for any link
L\subset S^{3}
and produce potentially new invariants for the link complement. In the third paper
[
Wan20b
, Theorem 1.10]
, we will establish a (3+1) TQFT structure comparable to the one for link Floer homology
[
Juh16
,
Zem19
,
Li18
]
.

The long range goal of this line of work is to develop a bordered version of monopole Floer homology, analogous to the bordered Heegaard Floer theory of Lipshitz-Ozsváth-Thurston
[
LOT08
]
, but via analytic methods. In the process of attacking this problem, the author realized that the geometric framework needed to understand the Seiberg-Witten equations (SWEQ) on the product 4-manifold
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is any compact Riemann surface of genus
\geq 1
, is gauged Landau-Ginzburg models
[
Wit93
]
. This interesting dictionary, which links gauge theory with symplectic topology, however, does not seem to be well-known in the literature. In short, this dictionary says that any
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on
\Sigma
specifies an infinite dimensional Kähler manifold
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
together with a holomorphic function

W:M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})\to\mathbb{C},

then the Seiberg-Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\Sigma
can be interpreted as the complex gradient flow equation (
1.2
) of
W
defined on
\mathbb{C}_{z}
.

This paper focuses on the already interesting problem of the analysis of these equations: we exploit this dictionary to establish two basic analytic results that are essential to all forthcoming development. The first one states that finite energy solutions to the Seiberg-Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\Sigma
, once perturbed by a suitable 2-form, must be trivial, while the second states that small energy solutions on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}\times\Sigma
must have energy decaying exponentially as
s\to\infty
. These results are completely general with regards to the genus of
\Sigma
and will play an important role in the proof of the compactness theorem in the second paper
[
Wan20a
]
. This 2-form is to perturb
W:M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})\to\mathbb{C}
into a holomorphic Morse function (interpreted suitably in the infinite dimensional case), a crucial property in order for these results to hold.

1.2.
Summary of Results

To state our main theorems, we first outline a monopole Floer theory for 3-manifolds with cylindrical ends. Given a compact oriented 3-manifold
Y
with boundary
\partial Y=\Sigma
, let
g_{Y}
be a metric cylindrical near
\Sigma
. Set
g_{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g_{Y}|_{\Sigma}
. In
[
Ngu12
,
Ngu18
]
, Nguyen studied the Seiberg-Witten equations directly on the 3-manifold
Y
and laid the analytic foundation for a Floer theory with Lagrangian boundary conditions along
\partial Y
. We will work instead with a 3-manifold with cylindrical ends:

\widehat{Y}=Y\ \bigcup\ [0,\infty)_{s}\times\Sigma.

This approach is also adopted in the PhD thesis of Yang
[
Yan99
]
and in an unpublished manuscript by Mrowka-Ozsváth-Yu. In the latter case, they investigated Seifert-fibered spaces with some regular fibers removed in attempt to generalize their earlier work
[
MOY97
]
.

As we shall focus on the geometry of the cylindrical end of
\widehat{Y}
in this paper, it is harmless to assume that
\Sigma
is connected and
g(\Sigma)\geq 1
from now on. A solution to the Seiberg-Witten equations on
[0,\infty)_{s}\times\Sigma
is a flowline of
-\nabla\re W
on the Kähler manifold
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
. The key observation is that the functional
\re W
is in fact the real part of a holomorphic function
W:M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})\to\mathbb{C}
. Without any perturbation, the critical set of
\re W
modulo gauge is a symmetric product of the surface
\Sigma
. While it is tempting to develop a Floer theory with respect to a Lagrangian submanifold of this symmetric product, we follow a different approach in this paper and perturb
W
into a holomorphic Morse function using a harmonic 1-form
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}_{h}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
; the
(1,0)
-part
\lambda^{1,0}\in\Omega^{1,0}(\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
is required to have
(2g-2)
simple zeros.

Returning to the 3-manifold
\widehat{Y}
, we choose a closed 2-form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(\widehat{Y},i\mathbb{R})
to perturb the 3-dimensional Seiberg-Witten equations such that
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
on
[0,\infty)_{s}\times\Sigma
for some 2-form
\nu\in\Omega^{2}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
on
\Sigma
.

This setup is inspired by the work of Meng-Taubes
[
MT96
]
, and this quadruple
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
will be called an
H
-surface in this paper. We are interested in
spin^{c}\
structures
\mathfrak{s}
on
Y
with

c_{1}(\mathfrak{s})[\Sigma]=2(d+g(\Sigma)-1)\text{ for some integer }0\leq d\leq 2g(\Sigma)-2.

Our first observation concerns these perturbed equations on the standard cylinder
\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma
.

Lemma 1.1

(Proposition
7.4
)
.

For any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, consider the perturbed Seiberg-Witten equations (
10.1
) on the 3-manifold
\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma
with
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
. Then for the
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
with
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s})[\Sigma]=2(d-g(\Sigma)+1)
, there are precisely
\binom{2g-2}{d}

\mathbb{R}_{s}
-invariant solutions up to gauge. These solutions are irreducible.

Remark 1.2
.

When
g(\Sigma)=1
, Lemma
1.1
is used by Meng-Taubes
[
MT96
]
to define the 3-dimensional Seiberg-Witten invariants of
Y
(even though this property is not stated explicitly in their paper); see
[
MT96
, Section 2.1-2.3]
. The 4-dimensional analogue of Lemma
1.1
is
[
Tau01
, Lemma 3.1]
. Lemma
1.1
is also used to compute the Seiberg-Witten invariants of the product manifold
S^{1}\times S^{1}\times\Sigma_{g}
, a well-known result since the very beginning of this subject. See
[
Wit94
, (4.11)–(4.16)]
.
∎

Lemma
1.1
says that the critical set of
W
modulo gauge is (at least) discrete after this perturbation. Let
\mathfrak{a}_{j},1\leq j\leq k=\binom{2g-2}{d}
be a collection of the special solutions to the Seiberg-Witten equations obtained in Lemma
1.1
, one for each gauge-equivalence class. For any
H
-surface
\TSigma
, the goal is to define the Floer homology of
Y
relative to the pair
(\TSigma,\mathfrak{a}_{j})
:

\HM_{*}(Y,\mathfrak{a}_{j}).

To this end, consider finite energy solutions on
\widehat{Y}
that approximate
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
along the cylindrical end
[0,\infty)_{s}\times\Sigma
. As critical points of the perturbed Chern-Simons-Dirac functional
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
(cf.
[
Wan20a
, Definition 3.8]
), they become non-degenerate after a further perturbation and form a compact moduli space of dimension
0
.

Now we have to analyze the 4-dimensional equations on the product manifold
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\widehat{Y}
carrying a planar end
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma
, where

\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}

is furnished with the Euclidean metric. Our convention is to use
t
for the time coordinate and
s
for the spatial coordinate on the cylindrical end of
\widehat{Y}
. The Seiberg-Witten moduli space on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\widehat{Y}
has another possible source of non-compactness coming from this planar end: for a sequence of Seiberg-Witten monopoles on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\widehat{Y}
, some amount of energy might slide off along the cylindrical end of
\widehat{Y}
and converges to a monopole on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma
with finite positive energy in the limit.

This is the sort of phenomena we must prevent in order to define a Floer homology, which is addressed successfully in Theorem
1.3
below. In some sense, the 4-manifold
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\widehat{Y}
is non-compact in two directions, but the energy can slide off only in the time direction.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, consider the
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on
\Sigma
with
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s})[\Sigma]=2(d-g(\Sigma)+1)
. Then any finite energy solution to the perturbed Seiberg-Witten equations (
7.1
) on
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\Sigma
with
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
, which is also called point-like, is gauge equivalent to the trivial solution obtained by extending
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
constantly over
\mathbb{C}_{z}
with
z=t+is
, for some
1\leq j\leq\binom{2g-2}{d}
.

In this sense, we say that point-like solutions on
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\Sigma
are trivial. In contrast, for the unperturbed Seiberg-Witten equations (with
\omega=0
), interesting point-like solutions do exist and were classified completely in an earlier work of the author
[
Wan18
]
.

The second problem that we address is the decay rate in the spatial direction of
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\widehat{Y}
. We state the result for the planar end
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma
. For any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
R\in[1,\infty)
, consider the rectangle
\Omega_{n,R}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}[n-1,n+1]\times[R-1,R+1]\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
. Denote by

(1.1)

\mathcal{E}_{an}(\gamma;\Omega_{n,R})

the analytic energy of the configuration
\gamma
on the 4-manifold
\Omega_{n,R}\times\Sigma
, called the local energy functional (see Lemma
8.2
).
\mathcal{E}_{an}(\gamma;\Omega_{n,R})
is the integral of the energy density function of
\gamma
over
\Omega_{n,R}
and therefore is non-negative and gauge-invariant; it bounds the
L^{2}_{1}
-norm of
\gamma
(with a gauge fixing condition) on
\Omega_{n,R}\times\Sigma
and also the
L^{2}_{k}
-norms of
\gamma
in the interior of
\Omega_{n,R}\times\Sigma
for any
k\geq 2
when
\gamma
is a solution.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, there exist constants
\epsilon(\TSigma),\zeta(\TSigma)>0
with the following significance. Suppose
\gamma
solves the perturbed Seiberg-Witten equations (
7.1
) on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma
with
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
and
\mathcal{E}_{an}(\gamma,\Omega_{n,R})<\epsilon
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
R\geq 1
, then

\mathcal{E}_{an}(\gamma;\Omega_{n,R})<e^{-\zeta R}.

From Theorem
1.4
, one can easily deduce the exponential decay of
L^{2}_{k}
-norms of a solution
\gamma
on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma
. Since the spatial direction (
s\to\infty
) is not the one for the downward gradient flow of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
, Theorem
1.4
is not a consequence of the standard theory, e.g.
[
KM07
, Section 13]
.

Remark 1.5
.

In the second paper
[
Wan20a
]
, we will carry out the construction of
\HM_{*}(Y,\mathfrak{a}_{j})
in detail for the special case that
(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma})
is a union of flat 2-tori and
\nu
is harmonic. While the general case is almost identical, the invariance of this Floer homology with
g(\Sigma)\geq 2
is not well-understood and is left as a topic for future research.
∎

1.3.
Gauged Landau-Ginzburg Models

The proofs of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
exploit the fundamental relation between the Seiberg-Witten equations and the gauged Witten equations, which links gauge theory with symplectic topology, and reducing Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
to some finite dimensional problems.

The gauged Witten equations were first introduced by Witten in his formulation of gauged
linear sigma models
[
Wit93
]
to explain the so-called
Landau-Ginzburg/Calabi-Yau correspondence. Its mathematical foundation has been recently developed by Tian-Xu
[
TX18a
,
TX18b
]
, to which readers are referred for the background. Since our focus is slightly different, we give a short discussion below with emphasis on Picard-Lefschetz theory.

When the dimension is finite and the structure group
G
is trivial, a Landau-Ginzburg model is a pair
(M,W)
where

•

M
is a complete non-compact Kähler manifold, and

•

W=L+iH:M\to\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic function, called the superpotential.

The Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W)
is called
Morse
if
L\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\re W:M\to\mathbb{R}
Morse function, so
(M,W)
defines a Lefschetz fibration over
\mathbb{C}
. From a symplectic viewpoint, one may define its Fukaya-Seidel category
\mathcal{A}
using Lagrangian Floer theory, following
[
Sei08
]
, and associate to each compact Lagrangian submanifold
\mathcal{L}_{0}\subset M
an
A_{\infty}
-module over
\mathcal{A}
. This construction is based on the Cauchy-Riemann equation, or more conveniently, the complex gradient flow equation,

(1.2)

\partial_{t}P+J\partial_{s}P+\nabla H=0,

where
H\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\im W
and
P:\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]_{s}\to M
is subject to Lagrangian boundary conditions.

We wish to generalize this picture in two directions, starting with

(1)

M
is acted on by an abelian Lie group
G
with a moment map
\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}
, and the superpotential
W
is
G
-invariant. The triple
(M,W,G)
is then called a gauged Landau-Ginzburg model; see Section
3
for the precise definition.

The replacement of (
1.2
) is the gauged Witten equations:

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-*_{2}F_{A}+\mu&=\vec{\delta},\\
\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P+J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P+\nabla H&=0,\end{array}\right.

where
A
is a connection on the trivial
G
-bundle
Q
over
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]_{s}
and
\vec{\delta}\in\mathfrak{g}
is a perturbation of the moment map
\mu
. The map
P
is now regarded as a section of the trivial bundle
Q\times_{G}M
. The replacement of the
Morse
condition is a notion of
stability
, cf. Definition
3.6
. In this context, the local energy functional (
1.1
) is defined as

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P;\Omega_{n,R})=\int_{\Omega_{n,R}}|\nabla^{A}P|^{2}+|\nabla H|^{2}+|F_{A}|^{2}+|\mu-\vec{\delta}|^{2}.

for any
\Omega_{n,R}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
. In particular,
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P;\Omega_{n,R})=0
implies that up to gauge,
A
is the trivial connection and
P
is a constant map with values in
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\Crit(H)
.

Here comes the second generalization:

(2)

the gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W,G)
can be infinite-dimensional.

The proofs of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
start with their counterparts for finite-dimensional Landau-Ginzburg models (as toy problems) and are concluded by the following observation.

Proposition 1.6

(Proposition
7.3
&
7.4
)
.

For any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, consider the
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on
\Sigma
with
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s})[\Sigma]=2(d+g(\Sigma)-1)
. Then we can construct an infinite-dimensional gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma))
such that the associated gauged Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}_{z}

) on
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\Sigma

are precisely given by the free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(\Sigma)
-orbits of
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{a}_{k}
with

.

.

From the standpoint of the gauged Witten equations (

1.4.
Future Directions: Fukaya-Seidel Categories

The complex gradient flow equation (
1.2
) is the Cauchy-Riemann equation perturbed by the special Hamiltonian function
H=\im W
and therefore exhibits interesting analytical properties due to the holomorphicity of
W
. Meanwhile, its solutions admit concrete geometric interpretation, suggesting a direct construction of Fukaya-Seidel categories even for the infinite dimensional gauged Landau-Ginzburg model in Proposition
1.6
. This may lead eventually to a bordered monopole theory. To develop the Picard-Lefschetz theory in this context, one may work directly with infinite-dimensional Lagrangian submanifolds as in the work of Nguyen
[
Ngu12
,
Ngu18
]
. But there might be a shortcut:
can we define the Floer cohomology of Lagrangian submanifolds without using boundary conditions?

When
G
is trivial and
\dim M<\infty
, this idea can be partly realized when the Lagrangian submanifold
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is a Lefschetz thimble, i.e. the stable (or unstable) submanifold of a critical point
q\in\Crit(\re W)
. Instead of a strip
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]_{s}
, we look at solutions to the complex gradient flow equation (
1.2
) on the upper half plane:

P:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}\to M

with a Lagrangian boundary condition at
s=0
and with an asymptotic condition as
s\to\infty
: we require the solution
P(t,s)
to converge to some
q\in\Crit(\re W)
uniformly as
s\to\infty
. The study of Fukaya-Seidel categories via this approach has been pioneered by the work of Haydys
[
Hay15
]
and Gaiotto-Moore-Witten
[
GMW15
]
. See also
[
FJY18
,
GMW17
,
KKS16
]
. We give a brief sketch of their proposal in Section
2
. The primary application in their cases is when

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\text{the space of }SL(2,\mathbb{C})\text{ connections on a closed oriented 3-manifold }Y,

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\text{the complex valued Chern-Simons functional},

and the gauged Witten equations (
1.3
) come down to the Haydys-Witten equations on the 5-manifold
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times Y
. This idea goes back at least to the seminar paper
[
DT98
]
by Donaldson-Thomas: if one takes

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\text{the space of $\bar{\partial}$-operators on a complex vector bundle }E\to\mathcal{Y},

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\text{the holomorphic Chern-Simons functional}.

where
\mathcal{Y}
is a compact Calabi-Yau 3-fold, then the gauged Witten equations (
1.3
) recover the
\Spin(7)
-instanton equation on
\mathbb{C}_{z}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

The Seiberg-Witten equations (SWEQ) should serve as the test for their programs in higher dimensions. To pursue a bordered monopole Floer theory along this line, we have to construct

\begin{array}[]{ccccl}\TSigma&\rightsquigarrow&(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma))&\rightsquigarrow&\text{ an $A_{\infty}$ category }\mathcal{A};\\
Y&\rightsquigarrow&\text{(SWEQ) on }\widehat{Y}&\rightsquigarrow&\text{ an $A_{\infty}$ right module $\mathcal{M}(Y)$ over }\mathcal{A};\\
Y^{\prime}&\rightsquigarrow&\text{ (SWEQ) on }\widehat{Y}^{\prime}&\rightsquigarrow&\text{ an $A_{\infty}$ left module $\mathcal{M}(Y^{\prime})$ over }\mathcal{A},\end{array}

where
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
is any
H
-surface, and
(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma))
is the gauged Landau-Ginzburg model in Proposition
1.6
. Here
Y,\ Y^{\prime}
are oriented 3-manifolds with
\partial Y\cong\Sigma\cong(-Y^{\prime})
. Denote by
Y\circ_{h}Y^{\prime}
the closed 3-manifold obtained by gluing
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
along their common boundary. The ultimate goal of this project is to establish a gluing theorem relating
(\mathcal{M}(Y),\mathcal{M}(Y^{\prime}))
with a suitable version of monopole Floer homology of
Y\circ_{h}Y^{\prime}
perturbed by a closed 2-form. The monopole Floer homology group

\HM_{*}(Y,\TSigma)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigoplus\HM_{*}(Y,\mathfrak{a}_{j}),

outlined as in Section
1.2
, should in principle compute the homology of the underlying chain complexes of
\mathcal{M}(Y)
. The group
\HM_{*}(Y,\TSigma)
may not be a topological invariant of
Y
: the full
A_{\infty}
structure might be required to formulate this invariance. A more detailed discussion on this bordered picture will continue in Section
2.3
below.

1.5.
Organization

Section
2
is a continuation of this introduction. The construction of the monopole Floer homology group
\HM_{*}(Y,\mathfrak{a}_{j})
is based on a variant of Lagrangian Floer cohomology as a finite dimensional model. Following the work of Haydys
[
Hay15
]
and Gaiotto-Moore-Witten
[
GMW15
]
, we work instead with “holomorphic upper half planes” with a boundary condition at
s=0
and an asymptotic condition as
s\to\infty
. Also, we introduce Lemma
2.7
&
2.5
as the toy model of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
, and explain their significance for the compactness theorem. The discussion in Section
2
is largely informal; no proofs will be presented.

In Part
II
, we introduce the notion of gauged Landau-Ginzburg models. The focus is on the geometric insights that motivate definitions and proofs in the infinite-dimensional setting. In Section
5
, we prove that point-like solutions must be trivial provided that the superpotential
W
is stable (Theorem
5.1
). In Section
6
, we prove the exponential decay result, Theorem
6.1
, using a Bochner-type formula for the energy density functional.

In the last part of this paper, we construct the fundamental Landau-Ginzburg Model associated to an
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
and prove Theorem
1.3

and
1.4
by generalizing Theorem
5.1
and
6.1
from Part
II
.

2.
A General Overview

In this section, we explain a variant of Lagrangian Floer cohomology defined by counting holomorphic upper half planes, following
[
Hay15
]
and
[
GMW15
]
. This serves as the finite dimensional model of the monopole Floer theory for 3-manifolds with boundary, proposed in the introduction. This analogy is only used as an inspiration or a guideline for future research; it is not our intention to relate these two theories in a precise way. To make it consistent with the existing literature, we will work with
cohomology
instead of homology in this section.

Analytically, the perturbed Seiberg-Witten equations on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma
and the complex gradient flow equation (
2.5
) on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
have many features in common. Lemma
2.7
and Lemma
2.5
below are the counterparts of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
in this toy model. Their proofs are postponed to the next part, where the corresponding results, Theorem
5.1
and
6.1
, are stated and proved for the gauged Witten equations.

2.1.
A Variant of Lagrangian Floer Cohomology

Recall that a Landau-Ginzburg Model is a pair
(M,W)
where

•

(M,\omega,J,g)
is a non-compact complete Kähler manifold with complex structure
J
and Kähler metric
h\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g-i\omega_{M}
. The underlying Riemannian metric is
g
, and
\omega_{M}
is the symplectic form.

•

W:M\to\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic function, called the superpotential.

Since
M
is Kähler,
J
is parallel. Write
W=L+iH
with
L=\re W
and
H=\im W
. Then the Cauchy-Riemann equation
(dW)^{0,1}=0
comes down to

(2.1)

\nabla L+J\nabla H=0,

which says the gradient
\nabla L
is the Hamiltonian vector field of
H
.

A Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W)
is called
Morse
if all critical points of
L
are non-degenerate. We always assume
(M,W)
is Morse in this section. Denote
\Crit(L)
by the set of critical points of
L
. Taking the covariant derivative of
(\ref{CRequation})
yields:

(2.2)

\Hess L+J\circ\Hess H=0.

Since
\Hess H
is a symmetric operator and
J
is skew-symmetric, (
2.2
) implies that

(2.3)

J\circ\Hess L+\Hess L\circ J=0.

For any
q\in\Crit(L)
, denote by
H_{q}^{\pm}\subset T_{q}M
the positive (negative) spectral subspace of
\Hess_{q}L
. Then (
2.3
) implies
J(H^{\pm}_{q})=H^{\mp}_{q}
. In particular, the index of
q
is
(n,n)
if
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M=2n
. Let
U_{q}
and
S_{q}
be the unstable and stable submanifolds of
q
respectively, which are also called Lefschetz thimbles:

\displaystyle U_{q}

\displaystyle=\{x\in M:\exists p:(-\infty,0]_{s}\to M,\partial_{s}p+\nabla L=0,p(0)=x,\lim_{s\to-\infty}p=q\},

\displaystyle S_{q}

\displaystyle=\{x\in M:\exists p:[0,\infty)_{s}\to M,\partial_{s}p+\nabla L=0,p(0)=x,\lim_{s\to\infty}p=q\}.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
Eli97
, Lemma 2.5]
[
Sei03
, Lemma 1.13]
)
.

U_{q}
and
S_{q}
are Lagrangian submanifolds of
(M,\omega_{M})
.

Lemma
2.1
has been well-known for a long time, but the Floer theory of Lefschetz fibrations was only explored after the works of Donaldson
[
Don99
]
and Seidel
[
Sei03
]
; see
[
Sei08
, Remark 16.10]
.

Assumption 2.2
.

To simplify our exposition in this section, we make the following assumptions on
(M,W)
.

•

(M,\omega_{M})
is an exact symplectic manifold, i.e.
\omega_{M}=d\theta_{M}
for a smooth 1-form
\theta_{M}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
called the primitive;

•

The superpotential
W:M\to\mathbb{C}
need not to be proper, but the function
|\nabla H|^{2}:M\to[0,\infty)
is;

•

L
has finitely many critical points
\{q_{1},\cdots,q_{k}\}
on
M
with
H(q_{i})
mutually distinct. ∎

Example 2.3
.

Let
M=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
W=z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{n}^{2},\ (z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then the unique critical point
q
is the origin.
∎

Take a pair of
compact
Lagrangian submanifolds
\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1}\subset M
intersecting transversely. Let
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
be the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space freely generated by the intersection
\mathcal{L}_{0}\cap\mathcal{L}_{1}
:

\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})=\bigoplus_{y\in\mathcal{L}_{0}\cap\mathcal{L}_{1}}\mathbb{F}_{2}\cdot y.

The differential
\partial
on
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
is defined by counting
J
-holomorphic strips of Maslov index
1
and with Lagrangian boundary conditions. They are smooth maps

P:\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]_{s}\to M

satisfying the equation

(2.4)

\partial_{t}P+J\partial_{s}P=0,

along with the boundary conditions
P(\cdot,0)\in\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
P(\cdot,1)\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
. At this point, some assumptions on
M
and
(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
are required to show that
\partial
is well-defined after suitable perturbations, but let us skip these technical steps here.

The equation (
2.4
) can be perturbed by a Hamiltonian function. In our case, we use the imaginary part of
W
:

(2.5)

\partial_{t}P+J\partial_{s}P+\nabla H=0.

The cochain complex
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
is then generated by Hamiltonian chords, which are smooth maps
p:[0,1]_{s}\to M
satisfying the relations

p(0)\in\mathcal{L}_{0},p(1)\in\mathcal{L}_{1},J\partial_{s}p+\nabla H=0.

By (
2.1
), the last condition is equivalent to

(2.6)

0=J(\partial_{s}p+\nabla L),s\in[0,1]_{s}.

i.e.
p
is a downward gradient flowline of
L
.

The Lagrangian Floer cohomology
\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
is the homology of the cochain complex
(\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1}),\partial)
. The underlying idea is an infinite-dimensional Morse theory. The configuration space is the path space

C^{\infty}([0,1],M;\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{p:[0,1]_{s}\to M:p\text{ smooth, }p(0)\in\mathcal{L}_{0},p(1)\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\},

and the Morse function defined on
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,1],M;\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
is the perturbed symplectic action functional:

\mathcal{A}_{H}(p)=\mathcal{A}(p)+\int_{[0,1]_{s}}H\circ p(s)ds.

A path
p
is a critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
if and only if
p
is a Hamiltonian chord. An
\mathbb{R}_{t}
-family of paths
\{p_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\subset C^{\infty}([0,1],M;L_{0},L_{1})
forms a downward gradient flowline of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
precisely when
P(t,s)=p_{t}(s)
solves the equation (
2.5
) on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]_{s}
.

We would like to generalize this setup for certain
non-compact
Lagrangian submanifolds, in particular for the unstable and stable thimbles
U=U_{q}
and
S=S_{q}
at any
q\in\Crit(L)
. This can not be done without further assumptions on the submanifolds
\mathcal{L}_{j},j=0,1
in order to control their geometry at infinity. Instead of making them precise, we give an (incomplete) list of axiomatic properties for these submanifolds:

(1)

there are two classes of non-compact Lagrangian submanifolds,
\mathcal{C}_{un}
and
\mathcal{C}_{st}
, which are of the unstable type and the stable type respectively;

(2)

for any
q\in\Crit(L)
,
U_{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{un}
and
S_{q}\in\mathcal{C}_{st}
, where
U_{q}
and
S_{q}
are Lefschetz thimbles at
q
;

(3)

for any
\mathcal{L}_{U}\in\mathcal{C}_{un}
and
\mathcal{L}_{S}\in\mathcal{C}_{st}
,
L=\re W
is bounded above on
\mathcal{L}_{U}
and below on
\mathcal{L}_{S}
.
\mathcal{L}_{U}
only intersect
\mathcal{L}_{S}
within a compact region of
M
;

(4)

the Lagrangian Floer cohomology
\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},\mathcal{L}_{S})
is well-defined, assuming transversality, by counting Hamiltonian chords and solutions of (
2.5
).

Our goal is to give an alternative construction of
\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})
and
\HFL^{*}(U_{q},\mathcal{L}_{S})
for all
q\in\Crit(L)
, and we focus on the first case for simplicity. Suppose
\mathcal{L}_{U}
is exact, so the primitive
\theta_{M}|_{\mathcal{L}_{U}}=dh
for some function
h:\mathcal{L}_{U}\to\mathbb{R}
. The cochain group
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})
is generated by the finite set
\mathcal{L}_{U}\cap S_{q}
. Each
x\in\mathcal{L}_{U}\cap S_{q}
corresponds to a path
p:[0,\infty)_{s}\to M
such that

\partial_{s}p+\nabla L=0,\ p(0)=x,\ \lim_{s\to\infty}p(s)=q.

The analogy with
(\ref{DGFlow})
suggest that we look at the Morse cohomology of the perturbed action functional:

\mathcal{A}_{H}(p)=-h\big(p(0)\big)+\int_{[0,\infty)_{s}}-p^{*}\theta_{M}+\big(H\circ p(s)-H(q)\big)ds,

defined on the space

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,\infty),M;\mathcal{L}_{U})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{p:[0,\infty)_{s}\to M:p\text{ smooth, }p(0)\in\mathcal{L}_{U},\lim_{s\to\infty}p(s)=q\}.

The differential
\widehat{\partial}
on
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})
is defined by counting solutions to (
2.5
) on the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}
with the boundary condition:

(2.7)

P(t,0)\in\mathcal{L}_{U},\ \lim_{s\to\infty}P(t,s)=q,\ \forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{t}.

To prove
\widehat{\partial}^{2}=0
, it is important to know a compactness property. The proof of the next lemma will be carried out in a forthcoming paper
[
Wan22
]
. It is included here only to motivate our work:

Lemma 2.4

(
[
Wan22
, Lemma 3.7]
)
.

With some additional assumptions on
(M,W)
, for any unstable type Lagrangian submanifold
\mathcal{L}_{U}
, one can find a function
\eta:[0,\infty)_{s}\to[0,\infty)
such that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\eta(s)=0
, and for any solution
P:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\to M
of
(\ref{P-holo})
subject to the boundary condition
(\ref{boundarycondition})
, we have

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}d\big(P(t,s),q\big)\leq\eta(s),

where
d
is the distance function of the Riemannian metric
g
.

This means that the convergence in the boundary condition (
2.7
) is also uniform for all possible solutions
P
. In fact, this decay is exponential. The next lemma is the toy model of Theorem
1.4
when
G
is trivial and
\dim M<\infty
.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exist constants
\epsilon(M,W),\zeta(M,W)>0
with the following significance. Let
P_{1}:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\to M
be any solution of
(\ref{P-holo})
such that for some
q\in\Crit(L)
,

d\big(P_{1}(t,s),q\big)<\epsilon,\forall(t,s)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, then

d\big(P_{1}(t,s),q\big)<e^{-\zeta s},\forall s\geq 0.

The exponent
\zeta(M,W)
is determined by the first positive eigenvalue of
\Hess_{q}L
. To derive the exponential decay of the distance function from Lemma
2.5
, set
P_{1}(t,s)=P(t,s+R)
for some
R\gg 0
in Lemma
2.4
.

Remark 2.6
.

Apparently, Lemma
2.5
holds when
P
is time-independent, since in this case
\{P(t,s)\}_{s\in[0,\infty)_{s}}
is a downward gradient flowline of
L=\re W
for any fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}_{t}
, and
L
is a Morse function. It is not clear to the author whether
\{P(\cdot,s)\}_{s\in[0,\infty)}
forms a downward gradient flowline (in the spatial direction) of some functional in general.
∎

The proof of uniform decay of the distance function in Lemma
2.4
will rely on the following fact:

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
P:\mathbb{C}_{z}\to M
be a solution of
(\ref{P-holo})
on the complex plane with finite energy. If
P(z)\to q
as
z=t+is\to\infty
, then
P\equiv q
.

Remark 2.8
.

As we will see later, for a gauged Landau-Ginzburg model, Lemma
2.7
is not true in general. An appropriate condition that ensures Lemma
2.7
is a notion of stability, cf. Definition
3.6
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

We will prove Lemma
2.7
and Lemma
2.5
in the context of gauged Landau-Ginzburg models in Part
II
, cf. Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
6.1
. Under Assumption
2.2
, the conclusion of Lemma
2.4
will hold, if one can verify an additional local compactness property; see
[
Wan22
, Section 3]
. The analogue of Lemma
2.4
in the context of the Seiberg-Witten equations is
[
Wan20a
, Theorem 6.3]
. The main results of this paper, however, do not relies on this lemma.
∎

2.2.
A Gluing Theorem

The Fukaya-Seidel category associated to the Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W)
is a directed
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{A}
with objects given by the thimbles
\{U_{q}\}_{q\in\Crit(L)}
or
\{S_{q}\}_{q\in\Crit(L)}
. Moreover, for each
\mathcal{L}_{U}\in\mathcal{C}_{un}
and
\mathcal{L}_{S}\in\mathcal{C}_{st}
, we assign:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{U}

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow\text{ an }A_{\infty}\text{-right module over }\mathcal{A},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S}

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow\text{ an }A_{\infty}\text{-left module over }\mathcal{A},

whose underlying cochain complexes are given respectively by

(2.8)

\bigoplus_{q\in\Crit(L)}\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})\text{ and }\bigoplus_{q\in\Crit(L)}\Ch^{*}(U_{q},\mathcal{L}_{S}).

A theorem of Seidel
[
Sei08
, Corollary 18.27]
then suggests a spectral sequence whose
E_{1}
-page is

(2.9)

\bigoplus_{q\in\Crit(L)}\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})\otimes\HFL^{*}(U_{q},\mathcal{L}_{S}),

and which abuts to
\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},\mathcal{L}_{S})
in the
E_{\infty}
-page. The underlying geometric picture was first observed by Donaldson and elaborated later in the monograph
[
GMW15
]
by Gaiotto-Moore-Witten. The Lagrangian Floer cohomology
\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},\mathcal{L}_{S})
is defined by counting holomorphic strips of width
1
with Lagrangian boundary conditions, but one can instead work with strips of width
R
for any
R>0
, and let
R\to\infty
.

This neck-stretching picture
makes more sense with the complex gradient flow equation (
2.5
), instead of (
2.4
). In
[
GMW15
]
, a general framework is proposed to understand the limit of solutions of (
2.5
) on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,R]_{s}
in
C^{\infty}_{loc}
-topology as
R\to\infty
, and also to rebuild the differential on the cochain complex
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},\mathcal{L}_{S})
with
R\gg 1
by gluing all limiting pieces. For instance, the differential on the
E_{0}
-page of (
2.9
) is obtained by gluing a holomorphic upper half plane contributing to
\Ch^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})
with a generator of
\Ch^{*}(U_{q},\mathcal{L}_{S})
, or vice versa. This explains why the
E_{1}
-page of (
2.9
) is a tensor product. We refer interested readers to
[
GMW17
]
for a nice exposition of their proposal.

If the
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{A}
turns out to be trivial, then the spectral sequence collapses after the
E_{1}
-page. There is one simple geometric condition that yields this triviality:

Lemma 2.10
.

If for any
e^{i\theta}\in S^{1}
the downward gradient flowline equation

\partial_{s}p(s)+\nabla\big(\re(e^{-i\theta}W)\big)=0,\ p:\mathbb{R}_{s}\to M

has only constant solutions, then the Fukaya-Seidel category of
(M,W)
is trivial.

As we shall see in Corollary
10.6
, this condition can be verified for the Seiberg-Witten equations for the special case in Remark
1.5
when
\langle\nu,[\Sigma]\rangle\neq 0
.

Although this neck-stretching picture is enlightening to keep in mind, the analytic foundation of the web-based formalism
[
GMW15
,
GMW17
]
is still missing. To implement their proposal for the Seiberg-Witten equations remains a challenging problem.

2.3.
Relations with Gauge Theory

As noted in Section
1.4
, our goal is to define the Floer cohomology for Lagrangian submanifolds without using boundary conditions, and we have achieved this goal partly for thimbles by considering holomorphic upper half planes. (The boundary condition on
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\{1\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]
is now removed). To deal with the remaining boundary component
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, we explain the geometric origin of
\mathcal{L}_{U}
and
\mathcal{L}_{S}
in our primary applications.

Suppose a closed oriented 3-manifold
Z=Y_{L}\ \bigcup_{\Sigma}Y_{R}
is separated by a closed connected surface
\Sigma
with
g(\Sigma)\geq 1
, and

\im\big(H_{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{R})\to H_{1}(Z;\mathbb{R})\big)\neq 0.

This means that
Y_{L},Y_{R}
are oriented 3-manifolds with
\partial Y_{L}\cong\Sigma\cong\partial(-Y_{R})
and
Z
is obtained by gluing
Y_{L}
with
Y_{R}
along their common boundary. Let
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}_{0})
be the infinite-dimensional Kähler manifold associated to
\Sigma
in Proposition
1.6
with
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{0})[\Sigma]=0
. As shown in the work of Nguyen
[
Ngu12
]
, the solution space of 3-dimensional Seiberg-Witten equations on
Y_{L}
(resp.
Y_{R}
), when restricted to
\Sigma
, gives rise to an infinite-dimensional Lagrangian submanifold of
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}_{0})
, which we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{S}
). The monopole Floer cohomology

\HM^{*}(Y_{L},\TSigma)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigoplus_{j}\HM^{*}(Y_{L},\mathfrak{a}_{j})

of
Y_{L}
, as we sketched in Section
1.2
, is the analogue of

\bigoplus_{q\in\Crit{L}}\HFL^{*}(\mathcal{L}_{U},S_{q})

defined using holomorphic upper half planes. By working with the extended 3-manifold

\widehat{Y}_{L}=Y_{L}\ \bigcup\ [0,\infty)_{s}\times\Sigma

and the 4-manifold
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\widehat{Y}_{L}
, the remaining boundary condition along
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
is also removed.

To see the relation of this monopole Floer theory with the monopole knot Floer cohomology, recall the construction from
[
KM10
]
. For any knot
K\subset S^{3}
, take a meridian
m\subset S^{3}\setminus K
. Then the link complement

Y_{K}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}S^{3}\setminus N(m\cup K).

is a 3-manifold with boundary
\partial Y_{K}=\Sigma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
. In this case,
\Sigma
is disconnected, with each
\Sigma_{i}\cong\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Using a suitable orientation reversing diffeomorphism
\varphi:\Sigma_{1}\to\Sigma_{2}
, we close up the boundary of
Y_{K}
and obtain a closed oriented 3-manifold
Z(S^{3},K;\varphi)
. Then define

\KHM^{*}(S^{3},K)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\HM^{*}(Z(S^{3},K;\varphi)),

where
\HM^{*}(Z(S^{3},K;\varphi))
is the monopole Floer cohomology of
Z(S^{3},K;\varphi)
defined using a suitable local coefficient system. It is shown in
[
KM10
]
that
\KHM^{*}(S^{3},K)
is independent of the isotopy class of
\varphi
up to isomorphisms.

On the other hand, one may equip the boundary
\Sigma=\partial Y_{K}
with suitable differential forms to obtain a (disconnected)
H
-surface
\TSigma
, and take
\HM^{*}(Y_{K},\TSigma)
as a candidate of the knot Floer cohomology of
K
. To see its relation with
\KHM^{*}(S^{3},K)
, regard
\varphi
as gluing two pieces:

Y_{K}\text{ and }[0,R]_{s}\times\Sigma_{1}

As
R\to\infty
, we are stretching the metric in a neighborhood of
\Sigma_{1}
in
Z(S^{3},K;\varphi)
, which is similar to the neck stretching picture underlying Seidel’s spectral sequence (
2.9
). The condition of Lemma
2.10
can be verified in this case (Corollary
10.6
), so one may recover
\KHM^{*}(S^{3},K)
from
\HM^{*}(Y_{K},\TSigma)
using an internal gluing theorem. This isomorphism is established in
[
Wan20b
, Theorem 1.9]
using a different argument.

As an ending remark for this expository section, the monopole Floer homology of 3-manifolds with toroidal boundary to be defined in the second paper
[
Wan20a
]
only gives the underlying cochain complexes (
2.8
). The construction of
A_{\infty}
-structures is left as an interesting future project; see
[
Wan22
]
for details.

G

M

4.1
) as the generalization of the Floer equation (

). Theorem

3.
Definitions and Examples

We generalize the setup from the previous section and introduce the notion of gauged Landau-Ginzburg Models.

Definition 3.1
.

The quadruple
(M,W,G,\rho)
is called an (abelian) gauged Landau-Ginzburg model if

(1)

(M,\omega,J,g)
is a complete non-compact Kähler manifold with complex structure
J
and Kähler metric
h\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g-i\omega_{M}
;
g
is the underlying Riemannian metric, and
\omega_{M}
the symplectic form.

(2)

(G,\rho)
is a compact abelian Lie group acting on
M
preserving the Kähler structure, i.e. for any
g\in G
, the action
\rho(g):M\to M
is a holomorphic isometry;

(3)

(G,\rho)
is a Hamiltonian group action, and it admits a moment map:

\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g},

where
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
. Since
G
is abelian,
\mu
is
G
-invariant;

(4)

The action of
(G,\rho)
extends to an action of the complex group
(G_{\mathbb{C}},\rho_{\mathbb{C}})
.
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}:G_{\mathbb{C}}\times M\to M
is holomorphic.
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
does not preserve the Riemannian metric
g
in general.

(5)

W:M\to\mathbb{C}
is a
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant holomorphic function called the superpotential. Write
W=L+iH
with
L=\re W
and
H=\im W
. ∎

Again, we assume
(M,\omega)
is an exact symplectic manifold, i.e.
\omega_{M}=d\theta_{M}
for some
\theta_{M}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
. For any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, let
\tilde{\xi}
be the vector field on
M
induced from the group action
(G,\rho)
:

\tilde{\xi}(p)=\frac{d}{dt}\rho(e^{t\xi})p\bigg|_{t=0}.

We adopt a non-standard (sign) convention of the moment map in this paper:

(3.1)

\iota(\tilde{\xi})\omega=-d\langle\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Since
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)=g(J\cdot,\cdot)
, (
3.1
) is equivalent to

(3.2)

\langle\nabla\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\nabla\langle\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=-J\tilde{\xi},

where
\nabla\mu\in\Gamma(M,TM\otimes\mathfrak{g})
is a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued vector field on
M
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
denotes a bi-invariant metric of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
G=S^{1},G_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{*},M=\mathbb{C}
and
W\equiv 0
. The group action is the standard complex multiplication. Using our sign convention (
3.2
), the moment map
\mu(x)=\frac{i}{2}|x|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Example 3.3
.

Let
G=S^{1},G_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{*},M=S^{2}
and
W\equiv 0
. Identify
M
with
\mathbb{CP}^{1}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. The action
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
is the same as in Example
3.2
.
\{0,\infty\}
is the fixed point set of
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Let
G=S^{1},G_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{*},M=\mathbb{C}^{2}=\{(x,y)|x,y\in\mathbb{C}\}
and
W(x,y)=xy
.
W
becomes
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant if we set the action
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
as

\rho_{\mathbb{C}}(u)(x,y)=(ux,u^{-1}y),

for any
u\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. The moment map is
\mu(x,y)=\frac{i}{2}(|x|^{2}-|y|^{2})
.
∎

Just as in Assumption
2.2
, we wish
(W,\mu)
to satisfy some good properties. The replacement of the Morse condition for gauged Landau-Ginzburg models is a notion of stability. There are two possible candidates; the second one turns out to be more useful.

Definition 3.5
.

A vector
\vec{\delta}\in\mathfrak{g}
is called
W
-stable if
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\Crit(L)
consists of finitely many free
G
-orbits and
L|_{\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})}
is Morse-Bott. In particular, the function induced by
L=\re W
on the symplectic quotient

\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})/G

is a Morse function with finitely many critical points.
∎

However, having a
W
-stable vector
\vec{\delta}
is not sufficient to deduce the triviality of point-like solutions in Theorem
5.1
(see Example
5.8
below). Note that the critical set
\Crit(L)=\{x\in M:\nabla L(x)=0\}
is closed and
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant.

Definition 3.6
.

The superpotential
W
is called stable if
\Crit(L)
consists of finitely many closed free
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit and
L
is Morse-Bott. In particular,
\ker\Hess_{x}L=T_{x}(G_{\mathbb{C}}\cdot x)
for all
x\in\Crit(L)
.
∎

In fact, any
\vec{\delta}\in\mathfrak{g}
is
W
-stable if
W
is stable. In Example
3.2
, any
\delta\in i(0,\infty)
is
W
-stable, but
W
itself is not a stable superpotential. Indeed,
\Crit(L)=M
consists of two
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits, one of which is not free. In Example
3.4
,
L
has a unique critical point
q=(0,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
with trivial
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-action. If we set instead
G=\{e\}
, then
W
is stable and
\vec{\delta}=0
is
W
-stable. Now we provide a more interesting example.

Example 3.7

(The Fundamental Toy Model)
.

Let
G=S^{1},G_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{*},M=\mathbb{C}^{3}=\{(x,y,b):x,y,b\in\mathbb{C}\}
and
W_{\lambda}(x,y,b)=(xy-\lambda)b
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is a fixed parameter. The
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-action
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
is defined as

\rho_{\mathbb{C}}(u)(x,y,b)=(ux,u^{-1}y,b),\ \forall u\in\mathbb{C}^{*}.

The moment map is
\mu(x,y,b)=\frac{i}{2}(|x|^{2}-|y|^{2})
and
\nabla L=(\bar{y}\bar{b},\bar{x}\bar{b},\bar{x}\bar{y}-\bar{\lambda})
. If
\lambda\neq 0
, then
\Crit(L)=\{b=0,xy=\lambda\}
consists of a single
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, and the superpotential
W
is stable. If
\lambda=0
, then
\Crit(L)=A_{xy}\cup A_{xb}\cup A_{yb}
where

A_{xy}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{x=0,y=0\},etc.

So
W
is not stable.
\vec{\delta}\in i\mathbb{R}
is
W
-stable if and only if
\vec{\delta}\neq 0
. For instance, take
\vec{\delta}\in i\cdot(0,\infty)
. If
(x,y,b)\in\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})
, then
x\neq 0
and so

\Crit(L)\cap\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})=\{(x,0,0):\frac{i}{2}|x|^{2}=\vec{\delta}\}\subset A_{yb},

which consists of a single free
G
-orbit.
Moreover, we compute
\Hess L
at
(x,y,b)\in M
:

\Hess L(v)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bar{b}&\bar{y}\\
\bar{b}&0&\bar{x}\\
\bar{y}&\bar{x}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{x}^{\prime}\\
\bar{y}^{\prime}\\
\bar{b}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\text{ with }v=\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
y^{\prime}\\
b^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in T_{(x,y,b)}M,

so
L
is Morse-Bott away from the origin. Note that the
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
(x,0,0)
is not closed. Its closure contains the origin.
∎

4.
The Gauged Witten equations

In this section, we introduce gauged Witten equations and explain its relation with downward gradient flow of the gauged action functional
\mathcal{A}_{H}
. This serves as a toy model for the Floer theory to be developed in the second paper
[
Wan20a
]
of this series. Some results are stated and proved only for inspiration; they are not closely related to the proof of Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
in the end.

4.1.
The Gauged Action Functional

Let
(M,W,G,\rho)
be a gauged Landau-Ginzburg model and take
\vec{\delta}\in\mathfrak{g}
to be
W
-stable (in the sense of Definition
3.5
). Take
\mathcal{L}_{U}\subset\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})
to be a
G
-invariant Lagrangian submanifold of
M
such that
L=\re W
is bounded above on
\mathcal{L}_{U}
. Since
\vec{\delta}
is
W
-stable,
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\Crit(L)
consists of finitely many free
G
-orbits; let them be
O_{1},\cdots,O_{k}
. Choose a reference point
q\in O_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq k
.

Assumption 4.1
.

We first summarize the assumptions we need in order to formally set up a Floer theory:

•

the Kähler form
\omega_{M}
is exact, i.e.
\omega_{M}=d\theta_{M}
for some
\theta_{M}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
;

•

the Lagrangian submanifold
\mathcal{L}_{U}
is exact, i.e. the primitive
1
-form
\theta_{M}|_{\mathcal{L}_{U}}=dh
for some smooth function
h:\mathcal{L}_{U}\to\mathbb{R}
;

•

the function
|\nabla H|^{2}+|\mu|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is proper;

•

the superpotential
W
is stable and
\vec{\delta}\in\mathfrak{g}
is
W
-stable.
∎

Let
Y=[0,\infty)_{s}
and
X=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times Y=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
. Consider a smooth map
P:X\to M
and a connection
A=d+a
of the trivial principal
G
-bundle
Q
over
X
:

Q=X\times G.

Write the connection 1-form
a
as
a_{t}dt+a_{s}ds
with
a_{t},a_{s}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+},\mathfrak{g})
. The smooth map
P
can be differentiated covariantly with respect to
A
:

\nabla^{A}_{V}P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}V\cdot P+\tilde{a}(V)

for any tangent vector
V\in TX
. Here
\tilde{a}(V)
is the induced tangent vector of
a(V)\in\mathfrak{g}
.

We are interested in the gauged Witten equations on
X=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
with boundary values in
\mathcal{L}_{U}
:

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-*_{2}F_{A}+\mu&=\vec{\delta},\\
\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P+J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P+\nabla H&=0,\\
P(t,0)&\in\mathcal{L}_{U}.\end{array}\right.

The first equation is a moment map constraint. The second one is the Cauchy-Riemann equation perturbed by the Hamiltonian
H=\im W
. When
H\equiv 0
, the first two equations of (
4.1
) reduce to the symplectic vortex equation
[
CGMiRS02
,
MiR03
]
discovered by Cieliebak-Gaio-Salamon
[
CGS00
]
and independently by Ignasi Munde i Rierra
[
MiR00
]
. The gauged Witten equations (
4.1
) can be viewed as a formal downward gradient flow equation in an infinite-dimensional space, as we explain now.

For either
Z=Y
or
X
, let
\mathcal{A}(Z)=d+\Gamma_{0}(Z,T^{*}Z\otimes\mathfrak{g})
be the space of smooth connections with decay in the spatial direction with

(4.2)

\Gamma_{0}(Z,T^{*}Z\otimes\mathfrak{g})=\{a\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(Z,T^{*}Z\otimes\mathfrak{g}):\lim_{s\to\infty}a=0\text{ and }\langle a,ds\rangle=0\text{ at }s=0\}.

A smooth map
p:Z\to M
can be viewed as a section of the trivial
M
bundle over
Z
:

\widetilde{M}=Z\times M=(Z\times G)\times_{G}M.

Consider the space of smooth sections of
\widetilde{M}\to Z
subject to the Lagrangian boundary condition at
s=0
and a asymptotic condition as
s\to\infty
:

(4.3)

\Gamma_{0}(Z,\widetilde{M};\mathcal{L}_{U})=\{p:Z\to\widetilde{M}:\lim_{s\to\infty}p(s)=q\text{ and }p(0)\in\mathcal{L}_{U}\}.

A gauge transformation must converge to the identity element
e
of
G
as
s\to\infty
:

(4.4)

\mathcal{G}(Z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Map_{0}(Z,G)=\{u:Z\to G:\lim_{s\to\infty}u=e\in G\}.

The configuration space is
\mathcal{C}(Z)=\mathcal{A}(Z)\times\Gamma_{0}(Z,\widetilde{M};\mathcal{L}_{U})
with
\mathcal{G}(Z)
acting on by the formula:

u(A,p)=(A-u^{-1}du,u\cdot p).

Remark 4.2
.

When
Z=X
, the 1-form
a
in (
4.2
) is required to decay smoothly to zero as
s\to\infty
and uniformly for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{t}
. More concretely, this means

\|\partial_{t}^{k}\partial_{s}^{l}a(\cdot,n+\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,1]_{s})}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
for all
k,l
. The same holds for the convergence in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
).
∎

Definition 4.3
.

The gauged action functional
\mathcal{A}_{H}
is defined on
\mathcal{C}(Y)
with
Y=[0,+\infty)_{s}
as:

(4.5)

\mathcal{A}_{H}(d+a,p)=-h(p(0))-\int_{Y}p^{*}\theta_{M}+\int_{Y}H\circ p(s)ds+\langle a,\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

where
\langle a,\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\langle a_{s},\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}ds
is understood as an 1-form on
Y
and
\theta_{M}=dh
on
\mathcal{L}_{U}
.
∎

For any
\gamma=(A,p)\in\mathcal{C}(Y)
, a tangent vector
(\delta a,\delta p)
in
T_{\gamma}\mathcal{C}(Y)
consists of a smooth form
\delta a\in\Gamma_{0}(Y,T^{*}Y\otimes\mathfrak{g})
and a vector field
\delta p
along the image
p(Y)
:

\delta p\in\Gamma_{0}(Y,p^{*}TM;\mathcal{L}_{U}).

The tangent space
T_{\gamma}\mathcal{C}(Y)
inherits a
\mathcal{G}
-invariant
L^{2}
-inner product from the Riemannian metric
g
of
M
. We compute the
L^{2}
-formal gradient of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
:

Lemma 4.4
.

\grad\mathcal{A}_{H}(d+a,p)=(\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p,J\nabla^{A}_{\partial s}p+\nabla H)
.

Proof.

Let
P:[0,1]_{t}\times Y\to M
be a smooth map such that
P(0,s)=p(s)
,
\partial_{t}P(0,s)=\delta p(s)
and
\lim_{s\to\infty}P(\cdot,s)=q
. Then
\gamma_{t}=(d+a+t\delta a,P(t,\cdot))
is a smooth variation of
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
. Note that

(4.6)

\int_{[0,t]\times Y}P^{*}\omega_{M}=\int_{[0,t]\times Y}dP^{*}\theta_{M}=h\big(P(t,0)\big)-h\big(P(0,0)\big)+\int_{\{t\}\times Y}P^{*}\theta_{M}-\int_{\{0\}\times Y}P^{*}\theta_{M},

which is computed also as

(4.7)

\int_{[0,t]\times Y}P^{*}\omega_{M}=\int_{[0,t]\times Y}\omega_{M}(\partial_{t}P,\partial_{s}P)dt^{\prime}ds=-\int_{[0,t]}dt^{\prime}\int_{Y}g(\partial_{t}P,J\partial_{s}P)ds.

Now compute the variation of
(\ref{areafunctional})
along a tangent vector
(\delta p,\delta a)
using (
4.6
) and (
4.7
):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{A}_{H}(\gamma_{t})|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}g(\delta p,J\partial_{s}P+\nabla H)ds+\langle\delta a,\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\nabla\mu,\delta p\otimes a\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}g(\delta p,J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P+\nabla H)ds+\langle\delta a,\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

where we used the relation
\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P=\partial_{s}P+J\langle\nabla\mu,a_{s}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

\mathcal{A}_{H}
is
\mathcal{G}(Y)
-invariant.

Proof.

Since elements of
\mathcal{G}(Y)
are subject to the asymptotic condition
\lim_{s\to\infty}u=e
,
\mathcal{G}(Y)
is contractible. It suffices to consider the infinitesimal action. The Lie algebra of
\mathcal{G}
is

\Lie(\mathcal{G})=\Gamma_{0}(Y,\mathfrak{g})=\{\xi:Y\to\mathfrak{g}:\lim_{s\to\infty}\xi(s)=0\}.

For
\xi\in\Lie(\mathcal{G})
, the tangent vector generated at
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(Y)
is

(4.8)

\bd_{\gamma}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(-\partial_{s}\xi,\tilde{\xi})=(-\partial_{s}\xi,J\langle\nabla\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}).

It suffices to verify this vector is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to
\grad\mathcal{A}_{H}
. For any path
p\in\Gamma_{0}(Y,\widetilde{M};\mathcal{L}_{U})
,
\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p(s)=0
for
s=0
and
\infty
. Hence, the boundary terms do not contribute to the integration by parts below:

\displaystyle\int_{Y}\langle\vec{\delta}-\mu\circ p,-\partial_{s}\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{Y}\langle\partial_{s}(\mu\circ p),\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=-\int_{Y}\langle\nabla\mu,\partial_{s}p\otimes\xi\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

On the other hand, we use Lemma
B.1
(
5
)(
6
) from Appendix
B
to compute:

\int_{Y}\big\langle J\nabla^{A}_{\partial s}p+\nabla H,J\langle\nabla\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big\rangle=\int_{Y}\langle\nabla\mu,\partial_{s}p\otimes\xi\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.\qed

Remark 4.6
.

In the expression (
4.5
), the first two terms come from the usual action functional, motivated by the integration by parts (
4.6
). The third part comes from the Hamiltonian perturbation. The last one is added by requiring
\mathcal{A}_{H}
to be gauge-invariant.
∎

Hence, the gauged Witten equations (
4.1
) is cast into the form

\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}+\grad\mathcal{A}_{H}(\gamma_{t})=0

with
\gamma_{t}=(d+a_{s}(t,\cdot)ds,P(t,\cdot))\in\mathcal{C}(Y)
when
a_{t}\equiv 0
. There is a classical notion of analytic energy associated to any downward gradient flow equation:

(4.9)

\displaystyle\infty>\mathcal{E}_{an}(\{\gamma_{t}\})

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{H}(\gamma_{t})+\lim_{t\to-\infty}\mathcal{A}_{H}(\gamma_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{t}}\langle-\partial_{t}\gamma_{t},\grad\mathcal{A}_{H}(\gamma_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{t}}|\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}|^{2}+|\grad\mathcal{A}_{H}(\gamma_{t})|^{2}\geq 0.

This formula is only valid when
A
is in the temporal gauge, i.e. when
a_{t}\equiv 0
. On the contrary, the gauged Witten equations (
4.1
) are invariant under a larger gauge group
\mathcal{G}(X)
(recall that
X=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times Y
). In fact, the left hand side of (
4.1
) defines a
\mathcal{G}(X)
-equivariant map:

\mathfrak{F}:\mathcal{C}(X)\to\Gamma_{0}(X,Q\times_{G}(TM\oplus\mathfrak{g})),

called the gauged Witten map.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
X=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}
. For any
(A,P)\in\mathcal{C}(X)
, define

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P\in\Gamma(X,P^{*}TM),

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P\in\Gamma(X,P^{*}TM),

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-*_{2}F_{A}\circ P\in\Gamma(X,\mathfrak{g}).

Then define the analytic energy of
(A,P)
as

(4.10)

\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P)=\int_{X}|T|^{2}+|JS+\nabla H|^{2}+|F|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2},

which is invariant under
\mathcal{G}(X)
and reduces to
\mathcal{E}_{an}(\{\gamma_{t}\})
in (
4.9
) in the temporal gauge.
∎

In terms of the shorthands introduced in Definition
4.7
, the equation (
4.1
) takes a more compact form:

(4.11a)

\displaystyle F+\mu

\displaystyle=\vec{\delta},

(4.11b)

\displaystyle T+JS+\nabla H

\displaystyle=0,

(4.11c)

\displaystyle P(t,0)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{L}_{U}.

We are interested in the moduli space of solutions of (
4.1
) with finite analytic energy. It is possible to impose a gauge-fixing condition, produce an elliptic theory and finally construct a Morse complex in this context. However, the full construction is carried out in
[
Wan20a
]
only for an infinite-dimensional case in which the gauged Witten equations (
4.1
) are identified with the Seiberg-Witten equations.

4.2.
The Extended Hessian

Although we will only get into linear analysis in the second paper
[
Wan20a
]
, it is enlightening to first work out the extended Hessian of the gauged action functional
\mathcal{A}_{H}
. The discussion below will be used in
[
Wan20a
, Section 11]
when the essential spectrum of the extended Hessian is computed for the perturbed Chern-Simons-Dirac functional on a 3-manifold with cylindrical ends.

At any
\gamma=(A,p)\in\mathcal{C}(Y)
, the linearized gauge action

\displaystyle\bd_{\gamma}:\Lie(\mathcal{G})=\Gamma_{0}(Y,\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle\to T_{\gamma}\mathcal{C}(Y)

defined by the formula (
4.8
) has a formal
L^{2}
-adjoint:

\displaystyle\bd_{\gamma}^{*}:T_{\gamma}\mathcal{C}(Y)

\displaystyle\to\Gamma_{0}(Y,\mathfrak{g}),

\displaystyle\big((\delta a_{s})ds,\delta p\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\partial_{s}(\delta a_{s})+\langle J\nabla\mu,\delta p\rangle.

By linearizing the expression in Proposition
4.4
, we obtain the Hessian of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
at
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}_{H}:T_{\gamma}\mathcal{C}(Y)

\displaystyle\to T_{\gamma}\mathcal{C}(Y),

\displaystyle\big((\delta a_{s})ds,\delta p\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\big(-\langle\nabla\mu,\delta p\rangle,J(\partial_{s}\delta p)-\langle\nabla\mu,a_{s}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+\Hess H(\delta p)\big).

The key observation is that these operators can be combined into a larger operator, the extended Hessian of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
, which is formally
L^{2}
-self-adjoint:

\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\textbf{d}_{\gamma}^{*}\\
\textbf{d}_{\gamma}&\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}_{H}\end{pmatrix}:\mathcal{H}^{2}_{1}\to L^{2}\big(Y,\mathfrak{g}\oplus(T^{*}Y\otimes\mathfrak{g})\oplus p^{*}TM\big),

where
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{1}
is a subspace of
L^{2}_{1}
-sections:

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{(f,(\delta a_{s})ds,\delta p)\in L^{2}_{1}\big(Y,\mathfrak{g}\oplus(T^{*}Y\otimes\mathfrak{g})\oplus p^{*}TM\big):\delta a_{s}(0)=0,\delta p(0)\in p^{*}T\mathcal{L}_{U}\}.

The operator
\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}
is cast into the form
\sigma(\partial_{s}+\widehat{D}_{p(s)})
with

(4.12)

\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&J\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\widehat{D}_{p(s)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\langle\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{T_{p(s)}M}\\
0&0&\langle J\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{T_{p(s)}M}\\
\langle\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}&\langle J\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}&\Hess L\end{pmatrix},

where we identify
T^{*}Y\otimes\mathfrak{g}
with the trivial
\mathfrak{g}
-bundle over
Y
(still denoted by
\mathfrak{g}
) by omitting the 1-form
ds
. The operator
\widehat{D}
is a self-adjoint endomorphism on the bundle

\widehat{TM}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathfrak{g}\oplus TM\to M.

Moreover,
\sigma
defines an almost complex structure on
\widehat{TM}
, which anti-commutes with
\widehat{D}
, i.e.

\sigma^{2}=-\Id,\ \sigma\widehat{D}+\widehat{D}\sigma=0.

The operator
\widehat{D}
is tied to the stability of
W
by the following observation:

Lemma 4.8
.

The superpotential
W
is stable in the sense of Definition
3.6
if and only if
\widehat{D}_{q}
is invertible at all
q\in\Crit(L)
and
\Crit(L)
consists of finitely many closed free
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits.

The linear analysis in
[
Wan20a
, Section 11]
relies essentially on this special structure of the extended Hessian
\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}
. As a preview, the essential spectrum of
\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}
is

(-\infty,-\lambda_{1}]\cup[\lambda_{1},+\infty)

where
\lambda_{1}
is the first non-negative eigenvalue of
\widehat{D}_{q}
. In particular,
\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}
is Fredholm if and only if
\lambda_{1}>0
.

We end this section with a remark on the domain
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{1}
of
\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}
. A section
(f,(\delta a_{s})ds,\delta p)\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{1}
is subject to the boundary condition:

\big(f(0),\delta a_{s}(0),\delta p(0)\big)\in\mathfrak{g}\oplus\{0\}\oplus p^{*}T\mathcal{L}_{U}\text{ at }s=0,

which is a Lagrangian subspace with respect to
\sigma
; otherwise,
\widehat{\Hess}_{\gamma}
is not formally
L^{2}
-self-adjoint. This is why we imposed the boundary condition

\langle a,ds\rangle=0\text{ at }s=0,

in the definition (
4.2
) of
\mathcal{A}(Z)
. Otherwise,
\bd_{\gamma}^{*}
is not the
L^{2}
-formal adjoint of
\bd_{\gamma}
.

5.
Point-Like Solutions

In this section, we study finite energy solutions of (
4.1
) on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, the so-called point-like solutions in terms of
[
GMW15
, Section 14.1]
. Assuming
W
is a stable superpotential, we will prove that all point-like solutions are trivial, i.e. they are gauge equivalent to the constant solutions. Interesting solutions may occur if
W
is not stable, cf. Example
5.8
.

Let
P:\mathbb{C}\to M
be a smooth map and
A
be a smooth connection in the trivial principal
G
-bundle
\mathbb{C}\times G\to\mathbb{C}
. Write
z=t+is
for the coordinate function on
\mathbb{C}
. In the sequel, we shall frequently use the shorthands from Definition
4.7
. The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
(M,W,G,\rho)
is a gauged Landau-Ginzburg model and
W
is stable in the sense of Definition
3.6
. Take any
\vec{\delta}\in\im\mu\subset\mathfrak{g}
. Suppose
(A,P)
is a solution of the gauged Witten equations

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}-*F_{A}+\mu=\vec{\delta},\\
\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P+J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P+\nabla H=0,\end{array}\right.

on
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P)<\infty
, and
(A,P)
is subject to the asymptotic condition

(5.2)

\lim_{z\to\infty}P(z)\to q,

for some base point
q\in\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\Crit(L)
,
then
(A,P)
is gauge equivalent to the constant solution
(A_{0}=d,P\equiv q)
.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is based on an interesting observation. Since
W
is holomorphic and
P
is
J
-holomorphic up to Hamiltonian perturbations, it is reasonable to ask if the composition:

W\circ P:\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{P}M\xrightarrow{W}\mathbb{C},

is still holomorphic. In fact, we have

Lemma 5.2
.

If
(A,P)
is a solution to the gauged Witten equations
(\ref{equation on C})
on
\mathbb{C}
, then

\bar{\partial}(W\circ P)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\partial_{t}+i\partial_{s})(W\circ P)=-i|\nabla H|^{2}.

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Riemann equation
\nabla L=-J\nabla H
and
(\ref{equation on C})
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}(W\circ P)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla L+i\nabla H,\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P+J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P\rangle=-i|\nabla H|^{2}.\qed

Remark 5.3
.

When
A=d
is the trivial connection and
\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P\equiv 0
,
P(t,\cdot)
is a downward gradient flowline of
L
. In this case, this lemma recovers the usual identity:

\partial_{s}(L\circ P)=-|\nabla L|^{2}.

P(t,\cdot)
is also a Hamiltonian flow, so
\partial_{s}(H\circ P)=0.

∎

We also need a more useful notion of energy:

Lemma 5.4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
5.1
, define

(5.3)

\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P;\mathbb{C})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\nabla_{A}P|^{2}+|\nabla H|^{2}+|F_{A}|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2}.

Then
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P;\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P)<\infty
.

Proof.

Using the shorthands from Definition
4.7
and the Cauchy-Riemann equation (
2.1
), we deduce that:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}}|JS+\nabla H|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|S+\nabla L|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|S|^{2}+|\nabla L|^{2}+\lim_{t^{\prime}\to\infty}\lim_{s^{\prime}\to\infty}\int_{[-t^{\prime},t^{\prime}]\times[-s^{\prime},s^{\prime}]}2\langle S,\nabla L\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|S|^{2}+|\nabla L|^{2}+\lim_{t^{\prime}\to\infty}\lim_{s^{\prime}\to\infty}\int_{[-t^{\prime},t^{\prime}]}2(L\circ P(t,s^{\prime})-L\circ P(t,-s^{\prime})).

By (
5.2
), the boundary term tends to zero as
s^{\prime}\to\infty
, so
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P;\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P)
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
5.1
,
\nabla L\equiv 0
, so
P(z)\in\Crit(L)
for all
z=t+is\in\mathbb{C}
and
W\circ P
is a constant function on
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Since
W
is Morse-Bott, for some
G
-invariant neighborhood
\Omega
of
G\cdot q\subset M
and
C>0
, the estimate

(5.4)

|W(x)-W(q)|\leq C|\nabla H(x)|^{2},

holds for any
x\in\Omega
. By the asymptotic condition
(\ref{boundarycondition2})
, for a large constant
R(\Omega)>0
,
P(z)\in\Omega
for all
|z|>R
. As a result,

(5.5)

|W\circ P(z)-W(q)|\leq C\big|\nabla H\big(P(z)\big)\big|^{2},

when
|z|>R
. Write
(W\circ P)(z)-W(q)=U+iV
with
U,V
real. Then Lemma
5.2
implies that

\partial_{t}U-\partial_{s}V=0,\partial_{t}V+\partial_{s}U=-|\nabla H|^{2}\leq 0.

Let
K(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{0}^{z}Vdt+Uds
. By the first equation above, this integral is independent of the path we choose. Therefore,

U=\partial_{s}K,V=\partial_{t}K\text{ and }\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}K=(-\partial_{s}^{2}-\partial_{t}^{2})K=|\nabla H|^{2}\geq 0.

Then the Morse-Bott inequality (
5.5
) is equivalent to
|\nabla K|=|W\circ P-W(q)|\leq C|\Delta K|.

Our goal is to show
K\equiv 0
. Let
Z(r)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\partial B(0,r)}\Delta K\geq 0
. Take
r>R(\Omega)
and integrate by parts:

\displaystyle 0\leq E(r)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{0}^{r}Z(r^{\prime})dr^{\prime}=\int_{B(0,r)}\Delta K=\bigg|\int_{\partial B(0,r)}\vec{n}\cdot\nabla K\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{\partial B(0,r)}\Delta K\bigg)\leq CE(r)^{\prime}.

Therefore, for any
r>r_{0}>R(\Omega)
,

(5.6)

0\leq E(r_{0})\leq E(r)e^{\frac{r_{0}-r}{C}}.

Let
r\to\infty
. Note that
\lim_{r\to\infty}E(r)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\nabla H|^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,P;\mathbb{C})<\infty
by Lemma
5.4
. Hence,
E(r_{0})\equiv 0
, and

\Delta K=|\nabla H|^{2}\equiv 0\Rightarrow W\circ P(z)\equiv W(q).\qed

Remark 5.6
.

The proof of Lemma
5.5
does not require
W
to be stable. It suffices to assume that
W
is Morse-Bott near the critical
G
-orbit
G\cdot q
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Since
W
is stable, the multiplication
g\mapsto g\cdot q
defines a closed embedding
\iota
of
G_{\mathbb{C}}
into
M
. By Lemma
5.5
,
\im P\subset\im\iota
, so
P(z)=g(z)\cdot q
for a unique element
g(z)\in G_{\mathbb{C}}
.

We first deal with the case when
G=S^{1}
and
G_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Since we are interested in solutions modulo gauge equivalences,
g(z)
may be assumed to be real. Suppose
g(z)=e^{\alpha(z)}
for some
\alpha:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
. The boundary condition (
5.2
) then says

(5.7)

\lim_{z\to\infty}\alpha(z)=0.

Moreover, the second equation of (
5.1
) implies
A=d+i*_{2}d\alpha.
Plug this into the first equation of (
5.1
) to obtain that

(5.8)

i\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\alpha+\big(\mu(e^{\alpha(z)}\cdot q)-\mu(q)\big)=0.

By (
5.7
),
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}}|\alpha(z)|
is attained at some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and let
\beta\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha(z_{0})\neq 0
. Then

\langle\mu(e^{\beta}\cdot q)-\mu(q),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=-\langle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\alpha(z_{0}),\beta\rangle\leq 0.

We claim that the inner product
\langle\mu(e^{\beta}\cdot q)-\mu(q),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
is always non-negative. Indeed,

\displaystyle\langle\mu(e^{\beta}\cdot q)-\mu(q),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\partial_{t}\mu(e^{t\beta}\cdot q),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}dt=\int^{1}_{0}\big\langle\langle\nabla\mu(e^{t\beta}\cdot q),\tilde{\beta}\rangle_{TM},i\beta\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Here
\tilde{\beta}_{x}\in T_{x}M
denotes the tangent vector
\frac{d}{dt}(e^{\beta}\cdot x)\big|_{t=0}
at
x\in M
. Since the
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-action is holomorphic (by Definition
3.1
), we set
\xi=i\beta
in (
3.2
) to obtain
\tilde{\beta}_{x}=\langle\nabla\mu(x),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
. Hence,

\langle\mu(e^{\beta}\cdot q)-\mu(q),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\int^{1}_{0}|\langle\nabla\mu(e^{t\beta}\cdot q),i\beta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}|^{2}dt\geq 0.

Since the base point
q
generates a free
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, when
\beta\neq 0
, this integrand is positive. So
\beta=0
. When
G
is a general abelian Lie group, the proof can proceed in a similar fashion.
∎

We end this section with a few examples.

Example 5.7
.

In our Fundamental Example
3.7
, suppose
\lambda=r_{+}r_{-},\ q=(r_{+},r_{-},0)
and
\vec{\delta}=\frac{i}{2}(r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2})
for some
r_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the equation (
5.8
) becomes

\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\big(r_{+}^{2}(e^{2\alpha}-1)+r_{-}^{2}(1-e^{-2\alpha})\big)=0.\qed

Example 5.8
.

For Example
3.2
, the gauged Witten equations come down to the vortex equation on
\mathbb{C}
(with
\delta=\frac{i}{2}
):

(5.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}\bar{\partial}_{A}P=0,\\
i*F_{A}+\frac{1}{2}(|P|^{2}-1)=0.\end{array}\right.

By
[
Tau80a
]
, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{n}
with
\mathcal{E}_{an}=2\pi n
is
\Sym^{n}\mathbb{C}
for any
n\geq 1
, so Theorem
5.1
fails.
W
is not stable in this case, even though
\delta=\frac{i}{2}
is
W
-stable. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is regular; its dimension agrees with the prediction of the index theory.
∎

Example 5.9
.

In Example
3.7
, let
\lambda=0
and
\vec{\delta}=\frac{i}{2}
. For a solution
(A,P)
of (
5.1
), write
P(z)=(x(z),y(z),b(z))
. Then Lemma
5.5
and Remark
5.6
imply
y(z)\equiv b(z)\equiv 0
. The equations are reduced to the previous example. However, in this case, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{\prime}
is not regular. Its formal dimension is always zero for any
n\geq 0
.
∎

6.
Exponential Decay in the Spatial Direction

In this section, we generalize Lemma
2.5
in the context of gauged Landau-Ginzburg models, as the analogue of Theorem
1.4
in the finite-dimensional case. We state and prove the theorem for the energy density function.

Theorem 6.1
.

For any stable gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W,G,\rho)
, there exist
\epsilon(M,W),\zeta(M,W)>0
with following significance. Given a solution
\gamma=(A,P)\in\mathcal{C}(X)
to the gauged Witten equations
(\ref{generalized-vortex-equation})
on the upper half plane
X=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}
, suppose the point-wise estimate

(6.1)

U_{\gamma}(t,s)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}|\nabla^{A}P|^{2}+|\nabla H|^{2}+|F_{A}|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2}<\epsilon

holds for all
(t,s)\in X
. Then

U_{\gamma}(t,s)<e^{-\zeta s},\ \forall s\geq 0.

The function
U_{\gamma}:X\to[0,\infty)
is called the energy density function.

Recall from Section
4.1
that any configuration
(A,P)\in\mathcal{C}(X)
is subject to the asymptotic condition

\lim_{s\to\infty}P(\cdot,s)\to q,

for some base point
q\in\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\Crit(L)
. Under the assumption of Theorem
6.1
, the energy density
U_{\gamma}
then provides an upper bound for the distance function:

(6.2)

U_{\gamma}(t,s)\geq|\nabla H|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2}\geq\epsilon^{\prime}\cdot\big(d(P(t,s),O_{*})\big)^{2},

when
0<\epsilon\ll 1
. Indeed, the tangent space
T_{x}M
can be decomposed orthogonally as
V_{0}\oplus JV_{\mathfrak{g}}\oplus V_{\mathfrak{g}}
with
V_{\mathfrak{g}}=T_{x}(G\cdot q)
and
JV_{\mathfrak{g}}\oplus V_{\mathfrak{g}}=T_{x}(G_{\mathbb{C}}\cdot x)
for all
x\in M
. At
x=q
, identify a normal neighborhood of
q
with a ball
\Omega\subset T_{q}M
centered at the origin and impose the gauge fixing condition
P(t,s)\in\Omega\cap(V_{0}\oplus JV_{\mathfrak{g}})_{q}\subset T_{q}M
when
\epsilon\ll 1
. This can be done since the function
|\nabla H|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is proper by Assumption
4.1
. Within this gauge slice, the map

x\mapsto(\nabla H,\vec{\delta}-\mu)

can be treated as a section of
\Gamma(\Omega\cap(V_{0}\oplus JV_{\mathfrak{g}})_{q},V_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g})
with a transverse zero at the origin by our stability condition. This implies the distance estimate (
6.2
). In particular, Theorem
6.1
implies Lemma
2.5
when
G=\{e\}
is trivial.

Remark 6.2
.

The analogue of Lemma
2.4
(the uniform
L^{\infty}
decay) continues to hold for the gauged Witten equation if
H(q_{j})\neq H(q_{k})
for any
q_{j},q_{k}
in different critical
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits and if an additional local compactness property can be verified. This uniform decay can be further improved into a uniform exponential decay using Theorem
6.1
. The proof of such a uniform decay result will be carried out for the Seiberg-Witten equations in
[
Wan20a
, Theorem 6.3]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

By the gauged Witten equations (
4.1
), it suffices to show the exponential decay for the quantity

(6.3)

u(t,s)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}|\nabla^{A}P|^{2}+|F_{A}|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}.

We use a lemma from Appendix
A
and verify its conditions:

Lemma 6.3

(Corollary
A.2
)
.

Take
\zeta>0
. Suppose
w:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-function on the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
such that

(U1)

(\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}+\zeta^{2})w\leq 0
, and

(U2)

for some
K>0
,
w(t,0)\leq K
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{t}
.

Then
w(t,s)\leq Ke^{-\zeta s}
for all
(t,s)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
.

The second condition
(U2)
holds for the function
u
in (
6.3
) by our assumption on
U_{\gamma}
in (
6.1
):

u(t,s)\leq U_{\gamma}(t,s)<\epsilon.

To verify
(U1)
, we find an explicit formula of
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}u(s,t)
. It is convenient to define a bundle map
D:TM\to\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathfrak{g}\oplus TM
such that

D_{x}(v)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big(\langle\nabla\mu,v\rangle_{TM},\langle J\nabla\mu,v\rangle_{TM},\Hess_{x}L(v)\big),\forall x\in M,v\in T_{x}M.

For the next lemma, we follow the shorthands from Definition
4.7
.

Lemma 6.4

(Corollary
B.9
)
.

We have the following Bochner-type formula for
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}u(t,s)
:

0=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}\big(|\nabla^{A}P|^{2}+|F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}\big)+I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}+I_{5}

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=|\Hess_{A}P|^{2}+|\nabla F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}},\qquad I_{2}=|D_{P}(\nabla^{A}P)|^{2}+|\langle\nabla\mu,F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}|^{2},\qquad I_{3}=2\langle R_{M}(S,T)S,T\rangle,

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=\langle(\nabla_{T}\Hess H)(\nabla H),T\rangle+\langle(\nabla_{S}\Hess H)(\nabla H),S\rangle,

\displaystyle I_{5}

\displaystyle=6\langle\Hess\mu(JS),T\otimes F\rangle-\langle\Hess\mu(T),T\otimes F\rangle-\langle\Hess\mu(S),S\otimes F\rangle,

and
R_{M}
is the Riemannian curvature tensor of
M
.

Remark 6.5
.

This identity was first proved by Taubes in
[
JT80
, Proposition 6.1]
for the vortex equation on
\mathbb{C}
, in which case
M=\mathbb{C}
is furnished with the flat metric,
W\equiv 0
and
\mu=\frac{i}{2}|x|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}
, cf. Example
3.2
. For more details, see Remark
B.11
.
∎

Let us digest the consequence of Lemma
6.4
.
I_{1}\geq 0
.
I_{4}
and
I_{5}
involve only trilinear tensors:

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\cdot}\Hess H(\cdot),\cdot\rangle

\displaystyle:TM\otimes TM\otimes TM\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\langle\Hess\mu(\cdot),\cdot\otimes\cdot\rangle

\displaystyle:TM\otimes TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}.

By (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), the image of
P
lies in a
G
-invariant neighborhood
\Omega
of
G\cdot q
with compact closure, where these trilinear maps have uniformly bounded operator norms. Hence,

|I_{4}|+|I_{5}|\leq Cu^{3/2},

for some
C>0
. Indeed, each entry that appears in
I_{4}
and
I_{5}
takes the form
T,S,\nabla H,F
or
JS
, with norm bounded above by
C^{\prime}u^{1/2}
. The same argument leads to an estimate for
I_{3}
with a different exponent of
u
:

|I_{3}|\leq Cu^{2}.

Since the critical orbit
G\cdot q
is free, for some small
\zeta_{1}>0

Part III
The Seiberg-Witten Equations on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma

In the third part of this paper, we associate an infinite-dimensional Landau-Ginzburg model to an
H
-surface
\TSigma
so that the associated gauged Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}
recover the Seiberg-Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
. Recall that an
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
is a quadruple, where

•

\Sigma
is a connected closed oriented surface of genus
g(\Sigma)\geq 1
and
g_{\Sigma}
is a metric;

•

\lambda\neq 0\in\Omega^{1}_{h}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
is a harmonic 1-form on
\Sigma
such that
\lambda^{1,0}\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
has precisely
(2g(\Sigma)-2)
simple zeros;
\nu\in\Omega^{2}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
is an imaginary-valued closed 2-form on
\Sigma
.

We generalize Theorem
5.1
and
6.1
from the previous part to this infinite-dimensional case. The main difference is that the topology of
M
depends on a Sobolev completion of smooth sections, and we need to specify the correct norms used in the estimates.

The main obstacle in defining a Floer homology for a 3-manifold
\widehat{Y}
with cylindrical ends is a compactness problem, and its resolution relies on three inputs:

(K1)

a uniform upper bound on the analytic energy;

(K2)

finite energy solutions are trivial on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
, namely, they have to be
\mathbb{C}
-invariant up to gauge;

(K3)

finite energy solutions on
\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma
are trivial, namely, they have to be
\mathbb{R}_{s}
-invariant up to gauge.

One way to attain these properties is to perturb the Seiberg-Witten equations on either
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
or
\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma
using the closed 2-form
\omega\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
. While
\lambda
is used to perturb the superpotential
W_{\lambda}
,
\nu
is to perturb the moment map equation in (
4.1
).

The first property
(K1)
will be addressed in the second paper
[
Wan20a
]
when we set up the cobordism category properly. The second property
(K2)
is established by Theorem
1.3
. In Section
10
, we address the last property
(K3)
using a theorem of Taubes.

The Fundamental Gauged Landau-Ginzburg Model

In this section, we construct the fundamental gauged Landau-Ginzburg model

\big(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)\big)

associated to an
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)

7.4
, we verify that the superpotential

7.1.
Review

Recall that a
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on a smooth 4-manifold
X
is a pair
(S_{X},\rho_{4})
where
S_{X}=S^{+}\oplus S^{-}
is the spin bundle, and the bundle map
\rho_{4}:T^{*}X\to\Hom(S_{X},S_{X})
defines the Clifford multiplication. A configuration
\gamma=(A,\Phi)\in\mathcal{C}(X,\mathfrak{s})
consists of a smooth
spin^{c}\
connection
A
and a smooth section
\Phi
of
S^{+}
. Take
A^{t}
to be the induced connection on
\Lambda^{2}S^{+}
. Let
\omega
be a closed 2-form on
X
and
\omega^{+}
denote its self-dual part. The Seiberg-Witten equations perturbed by
\omega
are defined on
\mathcal{C}(X,\mathfrak{s})
by the formula:

(7.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{4}(F_{A^{t}}^{+})-(\Phi\Phi^{*})_{0}-\rho_{4}(\omega^{+})=0,\\
D_{A}^{+}\Phi=0,\end{array}\right.

where
D_{A}^{+}:\Gamma(S^{+})\to\Gamma(S^{-})
is the Dirac operator and
(\Phi\Phi^{*})_{0}=\Phi\Phi^{*}-\frac{1}{2}|\Phi|^{2}\otimes\Id_{S^{+}}
denotes the traceless part of the endomorphism
\Phi\Phi^{*}:S^{+}\to S^{+}
.

The gauge group
\mathcal{G}(X)=\map(X,S^{1})
acts naturally on
\mathcal{C}(X,\mathfrak{s})
by the formula:

\mathcal{G}(x)\ni u:\mathcal{C}(X,\mathfrak{s})\to\mathcal{C}(X,\mathfrak{s}),\ (A,\Phi)\mapsto(A-u^{-1}du,u\Phi).

The monopole equations (
7.1
) are invariant under gauge transformations. Let
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
be any
H
-surface. In the special case that
X=\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
is equipped with the product metric and the complex orientation, the equations (
7.1
) can be understood more concretely as follows.

Let
L^{+}\to\Sigma
be a line bundle of degree
0\leq d\leq 2g(\Sigma)-2
and set
L^{-}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}L^{+}\otimes\Lambda^{0,1}\Sigma
. They pull back to line bundles over
X
, denoted also by
L^{+}
and
L^{-}
respectively. Define
S^{+}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}L^{+}\oplus L^{-}
and so

c_{1}(S^{+})[\Sigma]=2(d-g(\Sigma)+1).

The spinor
\Phi
decomposes as
(\Phi_{+},\Phi_{-})
with
\Phi_{\pm}\in\Gamma(X,L^{\pm})
. Let
z=t+is
be the complex coordinate on
\mathbb{C}
. The Clifford multiplication
\rho=\rho_{4}:T^{*}X\to\Hom(S,S)
is defined by setting

\displaystyle\rho_{4}(dt)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Id\\
\Id&0\\
\end{pmatrix},\ \rho_{4}(ds)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_{1}\\
\sigma_{1}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}:\ S^{+}\oplus S^{-}\to S^{+}\oplus S^{-},

where
\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\\
\end{pmatrix}:S^{+}=L^{+}\oplus L^{-}\to L^{+}\oplus L^{-}
is the first Pauli matrix. Moreover, for any
x\in\Sigma
and
w\in T_{x}\Sigma
, we have

\rho_{3}(w)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\rho_{4}(dt)^{-1}\cdot\rho_{4}(w)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\iota(\sqrt{2}w^{0,1})\ \cdot\\
\sqrt{2}w^{0,1}\otimes\cdot&0\\
\end{pmatrix}:S^{+}\to S^{+}.

Remark 7.1
.

We will frequently work with Clifford multiplications in dimension
2,3
and
4
, denoted by
\rho_{2}
,
\rho_{3}
and
\rho_{4}
respectively. Identify
\mathbb{C}
as
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\mathbb{R}_{s}
, then they are related by

\rho_{3}(w)=\rho_{4}(dt)^{-1}\cdot\rho_{4}(w),\ \rho_{2}(v)=\rho_{3}(ds)^{-1}\cdot\rho_{3}(v):S^{+}\to S^{+},

for any
w\in T^{*}(\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma)
and
v\in T^{*}\Sigma
.
∎

The decomposition
S^{+}=L^{+}\oplus L^{-}
is also parallel, so any
spin^{c}\
connection
A
splits as

\nabla_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{A_{+}}&0\\
0&\nabla_{A_{-}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\check{B}_{0}
be any reference
spin^{c}\
connection on
S^{+}\to\Sigma
. Then a reference
spin^{c}\
connection
A_{0}
on
S^{+}\to X
is obtained by

\nabla_{A_{0}}=\nabla_{\check{B}_{0}}+d_{\mathbb{C}}

where
d_{\mathbb{C}}=dt\otimes\partial_{t}+ds\otimes\partial_{s}
is the trivial connection on
\mathbb{C}
. Any
spin^{c}\
connection
A
on
X
differs from
A_{0}
by an imaginary-valued 1-form
a\in\Gamma(X,iT^{*}X)
. Their curvature tensors are related by

F_{A}=F_{A_{0}}+da\otimes\Id_{S}.

Using the product structure on
X
, the connection
\nabla_{A}=(\nabla^{\mathbb{C}}_{A},\nabla_{A}^{\Sigma})
is decomposed into a
\mathbb{C}
-direction part and a
\Sigma
-direction part. The curvature tensor
F_{A}
is decomposed accordingly as:

F_{A}=F_{A}^{\Sigma}+F_{A}^{\mathbb{C}}+F^{m}_{A}

where
F_{A}^{m}\in\Gamma(X,T^{*}\mathbb{C}\otimes T^{*}\Sigma\otimes\End(S))
is the mixed term. A similar decomposition applies to the induced curvature form
F_{A^{t}}
on
\Lambda^{2}S^{+}=L^{+}\otimes L^{-}
:

(7.2)

F_{A^{t}}=F_{A^{t}}^{\Sigma}dvol_{\Sigma}+F_{A^{t}}^{\mathbb{C}}dvol_{\mathbb{C}}+F_{A^{t}}^{m},

with
F^{m}_{A^{t}}\in\Gamma(X,iT^{*}\mathbb{C}\otimes T^{*}\Sigma)
. Our description of
F_{A}
shows that

(7.3)

F_{A}^{m}=\frac{1}{2}F_{A^{t}}^{m}\otimes\Id,

and

(7.4)

F^{\Sigma}_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{A_{+}}^{\Sigma}&0\\
0&F_{A_{-}}^{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix}dvol_{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}F_{A^{t}}^{\Sigma}+\frac{i}{2}K_{\Sigma}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{2}F_{A^{t}}^{\Sigma}-\frac{i}{2}K_{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix}dvol_{\Sigma},

where
K_{\Sigma}
is the Gaussian curvature of
(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma})
.

7.2.
The Fundamental Gauged Landau-Ginzburg Model

In this subsection, we provide a different perspective on the Seiberg-Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
using gauged Landau-Ginzburg models and the gauged Witten equations. The fundamental gauged Landau-Ginzburg model

\big(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)\big)

defined below will allow us to apply results from Part
II
to the Seiberg-Witten equations on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
and
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times\Sigma
.

\bullet

The Kähler manifold

M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is the configuration space on
\Sigma
:

M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\check{B}_{0},0)+\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})\oplus\Gamma(\Sigma,L^{+}\oplus L^{-}),

where
\check{B}_{0}
is an arbitrary reference
spin^{c}\
connection on
S^{+}\to\Sigma
. A configuration
\kappa\in M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is a triple
(\check{b},\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})
, where the sum
\check{B}=\check{B}_{0}+\check{b}
is a
spin^{c}\
connection on
\Sigma
and
\check{\Psi}=(\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})\in\Gamma(\Sigma,S^{+})
is a spinor. The complex structure of
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is defined by the bundle map

J=\big(*_{\Sigma},\rho_{3}(ds)\big)=\big(*_{\Sigma},\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix}\big),

while the Riemannian metric
g_{M}
of
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is the flat
L^{2}
metric:

\langle(\check{b}_{1},\check{\Psi}_{1}),(\check{b}_{2},\check{\Psi}_{2})\rangle=\int_{\Sigma}\langle\check{b}_{1},\check{b}_{2}\rangle+\re\langle\check{\Psi}_{1},\check{\Psi}_{2}\rangle.

Let
h_{M}
be the Hermitian metric on
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
induced from
J
and
g_{M}
.

\bullet

The gauge group

\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)=\Map(\Sigma,S^{1})
acts on
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
by the standard formula:

u(\check{b},\check{\Psi})=(\check{b}-u^{-1}du,u\check{\Psi}).

The Lie algebra of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\Lie(\mathcal{G})=\Gamma(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
. It is also useful to consider a smaller group
\mathcal{G}^{e}
, the identity component of
\mathcal{G}
, which fits into a short exact sequence:

0\to\mathcal{G}^{e}\to\mathcal{G}\xrightarrow{\pi}H^{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})\to 0,\ \pi(u)=[\frac{u^{-1}du}{2\pi i}].

\bullet

The moment map

\mu
is given by

\displaystyle\mu(\check{b},\check{\Psi})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}*_{\Sigma}F_{\check{B}^{t}}+\frac{i}{2}(|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}-|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2})

\displaystyle=-*_{\Sigma}d\check{b}+\frac{i}{2}(|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}-|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2})-\frac{1}{2}*_{\Sigma}F_{\check{B}^{t}_{0}}.

If
v=(\delta\check{b},\delta\check{\Psi})
is a tangent vector at
(\check{b},\check{\Psi})
, then we have

(7.5)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mu,v\rangle_{TM}

\displaystyle=-*_{\Sigma}d_{\Sigma}\delta\check{b}+i\re\langle i\delta\check{\Psi},\rho_{3}(ds)\check{\Psi}\rangle\in\Lie(\mathcal{G}),

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\mu,Jv\rangle_{TM}

\displaystyle=d^{*}_{\Sigma}\delta\check{b}+i\re\langle i\delta\check{\Psi},\check{\Psi}\rangle\in\Lie(\mathcal{G}).

\bullet

The superpotential

W_{\lambda}
is the perturbed Dirac functional. The Clifford multiplication on
\Sigma

\rho_{2}:T^{*}\Sigma\to\Hom(S^{+},S^{+}),

defines a Dirac operator for each
spin^{c}\
connection
\check{B}
on
S^{+}\to\Sigma
:

D^{\Sigma}_{\check{B}}:\Gamma(\Sigma,S^{+})\xrightarrow{\nabla_{\check{B}}}\Gamma(\Sigma,T^{*}\Sigma\otimes S^{+})\xrightarrow{\rho_{2}}\Gamma(\Sigma,S^{+}).

This operator is self-adjoint and switches the parity, i.e.

D^{\Sigma}_{\check{B}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&D^{-}_{\check{B}}\\
D^{+}_{\check{B}}&0\end{pmatrix}:\Gamma(\Sigma,L^{+}\oplus L^{-})\to\Gamma(\Sigma,L^{+}\oplus L^{-}).

The superpotential
W_{0}
is then defined as

W_{0}(\check{b},\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})=\int_{\Sigma}\langle D^{+}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-}\rangle=\int_{\Sigma}\langle\check{\Psi}_{+},D^{-}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi}_{-}\rangle.

The perturbation that we consider takes the form

W_{\lambda}(\check{b},\check{\Psi})=W_{0}-\langle\check{b},\lambda\rangle_{h_{M}},

where
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
and
h_{M}
is the Hermitian inner product.

\bullet

The complex gauge group

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}=\Map(\Sigma,\mathbb{C}^{*})
acts on
M
by the formula:

(7.6)

e^{\alpha}u(\check{b},\check{\Psi})=(\check{b}+i*_{\Sigma}d_{\Sigma}\alpha-u^{-1}du,e^{\alpha}u\check{\Psi}_{+},e^{-\alpha}u\check{\Psi}_{-}),

where
u\in\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
and
\alpha:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is real.

Lemma 7.2
.

The superpotential
W_{0}:M(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{C}
is invariant under
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

It suffices to verify that for all
\alpha\in\Gamma(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})
,
e^{-\alpha}D_{\check{B}^{\prime}}^{+}(e^{\alpha}\ \cdot\ )=D_{\check{B}}^{+}(\cdot)
if
\check{B}^{\prime}=e^{\alpha}\cdot\check{B}
, or equivalently

\rho_{2}(d\alpha)+\rho_{2}(i*_{\Sigma}d\alpha)=0:\Gamma(L^{+})\to\Gamma(L^{-}).

Note that when restricted to
\Gamma(L^{-})
, it becomes:

\rho_{2}(d\alpha)-\rho_{2}(i*_{\Sigma}d\alpha)=0:\Gamma(L^{-})\to\Gamma(L^{+}).\qed

As for the perturbed superpotential
W_{\lambda}
,

•

for
W_{\lambda}
to be invariant under
\mathcal{G}^{e}
,
\lambda
has to be co-closed;

•

for
W_{\lambda}
to be invariant under the identity component
\mathcal{G}^{e}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
\lambda
has to be harmonic;

•

for
W_{\lambda}
to be invariant under
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
\lambda
has to zero.

Write
W_{\lambda}=L+iH
. Then

(7.7)

\nabla L(\check{b},\check{\Psi})=\big(\rho_{2}^{-1}(\check{\Psi}\check{\Psi}^{*})_{\Pi}-\lambda,D^{-}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi}_{-},D^{+}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi}_{+}\big).

Here
(\check{\Psi}\check{\Psi}^{*})_{\Pi}
denotes the off-diagonal part of the endomorphism
\check{\Psi}\check{\Psi}^{*}:L^{+}\oplus L^{-}\to L^{+}\oplus L^{-}
. The equation
\nabla L=0
has solutions if and only if
\lambda
is a harmonic 1-form. If in addition its
(1,0)
-part
\lambda^{1,0}\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{C})
has
(2g(\Sigma)-2)
simple zeros, we will characterize the critical locus
\Crit(L)
concretely in Proposition
7.4
. Now consider a connection
\bar{A}
on the trivial principal bundle
\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
over
\mathbb{C}
:

\bar{A}=d_{\mathbb{C}}+a_{t}(z)dt+a_{s}(z)ds

with
a_{t},a_{s}\in\Gamma\big(\mathbb{C},\Lie\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)\big)=\Gamma(\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 7.3
.

With the gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma))
defined as above, the associated gauged Witten equations over
\mathbb{C}
with
\vec{\delta}=-*_{\Sigma}\nu\in\Lie(\mathcal{G})
are equivalent to the Seiberg-Witten equations
(\ref{SWEQ})
on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
with
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
. Let

\displaystyle P:\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\to M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),\hskip 10.00002ptz\mapsto\big(\check{b}(z),\check{\Psi}(z)\big)

be a smooth map defined on
\mathbb{C}
. Then the identification
(A,\Phi)\leftrightarrow(\bar{A},P)
is made by taking

\displaystyle A-A_{0}

\displaystyle=(\bar{A}-d_{\mathbb{C}})+(\check{B}(z)-\check{B}_{0})=a_{t}(z)dt+a_{s}(z)ds+\check{b}(z),

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\check{\Psi}(z)\text{ on }\{z\}\times\Sigma.

Proof.

The
J
-holomorphic equation in (
4.1
) in our case becomes

\nabla^{\bar{A}}_{\partial_{t}}\begin{pmatrix}\check{b}\\
\check{\Psi}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}*_{\Sigma}&0\\
0&\rho_{3}(ds)\end{pmatrix}\bigg(\nabla^{\bar{A}}_{\partial_{s}}\begin{pmatrix}\check{b}\\
\check{\Psi}\end{pmatrix}+\nabla L\bigg)=0.

More concretely, it is

(7.8)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\check{b}-d_{\Sigma}a_{t})+*_{\Sigma}\big(\partial_{s}\check{b}-d_{\Sigma}a_{s}+\rho_{2}^{-1}(\check{\Psi}\check{\Psi}^{*})_{\Pi}-\lambda\big)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\check{\Psi}+a_{t}\check{\Psi})+\rho_{3}(ds)(\partial_{s}\check{\Psi}+a_{s}\check{\Psi}+D^{\Sigma}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi})

\displaystyle=0.

The second equation gives rise to the Dirac operator
D_{A}^{+}\Phi=0
,
while the first equation gives the off-diagonal part of the curvature equation:

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{4}(F_{A^{t}}^{+})_{\Pi}-(\Phi\Phi^{*})_{\Pi}-\rho_{4}(\omega^{+})_{\Pi}=0

with
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
.
The diagonal part comes from the moment map equation in (
4.1
):

-*_{\mathbb{C}}d_{\mathbb{C}}(a_{t}dt+a_{s}ds)-*_{\Sigma}d_{\Sigma}\check{b}+\frac{i}{2}(|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}-|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2})-\frac{1}{2}*_{\Sigma}F_{\check{B}_{0}^{t}}=\vec{\delta}.

Indeed,
\frac{1}{2}F_{A^{t}}^{\Sigma}=d_{\Sigma}\check{b}+\frac{1}{2}F_{\check{B}_{0}^{t}}
and
\frac{1}{2}F_{A^{t}}^{\mathbb{C}}=d_{\mathbb{C}}(a_{t}dt+a_{s}ds)
in terms of the decomposition (
7.2
).
∎

7.3.
Stability

Now we examine the stability of the superpotential
W_{\lambda}
. Although
W_{\lambda}
is not fully
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant when
\lambda\neq 0
,
\nabla L=0
is a
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant equation on

.

Proposition 7.4
.

Given any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, consider the
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on
\Sigma
with

and the fundamental gauged Landau-Ginzburg model

constructed above. Then

consists of
\binom{2g-2}{d}

-orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\Crit(L)

Remark 7.5
.

In the sense of Proposition
7.4
, we say that

is stable and any
\vec{\delta}\in\Lie(\mathcal{G})
is

-stable. The only difference from Definition

and

is only invariant under the smaller group

∎

Proof of Proposition
7.4
.

The verification that
W_{\lambda}
is Morse-Bott is postponed to Proposition
7.8
, which concerns only the linearized operator at critical
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits. We focus on the other statements. The equation
\nabla L=0
reads:

(7.9)

\big(\rho_{2}^{-1}(\check{\Psi}\check{\Psi}^{*})_{\Pi}-\lambda,D^{-}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi}_{-},D^{+}_{\check{B}}\check{\Psi}_{+}\big)=0.

The last two equations in (
7.9
) imply that
\check{\Psi}_{+}
and
\check{\Psi}_{-}^{*}
are holomorphic with respect to some unitary connections on
L^{+}
and
(L^{-})^{*}
respectively, while the first one says that

(7.10)

\check{\Psi}_{+}\otimes\check{\Psi}_{-}^{*}=-\sqrt{2}\lambda^{1,0}.

Since
\lambda^{1,0}
is assumed to have only simple zeros, the zero loci
Z(\check{\Psi}_{+})
and
Z(\check{\Psi}_{-}^{*})
give rise to a partition of
Z(\lambda^{1,0})
. Since
|Z(\check{\Psi}_{+})|=\deg L^{+}=d
, there are

\binom{2g-2}{d}

such partitions in total, each of which corresponds to a free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit in
\Crit(L)
.

Fix a representative
\kappa=(\check{B},\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})
in a free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\Crit(L)
. To show this
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit
\mathcal{O}
is closed under the smooth topology of
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
, consider a sequence of configurations
\kappa_{j}=(\check{B}^{j},\check{\Psi}_{+}^{j},\check{\Psi}_{-}^{j})\in\mathcal{O}
converging smoothly to some
\kappa_{\infty}=(\check{B}^{\infty},\check{\Psi}_{+}^{\infty},\check{\Psi}_{-}^{\infty})\in M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
. We have to show
\kappa_{\infty}\in\mathcal{O}
. Since
Z(\check{\Psi}_{+}^{j})=Z(\check{\Psi}_{+})
for all
j
and the convergence is in smooth topology,
Z(\check{\Psi}_{+}^{\infty})=Z(\check{\Psi}_{+})
. Since
\Crit(L)
is closed, this implies that
\kappa_{\infty}
and
\kappa
lie in the same
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit.

Take any smooth function
\vec{\delta}\in\Gamma(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
. To show that
\vec{\delta}
is
W_{\lambda}
-stable, we have to find the unique function
\alpha:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
such that

e^{\alpha}\cdot\kappa=(\check{B}+i*_{\Sigma}d_{\Sigma}\alpha,e^{\alpha}\check{\Psi}_{+},e^{-\alpha}\check{\Psi}_{-}),

lies in the fiber
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})
, or equivalently, to solve the equation

(7.11)

i\big(\Delta_{\Sigma}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(e^{2\alpha}|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}-e^{-2\alpha}|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2})+\frac{i}{2}*_{\Sigma}F_{\check{B}^{t}}\big)=\vec{\delta},

and show that this solution is unique. The strategy is to use the variational principle to prove that the non-linear map:

\displaystyle\eta:L^{2}_{k}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}_{k-2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\mapsto\eta(\alpha)=\Delta_{\Sigma}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(e^{2\alpha}-1)|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2\alpha})|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2}

is a bijection for any
k\geq 2
. It suffices to prove the special case for
k=2
; the rest will follow by elliptic regularity. For any
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})
, define an energy functional as

\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha)=\|\eta(\alpha)-g\|^{2}_{2}.

If
\alpha_{0}
achieves the infimum
\inf_{\alpha\in L^{2}_{2}}\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha)
, let
f=\eta(\alpha_{0})-g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
. Then, for any tangent vector
v\in L^{2}_{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})
at the minimizer
\alpha_{0}
, we have

0=\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha_{0}+tv)=\langle f,\Delta_{\Sigma}v+(e^{2\alpha_{0}}|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}+e^{-2\alpha_{0}}|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2})v\rangle.

Since the linearized operator
\Delta_{\Sigma}+(e^{2\alpha_{0}}|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}+e^{-2\alpha_{0}}|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2})
is positive and self-adjoint on
L^{2}_{2}
,
f=0
. To find such a minimizer
\alpha_{0}
, let
\{\alpha_{n}\}\subset L^{2}_{2}
be a sequence that minimizes the energy
\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha)
, i.e.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha_{n})=\inf_{\alpha\in L^{2}_{2}}\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha).

At this point, we need an a priori estimate:

Lemma 7.6
.

For any fixed
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, there exists a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for any
\alpha\in L^{2}_{2}
,
\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha)<C
implies that
\|\alpha\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\leq\varphi(C)
.

Lemma
7.6
allows us to find a weakly convergent subsequence among
\{\alpha_{n}\}
in
L^{2}_{2}
. Let
\alpha_{0}
be the limit. Then

\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha_{0})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha_{n})=\inf\mathcal{E}_{g}(\alpha),

so the infimum is attained at
\alpha=\alpha_{0}
. This proves that
\eta:L^{2}_{2}\to L^{2}
is surjective and so a solution to the equation (
7.11
) exists. If
\eta(\alpha_{1}+\delta\alpha)=\eta(\alpha_{1})
, then

\Delta_{\Sigma}\delta\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(e^{2\delta\alpha}-1)e^{2\alpha_{1}}|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2\delta\alpha})e^{-2\alpha_{1}}|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2}=0.

By the maximum principle,
\delta\alpha\equiv 0
on
\Sigma
. This proves that
\eta
is injective and so the solution to the equation (
7.11
) is unique. It remains to verify Lemma
7.6
. This estimate follows from Lemma
7.7
, in which we set
w_{+}\equiv|\check{\Psi}_{+}|
and
w_{-}=|\check{\Psi}_{-}|
. Note that by (
7.10
),
w_{\pm}\not\equiv 0
.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Given smooth functions
w_{+},w_{-}:\Sigma\to[0,\infty)
such that
w_{+}\not\equiv 0
and
w_{-}\not\equiv 0
,
consider the non-linear map

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle:L^{2}_{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\mapsto\Delta_{\Sigma}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}(e^{2\alpha}-1)w_{+}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2\alpha})w_{-}^{2}.

Then for any
g\in L^{2}(\Sigma)
, there exists a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for any
\alpha\in L^{2}_{2}
,
\|\eta(\alpha)-g\|_{2}^{2}<C
implies that
\|\alpha\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\leq\varphi(C)
.

Proof of Lemma
7.7
.

By the triangle inequality,

\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}\leq\|\eta(\alpha)-g\|_{2}+\|g\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{C}+\|g\|_{2}.

So it suffices to prove this lemma for
g=0
. Note that

(7.12)

\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}\|\alpha\|_{2}\geq\langle\eta(\alpha),\alpha\rangle_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}=\|d\alpha\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}(e^{\alpha}-e^{-\alpha})\alpha(e^{\alpha}w_{+}^{2}+e^{-\alpha}w_{-}^{2}).

Let
\alpha_{+}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+|\alpha|)
be the positive part of
\alpha
. By (
7.12
) and Kato’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}\|\alpha\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|d\alpha_{+}\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{\Sigma}\alpha_{+}^{2}w_{+}^{2}\geq c_{+}\|\alpha_{+}\|_{L^{2}_{1}(\Sigma)}^{2}

for some
c_{+}>0
, because the weight function
w_{+}
is somewhere positive. The negative part
\alpha_{-}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{1}{2}(\alpha-|\alpha|)
of
\alpha
is estimated in a similar way. As a result,

\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}\|\alpha\|_{2}\geq 2c\big(\|\alpha_{+}\|_{L^{2}_{1}(\Sigma)}^{2}+\|\alpha_{-}\|_{L^{2}_{1}(\Sigma)}^{2}\big)\geq c\|\alpha\|_{L^{1}_{2}(\Sigma)}^{2}

for some
c>0
; so
\|\alpha\|_{L^{2}_{1}(\Sigma)}\leq c^{-1}\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}
. To estimate
\|\Delta\alpha\|_{2}^{2}
, note that the map

\alpha\mapsto\eta(\alpha)-\Delta\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(e^{2\alpha}-1)w_{+}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2\alpha})w_{-}^{2}

is continuous from
L^{2}_{1}(\Sigma)
to
L^{2}(\Sigma)
by Trudinger’s inequality
[
Tay11
, Chapter 13, Proposition 4.2]
or
[
Wan18
, Proposition A.3]
. As a result,

\|\Delta\alpha\|_{2}\leq\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}+\|\eta(\alpha)-\Delta\alpha\|_{2}\leq\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}+\varphi_{1}\big(\|\alpha\|_{L^{2}_{1}}\big)\leq\varphi\big(\|\eta(\alpha)\|_{2}\big)

for some functions
\varphi,\varphi_{1}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
7.7
.
∎

7.4.
The Morse-Bott Condition

In this subsection, we verify that
\re(W_{\lambda})
is a Morse-Bott function on
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
, which completes the proof of Proposition
7.4
.

Proposition 7.8
.

Given any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, consider the
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on
\Sigma
with
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s})[\Sigma]=2(d-g(\Sigma)+1)
and the fundamental gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma))
constructed in Subsection
7.2
. Then
L=\re(W_{\lambda})
is a Morse-Bott function. In particular, the Morse-Bott estimate (
5.4
) holds in our case.

Proof.

Since
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is a complex linear space, the tangent space at any
\kappa=(\check{B},\check{\Psi})\in M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is identified with

\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Omega^{1}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})\oplus\Gamma(\Sigma,L^{+}\oplus L^{-}).

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the completion of
\mathcal{H}
with respect to the
L^{2}_{k,\check{B}}
norm:

\|(\delta\check{b},\delta\check{\Psi})\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{k,\check{B}}}=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla^{k}\delta\check{b}|^{2}+|\nabla_{\check{B}}^{k}\delta\check{\Psi}|^{2}.

This family of norms on the tangent bundle of
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is equivariant under the gauge action of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
. The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
is
\Gamma(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
and let
\mathfrak{g}_{k}=L^{2}_{k}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})
be its
L^{2}_{k}
-completion for
k=0,1
. To prove Proposition
7.8
, by Lemma
4.8
, it suffices to verify that the extended operator

\widehat{D}_{\kappa}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\langle\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{TM}\\
0&0&\langle J\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{TM}\\
\langle\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}&\langle J\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}&\Hess L\end{pmatrix}:\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0},

is invertible for any
\kappa=(\check{B},\check{\Psi})\in\Crit(L)
. Since
\widehat{D}_{\kappa}
is
L^{2}
-self-adjoint and Fredholm, it suffices to show that
\widehat{D}_{\kappa}
is injective. Notice that the images of

\langle\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},\ \langle J\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},\ D_{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big(\langle\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{TM},\langle J\nabla\mu,\cdot\rangle_{TM},\Hess L\big)

are pairwise
L^{2}
-orthogonal in
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}
. The first two are injective, because the
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-action is free at
\kappa
. We focus on the last operator

D_{\kappa}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}.

Suppose
v=(\delta\check{b},\delta\check{\Psi})\in\ker D_{\kappa}
, then the tangent vector
v
solves the following equations by (
7.5
) and (
7.9
):

(7.13)

\displaystyle-*_{\Sigma}d_{\Sigma}\delta\check{b}+i\re\langle i\delta\check{\Psi},\rho_{3}(ds)\check{\Psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(7.14)

\displaystyle d^{*}_{\Sigma}\delta\check{b}+i\re\langle i\delta\check{\Psi},\check{\Psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

(7.15)

\displaystyle(\delta\check{\Psi}\check{\Psi}^{*}+\check{\Psi}\delta\check{\Psi}^{*})_{\Pi}

\displaystyle=0,

(7.16)

\displaystyle D^{\Sigma}_{\check{B}}\delta\check{\Psi}+\rho_{2}(\delta\check{b})\check{\Psi}

\displaystyle=0.

The key observation is that the third equation (
7.15
) is an algebraic constraint on the spinor
\delta\check{\Psi}
. Since
\check{\Psi}_{+}
and
\check{\Psi}_{-}
do not have common zeros by (
7.10
), the spinor
\check{\Psi}=(\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})
is nowhere vanishing on
\Sigma
and so by (
7.15
),

(7.17)

\delta\check{\Psi}=(h\check{\Psi}_{+},-\bar{h}\check{\Psi}_{-})=i\beta\check{\Psi}+(\alpha\check{\Psi}_{+},-\alpha\check{\Psi}_{-}),

for a complex valued function
h=\alpha+i\beta:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. By (
7.16
)(
7.17
) and the fact that
D_{\check{B}}^{\Sigma}\check{\Psi}=0
, we have

\rho_{2}(dh+\delta\check{b})\check{\Psi}_{+}=0,\rho_{2}(-d\bar{h}+\delta\check{b})\check{\Psi}_{-}=0.

By the non-vanishing property of
\check{\Psi}
, this means

\delta\check{b}=i(*_{\Sigma}d\alpha-d\beta).

In other words,
(\delta\check{b},\delta\check{\Psi})
is generated by the linearized action of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
at
\kappa
. By the gauge fixing condition (
7.14
),
\beta\equiv 0
. By
(
7.13
)
,
\alpha\equiv 0
.
∎

7.5.
A Remark on the 1-Form
\lambda

For any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\mu)
, the holomorphic 1-form
\lambda^{1,0}
is assumed to have
(2g(\Sigma)-2)
simple zeros. As we shall see in this subsection, Proposition
7.8
does not hold if this assumption is dropped.

To this end, let
\mathscr{L}_{i},1\leq i\leq 3
be holomorphic line bundles over
\Sigma
and
\sigma_{i}\neq 0\in H^{0}(\Sigma,\mathscr{L}_{i})
be holomorphic sections. Suppose that
\deg\mathscr{L}_{3}>0
and
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
do not have common zeros. Consider a solution
\kappa=(\check{B},\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})
to the equation
\nabla L=0
such that

-\sqrt{2}\lambda^{1,0}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}^{2},\ \check{\Psi}_{+}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}\text{ and }\check{\Psi}_{-}=(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3})^{*}.

The goal is to find a non-zero vector
v=(\delta b,\delta\check{\Psi})\in\ker D_{\kappa}\subset T_{\kappa}M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
, i.e. a solution to the equations (
7.13
)–(
7.16
). As in the proof of Proposition
7.8
, the algebraic equation (
7.15
) implies that

(\delta\Psi_{+},\delta\Psi_{-})=\big(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{4},-(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4})^{*}\big),

for some smooth section
\sigma_{4}\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma,\mathscr{L}_{3})
. If there is a harmonic 1-form
\delta\check{b}^{h}
on
\Sigma
such that

(7.18)

\bar{\partial}_{\mathscr{L}_{3}}\sigma_{4}+(\delta\check{b}^{h})^{0,1}\otimes\sigma_{3}=0,

then the tangent vector
v_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big(\delta\check{b}^{h},\sigma_{1}\sigma_{4},-(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4})^{*}\big)
solves (
7.15
) and (
7.16
). To fulfill (
7.13
) and (
7.14
), we replace
v_{0}
by

v_{0}+\big(i(*_{\Sigma}d\alpha-d\beta),(\alpha+i\beta)\check{\Psi}_{+},(-\alpha+i\beta)\check{\Psi}_{-}\big)

for some suitable functions
\alpha,\beta:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
. Thus it suffices to construct the pair
(\delta\check{b}^{h},\sigma_{4})
satisfying the equation (
7.18
). To this end, we find a harmonic form
\delta\check{b}^{h}\neq 0
such that

(\delta\check{b}^{h})^{0,1}\otimes\sigma_{3}
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to
\im(\bar{\partial}_{\mathscr{L}_{3}})^{\perp}.
Alternatively, we show that the composition

(7.19)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega_{h}^{1}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R})\to&C^{\infty}(\Sigma,\Lambda^{1,0}\Sigma\otimes\mathscr{L}_{3}^{*})&\to H^{0}(\Sigma,\Lambda^{1,0}\Sigma\otimes\mathscr{L}_{3}^{*})\\
\delta b^{h}\mapsto&(\delta b^{h})^{1,0}\otimes\sigma_{3}^{*}&\mapsto\Pi_{3}((\delta b^{h})^{1,0}\otimes\sigma_{3}^{*}),\end{array}

is not injective, where
\Pi_{3}
denotes the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
H^{0}(\Sigma,\Lambda^{1,0}\Sigma\otimes\mathscr{L}_{3}^{*})
. By the Riemann-Roch theorem and Serre duality,

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\Sigma,\Lambda^{1,0}\Sigma\otimes\mathscr{L}_{3}^{*})

\displaystyle=g-1-\deg\mathscr{L}_{3}+\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\Sigma,\mathscr{L}_{3})

\displaystyle\leq g=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Omega_{h}^{1}(\Sigma,i\mathbb{R}).

The equality is only achieved when
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\Sigma,\mathscr{L}_{3})=\deg\mathscr{L}_{3}+1
. However, this can only happen if
\deg\mathscr{L}_{3}=0
or
g(\Sigma)=0
. As a result, the composition (
7.19
) is not injective. It suffices to take
\delta b^{h}
that lies in the kernel of (
7.19
).

8.
Point-like Solutions Are Trivial

With all machineries developed so far, we are ready to study the monopole equations on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
. By
[
Wan18
]
, finite energy solutions to the unperturbed equations on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
are non-trivial in general and can be classified algebraically. In our case, we show the other extreme:

Theorem 8.1
.

Given any
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
, consider the
spin^{c}\
structure
\mathfrak{s}
on
\Sigma
with
c_{1}(\mathfrak{s})[\Sigma]=2(d-g(\Sigma)+1)
. For the fundamental Landau-Ginzburg model
(M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma))
constructed in Subsection
7.2
, let
(\bar{A},P)
be a solution to the gauged Witten equations on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
with
\vec{\delta}=-*_{\Sigma}\nu
:

(8.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}-*F_{\bar{A}}+\mu=\vec{\delta},\\
\nabla^{\bar{A}}_{\partial_{t}}P+J\nabla^{\bar{A}}_{\partial_{s}}P+\nabla H=0,\end{array}\right.

where
P:\mathbb{C}\to M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is a smooth map and
\bar{A}
is a smooth connection on the trivial
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
-bundle over
\mathbb{C}
. If the analytic energy

(8.2)

\mathcal{E}_{an}(\bar{A},P;\mathbb{C})=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\nabla_{\bar{A}}P|^{2}+|\nabla H|^{2}+|F_{\bar{A}}|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2}

is finite, then
(\bar{A},P)
is gauge equivalent to a constant configuration and so
\mathcal{E}_{an}(\bar{A},P;\mathbb{C})=0
.

Recall from Proposition
7.3
that each
(\bar{A},P)
is identified with a Seiberg-Witten configuration
(A,\Phi)
on
\mathbb{C}\times\Sigma
, where
A
is a
spin^{c}\
connection and
\Phi
is a spinor. Then the total energy (
8.2
) coincides with the classical analytic energy of
(A,\Phi)
for the monopole equations. The actual statement is slightly stronger:

Lemma 8.2

(
[
Wan18
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

For any region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
and any configuration
\gamma=(\bar{A},P)=(A,\Phi)
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{an}(\bar{A},P;\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\bar{A}}P|^{2}+|\nabla H|^{2}+|F_{\bar{A}}|^{2}+|\vec{\delta}-\mu|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,\Phi;\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Sigma}\frac{1}{4}|F_{A^{t}}|^{2}+|\nabla_{A}\Phi|^{2}+|(\Phi\Phi^{*})_{0}+\rho_{4}(\omega^{+})|^{2}+\frac{K_{\Sigma}}{2}|\Phi|^{2}+\re\langle F^{\Sigma}_{A^{t}},\vec{\delta}\rangle,

where
K_{\Sigma}
is the Gaussian curvature of
\Sigma
and
\omega=ds\wedge\lambda-\vec{\delta}dvol_{\Sigma}
.
Then

\mathcal{E}_{an}(\bar{A},P;\Omega)=\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,\Phi;\Omega).

We refer to either of them as the local energy functional of
\gamma
over
\Omega
.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
.

We follow the proof of Theorem
5.1
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{O}_{k}
be the free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits in
\Crit(L)
and
\kappa_{j}=(\check{b}^{j},\check{\Psi}^{j}_{+},\check{\Psi}^{j}_{-})
be a representative in
\mathcal{O}_{j}\cap\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})
for each
1\leq j\leq k
. Define a family of metrics on the quotient configuration space
M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})/\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
using
L^{2}_{l}
-Sobolev norms:

d_{l}([\kappa_{1}^{\prime}],[\kappa_{2}^{\prime}])\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|\kappa_{1}^{\prime}-g\cdot\kappa_{2}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{l}},\forall\kappa_{1}^{\prime},\kappa_{2}^{\prime}\in M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s}),l\geq 0.

We first verify the condition of Theorem
5.1
by showing that for some
1\leq j\leq k

(8.3)

d_{l}([P(t,s)],[\kappa_{j}])\to 0\text{ as }z=t+is\to\infty,

and the convergence holds for all
l\geq 0
. Let
n=(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{C}
and
B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{C}
be the disk of radius
R
. Define

(A_{n},\Phi_{n})(z,x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(A,\Phi)(z+n,x),\forall z\in\Omega\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\overline{B(0,10)}\text{ and }x\in\Sigma.

Since the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A,\Phi;\mathbb{C})=\mathcal{E}_{an}(\bar{A},P;\mathbb{C})
is finite,
\{(A_{n},\Phi_{n})\}
is a family of solutions on
\Omega\times\Sigma
with
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A_{n},\Phi_{n};\Omega)\to 0
as
|n|\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}|n_{1}|+|n_{2}|\to\infty
. Here
\mathcal{E}_{an}(A_{n},\Phi_{n};\Omega)
is the local energy functional defined in Lemma
8.2
.

Let
U_{j}
be an
L^{2}_{l}
-neighborhood of

10.
Finite Energy Solutions on
\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma

Fix an
H
-surface
\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
. In this section, we study the 3-dimensional Seiberg-Witten equations:

(10.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{3}(F_{B^{t}})-(\Psi\Psi^{*})_{0}-\rho_{3}(\omega)=0,\\
D_{B}\Psi=0.\end{array}\right.

on the cylinder
\mathbb{R}_{s}\times\Sigma
with
\omega=\nu+ds\wedge\lambda
. In terms of gauged Landau-Ginzburg models, the equations (
10.1
) gives rise to a downward gradient flowline of
L=\re W_{\lambda}
:

(10.2)

\frac{d}{ds}p(s)+\nabla L\circ p=0\text{ and }\mu(p(s))=\vec{\delta},

where
p(s)=(\check{b}(s),\check{\Psi}(s)):\mathbb{R}_{s}\to M(\Sigma,\mathfrak{s})
is a smooth path in the Kähler manifold

. The relation of (
10.2
) with (
10.1
) can be seen by setting

B=\frac{d}{ds}+\check{B}_{0}+\check{b}(s)\text{ and }\Psi=\check{\Psi}(s)\text{ on }\{s\}\times\Sigma,

for a reference
spin^{c}\
connection
\check{B}_{0}
. We require the path

to have finite analytic energy:

(10.3)

\mathcal{E}_{an}(p)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{s}}|\frac{d}{ds}p|^{2}+|\nabla L|^{2}<\infty.

By (
10.3
) and the stability of

, the path

is of finite length and the limit

q_{\pm}=\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}p(s)

lies in
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\Crit(L)
. By the Cauchy-Riemann equation (
2.1
),

follows the Hamiltonian flow of
H=\im W_{\lambda}
. Hence, a flowline connecting

and

exists only if

(10.4)

L(q_{-})\geq L(q_{+})\text{ and }H(q_{-})=H(q_{+}).

This motivates the following definition:

Definition 10.1
.

An

\TSigma=(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\lambda,\nu)
is called

\im a_{1}\neq\im a_{2}
.
∎

One can always make

by

for some

, since the set of critical values of

is at most countable. Indeed, the relation

(10.5)

implies that
(\check{b},\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})\in\Crit(\re(W_{\lambda}))
if and only if
(\check{b},\check{\Psi}_{+},e^{-i\theta}\check{\Psi}_{-})\in\Crit(\re(W_{{e^{-i\theta}\lambda}}))
, but now critical values are rotated by the angle

.

.

If an

-surface
\TSigma
is good, then finite energy solutions to (

) are necessarily
\mathbb{R}_{s}
-invariant.

When

The proof is borrowed from

Proof.

Appendix A
The Maximum Principle

In this appendix, we prove a version of maximum principle. The author is greatly indebted to Ao Sun for teaching him the proof of Lemma
A.1
. The Laplacian operator is always assumed to have a non-negative spectrum. In particular, over the complex plane,

\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2}.

This sign convention is adopted throughout this paper.

Proposition A.1
.

Take
\zeta>0
. Suppose
u:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-function on the upper half plane such that

(1)

(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}+\zeta^{2})u\leq 0
, and

(2)

u(t,0)\leq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{t}
.

Then
u(t,s)\leq 0
for any
(t,s)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
.

Proof.

Choose a smooth cut-off function
\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that
0\leq\psi\leq 1
,
\psi\equiv 1
on
[0,1]
and
\equiv 0
on
[2,\infty)
. Define
\phi_{R}(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\psi(|z|/R)
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

•

\phi_{R}\equiv 1
when
|z|<R
and
\equiv 0
when
|z|>2R
;

•

\nabla\phi_{R}=0
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{R}\equiv 0
when
|z|<R
or
|z|>2R
;

•

for some
C>0
,
|\nabla\phi_{R}|<\frac{C}{R}
and
|\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{R}|<\frac{C}{R^{2}}
.

Only the last property requires some explanation. In general, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{R}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\phi^{\prime}(\frac{|z|}{R})\partial_{r},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{R}(z)

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{r}^{2}\phi_{R}+\frac{1}{|z|}\partial_{r}\phi_{R})=-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\frac{|z|}{R})-\frac{1}{|z|R}\psi^{\prime}(\frac{|z|}{R}).

Suppose
u(z_{0})>0
at some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
. Consider the function
u_{R}(z)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}u(z)\cdot\phi_{R}(z-z_{0})
. Then
u_{R}(z)\equiv 0
when
|z-z_{0}|>2R
and
u_{R}(t,0)\leq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{t}
. Hence,
\max u_{R}
is attained at some
z_{1}
in the interior of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
. Let
N=\|u\|_{\infty}
, so

(A.1)

0<u(z_{0})=u_{R}(z_{0})\leq u_{R}(z_{1})\leq N\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0}).

At
z_{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, we have

0=(\nabla u_{R})(z_{1})=\nabla u(z_{1})\cdot\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0})+u(z_{1})\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0}),

so
\nabla u(z_{1})=-u(z_{1})\nabla\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0})/\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0})
. The relation
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}u\leq-\zeta^{2}u
then implies:

\displaystyle 0\leq(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}u_{R})(z_{1})

\displaystyle=\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0})\cdot\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}u(z_{1})+u(z_{1})\cdot\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0})-2\nabla\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0})\cdot\nabla u(z_{1}),

\displaystyle\leq u(z_{1})\cdot\bigg(-\zeta^{2}\phi_{R}+\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{R}+\frac{2|\nabla\phi_{R}|^{2}}{\phi_{R}}\bigg)(z_{1}-z_{0}).

However, this inequality can not hold when
R\gg 1
. Indeed, by (
A.1
),

\bigg|\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{R}+\frac{2|\nabla\phi_{R}|^{2}}{\phi_{R}}\bigg|(z_{1}-z_{0})\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}+\frac{2C^{2}}{R^{2}}\cdot\frac{N}{u(z_{0})}<\frac{\zeta^{2}u(z_{0})}{N}\leq\zeta^{2}\phi_{R}(z_{1}-z_{0}),

when
R\gg 1
. In the meantime,
u(z_{1})>0
. A contradiction. This completes the proof of Proposition
A.1
.
∎

Corollary A.2
.

Take
\zeta>0
. Suppose
u:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}=\mathbb{R}_{t}\times[0,\infty)_{s}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bounded
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-function on the upper half plane such that

(1)

, and

(2)

for some
K>0
,

for all

for any

Let

. Then
(\Delta_{\mathbb{C}}+\zeta^{2})v=0
and
v(t,0)=K
for any

A.1
to
u-v
to conclude.
∎

There are analogous statements for a strip of finite length. Their proofs are similar and omitted here.

. Suppose

Appendix B
A Bochner-Type Formula

The purpose of this appendix is to summarize some formulae for a gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W,G,\rho)
from Riemannian geometry. In particular, we will prove a Bochner-type formula for a solution
(A,P)
to the gauged Witten equations (
4.1
) on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
. For our primary application, we will take
M
to be a complex linear space, in which case most formulae will become simpler.

B.1.
Some Useful Formulae

Recall that
(M,\omega,J,g)
is a Kähler manifold and
(G,\rho)
is a compact abelian Lie group acting on
M
preserving the Kähler structure.
(G,\rho)
admits a moment map
\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}
which is
G
-invariant.
W=L+iH
is a
G_{\mathbb{C}}
-invariant holomorphic function on
M
, called the superpotential.

For any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, let
\tilde{\xi}
be the vector field on
M
induced from the action
(G,\rho)
. The convention of the moment map used in our paper is that

\iota(\tilde{\xi})\omega=-d\langle\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

or equivalently,

(B.1)

\tilde{\xi}=J\langle\nabla\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

since
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)=g(J\cdot,\cdot)
. Here
\nabla\mu\in\Gamma(M,TM\otimes\mathfrak{g})
is viewed as a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued vector field on
M
.

Lemma B.1
.

For a gauged Landau-Ginzburg model
(M,W,G,\rho)
defined as in Definition
3.1
, we have the following identities:

(1)

\nabla L+J\nabla H=0.

(2)

\Hess L+J\circ\Hess H=0
.

(3)

J\circ\Hess H+\Hess H\circ J=0
, i.e. the Hessian
\Hess H
of
H
anti-commutes with
J
.

(4)

J\circ\Hess\mu=\Hess\mu\circ J
, i.e. the Hessian
\Hess\mu
commutes with
J
.

(5)

\langle\nabla\mu,\nabla H\rangle_{TM}=\langle J\nabla\mu,\nabla H\rangle_{TM}=0
.

(6)

\langle\nabla\mu,\tilde{\xi}\rangle_{TM}=0
for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

The first identity (
1
) is the Cauchy-Riemann equation (
2.1
). Since
M
is Kähler, the complex structure
J
is parallel, i.e.
\nabla J=0
, so (
2
) follows from (
1
) by taking the covariant derivative.

Both
\Hess L
and
\Hess H
are symmetric operators with respect to the metric
g
, so by (
2
), we have

J\circ\Hess H=(J\circ\Hess H)^{T}=(\Hess H)^{T}\circ J^{T}=-\Hess H\circ J.

This proves (
3
). Since the metric
g
is
G
-invariant, for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, the Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\xi}}g
is zero. This implies that for any vector fields

v,w
on
M
,

(B.2)

\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi},w\rangle+\langle\nabla_{w}\tilde{\xi},v\rangle=0.

Using the defining equation (
B.1
), we conclude that
\langle J\Hess\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
is an anti-symmetric operator, so
\Hess\mu
commutes with
J
. This proves (
4
)

Finally, since
H
is
G
-invariant,
\langle\tilde{\xi},\nabla H\rangle=0
. By (
B.1
),
\langle J\nabla\mu,\nabla H\rangle_{TM}=0
. The other identity in (
5
) follows from the
G
-invariance of
L
and the first identity (
1
).

(
6
) follows from the fact that
\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}
is
G
-invariant.
∎

A smooth connection
A=d+a
in the trivial principal bundle
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\times G\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
allows us to take covariant derivatives of a map
P:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\to M
. It is also important to know the covariant derivative of a vector field
v
along
P
, i.e. a smooth map
v:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\to TM
with
v(z)\in T_{P(z)}M
. Recall that for any tangent vector
(z,V)\in T\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, the covariant derivative
\nabla^{A}_{V}P\in T_{P(z)}M
is defined by the property:

(B.3)

\nabla^{A}_{V}P=\frac{d}{dt}\rho(e^{t\cdot a(V)})P\big(\gamma(t)\big)\bigg|_{t=0}

where
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
is a path with
\gamma(0)=z
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=V
. The action of
G
extends to the tangent bundle
TM
of
M
:

\rho_{TM}(g)(p,v)\mapsto\big(\rho(g)p,\rho(g)_{*}v\big).

If
v
is a vector field along
P\circ\gamma(t)
, it is reasonable to define its derivative
\nabla^{A}_{V}v\in T_{P(z)}M
as:

(B.4)

\nabla^{A}_{V}v\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nabla_{\nabla^{A}_{V}P}\rho(e^{t\cdot a(V)})_{*}v.

Remark B.2
.

If the connection 1-form
a\equiv 0
is trivial, the formula (
B.4
) reduces to
\nabla_{P_{*}V}v
and should be understood as follows: pull back the bundle
TM
along with the Levi-Civita connection via
P\circ\gamma
and differentiate the pullback section
(P\circ\gamma)^{*}v
at
t=0
. In other words,
\nabla_{P_{*}V}v\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nabla_{V}(P^{*}v)
, which might be non-zero even if
P\circ\gamma(t)\equiv P(z)
is a constant path. In terms of local coordinates
\{y^{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
,

(B.5)

\nabla_{P_{*}V}v\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\partial_{t}v^{i}(t)\partial_{i}+\frac{\partial y^{j}\big(P\circ\gamma(t)\big)}{\partial t}v^{i}(t)\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k},

where
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
are Christoffel symbols and
v=v^{i}\partial_{i}:[0,1]_{t}\to TM
is the smooth section lying over
P\circ\gamma
. If
a
is non-trivial, first apply the transformation
\{\rho(e^{t\cdot a(V)})\}_{t\in[0,1]}
to kill
a
at
t=0
and then compute the derivative. The formula (
B.4
) is the practical formula only when
\nabla^{A}_{V}P\neq 0
.
∎

It is enlightening to find a concrete formula of
\nabla^{A}_{V}v
without using the group action. By the defining property of the moment map (
B.1
), we have

(B.6)

\nabla^{A}_{V}P=V\cdot P+\tilde{a}(V)=P_{*}(V)+J\langle\nabla\mu,a(V)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Lemma B.3
.

The covariant derivative of a vector field
v
equals:

\nabla^{A}_{V}v=\nabla_{P_{*}V}v+J\langle\Hess\mu(v),a(V)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

where
\nabla_{P_{*}V}v
denotes the covariant derivative with respect to the Levi-Civita connection.

Remark B.4
.

The correction term
J\langle\Hess\mu(v),a(V)\rangle
reflects the dependence on the connection 1-form
a
. It is linear in
a
,
v
and
V
as expected.
∎

Proof.

The formal proof is to linearize the equation (
B.6
) along the tangent vector
v(z)\in T_{P(z)}M
. Let us make this intuition precise. Consider a variation of
P\circ\gamma
along the vector field
v
:

Q(r,t,s)=\rho\big(e^{r\cdot a(V)}\big)\exp_{P\circ\gamma(t)}\big(sv(t)\big).

When
r\equiv 0
,
Q(0,t,s)
is a variation of the path
P\circ\gamma(t)
. Indeed,
Q(0,t,0)\equiv P\circ\gamma(t)
. When
s\equiv 0
, the covariant derivative of
P
is defined as (comparing (
B.3
)):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}Q(t,t,0)\bigg|_{t=0}=\nabla^{A}_{V}P

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}Q(0,t,0)+\frac{d}{dt}Q(t,0,0)\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=P_{*}(V)+J\langle\nabla\mu,a(V)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Let
U_{1}=\frac{d}{dt}Q(t,t,s)
and
U_{2}=\frac{d}{ds}Q(t,t,s)
. Then
U_{1}=U_{3}+U_{4}
with

U_{3}=(\partial_{2}Q)(t,t,s),U_{4}=(\partial_{1}Q)(t,t,s).

U_{4}=J\langle\nabla\mu,a(V)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
. When
t=s=0
,
U_{3}=P_{*}V
. Moreover,
[U_{2},U_{4}]=0
.
By (
B.4
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{V}v=\nabla_{U_{1}}U_{2}\bigg|_{t=s=0}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{U_{3}}U_{2}+\nabla_{U_{4}}U_{2}\bigg|_{t=s=0}=\nabla_{P_{*}V}v+\nabla_{U_{2}}U_{4}\bigg|_{t=s=0}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{P_{*}V}v+J\langle\Hess\mu(v),a(V)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.\qed

The next lemma concerns the curvature tensor of
\nabla^{A}
. Since we are merely interested in the manifold
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, it suffices to work with vector fields
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{s}
.

Lemma B.5
.

Write
T=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P
and
S=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P
for short. The following properties hold for any configuration
(A,P)
and any vector field
v
along
P
:

(1)

The connection
\nabla^{A}
is equivariant under the gauge transformation
u(A,P)=(A-u^{-1}du,u\cdot P)
, i.e.

u_{*}(\nabla^{A}_{V}P)=\nabla^{u(A)}_{V}u(P),\ u_{*}(\nabla^{A}_{V}v)=\nabla^{u(A)}_{V}u_{*}v,

where
u_{*}v
is the vector field along
u(P)
.

(2)

If
v
is the pull-back of a
G
-invariant vector field on
M
, then
\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v=\nabla_{S}v
.

(3)

(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}})P=J\langle\nabla\mu,F_{A}(\partial_{t},\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=-\tilde{F}
where
F=-*_{2}F_{A}
.

(4)

For any vector fields
v,w
on
\im P\subset M
,

\partial_{s}\langle v,w\rangle=\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v,w\rangle+\langle v,\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}w\rangle,

i.e. the connection
\nabla^{A}
is unitary.

(5)

The curvature tensor of
\nabla^{A}
is given by

(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}})v=R_{M}(T,S)v+J\langle\Hess\mu(v),F_{A}(\partial_{t},\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

where
R_{M}(\cdot,\cdot)\cdot
denotes the Riemannian curvature tensor on
M
.

Proof.

The property (1) follows from the defining property (
B.3
) and (

) of
\nabla_{A}
.

If
v
is induced from a
G
-variant vector field on

. By (

\nabla^{A}_{V}v\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nabla_{\nabla^{A}_{V}P}\rho(e^{t\cdot a(V)})_{*}v=\nabla_{\nabla^{A}_{V}P}v.

This proves (2). For (3), if
F_{A}\equiv 0
near a point

, then we apply a gauge transformation

so that the connection 1-form

near

. Thus,

u_{*}(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}})P=\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\partial_{s}u(P)-\nabla_{\partial_{s}}\partial_{t}u(P)=0.

This shows the commutator is at least proportional to
*F_{A}
. To work out the general case, we apply (

) and Lemma

:

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}S+J\langle\Hess\mu(S),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\partial_{s}P+\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}J\langle\nabla\mu,a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+J\langle\Hess\mu(S),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\partial_{s}P+J\langle\nabla\mu,\partial_{t}a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\Hess\mu(\langle\nabla\mu,a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\qquad+J\langle\Hess\mu(\partial_{t}P),a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+J\langle\Hess\mu(\partial_{s}P),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

At this point, we need the following fact. For any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g}

\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle\nabla\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),\eta\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle\nabla\mu,\eta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),\xi\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

This follows from the relation

(since

\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P

As for (4), we use the gauge invariance of
\nabla^{A}
. Alternatively, one uses Lemma
B.3

(B.7)

\langle J\Hess\mu(v),w\rangle_{TM}+\langle J\Hess\mu(w),v\rangle_{TM}=0,

which follows from (

).

The expression of the curvature tensor

follows from the definition of

. The actually proof is not very tidy. We follow the strategy of (3):

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v+J\langle\Hess\mu(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\nabla_{\partial_{s}P}v+J\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\langle\Hess\mu(v),a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+J\langle\Hess\mu(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\qquad+J\langle\Hess\mu(\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}v),a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+J\langle\Hess\mu(\nabla_{\partial_{s}P}v),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\qquad+J\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle J\Hess\mu(v),a(\partial_{s})\rangle\big),a(\partial_{t})\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

There are six terms in the expression. The fourth and fifth ones will also occur in that of

\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}v-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}v

with

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\nabla_{\partial_{s}P}v-\nabla_{\partial_{s}P}\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}v=R_{M}(\partial_{t}P,\partial_{s}P)v,

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}J\langle\Hess\mu(v),\partial_{t}a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-J\langle\Hess\mu(v),\partial_{s}a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=J\langle\Hess\mu(v),F_{A}(\partial_{t},\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

\displaystyle N_{3}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}J\langle(\nabla_{\partial_{t}P}\Hess\mu)(v),a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-J\langle(\nabla_{\partial_{s}P}\Hess\mu)(v),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

\displaystyle N_{6}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}J\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle J\Hess\mu(v),a(\partial_{s})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),a(\partial_{t})\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-J\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle J\Hess\mu(v),a(\partial_{t})\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),a(\partial_{s})\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

On the other hand, we have

R_{M}(T,S)-R_{M}(\partial_{t}P,\partial_{s}P)=R_{M}\big(\partial_{t}P,\tilde{a}(\partial_{s})\big)+R_{M}\big(\tilde{a}(\partial_{t}),\partial_{s}P\big)+R_{M}\big(\tilde{a}(\partial_{t}),\tilde{a}(\partial_{s})\big).

To complete the proof of (
5
), it remains to verify that

\displaystyle N_{3}

\displaystyle=R_{M}\big(\partial_{t}P,\tilde{a}(\partial_{s}))v-R_{M}(\partial_{s}P,\tilde{a}(\partial_{t})\big)v,

\displaystyle N_{6}

\displaystyle=R_{M}\big(\tilde{a}(\partial_{t}),\tilde{a}(\partial_{s})\big)v,

which is the content of Lemma
B.6
and
B.7
.
∎

Lemma B.6
.

For any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
and vector fields
v,w
on
M
, we have

R_{M}(w,\tilde{\xi})v=\langle\nabla_{w}(J\Hess\mu)(v),\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Proof of Lemma.

Differentiating (
B.7
) along a vector field
u
on
M
yields

\langle\nabla_{u}(J\Hess\mu)(v),w\rangle_{TM}+\langle\nabla_{u}(J\Hess\mu)(w),v\rangle_{TM}=0.

The key observation is that for any vector fields
u,v,w
, we have

\langle R_{M}(u,v)w,J\nabla\mu\rangle_{TM}=-\langle\nabla_{u}(J\Hess\mu)(v),w\rangle_{TM}-\langle\nabla_{v}(J\Hess\mu)(w),u\rangle_{TM}.

Indeed, we use the symmetry of curvature tensor to compute:

\displaystyle\langle R_{M}(u,v)w,J\nabla\mu\rangle_{TM}

\displaystyle=-\langle R_{M}(u,v)J\nabla\mu,w\rangle_{TM}

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla_{u}(J\Hess\mu)(v),w\rangle_{TM}+\langle\nabla_{v}(J\Hess\mu)(u),w\rangle_{TM}

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla_{u}(J\Hess\mu)(v),w\rangle_{TM}-\langle\nabla_{v}(J\Hess\mu)(w),u\rangle_{TM}.

This expression is unchanged if we permute
(u,v,w)
. Using the symmetry

R_{M}(u,v)w+R_{M}(v,w)u+R_{M}(w,u)v=0

from Riemannian geometry, we conclude that

\langle\nabla_{u}(J\Hess\mu)(v),w\rangle_{TM}+\langle\nabla_{v}(J\Hess\mu)(w),u\rangle_{TM}+\langle\nabla_{w}(J\Hess\mu)(u),v\rangle_{TM}=0.

In particular, this implies
\langle R_{M}(u,v)w,J\nabla\mu\rangle_{TM}=\langle\nabla_{w}(J\Hess\mu)(u),v\rangle_{TM}.
Finally, note that
\tilde{\xi}=\langle\nabla\mu,\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
and

\langle R_{M}(w,\tilde{\xi})u,v\rangle=\langle R_{M}(u,v)w,\tilde{\xi}\rangle=\langle\nabla_{w}(J\Hess\mu)(u),v\otimes\xi\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.\qed

Lemma B.7
.

For any
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g}
and any vector field
v
on
M
,

R_{M}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})v=J\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle J\Hess\mu(v),\eta\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),\xi\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-J\big\langle\Hess\mu\big(\langle J\Hess\mu(v),\xi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\big),\eta\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

Proof of Lemma.

This identity is equivalent to that

(B.8)

\langle R_{M}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})v,w\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{w}\tilde{\xi}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi},\nabla_{w}\tilde{\eta}\rangle,

for any vector field
w\in\Gamma(M,TM)
. We first rewrite the right hand side of (
B.8
):

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{w}\tilde{\xi}\rangle+\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi},\nabla_{w}\tilde{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=-v\cdot\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{w}\tilde{\xi}\rangle+\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{v}\nabla_{w}\tilde{\xi}\rangle+w\cdot\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi}\rangle-\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{w}\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi}\rangle.

By (
B.2
) and the relation
\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta}=\nabla_{\tilde{\eta}}\tilde{\xi}
, we have

\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{\tilde{\eta}}\tilde{\xi},v\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{\tilde{\xi}}\tilde{\eta},v\rangle=\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\xi}\rangle.

Therefore,
v\cdot\langle\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\xi}\rangle=2\langle\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}\rangle
. Moreover,

\displaystyle-v\cdot\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{w}\tilde{\xi}\rangle+w\cdot\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{v}\tilde{\xi}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}v\cdot w\langle\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\xi}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}w\cdot v\langle\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\xi}\rangle,

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}[v,w]\langle\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\xi}\rangle=-\langle\tilde{\eta},\nabla_{[v,w]}\tilde{\xi}\rangle.

Finally, we conclude that
I=\langle R_{M}(v,w)\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta}\rangle=\langle R_{M}(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{\eta})v,w\rangle
.
∎

B.2.
A Bochner-Type Formula

By abuse of notations, we denote the trivial bundle
\mathfrak{g}\times M\to M
also by
\mathfrak{g}
. Define a bundle map
D:TM\to\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathfrak{g}\oplus TM
as follows: for any tangent vector
v\in T_{x}M
, define its image as:

D_{x}(v)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big(\langle\nabla\mu,v\rangle_{TM},\langle\nabla\mu,Jv\rangle_{TM},\Hess_{x}L(v)\big)\in\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathfrak{g}\oplus T_{x}M.

Bochner’s formula
[
Pet16
, P. 334]
was originally stated for a harmonic function
u:N\to\mathbb{R}
on a Riemannian manifold
N
. It computes the Laplacian of
|\nabla u|^{2}
:

0=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{N}|\nabla u|^{2}+|\Hess u|^{2}+\Ric(\nabla u,\nabla u).

We provide a formula in the same spirit for a solution
(A,P)
to the gauged Witten equation (
4.1
) on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, with the bundle map
D
playing the role of
\Ric(\cdot,\cdot)
. The Laplacian operator
\Delta_{N}
or
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}
is always assumed to have a non-negative spectrum. In particular,

\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}=-(\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{s}^{2}).

Theorem B.8
.

Write
T=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P
,
S=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P
and
F=-*_{2}F_{A}
for short. For a solution
(A,P)
to the gauged Witten equation
(\ref{generalized-vortex-equation})
on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}
, we have identities:

(1)

The Laplacian
\frac{1}{2}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}})|T|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{s}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{2})|T|^{2}
of
|T|^{2}
is equal to

\displaystyle|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T|^{2}+|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}T|^{2}+|D_{P}(T)|^{2}+\langle R_{M}(S,T)S,T\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle(\nabla_{T}\Hess H)(\nabla H),T\rangle+\langle\Hess\mu(2JS-T),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

(2)

Similarly,
\frac{1}{2}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}})|S|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{s}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{2})|S|^{2}
is equal to

\displaystyle|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}S|^{2}+|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}S|^{2}+|D_{P}(S)|^{2}+\langle R_{M}(T,S)T,S\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle(\nabla_{S}\Hess H)(\nabla H),S\rangle+\langle\Hess\mu(-2JT-S),S\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

(3)

The Laplacian
\frac{1}{2}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}})|F|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}
of
|F|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}
is equal to

\displaystyle|\partial_{s}F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}+|\partial_{t}F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}+|\langle\nabla\mu,F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}|^{2}+2\langle\Hess\mu(JS),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

Define
\nabla^{A}P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}dt\otimes T+ds\otimes S
and
\Hess_{A}P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}\omega_{i}\otimes\omega_{j}\otimes\nabla^{A}_{e_{i}}\nabla^{A}_{e_{j}}P
with
(e_{1},e_{2})=(\partial_{t},\partial_{s})
and
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=(dt,ds)
.

Corollary B.9
.

We have the following identity for
\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}\big(|\nabla^{A}P|^{2}+|F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}\big)
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}\big(|\nabla^{A}P|^{2}+|F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}\big)+|\Hess_{A}P|^{2}+|\nabla F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}+|D_{P}(\nabla^{A}P)|^{2}+|\langle\nabla\mu,F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\langle R(S,T)S,T\rangle+\langle(\nabla_{T}\Hess H)(\nabla H),T\rangle+\langle(\nabla_{S}\Hess H)(\nabla H),S\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+6\langle\Hess\mu(JS),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\Hess\mu(T),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\Hess\mu(S),S\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

Proof of Corollary
B.9
.

It follows by adding up the three identities from Theorem
B.8
.
∎

Define the connection Laplacian of
P
as:

\Delta_{A}P\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}P-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}P=-(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}T+\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}S).

To prove Theorem
B.8
, we start with a useful formula of
\Delta_{A}P
.

Lemma B.10
.

-\Delta_{A}P=J\tilde{F}+\Hess H(\nabla H)
.

Proof.

Apply the operator
-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}+J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}
to the equation (
4.11b
). Using the fact that
\Hess H
anti-commutes with
J
(Lemma
B.1
(3)) and Lemma
B.5
(2)(3), we deduce that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}+J\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}})(T+JS+\nabla H)

\displaystyle=-(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}T+\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}S)-J(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}S-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T)-\Hess H(T+JS)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{A}P+J(\tilde{F})+\Hess H(\nabla H).\qed

Remark B.11
.

It is enlightening to work out Lemma
B.10
and Theorem
B.8
concretely in the special case of Example
3.2
, with
M=\mathbb{C},W\equiv 0
and
\mu=\frac{i}{2}|x|^{2}
. The metric on
\mathbb{C}
is flat, and
T_{x}M
is identified with
\mathbb{C}
for each
x\in M
. Hence,

\nabla\mu(x)=x\otimes i\text{ and }\Hess\mu=1_{\mathbb{C}}\otimes i.

Take
\delta=\frac{i}{2}\in\mathfrak{g}=i\mathbb{R}
. In this case, the equation (
4.1
) recovers the vortex equation on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\bar{\partial}_{A}P&=0\\
-*F_{A}+\mu&=\frac{i}{2}\end{array}\right.

where
A
is a unitary connection and
P:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
is a smooth function. Then Lemma
B.10
says

\Delta_{A}P=\langle F,i\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}P,

which follows from the Weitzenböck formula. Since
\nabla H\equiv 0
,
T=-JS
. The first two identities in Theorem
B.8
yield:

0=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}|\nabla_{A}P|^{2}+|\Hess_{A}P|^{2}+|D(\nabla_{A}P)|^{2}-3\langle F,i\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}|\nabla_{A}P|^{2},

where

and so

. In the meanwhile, we have

0=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{+}}|F|^{2}+|\nabla F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}+|P|^{2}|F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}-|\nabla_{A}P|^{2}\langle F,i\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

These formulae were first proved in
[
JT80
, Proposition 6.1]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
B.8
.

Let us start with
\partial_{s}^{2}|T|^{2}
. By Lemma
B.5
(4),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}^{2}|T|^{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{s}\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T,T\rangle=|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T|^{2}+\langle T,\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T\rangle.

By Lemma
B.5
(3)(5), we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T

\displaystyle=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}S+\tilde{F})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}S+R_{M}(S,T)S+\langle J\Hess\mu(S),F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\tilde{F},

which implies that

(B.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{s}^{2})|T|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T|^{2}+|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}T|^{2}+\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}(-\Delta_{A}P),T\rangle+\langle R_{M}(S,T)S,T\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle J\Hess\mu(S),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}+\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\tilde{F},T\rangle.

By using the equation (
4.11a
), we extract some positivity from the last term:

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\tilde{F},T\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\langle J\nabla\mu,F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},T\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle J\Hess\mu(S),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}+\big\langle\langle J\nabla\mu,\partial_{s}F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},T\big\rangle.

\displaystyle=\langle J\Hess\mu(S),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}+\langle\langle\nabla\mu,JT\rangle_{TM},\langle\nabla\mu,S\rangle_{TM}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}.

By Lemma
B.1
(5),
\langle\nabla\mu,J\nabla H\rangle_{TM}=0
. Using (
4.11b
), we have

\langle\nabla\mu,JT\rangle_{TM}=\langle\nabla\mu,S-J\nabla H\rangle_{TM}=\langle\nabla\mu,S\rangle_{TM}.

Hence,
\langle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}\tilde{F},T\rangle_{TM}=\langle J\Hess\mu(S),T\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}+|\langle\nabla\mu,JT\rangle_{TM}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}
.

Now we deal with the term involving
\Delta_{A}P
in (
B.9
), using Lemma
B.10
. We exploit the fact that
\Hess H(\nabla H)
is a
G
-invariant vector field on
M
and Lemma
B.5
(2):

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}(-\Delta_{A}P)

\displaystyle=\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\big(J\tilde{F}+\Hess H(\nabla H)\big)

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}\langle\nabla\mu,F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+(\nabla_{T}\Hess H)(\nabla H)+\Hess H^{2}(T),

\displaystyle=-\langle\Hess\mu(T),F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\nabla\mu,\partial_{t}F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}+(\nabla_{T}\Hess H)(\nabla H)+\Hess H^{2}(T).

Note that by (
4.11a
),
-\big\langle\langle\nabla\mu,\partial_{t}F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},T\big\rangle=\big\langle\langle\nabla\mu,\langle\nabla\mu,T\rangle_{TM}\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},T\big\rangle=|\langle\nabla\mu,T\rangle_{TM}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}
.

Combining all these together, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{s}^{2})|T|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}T|^{2}+|\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}T|^{2}+\langle R_{M}(S,T)S,T\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+|\langle\nabla\mu,T\rangle_{TM}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}+|\langle\nabla\mu,JT\rangle_{TM}|_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}+|\Hess H(T)|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle(\nabla_{T}\Hess H)(\nabla H),T\rangle+\langle\Hess\mu(2JS-T),T\otimes\mathfrak{g}\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

The formula of
\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{s}^{2})|S|^{2}
is proved in a similar may. Finally, we deal with the Laplacian of
|F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}
. By (
4.11a
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}^{2}|F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=|\partial_{s}F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\langle F,\partial_{s}^{2}(-\mu)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}},

\displaystyle=|\partial_{s}F|^{2}_{\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\Hess\mu(S),S\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}-\langle\nabla\mu,\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}S\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

By Lemma
B.10
, we have

\displaystyle\langle-\nabla\mu,(\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{t}}T+\nabla^{A}_{\partial_{s}}S)\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=\langle-\nabla\mu,J\tilde{F}\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}+\langle-\nabla\mu,\Hess H(\nabla H)\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle=|\langle\mu,F\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}|^{2}+\langle\Hess\mu(\nabla H),\nabla H\otimes F\rangle_{TM\otimes\mathfrak{g}}.

At the last step, we used the identity:

(B.10)

\langle\Hess\mu(X),\nabla H\rangle_{TM}+\langle\nabla\mu,\Hess H(X)\rangle_{TM}=0

with
X=\nabla H
. Note that by Lemma
B.1
(5),
\langle\nabla\mu,\nabla H\rangle_{TM}\equiv 0
. Expanding the expression
X\cdot\langle\nabla\mu,\nabla H\rangle_{TM}\equiv 0
yields (
B.10
). This completes the proof of Theorem
B.8
.
∎

Appendix C
Surfaces with Cylindrical Ends

In this appendix, we generalize Proposition
7.3
for oriented surfaces with cylindrical ends. To start, we fix a conformal structure on a closed oriented surface
\Sigma_{g}
of genus
g
and consider the punctured surface

\Sigma_{g,n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Sigma_{g}\setminus\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\},

where
p_{j},\ 1\leq j\leq n
are distinct points on
\Sigma_{g}
and let
\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum p_{j}
be the associated positive divisor. Later, we will specify the metric on
\Sigma_{g,n}
compatible with this complex structure. We require that

\chi(\Sigma_{g,n})=2-2g-n\leq 0.

Thus one may take
g=0
if
n\geq 2
. The harmonic 1-form
\lambda
is allowed to have poles at each puncture
p_{j},1\leq j\leq n
, but further conditions are required:

Assumption C.1
.

Take
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma_{g,n},i\mathbb{R})
and assume that its
(1,0)
-part
\lambda^{1,0}\in H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}))
extends to a holomorphic section of the canonical bundle
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Lambda^{1,0}\Sigma_{g}\to\Sigma_{g}
twisted by
\mathcal{D}
. We require that

•

the residue of
\lambda^{1,0}
at each puncture
p_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
is non-zero, and

•

\lambda^{1,0}
has
2g-2+n
simple zeros on
\Sigma_{g,n}
.

For such a 1-form
\lambda
to exist, we must have
n\geq 2
.
∎

In particular, for some holomorphic coordinate
z_{j}
near
p_{j}\in\Sigma_{g}
and
a_{j}\neq 0\in\mathbb{C}
,

\lambda^{1,0}=-\frac{a_{j}dz_{j}}{z_{j}},

with
z_{j}(p_{j})=0
. By rescaling the coordinate function
z_{j}
if necessary, we assume that the neighborhood
z_{j}^{-1}(B(0,1)),1\leq j\leq n
are pairwise disjoint in
\Sigma_{g}
. In terms of the polar coordinate
z_{j}=\exp(-(s_{j}+i\theta_{j}))
with
s_{i}\in[0,\infty)
and
\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\lambda^{1,0}=a_{j}(ds_{j}+id\theta_{j}).

Pick a Riemannian metric of
\Sigma_{g,n}
which restricts to the product metric on each cylindrical end

U_{j}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}[0,\infty)_{s_{j}}\times(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})_{\theta_{j}}\subset\Sigma_{g,n},1\leq j\leq n,

and is compatible with the given complex structure. Then on each
U_{j}
, the 1-form
\lambda=2i(\im a_{j}ds_{j}+\re a_{j}d\theta_{j})
is covariantly constant.

Because the surface
\Sigma_{g,n}
is not compact, we have to work instead with a relative
spin^{c}\
structure
\widehat{\mathfrak{s}}=(\mathfrak{s},\varphi)
on
\Sigma_{g,n}
, where
\mathfrak{s}=(S^{+}=L^{+}\oplus L^{-},\rho_{2})
is a
spin^{c}\
structure on
\Sigma_{g,n}
and

\varphi:L^{+}|_{\coprod U_{j}}\cong\mathbb{C}

is a trivialization of
L^{+}
over the cylindrical ends
\coprod U_{j}
. As in Section
7
, we still have
L^{-}=L^{+}\otimes\Lambda^{0,1}\Sigma_{g,n}
. In this case, the relative Chern classes and the relative degrees of
\widehat{\mathfrak{s}},L^{+}
and
\Lambda^{0,1}\Sigma_{g,n}
are well-defined. In particular,
\deg\Lambda^{0,1}\Sigma_{g,n}=-(2g-2+n)\leq 0
and

c_{1}(\widehat{\mathfrak{s}})[\Sigma_{g,n},\partial\Sigma_{g,n}]=2\deg L^{+}+\deg\Lambda^{0,1}\Sigma_{g,n}.

We are interested in the case when
d\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\deg L^{+}\in[0,2g-2+n]
.

When
g=0
and
n=2
, we simply take
\Sigma_{0,2}=\mathbb{R}_{s}\times(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})_{\theta}
to be the product manifold. For the standard relative
spin^{c}\
structure
\widehat{\mathfrak{s}}_{std}=(S^{+}_{std},\rho_{2})
, we have

S^{+}_{std}=\mathbb{C}\oplus\Lambda^{0,1}\Sigma_{0,2}.

We take
(d,\nabla^{LC})
as the reference
spin^{c}\
connection. Then for any
\delta\in i\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda=2i((\im a)ds+(\re a)d\theta)
, there is a unique covariantly constant spinor
(\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})\in\Gamma(\Sigma_{0,2},S^{+}_{std})
(up to the action of
S^{1}
) that solves the equations

(C.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\check{\Psi}_{+}\otimes\check{\Psi}_{-}^{*}&=-\sqrt{2}\lambda^{1,0},\\
\frac{i}{2}\big(|\check{\Psi}_{+}|^{2}-|\check{\Psi}_{-}|^{2}\big)&\equiv\delta.\end{array}\right.

Back to the case of
\Sigma_{g,n}
, the gauged Landau-Ginzburg model

(M(\Sigma_{g,n},\widehat{\mathfrak{s}};\vec{\delta}_{*}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma_{g,n}))

now relies on an auxiliary function
\vec{\delta}_{*}
and is constructed as follows:

•

\vec{\delta}_{*}:\Sigma_{g,n}\to i\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function such that
\vec{\delta}_{*}\equiv\delta_{j}
for some constant
\delta_{j}\in i\mathbb{R}
on each end
U_{j}\subset\Sigma_{g,n}
;

•

the reference

connection
\check{B}_{*}
is identified with
(d,\nabla^{LC})
under the trivialization
\varphi

U_{j}

•

the reference spinor

is identified with the solution
(\check{\Psi}_{+,j},\check{\Psi}_{-,j})

•

for some

\kappa_{*}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\check{B}_{*},\check{\Psi}_{*})

•

The moment map

\displaystyle\mu(\check{B},\check{\Psi})

Proposition C.2
.

Suppose that the harmonic 1-form
\lambda\neq 0\in\Omega^{1}_{h}(\Sigma_{g,n},i\mathbb{R})
is chosen as above and Assumption
C.1
holds. Then for the gauged Landau-Ginzburg model

\big(M(\Sigma_{g,n},\widehat{\mathfrak{s}};\vec{\delta}_{*}),W_{\lambda},\mathcal{G}(\Sigma_{g,n})\big)

defined above, the critical locus
\Crit(L)
consists of

\binom{2g-2+n}{d}

closed free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits with
d=\deg L^{+}
. For any
\vec{\delta}\in L^{2}_{k-1}(\Sigma_{g,n},i\mathbb{R})
and any free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\Crit(L)
,
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\mathcal{O}
consists of a unique
\mathcal{G}(\Sigma)
-orbit. Moreover,
W_{\lambda}
is a Morse-Bott function. In this sense, we say that
W_{\lambda}
is stable and any
\vec{\delta}\in\Lie(\mathcal{G})
is
W_{\lambda}
-stable.

Proof.

The proof follows from the same line of arguments as in Proposition
7.4
, and we point out where the proof is modified. To construct a configuration in
\mu^{-1}(\vec{\delta})\cap\mathcal{O}
, we have applied an a priori estimate from Lemma
7.7
with

w_{+}=|\check{\Psi}_{+}|\text{ and }w_{-}=|\check{\Psi}_{-}|.

Here
\kappa=(\check{B},\check{\Psi}_{+},\check{\Psi}_{-})
is some representative in a free
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
\Crit(L)
. However, Lemma
7.7
is stated only for closed surfaces. We must adapt its proof to the non-compact surface
\Sigma_{g,n}
. To start, we choose
\kappa
(within the
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit) such that

\kappa|_{U_{j}}=(\check{B}_{*},\check{\Psi}_{*}),

i.e. it agrees with the reference configuration on each end
U_{j}\subset\Sigma_{g,n}
. As a result, for some
c>0
, we have

w_{+},w_{-}>c

on the union
\coprod U_{j}
. This allows us to derive the estimate:

\|d\alpha_{\pm}\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{\Sigma_{g,n}}\alpha_{\pm}^{2}w_{\pm}^{2}\geq c_{\pm}\|\alpha_{\pm}\|_{L^{2}_{1}}^{2},

as required in the proof of Lemma
7.7
. Finally, Trudinger’s inequality also holds for non-compact spaces; cf.
[
Tay11
, Chapter 13, Proposition 4.2]
or
[
Wan18
, Proposition A.3]
. Now the proof of Proposition
7.4
can proceed with no difficulty.
∎

Finally, we explain why Assumption
C.1
is a generic condition. Let
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\Lambda^{1,0}\Sigma_{g}
be the canonical bundle of
\Sigma_{g}
. Then we have a long exact sequence:

(C.2)

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}0\to H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K})\hookrightarrow&H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}))&\xrightarrow{\Res}&\mathbb{C}^{n}&\xrightarrow{\sigma}\mathbb{C}\to 0,\\
&\eta&\mapsto&(\Res_{p_{j}}(\eta))_{1\leq j\leq n}&\end{array}

where
\eta
is a holomorphic 1-form with poles possibly along
\mathcal{D}
and
\Res_{p_{j}}(\eta)
is the residue of
\eta
at
p_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
. The penultimate map
\sigma
takes the sum of all residues. This sequence is exact, because
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{D}))=g+n-1
by the Riemann-Roch theorem.

Lemma C.5
.

If
g\geq 1
, then for any vector
v=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
b_{j}\neq 0
,
1\leq j\leq n
and
\sigma(v)=0
, there is a dense open subset of
\Res^{-1}(v)
such that any
\eta
in this subset has
2g-2+n
simple zeros.

Proof of Lemma.

Let
V
be the subset of
\Res^{-1}(v)
consisting of sections with simples zeros.
V
is clearly open. To show that
V
is dense, consider a form
\eta\in\Res^{-1}(v)
with zeros at
x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}\subset\Sigma_{g}\setminus\mathcal{D}
(possibly with multiplicities). It suffices to find some
\eta^{\prime}\in H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K})
such that
\eta^{\prime}(x_{j})\neq 0
for any
1\leq j\leq m
; then the sum
\eta+\epsilon\eta^{\prime}
lies in
V

for any sufficiently small
\epsilon
. Since
g\geq 1
, the canonical bundle
\mathcal{K}
is base-point free. Indeed, by the Riemann-Roch theorem and Serre duality, for any
q\in\Sigma_{g}
,

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K}(-q))=g-1<\dim H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K}).

Now we set
\eta^{\prime}=\sum\epsilon_{j}\eta_{j}^{\prime}
for some
\epsilon_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\eta_{j}^{\prime}\in H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},\mathcal{K})
with
\eta_{j}^{\prime}(q_{j})\neq 0
,
1\leq j\leq m
.
∎

The case when
g=0
is more rigid, since the map
\Res
in (
C.2
) is injective. For any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, one may vary the positive divisor
\mathcal{D}
instead to achieve Assumption
C.1
:

Lemma C.6
.

If
g=0
, then for any vector
v=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
b_{j}\neq 0
,
1\leq j\leq n
and
\sigma(v)=0
, the section
\eta=\Res^{-1}(v)
has
n-2
simple zeros in
\Sigma_{0,n}=\mathbb{CP}^{1}\setminus\mathcal{D}
for a generic choice of
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof of Lemma.

By an automorphism of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, we set
p_{n}=\infty
. Then the holomorphic 1-form
\eta=\Res^{-1}(v)
can be constructed explicitly as

\eta=dz\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{b_{j}}{z-p_{j}}.

As we vary
p_{1}
in a small neighborhood, the difference

\frac{1}{z-p_{1}}-\frac{1}{z-p_{1}^{\prime}}=\frac{p_{1}-p_{1}^{\prime}}{(z-p_{1})(z-p_{1}^{\prime})},p_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C},

is non-vanishing on
\mathbb{C}
. Now one can argue as in the proof of Lemma
C.5
.
∎
