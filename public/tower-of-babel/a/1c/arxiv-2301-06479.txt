Combinatorial Hopf species and algebras from preorder cuts
Gunnar Fløystad

Abstract
. We introduce new concepts and viewpoints on combinatorial Hopf species and algebras. We give a category 𝐬𝐞𝐭 ℕ {\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}} whose objects are sets, and (dualizable) morphisms represented by matrices of non-negative integers. For a bimonoid species ( B , Δ , μ ) (B,\Delta,\mu) in 𝐬𝐞𝐭 ℕ {\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}} we may then dualize the product μ \mu to get two intertwined coproducts Δ , Δ ′ \Delta,\Delta^{\prime} . We consider restriction species 𝖲 \mathsf{S} over 𝐬𝐞𝐭 ℕ {\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}} accompanied by pairs of natural transformations π 1 , π 2 : 𝖲 → Pre \pi_{1},\pi_{2}:\mathsf{S}\rightarrow\rm Pre to the species of preorders. A simple construction associates two comonoid species Δ 1 \Delta^{1} and Δ 2 \Delta^{2} , and we investigate when they are intertwined. We get new Hopf algebras: i. choosing an arbitrary set of permutations without global descents, we get associated a quotient Hopf algebra of the Malvenuto-Reutenauer Hopf algebra of permutations avoiding this chosen set, ii. a Hopf algebra of pairs of parking filtrations, and iii. three Hopf algebras of pairs of preorders.

Introduction

A basic tenet of
combinatorial
Hopf algebras is that they
come with a distinguished
basis
(or sometimes several).
The basis elements are typically isomorphism classes of combinatorial
objects. To make a more refined setting one may work with Hopf species
where one has labeled objects, and then derive a Hopf algebra by a suitable
functor (the Fock functor
[
4
, Sec.15]
). A species
is a functor from
{\rm\bf set^{\times}}
(sets with bijections) to usually either the
category
{\rm\bf vect}
of vector spaces, or
{\rm\bf set}
the category of sets.

For combinatorial Hopf species using
{\rm\bf vect}
is common, but again
since they come with a distinguished bases, on the face of it

{\rm\bf set}
appears more direct. Versions using
{\rm\bf set}
are found in

[
1
, Def.2.5]
and
in
[
3
, Sec.4]
. However drawbacks are:

i.

These are somewhat ad hoc defined, they are not purely categorical.
In fact comonoids in species over
{\rm\bf set}

do not exist in the categorical sense.

ii.

The definition does not work for most Hopf algebras, as
a product of two basis elements is typically a sum of
basis elements.

0.1.
New concepts and points of view

Based on many of the most ubiquitous combinatorial Hopf algebras, we
introduce several new points of view:

1.
Replace
{\rm\bf vect}
with a category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
.
The objects
in this category are sets,
but has many more morphisms than
{\rm\bf set}
, bringing it closer to

{\rm\bf vect}
. In particular the empty set is a null object, and a morphism

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y
has a dual morphism
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Df}}{{\longrightarrow}}X
. By this we may get:

2.

Two coproducts
\Delta^{1},\Delta^{2}
.

Given a bimonoid with coproduct
\Delta
and product

\mu
, the product
\mu
may now be dualized to a coproduct
\Delta^{\prime}
.
We take two coproducts
\Delta^{1},\Delta^{2}
as our
starting point
, and
ask: When do these give a bimonoid when you dualize
\Delta^{2}
?
The rationale for this is that coproducts, splitting up, is usually
simpler to work with than products, all possible ways to assemble together.
So our focus turns to making coproducts:

3.

Coproducts from restrictions.

The species
\mathsf{S}
we shall work with have restrictions and these will give the
coproducts. For every injection of
sets
Y\hookrightarrow X
there is a restriction map

\mathsf{S}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{S}[Y],\quad s\mapsto s_{Y}.

For
X
a disjoint union
A\sqcup B
, our coproduct will send:

(1)

\mathsf{S}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{S}[A]\times\mathsf{S}[B],\quad s\mapsto\text{ restriction
pair }(s_{A},s_{B})\text{ or }{\mathbf{0}}.

This requires a decision whether to map
s
to
{\mathbf{0}}
or not, leading
to:

4.

Species over preorders.
There is a restriction species

\rm Pre
over
{\rm\bf set}
where
\rm Pre[X]
is the set of preorders
on
X
. We require our species
\mathsf{S}
to have a
natural transformation
\pi:\mathsf{S}\rightarrow\rm Pre
.
To each
s\in\mathsf{S}[X]
we get a preorder
\pi(s)
. This preorder
determines whether the image of
s
in (
1
) is
(s_{A},s_{B})

or
{\mathbf{0}}
(see Subsection
0.3
.
4.
).

0.2.
New Hopf algebras introduced

From the above we get several new classes of Hopf algebras
based on the following.

A.

Avoidance of permutations.

Let
S=\cup_{n\geq 0}S_{n}
be the union of permutations of all sizes.
Let
A\subseteq S
a set of permutations which have
no global descents
,
and
S_{/A}\subseteq S
the set of
A
-avoiding permutations.
We show the associated
vector space
{\Bbbk}S_{/A}
is a quotient Hopf algebra of the
Malvenuto-Reutenauer (MR) Hopf algebra, Theorem
6.2
.
Examples include:

•

A=\{213\}
gives the Loday-Ronco Hopf algebra,

•

A=\{213,132\}
gives the Hopf algebra of quasi-symmetric functions,

•

A=\{3142,2413\}
gives the Hopf algebra

{\mathcal{W}}{\mathcal{PP}}
of
[
9
]
.

B.

Parking filtrations.
J-C. Novelli and J-Y.Thibon

[
12
]
give a Hopf algebra of parking functions
having the MR-algebra as a quotient Hopf algebra. We introduce a larger
“master” Hopf algebra of pairs of parking filtrations,
Section
7
,
with the Hopf algebra of parking functions as a
sub-Hopf algebra.

C.

Pairs of preorders.

We introduce three large Hopf algebras coming from bimonoid
species
\mathsf{B}
where
\mathsf{B}[X]
consists of pairs of preorders
(P,Q)
on

X
, Sections
8
and
9
.
Such pairs come in three classes
{\rm\bf{cc}},{\rm\bf{nc}},{\rm\bf{nn}}
, each giving
a large “master” Hopf algebra.

0.3.
Our approach to get bimonoid species

We describe in more detail our approach to

1, 2, 3, 4
above.

1a.
For sets
X
and
Y
in the category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
a morphism

f:X\rightarrow Y
is a map of sets
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\longrightarrow}}\text{Hom}(Y,{\mathbb{N}})
, associating
for each
x\in X
a multisubset of
Y
. Such a map may be represented
by and
|X|\times|Y|
-matrix with entries in non-negative integers.
By transposing the matrix we get a dual map
Df:Y\rightarrow X
.
The empty set is a null object in this category.

Combinatorial bimonoid species are then categorical bimonoid species

{\rm\bf set^{\times}}\rightarrow{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
. To get antipodes and combinatorial Hopf species one
just need to extend to
{\rm\bf set^{\times}}\rightarrow{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}
.

1b.
For species
\mathsf{S}
in vector spaces, a coproduct

\Delta:\mathsf{S}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{S}[A]\times\mathsf{S}[B]

usually takes a basis element
to
a single pair of basis element. But a product

(2)

\mu:\mathsf{S}[A]\times\mathsf{S}[B]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathsf{S}[X]

often takes a pair of basis
elements
(s_{A},s_{B})
to a sum of basis elements. However this sum can
usually be identified with a set (or multiset) of basis elements, so
we can work with species
{\rm\bf set^{\times}}\rightarrow{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
.

2.

In the category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
the product map
\mu
in (
2
)
may be dualized to a
coproduct map

\Delta^{\prime}:\mathsf{S}[X]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathsf{S}[A]\times\mathsf{S}[B].

Thus if
\mathsf{S}
is a bimonoid, we get two coproducts
\Delta
and

\Delta^{\prime}
. One may then turn things around, start from

\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
and inquire when do we get a bimonoid by
dualizing the latter. For restriction comonoids this is the notion of

intertwined
coproducts, Definition
3.2
.

3.
Often our species
\mathsf{S}
has restrictions.
For an injection
Y\hookrightarrow X
we have restriction maps

\mathsf{S}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{S}[Y],\quad s\mapsto s_{Y},

and the coproduct

\mathsf{S}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{S}[A]\times\mathsf{S}[B],\quad s\mapsto(s_{A},s_{B})\text{ or }{\mathbf{0}},

where
X=A\sqcup B
. This is the case for the Connes-Kreimer (CK) Hopf
algebra and the Malvenuto-Reutenauer Hopf algebra. For instance in
the CK-case a tree is mapped to a restriction pair
(t_{A},t_{B})
if

(A,B)
is an admissible cut, and otherwise
t
maps to
{\mathbf{0}}
.
What must be decided is
when
to map to
{\mathbf{0}}
.

4.
For this we introduce species over preorders.
Denote by
{\rm\bf set^{ci}}
the category of sets with coinjections
X\rightarrow Y
as
morphisms (i.e.
Y\hookrightarrow X
is an injection).

\rm Pre:{\rm\bf set^{ci}}\rightarrow{\rm\bf set}
is the species with
\rm Pre[X]
all preorders
on the set
X
. We work with restriction species
\mathsf{S}:{\rm\bf set^{ci}}\rightarrow{\rm\bf set}

with a natural transformation
\pi:\mathsf{S}\rightarrow\rm Pre
.
So for every
s\in\mathsf{S}[X]
we have a preorder
\pi(s)
. Then

s
maps to the restriction pair
(s_{A},s_{B})
iff
(A,B)
is a
cut

for
\pi(s)
, otherwise
s
maps to
{\mathbf{0}}
. By a cut we mean that

A
is a down-set in
\pi(s)
and
B
its complement up-set.

A restriction species over preorders then gives a coproduct on species.
If there are
two
structures as species over preorders

\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:\mathsf{S}\rightarrow\rm Pre
, we get two coproducts
\Delta^{1}

and
\Delta^{2}
. It is usually simple and direct to verify if they
are intertwined, Subsection
3.2
,
thus giving two bimonoid species by dualizing either
of these coproducts.

0.4.
Combinatorial Hopf algebras

At the start of the introduction we stated that a combinatorial Hopf
algebra (CHA) comes with a distinguished basis. This is by many examples.
However we mention that in well-known
definitions of CHA’s in the literature, bases are not explicitly required.

[
2
]
defines a CHA as a graded Hopf algebra with a distinguished
character.
[
10
]
requires the coalgebra to be isomorphic to
a cofree coalgebra, either cocommutative or coassociative.
A definition explicitly requiring a basis is in
[
7
, Def.3.3]
.
We do not venture a definition of CHA’s but note that our bimonoid
species being based on sets, gives a basis for the associated bi-algebras
or Hopf algebras.

0.5.
Organization of the article.

1
.

Bimonoid species in sets with multimaps.

The category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
is introduced and the notion of partial
pull-back diagrams for partial maps in this category. We
recall the notions of i. species, ii. species with restrictions and
iii. bimonoid species in this category.

2
.

The lattice of preorders.

Preorders and basic notions for these are recalled. We consider
the notion of global descents for pairs of total orders, and
the notion of refinement of preorders.

3
.

Bimonoids in species over Pre.

We see how a restriction species over
\rm Pre
give rise to a comonoid.
With two such structures we get two coproducts, and define the notion of
intertwined coproducts.

4
.

Sub-bimonoids by avoidance.

For a subspecies
\mathsf{A}
of a restriction species
\mathsf{S}
we introduce the

\mathsf{A}
-avoiding sub-species
\mathsf{S}_{/{\mathsf{A}}}
, and investigate when two intertwining
coproducts for
\mathsf{S}
are still intertwined for
\mathsf{S}_{/{\mathsf{A}}}
.

5
.

Hopf algebras from restriction
species over preorders.

We discuss how various Hopf algebras: of polynomials, of tensors,
of graphs and of preorders,
come from restriction species over preorders.

6
.

Hopf algebras of permutations.

The Malvenuto-Reutenauer (MR) Hopf algebras may be viewed in two ways
as coming from a restriction species over preorders. We give a main
general consequence, Theorem
6.2
: For
sets of permutations without global descents, the avoiding
subspecies of this gives quotient Hopf algebras of the MR Hopf algebra.
This drops almost immediately out of our setting, but does not
seem to have been stated in the literature before.
An example case is the Loday-Ronco Hopf algebra.

7
.

Hopf algebras of parking filtrations.

We introduce parking filtrations and
a new large master Hopf algebra consisting of pairs of
parking filtrations. It has the Hopf algebra of parking functions
[
12
]

as a sub Hopf algebra.

8
.

Bimonoid species of pairs of preorders.

We consider species
\mathsf{S}
such that
\mathsf{S}[X]
consists pairs
of preorders
(P,Q)
on
X
. We investigate when the resulting
two natural structures as species over preorders give two intertwined
coproducts. There are three basic types of such pairs:
{\rm\bf{cc}},{\rm\bf{nc}},{\rm\bf{nn}}
,
giving three species of preorders
\mathsf{CC},\mathsf{NC}
and
\mathsf{NN}
.
These give four large master Hopf algebras.

9
.

Pairs of preorders of types

{\rm\bf{cc}},{\rm\bf{nc}},{\rm\bf{nn}}
, and Hopf algebras by avoidance

We describe the three basic types of pairs of preorders in more detail,
and give examples of how we get other Hopf algebras by avoidance.

1.
Bimonoid species in sets with
multimaps

When considering species in a setting relating to combinatorial
Hopf algebras, one normally has a species in vector spaces.
However in the combinatorial setting these vector spaces
come with a distinguished basis (or even several). This suggests
it could be more natural to consider bimonoid or Hopf species in the category
of sets. However this is too restrictive, as the notion of comonoid is not
even defined for species in the category of sets. But there is an intermediate
solution which is fully satisfying, and gets one closer to a combinatorial
setting. One consider species in the category
of sets with
multimaps
. In this setting one has a null object, and one may
dualize maps.

1.1.
The category of sets with multimaps

Let
{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
be the natural numbers.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be sets. A
multimap

f:X\longrightarrow Y

is a set map
X\rightarrow\text{Hom}(Y,{\mathbb{N}})
.

This associates to each
x\in X
a multiset in
Y
.
A map
\tau:Y\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
sending
y
to
n_{y}
is written

\sum_{y\in Y}n_{y}y
.
Two multimaps
X\overset{f}{\longrightarrow}Y
and
Y\overset{g}{\longrightarrow}Z

may be composed as follows. If

x\overset{f}{\mapsto}\sum_{y\in Y}n^{x}_{y}y,\quad y\overset{g}{\mapsto}\sum_{z\in Z}m^{y}_{z}z,

then the composition sends

x\xmapsto{g\circ f}\sum_{z\in Z}\sum_{y\in Y}n^{x}_{y}m^{y}_{z}z.

We can represent
f
and
g
by matrices and then
g\circ f
is just
matrix multiplication.
To give a multimap
f:X\longrightarrow Y

is equivalent to give a set map
X\times Y\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
,
or a multisubset of
X\times Y
. The symmetry
X\times Y\cong Y\times X
,
shows that we equivalently get a dual multimap
Df:Y\longrightarrow X

(whose matrix is the transpose of
f
).

The multimap
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is an ordinary map if for each
x
the sum

\sum_{y\in Y}n^{x}_{y}=1
.

Definition 1.2
.

The map
f
is a
promap
if each

n^{x}_{y}
is either
0
or
1
.
This means that
f
factors as
X\rightarrow\text{Hom}(Y,\{0,1\})\rightarrow\text{Hom}(Y,{\mathbb{N}})
.
The image of
x\in X
may then be considered to be
a subset of
Y
.
The map
f
is a
partial map
if for each
x
the sum

\sum_{y\in Y}n^{x}_{y}
is
0
or
1

The following is easily verified as multimaps are given
by matrices of non-negative integers.

Lemma 1.3
.

A multimap
f:X\rightarrow Y
is an isomorphism iff it is an ordinary
map which is a bijection.

To give a promap
f
is equivalent to
give a map
X\times Y\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, or a subset of
X\times Y
which is
simply a relation between
X
and

Y
. The dual map
Df:Y\longrightarrow X
is then also a promap.
The composition of two
promaps will in general be a multimap and not a promap. However the
composition of a promap and a partial map is a promap, and the
composition of two partial maps is a partial map.

Let
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
be the category whose objects are sets and whose morphisms
are multimaps. Note that it is equivalent to the category of free
commutative monoids via the association
X\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{N}}X
.
The empty set is both an initial object and a terminal
object in
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
, so it is a null object. When the empty set is
considered to be in
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
we denote it as
{\mathbf{0}}
.

This category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
has all finite limits
and colimits. Both the product and coproduct are disjoint unions
of sets

X\prod Y=X\coprod Y=(X\times\{1\})\cup(Y\times\{2\}).

It is furthermore a symmetric monoidal category with product

X\times Y=\{(x,y)\,|\,x\in X,y\in Y\}

where the set
\{*\}
with one element is the unit.
The empty set, the null object
is absorbing for this product:
X\times{\mathbf{0}}={\mathbf{0}}
.
If
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\longrightarrow}}X^{\prime}
and
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y^{\prime}
are multimaps, we
get the multimap
X\times Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f\times g}}{{\longrightarrow}}X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
by

\displaystyle X\times Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f\times g}}{{\longrightarrow}}\text{Hom}(X^{\prime},{\mathbb{N}})\times\text{Hom}(Y^{\prime},{\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow

\displaystyle\text{Hom}(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime},{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\text{Hom}(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime},{\mathbb{N}}),

where the last map is induced by multiplication in
{\mathbb{N}}
.

Our point of view on Hopf species will be combinatorial rather than
algebraic. The category
{\rm\bf set}
is however not adequate, mainly because
it does not allow dualization of maps, and also does not have
a null object. The category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
does however.
It is closer to the category
of vector spaces, while still being based on sets.

1.4.
Species in the category
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}

Let
{\rm\bf set^{\times}}
be the category of sets with bijections as morphism.
A species in a category
\mathcal{C}
is a functor

\mathsf{P}:{\rm\bf set^{\times}}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
. Species becomes again a category with natural
transformations as morphisms. We shall consider species where

\mathcal{C}
is either
{\rm\bf set},{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
or
{\rm\bf vect}
. When
\mathcal{C}
is a monoidal category
with products, we can make species into a monoidal category.
For
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
and
{\rm\bf vect}
the monoidal products are respectively the
cartesian product
\times
and the tensor product
\otimes
.

For

{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
the monoidal product of species
\mathsf{P}
and
\mathsf{Q}
is defined as:

(\mathsf{P}\cdot\mathsf{Q})[X]=\coprod_{X=S\sqcup T}\mathsf{P}(S)\times\mathsf{Q}(T)

and the unit as:

1[X]=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{0}},&X\neq\emptyset\\
\{*\},&X=\emptyset\end{cases}.

The categories of species over
{\rm\bf set},{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
and
{\rm\bf vect}

are denoted as

{\mathsf{SpS}},\,\,{\mathsf{SpS}_{{\mathbb{N}}}},\,\,{\mathsf{SpV}}.

As usual in a monoidal category we then have the notion of a
monoid
.
It consists of natural transformations

\mu:\mathsf{P}\cdot\mathsf{P}\rightarrow\mathsf{P},\quad\iota:1\rightarrow\mathsf{P}.

For
{\mathsf{SpS}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}
the multiplication map
\mu
may for
X=S\sqcup T

be decomposed as multimaps

\mu_{S,T}:\mathsf{P}[S]\times\mathsf{P}[T]\longrightarrow\mathsf{P}[X].

The unit
i
also has a non-trivial multimap

\iota_{\emptyset}:\{*\}\longrightarrow\mathsf{P}[\emptyset]

which may be identified as an element of
\text{Hom}(\mathsf{P}[\emptyset],{\mathbb{N}})
.
These maps fulfill axioms
[
4
, 8.2.1]
, the most significant being that

\mu
is associative, corresponding to the commutativity of the following
diagram:

\textstyle{\mathsf{P}[R]\times\mathsf{P}[S]\times\mathsf{P}[T]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\mathsf{P}[R]\times\mathsf{P}[S\sqcup T]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\mathsf{P}[R\sqcup S]\times\mathsf{P}[T]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

[

, 8.2.2]
consisting of

which decompose into multimaps

Remark 1.7

In our settings we will always assume

The lattice of preorders

Our main notion in this article is restriction species over preorders.
We recall basics on preorders.

2.1.
Preorders

A
preorder

\leq
on a set
X
, is a relation on
X
which is:

1.

Reflexive:

a\leq a
for every
a\in X

2.

Transitive:
If
a\leq b
and
b\leq c
then
a\leq c
for

a,b,c\in X
.

Denote by
\rm Pre(X)
the set of all preorders on
X
.
It is a partially ordered set with order relation
\preceq

where

\leq_{1}\,\,\preceq\,\,\leq_{2}\text{ iff }a\leq_{1}b\Rightarrow a\leq_{2}b.

In fact
(\rm Pre(X),\preceq)
is a complete lattice. The smallest element is the
discrete order where no two distinct elements are comparable and
the largest element is the coarse order
\leq
where
a\leq b
for every
pair of elements
a,b\in X
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
order ideal
or
down-set
for a preorder
\leq
on
X
, is a subset
of
I
of
X
such that
y\in I
and
x\leq y
implies
x\in I
.
An
order filter
or
up-set
for
\leq
is a subset
F
of
X
such
that
x\in F
and
x\leq y
implies
y\in F
. Whenever
I
is an ideal
the complement set
F=X\backslash I
is a filter. Such a pair

(I,F)
is a
cut
for the preorder
\leq
.

Preorders on
X
correspond to finite topologies on
X
.
Given a preorder
P
, the open subsets of the topology
are the filters of
P
. So the open subsets are the upper sets
of cuts of
P
. In particular the discrete topology on
X

corresponds to the minimal element in
\rm Pre(X)
, the preorder
D

where the only comparability
relations are
a\leq a
for
a\in X
. The coarse topology,
where the only open subsets are
\emptyset
and
X
, corresponds
to the maximal element
C
in
\rm Pre(X)
, where
we have
a\leq b
for any two elements
a,b
in
X
.

For two preorders

P
and
Q
the meet

P\wedge Q
is the preorder
\leq
where
a\leq b
if
a\leq_{P}b

and
a\leq_{Q}b
.
The join
P\vee Q
is the unique smallest preorder
R

such that
P\preceq R
and
Q\preceq R
.
It is obtained by taking the transitive closure of the union of
the ordering relations for
P
and
Q
.
There is furthermore an involution on
\rm Pre(X)
by sending a preorder

P
to its opposite preorder
P^{\text{op}}
where
a\leq_{P^{\text{op}}}b
iff

b\leq_{P}a
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be preorders on
X
and

Y\subseteq X
. Then:

P_{|Y}\vee Q_{|Y}\preceq(P\vee Q)_{|Y},\quad P_{|Y}\wedge Q_{|Y}=(P\wedge Q)_{|Y}.

Furthermore if
Y
is either down-set or upset for
P\vee Q
,
we have equality in the first relation.

Proof.

It is clear that both
P_{|Y},Q_{|Y}\preceq(P\vee Q)_{|Y}
, giving
the first relation. The second is also clear.
For the last statement, suppose
a\leq b
in
(P\vee Q)_{|Y}
.
Then there is a sequence of elements in
X
:

a=x_{0}\leq_{P}x_{1}\leq_{Q}x_{2}\leq_{P}\cdots\leq_{Q}x_{r}=b.

If
Y
is a downset for
P\vee Q
, it is a down-set for both

P
and
Q
. Since
b=x_{r}\in Y
we
get
x_{r-1}\in Y
and then successicely all
x_{i}
in
Y
.
Hence we also have
a\leq b
in
P_{|Y}\vee Q_{|Y}
.
The argument when
Y
is an up-set is similar.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

Given a preorder
P
on
X
.
Define a relation on
X
by
a\circ b
if
a\leq b
and
b\leq a
.
This is an equivalence relation, and so it partitions
X
into
equivalence classes. Each such class is a
bubble
for
P
.
The preorder
P
is a poset if each bubble is a singleton.

If
a\leq b
and
a
and
b
are not in the same bubble, we write
a<b
.
If
a
and
b
are elements that are not comparable for the preorder
we write
a\,\mathrlap{{\hskip 5.12149pt}{\raisebox{1.4pt}{\scalebox{1}[0.45]{$\mid$}}}}{\raisebox{3.0pt}{\rule{9.3894pt}{0.5pt}}}_{\,}\,b
.

We have a
total preorder
if any two elements of
X
are comparable
for the preorder. If the total preorder is a poset it is a
total order
.
In this case for any two elements
a,b
in
X
precisely one
of the following holds:
a<b,a=b,a>b
.

The preorder is a
partition order
if
a\leq b
implies

a\circ b
. In other words only elements in the same bubble are comparable.
Such a preorder is the same as an equivalence relation.
Note that a preorder
P
is a partition order iff
P=P^{{\text{op}}}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
P
be a preorder.

a.
P^{\circ}=P\wedge P^{{\text{op}}}
is the partition order whose bubbles are the
bubbles of
P
. In particular
P
is a poset iff
P^{\circ}
is the
minimal element
D
.

b.
P^{\bullet}=P\vee P^{{\text{op}}}
is the partition order where the underlying sets
of the bubbles are the underlying sets of the connected components of

P
. In particular if
P
has only one component, then
P^{\bullet}
is
the maximal element
C
.

Proof.

Clearly
P^{\circ}=(P^{\circ})^{{\text{op}}}
, and so
P^{\circ}
is a partition order.
The same goes for
P^{\bullet}
. It is also clear that
a
and
b
are in the
same bubble of
P
iff they are in the same bubble of
P^{\circ}
.
Also
a
and
b
are in the same component of
P
iff they are in the
same bubble of
P^{\bullet}
.
∎

2.2.
Permutations and global descents

The
following will be used in Section
6
on
permutation Hopf algebras.
Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be total orders on
X
. Then
T_{1}
induces
an order preserving bijection
X\rightarrow[n]
. With this identification,
the pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
identifies as a permutation
\sigma
on
[n]
.

If one instead use
T_{2}
to give an order preserving bijection

X\rightarrow[n]
, the pair
(T_{2},T_{1})
identifies as the inverse
permutation
\sigma^{-1}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be total orders.

a. The preorder
T=T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{{\text{op}}}
is a total preorder.
It corresponds
to the global descent decomposition of
\sigma
: If we write

\sigma=n_{1}n_{2}\cdots n_{r-1}|n_{r}\cdots n_{s-1}|n_{s}\cdots

where we have global descents at
r-1,s-1,\cdots
and so on, then
each of the segments between the vertical markers
|
become
bubbles in
T
and where we have global descents, the ordering relation
in
T
is strict.

b. For the components of
S=T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
, their underlying
sets are precisely the underlying sets of
the bubbles in
T
, or formally
S^{\bullet}=T^{\circ}
.

Proof.

(T_{1},T_{2})
has no global descent.

First assume the permutation corresponding to
(T_{1},T_{2})
has no global
descents. We show that
T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{\text{op}}
is the maximal preorder
C
.
Let
x\leq_{1}y
. Since we have no global descent at
x
there are

z\leq_{1}x<_{1}w
such that
z\leq_{2}w
, and so
z\geq_{2}^{\text{op}}w
.
If
y\leq_{1}w
we get
x\leq_{1}y\leq_{1}w\leq_{2}^{\text{op}}z\leq_{1}x
, and
get
x
and
y
in the same bubble. If
w\leq_{1}y
, we get

x\leq_{1}w\leq_{2}^{\text{op}}z\leq_{1}x
and get
x,w
in the same bubble.
Then we may continue as above with the pair
w\leq_{1}y
, and get
successively
w=w_{1}<_{1}\cdots w_{r}
all in the same bubble, until
we end with
x
and
y
in the same bubble.

Still assuming
(T_{1},T_{2})
has no global descent,
we show that
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
is a connected poset, or

(T_{1}\wedge T_{2})\vee(T_{1}\wedge T_{2})^{\text{op}}=C
.
Let
x
be minimal for
T_{1}
. As we have no global descent, there
is
x<_{1}w
with
x<_{2}w
. So
x
and
w
are connected.
Let
x<_{1}y
be the covering. If
x>_{2}y
then
y<_{1}w
and

y<_{2}w
and so
y
and
w
are connected. If
x<_{2}y
then

x
and
y
are connected. In this way we may continue
and get the
\leq_{2}
-segment from
x
to
w
connected.
After that, as
w
is not a global descent, we may continue as above, and
get a connected
\leq_{2}
-segment from
w=w_{1}
to a large
w_{2}
.
Again we continue and eventually get
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
a connected poset.

The general situation.

From the above, if we have global descents at
x
and
y
and no
global descents in between, the interval
\langle x,y]
for

\leq_{1}
gets contained
in a bubble of
T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{\text{op}}
. Also any such interval
gets connected in
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
.

On the other hand, suppose there is
a global descent at
x
. Let
U=\{z\,|\,z\leq_{1}x\}
and
the complement
F=\{w\,|\,x<_{1}w\}
.
Then for any
z\in U
and
w\in F

we have
z<_{1}w
and
z>_{2}w
and so
z<_{2}^{\text{op}}w
. Hence for
such
z
and
w
we have strictly
z<w
in
T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{\text{op}}
.
Thus the ordering relation is strict at global descents.

Since
z<_{1}w
and
z>_{2}w
for
z\in U
and
w\in F
, we
cannot get
z
and
w
connected in
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
, as we cannot
get any transition from
U
to
F
.
Thus the global descents disconnect
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
.
∎

2.3.
Refinements of preorders

In Section
8
we investigate pairs of preorders
(P,Q)

and when they give intertwining comonoids.
In Section
9

we improve on the characterization
by the notion of refinement of partial orders which we now consider.

Definition 2.6
.

A preorder
P
is a
refinement
of
Q
if:

a.

Each bubble of
P
is contained in a bubble of
Q
:

a\circ_{P}b\Rightarrow a\circ_{Q}b.

b.

If
a,b
are
not
in the same
Q
-bubble, then:

a<_{Q}b\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a<_{P}b.

If the restriction of
P
to each bubble of
Q
is a partition order,
then
P
is a
bubble refinement
. This is equivalent to a. and

b’.

For any
a,b
:
a<_{Q}b\,\Leftrightarrow\,a<_{P}b
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Given a preorder
P
. There is a unique minimal total preorder
T
, such that

P
is a refinement of
T
.

Proof.

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are two total preorders with
P
as a refinement, then

T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
is also total: let
a,b
in
X
with
a\leq_{1}b
. If

a\leq_{2}b
then
a\leq_{T}b
. If
a<_{1}b
and
a>_{2}b
, then
we would have both
a<_{P}b
and
a>_{P}b
, impossible. Hence

T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
is total and it refines
P
. By intersecting all
total preorders refining
P
we get the minimal total preorder
T
.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
B
be a bubble in the unique minimal preorder
T
refining
P
.
Consider the incomparability relation
\,\mathrlap{{\hskip 5.12149pt}{\raisebox{1.4pt}{\scalebox{1}[0.45]{$\mid$}}}}{\raisebox{3.0pt}{\rule{9.3894pt}{0.5pt}}}_{\,}\,
of
P
restricted to

B
. If
B
is
not a bubble of
P
, the transitive closure of this relation is
an equivalence relation with

B
as the single class.

Proof.

Suppose
E
and
F
are distinct classes in
B
for this equivalence
relation. Let
x\in E
. Every element of
F
is comparable with

x
. We may then partition
F
into three classes:
F_{-1}
those elements

<x
,
F_{0}
those elements in the same bubble as
x
and
F_{1}
, those
elements
>x
. If
F_{0}
is non-empty, an element here would be
comparable to anything in
F
, which cannot be so.
That both
F_{-1}
and
F_{1}
are nonempty is not possible since

y<z
for any
y\in F_{-1}
and
z\in F_{1}
, and then
F
could
not be an equivalence class generated by
\,\mathrlap{{\hskip 5.12149pt}{\raisebox{1.4pt}{\scalebox{1}[0.45]{$\mid$}}}}{\raisebox{3.0pt}{\rule{9.3894pt}{0.5pt}}}_{\,}\,
.
Thus everything in
F
is say
>x
. Similarly picking a
y\in F
,
everything in
E
is either
<y
or
>y
, and the former
must be the case. The upshot is that
E<F
. In this way
we may totally order all classes. This contradicts
T
being
the unique minimal total preorder with
P
as refinement.
∎

4.
Sub-bimonoids by avoidance

Many Hopf algebras arise as sub-Hopf algebras or quotient Hopf algebra
of larger Hopf algebras, by requiring the basis elements to avoid
certain configurations. Both
the Connes-Kreimer and symmetric functions Hopf algebras arise
in this way, and also the Loday-Ronco Hopf algebra and quasi-symmetric
functions.

This raises the question of searching for “master Hopf algebras”,
those who do not apparently come from larger Hopf algebras by avoiding
certain configurations.
We shall see some such large Hopf algebras in the latter sections. But
let us here clarify the notions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a subspecies of the restriction species
\mathsf{S}
in
{\rm\bf set}
. (We do
not assume
\mathsf{A}
is also a restriction species.)

An
s\in\mathsf{S}[X]

avoids

\mathsf{A}

no injection

with
a=s_{Y}
in
\mathsf{A}[Y]

Otherwise
s

has an
\mathsf{A}
-part
.

For each finite set

let

be the
subset of
\mathsf{S}[X]
of
\mathsf{A}
-avoiding elements.
This is the
A
-avoiding subspecies
of
\mathsf{S}
.

We now consider
\mathsf{S}
as a species in
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}

\mathsf{S}

\mathsf{A}
-avoiding, both

s_{U}
and
s_{V}
will be

-avoiding. Thus
\mathsf{S}_{/{\mathsf{A}}}

\mathsf{S}

5.
Hopf algebras from restriction species over
preorders

We consider various well-known Hopf algebras and show how they
come from restriction species over preorders.
First we recall the Fock functor.

5.1.
The Fock functor

Let

\mathsf{S}:{\rm\bf set^{\times}}\rightarrow{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}

be a species. For
X=[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

write
\mathsf{S}[n]
for
\mathsf{S}[X]

Let
\mathsf{S}[n]_{S_{n}}

\mathsf{S}[n]
under the action of
the symmetric groups

The (bosonic) Fock functor of
\mathsf{S}

\overline{\mathcal{K}}(\mathsf{S})=\oplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\Bbbk}\mathsf{S}[n]_{S_{n}}.

In our cases we assume
\mathsf{S}[\emptyset]=\{*\}

\epsilon

the natural map, Definition
1.11

Then if
\mathsf{S}
is a bimonoid species
over
{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{N}}}}

{\rm\bf set_{{\mathbb{Z}}}}

The Fock functor applied to
\mathsf{S}
then becomes a Hopf algebra,

[
4

When considering species
\mathsf{S}
over vector spaces, one may also

\mathsf{S}[n]^{S_{n}}

In our situation with a species

\overline{\mathcal{K}}^{\vee}(\mathsf{S})

However, in general these Hopf algebras are isomorphic when the
characteristic of the field

When the species

By

we have

5.3.
The tensor algebra

Let
\mathsf{T}[X]
consists of pairs
(s,T)
where
s:X\rightarrow F
is a function,
and
T
is a total order on
X
.
Let the associated preorders be given by

\pi_{1}(s,T)=D,\quad\pi_{2}(s,T)=T.

For a cut
(U,V)
for the total order
T
, the restriction coproduct

\Delta^{2}:\mathsf{T}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{T}[U]\times\mathsf{T}[V],\quad\text{sends }(s,T)\mapsto(s_{U},T_{|U})\times(s_{V},T_{|V}).

Similarly for any decomposition
X=A\sqcup B
we get the restriction
coproduct

\Delta^{1}:\mathsf{T}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{T}[A]\times\mathsf{T}[B].

Species over preorders:

For
Y\subseteq X
:

\pi_{2}(s,T)_{|Y}=T_{|Y}=\pi_{2}(s_{Y},T_{|Y}),

and the similar holds obviously for
\pi_{1}
.

Extension:

Given the diagram (
10
), it
is clear that the maps
s_{AB},s_{AC},s_{CD}
and
s_{BD}
glue together to give
a unique map
s:X\rightarrow F
. Furthermore define the total order
T
by
its restrictions to
A\sqcup C
and
B\sqcup D
being given by
respectively
T_{AC}
and
T_{BD}
and if
x\in A\sqcup C
and
y\in B\sqcup D
then
x<_{T}y
. This is the unique possible order
if
A\sqcup B,C\sqcup D
is a cut for

.

Cuts:

It is clear that

(A\sqcup B,C\sqcup D)
and
(A\sqcup C,B\sqcup D)
are cuts
for
\pi_{1}(s,T)=D

\pi_{2}(s,T)=T
.

We get a bimonoid species
\mathsf{B}^{1}=(\mathsf{T},\Delta^{1},\mu_{2},\epsilon^{1},\iota_{2})

whose associated Hopf algebra by the Fock functor
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is the tensor
algebra

. The dual bimonoid

5.4.
Hopf algebra of graphs

Let
\mathsf{G}[X]
be the (simple) graphs with vertex set
X
.
For a graph
G
we let
\pi_{1}(G)=D
the discrete order and

\pi_{2}(G)
the partition order whose bubbles are the connected
components of
G
. So
x\leq_{2}y
if
x
and
y

are connected by a path in
G
. This gives
\mathsf{G}[X]
two structures
of species over preorders.
We may note that for
Y\subseteq X
then

\pi_{2}(G_{|Y})
in general
is a finer partition order than the restriction of
\pi_{2}(G)_{|Y}
,
since after restricting to a subset
Y
we may get more components
than before we restricted.
The coproduct

\Delta^{1}:\mathsf{G}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{G}[U]\times\mathsf{G}[V],\quad G\mapsto(G_{|U},G_{|V})

is simply restriction on each factor for any decomposition

X=U\sqcup V
. The coproduct for
\Delta^{2}
is
similar whenever
(U,V)
is a cut for
\pi_{2}(G)
.
It is straightforward to verify that these coproducts intertwined.

Applying the Fock functor to the bimonoid species

\mathsf{B}^{1}=(\mathsf{G},\Delta^{1},\mu_{2},\epsilon^{1},\iota_{2})
we get
Schmitt’s Hopf algebra of graphs
[
16
]
.

5.5.
Hopf algebras of posets or preorders

Let
\mathsf{P}[X]
be the set of all posets (alternatively we may consider
all preorders) on
X
.
For a poset
P
let
\pi_{1}(P)=P
and
\pi_{2}(P)=P^{\bullet}
, the
partition order whose bubbles are the connected components of
P
.
It gives the restriction coproduct

\Delta^{1}:\mathsf{P}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{P}[U]\times\mathsf{P}[V],\quad P\mapsto(P_{|U},P_{|V}),

when
(U,V)
is a cut for
\pi_{1}(P)=P
, and similarly for
\Delta^{2}
.
The corresponding restriction comonoids are easily checked to be
intertwined.

Applying the Fock functor to the bimonoid species

\mathsf{B}^{1}=(\mathsf{P},\Delta^{1},\mu_{2},\epsilon^{1},\iota_{2})
we get
Schmitt’s Hopf algebra of posets,
[
16
]
, or more
generally the Hopf algebra of finite topologies
[
8
]
.

Note that if we consider any class of posets closed under cuts
and disjoint unions
we get a Hopf sub-algebra of the Hopf algebra of posets.

Example 5.3
.

Connes-Kreimer.

The class of posets
avoiding the cherry poset, are the posets whose Hasse diagram is
a forest. Then
\Delta^{2}
is irreducible for this class, and
we get the Connes-Kreimer Hopf algebra as a sub-Hopf algebra.

Example 5.4
.

Symmetric functions.

The class of posets avoiding the cherry poset and its opposite,
the
V
-poset,
are those whose connected components are total orders.
Again
\Delta^{2}
is irreducible for this class, and
we get the Hopf algebra of symmetric functions as a sub-Hopf algebra.

6.
Hopf algebras of permutations

In this section the Malvenuto-Reutenauer (MR) Hopf algebra
[
11
]

is the master Hopf algebra,
and we see how various other Hopf algebras is obtained from it by
avoidance. We give

to get the MR-Hopf algebra
from intertwining restriction comonoids. The first corresponds
to the original way of defining the MR-Hopf algebra in
[

, sometimes
called the
F

The second corresponds to the
M

[
5

and
to the plane partition basis in
[

to be the effective one for considering sub- and quotient Hopf
algebras of avoidance,
Theorem
6.2
.

6.2.
Malvenuto-Reutenauer II

Consider again

\mathsf{S}[X]
consisting of pairs
s=(T_{1},T_{2})
of total orders on
X
.
In
[
9
]
, L.Foissy constructs a bijection between permutations in

and plane posets with

elements. The corresponding plane
poset has two orders, the vertical order
\leq_{v}
which is given
by
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
and the horizontal order
\leq_{h}
given by

T_{1}\wedge T_{2}^{{\text{op}}}
.

These two will not work as projection preorders for
\mathsf{S}[X]
, but it is
closely related to the following which does work.
Let
\pi_{1}(s)=T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{{\text{op}}}
and
\pi_{2}(s)=T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
.
Recall from Section

(T_{1},T_{2})
.
When
(U,V)
is a cut for
T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{{\text{op}}}

\Delta^{1}:\mathsf{S}[X]\rightarrow\mathsf{S}[U]\times\mathsf{S}[V],\quad(T_{1},T_{2})\mapsto(T_{1|U},T_{2|U};T_{1|V},T_{2|V}).

The coproduct
\Delta^{2}
is defined in the same way when
(U,V)
is
a cut for
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
.

Species over preorders:

Lemma
2.2
show this.

Extension:

Consider the diagram (
10
).
We determine both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
from the pair
s_{AB}
and
s_{CD}
.
Since

, the pair
(A\sqcup B,C\sqcup D)

is a cut for

also. Hence for

and

we must have
x<_{T_{1}}y
, and on

and

determine
T_{1}
from the restrictions
T_{1|AB}
and
T_{1|CD}
.
Similarly
T_{2}
may be determined from the cut
(A\sqcup B,C\sqcup D)
.

Cuts:

By construction

(A\sqcup B,C\sqcup D)
is a cut
for

. Let us argue that

(A\sqcup C,B\sqcup D)
is a cut for
T_{1}\wedge T_{2}
.
Let
x\in A\sqcup C

\mathsf{B}^{1}
be the bimonoid species

The bases

[

]

By letting
\mathsf{B}^{2}
be the bimonoid species
(\mathsf{S},\mu_{1},\Delta^{2},\iota_{1},\epsilon^{2})
, the dual of
\mathsf{B}^{1}
.
Then

6.4.
Hopf algebras of permutations with avoidance

Let
\mathsf{S}
again be the restriction species of pairs
(T_{1},T_{2})
of total orders,
and again we make this into two species over preorders by:

\pi_{1}(s)=T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{\text{op}},\quad\pi_{2}(s)=T_{1}\wedge T_{2}.

We saw in Subsection
6.2
that this gives two
intertwined restriction comonoids
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
.
Let
\mathsf{A}
be a subspecies (not necessarily restriction subspecies)
consisting of pairs
(T_{1},T_{2})
such that the associated permutation
has
no global descent
. This is equivalent to

T_{1}\vee T_{2}^{\text{op}}
being the coarse preorder
C
.
Let
A
be the set of permutations coming from the elements of
\mathsf{A}
.
They are permutations without global descents and may be of varying length.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
A
be a set of
permutations (of possibly different lengths) with no global descents.
Then the
A
-avoiding permutations form a Hopf algebra which is
a quotient Hopf algebra of the Malvenuto-Reutenauer Hopf algebra.

Formulated for species, let
\mathsf{S}
be the species of pairs
(T_{1},T_{2})

of total orders, and
\mathsf{A}
the sub-species
corresponding to the permutations in
A
.
The coproduct
\Delta^{1}
for
\mathsf{S}[X]
is
\mathsf{A}
-irreducible.
Hence the
\mathsf{A}
-avoiding subspecies
\mathsf{S}_{/{\mathsf{A}}}
of
\mathsf{S}
gives
a quotient bimonoid species
\mathsf{B}_{/{\mathsf{A}}}^{1}
of
\mathsf{B}^{1}
.

Applying the Fock functor we get the above statement for algebras:

7.1.
Parking functions and filtrations

Let

A

such that

as non-decreasing sequences

such that

becomes a parking function as above. So if an integer

are at least

<q

Definition 7.1

For a finite set

,
then

\geq i

In
[

,
14
]
, J-Y. Thibon and J-C. Novelli introduced
Hopf algebras of parking functions. To define such a Hopf algebra,
they needed to be able to “standardize” any sequence to a parking
function. For our purpose we need to standardize
any
exhaustive
filtration of
X
(meaning that

for
large
p
)

\emptyset=X_{0}\subseteq X_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq X_{p}\subseteq

to a parking filtration. We assume in the following that our filtrations
are always exhaustive.
