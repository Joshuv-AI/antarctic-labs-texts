Abstract
. In this paper we study germs of diffeomorphisms in the complex plane. We address the following problem: How to read a diffeomorphism f f knowing one of its orbits 𝔸 \mathbb{A} ? We solve this problem for parabolic germs. This is done by associating to the orbit 𝔸 {\mathbb{A}} a function that we call the dynamic theta function Θ 𝔸 \Theta_{\mathbb{A}} . We prove that the function Θ 𝔸 \Theta_{\mathbb{A}} is 2 ​ π ​ i ​ ℤ 2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\mathbb{Z} -resurgent. We show that one can obtain the sectorial Fatou coordinate as a Laplace-type integral transform of the function Θ 𝔸 \Theta_{\mathbb{A}} . This enables one to read the analytic invariants of a diffeomorphism from the theta function of one of its orbits. We also define a closely related fractal theta function Θ ~ 𝔸 \tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}} , which is inspired by and generalizes the geometric zeta function of a fractal string, and show that it also encodes the analytic invariants of the diffeomorphism.

1.
Introduction

Consider a germ of an analytic diffeomorphism of
({\mathbb{C}},0)
with a parabolic fixed point at the origin

(1.1)

f(x)=x+ax^{k+1}+\mathrm{h.o.t.}(x),\hskip 20.00003pta\neq 0,\hskip 10.00002ptk>0.

By the Leau–Fatou theorem (see
[
Bra10
,
IlY08
,
Lor06
]
), given a disc
D=\{x\in{\mathbb{C}},\ |x|<r\}
of sufficiently small radius
r>0
,
the set of points

(1.2)

D_{f}=\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}f^{\circ(-n)}(D)

whose all positive iterates stay in
D
, consists of
k
connected petals, disjoint except at the origin, centered around the
k
rays
\{-ax^{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\}
. Similarly for
D_{f^{\circ(-1)}}=\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}f^{\circ n}(D)
, the petals are centered around the rays
\{ax^{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\}
.
In fact, by a theorem of Camacho and Shcherbakov (see
[
Bra10
,
IlY08
,
Lor06
]
),
f
is topologically conjugated by a quasiconformal tangent-to-identity map to the germ

(1.3)

f_{\mathrm{mod}}(x)=\frac{x}{(1-akx^{k})^{\frac{1}{k}}}=\exp\big(ax^{k+1}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x\vphantom{\hat{X}}}\big)(x),

given by the time-1-flow of the model vector field
\xi_{\mathrm{mod}}=ax^{k+1}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x\vphantom{\hat{X}}}
.

Let
0\neq x_{0}\in D_{f}
be a point in one of the attractive petals, hence
f^{\circ n}(x_{0})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
, and let

(1.4)

\mathbb{A}=\{x_{n}=f^{\circ n}(x_{0}),\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\hskip 20.00003pt(\text{for us}\ {\mathbb{N}}:={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})

be its
forward orbit
by
f
.
A basic question is
how much information does one such orbit
\mathbb{A}
carry about
f
?

The answer is trivial:
Since
f
is an analytic germ at
0
, whose values on
\mathbb{A}
are determined by the relation
f(x_{n})=x_{n+1}
,
and
\mathbb{A}
accumulates at
0
, this means that
\mathbb{A}
determines
f
as a germ
.

Instead,
our question is whether one can somehow “read” the analytic invariants of
f
(i.e. the analytic conjugacy class of
f
)
“directly” from the discrete set
\mathbb{A}
?

That is, knowing a set
\mathbb{A}
and knowing that it is an orbit of some (unknown) analytic diffeomorphism, can one determine the analytic invariants of this diffeomorphism.

The orbit
\mathbb{A}=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
can be considered either as a subset of some neighborhood of the origin in
{\mathbb{C}}
or as a sequence of distinct points.
We shall alternate freely between the two points of view.

In order to read analytic properties of parabolic diffeomorphisms from its orbits, we introduce two theta functions: a
dynamic theta function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}
and a
fractal theta function
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}
. We prove two main theorems: Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
showing the properties of the dynamic theta function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}
. In Proposition
4
, we show the relationship between the two theta functions, hence transforming Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
to Corollaries
A
and
B
giving the analogous results for the fractal theta function
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}
.

1.1.
Dynamic theta function of an orbit.

The
dynamic theta function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}
of an orbit
\mathbb{A}
is defined by

(1.5)

\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s):=\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{A}}\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,t(x_{i})},

where

(1.6)

t(x)=\psi_{\mathrm{mod}}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{ak}x^{-k}

is the Fatou coordinate of the model (
1.3
).
The function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}
is well-defined and analytic for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
, and we prove in Theorem
A
that it can be indefinitely analytically continued as a multivalued function. The function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}
for real orbits is also known as
geometric partition function
of the fractal string
\big\{t(x_{i})^{-1}\big\}_{i=1}^{\infty}

[
LF13
]
. The definition of
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
is also similar to that of a
spectral theta function
of Voros
[
Vrs87
,
Vrs93
]
, thus the name.

Denoting
\delta_{x_{i}}(x)
the Dirac distribution supported at
x_{i}
, let

\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(x):=\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{A}}\delta_{x_{i}}(x).

Then
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
defined in (
1.5
) is a Laplace-type integral transform of
\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
with weight
t(x)
:

(1.7)

\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=\int_{\gamma}\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(x)\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,t(x)}\operatorname{d}\!x,

where
\gamma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is any curve through the points of
\mathbb{A}
in their order.

In particular, for an orbit
{\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{mod}}}
of the model
f_{\mathrm{mod}}
, one has

\Theta_{\mathbb{A}_{\mathrm{mod}}}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,(t(x_{0})+n)}=\frac{\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,t(x_{0})}}{1-\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s}},

which is a meromorphic function with simple poles at
2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The idea behind the function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
is to mimic the method of analysis of singularities of the Borel transform of a formal Fatou coordinate of
f
.
This was initiated in Écalles’ pioneering works on analytic classification of parabolic diffeomorphisms
[
Eca75
,
Eca74
]
, and was a starting point in his development of theory of resurgence
[
Eca81
]
.

The orbit
\mathbb{A}
is contained in some sector
W_{\mathbb{A}}
contained in one of the attractive petals of
D_{f}
(
1.2
).
By the Leau–Fatou theorem (Theorem
8
in §
3.1
), on
W_{\mathbb{A}}
there exists a sectorial Fatou coordinate
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
,
i.e. mapping verifying
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}\circ f=\psi_{\mathbb{A}}+1
. It is uniquely
determined by the initial condition
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x_{0})=0.

Then
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x_{m})=m
for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We denote
\hat{\psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
the formal Fatou coordinate to which
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}
is asymptotic on
W_{\mathbb{A}}
.

The first of our principal results is the following:

Theorem A
.

The
dynamic theta function

\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
extends analytically to the universal cover of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus 2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu{\mathbb{Z}}
and has at most exponential growth at infinity along any non-vertical direction.

The unique sectorial Fatou coordinate
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
on the petal containing the orbit
\mathbb{A}
such that
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x_{0})=0

can be obtained from
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
through the integral transform

(1.8)

\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu}\int_{\operatorname{Circ}({\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0})}\tfrac{\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)}{s}\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{s\,t(x)}\operatorname{d}\!s=:\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\big\{\tfrac{\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\,s}\big\}(x),

where
\operatorname{Circ}({\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0})
is the Hankel contour encircling the ray
{\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0}
(Figure
1
).

0
2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu
-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu
{}_{\operatorname{Circ}({\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0})}

.

.

The formula (
1.8
) means that one can recover the sectorial Fatou coordinate
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
, and thus the germ

f(x)=\psi_{\mathbb{A}}^{\circ(-1)}\circ(\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)+1),

from the knowledge of a single orbit
\mathbb{A}
.
One can summarize the process of reconstruction of
f
from an orbit
\mathbb{A}
by the following diagram:

\mathbb{A}\mapsto\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}\xmapsto{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\{\frac{\cdot}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\,s}\}}\psi_{\mathbb{A}}\mapsto f.

One can interpret (
1.8
) as a discrete version of the usual Borel summation process.
Let
\hat{\psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
be the formal Fatou coordinate for
f
, to which is
\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)
asymptotic. Let
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
be its
formal Borel transform defined term-wise,

\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu}\int_{t(x)\in t(x_{0})+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}\hat{\psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(x)\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,t(x)}\operatorname{d}\!t(x)=:\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}\{\hat{\psi}_{\mathbb{A}}\}(s).

Then, by the Borel-Laplace summation method,

(1.9)

\psi_{\mathbb{A}}(x)=\int_{\operatorname{Circ}({\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0})}\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{s\,t(x)}\operatorname{d}\!s=:\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}\{\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{A}}\}(x)

is a sectorial Fatou coordinate for
f
on some sector
W_{\mathbb{A}}
containing the orbit
\mathbb{A}
.
The identity between the integrals (
1.8
) and (
1.9
) is due to the difference
\frac{\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\,s}-\tilde{\Psi}(s)
being analytic on a neighborhood of the ray
{\mathbb{R}}_{\leq 0}
.
More details about the relation between the function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
and the Borel transform of the formal Fatou coordinate in the Borel plane are given in Proposition
13
.

Theorem B
.

The Birkhoff–Écalle–Voronin invariants of the germ
f

can be read from the leading term coefficients at the singularities of the analytic continuation of
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
.

Proof.

The analytic class of
f
, given by so called Birkhoff–Écalle–Voronin invariants (see §
3.1
),
can be read from the function
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
in terms of the leading term coefficients at some of its singularities (see §
3.3
). The same then holds for
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
, by formula (
4.5
) in Proposition
13
.
∎

1.2.
Fractal analysis: fractal theta function of an orbit.

Another way to analyze the orbit
\mathbb{A}
is by investigating its fractal properties.
In this subsection, we introduce a function
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}
,
which we call
fractal theta function
, as an integral transform of the
counting function
n_{\mathbb{A}}
of the orbit
, which encodes fractal properties of
\mathbb{A}
.
This is similar to the fractal zeta function defined by Lapidus
[
LRZ17
,
LF00
]
and studied in
[
MRR21
]
, but with an integral kernel well adapted to reveal intrinsic properties of parabolic dynamics.
The fractal theta function
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}
is related to the dynamic theta function
\Theta_{\mathbb{A}}
of the orbit
\mathbb{A}
of the previous section.
As a consequence, it is possible to read the analytic invariants of a germ
f
from fractal properties of its orbit.

In this section, let us assume that the orbit
\mathbb{A}
(
1.4
) lies in the positive real axis:

\mathbb{A}=\{x_{0}>x_{1}>x_{2}>\ldots\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}.

This means that

f:({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},0)\to({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},0)
is real analytic with
a<0
.
For each
\epsilon>0
, let

\mathbb{A}_{\epsilon}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb{A})<\epsilon\}

be the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of the orbit
\mathbb{A}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
,
and denote by
\big|\mathbb{A}_{\epsilon}\big|
its Lebesque measure.
The
tube function

V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon):\ \epsilon\mapsto\big|\mathbb{A}_{\epsilon}\big|-2\epsilon=\big|\mathbb{A}_{\epsilon}\cap[0,x_{0}]\big|

encodes the fractal properties of the orbit
\mathbb{A}
.
In particular, if
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{M}\,\epsilon^{1-D}+o(\epsilon^{1-D})
, then
D
is the
Minkowski
(
box
\,)
dimension
of
\mathbb{A}
, and
\mathcal{M}
is the
Minkowski content
of
\mathbb{A}
.

The tube function
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
of forward orbits of parabolic analytic diffeomorphisms has been extensively studied by the authors in
[
MRR21
,
MRRZ12
,
Res13
,
Res14
]
.
In particular, it has been determined
[
EZZ07
,
MRR21
]
that

(1.10)

D=\tfrac{k}{k+1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\ \mathcal{M}=\tfrac{k+1}{k}\big(\!-\!\tfrac{2}{a}\big)^{\frac{1}{k+1}}.

Moreover, it has been shown in
[
Res14
]
that an asymptotic expansion of
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
as a power-log series exists up to the order
\epsilon^{1+\frac{k}{k+1}}
, and that this finite jet determines the formal class of
f
.
Beyond this order some oscillatory terms appear, and a full asymptotic expansion containing these oscillatory terms has been given in
[
MRR21
]
.

Instead of looking at the tube function
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
, it is more convenient to look at the closely related
counting function

n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon):=\max\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}:\ |x_{n-1}-x_{n}|\geq 2\epsilon\},

counting the maximal number of iterates
n>0
such that the
\epsilon
-neighborhoods of the points
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
are disjoint.
For each
\epsilon>0
, the orbit
\mathbb{A}
is divided into two parts:
the
tail

T_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)-1}\}
, whose
\epsilon
-neighborhood consist of
n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
disjoint intervals of length
2\epsilon
, and
the
nucleus

N_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=\{x_{j}:j\geq n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)\}
, whose
\epsilon
-neighborhood spans the interval
]-\epsilon,x_{n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)}+\epsilon\,[
.
Hence the tube function is expressed in terms of the counting function as

(1.11)

V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=2\epsilon\,n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)+x_{n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)}.

Denoting

(1.12)

\epsilon_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{n-1}-x_{n})=\tfrac{1}{2}g(x_{n-1}),\hskip 20.00003ptg(x)=x-f(x),

we have:

n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=\#\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}:\ \epsilon_{n}\geq\epsilon\}=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{]0,\epsilon_{n}]}(\epsilon),

where
\mathbbm{1}_{]0,\epsilon_{n}]}(\epsilon)
is the characteristic function of the interval
]0,\epsilon_{n}]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
. The tube function
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
is an integral of
2n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
:

(1.13)

V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=2\int_{0}^{\epsilon}n_{\mathbb{A}}(\xi)\operatorname{d}\!\xi,\hskip 20.00003pt\epsilon\geq 0.

Proof.

By (
1.11
), the function
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
is continuous, piece-wise linear, with the derivative
\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon}V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
equal to
2n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)=2n
on each interval
\epsilon\in\ ]\epsilon_{n+1},\epsilon_{n}[
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
, and
V_{\mathbb{A}}(0)=0
. ∎

We define the
fractal theta function

\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
by

(1.14)

\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\tau(\epsilon_{n})},\hskip 20.00003pt\operatorname{Re}(s)>0,

where

(1.15)

\tau(\epsilon)=\tfrac{1}{2k}\big(\!-\!\tfrac{2}{a}\big)^{\frac{1}{k+1}}\epsilon^{-\frac{k}{k+1}}

is the leading asymptotic term of
n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
(cf. (
1.10
) and (
1.13
)).
Equivalently, it is equal to the following integral transform of the counting function

\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=s\int_{\tau(\epsilon)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\!\!\!\!n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,\tau(\epsilon)}\operatorname{d}\!\tau(\epsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}s\int_{\tau(\epsilon)\in\,[\tau(\epsilon_{n}),+\infty[}\!\!\!\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,\tau(\epsilon)}\operatorname{d}\!\tau(\epsilon).

Using Proposition
1
and integration by parts, one can also rewrite
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)
in terms of the tube function
V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\epsilon_{1}}\tfrac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon}V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)\,\tfrac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon}\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,\tau(\epsilon)}\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\epsilon_{1}}V_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)\,\tfrac{\operatorname{d}\!^{2}}{\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon^{2}}\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,\tau(\epsilon)}\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon\,+\,\tfrac{sx_{0}}{2}\,\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,\tau(\epsilon_{1})}{\tau}^{\prime}(\epsilon_{1}).

Another way to rewrite it is in terms of the distance function,
which maps each of the intervals
[x_{j},\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2}]
and
[\frac{x_{j}+x_{j-1}}{2},x_{j-1}]
bijectively to

[0,\epsilon_{j}]
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}
,

(1.16)

\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=-\tfrac{s}{2}\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-s\,\tau(\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb{A}))}{\tau}^{\prime}(\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb{A}))\operatorname{d}\!x.

Remark 2
.

One can define the function
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}
in a similar way also for hyperbolic germs
f(x)=\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-\alpha}x+\mathrm{h.o.t.}(x)
,
\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)>0
, with model

f_{\mathrm{mod}}(x)=\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{-\alpha}x=\exp(-\alpha x\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x\vphantom{\hat{X}}}),\hskip 20.00003pt\psi_{\mathrm{mod}}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\log x.

Then for
\,\tau(\epsilon)=-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\log\epsilon\,
one has
\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\epsilon_{n}^{\frac{s}{\alpha}}=\zeta_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{A}}}(\tfrac{s}{\alpha})
,
where the function

\zeta_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{A}}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\epsilon_{n}^{s}=s\!\int_{\epsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\!\!\!\!\!n_{\mathbb{A}}(\epsilon)\,\epsilon^{s-1}\!\operatorname{d}\!\epsilon=\tfrac{s}{2}\!\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\!\!\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathbb{A})^{s-1}\!\operatorname{d}\!x,\hskip 20.00003pt\operatorname{Re}(s)>0,

is known as the
geometric zeta function of the fractal string

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{A}}=\{\epsilon_{n}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}\}
.

Geometric zeta functions of fractal strings have been the object of great interest in fractal analysis, see e.g.
[
LRZ17
,
LF00
,
LF13
]
and can be considered as a special case of fractal theta functions
with the choice
\tau(\epsilon)=-\log\epsilon
.
Their application to orbits of parabolic diffeomorphisms has been extensively studied in
[

]
where the formal class of the diffeomorphism was then successfully read from the geometric zeta function.
However, in order to read its analytic class, in this paper, we have to adjust the kernel
\tau
as in (

).

Remark 3
.

The formula (

)

regardless of the curve

.

The fractal theta function is in fact the dynamic theta function of a conjugated object:

Proposition 4

(Relation between the dynamic and the fractal theta function)
.

Let
\phi(x)=x+\mathrm{h.o.t.}(x)
be the analytic germ such that
\phi(x)^{k+1}=\frac{f(x)-x}{a}
,
and let

\tilde{\mathbb{A}}=\{\tilde{x}_{n}=\phi(x_{n}),\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}=\phi(\mathbb{A})

be the image of the orbit
\mathbb{A}=\{x_{n},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
by
\phi
. Then
\tilde{\mathbb{A}}
is an orbit of the analytically conjugated diffeomorphism

\tilde{f}=\phi\circ f\circ\phi^{\circ(-1)},

and

\tilde{\Theta}_{\mathbb{A}}(s)=\Theta_{\tilde{\mathbb{A}}}(s),

where
\Theta_{\tilde{\mathbb{A}}}(s)
is the dynamic theta function (
1.5
) of the orbit
\tilde{\mathbb{A}}
.

Proof.

By comparing (
1.15
) and (
1.6
), and by definition of
\phi
, we have

2.
Hyperfunctions, Laplace transforms and Borel summability

The theory of hyperfunctions in dimension one by Köthe
[
Kot52
]
and Sato
[
Sat58
,
Sat59
]
, and its further development by Komatsu
[
Kom88
]

provides a good framework for treatment of the distributions that arise as Laplace transforms of analytic functions, and for Écalle’s theory of resurgence.
In particular, it allows, to avoid dealing with Dirac distributions through the use of residue theorem.
Let us here recall some basics and fix the notation.

Notation 5
.

For
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
we denote
c{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}}=c+\epsilon
(resp.
c{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{-}$}}=c-\epsilon
) with an arbitrarily small (infinitesimal)
\epsilon>0
.

2.1.
Hyperfunctions.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
be a locally closed subset of a line
\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
in the complex plane. Namely, for us
\Omega
will be one of the following: the whole line
\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
, a half-line
\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, or a single point
\{\omega\}
.
We denote

•

\mathcal{O}(\Omega)=\varinjlim_{U\supset\Omega\ \text{open}}\mathcal{O}(U)
the ring of functions analytic on neighborhoods of
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
,

•

\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Omega,\Omega)=\varinjlim_{U\supset\Omega\ \text{open}}\mathcal{O}(U\setminus\Omega)
the ring of functions
analytic on deleted neighborhoods of
\Omega
,

•

\mathcal{B}(\Omega)=\mathcal{O}({\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Omega,\Omega)\big/\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
the ring of
hyperfunctions with support on
\Omega
, consisting
of equivalence classes of functions
[\tilde{H}]_{\Omega}=\tilde{H}+\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
.

For each hyperfunction
[\tilde{H}]_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
we define its
boundary value

\operatorname{BV}[\tilde{H}(s)]_{\Omega}
,
s\in\Omega
, as the difference
“
\tilde{H}(s-\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}(0{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}}))-\tilde{H}(s+\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}(0{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}}))
” understood in a “distributional sense” as a shorthand for:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\!\!G(s)\operatorname{BV}[\tilde{H}(s)]_{\Omega}\!\operatorname{d}\!s

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\!\!G(s)\tilde{H}(s\!-\!\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}(0{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}}))\operatorname{d}\!s-\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!G(s)\tilde{H}(s\!+\!\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}(0{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}}))\operatorname{d}\!s

\displaystyle=\int_{\operatorname{Circ}(\Omega)}\!\!\!\!G(s)\tilde{H}(s)\operatorname{d}\!s,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for any }\ G\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega),

where
\operatorname{Circ}(\Omega)
is a contour encircling
\Omega
in the positive direction. Namely, depending on the set
\Omega
:

•

\operatorname{Circ}(\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}})=\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}(-\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu(0{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}})+{\mathbb{R}})\cup\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}(\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu(0{\scalebox{0.7}{$\boldsymbol{+}$}})-{\mathbb{R}})
is the difference between a pair of oriented parallel lines, below minus above of
\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}
,

•

\operatorname{Circ}(\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0})
is the Hankel contour around the ray
\mkern 0.75mu\mathrm{e}\mkern 1.19998mu^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 0.75mu\alpha}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
,

•

\operatorname{Circ}(\{\omega\})
is a small circle around the point
\omega
.

Thus we distinguish between an analytic function
\tilde{H}(s)
on a complement of
\Omega
, the associated hyperfunction
[\tilde{H}(s)]_{\Omega}
, and the distribution
\operatorname{BV}[\tilde{H}(s)]_{\Omega}
on
\Omega
defined by
[\tilde{H}(s)]_{\Omega}
.

Examples.

(1)

The

(2)

The

(4)

3.1.
Formal and sectorial Fatou coordinates.

Let
f
be a germ (
1.1
) and
f_{\mathrm{mod}}=\exp(\xi_{\mathrm{mod}})
be its model (
1.6
).
The germ
f
, being tangent to the identity, possesses a unique formal infinitesimal generator: a formal vector field
\hat{\xi}(x)=\big(ax^{k+1}+\mathrm{h.o.t.}(x)\big)\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x\vphantom{\hat{X}}}
, such that the Taylor series
\hat{f}(x)
for
f(x)
is equal to the formal time-
1

\hat{\xi}
:

\hat{f}(x)=\exp(\hat{\xi})(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\tfrac{1}{n!}\hat{\xi}^{n}.x\,,

(here
\hat{\xi}.\hat{h}
denotes the formal Lie derivative associated to
\hat{\xi}
applied on some formal power series
\hat{h}
).

The formal antiderivative
\hat{\psi}(x)=\int\hat{\xi}^{-1}
of the dual formal meromorphic 1-form
\hat{\xi}^{-1}

defines a
formal Fatou coordinate
for
f
of the form

(3.1)

\hat{\psi}(x)=r_{-k}x^{-k}+\ldots+r_{-1}x^{-1}+\rho\log x+C+\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}r_{j}x^{j},\hskip 20.00003ptC\in\mathbb{C},

where
\rho
is the
residual invariant
of
f
given by

(3.2)

\rho=\operatorname{res}_{x=0}\hat{\xi}^{-1}=\operatorname{res}_{x=0}\left(\tfrac{\operatorname{d}\!x}{f(x)-x}\right)+\tfrac{k+1}{2},

and the coefficients
r_{m}
are uniquely determined by
f
.
It satisfies the
Abel equation

\hat{\psi}\circ f=\hat{\psi}+1,

where
f
is identified with its Taylor series at
0
.

A
sectorial Fatou coordinate
for
f
is an analytic function
\psi_{W}
on some sector

that satisfies the
Abel equation

\psi_{W}\circ f=\psi_{W}+1
and
such that

is bounded on
W
(equivalently, one could ask
\psi_{W}:W\to\mathbb{C}
to be injective).

One can always prenormalize
f
through an analytic change of coordinate so that it takes the form
f(x)=x+ax^{k+1}+a^{2}(\tfrac{k+1}{2}-\rho)x^{2k+1}+\mathrm{h.o.t.}(x)
,
in which case the formal Fatou coordinate takes the form

(3.3)

\hat{\psi}(x)=t(x)-\tfrac{\rho}{k}\log t(x)+C^{\prime}+\hat{r}(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\ \hat{r}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}r_{n}x^{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\ C^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}.

Assuming
f
to be in a prenormalized form is not essential, but it is convenient since it simplifies the discussion.
The formal series
\hat{r}(x)
is in general divergent, but always Borel summable of order

.

Transition maps and moduli of analytic classification.

For each singular direction
