Multicategories from Symmetric Monoidal Categories
A. D. Elmendorf

Abstract
. This paper considers the possible underlying multicategories for a symmetric monoidal category, and shows that, up to canonical and coherent isomorphism, there really is only one. As a result, there is a well-defined forgetful functor from symmetric monoidal categories to multicategories, as long as all morphisms of symmetric monoidal categories are at least lax symmetric monoidal.



1.
Introduction

This paper aspires to give a thorough study of the underlying multicategories for a symmetric monoidal category. We begin by
giving an absurdly large collection of possible underlying multicategories, hopefully encompassing any conceivable candidate for
that title. We show that all such possibilities are canonically and coherently isomorphic, so up to coherent
isomorphism, there really is only “one” underlying multicategory for a symmetric monoidal category. In particular, any choices
of underlying multicategory give a functor from symmetric monoidal categories to multicategories, presuming a reasonable choice
of morphisms between symmetric monoidal categories. We show that a map of underlying multicategories is the same thing
as a lax symmetric monoidal functor between symmetric monoidal categories, so the morphisms of symmetric monoidal categories
need to be at least lax symmetric monoidal.

The underlying multicategory functor has a “weak” left adjoint, whose construction is the same as the actual left adjoint to the
underlying multicategory functor from permutative categories and strict maps, as constructed in
[
3
]
. However, the “counit”
of the adjunction is only natural with respect to strict maps, which is far too restrictive in general. The adjunction triangles do
however commute, so we do get an adjunction comonad on symmetric monoidal categories, which provides us with a strictification
construction different from that given in
[
4
]
.

We begin by describing the enormous array of possible underlying multicategories for a symmetric monoidal category, and this
relies on the categorical operad
Y
described in
[
1
]

whose algebras are precisely symmetric monoidal categories.
The objects of the component category
Y(n)
can be thought of
as a complete parenthesization of a list of length at least
n
, together with a way of permuting and inserting
n
variables into
slots in the list. The rest of the slots are to be filled with identity elements.
There is a unique morphism from any object of
Y(n)
to any other, so all diagrams in
Y(n)
commute.
Then we will describe an underlying multicategory
for a symmetric monoidal category
\mathcal{C}
for each sequence of functions

\kappa_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}^{n}\to{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}Y(n)

for
n\geq 0
, with no further structure.
We will write this underlying multicategory as
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
.
The idea is that for each
n
-tuple
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of objects of
\mathcal{C}
, the image
\kappa_{n}\mathbf{x}

tells us how to combine the objects into a single object using the symmetric monoidal structure of
\mathcal{C}
. Formally, we obtain
an object
\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x})
for each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
; we will abbreviate this, and write

\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x})=\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}.

For notation, we will always write a morphism set in a multicategory with a semicolon separating the source list
from the target object.
Then the formal definition of the morphism sets in
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
is as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Given an
n
-tuple
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of objects of
\mathcal{C}
and a single object
y
of
\mathcal{C}
, we define

U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x};y)=\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x},y).

Our first major theorem is then

Theorem 1.2
.

The morphism sets
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x};y)
form a multicategory, with objects the objects of
\mathcal{C}
.

The structure is given in Section
2
; the verification that the properties of a multicategory are
satisfied is somewhat lengthy, and
deferred until Section
6
.

Our next major theorem shows that these underlying multicategories are all essentially the same.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\lambda_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}^{n}\to{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}Y(n)
be another sequence of functions defining another underlying
multicategory
U_{\lambda}\mathcal{C}
. Then there is a canonical isomorphism of multicategories

U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}\to U_{\lambda}\mathcal{C}.

Further, given a third sequence
\rho_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}^{n}\to{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}Y(n)
, the diagram of canonical isomorphisms

\textstyle{U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{U_{\lambda}\mathcal{C}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{U_{\rho}\mathcal{C}}

commutes, so the isomorphisms are both canonical and coherent.

The proof is in Section
3
.

As a result of theorem
1.3
, any choice of underlying multicategories for any symmetric monoidal
category will give a functor from symmetric monoidal categories to multicategories, as long as the morphisms we choose
between symmetric monoidal categories give morphisms of underlying multicategories. This is resolved in the following
theorem:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
be symmetric monoidal categories, with arbitrary choices
U\mathcal{C}
and
U\mathcal{D}
of underlying multicategories.
Then a map of multicategories (a
multifunctor
)

U\mathcal{C}\to U\mathcal{D}

determines and is determined by a lax symmetric monoidal functor
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
.

The proof is in Section
4
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Any category of symmetric monoidal categories, all of whose morphism functors are at least lax symmetric monoidal, supports
an underlying multicategory functor from symmetric monoidal categories to multicategories.

Our last major theorem gives this underlying multicategory functor a weak left adjoint, where “weak” means that the counit map
is only lax natural.

Theorem 1.6
.

The forgetful functor from symmetric monoidal categories to multicategories has a weak left adjoint,
as long as we include strong
symmetric monoidal functors as maps of symmetric monoidal categories. The adjunction is weak in the sense that
the counit is only natural up to a natural transformation.

The construction is exactly the same as the left adjoint constructed in
[
3
]
, Theorem 4.2, in the context of permutative
categories and strict maps.
The proof is in Section
5
.
Since the unit of the weak adjunction is actually natural, we do get a comonad, although only a weak monad. We also
observe the following in Section
5
:

Theorem 1.7
.

The weak left adjoint from Theorem
1.6
converts multicategories and multifunctors into permutative categories
and strict maps, so the comonad of the weak adjunction converts symmetric monoidal categories and lax symmetric monoidal functors
into permutative categories and strict maps. The (weak) counit of the adjunction is a homotopy equivalence of categories.

This is not quite the same as the strictification construction due originally to Isbell
[
4
]
, and made explicit by May in

[
5
]
. We discuss the comparison and differences in Section
5
.

2.
Underlying Multicategories: Structure

In this section we give the structure of
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
as a multicategory, following the definition
of a multicategory
given in
[
2
]
, Section 2. The
objects of
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
are just the objects of
\mathcal{C}
, and we have already defined the morphism sets, namely, given
a list
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of objects of
\mathcal{C}
and a target object
y
, we define

U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x};y)=\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x},y)=\mathcal{C}(\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}),y).

We must specify identity elements in
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(a;a)=\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}(a),a)
for each object
a
of
\mathcal{C}
. Now
\overline{\kappa}(a)=\kappa_{1}(a)(a)
, and
\kappa_{1}(a)
is an object of
Y(1)
, which also has the canonical identity object
1\in Y(1)
. We therefore have
a unique isomorphism
\omega(a):\kappa_{1}(a)\to 1
in
Y(1)
, and applying this to
a
itself, we get a canonical morphism in
\mathcal{C}
:

\omega(a):\overline{\kappa}(a)=\kappa_{1}(a)(a)\to 1\cdot a=a,

which we take as our identity element in
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(a;a)=\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}(a),a)
.

We must give a right action of
\Sigma_{n}
on the morphism sets with source
n
-tuples of objects. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
,
let’s write

\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}=(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})

in accordance with the standard left action of
\Sigma_{n}
.
Then we need to define a map

\sigma^{*}:U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x};y)\to U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x};y)

which we will verify does give a right action in Section
6
. We define
\sigma^{*}
by appealing to the definition
of an operad action to see that

\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}=\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x})=(\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\sigma)(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}).

Since
\kappa_{n}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x})
and
\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\sigma
are both objects of
Y(n)
there is a unique isomorphism

\theta(\mathbf{x},\sigma):\kappa_{n}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x})\to\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\sigma.

As a result, we have a canonical map, which is an isomorphism,

\textstyle{\overline{\kappa}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x})=\kappa_{n}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x})(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\theta(\mathbf{x},\sigma)}
\textstyle{\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\sigma\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}=\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x})=\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}}

which induces our desired map

\sigma^{*}:U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x};y)=\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x},y)\to\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}),y)=U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x};y).

Explicitly, we can say
\sigma^{*}=\mathcal{C}(\theta(\mathbf{x},\sigma),1)
.

Finally, we must specify a multiproduct that gives the composition in
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
, and in order to do so efficiently, we need
some notation. Suppose we are given a final target object
z
, a tuple
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
that we will map to
z
, and for each
s
with
1\leq s\leq n
,
a tuple
\mathbf{x}_{s}=(x_{s1},\dots,x_{sj_{s}})
that we will map to
y_{s}
. Let
j=j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}
, and write
\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s}
for
the
j
-tuple that is
the concatenation
of all the
\mathbf{x}_{s}
’s. Then we must specify a composition multiproduct

\Gamma:U_{\kappa}(\mathbf{y};z)\times\prod_{s=1}^{n}U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x}_{s};y_{s})\to U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s};z).

But using the definition of
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
, this means giving a map

\Gamma:\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{y},z)\times\prod_{s=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s},y_{s})\to\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s}),z).

Now we need just a bit more notation.
First,
given a list of items such as
\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{n}
, we write the
entire list as
\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle
, with the subscript presumed to run over appropriate limits. Next,
since we are using
\Gamma
for our composition in
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
, we will use
\gamma
for the
operad operation in
Y
to avoid confusion. (We used
\Gamma
in
[
1
]
for the operation in
Y
.)

We can now rewrite

\prod_{s=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s},y_{s})=\mathcal{C}^{n}(\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle,\mathbf{y}),

and noting that since
\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{y})
is an object of
Y(n)
, it induces a functor
\mathcal{C}^{n}\to\mathcal{C}
.
Further, both
\kappa_{j}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s})
and
\gamma(\kappa_{n}\mathbf{y};\langle\kappa_{j_{s}}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle)
are objects
of
Y(j)
, and so there is a unique isomorphism

\phi(\mathbf{y},\langle\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle):\kappa_{j}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s})\to\gamma(\kappa_{n}\mathbf{y};\langle\kappa_{j_{s}}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle),

which we can apply to the object
\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s}
to obtain an isomorphism which we abusively denote
with the same notation:

\phi(\mathbf{y},\langle\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle):\overline{\kappa}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s})\to\gamma(\kappa_{n}\mathbf{y};\langle\kappa_{j_{s}}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle)(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s})=\kappa_{n}\mathbf{y}\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle,

and consequently an induced map

\mathcal{C}(\phi(\mathbf{y},\langle\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle),1):\mathcal{C}(\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{y})\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle,z)\to\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s}),z).

We now define our composition multiproduct
in
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
as the following composite:

\textstyle{\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{y},z)\times\prod_{s=1}^{n}\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s},y_{s})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{=}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{y},z)\times\mathcal{C}^{n}(\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle,\mathbf{y})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{1\times\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{y})}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{y},z)\times\mathcal{C}(\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{y})\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle,\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{y})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\circ}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}(\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{y})\langle\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle,z),\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{C}(\phi(\mathbf{y},\langle\mathbf{x}_{s}\rangle),1)}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}(\odot_{s}\mathbf{x}_{s}),z).}

This completes the specification of the structure of
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
as a multicategory.
The verification that this structure satisfies the properties of a multicategory is deferred to Section
6
.

3.
Isomorphisms of Underlying Multicategories

In this section we
prove Theorem
1.3
, which
shows that all the multicategories
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
for all possible values of
\kappa

are canonically and coherently isomorphic. This means that we can reasonably speak of “the” underlying
multicategory for a symmetric monoidal category, since any one is unique up to a unique canonical
isomorphism.

We suppose given two arbitrary sequences

\kappa_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}^{n}\to{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}Y(n)\text{ and }\lambda_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}\to{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}Y(n),

which determine underlying multicategories
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}
and
U_{\lambda}\mathcal{C}
.
The proof of the theorem consists of the definition of the canonical isomorphism
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}\to U_{\lambda}\mathcal{C}
,
the verification that the coherence diagram commutes, and finally
the verification that our map is an isomorphism of multicategories.
We begin with the definition of the canonical isomorphism.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\kappa_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}^{n}\to Y(n)
and
\lambda_{n}:{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}^{n}\to Y(n)
be any two sequences of functions. Then
given an object
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
, we have the objects
\kappa_{n}\mathbf{x}
and
\lambda_{n}\mathbf{x}
of
Y(n)
, and
we define

\alpha(\mathbf{x}):\lambda_{n}\mathbf{x}\to\kappa_{n}\mathbf{x}

to be the unique isomorphism in
Y(n)
with the given source and target.

To define an isomorphism of multicategories
U_{\kappa}\mathcal{C}\to U_{\lambda}\mathcal{C}
, we must give maps on objects
and on sets of morphisms. On objects, we just use
\mathrm{id}_{{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}}
, since both underlying multicategories
have
{\mathop{\textnormal{Ob}}}\mathcal{C}
as their objects. On morphisms, for each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
, we have the induced map

\textstyle{\overline{\lambda}\mathbf{x}=\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha(\mathbf{x})}
\textstyle{\kappa_{n}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x})=\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x},}

which in turn induces our desired map

\textstyle{U_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x};y)=\mathcal{C}(\overline{\kappa}\mathbf{x},y)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{C}(\alpha(\mathbf{x}),1)}
\textstyle{\mathcal{C}(\overline{\lambda}\mathbf{x},y)=U_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};y).}

It follows immediately from the definition that these maps are coherent, in the sense given in the theorem and
in greater detail in the following corollary.

Let

4.

We have now shown that all of the vast collection of possible underlying multicategories

and coherently isomorphic, so form the target of an underlying multicategory functor that is unique up to unique isomorphism. This still leaves open

is answered by Theorem

Before starting the proof of the theorem, it will be convenient to make some assumptions
about the sequences defining our underlying multicategories.
This is justified by the fact that all the underlying multicategories we have defined are canonically and coherently isomorphic, so
we can choose any one of them without loss of generality.

5.
The Weak Left Adjoint

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.6

As a result of Theorem

with source symmetric monoidal categories and lax monoidal functors, and target multicategories and multifunctors.
It is natural to ask for a left adjoint to this construction, and we almost have one: there is a functor

Construction 5.1
