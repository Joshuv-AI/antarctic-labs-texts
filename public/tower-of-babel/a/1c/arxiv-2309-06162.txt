Hamiltonian formulation of linear non-Hermitian systems
Qi Zhang

Abstract
For a linear non-Hermitian system, I demonstrate that a Hamiltonian can be constructed such that the non-Hermitian equations can be expressed exactly in the form of Hamilton’s canonical equations. This is first shown for discrete systems and then extended to continuous systems. With this Hamiltonian formulation, I am able to identify a conserved charge by applying Noether’s theorem and recognize adiabatic invariants. When applied to Hermitian systems, all the results reduce to the familiar ones associated with the Schrödinger equation.



keywords
Linear non-Hermitian system, Hamilton’s canonical equation, Adiabatic invariant, Conserved charge

2
Discrete linear non-Hermitian systems

2.1
Key aspects of the dynamics

Conventional quantum systems can be either discrete, such as spins, or continuous, such as electrons in atoms.
For clarity, we first consider a discrete linear non-Hermitian system, whose dynamical equation takes the form

\text{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle=h|\psi\rangle\,.

(2)

where, to distinguish from the Hermitian case,
h
is denoted as an
n\times n
non-Hermitian matrix that is diagonalizable and

|\psi\rangle=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},...,\psi_{n})^{T}
is a vector in an
n
-dimensional linear space.
This serves as a discrete model before extending the formulation to continuous non-Hermitian systems.
In general,
the diagonalizable matrix
h
has two sets of
n
eigenvectors
|a_{j}\rangle
and
|b_{j}\rangle
with
j=1,\ldots n
satisfying
F3
,

h|a_{j}\rangle=E_{j}|a_{j}\rangle,\quad\langle b_{j}|h=\langle b_{j}|E_{j}.

(3)

They are biorthonormal,

\langle b_{i}|a_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij},

(4)

and complete,

\sum_{j}|a_{j}\rangle\langle b_{j}|=1\,.

(5)

The eigenvalues
E_{j}
may be complex in general. The vectors
|a_{j}\rangle
and
|b_{j}\rangle
are referred to as the right and left eigenvectors of
h
, respectively. Since
h
is non-Hermitian, satisfying
h^{\dagger}\neq h
, the right and left eigenvectors are usually distinct, i.e.
|a_{j}\rangle\neq|b_{j}\rangle
.

This biorthogonal eigenbasis provides a framework to analyze the non-Hermitian dynamics. For example, consider a general state
|\psi(t)\rangle
satisfying dynamics (
2
), which can be expanded as (when
h
is diagonalizable such that Eq. (
5
) holds),

|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{j}c_{j}(t)|a_{j}\rangle,

(6)

where the coefficients

c_{j}(t)=c_{j}(0)e^{-\frac{\text{i}}{\hbar}E_{j}t}=\langle b_{j}|\psi(t)\rangle,

(7)

are given by the biorthonormal equation (
4
). Focusing on the subset of eigenstates with nonzero
c_{j}
, we can construct corresponding coefficients,

\bar{c}_{j}(t)c_{j}(t)=|C_{j}|^{2},

(8)

where the
|C_{j}|^{2}
are arbitrary constants. The variables
\bar{c}_{j}
apparently evolve as

\bar{c}_{j}(t)=\bar{c}_{j}(0)e^{\frac{\text{i}}{\hbar}E_{j}t},

(9)

This defines a conjugate left state

\langle\bar{\phi}(t)|=\sum_{j}\bar{c}_{j}(t)\langle b_{j}|,

(10)

which, by the eigenrelation in Eq. (
3
), satisfies the conjugate dynamics

-\text{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle\bar{\phi}|=\langle\bar{\phi}|h,

(11)

showing
|\bar{\phi}\rangle
evolves under the adjoint Hamiltonian
h^{\dagger}
.

The dynamics given by Eq. (
2
) and (
11
) lead directly to

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle\bar{\phi}|\psi\rangle=0

(12)

This shows that the overlap between the left and right states,
\langle\bar{\phi}|\psi\rangle
, is conserved over time. However, the norm of the right eigenstate
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle
is in general not a constant due to the non-Hermiticity of the Hamiltonian operator
h

Ryu
.

When the eigenvalues
E_{j}
of the Hamiltonian
h
are real, it is convenient to initialize the biorthogonal wavefunctions as

|\bar{c}_{j}(0)|=|c_{j}(0)|,

(13)

for which
|\bar{c}_{j}(t)|=|c_{j}(t)|=|C_{j}|
at any subsequent time t. In this case, the biorthogonal formulation retains the normalization condition at all times.
Additionally, when
h
becomes Hermitian, the left and right eigenvectors become identical, i.e.
|a_{j}\rangle=|b_{j}\rangle
. Consequently, the biorthogonal wavefunction
\langle\bar{\phi}|
reduces to the standard single wavefunction
\langle\psi|
, restoring the conventional form of the Schrödinger equation. Here, conservation of the overlap
\langle\bar{\phi}|\psi\rangle
corresponds to conservation of the total probability
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle
, as in standard quantum mechanics.
The biorthogonal framework therefore provides a natural generalization of the Hermitian theory that recovers the standard results when
h
is Hermitian.

2.2
The Hamiltonian formulation

The dynamics described by Eqs. (
2
) and (
11
) allow establishing a Hamiltonian formulation for non-Hermitian systems in a straightforward manner. In this formulation, the state should be represented by canonical variables. We can naturally take
q_{j}=i\hbar\psi_{j}
as the canonical variables.
Unlike the Hermitian case, since
h\neq h^{\dagger}
here, the pairs
(i\hbar\psi_{j},\psi_{j}^{*})
can no longer constitute canonical variables
Rego
;
Liu2
. This arises from the non-Hermiticity of
h
, which prevents
\psi_{j}
and
\psi_{j}^{*}
from satisfying the required Poisson bracket relations to be canonically conjugate. The non-Hermiticity induces additional constraints between the wavefunction and its complex conjugate, requiring a more careful construction of the phase space. Identifying an appropriate set of canonical variables is key to formulating a valid Hamiltonian framework for non-Hermitian systems.

Fortuitously, the right and left eigenstate dynamics in Eqs. (
2
) and (
11
) indicate that the components of the conjugate left state
\langle\bar{\phi}|=(\bar{\phi}_{1},\bar{\phi}_{2},\ldots,\bar{\phi}_{n})

constitute the canonical momenta conjugate to the canonical coordinates given by the right state
|\psi\rangle=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{n})^{T}
, with
q_{k}=\text{i}\hbar\psi_{k},p_{k}=\bar{\phi}_{k}
forming canonical variable pairs. The Hamilton’s equations then take the form

\frac{d(\text{i}\hbar\psi_{k})}{dt}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\bar{\phi}_{k}},\quad\frac{d(\bar{\phi}_{k})}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial(\text{i}\hbar\psi_{k})},

(14)

where the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
for these canonical equations is

\mathcal{H}(\bar{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\phi}_{n},\text{i}\hbar\psi_{1},\ldots,\text{i}\hbar\psi_{n})=\langle\bar{\phi}|h|\psi\rangle.

(15)

Thus, the phase space dimension is
2n
, consistent with the canonical dynamics. The biorthogonal framework thereby allows identifying a proper phase space description despite the non-Hermiticity of
h
. When
h
becomes Hermitian,
|\bar{\phi}\rangle
reduces to
|\psi\rangle
, restoring the canonical structure of the standard Schrödinger equation.

To clarify the canonical equations, we can make a time-independent canonical transformation

(\bar{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\phi}_{n},\text{i}\hbar\psi_{1},\ldots,\text{i}\hbar\psi_{n})\rightarrow(\bar{c}_{1},\ldots,\bar{c}_{n},\text{i}\hbar c_{1},\ldots,\text{i}\hbar c_{n}),

leaving the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
numerically unchanged. The canonical equations then become

\frac{d(\text{i}\hbar c_{j})}{dt}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\bar{c}_{j}},\quad\frac{d\bar{c}_{j}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial(\text{i}\hbar c_{j})},

(16)

with
\mathcal{H}
expressed as

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}\bar{c}_{j}c_{j}.

(17)

The transformed variables
c_{j}
and
\bar{c}_{j}
evolve as Eqs. (
7
) and (
9
).

The Lagrangian of the non-Hermitian dynamics can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}={\text{i}}\hbar\langle\bar{\phi}|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle-\langle\bar{\phi}|h|\psi\rangle,

(18)

where the Lagrangian equations

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\psi}_{j}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\psi_{j}}=0

(19)

are equivalent to the canonical equations in (
14
). This demonstrates the correspondence between the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations, with the conjugate left and right states providing the necessary structure to construct a valid Lagrangian despite
h
being non-Hermitian. The biorthonormal framework thereby allows deriving a self-consistent Lagrangian description in addition to the Hamiltonian dynamics.

2.3
An application: recognizing the adiabatic invariants

As an application, consider a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{R})=\langle\bar{\phi}|h(\mathbf{R})|\psi\rangle
that depends on slowly varying parameters
\mathbf{R}
. When the eigenenergies
E_{j}
are real, according to Eqs. (
6

8
) and (
10
), the variables
(\bar{c}_{j},c_{j})
and thus

undergo periodic oscillations. In this case, the
n

(20)

defined by the adiabatic theorem, are invariant. In the canonical representation of Eq. (
16
), this becomes

I_{j}=\frac{\text{i}\hbar}{2\pi}\oint\bar{c}_{j}d\hbar c_{j}=\hbar\bar{c}_{j}(0)c_{j}(0)=\hbar|C_{j}|^{2},

(21)

showing the occupation numbers remain constant when eigenenergies are real, consistent with previous results

. This demonstrates the non-Hermitian adiabatic theorem is identical to the conventional quantum one when energies are entirely real. Adiabatic invariance holds for non-Hermitian Hamiltonians just as in standard quantum mechanics.

3
Extension to continuous systems

Transitioning from a discrete to a continuous system is possible when the number of degrees of freedom becomes non-countably infinite. For clarity, we adopt the coordinate representation by denoting
|\mathbf{x}\rangle
as the coordinate eigenstate with eigenvalue
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.
\hat{x}|\mathbf{x}\rangle=\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}\rangle
where
\hat{x}
is the coordinate operator.
As in the discrete case, the eigenfunctions take the biorthonormal form of Eq. (
3
) but with the
j
th right and left eigenfunctions
a_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{x}|a_{j}\rangle
and
b_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{x}|b_{j}\rangle
now being continuous functions of
\mathbf{x}
. The biorthonormal and completeness conditions become

\int b_{j}(\mathbf{x})^{*}a_{k}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\delta_{jk},\quad\sum_{j}a_{j}(\mathbf{x})b_{j}(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})^{*}=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(27)

In terms of the wavefunction
\psi(\mathbf{x})=\langle\mathbf{x}|\psi\rangle
, the Schrödinger equation is

\text{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(\mathbf{x})=\hat{h}\psi(\mathbf{x}),

(28)

where
\hat{h}
is the Hamiltonian operator in coordinate representation, related to the matrix element
\langle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|h|\mathbf{x}\rangle=\hat{h}(\hat{x},-\text{i}\hbar\partial/\partial\mathbf{x})\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
.
The canonical variables follow identically to the discrete case: (i) expanding Š×(x) as

\psi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}c_{j}a_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

(29)

(ii) constructing the canonical conjugate field

\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\bar{\phi}|\mathbf{x}\rangle=\sum_{j}\bar{c}_{j}b_{j}^{*}(\mathbf{x}),

(30)

with

\bar{c}_{j}c_{j}=|C_{j}|^{2}.

(31)

As shown,
\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi(\mathbf{x})
satisfy the canonical equations

\frac{d(\text{i}\hbar\psi_{k})}{dt}=\frac{\partial\mathbb{H}}{\partial\bar{\phi}_{k}},\quad\frac{d(\bar{\phi}_{k})}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\mathbb{H}}{\partial(\text{i}\hbar\psi_{k})},

(32)

with Hamiltonian density

\mathbb{H}=\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\hat{h}\psi(\mathbf{x}).

(33)

The Lagrangian density is

\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{x})={\text{i}}\hbar\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\dot{\psi}(\mathbf{x})-\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\hat{h}\psi(\mathbf{x}).

(34)

The equations of motion satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equation for a continuous field,

\frac{\partial\mathbb{L}}{\partial\psi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbb{L}}{\partial(\nabla\psi)}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbb{L}}{\partial\dot{\psi}}\right)=0.

(35)

As an application, consider the linear non-Hermitian dynamics given by the 3D Schrödinger equation with a complex potential
V(\mathbf{x})

\text{i}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(\mathbf{x},t)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\psi(\mathbf{x},t)+V(\mathbf{x})\psi(\mathbf{x},t).

(36)

The Lagrangian density of this system is (dropping the edge term)

\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{x})={\text{i}}\hbar\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\dot{\psi}(\mathbf{x})-\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})V\psi(\mathbf{x})-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla\psi.

(37)

Consider an intrinsic symmetry of the non-Hermitian dynamics

\psi\rightarrow\psi^{\prime}=e^{\text{i}\alpha}\psi,\quad\bar{\phi}\rightarrow\bar{\phi}^{\prime}=e^{-\text{i}\alpha}\bar{\phi},

(38)

with
\alpha
a constant. This symmetry follows from the dynamics (
28
) and biorthogonal relations (
6
)-(
31
). For infinitesimal
\alpha

\psi\rightarrow\psi^{\prime}=\psi+\text{i}\alpha\psi,\quad\bar{\phi}\rightarrow\bar{\phi}^{\prime}=\bar{\phi}-\text{i}\alpha\bar{\phi}.

(39)

Applying Noether’s theorem for intrinsic symmetries
j^{\mu}=\frac{\partial\mathbb{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\psi)}\delta\psi
derives the conserved currents (up to an unimportant constant factor)

j^{0}=\bar{\phi}\psi,\quad j^{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\bar{\phi}\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\bar{\phi}).

(40)

Since
\psi
and
\bar{\phi}
are intimately related, the gradients
\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\bar{\phi}
must be treated equally as general velocities in deriving this.

Equation (
40
) gives,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\bar{\phi}\psi)+\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla\cdot(\bar{\phi}\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\bar{\phi})=0

(41)

This identifies a conserved charge

Q=\int\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\psi(\mathbf{x})d^{3}\mathbf{x}

(42)

When
V(\mathbf{x})
becomes real,

restores to the standard probability density of the Schrödinger wavefunction.

The conserved charge suggests that
\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\psi(\mathbf{x})
may represent the real probability amplitude of finding a non-Hermitian particle described by state

at position

. Thus, by employing the Lagrangian density (

), it should also be of considerable interest to derive the semiclassical trajectories of a wavepacket of a continuous-level non-Hermitian system, as has been done for traditional quantum systems

. This would provide insight into the motion and localization properties of non-Hermitian wavepackets, which could be a potentially fruitful direction for further research.

4
Summary

In summary, a Hamiltonian formulation has been established for linear non-Hermitian dynamics. As applications, adiabatic invariants and a conserved charge have been obtained. All of these results naturally reduce to familiar quantities in Hermitian quantum systems. This work advances the theoretical understanding of non-Hermitian systems and highlights promising directions for future research. In particular, quantization of non-Hermitian systems and deriving semiclassical trajectories of wavepackets remain areas of considerable interest. Generalization to nonlinear non-Hermitian systems is also of great interest. Overall, this Hamiltonian formulation provides a foundation to further develop analytical tools for linear non-Hermitian systems and potentially for nonlinear ones.
