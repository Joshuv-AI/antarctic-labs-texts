The fixed point iteration of positive concave mappings converges geometrically if a fixed point exists: implications to wireless systems
Tomasz Piotrowski, Renato L. G. Cavalcante

Abstract
We prove that the fixed point iteration of arbitrary positive concave mappings with nonempty fixed point set converges geometrically for any starting point. We also show that positivity is crucial for this result to hold, and the concept of (nonlinear) spectral radius of asymptotic mappings provides us with information about the convergence factor. As a practical implication of the results shown here, we rigorously explain why some power control and load estimation algorithms in wireless networks, which are particular instances of a fixed point iteration, have shown geometric convergence in simulations. These algorithms have been typically derived by considering fixed point iterations of the general class of standard interference mappings, so the possibility of sublinear convergence rate could not be ruled out in previous studies, except in special cases that are often more restrictive than those considered here.

More precisely, it provides an equivalent definition of a compact set in Thompson metric space, and note that this definition corresponds to the well known Heine-Borel theorem for finite-dimensional normed vector spaces.

A set

is compact in
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T})

is closed and bounded in
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T}).

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Let

(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T}).
In view of Remark

,

is closed and bounded in
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T}).

(\Leftarrow)
Let now

(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T}).
In view of Remark

is closed and bounded in any normed vector space
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|).
By the Heine-Borel Theorem,

is compact in

,

is compact in
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T}).

12
, Lemma 2.1.7]

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be an SI mapping and let
f_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu{\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})}}
be the restriction of
f
to
\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}).
Then
f_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu{\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})}}
is a para-contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric on
\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}).

III
Convergence of the fixed point iteration of continuous PC mappings

The main objective of this study is to prove geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration of an arbitrary continuous PC mapping with nonempty fixed point set, and we recall that the fixed point set of these mappings is a singleton as a consequence of Facts
2
and
3
. To establish geometric convergence, we start by proving that PC mappings
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
with
\mathrm{Fix}(f)\neq\emptyset
are local contractions w.r.t. Thompson’s metric
d_{T}
in (
8
) in a neighborhood
U
of the fixed point
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{Fix}(f)
. Therefore, since
(U,d_{T})
is a complete metric space, then standard arguments based on the Banach fixed point theorem guarantees geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration in
(U,d_{T})
for
x_{1}\in U
. These results are the main topic of Sect.
III-A
.

In engineering applications such as those in the wireless domain, we are typically not interested in geometric convergence of the fixed point algorithm in the metric space
(U,~d_{T})
for an unspecified neighborhood
U
of the fixed point, but in a normed vector space
(\mathbb{R}^{k},~\|\cdot\|)
(the choice of the norm is irrelevant because of the equivalence of norms in finite dimensional spaces). Unfortunately, the homeomorphism in (
9
) between
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),~d_{T})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{k},~\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
is nonlinear, so it may be unclear whether the results in Sect.
III-A
imply geometric convergence in a normed vector space. This problem is addressed in Sect.
III-B
, where we prove that geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration in
(U,~d_{T})
with a factor
c\in~[0,1)
implies geometric convergence in
(\mathbb{R}^{k},~\|\cdot\|)
with the same factor
c
for any starting point
x_{1}\in\real_{+}^{k}
.

Since the contraction factor
c
gives useful information about the convergence speed of the fixed point iteration [see (
2
)], in Sect.
III-C
we show that the concept of (nonlinear) spectral radius is useful not only for establishing the existence of a fixed point as shown in Fact
2
, but also for bounding
c
.

III-A

Local
c
-Lipschitz contractivity

Before delving into the convergence of the fixed point iteration of PC mappings, we first derive properties required for a mapping to be a contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric. We begin with the following lemma, which is an extension of
[
12
, Lemma 2.1.7]
to
c\in[0,1).

Lemma 1
.

Let
f\colon\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be monotonic. Then
f
is a
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric if and only if

\exists c\in[0,1)\quad\textrm{s.t.}\quad\forall x\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})\quad\forall\lambda>1\\
f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x).

(14)

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
x,y\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
, so that
d_{T}(x,y)=\ln\lambda
for some
\lambda\geq 1.
If
x=y
, then
d_{T}(x,y)=0
and
\lambda=1
, in which case
d_{T}(f(x),f(y))\leq c\,d_{T}(x,y)
for any
c\in[0,1).
Therefore, we assume below that
\lambda>1.

The case
\lambda>1
(
x\neq y
)
: From the definition of
d_{T}
, one has that
x\leq\lambda y
and
y\leq\lambda x.
From monotonicity of
f
we also obtain that
f(x)\leq f(\lambda y)
and
f(y)\leq f(\lambda x)
, and from (
14
) one has that
f(\lambda y)\leq\lambda^{c}f(y)
and
f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x)
for some
c\in[0,1).
As a result,

f(x)\leq f(\lambda y)\leq\lambda^{c}f(y)\textrm{ and }f(y)\leq f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x).

Thus,

\beta_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{\beta>0~|~f(x)\leq\beta f(y)\big\}\leq\lambda^{c}\textrm{ and }\\
\beta_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{\beta>0~|~f(y)\leq\beta f(x)\big\}\leq\lambda^{c},

and hence also
\max\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}\leq\lambda^{c}.
We now conclude that

d_{T}(f(x),f(y))=\ln(\max\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\})\leq\ln\lambda^{c}=\\
c\ln\lambda=c\,d_{T}(x,y).\

(\Rightarrow)
Conversely, assume that
f
is a
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric, and let
x\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
and
\lambda>1
be chosen arbitrarily. We note that
d_{T}(x,\lambda x)=\ln\lambda
, and thus

\displaystyle d_{T}(f(x),f(\lambda x))\leq c\,d_{T}(x,\lambda x)=c\ln\lambda=\ln\lambda^{c}.

Let

\beta_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{\beta>0~|~f(x)\leq\beta f(\lambda x)\big\}\textrm{ and }\\
\beta_{4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\inf\big\{\beta>0~|~f(\lambda x)\leq\beta f(x)\big\}.

From monotonicity of
f
we have that
f(x)\leq f(\lambda x)
because
\lambda>1
, thus
\beta_{3}\leq\beta_{4}.
Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{T}(f(x),f(\lambda x))=\ln\beta_{4}\leq\ln\lambda^{c}.

(15)

From the definition of
\beta_{4}
, we obtain that
f(\lambda x)\leq\beta_{4}f(x)
, and from (
15
) we obtain in particular that
\beta_{4}\leq\lambda^{c}
. As a result, we also have
\beta_{4}f(x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x)
, and the proof of the desired result
f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x)
is now complete.
\blacksquare

Remark 5
.

Mappings satisfying (
14
) are known as
c
-concave mappings in the mathematical literature, where they are considered in the context of partially ordered subsets (such as cones) of generic Banach spaces; see, for example,
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
.

Continuous PC mappings (and, in particular, positive affine mappings restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
) do not satisfy the properties in Lemma
1
in general, even if the mappings have a fixed point. Therefore, these mappings are not
global
contractions in the metric space
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),~d_{T})
, and thus Lemma
1
cannot be directly used with the Banach fixed point theorem to deduce geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration with PC mappings. Nevertheless, the following corollary shows that a weaker condition than (
14
) is sufficient for
f
to be a
local

c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric on a compact set
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
, and in Proposition
1
we show that this property is satisfied by PC mappings.

Corollary 3
.

Let
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a compact set containing a nonempty open set and let
d_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\max_{x,y\in U}d_{T}(x,y)=\ln\lambda_{0}
with
\lambda_{0}>1.
Let
f\colon\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be monotonic. Then
f
is a
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric on
U
if

\exists c\in[0,1)\quad\textrm{s.t.}\quad\forall x\in U\quad\forall\lambda\in(1,\lambda_{0}]\quad f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x).

(16)

Proof.

Let
x,y\in U.
Then,
d_{T}(x,y)=\ln\lambda
for some
1\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}.
The proof now follows from the
if
direction
(\Leftarrow)
of the proof of Lemma
1
, with
x,y\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
considered therein restricted to
x,y\in U
, and
\lambda\geq 1
considered therein restricted to
\lambda\in[1,\lambda_{0}].

\blacksquare

Thanks to Lemma
1
and Corollary
3
, we are able to prove the following proposition on local
c
-Lipschitz contractivity of continuous PC mappings.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a continuous and concave (w.r.t. to the cone order) mapping (i.e., a continuous PC mapping), and let
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a compact set containing a nonempty open set. Then
f
is a local
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric on
U.

Proof.

For convenience, we divide the proof into the following parts.

1) Global properties of
f
: From concavity of
f
, one has in particular that, for
t\in(0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
,

f(tx)=f(tx+(1-t)0)\geq tf(x)+(1-t)f(0).

Furthermore,

f(x)=f(t(x/t))\geq tf(x/t)+(1-t)f(0).

Let
\lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}t^{-1}>1.
Then, the above inequality can be rewritten as

f(x)\geq\lambda^{-1}f(\lambda x)+(1-\lambda^{-1})f(0),

thus

\lambda f(x)\geq f(\lambda x)+(\lambda-1)f(0),

and consequently

f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(0),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}.

(17)

2) Local properties of
f
on
U
: By our assumptions,
f
is continuous, and
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
is closed and bounded (see Corollary
2
), hence
f(U)
is also closed and bounded. In particular, there exists
\mu>0
such that
f(0)\geq\mu f(x)
for all
x\in U.
We note that we can always select
\mu\in(0,1)
because concavity and positivity of
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
implies its monotonicity on
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}

[
3
]
(see also Fact
3
). Noticing that
1-\lambda<0
, we obtain from (
17
) that

f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)\mu f(x),\quad x\in U.

Thus,

f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda\Bigg(1+\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda}\mu\Bigg)f(x)=\\
\lambda\Bigg(\frac{(1-\mu)\lambda+\mu}{\lambda}\Bigg)f(x)=\lambda\eta f(x),\quad x\in U,

(18)

where
\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\frac{(1-\mu)\lambda+\mu}{\lambda}>0
, because
\lambda>1
and
\mu\in(0,1).

3) Exponential form of
(\ref{c2})
: Using the
change of base
rule of logarithms, we note that
\log_{\lambda}(\lambda\eta)=\frac{\ln(\lambda\eta)}{\ln\lambda}
, and hence
\lambda\eta=\lambda^{\frac{\ln(\lambda\eta)}{\ln\lambda}}.
Thus, we obtain from (
18
) that

f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{\frac{\ln(\lambda\eta)}{\ln\lambda}}f(x)=\lambda^{c(\lambda)}f(x),\quad x\in U,

(19)

where

c(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\frac{\ln(\lambda\eta)}{\ln\lambda}=\frac{\ln((1-\mu)\lambda+\mu)}{\ln\lambda}

is a continuous function of
\lambda>1.

4) Monotonic increase of
c(\lambda)
: We show now that
c(\lambda)
is monotonically increasing for
\lambda>1.
Denote

s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}(1-\mu)\lambda+\mu,\quad\lambda>1,

and observe that
1<s<\lambda
for any
\mu\in(0,1).
Thus,

c(\lambda)=\frac{\ln s}{\ln\lambda}<1,\quad\lambda>1.

Moreover, we have

c^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\frac{(1-\mu)\ln\lambda}{s}-\frac{\ln s}{\lambda}}{(\ln\lambda)^{2}},\quad\lambda>1.

(20)

Define

c_{2}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\frac{(\ln s)^{\prime}}{(\ln\lambda)^{\prime}}=\frac{(1-\mu)\lambda}{s},\quad\lambda>1.

Then
c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)=\frac{(1-\mu)\mu}{s^{2}}>0
, hence
c_{2}(\lambda)
is increasing for
\lambda>1.

We note that both
\lambda\mapsto\ln s
and
\lambda\mapsto\ln\lambda
are continuous for
\lambda\geq 1
and differentiable for
\lambda>1
, with
1\mapsto 0
in both cases. Therefore, using Cauchy’s (extended) mean value theorem, we conclude that for any
\lambda_{1}>1
there exists
\lambda_{2}
such that
1<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{1}
with
c_{2}(\lambda_{2})=c(\lambda_{1}).
Thus, as
c_{2}(\lambda)
is increasing for
\lambda>1
, we obtain
c_{2}(\lambda_{1})>c(\lambda_{1})
for
\lambda_{1}>1.
Therefore, one has

c_{2}(\lambda)=\frac{(1-\mu)\lambda}{s}>\frac{\ln s}{\ln\lambda}=c(\lambda),\quad\lambda>1,

thus also
\frac{(1-\mu)\ln\lambda}{s}-\frac{\ln s}{\lambda}>0
for
\lambda>1.
In view of (
20
), we conclude therefore that
c(\lambda)
is increasing for
\lambda>1
and any
\mu\in(0,1).
We also note that

1-\mu<c(\lambda)<1,

as
\lim_{\lambda\to 1^{+}}c(\lambda)=1-\mu
and
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}c(\lambda)=1.

5) Explicit local contraction factor
: Let now
\lambda_{0}>1
satisfy
\max_{x,y\in U}d_{T}(x,y)=\ln\lambda_{0}
, and let

c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}c(\lambda_{0})<1.

Then, from (
19
) and the fact that
c(\lambda)\leq c
for all
1<\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}
, one has

f(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{c(\lambda)}f(x)\leq\lambda^{c}f(x),\quad x\in U,\ 1<\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}.

(21)

Therefore, we conclude that
f
satisfies inequality (
16
) in Corollary
3
, implying that
f
is a local
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric on
U.

\blacksquare

Interesting consequences of Proposition
1
are discussed below.

Remark 6
.

If
f
has a fixed point
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
, then
U
can be selected as a compact neighborhood of
x^{\star}.
In this case, Proposition
1
provides a local contraction factor for
f
on
U.
We also note that the convergence of a fixed point iteration of
f
on
U
is linear w.r.t. Thompson’s metric in this case.

Example 1
.

Let
k=1
and define
f_{1}(x)=(1/2)x+1/2
for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.
Let
U=[1/2,3/2].
Then,
(U,d_{T})
is a complete metric space with
f_{1}(U)\subseteq U
, and, in Proposition
1
, we may set
\mu=1/3
as
f_{1}(0)=1/2
and
f_{1}(3/2)=5/4.
Moreover, we have
\lambda_{0}=3
, thus
c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}c(\lambda_{0})\approx 0.771
, which is a local contraction factor of
f_{1}
on
U.
Thus, by the Banach fixed point theorem,
f_{1}
admits a unique fixed point over
U
, which is
x^{\star}=1
in this case.

Example 2
.

Let
k=1
and define
f_{2}(x)=x+1
for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.
As above, let
U=[1/2,3/2]
, so that
(U,d_{T})
is a complete metric space. Then, in Proposition
1
, we may choose
\mu=1/3
because
f_{2}(0)=1
and
f_{2}(3/2)=5/2
, and, as above, we have
\lambda_{0}=3
, thus
c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}c(\lambda_{0})\approx 0.771
, which is a local contraction factor of
f_{2}
on
U.
We note that we have the same values of
\mu,\lambda_{0}
, and
c
as in the preceding example. However, the Banach fixed point theorem cannot be applied in this case because
f_{2}(U)\subsetneq U.
Indeed, it is seen that
f_{2}
does not have a fixed point over
U.

III-B

Geometric convergence of fixed point iteration in normed vector spaces

Having established linear, and, hence, geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration with a continuous PC mapping in the metric space
(U,d_{T})
, where
U
is a neighborhood of the fixed point (which is assumed to exist), we now proceed to prove that we also have geometric convergence in any normed vector space
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|)
for any starting point in
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
, and the convergence factors in
(U,d_{T})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|)
can be selected to be the same. To this end, we need the next result.

Proposition 2
.

Let
f\colon\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric and let
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be the unique fixed point of
f.
Then, the fixed point iteration of
f
with
x_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
converges geometrically to
x^{\star}
with a factor
c\in[0,1)
w.r.t. any metric induced by a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
and let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence defined through (
1
). We first note
that, from Remark
4
,
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T}).
Thus, from Corollary
1
we conclude that the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also bounded in
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|)
for any norm
\|\cdot\|.
Let
b>0
be such that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\ \|x_{n}\|\leq b
for a given norm
\|\cdot\|.
We also have
\|x^{\star}\|\leq b
because
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
x^{\star}.
Now, let
\delta>0
be the normality constant of
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
for this norm (see Definition
8
in the Appendix). Then, using the property that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\ d_{T}(x_{n+1},x^{\star})\leq c^{n}\,d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})
(as
f
is a
c
-Lipschitz contraction w.r.t. Thompson’s metric) and Fact
5
in the Appendix
A
, we deduce

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq b(1+2\delta)(e^{d_{T}(x_{n+1},x^{\star})}-1)\\
\leq b(1+2\delta)(e^{c^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}-1)\\
=b(1+2\delta){(e^{c^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})})}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{e^{c^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}}\right).

(22)

Then, noticing that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
one has
c^{n}\in[0,1)
, we obtain
e^{c^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}\leq e^{d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}
, and, consequently,

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq s\left(1-\dfrac{1}{e^{c^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}}\right),

(23)

where
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}b(1+2\delta){e^{d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}}.
Using the well-known bound
1-1/v\leq\ln v
for
v>0
, we obtain

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq s\ln{e^{c^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})}}=\\
sc^{n}d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})=\gamma c^{n},

(24)

where
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}s\,d_{T}(x_{1},x^{\star})
, and the proof is complete. ∎

We now have all ingredients to establish geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration of PC mappings:

Proposition 3
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a continuous and concave (w.r.t. to the cone order) mapping (i.e., a continuous PC mapping) with a fixed point
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}).
Then, the fixed point iteration of
f
with
x_{1}\in\real_{+}^{k}
converges geometrically to
x^{\star}
with a factor
c\in[0,1)
w.r.t. any metric induced by a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Proof.

Without any loss of generality, we can assume that
x_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
because, if
x_{1}\in\real_{+}^{k}\backslash\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
, then
f(x_{1})\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
, so we can consider
f(x_{1})
as the starting point of the iterations. We divide the proof into two parts. We first prove geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration of
f
in a compact neighborhood
U
of
x^{\star}
, and then we extend this property to an arbitrary starting point
x_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}).

1) Geometric convergence on
U
: We first note that the fixed point
x^{\star}
of
f
satisfies
x^{\star}\geq f(x^{\star})
, thus
f
is feasible in the sense of
[
2
]
. Therefore, by Fact
6
in the Appendix
A
, the fixed point iteration of
f
with
x_{1}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
produces a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
x^{\star}
w.r.t. any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}.
Let
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a compact neighborhood of
x^{\star}.
Then, we can find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall n>n_{0}
one has
x_{n}\in U.
Thus, let
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset U
be a sequence defined as
y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}x_{n+n_{0}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}.
Let us also note that
f
is a local
c
-Lipschitz contraction on
U
w.r.t. Thompson’s metric in view of Proposition
1
. Moreover, using the isometry provided in (
9
) in Remark
2
, we conclude that
(U,d_{T})
is a complete metric space isometric to a complete metric subspace of
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})

[
35
]
. Therefore, as
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset U
is a fixed point iteration of
f
converging to
x^{\star}
in a complete metric space
(U,d_{T})
, and
f
is a local
c
-Lipschitz contraction on
U
w.r.t. Thompson’s metric, we conclude that
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is obviously bounded and that it converges linearly (and hence, also geometrically) to
x^{\star}
w.r.t. Thompson’s metric. Owing to this fact, we may reproduce now the proof of Proposition
2
, with the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
considered therein replaced with the sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset U
, and the complete metric space
(\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}),d_{T})
considered therein replaced with the complete metric space
(U,d_{T})
, to conclude that
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges geometrically to
x^{\star}
with a factor
c\in[0,1)
w.r.t. any metric induced by a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, with a constant
\gamma_{y}>0
determined through (
22
)-(
24
).

2) Geometric convergence on
\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
: If
n_{0}>1
, then we need to show that the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also has this property. To this end, let us define
\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\max_{n\in\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}}\|x_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|
, and assume that
c=0.
Then,
f
is constant on
U
, hence
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
one has
y_{n}=x_{n_{0}+n}=x^{\star}.
Thus, taking into consideration that for any
v\in(0,1)
, we obtain

\forall n\in\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq\eta=\Big(\frac{\eta}{v^{n}}\Big)v^{n}\leq\Big(\frac{\eta}{v^{n_{0}}}\Big)v^{n},

and we conclude that the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges geometrically to
x^{\star}
with a factor
v\in(0,1)
w.r.t. any metric induced by a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, with a constant
\eta/v^{n_{0}}.
Assume now that
c\in(0,1).
Then,

\forall n\in\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq\\
\|x_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|+\|y_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq\eta+\gamma_{y}c^{n}\\
=\Big(\frac{\eta}{c^{n}}+\gamma_{y}\Big)c^{n}\leq\Big(\frac{\eta}{c^{n_{0}}}+\gamma_{y}\Big)c^{n}=\gamma_{x_{1}}c^{n},

where
\gamma_{x_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\eta c^{-n_{0}}+\gamma_{y}.
Moreover,

\forall n>n_{0}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|=\|y_{n+1-n_{0}}-x^{\star}\|\leq\gamma_{y}c^{n+1-n_{0}}\\
=c^{1-n_{0}}\gamma_{y}c^{n}=\gamma_{x_{2}}c^{n},

where
\gamma_{x_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}c^{1-n_{0}}\gamma_{y}.
Let
\gamma_{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\max\{\gamma_{x_{1}},\gamma_{x_{2}}\}.
We have found
c\in(0,1)
and
\gamma_{x}>0
such that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
one has
\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq\gamma_{x}c^{n}.
∎

III-C

Spectral radius and the convergence rate

It follows from (
2
) that the convergence factor
c
gives an indication on the number of iterations required to obtain a good approximation of the fixed point of a PC mapping. Therefore, the results derived so far should be supplemented with a computationally simple means of obtaining information about
c
. A result of this type is provided in this section. In particular, we note that Proposition
4
proves that, under mild assumptions, the spectral radius of a PC mapping is a lower bound on
c
, and we recall that simple algorithms for computing the spectral radius are available in the literature
[
4
, Remarks 2 and 3]
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a continuous and concave (w.r.t. to the cone order) mapping (i.e., a continuous PC mapping) with a fixed point
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}).
Let
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a compact neighborhood of
x^{\star}.
Furthermore, let
c\in[0,1)
be a local contraction factor of
f
on
U
, and denote by
\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}\rho(f_{\infty})
the spectral radius of the asymptotic mapping
f_{\infty}
of
f
. Then
c\geq\rho.

Proof.

Denote by
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\backslash\{0\}
a (nonlinear) eigenvector associated with the spectral radius
\rho
; i.e., a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\backslash\{0\}
satisfying
f_{\infty}(v)=\rho v
, which is guaranteed to exist
[
36
]
. The result is trivial if
\rho=0
, so we focus on the case
\rho>0
. Let
x^{\star}\in U
be the unique fixed point of
f
(see Fact
2
) and let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be constructed via the fixed point iteration of
f
with
x_{1}\in U
such that
x_{1}\ll x^{\star}
or
x_{1}\gg x^{\star}.
Then, from Proposition
3
, we can find a constant
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|\leq\gamma c^{n}.

(26)

Furthermore, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
x_{1}\leq x^{\star}-\epsilon v
or
x_{1}\geq x^{\star}+\epsilon v
, so
[
19
, Proposition 5(iii)]
asserts that

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad\rho^{n}\epsilon\|v\|\leq\|x_{n+1}-x^{\star}\|.

(27)

By combining the inequalities in (
26
) and (
27
), we conclude that
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
one has that
\rho^{n}\epsilon\|v\|\leq\gamma c^{n}
. Therefore
0<\epsilon\|v\|\leq\gamma(c/\rho)^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}.
If
c<\rho
, then
c/\rho<1
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}(c/\rho)^{n}=0.
As a result, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\epsilon\|v\|>\gamma(c/\rho)^{n_{0}}.
This contradiction implies that we must have
c\geq\rho
, and the proof is complete. ∎

Remark 8
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a continuous PC mapping with a fixed point
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}).
Let
U\subset\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
be a compact neighborhood of
x^{\star}.
Then, it is important to highlight that the meaning of
c\in[0,1)
is consistent among

•

Proposition
1
, where
c
is used to denote the local contraction factor of
f
on
U
,

•

Proposition
4
, where
c
is used in the sense of Proposition
1
,

•

Proposition
3
, where
c
is used in the sense of Propositions
1
and
4
, and it is also recognized as a factor of geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration of
f
to the unique fixed point.

In particular, Part 5) of the proof of Proposition
1
provides a method to determine the value of
c
as

c\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}c(\lambda_{0})=\frac{\ln((1-\mu)\lambda_{0}+\mu)}{\ln\lambda_{0}}
, with
\lambda_{0}=e^{\max_{x,y\in U}d_{T}(x,y)}\in(1,\infty)
and
\mu\in(0,1)
such that
f(0)\geq\mu f(x)
for all
x\in U.
Therefore, it is seen that, if

is increasing slowly in the sense that
x^{\star}=f(x^{\star})
is not much larger than

, and the neighborhood

of

Thus, we obtain a useful insight into the relationship between

and

, and we also obtain an achievable lower bound for

is bounded below by the spectral radius of of the asymptotic mapping

of

IV
Applications

We now discuss two common applications of the fixed point iteration of positive concave mappings in wireless networks. We also show how the theory developed in the previous sections is able to explain, with rigorous mathematical statements, convergence properties of existing algorithms.

IV-A

Joint optimization of transmit power, user-base station assignment, and receive beamformers in massive MIMO systems

Consider the uplink of a wireless network with
k
single antenna users sending data to
m
base stations, each equipped with
L
antennas. The set of users and base stations are given by
\mathcal{U}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{1,\dots,m\}
, respectively. The set of base stations to which user
u\in\mathcal{U}
is allowed to connect is represented by the set
\mathcal{B}_{u}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. Denote by
x=[x[1],\dots,x[k]]\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
the transmit power vector, where the
u
th coordinate
x[u]\geq 0
of the vector
x
is the transmit power of user
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Let
h_{u,b}
, with
(u,b)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{B}
, represent the channel between user
u
and base station
b
. As common in the literature, for every
(u,b)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{B}
, we assume
h_{u,b}
to be a random vector taking values in
\mathbb{C}^{L}
. For simplicity, we further assume
h_{u,b}
to be zero mean with positive definite channel covariance matrix
R_{u,b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\tiny\mbox{df.}}}}{{=}}E[h_{u,b}h_{u,b}^{H}]\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L}
. In this setting, the signal-to-interference-noise ratio (SINR) of user
u
as a function of its receive beamformer
v\in\mathcal{V}_{u}
, base station assignment
b
, and transmit power vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
is given by
[
5
, Sect. 1.4.2]

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}s_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\times\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}&\to\real_{+}\\
(x,v,b)&\mapsto\dfrac{x[u]~v^{H}R_{u,b}v}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}\backslash\{u\}}v^{H}(x[j]R_{j,b}+\sigma^{2}~I_{L})v},\end{array}

where
\sigma^{2}>0
denotes the noise power;
I_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
is the identity matrix; and
\mathcal{V}_{u}\subset\mathbb{C}^{L}
is a set of allowed beamformers for user
u
, typically chosen to be
\mathcal{V}_{u}=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{L}~|~{v}^{H}{v}=1\}
. As in
[
23
]
, users are free to choose the base station to be served.

A standard power control problem is to find the transmit power, the user-base station assignments, and the beamformers that minimize the sum power while satisfying given SINR requirements. Formally, denoting by
\gamma_{u}>0
the minimum SINR requirement of user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, we pose the optimization problem as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{minimize}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}&\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}x[u]\\
\text{subject to}&\forall u\in\mathcal{U}\quad\max_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}s_{u}(x,v,b)\geq\gamma_{u}.\end{array}

For later reference, we say that a vector
x\in\real_{+}^{k}
is feasible to problem (
IV-A
) if it satisfies all constraints, even if
x
is not necessarily a solution. In the above formulation, the optimization of the beamformers and user-base station assignments is implicit in the constraint. More precisely, if a power allocation
x
is given (e.g., an optimal power allocation
x^{\star}
), then a beamformer
v^{\star}_{u}
and a base station assignment
b_{u}^{\star}
maximizing the SINR for user
u\in\mathcal{U}
can be recovered by computing:

\displaystyle(v^{\star}_{u},b_{u}^{\star})\in\mathrm{argmax}_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}s_{u}(x,v,b).

In practice, given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
and user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, the tuple
(v^{\star}_{u},b_{u}^{\star})
is obtained by, first, computing the beamformers maximizing the SINR for each of the
|\mathcal{B}_{u}|\leq m
possible assignments, and by, second, checking the choice that leads to the maximum SINR. The computational complexity of this approach is manageable if for each user
u
the number
|\mathcal{B}_{u}|
of candidate assignments is not too large, which is a typical property of cellular networks because base stations that are too far away are unable to provide adequate SINR, so they can be ruled out from consideration. We also note that, if fully digital beamformers are used, in which case we can choose
\mathcal{V}_{u}=\{v\in\mathbb{C}^{L}~|~{v}^{H}{v}=1\}
for every
u\in\mathcal{U}
, then the beamformer maximizing the SINR for a given user, base station assignment, and power vector can be easily computed by maximizing a generalized Rayleigh quotient. Nevertheless, we do not necessarily assume digital beamformers in this section; we only require the following very mild assumption:

Assumption 1
.

The set
C_{u,x}=\mathrm{argmax}_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}s_{u}(x,v,b)
is nonempty for every
u\in\mathcal{U}
and every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
.

A well-known result in the literature shows that, if a vector
x^{\star}
is a solution to the optimization problem (
IV-A
) (assuming that a solution exists), then the constraints have to be satisfied with equality; i.e.,

\displaystyle\forall u\in\mathcal{U}~\max_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}s_{u}(x^{\star},v,b)=\gamma_{u}.

(30)

This result can be easily proved by noticing that, if a strict SINR inequality is obtained for some user, then we can decrease the transmit power of this user (thus decreasing the cost function in
(\ref{problem.opt})
), until an equality is achieved. By decreasing the transmit power of the user, the SINR of other users cannot decrease, so the resulting power vector is also feasible. Therefore, feasible power allocations satisfying all constraints with at least one strict inequality cannot be optimal if problem
(\ref{problem.opt})
has a solution.

The above fact enables us to replace the inequalities in the constraints of problem (
IV-A
) with equalities. Therefore, using simple algebraic manipulations, we can equivalently express the constraints in (
IV-A
) as the fixed point set of the following mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
:

\displaystyle\forall u\in\mathcal{U}~\max_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}s_{u}(x^{\star},v,b)=\gamma_{u}\iff x^{\star}\in\mathrm{Fix}(f),

(31)

where

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}):x\mapsto[f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{k}(x)]

(32)

and
\forall u\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}f_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}&\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}_{+}})\\
x\mapsto&\min\limits_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}\gamma_{u}\dfrac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}\backslash\{u\}}v^{H}(x[j]R_{j,b}+\sigma^{2}~I_{L})v}{v^{H}R_{u,b}v}.\end{array}

To avoid digressions of little practical relevance, assume that
k\geq 2
and
\forall u\in\mathcal{U}\quad 0\notin\mathcal{V}_{u}
, so that
\forall v\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\quad v^{H}R_{u,b}v>0
because every matrix
R_{u,b}
is positive definite. Since
\gamma_{u}>0
and
\sigma^{2}>0
by assumption, we deduce
\forall x\in\real_{+}^{k}\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}
:

f_{u}(x)=\min\limits_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}\gamma_{u}\dfrac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}\backslash\{u\}}v^{H}(x[j]R_{j,b}+\sigma^{2}~I_{L})v}{v^{H}R_{u,b}v}\\
\geq\min\limits_{(v,b)\in\mathcal{V}_{u}\times\mathcal{B}_{u}}\gamma_{u}\sigma^{2}(k-1)\dfrac{v^{H}v}{v^{H}R_{u,b}v}=f_{u}(0)>0.

(33)

Furthermore, each function
f_{u}
can be seen as the minimum of affine functions, so
f_{u}
is not only positive but also concave, and thus
f_{u}
is a PC mapping. We can tacitly assume
f_{u}
to be continuous in the whole domain because, even if
f_{u}
were not continuous on the boundary of its domain, being concave,
f_{u}
is continuous in
\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
, and its restriction to
\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})
can be continuously extended to the whole domain
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}

[
12
, Theorem 5.1.5]
. Such an extension of
{f_{u}}_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu{\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})}}
to the domain
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
would preserve positivity because the range of
{f_{u}}_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu{\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})}}
is bounded away from zero as shown in (
33
).

Below we list some advantages of using the equivalence relation in (
31
) and the result in (
30
) to replace the inequality constraints in problem (
IV-A
) with the fixed point set of the PC mapping in (
32
):

Remark 9
.

(i)

We immediately deduce that problem (
IV-A
) has at most one solution because the set
\mathrm{Fix}(f)
is a singleton if not empty (Fact
2
);

(ii)

Problem (
IV-A
) has a solution if and only if
\rho(f_{\infty})<1
(Fact

), which is a condition that can be easily verified with the simple numerical approaches described in
[

, Remarks 2 and 3]
;

(iii)

If
x^{\star}
is the solution to problem (
IV-A
), then
x^{\star}
is the limit of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
produced via the fixed point iteration
x_{n+1}=f(x_{n})
,
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
(Fact
6
in the Appendix).

(iv)

The convergence of the fixed point iteration of
f
is geometric (Proposition
3
) if the fixed point exists.

In this particular application, the theory described in the previous sections goes beyond results available in the wireless literature w.r.t. the following aspects:

Remark 10
.

(i)

Assuming that the set
\mathcal{V}_{u}
is a singleton for every

, then the studies in
[

]
show

in (

) to have a fixed point. In contrast, Remark

(ii)

More importantly, if the set
\mathcal{V}_{u}
contains uncountably many beamformers for some user

(e.g., if base stations are equipped with fully digital beamformers, in which case

for every

), then the mapping

in (

) cannot be expressed as the coordinate-wise minimum of a

]
cannot be used to establish geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration of

. Furthermore, the study in

-contraction in the sense in

. In contrast to the results in

(iii)

shows simple techniques to obtain information about the contraction factor of the fixed point iteration of

We finish this section by showing that the theory developed in this study can also explain the convergence speed of extensions of the power control algorithm in Remark

(iii).

Remark 11
.

A possible drawback of the fixed point iteration in Remark
9
(iii), which is a consequence of the optimization problem posed in (
IV-A
), is that no maximum power constraints are imposed. To prevent users from transmitting with excessive power, we can replace the mapping
f
in the fixed point iteration with

\displaystyle\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}):x\mapsto[\min\{f_{1}(x),\bar{p}\},\ldots,\min\{f_{u}(x),\bar{p}\}],

where
\bar{p}>0
is the maximum allowed power. Note that
\bar{f}
is also concave because each coordinate function is the minimum of concave functions. Well-known power control algorithms emerge as particular cases of the fixed point iteration of
\bar{f}
. For example, by fixing the sets
(\mathcal{V}_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{U}}
to be singletons and the random channel vectors to be almost surely constant, we obtain the power control algorithm discussed in
[
37
, Eq. (7)]
as a special case of the fixed point iteration of the resulting mapping
\bar{f}
. Whether such simplifications are used or not, we always have
\rho(\bar{f}_{\infty})=0
because each coordinate of
\bar{f}
is upper bounded by
\bar{p}
, and thus
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\quad\bar{f}_{\infty}(x)=0
. As a result, Fact
2
shows that
\mathrm{Fix}(\bar{f})\neq\emptyset
, and the fixed point iteration of
\bar{f}
converges geometrically to the unique vector in
\mathrm{Fix}(\bar{f})
as a consequence of Proposition
3
.

IV-B

Load estimation in orthogonal frequency division multiple access (OFDMA) networks

As a second example of an application of the fixed point iteration of positive concave mappings, we describe the problem of load estimation in wireless networks
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
10
,
38
,
3
,
4
,
9
,
19
]
. We assume that a network uses the OFDMA technology, and we consider the same downlink scenario (i.e., data transmission is from base stations to users) used in the simulations in
[
19
]
. To pose the problem, we use the following definitions:

•

\mathcal{B}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
- set of base stations;

•

\mathcal{U}
- set of users;

•

\mathcal{U}_{b}\neq\emptyset
- set of users connected to base station
b
;

•

B
- bandwidth of each resource block;

•

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}\quad d[u]
- traffic requested by the
u
th user;

•

g[u,b]>0
- pathloss between base station
b
and user
u
;

•

R
- number of resource blocks in the OFDMA system;

•

\forall b\in\mathcal{B}\quad p[b]
- transmit power per resource block of base station
b
;

•

\sigma^{2}
- noise power per resource block.

•

x=[x[1],\ldots,x[k]]
- load vector. More precisely, the
b
th coordinate
x[b]
is the fraction of the total number of resource blocks that base station
b
requires to serve the traffic demand of its users in the set
\mathcal{U}_{b}
.

For a given load
x
, the modified Shannon capacity
r_{u,b}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
, which has been independently proposed in
[
16
,
15
]
and defined below, is often used as a proxy of the rate per resource block that a user
u\in\mathcal{U}
can achieve if connected to base station
b\in\mathcal{B}

[
15
,
16
,
17
,
10
,
38
,
3
,
4
,
9
,
19
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}r_{u,b}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to&\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\\
x\mapsto&B\log_{2}\left(1+\dfrac{p[b]g[u,b]}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\{b\}}x[j]p[j]g[u,j]+\sigma^{2}}\right).\end{array}

The above model assumes no intracell interference; i.e., users connected to a given base station use distinct resource blocks. However, there is intercell interference, which corresponds to the term
\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\{b\}}x[j]p[j]g[u,j]
in the definition of
r_{u,b}
. As a result, if the load vector
x
is given, and user
u
is connected to base station
b
, then this base station needs to reserve
d[u]/r_{u,b}(x)
resource blocks to satisfy the traffic demand of user
u
. By definition, the
b
th coordinate
x[b]
of the load vector
x
is the total number of resource blocks, normalized by number of resource blocks
R
in the system, that base station
b
reserves for all its users; i.e.,

\displaystyle\forall b\in\mathcal{B}\quad x[b]=\dfrac{1}{R}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{b}}\dfrac{d[u]}{r_{u,b}(x)}.

The above equations show that the load vector
x
can be equivalently expressed as the fixed point of the following mapping:

\displaystyle f:\real_{+}^{k}\to\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}):{x}\mapsto[f_{1}({x}),\cdots,f_{k}({x})],

(34)

where

\displaystyle\forall b\in\mathcal{B}\quad\begin{array}[]{rl}f_{b}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to&\operatorname{int}({\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}})\\
x\mapsto&\dfrac{1}{R}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{b}}\dfrac{d[u]}{r_{u,b}(x)}.\end{array}

Therefore, to estimate the load
x
of a network, we need to solve the following fixed point problem:

\displaystyle\text{Find }x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\text{ such that }x\in\mathrm{Fix}(f).

(35)

Standard arguments in convex analysis show that
f
is a continuous positive concave mapping
[
16
,
15
,
38
]
. Therefore, all the theory in the previous sections can be applied to analyze and solve problem (
35
). In particular, we deduce the following properties:

(i)

Problem (
35
) has at most one solution (Fact
2
);

(ii)

The asymptotic mapping associated with
f
is given by
[
4
]

f_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}:{x}\mapsto\mathrm{diag}({p})^{-1}{M}\mathrm{diag}({p}){x},

where
\mathrm{diag}({p})
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements given by
\forall b\in\mathcal{B}\quad[\mathrm{diag}({p})]_{bb}=p[b]
, and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}_{+}
is the matrix with its
i
th row and
b
th column given by:

\displaystyle[{M}]_{i,b}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }i=b\\
\sum\limits_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}\dfrac{\mathrm{ln}(2)d[u]g[u,b]}{{RBg[u,i]}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that the asymptotic mapping
f_{\infty}
is linear, so
\rho(f_{\infty})=\rho(\mathrm{diag}({p})^{-1}{M}\mathrm{diag}({p}))=\rho(M)
is simply the spectral radius
\rho(M)=\rho(\mathrm{diag}({p})^{-1}{M}\mathrm{diag}({p}))
of the matrix
{M}
, where the spectral radius of matrices should be understood in the conventional sense in linear algebra. It now follows from Fact
2
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(f)\neq\emptyset\iff\rho(M)<1.

This criterion for checking the existence of a feasible load vector has been first obtained in
[
10
]
, and it can be seen as a particular application of Fact
2
, as discussed in
[
4
]
.

(iii)

If
\mathrm{Fix}(f)\neq\emptyset
, then the unique fixed point of
f
is the limit of the sequence generated via the fixed point iteration of
f
(Fact
6
in the Appendix).

(iv)

The fixed point iteration of
f
converges geometrically (Proposition
3
), and the convergence factor
c
of the iteration is lower bounded by the spectral radius of the matrix
M
defined above (Proposition
4
).

We emphasize that points (i)-(iii) are known, but point (iv) is a new result that does not seem easy to derive from existing theory for reasons similar to those given in Remark
10
. More precisely, the mapping in (
34
) cannot be written as the coordinate-wise minimum of a finite number of affine mappings, as required in
[
21
]
. Furthermore, it is not clear whether that mapping satisfies the conditions in
[
20
, Definition 2]
. Even if this last condition could be established, the study in
[
20
]
does not discuss any simple computational means, such as those described in Sect.
III-C
, to determine bounds on the contraction factor.

Before we proceed with numerical examples to illustrate the main findings in this study, we recall one potential caveat of the load model discussed here:

Remark 12
.

By definition, the coordinates of the load vector
x
cannot exceed the value one, otherwise the corresponding base stations would be using more resource blocks than available in the system, which is physically impossible. Nevertheless, if any coordinate of the solution to problem (
35
) exceeds the value one, we obtain useful information about the unserved traffic demand. We refer the readers to
[
16
]
for more information on this point.

VI
Summary and final remarks

Previous studies in the wireless literature
[
3
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
[
5
, Ch. 5.3]
have devoted a great deal of attention to characterize the convergence speed of fixed point iterations of mappings that are common in power control and load estimation problems. In many of the these applications, the mappings are in fact positive and concave. However, as discussed in Sect.
IV
, previous results proving geometric convergence of the fixed point iteration of positive concave mappings require additional assumptions that are often too strong to address some problems of practical interest, or that are too difficult to verify in practice, or both. In this study we have proved that these additional assumptions are not required: we only need the existence of a fixed point for the fixed point iteration of positive concave mappings to converge geometrically. We have also shown that even by replacing the assumption of positivity with nonnegativity we lose the guarantees of geometric convergence, so the assumption of positivity is crucial. Finally, we have also derived results that provide us with clear indication of the local contraction factor of the mappings, which is useful information to assess whether many fixed point iterations are required to obtain a good estimate of the fixed point.
