Adaptive Learning of Density Ratios in RKHS
Werner Zellinger werner.zellinger@oeaw.ac.at, Stefan Kindermann kindermann@indmath.uni-linz.ac.at, Sergei V. Pereverzyev sergei.pereverzyev@oeaw.ac.at

Abstract
Estimating the ratio of two probability densities from finitely many observations of the densities is a central problem in machine learning and statistics with applications in two-sample testing, divergence estimation, generative modeling, covariate shift adaptation, conditional density estimation, and novelty detection. In this work, we analyze a large class of density ratio estimation methods that minimize a regularized Bregman divergence between the true density ratio and a model in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). We derive new finite-sample error bounds, and we propose a Lepskii type parameter choice principle that minimizes the bounds without knowledge of the regularity of the density ratio. In the special case of square loss, our method adaptively achieves a minimax optimal error rate. A numerical illustration is provided.

Introduction

1.1
Problem

Let
P
and
Q
be two probability measures on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
with compact
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
of its measurable subsets.
Assume that
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
.
Then, the Radon-Nikodým theorem grants us a
Q
-integrable function
\beta:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
satisfying, for any
U\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle P(U)=\int_{U}\beta(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x).

(1)

If both measures,
P
and
Q
, have densities with respect to another reference measure, e.g., the Lebesgue measure, then
\beta
equals the
ratio of their densities
.

The problem of learning
\beta
from samples

{\bf x}:=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
{\bf x}^{\prime}:=(x_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{n}
, drawn independently and identically (i.i.d.) from
P
and
Q
, respectively,
plays a central role in statistics and machine learning
(
Sugiyama et al. 2012a
)
; its applications include anomaly detection
(
Smola et al. 2009
;
Hido et al. 2011
)
, two-sample testing
(
Keziou and Leoni-Aubin 2005
;
Kanamori et al. 2011
)
, divergence estimation
(
Nguyen et al. 2007
;
Nguyen et al. 2010
)
, covariate shift adaptation
(
Shimodaira 2000
;
Gizewski et al. 2022
;
Dinu et al. 2023
)
, generative modeling
(
Mohamed and Lakshminarayanan 2016
)
, conditional density estimation
(
Schuster et al. 2020
)
, and classification from
positive and unlabeled data
(
Kato et al. 2019
)
.
Note, that this problem is different from supervised learning
(
Cucker and Smale 2001
)
as the given data do not include point evaluations
\beta(x)
from the desired target
\beta
.

It has been first observed by
Sugiyama et al. 2012b
that many algorithms for density ratio estimation compute finite-sample approximations of the objective
\beta
by discretizing the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}B_{F}\!\left(\beta,g(f)\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2},

(2)

where
g(f)
is a model of the Radon-Nikodým derivative
\beta
with
f\in\mathcal{H}

in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
,

B_{F}(\beta,\widetilde{\beta}):=F(\beta)-F(\widetilde{\beta})-\nabla F(\widetilde{\beta})[\beta-\widetilde{\beta}]
is a Bregman divergence generated by some convex functional
F:L^{1}(Q)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\left\lVert.\right\rVert
is a norm on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a regularization parameter; see Example
1
.
For the exact form of the
finite-sample approximations applied in practice, we ask the reader for patience until Eq. (
11
) is stated.

Example 1
.

•

As can be seen from
(
Sugiyama et al. 2012b
, Section 3.2.1)
, the kernel unconstrained least-squares importance fitting procedure (KuLSIF) in
(
Kanamori et al. 2009
)
uses Eq. (
2
) with
F(h)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}(h(x)-1)^{2}/2\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x)
and
g=\mathrm{id}
, where
\mathrm{id}(f)=f
is the identity (cf.
(
Kanamori et al. 2012
, Eq. (4))
).
This leads to a discretization of the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\beta-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}.

(3)

•

The logistic regression approach (LR) employed in
(
Bickel et al. 2009
, Section 7)
can be obtained from Eq. (
2
) using
F(h)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)\log(h(x))-(1+h(x))\log(1+h(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x)
and
g(f)=e^{f}
with
f(x)=\theta^{\text{T}}\varphi(x)
for some
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
; see
(
Sugiyama et al. 2012b
, Section 3.2.3)
.

•

The exponential function approach (Exp) introduced in
(
Menon and Ong 2016
, Section 7)
can be obtained using
F(h)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(x)^{-3/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x)
and
g(f)=e^{2f}
; see
(
Menon and Ong 2016
, Table 1)
.

•

The square loss (SQ) is used in
(
Menon and Ong 2016
, Table 1)
with
F(h)=\int_{X}1/(2h(x)+2)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x)
and
g(f)=\frac{-1+2f}{2-2f}
.

To the best of our knowledge, no error bounds are known for estimating Eq. (
2
) for the functionals in Example
1
that take into account the regularity of the density ratio
\beta
, except
(
Gizewski et al. 2022
, Section 3.1)
and
(
Nguyen et al. 2023
)
which hold only for KuLSIF.
Moreover, except for KuLSIF, no method for choosing the regularization parameter
\lambda
in Eq. (
2
) is known that adapts to the regularity of
\beta
, i.e., a method with error bounds provably decreasing for increasing regularity.

1.2
Results

Following
Menon and Ong 2016
, one can relate the minimization of the data fidelity term
B(f):=B_{F}(\beta,g(f))
from Eq. (
2
), to the minimization of the expected loss with a loss function
\ell:\{-1,1\}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
measuring by
\ell(-1,f(x))
and
\ell(1,f(x))
, respectively, the error of a classifier
f(x)
which predicts whether
x
is drawn from
P
or
Q
.

Our first result (Example
6
) shows that all loss functions
\ell
corresponding to Example
1
, satisfy a property called
generalized self-concordance

(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
.
This allows us to extend results from supervised learning in RKHSs to density ratio estimation.

Our second result (Proposition
2
) provides, in a well specified setting, for large enough sample sizes and with high probability, an error bound

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}}^{\lambda})\leq O\left((m+n)^{-\frac{2r\alpha+\alpha}{2r\alpha+\alpha+1}}\right)

(4)

for a minimizer
f_{{\bf x},{\bf x}^{\prime}}^{\lambda}
of the empirical estimation Eq. (
11
) of Eq. (
2
) with appropriately chosen regularization parameter
\lambda
.
The regularity index
r
and the capacity index
\alpha
in Eq. (
4
) originate from source conditions as used in inverse problems and learning theory.
In the special case of square loss (SQ), the error rate in Eq. (
4
) is minimax optimal in the same sense as it is minimax optimal in supervised learning, see
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Blanchard and Mücke 2018
)
.

Our third result (Eq. (
30
)) is a new method for choosing the regularization parameter
\lambda
that does not need access to the regularity index
r
but nevertheless achieves the same rate as in Eq. (
4
) (see Theorem
2
).
Our method is based on a Lepskii-type principle balancing bias and variance terms of a local quadratic approximation of the (generally not quadratic) data fidelity
B(f)
.
Our parameter choice strategy achieves the error rate in Eq. (
4
) and is thus optimal in the case of square loss.
A numerical example illustrates our theoretical findings.

1.3
Related Work

The representation of density ratio estimation methods as minimizers of empirical estimations of Eq. (
2
) has been first observed by
Sugiyama et al. 2012b
and later characterized in more detail by
Menon and Ong 2016
.
Our work extends
(
Sugiyama et al. 2012b
;
Menon and Ong 2016
)
by taking into account the effect of the regularization term
\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}
in Eq. (
2
).

In general, to the best of our knowledge, there are no error bounds similar to Eq. (
4
) for the class of discretized minimization problems Eq. (
2
).
For the special case of KuLSIF in Eq. (
3
), error rates have been discovered in
(
Kanamori et al. 2012
;
Gizewski et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
)
.
In particular, the error rate discovered in
(
Kanamori et al. 2012
)
, for RKHS with
bracketing entropy

\gamma\in(0,2)
, is

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})=O\!\left(\min\{m,n\}^{-\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\right).

(5)

This rate is worse than the one in Eq. (
4
), whenever
\alpha(2r+1)>\frac{2}{\gamma}
.
A similar result for KuLSIF and the special case of Gaussian kernel, is presented in
Que and Belkin 2013
, see Type I setting there.
We can also mention
(
Gizewski et al. 2022
, Remark 3)
which provides the error bound

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})=O\!\left(\left(m^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{4r^{\prime}+2}{2r^{\prime}+2}}\right)

(6)

with
r^{\prime}
originating from a source condition similar to our Assumption
5
but for the covariance operator
T=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(z,\cdot)\otimes k(z,\cdot)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(z)
of
Q
instead of
\rho
, where
k
denotes the kernel of
\mathcal{H}
and
(u\otimes v)[f]=\langle u,f\rangle v
the rank-
1
operator.
Our result in Eq. (
4
) improves Eq. (
6
) for
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
and
0<r^{\prime}\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
In the recent work
(
Nguyen et al. 2023
)
, Eq. (
6
) is improved by additionally taking into account the capacity of the space
\mathcal{H}
in terms of a new pointwise source condition on the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
that allows to bound the regularized Christoffel function.
Such condition can be used as alternative for the effective dimension as defined in our Assumption
6
.
As a result, an error bound similar to our Eq. (
4
) is obtained in
(
Nguyen et al. 2023
)
. However, the error is measured in the
\mathcal{H}
-norm and the bound holds only for the special case of the KuLSIF.

To the best of our knowledge, the balancing principle introduced in this work is the first parameter choice method for density ratio estimation with discovered error rate.
We can mention variants of cross-validation
(
Kanamori et al. 2012
)
, the quasi-optimality criterion
(
Nguyen et al. 2023
)
, and hints towards the use of balancing principles
(
Gizewski et al. 2022
)
, but, even in the special case of KuLSIF, we are not aware of any finite-sample error bounds related to these approaches.

Some of our proof techniques rely on
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
, where self-concordant loss functions in the context of kernel learning were studied first.
Our analysis extends
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
to density ratio estimation and adaptive parameter choice.

2.1
Estimation of Bregman Divergence

Menon and Ong 2016
study properties of

{\bf x}=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
from

{\bf x}^{\prime}=(x_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{n}

.
In the following, we will summarize some of these properties.

Let us start by introducing the underlying model: a probability measure
\rho

\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
\mathcal{Y}:=\{-1,1\}
, with conditional probability measures
1
1

1

We make the following assumption on the data generation process.

Assumption 1

(iid data)
.

The data

\displaystyle{\bf z}:=(x_{i},1)_{i=1}^{m}\cup(x_{i}^{\prime},-1)_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}

Assumption

Recall the goal of the binary classification problem is to find, based on the data

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\rho}[\ell(y,f(x))]=\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,f(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(x,y)

In this work, we focus on

Assumption 2
.

The function

Lemma 2

(

Let the Bregman generator

\displaystyle F(h):=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}(1+h(x))\cdot G\!\left(\frac{h(x)}{1+h(x)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q(x),\quad g(f):=\frac{\Psi^{-1}\circ f}{1-\Psi^{-1}\circ f},

for some link function

\displaystyle B_{F}(\beta,g(f))=2\left(\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f^{\ast})\right).

Indeed, for all approaches in Example

.

•

The goal of this work is to analyze the error

2.2
Learning in RKHS with Convex Losses

Lemma
2
identifies the methods in Example
1
as risk minimization problems with loss functions as detailed in Example
3
.
We will see in Section
3
that these loss functions satisfy the following property when applied to functions
f\in\mathcal{H}
from an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
.

Assumption 3

(generalized self-concordance
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
)
.

For any
z=(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the function
\ell_{z}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R},\ell_{z}(f):=\ell(y,f(x)),f\in\mathcal{H}
, is convex, three times differentiable, and it exists a set
\varphi(z)\subset\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{H},\forall h,k\in\mathcal{H}:|\nabla^{3}\ell_{z}(f)[k,h,h]|\leq\nabla^{2}\ell_{z}(f)[h,h]\cdot\sup_{p\in\varphi(z)}|k\cdot p|.

(12)

The definition of self-concordance originates from the analysis of Newton’s method
(
Nesterov and Nemirovskii 1994
)
and has been generalized in
(
Bach 2010
;
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
.
Before we review error bounds, we follow
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
and make the following technical assumptions.

Assumption 4

(technical assumptions)
.

For self-concordant
\ell_{z}(\cdot)
with the set
\varphi(z)\subset\mathcal{H}
in Eq. (
12
), it holds:

1.

There exists
R\geq 0
such that for any realization
z
of the random variable
Z
,
\sup_{g\in\varphi(z)}\left\lVert g\right\rVert\leq R
almost surely.

2.

The following quantities are almost surely bounded for any realization
z
of the random variable
Z
:
|\ell_{z}(0)|,\left\lVert\nabla\ell_{z}(0)\right\rVert,\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{z}(0))
.

3.

There exists a minimizer
f_{\mathcal{H}}:=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{R}(f)
.

In the sequel, the symbols
\varphi(Z)
and
\ell_{Z}
mean the varieties of sets
\varphi(z)
and functions
\ell_{z}
corresponding to the realizations
z
of the random variable
Z
.
The first point of Assumption
4
is standard in learning theory
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Steinwart et al. 2009
)
and, together with point 2, assures that
\mathcal{R}(f)
is well defined for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
.
Point 3 imposes that our model is
well specified
in the sense that the function class
\mathcal{H}
contains the minimizer of
\mathcal{R}
.
Under these assumptions, Theorem 3 in
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
holds, which we state in our notation as follows:

Lemma 4

(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
, Theorem 3)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
3
,
4
be satisfied,
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, and define
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
as

\displaystyle B_{1}:=\sup_{\left\lVert f\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert}\sup_{z\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho)}\left\lVert\nabla\ell_{z}(f)\right\rVert,\quad B_{2}:=\sup_{\left\lVert f\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert}\sup_{z\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho)}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{z}(f)).

Then, whenever
\lambda\leq B_{2}
and
m+n
is larger than

\displaystyle\max\left\{512\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert^{2}R^{2}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right),512\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right),24\frac{B_{2}}{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{8B_{2}}{\lambda\delta}\right),256\frac{R^{2}B_{1}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right\},

(13)

a minimizer

of Eq. (

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})-\mathcal{R}(f_{\mathcal{H}})\leq\frac{84B_{1}^{2}}{\lambda(m+n)}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)+2\lambda\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(14)

Lemma
4
is a general result, which does not take into account cases where a regularity of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
is assumed beyond
f_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
in Assumption

Such regularity can be encoded by so-called

as used in the regularization theory for inverse problems
(
Engl et al. 1996
;
Bauer et al. 2007
;
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;

;

.

Assumption 5

(source condition)
.

There exist
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}],v\in\mathcal{H}
such that
f_{\mathcal{H}}={\bf H}(f_{\mathcal{H}})^{r}v
with the expected Hessian
{\bf H}(f):=\mathbb{E}[\nabla^{2}\ell_{Z}(f)]
.

In the case of square loss, Assumption
5
recovers the polynomial source condition
f_{\mathcal{H}}=T^{r}v
with covariance operator
T=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(z,\cdot)\otimes k(z,\cdot)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(z)
, for the kernel
k
of
\mathcal{H}
and the rank-
1
operator
(u\otimes v)[f]=\langle u,f\rangle v
.
Starting from
r=0
(assuming only
f_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
), the regularity of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
increases with increasing
r
, essentially reducing the number of eigenvectors of
T
needed to well approximate
f_{\mathcal{H}}
.

It is well known in learning theory
(
Zhang 2002
;
Caponnetto et al. 2005
;
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)
that the rate of convergence of regularized learning algorithms is not only influenced by the regularity of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
, but also by the
capacity
of the space
\mathcal{H}
measured by the so-called
effective dimension
.
We therefore make the following assumption of
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
, Assumption 7)
, which generalizes
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
, Definition 1)
.

Assumption 6

(capacity condition)
.

There exist
\alpha\geq 1,Q_{0}\geq 0
such that
\mathrm{df}_{\lambda}\leq Q_{0}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}
with the degrees of freedom

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}_{\lambda}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert{\bf H}_{\lambda}(f_{\mathcal{H}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\ell_{Z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})\right\rVert^{2}\right]

(15)

and
{\bf H}_{\lambda}(f):={\bf H}(f)+\lambda I
.

The
degrees of freedom
term
\mathrm{df}_{\lambda}
appears as
Fisher information
in the asymptotic analysis of M-estimation
(
Van der Vaart 2000
;
Lehmann and Casella 2006
)
, appears similarly in spline smoothing
(
Wahba 1990
)
, and reduces to the
effective dimension

(
Caponnetto et al. 2005
;
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)

\mathrm{df}_{\lambda}=\mathrm{Tr}(TT_{\lambda}^{-1})
with
T_{\lambda}:=T+\lambda I
for the square loss.
In the latter case, Assumption
6
is equivalent to the condition
\sigma_{j}=O(j^{-\alpha})
for a sequence
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
of non-increasing positive eigenvalues of
T

(
Guo et al. 2022
, Theorem 5)
.
That is, a bigger
\alpha
implies that fewer eigenvectors are needed to approximate elements from the image space of
T
with a given capacity.
On the other hand, from a regularization theory perspective,
Assumption
6
can be interpreted as specifying the
ill-posedness
of the problem of inverting
T
(cf.
(
Blanchard and Mücke 2018
)
).

Under Assumptions
5
and
6
, the following result is stated in
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
and rephrased here in our notation.
See Subsection
6.4
for more details on the derivation.

Lemma 5

(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
, Theorem 38)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
3
–
6
be satisfied,
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, and define
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
by

\displaystyle B_{1}^{\ast}:=\sup_{z\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho)}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla\ell_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})),\quad B_{2}^{\ast}:=\sup_{z\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho)}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{z}(f_{\mathcal{H}})),\quad L:=\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}^{-2r}(f_{\mathcal{H}})},

where
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}:=\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\right\rVert
.
Whenever
0<\lambda\leq\min\{B_{2}^{\ast},(2LR\log\frac{2}{\delta})^{-1/r},Q_{0}^{2\alpha}(B_{2}^{\ast})^{\alpha}(B_{1}^{\ast})^{-2\alpha}\}
and
m+n
is larger than

\displaystyle\max\left\{5184\frac{B_{2}^{\ast}}{\lambda}\log\left(\frac{8\cdot 414^{2}B_{2}^{\ast}}{\lambda\delta}\right),\frac{1296Q_{0}^{2}}{L^{2}\lambda^{1+2r+1/\alpha}}\right\},

(16)

then a minimizer
f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}
of Eq. (
11
) satisfies, with probability at least
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})-\mathcal{R}(f_{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}\leq\left\lVert f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 414\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{(m+n)\lambda^{1/\alpha}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)+414L^{2}\lambda^{1+2r}.

(17)

In the special case of square loss (SQ), it is possible to find
\lambda
in
Lemma
5
that recovers the minimax optimal error rate discovered in
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)
; see
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
, Corollary 39)
.
However,
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
, Corollary 39)
uses an a priori choice of
\lambda
that depends on the regularity level

3
Error Rates under Regularity and Capacity

Let us start by Assumption
1
.
The goal of this section is to analyze the error of a density ratio model
g(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})
with a minimizer
f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}
of Eq. (
11
), in terms of the data fidelity
B(f):=B_{F}(\beta,g(f))
as specified in Eq. (

We note that this setting is general, in the sense that all methods in Example

are included.

Our analysis is based on the observation that all loss functions in Example

are self-concordant as defined in Assumption
3
.

Example 6
.

•

KuLSIF
(
Kanamori et al. 2009
)
uses the losses
\ell_{(x,-1)}(f)=\frac{1}{2}f(x)^{2},\ell_{(x,1)}(f)=-f(x)
which are three times differentiable and Eq. (
12
) holds with
\varphi((x,y))=\{0\}\subset\mathcal{H}
.

•

LR
(
Bickel et al. 2009
)
is self-concordant with
\varphi((x,y))=\{yK_{x}\}
, see
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
, Example 2)
and
(
Bach 2010
)
.

•

Exp
(
Menon and Ong 2016
)
uses
\ell_{(x,y)}(f)=e^{yf(x)}
which is self-concordant with
\varphi((x,y))=\{yK_{x}\}
.

•

SQ
(
Menon and Ong 2016
)
uses losses which are three times differentiable and Eq. (
12
) holds with
\varphi((x,y))=\{0\}\subset\mathcal{H}
.

Before discussing faster error rates with more advanced regularity concepts, let us discuss the special role of the parameter
\lambda
when chosen to balance the two terms in Eq. (
14
).
As an intermediate result, we will provide first error rates for regularized losses beyond the KuLSIF approach in Eq. (
3
).

3.1
Slow Error Rate with A Priori Parameter Choice

We have observed above that many methods for density ratio estimation are based on self-concordant loss functions.
From Lemma
4
we know that, for large enough sample size
m+n
, with probability larger than
1-2\delta
, a minimizer
f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}
of Eq. (
11
)
satisfies the following bound:

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})-B(f_{\mathcal{H}})\leq S(m+n,\delta,\lambda)+A(\lambda),

(18)

where

S(m+n,\delta,\lambda):=168\frac{B_{1}^{2}}{\lambda(m+n)}\log\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad A(\lambda):=4\lambda\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert^{2}.

The question is:
how to choose
\lambda
?

One strategy in learning theory is to
use the value
\lambda^{\ast}
which
balances

(
Lepskii 1991
;
Goldenshluger and Pereverzev 2000
;
Birgé 2001
;
Mathé 2006
;
De Vito et al. 2010
;
Mücke 2018
;
Blanchard et al. 2019
;
Lu et al. 2020
;
Zellinger et al. 2021
)

\displaystyle\eta\cdot S(m+n,\delta,\lambda^{\ast})=A(\lambda^{\ast})

(19)

for some
\eta\geq 1
ensuring that
\lambda^{\ast}
is in the admissible set of values as, e.g., specified by Eq. (
13
).
The value
\lambda^{\ast}
in Eq. (
19
) is achieved, due to the monotonicity of
S(m+n,\delta,\lambda)
(decreasing) and
A(\lambda)
(increasing, starting from
A(0)=0
).

The parameter choice
\lambda^{\ast}
is particularly interesting, as it minimizes the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) up to a constant factor and, as a consequence, allows to achieve error rates of optimal order; see Subsection
6.1
.

Proposition 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
4
be satisfied, and let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
Let further

\lambda^{\ast}
be the solution of Eq. (
19
) for
\eta:=\frac{256R^{2}}{42}\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert^{2}
, which we assume to be larger than
1
.
The there exists a quantity
C>0
not depending on
m,n,\delta
,
such that the minimizer
f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda^{\ast}}
of Eq. (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda^{\ast}})-B(f_{\mathcal{H}})\leq C(m+n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\log^{\frac{1}{2}}\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

(20)

with probability at least
1-2\delta
, for large enough sample size
m+n
, which depends on
\delta
.

Explicit bounds on the sample size
m+n
and the quantity
C
are given in Eq. (
36
) and Eq. (
38
), respectively.
The value for
\lambda^{\ast}
specified in Proposition
1
and Eq. (
35
) can be computed
a priori
.
That is, all information needed to achieve the error rate
(m+n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is available.
However, this situation changes in Subsection
3.2
when the regularity of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
is taken into account.

Remark 7
.

Together with Example
6
, our Proposition
1
provides (to the best of our knowledge) the first error rate for regularized density ratio estimation methods beyond KuLSIF.
At the same time, for
B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})=\left\lVert\beta-f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}
as used in KuLSIF, the error has been analyzed in
(
Kanamori et al. 2012
)
in terms of the so-called bracketing entropy
\gamma
of
\mathcal{H}
.
In our notation, this result tells us that, if
\gamma\in(0,2)
and
\beta\in\mathcal{H}
(so that
B(f_{\mathcal{H}})=0
), then, for
\epsilon
satisfying
1-\frac{2}{2+\gamma}<\epsilon<1
and
\lambda^{-1}=O\!\left(\min\{m,n\}^{1-\epsilon}\right)
, with probability

, it holds that

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})=O\!\left(\min\{m,n\}^{\epsilon-1}\right).

(21)

The rate in Eq. (
21
) is faster for smaller
\epsilon
and tends, for RKHSs with
\gamma\to 2
, to its optimum
\min\{m,n\}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, which is the same rate as given by our Proposition
1
.
For
m\approx n
as motivated by Assumption
1
, our result therefore recovers the state of the art
(
Kanamori et al. 2009
)
for KuLSIF and RKHS with bracketing entropy
\gamma\to 2
.
In the same case, the rate has been recently improved in
(
Gizewski et al. 2022
)
for regular
\beta
, which we discuss in the next Section

.

3.2
Fast Error Rate Requiring A Posteriori Parameter Choice

The following result is based on Lemma
5
and it takes into account the unknown regularity of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
by Assumption
5
.

Proposition 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
be satisfied for some

, and let

.
Let further

be a solution of Eq. (

) with

.
Then there exists a quantity
C>0
not depending on

, such that the minimizer

of Eq. (

) satisfies

, for large enough sample size

.

Remark 8
.

Together with Example
6
, Proposition
2
provides (the to the best of our knowledge first) error rates result for regularized density ratio estimation methods that takes into account both, the regularity of the density ratio

and the capacity of the space

.
As a result, even in the special case of KuLSIF with

To see this, consider the fastest error rate of
(

)
discovered for RKHS with bracketing entropy

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda})=O\!\left(\min\{m,n\}^{-\frac{2}{2+\gamma}}\right).

This rate is improved by

with

.

4
Balancing Principle using Approximation by Norm

4.1
Parameter Choice when the Norm is Known

The value
\lambda^{\ast}
in Proposition
2
cannot be computed directly as it depends on the regularity of
\beta
encoded in
r
; see Eq. (
40
).
One solution to this problem is to estimate
\lambda^{\ast}
with Lepskii’s
balancing principle

(
Lepskii 1991
;
Goldenshluger and Pereverzev 2000
;
Birgé 2001
;
Mathé 2006
;
De Vito et al. 2010
;
Lu et al. 2020
;
Zellinger et al. 2021
)
.
However, in general, no representation of
B(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda})-B(f_{\mathcal{H}})
as squared norm is available.
This prevents us from using the above referenced approaches directly, e.g., by using the norm estimates
(
De Vito et al. 2010
, Proposition 1)
or
(
Lu et al. 2020
, Proposition 4.1)
.

In the following, we propose a novel form of the balancing principle based on the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}
originating from the self-concordance-based upper bound in Eq. (
17
).
Since the estimation of
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}
requires some care, we postpone it to the next Subection
4.2
.

We start by defining a suitable discretization of increasing parameter candidates
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda^{\ast}\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{l}]
is in the range of admissible values (ensured by multiplication of
\eta
in Eq. (
19
)).
The balancing principle is then based on checking a necessary condition.
Namely, whenever
\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda^{\ast}
, Lemma
5
and the monotonicity of

S(m+n,\delta,\lambda):=\frac{414Q_{0}^{2}}{(m+n)\lambda^{1/\alpha}}\log\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad A(\lambda):=414L^{2}\lambda^{1+2r},

grant us the inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}+2\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}+2\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{i}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{j})+2A(\lambda_{j})+2S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{i})+2A(\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\leq 4S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{j})+4S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\leq 8S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{j})

\displaystyle\leq 8\eta\cdot S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{j}),

(25)

where we have used that
{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f)={\bf H}(f)+\lambda_{j}I\leq{\bf H}(f)+\lambda_{i}I={\bf H}_{\lambda_{i}}(f)
and
\eta
as defined in Proposition
2
.
Following the above reasoning, if the error bound in Lemma
5
is sharp, then the maximum
\lambda_{i}\in(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{l}
satisfying the above condition for all smaller
\lambda_{j},j\in\{1,\ldots,i-1\}
, can be expected to be close to
\lambda^{\ast}
.
This leads to the following estimate

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}:=\max\!\left\{\lambda_{i}:\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}\leq 8\eta\cdot S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{j}),j\in\{1,\ldots,i-1\}\right\}.

(26)

Importantly, Eq. (
25
) does not depend on the unknown regularity index
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
but only on the quantities
m,n,Q_{0},\alpha
which are either given or an upper bound can be easily estimated from data.
We may state the following preparatory result.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
be satisfied and let

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}:=\lambda_{0}\cdot\xi^{i},i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}

with
\xi>1
,
\lambda_{0}\leq\xi^{-l}\min\left\{B_{2}^{\ast},\left(2LR\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{-\frac{1}{r}},Q_{0}^{2\alpha}(B_{2}^{\ast})^{\alpha}(B_{1}^{\ast})^{-2\alpha}\right\}
,
l<\frac{e^{1296}}{4}-2
and
\delta\in[\frac{2}{e^{1296}},\frac{1}{4+2l}]
.
Then there exists a quantity
C>0
not depending on
m,n,\delta
, such that the minimizer
f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda_{+}}
of Eq. (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda_{+}})-B(f_{\mathcal{H}})\leq C(m+n)^{-\frac{2r\alpha+\alpha}{2r\alpha+\alpha+1}}

(27)

with probability at least
1-(4+2l)\delta
, for large enough sample size
m+n
depending on
\delta
.

Bounds on the sample size
m+n
and the quantity
C
are given in Eq. (
44
) and Eq. (
48
), respectively.
We note that Theorem
1
achieves the same error rate as Proposition
2
but uses the parameter choice
\lambda_{+}
which is a posteriori computable if we have access to the norm values
\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}\right\rVert_{{\bf H}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}^{2}
.
However, these values are not accessible, as we don’t have access to the measure
\rho
.
This issue is solved in the next Subsection
4.2
.

4.2
Parameter Choice with Estimated Norm

The following concentration result is proven in Subsection
6.4
and essentially combines analytical arguments for self-concordance losses
(
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2019
)
with concentration inequalities for Hermitian operators
(
Rudi and Rosasco 2017
)
.

Lemma 9
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
5
be satisfied, let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, denote the empirical Hessian by
\widehat{{\bf H}}(f):=\frac{1}{m+n}\sum_{z\in{\bf z}}\nabla^{2}\ell_{z}(f)
, and let us write
\mathbf{B}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{A}
iff
\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}
is positive semi-definite.
Whenever
\left\lVert{\bf H}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda})\right\rVert>0
and

\displaystyle m+n\geq\frac{16B_{2}^{\ast}}{\left\lVert{\bf H}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda})\right\rVert}\log\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right),

(28)

then, with probability at least
1-4\delta
,

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda})\preccurlyeq 6{\bf H}_{\lambda}(f_{\mathcal{H}})\preccurlyeq 48\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda}).

(29)

Let us denote by
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}
a geometric sequence with
\lambda_{i}:=\lambda_{0}\cdot\xi^{i},i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
as defined in Theorem
1
.
Then, Lemma
9
motivates the following assumption

Assumption 7
.

For the sequence
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{l}

there exists
b^{\ast}>0
with
b^{\ast}\leq\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}}\left\lVert{\bf H}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}})\right\rVert
.

Note that for strictly convex
loss functions, the Hessian is positive definite, and thus Assumption
7
is necessarily satisfied.

The same reasoning as done for Eq. (
25
) motivates, under Assumption
7
,
the estimate

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{BP}}:=\max\!\left\{\lambda_{i}:\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}\right\rVert_{\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}})}^{2}\leq 48\eta\cdot S(m+n,\delta,\lambda_{j}),j\in\{1,\ldots,i-1\}\right\}

(30)

with
\eta:=1296\log^{-1}(\frac{2}{\delta})
as defined in Proposition
2
.
Note that Eq. (
30
) uses only given data or information for which upper bounds are easily estimable.
Moreover, the following error rates result holds.

Theorem 2
.

Let all assumptions of Theorem
1
be satisfied for some
l<\frac{e^{1296}}{12}-1
and
\delta\in[\frac{2}{e^{1296}},\frac{1}{6+6l}]
.
Let further
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{l}
satisfy Assumption
7
.
Then there exists a quantity
C>0
not depending on
m,n,\delta
, such that the minimizer
f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{BP}}}
of Eq. (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle B(f_{{\bf z}}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{BP}}})-B(f_{\mathcal{H}})\leq C(m+n)^{-\frac{2r\alpha+\alpha}{2r\alpha+\alpha+1}}

(31)

with probability at least
1-(6+6l)\delta
, for large enough sample size
m+n
depending on
\delta
.

Exact bounds on the sample size
m+n
and the quantity
C
are given in Eq. (
57
) and Eq. (
61
), respectively.
Theorem
2
is the main result of this work.
For large enough sample sizes, it provides an a posteriori parameter choice
\lambda_{\mathrm{BP}}
that allows the minimizer
f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{BP}}}
of Eq. (
11
) to achieve an error rate that improves with the regularity of the true solution.

Remark 10
.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
2
gives the first proof of the adaptivity to the unknown target regularity, for a parameter choice strategy in density ratio estimation.
Even if similar adaptivity results hold for cross-validation in supervised learning
(
Caponnetto and Yao 2010
)
and the aggregation method in covariate shift domain adaptation
(
Gizewski et al. 2022
)
, their extension to density ratio estimation is not straight forward and an open future problem.
In the special case of square loss (SQ), the order of the error rate is optimal (see Remark
8
), and Eq. (
30
) thus provides, in this sense, adaptivity at optimal rate.

Figure 1:
The goal is to learn the density ratio
\beta=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q}
(black, solid).

To illustrate the behaviour of our approach, we rely on an example of
(

;

;

;

)
with data from two Gaussian distributions

.

We use two different density ratio estimation methods in Eq. (

.
In particular, we approximate a minimizer of Eq. (

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m+n}
of Eq. (

with reproducing kernel

of
\mathcal{H}
, as suggested by the representer theorem
(

Following our goal of choosing
\lambda
, we fix a geometric sequence
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{i}:=10^{i},i\in\{-3,\ldots,1\}
and compute

\displaystyle\lambda^{\circ}:=\max\!\left\{\lambda_{i}:\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{i}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda_{j}}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{j}})}\leq\frac{M}{\lambda_{j}(m+n)},j\in\{1,\ldots,i-1\}\right\}

(32)

for some
M\in\mathbb{R}
and the pessimistic choice
\alpha=1
in Assumption
6
.
The empirical norm in Eq. (
32
) can be computed, for two minimizers
f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{s}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m+n}\alpha_{i}k(x_{i},\cdot)
and
f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m+n}\beta_{i}k(x_{i},\cdot)
of Eq. (

Exp method

Let
\ell_{z}(f)=e^{-yf(x)}
for

Then, the reproducing property of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
with kernel

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{z}(f)=\nabla^{2}e^{-y\cdot f(x)}=\nabla^{2}e^{-y\cdot\langle k(x,\cdot),f\rangle}=e^{-y\cdot\langle k(x,\cdot),f\rangle}k(x,\cdot)k(x,\cdot).

For the minimizer
f_{\bf z}^{\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{m+n}\alpha_{j}k(x_{j},\cdot)
of Eq. (

(
Schölkopf et al. 2001

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda})=\frac{1}{m+n}\sum_{i=1}^{m+n}e^{-y_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{m+n}\alpha_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})}k(x_{i},\cdot)k(x_{i},\cdot)+\lambda I.

For

\displaystyle\left\lVert f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{s}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda_{t}}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}})}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda_{t}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}})(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{s}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}}),\widehat{{\bf H}}_{\lambda_{t}}^{\frac{1}{2}}(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}})(f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{s}}-f_{\bf z}^{\lambda_{t}})\right\rangle

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We proposed error bounds for a large class of kernel methods for density ratio estimation for which no error bounds were known before (to the best of our knowledge).
The class consists of methods which minimize a regularized Bregman divergence over a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.
We also proposed a Lepskii type principle for choosing the regularization parameter.
The principle minimizes the error bounds without knowledge of the unknown regularity of the density ratio.
As a consequence it achieves a minimax optimal error rate in the special case of square loss.
The key proof technique for both, the error rates and the adaptive parameter choice, is a local quadratic approximation of the Bregman divergence, which is equivalent to an expected risk for a proper composite self-concordant loss function.
The minimax optimality of our error bounds for non-square losses is an open problem.
