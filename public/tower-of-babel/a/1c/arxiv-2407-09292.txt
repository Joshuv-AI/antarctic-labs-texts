Counterfactual Explainable Incremental Prompt Attack Analysis on Large Language Models
Dong shu, Mingyu Jin, Tianle Chen, Chong Zhang, Yongfeng Zhang

Abstract
Content warning: This paper contains unfiltered content generated by LLMs that may be offensive to readers. This study sheds light on the imperative need to bolster safety and privacy measures in large language models (LLMs), such as GPT-4 and LLaMA-2, by identifying and mitigating their vulnerabilities through explainable analysis of prompt attacks. We propose Counterfactual Explainable Incremental Prompt Attack (CEIPA) , a novel technique where we guide prompts in a specific manner to quantitatively measure attack effectiveness and explore the embedded defense mechanisms in these models. Our approach is distinctive for its capacity to elucidate the reasons behind the generation of harmful responses by LLMs through an incremental counterfactual methodology. By organizing the prompt modification process into four incremental levels—word, sentence, character, and a combination of character/word—we facilitate a thorough examination of the susceptibilities inherent to LLMs. The findings from our study not only provide counterfactual explanation insight but also demonstrate that our framework significantly enhances the effectiveness of attack prompts.

Related Work

2.1
LLMs’ Application and Safety Hazard

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4
[
18
]
into applications demonstrates their capacity to address a wide range of queries. These models, including specialized ones like Bing Chat, generate responses based on pre-trained text data and, in some cases, real-time web searches. The inherent flexibility of LLMs, however, means that their outputs are unpredictable, posing challenges in controlling specific responses and preventing undesirable behavior
[
23

[

2.2
Prompt Injection

Prompt injection involves altering a natural language processing model’s input prompts to sway its output, categorized into direct and indirect types
[
17
]
.

Direct prompt injection is a significant risk for Large Language Models (LLMs), as it manipulates inputs to provoke non-compliant responses, undermining ethical guidelines. This method involves adding misleading commands to prompts, tricking the model into ignoring safety protocols, a strategy highlighted by Greshake
[
10
]
and examined by Perez et al.
[
20
]
, leading to security breaches.

Indirect prompt injection, conversely, targets the model’s data sources. Malicious actors might pollute the network with false information, leading the model to share these unreliable sources with users, posing threats to privacy and security, as discussed by Sarkar
[
22
]
. To counteract this, some developers restrict their models to trusted websites.

2.3.2
System Prompts Extraction

System Prompts Extraction is an attack to extract information from system prompts. The attacker can extract the information of the system by adding special characters, symbols, or words to the system prompt to induce the system to generate specific output. Examples include the use of placeholders during the attack and EL expressions that steal potential website and server system-level variables and commands

2.3.3
Prompt Hijacking

Prompt Hijacking is an attack in which an attacker manipulates LLMs to generate specific outputs by using elaborate hints. An attacker can induce LLMs to generate malicious or false messages by adding special characters, symbols, or words to the prompts. Such as inducing the generation of fake news, malicious code, phishing emails, etc. to disrupt the main tasks of LLMs

20

2.4
Defense Strategy

Defense strategies against prompt injection in Large Language Models (LLMs) are mainly categorized into Prevention-based and Detection-based Defenses.

Prevention-based Defenses proactively process inputs to thwart prompt injections. Techniques like sandwich prevention, which adds instructional prompts post-user input, and data prompt isolation, which separates data and instruction prompts, alongside rewriting user prompts (instructional prevention), help prevent targeted attacks
[
17
,
12
]
.

Detection-based Defenses, on the other hand, focus on identifying and addressing risks within prompts and responses. Methods include response-based detection, which screens outputs for inappropriate content, and LLM-based detection, employing LLMs themselves to evaluate prompts for potential threats
[
3
,
2
]
. These strategies are crucial for safeguarding against common attacks, though challenges remain in dealing with unconventional attack patterns.

Methodology

3.1
Problem Settings

We propose a Counterfactual Explainable Incremental Prompt Attack (CEIPA), a well-structured approach to evaluate the robustness of LLMs. We utilize four levels of intervention: word, character, sentence, and a combination of word/character levels, each serving a distinct purpose in our analysis. We start with a relatively weak attack prompt that cannot initially mislead the LLM. Then, systematically, we modify this prompt across the four levels in sequence. For each level, we incrementally adjust the attacking prompt based on the previous one. Let
P_{i}
represent the
i
-th attacking prompt, and we define
P_{i}
as a function of the previous prompt
P_{i-1}
, i.e.:

P_{i}=f_{w,s,c,w/c}(P_{i-1})

(1)

Here,
f
represents the type of intervention depending on the level being used. The core of our methodology involves generating counterfactual explanations by creating alternative scenarios through incremental input changes. In order to understand the limitations and vulnerabilities of LLMs’ language processing, we collect data during the attack to observe their reaction to different cues. The goal during the analysis of the results is to identify points that break through the LLM defence by progressively increasing the complexity of the cue.

3.1.2
Sentence Level Incremental Attack

In the sentence-level iteration of our Incremental Prompt Injection Attack, we employ a Summarize-and-Rewrite (S&R) technique to succinctly summarize each sub-sentence from the attack prompt, enhancing our capability to effectively mutate these individual sub-sentences
[
11
]
. The modification process also involves comparing the mutated sub-sentence with its original form, employing the BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) scoring system
[
19
]
, along with the judgment from a LLM . This approach focuses on manipulating individual sentences extracted from a prompt, identified by their separation with a period (“.”).

P=\{s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{n}\}

(6)

We summarize each sentence
s_{i}
based on LLM:

\text{Sentence Summary}_{i}=\text{LLM}(s_{i}\mid P)

(7)

Then, in each iteration, we randomly select a sentence
s_{i}
and employ an LLM to generate a similar sentence
s^{\prime}_{i}
based on
s_{i}

s^{\prime}_{i}=\text{LLM}(s_{i},\text{Sentence Summary}_{i}\mid P)

(8)

The mutated sentence

P^{\prime}

P^{\prime}=\{s_{1},s_{2},...,s^{\prime}_{i},...,s_{n}\}

(9)

The final step involves utilizing the BLEU score to evaluate the similarity between the original and transformed sentences, which is to make sure the original prompt and new prompt are similar. Additionally, the similarity is confirmed by an assessment from a large language model, such as GPT-4. A prompt is deemed ready for output only when it meets both criteria: a high BLEU similarity and a positive assessment from an LLM. This dual-validation approach ensures a thorough and effective evaluation of the language model’s robustness against sophisticated, prompt-based attacks.

3.1.3
Char Level Incremental Attack

We introduce the Char Transformer technique, which leverages the symbolic nature of language by introducing spelling errors into key terms, effectively transforming them into “unknowns” – terms not recognized by standard dictionaries
[
8
,
14
]
. These altered words are then mapped to “unknown” embedding vectors, testing the model’s ability to process unexpected inputs.

Tactics to generate these spelling errors include:

1. Insert: We introduce a space within the word. This method exploits the fact that English words are typically separated by spaces, allowing us to deceive classifiers without significantly changing the word’s appearance.

2. Delete: A random character, excluding the first and last, is removed from the word. It subtly changes the word while preserving most of its original structure, maintaining recognizably.

3. Swap: We switch the positions of two internal characters in the word, avoiding the first and last letters. This approach mimics common typing errors, offering a realistic yet simple alteration.

4. Substitute-Character (Sub-C): Characters are replaced with visually similar ones (e.g., “o” with “O”, “l” with “1”, “a” with “@”), or with adjacent characters on the keyboard (e.g., “m” with “n”). This method takes advantage of visual similarities between characters to create deceptive yet recognizable changes.

These methods are designed to maintain a minimal edit distance (Levenshtein distance), ensuring that the original and modified words are closely related visually or morphologically.

In each round of the char level attack, we randomly select one of these methods and apply it to the most important word (identified in Section 3.1) in the original attack prompt. To avoid redundancy, each “bug word” generated is recorded.

3.1.4
Word/Char Level Incremental Attack

In the Word/Char Level Incremental Attack, we synergize the Word Level with the Char Level. This is achieved through the introduction of a “Substitute-Word (Sub-W)” strategy, which generates synonyms as per the Word Level approach, combined with the four techniques utilized in the Char Level attack (examples in Appendix
D
Table
4
). The objective of this combined method is to subtly modify both words and characters, ensuring that their semantic and syntactic integrity is preserved.

In each round, we randomly select one of the five methods (including the newly introduced Sub-W technique) to apply to the most important word in the original attack prompt, and each ‘bug word’ generated during this process is recorded to avoid repetitive alterations.

Evaluation Metrics

To assess the efficacy of our testing methodology, we employ the following metrics:

•

Clean Attack Success Rate:
The Clean Attack Success Rate (CLEAN ASR) calculates the number of attack prompts from the original dataset that can successfully attack LLMs on the first attempt, without the need for iteration. This evaluation assigns a binary outcome to each prompt: 0 for an unsuccessful attack and 1 for a successful one.

•

Attack Success Rate:
The Attack Success Rate (ASR) calculates the number of attack prompts achieving success through iterative attempts, up to a maximum of 50 iterations in our analysis.

•

Average Number of Rounds:
The Average Number of Rounds (NOR) depicts the mean iterations required for a prompt’s successful attack

Our graphical analysis features two types of visual representations for clarity and precision. Bar graphs are utilized to present the cumulative ASR following the conclusion of iterations, with the caveat that only the first success of each attack prompt is taken into account. Concurrently, line graphs are employed to trace the trajectory of ASR over successive iterations.

Counterfactual Explanation

To gain a thorough understanding of the shift from failed to successful attack prompts, we undertook an in-depth analysis utilizing t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) graphs for each task. Considering the extensive size of our dataset and to enhance visualization clarity, we strategically selected a random subset of 10% of the transition pairs from each level for plotting in the graph.

Using the t-SNE graph for the Jailbreak task as an illustrative example, as depicted in Figure
6(a)
, we unveil compelling insights into the sentence-level dynamics inherent in the Jailbreak task. Our t-SNE analysis distinctly highlights the separation between failed attack prompts (represented in blue) and successful ones (in red) within the t-SNE space. Additionally, it is observed that the number of dots at the sentence level surpasses that of other levels, indicating a higher frequency of transition points during sentence-level incremental attacks. This observation aligns with the more intricate patterns seen in the sentence-level line graph compared to other levels. These findings prompt further investigation, as they hint at specific factors that might influence the effectiveness of attack prompts in this scenario.

Based on the results in Figure
5
, it is evident that in the context of same attack task, the peak of success rates at the sentence-level often precedes that of the word-level. In other words, achieving a higher success rate at the sentence-level may require fewer iterations compared to the word-level. This observation instigated an inquiry into the perceptual scope of the model. It is postulated that the model’s decision-making process leans more heavily on sentence-level contextual cues rather than individual words. Consequently, although modifications at the word level may influence local semantics or syntax, they may necessitate more iterations to capture the model’s attention effectively. Hence, the broader context within sentences may exert a more significant influence on the model’s decision-making due to its encompassment of a wider array of informational and semantic cues.

Beyond determining which mutation level enhances effectiveness the most, our analysis reveals that the char/word level exhibits the least improvement across the three tasks, with its performance even falling below the baseline after a number of iterations. This outcome suggests that the char/word level mutations can, after several iterations, act as a defensive mechanism by diluting the toxicity of the attack prompts. This insight could illuminate new strategies for securing LLMs. When an attack prompt targets an LLM, developers might consider implementing a specific number of char/word level mutations as a countermeasure to mitigate the attack’s effectiveness.

Upon closer examination of the transition dataset, shown in Table
3
, we uncovered an intriguing pattern. When seeking the most influential word within the attack prompts at the word level, our analysis consistently identified verbs and adjectives as pivotal elements. This finding implies that the selection of specific verbs and adjectives may play a crucial role in the transition from unsuccessful to successful attack prompts. This insight invites deeper investigation into the linguistic and contextual aspects of these words, as they appear to significantly impact the model’s responses and decision-making processes.

The t-SNE graphs and these initial observations provide valuable starting points for further research and refinement of attack prompt strategies. More t-SNE graphs are shown in Figure

Conclusion

This study enhances our comprehension of vulnerabilities within GPT, LLaMA-2, Gemma, Guanaco by introducing the Counterfactual Explainable Incremental Prompt Attack (CEIPA). Our methodology elucidates the impact of nuanced modifications on model outputs through the incremental counterfactual mutation of prompts across four distinct levels. Key findings from our research include:

•

Incremental prompt mutations at the sentence and word levels are notably effective, bolstering the robustness of prompts against common injection attacks. This underscores the utility of precise, strategic alterations in enhancing attack methodologies.

•

Our analysis provides critical insights into the observed experimental results, including the nuanced role of char/word level mutations. These insights suggest that certain mutations can serve defensive purposes, potentially diluting the efficacy of attack prompts.

•

We offer a wealth of counterfactual explanations and detailed examples that further elaborate on our findings, providing a comprehensive resource for understanding the subtleties of our approach.

7
Limitation

To enhance the scope and efficiency of our current strategies, which are primarily focused on a selected group of large language models, we should broaden our approach to include more diverse tasks, such as the acquisition of private information. Additionally, it’s crucial to seek more effective methods to optimize our processes, exploring alternatives that surpass the capabilities of tools like BERT-Attack. This expansion and refinement of our techniques will further improve the operational effectiveness.
