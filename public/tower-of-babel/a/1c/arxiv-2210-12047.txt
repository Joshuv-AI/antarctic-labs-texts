Holomorphic Floer theory and the Fueter equation
Aleksander Doan, Semon Rezchikov

Abstract
We outline a proposal for a 2 2 -category Fuet M \mathrm{Fuet}_{M} associated to a hyperkähler manifold M M , which categorifies the subcategory of the Fukaya category of M M generated by complex Lagrangian submanifolds. Morphisms in this 2 2 -category are formally the Fukaya–Seidel categories of holomorphic symplectic action functionals. As such, Fuet M \mathrm{Fuet}_{M} is based on counting maps [ 0 , 1 ] × ℝ 2 → M [0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M satisfying the Fueter equation with boundary values on complex Lagrangians. We make the first step towards constructing this category by establishing some basic analytic results about Fueter maps, such as an energy bound and a maximum principle. When M = T ∗ ​ X M=T^{*}X is the cotangent bundle of a Kähler manifold X X and L 0 , L 1 L_{0},L_{1} are the zero section and the graph of the differential of a holomorphic function F : X → ℂ F\colon X\to\mathbb{C} , we prove that Fueter maps correspond to the complex gradient trajectories of F F in X X , which relates our proposal to the Fukaya–Seidel category of F F . This is a complexification of Floer’s theorem on pseudo-holomorphic strips in cotangent bundles. Throughout the paper, we suggest problems and research directions for analysts and geometers that may be interested in the subject.

This paper studies a complexification of Lagrangian Floer homology. Like its real counterpart, this theory arises from a
\sigma

\begin{gathered}U\colon[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M,\\

As such, many analytical aspects of the Fueter equation are tractable and can be developed systematically by analogy with pseudo-holomorphic maps, although some core questions—most importantly, in compactness theory—remain challenging

On the other hand, due to the kinship of the Fueter equation with the pseudo-holomorphic map equation, it conjecturally gives rise to a host of algebraic structures generalizing the well-known structures appearing in symplectic topology, with rich connections to low-dimensional topology, representation theory, and mathematical physics. The goal of this paper is to explore some of these ideas and connections.

The Fueter equation has been previously studied from many different perspectives. We list some of them below:

•

The linear variant of the Fueter equation, with

•

Solutions to the Fueter equation whose domain

]

•

The Fueter equation arises naturally as a degeneration limit of various equations in gauge theory, such as the instanton equation on Riemannian manifolds with special holonomy

•

Maps satisfying the Fueter equation appear as a result of applying supersymmetric localization to the physical 3d

Our motivations are closely aligned with the last point above, although we learned this physical perspective after thinking about the results of this paper and much of the algebraic structure indicated below from a purely mathematical viewpoint. In symplectic geometry, pseudo-convex functions are often used to confine pseudo-holomorphic curves. In particular, due to the the Weinstein tubular neighborhood theorem, Floer’s result on pseudo-holomorphic strips in cotangent bundles gives a description of local pseudo-holomorphic strips between two Lagrangians in any symplectic manifold in terms of Morse-theoretic, or topological data, and thus gives the first approximation to any invariant in Lagrangian Floer theory. We expect that the convexity theory discussed in this paper can be used similarly to compute the local Fueter trajectories between complex Lagrangians in a general hyperkähler manifold.

This completes the summary of the results of this paper. These analytical results are motivated by a rich set of expectations regarding the algebraic and categorical structures that should arise from the study of the Fueter equation. 2

2.1
The Fueter
2
-category

Let
M
be a hyperkähler manifold with complex structures
I,J,K
. We consider
M
as a complex manifold with the distinguished complex structure
I
. The remaining part of the hyperkähler structure is encoded in the Riemannian metric and holomorphic symplectic form

\Omega=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}

where
\omega_{J}
,
\omega_{K}
are the Kähler forms corresponding to
J
,
K
. Fix also a background angle
\hat{\theta}\in[0,2\pi)
; the final structure will not depend on that choice up to isomorphism. To a very initial approximation, we expect to define a
2
-category
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}
with the following features.

•

The objects of
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}
are complex Lagrangians
L\subset M
, i.e. submanifolds which are
I
-holomorphic and Lagrangian with respect to
\Omega
and thus Lagrangian with respect to all real symplectic forms

\omega_{\theta}=\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega)=(\cos\theta)\omega_{J}+(\sin\theta)\omega_{K}\quad\text{for }\theta\in[0,2\pi).

•

A
hom-category

\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})
is, formally, the Fukaya–Seidel category of the
holomorphic symplectic action functional

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
, defined in (
1.5
), on the space of paths from
L_{0}
to
L_{1}

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(M;L_{0},L_{1})=\{\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M\ |\ \gamma(0)\in L_{0},\ \gamma(1)\in L_{1}\}.

Strictly speaking, what is well-defined is the differential of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
, which is a closed holomorphic
(1,0)
-form on
\mathcal{P}
given by

{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma)v=\int_{0}^{1}\Omega\left(v(\tau),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\right){\mathrm{d}}\tau

(2.1)

for
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
and
v\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{P}=\Gamma([0,1],\gamma^{*}TM)
, with
\tau
denoting the coordinate on
[0,1]
.
As usual
[
HS95
]
, this one-form may or may not be the differential of globally defined holomorphic function. We can pass to the covering space
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathcal{P}
with deck transformation group given by

\frac{\pi_{2}(M,L_{0}\cup L_{1})}{\ker(\alpha\mapsto\langle[\Omega],\alpha\rangle)}

so that the pullback of
{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
is the differential of a function
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
. The simplest setting is the
exact
setting, in which the deck transformation group is trivial. In this setting, we require that there is a holomorphic one-form
\Lambda
such that
\Omega={\mathrm{d}}\Lambda
, and
\Lambda|_{L_{i}}={\mathrm{d}}H_{i}
for some holomorphic functions
H_{i}:L_{i}\to\mathbb{C}
. In that case,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\Lambda-H_{1}(\gamma(1))+H_{0}(\gamma(0)).

(2.2)

The usual definition of the Fukaya–Seidel category using Lefschetz thimbles is not available in this infinite-dimensional setting. Instead, here
\mathrm{FS}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})
is meant to be defined via an infinite-dimensional version of the complex Morse theory model of the Fukaya-Seidel category, which we review in the next section.

•

In particular, the objects of
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})
, i.e. the
1-morphisms
of
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}
, are the critical points of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
, which are in bijection with the constant paths mapping to the intersection
L_{0}\cap L_{1}
. When
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
intersect transversely, these critical points are nondegenerate, which is a requirement to make sense of the Fukaya–Seidel category. Heuristically, these objects correspond to infinite-dimensional Lefschetz thimbles of the holomorphic Morse function
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

•

The morphism cochain complex
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})(p_{-},p_{+})
, for
p_{-},q_{+}\in L_{0}\cap L_{1}
is generated by the gradient trajectories of
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})
, where

\theta=\arg(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{-})-\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(p_{+}))

As in Lagrangian Floer theory, they correspond to
J_{\theta+\pi}
-holomorphic strips, or
J_{\theta}
-antiholomorphic strips, with
J_{\theta}
denoting the rotated complex structure (
1.6
). Explicitly, the cochain complex
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})(p_{-},p_{+})
is freely generated over
\mathbb{Z}
by the solutions to

\displaystyle u:\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to M,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+J_{\theta+\pi}(u)\partial_{\tau}u=0,

(2.3)

with boundary condition
u(t,i)\in L_{i}
for
i=0,1
and asymptotic boundary conditions
\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}u(t,\tau)=p_{\pm}
. As usual, we only consider solutions with finite Floer energy, which implies exponential converges with derivatives a
t\to\pm\infty
.

•

The differential on
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})(p_{-},p_{+})
counts solutions to the complex gradient flow equation for
e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
. This is an equation for maps

, which we interpret as maps

. As we will see in

is formally a complex gradient trajectory if it solves the Fueter equation

\begin{gathered}U:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M,\\
I(U)\partial_{\tau}U+J_{\theta}(U)\partial_{s}U+K_{\theta}(U)\partial_{t}U=0,\\
\end{gathered}

(2.4)

and boundary conditions

\displaystyle u(0,s,t)\in L_{0},\quad u(1,s,t)\in L_{1},

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}U(\tau,s,t)=u_{\pm}(t,\tau),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}u(\tau,s,t)=p_{\pm},

where again the convergence is assumed to be exponential. Here

is as in (

) and

, and

are solutions to (

for the moduli space of solutions to (

for the moduli space of solutions to (

{\mathrm{d}}u_{-}=\sum_{u_{+}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(p_{-},p_{+})}\#\mathcal{M}_{3}(u_{-},u_{+})u_{+},

(2.5)

where

defined by means of index theory.

•

2.2
Complex Morse theory

Fukaya–Seidel category.

Let
F\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic Morse function on a Kähler manifold. We assume that
X
is an exact symplectic manifold with a pseudo-convex end, and that
F
has a controlled behavior at infinity. For example,
X
could be a Stein manifold with the growth of
F
controlled by an exhausting plurisubharmonic function. Seidel
[
Sei08
]
defined an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
, the Fukaya–Seidel category of the pair
(X,F)
. This category enlarges the Fukaya category of closed Lagrangian submanifolds of
X
by adding in certain non-compact Lagrangians: the
Lefschetz thimbles

L_{p}
associated to critical points
p
of
F
. Up to exact Lagrangian isotopy, the thimbles are given by the unstable manifolds of the critical points of
F
for the gradient flow of
\mathrm{Re}(F)
. It is a remarkable insight of Seidel that this enlargement of the Fukaya category of
X
is in fact
simpler
than the original category, in the sense that it can be computed entirely from the Floer cohomology of the thimbles themselves
[
Sei01
]
.

Figure 4:
The Fukaya-Seidel category. Objects are closed Lagrangians and non-compact Lagrangians with prescribed asymptotic behavior. All objects can be computed from the distinguished objects corresponding to the Lefschetz thimbles.

Morse theory construction.

It has been proposed, simultaneously in the physics
[
GMW15a
]
and mathematics
[
Hay15
]
literature, to push this observation of Seidel further in order to construct an alternative model of
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
, which we call the
complex Morse theory model
, that never refers to any Lagrangian submanifolds of
X
. The motivation for this problem is the study of the Fukaya–Seidel categories of infinite-dimensional holomorphic functionals, especially the holomorphic Chern–Simons functional
[
Wit10
]
.

The basic problem with applying any of the usual models of the Fukaya–Seidel category to the infinite-dimensional setting is that working with infinite-dimensional Lagrangian submanifolds as boundary conditions for certain partial differential equations is typically not amenable to standard analytic methods. Instead, in the complex Morse theory model, one is expected to define the category
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
in terms of the gradient flows of
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}F)
and the solutions to the
complex gradient equation

\begin{gathered}u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to X,\\
\partial_{s}u+I(u)\partial_{t}u=\nabla\mathrm{Im}(e^{-i\theta}F)(u),\end{gathered}

(2.6)

where
(s,t)
are the coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
I
is the complex structure on
X
, and we are interested in solutions which converge exponentially to the real gradient trajectories of
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}F)
as
s\to\pm\infty
. Studying the complex gradient equation when
X
is a certain space of connections then reproduces previous elliptic differential equations of interest, such as the Kapustin–Witten equation
[
Hay15
]
.

The basic idea of the complex Morse theory model is that Lefschetz thimbles for
F
are in bijection with the critical points of
F
. To define the thimble
L_{p}
associated to a critical point
p\in\mathrm{Crit}(W)
, one fixes a
background angle

\hat{\theta}\in S^{1}
which is not the difference of arguments of any pair of critical values of
F
. For simplicity, we will take
\hat{\theta}=\pi
. One then defines the thimble
L_{p}
to be the unstable manifold of the gradient flow of
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)
. As such, a pair of Lefschetz thimbles
(L_{p},L_{q})
will not intersect, as each thimble will project to parallel lines in the
F
plane. Instead, one defines the intersections of
L_{p}
and
L_{q}
by rotating the angle at which
L_{p}
flows out of
p
slightly, or alternatively, flowing
L_{p}
by the the Hamiltonian flow of a function which is zero on the interior of
X
and agrees with
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)
on the ends of
X
. As usual, in the Fukaya category these intersections are the generators of the cochain complex
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)(L_{p},L_{q})
.

Figure 5:

\mathrm{FS}(X,F)

In the complex Morse theory model, one requires a definition this complex which does not rely on intersecting Lagrangian submanifolds. To accomplish this, one uses the fact that given two critical points
p,q
, with no critical points
r
such that

\mathrm{Im}(F(p))<\mathrm{Im}(F(r))<\mathrm{Im}(F(q)),

the intersection points between the Lefschetz thimbles
L_{p},L_{q}
of
p
and
q
are in bijection with the gradient flow lines of
-\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}F)
from
p
to

, where
\theta=\arg(F(p)-F(q))
; one sets these to be the generators of
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)(p,q)
in the complex Morse theory model.

.

The picture to keep in mind is when
F(p)
and

are on a vertical line in

above

. In that case, the gradient flow of

agrees with the Hamiltonian flow of
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)

In the Fukaya–Seidel category, the differential on
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)(L_{p_{-}},L_{p_{+}})
is given by counting holomorphic strips. These intersections are the critical points of the symplectic action functional
\mathcal{A}_{L_{p_{-}},L_{p_{+}}}:\mathcal{P}_{L_{p_{-}},L_{p_{+}}}\to\mathbb{R}
on the space of paths from
L_{p_{-}}
to
L_{p_{+}}

is holomorphic, the gradient flows of
-\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}F)
from
{p_{-}}
to
{p_{+}}
also Hamiltonian flows of
-\mathrm{Im}(e^{-i\theta}F)
. Thus, instead of the space of paths

from
L_{p_{-}}
to

with exponential decay condition near the critical points:

\mathcal{P}_{p_{-},p_{+}}=\{\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to X;\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\gamma(t)=p_{\pm}\text{ exponentially}\}

(2.7)

and defines the symplectic action functional
\mathcal{A}_{p_{-},p_{+}}
perturbed by this Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{A}_{p_{-},p_{+}}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\lambda+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Im}(e^{-i\theta}(F(\gamma(t))-F(p_{\pm}))){\mathrm{d}}t.

where

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}u(s,t)=p_{\pm},\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}=\gamma_{\pm}

where

are gradient trajectories of
-\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta F})
from

to

.

\mathrm{FS}(X,F)(p_{-},p_{+})

for (

Beyond this, the existing approaches become less precise. The essential challenge is that while there is a natural bijection between the gradient flow lines from
p_{-}
to
p_{+}
and the intersection of the corresponding thimbles
(L_{p_{-}},L_{p_{+}})
when there are no critical values in between the images of the thimbles, this identification fails for general pairs of critical points. Instead, the number of gradient trajectories from
p_{-}
to
p_{+}
is in bijection with the intersection points of a pair of
L_{p_{-}}
and a different Lagrangian
\tilde{L}_{p_{+}}
, which is related to
\tilde{L}_{p_{+}}
by a series of

; see
Figure 8
. Unfortunately, this Lagrangian
\tilde{L}_{p_{+}}
depends on the choice of the background angle
\hat{\theta}
in a strong sense: these Lagrangians are not isomorphic in
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
as one varies
\hat{\theta}
. Thus, the naive definition of the morphisms in the complex Morse theory model cannot be correct, as this precludes any natural map from the complex Morse theory model to the usual model of the Fukaya–Seidel category, which must send the critical points to well-defined objects of
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
.

There are two proposed approaches to resolve this problem, which are schematically compared in

. The first, proposed by Haydys
[

suggests to count broken Morse trajectories and broken complex gradient trajectories which first flow out from
p_{-}
to a point with

very large and then back to
p_{-}
, in order to define morphisms in the complex Morse theory model. As such, these have are always in bijection with the intersection points of the corresponding thimbles. This approach has recently been developed further by
[

]
, and shown to produce a category equivalent to the Fukaya–Seidel category. Crucially, the combinatorics of the
A_{\infty}
operations are the familiar ones from the Fukaya category. However, this approach has the downside of introducing many nontrivial perturbations to the complex and real gradient flow equation, which should make computations more difficult, especially when applying the complex Morse theory model to the infinite-dimensional setting; see
Section B.1
.

The work of Gaiotto–Moore–Witten
[
GMW15a
]
takes a different approach, and provides a combinatorial formula for defining an
A_{\infty}
-category in terms of standard, straight-line gradient trajectories between critical points together with solutions to the unperturbed complex gradient flow equation (
2.6
). The price of this advantageous geometric setup is a different proposed compactification of solutions to the complex gradient flow equation, which is not based on
A_{\infty}
combinatorics but instead produces the
A_{\infty}
category
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
via a highly involved process. A description of this formula in more traditional mathematical language was given in
[
KKS16
]
.

Hochschild homology.

There is a natural way to produce a vector space from an
A_{\infty}
-category
\mathcal{F}
, namely, its
Hochschild homology

HH_{*}(\mathcal{F})
. We will not go into this construction in detail, except to say that there is an explicit chain complex computing
HH_{*}(\mathcal{F})
written in terms of the
A_{\infty}
-operations on
\mathcal{F}
called the
cyclic bar complex
, and that when
\mathcal{F}
is a Fukaya category of a symplectic manifold
X
, there is an
open-closed map

[
Sei06
]

\mathcal{OC}:HH_{*}(\mathcal{F})\to HF^{*}(X,H)

(2.11)

for some appropriate Hamiltonian
H:X\to\mathbb{R}
, which depends on the variant of the Fukaya category being considered. The Hamiltonian Floer cohomology group
HF^{*}(X,H)
is object assigned to a circle by the open-closed field theory
Appendix A
) which assigns
\mathcal{F}(L_{0},L_{1})
to the interval with endpoints labeled by
(L_{0},L_{1})
, and the open-closed map is a chain-level version of all the cobordism maps associated to genus-zero cobordisms with one circle boundary and many interval boundaries. In many cases, including the case of the Fukaya–Seidel category, the open-closed map is known to be an isomorphism
[
Abo10
,
GPS19
]
.

Recall that to define the thimbles
L_{p}
generating the Fukaya–Seidel category, we had to choose a background angle
\hat{\theta}
. Denote by
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
the category defined for
\hat{\theta}
. The appropriate Hamiltonian to take for
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
is
H=-\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)
, or rather a small real rescaling of this Hamiltonian. As we take this Hamiltonian to zero (on some compact subdomain of
X
containing all critical points, which arises in the technical setup for the Fukaya–Seidel category),
HF^{*}(X,H)
is identified with the Morse homology group
HM_{*}(-\mathrm{Re}^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)
. This, in turn, agrees with the homology of
X
relative to the subset
\{x\in X|-\mathrm{Re}^{-i\hat{\theta}}F(x)\ll 0)
. For the conventional choice
\hat{\theta}=\pi
, this is simply
H_{*}(X,\{\mathrm{Re}(F)\gg 0\})
.

In the construction of
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
of
F
, which a priori depends on the choice of
\hat{\theta}
, one uses
\hat{\theta}
to define an ordering
p_{1}<\ldots<p_{r}
on the critical points
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
of
F
. This is used to define first the
directed Fukaya–Seidel category

\mathrm{FS}^{\to}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
, which only has morphisms from
L_{p_{j}}
to
L_{p_{k}}
if
j\leq k
, with the only endomorphism of
L_{p}
being the identity map. The full category
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
is then the category of
twisted complexes
on
\mathrm{FS}^{\to}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
. By algebraic properties of Hochschild homology, this implies that
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
has the same Hochschild homology as
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}^{\to}(X,F)
, which, because of the directedness, is easily seen to be a vector space freely generated by
\{L_{p_{1}},\ldots,L_{p_{r}}\}
. Geometrically, these cycles give a basis for the relative homology group
H_{*}(X,\{\mathrm{Re}(F)\gg 0\})
(with corresponding modification when
\hat{\theta}\neq\pi
). When
F
is a Morsification of a non-Morse holomorphic function
F_{0}
with a single singular fiber, this would be the group of
vanishing cycles
of the singular fiber.

Background angle and monodromy.

As we see in the description above, the Fukaya–Seidel category
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
a priori depends on the choice of an angle
\hat{\theta}
, and the directed subcategory
\mathrm{FS}^{\to}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
manifestly depends on
\hat{\theta}
because
\hat{\theta}
determines the ordering on the critical points.

However, using the topological interpretation of the Hochschild homology group
HH_{*}(\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F))
as
H_{*}(X,\{\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)\gg 0\})
, one sees that these groups form a local system of groups over the space of
\hat{\theta}\in S^{1}
. On the topological level, the dependence of the categories on
\hat{\theta}
corresponds to the fact that the thimbles
L_{p_{i}}
, which implicitly depend on
\hat{\theta}
, do not define a trivialization of the local system of relative homology groups. Now, as
\hat{\theta}
varies through angles avoiding the exceptional values

\hat{\theta}^{*}=\arg(F(p))-\arg(F(q))

for pairs
p,q\in\mathrm{Crit}(F)
, the ordering on the critical points does not change, the thimbles
L_{p}
stays the same up to exact Lagrangian isotopy, and the bases of
HH_{*}(\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F))
given by the open-closed map form a trivialization of the local system.

At the exceptional angles, a theorem of Seidel
[
Sei01
]
tell us that the difference between the directed subcategories
\mathrm{FS}^{\to}_{\hat{\theta}^{*}-\epsilon}(X,F)
and
\mathrm{FS}^{\to}_{\hat{\theta}^{*}+\epsilon}(X,F)
for a small
\epsilon>0
is given by an algebraic operation called a
mutation
, inducing an equivalence

F_{\hat{\theta}^{*}}:\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}^{*}-\epsilon}(X,F)\to\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}^{*}+\epsilon}(X,F).

(2.12)

Thus, in the end, the full Fukaya–Seidel category
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
up to equivalence does not depend on the choice of background angle
\hat{\theta}
. However, it is more precise to think of the categories
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
as forming a
local system of categories
over
S^{1}
. Indeed, the content of Picard–Lefschetz theory is that the bases of
H_{*}(X,\{\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}F)\gg 0\})
given by the thimbles
L_{p_{i}}
are related at the exceptional angles
\hat{\theta}^{*}
by an elementary integral change-of-basis matrix
\tau_{\hat{\theta}^{*}}
referred to as a Picard–Lefschetz transformation.
It turns out that the equivalences
F_{\hat{\theta}^{*}}
categorify the Picard–Lefschetz transformations on relative homology in the sense that

\mathcal{OC}(1_{L_{p}^{\hat{\theta}^{*}+\epsilon}})=\tau_{\hat{\theta}^{*}}(\mathcal{OC}(1_{L_{p}^{\hat{\theta}^{*}-\epsilon}})).

(2.13)

where
1_{O}
denotes the unit of the object
O
and
L_{p}^{\hat{\theta}}\in\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}
denotes the thimble defined using the background angle
\hat{\theta}
. In other words, as we change the background angles, when the homology classes of thimbles change by a Picard-Lefshetz transformations, the corresponding objects change by a mutation – the object associated to the new thimble is algebraically a
cone
on a morphism from the previous thimble to a thimble associated to the end of an exceptional gradient trajectory at angle
\hat{\theta}^{*}
. For many natural choices of function
F
, the total monodromy of the local system around
S^{1}
is not trivial. On the level of Fukaya–Seidel categories, this corresponds to the unpublished theorem
[
AG15
]
conjectured by Seidel
[
Sei06
]
that the composition of the functors
F_{\hat{\theta}}
is the Serre functor of
\mathrm{FS}_{\hat{\theta}}(X,F)
, which tends to be nontrivial as well.

2.3
The Fueter
2
-category from complex Morse theory

As explained in
Section 2.1
, the hom-category
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})
in the Fueter
2
-category is meant to be based off of the complex Morse theory model of the holomorphic symplectic action functional
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We now explain the correspondence, focusing on the exact setting as in (
2.2
). By (
2.1
) and the nondegeneracy of
\Omega
, the critical points of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
are the constant paths with values in
L_{0}\cap L_{1}
. We can view
M
as a real symplectic manifold with respect to any of the symplectic forms

\omega_{\theta}=\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\Omega)=(\cos\theta)\omega_{J}+(\sin\theta)\omega_{K}.

(2.14)

For each
\theta\in S^{1}
, we have that
\omega_{\theta}
is compatible with
J_{\theta}=\cos\theta J+\sin\theta K
. In the exact setting these forms have primitives given by
\lambda_{\theta}=\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\Lambda)
, and
\lambda_{\theta}|_{L_{i}}
has a primitive given by
H_{i}^{\theta}=\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}H_{i})
. The holomorphic symplectic action functional
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
thus gives rise to a family of real symplectic action functionals

\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\gamma):=\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma))=\int_{\gamma}\lambda_{\theta}-H_{1}^{\theta}(\gamma(1))+H_{0}^{\theta}(\gamma(0)),

(2.15)

which have differentials

{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\gamma)v=Re(e^{-i\theta}{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(\gamma)v)=\int_{0}^{1}\omega_{\theta}\left(v(\tau),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\right){\mathrm{d}}\tau.

(2.16)

In particular,
\mathcal{A}_{J}:=\mathcal{A}_{0}
is the symplectic action functional when
M
is thought of as symplectic with respect to
\omega_{J}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{K}:=\mathcal{A}_{\pi/2}
is the symplectic action functional when
M
is thought of as symplectic with respect to
\omega_{K}
. In these conventions, we can write
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{A}_{J}+i\mathcal{A}_{K}
.

By the standard computation of the Hessian of the real symplectic action functional
[
Flo89
]
, we see that the critical points of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}
are nondegenerate exactly when
L_{0}
intersects
L_{1}
transversely. Moreover, in our conventions, summarized in
Section D.3
, the gradient flow equation for
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
is the
J_{\theta}
-holomorphic curve equation

\begin{gathered}u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{P},\\
\partial_{t}u=\nabla\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(u(t))=-J_{\theta}(u)\partial_{\tau}u.\end{gathered}

(2.17)

In our conventions for the complex Morse theory model, the morphisms from
p
to
q
should be generated by gradient flows of
-\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})
where
\theta=\arg(p-q)
, i.e. gradient flows of
-\mathcal{A}_{\theta}=\mathcal{A}_{\theta+\pi}
, that is:
J_{\theta+\pi}
-holomorphic strips.

Finally, the differential, and more generally the
A_{\infty}
-operations, in the complex Morse theory model should be given by solutions to the complex gradient flow equation (
2.6
). Plugging in
F=\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
into (
2.6
) and using (
2.17
) to compute the gradient of the action functional, we get that complex gradient trajectories
U:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{P}
are equivalent to solutions of

\displaystyle U:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,1]\to M,

\displaystyle\partial_{s}U+I(U)\partial_{t}U=\nabla\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i(\theta+\pi/2)}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})=-J_{\theta+\pi/2}(U)\partial_{\tau}U=-K_{\theta}(U)\partial_{\tau}U,

where

K_{\theta}=IJ_{\theta}=\cos\theta K-\sin\theta J

(2.18)

is the complex structure such that
(I,J_{\theta},K_{\theta})
is a quaternionic triple. Moving all terms to the left hand side, and multiplying the equation by
J_{\theta}
we get that the above solutions solve the Fueter-type equation

I(U)\partial_{\tau}U+J_{\theta}(U)\partial_{s}U-K_{\theta}(U)\partial_{t}U=0.

(2.19)

In order to recover the more standard Fueter equation (
1.2
), we can instead define
\mathrm{FS}(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}})
via solutions to the
anti-complex gradient flow equation

\displaystyle U:\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{P},

\displaystyle\partial_{s}U-I(U)\partial_{t}U=\nabla\mathrm{Im}(-e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}).

Solutions to the above equation are in bijection with solutions to (
2.4
) as desired. The
s
-invariant asymptotics are the
J_{\theta}
-
anti
-holomophic strips, i.e. the
J_{\theta+\pi}
-holomorphic strips, that give generate the morphism complexes
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})(p,q)
. The coordinate change
(s,t)\leftrightarrow(-s,t)
induces a bijection between complex gradient trajectories and complex anti-gradient trajectories, and this explains why the opposing conventions in the differentials (
2.5
) and (
2.10
). We have thus reproduced the description of the Fueter hom-category of
Section 2.1
. We discuss further aspects of the formalism of the Fueter
2
-category in Appendix
B.1
.

2.4
Decategorifications of the Fueter
2
-category

The Fukaya category of a symplectic manifold
M
categorifies the topological invariants of
M
, such as the intersection form. In the same way, we expect that if
M
is hyperkähler, the Fueter
2
-category of
M
categorifies the symplectic invariants of
M
. Because
\mathrm{Fuet}(M)
is a
2
-category it has two natural decategorifications: these turn out to be the Fukaya category
\mathrm{Fuk}(M)
, and the complex Morse category of the loop space
LM
, respectively.

As should be clear from
Section 2.1
, the Fueter
2
-category takes into account the many nontrivial
J_{\theta}
-holomorphic strips stretching between pairs of complex Lagrangians. By analogy with the finite-dimensional case, discussed in
Section 2.2
, the Fueter category defined using background angle
\hat{\theta}
should satisfy

HH_{*}(\mathrm{Fuet}^{\hat{\theta}}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})))\simeq HM_{*}(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\theta}+\pi})=HF^{*}(L_{0},L_{1};\omega_{\hat{\theta}},J_{\hat{\theta}}).

(2.20)

On other words,
the Fueter
2
-category should categorify the subcategory of the Fukaya category containing complex Lagrangians
. In particular,
assuming that all complex Lagrangians under consideration intersect transversely
, applying Hochschild homology to the
A_{\infty}
-bifunctors (see
[
Sei08
]
for algebraic background),

\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{1})\otimes\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{1},L_{2})\to\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L_{0},L_{2})

(2.21)

should recover the composition in the Fukaya category. This identify follows from the definition of composition of
1
-morphisms in
Section 2.1
. We symbolically write this naive decategorification of the Fukaya category as

HH_{*}(\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}^{\hat{\theta}})\simeq\mathrm{Fuk}^{h}(M,\omega_{\theta}),

(2.22)

where
\mathrm{Fuk}^{h}(M,\omega_{\theta})\subset\mathrm{Fuk}(M,\omega_{\theta})
is the full subcategory with objects the complex Lagrangian submanifolds.

In the case when the holomorphic Lagrangians do not intersect transversely, we expect that the above identity is replaced with

HP_{*}(\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}^{\hat{\theta}})\simeq\mathrm{Fuk}^{h}(M,\omega_{\hat{\theta}})[u,u^{-1}]

(2.23)

where
HP
is the
periodic cyclic homology

[
Lod13
]
, which we apply to every hom-category
Fuet_{M}^{\hat{\theta}}(L_{0},L_{1})
individually, and on the right hand side we have tensored the Fukaya category with a ring
k[u,u^{-1}]
where
u
is a formal variable in degree
2
.

Let us motivate the expectation (
2.22
). The basic observation is that if two complex Lagrangian submanifolds intersect transversely, then the gradings of their intersection points are all the same. As such, one has that

HF^{*}(L_{0},L_{1})\simeq\mathbb{Z}(L_{0}\cap L_{1})\text{ when }L_{0}\text{ is transverse to }L_{1}.

(2.24)

In the case of non-transverse intersections, Solomon–Verbitsky have proven

Proposition 2.8

(
[
SV19
]
)
.

For generic values of
\theta\in S^{1}
, all
J_{\theta}
-holomorphic polygons with boundary on a finite collection of complex Lagrangians are constant.

The basic reason for both of these phenomena is explained by the fact that the Floer cohomology of
L_{0},L_{1}
is the gradient flow of the real part of a holomorphic Morse function. Indeed, when we compute
HF^{*}(L_{0},L_{1},\omega_{\theta})
, we are allowed to use any complex structure
tamed

[
MS12
, Chapter 1]
by
\omega_{\theta}
, which is an open condition. Thus, instead of using
J_{\theta}
to define the holomorphic curve equation, we can use
J_{\theta+\epsilon}
for a small generic
\epsilon
to compute
HF^{*}(L_{0},L_{1};\omega_{\theta})
. The image of a
J_{\theta+\epsilon}
-holomorphic curve under
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
will project to a straight line with argument
\theta+\epsilon
in the complex plane; as such, for generic
\epsilon
, there will be no such lines between a pair of critical values, and thus no such non-constant holomorphic curves between intersection points, and in the case of transverse intersection, and no differential on the resulting Floer complex. We discuss expectations around non-transversely intersecting holomorphic Lagrangians in
Section B.2
.

Thus, when
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
intersect
cleanly
, the action functional
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L_{0},L_{1}}
is Morse–Bott, and so
FS(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L_{0},L_{1}})
should have a semi-orthogonal decomposition with pieces given by the Fukaya categories of the connected components of
L_{0}\cap L_{1}
(see
Section B.5
, (
B.13
)). But
Section 2.4
implies in this case that
HF(L_{0},L_{1})=H^{*}(L_{0}\cap L_{1})
; meanwhile, known properties of nondegenerate Fukaya categories
[
Abo10
,
Sei01
,
Gan19
]
should identify periodic cyclic homology of
FS(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L_{0},L_{1}})
with a
2
-periodic version of
H^{*}(L_{0}\cap L_{1})
. This motivates (
2.23
) in the case of clean intersections; when the Lagrangians intersect transversely, (
2.23
) essentially reduces to (
2.22
).

Now, instead of applying Hochschild homology to morphisms, another way decategorifying a TQFT is to consider it on closed manifolds instead of on manifolds with boundary or corners. For example, as we saw in
Appendix A
, an open-closed 2d TQFT assigns a vector space not just to an interval but also to a circle; in the symplectic setting, the circle corresponds to the Hamiltonian Floer cohomology of
M
, and is defined by computing the Morse homology of the action functional on the
loop space
instead of on the space of paths. Algebraically, the Hamiltonian Floer cohomology of
M
is meant to be recovered by taking the Hochschild homology of the Fukaya category.

In the setting of the Fueter equation, we can formally do the same, and formally define a
1
-category

\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(S^{1})=\mathrm{FS}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}})

where
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}
is the holomorphic symplectic action functional on the loop space

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}:LX\to\mathbb{C},\\
{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}(\gamma)v=\int_{S^{1}}\Omega\left(v(\tau),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\right){\mathrm{d}}\tau.\end{gathered}

(2.25)

On a formal level,
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(S^{1})
has objects given by critical points of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}
, which are the constant loops into
X
. The
1
-morphisms would then be given by gradient flows of
\mathrm{Re}(e^{i\theta}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}})
between these points, which are the
J_{\theta}
-holomorphic spheres in
X
, for all
\theta\in S^{1}
. Correspondingly, the the differential on the morphism complexes in this category should be given by counts of Fueter maps

\begin{gathered}U:\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}\to M,\\
\partial_{s}U-I(U)(\bar{\partial}^{J_{\theta}}_{z}U)=0,\end{gathered}

(2.26)

with
s
denoting the coordinate on
\mathbb{R}
,
z
denoting the holomorphic coordinate on
S^{2}
, and with asymptotics given by
J_{\theta}
-holomorphic spheres in
M
. Similarly, the higher
A_{\infty}
-operations should count solutions Fueter maps from non-compact
3
-manifolds without boundary with ends modeled on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
.

Unfortunately,
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}
is only Morse–Bott rather than Morse, so the complex Morse theory model of the Fukaya-Seidel category is not available; see
Section B.5
for more on this problem. However, Ansatz (
B.10
) suggests that
Fuet_{M}(S^{1})
is a category built out of the Fukaya categories of the critical loci of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}
, e.g.

FS(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}})\simeq\mathrm{Fuk}(M,\omega_{\hat{\theta}}).

(2.27)

Thus, the two decategorifications would actually be the same, although if we consider
wrapped
Fueter categories (see
Section B.5
, equation (
B.13
)), this equivalence of decategorifications may no longer hold.

In any case, applying the finite dimensional relation between Hochschild homology and Morse homology again, we should expect that

HH_{*}(\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(S^{1}))\simeq HM_{*}(\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\hat{\theta}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{S^{1}}))=HF^{*}(X,\omega_{\hat{\theta}}),

(2.28)

with the right-hand side denoting the Hamiltonian Floer cohomology of
(X,\omega_{\hat{\theta}})
. We expect that there is a categorification of the isomorphism above to a map

\overline{HH}_{*}(\mathrm{Fuet}_{M})\to\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(S^{1}).

(2.29)

which (assuming that this map is an isomorphism) recovers (
2.28
) by taking
HH_{*}
of both sides
again
. Here,
\overline{HH}_{*}
is an invariant of a
2
-category described in
Section B.2
(see
Equation B.5
) which is distinct from the
1
-category (
2.22
) produced by taking Hochschild homology of all hom-categories. We note that an isomorphism like (
2.27
) implies (
2.28
) for nondegenerate symplectic manifolds
M
.

2.5
Cotangent bundles of Kähler manifolds

As the previous discussions show, there are significant analytical and combinatorial challenges that arise in the definition of the Fueter
2
-category. We now come to the first examples where we have explicit expectations as to the behavior of the Fueter
2
-category. These examples are cotangent bundles of Kähler manifolds. As we explain in the introduction, the following conjecture is a quaternionic version of Floer’s result.

Conjecture 2.9
.

Let
X
be a Kähler manifold with an exact symplectic form and pseudo-convex end (for example, a Stein manifold). Let
F:X\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic Morse function. Then, given certain geometric assumptions on
F
near the ends of
X
,

\mathrm{Fuet}_{T^{*}X}(L_{0},L_{1})\simeq\mathrm{FS}(X,F),

(2.30)

where
L_{0}
is the zero-section of
T^{*}X
and
L_{1}=\Gamma({\mathrm{d}}F)
is the graph of
{\mathrm{d}}F
.

This conjecture is supported by our
Section 1
. As a definition of the complex Morse theory model of
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
in the Haydys version is now available
[
Wan22
]
, this conjecture should be accessible by adapting that model to the infinite-dimensional setting and generalizing the analysis performed in the current paper.

This conjecture, together with the existence of the Fueter
2
-category, would have remarkable implications. Indeed, just as Lagrangian Floer cohomology groups are invariant under Hamiltonian flows, the Fueter equation and the associated categorical invariants enjoy natural invariance properties under the flows of
holomorphic Hamiltonians
. We explore this phenomenon further in
Section B.5
; for now, it suffices to note that a holomorphic function

H:M\to\mathbb{C}

determines a
holomorphic Hamiltonian vector field

X_{H}
on
M
by

\iota_{X_{H}}\Omega=-dH,

(2.31)

such that
X_{H}
is the Hamiltonian vector field of

implies, after applying the time one flow of

Conjecture 2.10

Let

Given a third holomorphic Morse function

3.1
Quaternion-antilinear maps

A map
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic if and only if its differential is complex-linear. The obvious quaternionic analog of this notion is not interesting as any map
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
with quaternionic-linear derivative is linear
[
Fue35
]
. There is, however, an interesting theory of maps whose derivative is, in an appropriate sense, quaternion-antilinear.

We begin with some basic linear algebra over quaternions. Let
V
be a quaternionic vector space, i.e. a real vector space equipped with a triple of complex structures
(I,J,K)
satisfying the quaternionic relation
IJ=K
. This quaternionic triple makes
V
into a module over
\mathbb{H}
. Given another quaternionic vector space
(U,i,j,k)
, the space of real homomorphisms from
U
to
V
decomposes into two
\mathbb{H}
-invariant subspaces:

\mathrm{Hom}(U,V)=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)\oplus\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V),

(3.1)

where
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)
is the space of
\mathbb{H}
-linear endomorphisms and
\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)
is the direct sum of the subspaces

\displaystyle\{A\in\mathrm{Hom}(U,V):Ai=IA,\ Aj=-JA,\ Ak=-KA\},

\displaystyle\{A\in\mathrm{Hom}(U,V):Aj=JA,\ Ak=-KA,\ Ai=-IA\},

\displaystyle\{A\in\mathrm{Hom}(U,V):Ak=KA,\ Ai=-IA,\ Aj=-JA\}.

While none of the three subspaces is
\mathbb{H}
-invariant, their direct sum is. By analogy with complex vector spaces, we will call endomorphisms in
\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)

quaternion-antilinear
or
IJK
-antilinear
. The projection
\mathrm{Hom}(U,V)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)
is given by

A\mapsto\frac{1}{4}(A-IAi-JAj-KAk),

so
A
is quaternion-antilinear if and only if

A-IAi-JAj-KAk=0.

Our chief interest is in the case
U=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{E}
, where
\mathbb{E}
is a three-dimensional vector space. A basis
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
of
\mathbb{E}
induces an identification
U=\mathbb{H}
by

1\mapsto 1,\quad e_{1}\mapsto i,\quad e_{2}\mapsto j,\quad e_{3}\mapsto k.

Given such a basis, we say that a linear map in
\mathbb{E}\to V
is quaternion-antilinear if the induced map
U\to V
is quaternion-antilinear with respect to this identification. Explicitly, we have an isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)\cong V

\displaystyle A\mapsto A(1)

and
A\colon\mathbb{E}\to V
is quaternion-antilinear if and only if it belongs to the kernel of the composition

\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E},V)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(U,V)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{H}}(U,V)\cong V,\\

which we denote by
A\mapsto A_{\mathbb{H}}
and which is given up to a constant by

A_{\mathbb{H}}=IAe_{1}+JAe_{2}+KAe_{3}.

Denote by
\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{E},V)
the space of quaternion-antilinear maps. We have

\begin{split}\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{E},V)&=\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{I}(\mathbb{E},V)\oplus\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{J}(\mathbb{E},V)\\
&=\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{J}(\mathbb{E},V)\oplus\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{K}(\mathbb{E},V)\\
&=\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{K}(\mathbb{E},V)\oplus\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{I}(\mathbb{E},V),\end{split}

(3.2)

where
\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{I}(\mathbb{E},V)
is the space of
I
-antilinear maps
, or, more precisely,
I
-antilinear maps from the orthogonal complement of
e_{1}
in
\mathbb{E}
to
V
(and similarly for
J
and
K
):

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{I}(\mathbb{E},V)=\{A\in\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{E},V)\ |\ Ae_{1}=0\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{J}(\mathbb{E},V)=\{A\in\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{E},V)\ |\ Ae_{2}=0\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{K}(\mathbb{E},V)=\{A\in\overline{\mathrm{Hom}}_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{E},V)\ |\ Ae_{3}=0\}.

3.2
Fueter maps

An
almost quaternionic manifold
is a manifold
M
of dimension
4n
equipped with a triple of almost complex structures
(I,J,K)
satisfying the quaternionic relation
IJ=K
; equivalently, with a reduction of the structure group of
TM
from
\mathrm{GL}(4n,\mathbb{R})
to
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})
. Since
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})
can be retracted to
\mathrm{Sp}(n)\subset\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})
, the structure group can be further reduced to
\mathrm{Sp}(n)
, yielding a Riemannian metric with respect to which
I,J,K
are orthogonal. Such a metric is hyperkähler if and only if the two-forms induced by the metric and
I,J,K
are closed, in which case it follows that
I,J,K
are integrable, see
Section 3.5
.

A
framed manifold
consists of a manifold together with a framing, i.e. trivialization of its tangent bundle. A framing induces an orientation and a Riemannian metric making the framing orthonormal. A classical theorem of topology says that every orientable three-manifold admits a framing.

Let
(E,e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
be a framed three-manifold and let
(M,I,J,K)
be an almost quaternionic manifold. A map
U\colon E\to M
is called a
Fueter map
if for every
x\in E
, the derivative

{\mathrm{d}}U(x)\colon T_{x}E\to T_{U(x)}M

is quaternion-antilinear. It is convenient to introduce the
Fueter operator

\mathscr{F}(U)=I(U){\mathrm{d}}U(e_{1})+J(U){\mathrm{d}}U(e_{2})+K(U){\mathrm{d}}U(e_{3}),

which maps a function
U\colon E\to M
to a section of
U^{*}TM
, so that
U
is a Fueter map if and only if it satisfies the nonlinear first order partial differential equation called the
Fueter equation
:

\mathscr{F}(U)=0.

Example 3.1
.

If
E
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with the standard coordinates
(\tau,s,t)
, then the Fueter operator is

\mathscr{F}(U)=I(U)\partial_{\tau}U+J(U)\partial_{s}U+K(U)\partial_{t}U.

Observe that

•

\tau
-independent solutions are
I
-antiholomorphic maps from the
st
-plane,

•

s
-independent solutions are
J
-antiholomorphic maps from the
t\tau
-plane,

•

t
-independent solutions are
K
-antiholomorphic maps from the
\tau s
-plane.

In this paper, we are interested in the case

E=[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Remark 3.2
.

As in pseudo-holomorphic map theory, it is natural to consider a domain-dependent version of the Fueter equation. Let
E
be a Riemannian three-manifold and suppose that we have a bundle map
\mathscr{I}
from the bundle
TE
over
E\times M
to
\mathrm{End}(TM)
such that for every orthonormal frame
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
of
TE
, the endomorphisms
\mathscr{I}(e_{1}),\mathscr{I}(e_{2}),\mathscr{I}(e_{3})
form a quaternionic triple on
M
. The domain-dependent Fueter equation for maps
U\colon E\to M
is

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathscr{I}(e_{i}){\mathrm{d}}U(e_{i})=0

for any local orthonormal frame
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
of
TE
. The equation does not depend on the choice of the local frame. For more details, see
[
HNS09
,
Hay12
,
Wal17
]
.

Recall that the Cauchy–Riemann equation for antiholomorphic maps from a Riemann surface
u\colon(\Sigma,i)\to(M,I)
can be succinctly written as

{\mathrm{d}}u\circ i+I\circ{\mathrm{d}}u=0.

There is a similar way to write the Fueter equation, with the complex structure
i
on
\Sigma
replaced by the Hodge star (equivalently, the cross product) on the three-manifold
E
. Given a map
U\colon E\to M
, its differential
{\mathrm{d}}U
is a
TM
-valued one-form whose Hodge dual
\ast{\mathrm{d}}U
is a
TM
-valued two-form. Define an
\mathrm{End}(TM)
-valued one-form

\mathcal{I}=I\otimes e^{1}+J\otimes e^{2}+K\otimes e^{3},

where
(e^{1},e^{2},e^{3})
is the dual frame of
T^{*}E
. Strictly speaking,
\mathcal{I}
is a one-form on
E\times M
, which, using
\mathrm{id}\times U\colon E\to E\times M
, we pull-back to get an element of
\Omega^{1}(E,U^{*}\mathrm{End}(TM))
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The Fueter equation for
U\colon E\to M
is equivalent to

\ast{\mathrm{d}}U+\mathcal{I}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}U=0,

(3.3)

where the right-hand side combines the wedge product of differential forms with the action of
\mathrm{End}(TM)
on
TM
.

Proof.

Let
U_{i}={\mathrm{d}}U(e_{i})
and
(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})=(I,J,K)
, so that

{\mathrm{d}}U=\sum_{i=1}^{3}U_{i}\otimes e_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\ast{\mathrm{d}}U=\sum_{\mathrm{cyclic}}U_{i}\otimes e_{j}\wedge e_{k}.

We compute

\mathcal{I}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}U=\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}I_{j}U_{k}\otimes e_{j}\wedge e_{k}=\sum_{j<k}(I_{j}U_{k}-I_{k}U_{j})\otimes e_{j}\wedge e_{k}.

Therefore, (
3.2
) is equivalent to

U_{i}+I_{j}U_{k}-I_{k}U_{j}=0

for every cyclic permutation
(i,j,k)
of
(1,2,3)
. After multiplying both sides by
I_{i}
each of these equations is the Fueter equation
\mathscr{F}(U)=0
.
∎

3.3
Boundary conditions

Let
(M,I,J,K)
be an almost quaternionic manifold and let
L_{0},L_{1}\subset M
be two submanifolds which are totally real with respect to
J
and
K
. A
Fueter strip
between
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
is a map

U\colon[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M

which satisfies the Fueter equation

I(U)\partial_{\tau}U+J(U)\partial_{s}U+K(U)\partial_{t}U=0,

together with the boundary conditions

U(0,s,t)\in L_{0},\quad U(1,s,t)\in L_{1}\quad\quad\text{for all }(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(3.4)

and the asymptotic boundary conditions

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}U(\tau,s,t)=u_{\pm}(t,\tau)\quad\quad\text{for all }(t,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}U(\tau,s,t)=p_{\pm}\quad\quad\text{for all }(\tau,s)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}.

Here
p_{-}
and
p_{+}
are points in
L_{0}\cap L_{1}
, and
u_{-},u_{+}
are
J
-antiholomorphic strips with boundary on
L_{0}
,
L_{1}
, that is: maps
u\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to M
satisfying

\partial_{t}u-J(u)\partial_{\tau}u=0,

with boundary on
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
and converging to
p_{\pm}\in L_{0}\cap L_{1}
as
t\to\pm\infty
.

More precisely, throughout the paper we consider Fueter strips which satisfy the following conditions:

U\in C^{2}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2},M),\quad\partial_{s}U(\tau,\cdot,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2},U^{*}TM)\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},U^{*}TM)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}U(\tau,s,t)=u_{\pm}(t,\tau)\quad\text{in }C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}U(\tau,s,t)=p_{\pm}\quad\text{in }C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}).

Remark 3.4
.

While the Fueter equation for maps from a closed, framed three-manifold is elliptic
[
HNS09
]
, a Fredholm theory for Fueter strips has not yet been developed and will be treated in subsequent papers. By analogy with complex gradient trajectories
[
Hay15
,
Wan22
]
, one expects that the technical assumptions listed above, as way as the exponential decay of Fueter strips along infinite ends, are automatically satisfied for Fueter strips of appropriate Sobolev class and with finite energy. Somewhat similar boundary value problems appear in the study of the Kapustin–Witten equations
[
MW20
]
and the Atiyah–Floer conjecture
[
DFL21
]
.

3.4
Taming triples

The area of a pseudo-holomorphic disc in a symplectic manifold is bounded by a topological constant depending on the relative homology class of the disc and the cohomology class of the symplectic form. We wish to establish an analogous result for Fueter strips. For that purpose, it is convenient to introduce the notion of a
taming triple
, which is a quaternionic analog of a two-form taming an almost complex structure. This notion is inspired by Donaldson and Segal’s discussion of
taming forms
in special holonomy geometry
[
DS11
]
.

We return to the linear algebra setup from
Section 3.1
.
Let
(\mathbb{E},e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
be a three-dimensional vector space with a basis and let
(V,I,J,K)
be a quaternionic vector space. Let
(e^{1},e^{2},e^{3})
be the dual basis of
\mathbb{E}^{*}
. An element of
\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
can be seen as a triple of two-forms
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
on
V
, or a three-form
\eta=\sum e^{i}\otimes\eta_{i}
on
\mathbb{E}\oplus V
using

\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{2}V^{*}\subset\Lambda^{3}(\mathbb{E}\oplus V)^{*}.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that
\eta\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
is an
IJK
-taming triple
if for every non-zero
IJK
-antilinear map
A\colon\mathbb{E}\to V
, the three-form

A^{*}\eta:=\sum_{i=1}^{3}e^{i}\wedge A^{*}\eta_{i}\in\Lambda^{3}\mathbb{E}^{*}

is a negative multiple of the volume form

\mathrm{vol}=e^{1}\wedge e^{2}\wedge e^{3}.

Equip
\mathbb{E}
and
V
with Euclidean inner products making
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
into an orthonormal basis and
I,J,K
into orthogonal endomorphisms. We will often implicitly identify
\Lambda^{3}V^{*}
with
\mathbb{R}
using the volume form and write
\alpha\leq\beta
, and so on, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda^{3}V^{*}.

Lemma 3.6
.

A triple of two-forms
\eta\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
is
IJK
-taming if and only if there is
c>0
such that for every
IJK
-antilinear map
A\colon\mathbb{E}\to V

|A|^{2}\mathrm{vol}\leq-cA^{*}\eta.

(3.5)

Proof.

If the above inequality holds, then
\eta
is clearly
IJK
-taming. Conversely, let
\eta
be an
IJK
-taming triple. Denote by
F\subset\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E},V)
the set of
IJK
-antilinear maps with unit norm. Since
F
is compact and the function

\eta\mapsto-A^{*}\eta

is positive on
F
, there is
c>0
such that
-A^{*}\eta\geq 1/c
for every
A\in F
. Since
A^{*}\eta
and
|A|^{2}
scale quadratically with
A
, the inequality follows.
∎

The taming condition is open in the following sense.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
\eta
is an
IJK

and of

in the set of quaternionic triples on

and

from these neighborhoods,

-taming triple.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{Q}
be the space of quaternionic triples on

. Given
(I,J,K)\in\mathscr{Q}
, let

F(I,J,K)\subset\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{E},V)

be the space of
IJK
-antilinear maps
\mathbb{E}\to V
with unit norm. The spaces

form a fiber bundle
\mathcal{F}\to\mathscr{Q}
with compact fibers. A triple
\eta\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
is

-A^{*}\eta>0\quad\text{for all }A\in F(I,J,K).

Therefore, the lemma follows from the continuity of the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\times(\mathbb{E}^{*}\otimes\Lambda^{2}V^{*})\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle(A,\eta)\mapsto-A^{*}\eta.\qed

The following computation, which is an analogue of Wirtinger’s inequality in Kähler geometry, shows that the set of

Proposition 3.8

(
[

]
)

If

, and so on, then
\eta=(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
is an

-taming triple. In fact, for every

|A|^{2}\mathrm{vol}=|A_{\mathbb{H}}|^{2}\mathrm{vol}-2A^{*}\eta.

Proof.

Let
(I,J,K)=(I_{1},I_{2},I_{3})
and
a_{i}=Ae_{i}
. We compute

\displaystyle|A_{\mathbb{H}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|I_{1}a_{1}+I_{2}a_{2}+I_{3}a_{3}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a_{1}|^{2}+|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2}+\sum_{ij}\langle I_{i}a_{i},I_{j}a_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=|A|^{2}+2\sum_{\text{cyclic}}\langle I_{i}a_{j},a_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=|A|^{2}+2\sum_{\text{cyclic}}\omega_{i}(a_{j},a_{k}).

Therefore,

.

Lemma 3.15
.

A

3.5
Energy bounds

Let
(M,I,J,K)
be an almost quaternionic manifold. Suppose that
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
is an
IJK
-taming triple of two-forms on
M
, in the sense that inequality (
3.5
) holds at every point of
M
with the constant independent on the point. By definition, this means that for every subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a Fueter map
U\colon E\to M
we have

\int_{E}|{\mathrm{d}}U|^{2}\leq-c\int_{E}A^{*}\eta_{1}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}\tau+A^{*}\eta_{2}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}s+A^{*}\eta_{3}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t.

(3.6)

If
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}
are closed, then this inequality is an analog of the energy bound for
J
-holomorphic maps, for an almost complex structure
J
tamed by a symplectic form. In particular, if
(M,I,J,K)
is a hyperkähler manifold and
(\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K})
is the triple of Kähler forms on
M
, so that we are in the situation of
Section 3.4
, this gives us the energy identity for Fueter maps proved in
[
HNS09
]
.

However, these integrals are typically infinite for Fueter strips asymptotic to
J
-antiholomorphic strips. This issue arises already in complex Morse theory: the
L^{2}
norm of the derivative of a complex gradient trajectory (
1.4
) asymptotic to real gradient trajectories is infinite, and one has to consider instead the notion of energy known from Hamiltonian Floer theory. By analogy, we define the
cylindrical energy
of a Fueter strip
U\colon[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M
by

E(U)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\partial_{s}U|^{2}+|\partial_{t}U-J(U)\partial_{\tau}U|^{2}

The next result, which is a more precise statement of
Section 1
from the introduction, gives sufficient conditions for the energy of Fueter strips to be bounded by a topological constant.

We begin with a general discussion of homology classes associated with maps from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Let
S\subset M
be a submanifold or, more generally, the image of a smooth map from a manifold. Suppose that
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2},M)
is such that for every
\eta\in\Omega^{2}(M)
the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{*}\eta

is convergent (for example,
\partial_{s}f
is in
L^{1}
), and
f
is asymptotic to maps into
S
as
t\to\pm\infty
and
s\to\pm\infty
, in the following sense:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}f(s,\cdot)=a_{\pm}\quad\text{in }C^{1}(\mathbb{R},M),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}f(\cdot,t)=b_{\pm}\quad\text{in }C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},M),

where
a_{\pm}
and
b_{\pm}
are
C^{1}
functions with values in
S
. These asymptotic conditions guarantee that if
\eta
is closed and vanishes along
S
, this integral depends only on the relative cohomology class
[\eta]\in H^{2}(M,S)
and the relative homotopy class of
f
among maps satisfying these conditions. More precisely, two such functions
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are homotopic relative
S
, if there is a map
F\in C^{1}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2},M)
such that the integral of
F_{\tau}^{*}\eta
converges for every
\tau\in[0,1]
and
\eta\in\Omega^{2}(M)
, on the boundary we have

F|_{\tau=0}=f_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad F|_{\tau=1}=f_{1},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\pm\infty}F(\cdot,s,\cdot)=A_{\pm}\quad\text{in }C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],M),

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}F(\cdot,\cdot,t)=B_{\pm}\quad\text{in }C^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R},M),

where
A_{\pm}
and
B_{\pm}
are
C^{1}
functions with values in
S
. The existence of such a homotopy and Stokes’ theorem imply that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{0}^{*}\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{1}^{*}\eta

for every
\eta
representing a class in
H^{2}(M,S)
. Therefore, with a relative homotopy class of a function
f
as above we associate a relative homology class

[f]\in H_{2}(M,S)

such that

\langle[\eta],[f]\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{*}\eta.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
(M,I,J,K)
be an almost quaternionic manifold. Let
\eta
be a closed,
KI
-taming complex two-form on
M
. If
L_{0},L_{1}\subset M
are submanifolds such that
\mathrm{Re}(\eta)|_{L_{i}}=0
, then there is a constant
C>0
with the following property. For every Fueter strip
U\colon[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M
with boundary on
L_{0},L_{1}
and asymptotic to
J
-antiholomorphic strips
u_{\pm}\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to M
and intersection points
p_{\pm}\in L_{0}\cap L_{1}
, we have

E(U)\leq-C\int_{\{\tau\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}U^{*}\mathrm{Im}(\eta),

where the integral on the right-hand side depends only on the relative classes

[\mathrm{Im}(\eta)]\in H^{2}(M,u_{+}\cup u_{-})\quad\text{and}\quad[U|_{\{\tau\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}]\in H_{2}(M,u_{+}\cup u_{-}).

Moreover, if there is a one-form
\lambda
on
M
such that
\mathrm{Im}(\eta)={\mathrm{d}}\lambda
, then

E(U)\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\{\tau\}}u_{-}^{*}\lambda-u_{+}^{*}\lambda.

Proof.

Since
u=u_{\pm}
is a
J
-antiholomorphic map and
\eta
is
J
-linear,

\mathrm{Im}(\eta)(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{\tau}u)=-\mathrm{Re}(\eta)(\partial_{t}u,J\partial_{\tau}u)=-\mathrm{Re}(\eta)(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}u)=0.

Therefore,
\mathrm{Im}(\eta)
vanishes on
u_{+}
,
u_{-}
and defines a relative cohomology class

[\mathrm{Im}(\eta)]\in H^{2}(M,u_{+}\cup u_{-}).

For
\tau\in[0,1]
set
U_{\tau}(s,t)=U(\tau,s,t)
. The asymptotic boundary conditions imply that
U_{\tau}
represents a relative homology class

[U_{\tau}]\in H_{2}(M,u_{+}\cup u_{-}),

which is independent of
\tau
. It follows from the general discussion above that the integral

\langle[\mathrm{Im}(\eta)],[U_{\tau}]\rangle=\int_{\{\tau\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}U^{*}\mathrm{Im}(\eta)

depends only on the relative classes
[\mathrm{Im}(\tau)]
and
[U_{\tau}]
.
To prove the energy bound, we use the
KI
-taming condition:

\displaystyle|\partial_{s}U|^{2}+|\partial_{t}U-J\partial_{\tau}U|^{2}\leq-C(U^{*}\mathrm{Re}(\eta)\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t+U^{*}\mathrm{Im}(\eta)\wedge{\mathrm{d}}\tau).

Therefore,

E(U)\leq-C\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}U^{*}\mathrm{Re}(\eta)\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t+U^{*}\mathrm{Im}(\eta)\wedge{\mathrm{d}}\tau.

(3.7)

Since
u_{\pm}
are
J
-antiholomorphic and
\mathrm{Re}(\eta)
vanishes on
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
,

u_{\pm}^{*}\mathrm{Re}(\eta)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Re}(\eta)|_{L_{i}}=0.

Therefore,
\mathrm{Re}(\eta)
represents a relative cohomology class

[\mathrm{Re}(\eta)]\in H^{2}(M,L_{0}\cup L_{1}\cup u_{+}\cup u_{-}).

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
the restriction
V_{t}(\tau,s)=U(\tau,s,t)
represents a relative homology class

[V_{t}]\in H_{2}(M,L_{0}\cup L_{1}\cup u_{+}\cup u_{-}),

which is independent of
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
V_{t}
converges to a constant map as
t\to\infty
, this homology class is trivial. Therefore,

\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}U^{*}\mathrm{Re}(\eta)\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle[\mathrm{Re}(\eta)],[V_{t}]\rangle{\mathrm{d}}t=0.

The second integral in (
3.7
) is

\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}U^{*}\mathrm{Im}(\eta)\wedge{\mathrm{d}}\tau=\int_{0}^{1}\langle[\mathrm{Im}(\eta)],[U_{\tau}]\rangle{\mathrm{d}}\tau=\int_{0}^{1}\langle[\mathrm{Im}(\eta)],[U_{0}]\rangle{\mathrm{d}}\tau=\langle[\mathrm{Im}(\eta)],[U_{0}]\rangle,

which proves the energy bound. The last statement follows from Stokes’ theorem.
∎

The proof of
Section 3.5
relies crucially on the assumption that
M
admits a closed
KI
-taming form
\eta
, which is rather restrictive. Since
\eta
is a non-degenerate closed form of type
(2,0)
with respect to
J
, it follows that the almost complex manifold
(M,J)
carries a holomorphic volume form
\eta^{n}
where

. This, in turn, implies that

must be integrable, as shown by from the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
(M,J)
be an almost complex manifold and let
n=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M
. If
M
admits a closed, nowhere vanishing complex
n
-form of type
(n,0)
with respect to
J
, then

is integrable.

Proof.

Let
T_{0,1}M\subset TM\otimes\mathbb{C}
be the subbundle of vectors of type
(0,1)
. If
\Omega
is a nowhere-vanishing form of type
(n,0)
, then

T_{0,1}M=\{v\in TM\otimes\mathbb{C}\ |\ v\lrcorner\Omega=0\}.

If

and

, then the formula for

in terms of contractions and Lie brackets gives us that

∎

4
Convexity theory

In this section, we introduce a quaternionic analog of the notion of pseudo-convexity known from almost complex geometry. We then use it to prove
Section 1
: a maximum principle for Fueter maps to convex almost quaternionic manifolds.

Remark 4.1
.

Other notions of convexity in quaternionic geometry were introduced by Alesker–Verbitsky
[
AV06
]
and Harvey–Lawson
[
HL09
]
. Our definition is different and adapted specifically to the study of Fueter maps.

4.1
Complex convexity and pseudo-holomorphic maps

We begin with a review of convexity theory for almost complex manifolds. For more details, see, for example,
[
CE12
, Chapter 2]
. Let
(M,J)
be an almost complex manifold. The
Levi form
of a function
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
J
is the two-form

\sigma_{\rho}^{J}=-{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ J).

(4.1)

The function is
J
-convex
if
\sigma_{\rho}^{J}
tames
J
, or, equivalently, if for
x\in M
and every non-zero
J
-antilinear map
a\colon\mathbb{C}\to T_{x}M
, we have

a^{*}\sigma_{\rho}^{J}<0

in the sense that the pull-back is a negative multiple of the volume form on
\mathbb{C}
. This characterization will be useful when we discuss quaternionic convexity. From the viewpoint of pseudo-holomorphic curve theory, the importance of
J
-convexity lies in the following maximum principle.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
be an
J
-convex function. If
u\colon\Sigma\to M
is an
J
-holomorphic or
J
-antiholomorphic map from a Riemann surface, then

\rho\circ u\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}

is a subharmonic function. More precisely,
\Delta(\rho\circ u)\geq 0
, with equality at those points where
{\mathrm{d}}u=0
. In particular, if
\Sigma
is a compact Riemann surface with boundary, then

\sup_{\Sigma}(\rho\circ u)=\sup_{\partial\Sigma}(\rho\circ u).

Proof.

Let
i
be the complex structure on
\Sigma
and set
\sigma=\sigma_{\rho}^{J}
. If
u
is
J
-holomorphic, then

\displaystyle\Delta(\rho\circ u)\mathrm{vol}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=-{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}(\rho\circ u)\circ i)=-{\mathrm{d}}u^{*}({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ J)

\displaystyle=-u^{*}{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ J)=u^{*}\sigma.

Let
s+it
be a local holomorphic coordinate on
\Sigma
. Since
u
is
J
-holomorphic,

u^{*}\sigma=\sigma(\partial_{s}u,\partial_{t}u){\mathrm{d}}s\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t=\sigma(\partial_{s}u,J\partial_{s}u){\mathrm{d}}s\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t\geq c|{\mathrm{d}}u|^{2}\mathrm{vol}_{\Sigma},

so that

\Delta(\rho\circ u)\geq c|{\mathrm{d}}u|^{2},

which completes the proof. The antiholomorphic case is proved in the same way.
∎

There is a variant of the maximum principle for pseudo-holomorphic maps with boundary on a conical submanifolds.

Definition 4.3
.

1.

Let
(M,J)
be a non-compact almost complex manifold. We say that
M
has an
J
-conical end
if there is an exhausting function
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
which, outside a compact subset, is
J
-convex and has no critical points.

2.

If
M
has an
J
-conical end as above, we say that a submanifold
L\subset M
is
J
-
conical
if, outside a compact subset, the restriction of
\rho
to
L
has no critical points and
{\mathrm{d}}\rho
vanishes on
J(TL)
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(M,J)
be an almost complex manifold with an
J
-conical end defined by a function
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
and let
L\subset M
be an
J
-conical submanifold. For every sufficiently large
c
, the subset
K_{c}=\{\rho\leq c\}
has the following property. If
u\colon\Sigma\to M
is an
J
-holomorphic from a Riemann surface with boundary such that

•

u^{-1}(M\setminus K_{c})
is compact,

•

u(\Sigma)\cap K_{c}\neq\emptyset
,

•

u(\partial\Sigma)\subset L
,

then
u(\Sigma)\subset K_{c}
.

Proof.

Let
c
be large enough so that
\rho
and
L
satisfy the conditions from
Section 4.1
outside
K_{c}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
c
is a regular value of
r=\rho\circ u
. Set
U=r^{-1}([c,\infty))
and assume that
U
is non-empty and compact. Since
r|_{U}
is subharmonic, it has to attain its maximum on the boundary

\partial U=\partial_{r}U\cup\partial_{\Sigma}U,

where

\partial_{r}U=U\cap r^{-1}(c)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\Sigma}U=U\cap\partial\Sigma.

Suppose that
r
is not constant and attains its maximum at
z\in\partial_{\Sigma}U
. By the Hopf lemma, if
n\in T_{z}\Sigma
is the vector normal to the boundary of
\Sigma
at
z
, we have

{\mathrm{d}}r(n)>0.

On the other hand, since
u
is
J
-holomorphic,

{\mathrm{d}}r(n)={\mathrm{d}}\rho({\mathrm{d}}u(n))=-{\mathrm{d}}\rho(J{\mathrm{d}}u(in)).

The right-hand side is zero because
in
is tangent to
\partial\Sigma
, so
{\mathrm{d}}u(in)
is tangent to
L
, and
{\mathrm{d}}\rho
vanishes on
J(TL)
. We conclude that
z
must lie in
\partial_{r}U
and
r\leq c
on
U
. By definition,
r\leq c
on
\Sigma\setminus U
, so
r\leq c
on all of
\Sigma
.
∎

4.2
Quaternionic convexity and Fueter maps

Let
(M,I,J,K)
be an almost quaternionic manifold. Denote by
(\tau,s,t)
the standard coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Definition 4.5
.

The
IJK
-Levi form
of a function
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is the three-form on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times M
defined by

\sigma_{\rho}=\sigma_{\rho}^{I}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}\tau+\sigma_{\rho}^{J}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}s+\sigma_{\rho}^{K}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}t.

We say that
\rho
is
IJK
-convex
if

A^{*}\sigma_{\rho}<0

for every

and every non-zero quaternion-antilinear map

. Here we pull back
\sigma_{\rho}
using the graph of

which is a map
\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\oplus T_{x}M
, and by the above inequality we mean that the pull-back is a negative multiple of the volume form on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In other words,
\rho
is
IJK
-convex if at every point
\sigma_{\rho}
is an
IJK
-taming triple in the sense of
Section 3.4
.

By (
4.1
), the
IJK
-Levi form can be written as

\sigma_{\rho}=-{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ\mathcal{I}),

where

\mathcal{I}=I\otimes{\mathrm{d}}\tau+J\otimes{\mathrm{d}}s+K\otimes{\mathrm{d}}t

is an
\mathrm{End}(TM)
-valued one-form on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times M
, and
\circ
denotes the composition of the wedge product with the action of
\mathrm{End}(TM)
on

. By
Section 3.4
, every
IJK
-convex function is
I
-convex,
J
-convex, and
K
-convex. However, the converse is not true, see
Section 3.4
.
The following is a quaternionic analog of
Section 4.1
.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is
IJK
-convex and
U\colon E\to M
is a Fueter map from a domain

, then

\rho\circ U\colon E\to\mathbb{R}

is a subharmonic function. More precisely,
\Delta(\rho\circ U)\geq 0
,with equality at those points where
{\mathrm{d}}U=0
. In particular, if
E
is compact and has smooth boundary, then

\sup_{E}(\rho\circ U)=\sup_{\partial E}(\rho\circ U).

Proof.

Let
r=\rho\circ U
and denote by
\ast
the Hodge start on
E
. By
Section 3.2
,

\displaystyle\ast{\mathrm{d}}r

\displaystyle=\ast({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ{\mathrm{d}}U)={\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ(\ast{\mathrm{d}}U)

\displaystyle=-{\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ(\mathcal{I}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}U)=U^{*}({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ\mathcal{I}).

The minus sign in the second line comes from anticommuting

. Using
Section 3.4
and
IJK
-convexity, we arrive at

\Delta r\,\mathrm{vol}_{E}={\mathrm{d}}(\ast{\mathrm{d}}r)=U^{*}{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}\rho\circ\mathcal{I})=-U^{*}\sigma_{\rho}\geq c|{\mathrm{d}}U|^{2}\mathrm{vol}_{E}.\qed

In order to obtain a maximum principle for Fueter maps with Lagrangian boundary conditions, we introduce the notion of an
IJK
-conical end.

Definition 4.7
.

1.

A non-compact almost quaternionic manifold

has an

-conical end
if there is an exhausting function
\rho\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
which, outside a compact subset, is

-convex and has no critical points.

If

has an
IJK
-conical end as above, a submanifold

is
JK
-conical
if, outside a compat subset,
{\mathrm{d}}\rho
is non-zero on
TL
and vanishes on

and

.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
(M,I,J,K)
be an almost quaternionic manifold with an
IJK
-conical end defined by a function

. Let

be a
JK
-conical submanifold. For every sufficiently large

has the following property. If

•

is compact,

•

•

,

This is an obvious generalization of

and the proof is similar. Let

\rho
is
L

where

∎

Applied to Fueter strips, i.e. solutions to the Fueter equation with boundary conditions from

4.3
Conical completions

Conical manifolds and conical submanifolds can be constructed by completing compact manifolds with convex boundary. We begin with the complex case.

Definition 4.9
.

1.

Let
M
be a compact manifold with boundary. A

is
J

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
(M,J)
be a compact almost complex manifold and let

be a boundary-defining function which is

-convex near
\partial M
. The almost complex structure

\displaystyle\rho(x,r)=e^{r}-1

. Moreover, any submanifold

in

Let

be an

-convex boundary-defining function. Let

be a Riemannian metric compatible with

in a neighborhood of
\partial M
and
\nabla\rho
is tangent to

. The existence of such a metric follows from the following observation. Around every point of

there is a vector field

tangent to

and such that
{\mathrm{d}}\rho(v)=1
. Since

vanishes on

is nowhere tangent to

compatible with

,

and

and the Levi form of

∎

5
Cotangent bundles of almost complex manifolds

The cotangent bundle
M=T^{*}X
of an almost complex manifold
X
is in a natural way an almost quaternionic manifold. The goal of this section to describe Fueter maps to
M
in terms of maps to
X
and to prove a quaternionic analog of Floer’s theorem discussed in the introduction.

5.1
Almost complex and quaternionic structures

Let
(X,g,I)
be an almost Hermitian manifold, that is a manifold equipped with a Riemannian metric and a compatible almost complex structure. Denote by
\omega(\cdot,\cdot)=g(I\cdot,\cdot)
the induced two-form. Let
\nabla
be the connection on
TX
which preserves
I
and
g
and whose torsion
N\in\Omega^{2}(X,TX)
consists of the Nijenhuis tensor of
I
and
{\mathrm{d}}\omega
. For example, if
{\mathrm{d}}\omega=0
and
\nabla^{0}
is the Levi–Civita connection, then

\nabla=\nabla^{0}-\frac{1}{2}I\nabla^{0}I,

see
[
MS12
, Appendix C.7]
. In general,
\nabla
agrees with the Levi–Civita connection if and only if
{\mathrm{d}}\omega=0
and
I
is integrable, so that
(X,g,I)
is a Kähler manifold. Using
\nabla
, we decompose the tangent space to
M=T^{*}X
into the horizontal and vertical subspaces

TM=H\oplus V\cong\pi^{*}TX\oplus\pi^{*}T^{*}X\cong\pi^{*}TX\oplus\pi^{*}TX

(5.1)

where
T^{*}X
with
TX
are identified using the metric. With respect to this decomposition, the canonical quaternionic triple
(I,J,K)
on
M
is defined by

I=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&-I\end{pmatrix},\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{id}\\
\mathrm{id}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad K=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix}.

(Since the first almost complex structure is the canonical almost complex structure on
T^{*}X
induced by one on
X
, when it is not likely to cause confusion we will denote both of them by
I
.)

5.2
Convexity

A basic fact about the almost complex geometry of cotangent bundles is that they are pseudo-convex. More precisely, the fiberwise norm function

\displaystyle r\colon T^{*}X\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle r(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}|\xi|^{2}

is
J
-convex in a neighborhood of the zero section with respect to the almost complex structure
J
introduced above.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is an
I
-convex function, then there are positive functions
\epsilon
and
\delta
on
X
such that the function

\rho=r+\epsilon\varphi

is
IJK
-convex in the neighborhood of the zero section given by

T_{\delta}^{*}X=\{\xi\in T^{*}X\ |\ |\xi|\leq\delta(\pi(\xi))\},

and the zero section is
JK
-conical with respect to
\rho
.

If
\varphi
is uniformly
I
-convex and the first and second derivatives of
\varphi
, the Riemannian metric, and
I
are uniformly bounded, then the functions
\epsilon
and
\delta
can be taken to be constant and
\rho
is uniformly
IJK
-convex.

The proof is preceded by a general fact about taming two-forms.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
V
be a vector space with a complex structure
I
and a Hermitian metric.
If
\sigma\in\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
tames
I
, then there are constants
C>0
and
c>0
such that

\sigma(v,w)\leq-c(|v|^{2}+|w|^{2})+C|v-Iw|(|v|+|w|)\quad\text{for all }v,w\in V.

Proof.

Set
u=v-Iw
, or equivalently
Iu=Iv+w
. Applying
\sigma(\cdot,w)
to the first equation and
\sigma(v,\cdot)
to the second yields

\displaystyle\sigma(v,w)

\displaystyle=-\sigma(w,Iw)+\sigma(u,w),\text{and}

\displaystyle\sigma(v,w)

\displaystyle=-\sigma(v,Iv)+\sigma(Iu,v).

The taming condition gives us
\sigma(v,Iv)\geq c|v|^{2}
and similarly for
w
, so that

\sigma(v,w)\leq-c(|v|^{2}+|w|^{2})+C|u|(|v|+|w|).\qed

Proof of
Section 5.2
.

To simplify the notation, we will consider the case when
\varphi
is uniformly
I
-convex and the first and second derivatives of
\varphi
,
g
, and
I
are uniformly bounded.
The proof in the general case is the same except that the constants in the estimates that follow have to be replaced by functions on
X
.

We want to show that the pull-back of the
IJK
-Levi form of
\rho
by any quaternion-antilinear map from
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to the tangent bundle to
T^{*}X
is negative. The first step is to estimate the Levi form of
r
with respect to
I,J,K
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{I}_{r}

\displaystyle=-{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}r\circ I),

\displaystyle\sigma^{J}_{r}

\displaystyle=-{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}r\circ J),

\displaystyle\sigma^{K}_{r}

\displaystyle=-{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{d}}r\circ K).

Let
\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K}
be the two-forms on
T^{*}X
induced by the Riemannian metric and quaternionic triple:

•

\omega_{J}
is the canonical symplectic form on
T^{*}X
,

•

\omega_{I}=\omega\oplus-\omega
with respect to decomposition (
5.1
),

•

\omega_{K}(\cdot,\cdot)=-\omega_{J}(I\cdot,\cdot)
.

Note that while
\omega_{J}
is closed, this is not necessarily true for
\omega_{I}
and
\omega_{K}
. By (
5.1
), the space of two-forms on
T^{*}X
decomposes into

\Lambda^{2}T^{*}(T^{*}X)=\Lambda^{2}H\oplus(\Lambda^{1}H\otimes\Lambda^{1}V)\oplus\Lambda^{2}V,

which we will refer to as subspaces of forms of type
(2,0)
,
(1,1)
, and
(0,2)
correspondingly. If
(q_{i})
are local coordinates on
X
with the Riemannian metric given by
g_{ij}
, and
(q_{i},p_{i})
are the induced coordinates on
T^{*}X
, then

{\mathrm{d}}r=\sum_{ij}g_{ij}p_{i}{\mathrm{d}}q_{j}+\sum_{ijk}\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial q_{k}}p_{i}p_{j}{\mathrm{d}}q_{k}.

A straightforward computation in coordinates, using this formula and the definitions of
I,J,K
, shows that

\displaystyle\sigma^{I}_{r}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{I}^{0,2}+\alpha_{I}

\displaystyle\sigma^{J}_{r}

\displaystyle=\omega_{J}+\alpha_{J},

\displaystyle\sigma^{K}_{r}

\displaystyle=\omega_{K}+\alpha_{K},

where
\alpha_{I},\alpha_{J},\alpha_{K}
satisfy

|\alpha_{\bullet}(\xi)|\leq C|\xi|\quad\text{for }\bullet=I,J,K,\text{ and }\xi\in T^{*}X,

where the constant depends on the derivatives of the metric and
I
.

Let
A=(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})
be a triple of vectors tangent defining a quaternion-antilinear map
\mathbb{R}^{3}\to T^{*}_{\xi}X
, that is:

IA_{1}+JA_{2}+KA_{3}=0.

We want to estimate

A^{*}\sigma_{r}=\sigma^{I}_{r}(A_{2},A_{3})+\sigma^{J}_{r}(A_{3},A_{1})+\sigma^{K}_{r}(A_{1},A_{2}),

where, as usual, we identify three-forms on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with scalars using the volume form.
Decomposing
A_{i}=a_{i}+b_{i}
into the horizontal and vertical part yields

\displaystyle a_{1}-I(b_{2}+Ib_{3})=0,

\displaystyle b_{1}-I(a_{2}-Ia_{3})=0.

(5.2)

Using these equations, we compute in the same way as in the proof of
Section 5.2
:

\displaystyle 2\omega^{0,2}_{I}(A_{2},A_{3})+\omega_{J}(A_{3},A_{3})+\omega_{K}(A_{1},A_{2})=-|b|^{2}.

Therefore

A^{*}\sigma_{r}\leq-|b|^{2}+C|\xi|(|a|^{2}+|b|^{2})

Next, we estimate the
IJK
-Levi form of
\varphi\colon T^{*}X\to\mathbb{R}
. For convenience we use the same notation for
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
and its composition with the projection
T^{*}X\to X
. Let
\sigma^{I}_{\varphi},\sigma^{J}_{\varphi},\sigma^{K}_{\varphi}
be the Levi forms of
\varphi\colon T^{*}X\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to
I
,
J
, and
K
. By
Section 5.2
,

\sigma_{\varphi}^{I}(A_{2},A_{3})\leq\sigma_{\varphi}^{I}(a_{2},a_{3})+C(|a|b|+|b|^{2})\leq-c(|a_{2}|^{2}+|a_{3}|^{2})+C(|a||b|+|b|^{2}).

We also have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varphi}^{J}(A_{3},A_{1})\leq C(|a||b|+|b|^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varphi}^{K}(A_{1},A_{2})\leq C(|a||b|+|b|^{2}),

since both terms contain
A_{1}=a_{1}+b_{1}
and
|a_{1}|\leq|b|
by (
5.2
). We conclude that the term

A^{*}\sigma_{\varphi}=\sigma^{I}_{\varphi}(A_{2},A_{3})+\sigma^{J}_{\varphi}(A_{3},A_{1})+\sigma^{K}_{\varphi}(A_{1},A_{2})

is bounded above by

A^{*}\sigma_{\varphi}\leq-c|a|^{2}+C(|a|b|+|b|^{2}).

Finally, we have

\displaystyle A^{*}\sigma_{\rho}=A^{*}\sigma_{r}+\epsilon A^{*}\sigma_{\varphi}.

Putting the estimates for
r
and
\varphi
together and assuming that
|\xi|\leq\delta
, we get

A^{*}\sigma_{\rho}\leq-|b|^{2}-c\epsilon|a|^{2}+C\epsilon(|a|b|+|b|^{2})+C\delta(|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}).

Therefore, for
\epsilon
and
\delta
sufficiently small,

A^{*}\sigma\leq-c\epsilon|A|^{2}

for every quaternion-antilinear map
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to T(T^{*}X)
.
∎

5.3
Cauchy–Riemann operators

Let
\partial_{I}
,
\overline{\partial}_{I}
be the Cauchy–Riemann operators corresponding to the almost complex structure
I
on
X
and acting on the spaces of maps
v\colon\mathbb{C}\to X
:

\displaystyle\partial_{I}=\partial_{s}-I\partial_{t},

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{I}=\partial_{s}+I\partial_{t}.

These operators take values in
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
forms on
\mathbb{C}
with values in
TX
with complex structure
I
. To simplify the notation we trivialize the spaces of forms using
{\mathrm{d}}z={\mathrm{d}}s+i{\mathrm{d}}t
and
{\mathrm{d}}\bar{z}={\mathrm{d}}s-i{\mathrm{d}}t
, which allows us to identify

\Lambda^{1,0}\otimes TX=T_{1,0}X,\quad\Lambda^{0,1}\otimes TX=T_{0,1}X,

both of which are isomorphic as complex vector bundles to
TX
, the first with complex structure
I
and the second with
-I
.

These operators extend to the Cauchy–Riemann operators the spaces of maps
U\colon\mathbb{C}\to M
, defined by the induced almost complex structure
I
on
M
. Every such map
U
corresponds to a pair
(v,\xi)
where
v\colon\mathbb{C}\to X
and
\xi\in\Gamma(\mathbb{C},v^{*}TX)
. We use here the anti-linear isomorphism
TX\cong T^{*}X
given by the metric. We have

\partial_{I}U=(\partial_{I}v,(\nabla\xi)^{0,1}).

(5.3)

In the upcoming discussion, we will consider a perturbed version of the Cauchy–Riemann operator (
5.3
). Denote by

L_{v}\colon\Gamma(v^{*}TX)\to\Omega^{0,1}(v^{*}TX)\cong\Gamma(v^{*}TX)

the linearization of (
5.3
) at
v
. Let
L_{v}^{*}
be its formal
L^{2}
adjoint. Consider a perturbation of
\partial_{I}
which, with respect to the splitting
U=(v,\xi)
, is given by

D_{I}U=(\partial_{I}v,-L_{v}^{*}\xi).

(5.4)

Lemma 5.3
.

There is a section
{\mathfrak{n}}\in\Gamma(M,\mathrm{End}TM)
such that

D_{I}U=\partial_{I}U+{\mathfrak{n}}(\overline{\partial}_{I}U)

and

|{\mathfrak{n}}(\xi)|\leq|N(\pi(\xi))|\xi|\quad\text{for }\xi\in M=T^{*}X,

where
N
is the torsion of
\nabla
, proportional to the Nijenhuis tensor of
I
and
{\mathrm{d}}\omega
. In particular,
D_{I}=\partial_{I}
if and only if
I
is integrable and
{\mathrm{d}}\omega=0
.

Proof.

The linearization of
\partial_{I}
at
v
is

\displaystyle L_{v}\xi=(\nabla\xi)^{1,0}-N(\xi,\overline{\partial}_{I}v),

where
N
is the torsion of
\nabla
, proportional to the Nijenhuis tensor of
I
and
{\mathrm{d}}\omega
, see
[
MS12
, Lemma C.7.3]
. With the identifications above,
L_{v}
is an operator acting on the space of sections of
v^{*}TX
. The formal
L^{2}
adjoint of
L_{v}
is

Lemma 5.4
.

Denote by

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\tilde{U}=(\varphi_{\tau})_{*}\partial_{\tau}U+\frac{\partial\varphi_{\tau}}{\partial\tau}\circ U,

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\tilde{U}=(\varphi_{\tau})_{*}\partial_{s}U,

Multiplying each of the three equations by the corresponding almost complex structure and adding them together we obtain the result.

We can rewrite equation (

Proof.

5.5
Remarks on the modified equation

If
(X,g,I)

The proof of

Lemma 5.7
.

In the situation of

Proof.

We proceed in the same way as in (

\begin{split}\nabla_{\tau}\xi+I\partial_{I}v+\nabla f(v)&=0,\\

We apply

To control these terms, we estimate the norm of the torsion term:

|N^{\dagger}(\xi,\overline{\partial}_{I}v)|\leq C|\xi||{\mathrm{d}}v|\leq C|\xi||{\mathrm{d}}v-\nabla f{\mathrm{d}}t|+C\|f\|_{C^{1}}|\xi|

Using asymptotic boundary conditions and the energy identity for the Cauchy–Riemann operator

\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\partial_{s}v|^{2}+|\partial_{t}v-\nabla f|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\partial_{s}v-I(\partial_{t}v-\nabla f)|^{2}+C=\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\nabla_{\tau}\xi|^{2}+C,

\displaystyle|\langle N^{\dagger}(\xi,\overline{\partial}_{I}v),L^{*}_{v}\xi\rangle_{L^{2}}|

Therefore, in the same way as in the proof of

we get

C\delta\geq(1-C(\epsilon+\delta))\|\partial_{\tau}U\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

which proves the statement for

and

∎

Suppose by contradiction that there is a sequence of solutions

We end this section by remarking that the modified Fueter equation (

Appendix A
Topological field theories

This section briefly summarizes the basic philosophy of topological quantum field theories (TQFT) and its connection to Floer homology. This material is completely standard, but may be of interest to those who are not completely familiar with the connection between partial differential equations and algebraic structures.

Algebraic structure.

According to Atiyah
[
Ati88
]
, a TQFT is a functor from a cobordism category of manifolds to a monoidal category, e.g. the category of graded vector spaces over a field
\mathbf{k}
. Concretely, the simplest notion of a
2
-dimensional TQFT
F:\mathrm{Cob}_{2}\to\mathrm{Vect}_{\mathbf{k}}
assigns in a functorial way

•

a vector space
V=F(S^{1})
to the circle
S^{1}
;

•

the vector space
V^{\otimes n}=F(\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S^{1})
to
n
copies of the circle
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S^{1}
;

•

a linear map
V^{\otimes n}\to V^{\otimes m}
to any cobordism
\Sigma
from
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n}S^{1}
to
\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{m}S^{1}
.

This gives us the following structure:

•

A commutative associative product
V\otimes V\to V
and cocommutative coassociative coproduct
V\to V\otimes V
correspoding to the pair of pants cobordism and its inverse, together with a unit
\mathbf{k}\to V
for the product and a counit
V\to\mathbf{k}
for the coproduct.

•

By composing the cocounit with the product one gets a pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\otimes V\to\mathbf{k}
, and dually, by composing the unit and the product, a copairing
\Delta:\mathbf{k}\to V\otimes V
. Decomposing
S^{1}\times I
(corresponding to the identity
V\to V
) into the composition of a pairing and a copairing implies that

V\simeq V\otimes\mathbf{k}\xrightarrow{1\otimes\Delta}V\otimes V\otimes V\xrightarrow{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\otimes 1}\mathbf{k}\otimes V\simeq V

is the identity map, and thus that the pairing is perfect. Writing the product of
a,b\in V
as
ab
, one verifies that
\langle ab,c\rangle=\langle a,bc\rangle
.

•

The data of a perfect pairing together with a commutative associative unital product satisfying the identity above is a
Frobenius algebra
, and every
2d
TQFT determines and is determined by a Frobenius algebra
[
Koc03
]
.

In summary, a TQFT is a functor from a cobordism category, which can be specified by collection of algebraic operations satisfying certain identities.

Floer cohomology.

Certain examples of TQFTs are naturally associated with physical theories, and indeed that was Atiyah’s original motivation for introducing the axioms pf TQFT. Given
M
in the cobordism category,
F(M)
is meant to be the space of quantum states of the field theory on
M
, while the map
F(M_{1})\to F(M_{2})
associated to a cobordism
N
from
M_{1}
to
M_{2}
is meant to be the Hamiltonian evolution of the quantum states through time. The cobordism map is then typically defined by evaluating a path-integral over some infinite-dimensional space of fields on
N

[
Fre12
]
. While the integration over the infinte-dimensional space of fields is hard to make rigorous, in certain cases, due to supersymmetry
[
SZ97
]
the path integral can be computed, or rigorously defined, by counting solutions to some partial differential equations. Many invariants of Floer-theoretic origin are of this form.

We now explain the general pattern by the example of Hamiltonian Floer cohomology of a symplectic manifold. A Hamiltonian function
H:M\times S^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
on a symplectic manifold
M
defines an action functional
\mathcal{A}_{H}:\mathcal{L}M\to\mathbb{R}
on the free loop space. Hamiltonian Floer cohomology
HF^{*}(H)
, which is formally the Morse homology of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
, computes the space of states of
2
-dimensional TQFT on
M

[
Wit82
,
Flo89
,
Wit88
]
. Defining
HF^{*}(H)
involves the study of the gradient flow equation of
\mathcal{A}_{H}
, the Floer equation:

\displaystyle u\colon\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}\to M,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+J(u)\partial_{\tau}u=\nabla H(u),

with
(t,\tau)
denoting the coordinates on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{1}
and
J
is an almost complex structure on
M
compatible with the symplectic form. This equation admits a natural generalization to surfaces, the inhomogeneous pseudo-holomorpic map equation

\begin{gathered}u:\Sigma\to M,\\
({\mathrm{d}}u-\alpha(u))_{J}^{0,1}=0,\end{gathered}

where
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma\times M,TM)
is an inhomogenous term generalizing
\nabla H
above. Here
\Sigma=\hat{\Sigma}\setminus S
is the complement of a finite collection of points
S
in a closed Riemann surface
\hat{\Sigma}
. Counting solutions to (
A
) with asymptotic boundary conditions at each point of
S
defines TQFT operations corresponding to a cobordism
\hat{\Sigma}\setminus U
, where
U
is a small open neighborhood of
S

[
Sei08
]
.

Note that in the formulation of the inhomogeneous pseudo-holomorphic map equation, one must fix boundary conditions
at infinity
for
\Sigma
, even though the corresponding cobordism is a compact manifold with boundary. In general, there can be different kinds of boundary conditions for partial differential equations, and these correspond to variations on the definition of a TQFT. It may be that the surface
\Sigma
needs to be decorated with certain structures in order to make sense of the equations. Moreover, in order to ensure compactness of the set of solutions to the equations, one may need to restrict or modify the associated cobordism category further. As such, the formal algebraic properties of the TQFT and the geometric and analytical structure of the associated partial differential equation are closely intertwined in a way that is challenging to fully axiomatize.

Category theory.

It is an old idea, formalized by the Baez–Dolan cobordism hypothesis
[
BD95
]
, that higher-dimensional TQFT are associated to structures in higher category theory. A cobordism is simply a manifold with boundary, interpreted as a morphism by dividing the boundary into two pieces. As such, one wishes to interpret a cobordism-between-cobordisms, i.e. a manifold with corners modeled on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
with a certain decomposition of its boundary, as a morphism-between-morphisms in the cobordism
2-category
; and more generally to define notions of cobordisms-between-cobordisms-between-cobordisms in terms of general manifolds with corners, viewed as higher morphisms in a cobordism
n
-category. An extended TQFT is then suppposed to be a functor from a higher category of cobordisms to another higher category, prototypically the
n
-category of
\mathbf{k}
-linear
(n-1)
-categories.

Figure 10:
A two-dimensional cobordism between a pair of
1
-dimensional cobordisms (in red). From the perspective of category theory this can be interpreted as a morphism between morphisms.

While this general notion can be made precise
[
Lur09
]
, when building a TQFT out of solutions to partial differential equations it pays to be explicit. Moreover, the analysis of the given equations may force us to modify the TQFT formalism in minor ways. For example, while many aspects of the Fukaya category can be formulated in a general extended TQFT framework, typically symplectic topologists work in a formalism that is better described by the language of an
open-closed
2d
TQFT

[
MS06
,
And09
]
. In this formalism:

•

We have a category
\mathcal{F}
.

Complex linearity and anti-linearity.

Upon first glance, one may find the appearance of the

Remark B.1
.

In a formal definition of the

Novikov rings.

In many cases, the holomorphic action functional

defined by its differential (

Problem B.2
.

Define the Fukaya–Seidel category of a closed holomorphic one-form on a Kähler manifold, both with a more classical symplectic approach and in the complex Morse theory model.

It is advantageous and nontrivial to give a definition where the one-form is not required to be holomorphic. Indeed, Fukaya–Seidel categories make sense for symplectic Lefschetz fibrations, which have a significant amount of flexibility. However, it is not immediate what is the best definition of a correspondingly flexible symplectic of a holomorphic one-form; the essential challenges in defining this notion are about establishing energy bounds for the relevant pseudo-holomorphic curves or corresponding complex Morse trajectories. The flexibility of such a definition would be helpful in the infinite-dimensional setting, where one wishes to perturb the holomorphic symplectic action functional or to work in some

In this section, we explain the meaning of the invariant

B.5
Further analytic aspects

To establish the existence categorical structures described above, a number of essential analytic aspects of the Fueter equation remain to be clarified. Excluding the well-known open problems regarding the compactness theory of the Fueter equation, we outline what we see as the basic conceptual challenges suggested by this categorical framework. These are the problems of transversality of the critical locus and Fueter trajectories, of wrapping Fueter maps, and the most general problem of the domain of definition of the Fueter
2
-category.

Morse–Bott theory.

An essential challenge in setting up the Fueter
2
-category is that the holomorphic action functional
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L}
is not Morse, but is instead
Morse-Bott
, with critical locus equal to
L
viewed as the set of constant paths from
L
to
L
. As such, one does not have access to the basic structures of the complex Morse theory model of
\mathrm{FS}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L})
, which require that the holomorphic function is Morse, and a Morse–Bott analog requires significant new ideas

Problem B.11
.

Make sense of the monoidal endomorphism category
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(L,L)
where
L
is a complex Lagrangian.

In the finite dimensional case, one way to define the Fukaya–Seidel category of a Morse–Bott symplectic Lefschetz fibration
F:X\to\mathbb{C}
by perturbing
F
through maps to a nearby map
\tilde{F}
which is Morse (if this is possible), and defining
\mathrm{FS}(X,F):=\mathrm{FS}(X,\tilde{F})
. It is crucial, to control the behavior of the perturbation
F\rightsquigarrow\tilde{F}
at infinity, since otherwise this definition of
\mathrm{FS}(X,F)
may be highly dependent on perturbation. For example, if the critical locus
\mathrm{Crit}(F)
of

is compact, one should use a perturbation of
F
which is constant near infinity. If
\mathrm{Crit}(F)
is non-compact, then there may be no preferred perturbation, and there may be multiple inequivalent ways of defining the complex Morse theory of
F
. Indeed, in the real case of the Floer cohomology of a non-compact Lagrangian (which amounts to the real Morse theory of a real Morse–Bott action functional with non-compact critical locus), there are essentially different variants of Lagrangian Floer cohomology, such as the wrapped
[
AS05
]
or infinitesimally wrapped
[
NZ09
]

and the associated

suggest that in the case of non-compact complex Lagrangians
L

through nearby exact complex Lagrangians can drastically change the behavior of the Fueter hom-category, essentially because different holomorphic functions can have very different asymptotic geometry.

Unfortunately, even the idea of perturbing the Morse–Bott functional
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L}
to a Morse action functional is non-trivial. The basic challenge is the control of the energy (
1.7
) of the associated Fueter trajectories, which are needed to have any hope of compactness of resulting moduli spaces.

Problem B.12

Find a way of perturbing
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L}

In the setting of Lagrangian Floer cohomology, when

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L}
in the complexified setup, there are not enough of these perturbations:

•

A complex submanifold may not admit a deformation to a nearby transversely intersecting complex submanifold. For example, every curve on a complex symplectic surface is a complex Lagrangian. An easy computation with the adjunction formula shows that any rational curve
C
on a K3 surface

has normal bundle

K3

•

Complex symplectic manifolds may have few global holomorphic functions, and the global holomorphic functions that do exist when viewed as complex Hamiltonians may be insufficient to perturb a complex Lagrangian
L
to a transversely intersecting submanifold. A natural example is the zero-section of

•

If such perturbations existed, one would expect that taking their real parts would give perturbations of the real symplectic action functionals
\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L})
. But the latter functionals have Morse homology equal to the homology of

\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\theta}\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L})

An alternative approach is to follow the analogy with finite-dimensional Fukaya–Seidel categories, where one leaves the holomorphic category entirely and perturbs

through symplectic rather than holomorphic maps. The difficulty in this case is that energy bounds, and the construction of symplectic perturbations of the holomorphic action functional

to an

\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{C}}_{L,L}
. To this end, we note that there is a well known ansatz for Fukaya–Seidel categories with Morse–Bott critical locus. Namely, the critical locus of a holomorphic function on a Kähler manifold is itself a symplectic manifold. Wrapping.

In the context of Lagrangian Floer cohomology of non-compact Lagrangians in non-compact symplectic manifolds, there is a well-developed theory of

B.6
Further geometric aspects

We hope that the previous sections have convinced the reader that the study of categorical aspects of the
3d
Fueter equation is an exciting problem at the intersection of mathematical physics, differential geometry, algebra, and topology. While the definition of the Fueter
2
-category, not to mention its computation, poses significant challenges, connections to other perspectives on the
3d

N=4

\sigma
-model should offer many examples, guiding principles, and predictions.

In this final section, we outline how in examples of interest to mathematical physics, the Fueter
2
-category should be connected to computations in more traditional enumerative geometry and representation theory.

Compact examples.

There are not many known examples of compact hyperkähler manifolds. Up to deformation equivalence, there are only two compact hyperkähler
4
-manifolds,
K3
surfaces and abelian surfaces. All known higher-dimensional compact hyperkähler manifolds are quotients by finite groups of products of manifolds which are deformation equivalent to: Hilbert schemes of points on
K3
surfaces or abelian surfaces, higher-dimensional abelian varieties, or one of two exceptional examples of O’Grady
[
Huy11
]
. As such, it is challenging to find examples of compact hyperkähler manifolds for which one can study the Fueter equation and
3d
mirror symmetry.

Nonetheless, the case of abelian surfaces
M
may prove to be tractable and interesting. Indeed, if
M
is an abelian surface, then the Fukaya category of
M
is known in fairly explicit form
[
Fuk02
]
, which is a significant first step towards the computation of
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}
due to equation (
2.20
). The computation of these Fukaya categories is possible because the all pseudoholomorphic polygons are related to the linear geometry of these tori, and the corresponding counts of polygons are given by elaborate combinatorial formulae involving theta functions. It may be the case that just like these holomorphic polygons, the Fueter maps relevant to
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}
are also computed by counting certain polyhedra in the universal covers of these tori. Since in this setting one can exclude all possible bubbling for the Fueter equations
[
HNS09
]
, the case of tori may be comparatively tractable.

The case of
K3
surfaces is related to Donaldson–Thomas theory and is the subject of upcoming work by Bousseau
[
Bou22
]
. It is possible that satisfactory solutions to
Section B.5
may give rise to a more flexible geometric world of compact weak or almost hyperkähler manifolds.

Physics and representation theory.

There are many more examples of non-compact hyperkähler manifolds which should have interesting Fueter
2
-categories. The already discussed cotangent bundles of Kähler manifolds admit incomplete hyperkähler metrics near the zero section
[
Kal97
]
. This paper can be seen as an exploration of this example, using the convexity theory studied in
Section 4
to define a weak analog of a hyperkähler structure on cotangent bundles, which should suffice to develop the theory discussed in this paper.

There is a richly studied set of non-compact complete hyperkähler manifolds arising from mathematical physics which seem to recur in gauge theory and representation theory. Many of these can be fit into the framework of resolutions of symplectic singularities
[
Bra+14
]
, which give a series of examples of conjecturally
3d
mirror manifolds. These manifolds have a beautiful three-Sasakian geometry at infinity
[
BG07
]
, as well as certain complex Lagrangian skeleta which control their Fukaya categories and are intimately related to categories of interest to representation theory
[

,
GMW19
]
. Another framework understanding these examples in the context of

[
Tel14
]
.

We cannot do any justice in this review section to the ideas of

-categories of these examples should give rise to categorifications of the corresponding partially wrapped Fukaya catgories, which by
[

]
are equivalent to categories of sheaves. These, in turn, are known to be related to problems of representation theory. As such, this is a setting where there are very helpful physical and representation-theoretic predictions for Fueter
2
-categories. A primary challenge in this setting is to relate the existing representation-theoretic examples to Fukaya-Seidel categories of explicit holomorphic functions, as opposed to the more general framework of partially wrapped Fukaya categories and constructible sheaves, where they naturally reside. Studying the Fueter
2
-category with wrapping given by the given holomorphic function as a holomorphic Hamiltonian should then categorify the given representation-theoretic invariant. A natural and tantalizing question, one of many, is the following.

Another source of interesting complex Lagrangian submanifolds is given by holomorphic Chern–Simons theory. Given a
3
manifold
Y
and a Heegaard splitting into two handlebodies
Y=H_{1}\cup_{\Sigma}H_{2}
, the complex flat connections on
\Sigma
which extend to either
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
give a pair of intersecting complex Lagrangians
(L_{0},L_{1})
in the complex character variety
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
. By modifying this construction, one can desingularize the complex character variety such that the resulting Lagrangians support perverse sheaves as in
Section 2.4
which have hypercohomology independent of the choice of Heegaard splitting, giving rise to a well defined invariant of
Y

[
AM20
]
. This invariant should agree with the putative Floer homology of the holomorphic Chern-Simons functional, related to recent work of Taubes
[
Tau13
]
. By a complex and categorical version of the Atiyah–Floer conjecture, the Fukaya–Seidel category of the holomorphic Chern–Simons functional should be equivalent to
\mathrm{Fuet}_{\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}}(L_{0},L_{1})
; this is supported by a formal adiabatic limit computation
[
Hay15
, Remark 4.5]
.

Geometric Langlands program.

Important examples of non-compact complete hyperkähler manifolds are the Hitchin moduli spaces
[
Hit87
]
and their variants. These moduli spaces have interesting singular complex Lagrangians associated to representation theory: the components of the nilpotent cone
[
BN18
]
. Since the moduli space of solutions to Hitchin’s equations on a surface
\Sigma
agrees with the character variety
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
discussed in the previous paragraph, the Lagrangians
L_{0},L_{1}
associated to Heegaard splittings also sit inside these spaces. The Hitchin moduli space is the base of the Hitchin fibration, whose large part resembles the cotangent bundle of the moduli space of stable bundles on
\Sigma
. Thus, a version of
Section 1
may apply to this setting, which may be connected to other adiabatic limit problems in gauge theory. In a different direction, there are physical conjectures that the categories
\mathrm{Fuet}_{M}(S^{1})
, (
Equation 2.25
,
Equation 2.29
) for certain Coloumb and Higgs branches
M
should be related to the work of Ben–Zvi–Sakellaridis–Venkatesh
[
Ben22
]
.

Appendix C
Floer’s theorem revisited

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a smooth manifold and let
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Morse function. A Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{X}
induces an almost complex structure
J
on
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
compatible with the canonical symplectic form. Floer’s adiabatic limit theorem concerns solutions of the Floer equation

\displaystyle u\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to T^{*}\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+J(u)\partial_{\tau}u+\nabla\mathcal{A}(u)=0

(C.1)

with boundary on the zero section and converging to Hamiltonian chords of
\mathcal{A}\colon T^{*}\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as
t\to\pm\infty
. Provided that the
C^{2}
norm of
\mathcal{A}
is sufficiently small, Floer proved that all such solutions satisfy
\partial_{\tau}u=0
and therefore correspond to gradient trajectories of
f
contained in the zero section.

There is a different perturbation of the
J
-holomorphic map equation, whose solutions correspond to gradient trajectories of
f
without the assumption that the perturbation is small. Let
V=\nabla\mathcal{A}
be the gradient vector field of
\mathcal{A}
on
\mathcal{X}
. There is a canonical lift of
V
to a Hamiltonian vector field
X_{\mathcal{H}}
on
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
. One way to see this is to consider the embedding

\mathrm{Diff}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathrm{Symp}(T^{*}\mathcal{X})

defined by pushing-forward one-forms by diffeomorphisms. The induced homomorphism of Lie algebra associates with every vector field
V
on
\mathcal{X}
a Hamiltonian vector field
X_{\mathcal{H}}
generated by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\colon T^{*}X\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\xi)=\xi(V).

Note that critical points of
g
are all contained in the zero section and correspond to critical point of
\mathcal{A}
. We consider a version of the Floer equation

\displaystyle u\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to T^{*}\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+J(u)\partial_{\tau}u+X_{\mathcal{H}}(u)=0.

(C.2)

Note that this is not a usual Hamiltonian perturbation of the pseudo-holomorphic map equation, which would be of the form (
C.1
). There is a version of Floer’s theorem for this equation which does not rely on
\mathcal{A}
being
C^{2}
small.

Proposition C.1
.

Any solution
u
of the Floer equation (
C.2
) with boundary on the zero section and converging to critical points of
\mathcal{H}
as
t\to\pm\infty
satisfies
\partial_{\tau}u=0
, is contained in the zero section, and corresponds to a gradient trajectory of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Using the metric, we identify
T\mathcal{X}
with
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
. Denote by
\pi\colon T^{*}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
the projection map. The Levi–Civita connection on
T\mathcal{X}
induces decomposition

T(T^{*}\mathcal{X})=\pi^{*}T\mathcal{X}\oplus\pi^{*}T\mathcal{X}

with respect to which

X_{\mathcal{H}}(\xi)=\nabla\mathcal{A}\oplus-(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{A})\xi\quad\text{for }\xi\in T^{*}\mathcal{X}

where
\nabla\mathcal{A}
,
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{A}
are the gradient and Hessian of
\mathcal{A}
at point
\pi(\zeta)
. Therefore if we write
u
as a pair
(v,\xi)
where
v\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{X}
and
\xi\in\Gamma(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1],v^{*}T\mathcal{X})
, equation (
C.2
) becomes

\displaystyle\nabla_{\tau}\xi+\partial_{t}v+\nabla\mathcal{A}(v)=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}v-\nabla_{t}\xi+(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{A})\xi=0.

Set
L_{v}=\nabla_{t}+\nabla^{2}\mathcal{A}(v)
. Applying
\nabla_{\tau}
to the first equation and using the second equation yields

\nabla_{\tau}^{*}\nabla_{\tau}\xi+L_{v}L_{v}^{*}\xi=0,

which, after integrating by parts, shows that
\nabla_{\tau}\xi=0
. The boundary conditions imply that
\xi=0
and the proposition follows.
∎

Remark C.2
.

Floer’s original theorem can be easily recovered from
Appendix C
using the following two observations.

First, the proof of
Appendix C
shows that, in fact, the correspondence between solutions and gradient trajectories of
\mathcal{A}
holds for any perturbation of (
C.2
) of the form

\partial_{t}u+J(u)\partial_{\tau}u+X_{\mathcal{H}}(u)+P(u)=0,

as long as the perturbation term
P(u)
is sufficiently small. For example, it suffices that it satisfies
|P(u)|\leq\epsilon\mathrm{dist}(u,\mathcal{X})
, where
\epsilon
is small and
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\mathcal{X})
denotes the distance from the zero section in
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
.

The second observation is that the difference between Floer’s original equation (
C.1
) and (
C.2
) is a perturbation term
P(u)
satisfying

|P(u)|\leq|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{A}(u)|\mathrm{dist}(u,\mathcal{X})

so, by the first observation,
Appendix C
applies to (
C.1
) as long as
\mathcal{A}
is
C^{2}
small. Another way of seeing the relationship between (
C.1
) and (
C.2
) is that these equations would agree if and only if the function
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}+i\mathcal{A}
on
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
were
J
-holomorphic. This is not the case. However, the difference
P(u)
between these two equations can be identified with
\overline{\partial}_{J}\mathcal{F}(u)
, whose magnitude is proportional to the distance from the zero section and the
C^{2}
norm of
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark C.3
.

From an infinite-dimensional viewpoint, the
J
-holomorphic strip equation is the gradient flow equation for the symplectic action functional on the space of paths
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to T^{*}\mathcal{X}
with boundary on the zero section. This functional is Morse–Bott with critical locus consisting of constant paths in the zero section. Floer’s equation (
C.1
) is obtained by perturbing the symplectic action functional by the function

\gamma\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}f(\gamma(\tau)){\mathrm{d}}\tau,

which restricts to a Morse function on the critical locus.

The non-standard equation (
C.2
) has a similar interpretation, as a non-gradient perturbation of the
J
-holomorphic strip equation by a vector field induced by
X_{\mathcal{H}}
. This vector field has the property that it is gradient-like on the space of paths and it restricts to a gradient vector field of
\mathcal{A}
along the critical locus. Therefore, it leads to well-defined Morse homology which recovers the usual intersection Floer homology of the zero section in
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
with itself. In general, equation (
C.2
) is a well-behaved perturbation of the
J
-holomorphic strip equation with boundary on a Lagrangian
L
in a symplectic manifold as long as the Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
is constant on
L
and its Hamiltonian vector field is gradient-like along
L
. If this is the case, modulo the usual bubbling issues, counting solutions to (
C.2
) leads to an alternative definition of the intersection Floer homology of
L
with itself.

Appendix D
Notation and conventions

There are many sign conventions in symplectic and hyperkähler geometry. Since signs play a crucial role in our discussion, we summarize below the notation and conventions used throughout this paper.

D.1
Coordinates

The standard coordinates on
[0,1]
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
will be denoted by
\tau
and
(s,t)
respectively, so that with natural orientations
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
has coordinates
(\tau,s,t)
and
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
has coordinates
(t,\tau)
. We identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{C}
using the holomorphic coordinate
z=s+it
, that is: the complex structure is given by
i\partial_{s}=\partial_{t}
. Similarly, we will equip
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
with the complex structure given by
i\partial_{t}=\partial_{\tau}
.

D.2
Symplectic geometry

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold. Given a function
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
, the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
is given by

\omega(\cdot,X_{H})={\mathrm{d}}H

A compatible almost complex structure
J
on
M
defines a metric
g
via

g(v,w)=\omega(v,Jw)

Thus,
X_{H}=J\nabla H
where
\nabla H
is the metric dual of
{\mathrm{d}}H
; indeed,

\omega(v,J\nabla H)=g(v,\nabla H)={\mathrm{d}}H(v).

Suppose now that
M=T^{*}X
is the cotangent bundle of a smooth manifold
X
. Let
\pi\colon T^{*}X\to X
be the projection map. The tautological
1
-form
\lambda
on
M
is defined by

\lambda_{\xi}=\pi^{*}\xi\quad\text{for }\xi\in T^{*}X.

If
q_{i}
are local coordinates on
X
and
p_{i}
are the dual coordinates on
T^{*}X
, then

\lambda=\sum_{i}p_{i}{\mathrm{d}}q_{i}.

The canonical symplectic form is

\omega={\mathrm{d}}\lambda=\sum_{i}{\mathrm{d}}p_{i}\wedge{\mathrm{d}}q_{i}.

These conventions reproduce Hamilton’s equations from classical mechanics. If
M=T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the Hamiltonian flow equation is

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{i}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\quad\frac{{\mathrm{d}}p_{i}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}.

Note that in this example, the standard almost complex structure on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
J\partial_{p_{i}}=\partial_{q_{i}}
, so
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
is identified with
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus i\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the opposite complex structure.

D.3
Lagrangian Floer theory

Let
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold as before and let
L_{0},L_{1}\subset M
be Lagrangian submanifolds. Suppose for simplicity that the symplectic form
\omega={\mathrm{d}}\lambda
on
M
is exact and
L_{0},L_{1}
are exact in the sense that there are functions
h_{i}\colon L_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda|_{L_{i}}={\mathrm{d}}h_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(M,L_{0},L_{1})
be the space of paths
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
starting on
L_{0}
and ending on
L_{1}
. The symplectic action functional perturbed by a Hamiltonian
H\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\lambda-h_{1}(\gamma(1))+h_{0}(\gamma(0))-\int_{0}^{1}H(\gamma(\tau)){\mathrm{d}}\tau.

The differential of
\mathcal{A}
at
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
is

{\mathrm{d}}\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}(v)=\int_{0}^{1}\omega\left(v,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}-X_{H}(\gamma)\right){\mathrm{d}}\tau\quad\text{for }v\in\Gamma([0,1],\gamma^{*}TM).

Therefore, the critical points of
\mathcal{A}
are Hamiltonian chords starting on
L_{0}
and ending on
L_{1}
. Let
J
be an almost complex structure on
M
compatible with
\omega
. The gradient of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the Riemannian metric induced by
J
is

\nabla\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=-J(\gamma)\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\gamma}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}-\nabla H(\gamma).

The gradient flow equation for
\mathcal{A}
is therefore the Floer equation:

\displaystyle u\colon\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]\to M,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+J(u)\partial_{\tau}u+\nabla H(u)=0,

or, equivalently,

\partial_{t}u+J(u)(\partial_{\tau}u-X_{H}(u))=0.

D.4
Complex gradient flow

Let
(X,I)
be an almost complex manifold. Let
F\colon X\to\mathbb{C}
be an
I
-holomorphic function. The complex gradient flow equation is

\displaystyle u\colon\mathbb{C}\to X,

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{I}u-i\nabla F(u)=0,

where
\overline{\partial}_{I}u
is the
I
-antilinear part of the differential
{\mathrm{d}}u
, which, after trivializing the bundle of
(0,1)
form on
\mathbb{C}
, we can identify with a
(0,1)
vector field on
X
. Here
\nabla F
is the complex vector field of type
(0,1)
dual to the
(1,0)
form
{\mathrm{d}}F
. If
F=f+ig
, with
f
and
g
real, then

\nabla F=\nabla f+i\nabla g.

In coordinates
(s,t)
on
\mathbb{C}
, interpreting
\overline{\partial}_{I}u
as a
(0,1)
vector field:

\overline{\partial}_{I}u=(\partial_{s}u+I(u)\partial_{t}u)+i(I(u)\partial_{s}u-\partial_{t}u)

so the complex gradient flow equation is

\partial_{s}u+I(u)\partial_{t}u+\nabla g(u)=0.

If
X
admits a symplectic from compatible with
I
, this is the Floer equation with the Hamiltonian perturbation by
g
. Let
X_{g}
be the Hamiltonian vector field. With our conventions, we have
X_{g}=I\nabla g=-\nabla f
, so the Hamiltonian trajectories of
g
are the gradient trajectories of
-f
. As
s\to\pm\infty
, we require that
u
converges to such trajectories.

Alternatively, we could consider the conjugate equation

\partial_{I}u+i\nabla\overline{F}(u)=0.

In coordinates,

\partial_{s}u-I(u)\partial_{t}u-\nabla g(u)=0,

or, equivalently,

\partial_{s}u-I(u)(\partial_{t}u+\nabla f(u))=0.

The antiholomorphic map
(s,t)\mapsto(-s,t)
induces a bijection between the sets of solutions to the gradient flow equation and its conjugate.

The complex gradient equation has a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
symmetry in the following sense. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
define
u_{\lambda}(z)=u(\lambda z)
; then
u
is a complex gradient trajectory of
F
if and only if
u_{\lambda}
is a complex gradient trajectory of
F_{\lambda}=\lambda F
:

\overline{\partial}_{I}u_{\lambda}-i\nabla F_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})=0.

Similarly,
u
is an anti-complex gradient trajectory of
F
if and only if
u_{\lambda}
is an anti-complex gradient trajectory of
F_{\bar{\lambda}}
.

D.5
Holomorphic Floer theory

Let
(M,I,J,K)
be a hyperkähler manifold with the corresponding symplectic forms
\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K}
. Let
L_{0},L_{1}\subset M
be submanifolds which are complex with respect to
I
and Lagrangian with respect to
\omega_{J}
and
\omega_{K}
. For simplicity, assume that all three symplectic forms are exact and that
L_{0},L_{1}
are exact Lagrangians.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(M,L_{0},L_{1})
be the space of paths in
M
starting on
L_{0}
and ending on
L_{1}
. This is an
I
-complex manifold. Let
\mathcal{A}_{J}
and
\mathcal{A}_{K}
be the symplectic action functionals with respect to
\omega_{J}
and
\omega_{K}
. The function

\mathscr{A}=\mathcal{A}_{J}+i\mathcal{A}_{K}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{C}

is
I
-holomorphic. The anti-complex gradient flow equation for maps
U\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathcal{P}

\partial_{I}U+i\nabla\overline{\mathscr{A}}(U)=0

is

\partial_{s}U-I(U)(\partial_{t}U+\nabla\mathcal{A}_{J}(U))=0,

which is equivalent to the Fueter equation for maps
U\colon[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M
,

\partial_{s}U-I(U)(\partial_{t}U-J(U)\partial_{\tau}U)=0,

or

I(U)\partial_{\tau}U+J(U)\partial_{s}U+K(U)\partial_{t}U=0.

As
s\to\pm\infty
we require that
U
converges to
J
-antiholomorphic strips from
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
with coordinates
(t,\tau)
to
M
.

The Fueter equation for maps
U\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to M
has an
\mathrm{SO}(3)
symmetry in the following sense. For
a\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
define
U_{a}=U\circ a^{-1}
and let
(I_{a},J_{a},K_{a})
be the quaternionic triple on
M
obtained by rotating
(I,J,K)
by
a
. Then the Fueter equation for
U
with respect to
(I,J,K)
is equivalent to the Fueter equation for
U_{a}
with respect to
(I_{a},J_{a},K_{a})
. For maps with domain
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the same holds for symmetries
a
in the subgroup
\mathrm{U}(1)\subset\mathrm{SO}(3)
preserving the
(s,t)
plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Explicitly, for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, define
z_{\theta}=s_{\theta}+it_{\theta}
in terms of
z=s+it
by
z_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta}z
and let

\displaystyle J_{\theta}+iK_{\theta}=e^{i\theta}(J+iK).

Then, the Fueter equation in coordinates
(\tau,s_{\theta},t_{\theta})
with respect to the triple
(I,J_{\theta},K_{\theta})
is equivalent to the Fueter equation in coordinates
(\tau,s,t)
with respect to the triple
(I,J,K)
.

We can perturb the holomorphic action functional
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}
by an
I
-holomorphic function
F\colon M\to\mathbb{C}
:

\mathscr{A}_{F}(\gamma)=\mathscr{A}(\gamma)+\int_{0}^{1}F(\gamma(\tau)){\mathrm{d}}\tau.

The resulting perturbed Fueter equation is

\partial_{s}U-I(U)(\partial_{t}U-J(U)\partial_{\tau}U+\nabla f(U))=0,

where
f=\mathrm{Re}(F)
. As before,
J
and
F
can be replaced by
J_{\theta}
and
F_{\theta}
for any
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
by writing the equation in coordinates
z_{\theta}=e^{-i\theta}z
.
