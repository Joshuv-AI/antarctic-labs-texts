The Generalized Fourier Transform: A Unified Framework for the Fourier, Laplace, Mellin and Z Z Transforms Journal: PRSA
Pushpendra Singh, Anubha Gupta, Shiv Dutt Joshi

Abstract
This paper introduces Generalized Fourier transform (GFT) that is an extension or the generalization of the Fourier transform (FT). The Unilateral Laplace transform (LT) is observed to be the special case of GFT. GFT, as proposed in this work, contributes significantly to the scholarly literature. There are many salient contribution of this work. Firstly, GFT is applicable to a much larger class of signals, some of which cannot be analyzed with FT and LT. For example, we have shown the applicability of GFT on the polynomially decaying functions and super exponentials. Secondly, we demonstrate the efficacy of GFT in solving the initial value problems (IVPs). Thirdly, the generalization presented for FT is extended for other integral transforms with examples shown for wavelet transform and cosine transform. Likewise, generalized Gamma function is also presented. One interesting application of GFT is the computation of generalized moments, for the otherwise non-finite moments, of any random variable such as the Cauchy random variable. Fourthly, we introduce Fourier scale transform (FST) that utilizes GFT with the topological isomorphism of an exponential map. Lastly, we propose Generalized Discrete-Time Fourier transform (GDTFT). The DTFT and unilateral z z -transform are shown to be the special cases of the proposed GDTFT. The properties of GFT and GDTFT have also been discussed.

x(t)
exists, if it is absolutely integrable, i.e.,
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
. There is a wide class of signals such as periodic sinusoidal signals, constant signals, and signum functions that do not satisfy this condition and hence, these signals are not Fourier transformable. This constraint is overcome by incorporating impulse functions within the framework of FT. This renders the condition of absolute integrability of FT as a sufficient, but not necessary condition. In other words, FT might exist even if this condition is not satisfied. FT is not only used to study signal properties, but also the system properties. It is also used in system modeling. In general, FT is very helpful in analyzing linear time-invariant (LTI) systems. Similar to signals, sometimes the impulse response of a system is not absolutely integrable. An implication of this is that the system is unstable. In order to study such systems, bilateral Laplace transform (BLT) was proposed
[

,

) and exponential (
e^{at}\,\mathrm{u}(t),\,a>0
) functions, where
\mathrm{u}(t)
is the unit step function. Furthermore, unilateral LT, simply known as the LT, is a special case of BLT, where the transform is computed only for one half of the real axis. In fact, BLT of functions such as polynomial and exponential functions, periodic functions, constant functions, and signum functions defined over the entire real line,
-\infty<t<\infty
, do not exist. While impulse function helped in computing the FT of many of the above stated functions (such as periodic functions), FT and BLT do not exist for almost all polynomially decaying functions (
1/t^{m},\,m\geq 1
). Hence, either the unilateral Laplace transform is used or the function is defined only for one half of the real line, such as
e^{at}\,\mathrm{u}(t),\,a>0
. In other words, one studies the causal LTI systems.
The above discussion motivated us to look for a better generalization than BLT because it appears that BLT itself is limited to a great extent. This study makes a significant scholarly contribution and defines generalized FT (GFT) that is a true generalized framework of FT. GFT eliminates all the above stated problems and provides an effective mathematical tool to deal with these issues. Eventually, it turns out that both FT and BLT are special cases of the proposed GFT.

The salient contributions of this study are as follows:

1.

We propose generalized Fourier transform that is a true generalization of FT and show that the unilateral LT is a special case of the proposed GFT.

2.

The proposed GFT can analyze and synthesize a much larger set of signals compared to the Fourier and Laplace transforms.

3.

Similar to Laplace transform, we demonstrate the efficacy of GFT in solving the initial value problems (IVPs). We also demonstrate the efficacy of GFT in solving those IVPs that cannot be solved using the LT.

4.

We define the Fourier scale transform (FST) from the GFT using the topological isomorphism of exponential map.

5.

Fourier theory has been used to solve differential equations. However, since inception of the FT (1807), there is a perception in the literature that the IVPs cannot be solved using the FT. We propose a solution to this issue and demonstrate the efficacy of the FT in solving the IVPs.

6.

We extend the GFT for the discrete-time systems and propose generalized discrete-time Fourier transform (GDTFT). The DTFT and unilateral
z
-transform are special cases of the proposed GDTFT. We also propose a solution of the difference equations with initial conditions using the DTFT, which is not available so far in the literature.

7.

Finally, this new proposed GFT simplifies both the continuous-time and discrete-time signal analysis. For example:
(i)
in case of GFT, the region of convergence (ROC) is the entire
s
-plane, if there are no poles; otherwise, it is always right-side of the rightmost pole and FT exists if the ROC includes imaginary axis.
(ii)
in case of GDTFT, the ROC is the entire
z
-plane, if there are no poles; otherwise, it is always outside of the outermost pole and DTFT exists if the ROC includes a unit circle.

Although, in this study we have provided theory related to one-dimensional signals, it can be easily extended for the multidimensional (e.g., image, video and time-space) signals. We have briefly mentioned the relevant equations regarding the same in Appendix-A. Rest of the study is organized as follows. In Section-2, we present the Generalized Fourier Transform, relation with FT, BLT, and LT, properties of GFT and solution of IVPs through GFT. In Section-3, we have extended this concept of GFT to the generalization of other Integral transforms, generalization of Gamma function, and discussed one application of GFT in the context of moment generating functions. The discrete-time counterpart, i.e., the generalized discrete-time Fourier transform (GDTFT) and its properties are discussed in Section-4. In the end, some concluding remarks along with the future directions are presented in Section-5.

2
The proposed Generalized Fourier Transform

The pair of Fourier transform (FT) and inverse FT (IFT) of a signal
x(t)
, which satisfies the Dirichlet conditions, is defined as

\displaystyle X(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad-\infty<\omega<\infty

(1)

\displaystyle\text{and \,\,}x(t)

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(\omega)\exp(j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega,\quad-\infty<t<\infty,

subject to the existence of the above integrals. This time-frequency pair is also denoted by
x(t)\rightleftharpoons X(f)
, where
\omega=2\pi f
,
\omega
denotes the angular frequency in radians/sec, and
f
denotes the frequency in Hz.
The bilateral Laplace transform (BLT), a generalization of FT, and its inverse are defined as

\displaystyle X(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-s\, t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(2)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad x(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi j}\int_{\sigma-j\infty}^{\sigma+j\infty}X(s)\exp(s\, t)\,\mathrm{d}s,

respectively, where the symbol ‘
s
’ denotes the complex frequency
s=\sigma+j\omega
. BLT is the FT of the signal multiplied with exponentially decaying function. In other words, it is the FT of
x(t)e^{-\sigma t}
.
The unilateral LT is the special case of BLT, when the considered signals (or impulse response of linear time-invariant systems) are causal, i.e., time support is only for
0\leq t<\infty
and hence,
X(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-s t)\,\mathrm{d}t
.
The signum (‘
sgn
’) and unit step functions are defined
[
6
]
as

sgn(t)=\begin{cases}1,\quad t>0,\\
0,\quad t=0,\\
-1\quad t<0.\end{cases}\quad\text{ and, }\quad u(t)=\begin{cases}1,\quad t\geq 0,\\
0,\quad t<0,\end{cases}\quad\text{ or }\quad u(t)=\begin{cases}1,\quad t>0,\\
\frac{1}{2},\quad t=0,\\
0,\quad t<0.\end{cases}

(3)

If two functions have identical values except at a countable number of points, their integration is also identical because Lebesgue measure of a set of countable points is always zero. These functions are said to belong to the same equivalent class of functions. In other words, from the theory of integral calculus, both the above definitions of the unit step function in (
3
) are equivalent.

Motivated with the theory of Fourier transform (
1
) and the Laplace transform (
2
), we define a generalized version of the FT, namely the generalized Fourier transform (GFT)
X(\omega,\sigma,\tau)
of a signal
x(t)
, as

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,h(\sigma,\tau,t)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(4)

\displaystyle x(t)\,h(\sigma,\tau,t)

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(\omega,\sigma,\tau)\exp(j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

A number of interesting special cases arise out of GFT, given as below:

1.

Fourier transform (FT): if
h(\sigma,\tau,t)=1
.

2.

Fourier Cosine transform: if
h(\sigma,\tau,t)=1
and
x(t)
is an even function.

3.

Fourier Sine transform: if
h(\sigma,\tau,t)=1
and
x(t)
is an odd function.

4.

Short-time Fourier transform (STFT): if
h(\sigma,\tau,t)=w(t-\tau)
, where
w(t)
is a window function.

5.

Unilateral LT (in short LT): if
h(\sigma,\tau,t)=\exp(-\sigma t)\mathrm{u}(t)
and
\sigma>c
, where
c
is a constant that depends on the signal
x(t)
.

6.

Bilateral LT: if
h(\sigma,\tau,t)=\exp(-\sigma t)
, where
\mathrm{u}(t)
is the unit step function as defined in (
3
) and
\sigma>c
, where
c
is a constant that depends on the signal
x(t)
.

7.

Generalized Fourier transform (GFT): if
h(\sigma,\tau,t,p)=(\text{sgn}(t)\,t)^{p}\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)
, where
\text{sgn}(t)
is the sign function (
3
). We designate this proposed representation as GFT. The development of this transform is the fundamental contribution of this study.

2.1
The GFT and its relation with Fourier and Laplace transforms

Our first aim of this study is to define and explore the GFT of a signal
x(t)
, hereby, presented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x(t)\}=X(\omega,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(5)

where the value of
\sigma
is such that the integral (
5
) converges to a finite value, i.e.,
\sigma\in\text{ROC}
or the region of convergence that depends on the signal
x(t)
. We also denote (
5
) as
X(\omega,\sigma)=X(s,s^{*})
where complex frequency
s=\sigma+j\omega
, its complex conjugate
s^{*}=\sigma-j\omega
, and
\text{sgn}(t)\,t=|t|

Using the ‘sgn’ function as defined in (
3

5

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}x(t)\exp(\sigma t)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-\sigma t)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(6)

We further simplify (
6

\displaystyle X(s,s^{*})=\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})+X(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(-t)\exp(-s^* t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-s t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(7)

where

\displaystyle X(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\mathrm{u}(t)\exp(-s t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-s t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(8)

and
\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})
is the new term, designated as the complementary LT (CLT), and hereby defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,\mathrm{u}(-t)\exp(s^* t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{0}x(t)\exp(s^* t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(-t)\exp(-s^* t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(9)

If there is a delta function at the origin, it can be included either in (
8
) or (
9

From (
5

8
) and (
9
), the following observations are in order:

1.

If
x(t)
is an even function,
X(s^{*})=\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})
.

2.

If
x(t)
is an odd function,
X(s^{*})=-\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})
.

3.

If function

is a sum of the even and odd functions, i.e.,

\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})=\mathfrak{X}_{e}(s^{*})+\mathfrak{X}_{o}(s^{*})
.

4.

If
\sigma=0

5
) becomes the FT (
1
), provided FT exists for the given signal.

5.

If
x(t)=0
for

, then GFT (

6.

If we simply consider

in (

Using the IFT (

\displaystyle x(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(\omega,\sigma)\exp(j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

(10)

One simple way to recover/synthesize the original signal

is by evaluating (

) at

\displaystyle x(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(\omega,\sigma)\exp(\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\exp(j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega,\quad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R},

On substituting

where the value of

2.2
Properties of the GFT

Remark 2.
It is pertinent to note that all the properties of the FT are valid for the proposed GFT.
For example, if we consider two signals and their GFTs using FT as:
x_{1}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\rightleftharpoons X_{1}(\omega,\sigma)
and
x_{2}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t)\, t)\rightleftharpoons X_{2}(\omega,\sigma)
, then,

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\times x_{2}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\rightleftharpoons\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[X_{1}(\omega,\sigma)\star X_{2}(\omega,\sigma)\right],

\displaystyle\left[x_{1}(t)\times x_{2}(t)\right]\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\rightleftharpoons\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[X_{1}(\omega,\sigma_{1})\star X_{2}(\omega,\sigma_{2})\right],

(15)

where
\star
is the convolution operation and,
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are suitably selected such that
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sigma
, yielding one degree of freedom in choosing the attenuation parameter depending on the application in hand. Similarly, we can show that

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\star x_{2}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\rightleftharpoons X_{1}(\omega,\sigma)\times X_{2}(\omega,\sigma),

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)\star x_{2}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\rightleftharpoons X_{1}(\omega,0)\times X_{2}(\omega,\sigma),

(16)

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(t) \, t)\star x_{2}(t)\rightleftharpoons X_{1}(\omega,\sigma)\times X_{2}(\omega,0).

Using these properties of (
15
) and (
16
), we can study those linear time-invariant (LTI) systems whose impulse response is not absolutely integrable. That is, we can analyze unstable systems by considering the range of damping factor
\sigma
and capture the behavior of systems by taking
\lim\sigma\to 0.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\tilde{x}(t)
be a periodic function of period
T
, i.e.,
\tilde{x}(t+T)=\tilde{x}(t),\,\forall\,t
. If GFT of
\tilde{x}(t)
exists, then

\displaystyle\tilde{X}(s,s^{*})=\left(\frac{e^{-s^{*}T}}{1-e^{-s^{*}T}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{x}(t)e^{s^{*}t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-sT}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{x}(t)e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\text{for}\quad\text{Re}\{s\}>0.

(17)

Proof.

Let
x(t)
be a time-limited signal defined as

x(t)=\begin{cases}\tilde{x}(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(18)

where
\tilde{x}(t)
is a periodic signal that can also be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{x}(t)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}x(t-mT)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{-1}x(t-mT)+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}x(t-mT).

(19)

The GFT of (
19
) yields

\displaystyle\tilde{X}(s,s^{*})

\displaystyle=\left(\dots+\int_{-3T}^{-2T}x(t+3T)e^{s^{*}t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{-2T}^{-T}x(t+2T)e^{s^{*}t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{-T}^{0}x(t+T)e^{s^{*}t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{0}^{T}x(t)e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{T}^{2T}x(t-T)e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{2T}^{3T}x(t-2T)e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t+\dots\right),

(20)

which can be simplified as

\displaystyle\tilde{X}(s,s^{*})=\left(\dots+e^{-3s^{*}T}+e^{-2s^{*}T}+e^{-s^{*}T}\right)\int_{0}^{T}x(t)e^{s^{*}t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(1+e^{-sT}+e^{-2sT}+\dots\right)\int_{0}^{T}x(t)e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(21)

Thus, we obtain (
17
),
which completes the proof.

1.

Linearity:

\displaystyle\text{ if }\quad\mathcal{G}\{x(t)\}

\displaystyle=X(s,s^{*})=\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})+X(s),\quad\text{ with ROC }R

2.3
Solution of IVPs using the proposed GFT

In this subsection, we demonstrate the utility of GFT for solving the initial value problems (IVP’s). Let us consider the IVP as

\displaystyle x^{\prime\prime}(t)+\omega_{c}^{2}\,x(t)=c_{1}\,\delta(t-t_{0})+c_{2}\,\delta(t+t_{0}),\quad x(0)=a_{1},\,x^{\prime}(0)=a_{2}\,\omega_{c},\,t_{0}\geq 0,

(42)

where constants
a_{1},\,a_{2},\,c_{1},\,c_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
,
t,\,\omega_{c}\in\mathbb{R},\,\omega_{c}\neq 0
. Using the differentiation property of the proposed GFT (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle s^{2}X(s)

\displaystyle-s\,x(0)-x^{\prime}(0)+\omega_{c}^{2}\,X(s)=c_{1}\,e^{-t_{0}\,s},\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0

(43)

\displaystyle X(s)

\displaystyle=a_{1}\,\frac{s}{s^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}+a_{2}\,\frac{\omega_{c}}{s^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}+c_{1}\,e^{-st_{0}}\frac{1}{s^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}},\quad\text{ for }t\geq 0,

(44)

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\left[a_{1}\,\cos(\omega_c t)+a_{2}\,\sin(\omega_c t)\right]\mathrm{u}(t)+\frac{c_{1}}{\omega_{c}}\,\sin\left((t-t_{0})\omega_{c}\right)\,\mathrm{u}(t-t_{0}),

(45)

and

\displaystyle(s^{*})^{2}\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})

\displaystyle-s^{*}\,x(0)+x^{\prime}(0)+\omega_{c}^{2}\,\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})=c_{2}\,e^{-t_{0}\,s^{*}},\text{ for }t\leq 0,

(46)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}(s^{*})

\displaystyle=a_{1}\,\frac{s^{*}}{(s^{*})^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}-a_{2}\,\frac{\omega_{c}}{(s^{*})^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}}+c_{2}\,e^{-t_{0}\,s^{*}}\frac{1}{(s^{*})^{2}+\omega_{c}^{2}},\quad\text{ for }t\leq 0,

(47)

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\left[a_{1}\,\cos(\omega_c t)+a_{2}\,\sin(\omega_c t)\right]\mathrm{u}(-t)-\frac{c_{2}}{\omega_{c}}\,\sin\left((t+t_{0})\omega_{c}\right)\mathrm{u}(-t-t_{0}).

(48)

Therefore, from (
45
) and (
48
), we can write,
x(t)
,
\forall t\in\mathbb{R}
, as

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=a_{1}\,\cos(\omega_c t)+a_{2}\,\sin(\omega_c t)+\frac{c_{1}}{\omega_{c}}\,\sin\left((t-t_{0})\omega_{c}\right)\mathrm{u}(t-t_{0})-\frac{c_{2}}{\omega_{c}}\,\sin\left((t+t_{0})\omega_{c}\right)\mathrm{u}(-t-t_{0}).

(49)

It is to be noted that this kind of IVP’s cannot be solved using the traditional Fourier and Laplace transforms.

2.3.1
Solution of the IVPs using the FT

We, hereby, observe that when
\sigma=0
(or
\lim\sigma\to 0
), then GFT will become the FT with
s=j\omega
,
s^{*}=-j\omega
and thus, FT can be used for finding the solution of IVPs by adopting the differentiation property discussed in (
26
), provided the FT of the functions under consideration exist. Thus, to obtain the solution of IVPs, we derive and propose the following derivative property of the FT as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x^{\prime}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[s\mathfrak{X}(s)+x(0)\right]+\left[sX(s)-x(0)\right],

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x^{\prime\prime}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[s^{2}\mathfrak{X}(s)+s\,x(0)+x^{\prime}(0)\right]+\left[s^{2}X(s)-s\,x(0)-x^{\prime}(0)\right],

(51)

and for the
m
th derivatives, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x^{(m)}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[s^{m}\mathfrak{X}(s)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}s^{(m-i)}x^{(i-1)}(0)\right]+\left[s^{m}X(s)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}s^{(m-i)}x^{(i-1)}(0)\right],

(52)

where,
s=j\omega
,
\mathfrak{X}(s)=\mathfrak{X}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}x(t)\,e^{-j\omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t
,
X(s)=X(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)\,e^{-j\omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t
,
x^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x(t)\Big|_{0}
and
x^{(m)}(0)=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}x(t)\Big|_{0}
. Here, the main observation from (
50
), (
51
) and (
52
) is that, in general, the initial conditions for positive value of time cancel the initial conditions for negative value of time. This is the main difficulty in solving the IVPs in the traditional way using the FT. Thus, to solve the IVPs, we should avoid the cancellation and retain the initial conditions as presented in (
50
), (
51
) and (
52
).

2.4
Generalized Fourier transform and polynomially decaying functions

The Fourier and Laplace transforms of the polynomially decaying functions do not exist. Therefore, in order to tackle the polynomial decay in the function, our next goal is to define and explore the generalized Fourier transform (GFT) as

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma,p)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,|t|^{p}\exp(-\sigma\, |t|)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(53)

which can be written as

\displaystyle X(s,s^{*},p)=\mathfrak{X}(s^{*},p)+X(s,p)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(-t)\,t^{p}\exp(-s^* t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)\,t^{p}\exp(-s t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(54)

Using the proposed definition (
53
), we can easily include the polynomially decaying class of functions.
For example, considering
x(t)=\frac{1}{t^{m}},\,m\geq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle X(s,s^{*},p)=\Gamma(p-m+1)\left[\frac{1}{(-1)^{m}}\frac{1}{(s^{*})^{p-m+1}}+\frac{1}{s^{p-m+1}}\right],\quad p>m-1.

(55)

To derive the Fourier and Laplace transforms of the HT kernel, we set
m=1
in (
55
) and obtain

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma,p)=\Gamma(p)\left[\frac{1}{s^{p}}-\frac{1}{(s^{*})^{p}}\right]=\Gamma(p)\left[\frac{(\sigma-j\omega)^{p}-(\sigma+j\omega)^{p}}{(\sigma^{2}+\omega^{2})^{p}}\right].

(56)

We can further simplify (
56
) by considering
\lim\sigma\to 0
and obtain

\displaystyle X(\omega,p)=\Gamma(p)\left[\frac{(-j\,\omega)^{p}-(j\,\omega)^{p}}{\omega^{2p}}\right],

(57)

which can be written as

\displaystyle X(\omega,p)=\Gamma(p)\frac{(-2j)}{\omega^{p}}\sin\left(\frac{p\pi}{2}\right),\,\omega>0\text{ and }X(\omega,p)=\Gamma(p)\frac{2j}{\omega^{p}}\sin\left(\frac{p\pi}{2}\right),\,\omega<0.

(58)

In (
57
), for
p=1
,
X(\omega)=-2j/\omega
, which is the FT of the signum function. From (
58
) we observe that,
\lim_{p\to 0}X(\omega,p)=-j\,\pi\,\text{sgn}(\omega)
. Thus, we obtain the FT of the HT kernel as
\frac{1}{\pi t}\rightleftharpoons-j\,\text{sgn}(\omega)
. This is the direct derivation of the FT of the HT kernel without using the duality property.

2.5
The GFT of super-exponential functions

The Fourier and Laplace transforms of more than exponentially growing functions (such as
e^{at^{m}},\,m>1,\,a>0
) do not exist.
Therefore, to include this class of functions, we define GFT as

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma,p,q)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,|t|^{p}\exp(-\sigma\, |t|^q)\exp(-j\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad p\geq 0,\,q\geq 0.

(59)

Using the proposed definition (
59
), we can easily include the class of functions growing at a rate faster than the exponential functions. However, this may be limited in applications because evaluation of such integrals would be quite tedious. Although GFT (
59
) is quite general, it is not sufficient to deal with the class of functions that explodes in a limited time support such as
\tan(\omega t)
,
\cot(\omega t)
,
\sec(\omega t)
,
\csc(\omega t)
, and
e^{1/(1-t)}
.

We can also obtain the GFT of a function
x(t)
by using the Taylor series expansion (TSE) and computing the GFT of each term of the series, provided the resultant series
X(s,s^{*})
in the GFT domain converges. For example, we consider the TSE of
x(t)=e^{t}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}t^{m}
and obtain its GFT as
\mathcal{G}\{e^{t}\}=X(s,s^{*})=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(s^{*})^{m+1}}+\frac{1}{s^{m+1}}\right]=\frac{1}{s^{*}+1}+\frac{1}{s-1},\quad\text{Re}\{s\}>0
and
|s|>1
, or
\text{Re}\{s\}>1
.

3.1
Integral transforms

The integral transform (IT) of a function
x(t)
is defined as

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma)=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}x(t)\,K(\omega,\sigma,t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(60)

where
K(\omega,\sigma,t)
is the kernel of the considered IT. The original signal
x(t)
can be recovered, if there exists an inverse kernel
K^{-1}(\omega,\sigma,t)

\displaystyle x(t)=\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}X(\omega,\sigma)\,K^{-1}(\omega,\sigma,t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

(61)

The IT (
60

61

4

\displaystyle X(\omega,\sigma)=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}x(t)\,h(\sigma,t)\,K(\omega,\sigma,t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(62)

\displaystyle x(t)\,h(\sigma,t)=\int_{u_{1}}^{u_{2}}X(\omega,\sigma)\,K^{-1}(\omega,\sigma,t)\,\mathrm{d}\omega,

(63)

where we may chose, e.g.,
h(\sigma,t)=\exp(-\sigma|t|)
in such a fashion that IT exists for the larger class of functions. There are many integral transforms such as fractional FT (FrFT), continuous wavelet transform (CWT), linear canonical transform (LCT), Abel transform, Fourier sine and cosine transform, Hankel transform, Hartley transform, Hermite transform, Hilbert transform, Jacobi transform, Laguerre transform, Legendre transform, Mellin transform, Poisson kernel, Radon Transform, Weierstrass transform, S-transform, etc. Similar to the GFT (
5
) and (
53
), we can extend the proposed methodology and include that class of signals which are not in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}),\,p\in(0,\infty)
. For example, the CWT of a signal
x(t)
is defined if
x(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and the original can be recovered by inverse CWT
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. We hereby define the CWT of a signal
x(t)
such that
x(t)\not\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
x(t)\exp(-\sigma\, |t|)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for some
\sigma>\sigma_{0}
as

\displaystyle X_{\psi}(a,b,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\exp(-\sigma\, |t|)\,\psi^{*}_{a,b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad a\neq 0,

(64)

and
X_{\psi}(a,b,\sigma)=0
for
a=0
, where
\psi_{a,b}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a|}}\psi\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)
is a family of daughter wavelets and
\psi(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a mother wavelet function with zero-mean and finite energy. The original signal

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\sigma\, |t|)}{C_{\psi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X_{\psi}(a,b,\sigma)\,\psi_{a,b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}b\frac{1}{a^{2}}\mathrm{d}a,\qquad C_{\psi}\neq 0,

(66)

We present another example of an integral transform where the Fourier cosine transform (FCT) and inverse FCT pairs can be defined for the larger classes of signals as

\displaystyle X_{c}(\omega,\sigma)

3.2
Fourier Scale Transform (FST)

The Mellin transform (MT) and inverse MT (IMT) are derived from the BLT (
2
) by the change of the variable as
e^{-t}=\tau\implies t=-\ln(\tau)
and is defined as

\displaystyle Y(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}y(\tau)\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(69)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad y(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi j}\int_{\sigma-j\infty}^{\sigma+j\infty}Y(s)\,\tau^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
x(-\ln(\tau))=y(\tau)
. We can define the lower and upper partial MT as

\displaystyle Y(s)=Y_{L}(s,\tau_{c})+Y_{U}(s,\tau_{c})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{c}}y(\tau)\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\tau_{c}}^{\infty}y(\tau)\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(70)

The MT and IMT presented in (
69
) are essentially Fourier/Laplace counterparts processed through an isomorphism. MT is a different realization of the BLT by the topological isomorphic exponential mapping,
\exp\colon(\mathbb{R,+})\to(\mathbb{R^{+},\cdot})
. Therefore all the limitations of the BLT, as already discussed, are enforced to the MT as well. For example, MT of a simple function
y(\tau)=1
does not exists.

To overcome these limitations, using the GFT (
5
), by the change of the variable as
e^{-t}=\tau\implies t=-\ln(\tau)
, we define the FST and inverse FST (IFST) as

\displaystyle Y(s,s^{*})=Y_{L}(s,1)+Y_{U}(-s^{*},1)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}y(\tau)\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{1}^{\infty}y(\tau)\,\tau^{-s^{*}-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(71)

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi j}\int_{\sigma-j\infty}^{\sigma+j\infty}Y_{L}(s,1)\,\tau^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad 0\leq t\leq 1,

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad y(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi j}\int_{\sigma-j\infty}^{\sigma+j\infty}Y_{U}(-s^{*},1)\,\tau^{s^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad 1\leq t<\infty.

Here, we consider a very interesting example of the MT. The gamma function is the MT of
y(\tau)=e^{-\tau}
and is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau}\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau=(s-1)\Gamma(s-1),

(72)

which can be written as the sum of the lower and upper partial gamma functions as

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)=\Gamma_{L}(s,\tau_{c})+\Gamma_{U}(s,\tau_{c})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{c}}e^{-\tau}\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\tau_{c}}^{\infty}e^{-\tau}\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(73)

The gamma function (
72
) can also be realized by the BLT of the function
x(t)=e^{-e^{-t}}
as

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-e^{-t}}\,e^{-s\,t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\Gamma(s+1)}{s},\quad s\neq 0.

(74)

Next, we consider the FST of the function
y(\tau)=e^{-\tau}
and obtain

\displaystyle Y(s,s^{*})=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\tau}\,\tau^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-\tau}\,\tau^{-s^{*}-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(75)

which can be written in terms of the incomplete gamma functions as

\displaystyle Y(s,s^{*})=\Gamma_{L}(s,1)+\Gamma_{U}(-s^{*},1).

(76)

Next, we derive the properties of the proposed FST that are listed below.

1.

Scaling:

\displaystyle\text{ if }\quad\mathcal{F}\{y(t)\}

\displaystyle=Y_{L}(s,1)+Y_{U}(-s^{*},1),

\displaystyle\text{ then }\quad\mathcal{F}\{y(at)\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a^{s}}Y_{L}(s,1)+a^{s^{*}}Y_{U}(-s^{*},1).

(77)

2.

Multiplication by
t^{m}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{t^{m}y(t)\}

\displaystyle=Y_{L}(m+s,1)+Y_{U}(m-s^{*},1),

(78)

which implies,
s\mapsto s+m
, and,
s^{*}\mapsto s^{*}-m
.

3.

The FST of derivatives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{y^{\prime}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[y(1)-(s-1)Y_{L}(s-1,1)\right]+\left[(s^{*}+1)Y_{U}(-s^{*}-1,1)-y(1)\right],

(79)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{y^{\prime\prime}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[y^{\prime}(1)-(s-1)y(1)+(s-1)(s-2)Y_{L}(s-2,1)\right]

\displaystyle+\left[-y^{\prime}(1)-(s^{*}+1)y(1)+(s^{*}+1)(s^{*}+2)Y_{U}(-s^{*}-2,1)\right],

(80)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{ty^{\prime}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[y(1)-sY_{L}(s,1)\right]+\left[s^{*}Y_{U}(-s^{*},1)-y(1)\right],

(81)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{t^{2}y^{\prime\prime}(t)\}

\displaystyle=\left[y^{\prime}(1)-(s+1)y(1)+s(s+1)Y_{L}(s,1)\right]

\displaystyle+\left[-y^{\prime}(1)-(s^{*}-1)y(1)+s^{*}(s^{*}-1)Y_{U}(-s^{*},1)\right].

(82)

It is also observed that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial{s}^{m}}Y(s,s^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\ln{t})^{m}\,y(t)\,t^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}t,

(83)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\quad\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial{s^{*}}^{m}}Y(s,s^{*})

(84)

3.3
Generalized Gamma function

The gamma function was introduced by Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) to generalize the factorial to non integer values. The gamma function (GF) is defined (Euler, 1730) as

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\,e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\text{Re}\{s\}>0,

(85)

where the above integral converges for
t\in[0,\infty)
.
Using the iteration of the gamma identity as
\Gamma(s+1)=s\Gamma(s)
, this function can be defined on the entire real axis and can be extended to the entire complex plane except for
s=0
and for
s
as negative integers, i.e.,
(s=0,-1,-2,\dots)

[
20
]
.
Motivated from above and the theory of GFT, we define the generalized gamma function (GGF) as

\displaystyle G(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\,e^{-\text{sgn}(t)\,t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\text{Re}\{s\}>0,

(86)

where the above integral converges for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The GGF (
86
) can be written as

\displaystyle G(s)=\Gamma_{c}(s)+\Gamma(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(-t)^{s-1}\,e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\,e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

(87)

where we can write complementary GF as
\Gamma_{c}(s)=(-1)^{(s-1)}\Gamma(s)=e^{j\pi(s-1)}\Gamma(s)
. Thus,
|\Gamma_{c}(s)|=|\Gamma(s)|
. The original GF
\Gamma(s)
has no zeroes. It has simple poles at zero and at the negative integers (i.e.,
s=0,-1,-2,-3,\dots
). However, the proposed GGF
G(s)=\left[(-1)^{s-1}+1\right]\Gamma(s)
has zeros that occur for even numbers, i.e., for
s=2m,\,m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
G(0)=0
; and
G(s)=2\Gamma(s)
for
s=2m+1,\,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. It has poles at negative odd integers. For all other values of
s
, it is a complex-valued function. Thus, the GGF is defined over the entire complex plane except at the negative odd integers.

3.4
Application: Moment generating functions

Using the traditional LT, the moment generating function (MGFs) are defined only for the pdfs of positive random variables and for finite duration pdfs (such as the uniform pdf). Since all random variables do not have finite moments, MGFs do not always exist. For example, consider a Cauchy random variable, whose moments are not defined because the integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{y^{m}}{1+y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad m=1,2,\dots,M

(88)

do not converge.
In this Subsection, using the theory of GFT, we show that MGF always exists, even for random variables with non-finite moments.

The moments of a random variable
Y
are defined as

\displaystyle E\{Y^{m}\}=\mathcal{M}_{m}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y^{m}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad m=1,2,\dots,M,

(89)

that can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}=\mathcal{M}_{nm}+\mathcal{M}_{pm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(-y)^{m}f(-y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{m}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

(90)

where
\mathcal{M}_{nm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(-y)^{m}f(-y)\,\mathrm{d}y
,
\mathcal{M}_{pm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{m}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
,
f(y)
is the probability density function (pdf), and
E
is the expectation operator.
There are many heavy-tailed pdfs whose moments are not finite. To deal with this issue, we define a new moment generating function
as

\displaystyle M_{Y}(\sigma,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)\exp(-\sigma|y|)\exp(-j\omega y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

(91)

that can be further simplified as

\displaystyle M_{Y}(s^{*},s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(-y)\exp(-s^* y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y)\exp(-s y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(92)

From (
92
), we observe the following moment generation property

\displaystyle M_{Y}(s^{*},s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(s^{*})^{m}}{m!}\mathcal{M}_{nm}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{s^{m}}{m!}\mathcal{M}_{pm}.

(93)

Thus,
the proposed MGFs (
93
) always exist. Similarly, for a Cauchy random variable, we can define other kind of moments as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{y^{m}\exp(-\sigma|y|)}{1+y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad\sigma>0,

(94)

which can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[(-1)^{m}+1\right]\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{m}\exp(-\sigma y)}{1+y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{m}\exp(-\sigma y)}{1+y^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad\text{ for }m=2,4,6,\dots,

(95)

and it is zero for the odd values of
m
. The integral (
95
) converges for
m>1
and its solution can be written in terms of the generalized hypergeometric function.

Generalized discrete time Fourier transform (GDTFT)

The discrete time Fourier transform (DTFT) and inverse DTFT pairs of a signal
x[n]
are defined as

\displaystyle X(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\exp(-j\Omega n),

(96)

\displaystyle x[n]

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(\Omega)\exp(j\Omega n)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega.

Corresponding to GFT, we hereby define the generalized discrete time Fourier transform (GDTFT) as

\displaystyle X(\Omega,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(n)\, n)\exp(-j\Omega n),

(97)

which can be written as

\displaystyle X(\Omega,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}x[n]\exp((\sigma-j\Omega) n)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x[n]\exp(-(\sigma+j\Omega) n),

(98)

\displaystyle X(z,z^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})+X(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x[-n](z^{*})^{-n}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x[n]z^{-n},

(99)

where
X(z)
is the unilateral
z
-transform defined as

X(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x[n]z^{-n},

(100)

and complementary
z
-transform
\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})
is hereby defined as

\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}x[n](z^{*})^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x[-n](z^{*})^{-n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x[-n](z^{*})^{-n}-x[0],

(101)

where
z=e^{\sigma+j\Omega}=re^{j\Omega}
, its complex conjugate
z^{*}=e^{\sigma-j\Omega}=re^{-j\Omega}
,
r=e^{\sigma}
. For
\sigma=0\implies r=1
, GFZT becomes the discrete time Fourier transform (DTFT).
The bilateral
z
-transform (BL
z
T) is defined as

X_{B}(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]z^{-n}.

(102)

We can obtain the original signal from the GDTFT by using the inverse GDTFT as

\displaystyle x[n]\exp(-\sigma\, \sgn(n)\,n)

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(\Omega,\sigma)\exp(j\Omega n)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(103)

and then evaluating (
103
) with
\lim\sigma\to 0
.

4.1
Properties of the GDTFT

We present the following properties of the GDTFT:

1.

Linearity:

\displaystyle\text{ if }\quad\mathcal{G}\{x[n]\}

\displaystyle=X(z,z^{*})=\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})+X(z),

(104)

\displaystyle\text{ then }\quad\mathcal{G}\left\{\sum_{i}a_{i}x_{i}[n]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}a_{i}X_{i}(z,z^{*})=\sum_{i}a_{i}\left[\mathfrak{X}_{i}(z^{*})+X_{i}(z)\right],\quad a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}.

(105)

2.

Time delay: We can write
x[n]=x[n]\,\mathrm{u}[-n-1]+x[n]\,\mathrm{u}[n]
and hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x[n+n_{0}]\,\mathrm{u}[-n-1-n_{0}]+x[n-n_{0}]\,\mathrm{u}[n-n_{0}]\}

\displaystyle={(z^{*})^{-n_{0}}}\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})+z^{-n_{0}}X(z),\quad n_{0}\geq 0,

(106)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x[n-m]\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{-\infty}^{-1}x[n-m)\,{(z^{*})^{n}}+\sum_{0}^{\infty}x[n-m]\,z^{-n},\quad m\geq 0,

\displaystyle={(z^{*})^{m}}\sum_{-\infty}^{-1-m}x[n]\,{(z^{*})^{n}}+{z^{-m}}\sum_{-m}^{\infty}x[n]\,z^{-n},

(107)

\displaystyle=\left[-\sum_{\ell=-m}^{-1}x[\ell]\,(z*)^{\ell+m}+(z*)^{m}X(z*)\right]+\left[\sum_{\ell=-m}^{-1}x[\ell]\,z^{-\ell-m}+z^{-m}X(z)\right].

(108)

3.

Time advance:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x[n+m]\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{-\infty}^{-1}x[n+m)\,{(z^{*})^{n}}+\sum_{0}^{\infty}x[n+m]\,z^{-n},\quad m\geq 0,

\displaystyle={(z^{*})^{-m}}\sum_{-\infty}^{-1+m}x[n]\,{(z^{*})^{n}}+{z^{m}}\sum_{m}^{\infty}x[n]\,z^{-n},

(109)

\displaystyle=\left[\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}x[\ell]\,(z*)^{\ell-m}+(z*)^{-m}X(z*)\right]+\left[-\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}x[\ell]\,z^{-\ell+m}+z^{m}X(z)\right].

(110)

4.

Time reversal:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x[-n]\}

\displaystyle=X(z^{*})+\mathfrak{X}(z).

(111)

5.

Time expansion (scaling):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x[n/k]\}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{X}\left((z^{*})^{k}\right)+X\left(z^{k}\right),\quad k\geq 1.

(112)

6.

The GDTFT of successive time difference:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{x[n]-x[n-1]\}

\displaystyle=\left[(1-z^{*})\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})+x(-1)\right]+\left[(1-z^{-1})X(z)-x(-1)\right].

(113)

7.

The GDTFT of accumulation across time:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\left\{\sum_{-\infty}^{n}x[k]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathfrak{X}\left(z^{*}\right)}{1-z^{*}}+\frac{X\left(z\right)}{1-z^{-1}}.

(114)

8.

The GDTFT of
x[n]
multiplied by various functions, i.e., amplitude scaling:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{a^{-n}\,x[n]\}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{X}(a^{-1}z^{*})+X(az),

(115)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{a^{-|n|}\,x[n]\}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{X}(az^{*})+X(az),

(116)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{n^{m}\,x(t)\}

\displaystyle={(z^{*})}^{-m}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}{z^{*}}^{m}}\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})+(-z)^{m}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}z^{m}}X(z),

(117)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\{|n|^{m}\,x[n]\}

\displaystyle=(-z^{*})^{-m}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}{z^{*}}^{m}}\mathfrak{X}(z^{*})+(-z)^{m}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}z^{m}}X(z).

(118)

5
Conclusion

Important and fundamental contributions of this study are the introduction of the generalized Fourier transform (GFT) in both continuous-time and discrete-time domains, the Fourier scale transform, and the solution of initial value problem using the GFT and Fourier transform.
This work has presented the GFT, its properties, discrete-time version, comparison with Laplace and
z
-transforms, along with many interesting aspects including solution of initial value problems. The most interesting aspect of this newly proposed GFT is that it overcomes the limitations generally observed in Fourier, Laplace and
z
transforms. It is applicable to a much larger class of signals and simplifies the analysis of both continuous-time and discrete-time signals and systems. This transform may find greater utility in applications in the near future.
