Abstract
Large-scale MIMO systems with a massive number N N of individually controlled antennas pose significant challenges for MMSE ( MMSE ) channel estimation, based on uplink pilots. The major ones arise from the computational complexity, which scales with N 3 N^{3} , and from the need for accurate knowledge of the channel statistics. This paper aims to address both challenges by introducing reduced-complexity channel estimation methods that achieve the performance of MMSE in terms of estimation accuracy and uplink spectral efficiency while demonstrating improved robustness in practical scenarios where channel statistics must be estimated. This is achieved by exploiting the inherent structure of the spatial correlation matrix induced by the array geometry. Specifically, we use a Kronecker decomposition for uniform planar arrays and a well-suited circulant approximation for uniform linear arrays. By doing so, a significantly lower computational complexity is achieved, scaling as N ​ N N\sqrt{N} and N ​ log ⁡ N N\log N for squared planar arrays and linear arrays, respectively.

Sect. I
Introduction and motivation

Communications theorists are always looking for new technologies to improve the speed and reliability of wireless communications. Among these technologies, the multiple antenna technology has made significant advances, with the latest implementation being massive
MIMO
(
MIMO
)
[
2
]
, introduced with the advent of 5G
[
3
]
. Researchers are now exploring improved deployment methods for massive
MIMO
, incorporating more antennas and optimized signal processing algorithms to take advantage of its potential benefits. This evolution of massive
MIMO
has been referred to as massive
MIMO
2.0
[
4
]
, leading to the exploration of
MIMO
systems with extremely larger antenna arrays. This is referred to as X-MIMO in industry terminology. Various terms, such as
ELAA

[
3
]
, extremely large-scale
MIMO
(XL-MIMO)
[
5
]
, and ultra-massive
MIMO
(UM-MIMO)
[
6
]
, have also been suggested in academic literature.
In this paper, we simply use the large-scale
MIMO
terminology.

Accurate
CSI
(
CSI
) is crucial for the efficient use of large-scale
MIMO
, enabling precise transmission beamforming in
DL
(
DL
) and coherent signal combining in
UL
(
UL
). The basic way to acquire
CSI
in time-division duplexing systems is to transmit a predefined pilot sequence and estimate the channel coefficients at the
BS
(
BS
). However, performing
MMSE
channel estimation in a large-scale
MIMO
system with a massive number
N
of independent
RF
(
RF
) chains (e.g., in the order of thousands) poses critical challenges in the implementation of the
MMSE
estimator (whose computational complexity may scale as
N^{3}
due to matrix inversion, e.g.,
[
7
,
8
]
) and in the acquisition of the channel statistics, i.e., the spatial correlation matrices. In practical communication scenarios, the latter are not known a priori and must be estimated based on observed channel measurements
[
2
]
. This is crucial for designing robust and efficient large-scale
MIMO
systems
[
4
]
. An alternative is to use the
LS
(
LS
) estimator
[
9
]
, which requires neither matrix inversion nor statistical information. However, its performance is significantly inferior to
MMSE
, e.g.,
[
2
]
. The objective of this paper is to tackle this challenge by developing channel estimation schemes that achieve accuracy levels comparable to the
MMSE
estimator, while also offering reduced complexity and enhanced robustness to the imperfect knowledge of channel statistics.

I-A

Related work

A variety of schemes exists in the literature to achieve the same performance of
MMSE
while handling complexity that scales with the number of antennas. These include, for example, methods based on exhaustive search
[
10
]
, hierarchical search
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
,
CS
(
CS
)
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, and tensor decomposition
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. The exhaustive method in
[
10
]
exploits a sequence of training symbols, whose overhead becomes prohibitively high as the number
N
of antennas increases. To overcome this issue, a hierarchical search based on a predefined codebook is proposed in
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. However, while significantly reducing the complexity, the limited codebook size severely affects the estimation accuracy. Alternative methods to reduce the pilot overhead take advantage of
CS
techniques, which exploit the fact that the propagation channels of most practical communication scenarios can be sparsely represented in the angular domain.
A parametric approach is used in
[
16
]
for
LoS
(
LoS
) communications. A different approach is pursued in
[
20
]
, which exploits the high rank deficiency observed in the spatial correlation matrices caused by the large-scale
MIMO
geometry
[
21
]
to derive a subspace-based channel estimation approach. The complexity saving is due to a compact eigenvalue decomposition, which allows the proposed
RS-LS
(
RS-LS
) estimator to attain the
MMSE
performance when the pilot
SNR
(
SNR
) becomes significantly large. However, this might not hold true, especially for practical transmitter-receiver distances.

The vast majority of the aforementioned literature assumes that the channel statistics are perfectly known. However, this assumption is questionable because the matrix dimensions grow with
N
and the statistics change over time (e.g., due to mobility). Practical covariance estimates are imperfect because the number of observations may be comparable to
N
. One promising approach to estimating a large-dimensional covariance matrix with a small number of observations is to regularize the sample covariance matrix
[
22
,
2
]
. This makes it scenario-dependent.

I-B

Main contributions

This paper focuses on the development of channel estimation schemes that, compared to the optimal MMSE estimator, offer both lower computational complexity and improved robustness in the presence of imperfect knowledge of channel statistics. This is accomplished by exploiting the inherent structure of the spatial correlation matrix shaped by the array geometry, which is known in a given deployment scenario. For a
UPA
(
UPA
), we use the Kronecker product decomposition
[
23
,
24
]
to effectively separate the horizontal and vertical components within the array. For a
ULA
(
ULA
), we use an appropriate circulant approximation
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, which allows the application of the discrete Fourier transform (DFT).
Numerical results are used to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed methods in terms of
NMSE
(
NMSE
) and
SE
(
SE
) in the uplink with different combining schemes. The results show that the proposed schemes achieve performance levels comparable to those with the
MMSE
estimator, while significantly reducing computational complexity. Specifically, the overall computational load, including the estimation of the spatial correlation matrices, scales as
N\sqrt{N}
and
N\log N
for squared planar arrays and linear arrays, respectively. Moreover, the schemes exhibit improved robustness in scenarios with imperfect knowledge of channel statistics, making them more suitable for dynamic environments.
Remarkably, this is achieved without any regularization factor, making the schemes applicable without the need for fine-tuning according to the specific scenarios.

Sect. III
Channel estimation

We assume that channel estimation is performed by using orthogonal pilot sequences of length
\tau_{p}\geq K
. We call

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{\tau_{p}}
the pilot sequence used by
UE

k
and assume

|[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{k}]_{i}|^{2}=1
, so that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{k}=\tau_{p}
. In the absence of pilot contamination, the observation vector is
[
2
, Sect. 3]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}

(5)

where
\rho
is the transmit power and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{w}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}_{N},\tau_{p}\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}_{N})
.

An arbitrary
linear
estimator of
\mathbf{h}_{k}
based on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
takes the form
[
2
, Sect. 3]

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}

(6)

for some deterministic matrix
\mathbf{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
that specifies the estimation scheme.
The
NMSE
of the estimation error
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}\triangleq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{k}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}

can be computed as

\displaystyle\!\!\mathsf{NMSE}\!=\!1-\frac{2\sqrt{\rho}\tau_{p}\Re\left(\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k})\right)+\rho\tau_{p}^{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\right)}{\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k})}\!\!

(7)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}=\frac{1}{\rho\tau_{p}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}}\right\}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}_{N}

(8)

with
\gamma=\tau_{p}\rho/\sigma^{2}
being the transmit
SNR
(i.e., measured at the UE side), during the channel estimation phase.

III-A

MMSE
and
LS
estimators

The scheme that minimizes (
7
) is the
MMSE
estimator, according to which

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{MMSE}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{-1}.

(9)

The
MMSE
estimator is optimal but requires the following operations:

1.

Estimation of the spatial covariance matrix
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}
;

2.

Computation of the inverse of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}
;

3.

Computation of the channel estimate

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{MMSE}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{MMSE}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}.

(10)

The main computational cost comes from the inversion of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}
, which scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
if structural properties of the matrix are not exploited. Therefore, an intense computational effort is required when
N
is large, as envisioned in large-scale
MIMO

[
6
]
.

An alternative is the
LS
estimator with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{LS}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}_{N}

(11)

which uses no prior information on the channel statistics and array geometry. Unlike the
MMSE
estimator, its computational complexity is
\mathcal{O}(N)
, due to the multiplication between the diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{LS}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
. However, this efficiency comes at the cost of reduced accuracy.

III-B

MMSE
estimators for low and rich scattering

Two estimators for low and rich scattering scenarios are described below. Both take advantage of the array geometry, but apply to two different channel propagation conditions. The first one assumes that propagation
takes place in a
LoS
scenario with a single plane-wave arriving from
\theta_{k}
and
\varphi_{k}
. Under this hypothesis,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{k}=g_{k}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})
and

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{LoS}}_{k}=\beta_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})^{\mathsf{H}}
.
Replacing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{LoS}}_{k}
into (
9
) yields

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{LoS}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{LoS}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{LoS}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}\frac{\beta_{k}\gamma}{1+N\beta_{k}\gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{a}}(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})^{\mathsf{H}}

(12)

whose complexity is
\mathcal{C}_{\mathsf{LoS}}=\mathcal{O}(N)
, due to the evaluation of the product between

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{LoS}}
in (
12
) and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}
(no pre-computation phase is required).
However, the
LoS
-based estimator works well only when the channel vector is generated by a single plane-wave arriving from

(\varphi_{k},\theta_{k})
, whose knowledge must be perfect at the
BS
.

Conversely, when the propagation scenario is highly scattered, and planar waves arrive uniformly within the angular domain in front of the
UPA
, we can make use of the

(

) approximation proposed in
[
20
]
. According to
[

]

\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}
is the (reduced-order) eigenvector matrix corresponding to the non-zero eigenvalues in
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}
, obtained through the

eigenvalue decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{ISO}}_{k}
whose

th entry is

\displaystyle[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{ISO}}_{k}]_{m,l}=\mathrm{sinc}\left(2\sqrt{\delta^{2}_{\mathsf{H},ml}+\delta^{2}_{\mathsf{V},ml}}\right)

(13)

with

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathsf{H},ml}

\displaystyle=\left[i(m)-i(l)\right]\Delta_{\mathsf{H}}/\lambda

(14)

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathsf{V},ml}

\displaystyle=\left[j(m)-j(l)\right]\Delta_{\mathsf{V}}/\lambda

(15)

and
\mathrm{sinc}(x)=\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)

[
28
]
.
Approximating
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}^{\mathsf{ISO}}
with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{ISO}}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}^{\mathsf{H}}
, and
replacing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}

, with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{ISO}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}}^{\mathsf{H}}.

The main advantage of (

Sect. IV
Reduced-complexity method for
UPA

We first consider a

The main advantage of using (

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
leads to the following approximation of

in (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{MMSE}}

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{Kron}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{2}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}_{N}\right)^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathsf{H}}_{2}\right)

(18)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
(
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{2}
, respectively) and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{1}
(
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{2}
, respectively) are the eigenvector and eigenvalue matrices obtained by the spectral decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{1}
(
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
, respectively), i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{1}^{\mathsf{H}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{U}}_{2}^{\mathsf{H}}
. Compared to the spectral decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
or the inversion of

and

requires much fewer operations, as will be discussed in Sect.

IV-A

Kronecker-based approximation

We begin by

) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}\iint_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}f_{k}(\varphi,\theta)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{V}}(\theta)\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,\theta)d\varphi d\theta,

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,\theta)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathsf{H}}\times N_{\mathsf{H}}}
and

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{V}}(\theta)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathsf{V}}\times N_{\mathsf{V}}}
are Hermitian Toeplitz matrices given by

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,\theta)]_{m,l}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathsf{j}2\pi(\Delta_{\mathsf{H}}/\lambda)[i(m)-i(l)]\sin\varphi\cos\theta},

(20)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{V}}(\theta)]_{m,l}

\displaystyle=e^{\mathsf{j}2\pi(\Delta_{\mathsf{V}}/\lambda)[j(m)-j(l)]\sin\theta}.

(21)

Computing the integral (
19
) with respect to
\varphi
yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}=\beta_{k}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{V}}(\theta)\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\theta)d\theta

(22)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\theta)=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}f_{k}(\varphi,\theta)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,\theta)d\varphi.

(23)

In general, the integral expression of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
given in (
22

a Kronecker product of two matrices. However, in the specific case where all the plane waves are impinging from the same elevation angle
\overline{\theta}

(which means no angular spread across the elevation angle), we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{V},k}(\overline{\theta})\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\overline{\theta})

(24)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\overline{\theta})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\tilde{f}_{k}(\varphi)\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi,\overline{\theta})d\varphi

(25)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{V},k}(\overline{\theta})

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathsf{V}}(\overline{\theta}).

(26)

Note that the representation of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
in (
24
) is not unique. Indeed, taking
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{H},k}=\alpha\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\overline{\theta})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathsf{H},k}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}]_{1:1:N_{\mathsf{H}},1:1:N_{\mathsf{H}}}

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathsf{V},k}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}]_{1:N_{\mathsf{H}}:N,1:N_{\mathsf{H}}:N}/[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}]_{1,1}

(28)

where we have adopted the well-known “colon notation” used by
MatLab
. In the remainder of this paper, the Kronecker decomposition of

by

Sect. V
Reduced-complexity methods for
ULA

The
UPA
model introduced in Sect.
II
is general enough to encompass both vertical and horizontal configurations, obtained by either
N_{\mathsf{H}}=1
or
N_{\mathsf{V}}=1
, respectively. In those cases, the
KBA
-based approach has exactly the same complexity of the optimal
MMSE
one. This is due to the fact that, when
N_{\mathsf{H}}
(respectively,
N_{\mathsf{V}}
) is equal to
1
, based on (
27
) [respectively, (
28
)], the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\overline{\theta})

[respectively,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathsf{V},k}(\overline{\theta})
] is
exactly
the scalar
1
, and hence

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathsf{V},k}(\overline{\theta})
[respectively,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathsf{H},k}(\overline{\theta})
]. For this reason,
not only the complexity, but also the channel estimation performance of the
KBA
-based method coincides with the
MMSE
one.

In this section, we develop a channel estimation scheme for a horizontal
ULA
(i.e.,
N=N_{\mathsf{H}}
) that exploits the correlation
induced by the array geometry and propagation conditions to approach
MMSE
performance, while having a computational complexity that
scales log-linearly with
N
. The adaptation to the vertical
ULA
configuration (with
N=N_{\mathsf{V}}
) is straightforward and
not reported here for the sake of brevity.

V-A

DFT
-based approximation

If a
ULA
is used, the covariance
matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
is Hermitian Toeplitz, and it can be approximated with a suitable circulant matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}_{k}

[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, whose first row
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{c}}_{k}=[c_{k}(0),c_{k}(1),\cdots,c_{k}(N-1)]
is related to the first
row
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}_{k}=[r_{k}(0),r_{k}(1),\cdots,r_{k}(N-1)]
of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
by
[
27
]

\displaystyle c_{k}(n)=\begin{cases}r_{k}(0)&\text{$n=0$},\\
\dfrac{(N-n)r_{k}(n)+nr^{*}_{k}(N-n)}{N}&\text{$n=1,\ldots,N-1$}.\end{cases}

(34)

Any circulant matrix can be unitarily diagonalized using the
DFT
(
DFT
) matrix, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}}_{k}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}^{\mathsf{H}}

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}=[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{0}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{1}\,\cdots\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{N-1}]
is the inverse
DFT
matrix, with

[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}}_{n}]_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}e^{\mathsf{j}2\pi mn/N}
for
0\leq m,n\leq N-1
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{k}
is
the diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}}_{k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{k}]_{n,n}=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N-1}c_{k}(m)e^{-\mathsf{j}2\pi mn/N}

(35)

which are obtained by taking the
DFT
of the first row of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}_{k}
. Replacing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}_{k}
into (
9
) yields
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{DFT}}={\bf A}_{k}^{\mathsf{DFT}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
,
with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}^{\mathsf{DFT}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}_{N}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}}^{\mathsf{H}}.

(36)

We call it the
DFT
-based channel estimator
. Its complexity derives from the pre-computation phase, which
is
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
due to the computation of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Lambda}}}_{k}
through (
35
), and from the
computation of the matrix-vector product, which is again
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
, since the
DFT
matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{F}}

and its inverse are involved. Hence, the complexity of the
DFT
-based estimator is
\mathcal{C}_{\mathsf{DFT}}=\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
.
Note that, similarly to the
KBA
case, the
DFT
-based estimator depends on the true covariance matrix
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}
, which
must be estimated as with the
MMSE
estimator.

Sect. VI
Channel estimation with imperfect knowledge of covariance matrices

So far, we have assumed perfect knowledge of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
. This may not be the case in practical scenarios since
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
changes for various reasons
[
4
]
. Measurements suggest that these changes are orders of magnitude slower than the fast variations of the channel
[
4
]
. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that they do not change over
\tau_{s}=B_{s}T_{s}/\tau_{c}
blocks
[
4
]
, where
B_{s}
is the system bandwidth,
T_{s}
is the timing interval over which the second-order statistics remain unaltered, and
\tau_{c}
is the length (in samples) of the channel coherence block, that equals the product between the coherence bandwidth and the coherence time of the channel. To quantify
\tau_{s}
, let us consider a mobile scenario using
B_{s}=10\,\mathrm{MHz}
and
T_{s}=0.5\,\mathrm{s}

[
22
]
. Using
\tau_{c}=200
, as considered throughout the paper, which is compatible with a coherence bandwidth of
200\,\mathrm{kHz}
and a coherence time of
1\,\mathrm{ms}
, we get
\tau_{s}=25000
.

Suppose the
BS
has the pilot symbols
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}_{k}
in
M\leq\tau_{s}
coherence
blocks. We denote the corresponding observations by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}[1],\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}[M]
. An estimate of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}

can be obtained by computing the sample correlation matrix given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{sample}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}[m]\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}[m]}{\rho\tau_{p}^{2}}.

(37)

The computation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{sample}}
requires
\mathcal{O}(MN^{2})
operations, as it involves
MN^{2}
complex multiplications.

A better estimation is typically obtained through matrix regularization by computing the convex combination
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}(\eta)=\eta\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{sample}}+(1-\eta)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{diag}},\quad\eta\in[0,1]

(38)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{diag}}
contains the main diagonal of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{sample}}
.
The regularization makes
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}(\eta)
a full-rank matrix for any
\eta\in[0,1)
, and
\eta
can be tuned
(for example by using numerical methods) to purposely underestimate the off-diagonal elements when these are considered unreliable.
Once
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}(\eta)
is computed, an estimate of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}
follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}(\eta)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}(\eta)-\frac{1}{\gamma}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}}_{N}

(39)

which requires only knowledge of
\gamma
, i.e., the
SNR
during the pilot transmission phase.

VI-A

Improved estimation of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}

We now develop an improved estimation scheme of the correlation matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}
that can be used with
UPA
.
For the ease of notation, we will drop the subscript
k
from now on.

To accomplish this task, we notice that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
, analogously to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
, is Hermitian
block
-Toeplitz (and not simply Toeplitz), with
N_{\mathsf{V}}\times N_{\mathsf{V}}
blocks with size
N_{\mathsf{H}}\times N_{\mathsf{H}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,2}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,3}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,N_{\mathsf{V}}}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,2}^{\mathsf{H}}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,N_{\mathsf{V}}-1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,3}^{\mathsf{H}}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,2}^{\mathsf{H}}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,N_{\mathsf{V}}-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,N_{\mathsf{V}}}^{\mathsf{H}}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,N_{\mathsf{V}}-1}^{\mathsf{H}}&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,N_{\mathsf{V}}-2}^{\mathsf{H}}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,1}\end{bmatrix},

(40)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}
denotes the
N_{\mathsf{H}}\times N_{\mathsf{H}}
block obtained by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
as
(using
MatLab
colon notation)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}=\left[\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}\right]_{1:1:N_{\mathsf{H}},\left(j-1\right)N_{\mathsf{H}}+1:1:jN_{\mathsf{H}}}.

(41)

This means that, due to the block-Toeplitz structure,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1+m,j+m}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}

(42)

for
j=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{V}}-1
and
m=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{V}}-j
, and that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,1}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}^{\mathsf{H}},

(43)

because of the Hermitian symmetry of the covariance matrix.

To proceed with the estimation, we denote by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{1,j}
the estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}

that takes the block-Toeplitz structure (
40
) into account, by averaging the blocks of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{sample}}
over the same (block) diagonal, i.e.:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{1,j}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathsf{V}}-j+1}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathsf{V}}-j+1}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{sample}}_{m,j+m-1}},

(44)

for
j=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{V}}
, where
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{sample}}_{m,j+m-1}}
is the
N_{\mathsf{H}}\times N_{\mathsf{H}}
block corresponding to the rows
(m-1)N_{\mathsf{H}}+1,\ldots,mN_{\mathsf{H}}-1
and to the columns
(j+m-2)N_{\mathsf{H}}+1,\ldots,(j+m-1)N_{\mathsf{H}}-1
of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{sample}}
in (
37
).

It is worth noting that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}
is an
N_{\mathsf{H}}\times N_{\mathsf{H}}
Toeplitz (but
not
Hermitian) matrix, and we can thus improve the estimation further, by adapting the matrix-based criterion (
44
) to
the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}_{1,j}
. In particular, the estimate

{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}}
can be obtained as follows: the first row is computed as

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}\right]_{1,\ell}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathsf{H}}-\ell+1}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathsf{H}}-\ell+1}{\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{1,j}\right]_{m,\ell+m-1}}

(45)

for
\ell=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{H}}
, whereas the first column can be computed as

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}\right]_{\ell,1}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathsf{H}}-\ell+1}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\mathsf{H}}-\ell+1}{\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{1,j}\right]_{\ell+m-1,m}}

(46)

for
\ell=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{H}}
. Exploiting the Toeplitz structure, the remaining elements of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{i,j}
can be found as

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}\right]_{1+m,\ell+m}

\displaystyle=\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}\right]_{1,\ell}

(47)

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}\right]_{\ell+m,1+m}

\displaystyle=\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}\right]_{\ell,1}

(48)

for
\ell=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{H}}-1
and
m=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{H}}-\ell
.

Sect. VII
Complexity analysis

Next, we assess the computational burden in terms of complex operations (additions and/or multiplications), required by the
entire
channel estimation procedure of the investigated estimators.
Without loss of generality, next we consider
N_{\mathsf{H}}\geq N_{\mathsf{V}}
. The analysis can be simply extended to the case
N_{\mathsf{H}}<N_{\mathsf{V}}
.

VII-A

Computation of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}}}

From (
44
), the calculation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{1,j}
, for
j=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{V}}
, requires a total of approximately
N^{2}/2
additions and
N_{\mathsf{H}}N
multiplications. Analogously, the evaluation of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{1,j}}
in (
45
), with
j=1,\dots,N_{\mathsf{V}}
, requires a total of
NN_{\mathsf{H}}/2
additions and
N
multiplications. Thanks to Toeplitz-block Toeplitz structure, the computation of
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}}}
has a complexity order of
\mathcal{O}(MN^{2}+N^{2}/2+N_{\mathsf{H}}N)\approx\mathcal{O}(MN^{2})
. No additional complexity is incurred compared to the unstructured
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{sample}}
.

VII-B

Computation of matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
via
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}}}
and
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}}}

The
MMSE
estimator is characterized by the matrix

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{MMSE}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}\sqrt{\rho}}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{k}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}}\right)^{-1}

(50)

obtained from (
9
) by replacing the true covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}_{k}
with its estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{k}
. Once
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}}}
is available,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{k}
can easily be obtained through (
49
) with
N
additions. As for the computation of
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{k})^{-1}
, an efficient algorithm for the inversion of a Toeplitz-block Toeplitz matrix can be found in
[
33
]
. It has a complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}N_{\mathsf{V}})
. The maximum complexity occurs for square
UPA
, where
N_{\mathsf{H}}=N_{\mathsf{V}}=\sqrt{N}
, resulting in
\mathcal{O}(N^{2.5})
.

Computation of channel estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}

Summary

The above results are summarized in Table

\tau_{s}

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}

\tau_{s}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
, plus
\tau_{s}
times the one due to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
. Using Table
III
, this means that the complexity order is
\mathcal{O}\left(MN^{2}+N^{2}N_{\mathsf{V}}+\tau_{s}N^{2}\right)
for the

, and
\mathcal{O}\left(MN^{2}+N_{\mathsf{H}}^{3}+\tau_{s}\left(N_{\mathsf{H}}+N_{\mathsf{V}}\right)N\right)
for the
KBA
. Assuming a square array, in which
N_{\mathsf{H}}=N_{\mathsf{V}}=\sqrt{N}
, the complexity orders become
\mathcal{O}((M+\sqrt{N}+\tau_{s})N^{2})
and
\mathcal{O}((MN+\sqrt{N}+2\tau_{s}\sqrt{N})N)
, respectively.

Sect. VIII
Uplink spectral efficiency evaluation

Channel estimation is the ancillary task to support data detection, which is the ultimate goal of a communication system.
Hence, we now compare the investigated solutions in terms of achievable uplink
SE
. This is obtained with the well-known use-and-then-forget bound
[
2
, Sect. 4.2]
, which yields

\displaystyle\underline{\mathsf{SE}}_{k}=\left(1-\frac{\tau_{p}}{\tau_{c}}\right)\mathbb{E}\left\{{\log_{2}\left(1+\underline{\gamma}_{k}\right)}\right\}\qquad\textrm{[bit/s/Hz]},

(55)

where the factor
{\tau_{p}}/{\tau_{c}}
accounts for the fraction of samples per coherence block used for channel estimation, with
\tau_{c}
being the length of each coherence block, and
\underline{\gamma}_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{k}}\right\}\right|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\mathbb{E}\left\{{\left|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{i}\right|^{2}}\right\}}-\left|\mathbb{E}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{k}}\right\}\right|^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left\{{\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\right\|^{2}}\right\}},

(56)

with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
being the receive combining vector associated to
UE

k
.
Notice that the expectations are computed with respect to all sources of randomness. We consider both
RZF
(
RZF
) combining, according to which

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{i}^{\mathsf{H}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}_{N}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}

(57)

and
MR
(
MR
) combining
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}}_{k}

Sect. IX
Conclusion

We developed channel estimation schemes for large-scale
MIMO
systems that achieve accuracy levels comparable to the optimal
MMSE
estimator, while also offering reduced complexity and enhanced robustness to the imperfect knowledge of channel statistics. Both
UPA
and
ULA
were studied. In the case of
UPA
, we exploited the inherent structure of the spatial correlation matrix to approximate it by a Kronecker product, and in the case of
ULA
, we used a circulant approximation to further reduce the computational complexity.
Comparisons were made with the optimal
MMSE
estimator and other alternatives with less complexity (e.g., the
LS
estimator). Numerical results confirmed the effectiveness of the proposed methods in terms of
UL

SE
with various combining schemes, approaching
MMSE

The estimation of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{h}}_{k}
calls for the product between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
. Accordingly, when
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}_{k}
is written as a product of matrices (as occurs, for instance, with the
MMSE
or the
KBA
estimators), it is convenient not to perform the products. Indeed, assume that we have to compute
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{2}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{2}
are
N\times N
matrices and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
is an
N
-dimensional vector. If we first multiply
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{2}
, and then the resulting matrix and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
, we have to perform
N^{3}+N^{2}
multiplications and
(N-1)N^{2}+(N-1)N
additions. On the other hand, if we first compute the product between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
, and then the product between
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{B}}_{1}
and the resulting vector,
2N^{2}
multiplications and
2(N-1)N
additions are needed. This means, for example, that when considering the
MMSE
estimator it is better to compute first
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}
and then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}}^{\mathsf{toe}}_{k}\left[(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}}_{k}^{\mathsf{toe}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\right]
. An analogous approach can be conveniently used with the
KBA

m\times m
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
is
n\times n
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
is an
mn
-dimensional vector. The evaluation of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C}}_{2})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}

(m+n)mn
