Efficient Approximation of Quantum Channel Fidelity Exploiting Symmetry
Yeow Meng Chee, Hoang Ta, and Van Khu Vu

Abstract
Determining the optimal fidelity for the transmission of quantum information over noisy quantum channels is one of the central problems in quantum information theory. Recently, [Berta-Borderi-Fawzi-Scholz, Mathematical Programming, 2021] introduced an asymptotically converging semidefinite programming hierarchy of outer bounds for this quantity. However, the size of the semidefinite programs (SDPs) grows exponentially with respect to the level of the hierarchy, thus making their computation unscalable. In this work, by exploiting the symmetries in the SDP, we show that, for a fixed output dimension of the quantum channel, we can compute the SDP in time polynomial with respect to the level of the hierarchy and input dimension. As a direct consequence of our result, the optimal fidelity can be approximated with an accuracy of ϵ \epsilon in poly ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ , input dimension ) \mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon,\text{input dimension}) time.

Introduction

One of the central problems in information theory is determining the minimum error probability that arises during data transmission through a noisy channel or when storing it on an unreliable storage medium. This task can be formulated as an optimization problem that maximizes the probability of determining the sent messages exactly over all valid encoders and decoders. This problem was initially investigated by Shannon
[
Sha48
]
. There, the author showed that when considering large numbers of independent copies of the same channel, this quantity can be characterized by a simple expression known as the channel capacity of the channel.

In terms of the algorithmic point of view regarding this problem, the study conducted in
[
BF17
]
focused on investigating the problem of determining the optimal encoder and decoder that leads to the maximum success probability over a noisy channel in the non-asymptotic regime. In this setting, given
N_{X\to Y}
that is a noisy channel from
X
to
Y
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathrm{p}(N,M)
to be the maximum success probability for transmitting a uniform
M
-dimensional message through the channel
N_{X\to Y}
. The study in
[
BF17
]
showed that the computation of
\mathrm{p}(N,M)
can be formulated as an optimization problem involving a submodular function. Building upon this insight, a simple and efficient greedy algorithm was introduced in
[
BF17
]
to find a code that achieves a
(1-e^{-1})
-approximation of
\mathrm{p}(N,M)
. Furthermore, the study in
[
BF17
]
provided an efficiently computable linear programming relaxation denoted by
\mathrm{LP}(N,M)
(also referred to as meta converse
[
Hay09
,
PPV10
]
). This relaxation provides upper bounds on
\mathrm{p}(N,M)
, and it was proven that
\mathrm{p}(N,M)\leq\mathrm{LP}(N,M)\leq(1-e^{-1})^{-1}\mathrm{p}(N,M)
. However, it was highlighted in
[
BF17
]
that the problem of obtaining an approximate solution for
\mathrm{p}(N,M)
with a better constant factor than
1-e^{-1}
is classified as
\mathrm{NP}
-Hard. Further research on this problem on variant models has been conducted in
[
BFS16
,
FSS19
,
BFGG22
,
FF23a
]
.

The problem in the analog quantum setting involves determining quantum channel fidelity (or short channel fidelity)
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
for transmitting one part of a maximally entangled state with dimension
M
through a noisy quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{A\to B}
from
A
to
B
, where
A
and
B
are finite dimensional
complex Hilbert spaces. Similar to the classical case, this problem can be formulated as a bilinear optimization problem with matrix-valued variables. In order to approximate
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
, an efficiently computable semidefinite programming relaxation was proposed in
[
LM15
]
. However, unlike the classical case, the gap between this relaxation and
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
is not well understood in the quantum setting. In other directions, numerical methods based on iterative seesaw techniques, such as those proposed in
[
RW05
,
FSW07
,
TKL10
,
JRO+17
]
, have been developed to provide lower bounds for
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
. These methods are computationally tractable semidefinite programs that often converge in practice. In different settings, several works in
[
TBR16
,
WD16
,
WFD18
,
KDWW19
]
have focused on the problem of determining the size of a maximally entangled state that can be transmitted through a noisy quantum channel while maintaining a fixed fidelity of
1-\epsilon
.

In a recent work
[
BBFS21
]
, the authors introduced an asymptotically converging semidefinite programming hierarchy
\{\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of outer bounds for
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
(defined in Section
2.1
). The hierarchy provides a measure of convergence speed and approaches the channel fidelity
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
as the parameter
n
tends to infinity. In particular, the authors showed that the inequality
0\leq\mathrm{OPT}(\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M))-\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)\leq\frac{\mathrm{poly}(d)}{\sqrt{n}}
holds, where
\mathrm{OPT}(\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M))
is the optimal value of the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
, and
d
depends only on the input dimension, output dimension,
M
. This result implies that for any
\epsilon>0
, estimating
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
with an additive error of
\epsilon
can be achieved by solving the semidefinite program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
, where
n=\frac{\mathrm{poly}(d)}{\epsilon^{2}}
. However, it is important to note that the size of the matrix variables in
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
grows exponentially with respect to
n
. Therefore, direct computation of the optimal value of the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
is inefficient.

Main result and techniques

In this paper, we aim to propose an efficient way to compute the optimal value of the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
, and therefore, have an efficient approximate algorithm for computing channel fidelity
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
, where
\mathcal{N}
is a quantum channel, and
M,n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, our main result is stated as follows.

Theorem
.

For
M,n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, let
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{A}\to B}
be a quantum channel, and let
d_{\bar{A}}
denote the input dimension of
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{A}\to B}
. The optimal value of the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
can be computed in
\mathrm{poly}(n,d_{\bar{A}})
time, for a fixed output dimension of the quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{A}\to B}
. As a direct consequence, the channel fidelity
\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)
can be estimated with an additive error of
\epsilon
in
\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon,d_{\bar{A}})
time.

To achieve this goal, we exploit the symmetries of the semidefinite program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
to obtain an equivalent representation and solve it efficiently. Specifically, in Lemma
3.1
, we demonstrate that the search space of the program can be restricted to an invariant subspace
\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathcal{H}^{n})
, which is obtained by taking a natural action of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
on the
\mathcal{H}^{n}
- the original search space. Utilizing tools from representation theory, particularly the representation theory of the symmetric group, we can construct a bijective linear map
\psi
from
\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathcal{H}^{n})
to
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{C}^{m_{i}\times m_{i}}
for some integers
t
and
m_{i},i=1,\dots,t
. This map ensures that for any
X\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathcal{H}^{n})
, the matrix
\psi(X)
takes on a block diagonal form with
\mathrm{poly}(n)
number of blocks, i.e.,
t=\mathrm{poly}(n)
, and each of which has a size bounded by a polynomial in
d_{\bar{A}}
and
n
, i.e.,
m_{i}=\mathrm{poly}(d_{\bar{A}},n)
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.

In addition, the bijective map
\psi
preserves positive semidefiniteness, i.e., for any
X\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathcal{H}^{n})
,
X\succeq 0
if and only if
\psi(X)\succeq 0
. Therefore, for any
X\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\mathcal{H}^{n})
, checking whether
X
is a positive semidefinite matrix can be reduced to checking if the smaller matrices
[\psi(X)]_{1},\dots,[\psi(X)]_{t}
are all positive semidefinite, where
[\psi(X)]_{i}
is the
i
-th block of
\psi(X)
for
i=1,\dots,t
.

From the construction of
\psi
, we can build a transformation
\Phi
that converts the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
into an equivalent semidefinite program, denoted as
\Phi(\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M))
, which has
\mathrm{poly}(d_{\bar{A}},n)
number of constraints, and each matrix variable has a polynomial size in
d_{\bar{A}}
and
n
(see Theorem
3.4
). Additionally, by utilizing the representation of the symmetric group and exploiting specific properties of the constraints involved in the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
, we show in Theorem
3.7
that the transformation can be implemented in
\mathrm{poly}(d_{\bar{A}},n)
time, while a direct implementation of the transformation would require exponential computation in
n
. Therefore, we can determine the optimal value of the program
\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M)
by solving the program
\Phi(\mathrm{SDP}_{n}(\mathcal{N},M))
, and this can be done in
\mathrm{poly}(d_{\bar{A}},n)
time. The Table

Algorithmic aspects of optimal channel coding have been extensively studied in various settings. In classical channels, the problem was explored in
[
BF17
]
for point-to-point channels, and a generalization was investigated in
[
BFGG22
]
. For entanglement-assisted classical channels, the research can be found in
[
BFS16
]
, and for the multiple-access channel, the study is investigated in

compared with the original form in

Exploiting symmetries to simplify convex programs, especially semidefinite programs, has been done for various problems and applications
[
LM11

. This approach has been seen in other surveys as well
[

,
DK10
]
. Specifically, it has been applied to the mutually unbiased bases problem

\displaystyle\pi\cdot(h_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes h_{n})=h_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes h_{\pi^{-1}(n)}\,,h_{i}\in\mathcal{H}\,,\forall\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\,.

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(\pi)\left(\rho_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}\right)\coloneqq U_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(\pi)\rho_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}U_{\mathcal{H}^{n}}(\pi)^{*}\,,

2.1
Approximate quantum error correction

In this section, we briefly present the mathematical setting of approximate quantum error correction. For further information, we refer to
[
BBFS21
,
Bor22
]
.

Let
A,B,\bar{A},\bar{B}
be finite dimensional complex Hilbert spaces. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{A}\to B}
(
\mathcal{N}
in short) be a quantum channel. The quantum channel fidelity (or short channel fidelity) for message dimension
M\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)\coloneqq\max_{\mathcal{D}_{B\to\bar{B}}\,,\mathcal{E}_{A\to\bar{A}}}

\displaystyle\,\,F\left(\Phi_{\bar{B}R},((\mathcal{D}_{B\to\bar{B}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\bar{A}\to B}\circ\mathcal{E}_{A\to\bar{A}})\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R})(\Phi_{AR})\right)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\mathcal{D}_{B\to\bar{B}}\,,\mathcal{E}_{A\to\bar{A}}\,\,\,\,\text{quantum channels}\,,

where
F(\rho,\sigma)\coloneqq\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}^{2}
denotes the fidelity,
\Phi_{AR}
denotes the maximally entangled state on
AR
, and
M=d_{A}=d_{\bar{B}}=d_{R}
.

Using the Choi-Jamiolkowski isomorphism, the authors in
[
BBFS21
]
demonstrated that the channel fidelity can be reformulated as a bilinear optimization problem as follows.

\begin{split}\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)=\max_{E_{A\bar{A}},D_{B\bar{B}}}\,\,&d_{\bar{A}}d_{B}\cdot\mathrm{tr}\,\left[(J_{\bar{A}B}^{\mathcal{N}}\otimes\Phi_{A\bar{B}})(E_{A\bar{A}}\otimes D_{B\bar{B}})\right]\\
&\text{s.t. }E_{A\bar{A}}\succeq 0,\,,D_{B\bar{B}}\succeq 0\\
&E_{A}=\frac{I_{A}}{d_{A}},\,D_{B}=\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}}\,.\end{split}

(1)

By the linearity of the objective function, we can write the above program in an equivalent form that optimizes over a convex hull of feasible solutions
[
BBFS21
]
as follows.

\begin{split}\mathrm{F}(\mathcal{N},M)=\max\,\,&d_{\bar{A}}d_{B}\cdot\mathrm{tr}\,\left[\left(J_{\bar{A}B}^{\mathcal{N}}\otimes\Phi_{A\bar{B}}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I}E_{A\bar{A}}^{i}\otimes D_{B\bar{B}}^{i}\right)\right]\\
&\text{s.t. }p_{i}\geq 0\,,\,\forall i\in I,\,\,\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}=1\,,\\
&E_{A\bar{A}}^{i}\succeq 0,\,D_{B\bar{B}}^{i}\succeq 0\;\;\forall i\in I\,,\\
&E_{A}^{i}=\frac{I_{A}}{d_{A}},\,D_{B}^{i}=\frac{I_{B}}{d_{B}}\;\;\forall i\in I\,.\end{split}

(2)

2

Representation theory

We recall some basic facts and notions in the representation theory of finite groups. For further information, we refer the reader to Refs.
[
Ser77
]
and
[
FH13
]
. Let
G
be a finite group and
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional complex Hilbert space. A group homomorphism
\varrho:G\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
is called a
linear representation
of
G
on
\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
is the general linear group on
\mathcal{H}
. The space
\mathcal{H}
is called a
G
-module
. For
v\in\mathcal{H}
and
g\in G
, we write
g\cdot v
as shorthand for
\varrho(g)v
. For
X\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H})
, the action of
g\in G
on
X
is given by
\varrho(g)X\varrho(g)^{*}
.

A representation
\varrho:G\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
of
G
is called
irreducible
if for any subspace
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
then
\{g\mathcal{H}^{\prime}:g\in G\}\subsetneq\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, in other words, it contains no proper submodule
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
such that
g\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
be
G
-modules, a
G
-equivariant map
from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is a linear map
\phi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
such that
g\cdot\phi(v)=\phi(g\cdot v)
for all
g\in G,v\in\mathcal{H}
. Two
G
-modules
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are called
G
-isomorphic
, write
\mathcal{H}\cong\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, if there is a bijective equivariant map from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. We denote by
\mathrm{End}^{G}(\mathcal{H})
, the set of all
G
-equivariant maps from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{End}^{G}(\mathcal{H})=\{T\in\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{H}):T(g\cdot v)=g\cdot T(v),\forall v\in\mathcal{H},g\in G\}.

Note that
\mathrm{End}^{G}(\mathcal{H})
is known to form a matrix
*
-algebra
[
LM11
]
, that is, a set of complex matrices that is closed under addition, scalar multiplication, matrix multiplication, and taking the conjugate transpose.

Let
G
be a finite group acting on a finite dimensional complex vector space
\mathcal{H}
. Then the space
\mathcal{H}
can be decomposed into a direct sum of subspaces as
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{H}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{t}
are
G
-modules and called the
G
-isotypical component
. In more detail, every
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is a direct sum of irreducible
G
-modules denoted as
\mathcal{H}_{i,1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{H}_{i,m_{i}}
and note that two irreducible
G
-modules
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}
and
\mathcal{H}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
are isomorphic (i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}\cong\mathcal{H}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
) if and only if
i=i^{\prime}
. The tuple
(m_{1},\dots,m_{t})
are called
multiplicities
of the corresponding irreducible representations.

For each
i\in[t]
and
j\in[m_{i}]
, let
u_{i,j}\in\mathcal{H}_{i,j}
be a nonzero vector such that for each
i
and all
j,j^{\prime}\in[m_{i}]
, there is a bijective
G
-equivariant map from
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}
to
\mathcal{H}_{i,j^{\prime}}
that maps
u_{i,j}
to
u_{i,j^{\prime}}
. For
i\in[t]
, we define a matrix
U_{i}
as
[u_{i,1},\dots,u_{i,m_{i}}]
, with
u_{i,j}
forming the
j
-th column of
U_{i}
.
The matrix set
\{U_{1},\dots,U_{t}\}
obtained in this way is called a
representative matrix set
for the action of
G
on
\mathcal{H}
. The columns of the matrices
U_{i}
can be viewed as elements of the dual space
\mathcal{H}^{*}
(by taking the standard inner product). Then each
U_{i}
is an ordered set of linear functions on
\mathcal{H}
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{i,j}
is the linear space spanned by
G\cdot u_{i,j}
(for each
i,j
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathbb{C}G\cdot u_{i,j}\;,

where
\mathbb{C}G=\left\{\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g:\alpha_{g}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
denotes the complex group algebra of
G
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{End}^{G}(\mathcal{H})=\dim\mathrm{End}^{G}\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\mathcal{H}_{i,j}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}^{2}\,.

(3)

Note that with the action of the finite group
G
on the space
\mathcal{H}
, any inner product

on
\mathcal{H}
gives rise to a

\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}
and

(4)

For

and

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\,_{(B\bar{B})_{2}^{n}}\left(\sum_{(c,d)\in O_{t}^{\mathcal{H}}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|\otimes|c_{1}\rangle\!\langle d_{1}|\otimes|c_{2}\rangle\!\langle d_{2}|\otimes\dots\otimes|c_{n}\rangle\!\langle d_{n}|\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}C(t)\left(|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|\otimes|a_{1}\rangle\!\langle b_{1}|\right)\text{ if }H(a,b)=1\text{ and }a_{1}\neq b_{1}\,,\\
0\;\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Here we used the fact that the Hamming distance is invariant under the action of the symmetric group, which is defined in Eq. (
6
). In addition,
C(t)
can be determined in
\mathrm{poly}(n)
time, which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
3.1

Proof.

For proving Claim (
10
), we express
\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}
in terms of the standard bases of
\mathscr{L}(A)
and
\mathscr{L}(\bar{A})
. We write

\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A}]\\
x,y\in[d_{\bar{A}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|\otimes\rho^{i,j,x,y}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{A}}(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A}]\\
x\in[d_{\bar{A}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes\rho^{i,j,x,x}\,.

Moreover, since
\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(A\bar{A}\otimes(B\bar{B})^{\otimes n}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A}]\\
x,y\in[d_{\bar{A}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)\rho^{i,j,x,y}U_{(B\bar{B})^{n}}(\pi)^{*}\text{ for any }\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{A}}(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A}]\\
x\in[d_{\bar{A}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)\rho^{i,j,x,x}U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(I_{A}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A}]\\
x\in[d_{\bar{A}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes\rho^{i,j,x,x}\right)\left(I_{A}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{A}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)\right)\left(\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{A}}(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}})\right)\left(I_{A}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}(\pi)^{*}\right)\,.

The above equality holds for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, which implies that

\displaystyle\rho_{\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{A}}(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}})\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(A\otimes(B\bar{B})^{\otimes n}\right)\,.

Similarly, we also have
\frac{I_{A}}{d_{A}}\otimes\rho_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(A\otimes(B\bar{B})^{\otimes n}\right)
. This proves the Claim (
10
).

For proving Claim (
11
), we write
\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A\bar{A}}]\\
u,v\in[d_{B_{n}}]\\
x,y\in[d_{\bar{B}_{n}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes\rho^{i,j,u,v,x,y}\otimes|u\rangle\!\langle v|\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|
. Thus,

\displaystyle\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}B_{n}}\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{B}_{n}}\left(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A\bar{A}}]\\
u,v\in[d_{B_{n}}]\\
x\in[d_{\bar{B}_{n}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes\rho^{i,j,u,v,x,x}\otimes|u\rangle\!\langle v|\,.

Note that, for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}
, the permutation matrix
U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)\otimes I_{B_{n}\bar{B}_{n}}
corresponds to a permutation in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that acts on the space
(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}
which fixes the system
(B\bar{B})_{n}
. This leads to

\displaystyle\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}=\left(I_{A\bar{A}}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)\otimes I_{B_{n}\bar{B}_{n}}\right)\left(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\right)\left(I_{A\bar{A}}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)^{*}\otimes I_{B_{n}\bar{B}_{n}}\right)\,.

Therefore, for any
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}

\displaystyle\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}B_{n}}\coloneqq\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{B}_{n}}(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A\bar{A}}]\\
u,v\in[d_{B_{n}}]\\
x\in[d_{\bar{B}_{n}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)\rho^{i,j,u,v,x,x}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)^{*}\otimes|u\rangle\!\langle v|

\displaystyle=\left(I_{A\bar{A}}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)\otimes I_{B_{n}}\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[d_{A\bar{A}}]\\
u,v\in[d_{B_{n}}]\\
x\in[d_{\bar{B}_{n}}]\end{subarray}}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes\rho^{i,j,u,v,x,x}\otimes|u\rangle\!\langle v|\right)\left(I_{A\bar{A}}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)^{*}\otimes I_{B_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(I_{A\bar{A}}\otimes\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)\otimes I_{B_{n}}\right)\left(\mathrm{tr}\,_{\bar{B}_{n}}(\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n}}\right)\left(I_{A\bar{A}}\otimes U_{(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}(\pi)^{*}\otimes I_{B_{n}}\right)\,.

This implies
\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}B_{n}}\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}}\left(A\bar{A}\otimes(B\bar{B})^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes B_{n}\right)

\rho_{A\bar{A}(B\bar{B})_{1}^{n-1}}\otimes\frac{I_{B_{n}}}{d_{B}}\in\mathrm{End}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n-1}}\left(A\bar{A}\otimes(B\bar{B})^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes B_{n}\right)
. This proves the Claim (
11
).
