When does a Gaussian process have its paths in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space?
Ingo Steinwart

Abstract
We investigate for which Gaussian processes there do or do not exist reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs) that contain almost all of their paths. In particular, we establish a new result that makes it possible to exclude the existence of such RKHSs in many cases. Moreover, we combine this negative result with some known techniques to establish positive results. Here it turns out that for many classical families of Gaussian processes we can fully characterize for which members of these families there exist RKHSs containing the paths. Similar characterizations are obtained for Gaussian processes, for which the RKHSs of their covariance functions are Sobolev spaces or Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness.

Primary 60G15, 60G17; Secondary 46E22, 46N30.

1
Introduction

Given a centered Gaussian process
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
on some (complete) probability space
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
,
a classical task is to describe its path properties by function spaces such as spaces of continuous functions.
One particular instance of this general task asks, whether the paths
X_{\bullet}
of the process are almost surely contained in a suitable
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
H

on
T
. Clearly, the most obvious choice of such a space is the RKHS
H_{X}
of the
the covariance function
k_{X}(s,t):=\mathbb{E}(X_{s}X_{t})
of
X
. In this case,
a classical result, which goes back to Parzen
[
33
]
, Kallianpur
[
22
]
, and LePage
[
32
]

shows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{X_{\bullet}\in H_{X}\})=1

if and only if
\dim H_{X}<\infty
. In other words, in basically no situation of interest is
H_{X}
an RKHS containing the paths of
X
.
However,
H_{X}
is, of course, by no means the only RKHS on
T
and beginning with Driscoll
[
12
]
, and
subsequently Fortet
[
18
]
,
more general
H
have been considered.
The most general investigation in this direction has been conducted by
Lukić and Beder
[
28
]
and since their results provide a basis of our analysis, we
quickly recall their findings. To this end,
we need the following notion.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be RKHSs on
T
. Then we write
H_{1}\ll H_{2}
and speak of nuclear dominance of
H_{2}
over
H_{1}
, if

H_{1}\subset H_{2}
and
the resulting embedding map
J:H_{1}\to H_{2}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

Note that
formally,
[
28
]
defines nuclear dominance differently, see the discussion following Theorem
6.2
below, but Lemma
6.3
shows that
our definition is actually equivalent to the one used in
[
28
]
. With these preparations,
[
28
, Theorem 7.4]

provides the following characterization for a
fixed
RKHS
H
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process on a complete probability space

(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
and
H
be an RKHS on
T
. Then the following statements hold true:

i)

If
H_{X}\ll H
, then there exists a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

ii)

If
H_{X}\not\ll H
, then for all versions
Y
of
X
we have
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=0
.

Here we note that strictly speaking
[
28
, Theorem 7.4]
only shows
ii)
for
Y=X
. However, if we have an
arbitrary version
Y
of
X
, then
Y
is a centered Gaussian process
with
k_{Y}=k_{X}
, which in turn gives
H_{Y}=H_{X}
. Applying the original result

[
28
, Theorem 7.4]
to
Y
then yields
ii)
.

Informally speaking, given a
fixed “candidate” RKHS
H
, Theorem
1.2
shows that we have either

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{X_{\bullet}\in H\})=1\qquad\qquad\mbox{ or }\qquad\qquad\mathrm{P}(\{X_{\bullet}\in H\})=0\,,

and in addition both situations
are characterized by
H_{X}\ll H
, respectively
H_{X}\not\ll H
.
In this sense, it provides a “test” for each RKHS
H
on
T
.
However, it does not tell us how to
find
an
H
with
H_{X}\ll H
, nor whether
H_{X}\ll H
is even possible for
suitable RKHSs
H
.

In this respect we note that
positive, constructive
results can sometimes be derived if we already have some knowledge on the paths of
X
.
To illustrate this,
let
E
be a
Banach space of functions (BSF) on
T
, that is,
E
is a Banach space consisting
of functions
f:T\to\mathbb{R}
such that the point evaluations
\delta_{t}:E\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\delta_{t}(f):=f(t)
for all
t\in T
and
f\in E
, are continuous.
If
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
is complete and we know that there exists a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in E\})=1
,
then for every “surrounding” RKHS

H
on
T
, that is
E\subset H
, we obviously also have

\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.
Unfortunately, however, this technique is limited to “sufficiently benign”
E
, since
there are various important BSFs
E
, for which
no such surrounding
H
exists. For example, for
the somewhat natural candidate
E=C(T)
, where
C(T)
denotes
the space of continuous functions
T\to\mathbb{R}
on a compact metric space
T
,
we cannot find a surrounding
H
as soon as
T
is not countable, see e.g.
[
45
]
.
Moreover,
[
42
]
has recently shown that even for many spaces
E
of smoother functions no such surrounding
H
with bounded kernel may exist.
For example,
if
\hoel^{\beta}([0,1])
denotes the space of all
\beta
-Hölder continuous functions
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, then there exists an RKHS
H
with bounded kernel and
\hoel^{\beta}([0,1])\subset H

if
\beta>1/2
, while there is no such RKHS if
\beta<1/2
, see
[
42
, Section 4.5, Theorem 22, and Lemma 24, respectively Theorem 12]
.
In particular, the approach of taking a surrounding RKHS does not work for the Wiener process, while for the
fractional Brownian motions with Hurst index
>1/2

it does work, see also Example
5.4
.
In summary, the approach of using a surrounding RKHS can only be used if we already know that the paths are contained in a sufficiently small BSF
E
.

A different approach
for constructing an RKHS
H
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
for some suitable version
Y
of
X

has been taken in
[
44
]
. In a nutshell, this approach is based
on a refined analysis of the Karhunen-Loève expansion for
X
. Unfortunately, this
approach, as well as its recent refinement in
[
23
]
, again fails to produce positive results in many situations. In particular, it does not
provide an RKHS containing the paths of the Wiener process.

The example of the Wiener process suggests that for some Gaussian processes
X
it may be
impossible

to find an RKHS containing the paths of
X
.
To the best of our knowledge, however, such
general
impossibility results, even for concrete processes, have not been established yet, despite the fact that
Gaussian processes with paths in an RKHS have attracted interests in diverse areas of applied
mathematics, see e.g.
[
37
,
17
,
20
,
49
]
and the references mentioned in
[
23
]
.
In particular, Theorem
1.2
cannot be directly used, since we would have to check
H_{X}\ll H
for
all

RKHS
H
on
T
, or at least
for a sufficiently rich class of such RKHSs. Similarly, a failure of the described two approaches for finding an
H
containing
the paths of the considered process does, of course, not imply a general impossibility result.

The main goal of this paper is close this gap by

characterizing
when
for a Gaussian process
X
there exists an RKHS
H
and a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.
To this end, we will first present
a
necessary
condition
for the existence of
H
with
H_{X}\ll H
. Combining this necessary condition with the
two approaches discussed above for finding
Y
and
H
with

\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
we can then characterize for various families of Gaussian processes, when such
Y
and

H
exist.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we collect the notions and notations that are necessary
for understanding our results. The general theory is then presented in
Section
3
,
and in Section
4
we apply our methods to Gaussian processes
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}

whose RKHSs
H_{X}
are essentially equal to a fractional Sobolev space on
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In addition, the case of Sobolev spaces with dominating mixed smoothness is also investigated.
Moreover, some concrete examples are presented in
Section
5
. Finally, all proofs as well as some additional material needed for these proofs
can be found in Section
6
.

2
Preliminaries

For sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset(0,\infty)
we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if there exist
constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
with
c_{1}a_{n}\leq b_{n}\leq c_{2}a_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.

If
E
and
F
are Banach spaces,
we write
E\hookrightarrow F
, if
E\subset F
and the resulting embedding map is continuous.
In addition, we write
E\circeq F
if
E\subset F\subset E
, and we write

E\cong F
if
E\circeq F
and the norms of
E
and
F
are equivalent. In other words, we have

E\cong F
if and only if
E\hookrightarrow F\hookrightarrow E
.
Obviously
E\cong F
implies
E\circeq F
, and if
E
and
F
are BSFs, then the converse implication is also true
by a simple application of the closed graph theorem, see e.g.
[
42
, Lemma 23]
. For RKHSs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
on
T

we thus have
H_{1}\circeq H_{2}
if and only if
H_{1}\cong H_{2}
.

Given a non-empty set
T
, we write
\ell_{\infty}(T)
for the space of all bounded functions
T\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the supremums norm. Clearly,
\ell_{\infty}(T)
is a BSF. Moreover, if
(T,{\cal B})
is a measurable space,
we write
{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
for the space of measurable functions
T\to\mathbb{R}
, where we equip

\mathbb{R}
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}
. Analogously,
{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}
stands for the Banach space of all bounded, measurable functions
T\to\mathbb{R}

equipped with the supremums-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
Furthermore, if
\nu
is some measure on
(T,{\cal B})
, then
{L_{2}(\nu)}
denotes the usual Hilbert space of
\nu
-equivalence classes
[f]_{\sim}
of square
\nu
-integrable functions
f
,
and
{L_{\infty}(\nu)}
stands for the Banach space of
\nu
-equivalence classes
[f]_{\sim}
of measurable,
\nu
-almost surely bounded functions
f
.
Note that the map

\displaystyle I_{\nu}:{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}

\displaystyle\to{L_{\infty}(\nu)}

(1)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto[f]_{\sim}\,,

is linear and bounded with

\|[f]_{\sim}\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
for all
f\in{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}
, where
\|[f]_{\sim}\|_{\infty}
denotes
the usual essential supremum norm of
[f]_{\sim}
in
{L_{\infty}(\nu)}
.
Finally, if
\lambda^{d}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is measurable with
\lambda^{d}(T)>0
, then we often write
{L_{2}(T)}:={L_{2}(\lambda^{d}_{|T})}
, where
\lambda^{d}_{|T}
is the Lebesgue measure on
T
.
Analogously, we write
{{\cal L}_{\infty}(T)}:={{\cal L}_{\infty}(\mathfrak{B}^{d}_{|T})}
, where
\mathfrak{B}^{d}_{|T}
denotes the restriction of the Borel
\sigma
-algebra

\mathfrak{B}^{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
T
.

Let us now assume that we have an RKHS
H
on
T
with kernel
k
, where we refer to e.g.
[
3
,
4
]
,
and
[
46
, Chapter 4]

for some background information on these spaces.
Then

k
is bounded if and only if all functions in
H
are bounded, see e.g.
[
46
, Lemma 4.23]
.
Moreover, if
{\cal B}
is some
\sigma
-algebra on
T
for which
k
is

{\cal B}\otimes{\cal B}
-measurable, then
H\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
, see e.g.
[
46
, Lemma 4.24]
, and
the converse implication is true for separable
H
, see e.g.
[
46
, Lemmas 4.24 and 4.25]
.
Finally, let us assume that we have a finite measure space
(T,{\cal B},\nu)
and an RKHS
H
on
T
with
H\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}
.
As mentioned above this yields
H\hookrightarrow{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}
,
and therefore the linear map

\displaystyle I_{k,\nu}:H

\displaystyle\to{L_{2}(\nu)}

(2)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\mapsto[h]_{\sim}

is well-defined and continuous.
Finally, we say that
\nu
is
H
-positive, if
I_{k,\nu}
is injective, that is,
\nu(\{h\neq 0\})>0
for all
h\in H
with

.
For example, if

consists of continuous functions and

for all non-empty, open

, then
\nu
is

-positive.
On the other hand, if e.g.
\nu
is an empirical measure based upon
n
points
and
\dim H=\infty
, then

cannot be injective.

Recall that
a probability measure
\mathrm{P}
on
\mathbb{R}
is a Gaussian measure, if
there are
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\in[0,\infty)
such that
the characteristic function of
\mathrm{P}
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathrm{P}}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t\mu}\cdot\exp\biggl(-\frac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}\biggr)\,,\qquad\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Clearly, if
\sigma>0
, then
\mathrm{P}
is a normal distribution with expectation
\mu

and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
In the case
\sigma=0
, the probability measure
\mathrm{P}
is a point mass on
\mu
, that is
\mathrm{P}(\{\mu\})=1
.

Now let
X=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be an
\mathbb{R}
-valued, stochastic process on some probability space
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
. As usual,
we call the map
X_{\bullet}(\omega):t\mapsto X_{t}(\omega)
, where
\omega\in\Omega
is fixed, a path of
X
. Moreover,

is called a Gaussian process, if for all

t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in T
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, the distribution of
\alpha_{1}X_{t_{1}}+\dots+\alpha_{n}X_{t_{n}}
is a Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}
.
Furthermore, the Gaussian process is centered, if
\mathbb{E}X_{t}=0
for all
t\in T
. In this case, its covariance function
k_{X}:T\times T\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle k_{X}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\mathbb{E}(X_{t_{1}}X_{t_{2}})\,,\qquad\qquad t_{1},t_{2}\in T.

It is well known and easy to verify that
k_{X}
is a symmetric and positive definite function, and thus a (reproducing) kernel.
We write
H_{X}
for the corresponding RKHS of
k_{X}
and we further use the
short hand
I_{X,\nu}:=I_{k_{X},\nu}
.

3
General Results

In this section we present and investigate necessary and sufficient conditions
for the existence of RKHSs containing the paths of a given Gaussian process.

To this end, we first need a notion that controls the growth behavior of the functions
in a given RKHS.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
H
be an RKHS on
T
. Then we say that a function
b:T\to[0,\infty)
is an envelope for
H
if, for all
h\in H
and
t\in T
, we have

\displaystyle|h(t)|\leq\|h\|_{H}\cdot b(t)\,.

(3)

At first glance, Condition (
3
) my look restrictive. However, given an arbitrary RKHS
H
on
T
, a simple estimate yields

\displaystyle|h(t)|=\bigl|\langle h,k(\,\cdot\,,t)\rangle_{H}\bigr|\leq\|h\|_{H}\cdot\|k(\,\cdot\,,t)\|_{H}=\|h\|_{H}\cdot\sqrt{k(t,t)}

(4)

for all
h\in H
and
t\in T
. In other words, every RKHS has an envelope function, namely the diagonal function

t\mapsto\sqrt{k(t,t)}
. In general, however, the
measurability of this diagonal function does not follow from the measurability of all
h\in H
, and this missing implication may
cause some technical issues if we would directly work with the diagonal function.
Moreover, the diagonal function
is closely related to the considered RKHS, whereas choosing an envelope function is to some extend independent of a specific RKHS. For example,
by considering all RKHSs
H
on
T
having the constant envelope function
b=1
, we actually work with all RKHSs
H

satisfying
\|\id:H\to\ell_{\infty}(T)\|\leq 1
. For more information
on the relation between diagonal and envelope functions we refer to Lemma
6.7
.

Let us now assume that we have a centered Gaussian process

X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
whose RKHS satisfies
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
, where
{\cal B}
is some
\sigma
-algebra on
T
.
If
b:T\to[0,\infty)
is a measurable envelope function for
H_{X}

then Lemma
6.8
shows that

I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
defined in (
2
)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator for all measures
\nu
on
(T,{\cal B})
with
b\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
.
Our first theorem shows that
I_{X,\nu}
is even nuclear if the paths of
a suitable version
Y
of
X
are contained in some RKHS
H
with envelope function
b
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

with RKHS
H_{X}
. Moreover, let

H
be an RKHS on
T

and
Y
be a version of
X
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})>0\,.

Then, for all
\sigma
-algebras
{\cal B}
on
T
with
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
, all measurable envelope functions
b:T\to[0,\infty)
of
H
,
and all
measures
\nu
on
(T,{\cal B})
with
b\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
, the operator
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is well-defined and nuclear.

Note that there is a trade-off between the considered class of RKHS
H
in Theorem
3.2
and
the strength of the derived necessary condition. Indeed, if we have two measurable envelope functions
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
with
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
,
then every RKHS
H
having envelope
b_{1}
also has envelope
b_{2}
. In contrast, not every measure
\nu
with
b_{1}\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}

also needs to satisfy
b_{2}\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
. In other words, by increasing the envelope function we potentially shrink the class of considered
operators
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
. An extreme case in this trade-off are the constant envelope functions
that describe RKHSs containing bounded functions. The following
corollary presents the results of Theorem
3.2
for such envelopes.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space,
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

with RKHS
H_{X}
, and
{\cal B}
be a
\sigma
-algebra on
T
with
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
.
If there exist an RKHS
H
on
T
with
H\subset\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\ell_{\infty}(T)\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

and a version
Y
of
X
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})>0

then, for all
finite measures
\nu
on
(T,{\cal B})
, the operator
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is
well-defined and nuclear.

Note that there is a conceptual difference between
Theorems
1.2
and
3.2
, respectively Corollary
3.3
. Indeed, Theorem
1.2
provides a “test” for every single candidate RKHS
H
as discussed in the introduction.
In contrast, Theorem
3.2
and its Corollary
3.3
provide a general impossibility result: For example, if for a given
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}
we find
a finite measure
\nu
on
(T,{\cal B})
for which
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is
not
nuclear, then
there exist no RKHS
H\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}

and no version

of
X
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})>0\,.

While at first glance finding such a

may seem to be a hard task, we will see in Sections
4
and

5
that for e.g. bounded, measurable

with non-empty interior the Lebesgue measure is typically a sufficient choice. In fact, we will leverage there from well-established
results on the “embedding”
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(T)}
if
H_{X}
belongs to particular classes of RKHSs such as fractional
Sobolev spaces.

We finish this discussion with another corollary showing
that a certain “localization” reduces the problem to
bounded RKHS
H
even if we are interested in more general RKHSs.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

with RKHS
H_{X}
.
Moreover, let

H
be an RKHS on
T

and
Y
be a version of
X
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})>0\,.

Then, for all
\sigma
-algebras
{\cal B}
on
T
with
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
, all measurable envelope functions
b:T\to[0,\infty)
of
H
, all
M>0
with
T^{*}:=\{t\in T:b(t)\leq M\}\neq\emptyset
, and
all
finite measures
\nu
on
(T^{*},{\cal B}_{|T^{*}})
, the operator

\displaystyle I_{X_{|T^{*}},\nu}:H_{X_{|T^{*}}}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}

is nuclear, where

H_{X_{|T^{*}}}\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B}_{|T^{*}})}
denotes the RKHS of the restricted process

X_{|T^{*}}:=(X_{t})_{t\in T^{*}}
.

Note that on
T^{*}
, every function
f\in H
is bounded, namely we have

|f(t)|\leq\|f\|_{H}\cdot M
for all
t\in T^{*}
. In other words, the restriction of
H
onto
T^{*}

consists of bounded, measurable functions, and therefore we are actually in the situation of
Corollary
3.3
. The proof of Corollary
3.4

uses this observation.

In some other situations, however, the RKHS
H_{X}
and the operator
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
may not be
as amenable as desired.
In the following, we will thus present two equivalent characterizations of
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
being nuclear, which
sometimes make it easier
to work with this property.

We will begin with a simple yet useful characterization that makes it possible to alter the space
H_{X}
by some finite dimensional space.
To this end, we need the following definition.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be RKHSs on
T
. Then we say that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are essentially equivalent, if there exist
RKHSs
H_{1}^{\star}
and
H_{2}^{\star}
on
T
with
\dim H_{1}^{\star}<\infty
,
\dim H_{2}^{\star}<\infty
, and

\displaystyle H_{1}+H_{1}^{\star}\circeq H_{2}+H_{2}^{\star}\,.

(5)

It is straightforward to check that the notion of being essentially equivalent
gives an equivalence relation on the set of all RKHSs on
T
, but in the following we do not need this.
In addition, another simple calculation shows that
if
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are essentially equivalent, then both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are also
essentially equivalent to
H_{1}+H_{2}
.
Furthermore, RKHSs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
with
H_{1}\circeq H_{2}
are obviously also essentially equivalent.
Finally,
the following lemma shows that in some situations the essential equivalence can be easily verified with
the help of the kernels of the involved RKHSs.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be RKHSs on
T
with kernels
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, and

k_{1}^{\star}
and
k_{2}^{\star}
be kernels on
T
with finite dimensional RKHSs.
Then
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are essentially equivalent, if we have

\displaystyle k_{1}-k_{2}=k_{2}^{\star}-k_{1}^{\star}\,.

Now, the following result shows that
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is nuclear if and only if
I_{H,\nu}:H\to{L_{2}(\nu)}

is nuclear for all RKHS
H
that are essentially equivalent to
H_{X}
. This will make it possible to
switch from
H_{X}
to an essentially equivalent

H
for which
I_{H,\nu}:H\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is better understood.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
(T,{\cal B},\nu)
be a measure space and

H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be essentially equivalent RKHSs on
T
with
H_{1}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
and
H_{1}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
.
If, in addition,
b\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
is an envelope function for both

H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, then
I_{H_{1},\nu}:H_{1}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is nuclear if and only if
I_{H_{2},\nu}:H_{2}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}

is nuclear.

Theorem
3.7
can be used to establish non-existence results with the help of
Theorem
3.2
and its Corollary
3.3
, see e.g. Example
5.2
.
Combining the
next result with Theorem
1.2
shows that in some situations
the essential equivalence of RKHSs can also be used to find RKHSs containing the paths of a given Gaussian process,
see e.g. Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let

X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process on a probability space
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

and
H
be an RKHS
that is essentially equivalent to
H_{X}
. If there exists an RKHS
H^{\dagger}
on
T
with
H\ll H^{\dagger}
, then there also
exists an RKHS
H_{X}^{\dagger}
on
T
that is essentially equivalent to
H^{\dagger}
and satisfies both
H_{X}^{\dagger}\subset H_{X}+H^{\dagger}

and

\displaystyle H_{X}\ll H_{X}^{\dagger}\,.

Our next goal is to present another equivalent way to
express that
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is nuclear as well as to
construct
an
H
with
H_{X}\ll H
in some situations.
To this end, we assume that
b\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
is an envelope function for
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
,
where we note that
in the situation of Theorem
3.2
,
we do have
H_{X}\subset H

by Theorem
1.2
, and therefore the envelope function of
H
in Theorem

3.2
is, modulo a constant, also an envelope function for
H_{X}
.
In this sense, the assumption of
b
being an envelope for
H_{X}
fits to
our previous results. Let us now
consider the operator

T_{X,\nu}:=I_{X,\nu}\circ I_{X,\nu}^{*}:{L_{2}(\nu)}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
, where
I_{X,\nu}^{*}:{L_{2}(\nu)}\to H_{X}
denotes the adjoint of
I_{X,\nu}
. Then
some elementary calculations, see Lemma
6.8
, show that
T_{X,\nu}
is given by

\displaystyle T_{X,\nu}f=\int_{T}k_{X}(\,\cdot\,,t)f(t)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(t)\,,\qquad\qquad f\in{L_{2}(\nu)},

where the right-hand side is viewed as a
\nu
-equivalence class in
{L_{2}(\nu)}
. By construction,
T_{X,\nu}
is positive and self-adjoint,
and it is also compact, since
I_{X,\nu}
is Hilbert-Schmidt by our assumption
of
b\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
being an envelope for
H_{X}
, see again Lemma
6.8
.
Consequently, the spectral theorem, see Section
6.1
, shows that

T_{X,\nu}
has at most countably many non-zero eigenvalues
(\mu_{i}(T_{X,\nu}))_{i\in I}
, where we have set either
I:=\{1,\dots,n\}
or
I:=\mathbb{N}
,
and where we have included the geometric multiplicities. Moreover, these eigenvalues are positive and
there exists an orthonormal system (ONS)
([f_{i}]_{\sim})_{i\in I}\subset{L_{2}(\nu)}
such that each
[f_{i}]_{\sim}

is an eigenfunction
of
T_{X,\nu}
with eigenvalue
\mu_{i}:=\mu_{i}(T_{X,\nu})
.
We now define

\displaystyle e_{i}:=\mu_{i}^{-1}I_{X,\nu}^{*}[f_{i}]_{\sim}\,,\qquad\qquad i\in I.

(6)

This gives
e_{i}\in H_{X}
and
[e_{i}]_{\sim}=I_{X,\nu}e_{i}=\mu_{i}^{-1}T_{X,\nu}[f_{i}]_{\sim}=[f_{i}]_{\sim}
for all
i\in I
.
In other words, we may choose representatives
e_{i}
of
[f_{i}]_{\sim}
that are contained
in
H
. Finally,
T_{X,\nu}
is nuclear since

I_{X,\nu}
is Hilbert-Schmidt, see Lemma
6.1
, and therefore
(\mu_{i}(T_{X,\nu}))_{i\in I}
is summable.

Now recall from
[
47
]
that in some cases the eigenfunctions and -values can be used to construct RKHSs surrounding
H_{X}
.
To briefly describe this construction, let us fix a
\beta>0
for which we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}^{\beta}\,e_{i}^{2}(t)<\infty\,,\qquad\qquad t\in T.

(7)

Then
[
47
, Lemma 2.6]
shows that

\displaystyle k_{X}^{\beta}(t,t^{\prime}):=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}^{\beta}e_{i}(t)e_{i}(t^{\prime})\,,\qquad\qquad t,t^{\prime}\in T

(8)

defines a kernel on
T
whose RKHS is given by

\displaystyle H_{X}^{\beta}:=\biggl\{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\mu_{i}^{\beta/2}e_{i}:(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in\ell_{2}(I)\biggr\}\,,

where the series converges pointwise and
\ell_{2}(I)
denotes the space of all 2-summable families

(a_{i})_{i\in I}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
(\mu_{i}^{\beta/2}e_{i})_{i\in I}
is an ONB of
H_{X}^{\beta}
and we have
H_{X}^{\beta}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
.
Obviously, Condition (
7
) is monotone in the sense that if it holds for some
\beta
then it also holds for all exponents

\alpha>\beta
.
In addition, for such pairs we have
H_{X}^{\alpha}\subset H_{X}^{\beta}
.
Finally, if
\nu
is
H
-positive, then
(\sqrt{\mu_{i}}e_{i})_{i\in I}
is an ONB of
H_{X}
, see

[
47
, Theorem 3.1]
, and thus we have
H_{X}^{1}=H_{X}
. Consequently, if in this case we have found a
\beta\in(0,1)
with
(
7
) then

H_{X}\subset H_{X}^{\beta}
.

With these preparations we can now present our last result of this section, where we note that
ii)
essentially simplifies
similar results of
[
44
, Section 5]
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a probability space,
(T,{\cal B},\nu)
be a measure space and

X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
such that

b\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\nu)}
is an envelope for
H_{X}
and
H_{X}\subset{{\cal L}_{0}({\cal B})}
. Moreover, let
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
be the non-zero eigenvalues of

T_{X,\nu}
and
(e_{i})_{i\in I}\subset H_{X}
be a family such that
([e_{i}]_{\sim})_{i\in I}\subset{L_{2}(\nu)}
is an ONS in
{L_{2}(\nu)}

and each
[e_{i}]_{\sim}

is an eigenfunction
of
T_{X,\nu}
to the eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
.
Then the following statements hold true:

i)

The operator
I_{X,\nu}:H_{X}\to{L_{2}(\nu)}
is nuclear if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\sqrt{\mu_{i}}<\infty.

(9)

ii)

Assume that
\nu
is
H_{X}
-positive and we have a
\beta\in(0,1)
satisfying (
7
). Then

if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}^{1-\beta}<\infty.

(10)

In this case, there exists a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H_{X}^{\beta}\})=1
.

To illustrate Theorem

we assume for a moment that
\nu
is
H_{X}
-positive and

.
Then, if (

Conversely, if (

, and hence

of

Furthermore, note that in this case

is bounded, and hence we have

.
In other words,
if we have an

Finally, recall from

Finally,

4
Results for RKHSs of Sobolev Type

In this section we investigate how our main results can be used to investigate Gaussian processes whose RKHSs are essentially equivalent
to a fractional Sobolev space, or equal a Sobolev space of dominating mixed smoothness.
To this end, recall that for
s\in(0,\infty)
, the fractional Sobolev spaces, or Bessel potential spaces, are defined by

\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\bigl\{f\in{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:(1+\|\cdot\|^{2})^{s/2}\hat{f}\in{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigr\}\,,

where

\hat{f}
is the Fourier transformation of
f
, see e.g.
[
30
, Section 3.1]
or
[
50
, Section 2.2.2]
.
By setting

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\bigl\|\,(1+\|\cdot\|^{2})^{s/2}\hat{f}\,\bigr\|_{{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}

the space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
becomes a Hilbert space and for
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have
H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cong W^{m}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where

W^{m}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the classical Sobolev space with smoothness
m
, see e.g.
[
50
, (3) in Chapter 2.5.6]
or
[
1
, Chapter 7, Paragraph 7.62]
. Moreover, the definition of the fractional Sobolev spaces immediately ensures

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
0<r<s<\infty
, and for
s>d/2
we have

\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\hookrightarrow C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(11)

by Sobolev’s embedding theorem, see the beginning of Section
6.7
for details and references.
In other words, in the case
s>d/2
the space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be viewed as an RKHS with bounded and continuous kernel.
Finally, for bounded, measurable
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s>d/2
we define

H^{s}(T):=\{f_{|T}:f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
and equip this space with the norm

\displaystyle\|h\|_{H^{s}(T)}:=\inf\bigl\{\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mbox{ with }f_{|T}=h\bigr\}\,.

(12)

Here, we followed
[
15
, Chapter 2.5.1]
and in particular we refer to the discussion there, when such restricted
spaces also have an
intrinsic
description.
In any case,
as inclusions between BSFs
E
and
F
on some set
T_{0}
remain unchanged when both spaces are restricted to some subset
T\subset T_{0}
,
see e.g.
[
42
, Proposition 3]
for a general statement, the inclusions above lead to

H^{s}(T)\hookrightarrow H^{r}(T)\hookrightarrow C_{b}(T)\hookrightarrow{{\cal L}_{\infty}(T)}

for all
s>r>d/2
. In this case, both
H^{s}(T)
and
H^{r}(T)
can thus be viewed to be RKHSs.

With these preparations, we can now exactly describe those Gaussian processes that have an
RKHS that is essentially equivalent to a fractional Sobolev space and whose paths are contained in some RKHS.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and measurable with non-empty interior. Moreover, let

(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

whose RKHS
H_{X}
consists of continuous functions and is essentially equivalent to
H^{s}(T)
for some
s>d/2
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

i)

We have
s>d
.

ii)

There exist an RKHS
H\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}(T)}

and a version
Y
of
X
such that
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

iii)

There exist an RKHS
H
on
T

and a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

In this case, for all
r\in(d/2,s-d/2)
there actually exists
such an
H
that is essentially equivalent to
H^{r}(T)
.

As the implication from
ii)
to
i)
is based upon Corollary
3.3
we see that the necessary condition
derived there is sharp in the setup of Theorem
4.1
. Below in Example
5.6

we apply Theorem
4.1
to the class of Matérn kernels that are popular in many applications, see
e.g.
[
39
,
38
]
.

Theorem
4.1
shows that we require a strong, dimension dependent regularity
to ensure that the paths are contained
in an RKHS. Interestingly, such a dimension dependent regularity disappears if we switch to (tensor) product kernels,
which are used in e.g. efficient implementations of Gaussian processes for machine learning
[
19
,
16
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let

(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space,
T=(0,1)^{d}
, and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
whose
covariance function is of the form

\displaystyle k_{X}(t,t^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}k_{0}(t_{i},t_{i}^{\prime})\,,\qquad\qquad t,t^{\prime}\in T,

where
k_{0}:(0,1)\times(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is a kernel with RKHS
H_{0}\circeq H^{s}(0,1)
for some
s>1/2
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

i)

We have
s>1
.

ii)

There exist an RKHS
H\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}(T)}

and a version
Y
of
X
such that
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

In this case, we may choose a Sobolev space of dominating mixed smoothness
H\cong B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)\otimes_{2}\dots\otimes_{2}B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)
for any
r\in(1/2,s-1/2)
.

5
Some Concrete Examples

In this section we present some explicit examples of Gaussian processes
that do or do not have their paths in a suitable RKHS.
To this end,
we assume throughout this section that the considered Gaussian processes are defined on some complete
probability space
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
.
Moreover,
\lambda
and
\lambda^{d}
denote the Lebesgue measures on the considered
subsets
T\subset\mathbb{R}
, respectively
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

With these preparations, our first example considers the Wiener process.

Example 5.1
.

Let
T:=[0,1]
and

W:=(W_{t})_{t\in T}
be the Wiener process.
Then
I_{W,\lambda}:H_{W}\to{L_{2}(T)}
is not nuclear and
there exists no RKHS
H
and
\mathrm{P}(\{W_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

The proof of Example
5.1
shows that
I_{W,\lambda}:H_{W}\to{L_{2}(T)}
fails to be nuclear by a thin margin.
This observation will be further investigated in Example
5.4
, which considers the fractional Brownian motion.

Since the Brownian bridge
(B_{t})_{t\in T}
on
T:=[0,1]
can be defined by
B_{t}:=(1-t)W_{t}
, it is not surprising
that the negative result of Example
5.1
carries over to the Brownian bridge. The details are formulated in the following example,
whose proof also illustrates the usefulness of the notion of essentially equivalent RKHSs.

Example 5.2
.

Let
T:=[0,1]
and
(B_{t})_{t\in T}
be the Brownian bridge. Then there exists no RKHS
H
such that
\mathrm{P}(\{B_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

Like the Brownian bridge, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is closely related to the Wiener process, and hence
it is again not surprising that the negative result of Example
5.1
carries over to
the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. The following example presents the details for two variants of this process.

Example 5.3
.

Let
T:=[0,1]

and

X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process whose
covariance function is given by either

\displaystyle k_{X}^{(1)}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle:=a\exp(-\sigma|t_{1}-t_{2}|)\,,

or

\displaystyle k_{X}^{(2)}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle:=a\exp(-\sigma|t_{1}-t_{2}|)-a\exp(-\sigma(t_{1}+t_{2}))\,,

where
a,\sigma>0
are some constants. Then there exist no RKHS
H
and no version
Y
of
X

with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
.

Our next example generalizes Example
5.1
to the
fractional Brownian motion. Here it turns out that the results depend on the Hurst parameter.

Example 5.4
.

Let
T:=[0,1]
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Moreover, let

B^{(\alpha)}=(B^{(\alpha)}_{t})_{t\in T}
be the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
\alpha
, that is, the centered Gaussian process with continuous paths and
covariance function

\displaystyle k_{B^{(\alpha)}}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(|s|^{2\alpha}+|t|^{2\alpha}-|t-s|^{2\alpha}\bigr)\,,\qquad\qquad t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,1]\,.

Then there exists an
RKHS
H
with
\mathrm{P}(\{B_{\bullet}^{(\alpha)}\in H\})=1

if and only if
\alpha>1/2
.
Moreover,
in the case
\alpha>1/2
we may choose

H=H^{s}(T)\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}(T)}
for any
s\in(1/2,\alpha)
.

In the case
\alpha<1/2
the paths of the fractional Brownian motion are “rougher”
than the paths of the Wiener process. Since we have already seen in Example

5.1
that the paths of the Wiener process are not contained in an RKHS,
it is therefore not surprising that the paths of
B^{(\alpha)}
are not contained in an
RKHS, either.
In the case
\alpha>1/2
, it is well-known that the paths of
B^{(\alpha)}
are
\beta
-Hölder-continuous for all
\beta\in(0,\alpha)
. The positive result then follows by standard
embeddings between Besov spaces, see the proof of Example
5.4
for details.

The fractional Brownian motion is closely related to the Riemann-Liouville process, see e.g.
[
27
, Example 3.4]
or
[
34
]
, which is considered in the following example.

Example 5.5
.

Let
T:=[0,1]
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Moreover, let
R^{(\alpha)}=(R^{(\alpha)}_{t})_{t\in T}
be
the Riemann-Liouville process with index
\alpha
, that is, the centered Gaussian process with continuous paths given by the Itô-Integral

\displaystyle R^{(\alpha)}_{t}:=\frac{1}{\Gamma{(\alpha+1/2})}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\,,\qquad\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then there exists an
RKHS
H
with
\mathrm{P}(\{R_{\bullet}^{(\alpha)}\in H\})=1

if and only if
\alpha>1/2
.
Moreover, in the case
\alpha>1/2
we may choose
H=H^{s}(T)\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}(T)}
for any
s\in(1/2,\alpha)
.

The next example, which considers Gaussian processes on a higher dimensional
T
, uses our result for RKHSs of Sobolev type
formulated in Theorem
4.1
. Analogous results can thus be obtained for other kernels of Sobolev type,
such as the Wendland kernels, which are often attractive from a computational point of view, see
[
51
, Chapter 9]
.

Example 5.6
.

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded and measurable with non-empty interior.
Moreover, let
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian
process whose covariance function is a Matérn kernel of order
\alpha>0
and width
\sigma>0
, that is

\displaystyle k_{X}(t_{1},t_{2})=\bigl(\sigma\|t_{1}-t_{2}\|\bigr)^{\alpha}h_{\alpha}\bigl(\sigma\|t_{1}-t_{2}\|\bigr)\,,\qquad\qquad t_{1},t_{2}\in T,

where
h_{\alpha}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second type of order
\alpha
. Then there
exist
an RKHS
H
and a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1

if and only if
\alpha>d/2
.
Moreover, in the case
\alpha>d/2
we may choose
H=H^{r}(T)
for any
r\in(d/2,\alpha)
.

To compare the smoothness of the paths and the elements of the RKHS
in Example
5.6
, we quickly assume that
\alpha>d/2
. Then
the paths of
X
are contained in
H^{\alpha-\varepsilon}(T)
for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.
Moreover,
the proof of Example
5.6
uses that the
RKHS
H_{\alpha,\sigma}
of the Matérn kernel equals
H^{\alpha+d/2}
up to equivalent norms.
Consequently, there is a
d/2+\varepsilon
-gap, when the smoothness is measured in the Sobolev sense. This gap coincides with the one observed in

[
44
]
, where it is also shown that it is essentially sharp,
see also
[
23
]
for a more detailed analysis and
[
41
]
for
similar results for weakly stationary processes. Finally,
[
44
, Example 4.8]
shows that in the case
d=1
these Sobolev results
improve classical results
on the continuous differentiability of the paths of
X
, of e.g.
[
9
]
.

Our last example considers stationary processes on compact Abelian groups
T
with Haar measure
\lambda
.
It mostly relies on the fact that for translation invariant,
\mathbb{C}
-valued kernels the characters of
T

form on ONB of
{L_{2}(\lambda,\mathbb{C})}
consisting of eigenvectors of
T_{k,\lambda}
.

Example 5.7
.

Let
(T,+)
be a compact Abelian group with Haar measure
\lambda

and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process whose covariance
function
k_{X}
is measurable and satisfies

\displaystyle k_{X}(t_{1},t_{2})=k_{X}(t_{1}-t_{2},0)\,,\qquad\qquad t_{1},t_{2}\in T.

Then there exist an RKHS
H\subset{{\cal L}_{\infty}({\cal B})}
and
a version
Y
of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\sqrt{\mu_{i}(T_{X,\lambda})}<\infty.

Moreover, in this case we may choose
H=H_{X}^{1/2}
and we have
H_{X}^{1/2}\subset C(T)
.

Since Example
5.7
is an application of Theorem
3.9
, it
can be easily extended to situations in which the spectral properties of
k_{X}
are well understood
in the sense of the discussion following Theorem
3.9
.
Such situations
include
e.g. isotropic Gaussian random fields on

\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and with some more work also on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
,
if
\lambda
is the surface measure. We refer to
[
26
]
for the details on the required spectral properties and to
[
21
]
for an application for neural networks.

6.1
Hilbert-Schmidt and Nuclear Operators

In this subsection we briefly recall some aspects of compact operators between Hilbert spaces.
To this end, let
H
be a Hilbert space and
R:H\to H
be a compact, positive, and self-adjoint operator.
Then the spectral theorem for such operators, e.g.
[
24
, Chapter V.2.3]
or
[
52
, Theorem VI.3.2]
, shows that
we have at most countably many non-zero eigenvalues
(\mu_{i}(R))_{i\in I}
, where we have set either
I:=\{1,\dots,n\}
or
I:=\mathbb{N}
,
and where we have included the geometric multiplicities. Moreover, there exists an ONS
(e_{i})_{i\in I}
in
H
such that

e_{i}
is an eigenvector
of
R
to the eigenvalue
\mu_{i}(R)
for all
i\in I
.
Finally, these eigenvalues are positive and in the case
I=\mathbb{N}
they can be ordered to form a decreasing sequence converging to
0
.
In the following, we always assume a decreasing order. Finally, the spectral theorem leads to the following representation

\displaystyle Rx=\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}(R)\langle x,e_{i}\rangle e_{i}\,,\qquad\qquad x\in H.

(13)

In particular, we have
H=\ker R\oplus\overline{\spann\{e_{i}:i\in I\}}^{H}
.

If
S:H_{1}\to H_{2}
is a compact operator between two Hilbert spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, we write

S^{*}:H_{2}\to H_{1}
for the adjoint of
S
. Then
the operator
S\circ S^{*}:H_{2}\to H_{2}

is compact, positive, and self-adjoint, and hence we can apply the spectral theorem to
S\circ S^{*}
.
By doing so, we can define the
singular numbers of
S
by

\displaystyle s_{i}(S):=\sqrt{\mu_{i}(S\circ S^{*})}\,,\qquad\qquad i\in I,

and
s_{i}(S):=0
if
I:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
i>n
.
Recall that for compact, positive, and self-adjoint
R:H\to H

we have

\displaystyle s_{i}(R)=\sqrt{\mu_{i}(R\circ R^{*})}=\sqrt{\mu_{i}(R^{2})}=\mu_{i}(R)\,,

(14)

where the last identity directly follows from the spectral series representation, see e.g.
[
24
, Equation (2.21) in Chapter V.2.3]
or
[
52
, Theorem VI.3.2]
.
For compact
S:H_{1}\to H_{2}
this implies

\displaystyle s_{i}^{2}(S)=\mu_{i}(S\circ S^{*})=s_{i}(S\circ S^{*})\,,

(15)

and in addition, there exist ONSs
(e_{i})_{i\in I}
in
H_{1}
and
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
in
H_{2}
with

\displaystyle Sx=\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}(S)\langle x,e_{i}\rangle_{H_{1}}f_{i}\,,\qquad\qquad x\in H_{1},

(16)

see e.g.
[
24
, Equation (2.23) in Chapter V.2.3]
or
[
52
, Theorem VI.3.6]
.
Finally, recall that a compact
S:H_{1}\to H_{2}
is nuclear if and only if
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}(S)<\infty\,,

(17)

see e.g.
[
5
, Chapter 11.2]
. Moreover, by definition
S
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator
if
\sum_{j\in J}\|Se_{j}\|_{H_{2}}^{2}<\infty
for some (and then all)
ONB(s)
(e_{j})_{j\in J}
of
H_{1}
, or equivalently,
if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}^{2}(S)<\infty\,,

(18)

see e.g.
[
5
, Chapter 11.3, Theorem 1]
. By (
15
) we then immediately obtain the following
elementary yet useful lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
S:H_{1}\to H_{2}
be a compact operator between two Hilbert spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.
Then
S
is a
Hilbert-Schmidt operator if and only if
S\circ S^{*}
is nuclear.

Finally, it is sometimes helpful to compare the singular numbers with some other
s
-number scales in the sense of
[
36
, Chapter 2]
.
To this end, let us first recall that for a given bounded linear operator
S:E\to F
between two Banach spaces
E
and
F
,
the
n
-th approximation number is defined by

\displaystyle a_{n}(S):=\inf\bigl\{\|S-A\|\,\bigl|\,A:E\to F\mbox{ bounded and linear with }\rank A<n\bigr\}\,.

(19)

Furthermore,
the
n
-th Kolmogorov number is defined by

\displaystyle d_{n}(S):=\inf\bigl\{\varepsilon>0:\exists F_{\varepsilon}\mbox{ subspace of $F$ with $\dim F_{\varepsilon}<n$ and }SB_{E}\subset F_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon B_{F}\bigr\}\,,

(20)

where,
B_{E}
and
B_{F}
denote the closed unit balls in
E
and
F
and we followed the exposition in
[
7
, Chapter 2.2]
.
It is well known that the approximation
and
Kolmogorov numbers form an
s
-scale
in the sense of
[
36
, Chapter 2.2.1]
. We refer to
[
36
, Chapters 2.3 and 2.5]
and
[
7
, Chapters 2.1 and 2.2]
for corresponding results. In addition, the
Kolmogorov numbers are a surjective
s
-scale, see e.g.
[
36
, Chapter 2.5]
.
Moreover,
S:E\to F
is compact if and only if
d_{n}(S)\to 0
, see
e.g.
[
7
, page 49]
. Furthermore, for operators
S:H_{1}\to H_{2}
between Hilbert spaces
we have
a_{n}(S)=d_{n}(S)
, see e.g.
[
35
, Theorem 11.3.4]
, and if

S:H_{1}\to H_{2}
is compact we even have

\displaystyle s_{n}(S)=a_{n}(S)=d_{n}(S)\,,\qquad\qquad n\geq 1,

(21)

by
[
35
, Proposition 11.3.3]
as (
16
) is a so-called Schmidt representation in
the sense of
[
35
, D.3.2]
.
In other words, we may freely switch between these quantities as soon as we know that
S
is compact.

6.2
Nuclear Dominance

In this subsection we show that the notion of nuclear dominance used in
[
28
]

is equivalent to ours. To this end, we begin with recalling
[
28
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be RKHSs over
T
with
H_{1}\subset H_{2}
.
Then the resulting embedding map

\displaystyle J:H_{1}

\displaystyle\to H_{2}

(22)

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto h_{1}

is linear and bounded. Moreover, there exists a unique linear
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}:H_{2}\to H_{2}
that satisfies both

\ran D_{H_{1},H_{2}}\subset H_{1}

and

\displaystyle\langle h_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{H_{2}}=\langle D_{H_{1},H_{2}}h_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{H_{1}}\,,\qquad\qquad h_{1}\in H_{1},h_{2}\in H_{2}.

(23)

In addition,
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}
is bounded, positive and self-adjoint.

Recall that
[
28
]
speaks of nuclear dominance if
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}
is nuclear.
The following simple lemma shows that this notion is equivalent to our Definition
1.1
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be RKHSs over
T
with
H_{1}\subset H_{2}
and
J:H_{1}\to H_{2}
be
the corresponding embedding map (
22
).
Then we have

\displaystyle D_{H_{1},H_{2}}=J\circ J^{*}\,.

In particular,
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}
is nuclear if and only if
J
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator.
If, in this case,
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
are the at most countably many non-zero eigenvalues
(including geometric multiplicities) of
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}
and
(f_{i})_{i\in I}\subset H_{2}
is an ONS of
corresponding eigenvectors, then we have

\displaystyle H_{2}=\ker D_{H_{1},H_{2}}\oplus\overline{\spann\{f_{i}:i\in I\}}^{H_{2}}=\ker D_{H_{1},H_{2}}\oplus\overline{\ran J}^{H_{2}}\,.

(24)

Moreover, the vectors
e_{i}:=\mu_{i}^{-1}J^{*}f_{i}\in H_{1}
satisfy
Je_{i}=f_{i}
and
(\sqrt{\mu_{i}}e_{i})_{i\in I}
is an ONS in
H_{1}
.

Proof of Lemma
6.3
:

Let us write
R:=J\circ J^{*}:H_{2}\to H_{2}
.
By construction, its range satisfies
\ran R\subset H_{1}
. Moreover, for
h_{1}\in H_{1}
and
h_{2}\in H_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\langle h_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{H_{2}}=\langle h_{2},Jh_{1}\rangle_{H_{2}}=\langle J^{*}h_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{H_{1}}=\langle(J\circ J^{*})h_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{H_{1}}=\langle Rh_{2},h_{1}\rangle_{H_{1}}\,.

Consequently,
R
satisfies (
23
), and by uniqueness stated in Theorem
6.2
we can thus conclude
R=D_{H_{1},H_{2}}
.
The equivalence is a direct consequence of Lemma
6.1
and
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}=J\circ J^{*}
.

Moreover, the first identity in (
24
) holds as noted below
(
13
). To establish the second identity
we first show

\displaystyle\ker J^{*}=\ker D_{H_{1},H_{2}}\,.

(25)

Here the inclusion “
\subset
” follows from
D_{H_{1},H_{2}}=J\circ J^{*}
, and
the converse inclusion follows from

\displaystyle\|J^{*}h_{2}\|_{H_{1}}=\langle J^{*}h_{2},J^{*}h_{2}\rangle_{H_{1}}=\langle D_{H_{1},H_{2}}h_{2},h_{2}\rangle_{H_{2}}=0\,,\qquad\qquad h_{2}\in\ker D_{H_{1},H_{2}}.

Now, combining the first identity in (
24
) with (
25
) yields

\displaystyle\overline{\spann\{f_{i}:i\in I\}}^{H_{2}}=(\ker D_{H_{1},H_{2}})^{\perp}=(\ker J^{*})^{\perp}=\overline{\ran J}^{H_{2}}\,,

(26)

where the last equation is a textbook formula, see e.g.
[
8
, Theorem II.2.19 with Corollary I.2.10]
.

Finally, the definition of the
e_{i}
immediately yields

\displaystyle Je_{i}=\mu_{i}^{-1}(J\circ J^{*})f_{i}=\mu_{i}^{-1}D_{H_{1},H_{2}}f_{i}=f_{i}\,,

as well as

\displaystyle\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle_{H_{1}}=\mu_{i}^{-1}\mu_{j}^{-1}\langle J^{*}f_{i},J^{*}f_{j}\rangle_{H_{1}}=\mu_{i}^{-1}\mu_{j}^{-1}\langle f_{i},D_{H_{1},H_{2}}f_{j}\rangle_{H_{2}}=\mu_{i}^{-1}\langle f_{i},f_{j}\rangle_{H_{2}}\,.

With the help of this identity we easily see that
(\sqrt{\mu_{i}}e_{i})_{i\in I}
is an ONS in
H_{1}
.
∎

6.3
Choosing Small RKHSs

In this subsection, we show how we can “shrink” the RKHS containing the paths
of a suitable version of a Gaussian process in the situation of Theorem
1.2
.

To this end,
let us recall that a map
\xi:\Omega\to H
from a measurable space
(\Omega,{\cal A})

into a Hilbert space
H
is weakly measurable, if
\langle\xi,f\rangle_{H}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

is measurable for all
f\in H
. Moreover, it is called strongly measurable
if it is weakly measurable and
\xi(\Omega)
is separable in
H
. In this case,
the function
\omega\to\|\xi(\omega)\|_{H}
is also measurable.

Now, our first result shows that we can always assume that the space
H
is separable as
long as we are willing to potentially switch the versions of
X
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

with RKHS
H_{X}
. Moreover, let

H
be an RKHS on
T
with kernel
k
and
Y
be a version of
X
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})>0\,.

Then there exists a separable RKHS
H^{\dagger}\subset H
and another version
Y^{\dagger}
of
X

with
Y_{\bullet}^{\dagger}(\omega)\in H^{\dagger}
for all
\omega\in\Omega

such that the resulting map
\Omega\to H^{\dagger}
given by
\omega\mapsto Y_{\bullet}^{\dagger}(\omega)

is strongly measurable and satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{P}}|\langle Y_{\bullet}^{\dagger},f\rangle_{H^{\dagger}}|^{2}=\langle D_{H_{X},H^{\dagger}}f,f\rangle_{H}\,,\qquad\qquad f\in H^{\dagger}.

Proof of Lemma
6.4
:

Theorem
1.2
gives
H_{X}\ll H
and a version
\tilde{Y}
of
X
with

\mathrm{P}(\{\tilde{Y}_{\bullet}\in H\})=1
. Obviously, we may assume without loss of generality that
this version actually satisfies
\tilde{Y}_{\bullet}(\omega)\in H
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Let us now define
\xi:\Omega\to H

by
\xi(\omega):=\tilde{Y}_{\bullet}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Then

[
28
, Lemma 2.1]
shows that
\xi
is weakly measurable and
the reproducing property of
k
yields

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{t}(\omega)=\langle\xi(\omega),k(t,\,\cdot\,)\rangle_{H}\,,\qquad\qquad\omega\in\Omega,t\in T.

(27)

Moreover, since
\tilde{Y}
is a version of
X
we have
H_{\tilde{Y}}=H_{X}\ll H
.
Consequently

[
28
, Corollary 3.1]

gives a strongly measurable
\xi^{\dagger}:\Omega\to H
with
\|\xi^{\dagger}\|_{H}\in{{\cal L}_{2}(\mathrm{P})}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\bigl(\bigl\{\omega\in\Omega:\langle\xi^{\dagger}(\omega),f\rangle_{H}=\langle\xi(\omega),f\rangle_{H}\bigr\}\bigr)=1\,,\qquad\qquad f\in H.

(28)

Alternatively, one can obtain
\xi^{\dagger}
by
[
14
, Theorem 5.1]
, see also
[
11
, page 89]
for a more detailed proof,
as
\xi
is Pettis integrable and Hilbert spaces have the Radon-Nikodym property.

We now define
H^{\dagger}:=\overline{\xi^{\dagger}(\Omega)}^{H}
. Since
\xi^{\dagger}
is strongly measurable,
H^{\dagger}
is separable. In addition, as a closed subspace of an RKHS it is
an RKHS. Let us now consider
process
Y^{\dagger}:=(Y^{\dagger}_{t})_{t\in T}
defined by

\displaystyle Y^{\dagger}_{t}(\omega):=\langle\xi^{\dagger}(\omega),k(t,\,\cdot\,)\rangle_{H}\,,\qquad\qquad t\in T,\omega\in\Omega.

Then our construction gives

Y_{\bullet}^{\dagger}(\omega)=\xi^{\dagger}(\omega)\in H^{\dagger}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.
Moreover, combining this definition with (
28
)
and (
27
) we see that
Y^{\dagger}
is a version of
\tilde{Y}
, and thus also a version of
X
.

Now, since
Y^{\dagger}
is a version of
X
with
\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}^{\dagger}\in H^{\dagger}\})=1

another application of Theorem
1.2
yields

H_{Y^{\dagger}}=H_{X}\ll H^{\dagger}
.
Applying

[
28
, Corollary 3.1]
, see also
[
28
, Equation (3.2)]
, to
Y^{\dagger}

then gives the last assertion.
∎

The next result, which is the main theorem of this subsection, refines the
“shrinking” to a somewhat minimal space.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})
be a complete probability space and
X:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
be a centered Gaussian process over
(\Omega,{\cal A},\mathrm{P})

with RKHS
H_{X}
. Moreover, let

H
be an RKHS on
T
and
Y
be a version of
X
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}\in H\})>0\,.

Then there exist a version
Y^{*}
of
X
and a separable RKHS
H^{*}
on
T
such that
H_{X}\subset H^{*}
is dense in
H^{*}
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}(\{Y_{\bullet}^{*}\in H^{*}\})=1\,.

Proof of Theorem
6.5
:

Without loss of generality we may assume that
Y=Y^{\dagger}
and
H=H^{\dagger}
, where

Y^{\dagger}
and
H^{\dagger}
are according to
Lemma
6.4
. In particular, we may assume that
H
is separable.
In the following we consider
Y^{*}:=Y
, so that it remains to find
H^{*}
.

Since Theorem
1.2
ensures
H_{X}\ll H
,
Lemma
6.3
gives us
an at most countable ONS
(f_{i})_{i\in I}\subset H

consisting of eigenvectors of
D_{H_{X},H}
with non-zero eigenvalues
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}

such that

\displaystyle H=\ker D_{H_{X},H}\oplus\overline{\spann\{f_{i}:i\in I\}}^{H}\,.

(29)

We define
H^{*}:=\overline{\spann\{f_{i}:i\in I\}}^{H}
and equip it with the norm of
H
.
Then
H^{*}
is a separable RKHS and
the second identity of (
24
) in
Lemma
6.3
shows

\displaystyle H^{*}=\overline{\spann\{f_{i}:i\in I\}}^{H}=\overline{\ran J}^{H}=\overline{H_{X}}^{H}\,,

where the third identity is true since
J:H_{X}\to H
is the embedding map. In particular,
we have
H_{X}\subset H^{*}
, and for every
h\in H^{*}
there exists a sequence

(f_{n})\subset H_{X}
with
\|f_{n}-h\|_{H}\to 0
. Since we know
f_{n}\in H^{*}
we conclude that

\|f_{n}-h\|_{H^{*}}\to 0
. In other words, we have shown
H^{*}\subset\overline{H_{X}}^{H^{*}}
.

Now, since
H
is separable, so is its subspace
\ker D_{H_{X},H}
. Consequently, there
exists a countable dense subset
\mathfrak{D}\subset\ker D_{H_{X},H}
.
For
f\in\mathfrak{D}
the last assertion of Lemma
6.4
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{P}}|\langle Y_{\bullet},f\rangle_{H}|^{2}=\langle D_{H_{X},H}f,f\rangle_{H}=0\,,

and hence there exists an
N_{f}\in{\cal A}
with
\mathrm{P}(N_{f})=0
and
\langle Y_{\bullet}(\omega),f\rangle_{H}=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega\setminus N_{f}
. Let us define

\displaystyle N:=\bigcup_{f\in\mathfrak{D}}N_{f}\,.

Clearly, we have
N\in{\cal A}
with
\mathrm{P}(N)=0
and
\langle Y_{\bullet}(\omega),f\rangle_{H}=0
for all
f\in\mathfrak{D}
and
\omega\in\Omega\setminus N
.

With these preparations we can now show
Y_{\bullet}(\omega)\in H^{*}
for all
\omega\in\Omega\setminus N
. To this end, we pick an
f\in\ker D_{H_{X},H}
. Then there exists a sequence

(f_{n})\subset\mathfrak{D}
with
f_{n}\to f
in
H
. For

\omega\in\Omega\setminus N
our construction then shows

\displaystyle\langle Y_{\bullet}(\omega),f\rangle_{H}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle Y_{\bullet}(\omega),f_{n}\rangle_{H}=0\,.

In other words, we have found
Y_{\bullet}(\omega)\in(\ker D_{H_{X},H})^{\perp}=H^{*}
,
where the last identity follows from (
29
) and the definition of
H^{*}
.
∎

6.4
Proof of Theorem
3.2
and its Corollaries

Before we can prove
Theorem
3.2
and its corollaries we need to establish a few auxiliary results.
We begin with an elementary lemma describing the effect of multiplying a kernel by some function.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
k
be a kernel on
T
with RKHS
H
and
\alpha:T\to\mathbb{R}
be a function. Then
the RKHS of the kernel

\alpha\star k:T\times T\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle(\alpha\star k)(t,t^{\prime}):=\alpha(t)\alpha(t^{\prime})k(t,t^{\prime})\,,\qquad\qquad t,t^{\prime}\in X,

is
H_{\alpha\star k}:=\{\alpha h:h\in H\}
with norm given by
\|f\|_{H_{\alpha\star k}}=\inf\bigl\{\|h\|_{H}:h\in H\mbox{ with }f=\alpha h\bigr\}
.

Proof of Lemma
6.6
:

Let
\Phi:T\to H
be the canonical feature map of
k
, that is
\Phi(t)=k(\,\cdot\,,t)
for all
t\in T
.
For
t_{1},t_{2}\in T
an elementary calculation then shows

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\alpha(t_{1})\Phi(t_{1}),\alpha(t_{2})\Phi(t_{2})\bigr\rangle_{H}=\alpha(t_{1})\alpha(t_{2})\langle\Phi(t_{1}),\Phi(t_{2})\rangle_{H}=\alpha(t_{1})\alpha(t_{2})k(t_{1},t_{2})\,.

Consequently,
\alpha\Phi:T\to H
is a feature map of
\alpha\star k
. Moreover, for
h\in H
and
t\in T

another simple calculation shows

\displaystyle\bigl\langle h,(\alpha\Phi)(t)\bigr\rangle_{H}=\alpha(t)\,\langle h,\Phi(t)\rangle_{H}=\alpha(t)h(t)\,.

Now the assertion follows from the fundamental theorem of RKHSs, see e.g.
[
46
, Theorem 4.21]
.
∎

The next auxiliary results describes the relationship between envelope functions and the behavior of the kernel on its diagonal.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
H
be an RKHS on
T
with kernel
k
. Then
b:T\to[0,\infty)
is an envelope for
H
, if and only if

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(t,t)}\leq b(t)\,,\qquad\qquad t\in T.

(30)

Proof of Lemma
6.7
:

If (
30
) is true, then the assertion immediately follows from (
4
).

To establish the converse implication, we define
\alpha:T\to[0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle\alpha(t):=\begin{cases}1/b(t),&\mbox{ if }b(t)>0\,,\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let us now fix an
f\in H_{\alpha\star k}
, where
H_{\alpha\star k}
is the RKHS considered in
Lemma
6.6
.
According to Lemma
6.6
there then exists an
h\in H
with
f=\alpha h
.

Our next, intermediate goal is to show that there is only one such

Lemma 6.8

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|e_{i}\|_{B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)}^{2}<\infty

as discussed around (
18
).
Now recall that the collection of elementary tensors

e_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{d}}
forms an ONB of
B^{s}_{2,2}(0,1)\otimes_{2}\dots\otimes_{2}B^{s}_{2,2}(0,1)=H_{X}
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{\infty}\|e_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{d}}\|_{B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)\otimes_{2}\dots\otimes_{2}B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{\infty}\dots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\|e_{i_{j}}\|_{B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|e_{i}\|_{B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)}^{2}\biggr)^{d}

\displaystyle<\infty\,,

which shows that the embedding
B^{s}_{2,2}(0,1)\otimes_{2}\dots\otimes_{2}B^{s}_{2,2}(0,1)\hookrightarrow B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)\otimes_{2}\dots\otimes_{2}B^{r}_{2,2}(0,1)
is Hilbert-Schmidt. Now
ii)
follows by Theorem
1.2
.
∎
