Non-Hermitian topological invariant of photonic band structures undergoing inversion
Paul Bouteyre 1, Dung Xuan Nguyen 2,3, Guillaume Gachon 1, Taha Benyattou 1, Xavier Letartre 1, Pierre Viktorovitch 1, Ségolène Callard 1, Lydie Ferrier 1, Hai Son Nguyen 1,4

Abstract
The interplay between symmetry and topology led to the discovery of symmetry-protected topological phases in Hermitian systems, including topological insulators and topological superconductors. However, the intrinsic symmetry-protected topological characteristics of non-Hermitian systems still await exploration. Here, we investigate experimentally the topological transition associated with the inversion of non-Hermitian band structures in an optical lattice. Intriguingly, we demonstrate that the winding number associated with the symmetry-protected bound state in the continuum is not a conserved quantity after band inversion. To define a topological invariant, we propose the skyrmion number given by spawning in momentum space a pseudo-spin with the polarisation vortex as the in-plane component and the band-index as the pseudo-spin direction at the origin. This leads to a topological transition from an antimeron to an meron-like texture through band inversion, while always conserving the half-charge skyrmion number. We foresee the use of skyrmion number to explore exotic singularities in various non-Hermitian physical system.



In 1929, Von Neumann and Wigner suggested, as an anecdote, the existence of peculiar solutions of Schrödinger equation which are perfect bound states although lying in the continuum spectrum of extended states
Von Neumann and Wigner 1929
. Six decades later, the origin of Bound States in the Continuum (BICs) was elegantly unraveled in 1985 by Friedrich and Wintgen, who explained the suppression of the leaking to the continuum as the result of destructive interference between two resonances when coupled to the same radiation channel
Friedrich and Wintgen 1985a
. Nowadays, BICs have been evidenced in many physical platform, such as atomic and molecular system
Friedrich and Wintgen 1985b
;
Cederbaum et al. 2003
;
Thomas et al. 2018
, water waves
Cobelli et al. 2011
, solid-state and mesoscopic physics
Capasso et al. 1992
;
Nöckel 1992
;
Ladrón de Guevara and Orellana 2006
, and photonic structures
Hsu et al. 2013
;
Kodigala et al. 2017
. In particular, engineering BICs with custom-cut design in nanophotonics has led to tremendous success in various applications such as lasing
Kodigala et al. 2017
;
Ha et al. 2018
, ultra-fast optical switch
Huang et al. 2020
and sensing
Romano et al. 2020
;
Tittl et al. 2018
.

The seminal work of Zhen
et al.
Zhen et al. 2014
has shown that each BIC in photonic lattices corresponds to a polarization singularity associated with a polarization vortex in momentum space. These vectorial vortices have now been experimentally demonstrated by different groups
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Doeleman et al. 2018
. Ultimately, the winding number of such a polarization vortex is suggested to be a conserved topological charge of non-Hermitian photonics
Zhen et al. 2014
;
Yin and Peng 2020
. This interpretation is now widely accepted since the winding number has been theoretically and experimentally demonstrated as a conserved quantity in different scenarios such as BIC merging, splitting, annihilation, and creation
Zhen et al. 2014
;
Jin et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Yoda and Notomi 2020
;
Kang et al. 2021
;
Kang et al. 2022
. Most of these works have focused on mechanisms either involving accidental BICs
Zhen et al. 2014
;
Jin et al. 2019
;
Kang et al. 2021
, or breaking lattice symmetry
Liu et al. 2019
;
Yoda and Notomi 2020
.

However, symmetries play a crucial role in classifying topological phases of matters
Schnyder et al. 2008
;
Hasan and Kane 2010
as well as in interpreting topological photonics systems
Ozawa et al. 2019
. On the other hand, the topological order can be altered with the band inversion mechanism while preserving the symmetries
Bernevig et al. 2006
;
Zhu et al. 2012
. Therefore investigating the topological nature of photonic symmetry-protected BICs under band inversion mechanism is fundamentally important.

In this work, we experimentally investigate the topological nature of symmetry-protected BICs undergoing band inversion transformation in leaky photonic lattice. Using a home-built band dispersion tomography setup, we map the polarization texture in momentum space together with the dispersion surface of each photonic band. Our results show that the polarization vortex associated with the symmetry-protected BIC changes sign after the band inversion. The results suggest that the winding number is not a good topological number as soon as the band inversion mechanism is involved. We then propose the definition of skyrmion number
Skyrme 1962
;
Heinze et al. 2011
for non-Hermitian topological charges, which combines the winding number of symmetry-protected BICs and their band index. The latter can be interpreted as the direction of the pseudo-spin at the origin, leading to a meron-like texture in the momentum space.

The photonic lattice that we examine, shown in Figs.

In this work, we will focus on Transverse Electric (TE) modes, keeping in mind that similar effects would be obtained for Transverse Magnetic (TM) modes (the only condition is that the splitting TE-TM is strong enough so that there is single polarization mode at the operating frequency). At the
\Gamma

On the other hand, the symmetric mode can radiate to the free space. Due to the presence of nonradiative losses via absorption in metal, both modes have a finite quality factor. Since some papers only use the term BIC when the quality factor is infinite (and quasi-BIC or ultrahigh-Q otherwise), to avoid any misleading, for the rest of the paper, the symmetry-protected BIC will be referred to as the
dark mode

We now focus on the polarization vortex pinned at the symmetry-protected BIC undergoing band inversion. Such a vortex is characterized by the accumulation of the orientation angle
\phi(\bm{q})
, the orientation of the farfield polarization corresponding to the dark branch, when encircling the symmetry-protected BIC. The pattern
\phi(\bm{q})
can be analytically calculated from the eigenvectors of the effective Hamiltonian (
1
) and one may show that the vortex winding number, defined by
Zhen et al. 2014

W=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}d\bm{q}\nabla_{\bm{q}}\phi
, is simply (see Supplemental Materials for full derivation):

W=sgn(U).

(2)

The result suggests that the vortex winding number changes sign after the band inversion and thus is not a conservative quantity. It is quite surprising because most of the reported works in the literature suggest the conservation of BIC winding number, which is a topological invariant. To verify experimentally and numerically this intriguing feature, we perform angle-resolved reflectivity contrast measurements and FEM simulations in different polarizations to obtain the tomography reconstruction of the farfield polarization pattern for both photonic branches. The results, presented in Fig.
2

U
changes sign, confirming that the winding number is not conserved through the band inversion mechanism and therefore is not a preserved topological number
(see Fig.
2

Since the symmetry-protected BIC switches band and the sign of its winding number under band inversion, we propose a definition of a non-Hermitian topological charge that combines the winding number of symmetry-protected BICs and their band index
p=sgn(U)
defined through the dimensionless diffracting coupling strength. We first consider a three-dimensional vector
\bm{M}
which is composed of the normalized electric field
\bm{\epsilon}=(E_{x},E_{y})/E_{0}
and the dimensionless diffracting coupling strength
U
(see fig.
3
(a)). Here,
E_{x}(q_{x},q_{y})
and
E_{y}(q_{x},q_{y})
are electric field amplitudes in the farfield of the photonic lattice, and
E_{0}
is the amplitude of the confined electric field of TE-guided mode in non-corrugated structure (More information in the theoretical section of the Supplemental Materials). This vector represents both the polarization vortices in its in-plane component, and band-flip in its out-of-plane component. Intuitively, the dynamic of our non-Hermitian band structures is captured by the precession in momentum space of the pseudo-spin that is defined by
\bm{m}=\bm{M}/||{\bm{M}||}
. Indeed, the lower panels in Figs.
3
(b) and (c) present the pseudo-spin
\bm{m}
along the
q_{x}
and
q_{y}
axes before (b) and after (c) the band inversion. At
\Gamma

(q_{x}=q_{y}=0)

\bm{\epsilon}=(0,0)
. As a consequence, the
pseudo-spin only has a vertical component
m_{z}
and is oriented in the direction determined by the band index:
\bm{m}=(0,0,p)
. Away from the
\Gamma

q=\sqrt{q_{x}^{2}+q_{y}^{2}}\gg U
, the norm of the polarization vector
\bm{\epsilon}
is non-null and becomes much greater than
U
. Consequently, the pseudo-spin
\bm{m}
is oriented in the plane, and its vertical component
m_{z}
is close to zero, resulting in a half flip of the pseudo-spin
\bm{m}
along the
q_{x}
and
q_{y}
axes. The upper panels of Figs.
3
(b) and (c) show the pseudo-spin
\bm{m}
in momentum space before (b) and after (c) the band inversion. As described previously, the pseudo-spin
\bm{m}
is half flipped in the vertical direction along the radial axes, while its radial components depend on the polarization vector winding around the BIC at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
as shown in Fig.
2
. The combination of the half-flip of the vertical component
m_{z}
and the winding of the polarization vector
\bm{\epsilon}
results in a precession of the pseudo-spin
\bm{m}
around the BIC, which can be described as a skyrmion in the momentum space: the precession of a flipping magnetic spin, and we use the definition of the skyrmion number
Heinze et al. 2011

n=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int dq_{x}dq_{y}\bm{m}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial q_{x}}\times\frac{\partial\bm{m}}{\partial q_{y}}\right).

(3)

The skyrmion number represents the number of times the pseudo-spin winds around the unit sphere; here is
n=pW/2=1/2
(from the half-flip of the pseudo-spin) corresponding to a meron. We note that the usual definition of pseudo-magnetic moment for photonic skyrmion number is only the polarization vector with the pseudo-spin given by the circular polarization degree
Guo et al. 2020
;
Król et al. 2021
. Here the polarization vector of our photonic mode is purely linear (see Fig.
2
) and only contributes to the in-plane component of
\bm{m}
, and the pseudo-spin is purely related to the band order. Interestingly, as shown in Figs.
3
(b) and (c), the pseudo-spin texture switchs from anti-meron to meron
Yu et al. 2018
through the band inversion but its skyrmion number of
1/2
is conserved and can be considered as the definition of non-Hermitian topological charges of symmetry-protected BICs. The skyrmion number defined in this paper reminds us of the definition of skyrmion number in the two-band model of Chern insulator
Bernevig et al. 2006
, in which the skyrmion number determines the Hall conductance. However, the skyrmion number (and thus the Hall conductance) defined in the model of Ref
Bernevig et al. 2006
can change after a band inversion. Therefore, the definition of our skyrmion number is not directly related to the skyrmion number in Chern insulators.

In conclusion
, we have shown experimentally that the winding number of the polarization around the
symmetry-protected bound state in the continuum
(BIC) of leaky photonic modes in a 1D optical lattice is not a conserved quantity as it changes sign during a band inversion. The experimental results were perfectly matched with numerical simulations and are nicely explained with a model of effective Hamiltonian. Our observations showed that the winding number of polarization vortex is not a topological constant in the general case. Consequently, we suggested combining the winding number and the band index to redefine the topological number as a skyrmion number in the momentum space. In our proposal, the band index can be interpreted as the direction of the pseudo-spin at the origin, leading to a meron-like texture in the momentum space. This picture associates the band inversion with a transformation between anti-meron and meron topological spin texture while always conserving the half-charge skyrmion number. Here our skyrmion number plays a similar role as the total Chern number in topological insulators, where the total Chern number is preserved before and after band inversion. In future work, we will apply our formalism to photonic band structures with multiple BICs. We expect the definition of skyrmion number in this paper can be applied to analyze the singularities of other non-Hermitian topological systems
Zhu et al. 2021
.

Construction and Analysis of the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

I.2.1
Coupling mechanisms: diffractive coupling and radiative coupling

Now the implementation of a periodic corrugation will induce a coupling
U
between
\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle
and
\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle
. Each of them also couples to the radiative continuum with the coupling strength
\gamma

\gamma_{nr}

\Psi=\left(\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle,\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle\right)

H(\bm{q})=\left(\begin{matrix}\hat{\omega}_{+1}&U\\
U&\hat{\omega}_{-1}\end{matrix}\right)+i\left(\begin{matrix}\gamma+\gamma_{nr}&\gamma\cos\alpha\\
\gamma\cos\alpha&\gamma+\gamma_{nr}\end{matrix}\right)

(S9)

The first term of
H
is Hermitian, describing the free oscillation of each mode and the coupling between them via diffractive coupling. The second term of this Hamiltonian is anti-Hermitian. Its diagonal components describe the total losses including both radiative an non-radiative ones; while its anti-diagonal components describes the coupling between
\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle
and
\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle
via the radiative continuum. The coefficient
\cos\alpha=\bm{u_{-1}}.\bm{u_{+1}}
is due to the fact that only the same electric field component can interfere. We note that in the limit of
q_{x},q_{y}\ll 1
, this coefficient can be effectively replaced by
q_{y}^{2}-1
.

,
\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
, the Hamiltonian (
S9
) can be rewritten as:

H=d_{0}\mathbb{I}_{2}+d_{1}\sigma_{1}+d_{3}\sigma_{3}

(S10)

where

\displaystyle d_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{y}^{2}}{2n_{g}}+i\left(\gamma+\gamma_{nr}\right),

(S11)

\displaystyle d_{1}

\displaystyle=U+i\gamma\cos\alpha,

(S12)

\displaystyle d_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{x}}{n_{g}}.

(S13)

.

The eigenvalues
\Omega_{\pm}
and corresponding eigenstates
\lvert\pm\rangle
of (
S10
) is given by:

\Omega_{\pm}=d_{0}\pm\sqrt{d_{1}^{2}+d_{3}^{2}},

(S14)

and

\lvert\pm\rangle=\left(\begin{matrix}1\\
C_{\pm}\end{matrix}\right)

(S15)

with

C_{\pm}=-\frac{d_{3}}{d_{1}}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{d_{3}}{d_{1}}\right)^{2}+1}.

(S16)

As eigenmodes of a non-Hermitian system,
\lvert\pm\rangle
are quasinormal modes
Lalanne et al. 2018
with many mathematical difficulties, for example the way of normalization. Another unusual behaviour of these quasinormal modes is that in general
\lvert+\rangle
and
\lvert-\rangle
are not orthogonal between each other. There are two peculiar configurations:

•

d_{3}=\pm id_{1}
: Simultaneous coalescence of eigenvalues and eigenstates:
\Omega_{+}=\Omega_{-}
and
\lvert+\rangle=\lvert-\rangle
. This configuration corresponds to
Exceptional points
Kawabata et al. 2019a
;
Kawabata et al. 2019b
;
Bergholtz et al. 2021
.

•

d_{3}=0
: The eigenmodes become normal with
C_{\pm}=\pm 1
. In this configuration,
\lvert+\rangle
and
\lvert-\rangle
are two orthogonal states as the ones in Hermitian system.

I.3
Photonic band structures and Farfield polarizations

I.3.1
Complex eigenvalues of Bloch resonances and band inversion mechanism

The explicit form of eigenvalues
\Omega_{\pm}
are given by:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\pm}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{y}^{2}}{2n_{g}}+i\left(\gamma+\gamma_{nr}\right)\pm\sqrt{\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{n_{g}^{2}}+\left(U+i\gamma\cos\alpha\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\omega}_{\pm}+i\gamma_{r\pm}

(S17)

The real part
\hat{\omega}_{\pm}
of
\Omega_{\pm}(\bm{q})
corresponds to the normalized eigen-frequency. The imaginary counter part
\gamma_{\pm}
corresponds to the losses including both radiative and nonradiative losses. Finally, the radiative losses
\gamma_{r\pm}
corresponding to the leaking of these eigenmodes to the radiative continuum) can be extracted by taking out the nonradiative part from the total losses (i.e. subtracting
\gamma_{nr}
from
\gamma_{\pm}
). One may show that:

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}_{\pm}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle\approx\frac{q_{y}^{2}}{2n_{g}}\pm U\left[1+\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{2n_{g}(U^{2}+\gamma^{2})}\right],

(S18a)

\displaystyle\gamma_{r\pm}(\bm{q})

\displaystyle\approx\gamma\left[1\mp 1\pm\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{2n_{g}(U^{2}+\gamma^{2})}\pm q_{y}^{2}\right]

(S18b)

for
q_{x},q_{y}\ll 1
.

Eq. (
S18a
) describes band dispersion of the eigenmodes in the vicinity of the two band-edges. It shows that the gap separating the two bands amounts to 2
U
. Moreover, the curvature of the dispersion of
\lvert+\rangle
has the same sign as the one of
U
. As consequence, for each value of
q_{y}
, we can define a
band inversion
corresponding to a “swapping” between two band dispersion along
q_{x}
when
U\rightarrow-U
. If
U>0
, the upper branch corresponds to
\lvert+\rangle
and if
U<0
, the upper branch corresponds to
\lvert-\rangle
.

Now we will show that the band inversion concept is applied not only for the band dispersion but also for whole the complex eigenvalues with complex gap. Indeed, from (
I.3.1
), for a given
q_{x}
and for
\forall q_{y}\ll 1
, the two complex eigenvalues are separated by:

\kappa(q_{x})\approx 2\sqrt{\frac{q_{x}^{2}}{n_{g}^{2}}+\left(U-i\gamma\right)^{2}}

(S19)

The gap
\Delta
is defined by the minimum of
|\kappa(q_{x})|
. One may show that this gap corresponds to the value
q_{x}=\pm n_{g}\sqrt{max(\gamma^{2}-U^{2},0)}
, and is given by:

\begin{cases}|U|>\gamma:\;\;\Delta=2(U-i\gamma)\\
|U|\leq\gamma:\;\;\Delta=2\left[sgn(U)-i\right]\sqrt{|U|\gamma}.\end{cases}

(S20)

As consequence, the system is always gapped except when
U=0
. When sweeping
U
from negative to positive, the complex gap is closed at
U=0
and then reopened again as soon as
U>0
. We note that
U=0
corresponds to the Exceptional Points configuration mentioned in the previous section.

Finally, from (
S18b
), we note that
\gamma_{r+}=0
at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
, and increasing as
q_{x}^{2}
and
q_{y}^{2}
. As consequence
\lvert+\rangle
is a BIC at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
. Since this state can still exhibit losses via nonradiative channel, we prefer to call it
dark state
to underline the nonradiating nature of
\lvert+\rangle
. Logically, the state
\lvert-\rangle
will be refereed to as
bright state
since the radiative loss is maximized at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
. As a consequence, the band inversion mechanism swaps the bright and the dark states.

I.3.2
Eigenmodes of the effective Hamiltonian

The explicit form of eigenstates
\lvert\pm\rangle
are given by:

\lvert\pm\rangle=\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle+C_{\pm}\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle

(S21)

with :

C_{\pm}=-\frac{q_{x}}{n_{g}(U-i\gamma\cos\alpha)}\pm\sqrt{1+\left[\frac{q_{x}}{n_{g}(U-i\gamma\cos\alpha)}\right]^{2}}.

(S22)

At
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
, Eq. (
S22
) gives us
C_{\pm}\rvert_{q_{x}=q_{y}=0}=\pm 1
, and the eigenmodes is simplifed as:

\lvert\pm_{q_{x}=q_{y}=0}\rangle=\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle\pm\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle

(S23)

The
\pi
phase between
\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle_{q_{x}=q_{y}=0}
and
\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle_{q_{x}=q_{y}=0}
implies that
\lvert+_{q_{x}=q_{y}=0}\rangle
is an antisymmetric state while
\lvert-_{q_{x}=q_{y}=0}\rangle
is a symmetric one. As a consequence,
\lvert+\rangle
cannot couple to the radiative continuum at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
due to symmetry mismatch and is a symmetry protected BIC. This result is quite inline with the one provided by the eigenvalue calculations.

I.3.3
Nearfield pattern

The nearfield of the guided modes
\lvert\bm{\beta_{\pm 1}}\rangle
is described by plane waves propagating in-plane (x,y) with vertical confinement profile
f(z)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\mathcal{E}_{\pm 1}}&=\mathcal{E}_{0}f(z)\exp{\left(i\bm{\beta_{\pm 1}}.\bm{r}_{||}\right)}\bm{u_{\pm 1}}\\
&=\mathcal{E}_{0}f(z)\exp{\left(i\frac{2\pi\bm{q}.\bm{r}_{||}}{a}\right)}\exp{\left(\pm i\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)}\bm{u_{\pm 1}}\end{split}

(S24)

where the propagation wavevector
\bm{\beta_{\pm 1}}
is from (
S2
), polarization vector
\bm{u_{\pm 1}}
is from (
S7
),
\bm{r}_{||}=(x,y)
is the in-plane coordinates,
\bm{q}=(q_{x},q_{y})
is the in-plane wavevector and the constant
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is a the field amplitude.

Since the eigenmodes
\lvert\pm\rangle
are given by superpositions of
\lvert\bm{\beta_{+1}}\rangle
and
\lvert\bm{\beta_{-1}}\rangle
, the nearfield patterns of
\lvert\pm\rangle
are also dictated by the same superposition rule:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\mathcal{E}_{\pm}}&=\bm{\mathcal{E}_{+1}}+C_{\pm}\bm{\mathcal{E}_{-1}}\\
&=\mathcal{E}_{0}f(z)\exp{\left(i\frac{2\pi\bm{q}.\bm{r}_{||}}{a}\right)}\left[\exp{\left(i\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)}\bm{u_{+1}}+C_{\pm}\exp{\left(-i\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)}\bm{u_{-1}}\right].\end{split}

(S25)

At
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
, Eqs. (
S7
),(
S8
) and(
S22
) imply that
\bm{u_{\pm 1}}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})=\pm\bm{u_{y}}
and
C_{\pm}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})=\pm 1
. As a consequence, the nearfield is given by::

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{\mathcal{E}_{+}}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})&=2i\mathcal{E}_{0}f(z)\sin{\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)}\bm{u_{y}}\;\;\rightarrow\textit{ odd parity with respect to the lateral mirror symmetry}\\
\bm{\mathcal{E}_{-}}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})&=2\mathcal{E}_{0}f(z)\cos{\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)}\bm{u_{y}}\;\;\rightarrow\textit{ even parity with respect to the lateral mirror symmetry}\end{cases}

(S26)

As a consequence,
\lvert+\rangle(\bm{q}=\bm{0})
cannot couple to the radiative continuum due to symmetry mismatch with plane waves of even parity in this continuum. Otherwise, the coupling between
\lvert-\rangle(\bm{q}=\bm{0})
and the radiative continuum is permitted. Therefore
\lvert+\rangle(\bm{q}=\bm{0})
corresponds to symmetry-protected BIC. We note that the nearfield distribution given by (
S26
) is in perfect aggrement with the numerical simulation results in Fig.  1(c) of the main text.

I.3.4
Polarization pattern of the farfield in momentum space

The Bragg scattering mechanism due to periodic corrugation fold the guided modes
\lvert\bm{\beta_{1}}\rangle
from
\bm{\beta_{\pm 1}}=\bm{k}\pm\frac{2\pi}{a}\bm{u_{x}}
to the same wavevector
\bm{k}
. As a consequence, these guided modes once folded radiate to the freespace as plane wave with
\bm{k}
as the in-plane component. In the vicinity of the normal emission, these radiating waves are approximately described as plane waves of the same wavevector
\frac{\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}}{c}\bm{u_{z}}
while preserving the polarization
\bm{u_{\pm 1}}
of the unfolded modes:

\bm{E_{\pm 1}}(\bm{q})=E_{0}\exp\left(i\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}z/c\right)\bm{u_{\pm 1}}(\bm{q})

(S27)

where the amplitude
E_{0}
of radiative waves is proportional to the amplitude
mathcal{E_{0}}
of the unfolded modes.

The farfield patterns of the hybrid modes
\lvert\pm\rangle
are also dictated by the same superposition rule of the egeinmodes and nearfield pattern:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{E_{\pm}}(\bm{q})&=\bm{E_{+1}}(\bm{q})+C_{\pm}\bm{E_{-1}}(\bm{q})\\
&=E_{0}\exp\left(i\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}z/c\right)\left(\bm{u_{+1}}+C_{\pm}\bm{u_{-1}}\right).\end{split}

(S28)

We remind that at
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
,
\bm{u_{\pm 1}}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})=\pm\bm{u_{y}}
and
C_{\pm}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})=\pm 1
. Therefore, the farfield at
\Gamma
point is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\bm{E_{+}}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})&=0\;\;\rightarrow\textit{ destructive interference}\\
\bm{E_{-}}(\bm{q}=\bm{0})&=2E_{0}\exp\left(i\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}z/c\right)\bm{u_{y}}\,\rightarrow\textit{ constructive interference}\end{cases}

This result shows that the farfield of
\lvert+\rangle
has a singularity at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
, which is in good agreement with its
dark
nature demonstrated previously.

For an arbitrary coordinate
(q_{x},q_{y})
, the electric field
\bm{E_{\pm}}
is reconstructed in Cartesian basis as:

\bm{E_{\pm}}=E_{x,\pm}\bm{u_{x}}+E_{y,\pm}\bm{u_{y}}

(S29)

where the complex components
E_{x,\pm}
and
E_{y,\pm}
are given by:

\displaystyle E_{x,\pm}

\displaystyle=E_{0}\exp\left(i\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}z/c\right)\left(\cos\theta_{+1}+C_{\pm}\cos\theta_{-1}\right),

(S30a)

\displaystyle E_{y,\pm}

\displaystyle=E_{0}\exp\left(i\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}z/c\right)\left(\sin\theta_{+1}+C_{\pm}\sin\theta_{-1}\right).

(S30b)

where
\sin\theta_{\pm 1}
,
\cos\theta_{\pm 1}
and
C_{\pm}
are from Eqs. (
S8
) and(
S22
). The orientation (i.e. angle
\phi_{\pm}
) of
\bm{E_{\pm}}(\bm{q})
with respect to
\bm{u_{x}}
(see Fig.
S2
a) is given by:
Dennis 2002

\tan 2\phi_{\pm}=\frac{2\text{Re}(E_{x,\pm}^{*}.E_{y,\pm})}{|E_{x,\pm}|^{2}-|E_{y,\pm}|^{2}}

(S31)

The ellipticity of the polarization, described by angle
\chi_{\pm}
(see Fig.
S2
a), is given by:
Dennis 2002

\sin 2\chi_{\pm}=\frac{2\text{Im}(E_{x,\pm}^{*}.E_{y,\pm})}{|E_{x,\pm}|^{2}+|E_{y,\pm}|^{2}}

(S32)

I.3.5
Winding number of the polarization vortex

From the orientation angle
\phi_{\pm}(\bm{q})
, the winding number around
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
of the vector field
\bm{E_{\pm}}(\bm{q})
is defined as:

W_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}d\bm{q}\nabla_{\bm{q}}\phi_{\pm}.

(S33)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a closed circulation encircling
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
. One may uses directly Eq. (
S31
) and calculating
W_{\pm}
for a arbitrary path
\mathcal{C}
. However, knowing that the only possible singularity is located at
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
,
W_{\pm}
does not depend on the choice of
\mathcal{C}
, we will choose an adequate
\mathcal{C}
so that the integral can be easily calculated. To do so, we define the complex number
z=\rho e^{i\varphi}
with:

\displaystyle\text{Re}(z)

\displaystyle=\rho\cos\varphi=\frac{q_{x}}{2n_{g}\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}},

(S34a)

\displaystyle\text{Im}(z)

\displaystyle=\rho\sin\varphi=q_{y}.

(S34b)

The winding number will be calculated by encircling in the counter-clockwise direction around
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
when fixing
\rho\ll 1
constant and varying
\varphi
. The condition
\rho\ll 1
implies that
\lvert q_{x}\rvert,\lvert q_{y}\rvert\ll 1
. Thus from (
S8
),(
S22
), we can approximate:

\begin{cases}\sin\theta_{\pm 1}\approx\pm 1\\
\cos\theta_{\pm 1}\approx-\rho\sin\varphi\\
C_{\pm}\approx\pm 1-2\rho\cos\varphi\frac{U+i\gamma}{\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}}\end{cases}

(S35)

Injecting to (
S30a
),(
S30b
), that leads to:

– For the
\lvert-\rangle
state :

\displaystyle\frac{E_{x,-}}{E_{0}}

\displaystyle\approx 2\rho^{2}\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\frac{U+i\gamma}{\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}}\approx 0

\displaystyle\frac{E_{y,-}}{E_{0}}

\displaystyle\approx 2+2\rho\cos\varphi\frac{U+i\gamma}{\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}}\approx 2

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{always vertical polarization,i.e. }\phi_{-}=\pi/2

\displaystyle\rightarrow\boxed{W_{-}=0}.

– For the
\lvert+\rangle
state :

\displaystyle\frac{E_{x,+}}{E_{0}}

\displaystyle\approx-2\rho\sin\varphi

\displaystyle\frac{E_{y,+}}{E_{0}}

\displaystyle\approx 2\rho\cos\varphi\frac{U+i\gamma}{\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}}

using (
S31
), we obtain then:

\displaystyle\tan 2\phi_{+}

\displaystyle=\tan 2\varphi\frac{U}{\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}}

\displaystyle=sgn(U)\tan{2\psi}

where angle
\psi
is obtained from
\varphi
by shrinking an ellipse of semi-major axe
\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}
and semi-mino axe
|U|
into a circle of radius
\sqrt{U^{2}+\gamma^{2}}
(see Fig.
S2
b).
Hence

\phi_{+}=sgn(U)\psi

(S36)

Moreover, we note that the pathway
\mathcal{C}
of counter-clockwise direction around
q_{x}=q_{y}=0
corresponds to
\varphi
varies from 0 to 2
\pi
, due to the definition (
S34a
) and (
S34b
). Moreover, as indicated in Fig.
S2
b,
\psi
will also sweep from 0 to 2
\pi
. Thus

\displaystyle\begin{split}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}d\varphi=2\pi.sgn(U)\rightarrow\boxed{W_{+}=sgn(U)}.\end{split}

(S37)

As a matter of fact, the winding number of the polarization vortex associated with
\lvert+\rangle
is given by the sign of
U
, thus changes sign after a band-inversion (i.e.
U
changes sign).
