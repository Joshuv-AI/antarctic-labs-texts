A domain decomposition solution of the Stokes-Darcy system in 3D based on boundary integrals
Svetlana Tlupova

Abstract
A framework is developed for a robust and highly accurate numerical solution of the coupled Stokes-Darcy system in three dimensions. The domain decomposition method is based on a Dirichlet-Neumann type splitting of the interface conditions and solving separate Stokes and Darcy problems iteratively. Second kind boundary integral equations are formulated for each problem. The integral equations use a smoothing of the kernels that achieves high accuracy on the boundary, and a straightforward quadrature to discretize the integrals. Numerical results demonstrate the convergence, accuracy, and dependence on parameter values of the iterative solution for a problem of viscous flow around a porous sphere with a known analytical solution, as well as more general surfaces.



Keywords:
Stokes-Darcy, domain decomposition methods, boundary integral equations, porous sphere.

1
Introduction

Many important environmental and biological phenomena that occur in our daily lives involve fluids partly flowing freely and partly filtrating through a porous medium. Such processes are of interest in physiology when studying cancer growth and filtration of blood through arterial vessel walls. The free/porous flow plays an important role in numerous industrial applications of air and oil filter design, oil exploration, or chemical reactor simulations. There is also considerable environmental interest in studies of groundwater remediation, geologic CO
2
sequestration, and bacterial biofilms.

Due to its wide applicability, the modeling of free/porous flow has recently received considerable attention, and mathematical and numerical analyses have been done. The flow is often modeled by the incompressible Stokes equations in the free domain and the Darcy equations in the porous domain, coupled across the interface through suitable conditions. The numerical solutions of these flows have been almost exclusively based on the finite element method

[

Despite the significant efforts in recent years to design numerical methods of coupled free/porous flows, these problems remain a challenge today, owing to the multiphysics nature of the system and the large differences in the physical parameters in the governing equations. The severe limitations of the direct solution necessitate the development of techniques that would enable one to split the system and solve the smaller Stokes and Darcy problems separately while exchanging information through the boundary conditions only. These techniques, called domain decomposition methods (DDM), have been used to solve various problems
[
47
]
. DDMs are based on dividing the computational domain into several subdomains with or without overlap, and formulating the so-called transmission conditions along the subdomain interfaces. The coupling across the interface is then replaced by an iterative procedure, where the smaller problems are solved independently at each iteration. For the coupled flow problem, similar techniques have been developed and investigated in the context of the finite element method, e.g.

In this paper we develop a simple sequential iterative algorithm inspired by the Dirichlet-Neumann methods to decouple the free and porous problems, and formulate highly accurate second-kind boundary integral equations to solve for the fluid quantities. Boundary integral equation methods (BIEM) are extremely powerful in solving many differential equations

,

Attempting a direct solution of the coupled Stokes-Darcy system with BIEM will lead to a formulation that is inevitably of mixed-kind, due to the incompatibility of the Stokes and Darcy differential operators. Once the system is split through the DDM procedure however, suitable formulations can be applied to each problem. The significant advantages of BIEM are the reduced dimensionality as the boundary alone needs to be discretized, high achievable accuracy at points on and off the boundary, the availability of well-conditioned formulations where the efficiency of the solution is maintained with grid refinement, and efficient solutions for problems where the free domain is large or infinite, with boundaries that have complicated geometry, as well as moving boundaries.

To address the singularities that develop in the integrands when solving the integral equations, we use a regularization technique developed in

As the weights for the quadrature points are found from a partition of unity on the unit sphere, they do not depend on the particular surface. With this approach, the overall formulation is simple to implement, and the data structure needed to describe the boundary is minimal.

2
Problem formulation

To describe the model equations, we denote the free flow quantities and the porous domain quantities by subscripts
S
and
D
, respectively. Figure
1
shows an example two-dimensional setting, where a free fluid flow in a bounded region
\Omega_{S}
and a flow in a porous region
\Omega_{D}
are coupled across the common interface
\Sigma
.

Figure 1:
The domain with the free fluid region
\Omega_{S}
and the porous medium region
\Omega_{D}
, with the common interface
\Sigma
.

In the region of free fluid flow
\Omega_{S}
, we assume the low Reynolds number regime modeled by the incompressible Stokes equations,

\textrm{In}\ \Omega_{S}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla p_{S}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}_{S}=0,\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_{S}=0,\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
\mu
is the fluid viscosity,
p_{S}
and
\mathbf{u}_{S}
are the fluid pressure and velocity, respectively. Darcy’s equations govern the flow in the porous medium,

\textrm{In}\ \Omega_{D}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla p_{D}+\mu K^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{D}=0,\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}_{D}=0.\end{array}\right.

(2)

Here
\mathbf{u}_{D}
and
p_{D}
are respectively the (averaged) fluid velocity and the hydrostatic pressure, and
K
is a symmetric and positive definite permeability tensor that depends on the microstructure of the porous medium. The medium is called homogeneous if the permeability
K(\mathbf{x})
is constant throughout the domain, and isotropic when permeability is a scalar,
K=\kappa I
. In this work, we treat porous media that are homogeneous and isotropic.

The interface between these two systems of partial differential equations serves as a replacement of the boundary layer through which the velocity changes rapidly. A lot of attention has been devoted to the formulation and analysis of appropriate coupling conditions
[
7
,
50
,
31
,
32
,
43
,
44
,
51
]
. The incompressibility condition leads to continuity of normal velocity components,

\mathbf{u}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=-\mathbf{u}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D},

(3)

where
\mathbf{n}_{S}
and
\mathbf{n}_{D}
are the unit normal vectors that point out of the regions
\Omega_{D}
and
\Omega_{S}
respectively, so that
\mathbf{n}_{D}=-\mathbf{n}_{S}
on
\Sigma
(see Fig.
1
). In addition, a suitable condition on the tangential velocity is the slip condition of Beavers-Joseph-Saffman
[
7
,
50
]
,

\bm{\tau}_{S}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\mathbf{u}_{S}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{S},

(4)

where
\bm{\sigma}_{S}=-p_{S}I+\mu[\nabla\mathbf{u}_{S}+(\nabla\mathbf{u}_{S})^{T}]
is the Stokes stress tensor,
\bm{\tau}_{S}
is the tangent vector to the interface, and
\gamma
is a dimensionless quantity that depends on the structure of the porous material. Well-posedness of the mathematical formulation with this condition has been analyzed for steady flows
[
11
,
21
,
24
,
37
]
as well as unsteady flows
[
13
]
. The final condition represents the balance of normal forces across the interface,

\mathbf{n}_{S}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=-p_{D},

(5)

which allows the pressure to be discontinuous across the interface.

A fundamental difficulty in the development of robust computational methods for the free/porous flow system (
1
)-(
5
) is due to the fact that the PDEs that govern the fluid flow in the free region and the flow in the porous medium have different properties. The differential operators in the two subdomains are incompatible, inhibiting the simultaneous solution of the equations by a direct method. In addition, the equations include parameters, the viscosity
\mu
and permeability
\kappa
, that could differ by several orders of magnitude in practice.

3
The Dirichlet-Neumann iterative method

The severe limitations of the direct solution necessitate the development of techniques that would enable one to split the system and solve the smaller Stokes and Darcy problems separately while exchanging information through the boundary conditions only. We develop a numerical scheme based on the sequential Dirichlet-Neumann (D-N) iterative method
[
47
,
21
]
. The outline of the algorithm is as follows. First, we define
q=-\mathbf{u}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}
as the iteration variable on the interface
\Sigma
, and choose an initial guess
q^{(0)}=0
. Then, for
k=1,2,3,...
until convergence, perform the following steps:

1.

Solve the Darcy problem with the interface condition (
3
),

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla p^{(k)}_{D}+\mu\kappa^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{D}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega_{D},\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{D}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega_{D},\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D}=q^{(k-1)}&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma.\end{array}\right.

(6)

The interface condition is a Neumann condition for
p_{D}
.

2.

Using
p^{(k)}_{D}
from Step 1, solve the Stokes problem with the interface conditions (
4
)-(
5
),

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla p^{(k)}_{S}-\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{S}=\mathbf{0}&\textrm{in}\ \Omega_{S},\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{S}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega_{S},\\[2.0pt]
\bm{\tau}_{S}\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{(k)}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\mathbf{u}^{(k-1)}_{S}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{S}&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma,\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{n}_{S}\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{(k)}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=-p^{(k)}_{D}&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma.\end{array}\right.

(7)

3.

Update the iteration variable

q^{(k)}=(1-\theta)\,q^{(k-1)}-\theta\,\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}

(8)

using
\mathbf{u}^{(k)}_{S}
from Step 2, where
\theta\in(0,1)
is a relaxation parameter.

The convergence criterion is taken as the relative error between
q^{(k+1)}
and
q^{(k)}
, which we call the DDM residual, falling below a prescribed tolerance. The solutions of the now separate Darcy (
6
) and Stokes (
7
) problems are based on the boundary integral formulations which we describe in Section
5
.

4
Convergence analysis

We analyze the convergence properties of the Dirichlet-Neumann iterative scheme using the spherical harmonics representation of both problems, assuming as the interface a sphere of radius
R
centered at the origin, with the Darcy problem inside and the Stokes problem outside the sphere.

4.1
Darcy problem inside a sphere

We consider the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{\kappa}\,\nabla p+\mathbf{u}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega,\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega,\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=q&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma,\end{array}\right.

(9)

where
\Omega
is the sphere of radius
R
centered at the origin,
\Sigma
is its boundary,
\mathbf{n}
is the outward unit normal vector, and
\tilde{\kappa}=\kappa/\mu
.

In spherical coordinates
(r,\theta,\phi)
, the general solution to Laplace’s equation
\Delta p=0
in
\Omega
can be written as

p=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n},

(10)

with

p_{n}=\sum_{m=-n}^{n}a_{n}^{m}\,r^{n}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi),

(11)

where
p_{n}
is a solid harmonic of order
n
,
Y_{n}^{m}
is the spherical harmonic function of degree
n
and order
m
, and
a_{n}^{m}\in\mathbb{C}
are constants.

With
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{x}/r
and

\nabla p=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(a_{n}^{m}\,r^{n})\,\frac{\mathbf{x}}{r}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)+a_{n}^{m}\,r^{n}\,\nabla Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\right],

(12)

for the normal velocity we get

\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{n}=-\tilde{\kappa}\,\nabla p\cdot\mathbf{n}=-\tilde{\kappa}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}\left[n\,a_{n}^{m}\,r^{n-1}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\right].

(13)

Using a similar representation for
q
on
\Sigma
,

q=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}q_{n}^{m}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi),

(14)

and imposing the Neumann boundary condition in (
9
) on
r=R
, we find for the coefficients
a_{n}^{m}
,

-\tilde{\kappa}\,n\,a_{n}^{m}\,R^{n-1}=q_{n}^{m}.

(15)

4.2
Stokes problem outside a sphere

We now consider the solution of the Stokes problem with a no-slip boundary condition for simplicity,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla p-\mu\,\Delta\mathbf{u}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega^{c},\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0&\textrm{in}\ \Omega^{c},\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{u}\cdot\bm{\tau}=0&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma,\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}=-p_{D}&\textrm{on}\ \Sigma,\end{array}\right.

(16)

where
p_{D}
is the Darcy pressure on
\Sigma
and
\Omega^{c}
is the exterior of the sphere of radius
R
.

The solution to the Stokes equations in spherical harmonics is referred to as Lamb’s general solution
[
36
]
. Since the Stokes pressure satisfies Laplace’s equation, it can be represented similar to (
10
)-(
11
), but for the exterior solution we keep only the negative harmonics,

p=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{-n-1},

(17)

with

p_{-n-1}=\sum_{m=-n}^{n}b_{n}^{m}\,r^{-n-1}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi).

(18)

The disturbance velocity field is represented by (see
[
34
]
)

\mathbf{u}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[-\frac{(n-2)\,r^{2}\,\nabla p_{-n-1}}{2\mu\,n(2n-1)}+\frac{(n+1)\,\mathbf{x}\,p_{-n-1}}{\mu\,n(2n-1)}+\nabla\phi_{-n-1}+\nabla\times(\mathbf{x}\,\chi_{-n-1})\right],

(19)

where

\phi_{-n-1}=\sum_{m=-n}^{n}c_{n}^{m}\,r^{-n-1}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi),

(20)

\chi_{-n-1}=\sum_{m=-n}^{n}d_{n}^{m}\,r^{-n-1}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi).

(21)

The first two terms in (
19
) correspond to a particular solution, while the rest corresponds to the homogeneous solution, constructed from a potential (the
\nabla\phi
term) and a toroidal field (the
\nabla\times(\mathbf{x}\,\chi)
term)
[
34
]
.

The stress vector acting across the sphere can be expressed in general as (see
[
29
]
equation (3-2.37) or
[
34
]
exercise 4.6, p.104),

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{n}=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{n(n+2)\,r^{2}\,\nabla p_{n}}{(n+1)(2n+3)}-\frac{(2n^{2}+4n+3)\,\mathbf{x}\,p_{n}}{(n+1)(2n+3)}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu}{r}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[2(n-1)\nabla\phi_{n}+(n-1)\nabla\times(\mathbf{x}\,\chi_{n})\right].

(22)

For the exterior problem, we again only keep the negative harmonics and replace
n
in (
4.2
) with
-n-1
, to obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{n}=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(n-1)(n+1)\,r^{2}\,\nabla p_{-n-1}}{n(2n-1)}-\frac{(2n^{2}+1)\,\mathbf{x}\,p_{-n-1}}{n(2n-1)}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu}{r}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[2(n+2)\nabla\phi_{-n-1}+(n+2)\nabla\times(\mathbf{x}\,\chi_{-n-1})\right].

(23)

We now turn to the boundary conditions. The no-slip condition on
r=R
implies (see
[
34
]
p.89) that
\chi
is not needed and

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[-\frac{n(n+1)\,R}{2\mu\,(2n-1)}\,p_{-n-1}|_{r=R}+\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{R}\phi_{-n-1}|_{r=R}\right]=0,

(24)

which leads to

\phi_{-n-1}|_{r=R}=\frac{n\,R^{2}}{2\mu\,(n+2)(2n-1)}p_{-n-1}|_{r=R}.

(25)

Note that for the radial component of velocity we have from (
19
),

u_{r}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[-\frac{(n-2)\,r^{2}}{2\mu\,n(2n-1)}\,\frac{\partial p_{-n-1}}{\partial r}+\frac{(n+1)\,r\,p_{-n-1}}{\mu\,n(2n-1)}+\frac{\partial\phi_{-n-1}}{\partial r}\right].

(26)

Using Euler’s theorem for homogeneous polynomials,

r\,\frac{\partial h_{n}}{\partial r}=n\,h_{n},

(27)

where
h_{n}
is any solid harmonic of order
n
, the radial velocity in (
26
) simplifies to

u_{r}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(n+1)\,r\,p_{-n-1}}{2\mu\,(2n-1)}-\frac{(n+1)}{r}\phi_{-n-1}\right].

(28)

Similarly, for the radial component of the stress vector
\bm{\sigma}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{nr}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(n-1)(n+1)\,r^{2}}{n(2n-1)}\,\frac{\partial p_{-n-1}}{\partial r}-\frac{(2n^{2}+1)\,r\,p_{-n-1}}{n(2n-1)}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu}{r}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[2(n+2)\frac{\partial\phi_{-n-1}}{\partial r}\right],

(29)

which simplifies again using (
27
),

\sigma_{nr}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[-\frac{(n^{2}+3n-1)}{(2n-1)}p_{-n-1}+\frac{2\mu}{r^{2}}(n+1)(n+2)\phi_{-n-1}\right].

(30)

On the boundary
r=R
using (
25
), this becomes

\sigma_{nr}|_{r=R}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[-p_{-n-1}\right]_{r=R},

(31)

and therefore enforcing the boundary condition on the normal stress

\bm{\sigma}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\sigma_{nr}|_{r=R}=-p_{D}

(32)

leads to

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}b_{n}^{m}\,R^{-n-1}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}a_{n}^{m}\,R^{n}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi).

(33)

Using (
15
), we write

b_{n}^{m}=-\frac{R^{n+2}}{\tilde{\kappa}\,n}q_{n}^{m}.

(34)

4.3
Spectrum of the Dirichlet-Neumann procedure

We now diagonalize the iteration operator of the Dirichlet-Neumann method defined on the sphere. To derive the spectrum of the iteration operator, note that the iteration variable
q
is updated at each iteration according to (
8
), so we need the normal component of Stokes velocity on the sphere. We evaluate the radial velocity (
28
) on the boundary
r=R
and use (
25
) to get for the normal Stokes velocity,

u_{r}|_{r=R}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(n+1)\,R}{\mu\,(n+2)(2n-1)}p_{-n-1}|_{r=R}\right].

(35)

Using (
34
), this becomes

u_{r}|_{r=R}=-\frac{R^{2}}{\kappa}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(n+1)}{n(n+2)(2n-1)}\sum_{m=-n}^{n}q_{n}^{m}\,Y_{n}^{m}(\theta,\phi)\right].

(36)

For the DDM update we get

q^{(k)}=(1-\theta)q^{(k-1)}+\theta u_{r}^{(k)}|_{r=R},

(37)

which we write in the form of the diagonalized iteration operator
q^{(k)}=\mathcal{A}q^{(k-1)}
as

q_{n}^{m(k)}=\mathcal{A}_{n}q_{n}^{m(k-1)},

(38)

where

\mathcal{A}_{n}=(1-\theta)-\theta\frac{R^{2}}{\kappa}\frac{(n+1)}{n(n+2)(2n-1)},\qquad n=1,2,3,...

(39)

Figure
2
shows these coefficients for
n=1,...,50
for different values of permeability
\kappa
as well as different
\theta
. We can observe that for
\kappa=1
, a larger value of
\theta
leads to smaller coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{n}
and therefore the iterations are expected to converge faster, as we will show in the numerical tests. For smaller values of
\kappa
however, the coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{n}
get close to 1 as
n
increases, independent of the value of
\theta
. This can hinder the convergence rate of the algorithm when using a successive approximation technique as in (
8
). We will show in our numerical tests that this indeed is what happens, and switch to using a more efficient method, and one that does not require all eigenvalues to be less than 1, such as GMRES to help mitigate this issue. Interesting approaches that will be investigated in future work are (i) different decoupling, e.g. Robin-Robin transmission conditions, and (ii) using in place of
\theta
non-local operators based on the boundary integral formulation to speed up the convergence rates. Similar techniques were shown to dramatically speed up the iterations in two dimensions
[
9
]
.

Harmonic coefficients
\mathcal{A}_{n}
from (
39
) of the iteration operator on the unit sphere, for permeability values
\kappa=1,10^{-2},10^{-4}
.

5
Boundary integral formulations

There are two main approaches to formulating boundary integral equations, usually referred to as the ’direct’ and ’indirect’ approaches. The direct approach is based on the Green’s representation formula and is the approach we take in this work. While the analytic properties of the integral operators are the same with both approaches, our primary interest in the direct approach is that the resulting equations are for the physically relevant Dirichlet or Neumann data.

5.1
Darcy problem

The Green’s representation formula,

p_{D}(\mathbf{y})=-\int_{\partial\Omega_{D}}\frac{\partial p_{D}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}\,G(\mathbf{y,x})\,dS(\mathbf{x})+\int_{\partial\Omega_{D}}p_{D}(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{y,x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}\,dS(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\Omega_{D},

(40)

represents any harmonic function
p_{D}
in terms of its Cauchy data, that is, its boundary values and its normal derivative on the boundary. The first integral in (
40
) is the single-layer potential and the second integral is the double-layer potential, where the kernels are
G(\mathbf{y,x})=-1/4\pi|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|
and its derivative in the outward normal
\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{y,x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}G(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})=-(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})/4\pi|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|^{3}
. The double layer potential undergoes a discontinuity across the boundary, and the jump relations

\int_{\partial\Omega_{D}}\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{y,x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}\,dS(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mathbf{y}\in\Omega_{D}\\
1/2,&\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{D}\\
0,&\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\bar{\Omega}_{D}\end{array}\right.

(41)

are used to extend (
40
) to the boundary,

\frac{1}{2}p_{D}(\mathbf{y})=-\int_{\partial\Omega_{D}}\frac{\partial p_{D}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}\,G(\mathbf{y,x})\,dS(\mathbf{x})+\int_{\partial\Omega_{D}}p_{D}(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{y,x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}\,dS(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{D}.

(42)

We write (
42
) as a second-kind integral equation assuming known Neumann data
\partial p_{D}/\partial n
on the boundary,

\frac{1}{2}p_{D}=Hp_{D}+b_{D},\qquad\qquad b_{D}=-L\frac{\partial p_{D}}{\partial n},

(43)

where by
L
and
H
we denote the integral operators corresponding to the single- and double-layer potentials, respectively. The integral equation (
43
) can be solved by successive approximations, otherwise known as a Neumann’s iteration scheme (see, for example,
[
35
]
or
[
30
]
, Sec. 5.6.7),

p_{D}^{(n+1)}=\frac{1}{2}p_{D}^{(n)}+Hp_{D}^{(n)}+b_{D},\qquad n=0,1,2,...

(44)

5.2
Stokes problem

We follow a similar ’direct’ approach for the Stokes problem and use the following integral representation for the velocity,

\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{u}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{S}}S(\mathbf{y,x})\,\mathbf{f}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,dS(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{S}}\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\cdot T(\mathbf{y,x})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,dS(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\Omega_{S},

(45)

where
\mathbf{n}_{S}
is the unit outward normal vector to the Stokes domain boundary,
\mathbf{f}_{S}=\bm{\sigma}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}
is the surface force, or traction, on the boundary
\partial\Omega_{S}
,
\bm{\sigma}_{S}
is the stress tensor, and

S_{ij}(\mathbf{y,x})=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{|\mathbf{\hat{y}}|}+\frac{\hat{y}_{i}\ \hat{y}_{j}}{|\mathbf{\hat{y}}|^{3}},\qquad\qquad T_{ijk}(\mathbf{y,x})=-6\frac{\hat{y}_{i}\hat{y}_{j}\hat{y}_{k}}{|\mathbf{\hat{y}}|^{5}},

(46)

are the Stokeslet and stresslet fundamental solutions of the Stokes equations, where
\mathbf{\hat{y}}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}
,
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta, and
i,j,k=1,2,3
are Cartesian coordinates. A background flow
\mathbf{u}^{\infty}
is added for external flows.

The representation (
45
) is based on the Lorentz reciprocal theorem, and again, the first integral is called the single-layer potential and the second integral is the double-layer potential of Stokes flow. The double-layer undergoes a discontinuity across the boundary much like (
41
), and we again use jump conditions to continuously extend the representation (
45
) to the boundary:

\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{u}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{S}}S(\mathbf{y,x})\,\mathbf{f}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,dS(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{S}}\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\cdot T(\mathbf{y,x})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}(\mathbf{x})\,dS(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{S}.

(47)

We write (
47
) as

\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{S}=K\mathbf{u}_{S}+b_{S},\qquad\qquad b_{S}=-M\mathbf{f}_{S}+\mathbf{u}^{\infty},

(48)

where by
M
and
K
we denote the integral operators corresponding to the Stokes single- and double-layer potentials, respectively. Assuming a known surface force, equation (
48
) is a second-kind integral equation for
\mathbf{u}_{S}
, and we solve it by successive approximations

\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(n+1)}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(n)}+K\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(n)}+b_{S},\qquad n=0,1,2,...

(49)

It is a well-known challenge in the numerical solution of the integral equations (
44
) and (
49
) that singularities develop in the integrands
H,L,K,M
, and we address this issue by using a high accuracy regularization technique which we describe next.

5.3
High-order regularization of kernels on the surface

The integrands in (
42
) and (
47
) exhibit singularities for
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}
. To address this issue, we first use subtraction in the double layer integrals to reduce the singularities,

\displaystyle(Hp_{D})(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega_{D}}[p_{D}(\mathbf{x})-p_{D}(\mathbf{y})]\,\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{y,x})}{\partial n(\mathbf{x})}\,dS(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}p_{D}(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{D},

(50a)

\displaystyle(K\mathbf{u}_{S})(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{S}}[\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{y})]\cdot T(\mathbf{y,x})\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})dS(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{S}(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{S}.

(50b)

Next, we apply the regularization method of
[
4
,
5
]
for Laplace’s equation, and recently developed for the Stokes equations in
[
54
]
. The approach is to replace
1/r^{p}
, where
r=|\mathbf{\hat{y}}|
, by a smooth version
s(r/\delta)/r^{p}
, where
s(r/\delta)
is a radial smoothing function and
\delta>0
is a smoothing, or regularization, parameter. As we are interested in evaluating the integrals on the boundary, the smoothing factor
s
can be chosen to have high order, so that the accuracy in solving the integral equations approaches
O(h^{5})
, where
h
is the grid size in the coordinate planes. The derivation of these smoothing functions can be found in
[
4
,
5
,
54
]
. For completeness, we state the regularization formulas for both the Darcy and Stokes problems. The kernels in the Darcy representation (
42
) are replaced with their regularized versions,

G^{\delta}(\mathbf{y,x})=-\frac{s_{1}(r/\delta)}{4\pi r},\qquad\qquad\nabla G^{\delta}(\mathbf{y,x})=\nabla G^{\delta}(\mathbf{y,x})s_{2}(r/\delta)=\frac{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}{4\pi r^{3}}s_{2}(r/\delta).

(51)

The regularized single and double layer Stokes kernels in the representation (
47
) are

S^{\delta}_{ij}(\mathbf{y,x})=\delta_{ij}\frac{s_{1}(r/\delta)}{r}+\hat{y}_{i}\ \hat{y}_{j}\frac{s_{3}(r/\delta)}{r^{3}},\qquad\qquad T^{\delta}_{ijk}(\mathbf{y,x})=-6\hat{y}_{i}\hat{y}_{j}\hat{y}_{k}\frac{s_{4}(r/\delta)}{r^{5}}.

(52)

The radial smoothing functions
s_{i}(r/\delta)
are chosen to yield high order accuracy when the integrals are evaluated on the surface, with the error approaching
O(h^{5})
in discretization size
h
as shown in
[
4
,
5
,
54
]
,

\displaystyle s_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=\erf(r)-\frac{2}{3\sqrt{\pi}}r(2r^{2}-5)e^{-r^{2}},

(53a)

\displaystyle s_{2}(r)

\displaystyle=\erf(r)+\frac{2}{3\sqrt{\pi}}r(2r^{2}-3)e^{-r^{2}},

(53b)

\displaystyle s_{3}(r)

\displaystyle=\erf(r)-\frac{2}{3\sqrt{\pi}}r(4r^{4}-14r^{2}+3)e^{-r^{2}},

(53c)

\displaystyle s_{4}(r)

\displaystyle=\erf(r)-\frac{2}{9\sqrt{\pi}}r(8r^{6}-36r^{4}+6r^{2}+9)e^{-r^{2}},

(53d)

where
\erf
is the error function. This approach is quite simple in that it does not require any special quadrature for evaluating the integrals on the boundary.

5.4
Quadrature

With the integrands smoothed out, to discretize the integrals a quadrature rule for closed surfaces from
[
59
,
6
]
is applied, and we briefly describe it here. First, an angle
\theta
is chosen and a partition of unity on the unit sphere is defined,
consisting of functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3}
with
\Sigma_{i}\psi_{i}\equiv 1
such that

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{n})=0
if
|\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i}|\leq\cos{\theta}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the

i
th coordinate vector. Here we use
\theta=70^{o}
. For mesh size
h
,
a set
R_{3}
of quadrature points consists of points
\bf{x}
on the surface of the form

(j_{1}h,j_{2}h,x_{3})
such that
|\mathbf{n(x})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{3}|\geq\cos{\theta}
, where

\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})
is the unit normal at
\mathbf{x}
, see Fig.
3
. Sets
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are
defined similarly. For a function
f
on the surface the integral is computed as

\int_{S}f(\mathbf{x})\,dS(\mathbf{x})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in R_{i}}\frac{\psi_{i}(\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}))\,f(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{i}|}\,h^{2}.

(54)

The partition of unity functions
\psi_{i}
are constructed from the
C^{\infty}
bump function
b(r)=e^{r^{2}/(r^{2}-1)}
for
|r|<1
and zero otherwise. The quadrature is effectively reduced to the trapezoidal rule without boundary. Thus for regular integrands the quadrature has arbitrarily high order accuracy, limited only by the degree of smoothness of the integrand and surface. The points in
R_{i}

can be found by a line search since they are well separated; see
[
59
]
and
[
6
]
.

Suppose the surface is given by
\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=0
, with
\phi>0
outside, and the normal vector
\nabla\phi
is predominantly in the
x_{3}
direction. Then the parameterization is given by
x_{3}=z(x_{1},x_{2})
, where
z
is the vertical coordinate on the surface. Derivatives of this parameterization are computed implicitly by
z_{i}\equiv\partial z/\partial x_{i}=-\phi_{i}/\phi_{3}
, the tangent vectors are defined as
\mathbf{T}_{1}=(1,0,z_{1})
and
\mathbf{T}_{2}=(0,1,z_{2})
. The outward normal is
\mathbf{n}=\nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|
or
\mathbf{n}=\pm(-z_{1},-z_{2},1)/\sqrt{1+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}}
, where ”+” is used if
x_{3}>z(x_{1},x_{2})
outside and ”-” is used otherwise.

In Figure
3
, we show the set
R_{3}
of quadrature points, given by the top and bottom coordinate grids
(j_{1}h,j_{2}h,x_{3})
, for the following three surfaces:

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-1,

(55a)

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{x_{3}^{2}}{c^{2}}-1,

(55b)

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{4}\exp(-|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{k}|^{2}/r^{2})-c.

(55c)

For the ellipsoid (
55b
) we set
a=1,b=0.6,c=0.4
, and for the ’four-atom molecule’ surface (
55c
) we use centers
\mathbf{x}_{1}=(\sqrt{3}/3,0,-\sqrt{6}/12)
,
\mathbf{x}_{2,3}=(-\sqrt{3}/6,\pm.5,-\sqrt{6}/12)
,
\mathbf{x}_{4}=(0,0,\sqrt{6}/4)
and
r=.5
,

, as in
[
6
]
.

This quadrature rule works well for general surfaces without requiring coordinate charts, using projections on coordinate planes instead. As the weights for the quadrature points are found from a partition of unity on the unit sphere, they do not depend on the particular surface. The quadrature points can be found efficiently if, for example, the surface is given analytically or numerically as the level set of a function. With this approach, the formulation is very simple to implement, the data structure and information needed to describe the boundary are minimal, and there are no parameters to fine-tune except the regularization parameter
\delta
(which is usually taken as a multiple of grid size
h
, see the discussion in
[
54
]
).

6
Algorithm

Now we summarize the algorithm for solving the Stokes-Darcy system. First, we note that

\frac{\partial p_{D}}{\partial n}=-\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\mathbf{u}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D},

(56)

and by combining the interface conditions (
4
) and (
5
),

\mathbf{f}_{S}=\bm{\sigma}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=-p_{D}\mathbf{n}_{S}-\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\kappa}}[\mathbf{u}_{S}-(\mathbf{u}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S})\mathbf{n}_{S}],

(57)

so we rewrite the Darcy (
43
) and Stokes (
48
) integral equations as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p_{D}

\displaystyle=Hp_{D}+b_{D},\qquad\qquad b_{D}=\frac{\mu}{\kappa}L(\mathbf{u}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D}),

(58a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{S}

\displaystyle=K\mathbf{u}_{S}+b_{S},\qquad\qquad b_{S}=-M\mathbf{f}_{S}+\mathbf{u}^{\infty},

(58b)

We outline the algorithm of the iterative Stokes and Darcy solutions using successive approximations approach, keeping in mind that the successive approximations are eventually replaced by GMRES for a more efficient solution.

1.

Let
k=0
,
\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
and
q^{(0)}=-\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(0)}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}=0
.

2.

For
k=1,2,...
until convergence, do:

(a)

Compute the Laplace single layer potential
b_{D}
in (
58a
) using
\mathbf{u}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D}=q^{(k-1)}
.

(b)

Solve the Darcy problem (
58a
) for
p_{D}^{(k)}
via (i) successive approximations (
44
) or (ii) GMRES.

(c)

Compute
\mathbf{f}_{S}
as in (
57
), using
p_{D}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{u}^{(k-1)}_{S}
.

(d)

Compute the Stokes single layer potential
b_{S}
in (
58b
).

(e)

Solve the Stokes problem (
58b
) for
\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(k)}
via (i) successive approximations (
49
) or (ii) GMRES.

(f)

Set
q^{(k)}=(1-\theta)q^{(k-1)}-\theta\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}
.

Here
\theta\in(0,1)
is a relaxation parameter. We assume convergence when the relative error between
q^{(k)}
and
q^{(k-1)}
, which we call the residual,

\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q^{(k)}_{i}-q^{(k-1)}_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q^{(k)}_{i}\right)^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

(59)

falls below a prescribed tolerance.

6.1
Discrete iteration operator

To analyze the convergence properties of DDM in the discrete solution, we look at
the iteration matrix obtained from the integral formulation and its eigenvalues. For simplicity, we assume
\mathbf{u}^{\infty}=0
and the slip coefficient
\gamma=0
. With the iteration variable defined as
q=-\mathbf{u}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}
, each Dirichlet-Neumann iteration includes solving the following two problems:

\frac{1}{2}p_{D}^{(k)}=\tilde{H}p_{D}^{(k)}+\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\tilde{L}q^{(k-1)},

(60)

\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(k)}=\tilde{K}\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(k)}+\tilde{M}_{n}p_{D}^{(k)},

(61)

where
\tilde{H},\tilde{K}
are the matrices corresponding to the double layer potentials for Laplace and Stokes, respectively, and
\tilde{L},\tilde{M}_{n}
are the matrices corresponding to the single layer for Laplace and Stokes (the Stokes single layer
\tilde{M}_{n}
including an extra multiplication by the normal). The ’tilde’ symbol denotes the discrete version of the operators, with the kernels smoothed out as in Section
5.3
and the quadrature in Section
5.4
applied. All these are dense matrices.

We can then write the discrete representation of the iteration operator as follows:

\displaystyle q^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\theta)q^{(k-1)}-\theta\mathbf{u}_{S}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\theta)q^{(k-1)}-\theta\left[(I/2-\tilde{K})^{-1}\tilde{M}_{n}p_{D}^{(k)}\right]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\theta)q^{(k-1)}-\theta\left[(I/2-\tilde{K})^{-1}\tilde{M}_{n}\right]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}\,p_{D}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\theta)q^{(k-1)}-\theta\left[(I/2-\tilde{K})^{-1}\tilde{M}_{n}\right]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}\,(I/2-\tilde{H})^{-1}\,\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\,\tilde{L}q^{(k-1)}.

We write (
62
) as

q^{(k)}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}q^{(k-1)},

(63)

with the iteration matrix

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}=I-\theta\left\{I+\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\big[(I/2-\tilde{K})^{-1}\tilde{M}_{n}\big]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{S}\ \big[(I/2-\tilde{H})^{-1}\tilde{L}\big]\right\}.

(64)

The operator in the first square brackets corresponds to solving the Stokes problem for
\mathbf{u}_{S}
, while the operator in the second square brackets corresponds to solving the Darcy problem for
p_{D}
.

•

Local problems: SA vs. GMRES
. Table
1
shows the number of successive approximations (local SA) performed at each D-N iteration, as well as the number of GMRES iterations (local GMRES) as an alternative. For SA, a range is shown because the number decreases slowly with each D-N iteration. It is clear that successive approximations do not converge quickly, and GMRES is far superior in solving the local Stokes and Darcy problems at each iteration. Both methods require evaluating the double layer potential at each iteration, and since GMRES performs far fewer double layer evaluations, we use GMRES in all our remaining tests.

•

Local problems: CPU time
. Both SA and GMRES require evaluating the double layer potential at each iteration, which in discretized form is a dense linear system. The Darcy system is
N\times N
, while the Stokes system is
3N\times 3N
, where
N
is the number of quadrature points. The computational cost is then
O(N^{2})
for each problem. The actual CPU times for each double layer integral evaluation are shown in Figure
7
. Note that the discretized integrals were implemented to take advantage of the target-source symmetry in all kernels, reducing the computational time in half. If very large systems are considered, the computational time could be reduced to
O(N\log N)
or
O(N)
using a fast summation algorithm such as a treecode
[
58
]
or a fast multipole method
[
56
]
.

8
Conclusions and future work

We have presented a domain decomposition method of a sequential Dirichlet-Neumann type to solve the Stokes-Darcy system of PDEs. The method is based on splitting the interface conditions so that first, the Darcy problem is solved with one of the conditions, and then the Stokes problem is solved with the other two. The information is exchanged at each step of this iterative procedure. The local Stokes and Darcy problems are solved by second kind boundary integral formulations. A regularization technique specifically designed to achieve high accuracy on the boundary is applied, and the quadrature method of
[
59
,
6
]
is used, which does not use coordinate charts and is simple to implement. Numerical results validate the algorithm against a problem with a known analytical solution of viscous flow past a porous sphere, as well as demonstrate the applicability of the method to more general geometries.

Our convergence analysis of the method shows strong dependence of the spectral radius of the iteration operator on the permeability
\kappa
. For small values of
\kappa
, the eigenvalues cluster close to 1, inhibiting convergence of successive approximations. While we demonstrate that using a Krylov subspace method such as GMRES mitigates this issue, more robust DDMs that are less dependent on the physical parameters are desirable and will be investigated. It was shown in the two-dimensional case
[
9
]
that a Robin-Robin type domain decomposition method with non-local operators (based on the boundary integral formulations) can be chosen in the de-coupling of transmission conditions to dramatically improve the convergence properties of the solution. The dependence of convergence rates on the physical parameters of viscosity
\mu
and permeability
\kappa
can be reduced as well. Similar approaches will be investigated in the three-dimensional case.

In many relevant applications, the porous medium has a heterogeneous structure, and the BIEM is not a suitable choice to model the flow there. This work can be extended to model such porous media, by coupling the BIEM in the domain of free flow with more appropriate techniques such as the finite element method (FEM) in the porous domain with variable properties. With this approach, the FEM can handle the porous domain where the heterogeneities occur, while the BIEM will efficiently deal with the Stokes domain, which can be large or even infinite.

To limit the scope of this paper, only the steady state problem was considered with a single interface. In future work, time-dependent problems and multi-surface cases will be considered. In such a simulation, several surfaces can be close to each other, and it is well-known that the integrands become nearly singular and therefore numerically non-trivial to integrate with sufficient accuracy. The correction methods of
[
4
,
5
,
54
]
will be used to improve the accuracy in the nearly singular case. Another challenge in such a simulation is the high computational cost of the direct evaluation of the resulting dense linear systems, which is
O(N^{2})
, where
N
is the number of unknowns. To be able to simulate large enough systems, the computational cost will be reduced using fast summation techniques such as the kernel-independent treecode
[
58
]
or a fast multipole method
[
56
]
.
