Probing rotational decoherence with a trapped-ion planar rotor
Neil Glikin, Benjamin A. Stickler, Ryan Tollefsen, Sara Mouradian, Neha Yadav, Erik Urban, Klaus Hornberger, Hartmut Häffner

Abstract
The quantum rotor is one of the simplest model systems in quantum mechanics, but only in recent years has theoretical work revealed general fundamental scaling laws for its decoherence. For example, a superposition of orientations decoheres at a rate proportional to the sine squared of the angle between them. Here we observe scaling laws for rotational decoherence dynamics for the first time, using a 4 µm-diameter planar rotor composed of two Paul-trapped ions. We prepare the rotational motion of the ion crystal into superpositions of angular momentum with well-defined differences ranging from 1 − 3 ​ ℏ 1-3\,\hbar , and measure the rate of decoherence. We also tune the system-environment interaction strength by introducing resonant electric field noise. The observed scaling relationships for decoherence are in excellent agreement with recent theoretical work, and are directly relevant to the growing development of rotor-based quantum applications.

Here we report on an experimental study of the decoherence dynamics of a planar rigid rotor. The rotor is composed of two trapped atomic ions separated by several micrometers and rotating with a frequency on the order of one hundred kilohertz. We prepare this system in a superposition of different rotation rates, which turns into a superposition of orientation states, and engineer a noisy electric-field environment to induce rotational decoherence on the millisecond timescale. Our results demonstrate a sine-squared scaling of the decoherence rate for orientational superpositions in the small-angle limit and the expected scaling with the angular momentum diffusion coefficient. Both findings are in excellent agreement with theory.

Controlled environment.
—We experimentally realize a controllable environment by applying a noisy voltage to the outer control electrodes of the trap, giving rise to a noisy electric field that couples to the rotor via the ions’ charge. Since the two-ion crystal has zero dipole moment, the interaction with the noise field is via its quadrupole component and thus is proportional to the local field gradients,

(1)

Rotational diffusion and decoherence.
—We present two types of measurements, (i) angular momentum diffusion of the quantum rotor, and (ii) decoherence of angular momentum superpositions. In both cases, we begin with the ions trapped in an pinning potential which fixes their locations. We first cool all motional degrees of freedom with Doppler cooling, then sideband cool the transverse librational motion and the motion normal to the rotor plane to their vibrational ground states. We then initialize the ions’ internal states to
\ket{S}

Discussion.
—In summary, we have presented an experimental study of the decoherence dynamics of a trapped-ion planar rotor due to a noisy electric field. We have verified the theoretical prediction that the decoherence rate of a superposition of two orientations is proportional to
\sin^{2}(\phi-\phi^{\prime})
in the small-angle limit. In addition, by independently engineering and measuring the rate of angular momentum diffusion, we have confirmed the dependence of rotational decoherence on angular momentum diffusion. These results serve as a controlled test of the theory of rotor decoherence, which constitutes a necessary step towards utilizing rotational quantum coherence for sensing, quantum information processing, and fundamental tests
DeMille 2002
;
Grimsmo et al. 2020
;
Albert et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2011
;
Lechner and Zoller 2013
;
Sundar et al. 2018
;
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Stickler et al. 2018a
;
Ma et al. 2020
. Our work may serve as a basis for the observation of decoherence-free subspaces in the rotational degree of freedom
Pedernales et al. 2020
, for future studies of surface noise beyond heating measurements with single ions
Martinetz et al. 2022
, and for first experiments creating massive superposition states with nanoscale spin rotors
Rusconi et al. 2022
. Finally, quantitatively understanding rotor decoherence in planar surface traps is a crucial step first step towards probing the exchange phase of distant atoms in trapped rotor interferometer

Theoretical description

A.1
Derivation of the master equation

We consider a planar rigid rotor formed by two ions with charges
e
at positions
r_{1,2}=z{\bf e}_{z}\pm r{\bf e}_{\rho}(\phi)
, where
z
is the height above the surface trap,
2r
is the ion separation, and the angle
\phi
describes the rotor orientation. The potential energy of the rotor in the quadrupole potential follows as

V(\phi)=-er^{2}{\bf e}_{\rho}(\phi)\cdot\left[\nabla\otimes{\bf E}(z{\bf e}_{z},t)\right]{\bf e}_{\rho}(\phi),

(7)

where
{\bf E}({\bf r},t)
is the local field strength at time
t
. The vector gradient
\nabla\otimes{\bf E}(z{\bf e}_{z},t)
is symmetric and traceless, and determined by the trap geometry. Using that
L_{\pm}=e^{\pm i\phi}
, this potential energy can be rewritten up to an additive constant as

V_{t}(\phi)=\frac{er^{2}}{4}\left(\varepsilon_{t}L_{+}^{2}+\varepsilon_{t}^{*}L_{-}^{2}\right),

(8)

where we defined the complex-valued combination of field gradients

\varepsilon_{t}=-\partial_{x}E_{x}+\partial_{y}E_{y}+2i\partial_{x}E_{y},

(9)

whose magnitude is determined by the trapping geometry as well as the time-dependent voltage applied to the control electrodes.

The quantum state
\ket{\psi_t}
of the rotor evolves according to the Schrödinger equation with the free Hamiltonian
H_{0}=L_{z}^{2}/2I
and the potential (
8
). Transformation to the interaction picture
\ket{\psi_t}=\exp(-iH_0t/\hbar)\ket{\chi_t}
shows that the rotor evolves according to the interaction picture potential
\widetilde{V}_{t}=\exp(i H_0 t/\hbar)V_{t}\exp(-iH_0 t/\hbar)
, which can be further evaluated by using that

L_{\pm}^{2}(t)=e^{iH_{0}t/\hbar}L_{\pm}^{2}e^{-iH_{0}t/\hbar}=e^{2i\hbar t/I}L_{\pm}^{2}e^{\pm 2itL_{z}/I},

(10)

as follows from the commutation relations
[L_{z},L_{\pm}]=\pm\hbar L_{\pm}
and
L_{\pm}L_{\mp}=\mathbb{I}
. The interaction picture Schrödinger equation is thus given by
i\hbar\partial_{t}\ket{\chi_t}=\widetilde{V}_{t}\ket{\chi_t}
with

\widetilde{V}_{t}=\frac{er^{2}}{4}\left(e^{2i\hbar t/I}\varepsilon_{t}L_{+}^{2}e^{2itL_{z}/I}+{\rm h.c.}\right).

(11)

We now integrate this Schrödinger equation over the time interval
\Delta t\gg 1/\omega_{\rm rot}
and iterate the resulting integral equation twice to obtain

\displaystyle\ket{\chi_{t + \Delta t}}=

\displaystyle\ket{\chi_t}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime}}\ket{\chi_t}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime}}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\ket{\chi_{t''}}.

(12)

To express that
\ket{\chi_t}
evolves little in
\Delta t
, we approximate
\ket{\chi_{t''}}\simeq\ket{\chi_t}
.

This equation describes how the dynamics of the pure state
\ket{\chi_t}
is influenced by the time-dependent voltage applied to the control electrodes. This voltage is chosen randomly, so that the rotor state is described by the density operator
\widetilde{\rho}=\mathbb{E}[\ket{\chi_t}\bra{\chi_t}]
, where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the ensemble average over many repetitions of the experiment. The equation of motion for
\widetilde{\rho}
can be derived by calculating

\Delta\widetilde{\rho}=\mathbb{E}(\ket{\chi_{t + \Delta t}}\bra{\chi_{t + \Delta t}})-\ket{\chi_t}\bra{\chi_t}

(13)

by inserting Eq. (
A.1
), which yields

\displaystyle\Delta\widetilde{\rho}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime}}\widetilde{\rho}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime}}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\right),\widetilde{\rho}\right\}.

(14)

Here,
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
denotes the anticommutator.

The time integrals appearing in Eq. (
A.1
) can be calculated for rapidly revolving rotor states
\ket{\chi_t}
, which are localized in angular momentum close to the quantum number
\overline{\ell}=I\omega_{\rm rot}/\hbar\gg 1
with angular momentum spread
\sigma_{\ell}\ll\ell
. Since this implies
\exp(\pm 2 i t L_z/I)\ket{\chi_t}\simeq e^{\pm 2i\omega_{\rm rot}t}\ket{\chi_t}
one is left with the integrals

\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{t^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{*}_{t^{\prime\prime}})e^{2i\omega_{\rm rot}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\simeq\Delta tS_{\varepsilon}(2\omega_{\rm rot})

(15a)

and

\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{t^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{*}_{t^{\prime\prime}})e^{2i\omega_{\rm rot}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\simeq\frac{\Delta t}{2}S_{\varepsilon}(2\omega_{\rm rot})

(15b)

upon inserting (
11
) and neglecting rapidly rotating terms
\exp[\pm i2\omega_{\rm rot}(t^{\prime}+t^{\prime\prime})]\simeq 0
in the rotating-wave approximation.
In both integrals we used that
\varepsilon_{t}
is described by a real, stationary, and time-inversion-invariant stochastic process with zero mean, multiplied by a fixed complex phase factor (as determined by (
9
)), to identify the power spectral density

S_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{t}\varepsilon^{*}_{t-\tau})e^{i\omega\tau}.

(16)

The statistical properties of the the applied voltage directly translate to those of
\varepsilon_{t}
since retardation effects in the field propagation can be neglected at the short distances of the experiment.
The time interval
\Delta t
will be taken large compared to the decay time of the noise correlations. Moreover, the noise filter bandwidth of 20 kHz is large in comparison to the width of the rotation frequencies
\hbar\sigma_{\ell}/I\simeq 1
kHz, justifying the approximation before Eq. (
15
).

Inserting the integrals (
15
) into equation (
A.1
) shows that the ensemble-averaged quantum state follows the Markovian master equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\rho}\simeq\frac{\Delta\widetilde{\rho}}{\Delta t}=\frac{D}{4\hbar^{2}}\left(L_{+}^{2}\widetilde{\rho}L_{-}^{2}+L_{-}^{2}\widetilde{\rho}L_{+}^{2}-2\widetilde{\rho}\right),

(17)

with the diffusion coefficient
D
given by
e^{2}r^{4}S_{\varepsilon}(2\omega_{\text{rot}})/4
as stated in the main text. Transforming back to the Schrödinger picture yields (under the same assumptions as above) the master equation (2).

Note that the diffusion coefficient can also be obtained in a classical picture. Starting from the equation of motion in the noisy potential (
8
),

\dot{L}_{z}=-i\frac{er^{2}}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{t}e^{2i\phi}-\varepsilon^{*}_{t}e^{-2i\phi}\right),

(18)

together with
\dot{\phi}=L_{z}/I
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}(L_{z})={\rm const}
. In order to obtain the diffusion coefficient, we first formally solve (
18
) for a time step
\Delta t
in the co-rotating frame
\phi\to\phi+\omega_{\rm rot}t
,

\displaystyle L_{z}(t+\Delta t)=

\displaystyle L_{z}(t)-i\frac{er^{2}}{2}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\left(\varepsilon_{t^{\prime}}e^{2i\phi+2i\omega_{\rm rot}t^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\varepsilon^{*}_{t^{\prime}}e^{-2i\phi-2i\omega_{\rm rot}t^{\prime}}\right).

(19)

Here we use that
\phi(t^{\prime})\simeq\phi(t)
under the same approximation as after (
A.1
). A rotating wave approximation and (
15a
) yield the second moment

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle[L_{z}^{2}(t+\Delta t)]-\mathbb{E}[L_{z}^{2}(t)]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{e^{2}r^{4}}{2}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon_{t^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{*}_{t^{\prime\prime}})e^{2i\omega_{\rm rot}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle\simeq 2D\Delta t.

(20)

Drawing the limit
\Delta t\to 0
, we thus obtain
\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}(L_{z}^{2})=2D
as also implied by the quantum master equation (
2
). While angular momentum diffusion can be understood classically, the observed decoherence of rotational superpositions is a genuine signature of the quantum master equation (
2
).

A.2
Time evolution in the noise field

The time evolution under the master equation (2) can be determined by using Eq. (
A.1
) for a pure state
\ket{\chi_t}
and then calculating the ensemble average over many repetitions of the experiment. Specifically, approximating
\ket{\chi_{t''}}
on the right hand side by
\ket{\chi_t}
(see above), the operator

\displaystyle W(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime}}\widetilde{V}_{t^{\prime\prime}}

(21)

propagates the state over the time interval
\Delta t
,
\ket{\chi_{t+\Delta t}}=W(t)\ket{\chi_t}
. Concatenating
N=t/\Delta t\gg 1
of these timesteps, we can express the Schrödinger-picture state at time
t
as

\ket{\psi_t}=e^{-iH_{0}t/\hbar}U_{t}\ket{\psi_0},

(22)

where
\ket{\psi_0}
is the initial state and

U_{t}\simeq W\left(t-\Delta t\right)\cdots W\left(\Delta t\right)W\left(0\right),

(23)

When calculating expectation values in the ensemble average, we can use that the random fields
\varepsilon_{t}
are uncorrelated between different time intervals
[n\Delta t,(n+1)\Delta t]
. One can thus decompose the ensemble average over the random noise trajectory
\mathbb{E}(\cdot)
by the product of ensemble averages
\mathbb{E}_{n}(\cdot)
at fixed times
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, so that

\mathbb{E}(\cdot)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{N-1}(\mathbb{E}_{N-2}(\cdots\mathbb{E}_{0}(\cdot))).

(24)

This property will be used below to determine the outcome of the Ramsey interference protocol.

The Ramsey scheme discussed below requires the time evolution (
22
) of a coherent rotor state
\ket{\Psi_{\ell}}
characterized by a mean angular momentum quantum number
\ell
and width
\sigma_{\ell}
with
\ell\gg\sigma_{\ell}\gg 1
. Using that
L_{\pm}^{2}(t)\ket{\Psi_\ell}\simeq e^{\pm 4i\ell\omega_{\rm r}t}\ket{\Psi_\ell}
, with
\omega_{\rm r}=\hbar/2I
, yields

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_t}

\displaystyle\simeq u_{\ell}(t)e^{-iH_{0}t/\hbar}\ket{\Psi_\ell}

\displaystyle\simeq u_{\ell}(t)e^{i\ell^{2}\omega_{\rm r}t}e^{-i2\ell\omega_{\rm r}tL_{z}/\hbar}\ket{\Psi_\ell}.

(25)

Here,
u_{\ell}(t)
is the c-number obtained by replacing in
U_{t}
all operators
L_{\pm}^{2}(t_{n})
by
\exp( \pm 4i \ell\omega_{\rm r} t_n)
. In the second expression, the unitary operator
\exp( - i 2 \ell\omega_{\rm r} t L_z/\hbar)
serves to displace the rotor state by the angle
2\ell\omega_{\rm r}t
, while the global phase factor results from neglecting dispersion.

The Ramsey scheme also requires a generalization of (
A.2
) for the the case of a coherent rotor state displaced by the angle
\varphi
. Using that

L_{\pm}^{2}(t)e^{-i\varphi L_{z}/\hbar}=e^{\pm 2i\varphi}e^{-i\varphi L_{z}/\hbar}L_{\pm}^{2}(t),

(26)

we obtain

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{t}}

\displaystyle\simeq u_{\ell}(t|\varphi)e^{-iH_{0}t/\hbar}e^{-i\varphi L_{z}/\hbar}\ket{\Psi_\ell}

\displaystyle\simeq u_{\ell}(t|\varphi)e^{i\ell^{2}\omega_{\rm r}t}e^{-i(\varphi+2\ell\omega_{\rm r}t)L_{z}/\hbar}\ket{\Psi_\ell}.

(27)

Like above, the c-numbers
u_{\ell}(t|\varphi)
are obtained by replacing in
U_{t}
all operators
L_{\pm}^{2}(t_{n})
by
\exp[\pm 2i(\varphi+2\ell\omega_{\rm r}t_{n})]
.

A.3
Rabi pulses

This section derives the action of a Rabi pulse on the four internal levels and the rotational degrees of freedom of the two-ion rotor. We start with the light-matter Hamiltonian

H_{\rm L}=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sigma^{+}_{j}f_{\rm L}({\bf r}_{j},t)+{\rm h.c.},

(28)

where
\sigma_{j}^{+}=\ket{D}_{j}\bra{S}_{j}
describes the excitation of the quadrupole transition of atom
j
, and
f_{\rm L}({\bf r}_{j},t)
is determined by the relevant field gradient at the position of the
j
-th atom. Assuming plane-wave illumination with inclination
\theta
to the rotor plane, we have

f_{\rm L}({\bf r}_{j},t)=\hbar g_{0}e^{-i(kz\cos\theta+\omega t)}e^{i(-1)^{j}kr\sin\theta\cos\phi},

(29)

where
g_{0}
is the coupling rate,
\omega
is the laser frequency, and
k=\omega/c
. Using that

\bra{\ell+ \Delta\ell}e^{\pm ikr\sin\theta\cos\phi}\ket{\ell}=(\pm i)^{\Delta\ell}J_{\Delta\ell}(kr\sin\theta),

(30)

one obtains

\displaystyle H_{\rm L}=

\displaystyle\hbar g_{0}e^{-i(kz\cos\theta+\omega t)}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\Delta\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{j\Delta\ell}i^{\Delta\ell}

\displaystyle\times J_{\Delta\ell}(kr\sin\theta)\sigma_{j}^{+}L_{+}^{\Delta\ell}+{\rm h.c.},

(31)

The light-matter Hamiltonian (
A.3
) can be further simplified by first transforming into the frame co-rotating with the laser field and then into the interaction picture with respect to
H_{\rm rot}=\hbar\Delta\omega(\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{1}^{-}+\sigma_{2}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{-})+H_{0}
, where
\Delta\omega=\omega_{0}-\omega
denotes the laser detuning from the electronic transition frequency
\omega_{0}
. This yields

\displaystyle H_{\rm L}(t)=

\displaystyle e^{iH_{\rm rot}t/\hbar}H_{\rm L}e^{-iH_{\rm rot}t/\hbar}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar g_{0}e^{-ikz\cos\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\Delta\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{j\Delta\ell}i^{\Delta\ell}J_{\Delta\ell}(kr\sin\theta)

\displaystyle\times e^{it[\Delta\omega+\Delta\ell(2\ell+\Delta\ell)\omega_{\rm r}]}\sigma_{j}^{+}\ket{\ell+ \Delta\ell}\bra{\ell}+{\rm h.c.}

(32)

For rapidly rotating states, where
2\ell+\Delta\ell\simeq 2\ell
, the laser frequency is tuned in resonance with the angular momentum transition
\Delta\ell
by choosing
\omega\simeq\omega_{0}+2\ell\omega_{\rm r}\Delta\ell
, so that

H_{\rm L}(t)\simeq\hbar g\left[\sigma_{1}^{+}L_{+}^{\Delta\ell}+(-1)^{\Delta\ell}\sigma_{2}^{+}L_{+}^{\Delta\ell}\right]+{\rm h.c.},

(33)

where we defined

g=g_{0}e^{-ikz\cos\theta}(-i)^{\Delta\ell}J_{\Delta\ell}(kr\sin\theta).

(34)

The excitation of the atom’s electronic state is thus accompanied by an angular momentum kick of strength
\Delta\ell
. Applying the laser field for a short time period
t
, as described by the unitary

\displaystyle U_{\rm L}(t)=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-igt\left(\sigma_{1}^{+}L_{+}^{\Delta\ell}+{\rm h.c.}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-igt(-1)^{\Delta\ell}\left(\sigma_{2}^{+}L_{+}^{\Delta\ell}+{\rm h.c.}\right)\right],

(35)

gives rise to Rabi oscillations, which can be used to implement
\pi/2
-pulses and
\pi
-pulses. Specifically, choosing
gt=-i\pi/4
implements a
\pi/2
-pulse,

\displaystyle X_{\pi/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{+}+(-1)^{\Delta\ell}\sigma_{2}^{+}}{2}L_{+}^{\Delta\ell}+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{-}+(-1)^{\Delta\ell}\sigma_{2}^{-}}{2}L_{-}^{\Delta\ell}

\displaystyle+\frac{(-1)^{\Delta\ell}}{2}\left(\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{+}L_{+}^{2\Delta\ell}+\sigma_{1}^{-}\sigma_{2}^{-}L_{-}^{2\Delta\ell}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{-}-\sigma_{1}^{-}\sigma_{2}^{+}\right).

(36)

Likewise, choosing
gt=-i\pi/2
gives the
\pi
-pulse

\displaystyle X_{\pi}=

\displaystyle(-1)^{\Delta\ell}\left(\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{+}L_{+}^{2\Delta\ell}+\sigma_{1}^{-}\sigma_{2}^{-}L_{-}^{2\Delta\ell}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{-}-\sigma_{1}^{-}\sigma_{2}^{+}\right).

(37)

. Using Eq. (

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi_3}=

A.5
Ensemble averaged signal

We are now in the position to evaluate the ensemble-averaged signal (
(6)
) by using the decomposition (
23
) and the rule (
24
). We start with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(a_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}a^{*}_{\overline{\ell}})=

\displaystyle\exp\left[i\frac{\omega_{\rm r}\tau}{2}\Delta\ell(2\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\left[u_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)u_{\overline{\ell}}^{*}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right].

(49)

The expectation value in the second line can be calculated by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)u_{\overline{\ell}}^{*}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right]=\prod_{n,n^{\prime}=0}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}(n\Delta t)w_{\overline{\ell}}^{*}(n^{\prime}\Delta t)\right],

(50)

where
\Delta t=\tau/2N
with
N\gg 1
, and we defined

\displaystyle w_{\ell}(n\Delta t)=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{n\Delta t}^{(n+1)\Delta t}dt^{\prime}v_{\ell}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{n\Delta t}^{(n+1)\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{n\Delta t}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}v_{\ell}(t^{\prime})v_{\ell}(t^{\prime\prime}),

(51)

with

v_{\ell}(t)=\frac{er^{2}}{4}(\varepsilon_{t}e^{4i\ell\omega_{\rm r}t}+{\rm c.c.}).

(52)

This latter function is obtained by replacing
L_{\pm}^{2}(t)
in the potential energy (
11
) by the c-numbers
\exp(\pm 4 i \ell\omega_{\rm r}t)
.

Inserting Eq. (
A.5
) into Eq. (
50
) and using the same steps as in the derivation of the master equation yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[u_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)u_{\overline{\ell}}^{*}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\prod_{n=0}^{N}\left[1-\frac{D\Delta t}{\hbar^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\Delta\ell\left(2n+1\right)\omega_{\rm r}\Delta t\right]\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\prod_{n=0}^{N}\exp\left[-\frac{D\Delta t}{\hbar^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\Delta\ell\left(2n+1\right)\omega_{\rm r}\Delta t\right]\right].

(53)

N\gg 1
the sum in the exponent can be approximated by an integral, leading to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(a_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}a^{*}_{\overline{\ell}})\simeq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{D\tau}{4\hbar^{2}}\left[1-{\rm sinc}\left(2\Delta\ell\omega_{\rm r}\tau\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[i\frac{\omega_{\rm r}\tau}{2}\Delta\ell(2\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell)\right].

(54)

In a similar fashion, one can evaluate the expectation value

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(a_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}a^{*}_{\overline{\ell}+2\Delta\ell})\simeq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{D\tau}{4\hbar^{2}}\left[1-{\rm sinc}\left(2\Delta\ell\omega_{\rm r}\tau\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-i\frac{\omega_{\rm r}\tau}{2}\Delta\ell(2\overline{\ell}+3\Delta\ell)\right]

(55)

It contains the same decoherence-induced decay as the expression above since only the relative angular momentum between the two superposition branches matters.

The expectation values containing
b_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}
coefficients can be calculated by a straight-forward generalization replacing
v_{\ell}(t)
by

v_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{er^{2}}{4}(\varepsilon_{t}e^{i2\ell^{\prime}\omega_{\rm r}\tau}e^{4i\ell\omega_{\rm r}t}+{\rm c.c.}).

(56)

This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[b_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}^{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}(b^{\overline{\ell}}_{\overline{\ell}+2\Delta\ell})^{*}\right]\simeq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{D\tau}{4\hbar^{2}}\left[1-{\rm sinc}\left(2\Delta\ell\omega_{\rm r}\tau\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-i\frac{\omega_{\rm r}\tau}{2}\Delta\ell(2\overline{\ell}+3\Delta\ell)\right]

(57)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[b_{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}^{\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell}(b_{\overline{\ell}}^{\overline{\ell}+2\Delta\ell})^{*}\right]\simeq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{D\tau}{4\hbar^{2}}\left[1-{\rm sinc}\left(2\Delta\ell\omega_{\rm r}\tau\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[i\frac{\omega_{\rm r}\tau}{2}\Delta\ell(2\overline{\ell}+\Delta\ell)\right].

(58)

Putting everything together, we obtain the measurement signal

\displaystyle P(D)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos\left(\omega_{\rm r}\tau\Delta\ell^{2}\right)C(\tau),

(59)

with the reduction factor of the Ramsey fringe contrast due to rotational decoherence

C(\tau)=\exp\left(-\frac{D\tau}{2\hbar^{2}}\left[1-{\rm sinc}\left(2\Delta\ell\omega_{\rm r}\tau\right)\right]\right).

(60)

Appendix B
Coherent effects on the contrast decay profile

From (
59
) we see that the phase contrast, which decays from decoherence as
C(\tau)
given by (4) in the main text, is additionally modulated by a factor

C_{\text{mod}}(\tau)=\cos(\omega_\text{r}\Delta\ell^2\tau).

(61)

This arises from interference between the two relevant coherences of the rotor state:
\ket{\ell}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ell+\Delta\ell}
and
\ket{\ell+\Delta\ell}\leftrightarrow\ket{\ell+2\Delta\ell}
, whose respective transition frequencies differ by
2\Delta\ell^{2}\omega_{\rm r}=2\pi\times 26
Hz
\times\,\Delta\ell^{2}
. This modulation is necessarily present in the coherence measurements presented in this work, which otherwise yield a contrast profile of the form
\exp[-(\gamma\tau)^{3}]
.
Equation (
61
) predicts a node in the fringe contrast at
\tau=\pi/(2\Delta\ell^{2}\omega_{\rm r})
. The modulation may therefore be safely neglected only if the coherence time is much shorter than the node time,
1/\gamma\ll\pi/(2\Delta\ell^{2}\omega_{\rm r})
. Otherwise, the effect of the modulation may be measurable before decoherence has occurred, and thus this effect must be accounted for in order to accurately estimate
\gamma
from the measurements. We find from numerical simulations that the modulation profile may be appreciably influenced by imperfect operations, where only a partial initial coherence is created, which we therefore must also take into account.

Appendix C
Non-rigid effects

In the main text, we approximate the ion crystal as a rigid rotor. The leading-order non-rigid effect is centrifugal distortion: the moment of inertia increases as the square of the rotation frequency due to centrifugal force, effectively reducing the rotational constant
\omega_{\rm r}
with increasing angular momentum. For an in-plane confinement strength such that the center-of-mass vibrational frequency is
\omega_{x}
, the fractional change to
\omega_{\rm r}
due to finite rotation frequency
\omega_{\text{rot}}
is given by
2\omega_{\text{rot}}^{2}/\omega_{x}^{2}=2.0\times 10^{-2}
for parameters used in this work. This quantity is smaller than the fractional uncertainty of any measurement presented, so we neglect this correction.

Appendix E
Additional decoherence sources

Two other potentially relevant effects are decoherence of the ions’ electronic state and changes of the rotor’s moment of inertia due to fluctuations of the trap frequency. We rule out both of these as significant contributions.
