The least primary factor of the multiplicative group
Greg Martin and Chau Nguyen

Abstract
. Let S ⁡ ( n ) S(n) denote the least primary factor in the primary decomposition of the multiplicative group M n = ( ℤ / n ​ ℤ ) × M_{n}=(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times} . We give an asymptotic formula, with order of magnitude x / ( log ⁡ x ) 1 / 2 x/(\log x)^{1/2} , for the counting function of those integers n n for which S ⁡ ( n ) ≠ 2 S(n)\neq 2 . We also give an asymptotic formula, for any prime power q q , for the counting function of those integers n n for which S ⁡ ( n ) = q S(n)=q . This group-theoretic problem can be reduced to problems of counting integers with restrictions on their prime factors, allowing it to be addressed by classical techniques of analytic number theory.

1.
Introduction

The
multiplicative group
modulo
n
is the finite abelian group
M_{n}=({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
of units in the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
, or equivalently the group of reduced residue classes modulo
n
under multiplication. Several common arithmetic functions are related to the multiplicative group: its size is the Euler phi-function
\phi(n)
, for instance, and its exponent is the Carmichael lambda-function
\lambda(n)
. Both of these functions have been investigated for their distributional properties (as well as pointwise bounds), which reflects the fact that the multiplicative groups
M_{n}
form a natural family of groups to study, and indeed a richer family of groups than the cyclic (additive) groups
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
themselves.

The structure of these multiplicative groups is best described in terms of one of the two canonical forms for finite abelian groups
G
. One canonical form is the invariant factor decomposition
G\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{d_{k}}
where each
d_{j}
divides
d_{j+1}
; indeed in this form we have
d_{k}=\lambda(n)
exactly. It turns out that the length
k
of the invariant factor decomposition is essentially
\omega(n)
, the number of distinct prime factors of
n
, whose distribution also has a robust theory. Chang and the first author
[
1
]
used a refinement of the Selberg–Delange method to show that
d_{1}=2
for almost all integers
n
and found an asymptotic formula for the number of
n\leq x
for which
d_{1}=d
for any fixed integer
d
.

In this paper we focus on the other canonical form for finite abelian groups, namely the
primary decomposition

G\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{q_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{q_{t}}
where each
q_{j}
is a prime power; this decomposition (which is also called the elementary factor decomposition) is uniquely determined by
G
up to the order of the primary factors
q_{j}
. The result mentioned above implies that the least primary factor of
M_{n}
equals
2
for almost all integers
n
(since every primary factor of an invariant factor of
G
is also a primary factor of
G
itself). More specifically, we can prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

The number of
n\leq x
for which
2
is not the least primary factor of
M_{n}
is

C_{3}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}+O\biggl(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2/3}}\biggr),

where

(1.1)

C_{3}=\frac{3}{4\sqrt{2}}\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigg)^{{1}/{2}}\approx 0.490694.

The main result of this paper is to use the same refinement of the Selberg–Delange method to establish an asymptotic formula for the number of integers
n\leq x
for which the least primary factor of
M_{n}
equals any fixed prime power
q
. We introduce the following notation:

Definition 1.2
.

Let

denote the smallest primary factor of

, that is, the least prime power appearing in the primary decomposition of

. (Note that

and

2.
Counting integers
n
with least primary factor not equal to
2

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
; the argument also serves as a foundation for the more involved proof of Theorem
1.3
in the next section.

We rely in an essential way on the fact that the primary decomposition of a direct product
G=\prod_{j=1}^{k}G_{k}
of finite abelian groups is simply the concatenation of the primary decompositions of the individual groups
G_{j}
. In particular, the least primary factor of
G
is equal to the minimum of the least primary factors of the
G_{j}
. This observation gives a particularly simple criterion for detecting the smallest possible primary factor
2
. Recall that
S(n)
denotes the smallest primary factor of the multiplicative group
M_{n}
. We also write
p^{r}\|n
to mean
p^{r}\mid n
but
p^{r+1}\nmid n
.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have
S(n)\neq 2
if and only if both
4\nmid n
and all odd primes dividing
n
are congruent to
1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}
.

Proof.

If the prime-power factorization of
n
is
n=\prod_{p^{r}\mid\mid n}p^{r}
, the Chinese remainder theorem tells us that
M_{n}\cong\prod_{p^{r}\|n}M_{p^{r}}
; in particular,
S(n)=2
if and only if
S(p^{r})=2
for some prime power
p^{r}\|n
.

When
p
is odd, classical results on primitive roots imply that
M_{p^{r}}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{\phi(p^{r})}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p^{r-1}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{p-1}
; the least primary factor of this direct sum is the least primary factor of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p-1}
, which is the smallest prime power dividing
p-1
. In particular, its least primary factor equals
2
precisely when
p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}
.

On the other hand,
M_{2}
is the trivial group, while
M_{2^{r}}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}_{2^{r-2}}
when
r\geq 2
. In particular, the least primary factor of
M_{2^{r}}
equals
2
as soon as
r\geq 2
.
∎

As we carry out the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we introduce notation that will foreshadow more general notation in the next section.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
be the set of all positive integers
n
not exceeding
x
such that
S(n)\geq 3
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(x)
be the set of all odd integers in
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
.

Note that
\{n\leq x\colon n\notin{\mathcal{E}}_{2}(x)\}=\{n\leq x\colon S(n)\neq 2\}=\{n\leq x\colon S(n)\geq 3\}={\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
, and so Theorem
1.1
is a claim about an asymptotic formula for
\#{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
, which we will deduce from an asymptotic formula for
\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(x)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have
\#{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)=\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(x)+\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(\frac{x}{2})
.

Proof.

The odd elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
are counted by
\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(x)
by definition. On the other hand, every even
n\in{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
is of the form
n=2m
where
m\leq\frac{x}{2}
is odd, since
4\nmid n
by Lemma
2.1
; therefore the even elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
are counted by
\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(\frac{x}{2})
.
∎

Note that
{\mathcal{A}}_{3}^{\prime}(x)=\{n\leq x\colon p\mid n\implies p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}\}
by Lemma
2.1
. Counting integers with this sort of restriction on their prime factors is a classical problem in analytic number theory (and indeed this particular case is almost the same as the seminal study by Landau
[
2
]
of the counting function of sums of two squares).

Lemma 2.4
.

We have
\displaystyle\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(x)=\frac{g_{4}x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}+O\bigg(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/4}}\bigg)
, where

g_{4}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigg)^{{1}/{2}}\approx 0.32713.

Proof.

The paper of Chang and first author
[
1
, Proposition 4.1]
contains the asymptotic formula

\#\{n\leq x\colon p\mid n\implies p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\}=\frac{g_{q}x}{(\log x)^{1-{1}/{\phi(q)}}}+O\bigg(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{{5}/{4}-{1}/{\phi(q)}}}\bigg)

for any even positive integer
q\geq 3
, where

(2.1)

g_{q}=\frac{1}{\Gamma({1}/{\phi(q)})}\bigg(\frac{\phi(q)}{q}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}L(1,\chi)}\bigg)^{{1}/{\phi(q)}}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\nmid q\\
p\not\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$q$)}}\end{subarray}}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{\mathop{\rm ord}_{q}(p)}}\bigg)^{{1}/{\mathop{\rm ord}_{q}(p)}}.

Here
\mathop{\rm ord}_{q}(a)
is the order of
a
in the multiplicative group
M_{q}
, and
\Gamma(s)
and
L(s,\chi)
are the Euler Gamma function and Dirichlet
L
-functions, respectively. The simplification of the constant
g_{4}
was carried out after Theorem 1.2 of that paper:

\displaystyle g_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma({1}/{2})}\bigg(\frac{2}{4}L(1,\chi_{-4})\bigg)^{{1}/{2}}{\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigg)^{{1}/{2}}}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Big(\frac{\pi}{8}\Big)^{{1}/{2}}\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\bigg)^{{1}/{2}}

as claimed.
∎

With these results in hand, the proof of Theorem
1.1
is a simple calculation:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
,

\displaystyle\#{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(x)+\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{3}(\tfrac{x}{2})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{g_{4}x}{(\log{x})^{1/2}}+O\bigg(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/4}}\bigg)\bigg)+\bigg(\frac{g_{4}x/2}{(\log{x/2})^{1/2}}+O\bigg(\frac{x/2}{(\log x/2)^{3/4}}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=g_{4}x\bigg(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{2(\log({x}/{2}))^{{1}/{2}}}\bigg)+O\bigg(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{{3}/{4}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=g_{4}x\bigg(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{2(\log x)^{{1}/{2}}}\bigg\{1+O\bigg(\frac{1}{\log x}\bigg)\bigg\}\bigg)+O\bigg(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{{3}/{4}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=g_{4}x\bigg(\frac{3/2}{(\log x)^{1/2}}\bigg)+O\bigg(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{{3}/{4}}}\bigg),

which is the desired asymptotic formula since
C_{3}=\frac{3}{2}g_{4}
.
∎

3.
Counting integers
n
with least primary factor equal to
q

Recall that
{\mathcal{E}}_{q}(x)
from Definition
1.2
is comprised of integers
m
such that the smallest primary factor
S(m)
is exactly equal to
q
, while
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)
from Definition
2.2
is comprised of integers
m
such that
S(m)
is at least
q
.
In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
, the asymptotic formula for
\#{\mathcal{E}}_{q}(x)
, using an elaboration on the method for finding an asymptotic formula for
\#{\mathcal{A}}_{3}(x)
in the previous section.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a prime power
q
, let
q^{+}
denote the smallest prime power that is greater than
q
.
Note that

\displaystyle\#{\mathcal{E}}_{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\{m\leq x\colon S(m)=q\}

(3.1)

\displaystyle=\{m\leq x\colon S(m)\geq q\}-\{m\leq x\colon S(m)\geq q^{+}\}=\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)-\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q^{+}}(x).

Thus to obtain an asymptotic formula for
\#{\mathcal{E}}_{q}(x)
for all prime powers
q
, it suffices to obtain an asymptotic formula for
\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)
for all prime powers
q
. This observation is helpful because, as it turns out, the property
S(m)\geq q
is easier to characterize than the property
S(m)=q
(see Proposition
3.6
below).

Definition 3.2
.

Given a prime power
q
and a prime
p<q
, let
m(p,q)
denote the largest positive integer such that
p^{m(p,q)}<q
, so that
m(p,q)=\lceil(\log q)/\log p\rceil-1
.

Definition 3.3
.

For any prime
p
, let
v_{p}(n)
denote the multiplicity with which
p
divides
n
. For example,
v_{2}(40)=3
and
v_{3}(21)=1
and
v_{3}(5)=0
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix primes
\ell
and
p
and a prime power
q\geq 3
. There exists
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\ell^{s}<q
and
\ell^{s}\|(p-1)
if and only if
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell$)}}
and
p\not\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
.

Proof.

Choose
s=v_{\ell}(p-1)
, the only possibility for satisfying
\ell^{s}\|(p-1)
. If
s=0
then the equivalence holds because both
s\notin{\mathbb{N}}
and
p\not\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell$)}}
. If
s>m(\ell,q)
then the equivalence holds as well because both
\ell^{s}\geq q
and
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
. Otherwise
1\leq s\leq m(\ell,q)
, and the equivalence holds because all statements are true in this case.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Fix an odd prime power
p^{k}
and a prime power
q\geq 3
. We have
S(p^{k})\geq q
if and only if for every prime
\ell<q
, either
p\not\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell$)}}
or
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, it suffices to show that
S(p^{k})<q
if and only if there exists a prime
\ell
and an integer
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\ell^{s}<q
and
\ell^{s}\|(p-1)
. But this is clear because
S(p^{k})
is the smallest prime power
\ell^{j}
such that
\ell^{j}\|(p-1)
, since
M_{p^{k}}
is cyclic of order
p^{k-1}(p-1)
. (We remark that the exponent
k
plays no role, as the second half of the claimed equivalence indicates.)
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
q\geq 3
be a prime power and
m\geq 3
an integer. We have
S(m)\geq q
if and only if:

(a)

4\nmid m
;

(b)

if
p\leq q
is an odd prime, then
p\nmid m
; and

(c)

if
\ell<q
is a prime and
p\mid m
is an odd prime, then either
p\not\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell$)}}
or
p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
.

(Technically part (b) is a special case of part (c), since there is always some prime divisor
\ell
of
p-1
and this
\ell
will automatically be less than
q
if
p\leq q
. But it will be helpful to record part (b) explicitly.)

Proof.

Since
M_{m}\cong\prod_{p^{k}\|m}M_{p^{k}}
by the Chinese remainder theorem, we see that
S(m)
is the minimum value of
S(p^{k})
over all
p^{k}\|m
; in particular,
S(m)\geq q
if and only if
S(p^{k})\geq q
for all
p^{k}\|m
. We check the implications of this inequality separately for
p=2
, for
3\leq p\leq q
, and for
p>q
.

(a)

If
2^{k}\|m
with
k\geq 2
, then
M(2^{k})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2^{k-2}}
and thus
S(2^{k})=2<q
. Otherwise
p=2
does not contribute to
M(m)
.

(b)

If
p\mid m
for some
3\leq p\leq q
, then
S(p)\leq p-1<q
, which makes
S(m)\geq q
impossible.

(c)

This last item follows directly from Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Recall that

was defined in Definition

Definition 3.10

Let

Proposition 3.11
.

For any prime power
q\geq 3
,

\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(x)=\frac{x}{{(\log x)}^{1-\beta_{q}}}\biggl(\frac{G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)}{\Gamma(\beta_{q})}+O((\log x)^{-{1}/{4}})\biggr).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.6
, we have
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(x)=\{n\leq x\colon n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}\}
, where
{\mathcal{B}}_{q}
was defined in Definition
3.8
. The proposition then follows directly from the asymptotic formula

\#\{n\leq x\colon n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{B}}\}=\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\beta}}\biggl(\frac{G_{\mathcal{B}}(1)}{\Gamma(\beta)}+O\bigl((\log x)^{-{1}/{4}}\bigr)\biggr)

derived by Chang and the second author
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
(in particular, that derivation verifies the claim that
G_{\mathcal{B}}(s)
has a removable singularity at
s=1
).
∎

Lemma
2.3
is straightforward to generalize:

Lemma 3.12
.

We have
\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)=\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(x)+\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(\frac{x}{2})
.

Proof.

The odd elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)
are counted by
\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(x)
by definition. On the other hand, every even
n\in{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)
is of the form
n=2m
where
m\leq\frac{x}{2}
is odd, since
4\nmid n
by Proposition
3.6
; therefore the even elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)
are counted by
\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(\frac{x}{2})
.
∎

Definition 3.13
.

Using the notation
{\mathcal{B}}_{q}
and
\beta_{q}
from Definition
3.8
and the function
G
from Definition
3.10
, define

C_{q}=\frac{3G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)}{2\Gamma(\beta_{q})}=\frac{3}{2\Gamma(\beta_{q})}\lim_{s\to 1}\biggl(\zeta(s)^{-\beta_{q}}\prod_{p\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}\biggr),

where
\Gamma
is the Euler Gamma-function.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By equation (
3.1
) and Lemma
3.12
,

\displaystyle\#{\mathcal{E}}_{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q}(x)-\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q^{+}}(x)

\displaystyle=\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(x)+\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q}(\tfrac{x}{2})+O\bigl(\#{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{q^{+}}(x)+\#{\mathcal{A}}_{q^{+}}(\tfrac{x}{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\beta_{q}}}\bigg(\frac{G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)}{\Gamma(\beta_{q})}+O({(\log x)}^{-{1}/{4}})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{{x}/{2}}{\log({x}/{2})^{1-\beta_{q}}}\bigg(\frac{G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)}{\Gamma(\beta_{q})}+O((\log{x}/{2})^{-{1}/{4}})\bigg)+O\biggl(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\beta_{q^{+}}}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\frac{G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)}{\Gamma(\beta_{q})}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\beta_{q}}}+O\biggl(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-\beta_{q^{+}}}}\biggr),

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
by virtue of Definition
3.13
.
∎

4.
Plan for evaluating the leading constants

The remainder of this paper analyzes the constant
C_{q}
from Definition
3.13
for
q\in\{3,4,5\}
, establishing Theorem
1.4
and illustrating that in principle
C_{q}
can be given a more concrete form for any prime power
q
. The difficult component of
C_{q}
is the constant
G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)
, which we now outline a method for evaluating.

Recall Definition
3.8
for the quantities
Q_{q}
and
B_{q}
and the set
{\mathcal{B}}_{q}
, as well as Definition
3.10
for the functions
F_{\mathcal{B}}
and
G_{\mathcal{B}}
. Chang and the first author showed
[
1
, equation (3.3)]
that

(4.1)

G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s)^{\phi(Q_{q})}=A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s){\prod_{p\mid Q_{q}}(1-p^{-s})^{B_{q}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$Q_{q}$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}L(s,\chi)^{{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}\overline{\chi}(b)}}}.

In the two products we can simply plug in
s=1
and evaluate, using equations (
7.4
) and (
7.5
) below for the
L(1,\chi)
terms. (It turns out that the exponent of
L(s,\chi)
is an integer—see Lemma
6.3
below—and in particular real-valued. Therefore the right-hand side is a positive real number at
s=1
, as we can see after pairing the terms
\chi
and
\overline{\chi}
in the product; thus taking the
\phi(Q_{q})
th root to solve for
G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)
is unproblematic.) As for the
A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s)
factor, Chang and the first author further showed
[
1
, equation (3.1)]
that

(4.2)

A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s)=F_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s)^{\phi(Q_{q})}\prod_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$Q_{q}$)}}}L(s,\chi)^{-\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}\overline{\chi}(b)}

To evaluate
A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)
, we will rewrite the right-hand side in terms of convergent Euler products over primes in various residue classes, such as the one arising in equation (
5.1
) whose value at
s=1
already appeared in equation (
2.2
).

5.
Calculating the leading constant
C_{3}

Implementing the plan from the previous section when
q=3
is essentially reproducing the calculation summarized in the proof of Lemma
2.4
. Carrying out this computation explicitly will be helpful practice before addressing the more complicated cases
q=4
and
q=5
in later sections.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
for
q=3
.

We have
Q_{3}=\prod_{\ell<3}\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}=2^{1+1}=4
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{3}=\{1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}\}
. Equation (
4.2
) becomes

\displaystyle A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(s)

\displaystyle=F_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(s)^{\phi(Q_{3})}\prod_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}L(s,\chi)^{-\overline{\chi}(1)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-s})^{-2}\cdot L(s,\chi_{0})^{-1}L(s,\chi_{-4})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-s})^{-2}\cdot\prod_{p\neq 2}(1-p^{-s})(1-\chi_{-4}(p)p^{-s}),

where
\chi_{0}
and
\chi_{-4}
are the principal and nonprincipal characters modulo
4
, respectively. Splitting the odd primes into their residue classes modulo
4
, this formula becomes

\displaystyle A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-s})^{-2}(1-p^{-s})(1-p^{-s})\cdot\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-s})(1+p^{-s})

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-2s}).

Notice that this product converges whenever
\Re s>\frac{1}{2}
; in particular we can evaluate
A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(1)
by simply plugging in
s=1
to the right-hand side.

Now equation (
4.1
) becomes

\displaystyle G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(s)^{2}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-2s})\cdot(1-2^{-s})^{1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}L(s,\chi)^{\overline{\chi}(1)}

and therefore, using equation (
5.1
),

\displaystyle G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(1)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\tfrac{1}{2}L(1,\chi_{-4})\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-2})\biggr)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-2})^{1/2}.

Finally, since
\beta_{3}=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta_{3^{+}}=\beta_{4}=\frac{1}{3}
, Theorem
1.3
becomes

\#{\mathcal{E}}_{3}(x)=\frac{C_{3}x}{(\log x)^{1/2}}+O\biggl(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2/3}}\biggr),

where by Definition
3.13
,

(5.2)

C_{3}=\frac{3}{2\Gamma(1/2)}G_{{\mathcal{B}}_{3}}(1)=\frac{3}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\prod_{p\equiv 3{\text{\rm\ (mod~$4$)}}}(1-p^{-2})^{1/2}

which is consistent with equation (
1.1
).
∎

6.
Evaluating
{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}}\chi(b)}

In order to evaluate the exponents in equation (
4.2
), we need to determine
{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}\overline{\chi}(b)}
in general. We accomplish this task in Lemma
6.3
below after introducing some necessary notation.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
q\geq 3
be a prime power and
\ell<q
be a prime. With
m(\ell,q)
as in Definition
3.2
:

•

Define
{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}=M_{\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}}
to be the full multiplicative group modulo
\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}
.

•

Define
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}=\{b\in M_{\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}}\colon b\equiv 1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell$)}}\}
, which is also the subgroup of
(\ell-1)
st powers in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}
.

•

Let
{\mathcal{I}}_{\ell,q}=\{1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}\}
be the singleton set containing only one residue class.

•

Set
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}=({\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}\setminus{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q})\cup{\mathcal{I}}_{\ell,q}
, which is the set of residue classes described in Remark
3.7
.

Remark 6.2
.

Considering the large multiplicative group
M_{Q_{q}}
where
Q_{q}
is from Definition
3.8
, we see that
M_{Q_{q}}\cong\prod_{\ell<q}{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}
by the Chinese remainder theorem; under this isomorphism, the collection
{\mathcal{B}}_{q}
of residue classes in
M_{Q_{q}}
from Definition
3.8
can be written exactly as
{\mathcal{B}}_{q}=\prod_{\ell<q}{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}
.

Moreover, every Dirichlet character
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$Q_{q}$)}}
can be uniquely written as the product of Dirichlet characters modulo
\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}
as
\ell
ranges over the primes less than
q
; more precisely, there exist characters
\{\chi_{\ell}\colon\ell<q\}
such that
\chi(b)=\prod_{\ell<q}\chi_{\ell}(b_{\ell})
, where the
b_{\ell}
are the components of the image of
b\in M_{Q_{q}}
under the isomorphism from
M_{Q_{q}}\cong\prod_{\ell<q}{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}
. It follows, for instance, that

(6.1)

\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}\chi(b)=\prod_{\ell<q}\sum_{b_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}}\chi_{\ell}(b_{\ell}).

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
q\geq 3
be a prime power and let
\ell<q
be a prime. For any Dirichlet character
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
,

\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)=\begin{cases}\ell^{m(\ell,q)}(\ell-1),&\text{if }\chi=\chi_{0},\\
0,&\text{if }\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{cases}\Biggr\}-\begin{cases}\ell^{m(\ell,q)},&\text{if }\chi^{\ell-1}=\chi_{0},\\
0,&\text{if }\chi^{\ell-1}\neq\chi_{0}\end{cases}\Biggr\}+1.

In particular, if
\ell=2
then
\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)=1
for any
\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
.

Proof.

We concentrate on the first assertion since the second one follows directly from it (or directly from the fact that
{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}=\{1{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}\}
when
\ell=2
).
By definition,

\displaystyle{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}

\displaystyle={\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}-{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}+{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\ell^{m(\ell,q)}(\ell-1),&\text{if }\chi=\chi_{0},\\
0,&\text{if }\chi\neq\chi_{0},\end{cases}\Biggr\}-{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}+1

by orthogonality. Comparing this expression to the desired formula, it suffices to prove that

{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}=\begin{cases}\ell^{m(\ell,q)},&\text{if }\chi^{\ell-1}=\chi_{0},\\
0,&\text{if }\chi^{\ell-1}\neq\chi_{0}.\end{cases}

But this is an immediate consequence of the fact that the subgroup
\{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)}$)}}\colon\chi^{\ell-1}=\chi_{0}\}
is the dual group to the subgroup
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}
which is the set of
(\ell-1)
st powers modulo
m(\ell,q)
. More concretely, if
\chi^{\ell-1}=\chi_{0}
, then for every
b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}
there exists
a\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}
such that
a^{\ell-1}\equiv b{\text{\rm\ (mod~$\ell^{m(\ell,q)+1}$)}}
, and therefore
\chi(b)=\chi(a^{\ell-1})=\chi^{\ell-1}(a)=\chi_{0}(a)=1
for each of the
\ell^{m(\ell,q)}
elements of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}
. On the other hand, if
\chi^{\ell-1}\neq\chi_{0}
, then we can choose
c\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\ell,q}
such that
\chi^{\ell-1}(c)\neq 1
; then, since
c^{\ell-1}
is in the subgroup
{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}
,

{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}={\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(c^{\ell-1}b)}=\chi^{\ell-1}(c){\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}

which forces
{\sum_{b\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell,q}}\chi(b)}=0
.
∎

By Definition
3.10
and Remark
6.2
, we can write equation (
4.2
) as

\displaystyle A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1-p^{-s})^{-\phi(Q_{q})}\cdot\prod_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$Q_{q}$)}}}\prod_{p}(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})^{\prod_{\ell<q}\sum_{b_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}}\chi_{\ell}(b_{\ell})}

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\biggl(\begin{cases}(1-p^{-s})^{-\phi(Q_{q})},&\text{if }p\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q},\\
1,&\text{if }p\notin B_{q}\end{cases}\Biggr\}\prod_{\chi{\text{\rm\ (mod~$Q_{q}$)}}}(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})^{\prod_{\ell<q}\sum_{b_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\ell,q}}\chi_{\ell}(b_{\ell})}\biggr).

The factor corresponding to a prime
p
is some rational function of
p^{-s}
that depends only upon the residue class of
p{\text{\rm\ (mod~$Q_{q}$)}}
. When we compute these
\phi(Q_{q})
rational functions, the following lemma implies that each one is actually a rational function of
p^{-ks}
for some integer
k\geq 2
(depending on the residue class). Consequently, while the above formulas are valid for
\Re s>1
, the entire expression for
A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(s)
will end up converging for
\Re s>\frac{1}{2}
at least; in particular, we can evaluate
A_{{\mathcal{B}}_{q}}(1)
by simply plugging
s=1
into the resulting expression.

Lemma 6.4
.

Given
Q\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
H
be any subgroup of the group of Dirichlet characters modulo
Q
. If
p\nmid Q
and the cardinality of
\{\chi(p)\colon\chi\in H\}
equals
k
, then

\prod_{\chi\in H}(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})=(1-p^{-ks})^{(\#H)/k}.

Proof.

Since the image
\{\chi(p)\colon\chi\in H\}
is a subgroup of
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
, it must equal the cyclic group of
k
th roots of unity. It follows easily that the image contains all
k
th roots of unity each with equal multiplicity
(\#H)/k
. Therefore

\prod_{\chi\in H}(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-e^{2\pi ij/k}p^{-s})^{(\#H)/k}=(1-p^{-ks})^{(\#H)/k}

by the factorization of
1-z^{k}
over the complex numbers.
∎

We now have all the tools we need to calculate the constant

from Theorem

We briefly describe how we calculated the numerical approximation
C_{4}\approx 0.4200344
. Standard computational software can calculate
{3^{2/3}}/{2\Gamma(1/3)}\approx 0.3882291057
to any desired accuracy. Furthermore, there are classical results that allow us to write

as a finite sum of easily calculated quantities. For example,
[

tells us that if

and

is a primitive character modulo

, then

(7.4)

L(1,\chi)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{i\pi\tau(\chi)}{q^{2}}\sum_{k\in M_{q}}\overline{\chi}(k)k,&\text{if }\chi\text{ is odd,}\\
\displaystyle-\frac{\tau(\chi)}{q}\sum_{k\in M_{q}}\overline{\chi}(k)\log\sin\frac{k\pi}{q},&\text{if }\chi\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

where
\tau(\chi)=\sum_{a\in M_{q}}\chi(a)e^{2\pi ia/q}
is the Gauss sum. Furthermore, if
\chi
is a Dirichlet character

\displaystyle\biggl|\log\prod_{p>10^{7}}(1-p^{-2})^{-1/2}\bigg|\leq\sum_{p>10^{7}}\frac{1}{2p^{2}}
