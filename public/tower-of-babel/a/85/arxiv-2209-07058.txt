Introduction

The study of the way in which structure can be preserved using random sampling is of central importance in modern mathematics. Various notions of structure have been considered over the years, resulting in numerous applications in pure mathematics, statistics and data science.

Let
\mu
be a centred probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
X
be distributed according to
\mu
.
We focus on the way in which a typical sample, consisting of
m
independent copies of
X
, inherits features of that measure.
Informally put, we explore the following:

Question 1.1
.

Given
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
selected independently according to
\mu
, how much “information” on
\mu
can be extracted (with high probability) from the set
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\}
?

Since the empirical measure
\mu_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}}
converges weakly to
\mu
almost surely, the main interest in Question
1.1
is of a quantitative nature.

To put this (still rather vague) question in some perspective, let us start with a natural notion that will prove to be instructive, but at the same time rather useless. Consider two independent samples
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
and
(X_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{m}
, both selected according to
\mu^{\otimes m}
. Intuitively, if for a typical sample
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
, the set
\{X_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}
inherits much of
\mu
’s structure, then
\{X_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}
and
\{X_{i}^{\prime}:1\leq i\leq m\}
should be “close” to each other. And, because the way the points are ordered is irrelevant, a natural notion of similarity between the two “clouds” of points is

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|X_{\pi(i)}-X_{i}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(1.1)

with the infimum taken over all permutations
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,m\}
.

As it happens, (
1.1
) is simply the
\mathcal{W}_{2}

Wasserstein distance
between the two empirical measures
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}^{\prime}}
. The
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance is defined on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
—the set of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moment—by

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tau,\nu)=\inf_{\Pi}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\,\Pi(dx,dy)\right)^{1/2}.

Here the infimum taken over all
couplings

\Pi
, that is, probability measures whose first marginal is
\tau
and their second marginal is
\nu
.
For detailed surveys on the Wasserstein distance, see, e.g.,
[
14
,
41
]
.

Setting
\mu_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
\mu_{m}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}^{\prime}}
, it is straightforward to verify that (
1.1
) is simply
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu_{m}^{\prime})
. Moreover, a standard convexity argument shows that obtaining high probability estimates on
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu_{m}^{\prime})
and on
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
are equivalent questions, and in what follows we focus on the latter.

While
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
is a natural way of comparing
\mu_{m}
and
\mu
, using the Wasserstein distance has a significant drawback. In the high-dimensional setup,
\mathcal{W}_{2}
is just
too sensitive
: the typical distance between
\mu_{m}
and
\mu
is almost diametric unless
m
is exponential in the dimension
d
.
Indeed, although
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\to 0
almost surely as
m\to\infty
, the pointwise best approximation of
\mu
by
m
points, i.e.,
\inf_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}},\mu)
, typically scales like
m^{-1/d}
even for well-behaved measures—like the gaussian measure or the uniform measure on the unit cube, see
[
13
]
.

The slow decay of
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
is a manifestation of the
curse of dimensionality
phenomenon, rendering that notion of similarity useless for our purposes. It also hints that if one is to overcome the curse of dimensionality, a useful notion of structure preservation should depend only on low-dimensional marginals of
\mu
.

One such notion is based on the behaviour of the
extremal singular values
of the random matrix whose rows are
X_{1},...,X_{m}
.
Assume for the sake of simplicity that the centred measure
\mu
is also isotropic (that is, its covariance is the identity) and consider
S^{d-1}
, the Euclidean unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, viewed as a class of linear functionals
\{\left\langle\theta,\cdot\right\rangle:\theta\in S^{d-1}\}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
. Let
X
be distributed according to
\mu
, set
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
to be independent copies of
X
and put

\Gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},\cdot\right\rangle e_{i}.

The random operator
\Gamma
is an embedding of
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}(\mu)})
into
\ell_{2}^{m}=(\mathbb{R}^{m},\|\cdot\|_{2})
, and it is natural to identify conditions on
X
and
m
under which
\Gamma
is a
1\pm\varepsilon
isomorphism, i.e., that

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left|\|\Gamma\theta\|_{2}^{2}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon.

(1.2)

Clearly, (
1.2
) means that the extremal singular values of
\Gamma
are close to
1
, and the extent to which the random sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
inherits the
L_{2}
structure endowed by
\mu
is measured by (
1.2
).

While (
1.2
) captures significant information on structure preservation, it is still rather crude: it yields very little information on the measure
\mu
—other than exhibiting that it is isotropic. And, if
\mu
is not isotropic, analogs of (
1.2
) allow one to recover
\mu
’s covariance structure (see, for example,
[
22
,
25
,
29
]
for results of that flavour), but nothing beyond that. In comparison, far more accurate information is ‘coded’ in estimates on the ‘worst’ Wasserstein distance of a one-dimensional marginal of
\mu
from its empirical counterpart.
To see why, consider a direction
\theta\in S^{d-1}
, let
\mu^{\theta}(A)=\mu(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\langle x,\theta\right\rangle\in A\})
be the marginal of
\mu
in direction
\theta
and set

F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t)=\mu^{\theta}((-\infty,t])\quad\text{and}\quad F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)=\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t)\geq u\right\}

to be the
distribution function
of
\mu^{\theta}
and its
(right-)inverse
.

Definition 1.2
.

For every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the
max-sliced Wasserstein distance
is defined by

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\mu^{\theta},\nu^{\theta}\right).

One can show that
\mathcal{SW}_{2}
is a metric on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that endows the same topology as
\mathcal{W}_{2}
.
For the proof of that fact and more information on similar notions, see, e.g.,
[
12
,
21
,
24
,
31
,
32
,
34
]
.

An observation that is used frequently in what follows is a closed-form of the Wasserstein distance between one-dimensional measures:

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2},

(1.3)

see, for example,
[
36
]
and Lemma
2.1
.

Using the representation (
1.3
), the following is of central importance:

An upper bound on
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
implies uniform concentration of the coordinate distribution of the vectors
(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
around a well-determined set of values, endowed by
\mu
.

To be more precise, let
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and
for
1\leq i\leq m
set

\lambda^{\theta}_{i}=m\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\,du.

One may show (see Section
2.1
for the proof) that

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda^{\theta}_{i}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu),

(1.4)

where here and throughout this article,
(a_{i}^{\sharp})_{i=1}^{m}
is non-decreasing rearrangement of
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.
Equation (
1.4
) means that if
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
is ‘small’, the random vectors
(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
all inherit—on the same event—the distribution of the corresponding one-dimensional marginals of
\mu
.

Roughly put, our main result is that
under minimal assumptions on
X
and with high probability,
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
is indeed small:

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq c\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/4},

(1.5)

where
c
is a suitable constant—and this estimate is optimal.

In particular, using the above notation, we have that

\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda^{\theta}_{i}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq c\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/4}.

To explain what is meant by “minimal assumptions on
X
”, let us compare (
1.5
) with (
1.2
).
Both estimates offer some form of uniform control on the one-dimensional marginals of
X
, with one (trivially) stronger than the other.
Indeed, set

\displaystyle\rho_{d,m}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{2}-1\right|=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left|\|\Gamma\theta\|_{2}^{2}-1\right|

(1.6)

and recall that by isotropicity
\mathbb{E}\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle^{2}=1
.
In particular,

\rho_{d,m}=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u))^{2}\,du-\int_{0}^{1}(F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u))^{2}\,du\right|;

thus,
\rho_{d,m}
is the maximal difference between the second moments of the true and empirical (inverse) distributions of marginals
\|F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}
and
\|F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}
.
In contrast, following (
1.3
),
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
is the maximal
L_{2}
-distance
\|F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\|_{L_{2}}
, and it is straightforward to verify that if
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle\rho_{d,m}\leq 3\cdot\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)

(1.7)

(see Lemma
2.2
for the proof).

A priori, there is no reason why (
1.7
) should be anything but a crude upper estimate: expecting that
F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}
and
F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}
are close in
L_{2}
solely because their second moments are similar seems unrealistically optimistic.
Surprisingly, our main result shows that (
1.7
) can be reversed to some extent, and
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
can be controlled in terms of
\rho_{d,m}
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X
be centred and isotropic, and assume that
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{q}}\leq L
for some
q\geq 4
.
Then there are absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
and a constant
c_{3}
that depends only on
q
and
L
such that the following holds.

Let
0<\Delta\leq c_{0}
and set
m\geq c_{1}\frac{d}{\Delta}
.
With probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq c_{3}\begin{cases}\rho_{d,m}^{1/2}+\Delta^{1/4}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\rho_{d,m}^{1/2}+\Delta^{1/4}\log\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

On the optimality of Theorem
1.3

The question of the optimality of Theorem
1.3
is explored in Section
3
.
For example, we show that the estimate of
(d/m)^{1/4}
is sharp in general—even if one allows for a significantly weaker probability estimate—of constant probability rather than of
1-\exp(-cd)
.
Moreover, the best estimate that one can hope to get with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\Delta m)
is
\Delta^{1/4}
, even if the random vector
X
is well-behaved, e.g., isotropic and
L
-subgaussian
1
1

1

Recall that if a random vector
X
is isotropic and
L
-subgaussian then
\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq L\sqrt{p}
for every
p\geq 2
and
\theta\in S^{d-1}
.
.
The latter stems from a simple one-dimensional phenomenon: it is straightforward to verify that for
\Delta\leq 1/4
and
m\geq 4
, if
\mu^{\theta}
is symmetric and
\{-1,1\}
-valued, then

\displaystyle\int_{1/4}^{3/4}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\geq c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}

(1.8)

with probability at least
c_{2}\exp(-c_{3}\Delta m)
(see Lemma
3.4
for the proof). Thus, invoking the representation (
1.3
) it is evident that if
X
is uniformly distributed in
\{-1,1\}^{d}
, then with probability at least
c_{2}\exp(-c_{3}\Delta m)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\geq\sqrt{c_{1}}\Delta^{1/4};

(1.9)

and the fact that
X
is subgaussian does not mean that the error estimate in Theorem
1.3
can be better than
\sim\Delta^{1/4}
.

At the same time, once the random vector
X
is ‘regular’ in a certain sense, the estimate in Theorem
1.3
can be improved to
\sqrt{\Delta}
(up to logarithmic factors)—in which case (
1.7
) truly can be reversed—see Theorem
1.6
.
This phenomenon is in line with the results from Bobkov-Ledoux
[
7
]
, which are focused on the behaviour of
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
in the one-dimensional framework.
In particular Bobkov and Ledoux explore conditions under which the error estimate is
m^{-1/2}
(up to logarithmic factors) and show that this can only happen under strong regularity assumptions on
F_{\mu}^{-1}
.
Since the focus of this article is on the effect of high dimensions in the context of
general
random vectors, we chose not pursue a similar path here.
We only consider one family of regular random vectors—isotropic, log-concave random vectors—see Theorem
1.6
.

Why Theorem
1.3
is surprising

Theorem
1.3
implies a significant improvement on the state-of-the-art geometric estimates related to the study of the extremal singular values of the random matrix
\Gamma
.
Sharp upper bounds on
\rho_{d,m}
follow from (crude) structural information on the vectors
\{\Gamma\theta:\theta\in S^{d-1}\}
(see, e.g.,
[
27
,
39
]
), and are based on (necessary) assumptions on the isotropic random vector
X
—namely that
\|X\|_{2}^{2}/d
has a well-behaved tail-decay and that
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{q}}\leq L
for some
q\geq 4
.
At the heart of the arguments in
[
27
,
39
]
is that under those assumptions the following holds on a high probability event:

\bullet

For each vector
w=\sqrt{m}\Gamma\theta=(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
, the contribution of its largest
\Delta m
coordinates to
\|w\|_{2}
is not “too big”.

\bullet

The remaining coordinates “live” within a multiplicative envelope, dictated by the assumed tail-decay.
For example, under an
L_{q}-L_{2}
norm equivalence assumption one has that for every such vector
w
and
k=\Delta m,\dots,m

w^{\ast}_{k}\leq c(L)(m/k)^{1/q},

(1.10)

where
w^{\ast}
denotes the monotone non-increasing rearrangement of
(|w_{i}|)_{i=1}^{m}
.

When
q>4
, this structural information suffices for establishing the optimal bound—that
\rho_{d,m}\leq c\sqrt{d/m}
with high probability.

Although the structural information in (
1.10
) plays an instrumental role in obtaining sharp estimates on
\rho_{d,m}
, it is still significantly weaker than the outcome of Theorem
1.3
.
Indeed, if
X
satisfies a non-asymptotic ‘Bai-Yin’ estimate, i.e.,
\rho_{d,m}\leq c\sqrt{\Delta}
with probability at least
1-\eta
, then it follows from Theorem
1.3
that with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)-\eta
,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq\begin{cases}c_{4}\Delta^{1/4}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
c_{4}\Delta^{1/4}\log\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

Thus, the monotone non-decreasing rearrangement of each
(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle)_{i=1}^{m}
satisfies that

\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda^{\theta}_{i}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\begin{cases}c_{5}\Delta^{1/4}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
c_{5}\Delta^{1/4}\log\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)&\text{if }q=4,\end{cases}

which is far more accurate than the existence of a one-sided multiplicative envelope function for the non-increasing rearrangement of vectors
(|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle|)_{i=1}^{m}
as in (
1.10
).

Remark 1.4
.

When
q=4
, the state-of-the-art estimate on
\rho_{d,m}
due to Tikhomirov
[
39
]
has an additional multiplicative logarithmic factor in
m/d
. And in a similar manner, the estimate in Theorem
1.3
when
q=4
also has an additional factor of
\log\frac{1}{\Delta}
when
\Delta\geq d/m
.
The Bai-Yin asymptotics
[
3
]
imply that when
q=4
the logarithmic factor in
\rho_{d,m}
should disappear when
d,m\to\infty
while keeping the ratio
d/m
fixed.
It is reasonable to expect that the same is true in regard to Theorem
1.3
and that the
\log\frac{1}{\Delta}
factor is superfluous.

Remark 1.5
.

One scenario of particular interest is when the random vector
X
is rotation invariant. In such a situation, an upper estimate on
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
combined with (
1.4
) implies that
\Gamma S^{d-1}
inherits
\mu
’s invariance, in the sense that

\sup_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\left\langle X_{i},\theta^{\prime}\right\rangle^{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq 2\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu).

In other words, after replacing vectors in
\Gamma S^{d-1}
by their monotone rearrangement, the Euclidean diameter of the resulting set is bounded by
2\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
.
In particular, on the event in which (
1.5
) holds,

\sup_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\left\langle X_{i},\theta^{\prime}\right\rangle^{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq 2c\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/4}.

(1.11)

The estimate in (
1.11
) offers a way of proving that certain random functional are almost constant on the sphere and plays an instrumental role in the construction of a non-gaussian Dvoretzky-Milman embedding in
[
5
]
.

A Warm up exercise: Theorem
1.3
in the log-concave case

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is rather involved.
It is useful to present some of the ideas used in the argument in a simpler context—when
X
is a centred, isotropic, log-concave random vector

2

Recall that a random vector is log-concave if it has a density that is a log-concave function.
.
As it happens, for such random vectors the estimate in Theorem

to

Theorem 1.6
.

There are absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
and

for which the following holds.
Let
X
be a centred, isotropic and log-concave random vector in

.
Let

and set

Then with probability at least

If

is, in addition,

depend on

, and with probability at least

,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq c_{3}\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{3/2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right).

Remark 1.7
.

In the setting of Theorem

, if
\Delta=c_{1}d/m
then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c^{\prime}_{2}\sqrt{d}\log^{2}(\frac{m}{d}))
,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq c^{\prime}_{3}\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}\log^{2}\left(\frac{m}{d}\right).

(1.12)

We show in Section

, then with probability at least

for an absolute constant

Below we sketch the main ideas used in the proof of Theorem

. The complete proof can be found in Section

1.2
Theorem
1.6
—highlights of the argument

As was noted previously,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}.

Set
\delta=\kappa\Delta\log^{2}(\frac{e}{\Delta})
for a well-chosen constant
\kappa\geq 1
to be specified in what follows, let
U=[0,\delta)\cup(1-\delta,1]
, and observe that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{1}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\\
&\leq\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du+2\int_{U}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du+2\int_{U}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\\
&=(1)+(2)+(3).\end{split}

(1.13)

Intuitively, (
1.13
) is sharp when
\Delta
is small, as it is unrealistic to expect cancellations between
F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)
and
F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)
when
u\in U
: that range corresponds to the “
\delta
-outliers” of
\mu^{\theta}
and the
\delta m
extremal values of its empirical counterpart, respectively.

From here on the argument relies heavily on
\mu
’s log-concavity—and hence on the log-concavity of each
\mu^{\theta}
.

Consider first the effect of the outliers, i.e.,
(2)
and
(3)
in Equation (
1.13
).
Clearly,

(3)\leq c_{0}(\kappa)\Delta\log^{4}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)

(1.14)

because marginals of a log-concave measure exhibit a sub-exponential tail-decay: setting

\gamma(u)=\min\{u,1-u\}\quad\text{for }u\in[0,1],

it follows from Borell’s Lemma (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
) that for
u\in(0,1)
,

\left|F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right|\leq c_{1}\log\left(\frac{1}{\gamma(u)}\right).

Next, it is standard to show that
(2)
is equivalent to
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{\delta m}(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\ast})^{2}
, assuming without loss of generality that
\delta m
is an integer. Moreover, the behaviour of

\displaystyle H_{s,m}=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\max_{|I|=s}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{s}(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\ast})^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(1.15)

has been studied extensively over the years in rather general situations (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
27
,
39
]
).
In particular, when
X
is isotropic and log-concave, the following estimate on (
1.15
) was established in
[
1
]
and also in
[
38
]
.

Theorem 1.8
.

There are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
\kappa
such that the following holds.
Let
m\geq 2d
and set
\Delta\geq d/m
.
Then with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\sqrt{\Delta m}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\Delta}))
,

H_{\delta m,m}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right).

While the estimates on
(2)
and
(3)
are either well-understood or trivial, the key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.6
is the estimate on
(1)
:
cancellations
that occur in the interval
[0,1]\backslash U
.
Those cancellations are due to a significant generalization of the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality that is
scale sensitive
and
holds uniformly in
\theta
. More accurately, we show that
with high probability, uniformly in
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying that
F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t)\in[\Delta,1-\Delta]
,

\displaystyle\left|F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(t)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t)\right|\leq\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t))}\cdot\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t))}\right);

(1.16)

in particular, for
u\in[\delta,1-\delta]
,

\displaystyle F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\in\left[F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(u-2\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(u)}\log\left(\tfrac{e}{\gamma(u)}\right)\right),F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(u+2\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(u)}\log\left(\tfrac{e}{\gamma(u)}\right)\right)\right].

(1.17)

The proofs of (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) can be found in Section
2.2
.

Once (
1.17
) is established, one may invoke the fact that a log-concave measure with variance 1 satisfies Cheeger’s isoperimetric inequality with an absolute constant
[
6
,
17
]
:
there is an absolute constant
h>0
such that for any
u\in(0,1)
and any
\theta\in S^{d-1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\leq\frac{1}{h\gamma(u)};

the formulation used here can be found in
[
8
]
.

By a first order Taylor expansion and in the high probability event on which (
1.17
) holds,

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(u)}\log(\frac{e}{\gamma(u)})}{h\gamma(u)}\right)^{2}\,du\sim\Delta\log^{3}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right).

Combining all these estimates it follows that

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\leq C\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right),

as claimed.
∎

Let us stress that this argument relies on log-concavity in a crucial way and that the answer in the general case has to follow a completely different path.
Most notably, in the log-concave case
F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}
is differentiable with a well-behaved derivative, but for a general measure
F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}
need not even be continuous.
In particular, the pointwise control on
|F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}|
one may use in the log-concave case is simply false when it comes to an arbitrary measure. Cancellations in the integral on
[\delta,1-\delta]
happen to be of a “global” nature, leading to a weaker bound, that nevertheless is optimal in the context of Theorem
1.3
.

2
Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.6
— Proofs

We start with a word about
notation
.

\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
is the standard inner product—though in what follows we do not specify the (finite) dimension of the underlying space.
Throughout,
c,c_{0},c_{1},C,C_{0},C_{1},\dots
are absolute constants whose values may change from line to line.
If a constant
c
depends on a parameter

, we write
c=c(a)
, and if
cA\leq B\leq CA
for absolute constants
c
and

, that is denoted by
A\sim B
.

2.1
Preliminary estimates

In what follows,
X
is a centred, isotropic random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies
L_{q}-L_{2}

norm equivalence
with constant
L
for some
q\geq 4
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{q}}\leq L\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}=L\quad\text{ for every }\theta\in S^{d-1}.

(2.1)

Let
\mu
be the probability measure endowed by
X
, and for
\theta\in S^{d-1}
,
\mu^{\theta}
is the marginal endowed by
\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle
.
Thus, for
t>0

\mu^{\theta}((-t,t)^{c})=\mathbb{P}(|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle|\geq t)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle|^{q}}{t^{q}}\leq\frac{L^{q}}{t^{q}}

and it follows that for every
u\in(0,1)

\left|F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right|\leq\frac{L}{\gamma(u)^{1/q}}.

(2.2)

where we recall that
\gamma(u)=\min\{u,1-u\}
.

Let us turn to several simple observations that play an instrumental role in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
We begin with the useful characterization of the Wasserstein distance between measures on the real line mentioned previously.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\nu,\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu,\tau)=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left(F_{\nu}^{-1}(u)-F_{\tau}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}.

(2.3)

The proof of Lemma
2.1
can be found, for example, in
[
36
, Theorem 2]
.
Let us sketch the simple argument for the upper estimate.
If
|\cdot|
is the uniform probability measure on
(0,1)
and

\Pi(A)=|\{u\in(0,1):(F_{\nu}^{-1}(u),F_{\tau}^{-1}(u))\in A\}|,

then
\Pi
is a coupling between
\nu
and
\tau
.
The
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance is the infimum over all such couplings and therefore,

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\tau)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}(x-y)^{2}\,\Pi(dx,dy)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(F_{\nu}^{-1}(u)-F_{\tau}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du.

∎

The next observation is equally straightforward: the (trivial) connection between
\mathcal{SW}_{2}
and
\rho_{d,m}=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}|\|\Gamma\theta\|_{2}^{2}-1|
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\mathcal{SW}_{2}\left(\mu_{m},\mu\right)\leq 1
then
\rho_{d,m}\leq 3\mathcal{SW}_{2}\left(\mu_{m},\mu\right)
.

Proof.

By the isotropicity of
X
, for
\theta\in S^{d-1}
it is evident that

\displaystyle\|\Gamma\theta\|_{2}^{2}-1

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)^{2}\,du-\int_{0}^{1}F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(u)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)-F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(u)\right)\left(F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)+F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(u)\right)\,du.

The claim follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, (
2.3
), the triangle inequality, and the isotropicity of
X
.
∎

The final two observations focus on connections between the Wasserstein distance and monotone ordering.
Let
a^{\sharp}
be the monotone non-decreasing rearrangement of a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and for
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and
1\leq i\leq m
, set
\lambda^{\theta}_{i}=m\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\,du
.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have that

\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda^{\theta}_{i}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu).

Proof.

Note that for every
1\leq i\leq m
and
u\in(\frac{i-1}{m},\frac{i}{m}]
,
F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)=\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}
. Thus, by Jensen’s inequality and invoking Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}-\lambda^{\theta}_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{(i-1)/m}^{i/m}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\mu^{\theta}_{m},\mu^{\theta}\right).\qed

From here on,
\kappa\geq 1
is a (large) absolute constant that will be specified in what follows: assume that
\Delta\leq(10\kappa)^{-2}
, set

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta\log^{2}\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\text{ and}

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=[0,\delta)\cup(1-\delta,1];

and observe that
\delta\leq\frac{1}{4}
.

Invoking (
2.3
) and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\mu^{\theta}_{m},\mu^{\theta}\right)&\leq\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}+\left(\int_{U}F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}\\
&\qquad+\left(\int_{U}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}=(1)+(2)+(3).\end{split}

The term
(3)
can be bounded trivially using the tail-estimate (
2.2
).
In contrast, estimating
(2)
is more subtle, and we defer that to Section
2.5
.
The key estimate is on
(1)
.
It is based on our multi-dimensional, scale sensitive generalization of the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz inequality:
we show in Section
2.2
that on a high probability event,

F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)

can be bounded (pointwise) using the (deterministic) difference

\displaystyle F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(\psi_{-}(u))-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(\psi_{+}(u)),

(2.4)

where
\psi_{\pm}
are suitable perturbations of the identity.
In particular, the heart of the matter is to control the “stability” of
F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}
—showing that (
2.4
) has a small
L_{2}
norm.
It is important to note that this small
L_{2}
norm
does not
follow from an
L_{\infty}
estimate on (
2.4
); the latter is true only under strong regularity assumptions on
\mu
.

2.2
A generalization of the DKW inequality for linear functions

A key component in the proof of Theorem
1.3
is the following uniform estimate on the deviation between the empirical and the true distribution functions for each marginal
\mu^{\theta}
.
Let

\mathcal{U}=\left\{\{\left\langle\theta,\cdot\right\rangle\in I\}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ I\subset\mathbb{R}\ \text{is a generalized interval}\right\},

where by “generalized interval” we mean an open/closed, half-open/closed interval in
\mathbb{R}
, including rays.

Theorem 2.4
.

There are absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1}
such that the following holds.
Let
\Delta\leq 1
and
m\geq c_{0}\frac{d}{\Delta}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
A\in\mathcal{U}
satisfying
\mu(A)\geq\Delta
, we have that

\left|\mu_{m}(A)-\mu(A)\right|\leq\sqrt{\Delta\mu(A)}\log\left(\frac{e}{\mu(A)}\right).

Remark 2.5
.

A version of Theorem
2.4
was proved in
[
26
]
, with an error estimate of the order
\sqrt{\Delta\mu(A)}
(without a logarithmic factor) but with the restriction that
\Delta\geq c\frac{d}{m}\log(\frac{em}{d})
.
That restriction results in a suboptimal estimate in Theorem
1.3
, while the optimal one, at least for
q>4
, can be derived from Theorem
2.4
.
We conjecture that the logarithmic factor in Theorem
2.4
can be removed, but its removal does not affect the estimate in Theorem
1.3
for
q>4
, nor will it eliminate the logarithmic factor when
q=4
.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is a simple outcome of Talagrand’s concentration inequality for classes of uniformly bounded functions
[
37
]
, applied to a class of binary valued functions that has a finite
\mathrm{VC}
dimension.
For more information on Talagrand’s inequality see
[
9
]
, and detailed surveys on
\mathrm{VC}
-classes can be found in
[
40
]
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class of subsets of
\Omega
.
A set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\}
is
shattered
by
\mathcal{H}
if for every
I\subset\{1,\dots,\ell\}
there exists
A\in\mathcal{H}
such that
x_{i}\in A
for
i\in I
and
x_{i}\notin A
for
i\notin I
.
The
{\rm VC}
-dimension
of
\mathcal{H}
is

{\rm VC}(\mathcal{H})=\sup\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\}\subset\Omega\text{ is shattered by }\mathcal{H}\right\}.

The following is an immediate corollary of Talagrand’s concentration inequality combined with entropy estimates for VC-classes, see, e.g.,
[
9
,
19
,
40
]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that the following holds.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class of subsets of
\Omega
, put
k=\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})
and set

\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(\mathcal{H})=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}}\mu(A)
.
Then, for every
t\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-t)
,

\sup_{A\in\mathcal{H}}|\mu_{m}(A)-\mu(A)|\leq c\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}\log\left(\frac{e}{\sigma}\right)}+\frac{k}{m}\log\left(\frac{e}{\sigma}\right)+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{t}{m}}+\frac{t}{m}\right).

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Using the notation introduced previously, set
j\geq 0
such that
2^{j}\Delta\leq 1
, and let

\mathcal{U}_{j}=\left\{A\in\mathcal{U}:\mu(A)\in[2^{j}\Delta,2^{j+1}\Delta)\right\}.

It is well-known that as a class of ‘slabs’ in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
{\rm VC}(\mathcal{U})\leq c_{0}d
for an absolute constant
c_{0}
(see, e.g.,
[
40
]
) and in particular
\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{U}_{j})\leq c_{0}d
.
Moreover,
\sigma^{2}(\mathcal{U}_{j})\in[2^{j}\Delta,2^{j+1}\Delta]
, and
by Theorem
2.7
, for every
t\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-t)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{A\in\mathcal{U}_{j}}|\mu_{m}(A)-\mu(A)|

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\left(\sqrt{2^{j+1}\Delta\frac{d}{m}\log\left(\frac{e}{2^{j}\Delta}\right)}+\frac{d}{m}\log\left(\frac{e}{2^{j}\Delta}\right)+\sqrt{2^{j+1}\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{t}{m}}+\frac{t}{m}\right).

Set
t=c_{2}\Delta m\log(\frac{e}{2^{j}\Delta})
and let
m\geq c_{3}\frac{d}{\Delta}
.
It is straightforward to verify that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m\log(\frac{e}{2^{j}\Delta}))
,

\displaystyle\sup_{A\in\mathcal{U}_{j}}|\mu_{m}(A)-\mu(A)|\leq\sqrt{\Delta 2^{j}\Delta}\log\left(\frac{e}{2^{j+1}\Delta}\right),

(2.5)

which is the required estimate for sets in
\mathcal{U}_{j}
.
In particular, if
j_{\Delta}
is the first integer such that
2^{j_{\Delta}}\Delta>1
, then by the union bound and by comparing to a suitable geometric progression, with probability at least

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{j=0}^{j_{\Delta}-1}2\exp\left(-c_{2}\Delta m\log\left(\frac{e}{2^{j}\Delta}\right)\right)\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{4}\Delta m),

(
2.5
) holds for each
0\leq j\leq j_{\Delta}-1
.
∎

An immediate outcome of Theorem
2.4
(applied to sets of the form
\{\left\langle\theta,\cdot\right\rangle\leq t\}
and
\{\left\langle\theta,\cdot\right\rangle>t\}
) is the following.

Corollary 2.8
.

There are absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1}
such that the following holds.
Let
\Delta\leq 1
and
m\geq c_{0}\frac{d}{\Delta}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
, for every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying that
F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t)\in[\Delta,1-\Delta]
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(t)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t)\right|\leq\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t))}\cdot\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t))}\right).

(2.6)

For the remainder of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we shall assume that
F_{\mu^{\theta}}
is invertible.
This assumption is only made to simplify notation and holds without loss of generality.
Indeed, one may always consider
\nu
distributed as
\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}X+\beta G
where
G
is the standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(independent of
X
) and
\beta
is arbitrarily small.
In particular,
\nu
is centred, isotropic, satisfies
L_{q}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with a constant
L+1
, and
F_{\nu^{\theta}}
is invertible for every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
.
Thus, one may replace the RHS in (
2.6
) by
\sqrt{2\Delta\gamma(F_{\nu^{\theta}}(t))}\log(\frac{e}{\gamma(F_{\nu^{\theta}}(t))})
, and
F_{\mu^{\theta}}
by
F_{\nu^{\theta}}
in all of the following arguments, finally taking
\beta
to 0.

By Lemma
2.1
, the key is to control the difference between empirical and true
inverse
distribution functions.
To that end, consider the two functions
\psi_{+},\psi_{-}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\psi_{\pm}(u)=u\pm 2\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(u)}\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(u)}\right).

\psi_{\pm}
are perturbations of the identity, in a sense described in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

There is an absolute constant
\kappa
such that the following hold. Let
\delta=\kappa\Delta\log^{2}(\frac{e}{\Delta})
.
Then for
u\in[\delta,1-\delta]
we have

(1)

\psi_{\pm}(u)\in[\Delta,1-\Delta]
,

(2)

|\psi_{\pm}(u)-u|\leq\frac{1}{10}\cdot\gamma(u)
,

(3)

\psi_{\pm}
are absolutely continuous, strictly increasing on
[\delta,1-\delta]
, and their derivatives satisfy

\left|\psi_{\pm}^{\prime}(u)-1\right|\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\gamma(u)}}\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(u)}\right).

Proof.

We only consider the case
u\in[\delta,\frac{1}{2}]
and thus
\gamma(u)=u
; the argument in the case
u\in[\frac{1}{2},1-\delta]
is identical and is omitted.

Note that if
\kappa\geq 1
then
u\geq\Delta
and in particular
\log\frac{e}{u}\leq\log\frac{e}{\Delta}
.
Moreover, for
u\geq\delta=\kappa\Delta\log^{2}(\frac{e}{\Delta})
we have that
\Delta\leq u/\kappa\log^{2}(\frac{e}{\Delta})
. Hence,

\displaystyle|\psi_{\pm}(u)-u|

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\Delta u}\log\left(\frac{e}{u}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{u}{\sqrt{\kappa}\log(\frac{e}{\Delta})}\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right)\leq\frac{2u}{\sqrt{\kappa}},

and (2) follows if
\kappa\geq 400
, while (1) is an immediate consequence of (2) and the fact that
\delta\geq 2\Delta
if
\kappa\geq 2
.

Turning to (3), it is evident that
\psi_{\pm}
are absolutely continuous and satisfy the claimed bound on their derivative.
The fact that
\psi_{\pm}
are strictly increasing on
[\delta,\frac{1}{2}]
follows because
\Delta\leq u/\kappa\log^{2}(\frac{e}{\Delta})
, and if
\kappa\geq 36
then

\left|\psi_{\pm}^{\prime}(u)-1\right|\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{u}}\log\left(\frac{e}{u}\right)\leq\frac{3}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.\qed

From now on, fix
\kappa
as in Lemma
2.9
. By combining Corollary
2.8
and Lemma
2.9
, the following holds.

Lemma 2.10
.

Fix a realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
for which (
2.6
) holds.
Then, for every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and every

,

F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\in\left[F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{-}(u)\right),F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{+}(u)\right)\right].

Proof.

We only consider the case
u\in[\delta,\frac{1}{2}]
, and in particular
\gamma(u)=u
.
The analysis when
u\in(\frac{1}{2},1-\delta]
is identical and is omitted.

First, let us show that
t_{u}=F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(\psi_{+}(u))
satisfies that

\displaystyle F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(t_{u})\geq u.

(2.7)

Note that if (
2.7
) holds, then by the definition of the (right-)inverse,
F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\leq F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(\psi_{+}(u))
.

To prove (
2.7
), consider
u\in[\delta,\frac{1}{2}]
. Thus,
F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t_{u})=\psi_{+}(u)
, and by Lemma

F_{\mu_{\theta}}(t_{u})\in[\Delta,1-\Delta]
.
In particular, by Corollary

F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(t_{u})\geq F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t_{u})-\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t_{u}))}\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t_{u}))}\right).

Applying Lemma
2.9
once again,
\frac{3}{4}u\leq\gamma(F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t_{u}))\leq\frac{5}{4}u
and therefore

\displaystyle F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(t_{u})

\displaystyle\geq u+2\sqrt{\Delta u}\log\left(\frac{e}{u}\right)-\sqrt{\Delta\tfrac{5}{4}u}\log\left(\frac{4e}{3u}\right)

\displaystyle=u+\sqrt{\Delta u}\left(2\log\left(\frac{e}{u}\right)-\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{4}}\log\left(\frac{4e}{3u}\right)\right).

Now it is straightforward to verify that
F_{\mu^{\theta}}(t_{u})\geq u
, as claimed.

Finally, using the same argument, if
t_{u}=F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(\psi_{-}(u))
, then

F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(t_{u})<u

and therefore
F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\geq F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(\psi_{-}(u))
.
∎

By Lemma
2.10
and the monotonicity of
F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}
, we immediately have the following.

Corollary 2.11
.

Fix a realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
that satisfies (
2.6
).
Then, for every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{+}(u)\right)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{-}(u)\right)\right)^{2}\,du.

Remark 2.12
.

It is worthwhile to note once again that the RHS in Corollary
2.11
depends only on
F_{\mu^{\theta}}
and not on
F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}
.

The analysis of

\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{+}(u)\right)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{-}(u)\right)\right)^{2}\,du

is the focus of the next section.

2.3
A global modulus of continuity of
F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}

Throughout this section we consider a distribution function
F
of a symmetric random variable, and in particular
F(0)=\frac{1}{2}
.
The formulations and proofs for general random variables require only trivial changes and are omitted for the sake of a simpler presentation.

Once again, we may and do assume without loss of generality that
F
is invertible, and that for some
q\geq 4
and every
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle F(-t)\leq\frac{L^{q}}{2t^{q}}.

(2.8)

Before formulating the main estimate of this section, let us introduce some notation.
Set
\mathrm{id}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
to be the identity function, and denote the derivative of an absolutely continuous function
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\psi^{\prime}
.
For
q\geq 4
, let

r=1+\frac{q}{2q-4},

and recall that
\delta=\kappa\Delta\log^{2}(\frac{e}{\Delta})
and
\gamma(u)=\min\{u,1-u\}
.
Since
\Delta\leq(10\kappa)^{-2}
we have that
\delta\leq\frac{1}{4}
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let

\psi\colon[\delta,1-\delta]\to[0,1]

be an absolutely continuous, strictly increasing function that satisfies

|\psi-\mathrm{id}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\cdot)
, and for which either
\psi\geq\mathrm{id}
or
\psi\leq\mathrm{id}
. Then there is a constant
c=c(\kappa,q)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(\psi(u))\right)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle\leq c\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\Delta}}\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

Proposition
2.13
replaces the key part of the argument that was presented in Section
1.2
and which was based on the assumption that
F^{-1}
has a well behaved modulus of continuity.
That assumption allowed one to control
|F^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(\psi(u))|
in terms of
|u-\psi(u)|
, but unfortunately, it is useless in the general case.
Instead, one may write

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(\psi(u))\right)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du+\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}\,du-2\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)F^{-1}(\psi(u))\,du,

and show that all three integrals are close to each other. The details of the proof are presented in Lemma
2.14
and Lemma
2.15
, but intuitively the reason that the three integrals are close is that
\psi
is a perturbation of the identity. As a result, the second integral should be close to the first one. Moreover, if
u
is sufficiently far away from the point of symmetry (
F(0)=\frac{1}{2}
), the terms
F^{-1}(u)
and
F^{-1}(\psi(u))
have the same sign; thus, by monotonicity, their product is sandwiched between
F^{-1}(u)^{2}
and
F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}
.

The formal proof is also based on the tail-estimate of
F
, namely that

\displaystyle\left|F^{-1}(u)\right|\leq\frac{L}{\gamma(u)^{1/q}}\quad\text{for }u\in(0,1),

(2.9)

which is an immediate consequence of (
2.8
).

Lemma 2.14
.

There is a constant
c=c(\kappa,q,L)
such that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}\,du-\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\Delta}+\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\Delta}}\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}&\text{if }q=4,\end{cases}

where, as always,
r=1+\frac{q}{2q-4}
.

Proof.

We only present the case
\psi\geq\mathrm{id}
and
q>4
. The case
\psi\leq\mathrm{id}
follows from an identical argument to the one presented here, while the case
q=4
requires only simple modifications.

By a change of variables
u\leftrightarrow\psi(u)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}\psi^{\prime}(u)\,du-\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}(\psi^{\prime}(u)-1)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\psi(\delta)}^{\psi(1-\delta)}F^{-1}(v)^{2}\,dv-\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}(\psi^{\prime}(u)-1)\,du=A+B.

First, observe that
A
is close to
\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du
.
Indeed, since
|\psi-\mathrm{id}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma
we have that

\psi(\delta)\in\left[\delta,2\delta\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\psi(1-\delta)\in\left[1-\delta,1-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right].

Hence, there is a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(\kappa,q,L)
such that

\displaystyle\left|A-\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(v)^{2}\,dv\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}+\int_{1-\delta}^{1-\delta/2}\right)F^{-1}(v)^{2}\,dv\leq c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta}

where the last inequality follows from the tail-estimate (
2.9
), using that
q>4
.

Second, to estimate
B
, apply Hölder’s inequality (with exponent
r
)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|F^{-1}(\psi(u))|^{2r^{\prime}}\,du\right)^{1/r^{\prime}}\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}=B_{1}\cdot B_{2}.

To complete the proof it suffices to show that
B_{1}\leq c_{1}(q,L)
.
To that end, note that
|\psi-\rm id|\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma
.
Thus, by the tail-estimate (
2.9
) there is an absolute constant
c_{2}
such that for any
u\in[\delta,1-\delta]
,

\left|F^{-1}(\psi(u))\right|\leq\frac{c_{2}L}{\gamma(u)^{1/q}}.

Finally, with the choice of
r
we have that
2r^{\prime}/q<1
; hence
\gamma(\cdot)^{-2r^{\prime}/q}
is integrable in
(0,1)
and
B_{1}\leq c_{1}(q,L)
.
∎

Lemma 2.15
.

There is a constant
c=c(\kappa,q,L)
such that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)F^{-1}(\psi(u))\,du-\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right|

\displaystyle\leq c\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\Delta}}\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

Proof.

Once again, we only present the proof in the case
\psi\geq\rm id
and
q>4
.
The other cases follow a similar path and are omitted.

If
\psi\geq\rm id
then
\psi^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and since
|\psi-\mathrm{id}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\rm{id}
then
\psi^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})\geq\frac{1}{4}\geq\delta
.
Now set

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)F^{-1}(\psi(u))\,du

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\delta}^{\psi^{-1}(1/2)}+\int_{\psi^{-1}(1/2)}^{1/2}+\int_{1/2}^{1-\delta}\right)F^{-1}(u)F^{-1}(\psi(u))\,du

\displaystyle=A+B+D.

For
u
in the range of integration of
B
, we have that
u,\psi(u)\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}]
.
By the tail-estimate (
2.9
), there is an absolute constant
c_{1}
such that for
v\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4}]
,
|F^{-1}(v)|\leq c_{1}L
; thus
B\leq(c_{1}L)^{2}|\psi^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}|
.

Turning to
A
, let us show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|A-\int_{\delta}^{1/2}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right|&\leq c_{2}\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}\right)\end{split}

(2.10)

for a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(\kappa,q,L)
.

Note that
F(\frac{1}{2})=0
since the underlying random variable is symmetric.
Thus, by the monotonicity of
F^{-1}
and recalling that
\psi\geq\mathrm{id}
,

F^{-1}(u)\leq F^{-1}(\psi(u))\leq 0\quad\text{for }u\in\left[\delta,\psi^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{2})\right].

Setting

\displaystyle A_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{\psi^{-1}(1/2)}F^{-1}(\psi(u))^{2}\,du\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle A_{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{\psi^{-1}(1/2)}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du,

it is evident that
A\in[A_{-},A_{+}]
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
A_{-}
and
A_{+}
are both sufficiently close to
\int_{\delta}^{1/2}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du
.

Using once again that
|F^{-1}(u)|\leq c_{1}L
for
u\in[\psi^{-1}(\frac{1}{2}),\frac{1}{2}]
, it is evident that

\left|A_{+}-\int_{\delta}^{1/2}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right|\leq(c_{1}L)^{2}\left|\psi^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|.

As for
A_{-}
, one may follow the same argument as used in the proof of Lemma
2.14
—a change of variables, Hölder’s inequality, and invoking (
2.9
)—to show that

\displaystyle\left|A_{-}-\int_{\delta}^{1/2}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right|\leq c_{4}\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}\right)

for a constant
c_{4}=c_{4}(\kappa,q,L)
.
This proves (
2.10
)

Finally, an identical argument can be used to show that

\displaystyle\left|D-\int_{1/2}^{1-\delta}F^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\right|

\displaystyle\leq c_{5}\left(\sqrt{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}\right)

for a constant
c_{5}=c_{5}(\kappa,q,L)
, completing the proof.
∎

2.4
The deterministic estimate

Recall that

\psi_{\pm}(u)=u\pm 2\sqrt{\Delta\gamma(u)}\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(u)}\right).

With Corollary
2.11
in mind, the following estimate is crucial:

Lemma 2.16
.

There exists an absolute constant
\kappa
and a constant
c(\kappa,q,L)
such that

\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{-}(u)\right)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{+}(u)\right)\right)^{2}\,du\leq c\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\Delta}&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

Again, we shall assume for the sake of simplicity that each
F_{\mu^{\theta}}
is symmetric and without loss of generality that it is invertible. The argument is based on applying Proposition
2.13
to
F=F_{\mu^{\theta}}
and the monotone functions
\psi_{+}
and
\psi_{-}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
F
falls within the scope of Proposition
2.13
.
Moreover
\psi_{+}\geq\rm id
and
\psi_{-}\leq\rm id
, and if
\kappa
is as in Lemma
2.9
, then by that lemma
\psi_{\pm}
satisfy the remaining conditions in Proposition
2.13
.
Hence, for the choice
r=1+\frac{q}{2q-4}
, there is a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(\kappa,q,L)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(\psi_{\pm}(u)\right)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}\left(u\right)\right)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}+\left|\psi^{-1}_{\pm}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi_{\pm}^{\prime}(u)-1|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\Delta}+\left|\psi_{\pm}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|+\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\Delta}}\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\psi_{\pm}^{\prime}(u)-1|^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

Clearly
|\psi_{\pm}^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})-\frac{1}{2}|\leq 10\sqrt{\Delta}
, and by Lemma
2.9

\left|\psi^{\prime}_{\pm}(u)-1\right|\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\gamma(u)}}\log\left(\frac{e}{\gamma(u)}\right).

Thus

\left(\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}\left|\psi^{\prime}_{\pm}(u)-1\right|^{r}\,du\right)^{1/r}\leq c_{2}\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{3/2}\frac{1}{\Delta}&\text{if }q=4,\end{cases}

for a suitable constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(\kappa,q)
.
The proof is completed by an application of the
L_{2}
triangle inequality.
∎

2.5
The empirical tail integrals

The last component needed in the proofs of Theorem
1.3
and
1.6
is an estimate on

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\int_{U}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}

(2.11)

where
U=[0,\delta)\cup(1-\delta,1]
.

We begin with a general estimate in terms of

\rho_{d,m}=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}|\|\Gamma\theta\|_{2}^{2}-1|
that suffices for the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Lemma 2.17
.

There are absolute constants
\kappa,c_{0},c_{1}
and a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(\kappa,q,L)
such that the following holds.
If
m\geq c_{0}\frac{d}{\Delta}
, then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left(\int_{U}\left(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)\right)^{2}\,du\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\begin{cases}\rho_{d,m}^{1/2}+\Delta^{1/4}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\\
\rho_{d,m}^{1/2}+\Delta^{1/4}\log\frac{1}{\Delta}&\text{if }q=4.\end{cases}

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in S^{d-1}
and set

\displaystyle A_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du-\int_{0}^{1}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du,

\displaystyle B_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du-\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du.

Thus

\displaystyle\int_{U}F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{U}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du+A_{\theta}+B_{\theta}.

By isotropicity,
A_{\theta}=\|\Gamma\theta\|_{2}^{2}-1
, and in particular
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}|A_{\theta}|\leq\rho_{d,m}
.
Moreover, by (
2.9
),

\int_{U}F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u)^{2}\,du\leq 2\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{L^{2}}{u^{2/q}}\,du\leq c_{1}\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log\frac{1}{\Delta}&\text{if }q=4,\end{cases}

for a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(\kappa,q,L)
.

As for
B_{\theta}
, using the estimates from Section
2.3
and Section
2.4
one may verify that with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta m)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}|B_{\theta}|\leq c_{3}\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}&\text{if }q>4,\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\frac{1}{\Delta}&\text{if }q=4,\end{cases}

(2.12)

for a constant
c_{3}=c_{3}(\kappa,q,L)
. ∎

Lemma
2.17
implies that if
\rho_{d,m}\leq\sqrt{\Delta}
then (
2.11
) is (at most) of order
\Delta^{1/4}
—up to a logarithmic factor.
While this estimate is sufficient for the proof of Theorem
1.3
, it is not enough when the goal is to obtain an upper bound of
\Delta^{1/2}
— when such an estimate is possible as in Theorem
1.6
.

That calls for a more careful analysis of (
2.11
), and to that end, let us re-write (
2.11
) in a more standard form.

Definition 2.18
.

For
1\leq s\leq m
, let

H_{s,m}=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\max_{|I|=s}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in I}\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Thus,
\sqrt{m}H_{s,m}
is the Euclidean norm of the largest
s
coordinates of
(|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle|)_{i=1}^{m}
taken in the ‘worst’ direction
\theta
.

It is straightforward to verify that (
2.11
) is equivalent to
H_{\delta m,m}
. Also, sharp estimates on
H_{s,m}
have been established as part of the study of
\rho_{d,m}
, (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
4
,
27
,
39
]
).
Let us outline two such cases:

Example 2.19
.

If
X
is
L
-subgaussian, then there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}
depending only on
L
such that, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}s\log(em/s))
,

\displaystyle H_{s,m}\leq c_{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{s}{m}\log\left(\frac{em}{s}\right)}\right).

The proof of Example
2.19
is standard and follows from a net argument and individual subgaussian tail-decay, see e.g.,
[
5
]
.

Example 2.20
.

If
X
is a
\psi_{1}
random vector (that is, it satisfies
L_{q}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
qL
for every
q\geq 2
), then there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}
depending on
L
, such that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\sqrt{s}\log(em/s))
,

H_{s,m}\leq c_{2}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{s}{m}}\log\left(\frac{em}{s}\right)\right).

Example
2.20
was established
[
1
]
, with a minor (but from our perspective important) restriction: an upper bound on
m
as a function of
d
.
This restriction was later removed in
[
38
]
, see Theorem 14.3.1 and Proposition 14.3.3 therein.

Theorem
1.8
follows by combining Example
2.20
and a well-known result on the tail-decay of the Euclidean norm of an isotropic log-concave random vector, due to Paouris
[
33
]
.

Theorem 2.21
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that if
X
is an isotropic log-concave random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u\geq 1
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\|X\|_{2}\geq cu\sqrt{d}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-u\sqrt{d}\right).

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

By Borell’s lemma, an isotropic log-concave random vector is
\psi_{1}
with an absolute constant
L
. Thus, following Example
2.20
, it remains show that there are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}\sqrt{\Delta m}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\Delta}))
,

\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right).

Let
u=\beta\sqrt{\frac{\Delta m}{d}}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\Delta})
and by Theorem
2.21
and the union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{\|X_{i}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}\geq c_{3}\beta\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)\right)\leq 2m\exp\left(-\beta\sqrt{\Delta m}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)\right).

Hence, recalling that
\Delta\geq d/m
and
m\geq 2d
, the claim follows if
\beta
is a sufficiently large absolute constant.
∎

2.6
Proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.6

Let us connect all the steps we have made, leading to the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.6
.

Set
\kappa
as in Lemma

.
Note that for an absolute constant

And in the general case, when

.

The empirical tail integral

(applied to

) in the log-concave case; by Example

As for
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\int_{\delta}^{1-\delta}(F_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}^{-1}(u)-F_{\mu^{\theta}}^{-1}(u))^{2}\,du
, fix a realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
for which (

) holds.
The log-concave case follows from the arguments presented in Section

, and the general case follows from Corollary

3
Lower bounds on the Wasserstein distance

This section is devoted to showing that Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.6
are optimal in a strong sense.

Estimates with constant probability

We begin with lower bounds on
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
that hold with constant probability, exhibiting that the dependence on
\Delta
in Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.6
cannot be improved even if one is willing to accept much weaker probability estimates.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be centred and isotropic, and set
0<\beta<1
such that
\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{2}\geq\beta\sqrt{d}
.
Then with probability at least
c_{0}(\beta)
,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\geq c_{1}(\beta)\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}.

(3.1)

Note that if
X
is isotropic and log-concave then
\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{2}\geq c\sqrt{d}
for an absolute constant
c
. In particular, the dependence on
\Delta
in Theorem
1.6
cannot be improved (up to the logarithmic factor) even in the constant probability regime.

The proof of Lemma
3.1
requires a standard observation that will be used again in what follows.

Let
\tau
be a centred probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
and set
Y
to be distributed according to
\tau
. Let
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be independent copies of
Y
and put
\tau_{m}
to be the corresponding empirical measure. Consider an optimal coupling
\Pi
of
\tau_{m}
and
\tau
in the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
sense. Since
\tau
is centred, it follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i}\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\tau_{m}(dx)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}(x-y)\,\Pi(dx,dy)\right|\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\tau_{m},\tau).

(3.2)

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

By (
3.2
) applied to all the one-dimensional marginals of
X
,

\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu).

(3.3)

In particular, to establish (
3.1
) it suffices to show that if
\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{2}\geq\beta\sqrt{d}
, then with probability at least
c_{0}(\beta)

\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\right\|_{2}\geq c_{1}(\beta)\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}.

To that end, note that by isotropicity,
\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{2}^{2}=d
. Invoking the Paley-Zygmund inequality (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
), there are constants
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
that depend on
\beta
for which

\mathbb{P}\left(\|X\|_{2}\geq c_{2}\sqrt{d}\right)\geq c_{3}.

A standard binomial estimate shows that there is a constant
c_{4}(\beta)
such that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{4}m)
,

\left|\left\{i:\|X_{i}\|_{2}\geq c_{2}\sqrt{d}\right\}\right|\geq\frac{c_{3}}{2}m,

and by symmetrization and the Kahane-Khintchine inequality (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
),

\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\right\|_{2}\geq c_{5}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\geq c_{6}(\beta)\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}.\qed

Next, let us establish the optimality of Theorem
1.3
by constructing an isotropic random vector
X
that satisfies
L_{4}-L_{2}
norm equivalence and with constant probability

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\geq c\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/4}.

(3.4)

In particular, setting
\Delta\sim d/m
, (
3.4
) implies that the term
\Delta^{1/4}
in Theorem
1.3
is the best one can hope for, even if one allows a probability estimate of a constant rather than of
1-\exp(-c_{1}\Delta m)
.

Remark 3.2
.

As it happens, the random vector
X
we construct satisfies that with constant probability,

\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{2}-1\right|\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}\log\left(\frac{em}{d}\right)},

(3.5)

showing that there can be a substantial gap between
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
and
\rho_{d,m}
.

Example 3.3
.

Let
\beta>0
to be named in what follows. Set
W
to be a random vector distributed uniformly in
S^{d-1}
and let
v
be a real-valued random variable that is independent of
W
and takes the values
\beta\sqrt{d}
with probability
1-\frac{1}{2m}
and
(md)^{1/4}
with probability
\frac{1}{2m}
.

The wanted random vector is
X=vW

it is also isotropic. Moreover, it is straightforward to verify that with that choice of

\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{4}}\leq C

, implying that

satisfies

norm equivalence.

Let

and observe that with probability at least

Thus

for

\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle|=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|X_{i}\|_{2}=(md)^{1/4},

and on that event there is some

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\mu_{m}^{\theta},\mu^{\theta}\right)\geq

\displaystyle\int_{1-1/m}^{1-1/2m}\left(F^{-1}_{\mu_{m}^{\theta}}(u)-F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(u)\right)^{2}\,du

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{1-1/m}^{1-1/2m}\left((md)^{1/4}-\beta\sqrt{d}\right)^{2}\,du\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{d}{m}\right)^{1/2},

provided that

.

satisfies (

) with constant probability, as claimed.

Regarding (

), that follows immediately from Tikhomirov’s estimate from

∎

The random vector

The Bernoulli vector

Let
X
be distributed uniformly in

. In particular,

L

. It is standard to verify that for subgaussian random vectors,

\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)\geq c_{4}\Delta^{1/4}

with probability at least

.

As it happens, the reason for this lower bound is actually one dimensional.
It is caused by the behaviour of

corresponding to a marginal of

in a coordinate direction.

Lemma 3.4
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
such that, for

and

, with probability at least

can be established via a standard lower bound on the binomial distribution and Lemma

.
For the sake of simplicity the argument we present is based on an well-known estimate due to Montgomery-Smith

]
. To formulate that fact, denote by

the monotone non-increasing rearrangement of

Lemma 3.5
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
such that the following holds.
Let
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
be distributed as
\nu^{\otimes m}
, consider

and set

.
Then, with probability at least

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\geq c_{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor u\rfloor}x^{\ast}_{i}+\sqrt{u}\left(\sum_{i=\lfloor u\rfloor+1}^{m}(x^{\ast}_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right).

(3.6)

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Let
\nu_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{\varepsilon_{i}}

for an absolute constant

, the RHS in (

) is at least

. Moreover, when (

4
Variations on a theme

The first order max-sliced Wasserstein distance

Recall that the first order Wasserstein distance is defined on
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
—the Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite first moment—by

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\nu,\tau)=\inf_{\Pi\text{ coupling}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|_{2}\,\Pi(dx,dy).

In particular, by Hölder’s inequality,
\mathcal{W}_{1}\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}
.
And, just like
\mathcal{SW}_{2}
, one can define the first order max-sliced Wasserstein distance by

\mathcal{SW}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu^{\theta},\nu^{\theta})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be centred and isotropic, and assume that
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{q}}\leq L
for some
q>4
.
Then there are constants
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
that depend on
q
and
L
and absolute constants
c_{2},c_{3}
such that the following holds.

Let
0<\Delta\leq c_{0}
and set
m\geq c_{2}\frac{d}{\Delta}
.
With probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{3}\Delta m)
,

\mathcal{SW}_{1}\left(\mu_{m},\mu\right)\leq c_{1}\left(\rho_{d,m}+\sqrt{\Delta}\right).

The dependence on
\Delta
in Proposition
4.1
is optimal, as can be seen using the arguments presented in Section
3
.
In particular, even if
X
is log-concave,
\mathcal{SW}_{1}(\mu_{m},\mu)\geq c_{1}\sqrt{d/m}
with constant probability and
\mathcal{SW}_{1}(\mu_{m},\mu)\geq c_{4}\sqrt{\Delta}
with probability at least
c_{2}\exp(-c_{3}\Delta m)
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
(sketch).

The proof follows along the same lines as the proof for
\mathcal{SW}_{2}
—making use of the representation
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu^{\theta}_{m},\mu^{\theta})=\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)-F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(u)|\,du
.
A minor modification is needed when estimating

(1)=\int_{[0,1]\setminus(\delta,1-\delta)}|F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)|\,du.

To that end, note that by the Paley–Zygmund inequality there are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
L
such that for every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
,
F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(c_{1})\leq-c_{2}
and
F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(1-c_{1})\geq c_{2}
.
Fix
\delta\leq c_{1}/2
and consider a realization of
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
for which (
2.6
) holds.
Lemma
2.9
and Lemma

imply that for

F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)\leq F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(\delta)\leq F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(2\delta)\leq F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}}(c_{1})\leq-c_{2};

and using identical arguments, for

Hence

(1)\leq\frac{1}{c_{2}}\int_{[0,1]\setminus(\delta,1-\delta)}|F^{-1}_{\mu^{\theta}_{m}}(u)|^{2}\,du

and the estimate on

2.17
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

It is likely that the assumption
q>4
in Proposition
4.1
can be relaxed, in which case
\rho_{d,m}
should be replaced by (an estimate on)
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{\delta m}|\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle|^{\ast}
.

We cut further discussions on
\mathcal{SW}_{1}
short because in the context of this note, controlling
\mathcal{SW}_{1}(\mu_{m},\mu)
is significantly less interesting than controlling
\mathcal{SW}_{2}(\mu_{m},\mu)
.
The reason for that is the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality:

\mathcal{SW}_{1}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ is $1$-Lipschitz}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(\left\langle\,\cdot\,,\theta\right\rangle)\,d\nu-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi(\left\langle\,\cdot\,,\theta\right\rangle)\,d\mu\right).

Therefore,
\mathcal{SW}_{1}
is the difference of integrals rather than the integral of differences (like in the case of
\mathcal{SW}_{2})
, making it significantly easier to control.

In the next section we present applications of a similar flavour, where the key is again controlling differences of certain integrals.

Uniform estimation of increasing functions

There are many diverse applications where one is concerned with estimating
\mathbb{E}\Phi(\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle)
uniformly over
\theta\in S^{d-1}
for certain choices of
\Phi
and the given data is an independent sample
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
.
For instance, the choice
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
corresponds to covariance estimation where the natural (yet surprisingly suboptimal) idea was to use the empirical quadratic mean as an estimator and bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle^{2}\right|.

(4.1)

When
X
is isotropic, (
4.1
) is simply
\rho_{d,m}
.
And, as noted previously, the question of controlling
\rho_{d,m}
has been studied extensively under various conditions on the isotropic vector
X
.

Another natural choice is
\Phi(x)=|x|^{p}
, see e.g.,
[
15
]
in the log-concave case, where again, the empirical
p
-mean was the chosen estimator and
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\langle X_{i},\theta\rangle|^{p}-\mathbb{E}|\langle X,\theta\rangle|^{p}|
was studied.

Unfortunately, the empirical mean has a major drawback (particularly in high dimensions): the error deteriorates dramatically when
X
has tails that are heavier than a gaussian—see
[
23
]
for a survey and recent developments in the direction.
For example, the main result in
[
26
]
focuses on the case where
\Phi(x)=|x|^{p}
, and it is shown that a modified estimator

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}(\Phi,\theta,\delta,(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=\lfloor\delta m+1\rfloor}^{\lfloor m-\delta m\rfloor}\Phi\left(\left\langle X_{i},\theta\right\rangle^{\sharp}\right)

almost recovers the best possible statistical error once the parameter
\delta
is suitably chosen.
The proof in
[
26
]
relies heavily on the fact that
\Phi(x)=|x|^{p}
, but as we show here, this actually has nothing to do with the particular structure of
\Phi
.
As it happens, the optimal estimate holds uniformly over monotone functions that merely satisfy a compatible growth condition.
Indeed, let
p\in[1,\infty)
,
C\geq 1
, and set

\mathcal{I}_{p,C}=\left\{\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}:\Phi\text{ non-decreasing and }\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\Phi(x)|}{1+|x|^{p}}\leq C\right\}.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
X
be centred and isotropic and assume that
\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}\|\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle\|_{L_{q}}\leq L
for some

.
Then there are absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
and a constant

that depends only on

,

and

such that the following holds.

Let

, set

, and consider

.

for all

and
\theta\in S^{d-1}
,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\mathcal{E}}(\Phi,\theta,\delta,(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m})-\mathbb{E}\Phi(\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle)\right|\leq c_{4}C\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}&\text{if }q>2p,\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)&\text{if }q=2p.\end{cases}

\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\Delta})&\text{if }q=2p.\end{cases}

Hence,

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}(\Phi,\theta,\delta,(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m})-\mathbb{E}\Phi(\left\langle X,\theta\right\rangle)\leq(c_{2}+c_{3})C\begin{cases}\sqrt{\Delta}&\text{if }q>2p,\\
\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{2}(\frac{1}{\Delta})&\text{if }q=2p.\end{cases}

The proof of the corresponding lower bound follows from an identical argument and is omitted.
