Elementary Proof of a Theorem of Hawkes, Isaacs and Özaydin
Matthé van der Lee

Abstract
We present an elementary proof of the theorem of Hawkes, Isaacs and Özaydin, which states that Σ ​ μ G ​ ( H , K ) ≡ 0 \Sigma\,\mu_{G}(H,K)\equiv 0 mod d d , where μ G \mu_{G} denotes the Möbius function for the subgroup lattice of a finite group G G , H H ranges over the conjugates of a given subgroup F F of G G with [ G : F ] [G:F] divisible by d d , and K K over the supergroups of the H H for which [ K : H ] [K:H] divides d d . We apply the theorem to obtain a result on the number of solutions of | ⟨ H , g ⟩ | | n |\langle H,g\rangle|\mid n , for said H H and a natural number n n . The present version of the article includes an additional result on a quantity studied by K.S. Brown.

Möbius function, arithmetic functions, subgroup lattice

Introduction

The purpose of this note is to present a simple proof for the theorem of Hawkes, Isaacs and Özaydin, an important tool in the area of counting problems in finite groups.

We refer to
[
3
]
, where the result has appeared as Theorem 5.1, for background information regarding the subject.

The exposition uses minimal group theory. The ingredients for the proof are Burnside’s Lemma, the incidence algebra of a finite partially ordered set (of which we use only the basic properties), and a feature of arithmetic functions: Corollary
3.4
.

Next, we derive a result on the number of solutions
g
of
|\langle H,g\rangle|\mid n
, for a finite group
G
, a subgroup
H\leq G
, and
n\mid|G|
(Theorem
6.1
), and some results on a quantity studied by K.S. Brown (Propositions
6.2
and
6.3
).

A group action

For positive integers
m
and
d
, we define the number
\boldsymbol{b_{m}(d)}
as the binomial coefficient

b_{m}(d)={dm\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1\choose d\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}.

This function will play a pivotal role.

Let
t,m
be positive integers, and
G
a finite group of order
t\cdot m
.
G
acts on the set
X=\{T\subseteq G\mid|T|=t\}
of its subsets of cardinality
t
in the natural way

g\cdot T:=gT=\{gx\mid x\in T\}.

This is in fact the group action used in Wielandt’s proof of Sylow’s theorem, see
[
4
]
, §1.7.

If
g\in G
fixes an element
T\in X
, then
HT=T
where
H=\langle g\rangle
is the subgroup generated by
g
. So
T
must be a union of right cosets of
H
in
G
. As
|H|
equals the order
\boldsymbol{o(g)}
of
g
in
G
, this means
o(g)
divides
|T|=t
.
Writing
t=o(g)\cdot d
, we find that
T
is the union of
d
of the
d\cdot m
right cosets of
H
. Conversely, any such union is an element of
X
and is fixed by
g
. It follows that the number of fixed points in
X
of an element
g\in G
of order
t/d
equals

{dm\choose d}={dm\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1\choose d\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}\cdot m=b_{m}(d)\cdot m.

And, clearly, if
o(g)
does not divide
t
,
g
cannot have any fixed points in
X
.

By Burnside’s Lemma, the number of orbits of
G
in
X
is equal to the average number of fixed points of the elements of
G
. Denoting the number of elements of order
n
in
G
by
\boldsymbol{\chi(n)}=\chi_{G}(n)
, the number of orbits is therefore equal to

(\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,b_{m}(d)\cdot m\cdot\chi(t/d))\,/\,(t\cdot m).

Because this must be an integer, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
m
and
t
are positive integers and
G
is a group of order
tm
, one has

\displaystyle\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,b_{m}(d)\,\chi_{G}(t/d)\equiv\,0\text{ mod }t

Arithmetic functions modulo
n

Let
n
be a fixed positive integer, and consider the set
\boldsymbol{Ar_{n}}=\{f\mid f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\}
of functions from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{Z}/n
. We can think of the standard arithmetic functions such as the Euler
\varphi
and Möbius
\mu
functions as elements of this set, taking their values modulo
n
.

The set
Ar_{n}
is a commutative ring under the operations of point-wise addition and convolution product multiplication

\displaystyle f+h:a

\displaystyle\mapsto f(a)+h(a)

\displaystyle f\,*\,h:a

\displaystyle\mapsto\Sigma_{d\mid a}\,f(d)\,h(a/d)

Unity is the function
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, given by
\varepsilon(1)=1
and
\varepsilon(a)=0
for
a>1
. The group of units of
Ar_{n}
is:

\displaystyle Ar_{n}^{*}=\{f\in Ar_{n}\mid f(1)\in(\mathbb{Z}/n)^{*}\}.

Indeed, if
f*h=\varepsilon
, then
f(1)\,h(1)\equiv 1
mod
n
and
\forall_{a\in\mathbb{N},\,a>1}\,\Sigma_{d\mid a}\,f(d)\,h(a/d)\equiv 0
mod
n
. So
h(1)
must be the inverse of
f(1)
in
(\mathbb{Z}/n)^{*}
, and the value of
h(a)
for
a>1
can recursively be determined in
\mathbb{Z}/n
from the second congruence, in which it has coefficient
f(1)
.

By the well-known formula
\Sigma_{d\mid a}\,\mu(d)=\delta_{1,a}=\varepsilon(a)
, the inverse
\mu^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}
of the ordinary Möbius
\mu
function in the ring
Ar_{n}
is the function
\boldsymbol{E}
given by
E(a)=1
for all
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,

\mu=E^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}

(1)

We now discuss the set
\boldsymbol{R_{n}}=\{f\in Ar_{n}\mid\forall_{t\mid n}\,f(t)\equiv 0
mod
t\}
of the arithmetic functions mod
n
that are, to give them a name,
special
.

Proposition 3.1
.

R_{n}
is a unitary subring of
Ar_{n}
, and one has
R_{n}^{*}=R_{n}\cap Ar_{n}^{*}
. That is, if
f\in R_{n}
is a unit in
Ar_{n}
, it is a unit in
R_{n}
.

Proof.

R_{n}
is closed under addition, and
\varepsilon\in R_{n}
. If
f
and
h
are special mod
n
and
t
is a divisor of
n
, each term in the sum
(f*h)(t)=\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,f(d)\,h(t/d)
is divisible by
d\cdot(t/d)=t
, and
f*h
is again special.

Finally, if
f\in R_{n}\cap Ar_{n}^{*}
, let
t\mid n
with
t>1
. Then
f(1)
is relatively prime to
n
, hence to
t
. Furthermore,
f(1)\,f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(t)\equiv\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}\Sigma_{1<d\mid t}\,f(d)\,f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(t/d)
mod
n
, so certainly modulo
t
. By induction, all terms on the right-hand side can be assumed to be multiples of
d\cdot(t/d)=t
. Hence
f(1)\,f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(t)
is a multiple of
t
, and therefore so is
f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(t).

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f,h\in Ar_{n}
, and assume
f*E\in R_{n}
. Then

(a)

h*\mu\in R_{n}\Rightarrow h*f\in R_{n}

If, moreover,
f\in Ar_{n}^{*}
, the following equivalences hold

2.

h*\mu\in R_{n}\Leftrightarrow h*f\in R_{n}

3.

h*E\in R_{n}\Leftrightarrow h*f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}\in R_{n}

Proof.

If
h*\mu\in R_{n}
, then since
f*E
is also in
R_{n}
, so is their product
h*\mu*f*E
. By equation (
1
), that is just
h*f
, giving (a).

Now if
f
is a unit, so is
f*E
. As this function is in
R_{n}
, by Proposition
3.1
its inverse
f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}*\mu
is also in
R_{n}
. So if
h*f\in R_{n}
, the product
h*f*f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}*\mu=h*\mu
is in
R_{n}
too, establishing (b).

For (c), note that
h*E\in R_{n}\Leftrightarrow h*(f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}*f)*E\in R_{n}\Leftrightarrow h*f^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}\in R_{n}
, the latter equivalence because of
f*E\in R_{n}^{*}
.
∎

Note that the Corollary applies in particular to
f=\varphi
. Indeed, as
\Sigma_{d\mid a}\,\varphi(d)=a
, one has
\varphi*E=I\in R_{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{I}
denotes the
identity function
,
I(a)=a
(taken modulo
n
). And
\varphi\in Ar_{n}^{*}
since
\varphi(1)=1
.

Viewing the binomial coefficient function
b_{m}:d\mapsto{dm\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1\choose d\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}
introduced in Section
2
as an element of
Ar_{n}
, we now have

Proposition 3.3
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in Ar_{n}
, the following are equivalent

(a)

h*E\in R_{n}

(b)

h*b_{m}\in R_{n}

Proof.

Let
t
be a divisor of
n
. We apply Proposition
2.1
to the cyclic group
G=\mathbb{Z}/tm
. It has
\chi_{G}(d)=\varphi(d)
for every divisor
d
of
tm
, and hence we obtain
\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,b_{m}(d)\,\varphi(t/d)\equiv 0\,(t)
. Writing
b
for
b_{m}
, it follows that
b*\varphi\in R_{n}
. By (b) of Corollary
3.2
, applied to
f=\varphi
and
h=b
, we find
b*\mu\in R_{n}
.

As
b(1)=1
, the function
b
is a unit in
Ar_{n}
. Taking inverses, we get
b^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}*E\in R_{n}
. Part (c) of the Corollary, for
f:=b^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}
, then gives the desired equivalence
h*E\in R_{n}\Leftrightarrow h*b\in R_{n}
for
h\in Ar_{n}
.
∎

A slightly stronger statement is:

Corollary 3.4
.

For
h\in Ar_{n}
, the following are equivalent

(i)

\forall_{t\mid n}\,\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,h(d)\equiv\,0\>(t)

(ii)

\forall_{t\mid n}\,\exists_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\,\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,h(d)\,b_{m}(t/d)\equiv\,0\>(t)

Proof.

(i) just paraphrases (a) of the Proposition, and (ii) is a trivial consequence of (b).
To see that (ii) implies (i), take a divisor
t
of
n
, and suppose one already knows that
\forall_{k\mid t,\,k<t}\,\Sigma_{d\mid k}\,h(d)\equiv 0\,(k)
. By the Proposition,
h*b_{m}\in R_{k}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and all
k\mid t
with
k<t
.
Using (ii), pick an
m
such that
\Sigma_{d\mid t}\,h(d)\,b_{m}(t/d)\equiv 0\,(t)
. Then clearly
h*b_{m}\in R_{t}
for this particular
m
. Applying Proposition
3.3
again, one finds that
h*E\in R_{t}
, and so (i) holds for
t
as well.
∎

The incidence algebra of a finite poset

Let
\langle P,\leq\rangle
be a finite poset (partially ordered set), and
A
a commutative ring. The
incidence algebra
of
P
over
A
is the set of functions in two variables on
P
with values in
A
, which can only assume non-zero values when the arguments are comparable:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W_{P,A}}=\{f:P\times P\to A\mid\forall_{x,\,y\,\in\,P}\,(x\nleq y\Rightarrow f(x,y)=0)\}.

The set
W_{P,A}
is a, generally non-commutative unitary ring under point-wise addition and
convolution product
multiplication

\displaystyle(f+h)(x,y)\,

\displaystyle=\,f(x,y)+h(x,y)

\displaystyle(f*h)(x,y)\,

\displaystyle=\,\Sigma_{x\leq z\leq y}\,f(x,z)\,h(z,y)

Unity is Kronecker’s delta function,
\boldsymbol{\delta}(x,y)=1
if
x=y
and
\delta(x,y)=0
otherwise.

W_{P,A}
is an
A
-module, with
a\in A
acting by
(a\cdot f)(x,y)=a\cdot f(x,y)
. And
A\to W_{P,A},\,a\mapsto a\cdot\delta,
is a ring homomorphism making it an
A
-algebra. Its group of units is given by:

\displaystyle W_{P,A}^{*}=\{f\in W_{P,A}\mid\forall_{x\in P}\,f(x,x)\in A^{*}\}.

For if
f(x,x)\in A^{*}
for all
x\in P
, we may compute the values
h(x,y)\in A
of the right inverse
h
of
f
(the
h
with
f*h=\delta
) for a given
y\in P
inductively for the
x\leq y
by means of

\displaystyle h(y,y)

\displaystyle=f(y,y)^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}

\displaystyle h(x,y)

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}f(x,x)^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}\cdot\Sigma_{x<z\leq y}\,f(x,z)\,h(z,y)

(This works because
P
is finite.) Similarly, the values of the left inverse
g
of
f
are found, given
x
, using

\displaystyle g(x,x)

\displaystyle=f(x,x)^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}

\displaystyle g(x,y)

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}f(y,y)^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}\cdot\Sigma_{x\leq z<y}\,g(x,z)\,f(z,y)

By associativity,
g=g*\delta=g*f*h=\delta*h=h
is the unique two-sided inverse of
f
.

Key elements of
W_{P,A}
are the following functions
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
:

\displaystyle\zeta(x,y)=\begin{cases}\,1&\mbox{if }x\leq y\\
\,0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mbox{the {zeta function} of }P

\displaystyle\eta(x,y)=\begin{cases}\,1&\mbox{if }x<y\\
\,0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mbox{the {chain function} of }P

\displaystyle\mu=\zeta^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}

\displaystyle\mbox{the {M\"{o}bius function} of }P

The latter has the following properties, resulting from these definitions

\left.\begin{aligned} &\mu(x,x)=1&&\quad\qquad\mbox{for all }x\in P\>\\
&\mu(x,y)=\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}\Sigma_{x<z\leq y}\>\mu(z,y)=\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}\Sigma_{x\leq z<y}\>\mu(x,z)&&\quad\qquad\mbox{for }x<y\>\\
&\Sigma_{x\leq z\leq y}\>\mu(x,z)=0=\Sigma_{x\leq z\leq y}\>\mu(z,y)&&\quad\qquad\mbox{for }x\neq y\>\end{aligned}\right\}

(2)

Many combinatorial aspects of the poset
\langle P,\leq\rangle
are reflected in the ring
W_{P,A}
. For instance, the number of elements of the
interval

[x,y]=\{z\in P\mid x\leq z\leq y\}
, and the number of chains of length
k\geq 0
from
x
to
y
are given by, respectively

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=|[x,y]|

\displaystyle\eta^{k}(x,y)

\displaystyle=|\{(z_{0},\cdots,z_{k})\in P^{k+1}\mid x=z_{0}<z_{1}<\cdots<z_{k}=y\}|

As
<
-chains of length
|P|
cannot exist, we have
\eta^{|P|}=0
. And since
\zeta=\delta+\eta
and
\delta=1
, it follows that

\mu=\zeta^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}=(\delta+\eta)^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}=\delta-\eta+\eta^{2}-\cdots\pm\eta^{|P|\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}

Remark 4.1
.

By this formula,
\mu(x,y)
is equal to (the image under
\mathbb{Z}\to A
of) the number of even-length chains from
x
to
y
in
P
minus the number of odd-length ones. (Here,
x=y
is considered to form a
<
-chain of length zero.) This result is due to P. Hall.

Proof of the Theorem

Let
G
be a finite group of order
|G|=t\cdot m
, and let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}(G)}=\{H\mid H\leq G\}
be its subgroup lattice. It is partially ordered by the inclusion relation
\subseteq
, which we shall denote as
\leq
. Using
A=\mathbb{Z}
as base ring, we will write
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}(G)}
for the associated incidence algebra
W_{\mathcal{L}(G),\mathbb{Z}}
.

We denote
\boldsymbol{\mu_{G}}
, the Möbius function of the poset
\mathcal{L}(G)
, simply by
\mu
, and similarly write just
\zeta
for the zeta function.

We revisit the action of
G

discussed in Section

. For

, define:

\displaystyle\Lambda(K)

\displaystyle=\{T\in X\mid G_{T}=K\}

\displaystyle\lambda(K)

\displaystyle=|\Lambda(K)|

where
\boldsymbol{G_{T}}=\{g\in G\mid g\cdot T=T\}
is the stabilizer of

. If
H=G_{T}
, then

is a union of right cosets of
H
in
G
, so
|H|
divides
t
. Putting

, the size of the orbit

equals
[G:H]=dm
.

A point
gT
in this orbit has stabilizer
G_{gT}=gHg^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}
, a conjugate of
H
. So if
\mathcal{H}
denotes the conjugacy class of

in
G
, it follows that
\bigcup_{L\in\mathcal{H}}\Lambda(L)
is a union of orbits of

in

, each of length

. As the

\Sigma_{L\in\mathcal{H}}\,\lambda(L)\equiv\,0\>(dm)
.

Hence if

and
\mathcal{E}
is any set of subgroups of order

of

\Sigma_{H\in\mathcal{E}}\>\lambda(H)\equiv\,0\>(dm)

(3)

Now let

. Then
H\leq G_{T}
, so

again divides

. With

,

, which is therefore the number of fixed points of

. It follows that for any

b_{m}(t/|H|)\cdot m=\Sigma_{H\leq K\leq G,\,|K|\mid t}\,\lambda(K)

(4)

Indeed,

, and

t

.

Let

, and consider the collection

\mathcal{F}=\{H\in\mathcal{L}(G)\mid H\,\text{contains a conjugate of}\,F\}.

The remaining computations will take place in the incidence algebra

of the poset
\langle\mathcal{F},\leq\rangle
. As
\mathcal{F}
is

\mathcal{L}(G)
, that is, for

\mathcal{L}(G)

, it is clear that, for

has the same value whether taken in

. (See also Remark

In order to apply Möbius inversion to (

\displaystyle\alpha(K,L)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\,\lambda(K)&\text{if }|K|\text{ divides }t\text{ and }L=G\\
\,0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\beta(K,L)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\,b_{m}(t/|K|)&\text{if }|K|\text{ divides }t\text{ and }L=G\\

In terms of these, we have for

For in case

Reformulating it as

And this is precisely the content of the theorem of Hawkes, Isaacs and Özaydin.

Theorem 5.1
.

(
[
3

)

If
G
is a finite group,
F
a subgroup,
\mathcal{C}
the conjugacy class of
F
in
G
, and
d
a divisor of
[G:F]
, the following congruence applies

\displaystyle\Sigma_{H\in\mathcal{C}}\,\Sigma_{H\leq K\leq G,\,[K:H]\mid d}\>\mu_{G}(H,K)\equiv\,0\text{ mod }d

Proposition 6.3
.

For

, the following formula holds:

\varphi^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(K,G)\,=\,\Sigma_{F\leq H\leq G,\,\langle H,K\rangle=G}\,\varphi^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(F,H)\,[G:H].

First, we note that for

We now prove the formula by induction on

, and the right-hand side is the sum

\displaystyle 1-[G:K]-\Sigma_{K<L<G}\,\varphi^{\scalebox{0.60}[0.75]{$-$}1}(K,L)\,[G:L].
