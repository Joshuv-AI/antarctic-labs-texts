1.
Introduction

Definition 1.1
.

Define a permutation
\sigma\colon[n]\to[n]
to be
coprime
if
\operatorname{gcd}(m,\pi(m))=1
for
m\in[n]
. Let
C(n)
be the number of coprime permutations on
[n]
.

In recent work, Pomerance
[
10
]
provided nontrivial bounds on
C(n)
proving that
n!/3.73^{n}<C(n)<n!/2.5^{n}
for
n
sufficiently large. Pomerance then conjectured the existence of a constant
c
such that
C(n)=n!(c+o(1))^{n}
(see
[
10
, Section 5]
); our main objective is to provide such an asymptotic.

Theorem 1.2
.

C(n)=n!\bigg(\prod_{p\emph{prime}}\frac{(p-1)^{2(1-1/p)}}{p\cdot(p-2)^{(1-2/p)}}+\exp(-\Omega(\sqrt{\log n\log\log n}))\bigg)^{n}.

Remark
.

The
p=2
term is assumed to be
1/2
. The same method demonstrates that for fixed
k
,
C_{k}(n)
, which counts permutations
\sigma\colon[n]\to[n]
with
\gcd(\ell,\sigma(\ell),k!)=1
(see
[
10
, Section 5]
), satisfies

C_{k}(n)=n!\bigg(\prod_{p\leq k}\frac{(p-1)^{2(1-1/p)}}{p\cdot(p-2)^{(1-2/p)}}\bigg)^{n}\cdot\exp(O(\log n)),

though we forgo the details. McNew
[
8
]
has independently achieved similar results for fixed
k
.

Theorem

1.2
is certainly not the only result regarding finding mappings between sets of integers such that the labels of the matched edges are coprime. One such example is work of Pomerance and Selfridge
[
12
]
, answering a question of Newman, demonstrating that such a coprime matching exists between any set of
n
consecutive integers
A
and
[n]
. Another is work of Bohman and Peng
[
2
]
, in connection with the lonely runner conjecture, proving that any two intervals
A,B
of length
2m
in
[n]
with
m=2^{\Omega((\log\log n)^{2})}
have a coprime matching. This was strengthened in later work of Pomerance
[
11
]
to require only
m=\Omega((\log n)^{2})
. We also mention that enumerating
C(n)
was also considered by Jackson
[
6
]
and is listed as A005326 on the OEIS
[
14
]
.

1.1.
Proof techniques

For the upper bound we study
C(n)
by looking at permutations where
\gcd(j,\pi(j))
has no prime divisor at most a growing function
W
. Defining
\tau=\prod_{p\leq W}p
and examining the portion of integers
j
which are
a\bmod\tau
and map to
b\bmod\tau
, we find that the problem essentially immediately reduces to an
entropy maximization
program. Using the concavity and subadditivity of entropy as well as the Chinese Remainder Theorem, we can obtain the upper bound, which is done in
Section

2
. Note that as written, this approach essentially necessarily requires
W=O(\log n)
as otherwise
\tau>n
and the integers
\{1,\ldots,n\}
are not equidistributed in residue classes
\bmod\tau
. In order to do better, we (a) restrict attention only to whether any prime
p\leq W
divides or does not divide an integer
j\in[n]
, and (b) focus only on possible prime divisor patterns which produce large buckets (specifically, buckets which have at most
k\approx(\log n)^{1/2}
prime divisors), and treat the rest as an error term. Since
\prod_{p\leq W}(1-p^{-1})=\Theta(1/\log W)
is a negligible factor, the condition on
W
we obtain is roughly that
W^{k}\leq n^{1/100}
, which allows for consideration of significantly more prime divisors.

For the proof of the lower bound in
Section

3
, the role of larger prime factors immediately becomes crucial. Let
B_{S}
be the set of integers in
[n]
with
S
being the precise set of prime divisors at most
W
. For the sake of this discussion
W=\exp(c(\log n)^{1/2}(\log\log n)^{1/2})
and
k=(\log n)^{1/2}(\log\log n)^{-1/2}
. Our proof is based on first taking a “template”, assigning the appropriate “entropy-maximizing” fraction of
B_{S}
to map to
B_{S^{\prime}}
. Noting the size of
W
it follows immediately that (say)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\\
p>W\end{subarray}}1/p\leq W^{-1/2}

as
n
has at most
2\log n/(\log\log n)
distinct prime divisors. This suggests that the effect of “large primes” is minimal and therefore the upper bound proof should be reversible.

To make this argument rigorous, one chooses random subsets of
B_{S}
to map to
B_{S^{\prime}}
in accordance with the entropy maximizing distribution. One then proves that with high probability the associated coprimality graph corresponding to
B_{S},B_{S^{\prime}}
, if
S
and
S^{\prime}
have size at most
k
, has at most approximately a
W^{-1/2}
fraction of edges missing at any vertex. This is done via the Chernoff bound and the prior estimate regarding large prime factors. Combined with estimates regarding the number of perfect matchings in graph of high minimum degree due to Alon, Rödl, and Ruciński
[
1
]
, one can (essentially) handle matching all integers with at most
k
prime factors at most
W
.

For the remaining integers, we “absorb” this small subset into the set of integers divisible by no primes at most
W
, which is a very large set, namely of size
\Omega(n/\log W)
. On the other hand, the number of integers with at least
k
prime factors less than
W
is
n\exp(-\Omega(k\log k))
. See
[
15
]
for a more extensive discussion of the absorbing method and its history. Our approach here can be thought of as analogous to a “robust absorption strategy”, which has appeared for instance in work of Montgomery
[
9
]
, in which bipartite graphs with a “resilient matching” property are utilized. Finally, we note that we have omitted certain special considerations at the prime
p=2
, which are handled via a reduction of Pomerance
[
10
]
(
Lemma

3.1
).

1.2.
Further directions

We end by mentioning that we believe obtaining sharper asymptotics for
C(n)
is an interesting open problem; our methods are fundamentally limited to error terms that are of the form
n^{-o(1)}
as we crucially rely on considering buckets whose shared prime divisors multiply to less than
n^{1/2}
(say) in order to guarantee that the interval has the expected number of elements in such buckets. Thus either we cannot consider many primes, or cannot consider buckets with many prime divisors, either of which is a serious issue. Given this, error terms of the form
O(n^{-\Omega(1)})
would require a substantial modification of our methods and even the truth of such a statement is not obvious. Additionally, obtaining an asymptotic expansion for
C(n)
appears unlikely (as speculated by Jackson
[
6
]
).

We note that a reduction of Pomerance
[
10
]
shows it is enough to count coprime bijections
f\colon\{1,3,\ldots,2n-1\}\to[n]
. This is the number of matchings in a bipartite graph
i\in\{1,\ldots,2n-1\}
on the left and
j\in[n]
on the right are connected if
\gcd(i,j)=1
. Our proof can be interpreted as considering the non-edges that occur due to small primes and noting that the “remaining non-edges” are sparse. For sharper bounds, it may be possible to analyze an edge-removal process that targets the maximum entropy distribution described in
Section

1.1
: at each step, uniformly randomly choose an edge
(i,j)
in the current graph with probability say proportional to some
g(i,j)
and then delete
i
from the left and
j
from the right. Here we might take

g(i,j)=\prod_{2<p\leq n}g_{p}(\mathbbm{1}_{p|i},\mathbbm{1}_{p|j})

with
g_{p}(0,0)=(p-2)/p
,
g_{p}(0,1)=g_{p}(1,0)=1/p
, and
g_{p}(1,1)=0
. Depending on how long one can control this process, the remaining vertices can be handled by the absorbing method. Our proof can be thought of as very similar to analyzing such a process where we truncate the weights at
p\leq W
.

Techniques involving “weighted removal processes” or that target an “entropy-maximizing distribution” along with the absorbing method have been recently used to give sharp estimates on the
n
-queens problem
[
13
]
, and may be applicable to further situations where one wishes to count objects with structured biases (such as number-theoretic considerations, as in the present work). This in turn builds on recent powerful techniques introduced for counting in quasirandom situations such as counting combinatorial designs
[
7
]
, in which polynomially strong error terms are known. It remains interesting to see the limits of such techniques in number-theoretic settings.

Notation

Throughout this paper we will reserve
p
for variables which range over the primes. We will also implicitly assume
n
is sufficiently large for certain inequalities to hold. We write
a=b\pm c
to mean that
a\in[b-c,b+c]
, and
f=O(g)
means
|f|\leq Cg
for some absolute constant
C
while
f=\Omega(g)
means
f\geq C|g|
for some absolute
C
. We use
\log^{(k)}n
to denote the
k
-times iterated logarithm, e.g.
\log^{(5)}n=\log\log\log\log\log n
. We may sometimes omit floors and ceilings without comment if not essential to the situation. We say an event occurs whp (with high probability) if it occurs with probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Given a set
S
, we write
\binom{S}{k},\binom{S}{\leq k},\binom{S}{<k}
for the collection of subsets of
S
of size
k
, size at most
k
, and size less than
k
, respectively. Finally, as above, we often refer to a bijective function or permutation as a matching between the domain and codomain, especially when dealing with extra constraints encoded via a graph.

2.
Upper Bound

2.1.
Setup

Fix
W=\exp(2^{-10}(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2})
and
k=2^{-5}(\log n/\log\log n)^{1/2}
. Let
\alpha=\exp(-k\log k)
, which will roughly correspond to the error term we derive. Our upper bound on the number of coprime permutations
\sigma\colon[n]\to[n]
comes via enumerating permutations such that
p\nmid\gcd(j,\sigma(j))
for all
p\leq W
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{W}=\{p\colon p\leq W\}
be the primes which are at most
W
.

We first define a series of sets with respect to the divisibility structure of
\mathcal{P}_{W}
. For each
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}
, let

A_{S}=[n]\cap\bigcap_{p\in S}\{m\colon p|m\}\cap\bigcap_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}\{m\colon p\nmid m\}

and

A_{\geq k}=[n]\setminus\bigg(\bigcup_{S\in\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}}{<k}}A_{S}\bigg).

To start, we prove that
A_{S}
has the “predicted size” from an independent heuristic on small primes if
|S|<k
, while
A_{\geq k}
is a vanishing small proportion of the set. This will allow us to efficiently encode the divisibility structure of all primes at most
W
. Note that this is nontrivial as a more naive bound based on the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) would restrict us to having
W
such that
\prod_{p\leq W}p\leq n^{1/2}
(say) and hence
W\leq\log n
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}
with
|S|<k
we have

|A_{S}|=(1\pm\alpha^{2})n\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1}).

Furthermore,

|A_{\geq k}|\leq n\alpha^{1/2}.

Proof.

We first handle
A_{\geq k}
. Let
Y
be a uniformly random positive integer in
[n]
and
Y_{p}
be the indicator of
p|Y
for
1\leq p\leq W
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Y\in A_{\geq k}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}\leq W}\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq k}Y_{p_{j}}\bigg]=\sum_{1\leq p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}\leq W}\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigwedge_{1\leq j\leq k}(p_{j}|Y)\bigg]

(2.1)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{1\leq p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}\leq W}\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}\frac{1}{p_{j}}\leq\frac{2}{k!}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq p\leq W}\frac{1}{p}\bigg)^{k}\leq\exp(-k\log k/2)

where we have used that
W^{k}\leq n
to derive the probability that
Y
is divisible by
\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}p_{j}
and used that
\sum_{1\leq p\leq W}1/p=O(\log\log n)
. To derive the necessary bound on
A_{S}
, we proceed via the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE), in particular with the use of Bonferroni’s inequalities
[
3
]
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Y\in A_{S}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{p\in S}Y_{p}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-Y_{p})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq 4k}(-1)^{j}\sum_{T\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}{j}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{p\in S}Y_{p}\prod_{p\in T}Y_{p}\bigg]\pm\sum_{T\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}{4k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{p\in S}Y_{p}\prod_{p\in T}Y_{p}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq 4k}(-1)^{j}\sum_{T\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}{j}}\prod_{p\in S\cup T}p^{-1}\pm\bigg(\bigg(\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\bigg)\exp(-3k\log k)+(2W)^{4k}/n\bigg)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1})\pm\bigg(\bigg(\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\bigg)\exp(-3k\log k)+(2W)^{4k}/n\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1\pm\exp(-2k\log k))\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1}).

The first error term in the third line comes from a bound similar to
Equation

2.1
, while the second error term in the third line comes from the slight deviations in probability that a
Y
is divisible by some product of primes at most
W^{k}\leq n
. The fourth line comes from bounding the tail terms of the product expansion of
\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1})
(e.g. by use of the Bonferroni inequalities once again) and the fifth line follows upon converting additive error to multiplicative error, using
W^{k}\leq n^{1/8}
and
\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}(1-p^{-1})=\Omega(1/\log W)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Given a coprime permutation
\sigma\colon[n]\to[n]
and
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}
we let

\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}(\sigma)=\#\{m\in[n]\colon m\in A_{S_{1}},~\sigma(m)\in A_{S_{2}}\}

and
\beta(\sigma)
be the tuple of all such counts. Note there are at most
(2W)^{k}\leq\exp(k\log k)\leq n^{1/4}
such sets
S
with
|S|<k
and that by
Lemma

2.1
, we have
|A_{\geq k}|\leq n\alpha^{1/2}
. This implies that there are at most

(n+1)^{\sqrt{n}}\cdot(2n^{2})^{n\alpha^{1/2}}\leq e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}

possible choices for
\beta(\sigma)
: first, for every pair
S_{1},S_{2}
with
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
we choose up to
n+1
possibilities, and then we bound the ways to simultaneously choose all
\beta_{S,S^{\prime}}(\sigma)
for
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}
and
|S^{\prime}|\geq k
(and similar for
\beta_{S^{\prime},S}(\sigma)
), which is a total of
t\leq n^{2}
values which must sum to
|A_{\geq k}|
.

Therefore we have that

(2.2)

C(n)\leq e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot\sup_{\beta}\{\sigma\colon[n]\to[n]\text{ coprime}\colon\beta(\sigma)=\beta\}

Finally we define the following parameters:

\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}=\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}/n,~\beta_{S_{1},\cdot}=\sum_{S_{2}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}=|A_{S_{1}}|,~\beta_{\cdot,S_{2}}=\sum_{S_{1}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}=|A_{S_{2}}|.

2.2.
Completing the upper bound

We now bound the number of choices of
\sigma
given
\beta(\sigma)
. This will complete the proof of the upper bound due to
Equation

2.2
. We bound the number of permutations
\sigma
satisfying
\beta(\sigma)=\beta
as follows:

•

For each pair of subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}
, choose which
\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}
elements of
A_{S_{1}}
which have image in
A_{S_{2}}
. Call such elements
V_{S_{1}\to S_{2}}
.

•

For each pair of subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}
, choose which
\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}
elements of
A_{S_{2}}
which have preimage in
A_{S_{1}}
. Call such elements
V_{S_{1}\leftarrow S_{2}}
.

•

Determine a precise mapping between
V_{S_{1}\to S_{2}}
and
V_{S_{1}\leftarrow S_{2}}
.

Note also that if
\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}\neq 0
where
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then the expression within the supremum in
Equation

2.2
is actually
0
. Thus we can restrict attention to values of
\beta
where
\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}=0
when
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\neq\emptyset
.

Given this procedure, we see that the number of
\sigma
such that
\beta(\sigma)=\beta
is bounded by

\displaystyle\prod_{S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!\prod_{S_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\frac{\beta_{S_{1},\cdot}!}{\prod_{S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!}\prod_{S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\frac{\beta_{\cdot,S_{2}}!}{\prod_{S_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!}

\displaystyle=\prod_{S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}(\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!)^{-1}\prod_{S_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\beta_{S_{1},\cdot}!\prod_{S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\beta_{\cdot,S_{2}}!

\displaystyle\leq|A_{\geq k}|!^{2}\prod_{|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k}(\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!)^{-1}\prod_{|S_{1}|<k}\beta_{S_{1},\cdot}!\prod_{|S_{2}|<k}\beta_{\cdot,S_{2}}!

(2.3)

\displaystyle\leq e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot\prod_{|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k}(\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!)^{-1}\prod_{|S|<k}|A_{S}|!^{2}

where we used that factorials are log-convex in the third line to combine the terms for
|S|\geq k
, and used
Lemma

2.1
and Stirling’s approximation in the fourth.

We now bound each term in the above product. Write
n_{S}=n\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1})
. Noting that
n!\leq 3(n+1)(n/e)^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
and
(2W)^{k}\leq n^{1/4}
, and applying
Lemma

2.1
we find that

\displaystyle\prod_{|S|\leq k}|A_{S}|!

\displaystyle\leq e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot\prod_{|S|<k}((1+\alpha^{2})n_{S}/e)^{(1+\alpha^{2})n_{S}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot\prod_{S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}\bigg((n/e)\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1})\bigg)^{n\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1})}

\displaystyle=e^{2n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot(n/e)^{n}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}(1-1/p)^{n(1-1/p)}(1/p)^{n/p}

Next noting that
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
, we find that

\displaystyle\prod_{|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k}(\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!)^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq e^{n}\prod_{|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k}{\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}}^{-\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}}\leq e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot e^{n}\prod_{S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}}{\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}}^{-\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}}

\displaystyle=e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot e^{n}\exp\Big(-n\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}}\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}\log\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot(e/n)^{n}\exp\Big(-n\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}}\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}\log\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}\Big).

Let
Z
be the random variable which takes on the value
(S_{1},S_{2})
with probability
\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}
. Treating each set
S_{i}
as a vector in
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{P}_{W}}
, we may equivalently view
Z=((\mathbbm{1}_{p\in S_{1}},\mathbbm{1}_{p\in S_{2}}))_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}\in\{0,1\}^{2|\mathcal{P}_{W}|}
. Let
Z_{p}
be
(\mathbbm{1}_{p\in S_{1}},\mathbbm{1}_{p\in S_{2}})
when
(S_{1},S_{2})
is drawn according to
Z
, and note that
Z
is determined upon knowing
Z_{p}
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}
. By the subadditivity of entropy we therefore find that

\exp\Big(-n\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}}\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}\log\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}\Big)=\exp(nH(Z))\leq\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}\exp(nH(Z_{p})).

Note that
Z_{p}
is supported on
(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)
due to the constraint
\rho_{S_{1},S_{2}}=0
whenever we have
p\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}
. Additionally,
\mathbb{P}[Z_{p}=(1,\cdot)]=\mathbb{P}[Z_{p}=(\cdot,1)]=\lfloor n/p\rfloor/n
since
\sigma
is a permutation and
|\{p|\ell\}\cap[n]|=\lfloor n/p\rfloor
. These constraints determine the law of
Z_{p}
and therefore
H(Z_{p})
. In particular, it follows that
\mathbb{P}[Z_{p}=(1,0)]=\mathbb{P}[Z_{p}=(0,1)]=1/p\pm 1/n
and
\mathbb{P}[Z_{p}=(0,0)]=1-2/p\pm 2/n
.

Putting everything together into
Equation

2.3
, this gives an upper bound of

\displaystyle e^{7n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot(n/e)^{n}\bigg(\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}(1-1/p)^{n(1-1/p)}(1/p)^{n/p}\bigg)^{2}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}(1-2/p)^{-n(1-2/p)}(1/p)^{-2n/p}

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{7n\alpha^{1/3}}\cdot(n/e)^{n}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}}(p-1)^{2(1-1/p)n}(p-2)^{-n(1-2/p)}p^{-n}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq e^{7n\alpha^{1/3}+n/W}\cdot(n/e)^{n}\prod_{p}(p-1)^{2(1-1/p)n}(p-2)^{-n(1-2/p)}p^{-n}

where we have used that
(p-1)^{2(1-1/p)}(p-2)^{-(1-2/p)}p^{-1}=1+O(1/p^{2})
which implies the inequality
\prod_{p>W}(p-1)^{2(1-1/p)}(p-2)^{-(1-2/p)}p^{-1}=1+O(1/(W\log W))\leq\exp(1/W)
. The desired result follows immediately using our expressions for
\alpha,W
.

3.
Lower Bound

3.1.
Setup and preliminaries

Recall that we have set
W=\exp(2^{-10}(\log n\log\log n)^{1/2})
and
k=2^{-5}(\log n/\log\log n)^{1/2}
, as well as
\alpha=\exp(-k\log k)
.

For the lower bound, note that any coprime permutation must map even numbers to odd numbers, which are sets of approximately the same size. In order to witness this phenomenon, we use the following reduction of Pomerance
[
10
]
. Define
[n]_{\mathrm{o}}=\{1,3,\ldots,2n-1\}
, the first
n
odd positive integers.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
10
, Lemma 1]
)
.

Let
C_{0}(n)
be the number of bijective functions
f\colon[n]_{\mathrm{o}}\to[n]
with
\gcd(j,f(j))=1
for all
j\in\{1,3,\ldots,2n-1\}
. Then
C(2n)=C_{0}(n)^{2}
and
C(2n+1)\geq 2C_{0}(n-1)^{2}
.

We will require slightly modified sets in order to capture the divisibility structures of
[n]
and
[n]_{\mathrm{o}}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus\{2\}
. For
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}
define

B_{S}=[n]\cap\bigcap_{p\in S}\{m\colon p|m\}\cap\bigcap_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}\{m\colon p\nmid m\},~B_{\geq k}=[n]\setminus\bigcup_{S\in\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}}{<k}}B_{S}

and the analogous

C_{S}=[n]_{\mathrm{o}}\cap\bigcap_{p\in S}\{m\colon p|m\}\cap\bigcap_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}\{m\colon p\nmid m\},~C_{\geq k}=[n]_{\mathrm{o}}\setminus\bigcup_{S\in\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}}{<k}}C_{S}.

Similar to
Lemma

2.1
, we have the following control on the set sizes
B_{S}
,
C_{S}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}
with
|S|<k
we have,

|B_{S}|,|C_{S}|=(1\pm\alpha^{2})n\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1}).

Furthermore,

|B_{\geq k}|,|C_{\geq k}|\leq\alpha^{1/2}n.

For the remainder of this section we focus on providing an asymptotic lower bound on
C_{0}(n)
, which we treat as a matching problem between
[n]_{\mathrm{o}}
and
[n]
with forbidden edges corresponding to non-coprime pairs. Now, heuristically, the lower bound should match the bounding scheme presented in
Section

2
(ignoring the prime
p=2
). However, to actually ensure we avoid coprimality with respect to larger primes
p
, we will require a number of estimates. The crucial point for our lower bound is noting that the divisibility structure of primes larger than
W
removes a sparse graph (of density bounded by say
W^{-1/2}
).

Fact 3.3
.

For
\ell\in[2n]
we have that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\ell\\
p>W\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}<W^{-1/2}.

Proof.

This is immediate noting that at most
2\log n/(\log\log n)
distinct primes divide an integer
\ell\in[2n]
.
∎

We will also require that for a given “large class” only a small portion of numbers are divisible by a fixed
p
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
W<p^{\prime}\leq n^{1/3}
and
|S|<k
, we have that

|B_{S}\cap\{m\colon p^{\prime}|m\}|\leq 2|A_{S}|/p^{\prime},~|C_{S}\cap\{m\colon p|m\}|\leq 2|B_{S}|/p^{\prime}.

For
p^{\prime}>n^{1/3}
we have that

|B_{S}\cap\{m\colon p^{\prime}|m\}|\leq 2n^{2/3},~|C_{S}\cap\{m\colon p|m\}|\leq 2n^{2/3}.

Proof.

The second claim follows immediately as
|[2n]\cap\{m:p^{\prime}|m\}|\leq 2n^{2/3}
. For the first claim, we focus on
B_{S}
as the analogous claim for
C_{S}
follows similarly. Let
Y
and
Y_{p}
be as in the proof of
Lemma

2.1
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[Y\in A_{S}

\displaystyle\wedge Y_{p^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y_{p^{\prime}}\prod_{p\in S}Y_{p}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}(1-Y_{p})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq 4k}(-1)^{j}\sum_{T\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}{j}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y_{p^{\prime}}\prod_{p\in S}Y_{p}\prod_{p\in T}Y_{p}\bigg]\pm\sum_{T\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}{4k}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[Y_{p^{\prime}}\prod_{p\in S}Y_{p}\prod_{p\in T}Y_{p}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq 4k}(-1)^{j}\sum_{T\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}{j}}(p^{\prime})^{-1}\prod_{p\in S\cup T}p^{-1}\pm\bigg((p^{\prime})^{-1}\bigg(\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\bigg)\exp(-3k\log k)+(2W)^{4k}/n\bigg)

\displaystyle=(1\pm\alpha^{2})(p^{\prime})^{-1}\prod_{p\in S}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S}(1-p^{-1})

where we have used that
n^{1/3}(W)^{4k}\leq n^{1/4}
. The result follows immediately.
∎

We will also require that in any bipartite graph with balanced vertex parts and a very large minimum degree there are a large number of perfect matchings. The argument is identical to that of Alon, Rödl, and Ruciński
[
1
, Theorem 1]
, but we reprove the result in order to quantify the dependencies.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
be a bipartite graph such that
|A|=|B|=n
and
\Delta=\Delta(K_{A,B}\setminus G)
, the maximum degree of the bipartite complement, satisfies
\Delta\leq n/3
. Then there are at least
((n-2\Delta)/e)^{n}
perfect matchings in
G
.

Proof.

By
[
1
, Theorem 3]
, if for any
X\subseteq A
,
Y\subseteq B
,

k(|X|+|Y|)+E(A\setminus X,B\setminus Y)\geq kn

holds, then
G
has a
k
-factor (and therefore a subgraph of
G
which is
k
-regular). Let
k=n-2\Delta
and note that for
|X|+|Y|\geq n
the above inequality is immediate. If
|X|+|Y|\leq n
, suppose that
|X|\geq|Y|
(the other case being analogous) and note that
E(A\setminus X,B\setminus Y)\geq(n-|X|)(n-|Y|)-(n-|X|)\Delta=(n-|X|)(n-|Y|-\Delta)
and hence

k(|X|+|Y|)+E(A\setminus X,B\setminus Y)\geq k(|X|+|Y|)+(n-|X|)(n-|Y|-\Delta).

Therefore it suffices to check that
k(|X|+|Y|)+(n-|X|)(n-|Y|-\Delta)\geq kn
which is equivalent to

k(n-|X|-|Y|)\leq(n-|X|)(n-|Y|-\Delta).

Fix
|X|+|Y|=t
and imagine varying
|X|
with
t\geq|X|\geq|Y|=t-|X|
. By concavity of the right side, it suffices to check when
|X|=t,|Y|=0
and
|X|=|Y|=t/2
. In the first case the inequality follows as
k\leq n-\Delta
and in the second case the inequality is equivalent to

k(n-t)\leq(n-t/2)(n-t/2-\Delta).

Viewing the difference of the right and left hand sides as functions of
t
, the derivative is seen to be
t/2+k-n+\Delta/2
and thus the inequality is closest when
t=2n-2k-\Delta=3\Delta
. It becomes

(n-2\Delta)(n-3\Delta)\leq(n-3\Delta/2)(n-5\Delta/2)

which is trivially true as
n\geq 3\Delta
.

Now let
G^{\prime}
be the corresponding subgraph of
G
which is exactly
k
-regular. Then by the solution of the van der Waerden conjecture on permanents due to Egorychev
[
4
]
and Falikman
[
5
]
it follows that the number of perfect matchings in
G^{\prime}
is at least
(k/n)^{n}n!\geq(k/e)^{n}
.
∎

Finally we will repeatedly require the Chernoff bound.

Lemma 3.6

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X
be either:

•

a sum of independent random variables, each of which takes values in
[0,1]

•

hypergeometrically distributed (with any parameters).

Then for any
\delta\geq 0
we have

\mathbb{P}[X\geq(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}X]\leq\exp(-\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}X/(2+\delta)).

3.2.
Proof of the lower bound

In order to complete the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, we devise a procedure that produces a triplet
(\sigma,R_{B},R_{C})
where
R_{B}\subseteq[n]
and
R_{C}\subseteq[n]_{\mathrm{o}}
with
|R_{B}|,|R_{C}|\leq n\exp(n\alpha^{c})
and such that
\sigma
is coprime. The number of possible outcomes of such a procedure is bounded above by

C(n)\exp(n\alpha^{c/2})
due to the size bounds on
R_{B},R_{C}
and therefore it suffices to provide a lower bound on the number possible outcomes.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{B}
denote the set of labels
\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\\
|S|<k\end{subarray}}\{S\}\cup\{\ast\}
and let
\mathcal{M}_{C}
denote the set of labels
\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\\
|S|<k\end{subarray}}\{S\}\cup\{\ast\}
. We first construct (via a randomized procedure) a pair of maps
\phi_{1}\colon[n]\to\mathcal{M}_{B}
and
\phi_{2}\colon[n]\to\mathcal{M}_{C}
which will serve as a template for the procedure producing
(\sigma,R_{B},R_{C})
.

Lemma 3.7
.

There exist assignment functions
\phi_{1}\colon[n]\to\mathcal{M}_{B}
and
\phi_{2}\colon[n]\to\mathcal{M}_{C}
such that:

•

\phi_{1}(m_{1})=\ast
for
m_{1}\in B_{\geq k}
and
\phi_{2}(m_{2})=\ast
for
m_{2}\in C_{\geq k}
.

•

If
S\cap T\neq\emptyset
then
x\in B_{S}
implies
\phi_{1}(x)\neq T
, and
y\in C_{T}
implies
\phi_{2}(y)\neq S
.

•

For
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
, we have

\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}:=|\{m_{1}\in B_{S_{1}}\colon\phi_{1}(m_{1})=S_{2}\}|=|\{m_{2}\in C_{S_{2}}\colon\phi_{2}(m_{2})=S_{1}\}|.

•

For all
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
,

\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}=(1\pm\exp(-\Omega((\log n\log\log n)^{1/2})))n\prod_{p\in S_{1}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in S_{2}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})}(1-2p^{-1})

•

|\{m_{1}\colon\phi_{1}(m_{1})=\ast\}|\leq 2\alpha^{1/2}n
.

•

|\{m_{2}\colon\phi_{2}(m_{2})=\ast\}|\leq 2\alpha^{1/2}n
.

Proof.

We construct initial random assignment functions which essentially only fail the third bullet point, and then “throw out” a small fraction of values by reassigning them to
\ast
in order to balance the two sides. For
m_{1}\in B_{\geq k}
deterministically assign
\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=\ast
and for
m_{2}\in C_{\geq k}
deterministically assign
\phi_{2}^{\prime}(m_{2})=\ast
. Given
m_{1}\in B_{S}
with
|S|<k
, for each
|T|<k
we assign
\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=T
with probability

\mathbbm{1}_{S\cap T=\emptyset}\cdot\prod_{p\in T}(p-1)^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus(S\cup T)}(1-(p-1)^{-1})

and otherwise set
\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=\ast
. Analogously, given
m_{2}\in C_{S}
with
|S|<k
, for
|T|<k
we assign
\phi_{2}^{\prime}(m_{2})=T
with probability

\mathbbm{1}_{S\cap T=\emptyset}\cdot\prod_{p\in T}(p-1)^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus(S\cup T)}(1-(p-1)^{-1})

and else set
\phi_{2}^{\prime}(m_{2})=\ast
. Evidently the stated probabilities add to at most
1
when summing over
|T|<k
, so this is well-defined.

By
Lemma

3.2
we have for
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
which are disjoint that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle|\{m_{1}\in B_{S_{1}}\colon\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=S_{2}\}|

\displaystyle=(1\pm\alpha^{2})n\prod_{p\in S_{1}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus S_{1}}(1-p^{-1})\prod_{p\in S_{2}}(p-1)^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})}(1-(p-1)^{-1})

\displaystyle=(1\pm\alpha^{2})n\prod_{p\in S_{1}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in S_{2}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})}(1-2p^{-1})

and analogously

\mathbb{E}|\{m_{2}\in C_{S_{2}}\colon\phi_{2}^{\prime}(m_{2})=S_{1}\}|=(1\pm\alpha^{2})n\prod_{p\in S_{1}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in S_{2}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}^{\prime}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})}(1-2p^{-1}).

Applying Chernoff (
Lemma

3.6
) it follows with probability at least
1/2
that if for
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
disjoint we set

\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}=\min(|\{m_{1}\in B_{S_{1}}\colon\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=S_{2}\}|,|\{m_{2}\in C_{S_{2}}\colon\phi_{2}^{\prime}(m_{2})=S_{1}\}|)

then

\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}=(1\pm 2\alpha^{2})n\prod_{p\in S_{1}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in S_{2}}p^{-1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{W}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})}(1-2p^{-1}).

To construct
\phi_{1}
, for each pair of disjoint
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|
of size less than
k
assign
\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}
values of
S_{1}
to map to the label
S_{2}
. Send all remaining values in
S_{1}
, if any, to
\ast
(and set
B_{\geq k}=\ast
as before). For
\phi_{2}
, assign
\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}
values of
S_{2}
to map to the label
S_{1}
and send all remaining values in
S_{2}
, if any, to
\ast
. This is well-defined since clearly

\sum_{|S_{2}|<k}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}\leq\sum_{|S_{2}|<k}|\{m_{1}\in B_{S_{1}}\colon\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=S_{2}\}|\leq|B_{S_{1}}|

and similar for
\phi_{2}^{\prime}
.

The first four properties follow immediately. The final two properties follow from noting that

\Big||\{m_{1}\in B_{S_{1}}\colon\phi_{1}^{\prime}(m_{1})=S_{2}\}|-|\{m_{2}\in C_{S_{2}}\colon\phi_{2}^{\prime}(m_{2})=S_{1}\}|\Big|\leq 8\alpha^{2}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}},

so the extra amount of values assigned the label
\ast
is not significant, and by citing the second part of
Lemma

3.2
.
∎

To construct
(\sigma,R_{B},R_{C})
, we define
\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}
as in
Lemma

3.7
,
\beta_{\ast}^{1}=|\{m_{1}\colon\phi_{1}(m_{1})=\ast\}|
, and
\beta_{\ast}^{2}=|\{m_{2}\colon\phi_{2}(m_{2})=\ast\}|
. Next we consider the following procedure:

•

Fix
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
satisfying
Lemma

3.7
with these values of
\beta
.

•

Choose
\sigma_{1}
uniformly randomly among all permutations of
[n]
with
\sigma_{1}(B_{S})=B_{S}
for all
S
and
\sigma_{2}
uniformly randomly among all permutations of
[n]_{\mathrm{o}}
with
\sigma_{2}(C_{S})=C_{S}
for all
S
.

•

Let
B_{S_{1},S_{2}}=\{m_{1}\in B_{S_{1}}\colon\phi_{1}(\sigma_{1}(m_{1}))=S_{2}\}
and
C_{S_{1},S_{2}}=\{m_{2}\in C_{S_{2}}\colon\phi_{2}(\sigma_{2}(m_{2}))=S_{1}\}
for
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
. Furthermore let
R_{B}=\{m_{1}\colon\phi_{1}(\sigma_{1}(m_{1}))=\ast\}
and
R_{C}=\{m_{2}\colon\phi_{2}(\sigma_{2}(m_{2}))=\ast\}

•

For disjoint
S_{1},S_{2}
of size at most
k
with
(S_{1},S_{2})\neq(\emptyset,\emptyset)
, let
\sigma
form a uniformly random matching between
B_{S_{1},S_{2}}
and
C_{S_{1},S_{2}}
not violating the coprimality condition.

•

For
(S_{1},S_{2})=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
let
\sigma
be a uniformly random matching between
B_{\emptyset,\emptyset}\cup R_{B}
and
C_{\emptyset,\emptyset}\cup R_{C}
.

•

Output
(\sigma,R_{B},R_{C})
.

Note that this fully specifies
\sigma
since if
S_{1}\cap S_{2}\neq\emptyset
we have
x\in B_{S_{1}}
implies
\phi_{1}(x)\neq S_{2}
and similar for
\phi_{2}
. Furthermore, given the output
(\sigma,R_{B},R_{C})
we can mostly reconstruct the procedure. In particular, for
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
,
B_{S_{1},S_{2}}
is the set of values in
B_{S_{1}}
mapped to
C_{S_{2}}
by
\sigma
for all

, while

is the set of values in
B_{\emptyset}\setminus R_{B}
mapped to
C_{\emptyset}\cup R_{C}
. This determines
\sigma_{1}
up to right-multiplication by some
\xi_{1}
which preserves the sets
B_{S_{1},S_{2}}
for all
|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k
as well as
R_{B}
, and similar for
\sigma_{2}
.

Therefore, if
X
is the number of possible outputs
(\sigma,R_{B},R_{C})
where
\sigma
is coprime and

is the number of ways to run the above procedure where the output is coprime, we have

(3.1)

Y\leq X\cdot\beta_{\ast}^{1}!\beta_{\ast}^{2}!\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k\\
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!^{2}\leq C_{0}(n)\cdot n^{\beta_{\ast}^{1}+\beta_{\ast}^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|<k\\
S_{1}\cap S_{2}=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\beta_{S_{1},S_{2}}!^{2}.

Now we provide a lower bound on

.

Note that by
Lemma

3.4
and the Chernoff bound (

3.6
), with probability at least
3/4
we have that for all disjoint

the coprimality bipartite graph between parts

(3.2)

possible valid ways to run the procedure. Now, given
