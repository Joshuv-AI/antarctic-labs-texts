Abstract
. In his monograph on Infinite Abelian Groups, I. Kaplansky raised three “test problems” concerning their structure and multiplicity. As noted by Azoff, these problems make sense for any category admitting a direct sum operation. Here, we are interested in the operator theoretic version of Kaplansky’s second problem which asks: if A A and B B are operators on an infinite-dimensional, separable Hilbert space and A ⊕ A A\oplus A is equivalent to B ⊕ B B\oplus B in some (precise) sense, is A A equivalent to B B ? We examine this problem under a strengthening of the hypothesis, where a “primitive” square root J 2 ​ ( A ) J_{2}(A) of A ⊕ A A\oplus A is assumed to be equivalent to the corresponding square root J 2 ​ ( B ) J_{2}(B) of B ⊕ B B\oplus B . When “equivalence” refers to similarity of operators and A A is a compact operator, we deduce from this stronger hypothesis that A A and B B are similar. We exhibit a counterexample (due to J. Bell) of this phenomenon in the setting of unital rings. Also, we exhibit an uncountable family { U α } α ∈ Ω \{U_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Omega} of unitary operators, no two of which are unitarily equivalent, such that each U α U_{\alpha} is unitarily equivalent to J n ​ ( U α ) J_{n}(U_{\alpha}) , a “primitive” n t ​ h n^{th} root of U α ⊕ U α ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ U α U_{\alpha}\oplus U_{\alpha}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{\alpha} .

1.
Introduction

1.1.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex, separable, infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, and let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the algebra of bounded linear operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
. By
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
we denote the closed, two-sided ideal of compact operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The
spectrum
of an element
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is denoted by
\sigma(T):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:T-\lambda I\mbox{ is not invertible}\}
, and the
spectral radius
of
T
is
\mathrm{spr}(T):=\max\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}
. The Beurling-Gelfand Spectral Radius Formula is the statement that
\mathrm{spr}(T)=\lim_{n}\|T^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}
. An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
Fredholm
if its range is closed, and if
\ker\,T
and
\ker\,T^{*}
are both finite-dimensional, in which case the
Fredholm index
of
T
is defined as
\mathrm{ind}\,T:=\mathrm{nul}\,T-\mathrm{nul}\,T^{*}
. (Equivalently,
T
is Fredholm if its image
\pi(T)
in the Calkin algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})/\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
under the canonical map
\pi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})/\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
defined by
\pi(T):=T+\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
is invertible.) We say that
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
biquasitriangular
provided that whenever
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is such that the range of
T-\lambda I
is closed and at least one of
\mathrm{nul}\,T
and
\mathrm{nul}\,T^{*}
is finite, then both are finite and
\mathrm{ind}\,T=0
. (Although this is not the original definition of biquasitriangularity, it is equivalent to it by a deep result of Apostol, Foiaş and Voiculescu
[
1
]
.).

We adopt the standard notation:
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
,
\mathbb{T}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
, and for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
1\leq n<\infty
, we write
A^{(n)}:=A\oplus A\oplus\cdots\oplus A
(
n
times).

In a recent article
[
19
]
, the first, second and fourth authors characterised the biquasitriangular operators which lie in the closure
\textsc{clos}\,(\mathfrak{C}_{\mathfrak{E}})
of the set
\mathfrak{C}_{\mathfrak{E}}:=\{EF-FE:E,F\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),E=E^{2},F=F^{2}\}
of commutators of idempotent operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. While investigating the question of which
non
-biquasitriangular operators lie in
\textsc{clos}\,(\mathfrak{C}_{\mathfrak{E}})
, they demonstrated that if
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the unilateral forward shift (i.e. if there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathcal{H}
with respect to which
Se_{n}=e_{n+1}
for all
n\geq 1
) and
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, then
\mu S\in\textsc{clos}(\mathfrak{C}_{\mathfrak{E}})
if and only if
|\mu|\leq\frac{1}{2}
. A key step in this proof of this result is the fact that the unilateral shift operator
S
is unitarily equivalent to the operator
J_{2}(S):=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
S&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
.

In the present article, we investigate which other Hilbert space operators satisfy this and closely related conditions. A principal reason for our being interested in operators of the form
J_{2}(T)
(or more generally of the form
J_{n}(T)
as defined below) – other than their relation to the set of commutators of idempotent operators mentioned above – is their connection to a problem of Kaplansky
[
17
]
originally regarding abelian groups, and later adapted to operator theory by Azoff
[
4
]
. Kaplansky considered two abelian groups
G
and
H
such that
G\oplus G
is (group) isomorphic to
H\oplus H
, and asked whether it necessarily follows that
G
is isomorphic to
H
? He recognised that analogues of this problem “can be formulated for very general mathematical systems”. Azoff’s question asks: if
A
and
B
are two Hilbert space operators and
A\oplus A
is equivalent to
B\oplus B
(in some sense to be made precise), is
A
equivalent to
B
? In the finite-dimensional setting, the most important notions of equivalence of operators are unitary equivalence and similarity. As we shall now see, the answer to Azoff’s question is “yes” in both cases.

1.2.

Definition.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. We say that
A
and
B
are
unitarily equivalent
if there exists a unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
B=U^{*}AU
, in which case we write
A\simeq B
. We say that
A
and
B
are
approximately unitarily equivalent
if there exists a sequence
(U_{n})_{n}
of unitary operators such that
B=\lim_{n}U_{n}^{*}AU_{n}
, in which case we write
A\simeq_{a}B
. Finally, we say that
A
and
B
are
similar
, and we write
A\sim B
, if there exists an invertible operator
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
B=S^{-1}AS
.

It is (very) well-known that these define equivalence relations on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Note that
A
and
B
are approximately unitarily equivalent if and only if each belongs to the closure of the unitary orbit of the other. (We can also define a relation on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by asking that
A
belong to the closure of the similarity orbit of
B
, but this relation is not symmetric and we do not discuss it here.)

1.3.

When
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, the unitary group of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is compact, and therefore the relations of unitary equivalence and of approximate unitary equivalence coincide. Furthermore, in this setting, both unitary equivalence and similarity admit a notion of “multiplicity”, and so we can “recover” the operator
A
from
A\oplus A
. For example, if
A\oplus A
is unitarily equivalent to
B\oplus B
, then it is routine to verify the trace condition

\mathrm{tr}(w((A\oplus A),(A\oplus A)^{*}))=\mathrm{tr}(w((B\oplus B),(B\oplus B)^{*}))

holds for all words
w(x,y)
in two non-commuting variables
x
and
y
. But then

\mathrm{tr}(w(A,A^{*}))=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(w((A\oplus A),(A\oplus A)^{*}))=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(w((B\oplus B),(B\oplus B)^{*}))=\mathrm{tr}(w(B,B^{*}))

for all words
w(x,y)
as above, and thus a theorem of Specht
[
26
]
implies that
A
is unitarily equivalent to
B
.
If
A\oplus A
is similar to
B\oplus B
, then these two operators share a common Jordan form, and thus so do
A
and
B
(as the Jordan form of
A
admits the same Jordan blocks as that of
A\oplus A
, only with half the multiplicities, and an analogous statement holds regarding
B\oplus B
and
B
). It follows that
A
is similar to
B
.

When
\mathcal{H}
is infinite-dimensional and separable, the answer to Azoff’s question is still “yes” for unitary equivalence (Kadison and Singer
[
16
]
) and for approximate unitary equivalence (c.f. Proposition
4.4
below). It is not known, however, whether the same conclusion holds when the equivalence relation under consideration is
similarity
. That is, it is not known whether the existence of an invertible operator
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
for which
S^{-1}(A\oplus A)S=(B\oplus B)
implies the existence of an invertible operator
R\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
R^{-1}AR=B
,
even in the case where
A
(and therefore
B
) is compact.

Given
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, let us define
J_{2}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
A&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
. Observe that
J_{2}(A)
is a square root of
A\oplus A
, as
J_{2}(A)^{2}=A\oplus A
. It follows that if
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
J_{2}(A)
is similar to
J_{2}(B)
, then clearly
A\oplus A
is similar to
B\oplus B
. Whereas Azoff’s question remains open for compact operators
A
and
B
, as we shall see below, when
A
is compact, the special structure of
J_{2}(A)
as a “primitive” square root of
A\oplus A
will allow us to prove that if
A
is compact and
J_{2}(A)
is similar to
J_{2}(B)
, then
A
is similar to
B
(Theorem
4.10
below).

Given
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we may generalise the notion of the square root
J_{2}(A)
of
A\oplus A
as follows.

1.4.

Definition.

Let
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Given
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we define

J_{n}(T):=\begin{bmatrix}0&I&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&I&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&0&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\cdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&I\\
T&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{(n)}),

and we refer to this as the
primitive
n^{th}
root
of
T^{(n)}
.
We then say that
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is

•

\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-
stable
if
T\simeq J_{n}(T)
;

•

\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-
stable
if
T\simeq_{a}J_{n}(T)
;

•

\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-
stable
if
T\sim J_{n}(T)
;

The choice of the adjective “primitive” is a nod to the fact that if
I
denotes the identity operator in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, then
\sigma(I)=\{1\}
, whereas
J_{n}(I)\simeq\oplus_{k=1}^{n}\omega^{k}I
, and thus
\sigma(J_{n}(I))=\{\omega^{k}:1\leq k\leq n\}
, where
\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}
is a primitive
n^{th}
root of
1
.
We also direct the reader’s attention to Lemma
2.17
for further justification of this terminology.

In Section Two below, we investigate the basic spectral properties of
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stable operators, and we show that if
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stable (in any of the above three ways), then
\sigma(T)
is either
\mathbb{T}
or
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. In Section Three, we exhibit a host of examples of non-unitarily equivalent, cyclic unitary operators, each of which is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable. To do so, we must embark on a detailed analysis of the corresponding spectral measures of these unitary operators. In Section Four, we study our variant of the Kaplansky/Azoff problem, obtaining the result mentioned above that if
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
A
is compact, then
J_{2}(A)
similar to
J_{2}(B)
implies that
A
is similar to
B
. Finally, in Section Five, we show that this last result cannot be generalised in a purely algebraic way. More specifically, we reproduce an example given to us by J. Bell of a unital ring
R
and two elements
x,y\in R
such that
J_{2}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
x&0\end{bmatrix}
is conjugate in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(R)
to
J_{2}(y)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
y&0\end{bmatrix}
, yet
x
is not conjugate to
y
.

Some results we shall mention below apply equally well to all notions of
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stability. To avoid repetition, when proving such results we shall write that
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{x}]}
-stable, meaning that
[\textsc{x}]\in\{[\textsc{u}],[\textsc{au}],[\textsc{s}]\}
.

1.5.

Remark.

The study of
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stability of operators extends the work – initiated by Conway, Prǎjiturǎ and Rodriguez-Martínez
[
11
]
– of those operators
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
T^{2}
is similar (or unitarily equivalent) to
T^{(2)}
. Since
J_{n}(T)^{n}=T^{(n)}
, it follows that if
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable, then
T^{n}\simeq T^{(n)}
, and analogous statements hold regarding
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stability and
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stability.

Moreover, it is clear that if
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable, then
T
is both
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable and
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable. In general, neither
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stability nor
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stability implies
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stability, as we shall see.

1.6.

Example.

Having defined various notions of “stability” of operators, it is worthwhile verifying that the various sets of
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stable operators are non-empty.

If
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is any unitary operator satisfying
\sigma(U)=\mathbb{T}
, then
U
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable for all
n\geq 2
. This is because
J_{n}(U)
is again a unitary operator with
\sigma(J_{n}(U))=\mathbb{T}
, and any two unitary operators with spectrum equal to
\mathbb{T}
are approximately unitarily equivalent by the Weyl-von Neumann-Berg Theorem
[
12
, Theorem II.4.4]
.

To see that
\sigma(J_{n}(U))=\mathbb{T}
, note that if
A\simeq_{a}B
, then a routine calculation shows that
J_{n}(A)\simeq_{a}J_{n}(B)
for any
n\geq 2
. As such, using the Weyl-von Neumann-Berg Theorem, we may assume without loss of generality that
U=\textsc{diag}(u_{m})_{m}
, where
\{u_{m}\}_{m}
is dense in
\mathbb{T}
. With
U
diagonal as above,

J_{n}(U)\simeq\oplus_{m}\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&0&\ddots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\cdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
u_{m}&0&0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Clearly
J_{n}(U)
is unitary, and
\sigma(J_{n}(U))
contains all
n^{th}
roots of
u_{m}
for each
m\geq 1
. Moreover, the set of all such
n^{th}
roots of
\{u_{m}\}_{m}
is dense in
\mathbb{T}
, since
\{u_{m}\}_{m}
itself is. Thus
J_{n}(U)
is a unitary operator whose spectrum is the circle, whence
J_{n}(U)\simeq_{a}U
.

1.7.

Example.

It is routine to verify that the unilateral forward shift operator
S
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable (and thus both
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable and
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable) for all
n\geq 2
. The computation is omitted.

1.8.

Example.

There exists an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable for all
n\in 2\mathbb{N}
, but
T
is not
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for any
n\in 2\mathbb{N}
.

Let
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
, and
S
be the unilateral forward shift operator with respect to
\{e_{k}\}_{k}
(i.e.
Se_{k}=e_{k+1}
for all
k\geq 1
). Define an invertible diagonal operator
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by

De_{k}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}e_{k}&\mbox{ if }k\mbox{ is odd}\\
2e_{k}&\mbox{ if }k\mbox{ is even.}\end{cases}

Set
T:=D^{-1}SD
. It is straightforward to check that
T^{2}\simeq S\oplus S
is an isometry.
Since
S
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable and
J_{2m}(T)=(D^{-1})^{(2m)}J_{2m}(S)D^{(2m)}
for all
m\geq 1
, it follows that
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable for all
n\in 2\mathbb{N}
.

If
T\simeq J_{2n}(T)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
T^{2n}\simeq T^{(2n)}
.
This is impossible as
T^{2n}
is an isometry, whereas
T^{(2n)}
is not.
Hence,
T
is not
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for any
n\in 2\mathbb{N}
.

2.
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stability

2.1.

We begin with an examination of the spectral properties shared by
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stable operators. It is worth noting that these properties may often be derived by observing that if
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable (resp.
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable,
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable), then
T^{n}\simeq T^{(n)}
(resp.
T^{n}\simeq_{a}T^{(n)}
,
T^{n}\sim T^{(n)}
). This last property (for unitary equivalence and similarity) was originally investigated for the case where
n=2
in the paper
[
11
]
by Conway, Prǎjiturǎ and Rodríguez-Martínez, who referred to these as
\textsc{Property }U
and
\textsc{Property }S
respectively. Amongst (many) other things, they proved that the only compact operator
K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
which satisfies
K^{2}\sim K^{(2)}
is the zero operator.

2.2.

Proposition.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
T^{n}\equiv T^{(n)}

(
where
\equiv
denotes one of
\simeq,\simeq_{a}
or
\sim
)
, then
T^{n^{m}}\equiv T^{(n^{m})}
for all
m\geq 1
. Consequently,

\sigma(T)=\sigma(T)^{n^{m}}\mbox{\ \ \ \ \ for all }m\geq 1.

In particular, if
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{x}]}
-stable, then
\sigma(T)=\sigma(T)^{n^{m}}
for all
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

We argue the case where
\equiv
denotes approximate unitary equivalence. The proof is an easy adaptation of Proposition 2.7 of
[
11
]
, as are the corresponding proofs for the other two cases.

In this case we see that

T^{n^{2}}=(T^{n})^{n}\simeq_{a}(T^{(n)})^{n}=(T^{n})^{(n)}\simeq_{a}(T^{(n)})^{(n)}=T^{(n^{2})},

and by an easy induction argument,

T^{n^{m}}\simeq_{a}T^{(n^{m})}\mbox{ for all }m\geq 1.

From this it follows that for all
m\geq 1
,

\sigma(T)^{n^{m}}=\sigma(T^{n^{m}})=\sigma(T^{(n^{m})})=\sigma(T).

That the last statement of the proposition holds is clear from Paragraph
2.1
.

\Box

2.3.

Proposition.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
, and suppose that
T^{n}\equiv T^{(n)}

(
where
\equiv
denotes one of
\simeq,\simeq_{a}
or
\sim
)
. Then

(a)

\mathrm{spr}(T)\in\{0,1\}
; and

(b)

if
T
is invertible, then
\sigma(T)\subseteq\mathbb{T}
.

In particular, these properties hold if
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{x}]}
-stable, where
[\textsc{x}]\in\{[\textsc{u}],[\textsc{au}],[\textsc{s}]\}
.

Proof.

(a)

Using Proposition
2.2
, we find that

0\leq\mathrm{spr}(T)=\mathrm{spr}(T^{n})=\mathrm{spr}(T)^{n},

from which the result clearly follows.

(b)

By part (a) above,
\sigma(T)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Suppose that
\alpha\in\sigma(T)
and that
0<|\alpha|<1
. By Proposition
2.2
,

\alpha^{n^{m}}\in\sigma(T)^{n^{m}}=\sigma(T),

and since
\lim_{m}\alpha^{n^{m}}=0
and
\sigma(T)
is closed, we have that
0\in\sigma(T)
. Thus the invertibility of
T
, combined with the hypothesis that
T^{n}\equiv T^{(n)}
, implies that
\sigma(T)\cap\mathbb{D}=\varnothing
. Hence
\sigma(T)\subseteq\mathbb{T}
.

\Box

2.4.

Lemma.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(a)

If
A\simeq B
, then
J_{n}(A)\simeq J_{n}(B)
.

(b)

If
A\simeq_{a}B
, then
J_{n}(A)\simeq_{a}J_{n}(B)
.

(c)

If
A\sim B
, then
J_{n}(A)\sim J_{n}(B)
.

Proof.

Since all three proofs are essentially the same, we shall only prove (c).

If
A\sim B
, say
B=R^{-1}AR
for some
R\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
invertible, we set
S=R^{(n)}
and note that
S
is invertible in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{(n)})
. A simple computation then shows that

J_{n}(B)=S^{-1}J_{n}(A)S,

whence
J_{n}(B)\sim J_{n}(A)
.

\Box

We next prove the analogue of Proposition
2.2
for
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stable operators.

2.5.

Proposition.

Let
R,T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, and let
[\textsc{x}]\in\{[\textsc{u}],[\textsc{au}],[\textsc{s}]\}
.

(a)

Let
m,n\geq 1
. Then
J_{m}(J_{n}(R))\simeq J_{mn}(R)
.

(b)

If
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{x}]}
-stable for some
n\geq 2
, then
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n^{m}}^{[\textsc{x}]}
-stable for all
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

(a)

This is a routine computation which is omitted.

(b)

We argue by induction on
m
. Let
[\textsc{x}]=[\textsc{s}]
. By hypothesis, the result holds for
m=1
. Suppose that it holds for
m=k\geq 1
. Then by part (a) and Lemma
2.4
(c),

T\sim J_{n^{k}}(T)\sim J_{n^{k}}(J_{n}(T))\simeq J_{n^{k+1}}(T).

That is, the result holds for

. By induction, the result holds for all
m\geq 1
.

The cases where
[\textsc{x}]=[\textsc{u}]
or
[\textsc{x}]=[\textsc{au}]
are handled in a similar manner.

\Box

2.6.

Proposition.

Let
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable. Then

(a)

\|T^{m}\|=\|T\|
, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(b)

The spectral radius of
T
is
\mathrm{spr}(T)=1
.

Proof.

(a)

First observe that
\|J_{n}(T)\|\geq 1
for any
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and any
n\geq 2
. Thus if
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable, then
\|T\|\geq 1
.

We shall argue by induction on
m
. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P(m)
be the statement that
\|T^{m}\|=\|T\|
. Obviously
P(1)
is true. Now fix
2\leq m\in\mathbb{N}
, and suppose that
P(j)
is true,
1\leq j\leq m-1
.

Choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(k-1)n<m\leq kn
. A routine calculation shows that
(J_{n}(T))^{m}
admits an operator-matrix form
[X_{ij}]\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{(n)})
, each of whose rows and columns contains exactly one non-zero entry, and that entry is either of the form
T^{k-1}
or
T^{k}
with
T^{k}
appearing
m-(k-1)n
times amongst the non-zero entries. Hence, whether
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable or
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{au}]}
-stable,

\|T^{m}\|=\max(\|T^{k-1}\|,\|T^{k}\|).

If
k=1
, then
\|T^{k-1}\|=\|I\|=1\leq\|T\|=\|T^{k}\|
, so
\|T^{m}\|=\|T\|
. If
k>1
, then we note that
k<m
, and thus by our induction step,
\|T^{m}\|=\max(\|T^{k-1}\|,\|T^{k}\|)=\|T\|
.

This completes the induction and the proof of (a).

(b)

By part (a),
\|T\|\geq 1
, and so
\|T\|\neq 0
. Applying Beurling’s Spectral Radius Formula, we find that

\mathrm{spr}(T)=\lim_{m}\|T^{m}\|^{\frac{1}{m}}=\lim_{m}\|T\|^{\frac{1}{m}}=1.

\Box

2.8.

Proposition.

Let

and suppose that

(a)

and

.

(b)

If
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{x}]}

\sigma(T)
has circular symmetry.

(c)

If
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{x}]}
-stable and invertible, then

.

Proof.

(a)

Since

is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable, for each

is unitarily equivalent to

by Proposition

. Let

Then

is clearly unitary and

Of course,

implies that
T\simeq\theta_{m}\,T\simeq\theta_{m}\,(\theta_{m}\,T)=\theta_{m}^{2}\,T
, and

for all

. But
\theta_{m}^{k}=\theta_{m}^{k+n^{m}}

for all

,

.

(b)

•

It is routine to verify that by replacing each instance of
\simeq
by

in the proof of (a) above, we arrive at the conclusion that if

But the set

From this it easily follows that if

In particular, if

is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable, then
\sigma(T)
has circular symmetry.

•

If
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable, then
T\sim J_{n^{m}}(T)
for all

, again by Proposition
2.5
. Let
V_{m}
denote the unitary operator from (a). Then

T\sim J_{n^{m}}(T)\simeq V_{m}^{*}J_{n^{m}}(t)V_{m}=\theta_{m}J_{n^{m}}(T)\sim\theta_{m}T,

whence
T\sim\theta_{m}T\sim\theta_{m}(\theta_{m}T)=\theta_{m}^{2}T
, and as before, by a simple induction argument,

for all

.
Thus

is invariant under rotation by
\theta_{m}^{k}
for all

has circular symmetry.

(c)

This follows from (b), together with Proposition
2.3
.

\Box

2.9.

Proposition.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
n\geq 2
be an integer. Suppose that
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable. Then

(a)

\sigma(T)=\mathbb{T}
, if
T
is invertible; and

(b)

\sigma(T)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, if
T
is not invertible.

Proof.

(a)

This is Proposition
2.8
(c).

(b)

Since
T\sim J_{n}(T)
, there exists an invertible operator
R
, such that
T=RJ_{n}(T)R^{-1}
.

Step One.
Since
0\in\sigma(T)
, it follows that either
T
is not bounded below, or that the range of
T
is not
dense. In the latter case, observe that
T^{*}
is not bounded below.
Since
T\sim J_{n}(T)
if and only if
T^{*}\sim J_{n}(T^{*})
, and since
\sigma(T^{*})=\sigma(T)^{*}=\{\overline{\lambda}:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}
, it suffices to consider the case where
T
is not bounded below, which we now do.

Fix
0<\lambda<1
. For each
m\geq 1
, we can find
x_{m}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\|x_{m}\|=1
but
\|Tx_{m}\|\leq\lambda^{n^{m}}
.
Noting that
T\sim J_{n}(T)
implies that
T\sim J_{n^{m}}(T)
for all
m\geq 1
, we set

y_{m}:=(\lambda x_{m},\lambda^{2}x_{m},\cdots,\lambda^{n^{m}-1}x_{m},\lambda^{n^{m}}x_{m})^{t}\in(\mathcal{H})^{(n^{m})}.

Then

\|y_{m}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n^{m}}\lambda^{2k}=\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-(\lambda^{2})^{n^{m}+1})}{1-\lambda^{2}}.

Note that
\lim_{m}\|y_{m}\|^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1-\lambda^{2}}
, which is obviously independent of
m
.

Moreover,

(J_{n^{m}}(T)-\lambda I)y_{m}=(0,0,\cdots,0,\lambda(Tx_{m}-\lambda^{n^{m}}x_{m}))^{t},

implying that

\|(J_{n^{m}}(T)-\lambda I)y_{m}\|<\lambda(\lambda^{n^{m}}+\lambda^{n^{m}})=2\lambda^{n^{m}+1}<2\lambda^{n^{m}}.

Step Two.
Next, observe that as
J_{n}(T)=R^{-1}TR
, for each
m\geq 1
, there exists an invertible operator

R_{m}
such that

J_{n^{m}}(T)=R_{m}^{-1}TR_{m},

and
\|R_{m}\|\leq\|R\|^{m}
,
\|R_{m}^{-1}\|\leq\|R^{-1}\|^{m}
.

It follows that

\|R_{m}^{-1}(T-\lambda I)R_{m}y_{m}\|<2\lambda^{n^{m}}.

Now
R
is bounded below by
\frac{1}{\|R^{-1}\|}
,
and thus
R_{m}
is bounded below by
\frac{1}{\|R^{-1}\|^{m}}
,
implying that if
z_{m}:=R_{m}y_{m}
, then

\|z_{m}\|\geq\frac{1}{\|R^{-1}\|^{m}}\|y_{m}\|.

Similarly,
R^{-1}
is bounded below by
\frac{1}{\|R\|}
,
and thus
R_{m}^{-1}
is bounded below by
\frac{1}{\|R\|^{m}}
.

Thus (for some
m
sufficiently large),

\displaystyle 2\lambda^{n^{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\|R_{m}^{-1}(T-\lambda I)R_{m}y_{m}\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\|R\|^{m}}\|(T-\lambda I)z_{m}\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\|R\|^{m}}\frac{1}{\|R^{-1}\|^{m}}\ \sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2(1-\lambda^{2})}}\ \|(T-\lambda I)[\frac{z_{m}}{\|z_{m}\|}]\|.

In other words, (for
m
sufficiently large),

\|(T-\lambda I)[\frac{z_{m}}{\|z_{m}\|}]\|\leq\|R\|^{m}\|R^{-1}\|^{m}\ \sqrt{\frac{2(1-\lambda^{2})}{\lambda^{2}}}\ 2\lambda^{n^{m}}.

But

\underset{m}{\lim}\|R\|^{m}\|R^{-1}\|^{m}\cdot\lambda^{n^{m}}=0.

This proves that
T-\lambda I
is not bounded below, so
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
.
Thus,
\sigma(T)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, since
\sigma(T)
has circular symmetry and
\mathrm{spr}(T)=1
(by Proposition
2.3
).

\Box

Since

-stability obviously implies
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stability, the next result is immediate.

We now prove a couple of results which will be useful in analysing the examples of the next section.

Our first Lemma is well-known; we include its proof for the convenience of the reader. Recall that if
\mathcal{H}_{1}

are complex Hilbert spaces,

[

.

2.16.

Lemma.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two complex Hilbert spaces. Let
A_{1},B_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
A_{2},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
. Suppose that
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are two non-empty, disjoint, compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
and that
\sigma(A_{1})=\Omega_{1}=\sigma(B_{1})
, while
\sigma(A_{2})=\Omega_{2}=\sigma(B_{2})
.

If
A_{1}\oplus A_{2}
is similar to
B_{1}\oplus B_{2}
, then
A_{j}
is similar to
B_{j}
,

.

Proof.

Suppose that
T=[T_{ij}]
is invertible and
T(A_{1}\oplus A_{2})=(B_{1}\oplus B_{2})T
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}A_{1}&T_{2}A_{2}\\
T_{3}A_{1}&T_{4}A_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}\\
T_{3}&T_{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&A_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
0&B_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}\\
T_{3}&T_{4}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}T_{1}&B_{1}T_{2}\\
B_{2}T_{3}&B_{2}T_{4}\end{bmatrix}.

From this it follows that

and that

and

are invertible. But then

and

implies that

is similar to

and
A_{2}
is similar to
B_{2}
.

3.
Examples

3.1.

We now turn our attention to the question of determining which normal operators are
\textsc{j}_{n}
-stable.

Suppose that
N\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is normal and
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for some
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
J_{n}(N)
is normal, and thus

I^{(n-1)}\oplus NN^{*}=J_{n}(N)J_{n}(N)^{*}=J_{n}(N)^{*}J_{n}(N)=N^{*}N\oplus I^{(n-1)}.

This shows that
NN^{*}=N^{*}N=I
, implying that
N
is in fact a unitary operator.

Unitary operators, and more generally normal operators, are described up to unitary equivalence by their spectrum and their spectral measures, and we shall investigate
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stability of unitary operators in terms of these. A key observation lies in the fact that any
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfies
T^{n}\simeq T^{(n)}
, which imposes a great deal of structure on the spectral measure of
T
when
T
is unitary.

We first concentrate on the case where the spectral measure is atomic; in other words, where the unitary operator is diagonalisable with respect to some orthonormal basis. The answer here is quite satisfactory.

3.2.

Notation.

Let
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
, we define

\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha):=\{\omega\in\mathbb{T}:\omega^{n^{k}}=\alpha\mbox{ for some }k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Observe that
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(1)
denotes all possible
n^{k}
-th roots of
1
, and in general,
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)
contains all
n^{j}
-th powers and
n^{j}
-th roots of
\alpha
,
j\geq 1
. Moreover, if
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
, then
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\beta)
if and only if
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)\cap\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\beta)\neq\varnothing
.

3.3.

Example.

It is now easy to verify the following “minimal” examples of unitary operators satisfying the condition
T^{(n)}\simeq T^{n}
for some
n\geq 2
(resp. for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
).

Given a sequence
\theta:=(\theta_{n})_{n}
of complex numbers of absolute value
1
, define the diagonal unitary operator
U_{\theta}:=\textsc{diag}(\theta_{n})_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\ell_{2})
. It is well-known that the
point spectrum

\sigma_{p}(U_{\theta})
(i.e. the set of
eigenvalues
of
\theta
) is just the set
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n}
. This last statement also holds for the
infinite ampliation

U:=U_{\theta}^{(\infty)}
of
U_{\theta}
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us denote by
\alpha^{\frac{1}{m}}
the
m
-th root of
\alpha

with the smallest argument
in
[0,2\pi)
.

(a)

Fix
2\leq n
. The set
\{\alpha^{n^{k}}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is clearly countable, and so we can find a sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n}
such that
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n}=\{\alpha^{n^{k}}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
Let
U:=U_{\theta}^{(\infty)}
. Then
U^{n}\simeq U^{(n)}
.

(b)

The set
\{\alpha^{m^{\pm 1}}:m\in\mathbb{N}\}
is also countable, and so we can find a sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n}
such that
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n}=\{\alpha^{m^{\pm 1}}:m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Let
U:=U_{\theta}^{(\infty)}
. Then
U^{n}\simeq U^{(n)}
for all
n\geq 1
.

3.4.

Lemma.

If
U
is a diagonal unitary and
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for a fixed
n\geq 2
, then every
\alpha\in\sigma_{p}(U)
has infinite multiplicity,
and all its
n^{k}
-th powers and all its
n^{k}
-th roots belong to
\sigma_{p}(U)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Since
U\simeq J_{n}(U)
implies that
U^{n}\simeq U^{(n)}
, we need only verify the last statement about the roots.
To do this, it suffices (by induction) to show that if
\alpha\in\sigma_{p}(U)
, then all
n
-th roots of
\alpha
are in
\sigma_{p}(U)
.

Let
V
be any
n^{th}
root of
U
. Then
\sigma_{p}(V)
contains at least one
n
-th root
\beta
of
\alpha
.
It follows from Lemma
2.17
that
\omega^{j}\beta\in\sigma_{p}(U)
for all
j
, where
\omega
is a primitive
n
-th root of 1.

\Box

3.5.

Proposition.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
and
U
be the diagonal unitary whose point spectrum is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)
with uniform infinite multiplicity. Then
U\simeq J_{n}(U)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.17
, we must show that

U\simeq\textup{diag}(V,\omega V,\cdots,\omega^{n-1}V),

where
V
is an
n
-th root of
U
. This follows from the structure of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)
and the fact that every eigenvalue of
V
has infinite multiplicity.

\Box

The diagonal unitary given in Proposition
3.5
turns out to be the building block for all
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable
diagonal
unitaries
U
. Let us denote it by
U_{n}(\alpha)
.

3.6.

Proposition.

Let
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
U
be any
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable diagonal unitary operator. Then there exists a (finite or countably infinite) set
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
U
is a direct sum

\oplus_{j}U_{n}(\alpha_{j}).

The direct summands are unique
(
up to permutation of the terms
)
.

Proof.

For any
\alpha\in\sigma_{p}(U)
, it follows from Lemma
3.4
that
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)\subset\sigma_{p}(U)
. Thus
U_{n}(\alpha)

is a summand of
U
.

If
\sigma_{p}(U)=\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)
, we are done. Otherwise, pick a
\beta\in\sigma_{p}(U)\setminus\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)
. We have seen that

\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)\cap\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\beta)=\varnothing
, and
U_{n}(\beta)
is another summand of
U
.
Now it is easy to take a maximal subset
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots\}
of
\sigma_{p}(U)

such that the sets
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha_{j})
are disjoint and their union is
\sigma_{p}(U)
. Since
every member
\alpha
of
\sigma_{p}(U)
uniquely determines
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(\alpha)
, we obtain the desired decomposition.

\Box

3.7.

We now consider a stronger assumption on a diagonal unitary operator
U
, namely: when is
U

\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
? To answer this question, we need only a
slight modification of our notation. If
\alpha
is in
\sigma_{p}(U)
for such a diagonal unitary, then all
n
-th powers and all
n
-th roots of
\alpha
are in
\sigma_{p}(U)
. Furthermore if
\omega
is any primitive
n
-th root of 1,
\omega\alpha\in\sigma_{p}(U)
. So, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
, let us denote by
\mathfrak{S}(\alpha)
the set of all
\omega\lambda
, where
\omega
is any

n
-th root of 1, and
\lambda
is any
m
-th power or any
j
-th root of
\alpha
.

With a reasoning similar to what we used in the case of fixed
n
, we can verify that
\mathfrak{S}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{S}(\beta)
if and only if

\mathfrak{S}(\alpha)
and
\mathfrak{S}(\beta)
intersect. Now the following proposition is easy to prove.

3.8.

Proposition.

Let
U
be a diagonal unitary operator and suppose that
U
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for all
n\geq 2
. Then
U
is a
(
finite or infinite
)
direct
sum
\oplus_{j}U_{j}
, where for each
j
,
\sigma_{p}(U_{j})=\mathfrak{S}(\alpha_{j})
for some
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{T}
with uniform infinite multiplicity.

3.9.

Example.

As a corollary to Proposition
3.6
, we have the following: there exist
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
unitary and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
U
is
\textsc{j}_{2}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable, but
\theta U
is not
\textsc{j}_{2}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable.

Let
U=I\oplus D
, where
D=\textsc{diag}(d_{n})_{n}
, and where
\{d_{n}\}_{n}
consists of all
2^{k}
-roots of unity repeated with infinite multiplicity. By Proposition
3.6
,
U
is
\textsc{j}_{2}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable. Let
\theta=e^{2\pi i/3}
, so that
\theta
is a third root of unity.

Then
V:=\theta U=\theta I\oplus\theta D
is diagonal, and
V
has
\theta
as an eigenvalue. As such, for
V
to be
\textsc{j}_{2}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable we require that
\theta^{\frac{1}{2}}=e^{2\pi i/6}
should also be an eigenvalue of
V
. But then

e^{2\pi i/6}=\omega_{k}^{j}e^{2\pi i/2^{k}}e^{2\pi i/3}\mbox{ for some }k\geq 1\mbox{ and }1\leq j\leq 2^{k},

where
\omega_{k}
is a primitive
2^{k}
-th root of unity.

Raising both terms to the power
2^{k}
, we see that

e^{2^{k}(2\pi i)/6}=e^{2^{k}(2\pi i)/3},

so that
e^{2^{k}(2\pi i)/6}=1
, whence
2^{k}(2\pi i)/6\in 2\pi i\,\mathbb{Z}
.

Thus
2^{k}/6\in\mathbb{Z}
, or equivalently
2^{k}\in 6\mathbb{Z}
, which is clearly impossible.

3.10.

Having described which
diagonal
unitary operators
U
are
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for some
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
, we now investigate the
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stability of unitary operators whose spectral measures are non-atomic.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
cyclic
if there exists
0\neq x\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{H}=\overline{\textup{span}}\{T^{n}x:n\geq 0\}
, where
T^{0}:=I
.
If
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a
cyclic
unitary operator, then –
by the Spectral Theorem for unitary operators – there exists a Borel probability measure
\nu
with support
\sigma(U)
such that

U
is unitarily equivalent to the multiplication operator
M_{z,\nu}
on
L^{2}(\sigma(U),\nu)
, where

M_{z,\nu}f(z)=zf(z),~~z\in\mathbb{T},f\in L^{2}(\sigma(U),\nu).

If, furthermore,
U
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable for some
n\geq 2
, then by Proposition
2.9
,
\sigma(U)
is equal to
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
.

3.11.

Theorem.

Let
U
be a cyclic unitary operator with
\sigma(U)=\mathbb{T}
and
\nu
be the Borel probability measure corresponding to
U
as above. Suppose that
\nu
is a non-atomic measure. Let
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. Set
\mathbb{T}_{0}:=\{e^{2\pi i\theta}:\theta\in[0,\frac{1}{n}]\}
, and for
1\leq k\leq n-1
, set
\mathbb{T}_{k}=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{n}}\mathbb{T}_{0}
.

Given a Borel set
X\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{0}
, define
\nu_{k}(X):=\nu(e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{n}}\cdot X),k=0,1,\cdots,n-1
, where
\alpha\cdot X:=\{\alpha x:x\in X\}
.

Then
U
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable if and only if
\nu
satisfies the following conditions.

(a)

The Borel measures
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n-1}
on
\mathbb{T}_{0}
are mutually absolutely continuous with each other.

(b)

The measure
\nu_{0}
is mutually absolutely continuous with
\varphi
, where
\varphi
is a continuous measure with
\mathrm{supp}\,\varphi=\mathbb{T}_{0}
such that for any Borel set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{0}
,

\varphi(Y):=\nu(Y^{n}),

where
Y^{n}:=\{y^{n}:y\in Y\}
.

Proof.

Since
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stability is clearly invariant under unitary equivalence, we may assume without loss of generality that
U=M_{z,\nu}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\nu)
.
Let us denote by
\alpha^{\frac{1}{n}}
the
n
-th root of
\alpha

with the smallest argument
in
[0,2\pi)
.
Then
V:=M_{z^{\frac{1}{n}},\nu}
is a
n^{th}
root of
U
.
Consider the map
W:L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\nu)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{0},\varphi)
which sends
g(z)
to
g(z^{n})
. It is straightforward to check that
W

is a unitary operator which implements the unitary equivalence of
V
and
M_{z,\varphi}
, where
M_{z,\varphi}
is the multiplication operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{0},\varphi)
.

By Lemma
2.17
,
U
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable if and only if

U\simeq\textup{diag}(V,\omega V,\cdots,\omega^{n-1}V),

where
\omega=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}
is a primitive
n
-th root of 1. Note that
\mathbb{T}_{k}:=\omega^{k}\mathbb{T}_{0}
,
k=0,1,\cdots,n-1
.
Let
\varphi_{k}
be the Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
defined by
\varphi_{k}(Y)=\varphi(\omega^{-k}\cdot(Y\cap\mathbb{T}_{k}))
, where
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}
is a Borel set, and define
\eta=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{k}
.
Then
\eta
is a Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
.
Note that
\textup{diag}(V,\omega V,\cdots,\omega^{n-1}V)
is
unitarily equivalent to the multiplication operator
M_{z,\eta}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\eta)
, where
z\in\mathbb{T}
.
So
U
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable if and only if
M_{z,\nu}
is unitarily equivalent to
M_{z,\eta}
; that is, if and only if
\nu
is mutually absolutely continuous with
\eta
.

\Box

3.12.
A measure-theoretic interlude.

The above shows that the problem of characterising
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable cyclic non-atomic unitary operators is purely a measure-theoretic one.
Clearly, normalised Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
satisfies the measure-theoretic assumptions of Theorem
3.11
(and this, incidentally, yields an alternative way of showing that the bilateral shift operator is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable,
n\geq 2
). Our next goal is to show that Lebesgue measure is far from being unique in this respect. We next describe a construction of a continuum of
mutually singular
such measures based on self-similar measure theory (c.f. Theorem
3.14
). As a consequence, we conclude that there exists a continuum of unitary operators, no two of which are unitarily equivalent, yet each of which is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable.

We first need to recall some measure-theoretic notions.
Let
X
(resp.
Y
) be a metric space, and denote by
\mathfrak{Bor}(X)
(resp.
\mathfrak{Bor}(Y)
) the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets of
X
(resp. of
Y
). Given two Borel measures
\mu
and
\nu
, we write
\mu\asymp\nu
to indicate that
\mu
and
\nu
are mutually absolutely continuous with respect to each other.
Let
f
be a Borel measurable map from
X
to
Y
.
The
pushforward

f_{*}\mu
of a Borel measure
\mu
on
X
under the map
f
is the Borel measure on
Y
defined by
(f_{*}\mu)(B)=\mu(f^{-1}(B))
for all
B\in\mathfrak{Bor}(Y)
.

We also bring to the attention of the reader the fact that in measure-theory parlance, one says that a sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n}
of Borel measures on a metric space
X
is said to
converge weakly
to the Borel measure
\mu
on
X
if

\lim_{n}\mu_{n}(f):=\lim_{n}\int_{X}f\,d\mu_{n}=\int_{X}f\,d\mu=:\mu(f)

for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(X,\mathbb{C}):=\{f:X\to\mathbb{C}\mid f\mbox{ is continuous and bounded}\}
. (We mention this because if
X
is compact, this is what functional analysts normally refer to as
weak
∗
-
convergence
.)

The following result is known as the
Portmanteau Theorem
.

3.13.

Theorem.
[
6
, Theorem 2.2.5, Corollary 2.2.6, Theorem 2.4.1]

Suppose that we are given a sequence of finite Borel measures
(\mu_{n})_{n}
and a finite Borel measure
\mu
on a metric space
X
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

the sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
;

(ii)

for every closed set
F
in
X
, one has

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\sup}\,\mu_{n}(F)\leq\mu(F),

and
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mu_{n}(X)=\mu(X)
;

(iii)

for every open set
U
, one has

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\inf}\mu_{n}(U)\geq\mu(U),

and
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mu_{n}(X)=\mu(X)
;

(iv)

for every bounded, upper semicontinuous function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, one has

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim\sup}\int_{X}fd\mu_{n}\leq\int_{X}fd\mu,

and
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mu_{n}(X)=\mu(X)
;

(v)

for every Borel set
E
of
X
satisfying
\mu(\overline{E}\setminus(\mathrm{int}\,E))=0
, one has

\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mu_{n}(E)=\mu(E).

Our next goal is to obtain a continuum of non-atomic measures on
\mathbb{T}
which fulfill the assumptions of Theorem
3.14
, no two of which are mutually absolutely continuous with respect to one another. In so doing, we obtain a continuum of
\textsc{j}_{N}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable cyclic non-atomic unitary operators. In fact, there is no loss of generality in replacing the circle
\mathbb{T}
by the interval
[0,1]
, since one then simply considers the associated family of pushforwards of the measures on
[0,1]
by the continuous function
g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{T}
defined by
g(t)=e^{2\pi it}
.

3.14.

Theorem.

Let
N\geq 3
. There exist an uncountable set
\Omega
and a continuum of non-atomic Borel probability
measures
(\mu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Omega}
, each with support equal to
[0,1]
, such that

(i)

\mu_{\alpha}(A+\tfrac{i}{N})=\mu_{\alpha}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}([0,\frac{1}{N}])
and
1\leq i\leq N-1
.

(ii)

\mu_{\alpha}|_{[0,\tfrac{1}{N}]}
is mutually absolutely continuous to
f_{*}\mu_{\alpha}
, where
f(x)=\tfrac{x}{N}
.

(iii)

If
\alpha\neq\beta\in\Omega
, then
\mu_{\alpha}
and
\mu_{\beta}
are mutually singular.

The proof is constructive and we will exhibit a method for constructing a measure satisfying conditions (i) and (ii) of the above theorem as the weak limit of a sequence of specifically defined measures. First, we shall require a number of lemmas, definitions and notations.

Fix
N\geq 3

, and if we order the elements of

by length, then the same four properties hold for the elements of
B
.

We now consider an arbitrary but fixed subset
B\subseteq B_{0}
.
Our goal is to construct a non-atomic probability measure
\nu
(which is uniquely determined by
B
) with support
[0,1]
, and to associate to
\nu
a push-forward measure
\mu_{0}:=f_{*}\,\nu
under the function
f(x)=x/N
,
x\in[0,1]
. The measure
\mu_{0}
is then used to construct a measure satisfying conditions (i) and (ii) of Theorem
3.14
. The dependence of this construction upon the initial choice of
B\subseteq B_{0}
is what will guarantee that condition (iii) of Theorem
3.14
is met.

The construction of
\nu
is, alas, somewhat elaborate. It will be defined as a weak limit of convex combinations of certain non-atomic probability measures
L_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
defined on subintervals of
[0,1]
. However, in order to define the probability measures
L_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
, we shall first construct a non-atomic measure
\widetilde{\nu}
whose support is a Cantor-like subset
\mathcal{C}
(also uniquely determined by
B
) of
[0,1]
which avoids the forbidden words appearing in
B
. As we shall soon see, this measure
\widetilde{\nu}
is itself a weak limit of a sequence of weighted combinations of non-atomic probability measures
\widetilde{L}_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
defined on subintervals of
[0,1]
. In fact, the measures
\widetilde{L}_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
are nothing more than the usual Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}
restricted to the subinterval
\pi([\mathtt{i}])
and then normalised. We will then define
L_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}={F_{\mathtt{i}}}_{*}\widetilde{\nu}
as the push-forward measure of
\widetilde{\nu}
under the linear mapping
F_{\mathtt{i}}(x)=N^{-|\mathtt{i}|}x+\pi(\mathtt{i})
.

In order to improve the readability of the arguments below, we shall omit the subscript
B
from our sets, functions and measures until the proof of Theorem
3.14
.

We begin by describing the weights that will appear in the linear combinations of probability measures we shall require to obtain
\widetilde{\nu}
and subsequently
\nu
.

Given
\mathtt{i}=(i_{1}\,i_{2}\,\cdots\,i_{K})\in\Sigma_{*}
, we define

B(\mathtt{i})=\begin{cases}\{(i_{1}\,i_{2}\,\cdots\,i_{K}\,2)\}&\text{ if some }\mathtt{b}\in B\text{ is a suffix of }(i_{1}\,i_{2}\,\cdots\,i_{K}\,2)\\
\varnothing&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Observe that
B(\mathtt{i}^{-})=\{\mathtt{i}\}
precisely if
\mathtt{b}
is a suffix of
\mathtt{i}
for some
\mathtt{b}\in B
.
A necessary (but very insufficient) condition to have
B(\mathtt{i}^{-})=\{\mathtt{i}\}
is that
i_{K}=2
.

Given a set
Z
, let
\#Z
denote the cardinality of
Z
.
We define weight functions
p,\widetilde{p}:\Sigma_{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}:=\{t:0\leq t\in\mathbb{R}\}
(which again depends upon
B
) inductively by

\displaystyle p(\varnothing)=\widetilde{p}(\varnothing)=1\quad\text{,}\quad p(\mathtt{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}p(\mathtt{i}^{-})2^{-|\mathtt{i}|}&\text{if }B(\mathtt{i}^{-})=\{\mathtt{i}\}\\
p(\mathtt{i}^{-})\frac{1-\#B(\mathtt{i}^{-})\cdot 2^{-|\mathtt{i}|}}{N-\#B(\mathtt{i}^{-})}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }B(\mathtt{i}^{-})=\{\mathtt{i}\}\\
\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i}^{-})\frac{1}{N-\#B(\mathtt{i}^{-})}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Writing

p_{n}(\mathtt{i}|_{n})=\begin{cases}2^{-n}&\text{if }B(\mathtt{i}|_{n-1})=\{\mathtt{i}|_{n}\}\\
\frac{1-\#B(\mathtt{i}|_{n-1})\cdot 2^{-n}}{N-\#B(\mathtt{i}|_{n-1})}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

we obtain

p(\mathtt{i})=\prod_{k=1}^{|\mathtt{i}|}p_{k}(\mathtt{i}|_{k}).

A similar formula holds for
\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})
, replacing
2^{-n}
with
0
.

The weight functions are defined in such a way to allow for the following consistency relation.
Let
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}
.
If no element of
B
is a suffix of
\mathtt{i}2
, then
B((\mathtt{i}j)^{-})=B(\mathtt{i})=\varnothing
for all
0\leq j\leq N-1
, whence

(1)

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}p(\mathtt{i}\,j)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}p(\mathtt{i})\frac{1}{N}=p(\mathtt{i}),

whereas if there exists
\mathtt{b}\in B
which is a suffix of
\mathtt{i}2
, then

(2)

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}p((\mathtt{i}\,j))=\sum_{j\in\Sigma_{1}\setminus\{2\}}p(\mathtt{i})\frac{1-2^{-(K+1)}}{N-1}+p(\mathtt{i})2^{-(K+1)}=p(\mathtt{i}).

A similar consistency relation can be derived for
\widetilde{p}
.
Heuristically, the weights
p(\mathtt{i})
an
\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})
of any
word
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*}
get split amongst its successor words
\mathtt{i}j
.

Let
[\mathtt{i}]
be the set of (infinite) codings with initial sequence
\mathtt{i}
followed by arbitrary codings,

[\mathtt{i}]=\big\{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:i_{k}=j_{k}\text{ for all }k\leq|\mathtt{i}|\big\}.

The set
[\mathtt{i}]
is clearly independent of
B
and
it is useful to keep in mind that
\pi([\mathtt{i}])
is a closed subinterval of
[0,1]
.

3.16.

Suppose that we are given non-atomic probability measures
\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
on the the interval
\pi([\mathtt{i}]):=[\pi(\mathtt{i}),\pi(\mathtt{i})+N^{-|\mathtt{i}|}]
,
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*}
. Then for each
K\geq 1
, one may define the probability measures

\nu_{K,\lambda}:=\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}p(\mathtt{i})\lambda_{\pi{([\mathtt{i}])}}\mbox{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ and \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\widetilde{\nu}_{K,\lambda}:=\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})\lambda_{\pi{([\mathtt{i}])}}

Heuristically, these measures split the measure at coding
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}
into its “children”

\mathtt{i}\mathtt{j}
(
\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}
) evenly, giving any forbidden word (of which there is at most one) exponentially small weight (in the case of
\nu_{K,\lambda}
) or zero weight (in the case of
\widetilde{\nu}_{K,\lambda})
.
Since
\nu_{K,\lambda}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{K,\lambda}
are non-atomic and
\pi([\mathtt{i}])\cap\pi([\mathtt{j}])

contains at most one point if
\mathtt{i},\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{K}
are distinct, we have

\nu_{K,\lambda}(\pi([\mathtt{i}]))=p(\mathtt{i})
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{K,\lambda}(\pi([\mathtt{i}]))=\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})

for all
K\geq|\mathtt{i}|
, independent of our choice of
\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
.
Perhaps surprisingly, the weak limit of
(\nu_{K,\lambda})_{K}
and of
(\widetilde{\nu}_{K,\lambda})_{K}
not only exist, but can be shown to be independent of the actual choice of
\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
(even without the assumption that each
\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
is non-atomic). We shall not require this, however, and so the proof is omitted.

The following estimates on the size of
p(\mathtt{i})
and
\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})
will prove useful below.

3.17.

Lemma.

Let
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*}
and let
p(\mathtt{i})
and
\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})
be as above.
Then, there exists a constant
C_{N}
only depending on
N
such that

p(\mathtt{i})\leq C_{N}\cdot(N-1)^{-|\mathtt{i}|}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})\leq(N-1)^{-|\mathtt{i}|}.

In particular,
p(\mathtt{i})\to 0
and
\widetilde{p}(\mathtt{i})\to 0
as
|\mathtt{i}|\to\infty
.

Proof.

By definition,

p(\mathtt{i})=\prod_{k=1}^{|\mathtt{i}|}p_{k}(\mathtt{i}|_{k})
and
for
1\leq k\leq|\mathtt{i}|
we have

p_{k}(\mathtt{i}|_{k})\leq\max\left(2^{-k},\tfrac{1}{N-1}\right)
.
Notice that
2^{-k}<\tfrac{1}{N-1}
for
k>\log_{2}(N-1)
and

2^{-k}\geq\tfrac{1}{N-1}
for
k\leq\log_{2}(N-1)
.
Now
2^{-k}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
and so
2^{-k}\leq\tfrac{N-1}{2}\tfrac{1}{N-1}
.
Thus,

\prod_{k=1}^{|\mathtt{i}|}p_{k}(\mathtt{i}|_{k})\leq\left(\tfrac{N-1}{2}\right)^{\log_{2}(N-1)}\prod_{k=1}^{|\mathtt{i}|}\tfrac{1}{N-1}=\left(\tfrac{N-1}{2}\right)^{\log_{2}(N-1)}\left(\tfrac{1}{N-1}\right)^{|\mathtt{i}|}.

The conclusion for
\widetilde{p}
follows as
p_{k}(\mathtt{i}|_{k})\leq(N-1)^{-1}
for all
k
.

\Box

3.18.

Keeping
B\subseteq B_{0}
and the family
\{\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i]})}:\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*}\}
fixed as above, our next goal is to show that the sequences
(\nu_{n,\lambda})_{n}
and
(\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\lambda})_{n}

of probability measures converge weakly to non-atomic probability measures
\nu_{\lambda}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
, respectively.

The Kantorovich-Rubinstein metric (also known as the Wasserstein metric)
W_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric on the set of probability measures on a metric space
[
28
]
.
We shall not need the general definition of this metric.
For our purposes, we only need to know the following two things.

•

Convergence of the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n}
with respect to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance implies weak convergence of that sequence (see e.g.
[
27
, Theorem 7.12]
).

•

If
\mu
and
\nu
are probability measures, there is the following equivalent formulation of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein metric in terms of Lipschitz functions
\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
with constant
1
(see
[
27
, Equation (7.1)]
):

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}\left(\int h(x)d\mu(x)-\int h(x)d\nu(x)\right).

In the next few results, we shall require the Cantor-like set:

\mathcal{C}:=\{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:\text{there does not exist }\mathtt{b}\in B\text{ such that }\mathtt{b}\text{ is a subword of }\mathtt{i}\}.

3.19.

Lemma.

Let
B\subseteq B_{0}
. Given non-atomic probability measures
\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
on the interval
\pi([\mathtt{i}]):=[\pi(\mathtt{i}),\pi(\mathtt{i})+N^{-|\mathtt{i}|}]
,
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*}
, let
(\nu_{n,\lambda})_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
(\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\lambda})_{n=1}^{\infty}
denote the corresponding sequences of probability measures constructed as above.
Then the weak limits
\nu_{\lambda}=\lim_{n}\nu_{n,\lambda}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}=\lim_{n}\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\lambda}
exist
and are non-atomic probability measures.
Further,
\nu_{\lambda}
is fully supported on
[0,1]
, whereas
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
is fully supported on
\pi(\mathcal{C})\subseteq[0,1]
.

Proof.

The convergence proofs for
\nu_{\lambda}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
are identical and we will only show the former. To aid the exposition, for the remainder of this Lemma we shall abbreviate the notation
\nu_{n,\lambda}
to
\nu_{n}
,
n\geq 1
.

Recall that the space of probability measures on
[0,1]
is itself compact with respect to the
weak-
*
topology it inherits as a subset of the dual space
(\mathcal{C}([0,1],\mathbb{C}))^{*}
of the space
\mathcal{C}([0,1],\mathbb{C})
of continuous, complex-valued functions on
[0,1]
.
In particular, it is complete in that topology.
Furthermore, since
[0,1]
is compact, measure-theoretic weak convergence of these measures (as defined in paragraph
3.12
) coincides with weak-
*
convergence in the functional-analytic sense.

Thus to prove that
(\nu_{n})_{n}
converges weakly to some measure
\nu_{\lambda}(=\nu_{B,\lambda})
on
[0,1]
, we see from above that it suffices to show that it is Cauchy with respect to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein metric.
To that end, we first calculate
W_{1}(\nu_{n},\nu_{n+1})
,
n\geq 1
.

Fix
K\geq 1
and consider the two measures
\nu_{K},\nu_{K+1}
.
Let
h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
.
The Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance between
\nu_{K}
and
\nu_{K+1}
becomes

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu_{K},\nu_{K+1})

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}\left(\int h(x)d\nu_{K}(x)-\int h(x)d\nu_{K+1}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}\left(\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\int_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}h(x)d\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}(x)-\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K+1}}\int_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}h(x)d\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\left(\int_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}h(x)d\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}(x)-\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}\int_{\pi([\mathtt{ij}])}h(x)d\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{ij}])}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\sup_{h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}\left(\int_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}h(x)d\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}(x)-\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}\int_{\pi([\mathtt{ij}])}h(x)d\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{ij}])}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\sup_{h\in\textup{Lip}_{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}\left(h(x(\mathtt{i}))p(\mathtt{i})-\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}^{N-1}h(x^{\prime}(\mathtt{ij}))p(\mathtt{ij})\right)

for some
x(\mathtt{i})\in\pi([\mathtt{i}])
and
x^{\prime}(\mathtt{ij})\in\pi([\mathtt{ij}])
.

Since
\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}p(\mathtt{ij})=p(\mathtt{i})
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\nu_{K},\nu_{K+1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}p(\mathtt{ij})|x(\mathtt{i})-x^{\prime}(\mathtt{ij})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}\sup_{x,y\in\pi([\mathtt{ij}])}p(\mathtt{ij})|x-y|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}\sum_{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{1}}p(\mathtt{ij})N^{-K-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{K}}p(\mathtt{i})N^{-K}=N^{-K}.

Thus, for
m>K
, we have

W_{1}(\nu_{K},\nu_{m})\leq W_{1}(\nu_{K},\nu_{K+1})+\dots+W_{1}(\nu_{m-1},\nu_{m})\leq N^{-K}+N^{-(K+1)}+\dots+N^{-m}\leq N^{-(K-1)},

and so
(\nu_{n})_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence with respect to
W_{1}
.
As argued above,
(\nu_{n})_{n}
converges weakly to some measure
\nu_{\lambda}
in the space of probability measures on
[0,1]
.
As noted above, the argument for
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
is identical.

We proceed to show that
\nu_{\lambda}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
are non-atomic.
Fix
x\in[0,1]
and
n\geq 1
.
As remarked above, there are at most two
\mathtt{i},\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{\infty}
with
\pi(\mathtt{i})=\pi(\mathtt{j})=x
.
Note also that for all
k>n
, we have
\nu_{k}(\mathrm{int}(\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}])))=\nu_{k}(\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}]))=p(\mathtt{i}|_{n})
.
By Theorem
3.13
and Lemma
3.17
,

\displaystyle\nu_{\lambda}(\left\{x\right\})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:\\
\pi(\mathtt{i})=x\end{subarray}}\nu_{\lambda}(\mathrm{int}(\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}])))

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:\\
\pi(\mathtt{i})=x\end{subarray}}\nu_{k}(\mathrm{int}(\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}])))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:\\
\pi(\mathtt{i})=x\end{subarray}}p(\mathtt{i}|_{n})\leq 2C_{N}(N-1)^{-n}.

The claim now follows for
\nu_{\lambda}
.
The case for
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
is similar and left to the reader.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset[0,1]
be open. Since
\pi(\Sigma_{\infty})=[0,1]
, there exists an infinite word
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{\infty}
such that
\pi(\mathtt{i})\in\mathcal{O}
.
Since
\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}])
is an interval of length
N^{-n}
, we must have
\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}])\subseteq\mathcal{O}
for large enough
n
.
Hence, by Theorem
3.13
,

\nu_{\lambda}(\mathcal{O})\geq\nu_{\lambda}(\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}]))\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\nu_{k}(\pi([\mathtt{i}|_{n}]))=p(\mathtt{i}|_{n})>0.

Thus,
\nu_{\lambda}

must have support
[0,1]
.

By considering open sets
\mathcal{O}
that intersect
\pi(\mathcal{C})
, it can similarly shown
that the support of
\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}
must contain
\pi(\mathcal{C})
.
Details are left to the reader.
To see that the support is exactly
\pi(\mathcal{C})
, consider the countable decomposition

\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}(\pi(\mathcal{C})^{c})\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{n}}\sum_{\mathtt{b}\in B}\widetilde{\nu}_{\lambda}(\pi([\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}]))\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{n}}\sum_{\mathtt{b}\in B}\widetilde{p}_{n+|\mathtt{b}|}(\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b})=0.

Hence our claim follows.

\Box

We now move from the abstract non-atomic measures
\lambda_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
above to the specific measures
\widetilde{L}_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
and
L_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}
that we require. We begin by considering the measures

\widetilde{L}_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}:=\frac{\chi_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}(x)}{\mathcal{L}(\pi([\mathtt{i}]))}\cdot d\mathcal{L}(x),\ \ \ \ \ \mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*},

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. By Lemma
3.19
, we find that the sequence
(\nu_{n,\widetilde{L}})_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges to a weak limit
\widetilde{\nu}_{\widetilde{L}}
which is defined on
\pi(\mathcal{C})
, the Cantor-like set avoiding forbidden words from
B
. Since we have now fixed
\widetilde{L}
, let us abbreviate
\widetilde{\nu}_{\widetilde{L}}
to
\widetilde{\nu}
, and
\widetilde{\nu}_{n,\widetilde{L}}
to
\widetilde{\nu}_{n}
,
n\geq 1
. Note that by Lemma
3.19
,
\widetilde{\nu}
is a non-atomic probability measure.

Next, we set
L_{\pi([\mathtt{i}])}:=F_{\mathtt{i}}{}_{*}\widetilde{\nu}
,
where
F_{\mathtt{i}}(x)=N^{-|\mathtt{i}|}x+\pi(\mathtt{i})
is the linear mapping that maps
[0,1]
into
\pi([\mathtt{i}])
.
A second application of Lemma
3.19
yields a non-atomic probability measure
\nu_{L}
with support
[0,1]
as the weak limit of the corresponding sequence
(\nu_{n,L})_{n}
. Again, since
L
is now fixed (since we fixed
\widetilde{L}
and thus
\widetilde{\nu}
), we abbreviate
\nu_{L}
to
\nu
, and
\nu_{n,L}
to
\nu_{n}
,
n\geq 1
. We then define
\mu_{0}:=f_{*}\nu
on
[0,\tfrac{1}{N}]
as the push-forward measure of
\nu
under
f(x)=x/N
, that is:

\mu_{0}(E)=\nu(N\cdot E)\text{ for all measurable }E.

(As always, we should keep in mind that
\nu
and thus that
\mu_{0}
depend upon our original choice of
B
.)

Now
\mu_{0}
is a non-atomic probability measure supported on
[0,\tfrac{1}{N}]
.
It will be convenient to think of
\mu_{0}
as a measure defined on
\mathbb{R}
, taking the value
0
for sets contained in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,\frac{1}{N}]
.
We construct
\mu
(
=\mu_{B}
) satisfying the translational invariance in condition (i) of Theorem
3.14
by setting

\mu:=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}g_{i}{}_{*}\mu_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}g_{i}{}_{*}(f_{*}\nu)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(g_{i}\circ f)_{*}\nu,

where
g_{i}(x)=x+\frac{i}{N}
,
i=0,1,\cdots,N-1
.
Observe that
\mu
is by construction a non-atomic Borel measure, supported on
[0,1]
.

Next we set up some additional notation.
For finite sequences
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{*}
, we write

\langle\mathtt{i}\rangle:=\big\{\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:i_{k}=j_{k}\text{ for all }k\leq|\mathtt{i}|\\
\text{ and if $\mathtt{j}=\mathtt{k}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{l}$ for some $\mathtt{b}\in B,\mathtt{k}\in\Sigma_{*},\mathtt{l}\in\Sigma_{\infty}$ then }|\mathtt{k}\mathtt{b}|\leq|\mathtt{i}|\big\}.

Thus if
\mathtt{j}\in\langle\mathtt{i}\rangle
, then
\mathtt{j}
admits
\mathtt{i}
as a prefix, and any element
\mathtt{b}\in B
which appears as a subword of
\mathtt{j}
appears only as a subword of the prefix
\mathtt{i}
.
To conform to this new notation, we may also write
\langle.\rangle=\mathcal{C}
for all codings not containing any subword
\mathtt{b}\in B
.

We write
\Lambda
for the set of codings that contain only finitely many forbidden words from
B
.
That is,

\Lambda=\langle.\rangle\cup\Bigg(\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{k}}\bigcup_{\mathtt{b}\in B}\langle\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}\rangle\Bigg).

We note here that the union above is disjoint by our careful choice of
B
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\mathtt{b}\in B
, define

E_{k,\mathtt{b}}=\{\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{k},\mathtt{j}\in\Sigma_{\infty}\}=\bigcup_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{k}}[\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}].

Clearly the boundary of
\pi(E_{k,\mathtt{b}})
is a finite set for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

\mathtt{b}\in B
as
\pi([\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}])
is an interval.

We claim that
\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\nu_{n}(\pi(E_{k,\mathtt{b}}))\leq 2^{-(k+|\mathtt{b}|)}.
To see this, let
\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{k}
, and consider
[\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}]
.
For
n\geq k+|\mathtt{b}|
, we obtain from the definition of
\nu_{n}
that

\nu_{n}(\pi([\mathtt{i}\mathtt{b}]))=p(\mathtt{ib})=\prod_{l=1}^{|\mathtt{i}|+|\mathtt{b}|}p_{l}(\mathtt{ib}|_{l})=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{k}p_{l}(\mathtt{ib}|_{l})\right)\,\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{k+|\mathtt{b}|}p_{l}(\mathtt{ib}|_{l})\right).

However, from paragraph
3.15
, we see that

•

for
1\leq l\leq k=|\mathtt{i}|
,
p_{l}(\mathtt{ib}|_{l})=p_{l}(\mathtt{i}|_{l})
;

•

for
k+1\leq l\leq k+|\mathtt{b}|-1
,

\nu_{\beta}
are not mutually absolutely continuous.
For this we introduce the sets:

\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n}=\left\{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{\infty}:\text{there are no forbidden words $\mathtt{b}$ in $\mathtt{i}$ after digit $n$}\right\}

and

\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n,k}=\left\{\mathtt{i}\mathtt{j}^{1}\mathtt{j}^{2}\cdots\mathtt{j}^{k}\mathtt{l}:\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{n},\mathtt{j}^{m}\in(\Sigma_{|\mathtt{b}|}\setminus\left\{\mathtt{b}\right\})\text{ for all }1\leq m\leq k,\mathtt{l}\in\Sigma_{\infty}\right\}.

Clearly,
\Lambda_{\mathtt{b}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n}
and

\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n}\subseteq\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n,k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now,

\displaystyle\nu_{\beta}(\pi(\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n,k}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathtt{j}^{m}\in(\Sigma_{|\mathtt{b}|}\setminus\left\{\mathtt{b}\right\})\\
1\leq m\leq k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n+|\mathtt{b}|k}p_{i}(\mathtt{i}\mathtt{j}^{1}\ldots\mathtt{j}^{k}|_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathtt{i}\in\Sigma_{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(\mathtt{i}|_{i})\right)\cdot\prod_{m=1}^{k}\left(1-p_{n+m|\mathtt{b}|}(\mathtt{i}\mathtt{j}^{1}\ldots\mathtt{j}^{k}|_{n+m|\mathtt{b}|})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{m=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1-2^{-(n+m|\mathtt{b}|)}}{N}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^{k}.

Thus
\nu_{\beta}(\pi(\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n}))\leq(1-1/(2N))^{k}
for all

and hence

\nu_{\beta}(\pi(\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n}))=0
.
Since

\Lambda_{\mathtt{b}}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Lambda_{\mathtt{b},n}

is a countable union, we necessarily have
\nu_{\beta}(\pi(\Lambda_{\mathtt{b}}))=0
, proving that the two measures
\nu_{\alpha}
and
\nu_{\beta}
are in fact singular to one another. Correspondingly,
\mu_{\alpha}
and
\mu_{\beta}
are also mutually singular.

By rescaling
\mu_{\alpha}
by
\frac{1}{N}
for all

, we can ensure that each
\mu_{\alpha}
is a probability measure.

\Box

For

4.
Around a problem of Kaplansky’s

4.1.

In his study of infinite abelian groups
[
17
]
, Kaplansky raised “
three test problems
” to test the usefulness of a structure theorem for abelian groups.
As Kaplansky himself noted: “
all three problems can be formulated for general mathematical systems
”.

In the context of Hilbert space operators, Kaplansky’s second problem becomes: if
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
A\oplus A
is equivalent to
B\oplus B
, is
A
equivalent to
B
? In essence, one is seeking for an appropriate multiplicity theory for the notion of equivalence involved. Of course, in order to solve Kaplansky’s second problem, one must first define what is meant by “equivalent”, and the three notions we have already defined – unitary equivalence, approximate unitary equivalence, and similarity are a good place to start. We begin with a result of Kadison and Singer
[
16
, Theorem 1]
.

4.2.

Theorem.

[
Kadison-Singer
]

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and suppose that
A\oplus A\simeq B\oplus B
. Then
A\simeq B
.

As pointed out in
[
24
, Proposition A.1]
, if
A^{(n)}\simeq B^{(n)}
for some
n\geq 2
, then
A\simeq B
.
As an immediate consequence of this result, we obtain:

4.3.

Corollary.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and suppose that there exists
n\geq 2
such that
J_{n}(A)\simeq J_{n}(B)
. Then
A\simeq B
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the above result (see also
[
4
,
24
]
), and the fact that
J_{n}(A)\simeq J_{n}(B)
implies that

A^{(n)}=J_{n}(A)^{n}\simeq J_{n}(B)^{n}=B^{(n)}.

\Box

More generally, this shows that
J_{n}(A)\simeq J_{n}(B)
for some
n\geq 2
if and only if
J_{n}(A)\simeq J_{n}(B)
for all
n\geq 2
.

We now argue that
A\oplus A

approximately
unitarily equivalent to
B\oplus B
implies that
A
is
approximately
unitarily equivalent to
B
. In fact, slightly more is true.

4.4.

Proposition.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, and suppose that
A^{(n)}\simeq_{a}B^{(n)}
for some integer
n\geq 2
. Then
A\simeq_{a}B
.

Proof.

First note that
C^{*}(A)\simeq^{*}C^{*}(A^{(n)})
via the
∗
-isomorphism
\theta_{1}(X)=X^{(n)}
for all
X\in C^{*}(A)
. Similarly, the map
\theta_{2}(Y)=Y^{(n)}
for all
Y\in C^{*}(B)
defines an isometric
∗
-isomorphism of
C^{*}(B)
onto
C^{*}(B^{(n)})
.

Also, since
A^{(n)}\simeq_{a}B^{(n)}
, the map
\varrho(A^{(n)})=B^{(n)}
extends to an isometric
∗
-isomorphism
\varrho
of
C^{*}(A^{(n)})
onto
C^{*}(B^{(n)})
which is approximately unitarily equivalent to the identity representation on
C^{*}(A^{(n)})
. In particular, therefore,

\mathrm{rank}\,(X^{(n)})=\mathrm{rank}\,\,\varrho(X^{(n)})\mbox{ for all }X\in C^{*}(A).

Let
\varphi:C^{*}(A)\to C^{*}(B)
be the map

\varphi=\theta_{2}^{-1}\circ\varrho\circ\theta_{1}.

Then
\varphi
is an isometric
∗
-isomorphism and

\varphi(A)=\theta_{2}^{-1}\circ\varrho\circ\theta_{1}(A)=\theta_{2}^{-1}\circ\varrho(A^{(n)})=\theta_{2}^{-1}(B^{(n)})=B.

It also follows that

(\varphi(X))^{(n)}=\theta_{2}\circ\varphi(X)=\varrho\circ\theta_{1}(X)=\varrho(X^{(n)})

for all
X\in C^{*}(A)
.

Thus for all
X\in C^{*}(A)
, we have

\displaystyle n\ \mathrm{rank}\,(\varphi(X))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rank}\,(\varphi(X)^{(n)})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rank}\,(\varrho(X^{(n)}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rank}\,(X^{(n)})

\displaystyle=n\ \mathrm{rank}\,(X),

and so
\mathrm{rank}\,\varphi(X)=\mathrm{rank}\,X
.

By Voiculescu’s Theorem
[
12
, Theorem II.5.8]
,
\varphi
is approximately unitarily equivalent to the identity map on
C^{*}(A)
. In particular,

B=\varphi(A)\simeq_{a}A.

\Box

4.5.

Remark.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space. We recall that an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
quasidiagonal
if there exists an increasing sequence
(P_{n})_{n}
of finite-rank projections tending in the strong operator topology to the identity operator such that

\lim_{n}\|P_{n}T-TP_{n}\|=0.

L. Brown
[
8
]
has demonstrated the remarkable result that there exist non-quasidiagonal operators
T_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),\ n\geq 2
such that
T_{n}^{(n)}
is quasidiagonal while
T_{n}^{(k)}
is not quasidiagonal for any
2\leq k<n
.

It is an easy consequence of Proposition
4.4
that we cannot write any such
T_{n}^{(n)}
in the form
Q^{(n)}
where
Q
is quasidiagonal, for then
T_{n}
would be approximately unitarily equivalent to
Q
, and any operator approximately unitarily equivalent to a quasidiagonal operator is itself quasidiagonal.

4.6.

Corollary.

Suppose that
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The following statements are equivalent.

(a)

A\simeq_{a}B
.

(b)

For all
n\geq 2
,
J_{n}(A)\simeq_{a}J_{n}(B)
.

(c)

For some
n\geq 2
,
J_{n}(A)\simeq_{a}J_{n}(B)
.

Proof.

The only implication that is not completely routine is that (c) implies (a), and so we suppose that for some
n\geq 2
,
J_{n}(A)\simeq_{a}J_{n}(B)
.

Since
J_{n}(A)\simeq_{a}J_{n}(B)
, it follows that
A^{(n)}\simeq(J_{n}(A))^{n}\simeq_{a}(J_{n}(B))^{n}\simeq B^{(n)}
. The result now follows from Proposition
4.4
.

\Box

Kaplansky’s second test problem in the context of similarity of Hilbert space operators reads as follows: suppose that
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
A\oplus A
is similar to
B\oplus B
. Is
A
similar to
B
?

At this level of generality, the answer is not yet known (unless
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional, in which case the Jordan form is easily seen to provide an affirmative solution to the problem). For certain classes of operators, the problem does indeed admit a positive solution. For example, if both
A
and
B
are normal, then similarity of
A\oplus A
to
B\oplus B
implies that they are unitarily equivalent as well
[
10
, Corollary IX.6.11]
, and the result follows from the previously cited result of Kadison and Singer
[
16
]
, or from general multiplicity theory for normal operators as outlined in
[
12
, Chapter II]
or
[
10
, Chapter IX.10]
.

We now consider a variant of Kaplansky’s second problem which imposes a somewhat stronger hypothesis on the operators
A
and
B
. Recalling that
J_{2}(A)
is a square root of
A\oplus A
and
J_{2}(B)
is a square root of
B\oplus B
, one may ask whether or not
J_{2}(A)
similar to
J_{2}(B)
implies that
A
is similar to
B
. The main result of this section of the paper asserts that the answer is yes if one of
A
and
B
is compact (in which case both are).

We first seek a structure theorem for invertible operators which implement the similarity of
J_{2}(A)
to
J_{2}(B)
.

4.8.

Lemma.

Let
X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and suppose that
Z:=\begin{bmatrix}X&Y\\
0&X\end{bmatrix}
is a Fredholm operator of index zero in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
. Then
X
is Fredholm of index zero in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

Let
z:=\pi(Z)=\begin{bmatrix}x&y\\
0&x\end{bmatrix}
denote the image of
Z
in the Calkin algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})/\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
, and observe that
z
is invertible. Write
z^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}
.

Then

\begin{bmatrix}xa+yc&xb+yd\\
xc&xd\end{bmatrix}=zz^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=z^{-1}z=\begin{bmatrix}ax&ay+bx\\
cx&cy+dx\end{bmatrix}.

In particular,
ax=1=xd
, and so
x
is invertible in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})/\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
, i.e.
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is Fredholm. Define
m:=\mathrm{ind}\,X\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
X\oplus X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is Fredholm of index
2m
. Since the Fredholm operators of any fixed index form an open set, there exists
\delta>0
such that if
W\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
and
\|W-(X\oplus X)\|<\delta
, then
W
is Fredholm of index
2m
.

Note that for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have that
Z
is similar to

Z_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon I&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}X&Y\\
0&X\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}I&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}X&\varepsilon Y\\
0&X\end{bmatrix}.

If
0<\varepsilon<\frac{\delta}{2\|Y\|+1}
, then
\|Z_{\varepsilon}-(X\oplus X)\|<\delta
, and so

0=\mathrm{ind}\,Z_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{ind}\,(X\oplus X)=2m.

In particular,
m=\mathrm{ind}\,X=0
.

\Box

4.9.

Lemma.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Suppose that
S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{3}&S_{4}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is invertible and that

J_{2}(B)=SJ_{2}(A)S^{-1}.

Then

(a)

S_{1}=S_{4}
,
S_{3}=S_{2}A=BS_{2}
, and
S_{1}A=BS_{1}
.

(b)

Writing
S^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}\\
T_{3}&T_{4}\end{bmatrix}
, we also have that
T_{1}=T_{4}
and
T_{3}=T_{2}B=AT_{2}
.

(c)

S_{i}T_{j}
commutes with
B
and
T_{j}S_{i}
commutes with
A
for all
1\leq i,j\leq 4
.

(d)

If
A
is compact, then
S_{1}
and
T_{1}
are Fredholm operators of index zero.

Proof.

(a)

We have
SJ_{2}(A)=J_{2}(B)S
, which means

\begin{bmatrix}S_{2}A&S_{1}\\
S_{4}A&S_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{3}&S_{4}\\
BS_{1}&BS_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

From this (a) immediately follows.

(b)

The proof is similar to that of (a) and is omitted.

(c)

The equation
SJ_{2}(A)=J_{2}(B)S
implies that
S(A\oplus A)=SJ_{2}(A)^{2}=J_{2}(B)^{2}S=(B\oplus B)S
, from which we find that
S_{i}A=BS_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 4
. Similarly,
AT_{j}=T_{j}B
,
1\leq j\leq 4
.

But then for
1\leq i,j\leq 4
, we get

(T_{j}S_{i})A=T_{j}(S_{i}A)=T_{j}(BS_{i})=(T_{j}B)S_{i}=(AT_{j})S_{i}=A(T_{j}S_{i}),

and

(S_{i}T_{j})B=S_{i}(T_{j}B)=S_{i}(AT_{j})=(S_{i}A)T_{j}=(BS_{i})T_{j}=B(S_{i}T_{j}).

(d)

From (a), we have that
S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{2}A&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}
. Clearly
S
is Fredholm of index zero, since it is invertible in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
. But
S_{2}A
is compact, and therefore

Z:=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
0&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}

is also a Fredholm operator of index zero. By Lemma
4.8
,
S_{1}
is Fredholm of index zero. A similar argument applied to
S^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&T_{2}\\
AT_{2}&T_{1}\end{bmatrix}
yields that
T_{1}
is Fredholm of index zero.

\Box

The next result is the main result of this section.

4.10.

Theorem.

Let
A\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and suppose that
J_{2}(A)
is similar to
J_{2}(B)
. Then
A
is similar to
B
.

Proof.

Under the given hypotheses, first observe that

B\oplus B=J_{2}(B)^{2}\sim J_{2}(A)^{2}=A\oplus A\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}),

from which it immediately follows that
B
is also compact.

By Lemma
4.9
, if
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is invertible and
J_{2}(B)=SJ_{2}(A)S^{-1}
, then

S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{2}A&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}

for an appropriate
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Furthermore, by Lemma
4.8
,
S_{1}
has Fredholm index zero, and by Lemma
4.9
,

S_{1}A=BS_{1}.

This has a number of interesting consequences.
For example, the equation
(\ast)
implies that
\ker\,S_{1}
is invariant for
A
. As a result of this, relative to
\mathcal{H}=(\ker\,S_{1})\oplus(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}
, we may write

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
0&A_{4}\end{bmatrix}.

One crucial observation for the argument below is that
0\not\in\sigma(A_{1})
. Indeed, since
A_{1}
acts on the finite-dimensional space
\ker\,S_{1}
, if
0\in\sigma(A_{1})
, then
0
must be an eigenvalue of
A_{1}
. If
0\neq x_{0}\in\ker\,A_{1}
, then
Ax_{0}=A_{1}x_{0}=0
, implying that
x_{0}\in\ker\,S_{1}
and
x_{0}\in\ker\,S_{2}A
. But then
\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in\ker\,S
, a contradiction.

Similarly, equation
(\ast)
implies that
\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1}
is invariant for
B
. Hence relative to
\mathcal{H}=(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})^{\perp}\oplus(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})
, we may write

B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
B_{3}&B_{4}\end{bmatrix}.

As a map from
\mathcal{H}=(\ker\,S_{1})\oplus(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}
to
\mathcal{H}=(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})^{\perp}\oplus(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})
, we may write

S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&S_{1}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix},

where
S_{1}^{\circ}:(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}\to\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1}
is invertible.

Then
S_{1}A=BS_{1}
, as a map from
\mathcal{H}=(\ker\,S_{1})\oplus(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}
to
\mathcal{H}=(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})^{\perp}\oplus(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})
, becomes

\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&S_{1}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
0&A_{4}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
B_{3}&B_{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&S_{1}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix},

or equivalently

\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&S_{1}^{\circ}A_{4}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&B_{4}S_{1}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix}.

In other words,
A_{4}\in\mathcal{B}((\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp})
is similar to
B_{4}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})
. Since
S_{1}
is Fredholm of index zero, we know that
\dim(\ker\,S_{1})=\dim\,(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})^{\perp}
and
\dim\,(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}=\dim(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})
, and so we can find a unitary operator
U
such that
U(\ker\,S_{1})=(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})^{\perp}
and
U(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}=(\mathrm{ran}\,S_{1})
.

Then
UBU^{*}
and
A
are of the form

UBU^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
B_{3}&B_{4}\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
0&A_{4}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}((\ker\,S_{1})\oplus(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}),

and
A_{4}
is similar to
B_{4}
in
\mathcal{B}((\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp})
. (Note: technically, these are not the same
B_{1},B_{3}
and
B_{4}
as above, but copies of these acting between the spaces
\ker\,S_{1}
and
(\ker\,S_{1})^{\perp}
via the unitary
U
.)

Let
\Omega=\sigma(A_{1})
, and set
\Gamma:=\Omega\cap\sigma(A_{4})
. Note that
0\not\in\sigma(A_{1})
implies that
\Omega
consists of non-zero eigenvalues of
A_{1}
, and hence of
A
. Then
A_{4}\sim A_{41}\oplus A_{44}
, where
\sigma(A_{41})=\Gamma
and
\sigma(A_{44})\cap\Gamma=\varnothing
. Since
B_{4}\sim A_{4}
, we may also write
B_{4}=B_{41}\oplus B_{44}
, where
\sigma(B_{41})=\Gamma
and
\sigma(B_{44})\cap\Gamma=\varnothing
. (We point out the fact that the fact that
A\oplus A\sim B\oplus B
implies that the non-zero eigenvalues of
A
coincide with the non-zero eigenvalues of
B
, and that their algebraic multiplicities coincide. Since
A_{4}\sim B_{4}
, the same statement holds for the non-zero eigenvalues of
A_{4}
and of
B_{4}
respectively. From this it follows that
\sigma(A_{1})=\sigma(B_{1})
, including the algebraic multiplicities of these (necessarily) non-zero eigenvalues.)

Moreover, by Lemma
2.16
(i.e. as deduced from an application of Rosenblum’s operator), the fact that
A_{4}
and
B_{4}
are similar implies that
A_{41}
is similar to
B_{41}
and
A_{44}
is similar to
B_{44}
.

Let
Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be the invertible operator such that

Z^{-1}AZ=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{21}&A_{22}\\
0&A_{41}&0\\
0&0&A_{44}\end{bmatrix}.

Then
\sigma(A_{44})\cap\Omega=\varnothing
implies that

\widehat{A}:=Z^{-1}AZ\sim\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{21}&0\\
0&A_{41}&0\\
0&0&A_{44}\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly,
B
is similar to

\widehat{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0&0\\
B_{21}&B_{41}&0\\
0&0&B_{44}\end{bmatrix},

where, as we noted above,
A_{44}
is similar to
B_{44}
.

Now

(i)

\widehat{A}\oplus\widehat{A}
is similar to
\widehat{B}\oplus\widehat{B}
;

(ii)

\sigma(\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{21}\\
0&A_{41}\end{bmatrix})=\Omega=\sigma(\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
B_{21}&B_{41}\end{bmatrix});\mbox{\ \ \ \ \ and }

(iii)

\Omega\cap\sigma(A_{44})=\varnothing
,
\Omega\cap\sigma(B_{44})=\varnothing
.

It follows that
(\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{21}\\
0&A_{41}\end{bmatrix})^{(2)}\sim(\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
B_{21}&B_{41}\end{bmatrix})^{(2)}
. But these are finite matrices, so by considering their Jordan forms, we immediately conclude that

\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{21}\\
0&A_{41}\end{bmatrix}\sim\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
B_{21}&B_{41}\end{bmatrix},

from which we deduce that
\widehat{A}\sim\widehat{B}
, and thus
A
is similar to
B
.

\Box

When
A
and
B
are not assumed to be compact, we may no longer conclude that
S_{1}
is Fredholm of index zero, and the above proof immediately breaks down. Indeed, the question of whether or not
A\oplus A
similar to
B\oplus B
implies that
A
is similar to
B
seems to be very difficult. There exist some cases, however, where we can assert positive results. First, we propose a possible “line of attack” to solving the problem, in the hope that the reader may have more luck than we did.

4.11.

Proposition.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(a)

Every operator
Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
in the commutant of
J_{2}(A)
is of the form

\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\\
Z_{2}A&Z_{1}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
commute with
A
.

(b)

Suppose that
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is invertible and that
J_{2}(B)=SJ_{2}(A)S^{-1}
. By Lemma
4.9
(a), we may write
S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{2}A&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}
.

Then
A
is similar to
B
if and only if there exists an invertible operator
Z=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\\
Z_{3}&Z_{4}\end{bmatrix}
in the commutant of
J_{2}(A)
such that

W_{1}:=S_{2}AZ_{2}+S_{1}Z_{1}

is invertible.

Proof.

(a)

This follows immediately from Lemma
4.9
(a), setting
B=A
in that Lemma.

(b)

First, suppose that there exists an invertible operator
Z=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\\
Z_{3}&Z_{4}\end{bmatrix}
in the commutant of
J_{2}(A)
such that

W_{1}:=S_{2}AZ_{2}+S_{1}Z_{1}

is invertible. Since
Z
commutes with
J_{2}(A)
, we observe that

J_{2}(B)=SJ_{2}(A)S^{-1}=SZJ_{2}(A)Z^{-1}S^{-1}.

By part (a) above, we have that
Z_{3}=Z_{2}A
and
Z_{4}=Z_{1}
, so that
Z=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\\
Z_{2}A&Z_{1}\end{bmatrix}
. Now, by Lemma
4.9
(a),

SZ=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{2}A&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\\
Z_{2}A&Z_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&W_{2}\\
W_{2}A&W_{1}\end{bmatrix}

where
W_{2}=S_{1}Z_{2}+S_{2}Z_{1}
. By considering the
(1,1)
-entry of
(SZ)J_{2}(A)=J_{2}(B)(SZ)
, we see that
W_{1}A=BW_{1}
. Since
W_{1}
is invertible by hypothesis,
B
is similar to
A
.

Conversely, suppose that
B=W_{1}AW_{1}^{-1}
, where
W_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is invertible. With
W:=W_{1}\oplus W_{1}
, it follows
that
J_{2}(B)=WJ_{2}(A)W^{-1}
.

Next, suppose that
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
and

SJ_{2}(A)S^{-1}=J_{2}(B)=WJ_{2}(A)W^{-1}.

Then
S^{-1}WJ_{2}(A)=J_{2}(A)S^{-1}W
, and so
Z:=S^{-1}W
is invertible and lies in the commutant of
J_{2}(A)
. From (a) above, we may write
Z=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&Z_{2}\\
Z_{2}A&Z_{1}\end{bmatrix}
where
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
commute with
A
. Using Lemma
4.9
(a), we see that we may also write
S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}&S_{2}\\
S_{2}A&S_{1}\end{bmatrix}
, and therefore

\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&0\\
0&W_{1}\end{bmatrix}=W=SZ=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}Z_{1}+S_{2}Z_{2}A&S_{1}Z_{2}+S_{2}Z_{1}\\
S_{2}AZ_{1}+S_{1}Z_{2}A&S_{2}AZ_{2}+S_{1}Z_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

In particular,
W_{1}
is invertible and

W_{1}=S_{1}Z_{1}+S_{2}Z_{2}A=S_{2}AZ_{2}+S_{1}Z_{1}.

\Box

If the spectrum of
A
(and hence of
B
) is sufficiently nice, we can again obtain positive results.

4.12.

Proposition.

Suppose that
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are invertible, and that there exists a net
(p_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of polynomials such that
X:=\textsc{wot}-\lim_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}(A)
and
Y:=\textsc{wot}-\lim_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}(B)
both exist,
X^{2}=A
, and
Y^{2}=B
. If

J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B),

then
A\sim B
.

Proof.

Note that
(\sigma(X))^{2}=\sigma(X^{2})=\sigma(A)
implies that
X
is invertible. Now

J_{2}(A)\sim\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&X^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
A&0\end{bmatrix}\ \begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&X\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&X\\
X^{-1}A&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&X\\
X&0\end{bmatrix}\sim X\oplus-X.

Similarly,
J_{2}(B)\sim Y\oplus-Y
.
Thus

X\oplus-X\sim J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B)\sim Y\oplus-Y.

Choose
R\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
invertible such that

R^{-1}(X\oplus-X)R=Y\oplus-Y.

Squaring both sides, we obtain

R^{-1}(A\oplus A)R=B\oplus B.

Let
(p_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a net of polynomials such that
\textsc{wot}-\lim_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}(A)=X
. Then

\displaystyle Y\oplus Y

\displaystyle=\textsc{wot}-\lim_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}(B\oplus B)

\displaystyle=\textsc{wot}-\lim_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}(R^{-1}(A\oplus A)R)

\displaystyle=R^{-1}(\textsc{wot}-\lim_{\alpha}(p_{\alpha}(A\oplus A)))R

\displaystyle=R^{-1}(X\oplus X)R.

Thus

\displaystyle R^{-1}(2X\oplus 0)R

\displaystyle=R^{-1}((X\oplus-X)+(X\oplus X))R

\displaystyle=R^{-1}(X\oplus-X)R+R^{-1}(X\oplus X)R

\displaystyle=(Y\oplus-Y)+(Y\oplus Y)

\displaystyle=2Y\oplus 0.

Since
X
and
Y
are invertible, this implies (by Lemma
2.16
) that
X
is similar to
Y
, and therefore that
A=X^{2}
is similar to
Y^{2}=B
.

\Box

4.13.

Corollary.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be invertible operators, and suppose that there exists a simply connected domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\sigma(A)\subseteq\Omega
. If
J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B)
, then
A
is similar to
B
.

Proof.

First note that – as we have already seen –
J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B)
implies that
\sigma(B)=\sigma(A)
.

Since
0\not\in\Omega
, it follows that the identity function
\iota(z)=z
,
z\in\Omega
admits a holomorphic square root, say
q
. Thus
q(A)
is a norm-limit of polynomials in
A
, and
q(B)
is a norm-limit of the same polynomials in
B
.
The result now follows from Proposition
4.12
.

\Box

In the case where one of the operators
A,B
is an isometry, we can dispense with the stronger hypothesis that
J_{2}(A)
is similar to
J_{2}(B)
and we can obtain a positive solution to Kaplansky’s original problem in the context of similarity (see Theorem
4.17
below). Before doing so, however, we need to recall a few definitions and results from the literature. The monograph
[
22
]
is an excellent reference.

4.14.

Definition.

Recall that an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
polynomially bounded
if there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that for all polynomials
p\in\mathbb{C}[x]
, we have

\|p(T)\|\leq\kappa\|p\|_{\infty},

where
\|p\|_{\infty}:=\sup\{|p(z)|:|z|\leq 1\}.

We say that
T
is
completely polynomially bounded
if there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that
for all
q=[q_{ij}]\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}[x])
, we have

\|[q_{ij}(T)]\|\leq\kappa\|q\|_{\infty},

where
\|q\|_{\infty}:=\sup\{\|q_{ij}(z)\|:|z|\leq 1\}
.

4.15.

We note that von Neumann’s Inequality
[
22
, Corollary 1.2]
is the assertion that every contraction
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is polynomially bounded, while Arveson
[
2
,
3
]
observed that the Sz.-Nagy Dilation theorem implies that every operator
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
which is similar to a contraction is in fact completely polynomially bounded.

The converse of this, namely that every completely polynomially bounded operator is similar to a contraction, is due to Paulsen
[
21
]
.

We also remind the reader that an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
quasinormal
if
T
commutes with
T^{*}T
. It follows from the work of Brown
[
7
]
that
T
is quasinormal if and only if
T
is unitarily equivalent to an operator of the form
N\oplus(S\otimes P)
, where
S
denotes the unilateral forward shift,
N
is normal and
P
is a positive definite operator.

If
W\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is an isometry, then
W^{*}W=I
, and so clearly
W
is quasinormal. The Wold Decomposition for isometries acting on infinite-dimensional, separable Hilbert spaces asserts that there exists a unitary operator
U_{W}
(perhaps acting on a space of dimensional zero) and a unique cardinal number
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\aleph_{0}\}
such that
W\simeq U_{W}\oplus S^{(\alpha)}
. Observing that
S^{(\alpha)}\simeq(S\otimes I_{\alpha})
, where
\alpha
denotes the identity operator acting on a Hilbert space of dimension
\alpha
, we see for isometries, the corresponding positive definite operator in Brown’s structure theorem is nothing more than
I_{\alpha}
.

We shall need one more result, due to Popescu
[
23
]
.

4.16.

Theorem.

[Popescu]

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be such that

P_{A}:=\textsc{sot}-\lim_{m}(A^{*})^{m}A^{m}

exists. Then
A
is similar to an isometry if and only if
P_{A}
is invertible.

4.17.

Theorem.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
W\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an isometry. If
T^{(n)}
is similar to
W^{(n)}
, then
T
is similar to
W
. In particular, if
J_{n}(T)
is similar to
J_{n}(W)
, then
T
is similar to
W
.

Proof.

Note that
\|W\|=1=\|W^{(n)}\|
. It follows from Arveson’s results that
T^{(n)}
is completely polynomially bounded, which is easily seen to imply that
T
is completely polynomially bounded, and thus by Paulsen’s Theorem, that
T
is similar to a contraction
X
.

If
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a contraction, then the sequence
((A^{*})^{m}A^{m})_{m}
is a decreasing sequence of positive operators, and so
P_{A}:=\textsc{sot}-\lim_{m}(A^{*})^{m}A^{m}
exists. In our case, we conclude that

P_{X}:=\textsc{sot}-\lim_{m}(X^{*})^{m}X^{m}\mbox{\ \ \ \ and that \ \ \ \ }P_{X^{(n)}}:=\textsc{sot}-\lim_{m}((X^{(n)})^{*})^{m}(X^{(n)})^{m}

both exist, and clearly we must have

P_{X^{(n)}}=(P_{X})^{(n)}.

But
X^{(n)}
is similar to the isometry
W^{(n)}
, and so from Theorem
4.16
,
P_{X^{(n)}}
is invertible. This in turn implies that
P_{X}
is invertible. Once again applying Theorem
4.16
, we conclude that
X
is similar to an isometry
V
, and consequently that
T
is similar to
V
.
By the Wold decomposition,
V
is unitarily equivalent to
U_{V}\oplus S^{(\alpha)}\simeq U_{V}\oplus(S\otimes I_{\alpha})
, where
U_{V}
is unitary
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\aleph_{0}\}
, and
W
is unitarily equivalent to
U_{W}\oplus S^{(\beta)}\simeq U_{W}\oplus(S\otimes I_{\beta})
, where
U_{W}
is unitary and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\beta\}
.
In particular,

U_{V}^{(n)}\oplus(S\otimes I_{n\cdot\alpha})\simeq V^{(n)}\sim T^{(n)}\sim W^{(n)}\simeq U_{W}^{(n)}\oplus(S\otimes I_{n\cdot\beta}).

Now
V^{(n)}
and
W^{(n)}
are quasinormal, and so by
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
,
U_{V}^{(n)}\simeq U_{W}^{(n)}
and for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,

\mathrm{nul}(I_{n\cdot\alpha}-\lambda I_{n\cdot\alpha})=\mathrm{nul}(I_{n\cdot\beta}-\lambda I_{n\cdot\beta}).

By the results of Kadison and Singer
[
16
]
(see Theorem
4.2
above),
U_{V}\simeq U_{W}
, and the condition on the nullity for
\lambda=1
implies that
\alpha=\beta
. Thus
V
is unitarily equivalent to
W
, and so
T
is similar to
W
.

The last statement easily follows from the above argument and the fact that if
J_{n}(T)\sim J_{n}(W)
, then
T^{(n)}=J_{n}(T)^{n}\sim J_{n}(W)^{n}=W^{(n)}
.

\Box

5.
Remarks and observations.

5.1.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
2\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
. As we have seen multiple times,
J_{n}(T)^{n}=T^{(n)}
, and thus if
T
is
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{u}]}
-stable (resp.
\textsc{j}_{n}^{[\textsc{s}]}
-stable), then
T^{n}\simeq T^{(n)}
(resp.
T^{n}\sim T^{(n)}
). In their paper
[
11
]
, the authors studied operators satisfying this last relation in the case where
n=2
, referring to these phenomena as
condition U
(i.e.
T^{2}\simeq T^{(2)}
) and as
condition S
(i.e.
T^{2}\sim T^{(2)}
) respectively. Unfortunately, they mistakenly state that “a scalar multiple of an operator satisfying
condition S
(or
condition U
) satisfies the same condition only if the scalar is either
0
or
1
.”

A counterexample to this claim comes in the form of the forward unilateral shift operator
S
, as for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
,

(\alpha S)^{2}=\alpha^{2}S^{2}\simeq S^{2}\simeq S\oplus S\simeq(\alpha S\oplus\alpha S).

5.2.

Example.

There exist (positive, invertible) operators
H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
H_{1}\sim H_{2}
and
H_{1}\simeq_{a}H_{2}
, but
H_{1}
is not unitarily equivalent to
H_{2}
.

Choose two non-atomic Borel measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on the interval
[1,2]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, with the supports of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
both equal to
[1,2]
, such that
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are not mutually absolutely continuous. Let
H_{k}
denote the (hermitian) multiplication operator
M_{x}
acting on
L^{2}([1,2],\mu_{k}),\ k=1,2
.

Then
\sigma(H_{1})=[1,2]=\sigma(H_{2})
, and so
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are approximately unitarily equivalent, by the Weyl-von Neumann-Berg Theorem
[
12
, Theorem II.4.4]
, but they are not unitarily equivalent, since the underlying measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are not mutually absolutely continuous
[
12
, Corollary II.3.6]
.

Let
Z_{1}=S\otimes H_{1}
,
Z_{2}=S\otimes H_{2}
, where
S
is the usual unilateral forward shift operator. It is easy to check that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are approximately unitarily equivalent since
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are. Furthermore, it follows from
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are similar.

Note, however, that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are not unitarily equivalent. For suppose otherwise. Then

H_{1}^{2}\oplus 0=(Z_{1}^{*}Z_{1}-Z_{1}Z_{1}^{*})\simeq(Z_{2}^{*}Z_{2}-Z_{2}Z_{2}^{*})=H_{2}^{2}\oplus 0,

and so by
[
16
, Theorem 1]
, we have that
H_{1}^{2}\simeq H_{2}^{2}
. Since
H_{1},H_{2}
are invertible and positive, we conclude that
H_{1}\simeq H_{2}
, a contradiction.

5.3.

Remark.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The problem of whether or not
J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B)
implies that
A\sim B
remains open. In the hope that it may be of use to someone wishing to try their hand at this problem, we make a minor observation, which may be of some use: if
J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B)
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
, then
J_{2}(\kappa A)\sim J_{2}(\kappa B)
.

The result is obvious if
\kappa=0
. If
\kappa\not=0
, then
J_{2}(A)\sim J_{2}(B)
implies that

\kappa^{1/2}J_{2}(A)\sim\kappa^{1/2}J_{2}(B).

But then

J_{2}(\kappa A)\sim\kappa^{1/2}J_{2}(A)\sim\kappa^{1/2}J_{2}(B)\sim J_{2}(\kappa B).

5.4.

Example.
(due to
J. Bell
)
It is natural to ask whether Kaplansky’s second problem can be positively answered in the general setting of rings, which would obviously imply a positive answer in the setting of operators on a Hilbert space. More specifically, is it true that if
R
is a unital ring, and
x,y\in R
are such that
x\oplus x
is
conjugate
(the ring-theoretic nomenclature for similarity) to
y\oplus y
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(R)
, then
x
and
y
must be conjugate? In general, the answer is no. In fact, in the general setting of unital rings, the analogue of Theorem
4.10
fails. The following counterexample is due to our colleague Jason Bell, and we thank him for allowing us to reproduce it here.

There exists a unital ring
R
and elements
x,y\in R
such that if
J_{2}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
x&0\end{bmatrix}
and
J_{2}(y):=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
y&0\end{bmatrix}
then
J_{2}(x)
is conjugate to
J_{2}(y)
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(R)
, but
x
is not conjugate to
y
in
R
.

Let
S=\mathbb{Q}[a,b,c]/(a^{2}-bc=1)
. Notice that
S
is an integral domain since
a^{2}-bc
is irreducible. We claim that the group of units of
S
is
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
. To see this, notice that
S
admits a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading, by giving
a
degree
0
,
b
degree
1
and
c
degree
-1
. Since
S
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded domain, the only units of
S
are contained in
S_{0}
, the homogeneous part of
S
of degree
0
. Indeed, since
S
is a domain the set of units is homogeneous. Consider
S
as a module over
S_{0}
, and let
S_{n}
denote the set of elements of
S
of degree
n
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then for
n\geq 1
,
S_{n}=S_{0}b^{n}
, and
S_{-n}=S_{0}c^{n}
. Note further that
b
and
c
are not units since
bc=a^{2}-1
is not a unit in
S_{0}
. Hence the group of units of
S
lies in
S_{0}
.

Observe that
S_{0}
is the subring of
S
generated by
a
and
bc
. Thus

S_{0}\cong\mathbb{Q}[a,bc]/(a^{2}-bc-1)\cong\mathbb{Q}[a].

Since the group of units of
\mathbb{Q}[a]
is
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, the claim follows.

Next, let
T
denote the free product (in the category of associative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras) of
S
and
\mathbb{Q}\{x,y\}
, the free associative algebra on
x
and
y
. Consider the ideal
I
of
T
generated by
ax-ya
,
bx-yb
, and
cx-yc
.
If we let
R=T/I
, then in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(R)
, the matrix
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&a\end{bmatrix}
is invertible (since it lies in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(S)
,
S
is commutative, and the determinant is
a^{2}-bc=1
).
Furthermore,

\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&a\end{bmatrix}(x\oplus x)=(y\oplus y)\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&a\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(R),

by our definition of
I
. Thus
x\oplus x
and
y\oplus y
are conjugate in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(R)
.

Notice that
R
admits an
\mathbb{N}
-grading (not compatible with the one on
S
) obtained by assigning degree
0
to
a,b
and
c
, and degree
1
to
x
and
y
, since the generators of
I
and the relation
a^{2}-bc=1
are all homogeneous. Now
R/(x,y)\cong S
and so
S
is the degree
0
component of
R
.

We now argue by contradiction. Suppose that
x
and
y
are conjugate in
R
. Then there is some
unit
u
of
R
such that
ux=yu
. Write
u=u_{0}+\cdots+u_{d}
, where
u_{i}\in R
is
homogeneous of degree
i
. Let
v=v_{0}+\cdots+v_{e}
be the inverse of
u
.
By considering the degree
0
terms of both sides of
1=uv
, we see that
u_{0}v_{0}=1
and since
u_{0},v_{0}
are in the degree
0
component of
R\cong S
, we see that
u_{0},v_{0}
must be in
\mathbb{Q}^{*}
.

Now we have that
(u_{0}+\dots+u_{d})x=y(u_{0}+\dots+u_{d})
and so looking at the degree one part of both sides we see that
u_{0}x=yu_{0}
. But
u_{0}
is a nonzero scalar, from which it follows that
x=y
in
R
. Thus to show that
x
and
y
are not conjugate in
R
, it suffices to show that
x\neq y
in
R
. To do this, we shall define a homomorphism
\Phi:R\to\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
such that
\Phi(x)\neq\Phi(y)
.

We consider the map
\Phi
induced by setting

•

\Phi(1)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
;

•

\Phi(a)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
;
\Phi(b)=\Phi(c)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
;

•

\Phi(x)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}
; and
\Phi(y)=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
.

By definition of
R
as the quotient of the free product
T
of
S
and
\mathbb{Q}\{x,y\}
by the ideal
I
, to see that
\Phi
extends to a ring homomorphism on
R
, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Phi(a)\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\Phi(a)

\displaystyle=\Phi(b)\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\Phi(b)

\displaystyle=\Phi(c)\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\Phi(c),\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\Phi(1)

\displaystyle=\Phi(a)^{2}-\Phi(b)\Phi(c),

all of which are routine calculations.

This implies that
\Phi
indeed induces a homomorphism from
R\to\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
. But
\Phi(x)\neq\Phi(y)
, and thus
x\neq y
in
R
, completing the argument.

As a corollary to this example, we note that
x\oplus x=J_{2}(x)^{2}
is conjugate to
y\oplus y=J_{2}(y)^{2}
, and yet
x
is not conjugate to
y
, and so this example also shows that this formulation of Kaplansky’s second problem admits a negative answer in the setting of unital rings.
