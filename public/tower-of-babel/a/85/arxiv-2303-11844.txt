Doubly Regularized Entropic Wasserstein Barycenters
Lénaïc Chizat

Abstract
We study a general formulation of regularized Wasserstein barycenters that enjoys favorable regularity, approximation, stability and (grid-free) optimization properties. This barycenter is defined as the unique probability measure that minimizes the sum of entropic optimal transport (EOT) costs with respect to a family of given probability measures, plus an entropy term. We denote it ( λ , τ ) (\lambda,\tau) -barycenter, where λ \lambda is the inner regularization strength and τ \tau the outer one. This formulation recovers several previously proposed EOT barycenters for various choices of λ , τ ≥ 0 \lambda,\tau\geq 0 and generalizes them. First, in spite of – and in fact owing to – being doubly regularized, we show that our formulation is debiased for τ = λ / 2 \tau=\lambda/2 : the suboptimality in the (unregularized) Wasserstein barycenter objective is, for smooth densities, of the order of the strength λ 2 \lambda^{2} of entropic regularization, instead of max ⁡ { λ , τ } \max\{\lambda,\tau\} in general. We discuss this phenomenon for isotropic Gaussians where all ( λ , τ ) (\lambda,\tau) -barycenters have closed form. Second, we show that for λ , τ > 0 \lambda,\tau>0 , the barycenter has a smooth density and is strongly stable under perturbation of the marginals. In particular, it can be estimated efficiently: given n n samples from each of the probability measures, it converges in relative entropy to the population barycenter at a rate n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} . And finally, this formulation lends itself naturally to a grid-free optimization algorithm: we propose a simple noisy particle gradient descent which, in the mean-field limit, converges globally at an exponential rate to the barycenter.

To overcome these limitations and following the idea of entropic regularization of optimal transport, a.k.a the Schrödinger bridge problem

2.1
Preliminaries: regularity of EOT

Let us begin with some useful facts about EOT. The problem (
1
) that defines
T_{\lambda}
has a unique solution
\gamma^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})
and admits the dual formulation

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{\phi\in L^{1}(\mu),\psi\in L^{1}(\nu)}\int\phi\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\mathrm{d}\nu+\lambda\Big(1-\int e^{(\phi(x)-\psi(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\Big).

(6)

This dual problem has a solution
(\phi^{*},\psi^{*})
which is unique in
L^{1}(\mu)\times L^{1}(\nu)
up to the transformation
(\phi^{*}+c,\psi^{*}-c)
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
. At optimality, we have
T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\int\phi^{*}\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi^{*}\mathrm{d}\nu
and the primal-dual relation

\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=e^{(\phi(x)+\psi(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\nu(\mathrm{d}y).

Moreover, the potentials satisfy for
\mu\otimes\nu
almost every

, the optimality condition

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \phi^{*}(x)&=-\lambda\log\Big(\int e^{(\psi^{*}(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\Big)\\
\psi^{*}(y)&=-\lambda\log\Big(\int e^{(\phi^{*}(x)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Big)\end{aligned}\right..

(7)

These equations can be used to extend
\phi^{*}
and
\psi^{*}
as continuous functions (in fact of class
\mathcal{C}^{p}
when

) over
\mathcal{X}
, which satisfy these equations everywhere

]

Proposition 2.2

(First-variation of entropic optimal transport)
.

Fix
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
let
(\phi[\mu],\psi[\mu])
be the Schrödinger potentials associated to the pair
(\mu,\nu)
. Then:

(i)

The map
\mu\mapsto\phi[\mu]
(as well as the map
\mu\mapsto\psi[\mu]
) satisfies the following Lipschitz continuity property: there exists
L>0
such that

\|\phi[\mu]-\phi[\mu^{\prime}]\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{p-1}(\mathcal{X})}\leq L\,W_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}),\quad\forall\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}).

where

denotes the maximum supremum norm of all partial derivatives up to order

quotiented by the invariance by addition of a constant (as in Def.
2.1

(ii)

the function

\mu\mapsto T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)
is convex, weakly continuous, and admits

as first-variation, i.e.

\displaystyle\forall\mu,\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\;\lim_{\epsilon\,\downarrow\,0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Big(T_{\lambda}((1-\epsilon)\mu+\epsilon\tilde{\mu},\nu)-T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)\Big)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi[\mu](x)\mathrm{d}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)(x).

(8)

(iii)

For any
p^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,p\}
there exists
C_{p^{\prime}}>0
independent of
\mu,\nu
and
\lambda
such that

\|\phi[\mu]\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{p^{\prime}}}\leq C_{p^{\prime}}\lambda^{\min\{0,1-p^{\prime}\}}.

Proof.

The first claim is technical and is proved in
[
CCM22
]
via the implicit function theorem on the Schrödinger system (
7

is clear by Eq. (

is the first-variation as in
[

]

(resp.
\phi[\mu_{\epsilon}]

at

\displaystyle\int\phi[\mu]\mathrm{d}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Big(T_{\lambda}(\mu_{\epsilon},\nu)-T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)\Big)\leq\int\phi[\mu_{\epsilon}]\mathrm{d}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu).

The point

. Finally

We first gather useful regularity properties of

Proposition 2.3

(Regularity of
G_{\lambda}
)
.

For any
(\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{K}
, the function
G_{\lambda}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined in Eq. (

\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
it admits a first-variation

\displaystyle V[\mu]\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{k}[\mu]

(9)

where
\phi_{k}[\mu]\in\mathcal{C}^{p}(\mathcal{X})
is the Schrödinger potential from
\mu
to
\nu_{k}
.
The map
\mu\mapsto\nabla V[\mu]
is Lipschitz continuous in the sense that there exists
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\|V[\mu]-V[\mu^{\prime}]\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{p-1}}\leq L\,W_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}),\qquad\forall\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}).

We moreover have that for
p^{\prime}\leq p
, there exists
C_{p^{\prime}}>0
independent of

,

and

such that
\|V[\mu]\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{p^{\prime}}}\leq C_{p^{\prime}}\lambda^{\min\{0,1-p^{\prime}\}}

Conveniently, the objective is also strongly convex.

Proposition 2.4

(Strong convexity of
F_{\tau,\lambda}
)
.

For
\lambda\geq 0
, the objective

is

-strongly convex on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
for the total variation norm.

Proof.

It is well known that
\mu\mapsto H(\mu)
is

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
for the total variation norm, see e.g.
[
Yu13

. Since

is convex, the result follows.
∎

Strong convexity in

(which follows from Prop.

-barycenter functional in
[

with a technical proof specific to that case. The equivalent formulation as a doubly-regularized problem makes this property immediate.

As a consequence of all these regularity results, we now show that
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters can be written as a smooth Gibbs density.

Theorem 2.5

(Existence and regularity)
.

For any
\lambda,\tau>0
,
F_{\lambda,\tau}
admits a unique minimizer
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. It is an absolutely continuous measure with density

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}}{\mathrm{d}x}\propto e^{-V[\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}]/\tau}

(10)

where
V[\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{k}[\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}]\in\mathcal{C}^{p}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies the regularity estimates of Prop.
2.3
.

Proof.

The functional
G_{\lambda}
is weakly continuous and
H
is weakly lower-semicontinuous
[
San15
, Sec. 7.1.2]
so
F_{\lambda}
is weakly lower-semicontinuous. It is not identically
+\infty
since the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{X}
is a feasible point. Since
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is weakly compact, the direct method of the calculus of variations tells us that there exists at least one minimizer
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}
. Moreover
F_{\lambda,\tau}
is strictly convex (Prop.
2.4
), so the minimizer is unique and since
H(\mu^{*}_{\lambda})<\infty
this measure is absolutely continuous. The first order optimality condition states that there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{k}[\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}](x)+\tau\log\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x)\Big)=C,\quad\text{for $\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}$-almost every $x$}.

(11)

It remains to observe that
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}
must have full support on
\mathcal{X}
(this is because of the infinite negative slope of
s\log(s)
at
0
, see e.g.
[
San15
, Prop. 8.7]
for details in a similar context) to obtain the formula for the minimizer.
∎

Interestingly, the minimizer of
F_{\lambda,\tau}
for
\tau>0
has always full support in
\mathcal{X}
even when none of the marginals
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{K}
have. This property is not satisfied when
\tau=0
or for the Sinkhorn Divergence barycenter (see Section

2.3
Dual formulations

There are several ways to derive a dual formulation for (
4
). Let us detail one of them which stands out as an elegant composition of two soft-max (log-sum-exp) functions – which is the dual consequence of the double regularization. It can be used to compute
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters in practice (Section
6
).

Proposition 2.6
.

For
\lambda,\tau>0
, one has the dual formulation

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu)=\max_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X})^{K}}E({\boldsymbol{\psi}})

(12)

where
E({\boldsymbol{\psi}})
is defined as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{k}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}-\tau\log\left\{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp\left[\frac{\lambda}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}\exp\Big(\frac{\psi_{k}(y)-c(x,y)}{\lambda}\Big)\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}(y)\right]\mathrm{d}x\right\}.

(13)

The function
E
is concave,
1
-Lipschitz continuous and
\min\{\lambda,\tau\}^{-1}
-smooth for the seminorm
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\mapsto\sum w_{k}\|\psi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{osc}}
where
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{osc}}=\sup_{y}\psi(y)-\inf_{y}\psi(y)
. It admits a maximizer with semi-norm smaller than
\|c\|_{\mathrm{osc}}
(which is unique up to shifting each
\psi_{k}
by constants).
Moreover, the barycenter
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}
is a Gibbs distribution associated the solution of the dual problem (see (
16
)).

Proof.

We start from the dual formulation (
6
) of
T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)
. Maximizing over
\phi
gives
\phi=\phi_{\psi}

\nu
-a.e. with

\displaystyle\phi_{\psi}(x)=-\lambda\log\Big(\int e^{(\psi(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\Big)

(14)

(this is half of the Schrödinger system (
7
)). The so-called “semi-dual” formulation of EOT follows

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{\psi\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X})}\int\psi\mathrm{d}\nu+\int\phi_{\psi}\mathrm{d}\nu.

(15)

We can thus rewrite the objective of the barycenter as

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\max_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X})^{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\int\psi_{k}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}+\int\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{\psi_{k}}\Big)\mathrm{d}\mu+\tau H(\mu)

where
\phi_{\psi_{k}}
is defined as in (
14
) but with
\nu_{k}
in place of
\nu
. By Sion’s minimax theorem (using in particular that
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is weakly compact and that the semi-dual objective (
15
) is continuous for the sup-norm), we can exchange the order of min and max. Maximizing in
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
gives

\displaystyle\mu=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}=\exp\left((\chi_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-V_{\boldsymbol{\psi}})/\tau\right)

with

\displaystyle V_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{\psi_{k}}

and

\displaystyle\quad\chi_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}=-\tau\log\int e^{-V_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}/\tau}\mathrm{d}x

(16)

and the objective becomes

\max_{(\psi_{k})_{k}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X})^{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\int\psi_{k}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}+\chi_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}.

The other claims follow mostly from the general properties of the log-sum-exp operator
f\mapsto\mathrm{LSE}(f):x\mapsto\alpha\log\int e^{f(x,y)/\alpha}\mathrm{d}\nu(y)
for
\alpha>0
and
\nu
a nonnegative measure, which is such that :

•

if
f(\cdot,y)
is convex for
\nu
-a.e
y
then
\mathrm{LSE}(f)
is convex in
x

[
BV04
, Ex. 3.14]
;

•

\mathrm{LSE}
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous and
1/\alpha
-smooth for the sup-norm, which can be seen from the expression of the first and second order differential:

\displaystyle D\mathrm{LSE}[f](\delta f)=x\mapsto\mathbf{E}_{Y_{x}}[\delta f(x,Y_{x})]

\displaystyle D^{2}\mathrm{LSE}[f](\delta f,\delta f)=x\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{Var}_{Y_{x}}[\delta f(x,Y_{x})]

where
\mathbf{E}_{Y_{x}}
(resp.
\mathbf{Var}_{Y_{x}}
) denote the expectation (resp. centered variance) under
Y_{x}
distributed according to the probability measure proportional to
e^{f(x,y)/\alpha}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)
.

Let us apply these properties in the context of
E
. The second term of (
12
) is concave (as minus the composition of: an affine function, by a LSE, by a sum with nonnegative weights, by a LSE). It is also
1
-Lipschitz continuous, as a composition of
1
-Lipschitz continuous functions and its differential is given by

\displaystyle DE[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}](\delta\psi_{k})=w_{k}\int\delta\psi_{k}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}-w_{k}\int\delta\psi_{k}(y)e^{(\phi_{\psi_{k}}(x)+\psi_{k}(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda+(\chi_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(x)-V_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(x))/\tau}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}(y)

Note that the second integral is the integral of
\delta\psi_{k}
against a probability measure
\gamma_{\psi_{k}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\in\Pi(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\psi}},\nu_{k})
which can be disintegrated as
\gamma_{\psi_{k}}(\mathrm{d}y|x)\mu_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\mathrm{d}x)
. Differentiating once more, we obtain

\displaystyle D^{2}E[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}](\delta\psi,\delta\psi)=-\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf{Var}_{x\sim\mu_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\mathbf{E}_{\gamma_{\psi_{k}}(\cdot|x)}[\delta\psi_{k}]\Big]-\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{E}_{x\sim\mu_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\mathbf{Var}_{\gamma_{\psi_{k}}(\cdot|x)}(\delta\psi_{k})\Big].

It follows by the law of total variance that

-\frac{1}{\max\{\tau,\lambda\}}\mathbf{Var}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\delta\psi_{k}(y_{k})\Big]\geq D^{2}E[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}](\delta\psi,\delta\psi)\geq-\frac{1}{\min\{\tau,\lambda\}}\mathbf{Var}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\delta\psi_{k}(y_{k})\Big]

where the variance is under
(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
such that
(x,y_{k})\sim\gamma_{\psi_{k}}
. This shows that
E
is
\frac{1}{\min\{\tau,\lambda\}}
-smooth for the norm
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\mapsto\sum w_{k}\|\psi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{osc}}
and the uniqueness of the dual solution up to constant shifts. The existence of a maximizer can be obtained by taking the Schrödinger potentials associated to the minimizers (
11
) which satisfy the optimality conditions and the upper-bound on its semi-norm follows from the Schrödinger system (
7
).
∎

Dual formulation for
\lambda=\tau

For the sake of completeness, let us mention that when
\tau=\lambda
, another useful dual formulation becomes available. Indeed, the problem then writes

\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\max_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}\sum_{k}w_{k}\int\varphi_{k}\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi_{k}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}+\lambda\Big(1-\int e^{(\phi_{k}(x)+\psi_{k}(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}(y)\Big)

where we have used Lemma
3.5
below to change the reference measure from
\mu\otimes\nu_{k}
to
(\mathrm{d}x)\otimes\nu_{k}
. Exchanging min/max and minimizing over
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and one gets the dual problem
[
PC19
, Prop. 9.1]

\displaystyle\max_{{\boldsymbol{\phi}},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\int\psi_{k}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}+\lambda\sum_{k}^{K}w_{k}\Big(1-\int e^{(\phi_{k}(x)+\psi_{k}(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}(y)\Big)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\phi_{k}=0

with, at optimality,
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int e^{(\phi_{k}(x)+\psi_{k}(y)-c(x,y))/\lambda}\mathrm{d}\nu_{k}(y)\mathrm{d}x
(valid for
k\in[K]
). Alternate maximization on the blocks
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
and

leads to a convenient Sinkhorn-like algorithm when the Lebesgue measure is discretized (see

]
for a complexity analysis).

2.4
Extensions

Let us conclude this section with a discussion of our setting and potential extensions.

•

(General ambiant space) The definition of
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters would make sense in the more general context where
\mathcal{X}
is a Polish space with a reference measure
\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
(replacing the Lebesgue measure). In particular, the compactness assumption is not necessary, provided that the cost satisfies certain integrability conditions (see
[
Nut21
]
for a review of EOT under weak assumptions). In this paper, we focus on the compact case on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for simplicity and because, to date, Prop.
2.2
-(i) and Prop.
3.1
-(i) which we use below are only known in this setting.

•

(Infinite number of marginals) The problem of Wasserstein barycenter is often formulated
[
AC17
]
in the more general form where the EOT barycenter functional is an expectation under some distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}))
instead of a finite sum, i.e.

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(\mu)=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)\mathrm{d}P(\nu).

(17)

The
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters could also be studied in this setting, where interesting questions arise related to estimation rates and stability.

3
Approximating the Wasserstein barycenter

In this section, we study the difference between
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters and the
(0,0)
-barycenter; showing in passing a result for the Sinkhorn divergence barycenter. Our goal is to show that the double regularization is not just a convenient trick to get nice properties: the resulting object in fact preserves the geometric naturality of unregularized barycenters when
\tau=\lambda/2
(at least better so than when
\tau=0
,
\lambda=0
or
\tau=\lambda
). We mention however that we do not specially advocate choosing a small
\lambda
in practice, as the other desirable properties of
(\lambda,\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}})
-barycenters degrade very quickly as
\lambda
decreases (see
[
CRLVP20
]
for an analysis of the trade-offs in choosing
\lambda
in a similar context).

3.1
The “debiasing effect” for smooth densities

In this section we discuss the case of the quadratic cost and smooth marginals. At the heart of our approximation result is the following known comparison between
T_{\lambda}
and
T_{0}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
\mu
and
\nu
have bounded densities with compact support and let
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|_{2}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle T_{0}(\mu,\nu)\leq T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{d\lambda}{2}\log(2\pi\lambda)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(H(\mu)+H(\nu))\leq T_{0}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}I(\mu,\nu)

(18)

where
I(\mu,\nu)
is the integrated Fisher information of the Wasserstein geodesic
(\rho_{t}\mathrm{d}x)_{t\in[0,1]}
that connects
\mu
to
\nu
, i.e.
I(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|\nabla\log\rho_{t}(x)\|^{2}\rho_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\geq 0
. Moreover, if
I(\mu,\nu)<\infty
then

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{d\lambda}{2}\log(2\pi\lambda)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(H(\mu)+H(\nu))=T_{0}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}I(\mu,\nu)+o(\lambda^{2}).

(19)

The logarithmic derivative
\nabla\log\rho_{t}
appearing in the statement is the density of the distributional gradient
\nabla\rho_{t}
with respect to
\rho_{t}
when it exists, and
I_{0}(\mu,\nu)=+\infty
if this quantity is not defined for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
. As shown in
[
CRLVP20
, Thm. 1]
, the first claim is a direct consequence of a dynamical formulation of
T_{\lambda}

[
CGP16
]
. The second claim was proved in
[
CRLVP20
, Lem. 1]
and then in
[
CT21
]
(who first formulated the ansatz) in a more general setting. It is a more precise version of previous first-order expansions
[
DLR13
,
EMR15
,
Pal19
]
. Let us also mention that
[
CRLVP20
, Prop. 1]
gives a priori bounds on
I(\mu,\nu)
in terms of the derivatives up to order
3
of Kantorovich potentials.

Formula (
19
) shows that the choice
\tau=\lambda/2
stands out, as this cancels exactly the first order (non-constant) error term between
F_{\lambda,\tau}
and
F_{0,0}=G_{0}
. An approximation bound in terms of suboptimality gap for the Wasserstein barycenter functional easily follows.

Theorem 3.2

(Approximation bound)
.

Assume that
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{K}
have bounded densities and let
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y-x\|_{2}^{2}
. Then for
\lambda>0
the
(\lambda,\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}})
-barycenter satisfies

\displaystyle G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2})-G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}I(\mu^{*}_{0,0},\nu_{k})

(20)

where
I
is defined in Prop.
3.1
. For the Sinkhorn divergence barycenter, it holds:

G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})-G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\Big(I(\mu^{*}_{0,0},\nu_{k})+\frac{1}{2}I(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})+\frac{1}{2}I(\nu_{k})\Big)

where
I(\mu)\coloneqq I(\mu,\mu)
denotes the Fisher information of
\mu
.

Proof.

First notice that Prop.
3.1
indeed applies for any couple of the form
(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2},\nu_{k})
: the bounded density assumption holds by Thm.
2.5
for
\lambda,\tau>0
and by
[
AC11
, Thm. 5.1]
for
\lambda=0
since the Wasserstein barycenter has bounded density.
Let us call
\tilde{T}_{\lambda}
the quantity that is sandwiched in Eq. (
18
) and let us define
\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\tilde{T}_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu_{k})
which differs from
F_{\lambda,\lambda/2}
only by a constant. Since
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2}
is the minimizer of
F_{\lambda,\lambda/2}
, it is also the minimizer of
\tilde{F}_{\lambda}
, so for any
\lambda\geq 0
,
\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2})\leq\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})
.
By Prop.
3.1
, it holds for any admissible
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
that

0\leq\tilde{T}_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu_{k})-T_{0}(\mu,\nu_{k})\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}I(\mu,\nu_{k}).

Taking the weighted sum over
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, we get
0\leq\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu)-G_{0}(\mu)\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}I(\mu,\nu_{k})
. It follows

G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2})-G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})\\
\leq[G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2})-\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2})]+[\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\lambda/2})-\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})]+[\tilde{F}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})-G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})]\\
\leq 0+0+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}I(\mu^{*}_{0,0},\nu_{k}).

As for the Sinkhorn divergence barycenter, it is the minimizer of
G^{\mathrm{div}}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}S_{\lambda}(\cdot,\nu_{k})
where

S_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{1}{2}T_{\lambda}(\mu,\mu)-\frac{1}{2}T_{\lambda}(\nu,\nu)

is the Sinkhorn divergence. After manipulating inequalities (
18
), we obtain for admissible
\mu,\nu
that

T_{0}(\mu,\nu)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{16}(I(\mu)+I(\nu))\leq S_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)\leq T_{0}(\mu,\nu)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}I(\mu,\nu).

As before, we sum these inequalities over
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and get

\displaystyle G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})-G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})

\displaystyle\leq[G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})-G^{\mathrm{div}}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})]+[G^{\mathrm{div}}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})-G^{\mathrm{div}}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\;+[G^{\mathrm{div}}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})-G_{0}(\mu^{*}_{0,0})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{16}I(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\mathrm{div}})+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{16}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}I(\nu_{k})+0+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}I(\mu^{*}_{0,0},\nu_{k}).\qed

Note that for other barycenters, the suboptimality bound is of the order
\max\{\lambda,\tau\}H(\mu)
, which shows that the
(\lambda,\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}})
-barycenters and the Sinkhorn divergence barycenters stand out as particularly good approximations of the unregularized barycenter, of order
\lambda^{2}
. Regarding the generality of this result, we can also make the following comments:

•

the choice
\tau=\lambda/2
leads to approximation benefits for all costs of the form
(\alpha/2)\|y-x\|_{2}^{2}
for
\alpha>0
. Indeed, insisting on the dependency in
c
in the notation, we can always bring ourselves back to the case
\alpha=1
using
F_{\alpha c,\alpha\lambda,\alpha\tau}=\alpha F_{c,\lambda,\tau}.

•

In contrast, for costs with a non-constant Hessian, there is no natural way to debias the problem since in those cases, as shown in
[
Pal19
]
, the first order term in the expansion (
19
) depends on the optimal transport map itself, which is not known a priori.

•

Note that these bounds involve quantities related to the regularity of the unregularized barycenter for which no a priori bound exist unfortunately.

4
Stability and statistical estimation

4.1
General stability result

The goal of this subsection is to prove the following general stability result, where the strongest point is (ii). In that statement,
W_{1}
denotes the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance and
\dot{H}^{q}
denotes the homogeneous Sobolev norm of order
q
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(\nu_{k})_{k=1}^{K},(\hat{\nu}_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})^{K}
and let
\hat{\mu},\mu^{*}
be their respective
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters with same weights
(w_{k})_{k=1}^{K}
.

(i)

Assume that
c(x,\cdot)
is
L
-Lipschitz for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then for
\tau>0
and
\lambda\geq 0
, it holds

H(\hat{\mu}|\mu^{*})\leq\frac{2L}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}W_{1}(\nu_{k},\hat{\nu}_{k}).

(ii)

Assume that
c\in\mathcal{C}^{p}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})
for
p\geq 2
and
\lambda>0
. Then there exists
C
that depends on
c
and
\mathcal{X}
only such that

H(\hat{\mu}|\mu^{*})\leq\frac{C(1+\lambda^{1-p})}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\|\nu_{k}-\hat{\nu}_{k}\|_{\dot{H}^{-p}}.

Note that the first bound, adapted from
[
BCP19a
, Thm. 3.3]
does not require
\lambda>0
and only exploits the first-order regularity of the cost while with
\lambda>0
, the barycenter is able to exploit the regularity of the cost to obtain stability under norms even weaker than
W_{1}
. Another stability result in the literature is
[
TK22
, Thm. 1]
which proves
\ell_{2}
-stability of
(\lambda,\lambda)
-barycenters in a discrete setting, under
\ell_{\infty}
perturbations of the cost matrix (with an exponential dependence in
\lambda^{-1}
).

Before we start the proof, let us state a lemma that only uses convexity of
G_{\lambda}
; the lower-bound is classical and the upper-bound is used later in Section
5
.

Lemma 4.2

(Entropy sandwich)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and consider the “tangent Gibbs distribution”
\nu\propto\exp(-V[\mu]/\tau)
where
V
is the first-variation (
9
) of
G_{\lambda}
. It holds

\displaystyle\tau H(\mu|\mu_{\lambda,\tau})\leq F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu)-F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau})\leq\tau H(\mu|\nu).

Proof.

For brevity, let us write
F=F_{\lambda,\tau}
and
\mu^{*}=\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}
. By convexity of
G_{\lambda}
, we have

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(\mu^{*})+\int V[\mu^{*}]\mathrm{d}(\mu-\mu^{*})\leq G_{\lambda}(\mu)\leq G_{\lambda}(\mu^{*})+\int V[\mu]\mathrm{d}(\mu-\mu^{*}).

Adding
\tau H(\mu)-G_{\lambda}(\mu^{*})-\tau H(\mu^{*})
we get

\displaystyle\int V[\mu^{*}]\mathrm{d}(\mu-\mu^{*})+\tau H(\mu)-\tau H(\mu^{*})\leq F(\mu)-F(\mu^{*})\leq\int V[\mu]\mathrm{d}(\mu-\mu^{*})+\tau H(\mu)-\tau H(\mu^{*})

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tau H(\mu|\mu^{*})-\tau H(\mu^{*}|\mu^{*})\leq F(\mu)-F(\mu^{*})\leq\tau H(\mu|\nu)-\tau H(\mu^{*}|\nu)

using
\mu^{*}\propto e^{-V[\mu^{*}]/\tau}
(Thm.
2.5
). The claim follows from
H(\mu^{*}|\mu^{*})=0
and
H(\mu^{*}|\nu)\geq 0
.
∎

Proof of Thm.
4.1
.

Let us start with an application of the previous lemma (we put hats on the quantity defined using the empirical measures
(\hat{\nu}_{k})_{k=1}^{K}
):

\displaystyle\tau H(\hat{\mu}|\mu^{*})

\displaystyle\leq F(\hat{\mu})-F(\mu^{*})

\displaystyle=[F(\hat{\mu})-\hat{F}(\hat{\mu})]+[\hat{F}(\hat{\mu})-\hat{F}(\mu^{*})]+[\hat{F}(\mu^{*})-F(\mu^{*})]

\displaystyle\leq G_{\lambda}(\hat{\mu})-\hat{G}_{\lambda}(\hat{\mu})+0+\hat{G}_{\lambda}(\mu^{*})-G_{\lambda}(\mu^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\big(T_{\lambda}(\hat{\mu},\nu_{k})-T_{\lambda}(\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu}_{k})\big)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\big(T_{\lambda}(\mu^{*},\hat{\nu}_{k})-T_{\lambda}(\mu^{*},\nu_{k})\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}|T_{\lambda}(\mu,\hat{\nu}_{k})-T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu_{k})|

Now by convexity of
T_{\lambda}
in
\nu_{k}
, we have (denoting
\psi_{\mu,\nu}
the Schrödinger potential from
\nu
to
\mu
):

\int\psi_{\mu,\hat{\nu}_{k}}\mathrm{d}[\nu-\hat{\nu}]\leq T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu_{k})-T_{\lambda}(\mu,\hat{\nu}_{k})\leq\int\psi_{\mu,\nu_{k}}\mathrm{d}[\nu-\hat{\nu}].

Given any norm
\|\cdot\|
on the set of continuous functions defined up to constants, denoting
\|\sigma\|_{*}=\sup_{\|f\|\leq 1}\int f\mathrm{d}\sigma
the dual norm on the space of signed measures with
0
total mass, it follows

\tau H(\hat{\mu}|\mu^{*})\leq 2C\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\|\nu_{k}-\hat{\nu}_{k}\|_{*}

where
C=\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\|\psi_{\mu,\nu}\|
. Let us now consider the two claims separately.

(i)
If
c(x,\cdot)
is
L
-Lipschitz for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then it can be seen from the Schrödinger system (
7
) that
\psi_{\mu,\nu}
is also
L
-Lipschitz. Taking the Lipschitz semi-norm
\|f\|=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|y-x|}
, the dual of which is the Kantorovich-Rubinstein norm
\|\nu_{k}-\hat{\nu}_{k}\|_{*}=W_{1}(\nu_{k},\hat{\nu}_{k})
gives the first claim.

(ii)
If
c\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{p}
then by differentiating the Schrödinger system
p
times it follows that
\psi_{\mu,\nu}
is
p
times differentiable with
\|\psi_{\mu,\nu}\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{p}}\leq C(1+\lambda^{1-p})
where
C>0
is a constant deoending on the cost
[
GCBCP19
]
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is assumed compact, it follows that the homogeneous Sobolev norm
\|\psi_{\mu,\nu}\|_{\dot{H}^{p}}
admits the same bound (up to a constant depending on the diameter of
\mathcal{X}
). The conclusion follows from the fact that the norms
\dot{H}^{p}
and
\dot{H}^{-p}
are dual to each other.
∎

4.2
Estimation from independent samples

In this section, we consider the statistical properties
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters. Assume that we dispose of
n
independent samples
x^{(k)}_{1},\dots,x^{(k)}_{n}
from each of the marginals
\nu_{k}
. Let
\hat{\mu}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
be the the plug-in estimator of the barycenter, defined as the
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenter between the empirical marginals
\hat{\nu}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}^{(k)}}
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\tau,\lambda>0
, define
d^{\prime}=2\lfloor d/2\rfloor
and assume that
c\in\mathcal{C}^{1+d^{\prime}/2}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})
. Let
\hat{\mu}_{\lambda,\tau}
be the empirical barycenter and
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}
the population barycenter. Then there is
C>0
independent of
(\nu_{k})_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[H(\hat{\mu}_{\lambda,\tau}|\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau})]\leq C\tau^{-1}(1+\lambda^{-d^{\prime}/2})n^{-1/2}.

Proof.

We follow a strategy similar to that used for the sample complexity of EOT
[
GCBCP19
]
. In Thm.
4.1
, one can replace the homogeneous Sobolev norm
\dot{H}^{-p}
by the (larger) inhomogeneous norm
H^{-p}
. For
p=1+d^{\prime}/2
, it is known that
H^{p}
is a Reproducible Kernel Hilbert space norm, and by standard empirical process theory results
[
BM02
]
one has

\mathbf{E}\|\hat{\nu}_{k}-\nu_{k}\|_{H^{-p}}\leq Cn^{-1/2}.\qed

Related work

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first estimation rate for an OT-like barycenter that does not suffer from the curse of dimensionality. The rate of estimation for
(0,\tau)
-barycenter was studied in
[
Big20
]
, where the rate is cursed by the dimension because then the bound of Thm.
4.1
-(i) involves the quantity
W_{1}(\nu_{k},\hat{\nu}_{k})
which is of order
n^{-1/d}
for
d>2

[
FG15
]
. In the same paper, they also studied
(\lambda,\lambda)
-barycenter but on a discrete space. The estimation of barycenters on discrete spaces is a rich topic but with a very different behavior
[
HKM23
,
HMZ22
]
. See
[
PZ19
]
for an introduction to statistical aspects of OT, including barycenters. We also note that there is a line of works (see e.g.
[
ALP20
,
LPRS22
]
) that studies the estimation of barycenters given marginals
(\nu_{k})_{k}
sampled from a distribution in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}))
(as in (
17
)) which is a rich but different problem.

5
Optimization with Noisy Particle Gradient Descent

We consider the computation of
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters when the marginals
(\nu_{k})_{k}
are discrete with
n
atoms each. In this case, the size of the problem is given by
n
(the number of atoms),
K
(the number of marginals) and
d
(the ambiant dimension).

For small scale problems where one of these quantities is small, plenty of algorithms from the literature exist. For instance when
n
or
K
is small, one can directly solve a linear program of size
O(n^{K})
– the multimarginal formulation
[
AC11
]
– to compute the
(0,0)
-barycenter. When
d
is small, there exists efficient exact methods
[
AB21
]
. In that case, an alternative is to discretize the space, and use convex optimization algorithms to solve Eq. (
4
). This includes approaches based on linear programming
[
ABM16
,
GWXY19
]
, entropic regularization
[
CD14
,
BCCNP15
]
or decentralized and randomized algorithms
[
DDGUN18
,
SCSJ17
,
HMZ22
]
, see
[
PC19
]
for a review. This approach also applies for
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters (we compute 1D barycenters with this method in Section
6
).

In this section, we focus on large scale problems (
n,d,K\gg 1
) where these discrete approaches are intractable and “free support” methods become relevant. A stream of recent work proposed methods based on neural networks
[
KLSB20
,
CAD20
,
LGYS20
,
FTC20
]
. These methods come with the advantages (useful statistical prior, reasonable iteration complexity) and the drawbacks (lack of optimization guarantees) of neural networks. Particle-based methods, which are closer in spirit to what follows, have been proposed such as fixed-point methods akin to Loyd’s algorithm
[
ÁDCM16
,
CCS18
,
Lin23
,
BFRT22a
]
and a particle gradient method
[
DGLT21
]
for Wasserstein barycenters. Note that it is shown in
[
AB22
]
that Wasserstein barycenters are NP-hard to compute in large dimension. A Franck-Wolfe algorithm
[
LSPC19
]
was proposed for Sinkhorn divergence barycenters.

In what follows, we propose a grid-free numerical method which is particularly well-suited to the structure of the problem of Eq. (
4
), called Noisy Particle Gradient Descent (NPGD). We defer a detailed complexity analysis of this method to future works, and limit ourselves to a introduction of the algorithm with its exponential guaranty in the mean-field limit, which is an application of
[
Chi22
,
NWS22
]
.

5.1
Noisy Particle Gradient Descent

We parameterize the unknown measure as a mixture of
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
particles
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{j}}
. Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{m}
encode the position of all particles and consider the function

G_{m}(\mathbf{X})\coloneqq G_{\lambda}\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{j}}\Big).

(25)

The NPGD algorithm we consider is simply noisy gradient descent on
G_{m}
, with an initialization sampled from some
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. It is defined, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, as

\mathbf{X}[\ell+1]=\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}\big(\mathbf{X}[\ell]-m\eta\nabla G_{m}(\mathbf{X}[\ell])+\sqrt{2\eta\tau}\mathbf{Z}[\ell]\big),\quad\mathbf{X}[0]\sim\mu_{0}^{\otimes m}

(26)

where
\tau>0
is the outer-regularization strength,
\eta>0
is the step-size,
\mathbf{Z}[1],\mathbf{Z}[2],\dots
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian vectors and
\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{X}}
is the Euclidean projection on
\mathcal{X}
.
Note that to compute
\nabla G_{m}
one needs to solve, at each iteration,
K
regularized optimal transport problems. In the small step-size limit
\eta\to 0
and setting
t=k\eta
, NPGD leads to a system of SDEs coupled via the empirical distribution of particles
\hat{\mu}_{t}
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}X_{i}(t)&=-\nabla V[\hat{\mu}_{t}](X_{i}(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\tau}\mathrm{d}B_{t,i}+\mathrm{d}\Phi_{t,i},\quad X_{i}(0)\sim\mu_{0}\\
\hat{\mu}_{t}&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{X_{i}(t)}\end{aligned}\right.

(27)

where
(B_{t,i})_{t\geq 0}
are independent Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathrm{d}\Phi_{t,i}
is a boundary reflection (in the sense of Skorokhod problem) and
V[\mu]\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{X})
is the
first-variation
of
G_{\lambda}
at
\mu
(see Definition
9
). The latter satisfies
V[\hat{\mu}](X_{i})=m\nabla_{X_{i}}G_{m}(\mathbf{X})
, hence (
26
) is just the Euler-Maruyama discretization of (
27
) below.

5.2
Mean-Field Langevin dynamics

In the many-particle
m\to\infty
limit, it can be shown that the particles behave like independent sample paths from the nonlinear SDE of McKean-Vlasov type:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}X_{t}&=-\nabla V[\mu_{t}](X(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\tau}\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathrm{d}\Phi_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}\\
\mu_{t}&=\mathrm{Law}(X_{t})\end{aligned}\right.

(28)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion and
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a boundary reflection.
Moreover, the distribution
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of particles solves the evolution equation

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\nabla\cdot\big(\mu_{t}\nabla V_{\lambda}[\mu_{t}]\big)+\tau\Delta\mu_{t}

(29)

starting from
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
where
\nabla\cdot
stands for the divergence operator. By solution of (
29
) here, we mean an curve
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from
\mu_{0}
that is absolutely continuous in Wasserstein space and satisfies Eq. (
29
) in the sense of distributions with no-flux boundary conditions.

This drift-diffusion equation is an instance of
Mean-Field Langevin
dynamics, a class of drift-diffusion dynamics studied in
[
MMN18
,
HRŠS21
,
NWS22
,
Chi22
]
. It can be interpreted as the gradient flow of the functional
F_{\lambda}
of Eq. (
4
) under the
W_{2}
Wasserstein metric.

In our theoretical analysis, we focus on the analysis of the mean-field limit (
29
). For quantitative convergence results in the many-particle
m\to\infty
and small step-size
\eta\to 0
limits, one can refer to the classical work
[
Szn91
]
. The particular case of reflecting boundary conditions has been treated in
[
JMM20
]
, following earlier works on the analysis of SDEs with reflection
[
Tan79
,
LS84
]
.

5.3
Exponential convergence

It has been shown independently in
[
NWS22
]
and
[
Chi22
]
that if the function
G_{\lambda}
is convex, regular enough and that a certain family of log-Sobolev inequalities holds, then dynamics of the form Eq. (
29
) converge at an exponential rate to the unique minimizer of
F_{\lambda}
. Let us apply this result in our context, where this leads to a dynamics that directly converges to the
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenter.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
c\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
(where, we recall,
\mathcal{X}
is compact) and let
\lambda,\tau>0
. Then there exists a unique solution to (
29
). Moreover, there exists
\rho_{\lambda}>0
such that if
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is such that
F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu_{0})<\infty
, then it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}H(\mu_{t}|\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau})\leq F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu_{t})-F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau})\leq e^{-\rho_{\lambda}t}\big(F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu_{0})-F_{\lambda,\tau}(\mu^{*}_{\lambda})\big).

Proof.

We have semi-convexity of
F_{\lambda,\tau}
along Wasserstein geodesics, by
[
CCM22
, Thm. 4.1]
for the first component
G_{\lambda}
and by a standard result
[
San15
]
for the
H
component. Thus the general well-posedness results from
[
AGS05
]
applies. For the exponential convergence – in function value and in relative entropy – we apply the result from
[
Chi22
, Thm. 3.2]
, see also
[
NWS22
]
where the same argument was discovered independently (although stated on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the argument goes through on a compact domain).

The main assumptions to check are that (i)
\mu\mapsto G_{\lambda}(\mu)
is convex (Prop.
2.3
), (ii) that a global minimizer
\mu^{*}_{\lambda,\tau}
exists (Thm.
2.5
) and finally we need to check that the probability measure
\hat{\mu}_{t}\propto e^{-V[\mu_{t}]}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality, uniformly in
t
(Assumption 3 in
[
Chi22
]
).

Since
\mathcal{X}
is bounded, the normalized Lebesgue measure satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality
[
Led99
, Thm. 7.3]
. By the Holley-Stroock perturbation criterion
[
HS86
]

\hat{\mu}_{t}
satisfies it as well; this criterion applies here because
\sup_{x}V[\mu](x)-\inf_{x}V[\mu](x)
is bounded, uniformly in
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
by Prop.
2.3
.
∎

The contraction rate
\rho_{\lambda}
is of the form
\tau e^{-L\cdot\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})/\tau}
where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
c
(and
L\cdot\mathrm{diam}(\mathcal{X})
is a uniform bound on
\|V[\mu]\|_{\mathrm{osc}}
). It thus approaches
1
exponentially fast as
\tau
decreases. We also note that both inner and outer regularizations are needed to obtain Thm.
5.1
: in particular the inner-regularization is necessary to obtain well-posedness of the PDE (
29
).

Conclusion

We have proposed doubly-regularized EOT barycenters which is a formulation of barycenters over
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
that has several benefits: (i) it gathers most previously studied OT-like barycenters in a single framework, (ii) it leads to several desirable properties such as regularity, stability and approximation of Wasserstein barycenters (for
\tau=\lambda/2
), and (iii) it lends itself naturally to both convex optimization methods and grid-free methods.

In this paper, we have covered a diverse range of theoretical topics (regularity, approximation, statistics, optimization) as it is really all these aspects that together make this family of objects stand out. In future work, it will be desirable to develop in depth analyses of specialized points, such as the computational complexity of
(\lambda,\tau)
-barycenters.

Closed forms for isotropic Gaussians

In this section, we consider the simplest setting of centered isotropic Gaussian probability measures and rely on closed forms for entropic optimal transport. We consider

\displaystyle\min_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)+\tau H(\nu)

(30)

for
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,a)
where
a>0
is the variance. This is enough to recover the general case of barycenters of isotropic Gaussians with identical variances since the effect of having different means can be factored out as in
[
JMPC20
]
. Note that
[
BBB20
]
also considered this problem but did not obtain a closed form solution. Let us look for a Gaussian solution of the form
\nu=\mathcal{N}(0,b)
. By
[
JMPC20
]
, we have that the optimal dual potentials are
\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{u}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
and
\psi=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{v}\|y\|^{2}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2b}{\xi+\lambda},

\displaystyle\tilde{v}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2a}{\xi+\lambda},

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4ab+\lambda^{2}}

where the variables from
[
JMPC20
]
and our variables are related by
\sigma^{2}\rightarrow\lambda
,
U\rightarrow-\lambda\tilde{u}I
,
V\rightarrow-\lambda\tilde{v}I
,
C\rightarrow I\cdot(\xi-\lambda)/2
. Also the EOT cost and entropy is

\displaystyle\frac{2}{d}T_{\lambda}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=a+b-\xi+\lambda\log(\xi+\lambda)+C,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{d}\tau H(\mu)

\displaystyle=-\tau\log(b)+C

where
C
denotes a constant independent of
a
and
b
. When restricted to
\nu
of the form
\mathcal{N}(0,b)
, the optimization problem thus becomes (keeping in mind that
\xi
depends on
b
)

\min_{b>0}a+b-\xi+\lambda\log(\xi+\lambda)-\tau\log(b).

The first order optimality condition reads

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2a}{\xi+\lambda}-\frac{\tau}{b}=0

(31)

Now, let us argue that if
b
satisfies this equation, then
\nu
is in fact the unique minimizer of (
30
). Indeed, (
30
) admits a unique solution characterized by the first order optimality condition (which can be justified, in this non-compact setting, as in
[
JMPC20
]
):

\log\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big)+\frac{\psi_{\mu,\nu}}{\tau}=C

But if
\nu=\mathcal{N}(0,b)
with
b
solution to (
31
) then it holds

\displaystyle\tilde{v}

\displaystyle=\tau/b

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{\mu,\nu}(y)}{\tau}-\frac{\|y\|^{2}_{2}}{2b}=0

(32)

which proves that
\nu
is indeed the unique solution to our problem.

Explicit solution.

Using
b=(\xi^{2}-\lambda^{2})/(4a)
, let us express (
31
) in terms of
\xi
:

\displaystyle b(\xi+\lambda)-2ab-\tau(\xi+\lambda)=0\Leftrightarrow\frac{\xi+\lambda}{4a}(\xi^{2}-2a\xi-\lambda^{2}+2a\lambda-4a\tau)=0.

Solving for
\xi
and taking the largest solution (which must be the unique solution leading to
b\geq 0
) leads to

\displaystyle\xi=a+\sqrt{(a-\lambda)^{2}+4a\tau}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle b=\frac{\big(a+\sqrt{(a-\lambda)^{2}+4a\tau}\big)^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{4a}.

Let us now consider some particular cases:

•

In the limit
\tau\to 0
, we have

b=\frac{(a+|a-\lambda|)^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{4a}=\begin{cases}a-\lambda&\text{if $a>\lambda$}\\
0&\text{if $a\leq\lambda$}\end{cases}

•

In the limit
\lambda\to 0
, we have

b=\frac{a}{4}\big(1+\sqrt{1+4\tau/a}\big)^{2}=a+2\tau+O(\tau^{2})

•

for
\tau=\lambda
, we have

b=\frac{(a+|a+\lambda|)^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{4a}=a+\lambda

•

for
\tau=\lambda/2
, we have

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{(a+\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda^{2}})^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{4a}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}+a^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2a\sqrt{a^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\lambda^{2}}{4a}

\displaystyle=\frac{2a^{2}+2a^{2}\sqrt{1+(\lambda/a)^{2}}}{4a}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+(\lambda/a)^{2}})=a+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4a}+O(\lambda^{4})

Best choice of
\tau

Let us now solve for
\tau
such that
b=a
, which we know is asymptotically
\tau\sim\lambda/2
. It holds

\displaystyle b-a=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(a+\sqrt{(a-\lambda)^{2}+4a\tau})^{2}-\lambda^{2}-4a^{2}=0.

Let us define the intermediate unknown
\chi=\sqrt{(a-\lambda)^{2}+4a\tau}\Leftrightarrow\tau=(\chi^{2}-(a-\lambda)^{2})/(4a)
. It follows

\displaystyle(a+\chi)^{2}-\lambda^{2}-4a^{2}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\chi^{2}+2a\chi-\lambda^{2}-3a^{2}=0.

Solving for
\chi
, with the constraint
\chi\geq 0
gives

\displaystyle\chi=\sqrt{4a^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-a

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{(\sqrt{4a^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-a)^{2}-(a-\lambda)^{2}}{4a}.

Developing the squares and rearranging we get

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{4a^{2}+-2a\sqrt{4a^{2}+\lambda^{2}}+2a\lambda}{4a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}+a\Big(1-\Big(1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4a^{2}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8a}+O(\lambda^{4}/a^{3}).
