Canonization of a random graph by two matrix-vector multiplications A preliminary version of this paper was presented at the 31st Annual European Symposium on Algorithms, ESA 2023 [ 21 ] .
Oleg Verbitsky, Maksim Zhukovskii

Abstract
We show that a canonical labeling of a random n n -vertex graph can be obtained by assigning to each vertex x x the triple ( w 1 ​ ( x ) , w 2 ​ ( x ) , w 3 ​ ( x ) ) (w_{1}(x),w_{2}(x),w_{3}(x)) , where w k ​ ( x ) w_{k}(x) is the number of walks of length k k starting from x x . This takes time 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2}) , where n 2 n^{2} is the input size, by using just two matrix-vector multiplications. The linear-time canonization of a random graph is the classical result of Babai, Erdős, and Selkow. For this purpose they use the well-known combinatorial color refinement procedure, and we make a comparative analysis of the two algorithmic approaches.

Introduction

A
walk
in a graph
G=(V,E)
is a sequence of vertices
x_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{k}
such that

(x_{i},x_{i+1})\in E
for every
0\leq i<k
. We say that
x_{0}x_{1}\ldots x_{k}

is a walk of
length
k
from
x_{0}
to
x_{k}
.
For a vertex
x\in V
, let
w^{G}_{k}(x)
denote the total number of walks of length
k
in
G
starting from
x
.
Furthermore, we define
\mathbf{w}^{G}_{k}(x)=(w^{G}_{1}(x),\ldots,w^{G}_{k}(x))
.

The Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
is a graph on the vertex set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}

where each pair of distinct vertices
x
and
y
is adjacent with probability
p
independently
of the other pairs. In particular,
G(n,1/2)
is a random graph chosen equiprobably from among
all graphs on
[n]
.
Our main result in this paper shows that every vertex
x
in
G(n,1/2)
is with high probability
individualized by the numbers of walks from
x
of lengths 1, 2, and 3, and that the maximum length 3
is optimal for this purpose.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G=G(n,1/2)
. Then

1.

\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(x)\neq\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(y)
for all
x\neq y

with probability at least
1-O(\sqrt[4]{\ln n/n})
;

2.

\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(x)\neq\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(y)
for all
x\neq y

with probability approaching 0 as
n\to\infty
.

If
\alpha
is an isomorphism from a graph
G
to a graph
H
,
then clearly
\mathbf{w}^{G}_{k}(x)=\mathbf{w}^{H}_{k}(\alpha(x))
.
Theorem
1
, therefore, shows that the map
x\mapsto\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(x)

is a canonical labeling of
G
for almost all
n
-vertex graphs
G
.
This labeling is easy to compute. Indeed,
if
A
is the adjacency matrix of
G
and
\mathbbm{1}
is the all-ones vector-column of length
n
, then

(w^{G}_{k}(1),\ldots,w^{G}_{k}(n))^{\top}=A^{k}\mathbbm{1}.

After noting that
w^{G}_{1}(x)=d(x)
, where
d(x)
denotes the degree of a vertex
x
,
this yields the following simple canonical labeling algorithm.

Algorithm A

Input:
a graph
G
on
[n]
with adjacency matrix
A
.

1.

Form a vector
D_{1}=(d(1),\ldots,d(n))^{\top}
.

2.

Compute
D_{2}=AD_{1}
and
D_{3}=AD_{2}
.

3.

Let
W
be the matrix formed by the three columns
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}

and let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}
be the rows of
W
.

4.

If there are identical rows
W_{x}=W_{y}
for some
x\neq y
, then give up. Otherwise,

5.

to each vertex
x
, assign the label
W_{x}
.

Corollary 2
.

Algorithm A with high probability canonizes a random
n
-vertex graph, taking
time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
on every input.

The notation
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
in the time bound means a linear function up to
the logarithmic factor
\log n\log\log n
corresponding to the complexity
of integer multiplication
[
11
]
, that is,
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})=O(n^{2}\log n\log\log n)
.
If the model of computation assumes that multiplication of two integers
takes a constant time, then we just set
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})=O(n^{2})
.
This time bound stems from the fact that
the two matrix-vector multiplications in Step 2 are the most expensive
operations performed by the algorithm. Note that this bound is essentially linear
because a random graph is with high probability dense, having
(1/4+o(1))n^{2}
edges.

The linear-time canonization of almost all graphs is a classical result of
Babai, Erdős, and Selkow
[
3
]
, which was a basis for settling
the average-case complexity of graph isomorphism in
[
4
]
.
While our algorithm is based solely on basic linear-algebraic primitives,
the method used in
[
3
,
4
]
is purely combinatorial.
Before comparing the two approaches, we put Theorem
1

in the context of the earlier work on walk counts and their applications
to isomorphism testing.

Of course, Algorithm A can be enhanced by taking into account
also longer walks, that is, by involving also other vector-columns
A^{k}\mathbbm{1}
for
k>3
.
Note that there is no gain in considering these vectors for
k\geq n
.
Indeed, if
A^{k}\mathbbm{1}
is a linear combination of the vectors
\mathbbm{1},A\mathbbm{1},A^{2}\mathbbm{1},\ldots,A^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}
,
the same is obviously true also for
A^{k+1}\mathbbm{1}
(cf.
[
18
, Lemma 1]

and see also
[
10
]
for a more detailed linear-algebraic analysis). Therefore, it suffices to
start our matrix
W
from the column
\mathbbm{1}
and add a subsequent column
A^{k}\mathbbm{1}

as long as this increases the rank of
W
, which is possible only up to
k=n-1
.
The
n\times n
matrix
W=W^{G}
formed by the columns
\mathbbm{1},A\mathbbm{1},\ldots,A^{n-1}\mathbbm{1}

is called the
walk matrix
of the graph
G
(
WM
for brevity).
The entries of
W^{G}=(w_{x,k})_{1\leq x\leq n,\,0\leq k<n}

are nothing else as the walk counts
w_{x,k}=w^{G}_{k}(x)
. Note that
w^{G}_{0}(x)=1
as there is a single
walk of length
0
from
x
.

We say that a graph
G
is
WM-discrete
if all rows of the walk matrix
W^{G}
are pairwise different, i.e.,

W^{G}_{x}\neq W^{G}_{y}
for all
x\neq y
, where
W^{G}_{x}=(w_{0}^{G}(x),w_{1}^{G}(x),\ldots,w_{n-1}^{G}(x))
.
If
G
is WM-discrete, then the walk matrix yields a canonical labeling
by assigning each vertex
x
the vector
W^{G}_{x}
.
A useful observation is that if
W^{G}
has identical rows, then this matrix is singular; cf.
[
9
, Section 7]
.
O’Rourke and Touri
[
17
]
prove that the walk matrix of a random graph
is non-singular with high probability. This implies that a random graph is WM-discrete
with high probability and, hence, almost all graphs
are canonizable by computing the
n\times n
walk matrix similarly to
Algorithm A. We remark that this takes time
O(n^{3})
,
which is outperformed by our Corollary
2
due to using the truncated
variant of WM of size
n\times 4
.

Remarkably, non-singular walk matrices can be used to test isomorphism
of two given graphs directly rather than by computing their canonical forms.
If graphs
G
and
H
are
isomorphic, then their walk matrices
W^{G}
and
W^{H}
can be obtained from one another by rearranging the rows.
If the last condition is satisfied, we write
W^{G}\approx W^{H}
. This relation between
matrices is efficiently checkable just by sorting the rows in the lexicographic order.
We say that a graph
G
is
WM-identifiable
if, conversely,
for all
H
we have
G\cong H
whenever
W^{G}\approx W^{H}
.
Liu and Siemons
[
13
]
prove that if the walk matrix of a graph
is non-singular, then it uniquely determines the adjacency matrix.
This implies by
[
17
]
that a random graph is WM-identifiable with high probability.

Note that, by a simple counting argument, almost all
n
-vertex graphs
cannot
be identified
by the shorter version of the walk matrix of size
n\times k
as long as
k=o(\sqrt{n/\log n})
.
In particular, Theorem
1
cannot be extended to the identifiability concept.

The combinatorial approach of Babai, Erdős, and Selkow
[
3
]
is based on the
color refinement
procedure
(
CR
for brevity) dating back to the sixties (e.g.,
[
15
]
).
CR begins with a uniform coloring of all vertices in an input graph
and iteratively refines a current coloring according to the following principle:
If two vertices are equally colored but have
distinct color frequencies in their neighborhoods, then they get distinct colors in the next refinement
step. The refinement steps are executed as long as the refinement is proper. As soon as
the color classes stay the same, CR terminates and outputs the current coloring
(a detailed description of the algorithm is given in Section
3.1
).
CR
distinguishes
graphs
G
and
H
if their color palettes are distinct.
A graph
G
is called
CR-identifiable
if it is distinguishable by CR from every non-isomorphic
H
.
CR can also be used for computing a canonical labeling of a single input graph.
We say that a graph
G
is
CR-discrete
if CR assigns a unique color to each vertex of
G
.
It is easy to prove that every CR-discrete graph is CR-identifiable.
We do not know whether or not this is true also for the corresponding WM concepts.

Powers and Sulaiman
[
18
]
discuss examples when the CR-partition and the WM-partition are different,
that is, CR and the WM-based vertex-classification algorithm give different results. In particular,

[
18
, Fig. 3]
shows a graph which is, in our terminology, CR-discrete but not WM-discrete.
We give a finer information about the relationship between the two algorithmic approaches.

Theorem 3
.

1.

Every WM-discrete graph is also CR-discrete.

2.

Every WM-identifiable graph is also CR-identifiable.

3.

There is a graph that is

(a)

CR-discrete (hence also CR-identifiable) and

(b)

neither WM-discrete

(c)

nor WM-identifiable.

Theorem
3
shows that color refinement is at least as good as the WM approach
with regard to canonization of a single input graph as well as testing isomorphism of two
input graphs. Moreover, CR is sometimes more successful with respect to both algorithmic problems.
Thus, WM can be regarded as a weaker algorithmic tool for canonical labeling and isomorphism testing,
which is not so surprising as this approach is actually based on a single basic linear-algebraic primitive,
namely matrix-vector multiplication.
Corollary
2
demonstrates, therefore, that a random graph can be canonized in an essentially
linear time even with less powerful computational means than color refinement.

Theorems
1
and
3
are proved in Sections
2

and
3
respectively. We conclude the current section with giving a high level otline
of the proofs.

For Part 1 of Theorem
1
, it suffices to fix an arbitrary pair of vertices, say, 1 and 2,
and to show that
\mathbf{w}_{3}^{G}(1)=\mathbf{w}_{3}^{G}(2)
with probability
O\left((\ln n/n)^{1/4}/n^{2}\right)
.
While the asymptotics of
\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(1)=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(2)]
can be determined exactly by using
the local de Moivre–Laplace limit theorem and exponential tail bounds for binomial distributions
(cf. Equality (
12
)), including the walks of lengths 3 in the analysis is more challenging.
The main idea that allows us to address this challenge is that after exposing the 2-neighbourhoods of
the vertices 1 and 2
under the condition
\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(1)=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(2)
, with high probability there is a rather large set
S

of pairs of vertices
i,j
adjacent neither to 1 nor to 2 such that all edges
\{i,j\}\in S
contribute to
the difference
w_{3}^{G}(1)-w_{3}^{G}(2)
with exactly the same weight
\Delta(i,j)
(see the details in Section
2.2
).
It follows that if we additionally expose the adjacencies for all pairs of vertices outside
S
,
then
\mathbf{w}_{3}^{G}(1)=\mathbf{w}_{3}^{G}(2)
exactly when the total number of edges within
S

is equal to some fixed number uniquely determined by the exposed edges. The last probability is
easily estimated by standard tools.

For Part 2 of Theorem
1
, we define the random variable
X
as the total number of
vertex pairs
i,j
such that
\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(i)=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(j)
. The expected number of such pairs
increases together with
n
, as the aforementioned tight bounds for
\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(1)=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(2)]

imply that
\mathsf{E}X=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. This is still not enough to conclude that
X\geq 1
with
high probability. The main part of the proof in Section
2.3
is devoted to showing that

\var(X)=o((\mathsf{E}X)^{2})
, which implies the desired assertion by Chebyshev’s inequality.
Note that for disjoint vertex pairs
i,j
and
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
, the events
\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(i)=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(j)

and
\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(i^{\prime})=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(j^{\prime})
are not independent. Thus, they contribute non-trivially
to the variance, making the estimation of
\var(X)
difficult. The main ingredient of the proof is
Claim
2.3
saying that this contribution in
o(n^{-3})
. This estimate follows from the asymptotic
independence of the events
\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(i)=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(j)
and
\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(i^{\prime})=\mathbf{w}_{2}^{G}(j^{\prime})

subject to the typical 1-neighbourhood of
\{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
and the equalities
w_{1}^{G}(i)=w_{1}^{G}(j)
and

w_{1}^{G}(i^{\prime})=w_{1}^{G}(j^{\prime})
. This asymptotic independence is quite unexpected since the sets of edges adjacent
to the 1-neighbourhoods of
\{i,j\}
and
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
have a large intersection and their impacts on the
two events are, therefore, far from being independent. The proof of this surprising fact reduces to
establishing a local limit theorem for certain random vectors
\Psi_{n}
represented as sums of independent
but not identically distributed random vectors. To this end, we first derive an integral limit theorem
based on a helpful observation that the non-diagonal elements of the covariance matrices of
\Psi_{n}

are much smaller than their diagonal elements. The final step is made by using the general framework
for conversion of an integral limit theorem into a local limit theorem provided in
[
16
]
.

The first two parts of Theorem
3
follow from the fact that the walk numbers

w^{G}_{k}(x)
are determined by the color assigned to the vertex
x
by the CR algorithm; cf.
[
7
,
18
]
.
The similar relationship holds true for the number
w^{G}_{k}(x,y)
of
k
-walks between two
vertices
x
and
y
and the 2-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm, which can be seen
as a natural extension of CR for computing a canonical coloring of vertex pairs.
In the special case of strongly regular graphs, this relationship just means that
the value of
w^{G}_{k}(x,y)
is the same for all pairs of adjacent
x
and
y
as well as
for all pairs of non-adjacent
x
and
y
. This property is useful for proving Part 3
of Theorem
3
as it suggests searching for two partial colorings of
the same strongly regular graph that are distinguished by CR but are indistinguishable
in terms of the walk counts
w^{G}_{k}(x,y)
. We find such two colorings for the Shrikhande graph.
The rest of the proof consists in removing the auxiliary vertex colors by gluing two colored versions
of the Shrikhande graph together via a simple connector-gadget so that the colors are simulated
by the changed vertex degrees. We remark that this idea proves to be useful also
in another context. In the subsequent work
[
2
]
, a similar approach is used to
separate the power of the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm and some spectral characteristics of graphs.

2
Canonization of a random graph

2.1
Probability preliminaries

Let
X
be a binomial random variable with parameters
n
and
p
,
that is,
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
where
X_{i}
’s are mutually independent and, for each
i
, we have
X_{i}=1
with probability 1 and

X_{i}=0
with probability
1-p
. We use the notation
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
when
X
has this distribution.
As well known,
X
is well-concentrated around its expectation
np
.

Lemma 4

(Chernoff’s bound; see, e.g.,
[
1
, Corollary A.1.7]
)
.

If
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
, then

\mathsf{P}[|X-np|>t]\leq 2e^{-2t^{2}/n}

for every
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 5
.

If
X
and
Y
are independent random variables, each having the probability distribution
\mathrm{Bin}(n,1/2)
,
then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\left(1-\frac{1}{8n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right)\leq\mathsf{P}[X=Y]<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.

Proof.

Note that

\mathsf{P}[X=Y]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left({n\choose k}2^{-n}\right)^{2}=2^{-2n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\choose k}^{2}=2^{-2n}{2n\choose n},

where the last equality is a special case of Vandermonde’s convolution.
The lemma now follows from the well-known bounds for the central binomial coefficient, namely the upper bound

{2n\choose n}<\frac{2^{2n}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}

(1)

and the asymptotics
{2n\choose n}=\frac{2^{2n}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\left(1-\frac{1}{8n}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})\right)
; see, e.g.,
[
14
]
.
∎

A version of Lemma
5
could alternatively be proved by considering
the random variable
X+(n-Y)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2n,1/2)
and estimating the probability that
X+(n-Y)=n

with the help of the following classical result (which we state in the form restricted to our purposes).

Lemma 6

(The local de Moivre–Laplace theorem; see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 1, Chapter VII.3]
)
.

Let
\phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}
denote the density function of the standard normal distribution.
Fix a sequence of positive reals
K_{n}=o(n^{2/3})
.
Let
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,1/2)
and suppose that
n
is even. Then

\mathsf{P}[X=\frac{n}{2}+k]=(1+o(1))\frac{2\phi(2k/\sqrt{n})}{\sqrt{n}}=(1+o(1))\frac{e^{-2k^{2}/n}}{\sqrt{\pi n/2}}

where the infinitesimal
o(1)
approaches 0, as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over
k\in[-K_{n},K_{n}]
.

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1
: Part 1

For a vertex
i\in[n]
, recall that
\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(i)=(w^{G}_{1}(i),w^{G}_{2}(i),w^{G}_{3}(i))
.
By the union bound,

\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(i)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(j)\text{ for some }i,j]\leq\sum_{i,j}\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(i)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(j)]={n\choose 2}\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(1)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(2)].

Therefore, it suffices to prove that

\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(1)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(2)]=O(n^{-9/4}\ln^{1/4}n).

(2)

Let
N_{H}(v)
denote the neighborhood of a vertex
v
in a graph
H
.
Given two sets
U_{1}\subset[n]\setminus\{1\}
and
U_{2}\subset[n]\setminus\{2\}
,
let
G^{\prime}=G^{\prime}(U_{1},U_{2})
be the random graph
G
subject to the conditions
N_{G}(1)=U_{1}
and
N_{G}(2)=U_{2}
.
In other terms,
G^{\prime}
is a random graph on
[n]
chosen equiprobably among all graphs
satisfying these conditions.
Let
w^{\prime}_{k}(i)=w^{G^{\prime}}_{k}(i)
denote the number of walks of length
k
emanating from
i
in
G^{\prime}

(the dependence of
w^{\prime}_{k}(i)
on the pair
U_{1},U_{2}
will be dropped for the sake of notational convenience). Define

p(U_{1},U_{2})=\mathsf{P}\left[\sum_{i\in U_{1}}w^{\prime}_{1}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}}w^{\prime}_{1}(i)\text{ and }\sum_{i\in U_{1}}w^{\prime}_{2}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}}w^{\prime}_{2}(i)\right].

We have

\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(1)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(2)]\\
=\sum_{U_{1},U_{2}\,:\,|U_{1}|=|U_{2}|}\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(1)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(2)\mid N_{G}(1)=U_{1},\ N_{G}(2)=U_{2}]\\
\mbox{}\hskip 85.35826pt\times\mathsf{P}[N_{G}(1)=U_{1},\,N_{G}(2)=U_{2}]\\
=\sum_{U_{1},U_{2}\,:\,|U_{1}|=|U_{2}|}p(U_{1},U_{2})\times\mathsf{P}[N_{G}(1)=U_{1},\,N_{G}(2)=U_{2}].

(3)

Note first that

\sum_{|U_{1}|=|U_{2}|}\mathsf{P}\left[N_{G}(1)=U_{1},\,N_{G}(2)=U_{2}\right]=\mathsf{P}\left[|N_{G}(1)|=|N_{G}(2)|\right]\\
=\mathsf{P}\left[|N_{G}(1)\setminus\{2\}|=|N_{G}(2)\setminus\{1\}|\right]=O(n^{-1/2})

by Lemma
5
because
|N_{G}(1)\setminus\{2\}|\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-2,1/2)

and
|N_{G}(2)\setminus\{1\}|\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-2,1/2)
are independent binomial random variables.
This allows us to derive (
2
) from (
3
) if we prove that

p(U_{1},U_{2})=O(n^{-7/4}\ln^{1/4}n)

(4)

for the neighborhood sets
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
.

In fact, we do not need to prove (
4
) for all pairs
U_{1},U_{2}
because the
contribution of some of them in (

) is negligible. Indeed, set
\varepsilon(n)=n^{-1/4}
.
Note that
|N_{G}(j)|\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,1/2)
for

and
|(N_{G}(1)\cap N_{G}(2))\setminus\{1,2\}|\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-2,1/4)
.
By the Chernoff bound (see Lemma
4
), we have

(1/2-\varepsilon(n))n\leq|N_{G}(j)|\leq(1/2+\varepsilon(n))n

and

(1/4-\varepsilon(n))n\leq|N_{G}(1)\cap N_{G}(2)|\leq(1/4+\varepsilon(n))n
with probability

1-e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
. Call a pair
U_{1},U_{2}

standard
if
|U_{j}|
for

and
|U_{1}\cap U_{2}|

are in the same ranges. Thus, all non-standard pairs make a negligible contribution in (

),
and we only have to prove (

) for each standard pair

.

For a graph
H
and a subset
U\subset V(H)
, let
E_{H}(U)
denote the set of edges of

H
with at least one vertex in
U
. Given two sets of edges
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and

U_{2}\setminus\{1\}

G^{\prime\prime}=G^{\prime\prime}(U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})

subject to the conditions

E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{1}\setminus\{2\})=\mathcal{E}_{1}\text{ and }E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{2}\setminus\{1\})=\mathcal{E}_{2}.

(5)

Let
w^{\prime\prime}_{k}(i)=w^{G^{\prime\prime}}_{k}(i)
denote the number of walks of length
k
emanating from
i
in
G^{\prime\prime}

(the dependence of

on

Note that Condition (

.
If this equality is true, we call the pair

Using this terminology and notation, we can write

p(U_{1},U_{2})=\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}:\text{ balanced}}\mathsf{P}\left[E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{1}\setminus\{2\})=\mathcal{E}_{1},\,E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{2}\setminus\{1\})=\mathcal{E}_{2}\right]\\
\times\mathsf{P}\left[\sum_{i\in U_{1}}w^{\prime\prime}_{2}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}}w^{\prime\prime}_{2}(i)\right].

(6)

We first show that

\sum_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}:\text{ balanced}}\mathsf{P}\left[E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{1}\setminus\{2\})=\mathcal{E}_{1},\,E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{2}\setminus\{1\})=\mathcal{E}_{2}\right]=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(7)

Note that the sum in the left hand side of (
7
) is equal to the probability that

\sum_{i\in U_{1}}w_{1}^{\prime}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}}w_{1}^{\prime}(i)
. This equality is equivalent to

\sum_{i\in U_{1}\setminus(U_{2}\cup\{2\})}w_{1}^{\prime}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}\setminus(U_{1}\cup\{1\})}w_{1}^{\prime}(i)
,
which in its turn is true if and only if
U_{1}\setminus(U_{2}\cup\{2\})
and
U_{2}\setminus(U_{1}\cup\{1\})

send the same number of edges to
[n]\setminus[(U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\})\setminus(U_{1}\cap U_{2})]
.
Since the pair
U_{1},U_{2}
is standard, these numbers are independent binomial random variables
with
\Theta(n^{2})
trials. Equality (
7
) now follows by Lemma
5
.

We now can derive Equality (
4
) from Equality (
7
) by proving that

\mathsf{P}\left[\sum_{i\in U_{1}}w^{\prime\prime}_{2}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}}w^{\prime\prime}_{2}(i)\right]=O(n^{-3/4}\ln^{1/4}n)

(8)

for each potential pair
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Again, it is enough to
do this only for most probable pairs whose contribution in (
6
) is overwhelming.
Specifically, let
w^{\prime}_{2}(u,v)
denote the number of all paths of length
2
between
u
and
v
in
G^{\prime}

and define

\Delta(i,j)=(w^{\prime}_{2}(i,1)+w^{\prime}_{2}(j,1))-(w^{\prime}_{2}(i,2)+w^{\prime}_{2}(j,2)).

Note that the numbers
w^{\prime}_{2}(i,1),w^{\prime}_{2}(j,1),w^{\prime}_{2}(i,2),w^{\prime}_{2}(j,2)
and, hence, the numbers
\Delta(i,j)

are completely determined by specifying
E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{1}\setminus\{2\})=\mathcal{E}_{1}
and

E_{G^{\prime}}(U_{2}\setminus\{1\})=\mathcal{E}_{2}
. We call a pair
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}

standard
if
\Delta(i,j)
takes on
O(\sqrt{n\ln n})
different values as

i\neq j
ranges over the pairs of vertices from
[n]\setminus(U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\})
.
The following fact shows that it is enough if we prove (
8
)
for each standard pair
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

Claim A.

If a pair
U_{1},U_{2}
is standard, then

|\{\Delta(i,j)\,:\,\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}i,j\in[n]\setminus(U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\}),\,i\neq j\end{array}\}|=O(\sqrt{n\ln n})

with probability
1-O(n^{-6})
.

Proof of Claim A.

Let
u_{1}=|U_{1}|
,
u_{2}=|U_{2}|
, and
u=|U_{1}\cap U_{2}|
. Note that

\Delta(i,j)=|N_{G^{\prime}}(i)\cap(U_{1}\setminus U_{2})|+|N_{G^{\prime}}(j)\cap(U_{1}\setminus U_{2})|-|N_{G^{\prime}}(i)\cap(U_{2}\setminus U_{1})|-|N_{G^{\prime}}(j)\cap(U_{2}\setminus U_{1})|.

The four terms in the right hand side are independent random variables

\mathrm{Bin}(u_{1}-u,1/2)
,
\mathrm{Bin}(u_{1}-u,1/2)
,
\mathrm{Bin}(u_{2}-u,1/2)
,
\mathrm{Bin}(u_{2}-u,1/2)
respectively.
Since
N-\mathrm{Bin}(N,p)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,1-p)
, we conclude that

\Delta(i,j)\sim 2u-2u_{2}+\mathrm{Bin}(2u_{1}+2u_{2}-4u,1/2)
.
The Chernoff bound (see Lemma
4
) implies that, for each pair
i,j
,
the inequalities

2u-2u_{2}+(u_{1}+u_{2}-2u)\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{2\ln n}}{\sqrt{u_{1}+u_{2}-2u}}\right)\\
\leq\Delta(i,j)\leq 2u-2u_{2}+(u_{1}+u_{2}-2u)\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2\ln n}}{\sqrt{u_{1}+u_{2}-2u}}\right)

are violated with probability at most
O(n^{-8})
.
By the union bound, the probability that not all values
\Delta(i,j)
fall in an integer interval
of length at most

2\sqrt{2\ln n(u_{1}+u_{2}-2u)}=O(\sqrt{n\ln n})

is bounded by
O(n^{-6})
.

\,\triangleleft

It remains to prove (
8
) for a fixed standard pair
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Note that all walks of length 3 starting from
1
and
2
and having at least 2 vertices
inside
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\}
are determined by
U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Let
\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{k}(U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
denote the number of such walks
starting at
k
for
k=1,2
.
Let
e^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}
be the indicator random variable of the presence of the edge
\{i,j\}
in
G^{\prime\prime}
.
The equality
\sum_{i\in U_{1}}w^{\prime\prime}_{2}(i)=\sum_{i\in U_{2}}w^{\prime\prime}_{2}(i)
can be rewritten as

\gamma_{1}+\sum_{i,j\notin U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\}}e^{\prime\prime}_{ij}(w^{\prime}_{2}(i,1)+w^{\prime}_{2}(j,1))=\gamma_{2}+\sum_{i,j\notin U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\}}e^{\prime\prime}_{ij}(w^{\prime}_{2}(i,2)+w^{\prime}_{2}(j,2)),

(9)

where the sums count the walks of length 3 from
1
and
2
whose last two vertices are outside
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\{1,2\}
.
Since
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
is a standard pair, there exists an integer
x\neq 0
such that

\Delta(i,j)=x
for
\Omega(n^{3/2}/\sqrt{\ln n})
pairs
i,j
. Let
S_{x}
be the set of all such pairs.
Let
G^{*}=G^{*}(U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}^{*})
be obtained from
G^{\prime\prime}
by exposing all edges
except those between
i,j
in
S_{x}
, where
\mathcal{E}^{*}
is the set of exposed edges.
Equality (
9
) is fulfilled if and only if

\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{x}}e^{\prime\prime}_{ij}x=\gamma(U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}^{*})

(10)

for some integer
\gamma(U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}^{*})
which is completely determined by

U_{1},U_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}^{*}
. It remains to note that the binomial random variable

\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{x}}e^{\prime\prime}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(|S_{x}|,1/2)
takes on any fixed value
with probability at most
{|S_{x}|\choose\lfloor|S_{x}|/2\rfloor}/2^{|S_{x}|}=O(|S_{x}|^{-1/2})=O(n^{-3/4}\ln^{1/4}n)
,
where the first equality is due to (
1
).
This completes the proof of Equality (
8
) and of the whole theorem.

Remark 7
.

The probability bound in Theorem
1
cannot be significantly improved because

\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(1)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{3}(2)]=n^{-\Omega(1)}
. To see this, note first that

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{1}(1)=w^{G}_{1}(2)]=\Theta(n^{-1/2})
(see the proof of Lemma
5
).
Assuming that a standard pair
U_{1},U_{2}
with
|U_{1}|=|U_{2}|
is fixed, we can similarly show that

p(U_{1},U_{2})=\Theta(n^{-1})
, which implies that
\mathsf{P}[\left(w^{G}_{1}(1),w^{G}_{2}(1)\right)=\left(w^{G}_{1}(2),w^{G}_{2}(2)\right)]=\Theta(n^{-3/2})
.
Showing a polynomial lower bound for
\mathsf{P}[\left(w^{G}_{1}(1),w^{G}_{2}(1),w^{G}_{3}(1)\right)\allowbreak=\left(w^{G}_{1}(2),w^{G}_{2}(2),w^{G}_{3}(2)\right)]

is a slightly more delicate issue. Following the same proof strategy as for the upper bound,
we have to ensure that the equation (
10
) has at least one integer solution

\sum_{\{i,j\}\in S_{x}}e^{\prime\prime}_{ij}
. We can do this because we have
enough freedom in adjusting the right hand side of (
10
)
by choosing an appropriate value of
\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
. Indeed, first of all,
|x|
does not exceed
2n
with probability 1.
Second, we have an interval of length at least
100n
for the values of
\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
that are reachable
with probability
\Omega(n^{-1})
. As easily seen, this is enough for obtaining a desired lower bound.

2.3
Proof of Theorem
1
: Part 2

We have to show that with high probability there are vertices
i\neq j
such that
\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(i)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(j)
.
Let
X
be the number of all such vertex pairs. Thus, we have to prove that
X>0
with high probability
or, equivalently, that
\mathsf{P}[X=0]=o(1)
. By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathsf{P}[X=0]\leq\frac{\var(X)}{(\mathsf{E}X)^{2}}.

Therefore, it suffices to prove that
\var(X)=o\left((\mathsf{E}X)^{2}\right)
, where the variance
\var(X)
and
the mean
\mathsf{E}X
are seen as functions of
n
. We split the proof into two parts.
First, we determine the exact asymptotics of
\mathsf{E}X
and, second,
we establish an upper bound for
\var(X)
showing that it is sufficiently small
if compared to
(\mathsf{E}X)^{2}
.

Estimation of
\mathsf{E}X
.

We claim that

\mathsf{E}X=\frac{1+o(1)}{\pi}\,\sqrt{n}.

(11)

Let
X_{i,j}
denote the indicator random variable of the event that
\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(i)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(j)
.
Thus,
X=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}X_{i,j}
. Since all
X_{i,j}
are identically distributed,
we can fix a pair of distinct vertices
i
and
j
arbitrarily and conclude, by the linearity of the expectation, that

\mathsf{E}X={n\choose 2}\mathsf{E}X_{i,j}={n\choose 2}\mathsf{P}[X_{i,j}=1].

Equality (
11
) is, therefore, equivalent to the equality

\mathsf{P}[X_{i,j}=1]=\frac{2+o(1)}{\pi n^{3/2}},

(12)

which we will now prove.

Let
\xi_{i}
and
\xi_{j}
be the number of neighbors of the vertices
i
and
j
in
[n]\setminus\{i,j\}
respectively.
Note that
\xi_{i}
and
\xi_{j}
are independent random variables with distribution
\mathrm{Bin}(n-2,1/2)
.
By Lemma
5
, we have

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]=\mathsf{P}[\xi_{i}=\xi_{j}]=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.

(13)

It follows that

\mathsf{P}[X_{i,j}=1]=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\,\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)],

(14)

and our task reduces to estimating the conditional probability in the right hand side.

Let
N_{G}[v]=N_{G}(v)\cup\{v\}
denote the closed neighborhood of a vertex
v
in
G
.
For two disjoint sets
U,V\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i,j\}
, define the event

\mathcal{B}_{U,V}=\mathcal{B}_{U,V}(i,j)
by the conditions

N_{G}(i)\setminus N_{G}[j]=U\text{ and }N_{G}(j)\setminus N_{G}[i]=V.

(15)

Note that

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]\\
=\sum_{|U|=|V|}\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid\mathcal{B}_{U,V}]\mathsf{P}[\mathcal{B}_{U,V}\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)],

(16)

where the summation goes over all
U,V
of equal size.
Set
\varepsilon(n)=n^{-1/3}
.
Note that
|N_{G}(i)\setminus N_{G}[j]|\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-2,1/4)
and, by symmetry,
|N_{G}(j)\setminus N_{G}[i]|

has the same distribution.
By the Chernoff bound (Lemma
4
), both numbers belong to the interval

[(1/4-\varepsilon(n))n,(1/4+\varepsilon(n))n]
with probability

1-e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})}
. Call a pair
U,V

standard
if
|U|=|V|

is in the same range. Thus, all non-standard pairs make a negligible contribution in the sum in (
16
).
More precisely, the restriction of this sum to the non-standard pairs
is bounded by

\sum_{U,V\text{ non-standard}}\mathsf{P}[\mathcal{B}_{U,V}\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]\\
\leq\frac{\mathsf{P}[N_{G}(i)\setminus N_{G}[j],\,N_{G}(j)\setminus N_{G}[i]\text{ is a non-standard pair}]}{\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]}\\
=O(\sqrt{n})e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})}=e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})},

(17)

where for the last but one equality we use Equality (
13
).
To complete the estimation of
\mathsf{E}X
, it suffices to prove the following fact.

Claim B.

For each standard pair
U,V
,

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid\mathcal{B}_{U,V}]=\frac{2+o(1)}{n\sqrt{\pi}},

where the infinitesimal
o(1)
is the same for all standard pairs.

Indeed, from Equality (
16
) we obtain

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]\\
=\sum_{U,V\text{ standard}}\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid\mathcal{B}_{U,V}]\mathsf{P}[\mathcal{B}_{U,V}\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]+e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})}\\
=\frac{2+o(1)}{n\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{U,V\text{ standard}}\mathsf{P}[\mathcal{B}_{U,V}\mid w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)]+e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})}\\
=\frac{2+o(1)}{n\sqrt{\pi}}(1-e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})})+e^{-\Omega(\sqrt[3]{n})}=\frac{2+o(1)}{n\sqrt{\pi}}.

We here used Estimate (
17
) for the first equality, Claim
2.3
for the second equality,
and once again Estimate (
17
) for the third equality.
Plugging it into Equality (
14
), we get (
12
) and hence also (
11
).
It remains to prove Claim
2.3
.

Proof of Claim B.

Let

•

\xi_{U,V}
be the number of edges between
U
and
[n]\setminus(U\cup V\cup\{i,j\})
, and

•

\xi_{U}
be the number of edges induced by
U
.

Define
\xi_{V,U}
and
\xi_{V}
in the same way. Set

N=|U|(n-2|U|-2)\text{ and }M={|U|\choose 2}.

Since
U,V
is a standard pair,
N=(1/8+o(1))n^{2}
and
M=(1/32+o(1))n^{2}
.
Here and below, the infinitesimals do not depend on a particular standard pair
U,V
.
Note that
\xi_{U,V},\,\xi_{V,U}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N,1/2)
and
\xi_{U},\xi_{V}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(M,1/2)
,
and that the four random variables are independent. The relevance of these random variables lies in
the equality

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid\mathcal{B}_{U,V}]=\mathsf{P}[\xi_{U,V}+2\xi_{U}=\xi_{V,U}+2\xi_{V}].

(18)

To see this equality, assume that
\mathcal{B}_{U,V}
occurs.
Notice that the number of 2-walks from
i
and
j
via one of their common neighbors
is the same for these two vertices, and the same holds true for the number of 2-walks containing
an edge between
U
and
V
. The number of remaining 2-walks from
i
is clearly equal to
\xi_{U,V}+2\xi_{U}
,
and this number is equal to
\xi_{V,U}+2\xi_{V}
for
j
.

Rewriting the equality
\xi_{U,V}+2\xi_{U}=\xi_{V,U}+2\xi_{V}
as
\xi_{U,V}-\xi_{V,U}=2(\xi_{V}-\xi_{U})
,
we can rephrase it as follows: There is an integer
x
in the range from
-M
to
M
such that

\xi_{V}+(M-\xi_{U})=M+x
and
\xi_{U,V}+(N-\xi_{V,U})=N+2x
. Thus,

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid\mathcal{B}_{U,V}]=\sum_{x=-M}^{M}\mathsf{P}[\xi_{U,V}+(N-\xi_{V,U})=N+2x]\mathsf{P}[\xi_{V}+(M-\xi_{U})=M+x].

(19)

The advantage of considering these two
random variables is that
\xi_{V}+(M-\xi_{U})\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2M,1/2)
and
\xi_{U,V}+(N-\xi_{V,U})\sim\mathrm{Bin}(2N,1/2)

and that they are independent. By Chernoff’s bound,

\mathsf{P}[\xi_{V}+(M-\xi_{U})\notin(M-M^{5/9},M+M^{5/9})]=e^{-\Omega(M^{1/9})}=e^{-\Omega(n^{2/9})}.

This shows that the restriction of the sum in Equality (
19
)
to
x\notin(-M^{5/9},M^{5/9})
is bounded by
e^{-\Omega(n^{2/9})}
. For
x\in(-M^{5/9},M^{5/9})
,
the local de Moivre–Laplace theorem (Lemma
6
) yields

\mathsf{P}[\xi_{U,V}+(N-\xi_{V,U})=N+2x]=(1+o(1))\frac{e^{-4x^{2}/N}}{\sqrt{\pi N}}

and

\mathsf{P}[\xi_{V}+(M-\xi_{U})=M+x]=(1+o(1))\frac{e^{-x^{2}/M}}{\sqrt{\pi M}}

with the infinitesimal approaching 0 uniformly over
x
in the specified range.
It follows that

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)\mid\mathcal{B}_{U,V}]=\sum_{-M^{5/9}<x<M^{5/9}}(1+o(1))\frac{e^{-4x^{2}/N-x^{2}/M}}{\pi\sqrt{NM}}+e^{-\Omega(n^{2/9})}\\
=\sum_{-M^{5/9}<x<M^{5/9}}\frac{16+o(1)}{\pi n^{2}}e^{-64x^{2}/n^{2}}+e^{-\Omega(n^{2/9})}\\
=\frac{2+o(1)}{\sqrt{\pi}n}\sum_{-M^{5/9}<x<M^{5/9}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{128x^{2}}{2n^{2}}\right)\frac{8\sqrt{2}}{n}+e^{-\Omega(n^{2/9})}\\
=\frac{2+o(1)}{\sqrt{\pi}n}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx-o(1)\right)+e^{-\Omega(n^{2/9})}=\frac{2+o(1)}{\sqrt{\pi}n},

(20)

completing the proof of the claim.

\,\triangleleft

Estimation of
\var(X)
.

In view of Equality (
11
), the proof of Part 2 of the theorem will be completed
if we show that

\var(X)=o(n).

(21)

Since
X=\sum_{i,j}X_{i,j}
, we have

\var(X)=\sum_{i,j;\,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\cov(X_{i,j},X_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}),

where the summation is over all possible pairs of 2-element sets
\{i,j\}
and
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
.
Note that there are
{n\choose 2}<n^{2}
pairs with
\{i,j\}=\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}

and
n(n-1)(n-2)<n^{3}
pairs with
|\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}|=1
.
The number of the remaining pairs with
\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}=\emptyset
is bounded by
n^{4}
.
Therefore, Equality (
21
) follows from the three estimates below.

Claim C.

\cov(X_{i,j},X_{i,j})=O(n^{-3/2})
.

Claim D.

If
i^{\prime}=i
and
j^{\prime}\neq j
, then
\cov(X_{i,j},X_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})=O(n^{-3})
.

Claim E.

If
\{i,j\}\cap\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}=\emptyset
, then
\cov(X_{i,j},X_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})=o(n^{-3})
.

The rest of the proof consists of the proofs of these three claims.

Proof of Claim
2.3
.

We have

\cov(X_{i,j},X_{i,j})=\mathsf{E}X_{i,j}-(\mathsf{E}X_{i,j})^{2}=\mathsf{E}X_{i,j}(1-\mathsf{E}X_{i,j})=O(n^{-3/2}),

where the last equality is due to (
12
).

\,\triangleleft

Proof of Claim
2.3
.

It is enough to prove that

\mathsf{E}(X_{i,j}X_{i,j^{\prime}})=O(n^{-3})

(22)

because, along with Equality (
12
), this will imply that

\cov(X_{i,j},X_{i,j^{\prime}})=\mathsf{E}(X_{i,j}X_{i,j^{\prime}})-\mathsf{E}X_{i,j}\mathsf{E}X_{i,j^{\prime}}=O(n^{-3})

Note that
\mathsf{E}(X_{i,j}X_{i,j^{\prime}})=\mathsf{P}[\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(i)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(j)=\mathbf{w}^{G}_{2}(j^{\prime})]
.
We first estimate the probability that
w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)=w^{G}_{1}(j^{\prime})
.
Fix pairwise distinct
i,j,j^{\prime}
and expose the edges between these three vertices as well as
all edges from
i
. This determines
w^{G}_{1}(i)
and, hence, also two integers
x
and
x^{\prime}
such that

w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)=w^{G}_{1}(j^{\prime})
if and only if
\xi=x
and
\xi^{\prime}=x^{\prime}
for
\xi=|N_{G}(j)\setminus\{i,j^{\prime}\}|

and
\xi^{\prime}=|N_{G}(j^{\prime})\setminus\{i,j\}|
. Since
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
are independent random variables,
both with distribution
\mathrm{Bin}(n-3,1/2)
, we conclude that

\mathsf{P}[w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)=w^{G}_{1}(j^{\prime})]\leq\left(\max_{z}\mathsf{P}[\mathrm{Bin}(n-3,1/2)=z]\right)^{2}\\
=\left(\frac{1}{2^{n-3}}{n-3\choose\lfloor(n-3)/2\rfloor}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2+o(1)}{\pi n},

(23)

where the last inequality is provided by Bound (
1
) if
n
is even
and easily follows from (
1
) if
n
is odd.

Next, we estimate the probability of
w^{G}_{2}(i)=w^{G}_{2}(j)=w^{G}_{2}(j^{\prime})
under the condition

w^{G}_{1}(i)=w^{G}_{1}(j)=w^{G}_{1}(j^{\prime})
. Let us split this condition into a set of pairwise disjoint
events. For two disjoint sets
A,A^{\prime}\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i,j,j^{\prime}\}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{A,A^{\prime}}

be the event that

•

i
,
j
, and
j^{\prime}
have the same degree,

•

N_{G}(j)\setminus(N_{G}[i]\cup N_{G}[j^{\prime}])=A
and, similarly,

•

N_{G}(j^{\prime})\setminus(N_{G}[i]\cup N_{G}[j])=A^{\prime}

We begin with a formal description of the

CR operates on vertex-colored graphs but applies also to uncolored graphs

An input to the algorithm consists either of a single graph or a pair of graphs.
Consider the former case first.
For an input graph

.
