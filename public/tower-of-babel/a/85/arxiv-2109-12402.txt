Phase mixing for solutions to 1D transport equation in a confining potential
Sanchit Chaturvedi, Jonathan Luk

Abstract
. Consider the linear transport equation in 1 1 D under an external confining potential Φ \Phi : ∂ t f + v ​ ∂ x f − ∂ x Φ ​ ∂ v f = 0 . \partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f-\partial_{x}\Phi\partial_{v}f=0. For Φ = x 2 2 + ϵ ​ x 4 2 \Phi=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{\epsilon x^{4}}{2} (with ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 small), we prove phase mixing and quantitative decay estimates for ∂ t φ := − Δ − 1 ∫ ℝ ∂ t f d v \partial_{t}\varphi:=-\Delta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{t}f\,\mathrm{d}v , with an inverse polynomial decay rate O ⁡ ( ⟨ t ⟩ − 2 ) O(\langle t\rangle^{-2}) . In the proof, we develop a commuting vector field approach, suitably adapted to this setting. We will explain why we hope this is relevant for the nonlinear stability of the zero solution for the Vlasov–Poisson system in 1 1 D under the external potential Φ \Phi .

1.
Introduction

Consider the linear transport equation in
1
D

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f-\partial_{x}\Phi\partial_{v}f=0,

for an unknown function
f:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{v}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

with a smooth external confining potential
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

The following is the main result of this note:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\Phi(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{\epsilon x^{4}}{2}
. Consider the unique solution
f
to (
1.1
) with initial data
f\restriction_{t=0}=f_{0}
such that

•

f_{0}:\mathbb{R}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{v}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is smooth, and

•

there exists
c_{s}>0
such that
\mathrm{supp}(f_{0})\subseteq\{(x,v):c_{s}\leq\frac{v^{2}}{2}+\Phi(x)\leq c_{s}^{-1}\}
.

Then, for
\epsilon
sufficiently small, there exists
C>0
depending on
\epsilon
and
c_{s}
such that the following estimate holds:

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{t}\varphi|(t,x)\leq C\langle t\rangle^{-2}\sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq 2}|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}|(x,v),

where
\varphi
is defined by

(1.2)

\partial_{xx}^{2}\varphi(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v,\quad\varphi(t,0)=\partial_{x}\varphi(t,0)=0.

A few remarks of the theorem are in order.

Remark 1.2

(Nonlinear Vlasov–Poisson system)
.

The reason that we are particularly concerned with
\partial_{t}\varphi
is that it appears to be the quantity relevant for the stability of the
zero solution for the nonlinear Vlasov–Poisson system in
1
D; see Section
2
.

It should be noted that
\varphi
itself is not expect to decay to
0
(since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\,\mathrm{d}v\geq 0
). Thus the decay for
\partial_{t}\varphi
can be viewed as a measure of the rate that
\varphi
approaches the limit
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\varphi(t,x)
.

Remark 1.3

(Derivatives of
\partial_{t}\varphi
)
.

For the applications on the Vlasov–Poisson system, one may also wish to obtain estimates for the derivatives of
\partial_{t}\varphi
. It is easy to extend our methods to obtain

|\partial_{x}\partial_{t}\varphi|\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-1},\quad|\partial_{x}^{2}\partial_{t}\varphi|\lesssim 1.

Notice that these decay rates, at least by themselves, do not seem sufficient for a global nonlinear result.

Remark 1.4

(Phase mixing and the choice of
\Phi
)
.

The result in Theorem
1.1
can be interpreted as a quantitative phase-mixing statement. It is well-known that for

\Phi(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2},

the solution to (
1.1
) does
not
undergo phase mixing (see chapter 3 in
[
6
]
). It is therefore important that we added the
\frac{\epsilon x^{4}}{2}
term in the definition of the potential.

On the other hand, there are other choices of
\Phi
for which analogues of Theorem
1.1
hold. We expect that as long as
\Phi
is even and satisfies the non-degeneracy condition of
[
23
]
, then a similar decay estimate holds. The particular example we used is only chosen for concreteness.

Remark 1.5

(Method of proof)
.

It is well-known that the linear transport equation (
1.1
) can be written in action-angle variables, say
(Q,K)
, in which case (
1.1
) takes the form

(1.3)

\partial_{t}f-c({K})\partial_{{Q}}f=0.

When
c^{\prime}(K)
is bounded away from
0
, phase mixing in the sense that
f
converges
weakly
to a limit can be obtained after solving (
1.3
) with a Fourier series in
Q
; see
[
23
]
. The point here is that
\varphi
is a (weighted) integral of
f
over a region of phase space that is most conveniently defined with respect to the
(x,v)
(as opposed to the action-angle) variables.

We quantify the strong convergence of
\varphi_{t}\to 0
by finding an appropriate commuting vector field
Y
that is adapted to the action-angle variables. The fact that
\varphi
is naturally defined as an integral over
v
in
(x,v)
coordinates makes it tricky to prove decay using this vector field. Furthermore, we are only able to prove
1/\left\langle{t}\right\rangle^{2}
decay; this is for instance in contrast to the decay of the density for the free transport equation on a torus.

2.
The Vlasov–Poisson system

The motivation of our result is the Vlasov–Poisson system:

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f-(\partial_{x}\Phi+\partial_{x}\varphi)\partial_{v}f=0,\\
-\partial^{2}_{x}\varphi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\operatorname{d}\!{v}.\end{cases}

Note that (
2.1
) can be rewritten as

(2.2)

\partial_{t}f+\{H,f\}=0,

where
H
is the Hamiltonian given by

(2.3)

H(x,v)=\frac{v^{2}}{2}+\Phi(x)+\varphi(t,x).

Notice that
f\equiv 0
is a solution to (
2.1
), and the transport equation (
1.1
) is
the linearization of (
2.1
) near the zero solution.

One cannot hope that the term
\partial_{x}\varphi
in the nonlinear term decays as
t\to+\infty
.
(This can be seen by noting that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\operatorname{d}\!{v}\geq 0
pointwise.) At best one can hope
that
\partial_{x}\varphi
converges to some (non-trivial) limiting profile as
t\to+\infty
. For
f
satisfying
the linear equation (
1.1
), such convergence (without a quantitative rate) has been shown
in
[
23
]
.

In anticipation of the nonlinear problem, it is important to understand the quantitative convergence. Since

\partial_{x}\varphi
does not converge to
0
, it is natural to understand the decay rate of
\partial_{t}\partial_{x}\varphi
.

As a first step to understand (
2.1
), we look at the linearized problem (
1.1
) around the zero solution and prove that we get integrable decay for
\varphi_{t}
in the linearized dynamics.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that the Poisson’s equation above reads

-\partial^{2}_{x}\varphi=\rho.

In particular,
\varphi
is only defined up to a harmonic function, i.e. a linear function a
x
.
In Theorem
1.1
, we remove this ambiguity by setting
\varphi(0)=(\partial_{x}\varphi)(0)=0
.
Notice that other normalization, e.g.,
\varphi(-\infty)=(\partial_{x}\varphi)(-\infty)=0
would not change the function
\varphi_{t}=\partial_{t}\varphi
.

3.
The action-angle variables

3.1.
First change of variables

From now on we will consider the Hamiltonian

H=\frac{v^{2}}{2}+\Phi(x).

This is the Hamiltonian for the equations (
1.1
), which is also (
2.3
) without the
\varphi
(the self-interaction term). As an intermediate step to getting the action-angle variables we use the change of coordinates

\displaystyle(t,x,v)\mapsto(t,\chi,H)

when
x>0

\displaystyle(t,x,v)\mapsto(t,\pi-\chi,H)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt\text{when $x\leq 0$},

where
\chi:=\arcsin\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{2H}}\right).

First we check if the change of variables is well defined by calculating the Jacobian for
x>0
,

J=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}t&\partial_{x}t&\partial_{v}t\\
\partial_{t}H&\partial_{x}H&\partial_{v}H\\
\partial_{t}\chi&\partial_{x}\chi&\partial_{v}\chi\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\Phi_{x}&v\\
0&-\frac{v}{2H}\cdot\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}&\frac{\sqrt{\Phi}}{H}\end{pmatrix}

Now

\displaystyle\det(J)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}\frac{(\Phi+v^{2}/2)}{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}.

Similarly for
x\leq 0
,

\displaystyle\det(J)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\det(J)

\displaystyle=\sign{x}\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}.

Next by chain rule and using that
H
is independent of
t
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{x}

\displaystyle=\sign{x}\partial_{x}\chi\partial_{\chi}+\partial_{x}H\partial_{H}

\displaystyle=-\frac{v}{2H}\cdot\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}\partial_{\chi}+\Phi_{x}\partial_{H},

\displaystyle\partial_{v}

\displaystyle=\sign{x}\partial_{v}\chi\partial_{\chi}+\partial_{v}H\partial_{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\Phi}}{H}\partial_{\chi}+v\partial_{H}.

Pluggin this in (
2.2
), we get the equation

(3.1)

\partial_{t}f-\sign{x}\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}\partial_{\chi}f=0.

3.2.
Second change of variables

The coefficient in front of
\partial_{\chi}f
in (
3.1
) depends on both
\chi
and
H
. To take care of this, we reparametrize
\chi
(in a manner depending on
H
). More precisely, for a fixed
H
, we define
Q(\chi,H)
such that

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!{Q}}{\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}}=\frac{c(H)}{a(\chi,H)},\quad Q(0,H)=0,

where
a(\chi,H)=\sign{x}\frac{\Phi_{x}(x)}{\sqrt{\Phi(x)}}
such that
x=x(\chi,H).
To fix
c(H)
, we require that for every
H
,

(3.2)

2\pi=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}=c(H)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}.

Now we define the change of variables,
(\chi,H)\mapsto(Q,K)
where
K=H
. Then note,

a(\chi,H)\partial_{\chi}=c(H)\partial_{Q}

and

\partial_{H}=\partial_{K}+\frac{\partial Q}{\partial H}\partial_{Q}.

Thus in these coordinates, we can rewrite (
3.1
) as

(3.3)

\partial_{t}f-c({K})\partial_{Q}f=0.

Further, the Jacobian is

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{H}K&\partial_{\chi}K\\
\partial_{H}Q&\partial_{\chi}Q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\partial_{H}Q&\frac{c(H)}{a(\chi,H)}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that the determinant is
\frac{c(H)}{a(\chi,H)}
. Further, since

\displaystyle a(\chi,H)

\displaystyle=\sign{x}\frac{\Phi_{x}(x)}{\sqrt{\Phi(x)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\frac{1+2\varepsilon x^{2}}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon x^{2}}},

we have that
a(\chi,H)\approx 1
when
x
is in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
. As a result the determinant is bounded away from zero. For more details see Lemma
4.3
.

4.
The commuting vector field

We first define the vector field

Y=tc^{\prime}(H)\partial_{Q}-\partial_{K}.

In this section we prove that this vector field commutes with the transport operator as in (
3.3
) and that
|c^{\prime}(H)|>0.

4.1.
Commutation property

The following commutation formula is an easy computation and thus we leave out the details.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Y=tc^{\prime}(H)\partial_{Q}-\partial_{K}
. Then

[\partial_{t}-c(H)\partial_{Q},Y]=0.

The following is an easy consequence of Lemma
4.1
:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f
be a solution to (
3.3
) with initial data satisfying assumptions of Theorem
1.1
. Then

\sup_{(t,Q,K)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{1}\times[c_{s},c_{s}^{-1}]}\hskip 1.00006pt\sum_{\ell\leq 2}|Y^{\ell}f|(t,Q,K)\lesssim\sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\hskip 1.00006pt\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq 2}|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}|(x,v).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
, we have that
Y^{\ell}f
satisfies the transport equation (
3.3
) for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cap\{0\}
. Hence we get the estimate

\sup_{(t,Q,K)\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{1}\times[c_{s},c_{s}^{-1}]}\hskip 1.00006pt\sum_{\ell\leq 2}|Y^{\ell}f|(t,Q,K)\lesssim\sup_{(Q,K)\in\mathbb{T}^{1}\times[c_{s},c_{s}^{-1}]}\hskip 1.00006pt\sum_{\ell\leq 2}|\partial_{K}^{\ell}f_{0}|(Q,K).

Since the change of variables
(Q,K)\to(x,v)
is well-defined and bounded away from zero, we get the required result.
∎

4.2.
Positivity of
|c^{\prime}(K)|

We prove that
|c^{\prime}(K)|
is uniformly bounded below on the support of
f
. This plays a key role in the next section ensuring phase mixing.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every
c_{s}<+\infty
, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that whenever
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
, there is a small constant
\delta>0
(depending on
c_{s}
and
\epsilon
) such that

\inf_{K\in[c_{s},c_{s}^{-1}]}|c^{\prime}(K)|=\inf_{H\in[c_{s},c_{s}^{-1}]}|c^{\prime}(H)|\geq\delta.

Proof.

By definition of
c(H)
, we have

\frac{2\pi}{c(H)}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left|\frac{\sqrt{\Phi}}{\Phi^{\prime}}\right|\operatorname{d}\!{\chi},

so that using
\Phi=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}x^{4}
, we obtain

\frac{2\pi}{c(H)}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}(1+2\epsilon x^{2})}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}.

Notice that for
H\in[c_{s},c_{s}^{-1}]
,
|x|
is bounded. It follows that
c(H)\approx 1
. Therefore, to prove strict positivity of
|c^{\prime}(H)|
, it suffices to prove positivity of
\left|\frac{c^{\prime}(H)}{c^{2}(H)}\right|.
Note that

(4.1)

\begin{split}\frac{-2\pi c^{\prime}(H)}{c^{2}(H)}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{H}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+\epsilon x^{2}}}{1+2\epsilon x^{2}}\right)\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{H}x\left[\frac{\epsilon x}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon x^{2}}(1+2\epsilon x^{2})}-\frac{4\epsilon x\sqrt{1+\epsilon x^{2}}}{(1+2\epsilon x^{2})^{2}}\right]\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{H}x\left[\frac{-3\epsilon x-2\epsilon^{2}x^{3}}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon x^{2}}(1+2\epsilon x^{2})^{2}}\right]\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}.\end{split}

Now we calculate
\partial_{H}x.
First we use the equation,
H=\frac{v^{2}}{2}+\Phi(x).
Precisely, we have

1=v\partial_{H}v+\Phi^{\prime}(x)\partial_{H}x.

Thus

(4.2)

\partial_{H}x=\frac{1-v\partial_{H}v}{\Phi^{\prime}}.

Next we use that
\frac{v}{\sqrt{2H}}=\sin\chi,

0=\frac{\partial_{H}v}{\sqrt{2H}}-\frac{v}{(2H)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

Thus
\partial_{H}v=\frac{v}{2H}.
Plugging this into (
4.2
), we get that

(4.3)

\partial_{H}x=\frac{1-\frac{v^{2}}{2H}}{\Phi^{\prime}}=\frac{\cos^{2}\chi}{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}=\frac{\cos^{2}\chi}{x+2\epsilon x^{3}},

where in the last equality we used
\Phi=\frac{x^{2}+\epsilon x^{4}}{2}
.

Plugging (
4.3
) back into (
4.1
), we get that

\displaystyle\frac{-2\pi c^{\prime}(H)}{c^{2}(H)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos^{2}\chi}{\sqrt{2}x(1+2\epsilon x^{2})}\left[\frac{-x(3\epsilon+2\epsilon^{2}x^{2})}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon x^{2}}(1+2\epsilon x^{2})^{2}}\right]\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\cos^{2}\chi}\left[\frac{(3\epsilon+2\epsilon^{2}x^{2})}{\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon x^{2})}(1+2\epsilon x^{2})^{3}}\right]\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}.

Finally note that since
|x|
is bounded on the region of interest, after choosing
\epsilon_{0}
sufficiently small, we have

\frac{(3\epsilon+2\epsilon^{2}x^{2})}{\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon x^{2})}(1+2\epsilon x^{2})^{3}}\approx\epsilon,

and thus
|c^{\prime}(H)|>\delta
. ∎

5.
Decay for
\varphi_{t}

In this section we finally prove the decay for
\varphi_{t}
(recall Theorem
1.1
).

To keep the notation lean, we will often suppress the explicit dependence on
t
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
f
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, and
\varphi
defined as in (
1.2
), we have the following formula

\varphi_{t}(x^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{x^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v[f(y,v)-f(0,v)]\operatorname{d}\!{v}\operatorname{d}\!{y}.

Proof.

By the continuity equation (following directly from (
1.1
)), we have that

\rho_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\partial_{x}f\operatorname{d}\!{v}.

Thus

-\partial^{2}_{x}\varphi_{t}=\rho_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\partial_{x}f\operatorname{d}\!{v}.

Solving the Laplace’s equation (with boundary conditions (
1.2
)), we get

\varphi_{t}(x^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{x^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{y}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\partial_{x}f(z,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}\operatorname{d}\!{z}\operatorname{d}\!{y}.

Integrating by parts in
z
, we get

\varphi_{t}(x^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{x^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v[f(y,v)-f(0,v)]\operatorname{d}\!{v}\operatorname{d}\!{y}.

∎

In view of Lemma
5.1
, it suffices to bound
\int_{0}^{x^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(0,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}\operatorname{d}\!{y}
and
\int_{0}^{x^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(y,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}\operatorname{d}\!{y}
, which will be achieved in the next two subsections respectively.

5.1.
Decay for the term involving
f(0,v)

We first prove decay for
\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(0,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}.
Before proving the main estimate in Proposition
5.3
, we first prove a lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

The level set
\{x=0\}
corresponds to the level sets
\{Q=\frac{\pi}{2}\}\cap\{Q=-\frac{\pi}{2}\}\cup\{(x,v)=(0,0)\}
.

Proof.

First note that level set
\{x=0\}
corresponds to the level sets
\{\chi=\frac{\pi}{2}\}\cap\{\chi=-\frac{\pi}{2}\}\cup\{(x,v)=(0,0)\}
. This is because when
x=0
,
\Phi(x)=0
, and thus by definition (when
v\neq 0
)
\chi:=\arcsin\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{2H}}\right)=\arcsin(\pm 1)=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}
.

It thus remains to show that

(5.1)

\chi=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\iff Q=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}.

Fix
H
, then since
a(\chi,H)=\lvert\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}\rvert
is independent of
v
, we have

c(H)\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}=c(H)\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}.

Further, by the evenness of
\Phi
, we have

c(H)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}=c(H)\int_{\pi/2}^{\pi}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}.

Finally, since we have by construction,

c(H)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}=2\pi,

we have that

Q(\chi=\pi/2,H)=c(H)\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{a(\chi,H)}\operatorname{d}\!{\chi}=\pi/2.

Similarly,
Q(\chi=-\pi/2,H)=-\pi/2.
Combining these, we obtain (
5.1
). ∎

Proposition 5.3
.

For
f
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, we have the following estimate:

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(0,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}\Big|\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-2}\sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\hskip 1.00006pt\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq 2}|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}|(x,v).

Proof.

The transport equation preserves
L^{\infty}
bounds so that by the support properties, we obviously have

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(0,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}\Big|\lesssim\sup_{(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\hskip 1.00006pt|f_{0}|(x,v).

In other words, it suffices to prove the desired bound with
t^{-2}
instead of
\langle t\rangle^{-2}
.

Now note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(0,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}=\int_{0}^{\infty}v[f(0,v)-f(0,-v)]\operatorname{d}\!{v}.

For clarity of notation, we let

\overline{f}(t,Q,K)=f(t,x,v).

Now writing in the
(K,Q)
variables, and using Lemma
5.2
together with the fact that
K=H=\frac{v^{2}}{2}
when
x=0
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}vf(0,v)\operatorname{d}\!{v}=\int_{0}^{\infty}v[f(0,v)-f(0,-v)]\operatorname{d}\!{v}=\int_{0}^{\infty}[\overline{f}(\pi/2,K)-\overline{f}(-\pi/2,K)]\operatorname{d}\!{K}.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}[\overline{f}(\pi/2,K)-\overline{f}(-\pi/2,K)]\operatorname{d}\!{K}=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}.

Next, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now using Poincare’s inequality we get that for any
\ell\geq 2

(5.2)

\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial^{\ell}_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now take

. We write

\begin{split}&\>\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial^{2}_{Q}\overline{f}(Q,K)\operatorname{d}\!{K}\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{Q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\

where in the last step we have integrated by parts in

\frac{|c^{\prime\prime}(K)|}{|c^{\prime}(K)|}

Finally, since

Lemma 5.5
.

Under the change of variables
(x,v)\mapsto(Q,K)
as in Section
3
, the volume form transforms as follows:

\operatorname{d}\!{v}\operatorname{d}\!{x}=c(K)\,\operatorname{d}\!{Q}\operatorname{d}\!{K}.

Proof.

The Jacobian determinant for the change of variables
(x,v)\mapsto(\chi,H)
is
a(\chi,H)=\lvert\frac{\Phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\Phi}}\rvert
. Further the Jacobian determinant for the change of variables
(\chi,H)\to(Q,K)
is
\frac{c(H)}{a(\chi,H)}
and hence the Jacobian determinant for
(x,v)\to(Q,K)
is
c(H)=c(K)
. ∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\overline{f}(Q,K)=f(x,v)
as above. There exists a function
\overline{g}(Q,K)
such that

(5.3)

\partial_{Q}^{2}\overline{g}=\partial_{Q}\overline{f}

(5.4)

\max_{\ell\leq 2}\sup_{K}\lVert Y^{\ell}\overline{g}\rVert_{L^{2}_{Q}}\lesssim\max_{\ell\leq 2}\sup_{Q,K}|Y^{\ell}\overline{f}|.

Proof.

We use the Fourier series of
f
in
Q
to get that

\overline{f}(Q,K)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{k=\infty}\widehat{\overline{f}}_{k}(K)e^{ikQ}.

Now we define

\overline{g}(Q,K):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{1}{ik}\widehat{\overline{f}}_{k}(K)e^{ikQ}.

Then we see that
\partial^{2}_{Q}\overline{g}=\partial_{Q}\overline{f}.

Using Plancheral’s theorem we can easily see that

\max_{\ell\leq 2}\sup_{K}\lVert Y^{\ell}\overline{g}\rVert_{L^{2}_{Q}}\lesssim\max_{\ell\leq 2}\sup_{K}\lVert Y^{\ell}\overline{f}\rVert_{L^{2}_{Q}}.

Finally, the result follows by taking supremum in
Q
. ∎
