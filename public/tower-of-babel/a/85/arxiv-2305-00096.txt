Pointless parts of completely regular frames
Richard N. Ball

Abstract
. (Completely regular) locales generalize (Tychonoff) spaces; indeed, the passage from a locale to its spatial sublocale is a well understood coreflection. But a locale also possesses an equally important pointless sublocale, and with morphisms suitably restricted, the passage from a locale to its pointless sublocale is also a coreflection. Our main theorem is that every locale can be uniquely represented as a subdirect product of its pointless and spatial parts, again with suitably restricted projections. We then exploit this representation by showing that any locale is determined by (what may be described as) the placement of its points in its pointless part.

1.
Introduction

The primary motivation for point free topology comes from classical point set topology. When expressed in terms of the underlying frames, the connection between these two worlds is the well known functor
\sigma\colon\mathbf{F}\to\mathbf{sF}
, which assigns to each (completely regular) frame
L
its spatial part
\sigma L
. (Here
\mathbf{F}
is the category of completely regular frames with frame homomorphisms, and
\mathbf{sF}
is its full subcategory of spatial frames, i.e., frames in which every element is the meet of the maximal elements above it.) The functor
\sigma
is an epireflection, and the reflector for a given frame
L
is the surjection

\sigma_{L}\colon L\to\sigma L\equiv\left\{\,b\in L:b=\bigwedge\left\uparrow{b}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\,\right\}=\left(b\mapsto\bigwedge\left\uparrow{b}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\right),\qquad b\in L.

One of the principal advantages of the point free approach to general topology is its increase in extent beyond the spatial situation, and this article provides evidence of the benefits of that generality. We call a frame
pointless
if it has no maximal elements, and we show that every frame
L
has a pointless part
\pi L
which plays a role roughly complementary to its spatial part. We need to restrict the homomorphisms slightly to those we term
skinny
, and work with the restricted cateogory
\mathbf{Fs}
of frames with skinny morphisms and its full subcategory of pointless frames
\mathbf{plFs}
. (When the domain and codomain are spatial, the skinny frame homomorphisms are those whose pointed continuous functions have scattered fibers.) In that context the functor
\pi\colon\mathbf{Fs}\to\mathbf{plFs}
is an epireflection, and a reflector for the frame
L
is the surjection

\pi_{L}\colon L\to\pi L\equiv\left\{\,a\in L:\forall b>a\,\exists c\ (b>c>a)\,\right\}=\left(b\mapsto\bigwedge\left\uparrow{b}\right\uparrow_{\pi L}\right),\ b\in L.

Though disjoint, the two sublocales
\sigma L
and
\pi L
are not complementary. Nevertheless, their reflectors
\sigma_{L}
and
\pi_{L}
are diagnostic when taken together, in the sense that every frame
L
has a unique representation as a subdirect product of its pointless and spatial parts, with suitably restricted projection maps. This is our main Theorem
5.2.2
. The targets of this representation, here termed
fat
, form a monoreflective subcategory of
\mathbf{Fs}
, and even though we would like to know more about these objects (cf. Question
5.4.1
), Theorem
6.3.3
provides a fairly concrete description of the reflector arrow.

Thus a frame
L
is determined by the interaction of its spatial and pointless parts, and this interaction is governed by two principal connections. The first of these is the arrow
\lambda_{L}\colon\pi L\to\pi\sigma L
induced by applying the
\pi
functor to the
\sigma_{L}
arrow.

The second principal connection between the spatial and pointless parts of a frame is motivated by the crucial observation that each maximal element
a
of a frame
L
is associated with the filter
y_{a}\equiv\left\{\,b\in\pi L:b\nleq a\,\right\}
on
\pi L
. Such filters have two key features: they are
round,
i.e., for each
b\in y_{a}
there exists an element
c\in y_{a}
such that
c\prec\!\!\prec b
, and
\bigvee_{y_{a}}b^{*}=\top
. We call such filters
regular,
and we show that each regular filter on a pointless frame
E
arises as
y_{a}
for some frame
L
having pointless part
E
and maximal element
a
. Furthermore, the filters produced by distinct maximal elements are
independent,
i.e., contain disjoint elements of
\pi L
. We refer to the family
W\equiv\left\{\,y_{a}:a\in\max L\,\right\}
of such filters as the
spatial support of
L
.
Together with the pointless part of
L
,
W
determines the fat reflection of an atomless frame (Theorem
6.3.3
).
W
also determines whether or not
L
is spatial, i.e., whether
\sigma_{L}
is an isomorphism (Proposition
7.1.2
), and whether or not
L
is compact (Proposition
7.1.4
).

In Section
8
we take up the situation that arises when all of the filters of the spatial support
W
are maximal proper round filters. This assumption has the great advantage that it guarantees the complete regularity of the synthetic construction of a frame from its pointless part
E
and its spatial support
W
. However, it has the disadvantage that the pointless part can grow bigger than
E
, at least if
W
is taken to be the entire family of maximal proper round filters on
E
. In fact, in the latter case we show in Proposition
8.1.5
that the synthetically constructed frame is
\beta E
, the compact coreflection of
E
. That fact motivates a final digression, in which we establish the existence of what in spatial terms is a particularly exotic compactification in compact Tychonoff spaces without isolated points (Corollary
8.3.6
and Proposition
8.3.1
.)

2.
Preliminaries

For purposes of handy reference and to fix notation, we record here the few background results which underlie what follows. The material is almost entirely folklore and should be skipped upon a first reading, and then consulted only as necessary.

2.1.
Naked frames, completely regular frames, and witnessing families

The context for these remarks is the category
\mathbf{F}
of completely regular frames with frame homomorphisms. Unless otherwise explicitly stipulated, all frames are assumed completely regular and all spaces are assumed Tychonoff. A couple of constructions require an excursion into the category
\mathbf{nF}
of
naked frames,
i.e., frames without the hypothesis of complete regularity, and then a return to
\mathbf{F}
by way of the completely regular coreflection. That is, we first construct a naked frame, labeled for instance
L^{\prime}
, and then extract its largest completely regular subframe, labeled for instance
L
.

The issue of whether a particular naked frame is completely regular thus plays an important role in what follows, and our working definition of this important notion is as follows. When speaking of two elements
a_{i}
of a frame
L
, to say that
a_{1}
lies completely below
a_{2}
is to say that there is a family of witnesses
\left\{b_{p}\right\}_{\mathbb{Q}}\subseteq L
such that
a_{1}\leq b_{p}\prec b_{q}\leq a_{2}
for all
p<q
in
\mathbb{Q}
. We write
a_{1}\prec\!\!\prec a_{2}
, and we refer to
\left\{b_{p}\right\}_{\mathbb{Q}}
as a
witnessing family for
a_{1}\prec\!\!\prec a_{2}
.

2.2.
Nuclei and prenuclei

Notation

(nucleus notation)
.

We make use of the following notational conventions for nuclei.

•

We use lower case Greek letters to denote nuclei on frames, often subscripted with the name of the frame, as in
\delta_{L}
.

•

We use primed lower case Greek letters to denote prenuclei on frames, often subscripted with the name of the frame, as in
\delta_{L}^{\prime}
. The corresponding nucleus is then designated by the same Greek letter without the prime. See Lemma
2.2.1
.

•

We define and denote the
kernel of a nucleus
\delta
on a frame
L
to be

\ker\delta\equiv\left\{\,a\in L:\delta(a)=\top\,\right\}.

See Lemma
2.2.3
.

•

For a nucleus
\delta
on a frame
L
, we denote its fixed point set, aka its sublocale, by

Lemma 2.2.2
.

For a filter

, the map

\delta_{F}^{\prime}\colon L\to L\equiv\left(a\mapsto\bigvee_{F}(b\to a)\right),\qquad a\in L,

functions as a prenucleus on

.

Proof.

It is clear that

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}_{F}(a_{1}\wedge a_{2})=\bigvee_{F}(b\to(a_{1}\wedge a_{2}))=\bigvee_{F}((b\to a_{1})\wedge(b\to a_{2}))

\displaystyle\geq\bigvee_{F}(a_{1}\wedge(b\to a_{2}))=a_{1}\wedge\bigvee_{F}(b\to a_{2})=a_{1}\wedge\delta_{F}^{\prime}(a_{2}).\qed

Definition

(normal filter)
.

A filter
F
on a frame

is said to be

if
\delta_{F}(a)\in F
implies

for all

. Here
\delta_{F}
is the nucleus associated per Lemma

with the prenucleus

of Lemma

.

.

is a nucleus on a frame

is a normal filter, and if

, and since

and

. On the other hand, suppose that

2.3.
Congruences

Notation

(congruence notation)
.

We make use of the following notational conventions for frame congruences.

•

We use capital Greek letters to denote congruences on a frame
L
, and we denote the congruence frame itself, aka the assembly of
L
, by
\con L
.

•

(
\Phi_{a}
,
o_{a}
,
O_{a}
) We denote the congruence of the open quotient associated with an element
a
of a frame
L
by

\Phi_{a}\equiv\left\{\,(a_{1},a_{2}):a\wedge a_{1}=a\wedge a_{2}\,\right\},

and the quotient map by
o_{a}\colon L\to L/\Phi_{a}
. We denote the quotient frame
L/\Phi_{a}
by
O_{a}
, and often identify it with its sublocale
\{a\to b:b\in L\}
, in which case we also identify the map
o_{a}
with (the range restriction of) its nucleus
(b\mapsto a\to b)
,
b\in L
.

•

We denote the congruence of the closed quotient associated with an element
a
of a frame
L
by

\Psi_{a}\equiv\left\{\,(a_{1},a_{2}):a\vee a_{1}=a\vee a_{2}\,\right\},

and the quotient map by
c_{a}\colon L\to L/\Psi_{a}
. We denote the quotient frame
L/\Psi_{a}
by
C_{a}
, and often identify it with its sublocale
\left\uparrow{a}\right\uparrow\equiv\left\{\,b:b\geq a\,\right\}
, in which case we also identify the map
c_{a}
with (the range restriction of) its nucleus
(b\mapsto a\vee b)
,
b\in L
.

•

We denote the coarsest dense congruence on a frame
L
by

\Delta_{L}\equiv\left\{\,(a_{1},a_{2}):a_{1}^{*}=a_{2}^{*}\,\right\},

and the quotient map by
\delta_{L}\colon L\to L/\Delta_{L}\equiv\Delta L
. We often identify the quotient with its sublocale
L^{**}\equiv\left\{a^{**}\right\}_{L}
, in which case we also identify the map
\delta_{L}
with (the range restriction of) its nucleus
(a\mapsto a^{**})
,
a\in L
. We refer to the map or it codomain as the
skeleton of
L
.

•

We denote the congruence of a frame surjection
m\colon L\to M
by

\Theta_{m}\equiv\left\{\,(a_{1},a_{2}):m(a_{1})=m(a_{2})\,\right\}.

Lemma 2.3.1
.

For elements
a\leq b
and congruence
\Xi
on a frame
L
,

(a,b)\in\Xi\iff\Phi_{a}\wedge\Psi_{b}\leq\Xi.

In particular,

(a,\top)\in\Xi\iff\Phi_{a}\leq\Xi\quad\text{and}\quad(\bot,b)\in\Xi\iff\Psi_{a}\leq\Xi.

Lemma 2.3.2
.

For any frame surjection
m\colon L\to M
,

\Theta_{m}\geq\bigvee_{m^{-1}(\top)}\Phi_{a}\vee\bigvee_{m^{-1}(\bot)}\Psi_{a}.

Proof.

If
a\in m^{-1}(\top)
and
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\Phi_{a}
then

\displaystyle m(a_{1})

\displaystyle=m(a_{1})\wedge\top=m(a_{1})\wedge m(a)=m(a_{1}\wedge a)=m(a_{2}\wedge a)

\displaystyle=m(a_{2})\wedge m(a)=m(a_{2})\wedge\top=m(a_{2}).\qed

Lemma 2.3.3
.

Let
m\colon L\to M
be a frame surjection.

(1)

The map

\displaystyle m^{-1}\colon\con M\to\left\uparrow{\Theta_{m}}\right\uparrow_{\con L}

\displaystyle=\big(\Delta\mapsto m^{-1}(\Delta)\equiv\left\{\,(a_{1},a_{2}):(m(a_{1}),m(a_{2}))\in\Delta\,\right\}\big)

is a frame isomorphism.

(2)

For any element
b\in M
,
m^{-1}(\Phi_{b})=\Theta_{m}\vee\Phi_{m_{*}(b)}
.

Proof.

(2) To show that
\Theta_{m}\vee\Phi_{m_{*}(b)}\leq m^{-1}(\Phi_{b})
, first note that for elements
a_{i}\in L
such that
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\Theta_{m}
we have

\displaystyle m(a_{1})=m(a_{2})

\displaystyle\implies m(a_{1})\wedge b=m(a_{2})\wedge b\iff(m(a_{1}),m(a_{2}))\in\Phi_{b}

\displaystyle\iff(a_{1},a_{2})\in m^{-1}(\Phi_{b}).

Then observe that for any elements
a_{i}\in L
such that
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\Phi_{m_{*}(b)}
we also have

\displaystyle a_{1}\wedge m_{*}(b)=a_{2}\wedge m_{*}(b)\implies

\displaystyle m(a_{1})\wedge b=m(a_{1})\wedge m\circ m_{*}(b)=m(a_{2})\wedge m\circ m_{*}(b)=m(a_{2})\wedge b

\displaystyle\iff(m(a_{1}),m(a_{2}))\in\Phi_{b}\iff(a_{1},a_{2})\in m^{-1}(\Phi_{b}).

To show that
\Theta_{m}\vee\Phi_{m_{*}(b)}\geq m^{-1}(\Phi_{b})

\displaystyle(m(a_{1}),m(a_{2}))\in\Phi_{b}\iff m(a_{1})\wedge b=m(a_{2})\wedge b\implies

\displaystyle m_{*}\circ m(a_{1})\wedge m_{*}(b)=m_{*}\circ m(a_{2})\wedge m_{*}(b)\iff

\displaystyle(m_{*}\circ m(a_{1}),m_{*}\circ m(a_{2}))\in\Phi_{m_{*}(b)}.

2.4.
Successors and predecessors, atoms and maximal elements

Definition

(successor, predecessor,
a^{+}
, atom, maximal element,
\max L
, pointless frame, interpolative lattice)
.

When speaking of two elements
a
and
c
of a distributive lattice
L
, we say that
c
is a successor of
a
, or that
a
is a predecessor of
c
, if
c>a
and for any element
b
such that
a\leq b\leq c
, either
b=a
or
b=c
. We denote the set comprised of
a
together with its successors by

a^{+}\equiv\{a\}\cup\left\{\,c:\text{$c$ is a successor of $a$}\,\right\}.

A successor of
\bot
is called an
atom of
L
,
and an element having
\top
as a successor is called
maximal
. We denote the set of maximal elements of a frame
L
by

\max L\equiv\left\{\,a:\text{$a$ is maximal in $L$}\,\right\}.

A frame
L
is called
pointless
if
\max L=\emptyset
. Thus the
“empty frame”

\{\bot=\top\}
, i.e., the topology of the empty space, is pointless, whereas the two element frame
\mathbb{2}=\{\bot\neq\top\}
, i.e., the topology of the singleton space, is not. A lattice
L
is called
interpolative
if it has no successors or predecessors, i.e., if for all elements
a<c
there exists an element
b
such that
a<b<c
.

Lemma 2.4.1
.

The following hold for elements
a
and
b
in a distributive lattice
L
.

(1)

The maps

\displaystyle[a\wedge b,a]\ni c

\displaystyle\longrightarrow c\vee b\ \ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle c\wedge a

\displaystyle\longleftarrow c\in[b,a\vee b]

are inverse lattice isomorphisms.

(2)

a\wedge b^{+}\subseteq(a\wedge b)^{+}
.

Proof.

(2) Consider a successor
c
of
b
. Since

b\leq(a\wedge c)\vee b=(a\vee b)\wedge c\leq c,

either
(a\wedge c)\vee b=b
or
(a\vee b)\wedge c=c
. The first possibility implies that
a\wedge c\leq b
and hence
a\wedge c=a\wedge b
, while the second implies that
a\vee b\geq c
and hence that
a\wedge c
is a successor of

by part (1). In either case we get that

.
∎

(6)

Every complemented successor in
L
arises as in (3). That is, if
c
is a successor of
a
in
L
and
c
is complemented then the map

m\colon L\to\left\downarrow{c}\right\downarrow\times\left\downarrow{c^{*}}\right\downarrow=(b\mapsto(b\wedge c,b\wedge c^{*}))

is an isomorphism, and
m(c)=(c,\bot)
is a successor of
m(a)=(a,\bot)
.

Proof.

The assumption that
L
is regular implies the equivalence of maximality and primeness in (1). Likewise in (2), since an atom
a\in L
is the join of a set
A
of elements rather below it, there must be at least one element
b\in A
such that
b>\bot
. This implies that
b=a
, but more to the point, that
a
is rather below itself, hence
a
is complemented.

(3) If
a
is a successor of
b
then
b
is maximal in
\left\downarrow{a}\right\downarrow
, and since the map
\left\downarrow{a}\right\downarrow\to O_{a}=(c\mapsto a\to c)
is a frame isomorphism,
a\to b
is maximal in
O_{a}
. Thus
a\to b
is prime in
O_{a}
by (1), and
o_{a*}(a\to b)=a\to b
is prime in
L
by Lemma
2.4.2
(1).

On the other hand, if
a>b
and
a\to b\in\max L
then
a\vee(a\to b)=\top
since
a\nleq a\to b
. Therefore Lemma
2.4.1
(1) provides an isomorphism between the intervals
[b,a]
and
[a\to b,\top]
, from which we see that
a
is a successor of
b
.
∎

Lemma 2.4.4
.

For any frame
L
,
\max(\con L)=\left\{\,\Psi_{b}:b\in\max L\,\right\}
. Consequently the set of atoms of
\con L
is
\left\{\Phi_{b}\right\}_{\max L}
.

Proof.

For an element
b\in\max L
, consider a pair
(a_{1},a_{2})\notin\Psi_{b}
, so that
a_{i}\nleq b\geq a_{j}
for
i\neq j
, say
a_{1}\nleq b\geq a_{2}
. Then any congruence which contains both
\Psi_{b}
and
(a_{1},a_{2})
must contain every pair
(a_{3},a_{4})
. For if
(a_{3},a_{4})\notin\Psi_{b}
, say
a_{3}\nleq b\geq a_{4}
, then
\Psi_{b}
contains both
(a_{1},a_{3})
and
(a_{2},a_{4})
, hence also
(a_{3},a_{1})
by symmetry, and then finally
(a_{3},a_{4})
by transitivity. We have proven that
\Psi_{b}\in\max(\con L)
.

On the other hand, suppose
\Xi\in\max(\con L)
. Then
L/\Xi
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{2}\equiv\{\bot,\top\}
, and if
q\colon L\to\mathbb{2}
is the quotient map then
b\equiv\bigvee q^{-1}(\bot)\in\max L
is such that
\Xi=\Psi_{b}
. The second sentence of the lemma follows from the first via Lemma
2.4.3
(2). ∎

Lemma 2.4.5
.

A frame
L
is pointless if and only if it is interpolative.

Proof.

An interpolative frame is clearly pointless. And in any frame
L
, if elements
a<c
admit no element
b
such that
a<b<c
then
a
is maximal in
\left\downarrow{c}\right\downarrow
, and since
\left\downarrow{c}\right\downarrow\approx O_{c}=\left\{c\to b\right\}_{L}
, it follows that
c\to a
is maximal in
O_{c}
. Consequently
o_{c*}(c\to a)=c\to a
is maximal in
L
by Lemma
2.4.2
(1).
∎

2.5.
Round and regular filters

Definition

(filter, independent family of filters, round filter, regular filter,
\regfltr L
, round ideal)
.

A filter on a frame
L
is a nonempty upset
F\subseteq L
which is closed under binary meets. The filter is said to be
proper
if it does not contain
\bot
. A family of filters is said to be
independent
if any two distinct filters of the family contain disjoint elements. A filter
F
is said to be
round
if for every element
a\in F
there exists an element
b\in F
such that
b\prec\!\!\prec a
. Round ideals are defined dually. A filter
F
is said to be
regular
if it is round and
\bigvee_{F}b^{*}=\top
. We denote the family of proper regular filters on a frame
L
by
\regfltr L
.

Notation

(
x_{a}
)
.

We use lowercase letters near the end of the Latin alphabet, e.g., x, y, z, to designate filters on a frame. For a maximal element
a
, we denote the filter
\left\{\,b:b\nleq a\,\right\}
by subscript, e.g.,
x_{a}
,
y_{a}
,
z_{a}
. We use uppercase letters near the end of the alphabet, e.g., W, X, Y, Z, to designate families of filters on a frame.

Lemma
2.5.1
records the basic information about round and regular filters.

Lemma 2.5.1
.

The following hold in any frame
L
.

(1)

Every proper round filter is contained in a maximal proper round filter.

(2)

For every proper filter
x\subseteq L
,

\mathring{x}\equiv\left\{\,a\in x:\exists b\in x\ (b\prec\!\!\prec a)\,\right\}

is the largest round filter contained in
x
.

(3)

A proper round filter
x
is maximal if and only if for every element
a\notin x
and every element
b\prec\!\!\prec a
there exists an element
c\in x
such that
b\wedge c=\bot
.

(4)

Distinct maximal proper round filters contain disjoint elements. Therefore any family of maximal proper round filters is independent.

(5)

If
x
is a maximal proper round filter then
\bigvee_{x}b^{*}
is a prime element of
L
. That is, either
x
is regular, i.e.,
\bigvee_{x}b^{*}=\top
, or
\bigvee_{x}b^{*}=a\in\max L
. In the latter case
x=x_{a}
.

(6)

If
a\in\max L
then
x_{a}
is a maximal proper round filter such that
\bigvee_{x_{a}}b^{*}=a
.

(7)

For any
a\in\max L
,
x_{a}
is completely prime, i.e.,
\bigvee A\in x_{a}
implies
A\cap x_{a}\neq\emptyset
for any subset
A\subseteq L
.

(8)

A proper round filter is maximal if and only if it is of the form
\mathring{y}
for some ultrafilter
y
on
L
.

(9)

A maximal proper round filter
x
on
L
has the following sort of primeness:

(a_{i}\prec\!\!\prec b_{i}\text{ and }a_{1}\vee a_{2}\in x)\implies(b_{1}\in x\text{ or }b_{2}\in x).

Conversely, any proper round filter having this sort of primeness is maximal (among proper round filters).

(10)

The maps

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\langle a^{*}:a\in x\rangle_{\text{idl}}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\langle b^{*}:b\in I\rangle_{\text{fltr}}

\displaystyle\longleftarrow I

constitute inverse order preserving bijections between the sets of round filters and round ideals on
L
.

Proof.

(3) Any proper round filter
x
satisfying this condition is clearly maximal, and if a proper round filter
x
and element
a\notin x
violate this condition, i.e., if there exists an element
b\prec\!\!\prec a
such that
b\wedge c>\bot
for all
c\in x
, then the filter
y
generated by
x\cup\{b\}
has the feature that
\mathring{y}
is a proper round filter properly containing
x
.

(4) If maximal round filters
x_{i}
are distinct then there exist elements
a_{i}\in x_{i}\smallsetminus x_{j}
and elements
b_{i}\in x_{i}
such that
b_{i}\prec\!\!\prec a_{i}
. By (3) there exist elements
c_{i}\in x_{i}
such that
c_{i}\wedge b_{j}=\bot
. But then
c_{i}\wedge b_{i}\in x_{i}
and
(c_{1}\wedge b_{1})\wedge(c_{2}\wedge b_{2})=\bot
.

(5) Suppose that
x
is a maximal proper round filter, and for the sake of argument suppose that
\bigvee_{x}b^{*}=a<c<\top
. We claim that
c\notin x
, for otherwise there exists an element
b\in x
such that
b\prec\!\!\prec c
. But if
d
witnesses
b\prec c
, i.e., if
b\wedge d=\bot
and
c\vee d=\top
, then we get
d\leq b^{*}\leq a\leq c
, resulting in the contradiction
c=c\vee d=\top
.

Since
a<c
there exists an element
d\prec\!\!\prec c
such that
d\nleq a
, whereupon part (3) above produces an element
b\in x
such that
b\wedge d=\bot
. We are led to the contradiction
d\leq b^{*}\leq a
. We conclude that
\bigvee_{x}b^{*}
is prime.

If
b\in x
then since
x
is round there exists an element
c\in x
such that
c\prec b
. Let
d
witness
c\prec b
, i.e.,
c\wedge d=\bot
and
d\vee b=\top
, so that
d\leq c^{*}\leq a
. Then
b\nleq a
, for otherwise
\top=d\vee b\leq a
, contrary to assumption. On the other hand, if
b\notin x
then every element
c\prec\!\!\prec b
is disjoint from some element
d\in x
, hence
c\leq d^{*}\leq a
. It follows that
b=\bigvee\left\{\,c:c\prec\!\!\prec b\,\right\}\leq a
.

(6) If
a\in\max L
and
b\in x_{a}
then, since
b=\bigvee C
for
C\equiv\left\{\,c:c\prec\!\!\prec b\,\right\}
, it must be the case that
C\cap x_{a}\neq\emptyset
, for otherwise the fact that
C\subseteq\left\downarrow{a}\right\downarrow
would lead to the contradiction
b=\bigvee C\in\left\downarrow{a}\right\downarrow
. Thus
x_{a}
is round. To see that
x_{a}
is maximal among proper round filters, consider elements
d\prec\!\!\prec c\notin x_{a}
. Then
c\leq a
, so there exists an element
b
witnessing
d\prec a
, i.e.,
b\wedge d=\bot
and
b\vee a=\top
. Then
b\nleq a
, for otherwise we would have
a=b\vee a=\top
, contrary to hypothesis. That is,
b\in x_{a}
. The fact that
\bigvee_{x_{a}}b^{*}=a
now follows from (5).

(7) If for some subset
A\subseteq L
and element
a\in\max L
we have
\bigvee A=b\in x_{a}
then
A\nsubseteq\left\downarrow{a}\right\downarrow
, for otherwise
b=\bigvee A\leq a
, contrary to assumption.

(8) If a proper round filter
x
is maximal then it is the largest round filter contained in any filter containing it. And if
x=\mathring{y}
for some ultrafilter
y
on
L
and if
b\prec\!\!\prec a\notin x
then there must exist an element
b_{1}
such that
b\prec\!\!\prec b_{1}\prec\!\!\prec a
, for which we know that
b,b_{1}\notin y
by (3). By virtue of the maximality of
y
, therefore,
b^{*},b_{1}^{*}\in y
, and since
b_{1}^{*}\prec\!\!\prec b^{*}
we get
b^{*}\in x
by (2). We can conclude that
x
is maximal among proper round filters by (3).

(9) Let
x
be a maximal proper round filter and let
y
be an ultrafilter for which
x=\mathring{y}
, and suppose that
a_{i}\prec\!\!\prec b_{i}
. If
a_{1}\vee a_{2}\in x
then
a_{1}\vee a_{2}\in y
, hence either
a_{1}\in y
or
a_{2}\in y
, with the result that either
b_{1}\in\mathring{y}=x
or
b_{2}\in\mathring{y}=x
.

Conversely, suppose a proper round filter
x
enjoys this sort of primeness, and consider elements
b\prec\!\!\prec a\notin x
with witnessing family
\left\{c_{p}\right\}_{\mathbb{Q}}
for
b\prec\!\!\prec a
. Fix
p<q<r
in
\mathbb{Q}
. Then
c_{q}^{*}\vee c_{r}=\top\in x
, and since
c_{q}^{*}\prec\!\!\prec c_{p}^{*}
with witnessing family
\left\{c_{s}^{*}\right\}_{p<s<q}
and
c_{r}\prec\!\!\prec a
with witnessing family
\left\{c_{s}\right\}_{r<s}
, the primeness of
x
yields
c_{p}^{*}\in x
or
a\in x
, and the latter condition is ruled out by assumption. We conclude that
x
is maximal among proper round filters by (3).

(10) This follows from the fact that for elements
a
and
b
in any frame,
a\prec\!\!\prec b
if and only if
b^{*}\prec\!\!\prec a^{*}
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Lemma
2.5.1
(5) will be important in what follows.

Corollary 2.5.2
.

A maximal proper round filter on a pointless frame is regular.

.

Let

(1)

If

then
m(x)

.

(2)

The filter generated by

m^{-1}(y)\mathring{}=x

and

.

3.
Two nuclei

In the section we introduce and investigate the nuclei of interest.

3.1.
The nucleus
\sigma
and the spatial part of a frame

Lemma 3.1.1
.

On a frame
L
, the map

\sigma_{L}\colon L\to L\equiv\left(a\mapsto\bigwedge\left\uparrow{a}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\right)

is a nucleus with fixed point set

\fix\sigma_{L}=\left\{\,a:a=\bigwedge\left\uparrow{a}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\,\right\}\equiv\sigma L

and kernel

\ker\sigma_{L}=\left\{\,a:\left\uparrow{a}\right\uparrow_{\max L}=\emptyset\,\right\}.

Proof.

The map
\sigma_{L}
is clearly increasing and order preserving; to verify that it preserves binary meets, simply compute

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}(a_{1})\wedge\sigma_{L}(a_{2})=\bigwedge\left\uparrow{a_{1}}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\wedge\bigwedge\left\uparrow{a_{2}}\right\uparrow_{\max L}=

\displaystyle\bigwedge\big(\left\uparrow{a_{1}}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\wedge\left\uparrow{a_{2}}\right\uparrow_{\max L}\big)=\bigwedge\left\uparrow{a_{1}\wedge a_{2}}\right\uparrow_{\max L}=\sigma_{L}(a_{1}\wedge a_{2}).

The penultimate equality is attributable to the primeness of maximal elements.
∎

Recall that a frame is called spatial if every element is the meet of the maximal elements above it. See
[
16
, II 5.3]
.

Proposition 3.1.2
.

The following hold for any frame
L
.

(1)

\sigma L
is spatial.

(2)

L
is spatial if and only if
\sigma_{L}
is an isomorphism if and only if
\ker\sigma_{L}=\{\top\}
if and only if

\forall a<\top\,\exists b\in\max L\ (a\leq b).

(3)

L
is pointless if and only if
\sigma L
is empty, i.e.,
\sigma L=\{\top\}
.

Proof.

The nucleus
\sigma_{L}
fixes each maximal element, and each maximal element of
L
remains maximal in
\sigma L
.
∎

Definition

(spatial part of a frame,
\mathbf{sF}
)
.

For a frame
L
, we refer to the map
\sigma_{L}\colon L\to\sigma L
or to its codomain
\sigma L
as the
spatial part of
L
.
We designate the full subcategory of
\mathbf{F}
comprised of the spatial frames by
\mathbf{sF}
.

.

m\colon L\to M

\sigma_{L}

3.2.
The nucleus
\pi
and the pointless part of a frame

Proposition 3.2.1
.

In a frame
L
, the map

\pi_{L}^{\prime}(a)\equiv\bigvee a^{+}

is a prenucleus, with fixed point set

\pi L\equiv\left\{\,a:\text{$a$ has no successor}\,\right\}=\left\{\,a:\forall c>a\>\exists b\ (a<b<c)\,\right\},

and with kernel being the normal filter generated by
\max L
, namely

\ker\pi_{L}=\left\{\,a:\forall b\geq a\ (b<\top\implies\text{$b$ has a successor})\,\right\}.

Proof.

The map
\pi_{L}^{\prime}
is increasing by construction. To show that
\pi_{L}^{\prime}
is order-preserving, consider
a\leq b
in
L
. Then for each
c\in a^{+}
, either
c\leq b
or
b\vee c\in b^{+}
by Lemma
2.4.1
, with the result that

\pi_{L}^{\prime}(a)=\bigvee a^{+}\leq\bigvee b^{+}=\pi_{L}^{\prime}(b).

To check that
\pi_{L}^{\prime}(a\wedge b)\geq a\wedge\pi_{L}^{\prime}(b)
, simply observe that by Lemma
2.4.1
(2) we have

\pi_{L}^{\prime}(a\wedge b)=\bigvee(a\wedge b)^{+}\geq\bigvee(a\wedge b^{+})=a\wedge\bigvee b^{+}=a\wedge\pi_{L}^{\prime}(b).

Certainly
\max L\subseteq\ker\pi_{L}
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(a)=\top
for each
a\in\max L
; after all,
\top\in a^{+}
. This establishes that
\delta_{\max L}\leq\pi_{L}
, where
\delta_{\max L}
represents the nucleus corresponding as in Lemmas
2.2.1
and
2.2.2
to the filter generated by
\max L
. We claim that
\delta_{\max L}(a)\geq c
whenever
c
is a successor of
a
. For in that case
b\equiv c\to a\in\max L
by Lemma
2.4.3
(3), hence

\displaystyle\top

\displaystyle=\delta_{\max L}(c\to a)\leq\delta_{\max L}(c)\to\delta_{\max L}(a)

\displaystyle\implies c\leq\delta_{\max L}(c)\leq\delta_{\max L}(a).

The claim implies that
\pi_{L}\leq\delta_{\max L}
.
∎

The fact that
\ker\pi_{L}
is generated by the maximal elements of
L
(Proposition
3.2.1
) implies that the congruence of
\pi_{L}
is the join of the open congruences of the elements of
\max L
.

Corollary 3.2.2
.

For any frame

\Theta_{\pi_{L}}=\bigvee_{\max L}\Phi_{a}.

In terms of sublocales,

\pi L=\fix\pi_{L}=\bigcap_{\max L}O_{a}.

For a frame

The pointless part of a frame can be characterized in terms of its maximal elements.

Proposition 3.2.3
.

For any frame

,

\pi L=\left\{\,a:\forall c\ (a\leq c\in\max L\implies c\to a=a)\,\right\}.

Proof.

Suppose that

a\leq c
for some

such that

. Then

is a successor of

\pi_{L}(a)\geq c\to a>a

3.3.
An example: the pointless real numbers
\pi\mathscr{O}\mathbb{R}

Consider the topology
\mathscr{O}\mathbb{R}
of the real numbers, whose skeleton
\delta\mathscr{O}\mathbb{R}
is the complete and atomless boolean algebra of regular open subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. An open subset of the reals is uniquely expressible as a disjoint union of open intervals, and such a set is unpunctured if and only if no pair of its intervals are abutting, i.e., share an endpoint. A familiar example of an unpunctured open set is the complement of the Cantor set. The sublocale of unpuntured subsets of the reals forms a sublocale which evidently strictly contains the skeleton.

.

3.4.
Decomposing a frame into its scattered and atomless parts

In classical topology a space is called
scattered
if every nonempty closed subset contains an isolated point. We take the frame counterpart of this notion as our definition, while acknowledging that another definition is the starting point of the extensive and scholarly treatment of the topic in Chapter IX of the excellent reference
[
17
]
. See also
[
7
]
.

Definition

(scattered frame, scattered element, coscattered element)
.

A frame
L
is called
scattered
if every element
a<\top
has a successor. An element
a
of a frame
L
is called
scattered
if
\left\downarrow{a}\right\downarrow_{L}
(or
O_{a}
) is a scattered frame, and it is called
coscattered
if
\left\uparrow{a}\right\uparrow_{L}
(or
C_{a}
) is a scattered frame.

Lemma 3.4.1
.

The following hold in an arbitrary frame
L
with
e\equiv\pi_{L}(\bot)
.

(1)

e
is the largest scattered element of
L
, and
o_{e}\colon L\to O_{e}
is the largest scattered open quotient of
L
.

(2)

L
is scattered if and only if
e=\top
if and only if
\bot
is coscattered if and only if the pointless part of
L
is empty.

(3)

e=\bot
if and only if the pointless part
\pi L
is a dense quotient of
L
if and only if
L
is atomless.

Proof.

The condition that
\pi L=\{\top\}
is equivalent to the condition that
\pi_{L}^{*}(\bot)=\top
by Proposition
3.2.1
, which is to say that
\pi_{L}^{\prime}(a)>a
for all
a<\top
.
∎

Definition

(scattered and atomless parts of a frame)
.

For a frame
L
, let
e\equiv\pi_{L}(\bot)
. We refer to the map
o_{e}\colon L\to O_{e}
or to its codomain as the
scattered part of
L
. We refer to the map
c_{e}\colon L\to C_{e}
or to its codomain as the
atomless part of
L
.

Definition

(subdirect product of frames)
.

A subdirect product is a subobject of a product for which the projection morphisms are surjective.

Proposition 3.4.2
.

Every frame is (isomorphic to) a subdirect product of its scattered and atomless parts. Its scattered part is an open quotient and its atomless part is the complementary closed quotient. The frame is scattered if and only if its atomless part is empty and atomless if and only if its scattered part is empty.

Proof.

We have the product map
o_{e}\times c_{e}\colon L\to O_{e}\times C_{e}
, which is one-one because the corresponding congruences
\Phi_{e}
and
\Psi_{e}
are complements in
\con L
and therefore intersect to the identity congruence. ∎

3.5.

Lemma 3.5.1
.

For any frame
L

\sigma_{L}\wedge\pi_{L}=\bot_{\con L}=1_{L}

Proof.

We prove by induction that
\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(a)=a
for any

\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge\pi_{L}^{0}(a)=\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge a=a

If
c\in\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)^{+}
then either
c=\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)
or
c
is a successor of
\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)
. In the first case
\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge c=a
by the inductive hypothesis. In the second case
b\equiv c\to\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)\in\max L
by Lemma
2.4.3
(3), and since
b\geq\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)\geq a
, it follows that
b\geq\bigwedge\left\uparrow{a}\right\uparrow_{\max L}=\sigma_{L}(a)
. Therefore
\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge c\leq b\wedge c\leq\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)
, so that by the inductive hypothesis we get
\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge c\leq\sigma_{L}(a)\wedge\pi_{L}^{\beta}(a)=a
. Since the persistence of the assertion through limit ordinals is evident, the induction is complete.
∎

4.

7.

In this section we focus on the interaction between a frame and its spatial support. In Subsection

7.1.
The spatial support determines spatiality and compactness

We show in Proposition

that a necessary condition for an atomless frame to be spatial is that it must contain enough regular filters in its spatial support to distinguish the elements of its pointless part. This requires the preparatory Lemma

Lemma 7.1.1
.

For an element
b
of a frame
L
, define

A^{\prime}(b)\equiv\left\{\,a\in\max L:a\geq b\text{ and }a\to b>b\,\right\},

and for ordinal numbers
\beta
define

\displaystyle A^{\beta}(b)

\displaystyle\equiv\{b\}

if
\beta=0
,

\displaystyle A^{\beta}(b)

\displaystyle\equiv A^{\alpha}(b)\cup A^{\prime}(\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b))

if
\beta=\alpha+1
,

\displaystyle A^{\beta}(b)

\displaystyle\equiv\bigcup_{\alpha<\beta}A^{\alpha}(b)

if
\beta
is a limit ordinal, and

\displaystyle A(b)

\displaystyle\equiv A^{\beta}(b)

for some (any)
\beta
such that
A^{\beta}(b)=A^{\beta+1}(b)
.

Then
b=\pi_{L}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A(b)
.

Proof.

We first claim that
b=\pi_{L}^{\prime}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\prime}(b)
for any
b\in L
. For

\displaystyle\left(\bigwedge A^{\prime}(b)\right)\wedge\pi^{\prime}_{L}(b)=\left(\bigwedge A^{\prime}(b)\right)\wedge\left(b\vee\bigvee_{A^{\prime}(b)}(a\to b)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(b\wedge\bigwedge A^{\prime}(b)\right)\vee\bigvee_{A^{\prime}(b)}\left(\left(\bigwedge A^{\prime}(b)\right)\wedge(a\to b)\right)

\displaystyle=b\vee\bigvee_{A^{\prime}(b)}\left(\left(\bigwedge A^{\prime}(b)\right)\wedge(a\to b)\right)=b.

We then use induction to show that
b=\pi_{L}^{\beta}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\beta}(b)
for all
\beta
. The assertion clearly holds for
\beta=0
, so assume it holds for all ordinals
\alpha<\beta
. If
\beta=\alpha+1
then

\displaystyle\pi_{L}^{\beta}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\beta}(b)=\pi_{L}^{\alpha+1}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\alpha+1}(b)=

\displaystyle\pi_{L}^{\prime}\circ\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b)\wedge\bigwedge\left(A^{\alpha}(b)\cup A^{\prime}(\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b))\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\pi_{L}^{\prime}\circ\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\prime}(\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b))\right)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\alpha}(b)=

\displaystyle\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\alpha}(b)=b.

(The penultimate equality holds by the claim and the ultimate equality by the induction hypothesis.) If
\beta
is a limit ordinal then we get

\displaystyle b\leq\pi_{L}^{\beta}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\beta}(b)=\bigvee_{\alpha<\beta}\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b)\wedge\bigwedge\bigcup_{\gamma<\beta}A^{\gamma}(b)=

\displaystyle\bigvee_{\alpha<\beta}\left(\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b)\wedge\bigwedge\bigcup_{\gamma<\beta}A^{\gamma}(b)\right)\leq\bigvee_{\alpha<\beta}\left(\pi_{L}^{\alpha}(b)\wedge\bigwedge A^{\alpha}(b)\right)=b.\qed
