The sticky particle dynamics of the 1D pressureless Euler-alignment system as a gradient flow
Sondre Tesdal Galtung

Abstract
. We show how the sticky dynamics for the one-dimensional pressureless Euler-alignment system can be obtained as an L 2 L^{2} -gradient flow of a convex functional. This is analogous to the Lagrangian evolution introduced by Natile and Savaré for the pressureless Euler system, and by Brenier et al. for the corresponding system with a self-interacting force field. Our Lagrangian evolution can be seen as the limit of sticky particle Cucker–Smale dynamics, similar to the solutions obtained by Leslie and Tan from a corresponding scalar balance law, and provides us with a uniquely determined distributional solution of the original system in the space of probability measures with quadratic moments and corresponding square-integrable velocities. Moreover, we show that the gradient flow also provides an entropy solution to the balance law of Leslie and Tan, and how their results on cluster formation follow naturally from (non-)monotonicity properties of the so-called natural velocity of the flow.

1.
Introduction

We consider the one-dimensional pressureless Euler-alignment system for density
\rho_{t}
and scalar velocity
v_{t}
depending on time
t\geq 0
and position
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which reads

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v_{t})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho_{t}v_{t})+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v^{2}_{t})

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}(\phi\ast(\rho_{t}v_{t}))-\rho_{t}v_{t}(\phi\ast\rho_{t}).

Here
\phi
is a communication protocol appearing in convolutions with the density and momentum,
which we assume to be symmetric, radially nonincreasing and locally integrable, i.e.,
\phi\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.
In particular,
\phi
may be
weakly singular
of order
\beta\in(0,1)
, that is, there exist constants
R>0
and
c>0
such that

(1.2)

\phi(r)\geq cr^{-\beta}\quad\text{for all }r\in(0,R).

A different facet of the communication protocol concerns its asymptotic behavior.
Here
\phi
can have a thin or fat tail, respectively defined by

(1.3)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty\qquad\text{or}\qquad\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\infty.

That is, the tail of
\phi
is either integrable or not.
A thin tail implies that there is only weak or no communication over larger distances, while a fat tail means there is strong communication.

The Euler-alignment system originates from the theory of collective behavior, see
[
32
]
for a comprehensive overview of such models.
It has been studied under various regularity assumptions, and one may even consider measure-valued solutions.
A formal integration of (
1.1
) shows that both the total mass and momentum are conserved, the latter by virtue of the symmetry of the communication protocol.

The corresponding particle dynamics, which can be obtained by considering (
1.1
) for the empirical measures

\rho_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\delta(x-x_{i}),\qquad\rho_{t}v_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}v_{i}\delta(x-x_{i}),

where
\delta
denotes a Dirac measure, is described by the following system of ordinary differential equations,

(1.4)

\dot{x}_{i}=v_{i},\qquad\dot{v}_{i}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
x_{j}\neq x_{i}\end{subarray}}^{N}m_{j}\phi(x_{i}-x_{j})(v_{i}-v_{j})

for masses
\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, positions
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and velocities
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
N
particles.
This corresponds to the well-known Cucker–Smale model for swarming
[
12
]
.

Such alignment models, especially those with Cucker–Smale-type communication protocols, have been studied intensively in both one and more dimensions,
covering both hydrodynamic and particle systems like (
1.1
) and (
1.4
), as well as kinetic equations
[
11
,
28
]
.
Topics of interest include well-posedness of solutions and critical thresholds for blow-up
[
33
,
9
,
4
]
, and asymptotic behavior
[
17
,
15
,
19
]
.
An important notion in this context is flocking, that the support of the solutions remain bounded in time; it has been shown in
[
16
,
33
]
that fat-tailed protocols lead to flocking for both the particle system (
1.4
) and strong solutions of (
1.1
).

When the communication protocol is too singular, i.e., not locally integrable, concentration of mass cannot happen, cf.
[
9
]
.
Since we are specifically interested in mass which may collide and remain concentrated, we will here assume
\phi\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, as detailed below, we will study the connection between (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) through a gradient flow structure. On this note, we mention recent the works
[
28
,
27
]
which concern a gradient flow framework for kinetic alignment systems.

1.1.
Reduced systems

Here we give the ideas for how the second-order Euler-alignment system has been studied through reduction to first-order systems, both in the particle and continuum settings.

Leveraging the fact that we are on the line, the alignment force driving the acceleration in (
1.4
) can be written as an exact derivative, involving the primitive of the communication kernel
\phi
,

(1.5)

\Phi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

From our assumptions it follows that this is a continuous and odd function.
An extreme case is the so-called all-to-all communication
\phi\equiv K
for some positive constant
K>0
, cf.
[
1
]
, in which case
\Phi(x)=Kx
.
Figure
1
illustrates both bounded and weakly singular communication protocols
\phi
, as well as their corresponding primitives
\Phi
.

Figure 1
.
Illustration of the function
\Phi
in the case of (a) bounded and (b) weakly singular communication protocols
\phi
. We will return to the convex function
W_{\Phi}
, the primitive of
\Phi
, in Section
4
.

For future reference, we list some useful properties of
\Phi
.

Lemma 1.1
.

The function
\Phi
in (
1.5
) has the following properties:

(a)

It is continuous, nondecreasing and odd,
\Phi(-x)=-\Phi(x)
.

(b)

It is concave and subadditive for
x\geq 0
, and so by (a), it is convex and superadditive for
x\leq 0
,

\Phi(\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert)\leq\Phi(\lvert x\rvert)+\Phi(\lvert y\rvert).

In particular, if
\phi\neq 0
, then
\Phi
is a modulus of continuity, that is, a concave and continuous function
\Phi\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
which satisfies
0=\Phi(0)<\Phi(r)
for any
r>0
.

(c)

It is pointwise linearly bounded, i.e.,

(1.6)

\lvert\Phi(x)\rvert\leq\Phi(1)\max\{1,\lvert x\rvert\}\leq\Phi(1)(1+\lvert x\rvert).

(d)

By (a), (b) and the (reverse) triangle inequality,

\lvert\lvert\Phi(x)\rvert-\lvert\Phi(y)\rvert\rvert\leq\Phi(\lvert\lvert x\rvert-\lvert y\rvert\rvert)\leq\Phi(\lvert x-y\rvert).

Proof.

Property (a) follows from the definition (
1.5
) and
\phi\geq 0
.
Property (b) is a consequence of
\phi
being nonincreasing for
x\geq 0
. Since
\phi(x)
is nonincreasing for
x\geq 0
, we furthermore have that
\Phi(x)
is concave and subadditive for
x\geq 0
.
For property (c), observe that for
x\in[0,1]
,
\Phi(x)\leq\Phi(1)=\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}(0,1)}
. On the other hand, for
x>1
,
\Phi(x)-\Phi(1)\leq\Phi(1)(x-1)
since the integrand
\phi
is nonincreasing and must be less than its average
\Phi(1)
on
[0,1]
. Hence, by symmetry,
\lvert\Phi(x)\rvert\leq\Phi(1)\max\{1,\lvert x\rvert\}
.
The second inequality in (d) is a consequence of
\Phi
being nondecreasing and the reverse triangle inequality
\lvert\lvert x\rvert-\lvert y\rvert\rvert\leq\lvert x-y\rvert
. The first inequality can be rephrased as
\lvert\int_{\lvert y\rvert}^{\lvert x\rvert}\phi(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{\lvert\lvert x\rvert-\lvert y\rvert\rvert}\phi(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z
, which is true due to
\phi
being nonincreasing.
∎

The function
\Phi
in (
1.5
) plays a central role in several works, e.g.,
[
17
,
20
]
where one takes advantage of working on the line in order to reduce the second order system (
1.4
) to a first-order particle system, and similarly in
[
21
]
where (
1.1
) is studied using a scalar balance law.

1.1.1.
The particle case

In the discrete, or particle case, the initial value problem for (
1.4
) can be rewritten as

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\left(\dot{x}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(x_{i}-x_{j})\right)=0,\quad x_{i}(0)=\bar{x}_{i},\quad v_{i}(0)=\bar{v}_{i},

which in turn can be integrated to yield the Kuramoto-type equation

(1.7)

\dot{x}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(x_{i}-x_{j})=\bar{v}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(\bar{x}_{i}-\bar{x}_{j})\eqqcolon\bar{\psi}_{i}.

Observe how in the reduction to a first-order system,
\bar{v}_{i}
goes from an initial condition to a parameter through the quantity
\bar{\psi}_{i}
, known as the
natural velocity
, cf.
[
17
]
.
This is likely in analogy to the corresponding quantity for the original Kuramoto system
[
18
]
, which models coupled, nonlinear oscillators and reads

(1.8)

\dot{\theta}_{i}=\omega_{i}-\frac{\nu}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,N\},\quad\nu>0.

Here
\theta_{i}
is the phase of the
i
th
oscillator, while the constant
\omega_{i}
is often called its
natural frequency
.
There is an extensive literature on the Kuramoto model, where one has studied equilibria and phenomena like phase-locking and synchronization, as well as extensions to kinetic models like the Kuramoto–Sakaguchi equation
[
23
]
; we will merely refer to the aforementioned recent work and the references therein.
It was observed in
[
34
]
that (
1.8
) has a gradient flow structure; however, the corresponding energy functional is not convex.
Applying this idea to the reduction of the Cucker–Smale system, it can be seen as a gradient flow for the scalar potential

(1.9)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}m_{j}m_{k}\int_{0}^{x_{j}-x_{k}} \Phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\bar{\psi}_{j}x_{j},

and the study of clustering in
[
17
,
15
,
37
]
shows that the corresponding trajectories are well-defined between collisions.
In their analysis, the trajectories are allowed to cross, and the natural velocities
\{\bar{\psi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
provide an ordering of the asymptotic trajectories.
However, Leslie and Tan
[
22
]
argue that this crossing of trajectories is not well-suited for hydrodynamic limits of the particle system, and so they advocate for a different point of view, which we describe next.

1.1.2.
An associated scalar balance law

Returning to the continuum setting, we introduce the quantity

(1.10)

\psi_{t}\coloneqq v_{t}+\Phi\ast\rho_{t},

which can be seen as the continuum version of the discrete conserved variable from the previous section.
Then one can formally rewrite (
1.1
) as

(1.11a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v_{t})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.11b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho_{t}\psi_{t})+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}\psi_{t}v_{t})

\displaystyle=0.

Based on the above system and drawing inspiration from
[
7
]
, Leslie and Tan
[
21
]
derive an associated scalar balance law for
M_{t}=\rho_{t}((-\infty,x])
,

(1.12)

\partial_{t}M_{t}+\partial_{x}A(M_{t})=(\phi\ast M_{t})\partial_{x}M_{t},

where the flux function
A
depends only on the initial data.
The initial density
\bar{\rho}
is assumed to be a compactly supported probability measure, i.e.,
\bar{\rho}\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R})
, while the initial velocity
\bar{v}
is essentially bounded with respect to
\bar{\rho}
, i.e.,
\bar{v}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
.
After establishing existence and uniqueness of entropy solutions of (
1.12
) in the sense of Kružkov, they show, using the
BV
-calculus of Vol’pert
[
36
]
, that these solutions provide distributional solutions of (
1.1
) exhibiting “sticky dynamics”.
Furthermore, with this framework they study clustering for the Euler-alignment system (
1.1
) in
[
22
]
, and show that the global clustering behavior can be deduced from the initial data through the flux function
A
.

1.2.
The aim of this paper

Before stating the aim and main results of this paper, we present the motivation for our study.

1.2.1.
Gradient flows and entropy solutions for pressureless Euler systems

Pressureless Euler systems similar to (
1.1
) have previously been studied using gradient flows in Hilbert spaces.
Suppose
\rho_{t}
is a probability measure for
t\geq 0
, denoted
\rho_{t}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R})
, and that
v_{t}
is such that the momentum
v_{t}\rho_{t}
is a Radon measure, denoted
v_{t}\rho_{t}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R})
.
In particular, we are interested in probability measures
\rho_{t}
with finite second moment and velocities
v_{t}
which are square-integrable with respect to
\rho_{t}
.
That is,
\rho_{t}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
v_{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho_{t})
, meaning

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert x\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert v_{t}\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}<\infty.

Then it turns out that
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is isometric to the convex cone
\mathscr{K}\subset L^{2}(0,1)
of nondecreasing functions.
That is, any
\rho_{t}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
can be associated with an
X_{t}\in\mathscr{K}
, and vice versa.

This framework was used by Natile and Savaré to study the pressureless Euler system
[
24
]
, corresponding to (
1.1
) with
\phi\equiv 0
.
The idea is to use a similar reduction to a first-order system to see the initial (natural) velocity as the gradient of a linear functional.
To ensure that solutions remain in
\mathscr{K}
, this functional is augmented with the indicator function
I_{\mathscr{K}}
, which in turn replaces the gradient with a set-valued subdifferential.
The convexity of the functional makes the subdifferential a maximally monotone operator. Hence, by the theory of Brézis
[
8
]
, there is a unique solution
X_{t}
, with velocity
V_{t}
, evolving according to the minimal element of the subdifferential.
This gives rise to a distributional solution of the original system which turns out to be globally sticky, that is, mass that clusters will remain clustered.
In particular, they recover results of
[
7
]
obtained with a conservation law-approach.

In what can be seen as an extension of the above framework, called the Lagrangian evolution, Brenier et al.
[
6
]
study the pressureless Euler system with a self-interacting force field
f[\rho_{t}]
driving the momentum equation.
Under certain assumptions on
f
, the resulting force can be seen as a Lipschitz perturbation of the subdifferential in
[
24
]
, which is still covered by the results of Brézis, i.e.,
[
8
, Theoreme 3.17, Remarque 3.14]
.
A specific case covered by their theory is the Euler–Poisson system, which is also shown to have globally sticky solutions in the case of an attractive force.
Note that this form of differential inclusion can be framed as an evolution variational inequality, and both
[
24
]
and
[
6
]
make use of additional results from
[
31
]
, see also
[
30
]
, to have better estimates for the velocity
V_{t}
.

On a slightly different note,
[
5
]
concerns a singular, nonlocal interaction equation, which can be regarded as a first-order version of the Euler–Poisson system.
For this equation one can also formulate
L^{2}
-gradient flow solutions, as well as, in the spirit of
[
7
]
, entropy solutions of an associated conservation law.
The authors prove that these notions of solution are in fact equivalent, by means of passing to a limit in particle approximations.
Based on an observation relating the minimal evolution of the gradient flow and the Oleĭnik E condition for conservation laws,
[
10
]
extended the equivalence from
[
5
]
to pressureless Euler systems with appropriate forcing terms
f
.
Specifically, one finds that the solutions of Euler–Poisson obtained in
[
6
]
using gradient flows and solutions obtained from entropy solutions of a conservation law in
[
25
]
are equivalent.
We also mention the work
[
3
]
, where
[
5
]
and
[
21
]
inspired the use of a balance law to study the mean-field limit of a second-order particle system for opinion dynamics.

1.2.2.
Aim and main results

We mentioned before how sticky solutions of (
1.1
) can be obtained from entropy solutions of the balance law (
1.12
) for initial data
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
.
Considering the aforementioned equivalences between gradient flows and entropy solutions, it is then tempting to follow
[
24
,
6
]
in extending to
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})

by formulating an associated gradient flow solution.
However, there are some apparent obstacles.
For one, (
1.1
) is not covered directly by the theory in
[
6
]
, as the forcing term is of the form
f[\rho_{t},v_{t}]
rather than
f[\rho_{t}]
.
Moreover, in
[
21
]
they allow for weakly singular
\phi
, for which the corresponding alignment force cannot be seen as a Lipschitz perturbation of the subdifferential in
[
24
]
.

We take a different approach to overcome this problem, regarding instead the alignment force as part of the subdifferential.
Indeed, unlike for the Kuramoto model, the functional (
1.9
) is in fact convex and its
L^{2}
-version can then be augmented with the indicator function
I_{\mathscr{K}}
to form a convex functional.

Yet another indication that the framework of
L^{2}
-gradient flows is suitable for the problem at hand is found in
[
22
]
.
There the authors use the flux function
A
from (
1.12
), which can be regarded as the primitive of the
L^{2}
-version of the natural velocities, and its lower convex envelope
A^{**}
to predict the clustering behavior of the sticky solutions.
The suggestion comes from a relation in
[
24
]
which connects the projection of a function onto the convex cone
\mathscr{K}
and the lower convex envelope of its primitive.
In this sense,
A^{**}
encodes the projection of the natural velocity onto this cone.

With this in mind, we seek the following type of solutions to the Euler-alignment system.

Definition 1.2

(Distributional solutions of the Euler-alignment system)
.

The pair
(\rho_{t},v_{t})\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho_{t})
is a distributional solution of the initial value problem for the Euler-alignment system if it satisfies (
1.1
) in the distributional sense, and for initial values
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})
we have

(1.13)

\lim\limits_{t\to 0+}\rho_{t}=\bar{\rho}\quad\text{in}\ \mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{t\to 0+}v_{t}\rho_{t}=\bar{v}\bar{\rho}\quad\text{in}\ \mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}).

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
provides a motivation of the gradient flow solution for the particle case.
Some auxiliary results on optimal transport and convex analysis are presented in Section
3
, leading to the associated Lagrangian solutions in Section
4
.
Section
5
details how the Lagrangian solution yields a solution of the original Euler-alignment system, as well as for the associated balance law.
Finally, in Section
6
we show how the Lagrangian solutions can be used to derive the clustering properties of the sticky solutions.

We finish this section with a colloquial presentation of our main results, while we point to later theorems and corollaries for precise statements and full-fledged details.

Result 1

(Lagrangian solution)
.

There is a unique Lagrangian, or
L^{2}
-gradient flow, solution concept associated with the Euler-alignment system (
1.1
).

That is, given the initial value problem for (
1.1
), we can define an associated convex potential, or functional, which includes the term
I_{\mathscr{K}}
, the indicator function of the convex cone
\mathscr{K}
of nondecreasing
L^{2}(0,1)
-functions.
Because of this term, we must relax the notion of a gradient and instead consider the set-valued subdifferential of the functional, where an element is often called a subgradient.
Then there is a unique map
t\mapsto X_{t}\in\mathscr{K}
which evolves according to the subgradient of minimal
L^{2}
-norm.
This is the Lagrangian solution, and it can be seen as a generalization of the particle positions for the Cucker–Smale-type system (
1.7
), which we recall had a gradient-flow structure based on the potential (
1.9
).
The details of Result
1
can be found in Theorem
4.6
.

Result 2

(Particle approximation)
.

The Lagrangian solution can be realized as the limit of solutions of sticky-particle Cucker–Smale dynamics.
From these approximations, we deduce that the solution is globally sticky and features projection formulas and a semigroup property.

The solutions of the sticky-particle Cucker–Smale dynamics evolve according to (
1.7
), with the additional rule that particles which collide stick together in such a way that momentum is conserved.
For such a particle solution we can construct a step function on the interval
[0,1)
, where the length of the
i
th
step is the mass of the
i
th
particle, while the value at that step is the position of said particle; this turns out to be a Lagrangian solution.
We can then approximate the Lagrangian solution
X_{t}
by first approximating its initial data and velocity with step functions, and then let these evolve according to the corresponding sticky-particle dynamics.
The convergence then follows from stability properties of Lagrangian solutions and convergence of the initial approximations, see Theorem
4.16
for details.

Since the particle solutions are globally sticky, we can prove that also the limiting Lagrangian solution has this property, meaning that concentrated mass remains concentrated for all time.
This allows us to express the Lagrangian solutions in terms of projection formulas related to the cone
\mathscr{K}
. These projections provide a semigroup property for the solutions, and may be of interest for numerical implementations.
Further details on the stickiness property and the projection formulas are respectively provided in Corollary
4.17
and Proposition
4.11
.

Result 3

(Distributional solution)
.

The globally sticky Lagrangian solution gives rise to a distributional solution of the Euler-alignment system (
1.1
) and an entropy solution of the balance law (
1.12
), both of which are uniquely defined.

Let
X_{t}
be the Lagrangian solution associated with a set of initial data for (
1.1
), and define
\rho_{t}
as the pushforward of the Lebesgue measure on
(0,1)
through the map
X_{t}
.
Then there is a unique
v_{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho_{t})
for which
v_{t}\circ X_{t}
coincides with the velocity of
X_{t}
, and we can show that the pair
(\rho_{t},v_{t})
satisfies (
1.1
) in the distributional sense.
On the other hand, introducing
M_{t}
as the generalized inverse of
X_{t}
, we can show that this in fact satisfies (
1.12
) in the weak sense.
This relaxes the assumptions on initial data for (
1.1
) from
[
21
]
, since
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R})\subset\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\rho)\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho)
for
\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R})
.
We refer to Theorems
5.1
and
5.3
for the specifics of Result
3
.

Result 4

(Clustering)
.

We can deduce the clustering behavior from the Lagrangian solution.
In particular, clusters are identified from monotonicity properties of the associated natural velocity.

In analogy with the natural velocities
\{\bar{\psi}_{i}\}_{i}
for the Cucker–Smale-type system (
1.7
), there is also a natural velocity
\bar{\Psi}\in L^{2}(0,1)
for the Lagrangian solution.
For instance, it turns out that in the same way that particles will collide whenever
\bar{\psi}_{i}
are not in increasing order, mass will cluster in the Lagrangian solution if
\bar{\Psi}\notin\mathscr{K}
.
To this end, denote by
A
the primitive of
\bar{\Psi}
, this is in fact how the flux function in (
1.12
) is defined, and let
A^{**}
be its lower convex envelope on
[0,1]
.
By a result in
[
24
]
, the right-hand derivative of
A^{**}
is the projection of
\bar{\Psi}
on
\mathscr{K}
.

The image of the projection partitions the domain
(0,1)
into sets of strictly increasing averaged natural velocity, and these sets will never cluster with one another.
In order to study the formation of clusters, we further subdivide these sets by comparing
A
with
A^{**}
.
We can show that segments of
(0,1)
where
A
deviates from
A^{**}
correspond to mass which clusters in finite time; on these segments
\bar{\Psi}
necessarily deviates from its projection on
\mathscr{K}
.
On the other hand, mass will never cluster on segments where
A
coincides with
A^{**}
and
\bar{\Psi}
is strictly increasing.
Finally, a segment where
A
coincides with
A^{**}
and both are linear is also a cluster, but whether the mass clusters in finite or infinite time depends on the regularity of the communication protocol
\phi
.
These three cases are respectively coined
supercritical
,
subcritical
and
critical
in
[
22
]
, and now we have seen the bridge between their study of the lower convex envelope
A^{**}
and the convex cone
\mathscr{K}
containing the Lagrangian solutions.
Theorem
6.2
provides the details of Result
4
.
On a final, related note, we can deduce whether different subsets or clusters flock, i.e., remain close, by considering the communication strength of
\phi
over large distances, i.e., its tail (
1.3
).
For a thin tail, whether two subsets drift apart or not is the result of a competition between the ‘size of the tail’
\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}}
and the difference in natural velocity for the subsets.
If instead the tail is fat, there is always an upper bound on the distance between two subsets.

We emphasize that the techniques we use are inherently one-dimensional in nature, and so these ideas are not directly applicable in higher dimensions.

2.
Motivation in particle case

We will follow
[
6
]
in first motivating the
L^{2}
-gradient flow using the particle system.

2.1.
Deriving the particle dynamics

We denote
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
denotes the elements of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with strictly positive entries.
Let us introduce the auxiliary functions

(2.1)

\psi_{i}(t)\coloneqq v_{i}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi\left(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)\right),\qquad\bar{\psi}_{i}\coloneqq\bar{v}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi\left(\bar{x}_{i}-\bar{x}_{j}\right),\quad i\in\{1,\dots,N\},

which we collect in vectors
\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})
, where we have introduced
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
as a shorthand notation for the convolution-like quantities in (
2.1
).
Then the particle dynamics (
1.4
) can be rephrased as the following system of differential equations,

(2.2)

\dot{x}_{i}=\bar{\psi}_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(x_{i}-x_{j}),\quad x_{i}(0)=\bar{x}_{i},\quad i\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

We do not want trajectories of this system to cross, and so we require the particles to retain their initial ordering.
To this end we introduce the closed, convex cone

(2.3)

\mathbb{K}^{N}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{N}\right\},

and assume our initial data belongs to this cone, that is,
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in \mathbb{K}^{N}
.
Note that for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
we can define the
\boldsymbol{m}
-weighted Euclidean
p
-norm

(2.4)

\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m},p}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\lvert x_{i}\rvert^{p}\right)^{1/p},\qquad\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m},\infty}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\infty}.

For
p=2
we can define an associated
\boldsymbol{m}
-weighted inner product

\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{m}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}y_{i}x_{i},

and for convenience we write
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}\coloneqq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m},2}
.

2.1.1.
Short-time existence and uniqueness of solutions

We note that the right-hand side of (
2.2
) is a continuous function in
x_{i}
, and so existence of solutions
\boldsymbol{x}
in
C^{1}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{N})
is guaranteed.
On the other hand, we cannot expect Lipschitz-continuity of the right-hand side, e.g., if
\phi
is weakly singular in the sense of (
1.2
).
However, owing to the monotonicity of
\Phi^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
we can obtain uniqueness anyway, at least until particles meet, i.e., when
\boldsymbol{x}
hits the boundary
\partial\mathbb{K}^{N}
given by

(2.5)

\partial\mathbb{K}^{N}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}\colon\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq\emptyset\},\qquad\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\coloneqq\{j\colon x_{j}=x_{j+1},j\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}\}.

Let us specify what is meant by monotonicity here. For
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we compute

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y},\Phi^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\Phi^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{y})\rangle_{\boldsymbol{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\left[\Phi(x_{i}-x_{j})-\Phi(y_{i}-y_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{i}m_{j}[(x_{i}-x_{j})-(y_{i}-y_{j})]\left[\Phi(x_{i}-x_{j})-\Phi(y_{i}-y_{j})\right]\geq 0,

where the second equality follows from
\Phi
being odd, and the final inequality from
\Phi
being nondecreasing.
Hence, this monotonicity yields a one-sided Lipschitz condition, and we can show a stability estimate.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t)
be a solution of (
2.2
) with initial data
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(0),\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(0))=(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})
so that one has
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}=\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}_{i})
.
Then we have

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{2}=\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{m}}\leq\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{2}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{m}}\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{2}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}},

which implies

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}\leq\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{2}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}},

and in turn

\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}(t)\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}(s)-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}(s)\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}+(t-s)\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{2}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m}}.

Suppose we start with distinct initial particles, i.e.,
\bar{x}_{1}<\bar{x}_{2}<\dots<\bar{x}_{N}
, meaning
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbb{K}^{N})
, the interior of
\mathbb{K}^{N}
.
Then we have a unique solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathbb{K}^{N}
at least for a short time, until
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\partial\mathbb{K}^{N}
.
To ensure that the solution remains in
\mathbb{K}^{N}
after particles meet, we will in the following provide a well-defined procedure for resolving the dynamics in this case.

2.1.2.
Collision dynamics and the differential inclusion

For
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}
, the
tangent cone
to
\mathbb{K}^{N}
at
\boldsymbol{x}
, cf.
[
2
, Definition 6.38]
, is defined as the following closure,

(2.6)

T_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}\coloneqq\mathrm{cl}\{\vartheta(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x})\colon\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{K}^{N},\vartheta\geq 0\}.

This is the cone in which the velocity of
\boldsymbol{x}
should belong for
\boldsymbol{x}
to remain within
\mathbb{K}^{N}
, and in our setting (
2.6
) is equivalent to

T_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon v_{j}\leq v_{j+1}\ \text{for all}\ j\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}\},

where we recall
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
from (
2.5
).
In particular, we see that if
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\emptyset
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbb{K}^{N})
, then
T_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Assume a subset of particles
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\subset\{1,\dots,N\}
collides at time
t
, where we define

\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\coloneqq\left\{j\colon x_{j}(t)=x_{i}(t)\right\},\qquad i_{*}(t)=\min\mathcal{J}_{i}(t),\qquad i^{*}(t)\coloneqq\max\mathcal{J}_{i}(t).

Furthermore we assume the completely inelastic collision rule

(2.7)

\boldsymbol{v}(t+)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}}\boldsymbol{v}(t-)

leading to

v_{i}(t+)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}v_{j}(t-)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}},

which is natural in the sense that it conserves momentum.
Now, as noted in
[
21
]
, by continuity of the trajectory
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
and the continuity of
\Phi
it follows that
\psi_{j}(t-)-v_{j}(t-)=\psi_{i}(t-)-v_{i}(t-)
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)
.
This in turn leads to

(2.8)

\psi_{j}(t+)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}\psi_{j}(t-)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}\bar{\psi}_{j}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}}

where the final identity comes from
\psi_{i}(t)
being constant between collisions by virtue of (
2.2
).
For convenience we take the velocities
v_{i}(t)
, and then consequently also
\psi_{i}(t)
, to be right-continuous functions of
t
.
Afterwards and until the next collision, the clustered particle will only be affected by particles not belonging to the cluster.
Indeed, for
k\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)
we have

\dot{v}_{k}(t)=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
x_{j}(t)\notin\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)\end{subarray}}^{N}m_{j}\phi(x_{k}(t)-x_{j}(t))(v_{k}(t)-v_{j}(t))

or, since
\Phi(0)=0
,

v_{k}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}\Phi(x_{k}(t)-x_{j}(t))=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}\bar{\psi}_{j}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}}.

Moreover, amassed particles will not break apart again, since every particle in labeled by
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)
moves with the same velocity.
As pointed out in
[
6
]
, this sticky evolution can equivalently be defined by relabeling the amassed particles as a single new particle with mass given by the sum of the previous masses.
At every collision, the number of particles in the system would then decrease from the initial
N
.
Recalling (
2.8
), the collision dynamics can therefore be seen as a projection of the initial vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
of natural velocities onto the tangent cone of
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
.
That is,

(2.9)

\boldsymbol{\psi}(t+)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}}\boldsymbol{\psi}(t-)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}.

From this we see how the collision, or clustering, dynamics only depends on
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
, as expected from the particle dynamics in
[
22
]
.
From the above arguments, we have the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\mathrm{int(\mathbb{K}^{N})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}}
and define
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as in (
2.1
).
Then, for
t\geq 0
there is a uniquely defined, globally sticky solution
(\boldsymbol{x}(t),\boldsymbol{v}(t))\in\mathbb{K}^{N}\times T_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}
of (
2.2
) satisfying
(\boldsymbol{x}(0),\boldsymbol{v}(0))=(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}})
.

Following
[
6
, Section 1.2]
, we then argue that the instantaneous force that changes the velocity on impact at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\mathbb{K}^{N}
should belong to the
normal cone
1
1

1

A cone very similar to
\mathbb{K}^{N}
, and its polar cone, is treated in
[
2
, Exercise 6.16]
.

N_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}
, defined as

(2.10)

N_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon\langle\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{m}}\leq 0\ \text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}\right\}.

Then we can incorporate the collision dynamics in (
1.4
) by rephrasing it as the second-order differential inclusion

(2.11)

\dot{x}_{i}=v_{i},\qquad\dot{v}_{i}+N_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}\ni-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
x_{j}\neq x_{i}\end{subarray}}^{N}m_{j}\phi(x_{i}-x_{j})(v_{i}-v_{j}).

or equivalently, using (
2.1
),

(2.12)

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{v},\qquad\dot{\boldsymbol{\psi}}+N_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}\ni\boldsymbol{0}.

As observed in
[
24
]
, if
\boldsymbol{x}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{K}^{N}
satisfies a global stickiness condition, there is a monotonicity property for the normal cones, namely

N_{\boldsymbol{x}(s)}\mathbb{K}^{N}\subset N_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}\quad\text{for all }s<t.

Then, for
\boldsymbol{\xi}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\in N_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}
, e.g.,
-\dot{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)
in (
2.12
), we obtain

\int_{s}^{t}\boldsymbol{\xi}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\in N_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}\quad\text{for all }s<t.

Combining the above with a formal integration of the second equation of (
2.12
) then yields

\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)+N_{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\mathbb{K}^{N}\ni\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}},

which we in turn rephrase as the first-order differential inclusion

(2.13)

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{x})+N_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathbb{K}^{N}\ni\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}.

This relation lays the foundation for our study of the problem in the continuum case.

2.1.3.
The barycentric lemma

Consider again a subset of particles
\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)
colliding at time
t
; their velocities must necessarily satisfy

v_{i_{*}(t)}(t-)\geq v_{i_{*}(t)+1}(t-)\geq\dots\geq v_{i^{*}(t)-1}(t-)\geq v_{i^{*}(t)}(t-),

or else they would not have collided in the first place.
However, by the continuity of the trajectories
x_{i}
and the continuity of
\Phi
we also have

\psi_{i_{*}(t)}(t-)\geq\psi_{i_{*}(t)+1}(t-)\geq\dots\geq\psi_{i^{*}(t)-1}(t-)\geq\psi_{i^{*}(t)}(t-).

Combining the above chain of inequalities with the collision dynamics (
2.7
), we obtain

(2.14)

\frac{\sum_{j=k}^{i^{*}(t)}m_{j}\psi_{j}(t-)}{\sum_{j=k}^{i^{*}(t)}m_{j}}\leq\psi_{i}(t+)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}\psi_{j}(t-)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}}\leq\frac{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t)}^{k}m_{j}\psi_{j}(t-)}{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t)}^{k}m_{j}},

and, relying once more on
\psi_{i}(t)
being constant between collisions, we can write this as

(2.15)

\frac{\sum_{j=k}^{i^{*}(t)}m_{j}\bar{\psi}_{j}}{\sum_{j=k}^{i^{*}(t)}m_{j}}\leq\psi_{i}(t+)=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}\bar{\psi}_{j}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{i}(t)}m_{j}}\leq\frac{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t)}^{k}m_{j}\bar{\psi}_{j}}{\sum_{j=i_{*}(t)}^{k}m_{j}}.

This is the
barycentric lemma
used in
[
7
]
for the case
\phi\equiv 0
, and which was shown in
[
21
]
to still hold in our current case.
It turns out that the barycentric lemma is a particular, in fact, particle case of the Oleĭnik E condition
[
26
]
for the flux function
A
from the balance law (
1.12
).
We return to this matter in Section
5
.

m_{i}

\bar{\psi}_{i}

\bar{\psi}_{i}
of the
i

particle is the slope of the

A
with breakpoints at
\theta_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}m_{j}
.
If particles 3, 4 and 5 were to collide, the weighted average of their natural velocities is the slope of the dash-dotted line segment, which is the lower convex envelope of the interpolating function on the interval
[\theta_{2},\theta_{5}]
. Observe that (
2.15
) holds for this case.

3.
Auxiliary results

In order to define Lagrangian, or
L^{2}
-gradient flow, solutions for the Euler-alignment system, we will need some auxiliary results which play a central role in
[
24
,
6
]
.

3.1.
Some results from optimal transport

Consider a probability measure
\rho\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R})
, for which we define its right-continuous cumulative distribution function

(3.1)

M_{\rho}(x)=\rho((-\infty,x]),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Then
\partial_{x}M_{\rho}=\rho
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e., in the distributional sense.
We can then define its right-continuous monotone rearrangement, or generalized inverse, through

(3.2)

X_{\rho}\coloneqq\inf\left\{x\colon M_{\rho}(x)>m\right\},\quad m\in\Omega,

where
\Omega\coloneqq(0,1)
.
We denote the restriction of the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{1}
to
\Omega
by
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}^{1}|_{\Omega}
, such that we have the push-forward relations

(3.3)

X_{\rho\#}\mathfrak{m}=\rho,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(x)=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(X_{\rho}(m))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m

for any Borel function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
.

The
p
-Wasserstein distance, or Kantorovich–Rubinstein metric, between two measures is defined as

(3.4)

W_{p}^{p}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\coloneqq\min\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varrho(x,y)\colon\varrho\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}),\varpi^{i}_{\#}\varrho=\rho_{i}\right\},

where
\varpi^{i}
is the projection on the
i
th
coordinate, i.e.,
\varpi^{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{i}
.
In one dimension, the unique optimal coupling of measures can be explicitly found using the monotone rearrangement. Indeed, by the Hoeffding–Frechét theorem
[
35
, Theorem 2.18]
the optimal coupling is given by

(3.5)

\varrho=X_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\#}\mathfrak{m},\qquad X_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}=(X_{\rho_{1}},X_{\rho_{2}}).

A direct consequence of this is the identity

W_{p}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert X_{\rho_{1}}-X_{\rho_{2}}\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)^{1/p}=\lVert X_{\rho_{1}}-X_{\rho_{2}}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

That is, the
p
-Wasserstein distance of two probability measures equals the
L^{p}
-distance of their monotone rearrangements.
Let us then for
p\in[1,\infty)
introduce the space

(3.6)

\mathscr{T}_{p}\coloneqq\left\{(\rho,v)\colon\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}),v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R},\rho)\right\},

for which we can define a semidistance
U_{p}
as follows,

(3.7)

U_{p}^{p}((\rho_{1},v_{1}),(\rho_{2},v_{2}))\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}\lvert v_{1}(x)-v_{2}(y)\rvert^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\varrho(x,y)=\lVert v_{1}\circ X_{1}-v_{2}\circ X_{2}\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p},

where the final identity again follows from (
3.5
).
In turn, we can define a metric
D_{p}
through

(3.8)

D_{p}^{p}((\rho_{1},v_{1}),(\rho_{2},v_{2}))\coloneqq W_{p}^{p}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})+U_{p}^{p}((\rho_{1},v_{1}),(\rho_{2},v_{2})),

so that
(\mathscr{T}_{p},D_{p})
is a metric, but not complete, space, see
[
24
, Proposition 2.1]
.

3.2.
Some results from convex analysis

Let
f\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an extended real-valued function for some set
\mathscr{X}
.
Its
effective domain
, or just
domain
,
\dom(f)
is the set of points where
f
is finite, i.e.,

\dom(f)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathscr{X}\colon f(x)<+\infty\}.

If
\dom(f)\neq\emptyset
, then
f
is called
proper
.
Throughout the paper we will consider the real-valued Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega)
, where we write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for its associated inner product and, sometimes for brevity,
\lVert\cdot\rVert=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for its norm.

The metric projection onto a nonempty, closed, convex subset
\mathscr{C}
of a Hilbert space is a well-defined Lipschitz map
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{C}}\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathscr{C}
, and for all
X\in L^{2}(\Omega)
it is characterized by

(3.9)

Y=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{C}}X\iff Y\in\mathscr{C},\quad\langle X-Y,Z-Y\rangle\leq 0\ \text{for all}\ Z\in\mathscr{C},

see, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 3.16]
or
[
29
, Proposition 1.37]
.
Denoting by
\mathscr{C}^{\circ}
the element of minimal norm in a closed and convex set
\mathscr{C}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
, (
3.9
) is equivalent to

\lVert X-Y\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\min_{Z\in\mathscr{C}}\lVert X-Z\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\iff Y=\left(X-\mathscr{C}\right)^{\circ}.

Let us now consider what can be seen as a generalization of
\mathbb{K}^{N}
from (
2.3
), namely

(3.10)

\mathscr{K}\coloneqq\{X\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon X\text{ is nondecreasing and right-continuous}\},

which is a closed, convex cone in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
The indicator function
I_{\mathscr{K}}
of
\mathscr{K}
is then given by

(3.11)

I_{\mathscr{K}}(X)=\begin{cases}0,&X\in\mathscr{K},\\
+\infty,&X\notin\mathscr{K}.\end{cases}

Since the set
\mathscr{K}
is convex,
I_{\mathscr{K}}
is a convex functional.

For a given proper and convex functional
\mathcal{F}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
, its
subdifferential
at
X\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is defined as the set

\partial\mathcal{F}(X)=\left\{Y\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon\mathcal{F}(Z)-\mathcal{F}(X)\geq\langle Y,Z-X\rangle\ \forall\,Z\in L^{2}(\Omega)\right\}

For
X\in\mathscr{K}
, the subdifferential
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X)
coincides with the normal cone
N_{X}\mathscr{K}
of
\mathscr{K}
at
X
, which in analogy with the particle case (
2.10
) can be defined as

(3.12)

N_{X}\mathscr{K}\coloneqq\left\{W\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon\langle W,Y-X\rangle\leq 0\ \forall\,Y\in\mathscr{K}\right\}.

Comparing (
3.9
) and (
3.12
) we find the following equivalence.
For any
X,Y\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we have

(3.13)

Y=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}X\iff X-Y\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(Y).

The tangent cone
T_{X}\mathscr{K}
of
\mathscr{K}
at
X\in\mathscr{K}
can then be defined similarly to the particle case (
2.6
) as

(3.14)

T_{X}\mathscr{K}\coloneqq\mathrm{cl}\left\{\vartheta(Y-X)\colon Y\in\mathscr{K},\vartheta\geq 0\right\}.

Alternatively, it can be characterized as
the as the polar cone of the normal cone, that is,

(3.15)

T_{X}\mathscr{K}=\left\{U\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon\langle U,W\rangle\leq 0\ \forall\,W\in N_{X}\mathscr{K}\right\}.

It is convenient to introduce the following set, generalizing
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{x}}
in (
2.5
),

(3.16)

\Omega_{X}\coloneqq\{m\in\Omega\colon X\text{ is constant in a neighborhood of }m\},

which can be thought of as the set of “concentrated mass” for
\rho\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R})
corresponding to
X_{\rho}=X
.
Note that this set can be written as a countable union of disjoint intervals,
\Omega_{X}=\sqcup_{i}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
.
Then we may equivalently characterize the tangent cone as follows, cf.
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
,

(3.17)

T_{X}\mathscr{K}=\left\{U\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon U\text{ is nondecreasing on each maximal interval }(\alpha,\beta)\subset\Omega_{X}\right\}.

The following characterization of
N_{X}\mathscr{K}
can be found in
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
and builds upon
[
24
, Theorem 3.9]
.

Lemma 3.1

(Characterization of the normal cone
N_{X}\mathscr{K}
)
.

Let
X\in\mathscr{K}
and
W\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be given, and write

(3.18)

\Xi_{W}(m)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{m}W(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\quad\text{for all }m\in[0,1].

Then
W\in N_{X}\mathscr{K}
if and only if
\Xi_{W}\in\mathscr{N}_{X}
, where
\mathscr{N}_{X}
is the convex cone defined as

\mathscr{N}_{X}\coloneqq\left\{\Xi\in C([0,1])\colon\Xi\geq 0\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{in}\kern 5.0pt[0,1]\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{and}\kern 5.0pt\Xi=0\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{in}\kern 5.0pt[0,1]\setminus\Omega_{X}\right\}.

In particular, for every
X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathscr{K}
we have

(3.19)

\Omega_{X_{1}}\subset\Omega_{X_{2}}\implies N_{X_{1}}\mathscr{K}\subset N_{X_{2}}\mathscr{K}.

An example of a function belonging to the convex cone
\mathscr{N}_{X}
is illustrated in Figure
3
.

Figure 3
.

\Xi_{W}\in\mathscr{N}_{X}
where
\Omega_{X}=(\omega_{1}^{-},\omega_{1}^{+})\sqcup(\omega_{2}^{-},\omega_{2}^{+})
.

Based on (
3.16
) we introduce the closed subspace
\mathscr{H}_{X}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
given by

(3.20)

\mathscr{H}_{X}\coloneqq\left\{U\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon U\text{ is constant on each interval }(m_{l},m_{r})\subset\Omega_{X}\right\},

for which we have the implications

(3.21)

\Omega_{X_{1}}\subset\Omega_{X_{2}}\implies\mathscr{H}_{X_{2}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{X_{1}},\qquad X_{1},X_{2}\in\mathscr{K}

and

U\in\mathscr{H}_{X}\iff\pm U\in T_{X}\mathscr{K}.

We can define the projection of
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
onto the subspace (
3.20
) as

(3.22)

\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X}}f=\begin{cases}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}f(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\coloneqq\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}f(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega,&m\in(\alpha,\beta)\text{ a maximal interval of }\Omega_{X},\\
f,&\text{for a.e. }m\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{X}.\end{cases}

From
[
6
, Lemma 2.6]
we have

(3.23)

X\in\mathscr{K},\quad U\in T_{X}\mathscr{K}\implies\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X}}U-U\in N_{X}\mathscr{K}

The characterization (
3.17
) of the tangent cone
T_{X}\mathscr{K}
leads us to consider the closed, convex cone defined by

(3.24)

\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}\coloneqq\left\{X\in L^{2}((\alpha,\beta))\colon X\text{ is nondecreasing and right-continuous}\right\}

for
(\alpha,\beta)\subset(0,1)
, such that
\mathscr{K}_{(0,1)}=\mathscr{K}
.
In
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
, a characterization of the projection of
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
onto
\mathscr{K}
is given in terms of the right-derivative of its primitive’s lower convex envelope on
\Omega=(0,1)
.
We recall that the lower convex envelope
F^{**}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
of a function
F\in C([\alpha,\beta],\mathbb{R})
is defined as

(3.25)

F^{**}_{(\alpha,\beta)}(m)=\sup\left\{a+bm\colon a,b\in\mathbb{R},\ a+b\omega\leq F(\omega)\ \forall\>\omega\in[\alpha,\beta]\right\},\quad m\in[\alpha,\beta].

This is the greatest bounded, (lower semi-)continuous and convex function
G
satisfying
G\leq F
in
[\alpha,\beta]
; hence it is left- and right-differentiable for every
m\in(\alpha,\beta)
, and its right-derivative
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m}F^{**}
is nondecreasing and right-continuous.
We note that the notation involving
**
alludes to the fact that the lower convex envelope in this case coincides with the biconjugate, or twice the Legendre–Fenchel transform, of
F
.
However, there is nothing in the proof of
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
which relies on the domain being
(0,1)
; hence, with the appropriate changes we have the following result.

Proposition 3.2

(Projection on
\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}((\alpha,\beta))
for
(\alpha,\beta)\subset(0,1)
and let
F(m)=\int_{\alpha}^{m}f(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega
.
Then

(3.26)

\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}f=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m}F^{**}_{(\alpha,\beta)},

where
F^{**}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
is the lower convex envelope in (
3.25
).
Moreover, for any convex, lower semi-continuous function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
and
f,g\in L^{2}((\alpha,\beta))
we have

\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\varphi\left(\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}f-\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}g\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m\leq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\varphi(f-g)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m.

In particular,
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}
is a contraction in every space
L^{p}((\alpha,\beta))
,
p\in[1,\infty]
.

A useful auxiliary result for proving the above result is
[
24
, Lemma 3.2]
, which below is appropriately modified to match our setting.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
f\in L^{2}((\alpha,\beta))
and
F\in C([\alpha,\beta],\mathbb{R})
as defined in Proposition
3.2
,
F^{**}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
is continuous on
[\alpha,\beta]
, locally Lipschitz on
(\alpha,\beta)
, and coincides with
F
for
m=\alpha,\beta
. If moreover
f\in L^{\infty}((\alpha,\beta))
, then
F
and
F^{**}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
are Lipschitz-continuous on
[\alpha,\beta]
.

As a consequence of the above results, for any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we can consider its restriction to
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\Omega
, namely
f|_{(\alpha,\beta)}\in L^{2}((\alpha,\beta))
, and slightly abusing notation we will still denote this by
f
.
Hence, when we write
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}f\in L^{2}((\alpha,\beta))
, we mean the projection of
f|_{(\alpha,\beta)}
on
\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}
from (
3.24
).
Then, if we identify
\mathscr{K}=\mathscr{K}_{(0,1)}
and
F^{**}=F^{**}_{(0,1)}
in the above, we recover the original results.

Now, combining the characterization (
3.17
), (
3.24
) and Proposition
3.2
, we see that for
X\in\mathscr{K}
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the projection of
f
on
T_{X}\mathscr{K}
can be written as

(3.27)

\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X}\mathscr{K}}f=\begin{cases}\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}f,&m\in(\alpha,\beta)\text{ a maximal interval of }\Omega_{X},\\
f,&\text{for a.e. }m\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{X}.\end{cases}

Let us write
F(m)=\int_{0}^{m}f(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega
,
F_{(\alpha,\beta)}=F(\alpha)+\int_{\alpha}^{m}f(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega
and let
F_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{**}
be the lower convex envelope of
F_{(\alpha,\beta)}
, as in (
3.25
), such that
F_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{**}\leq F_{(\alpha,\beta)}
.
Then it follows from (
3.26
) and Lemma
3.3
that

F_{(\alpha,\beta)}(\beta)-F_{(\alpha,\beta)}(\alpha)=F_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{**}(\beta)-F_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{**}(\alpha)=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}_{(\alpha,\beta)}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m.

Consequently, since
\Omega_{X}=\sqcup_{i}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
, it follows from (
3.27
) that

F(m)\geq\int_{0}^{m}\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X}\mathscr{K}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega=\begin{cases}F_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{**}(m),&m\in(\alpha,\beta)\text{ a maximal interval of }\Omega_{X},\\
F(m),&m\in[0,1]\setminus\Omega_{X}.\end{cases}

In particular, recalling (
3.22
), we find

\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m=\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X}\mathscr{K}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m=\int_{\Omega}\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X}}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m=\int_{\Omega}f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m.

That is, although the projections onto the convex cones
\mathscr{K}
,
T_{X}\mathscr{K}
and
\mathscr{H}_{X}
are contractions in the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm, they do not change the average of the function being projected.
Such considerations will turn out useful when studying clustering properties in Section
6
.

.

4.
Lagrangian solutions of the Euler-alignment system

We recall the assumption
\bar{\rho}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, for which we use equations (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) to define the following monotone rearrangement
\bar{X}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfying
\bar{X}_{\#}\mathfrak{m}=\bar{\rho}
.
Further recalling
\bar{v}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
as well as
\psi_{t}
defined in (
1.10
), we introduce the quantities
\bar{V}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\bar{\Psi}\coloneqq\Psi[\bar{X},\bar{V}]
defined through

(4.1)

\Psi[X,V]\coloneqq V+\int_{\Omega}\Phi(X-X(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega=V+\Phi\ast\rho(X),\ \text{where}\ X_{\#}\mathfrak{m}=\rho.

Then it also follows, recall (
1.6
), that
\bar{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Here
\bar{X}
and
\bar{V}
respectively play the roles of initial position and velocity, in analogy with
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}
from Section
2
.
Keeping in mind our original problem (
1.1
) and its initial data
\bar{\rho}
and
\bar{v}
, a natural choice for
\bar{V}
is
\bar{v}\circ\bar{X}
; we return to this point in Remark
4.8
.
Likewise, the quantity
\bar{\Psi}
, analogous to
\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
from Section
2
, represents the natural velocity.
Mimicking the arguments in the particle case, we arrive at a first-order differential inclusion corresponding to (
2.13
),

(4.2)

\dot{X}_{t}+\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\ni\bar{\Psi}-\Phi\ast\rho_{t}(X_{t}).

We will always use the isometry between
\mathscr{K}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
to have
X_{t\#}\mathfrak{m}=\rho_{t}
.
The relation (
4.2
) provides us with an associated “prescribed” velocity

(4.3)

U[X_{t}]\coloneqq\bar{\Psi}-\int_{\Omega}\Phi(X_{t}-X_{t}(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\in L^{2}(\Omega),

such that
U[\bar{X}]=\bar{V}
.
Observe that
U[X_{t}]
is the velocity
X_{t}\in\mathscr{K}
would have if
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})=\{0\}
, i.e.,
T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}=L^{2}(\Omega)
; that is, in absence of mass concentration.

The next result ensures that strong convergence of
X
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
yields strong convergence of
\Phi\ast\rho(X)
.

Lemma 4.1

(Uniform continuity of
\Phi\ast\rho
)
.

Assume
X_{i}\in\mathscr{K}
and
\rho_{i}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\rho_{i}=X_{i\#}\mathfrak{m}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{\Phi}
, depending only on
\Phi
, such that

\displaystyle\lVert(\Phi\ast\rho_{1})\circ X_{1}-(\Phi\ast\rho_{2})\circ X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{\Phi}(\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}),

or equivalently, using the semidistance
U_{2}
(
3.7
) and the Kantorovich–Rubinstein metric
W_{2}
(
3.4
),

U_{2}((\rho_{1},\Phi\ast\rho_{1}),(\rho_{2},\Phi\ast\rho_{2}))\leq\omega_{\Phi}(W_{2}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})).

Proof.

We will make use of the following estimate; let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, then for
\rho\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R})
we find

(4.4)

\displaystyle\lvert(\Phi\ast\rho)(x_{2})-(\Phi\ast\rho)(x_{1})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert\int^{x_{2}}_{x_{1}}\phi(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert\int^{x_{2}}_{x_{1}}\phi\left(x-\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(y)=2\Phi\left(\frac{\lvert x_{2}-x_{1}\rvert}{2}\right).

Here the second inequality follows from
\phi
being symmetric and radially decreasing; indeed, the quantity
\lvert\int^{x_{2}}_{x_{1}}\phi(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\rvert
attains its maximal value at the midpoint
y=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}+x_{2})
.
Now we estimate

\lVert(\Phi\ast\rho_{1})\circ X_{1}-(\Phi\ast\rho_{2})\circ X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2(I_{1}+I_{2})

where

I_{1}\coloneqq\lVert(\Phi\ast\rho_{1})\circ X_{2}-(\Phi\ast\rho_{2})\circ X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\qquad I_{2}\coloneqq\lVert(\Phi\ast\rho_{1})\circ X_{1}-(\Phi\ast\rho_{1})\circ X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\qquad.

Then, from (
4.4
), the concavity of
\Phi
and Jensen’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\Phi(X_{2}(m)-X_{1}(\omega))-\Phi(X_{2}(m)-X_{2}(\omega))\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(2\int_{\Omega}\Phi\left(\frac{\lvert X_{1}(\omega)-X_{2}(\omega)\rvert}{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\right)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m\leq 4\Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right)^{2}.

On the other hand, defining
\Omega_{-}\coloneqq\{m\in\Omega\colon\lvert X_{1}-X_{2}\rvert\leq 2\}
and
\Omega_{+}\coloneqq\Omega\setminus\Omega_{-}
, the same inequalities together with (
1.6
) yield

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\int_{\Omega}\Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\lvert X_{1}(m)-X_{2}(m)\rvert\right)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m

\displaystyle\leq 4\Phi(1)\int_{\Omega_{-}}\Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\lvert X_{1}(m)-X_{2}(m)\rvert\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m+4\int_{\Omega_{+}}\left(\Phi(1)\frac{1}{2}\lvert X_{1}(m)-X_{2}(m)\rvert\right)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m

\displaystyle\leq 4\Phi(1)\Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right)+\left(\Phi(1)\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{2}.

Since
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)=1
, we have
\lVert X\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert X\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, and the result follows from collecting all the bounds,

\displaystyle\lVert(\Phi\ast\rho_{1})\circ X_{1}-(\Phi\ast\rho_{2})\circ X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 8\Phi\left(\max\left\{1,\frac{1}{2}\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right\}\right)\Phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\Phi(1)\lVert X_{1}-X_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{2}.

That we in the end obtain a modulus of continuity is then a consequence of
\Phi
being such a function, cf. Lemma
1.1
(b).
∎

4.1.
The associated functional and gradient flow structure

Next we will see how (
4.2
) can be regarded as a gradient flow for a certain convex and lower semi-continuous functional.
Based on the gradient flow structure in the particle case, cf. (
1.9
), we are led to consider the following functional

(4.5)

\mathcal{V}(X)=\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}(X)-\langle\bar{\Psi},X\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}W_{\Phi}(X(m)-X(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m-\int_{\Omega}\bar{\Psi}(m)X(m)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m,

where
W_{\Phi}
is the primitive of the odd function
\Phi
from (
1.5
) satisfying
W_{\Phi}(0)=0
, i.e., the even function

(4.6)

W_{\Phi}(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{x}\Phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

illustrated in Figure
1
.
This function is also convex on the whole of
\mathbb{R}
due to
\Phi(x)
being nondecreasing, and its second derivative is the nonnegative communication protocol
\phi
.
We observe that
\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}
is a proper functional for
X\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, as it can be bounded with the pointwise linear bound (
1.6
).
The second term of
\mathcal{V}
is linear in
X
, and hence bounded by Cauchy–Schwarz and
\bar{\Psi}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
We conclude that
\mathcal{V}
is a proper functional with
\dom(\mathcal{V})=L^{2}(\Omega)
.
One can check that the Gâteaux derivative
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}(X)
of
\mathcal{V}
is exactly the negative of the right-hand side in (
4.2
), that is,
-U[X]
from (
4.3
), owing to the oddness of
\Phi
.
That is,

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}(X)=\Phi\ast\rho(X),\qquad\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}(X)=\Phi\ast\rho(X)-\bar{\Psi}=-U[X].

We would like to show that
\mathcal{V}(X)
is convex in
X\in\mathscr{K}
.
To this end, for
Y_{0},Y_{1}\in\mathscr{K}
and
\vartheta\in(0,1)
we introduce the convex combination
Y_{\vartheta}\coloneqq(1-\vartheta)Y_{0}+\vartheta Y_{1}
.
Our goal is then to show

\mathcal{V}(Y_{\vartheta})\leq(1-\vartheta)\mathcal{V}(Y_{0})+\vartheta\mathcal{V}(Y_{1}).

The second term of (
4.5
) is already linear in
X
, and so we turn to the first term.
Observe next that we can write the argument of the inner integrand as

Y_{\vartheta}(m)-Y_{\vartheta}(\omega)=(1-\vartheta)[Y_{0}(m)-Y_{0}(\omega)]+\vartheta[Y_{1}(m)-Y_{1}(\omega)].

Then it follows directly from the convexity of
W_{\Phi}
in (
4.6
) that also the first term of (
4.5
) is convex in
X\in\mathscr{K}
, and we conclude that the entire functional
\mathcal{V}(X)
is convex in
X\in\mathscr{K}
.

Now, since
\mathcal{V}(X)
is convex for
X\in\mathscr{K}
and has Gâteaux derivative
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}(X)=-U[X]
, it follows from
[
29
, Proposition 3.20]
that its subdifferential then reduces to a single element.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
X\in\mathscr{K}
, the subdifferential
\partial\mathcal{V}(X)
of
\mathcal{V}
defined in (
4.5
) consists of a single element.
In particular, we have
\partial\mathcal{V}(X)=\{-U[X]\}
for
U[X]
defined in (
4.3
).

In order to ensure that our flow remains in the cone
\mathscr{K}
, we have to consider a modified version of the functional
\mathcal{V}
, namely

(4.7)

\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X)\coloneqq\mathcal{V}(X)+I_{\mathscr{K}}(X),

where we recall the indicator function
I_{\mathscr{K}}
from (
3.11
).
We would like to show that this is convex for
X\in L^{2}(\Omega)
; that is, considering again the convex combination
Y_{\vartheta}
from before, now with
Y_{0},Y_{1}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we want to show

(4.8)

\bar{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{\vartheta})\leq(1-\vartheta)\bar{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{0})+\vartheta\bar{\mathcal{V}}(Y_{1}).

Suppose either of
Y_{0}
,
Y_{1}
does not lie in the cone
\mathscr{K}
; then according to (
3.11
) and (
4.7
), the right-hand side of (
4.8
) is infinite, and the inequality is trivially satisfied.
It remains to consider the case when both
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}
belong to
\mathscr{K}
.
In this case the indicator function vanishes due to the convexity of
\mathscr{K}
, such that
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
reduces to
\mathcal{V}
, which is the case we treated before.
Moreover, since
\mathcal{V}
is continuous and
\mathscr{K}
is closed, the functional
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
is lower semi-continuous.
We have therefore proved the following result, which will be useful for establishing existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
4.2
).

Proposition 4.3
.

The functional
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
in (
4.7
) is convex and lower semi-continuous in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Like in the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 2.13]
, we will make use of the fact that the subdifferential
\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}=\partial(\mathcal{V}+I_{\mathscr{K}})
is additive in this case.
Indeed, since
\mathcal{V}
is proper, lower semi-continuous and convex, as well as bounded on the whole domain of
\dom(I_{\mathscr{K}})=\mathscr{K}
, this follows from the Moreau–Rockafellar theorem
[
29
, Theorem 3.30]
, see also the the proof of
[
29
, Proposition 3.61]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

For
X\in\mathscr{K}
, the subdifferential
\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X)
of
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
in (
4.7
) can be written as

\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X)=\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X)-U[X]=\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X)-\bar{\Psi}+\int_{\Omega}\Phi(X-X(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega.

Now we will provide the definition of an
L^{2}
-gradient flow solution associated with the functional (
4.7
), cf.
[
5
, Definition 2.11]
.
This will justify the differential inclusion (
4.2
) for the Euler-alignment system.

Definition 4.5

(
L^{2}
-gradient flow)
.

An absolutely continuous curve
X_{t}\colon[0,\infty)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is an
L^{2}
-gradient flow for the functional
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
in (
4.7
) if it satisfies the differential inclusion

(4.9)

-\dot{X}_{t}\in\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X_{t})=-U[X_{t}]+\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\quad\text{for a.e.}\ t>0.

Observe that (
4.9
) is exactly (
4.2
), which was derived heuristically from the particle case in Section
2
.

4.2.
Lagrangian solutions

Since
L^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space, we will, like the works
[
24
,
6
,
5
]
, rely on the theory of
[
8
]
to establish existence and uniqueness of a solution
X_{t}
evolving according to the minimal element of the subdifferential
\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X_{t})
.
This element is sometimes called the principal section, and we denote it by
\partial^{\circ}\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X_{t})
.
The subdifferential
\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}
is a maximally monotone operator, cf.
[
8
, Exemple 2.3.4]
, and we can apply
[
8
, Theoreme 3.1]
to deduce existence and uniqueness of such solutions to (
4.9
).
In fact, since
\bar{\mathcal{V}}
is proper, lower semi-continuous and convex we can directly apply
[
8
, Theoreme 3.2]
to obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.6

(Lagrangian solution)
.

Let
\bar{X}\in\mathscr{K}
and
\bar{V}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, such that
\bar{\Psi}=\Psi[\bar{X},\bar{V}]
given by (
4.1
). Then there exists a unique gradient flow solution
X_{t}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathscr{K}
in the sense of Definition
4.5
with initial data
\bar{X}
which evolves according to the minimal selection of
\partial\bar{\mathcal{V}}
, that is,

-\dot{X}_{t}\in\partial^{\circ}\bar{\mathcal{V}}(X_{t})\quad\text{for a.e.}\ t>0.

We will call this solution the Lagrangian solution associated with the Euler-alignment system.

From the same theory, the Lagrangian solution in Theorem
4.6
enjoys many useful properties, and following
[
6
, Theorem 3.5]
, we list some of them here.

Corollary 4.7

(Properties of the Lagrangian solution)
.

Let
X_{t}
be the Lagrangian solution from Theorem
4.6
, with corresponding prescribed velocity
U[X_{t}]
as defined in (
4.3
). Then it follows:

(a)

The right-derivative
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X_{t}\eqqcolon V_{t}
exists for all
t\geq 0
.

(b)

The velocity
V_{t}
is the minimal element of the subdifferential, that is,

(4.10)

V_{t}=\left(U[X_{t}]-\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\right)^{\circ}.

In particular, (
4.2
) holds true for all
t\geq 0
if we replace
\dot{X}_{t}
with
V_{t}
.

(c)

The velocity
V_{t}
is the projection of
U[X_{t}]
on the tangent cone
T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}
:

(4.11)

V_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}}U[X_{t}],\qquad\Psi_{t}\coloneqq V_{t}+\Phi\ast\rho_{t}(X_{t})=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}}\bar{\Psi}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(d)

Continuity of the velocity:
t\mapsto V_{t}
is right-continuous for all
t\geq 0
, in particular

(4.12)

\lim\limits_{t\to 0+}V_{t}=\bar{V}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\bar{V}\in T_{\bar{X}}\mathscr{K}.

Moreover, the map
t\mapsto\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is nonincreasing, and so there is an at most countable set of times
\mathcal{T}\subset(0,\infty)
where it is discontinuous.
Then
t\mapsto X_{t}
is continuously differentiable in
(0,\infty)\setminus\mathcal{T}
. Defining
\rho_{t}\coloneqq X_{t\#}\mathfrak{m}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a unique map
v_{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho_{t})
such that

(4.13)

\dot{X}_{t}=V_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}U[X_{t}]=v_{t}\circ X_{t}\in\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}\ \text{for every }t\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathcal{T}.

(e)

Dissipation identity:

(4.14)

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{V}(X_{t})=-\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\quad\text{for $t>0$, implying}\quad\mathcal{V}(X_{s})-\mathcal{V}(X_{t})=\int_{s}^{t}\lVert V_{r}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

(f)

Stability estimates: Let
(X^{i}_{t},V_{t}^{i})
be Lagrangian solutions of (
4.2
) with respective initial data
(\bar{X}^{i},\bar{V}^{i})
, such that
\bar{\Psi}^{i}=\Psi[\bar{X}^{i},\bar{V}^{i}]
. Then we have

(4.15)

\lVert X_{t}^{1}-X_{t}^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert X_{s}^{1}-X_{s}^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(t-s)\lVert\bar{\Psi}^{1}-\bar{\Psi}^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for
s\leq t
, as well as

(4.16)

\int_{0}^{T}\lVert V_{t}^{1}-V_{t}^{2}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}(\lVert\bar{X}^{i}\rVert+\lVert\bar{V}^{i}\rVert+\sqrt{T}\lVert\bar{\Psi}^{i}\rVert)\right)\left(\lVert\bar{X}^{1}-\bar{X}^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\sqrt{T}\lVert\bar{\Psi}^{1}-\bar{\Psi}^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

for some constant
C>0
independent of
T
and the initial data.

Proof.

Properties (a), (b) and the right-continuity of
V_{t}
are consequences of
[
8
, Theoreme 3.1]
, while (e) follows from
[
8
, Theoreme 3.2]
.

By property (b),
V_{t}
is the
L^{2}
-projection of the zero function onto the closed, convex subset
U[X_{t}]-\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
, and so by (
3.9
) it follows that
\langle V,U-V-\xi\rangle\geq 0
for all
\xi\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
.
Furthermore, since
U[X_{t}]-V_{t}
belongs to the cone
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
, it follows that both
0
and
2(U[X_{t}]-V_{t})
belong to
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
; hence by the previous inequality
U[X_{t}]-V_{t}\perp V_{t}
.
On the other hand, by property (a) and (
3.14
) it is clear that
V_{t}\in T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}
. Then it follows from the characterization (
3.9
) that
V_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}}U[X_{t}]
.
Since
\Phi\ast\rho_{t}(X_{t})\in\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}
, it is clear from (
3.17
) that
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}}(U[X_{t}])=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}}\bar{\Psi}-\Phi\ast\rho_{t}(X_{t})
.

The stability estimates (f) follow from instead considering (
4.9
) from the point of view of the proper, convex and lower semi-continuous functional
\bar{\mathcal{W}}_{\Phi}(X)=\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}(X)+I_{\mathscr{K}}(X)
, and that (
4.9
) is equivalent to
\dot{X}_{t}+\partial\bar{\mathcal{W}}_{\Phi}(X_{t})\ni\bar{\Psi}
.
Then (
4.15
) follows from
[
8
, Lemme 3.1]
.
On the other hand, (
4.16
) is a consequence of
[
31
, Theorem 2]
; in their notation, our setting translates to the Hilbert triple
V=H=V^{\prime}=L^{2}(\Omega)
and quantities
w(u_{0},f)=\lVert\bar{X}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert\bar{V}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
\lVert f\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;V^{\prime})}^{2}=T\lVert\bar{\Psi}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
∎

Remark 4.8

(The initial velocity)
.

Corollary
4.7
allows us to have
\bar{V}\in T_{\bar{X}}\mathscr{K}\setminus\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}}
, and still have the right-derivative
V_{t}\to\bar{V}
as
t\to 0+
;
this would then mean that the initial concentrated mass has different initial velocities.
However, if our aim is to study (
1.1
) with initial data
\bar{\rho}
and
\bar{v}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
, this does not make much sense, as the natural choice of
\bar{V}
would be
\bar{v}\circ\bar{X}\in\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}}
.

Remark 4.9

(Energy dissipation)
.

We will not directly make use of the energy dissipation relation (
4.14
) in this study, but merely point out that energy dissipation plays a role in a recent study of a kinetic Cucker–Smale model
[
27
, Section 3]
.

We can think of picking the minimal element in the set
U[X_{t}]-\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
, cf. (
4.10
), as finding the velocity
V_{t}
closest to the prescribed velocity
U[X_{t}]
which still allows
X_{t}
to remain in
\mathscr{K}
.
Note that in (
4.11
) we have, in analogy with the discrete quantity
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)
from Section
2
, introduced
\Psi_{t}
, which satisfies the corresponding continuum version of the discrete relation (
2.9
).
That is, it is the projection of the natural velocity
\bar{\Psi}
on the tangent cone
T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}
.

Observe that
\frac{1}{h}(X_{t-h}-X_{t})\in T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}
and is uniformly bounded for any for any
0<h<t
, hence it has a subsequence converging weakly to
-V^{\prime}\in T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}
by the definition (
3.15
) and the cone being weakly closed.
Then, by the characterization (
3.17
), any such weak limit must be nonincreasing on connected components of
\Omega_{X_{t}}
, which is analogous to the monotonicity relation leading to the barycentric lemma (
2.14
) in the particle case.

If the relation
V_{t}\in\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}
in (
4.13
) were to hold for all times, this is one of the hallmarks of the so-called sticky Lagrangian solutions, and we recall their definition
[
6
, Definition 3.7]
next.

Definition 4.10

(Sticky Lagrangian solutions)
.

A Lagrangian solution is called
sticky
if

(4.17)

\text{for any}\ s\leq t\ \text{we have}\ \Omega_{X_{s}}\subset\Omega_{X_{t}}.

Because of the implications (
3.19
) and (
3.21
),
any sticky Lagrangian solution satisfies the monotonicity condition

(4.18)

\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{s})\subset\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t}),\quad\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}\subset\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}\kern 5.0pt\text{for any }s\leq t.

Another property of sticky solutions which will prove useful below is the following,

(4.19)

\xi\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\implies\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}}\xi\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t}).

To prove this we will use the characterization from Lemma
3.1
.
Let
\Xi
be the corresponding primitive (
3.18
) of
\xi
, then we know that
\Xi(m)=0
for
m\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{X_{t}}
, and
\Xi(m)\geq 0
for
m\in\Omega_{X_{t}}
.
By (
4.18
),
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}}
will only modify
\xi
in
\Omega_{X_{s}}\subset\Omega_{X_{t}}
, so let us consider a maximal interval
(\alpha_{s},\beta_{s})\subset\Omega_{X_{s}}
which is necessarily contained in a maximal interval
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})\subset\Omega_{X_{t}}
.
On
(\alpha_{s},\beta_{s})
, the projection will replace
\xi
with its average value over this interval, which means that the corresponding primitive of
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}}\xi
on this interval will be
\Xi(\alpha_{s})+\frac{m-\alpha_{s}}{\beta_{s}-\alpha_{s}}(\Xi(\beta_{s})-\Xi(\alpha_{s}))\geq 0
.
An analogous expression holds on any other maximal interval of
\Omega_{X_{s}}
contained in
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
, while on the remaining part
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})\setminus\Omega_{X_{s}}
its value is
\Xi(m)\geq 0
.
Hence, the primitive of
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}}\xi
also belongs to
\mathscr{N}_{X_{t}}
, meaning (
4.19
) holds.

Sticky Lagrangian solutions satisfy additional properties, and analogous to
[
6
, Proposition 3.8]
we showcase some of these below.

Proposition 4.11

(Projection formulas for sticky Lagrangian solutions)
.

Let
X_{t}\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathscr{K}
be a sticky Lagrangian solution of (
4.2
) in the sense of Definition
4.10
.
Then

(4.20)

V_{t}\in\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}},\ \text{and so also}\ \Psi_{t}\in\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}},\ \text{for all}\ t\geq 0.

Furthermore, for
0\leq s\leq t
,
X_{t}
and
\Psi_{t}
satisfy

(4.21)

\Psi_{s}-\Psi_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\implies\Psi_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\Psi_{s},

(4.22)

X_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\left(X_{s}+(t-s)\Psi_{s}-\int_{s}^{t}\Phi\ast\rho_{r}(X_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\right).

In particular, we have the formulas

(4.23)

V_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}U[X_{t}]=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\bar{\Psi}-\Phi\ast\rho_{t}(X_{t}),\ \text{and so also}\ \Psi_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\bar{\Psi},

(4.24)

X_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\left(\bar{X}+\int_{0}^{t}U[X_{s}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\left(\bar{X}+t\bar{\Psi}-\int_{0}^{t}\Phi\ast\rho_{s}(X_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right).

Proof.

The inclusion (
4.20
) follows from the right-continuity of
V_{t}
and (
4.13
) in Corollary
4.7
together with the monotonicity property (
4.18
).
The identity (
4.23
) follows from applying the projection to (
4.2
) with
\dot{X}_{t}
replaced with
V_{t}
.
As a consequence of (
4.23
) and (
4.18
) we then have
\Psi_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\Psi_{s}
.
On the other hand,
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}}\Psi_{t}=\Psi_{t}
since
\Psi_{t}
is already constant where
X_{s}
is constant.
Then, from (
4.2
) it follows that
\bar{\Psi}-\Psi_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
, and applying
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{s}}}
to the left-hand side we obtain
\Psi_{s}-\Psi_{t}
, which means that (
4.21
) follows from (
4.19
);
this also implies
\Psi_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\Psi_{s}
, since
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\subset\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}^{\perp}
.

We then note that for
0\leq s\leq r\leq t
we have

\Psi_{s}-\Phi\ast\rho_{r}(X_{r})-V_{r}=\Psi_{s}-\Psi_{r}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{r})\subset\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t}),

and integrating this expression we obtain, cf. Remark
3.4
,

(t-s)\Psi_{s}-\int_{s}^{t}\Phi\ast\rho_{r}(X_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r+X_{s}-X_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t}).

Then (
4.22
) is a consequence of (
3.13
), while (
4.24
) follows from
s=0
and
\Psi_{0}=\bar{\Psi}
.
∎

Remark 4.12

(Semigroup property)
.

From (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) we see that the sticky Lagrangian solution is a semigroup
\hat{S}_{t}\colon(\bar{X},\bar{\Psi})\to(X_{t},\Psi_{t})
.
Furthermore, since
\bar{V}=\bar{\Psi}-\Phi\ast\bar{\rho}(\bar{X})
,
\rho_{t}=X_{t\#}\mathfrak{m}
, and
V_{t}=\Psi_{t}-\Phi\ast\rho_{t}(X_{t})
, this shows that also
\check{S}_{t}\colon(\bar{X},\bar{V})\to(X_{t},V_{t})
is a semigroup.

Remark 4.13

(Reduction to pressureless Euler)
.

In the case
\phi\equiv 0
we have
\Phi\equiv 0
and
\bar{\Psi}=\bar{V}
, thereby recovering the projection formulas in
[
24
, Theorem 2.6]
for the pressureless Euler system.

The formulas of Proposition
4.11
could be useful for numerical implementations of the Euler-alignment system.
For instance, in the case of pressureless Euler, i.e.,
\phi\equiv 0
,
[
7
, Section 4]
presents a computational algorithm based on the convex envelope, which is connected to the projection
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}
through Proposition
3.2
, see
[
24
, Theorem 6.1]
for details.
However, in our case such formulas are less straightforward, since (
4.24
) depends on
X_{s}
for
0\leq s\leq t
;
we will return to this point in Remark
4.18
, after we have established that our Lagrangian solutions are indeed sticky.
To this end we will make use of a natural framework for a numerical method in this setting, namely particle approximations, which were the foundation for the front tracking approximations in
[
21
]
.

4.3.
Sticky particle solutions

Since the dynamics derived in
[
21
]
is based on sticky particles, and both
[
24
]
and
[
6
]
use limits of particle solutions to deduce sticky behavior, we will do the same here.
Let us recall the motivation of the particle solutions from Section
2.1
, in particular we had
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
.
For convenience, following
[
6
]
, we introduce the set
\mathbb{M}^{N}
defined as

\mathbb{M}^{N}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}=1\right\}.

For
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{M}^{N}
we can define a partition of
[0,1)
through the quantities

(4.25)

\theta_{0}\coloneqq 0,\qquad\theta_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{i}m_{j},\quad i\in\{1,\dots,N\}.

Let us denote by
\chi_{A}
the characteristic function of a subset
A\subset[0,1]
.
Then we can define the finite-dimensional Hilbert space

(4.26)

\mathscr{H}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\coloneqq\left\{X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})}\colon(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\right\}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)

and its convex cone

(4.27)

\mathscr{K}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\coloneqq\left\{X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})}\colon(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}\right\}\subset\mathscr{K}\subset L^{2}(\Omega).

For
X^{N}\in\mathscr{K}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
defined through
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{M}^{N}
, we can introduce the empirical measure
\rho^{N}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and its cumulative distribution
M^{N}
,

\rho^{N}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},\qquad M^{N}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}H(x-x_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}\chi_{[x_{i},x_{i+1})}(x),

where
H(x)
is the right-continuous Heaviside function.
Then we know from before that
M^{N}
is the generalized inverse of
X^{N}
.
Moreover, we see that convolution of
\Phi
with
\rho^{N}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
yields exactly the “discrete convolution”
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\ast}
from Section
2
,

(\Phi\ast\rho^{N})(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(x-x_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\theta_{j-1}}^{\theta_{j}}\Phi(x-X^{N}(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega=\int_{\Omega}\Phi(x-X^{N}(\omega))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega.

Remark 4.14
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}
and respectively define
X,Y\in\mathscr{K}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
for
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{M}^{N}
and the empirical measures
\rho_{\boldsymbol{x}},\rho_{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then we have the following identities

W_{p}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{x}},\rho_{\boldsymbol{y}})=\lVert X-Y\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\boldsymbol{m},p},

that is, we recover the
\boldsymbol{m}
-weighted Euclidean
p
-norm from (
2.4
).

Next we want to formulate the particle dynamics from Section
2
using the above framework.
For a given
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{M}^{N}
, suppose
\bar{X}^{N}\in\mathscr{K}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
and
\bar{V}^{N}\in\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}^{N}}
.
We may without loss of generality assume the initial particles to be separate, i.e.,
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbb{K}^{N})
; otherwise, we could reduce and relabel the particles.
Then we may take
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, since
T_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathbb{K}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and we define

(4.28)

X^{N}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}(t)\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})},\qquad\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X_{t}^{N}=V^{N}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}(t)\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})}.

Recalling (
2.1
), we compute

\bar{\Psi}^{N}=\Psi[\bar{X}^{N},\bar{V}^{N}]=\bar{V}^{N}+(\Phi\ast\bar{\rho}^{N})(\bar{X}^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\bar{v}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(\bar{x}_{i}-\bar{x}_{j})\right]\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bar{\psi}_{i}\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})}

and

U[X_{t}^{N}]=\bar{\Psi}^{N}-(\Phi\ast\rho_{t}^{N})(X_{t}^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\bar{\psi}_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\Phi(x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t))\right]\chi_{[\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i})}

If we now let
X_{t}^{N}
satisfy

\dot{X}^{N}_{t}=U[X_{t}^{N}]=\bar{\Psi}^{N}-(\Phi\ast\rho^{N}_{t})(X^{N}_{t}),\quad X_{0}^{N}=\bar{X}^{N},

this corresponds exactly to the particle evolution (
2.2
) for the coefficients of the step functions (
4.28
), which by Lemma
2.1
is uniquely determined with our inelastic collision rules.
Here we have by construction
V^{N}_{0}=\bar{V}^{N}\in\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{0}}
.
Since there are
N
discontinuities in the initial
\bar{X}
, i.e.,
N
particles, there can at most be
N-1
collisions.
Hence there will be a set of strictly increasing collision times
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N^{*}+1}
, where for convenience we have included
t_{0}=0
and
t_{N^{*}+1}=+\infty
, so that
N^{*}\leq N-1
is the number of actual collisions.
If we now consider
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, by construction we have the relations

\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t}}=\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t_{k}}},\qquad V_{t}+\Phi\ast\rho^{N}_{t}(X^{N}_{t})=V_{t_{k}}^{N}+\Phi\ast\rho^{N}_{t_{k}}(X^{N}_{t_{k}})\iff\Psi_{t}^{N}=\Psi_{t_{k}}^{N}.

At the time of collisions
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N^{*}}
, by the continuous trajectory of
\boldsymbol{x}
and inelastic collision rule (
2.7
) for
\boldsymbol{v}
, we have

X^{N}(t_{k}+)=X^{N}(t_{k}-),\qquad V^{N}(t_{k}+)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}(t_{k})}}V^{N}(t_{k}-)\iff\Psi^{N}(t_{k}+)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}(t_{k})}}\Psi^{N}(t_{k}-).

Note that we will occasionally write
X^{N}(t_{k}\pm)
rather than
X^{N}_{t_{k}\pm}
, etc., to avoid cluttered subscripts.
Now, since (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) hold by construction, it follows that
\Psi^{N}_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t}}}\bar{\Psi}^{N}
.
It remains to show that this sticky evolution is in fact a Lagrangian solution of the differential inclusion (
4.2
), and the proof is similar to those of
[
24
, Theorem 4.2]
and
[
6
, Theorem 5.2]
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{m})\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbb{K}^{N})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{M}^{N}
, and let
(\bar{X}^{N},\bar{V}^{N})\in\mathscr{K}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\times\mathscr{H}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
be the corresponding step functions defined through (
4.25
)–(
4.27
).
Then the corresponding sticky particle solution
X^{N}_{t}
is a sticky Lagrangian solution of (
4.2
). In particular, it satisfies the relations of Proposition
4.11
.

Proof.

From construction it is clear that the monotonicity property (
4.17
) is satisfied.
We will prove by induction on the collision times that
X^{N}_{t}
satisfies (
4.2
),
which by right-continuity is equivalent to

(4.29)

U[X^{N}_{t}]-V_{t}^{N}=\bar{\Psi}^{N}-\Psi^{N}_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t}^{N}).

Consider
t\in[0,t_{1})
, then by construction
\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t}}=\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}^{N}}
.
Therefore,
V_{t}^{N}=U[X_{t}^{N}]
, or
\Psi_{t}^{N}=\bar{\Psi}^{N}
, leading to
0\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X^{N}_{t})
, which is true.

Assume next that (
4.2
) is satisfied for
t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})
, and let us consider
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
instead.
By construction we have
\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t}}=\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t_{k}}}
,
V^{N}(t_{k}+)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t}}}V^{N}(t_{k}-)
,
\Psi^{N}(t_{k}+)=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X^{N}_{t}}}\Psi^{N}(t_{k}-)
and by hypothesis

U[X^{N}(t_{k})]-V^{N}(t_{k}-)=\bar{\Psi}^{N}-\Psi^{N}(t_{k}-)\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X^{N}_{t_{k}}).

On the other hand,
-V^{N}(t_{k}-)\in T_{X^{N}_{t_{k}}}\mathscr{K}
, and also
-\Psi^{N}(t_{k}-)\in T_{X^{N}_{t_{k}}}\mathscr{K}
, which by (
3.23
) yields
\Psi^{N}(t_{k}-)-\Psi^{N}_{t_{k}}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X^{N}_{t_{k}})
.
Now, since
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t_{k}})
is a cone, we add the previous equations together, use
\Psi^{N}_{t}=\Psi^{N}_{t_{k}}
and the monotonicity (
4.18
) to deduce that (
4.29
) holds.
∎

4.3.1.
The globally sticky evolution

We now return to the Lagrangian solution from Theorem
4.6
, and as in
[
24
, Lemma 5.1]
, see also
[
6
, Remarks 5.3, 5.4]
, we want to use the sticky particle evolution above to show that it is globally sticky in the sense of Definition
4.10
.
To this end we will use the fact that functions of the form (
4.26
) are dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and so there are sequences
\{N_{n}\}
and
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n},\bar{\boldsymbol{m}}_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{N_{n}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}}\times\mathbb{M}^{N_{n}}
such that the corresponding
(\bar{X}^{N_{n}},\bar{V}^{N_{n}})
converge strongly to
(\bar{X},\bar{V})
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
For ease of notation we will then use the superscript
n
rather than
N_{n}
in the corresponding quantities.
The strong convergence and Lemma
4.1
then shows that also
\Psi[\bar{X}^{n},\bar{V}^{n}]
converges strongly to
\Psi[\bar{X},\bar{V}]
.
On the other hand, assuming only weak convergence for the velocity, we have

X^{n}\to X,\ V^{n}\rightharpoonup V\ \text{in }L^{2}(\Omega)\implies\Psi[X^{n},V^{n}]\rightharpoonup\Psi[X,V]\ \text{in }L^{2}(\Omega).

Then we have the following result.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
(X^{n}_{t},V^{n}_{t})\in\mathscr{K}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\times\mathscr{H}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
, where
V^{n}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X^{n}_{t}
, be sticky particle solutions of (
4.2
), as defined in Proposition
4.15
, for which
\bar{X}_{n}
and
\bar{V}_{n}
respectively converge to
\bar{X}
and
\bar{V}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Then:

(a)

X^{n}_{t}
converges to
X_{t}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
uniformly in each compact interval, where
X_{t}
is Lipschitz curve with values in
\mathscr{K}
.

(b)

The Lipschitz curve
X_{t}
is a sticky Lagrangian solution of (
4.2
), in particular
X_{t}
and
V_{t}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}X_{t}
satisfy the properties of Corollary
4.7
and Proposition
4.11
.

(c)

V^{n}_{t}
converges strongly to
V_{t}
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
for every
T>0
.

(d)

For any weak accumulation point
V^{\prime}
of
V^{n}_{t}
, we have
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}V^{\prime}=V_{t}
. Similarly, for
\Psi^{\prime}\coloneqq V^{\prime}+\Phi\ast\rho(X_{t})
we have
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\Psi^{\prime}=\Psi_{t}
.

(e)

Let
\mathcal{T}\subset(0,\infty)
be the countable set of discontinuities for
t\mapsto\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
Then
V^{n}_{t}\to V_{t}
and
\Psi^{n}_{t}\to\Psi_{t}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for every
t\in[0,\infty)\setminus\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Property (a) follows from the stability estimate (
4.15
); indeed, on every compact time interval
X^{n}_{t}
is a Cauchy sequence in the closed cone
\mathscr{K}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Furthermore, by property (d) of Corollary
4.7
,
X^{n}_{t}
has Lipschitz-constant bounded by
\lVert\bar{V}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
From the uniform convergence on each compact and the strong convergence
\bar{V}^{n}\to\bar{V}
it follows that the limit function
X_{t}
has Lipschitz-constant bounded by
\lVert\bar{V}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.

Property (b): That
X_{t}
is a Lagrangian solution follows from stability results for gradient flows in Hilbert spaces; indeed, by assumption it is a weak solution in the sense of
[
8
, Definition 3.1]
.
That it is a Lagrangian solution according to Definition
4.5
, i.e., a strong solution in the sense of
[
8
, Definition 3.1]
, follows from
[
8
, Proposition 3.2]
and
X_{t}
being absolutely continuous on each compact due to (a).
That
X_{t}
is sticky follows from that
X^{n}_{t}
is sticky and the stability estimate (
4.15
), cf.
[
6
, Remark 5.4]
.
Indeed, from the strong convergence
\bar{X}^{n}\to\bar{X}
it follows that
\Omega_{\bar{X}}\subset\Omega_{X_{t}}
.
By uniqueness, we obtain the same solution
(X_{t},V_{t})
by taking
(\bar{X},\bar{V})=(X_{s},V_{s})
for
0<s<t
. and evolve this to time
t-s
; then by the same argument
\Omega_{X_{s}}\subset\Omega_{X_{t}}
.

Property (c) follows directly from the stability estimate (
4.16
).

Property (d): Let
n_{k}
be an arbitrary subsequence such that
V^{n_{k}}_{t}\rightharpoonup V^{\prime}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Since the subdifferential of
\mathcal{V}(X)
is maximally monotone, its graph is also strongly-weakly closed, meaning we can pass to the limit in
V^{n}_{t}\in U[X_{t}^{n}]-\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X^{n}_{t})
to obtain
V^{\prime}\in U[X_{t}]-\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
.
Recalling that
\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X)\subset\mathscr{H}_{X}^{\perp}
, we can project the previous relation to find
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}V^{\prime}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}U[X_{t}]
. Since
X_{t}
is a sticky solution it then follows from (
4.23
) that
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}V^{\prime}=V_{t}
.

Property (e): Let
t\in(0,\infty)\setminus\mathcal{T}
, and let
n_{k}
,
V^{\prime}
be as in the previous point.
From the strong convergence in point (c), there exists a dense set
\mathcal{S}\subset(0,\infty)
we can extract a further subsequence, not relabeled, such that
V^{n_{k}}(s)\to V(s)
for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
In particular, for
s\in\mathcal{S}
such that
s<t
we have

\lVert V^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\lVert V_{t}^{n_{k}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\lVert V_{s}^{n_{k}}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\lVert V_{s}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Since
t
is a point of continuity, we may approach it from below to obtain

\lVert V^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert U[X_{t}]-\xi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall\>\xi\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t}).

Then, as
V_{t}
is the unique minimal element of
U[X_{t}]-\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
, it follows that
V^{\prime}=V_{t}
and

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\lVert V^{n_{k}}_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert V_{t}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which in turn gives the strong convergence of
V^{n_{k}}_{t}
to
V_{t}
. Furthermore, since the subsequence was arbitrary, it follows that the whole sequence
V^{n}_{t}
converges strongly to
V_{t}
.
∎

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
\bar{X}\in\mathscr{K}
and
\bar{V}\in\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}}
. The corresponding Lagrangian solution
X_{t}
of Theorem
4.6
is globally sticky. In particular, the results of Proposition
4.11
apply to
X_{t}
and its velocity
V_{t}
from Corollary
4.7
.
Furthermore, the identity
V_{t}=v_{t}\circ X_{t}
from (
4.13
) holds for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since step functions are dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we can approximate
\bar{X}
,
\bar{V}
with
\bar{X}^{n}
,
\bar{V}^{n}
which converge strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and apply Theorem
4.16
.
∎

Remark 4.18

(Projection formulas)
.

The attractive Euler–Poisson system, obtained by replacing the velocity-alignment forcing term in (
1.1b
) with the Poisson force
-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}(\sgn\ast\rho_{t})\rho_{t}
for
\alpha>0
, also has sticky Lagrangian solutions.
Its corresponding projection formula, cf.
[
6
, Example 6.9]
, is

X_{t}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\left(\bar{X}+t\bar{V}-\frac{\alpha}{4}t^{2}(2m-1)\right),

which for
\alpha=0
reduces to the formula for the pressureless Euler system from
[
24
]
mentioned in Remark
4.13
.
This projection is particularly simple in that the expression being projected can be computed independently of the intermediate values
X_{s}
for
0<s<t
; indeed, it is uniquely determined by the initial data.
This differs from the projection formula (
4.24
), which is more similar to the projection formula in
[
6
, Proposition 3.8]
for a more general sticking force term
f[\rho_{t}]
with Lagrangian representation
F[X_{t}]
.
This aligns with the observation in
[
22
]
that one cannot take the (free-flow) particle trajectories of
[
15
]
, which evolve according to the natural velocities
\bar{\psi}_{i}
and allow for crossings, and project these to recover the sticky particle trajectories.

5.
From gradient flows to other solutions

In this section we will use the sticky Lagrangian solution established in the previous section to obtain solutions for other equations.
Naturally, in the spirit of
[
24
,
6
]
, we will return to the original Euler-alignment system (
1.1
) and show that the Lagrangian solution provides us with a distributional solution according to Definition
1.2
.

On the other hand, another main objective of this study is to show that the sticky particle dynamics obtained in
[
21
]
by means of the scalar balance law (
1.12
) can be realized from a gradient flow point-of-view.
To this end, following the ideas of
[
10
]
, we will see how this balance law can be formally derived from a scalar conservation law, where the flux function is the primitive of the prescribed velocity
U[X_{t}]
in (
4.3
).
More importantly, we show that the Lagrangian solution also provides us with an entropy solution of (
1.12
).

5.1.
A distributional solution of the Euler-alignment system

Now we return to the Euler-alignment system, recalling the (non-complete) metric space
(\mathscr{T}_{2},D_{2})
from (
3.6
), (
3.8
), and propose the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\in\mathscr{T}_{2}
. Define
\bar{X}
according to (
3.2
), i.e.,
\bar{\rho}=\bar{X}_{\#}\mathfrak{m}
, and
\bar{V}=\bar{v}\circ\bar{X}
.
Then the Lagrangian solution of Theorem
4.6
provides us with a distributional solution
(\rho_{t},v_{t})\in\mathscr{T}_{2}
of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.2
, with
\rho_{t}
and
v_{t}
given by Corollaries
4.7
and
4.17
.
In particular, since the Lagrangian solution is globally sticky,
S_{t}\colon(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\to(\rho_{t},v_{t})
is a semigroup in the metric space
(\mathscr{T}_{2},D_{2})
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.17
there is
v_{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho_{t})
such that
V_{t}=v_{t}\circ X_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
In turn, we can define
\psi_{t}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\rho_{t})
as in (
1.10
).
We start by showing that
(\rho_{t},\psi_{t})
is a distributional solution of (
1.11
), and proceed as in the proof of
[
6
, Theorem 3.5]
.
Given
\varphi\in C_{\mathrm{c}}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
we use (
3.3
) to write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)\psi_{t}(t,x)+\partial_{x}\varphi(t,x)v_{t}(t,x)\psi_{t}(t,x)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,X_{t}(m))+\partial_{x}\varphi(t,X_{t}(m))V_{t}(m)\right]\Psi_{t}(m)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\varphi(t,X_{t}(m))\right]\bar{\Psi}(m)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(t,X_{t}(m))\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\bar{\Psi}(m)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0,

where we have used
\bar{\Psi}-\Psi_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\subset\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}^{\perp}
.
Therefore, the “momentum” equation (
1.11b
) is satisfied in distributions.
A similar, even more straightforward, argument shows that the continuity equation (
1.11a
), which coincides with (
1.1a
), is satisfied.
Next, as in the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 6.3]
, we will make use of that (
1.11
) holds distributionally to show that the momentum equation (
1.1b
) holds in distributions.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho_{t}v_{t})+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\rho_{t}\psi_{t})+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v_{t}\psi_{t})-\partial_{t}(\rho_{t}(\Phi\ast\rho_{t}))-\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v_{t}(\Phi\ast\rho_{t}))

\displaystyle=-(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})[\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\partial_{x}(\rho_{t}v_{t})]-\rho_{t}[\partial_{t}(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})+v_{t}\partial_{x}(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})]

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}(\phi\ast(\rho_{t}v_{t}))-\rho_{t}v_{t}(\phi\ast\rho_{t}).

It remains to verify the initial conditions (
1.13
).
The first limit follows from the relation
\rho_{t}=X_{t\#}\mathfrak{m}
and the strong limit
X_{t}\to\bar{X}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
For the second limit we need to show that

\lim\limits_{t\to 0+}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)v_{t}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)\bar{v}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\rho}(x)\quad\text{for every}\ \varphi\in C_{\text{b}}(\mathbb{R}),

where
C_{\text{b}}(\mathbb{R})
is the space of continuous, bounded functions.
However, this follows from
\bar{V}=\bar{v}\circ\bar{X}\in\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}}\subset T_{\bar{X}}\mathscr{K}
,
V_{t}=v_{t}\circ X_{t}
and (
4.12
).
The semigroup property is a consequence of the semigroup property of the sticky Lagrangian solution, cf. Remark
4.12
.
∎

5.2.
An entropy solution of the scalar balance law

Let us now see how our notion of gradient flow solutions relate to the entropy solutions studied by Leslie and Tan
[
21
]
.

5.2.1.
Recovering the balance law

As noticed in
[
10
]
, a Lagrangian solution
(X_{t},V_{t})
satisfies a conservation law of the form

(5.1)

\partial_{t}M_{t}+\partial_{x}\mathcal{U}(t,M_{t})=0,

where the flux function
\mathcal{U}(t,m)
is the primitive of the prescribed velocity
U_{t}
satisfying
U_{t}-V_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
.
Define the primitive of
U[X_{t}]
from (
4.3
),

\int_{0}^{m}U[X_{t}](\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega=\int_{0}^{m}(\bar{\psi}\circ\bar{X})(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega-\int_{0}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(X_{t}(\omega)-X_{t}(\tilde{m}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{m}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\eqqcolon A(m)-S[X_{t}](m),

where

(5.2)

A(m)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{m}\bar{\Psi}(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega

corresponds to the flux function
A
in (
1.12
), as defined in
[
21
]
.
The second term evaluated at
m=M_{t}(x)
can be written as

S[X_{t}](M_{t}(x))=\int_{0}^{M_{t}(x)}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(X_{t}(\omega)-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega

Applying the Vol’pert
BV
-chain rule, see
[
10
, Lemma 4.2]
, and the fact that
X_{t}(M_{t}(x))=x
for
\rho_{t}
-a.e.
x
, with
\rho_{t}=\partial_{x}M_{t}
, we obtain

\partial_{x}S[X_{t}](M_{t}(x))=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(y)\right)\partial_{x}M_{t}=(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})\partial_{x}M_{t}

Combining the above with (
5.1
) and rearranging the source term
\partial_{x}S[X_{t}](M_{t})
we obtain exactly the balance law (
1.12
).
Note that in
[
21
]
they work with a shifted flux function
A\colon[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]\to\mathbb{R}
,
where the lower integral limit in (
5.2
) is
-1/2
, since they instead work with the primitive
\tilde{M}_{t}=M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}
.
The reason for this, in a sense, more symmetric choice of primitive is to justify the relation
\Phi\ast\rho_{t}=\phi\ast M_{t}
for their compactly supported
\rho_{t}
.
We will see below that under our assumptions,
\Phi\ast\rho_{t}
is a continuous and linearly bounded function, meaning
(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})\rho_{t}
is a well-defined measure.
Hence we prefer to work with this quantity as it is.

5.2.2.
Entropy solutions, the Rankine–Hugoniot and Oleĭnik E conditions

Let
\eta\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz and convex function, and suppose
q\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
q^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}A^{\prime}
.
Then
(\eta,q)
is called an entropy-entropy flux pair, and the entropy inequality associated with the balance law (
1.12
) is

(5.3)

\partial_{t}\eta(M_{t})+\partial_{x}q(M_{t})\leq(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})\partial_{x}\eta(M_{t}).

Definition 5.2
.

A function
M_{t}\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is an entropy solution to (
1.12
) if it is nondecreasing, and satisfies (
5.3
) in the weak sense for every entropy-entropy flux pair.

By an approximation argument, one can equivalently require that the inequality holds with the Kružkov entropy-entropy flux pair

(5.4)

\eta_{k}(m)=\lvert m-k\rvert,\qquad q_{k}(m)=\sgn(m-k)(A(m)-A(k))=\int_{k}^{m}\sgn(\omega-k)\bar{\Psi}(\omega)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega

for
k
in a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
, see
[
14
, Section 3]
.
We can then, as in
[
21
]

5.2.3.
Verifying the entropy admissibility

As mentioned in the introduction, in
[
21
]
the initial data is assumed to be
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
.
In particular, this ensures that the flux function
A
is Lipschitz.
Moreover, since the solution
(\rho_{t},v_{t})
retains these properties,
\Phi\ast\rho_{t}
remains a bounded, continuous function for all
t\in[0,T]
.
In turn, the source term
(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})\rho_{t}
remains a well-defined measure for these times, and the distributional formulation of the balance law (
1.12
) makes sense.

We now show that the balance law, in particular the source term, also makes sense for our solution
(\rho_{t},v_{t})\in\mathscr{T}_{2}
.
By the pointwise linear bound on
\Phi
in Lemma
1.1
we have

\lvert\Phi\ast\rho(x)\rvert\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert\Phi(x-y)\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(y)\leq\Phi(1)\left(1+\lvert x\rvert+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert y\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(y)\right),

which shows that the map
f[\rho]\colon\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R})
given by
f[\rho]=(\Phi\ast\rho)\rho
is pointwise linearly bounded, cf.
[
6
, Definition 6.1]
.
Moreover, by Lemma
4.1
, we even have that
f[\rho]
is uniformly continuous in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 6.2]
.
In particular, the source term in (
1.12
) is well-defined, and its distributional formulation makes sense.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\in\mathscr{T}_{2}
, so that
\bar{\psi}=\bar{v}+\Phi\ast\bar{\rho}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
.
Define
\bar{M}(x)=\bar{\rho}((-\infty,x])
and the flux function
A
.
Let
M_{t}
be the generalized inverse of the corresponding Lagrangian solution
X_{t}
and recall
\rho_{t}=X_{t\#}\mathfrak{m}
.
Then
M_{t}
is an entropy solution of the scalar balance law (
1.12
) with initial value
\bar{M}
.

Proof.

The initial data is clear from
\bar{M}
being the generalized inverse of
X_{0}=\bar{X}
.
The fact that
M_{t}
satisfies (
1.12
) in the weak sense follows from that
(\rho_{t},v_{t})
satisfies the continuity equation (
1.1a
) and integration by parts, using once more

\bar{\psi}\circ\bar{X}-\psi_{t}\circ X_{t}=\bar{\Psi}-\Psi_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})\subset\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}^{\perp}.

In fact, for this part we only use that
\bar{\Psi}-\Psi_{t}\in\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}^{\perp}
, equivalent to the Rankine–Hugoniot condition.
On the other hand, to show that the entropy inequality (
5.3
) holds, we need
\bar{\Psi}-\Psi_{t}\in\partial I_{\mathscr{K}}(X_{t})
, equivalent to the Oleĭnik E condition.

Let
M(x)=\rho((-\infty,x])
for some
\rho\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\partial_{x}M=\rho

Then for

[

.
Following
[
10
, Section 5]
, for a.e.

\eta_{k,M}^{\prime}(X_{t})=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}(\sgn(m-k))
,
A_{M}^{\prime}(X_{t})=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}\bar{\Psi}=\Psi_{t}
, and
q_{k,M}^{\prime}(X_{t})=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{H}_{X_{t}}}(\sgn(m-k)\bar{\Psi})
.
Let
0\leq\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
be a smooth test function with compact support; we use this to show that (
5.3
) holds by integrating by parts and using the push-forward relation (
3.3
). That is,
using that
M_{t}
is a weak solution, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\eta_{k}(M_{t})\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)+q_{k}(M_{t})\partial_{x}\varphi(t,x)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t,x)\eta^{\prime}_{k,M}(M_{t})(\Phi\ast\rho_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t,x)\left[q_{k,M}^{\prime}(t,x)-\eta_{k,M}^{\prime}(t,x)A_{M}^{\prime}(t,x)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(t,X_{t})\left[q_{k,M}^{\prime}(X_{t})-\eta_{k,M}^{\prime}(X_{t})A^{\prime}_{M}(X_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

where the final inequality follows from (
3.15

Indeed, since
\varphi\geq 0
it follows from (

) that
\varphi(t,X_{t})\sgn(m-k)\in T_{X_{t}}\mathscr{K}
for any time

and

.
∎

Possible uniqueness of entropy solutions

In
[
21
]
the authors extend the Kružkov doubling-of-variables argument to account for the additional source term, thereby proving that entropy solutions for the balance law (
1.12
) with initial data
(\bar{\rho},\bar{v})\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\bar{\rho})
are unique, relying on the fact that the measure remains compactly supported, and that
\bar{v}
is essentially bounded, meaning the flux function is Lipschitz.

In our case, the solutions belong to the space
\mathscr{T}_{2}
, and the flux function is continuous, but not Lipschitz.
However, uniqueness of entropy solutions has been proved in
[
14
]
for
conservation
laws with the type of flux function we have here.
This suggests that the same may be true for balance laws of the form (
1.12
), most likely with some requirements for the source term.
However, we deem the investigation of this possibility to be outside the scope of our current study.

6.
Clustering formation for sticky dynamics

In their follow-up paper
[
22
]
, the authors of
[
21
]
derive results on the finite- and infinite-time clustering of their sticky-particle solutions of (

).
Here clusters refer to the connected components, or maximal intervals, of
\Omega_{X_{t}}
.
Their analysis is based on the flux function
A
for the balance law (
1.12
) and its lower convex envelope
A^{**}
.
It is perhaps not surprising that the flux function

, which encodes the continuum natural velocity
\bar{\Psi}
, determines the clustering behavior of the Euler-alignment system;
in
[
17
]
it is shown that for the Cucker–Smale particle dynamics and initial positions

\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\notin\mathbb{K}^{N}
, where we recall (
2.1
) and (

).
In our continuum case, recalling
\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\bar{\Psi}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m}A^{**}
from Proposition
3.2
, we observe that any deviation of
A
from
A^{**}
means that
\bar{\Psi}
is not nondecreasing in this region, hence
\bar{\Psi}\notin\mathscr{K}
, the convex cone from (
3.10
).
Indeed, what is called the

in
[

]
, which for our definition (

Similarly, their

\Sigma_{+}

is for us

\Sigma_{+}\coloneqq\left\{m\in[0,1)\colon A^{**}\text{ is not linear on any interval }[m,m^{\prime})\right\},

while the

region

becomes

\Sigma_{0}\coloneqq\left\{\bigcup[m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime})\colon A^{**}\text{ is linear and equal to }A\text{ on }[m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime})\right\}.

Since

Therefore, its range

Suppose

, and write
\psi=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\bar{\Psi}(m)\in\ran(\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m}A^{**})

This set then turns out to be analogous to the set

[

is defined as the maximal half-open interval containing

on which

\{m\}
otherwise.
Figure

6.2.
Clustering from the Lagrangian solutions

Note that
\Sigma_{-}
, like
\Omega_{X_{t}}
, is an open set in
\Omega
, and so can be written as a countable union of disjoint open intervals.
Let us consider one such maximal interval
(m_{-},m_{+})\subset\Sigma_{-}
.
Note that if
\Omega_{\bar{X}}\cap\Sigma_{-}\neq\emptyset
, then any maximal interval
(\alpha,\beta)\subset\Omega_{\bar{X}}
intersecting
(m_{-},m_{+})
must necessarily be contained in
(m_{-},m_{+})
.
If not,
\alpha<m_{+}<\beta
, say, we have

\frac{A(m_{+})-A(m_{-})}{m_{+}-m_{-}}>\frac{A(m_{+})-A(m)}{m_{+}-m},\quad m_{-}<m<m_{+},

by definition of
\Sigma_{-}
, and in particular this holds for
m\in(\alpha,m_{+})
.
However, since
\bar{\Psi}\in\mathscr{H}_{\bar{X}}
, its slope must be constant on
(\alpha,\beta)
, and so the previous inequality implies
A^{**}(m_{+})=A(m_{+})>A(\beta)\geq A^{**}(\beta)
, such that

\frac{A^{**}(m_{+})-A^{**}(m_{-})}{m_{+}-m_{-}}>\frac{A^{**}(\beta)-A^{**}(m_{-})}{\beta-m_{-}}.

This contradicts
A^{**}
being convex, cf.
[
22
, Lemma 2.2]
.
A similar argument holds for the case
\alpha<m_{-}<\beta
.
This means that if
m^{\prime}<m^{\prime\prime}
and
L(m^{\prime})\neq L(m^{\prime\prime})
, we must have
\bar{X}(m^{\prime})<\bar{X}(m^{\prime\prime})
, which is also the content of
[
22
, Lemma 3.5]
.

Based on our Lagrangian solutions, we now present a result corresponding to
[
22
, Theorem 1.7]
, where steps of our proof are very much inspired by theirs.

.

If

II.

If

for sufficiently large

(i)

Proof.

Consider

Then

Comparing Theorem

The assumption of bounded

is covered by (i).
Indeed, then
\Phi(x)\leq\phi(0)x
for

\Phi
is not integrable at the origin, and one finds the estimate
l(t)\geq l(0)\mathrm{e}^{-\phi(0)t}
.

In the subcase (ii) they make use of the bounded support of their measure, ensuring that the image of
L(m)
through
X_{t}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
can be made arbitrarily small.
For the same reasons, we assume that
\bar{X}(\omega)
is bounded for
\omega\in L(m)
, so that we start with a finite interval.
To get the lower bound, they assume a heavy tail,
\int_{1}^{\infty}\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\infty
, which implies global communication
\phi(x)>0
.
Under this assumption, we could combine it with
\Phi(x)\geq\phi(x)x
to find
\Phi(l(t))\geq\phi(l(0))l(t)
.
In turn this leads to
l(t)\leq l(0)\mathrm{e}^{-(m_{+}-m_{-})\phi(l(0))t}
, which is similar to their bound.

In subcase (iii), the assumption of weakly singular
\phi
, cf. (
1.2
), is covered by the integrability of the reciprocal of
\Phi
at the origin.
Moreover, with the global communication assumption one has that
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}l(t)\leq-(m_{+}-m_{-})\phi(R)l(t)
as long as

.
Then, when

, one follows
[
22
]
in using (

) to show finite-time clustering.

6.3.
Tails and flocking of subgroups

Case III of Theorem
6.2
featured some assumptions on the singularity of
\phi
at the origin, through whether or not the reciprocal of
\Phi
is integrable at the origin.
Here we saw that the mass contained in a non-singleton
L(m)
exhibits a kind of “local flocking”, in the sense that the diameter of
X_{t}(L(m))=\{X_{t}(\omega)\colon\omega\in L(m)\}
cannot increase.
Moreover, if
\phi
is nonsingular there is no finite-time clustering except for existing clusters or supercritical regions.
On the other hand, if
\phi
is weakly singular, mass in
L(m)
will cluster in finite time.

In Theorem
6.2
we have seen that the continuum natural velocity
\bar{\Psi}
, through its projection on the convex cone
\mathscr{K}
, partitions
\Omega
into subgroups
L(m)
of mass which remain uniformly separated.
Let us see how the tail conditions in (
1.3
) affect the “flocking” of two distinct subgroups
L(m^{\prime})
and
L(m^{\prime\prime})
with
\psi^{\prime}=\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\bar{\Psi}(m^{\prime})<\mathop{}\mathrm{P}_{\mathscr{K}}\bar{\Psi}(m^{\prime\prime})=\psi^{\prime\prime}
.
As we have seen in the proof of Theorem
6.2
, the dynamics of the Euler-alignment system is determined by a competition
between the difference in natural velocities
\psi^{\prime\prime}-\psi^{\prime}
, which drives
L(m^{\prime})
and
L(m^{\prime\prime})
apart, and the strength of the communication
\Phi
which tends to align them.

First, suppose
\phi
is integrable on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., has a thin tail.
Then
\Phi(x)
is bounded and we have
\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}2\Phi(x)=\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}}
.
In particular, we see that the communication is not strong enough to prohibit the subgroups from drifting apart if the difference in natural velocities is large.
Indeed, suppose
c=\psi^{\prime\prime}-\psi^{\prime}-\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}}>0
, and let
l(t)
be the distance between the centers of mass for the two subgroups.
Then it follows from (
6.3a
) that

l(t)\geq l(0)+ct
, i.e., the distance increases linearly in time.

On the other hand, if
\phi
is not integrable, i.e., has a fat tail, then
\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\Phi(x)=\infty
.
In particular, it is invertible for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then it follows that
\Phi
always can become large enough to balance
\psi^{\prime\prime}-\psi^{\prime}
.
Indeed, suppose for simplicity that
\bar{X}(L(m^{\prime}))
and
\bar{X}(L(m^{\prime\prime}))
are bounded, and write
m_{-}^{\prime}=\min L(m^{\prime})<\sup L(m^{\prime\prime})=m_{+}^{\prime\prime}
.
Then, writing
l(t)=X_{t}(m_{+}^{\prime\prime}-)-X_{t}(m_{-}^{\prime})
and following the derivation of (
6.4
), we find

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{+}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}l(t)\leq\psi^{\prime\prime}-\psi^{\prime}-(m_{+}^{\prime\prime}-m_{-}^{\prime})\Phi\left(l(t)\right).

In particular, we see that if
l(t)>\Phi^{-1}((\psi^{\prime\prime}-\psi^{\prime})/(m_{+}^{\prime\prime}-m_{-}^{\prime}))
, the distance

between the outer edges of
L(m^{\prime})
and
L(m^{\prime\prime})
must decrease to this threshold or less.
On the other hand, from (
6.3a
) we see that there is a corresponding lower threshold for the distance between
L(m^{\prime})
and
L(m^{\prime\prime})
given by
2\Phi^{-1}((\psi^{\prime\prime}-\psi^{\prime})/2)
.
