Finite de Finetti bounds in relative entropy
Lampros Gavalakis, Oliver Johnson, Ioannis Kontoyiannis

Abstract
We review old and recent finite de Finetti theorems in total variation distance and in relative entropy, and we highlight their connections with bounds on the difference between sampling with and without replacement. We also establish two new finite de Finetti theorems for exchangeable random vectors taking values in arbitrary spaces. These bounds are tight, and they are independent of the size and the dimension of the underlying space.

Information in probability

Early history.

Classical information theory is built on probability theory. Not only
is its core toolbox and vocabulary probabilistic but, since its early days,
sophisticated probabilistic techniques were effectively utilised
in many core information-theoretic problems.
For example, what is arguably the crowning achievement of information theory
– Shannon’s channel coding theorem
[
90
]
– is proved
with one of the earliest applications of
the “probabilistic method”
[
1
]
.
Since then, there has been a consistent influx of
modern probabilistic
ideas and tools, employed in a very rich manner for the analysis
of information-theoretic questions.

Equally remarkable has been the mathematical
“traffic” in the reverse direction.
Information-theoretic results and intuition
have found applications in the study of
a rapidly growing
number of fundamental probabilistic phenomena.
In most cases, information theory not only informs our
understanding of different aspects of stochastic behaviour,
but it also offers new avenues for exploring this
behaviour rigorously.

In 1958, Hájek used the properties of the
relative entropy to explore the absolute
continuity between Gaussian measures
[
56
,
57
]
.
But the first major results established by the
application of information-theoretic tools for
the proof of purely probability-theoretic results
came in 1959, when
Linnik suggested an information-theoretic proof of the
central limit theorem
[
77
]
,
and the following year, in 1960, when
Rényi used information-theoretic methods to
prove that finite-state Markov chains with all
positive transitions convergence to equilibrium
in relative entropy
[
87
]
. Then
in the 1970s and 80s, Csiszár
used his ‘method of types’
[
29
]

to establish strong versions of a number of
the standard results in
large deviations
[
26
,
27
]
.

Barron’s influence.

Over the past 40 years,
Andrew Barron has played a major role in promoting the information-theoretic
approach to probabilistic fundamentals. Here we briefly outline some of his
main contributions in this direction.

The Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem is probably the most
important foundational result of classical information
theory, with important links to probability, statistics,
ergodic theory, dynamical systems, and beyond. Andrew Barron entered
the world of information theory in
1985 with his proof of the most general version of the
Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem for general ergodic
processes with densities
[
8

In a very different direction, only a year later, he proved
what by now is the most well-known version of what has come
to be known as

[

entropy between the distribution of the standardised sum
of independent and identically distributed random variables,
and the Gaussian law, converges to zero if and only if it is
ever finite. This paper has been very influential, leading
to a number of subsequent breakthroughs
including
[
3
,

,

,

and
[

.
Then in 1991, Barron gave an information-theoretic proof

-algebras
[

]
.
Building on earlier work by Rényi
[

]

and Fritz
[

for a reversible Markov chain (on a general state space)

It is certainly fair to say that,

and the late 1990s, Andrew Barron was the main
driving force of the information-theoretic

de Finetti’s representation theorem

A random vector
X_{1}^{n}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
is
exchangeable

if its distribution is invariant under permutations of the indices

\{1,\ldots,n\}
. A process
\{X_{n}\;;\;n\geq 1\}
is

exchangeable

if
X_{1}^{n}
is exchangeable for every
n\geq 1
.
[Throughout, we use the notation
X_{i}^{j}
to denote the vector
of random variables
(X_{i},\ldots,X_{j}),i\leq j
,
and the corresponding lower-case notation
x_{i}^{j}
for
individual realisations
(x_{i},\ldots,x_{j})
of
X_{1}^{j}
.]
De Finetti’s
celebrated representation theorem, established
in the 1930s, states that a binary process
is exchangeable if and only if it is a mixture of independent
and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sequences.

Theorem 2.1

(de Finetti
[
32
,
33
]
)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}
be an exchangeable process where each

X_{n}
takes values in
\{0,1\}
. Then there is a unique
Borel probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
such that,
for every
n\geq 1
,

{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}^{n}=x_{1}^{n})=\int_{[0,1]}{Q^{n}_{p}(x_{1}^{n})\,d\mu(p)}=\int_{[0,1]}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q_{p}(x_{i})\,d\mu(p)},\quad x_{1}^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n},

(1)

where
Q_{p}(1)=1-Q_{p}(0)=p
is the
probability mass function of the Bernoulli
distribution with parameter
p
.

De Finetti’s theorem holds much more generally.
For a measurable space
(S,\mathcal{S})
we write
\mathcal{M}(S)

for the
space of probability measures on
S
,
and
{\cal F}
for the smallest

\sigma
-algebra that makes the maps

\{\pi_{A}\;;\;A\in\mathcal{S}\}
measurable,
where each
\pi_{A}:{\cal M}(S)\to[0,1]
is
defined by
P\mapsto\pi_{A}(P):=P(A)
.
For a probability measure
\mu
on
(\mathcal{M}(S),{\cal F})
and
k\geq 1
,
we write
M_{k,\mu}
for the mixture of i.i.d. measures on

(S^{k},{\cal S}^{k})
, defined by:

M_{k,\mu}(A):=\int_{\mathcal{M}(S)}{Q^{k}(A)\,d\mu(Q)},\quad A\in\mathcal{S}^{k}.

(2)

In the case when
S
is a finite set,
we often identify probability measures
P
on
S

with the corresponding probability mass functions (p.m.f.s),
so that
P(x)=P(\{x\})
,
x\in S
. Similarly
we identify
{\cal M}(S)
with
the corresponding simplex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{c}

consisting of all probability vectors

(P(x)\;;x\in S)
, and we equip
{\cal M}(S)

with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated
by the open subsets of
{\cal M}(S)
in the
induced subspace topology.

The most general form of de Finetti’s theorem
is due to Hewitt and Savage:

Theorem 2.2

(Hewitt and Savage
[
63
]
)
.

Let
S
be a compact Hausdorff space
equipped with its Baire
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}
.
If
\{X_{n}\}
is an exchangeable process
with values in
S
,
then there exists a unique measure
\mu
on
the Baire
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{M}(S)
such that,
for each
k\geq 1
, the law
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}

admits the representation:

P_{k}=M_{k,\mu},

Recall that
the Baire
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal B}
of a topological space
B

is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra that makes
all continuous functions
f:B\to{\mathbb{R}}

measurable
[
44
,
21
]
.
A key idea in the proof of Hewitt and Savage is
the geometric interpretation of i.i.d. measures
as the extreme points of the convex set of exchangeable measures,
so that any point in this convex set can be expressed as a mixture
of sufficiently many extreme points. This idea also plays an important
role in the
finite
de Finetti regime discussed in
the following sections.

There is extensive literature extending de Finetti’s theorem
in a number of different directions. Although in this paper we
focus on
finite
de Finetti bounds and their
connection with sampling bounds,
we briefly mention some other interesting connections.
One exciting such connection is between de Finetti-style theorems
and what in the probability literature is known as
Gibbs’
conditioning principle

[
37
]
,
also referred to as the

conditional limit theorem

[
24
]
.
Diaconis and Freedman
[
41
]

showed that the first
k
coordinates of an orthogonally
invariant random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are approximately
mixtures of independent Gaussian random variables.
This is very similar, in spirit, both to
de Finetti’s theorem and to the conditional
limit theorem, which states the following:
Suppose
n
is large and
k
is fixed,
and let
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
denote the empirical measure
induced by the i.i.d. vector
X_{1}^{n}\sim P^{n}
.
Then, conditional on
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}

belonging to an atypical set
E
of probability measures,
the law of
X_{1}^{k}
is approximately
equal to the i.i.d. law
(P^{*})^{k}
,
where
P^{*}
is the relative entropy-closest
member of
E
to
P
.
Exploring this connection further, a finite
de Finetti theorem was proved in
[
42
]

using a finite form of the conditional limit theorem.
Interestingly, one of the information-theoretic
proofs
[
52
]

recently developed (see Section
6
)
is also based on the proof of the conditional limit theorem.

Another fascinating area is that of exchangeable random graphs,
which is also connected to the well known array-version of
de Finetti’s theorem, known as the Aldous-Hoover theorem;
see. e.g.,
[
43
]
and references therein.
Extensions of de Finetti-style
theorems to mixtures of Markov chains were also established
by Diaconis and Freedman
[
39
]
.
A conjecture on partial exchangeability made in that work
was recently proven in
[
58
]
.

Finally, we mention that there has been renewed interest
in exchangeability in statistics, in part motivated
by the success of conformal prediction and related
methods
[
48
,
89
]
,
leading among other things to the notion of weighted exchangeability;
see, e.g., the recent work in
[
6
,
94
]
.

3
Finite de Finetti bounds in total variation

De Finetti’s theorem offers an explicit characterisation of
exchangeable processes, which is general, natural, and useful.
Historically, it has also been viewed as a powerful justification
of the subjective probability point of view
in Bayesian statistics
[
38
,
13
]
.
In this context, it is interpreted as stating that,
an exchangeable binary sequence, for example,
can equivalently be viewed as the realisation
of an i.i.d. Bernoulli sequence, conditional on the
value of the Bernoulli parameter, which is distributed
according to a unique prior distribution.

In terms of applications, it is natural to ask whether
de Finetti’s theorem also holds for finite exchangeable
sequences. The answer is “yes and no”.
Strictly speaking, the exact representation of the
distribution of a finite exchangeable vector as a mixture
of product distributions does
not
hold in general,
but it does hold approximately.
Consider, e.g., a pair
(X_{1},X_{2})
of binary
random variables with,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}=0,X_{2}=1)={\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}=1,X_{2}=0)=\frac{1}{2}.

(3)

The random vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
is clearly exchangeable, but if
a representation like (
1
) were true for some
probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
, then we would have,

\int_{[0,1]}p^{2}\,d\mu(p)=\int_{[0,1]}(1-p)^{2}\,d\mu(p)=0,

which implies that
\mu(\{0\})=\mu(\{1\})=1
, a contradiction.

The example (
3
) was given by Diaconis
[
38
]
.
In that work, the set of exchangeable binary measures for which a de
Finetti-style representation fails was interpreted in a geometric way,
and it was observed that the volume of the region representing those measures
decreases, in the following sense: If
X_{1}^{k}
are the
first
k
coordinates of a longer exchangeable binary sequence
X_{1}^{n}
,
then for
n
significantly larger than
k
the distribution of
X_{1}^{k}

is close to a product distribution. More specifically,
it was shown that for each
k\leq n
there exists a
mixing measure
\mu_{n}
, depending on
n
but not on
k
,
such that the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}

satisfies,

\|P_{k}-M_{k,\mu_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{C_{k}}{n},

(4)

where
C_{k}
is a constant depending only on
k
.
Here the total variation distance (TV) between two
measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(S)
is defined as:

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}:=2\sup_{A\in\mathcal{S}}{|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|}.

Not coincidentally, as we will see below,
the hypergeometric probabilities appear in the
geometric proof of (
4
) as the extreme points of
the convex set of exchangeable measures embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Results of the form (
4
) are referred to as

finite de Finetti theorems
, and they typically state that,
if
X_{1}^{n}
is exchangeable and
k
is small compared to
n
,
then the distribution of
X_{1}^{k}
is in some sense close
that of of an i.i.d. mixture.

The binary assumption was removed and the the sharpest rates were
obtained in Diaconis and Freedman
[
40
]
,
for exchangeable random vectors with values in an arbitrary measurable
space:

Theorem 3.1

(Diaconis and Freedman
[
40
]
)
.

Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an exchangeable random vector with values in
a measurable space

(S,\mathcal{S})
.
Then there exists
a probability measure
\mu_{n}

on
(\mathcal{M}(S),{\cal F})
such that, for every
k\leq n
,
the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}
satisfies,

\|P_{k}-M_{k,\mu_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2n},

(5)

where
M_{k,\mu_{n}}
is the mixture of product distributions
in (
2
).

Unlike the geometric proof of (
4
), the proof
of (
5
) was based on the
elegant connection of finite de Finetti representations
with bounds on the difference of
sampling without and with replacement, described next.
We will make use of a similar argument
in Section
7
,
adapted for relative entropy.

Empirical measures and types.

Let
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
denote the
empirical measure

induced on
(S,\mathcal{S})
by the
random vector, namely,

\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}},

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure that places a unit
mass at
x\in S
.
Similarly, let
\hat{P}_{x_{1}^{n}}
denote the empirical measure
induced by
a fixed string
x_{1}^{n}\in S^{n}
.
We refer to
\hat{P}_{x_{1}^{n}}
as the

type
of
x_{1}^{n}
.
If
Q
is a p.m.f. on a finite set
S_{0}
,
or a probability measure supported on a finite
subset
S_{0}\subset S
, we call
Q
an
n
-
type

if
nQ(x)
is an integer for each
x\in S_{0}
.

Exchangeability and sampling.

The key observation here is the following: Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an
exchangeable random vector. Then,
conditional on
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}=Q
,
the distribution of

X_{1}^{n}
is uniform on the set of sequences

x_{1}^{n}
with the same type
Q
. Moreover, for any
k\leq n
,
the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}
is the
distribution of sampling without replacement from an urn
containing the balls
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, where
x_{1}^{n}

is any string with
\hat{P}_{x_{1}^{n}}=Q
. Therefore,
letting
\mu_{n}
denote the law of
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}

on
({\cal M}(S),{\cal F})
, we have,

P_{k}(A)=\int h(Q,n,k;A)\,d\mu_{n}(Q),\quad A\in{\cal S}^{k},

(6)

where
h(Q,n,k;\cdot)
denotes the

multivariate hypergeometric

law of drawing
k
balls
without

replacement from
an urn containing the balls
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, …,
x_{n}
,
where
x_{1}^{n}
is any string in
S^{n}
with type
\hat{P}_{x_{1}^{n}}=Q
.
Similarly, the mixture
M_{k,\mu_{n}}
with
respect to the same mixing measure
\mu_{n}
can be
written,

M_{k,\mu_{n}}(A)=\int b(Q,n,k;A)\,d\mu_{n}(Q),\quad A\in{\cal S}^{k},

(7)

where
b(Q,n,k;\cdot)=Q^{k}(\cdot)
denotes the

multinomial

law of drawing
k
balls
with

replacement from an urn containing

x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, …,
x_{n}
, for a string

x_{1}^{n}\in S^{n}
with type
\hat{P}_{x_{1}^{n}}=Q
.
In view of the expressions (
6
)
and (
7
),
comparing
P_{k}
with
M_{k,\mu_{n}}
reduces
to comparing the hypergeometric and multinomial
distributions, as described in more detail
in Section
4
.

Note that, as mentioned in
[
40
]
,
the rigorous justification of the representations (
6
)
and (
7
) follows from the measurability
of
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
discussed in the Appendix,
and the obvious measurability of the
p.m.f.s
h(Q,n,k;\cdot)
and
b(Q,n,k;\cdot)
as
functions of
Q=\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
.

The above argument strongly indicates
that the “natural” mixing measure
to consider for finite de Finetti theorems
is the law of
the empirical measure
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
induced by
X_{1}^{n}
.

Diaconis and Freedman also showed that the
O(k^{2}/n)
rate
of Theorem
3.1
can
be improved to
O(k/n)
when the space

S
is finite:

Theorem 3.2

(Diaconis and Freedman
[
40
]
)
.

Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an exchangeable random vector with values in a finite
S

of cardinality
c
.
Then there exists a Borel probability measure
\mu_{n}
on

\mathcal{M}(S)
such that, for every
k\leq n
,
the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}
satisfies,

\|P_{k}-M_{k,\mu_{n}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{2ck}{n},

(8)

where
M_{k,\mu_{n}}
is the mixture of product distributions
in (
2
).

The rates in both bounds (
5
)
and (
8
)
were shown in
[
40
]
to be tight. We explain
the tightness of (
5
) at the end of
Section
4
below, as it follows from the tight
approximation of sampling without and with replacement.

4
Sampling bounds in total variation

Consider an urn containing
n
balls, each ball having one
of
c\geq 2
different colours, and suppose we draw out
k\leq n
of them.
Beyond the motivation offered above in connection
with de Finetti-style representations,
comparing the distributions of sampling with and without replacement
is a fundamental problem with a long
history in probability and statistics;
see, e.g.,
[
86
,
96
]
.
Intuitively,
if the number
n
of balls is large compared to the number
k

of draws, there should only be a negligible difference in the results
of sampling with and without replacement.
Write
\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$}=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{c})
for the vector
representing the number of balls of each colour in the urn,
so that there are
\ell_{j}
balls of colour
j
,
1\leq j\leq c

and
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{c}=n
.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{c})
denote the vector
of the numbers of balls of each colour drawn,
so that
s_{1}+s_{2}+\cdots+s_{c}=k
.

When sampling without replacement, the probability that the
colours of the
k
balls drawn are given by
s

is given by the multivariate hypergeometric
p.m.f.,

H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\mbox{\boldmath$s$}):=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{c}\binom{\ell_{i}}{s_{j}}}{\binom{n}{k}}

(9)

for all
s
with

0\leq s_{j}\leq\ell_{j}
for all
j
, and

s_{1}+\cdots+s_{c}=k
.
On the other hand, the corresponding p.m.f.
B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\mbox{\boldmath$s$})

of sampling with replacement, is the multinomial,

B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\mbox{\boldmath$s$}):=\binom{k}{s_{1},\ldots,s_{c}}\prod_{j=1}^{c}\left(\frac{\ell_{j}}{n}\right)^{s_{j}},

(10)

for all
s
with
s_{j}\geq 0
and

s_{1}+\cdots+s_{c}=k
,
where
\binom{k}{s_{1},\ldots,s_{c}}=\frac{k!}{\prod_{j=1}^{c}s_{j}!}

is the multinomial coefficient.

Note that the p.m.f.s
H
and
B

in (
9
) and (
10
) involve only
the numbers of balls of each colour that are drawn,
whereas the corresponding distributions
h
and
b

defined in the previous section
are over the entire sequence of colours drawn
from the urn.
Of course the two are simply related:
Suppose the composition of the urn is described
by the vector
\ell
or,
equivalently, by the
n
-type

Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})}
defined by

Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})}(j):=\ell_{j}/n
, for each colour

j=1,2,\ldots,c
.
For the sake of simplicity (and without
loss of generality), take the set of colours
S

to be
S=\{1,2,\ldots,c\}
.
Then, by the definitions of
H,B,h
and
b
,
for any
x_{1}^{k}\in S^{k}
,
we have,

\displaystyle h(Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})},n,k;x_{1}^{k})

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{s_{1},\ldots,s_{c}}^{-1}H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\mbox{\boldmath$s$})

(11)

\displaystyle b(Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})},n,k;x_{1}^{k})

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{s_{1},\ldots,s_{c}}^{-1}B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\mbox{\boldmath$s$}),

(12)

where
s
is the composition of
x_{1}^{k}
,
i.e., each
s_{j}
is the number of occurrences
of
j
in
x_{1}^{k}
,
1\leq j\leq c
.

Diaconis and Freedman established the following bound
between
h
and
b
, and used it to prove
Theorem
3.2
using the
connection between exchangeability and sampling explained in the
previous section.

Theorem 4.1

(Diaconis and Freedman
[
40
]
)
.

Let
h(Q,n,k;\cdot)
and
b(Q,n,k;\cdot)
denote
p.m.f.s of sampling
k
balls without and with replacement,
respectively, from an urn containing
n
balls of
c
different
colours, where the
n
-type
Q
describes the composition
of the balls in the urn. Then:

\|h(Q,n,k;\cdot)-b(Q,n,k;\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{2ck}{n}.

However, if one wants a bound that is independent of the number of colours,
then one has to pay a factor
k
in the bound:

Theorem 4.2

(Freedman
[
46
]
)
.

In the notation of Theorem
4.1
,
suppose
k
balls are drawn from an urn containing

n
balls of
n
different colours, so that
c=n
and

Q=Q_{U}
with
Q_{U}(j)=1/n
for each
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.
Then:

2\bigl(1-e^{-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n}}\bigr)\leq\|h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)-b(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{n}.

(13)

The proof of (
13
) is based on considering
the set,

B=\{x_{1}^{k}:x_{i}=x_{j}\text{ for some }1\leq i<j\leq k\},

(14)

and noting that
h(Q_{U},n,k;x_{1}^{k})=0
for all
x_{1}^{k}\in B
,
which implies,

\frac{1}{2}\|h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)-b(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=1-\frac{n!}{(n-k)!n^{k}}.

(15)

Getting back to de Finetti’s theorem, Diaconis
and Freedman
[
40
, Proposition 31]

show the inequality,

\|h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)-M_{k,\mu}(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\geq\|h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)-b(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

for any mixing measure
\mu
. Therefore,
since
h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)
is the distribution of
X_{1}^{k}

when
X_{1}^{n}
is the (exchangeable) vector obtained by
a random permutation of
S=\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
the sharpness of (
5
)
follows from the
sharpness of the upper bound in (
13
).

5
Sampling bounds in relative entropy

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(S)
, the relative entropy
between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as,

D(\mu\|\nu):=\int_{S}{\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\log{\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}}\,d\nu},\quad\text{if }\mu\ll\nu,

and
D(\mu\|\nu)=\infty
otherwise, where
\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}
stands
for the Radon-Nikodým derivative of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
,
and where
\log
denotes the natural logarithm throughout.
In particular, if
S
is discrete and
P,P^{\prime}
are the p.m.f.s
corresponding to
\mu,\nu
, then,

D(\mu\|\nu)=D(P\|P^{\prime})=\sum_{x\in S:P(x)>0}P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{P^{\prime}(x)}.

In view of Pinsker’s inequality
[
25
,
73
]
,

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\big[2D(\mu\|\nu)\big]^{1/2},

relative entropy is considered
a stronger notion of “distance” than total variation.
It is
0
if and only if
\mu=\nu
,
and it is locally quadratic around
\mu=\nu

[
83
]
.
Moreover, although not a proper metric, relative entropy is
often thought of
as a notion of distance between the two measures
\mu
and
\nu
,
justified in part by important results in probability and
statistics
[
30
]
.

The difference between sampling with and without replacement
has also been studied in terms of relative entropy:

Theorem 5.1

(Stam
[
93
]
)
.

Let
H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)
and
B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)
denote
p.m.f.s of sampling without and with replacement
from an urn with balls of
c
colours,
as in (
9
) and (
10
).
Then, for any
\ell
and any
k\leq n
:

D\big(H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\|B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\big)\leq\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{2(n-1)(n-k+1)}.

(16)

Based on (
11
) and (
12
),
Stam
[
93
, Theorem 2.2]
observed that,

D\bigl(h(Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})},n,k;\cdot)\|b(Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})},n,k;\cdot)\bigr)=D\big(H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\|B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\big),

(17)

so that we also have:

D\bigl(h(Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})},n,k;\cdot)\|b(Q^{(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\ell$})},n,k;\cdot)\bigr)\leq\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{2(n-1)(n-k+1)}.

(18)

Moreover, Stam established a closely matching lower bound,
showing that the
O(k^{2}/n^{2})
upper bound in Theorem
5.1

is of optimal order in terms of its dependence on
k
and
n
,
but in general it can be improved:
Harremoës and Matúš
[
62
, Theorem 4.5]

showed that:

D\big(H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\|B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\big)\leq(c-1)\left(\log\left(\frac{n-1}{n-k}\right)-\frac{k}{n}+\frac{1}{n-k+1}\right).

(19)

In the special case
c=n
as in Theorem
4.2
,
an exact expression is derived in
[
66
, eq. (29)]
,
which is interesting to compare with (
15
) above:

D\big(H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\|B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\big)=\log\Big(\frac{n^{k}(n-k)!}{n!}\Big).

Note that all the bounds
in (
16
), (
18
) and (
19
) hold
uniformly in
\ell
. Sharper bounds can be obtained
if we allow dependence on
\ell
. Indeed, a bound which
is often sharper was recently given
in
[
66
, Theorem 1.1]
:
For any
\ell
and all
1\leq k\leq n/2
:

\displaystyle D\big(H(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\|B(\mbox{\boldmath$\ell$},n,k;\cdot)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c-1}{2}\Big(\log\Big(\frac{n}{n-k}\Big)-\frac{k}{n-1}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{k(2n+1)}{12n(n-1)(n-k)}\sum_{i=j}^{c}\frac{n}{\ell_{j}}+\frac{1}{360}\Big(\frac{1}{(n-k)^{3}}-\frac{1}{n^{3}}\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{c}\frac{n^{3}}{\ell_{j}^{3}}.\qquad

(20)

See
[
66
]
for some
detailed comparisons
between (
16
), (
18
), (
19
)
and (
20
).

Finally, we emphasise that all the bounds in this section depend
(in fact, linearly) on the number of colours
c
.

6
Finite de Finetti bounds in relative entropy

A number of finite de Finetti bounds in relative entropy have
recently been established
in
[
51
,
52
,
14
,
66
,
91
]
.
Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an exchangeable random vector with
values in some space
(S,{\cal S})
, let
P_{k}
denote
the law of
X_{1}^{k}
for
k\leq n
, and
for any probability measure
\mu
on

({\cal M}(S),{\cal F})
, recall
the definition of the mixture of product distributions

M_{k,\mu}
in (
2
).

In
[
51
]
, it was shown that,
if
S=\{0,1\}
, then
there is
a mixing measure
\mu_{n}
such that, for
k\leq n
,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})\leq\frac{5k^{2}\log n}{n-k}.

(21)

Then in
[
52
]
it was shown that,
if
S
is a finite set, then
there is a mixing measure
\mu_{n}
such that
a weaker bound of the following form holds
for all
k
sufficiently smaller than
n
:

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})=O\left(\Big(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{1/2}\log{\frac{n}{k}}\right).

(22)

The proofs of both of these results are information-theoretic,
and in both cases the mixing measure
\mu_{n}
is the law of the
empirical measure
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
.
The bound (
21
) was proved via conditional entropy
estimates, while the proof of (
22
) explored the
connection between exchangeability and the Gibbs conditioning principle.

In the more general case when
S
is an arbitrary
discrete (finite or countably infinite)
set, it was shown in
[
14
]

that (a different) mixing measure

\mu^{*}_{n}
exists, such that the following sharper bound holds
for all
k<n
:

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu^{*}_{n}})\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2(n-k-1)}H(X_{1}).

(23)

Note that this meaningful as long as
H(X_{1})
is finite, and
that it gives potentially much sharper estimates when

H(X_{1})
is small.
De Finetti-type bounds for random variables with values
in abstract spaces
(S,{\cal S})
were also derived in
[
14
]
.
The proof of (
23
) was based on an argument that originated
in the quantum information theory literature.

The derivations of (
21
)–(
23
)
employed purely information-theoretic
ideas and techniques, but the actual bounds
are of sub-optimal rate. A sharp rate
was more recently obtained in
[
66
]
,
using Stam’s sampling bound
in Theorem
5.1
combined with the convexity
of relative entropy:

Theorem 6.1

(Johnson, Gavalakis and
Kontoyiannis
[
66
]
)
.

Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an exchangeable random vector with values
in a finite set
S
of cardinality
c
.
Then there is a Borel probability measure
\mu_{n}
on
{\cal M}(S)

such that, for every
k\leq n
, the distribution
P_{k}

of
X_{1}^{k}
satisfies:

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})\leq\frac{(c-1)k(k-1)}{2(n-1)(n-k+1)}.

(24)

Theorem
6.1
gives
a bound with rate
O(k^{2}/n^{2})
.
In view of Pinsker’s inequality and the lower bound
in total variation obtained by Diaconis
and Freedman
[
40
]
,
this bound is of optimal order in its dependence
on
k
and
n
.

Even more recently,
Song, Attiah and Yu
[
91
]
used an adaptation
of Freedman’s argument
[
46
]
,
again based on considering the set (
14
),
to establish a finite de Finetti theorem
with a relative entropy bound that is weaker
when
S
is finite,
but which is independent
of the alphabet size:

Theorem 6.2

(Song, Attiah and Yu
[
91
]
)
.

Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an exchangeable random vector with values
in a discrete (finite or countably infinite) set
S
.
Then there exists a probability measure
\mu_{n}
on
(\mathcal{M}(S),{\cal F})
, such that,
for all
k<n
, the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}
satisfies,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})\leq\log{\Bigl(\frac{n^{k}(n-k!)}{n!}\Bigr)}\leq-\log{\Bigl(1-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n}}\Bigr),

(25)

where the second inequality holds as long as
k(k-1)<2n
.

In the same work, the bound of Theorem
6.2

was shown to be tight.
Recall the notation

h(Q,n,k;\cdot)
for the law of sampling without
replacement as in Section
4
.
Recall also the exact expression in (
15
) above.

Theorem 6.3

(Song, Attiah and Yu
[
91
]
)
.

Let
h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)
denote the law of sampling

k
balls without replacement from an urn
containing
n
balls of
n
different
colours, where
Q_{U}(j)=1/n
for
j\in S=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Then, for any mixing measure
\mu
on
{\cal M}(S)

and any
k<n
:

D\big(h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)\|M_{k,\mu}(\cdot)\big)\geq\log{\Bigl(\frac{n^{k}(n-k)!}{n!}\Bigr)}.

(26)

Letting as before
X_{1}^{n}
denote the exchangeable random
vector obtained as a random permutation
of the set
S=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and noting that

h(Q_{U},n,k;\cdot)
is then the same
as the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}
, Theorem
6.3

shows that the bound (
25
) is indeed tight.

It is interesting to note that the
finite de Finetti bound in (
25
)
is used in
[
91
]
in the proof of a strong achievability
result for a certain source coding scenario, where an encoder
transmits a
k
-letter information sequence to a randomly
activated subset of
k
out of
n
possible users.

In Section
7
we give two new finite
de Finetti bounds in relative entropy, that
are essentially tight, for exchangeable random
vectors with values in arbitrary measurable spaces.
Theorem
7.1
is proved by combining
Stam’s sampling bound in Theorem
5.1

with the representations of
P_{k}
and
M_{k,\mu_{n}}

in terms of sampling distributions in (
6
)
and (
7
).
The proof of Theorem
7.2
is
a generalization of the proof of Theorem
6.2

in
[
91
]
,
combined with the classical representation of relative entropy
as a supremum over finite partitions.

7
New finite de Finetti bounds on abstract spaces

Recall from Section
2

the definition of the
\sigma
-algebra
{\cal F}

associated with the space
{\cal M}(S)
of probability measures
on an arbitrary measurable space
(S,{\cal S})
,
and the definition of the mixture of i.i.d. measures
M_{n,\mu}

in (
2
).

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
X_{1}^{n}
be an exchangeable random vector with
values in a measurable space
(S,{\cal S})
.
Then
there is a probability measure
\mu_{n}
on

({\cal M}(S),{\cal F})
such that, for each
1\leq k\leq n
,
the distribution
P_{k}
of
X_{1}^{k}
satisfies:

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2(n-k+1)}.

Theorem 7.2
.

Under the same assumptions
as Theorem
7.1
,
there is a probability measure
\mu_{n}
on

({\cal M}(S),{\cal F})
such that, for each
1\leq k\leq n
,

D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})\leq\log{\Bigl(\frac{n^{k}(n-k!)}{n!}\Bigr)}\leq-\log{\Bigl(1-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n}}\Bigr),

where the second inequality holds as long as
k(k-1)<2n
.

Remarks.

Before giving the proofs of Theorems
7.1

and
7.2
, some remarks
are in order.

1.

In both theorems, the mixing measure

\mu_{n}
is the law of the empirical measure
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}

on
{\cal M}(S)
.

2.

Also both theorems give bounds with the same asymptotic behaviour

\sim k^{2}/2n
.
The bound in
Theorem
7.2
is slightly stronger
than that in Theorem
7.1
,
and in fact,
in view of the lower bound in Theorem
6.3
,
it is tight. On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
7.1

is very short and quite satisfying in that
it is based on a very direct connection with Stam’s
[
92
]
sampling
bound (
18
).

3.

As discussed by Diaconis and Freedman
in
[
40
]
, we note that it is
curious that we have finite de Finetti bounds, both in
relative entropy and in total variation, under no assumptions
whatsoever on the underlying space
(S,{\cal S})
, whereas for the seemingly
weaker infinite-dimensional representation of the classical
de Finetti theorem in Theorem
2.2
more structure is required.

4.

The choice of the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}

on
{\cal M}(S)
is essential for the
measurability of the empirical measure
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
,
which is needed to define
\mu_{n}
for both theorems.
Indeed, for stronger

\sigma
-algebras, the measurability of Dirac measures may fail.
For example, if
S
is a Polish space and we equip
\mathcal{M}(S)

with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra with respect to the
\tau
-topology,
the Dirac measures are not measurable any more, and therefore
neither is the empirical measure
\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}
;
see, e.g., the relevant discussion
in
[
37
, Section 6.2]
.

5.

As also mentioned in
[
14
]
, the dependence
of de Finetti-relative entropy upper bounds on the alphabet size is
of interest in applications. For example, it is related to the running time
of approximation schemes for the minimisation of polynomials
of fixed degree over the simplex
[
34
,
35
]
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

As it turns out, we have already done almost all of the
work for this. Using the definition
of
M_{k,\mu_{n}}
, the sampling representations
in (
6
) and (
7
),
and Stam’s bound (
18
),

\displaystyle D(P_{k}\|M_{k,\mu_{n}})

\displaystyle=D\left(\int h(Q,n,k;\cdot)\,d\mu_{n}(Q)\right\|\left.\int b(Q,n,k;\cdot)\,d\mu_{n}(Q)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int D\big(h(Q,n,k;\cdot)\|b(Q,n,k;\cdot)\big)\,d\mu_{n}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2(n-k+1)},

where the first inequality follows from the joint convexity
of relative entropy in its two arguments
[
84
]
,
and the second inequality follows from the fact that, for any
specific
n
-type
Q
, the urn with composition described
by
Q
contains balls of at most
n
different colours,
so we can take
c\leq n
in (
18
).

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

We begin by generalising a lower bound
in
[
91
]

based on an argument by Freedman
[
46
]
.
Take
W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{k}
to
be i.i.d. and uniformly distributed in
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
,
independent of
X_{1}^{n}
.
For any
A\in{\cal S}
and any
n
-type
Q\in{\cal M}(S)
,
from the definitions
it is easy to see that,

{\mathbb{P}}\big((X_{W_{1}},X_{W_{2}},\ldots,X_{W_{k}})\in A\big|\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}=Q\big)=Q^{k}(A).

Therefore, the mixture measure
M_{k,\mu_{n}}
can be written,

\displaystyle M_{k,\mu_{n}}(A)

\displaystyle=\int{\mathbb{P}}\big((X_{W_{1}},X_{W_{2}},\ldots,X_{W_{k}})\in A\big|\hat{P}_{X_{1}^{n}}=Q\big)\,d\mu_{n}(Q)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\big((X_{W_{1}},X_{W_{2}},\ldots,X_{W_{k}})\in A\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1}^{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}}\frac{1}{n^{k}}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big((X_{w_{1}},X_{w_{2}},\ldots,X_{w_{k}})\in A\big).

Summing instead over all the index vectors
w_{1}^{k}

in the subset
D_{k}
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}^{k}

that consists of all those
w_{1}^{k}
that have
k
distinct
elements, and using the exchangeability of
X_{1}^{n}
,

\displaystyle M_{k,\mu_{n}}(A)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{w_{1}^{k}\in D_{k}}\frac{1}{n^{k}}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big((X_{w_{1}},X_{w_{2}},\ldots,X_{w_{k}})\in A\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1}^{k}\in D_{k}}\frac{1}{n^{k}}\,{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}^{k}\in A\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!n^{k}}\,P_{k}(A).

Now let
{\cal A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}\}
be any finite partition of
S
.
Applying the last bound above to each
A_{i}
and summing over

i=1,\ldots,N
,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}P_{k}(A_{i})\log\frac{P_{k}(A_{i})}{M_{k,\mu_{n}}(A_{i})}\leq\log\Big(\frac{n^{k}(n-k)!}{n!}\Big).

Taking the supremum of this over all finite partitions
{\cal A}
and
recalling
[
84
]
that
the relative entropy between any two probability
measures is equal to the supremum over
all finite partitions
{\cal A}
, completes the proof.

.

The Dirac measures
\delta_{s}

\sigma

\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{S}}\sigma\bigl(\{P:\pi_{A}(P)\in B\}_{B\in\mathcal{B}([0,1])}\bigr),

\mathcal{B}([0,1])

\sigma

\sigma\bigl(\{P:\pi_{A}(P)\in B\}_{B\in\mathcal{B}([0,1])}\bigr)\subset\mathcal{S}.

(27)

Now we claim that, for every
A\in\mathcal{S}
,

\{s:\delta_{s}\in\{P:\pi_{A}(P)\in B\}\}_{B\in\mathcal{B}([0,1])}\in\mathcal{S},

(28)

implies (
27
).

]
.
