Abstract
. Landmark manifolds consist of distinct points that are often used to describe shapes. We show that in the Euclidean space, we can preselect two vector fields such that their flows will be able to take any collection of landmarks to another, regardless of the number of landmarks we choose.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we consider the controllability of finite approximations of shapes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; in particular we explicitly determine a pair of vector fields that allow for complete controllability of an
n
-shape independently of the number of points used in the approximation.

For motivation, consider the classical problem of controllability: If one has a subbundle
\mathcal{E}\subseteq TM
it is reasonable to ask whether a point
x\in M
can be transformed along flows generated by vector fields in
\mathcal{E}
to another given point
y
. See
[
19
]
for an overview or
[
2
,
8
]
for a modern view.

One can consider controllability in much more general settings, particularly the so called shape spaces, which have been previously considered from the perspective of optimal control theory. Young
[
21
]
gives the foundations of this perspective, and Arguillère
[
5
,
4
,
3
]
gives a development of the framework. This is used, for example, in
[
10
]
to give a notion of modular diffeomorphisms that can be used in the analysis of shape spaces. We note particularly
[
1
]
, in which the complete group of diffeomorphisms (in the connected component of the identity) is shown to be generated by any bracket-generating family of vector fields.

In our setting we are interested in the controllability of imbedded submanifolds
N\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. One approach is to approximate
N
as a finite collection of points, and the question becomes under what conditions can each of the points in the finite approximation be simultaneously transformed to another given set of points along the flows of some finite collection of vector fields. Towards this end one can consider a specific type of shape space, known as an
n
-landmark manifold
\mathcal{M}
, consisting of
n
-tuples of distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A point
p\in\mathcal{M}
, referred to as landmark, can then be interpreted as constituting a finite approximation of a shape
N\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Of particular interest, then, is the relationship between the dimension of the ambient space, the number of points
n
determining the approximation, and the generating set of vector fields. Recently,
[
14
,
20
]
consider the geometry of diffeomorphisms of landmark manifolds, and
[
6
]
presents a general model of diffeomorphisms on landmark manifolds.

Our main result is that for a given dimension
d
for the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we construct a pair of vector fields that give complete controllability of an
n
-landmark, independent of the number of points
n
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
d\geq 1
, there exist two vector fields
\sigma
and
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the following property: For any
n\geq 1
and any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

x_{i_{1}}\neq x_{i_{2}},\qquad y_{i_{1}}\neq y_{i_{2}},\qquad\text{whenever $i_{1}\neq i_{2}$,}

there exists a finite collection of numbers
s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{l},t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{l}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

e^{s_{1}\sigma}\circ e^{t_{1}\tau}\circ e^{s_{2}\sigma}\circ e^{t_{2}\tau}\circ\cdots\circ e^{s_{l}\sigma}\circ e^{t_{l}\tau}(x_{i})=y_{i},\qquad\text{for all $i=1,\dots,n$.}

This results follows from three theorems stated in
Theorem

3.1
,
Theorem

3.2
and
Theorem

4.1
corresponding to the cases
d=1
,
d=2
and
d\geq 3
respectively. In all three cases, we give an explicit expression for our two vector fields. We emphasize that the interesting part of our result is that we are able to choose our vector fields
\sigma
and
\tau
before choosing the number of points
n
. After all, if we first choose
n
, then we will are considering controllability on the
nd
-dimensional landmark manifold
\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it is a generic property to have complete controllability with respect to a subbundle of
T\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of rank two or higher in the sense of
[
17
]
. However, we note that in our result, we can increase the number of points
n
without modifying our vector fields. As application, we mention
[
11
, Section 6]
, where finding the most probable way a shape, represented by landmarks
x_{1}
,
\dots
,
x_{n}
, can be transformed another shape under the stochastic dynamics
d\varphi_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}(\varphi_{t})\circ dW^{j}+u_{t}(\varphi_{t})\,dt
, with
W_{t}=(W_{t}^{1},\dots,W_{t}^{k})
. Solving this problem turns into an affine optimal control problem in the vector fields
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
and
u_{t}
, and adding an arbitrary small noise
\varepsilon(\sigma dW^{k+1}+\tau dW^{k+2})
to the equation would ensure that the problem always has a solution.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Shapes, flows of vector fields and landmarks

We will describe shapes in this paper in the following manner. For
k\leq d
, we consider
a
k
-dimensional shape
as an embedding
f:N\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of a compact
k
-dimensional manifold
N
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
f_{0},f_{1}:N\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two shapes, we say that
a transformation
from
f_{0}
to
f_{t}
is a smooth homotopy
f_{t}
,
0\leq t\leq 1
, between them. Such a homotopy can be constructed using a curve
\varphi_{t}
in the diffeomorphism group
\Diff(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. These have been previously studied, the setting for which is laid out in
[
13
]
. For example,
[
15
,
16
]
considers the space of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
seen as equivalent up to various notions of diffeomorphism, and in later work
[
7
]
introduces a collection of Sobolev Riemannian metrics on spaces of embedded manifolds seen as quotients of submanifolds equivalent up to such diffeomorphisms.

If we discretize
N
, which is often what is needed in many practical numerical applications
[
12
]
, we can regard it as a list of points
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
, and then any mapping
f:N\to M
need only be described up to their values
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
where
f(y_{j})=x_{j}
. Since we have an embedding, these points will all be distinct. Hence we consider the
landmark manifold

\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,:\,x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},x_{i}\neq x_{j}\text{ whenever $i\neq j$}\}.

Describing a shape in this way gives us a finite dimensional representation.

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have a canonical mapping
\ell:\Diff(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\Diff(\mathcal{M})
defined such that

\ell\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{n})).

Remark that if
\sigma
is a vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with local flow
e^{t\sigma}
, then we can describe the corresponding flow
\ell e^{t\sigma}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=(e^{t\sigma}(x_{1}),\dots,e^{t\sigma}(x_{n}))
as

\ell e^{t\sigma}=e^{t\ell\sigma},\qquad\ell\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{n})).

We observe the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

The mapping
\ell:\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
of vector fields
\sigma\mapsto\ell\sigma
is a Lie algebra homomorphism, i.e., for any
\sigma,\tau\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

[\ell\sigma,\ell\tau]=\ell[\sigma,\tau].

We will do the proof very explicitly using notation that will be helpful later. Define
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
be the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\partial_{j}=\partial_{x^{j}}
be the derivative in the
j
-th coordinate. We introduce vector fields
\partial^{i}_{j}
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
j=1,\dots,d
on
\mathcal{M}
defined by

(2.1)

\partial^{i}_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{d}{dt}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i}+te_{j},\dots,x_{n})|_{t=0}.

Note that these vector fields span the entire
T\mathcal{M}
and that that
[\partial^{i_{1}}_{j_{1}},\partial^{i_{2}}_{j_{2}}]=0
.

Proof.

If we have two vector fields
\sigma=\sigma^{j}\partial_{j}
and
\tau=\tau^{j}\partial_{j}
, then
\ell\sigma=\sigma^{j}(x_{i})\partial^{i}_{j}
, and since
\partial^{i_{1}}_{j_{1}}\sigma^{j_{2}}(x_{i_{2}})=\delta^{i_{1}}_{i_{2}}(\partial_{j_{1}}\sigma^{j_{2}})(x_{i_{2}})

(2.2)

\displaystyle{[\ell\sigma,\ell\tau]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{j}(x_{i})(\partial_{j}\tau^{j})(x_{i})\partial^{i}_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{j}(x_{i})(\partial_{j}\sigma^{j})(x_{i})\partial^{i}_{j}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\ell\left(\sigma^{j}(\partial_{j}\tau^{j})\partial_{j}-\tau^{j}(\partial_{j}\sigma^{j})\partial_{j}\right)=\ell[\sigma,\tau].

The result follows.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

We can similarly consider shapes and landmark in a general connected manifold
M
, and we can still prove
Lemma

2.1
in a similar way using local coordinates.

2.2.
Controllability and the bracket-generating condition

Let
M
be an
m
-dimensional manifold. Consider a subbundle
\mathcal{E}\subseteq TM
. An absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
is called
horizontal
if
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\mathcal{E}_{\gamma(t)}
for almost every
t
. For every
q\in M
, we define the orbit
O_{q}
at
q
by

O_{q}=\left\{p\in M\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}\text{there is an horizontal curve}\\
\text{with $\gamma(0)=q$ and $\gamma(1)=p$}\end{array}\right\}.

We say that we have
complete controllability
with respect to
\mathcal{E}
if
O_{q}=M
for any
q\in M
. For every
q\in M
, we define

\Lie(\mathcal{E})_{q}=\left\{X_{i_{1}}|_{q},[X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}}]|_{q},[X_{i_{1}},[X_{i_{2}},X_{i_{3}}]]|_{q},\dots,\,:\,X_{i_{j}}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E})\right\}\subseteq T_{q}M.

We say that
\mathcal{E}
is
bracket-generating
if
\Lie(\mathcal{E})_{q}=T_{q}M
for any
q\in M
.

Theorem 2.3

(Chow-Rashevsky Theorem
[
18
,
9
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{E}
is bracket-generating, then
O_{q}=M
for any
q\in M
.

We remark that
q\in O_{p}
if and only if
p\in O_{q}
, and hence if
O_{q}=M
for one
q\in M
, then it will hold for all points. We remark that
Theorem

2.3
is a special case, see e.g.
[
2
, Chapter 5]
. Following the latter references, we also observe that if
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
is a collection of vector fields spanning
\mathcal{E}
, then

O_{q}=\{e^{t_{1}\sigma_{i_{1}}}\circ e^{t_{2}\sigma_{i_{2}}}\circ\cdots\circ e^{t_{l}\sigma_{i_{l}}}(q)\,:t_{1},\dots,t_{l}\in\mathbb{R},i_{j}=1,\dots,k\}.

Consider now our special case where the manifold is the space of landmarks
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We want to show that we can choose a pair of vector fields
\sigma
,
\tau
independently of
n
such that we have complete controllability with respect to
\mathcal{E}=\spn\{\ell\sigma,\ell\tau\}
. This means that if we have collection of distinct points
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
, there are values
t_{1},\dots,t_{l},s_{1},\dots,s_{l}
such that

e^{s_{1}\ell\sigma}\circ e^{t_{1}\ell\tau}\circ\cdots\circ e^{s_{l}\ell\sigma}\circ e^{t_{l}\ell\tau}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}),

which is equivalent to

e^{s_{1}\sigma}\circ e^{t_{1}\tau}\circ\cdots\circ e^{s_{l}\sigma}\circ e^{t_{l}\tau}(x_{i})=(y_{i}),

simultaneously for
i=1,2,\dots,n.

3.
Low Dimensional Cases

We will need special cases for one and two-dimensional spaces, which is why we will treat these separately. These also serve at helpful examples for understanding the higher dimensional case. Recall the basis
\partial_{j}^{i}
for
T\mathcal{M}=T\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in (
2.1
).

3.1.
Dimension one

We consider first
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e., the case with
d=1
. We note that
T\mathcal{M}
is then spanned by
\partial^{i}_{1}=\partial^{i}
where
i=1,\dots,n
. We note that if
\sigma(x)=f(x)\partial_{1}=f(x)\partial
is a vector field on
\mathbb{R}
, then

\ell\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sigma(x_{1})\partial^{1}+\cdots+\sigma(x_{n})\partial^{n}.

Theorem 3.1

(Controllability for
d=1
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the
n
-landmark manifold. If
\sigma_{0}(x)=\partial
,
\sigma_{3}(x)=x^{3}\partial
and

\mathcal{E}=\spn\{\ell\sigma_{0},\ell\sigma_{3}\}\subseteq T\mathcal{M},

then we have complete controllability with respect to
\mathcal{E}
independent of
n
.

Proof.

For any integer
\alpha\geq 0
, define
\sigma_{\alpha}(x)=x^{\alpha}\partial
. We observe that

[\sigma_{\alpha},\sigma_{\beta}]=(\beta-\alpha)\sigma_{\alpha+\beta-1},\qquad\text{meaning that}\qquad[\ell\sigma_{\alpha},\ell\sigma_{\beta}]=(\beta-\alpha)\ell\sigma_{\alpha+\beta-1}.

We observe in particular that

(3.1)

\displaystyle{[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{3}]}

\displaystyle=3\sigma_{2},

\displaystyle{[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2}]}

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{1}

and furthermore, for any
\alpha\geq 3
, we have

[\sigma_{2},\sigma_{\alpha}]=(\alpha-2)\sigma_{\alpha+1}.

which means that starting with
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{3}
, we can obtain
\sigma_{\alpha}
of any order. In summary,

\spn\{\ell\sigma_{\alpha}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\}_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}.

The proof is now completed from the fact that
\sigma_{0}
,
\sigma_{1}
,
\dots
,
\sigma_{n-1}
is linearly independent to see the latter part, we observe that in the basis
\partial^{1}
,
\dots
,
\partial^{n}
we can identify the mentioned vector fields as the rows of
the Vandermonde matrix

(3.2)

W_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&&x_{n}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
(x_{1})^{n-1}&(x_{2})^{n-1}&\cdots&(x_{n})^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}

which has well-known determinant

(3.3)

\mathrm{det}(W_{x})=\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{i}-x_{j})\neq 0

as all of our points are distinct. It follows that
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}=T_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}\mathcal{M}
for any point and the proof is complete.
∎

3.2.
Dimension two

We can follow the same procedure for the case
d=2
.

Theorem 3.2

(Controllability for
d=2
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the
n
-landmark manifold. If
\sigma_{0,0}(x)=\partial_{x}
,
\sigma_{1,3}(x)=-y(3x^{2}-y^{2})\partial_{x}+x(x^{2}-3y^{2})\partial_{y}
and

\mathcal{E}=\spn\{\ell\sigma_{0},\ell\sigma_{1,3}\}\subseteq T\mathcal{M},

then we have complete controllability with respect to
\mathcal{E}
independent of
n
.

In order to generalize our proof for dimension one to the case
d=2
, we will consider
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with coordinates
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
with
z=x+iy
. Its real tangent bundle
T\mathbb{C}=\spn_{\mathbb{R}}\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y}\}
, while its complexified bundle
T^{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}=\coprod_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}\otimes T_{z}\mathbb{C}=\spn_{\mathbb{C}}\{\partial_{z},\partial_{\bar{z}}\}
,
where

\partial:=\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}\right),\qquad\bar{\partial}=\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}\right).

Write
T^{1,0}\mathbb{C}=\spn_{\mathbb{C}}\{\partial\}
for the holomorphic tangent bundle. We denote its complex sections by

\Gamma(T^{1,0}\mathbb{C})=\{f(z)\partial\,:\,f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})\}.

We will use the following property.

Lemma 3.3
.

The map
\mathscr{F}:\Gamma(T\mathbb{C})\to\Gamma(T^{1,0}\mathbb{C})
given by

\mathscr{F}(f_{0}\partial_{x}+f_{1}\partial_{y})=(f_{0}+if_{1})\partial,\qquad f_{0},f_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{R}).

is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear isomorphism with inverse
\mathscr{F}^{-1}(f\partial)=2\Re(f\partial)=(\Re f)\partial_{x}+(\Im f)\partial_{y}
for any
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
. Moreover, assume that we have real vector fields
\sigma=\sigma^{x}\partial_{x}+\sigma^{y}\partial_{y}
and
\tau=\tau^{x}\partial_{x}+\tau^{y}\partial_{y}
, such that

\sigma^{z}=\sigma^{x}+i\sigma^{y},\qquad\tau^{z}=\tau^{x}+i\tau^{y}

are holomorphic functions. Then

[\mathscr{F}\sigma,\mathscr{F}\tau]=\mathscr{F}[\sigma,\tau].

Proof.

Define
\bar{\mathscr{F}}\sigma=\bar{\sigma}^{z}
. We observe that for the vector field
\sigma
, we have
\sigma=\mathscr{F}\sigma+\bar{\mathscr{F}}\sigma=\sigma^{z}\partial+\bar{\sigma}^{z}\bar{\partial}
and similarly for
\tau
. Note that since
\sigma^{z}
and
\tau^{z}
are holomorphic, we have that
\bar{\partial}\sigma^{z}=\bar{\partial}\tau^{z}=\partial\bar{\sigma}^{z}=\partial\bar{\tau}^{z}=0
.

(3.4)

\displaystyle[\sigma,\tau]

\displaystyle=\mathscr{F}[\sigma,\tau]+\bar{\mathscr{F}}[\sigma,\tau]=[\sigma^{z}\partial+\bar{\sigma}^{z}\bar{\partial},\tau^{z}\partial+\bar{\tau}^{z}\bar{\partial}]

(3.5)

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{z}\partial\tau^{z}-\tau^{z}\partial\sigma^{z})\partial+\overline{(\sigma^{z}\partial\tau^{z}-\tau^{z}\partial\sigma^{z})\partial}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=[\mathscr{F}\sigma,\mathscr{F}\tau]+[\bar{\mathscr{F}}\sigma,\bar{\mathscr{F}}\tau].

Projecting to
T^{1,0}\mathbb{C}
, we have the result.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

For
\alpha=0,1,2\dots
, define
\sigma_{0,\alpha}
and
\sigma_{1,\alpha}
respectively by

\mathscr{F}\sigma_{0,\alpha}=z^{\alpha}\partial\qquad\mathscr{F}\sigma_{1,\alpha}=iz^{\alpha}\partial,

Since
\mathscr{F}\sigma_{0,0}=\partial
and
\mathscr{F}\sigma_{1,3}=iz^{3}\partial
, we note that

(3.7)

\displaystyle{[\partial,iz^{3}\partial]}

\displaystyle=i3z^{2}\partial,

\displaystyle{[\partial,iz^{2}\partial]}

\displaystyle=2iz\partial

(3.8)

\displaystyle{[\partial,iz\partial]}

\displaystyle=i\partial,

\displaystyle{[i\partial,iz^{3}\partial]}

\displaystyle=-3z^{2}\partial

(3.9)

\displaystyle{[iz\partial,iz^{3}\partial]}

\displaystyle=-z^{3}\partial,

Finally, using that
[z^{2}\alpha,z^{\alpha}\partial]=(\alpha-2)z^{\alpha+1}\partial
and
[z^{2}\alpha,iz^{\alpha}\partial]=(\alpha-2)iz^{\alpha+1}\partial
, we can generate any vector field of the form
z^{\alpha}\partial
and
iz^{\alpha}\partial
with iterated brackets. It follows that

\spn\{\ell\sigma_{0,\alpha}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n}),\sigma_{1,\alpha}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\}_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}\}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}}.

Now to show that these vector fields are all linearly independent, let
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})
and
\GL(n,\mathbb{R})
be the general linear group of respectively the complex numbers and the real numbers, which are the groups consisting of
n\times n
invertible matrices. Recall that we have an injective group homomorphism
\GL(n,\mathbb{C})\to\GL(2n,\mathbb{R})
by sending each complex entry
a_{jk}
to a
2\times 2
real block

a_{jk}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\Re(a_{jk})&\Im(a_{jk})\\
-\Im(a_{jk})&\Re(a_{jk})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Re(a_{jk})&\Im(a_{jk})\\
\Re(ia_{jk})&\Im(ia_{jk})\end{pmatrix}.

If we write
\ell\sigma_{0,1},\ell\sigma_{1,1},\dots,\ell\sigma_{0,n-1},\sigma_{0,n-1}
as rows in an
2n\times 2n
matrix in the basis
\partial^{1}_{x},\partial^{1}_{y},\dots,\partial^{n}_{x},\partial^{n}_{y}
, it is clear that this is just the image of the
n\times n
complex matrix

(3.10)

W_{z}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
z_{1}&z_{2}&&z_{n}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
(z_{1})^{n-1}&(z_{2})^{n-1}&\cdots&(z_{n})^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}

which is again invertible using the Vandermonde determinant. The result follows.
∎

4.
High Dimensional Cases

Having dealt with the special case of dimension one and two, we will give a general approach for all dimensions higher than
3
.
For clarity, we reserve the Latin
i
,
j
for indices and the Greek
\alpha
,
\beta
for exponents. Furthermore, in what follows, it will be convenient to denote coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
modulo
d
, such that for example
x^{0}=x^{d}
and
\partial_{x^{d+1}}=\partial_{d+1}=\partial_{1}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 3
,

(4.1)

\Phi=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x^{j})^{2}\right)x^{k}\partial_{k+1}=\|x\|^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}x^{k}\partial_{k+1}\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{d})

Write
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and define

\spn\{\ell\partial_{1},\ell\Phi\}=\mathcal{E}\subseteq T\mathcal{M}.

then we have complete controllability with respect to
\mathcal{E}
independent of
n
.

Let
\SO(d)
be the space of
d\times d
orthogonal matrices with determinant 1, representing a rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can let
A\in\SO(d)
act on
\mathcal{M}
by
A\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(Ax_{1},\dots,Ax^{d})
. We first make the following observation.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, there is an
A\in\SO(d)
such that
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=A\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
such that

y_{j}^{i}\neq y_{j}^{i_{2}},\qquad i\neq i_{2},\quad j=1,\dots,n,\quad,i,i_{2}=1,\dots,d.

Proof.

Let us first remark the following. If
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
be a finite collection of distinct points
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
p_{i}=(p_{i}^{x},p_{i}^{y})
, then there are only finite angles for which there exists
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
, such that
p_{i_{1}}^{x}=p_{i_{2}}^{x}
or
p_{i_{1}}^{y}=p_{i_{2}}^{y}
. Hence, we can always find an angle to rotate such that all coordinates are distinct.

Let
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be any collection of
n
-distinct points
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
1\leq k<l\leq d
, let
\pi^{k,l}:
denote the projection from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the
x^{k}x^{l}
plane. Order these planes lexicographically. Then starting with the
x^{1}x^{2}
planes, we consider the projections
(\pi^{1,2}(x_{1}),\dots,\pi^{1,2}(x_{n}))
, and rotate these points so that all distinct points have distinct coordinates. Define
A^{1,2}
as the corresponding rotation in the
x^{1}x^{2}
-plane, leaving the other coordinates unchanged. We define
(x_{2,1},\dots,x_{2,n})=A^{1,2}\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Project these points in the
x^{1}x^{3}
-plane and proceed in the same manner. In the end we obtain

(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=A^{d-1,d}A^{d-2,d}A^{d-2,d-1}\cdots A^{1,3}A^{1,2}\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).

Now for any
i_{1},i_{2}=1,\dots,d
, there exists at least one
l
such that
x_{i_{1}}\neq x_{i_{2}}^{l}
. By the construction we have that
y_{i_{1}}
and
y_{i_{2}}
must have distinct coordinates when projected to any
x^{k}x^{l}
-plane,
k\neq l
. However, this implies that
y_{i_{1}}
and
y_{i_{2}}
have all different coordinates. The result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
j,k=1,\dots,d
and any positive integer
\alpha=0,1,2,3,\dots
, define vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\sigma_{k,\alpha,j}(x)=(x^{k})^{\alpha}\partial_{j}.

Then for any point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, we have

\spn\left\{\ell\sigma_{k,\alpha,j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}k,j=1,2,\dots,d\\
\alpha=0,1,\dots,n-1\end{array}\right\}=T_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}\mathcal{M}.

Proof.

Consider first the case when
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
are such that no point has identical first coordinate. Then the vector fields

\ell\sigma_{1,0,j},\dots,\ell\sigma_{1,n-1,j}

are all linearly independent, since in the basis
\partial^{1}_{j},\partial^{2}_{j},\dots,\partial_{j}^{n}
, we can write these vector fields as the rows of the matrix

(4.2)

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
x^{1}_{1}&x^{1}_{2}&&x^{1}_{n}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
(x^{1}_{1})^{n-1}&(x^{1}_{2})^{n-1}&\cdots&(x^{1}_{n})^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}

which again is a Vandermonde matrix which is non-zero from our assumption on the points
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. It follows that

\spn\left\{\ell\sigma_{1,\alpha,j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}j=1,2,\dots,d\\
\alpha=0,1,\dots,n-1\end{array}\right\}=\spn\{\partial_{j}^{i}|_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}\colon i,j=1,\dots,d\}.

For a general point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, we can find an
A\in\SO(d)
such that
A\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
have all different coordinates by
Lemma

4.2
. Observe that

\spn\left\{\sigma_{k,\alpha,j}(A\cdot)\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}k,j=1,2,\dots,d\\
\alpha=0,1,\dots,n-1\end{array}\right\}=\spn\left\{\sigma_{k,\alpha,j}\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}k,j=1,2,\dots,d\\
\alpha=0,1,\dots,n-1\end{array}\right\}.

Hence, we can complete the proof with
\spn\left\{\ell\sigma_{k,\alpha,j}(A\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))\right\}
as above, which gives the result.

∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Observe that

(4.3)

\displaystyle[\partial_{k},\Phi]

\displaystyle=\left(3(x^{k})^{2}+\sum_{l\neq k}(x^{l})^{2}\right)\partial_{i+1}+\sum_{l\neq k}2x^{k}x^{l}\partial_{k+1},

(4.4)

\displaystyle[\partial_{k},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]

\displaystyle=6x^{k}\partial_{k+1}+\sum_{l\neq k}2x^{l}\partial_{l+1},

(4.5)

\displaystyle[\partial_{k},[\partial_{k},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]]

\displaystyle=6\partial_{k+1}.

Therefore, starting with
\partial_{1}
, it follows that we can inductively generate all degree 0 monomials
\partial_{k}
.

In degree one, observe that for
j,j+1\neq k

(4.6)

\displaystyle[\partial_{j},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]

\displaystyle=2(x^{j}\partial_{k+1}+x^{k}\partial_{j+1})

(4.7)

\displaystyle[[\partial_{j},[\partial_{k},\Phi]],[\partial_{j},[\partial_{k+1},\Phi]]]

\displaystyle=4x^{j}\partial_{j+1}

giving us all monomials of the form
x^{j}\partial_{j+1}
. We can then proceed inductively for all
l+1\neq j
by

(4.8)

[x^{j}\partial_{l},x^{l}\partial_{l+1}]=x^{j}\partial_{l+1}

giving all degree one monomials
x^{j}\partial_{l},j\neq l
. Finally,

(4.9)

[\partial_{k-1},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]=2(x^{k-1}\partial_{k+1}+x^{k}\partial_{k})

and so by linear combination with previously acquired terms we have all degree one monomials
x^{j}\partial_{k}
.

In degree two, we consider the brackets

(4.10)

\displaystyle[x^{k}\partial_{k},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]

\displaystyle=6(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{k+1}+\sum_{l\neq k,k-1}2x^{k}x^{l}\partial_{l+1}

(4.11)

\displaystyle[x^{k}\partial_{k},[x^{k}\partial_{k},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]]

\displaystyle=12(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{k+1}+\sum_{l\neq k,k-1}2x^{k}x^{l}\partial_{l+1}

which we can sum to generate all terms of the form
(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{k+1}
. Then for
j\neq k+1
it holds

(4.12)

\displaystyle[x^{j}\partial_{i},(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{k+1}]

\displaystyle=2x^{k}x^{j}\partial_{k+1}

(4.13)

\displaystyle[x^{j}\partial_{k},[x^{j}\partial_{k},(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{k+1}]]

\displaystyle=2(x^{j})^{2}\partial_{k+1}

giving us all monomials
(x^{j})^{2}\partial_{i}
with
k\neq j
. Finally, we iterate the bracket

(4.14)

\displaystyle[x^{k+1}\partial_{k+1},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]

\displaystyle=\left((x^{k+1})^{2}-3(x^{k})^{2}-\sum_{l\neq k,k+1}(x^{l})^{2}\right)\partial_{k+1}+2x^{k}x^{k+1}\partial_{k+2}

(4.15)

\displaystyle[x^{k+1}\partial_{k+1},[x^{k+1}\partial_{k+1},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]

\displaystyle=\left((x^{k+1})^{2}+3(x^{k})^{2}+\sum_{l\neq k,k+1}(x^{l})^{2}\right)\partial_{k+1}+2x^{k}x^{k+1}\partial_{k+2}

and so

(4.17)

[x^{k+1}\partial_{k+1},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]+[x^{k+1}\partial_{k+1},[x^{k+1}\partial_{k+1},[\partial_{k},\Phi]]-2[x^{k}\partial_{k+1},(x^{k+1})^{2}\partial_{k+2}]=2(x^{k+1})^{2}\partial_{k+1}

giving all degree two monomials
(x^{j})^{2}\partial_{k}
.

In degree three it follows by direct computation that

(4.18)

[x^{k+1}\partial_{i},[x^{k+1}\partial_{k},[x^{k+1}\partial_{k},\Phi]]]=6(x^{k+1})^{3}\partial_{k+1}-18(x^{k+1})^{2}x^{k}\partial_{k}

and for
k\neq j

(4.19)

\displaystyle[(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{l},(x^{l})^{2}\partial_{j}]

\displaystyle=2(x^{k})^{2}x^{l}\partial_{j}

(4.20)

\displaystyle[x^{k}\partial_{l},[(x^{k}i)^{2}\partial_{l},(x^{l})^{2}\partial_{j}]

\displaystyle=2(x^{k})^{3}\partial_{j}

in particular

(4.21)

[x^{k+1}\partial_{i},[x^{k+1}\partial_{i},[x^{k+1}\partial_{k},\Phi]]]+6[(x^{k+1})^{2}\partial_{k},(x^{k})^{2}\partial_{k}]=6(x^{k+1})^{3}\partial_{k+1}

which together give all pure degree three monomials
(x^{j})^{3}\partial_{k}
.

Finally, for
\alpha\geq 3
it holds that

(4.22)

[(x^{i})^{2}\partial_{i},(x^{i})^{\alpha}\partial_{j}]=\begin{cases}\alpha(x^{i})^{\alpha+1}\partial_{j}&i\neq j\\
(\alpha-2)(x^{i})^{\alpha+1}\partial_{i}&i=j\\
\end{cases}

from which we finally generate all pure monomials
(x^{j})^{\alpha}\partial_{i}
. The result now follows from
Lemma

4.3
.
∎
