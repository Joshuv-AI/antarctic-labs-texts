William Dallaporta

Abstract

Genus Theory is a classical feature of integral binary quadratic forms. Using the author’s generalization of the well-known correspondence between quadratic form classes and ideal classes of quadratic algebras, we extend it to the case when quadratic forms are twisted and have coefficients in any PID
R
. When
{R=\mathbb{K}[X]}
, we show that the Genus Theory map is the quadratic form version of the
2
-descent map on a certain hyperelliptic curve. As an application, we make a contribution to a question of Agboola and Pappas regarding a specialization problem of divisor classes on hyperelliptic curves. Under suitable assumptions, we prove that the set of non-trivial specializations has density
1
.

Introduction

It has been well-known since the work of Gauss in his
Disquisitiones Arithmeticae
that, given
{\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}}
, the set

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{equivalence~classes~of~primitive~binary~quadratic~forms}\\
ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}\text{~with~}a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}\text{~and~discriminant~}b^{2}-4ac=\Delta\end{array}\right\}

can be endowed with a group structure, whose group operation is called the composition law (see Section
2
for the definitions).

Before going further, we must take care which notion of equivalence class we use. Over
\mathbb{Z}
, the natural action of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on quadratic forms is usually considered. In that setting, and when
\Delta
is a negative integer, it is a classical fact that the above group (restricted to classes of positive definite quadratic forms) is isomorphic to the Picard group of the quadratic
\mathbb{Z}
-algebra of discriminant
\Delta
(see
[
Cox13
, Theorem 7.7]
for a modern exposition). There have been numerous generalizations of this group structure and of this correspondence to other rings than
\mathbb{Z}
, possibly with a different action (see for example
[
Tow80
]
with
\SL_{2}
or
[
Kne82
]
with
\gl_{2}
). More recently, Wood gave a set-theoretical bijection over an arbitrary base scheme
[
Woo11
]
, and the present author derived from her work the sought group isomorphism, when
2
is not a zero divisor on the base scheme
[
Dal21
]
. In her work, Wood pointed out the importance of the
twisted action
of
\gl_{2}
, which we denote by
\gl_{2}^{tw}
(see Definition
2.2
). This is the only action we consider through this article, except in Subsection
3.2
, where we make the link with the classical
\SL_{2}
action over
\mathbb{Z}

This general group isomorphism from
[
Dal21
]
is stated over any base scheme
S

{S=\spec(R)}
, where
R
is an integral domain of characteristic different from
2
such that every locally free
R

2.4
), the set of (twisted-)equivalence classes of primitive binary quadratic forms with coefficients in
R
and with discriminant
{\Delta\in R}
is a group, which we denote by
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
(Proposition
2.4
). The neutral element of
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
is called the
principal form class
.

Given a primitive binary quadratic form, it is natural to wonder if it lies in the principal form class or not. A classical feature of quadratic forms over
\mathbb{Z}
is
Genus Theory
, which partially answers this question and which is the main topic of this paper. Roughly speaking, the operation associating a class of quadratic forms to its set of values modulo its discriminant

In this article, we extend Genus Theory (for the twisted action

The fact that our base ring is a principal ideal domain is heavily used to find an adequate representative of a given class of quadratic forms (Lemma

Regarding hyperelliptic curves, several results have already been established about non-trivial specializations:

•

when
\mathcal{C}
is an elliptic curve over

\mathbb{Q}
whose order is unbounded as

[

•

when
{\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}}
and
I

in imaginary quadratic extensions of
\mathbb{Q}

[
GL12

•

{\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}}
and
I
has infinite order, Gillibert showed that there exist infinitely many negative integers

is a non-trivial ideal class of the order

Let us assume that
{\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}}
for the time being. Given a non-trivial ideal class

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}

Numerical experiments show that, depending on the ideal class

As in the work of Gillibert, we use Wood’s bijection between invertible ideal classes of quadratic algebras and equivalence classes of primitive binary quadratic forms as described in
[
Dal21

. In this setting, the ideal class
I

{q(x,y)=ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}}

The presentation of Genus Theory in this article requires us to work over a principal ideal domain. As
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}

is the

AP00
]
. More precisely, following
[
Gil21
, §2.1]
, there exists a smooth projective model
{\overline{\mathcal{W}}\longrightarrow\spec(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}})}
of
\mathcal{C}
such that, set-theoretically,

\overline{\mathcal{W}}=\spec\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\right)\cup\overline{\left\{\infty\right\}}

together with an isomorphism
{\pic\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\right)\simeq\pic^{0}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})}
, where
\overline{\left\{\infty\right\}}
is the scheme-theoretic closure of the point at infinity of
\mathcal{C}
. The result of Theorem
1.4
implies that a degree
0
line bundle on
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
which is not a square has infinitely many non-trivial specializations over suitable quadratic
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
-orders.

Notations.

All through this paper, the rings we consider are commutative and endowed with a multiplicative identity denoted by
1
. If
R
is a ring,
R^{\times}
denotes its group of units. Given
{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\in R}
, the ideal generated by
{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}}
is denoted by
{\left\langle r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\right\rangle}
. If
G
is a group, then
G^{\square}
denotes its subgroup of squares (thinking of the group law multiplicatively).

Given two integers
{a<b}
, we denote by
{\llbracket a,b\rrbracket}
the set of integers
n
such that
{a\leq n\leq b}
.

If
T
is a scheme, we denote by
\pic(T)
the Picard group of
T
. When
T
is Noetherian and reduced, we shall identify
\pic(T)
with the group of Cartier divisors modulo linear equivalence. When
R
is a domain, we write by abuse of notations
\pic(R)
instead of
\pic(\spec(R))
, which we also identify with the group of invertible fractional ideals modulo principal ones.

We refer the reader to Definition
2.2
and Proposition
2.4
for the meaning of
\gl_{2}^{tw}
and of
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
respectively.

2
Binary quadratic forms and Picard groups of quadratic algebras

The kind of rings
R
we consider in this paper are mostly of the form
R^{\prime}
or
R^{\prime}[X]
with
R^{\prime}
a principal ideal domain of characteristic different from
2
. An important property they share is the fact that every locally free
R
-module of finite rank must be free, according to
[
Ses58
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring such that every locally free
R
-module of finite rank is free. A (binary)
quadratic form

q
over
R
is a homogeneous degree
2
polynomial in
R[x,y]
. It is of the form
{ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}}
for some
{a,b,c\in R}
, and is denoted by
[a,b,c]
. It is called
primitive
if the ideal generated by
a,b,c
in
R
is the unit ideal. Its
discriminant
is the quantity
{\Delta:=b^{2}-4ac\in R}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\in\gl_{2}(R)}
. Following
[
Woo11
]
, we define the
twisted action of
\gl_{2}(R)
over the set of quadratic forms via
{M\cdot q:=\frac{1}{\det(M)}(q\circ M)}
, that is

\left(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\cdot q\right)(x,y):=\frac{1}{\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma}q(\alpha x+\beta y,\gamma x+\delta y)\hskip 28.45274pt\forall~x,y\in R.

We denote this action by
\gl_{2}^{tw}
. Two quadratic forms
q
and
q^{\prime}
are
\gl_{2}^{tw}
-equivalent
(
equivalent
for short) if there exists
M\in\gl_{2}(R)
such that
{q^{\prime}=M\cdot q}
. In the following, a
class of quadratic forms

[a,b,c]
refers to the equivalence class of the quadratic form
[a,b,c]
.

Remark 2.3
.

With the same notations, if
{q=[a,b,c]}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\cdot[a,b,c]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma}[a\alpha^{2}+b\alpha\gamma+c\gamma^{2},b(\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma)+2(a\alpha\beta+c\gamma\delta),a\beta^{2}+b\beta\delta+c\delta^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma}[q(\alpha,\gamma),q(\alpha+\beta,\gamma+\delta)-q(\alpha,\gamma)-q(\beta,\delta),q(\beta,\delta)].

We recall the main link between quadratic forms and ideals in our setting (
[
Dal21
, Corollary 3.25]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
R
be an integral domain of characteristic different from
2
such that every locally free
R
-module of finite rank is free. Let
{\Delta\in R}
be such that the equation
{\Delta\equiv x^{2}\pmod{4R}}
has a unique solution
x
modulo
2R
, and let
\pi
be any lift of
x
to
R
. Denote by
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
the set of
\gl_{2}^{tw}
-equivalence classes of primitive quadratic forms over
R
with discriminant
\Delta
. Then we have a bijection

\begin{aligned} \cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)&\overset{1\colon 1}{\longleftrightarrow}\pic\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{R[\omega]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle\omega^{2}+\pi\omega-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{R[\omega]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle\omega^{2}+\pi\omega-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{R[\omega]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle\omega^{2}+\pi\omega-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R[\omega]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle\omega^{2}+\pi\omega-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\right\rangle}$}}}\right)\\
\bigg[[a,b,c]\text{~with~}a\neq 0\bigg]&\longmapsto\bigg[\langle\omega+\frac{\pi-b}{2},a\rangle\bigg]\end{aligned}.

(2.5)

This allows us to endow
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
with a group structure, whose operation
\ast
is called the
composition law
.

Remark 2.6
.

In general, one must care about the existence of different solutions
{x\pmod{2R}}
of the equation
{\Delta\equiv x^{2}\pmod{4R}}
in Proposition
2.4
, for instance when
{R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{8}]}
(
[
Dal21
, Example 2.28]
). The value of such an
x
modulo
2R
is called the
parity
and is an invariant of our quadratic forms. For the rings
R
we shall consider, the parity is completely determined by the discriminant
\Delta
, that is, there is a unique solution
{x\pmod{2R}}
. Indeed, if
R
is a PID, or satisfies either the fact that
2
is a unit or the fact that
\left\langle 2\right\rangle
is a prime ideal, as will always be the case in the following, then uniqueness is guaranteed by
[
Dal21
, Proposition 2.26]
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
principal form class
is the neutral element of
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
. A representative of this class is
{[1,\pi,-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}]}
, for every
{\pi\in R}
such that
{\Delta\equiv\pi^{2}\pmod{4R}}
.

Composition of quadratic forms classes over
\mathbb{Z}
has been well-known since Gauss, and has already been extended to other rings. For example, Cantor described it over
{R=\mathbb{K}[X]}
for every field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic different from
2
(
[
Can87
, §3]
). At least over
\mathbb{Z}
, there exist various formulae to compute the composition of two given quadratic forms. We extend one such formula to the case when
R
is a PID, without any particular difficulty. However, our formula requires a coprimality condition on the first coefficients of the quadratic forms, which will be enough for our purpose and easily fulfilled (Lemma
3.4
).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
R
be a PID with
{2\neq 0}
. Let
{q_{1}=[a_{1},b_{1},c_{1}],q_{2}=[a_{2},b_{2},c_{2}]\in\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)}
, and assume that
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are nonzero and coprime. Let
{a_{1}r_{1}+a_{2}r_{2}=1}
be a Bézout relation. Then, the composition
{q_{1}\ast q_{2}}
of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
is the class of
{[a_{1}a_{2},B,-\frac{\Delta-B^{2}}{4a_{1}a_{2}}]}
, where
{B=a_{1}r_{1}b_{2}+a_{2}r_{2}b_{1}}
.

Remark 2.9
.

When
{R=\mathbb{Z}}
, the quadratic form
{[a_{1}a_{2},B,-\frac{\Delta-B^{2}}{4a_{1}a_{2}}]}
with
B
as above is known as the
Dirichlet composition
of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
(
[
Cox13
, (3.7)]
).

Proof.

Denote
{\alpha_{i}:=\frac{\pi-b_{i}}{2}}
, where
{\pi\in R}
is any element such that
{\Delta\equiv\pi^{2}\pmod{4R}}
. By definition of
\ast
from Proposition
2.4
, using the same notations,
{q_{1}\ast q_{2}}
corresponds to the product

\left\langle a_{1},\omega+\alpha_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle a_{2},\omega+\alpha_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle a_{1}a_{2},a_{1}(\omega+\alpha_{2}),a_{2}(\omega+\alpha_{1}),-\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}}{2}\omega+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\right\rangle.

Observe the relation

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&a_{2}&-a_{1}&0\\
0&r_{1}\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}}{2}&r_{2}\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}}{2}&1\\
0&-r_{1}&-r_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}a_{2}\\
a_{1}(\omega+\alpha_{2})\\
a_{2}(\omega+\alpha_{1})\\
-\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}}{2}\omega+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}a_{2}\\
a_{1}a_{2}\frac{b_{1}-b_{2}}{2}\\
-a_{1}a_{2}(r_{2}c_{1}+r_{1}c_{2})\\
-\omega-\frac{\pi-B}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Since the square matrix on the left hand side has determinant
1
, the above ideal is the same as the one spanned by the elements of the vector on the right hand side. Therefore,

\left\langle a_{1},\omega+\alpha_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle a_{2},\omega+\alpha_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle a_{1}a_{2},\omega+\frac{\pi-B}{2}\right\rangle.

The corresponding quadratic form in bijection (
2.5
) is the class of
{[a_{1}a_{2},B,-\frac{\Delta-B^{2}}{4a_{1}a_{2}}]}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

One can also prove Proposition
2.8
with a composition formula in Gauss’ style, checking that the relation

(a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+b_{1}x_{1}y_{1}+c_{1}y_{1}^{2})(a_{2}x_{2}^{2}+b_{2}x_{2}y_{2}+c_{2}y_{2}^{2})=a_{1}a_{2}X^{2}+BXY+\frac{B^{2}-\Delta}{4a_{1}a_{2}}Y^{2}

(2.11)

is true, where

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=x_{1}x_{2}+r_{2}\frac{b_{2}-b_{1}}{2}x_{1}y_{2}+r_{1}\frac{b_{1}-b_{2}}{2}x_{2}y_{1}-(r_{2}c_{1}+r_{1}c_{2})y_{1}y_{2}

\displaystyle\text{and}~~~~Y

\displaystyle=a_{1}x_{1}y_{2}+a_{2}x_{2}y_{1}+\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}}{2}y_{1}y_{2}.

Remark 2.12
.

If
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are not supposed to be coprime, we are still able to give a composition algorithm over a PID, but the gcd of
a_{1},a_{2}
and
\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}}{2}
shows up and
B
must be modified (see
[
Bue89
, Theorem 4.10]
).

3
Genus theory over a PID

Throughout this Section,
R
is a PID of characteristic different from
2
. In this paper, Genus Theory will always refer to the construction of a particular group homomorphism from
\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)
to a quotient of
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
. This homomorphism essentially maps a quadratic form
q
to its set of values modulo the discriminant
\Delta
. This construction was introduced by Gauss over
\mathbb{Z}
, and we shall check that it extends to arbitrary PIDs.

All the techniques of Subsection
3.1
are classical, mainly adapted from the case of
\mathbb{Z}
treated in
[
Cox13
, §1-§3]
. However, we must keep in mind a difference about the quadratic forms we consider: Genus Theory over
\mathbb{Z}
is usually done with
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalence classes, while we are dealing with
\gl_{2}^{tw}
-ones. We describe the connection between these two cases in Subsection
3.2
.

3.1
The general case

Definition 3.1
.

Let
q
be a primitive quadratic form of discriminant
{\Delta\in R}
. Its
set of values in
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
is given by

\val_{\Delta}(q):=\left\{q(x,y)\enskip\middle|\enskip x,y\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right\}\cap\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}.

The
set of values in
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
of the class of
q
is
{H_{q}:=\displaystyle{\bigcup_{q^{\prime}\sim q}\val_{\Delta}(q^{\prime})}}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Two equivalent quadratic forms represent the same values up to units of
R
, because of the twist by the determinant of the acting matrix. Therefore, for all
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)}
, we have
{H_{q}=R^{\times}\val_{\Delta}(q)}
, where by abuse of notations
R^{\times}
stands for its image by the canonical projection
{R\longrightarrow{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
H_{0}
be the set of values taken by the principal form class in
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
. Then
H_{0}
is a subgroup of
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
, containing the squares. Moreover, if
2
is invertible in
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}
, then
{H_{0}=R^{\times}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times\square}}
, where the exponent
□
denotes the subgroup of squares.

Proof.

As already noticed in Remark
3.2
,
{H_{0}=R^{\times}\val_{\Delta}(q_{0})}
where
{q_{0}=[1,\pi,-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}]}
is a representative of the principal form class as in Definition
2.7
. We focus on
\val_{\Delta}(q_{0})
.

It is a straightforward computation to check the following equation, which is a particular case of the composition formula (
2.11
):

\left(x_{1}^{2}+\pi x_{1}y_{1}-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}y_{1}^{2}\right)\left(x_{2}^{2}+\pi x_{2}y_{2}-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}y_{2}^{2}\right)=X^{2}+\pi XY-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}Y^{2}

for all
{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in R}
, where
{X:=x_{1}x_{2}+\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}y_{1}y_{2}}
and
{Y:=x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1}+\pi y_{1}y_{2}}
. This formula proves that
\val_{\Delta}(q_{0})
is stable under multiplication. Furthermore, it is also stable under inversion: if
{\alpha\in\val_{\Delta}(q_{0})}
, then there exist
{x,y\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}}
such that
{\alpha\equiv x^{2}+\pi xy-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}y^{2}\pmod{\Delta}}
, hence

\alpha^{-1}\equiv(x\alpha^{-1})^{2}+\pi(x\alpha^{-1})(y\alpha^{-1})-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}(y\alpha^{-1})^{2}\in\val_{\Delta}(q_{0}).

Therefore,
\val_{\Delta}(q_{0})
and
H_{0}
are indeed subgroups of
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
.

Clearly,
\val_{\Delta}(q_{0})
contains the squares since
{[1,\pi,-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}](x,0)=x^{2}}
for all
x
. If
2
is invertible modulo
\Delta
, then for all
{x,y\in R}
, we have

\left[1,\pi,-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}\right](x,y)\equiv\left(x+\frac{\pi}{2}y\right)^{2}\pmod{\Delta},

hence the result.
∎

The key point of most of the following results is the next Lemma, which makes heavy use of factorization and Bézout relations. Its main outcome for our purpose is the Bézout relation it induces.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)}
and let
{h\in R\setminus\left\{0\right\}}
. Then
q
is equivalent to some form
[a,b,c]
where
{a\neq 0}
is coprime to
h
.

Proof.

Denote
{q=[a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}]}
. First, note that if there exist
{x_{0},y_{0}\in R}
coprime such that
{q(x_{0},y_{0})=a}
, then
q
is equivalent to
[a,b,c]
for some
{b,c\in R}
. Indeed, if we find such
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, then there exist
{\beta,\delta\in R}
such that
{x_{0}\delta-y_{0}\beta=1}
(since
R
is a PID). By Remark
2.3
, we deduce that
{\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&\beta\\
y_{0}&\delta\end{pmatrix}\cdot[a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}]=[a,b,c]}
for some
{b,c\in R}
, where
a
is coprime to
h
.

As there is nothing to prove if
{h\in R^{\times}}
, we may assume that
h
is not a unit. Decompose it as a product of irreducibles:
{h=\underset{i}{\prod}p_{i}^{r_{i}}}
where
p_{i}
is prime and
{r_{i}\geq 1}
for all
i
. Since
q
is primitive, a given
p_{i}
cannot divide
{q(1,0)=a_{0}}
,
{q(0,1)=c_{0}}
and
{q(1,1)=a_{0}+b_{0}+c_{0}}
at the same time. Hence, for all
i
, there exist
x_{i},y_{i}
coprime elements of
R
such that
{q(x_{i},y_{i})\not\equiv 0\pmod{p_{i}}}
. We lift the pairs
{((x_{i},y_{i})\pmod{p_{i}})}
with the Chinese Remainder Theorem to some
{(x,y)\in R^{2}}
, and we set
{(x_{0},y_{0}):=\left(\frac{x}{\gcd(x,y)},\frac{y}{\gcd(x,y)}\right)}
. Then
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
are coprime, and
{a:=q(x_{0},y_{0})}
is nonzero and coprime to
h
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

We actually proved that every primitive quadratic form over
R
is
\SL_{2}(R)
-equivalent to one whose first coefficient is coprime to
h
.

Remark 3.6
.

Notice that
{[a,b,c]\sim[c,-b,a]}
via the matrix
{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\SL_{2}(R)}
. Hence,
q
is also equivalent to some form
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}]
where
c^{\prime}
is coprime to
h
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
{\Delta\in R\setminus\left\{0\right\}}
and let
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{R}(\Delta)}
. Denote by

its set of values in
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
, and by

the set of values of the principal form class. Then

is a coset of
H_{0}
in
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}

[a,b,c]
is a representative of
q
with
{a\neq 0}
coprime to
\Delta
, then
{H_{q}=a^{-1}H_{0}}
.

Proof.

We shall prove the last part of the statement, from which the result follows. As seen in Lemma
3.4
, there exists a representative
[a,b,c]
of the class
q
of quadratic forms such that
a
is nonzero and coprime to
\Delta
. Let
{x,y\in R}
, let
{\pi\in R}
such that
{\Delta\equiv\pi^{2}\pmod{4R}}
, then

\displaystyle ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv a^{-1}\left(ax+\frac{b-\pi}{2}y\right)^{2}+\pi xy+\left(c-a^{-1}\left(\frac{b-\pi}{2}\right)^{2}\right)y^{2}\pmod{\Delta}

\displaystyle\equiv a^{-1}\left(\left(ax+\frac{b-\pi}{2}y\right)^{2}+\pi\left(ax+\frac{b-\pi}{2}y\right)y\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+\left(-a^{-1}\pi\frac{b-\pi}{2}+c-a^{-1}\left(\frac{b-\pi}{2}\right)^{2}\right)y^{2}\pmod{\Delta}.

We compute the
y^{2}
-term: we have

\displaystyle-a^{-1}\pi\frac{b-\pi}{2}+c-a^{-1}\left(\frac{b-\pi}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{-2b\pi+2\pi^{2}+4ac-b^{2}+2b\pi-\pi^{2}}{4a}\pmod{\Delta}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\pi^{2}-\Delta}{4a}\pmod{\Delta}.

Thus, we have

ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}\equiv a^{-1}\left(X^{2}+\pi XY-\frac{\Delta-\pi^{2}}{4}Y^{2}\right)

where
{X:=ax+\frac{b-\pi}{2}y}
and
{Y:=y}
.

This being true for all
{x,y\in R}
, we infer that
{H_{q}\subseteq a^{-1}H_{0}}
. For the reverse inclusion, notice that
{\begin{pmatrix}ax+\frac{b-\pi}{2}y\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&\frac{b-\pi}{2}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}}
, and the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}a&\frac{b-\pi}{2}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
is invertible in
\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{R}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Delta R}$}}}\right)^{\times}
. Hence, for all

Thus,
{H_{q}=a^{-1}H_{0}}
, as desired.
∎

Remark 3.9

{\tilde{c}=\varepsilon\tilde{a}}

and
\tilde{c}

\alpha\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
{f\in\mathbb{K}[X]}
be a square-free monic polynomial of odd degree
{2g+1}
with
{g\geq 1}
. Let
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)}
, and let
[a,2b,c]
be a representative of
q
with
a
coprime to
f
(possible by Lemma
3.4
). Then we have

\lambda(\mathcal{D}(q))=\frac{(-1)^{\deg(a)}}{\lc(a)}aL^{\times\square},

where
\lc(a)
denotes the leading coefficient of
a
.

In particular, Diagram (
3.18
) is commutative.

Proof.

Write
{\dv(a)\cap\dv(Y-b)=\underset{i=1}{\overset{\deg(a)}{\sum}}(x_{i},y_{i})}
with
{a(x_{i})=0}
and
{b(x_{i})=y_{i}}
. If
{y_{i}=0}
, then
x_{i}
is both a root of
a
and
b
, hence a root of
{b^{2}-ac=f}
. Since
a
and
f
are coprime by assumption, this cannot happen, and
y_{i}
must be nonzero.

First, assume that
a
is an irreducible polynomial, and denote by
d
its degree. Since the points
{(x_{i},y_{i})}
appearing in
\mathcal{D}(q)
are such that
{a(x_{i})=0}
, we have

\dv(a)\cap\dv(Y-b)-d\infty=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i},y_{i})-d\infty=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}((x_{1},y_{1}))-d\infty,

where the
\sigma_{i}((x_{1},y_{1}))
are all the conjugates in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
over
\mathbb{K}
of
{(x_{1},y_{1})}
. Applying
[
Sch95
, Lemma 2.2]
, we get

\lambda(\mathcal{D}(q))=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}-X)\right)L^{\times\square}=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{\lc(a)}aL^{\times\square}.

In the general case, if
{\displaystyle{a=\prod_{i}a_{i}}}
is the decomposition of
a
into irreducible factors, then

\dv(a)\cap\dv(Y-b)-\deg(a)\infty=\sum_{i}\Big(\dv(a_{i})\cap\dv(Y-b)-\deg(a_{i})\infty\Big),

hence the result follows from the irreducible case.

To conclude, the diagram is commutative since

pr\circ\lambda\circ\mathcal{D}(q)=pr\left(\frac{(-1)^{\deg(a)}}{\lc(a)}aL^{\times\square}\right)=a\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}=\Psi(q),

hence the result
∎

Thus, the commutativity of Diagram (
3.18
) reveals a strong relation between Genus Theory over
\mathbb{K}[X]
and
2
-descent on hyperelliptic curves over
\mathbb{K}
, at least when
\deg(f)
is odd.

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
{f\in\mathbb{K}[X]}
be a square-free monic polynomial of odd degree
{2g+1}
with
{g\geq 1}
. Then the Genus map
\Psi
from (
3.15
) is injective.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is an isomorphism in Diagram (
3.18
), it is enough to show that
{pr\circ\lambda}
is injective. Let
{D\in\ker(pr\circ\lambda)}
. Then
{\lambda(D)=\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}
, and we can choose a representative
\varepsilon a^{2}
of
{\lambda(D)}
for some
{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}}
and some
{a\in L^{\times}}
.

According to
[
Sch95
, Theorem 1.1]
, the quantity
\varepsilon a^{2}
is in the kernel of the
norm

{N\colon{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{L^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{L^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{L^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{L^{\times\square}}$}}}\longrightarrow{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times\square}}$}}}}
, which is the restriction of the usual norm
{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\overline{\mathbb{K}}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\overline{\mathbb{K}}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.83888pt}{$\scriptstyle{\overline{\mathbb{K}}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.83888pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\overline{\mathbb{K}}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\simeq\overline{\mathbb{K}}^{2g+1}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{K}}}
given by
{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2g+1})\mapsto\underset{i=1}{\overset{2g+1}{\prod}}\alpha_{i}}
. We have
{N(\varepsilon a^{2})=N(\varepsilon)N(a)^{2}=N(\varepsilon)}
, since squares are in the kernel of
N
. As
{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{K}}
, we get
{N(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2g+1}}
, which is in the same class as
\varepsilon
modulo the squares. From all of this we deduce that
\varepsilon
must be a square, and
{\lambda(D)=L^{\times\square}}
, meaning that
{D\in\ker(\lambda)}
. But
\lambda
is injective by
[
Sch95
, Theorem 1.2]
, hence
{D=0}
and
{pr\circ\lambda}
is injective, as desired.
∎

.

4.1

such that the “specialization” of

is a non-trivial ideal class in

Example 4.1

. This ideal

is not principal in
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-n^{3}+n-9\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{Z}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-n^{3}+n-9\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{Z}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-n^{3}+n-9\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{Z}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-n^{3}+n-9\right\rangle}$}}}

This problem can be restated in terms of quadratic forms, using Proposition

. For which
{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
can we say that the specialized integral quadratic form
{q(n):=[n,6,-n^{2}+1]}
of discriminant
{4n^{3}-4n+36}
is not
\gl_{2}^{tw}
-equivalent to the principal form
{q_{0}(n):=[1,0,-n^{3}+n-9]}

The theory of reduced quadratic forms gives algorithms to compute whether a given quadratic form (of nonsquare discriminant) is equivalent to the principal form or not (see
e.g.

, §5.4.2, 5.6.1]
). Using them, we look for simple patterns in the distribution of non-trivial specializations. Figure

summarizes the data obtained for integers

is not equivalent to

We observe that the second column in Figure

does not contain any red cell. This leads us to conjecture that when
{n\equiv 2\pmod{5}}
, then
q(n)
is never equivalent to
q_{0}(n)
. Let us show this: when
{n\equiv 2\pmod{5}}
, we have
{f(n)\equiv 2^{3}-2+9\equiv 0\pmod{5}}

{q(n)\equiv[2,1,-3]\pmod{5}}
, and
{q_{0}(n)\equiv[1,0,0]\pmod{5}}

were equivalent to

{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}

Thus, in this example, we have found an infinite family of non-trivial specializations of

.

Our goal is to prove that such congruence classes exist in general. As stated in Theorem
4.14
, Genus Theory gives a way to produce such classes. Although this process is constructive, and based on the same arguments as in Example
4.1
, it relies on certain properties of the rings of integers of the fields generated by the roots of

. Providing effective congruence classes would certainly require some additional work.

Let us come back to the general case, over a number field
\mathbb{K}
. For technical purposes, we are led to invert a suitable set of places. For
\mathcal{S}
a finite set of nonzero prime ideals of

where
\nu_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)
is the
\mathfrak{p}
-adic valuation of
x
. Then, for a choice of
\mathcal{S}
that we will make precise soon, we modify the problem as follows: given a non-trivial ideal class
I
in
\pic\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\right)
, we look for
{n\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
such that
I
is non-trivial in
\pic\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(n)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(n)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(n)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(n)\right\rangle}$}}}\right)
after specialization at
{X=n}
.

The following Proposition tells us what set
\mathcal{S}
we should consider in order to take advantage of Genus Theory over
\mathbb{K}[X]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
{f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}[X]}
be a square-free monic polynomial of odd degree at least
3
and let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite set of nonzero prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
. The restriction to the generic fibre induces a homomorphism of abelian groups

\theta\colon\pic\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\right)\longrightarrow\pic\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\right).

Moreover,

\bullet

if
\mathcal{S}
contains all the prime ideals dividing
2\disc(f)
, then
\theta
is surjective;

\bullet

if
\mathcal{S}
is such that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
is a PID, then
\theta
is injective.

Proof.

The map
\theta
corresponds to the restriction of a given divisor on
{\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}=\spec\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\right)}
to the generic fibre. The main steps of the proof are extracted from the first part of the proof of
[
Gil21
, Lemma 2.4]
, which is stated over
\mathbb{Z}
, but directly extends to
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
.

Given a divisor on the generic fibre, its scheme-theoretic closure on the integral model
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
gives a Weil divisor on
\spec(\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}})
. When the prime ideals dividing
2\disc(f)
are inverted, the Jacobian criterion shows that the affine
\spec(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}})
-scheme
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
is smooth over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
, hence Weil and Cartier divisors coincide on
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
. This proves surjectivity of
\theta
in that case.

For injectivity, let
{D\in\ker(\theta)}
. Then
{\theta(D)=\dv_{\mathbb{K}}(h)}
for some
{h\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X,Y]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle Y^{2}-f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}}
. Since
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{C}\setminus\left\{\infty\right\}}
have the same function field, we can consider
\dv(h)
as a principal divisor over
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Thus, we see that
D
and
\dv(h)
have the same generic fibre, hence
{D-\dv(h)}
is a vertical divisor. On the other hand, since
f
is monic of odd degree,
f
cannot be a square modulo any prime ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
, implying that the fibres of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
are irreducible. Therefore, vertical divisors on
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
are sum of fibres. When the prime ideals dividing a chosen set of generators of
\pic(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}})
are inverted,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
is a PID and the fibres of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{S}}
are principal. If this happens,
D
is principal, and
\theta
is injective.
∎

Now,
we fix once and for all a finite set
\mathcal{S}
of nonzero prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
, such that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
is a PID
.
Then the homomorphism
\theta
from Proposition
4.2
is injective.

Remark 4.3
.

In particular, we can choose
\mathcal{S}
such that all the prime ideals dividing
2\disc(f)
belong to
\mathcal{S}
. In that case,
\mathcal{S}
contains all the primes of bad reduction for the curve
\mathcal{C}
. Furthermore, the homomorphism
\theta
from Proposition
4.2
is then an isomorphism, and the link with Genus Theory over
\mathbb{K}[X]
is strengthened. This is the kind of set to consider while addressing Agboola and Pappas’ question, mentioned in the end of the Introduction.

Remark 4.4
.

When the homomorphim
\theta
in Proposition

[

).

Since

is a PID, every locally free

. Therefore, every ideal class in

has a representative of the form

with

, and corresponds to the class of the quadratic form

, by Proposition
2.4
. Thus, we deduce the quadratic form version of Proposition
4.2
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let

From now on we will mainly work with quadratic forms.

Let

. Given an ideal class in

Remark 4.9
.

Our approach enables us to do a little bit more than finding non-trivial specializations of a given class of quadratic forms. Indeed, since the genus maps are group homomorphisms, we can directly extend our considerations to the question of finding non-equivalent specializations in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}(4f(n))
of two given distinct classes of quadratic forms
{q,q^{\prime}\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
. When
q
and
q^{\prime}
are not in the same genus over
\mathbb{K}[X]
, that is, when they do not have the same image through
\Psi
, the arguments of this paper apply to
{q^{\prime}\ast q^{-1}}
, implying that their specializations are non-equivalent.

The following result is an integral version of Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Every class of quadratic forms in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)
has a representative of the form
[A,2B,C]
where
A
is coprime to
f
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
.

Proof.

Let
{q=[A_{0},2B_{0},C_{0}]\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
. By Remark
2.3
, every quadratic form equivalent to
q
has its first coefficient of the form
{\varepsilon q(\alpha,\gamma)=\varepsilon(A_{0}\alpha^{2}+2B_{0}\alpha\gamma+C_{0}\gamma^{2})}
for some
{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}
and some
{\alpha,\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}
such that
{\left\langle\alpha,\gamma\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}
. Actually, restricting our search to
{\varepsilon=1}
,
{\gamma=1}
and
{\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
will be enough for our purpose.

Let
\mathbb{K}(f)
be the splitting field of
f
. For all
{\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
, the polynomial
{q(\alpha,1)}
is coprime to
f
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
if and only if we have
{q(\alpha,1)(\rho)\neq 0}
for all
\rho
roots of
f
in
\mathbb{K}(f)
. In other words, we look for
{\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
such that

P(\rho):=A_{0}(\rho)\alpha^{2}+2B_{0}(\rho)\alpha+C_{0}(\rho)\neq 0~~~~\text{~for~all~}\rho\text{~root~of~}f.

Fix some root
\rho
of
f
. Since the quadratic form
[A_{0},2B_{0},C_{0}]
is primitive, we cannot have
{A_{0}(\rho)=B_{0}(\rho)=C_{0}(\rho)=0}
at the same time, hence
P(\rho)
is a nonzero polynomial in the variable
\alpha
, and has at most
2
roots in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
. Doing this for all
\rho
, we have at most
2\deg(f)
values of
\alpha
to avoid. Since
\mathbb{K}
is infinite, we can take
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
not in the set of those values. Then the quadratic form
{[A,2B,C]:=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\cdot[A_{0},2B_{0},C_{0}]}
is another representative of our class
q
, with
A
coprime to
f
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
.
∎

Given a class of quadratic forms
{[A,2B,C]\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
whose image through
\Psi
is non-trivial, we now analyse the consequences on
A
. We start with the case of quadratic forms over
\mathbb{K}[X]
.

For the sake of reader-friendlyness, uppercase letters are used for quadratic forms
[A,2B,C]
with coefficients in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]
, whereas lowercase letters are used for quadratic forms
[a,2b,c]
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[X]
.

Proposition 4.11
.

If
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)\setminus\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)^{\square}}
, and if
[a,2b,c]
is a representative of
q
with
a
coprime to
f
, then for all
{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}}
, there exists a root
\rho_{\varepsilon}
of
f
such that
{\varepsilon a(\rho_{\varepsilon})\notin\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon})^{\times\square}}
.

Proof.

Let
{L:={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}}
, and let

\Psi\colon{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.21724pt}{$\displaystyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)^{\square}}$}}{\raisebox{4.21724pt}{$\textstyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)^{\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.60216pt}{$\scriptstyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.60216pt}{$\scriptstyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)^{\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.60216pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.60216pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)^{\square}}$}}}\longrightarrow{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{L^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}$}}}

be the Genus Theory homomorphism over
\mathbb{K}[X]
defined in (
3.15
). Since
a
is coprime to
f
, we have
{\Psi(q)=a^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}=a\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}
by Proposition
3.7
. On the other hand,
q
is not a square, and
\Psi
is injective by Theorem
3.20
, hence
{a\notin\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}
.

Decompose
{f=\underset{i=1}{\overset{r}{\prod}}f_{i}}
as a product of irreducible polynomials in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]
. Recall that
f
is assumed to be square-free, hence the

’s are all distinct. For all

, denote by
\rho_{i}
a root of

. We then have

L={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle f(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\simeq\underset{i=1}{\overset{r}{\prod}}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\left\langle f_{i}(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\left\langle f_{i}(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\left\langle f_{i}(X)\right\rangle}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathbb{K}[X]}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\left\langle f_{i}(X)\right\rangle}$}}}\simeq\underset{i=1}{\overset{r}{\prod}}\mathbb{K}(\rho_{i}).

The image of
{a\in\mathbb{K}[X]}
through these isomorphisms is
{(a(\rho_{1}),\ldots,a(\rho_{r}))}
.

By contraposition, if there exists some
{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}}
such that
\varepsilon a(\rho_{i})
is a square in
\mathbb{K}(\rho_{i})
for all

, then
\varepsilon a
is a square in
L
. Recall that
a
is coprime to
f
, hence
{\varepsilon a(\rho_{i})\neq 0}
for all

, so
{\varepsilon a\in L^{\times\square}}
, and
{a\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}L^{\times\square}}
. Thus,

must be a square in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)
, and this concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
. Let
[A,2B,C]
be a representative of
q
with
A
coprime to
f
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
(see Lemma
4.10
). If
q
is not a square in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)
, then for all
{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}
, there exists a root
\rho_{\varepsilon}
of
f
such that
{\varepsilon A(\rho_{\varepsilon})}
is not a square in
{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}}
(where
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
denotes the set of prime ideals above
\mathcal{S}
in
\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon})
).

Remark 4.13
.

With our choice of
\mathcal{S}
, requiring
q
to be nonsquare in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)
might not be enough to ensure that
q
is not a square in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathbb{K}[X]}(4f)
, since the homomorphism from Corollary
4.5
is only injective. As seen in Remark
4.3
, assuming that
\mathcal{S}
also contains the prime ideals dividing

solves this issue.

Proof.

We apply Proposition
4.11
: for all
{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}}
, there exists
\rho_{\varepsilon}
a root of
f
such that
{\varepsilon A(\rho_{\varepsilon})\notin\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon})^{\times\square}}
. On the other hand, for all
{\varepsilon\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}
, we have
{\varepsilon A(\rho_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}}
. Since this ring is integrally closed,
\varepsilon A(\rho_{\varepsilon})
is a square in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}
if and only if it is a square in
\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon})
. We end up with the desired conclusion.
∎

The following Theorem relates Genus Theory over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]
to Genus Theory over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
, providing a modular criterion for non-trivial specializations.

Principal Genus Specialization Theorem 4.14
.

Let
{f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}[X]}

, and let

, and assume that

is not in the principal genus over
\mathbb{K}[X]
.

Let
[A,2B,C]
be a representative of

with
A
coprime to
f
in
\mathbb{K}[X]
(see Lemma
4.10
). Let
{n\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
such that
{f(n)\neq 0}
. For all
{\varepsilon\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}}}
, let
\rho_{\varepsilon}
be a root of

such that
\varepsilon A(\rho_{\varepsilon})
is not a square in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}
, as in Corollary

.

If, for all
{\varepsilon\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}}}
, there exists

such that

is inert in the extension

and

, then the quadratic form
{[A(n),2B(n),C(n)]}
is not in the principal genus over

.

Remark 4.15

There are only finitely many

we would need

and Remark

that there exists another representative of

whose first coefficient

is nonzero and coprime to

, and such that

for some coprime
\mathcal{S}

and
\gamma
. By Proposition

, we have
{\psi_{n}(q_{n})=A_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}M_{n}^{\times\square}=A_{n}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}M_{n}^{\times\square}}
, hence

is in the principal genus if and only if
{\Big(A_{n}+f(n)\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}\Big)\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}M_{n}^{\times\square}}
. This means that for all
{\varepsilon\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}}}

.

{\varepsilon\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}}}
. Let

satisfying the hypotheses. Since

By contraposition, if
\varepsilon A_{n}
is a square in

, then
\varepsilon A_{n}
is a square in
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}_{\varepsilon}}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\textstyle{\mathfrak{p}_{\varepsilon}}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.50694pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathfrak{p}_{\varepsilon}}$}}{\raisebox{1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathfrak{p}_{\varepsilon}}$}}}

Since
\mathfrak{p}_{\varepsilon}
is inert in the quadratic extension
{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{K}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\sqrt{\varepsilon A(\rho_{\varepsilon})}\right)}

cannot be a square modulo

{{\Big(\varepsilon A_{n}+f(n)\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}\Big)}\notin M_{n}^{\times\square}}

, hence
{\psi_{n}(q_{n})\neq\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}M_{n}^{\times\square}}

∎

Corollary 4.18
.

With the same notations as above, let
{q\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
. Recall that for
{n\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
,
ev_{n}
is the specialization map defined by (
4.6
).

If
q
is not in the principal genus over
\mathbb{K}[X]
, then there exist infinitely many
{n\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
such that the specialized class of quadratic forms
ev_{n}(q)
is non trivial in
\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}(4f(n))
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be a root of
f
in some extension, and let
\nu
be a non-square element of
\mathbb{K}(\rho)
. In view of the PGS Theorem
4.14
and the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and because the set
\mathcal{S}
is finite, it is enough to show that there exist infinitely many different prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho)}
satisfying:

\bullet

\mathfrak{p}
is inert in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}\left(\rho,\sqrt{\nu}\right)}
;

\bullet

\mathfrak{p}
has inertia degree
1
over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
(this ensures that
{n\equiv\rho\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}}
has a solution
{n\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}
).

The first point is guaranteed by Chebotarev’s Density Theorem: the set of prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho)}
inert in some (Galois) quadratic extension has Dirichlet density
\frac{1}{2}
. For the second point, it is a standard fact that the set of prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho)}
having inertia degree
1
over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
has Dirichlet density
1
(see for example
[
Nar04
, 7.2.1, Corollary 3]
). Therefore, we may choose our prime ideals among a set of Dirichlet density
\frac{1}{2}
, hence there are infinitely many such ideals, as desired.
∎

4.3
Density of non-trivial specializations in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}

As in the previous Subsections, let
{f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}[X]}
be a square-free monic polynomial of odd degree at least
3
, and let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite set of nonzero prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}

{q\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
, and assume that

is not in the principal genus over
\mathbb{K}[X]
. Then the PGS Theorem
4.14
and its Corollary

tell us that the set of
\mathcal{S}
-integers

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
. In general, there are various notions of density one can choose, depending on the problem one considers. Therefore, we give a list of properties that our density should satisfy.

Definition 4.19
.

Denote by
\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}})
the set of subsets of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
. In this paper, a

is a map
{\delta\colon\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}})\longrightarrow[0,1]}
satisfying the following properties:

(

)

;

(

)

in

\delta(P)\leq\delta(Q)
;

(

)

, then

;

(
d_{4}
)

is translation invariant, that is,
\delta(\alpha+P)=\delta(P)
for all
{\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}
and
{P\in\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}})}
;

(

)

for any

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
, we have
{\delta(I)=\frac{1}{N(I)}}
, where
{N(I):=\left\lvert{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{I}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{I}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{I}$}}{\raisebox{1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{I}$}}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{I\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{I\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{I\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}}{\raisebox{1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{I\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}}$}}}\right\rvert}
is the norm of
I
.

.

\mathcal{S}_{\infty}

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}}

Theorem 4.26
.

Let
{f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}[X]}
be a square-free monic polynomial of odd degree at least
3
, let
\mathcal{S}
be a finite set of nonzero prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
is a PID, let
\delta
be a density, and let
{q=[A,2B,C]\in\cl^{tw}_{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}[X]}(4f)}
.

If
q
is not in the principal genus over
\mathbb{K}[X]
, then the set

\mathcal{T}_{triv}=\left\{n\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}\enskip\middle|\enskip[A(n),2B(n),C(n)]\text{~is~}\gl_{2}^{tw}\!{}\mathchar 45\relax\text{equivalent~over~}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}\text{~to~}[1,0,-f(n)]\right\}

has density
{\delta(\mathcal{T}_{triv})=0}
in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

In view of Theorem
4.25
, it remains to compute the product
{\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1-\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}\right)}
for all
{\varepsilon\in{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.23112pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.66862pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-4.41862pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.55762pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}{\raisebox{2.3889pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.77094pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K},\mathcal{S}}^{\times\square}}$}}}}
. Let us fix such an
\varepsilon
. As already noticed in the proof of Corollary
4.18
, the set
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
from Definition
4.22
has Dirichlet density
\frac{1}{2}
, being the intersection of a set of Dirichlet density
1
(the prime ideals having inertia degree
1
over
\mathbb{K}
) and another one of density
\frac{1}{2}
(the prime ideals inert in some quadratic extension). This implies that
{{\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}}\underset{s\to 1^{+}}{\longrightarrow}+\infty}
, since otherwise, the Dirichlet density of
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
would vanish by definition.

Beside this, one can order prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}(\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}
such that the sequence
(N(\mathfrak{p}_{j}))_{j}
is increasing. Applying
[
Jan96
, Lemma IV.4.4]
with
{u_{j}:=N(\mathfrak{p}_{j})}
leads to

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}=-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\right)+\underset{s\to 1^{+}}{O}(1)

for all
{s>1}
. Taking the exponential, we get

\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1-\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{s}}\right)\exp\left(\underset{s\to 1^{+}}{O}(1)\right)\underset{s\to 1^{+}}{\longrightarrow}0.

The conclusion is now straightforward, applying Theorem
4.25
.
