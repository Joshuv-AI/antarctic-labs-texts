On degenerations and extensions of symplectic and orthogonal quiver representations
Magdalena Boos, Giovanni Cerulli Irelli

Abstract
. We discuss degenerations of symplectic and orthogonal representations of symmetric quivers and algebras with self-dualities. As in the non-symmetric case, we define a partial ordering, that we call symmetric Ext-order which gives a sufficient criterion for a symmetric degeneration. Then a detailed discussion of type A quivers and their (symmetric) representation theory via Auslander-Reiten theory leads to our main theorem which states that the symmetric degeneration order of a symmetric quiver of finite type is induced by the ”usual” degeneration order between representations of the underlying quiver.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a complex algebraic variety acted upon by a group
\G
. Suppose that the pair
(\G,X)
satisfies the following:

(1)

both
\G
and
X
are equipped with involutions
\rho:\G\rightarrow\G
,
g\mapsto g^{\rho}
and
\Delta:X\rightarrow X
,
x\mapsto\Delta x
such that
\Delta(g\cdot\Delta x)=g^{\rho}\cdot x
. Denote by
\G^{\rho}\subset\G
and
X^{\Delta}\subset X
the sets of fixed points;

(2)

the group
\G
is a subgroup of the group of invertible elements
E^{\times}
of a finite-dimensional associative algebra
E
over
\mathbb{C}
;

(3)

the anti-involution of
\G
given by
g\mapsto g^{\ast}:=(g^{\rho})^{-1}
extends to a
\mathbb{C}
–linear anti-involution
f\mapsto f^{\ast}
on the algebra
E
;

(4)

for every fixed point
x\in X^{\Delta}
, its stabilizer
H=\textrm{Stab}_{\G}(x)
is the group of invertible elements of its linear span
\textrm{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}(H)\subset E
.

Then Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinsky
[
20
, Section 2.1]
(generalizing a result of Richardson
[
21
]
and one of Derksen and Weyman
[
15
, Theorem 2.6]
) prove that for every fixed point
x\in X^{\Delta}

(1.1)

\G x\cap X^{\Delta}=\G^{\rho}x.

Equation (
1.1
) means that the orbit of a fixed point by the “small” group
\G^{\rho}
is the intersection of its orbit by the “big” group
G
with the set of fixed points. It is then natural to ask if the same happens for the Zariski orbit closures:

Question 1.1
.

Is it true that
\overline{\G x}\cap X^{\Delta}=\overline{\G^{\rho}x}
, for every
x\in X^{\Delta}
?

Example 1.1
.

Let
X\subset\textrm{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the variety of
n\times n
nilpotent complex matrices and
\G=\GL_{n}
acting on
X
by conjugation. Let
\Delta
and
\rho
be the involutions on
X
and
\G
such that
\G^{\rho}=SP_{n}
is the symplectic group (or
\G^{\rho}=\OO_{n}
is the orthogonal group) and
X^{\Delta}\subset\mathfrak{sp}_{n}=\Lie(\SP_{n})
(or
X^{\Delta}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{n}=\Lie(\OO_{n})
) is the nilpotent cone. It is straightforward to check that the hypotheses (1)–(4) are satisfied for the pair
(X,\G)
.
By classical work of Freudenthal, Gerstenhaber and Hesselink (compare with
[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
) it is known that the answer to Question
1.1
is positive in this case.

A pair
(X,\G)
as above can be found in the context of representation theory of finite dimensional algebras endowed with a self-duality (see Section
2.4
) and we give another proof of Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinsky’s result in this specific context in Section
2.6
. We are aware of an example arising in this context where the answer to Question
1.1
is negative
[
9
]
. In this paper we provide a positive answer to Question
1.1
in case of
symmetric quiver algebras of finite representation type
.

Let us explain the context, the results and our motivations to seek Question
1.1
.

Quiver algebras

Let
\mathrm{k}=\mathbb{C}
be the field of complex numbers. A
quiver

\mathcal{Q}
is a finite oriented graph given by a quadruple
\mathcal{Q}=(\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q}_{1},s,t)
where
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
denotes the finite set of
vertices
of
\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
denotes the finite set of
edges
and
s,t:\mathcal{Q}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{0}
are two functions that provide the orientation
\alpha:s(\alpha)\rightarrow t(\alpha)
of the edges. For simplicity of notation we consider the elements of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
as oriented edges or arrows. A
path
in
\mathcal{Q}
is defined to be a sequence of arrows
\omega=\alpha_{s}\cdots\alpha_{1}
, such that
t(\alpha_{k})=s(\alpha_{k+1})
for all
k
; formally we include a path
\varepsilon_{i}
of length zero for each
i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
starting and ending in
i
. The
path algebra

\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}
of
\mathcal{Q}
is defined to be the
\mathrm{k}
-vector space with a basis given by the set of all paths in
\mathcal{Q}
. The multiplication of two paths is defined by concatenation of paths. Let
R
be the
arrow ideal
of
\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}
which is the (two-sided) ideal generated by the arrows of
\mathcal{Q}
. Let
I\subseteq\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}
be an ideal such that there is an integer
s
with
R^{s}\subseteq I\subseteq R^{2}
(i.e. it is
admissible
). The quotient algebra
\mathcal{A}:=\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}/I
is finite-dimensional and associative, and we refer to it as a
quiver algebra
(standard references are
[
4
,
6
,
12
,
23
]
).

Symmetric quiver algebras

A
symmetric quiver
as defined in
[
15
]
is a pair
(\mathcal{Q},\sigma)
where
\mathcal{Q}
is a finite quiver and
\sigma:Q\rightarrow Q^{op}
is an involutive isomorphism of
\mathcal{Q}
with its opposite
\mathcal{Q}^{op}
, i.e. it is an involution of
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
and of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, reversing the orientation of arrows. Notice that
\sigma
is defined on the underlying graph of
\mathcal{Q}
and once this is fixed we say that
\mathcal{Q}
and
\sigma
are
compatible
. For example, let us consider the Dynkin diagram of type
A_{3}
,
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\beta}
\textstyle{3}
, and let
\sigma
be its non-trivial involution given by
\sigma(1)=3
,
\sigma(2)=2
, and
\sigma(\alpha)=\beta
. Let
\textstyle{\mathcal{Q}(1)=1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\beta}
\textstyle{3}
and
\textstyle{\mathcal{Q}(2)=1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{2}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces 3}
\scriptstyle{\beta}
be two quivers of type
A_{3}
. Then
\mathcal{Q}(1)
is compatible with
\sigma
, i.e.
(\mathcal{Q}(1),\sigma)
is a symmetric quiver, but
\mathcal{Q}(2)
is not compatible with
\sigma
. Actually,
\mathcal{Q}(2)
cannot be endowed with the structure of a symmetric quiver, since
\sigma
is the only non-trivial involution of its underlying graph.

Let
(\mathcal{Q},\sigma)
be a symmetric quiver. For every admissible ideal
I\subset\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}
such that
\sigma(I)=I
, the quiver algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}/I
is isomorphic (via
\sigma
) to its opposite
\mathcal{A}^{op}=\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}^{op}/\sigma(I)
. The pair
(\mathcal{A},\sigma)
is called a
symmetric quiver algebra
.

(Symmetric) representation theory

The representation theory of symmetric quivers or
generalized quivers with dimension vectors
was developed by Derksen and Weyman in
[
15
]
and it has strong connections with the theory of symmetric spaces and
\theta
-groups of Vinberg
[
26
]
and the results of Kac
[
17
,
18
]
, as explained in
[
15
]
and
[
25
]
.
We recall here a few facts about it. Let
(\mathcal{A},\sigma)
be a symmetric quiver algebra. An
\mathcal{A}
-module or an
\mathcal{A}
-representation is a representation of the quiver
\mathcal{Q}
satisfying the relations from
I
. Thus, a representation is a pair
M=(V,f)
where
V=\oplus_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}V_{i}
is a finite dimensional
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
-graded vector space and
f=(f_{\alpha}:V_{s(\alpha)}\rightarrow V_{t(\alpha)})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}}
is a collection of linear maps such that
f_{\pi}=0
for every
\pi\in I
. The vector space
V
is called the
underlying vector space
of the representation
M
and its graded dimension
\mathbf{d}={\bf dim}\,V=(\dim V_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}
is called the
dimension vector
of
M
or
V
. Notice that
V
is itself an
\mathcal{A}
-module, by choosing all the linear maps to be zero; it is called the semisimple module of dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
. A morphism of
\mathcal{A}
-modules
h:(V,f)\rightarrow(W,g)
is a collection of linear maps
(h_{i}:V_{i}\rightarrow W_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}
such that
h_{t(\alpha)}\circ f_{\alpha}=g_{\alpha}\circ h_{s(\alpha)}
. We denote by
\textrm{Rep}(\mathcal{A})
the category of finite dimensional representations of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
V=\oplus_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}V_{i}
be a
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
-graded complex vector space
of dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
. We denote by
R(\mathcal{A},V)
the variety of
\mathcal{A}
-modules having
V
as underlying vector space. Thus

R(\mathcal{A},V)\subseteq R(\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q},V):=\!\!\!\!\bigoplus_{\alpha:i\rightarrow j\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}}\Hom_{\mathrm{k}}(V_{i},V_{j}).

We denote by
\GL^{\bullet}(V):=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}\textrm{GL}(V_{i})
the group of graded automorphisms of
V
. We sometimes think of
\GL^{\bullet}(V)
as embedded into
\GL(V)
as diagonal block matrices (see
[
15
, Section 1]
). The group
\GL^{\bullet}(V)
acts on
R(\mathcal{A},V)
by change of basis, i.e. given
g=(g_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}\in\GL^{\bullet}(V)
and
M=(M_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}}\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
the representation
g\cdot M
is defined by
(g\cdot M)_{\alpha}=g_{t(\alpha)}\circ M_{\alpha}\circ g_{s(\alpha)}^{-1}
. The
\GL^{\bullet}(V)
-orbits are the isomorphism classes of
\mathcal{A}
-representations with underlying vector space
V
.

Let
\varepsilon
be
+1
or
-1
. We say that
a bilinear form
\langle-,-\rangle:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
on
V
is a
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form
on
V
if:

(i)

the form
\langle-,-\rangle
is non-degenerate;

(ii)

the form
\langle-,-\rangle
is
compatible with
\sigma
, i.e.
\langle-,-\rangle|_{V_{i}\times V_{j}}=0
if
j\neq\sigma(i)
;

(iii)

the form
\langle-,-\rangle
is an
\varepsilon
-form
: i.e.
\langle v,w\rangle=\varepsilon\langle w,v\rangle
for every
v,w\in V
.

The pair
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
is called an
\varepsilon
-quadratic space for
(\mathcal{Q},\sigma)
or for
(\mathcal{A},\sigma)
. Notice that the dimension vector of
V
is
\sigma
-symmetric
in this case, i.e.
\mathbf{d}_{\sigma(i)}=\mathbf{d}_{i}
for every
i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
. Indeed, by (ii), the non-degenerate bilinear form
\langle-,-\rangle
induces an isomorphism between
V_{i}
and the linear dual of
V_{\sigma(i)}
.

Every endomorphism
f
of
V
has a unique
adjoint

f^{\star}
with respect to the non-degenerate bilinear form
\langle-,-\rangle
defined by the condition
\langle f(v),w\rangle=\langle v,f^{\star}(w)\rangle
, for all
v,w,\in V
.
We denote by
\G(V,\langle-,-\rangle)=\{g\in\GL(V)|g=(g^{\star})^{-1}\}
the group of isometries of
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
.

A point
M\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
can be considered as an endomorphism of
V
. Following
[
15
]
, we say that
M\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
is an
\varepsilon
-representation
of
(\mathcal{A},\sigma)
with respect to
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
if

(iv)

M^{\star}+M=0
.

Condition (iv) means that
M
lies in the Lie algebra of the group
\G(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
and condition (ii) implies that
M_{\alpha}^{\star}=-M_{\sigma(\alpha)}
, i.e.
\langle M_{\alpha}(v),w\rangle+\langle v,M_{\sigma({\alpha})}(w)\rangle=0
for every arrow
\alpha:i\rightarrow j
,
v\in V_{i}
and
w\in V_{\sigma(j)}
. For
\varepsilon=+1
,
M
is hence called an
orthogonal
representation of
(\mathcal{A},\sigma)
and for
\varepsilon=-1
, it is called a
symplectic
representation of
(\mathcal{A},\sigma)
.

We denote by

R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\langle-,-\rangle,\varepsilon}=\{M\in R(\mathcal{A},V)|\,M^{\star}+M=0\}

the variety of
\varepsilon
-representations of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
. We denote by

\G^{\bullet}(V,\langle-,-\rangle):=\G(V,\langle-,-\rangle)\cap\GL^{\bullet}(V)
the group of graded isometries of
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
. Thus
g=(g_{i})\in\GL^{\bullet}(V)
belongs to
\G^{\bullet}(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
if and only if
g_{\sigma(i)}=(g_{i}^{\star})^{-1}
. The action of
\GL^{\bullet}(V)
on
R(\mathcal{A},V)
induces an action of
\G^{\bullet}(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
on
R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\langle-,-\rangle,\varepsilon}
by change of basis (see Section
2.4
).

Example 1.2
.

[Type
(A_{2},\varepsilon)
]. Let
R=\mathrm{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})
be the vector space of complex
n\times n
matrices.
For
\varepsilon=-1
let
R^{\varepsilon}=Sym_{n}
denote the subspace of symmetric matrices and for
\varepsilon=1
let
R^{\varepsilon}=ASym_{n}
denote the space of anti-symmetric matrices. Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We view
R\subset\End(V)
as
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\scriptstyle 0}&{\scriptstyle 0}\\
{\scriptstyle R}&{\scriptstyle 0}\end{array}\right)
so that the elements of
R
are considered as linear maps from the first copy of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to the second one.
Let
\textstyle{\mathcal{Q}=1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{2}
be a quiver of type
A_{2}
and let
\sigma
be the anti-involution on
\mathcal{Q}
. Then
R
can be seen as the representation variety
R_{\mathbf{(n,n)}}(\mathbb{C}\mathcal{Q})
. On
V
we define the
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form
\langle v,w\rangle:=v^{t}Jw
where
J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\scriptstyle 0}&{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_{n}}\\
{\scriptstyle\mathbf{1}_{n}}&{\scriptstyle 0}\end{array}\right)
. Then, a short calculation shows that the variety of
\varepsilon
-representations of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
is
R^{\varepsilon}=\{A\in R|A^{t}+\varepsilon A=0\}
and thus we recover the two subspaces defined above. The group
\GL^{\bullet}(V)=\GL_{n}\times\GL_{n}
acts on
R
by base change
((g_{1},g_{2}),A)\mapsto g_{2}Ag_{1}^{-1}
, and the group
\G^{\bullet}(V,\langle-,-\rangle)=\{(g_{1},g_{2})|\,g_{1}=(g_{2}^{t})^{-1}\}\simeq\GL_{n}
acts on
R^{\varepsilon}
by congruence
(g_{2},A)\mapsto g_{2}Ag_{2}^{t}
.
Two
n\times n
matrices are in the same
\GL^{\bullet}(V)
-orbit if and only if they have the same rank. The theorem of Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinky applies here (see Section
2.4
) and states the well-known fact that two symmetric (or anti-symmetric) matrices are congruent if and only if they have the same rank.

Example 1.3
.

[Type
(A_{3},\varepsilon)
] Let
U
be a finite dimensional complex vector space and let
U^{\ast}
denote its linear dual. Let
\varepsilon
be
+1
or
-1
and let
(W,\langle-,-\rangle)
be an
\varepsilon
-quadratic vector space. The isometry group
\G(W,\langle-,-\rangle)
equals
\SP(W)
if
\varepsilon=-1
and
\OO(W)
if
\varepsilon=1
. Let
R=\Hom(U,W)\oplus\Hom(W,U^{\ast})
. Then
R
is a representation variety for the quiver
\textstyle{\mathcal{Q}=1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\beta}
\textstyle{3}
of type
A_{3}
and we denote its elements as
(f_{\alpha},f_{\beta})
. Given
f_{\alpha}\in\Hom(U,W)
we denote by
f_{\alpha}^{\star}\in\Hom(W,U^{\ast})
the linear map defined by
f_{\alpha}^{\star}(w)(u)=\langle f_{\alpha}(u),w\rangle
for all
w\in W
and
u\in U
. Let
R^{\varepsilon}=\{(f_{\alpha},f_{\alpha}^{\star})\}\subset R
be the subspace of fixed points for the involution
(f_{\alpha},f_{\beta})\mapsto(f_{\beta}^{\star},f_{\alpha}^{\star})
. It is easy to see that the fixed point set
R^{\varepsilon}
can be realized as a variety of
\varepsilon
-representations for the symmetric quiver
(\mathcal{Q},\sigma)
.

Let us consider the group
\G=\GL(U)\times\GL(W)\times\GL(U^{\ast})
. As usual, for an endomorphism
h
of
U
we denote by
h^{\ast}
the endomorphism of
U^{\ast}
given by
h^{\ast}(g)(u)=g(h(u))
for all
g\in U^{\ast}
and
u\in U
. Similarly, for an endomorphism
h
of
W
we denote by
h^{\star}
its adjoint with respect to
\langle-,-\rangle
which is the unique endomorphism of
W
such that
\langle h(w_{1}),w_{2}\rangle=\langle w_{1},h^{\star}(w_{2})\rangle
for every
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
. Let us consider the involution on
\G
given by
(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})\mapsto((g_{3}^{\ast})^{-1},(g_{2}^{\star})^{-1},(g_{1}^{\ast})^{-1})
and let
\G^{\varepsilon}
be the subgroup of fixed points. We notice that
\G^{\varepsilon}\simeq\GL(U)\times\G(W,\langle-,-\rangle)
.

The group
\G
acts on
R
by change of basis, i.e. given
g=(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})
and
f=(f_{\alpha},f_{\beta})\in R
,
g\cdot f=(g_{2}f_{\alpha}g_{1}^{-1},g_{3}f_{\beta}g_{2}^{-1})
. It is well-known that the only invariants for this action are
\underline{rk}(f)=(rk(f_{\alpha}),rk(f_{\beta}),rk(f_{\beta}\circ f_{\alpha}))
and thus
f^{\prime}=(f^{\prime}_{\alpha},f^{\prime}_{\beta})
lies in the
\G
-orbit of
f=(f_{\alpha},f_{\beta})
if and only if
\underline{rk}(f)=\underline{rk}(f^{\prime})
.

The action of
\G
on
R
restricts to an action of
\G^{\varepsilon}
on
R^{\varepsilon}
.
We notice that for a point
f=(f_{\alpha},f_{\alpha}^{\star})
in
R^{\varepsilon}
, the rank of
f_{\alpha}^{\star}
is equal to the rank of
f_{\alpha}
and the rank of
f_{\alpha}^{\star}\circ f_{\alpha}
is the rank of the form on
W
restricted to the image of
f_{\alpha}
, and thus these two ranks are preserved by the action of
\G^{\varepsilon}
on
R^{\varepsilon}
.

The theorem of Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinsky applies to this example (see Section
2.4
) and states that two points
f=(f_{\alpha},f_{\alpha}^{\star})
and
f^{\prime}=(f^{\prime}_{\alpha},(f^{\prime}_{\alpha})^{\star})
in
R^{\varepsilon}
are in the same

-orbit if and only if
rk(f_{\alpha})=rk(f^{\prime}_{\alpha})
and the rank of the form restricted to the image of
f_{\alpha}
is equal to the rank of the form restricted to the image of
f^{\prime}_{\alpha}
.

(Symmetric) degenerations and orderings

For
M,N\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
we denote
M\leq_{\deg}N
if and only if
N\in\overline{\GL^{\bullet}(V)M}
and we say that
M
degenerates to

is a degeneration of

or that

. In Section

we show that the theorem of Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinsky applies to this situation and a positive answer to Question

means that

and

coincide on

. In Section

we define a third order that we call

, denoted by

(see Definition

). We obtain Corollary

,

Algebras of finite representation type

Question
1.1
is particularly interesting when the orbit closures can be described by a finite number of invariants. In the context of symmetric representation varieties, this requirement is fullfilled by an algebra
\mathcal{A}
which is of finite representation type. Indeed, in this case, Zwara
[
27
]
proved that the degeneration order
\leq_{\deg}
is equivalent to the so-called
Hom-order
(see Section
4.1
). This implies that the orbit closures in
X
are described by checking a finite number of inequalities which is independent on
X
and
G
. Even in this special case, the answer to Question
1.1
is negative
[
9
]
. It is then an open problem to find conditions on
(X,G)
or on the algebra
\mathcal{A}
such that the answer to Question
1.1
is positive. In this paper we investigate this problem in the case of a symmetric hereditary quiver algebras of finite representation type. These algebras are the path algebras of symmetric quivers of finite type. A symmetric quiver is called of
finite type
if it admits only a finite number of

\textstyle{\leq_{\Ext}^{\varepsilon}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Motivations

One of our motivations to seek Theorem
7.1
comes from the study of linear degenerations of the symplectic (complete) flag variety, in analogy with
[
13
,
14
]
. Indeed, let
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}
be the path algebra of the equioriented quiver of type
A_{2n-1}
and let
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
be a
(-1)
-quadratic space for
(\mathcal{Q},\sigma)
of dimension vector
\mathbf{d}=(2n,2n,\cdots,2n)
. Then one can construct a
\GL^{\bullet}(V)^{\langle-,-\rangle,(-1)}
-equivariant family
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\langle-,-\rangle,(-1)}
whose fiber over a symplectic representation consists of its
isotropic
(actually Lagrangian) subrepresentations of dimension vector
\mathbf{e}=(1,2,\cdots,2n-1)
. One sees immediately that the fiber over the generic point is the complete symplectic flag variety. It is then an interesting problem to understand the geometric properties of the special fibers, which can be considered as “linear” degenerations of the symplectic flag variety. The first step to study this family, is to understand the orbit closures in the base. This is now clear thanks to Theorem
7.1
.

The general answer of Question
1.1
is furthermore interesting from an algebraic Lie-theoretic point of view: It helps to answer questions about orbit closures in the nilpotent cone. In more detail, the above described classical example of reductive actions on the nilpotent cone can be generalized in many ways (e.g. restrict to Borel-actions on certain subvarieties of nilpotent matrices as in
[
10
]
). This setup can be translated via an associated fibre bundle to the (symmetric) representation theory of a particular quiver with relations.

(1)

\displaystyle\Delta(g\cdot\Delta M)_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(g\cdot\Delta M)_{\sigma(\alpha)}^{\star}=(g_{\sigma(i)}\circ M_{\alpha}^{\star}\circ g_{\sigma(j)}^{-1})^{\star}

\displaystyle(g_{\sigma(j)}^{-1})^{\star}\circ M_{\alpha}\circ g_{\sigma(i)}^{\star}=(g^{\rho}\cdot M)_{\alpha}.

(2)

The group

(3)

\Aut(M,\Psi)
the group of automorphisms of
(M,\Psi)
. In particular,
\Aut(M,\Psi)
is a subgroup of
\GL^{\bullet}(V)=\prod_{i\in Q_{0}}\GL(V_{i})
. The Lie algebra
\Lie\Aut(M,\Psi)
consists of those
\theta\in\Hom(M,M)
such that

\Psi\circ\theta+\nabla\theta\circ\Psi=0

i.e.
\langle\theta(v_{1}),v_{2}\rangle+\langle v_{1},\theta(v_{2})\rangle=0
for every
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
.
The dimension of
\Aut(M,\Psi)
can be computed as follows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(M,\Psi)
be an
\varepsilon
-representation. Then

(2.4)

\Lie\Aut(M,\Psi)\simeq\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{-\varepsilon\nabla}

In particular,
\dim\Aut(M,\Psi)=\dim\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{-\varepsilon\nabla}
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\zeta:\Lie\Aut(M,\Psi)\rightarrow\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{-\varepsilon\nabla}:\theta\mapsto\Psi\circ\theta
.
This map is well-defined:
\Psi\circ\theta
is indeed a homomorphism
M\rightarrow\nabla M
and moreover

\nabla(\Psi\circ\theta)=\nabla\theta\circ\nabla\Psi=\varepsilon\nabla\theta\circ\Psi=-\varepsilon\Psi\circ\theta.

The map
\zeta
is an isomorphism of vector spaces: indeed it is clearly linear and the inverse is given by

\zeta^{\prime}:\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{-\varepsilon\nabla}\rightarrow\Lie\Aut(M,\Psi):f\mapsto\Psi^{-1}\circ f
. ∎

2.6.
Isomorphism classes of symmetric representations

The following result was proved by Derksen and Weyman in
[
15
, Theorem 2.6]
for symmetric quivers without relations and it is a special case of the result proved by Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinsky mentioned in the introduction (see Section
2.4
). We provide a different proof for symmetric quiver algebras.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
M,N\in R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\Psi,\varepsilon}
be two
\varepsilon
-representations of
\mathcal{A}
. Then they are isomorphic as
\varepsilon
-representations if and only if they are isomorphic as
\mathcal{A}
-representations.

Proof.

If
M
and
N
are isomorphic as
\varepsilon
-representations, then they are isomorphic as
\mathcal{A}
-representations since the group
\G^{\bullet}(V,\Psi)
is a subgroup of
\GL^{\bullet}(V)
. To prove the converse, we choose an isomorphism
\theta:M\rightarrow N
and we prove that there exists
\rho\in\Aut(M)
, such that
\theta\circ\rho
fulfills
\nabla(\theta\circ\rho)\circ\Psi\circ(\theta\circ\rho)=\Psi
.

The group
\Aut(M)
acts from the right on
\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
as follows

\textstyle{\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}\times\Aut(M)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla},}
\textstyle{(\xi,\rho)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\xi\cdot\rho:=\nabla\rho\circ\xi\circ\rho.}

Let
\Hom^{0}(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}\subseteq\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
denote the open subset consisting of invertible
(\varepsilon\nabla)
-invariant homomorphisms. Since
\Psi\in\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
is an isomorphism, we see that
\Hom^{0}(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
is non-empty and hence dense in the vector space
\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
.

The
\Aut(M)
-action above descends to an action on
\Hom^{0}(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
. We denote by
\Stab_{\Aut(M)}(\pi)
the stabilizer of a point
\pi\in\Hom^{0}(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
for this action. Then
\Stab_{\Aut(M)}(\pi)=\Aut(M,\pi)
. By Lemma
2.4
we get
\dim\Stab_{\Aut(M)}(\pi)=\dim\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{-\varepsilon\nabla}
.
It follows that

\dim\Aut(M)=\dim\Hom(M,\nabla M)=\dim\Stab_{\Aut(M)}(\pi)+\dim\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}.

We hence see that every point in
\textrm{Hom}^{0}(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
has a dense
\Aut(M)
-orbit. Since
\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
is irreducible, being a vector space, two such orbits meet. Both
\Psi
and
\nabla\theta\circ\Psi\circ\theta
lie in
\textrm{Hom}^{0}(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
. It follows that there exists
\rho\in\textrm{Aut}(M)
such that
(\nabla\theta\circ\Psi\circ\theta)\cdot\rho=\Psi
, which is what we wanted to prove.
∎

The following fact is very well-known. It basically says that two complex symmetric or anti-symmetric non-degenerate forms are conjugate under
\GL
. In terms of symmetric representation theory, it says that if
M
is an
\varepsilon
-representation with respect to some
\varepsilon
-quadratic space
(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
, then for any other choice
\langle-,-\rangle^{\prime}
of a
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form on
V
there is
M^{\prime}\simeq M
such that
M^{\prime}
is an
\varepsilon
-representation with respect to
(V,\langle-,-\rangle^{\prime})
. This fact is very useful when one makes computations with
\varepsilon
-representations, since it allows to change the form at the best convenience.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\Psi,\Psi^{\prime}\in\Hom^{0}(V,\nabla V)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
be two
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-forms on a
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
-graded vector space
V
. Then there exists
g\in\GL^{\bullet}(V)
such that
\nabla g\circ\Psi\circ g=\Psi^{\prime}
. In particular, if
M
is an
\varepsilon
-representation with respect to
\Psi
, then
M^{\prime}=g^{-1}\cdot M
is an
\varepsilon
-representation with respect to
\Psi^{\prime}
.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
2.5
replace
M
with the semisimple representation
V
. Since
\Aut(V)=\GL^{\bullet}(V)
we get the first part. To get the second part, we notice that if
\Psi\in\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
then
\Psi^{\prime}=\nabla g\circ\Psi\circ g\in\Hom(M^{\prime},\nabla M^{\prime})^{\varepsilon\nabla}
where
M^{\prime}=g^{-1}\cdot M
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

In view of Corollary
2.6
we can omit to mention the form with respect to which a representation is an
\varepsilon
-representation and we just say that a representation is an
\varepsilon
-representation, without specifying the form. We hence sometimes use the notation
R_{\mathbf{d}}^{\varepsilon}
instead of
R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\langle-,-\rangle,\varepsilon}
or
R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\Psi,\varepsilon}
to denote the variety of
\varepsilon
-representations of a fixed dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
. This is sloppy, since its points are defined only up to isomorphisms, but we will be very careful and use it only when there is no possibility of confusion. Similarly, we use the notation
\G_{\mathbf{d}}^{\varepsilon}
instead of
\G^{\bullet}(V,\langle-,-\rangle)
or
\G^{\bullet}(V,\Psi)
when the dependency on the form is not so relevant.

2.7.
Isotropic subrepresentations

Let
M\in R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\Psi,\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
–representation with respect to the isomorphism
\Psi
induced by the
\varepsilon
-form
\langle-,-\rangle
on the underlying vector space

In terms of the isomorphism

\iota(N)^{\perp}=\textrm{Ker}(\nabla\iota\circ\Psi)\simeq\nabla(M/\iota(N))

By definition

\textstyle{N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\textstyle{M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{0}

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\iota(N)^{\perp}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

∎

By definition,

Corollary 2.9
.

Let

\mathcal{A}
–representation, and

be an
\varepsilon
–representation. Let

.

2.8.
Indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representations

Given two
\varepsilon
-representations
(M_{1},\Psi_{1})
and
(M_{2},\Psi_{2})
their

is the
\varepsilon
-representation
(M_{1}\oplus M_{2},\Psi_{1}\oplus\Psi_{2})
. An
\varepsilon

\varepsilon

if it is not the direct sum of two non-trivial

-representations.
By the Theorem of Krull, Remak and Schmidt and by Theorem

, every
\varepsilon
–representation can be written in an essentially unique way as a direct sum of indecomposable
\varepsilon
–representations.
The following description of the indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representations is proved in [Proposition 2.7]
[
15
]
for symmetric quivers without relations (see also
[
25
, Lemma 4.5]
);

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
M
be an indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representation. Then one and only one of the following three cases can occur:

(I)

M
is indecomposable as an
\mathcal{A}
-representation. In this case
M
is called of type
(I)
, for “indecomposable”.

(S)

There exists an indecomposable
\mathcal{A}
-representation
T
such that
M=T\oplus\nabla T
and
T\not\simeq\nabla T
. In this case

is called of type
(S)
, for “split”.

(R)

There exists an indecomposable
\mathcal{A}
-representation

T\simeq\nabla T
. In this case

Proof.

The proof of Derksen and Weyman [Proposition 2.7]
[
15
]
uses only the fact that the endomorphism ring of an indecomposable representation is local; since this fact is true for every algebra by Fitting’s lemma

6

, the same proof applies to the case of symmetric quiver algebras.
∎

3.
Isotropic degenerations

In order to approach the degeneration order for (non-symmetric)
\mathcal{A}
-representations there is a famous second ordering, namely the Ext-order. It is defined on the isoclasses of
\mathcal{A}
–representations of dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
as follows (
[
1
,
7
,
22
,
21
]
):
Given
M,N\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
,

(3.1)

\textstyle{M\leq_{\Ext}N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
def
\textstyle{\textrm{There exist representations }M_{1},\cdots,M_{k}\textrm{ such that for }}
\textstyle{\textrm{ every }i\textrm{ there exists a short exact sequences }}
\textstyle{0\rightarrow U_{i}\rightarrow M_{i-1}\rightarrow V_{i}\rightarrow 0}
\textstyle{\textrm{ such that }M_{1}=M,M_{k}=N,\,M_{i}\simeq U_{i}\oplus V_{i}.}

Indeed, by
[
7
, Lemma 2.1]

\textstyle{\leq_{\Ext}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\leq_{\deg}}
.
In this section we provide an analogue of this result for
\varepsilon
-representations.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
M\in R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\Psi,\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
–representation and let
\iota:L\hookrightarrow M
be an isotropic subrepresentation. Then

(3.2)

M\leq_{\deg}^{\varepsilon}L\oplus\nabla L\oplus(\iota(L)^{\perp}/\iota(L)).

More precisely, there is a one-parameter subgroup
\lambda(t)\in\G^{\bullet}(V,\Psi)
and a point

such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\lambda(t).m\in\G^{\bullet}(V,\Psi)(L\oplus\nabla L\oplus(\iota(L)^{\perp}/\iota(L)))

Proof.

Since
\iota(L)\subset V
is isotropic, for every

we can choose a basis of

, extend it to a basis of
\iota(L)^{\perp}_{i}\subset V_{i}
and then complete it to a basis
\mathcal{B}_{i}
of

so that the
\varepsilon

is represented in the basis

and the dual basis

by the matrices

\Psi_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&\varepsilon\mathbf{1}\\
0&\varphi_{i}&0\\

where

which do not belong to

and

is the induced

Let

be an arrow of

M_{\alpha}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}L_{\alpha}&\nu_{\alpha}&\xi_{\alpha}\\

where

(there are zeros below the diagonal because both

and
\iota(L)^{\perp}
are subrepresentations of

). For

\lambda(t)_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}t\mathbf{1}&0&0\\

for

and
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}M(t)\cong L\oplus Y\oplus\nabla L

Definition 3.2

Let

\textstyle{M\leq_{\Ext}^{\varepsilon}N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{def}
\textstyle{\textrm{There exist }\varepsilon-\textrm{representations }M_{1},\cdots,M_{k}\in R(\mathcal{A},V)^{\varepsilon}}

Theorem

implies at once:

\textstyle{\leq_{\Ext}^{\varepsilon}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

4.
Degenerations and Bongartz’s cancellation theorem

In this section we recall the definition of the degeneration, the Hom, and the Ext order on the variety of
\mathcal{A}
-representations of a fixed dimension vector. Then we recall Bongartz’s cancellation theorem and how to use it to show that these three orders coincide for Dynkin quivers. This argument is analogous to the argument that we will use to prove the main theorem in Section

.

4.1.
Degeneration, Hom and Ext order

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a quiver algebra. Given two
\mathcal{A}
-representations
X
and
Y
we use the notation
[X,Y]:=\textrm{dim Hom}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,Y)
and
[X,Y]^{1}:=\textrm{dim Ext}^{1}_{\mathcal{A}}(X,Y)
. We denote by
\ind(\mathcal{A})
the set of isoclasses of indecomposable
\mathcal{A}
-modules.
Let
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{Q_{0}}
be a dimension vector and let
V
be a
Q_{0}
-graded complex vector space of dimension vector
\mathbf{d}
.

•

The
degeneration order

\leq_{\deg}
on
R(\mathcal{A},V)
is defined by

\textstyle{M\leq_{\deg}N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{def}
\textstyle{N\in\overline{\GL^{\bullet}(V)M}.}

•

The Hom-order
\leq_{\Hom}
on
R(\mathcal{A},V)
is defined by

\textstyle{M\leq_{\Hom}N\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{def}
\textstyle{[M,E]\leq[N,E]\textrm{ for every }E\in\ind(\mathcal{A}).}

•

The Ext-order
\leq_{\Ext}
on

is defined in (
3.1
).

These orders were first considered by Abeasis-Del Fra for quivers of type

[
1
,
2
]
, and then extended to general quiver algebras by Riedtmann
[
22
]
, Bongartz
[
7
]
and Zwara
[

]
.
It is well-known that

\textstyle{\leq_{\Ext}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\leq_{\deg}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\leq_{\Hom}}

(see
[

,
[

It is a remarkable fact that these three orders are equivalent for every algebra of finite representation type whose indecomposables are all rigid

. In particular, they are all equivalent for Dynkin quivers. In the subsequent Section

we recall the proof of this result.

4.2.
Generic quotients and generic kernels

We recall from
[
7
, Section 2.3]
the crucial definition of generic quotients and of generic kernels.

Let
M\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
. Let
\iota:L\hookrightarrow M
be a subrepresentation of dimension vector
\mathbf{e}
. For every
i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}
, we fix a basis
\{v_{1}^{(i)},\cdots,v_{e_{i}}^{(i)}\}
of the vector subspace
\iota(L)_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
and extend it to a basis
\mathcal{B}_{i}
of
V_{i}
. With this choice we identify
E_{i}=\textrm{End}(V_{i})
with the vector space of
d_{i}\times d_{i}
-matrices and
\GL^{\bullet}(V)\subset E=\oplus_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}E_{i}
as the open subset of tuples of invertible matrices. Moreover,
M=(M_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is represented in these bases as a collection of
2\times 2
upper triangular block matrices of the form
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\scriptstyle L_{\alpha}}&{\scriptstyle\star}\\
{\scriptstyle 0}&{\scriptstyle\star}\end{array}\right)
. Let
G(L,M)\subset\GL^{\bullet}(V)
be the set of all collections
g=(g_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}
of invertible matrices such that for every arrow
\alpha:i\rightarrow j
of
\mathcal{Q}
the matrix
g_{j}^{-1}M_{\alpha}g_{i}
is a
2\times 2
upper triangular block matrix having
L_{\alpha}
as upper left block. Let
U=\iota(L)\subset V
and let us consider the representation variety
R(\mathcal{Q},V/U)
. Bongartz considers the map
\varphi:G(L,M)\rightarrow R(\mathcal{Q},V/U)
which associates to
g
the collection
Q=(Q_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of matrices such that
Q_{\alpha}
is the lower right block of
g_{j}^{-1}M_{\alpha}g_{i}
. The image of
\varphi
is the set of all possible quotients of
M
by
L
and it is denoted by
\textrm{Quot}(L,M)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

\Quot(L,M)
is an irreducible, constructible subset of
R(\mathcal{Q},V/U)
which is stable by the action of
\GL^{\bullet}(V/U)
.

Proof.

Given
f_{i}\in E_{i}
we denote by
\overline{f}_{i}
the matrix consisting of the first
e_{i}
columns of
f_{i}
. Then Bongartz points out that
g\in G(L,M)
if and only if
M_{\alpha}\overline{g_{s(\alpha)}}=\overline{g_{t(\alpha)}}L_{\alpha}
. These equations are linear in the entries of the matrices
\overline{g}
and hence of
g
. Thus
G(L,M)
is an open and dense subset of the vector subspace
E(L,M)\subset E
consisting of all
f=(f_{i})
such that
M_{\alpha}\overline{f_{s(\alpha)}}=\overline{f_{t(\alpha)}}L_{\alpha}
. In particular,
G(L,M)
is an irreducible locally closed set. Thus,
\textrm{Quot}(L,M)
is a constructible irreducible set, by Chevalley’s theorem. The fact that it is stable by base change is obvious, since if
Q
is a quotient of
M
by
L
and
Q^{\prime}\simeq Q
then
Q^{\prime}
is a quotient of
M
by
L
.
∎

A quotient
Q
of
M
by
L
is called a
generic quotient
of
M
by
L
if its orbit is dense in
\textrm{Quot}(L,M)
. In this case, a monomorphism
\iota:L\hookrightarrow M
such that
Q\simeq M/\iota(L)
is called a

L
into
M
. Of course, generic quotients and generic embeddings might not exist. They exist in case
\mathcal{A}
has finite representation type. In particular, they exists in case
\mathcal{A}
is the path algebra of a Dynkin quiver.

Dually, let

4.3.
The cancellation theorem of Bongartz

We recall the famous “cancellation theorem” of Bongartz
[
7
, Theorem 2.4]
and how to use it to prove the equivalence of the three orders of Subsection
4.1
for Dynkin quivers.

Theorem 4.2

(Cancellation theorem)
.

[
7
, Theorem 2.4]

Let
M,N\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
such that
M\leq_{\deg}N
.

(i)

Let
L\in\Rep(\mathcal{Q})
such that
[L,M]=[L,N]
. Then if
L
embeds into
N
,
L
embeds into
M
, too. In this case, if the generic quotient of
M
by
L
exists, it degenerates to every quotient of
N
by
L
.

(ii)

Let
Q\in\Rep(\mathcal{Q})
such that
[M,Q]=[N,Q]
. Then if
N
surjects onto
Q
,
M
surjects onto
Q
, too. In this case, if the generic kernel of
M
onto
Q
exists, it degenerates to the kernel of every surjective map from
N
to
Q
.

A connected quiver
\mathcal{Q}
is called a Dynkin quiver if it is an orientation of a simply-laced Dynkin diagram of type
A
,
D
or
E
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a Dynkin quiver and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{k}\mathcal{Q}
be its path algebra. Then the three partial orders
\leq_{\Ext}
,
\leq_{\deg}
and
\leq_{\Hom}
are equivalent on
R(\mathcal{A},V)
, for all
V
.

Proof.

Let
M,N\in R(\mathcal{A},V)
such that
M\leq_{\deg}N
. Let us prove that
M\leq_{\Ext}N
by induction on their total dimension. If the dimension is zero, there is nothing to prove. Suppose that the dimension is greater than zero. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is Dynkin, its path algebra
\mathcal{A}
is representation-directed (
[
6
, Lemma IX.1.1 and Definition IX.3.2]
). In particular, there exists a partial order
\preceq
on
\ind(\mathcal{A})
defined by
L\preceq E
if there is a sequence of non-zero non-isomorphisms
L=L(1)\rightarrow L(2)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow L(h)=E
for some
L(i)\in\ind(\mathcal{A})
. Let
L
be a
\preceq
-minimal indecomposable direct summand of
N
. Then
[L,N]^{1}=0
. By upper semicontinuity, we have
[L,M]^{1}\leq[L,N]^{1}=0
which implies, using the homological interpretation (
5.1
) of the Euler-Ringel-form for hereditary algebras, that
[L,N]=[L,M]
. By Theorem
4.2
,
L
embeds into
M
. Let
Q
be the generic quotient of
M
by
L
. Then
Q\leq_{\deg}\overline{N}
where
\overline{N}
is the direct complement of
L
in
N
. By induction we get
Q\leq_{\Ext}\overline{N}
. Since there is a short exact sequence
0\rightarrow L\rightarrow M\rightarrow Q\rightarrow 0
we get
M\leq_{\Ext}L\oplus Q\leq_{\Ext}L\oplus\overline{N}=N
, as claimed.
The proof of the implication
\textstyle{\leq_{\Hom}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\leq_{\deg}}
is given in
[
7
, Proposition 3.2]
for representation-directed algebras and it does not seem to simplify when restricted to Dynkin quivers.
∎

5.1.
Representation Theory

We denote the category of finite-dimensional complex
\mathcal{Q}
-representations by

The category

The set

its projective cover, and by

are the vertices (possibly equal to

\textstyle{j\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{t_{k}}
and
\textstyle{s_{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

and

are the terminal vertices of the arrows of

starting from

An indecomposable
\mathcal{Q}
–representation

Every

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

or equivalently the subrepresentations of a projective are projective. Moreover, for every

to the standard resolution of

(5.1)

[M,N]-[M,N]^{1}=\langle\mathbf{dim}\,M,\mathbf{dim}\,N\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}.

Here, for

the

\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{Q}_{0}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{Q}_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}

\langle\mathbf{e},\mathbf{d}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}}e_{i}d_{i}-\sum_{\alpha:i\rightarrow j\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}}e_{i}d_{j}.

We notice that

(5.2)

d_{k}=\langle\mathbf{d},{\bf dim}\,I_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}=\langle{\bf dim}\,P_{k},\mathbf{d}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}.

Since
\ind(\mathcal{Q})
is finite, for every dimension vector
\mathbf{d}

contains a dense

-orbit. A point of this orbit is called a

\mathcal{Q}
-representation of dimension vector

The

(5.3)

For every

A

Lemma 5.1

Let

(i)

[L,E]\leq 1

(ii)

Let

For (i), we notice that

In this subsection we recall the definition of the Auslander-Reiten quiver of

and how to use it to get a basis of the Hom and Ext-space between two indecomposable

Let

–representation. Since

The

; the algorithm stops when

Proposition 5.9
.

Let

. Then

from

to

to

to

We fix

and let

\ind(\mathcal{Q})

and there is a unique (trivial) path from

to

let

Suppose, first, that

and there are no paths from

to

. If

is projective, then

if and only if the vertices

and

\mathcal{Q}
are joined by a directed path
\textstyle{i\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{j}
of
\mathcal{Q}
. In this case, there is a unique path from

it is not a zero-relation. This proves the lemma in the case when

Let us assume that

is not projective, in particular

) ending in

We assume from now on that there exists a path from

Let

Remark 5.12
.

exists if and only if there exists a rectangle from

to

. By Proposition

, this happens if and only if there exists a short exact sequence

. Moreover, by Lemma

,

and

exist if and only if the sectional paths

and

are mono. We write

{\bf dim}\,T^{0}-{\bf dim}\,L=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\left({\bf dim}\,T^{0}_{i}+{\bf dim}\,L_{(i+1)}-{\bf dim}\,L_{(i)}\right)

with the convention that
L_{(0)}=L
,
T^{0}_{0}
and

are zero. We show that

is defined if and only if
{\bf dim}\,T^{0}-{\bf dim}\,L
is a dimension vector. In this case, it equals
{\bf dim}\,T^{1}
by the discussion above. We put
\mathbf{t}_{i}:={\bf dim}\,T^{0}_{i}+{\bf dim}\,L_{(i+1)}-{\bf dim}\,L_{(i)}
.

Let us prove that if there exists an index

such that

does not exist then

is not a dimension vector. The argument we use is slightly different in the case when

, and we include this case in the general discussion by highlighting the differences in parentheses. The SE sectional path starting from

(or the

. The almost split sequence ending in

is not a zero-relation, because, otherwise,

and

would exist by Lemma

. Thus, there exists an indecomposable injective

and an NE arrow (or SE arrow for

)

in

. Then,
[L_{(i)},I_{k}]=1
for every

,
[L_{(i)},I_{k}]=0
for every

. By (

is

(\mathbf{t}_{i})_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&\textrm{if }i=j;\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We conclude that

{\bf dim}\,T^{0}-{\bf dim}\,L
is not a dimension vector, and hence

cannot embed into

and a fortiori into
M
.

Suppose that

is defined. Then
{\bf dim}\,T^{1}={\bf dim}\,T^{0}-{\bf dim}\,L
. Obviously,

embeds into

and

is rigid, it is generic and hence

. By Bongartz’s Theorem

embeds into
T^{0}
and since

is rigid,

is the generic quotient of

by

∎

6.1.
Split and non-split types

There are four possible types for the pair
(\mathcal{Q},\varepsilon)
:
(A_{odd},\pm 1)
and
(A_{even},\pm 1)
. The main difference between the four types
arises in the structure of their indecomposable
\varepsilon
–representations (see Section
2.8
): only in types
(A_{odd},1)
and
(A_{even},-1)
, there are indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representations of type
(I)
. In the other types
(A_{odd},-1)
and
(A_{even},1)
the indecomposable
\varepsilon
-representations are all of the form
L\oplus\nabla L
, for
L\in\ind(\mathcal{Q})
. This is due to the fact that the indecomposable
\mathcal{Q}
-representations are thin and a symplectic vector space must be even dimensional, compare with
[
15
, Proposition 3.6 and 3.8]
. For this reason we write that
(A_{odd},-1)
and
(A_{even},1)
are the
split types
while
(A_{odd},1)
and
(A_{even},-1)
are the
non-split types
. Accordingly, we say that
(\mathcal{Q},\varepsilon)
is of split or non-split type.

In the next lemmas we use the notation introduced in (
2.3
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
M\in\Rep(\mathcal{Q})
and let
f\in\Hom(M,\nabla M)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
.
Then the image of
f
is an
\varepsilon
–representation.

Proof.

On
\textrm{Im}(f)
we define the bilinear form
\langle f(x),f(y)\rangle=f(x)(y)
. This is a non-degenerate,
\sigma
-compatible
\varepsilon
-form on the
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
-graded vector space
\textrm{Im}(f)
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
L\in\ind(\mathcal{Q})
.

(i)

In type
A_{odd}
,
\Hom(L,\nabla L)=\Hom(L,\nabla L)^{\nabla}
.

(ii)

In type
A_{even}
,
\Hom(L,\nabla L)=\Hom(L,\nabla L)^{-\nabla}
.

In other words,
\Hom(L,\nabla L)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
is zero if
(\mathcal{Q},\varepsilon)
is of split type.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
,
[L,\nabla L]\leq 1
. If
[L,\nabla L]=0
, then there is nothing to prove. If
[L,\nabla L]=1
, let
f:L\rightarrow\nabla L
be a non-zero homomorphism. Suppose that
\Hom(L,\nabla L)=\Hom(L,\nabla L)^{\varepsilon\nabla}
for some
\varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\}
. Then, by Lemma
5.2
, the image of
f
is indecomposable and by Lemma
6.1
it is an
\varepsilon
-representation. Thus
(\mathcal{Q},\varepsilon)
must be of non-split type, i.e.
\varepsilon=+1
for
A_{odd}
and
\varepsilon=-1
for
A_{even}
.
∎

\displaystyle[N,E]^{1}-[M,E]^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\tau^{-}E,N]-[\tau^{-}E,M]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[N,\nabla\tau^{-}E]-[M,\nabla\tau^{-}E]

\displaystyle[N,\tau\nabla E]-[M,\tau\nabla E].\hskip 85.35826pt\qed

Lemma 6.8

Let

-fixed if and only if

is

-invariant.

Suppose that

-fixed. If

6.3.
Generic isotropic embeddings

We state and prove a surprising result that says that under certain mild hypotheses, it is always possible to embed an indecomposable inside an
\varepsilon

Lemma 6.11
