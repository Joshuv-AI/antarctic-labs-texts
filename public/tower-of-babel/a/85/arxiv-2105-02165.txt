Convergence of first-order Finite Volume Method based on Exact Riemann Solver for the Complete Compressible Euler Equations
Mária Lukáčová-Medvi d’ ová, Yuhuan Yuan

Abstract
Recently developed concept of dissipative measure-valued solution for compressible flows is a suitable tool to describe oscillations and singularities possibly developed in solutions of multidimensional Euler equations. In this paper we study the convergence of the first-order finite volume method based on the exact Riemann solver for the complete compressible Euler equations. Specifically, we derive entropy inequality and prove the consistency of numerical method. Passing to the limit, we show the weak and strong convergence of numerical solutions and identify their limit. The numerical results presented for the spiral, Kelvin-Helmholtz and the Richtmyer-Meshkov problem are consistent with our theoretical analysis.



1
Introduction

Hyperbolic conservation laws play an important role in describing many physical and engineering process.
An iconic example is the nonlinear system of compressible Euler equations, which governs the dynamics of a compressible material and incorporates mass, momentum and energy conservation.

A characteristic feature of nonlinear conservation laws is that discontinuities (shock waves) may develop after finite time even if the initial condition is smooth.
A natural way to overcome this difficulty would be to work with a concept of weak distributional solution.
However, it is well-known that such weak solutions fail to be unique.
Consequently, the second law of thermodynamics have been proposed as a selection criterion to rule out nonphysical solutions.
The entropy production principle have been successfully applied in the scalar multidimensional equations
[
27
]
and one-dimensional systems
[
3
,
4
]
.
Unfortunately, it completely fails for multidimensional systems.
Recently, De Lellis and Székelyhidi
[
8
]
and Chiodaroli et al.
[
7
]
showed non-uniqueness of weak entropy solutions for the multidimensional isentropic compressible Euler system, see also
[
8
]
for similar non-uniqueness results for the incompressible Euler system.
These results have been extended to the complete Euler system in Feireisl et al.
[
13
]
.

In order to describe oscillations arising in the limits of singular perturbations of hyperbolic conservation laws, a more generalized solution, i.e. measure-valued (MV) solution, was suggested by DiPerna.
In 1985 he showed for one-dimensional hyperbolic conservation laws that regularized solutions of associated diffusive and dispersive regularized systems converge to a MV solution, as a regularized parameter vanishes
[
10
]
.
Recently the concept of MV solution was adapted and applied for multidimensional compressible Euler system
[
26
]
and three-dimensional incompressible Euler equations
[
11
]
,
see also
[
2
,
28
]
.

In this paper we work with the concept of
dissipative measure-valued (DMV) solution
for the complete Euler system,
which enjoys the weak-strong uniqueness principle
[
5
]
.
Analogous concept has been adopted for the insentropic Euler system
[
23
]
, compressible Navier-Stokes system
[
12
]
, elastodynamics
[
9
]
and other related problems.

In the convergence analysis of numerical schemes for hyperbolic conservation laws
the entropy stability plays a crucial role, we refer a reader to a seminal paper of Tadmor
[
29
]
.
For multidimensional hyperbolic conservation laws the convergence analysis to MV solutions was studied by
Fjordholm et al.

[
22
,
21
]
.
For the Lax-Friedrichs and vanishing viscosity finite volume method,
Feireisl, Lukáčová and Mizerová generalized the above convergence result for the Euler system and proved the convergence to the DMV solution and the classical solution on its lifespan
[
15
,
14
]
.
For generalization to viscous compressible flows we refer a reader to
[
17
,
18
]
.
In

[
16
,
19
]
a new tool,
\mathcal{K}
-convergence, has been developed to compute strong limits of oscillatory sequences of numerical solutions.

In the present paper we focus on the first-order finite volume method based on the exact Riemann problem solver for the complete compressible Euler system and show its convergence via
the concept of DMV solutions.
We will only assume that our numerical solutions stay in a physically non-degenerate region, i.e. density is bounded from below and energy is bounded from above.
Interestingly, this assumption is equivalent to the strict convexity of the mathematical entropy, see Lemma
B.4
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2 we introduce suitable notations and describe the finite volume method with a numerical flux based on the exact solution of the local Riemann problem, cf. Godunov method.
In Section 3 we analyze the entropy inequality and give an explicit lower bound of the entropy Hessian matrix, see Appendix
B
.
A crucial step is to show the consistency of our numerical method.
Section 4 is devoted to the limiting process.
We prove that the numerical solutions will generate a weakly-(*) convergent subsequence and a Young measure, a DMV solution, to the Euler system.
Furthermore, employing the theory of
\mathcal{K}
-convergence and DMV–strong uniqueness principle, we obtain the strong convergence of the Cesàro averages and strong convergence of numerical solutions to the weak/strong/classical solution.
Finally, in Section 5 we present numerical simulations and illustrate the effects of
\mathcal{K}
-convergence of numerical solutions.
The numerical results clearly demonstrate convergence results being consistent with our analysis.
As far as we know this is the first result in literature where the convergence of a well-known Godunov method has been proved rigorously for multidimensional Euler equations.

2
Numerical method

The complete Euler system can be written in the divergence form

\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}+\mbox{div}_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$})=0,\quad(t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$})\in(0,T)\times\Omega,

(2.1)

where
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$},\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}
denote the conservative vector and flux, defined by

\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}=(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},E)^{T},\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$})=(\rho u_{i},\,u_{i}\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}+p\mbox{\boldmath\small$e$}_{i},u_{i}(E+p))^{T},\hskip 28.45274pti=1,\cdots,d.

Here
\rho
denotes density,
\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}:=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})
velocity,
\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}:=\rho\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}
momentum,
E:=\frac{\rho|\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}|^{2}}{2}+\rho e
total energy,
p
pressure,
e
internal energy, and the row vector
\mbox{\boldmath\small$e$}_{i}
represents the
i
-th row of the unit matrix of size
d:=\mbox{dim}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}),d=1,2,3
.

Throughout the whole text, we consider the bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
together with the space-periodic or no-flux boundary condition.
Moreover, the equation of state is restricted to

p=(\gamma-1)\rho e,

(2.2)

where
\gamma\in(1,2]
is the adiabatic constant.

Remark 2.1
.

For the Euler system (
2.1
) the mathematical entropy pair can be given by

\eta=\frac{-\rho S}{\gamma-1},\quad\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}=\frac{-\rho S\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}}{\gamma-1}=\eta\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}

(2.3)

with thermodynamic entropy
S
defined by

S:=\ln(p)-\gamma\ln(\rho).

(2.4)

The corresponding entropy variable and entropy potential are given by

\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}:=\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}}\eta=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\gamma-S}{\gamma-1}-\frac{\rho|\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}|^{2}}{2p}\\
\frac{\rho\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}}{p}\\
-\frac{\rho}{p}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{\boldmath\small$\psi$}=\rho\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$},

(2.5)

respectively.
We require that in addition to (
2.1
) the entropy inequality holds, i.e.

\partial_{t}\,\eta(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$})+\rm{div}_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$})\leq 0.

(2.6)

Spatial discretization and notations

The computational domain
\Omega
consists of rectangular meshes
\overline{\Omega}:=\bigcup_{K}\overline{K}
.
We denote the set of all mesh cells as
\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
\sigma
stands for cell face, a unit normal vector to
\sigma
is
n

the face between two neighboring cells
K

L

\sigma_{K,L}=K|L
,
and the set of all interior faces is given as
\Sigma_{\tt int}=\{\sigma\in\Sigma~:~\sigma\cap\partial\Omega=\emptyset\}

Moreover, for

a^{\tt out}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$})=\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0+}a(\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}+\delta\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}),\quad a^{\tt in}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$})=\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0+}a(\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}-\delta\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$})

(2.7)

and introduce the standard average- and jump-operators

\displaystyle\left\{a\right\}=\frac{a^{\tt in}+a^{\tt out}}{2},\quad\left[\left[a\right]\right]=a^{\tt out}-a^{\tt in}.

(2.8)

We use the notation

if there exists a generic constant

independent on the mesh discretization, such that
a\leq C\cdot b
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.2
Discrete function space and finite volume method

Consider the space of piecewise constant functions

\mathcal{Q}_{h}(\Omega)=\{v:v|_{K^{o}}=\mbox{constant},~\mbox{for all}~K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

(2.9)

We can define the projection

\Pi_{h}:L^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{h}(\Omega),\quad\Pi_{h}[\phi]_{K}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\phi(x)~dx,

(2.10)

where
|K|
is the Lebesgue measure of
K
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{h}\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})
.
We consider a semi-discrete finite volume method

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K}(t)+(\mbox{div}_{h}\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{h}(t))_{K}=0,\quad t>0,~K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K}(0)=\varPi_{h}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{0})_{K},\quad K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

(2.11b)

where the numerical flux function
\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{h}
is given by means of the exact Riemann solver
[
30
]
.
Consequently, (
2.11a
) can be rewritten as

\int_{K}\frac{d}{dt}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K}(t)~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}+\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma_{K,L}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}=0,

(2.12)

where
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma_{K,L}}
is the solution of a local Riemann problem at face
\sigma_{K,L}
.
A piecewise constant solution
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{h}=\{\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K};K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
satisfies an equivalent weak form

\int_{\Omega}\phi\frac{d}{dt}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{h}~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}-\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\tt int}}\int_{\sigma}\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}\left[\left[\phi\right]\right]~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}=0,

(2.13)

where
\phi\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}(\Omega)
.

Remark 2.2
.

From here on, we write
(x,y)^{T}
rather than
x
for
d=2
, and
(x,y,z)^{T}
for
d=3
.
For a two-dimensional uniform mesh a general cell is denoted by
K_{ij}=(x_{i-\frac{1}{2}},x_{i+\frac{1}{2}})\times(y_{j-\frac{1}{2}},y_{j+\frac{1}{2}})

with
(x_{i},y_{j})
as the center,
\Delta x=x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{i-\frac{1}{2}}
and
\Delta y=y_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-y_{j-\frac{1}{2}}
.
Hence, (
2.12
) can be rewritten as

\frac{d}{dt}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{{ij}}(t)+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j})-\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j})\right)+\frac{1}{\Delta y}\left(\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}})-\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{2}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}})\right)=0,

where
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{ij}:=\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K_{ij}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}
is the solution at
(x_{i+\frac{1}{2}},t+0)
of the following local Riemann problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{\tau}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}+\partial_{x}\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{1}=0,\quad\tau>t,\\
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{{i,j}}(t),&\mbox{ if }~x<x_{i+\frac{1}{2}},\\
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{{i+1,j}}(t),&\mbox{ if }~x>x_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\end{cases}\end{cases}

and
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}
is the solution at
(y_{j+\frac{1}{2}},t+0)
of local Riemann problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{\tau}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}+\partial_{y}\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{2}=0,\quad\tau>t,\\
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}(y,t)=\begin{cases}\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{{i,j}}(t),&\mbox{ if }~y<y_{j+\frac{1}{2}},\\
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{{i,j+1}}(t),&\mbox{ if }~y>y_{j+\frac{1}{2}}.\end{cases}\end{cases}

3
Consistency

Before proving the consistency of finite volume method (
2.13
) we formulate a physically reasonable assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that

0<\underline{\rho}\leq\rho_{h},\quad E_{h}\leq\overline{E}\quad\mbox{uniformly for }h\rightarrow 0,~t\in[0,T],

(3.1)

where
\underline{\rho},\overline{E}
are positive constants.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under Assumption
3.1

it holds

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\rho}\leq\rho_{h}\leq\overline{\rho},\quad|\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}|\leq\overline{u},\quad 0<\underline{p}\leq p_{h}\leq\overline{p},~

(3.2)

\displaystyle|\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}|\leq\overline{m},\quad 0<\underline{E}\leq E_{h}\leq\overline{E},\quad 0<\underline{\vartheta}\leq\vartheta_{h}\leq\overline{\vartheta}

(3.3)

uniformly for
h\rightarrow 0,t\in[0,T]
with positive constants
\underline{\rho},\,\overline{\rho},\,\overline{u},\,\underline{p},\,\overline{p},\,\overline{m},\,\underline{E},\,\overline{E},\,\underline{\vartheta},\,\overline{\vartheta}
, where
\vartheta:=\frac{p}{\rho}
is the temperature.

Proof.

Here we only give the idea and framework of the proof. More details could be found in
[
15
]
.

It is easy to observe that

\displaystyle p=(\gamma-1)\left[E-\frac{|\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}|^{2}}{2\rho}\right]\leq(\gamma-1)E\leq(\gamma-1)\overline{E},\quad|\mbox{\boldmath\small$u$}|^{2}\leq\frac{2E}{\rho}\leq\frac{2\overline{E}}{\underline{\rho}},

which implies

\overline{p}=(\gamma-1)\overline{E},\quad\overline{u}=\sqrt{\frac{2\overline{E}}{\underline{\rho}}},\quad\overline{\vartheta}=\frac{\overline{p}}{\underline{\rho}}.

On the other hand, we use the fact that the finite volume method (
2.13
) is entropy stable, cf.
[
25
,
6
]
.
Thus, using the renormalized entropy

\displaystyle\eta=\rho\chi(S),\quad\chi^{\prime}(z)\geq 0,\quad\chi(z)=\begin{cases}<0,&\mbox{\it if }~z\leq z_{0},\\
=0,&\mbox{\it if }~z\geq z_{0},\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle z_{0}=(\gamma-1)\ln(1/\overline{C}),\quad\overline{C}:=\max_{K}\frac{\rho_{K}(0)}{\vartheta_{K}(0)^{1/(\gamma-1)}}

implies

0<\rho_{h}\leq\overline{C}\vartheta_{h}^{1/(\gamma-1)}.

(3.4)

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\rho}^{\gamma}\leq\rho_{h}^{\gamma}\leq\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}\rho_{h}\vartheta_{h}=\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}p_{h}\leq\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}(\gamma-1)E_{h}\leq\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}(\gamma-1)\overline{E},

which gives

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}=\big(\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}(\gamma-1)\overline{E}\big)^{1/\gamma},\quad\overline{m}=\sqrt{\overline{\rho}\overline{E}},\quad\underline{p}=\frac{\underline{\rho}^{\gamma}}{\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}},\quad\underline{E}=\frac{\underline{\rho}^{\gamma}}{(\gamma-1)\overline{C}^{\gamma-1}},\quad\underline{\vartheta}=\frac{\underline{p}}{\overline{\rho}}

and concludes the proof.
∎

3.1
Entropy inequality

In
[
25
]
Harten proved that the Godunov finite volume method based on the exact solution of the Riemann problem satisfies the entropy inequality (in one-dimensional case)

\eta(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{i}(t_{n+1}))-\eta(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{i}(t_{n}))\leq\frac{\tau}{\Delta x}\left(-\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i+1/2})+\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{i-1/2})\right),

where
\tau=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
is the time-step.
Generalizing to multi-dimensions and writing it in the semi-discretization form give

\int_{K}\frac{d}{dt}\eta_{K}(t)~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}+\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma_{K,L}})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\leq 0.

(3.5)

Further, we get the equivalent weak form

\int_{\Omega}\phi\frac{d}{dt}\eta_{h}~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}-\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\tt int}}\int_{\sigma}\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}\left[\left[\phi\right]\right]~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\leq 0

(3.6)

with
\phi\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}(\Omega),\phi\geq 0
and
\eta_{h}=\eta(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{h})
.

On the other hand, Chen and Shu in
[
6
]
showed that the Godunov finite volume method is entropy stable, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{K}\frac{d}{dt}\eta_{K}(t)~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}+\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\mbox{\boldmath\small$Q$}_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\leq 0

(3.7)

with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath\small$Q$}_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right\}_{\sigma}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K})-\left\{\mbox{\boldmath\small$\psi$}\right\}_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}.

Writing explicitly the entropy dissipation we get

\displaystyle\int_{K}\frac{d}{dt}\eta_{K}(t)~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}+\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\mbox{\boldmath\small$Q$}_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}r_{\sigma}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}},

(3.8)

where

\displaystyle r_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$})-\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\psi$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}

\displaystyle=\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}\cdot\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\bigg[\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}^{\it RP}_{\sigma}))-\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}(\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}(s))\bigg]\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}~ds:=\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$D$}_{\sigma}\cdot\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right]\right]_{\sigma},

and
\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}(s)=\left\{\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right\}_{\sigma}+s\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}
.
Note that matrix
\mbox{\boldmath\small$D$}_{\sigma}
is symmetric because
\frac{d\mbox{\boldmath\small$F$}_{i}}{d\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}},(i=1,\cdots,d)
is symmetric.
Hence, (
3.7
) implies
r_{\sigma}\leq 0
, i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath\small$v$}\cdot D_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$v$}\leq 0
for any
\mbox{\boldmath\small$v$}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
.
Combining Lemma
3.1
we have
\mbox{\boldmath\small$v$}\cdot D_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$v$}\leq\underline{d}<0
uniformly for
h\rightarrow 0
.
Therefore, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{K}\frac{d}{dt}\eta_{K}(t)~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}+\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\mbox{\boldmath\small$Q$}_{\sigma}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}_{K}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\frac{\underline{d}}{2}\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\|\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$\nu$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\leq\frac{\underline{d}\underline{\eta}}{2}\sum_{\partial K\cap\partial L\neq\emptyset}\int_{\partial K\cap\partial L}\|\left[\left[\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}\right]\right]_{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}~dS_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}},

which implies the weak BV estimate

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L}
and
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}
are connected with the left rarefaction.
Denote

\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{\it Lfan}:=(\rho_{*},\rho_{*}u_{*},E(\rho_{*},u_{*},p_{*}))^{T}

as the value inside left rarefaction fan.
Then it holds

\displaystyle\rho_{L}\geq\rho_{*}\geq\rho_{R},\quad u_{L}\leq u_{*}\leq u_{R},\quad p_{L}\geq p_{*}\geq p_{R}.

(3.20)

Proof.

Since
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L}
and
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}
are connected with the left rarefaction,
then it holds

\displaystyle p_{L}\geq p_{*}\geq p_{R},\quad u_{*}=u_{L}-f_{r}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L}),\quad u_{R}=u_{L}-f_{r}(p_{R},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L}),

where
f_{r}
is defined in (
3.14
).
From
p_{L}\geq p_{*}\geq p_{R}
and
f_{r}(p_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L})=0,~f_{r}^{\prime}>0
we have

u_{L}\leq u_{*}\leq u_{R}.

On the other hand, with the Riemann Invariant
S:=\ln(p/\rho^{\gamma})
we have

\rho_{L}\geq\rho_{*}\geq\rho_{R},

which concludes the proof.
∎

\mbox{\boldmath\small$V$}_{Lfan}

).
Clearly, it is also true for the right rarefaction wave.

\|\mbox{\boldmath\small$V$}_{\it fan}-\mbox{\boldmath\small$V$}_{K}\|
can be controlled by

Lemma 3.4

(Left rarefaction, right rarefaction)
.

Assume the initial data
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}
generate left and right rarefaction waves.
Then it holds

\displaystyle|u_{*}-u_{K}|\leq\left[\left[u\right]\right],

(3.21)

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{K}-p_{*}\leq\rho_{K}a_{K}\left[\left[u\right]\right],

(3.22)

\displaystyle|\rho_{*K}-\rho_{M}|\leq\rho_{K}a_{K}^{-1}\left[\left[u\right]\right]+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

(3.23)

where
K=L,R
and
M=L,R
.

Proof.

Since the initial data
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}
generate left and right rarefaction waves, we have

\displaystyle p_{*}\leq p_{L},\quad p_{*}\leq p_{R}

(3.24)

and

u_{*}=u_{L}-f_{r}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L}),\quad u_{*}=u_{R}+f_{r}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}).

(3.25)

Because of
f_{r}(p_{K},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K})=0,(K=L,R)
and
f^{\prime}_{r}>0
, it holds

u_{*}\geq u_{L}-f_{r}(p_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L})=u_{L},\quad u_{*}\leq u_{R}+f_{r}(p_{R},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R})=u_{R},

(3.26)

which gives

0\leq u_{*}-u_{L}\leq\left[\left[u\right]\right],\quad 0\leq u_{R}-u_{*}\leq\left[\left[u\right]\right].

(3.27)

Consider the left rarefaction wave.
The Riemann Invariant

u_{L}+\frac{2a_{L}}{\gamma-1}=u_{*}+\frac{2a_{*L}}{\gamma-1}

gives

\displaystyle 0\leq a_{L}-a_{*L}=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}(u_{*}-u_{L})\leq\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\left[\left[u\right]\right].

(3.28)

On the other hand, with the help of the Riemann Invariant
S
we have

\displaystyle a_{L}-a_{*L}=\gamma^{1/2}e^{S_{L}/(2\gamma)}\left(p_{L}^{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)}-p_{*}^{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)}\right)=\gamma^{1/2}e^{S_{L}/(2\gamma)}\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}p_{1}^{-(\gamma+1)/(2\gamma)}(p_{L}-p_{*})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\gamma^{1/2}e^{S_{L}/(2\gamma)}\cdot\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}\cdot p_{L}^{-(\gamma+1)/(2\gamma)})(p_{L}-p_{*})=\frac{\gamma^{1/(\gamma-1)}(\gamma-1)}{2}e^{S_{L}/(\gamma-1)}a_{L}^{-(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)}(p_{L}-p_{*})

and

\displaystyle a_{L}-a_{*L}=\gamma^{1/2}e^{S_{L}/2}\left(\rho_{L}^{(\gamma-1)/2}-\rho_{*L}^{(\gamma-1)/2}\right)=\gamma^{1/2}e^{S_{L}/2}\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\rho_{1}^{(\gamma-3)/2}(\rho_{L}-\rho_{*L})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\gamma^{1/2}e^{S_{L}/2}\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\rho_{L}^{(\gamma-3)/2}(\rho_{L}-\rho_{*L})=\frac{\gamma^{1/(\gamma-1)}(\gamma-1)}{2}e^{S_{L}/(\gamma-1)}a_{L}^{(\gamma-3)/(\gamma-1)}(\rho_{L}-\rho_{*L}),

where
p_{1}\in(p_{*},p_{L}),~\rho_{1}\in(\rho_{*L},\rho_{L})
.
Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{L}-p_{*}\leq\gamma^{-1/(\gamma-1)}\cdot e^{-S_{L}/(\gamma-1)}a_{L}^{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)}\left[\left[u\right]\right]=\rho_{L}a_{L}\left[\left[u\right]\right],

\displaystyle 0\leq\rho_{L}-\rho_{*L}\leq\gamma^{-1/(\gamma-1)}e^{-S_{L}/(\gamma-1)}a_{L}^{(3-\gamma)/(\gamma-1)}\left[\left[u\right]\right]=\rho_{L}a_{L}^{-1}\left[\left[u\right]\right].

Analogously, for the right rarefaction wave we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{R}-p_{*}\leq\rho_{R}a_{R}\left[\left[u\right]\right],\quad 0\leq\rho_{R}-\rho_{*R}\leq\rho_{R}a_{R}^{-1}\left[\left[u\right]\right].

Consequently,

\displaystyle|\rho_{R}-\rho_{*L}|\leq|\rho_{R}-\rho_{L}|+|\rho_{L}-\rho_{*L}|\leq\rho_{L}a_{L}^{-1}\left[\left[u\right]\right]+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

\displaystyle|\rho_{L}-\rho_{*R}|\leq|\rho_{R}-\rho_{L}|+|\rho_{R}-\rho_{*R}|\leq\rho_{R}a_{R}^{-1}\left[\left[u\right]\right]+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(Left shock, right shock)
.

Assume the initial data
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}
generate left and right shock waves.
Then it holds

\displaystyle|u_{*}-u_{K}|<|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|,

(3.29)

\displaystyle 0<p_{*}-p_{K}<\left(\gamma\rho_{K}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{K}a_{K}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|,

(3.30)

\displaystyle|\rho_{*K}-\rho_{M}|<a_{K}^{-2}\left(\gamma\rho_{K}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{K}a_{K}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

(3.31)

where
K=L,R
and
M=L,R
.

Proof.

Since the initial data
\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R}
generate left and right shock waves,
we have

\displaystyle p_{*}>p_{L},\quad p_{*}>p_{R},

(3.32)

where
s_{L},s_{R}
are the velocities of the left and right shocks respectively.
According to

u_{*}=u_{L}-f_{s}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L}),\quad u_{*}=u_{R}+f_{s}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R})

and
f_{s}(p_{K},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{K})=0,(K=L,R),~f^{\prime}_{s}>0
, we obtain

u_{*}<u_{L}-f_{s}(p_{L},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{L})=u_{L},\quad u_{*}>u_{R}+f_{s}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R})=u_{R},

(3.33)

which means

0<u_{L}-u_{*}<-\left[\left[u\right]\right],\quad 0<u_{*}-u_{R}<-\left[\left[u\right]\right].

(3.34)

Consider the right shock wave.
With
u_{*}=u_{R}+f_{s}(p_{*},\mbox{\boldmath\small$U$}_{R})
and

s_{R}=u_{R}+a_{R}\left[\frac{(\gamma+1)}{2\gamma}\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}+\frac{(\gamma-1)}{2\gamma}\right]^{1/2}<u_{R}+a_{R}\left(\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}\right)^{1/2},

consequently we derive after some algebraic manipulations

\displaystyle p_{*}-p_{R}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{(\gamma+1)p_{*}+(\gamma-1)p_{R}}{2/\rho_{R}}\right]^{1/2}(u_{*}-u_{R})=\rho_{R}(s_{R}-u_{R})(u_{*}-u_{R})

\displaystyle<\rho_{R}(s_{R}-u_{R})|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|<\rho_{R}a_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|\left(\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}\right)^{1/2}.

Thus,

\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}-\frac{\rho_{R}a_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|}{p_{R}}\left(\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}\right)^{1/2}-1<0,

which gives

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\rho_{R}a_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|}{p_{R}}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\rho_{R}a_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|}{p_{R}}\right)^{2}+4}\right]<\frac{\rho_{R}a_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|}{p_{R}}+2.

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{*}-p_{R}<\left(\gamma\rho_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{R}a_{R}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|.

On the other hand, using

\rho_{*R}=\rho_{R}\left[\frac{\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}}{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\frac{p_{*}}{p_{R}}+1}\right]

we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{*R}-\rho_{R}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{2\rho_{R}}{(\gamma-1)p_{*}+(\gamma+1)p_{R}}\right](p_{*}-p_{R})<\left[\frac{2\rho_{R}}{(\gamma-1)p_{R}+(\gamma+1)p_{R}}\right](p_{*}-p_{R})

\displaystyle=a_{R}^{-2}(p_{*}-p_{R})\leq a_{R}^{-2}\left(\gamma\rho_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{R}a_{R}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|,

which yields
\rho_{*R}-\rho_{R}>0
.

Analogously analyzing the left shock wave we obtain

\displaystyle 0<p_{*}-p_{L}<\left(\gamma\rho_{L}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{L}a_{L}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|,

\displaystyle 0<\rho_{*L}-\rho_{L}<a_{L}^{-2}\left(\gamma\rho_{L}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{L}a_{L}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|.

Further, we have

\displaystyle|\rho_{*L}-\rho_{R}|<a_{L}^{-2}\left(\gamma\rho_{L}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{L}a_{L}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

\displaystyle|\rho_{*R}-\rho_{L}|<a_{R}^{-2}\left(\gamma\rho_{R}|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+\rho_{R}a_{R}\right)|\left[\left[u\right]\right]|+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

which concludes the proof.
∎

(Left rarefaction, right shock)

u_{*}

\displaystyle u_{*}-u_{L}

S

\displaystyle<\gamma^{-1}\exp^{-S_{L}/\gamma}\cdot p_{R}^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma}|\left[\left[p\right]\right]|=\left(\frac{\rho_{L}a_{L}^{2}}{\rho_{R}a_{R}^{2}}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}a_{L}^{-2}=\left(\frac{p_{L}}{p_{R}}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}a_{L}^{-2}|\left[\left[p\right]\right]|,

where

Further, we have

\displaystyle|\rho_{*L}-\rho_{R}|<\left(p_{L}/p_{R}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}a_{L}^{-2}|\left[\left[p\right]\right]|+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

\displaystyle|\rho_{*R}-\rho_{L}|<a_{R}^{-2}|\left[\left[p\right]\right]|+|\left[\left[\rho\right]\right]|,

.

\frac{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}}\otimes\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}}}{\rho_{h_{n}}},\quad p_{h_{n}}:=p(\rho_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}}),\quad E_{h_{n}}:=E(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}}),\quad\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}_{h_{n}}:=\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})

weakly-(*) converge to

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\frac{\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}\otimes\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}}{\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$\rho$}}}\rangle,\quad\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,p(\widetilde{\rho},\widetilde{\eta})\rangle,\quad\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,E(\widetilde{\rho},\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}},\widetilde{\eta})\rangle,\quad\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\widetilde{\rho},\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}},\widetilde{\eta})\rangle

in
L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, respectively.
Consequently, the concentration defects vanish, i.e.
\mathfrak{E}_{cd}\equiv 0,~\mathfrak{R}_{cd}\equiv 0
.

Hence, passing to the limit
h\rightarrow 0
, (
3.46
) in Theorem
3.1
gives

\left[\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\phi~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau}=\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\partial_{t}\phi+\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}\rangle\cdot\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\phi~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}dt

(4.8)

for
\phi\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
Analogously, (
2
) and (
3.48
) in Theorem
3.1
yield

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}\rangle\mbox{\boldmath\small$\phi$}~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}\rangle\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath\small$\phi$}+\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\frac{\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}\otimes\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}}{\widetilde{\rho}}\rangle:\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath\small$\phi$}~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}dt

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,p(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\eta})\rangle\mbox{div}_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\mbox{\boldmath\small$\phi$}~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}dt

for
\mbox{\boldmath\small$\phi$}\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mbox{\boldmath\small$\phi$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath\small$n$}=0
for no-flux boundary condition,
and

\left[\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\tilde{\eta}\rangle\phi~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}\right]^{t=\tau_{2}+}_{t=\tau_{1}-}\leq\int_{\tau_{1}}^{\tau_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\tilde{\eta}\rangle\partial_{t}\phi+\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\mbox{\boldmath\small$q$}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}},\tilde{\eta})\rangle\cdot\nabla_{\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\phi~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}dt

(4.9)

for
\phi\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, respectively.
Finally, (
3.49
) in Theorem
3.1
implies

\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathcal{V}_{\tau,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,E(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}},\tilde{\eta})\rangle~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}=\int_{\Omega}E(\rho_{0},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{0},\eta_{0})~d\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}

(4.10)

and concludes that
\{\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\}_{(t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$})\in(0,T)\times\Omega}
is a DMV solution of the complete Euler system.
∎

Having shown weak convergence to a DMV solution allows us to look for strong convergence to the observable quantities, such as the expected value and first variance.
To this end we apply a novel technique of
\mathcal{K}
-convergence as introduced in
[
16
,
18
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

(
\mathcal{K}
-convergence
)

Let
\{\rho_{h},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h},\eta_{h}\}_{h\downarrow 0}
be the family of numerical solutions obtained by the finite volume method (
2.13
).
Assumption
3.1
holds.
Then there exist a subsequence

\{\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}}\}_{{h_{n}}\downarrow 0}
such that

1.

strong convergences of Cesàro average

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,(\widetilde{\rho},\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}},\widetilde{\eta})\rangle\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})

for
N\to\infty
and any
1\leq q<\infty
;

2.

L^{q}
convergence to Young measure

d_{W_{s}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta_{(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})};\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}\right]\longrightarrow 0\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)

for
N\to\infty
and any
1\leq q<s<\infty
;

3.

L^{1}
convergence of the first variance

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg\|(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\bigg\|\longrightarrow 0\hskip 9.24994pt\mbox{in~}L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)

for
N\to\infty.

Applying techniques developed in
[
18
]
we directly obtain the following strong convergence results.

Theorem 4.3
.

(
Strong convergence
)

Let
\{\rho_{h},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h},\eta_{h}\}_{h\downarrow 0}
be the family of numerical solutions obtained by the finite volume method (
2.13
) and with initial data
\rho_{0,h}=\Pi_{h}[\rho_{0}],\,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{0,h}=\Pi_{h}[\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{0}],\,\eta_{0,h}=\Pi_{h}[\eta_{0}]
.
Let Assumption
3.1
hold.
Let the subsequence

(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)\quad\mbox{as}~h\longrightarrow 0

in the sense specified in Theorem
4.1
, where the barycenters
\rho:=\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\rho}\rangle
,
\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}:=\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}}\rangle
and
\eta:=\langle\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}};\,\widetilde{\eta}\rangle
.
Then the following holds:

1.

weak solution

If
(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)
is a weak entropy solution of the Euler system with initial data
(\rho_{0},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{0},\eta_{0})
, then

\mathcal{V}_{t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}}=\delta_{(\rho(t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}),\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}(t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}),\eta(t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$}))}\quad\mbox{for a.a.}~(t,\mbox{\boldmath\small$x$})\in(0,T)\times\Omega

and the strong convergence holds, i.e.

\displaystyle(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow~(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)\hskip 28.45274pt\quad\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})

\displaystyle E(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow~E(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 22.76228pt\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)

for any
1\leq q<\infty
.

2.

classical solution

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)
such that

\rho,\eta\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}),~\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}\in C^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\rho\geq\underline{\rho}>0~\mbox{in}~[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}.

Then
(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)
is a classical solution to the Euler system and

\displaystyle(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow~(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)\quad\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})

for any
1\leq q<\infty
.

3.

strong solution

Let periodic boundary conditions are applied.
Suppose that the Euler system admits a strong solution
(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)
in the class

\rho,\eta\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega),~\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\rho\geq\underline{\rho}>0~\mbox{in}~[0,T)\times\Omega

emanating from initial data
(\rho_{0},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{0},\eta_{0})
. Then it holds

\displaystyle(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow~(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)\hskip 28.45274pt\quad\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d+2})

\displaystyle E(\rho_{h_{n}},\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$}_{h_{n}},\eta_{h_{n}})\longrightarrow~E(\rho,\mbox{\boldmath\small$m$},\eta)\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 22.76228pt\mbox{in~}L^{q}((0,T)\times\Omega)

for any
1\leq q<\infty
.

Numerical results

In this section we simulate a spiral problem, i.e. two-dimensional Riemann problem,

In our computations the computational domain is
[0,1]\times[0,1]
and divided into

uniform cells.
Denote the Cesàro average of the numerical solutions and their first variance

\tilde{U}_{h_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{h_{j}},\quad\tilde{U}^{\dagger}_{h_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|U_{h_{j}}-\tilde{U}_{h_{n}}|,

Let

be the reference solution computed on the finest mesh with

cells.
Analogously to
[

we compute four errors

E_{1}=\|U_{h_{n}}-U_{h_{N}}\|,E_{2}=\|\tilde{U}_{h_{n}}-\tilde{U}_{h_{N}}\|,E_{3}=\|U^{\dagger}_{h_{n}}-U^{\dagger}_{h_{N}}\|,E_{4}=\|W_{1}(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{t,x}^{n},\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{t,x}^{N})\|,

(5.1)

where

\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{t,x}^{n}
is the Cesàro average of the Dirac measures concentrated on numerical solution
U_{h_{n}}
.

Moreover, the CFL number is set to

and the adiabatic index
\gamma

.

(

)
.

This example describes complex interactions of strong shocks with unstable interfaces.

.

6
Conclusions

We have shown the convergence of the Godunov finite volume method (
2.13
), which is based on the exact solution of a local Riemann problem for the complete compressible Euler system.
Hereby we have only assumed that our numerical solutions belong to a physically non-degenerate region, i.e. we have a uniform lower bound on density and an upper bound on energy. The latter is equivalent to the existence of a uniform lower bound on entropy Hessian matrix, see Lemma
B.4
.
Using the fact that the finite volume method (
2.13
) is entropy stable, the entropy inequality together with the explicit lower bound of the entropy Hessian matrix yield the weak BV estimate (
3.9
).
Further, we have shown that the difference between the exact solution of the local Riemann problem and its initial data, i.e. numerical solution at time
t_{n}
, can be controlled by the jump of numerical solution itself.
Consistency of the method is proved in Theorem
3.1
.
Weak convergence to a generalized solution, the dissipative measure-valued (DMV) solution, is showed in Theorem
4.1
.
Applying the novel tool of
\mathcal{K}
-convergence we obtained strong convergence to the expected value and first variance of a dissipative measure-valued solution, see Theorem
4.2
.
Theorem
4.3
summarizes the cases of strong convergence of numerical solutions of (
2.13
).
The latter happens if the limit is a weak or
C^{1}
solution to the Euler system (
2.1
).
In addition, applying the DMV-strong uniqueness principle
[
5
]
we also have strong convergence of our numerical solutions on the lifespan of the strong solution.

Numerical results for the spiral problem, the Kelvin-Helmholtz problem and the Richtmyer-Meshkov problem confirm results of theoretical analysis.
In particular, we observe that for highly oscillatory limit, as is the case of the Richtmyer-Meshkov test, single numerical solutions do not converge strongly.
On the other hand, we obtain the strong convergence of observable quantities, i.e. the expected values and first variances.

Although the Godunov finite volume method is one of the most classical schemes for hyperbolic conservation laws, its numerical analysis for multidimensional systems still remains open in general.
The main goal of this paper was to fill this gap and illustrate the application of the recently developed concepts of generalized DMV solution and
\mathcal{K}
-convergence on an iconic example, the multidimensional Euler system.
In future it will be interesting to extend the obtained results to general multidimensional hyperbolic conservation laws.
