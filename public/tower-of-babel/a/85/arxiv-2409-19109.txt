Trust, But Verify, Operator-Reported Geolocation CCS: Networks Network measurement
Katherine Izhikevich, Ben Du, Sumanth Rao, Alisha Ukani, Liz Izhikevich

Abstract
. Geolocation plays a critical role in understanding the Internet. In this work, we provide an in-depth analysis of operator- mis reported geolocation. Using a bandwidth-efficient methodology, we find in May 2024 that only a small percentage (1.5%) of vantage points in the largest community-vantage point collection, RIPE Atlas, do not respond from their operator-reported geolocation. However, misreported geolocations disproportionately affect areas with limited coverage and cause entire countries to be left with no vantage points. Furthermore, the problem is escalating: within the past five years, the number of probes reporting the wrong location has increased ten-fold. To increase the accuracy of future methodologies and studies that rely upon operator-reported geolocation, we open source our methodology and release a continually updated dataset of RIPE Atlas vantage points that misreport geolocation.



1.
Introduction

IP address geolocation plays a central role in networking and security:
understanding the Internet’s topology to improve last mile latency
(
Bajpai et al. 2017
;
Fontugne et al. 2020
)
, filtering traffic to prevent cyber attacks
(
Wan et al. 2020
)
, and identifying malicious activity to bring attackers to justice
(
Izhikevich et al. 2024b
;
Randall et al. 2021
)
, all rely on geolocation.
To geolocate an IP address, methodologies often rely upon a global, human-reported set of vantage points (i.e., probes) to measure from and verify accuracy against.
For example, geolocation methods based on latency measurements often require a network of probes, each with operator-attested geolocation, to be located near every target to accurately estimate every target’s geolocation
(
Hu et al. 2012
;
Gueye et al. 2004
;
Katz-Bassett et al. 2006
;
Wong et al. 2007
)
.

While necessary, relying on human operators to report and maintain accurate probe geolocation can introduce human-error to the geolocation process.
For example, if an operator moves their probe, but fails to update its location metadata, geolocation methodologies relying on the outdated information risk inaccurate conclusions.
To account for incorrect metadata, a few geolocation studies take an additional precaution to filter out probes reporting unlikely locations
(
Darwich et al. 2023
;
Gharaibeh et al. 2017
)
.
Unfortunately, existing filtering methodologies require high bandwidth
(
Darwich et al. 2023
)
and risk filtering accurately geolocated probes
(
Gharaibeh et al. 2017
;
Darwich et al. 2023
)
.
Furthermore, probes with misreported geolocations are still not well understood, including where they are most likely to be located, what their magnitude of error is, and how likely they are to eventually be corrected.
As operator-reported geolocations continue to serve as a foundation for many studies, it is critical that we understand the successes and pitfalls of our community-contributed datasets and work towards an accurate ground truth.

In this work, we provide an in-depth analysis of operator misreported geolocation.
We apply a simple methodology to identify probes that do not respond from their operator-reported geolocation.
The methodology (1) uses speed of Internet calculations to derive a minimum bound of expected latency between two vantage points—the target vantage point and a vantage point we control—and
(2) applies the minimum bound to verify whether operator-reported geolocations are physically possible.

In May 2024, we deploy our methodology from Ark
(
CAIDA 2024
)
, 170 globally distributed probes we control, to measure the validity of operator-reported geolocation of the largest and most geographically distributed community-vantage point collection: RIPE Atlas
(
NCC 2024a
)
.
Our methodology discovers 197 (1.5%) present-day “violating” probes: probes that are likely not responding from the operator-reported geolocation, because otherwise they would surpass the speed of Internet (e.g., speed of light in fiber).
Compared to prior work
(
Darwich et al. 2023
;
Gharaibeh et al. 2017
)
, our methodology identifies over 2 times the amount of violating vantage points while using 9 times less bandwidth.

The magnitude of error for probes misreporting geolocation is significant; roughly 50% of violating probes respond at least 1,000 miles away from their reported location.
Inaccurate geolocation disproportionately affects countries and states with low vantage point coverage, such as southern Africa and Alaska.
Consequently, entire countries (e.g., Lesotho, Eswatini) previously believed to have at least one vantage point are left with no RIPE Atlas coverage, potentially impacting the accuracy of the prior work that depends upon them
(
Corneo et al. 2021
;
Zennaro et al. 2020
;
Fanou et al. 2017
)
.

We consider how inaccuracies prevail over time, finding that operators are slow to update metadata and that the number of violating probes is increasing. The majority (52%) of geolocations are updated after the vantage point violates the speed of Internet for at least 7 weeks, suggesting most violating vantage points misreport locations for months.
Furthermore, the number of violating probes increases every year, highlighting that this problem is not diminishing.

Today, the majority of studies do not report filtering for violating probes.
We encourage future studies relying on operator-reported geolocation (e.g., geolocation studies) to use our open-source methodology to filter for a more-validated set of probes.
We show that this filtering step is simple, but often necessary, to avoid exposing the ground truth to outliers that are thousands of miles away from their reported location.
To further lower the barrier to validate the dataset that numerous studies rely upon
(
Corneo et al. 2021
;
Zennaro et al. 2020
;
Fanou et al. 2017
;
Darwich et al. 2023
;
Randall et al. 2021
;
Fontugne et al. 2020
;
Bajpai et al. 2017
)
, we release a list of 391 likely-inaccurately-geolocated RIPE Atlas probes from the past five years and maintain a weekly-updated list of currently-violating probes at

https://github.com/kizhikevich/violating_ripe_probes
.

2.
Background and Related Work

Many studies rely on the RIPE Atlas platform to develop new geolocation methodologies and, more broadly, understand the Internet. These studies are inherently dependent on the accuracy of operator-reported geolocations, motivating us to find vantage points with misreported geolocations, more deeply understand the inaccuracies, and lower the barrier for researchers to validate probes.

2.1.
RIPE Atlas

RIPE Atlas is a measurement platform consisting of tens of thousands of networked devices (i.e., vantage points) distributed across the globe.
As of May 2024, RIPE Atlas has 12,903 vantage points across 180 countries and 3,800 ASes
(
NCC 2024a
)
.
There are two types of vantage points in the RIPE Atlas network: anchors (hosted by organizations) and probes (hosted by volunteers).
For simplicity, we refer to anchors and probes as RIPE Atlas probes unless otherwise specified.

The RIPE NCC provides a convenient interface for participants to manage their probes: after the probe is connected, the probe host can see their probe metadata (e.g. prefix and AS number) on their RIPE Atlas dashboard and report the physical location of their probe by placing a pin on a map. The reported probe location will be skewed at most by 1 kilometer to protect the probe host’s privacy.

We observed the same anonymization for the 6 RIPE Atlas probes we control.
When a probe becomes disconnected, the RIPE Atlas platform will regularly send the host email notifications to remind them of the disconnection.
Critically, if a host physically moves the probe, they are expected to manually update the reported location of their probe.
Should a probe’s metadata location be incorrect, the RIPE platform allows users to dispute operator-reported geolocation, but as of May 2024, there are only 4 probes whose location is disputed.

2.2.
Research Relying on RIPE Atlas

The distributed architecture of the RIPE Atlas platform has proven useful in many research areas, especially in Internet infrastructure geolocation
(
Randall et al. 2021
;
Fontugne et al. 2020
;
Bajpai et al. 2017
;
Corneo et al. 2021
;
Zennaro et al. 2020
;
Fanou et al. 2017
)
.
Scheitle et al.
(
Scheitle et al. 2017
)
used RIPE Atlas probes to verify geolocation inferred from location hints embedded in reverse DNS records.
Weinbery et al.
(
Weinberg et al. 2018
)
used RIPE Atlas probes to measure the latency to 2K proxy and VPN providers, and found at least 1/3 of VPNs falsified their advertised geolocation.
Darwich et al.
(
Darwich et al. 2023
)
used RIPE Atlas to replicate the results of previous geolocation works.
Gharaibeh et al.
(
Gharaibeh et al. 2017
)
used RIPE Atlas probes to validate four geolocation databases.
RIPE themselves use RIPE Atlas probes to power a public IP geolocation tool, IPmap
(
NCC 2024b
;
Du et al. 2020
)
.

Geolocation research relying on RIPE Atlas probes—as vantage points or targets—often use latency-based methodologies, and thus are sensitive to the accuracy of the reported location of the probe.
An Atlas probe with an incorrect reported location may cause the geolocation result to be inconclusive or incorrect.
For example, if the RIPE IPmap tool measures a target IP address with 0.2ms round trip time (RTT) to a RIPE Atlas probe reporting an incorrect location, it will assume the target IP to be in the same incorrect location as that probe wrongly reports.

Probes with incorrect reported locations have a larger impact on results where RIPE Atlas probes are sparsely deployed.
Candela et al.
(
Candela et al. 2019
)
studied the maximum theoretically achievable geolocation accuracy for RIPE Atlas-based techniques, and found that regions with limited probes, such as Africa and Asia, have lower maximum accuracy.
Trammell and Kühlewind
(
Trammell and Kühlewind 2018
)
also found that high accuracy relies on the luck of selecting a vantage point (Atlas probe) that happens to be geographically close to the target.
Such luck would be defeated in a situation where a probe reporting an incorrect location is selected.

To the best of our knowledge, few works have validated the correctness of RIPE Atlas probes.
Gharaibeh et al.
(
Gharaibeh et al. 2017
)
in 2017 were the first to include a preliminary “sanity check” of RIPE Atlas probe locations in their work.
They considered the reported locations untrustworthy if they had default country coordinates (typically geographic centers) or if two probes were connected to the same router, but reported locations more than 100km apart.
They identified 24 probes with potentially incorrect geolocation.
In 2020, Darwich et al.
(
Darwich et al. 2023
)
introduced a recursive-like methodology, described in Section
4.4.2
, to identify probes that violated the speed-of-Internet constraint, filtering 9/723 anchors and 96/10K probes.
Notably, both methodologies identify groups of probes with at least one violation, but not the violating probe itself.
Thus, both methodologies risk filtering probes that accurately report their geolocation.

Our work applies a modified methodology to identify over 2 times the amount of violating probes using 9 times less bandwidth (evaluated in Section
4.4
) than prior work. Additionally, we provide the first analysis of where violating probes are located, why they might be misreporting location, and how their inaccuracy affects ground truth coverage.

3.
Methodology

Our objective is to identify whether a probe responding from its operator-reported geolocation is physically impossible.
We rely on simple physics to verify whether measured latency between our measurement source and the destination’s reported location exceeds the speed at which light travels in either a fiber optic cable or an inter-satellite laser.
We measure latency using a network of probes from the CAIDA Ark platform, as well as historical RIPE Atlas measurement data.

Vantage Points.
Our methodology requires measuring latency to and from vantage points with validated locations.
First, we use the CAIDA Ark Measurement Infrastructure
(
CAIDA 2024
)
, a set of 170 globally distributed vantage points that we control and whose exact latitude and longitude we know.
As of May 2024, Ark has 170 nodes in 133 autonomous systems, across 135 cities, in 58 countries, on 6 continents (see Figure
7
in the Appendix for a map of Ark coverage).
The CAIDA Ark platform is open to researchers upon request.
Additionally, we use seven RIPE operated and validated “central” servers, located in
Fremont, California, USA; Newark, New Jersey, USA; Singapore; two in Amsterdam, Netherlands; and two in Nuremberg, Germany.

Latency Measurement.
First, we measure the latency from all of our vantage points (VPs) to all 13K RIPE Atlas probes.
We use our Ark network to ICMP ping every RIPE probe every twelve hours for one week in May 2024.
Between every <VP, probe> pair, we save the minimum round trip time of all pings as the representative RTT.
For the RIPE central server vantage points, we use their API to collect historical ping data from each RIPE Atlas probe to each server.
We collect the minimum round trip times three times a day (6h,12h,18h UTC).
We repeat this process one day a week, for the past five years.
Thus, we derive one latency measurement for every source and destination (RIPE Atlas probes) pair.
We filter out continuously unresponsive probes.

Estimating Distance Between Source and Destination.
Second, to help derive the theoretical minimum latency between a vantage point and probe, we estimate the minimum (direct) distance.
We calculate the direct distance between the reported coordinates of every RIPE Atlas probe to every RIPE central server.
RIPE does not disclose the exact locations of the seven central servers, so we calculate a maximum error radius
around each city center.
2
2

2

In Section
4
, we discover that speed of Internet violations were large enough such that the use of radius calculations did not change the outcome of our results.

We subtract the error radius from the direct distance.
We also calculate the direct distance between the locations reported by the probes on the day we send pings to the location of our Ark vantage points.
Since we know the exact location of the Ark vantage points, we do not need an error radius.

Deriving Speed of Internet Theoretical RTTs.
Once we estimate the minimum distance between each probe and server, we convert it to a minimum theoretical round-trip time.
To the best of our knowledge, Starlink satellites—and only Starlink satellites—are currently the theoretically fastest networking infrastructure due to their use of inter-satellite lasers, which operate at the speed of light in space
(
Chaudhry and Yanikomeroglu 2022
)
.
To find probes hosted over Starlink, we look for probes whose IPv4 or IPv6 addresses are announced by Starlink’s autonomous system (ASN 14593)
(
Izhikevich et al. 2024a
)
.
For Starlink-hosted probes, we calculate the theoretical
absolute minimum
RTT as
direct\_distance/c
, where
c
is the speed of light.
Otherwise, we calculate the theoretical
absolute minimum
RTT if the pings were sent at the speed of light in optical fiber,
direct\_distance/\frac{2}{3}c

(
Chaudhry and Yanikomeroglu 2022
)
. In the remainder of the text we refer to the speed of Internet as SOI.

Identifying Violating Probes.
Finally, we identify a RIPE Atlas probe as a “violating probe” (i.e., it does not respond from its operator-reported geolocation) if any RIPE Atlas probe measured latency is lower than the theoretical minimum latency to any of the seven RIPE central servers or any of the 170 Ark servers.
For RIPE central server historical data, we take note of when within the past 5 years the RTT violation happened, if not presently occurring.

Precautions to Minimize False Positives.
We take extra precautions to avoid overclaiming and false positives.
For example, it is possible that a probe’s operator-reported geolocation is accurate, but a middlebox (e.g., a NAT) located away from the operator-reported geolocation might be responding on the probe’s behalf.
Thus, we only make claims about the geolocation of where the probe responds from and not the probe itself.

We never attempt to identify the true geolocation of a probe, which is an open problem
(
Darwich et al. 2023
)
.
We only identify when the probe’s response from an operator-reported geolocation is physically impossible.
We discuss the implications and limitations of our claims in Section
5
.

4.
Results

Nearly 200 (1.5% of) probes violate the speed of Internet in May 2024.
Violating probes are geographically widespread, often hundreds of miles away from their operator-reported geolocation, and cause entire countries to lose the majority of their probe coverage.
The majority (76%) of violating probes are likely due to operators misreporting geolocation.
Operator speed exacerbates the problem: 50% of probes violate for over 7 weeks until operators update their geolocation.

4.1.
Distribution of Violating Probes

Violating probes exist all over the world.
In Table
1
, we list the countries with the most violating probes.
Nearly half of all violating probes originate in the USA (22%), Germany (21%), or France (6%).
Within the US, the most (17%) violating probes are located in Missouri, causing 40% of probes hosted in Missouri to violate speed of Internet.
Additionally, within the US, 50% (2/4) of Alaska’s probes and 67% (6/9) of Kansas’ probes also violate the speed of Internet.
We verify that all probes in Kansas are more than 200 km from the geographic center of the US
(
Google 2024
)
, suggesting that the geolocation is likely misreported, not that RIPE automatically used the default US location.
Thus, any study that relies on Alaska or Kansas probes is more likely than not using a violating probe.

Countries in southern Africa are most likely to host at least one violating probe.
In Table
2
, we list the top 10 countries with the highest proportion of probes violating the speed of Internet.
Half of the top 10 countries are in southern Africa: Eswatini (100% of probes violate), Lesotho (100%), Mozambique (67%), Botswana (67%), Zambia (14%).
Notably, the southern African countries have extremely low vantage point coverage to begin with (i.e., only one to seven probes each), thereby exacerbating the effects of a violating probe.

Violating probes in southern Africa range in their geolocation error.
In the best case, a Botswana probe violates SOI by 2.5 ms, however, we see RTTs of 0.33 ms to an Ark vantage point in Johannesburg, South Africa. Such an RTT encompasses a 20 mile radius around Johannesburg, which does not include Botswana.
In the worst case, a Zambia probe violates SOI by 10.8 ms, but we see RTTs of 1.16 ms to the same Johannesburg vantage point, which encompasses a 100 mile radius around Johannesburg, which does not include Zambia.
Both cases indicate that the infrastructure responding on behalf of the probes (e.g., the probe itself, a middlebox) are not in their reported countries.
By not filtering for these regions in geolocation-dependent studies, we risk invalid conclusions.

4.3.
Understanding Contributing Factors Through Case Studies

In this section, we investigate what operator actions likely contribute to SOI violations.
Through a manual analysis of reported geolocations and round trip times, we discover that tardiness in updating geolocation after a move, and initial misreporting of geolocation are likely contributors to SOI violations.

4.3.1.
Tardiness

Figure 5
.
Moving from Germany to Brunei Case Study—The operator’s probe reports a SOI violation between February 2022 and April 2023, before updating their location metadata from Germany to Brunei in April 2023.

At times, probes reporting RTTs that violate SOI are simply late to report a move.
In Figure
5
, we show longitudinal ping measurements from probe 822 whose operator reported a location in Germany.
From 2019 to 2022, its RTTs to the Nuremberg RIPE servers were a minimum of 17.2 ms, indicating the probe was likely within 1.1K miles of Nuremberg.
After a disconnect between November 2021 and February 2022, the probe reported a minimum RTT of 23 ms to the Singapore RIPE server, indicating it was likely within 1.5K miles of Singapore. However, until April 2023, the probe reported a location in Germany, which is, of course, not within 1.5K miles of Singapore.
Eventually, the operator updated their metadata to Brunei, which is within 1.5K miles of Singapore.
The probe no longer reports RTTs in violation of the SOI.

4.3.2.
Initial Misreporting.

In other scenarios, probes instantiated with the wrong geolocation metadata contribute to long-term violations.
We study one of our most persistently violating probes—Probe 1000011—which violated SOI from October 2019 until June 2023.
Probe 1000011 reported a location in Marina Del Ray, California, USA from deployment until June 2023, when the location changed to Arlington, Virginia, USA.
However, the probe’s minimum RTT to all RIPE servers remained stable across our historical data, which is unexpected given a transcontinental move.
Further, the minimum RTT to the Fremont, California
and
the Newark, New Jersey RIPE servers violated the SOI, given the reported Marina Del Ray location.
Once the location updated, neither the California
nor
the New Jersey RIPE servers violated the SOI.
Instead of assuming a location change, we conclude that Probe 1000011 was located near New Jersey since deployment, meaning the probe was reporting the wrong location from 2019 until 2023.
At the time of the reported location change, we
find that the upstream provider changes from Cogent Communications to Lumen, hinting that perhaps a network change prompted the operator to verify the configuration of the probe and update its geolocation.

Evaluating Detection Methodologies

We evaluate our methodology (Section
3
), by analyzing what biases are introduced when using different vantage points and how its performance compares to prior work.
Notably, our methodology uses an order of magnitude less bandwidth and time.

Impact of Vantage Points

While coupling RIPE and Ark to detect SOI violations maximizes finding violating probes, we find that certain geographical regions benefit from using Ark more than others. In Figure

4.4.2.
Comparison to Prior Work

We show that existing methodologies require significantly more bandwidth and flag significantly more false positives than our proposed methodology.

Darwich et al.
(
Darwich et al. 2023
)
Comparison.
Darwich et al. propose a methodology that consists of
(1) RIPE Atlas anchors all pinging each other,
(2) iteratively removing anchors with the largest number of SOI violations until no more anchors violate the SOI, and
(3) running ping tests from every probe to the set of validated anchors (step 2) to produce a list of probes that violate the SOI.

Our methodology runs in an order of magnitude less time and uses nearly an order of magnitude less bandwidth.
Darwich et al. state their methodology runs between several hours to several days (bottlenecked by RIPE Atlas measurement limits) to collect 9M ping measurements on the RIPE Atlas platform.
In comparison, our methodology—pulling historical data from RIPE’s API and running Ark measurements—takes only 18 minutes to collect 1.6M ping measurements.
Darwich et al.’s methodology uses “millions of RIPE credits,” deploying
1300\times 1300
iterative measurements for the anchors, plus the
1300\times 13K
iterative measurements for the probes at one point in time.
Our methodology uses substantially fewer (verified) vantage points, requiring 9 times less (
157\times 13K
) iterative measurements for one point in time.

Unfortunately, we cannot compare the number of violating probes Darwich et al.’s methodology finds compared to ours.
At the time of their study, Darwich et al.’s methodology finds 105 violating probes.
However, our methodology cannot be applied to the time of their study, as we do not have historical data for our Ark platform.
Today, our methodology finds 197 violating probes.
However, replicating their study requires millions of RIPE credits that we simply do not have access to (and by default are not given to researchers).
While their methodology does not find violating probes in low-coverage areas (e.g., Alaska, Lesotho, Eswatini, Botswana), it is not clear if this is due to a methodological flaw or a temporal change.

Gharaibeh et al.
(
Gharaibeh et al. 2017
)
Comparison.
Gharaibeh et al.
consider reported geolocations untrustworthy if (1) they report default country coordinates (typically geographic centers) or
(2) two probes are connected to the same router, but report locations more than 100km apart.
We replicate their methodology on built-in RIPE Atlas traceroutes and find that it flags roughly one quarter of probes as likely having misreported geolocation.
Upon investigation, we discover that approximately 3,000 probes share the same router in their traceroute, causing the entire group to be considered untrustworthy.
Within that group is RIPE Atlas’ own probe (probe #1), which RIPE Atlas confirms to us is reporting the correct geolocation.

5.
Limitations

Our work faces several limitations in providing an in-depth understanding of operator-misreported geolocation.
First, we focus on the RIPE Atlas dataset, which may not be representative of all vantage points with operator-reported geolocations.
It is critical that future work verify how the prevalence of violating probes affects other vantage point platforms.
Nevertheless, RIPE Atlas is the largest community-vantage point collection, underscoring the importance of our results.

Second, our methodology is not able to distinguish between the probe’s actual geolocation and the geolocation of what is truly responding on behalf of the probe (e.g., a middlebox).
Nevertheless, given that many geolocation methodologies rely on the same latency measurements (e.g., pings) that target the same probes, it is the geolocation of what is responding that is always being measured and matters the most.
Third, our methodology produces a strict lower bound, holding vantage points to a requirement that their connection be slower than a direct optical fiber (or inter-satellite laser) connection with zero congestion.
Thus, there may be more violating probes.

6.
Recommendations and Future Work

As operator-reported geolocations continue to be used as a foundation for many research studies, it is critical that we understand and maintain an accurate ground truth.
Encouragingly, we found that only 1.5% of probes that violate SOI.
However, we found regions that now lack the majority of their originally reported coverage, thereby creating hundreds of miles of error for regional studies.

Our filtering methodology is efficient and effective, so we recommend that anyone using RIPE Atlas’s operator-reported geolocation should run our methodology and verify that the geolocation of a probe’s response is not physically impossible.
To increase coverage, researchers should use our Ark platform to perform our SOI methodology to minimize overall time and bandwidth.
Future work should explore new methods to detect SOI violations, including providing tighter—but still accurate—bounds for detecting violations.

We also recommend that RIPE Atlas extend their current communication infrastructure to account for SOI violations. RIPE Atlas currently emails operators when their probe disconnects; therefore emailing operators when a reported location
seems
false by physical standards could be a natural extension to an existing system. By doing so, RIPE Atlas could minimize the bandwidth researchers would use to detect the violations on their own and could perhaps deploy more stationary probes in the regions now lacking coverage.

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we provide an in-depth analysis of operator-
mis
reported geolocations.
As of May 2024, we find only 197 (1.5%) probes that do not respond from their operator-reported geographic location.
However, misreported geolocations are often hundreds of miles away from their reported locations, causing entire countries to lose the majority—if not all—of their probe coverage.
Furthermore, the problem is only getting worse: within the past five years, the number of probes reporting the wrong location has increased tenfold.
We find that operator error is often at fault, with 76% of probes that historically violated SOI no longer violating SOI due to updated metadata.
Furthermore, it takes most operators over 7 weeks to update metadata after SOI violations begin.
We open-source our methodology, release a list of 391 likely inaccurately geolocated RIPE Atlas probes from the past five years, and maintain a weekly-updated list of currently violating RIPE Atlas probes
to help researchers sanity check the dataset they rely upon.
