Simple and Tighter Derivation of Achievability for Classical Communication over Quantum Channels
Hao-Chung Cheng

Abstract
. Achievability in information theory refers to demonstrating a coding strategy that accomplishes a prescribed performance benchmark for the underlying task. In quantum information theory, the crafted Hayashi–Nagaoka operator inequality is an essential technique in proving a wealth of one-shot achievability bounds since it effectively resembles a union bound in various problems. In this work, we show that the so-called pretty-good measurement naturally plays a role as the union bound as well. A judicious application of it considerably simplifies the derivation of one-shot achievability for classical-quantum channel coding via an elegant three-line proof. The proposed analysis enjoys the following favorable features. (i) The established one-shot bound admits a closed-form expression as in the celebrated Holevo–Helstrom theorem. Namely, the average error probability of sending M M messages through a classical-quantum channel is upper bounded by the minimum error of distinguishing the joint channel input-output state against ( M − 1 ) (M-1) decoupled product states. (ii) Our bound directly yields asymptotic achievability results in the large deviation, small deviation, and moderate deviation regimes in a unified manner. (iii) The coefficients incurred in applying the Hayashi–Nagaoka operator inequality or the quantum union bound are no longer needed. Hence, the derived one-shot bound sharpens existing results relying on the Hayashi–Nagaoka operator inequality. In particular, we obtain the tightest achievable ε \varepsilon -one-shot capacity for classical communication over quantum channels heretofore, improving the third-order coding rate in the asymptotic scenario. (iv) Our result holds for infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. (v) The proposed method applies to deriving one-shot achievability for classical data compression with quantum side information, entanglement-assisted classical communication over quantum channels, and various quantum network information-processing protocols.

Introduction

Communicating classical information over a noisy quantum channel is a foundational task in quantum information science.
To protect the transmitted messages against potential noise, an indispensable coding strategy is employed.
At the transmitter, Alice initiates the procedure by encoding each message
m\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
into an
n
-qubit quantum state.
Suppose in the communication process that each qubit suffers from independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) quantum noise, which is characterized by an i.i.d. quantum channel.
Then, at the receiver Bob performs a quantum measurement on the corrupted quantum system to extract the decoded message
\hat{m}
.

Via a coding strategy based on the so-called
quantum typicality

[

n\to\infty

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M
) is below the
channel capacity
.

The HSW theorem extends the seminal work of Shannon
[
8
]
to the quantum scenario, and hence, it is one of the fundamental core stones in quantum information theory.
However, the HSW coding strategy relies on certain technical assumptions that could be physically demanding.
First, the asymptotically large qubit number
n
requires the quantum devices to implement arbitrarily large encoding and decoding.
Second, the actual quantum noise may be correlated among several qubit systems; hence, the underlying quantum noises are not independent.
Third, even if the noises are independent, they may not be stationary; namely, the noise acting on the first qubit is not identical to that on the last qubit.

To circumvent the aforementioned technical requirements,
one-shot
quantum information theory emerges as a new research stream to consider the scenario that no structural hypotheses are imposed on the underlying quantum state or channel.
The ultimate goal is to characterize the
optimal trade-off
between the error probability
\varepsilon
and the message size
M
of transmission, for which the channel is used only once.
Such a study allows us to better understand the fundamental capability of one-shot communication. Therefore it may serve as a general guideline for designing the next-generation quantum information-processing systems.
However, without the i.i.d. repetitions of channel use, conventional methods based on quantum typicality are no longer applicable.
Hence more refined and sophisticated coding techniques are requisite for the one-shot analysis

Why is it challenging to design and analyze good coding strategies for a one-shot quantum information-processing task?
Essentially, a proper coding scheme aims to enforce the error probability,
\Pr\{\bigcup_{\hat{m}\neq m}\mathcal{E}_{\hat{m}\mid m}\}

m

Here,
\mathcal{E}_{\hat{m}\mid m}

Yet, the analysis and computational evaluation of such a union error event for a nontrivial quantum measurement could be quite difficult (even in the classical scenario).

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\bigcup\nolimits_{\hat{m}\neq m}\mathcal{E}_{\hat{m}\mid m}\right\}\leq\sum\nolimits_{\hat{m}\neq m}\Pr\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\hat{m}\mid m}\right\}.

3.
Main Result: A One-Shot Achievability for Classical-Quantum Channel Coding

In this section, we prove our main result of establishing a one-shot achievability bound for classical-quantum channel coding via a direct application of the pretty-good measurement (PGM)
[
72
,
73
]
.

Definition 1

(Classical-quantum channel coding)
.

Let
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}}:x\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}
be a classical-quantum channel, where each channel output
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}
is a density operator (i.e. a positive semi-definite operator with unit trace).

(1)

Alice holds classical registers
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{X}
, and Bob holds a quantum register
\mathsf{B}
.

(2)

An encoding
m\mapsto x(m)
maps equiprobable messages in
\mathsf{M}
to a codeword in
\mathsf{X}
.

(3)

The classical-quantum channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}}
is applied on Alice’s register
\mathsf{X}
and outputs a state on
\mathsf{B}
at Bob.

(4)

A decoding measurement described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
\left\{{\Pi}_{\mathsf{B}}^{m}\right\}_{m\in\mathsf{M}}
is performed on Bob’s quantum register
\mathsf{B}
to extract the sent message
m
.

An
(M,\varepsilon)
-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}}
is a protocol such that
|\mathsf{M}|=M
and the average error probability satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in\mathsf{M}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{B}}-{\Pi}_{\mathsf{B}}^{m}\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon.

The encoding is the standard random coding strategy.

•

Encoding.

Consider a random codebook
\mathcal{C}=\{x(1),x(2),\ldots,x(M)\}
, where each of the codewords
x(m)\in\mathsf{X}
is pairwise independently drawn from a probability distribution
p_{\mathsf{X}}
.
Alice sends codewords according to the realization of the codebook
\mathcal{C}
.

•

Decoding.
At the receiver, given a realization of the random codebook
\mathcal{C}
and the corresponding channel output states
\{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\}_{m\in\mathsf{M}}
,
Bob performs the PGM to decode each message
m\in\mathsf{M}
:

(7)

\displaystyle{\Pi}^{m}_{\mathsf{B}}:=\frac{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}}{\sum_{\bar{m}\in\mathsf{M}}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}},\quad\forall m\in\mathsf{M}.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1

(A one-shot achievability bound for classical-quantum channel coding)
.

Consider an arbitrary classical-quantum channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}}:x\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}
. Then, there exists an
(M,\varepsilon)
-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}}
such that for any probability distribution
p_{\mathsf{X}}
,

(8)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}\wedge(M-1)\rho_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}\right].

Here,
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}:=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}p_{\mathsf{X}}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}
and the noncommutative minimal is
A\wedge B=\frac{1}{2}(A+B-|A-B|)
(see Fact
1
).

Proof.

The claim follows from the lower bound of the noncommutative minimal “
\wedge
” given in Fact
7
for relating the pretty good measurement to the optimal measurement, and the concavity of “
\wedge

4

Precisely,
given any realization of codebook
\mathcal{C}=\{x(m)\}_{m\in\mathsf{M}}
, we calculate the average probability of erroneous decoding using the PGM given in Eq. (

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in\mathsf{M}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\frac{\sum_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}}{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}+\sum_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{m}}}\right]

\displaystyle{\leq}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in\mathsf{M}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\wedge\left(\sum\nolimits_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}\right)\right],

where we have applied Fact

to relate the error probability under the PGM to the expression in terms of the noncommutative minimal.
Next, we take the expectation for each

to bound the expected average error probability (which is also called the random-coding error probability), i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in\mathsf{M}}\mathds{E}_{x(m),x(\bar{m})\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\wedge\left(\sum\nolimits_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in\mathsf{M}}\mathds{E}_{x(m)\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\wedge\left(\mathds{E}_{x(\bar{m})|x(m)}\left[\sum\nolimits_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}\right]\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{=}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in\mathsf{M}}\mathds{E}_{x(m)\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}\wedge(M-1)\rho_{\mathsf{B}}\right]

Invoking the direct sum formula given in Fact

8

Lastly, since the random-coding error probability using any

Below, we provide a detailed explanation of how PGM works.
An important feature of PGM is that the POVM element
{\Pi}_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
given in Eq. (
7
) is
proportional
to the sent state
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
.
On the other hand, the complement of the POVM element, i.e.
\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{B}}-{\Pi}_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
, is proportional to the sum of the remaining states
\sum_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}
.
Hence, the average error probability of discriminating
M
channel output states, i.e. the left-hand side of Eq. (
9
), is equivalent to the error of deciding each sent state
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
using the following two-outcome PGM:

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}}{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}+\sum_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}},\frac{\sum_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}}{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}+\sum_{\bar{m}\neq m}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(\bar{m})}}\right\}.

Such the discrimination between
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
with prior probability
\frac{1}{M}
against the sum of the remaining states (again each with prior probability
\frac{1}{M}
) reflects the nature of the union bound inherited in the PGM; cf. the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
).
Next, taking the expectation on the remaining states ensures that we are discriminating
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
with prior probability
\frac{1}{M}
against
(M-1)

\rho_{\mathsf{B}}
each with prior probability
\frac{1}{M}
.
Equivalently, this amounts to a binary hypothesis testing between
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m)}
with prior probability
\frac{1}{M}
against the marginal states
\rho_{\mathsf{B}}
with prior probability
\frac{M-1}{M}
.
Lastly, after taking the summation over
m\in\mathsf{M}
,
the above is equal to the discrimination of the joint state
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}
against the scaled decoupled product state
(M-1)\rho_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}

(See Figure

Definition 3

Proof.

We use the pretty-good measurement given in Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\mathds{E}_{m\sim\frac{1}{M}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}\frac{\sum_{\bar{x}\neq x,\,\mathscr{E}(\bar{x})=m}p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}}{\sum_{\bar{x}:\mathscr{E}(\bar{x})=m}p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(a)}}{\leq}\mathds{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\mathds{E}_{m\sim\frac{1}{M}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\left(\sum_{\bar{x}\neq x,\,\mathscr{E}(\bar{x})=m}\frac{p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})}{p_{\mathsf{X}}({x})}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(b)}}{\leq}\mathds{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\,\mathds{E}_{m\sim\frac{1}{M}}\left[\sum_{\bar{x}\neq x}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathscr{E}(\bar{x})=m\}}\frac{p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})}{p_{\mathsf{X}}({x})}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(c)}}{=}\mathds{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\bar{x}\neq x}\frac{p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})}{p_{\mathsf{X}}({x})}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(d)}}{\leq}\mathds{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathsf{X}}}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\left(\frac{1}{Mp_{\mathsf{X}}(x)}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(e)}}{\leq}\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}\right)\right],

where (a) uses the lower bound of the noncommutative minimal given in Fact
7
;
(b) follows from the concavity given in Fact
4
;
(c) follows from the pairwise-independent and uniform random encoding;
(d) follows from the monotone increase in the Loewner ordering and
\sum_{\bar{x}\neq x}p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}\leq\sum_{\bar{x}}p_{\mathsf{X}}(\bar{x})\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{x}}=\rho_{\mathsf{B}}
;
and lastly, (e) follows from the direct sum formula given in Fact
5
.
∎

Using the same reasoning as in Propositions
1
and
2
of Section
3
, we have the following one-shot bounds for CQSW.

Proposition 5

(Bounding coding error given fixed coding rate)
.

Consider an arbitrary classical-quantum state
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}p_{\mathsf{X}}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}
.
Then, for any
R>0
, there exists an
\left(\mathrm{e}^{R},\varepsilon\right)
-CQSW-code for
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}
such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\e^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\left(R-H_{2-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}^{\downarrow}(\mathsf{X}{\,|\,}\mathsf{B})_{\rho}\right)},\quad\forall\alpha\in(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1),

where
H_{\alpha}^{\downarrow}(\mathsf{X}{\,|\,}\mathsf{B})_{\rho}:=-D_{\alpha}(\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}\,\|\,\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}})
.

Proposition 6

(Bounding coding rate given fixed coding error)
.

Consider an arbitrary classical-quantum state
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{X}}p_{\mathsf{X}}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x}
.
Then, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists an
\left(M,\varepsilon\right)
-CQSW-code for
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}
such that for any
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle\log M\leq H_{\textnormal{h}}^{\varepsilon-\delta}\left(\mathsf{X}{\,|\,}\mathsf{B}\right)_{\rho}+\log\tfrac{1}{\delta},

where
H_{\textnormal{h}}^{\varepsilon-\delta}\left(\mathsf{X}{\,|\,}\mathsf{B}\right)_{\rho}:=-D_{\textnormal{h}}^{\varepsilon-\delta}(\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{B}}\,\|\,\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}})
.

Definition 4

(Classical-quantum multiple-access channel coding)
.

Let
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{C}}:(x,y)\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x,y}
be a classical-quantum multiple-access channel.

(1)

Alice holds classical registers
\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}
and
\mathsf{X}
, Bob holds
\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}
and
\mathsf{Y}
, and Charlie holds quantum register
\mathsf{C}
.

(2)

Alice performs an encoding
m_{\mathsf{A}}\mapsto x(m_{\mathsf{A}})\in\mathsf{X}
for any equiprobable message
m_{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}
she wanted to send.
Bob performs an encoding
m_{\mathsf{B}}\mapsto y(m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{Y}
for any equiprobable message
m_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}
he wanted to send.

(3)

The channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{C}}
is applied on Alice and Bob’s registers
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
and outputs a state on
\mathsf{C}
at Charlie.

(4)

A decoding measurement
\{{\Pi}_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}\}_{(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}}
is performed on register
\mathsf{C}
to extract the sent message
(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})
.

An
(M_{\mathsf{A}},M_{\mathsf{B}},\varepsilon)
-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{C}}
is a protocol such that
|\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}|=M_{\mathsf{A}}
,
|\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}|=M_{\mathsf{B}}
, and the average error probability satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{\mathsf{A}}M_{\mathsf{B}}}\sum_{(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}}\Tr\left[\left(\mathds{1}-{\Pi}_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}\right)\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x(m_{\mathsf{A}}),y(m_{\mathsf{B}})}\right]\leq\varepsilon.

We follow the strategy presented in Section
3
.

•

Encoding.
Each pair of messages
(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}
is mapped to a codeword
\left(x(m_{\mathsf{A}}),y(m_{\mathsf{B}})\right)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
pairwise independently according to some probability distribution
p_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes p_{\mathsf{Y}}
.

•

Decoding.
We use the pretty-good measurement with respect to the corresponding channel output states (given the realization of the random codebook):

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}=\frac{\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x(m_{\mathsf{A}}),y(m_{\mathsf{B}})}}{\sum_{(\bar{m}_{\mathsf{A}},\bar{m}_{\mathsf{B}})}\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x(\bar{m}_{\mathsf{A}}),y(\bar{m}_{\mathsf{B}})}},\quad(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}.

Then, we obtain the following result (without duplicating the proof).

Theorem 5

(A one-shot achievability bound for classical-quantum MAC coding)
.

Consider an arbitrary classical-quantum multiple-access channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{C}}:(x,y)\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x,y}
. Then, there exists an
\left(M_{\mathsf{A}},M_{\mathsf{B}},\varepsilon\right)
-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\to\mathsf{C}}
such that for any probability distributions
p_{\mathsf{X}}
and
p_{\mathsf{Y}}
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{C}}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\left((M_{\mathsf{A}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{C}}+(M_{\mathsf{B}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{Y}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{C}}+(M_{\mathsf{A}}-1)(M_{\mathsf{B}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{Y}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{C}}\right)\right],

where
\rho_{\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{C}}:=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}p_{\mathsf{X}}(x)|x\rangle\langle x|_{\mathsf{X}}\otimes p_{\mathsf{Y}}(y)|y\rangle\langle y|_{\mathsf{Y}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x,y}
.

Next, we consider the entanglement-assisted setting.

Definition 5

(Entanglement-assisted classical communication over quantum multiple-access channels)
.

Let
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}}
be a quantum multiple-access channel.

(1)

Alice holds classical register
\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}
and quantum registers
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{A}^{\prime}

Bob holds classical register
\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}

\mathsf{B}

\mathsf{B}^{\prime}

\mathsf{C}
,
\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}

(2)

Charlie and Alice share an arbitrary state
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}
.
Charlie and Bob share an arbitrary state
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}
.

(3)

Alice performs an encoding
\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{A}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}}}
on
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}

m_{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}

\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{B}}^{m_{\mathsf{B}}}
on
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}

m_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}

(4)

The channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}}
is applied on Alice and Bob’s registers
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, and outputs a state on quantum register
\mathsf{C}
at Charlie.

(5)

Charlie performs a decoding measurement
\{{\Pi}_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}\}_{(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}}

\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{C}

-EA-code for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{\mathsf{A}}M_{\mathsf{B}}}\sum_{(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}}\Tr\left[\left(\mathds{1}-{\Pi}_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}\right)\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}}\left(\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{A}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{A}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}}}\left(\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}\right)\otimes\mathscr{E}_{\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{B}}^{m_{\mathsf{B}}}\left(\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}\right)\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon.

4.2

]

•

Preparation.

M_{\mathsf{A}}
-fold product states
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{\prime}\mathsf{A}^{\prime}}:=\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{A}}^{\otimes M_{\mathsf{A}}}

-fold product states
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}\mathsf{B}^{\prime}}:=\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{B}}^{\otimes M_{\mathsf{B}}}

•

Encoding.
Alice adopts encoding

Decoding.
Denote the corresponding channel output state for sending message

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{M_{\mathsf{A}}}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{M_{\mathsf{B}}}\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}:=\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}}^{\otimes(m_{\mathsf{A}}-1)}\otimes\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}}^{\otimes(m_{\mathsf{B}}-1)}\otimes\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}}\left(\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{A}}\otimes\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{B}}\right)\otimes\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}}^{\otimes(M_{\mathsf{A}}-m_{\mathsf{A}})}\otimes\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}}^{\otimes(M_{\mathsf{B}}-m_{\mathsf{B}})}.

Then, we use the associated pretty-good measurement:

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}=\frac{\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{M_{\mathsf{A}}}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{M_{\mathsf{B}}}\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}}}}{\sum_{(\bar{m}_{\mathsf{A}},\bar{m}_{\mathsf{B}})}\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}^{M_{\mathsf{A}}}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}^{M_{\mathsf{B}}}\mathsf{C}}^{\bar{m}_{\mathsf{A}},\bar{m}_{\mathsf{B}}}},\quad(m_{\mathsf{A}},m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{A}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}.

Following the analysis presented in Section

Theorem 6

(A one-shot achievability bound for EA classical communication over quantum MAC)

Consider an arbitrary quantum multiple-access channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}}

\left(M_{\mathsf{A}},M_{\mathsf{B}},\varepsilon\right)
-EA-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}\to\mathsf{C}}
such that for any
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{A}}
and
\theta_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{B}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{C}}\,\mathchoice{{\textstyle\bigwedge}}{{\bigwedge}}{{\textstyle\wedge}}{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}\left((M_{\mathsf{A}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}\mathsf{C}}+(M_{\mathsf{B}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{C}}+(M_{\mathsf{A}}-1)(M_{\mathsf{B}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{A}}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{B}}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{C}}\right)\right],

where

Definition 6

(Classical-quantum broadcast channel coding)

Let
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}:x\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}^{x}

(1)

Alice holds classical registers
\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}

\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}

\mathsf{X}

\mathsf{B}

\mathsf{C}

(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})\mapsto x(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})\in\mathsf{X}
for any equiprobable message
(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}

(3)

\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}
is applied on Alice’s register
\mathsf{X}
, and outputs a marginal state on
\mathsf{B}
at Bob and a marginal state on
\mathsf{C}

m_{\mathsf{B}}

\{{\Pi}_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{C}}}\}_{m_{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}}
on register
\mathsf{C}
to extract the sent message
m_{\mathsf{C}}

(M_{\mathsf{B}},M_{\mathsf{C}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{B}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{C}})
-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}:x\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}^{x}
is a protocol such that
|\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}|=M_{\mathsf{B}}
,
|\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}|=M_{\mathsf{C}}
, and the average error probabilities satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{M_{\mathsf{B}}M_{\mathsf{C}}}\sum_{(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}}\Tr\left[\left(\mathds{1}-{\Pi}_{\mathsf{B}}^{m_{\mathsf{B}}}\right)\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{\mathsf{B}};&\\
\frac{1}{M_{\mathsf{B}}M_{\mathsf{C}}}\sum_{(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}\times\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}}\Tr\left[\left(\mathds{1}-{\Pi}_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{C}}}\right)\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x(m_{\mathsf{B}},m_{\mathsf{C}})}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{\mathsf{C}}.&\\

We follow the analysis proposed in Section
3
by considering communication from Alice to Bob and from Alice to Charlie, separately.

•

Encoding.

We introduce two auxiliary classical registers
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{V}
for precoding.
Message
m_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}
for Bob is encoded to a pre-codeword
u(m_{\mathsf{B}})\in\mathsf{U}
pairwise independently according to some probability distribution
p_{\mathsf{U}}
; message
m_{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}
for Charlie is encoded to a pre-codeword
v(m_{\mathsf{C}})\in\mathsf{V}
pairwise independently according to some probability distribution
p_{\mathsf{V}}
.
Then, Alice picks a (deterministic) encoding
(u(m_{\mathsf{B}}),v(m_{\mathsf{C}}))\mapsto x(u(m_{\mathsf{B}}),v(m_{\mathsf{C}}))\in\mathsf{X}
.

•

Decoding.

Denoting (with a slight abuse of notation) the marginal channel output states at Bob by

\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{m_{\mathsf{B}}}:=\frac{1}{M_{\mathsf{C}}}\sum_{m_{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{x(u(m_{\mathsf{B}}),v(m_{\mathsf{C}}))}
,
m_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}
, Bob performs the corresponding pretty-good measurement:

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{\mathsf{B}}^{m_{\mathsf{B}}}:=\frac{\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{m_{\mathsf{B}}}}{\sum_{\bar{m}_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}}\rho_{\mathsf{B}}^{\bar{m}_{\mathsf{B}}}},\quad m_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}.

Similarly, denoting (with a slight abuse of notation again) the marginal channel output states at Charlie by

\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{C}}}:=\frac{1}{M_{\mathsf{B}}}\sum_{m_{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{B}}}\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{x(u(m_{\mathsf{B}}),v(m_{\mathsf{C}}))}
,
m_{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}
, Charlie performs the corresponding pretty-good measurement:

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{C}}}:=\frac{\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{m_{\mathsf{C}}}}{\sum_{\bar{m}_{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}}\rho_{\mathsf{C}}^{\bar{m}_{\mathsf{C}}}},\quad m_{\mathsf{C}}\in\mathsf{M}_{\mathsf{C}}.

Then, we obtain the following result (without duplicating the proof).

Theorem 7

(A one-shot achievability bound for classical-quantum broadcast channel coding)
.

Consider an arbitrary classical-quantum broadcast channel
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}:x\mapsto\rho_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}^{x}
. Then, there exists an
\left(M_{\mathsf{B}},M_{\mathsf{C}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{B}},\varepsilon_{\mathsf{C}}\right)
-code for
\mathscr{N}_{\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}
such that for any probability distributions
p_{\mathsf{U}}
and
p_{\mathsf{V}}
, and (deterministic) encoding
(u,v)\mapsto x(u,v)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathsf{B}}\leq\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{U}\mathsf{B}}\wedge(M_{\mathsf{B}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{U}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}}\right];

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathsf{C}}\leq\Tr\left[\rho_{\mathsf{U}\mathsf{C}}\wedge(M_{\mathsf{C}}-1)\rho_{\mathsf{V}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{C}}\right],

where
\rho_{\mathsf{U}\mathsf{V}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}:=\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathsf{U}\times\mathsf{V}}p_{\mathsf{U}}(u)|u\rangle\langle u|_{\mathsf{U}}\otimes p_{\mathsf{V}}(v)|v\rangle\langle v|_{\mathsf{V}}\otimes\rho_{\mathsf{B}\mathsf{C}}^{x(u,v)}

Conclusions

We propose a conceptually simple analysis of one-shot achievability for processing classical information in quantum systems.
The key point of this work is to demonstrate that the pretty-good measurement directly translates the conditional error probability of a multiple-state discrimination to the error of discriminating a state against the rest.
This can be viewed as the
one-versus-rest
strategy, and, hence, the pretty-good measurement effectively resembles the quantum union bound in quantum coding design and analysis.
We obtain an elegant closed-form expression of the average error probability for classical communication over quantum channels with standard random coding and basic properties of the noncommutative minimal.
The proposed method is tight in the sense that it gives tighter one-shot achievability bounds for channels without further constraints such as symmetry (see Section
3.1
), and it unifies the asymptotic derivations in the large, small, and moderate deviation regimes (Figure
2
)
Moreover, the analysis naturally applies to various quantum information-theoretic tasks (see Section
4
and Table
1
).
This manifests that the proposed method may be considered a fundamental and unified approach to deriving achievable error bounds in quantum information theory.
In this regard, we may term it as a
one-shot quantum packing lemma
(Theorem
3
).
Essentially, the proposed analysis can be applied to and can sharpen almost all existing results that rely on the Hayashi–Nagaoka operator inequality
[

; see e.g. Refs.
[

.
The improvement is crucial because every bit counts in a one-shot bound because weaker one-shot bounds could be trivial in certain practical scenarios.
Hence, we expect more applications of the proposed analysis to emerge.
As for the computational aspect, we point out a recently developed quantum algorithm for implementing the pretty-good measurement
[

.
Last but not least, the proposed achievability analysis also applies to the converse analysis for the covering-type problems

A Trace Inequality

This section is devoted to proving the trace inequality in Fact
7
:

(27)

\displaystyle\Tr[A\wedge B]\geq\Tr\left[A\frac{B}{A+B}\right].

Note that a special case of Eq. (
27
) for
\Tr[A]=\Tr[B]=1
is a consequence of Barnum and Knill’s theorem
[
93
]
(see also
[
86
, Theorem 3.10]
).
The result has been extended to the case of general positive semi-definite
A
and
B
in the author’s previous work
[
30
, Lemma 3]
.
For completeness, we provide a strengthened proof in the following Lemma
1
that implies the desired Eq. (
27
) by extending Sason–Verdú
[
158
]
and Renes’ idea
[
159
]
.

Lemma 1

(A trace inequality for noncommutative parallel sum)
.

Let
A
and
B
be arbitrary positive semi-definite operators satisfying
\Tr\left[A+B\right]>0
.
Then, the following holds,

(28)

\displaystyle\Tr\left[A\frac{B}{A+B}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\cdot\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right]}{\Tr[A+B]}

\displaystyle\leq\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right].

Here,
A\vee B:=\frac{A+B+|A-B|}{2}
and
A\wedge B:=\frac{A+B-|A-B|}{2}
.

Remark 11
.

In the scalar case of positive
a
and
b
, the inequality
\frac{ab}{a+b}\leq a\wedge b
is obvious, and the term
\frac{ab}{a+b}=(a^{-1}+b^{-1})^{-1}
is called the
parallel sum
of
a,b
. Hence, Lemma
1
may be viewed as a noncommutative generalization of it.
We note that an operator parallel sum
(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}
has been studied before (e.g.
[
160
, §3]
,
[
161
, §5]
), and it is related to the Kubo–Ando operator (Harmonic) mean
[
162
,
163
]
.
Further, it can be shown that
\Tr[(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}]\leq\Tr[A\frac{B}{A+B}]
, and hence Lemma
1
also provides an upper bound (in trace) to the operator parallel sum.

Proof.

We define the
collision divergence

[
24
]
for
A,B\geq 0
as

\displaystyle D^{*}_{2}(A\,\|\,B):=\log\Tr\left[\left(B^{-\frac{1}{4}}AB^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)^{2}\right].

Let
\{{\Pi}_{A},{\Pi}_{B}\}
be the optimal measurement for distinguishing positive semi-definite operators
A
and
B
, i.e. by recalling the Holevo–Helstrom theorem
[
14
,
74
,
75
]
,

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq T\leq\mathds{1}}\Tr[AT]+\Tr[B(\mathds{1}-T)]=\Tr[A{\Pi}_{A}]+\Tr[B{\Pi}_{B}]=\Tr[A\vee B].

Denote by “
\oplus
” the direct sum operation. We introduce a measure-and-prepare operation
\Lambda
, which is a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map:

\displaystyle\Lambda:(\,\cdot\,)\mapsto\Tr\left[(\,\cdot\,){\Pi}_{A}\oplus{\Pi}_{B}\right]\oplus\Tr\left[(\,\cdot\,)(\mathds{1}-{\Pi}_{A}\oplus{\Pi}_{B})\right].

We calculate the following:

\displaystyle\Lambda(A\oplus B)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\oplus\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right];

(29)

\displaystyle\Lambda\left((A+B)^{\oplus 2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[A+B\right]\oplus\Tr\left[A+B\right].

Since the map
\exp\{D_{2}^{*}(\,\cdot\,\|\,\cdot\,)\}
is monotonically decreasing under CPTP maps
[
164
,
165
,
166
]
, we obtain

(30)

\displaystyle\Tr\left[A\frac{A}{A+B}\right]+\Tr\left[B\frac{B}{A+B}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{D_{2}^{*}\left(A\oplus B\,\|\,(A+B)^{\oplus 2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left\{D_{2}^{*}\left(\Lambda(A\oplus B)\,\|\,\Lambda\left((A+B)^{\oplus 2}\right)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{D_{2}^{*}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]&0\\
0&\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right]\end{bmatrix}\,\left\|\,\begin{bmatrix}\Tr\left[A+B\right]&0\\
0&\Tr\left[A+B\right]\end{bmatrix}\right.\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\right)^{2}}{\Tr[A+B]}+\frac{\left(\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right]\right)^{2}}{\Tr[A+B]}.

Noting that the left-hand side of Eq. (
30
) can be written as

\displaystyle\Tr\left[A\frac{A}{A+B}\right]+\Tr\left[B\frac{B}{A+B}\right]=\Tr\left[A+B\right]-2\Tr\left[A\frac{B}{A+B}\right],

then the above inequality translates to

\displaystyle 2\Tr\left[A\frac{B}{A+B}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Tr\left[A+B\right]-\frac{\left(\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\right)^{2}}{\Tr[A+B]}-\frac{\left(\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right]\right)^{2}}{\Tr[A+B]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\Tr\left[A+B\right]\right)^{2}-\left(\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\right)^{2}-\left(\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right]\right)^{2}}{\Tr\left[A+B\right]}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(a)}}{=}\frac{2\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\cdot\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right]}{\Tr\left[A+B\right]}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(b)}}{\leq}2\Tr\left[A\wedge B\right].

Here, (a) follows from the identity,
A+B=A\vee B+A\wedge B
;
and the inequality
\Tr\left[A\vee B\right]\leq\Tr\left[A+B\right]
used in (b) is because that
A+B
is a feasible solution to the infimum representation of the noncommutative maximum
[
74
,
84
,
75
]
:

\displaystyle A\vee B=\frac{A+B+|A-B|}{2}=\argmin_{M=M^{\dagger}}\left\{\Tr[M]:M\geq A,\,M\geq B\right\}.

Hence, we complete the proof.
∎
