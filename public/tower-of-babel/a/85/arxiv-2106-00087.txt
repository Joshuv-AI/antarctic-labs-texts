Lecture Notes on Stationary Gamma Processes
Robert L. Wolpert

Abstract
For each λ > 0 \lambda>0 and every square-integrable infinitely-divisible (ID) distribution there exists at least one stationary stochastic process t ↦ X t t\mapsto X_{t} with the specified distribution for X 1 X_{1} and with first-order autoregressive ( AR (1)) structure in the sense that the autocorrelation of X s X_{s} and X t X_{t} is exp ⁡ ( − λ ​ | s − t | ) \exp(-\lambda|s-t|) for all indices s , t s,t . For the special case of the standard Normal distribution, the process X t X_{t} is unique— namely, the first-order autoregressive Ornstein-Uhlenbeck velocity process. The process X t X_{t} is also uniquely determined if X 1 X_{1} is accorded the unit rate Poisson distribution. For the Gamma distribution, however, X t X_{t} is not determined uniquely. In these lecture notes we describe six distinct processes with the same univariate marginal distributions and AR (1) autocorrelation function. We explore a few of their properties and describe methods of simulating their sample paths.



1
Introduction

For fixed
\alpha,\beta>0
these notes present six different stationary
time series, each with Gamma
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
univariate
marginal distributions and autocorrelation function
\rho^{|s-t|}
for

X_{s},~X_{t}
. Each will be defined on some time index set
{\mathcal{T}}
, either

{\mathcal{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}
or
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathbb{R}}
.

Five of the six constructions can be applied to other Infinitely Divisible
(ID) distributions as well, both continuous ones (normal,
\alpha
-stable,

etc
.) and discrete (Poisson, negative binomial,
etc
.). For specifically
the Poisson and Gaussian distributions, all but one of them (the Markov
change-point construction) coincide— essentially, there is just one
“
AR
(1)-like” Gaussian process (namely, the
AR
(1) process in discrete time,
or the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in continuous time), and there is just one

AR
(1)-like Poisson process. For other ID distributions, however, and in
particular for the Gamma, each of these constructions yields a process with
the same univariate marginal distributions and the same autocorrelation but
with different joint distributions at three or more times.

First, by “
\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
” we mean the Gamma distribution with mean

\alpha/\beta
and variance
\alpha/\beta^{2}
,
i.e.
, with shape parameter

\alpha
and
rate
parameter
\beta
. The pdf and chf are given by:

\displaystyle f(x\mid\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}~x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}\mathbf{1}_{\{x>0\}}

\displaystyle\chi(\omega\mid\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=(1-i\omega/\beta)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{i\omega u}~\alpha e^{-\beta u}\,u^{-1}\,du\right\}.

(1)

The sum
\xi_{+}:=\sum\xi_{j}
of independent random variables
\xi_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{ind}}}\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{j},\beta)
with the same rate parameter also has a Gamma
distribution,
\xi_{+}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{+},\beta)
, if
\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
are summable with sum
\alpha_{+}:=\Sigma\alpha_{j}<\infty
.
Eqn (
1
) shows that the “Lévy measure” for this distribution is

\nu(du)=\alpha e^{-\beta u}\,u^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{u>0\}}du
.

From the Poisson representation of ID distributions

X=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathcal{N}(du)

for
\mathcal{N}(du)\sim\mathsf{Po}\big(\nu(du)\big)
we can show that the extremal
properties of ID random variables depend only on the Lévy measure

\nu(du)
: for large
u\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}[X>u]

\displaystyle\approx{\mathsf{P}}\big[\mathcal{N}\big((u,\infty)\big)\neq\emptyset]

\displaystyle=1-\exp\big(-\nu\big((u,\infty)\big)

\displaystyle\approx\nu\big((u,\infty)\big)\approx(\alpha/\beta u)e^{-\beta u},

(2)

from which one might mount a study of the multivariate extreme properties
of the six processes presented below.

2
Six Stationary Gamma Processes

2.1
The Gamma
\textsf{AR}(1)
Process

Fix
\alpha,\beta>0
and
0\leq\rho<1
. Let
X_{0}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
and
for
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
define
X_{t}
recursively by

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle:=\rho X_{t-1}+\zeta_{t}

(3)

for iid
\left\{\zeta_{t}\right\}
with chf

{\mathsf{E}}e^{i\omega\zeta_{t}}=(1-i\omega/\beta)^{-\alpha}(1-i\rho\omega/\beta)^{\alpha}=\big[\frac{\beta-i\omega}{\beta-i\rho\omega}\big]^{-\alpha}
(easily seen to be positive-definite,
with Lévy measure

\nu_{\zeta}(du)=\alpha\left[e^{-\beta u}-e^{-\beta u/\rho}\right]u^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{u>0\}}du
). A simple way of generating
\left\{\zeta_{t}\right\}
with this
distribution from
[
Walker 2000
]
is presented in the Appendix. The
process
\{X_{t}\}
has Gamma univariate marginal distribution

X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and, at consecutive times

0,1
, joint chf

\displaystyle\chi(s,t)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp(isX_{0}+itX_{1})

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp(i(s+\rho t)X_{0}+it\zeta_{1})

\displaystyle=\Big[\frac{(1-i(s+\rho t)/\beta)~(1-it/\beta)}{1-it\rho/\beta}\Big]^{-\alpha}.

(4)

Since this is asymmetric in
s,t
, the process is not time-reversible; this
is also evident from the observation that

{\mathsf{P}}[X_{t}\geq\rho X_{t-1}]={\mathsf{P}}[\zeta_{t}\geq 0]=1>{\mathsf{P}}[\zeta_{t}\leq X_{t-1}(1-\rho^{2})/\rho]={\mathsf{P}}[X_{t-1}\geq\rho X_{t}].

This process has marginal distribution
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
at all
times
t
, and has autocorrelation
{\mathsf{Corr}}\big(X_{s},X_{t})=\rho^{|s-t|}

(easily found from either (
3
) or (
4
)). It is
clearly Markov (from (
3
)), and can be shown to have
infinitely-divisible (ID) multivariate marginal distributions of all
orders, since the
\{\zeta_{t}\}
are ID.

2.2
Thinned Gamma Process

If
X\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{1},\beta)
and
Y\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{2},\beta)
are
independent, then
Z:=X+Y\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{+},\beta)
and
U:=X/Z\sim\mathsf{Be}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
are also independent (where
\alpha_{+}:=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
), because under the change of variables
x=uz
,
y=(1-u)z

with Jacobian
J(u,z)=z
,

\displaystyle f(u,z)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{\beta^{\alpha_{1}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})}x^{\alpha_{1}-1}e^{-\beta x}\right\}\left\{\frac{\beta^{\alpha_{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{2})}y^{\alpha_{2}-1}e^{-\beta y}\right\}\,J(u,z)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{\beta^{\alpha_{+}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\Gamma(\alpha_{2})}\right\}u^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-u)^{\alpha_{2}-1}z^{\alpha_{+}-2}e^{-\beta z}z

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{+})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\Gamma(\alpha_{2})}u^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-u)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\right\}\quad\left\{\frac{\beta^{\alpha_{+}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{+})}z^{\alpha_{+}-1}e^{-\beta z}\right\}.

Thus if
Z\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\beta)
and

U\sim\mathsf{Be}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
are independent then

X=UZ\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{1},\beta)
and
Y=(1-U)Z\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha_{2},\beta)
are
independent too.
Let

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)

and, for
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
(or
t\in-{\mathbb{N}}
, resp.) set

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle:=\xi_{t}+\zeta_{t}

where

\displaystyle\xi_{t}

\displaystyle:=B_{t}\cdot X_{t-1},\qquad B_{t}\sim\mathsf{Be}(\alpha\rho,\alpha{\bar{\rho}}),

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}

\displaystyle\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha{\bar{\rho}},\beta)

(or
\xi_{t}=B_{t}\cdot X_{t+1}
, resp.), where
{\bar{\rho}}:=(1{-}\rho)

and all the
\left\{B_{t}\right\}
and
\left\{\zeta_{t}\right\}
are independent.

Then, by induction,
\xi_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha\rho,\beta)
and
\zeta_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha{\bar{\rho}},\beta)
are independent, with sum
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
. Thus
\{X_{t}\}
is a Markov process with Gamma univariate marginal
distribution
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
, now with symmetric joint chf

\displaystyle\chi(s,t)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp(isX_{0}+itX_{1})

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp\left\{is(X_{0}-\xi_{1})+i(s+t)\xi_{1}+it\zeta_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=(1-is/\beta)^{-\alpha{\bar{\rho}}}(1-i(s+t)/\beta)^{-\alpha\rho}(1-it/\beta)^{-\alpha{\bar{\rho}}}

(5)

and autocorrelation
{\mathsf{Corr}}\big(X_{s},X_{t})=\rho^{|s-t|}
. The process of
passing from
X_{t-1}
to
\xi_{t}=X_{t-1}B_{t}
is called
thinning
, so

X_{t}
is called the
thinned gamma process
. A similar construction
is available for any ID marginal distribution.

2.3
Random Measure Gamma Process

Let
{\mathcal{G}}(dx\,dy)
be a countably additive random measure that assigns
independent random variables
{\mathcal{G}}(A_{i})\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha|A_{i}|,\beta)
to
disjoint Borel sets
A_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
of finite area
|A_{i}|
(this is
possible by the Kolmogorov consistency conditions, and is illustrated in
the Appendix) and, for
\lambda:=-\log\rho
, consider the collection of
sets:

G_{t}:=\left\{(x,y):~x\in{\mathbb{R}},~0\leq y<\lambda e^{-2\lambda|t-x|}\right\}

Figure 1:
Random measure construction of process
X_{t}={\mathcal{G}}(G_{t})

shown in Figure (
1
) whose intersections have area
|G_{s}\cap G_{t}|=e^{-\lambda|s-t|}
. For
t\in{\mathcal{T}}={\mathbb{R}}
, set

X_{t}:={\mathcal{G}}(G_{t}).

(6)

For any
n
times
t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{n}
the sets
\left\{G_{t_{i}}\right\}
partition

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
into
n(n+1)/2
sets of finite area (and one with infinite area,

(\cup G_{t_{i}})^{c}
), so each
X_{t_{i}}
can be written as the sum of some
subset of
n(n+1)/2
independent Gamma random variables. In particular,
any
n=2
variables
X_{s}
and
X_{t}
can be written as

X_{s}={\mathcal{G}}(G_{s}\backslash G_{t})+{\mathcal{G}}(G_{s}\cap G_{t}),\qquad X_{t}={\mathcal{G}}(G_{t}\backslash G_{s})+{\mathcal{G}}(G_{s}\cap G_{t})

just as in the thinning approach of Section (
2.2
), so both 1-dimensional and
2-dimensional marginal distributions for the random measure process coincide
with those for the thinning process. Again the joint chf is

\displaystyle\chi(s,t)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp(isX_{0}+itX_{1})

\displaystyle=(1-is/\beta)^{-\alpha{\bar{\rho}}}(1-i(s+t)/\beta)^{-\alpha\rho}(1-it/\beta)^{-\alpha{\bar{\rho}}}

(7)

and the autocorrelation is
{\mathsf{Corr}}\big(X_{s},X_{t})=\exp\big(-\lambda|s-t|\big)
or, for integer times,
\rho^{|s-t|}
for
\rho:=\exp(-\lambda)
. The distribution for consecutive
triplets

differs from those of the Thinned Gamma Process, however, an illustration
that the thinning process is Markov but the random measure is not. The
Random Measure process does feature infinitely-divisible (ID) marginal
distributions of all orders, while the thinned process does not.

2.4
The Markov change-point Gamma Process

Let
\{\zeta_{n}:~n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\mathrel{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{iid}}}\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
be iid Gamma random
variables and let
N_{t}
be a standard Poisson process indexed by
t\in{\mathbb{R}}

(so
N_{0}=0
and
(N_{t}-N_{s})\sim\mathsf{Po}(t-s)
for all
-\infty<s<t<\infty
, with
independent increments), and set

X_{t}:=\zeta_{n},\qquad n={N_{\lambda t}}.

Then each
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
and, for
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
X_{s}
and
X_{t}

are either identical (with probability
\rho^{|s-t|}
) or independent—
reminiscent of a Metropolis MCMC chain. The chf is

\displaystyle\chi(s,t)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp(isX_{0}+itX_{1})

\displaystyle=\rho\big(1-i(s+t)/\beta\big)^{-\alpha}+{\bar{\rho}}(1-is/\beta)^{-\alpha}(1-it/\beta)^{-\alpha}

(8)

and once again the marginal distribution is
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
and
the autocorrelation function is
{\mathsf{Corr}}\big(X_{s},X_{t})=\rho^{|s-t|}
.

2.5
The Squared O-U Gamma Diffusion

Let
\{Z_{i}\}\mathrel{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{\mathrm{iid}}}\textsf{OU}(\lambda/2,1)
be independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
velocity processes, mean-zero Gaussian processes with covariance

{\mathsf{Cov}}\big(Z_{i}(s),Z_{j}(t)\big)=\exp\big(-{\frac{\lambda}{2}}|s-t|\big)\delta_{ij}
, and set

X_{t}:=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(t)^{2}

for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Note
Z_{i}(t)\sim\mathsf{No}(0,1)
, so
{\mathsf{E}}Z_{i}(t)^{2}=1
and
{\mathsf{E}}Z_{i}(t)^{4}=3
; it follows that
{\mathsf{E}}Z_{i}(s)^{2}Z_{i}(t)^{2}=1+2\exp(-\lambda|s-t|)
. Then
X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
for

\alpha=n/2
, with
{\mathsf{E}}X_{s}=\alpha/\beta
and

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}X_{s}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\left\{n\big(1+2\exp(-\lambda|s-t|)\big)+n(n-1)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}+\alpha\exp\big(-\lambda|s-t|\big)}{\beta^{2}}

so the autocovariance is
{\mathsf{Cov}}\big(X_{s},X_{t})=\frac{\alpha}{\beta^{2}}~e^{-\lambda|s-t|}
and the autocorrelation at
integer times is
{\mathsf{Corr}}\big(X_{s},X_{t})=\rho^{|s-t|}
for
\rho:=\exp(-\lambda)
. The chf at consecutive integer times is

\displaystyle\chi(s,t)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}\exp(isX_{0}+itX_{1})

\displaystyle=\big(1-i(s+t)/\beta-st(1-\rho)/\beta^{2}\big)^{-\alpha},

(9)

distinct from (
4
), (
5
)=(
7
), and
(
8
), so this process is new. Itô’s formula is used in

[
Wolpert and Brown 2021
]
to show that
X_{t}
has stochastic differential
equation (SDE) representation

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=X_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}2\lambda\big(X_{s}-\alpha/\beta\big)\,ds+2\sqrt{\lambda/\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{X_{s}}\,dW_{s}

(10)

and hence has generator
\mathfrak{A}\phi(x)=(\partial/\partial\epsilon){\mathsf{E}}[\phi(X_{t+\epsilon})\mid X_{t}=x]\big|_{\epsilon=0}
given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=-2\lambda(x-\alpha/\beta)\phi^{\prime}(x)+(2\lambda/\beta)x\phi^{\prime\prime}(x),

(11)

which we will use to distinguish this process from that of Section (
2.6
).
While the construction above required half-integer values for
\alpha
,
(
9
) is positive-definite and the SDE (
10
) has a
unique strong solution for all
\alpha>0
, so a time-reversible stationary
Markov diffusion process exists with this distribution.

Cox et al. 1985
, building on
[
Feller 1951
]
,
found the transition kernel for this process:

\displaystyle f(y,t\mid x,s)

\displaystyle=ce^{-u-v}\Big(\frac{v}{u}\Big)^{(\alpha-1)/2}I_{(\alpha-1)}\big(2\sqrt{uv}\big),

where

c:=\beta/\big[1-e^{-2\lambda|t-s|}\big],\qquad u:=cye^{-2\lambda|t-s|},\qquad v:=cx.

where
I_{q}(x)
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind of
order
q
. With this one can construct likelihood functions and generate
posterior samples, MLEs,
etc
. for the parameters of this process.

Cox et al. 1985
show that the process
X_{t}
is strictly
positive at all times if
\alpha\geq 1
, but occasionally reaches zero for

\alpha<1
.

2.6
Continuously Thinned Gamma Process

Pick a large integer
n
and set
\epsilon:=1/n
,
q:=\exp(-\lambda\epsilon)
, and

p:=1-q=\lambda\epsilon+o(\epsilon)
. Draw
X_{0}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
and, for
integers
i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, draw independently

\zeta_{i}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha p,\beta)\qquad b_{j}\sim\mathsf{Be}(\alpha p,\alpha q).

Set:

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=X_{0}

\displaystyle X_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{0}(1-b_{1})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}

\displaystyle X_{2\epsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{\epsilon}\,(1-b_{2})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}

\displaystyle=X_{0}(1-b_{1})(1-b_{2})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(1-b_{2})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}

\displaystyle X_{3\epsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{0}(1-b_{1})(1-b_{2})(1-b_{3})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(1-b_{2})(1-b_{3})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}(1-b_{3})

\displaystyle\,+\,

\displaystyle\zeta_{3}

and, in general,

\displaystyle X_{k\epsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-b_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\{\zeta_{i}\prod_{i<j\leq k}(1-b_{j})\right\}.

(12a)

In the limit as
n\to\infty
and
k\epsilon\to t
the products
converge to the product integral of the beta process introduced by

Hjort 1980

[and described lucidly by
Thibaux and Jordan 2007
, §2]
and the sum to an ordinary gamma stochastic integral,

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=X_{0}\prod_{s\in(0,t]}[1-dB(s)]+\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\prod_{s\in(r,t]}[1-dB(s)]\right\}\,\zeta(dr),

(12b)

where
\zeta(dr)\sim\mathsf{Ga}\big(\alpha\lambda\,dr,\beta\big)
is a Gamma random
measure and
B(s)\sim\textsf{BP}\big(\alpha,\lambda\,ds\big)
is a Beta process,
i.e.
,
an SII Lévy process with Lévy measure

\nu_{B}(du)=\lambda\alpha\,u^{-1}(1-u)^{\alpha-1}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{0<u<1\}}\,du

with constant “concentration function”
\alpha(s)\equiv\alpha
and
translation-invariant “base measure”
\lambda(ds)=\lambda ds
. The
product integral can be written as a ratio

\displaystyle\prod_{s\in(r,t]}[1-dB(s)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-F(t)}{1-F(r)}

where
F(t)=\prod_{s\in(t,\infty)}[1-dB(s)]
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{dF(t)}{1-F(t)}

\displaystyle=dB(t),\qquad B_{t}=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{dF(s)}{1-F(s)}.

(13)

2.6.1
Generator

The Gamma process of Eqn (
12b
) is stationary and Markov, with generator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\phi(x)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[\phi(x+u)-\phi(x)\big]\,\alpha\lambda u^{-1}e^{-\beta u}\,du

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\big[\phi(x-u)-\phi(x)\big]\,\alpha\lambda u^{-1}(1-u/x)^{\alpha-1}\,du

(14)

Because this differs from (
11
) (and in particular because it is
a non-local operator, showing
X_{t}
has jumps), this process is new.
Once again the one-dimensional marginal distributions are

X_{t}\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
and the autocorrelation is
{\mathsf{Corr}}(X_{s},X_{t})=\exp\big(-\lambda|s-t|\big)
.

3
Discussion

We have now constructed six distinct processes that share the same
univariate marginal distribution and autocorrelation function, but which
all differ in their
n
-variate marginal distributions for
n\geq 3
.
Some are Markov, some not; some are time-reversible, some not; some have ID
marginal distributions of all orders, some don’t. Similar methods can be
used to construct
\textsf{AR}(1)
-like processes with
any
ID marginal
distribution, such as those listed on
p.

ID Distributions
; only in the two
cases of Gaussian and Poisson do all these constructions coincide. Many of
these are useful for modeling time-dependent phenomena whose dependence
falls off over time, but for which traditional Gaussian methods are
unsuitable because of heavy tails, or integer values, or positivity, or for
other reasons. I know of very little work (yet!) exploring inference for
processes like these; a beginning appears in
[
Wang 2013
, §3]
. Code
in
R
to generate samples from each of these six processes is
available on request.

The author would like to thank Lawrence D. Brown for many fruitful
discussions about ID processes, and Victor Peña for helping develop
methods of distinguishing these six processes from observations.

Poisson and Gamma SII Processes

The chf for a Poisson random variable

is

\displaystyle\chi_{X}(\theta)={\mathsf{E}}[e^{i\theta X}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{i\theta k}\left\{\frac{\nu^{k}}{k!}e^{-\nu}\right\}=e^{(e^{i\theta}-1)\nu}

\displaystyle\chi_{uX}(\theta)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}[e^{i\theta uX}]=\chi_{X}(u\theta)

\displaystyle=e^{(e^{i\theta u}-1)\nu}

Y:=\sum u_{j}X_{j}

X_{j}\sim\mathsf{Po}(\nu_{j})

\displaystyle\chi_{Y}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j}\left\{e^{(e^{i\theta u_{j}}-1)\nu_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\sum_{j}{(e^{i\theta u_{j}}-1)\nu_{j}}\right\}

(15a)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}{(e^{i\theta u}-1)\nu(du)}\right\}

(15b)

for the discrete measure

that assigns mass
\nu_{j}
to each point

, provided the sum in (

) converges. Of course the sum converges if it has only

(because

), but that condition isn’t

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\big(1\wedge|u_{j}|\big)\nu_{j}

\displaystyle<\infty

(16a)

or, in integral form,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(1\wedge|u|\big)\nu(du)

\displaystyle<\infty.

(16b)

A random variable with chf of form (
15a
) for a sequence

\displaystyle X_{t}

\nu(E)=\nu\mathbf{1}_{\{1\in E\}}

\Delta_{t}=1

The defining condition for a random variable
Y
to be “Infinitely
Divisible” (ID) is that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there must exist iid random
variables
\{\zeta_{j}:~1\leq j\leq n\}
such that
Y
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}
have the same distribution. This is clearly equivalent to the
condition that every power
\chi^{\alpha}(\omega)
of the characteristic
function
\chi(\theta):={\mathsf{E}}\exp(i\theta Y)
must
also
be a
characteristic function (
i.e.
, must be positive-definite) for each inverse
integer
\alpha=1/n
, because we can just take
\{\zeta_{j}\}
to be iid with
chf
\chi^{1/n}(\omega)
and verify that their sum has chf
\chi(\omega)
.
Less obvious but also true is that
Y

\alpha>0

\displaystyle\chi(\theta)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{i\theta\delta-\theta^{2}\sigma^{2}/2+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[e^{i\theta u}-1\right]\,\nu(du)\right\}

,

, and Borel measure

\nu
satisfying

and (

\displaystyle\chi(\theta)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{i\theta\delta-\theta^{2}\sigma^{2}/2+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[e^{i\theta u}-1-i\theta h(u)\right]\,\nu(du)\right\}

for any bounded function

or

) and a

Theorem 1

(S&vH, Thm 2.13)
.

Let
\chi(\theta)
be the chf of an Infinitely Divisible distribution.
Then
(\forall\theta\in{\mathbb{R}})\left\{\chi(\theta)\neq 0\right\}
. Also, if

\chi(\theta)
is analytic in some open domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, then

(\forall\theta\in\Omega)\left\{\chi(\theta)\neq 0\right\}
. Thus, ID chfs do not
vanish on
{\mathbb{R}}
or anywhere in
{\mathbb{C}}
that
\chi(\theta)
is analytic.

Theorem 2

(S&vH, Thm 9.8)
.

Let
X
be an infinitely divisible random variable that is not normal or
degenerate. Then the two-sided tail of
X
satisfies

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{-\log{\mathsf{P}}[|X|>x]}{x\log x}=c

for a number

0\leq c<\infty
given by
c^{-1}=\max[\nu({\mathbb{R}}_{+}),\nu({\mathbb{R}}_{-})]
. An ID
random variable that is not degenerate has a normal distribution if and
only if the same limit is
c=\infty
.

Theorem 3

(S&vH, Prop 2.3)
.

No non-degenerate bounded random variable is ID

This one’s easy enough to prove. If
\|X\|_{\infty}=B<\infty
and
X
has
the same distribution as
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\zeta_{j}
for iid
\{\zeta_{j}\}
then

\|\zeta_{j}\|_{\infty}=B/n
and
\sigma^{2}:={\mathsf{V}}(X)=n{\mathsf{V}}(\zeta_{j})\leq n{\mathsf{E}}\zeta_{j}^{2}\leq B^{2}/n
, so

Gamma Variables & Processes

The Gamma distribution
X\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
with mean
\alpha/\beta

and variance
\alpha/\beta^{2}
has chf

\displaystyle\chi_{X}(\theta)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{E}}[e^{i\theta X}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\theta x}\left\{\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}\right\}\,dx

\displaystyle=\big(1-i\theta/\beta\big)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\alpha\log(1-i\theta/\beta)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(e^{i\theta u}-1\big)\alpha u^{-1}e^{-\beta u}\,du\right\},

exactly of the form of (
15b
) with Lévy measure

\displaystyle\nu(du)

\displaystyle=\alpha u^{-1}e^{-\beta u}\mathbf{1}_{\{u>0\}}\,du.

This measure, while infinite, does satisfy condition (
16b
):

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(1\wedge|u|\big)\nu(du)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(|u|\big)\alpha u^{-1}e^{-\beta u}\,du+\int_{1}^{\infty}\big(1\big)\alpha u^{-1}e^{-\beta u}\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha e^{-\beta u}\,du=\alpha/\beta<\infty.

Thus gamma-distributed random variables are ID
and the SII gamma
process

X_{t}=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,t]}u\mathcal{N}(du\,ds)

has infinitely-many non-negative “jumps”
\Delta_{t}=X_{t}-X_{t-}
in any
time interval
a<t\leq b
. Their sum
\sum\{\Delta_{t}:~a<t\leq b\}=X_{b}-X_{a}

is finite, however, with probability distribution

X_{b}-X_{a}\sim\mathsf{Ga}\big(\alpha(b-a),~\beta\big).

The gamma
random measure

{\mathcal{G}}(dx\,dy)\sim\mathsf{Ga}(\alpha\,dx\,dy,~\beta)

of Section (
2.3
) has a similar construction,

{\mathcal{G}}(A)=\iiint_{{\mathbb{R}}\times A}u\mathcal{N}(du\,dx\,dy)

as the sum of the heights
u_{j}
of a Poisson cloud of points

(u_{j},x_{j},y_{j})
for which
(x_{j},y_{j})\in A
.
Wolpert and Ickstadt 1998
show
how to simulate such random measures very efficiently, drawing the jumps

\{u_{j}\}
in monotone decreasing order.
