Spectral methods on a triangle and W-systems
Jing Gao, Arieh Iserles

Abstract
We present an overarching framework for stable spectral methods on a triangle, defined by a multivariate W-system and based on orthogonal polynomials on the triangle. Motivated by the Koornwinder orthogonal polynomials on the triangle, we introduce a Koornwinder W-system. Once discretised by this W-system, the resulting spatial differentiation matrix is skew symmetric, affording important advantages insofar as stability and conservation of structure are concerned. We analyse the construction of the differentiation matrix and matrix vector multiplication, demonstrating optimal computational cost. Numerical convergence is illustrated through experiments with different parameter choices. As a result, our method exhibits key characteristics of a practical spectral method, inclusive of rapid convergence, fast computation and the preservation of structure of the underlying partial differential equation.

W-systems on a triangle

When

\mathcal{H}
such that

is skew Hermitian. This confers a number of important advantages:

1.

The exponential

is unitary, therefore (in the standard

\|{\mathrm{e}}^{t\mathcal{D}}\|\equiv 1
;

2.

Because
\mathcal{D}^{*}=-\mathcal{D}

-\mathcal{D}^{*}\mathcal{D}

3.

The matrix

\|{\mathrm{e}}^{-t\mathcal{D}^{*}\mathcal{D}}\|\leq 1

(again, in the
\mathrm{L}_{2}

Item 1 means that the discretisation of the advection equation
\partial u/\partial t=\partial u/\partial x
is stable, while item 3 implies that the solution of the diffusion equation is dissipative (hence stable) and the solution of the linear Schrödinger equation is unitary (and, again, stable). In general, once

Unfortunately, no orthonormal basis composed of polynomials can lead to a skew-Hermitian differentiation matrix: it is easy to observe this in a univariate case, when
\mathcal{D}
is strictly lower triangular. An alternative within the setting of zero Dirichlet boundary conditions is to use

[
11
]

Explicit expressions for differentiation matrices

In the subsection, we focus on the derivation of explicit expressions for (

3.3
A recursive relation

In this subsection, we focus on the recursive relation of
\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}
which plays an important role in the differentiation matrix. It is not enough to obtain formal expressions for the elements of the differentiation matrix: in practical computation we need a convenient means to derive them explicitly at a reasonable cost. Of course, in practical computations we need to truncate the differentiation matrix to the range
m=0,\cdots,M
,
n=0,1,\cdots,N
, resulting in a
(\frac{1}{2}(M+1)(M+2))\times(\frac{1}{2}(N+1)(N+2))

In this subsection we present a recursive form for rapid computation of the elements of the differentiation matrix. First we need to consider some properties of Jacobi polynomials. We recall the formula
[

\displaystyle(1-u)\mathrm{P}^{(\alpha+1,\beta)}_{n}\!(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{n,\alpha,\beta}\mathrm{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(u)-b_{n,\alpha,\beta}\mathrm{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n+1}(u),

\displaystyle a_{n,\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(n+\alpha+1)}{2n+\alpha+\beta+2},\qquad b_{n,\alpha,\beta}=\frac{2(n+1)}{2n+\alpha+\beta+2}.

Increasing the first parameter by

\displaystyle c_{n,\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+\beta}{n+\alpha+\beta+2},\qquad d_{n,\alpha,\beta}=\frac{2n+\alpha+\beta+2}{n+\alpha+\beta+2}.

It follows easily that

\displaystyle c_{n,\alpha,\beta}(1-u)\mathrm{P}^{(\alpha+2,\beta)}_{n-1}(u)+d_{n,\alpha,\beta}a_{n,\alpha,\beta}\mathrm{P}^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{n}(u)

Lemma 2
.

The integral

\displaystyle\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}

\displaystyle q_{m,\ell}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{((m-1)-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}+d^{[1]}_{m,\ell}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{((m-1)-(\ell-1),\ell-1),(n-k,k)}

\displaystyle\mbox{}-d^{[2]}_{n,k}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-(k-1),k-1)},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}}{2^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\ell+k+1}},

\displaystyle d^{[1]}_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle\frac{d_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}a_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}}{2^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\ell+k+1}},

\displaystyle\frac{d_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}b_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}}{2^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\ell+k+1}}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\ell+k+1}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u)^{k+(\ell-1)+\beta+\gamma}(1+u)^{\alpha}\mathrm{P}_{n-k}^{(\beta+\gamma+2k+1,\alpha)}(u)

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times\!\left[c_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}(1-u)\mathrm{P}^{(\beta+\gamma+2\ell+1,\alpha)}_{m-\ell-1}(u)+d_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}\right.

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times\left.a_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}\mathrm{P}^{(\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha)}_{m-\ell}(u)-d_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}\right.

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times\left.b_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}\mathrm{P}^{(\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha)}_{m-\ell+1}(u)\right]\!\!{\hbox{d}}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}}{2^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\ell+k+1}}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{((m-1)-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}+\frac{d_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}}{2^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\ell+k+1}}

\displaystyle\mbox{}\times a_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{((m-1)-(\ell-1),\ell-1),(n-k,k)}-d_{m-\ell,\beta+\gamma+2(\ell-1)+1,\alpha}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{llllllll}\tilde{I}_{(m-0,0),(n-0,0)}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\tilde{I}_{(m-1,1),(n-0,0)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-1,1),(n-1,1)}&0&\cdots&0\\
\tilde{I}_{(m-2,2),(n-0,0)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-2,2),(n-1,1)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-2,2),(n-2,2)}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{I}_{(m-n,n),(n-0,0)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-n,n),(n-1,1)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-n,n),(n-2,2)}&\cdots&\tilde{I}_{(m-n,n),(n-n,n)}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{I}_{(m-m,m),(n-0,0)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-m,m),(n-1,1)}&\tilde{I}_{(m-m,m),(n-2,2)}&\cdots&\tilde{I}_{(m-m,m),(n-n,n)}\end{array}\right]

with the dimensionality
(m+1)\times(n+1)
for
m>n
.

3.4
Powers of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

The differentiation matrices
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are indexed over integers and they are not banded. Therefore, we cannot take for granted that the individual entries of their powers (necessary, for example, to approximate higher derivatives) are bounded. The boundedness of the (univariate) differentiation matrix
\mathcal{D}
made up for a significant part of the narrative in
[
11
]
. Given a univariate weight
w
, there exists
s\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\mathcal{D}}^{k}
is bounded for
k=0,\ldots,s
and at least some entries of
\mathcal{D}^{s+1}
are unbounded: it is then said that
\mathcal{D}
has
index

s
. The boundedness of the index is important because, once the support of
w
is, for example, a bounded interval, an index equal to infinity would lead to exceedingly poor approximation
[
11
]
.

The extension of this work to orthogonal systems in triangles will not be pursued in this paper. The method of proof in
[
11
]
is unlikely to extend to bivariate setting and, at any rate, because
\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}
is at present unknown (although available in a recursive form), the method of proof from
[
11
]
is probably unsuitable. Having said so, it is highly likely that an important consequence of
[
11
]
remains valid, namely that in the ‘ultraspherical case’
\alpha=\beta=\gamma
the index increases as
\alpha
grows. In Section 5 we argue that an optimal choice for analytic function
f
is for the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
to be even natural numbers and it is likely that, similarly to
[
11
]
, the index of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
grows with
\alpha=\beta=\gamma
. Yet, this is matter for future research.

3.5
Matrix-vector multiplication of the differentiation matrices

Univariate W-systems allow for rapid numerical linear algebra: multiplication of a vector by the differentiation matrix (an action corresponding to differentiation), exponentiation of the differential matrix and solution of linear algebraic systems with a matrix polynomial in the differentiation matrix
[
11
]
. The reason is that the differentiation matrix in two major instances (ultraspherical weights in
(-1,1)
, Laguerre weights in
(0,\infty)
) is either
semi-separable
of rank 1 or can be decomposed into ‘even’ and ‘odd’ matrices with this feature. Specifically, each submatrix of
\mathcal{D}
strictly above (or, by skew symmetry, strictly below) the main diagonal is of rank 1.

The situation is more complicated in our bivariate case. In this subsection, we describe fast matrix/vector multiplication for the differentiation matrices
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. This is important because functions in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
are described in our setting by
\ell_{2}[\mathbb{Z}_{+}]
vectors: once
f\in\mathcal{H}
corresponds to
\mathbcal{f}
,
\partial f/\partial x
and
\partial f/\partial y
correspond to the vectors
\mathcal{X}\mathbcal{f}
and
\mathcal{Y}\mathbcal{f}
respectively.

Bearing in mind (
10
), we separate
\mathcal{X}
into two sub-matrices, denoted by
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{E}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\alpha h^{\gamma,\beta}_{k}\delta_{\ell,k}I^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-k,k),(n-k,k)}\right]_{0\leq k\leq\min\{m,n\}}\!,\qquad\mathcal{F}=\left[\mathcal{F}_{m,n}\right]_{0\leq m,n\leq M}\!,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{m,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\gamma S^{\gamma,\beta}_{\ell,k}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}\right]_{0\leq\ell\leq m,0\leq k\leq n}\!,\qquad\mathcal{E}=\left[\mathcal{E}_{m,n}\right]_{0\leq m,n\leq M}\!.

In particular, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(m-k,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(m-k,k)}=0,\quad\ell\neq k,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(m-k,k),(m-k,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(m-k,k),(m-k,k)},\quad\ell=k

which also indicates the diagonal entries of
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{E}
vanish. Alternatively,
\mathcal{F}_{m,m}=\mathcal{E}_{m,m}
. Furthermore, since
\mathcal{X}
is skew-symmetric, we set the truncated matrix with the symmetry by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{M,M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathcal{F}_{0,0}&-\mathcal{F}_{1,0}^{\top}&-\mathcal{F}_{2,0}^{\top}&\cdots&-\mathcal{F}_{M,0}^{\top}\\
\mathcal{F}_{1,0}&\mathcal{F}_{1,1}&-\mathcal{F}_{2,1}^{\top}&\cdots&-\mathcal{F}_{M,1}^{\top}\\
\mathcal{F}_{2,0}&\mathcal{F}_{2,1}&\mathcal{F}_{2,2}&\cdots&-\mathcal{F}_{M,2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{F}_{M,0}&\mathcal{F}_{M,1}&\mathcal{F}_{M,2}&\cdots&\mathcal{F}_{M,M}\end{array}\right]\!,\qquad\mathcal{F}_{n,m}=-\mathcal{F}_{m,n}^{\top},

where
\mathcal{F}_{m,n}
is a diagonal matrix of
(m+1)\times(n+1)
whose entries are

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{(m-k,k),(m-k,k)}=\alpha h^{\gamma,\beta}_{k}\mathfrak{a}_{m,k}\mathfrak{b}_{n,k},\qquad k=0,1,\cdots,\min\{m,n\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{m,k}=(-1)^{m}\frac{\alpha h^{\gamma,\beta}_{k}\mathrm{\Gamma}\!(\beta+\gamma+m+k+2)}{\mathrm{\Gamma}\!(\alpha+\beta+\gamma+m+k+2)},\quad\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}=(-1)^{n}\frac{\mathrm{\Gamma}\!(\alpha+n-k+1)}{(n-k)!}

from the expression (
13
). An illustrative structure of

is

\displaystyle\mathbcal{g}

\displaystyle\sigma_{m,j}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{0,0}f_{0,0},\qquad\rho_{0,0}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathfrak{a}_{n,0}f_{n,0}

Counting the cost of each
\mathbcal{g}_{m}
results in

flops which implies that the entire cost of a matrix/vector multiplication is
\sum_{m=0}^{M}4(m+1)=2(M+1)(M+2)
. This should be compared with the dimension
\frac{1}{2}(M+1)(M+2)\times\frac{1}{2}(M+1)(M+2)
of
\mathcal{F}_{M,M}
.

The other sub-matrix is
\mathcal{E}
composed of
\gamma S^{\gamma,\beta}_{\ell,k}\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}

\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(m-k,k)}=0,\qquad\ell\neq k

implies that
\mathcal{E}_{m,m}
is diagonal. Although

has different expressions for

and
\ell<l

11
) because of
\tilde{I}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}=0
for
\ell<k
. We write the illustrative structure of
\mathcal{E}
truncated by
M
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M,M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathcal{E}_{0,0}&-\mathcal{E}_{1,0}^{\top}&-\mathcal{E}_{2,0}^{\top}&\cdots&-\mathcal{E}_{M,0}^{\top}\\
\mathcal{E}_{1,0}&\mathcal{E}_{1,1}&-\mathcal{E}_{2,1}^{\top}&\cdots&-\mathcal{E}_{M,1}^{\top}\\
\mathcal{E}_{2,0}&\mathcal{E}_{2,1}&\mathcal{E}_{2,2}&\cdots&-\mathcal{E}_{M,2}^{\top}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\

\end{array}\right]\!,

where
\mathcal{E}_{m,n}
is a square matrix with dimension
(m+1)\times(n+1)
and proceed with the matrix vector multiplication, letting first

\mathbcal{h}=\mathcal{E}_{M,M}\mathbcal{f},\qquad\mathbcal{h}_{M}=[\mathbcal{h}_{0},\mathbcal{h}_{1},\cdots,\mathbcal{h}_{M}]^{\top}\!,\qquad\mathbcal{h}_{m}=[h_{m,0},h_{m,1},\cdots,h_{m,m}]^{\top}\!.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{(m-\ell,\ell),(n-k,k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\tilde{d}^{[2]}_{m,\ell}\mathcal{E}_{(m-(\ell-1),\ell-1),(n-k,k)},

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{[1]}_{m,\ell}

we rewrite the recursive formula from Theorem 2 in a matrix form,

which we rewrite as

The above construction applies for the differentiation matrix
\mathcal{X}
. but an identical procedure applies to the matrix
\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{E}
and the diagonal block matrix is a zero matrix which means that the cost is

4
Convergence of Koornwinder-type W-functions on a triangle

The immediate purpose of constructing the W-system
\{\varphi_{n,k}(x_{1},x_{2})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\;k=0,\ldots,n}
is to approximate bivariate
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathcal{T})
functions
f(x_{1},x_{2})
which obey zero boundary conditions along
\partial\mathcal{T}
. Within the context of this paper, we intend this approximation to be used as a major ingredient of a spectral method, while acknowledging its wider applications. The main weapon in our endeavour, to construct fast-approximating W-systems, is the choice of the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
.

In a univariate setting this has been already considered for
\alpha=\beta>0
in
[
11
]
and our analysis threads the same ground. Thus, we associate to
f\in\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathcal{T})
the sequence

\hat{\mathbcal{f}}=\{\hat{f}_{m,\ell}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\;\ell=0,\ldots,m},\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\hat{f}_{m,\ell}=\int_{\mathcal{T}}f(x,y)\varphi_{m,\ell}(x,y){\hbox{d}}x{\hbox{d}}y.

We are concerned with the convergence of

g_{M}:=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\hat{f}_{m,\ell}\varphi_{m,\ell}

to
f
(we need to assume
f\in\mathrm{L}_{2}
but for present purposes it is easier to stipulate that
f
is analytic in the closure of
\mathcal{T}
or, at the very least, lives in
\mathrm{C}^{p}(\mathcal{T})
for sufficiently large
p
) as
M\rightarrow\infty
. Note however that

\hat{f}_{m,\ell}=\int_{\mathcal{T}}f(x,y)\sqrt{w(x,y)}p_{m,\ell}(x,y){\hbox{d}}x{\hbox{d}}y=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\frac{f(x,y)}{\sqrt{w(x,y)}}p_{m}(x,y)w(x,y){\hbox{d}}x{\hbox{d}}y.

Therefore, the speed of convergence for
f
in the context of W-systems is identical to that of
f/\sqrt{w}
in the context of orthogonal polynomials. Note that
w>0
in the interior of
\mathcal{T}
, while both
f=0
and
w=0
along its boundary.

Once
f
is an analytic function, the convergence of
g_{M}
to
f
is very fast: in the univariate case, in the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm, it is
spectral:

\limsup_{M\rightarrow\infty}\|f-g_{M}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}}^{1/M}\in[0,1)
and, although we are not aware of general results of this kind in a triangle, there are good reasons to suspect that this remains the case. Thus, we should choose
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
so that
f/\sqrt{w}
remains analytic. A good choice is
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=2
because then also
\sqrt{w}
is analytic, while the singularity along the boundary caused by the division by
\sqrt{w}
is removable.

With greater generality, let
f(x,y)=x^{\kappa}y^{\theta}(1-x-y)^{\eta}\tilde{f}(x,y)
, where
\kappa,\theta,\eta>0
are non-integer, while
\tilde{f}
is analytic in
\mathcal{T}
. Then the choice
\alpha=2(1+\kappa)
,
\beta=2(1+\theta)
and
\gamma=2(1+\eta)
means that
f/\sqrt{w}
is analytic!

As an example of a function with weak singularity along
\partial\mathcal{T}
we let

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{e}}^{x-2y}\sqrt{xy(1-x-y)},\quad(x,y)\in\mathcal{T},

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\mathcal{T},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\leq x\leq 1,\ 0\leq y\leq 1-x\}.

The approximate series is

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{n_{\max}}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{n,k}\varphi_{n,k}(x,y),

5
General Dirichlet boundary conditions

A major assumption in the definition of W-systems is that they (and, by implication, the differential equations that we endevour to discretise) obey zero Dirichlet boundary conditions. In one dimension this assumption is not very restrictive. For example, given a PDE

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}={\mathcal{L}}u+f(t,u),\qquad t\geq 0,\quad x\in[-1,1],

where
\mathcal{L}
is a linear differential operator, given in tandem with an initial condition
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)
and the boundary conditions
u(-1,t)=a_{-}(t)
,
u(1,t)=a_{+}(t)
,
t\geq 0
, we construct for every
t\geq 0
a linear interpolation
\mu(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}(1-x)a_{-}(t)+\frac{1}{2}(1+x)a_{+}(t)
and let
v(x,t)=u(x,t)-\mu(x,t)
. Then

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}={\mathcal{L}}v+f(t,v+\mu)+\mathcal{L}\mu-\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial t}

(32)

with the initial condition
v(x,0)=u_{0}(x)-\mu(x,0)
and
zero
Dirichlet boundary conditions. All that is required is to solve (
32
) and let
u(x,t)=v(x,t)+\mu(x,t)
.

Similar idea works also in a multivariate setting, except that the formation of the ‘boundary function’
\mu
is considerably more complicated, even in a right triangle. In this paper we adopt similar reasoning to
[
10
]
, based upon ideas originating in computer-aided geometric modelling. First, however, we note that the benefits of this approach are not restricted to spectral methods for differential equations given in a triangle with non-zero Dirichlet boundary conditions but also extend to spectral element methods
[
13
]
and to
p
and
hp
-adapted finite elements
[
1
]
.

\displaystyle\mu(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-x-y}{2(1-x)}\mu(x,0)+\frac{1-x-y}{2(1-y)}\mu(0,y)+\frac{y}{2(1-x)}\mu(x,1-x)

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\frac{x}{2(1-y)}\mu(1-y,y)+\frac{x}{2(x+y)}\mu(x+y,0)+\frac{y}{2(x+y)}\mu(0,x+y)

\displaystyle\frac{1-x-y}{2}\!\left[\frac{\mu(x,0)}{1-x}+\frac{\mu(0,y)}{1-y}-\mu(0,0)\right]
