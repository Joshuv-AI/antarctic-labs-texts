Bosonic coding: introduction and use cases
Victor V. Albert

Abstract
Bosonic or continuous-variable coding is a field concerned with robust quantum information processing and communication with electromagnetic signals or mechanical modes. I review bosonic quantum memories, characterizing them as either bosonic stabilizer or bosonic Fock-state codes. I then enumerate various applications of bosonic encodings, four of which circumvent no-go theorems due to the intrinsic infinite-dimensionality of bosonic systems.

Conventional quantum error correction

In an error-correcting protocol, a message
\rho
is encoded by the sender (
a.k.a.
source) using an encoding map
{\cal{E}}
, passed through a noisy channel
{\cal{N}}
, and extracted by the receiver (
a.k.a.
sink) using a decoding map
{\cal{D}}
.
The noisy channel can represent transmission through space
or storage in a stationary memory device.
If error correction succeeds, then the message should remain roughly intact such that noisy transmission is nearly equivalent to the noiseless case,

{\cal{D}}{\cal{N}}{\cal{E}}(\rho)\approx\rho\quad\quad\text{(error correction).}

(1)

The same picture holds irrespective of whether the information is classical or quantum, with the difference lying in the structure of the message and the inputs and outputs of the maps
{\cal{E}}
,
{\cal{N}}
and
{\cal{D}}
.

In a computational setting, one is also interested in performing
operations, or
logical gates
, on the stored information.
The subfield of fault tolerance
[
16
]
is concerned with successfully applying logical gates and other operations on the message within a noisy environment.

The smallest non-trivial quantum system is a two-level system,
a.k.a.
a qubit.
Multiple qubits can be used to store and process quantum information in a sufficiently redundant way so as to protect against noise.
Most of quantum error correction is concerned with such qubit codes.

Quantum information is stored in quantum superpositions of system states, e.g., superpositions of states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
for the case of a single qubit.
Multi-qubit states are labeled by binary strings, and the message
\rho
to be transmitted is in general a positive-semidefinite operator on the vector space of such states.

A qubit encoding

​
maps logical quantum information redundantly into the
logical
or
code space
— a subspace of the Hilbert space of several
physical qubits
.
A basis for the code space is spanned by basis elements called
logical codewords
.
A simple qubit encoding is the repetition code, mapping a single logical qubit into three physical qubits as
|\mu\rangle\to|\mu_{L}\rangle=|\mu,\mu,\mu\rangle
for
\mu\in\{0,1\}
.

An error can map the codespace into an
error space
spanned by basis elements called
error words
.
Determining which errors can be detected and corrected with a particular code can be done using the Knill-Laflamme
error-correction conditions

[
17
]

attr/Border[0 0 0] user/Subtype /Link /A ¡¡ /S /URI /URI (https://errorcorrectionzoo.org/c/qecc_finite) ¿¿
​
.
A code is said to have
distance

d
if errors occurring on at most
d-1
physical qubits can be detected.

The quantum superpositions storing logical information are susceptible not only to bit-flip errors
X=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
that permute the bitstring-labeled computational qubit states, but also phase-flip errors
Z=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
that modify the relative amplitude and phase of the superposition.
One also has to worry about combinations of the two,
Y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, which are often assumed to occur at a similar rate as individual bit- and phase-flips.
Multi-qubit errors are typically represented as tensor products of Pauli matrices
X,Y,Z
and the identity matrix
I=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
This is in part because Pauli matrices conveniently form a basis for the set of all error operators, meaning that any error can be expressed as a linear combination of tensor-product
Pauli strings
.

In order to detect and correct errors, one performs a
round
(
a.k.a.
cycle) of error correction.
Detection is made possible by
a set of “optimally coarse-grained” observables called
check operators
, whose measurement distinguishes error spaces from the codespace without collapsing logical quantum superpositions within the respective spaces.
One round consists of (A) detecting the error via quantum measurement of check-operator eigenvalues,
which are usually called
syndromes
; and (B)
decoding
, i.e., applying a correction operation conditional on the syndrome that maps the state in the identified error space back into the codespace.

Check operators that are simple to express and measure are generally difficult to determine for a given code.

This set forms a group, and each element of this group can be represented as a product of powers of a set of generating elements.
Such

Detectable errors fail to commute with at least one stabilizer generator.

3
Bosonic modes

The formalism of bosonic coding begins by quantizing the electromagnetic field
[
21
]
and forming bosonic modes — quantum systems described by continuous conjugate variables (
a.k.a.
quadratures).
A bosonic or continuous-variable (CV) mode (
a.k.a.
a qumode or, more colloquially, a harmonic oscillator) can correspond to an
electromagnetic
mode of a microwave cavity, an optical cavity/fiber, or free space.
Alternatively, bosonic modes can be
mechanical
(
a.k.a.
motional or phononic), representing vibrating atomic or molecular nuclei or acoustic or nano-mechanical resonators.

While qubit canonical or computational basis states are labeled by binary strings, there exists a somewhat analogous set of canonical single-mode “states”
|x\rangle
labeled by the real number
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, corresponding to the configuration space of a particle on a real line.
The word basis is in quotes because such
position “states”
are orthogonal, but not normalizable:
\langle x|y\rangle=\delta(x-y)
, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function.
Despite this, they retain several favorable and extensively utilized features:

1.

Position states are “complete”, i.e., they can be used to express vectors
|f\rangle
within the mode’s Hilbert space as

|f\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}x|x\rangle\langle x|f\rangle\equiv\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}xf(x)|x\rangle~.

(4)

This is analogous to expressing an arbitrary qubit state in the computational basis.

2.

The position “basis” provides a set of generalized eigenstates with which one can diagonalize the oscillator
position operator
(
{\hat{x}}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}x|x\rangle x\langle x|
).
A dual (i.e., Fourier-transformed) non-normalizable set of
momentum “states”
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\text{d}}xe^{ipx}|x\rangle
, form a bosonic analogue of the qubit
|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle
basis and diagonalize the oscillator’s
momentum operator
(
{\hat{p}}=-i\frac{{\text{d}}}{{\text{d}}x}
).

3.

Completeness of the position “basis” implies that the probability
|\langle x|f\rangle|^{2}
can be sampled with a bosonic
quantum measurement
.
Similar continuous-parameter measurements can be formulated for momentum states and other complete sets of non-normalizable “states”
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

Position and momentum operators satisfy the famous commutation relation
[{\hat{x}},{\hat{p}}]=i
.
Realizing such a commutation relation requires an intrinsically infinite-dimensional space.
To see this, try taking the trace of both sides of the commutation relation.
Cyclic permutation of
{\hat{x}}
and
{\hat{p}}
under the trace, a well-defined operation for finite-dimensional spaces, yields zero for the left-hand side and thus contradicts the right-hand side.

A bona-fide orthonormal basis for a single mode consists of the
Fock states

\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
— eigenstates of the
occupation-number operator

{\hat{n}}=\frac{1}{2}({\hat{p}}^{2}+{\hat{x}}^{2}-1)
(
a.k.a.
the harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian).
This Hamiltonian can be conveniently written in terms of the mode’s
lowering operator

{\hat{a}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\hat{x}}+i{\hat{p}})
and its conjugate as

{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}{\hat{a}}={\hat{n}}
, and Fock states satisfy the eigenvalue relation
{\hat{n}}|n\rangle=n|n\rangle
.
Such states provide an alternative to Eq. (
4
), expanding vectors as

|f\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 0}|n\rangle\langle n|f\rangle\equiv\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{n}|n\rangle~.

(5)

The expectation value of the oscillator Hamiltonian,
\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle=\langle f|{\hat{n}}|f\rangle
, corresponds to the
average energy
of the mode in terms of the number of bosonic energy carriers, often called photons or phonons.
Similar
occupation-number moments

\langle{\hat{n}}^{\ell}\rangle
are defined for higher powers
\ell\geq 1
.

Normalizable vectors
|f\rangle

may admit non-normalizable “excitations”
{\hat{n}}^{\ell}|f\rangle
— a subtlety not present in finite dimensions.
This is equivalent to the fact that normalizable vectors may have infinite occupation-number moments.
For example, a vector with Fock-state coefficients
f_{n}=1/\sqrt{n^{1+\epsilon}}
is normalizable, but has infinite average energy for any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
.
I define
bosonic pure states
as those vectors whose moments are finite
for all

\ell
; the space of such vectors is called
Schwartz space

1
1

1

Schwartz space
S({\mathbb{R}})

[
25
,
26
]
[
27
, Appx. C.3]

is a subspace of the space of normalizable vectors,
S({\mathbb{R}})\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
The position “states”
\langle x|
live in the space dual to Schwartz space, the space of tempered distributions
S({\mathbb{R}})^{\prime}\supset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Together, the three spaces make up the rigged Hilbert space construction.
.
This definition is both physically meaningful and mathematically consistent, as position-“state” overlaps
f(x)=\langle x|f\rangle

may be infinite for normalizable vectors not in Schwartz space, thereby “breaking” any position-“state” quantum measurements.
In other words, while each normalizable vector
|f\rangle
is in one-to-one correspondence with a square-integrable function
f(x)
on the real line per Eq. (
4
), not all such functions
ought to be regarded as reasonable quantum states.

While I maintain that we should define our state space such that
{\hat{n}}^{\ell}|f\rangle
remains a physical quantum state, it is interesting to note that the excitation operator
{\hat{n}}^{\ell}
cannot be applied on a vector as a result of any quantum channel.
Quantum channels — the most general maps on quantum states — consist of
Kraus operators

K
such that the sum
\sum_{K}K^{\dagger}K
has all eigenvalues
\leq 1

[
28
]
.
Since the eigenvalues of
{\hat{n}}
grow without bound,
{\hat{n}}
or its powers cannot be Kraus operators.
Kraus operators for common bosonic noise channels (see Sec.
6
) turn out to be much tamer, belonging to the set of
trace-class
operators, for which
\text{Tr}(\sqrt{K^{\dagger}K})
is finite.

Bosonic stabilizer codes
[
29
]

Oscillator generalizations of the unitary Pauli bit and phase flips are represented by
displacements
or
shifts
.
For a single mode, the two types of displacements — position shifts and momentum shifts — act on position states as
e^{-iq{\hat{p}}}\left|y\right\rangle=\left|y+q\right\rangle
and
e^{iq{\hat{x}}}\left|y\right\rangle=e^{iqy}\left|y\right\rangle
,
where
q
is any real number.
The opposite relations hold for the momentum states:
e^{iq{\hat{x}}}
shifts the momentum while
e^{-iq{\hat{p}}}
is diagonal, e.g.,

e^{-iq{\hat{p}}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}xe^{ipx}|x\rangle\right)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}xe^{ipx}|x+q\rangle=e^{-iqp}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}xe^{ipx}|x\rangle\right)~.

(6)

Trace-class operators acting on multiple modes can be expanded in terms of tensor products of single-mode displacements
[
30
]
, akin to Pauli strings in the qubit case.

Commuting sets of displacement operators define bosonic stabilizer codes in a natural extension of the qubit stabilizer formalism to bosonic modes.
One can define bosonic stabilizer codes for a single mode or multiple modes.
Groups of displacements can be continuous, yielding analog stabilizer codes, or discrete, yielding either GKP or GKP-stabilizer codes.
The logical subspace can be finite- or infinite-dimensional, depending on which group is considered.
Ideal codewords are not normalizable, and recipes for creating approximate states (lying in Schwartz space) are postponed until Sec.
4.4
.

Position states yield an immediate generalization of the qubit repetition encoding

|x\rangle\to|x,x,x\rangle

This code encodes one
logical mode
into three physical modes, protecting from errors that cause shifts in the position of any one of the physical modes. For correcting errors that induce relative phases between position states, a dual momentum-state repetition code can be used.

Concatenating two-mode versions of the two repetition codes yields the four-mode code, an analogue of the four-qubit code (
2

|x_{\text{four-mode}}\rangle=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}ye^{ixy}|y,y\rangle\right)^{\otimes 2}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}y\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}z\,e^{ix\left(y+z\right)}|y,y,z,z\rangle~.

(7)

The above four-mode code detects any error on a single mode, be it a shift in position or momentum.

Notice how the four-mode codewords are
+1
-eigenvalue eigenvectors of the displacements
S_{1}=e^{i({\hat{x}}_{1}-{\hat{x}}_{2})}
,
S_{2}=e^{i({\hat{x}}_{3}-{\hat{x}}_{4})}
, and
S_{3}=e^{-i({\hat{p}}_{1}+{\hat{p}}_{2}-{\hat{p}}_{3}-{\hat{p}}_{4})}
. Products of powers of these three elements constitute the stabilizer group of the four-mode code,

\mathsf{S}_{\text{four-mode}}=\{S_{1}^{a}S_{2}^{b}S_{3}^{c}~|~a,b,c\in{\mathbb{R}}\}~,

(8)

whose joint
+1
-eigenvalue eigenspace is exactly the codespace.
The above group is a continuous analogue of the four-qubit code’s stabilizer group (
3
).

All single-mode displacements do not commute with a power of at least one
S_{i}
, meaning that the distance of the four-mode code (
7

[[4,1,2]]_{\mathbb{R}}

analog stabilizer
encoding
[

a,b,c
of the elements
S_{i}

Since any real power
q
of the
S_{i}
yields a distinct group element, it is convenient to instead study the
nullifiers
— the set of mutually commuting operators in the exponentials of the elements
S_{i}
that form the stabilizer group’s Lie algebra. For the four-mode code, the nullifiers are
{\hat{x}}_{1}-{\hat{x}}_{2}

{\hat{x}}_{3}-{\hat{x}}_{4}

{\hat{p}}_{1}+{\hat{p}}_{2}-{\hat{p}}_{3}-{\hat{p}}_{4}

({\hat{x}}_{1}-{\hat{x}}_{2})|x_{\text{four-mode}}\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}y\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}z\,e^{ix\left(y+z\right)}(y-y)|y,y,z,z\rangle=0~.

(9)

4.2
GKP codes
[
38
]

The four-mode code’s stabilizer group (
8
) is continuous, admitting an
infinite
-dimensional logical space protected against arbitrarily large displacements acting on only
a few
modes. Discrete stabilizer groups are also possible, admitting a
finite
-dimensional logical space protected against sufficiently small displacements acting on
all
modes.

A discrete stabilizer group of commuting displacements can exist even for one mode. While displacements do not generally commute,

e^{-iq_{1}{\hat{p}}}e^{iq_{2}{\hat{x}}}=e^{iq_{1}q_{2}}e^{iq_{2}{\hat{x}}}e^{-iq_{1}{\hat{p}}}\quad\quad\text{(position-momentum group commutator)},

(10)

they
can
commute for fine-tuned values of the displacement magnitudes
q_{1,2}
. Consider the case
q_{1}=q_{2}=\sqrt{2\pi N}
for some natural number
N
, for which the commutator phase reduces as
e^{iq_{1}q_{2}}=e^{i2\pi N}=1
. The displacements
S_{1}=e^{i\sqrt{2\pi N}{\hat{x}}}
and
S_{2}=e^{-i\sqrt{2\pi N}{\hat{p}}}
thus commute, forming a stabilizer group of the GKP code,

\mathsf{S}_{\text{GKP}}=\langle S_{1},S_{2}\rangle=\langle e^{i\sqrt{2\pi N}\hat{x}},e^{-i\sqrt{2\pi N}\hat{p}}\rangle=\{S_{1}^{a}S_{2}^{b}\,|\,a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}~.

(11)

The above stabilizers admit a joint
N
-dimensional qudit
+1
-eigenvalue eigenspace, with qudit logical operators
Z_{L}=S_{1}^{1/N}
and
X_{L}=S_{2}^{1/N}
. This eigenspace is spanned by codewords that are invariant under the discrete phase-space shifts induced by the stabilizers. Therefore, such states can be expressed as a particular
lattice
of position states. For the logical-qubit case (
N=2
), the codewords are

|0_{\text{GKP}}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\left(2\ell\right)\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad|1_{\text{GKP}}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\left(2\ell+1\right)\sqrt{\pi}\rangle\,,

(12)

consisting of position states labeled by points on two alternating 1D lattices whose points are spaced apart by
2\sqrt{\pi}
. Each position state is a
+1
-eigenvalue eigenvector of the stabilizer
S_{1}
, and the lattice spacing ensures that both lattices are invariant under position shifts (by discrete multiples of
2\sqrt{\pi}
) induced by
S_{2}
and its powers.

p\in(-\sqrt{\pi},\sqrt{\pi})

There are no nullifiers for this code since the stabilizer group is discrete.
Detectable displacements fail to commute with
S_{1}
and/or
S_{2}

\sqrt{\pi}

(-\sqrt{\pi}/2,\sqrt{\pi}/2)
of the lattice formed by
both
the logical-zero and logical-one position-state labels [black and white circles in Fig.

The GKP stabilizer generators

can be thought of as generators of translations of a 2D square lattice in the mode’s phase space. Generalizations of the square-lattice GKP code include rectangular-lattice GKP codes protecting against larger displacements in one quadrature at the expense of lesser protection against displacements in the other quadrature. Extending to codes on
n
modes, the stabilizer group generated by a pair of square-lattice GKP stabilizers for each mode yields a multimode GKP code, corresponding to a
2n

4.3
GKP-stabilizer codes
[
40
]

attr/Border[0 0 0] user/Subtype /Link /A ¡¡ /S /URI /URI (https://errorcorrectionzoo.org/c/gkp-stabilizer) ¿¿
​

GKP codes on
n
modes with
2n
stabilizer generators support a finite-dimensional logical subspace,
but removing some of the GKP stabilizers makes the logical dimension infinite while maintaining some of the protection.
These codes are the most recent addition to the bosonic stabilizer class, so I devote more space to describe their abilities.

Consider a two-mode GKP stabilizer group generated by square-lattice generators (
11
) with
N=1
supported on mode two. The joint
+1
-eigenvalue eigenspace of such a lopsided group consists of any state on the first mode (since there are no stabilizers supported there) tensored with the “canonical” GKP state on the second mode,

|\textsc{GKP}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\ell\sqrt{2\pi}\rangle,\quad\quad\text{with stabilizer generators}\quad\quad e^{i\sqrt{2\pi}{\hat{x}}_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad e^{-i\sqrt{2\pi}{\hat{p}}_{2}}~.

(13)

To suppress shift errors on the first mode, we apply an entangling operation
before and after
sending the code though a displacement-noise channel. The
csum
gate transforms the four quadratures
\{{\hat{x}}_{1},{\hat{p}}_{1},{\hat{x}}_{2},{\hat{p}}_{2}\}
as
{\hat{x}}_{2}\to{\hat{x}}_{2}+{\hat{x}}_{1}
,
{\hat{p}}_{1}\to{\hat{p}}_{1}-{\hat{p}}_{2}
, and leaves the other two intact. The inverse of
csum
is applied after the displacement noise. After the complete
\textsc{csum}\to\textsc{noise}\to\textsc{csum}^{\dagger}
sequence, the four quadratures transform as

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\begin{aligned} {\hat{x}}_{1}&\to{\hat{x}}_{1}+\delta x_{1}\\
{\hat{p}}_{1}&\to{\hat{p}}_{1}+\delta p_{1}+\delta p_{2}\end{aligned}&&\begin{aligned} {\hat{x}}_{2}&\to{\hat{x}}_{2}+\delta x_{2}-\delta x_{1}\\
{\hat{p}}_{2}&\to{\hat{p}}_{2}+\delta p_{2}\end{aligned}\end{array}\,,

(14)

where
\{\delta x_{1},\delta p_{1},\delta x_{2},\delta p_{2}\}
are small random shifts induced by the noise channel.

Information about the first mode’s displacement errors that was transferred to the second mode via the
csum
gate can now be extracted via stabilizer measurements. Measuring the code’s stabilizer generators (
13
) extracts the second mode’s displacement errors, yielding outcomes
x^{\text{obs}}=\delta x_{2}-\delta x_{1}
and
p^{\text{obs}}=\delta p_{2}
. Both errors are measured modulo
\sqrt{2\pi}
— the spacing of that mode’s GKP state (
13
) — but this can be disregarded for simplicity by assuming that both shifts are sufficiently small.

To recover against the momentum shift in the first mode, a momentum displacement by
-p^{\text{obs}}=-\delta p_{2}
is applied, thereby correcting the part of the shift from Eq. (
14
) that came from mode two,
{\hat{p}}_{1}+\delta p_{1}+\delta p_{2}\to{\hat{p}}_{1}+\delta p_{1}
.

On the other hand, exact correction of the position shift
\delta x_{1}
is not possible because its value cannot be obtained from the measurement outcome
x^{\text{obs}}
.
Instead, one obtains a maximum-likelihood estimate
\delta x_{1}^{\text{est}}
of the true shift error using knowledge of
x^{\text{obs}}
and the assumption that the most likely overall shift magnitude is the smallest one,

\delta x_{1}^{\text{est}}=\argmin_{\delta x_{1}}~(\delta x_{1})^{2}+(\delta x_{2})^{2}=\argmin_{\delta x_{1}}~(\delta x_{1})^{2}+(x^{\text{obs}}+\delta x_{1})^{2}=-\frac{x^{\text{obs}}}{2}\,.

(15)

Displacing the the first mode’s position back by the above amount transforms the first position quadrature from Eq. (
14
) as
{\hat{x}}_{1}+\delta x_{1}\to{\hat{x}}_{1}+\delta x_{1}+\frac{x^{\text{obs}}}{2}={\hat{x}}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(\delta x_{1}+\delta x_{2})
.

In summary, the combination of
csum
conjugation and GKP recovery yields output displacement errors
\delta p_{1}
and
\frac{1}{2}(\delta x_{1}+\delta x_{2})
on the position and momentum quadratures of the logical mode, respectively. How is this an improvement?
For simplicity, let’s assume that the four input shift errors
\{\delta x_{1},\delta p_{1},\delta x_{2},\delta p_{2}\}
coming from a displacement-noise channel are independent and distributed according to a Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
Due to the tendency of sums of such random variables to converge to their mean, the sum
\frac{1}{2}(\delta x_{1}+\delta x_{2})
follows a distribution with variance
\sigma^{2}/2
,
half
as much as that of the noise
\delta x_{1}
in an unencoded mode.

The above GKP-repetition
[
40
]

attr/Border[0 0 0] user/Subtype /Link /A ¡¡ /S /URI /URI (https://errorcorrectionzoo.org/c/gkp-stabilizer) ¿¿
​
encoding yields a reduction in the position noise of a logical mode
without
increasing the noise in the momentum. Using more modes and other gates in addition to
csum
, it is possible to reduce noise in
both
quadratures in similar fashion. Other GKP-stabilizer codes can
suppress the output variance by some power (up to constants and logarithmic corrections), e.g.,
\sigma^{2}\to\sigma^{4}
for the GKP-two-mode-squeezing code. The above maximum-likelihood decoder holds for general versions of such codes
[
41
]
.

4.4
Normalization and precision

The bosonic stabilizer codewords that we have discussed so far are unphysical.
Logical position states of the analog repetition encoding,
|x_{L}\rangle=|x,x,x\rangle
, overlap as
\langle x_{L}|y_{L}\rangle=[\delta(x-y)]^{3}
, which is not the proper
\delta
-function normalization condition of single-more position states. GKP states span a finite-dimensional logical subspace, yet are also not normalizable. As a result, error correction as prescribed above will not be possible for such codes in the real world, and approximations have to be made.

Approximate codewords can be created by substituting each position state
|x\rangle
in the ideal codewords as a Gaussian of width
\Delta
centered at
x
. This is equivalent to applying a linear combination of Gaussian-weighed position displacements to the ideal position state (up to a normalization constant),

|x\rangle\to|x^{(\Delta)}\rangle\propto e^{-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}{\hat{p}}^{2}}|x\rangle={\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\textstyle{\text{d}}y}e^{-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}/\Delta^{2}}e^{-iy{\hat{p}}}|x\rangle={\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\text{d}}ye^{-\frac{1}{2}(y-x)^{2}/\Delta^{2}}|y\rangle~.

(16)

The first equality is obtained by treating
{\hat{p}}
as a real number (by temporarily inserting a resolution of identity in terms of momentum states) and expressing the exponential in terms of its Fourier transform.
The second equality results from applying the displacement and changing variables in the integral.
The new state can be thought of as squeezed in the position quadrature, reducing to the infinitely squeezed position state as
\Delta\to 0
.

In case of GKP codewords, a dual position-state envelope
e^{-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}{\hat{x}}^{2}}
is also required because there is an infinite number of position states present in each codeword. Up to a normalization constant, this yields

|\textsc{GKP}\rangle\to|\textsc{GKP}^{(\Delta)}\rangle\propto e^{-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}{\hat{p}}^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{2}{\hat{x}}^{2}}\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\ell\sqrt{2\pi}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\pi\Delta^{2}\ell^{2}}|\ell\sqrt{2\pi}\,^{(\Delta)}\rangle

(17)

for the canonical GKP state (
13
). The joint effect of momentum- and position-state envelopes can be implemented with a
cooling operator
(
a.k.a.
damping operator or regularizer)
[
42
, Appx. B]
,

e^{-\beta{\hat{n}}}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left({\hat{p}}^{2}+{\hat{x}}^{2}-1\right)}\quad\quad\text{for}\quad\quad\beta\in(0,\infty)~.

(18)

Cooled GKP codewords yield an intrinsic probability of misidentification
due to non-orthogonality of the codewords, but such an error is exponentially suppressed as
\Delta\to 0
. Displacement noise interacts very simply with cooled GKP states, merely increasing the sizes of the position and momentum envelopes.

Another complication of bosonic stabilizer codes is that it is impossible to keep track of a continuum of error spaces due to the finite precision of any measurement apparatus,
which leads to additional imperfections
[
43
]
.
Coarse-graining this continuum into a finite number
M
of possible outcomes requires an ancillary system of at least dimension
M
,
presenting overhead not present in the qubit case, in which measurements are typically binary.
Remarkably, recent experiments
[
44
,
45
]
(for theory, see
[
46
, Supplement]
) have performed beneficial GKP error correction by extracting syndromes to only
one bit
of precision, i.e., requiring the smallest possible overhead of
M=2
.

5.1
Number-phase codes
[
47
]

attr/Border[0 0 0] user/Subtype /Link /A ¡¡ /S /URI /URI (https://errorcorrectionzoo.org/c/number_phase) ¿¿
​

GKP codes are based on lattices in (linear) position and momentum. Analogues of GKP codes for the rotational degrees of freedom of a
planar rotor

[
48
]
are called rotor GKP codes
[
38
,
49
]

attr/Border[0 0 0] user/Subtype /Link /A ¡¡ /S /URI /URI (https://errorcorrectionzoo.org/c/rotor_gkp) ¿¿
​
.

The canonical set of states for a planar rotor are its position “states”
\{|\phi\rangle_{\text{rot}}~,~\phi\in[-\pi,\pi)\}
, whose label corresponds to the position of a particle on a ring (as opposed to a particle on a line in the case of a bosonic mode).
Since the position degree of freedom is compact, the resulting momentum degree of freedom is disrete, with corresponding momentum states
|\ell\rangle_{\text{rot}}
labeled by an integer
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
The Fourier series maps between the two sets of states, e.g.,
|\phi\rangle_{\text{rot}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{i\phi\ell}|\ell\rangle_{\text{rot}}
.

In an analogue of linear GKP states [Fig.
1
(a)], rotor GKP codewords consist of alternating combs of rotor position “states” [Fig.
1
(b)].
Such codes protect against sufficiently small rotations of the rotor’s angular position as well as kicks in its angular momentum.

Poor man’s versions of planar-rotor states can be embedded into a single bosonic mode.
The oscllator phase “states” [see Fig.
2
(a)],

|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{i\phi n}|n\rangle\quad\quad\text{(phase states)}~,

(19)

are bosonic analogues of angular position states.
Fock states
|n\rangle
play the role of a “dual” angular-momentum basis, with the generator
{\hat{n}}
playing the role of a pseudo-momentum operator.
The significant obstruction to a full-fledged rotor analogy is that the momentum
is never negative.
Because of this, phase “states” are not only non-normalizable, but they are also not quite orthogonal.
This makes for more subtle calculations, but does not qualitatively obscure the rotor interpretation.

Number-phase codes are bosonic analogues of rotor GKP codes.
Codewords can be expressed as equal superpositions of phase states at alternating “combs” of
N
angular positions; for
N=3
[see Fig.
1
(c)],

\displaystyle|0_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\phi=0\right\rangle+\left|\phi={\textstyle\frac{2\pi}{3}}\right\rangle+\left|\phi={\textstyle\frac{4\pi}{3}}\right\rangle\right)/\sqrt{3}

(20a)

\displaystyle|1_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\phi={\textstyle\frac{\pi}{3}}\right\rangle+\left|\phi=\pi\right\rangle+\left|\phi={\textstyle\frac{5\pi}{3}}\right\rangle\right)/\sqrt{3}~.

(20b)

Such states protect against angular rotations
e^{i\varphi{\hat{n}}}
that shift the position-like phase states as
e^{i\varphi{\hat{n}}}|\phi\rangle=|\phi+\varphi\rangle
. Per an argument identical to the linear GKP case, rotations with
|\varphi|<\frac{\pi}{N}

should be
detectable, and those with
|\varphi|<\frac{\pi}{2N}

should be
correctable. However, since phase states are not quite orthogonal, a finer analysis in the limit of small rotations has to be performed (see below).

An alternative basis for the number-phase codespace at
N=3
,

\displaystyle|+_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(|0_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle+|1_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle\right)/\sqrt{2}\propto\sum_{n\geq 0}|6n\rangle

(21a)

\displaystyle|-_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(|0_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle-|1_{\text{num-ph}}\rangle\right)/\sqrt{2}\propto\sum_{n\geq 0}|6n+3\rangle~,

(21b)

reveals codewords consisting of alternating combs of Fock states. Generally, the logical-plus is supported on
|0\rangle,|2N\rangle,\cdots
, while logical-minus is supported on
|N\rangle,|3N\rangle,\cdots
. This yields protection against noise that changes the occupation number: kicks with magnitude at most
N-1
are detectable, and kicks with magnitude at most
\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor
are correctable.

While a natural candidate for a momentum-kick operator is the lowering operator
{\hat{a}}
, a closer analogue is its “regularized” version,
\hat{E}_{-1}={\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\hat{n}}+1}}}{\hat{a}}
, cleansed of unwieldy square-root factors.
Powers of this
phase operator

\hat{E}_{-1}

[
50
]
and its adjoint (
a.k.a.
phasors
[
51
]
),

\hat{E}_{\ell}=\begin{cases}~{\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}}|n+\ell\rangle\langle n|&\ell\geq 0\\
~{\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}}|n\rangle\langle n+|\ell||=\hat{E}_{|\ell|}^{{\dagger}}&\ell<0\end{cases}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}~,

(22)

form the closest bosonic analogue of rotor momentum kicks.

For
\{\hat{E}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
to be bona-fide momentum kicks, they would need to raise or lower the angular momentum by
\ell
and to be diagonal in the position basis.
These operators fail gracefully to accomplish both tasks due to the lack of negative Fock states.
They act as momentum kicks by raising or lowering Fock states, but sometimes annihilate Fock states since there is nowhere to go below
n=0
.
They are “almost” diagonal in the position basis, admitting phase states as either right or left eigenstates.
For example,
\hat{E}_{\ell}^{\dagger}
for
\ell>0
admit phase states (
19
) as right eigenstates, analogous to
{\hat{a}}^{\ell}
, which is also not unitary but admits coherent states as right eigenstates.
Despite such setbacks, rotations and Fock-state momentum kicks retain the proper angular commutation relation,

e^{i\phi{\hat{n}}}\hat{E}_{\ell}=e^{i\phi\ell}\hat{E}_{\ell}e^{i\phi{\hat{n}}}\quad\quad\text{(number-phase commutator)}~,

(23)

complementary to the linear position-momentum commutator (
10
).

Linear combinations of Fock-space rotations and the above “momentum kicks” can express any trace-class operator
[
47
, Sec. VI.A]
[
52
]
in a
polar-like
decomposition, akin to the
cartesian-like
decomposition in terms of position and momentum shifts. However, due to non-orthogonality of the phase states, this set fails to be an orthonormal operator basis.
These features can be summarized by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}{\textstyle\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}}\langle m|\hat{E}_{\ell}e^{i\phi{\hat{n}}}|n\rangle\langle n^{\prime}|e^{-i\phi{\hat{n}}}\hat{E}_{\ell}^{\dagger}|m^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{(completeness)}\,,

(24a)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(e^{-i\phi{\hat{n}}}\hat{E}_{\ell}^{\dagger}\hat{E}_{\ell^{\prime}}e^{i\phi^{\prime}{\hat{n}}}\right)=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\langle\phi|\phi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\neq\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta\left(\phi-\phi^{\prime}\right)\quad\quad\text{(non-orthogonality)}~.

(24b)

Rotations and kicks partially preserve the stabilizer formalism. The number-phase codespace is a
+1
-eigenvalue eigen-subspace of the rotation
e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}{\hat{n}}}
.
Since phase states
|\phi\rangle
are right eigenstates of
\hat{E}_{\ell}^{\dagger}
with eigenvalue
e^{i\phi\ell}
, number-phase codes form the joint
+1

right
eigenspace of the rotation and the kick
\hat{E}_{2N}^{\dagger}
. These “stabilizer generators” generate

\textsf{S}_{\text{num-phase}}=\{e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}{\hat{n}}a}\hat{E}^{\dagger}_{2Nb}~|~a\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N},\,b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}~,

(25)

that fails to be a group since the kick is not unitary (
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the natural numbers).
Nevertheless, the generators correspond to measurable number-phase error syndromes.

Eigenvalues of the
\frac{2\pi}{N}
-rotation are
N
th roots of unity,
e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}j}
for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{N}
, whose
j
th eigenspace is supported by Fock states with labels
n=j
modulo
N
. The
modular number
measurement extracting the syndrome value

can be performed using an ancilla with
N

Using a superconducting qubit ancilla, the occupation-number parity measurement for
N=2
has become a standard subroutine in microwave cavity modes
[
53

54
]
.

Measuring in the right-eigenstate (i.e., phase-state) “basis” of the phase operator
\hat{E}_{1}^{\dagger}
corresponds to measuring the phase induced on a state by a rotation
[
55
,
24

47

is the phase

\frac{2\pi}{2N}

5.2
Cat codes
[
56

Number-phase codewords (
20

12

for
coherent states
[see Fig.

|\phi\rangle\propto\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{i\phi n}|n\rangle\quad\to\quad e^{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}e^{i\phi n}|n\rangle\quad\quad\text{for large real $\alpha$}~,

(26)

Cat codewords are superpositions of
N
coherent states
distributed equidistantly on a circle in the mode’s phase space.
Since coherent states can be thought of as regularized phase states, the Fock-state spacing of Eq. (
21

e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}{\hat{n}}}
remains a “stabilizer”, and measuring the modular occupation number detects such shifts.

Coherent states, like phase states, are not orthogonal, and a proper way to determine protection against rotation errors
is to examine small rotation angles. Expanding
e^{i\phi{\hat{n}}}\approx I+i\phi{\hat{n}}
and inspecting the Knill-Laflamme error-correction conditions
[
17
]

dephasing errors

{\hat{n}}
up to corrections exponential in
-\alpha^{2}

P_{\text{cat}}{\hat{n}}P_{\text{cat}}=\alpha^{2}P_{\text{cat}}+O(\alpha^{2}e^{-\alpha^{2}})Z_{\text{cat}}~.

(27)

Above,
P_{\text{cat}}
is the projection onto the cat-state codespace, and
Z_{\text{cat}}=|+_{\text{cat}}\rangle\langle+_{\text{cat}}|-|-_{\text{cat}}\rangle\langle-_{\text{cat}}|
.
A similar equation holds for powers
{\hat{n}}^{\ell}
. The
Z
-type uncorrectable term stems from unequal expectation values of
{\hat{n}}
in the codewords,
\langle+_{\text{cat}}|{\hat{n}}|+_{\text{cat}}\rangle\neq\langle-_{\text{cat}}|{\hat{n}}|-_{\text{cat}}\rangle

Besides exponential suppression of the
Z
-type undetectable-error term in the large-
\alpha

\alpha
at which the term is identically zero (e.g.,
[
57

).
Since
{\hat{n}}
does not cause transitions between Fock states,

Cat codewords are not right eigenstates of the number-phase “stabilizer”
\hat{E}_{-2N}

The modular-phase measurement is thus not compatible with cat codes because it collapses cat codewords into number-phase states.
However, the partial phase information extracted from such a measurement can be combined with freshly initialized logical states
[

to yield a teleportation-based correction scheme
[

.

Cat codewords
are
right eigenstates of a power of the lowering operator,

5.3
Binomial codes
[

Binomial codes are the third class of single-mode codes that, along with number-phase and cat codes, share the same mechanism for
protecting against Fock-space shifts; these three code families make up the bosonic rotation codes
[
47
]

​

The difference with binomial codes is that they are supported on only a finite set of Fock states. They are also more “digital”
in their protection against dephasing errors, detecting powers of
{\hat{n}}

exactly
up to a maximum power
D
.

The simplest binomial code can be obtained from the four-qubit code (
3
), whose codewords are
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0000\rangle+|1111\rangle\right)
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0011\rangle+|1100\rangle\right)
. Adding up each of the digits in the codewords’ binary strings,
|0000\rangle\to|0\rangle
,
\{|1100\rangle,|0011\rangle\}\to|2\rangle
, and
|1111\rangle\to|4\rangle
, yields the single-mode “0-2-4” binomial code with codewords

|0_{\text{bin}}\rangle=(|0\rangle+|4\rangle)/\sqrt{2}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad|1_{\text{bin}}\rangle=|2\rangle~.

(28)

Due to the Fock-state spacing of
N=2
between the codewords [see Fig.
2
(c)], the above code detects a single Fock-state shift, either up or down (but not both). Because the average occupation number
\langle{\hat{n}}\rangle
of both codewords is two, the code also detects a dephasing error
{\hat{n}}
. Together, these imply
correction
of the error set
\{I,{\hat{a}}\}
, i.e., correction of a single
loss error
. This is analogous to the four-qubit code’s ability to correct a single decay event
\sigma_{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(X-iY)
on any physical qubit. A similar argument yields protection against a single
excitation

{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}
.

General binomial codes, with codewords

|0/1_{\text{bin}}\rangle={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2^{D/2}}}}\sum_{m\sim\text{even}/\text{odd}}\sqrt{\binom{D+1}{m}}|Nm\rangle~,

(29)

are defined by non-negative integer parameters
N,D\geq 0
; for the “
0,2,4
” code (
28
),
N=2
and
D=1
. The parameter
N
sets the Fock-state spacing of the code, relying on modular number measurements to detect photon losses and excitations.
The parameter
D
sets the coefficient in front of each Fock state to ensure that both codewords have equal moments,
\langle 0_{\text{bin}}|{\hat{n}}^{\ell}|0_{\text{bin}}\rangle=\langle 1_{\text{bin}}|{\hat{n}}^{\ell}|1_{\text{bin}}\rangle
, for
\ell\leq D
; this allows for detection of up to
D
-order dephasing errors. Correcting dephasing and loss errors simultaneously remains difficult, but some progress has been made by relating binomial codes to spin-coherent states that are staggered in Fock space
[
57
]
.

5.4
Chebyshev codes
[

]

A related “digital” code called the Chebyshev code protects against the same dephasing errors
\{{\hat{n}}^{\ell}~|~\ell\leq D\}
as the binomial codes, but optimizes the leading-order logical-
Z

\langle 0_{\text{cheb}}|{\hat{n}}^{D+1}|0_{\text{cheb}}\rangle-\langle 1_{\text{cheb}}|{\hat{n}}^{D+1}|1_{\text{cheb}}\rangle

for sensing applications.
This error also yields a logical gate
\exp(it{\hat{n}}^{D+1})
on the codespace.
In the context of local quantum sensing, such a gate can be interpreted as an external signal whose corresponding time
t
is a parameter that needs to be “sensed” or measured.
Chebyshev codes achieve near-optimal sensitivity to such a signal while maintaining error-correcting properties.

6
The fight against physical noise

Figure 3:
Three types of common noise channels in bosonic systems —
(a)
loss,
(b)
dephasing, and
(c)
displacement noise. The effect of each channel on a coherent state (blue circle) in
(x,p)
-phase space is sketched using red arrows. Kraus operators are shown below, with
\chi
the noise strength,
\ell\geq 0
, and
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})
.

Quantum information stored in a mode incurs one or more of the following main types of physical noise. Leakage of particles out of a mode (
a.k.a.
fiber attenuation) is represented by the
loss
channel (
a.k.a.
bosonic amplitude damping), dominant in electromagnetic modes
[
4
]
. Cavity
dephasing
, whose errors are fluctuations of the oscillator phase and are functions of
{\hat{n}}
, is a prominent noise source in mechanical modes. We have already seen
displacement
noise (
a.k.a.
quantum additive Gaussian white noise or AGWN),
present in an optical fiber experiencing attenuation and amplification
[
81
]
.
Qualitatively, loss causes radial contraction, dephasing causes angular diffusion, and displacement noise causes uniform diffusion in phase space (see Fig.
3
).

Any noise channel
{\cal{N}}
can be expressed in Kraus form,
{\cal{N}}(\rho)=\sum_{\ell}N_{\ell}\rho N_{\ell}^{\dagger}
, with Kraus operators
N_{\ell}
.
Loss Kraus operators, shown in Fig.
3
(a), are proportional the
\ell
th power of the lowering operator
{\hat{a}}
,
multiplied by a cooling operator (
18
) that drives the population of the system to the vacuum Fock state
[
4
]
. Operators corresponding to
\ell
th-order dephasing noise, shown in Fig.
3
(b), are proportional to the
\ell
th power of
{\hat{n}}

[
82
]
. Expanding exponentials of
{\hat{n}}
in the loss and dephasing operators makes contact with loss and dephasing errors,
{\hat{a}}^{\ell}
and
{\hat{n}}^{\ell}
, respectively.

The strength of the noise is quantified by
\chi\in[0,\infty)
.
Typically,
\chi=\kappa t
, where
t
is how long the channel is “turned on”, and
\kappa
is a noise rate. For optical fibers,
\chi
is the fiber’s length divided by its attenuation length.
For displacement noise,
\kappa t=\sigma
is the displacement distribution’s standard deviation, discussed in the context of GKP-stabilizer codes in Sec.
4.3
.

Suppose we want to use a particular mode to store or communicate information; which code do we pick? The answer is, as always, a function of (A) the system’s noise profile, (B) the code’s error-correcting properties, and (C) the engineer’s level of control.
Leaving (C) to the more knowledgeable, I summarize work on (A-B).

GKP stabilizer codes, designed with displacement noise in mind, are suitable for AGWN channels. Multimode GKP codes can be used to achieve the communication capacity of the quantum AGWN channel, up to a constant offset
[
39
,
83
,
84
,
85
]
.

Fock-state codes, protecting against shifts in the occupation number as well as dephasing errors, should be a suitable counter to loss and dephasing. Indeed, large-
\alpha
cat codes and related binomial and number-phase codes substantially outperform GKP codes against pure dephasing noise
[
9
,
86
]
(see also Ref.
[
87
]
). A similar performance comparison shows
the opposite
is true for pure loss
[
57
]
.
This is because loss Kraus operators, when expanded in terms of displacements, are mostly supported by small, and therefore correctable, position and momentum shifts
[
57
, Eq. (7.12)]
.
There is a more transparent effect on the channel level: since loss combined with
amplification
— a channel proportional to the adjoint of loss — yields displacement noise, amplification followed by GKP stabilizer-based recovery is a well-performing decoder.
In fact, GKP codes
also
achieve the capacity of pure loss and related channels
[
83
,
84
,
85
]
.

Combinations of non-negligible loss and dephasing may prove too noisy for error correction to be useful: optimization favors
codewords supported on the first few Fock states
[
52
,
86
]
, close to the trivial Fock-state encoding
\{|n=0\rangle,|n=1\rangle\}
. Similar results are seen when loss is combined with coherent errors due to the Kerr Hamiltonian
{\hat{n}}^{2}

[
57
]
.

7
Use cases of bosonic encodings

Why should we consider bosonic platforms (and their associated codes) over others?
For one thing, such platforms are sometimes the
only
ones available for a given task.
For example, if one wants to communicate quantum information over long distances, one would find it very difficult to avoid dealing with either optical-fiber or free-space quantum links.
But besides such ubiquity,
bosonic platforms also yield unique advantages.
I conclude by highlighting several ongoing developments.

7.1
Hardware efficiency

Use of bosonic platforms for a particular task may be
hardware efficient
, which means, loosely speaking, that a bosonic platform requires fewer physical resources to perform a task than other platforms.

A small bosonic code can maintain the same degree of protection as a few-qubit code, but require fewer quantum states.
For example,
the “0-2-4” binomial code (
28
) is more hardware efficient than the four-qubit code
[
4
, Table 4]
.
Both codes protect against the same type of noise — amplitude damping (qubit and bosonic, respectively) — to first order in the damping parameter
\chi
(against the operators
\{I,\sigma_{-}
} and
\{I,{\hat{a}}\}
, respectively). However, the binomial code requires only
5
Fock states and
3
error spaces, compared to the four-qubit code’s
2^{4}=16
states and
2^{3}=8
error spaces.

For a more drastic comparison, families of cat codes are more hardware efficient than families of repetition codes.
Both codes offer excellent protection against one type of noise: cat-code schemes suppress dephasing noise exponentially with
\alpha^{2}
[see Eq. (
27
)], while
n
-qubit repetition-code schemes suppress bit-flip noise exponentially with
n
. However, repetition codes require an exponentially increasing
n
-qubit space, while cat codes fit into a single mode for any
\alpha
.
The hardware-efficient stability offered by cat codes has been linked to several favorable properties of phases of matter associated with the repetition-code Hamiltonian — the classical 2D Ising model
[
88
,
89
]
— and continues to be actively studied
[
90
,
91
]
.

7.2
Boosting performance with analog decoding

A multi-qubit code can be concatenated with a single-mode bosonic code to yield a better performing multi-mode code, at the expense of the extra bosonic overhead. In the typical example of such a concatenation, each physical qubit of an outer
[[n,k,d]]
qubit stabilizer code
[
18
,
19
]

attr/Border[0 0 0] user/Subtype /Link /A ¡¡ /S /URI /URI (https://errorcorrectionzoo.org/c/qubit_stabilizer) ¿¿
​
is further encoded in an inner single-mode bosonic code.

To take advantage of the concatenation, the qubit code’s decoder, which uses only
digital
information from physical qubit measurements, can be modified to take in the
soft
or
analog
information
[
92
]
of the often continuous syndromes of the bosonic code.
The resulting performance boost is evident even for small codes, e.g., a certain decoder for a three-mode repetition code concatenated with GKP qubits can accommodate some two-mode errors
[
93
]
.

An important feature of infinite families of qubit codes is the existence of a
threshold
— the largest strength of a given physical noise channel for which the inaccuracy of correction
\epsilon
[see Eq. (
1
)] decreases exponentially with the distance
d
of the code family.
Threshold values enable engineers to predict the maximum noisiness that can be tolerated with error correction.
A multi-qubit
\{[[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]]\}_{i\geq 1}
code family admitting a threshold can be concatenated with a bosonic code, such as the two-component cat
[
94
,
95
,
96
]
or square-lattice GKP

[
97
,
98
,
99
,
100
,
101
]
code, to increase the threshold. Such concatenation schemes are compatible not only with gate-based computation, but also with alternative measurement-based approaches tailored to optical platforms

[
102
,
103
,
104
,
105
]
.

7.3
Continuous transversal gates

A
transversal gate
acting on an
n
-qubit code is a gate that can be implemented as a tensor product of unitary operations, each acting on a subset of qubits whose size is independent of
n
.
Such gates are particularly useful for fault tolerance because any single-qubit error occurring before a transversal gate can only spread to the few-qubit subset that interacts with that qubit via the gate.

The Eastin-Knill theorem
[
106
]
states that a finite-dimensional multi-qubit code detecting few-qubit errors cannot have a continuous-parameter set of transversal gates. The argument is that any purported transversal gates would be exponentials of a sum of few-qubit terms corresponding to each qubit subset, and such terms would yield identity when projected into the codespace since the code is assumed to detect few-qubit noise.

Due to infinite dimensionality, bosonic modes offer one way to circumvent the Eastin-Knill theorem. For example, the four-mode code (
7
) admits an infinite family of logical displacements
\{e^{-iq({\hat{p}}_{1}+{\hat{p}}_{2})}\}_{q\in{\mathbb{R}}}
. More involved codes exist for oscillators, rotors, and other spaces
[
107
]
, allowing for arbitrarily large transversal-gate families. Such gates also hold for “cooled” normalizable versions of exact codewords, albeit the error correction becomes approximate (see Sec.
4
).

7.4
Noise-bias preservation

The interplay of gates with noise is important for fault tolerance.
Generic gates often change the
type
of noise upon permutation, e.g., converting bit to phase noise.

Bias-preserving gates

[
108
]
are gates that
do not
change the type of noise upon such a permutation. Such gates are useful for maintaining fault tolerance in systems whose noise profile is biased toward either bit or phase noise.

It is impossible to obtain a
cnot
gate using a Hamiltonian-based bias-preserving rotation for qubit codes
[
108
]
.
For any unitary
e^{is\hat{H}}
generated by a two-qubit Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
such that
e^{is\hat{H}}|_{s=1}={\textsc{cnot}}
, there is an intermediate
s
at which the unitary converts phase-flip to bit-flip noise
[
109
, Appx. A]
.

Due to infinite dimensionality, oscillator codes can circumvent this no-go theorem, implementing continuous Hamiltonian-based
cnot
gates that maintain noise bias throughout.
A bias-preserving logical
cnot
gate exists for the “two-component” cat code
[
94
,
109
]
. It is based on the logical Pauli gate
e^{is\pi{\hat{n}}}|_{s=1}
that permutes the code’s antipodal coherent-state codewords,
|\alpha\rangle
and
\left|-\alpha\right\rangle
, while maintaining the phase-space distance between them for all
s
(see notes
[
110
]
).
The logical
cnot
gate for GKP qubits, achieved by a Hamiltonian-based symplectic transformation, similarly preserves noise bias in either the position or momentum quadratures
[
38
, Eq. (104)]
.

Due to the ability to preserve bias, cat codes concatenated with particular stabilizer codes yield substantial increases in performance for systems whose noise is substantially biased
[
111
,
109
,
95
,
96
]
.
The bias in cat codes also makes for a good ancilla, yielding fault-tolerant syndrome measurement schemes
[
112
]
.

7.5
GKP states as non-Gaussian resources

Multi-qubit codes typically admit a set of “easy” gates called the Clifford-group gates, and a “hard” non-Clifford element is required to achieve universal computation.
The same relationship holds between GKP codes and
Gaussian operations
— “easy” oscillator unitaries consisting of Fock-space rotations, displacements, and two other elements called beam splitters and squeezers
[
14
,
15
]
.
Gaussian operations allow for only logical Clifford operations on encoded GKP qubits,
so a non-Gaussian resource is required for universal quantum computation.

In related fashion, almost all bosonic codewords are outside of the set of
Gaussian states
, which can be made by applying Gaussian operations on the vacuum Fock state or the thermal state
e^{-\beta{\hat{n}}}
. The important exception is analog stabilizer codewords [e.g., the four-mode codewords (
7
)], which are generated using only Gaussian elements acting on position states, which in turn can be thought of as infinitely squeezed vacuum states.

Magic states
are non-Gaussian resource states that are used to perform non-Clifford GKP logical gates via gate teleportation.
Magic states are typically generated in a separate and often cumbersome distillation process requiring other non-Gaussian resources.
This is not the case for GKP codes, whose magic states can be distilled directly by applying GKP error correction to Gaussian initial states, circumventing the need for non-Gaussian elements other than GKP error correction
[
113
]
.

GKP-stabilizer codes can be thought of as analog stabilizer codes “upgraded” with a non-Gaussian resource, allowing for error correction that is not possible with purely Gaussian resources.
Analog stabilizer codewords can be initialized by applying a Gaussian circuit to tensor-product states consisting of the desired quantum information
|\psi\rangle
in the first
k
physical mode and position states
|x=0\rangle
in the remaining modes.
GKP-stabilizer codes can be initialized in the same way, but with the substitution of canonical GKP states (
13
) for the initial position states.

The GKP resource is what allows GKP-stabilizer codes to protect an entire mode against Gaussian shifts, as there is a no-go theorem
[
114
,
97
]
showing that analog stabilizer codes cannot protect against such shifts (see also
[
115
,
116
]
).
However, there are no such restrictions for analog stabilizer codes against
non
-Gaussian noise
[
117
]
.
Moreover, the existence of a threshold for protecting logical modes against Gaussian displacement noise using GKP-stabilizer codes is an open question and at the very least requires ideal (i.e., non-normalizable) codewords
[
118
]
.

7.6
Other curiosities

Bosonic modes may be helpful in providing simple (i.e., commuting-projector) models for exotic
topological
phases of quantum matter, such as fractional quantum Hall systems.
This task is shown to be impossible for systems consisting of finite-dimensional qudits
[
119
]
, and there has been some progress in developing models on planar rotors

[
120
,
121
]
.

Bosonic modes may be useful for quantum simulation of, unsurprisingly, other bosonic modes.
Boson sampling
[
122
]
, a fundamentally difficult problem in terms of its computational complexity, is naturally baked into bosonic degrees of freedom.
Decoding of multimode GKP codes is interestingly tied to gauge theory
[
97
]
.
Recent work has shown how to extract parameters relevant to chemical processes involving molecular vibrational modes
[
123
]
, although work on quantum-inspired classical algorithms has fired back at the claim that a quantum device makes the extraction more efficient
[
124
]
.

Infinite dimensions also bear fruit for quantum control. Amplifying the time of evolution under the harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian is possible using a sequence of single-mode squeezing operations, but similar amplifications are not possible in finite dimensions
[
125
]
.

8
Summary and discussion

The primary application of bosonic codes is the storage and processing quantum information in phononic and photonic devices.
Due to their formally infinite-dimensional space, bosonic systems yield quantum protocols that are forbidden in the discrete-variable (DV) setting.
I review bosonic memories and highlight several such advantageous protocols.

The primary goal of error correction is to yield a probability of corruption of the encoded information (
p_{\text{logical}}
) that is smaller than a reference probability of unencoded information corruption (
p_{\text{physical}}
). Defining the
coding gain
of a given error correcting protocol as the ratio of the two probabilities, an error-correcting protocol is useful if it yields a gain larger than one.
A more ambitious goal in the quantum setting is made possible by the notion of an error-correcting threshold (
p_{\text{threshold}}
), below which it should be possible to construct error-correcting protocols with
arbitrarily large
gain by scaling up the physical degrees of freedom (see Sec.
7.2
). The equation below summarizes the three important regimes of the gain, which are independent of the quantitative formulations of the three probabilities:

\text{gain}=\frac{p_{\text{physical}}}{p_{\text{logical}}}\,\,\begin{cases}~=1&\text{break-even QEC}\\
~>1&\text{beyond break-even}\\
~\to\infty&\text{below threshold,}~p_{\text{physical}}<p_{\text{threshold}}\end{cases}~.

(31)

Bosonic codes have initially outpaced DV codes in terms of approaching and surpassing unity gain. The first demonstration of break-even correction was achieved using cat codes several years ago
[
53
]
, and the first substantial greater-that-unity gain has been achieved recently using GKP codes
[
126
]
. Qubit platforms are quickly catching up: a recent trapped-ion qubit implementation has made progress toward going beyond break-even for two-qubit gates
[
127
]
.

Few-qubit and few-mode bosonic codes are limited by a ceiling in the gain because the only (known) way of achieving arbitrarily large gain is to be below the threshold physical error rate of an infinite family of codes whose Hilbert-space dimension increases exponentially and which admits a threshold.
In accordance with that notion, large-scale industrial experiments instead focus on observing the proper scaling of the gain with system size
[
128
,
129
]
, betting that a higher-than-unity gain would eventually follow once such scaling is established.

The future of quantum error correction likely lies in the synthesis of both bosonic and DV error correction paradigms, resulting in multiple layers of protection tailored to specific devices and applications. Bosonic and DV strategies are not competing technologies, but two pieces of a puzzle that should be integrated to maximize progress.
