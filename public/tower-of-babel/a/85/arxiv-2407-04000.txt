Introduction

0.1
The smoothing problem for algebraic cycles

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
0
(which the reader may choose to be the field
{\mathbb{C}}
of complex numbers).
Let
X
be a smooth projective algebraic variety of dimension
n
over
k
.
A central theme in algebraic geometry is the study of algebraic cycles on
X
, that is of the
collection of all the algebraic subvarieties of
X
. We will always denote by
d
the dimension of the subvarieties we consider.

The main players of this line of research are the Chow groups
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)=\mathrm{CH}^{n-d}(X)

of
X
. Their elements are
cycles
:
linear combinations with integral coefficients of (closed, integral)
d
-dimensional algebraic subvarieties of
X
, considered up to
rational equivalence
. Here, two cycles are said to be rationally equivalent if both belong to the same algebraic family of cycles on
X
parametrized by the projective line
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{k}
.
We refer to
[
7
]
for precise definitions, as well as for a study of functorial properties of Chow groups.

One should think of these groups as algebro-geometric analogues of singular homology groups, where both the generators and the relations are constrained to have an algebraic (as opposed to topological) origin. No conditions are imposed on the algebraic subvarieties of
X
that generate its Chow groups; in particular, they may carry arbitrary singularities. The next question, first asked
by Borel and Haefliger
in
[
4
, §5.17]
, is therefore of interest.

Question 0.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective algebraic variety over
k
. Are the Chow groups
\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)
of
X
generated by classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
?

In other words, is it possible to smooth algebraic cycles up to rational equivalence? To be precise, the original question of Borel and Haefliger was slightly weaker. They considered, over the field
k={\mathbb{C}}
, the coarser
homological equivalence
relation, where two algebraic cycles are identified if they have the same image by the cycle class map
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)\to H_{2d}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
.

One of the goals of this introduction is to review the state of the art concerning Question
0.1
(see §
0.4
for positive results and §
0.5
for negative results).

0.2
Smoothing algebraic cycles in the Whitney range

Let us state right away the main theorem presented in this survey, which has been recently proved by Kollár and Voisin
[
16
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 0.2
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective algebraic variety of dimension
n
over
k
. If
d<\frac{n}{2}
, then
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
is generated by classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
.

At the time when Borel and Haefliger asked Question
0.1
, resolution of singularities was not available. A positive answer to this question could therefore have been used as a substitute to resolution of singularities in the study of algebraic cycles. This original motivation has nowadays disappeared. On the contrary, the proof of Theorem
0.2
does use Hironaka’s theorem
[
12
]
on resolution of singularities (exactly once, in the proof of Theorem
0.7
given at the end of §
0.3
).

It is verified in
[
15
, Theorem 39]
that the proof of Theorem
0.2
can be extended to base fields
k
of characteristic
p\geq n-d
, at least if
k
is assumed to be infinite and perfect.
In this argument, resolution of singularities is replaced with Gabber’s improvement
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
of de Jong’s alteration theorem.

Let us explain the significance and the importance of the restrictive hypothesis
d<\frac{n}{2}
in the statement of Theorem
0.2
.
It plays the exact same role as in Whitney’s weak embedding theorem
[
21
]
in differential topology (according to which any compact
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
manifold of dimension
d
embeds in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if
d<\frac{n}{2}
).
The heuristic in our algebraic situation is that a morphism
f:Y\to X
of smooth projective varieties over
k
which is sufficiently generic is an embedding if
\dim(Y)<\frac{n}{2}
.
Its image
f(Y)
is then a smooth subvariety of
X
. This suggests that, under the hypothesis
d<\frac{n}{2}
, the variety
X
should contain many smooth subvarieties constructed by general projection arguments, therefore increasing the likelihood that
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
is generated by classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
. Of course, the difficulty is to enforce this genericity condition on
f
by algebraic means.

A Whitney-type hypothesis had already appeared in two earlier works on Question
0.1
. On the one hand, Hironaka had given a proof of Theorem
0.2
under the additional assumption that
d\leq 3
(see
[
12
, Theorem p. 50]
). On the other hand, I had constructed counterexamples to Question
0.1
on the boundary
d=\frac{n}{2}
of the Whitney range, for infinitely many values of
d
(see
[
1
, Theorem 0.3]
), thereby showing that the hypothesis
d<\frac{n}{2}
in Theorem
0.2
is optimal (for these values of
d
). These works will be discussed in more details in §
0.4.1
and §
0.5.2
respectively.

0.3
Flat pushforwards of complete intersections

The approach of Kollár and Voisin relies chiefly on the following definition. Recall that a morphism
f:Y\to X
of connected smooth projective varieties over
k
is
flat
if and only if it is equidimensional, i.e. if all its fibers have dimension
{\dim(Y)-\dim(X)}
.

Definition 0.3
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
. Define
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)_{\mathrm{KV}}
to be the subgroup of
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
generated by cycles of the form
f_{*}(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{c})
for some flat morphism
{f:Y\to X}
of smooth projective varieties over
k
and some codimension
1
classes
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{c}\in\mathrm{CH}^{1}(Y)
, where
c+d=\dim(Y)
.

In short, the subgroup
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)_{\mathrm{KV}}\subset\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
is generated by those cycles that may be written as flat pushforwards of intersections of divisor classes. Theorem
0.2
results from the combination of the following two theorems.

Theorem 0.4

(
[
16
, Proposition 1.5]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
over
k
. If
{d<\frac{n}{2}}
, then any element of
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)_{\mathrm{KV}}
may be written as a linear combination with integral coefficients of classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
.

Theorem 0.5

(
[
16
, Theorem 1.6]
)
.

If
X
is a smooth projective variety over
k
, then

{\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)_{\mathrm{KV}}=\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)}.

Theorem
0.4
is the part of the proof of Theorem
0.2
where the smoothing actually takes place. It is also the part in which the Whitney-type hypothesis
d<\frac{n}{2}
is used. It is directly inspired by Hironaka’s work
[
12
]
on the topic. We discuss it more in §
0.4.1
, and present its proof in Section
1
.

In contrast, Theorem
0.5
holds with no restriction on the dimension of the cycles. It constitutes a fundamentally new structural result on the Chow groups of arbitrary smooth projective varieties. Its formulation and its proof are the main achievements of the article
[
16
]
.
It is not known whether Theorem
0.5
would still hold if one required the morphisms
f
in Definition
0.3
to be smooth instead of only flat (see
[
16
, Question 1.11]
).
The flexibility gained by allowing flat morphisms that are possibly not smooth is used exactly once in the proof of Theorem
0.5
(in the proof of Proposition
2.1
).

As noted in
[
15
]
, the proof of Theorem
0.5
given in
[
16
]
yields a slightly stronger result, valid at the level of subvarieties (as opposed to Chow groups).
To state it, we rely on the next geometric definition. Recall that a subvariety of codimension
c
in a smooth projective variety
X
over
k
is said to be a
complete intersection
if it can be written as the intersection of
c
hypersurfaces in
X
.

Definition 0.6
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
. An integral subvariety
Z\subset X
is said to be a smooth complete intersection image (or
sci-image
for short) if there exist a flat morphism
f:Y\to X
of smooth projective varieties over
k
and a smooth complete intersection
V\subset Y
such that
f(V)=Z
and
f|_{V}:V\to Z
is birational.

Theorem 0.7

(
[
15
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
. Any integral subvariety
Z\subset X
is an sci-image.

Theorem
0.5
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
0.7
. In turn, the proof of Theorem
0.7
has two steps. In the first step, one studies functorial properties of sci-images. The culmination of this analysis is the next proposition. In its statement, one makes use of the following terminology: a subvariety of codimension
c
in a smooth projective variety
X
over
k
is said to be a
complete bundle section
(or
cbs
for short) if it can be written as the zero locus of a section of a vector bundle of rank
c
on
X
.

Proposition 0.8

(
[
16
, Proposition 3.11]
,
[
15
, Lemma 16]
)
.

Let
\pi:X^{\prime}\to X
be the blow-up of a smooth cbs in a smooth projective variety over
k
. Let
Z^{\prime}\subset X^{\prime}
be an sci-image such that
\pi|_{Z^{\prime}}:Z^{\prime}\to Z:=\pi(Z^{\prime})
is birational. Then
Z\subset X
is an sci-image.

To conclude the proof of Theorem
0.7
, one would like to show that any integral subvariety of
X
can be turned into a smooth complete intersection after repeatedly blowing up smooth cbs. This is not known to hold (see e.g.
[
16
, Question 4.1]
) and the second step is a weaker statement, which is sufficient for our purposes.

Proposition 0.9

(
[
16
, Theorem 1.9]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
over
k
. Let
Z\subset X
be a smooth subvariety of dimension
<\frac{n}{4}
. Then there exist a composition
\pi^{+}:X^{+}\to X
of blow-ups of smooth cbs and a smooth complete intersection
V\subset X^{+}
such that
\pi^{+}(V)=Z
and
\pi^{+}|_{V}:V\to Z
is birational.

Proposition
0.9
is the technical heart of
[
16
]
.
Propositions
0.8
and
0.9
will be proved in Sections
2
and
3
respectively.
Together, they imply Theorem
0.7
, as we now explain.

Proof of Theorem
0.7
.

Let
{\widetilde{Z}}\to Z
be a resolution of singularities (see
[
12
]
). Embed
{\widetilde{Z}}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}
for some
N\geq 0
.
View
{\widetilde{Z}}
as a subvariety of
Y:=X\times{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}
using the natural diagonal embedding. After possibly increasing
N
, one can apply Proposition
0.9
to the subvariety
\widetilde{Z}
of
Y
. In this way, we obtain a composition
\pi^{+}:Y^{+}\to Y
of blow-ups of smooth cbs and a smooth complete intersection
V\subset Y^{+}
such that
\pi^{+}(V)=\widetilde{Z}
and
\pi^{+}|_{V}:V\to\widetilde{Z}
is birational. Using Proposition
0.8
, we deduce that the subvariety
{\widetilde{Z}}\subset Y
is an sci-image. It follows that
Z\subset X
is also an sci-image, by flatness of the first projection morphism
Y=X\times{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}\to X
.
∎

0.4
Smoothing techniques

We now focus on the various techniques that have been used to smooth algebraic cycles up to rational equivalence, both in the early positive results about Question
0.1
and in the recent work of Kollár and Voisin. These techniques fall into two categories, depending on whether the algebraic cycles under consideration have small dimension, or small codimension. In the first case, they are best thought of homologically and presented as pushforwards (see §
0.4.1
). In the second case they are best thought of cohomologically and presented as pullbacks (see §
0.4.2
).
Combining the results presented in §
0.4.1
and §
0.4.2
shows that Question
0.1
has a positive answer when
n\leq 5
.

0.4.1
Cycles of small dimension

The first progress on Question
0.1
was due to Hironaka
[
12
, Theorem p. 50]
who answered it positively when
d<\frac{n}{2}
(the Whitney-type condition discussed in §
0.2
) and
d\leq 3
. Let us sketch his proof.

Let
Z\subset X
be an integral subvariety of dimension
d
, and let
{\widetilde{Z}}\to Z
be a resolution of singularities.
Choosing an embedding of
{\widetilde{Z}}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}
for some
N\geq 0
allows one to view
{\widetilde{Z}}
as a subvariety of
Y:=X\times{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}
. (That Hironaka’s argument inspired the proof of Theorem
0.7
given at the end of §
0.3
should be obvious.) At that point, the idea is to find a cycle
{\widetilde{Z}}^{\prime}
in
Y
, which is rationally equivalent to
{\widetilde{Z}}
, and whose components are smooth and in general position. The image
Z^{\prime}
of
{\widetilde{Z}}^{\prime}
in
X
is then rationally equivalent to
Z
, and has smooth components precisely because of the Whitney-type hypothesis.

To construct
{\widetilde{Z}}^{\prime}
from
{\widetilde{Z}}
, one needs some kind of moving lemma. To this effect, Hironaka devises a
moving by linkage
technique. Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a sufficiently ample line bundle on
Y
, and let
c
be the codimension of
{\widetilde{Z}}
in
Y
. Let
D_{1},\dots,D_{c}
be general elements of the linear system
|{\mathcal{L}}|
, and let
E_{1},\dots,E_{c}
be general elements of
|{\mathcal{L}}|
containing
{\widetilde{Z}}
. One can write
E_{1}\cap\dots\cap E_{c}={\widetilde{Z}}\cup W
for some subvariety
W\subset Y
of codimension
c
. One says that the subvarieties
{\widetilde{Z}}
and
W
of
Y
are
linked
. Our choices ensure that the cycle
{\widetilde{Z}}^{\prime}:=(D_{1}\cap\dots\cap D_{c})-W
is rationally equivalent to
{\widetilde{Z}}
. The subvariety
W
is in general singular in codimension
4
, so it is smooth when
d\leq 3
. To complete the proof, repeat the linkage procedure a few times to enforce the general position hypothesis.

For cycles of dimension
\geq 4
, Hironaka’s method fails because of the singularities that linkage inevitably creates. However, combined with additional arguments to control these singularities, these ideas may still be useful (see
[
1
, Theorems 0.4 and 0.6]
for applications to real algebraic cycles).

The perspective of Kollár and Voisin is very different. They do not attempt to control the singularities that appear. Neither do they try to develop another moving technique applicable to general cycles. Instead, they prove the structural result that all cycles come by flat pushforward from complete intersections (Theorem
0.5
) and they use that complete intersections can be moved around very easily. A small price to pay is that they need to apply the Whitney-type general projection argument to a morphism
f:Y\to X
that is only assumed to be flat, and not smooth as is the first projection
X\times{\mathbb{P}}^{N}_{k}\to X
in Hironaka’s proof.
This is the content of Theorem
0.4
.

0.4.2
Cycles of small codimension

The case
d=n
of Question
0.1
is trivial (as the
n
-dimensional subvarieties of
X
are its connected components, which are smooth).

It is also true that Question
0.1
has a positive answer for
d=n-1
. Indeed, if
D\subset X
is an irreducible divisor, one can write
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(D)={\mathcal{L}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{2}^{-1}
, where the
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
are very ample line bundles on
X
. General divisors
D_{i}
in the linear systems
|{\mathcal{L}}_{i}|
are then smooth by the Bertini theorem. To conclude, write
[D]=[D_{1}]-[D_{2}]
in
\mathrm{CH}_{n-1}(X)=\mathrm{Pic}(X)
.

The article
[
14
]
of Kleiman can be thought of as extending this argument to higher-codimensional cycles. This led him, in particular, to give positive answers to Question
0.1
when
n=4
and
d=2
, and when
n=5
and
d=3
.

For the sake of completeness, let us briefly explain Kleiman’s argument. The Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem without denominators
shows that

[Z]=(-1)^{c-1}(c-1)!\,c_{c}({\mathcal{O}}_{Z})

(0.10)

in
\mathrm{CH}^{c}(X)
, for any subvariety
Z\subset X
of codimension
c
(see e.g.
[
11
]
).
Applying (
0.10
) with
{c=2}
, and using a resolution of the coherent sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{Z}
by locally free sheaves, one can see that
\mathrm{CH}^{2}(X)
is generated (integrally) by second Chern classes of vector bundles.
Tensoring these vector bundles with high enough powers of a fixed ample line bundle, one may even assume that these vector bundles are globally generated.

In other words, the Chow group
\mathrm{CH}^{2}(X)
is generated by classes of the form
f^{*}c_{2}({\mathcal{E}})
, where
f:X\to G
is a morphism to a Grassmannian, and
{\mathcal{E}}
is the tautological vector bundle on
G
. The class
c_{2}({\mathcal{E}})\in\mathrm{CH}^{2}(G)
is represented by a Schubert cell
S\subset G
, whose singularities are in codimension
4
. Assuming that
f
is in general position with respect to
S
, which can be ensured by postcomposing
f
with a generic automorphism of
G
, the class
f^{*}c_{2}({\mathcal{E}})
is represented by the subvariety
f^{-1}(S)
of
X
, which is smooth if
d\leq 3
.

We also note, following
[
16
, Lemma 2.5]
, that this circle of ideas yields an easy proof of Theorem
0.5
with rational coefficients. Indeed, if
\alpha\in\mathrm{CH}^{c}(X)
, then (
0.10
) shows that
(c-1)!\,\alpha
belongs to the subring of
\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)
generated by Chern classes of vector bundles, and hence to the subring of
\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)
generated by Segre classes of vector bundles (since the total Chern class
c({\mathcal{E}})
and the total Segre class
s({\mathcal{E}})
of a vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}
on
X
are related by the identity
c({\mathcal{E}})\cdot s({\mathcal{E}})=1
). It is however obvious, from their definition given in
[
7
, §3.1]
, that (products of) Segre classes belong to
\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)_{\mathrm{KV}}
.

0.5
Nonsmoothable algebraic cycles

At last, we discuss the existing counterexamples to Question
0.1
.

0.5.1
A topological variant

Even before the work of Borel and Haefliger, an analogue of Question
0.1
in differential topology was considered by Thom in his influential article
[
20
]
.

Question 0.11
.

Let
M
be an oriented compact
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
manifold. Are the homology groups

H_{*}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
generated by fundamental classes of oriented
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
submanifolds of
M
?

One of Thom’s results is that a class in
H_{d}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
can be realized as the fundamental class of an oriented
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
submanifold of dimension
d
of
M
if and only if its Poincaré dual cohomology class is induced by pullback from some universal cohomology class: the Thom class of the universal oriented real vector bundle of rank
{\dim(M)-d}
(see
[
20
, Théorème II.5]
).
The vanishing of certain cohomological operations applied to this Thom class then give rise to restrictions on the fundamental classes of
d
-dimensional oriented compact
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
submanifolds of
M
. Thom used these restrictions in
[
20
, Théorème III.9]
to settle Question
0.11
in the negative in general.

Let us point out that one can use Thom’s criterion to verify that Question
0.11
has a positive answer in the Whitney range (that is, for classes in
H_{d}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
with
d<\frac{\dim(M)}{2})
.
The counterpart of Theorem
0.2
in differential topology is therefore true.

0.5.2
Counterexamples to Question
0.1

By applying Thom’s ideas to complex algebraic varieties, Hartshorne, Rees and Thomas
[
10
, Theorem 1]
found the first counterexample to Question
0.1
. Their precise result is as follows. Let
X:=G(3,6)
be the Grassmannian parametrizing the
3
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{C}}^{6}
, and let
\mathcal{E}
be the tautological rank
3
vector bundle on
X
. Then
c_{2}(\mathcal{E})\in\mathrm{CH}^{2}(X)
is not a linear combination with integral coefficients of classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
.

Since then, many other counterexamples to Question
0.1
have been discovered, by combining topological obstructions as above with Hodge-theoretic arguments (exploiting that the image of the cycle class map
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)\to H_{2d}(X,{\mathbb{Z}})
consists of Hodge classes).
Debarre
[
5
]
constructed counterexamples on abelian varieties, and these examples were expanded by him and myself in
[
2
]
. In particular, we show in
[
2
, Corollary 1.3]
that Question
0.1
may have a negative answer for codimension
2
cycles on abelian varieties of dimension
6
(as we explained in §
0.4
, this is the lowest possible dimension for the ambient variety
X
).

In addition, I have found counterexamples to Question
0.1
with
d=\frac{n}{2}
, for infinitely many values of
d
(the smallest one being
d=6
). For all these values of
d
, Theorem
0.2
is therefore optimal.
In the statement of this result, we let
\alpha(m)
denote the number of ones in the binary expansion of the integer
m
.

Theorem 0.12

(
[
1
, Theorem 0.3]
)
.

Let
d\geq 0
be such that
\alpha(d+1)\geq 3
. Then there exists a smooth projective variety
X
of dimension
2d
over
{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
is not generated by classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
.

The proof of Theorem
0.12
is discussed in Section
4
. For now, let us only explain in what way its principle relies on the Whitney heuristic discussed in §
0.2
.
Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
2d
over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Fix
\beta\in\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
.
As a first step towards proving Theorem
0.12
, one might want to check that, if
\beta
is the class of an integral subvariety
Z\subset X
, then
Z
cannot be smooth. To do so, imagine first that
Z
has been constructed by some kind of generic projection argument. In the range of dimensions we consider, the subvariety
Z
would then have finitely many singular points at which it self-intersects in
X
. To show that
Z
cannot be smooth, compute this number of singularities using a double point formula, and prove
(only relying on the knowledge of
\beta
and not of
Z
itself) that this number must be nonzero.
If
Z
has more complicated singularities, the double point formula still computes a virtual number of double points, allowing one to proceed in a similar way.

For what values of
d
(besides those found in Theorem
0.12
) is Theorem
0.2
optimal? A full answer to this question is not known.
The first case that is open is
d=3
.

Question 0.13
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
6
over
k
. Is the Chow group
\mathrm{CH}_{3}(X)
generated by classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
?

0.5.3
Mildly singular cycles

It is also interesting to consider weakenings of Question
0.1
, in which one allows subvarieties with controlled singularities.

Maggesi and Vezzosi put forward in
[
18
]
the case of subvarieties with locally complete intersection (lci) singularities. (The article
[
18
]
works with Chow groups with rational coefficients, but we defer until §
0.6
the consideration of rational Chow groups). It turns out that this variant of Question
0.1
still has a negative answer. In fact, in the counterexamples given by Theorem
0.12
, the group
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
is not even generated by classes of lci subvarieties of
X
(as we will prove in Section
4
, see Theorem
4.1
).

In contrast, we do not know the answer to the next question.

Question 0.14
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
. Are the Chow groups
\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)
of
X
generated by classes of normal subvarieties of
X
?

In this direction, the arguments of Kleiman described in §
0.6
imply that
\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)_{{\mathbb{Q}}}
is generated by classes of normal subvarieties, whereas the Kollár–Voisin method shows that
\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)
is generated by classes of subvarieties whose normalization is smooth.

0.6
Algebraic cycles with rational coefficients

We conclude this introduction by discussing a fascinating
smoothing problem for algebraic cycles: the case of Chow groups with rational coefficients.

Question 0.15
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety over
k
. Are the rational Chow groups
\mathrm{CH}_{*}(X)_{{\mathbb{Q}}}
of
X
generated by classes of smooth subvarieties of
X
?

Let us first indicate that the analogue of Question
0.15
in differential topology does have a positive answer. Namely, Thom has proved that, for any oriented compact
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
manifold
M
, the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space
H_{*}(M,{\mathbb{Q}})
is generated by fundamental classes of oriented
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
submanifolds of
M
(see
[
20
, Théorème II.29]
). This contrasts sharply with Thom’s negative answer to Question
0.11
.
In
[
3
, Theorem 0.4]
, Voisin and I obtained a symplectic extension of Thom’s result. More precisely, we showed that if
M
is a symplectic compact
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
manifold, then the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space
H_{2d}(M,{\mathbb{Q}})
is generated by fundamental classes of
d
-dimensional symplectic
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
submanifolds of
M
.
We believe that this result dashes any hope of finding topological obstructions to Question
0.15
.

Question
0.15
was first investigated by Kleiman
[
14
]
, using the techniques presented in §
0.4.2
. Thanks to the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem without denominators (see (
0.10
)), he reduces first to the case of Chern classes, and then to the case of cycles that are in the image of
f^{*}
for some algebraic morphism
f:X\to G
to a Grassmannian. He further notes that
f
may be chosen to be in generic position (with respect to any subvariety of
G
) by composing it with a generic automorphism of
G
.

On the one hand, this line of reasoning shows that it suffices to solve Question
0.15
when
X
is a Grassmannian. This case is by no means easy: it is not even known if the second Chern class of the tautological bundle on
G(3,6)
(the Hartshorne–Rees–Thomas counterexample to the original question of Borel and Haefliger, see §
0.5.2
) is a linear combination with rational coefficients of classes of smooth subvarieties of
G(3,6)
.

On the other hand, combining the above arguments with a study of the singularities of Schubert cells, Kleiman answered positively Question
0.15
for cycles of dimension
{d<\frac{n}{2}+1}
(see
[
14
, Theorem 5.8]
). In particular, the rationalized version of Theorem
0.2
has been known for a long time, with slightly better bounds on the dimension of the cycles. It is striking that Kleiman’s result has not been improved in the past fifty years.

In
[
3
, Theorem 0.3]
, Voisin and I have put forward a curious connection between Question
0.15
and Hartshorne’s famous conjecture on complete intersections in projective space (according to which any smooth subvariety of codimension
2
in
\mathbb{P}^{N}_{{\mathbb{C}}}
with
N\gg 0
is a complete intersection; Hartshorne suggests the bound
N\geq 7
but the assertion might hold for
N\geq 5
). Namely, we prove that if Hartshorne’s conjecture holds for some
N\geq 5
, then Question
0.15
has a negative answer for codimension
2
cycles on the Grassmannian
G(N,2N)
. We leave it to the readers to decide for themselves if they believe more in Hartshorne’s conjecture or in a positive answer to Question
0.15
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let

be a flat morphism of smooth projective varieties over
k

{\mathcal{L}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{L}}_{c}
be very ample line bundles on

.
If

,
then

is an embedding of smooth projective varieties over
k
.

To deduce Theorem
0.4
from Proposition
1.1
, note that any class in
\mathrm{CH}^{1}(Y)=\mathrm{Pic}(Y)
is a difference of very ample line bundles. As a consequence, under the hypotheses of Theorem
0.4
, any element of
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)_{\mathrm{KV}}
can be represented by a linear combination with integral coefficients of smooth subvarieties of

constructed as in Proposition

.

Proof.

Let us first prove that

is injective. The set of pairs
(y_{1},y_{2})\in Y^{2}
such that

and
f(y_{1})=f(y_{2})
is an algebraic variety of dimension
{2\dim(Y)-\dim(X)}
. Once such a pair
(y_{1},y_{2})
is fixed, the codimension in
|{\mathcal{L}}_{1}|\times\dots\times|{\mathcal{L}}_{c}|
of the set of
(D_{1},\dots,D_{c})
such that

is equal to
2c
. The codimension, in this parameter space, of the locus where

is not injective is therefore

\geq 2c-(2\dim(Y)-\dim(X))>0
.

The proof that
f|_{Z}
is immersive is similar, and based on the stratification

Y_{r}:=\{y\in Y\mid\mathrm{rk}(df_{y})\leq r\}

of

by the rank of the differential of

. Fix

. The set of pairs

with

and

such that

is an algebraic variety of dimension

\leq\dim(Y_{r})+(\dim(Y)-r)\leq\dim\big(\overline{f(Y_{r})}\big)+(\dim(Y)-\dim(X))+(\dim(Y)-r).

The rank at any smooth point of

of the differential of

is
\leq r
.

we were considering has dimension
\leq 2\dim(Y)-\dim(X)
.

Once such a pair
(y,v)
is fixed, the codimension in

of the set of
(D_{1},\dots,D_{c})
such that

is equal to

.
We deduce that the codimension, in this parameter space, of the locus where

is not immersive at some point of

is
{\geq 2c-(2\dim(Y)-\dim(X))>0}

∎

of smooth projective varieties over

and a smooth complete intersection

such that

and
{f|_{V}:V\to b(Z^{\prime})}
is birational.
Since

is flat and since

is birational, the flat morphism
\pi\circ f:Y\to X
and the smooth complete intersection
{V\subset Y}

certify that the subvariety
\pi\circ b(Z^{\prime})=\nu(Z^{\prime})
of

is an sci-image.
∎

3
Turning smooth subvarieties into smooth complete intersections

The goal of this section is to prove the next proposition (see

Proposition 3.1
.

Proof of Proposition

.

Let the morphism

In §
3.1
, we describe a strategy to prove Proposition
3.1
. This outline
will turn out to be insufficient, and we will explain in §
3.2
and §
3.3
how this strategy must be modified to give rise to a rigorous proof. The hypothesis that
Z
has dimension
<\frac{n}{4}
will only play a role in §
3.2
and §
3.3
. The proofs of a few lemmas are postponed to §
3.4
.

3.1
A simplified sketch of the proof of Proposition
3.1

Step 1

(Induction on
d
)
.

We argue by induction on
d
. The case when
{d=0}
is obvious: it is not even necessary to blow up
X
. From now on, fix
d\geq 1
.

During the whole proof we will repeatedly blow up
X
along smooth cbs of dimension
{<d}
and replace
Z
with its strict transform in these blow-ups. To avoid creating singularities on
Z
, we always assume that the connected components of the blown-up cbs are either included in
Z
or disjoint from
Z
.
The arguments given in this paragraph are not strong enough to ensure that. This is one of the reasons why they will need to be modified as described in §
3.2
and §
3.3
.

Step 2

(Induction on
e
)
.

Fix
d\leq e\leq n
. We claim that, after maybe having repeatedly blown up
X
along smooth cbs and replaced
Z
by its strict transform, there exists a smooth cbs
Y_{e}\subset X
of dimension
e
that contains
Z
. This claim is proved by decreasing induction on
e
. The case
e=n
is obvious: just take
Y_{n}=X
. The case
e=d
is exactly the conclusion of Proposition
3.1
that we are trying to reach.

Step 3

(A hyperplane section)
.

To perform the induction step of the proof of the claim, we assume that
Z\subset Y_{e+1}\subset X
has been constructed for some
d\leq e<n
.
Choose a sufficiently ample line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}
on
X
. Let
s\in H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{L}})
be general section vanishing on
Z
and set
H:=\{s=0\}
. Then
H\subset X
is a hypersurface containing
Z
. Define
Y_{e}:=Y_{e+1}\cap H
.

The proof is not yet complete because the subvariety
Y_{e}
may have singularities.
Local computations (more precisely: Lemma
3.4
applied to the variety
Y_{e+1}
) allow one to describe them precisely.
Let
\Sigma\subset Y_{e}
denote the singular locus of
Y_{e}
. Note that
\Sigma\subset Z
by the Bertini theorem.
If
e\geq 2d
, then
\Sigma=\varnothing
and the proof is finished.
Otherwise, the variety
\Sigma
is smooth of dimension
2d-e-1
(in particular, of dimension
<d
)
and
Y_{e}
has ordinary quadratic singularities along
\Sigma
.

Step 4

(Making use of the induction hypothesis)
.

This description of the singularities of
Y_{e}
shows that, to render
Y_{e}
smooth, it would suffice to blow up
\Sigma
. However, we are only allowed to blow up smooth cbs and not arbitrary smooth subvarieties.

To overcome this obstacle, we apply the induction hypothesis on
d
. After repeatedly blowing up smooth cbs of dimension
<\dim(\Sigma)
(whose components we assume, for the purpose of this simplified sketch of proof, to be either included in
\Sigma
or disjoint from
Y_{e+1}
)
and replacing all the varieties in sight with their strict transforms in these blow-ups, we may assume that
\Sigma\subset X
is a union of connected components of a smooth cbs.

It is easy to check that, after this sequence of blow-ups, the equality
Y_{e}=Y_{e+1}\cap H
still holds.
One further verifies that the subvariety
Y_{e+1}\subset X
is still a cbs (see Lemma
3.6
(i)) and hence that so is
Y_{e}=Y_{e+1}\cap H
, and that
Y_{e}
still has ordinary quadratic singularities along
\Sigma
(see Lemma
3.5
).

Step 5

(Blowing up the singular locus)
.

Let
C\subset X
be the smooth cbs of which
\Sigma
is a union of connected components. Again, we assume for the purpose of this simplified sketch of proof that
C
only intersects
Y_{e}
along
\Sigma
.
Blowing up
C
in
X
and replacing
Y_{e}
with its strict transform in this blow-up then completes the proof. Indeed, our choice of blow-up center implies that the subvariety
Y_{e}\subset X
is now a smooth cbs (see Lemma
3.6
(ii)).

3.2
Controlling the blown-up loci

At several points in the sketch of proof described in §
3.1
, we took for granted that our blow-up centers
were in good position with respect to various other subvarieties
V_{i}\subset X
of
importance in the proof. It is for this reason, and for this reason alone, that this sketch is incomplete.

Following
[
16
, Definition 4.3]
, we say that a subvariety
C\subset X
has
full intersection
with another subvariety
V\subset X
if all the connected components of
C
are either included in
V
or disjoint from
V
. The key to fixing the proof will be to ensure that our blow-up centers have full intersection with the relevant subvarieties
V_{i}\subset X
.
One could hope that if the choices made in §
3.1
are generic, then the blown up loci satisfy the required full intersection conditions.
Unfortunately, as noted in
[
16
, Remark 4.7]
, this is not clear.

The solution of Kollár and Voisin to this difficulty is radical. Instead of making generic choices and hoping for the best, they make particular choices. To be precise, they constrain the blown up loci inside auxiliary smooth subvarieties of
X
that are as disjoint as possible from the subvarieties
V_{i}
of
X
that are relevant to the proof. This forces the blow-up centers to have full intersection with the
V_{i}
.

This strategy can only be made to work if the
V_{i}
and the auxiliary varieties all have dimension
<\frac{n}{2}
(they will then be as disjoint as possible from each other for purely dimensional reasons). Since the auxiliary varieties will be constructed as general complete intersections containing
\Sigma
(which is smooth of dimension
<d
), their smoothness is ensured if
d<\frac{n}{4}
(by Lemma
3.4
). This
explains why this hypothesis appears the statement of Proposition
3.1
.

3.3
The inductive statements

In practice, the resulting enhancement of Proposition
3.1
is stated as the combination of two twin propositions (see
[
16
, Properties 4.6 and 4.8]
), which are proved simultaneously by induction on
d
. Their statements is complicated by the necessity of keeping track of all the auxiliary varieties that have been introduced at various stages of the induction process.

As before, we let
X
be a smooth projective variety of dimension
n
over
k
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
Z\subset Y\subset X
and
Z\subset W_{j}\subset X
be subvarieties. Suppose that
Z
is smooth of dimension
d<\frac{n}{4}
, that the
W_{j}
are smooth of dimension
<\frac{n}{2}
and that
Y
is a smooth cbs of dimension
<\frac{n}{2}
.

Then there exist a composition
\pi:X^{\prime}\to X
of blow-ups of smooth cbs of dimension
{<d}
that have full intersection with the strict transforms of
Z
,
of
Y
and of the
W_{j}
, and a smooth cbs
C\subset X^{\prime}
, such that
Z^{\prime}\subset C\subset Y^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
is a union of connected components of
C
(where
Z^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
denote the strict transforms of
Z
and
Y
in
X^{\prime}
).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
Z\subset Y\subset X
and
Z\subset W_{j}\subset X
be subvarieties. Suppose that
Z
is smooth of dimension
d<\frac{n}{4}
, that the
W_{j}
are smooth of dimension
<\frac{n}{2}
and that
Y
is a smooth cbs of dimension
<\frac{n}{2}
. Suppose moreover that
Y
has dimension
>d
.

Then there exist a composition
\pi:X^{\prime}\to X
of blow-ups of smooth cbs of dimension
{<d}
that have full intersection with the strict transforms of
Z
,
of
Y
and of the
W_{j}
, and a smooth cbs
C\subset X^{\prime}
, such that
Z^{\prime}\subset C\subset Y^{\prime}
and
\dim(C)=\dim(Y^{\prime})-1
(where
Z^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
denote the strict transforms of
Z
and
Y
in
X^{\prime}
).

Proposition
3.1
follows from Proposition
3.2
applied with no

and with
Y
a general complete intersection containing
Z
(which may be chosen smooth thanks to Lemma
3.4
).

Proposition
3.2
(for a fixed

) is an immediate consequence of Proposition
3.3
(for the same

).
To see it, apply Proposition
3.3
and replace
X
with
X^{\prime}
, the

and
Z
with their strict transforms in

, and
Y
with
C
. In this way the dimension of

drops. Repeat this process until

and the conclusion of Proposition
3.2
is reached.

We finally explain how Proposition
3.3
(for a fixed
d
) can be deduced from Proposition
3.2
(for lower values of
d
). The argument is (a rigorous variant of) the induction step on the parameter
e
that was presented in Steps
3
,
4
and
5
of §
3.1
. Let
H\subset X
be a general hypersurface containing
Z
and set
C:=Y\cap H
. The singular locus
\Sigma
of
C
is a smooth subvariety of dimension
<d
of
Z
(see Lemma
3.4
).
Our plan is to turn
\Sigma
into a union of components of a smooth cbs and then to blow up this smooth cbs.

As we explained in §
3.2
, the difficulty is to control the location in
X
of the other components of this smooth cbs. To this effect, we introduce an auxiliary variety: a general complete intersection
\overline{Y}\subset X
containing
\Sigma
, chosen smooth of dimension
<\frac{n}{2}
(this is possible by Lemma
3.4
).
A simple dimension count shows that
\overline{Y}\cap Y=\Sigma
and
\overline{Y}\cap W_{j}=\Sigma
scheme-theoretically.

We now apply Proposition
3.2
to
\Sigma\subset\overline{Y}\subset X
, with
Z

and
Y
added to the collection of the

. In this manner, after repeatedly blowing up smooth cbs (with full intersection with the relevant subvarieties) and replacing all the varieties in sight by their strict transforms, we can arrange that
\Sigma
is a union of connected components of a smooth cbs

which is included in
\overline{Y}
.

The equality

still holds after this sequence of blow-ups. The subvariety

is still a cbs (by Lemma

. Moreover, the variety

still has ordinary quadratic singularities along

).

Local computations show that the scheme-theoretic equalities

and
{\overline{Y}\cap W_{j}=\Sigma}
are preserved during the blow-up process.
The cbs

can therefore only intersect

,
Y
and the
W_{j}
along
\Sigma
and hence has full intersection with them. It is therefore legitimate to blow it up. The strict transform of

in this blow-up is a smooth cbs (by Lemma

(ii)) which has the properties required in the statement of Proposition
3.3
.

3.4.1
Ordinary quadratic singularities

The next two lemmas are standard, and we omit their proofs. The first is a Bertini-type theorem appearing in
[

[

[

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Z\subset X
be a smooth subvariety of dimension

be a sufficiently ample line bundle on

be a general hypersurface containing

If

.

Let

. Let
Z\subset\Sigma
be a smooth subvariety of dimension
{<\dim(\Sigma)}
. Let

of

3.4.2
Blowups of complete bundle sections

The following lemma (for which see
[
16
, Lemmas 4.4 and 4.5]
) is crucial to the mechanism of proof of Proposition
3.1
. In particular, Lemma
3.6
(ii) is the reason why it is necessary to blow up smooth cbs and not only smooth complete intersections in Proposition
3.1
(and hence in Proposition
0.9
).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
C\subset X
be a cbs. Let
\pi:X^{\prime}\to X
be the blow-up of a smooth subvariety
Z\subset C
of dimension
<\dim(C)
.
Let
C^{\prime}\subset X^{\prime}
be the strict transform of
C
.

(i) If
C
is smooth, then
C^{\prime}\subset X^{\prime}
is a smooth cbs.

(ii) If
Z
is the singular locus of
C
and
C
has ordinary quadratic singularities along
Z
, then
C^{\prime}\subset X^{\prime}
is a smooth cbs.

Proof.

In both cases, it is clear that
C^{\prime}
is smooth and we must check that it is a cbs.

Let
c
be the codimension of
C
in
X
. Write
C=\{s=0\}
, where
{s\in H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{E}})}
for some vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}
of rank
c
on
X
. Let
E
be the exceptional divisor of
\pi
.
As the section
\pi^{*}s\in H^{0}(X^{\prime},\pi^{*}{\mathcal{E}})
vanishes on
E
, it gives rise to a section
s^{\prime}\in H^{0}(X^{\prime},\pi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}(-E))
.

In case (i), a local computation shows that
C^{\prime}=\{s^{\prime}=0\}
, which concludes the proof.

In case (ii), the inclusion
C^{\prime}\subset\{s^{\prime}=0\}
is strict and one must modify
\pi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}(-E)
to reach the desired conclusion.
Differentiating the section
s
along
Z
gives rise to a morphism
{ds:N_{Z/X}\to{\mathcal{E}}|_{Z}}
of vector bundles of rank
c
on
Z
.
Since
C
has hypersurface singularities along
Z
, the kernel of the linear map
(ds)_{x}
has dimension
1
for all
x\in Z
. We deduce that cokernel of
(ds)_{x}
also has dimension
1
for all
x\in Z
, and hence that the cokernel of
ds
is a line bundle
{\mathcal{L}}
on
Z
. Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the kernel of the composition

\pi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}(-E)\to(\pi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}(-E))|_{E}=(\pi|_{E})^{*}{\mathcal{E}}|_{Z}(-E)\to(\pi|_{E})^{*}{\mathcal{L}}(-E)

of the restriction map and the quotient map. Computations based on the shape of local equations for subvarieties with ordinary quadratic singularities then show that
{\mathcal{F}}
is a vector bundle on
X^{\prime}
, that the image of
s^{\prime}\in H^{0}(X^{\prime},\pi^{*}{\mathcal{E}}(-E))
in
H^{0}(X^{\prime},(\pi|_{E})^{*}{\mathcal{L}}(-E))
vanishes (so
s^{\prime}
lifts to a section

), and that
C^{\prime}=\{t=0\}
.
∎

4
Half-dimensional cycles that are not smoothable

In this last section, we illustrate the sharpness of the Kollár–Voisin technique by explaining the ideas of the proof of the following theorem. Recall that
\alpha(m)
denotes the number of ones in the binary expansion of the integer
m
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d\geq 0
be such that
\alpha(d+1)\geq 3
. Then there exists a smooth projective variety
X
of dimension
2d
over
{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
is not generated by classes of lci subvarieties
of pure dimension
d
in
X
.

The varieties used in the proof of Theorem
4.1
are provided by the next proposition (see
[
1
, Proposition 4.13]
).

Proposition 4.2
.

For all
d\geq 1
, there exist a connected smooth projective variety
X
of dimension
2d
over
{\mathbb{C}}
and a class
\beta\in\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
such that:

1.

If
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in H^{2d}(X({\mathbb{C}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
are Hodge, then
\mathrm{deg}(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime})
is even.

2.

One has
\mathrm{deg}(\beta^{2})\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.

3.

All the higher Chern classes of
X
are torsion, that is
c(T_{X})=1
in
\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proof.

Let
(A,\lambda)
be a very general principally polarized abelian variety of dimension
d
over
{\mathbb{C}}
and let
\tau\in A({\mathbb{C}})
be a
2
-torsion point. Let
{\mathbb{Z}}/4
act on
A\times A
by means of the order
4
automorphism
\phi:(x,y)\mapsto(y+\tau,x)
. Set
X:=(A\times A)/({\mathbb{Z}}/4)
and let
p:A\times A\to X
be the quotient morphism. In addition choose
\beta\in\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
to be the class of
p(A\times\{0\})
in
\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X)
.

Since
{\mathbb{Z}}/4
acts freely on
A\times A
, the quotient morphism
p
is étale. As the tangent bundle of
A
is trivial, one computes that
p^{*}c(T_{X})=c(p^{*}T_{X})=c(T_{A\times A})=1
. Apply
p_{*}
to that identity to show (iii).

The class
p^{*}\beta
is represented by the
{\mathbb{Z}}/4
-orbit of
A\times\{0\}
. A direct computation therefore shows that
\deg(p^{*}\beta^{2})=8.
Since
\deg(p)=4
, one gets
\deg(\beta^{2})=2
, proving (ii).

It remains to prove (i). The detailed argument being quite lengthy, we only explain its principle and refer to
[
1
, §4.2]
for more details. Since the principally polarized abelian variety
(A,\lambda)
is very general, a Mumford–Tate group argument (see
[
1
, Lemma 4.6]
) shows that the algebra
\mathrm{Hdg}^{2*}(A({\mathbb{C}})\times A({\mathbb{C}}),{\mathbb{Q}})
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
-Hodge classes on
A\times A
is generated by the principal polarizations of both factors and by the class of the Poincaré bundle (where we identify the second factor to the dual of
A
by means of
\lambda
). Using this piece of information, one can compute a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis of the group
\Xi:=\mathrm{Hdg}^{2d}(A({\mathbb{C}})\times A({\mathbb{C}}),{\mathbb{Z}})^{{\mathbb{Z}}/2}
of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-Hodge classes of degree
2d
on
A\times A
that are fixed by the involution exchanging the two factors (see
[
1
, Lemma 4.7 (ii)]
). A direct computation on these basis elements shows that
\deg(\delta\cdot\delta^{\prime})\equiv 0\pmod{2}
for all
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Xi
(see
[
1
, Lemma 4.9]
). Now, if
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
are elements of
\mathrm{Hdg}^{2d}(X({\mathbb{C}}),{\mathbb{Z}})
, one verifies that the elements
p^{*}\gamma
and
p^{*}\gamma^{\prime}
of
\Xi
are divisible by
2
in
\Xi
(see
[
1
, Lemma 4.7 (iii)]
). Applying the above congruence to
\delta:=\frac{p^{*}\gamma}{2}
and
\delta^{\prime}:=\frac{p^{*}\gamma^{\prime}}{2}
yields
\deg(p^{*}\gamma\cdot p^{*}\gamma^{\prime})\equiv 0\pmod{8}
.
Using that
\deg(p)=4
, we get
\deg(\gamma\cdot\gamma^{\prime})\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
X
and
\beta
be as in Proposition
4.2

\beta=\sum_{i}n_{i}[Z_{i}]\textrm{\, in\, }\mathrm{CH}_{d}(X),

(4.3)

The double point formula
[

shows that

\deg([Z_{i}]^{2})=\deg(c_{d}(N_{Z_{i}/X}))
.
As
c(TX)=1
in
\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Q}}
by Proposition

(iii), we see that

c(N_{Z_{i}/X})=c(T_{Z_{i}})^{-1}=s(T_{Z_{i}})

in
\mathrm{CH}^{*}(X)\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{Q}}
, where

denotes the total Segre class of

.
A topological computation due to Rees and Thomas (see
[

{\alpha(d+1)\geq 3}

in (

.

On the other hand, the numbers
\deg([Z_{i}]\cdot[Z_{j}])

(i) because the image of the cycle class map consists of Hodge classes. So the term
2\sum_{i<j}n_{i}n_{j}\deg([Z_{i}]\cdot[Z_{j}])
