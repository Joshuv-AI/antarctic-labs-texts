Abstract
. In this paper we deal with a scale of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces H 2 ( n ) H^{(n)}_{2} , n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 , which are linear subspaces of the classical Hilbertian Hardy space on the right-hand half-plane ℂ + \mathbb{C}^{+} . They are obtained as ranges of the Laplace transform in extended versions of the Paley-Wiener theorem which involve absolutely continuous functions of higher degree. An explicit integral formula is given for the reproducing kernel K z , n K_{z,n} of H 2 ( n ) H^{(n)}_{2} , from which we can find the estimate ∥ K z , n ∥ ∼ | z | − 1 / 2 \|K_{z,n}\|\sim|z|^{-1/2} for z ∈ ℂ + z\in\mathbb{C}^{+} . Then composition operators C φ : H 2 ( n ) → H 2 ( n ) C_{\varphi}:H_{2}^{(n)}\to H_{2}^{(n)} , C φ ​ f = f ∘ φ C_{\varphi}f=f\circ\varphi , on these spaces are discussed, giving some necessary and some sufficient conditions for analytic maps φ : ℂ + → ℂ + \varphi:\mathbb{C}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{+} to induce bounded composition operators.

1.
Introduction

As is well known,
C_{0}
-semigroups provide solutions to abstract Cauchy equations,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u^{\prime}(t)=Au(t),&t\geq 0,\\
u(0)=x,&x\in D(A),\end{array}\right.

whenever such equations are well-posed

[
2
]
. There still are other important Cauchy equations for which

C_{0}
-semigroups do not work and that must be treated using a variety of methods or theories.
Among such theories, one finds in the literature those concerning
integrated semigroups or distribution semigroups.
At the intersection of these two settings, one has
the space
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}
of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{+}:=(0,\infty)
defined as the completion of the test function space

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
in the norm

\|f\|_{1,(n)}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f^{(n)}(t)|t^{n}\ dt<\infty,\quad f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}).

We have the continuous inclusions

{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(m)}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(0)}=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})

for every
m\geq n
. Moreover, every
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}
is a convolution Banach algebra for
the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1,(n)}
.
Being primarily introduced in
[
3
]
to approach Cauchy problems (with a different notation), the space

{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}

has not been studied as a Banach algebra in itself until quite recently; as a sample,
see
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and references therein. The character space of the Banach algebra

{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}
is the closure, in the usual (Euclidean) topology, of the right-hand half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re z>0\}
and the associated Gelfand mapping coincides with the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
. In
[
9
]
, the range of the mapping
\mathcal{L}
restricted on

{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}
is studied, showing that it is dense in a certain algebra of holomorphic functions with continuous derivatives up to
i\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

The space
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}
is a particular case of spaces
{\mathcal{T}}_{p}^{(n)}
defined for

1\leq p<\infty
by
g\in{\mathcal{T}}_{p}^{(n)}
if and only if
g
is a measurable function on
\mathbb{R}^{+}

which belongs to the closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
in the norm

\|f\|_{(n),p}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|f^{(n)}(t)t^{n}|^{p}\ dt\right)^{1/p}<\infty,\quad f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}).

As in the case of
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{(n)}
and in analogy with spaces
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
,
1\leq p\leq 2
, it makes sense to study the range of the
Laplace transform acting on

{\mathcal{T}}_{p}^{(n)}
. Naturally, the first case to be considered is
p=2
. In
[
21
]
,
the range
\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{T}}_{2}^{(n)})
is characterized as the (Hilbert) space
H_{2}^{(n)}
of holomorphic functions in
H_{2}
such
that
z^{k}F^{(k)}\in H_{2}
for every
k=0,1,\dots,n
. Here, we denote by
H_{2}
the Hardy space of all analytic functions
F
over
\mathbb{C}^{+}
satisfying condition

(1.1)

\|F\|_{2}:=\sup_{x>0}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|F(x+iy)|^{2}\,dy\right)^{1\over 2}<\infty.

In fact,
H_{2}
is a Hilbert space with the norm given by (
1.1
).

Point evaluations are continuous on the Hardy space
H_{2}
so that it is a reproducing kernel space (RKHS for short)
of holomorphic functions. In fact, its reproducing kernel
K(z,w)=K_{w}(z)
and corresponding norm are given by

K_{w}(z):={1\over(z+\overline{w})},\hbox{ and }{\|K_{w}\|_{2}={1\over\sqrt{2(\Re w)}}},\quad z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

For more details on the space
H_{2}
, the reader is referred to
[
6
, Chapter VI]
and
[
14
, Chapter 8]
; see also
[
17
,
20
]
.

Endowed with the norm
\|F\|_{2,(n)}:=\|z^{n}F\|_{2}
,
the space
H_{2}^{(n)}
is contained in

H_{2}
continuously and so it is a RKHS. One of the main purposes in the present paper is to determine
the reproducing kernel of
K_{n}(z,w)=K_{n,w}(z)
for
H_{2}^{(n)}
and to estimate the norm
\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}
.
In effect, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we find such a kernel in the form

K_{n,w}(z)=\frac{1}{((n-1)!)^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{n-1}(1-s)^{n-1}}{zt+s\overline{w}}\,ds\,dt,\quad z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

which can be regarded as an integrated version of the kernel
K(z,w)
described above. Then a detailed analysis of that integral
gives us the estimate

\frac{1}{(n-1)!\sqrt{{(2n-1)}}}\ {1\over\sqrt{|w|}}\leq\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{(n-1)!\sqrt{{n}}}\ {1\over\sqrt{|w|}},\quad w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

A notable difference between norms
\|K_{w}\|_{2}
and
\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}
deserves attention.
Whereas the norm
\|K_{w}\|_{2}
is equivalent to the inverse of the distance of point
w
to
the boundary of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, as is the case of some other RKHS of analytic functions on their domains, the norm

\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}
depends on the
radial
distance of
w
to the origin in the complex plane.

The estimate of the norm
\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}
given above suggests studying
composition operators

C_{\varphi}

on
H_{2}^{(n)}
, that is,
C_{\varphi}\colon H_{2}^{(n)}\to H_{2}^{(n)}
defined by

C_{\varphi}(f):=f\circ\varphi
for all
f\in H_{2}^{(n)}
and suitable analytic selfmaps
\varphi
of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Here
\varphi
is said to be the symbol inducing
C_{\varphi}
.

The motivation for such a search comes naturally from the well known Caughran–Schwartz identity, which
applied to the kernel function
K_{n,w}
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
reads

C^{*}_{\varphi}(K_{n,w})=K_{n,\varphi(w)}
for
w\in\mathbb{C}^{+},
where
C_{\varphi}
is bounded and
C^{*}_{\varphi}
is its adjoint (
[
20
]
). This identity readily implies

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\{\|K_{n,\varphi(w)}\|_{2,(n)}\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}^{-1}\}\leq\|C_{\varphi}\|,

from which we get the necessary condition

(1.2)

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{|w|}{|\varphi(w)|}<\infty,

clearly related to the angular derivative
\lim_{z\to\infty}|w||\varphi(w)|^{-1}
.

Thus the finiteness property given by (
1.2
) is a necessary condition for the boundedness of
C_{\varphi}
.
Conversely, we will show that the following more restrictive
condition implies the boundedness of
C_{\varphi}
:

(1.3)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\left|z^{k}\frac{\varphi^{(k)}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right|<\infty,\quad k=1,2,3,\dots,n.

We do not know whether any of conditions (
1.2
) or (
1.3
) is both necessary and sufficient for the boundedness of
C_{\varphi}
.

2.
The Sobolev-Lebesgue space
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}

Let
W^{-1}
be the integration operator on the test space
C_{c}^{\infty}[0,\infty)
given by

W^{-1}\varphi(t):=\int_{t}^{\infty}\varphi(s)ds,\quad\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}[0,\infty),\ t\geq 0.

As a matter of fact,
W^{-1}\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}[0,\infty)
for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}[0,\infty)
so that we can define the

n
–th iterate operator
W^{-n}:=W^{-1}W^{-(n-1)}
of
W^{-1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By straightforward
n
-times application of Fubini’s theorem we have

W^{-n}\varphi(t)={1\over(n-1)!}\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)^{n-1}\varphi(s)ds,\quad\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}[0,\infty),\ t\geq 0.

The integration operator
W^{-n}
can be extended in order to act on a number of spaces. Here we consider a Hilbertian setting.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
L^{2}(t^{n})
be the Hilbert space of the (classes of) complex measurable functions

\varphi
on
(0,\infty)
such that

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(t^{n})}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\varphi(t)t^{n}|^{2}\,dt\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Proposition 2.1
.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varphi\in L^{2}(t^{n})
. Then,
for
1\leq j\leq n-1
, the mapping

(W^{-j}\varphi)(t):=\int_{t}^{\infty}\int_{t_{j-1}}^{\infty}\dots\int_{t_{1}}^{\infty}\varphi(s)\,ds\,dt_{1}\,\dots\,dt_{j-1},\quad t>0,

is well defined and
W^{-j}\varphi\in L^{1}(a,\infty)
for every
a>0
.

Moreover, for
1\leq k\leq n
, we have

(W^{-k}\varphi)(t)={1\over(k-1)!}\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)^{k-1}\varphi(s)\,ds,\quad t>0,

W^{-k}\varphi
is
(k-1)
-times differentiable with
(W^{-k}\varphi)^{(l)}(t)=(-1)^{l}W^{-(k-l)}\varphi
, that is,

(W^{-k}\varphi)^{(l)}(t)={(-1)^{l}\over(k-l-1)!}\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)^{k-l-1}\varphi(s)\,ds,\quad t>0,

for every
1\leq l\leq k-1
, and
(W^{-k}\varphi)^{(k-1)}
is absolutely continuous such that

(-1)^{k}(W^{-k}\varphi)^{(k)}=\varphi
.

Proof.

For
t>0
,
\varphi\in L^{2}(t^{n})
and
1\leq j\leq n-1
, applying Fubini-Tonelli’s rule one has

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\int_{t_{j-1}}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{\infty}|\varphi(s)|\,ds\,dt_{1}\,\dots\,dt_{j-1}={1\over(j-1)!}\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)^{j-1}|\varphi(s)|\,ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(s^{n})}\over(j-1)!}\,\left(\int_{t}^{\infty}(s-t)^{2j-2}s^{-2n}\,dt\right)^{1/2}=C_{k}t^{-n+j-(1/2)}<\infty,

where
C_{k}
is a constant involving the Beta function.

The above estimate implies every statement in the text of the current proposition.
∎

Note that the above formulas also appear in studying the Volterra operator. Recall Hardy’s inequality

(2.1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(W^{-m}\varphi(t)\right)^{2}dt\leq\left({\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\over\Gamma(m+{1\over 2})}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(t^{m}\varphi(t))^{2}dt,

where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a Borel function (
[
13
, p. 245]
).

Using this inequaliy (with
m=n
) one gets
W^{-n}\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
for all

\varphi\in L^{2}(t^{n})
, where
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})=L^{2}(t^{0})
.
Also, the linear operator
W^{-n}\colon L^{2}(t^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
is injective since

(-1)^{n}(W^{-n}\varphi)^{(n)}=\varphi
for all
\varphi\in L^{2}(t^{n})
in accordance with the last part of the above proposition. These facts enable us to define the Hardy-Lebesgue space
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
denote the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
obtained as the image
of
L^{2}(t^{n})
under
W^{-n}
, i.e.,

{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}=W^{-n}(L^{2}(t^{n})).

Note that
f^{(n)},g^{(n)}\in L^{2}(t^{n})
and we endow
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
with the inner product, transferred from
L^{2}(t^{n})
,

\langle f,g\rangle_{(n)}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{(n)}(t)\overline{g^{(n)}(t)}t^{2n}dt,\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2},

and corresponding norm

\|f\|_{2,(n)}:=\|f^{(n)}\|_{L^{2}(t^{n})}=\|t^{n}f^{(n)}\|_{2},\quad f\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2},

so that the space
({\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2},\|\quad\|_{2,(n)})
is a Hilbert space.
(In the case
n=0
, we write
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{(0)}=\langle\,,\,\rangle
for the usual inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.)

By applying again Hardy’s inequality (
2.1
) to

\varphi(t)=|f^{(n)}(t)|t^{k}
and
m=n-k
, for
n\geq k\geq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2,(k)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(W^{-(n-k)}\varphi(t)\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\pi\over\Gamma(n-k+{1\over 2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(t^{n}|f^{(n)}(t)|)^{2}dt={\pi\over\Gamma(n-k+{1\over 2})^{2}}\|f\|_{2,(n)}^{2},

for
f\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
. Thus we have the continuous inclusions

(2.2)

{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}^{(k)}_{2}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),\quad n\geq k\geq 0.

In particular we notice that
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
consists of all functions

f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
such that the derivatives
f^{\prime},\cdots,f^{(n-1)}
on
(0,\infty)
exist,
f^{(n-1)}

is absolutely continuous on
(0,\infty)
, and the maps
t\mapsto t^{k}f^{(k)}(t)
belong to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
for all
k=0,1,\dots,n
.
Moreover, taking

\varphi=(-1)^{n}f^{(n)}
in the statement and proof of Proposition
2.1
one obtains

(2.3)

|f^{(k)}(t)|\leq C_{n,k}t^{-k-(1/2)}\|f\|_{2,(n)},\quad 0\leq k\leq n-1,\ t>0.

Remark 2.3
.

The spaces
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, have been introduced in
[
21
]
. The presentation of the properties of such spaces done before is slightly different from the one of
[
21
]
.

Now, our aim is to present an equivalent expression of the inner product of the space
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Given
n\geq 0
, we define the
(n+1)
-square matrix
C_{n}=(c_{i,j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}
by

c_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}0,&\text{for }i<j;\\
\displaystyle{\binom{i}{j}{i!\over j!},}&\text{for }i\geq j.\end{array}\right.

(i)

Then the matrix
C_{n}
is invertible and
C^{-1}_{n}=((-1)^{i+j}c_{i,j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n}
.

(ii)

For
f\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
and
t>0
, we have that

\displaystyle(t^{n}f)^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}t^{k}f^{(k)}(t),

\displaystyle t^{n}f^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k+n}c_{n,k}(t^{k}f)^{(k)}(t).

Proof.

Part (i) of the lemma is a technical exercise and it is left to the reader. The first equality in part (ii) is the
n
-th derivation rule applied to the product
t^{n}f
, and then the second equality in (ii) is a consequence of part (i).
∎

As an interesting fact we remark that
c_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{n,k}
,
n\geq 0
, is a noted sequence (see reference A002720 in “The On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences” by N.J.A. Sloane).

In the next lemma,
e_{\lambda}(t):=e^{-\lambda t}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
n
be a nonnegative integer.

(i)

For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, the function
e_{\lambda}
belongs to
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
and

\|e_{\lambda}\|_{2,(n)}=\left({(2n)!\over 2^{2n+1}}\right)^{1\over 2}{|\lambda|^{n}\over(\Re\lambda)^{n+{1\over 2}}}.

(ii)

The linear subspace
\mathcal{E}
spanned by
\{e_{\lambda}:\Re\lambda>0\}
is dense in
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
.

Proof.

The proof of part (i) is straightforward. Now take
f\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
such that

\left\langle f,e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{(n)}=0
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, i.e.,

0=(-1)^{n}\lambda^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2n}e^{-\lambda t}f^{(n)}(t)dt=\lambda^{n}{\mathcal{L}}(t^{n}f^{(n)})^{(n)}(\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

whence one has that
{\mathcal{L}}(t^{n}f^{(n)})(\lambda)
must be a polynomial. Since
t\mapsto t^{n}f^{(n)}
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
that polynomial is zero by the Paley-Wiener theorem. Then, as
the Laplace transform
{\mathcal{L}}
is one-to-one, we conclude that
t^{n}f^{(n)}(t)=0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, whence
f=0
in
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
and therefore
\mathcal{E}
is dense in
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
.
∎

The following result gives us the desired expression of the inner product of the space
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
. In the proof we use Laguerre polynomials and Legendre polynomials.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
. Then

(2.4)

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{(n)}=\left\langle(t^{n}f)^{(n)},(t^{n}g)^{(n)}\right\rangle.

Proof.

Let
e_{\lambda}(t):=e^{-\lambda t}
and
e_{\mu}(t):=e^{-\mu t}
, with
\lambda,\mu>0
. Then, by definition
2.2
,

\left\langle e_{\lambda},e_{\mu}\right\rangle_{(n)}=\left\langle t^{n}e_{\lambda}^{(n)},t^{n}e_{\mu}^{(n)}\right\rangle=(\lambda\mu)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{2n}e^{-(\lambda+\mu)t}dt=\frac{(\lambda\mu)^{n}(2n)!}{(\lambda+\mu)^{2n+1}}.

For the second inner product in (
2.4
), note that

(t^{n}e_{\lambda})^{(n)}(t)=n!\,\textrm{L}_{n}(\lambda t),

where
\textrm{L}_{n}
is the Laguerre polynomial of degree
n
. Therefore,

\left\langle(t^{n}e_{\lambda})^{(n)},(t^{n}e_{\mu})^{(n)}\right\rangle=(n!)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{L}_{n}(\lambda t)\textrm{L}_{n}(\mu t)e^{-(\lambda+\mu)t}dt.

Then, according to
[
12
, 7.414 (2)]
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{L}_{n}(\lambda x)\textrm{L}_{n}(\mu x)e^{-bx}dx=\frac{(b-\lambda-\mu)^{n}}{b^{n+1}}\textrm{P}_{n}\left(\frac{b^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)b+2\lambda\mu}{b(b-\lambda-\mu)}\right),

for
\Re b>0
, where
\textrm{P}_{n}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
n
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle(t^{n}e_{\lambda})^{(n)},(t^{n}e_{\mu})^{(n)}\rangle=(n!)^{2}\displaystyle\lim_{b\rightarrow\lambda+\mu}\frac{(b-\lambda-\mu)^{n}}{b^{n+1}}\textrm{P}_{n}\left(\frac{b^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)b+2\lambda\mu}{b(b-\lambda-\mu)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=(n!)^{2}\displaystyle\lim_{b\rightarrow\lambda+\mu}\frac{(b-\lambda-\mu)^{n}}{b^{n+1}}\frac{(2n)!}{2^{n}(n!)^{2}}\left(\frac{b^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)b+2\lambda\mu}{b(b-\lambda-\mu)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\qquad=\displaystyle\frac{(2n)!(\lambda\mu)^{n}}{(\lambda+\mu)^{2n+1}},

because
(2n)!\ 2^{-n}(n!)^{-2}
is the leading coefficient of
\textrm{P}_{n}
, as can be seen in
[
16
, Section 5.4.2]
. We conclude that

\left\langle e_{\lambda},e_{\mu}\right\rangle_{(n)}=\left\langle(t^{n}e_{\lambda})^{(n)},(t^{n}e_{\mu})^{(n)}\right\rangle,\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

and then by linearity

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{(n)}=\left\langle(t^{n}f)^{(n)},(t^{n}g)^{(n)}\right\rangle,

for
f,g\in\mathcal{E}
.
Finally for arbitrary
f,g\in{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
it suffices to apply
Lemma
2.4
(ii), (
2.2
) and the density of

\mathcal{E}
in
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
(Lemma
2.5
(ii)) to obtain

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{(n)}=\left\langle(t^{n}f)^{(n)},(t^{n}g)^{(n)}\right\rangle
. The proof is over.
∎

To finish this section we show an example of function in the space

{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
which will be used, in Section 5 below, as a key to introduce reproducing kernels in Hardy-Sobolev spaces on the half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

For
w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
, define

\displaystyle g_{w,n}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{(s-t)^{n-1}}{s^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{((n-1)!)^{2}}e^{-sxw}dxds

Then
g_{\omega,n}
belongs to
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
with
n
-th derivative satisfying

t^{n}g_{\omega,n}^{(n)}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t^{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-ws}ds.

In particular,
\displaystyle\|g_{w,n}\|_{2,(n)}\leq{2\log 2\over\sqrt{\Re w}}
.

Proof.

Take
w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Put

\varphi_{w,n}(t):=t^{-n}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\ e^{-txw}dx,\quad t>0.

Then
\varphi_{w,n}\in L^{2}(t^{n})
. In fact,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|\varphi(t)|^{2}t^{2n}\ dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{1}{(1-x)^{n-1}\over(n-1)!}\ e^{-(\Re w)tx}dx\right)^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2(\Re w)tx}dt\right)^{1/2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sqrt{\pi/2}\over{\Gamma(n+(1/2))\sqrt{\Re w}}}

where the second inequality is Minkowski‘s inequality .

Hence,
g_{w,n}=W^{-n}\varphi_{w,n}
is in
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
and, moreover, by
Proposition
2.1
,

{g_{\omega,n}^{(n)}(t)}={(-1)^{n}\over t^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-txw}dx=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{t^{2n}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-ws}ds,

for every
t>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|g_{\omega,n}\|_{2,(n)}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|t^{n}g_{\omega,n}^{(n)}\|_{2}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-txw}dx\Big|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\Re wtx}dx\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n-1)!(\Re w)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-e^{-\Re wt})^{2}}{t^{2}}dt=\displaystyle\frac{2\log 2}{(n-1)!\Re w}.

∎

3.
The Hardy-Sobolev spaces
H^{(n)}_{2}

Recall that
H_{2}
is the Hilbertian Hardy space over the right-hand half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
endowed with the norm defined in (
1.1
), which corresponds to the inner product

(F\mid G)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F^{*}(it)\overline{G^{*}(it)}dt,\quad F,G\in H_{2},

where
F^{*}(t)=\lim_{z\to it}F(s)
a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
for every
F,G\in H_{2}
; see
[
6
, Chapter VI]
and
[
14
, Chapter 8]
.
The classical Paley-Wiener theorem states that the Laplace transform
{\mathcal{L}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\to H_{2}
,

{\mathcal{L}}(f)(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-zt}dt,\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

is an isometric isomorphism; i.e.,
F,G\in H_{2}
if and only if there exist unique
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that

F=\mathcal{L}f
and
G=\mathcal{L}g
and

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\left(\mathcal{L}f\mid\mathcal{L}g\right),\quad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),

(see for example
[
22
, Theorem 19.2]
).
Recall that the space
{\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2}
introduced in the preceding section is a subspace of
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. The scale of range spaces
\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{T}}^{(n)}_{2})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is characterized in
[
21
]
. In the following we provide a shorter proof of that characterization.

Let
k
a positive integer. Integrating by parts
k
times, one gets

(3.1)

z^{k}\mathcal{L}h(z)=\mathcal{L}(h^{(k)})(z)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}z^{k-1-j}h^{(j)}(0),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

provided the function
h
is differentiable
k-1
times on the interval
(0,\infty)
, the derivatives
h^{(j)}
are continuous
on
[0,\infty)
, and the functions

h^{(j)}e^{-z(\cdot)}
belong to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
j=0,1,\dots,k
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}^{+}\setminus\{0\}
and

f\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
,

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}z^{k}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(k)}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}{k!\over j!}\mathcal{L}(t^{j}f^{(j)})(z)\ ,\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n;

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\mathcal{L}(t^{k}f^{(k)})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}{k!\over j!}z^{j}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(j)}(z)\ ,\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n;

Proof.

Let
j,k
be integers such that
0\leq j\leq k\leq n
. Using Leibniz’s rule of derivation in
(t^{k}f)^{(j)}
and the bound given by
(
2.3
) one finds that

(t^{k}f)^{(j)}(0):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}(t^{k}f)^{(j)}(t)=0,\quad j\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}.

Then taking
h=t^{k}f
in (
3.1
) we get

(3.2)

\mathcal{L}((t^{k}f)^{(k)})(z)=z^{k}\mathcal{L}(t^{k}f)(z)=(-1)^{k}z^{k}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(k)}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

Now, using Lemma
2.4
, part (ii), we obtain

(-1)^{k}z^{k}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(k)}(z)=\sum_{j=o}^{k}c_{k,j}\mathcal{L}(t^{j}f^{(j)})(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where coefficients
c_{k,j}
are as in that lemma. Applying the invertibility of the matrix
(c_{k,j})
one also derives

\mathcal{L}(t^{j}f^{(j)})(z)=(-1)^{j}\sum_{k=o}^{j}c_{j,k}z^{k}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(k)}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

and the proof is over.
∎

Definition 3.2
.

Given
n\geq 0
a fixed integer, let
H^{(n)}_{2}
denote the linear space consisting of all analytic functions
F
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
such that

(3.3)

z^{k}F^{(k)}\in H_{2},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n.

We use the notation
H^{(0)}_{2}=H_{2}
.

Here is the Paley-Wiener theorem for Hardy-Lebesgue spaces.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Extended Paley-Wiener Theorem)
Let
\mathcal{L}
be the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}:L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})\to H_{2}
. Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2})={H}_{2}^{(n)}.

Consequently,
{H}_{2}^{(n)}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product

\left(F\mid G\right)_{(n)}:={1\over 2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^{2n}(F^{*})^{(n)}(it)\overline{(G^{*})^{(n)}(it)}\ dt,\quad F,G\in{H}_{2}^{(n)},

and corresponding norm

\|F\|_{2,(n)}:=\|z^{n}F^{(n)}\|_{2},\quad F\in{H}_{2}^{(n)},

and the Laplace transform is an isometric isomorphism from
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
onto
{H}_{2}^{(n)}
;
that is, it is an onto linear isomorphism
having the property

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{(n)}=\left(\mathcal{L}f\mid\mathcal{L}g\right)_{(n)},\quad f,g\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}.

Proof.

Given
f\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
, then
t^{k}f^{(k)}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and therefore

z^{k}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(k)}\in{H}_{2}
for
0\leq k\leq n
by Lemma
3.1
. Thus
\mathcal{L}f\in{H}_{2}^{(n)}
.

Conversely, take
F\in{H}_{2}^{(n)}\subset H_{2}
. By the classical Paley-Wiener theorem, there exists

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that
F=\mathcal{L}f
.
We want to prove that, in fact,
f\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
. To this aim denote by
G
the analytic function defined by

G(z):=(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}{n!\over j!}z^{j}F^{(j)}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

Since
F\in{H}_{2}^{(n)}
, we have that
G
belongs to
H_{2}
and so there also exists
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that

G=\mathcal{L}g
.
As in Definition
2.2
, the function
\tilde{f}
belongs to
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
, where

\tilde{f}(s):={1\over(n-1)!}\int_{s}^{\infty}{(s-t)^{n-1}\over t^{n}}g(t)dt,\qquad s>0.

Note that
g=t^{n}(\tilde{f})^{(n)}
by Theorem
2.1
. Now we apply Lemma
3.1
to get, for

z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

G(z)=\mathcal{L}g(z)=\mathcal{L}(t^{n}(\tilde{f})^{(n)})(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{n}{n\choose j}{n!\over j!}z^{j}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f})^{(j)}(z).

Since
F=\mathcal{L}f
, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that

\left(z^{n}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f}-f)\right)^{(n)}(z)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}{n!\over j!}z^{j}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f}-f)^{(j)}(z)=0,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

so that
z^{n}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f}-f)(z)=P_{n}(z)
where
P_{n}
is a polynomial of degree less than or equal to
n-1
. Since

P_{n}(z)z^{-n}
lies in
H_{2}
it must be identically null on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
; that is,
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f}-f)=0
. Hence

f=\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
by the injectivity of
\mathcal{L}
. All in all, we have shown that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2})={H}_{2}^{(n)}
.

In order to finish the proof of the theorem we must only show the equality between the inner products in

\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
and
{H}_{2}^{(n)}
, respectively. This is as follows.

Take
f,g\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
with
F=\mathcal{L}f,G=\mathcal{L}g
in
{H}_{2}^{(n)}
.
By Theorem
2.6
and the isometry provided by the classical Paley-Wiener Theorem,

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{(n)}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\langle t^{n}f^{(n)},t^{n}g^{(n)}\right\rangle=\left\langle(t^{n}f)^{(n)},(t^{n}g)^{(n)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}((t^{n}f)^{(n)})\mid\mathcal{L}((t^{n}g)^{(n)})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((-1)^{n}z^{n}(\mathcal{L}f)^{(n)}(z)\mid(-1)^{n}z^{n}(\mathcal{L}g)^{(n)}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(F\mid G\right)_{(n)},

where we have used (
3.2
) in the last but one equality.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

For every
m\geq n\geq 0
, the linear inclusion

H^{(m)}_{2}\hookrightarrow H^{(n)}_{2}

is continuous with dense range.

Proof.

The continuity of the inclusion mapping
H^{(m)}_{2}\hookrightarrow H^{(n)}_{2}
follows from Theorem
3.3

and (
2.2
). As for the density, let
\mathcal{K}
be the linear space spanned by all functions of type
K_{\lambda}
,

\Re\lambda>0
. Then
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{K}
where
\mathcal{E}
is the space defined in Lemma
2.5
(ii). Clearly,

\mathcal{K}\hookrightarrow H^{(m)}_{2}\hookrightarrow H^{(n)}_{2}
for
m\geq n\geq 0
, whence the desired density follows from
Lemma
2.5
(ii) and Theorem
3.3
again.
∎

4.
The Hardy-Sobolev spaces on the disc

Recall that the Hilbertian Hardy space
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
over the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
is endowed with the norm

\|F\|_{2,\mathbb{D}}:=\sup_{0<r<1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\theta\in[0,2\pi]}|F(re^{i\theta})|^{2}\,d\theta\right)^{1\over 2}.

It is well known that one can use Cayley’s transform to navigate between spaces
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
and
H_{2}
, and subspaces of them: The Cayley transform is defined by

\gamma(\lambda)=(1+\lambda)/(1-\lambda)
, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
.
Then,

F\in H_{2}
if and only if
F_{\mathbb{D}}\in(1-\lambda)H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
, where

(4.1)

F_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda):=F\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{D};

see
[
14
, Theorem p. 129]
. See also
[
17
, Theorem 13.17]
for the relations between
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
and
H^{p}(\mathbb{C}^{+})
for
0<p\leq\infty
.
Functions in
{H^{(1)}_{2}}
can be transferred to a subspace contained in

H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
as follows.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
F
be an analytic function on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
F_{\mathbb{D}}
the function on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
given by
(
4.1
)
.
Then
F\in{H^{(1)}_{2}}
if and only if

(4.2)

F_{\mathbb{D}}\in(1-\lambda)H_{2}(\mathbb{D})\quad\mbox{and}\quad F_{\mathbb{D}}^{\prime}\in(1+\lambda)^{-1}H_{2}(\mathbb{D}).

If so, then the following equality holds:

(4.3)

\sqrt{2}\|F\|_{2,(1)}=\|(1+\lambda)F^{\prime}_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)\|_{2,\mathbb{D}}.

Proof.

The condition
zF^{\prime}\in H_{2}
is equivalent to

(4.4)

\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}F^{\prime}\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\in(1-\lambda)H_{2}(\mathbb{D}).

Since, obviously,

(F_{\mathbb{D}})^{\prime}(\lambda)=F^{\prime}\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\frac{2}{(1-\lambda)^{2}},

it follows that relation (
4.4
) is equivalent to
F^{\prime}_{\mathbb{D}}\in(1+\lambda)^{-1}H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
.
Thus
F\in{H^{(1)}_{2}}
if and only if (
4.2
) holds. Also, the norm equality (
4.3
) is valid,
by
[
14
, p. 130]
.
∎

Of course Proposition
4.1
can be extended for
n>1
, i.e., to identify the subspaces in

(1-\lambda)H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
formed by functions
F_{\mathbb{D}}
with
F\in H_{2}^{(n)}
for
n>1
. For example,
for
n=2
and
F\in H_{2}^{(2)}
, we have that

(F_{\mathbb{D}})^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)={-4\over(1-\lambda)^{3}}F^{\prime}\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1-w}\right)+{4\over(1-\lambda)^{4}}F^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{1+\lambda}{1-w}\right),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{D},

and we conclude that
(1+\lambda)^{2}(1-\lambda)F^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbb{D}}\in H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

In this way one could deal with Hardy-Sobolev type spaces on the unit disc instead on the right-hand half-plane.
However we stay working in the half-plane setting, where one can make use of the Laplace transform.

Nonetheless, let us show an interesting property of
H_{2}^{(n)}
by arguing on its copy in
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})

Corollary 4.2
.

If
F\in H^{(n)}_{2}
, then the functions
z^{k-1}F^{(k-1)}
are extensible by continuity at all nonzero points
on the imaginary axis, the point at infinity included, for
k=1,2,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Take
F\in H^{(1)}_{2}
. By condition (
4.2
),

F_{\mathbb{D}}\in(1-\lambda)H_{2}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
,
(1+\lambda)F^{\prime}_{\mathbb{D}}\in H_{2}(\mathbb{D})

and clearly
[(1+\lambda)F_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)]^{\prime}=(1+\lambda)F^{\prime}_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)+F_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)
.
It follows that

[(1+\lambda)F_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)]^{\prime}\in H_{2}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq H_{1}(\mathbb{D})
. Then, by
[
6
, Theorem 3.11]
,
the function
(1+\lambda)F_{\mathbb{D}}(\lambda)
is extensible by continuity on the unit circle and so,
F_{\mathbb{D}}
extends by continuity to that circle except possibly at
-1
. Since the Cayley transform
\gamma

maps
-1
to
0
and
1
to
\infty
, the conclusion on the extensibility of
F
by continuity follows.

Now take
F\in H^{(n)}_{2}
for
n>1
. Then the identity

z\frac{d}{dz}\left(z^{k-1}F^{(k-1)}(z)\right)=(k-1)z^{k-1}F^{(k-1)}(z)+z^{k}F^{(k)}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

implies that
z^{k-1}F^{(k-1)}(z)
belongs to
H^{(1)}_{2}
for
1\leq k\leq n
. Thus the continuity on the imaginary axis of the functions

z^{k}F^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,n-1
, follows by that and the extensibility by continuity of
H^{(1)}_{2}
–functions.
∎

5.
Reproducing kernel of
H^{(n)}_{2}

Given a set
\Omega
one says that
{\mathcal{H}}
is a (complex) reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS, for short) on
\Omega
if it is a Hilbert space of complex functions on
\Omega
with inner product
\langle\cdot\mid\cdot\rangle
such that the evaluation functional

\delta_{s}\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{C},\ f\mapsto f(s),

is bounded for all
s\in\Omega
. Thus for every
s\in\Omega
there exists a unique

k_{s}\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
f(s)=\langle f\mid k_{s}\rangle
for every
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Functions
k_{s}
are called kernel-functions, and the span generated by of all kernel-functions is dense in
\mathcal{H}
. One defines

K(s,t)=\langle k_{t}\mid k_{s}\rangle
,
s,t\in\Omega
, which is said to be the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
. This map
K
is a
nonnegative, or positive semidefinite, kernel (called sometimes positive semidefinite Moore matrix) in the sense of Aronszajn–Moore, meaning that it satisfies

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}K(s_{i},s_{j})c_{i}\overline{c_{j}}\geq 0,\quad n\geq 1,\,\,c_{i}\in\mathbb{C},\,\,s_{i}\in\Omega,\,\,i,j=1,2,\dots,n.

A basic reference for nonnegative kernels is
[
1
]
.

Point evaluation functionals on
H_{2}
are continuous, so that
H_{2}
is a RKHS. In fact,

F(w)=(F\mid K_{w})
with
K_{w}:=(z+\overline{w})^{-1}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, as mentioned in
Section
1
. Let
n
be a natural number. Since
H_{2}^{(n)}
is continuously contained in
H_{2}
it follows that
H_{2}^{(n)}
has all point evaluations continuous, so it is also a RKHS.
In this section we determine the nonnegative kernel of
H_{2}^{(n)}
and provide estimates of the norm of kernel functions.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
n
be a natural number. Then the function
K_{n}
defined on
\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{+}
by

K_{n}(z,w)=\frac{1}{((n-1)!)^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{n-1}(1-s)^{n-1}}{zt+s\overline{w}}\,ds\,dt,\ z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

is the reproducing kernel of
H_{2}^{(n)}
; that is, if
K_{n,w}(z):=K_{n}(z,w)
then

K_{n,w}\in H_{2}^{(n)}\ \hbox{ and }F(w)=(F\mid K_{n,w})\ \hbox{ for all }F\in H_{2}^{(n)}.

Proof.

Let
w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and let
g_{{w},n}
be the function discussed in Proposition
2.7
.
Then
g_{{w},n}\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
and moreover, using the change of variables
s=ty^{-1}
and taking the Laplace transform
we have for every
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(g_{{w},n})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}{\mathcal{L}}(e^{-(\cdot)\ wx/y})(z)dx{dy\over y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\frac{1}{xw+zy}dxdy=K_{n,\overline{w}}(z).

On the other hand, using the integral formula given for
g_{w,n}^{(n)}
in Proposition
2.7
we have,
for any
f\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(f)(\overline{w})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-\overline{w}t}dt=(-1)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{(t-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f^{(n)}(s)ds\ e^{-\overline{w}t}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{(n)}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(t-s)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-\overline{w}s}dsdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{(n)}(t)t^{2n}\overline{g_{\omega,n}^{(n)}(t)}dt=\langle f,g_{w,n}\rangle_{2,(n)}.

Now take
F\in H^{(n)}_{2}
. By Theorem
3.3
, there exists a unique
f\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{2}

such that
{\mathcal{L}}(f)=F
with

(F\mid K_{n,w})_{(n)}=\langle f,g_{\overline{w},n}\rangle_{2,(n)}=F(w),\quad w\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

as we needed to show.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

By expanding
(1-t)^{n-1}
and
(1-s)^{n-1}
in the integral expression of
K_{n}
one gets

\displaystyle K_{n,w}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{(n+k)!(n-1-k)!}\frac{wc^{k}}{z^{k+1}}\right)\log\left(\displaystyle\frac{z+wc}{wc}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{(n+k)!(n-1-k)!}\frac{z^{k}}{wc^{k+1}}\right)\log\left(\displaystyle\frac{z+wc}{z}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+j}}{(k-j)(n+k)!(n-1-k)!}\frac{z^{j}}{wc^{j+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+j}}{(k-j)(n+k)!(n-1-k)!}\frac{wc^{j}}{z^{j+1}}.

In particular, for
n=1
,
n=2
, and
n=3
we have respectively

\displaystyle K_{1,w}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over z}\log\left({\overline{w}+z\over\overline{w}}\right)+{1\over\overline{w}}\log\left({\overline{w}+z\over z}\right),

\displaystyle K_{2,w}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 6z}+{3z+\overline{w}\over 6z^{2}}\log\left({\overline{w}+z\over\overline{w}}\right)-{1\over 6\overline{w}}+{3\overline{w}+z\over 6\overline{w}^{2}}\log\left({\overline{w}+z\over z}\right),

\displaystyle K_{3,w}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{(9z+2\overline{w})\over 240z^{2}}+{10z^{2}+5z\overline{w}+\overline{w}^{2}\over 120z^{3}}\log\left({\overline{w}+z\over\overline{w}}\right)

\displaystyle-{(9\overline{w}+2z)\over 240\overline{w}^{2}}+{10\overline{w}^{2}+5z\overline{w}+z^{2}\over 120\overline{w}^{3}}\log\left({\overline{w}+z\over z}\right).

Next, we proceed to estimate the norm of the kernel function. We claim that

\|K_{n,w}\|_{2,(n)}\sim|w|^{-1/2}
for
w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
(up to lower and upper constants depending on
n
). To show this, we appeal to the trigonometric form of the integral definining
K_{n}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\theta\in({-\pi/2},{\pi/2})
, put

I(\theta):=\displaystyle{\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\cos\theta}{t^{2}+1+2t\cos(2\theta)}dt}.

Then
\displaystyle{I(0)={1\over 2}}
and

I(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta},\hbox{ if }\theta\not=0,

whence
\displaystyle{{1\over 2}\leq I(\theta)\leq{\pi\over 4}}
.

Proof.

For
\theta=0
, it is straightforward to check that
I(0)=1/2
.

For
0<\theta<{\pi\over 2}
, we have

I(\theta)=I(-\theta)
and

\displaystyle I(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cos\theta}{}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{(t+\cos(2\theta))^{2}+\sin^{2}(2\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|\sin\theta|}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{|\sin(2\theta)|}}\frac{dx}{\left(x+\frac{\cos(2\theta)}{|\sin(2\theta)|}\right)^{2}+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2|\sin\theta|}\int_{\frac{\cos(2\theta)}{|\sin(2\theta)|}}^{\frac{\cos\theta}{|\sin\theta|}}\frac{dy}{y^{2}+1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\theta}{\sin\theta},

where we have changed the variables
\displaystyle{dy=dx+\cos(2\theta)|\sin(2\theta)|^{-1}}
.

Finally, since
1\leq(\theta/\sin\theta)\leq\pi/2
the desired result follows.
∎

In the following, we use the relation

\|K_{n,z}\|^{2}_{2,(n)}=\left(K_{n,z}\mid K_{n,z}\right)_{(n)}=K_{n,z}(z),\quad n\geq 1,z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
z=|z|e^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
.
Then

(5.1)

\frac{1}{\Gamma(n)\sqrt{{(2n-1)}}}{1\over\sqrt{|z|}}\leq\|K_{n,z}\|_{2,(n)}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(n)\sqrt{{n}}}{1\over\sqrt{|z|}}.

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
and
z=|z|e^{i\theta}\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\|K_{n,z}\|_{2,(n)}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{n,z}(z)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{n-1}}{\Gamma(n)}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{\Gamma(n)}\frac{1}{x\overline{z}+yz}dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|z|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{n-1}}{\Gamma(n)}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{\Gamma(n)}\frac{(x+y)\cos\theta}{x^{2}+y^{2}+2xy\cos(2\theta)}dxdy,

since
\Im(K_{n,z}(z))=0
(use symmetry in the imaginary part of the integral). Thus using symmetry first and then the change of variables
x=yt
one obtains

\displaystyle K_{n,z}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\cos\theta}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{y}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}(1-y)^{n-1}(x+y)}{x^{2}+y^{2}+2xy\cos(2\theta)}dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\cos\theta}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1+t)(1-yt)^{n-1}(1-y)^{n-1}}{t^{2}+1+2t\cos(2\theta)}dtdy.

Given that, one has
(1+t)(1-yt)^{n-1}\leq 2
if
t,y\in(0,1),
it follows that the following upper estimate holds

\displaystyle K_{n,z}(z)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4\cos\theta}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{n-1}}{t^{2}+1+2t\cos(2\theta)}dtdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{n\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}I(\theta)\leq\frac{\pi}{n\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}.

Notice that we have used Lemma
5.3
in the last inequality.

For the lower norm estimate now, observe that, for
t,y\in(0,1)
one has
(1+t)(1-yt)^{n-1}\geq(1-y)^{n-1}
and therefore

\displaystyle K_{n,z}(z)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2\cos\theta}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{2n-2}}{t^{2}+1+2t\cos(2\theta)}dtdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|(2n-1)}I(\theta)\geq\frac{1}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|(2n-1)},

by Lemma
5.3
again.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

With regards to the relationship between norms of the kernels note that, for
n=0
, we have

\|K_{0,z}\|_{2}=\|K_{z}\|_{2}=\displaystyle{1\over\sqrt{2\Re z}}
and then

\|K_{1,z}\|_{2,(1)}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|K_{0,z}\|_{2},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

For
n\geq 2
and
z=|z|e^{i\theta}
, it follows easily from the equality

K_{n,z}(z)=\frac{2\cos\theta}{\Gamma(n)^{2}|z|}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1+t)(1-yt)^{n-1}(1-y)^{n-1}}{t^{2}+1+2t\cos(2\theta)}dt\ dy

that

K_{n,z}(z)\leq{1\over(n-1)^{2}}K_{n-1,z}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

Concerning point estimates of functions in the Hardy-Sobolev spaces, recall that it is well known that for every

F\in H_{2}
,

|F(z)|^{2}\leq\frac{\|F\|^{2}_{2}}{4\pi\Re z},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where the constant
1/4\pi
is the best possible, see for example
[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
and
[
6
, Chapter VI.C]
.
Similar bounds are available now for functions
F\in H_{2}^{(n)}
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
F\in H^{(n)}_{2}
. Then for each
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}

|F(z)|^{2}\leq\frac{\pi\|F\|^{2}_{2,(n)}}{\Gamma(n)^{2}n|z|}.

Proof.

Let
F\in H^{(n)}_{2}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Theorem
5.4
, we have

|F(z)|^{2}=|\left(F\mid K_{n,z}\right)_{(n)}|^{2}\leq\|F\|^{2}_{2,(n)}\|K_{n,z}\|^{2}_{2,(n)}\leq\frac{\pi\|F\|^{2}_{2,(n)}}{\Gamma(n)^{2}n|z|},

and the proof is over.
∎

6.
Analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{C}^{+}

Here we point out some properties of analytic selfmaps
\varphi:\mathbb{C}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{+}
, in preparation for discussing
composition operators on
H_{2}^{(n)}
in Section
7
.
So in particular, in the rest of the paper we deal with selfmaps
\varphi
which fix the point at infinity and have a finite angular derivative at
\infty
in the sense of Carathéodory; that is,

\varphi(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)<\infty
where

\varphi(\infty)=\lim_{|z|\to+\infty}\varphi(z),\qquad\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\lim_{z\to\infty}{z\over\varphi(z)},

the second limit above being non-tangential (see details about angular derivatives and nontangential limits in
[
23
, Chapter 4]
). In
[
8
, Proposition 2.2]
, a version for
\mathbb{C}^{+}
of the Julia-Carathéodory theorem is established. That result states that
the following three conditions are equivalent:

(a)

\varphi(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)<\infty
;

(b)

\displaystyle{\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}{\Re z\over\Re(\varphi(z))}}<\infty
;

(c)

\displaystyle{\limsup_{z\to\infty}{\Re z\over\Re(\varphi(z))}}<\infty
.

Further, if (a), (b) or (c) hold then

(6.1)

\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\displaystyle{\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}{\Re z\over\Re(\varphi(z))}}=\displaystyle{\limsup_{z\to\infty}{\Re z\over\Re(\varphi(z))}},

We now show a simple condition for the existence and finiteness of
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)
. The proof is inspired in the proof of Julia’s inequality. We include it to avoid the lack of completeness.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\varphi
be an analytic selfmap of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
such that

(6.2)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{|z|}{|\varphi(z)|}<\infty.

Then the map
\varphi
satisfies (a), (b), (c) prior to (
6.2
). Moreover

\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\Re z}{\Re\varphi(z)}\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\left|z\right|}{\left|\varphi(z)\right|}.

Proof.

Let
\phi
denote the conformal conjugate
\phi=\gamma^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\gamma
of

\varphi
via the Cayley transform
\gamma
introduced in Section 3. Then, one can easily check that

(6.3)

\frac{z}{\varphi(z)}=\frac{1-\phi(\lambda)}{1-\lambda}\frac{1+\lambda}{1+\phi(\lambda)},

for
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
and
z=\gamma(\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Set
M=\sup\{|z|/|\varphi(z)|:z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\}
.
One has
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
since
M<\infty
, and so
\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\phi(\lambda)=1
.
By (
6.3
), one can write

\left|\frac{1-\phi(\lambda)}{1-\lambda}\right|\leq M\left|\frac{1+\phi(\lambda)}{1+\lambda}\right|,\ \mbox{whence}\ \limsup_{\lambda\to 1}\left|\frac{1-\phi(\lambda)}{1-\lambda}\right|\leq M<\infty.

By
[
19
, Remark 2]
, the consequence of the above condition is the fact that
\phi^{\prime}(1)<\infty
, hence

\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)<\infty
and so (a), (b), (c) hold.

It is clear that the nontangential limit

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{|z|}{\left|\varphi(z)\right|}

is less than or equal to
\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\left|z\right|}{\left|\varphi(z)\right|}
, whence

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\Re z}{\Re\varphi(z)}\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\left|z\right|}{\left|\varphi(z)\right|},

and the proof is over.
∎

Example 6.2
.

(a)

Let us consider the rational map

r(z)=\frac{a_{l}z^{l}+\cdots+a_{1}z+a_{0}}{b_{m}z^{m}+\cdots+b_{1}z+b_{0}}

with
a_{l},b_{m}\neq 0
. In
[
7
, Proposition 16]
it is proved that, if
r
is such that
r(\infty)=\infty
and
r(\mathbb{C}^{+})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{+}
then necessarily

(i)

l=m+1
,

(ii)

a_{l}/b_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, and in particular,
a_{l}/b_{m}>0
,

(iii)

\Im(a_{0}/b_{0})\geq 0
.

In this case
r^{\prime}(\infty)=b_{m}a_{m+1}^{-1}
.

In the particular case when
r
is a Möbius selfmap of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
such that
r(\infty)=\infty
and
r^{\prime}(\infty)<\infty
, the map
r
has the form

r(z)=\alpha z+\beta\qquad\alpha>0\,,\,\Re\beta\geq 0.

(b)

The same can be said on a slightly larger class of functions which are quotients of linear combinations of fractional powers of
z
and map the set
\mathbb{C}^{+}
into itself.
Such a class of functions is denoted by
QLP(\mathbb{C}^{+})
and consists of maps

\rho(z)=\frac{a_{1}z^{\alpha_{1}}+a_{2}z^{\alpha_{2}}+\cdots+a_{m}z^{\alpha_{m}}}{b_{1}z^{\beta_{1}}+b_{2}z^{\beta_{2}}+\cdots+b_{p}z^{\beta_{p}}},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}>0
are not necessarily integers. Without loss of generality, one can assume that properties
\alpha_{i}>\alpha_{i+1}
and
\beta_{j}>\beta_{j+1}
hold, see
[
7
, Section 5]
. If
\rho\in QLP(\mathbb{C}^{+})
and
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}+1
then
\rho(\infty)=\infty
and
\rho^{\prime}(\infty)<\infty
. Moreover

\rho^{\prime}(\infty)=\displaystyle{b_{1}/a_{1}}
.

In particular, the map

\rho(z)=az+b\sqrt{z}+c,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

with
a,b>0
and
\Re c\geq 0
has been considered in
[
18
, Example 2.9]
.

(c)

As a last example, let
\varphi
be given by
\varphi(z)=az+b\log(1+z)
with
a,b>0
. It is clear that
\varphi(\mathbb{C}^{+})\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=a^{-1}
.

Remark 6.3
.

Note that the quantities involved in Proposition
6.1
are not necessarily equal, and that the finiteness of

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}|z||\varphi(z)|^{-1}
is not necessary for the existence and finiteness of

\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)
. For example, for
\varphi
given by
\varphi(z)=z+i
,
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, one has

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\Re z}{\Re\varphi(z)}=1,\quad\hbox{and}\quad\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\left|z\right|}{\left|\varphi(z)\right|}=\infty.

In general, a Möbius map
\varphi(z)=az+b
satisfies condition (
6.2
)
if and only if
a>0
and
\Re b>0
.

We now introduce a stronger condition than (
6.2
) which will be considered in the next section.

Definition 6.4
.

Take
\varphi
an analytic self map of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. We say that
\varphi
verifies the
n
-times boundedness condition
(

-BC) when

(6.4)

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\left|z^{k}\frac{\varphi^{(k)}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right|<\infty,\quad k=1,2,3,\dots,n,

for
n\geq 1
.

Example 6.5
.

(i)

Recall the rational map
r
given in Example
6.2
(a)
such that

. Then the map

satisfies the

-BC for all
n\geq 1
.

(ii)

A map
\rho\in QLP(\mathbb{C}^{+})
satisfying the conditions in Example

(b) and such that

must also satisfy the
n
-BC for all
n\geq 1
.

(iii)

Let
\varphi
be a self-map on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
satisfying conditions (

) and such that

. Then property

-BC holds for

. In particular the map

\varphi(z)=az+b\log(1+z)

,

7.
Boundedness of composition operators on
H^{(n)}_{2}

An operator
C_{\psi,\varphi}
of type

C_{\psi,\varphi}f:=\psi f\circ\varphi

acting on some function space is called a weighted composition operator. Suppose that such an operator

C_{\psi,\varphi}
is bounded on some RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, with kernel
\mathcal{K}
,
formed by scalar valued functions on some set
\Omega
.
Then the following extended version of the Caughran–Schwarz equation holds (see
[
19
, Theorem 5]
):

(7.1)

C^{*}_{\psi,\varphi}\mathcal{K}_{x}=\overline{\psi(x)}\mathcal{K}_{\varphi(x)},\quad x\in\Omega,

from which one obtains the bound

(7.2)

\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\overline{\psi}(x)|{\|\mathcal{K}_{\varphi(x)}\|\over\|\mathcal{K}_{x}\|}\leq\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|.

Here we are interested in operators

C_{\varphi}:=C_{1,\varphi}
acting on
H^{(n)}_{2}
,
with
\varphi
an analytic selfmap of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Let us recall that, as
an immediate consequence of Littlewood’s subordination principle (see for example
[
23
, p.16]
),
every
selfmap
\varphi
on the disc
\mathbb{D}
induces a bounded composition operator
C_{\varphi}
on the space

H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
, with norm estimate

\|C_{\varphi}\|\leq\sqrt{1+|\varphi(0)|}\left(\sqrt{1-|\varphi(0)|}\right)^{-1}
(
[
5
]
).
Unlike the disc case,
not all
analytic selfmaps
\varphi
of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
induce bounded composition operators on

H_{2}
, the necessary and sufficient condition for boundedness being (see
[
8
,
24
]
)

(7.3)

\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\Re z}{\Re\varphi(z)}<\infty.

The need of condition (
7.3
) for the continuity of
C_{\varphi}
is a consequence of applying estimate
(
7.2
) (with
\psi\equiv 1
)
to the kernel
K(z,w)=(z+\overline{w})^{-1}
in
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
(as it was noticed in

[
24
, Theorem 2.4]
).
The proof of the sufficiency was obtained initially in
[
19
, Corollary 4]
, and, by significantly simpler methods in
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
.
Actually the norm of
C_{\varphi}
is

\|C_{\varphi}\|=r(C_{\varphi})=\|C_{\varphi}\|_{e}=\sqrt{\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)}
,
where
r
denotes the spectral radius and
\|\quad\|_{e}
the essential norm (see
[
8
, Theorem 3.1, 3.4]
).
In particular, by (
7.3
),
it is readily seen which Möbius selfmaps of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
induce bounded composition operators on
H_{2}
, namely only the maps of type

(7.4)

\varphi(z)=\alpha z+\beta\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

for
\alpha>0
and
\Re\beta\geq 0
have that property.

In connection with the above results, the main idea underlying papers

[
8
]
and
[
15
]
is to approach the adjoint of a (weighted or unweighted) composition operator by using nonnegative kernels. Next, we state in full generality and prove the boundedness principle which is behind some of the results
in
[
15
]
and
[
8
]
. For this, recall that given two nonnegative kernels
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
on the same set

\Omega
, we write
K_{1}\leq K_{2}
if
K_{2}-K_{1}
is a nonnegative kernel on
\Omega
. Also, for every nonnegative kernel
K
on
\Omega
and every selfmap

\varphi
on
\Omega
, we denote by
K\circ\varphi
the mapping

K\circ\varphi(x,y):=K(\varphi(x),\varphi(y)),\qquad x,y\in\Omega.

It is not difficult to check that
K\circ\varphi
is a nonnegative kernel.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a RKHS consisting of scalar valued functions on some set

\Omega
, having nonnegative kernel
\mathcal{K}
,
and the property that the set of all kernel functions of
\mathcal{H}
is a linearly independent set.
Then a weighted composition operator
C_{\psi,\varphi}
is a bounded operator on

\mathcal{H}
if and only if there is some

M\geq 0
such that

(7.5)

M^{2}{\mathcal{K}}(x,y)\geq\overline{\psi(x)}{\psi(y)}{\mathcal{K}}\circ\varphi(x,y),\quad x,y\in\Omega.

Furthermore, the following equality holds:

(7.6)

\min\{M\geq 0:(\ref{ConditionJury})\,\,\mbox{\rm holds}\}=\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|.

If
M^{2}=\sup\{|\psi(x)|^{2}\mathcal{K}\circ\varphi(x,x)/\mathcal{K}(x,x):x\in S\}
is finite and satisfies
(
7.5
)
, then
C_{\psi,\varphi}
is bounded and
\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|=M
.

Clearly,
C_{1,\varphi}=C_{\varphi}
and so, condition
(
7.5
)
looks as follows in this particular case:

(7.7)

\mathcal{K}\circ\varphi\leq M^{2}\mathcal{K}.

Proof.

Let
T
be the following transform on the space
\mathcal{S}
spanned by the kernel functions of
\mathcal{H}
:

T\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\right):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\overline{\psi(x_{j})}k_{\varphi(x_{j})},\qquad f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\in\mathcal{S}.

The above (obviously linear) transform is well defined, since the set of all kernel functions of
\mathcal{H}
is linearly independent. It is easy to see that
T
is bounded since, by condition (
7.5
) one easily gets

\left\|T\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\right)\right\|\leq M\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\right\|,\qquad f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\in\mathcal{S}.

Thus
T
extends to
\mathcal{H}
, the closure of
\mathcal{S}
, and this extension by linearity and boundedness, denoted also by
T
, satisfies

\|Tf\|\leq M\|f\|
, for
f\in\mathcal{H}
.

Since
T
is bounded, so is
T^{*}
. Further, it is readily seen that

T^{*}=C_{\psi,\varphi}

and so
C_{\psi,\varphi}
is bounded. Indeed,

\langle T^{*}k_{x},k_{y}\rangle=\psi(y)k_{x}(\varphi(y))=\langle C_{\psi,\varphi}k_{x},k_{y}\rangle,\quad x,y\in\Omega.

Note also that
\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|\leq M
.

Conversely, assume
\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|<\infty
. Then writing

\left\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|^{2}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\right\|^{2},\quad f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}k_{x_{j}}\in\mathcal{S},

and using (
7.1
), one gets that
M=\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|
satisfies condition
(
7.5
). Hence, if that condition holds for some
M>0
, then the least value of
M>0
for which it holds is
M=\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|
.

Finally, combining (
7.1
) with

\sup\{\|C^{*}_{\psi,\varphi}k_{x}\|/\|k_{x}\|:x\in S\}\leq\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|

one gets that, if
M^{2}=\sup\{|\psi(x)|^{2}\mathcal{K}\circ\varphi(x,x)/\mathcal{K}(x,x):x\in S\}
is finite and satisfies (
7.5
) then
T_{\psi,\varphi}
is bounded and
\|C_{\psi,\varphi}\|=M
.
∎

Straightforward application of relation (
7.7
) in the above theorem gives us the following characterization, in the case of the Hardy-Sobolev space
H_{2}^{(n)}
. We use the kernel of
H_{2}^{(n)}
given in Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\varphi
be an analytic selfmap of
\mathbb{C}^{+}.
The composition operator
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded operator on
H_{2}^{(n)}
for
n\geq 1
if and only if there exists
M>0
such that the kernel
L_{n}
given, for
(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{+}
, by

\displaystyle L_{n}(z,w):=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)^{n-1}(1-s)^{n-1}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{zt+s\overline{w}}-\frac{1}{\varphi(z)t+s\overline{\varphi(w)}}\right)ds\ dt,

is nonnegative.

Remark 7.3
.

Theorem
7.1
extends
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
concerning composition operators on
H_{2}
, where the main idea of the proof is to show that the function
L_{0}
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}\times\mathbb{C}^{+}
defined by

L_{0}(z,w):={\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)\over z+\overline{w}}-{1\over\varphi(z)+\overline{\varphi(w)}},\quad z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

is a nonnegative kernel of
H_{2}
. To do this, note that
\varphi(z)-(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\infty))z
is an analytic selfmap
of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
if (
7.3
) holds, and then the kernel

\frac{\varphi(z)-(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\infty))z+\overline{(\varphi(w)-(1/\varphi^{\prime}(\infty))w}}{z+\overline{w}},\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

is a nonnegative kernel on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
by the Nevanlina–Pick interpolation theorem (
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
).
Since the product of two nonnegative kernels on the same set is also a nonnegative kernel on that set, it follows that

\frac{1}{\varphi(z)+\overline{\varphi(w)}}\left(\frac{\varphi(z)+\overline{\varphi(w)}}{z+\overline{w}}-\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{z+\overline{w}}-\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)}\frac{1}{\varphi(z)+\overline{\varphi(w)}}\right)

is a nonegative kernel on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
is over.

With regards to Corollary
7.2
, since the kernel
L_{n}
is an integral version of kernel
L_{0}
, one may expect that some similar ideas could work –under condition (
6.2
) at least, on account of Proposition
7.4
below–
for suitable
M>0
.
However the authors have not been able to prove or find an integral version of the Nevanlina-Pick interpolation theorem to conclude the nonnegativity of this kernel.

In fact, the characterization of the boundeness of composition operators
C_{\varphi}
on the Hardy-Sobolev space
H^{(n)}_{2}

does not seem to be simple. In the sequel, we present some partial results in this direction.

Estimate (
7.2
) together with
(
5.1
) and Proposition
6.1
give us the following result.

Proposition 7.4
.

If
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded composition operator on
H^{(n)}_{2}
for some
n\geq 1
,
then the map
\varphi
satisfies condition (
6.2
).
Hence,
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)<\infty
and
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded operator on
H_{2}
.

Remark 7.5
.

The above proposition tells us that the semigroup of analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
inducing bounded composition operators on
H^{(n)}_{2}
is a (proper, as we will see in Corollary
7.7
)
sub–semigroup of the semigroup of analytic selfmaps of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
inducing bounded composition operators on
{H}_{2}
.

Now, we give some sufficient conditions to conclude that the composition operator
C_{\varphi}
on
{H^{(n)}_{2}}
is bounded on
{H^{(n)}_{2}}
. The following theorem provides a point of view different from that contained by Theorem
7.1
.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
\varphi
be an analytic selfmap of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
satisfying condition
(
7.3
)
and the
n
-BC property
(
6.4
)
; that is,

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{\Re z}{\Re\varphi(z)}<\infty\quad\hbox{ and }\ \sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\left|z^{k}\frac{\varphi^{(k)}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right|<\infty,\ k=1,2,3,\dots,n.

Then the map
\varphi
induces a bounded composition operator
C_{\varphi}
on
{H^{(n)}_{2}}
.

Proof.

Since
{H^{(n)}_{2}}
is a RKHS,
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded composition operator on
{H^{(n)}_{2}}
if and only if
C_{\varphi}{H^{(n)}_{2}}\subseteq{H^{(n)}_{2}}
which follows by a straightforward application of the Closed Graph Principle. Thus, if
f
is an arbitrary function in
{H^{(n)}_{2}}
, showing that
f\circ\varphi\in{H^{(n)}_{2}}
will end the current proof. We will prove the aforementioned fact in the following. First note that the
n
–th derivative of the composite of two analytic functions has the following form:

(7.8)

(f\circ\varphi)^{(n)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}(f^{(k)}\circ\varphi)(\varphi^{\prime})^{m_{k_{1}}}(\varphi^{(2)})^{m_{k_{2}}}\dots(\varphi^{(n)})^{m_{k_{n}}}

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
and
m_{i_{j}}
are nonnegative integers for all
i,j=1,2,\dots,n
and satisfy the conditions

(7.9)

1\cdot m_{k_{1}}+2\cdot m_{k_{2}}+\dots+n\cdot m_{k_{n}}=n,\quad k=1,2,\dots,n,

and

(7.10)

m_{k_{1}}+m_{k_{2}}+\dots+m_{k_{n}}=k,\quad k=1,2,\dots,n.

The above description of the
n
–th derivative of the composition of two analytic functions can be easily checked by induction, (alternatively one can use an exact description of the coefficients
c_{k}
known as the Fàa di Bruno formula). Our goal is to show that
\|z^{n}(f\circ\varphi)^{(n)}(z)\|_{2}<\infty
. To that aim, note that, by (
7.8
)–(
7.10
), one can write

\displaystyle\|z^{n}(f\circ\varphi)^{(n)}(z)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\|z^{n}(f^{(k)}\circ\varphi)(z)(\varphi^{\prime}(z))^{m_{k_{1}}}(\varphi^{(2)}(z))^{m_{k_{2}}}\dots(\varphi^{(n)}(z))^{m_{k_{n}}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\|(f\circ\varphi)^{(k)}(z)\left(\frac{z\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right)^{m_{k_{1}}}\dots\left(\frac{z^{n}\varphi^{(n)}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right)^{m_{k_{n}}}(\varphi(z))^{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\frac{z^{j}\varphi^{(j)}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right\|_{\infty}^{m_{k_{j}}}\|(\varphi(z))^{k}(f\circ\varphi)^{(k)}(z)\|_{2}<\infty.

The quantity above is finite by conditions (
7.3
) and (
6.4
).
Indeed, given that
z^{k}f^{(k)}(z)\in H_{2}
for all
k=1,2,\dots,n
and
C_{\varphi}
is a bounded operator on
H_{2}
,
it follows that

\|C_{\varphi}(z^{k}f^{(k)}(z))\|_{2}=\|(\varphi(z))^{k}(f\circ\varphi)^{(k)}(z)\|_{2}<\infty,\qquad k=1,2,\dots,n.

The proof is over.
∎

An immediate consequence of the above theorem is the following.

Corollary 7.7
.

The Möbius maps
\mu
inducing bounded composition operators on
H^{(n)}_{2}
for
n=1,2,3,\dots
are exactly those of the form

\mu(z)=az+b,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

with
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{C}
such that
a,\Re b>0
.

Proof.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Proposition
7.4
every Möbius map
\mu
such that
C_{\mu}
is bounded on
H^{(n)}_{2}
must satisfy condition (
6.2
), and then is
\mu(z)=az+b
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
with
a>0
,
\Re b>0
; see Remark
6.3
.
Conversely, every Möbius map
\mu
of type
\mu(z)=az+b
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
with
a>0
and
\Re b>0
satisfies condition
(
7.3
) and the
n
-(BC) property (
6.4
). Thus by
Theorem (
7.6
)
C_{\mu}
is bounded on
H^{(n)}_{2}
.
∎

\varphi
of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and discuss the boundeness of the corresponding composition operators
C_{\varphi}
.

Example 7.8
.

Note that the contrapositive of Proposition
6.1
and
Proposition
7.4
state that, if
\varphi
does not induce a bounded composition operator
C_{\varphi}
on
H_{2}
(i.e.
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=\infty
), then
C_{\varphi}
cannot be bounded on
H_{2}^{(n)}
. Then one can use
[
18
, Corollary 2.2 and Example 2.4]
to deduce that neither a
bounded mapping
\varphi
nor the map
\varphi(z)=\sqrt{z}
can induce a bounded composition operator on

H^{(n)}_{2}
.

Example 7.9
.

Let
r
be a rational map like in Example
6.5
(a). Then the operator
C_{r}
is bounded on
H_{2}
(
[
7
, Corollary 17]
). By Theorem
7.6
,
C_{r}
is also bounded on
H_{2}^{{(n)}}
for
n\geq 1
.
Similarly, the theorem can be applied to maps
\rho
of
QLP(\mathbb{C}^{+})
in Example
6.5
(b). In particular the map

\varphi(z)=az+b\sqrt{z}+c,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

with
a,b>0
and
\Re c\geq 0
, induces a bounded composition operator on

for
n\geq 1
.
The case
n=0
was proved in
[
18
, Example 2.9]
.

As a last example, let
\varphi
be given by
\varphi(z)=az+b\log(1+z)
with

as in Example

(c).
Then the operator

is bounded on

for

.

8.
Concluding remarks

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{C}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}^{+}
be an analytic function and let
C_{\varphi}
be the composition operator of symbol
\varphi
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have shown that that the finiteness condition (
6.2
), i. e.,

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\frac{|w|}{|\varphi(w)|}<\infty,

is necessary for
C_{\varphi}
to be bounded on
H_{2}^{(n)}
.

Question 8.1
.

Let
n
be a natural number. Is (
6.2
) also sufficient in order to get
C_{\varphi}
bounded
on
H_{2}^{(n)}
?

Remark 8.2
.

Since condition (
6.2
) is independent of
n
, if the above question
has a positive answer then certainly a map
\varphi
simultaneously induces bounded composition operators on all spaces
H_{2}^{(n)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Nonetheless, since spaces
H_{2}^{(n)}
involve derivatives significantly, it sounds natural –even more if the answer to Question 8.1 is negative– to raise some questions about

\varphi
and its derivatives.

On account of Theorem

7.6
, where condition (
6.4
),

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}}\left|z^{k}\frac{\varphi^{(k)}(z)}{\varphi(z)}\right|<\infty,\quad k=1,2,3,\dots,n,

is considered, we pose also the following.

Question 8.3
.

Let
n
be a natural number. Does an analytic selfmap of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
induce a bounded composition operator on
H_{2}^{(n)}
if and only if (
6.4
) holds true ? If the answer were positive then condition
(
6.4
) would imply condition (
7.3
), which looks not very likely. So it is possible that we have to include condition (
7.3
) in the question.

Remark 8.4
.

Finally, it seems natural giving connections between composition operators acting on spaces

H_{2}^{(n)}
and composition operators on weighted subspaces of
H_{2}(\mathbb{D})
, using
Proposition
4.1
for
n=1
and what it is noted after that proposition, for
n=2
.
