Hirota, Fay and Geometry
B. Eynard

Abstract




Abstract

This is a review of the relationship between Fay identities and Hirota equations in integrable systems, reformulated in a geometric language compatible with recent Topological Recursion formalism.
We write Hirota equations as trans-series, and Fay identities as spinor functional relations. We also recall several constructions of how some solutions to Fay/Hirota equations can be built from Riemann surface geometry.

1
Introduction: Tau functions and Hirota

Tau functions are central to the theory of integrable systems, we shall refer to
[
BBT03
,
HB21
,
AHH23
]
for further references on integrable systems and Tau functions.

Tau functions are usually presented as functions of an infinite set of variables and are solutions of an infinite set of non-linear partial differential equations.

The most famous are “KdV”, “KP” and “Toda” Tau functions.

The set of equations that they satisfy is called “Hirota equations”, and can be rephrased as Fay identities.

In the form of Fay identities, the Hirota equations become equations for germs of functions of a finite number of complex variables.

It is thus natural to look for solutions of Hirota/Fay equations where the complex variables are actually local coordinates on a Riemann surface, and thus associate Tau functions to Riemann surfaces.

We recall here some Riemannian geometric solutions of Hirota/Fay identities.

Outline:

Section 1
is a brief introduction to Tau functions, Sato vectors and Hirota equations in the formalism of Hirota, Sato, Segal-Wilson, and others.

Section 2
: in the usual Hirota equations, Tau functions are formal series and are multiplied by exponentials of negative powers, and Hirota equations involve a residue i.e. an integral with a differential form
d\xi
. It is thus natural to reformulate Tau functions including exponentials and differentials, as spinor trans-series.

Section 3
: we recall how Hirota equations lead to Fay identities. In the sense of formal series this would be equivalent, but Fay identities can be extended beyond formal series.

Section 4
: Fay identities are equations relating germs of analytic functions, and it is thus natural to view them as equations of analytic functions on a Riemann surface.
In other words one can look for solutions of Fay identities in the world of Riemann surfaces.
Section 4
is a brief summary of the geometry of Riemann surfaces.

Section 5
: we show one classical solution of Fay identities on Riemann surfaces, using Theta functions, this is the famous “reconstruction formula” for integrable systems.

Section 6
: we show another solution of Fay identities on a moduli space of spectral curves (Riemann surface with an immersion into a cotangent space), as an asymptotic formula using “Topological Recursion”.

Section 7
: we mention other solutions, and particularly the “matrix integrals”.

Section 8
: we gather a number of concluding remarks.

1.1
Tau functions as graded formal series

A now standard formulation of Tau functions, was introduced by Sato, Miwa, Jimbo and the Japanese school
[
JM83
,
Sat83
,
Str83
,
JMU81
,
JM81
,
JM81a
]
,
and in another point of view by Segal and Wilson
[
SW85
]
.
Here we stay closer to the formulation of Sato, which we present here as a
formal
Tau function that depends on an infinite set of graded coordinates called “times”

\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\dots)\qquad,\quad\deg t_{k}=k.

(1-1)

In the formulation of Sato and Segal-Wilson, the times are coordinates on an Abelian group
\Gamma
of transformations acting on an infinite dimensional Grassmannian, but we shall not need these notions here.

The Tau function
(we denote it
\hat{\cal T}
to distinguish from the slightly renormalized version
{\cal T}
that we shall consider below)

\hat{\cal T}(\mathbf{t})\in{\mathbb{C}}[[\Gamma]]={\mathbb{C}}[[t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\dots]],

(1-2)

is a formal graded series, whose truncation to any degree is a polynomial in the times, and which obeys an infinite set of partial differential or functional equations that we shall review below.

Definition 1.1
(Sato’s vector)

Let
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}
, define the following vector (called after Sato):

[\xi]:=(\xi,\xi^{2},\xi^{3},\xi^{4},\dots)\in\Gamma.

(1-3)

In the filtration,
\xi
has degree
1
.

Remark 1.1

Here we slightly change the usual Sato’s normalization of times, by multiplying by
k
. Our times are

t_{k}=k\times\text{Sato's time }t_{k}.

(1-4)

This multiplication by
k
is useful for the comparison with TR (Topological Recursion) formalism, and is necessary to match the times with the canonical Darboux coordinates in the space of generalized homology cycles
[
Eyn23
]
.

Definition 1.2
(Divisors)

A divisor
D
is a set of distinct points
z_{1},\dots,z_{\ell}
of the complex plane
z_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, with some respective weights
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Weights are also called “charge”.
It is often denoted (this is merely a notation) as a weighted sum of points:

D:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}\ .

(1-5)

The sum of weigths, the total charge, is called the degree of the divisor:

\deg D:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.

(1-6)

The support of the divisor is the set of points with non-zero weight:

\operatorname{supp}D:=\{z_{i}\ |\ \alpha_{i}\neq 0\}.

(1-7)

The set of divisors is a module by addition and scalar multiplication of the weights.

A divisor
D
is said
neutral
if
\deg D=0
.
A divisor is said
integer
if weights are integers, and is
unitary
if weights are
\pm 1
.
A divisor is said
positive
or
bosonic
(resp.
negative
or
fermionic
) if all weights are positive (resp. negative).
A divisor is said
supsersymmetric
if it is of degree
0
and all weights are
\pm 1
. A supersymmetric divisor must have an even number of points, half of them have weight
+1
(bosons) and half of them have weight
-1
(fermions).

Definition 1.3
(Sato’s vector for divisors)

We associate a Sato vector to a divisor
D=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}
, equal to the weighted linear combination of Sato vectors of its points:

[D]=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}[z_{i}]=(D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},\dots)\in\Gamma\quad\text{where}\quad D_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}z_{i}^{k}.

(1-8)

It is convenient to think of
D
as a diagonal matrix with eigenvalues
z_{i}
and multiplicities
\alpha_{i}
(
\alpha_{i}<0
means “super-matrix” with “fermionic” eigenvalues).
With the diagonal matrix notation we have:

D_{k}={\operatorname{Tr}}D^{k}.

(1-9)

Remark 1.2

[
Screening:
Divisors of degree 0]
Remark that if
z=0
, then the Sato vector
[z]=(0,0,0,\dots)
is the null vector in
\Gamma
.
Therefore if a divisor
D=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}[z_{i}]
has a non-zero degree,
\deg D\neq 0
, we can always suplement it by appending to it the point

z_{0}=0,\hskip 18.49988pt\alpha_{0}=-\deg D.

We get a new divisor whose degree is 0

\tilde{D}=D+\alpha_{0}.0,\hskip 9.24994pt\deg\tilde{D}=0

(1-10)

and such that

\forall\ k>0\ \hskip 18.49988pt\tilde{D}_{k}=D_{k}={\operatorname{Tr}}\tilde{D}^{k}={\operatorname{Tr}}D^{k},

(1-11)

i.e. as a vector in
\Gamma

[\tilde{D}]=(D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},\dots)=[D].

(1-12)

In other words it is not restrictive to require that a divisor has degree 0, it can be completed by the point
0
with the complementary weight. This is called “screening charge”.

1.2
Hirota Equation

Hirota equation is a set of non-linear PDEs satisfied by
\hat{\cal T}
, and gathered into a bilinear relation that generates all of them:

Definition 1.4
(Hirota Equation)

\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})
is said to satisfy Hirota equation iff for all

\ell
a positive integer, and all
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
a
\ell
-upple, we have

\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{\xi\to 0}[u_{1}^{\mu_{1}}u_{2}^{\mu_{2}}\dots u_{\ell}^{\mu_{\ell}}]\left(\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}+\vec{u}+[\xi])\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}-\vec{u}-[\xi])e^{-2\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{u_{k}}{k}\xi^{-k}}\right)\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}=0.

(1-13)

The notation
[u^{\mu}](.)=[u_{1}^{\mu_{1}}u_{2}^{\mu_{2}}\dots u_{\ell}^{\mu_{\ell}}]\left(.\right)
means that we first expand the parenthesis as a power series of the
u_{k}
s and extract the coefficient of the monomial
u_{1}^{\mu_{1}}u_{2}^{\mu_{2}}\dots u_{\ell}^{\mu_{\ell}}
, and then we compute the residue at
\xi\to 0
.

This is well defined, because the exponential is equal to its Taylor series in the
u_{k}
s (absolutely convergent moreover), and since
\hat{\cal T}
is a graded formal series, for a given
\ell
-upple
\mu
, there is at most a finite number of terms contributing to the monomial
[u^{\mu}]
. The expression is then a polynomial of
\xi^{-1}
, times a formal power series of
\xi
, therefore its residue is well defined (the coefficient of
\xi^{-1}
).

In fact
Hirota
introduced it, because he had realized that this equation was common to almost all integrable systems known at that time:

Theorem 1.1
(Hirota)

The Tau functions of the KdV, KP, Toda hierarchies satisfy the Hirota equation.

For the proof we refer to the litterature

[
Hir71
,
Hir81
,
BBT03
,
HB21
,
AHH23
]
.

1.2.1
Turning Hirota to PDEs

Let us review
[
BBT03
,
HB21
,
AHH23
]
how Hirota equation yields an infinite set of non-linear PDEs.

We have, in the sense of formal series in
{\mathbb{C}}[[\xi,u_{1},u_{2},\dots]]
, the Taylor formula:

\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}+\vec{u}+[\xi])=e^{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(u_{k}+\xi^{k})\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}).

(1-14)

Since Hirota equation is bilinear and involves two Tau functions, some derivatives will act onto the 1st Tau function, and some onto the 2nd.
We shall write one Tau function at the left, the other at the right, and define two operators
\overset{\leftarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}
and
\overset{\rightarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}
.
We also introduce the differential bi-operator :

\mathcal{D}_{k}:=\overset{\leftarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}-\overset{\rightarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}.

(1-15)

This means that in
{\mathbb{C}}[[\xi,u_{1},u_{2},\dots]]

\displaystyle\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}+\vec{u}+[\xi])\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}-\vec{u}-[\xi])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})e^{\sum_{k}(u_{k}+\xi^{k})\overset{\leftarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}}e^{-\sum_{k}(u_{k}+\xi^{k})\overset{\rightarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}}\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})

(1-16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})e^{\sum_{k}(u_{k}+\xi^{k})D_{k}}\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}).

(1-17)

Hirota equations can then be written as follows

Theorem 1.2
(Hirota as PDEs)

For all
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{\ell})

\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})\ \mathcal{D}_{\mu}\ \hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})=0

(1-18)

where
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
is a differential bi-operator defined as

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=\left(\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{\xi\to 0}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}}\ e^{\sum_{k}\xi^{k}D_{k}}\ \prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{\left(\mathcal{D}_{j}-\frac{2}{j}\xi^{-j}\right)^{\mu_{j}}}{\mu_{j}!}\right).

(1-19)

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
is a polynomial of
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\mathcal{D}_{3},\dots
of graded homogeneous degree
1+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}i\mu_{i}
.
More explicitely:

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=\sum_{\sum_{i}im_{i}+\sum_{j}j\alpha_{j}=1+\sum_{j}j\mu_{j}}\ \ \prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(\frac{-2}{j}\right)^{\mu_{j}-\alpha_{j}}\ \frac{\mathcal{D}_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}{\alpha_{j}!(\mu_{j}-\alpha_{j})!}\ \prod_{i\geq 1}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{m_{i}}}{m_{i}!}

(1-20)

Remark 1.3

Remark that
\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})\ \mathcal{D}_{\mu}\ \hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})
is symmetric in the exchange of the
{\cal T}
at the left and at the right.
This implies that
\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})\ \mathcal{D}_{\mu}\ \hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})
is unchanged if we change each
\mathcal{D}_{k}\to-\mathcal{D}_{k}
.
In other words, we may keep in
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
only the terms that are even in the change
\mathcal{D}_{k}\to-\mathcal{D}_{k}
.
Any odd polynomial of
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\mathcal{D}_{3},\dots
will trivially gives 0 in the equation
\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})\ \mathcal{D}_{\mu}\ \hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})
.

Example 1.1

\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,0,\dots)}=\mathcal{D}_{1},\quad\mathcal{D}_{(1,0,0,\dots)}=-2\mathcal{D}_{2},\quad\mathcal{D}_{(0,1,0,\dots)}=-\frac{1}{6}\mathcal{D}_{1}^{3}-\mathcal{D}_{3}

(1-21)

\mathcal{D}_{(2,0,0,\dots)}=-\frac{1}{6}\mathcal{D}_{1}^{3}+2\mathcal{D}_{3},\quad\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,1,0,\dots)}=-\frac{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{4}}{36}-\frac{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{2}}{3}+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{3}}{3}-\frac{\mathcal{D}_{2}^{2}}{3}-\frac{2\mathcal{D}_{4}}{3}

(1-22)

\mathcal{D}_{(2,1,0,\dots)}=-\frac{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{5}}{60}+\frac{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{3}}{2}-2\mathcal{D}_{5}.

(1-23)

For example,
\hat{\cal T}\mathcal{D}_{(0,0,1,0,\dots)}\hat{\cal T}=0
implies the following differential equation

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\cal T}\ \partial_{t_{2}}^{2}\hat{\cal T}-\partial_{t_{2}}\hat{\cal T}\ \partial_{t_{2}}\hat{\cal T}

(1-26)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12}\Big(\hat{\cal T}\ \partial{t_{1}}^{4}\hat{\cal T}-4\partial_{t_{1}}\hat{\cal T}\ \partial{t_{1}}^{3}\hat{\cal T}+3\partial_{t_{1}}^{2}\hat{\cal T}\ \partial{t_{1}}^{2}\hat{\cal T}\Big)

If we write

Insertion operator

Definition 1.5
(Insertion operator (perturbative))

We define

\Delta_{\xi}:=d\xi\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\xi^{k-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}=d\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi^{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}\right).

(1-28)

\Delta
is a 1-form valued differential operator.
As defined here, it acts on the space
{\mathbb{C}}[[\Gamma]]
of graded formal series of the times, and results in a formal series of
\xi

d\xi
:

\Delta:{\mathbb{C}}[[t_{1},t_{2},\dots]]\to{\mathbb{C}}[[t_{1},t_{2},\dots]][[\xi]]d\xi.

(1-29)

Lemma 1.1
(Insertion operator and shifted times)

For any
f(\vec{t})\in{\mathbb{C}}[[\Gamma]]
, we have (in the sense of formal graded series)

f(\vec{t}+\alpha[\xi])=e^{\alpha\int_{0}^{\xi}\Delta}f(\vec{t}),

(1-30)

\Delta_{\xi}f(\vec{t})=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}d\xi\ \frac{d}{d\xi}f(\vec{t}+\alpha[\xi])\right).

(1-31)

f(\vec{t}+\alpha[\xi]-\alpha[\tilde{\xi}])=e^{\alpha\int_{\tilde{\xi}}^{\xi}\Delta}f(\vec{t}),

(1-32)

\Delta_{\xi}f(\vec{t})=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}d\xi\ \frac{d}{d\xi}f(\vec{t}+\alpha[\xi]-\alpha[\tilde{\xi}])\right).

(1-33)

Proof:

From
Definition 1.5
we can write:

\displaystyle e^{\alpha\int_{\tilde{\xi}}^{\xi}\Delta}f(\vec{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha\left(\xi^{k}-\tilde{\xi}^{k}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}\ f(t_{1},t_{2},\dots)

(1-34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\vec{t}+\alpha[\xi]-\alpha[\tilde{\xi}]).

(1-35)

The exponentiel term, viewed as an element of

, can be written as:

e^{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha\left(\xi^{k}-\tilde{\xi}^{k}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}}f(\vec{t})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{l}}{l!}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\xi^{k}-\tilde{\xi}^{k})\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}\right)^{l}f(\vec{t}).

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}d\xi\ \frac{d}{d\xi}f(\vec{t}+\alpha[\xi]-\alpha[\tilde{\xi}])\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\xi^{k-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{k}}f(\vec{t})=\Delta_{\xi}f(\vec{t}).

2
Reformulation as spinor trans-series

2.1
Motivation

Let us make some observations:

•

A key ingredient in Hirota equation, is that a shift
\vec{t}\mapsto\vec{t}+\vec{u}+[\xi]
is understood as a formal series in
u_{k}
s and in
\xi
, computed by the Taylor series formula.

•

in Hirota equation, the Sato-shifted Tau functions always appear multiplied by an exponential, which is not a power series of
\xi
, instead it contains exponential of negative powers of
\xi
(and which are useful to produce residues).
Somehow, shifting is accompanied by an exponential term.

Formal series multiplied by exponentials of negative powers are not formal series, they are called “
trans-series
” (see
[
Eca81
,
Eca81a
,
Eca85
,
MS16
,
Sau14
]
).

•

The insertion operator
\Delta
is formally an operator of degree
0
in the filtration (indeed
\partial/\partial t_{k}
has degree
-k
while
\xi^{k}
has degree
k
), but is made of infinitely many terms.
This means that associativity and commutativity of using
e^{\alpha\int\Delta}
is not guaranteed. In some sense such operators need to be defined outside of the ring
{\mathbb{C}}[[\Gamma]]
of graded formal series, they need to take into account exponential prefactors.

•

Another observation is that Residues are defined for 1-forms, and they are invariant under change of variables only if one includes the Jacobian coming from the
d\xi
. In other words, the nature of the product of Tau functions inside the residue, should be a 1-form, which means that each factor
\hat{\cal T}
transforms as a power of
d\xi
, such that the product of the two factors transforms as
d\xi
. We shall redefine
\hat{\cal T}
to include a power of
d\xi
, this will make
\hat{\cal T}
a spinor (a differential with fractional power of
d\xi
), typically each
\hat{\cal T}
will carry a half-integer power
\sqrt{d\xi}
.

•

In actual realizations of integrable systems, for example as integrals (example matrix integrals in random matrix theory, see
Section 7.1
below), Tau functions have asymptotic expansions that often are not convergent series, they are usually factorially divergent series, and they usually have an exponential prefactor, which is not a series in
\xi
.
The good formalism to describe asymptotic expansions of exponential integrals or random matrix integrals, is again the notion of trans–series rather than series.
Moreover, the theory of resurgence
[
Eca81
,
Eca81a
,
Eca85
,
MS16
,
Sau14
]
, claims that whenever we have a divergent series, it should always be considered as trans–series.

All this suggest that the good framework for Tau functions should not be “series”, but rather “trans-series”, and moreover spinors (with a
\sqrt{d\xi}
).

Trans-series are linear combinations of series multiplied by some exponential. They have a rather different behavior than series, and in particular the Taylor formula and other operations can’t be applied naively.
This shows that Hirota equations should better be reformulated in
spinor trans-series
rather than series.

2.2
Tau function as trans-series

Definition 2.1
(Tau function)

We define a Tau function
{\cal T}
by the following functional properties:
we say that
{\cal T}
is a “Tau function” iff:

•

{\cal T}:=e^{F}\hat{\cal T}

(2-1)

where
\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})\in{\mathbb{C}}[[\Gamma]]
is a graded formal series, and
F
is a function on
\Gamma
homogeneous of degree 2:

F(\lambda\vec{t})=\lambda^{2}F(\vec{t}),

(2-2)

(but
F\notin{\mathbb{C}}[[\Gamma]]
),

•

for every divisor
D=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}
of degree
\deg D=0
:

{\cal T}(\vec{t}+[D])={\cal T}(\vec{t})e^{-\sum_{k\geq 1}t_{k}\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\frac{z_{i}^{-k}}{k}}\prod_{i<j}E(z_{i},z_{j})^{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}\ \frac{\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}+[D])}{\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t})}

(2-3)

where
E(z_{i},z_{j})
is the Fay’s prime form
[
Fay73
]
:

E(z,z^{\prime}):=\frac{z-z^{\prime}}{\sqrt{dzdz^{\prime}}},

(2-4)

and where
\hat{\cal T}(\vec{t}+[D])

Lemma 2.1
(Residues of formal series)

If

\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{0}\frac{d\xi}{\xi}f(\xi)\ (1-z/\xi)^{-1}=f(z)\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{C}}[[z]]

\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{0}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{n+1}}f(\xi)\ (1-z/\xi)^{-n-1}=\frac{1}{n!}f^{(n)}(z)\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{C}}[[z]]

\displaystyle\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{0}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{n+1}}f(\xi)\ (1-z/\xi)^{-n-1}

\displaystyle\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{0}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{n+1}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{f_{j}}{j!}\xi^{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(n+k)!}{k!n!}\frac{z^{k}}{\xi^{k}}\right)

(2-84)

3.2
Conclusion on formal
{\cal T}
-functions

Here we recalled how Hirota equations are equivalent to Fay identities in the case of formal series times exponentials.

Let us summarize the main points:

•

{\cal T}
function here was a formal series of times, multiplied by an exponential factor, that is a single trans-series, or also called a trans-monomial.

•

Hirota equations are an infinite set of compatible partial differential equations. We insist that this requires infinitely many variable times: the Tau function has to be defined on an Abelian group
\Gamma
(which is a group acting on a Grassmannian in the Sato Segal-Wilson formalism), or more generally on an infinite dimensional space for which times are local coordinates.

•

Fay identities, are expressed as relations between function of
2n
complex variables
(\xi_{i},\tilde{\xi}_{i})_{i=1,\dots n}
rather than times. Somehow we have traded an infinite-time-dependence to functions of a finite number of complex variables.

The points
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n},\tilde{\xi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\xi}_{n}
are complex variables in which we take residues. Residue is a notion attached to complex variables, i.e. living on a Riemann surface.

•

This justifies that we are going to extend the present formalism to the case where
\xi_{i},\tilde{\xi}_{i}
s belong to a Riemann surface. And Fay identities are then functional relations among functions on a Riemann surface at fixed times. This doesn’t need to have a “space” of times, and this doesn’t need series expansions.

•

We are thus going to enlarge the context of Fay identities to genuine functions on a Riemann surface, rather than series, trans-monomials or transseries (trans-series are linear combinations of trans-monomials).

•

In some sense it is wrong to say that Hirota
\Leftrightarrow
Fay, because Fay is more general, it makes sense even when we don’t have series times exponentials. Hirota is somehow only the Taylor expansion, i.e. the germ of Fay.

4
Tau functions and Riemann Surfaces

4.1
Introduction and motivation

The goal is to exhibit some solutions of Fay identities.
This means exhibiting a space
\Gamma
with some coordinates
\vec{t}
, and some function
{\cal T}
on
\Gamma
satisfying Hirota equations, or equivalently Fay identities.

Fay identities are relations among
{\cal T}
evaluated at times Sato–shifted by some
\xi_{i}
s.
Each
\xi_{i}
is a complex variable, and can be viewed as a local coordinate in a chart of a Riemann surface, so that
{\cal T}(\vec{t}+[\xi])
is an analytic function on the Riemann surface.

Punctures are defined as the places where
{\cal T}(\vec{t}+[\xi])
is not analytic, this is related to the exponentials of negative powers.
This means by definition, that
{\cal T}(\vec{t}+[\xi])
should be analytic elsewhere, on the whole pointed surface without the punctures. So it is natural to associate a Tau functions to Riemann surfaces. There are several classical ways of associating a space
\Gamma
to Riemann surfaces, and finding a Tau function on it.

Solutions presented here:

In each case, the “times” are local coordinates in a space
\Gamma
.

1.

Isospectral Tau functions (
Section 5
below):
here we shall consider
\Gamma
to be the space of meromorphic 1-forms on a fixed Riemann surface
{\Sigma}
.
The times are “moments”: if
\Omega\in\Gamma
is a meromorphic 1-form times are
t_{k}(\Omega)=\underset{0}{\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}}\xi^{k}\Omega
.
The Sato vector
[\xi]
corresponds to a meromorphic 1-form
\Omega_{\xi}
having a simple pole at position
\xi
, with residue 1. For a divisor
D
, the Sato vector corresponds to a meromorphic 1-form
\Omega_{D}
with simple poles at
\operatorname{supp}D
with residues = weights. Fay identities are then relations between analytic functions on the Riemann surface.
The solutions of Fay identities in this case are known explicitely, they are expressed with Theta-functions.
This is also called the “reconstruction formula” or “finite-gap solution”, explicited in
Section 5
below.

2.

Isomonodromic Tau functions (
Section 6
below):
Here we shall consider
\Gamma
to be a moduli space of “spectral curves”. A spectral curve is a Riemann surface
{\Sigma}
equipped with an immersion into a cotangent space
{\Sigma}\hookrightarrow T^{*}\underline{{\Sigma}}
,
z\mapsto(x,y)
.
Times are moments
t_{k}=\underset{0}{\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}}\xi^{k}ydx
.
A Sato-shifted spectral curve by a Sato vector
[\xi]
corresponds to a spectral curve whose immersion
y
has a simple pole at position
\xi
with residue 1, and more generally for a divisor
D
the Sato-shifted spectral curve by the Sato vector
[D]
corresponds to a spectral curve whose immersion
y
has poles at
\operatorname{supp}D
with residues = weights.

The solutions of Fay identities in this case are called “isomonodromic Tau functions”.

Small deformations of spectral curves are meromorphic 1-forms
\Omega=\delta(ydx)
, i.e. the tangent space of the moduli space of spectral curve is the space of meromorphic 1-forms.
This means that isospectral Tau functions can be recovered as an “infinitesimal” limit of isomonodromic Tau functions.

This Tau function of spectral curves is developed in
Section 6
below.

4.2
Riemann surfaces

Let us first introduce some geometry of Riemann surfaces.

Let
\underline{{\Sigma}}={\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
, we call it the “base” curve. The base curve may be any Riemann surface but, in this section, for simplicity we restrict to
\underline{{\Sigma}}=
Riemann sphere =
{\mathbb{C}}P^{1}
=
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Definition 4.1
(Ramified cover)

Let
{\Sigma}
a compact Riemann surface of some genus
{\mathfrak{g}}
, equipped with a holomorphic ramified covering map

{\rm x}:{\Sigma}\to\underline{{\Sigma}}.

(4-1)

Definition 4.2
(Canonical local coordinates)

At each point
p
of
{\Sigma}
, we define
x_{p}={\rm x}(p)
if
{\rm x}(p)\neq\infty
and
x_{p}=0
if
{\rm x}(p)=\infty
.
Let

a_{p}:={\operatorname{index}}_{p}({\rm x}-x_{p}).

(4-2)

If
{\rm x}(p)\neq\infty
, we have
a_{p}>0
, and the canonical local coordinate is
\xi_{p}=({\rm x}-x_{p})^{\frac{1}{a_{p}}}
.

If
{\rm x}(p)=\infty
, we have
a_{p}<0
, and the canonical local coordinate is
\xi_{p}={\rm x}^{\frac{1}{a_{p}}}={\rm x}^{-\frac{1}{|a_{p}|}}
.

In both cases

{\rm x}=x_{p}+\xi_{p}^{a_{p}}.

(4-3)

Remark that if
p
is a ramification point, we have
|a_{p}|\geq 2
, and thus
\xi_{p}=({\rm x}-x_{p})^{1/a_{p}}
is ambiguous.
It is defined up to a root of unity, or alternatively, there are
|a_{p}|
possible different choices for
\xi_{p}
:

\xi_{p,(k)}=\rho_{a_{p}}^{k}\xi_{p}\ ,\ k=1,\dots,|a_{p}|\ ,\qquad\text{where}\ \rho_{n}=e^{\frac{1}{n}2\pi i}.

(4-4)

Definition 4.3

Let

\mathfrak{M}^{1}({\Sigma})

(4-5)

the complex vector space of meromorphic 1-forms on
{\Sigma}
.
It is an infinite dimensional complex vector space.

We associate to a meromorphic 1-form
\Omega
on
{\Sigma}
a family of times.

Definition 4.4
(Times of a meromorphic 1-form)

Let
\Omega\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}({\Sigma})
a meromorphic 1-form on
{\Sigma}
.
Since
{\Sigma}
is compact,
\Omega
has a finite number of poles.

For a pole
p
of
\Omega
, we can write its polar part as

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{-1+\deg_{p}\Omega}t_{p,k}(\Omega)\xi_{p}^{-k-1}d\xi_{p}+\text{holomorphic at }p

(4-6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{p}}\sum_{k=0}^{-1+\deg_{p}\Omega}t_{p,k}(\Omega)({\rm x}-x_{p})^{-\frac{k}{a_{p}}-1}d{\rm x}+\text{holomorphic at }p.

(4-7)

The coefficients
t_{p,k}(\Omega)
of the polar part are worth

t_{p,k}(\Omega)=\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{p}\xi_{p}^{k}\ \Omega.

(4-8)

We define the infinite vectors

\vec{t}_{p}(\Omega):=(t_{p,1}(\Omega),t_{p,2}(\Omega),\dots,t_{p,-1+\deg_{p}\Omega}(\Omega),0,0,\dots).

(4-9)

Since
\Omega
is meromorphic, there can be only a finite number of non-vanishing times.

We write collectively:

\mathbf{t}(\Omega)=\{\vec{t}_{p}(\Omega)\}_{p=\text{poles of }\Omega}.

(4-10)

Remark that if
p
is at the same time a pole and a ramification point, we have several families of times, related by a root of unity:

\vec{t}_{p,(k)}(\Omega)=(\rho_{a_{p}}^{k}t_{p,1}(\Omega),\rho_{a_{p}}^{2k}t_{p,2}(\Omega),\dots,\rho_{a_{p}}^{jk}t_{p,j}(\Omega),\dots):=\rho_{a_{p}}^{k}(\vec{t}_{p}(\Omega)).

(4-11)

Remark that if
p
is not a pole of
\Omega
we have
\vec{t}_{p}(\Omega)=0
.

4.2.1
Monodromies and periods

The curve
{\Sigma}
has some genus
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
It means that there are non-contractible cycles, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}({\Sigma})
has rank
2{\mathfrak{g}}
.

Definition 4.5
(Marking)

It is possible (not unique) to find a basis of
2{\mathfrak{g}}
Jordan loops,
({\cal A}_{j},{\cal B}_{j})_{j=1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}}
that generate
\pi_{1}({\Sigma})
, and that have transverse symplectic intersections:

{\cal A}_{i}\cap{\cal B}_{j}=\delta_{i,j}\quad,\quad{\cal A}_{i}\cap{\cal A}_{j}=0\quad,\quad{\cal B}_{i}\cap{\cal B}_{j}=0,

(4-12)

for all
i,j
in
[1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}]
.

Remark 4.1

We choose Jordan loops rather than cycles (cycles are homotopy classes of Jordan loops and their linear combinations), because the exact support will matter, since we will integrate meromorphic forms that can have poles.

Definition 4.6
(Periods of a meromorphic 1-form)

Let
\Omega\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}({\Sigma})
a meromorphic 1-form.
Let us assume that the poles of
\Omega
are not on the support of our marking symplectic Jordan loops.
We define the “periods” of
\Omega
as follows:
for all
j\in[1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}]

\epsilon_{j}(\Omega):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{{\cal A}_{j}}\Omega.

(4-13)

Periods are also called 1st kind times.

Remark 4.2

If
\Omega
would have poles on the support of the Jordan loop, one should replace the integral
\oint_{{\cal A}_{j}}\Omega
by the regularized integral defined in
[
Eyn23
]
, so that all what follows below remains valid for all
\Omega
.
But here, to simplify, let us focus on the case where
\Omega
has no pole on the marked Jordan loops, and regularized integrals are just usual integrals.

Definition 4.7
(3rd kind times)

For each pole
p
of
\Omega
, let

t_{p,0}(\Omega)=\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{p}\Omega.

(4-14)

4.2.2
Canonical decomposition of meromorphic forms

Lemma 4.1
(1st kind differentials)

For each
i=1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}
, there exists a unique holomorphic 1-form on
{\Sigma}
, denoted
\omega^{\prime}_{i}
, with no pole,
and such that

\forall\ j=1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}\ ,\qquad\oint_{{\cal A}_{j}}\omega^{\prime}_{i}=\delta_{i,j}.

(4-15)

\omega^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\omega^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}
form a basis of
\Omega^{1}({\Sigma})
, the space of holomorphic 1-forms on
{\Sigma}
, it is called the basis dual to the marking symplectic Jordan loops.

Proof:

This is a classical theorem of Riemann surfaces, see
[
FK12
]

\square
.

Lemma 4.2
(3rd kind differentials)

Let
p,q
two points on
{\Sigma}
(we assume that they are not on the support of the marking Jordan loops).

There exists a unique meromorphic 1-form, denoted
\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{p,q}
, that has a simple pole at
p
of residue
+1
and a simple pole at
q
of residue
-1
and no other pole.

\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{p}\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{p,q}=1=-\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{q}\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{p,q}

(4-16)

and normalized on
{\cal A}
-cycles

\forall\ i=1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}\ ,\qquad\oint_{{\cal A}_{i}}\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{p,q}=0.

(4-17)

If
D=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.[z_{i}]
is a divisor of degree
\deg D=0
, we define

\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{D}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z_{i},o}

(4-18)

where
o
is a generic point of
{\Sigma}
, and in fact
\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{D}
is independent of it.
Remark that
\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{p,q}=\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{[p]-[q]}
.

\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{D}
is the unique meromorphic 1-form that has simple poles at the support of
D
with residues = weights and no other poles, and vanishing
{\cal A}
-cycle integrals.

Proof:

This is a classical theorem, see
[
FK12
]
.
\square

Lemma 4.3
(2nd kind differentials)

Let
p
a point on
{\Sigma}
, and
k\geq 1
(we assume that
p
is not on the support of the marking Jordan loops).

There exists a unique meromorphic 1-form, denoted
\omega^{\prime\prime}_{p,k}
, that has a pole at
p
of degree
k+1
and no other pole, and behaving like

\omega^{\prime\prime}_{p,k}=\xi_{p}^{-k-1}d\xi_{p}+\text{holomorphic at }p,

(4-19)

and normalized on
{\cal A}
-cycles

\forall\ i=1,\dots,{\mathfrak{g}}\ ,\qquad\oint_{{\cal A}_{i}}\omega^{\prime\prime}_{p,k}=0.

(4-20)

Proof:

This is a classical theorem, see
[
FK12
]

\square

Lemma 4.4
(Decomposition)

Every meromorphic 1-form
\Omega
can be uniquely written

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=\text{poles of }\Omega}\sum_{k=1}^{-1+\deg_{p}\Omega}t_{p,k}(\Omega)\ \omega^{\prime\prime}_{p,k}

(4-23)

\displaystyle+\sum_{p=\text{poles of }\Omega}t_{p,0}(\Omega)\ \omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{p,o}

\displaystyle+2\pi{\rm i}\,\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathfrak{g}}}\epsilon_{i}(\Omega)\ \omega^{\prime}_{i}.

(here
o
is an arbitrary generic point of
{\Sigma}
, and the decomposition is actually independent of it).

In other words the times
\mathbf{t}(\Omega)
are the coordinates of
\Omega
in the cannonical basis of forms.

Proof:

This is a classical theorem, see
[
FK12
]
.
The main idea is that the difference between
\Omega
and the right hand side is a 1-form without poles, and with vanishing
{\cal A}_{i}
integrals, so it must vanish.

\square

4.3.1
Holomorphic forms, Riemann periods, Theta

Proposition 4.1
(Riemann)

Define the Riemann matrix of periods using the normalized holomorphic 1-forms of

\tau_{i,j}=\oint_{{\cal B}_{i}}\omega^{\prime}_{j}.

(4-24)

is a
{\mathfrak{g}}\times{\mathfrak{g}}
Siegel matrix, which means it is symmetric and its imaginary part is positive definite:

\tau=\tau^{t}\quad,\quad\Im\tau>0.

(4-25)

Proof:

This is a very classical theorem going back to Riemann
[

]
.
\square

Definition 4.8
(Abel map)

Let
o
a generic point of
{\Sigma}
fixed once for all, called the origin.
We define the Abel map:

\displaystyle{\Sigma}^{\text{universal cover}}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}

(4-26)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}(z)=(\mathfrak{a}_{1}(z),\dots,\mathfrak{a}_{\mathfrak{g}}(z))\qquad\text{where }\ \mathfrak{a}_{i}(z)=\int_{o}^{z}\omega^{\prime}_{i}.

(4-27)

They have monodromies

\mathfrak{a}_{i}(z+{\cal A}_{j})=\mathfrak{a}_{i}(z)+\delta_{i,j},

(4-28)

\mathfrak{a}_{i}(z+{\cal B}_{j})=\mathfrak{a}_{i}(z)+\tau_{i,j}

(4-29)

and therefore,

\displaystyle{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}={\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}/({\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}}+\tau{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}})

(4-30)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}(z)\ \mod{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}}+\tau{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}}

(4-31)

is well defined.

\mathbb{J}={\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}/({\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}}+\tau{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}})
is called the Jacobian of
{\Sigma}
.

If
D=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}
is a divisor of points of
{\Sigma}
, we define by linearity

\mathfrak{a}(D)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\mathfrak{a}(z_{i}).

(4-32)

If
\deg D=0
,
\mathfrak{a}(D)
is independent of the origin
o
.

Definition 4.9
(Riemann Theta function)

Let
\tau
a Siegel matrix.
We define the function

\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}

(4-33)

\displaystyle u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{\mathfrak{g}})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\Theta(u)=\Theta(u;\tau)=:\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}}}e^{2\pi{\rm i}\,(u,\mathbf{n})}\ e^{\pi{\rm i}\,(\mathbf{n},\tau\mathbf{n})}

(4-34)

(very often we shall consider
\tau
fixed and view
\Theta
as only a function of
u\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}
).
Thanks to
\Im\tau>0
, the series is absolutely convergent for all
u\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}
and is an anlytic entire function on
{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathfrak{g}}
.

Proposition 4.2
(Riemann)

\Theta
obeys the following properties:

\displaystyle\Theta(-u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(u)

(4-35)

\displaystyle\Theta(u+\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(u)

(4-36)

\displaystyle\Theta(u+\tau\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(u)\ e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(u,\mathbf{n})}e^{-\pi{\rm i}\,(\mathbf{n},\tau\mathbf{n})}

(4-37)

\displaystyle\Theta(u+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{n}+\frac{1}{2}\tau\mathbf{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\qquad\text{iff}\ (\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1

(4-38)

Definition 4.10
(Half-integer characteristic)

Let
\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathfrak{g}}
.

\chi:=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{n}+\frac{1}{2}\tau\mathbf{m}

(4-39)

is called a half-integer characteristics.

It is called odd iff
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
.

It is called non-singular iff the vector
\Theta^{\prime}(\chi)=(\Theta^{\prime}_{1}(\chi),\dots,\Theta^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\chi))\neq 0
.

It is known that there always exists some non-singular odd half-integer characteristic.

From now on, let us choose one of them, fixed once for all.
Let us define the following holomorphic 1-form

h_{\chi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}\Theta^{\prime}_{i}(\chi)\omega_{i}.

(4-40)

It is well known that
h_{\chi}
is a holomorphic 1-form, that has
{\mathfrak{g}}-1
double zeros on
{\Sigma}
.

4.3.2
Fay’s prime forms

Definition 4.11
(Fay’s prime form)

Let

E(z_{1},z_{2}):=\frac{\Theta(\mathfrak{a}(z_{1})-\mathfrak{a}(z_{2})+\chi)}{\sqrt{h_{\chi}(z_{1})\ h_{\chi}(z_{2})}}.

(4-41)

If
D=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}
is a divisor, we define

E(D):=\prod_{i<j}E(z_{i},z_{j})^{-\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}.

(4-42)

Remark:

E(z_{1},z_{2})=E([z_{1}]-[z_{2}]).

(4-43)

Proposition 4.3
(Fay)

E(z_{1},z_{2})
is locally analytic of
z_{1}
(resp.
z_{2}
), or more precisely is analytic on the universal cover. In particular it has no poles (the
{\mathfrak{g}}-1
doubles zeros of
h_{\chi}
happen to be exactly
{\mathfrak{g}}-1
zeros of
\Theta
in the numerator).
It vanishes only at
z_{1}=z_{2}
and vanishes linearly.
In any local coordinate
\xi_{i}=\xi(z_{i})
it behaves as

E(z_{1},z_{2})\mathop{\sim}_{z_{1}\to z_{2}}\frac{\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}}{\sqrt{d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}}}\ (1+O(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})).

(4-44)

Definition 4.12
(Szegö kernel)

Let
\Omega
a meromorphic 1-form on
{\Sigma}
.
Let

\zeta(\Omega)=(\zeta_{1}(\Omega),\dots,\zeta_{\mathfrak{g}}(\Omega)):=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}\,}\oint_{{\cal B}-\tau{\cal A}}\Omega,

(4-45)

\text{i.e.}\qquad\zeta_{i}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}\,}\left(\oint_{{\cal B}_{i}}\Omega-\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}\tau_{i,j}\oint_{{\cal A}_{j}}\Omega\right).

(4-46)

Let the Szegö kernel

\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega):=\frac{\Theta(\zeta(\Omega)+\mathfrak{a}(z_{1})-\mathfrak{a}(z_{2})+\chi)}{E(z_{1},z_{2})\Theta(\zeta(\Omega)+\chi)}\ e^{\int_{z_{2}}^{z_{1}}\Omega}\ e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(\epsilon(\Omega),\mathfrak{a}(z_{1})-\mathfrak{a}(z_{2}))}.

(4-47)

More generally, for a divisor
D
of degree 0:

\psi(D;\Omega)=\frac{\Theta(\zeta(\Omega)+\mathfrak{a}(D)+\chi)}{E(D)\ \Theta(\zeta(\Omega)+\chi)}e^{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\int_{o}^{z_{i}}\Omega}e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(\epsilon(\Omega),\mathfrak{a}(D))}.

(4-48)

Lemma 4.5

If
\Omega
has poles with integer residues, then the Szegö kernel has no monodromies:

\psi([z_{1}+\gamma]-[z_{2}];\Omega)=\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)=\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}+\gamma];\Omega)

(4-49)

for all cycle
\gamma
.

Proof:

\bullet
If
\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}n_{i}{\cal A}_{i}
we have

\mathfrak{a}(z_{1}+\gamma)=\mathfrak{a}(z_{1})+\mathbf{n}\qquad\text{ where }\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{\mathfrak{g}})

(4-50)

and
\Theta(u+\mathbf{n})=\Theta(u)
, this implies

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}+\gamma]-[z_{2}];\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)\ e^{\int_{\gamma}\Omega}\ e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(\epsilon(\Omega),\mathbf{n})}

(4-51)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega),

(4-52)

because

\int_{\gamma}\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}n_{i}\oint_{{\cal A}_{i}}\Omega=2\pi{\rm i}\,\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}n_{i}\epsilon_{i}(\Omega)=2\pi{\rm i}\,(\epsilon(\Omega),\mathbf{n}).

(4-53)

\bullet
If
\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}n_{i}{\cal B}_{i}
we have

\mathfrak{a}(z_{1}+\gamma)=\mathfrak{a}(z_{1})+\tau\mathbf{n}\qquad\text{ where }\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{\mathfrak{g}})

(4-54)

and
\Theta(u+\tau\mathbf{n})=\Theta(u)e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,((u,\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{n},\tau\mathbf{n}))}
, this implies

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}+\gamma]-[z_{2}];\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)\ \frac{e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,((\zeta(\Omega)+\mathfrak{a}(D)+\chi,\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{n},\tau\mathbf{n}))}}{e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,((\mathfrak{a}(D)+\chi,\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{n},\tau\mathbf{n}))}}\ e^{\int_{\gamma}\Omega}\ e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(\epsilon(\Omega),\tau\mathbf{n})}

(4-55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)\ e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(\zeta(\Omega),\mathbf{n})}\ e^{\int_{\gamma}\Omega}\ e^{-2\pi{\rm i}\,(\epsilon(\Omega),\tau\mathbf{n})}

(4-56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)

(4-57)

because

\int_{\gamma}\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{g}}n_{i}\oint_{{\cal B}_{i}}\Omega=2\pi{\rm i}\,(\zeta(\Omega)+\tau\epsilon(\Omega),\mathbf{n}).

(4-58)

\bullet
If
\gamma
is a small circle around a pole
p
of
\Omega
with residue
\mathop{\,\rm Res\,}_{p}\Omega=t_{p,0}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}+\gamma]-[z_{2}];\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)\ e^{2\pi{\rm i}\,t_{p,0}(\Omega)}

(4-59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi([z_{1}]-[z_{2}];\Omega)

(4-60)

because we assumed
t_{p,0}(\Omega)\in\mathbb{Z}
.

\square

5
1st construction:
\Gamma=
space of meromorphic forms

Here we present the so called “Theta-function” solution of Fay identities, also called the finite-gap Tau function or Isospectral Tau function, or reconstruction formula.

Let
{\Sigma}
a compact Riemann surface of genus
{\mathfrak{g}}
, equipped with a Jordan loops marking, and with a degree
d
covering map
{\rm x}:{\Sigma}\to\underline{{\Sigma}}
.

The Abelian space
\Gamma
considered in this 1st construction is the vector space of meromorphic 1-forms on
{\Sigma}
, modulo 1st kind and 3rd kind forms.

Definition 5.1

Let
\mathcal{P}
a finite set of points of
{\Sigma}
, called the “poles” (chosen out of the support of Jordan loops markings).
Let

\mathfrak{M}^{1}_{\mathcal{P}}({\Sigma}):=\{\Omega\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}({\Sigma})\ |\ \Omega\text{ has poles only at }\mathcal{P}\text{ or no pole}\}.

(5-1)

\mathfrak{M}^{1}_{\mathcal{P}}({\Sigma})^{\prime\prime\prime}:=\{\Omega\in\mathfrak{M}^{1}({\Sigma})\ |\ \Omega\text{ has only simple poles at }\mathcal{P}\text{ or no pole}\}.

(5-2)

\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathfrak{M}^{1}_{\mathcal{P}}({\Sigma})/\mathfrak{M}^{1}_{\mathcal{P}}({\Sigma})^{\prime\prime\prime}.

(5-3)

With the time map on
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}}
:

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\{t_{p,k}(\Omega)\}_{p\in\mathcal{P},\ k\geq 1}.

(5-4)

For any
\Omega
, only finitely many times are non-zero.
Times are coordinates on
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proposition 5.1
(Preimage of the Sato vector)

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
U_{p}\subset{\Sigma}
an open simply connected neighborhood of
p
in which the canonical local coordinate
\xi_{p}
is defined.

Let
z\in{\Sigma}
.
If
z\in U_{p}
, let
\xi=\xi_{p}(z)
.

The 3rd kind differential of
Lemma 4.2
,
\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z,p}
is the preimage of the Sato vector
[\xi_{p}(z)]
.
Indeed as a formal series of
\xi
, we have

\vec{t}_{p}(\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z,p})=[\xi]=[\xi_{p}(z)].

(5-5)

If
D=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}
is a divisor whose points are in
U_{p}
, we have, as a formal series of
\xi_{p}(z_{i})s
the time map of the 3rd kind differential
\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{D}
is the Sato vector
[D]
:

\vec{t}_{p}\left(\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{D}\right)=\vec{t}_{p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z_{i},p}\right)=[D].

(5-6)

Proof:

Let
z\in U_{p}
, and consider the 1-form
\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z,p}(\tilde{z})
, it behaves as

\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z,p}(\tilde{z})\sim\frac{d\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})}{\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})-\xi_{p}(z)}-\frac{d\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})}{\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})}+\text{analytic}

(5-7)

and notice that the times will not depend on the analytic part.

If
z
is close to
p
, so that
|\xi_{p}(z)|<|\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})|
we have the Taylor expansion in
{\mathbb{C}}[[\xi_{p}(z)]]
:

\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z,p}(\tilde{z})\sim\frac{d\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})}{\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})-\xi_{p}(z)}-\frac{d\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})}{\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})}\sim\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\xi_{p}(z)^{k}}{\xi_{p}(\tilde{z})^{k+1}}\ d\xi_{p}(\tilde{z}).

(5-8)

This implies that, in the sense of formal series in
{\mathbb{C}}[[\xi_{p}(z)]]
, we have

t_{p,k}(\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}_{z,p})=\xi_{p}(z)^{k}

(5-9)

Remark 5.1

Theorem 5.1
(Fay’s Theorem “Theta functions satisfy Fay identities”)

let

Proof:

Proof:

7
Other Tau functions

We shall not be exhaustive and mention only the following example: the 1–Matrix model prototype, which is extremely useful and used with many applications from combinatorics to quantum gravity and string theory.

7.1
Matrix integrals

Typical examples of Tau functions are matrix integrals.
It is usual to consider the punctures at
\infty
and write
x=1/\xi
, i.e.
\xi=0
corresponds to
x=\infty
.

Let
\Gamma={\mathbb{C}}[x]
the vector space space of complex polynomials.
To
\vec{t}\in\Gamma
associate the polynomial, called the “potential”

V(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\deg V}\frac{t_{k}}{k}x^{k}.

(7-1)

Let
N
a positive integer and
H_{N}
the space of
N\times N
Hermitian matrices, equipped with the Lebesgue measure
dM
.
Let:

{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})=\int_{H_{N}}dMe^{-{\operatorname{Tr}}V(M)}

(7-2)

where we assume that the integral is absolutely convergent (if it is not absolutely convergent on
H_{N}
, replace
H_{N}
by
H_{N}(\gamma)
the space of
N\times N
normal matrices with eigenvalues on a Jordan arc
\gamma
, see
[
EKR15
]
).

For a divisor
D=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}.z_{i}
, the Sato shift
\vec{t}\mapsto\vec{t}+[D]
amounts to

V(x)\mapsto V(x)+\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\ln(x-z_{i}),

(7-3)

(notice that the Sato shifted potential is not a polynomial, it is a polynomial only order by order as formal series),
in other words

{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t}+[D])=\int_{H_{N}}dMe^{-{\operatorname{Tr}}V(M)}\ \prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\det(M-z_{i})^{-\alpha_{i}}.

(7-4)

In particular with the divisor
D=[\infty]-[x]
we consider

\psi_{N}(\vec{t};x)=\frac{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t}-[x]+[\infty])}{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})}=\frac{1}{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})}\int_{H_{N}}dMe^{-{\operatorname{Tr}}V(M)}\ \det(x-M),

(7-5)

and with the divisor
D=[x]-[\infty]
we consider

\phi_{N}(\vec{t};x)=\frac{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t}+[x]-[\infty])}{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})}=\frac{1}{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})}\int_{H_{N}}dMe^{-{\operatorname{Tr}}V(M)}\ \frac{1}{\det(x-M)}.

(7-6)

It is well known in the theory of random matrices
[
Kos96
]
that they satisfy an orthogonality relationship

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{n}(\vec{t};x)\phi_{m}(\vec{t};x)dx=\delta_{n,m}.

(7-7)

Moreover, it is well known
[
AP04
,
Ber03
,
Ber04
,
Ber06
]
that for supersymmetric divisors they satisfy Hirota equations

\frac{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}[z_{i}]-[\tilde{z}_{i}])}{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})}=\det\frac{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t}+[z_{i}]-[\tilde{z}_{j}])}{{\cal T}_{N}(\vec{t})}.

(7-8)

Matrix integrals do satisfy Hirota equations and Fay identities.

More generally, matrix integrals are known to be Tau functions
[
BEH06
,
BEH03
,
Har11
,
EKR15
]
.

8
Conclusion

This article presented a more modern and TR compatible reformulation of Hirota and Fay equations.

- The usual formulation of Hirota equations is with formal series, and if we multiply by the appropriate exponential factor and differential form factor, this is formulated with a spinor trans-monomial.

- In the language of formal series or trans-monomials, Hirota equations are equivalent to Fay identities.
But then Fay identities can be formulated not only as formal series, they can be extended to functions on Riemann surfaces.

- Fay identities are then naturally formulated with Riemann surfaces, and some solutions can be found in terms of Riemann surfaces.

- We recalled two solutions, one where
{\cal T}
is defined on the space of meromorphic forms on a fixed Riemann surface, and one where
{\cal T}
is defined on the moduli space of spectral curves, and is given asymptotically by topological recursion.

- In Topological Recursion, people often mention the “quantum curve conjecture” claiming that Tau functions Sato–shifted by unitary divisors should obey some ODE.
The ODE is in fact a consequence of integrability and thus the “quantum curve conjecture” is in fact a consequence of a stronger “integrability” conjecture, i.e. the fact that the Topological-Recursion Tau function eq (
6-38
) obeys Fay identities. This conjecture has been proved in number of cases but it is still to be proved for more general cases.
