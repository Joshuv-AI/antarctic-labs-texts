Abstract
Empirical best prediction (EBP) is a well-known method for producing reliable proportion estimates when the primary data source provides only small or no sample from finite populations. There are at least two potential challenges encountered in implementing the existing EBP methodology. First, one must accurately link the sample to the finite population frame. This may be a difficult or even impossible task because of absence of identifiers that can be used to link sample and the frame. Secondly, the finite population frame typically contains limited auxiliary variables, which may not be adequate for building a reasonable working predictive model. We propose a data linkage approach in which we replace the finite population frame by a big sample that does not have the outcome binary variable of interest, but has a large set of auxiliary variables. Our proposed method calls for fitting the assumed model using data from the smaller sample, imputing the outcome variable for all the units of the big sample, and then finally using these imputed values to obtain standard weighted proportion using the big sample. We develop a new adjusted maximum likelihood method to avoid estimates of model variance on the boundary encountered in the commonly used maximum likelihood estimation method. We propose an estimator of mean squared prediction error (MSPE) using a parametric bootstrap method and address computational issues by developing efficient EM algorithm. We illustrate the proposed methodology in the context of election projection for small areas.



Keywords:
Missing data; Model based imputation; Election survey; Parametric Bootstrap.

INTRODUCTION

Estimation of proportions has been a topic of great interest to statisticians for a long time. To estimate a finite population proportion from a probability sample survey, a weighted proportion is generally used, where weights assigned to the sampled units depend on the sample design. The method is robust in the sense that modeling is not needed for obtaining estimates and associated uncertainty measures and the methodology can be validated from the well-known randomization theory in finite population sampling; see
Cochran 1977
. However, when a small sample is available from the finite population, weighted proportion may yield unreasonable estimate (e.g., 0 or 1). Moreover, in such a situation, the associated standard error estimate could be misleading (e.g., 0).

The survey-weighted proportion based on a small sample can be significantly improved using small area estimation techniques that essentially combine information from multiple sources of data using a suitable statistical model. We refer to
Rao and
Molina 2015
;
Jiang 2007
;
Pfeffermann 2013
;
Ghosh 2020
, and others for a comprehensive review of small area estimation methodology and applications.

Ghosh and
Lahiri 1987
put forward an empirical Bayes small area estimation approach to estimate several strata means when a stratified simple random sample is drawn from the finite population of interest. Their approach covers binary data and thus can be applied to estimate several strata proportions. The authors treated the problem of finite population mean estimation as a prediction problem in which the outcome variable for the unsampled units are predicted using a robust model assumed for the entire finite population and data on the outcome variable from the sampled units and auxiliary variables from the unsampled units. There are several follow-up papers on estimation of small area finite population proportions; see
Rao and
Molina 2015
.

There are at least two potential challenges encountered in implementing existing methodology for obtaining empirical Bayes estimates for the finite population proportions. First, in order to implement existing methods, one must accurately link the sample to the finite population frame. This may be a difficult or even impossible task in some practical situations because the sample may not have hard identifiers in order to protect the confidentiality of the sampled units. Secondly, the finite population frame typically contains limited auxiliary variables, which may not be adequate for building a reasonable working predictive model.

In order to circumvent the two issues mentioned in the preceding paragraph, we propose a new data linkage approach. We replace the finite population frame by a big sample that, like the frame, does not have the outcome variable of interest, but has a large set of auxiliary variables useful to build a reasonable working model connecting outcome variable to the auxiliary variables, which are common to a smaller sample containing the outcome variable. Our proposed method calls for fitting the assumed model using data from the smaller sample, imputing the outcome variable for all the units of the big sample, and finally using these imputed values to obtain standard weighted proportion estimate using the big sample.

Parametric bootstrap method is a convenient way to estimate mean squared prediction errors (MSPE) of empirical best linear predictors of small area parameters; see
Butar 1997
;
Chatterjee and
Lahiri 2013
;
Chatterjee
et al. 2008
. We develop a parametric bootstrap method for estimating MSPE of our proposed estimators.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In section 2, we introduce the problem of interest and discuss point estimation. In this section, we also discuss potential working models that can be used for solving the estimation problem. In section 3, we outline our parametric bootstrap method to estimate MSPE. In section 4, we introduce an adjusted maximum likelihood method to deal with a potential problem when model parameters are estimated on the boundary. Our proposed adjusted maximum likelihood method is for a mixed logistic model. This is indeed a nontrivial extension of the adjusted maximum likelihood methods previously developed by
Lahiri and
Li 2009
;
Li and
Lahiri 2010
;
Yoshimori and
Lahiri 2014
;
Hirose and
Lahiri 2018
, and others for linear mixed models. The proposed method has a clear edge over the commonly used maximum likelihood method as implemented by
glmer
. In section 5, we describe the data used in the data analysis. In section 6, we carry out our data analysis. We demonstrate the superiority of our proposed EBP method over the direct method for predicting 2016 United States presidential election results for the 50 states and the District of Columbia. Finally, in section 7, we make some concluding remarks and direction for future research.

PROBLEM AND METHODS

Let
N_{i}
be the finite population size for the
i
th area (e.g., state in a nationwide sample survey) and
m
be number of areas of interest.
Let
Y_{ij}
be the value of the outcome variable for the
j
th unit of the
i
th area,
i=1,\cdots,m;\;j=1,\cdots,N_{i}
and our parameter of interest is

\bar{Y}_{i}=N_{i}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}Y_{ij},\;i=1,\cdots,m.

We have a small sample
\tilde{s}
of size
\tilde{n}
from the finite population that contains information on the study variable
Y
and a vector of auxiliary variables
X
related to
Y
for all units. The area sample sizes
\tilde{n}_{i}
of
\tilde{s}
are small and could even be zero for some areas. Units of
\tilde{s}
cannot be linked to the finite population units. We also have a big sample
s
of size
n
from the same finite population that does not contain information on
Y
for any unit, but contains the same vector of auxiliary variables
X
. The area sample sizes
n_{i}
of
s
are large. Units of
s
cannot be linked to the finite population. There is no or negligible overlap of units between the big sample
s
and small sample
\tilde{s}
.
Making use of the bigger survey, we define the weighted proportion of voters in area
i
as

\bar{Y}_{iw}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}w_{ij}Y_{ij},

where
Y_{ij}
is unobserved study variable for the
j
th unit of
i
th area in the big sample
s
;
w_{ij}
is known weight assigned to the
j
th unit of the
i
th area;
n_{i}
is sample size for the
i
th area
(i=1,\cdots,m;\;j=1,\cdots,n_{i}.)
We assume that
n_{i}
is large for each area so that

\bar{Y}_{i}\approx\bar{Y}_{iw},\;i=1,\cdots,m.

Under certain assumptions, such an approximation can be justified appealing to the law of large numbers since
n_{i}
’s are large for all
i
. Thus we have described how we will make use of the bigger survey to produce state level estimates, but as
\bar{Y}_{iw}
is unknown we next describe how to estimate it by combining the smaller survey with the bigger survey using a working model.

2.1
Working model for prediction

We assume a
working
model for the entire finite population and a non-informative sampling so that the population working model will hold for both
s
and
\tilde{s}
. We predict
Y_{ij}
for all units of
s
using information on both
Y
and
X
from
\tilde{s}
,
X
contained in
s
, and other state level available auxiliary variables. The working model can be fitted using
\tilde{s}
because it contains information on both
Y
and
X
for all units. For all units in
s
, we predict
Y_{ij}
by
\hat{Y}_{ij}=E(Y_{ij}|\tilde{s})
because this will minimize the mean squared prediction error (MSPE). We assume the following multi-level working model for binary response with normally distributed random effect for the finite population.

For
i=1,\cdots,m;j=1,\cdots,N_{i}
, assume

Level 1:

\displaystyle Y_{ij}|\theta_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Bernoulli}(\theta_{ij}),

Level 2:

\displaystyle\theta_{ij}=H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i}),

Level 3:

\displaystyle v_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,\sigma^{2}),

where
H(\cdot)
is a known cumulative distribution function (cdf);
\beta
is a vector of unknown fixed effects;
v_{i}
is random effect specific to the
i
th area with unknown variance component
\sigma^{2}
. A special case of the general model is mixed logistic model in which
H(\cdot)
is the cdf of a logistic distribution, i.e.,
H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})=\frac{\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}{1+\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}
.

2.2
Empirical Best Prediction (EBP) Approach

With known
\beta
and
\sigma^{2}
the best predictor (BP) of
Y_{ij}
for any unit in
s
is given by:

\hat{Y}_{ij}^{BP}\equiv\hat{Y}_{ij}^{BP}(\beta,\sigma^{2})=E(Y_{ij}|\tilde{s})=E\left[E(Y_{ij}|\tilde{s},\theta_{ij})|\tilde{s}\right]=E\left[H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\mid\tilde{s})\right],

where the expectation is with respect to the conditional distribution of
v_{i}
given
\tilde{s}.
For the mixed logistic model,

\hat{Y}_{ij}^{BP}\equiv\hat{Y}_{ij}^{BP}(\beta,\sigma^{2})=E\left[\frac{\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}{1+\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}\mid\tilde{s}\right].

In the EBP approach, we estimate
\beta
and
\sigma^{2}
by a classical method (e.g., maximum likelihood, residual likelihood, adjusted maximum likelihood). Let
(\hat{\beta},\hat{\sigma}^{2})
be an estimator of
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
. Then EBP of
Y_{ij}
for any unit in
s
is given by

\hat{Y}_{ij}^{EBP}=\hat{Y}_{ij}^{BP}(\hat{\beta},\hat{\sigma}^{2}).

and that of
\bar{Y}_{i}
is given by

\hat{\bar{Y}}_{i}^{EBP}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}w_{ij}\hat{Y}_{ij}^{EBP}
.

3
MEAN SQUARED PREDICTION ERROR USING A PARAMETRIC BOOTSTRAP

We now discuss estimation of the mean squared prediction error (MSPE) of EBP using a parametric bootstrap method. Let
\tilde{s}_{b}
denote parametric bootstrap samples for the smaller survey,
b=1,\cdots,B
. To generate these
\tilde{s}_{b}
from the original sample
\tilde{s}
we first independently generate
v_{bi}
from
N(0,\hat{\sigma}^{2})
and then compute
\theta_{bij}=H(x_{ij},\hat{\beta},v_{bi}),\;b=1,\cdots,B;\;i=1,\cdots,m;\;j=1,\cdots,\tilde{n}_{i}.
Finally, we independently generate
y_{bij}
from Bernoulli
(\theta_{bij}),\;i=1,\cdots,m;\;j=1,\cdots,\tilde{n}_{i}.
Using the
b
th parametric bootstrap sample
\tilde{s}_{b}
, we can re-estimate
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
and EBP of
Y_{ij}
. We denote them by
(\hat{\beta}_{b},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{b})
. We can then produce
\hat{Y}_{bij}^{EBP}
based on
\tilde{s}_{b}
, auxiliary variables in
s
, and area level auxiliary variables from other sources. Thus EBP of
\bar{Y}_{i}
based on the
b
th bootstrap sample
\tilde{s}_{b}
is given by

\hat{\bar{Y}}_{bi}^{EBP}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}w_{ij}\hat{Y}_{bij}^{EBP},\;b=1,\cdots,B.

For
s
, we can also generate
y_{bij},\;b=1,\cdots,B;i=1,\cdots,m;j=1,\cdots,n_{i}
and define

\bar{y}_{biw}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}w_{ij}y_{bij},\;b=1,\cdots,B;i=1,\cdots,n_{i}.

We propose the following parametric bootstrap estimator of MSPE:

\mbox{MSPE}(\hat{\bar{Y}}_{i}^{EBP})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left[\hat{\bar{Y}}_{bi}^{EBP}-\bar{y}_{biw}\right]^{2}.

Square root of
\mbox{MSPE}(\hat{\bar{Y}}_{i}^{EBP})
can be treated as the estimated standard error of EBP of
\bar{Y}_{i}
.

4
ADJUSTED MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD METHOD

For the general model defined in section
2.1
, the distribution of
Y_{ij}
given
\theta_{ij}
is given by

f(y_{ij}|\theta_{ij})=\theta_{ij}^{y_{ij}}\left(1-\theta_{ij}\right)^{(1-y_{ij})},\quad y_{ij}\in\left\{0,1\right\}.

Note that

\theta_{ij}=H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})
and

(1-\theta_{ij})=1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i}).

Now using independence we obtain the distribution of
Y_{i}=(Y_{i1},\cdots,Y_{in_{i}})^{\prime}
given
v_{i}
as

f(y_{i}|v_{i};\beta,\sigma^{2})=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[{\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{y_{ij}}{\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}\right].

The distribution of
v_{i}
given
y_{i}
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &f(v_{i}|y_{i};\beta,\sigma^{2})\propto f(y_{i}|v_{i};\beta)\times f(v_{i}|\sigma^{2})\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left({-\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[{\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{y_{ij}}{\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}\right]\\
&\propto\exp\left({-\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}^{y_{ij}}\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}\right].\end{aligned}

For this problem
y_{ij}
and
x_{ij}
are observed but the area specific random effects
\{v_{i},\;i=1\dots m\}
are unobserved (missing). Thus the complete data likelihood function of
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
, ignoring the constants, is given by

L(\beta,\sigma^{2}|y,x,v)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[{\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{y_{ij}}{\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left({-\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)\right].

For ease of computation, we express log-likelihood as sum of separate functions of
\sigma^{2}
and
\beta
as

l(\beta,\sigma^{2}|y,x,v)=h_{1}(\sigma^{2}|v)+h_{2}(\beta|y,x,v),

where

\displaystyle h_{1}(\sigma^{2}|v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}}{2}\mbox{log}\left(\sigma^{2}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{n_{i}v_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},

\displaystyle h_{2}(\beta|y,x,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[y_{ij}\mbox{log}\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}+\left(1-y_{ij}\right)\mbox{log}\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}\right]

But
v_{i}
being unknown, the observed data likelihood function of
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
is obtained by integrating the complete data likelihood over
v_{i}
as follows:

L(\beta,\sigma^{2}|y,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}{\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{y_{ij}}{\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}}^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}g(v_{i})\right]\,dv_{i},\\

where
g(v_{i})
is the pdf of
N(0,\sigma^{2})
.

4.1
Motivation for the proposed adjusted likelihood

We are interested in obtaining estimates of
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
by maximizing the above likelihood function. But it involves an integration because of the unknown random effects
v_{i}
. In the parametric bootstrap sample generation, we noticed a problem while refitting the mixed effects model using R function
glmer
. Estimates of standard errors of random effect due to state were either exactly zero or very small, i.e., estimates falling on the boundary with
\hat{\sigma}^{2}\approx 0
. One possible explanation may be that with too many predictors there is less variation to be captured, i.e.,
\sigma^{2}
is small. Also there are cases when the model fails to converge. The problem is quite serious as in about 22% of the bootstrap samples
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=0
as noted in Table
1
. We thus propose an adjusted maximum likelihood method for parameter estimation, which is implemented using the Expectation Maximization (EM) algorithm applied to adjusted likelihood function.

4.2
Positivity of the adjusted maximum likelihood estimator of
\sigma^{2}

Let us define the adjusted likelihood of
\sigma^{2}
as
L_{adj}(\sigma^{2})=h(\sigma^{2})\times L(\sigma^{2})
, where
h(\cdot)
is a bounded smooth function with
h(0)=0
. An example satisfying the conditions is
h(\sigma^{2})=\tan^{-1}(\sigma^{2})
.
In this subsection, for simplicity of exposition, we assume
\beta
is known and denote
H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})=H(v_{i})
. Since
H(v_{i})
and
1-H(v_{i})
are both probabilities,
|H(v_{i})|\leq 1
and
|1-H(v_{i})|\leq 1
and
y_{ij}
takes on values
{0,1}
only. Thus,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L_{adj}(\sigma^{2})&=h(\sigma^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[g(v_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\{H(v_{i})^{y_{ij}}(1-H(v_{i}))^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}\}\right]\,dv_{i}\\
&\leq h(\sigma^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(v_{i})\,dv_{i}\right]=h(\sigma^{2})\end{aligned}

Hence,
L_{adj}(0)=0
, i.e., whenever
\sigma^{2}=0
and as a result the adjusted likelihood estimator is never maximized at
\hat{\sigma}^{2}=0
.

Next, we need to show that
Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}L_{adj}(\sigma^{2})=Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}h(\sigma^{2})L(\sigma^{2})=0
. Let for area
i
,
Z_{i}=\frac{V_{i}}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)
and
\phi(z_{i})
be the pdf. For area
i
, let
n_{i}^{*}
be the number of
y_{ij}=0
and thus number of
y_{ij}=1
is
n_{i}-n_{i}^{*}
. For the result to hold, we impose a condition that out of total
n=\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}
observations in the entire sample at least one
y_{ij}=0
, i.e.,
n_{i}^{*}>0
for at least one
i
. Such a condition is not restrictive and would easily be satisfied. We define
\psi(\sigma,z_{i})=\left(H(\sigma z_{i})\right)^{(n_{i}-n_{i}^{*})}\left(1-H(\sigma z_{i})\right)^{n_{i}^{*}}\leq 1
, as
n_{i},n_{i}^{*}
are fixed.
Then the adjusted likelihood can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \\
h(\sigma^{2})L(\sigma^{2})&=h(\sigma^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(z_{i})\{\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\{H(\sigma z_{i})\}^{y_{ij}}\{1-H(\sigma z_{i})\}^{1-y_{ij}}\}\,dz_{i}\right]\\
&=h(\sigma^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{\left(H(\sigma z_{i})\right)^{(n_{i}-n_{i}^{*})}\left(1-H(\sigma z_{i})\right)^{n_{i}^{*}}\phi(z_{i})\}\,dz_{i}\right]\\
&=h(\sigma^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(\sigma,z_{i})\phi(z_{i})\,dz_{i}\right]\end{aligned}

Since
h(\sigma^{2})
is bounded we have
h(\sigma^{2})\leq c
(special case is
\tan^{-1}(\sigma^{2})\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
) and
\psi(\sigma,z)\leq 1
, for any fixed z, i.e., the integral is finite. For
z>0
and
z<0
we make use of the functions
H(\sigma z_{i})
and
(1-H(\sigma z_{i}))
accordingly, which behave in opposite directions, such that limiting value is 0. For example, with logistic cdf, for
z>0
,
Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\left\{h(\sigma^{2})\left(\frac{1}{1+\exp(\sigma z)}\right)\right\}=0
. Similarly for
z<0
,
Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\left\{h(\sigma^{2})\left(\frac{\exp(\sigma z)}{1+\exp(\sigma z)}\right)\right\}=0
.
By the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem, we conclude for
i^{th}
area

Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[h(\sigma^{2})\psi(\sigma,z_{i})\phi(z_{i})\right]\,dz_{i}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}h(\sigma^{2})\psi(\sigma,z_{i})\right]\phi(z_{i})\,dz_{i}=0.

Again for all other areas
i^{*}\neq i
, using a similar logic the limiting value is 0. Finally, using the condition on
n_{i}^{*}
, we get
Lim_{\sigma\to\infty}h(\sigma^{2})L(\sigma^{2})=0
. Also note that
H(\cdot)
and
(1-H(\cdot))
are monotonic functions and
\phi(\cdot)
being standard normal density, we have for any real numbers a and b with
-\infty<a<b<\infty
so that,

L_{adj}(\sigma^{2})\geq h(\sigma^{2})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[H(\sigma a)^{(n_{i}-n_{i}^{*})}(1-H(\sigma b))^{n_{i}^{*}}\int_{a}^{b}\phi(z_{i})\,dz_{i}\right]>0.

Thus we have shown that the adjusted likelihood,
L_{adj}(\cdot)
, is 0 at
\sigma^{2}=0
and tends to 0 as
\sigma^{2}
goes to infinity and it has at least one positive value.

4.2.1
EM algorithm and its application to this problem

After getting around the boundary problem, we can make use of Monte-Carlo integration to approximate the integration by a summation in the adjusted likelihood function, but first we need to predict
v_{i}
. So we apply the idea of the EM algorithm, where we treat
v_{i}
as missing and
(y,x)
as observed.
Let
l(\theta|y)
be the log-likelihood when there is no missing,
y_{(0)}
be observed data,
y_{(1)}
be unobserved so that complete data is
y=(y_{(0)},y_{(1)})
and we want to maximize
l(\theta|y)
over
\theta
. If
\theta^{(t)}
is the estimate of
\theta
at iteration
t
then

E Step:
Obtain the expected value of complete likelihood
l(\theta|y)
under the distribution of
(y_{(1)}|y_{(0)})
which is a function of
\theta
given
\theta^{(t)}
as follows:

\\
Q(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=E_{\theta^{(t)}}\left[l(\theta|y)|y_{(0)}\right]=\int l(\theta|y)f_{\theta^{(t)}}(y_{(1)}|y_{(0)})dy_{(1)}.

M Step:
Maximize this function
Q(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
of
\theta
to obtain estimate
\theta^{(t+1)}
as follows:

Q(\theta^{(t+1)}|\theta^{(t)})=\mbox{max}_{\theta}Q(\theta|\theta^{(t)})

In this problem we want to estimate
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
with given or known values as
y_{(0)}=(y,x)
and missing or unknown values
y_{(1)}=v
.
Now aligning this problem in the structure of EM algorithm we do the following for the E and M steps.

E step
:

Step 1.

Assuming some starting values of
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
in the E-step we need to calculate

Q(\beta,\sigma^{2}|\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})
. Due to missing values of
v_{i}
we first approximate the distributional form of
(v_{i}|y_{i})
, using Laplace technique, by a Normal distribution with mean
\hat{v_{i}}=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{v_{i}}f({v_{i}}|y_{i})

and variance given by
\hat{\tau}_{i}^{2}={\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}\mbox{log}f({v_{i}}|y_{i};\beta,\sigma^{2})}{\partial v_{i}^{2}}|_{\hat{v_{i}}}\right]}^{-1},
which is the inverse of the observed information i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f(v_{i}|y_{i},\beta,\sigma^{2})&\propto\exp\left(-\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[{\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\}}^{y_{ij}}{\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\}}^{\left(1-y_{ij}\right)}\right]\\
&=g(v_{i})\approx N\left(\hat{v_{i}},\hat{\tau}_{i}^{2}\right).\end{aligned}

Step 2.

To calculate
\hat{v_{i}}
for
i=1,\cdots,m
, we numerically maximize the function
g(v_{i})
of
v_{i}
and hence obtain
\hat{v_{i}}
where the maximum value of
g(v_{i})
is achieved. To calculate
\hat{\tau}_{i}^{2}
we first differentiate
-\mbox{log}\left(f(v_{i}|\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})\right)
or equivalently
-\mbox{log}\left(g(v_{i}|\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})\right)
twice with respect to
v_{i}
, then plug in values of
\hat{v_{i}}
and take inverse.
We know

\displaystyle\mbox{log }g({v_{i}})=-\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[y_{ij}\mbox{log}\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}+\left(1-y_{ij}\right)\mbox{log}\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})\right\}\right]

For
H(x_{ij},\beta,v_{i})
as logistic cdf, the derivatives are as follows:

\frac{\partial\mbox{log}f({v_{i}}|y_{i};\beta,\sigma^{2})}{\partial v_{i}}=-\frac{v_{i}}{\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[\frac{y_{ij}}{1+\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}-(1-y_{ij})\left\{\frac{\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}{1+\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}\right\}\right]\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\mbox{log}f({v_{i}}|y_{i};\beta,\sigma^{2})}{\partial v_{i}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\frac{\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})}{\left\{1+\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+v_{i})\right\}^{2}}\Rightarrow\hat{\tau_{i}}^{2}=\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\frac{\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+\hat{v_{i}})}{\left\{1+\exp(x^{\prime}_{ij}\beta+\hat{v_{i}})\right\}^{2}}\right]^{-1}

Step 3.

Draw sample
z_{i},\;i=1,\cdots,m
independently
R
times from
N(0,1)
and obtain sample
\tilde{v_{i}}
from
N(\hat{v_{i}},\hat{\tau}^{2}_{i})
by the formula
\tilde{v_{i}}=\hat{v_{i}}+\hat{\tau}_{i}\times z_{i},\;i=1,\cdots,m
.

Step 4.

We progress further in the E-step where we need to approximate

E_{(\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})}\left[l(\beta,\sigma^{2}|y,v)\right].

Having obtained values of
\tilde{v_{i}}
i.e. samples from the distribution
f(v_{i}|\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})
we are now at a position to be able to approximate the expected complete data log-likelihood with respect to the distribution of
f(v_{i}|\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})
. For this we use Monte-Carlo approximation methods and also adjust the log-likelihood to avoid the problem of boundary estimates. We rename
(\beta^{(0)},{\sigma^{(0)}}^{2})=\theta^{(0)}
for the sake of simplifying notations. Thus the function to maximize in the M step is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Q(\theta|\theta^{(t)})&=E_{\theta^{(t)}}\left[l(\theta|y)|y_{(0)}\right]=E_{\theta^{(t)}}[l(\theta|y,x,\tilde{v})]\\
&\approx\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}h_{1}(\sigma^{2}|\tilde{v_{r}})+\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}h_{2}(\beta|y,x,\tilde{v_{r}}),\end{aligned}

where,

h_{1}(\sigma^{2}|v_{r})=\mbox{log}\left\{h(\sigma^{2})\right\}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}}{2}log\left(\sigma^{2}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{n_{i}\tilde{v_{ri}}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\\
h_{2}(\beta|y,x,v_{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left[{y_{ij}\mbox{log}\left\{H(x_{ij},\beta,\tilde{v_{ri}})\right\}+\left(1-y_{ij}\right)\mbox{log}\left\{1-H(x_{ij},\beta,\tilde{v_{ri}})\right\}}\right].

M step
: Now we have a function of
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
which we need to maximize to obtain our next iterate
(\beta^{(1)},{\sigma^{(1)}}^{2})
. We can do this in R using available optimization functions.

5.1
PEW and CPS Survey data

Figure 1:
Multiple survey data and structure

The Pew Research Center provides information on social issues, public opinion, and demographic trends shaping the United States and the world. We consider their October 2016 Political Survey before the Presidential election which contains 2,583 interviews, conducted during October 20-25, 2016. It is a telephone survey of national samples. We get data for 1.6k registered voters from 49 areas (48 states and DC) as there is no sample for the states of Montana and South Dakota. The PEW survey contains information on voting preference along with individual characteristics like age, gender, race etc.. Participants are asked about their voting preference. The original data can be found at their website http://www.people-press.org/dataset/october-2016-political-survey/. We create a binary variable taking on the value 1 if a person prefers to vote for Clinton and 0 otherwise. We want to estimate the percentage of voters for Clinton for US states and DC. Direct survey based estimates would not serve the purpose as such estimates may not be trustworthy
due high standard error (SE). Moreover, direct estimates are nonexistent for two states as there is no observation from PEW sample for these two states. So we take the help of a bigger survey.

The Current Population Survey (CPS), conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau for the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), is the primary source of labor force statistics for the population of the United States. In addition to being the primary source of monthly labor force statistics, the CPS is used to collect data for a variety of other studies that keep the nation informed of the economic and social well-being of its people. This is done by adding a set of supplemental questions to the monthly basic CPS questions. Supplemental inquiries vary month to month and cover a wide variety of topics such as child support, volunteerism, health insurance coverage, and school enrollment. Supplements are usually conducted annually or biannually. The November 2016 Voting and Registration Supplement of CPS is used for this analysis. It contains data of about 58.5k individuals from 50 states and DC. We note similar individual characteristic variables as that of PEW like age, gender, race etc. The full dataset can be downloaded from ¡www.nber.org/cps/¿.

We obtain auxiliary variables for the analysis from both PEW and CPS data. We consider individual characteristics like age, race etc., which are converted into categorical variables.
We also consider a group level predictor: the state wise percentage of voters for Obama in the 2012 Presidential election, which is available as 51 values for 51 small areas. Only the PEW sample contains information on the outcome variable of interest, so we can view this as a missing data problem as well as prediction problem as CPS does not contain information on voting preference. We use PEW data for fitting models and use CPS in predicting the outcome at state level. Essentially we have to assume that underlying population distribution of the two surveys are similar, i.e. we consider non-informative sampling. If the distribution of these two surveys vary a great deal, which occurs in case of informative sampling, we would not be able to apply such prediction techniques.

Parametric Bootstrap and MSPE calculation

In this section we discuss how to compute estimates of mean square prediction errors (MSPE) of EBPs in R using the proposed parametric bootstrap method on PEW and CPS data. Essentially we want to compute the squared differences between true value and estimated value and find the mean, take its square root to get Square root MSPE. We compare the square root of the estimated MSPE from the bootstrap method with the standard error of direct estimates at the state level, which helps us understand the performance of the EBP estimator in comparison to the direct estimator. In R we follow the procedure described in section
3
, which involves both PEW and CPS data. Let bootstrap size be
B

b^{th}
bootstrap step we perform the following steps and repeat independently for
B
bootstraps.

Step 2.

Using the above subroutine we generate PEW bootstrap sample.

Step 3.

Next using the subroutine (1) we similarly generate CPS bootstrap sample.

Step 5.

Next we write a subroutine (2) to calculate weighted estimate at area level.

CONCLUSION

In this paper, we develop a new data linkage method for estimating small area proportions. Unlike the existing methods in finite population sampling, our data linkage method takes advantage of a large set of potentially useful auxiliary variables in obtaining a reasonable predictive model. This, in turn, improves estimation of small area proportions. Our empirical results demonstrate superiority of the proposed empirical best predictors over the usual survey-weighted proportions. In our empirical investigation we observe that estimates of model parameters could fall on the boundary for some parametric bootstrap samples. The problem is especially prominent for over-fitted models. Thus we reiterate the importance of model building exercise. We propose an adjusted maximum likelihood method that ensures parameter estimates to be always within boundaries. The method has an edge over the commonly used maximum likelihood method as implemented by the popular R package
glmer.
The proposed data integration method can be used even if the bigger sample is a nonprobability survey using weights constructed by methods such as the ones described in
Chen
et al. 2020
,
Wang
et al. 2021
, and
Savitsky et al. 2023
.
