Matrix method of polynomial solutions to constant coefficient PDE’s
Victor G. Zakharov

Abstract
In the paper, we introduce a matrix method to constructively determine spaces of polynomial solutions (in general, multiplied by exponentials) to a system of constant coefficient linear PDE’s with polynomial (multiplied by exponentials) right-hand sides. The matrix method reduces the funding of a polynomial subspace of null-space of a differential operator to the funding of the null-space of a block matrix. Using this matrix approach, we investigate some linear algebra properties of the spaces of polynomial solutions. In particular, for a solution space containing polynomials up to some arbitrarily large degree, we can determine dimension and basis of the space. Some examples of polynomial (multiplied by exponential, in general) solutions of the Laplace, Helmholtz, Poisson equations are considered.



Keywords:
polynomial solution to linear constant coefficient PDE’s,
exponential solution to nonhomogeneous PDE, null-space of matrix

Introduction

The polynomial solutions to linear constant coefficient PDE’s is the well-known problem of algebra,
see, for example,
[
1
,
5
,

,
8
,
9

However, under the Fourier transform, a linear constant coefficient PDE is dual
to an algebraic polynomial. Thus the problem to find a polynomial solution to linear constant coefficient PDE’s
can be transformed to solve a linear algebraic system. So, the linear algebra approach allows to solve
nonhomogeneous and induced by nonhomogeneous polynomials PDE’s. Hence the matrix method allows to find
exponential solutions (in general, multiplied by algebraic polynomials) and to solve to PDE’s with
polynomial (multiplied by exponentials) right-hand sides. Moreover, the matrix method enables to determine
some (linear algebra) characteristics such as dimension, basis, affinely-invariance, maximal total degrees
of polynomials, etc., of a solution space. Note that the matrix method is valid for polynomials that induce
PDE’s with coefficients from any algebraically closed field. Note also that the matrix methods can be easily
algorithmized.

In addition, the matrix approach can be generalized to find polynomial solutions to PDE’s with polynomial
coefficients. This will be discussed elsewhere.

Note that the discussed in this paper matrix approach to solve linear constant coefficient PDE’s
was stimulated by a generalization of the Strang-Fix conditions, see

In particular, the polynomial

when we take a root of the operator symbol that the root is not the origin, were obtained, see

&\widetilde{d}({l})&&\mbox{ if }{l}<|\alpha|.\end{aligned}\right.

The theorem is a direct consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.6
, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{d}({l}-|\alpha|)&&\mbox{ if }{l}\geq|\alpha|>0;\\
&0&&\mbox{ if }{l}<|\alpha|.\end{aligned}\right.

Sketch of the proof of Lemma
3.7
.

For the case
{l}\geq|\alpha|>0
,
by Theorem
3.5
and Corollary
3.3
,
each block on the
|\alpha|
th block diagonal of the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})

is a full rank matrix. However, since the blocks of
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
are not, generally, square matrices;
the problem to determine the rank of the matrix is not trivial.

Using an analog of the Gaussian elimination algorithm (applied to columns instead of rows) and moving
(actually permutating)
zero columns to the left, the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
can always be transformed
into a
strictly
upper triangular matrix, where the lowest
non-zero diagonal goes to
the lower right corner of the last submatrix
\mathbf{D}^{{l}-|\alpha|}_{l}
. So

\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})=\widetilde{d}({l}-|\alpha|)
.

Since, in the case
{l}<|\alpha|
, the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
is a zero matrix;
the case
{l}<|\alpha|
is trivial.
∎

Introduce some notation.
We always can consider
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}
,
{l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, as a space with a Cartesian
coordinate system,
where the dot product of the Cartesian coordinates
is
x\mathbin{\cdot}y:=\sum_{{{q}}}x_{{q}}\overline{y_{{q}}}
.
Namely, we have

\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}:=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{e_{{q}}:1\leq{{q}}\leq\widetilde{d}({l})\right\},

where the span
is over
\mathbb{C}
and
e_{{q}}
is the
{{q}}
th basis vector:
e_{{q}}:=\left(\delta_{{{q}}1},\dots,\delta_{{{q}},\widetilde{d}({l})}\right)
;
and we can decompose
\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}
as follows

\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}=\mathstrut^{0}\!\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}\oplus\mathstrut^{1}\!\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathstrut^{{l}}\!\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})},

(3.9)

where

\mathstrut{}^{0}\!\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}:=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{e_{1}\right\},

\mathstrut{}^{{m}}\!\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}:=\mathop{\mathrm{span}}\left\{e_{{q}}:\widetilde{d}({{m}}-1)+1\leq{{q}}\leq\widetilde{d}({{m}})\right\},\qquad{{m}}=1,\dots,{l},

and the direct sums in (
3.9
) are orthogonal.

Remark 3.2
.

Decomposition (
3.9
) corresponds to
the block structure of the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}
,
{l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
(as well as structures of
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{l}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}
).

Definition 3.3
.

By
\mathfrak{P}_{{m}}
, denote
the orthogonal projection on the subspace
\mathstrut{}^{{{m}}}\!\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}
,
{{m}}=0,\dots,{l}
;
and define subspaces of the null-space
V_{l}
, see Definition
3.1
, as

\mathstrut^{{{m}}}\!V_{l}:=\mathfrak{P}_{{m}}V_{l},\qquad{{m}}=0,1,\dots,{l}.

(3.10)

Remark 3.3
.

Note that generally

V_{l}
is not a sum, like (
3.9
), of
\mathstrut{}^{{{m}}}\!V_{l}
.

Now we can formulate the following theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
{l}\in\mathbb{N}
. Let the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
be singular and
V_{l}:=\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
.
Then the subspace
\mathstrut{}^{{l}}\!V_{l}:=\mathfrak{P}_{l}V_{l}
is non-zero.

Proof.

If the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
is a zero matrix, there is nothing to prove.

Assume the converse, i. e., suppose
\mathstrut{}^{{l}}\!V_{l}
is a zero space; then

\dim\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})=\dim\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}-1}f(x_{0}).

Since the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}f(x_{0})
is singular and non-zero; there exists a number
{{r}}\in\mathbb{N}
,
1\leq{{r}}\leq{l}
, that the block
{{r}}
-diagonal is the lowest non-zero block diagonal. By Theorem
3.6
, we have

\dim\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}-1}f(x_{0})=\widetilde{d}({l}-1)-\widetilde{d}({l}-1-{{r}})<\widetilde{d}({l})-\widetilde{d}({l}-{{r}})=\dim\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}}f(x_{0}).

This contradiction proves the theorem. ∎

4
Solution methods

4.1
Homogeneous PDE’s

In this subsection, we consider
homogeneous
PDE’s
with constant coefficients from the field
\mathbb{C}
. Note the polynomials that induce the differential equations are not
necessary homogeneous; i. e., the polynomials are, in general, sums of terms of different degree.

4.1.1
One equation

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P
be a polynomial with constant coefficients from the field
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
x_{0}
be a point of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}
,
{l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, be given
by (
3.3
). Then the following non-zero function
e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{l}(x)\right]v
,

v\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}
, belongs to

\ker P(-iD)
if and only if
P(x_{0})=0
and

v\in\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}P(x_{0})
.

Proof.

As it has been said, see Theorem
3.4
, a necessary and
sufficient condition for the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}P(x_{0})
to be
singular is that the point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a root of the polynomial

P
.

Consider the function

f(x):=P(-iD)\left(e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{l}(x)\right]v\right).

Taking the Fourier transform of the previous function,
we obtain

\hat{f}(\xi):=P(\xi)\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}\delta(\xi-x_{0})\right]v,\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

(4.1)

The adjoint operator (set of operators)
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}^{\,\ast}
satisfies a property

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}^{\,\ast}=\overline{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}}

(4.2)

(the adjunction, like the complex conjugation, is distributive over the comma).
Using Definition
2.2
, Theorem
3.1
, and property (
4.2
);
for any test function
\phi\in S(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, the functional
T_{\hat{f}}(\phi)
(where
\hat{f}
is distribution (
4.1
)) is of the form

\displaystyle T_{\hat{f}}(\phi)=\overline{\left\langle P(\cdot)\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}\delta(\cdot-x_{0})\right]v,\phi\right\rangle}=\overline{\left\langle\delta(\cdot-x_{0}),\left[\overline{\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}}\left(\overline{P}\phi\right)\right]\overline{v}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}\left(P(x_{0})\overline{\phi(x_{0})}\right)\right]v\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{by~\eqref{Dfg}}}}{{=}}\ \left[\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{l}\overline{\phi(x_{0})}\right]\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}P(x_{0})\right]v.

(4.3)

Using the expression in the right-hand side of (
4.3
), the proof is trivial.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Theorem
4.1
can be proved for other (linear and linear-conjugate,
see for example
[
6
]
)
functionals like (
2.4
).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
{l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Let
P
be a polynomial. Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a root of the polynomial
P
.
Let the matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}
be given by (
3.3
).
Let the polynomial spaces be of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{l}:=\left\{\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{l}\right]v:v\in\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}}P(x_{0})\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}:=\left\{\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{l}+1}\right]v:v\in\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})\right\}.

Then

\mathcal{V}_{l}=\Pi_{\leq{l}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}.

(4.4)

The proof of Theorem
4.2
is given in Appendix
C
.

Remark 4.2
.

The previous theorem reflects a property of differentiation
to commutate with translation.

Below we state a corollary of
Theorem
3.8
and Theorem
4.2
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
,
we see that
the null-space of the operator
P(-iD)
contains polynomials (multiplied by the exponential

e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}
) up to an arbitrary large total degree.

Remark 4.3
.

Corollary

expresses a fundamental property, see
[
1
]
,
of polynomial solutions to a single PDE with constant coefficients.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
{l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Let
P
be an algebraic polynomial, let
x_{0}
be a root of
P
.
Let
D^{\alpha}P(x_{0})
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\geq 0}
, be a non-zero derivative of the least order.
Then we have

\dim\left(e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}\Pi_{\leq{l}}\cap\ker P(-iD)\right)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{d}({l})-\widetilde{d}({l}-|\alpha|)&&\mbox{ if }{l}\geq|\alpha|>0;\\
&\widetilde{d}({l})&&\mbox{ if }{l}<|\alpha|.\end{aligned}\right.

Moreover, if

; then

.

System of equations

For the case of a system of PDE’s, i. e., if we have several algebraic polynomials

,

the polynomial solution of the corresponding system

\left\{\begin{aligned} P_{1}&(-iD)\cdot=0,\\

,

Namely we have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let

,
n^{\prime}=1,2,\dots,{{n}}
, be algebraic polynomials.
The non-zero expression
e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{l}\right]v

where

4.2
PDE with polynomial right-hand side

In the previous subsection, we have considered polynomial
solutions of homogeneous constant coefficient PDE’s; i. e., actually, PDE’s have zero right-hand
sides. Nevertheless the matrix approach allows generalizing PDE’s
to polynomial right-hand sides (multiplied by an exponential).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
{l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Let
P,F
be algebraic polynomials with constant coefficients from
\mathbb{C}
,

x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a root of
P
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
be a multi-index that defines the least order derivative
such that
D^{\alpha}P(x_{0})\neq 0
. Let the polynomial
F
be defined as follows:
F(x):=\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\deg F}(x)\right]w
,

w\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}(\deg F)}
. Let
{l}\geq\deg F+|\alpha|
, the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}
be given by (
3.3
).
Let
v\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}
be a column vector and
p:=\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{l}}\right]v
be the corresponding polynomial. Then
the algebraic polynomial
p
is a solution to PDE

P(-iD)\left(e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}p(x)\right)=e^{ix_{0}\mathbin{\cdot}x}F(x)

(4.6)

iff
the vector
v
is a solution to linear algebraic equation

\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{l}P(x_{0})\right]v=\begin{bmatrix}w^{T}&\underbrace{\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}}_{\widetilde{d}({l})-\widetilde{d}\left(\deg F\right)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

(4.7)

Remark 4.4
.

Under the conditions of the previous theorem, introduce notation.
Denote by
V_{l}
a linear space such that any vector

is a solution to linear algebraic
equation (
4.7
) and by
\mathcal{V}_{l}:=\left\{\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{l}\right]v:v\in V_{l}\right\}
denote
the corresponding space of polynomials that the polynomials are solutions to PDE (
4.6
).
So we can state 3 remarks:

1.

\dim\mathcal{V}_{l}=\widetilde{d}({l})-\widetilde{d}({l}-|\alpha|)
;

2.

for any polynomial
F
such that
\deg F\leq{l}-|\alpha|
, linear system (
4.7
) is consistent;

3.

there exists a polynomial of an arbitrary large degree to satisfy PDE (
4.6
).

However, in the previous theorem, the point

can be no root of
P
. Below we state the corresponding
remark.

Remark 4.5
.

Under the conditions of the previous theorem and suppose

; we see that
the polynomial
p
is defined uniquely and
does not depend on the choice of
{l}
.

The proof of the previous theorem and remarks is left to the reader. Note only that the theorem and remarks
are based on Theorem
3.6
, Theorem
4.1
,
and the classical Rouché-Capelli theorem.

p\in\Pi_{\leq{l}}.

(C.1)

The matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})
can be presented as a block matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&\mathbf{D}^{0}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})\\
&\raisebox{3.13286pt}{$\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}}P(x_{0})$}&&\vdots\\
&&&\mathbf{D}^{{l}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})\\
\cline{1-3}\cr 0&\dots&\lx@intercol\hfil 0\hfil\lx@intercol&\mathbf{D}^{{l}+1}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})\end{array}\right],

(C.2)

where the submatrices
\mathbf{D}^{0}_{{l}+1},\dots,\mathbf{D}^{{l}+1}_{{l}+1}
are given by (
3.2
).
Introduce an auxiliary column vector as follows
v^{\sharp}:=\begin{bmatrix}v^{T}&\underbrace{\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}}_{d({l}+1)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
.

Then, using block form (
C.2
) of the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})
,
we get
v^{\sharp}\in\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})
. Since
p=\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{l}+1}\right]v^{\sharp}
, it follows that

p\in\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}
; and, by(
C.1
), we obtain
p\in\Pi_{\leq{l}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}
.
Thus we have
\mathcal{V}_{l}\subseteq\Pi_{\leq{l}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}
.

Contrary. Suppose a polynomial
p\in\Pi_{\leq{l}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}
. Since
p\in\Pi_{\leq{l}}
, it follows that
the polynomial can be presented as follows
p=\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{l}+1}\right]v^{\sharp}
,
v^{\sharp}:=\begin{bmatrix}v^{T}&\underbrace{\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}}_{d({l}+1)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, where
v\in\mathbb{C}^{\widetilde{d}({l})}
. Since
v^{\sharp}\in\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}+1}P(x_{0})
and, arguing as above;
v\in\ker\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{{l}}P(x_{0})
. Thus
p\in\mathcal{V}_{l}
; i. e., we have
\mathcal{V}_{l}\supseteq\Pi_{\leq{l}}\cap\mathcal{V}_{{l}+1}
.

This concludes the proof.
