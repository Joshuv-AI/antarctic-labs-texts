Dynamic stability of nano-scale ferroelectric domains by molecular dynamics modeling
Arne J. Klomp, Ruben Khachaturyan, Theophilus Wallis, Anna Grünebohm, Karsten Albe

Abstract
Ultra-dense domain walls are increasingly important for many devices but their microscopic properties are so far not fully understood. In this study we combine atomistic and coarse-grained molecular dynamic simulations to study the domain wall stability in the prototypical ferroelectric BaTiO 3 \text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} . We transfer the discussion of the field-driven nucleation and motion of domain walls to thermally induced modifications of the wall without an external driving force. Our simulations show that domain wall dynamics and stability depend crucially on microscopic thermal fluctuations. Enhanced fluctuations at domain walls may result in the formation of critical nuclei for the permanent shift of the domain wall. If two domain walls are close – put in other words, when domains are small – thermal fluctuations can be sufficient to bring domain walls into contact and lead to the annihilation of small domains. This is even true well below the Curie temperature and when domain walls are initially as far apart as 6 unit cells. Such small domains are, thus, not stable and limit the maximum achievable domain wall density in nanoelectronic devices.



I
Introduction

In the past two decades ferroelectric domains and especially the domain walls (DWs) separating these domains have come to be viewed in a different light.
This was fueled by the ever increasing demand for micro- and nanoscopic devices and electronic components.
While domains carry the permanent polarization in a ferroelectric, it has been recognized that DWs in themselves offer a variety of intriguing functionalities.
On the one hand, DWs can enhance existing functional properties such as macroscopic piezoelectric response, dielectric coefficients and conductivity
Liu and Cohen 2017
;
Kämpfe et al. 2014
;
Zuo et al. 2014
, and influence phase transitions
Grünebohm and Marathe 2020
;
Grünebohm et al. 2022
.
On the other hand, novel functionality opens the route to future devices
Catalan et al. 2012
;
Said et al. 2017
;
Bednyakov et al. 2018
;
Sharma et al. 2019
, such as data storage devices
Garcia and Bibes 2014
, diodes
Whyte and Gregg 2015
, memristors
Bai et al. 2018
;
McConville et al. 2020
, and ferroelectric transistors
Chai et al. 2020
.
Consequently, the research field of ”domain wall nanoelectronics” has emerged using DWs as their functional component
Catalan et al. 2012
.
Naturally, in order to maximize the impact of DWs on a material a high density of DWs is desired
Wada et al. 2005
;
Hlinka et al. 2009
;
Liu and Cohen 2017
;
Grünebohm et al. 2022
.

Yet, surprisingly few microscopic studies tackle the problem of the stability of nano-domains in ferroelectrics at finite temperatures
Prosandeev et al. 2021
.
Thus, there is it the need for a comprehensive study of the dynamics of closely spaced ferroelectric DWs.
In this work we address the important questions,
(a) how the dynamics of ultra-dense DWs can be investigated efficiently, and
(b) what the highest possible DW density and its temperature-dependence are.
Is there a lower limit to the distance between individual
180\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
DWs in the prototypical ferroelectric tetragonal phase of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
?
We show that DW spacings on the order of few unit cells are not stable against thermal fluctuations even well below the Curie temperature.
The time it takes for a small domain to collapse depends on its thickness and the magnitude of thermal fluctuations.

We recognize that modern imaging techniques give impressive spatial resolution of ferroelectric structures
Grünebohm et al. 2022
.
However, only computer simulations allows studying ferroelectric systems with quasi-arbitrary temporal resolution and without superimposed impact of surfaces and possible defects.
We can, therefore, give details and explanations on the microscopic processes related to the stability of nano-domains.
Several computational models on different scales exist to study ferroelectric effects.
On the micro- and mesoscopic scale, Landau-type or phase field models are often employed
Levanyuk et al. 2020
.
However, thermal fluctuations cannot be treated directly be these methods and are only rarely mimicked using random fields
Yang et al. 2020
.
Thus, we decided to use classical atomistic models that capture all the relevant fluctuations and dynamics on an atomic scale
Mitchell and Fincham 1993
.
This approach is supplemented by the computationally more efficient effective Hamiltonian (
H^{\text{eff}}
) approach that is built around the material response on a unit cell level
Rabe and Joannopoulos 1987
.
Both approaches are based on ab initio calculations.
So far, a reliable cross-validation for the two models on a relevant case such as a ferroelectric DW is missing in literature.
The former type of model has been pioneered by Tinte et al.
Tinte et al. 1999
and recent applications of core-shell atomistic models include Refs.
Shin et al. 2007
;
Boddu et al. 2017
.
Using the electric field as a driving force,
Shin et al. 2007
found that the propagation of DWs in lead titanate occurs by nucleation and nucleus growth on the DW.
The study found that the energy barrier for growth of a 2D nucleus on the DW is much smaller than for the formation of an initial nucleus inside a domain far away from any DW.
Moreover, the growth of nuclei occurs preferentially in the DW plane.
As the nucleus formation at the DW is easy, we deem it possible that nucleation also occurs randomly through activation by thermal fluctuations in the absence of electric fields.

Such thermal fluctuations occur naturally and it has been shown recently by
Caballero et al. 2020
that the resulting roughening of ferroelectric DWs is an essential and intrinsic feature for ferroelectrics.
Currently, we lack information about how the process of roughening of DWs and the motion of DWs behaves as temperatures approach the Curie temperature and when DWs are close to each other, i.e., at high DW density.

\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is a prototype material for ferroelectrics
Acosta et al. 2017
and many of the relations investigated with this material could be transferred to other materials, too.
Because thermal fluctuations and DW roughening are ubiquitous in ferroelectrics we expect good transferability to related scenarios.

As the ground to study nanoscopic domains and validate the agreement of atomistic and
H^{\text{eff}}
-model we choose charge neutral and stress-free DWs in the tetragonal phase of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.
Therefore, we construct
180\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
DWs located on the
(0\,1\,0)
plane separating regions of antiparallel polarization.
While
90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
DWs are also charge neutral because of the head-to-tail orientation of the polarization vectors at the DW, they carry a strain which significantly complicates our analysis.
The
180\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
DW in tetragonal
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is referred to as T180 DW for brevity.

II
Modeling

We simulate single crystalline
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(BTO) in an orthogonal simulation cell with typical side lengths of
48\text{\times}48\text{\times}48\text{\,}\mathrm{u.c.}
, i.e., a total of
110\,592\text{\,}\mathrm{u.c.}
(corresponds to approx. 5.5m particles in the atomistic model), see
Figure

1

Note that this system size guarantees convergence of energies and polarization profiles.
We focus on the tetragonal phase with main polarization direction along
+z

-z

1\text{\,}\mathrm{u.c.}
and
24\text{\,}\mathrm{u.c.}
.
DWs are in the
(0\,1\,0)

y

We initialize these domain structures by poling with local electric fields.
By varying the regions of the applied electric field, domains of different size separated by T180
(0\,1\,0)

After equilibrating, we remove the field and study the polarization and DW dynamics in the absence of electric fields.

Supplementing the atomistic model with an effective Hamiltonian approach speeds up the calculations significantly because the
H^{\text{eff}}

It also gives verification of our results using a second independent approach.
The poling procedures are slightly different for the different models, as explained below.
We confirmed that both approaches yield the same microscopic properties (polarization and velocity distributions) and identical trends for the properties discussed.

Regarding temperature, we focus on the range from
70\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
below to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
above the tetragonal to cubic phase transitions temperature
T_{T\rightarrow C}
upon heating.

II.1
Atomistic Simulations

For the atomistic simulations we employ the adiabatic core-shell model from
Vielma and Schneider 2013

All dynamic simulations use the RESPA integrator

0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{fs}

1.2\text{\,}\mathrm{fs}

The interatomic interactions are integrated explicitly up to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}

(as implemented in
LAMMPS

Plimpton 1995
;
Thompson et al. 2022
). The thermostat time constant is set to
0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
and the barostat time constant to
2.0\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}

The thermostat uses the center of mass of each core-shell pair for a Nosé-Hoover velocity scaling.

After equilibration in the cubic phase (
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
at
400\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
) an electric field

is applied while cooling the system to the target temperature for

(0\,1\,0)

.
Due to the large computational expense we remove the electric field instantaneously.
We checked that instantaneous and gradual removal of electric field lead to same qualitative results using the
H^{\text{eff}}
-model.

The evolution of the local polarization is tracked by the local polarization vector per unit cell
\bm{P}_{i}

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{i}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{j}{\frac{q^{j}}{n^{j}}(\bm{r}_{i}^{j}-\bm{r}_{i}^{\text{Ti}})}\text{ .}

(1)

To reduce noise we average the ionic positions over

II.2
Effective Hamiltonian Simulations

As gathering statistics on the DW evolution while treating all 5.5m atoms, with 6 degrees of freedom each, explicitly is unfeasible, we use the common coarse-graining to the effective Hamiltonian approach

;
Zhong et al. 1994

1

;
Kornev et al. 2006

;

III.1.3
Reliable estimation of DW energy from dynamic calculations

In the bulk as well as on the DW, thermal fluctuations result in the switching of local dipoles against their surrounding polarization.
These fluctuations appear as small clusters of needle-like shape, as illustrated in
Figure

3
(b).
Such clusters form and disappear spontaneously anywhere in the simulation cell at finite temperature.
Because of these fluctuations the DW close to the transition temperature is not flat but thermal roughening of the DW increases the effective wall area
Caballero et al. 2020
.
Consequently, we hypothesize that the energy per area does indeed decrease as suggested by Landau theory, but that the decrease is overcompensated by the increase in area due to roughening.

In order to test this hypothesis we take a different approach to estimating the energy penalty per element of DW area.
Assume that needle-like clusters of height
s
(in unit cells) in a single-domain state carry an excess energy that only consists of contributions from its nearest neighboring unit cells.
In this case, one has to distinguish between the four interfaces parallel to the polarization (like T180 DWs) and the unfavorable head-to-head/tail-to-tail walls along the
\pm z
-direction.
With the energy density per unit cell area for head-to-head/tail-to-tail contributions
E_{c}
and T180 contributions
E_{\text{DW}}
, the excess energy of a single cluster inside the domain is:

\displaystyle E_{a}=2E_{c}+4sE_{\text{DW}}\text{.}

(5)

These clusters are appearing due to thermally activated fluctuations which commonly follow an Arrhenius behavior.
Thus, the probability
f
that a needle-like cluster of size
s
exists at a given temperature
T
is given by:

\displaystyle f=f_{0}\exp{\left[-\left(2E_{c}+4sE_{\text{DW}}\right)/\left(k_{b}T\right)\right]}

(6)

If we assume
E_{c}
and
E_{\text{DW}}
to be constant, we can obtain
E_{\text{DW}}
by counting the frequency with which a cluster of size
s
appears.

As shown in
Appendix

D
, we obtain DW energy densities
E_{\text{DW}}
that decrease from
5.86\text{\,}\mathrm{mJ}\text{/}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
at
\Delta T=$-90\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
to
4.73\text{\,}\mathrm{mJ}\text{/}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
at
\Delta T=$-30\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
from the atomistic model.
Despite the global increase in energy penalty of the DW
E^{*}_{\text{DW}}
with increasing temperature, the actual energy per element of DW area decreases with increasing temperature due to the reduction of spontaneous polarization.
The reason is that fluctuations lead to a roughening of the DW increasing its area.

Our simulations, thus, nicely demonstrate that the temperature evolution of DW energy and width can only be understood in the light of thermal fluctuations which are neither featured in Landau theory (by design) nor DFT calculations (too costly for large dynamic simulations).

III.2
Spontaneous Switching/Domain Collapse

III.2.1
Phenomenological observations

Fluctuations & driving force

DWs carry excess energy, thus, there is a driving force to remove DWs in a ferroelectric.
However, with two parallel, flat, and infinitely thin DWs the system does not
see
an energy gradient and has no immediate means of evolving towards the energy minimum.
Can thermal fluctuations of local dipoles
Kumar and Waghmare 2010
enable two closely spaced DWs to activate this path and ultimately lead to the collapse of small domains?

On the one hand, clusters of reversed polarization are ubiquitous and form and disappear on a sub-picosecond timescale.
At the domain wall, the energy of reversed clusters is reduced by
1\times E_{\text{DW}}
as one interface is anyway antiparallel and as both
E_{\text{DW}}
and
E_{c}
are reduced due to lower polarization in the vicinity of the DW, see
Figure

2
(b).
Thus, the frequency
f
of flipped clusters is enhanced at the DW, see
Appendix

A
.
For example, at
\Delta T=$-49\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
in the atomistic model,
we find
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
and
7.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of local dipoles flipped inside a single-domain state and at the DW (
2\text{\,}\mathrm{u.c.}
right and left of the DW), respectively.
The enhanced probability for switched dipoles on the wall can be understood as a flattening of the energy landscape and locally reduced polarization.

On the other hand, DWs induce an energy penalty on the order of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{mJ}\text{/}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
, see calculations above.
Thus, the system can gain energy if one of the domains vanishes with time.
However, without thermal activation (
0\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
), there is an energy barrier of about
77\text{\,}\mathrm{mJ}\text{/}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
to rigidly shift a domain wall through the unfavorable TiO
2

Furthermore, without electric field, there is no force favoring one polarization direction over the other.
Without fluctuations, the multi-domain structure at
0\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
stays forever.

III.2.3
Energy landscape of domain switching

Driving force

As domain collapse occurs spontaneously there must be a driving force for this process.
Tracking the system’s potential energy during the collapse of the small domain we indeed observe the reduction in energy of twice the energy of a single domain wall, see
Appendix

F
.
However, the large thermal noise prevents the calculation of any activation barrier by simply tracking the system’s potential energy.

A single switched unit cell on the DW can in principle grow (i) along the polarization axis (

As this process happens back and forth at finite temperature the DW could fluctuate by switching column-wise at no net energy cost.
Consequently, many meta-stable states with full
z

In case all
z

Only when two flat DW are so close that the switching of an additional column bridges the small reversed domain, the collapse of the reversed domain may start.
From state III to state IV the switching column bridges the gap between the DWs.
DW area in that scenario is constant, therefore, it does not come with a net energy penalty.

Each consecutive
z

The lowest energy is finally achieved when all DWs have disappeared (state V).

IV
Discussion & Summary

Using two independent computational models, we have observed that very small ferroelectric domains in barium titanate can collapse spontaneously.
This effect becomes more pronounced the closer the temperature comes to the phase transition and the closer the DWs are initially.
Our simulations showed that thermal fluctuations are the reason why DWs that do not interact initially come into contact leading to the annihilation of very small domains.
Different intermediate steps of the domain collapse were identified, but the analytic description of the collapse probability is left to further work.
We expect that this aspect is critical for the discussion of the lower size limits to electronic components based on ferroelectrics.

Further simulations are required to firmly establish a limit in terms of domain size and temperature beyond which domain collapse becomes unlikely.
For applications relying on very high densities of DWs it could, however, be beneficial to introduce locations of DW pinning to increase possible DW density even further.
In this context research into the pinning by point defects and doping can be found in literature but also using dislocations as pinning defects appears to be a feasible approach.

Due to the model character of
\text{BaTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
we expect that our results are transferable to other ferroelectric perovskites.
Yet, it remains to be shown that the observations on T180 DWs also hold true for other DW types such as
90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
DW.
Some of our preliminary calculations suggest that this is indeed the case but that time and length scales are changed due to the significantly wider T90 DWs.
