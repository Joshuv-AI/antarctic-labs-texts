Efficient quantum Gibbs samplers with Kubo–Martin–Schwinger detailed balance condition
Zhiyan Ding, Bowen Li, Lin Lin

Abstract
. Lindblad dynamics and other open-system dynamics provide a promising path towards efficient Gibbs sampling on quantum computers. In these proposals, the Lindbladian is obtained via an algorithmic construction akin to designing an artificial thermostat in classical Monte Carlo or molecular dynamics methods, rather than treated as an approximation to weakly coupled system-bath unitary dynamics. Recently, Chen, Kastoryano, and Gilyén (arXiv:2311.09207) introduced the first efficiently implementable Lindbladian satisfying the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) detailed balance condition, which ensures that the Gibbs state is a fixed point of the dynamics and is applicable to non-commuting Hamiltonians. This Gibbs sampler uses a continuously parameterized set of jump operators, and the energy resolution required for implementing each jump operator depends only logarithmically on the precision and the mixing time. In this work, we build upon the structural characterization of KMS detailed balanced Lindbladians by Fagnola and Umanità, and develop a family of efficient quantum Gibbs samplers using a finite set of jump operators (the number can be as few as one), akin to the classical Markov chain-based sampling algorithm. Compared to the existing works, our quantum Gibbs samplers have a comparable quantum simulation cost but with greater design flexibility and a much simpler implementation and error analysis. Moreover, it encompasses the construction of Chen, Kastoryano, and Gilyén as a special instance.



1.
Introduction

For a given quantum Hamiltonian
H\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, preparing the associated Gibbs state

\sigma_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H}/\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
(also called quantum Gibbs sampling) has a wide range of applications in condensed matter physics, quantum chemistry, and optimization.
Here
N=2^{n}
is the dimension of the underlying Hilbert space,
\beta
is the inverse temperature, and

\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\tr(e^{-\beta H})
is the partition function.
In this work,
we assume efficient quantum access to the Hamiltonian simulation
\exp(-itH),t\in\mathbb{R}
,
and we measure
the cost of a quantum algorithm
in terms of
the total Hamiltonian simulation time of
H
.

Recently, there has been a revival of interest in designing quantum Gibbs samplers based on the Lindblad dynamics
[
ML20
,
RWW23
,
CB21
,
CKBG23
,
CKG23
,
WT23
]

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{L}^{{\dagger}}\rho_{t}\,,

which is a Markovian semigroup on quantum states. For ease of exposition, we shall refer to both the generator
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
and its adjoint
\mathcal{L}
as Lindbladian, which are
related to each other via

(1.1)

\langle X,\mathcal{L}(Y)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(X),Y\rangle\,,\quad\text{for $X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}$}\,.

Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product for matrices, and we follow the convention that the adjoint
\mathcal{L}^{{\dagger}}
denotes the Lindbladian
in the Schrödinger picture acting on a quantum state.
A common choice of Lindbladian for quantum Gibbs sampling is the Davies generator, which is originally derived from the weak-coupling limit of open quantum dynamics
[
Dav74
,
Dav76
,
Dav79
]
:

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\rm Davies}(\rho)=-i[H+H_{\rm LS},\rho]+\sum_{a,\,\omega}\gamma_{a}(\omega)\left(\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)\rho\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)^{\dagger}\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega),\rho\right\}\right)\,,

with the functions
\gamma_{a}(\omega)
and operators
\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)
satisfying

\gamma_{a}(-\omega)=e^{\beta\omega}\gamma_{a}(\omega)\,,\quad\sigma_{\beta}\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)\sigma_{\beta}^{-1}=e^{-\beta\omega}\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)\,,

where
\{X,Y\}=XY+YX
stands the anticommutator and the sum is over
a
and
\omega
from some discrete index sets, representing the number of couplings to the environment and Bohr frequencies, respectively. Here the commutator term involving
H+H_{\rm LS}
is called the coherent part, and
H_{\rm LS}
is a correction Hamiltonian called the Lamb shift satisfying
[H_{\rm LS},H]=0
.
A key property for using the Davies generator as a quantum Gibbs sampler
[
RWW23
,
WT23
]
is that the associated dynamics
e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\rm Davies}}
admits the Gibbs state as a fixed point, equivalently,
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\rm Davies}(\sigma_{\beta})=0
, which can be directly checked from (
1.2
) (see
Section

2.1
for a more detailed discussion).

In general, for a fixed Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}
, it is not an easy task to design a Lindbladian satisfying the fixed-point property
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\sigma_{\beta})=0
. A sufficient condition guaranteeing this is the quantum generalization of the detailed balance condition (DBC)
[
KFGV77
,
FU07
,
FU10
]
(see
Definition

4
). The quantum DBC is a set of criteria for the equilibria of quantum systems, and is crucial in understanding the dynamics and thermodynamics of such systems.

Moreover, for a given precision
\epsilon>0
, we define the mixing time of a Lindblad dynamic with
\sigma_{\beta}
as a unique fixed point by

(1.3)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,;\ \left\|e^{t\mathcal{L^{{\dagger}}}}\left(\rho\right)-\sigma_{\beta}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon,\ \forall\,\text{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0} quantum states} $\rho$}\right\}\,,

which quantifies the convergence speed of the dynamics,
where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
denotes the trace norm. The quantum DBC is also instrumental for proving the finite mixing time (if it is the case), and its scaling with respect to
\beta
and the system size
[
TKR+10
,
KT13
,
BCG+23
,
RFA24
]
.
However, due to the non-commutativity of operators in quantum mechanics, there is no unique definition of
quantum DBC
[
TKR+10
,
CM17
,
CM20
]
.

1.1.
Related works

The most widely studied form of quantum DBC is in the sense of Gelfand–Naimark–Segal (GNS). The seminal result by Alicki
[
Ali76
]
characterizes the class of Lindbladians satisfying the GNS DBC, which turns out to have the same dissipative part as that of the Davies generator; see
Section

2.1
.

The Davies generator
\mathcal{L}_{\rm Davies}
is typically derived as a simplified representation of weakly interacting system-bath models, following the Born-Markov-Secular
1
1

1

The secular approximation is also referred to as the rotating wave approximation (RWA).
approximation route
[
BP02
,
Lid19
]
, and its applicability range seems to be constrained by the limitations of these ideal approximations. However, for the purpose of Gibbs sampling, we may also view the Davies generator as a purely algorithmic process, without mimicking any specific system-bath unitary dynamics in nature. A much more severe issue is that the implementation of
\widehat{A}^{a}(\omega)
requires the resolution of all Bohr frequencies, which is very challenging for most Hamiltonians (see also
Remark

7
). For this reason, earlier works on the quantum thermal state preparation based on Davies generators often suffer from some unphysical assumptions, such as the rounding promise
[
RWW23
,
WT23
]
.

In what follows,
\mathcal{A}
denotes a discrete index set.
The key object in the work
[
CKBG23
]
is the following frequency-dependent jump operator:

(1.4)

\widehat{A}^{a}_{f}(\omega):=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(t)e^{-i\omega t}e^{iHt}A^{a}e^{-iHt}\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R}\,,\quad a\in\mathcal{A}\,,

which is a
f
-weighted Fourier transform of the Heisenberg evolution
A^{a}(t):=e^{iHt}A^{a}e^{-iHt}
of
A^{a}
.
Here
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is a
real
integrable filtering function,
and

\{A^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
is a set of (frequency-independent) coupling operators provided by the user, which represent the coupling between the system and the fictitious environment, akin to designing an artificial thermostat in the classical Monte Carlo or molecular dynamics methods
[
FS02
]
. The choice of
\{A^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
can be flexible and relatively simple (such as Pauli operators). The algorithmic Lindbladian in the Schrödinger picture then takes the form

(1.5)

\mathcal{L}^{{\dagger}}(\rho)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\gamma(\omega)\left(\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega)\rho\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega)^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega)^{\dagger}\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega),\rho\Big\}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,,

where
\gamma(\omega):\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is the transition weight function satisfying
\gamma(-\omega)=e^{\beta\omega}\gamma(\omega)
.
One may easily see from (
1.2
) that the Davies generator can formally correspond to taking the filtering function
f(t)\equiv 1
in (
1.5
), up to a coherent part.

Unfortunately, in this case, since
f
does not decay in
t
, the exact implementation of
\widehat{A}^{a}_{f}(\omega)
in
(
1.4
) requires simulating the Heisenberg evolution
A^{a}(t)
for an infinitely long period. This implies that
in the frequency space, the energy levels of
H
have to be distinguished to infinite precision, which cannot be achieved
in general, except for some special systems such as Hamiltonians with commuting terms.
A natural idea is
to select certain decaying filtering functions
f
in (
1.5
) such that
\mathcal{L}
approximates a Davies generator,
whereas in this case, the generators can only satisfy the
approximate
GNS DBC and their fixed points are not known
a priori
.
Consequently, estimating the deviation of the fixed point of the approximate dynamics from
\sigma_{\beta}
involves tracking the accumulated error along the dynamic trajectory.
It was proved in

[
CKBG23
, Theorems I.1, I.3]

that
to approximate the Gibbs state to precision
\epsilon
, the dynamics should distinguish the energy levels of
H
to precision
{\mathrm{poly}}(\beta^{-1}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{-1}\epsilon)
, where
t_{\rm mix}
is given in
Eq.

1.3
.
It means that the integral in
Eq.

1.4
could be truncated to
T={\mathrm{poly}}(\beta t_{\mathrm{mix}}\epsilon^{-1})
. However, this cost may still be prohibitively high for practical applications.

In the
follow-up
work
[
CKG23
]
, Chen et al. introduced a new quantum Gibbs sampler based on (
1.5
),
which requires a lower energy resolution in simulating
\widehat{A}^{a}_{f}(\omega)
and hence has the reduced computational complexity, by constructing a coherent term
-i[G,\rho]
to
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
in (
1.5
) to make the total Lindbladian
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(X)=i[G,X]+\mathcal{L}(X)
exactly satisfies a less stringent version of DBC called the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) DBC.
To be specific,
[
CKG23
, Section II.C]
showed that the transition part of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
in (
1.5
):

(1.6)

\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\gamma(\omega)\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega)\rho\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega)^{\dagger}\,\mathrm{d}\omega

satisfies the
\sigma
-KMS DBC if
f
and
\gamma
are Gaussians with some parameter constraints. Furthermore, if
f(t)
is chosen to be a real function, then the Gaussian function turns out to be the unique choice (up to a linear combination of
\gamma
, see
[
CKG23
, Appendix D]
). On the other hand,
[
CKG23
, Lemma II.1]
showed one can always design a coherent term
G
such that the superoperator
\mathcal{R}(\rho)-i[G,\rho]
is KMS detailed balanced, where
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is the decay part in (
1.5
):

(1.7)

\mathcal{R}(\rho)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\gamma(\omega)\Big\{\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega)^{\dagger}\widehat{A}_{f}^{a}(\omega),\rho\Big\}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,.

Thanks to the
exact
KMS DBC, the Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}
remains a fixed point of the Lindblad dynamics, and one no longer needs to keep track of the accumulated deviation and distinguish the energy levels of
H
to very high precision.

In this setup, it was proved
[
CKG23
, Theorem I.2]
that

to prepare
\sigma_{\beta}
to precision
\epsilon
, the integral in
Eq.

1.4
can be truncated to
T=\mathcal{O}(\beta\log(t_{\mathrm{mix}}/\epsilon))
and the cost can be significantly reduced.

It is worth pointing out that for each jump proposal
A^{a}
,
the integral with respect to
\omega
in
Eq.

1.5
involves a continuously parameterized set of jump operators
if
\gamma(\omega)
is a continuous function. Although one can discretize
such an integral using a quadrature scheme, the algorithm must be
meticulously designed to efficiently simulate the resulting Lindblad
dynamics
[
CW17
,
CKBG23
,
CKG23
]
. To the best of our knowledge, high-order
Lindblad simulators designed for a finite number of jump operators,
which allow for simpler implementations
[
LW23
,
DLL24
]
, are not suitable for this task.

Along with
[
CKBG23
,
CKG23
]
, there are other algorithms
[
RWW22
]
for the thermal state preparation also aiming to approximate the GNS detailed balanced Lindbladian (i.e., the Davies generator).
Similar to
[
CKBG23
]
, the truncation time of these methods often depends polynomially on
1/\epsilon
, resulting in the total cost scales polynomially with
1/\epsilon
.

In parallel to Alicki’s characterization of Lindblad generators satisfying the GNS DBC, Fagnola, and Umanità
[
FU07
, Theorems 7.2 and 7.3]
have prescribed the necessary and sufficient conditions for a Lindblad dynamics to satisfy the KMS DBC.
This leads to a set of conditions on the jump operators and the Hamiltonian for its corresponding Lindblad generator; see also
[
FU10
,
AC21
]
.

However, it is unclear from the results in
[
FU07
]
how to choose suitable jump operators for the quantum thermal state preparation, and they also did not provide an explicit form of the coherent term required for the KMS detailed balance. In this sense,
[

1.2.
Contribution and outline

In this work, we first provide a detailed introduction to the canonical forms of Lindbladians with various detailed balance conditions in
Section

2
. In particular, in
Section

2.2
, we revisit the arguments in
[
FU07
]
, along the lines of the recent work
[
AC21
]
, and provide a self-contained construction for the
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS detailed balanced Lindbladian with explicit formulations of the jumps and the coherent term (see
Theorem

10
and
Eq.

3.1
). This is the starting point of our quantum Gibbs
samplers constructed and analyzed in
Section

3
.

The idea underlying our quantum Gibbs sampler is analogous to the classical Markov chain-based sampling. In the classical context, a state undergoes a candidate jump according to a transition matrix. The proposed new state is then accepted with a certain probability to ensure that the resulting modified transition matrix satisfies a detailed balance. A natural question that arises when designing efficient quantum Gibbs samplers is:

How to modify a predefined set of jump proposals (i.e., coupling operators) such that the resulting Lindblad dynamics satisfies the quantum detailed balance for the target Gibbs state and meanwhile can be efficiently simulated on a quantum computer?

Based on the structural characterization in
Theorem

10
, we provide an affirmative answer to this question. Our approach enables the use of general filter functions to modify a set of predefined coupling operators, resulting in dynamics that can be efficiently simulated and exactly satisfy the KMS detailed balance condition.

In contrast to
[
CKBG23
,
CKG23
]
, which can be regarded as smoothened approximations of Davies generators, our new framework provides significantly more flexibility in designing quantum Gibbs samplers. Recall from
[
CKG23
, Appendix D]
that to make the transition part (
1.6
) satisfy the KMS DBC, the filtering function
f
in (
1.4
) has to be a Gaussian if it is assumed to be real, and then the outer integration in (
1.6
) with respect to a continuous function
\gamma(\omega)
is necessary. Intuitively, this is because when
f
is real, its Fourier transform must be conjugate symmetric. We need an additional integration to induce the biased energy transition. However, if the main goal is to ensure the KMS detailed balance (instead of approximating Davies generators), by considering a general class of filtering functions with a mild symmetry condition (see (
1.8
)–(
1.9
) below), we find that
f
can be relaxed to be
complex
and the outer integration in
\omega
can be removed.

To be specific, in
Section

3.1
, we show that the admissible jump
L
for KMS detailed balance is of the form: for a self-adjoint operator
A
with
A_{\nu}=\sum_{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}=\nu}P_{i}AP_{j}
,

L=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}e^{-\beta\nu/4}A_{\nu}\,,

where
B_{H}
is the set of Bohr frequencies (see (
2.11
)–(
2.13
) for related definitions). We may overparameterize it
by adding a
weighting function

q(\nu)
:

(1.8)

L=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\widehat{f}(\nu)A_{\nu}\,,\quad\widehat{f}(\nu)=e^{-\beta\nu/4}q(\nu)\,,

with
q
being conjugate symmetric
q(-\nu)=\overline{q(\nu)}
, and then, without loss of generality, we can assume the operator norm
\|A\|\leq 1
. Here, intuitively, the Fourier transform of the filtering function
\widehat{f}
helps modify the transition probability between eigenstates with an energy difference
\nu
to satisfy the KMS detailed balance. This jump operator can be efficiently simulated in the time domain:

(1.9)

L=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{iHt}Ae^{-iHt}\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{with}\quad f(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(\nu)e^{-i\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

when
f(t)
decays rapidly as
\left\lvert t\right\rvert\to\infty
(see also (
2.15
)–(
2.16
)). This is the main motivation for us to consider the
Gevrey function
class with compact support in
Assumption

13
as weighting functions
q(\nu)

[
AHR17
,
HR19
]
, whose Fourier transform has the desired sub-exponential decay as
|t|\to\infty
(see Lemmas
29
and
30
), so that one could approximate the integral (
1.9
) accurately with a moderate truncation time
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\beta\mathrm{log}^{1+o(1)}(t_{\rm mix}/\epsilon))
(see
Proposition

15
and
Theorem

18
).
Once we design a family of efficient jumps
\{L_{a}\}
via
\{(A_{a},f^{a})\}
, the coherent term can be readily defined via
Eq.

2.33
in
Theorem

10
, admitting a similar time integral formulation as (
1.9
) (see
Eqs.

3.10
and
3.11
).
The quantum algorithm for the proposed quantum Gibbs sampler is discussed in detail in
Section

3.3
. In
Section

4
, we elaborate on how to fit the construction of
[
CKG23
]
into our algorithmic framework as a special instance.

In addition, we emphasize that compared to the algorithm in
[
CKG23
]
, our Gibbs sampler only uses one jump operator per jump proposal
A^{a}
. In particular, we can directly achieve a Metropolis-type transition using our framework while exactly satisfying the KMS DBC (see
Fig.

). This greatly simplifies the quantum simulation process. Thanks to

built on the technical

our jump operators can be constructed using the standard linear combination of

[

. As a result, our Lindblad dynamics can be efficiently simulated using
any

[

,

.
We show in

[

have a comparable computational cost when the best

One should note that

1.3.
Discussion and open questions

Despite their importance, the computational complexity of preparing quantum Gibbs states, and the associated task of computing partition functions, is a topic that is being actively debated in the literature
[
BCGW21
,
BCG+24
,
BLMT24
]
. It is particularly challenging to establish quantum computational advantage for Gibbs state preparation at a constant temperature. This is because on one hand, at high enough (constant) temperatures, there exist polynomial-time classical algorithms to sample from Gibbs states and to estimate partition functions
[
BCGW21
,
MH21
,
YL23
,
BLMT24
]
. On the other hand, in the low-temperature regime, preparing classical Gibbs states is already
\mathrm{NP}
-hard in the worst case
[
Bar82
,
Sly10
]
.
When the temperature is sufficiently low, the quantum Gibbs state can have high overlap with the ground state, and the task of cooling to these temperatures should be
\mathrm{QMA}
-hard in the worst case. So we do not expect generically efficient quantum algorithms in these cases. In this line, recent developments
[
RFA24
,
BCL24
,
RW24
]
have used Gibbs samplers to provide new insights from the complexity theory perspective. Specifically, let
n
be the number of qubits.
[
BCL24
]
proved that there exists a family of
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
-local Hamiltonians such that sampling from their associated Gibbs states at constant temperatures can be achieved in time
T={\mathrm{poly}}(n)
by the Davies generator
[
Dav74
]
with the block-encoding framework
[
CKBG23
]
, but is classically intractable assuming no collapse of the polynomial hierarchy. Under the same assumptions,
[
RW24
]
recently improved this result to
k
-local Hamiltonians at a constant temperature lower than the classically simulatable threshold. Furthermore,
[
RFA24
]
showed that simulating the Lindbladian in
[
CKG23
]
at
\beta=\Omega(\log(n))
to runtime
T=\mathcal{O}({\mathrm{poly}}(n))
for a
k
-local Hamiltonian is
\mathrm{BQP}
-complete.
Consequently, the development of practical quantum Gibbs samplers, which is the focus of our work, emerges as a critical endeavor in realizing these potential quantum advantages.

There exist a series of algorithms
[
PW09
,
CS17
,
VAGGdW17
,
GSLW19
,
ACL23
]
that require only quantum access to
H
without additional information (such as coupling operators). The cost of these algorithms is deterministic and scales as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N/\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}{\mathrm{poly}}(\beta,\log\epsilon^{-1}))
.
These algorithms can perform efficiently in the extremely high-temperature regime, where
\beta
approaches to zero and
\sqrt{N/\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}\sim 1
(assuming the smallest eigenvalue of
H
is zero).
However, they become significantly less efficient in the low-temperature regime, where
\beta
is large, and
\sqrt{N/\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}\sim\sqrt{N}
.
Furthermore, the factor
\sqrt{N/\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}
is explicitly present in the algorithm, and the average-case complexity is not very different from the worst-case scenario.
On the other hand, the computational cost of open-system quantum dynamics is primarily determined by the mixing time, which can vary significantly across different systems. Besides the Lindblad dynamics, alternative open-system dynamics formalisms are also viable
[
TOV+11
,
YAG12
,
SM23
,
Cub23
]
for Gibbs state preparation.
We may anticipate that for certain classes of physical Hamiltonians, even at low temperatures, Gibbs sampling could be executed efficiently. This possibility does not contradict the statement that preparing the ground state of
H
(when
\beta=\infty
) remains QMA-hard in the worst-case scenario
[
KSV02
,
AGIK09
]
.

We emphasize that in this work, an
efficient
quantum Gibbs sampler means that the proposed Gibbs sampler can be efficiently simulated on a quantum computer, while in classical MCMC literature,
efficient
often indicates rapid mixing. The rigorous bounding of the mixing time has been achieved for Davies generators for some general families of local commuting Hamiltonians
[
KB16
,
BCG+23
,
KACR24
]
,
whereas establishing the mixing time for non-commuting Hamiltonians at moderate or even low temperatures presents a substantial theoretical challenge. There are two interesting works along this line. The first is that Rouzé et al
[
RFA24
]
established the spectral gap of KMS detailed balanced Lindbladians for certain
k
-local Hamiltonians at high temperatures using Lieb-Robinson estimates.
The analysis in
[
RFA24
]
may be applicable in our setting and we plan to investigate this in detail in a future work.
On the other hand, Bakshi et al
[
BLMT24
]
demonstrated that at the high temperature, the Gibbs State of certain
k
-local Hamiltonian becomes a linear combination of tensor products of stabilizer states, can be prepared in polynomial time using randomized classical algorithms. This suggests that exploring the relationship between the complexity of Gibbs states and mixing times could be a fruitful avenue for future research.

It is also noteworthy that introducing a nontrivial coherent term
i[G,\cdot]
with
[G,\sigma_{\beta}]=0
to any

\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS detailed balanced Lindbladian
potentially disrupts
the detailed balance condition, but the Gibbs state remains a fixed point. The influence of the coherent term on the mixing time may be significant and its characterization
remains a largely open question. We refer interested readers to
[
FLT24
,
LL24
]
for the recent progress on analyzing the coherent term effect via hypocoercivity.

Finally, the mixing time
t_{\rm mix}
may be very different across different quantum Gibbs samplers. Both theoretical and numerical evidence are needed in order to quantify the mixing time and to compare the efficiency of quantum Gibbs samplers for physical systems of interest.

The diamond norm of a superoperator

We adopt the following asymptotic notations beside the usual big
\mathcal{O}

2.
Structures of detailed balanced Lindbladians

In this section, we present the canonical forms of the Lindbladians with detailed balance conditions and discuss their feasibility for implementation on a quantum computer.

We first recall that a quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) map, and a quantum Markov semigroup (QMS)
2
2

2

With some abuse of terminology, we will refer to both
\mathcal{P}_{t}
and its adjoint dynamics
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}_{t}
as a QMS.

(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, also called Lindblad dynamics, is defined as a
C_{0}
-semigroup of completely positive, unital maps. The generator

\mathcal{L}(X):=\lim_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t\to 0^{+}}}t^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_{t}(X)-X)

is usually referred to as the Lindbladian, which has the following GKSL form
[
Lin76
,
GKS76
]
.

Lemma 1
.

For any generator
\mathcal{L}
of a QMS
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, there exist operators
L_{j},K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}(X)=\Psi(X)+K^{\dagger}X+XK\,,

where
\Psi(\cdot)
is completely positive with the Kraus representation:

(2.2)

\Psi(X)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}^{\dagger}XL_{j}\,,

with
\mathcal{J}
being an index set with cardinality
|\mathcal{J}|\leq N^{2}
.
In particular, letting
G:=\frac{K^{\dagger}-K}{2i}
, we have the Lindbladian form:

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}(X)=i[G,X]+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left(L_{j}^{\dagger}XL_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},X\right\}\right)\,,

where
i[G,X]
and
\mathcal{L}(X)-i[G,X]
are the coherent and dissipative parts of dynamics, respectively.

The operators
L_{j}
in (
2.3
) are called jump operators, which are non-unique for a given Lindbladian.
The formula (
2.3
) is a direct consequence of (
2.1
). In fact, from
\mathcal{L}({\bf 1})=0
with (
2.1
), the operator
K
can be written as
K=V-iG
with
V=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}
, where
V:=\frac{K^{\dagger}+K}{2}
and
G:=\frac{K^{\dagger}-K}{2i}
are self-adjoint operators. Plugging this into (
2.1
) readily gives (
2.3
).

For the purpose of quantum state preparation, we are interested in those QMS converging to a given full-rank state
\sigma>0
, i.e.,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}_{t}(\rho)=\sigma\,,\quad\forall\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\,,

equivalently, the irreducible QMS
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}_{t}

[
Wol12
, Proposition 7.5]
.
For readers’ convenience, we recall the definition of irreducibility and some equivalent conditions. We say that a quantum channel
\Phi
is irreducible if all the orthogonal projections
P
satisfying
\Phi(P\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})P)\subset P\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})P
are trivial, i.e., zero or identity. The following results are adapted from
[
Wol12
,
ZB23
]
.

Lemma 2
.

A QMS
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}
is irreducible if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

•

\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}_{t}
(as a quantum channel) is irreducible for some
t_{0}>0
.

•

There exists a unique full-rank invariant state
\sigma
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\sigma)=0
.

•

The
\mathbb{C}
-algebra generated by the jump operators
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
and
K=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}-iG
gives the whole algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

•

The operators
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
and
K
have no trivial common invariant subspace.

Lemma 3

(
[
Wol12
, Theorem 7.2]
)
.

If the QMS
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}_{t}
admits a full-rank invariant state, then

\{G,L_{j},L_{j}^{\dagger}\}^{\prime}=\ker(\mathcal{L})\,,

where the commutant
\{G,L_{j},L_{j}^{\dagger}\}^{\prime}
is defined by all the operators commuting with
L_{j}
,
L_{j}^{\dagger}
and
G
. It follows that in this case, the irreducibility is also equivalent to

(2.5)

\{G,L_{j},L_{j}^{\dagger}\}^{\prime}=\{z{\bf 1}\,;\ z\in\mathbb{C}\}\,.

We next discuss the quantum detailed balance condition (DBC), which provides a sufficient criterion to guarantee steady states of a Lindbladian.
For a given full-rank state

\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
, we define the modular operator:

\Delta_{\sigma}(X)=\sigma X\sigma^{-1}:\ \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,,

and the weighting operator:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\sigma}X=\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}X\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}:\ \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,.

We also let
L_{\sigma}(X)=\sigma X
and
R_{\sigma}(X)=X\sigma
be the left and right multiplication operators, respectively.
Then, for any
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
satisfying
f(1)=1
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
, we define the operator:

(2.6)

\displaystyle J_{\sigma}^{f}:=R_{\sigma}f(\Delta_{\sigma}):\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,,

and the associated inner product:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{\sigma,f}:=\langle X,J_{\sigma}^{f}(Y)\rangle\,.

In particular, for
f=x^{1-s}
with
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the above inner product gives

(2.8)

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{\sigma,s}:=\tr(\sigma^{s}X^{\dagger}\sigma^{1-s}Y)\,,\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma,1}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma,1/2}
are the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS) and Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) inner products, respectively.

Definition 4
.

A QMS
\mathcal{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
satisfies the
J_{\sigma}^{f}
-DBC for some
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
if the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma,f}
, equivalently,

\displaystyle J_{\sigma}^{f}\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}J_{\sigma}^{f}\,.

In the cases of
f=1
and
x^{1/2}
, it is called
\sigma
-
GNS DBC
and
\sigma
-
KMS DBC
, respectively.

By the above definition, we find that if
\mathcal{P}_{t}
satisfies the
J_{\sigma}^{f}
-DBC, there holds

0=\langle X,\mathcal{L}({\bf 1})\rangle_{\sigma,f}=\langle\mathcal{L}(X),J_{\sigma}^{f}({\bf 1})\rangle=\langle X,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\rangle\,,\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,,

which gives
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}(\sigma)=0
, namely,
\sigma
is an invariant state of
\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\dagger}
. The following lemma relates different concepts of detailed balance conditions; see
[
CM17
, Lemma 2.5 and Theorem 2.9]
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
be a full-rank quantum state and
\mathcal{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
be a QMS. Then,

•

If
\mathcal{P}_{t}
satisfies the
\sigma
-
GNS DBC
, then it satisfies the
J_{\sigma}^{f}
-DBC for any
f
and the generator
\mathcal{L}
commutes with the modular operator
\Delta_{\sigma}
.

•

If
\mathcal{P}_{t}
satisfies the
J_{\sigma}^{f}
-DBC for
f=x^{1-s}
,
s\in[0,1]\backslash\{\frac{1}{2}\}
, then it also satisfies
\sigma
-
GNS DBC
.

The above lemma means that the quantum DBC for the inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma,s}
with
s\in[0,1]\backslash\{\frac{1}{2}\}
are all equivalent, and they are stronger notions than
\sigma
-KMS DBC (i.e.,
s=\frac{1}{2}
). In fact, one can show that the class of QMS with
\sigma
-KMS DBC is strictly larger than the class of QMS satisfying
\sigma
-GNS DBC
[
CM17
, Appendix B]
. These properties underscore the special roles played by the KMS and GNS detailed balance when analyzing Lindblad dynamics.

2.1.
Davies generator and GNS-detailed balance

Let
H
be a quantum Hamiltonian on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with the eigendecomposition:

(2.9)

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}P_{i}\,,

where
P_{i}
is the orthogonal projector to the eigenspace associated with the energy
\lambda_{i}
. Given an inverse temperature
\beta>0
, the corresponding Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}
is defined by

(2.10)

\sigma_{\beta}:=e^{-\beta H}/\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\,,

with
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\tr(e^{-\beta H})
being the normalization constant called the partition function. It is easy to see that any full-rank quantum state can be written as a Gibbs state
\sigma=e^{-h}
with
h=-\log(\sigma)
.

Recall that our main aim is to design an efficient quantum Gibbs sampler via QMS. An important class of Lindbladians for this purpose are Davies generators, which describe the weak coupling limit of a system coupled to a large thermal bath
[
Dav76
,
Dav79
]
. It has natural applications in thermal state preparations but with inherent difficulties from the energy-time uncertainty principle
[
ML20
,
RWW23
,
CKBG23
]
; see
Remark

7
below.
We next review the canonical form of Davies generators and show that they essentially characterize the Lindbladians with GNS-DBC
[
KFGV77
]
.

For the Hamiltonian (
2.9
), we define the set of Bohr frequencies by

(2.11)

B_{H}=\left\{\nu=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\,;\ \lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\in{\rm Spec}(H)\right\}\,,

which is a sequence of real numbers symmetric with respect to
0
. Here,
{\rm Spec}(H)
denotes the spectral set of
H
. Then, for any bounded operator
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, one can write

(2.12)

\displaystyle A=\sum_{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\in{\rm Spec}(H)}P_{i}AP_{j}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}A_{\nu}\,,

where

(2.13)

A_{\nu}:=\sum_{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}=\nu}P_{i}AP_{j}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad(A_{\nu})^{\dagger}=(A^{\dagger})_{-\nu}\,,

is an eigenstate of the modular
operator
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}
(see
Eq.

2.21
below).
Such a decomposition (
2.12
) naturally relates to the Heisenberg evolution of
A
:

(2.14)

\displaystyle A(t):=e^{iHt}Ae^{-iHt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}A_{\nu}e^{i\nu t}\,,\quad\text{equivalently},\quad[H,A_{\nu}]=\nu A_{\nu}\,.

For our later use, we introduce a weighted integral
A_{f}
of the Heisenberg evolution
A(t)
with respect to an
L^{1}
-integrable function
f
: for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

(2.15)

A_{f}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(t)f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}A_{\nu}\widehat{f}(\nu)\,,

where
\widehat{f}(\nu)
is the inverse Fourier transform of
f(t)
:

\widehat{f}(\nu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{i\nu t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

Moreover, by a direct computation, we have

(2.16)

A_{f}=\widehat{f}(-\beta^{-1}\log\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}})A\,,

where the operator
\widehat{f}(-\beta^{-1}\log\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}})
is defined by functional calculus based on the modular operator
\Delta_{\sigma}
, noting that the Fourier transform of an
L^{1}
function is bounded and uniformly continuous.

The Davies Lindbladian is generally of the form:

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\beta}(X):=i[H,X]+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\mathcal{L}_{a,\nu}(X)\,,\quad X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,,

where the dissipative generators are given by

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a,\nu}(X)=\gamma_{a}(\nu)\left((A_{\nu}^{a})^{\dagger}XA_{\nu}^{a}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{(A_{\nu}^{a})^{\dagger}A_{\nu}^{a},X\right\}\right)\,.

Here, the index
a\in\mathcal{A}
sums over all the coupling operators
A^{a}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
to the environment that satisfy
\{A^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}=\{(A^{a})^{{\dagger}}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, and
\gamma_{a}(\cdot)
are the Fourier transforms of
the bath correlation functions, which are nonnegative and bounded. The jump operators
\{A_{\nu}^{a}\}
associated with a coupling
A^{a}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are defined by
Eqs.

2.12
and
2.13
:

(2.19)

A^{a}=\sum_{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\in{\rm Spec}(H)}P_{i}A^{a}P_{j}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}A_{\nu}^{a},

which gives the transitions from the eigenvectors of
H
with energy
E
to those with
E+\nu
. In addition, the following relations hold, for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\nu
,

(2.20)

\gamma_{a}(-\nu)=e^{\beta\nu}\gamma_{a}(\nu)\,,

and

(2.21)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}(A_{\nu}^{a})=e^{-\beta\nu}A_{\nu}^{a}\,,

that is,
A_{\nu}^{a}
is an eigenvector of
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}
with the eigenvalue
e^{-\beta\nu}
.
Note that the condition (
2.21
) holds by the definition of
A_{\nu}^{a}
, while
the other one
(
2.20
) is often referred to as KMS condition
3
3

3

The KMS condition should not be confused with the KMS detailed balance condition. These two terms are mathematically unrelated.

[
KFGV77
]
.

These two conditions,
as we shall see below, ensure the GNS-reversibility of the Lindbladian.

The following canonical form for the QMS that satisfies the
\sigma
-GNS DBC is due to Alicki
[
Ali76
]
.

Lemma 6
.

For a Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
satisfying
\sigma_{\beta}
-
GNS DBC
, there holds

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}^{\dagger}[X,L_{j}]+e^{\omega_{j}/2}[L_{j},X]L^{\dagger}_{j}\right)\,,

with
\omega_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, where

\mathcal{J}
is an index set with cardinality
|\mathcal{J}|\leq N^{2}-1
, and

L_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfies

(2.23)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}(L_{j})=e^{-\omega_{j}}L_{j}\,,\quad\tr(L^{\dagger}_{j}L_{k})=c_{j}\delta_{j,k}\,,\quad\tr(L_{j})=0\,,

for normalization constants
c_{j}>0
, and for each
j
, there exists
j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}
such that

(2.24)

\displaystyle L_{j}^{\dagger}=L_{j^{\prime}}\,,\quad\omega_{j}=-\omega_{j^{\prime}}\,.

It is easy to see that the Davies semigroup (
2.17
) is exactly the class of QMS with GNS-DBC, up to the coherent term
i[H,\cdot]
. Indeed, we define
g_{a}(\nu):=e^{\beta\nu/2}\gamma_{a}(\nu)
and find
g_{a}(\nu)=g_{a}(-\nu)
by the KMS condition (
2.20
). Then, letting
L_{a,\nu}:=g_{a}(\nu)^{1/2}A_{\nu}^{a}
, it follows from (
2.17
) and (
2.18
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\beta}(X)

\displaystyle=i[H,X]+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}e^{-\beta\nu/2}\left(L_{a,\nu}^{\dagger}XL_{a,\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{a,\nu}^{\dagger}L_{a,\nu},X\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=i[H,X]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}e^{-\beta\nu/2}L_{a,\nu}^{\dagger}[X,L_{a,\nu}]+e^{\beta\nu/2}[L_{a,\nu},X]L_{a,\nu}^{\dagger}\,,

where the dissipative part exactly satisfies the conditions in
Lemma

6
by re-indexing
j=(a,\nu)
.

Remark 7
.

As reviewed in the introduction, the Davies generator
\mathcal{L}_{\beta}
in (
2.17
) has been adopted for the thermal state preparation
[
RWW23
,
CKBG23
,
CKBG23
]
.
However, implementing it with a small error
requires accurately resolving
all the Bohr frequencies
\nu
, while the minimal gap between two Bohr frequencies
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
could be exponentially small in the worst case.

By the energy-time uncertainty principle, this
means an impractically long Hamiltonian simulation time and is a key obstacle in directly leveraging the Davies semigroup as an
efficient
quantum Gibbs sampler
[
CKBG23
]
.

It is also worth mentioning that the sum over Bohr frequencies (
2.17
) is derived from a secular approximation
[
BP02
]
, which may be regarded as theoretical evidence that the GNS detailed balance is an idealized construction and thus is difficult to
be efficiently implemented with desired accuracy.

2.2.
KMS-detailed balanced generators

Recalling that the KMS DBC is a weaker property compared to the GNS one, but can still guarantee the Gibbs state as a fixed point of the dynamics,
one may expect that the KMS-detailed balanced QMS can provide a more efficient class of Gibbs state preparation algorithms.
The first complete characterization of the
\sigma
-KMS detailed balanced Lindbladian was given in
[
FU07
, Theorems 7.2 and 7.3]
, where some equivalent conditions on the coherent term and jump operators were proposed. In this section, we revisit their arguments along the lines of the recent work
[
AC21
]
and
introduce the canonical form of the QMS satisfying
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC with a self-contained proof, which are the starting points of our quantum Gibbs samplers.

Let
\mathfrak{H}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
be the space of superoperators. We define the subspace
\mathfrak{H}_{S}
consisting of
\Phi\in\mathfrak{H}
of the form: for some
X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

(2.25)

\Phi(A)=XA+AY\,,

and denote by
\mathfrak{H}_{S}^{\perp}
its orthogonal complement
for the normalized Hilbert–Schmidt inner product on superoperators, which is defined as follows: for
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathfrak{H}
,

\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\langle\Phi(X_{i,j}),\Phi(X_{i,j})\rangle\,,

with
\{X_{i,j}\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
being any orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\langle X_{i,j},X_{k,l}\rangle=\tr(X_{i,j}^{\dagger}X_{k,l})=\delta_{(i,j),(k,l)}\,.}

The following useful lemma characterizes the freedom of
X,Y
in (
2.25
)
4
4

4

This result is from
[
AC21
, Lemma 3.10 and Remark 3.11]
, which include some typos in the arguments. We provide a short proof here for the reader’s convenience.
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\Phi
be a superoperator with the representation (
2.25
). If some
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
gives the same
\Phi
via (
2.25
), then
X^{\prime}=X+\eta{\bf 1}
and
Y^{\prime}=Y-\eta{\bf 1}
for some
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Let
F_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=(i,j)
and
1\leq i,j\leq N
be a basis of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying

\langle F_{\alpha},F_{\beta}\rangle/N=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
,

F_{(1,1)}={\bf 1}
, and
F^{\dagger}_{(i,j)}=F_{(j,i)}
, and let
E_{i,j}
be
another basis defined by
E_{i,j}=\ket{u_{i}}\bra{u_{j}}
, where
\{u_{j}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Suppose that
\Phi(A)=XA+AY
for some
X,Y
.
Note that
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(X)=F_{\alpha}^{\dagger}AF_{\beta}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{H}
by computing

\langle\Phi_{\alpha,\beta},\Phi_{\gamma,\eta}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}=\frac{1}{N}\langle F_{\gamma},F_{\alpha}\rangle\frac{1}{N}\langle F_{\beta},F_{\eta}\rangle=\delta_{(\alpha,\beta),(\gamma,\eta)}\,.

We then
compute the coefficients for the
orthogonal
expansion of
\Phi(A)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(C_{\Phi})_{\alpha,\beta}F_{\alpha}^{\dagger}AF_{\beta}
:

\displaystyle(C_{\Phi})_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{N^{2}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\tr\left[(F_{\alpha}^{\dagger}E_{i,j}F_{\beta})^{\dagger}\Phi(E_{i,j})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\tr[F_{\beta}^{\dagger}]\tr[F_{\alpha}X]+\tr[F_{\alpha}]\tr[F_{\beta}^{\dagger}Y]\right)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\delta_{\beta,(1,1)}\tr[F_{\alpha}X]+\delta_{\alpha,(1,1)}\tr[F_{\beta}^{\dagger}Y]\right)\,,

which is zero if
\alpha,\beta\neq(1,1)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Phi(A)=(C_{\Phi})_{(1,1),(1,1)}A+\sum_{\alpha\neq(1,1)}(C_{\Phi})_{\alpha,(1,1)}F_{\alpha}^{\dagger}A+\sum_{\beta\neq(1,1)}(C_{\Phi})_{(1,1),\beta}AF_{\beta}\,.

Therefore, any
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
such that
\Phi(A)=X^{\prime}A+AY^{\prime}
satisfy

\displaystyle X^{\prime}=\sum_{\alpha\neq(1,1)}(C_{\Phi})_{\alpha,(1,1)}F_{\alpha}^{\dagger}+a{\bf 1}\,,\quad Y^{\prime}=\sum_{\beta\neq(1,1)}(C_{\Phi})_{(1,1),\beta}F_{\beta}+b{\bf 1}\,,

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
with
a+b=(C_{\Phi})_{(1,1),(1,1)}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We next discuss the structure of QMS satisfying
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC for some Gibbs state
\sigma_{\beta}
(
2.10
).

Lemma 9
.

A Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC if and only if
\mathcal{L}
has the form:

(2.26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)=\Psi(X)+\Phi(X)\,,\quad

with the CP operator
\Psi(\cdot)
admitting the Kraus representation (
2.2
) and the operator

(2.27)

\Phi(X):=K^{\dagger}X+XK\,,\quad\text{for some $K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})$}\,,

and both
\Psi
and
\Phi
are self-adjoint with respect to the KMS inner product. In this case, there exist the jump operators
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
and the operator
K
in
Eq.

2.27
satisfying

(2.28)

\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}L_{j}=L_{j}^{\dagger}\,,

and

(2.29)

\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}K=K^{\dagger}\,.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the
only if
part. Recalling the structure of a Lindbladian in
Lemma

1
, without loss of generality, we assume
\tr(L_{j})=0
by replacing
L_{j}
with
L_{j}-\tr(L_{j}){\bf 1}
and
K
with
K+\sum_{j}\tr(L_{j}^{\dagger})L_{j}-\frac{1}{2}|\tr(L_{j})|^{2}
. Then, by
[
AC21
, Lemma 3.12]
, there holds
\Psi(\cdot)\in\mathfrak{H}_{S}^{\perp}
. According to
[
AC21
, Lemma 3.13]
, the subspaces
\mathfrak{H}_{S}
and
\mathfrak{H}_{S}^{\perp}
are invariant under the adjoint with respect to the KMS inner product. By
\Phi\in\mathfrak{H}_{S}
and
\Psi\in\mathfrak{H}_{S}^{\perp}
, it holds that adjoints
\Phi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}\in\mathfrak{H}_{S}
and
\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}\in\mathfrak{H}_{S}
, where
\Phi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}
and
\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}
are adjoints of
\Phi
and
\Psi
for the KMS inner product. Thus, the self-adjointness
\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}+\Phi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}=\Psi+\Phi
implies

\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}=\Psi
and
\Phi^{\dagger}_{{\rm KMS}}=\Phi
.

Next, since
\Psi
is a KMS-detailed balanced CP map, (
2.28
) is implied by the structure result
[
AC21
, Theorem 4.1]
. To show (
2.29
), by the invariance of
\Phi=K^{\dagger}X+XK
for adding a pure imaginary
ic{\bf 1}
(
c\in\mathbb{R}
) to
K
, without loss of generality, we can assume
\tr(K)\in\mathbb{R}
, which further implies
\tr(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}K)\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows from
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC of
\Phi
that
\Gamma_{\sigma_{\beta}}\Phi=\Phi^{\dagger}\Gamma_{\sigma_{\beta}}
, equivalently,

K^{\dagger}X+XK=(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}K)X+X(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}K^{\dagger})\,.

Then, by
Lemma

8
, we derive
K^{\dagger}=\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}K+\eta{\bf 1}
for some
\eta\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\eta
must be zero, thanks to
\tr(K^{\dagger})=\tr(K)=\tr(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}K)\in\mathbb{R}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We proceed to derive an explicit formula for the operator
K
. Recalling the decomposition
K=V-iG
with
V=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}
(
see after
Lemma

1
), it suffices to find an expression for
G
. To do so, we reformulate the constraint (
2.29
) above as a Lyapunov equation:

\displaystyle G\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}+\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}G=i(\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}V-V\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2})\,.

It can be uniquely solved as

(2.30)

G=i\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}}(\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}V-V\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2})e^{-t\sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}t\,.

To simplify the formula, we note that for any
\lambda,\mu>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\lambda^{1/2}}e^{-t\mu^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{\lambda^{1/2}+\mu^{1/2}}\,,

and
\tanh(\log(x^{1/4}))=\frac{x^{1/2}-1}{x^{1/2}+1}
. Then, by functional calculus and (
2.30
),
it holds that

(2.31)

G=i\frac{L_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}-R_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}}{L_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}+R_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}}(V)=i\frac{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}-I}{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}+I}(V)=i\tanh\circ\log(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/4})(V)\,.

We summarize the above discussion in the following proposition.

Theorem 10
.

A Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC if and only if there exist linear operators
L_{j},G\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)=i[G,X]+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left(L_{j}^{\dagger}XL_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},X\right\}\right)\,,

with
L_{j}

satisfying

\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}L_{j}=L_{j}^{\dagger}\,,

and

G
being self-adjoint and given by

(2.33)

G:=-i\tanh\circ\log(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/4})\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}\right)\,.

We have shown in
Lemma

5
that the Lindbladians with
\sigma_{\beta}
-GNS DBC is a subclass of those with
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC, but this property cannot be easily seen from the corresponding structural results (cf.
Lemma

6
and
Theorem

10
), noting that an eigenvector
L
of the operator
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}
is generally not a solution to (
2.28
). To fill this gap, we next show that the canonical form (
2.22
) of a QMS with GNS DBC can be indeed reformulated as the one (
2.32
) for KMS DBC.

Corollary 11
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindbladian with
\sigma_{\beta}
-
GNS DBC
of the form:

(2.34)

\mathcal{L}(X)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{L}_{j}(X)\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{L}_{j}(X)=2e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\left(L_{j}^{\dagger}XL_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},X\right\}\right)\,,

where the jumps
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
satisfy the conditions in
Lemma

6
. Then, we can reformulate it in the form of a KMS detailed balanced Lindbladian (
2.32
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J},\,L_{j}^{\dagger}=L_{j}}\mathcal{L}_{j}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J},\,L_{j}^{\dagger}\neq L_{j}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(X)\,,

with

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{j}(X)=\widetilde{L}_{j,1}^{\dagger}X\widetilde{L}_{j,1}+\widetilde{L}_{j,2}^{\dagger}X\widetilde{L}_{j,2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\widetilde{L}_{j,1}^{\dagger}\widetilde{L}_{j,1},X\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\widetilde{L}_{j,2}^{\dagger}\widetilde{L}_{j,2},X\right\}\,,

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{j,1}:=e^{-\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}+e^{\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}^{\dagger}\,,\quad\widetilde{L}_{j,2}:=i(-e^{-\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}+e^{\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}^{\dagger})

satisfy the constraint (
2.28
).

Proof.

If
L_{j}
is self-adjoint, there hold
\omega_{j}=0
and

\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}L_{j}=L_{j}=L_{j}^{\dagger}
. By the formula (
2.31
) of
G
, the associated Hamiltonian is given by

G_{j}:=-\frac{i}{2}\frac{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}-I}{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}+I}(L_{j}^{2})=0\,.

Thus, in this case,
\mathcal{L}_{j}
in (
2.34
) satisfies the canonical form (
2.32
) in
Theorem

10
.

We next consider the case where
L_{j}
is not self-adjoint. Let
j^{\prime}
be the adjoint index for
j
specified in (
2.24
). It follows that

\mathcal{L}_{j}(X)+\mathcal{L}_{j^{\prime}}(X)=2e^{-\omega_{j}/2}\left(L_{j}^{\dagger}XL_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},X\right\}\right)+2e^{\omega_{j}/2}\left(L_{j}XL_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{j}L_{j}^{\dagger},X\right\}\right)\,.

We consider the equation
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}\widetilde{L}=\widetilde{L}^{\dagger}
with ansatz
\widetilde{L}=aL_{j}+bL_{j}^{\dagger}
,
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
:

\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}(aL_{j}+bL_{j}^{\dagger})=ae^{\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}+be^{-\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}^{\dagger}=\overline{a}L_{j}^{\dagger}+\overline{b}L_{j}\,.

It is easy to check that the coefficients
(a,b)
being real linear combinations of vectors
(e^{-\omega_{j}/2},1)
and
(-e^{-\omega_{j}/2}i,i)
satisfy
a=\overline{b}e^{-\omega_{j}/2}
, and the corresponding
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
solves
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}\widetilde{L}=\widetilde{L}^{\dagger}
. We define

(2.35)

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{1}=e^{-\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}+e^{\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}^{\dagger}\,,\quad\widetilde{L}_{2}=i(-e^{-\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}+e^{\omega_{j}/4}L_{j}^{\dagger})\,.

A direct computation gives, for
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,

\widetilde{L}_{1}^{\dagger}X\widetilde{L}_{1}+\widetilde{L}_{2}^{\dagger}X\widetilde{L}_{2}=2e^{-\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}^{\dagger}XL_{j}+2e^{\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}XL_{j}^{\dagger}\,,

and

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\frac{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}-I}{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}+I}\left(\widetilde{L}_{1}^{\dagger}\widetilde{L}_{1}+\widetilde{L}^{\dagger}_{2}\widetilde{L}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-i\frac{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}-I}{\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/2}+I}\left(e^{-\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}+e^{\omega_{j}/2}L_{j}L_{j}^{\dagger}\right)=0\,,

by noting that
L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}
and
L_{j}L_{j}^{\dagger}
are eigenvectors of
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}
associated with eigenvalue one. Therefore, for non-self-adjoint
L_{j}
, the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{j}+\mathcal{L}_{j^{\prime}}
also matches with the form (
2.32
). The proof is complete by the linearity of Lindbladians.
∎

3.
A family of efficient quantum Gibbs samplers

In this section, we present a general framework for designing
efficiently simulable
quantum Gibbs samplers via Lindblad dynamics satisfying
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC,
based on the representation
Theorem

10
.

3.1.
Quantum Gibbs samplers via KMS-detailed balanced Lindbladian

By
Theorem

10
, the class of KMS detailed balanced Lindbladians can be parameterized by

•

a set of jump operators for the Lindbladian
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
satisfying (
2.28
):
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/2}L_{j}=L_{j}^{\dagger}
;

•

a coherent term
G
defined as in (
2.33
) via
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
.

We first reformulate the condition (
2.28
) into a more convenient form.

Note that (
2.28
) is equivalent to
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/4}L_{j}=\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/4}L_{j}^{\dagger}
, namely,
\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{-1/4}L_{j}
is self-adjoint. Then the admissible set of jump operators can be given by

\{L\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\,;\ L=\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/4}\widetilde{A}\ \ \text{with}\ \ \widetilde{A}=\widetilde{A}^{\dagger}\}\,.

From the eigendecomposition of
H
in (
2.9
), we have

(3.1)

L=\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/4}\widetilde{A}=\sum_{i,j}e^{-\beta(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})/4}P_{i}\widetilde{A}P_{j}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}e^{-\beta\nu/4}\widetilde{A}_{\nu}\,,

where
\widetilde{A}_{\nu}
is defined by (
2.13
) for some self-adjoint
\widetilde{A}
.

Suppose that we are given a set of self-adjoint coupling operators
\{A^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}

as predefined jump proposals (coupling operators), where
\mathcal{A}
is a discrete index set
. For each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we choose a weighting function
q^{a}(\nu):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

(3.2)

q^{a}(-\nu)=\overline{q^{a}(\nu)},\quad\widehat{f^{a}}(\nu):=q^{a}(\nu)e^{-\beta\nu/4}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\,.

Here
q^{a}
is chosen to be a symmetric function so that
L_{a}
is an admissible jump (i.e., satisfies the condition (
2.28
)), see
Eq.

3.5
.

We then construct the jump operator
associated with the pair
(A^{a},q^{a})
:

(3.3)

L_{a}:=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}q^{a}(\nu)e^{-\beta\nu/4}A^{a}_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\widehat{f^{a}}(\nu)A^{a}_{\nu}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f^{a}(t)A^{a}(t)\mathrm{d}t\,,

as a weighted integral of
A^{a}
(see
Eq.

2.15
),
where the Fourier transform of
\widehat{f^{a}}
:

(3.4)

f^{a}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}q^{a}(\nu)e^{-\beta\nu/4}e^{-it\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\nu

gives the filtering function in the time domain.
To verify that
L_{a}
satisfies the condition (
2.28
), it suffices to note that
\widetilde{A}^{a}_{\nu}:=q^{a}(\nu)A^{a}_{\nu}
corresponds to a self-adjoint operator defined by
\widetilde{A}^{a}:=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\widetilde{A}^{a}_{\nu}
:

(3.5)

(\widetilde{A}^{a})^{{\dagger}}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}(\widetilde{A}^{a})^{{\dagger}}_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\overline{q^{a}(\nu)}(A^{a}_{\nu})^{{\dagger}}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}q^{a}(-\nu)A^{a}_{-\nu}=\widetilde{A}^{a}\,.

We see from (
3.3
) that
each
L_{a}
is a linear combination of the Heisenberg evolution
A^{a}(t)=e^{iHt}A^{a}e^{-iHt}
, and
by (
2.16
),
L_{a}
has an alternative representation:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L_{a}=\widehat{f^{a}}(-\beta^{-1}\log\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}})A^{a}\,.}

Here, the filtering function
\widehat{f^{a}}(\nu)
is used to modify the transition between eigenvectors of
H
with an energy difference of

\nu
, similar to the rejection step in the classical sampling, and the associated operator
\widehat{f^{a}}(-\beta^{-1}\log\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}})
maps the predefined coupling operators
A^{a}
to the desired jumps
L_{a}
for the
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS detailed balance. Moreover, due to the introduction of
q^{a}
in
L_{a}
, without loss of generality, we can let
\left\lVert A^{a}\right\rVert\leq 1
by rescaling the norm.

We proceed to construct the coherent part via the formula (
2.33
):

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle:=-i\tanh\circ\log(\Delta_{\sigma_{\beta}}^{1/4})\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\tanh\left(-\frac{\beta\nu}{4}\right)\left(L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}\right)_{\nu}\,,

where
\left(L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}\right)_{\nu}
is defined as in (
2.13
). However, the function
\tanh\left(-\beta\nu/4\right)
is not
L^{1}
-integrable, and hence the useful time integral formulation as the one (
3.3
) for the jump cannot readily follow. To remedy this issue, thanks to
|\nu|\leq 2\|H\|
for a Bohr frequency, for any smooth and compactly supported function
\kappa(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

(3.6)

\kappa(\nu)=1\quad\text{for}\quad\nu\in[-2\|H\|,2\|H\|]\,,

we define the filtering function

(3.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(\nu):=-\frac{i}{2}\tanh\left(-\frac{\beta\nu}{4}\right)\kappa(\nu)\,,

and then have

(3.8)

G=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\hat{g}(\nu)\left(L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}\right)_{\nu}\,.

Since
\widehat{g}(\nu)
is an
L^{1}
-integrable function, its Fourier transform is well-defined:

(3.9)

g(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\widehat{g}(\nu)e^{-it\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

and
it is direct to compute

(3.10)

G=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\widehat{g}(\nu)\left(L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}\right)_{\nu}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}g(t)H_{L}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where

(3.11)

H_{L}(t):=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}e^{iHt}\left(L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}\right)e^{-iHt}\,.

We emphasize that the choices of
q^{a}
in (
3.4
) and
\kappa
in (
3.7
) are key components in our algorithm and will be discussed in detail in
Section

3.2
.

With
G
and
\{L_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
be constructed in (
3.10
) and (
3.3
), respectively, the Lindbladian

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X)=i[G,X]+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(L_{a}^{\dagger}XL_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a},X\right\}\right)\,,

satisfies
\sigma_{\beta}
-KMS DBC by
Theorem

10
. Then the corresponding Lindblad master equation reads

(3.13)

\partial_{t}\rho=\mathcal{L}^{{\dagger}}(\rho)=-i[G,\rho]+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(L_{a}\rho L^{\dagger}_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a},\rho\right\}\right)\,.

We have discussed the case where coupling operators
\{A^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
are self-adjoint. In fact, one can generally consider a set of couplings such that
\{A^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}=\{(A^{a})^{\dagger}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, and construct the corresponding jump operators
\{L_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, the coherent term
G
, and the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
as in

Eqs.

3.3
,
3.10
and
3.12
, respectively. It is easy to see that the Lindblad dynamics defined in this way still satisfies the KMS detailed balance. Indeed, let
L_{a}
and
L_{a,{\rm adj}}
be the jumps associated with some
A^{a}
and
(A^{a})^{\dagger}
by (
3.3
) (without loss of generality,
A^{a}\neq(A^{a})^{\dagger}
). We then define self-adjoint operators

A^{a}_{+}=\frac{A^{a}+(A^{a})^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\quad A^{a}_{-}=\frac{A^{a}-(A^{a})^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}i}\,,

such that
\sqrt{2}A^{a}=A_{+}^{a}+iA_{-}^{a}
and denote by
L_{a,+}
and
L_{a,-}
the associated jumps (
3.3
). A direct computation by using the time-domain representation (
3.3
) gives

\displaystyle L_{a}\rho L_{a}^{\dagger}+L_{a,{\rm adj}}\rho L_{a,{\rm adj}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{a}(t)\overline{f^{a}(t^{\prime})}\left(A^{a}(t)\rho(A^{a}(t^{\prime}))^{\dagger}+(A^{a}(t))^{\dagger}\rho A^{a}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{a}(t)\overline{f^{a}(t^{\prime})}\left(A_{+}^{a}(t)\rho A_{+}^{a}(t^{\prime})+A_{-}^{a}(t)\rho A_{-}^{a}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=L_{a,+}\rho L_{a,+}^{\dagger}+L_{a,-}\rho L_{a,-}^{\dagger}\,,

thanks to

\displaystyle A^{a}(t)\rho(A^{a}(t^{\prime}))^{\dagger}+(A^{a}(t))^{\dagger}\rho A^{a}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(A_{+}^{a}(t)+iA_{-}^{a}(t)\right)\rho\left(A_{+}^{a}(t^{\prime})-iA_{-}^{a}(t^{\prime})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(A_{+}^{a}(t)-iA_{-}^{a}(t)\right)\rho\left(A_{+}^{a}(t^{\prime})+iA_{-}^{a}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{+}^{a}(t)\rho A_{+}^{a}(t^{\prime})+A_{-}^{a}(t)\rho A_{-}^{a}(t^{\prime})\,.

Here

is defined via (

) with

satisfies (

). Similarly, one can check

\displaystyle\{L^{\dagger}_{a}L_{a},\rho\}+\{L^{\dagger}_{a,{\rm adj}}L_{a,{\rm adj}},\rho\}=\{L^{\dagger}_{a,+}L_{a,+},\rho\}+\{L^{\dagger}_{a,-}L_{a,-},\rho\}\,,

and hereby our claim holds.

3.2.
Choice of weighting function
q(\nu)
and
\kappa(\nu)

In order to efficiently implement the jump operators
\{L_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
in (
3.3
) and the coherent term
G
in (
3.10
), we need to approximate the involved
time–domain integrals
by a numerical quadrature in a finite region.
Specifically, we fix a truncation time
T>0
and approximate the integral
\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}
by
\int_{-T}^{T}
, which is then further approximated by numerical quadrature. To ensure efficiency, we need a small
T
, meaning that
f^{a}(t),g(t)
should be smooth functions that decay rapidly as
\left\lvert t\right\rvert\to\infty
. To this end, we shall assume that
q^{a}
and
\kappa
are compactly supported Gevrey functions. We first recall the definition of Gevrey functions below
[
AHR17
]
.

Definition 12

(Gevrey function)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain. A complex-valued
C^{\infty}
function
h:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
is a
Gevrey function
of order
s\geq 0
, if there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for every
d
-tuple of nonnegative integers
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})

Proposition 20

(Quasi-locality of jump and coherent operators)
.

Let

be a
(k,l)
-local Hamiltonian defined on a finite lattice
\Lambda
, and let each coupling operator
A^{a}
be a local operator. Then, the jump operator
L_{a}
constructed via a Gaussian-type weighting function is quasi-local, and the associated coherent operator
G
is a sum of quasi-local operators.

Proof.

For simplicity, assume that each coupling
A^{a}
is supported on a single site of the lattice denoted by
u_{a}
.
We consider the Gaussian weighting function
q(\nu)=\exp(-(\beta\nu)^{2}/8)\omega(\nu/S)
as the Gaussian-type one in (
3.22
) and set
S\geq 4\|H\|
. Define
\widehat{h}(\nu)=\exp(-(\beta\nu)^{2}/8)
. Recalling the formula for
L_{a}
in (
3.3
), we have

(3.36)

L_{a}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\widehat{f}(\nu)A^{a}_{\nu}=\sum_{\nu\in B_{H}}\widehat{h}(\nu)A^{a}_{\nu}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}h(t)e^{iHt}A^{a}e^{-iHt}\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

where we use
\omega(\nu/S)=1
when
|\nu|\leq 2\|H\|
in the second equality. Then, from the Lieb-Robinson bound
[
HHKL23
, Lemma 5]
, there exists a constant
J
that only depends on
k,l,\sup_{X}\|h_{X}\|
such that

\left\|e^{iHt}A^{a}e^{-iHt}-e^{iH_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}t}A^{a}e^{-iH_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}t}\right\|\leq\|A^{a}\|\min\left\{\frac{(J|t|)^{r}}{r!},2\right\},

for any
r>0
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Here
B_{u_{a}}(r)\subset\Lambda
is a ball centered at

with radius
r

H_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}=\sum_{X\cap B_{u_{a}}(r)\neq\emptyset}h_{X}
. This, along with (
3.36

(3.37)

\displaystyle\bigg\|L_{a}-\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)e^{iH_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}t}A^{a}e^{-iH_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}t}\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{\rm supported on }B_{u_{a}}(r)}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{J^{r}\|A^{a}\|}{r!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|h(t)||t|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t=\|A^{a}\|\left(\mathcal{O}\left(J\beta/\sqrt{r}\right)\right)^{r}\,,

for any
r>0
and
\beta>0
, where the last equality is from
h(t)=\exp\left(-\Theta((t/\beta)^{2})\right)
. This implies that
L_{a}
is quasi-local, and so is the operator
L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a}
.

Define
\widehat{j}(\nu)=-\frac{i}{2}\tanh\left(-\beta\nu/4\right)
with Fourier transform

, defined in distribution sense, which decays as
\exp(-\Theta(|t|))
as

and has singularity

as

. We recall the definition (

) of

and find

\displaystyle\bigg\|G-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}j(t)e^{iH_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}t}(L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a})e^{-iH_{B_{u_{a}}(r)}t}\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{\rm quasi local}}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\|L_{a}\|^{2}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|j(t)|\min\left\{\frac{J^{r}|t|^{r}}{r!},2\right\}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\|L_{a}\|^{2}\right)\left(\int_{|t|\leq\frac{r}{2eJ}}\frac{J^{r}|t|^{r}}{r!}|j(t)|\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{|t|>\frac{r}{2eJ}}|j(t)|\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\|L_{a}\|^{2}\right)\mathcal{O}\left(\int_{|t|\leq\frac{r}{2eJ}}\frac{J^{r}|t|^{r-1}}{r!}\mathrm{d}t-\log\tanh\left(\frac{\pi r}{2eJ\beta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\|L_{a}\|^{2}\right)\left(\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{2^{r}}\right)+e^{-\Theta(\frac{r}{J\beta})}\right)\,,\quad r\geq 1\,.

This implies that

is the sum of quasi-local operators.
∎

The above computation can be easily extended to the case where

Appendix A
Block encoding

Block encoding (see
[
LC19
,
GSLW19
]
) provides a general framework for encoding a non-unitary matrix using unitary matrices, which can be implemented on quantum devices.

Definition 24

(Block encoding)
.

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
, if we can find
\alpha,\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a unitary matrix
U_{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n+m}\times 2^{n+m}}
so that

(A.1)

\|A-\alpha\left(\langle 0^{m}|\otimes I_{n}\right)U_{A}\left(|0^{m}\rangle\otimes I_{n}\right)\|\leq\epsilon,

then
U_{A}
is called an
(\alpha,m,\epsilon)
-block-encoding of
A
. The parameter
\alpha
is referred to as the block encoding factor, or the subnormalization factor.

Intuitively, the block encoding matrix
U_{A}
encodes the rescaled matrix
A/\alpha
in its upper left block:

U_{A}\approx\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A/\alpha&*\\
&*\end{array}\right).

There have been substantial efforts on implementing the block encoding of certain structured matrices of practical interest
[
GSLW19
,
NKL22
,
CLVBY24
,
SCC23
]
.
In this work, we assume that the query access to the block encoding of relevant matrices is available. We also assume that there is no error in the block encodings of the input matrices
H

Appendix B
Linear combination of unitaries

The linear combination of unitaries (LCU)
[
CW12
]
is an important quantum primitive, which allows matrices expressed as a superposition of unitary matrices to be coherently implemented using block encoding. Here we follow
[
GSLW19
]
and present a general version of the LCU that is applicable to possibly complex coefficients.

LCU implements a block encoding of
\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}c_{j}U_{j}
, where
U_{j}
are unitary operators and
c_{j}
are complex numbers.
For the coefficients, we assume access to a pair of
state preparation oracles
(also called prepare oracles for short)
(\textbf{Prep}_{\overline{\gamma_{l}}},\textbf{Prep}_{\gamma_{r}})
acting as (assume
J=2^{\ell}
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Prep}_{\overline{\gamma_{l}}}

\displaystyle:\ket{0^{\ell}}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\left\lVert\gamma_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\overline{\gamma_{l,j}}\ket{j},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Prep}_{\gamma_{r}}

\displaystyle:\ket{0^{\ell}}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\left\lVert\gamma_{r}\right\rVert_{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\gamma_{r,j}\ket{j}.

Here the coefficients should satisfy
\gamma_{l,j}\gamma_{r,j}=c_{j},0\leq j\leq J-1
.
To minimize the block encoding factor, the optimal choice is
\left\lvert\gamma_{l,j}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\gamma_{r,j}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\sqrt{c_{j}}\right\rvert
, and where
\sqrt{z}
refers to the principal value of the square root of
z
. In this case,
\left\lVert\gamma_{l}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\gamma_{r}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\left\lVert c\right\rVert_{1}}
, where
\left\lVert c\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{j}\left\lvert c_{j}\right\rvert
is the
1
-norm of the vector
c
.

The unitaries
U_{j}
needs to be accessed via a
select oracle

Select
as

\mathbf{Select}=\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\ket{j}\bra{j}\otimes U_{j},

which can be constructed using controlled versions of the block encoding matrices
U_{j}
.

Lemma 25

(LCU)
.

Assume
J=2^{\ell}
,
\left\lVert\gamma_{l}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\gamma_{r}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\left\lVert c\right\rVert_{1}}
, then the matrix

W=(\mathbf{Prep}_{\overline{\gamma_{l}}}^{\dagger}\otimes I)\;\mathbf{Select}\;(\mathbf{Prep}_{\gamma_{r}}\otimes I)

is a
(\left\lVert c\right\rVert_{1},\ell,0)
-block-encoding of the linear combination of unitaries
\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}c_{j}U_{j}
.

Appendix C
Gevrey functions

In this section, we collect a few results for Gevrey functions that are useful for this
work. While similar findings have been previously demonstrated in the
literature, notably in
[
AHR17
]
and
[
HR19
]
, we
provide self-contained proofs here with explicit expressions for the constants involved.

Lemma 26

(Product of Gevrey functions)
.

Given
h\in\mathcal{G}^{s}_{C_{1},C_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{s^{\prime}}_{C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

h\cdot h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}^{\max\{s,s^{\prime}\}}_{C_{1}C^{\prime}_{1},C_{2}+C^{\prime}_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,.

Proof.

For a index vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, a direct application of Leibniz rule gives

\displaystyle\left\|\partial^{\alpha}\left(h\cdot h^{\prime}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{|\alpha|}_{j=0}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha,|\beta|=j}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\left\|\partial^{\beta}h\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\|\partial^{\alpha-\beta}h^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C^{\prime}_{1}\sum^{|\alpha|}_{j=0}\left((C_{2})^{j}j^{js}(C^{\prime}_{2})^{|\alpha|-j}(|\alpha|-j)^{(|\alpha|-j)s^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha,|\beta|=j}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C^{\prime}_{1}\sum^{|\alpha|}_{j=0}\left(\binom{|\alpha|}{j}(C_{2})^{j}j^{js}(C^{\prime}_{2})^{|\alpha|-j}(|\alpha|-j)^{(|\alpha|-j)s^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C^{\prime}_{1}\left(C_{2}+C^{\prime}_{2}\right)^{|\alpha|}|\alpha|^{|\alpha|\max\{s,s^{\prime}\}}\,,

where the third inequality is by
\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha,|\beta|=j}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}=\binom{|\alpha|}{j}
.
∎

We next show that
\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4})

is a Gevrey function and its derivative is
L^{1}
-integrable. We first recall the Faà di Bruno’s formula and the partial Bell polynomial for the chain rule of high-order derivatives
[
Com74
, p.139]
.

Lemma 27

(Faà di Bruno’s formula and partial Bell polynomial)
.

Let
h,g
be smooth function from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}
, and
f(x):=h(g(x))
. Then the
k
-th order derivative of
f
is given by

f^{(k)}(x)=\sum\frac{k!}{q_{1}!(1!)^{q_{1}}q_{2}!(2!)^{q_{2}}\cdots q_{k}!(k!)^{q_{k}}}h^{(\sum q_{i})}(g(x))\prod^{k}_{i=1}\left(g^{(i)}(x)\right)^{q_{i}}

where the sum is over all
k
-tuples of nonnegative integers
(q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{k})
satisfying
\sum iq_{i}=k
.
The above formula can also be rewritten as

f^{(k)}(x)=\sum^{k}_{j=1}h^{(j)}(g(x))B_{k,j}\left(g^{(1)}(x),g^{(2)}(x),\dots,g^{(k-j+1)}(s)\right)\,,

where
B_{k,j}
is the partial Bell polynomial:

B_{k,j}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k-j+1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\,,\,q_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\sum^{k}_{i=1}iq_{i}=k\\
\sum^{k}_{i=1}q_{i}=j\end{subarray}}\frac{k!}{q_{1}!q_{2}!\cdots q_{k-j+1}!}\prod^{k-j+1}_{i=1}\left(\frac{z_{i}}{i!}\right)^{q_{i}}\,.

Using Faà di Bruno’s formula, we can calculate the high order derivatives of
\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4})
and verify that it belongs to a Gevrey class.

Lemma 28
.

It holds that

e^{-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4}}\in\mathcal{G}^{1}_{1,\frac{7}{2}}\,,\quad\left(e^{-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4}}\right)^{(1)}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\,.

Proof.

We first use the second formula of
Lemma

27
and some properties of the partial Bell polynomials
B_{n,k}
to calculate
k
-th order derivative for
\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
. For
k\geq 2
, we have (define
\binom{j}{k-j}=0
if
k>2j
)

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\right)^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}(2j-3)!!}{2^{j}(1+x^{2})^{j-1/2}}B_{k,j}(2x,2,0,\dots,0)=\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}2^{j}(2j-3)!!}{2^{j}(1+x^{2})^{j-1/2}}B_{k,j}(x,1,0,\dots,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}2^{j}(2j-3)!!}{2^{j}(1+x^{2})^{j-1/2}}\frac{1}{2^{k-j}}\frac{k!}{j!}\binom{j}{k-j}x^{2j-k}=\frac{k!}{2^{k}}\sum^{k}_{j=1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}2^{j}(2j-3)!!}{j!}\binom{j}{k-j}\frac{x^{2j-k}}{(1+x^{2})^{j-1/2}}\,.

Using the fact that
\left|\frac{x^{2j-k}}{(1+x^{2})^{j-1/2}}\right|\leq 1
,
(2j-3)!!\leq 2^{j}j!
, and
(1+2^{2})^{k}=\sum^{k}_{j=0}\binom{k}{k-j}2^{2j}
, we have

\left|\left(\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\right)^{(k)}\right|\leq\frac{k!}{2^{k}}\sum^{k}_{j=0}\frac{2^{j}(2j-3)!!}{j!}\binom{j}{k-j}\leq\frac{k!}{2^{k}}\sum^{k}_{j=0}4^{j}\binom{k}{k-j}\leq\left(5/2\right)^{k}k!\,.

Next, by Faà di Bruno’s formula for
\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4})
, we obtain

(C.1)

\displaystyle\left(e^{-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4}}\right)^{(k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\frac{k!}{q_{1}!(1!)^{q_{1}}q_{2}!(2!)^{q_{2}}\cdots q_{k}!(k!)^{q_{k}}}\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{k}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4}}\prod^{k}_{j=1}\left(\left(\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\right)^{(j)}+x^{(j)}\right)^{q_{j}}\,.

Plugging the upper bound
\left|\left(\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\right)^{(j)}\right|
gives

\displaystyle\left|\left(e^{-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4}}\right)^{(k)}\right|<\sum\frac{k!}{q_{1}!(1!)^{q_{1}}q_{2}!(2!)^{q_{2}}\cdots q_{k}!(k!)^{q_{k}}}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{k}\prod^{k}_{j=1}(7/2)^{jq_{j}}(j!)^{q_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\frac{k!}{q_{1}!q_{2}!\cdots q_{k}!}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{k}\prod^{k}_{j=1}(7/2)^{jq_{j}}=\sum\frac{(7/8)^{k}k!}{q_{1}!q_{2}!\cdots q_{k}!}\leq(7/8)^{k}k!\sum 1\leq(7/2)^{k}k!\,,

where we use the fact that the number of
k
-tuples of nonnegative integers
(q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{k})
satisfying
\sum jq_{j}=k
is less than
\binom{2k}{k}\leq 2^{2k}
. This concludes
\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4})\in\mathcal{G}_{1,\frac{7}{2}}^{1}
. The
L^{1}
-integrablity of
(\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}{4}))^{(1)}
is a simple consequence of
Eq.

C.1
.
∎

Finally, we show that the Fourier transform of the Gevrey class with compact support decays rapidly, by a Paley-Wiener type estimate.

Lemma 29
.

Given
h\in\mathcal{G}^{s}_{C_{1},C_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with compact support
\Omega=\mathrm{supp}(h)
and
s\geq 1
, define

H(y)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(x)e^{-ix\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Then, for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there holds

\left|H(y)\right|\leq\frac{C_{1}|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,e^{\frac{esd}{2}-\frac{s}{C^{1/s}_{2}e}\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}^{1/s}}\,,

where
|\Omega|=\int_{\Omega}1\,\mathrm{d}x
is the volume of
\Omega
and
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}
is the
2
-norm of the vector
y
.

Proof.

By
Definition

12
, we have, for every
d
-tuple of nonnegative integers
\alpha
with
|\alpha|=\sum_{i}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert
,

\left\|\partial^{\alpha}h\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{1}C^{|\alpha|}_{2}|\alpha|^{|\alpha|s}\,.

It follows that

\left|y^{\alpha}\right|\left|H(y)\right|=\left|y^{\alpha}H(y)\right|=\left|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\Omega}\partial^{\alpha}h(x)e^{-ix\cdot y}\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\frac{C_{1}|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{d}}C^{|\alpha|}_{2}|\alpha|^{|\alpha|s}\,,

where
y^{\alpha}:=\Pi^{d}_{i=1}y^{\alpha_{i}}_{i}
. Recall from
[
AHR17
, Proposition 3.1]
that

\inf_{m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\left\{\left(\frac{s}{ae}\right)^{ms}\frac{m^{ms}}{|t|^{m}}\right\}\leq e^{es/2}e^{-a|t|^{1/s}}\,,\quad\text{for any $a,s,t>0$}\,.

Letting
a:=s/(C^{1/s}_{2}e)
and using the above inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|H(y)\right|\leq\frac{C_{1}|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{d}}\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{C^{|\alpha|}_{2}|\alpha|^{|\alpha|s}}{|y^{\alpha}|}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{d}}\prod_{i}\inf_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\left|\frac{C^{\alpha_{i}}_{2}|\alpha_{i}|^{\alpha_{i}s}}{|y_{i}|^{\alpha_{i}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{esd/2}e^{-a\sum^{d}_{i=1}|y_{i}|^{1/s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{esd/2}e^{-a\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}^{1/s}}\,.\qed
