Exact values of generic subrank
Paweł Pielasa, Matouš Šafránek, Anatoli Shatsila

Abstract
In this article we prove the subrank of a generic tensor in ℂ n , n , n \mathbb{C}^{n,n,n} to be Q ⁡ ( n ) = ⌊ 3 ​ n − 2 ⌋ Q(n)=\lfloor\sqrt{3n-2}\rfloor by providing a lower bound to the known upper bound. More generally, we find the generic subrank of tensors of all orders and dimensions. This answers two open questions posed in [ DMZ24 ] . Finally, we compute dimensions of varieties of tensors of subrank at least r r .



1
Introduction

Geometry of tensors is a subject of great importance in mathematics, physics and computer science with numerous applications in complexity theory (fast matrix multiplication, P vs NP problem, etc.)
[
Lan12
,
Lan17
]
, quantum information theory
[
Lan24
]
and other areas of science ranging from signal processing to chemistry
[
KB09
]
.

The subrank of a tensor is an important notion in algebraic complexity theory introduced by Strassen in
[
Str87
]
to analyse the complexity of matrix multiplication. As a dual notion to tensor rank, it serves as one of the crucial invariants of tensors that has been extensively researched over the years. Recently, numerous other invariants of tensors, such as the slice rank
[
Tao16
]
, the analytic rank
[
GW11
]
, and the geometric rank
[
KZ20
]
, have been introduced and investigated. It is known that the subrank provides a lower bound for all invariants mentioned above.

In this work we are interested in generic values of the subrank, that is, the subrank of ”almost all” tensors in a given tensor space. More precisely, one can show
[
DMZ24
, Proposition 2.1]
that for any tensor space there is a Zariski open subset of tensors where subrank is constant. We call this number
the generic subrank
and denote the subrank of a generic tensor
T\in K^{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}
by
Q(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
with a shorthand
Q(n)=Q(n,n,n)
.

Strassen
[
Str91
]
and Bürgisser
[
Bü90
]
found the upper bound for the generic subrank
Q(n)\leq n^{2/3+o(1)}
but exact asymptotic values have been unknown for a long time. Only recently it has been shown in
[
DMZ24
]
that, in fact,
Q(n)=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. Interestingly, the generic value of tensor parameters introduced above is the maximal value
n
, hence the subrank is much lower than aforementioned ranks for a generic tensor.

We note that determining precise generic values for tensor invariants is a difficult problem. For instance, generic values for a rank are unknown in general (see
[
Str83
,
Fri12
]
and Subsection
5.1
).
We highlight one example where generic values are known. Namely, Alexander and Hirschowitz (see
[
AH95
]
and
[
Lan17
, Chapter 5.4 and Chapter 15]
) showed that a generic symmetric
d
-tensor in
K^{n,\ldots,n}
has generic symmetric rank
\left\lceil{\frac{\binom{n+d-1}{n}}{n}}\right\rceil
apart from a known list of exception where the values are also known.

In this paper we find precise values of the generic subrank of tensors of any order and shape, thus providing a complete answer to the problem of determining values of the generic subrank.

1.1
Results

It was shown in
[
DMZ24
, Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3]
that

3(\lfloor\sqrt{n/3+1/4}-1/2\rfloor)\leq Q(n)\leq\lfloor\sqrt{3n-2}\rfloor.

They conjectured (see
[
DMZ24
, Section 6]
) that the upper bound on
Q(n)
is exactly tight and checked that it is indeed the case for
n\leq 100
on a computer. In this note we show using a combinatorial method that the upper bound is indeed tight.

{restatable*}

corollarySimpleMainCor

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the generic subrank
Q(n)
is equal to
\lfloor\sqrt{3n-2}\rfloor
over any infinite field.

Our proof works in a more general setting. We can thus confirm that other upper bounds given in
[
DMZ24
]
are exactly tight. The following general
Section
is proved in Section
2
.

{restatable*}

theoremMainCor

For any
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
we have the generic subrank

Q(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=\min\left\{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},\left\lfloor\sqrt{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}-2}\right\rfloor\right\}

over any infinite field.

We build on the result (see
Theorem 2.2
) from
[
DMZ24
]
which says that the generic subrank
Q(n)
is at least
r
if certain subspaces of
K^{r,r,r}
generate the whole space. In
2.4

we straightforwardly reformulate this condition to a statement about linear independence of rows of a projection matrix
M
whose non-zero entries are generic elements of
K
(with possible repetitions). Finally, using a combinatorial argument we show that
M
indeed has the linearly independent rows in
Theorem 2.15
.

The proof can be carried out for higher order tensors analogously. This is carried out in Section
3
, where we get the following generalisation.

{restatable*}

theoremHighOrderMainCor
Let
k\geq 3
. For any
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
we have

Q(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=\min\left\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},\left\lfloor(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}-(k-1))^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\right\rfloor\right\}

over any infinite field.
In particular,
Q(\underbrace{n,\ldots,n}_{k})=\lfloor(kn-k+1)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\rfloor
.

Thus, the bound from
[
DMZ24
, Theorem 1.5]
is exactly tight .

Further, we adapt our combinatorial method to produce results not only about the locus of generic-subrank tensors, but also about the locus of higher subrank tensors. We confirm that sets of tensors in
K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
of subrank at least
r
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
, have expected dimension.

{restatable*}

theoremDimensionThm

Let
k\geq 3
and the underlying field be of characteristic
0
. For any
n_{1},\dots n_{k},r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Q(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})<r\leq n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
the following holds:

\dim(\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-r\left(r^{k-1}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}+(k-1)\right).

The conditions on
r
and
n_{i}
only restrict them to the non-trivial range, i.e. where
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
is non-empty and not full-dimensional. We are thus able to calculate the dimensions of all
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
.

2
Main result

2.1
Set-up

We denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and by
P_{r}
the set of triples
(i,j,k)\in[r]^{3}
with
i,j,k
all different. We then recall the notation introduced in
[
DMZ24
]
.
For a field
K
we denote

K^{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}}:=K^{n_{1}}\otimes K^{n_{2}}\otimes\ldots\otimes K^{n_{k}}.

For a tensor
T\in K^{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}}
we define its
subrank
as the maximal non-negative integer
s\leq n
such that
I_{s}\leq T
, where

I_{s}:=I_{1}^{\oplus s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}e_{j}^{(i)}

and
\{e_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}
is a basis of the corresponding
K^{n_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Note that this definition is independent of the choice of the bases.
We denote by

\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}=\{T\in K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\>|\>Q(T)\geq r\}

the set of tensors of subrank at least
r
in
K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
and by
Q(n_{1},\dots n_{k})
the subrank of a generic tensor in
K^{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}
(more simply called generic subrank). For proof of existence see
[
DMZ24
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Remark 2.1
.

Generic subrank is the highest
r
such that
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
is Zariski-dense. Note that if
K=\mathbb{R}
, Zariski-dense
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
may not be dense in the Euclidean topology. Indeed, one can show that, for example,
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{(2,2,2)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2,2,2}
is not dense in the Euclidean sense, even though 2 is the generic subrank (
subsection 1.1
). See
[
Kru89
]
and

[
Fri12
, Section 7]
for details.

We define a linear subspace of
K^{r,r,r}
as follows:

W_{r}:=\{T\in K^{r,r,r}\>|\>T_{ijk}=0\text{ if }(i,j,k)\in P_{r}\}\subseteq K^{r,r,r}.

Equivalently,

W_{r}=\left\langle e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\otimes e_{k}\right\rangle_{(i,j,k)\in[r]^{3}\setminus P_{r}}.

For a linear subspace
\mathcal{X}
of
K^{n_{2}}\otimes K^{n_{3}}
we define a linear subspace

\mathcal{X}[1]:=K^{n_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{X}\subseteq K^{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}.

This linear space consists precisely of the tensors whose slices in the first direction belong to
\mathcal{X}
.
We define
\mathcal{X}[2]
and
\mathcal{X}[3]
analogously.

Our proof relies on the following result.

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem 3.9
[
DMZ24
]
)
.

Let
r\leq n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
such that
n_{i}-r\leq r^{2}
for
i=1,2,3
. Let
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq K^{r,r}
be a generic subspace of dimension
n_{i}-r
for
i=1,2,3
. Suppose

\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\mathcal{X}_{2}[2]+\mathcal{X}_{3}[3]+W_{r}=K^{r,r,r},

then
Q(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\geq r
. Further, if characteristic of
K
is 0, then we have the converse.

Working modulo
W_{r}
we can rephrase the condition above as a statement about linear independence of rows of a projection matrix.

Definition 2.3
.

Assume we are given
r,n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r\leq n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
. We define a matrix
M\in K^{r(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}-3r)\times r(r-1)(r-2)}
, whose columns are labelled by triples
(t,m,s)
with
t\in[3],m\in[r],s\in[n_{t}-r]
, and rows by triples
(i,j,k)\in P_{r}
. The matrix
M
is defined entry-wise by

M^{t,m,s}_{i,j,k}=\begin{cases}a^{1,s}_{j,k},&t=1,m=i;\\
a^{2,s}_{i,k},&t=2,m=j;\\
a^{3,s}_{i,j},&t=3,m=k;\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

with variable entries
a^{t,s}_{i_{1},i_{2}}
.

Here the assumption
r\leq n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
is only to have the set
[n_{t}-r]
defined.

Lemma 2.4
.

With the assumptions of
Theorem 2.2
we have

\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\mathcal{X}_{2}[2]+\mathcal{X}_{3}[3]+W_{r}=K^{r,r,r}

if and only if the matrix
M
has generic rank (i.e. for generic values of
a^{t,s}_{i_{1},i_{2}}\in K
) equal to
r(r-1)(r-2)
, that is full row rank.

Proof.

Denote the basis of
K^{r}
by
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
. Since
\mathcal{X}_{t}
is generic for
t=1,2,3
we can write

\mathcal{X}_{1}=\left\langle\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq r}a_{j,k}^{1,s}\;e_{j}\otimes e_{k}\right\rangle_{1\leq s\leq n_{1}-r}

\mathcal{X}_{2}=\left\langle\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq r}a_{i,k}^{2,s}\;e_{i}\otimes e_{k}\right\rangle_{1\leq s\leq n_{2}-r}

\mathcal{X}_{3}=\left\langle\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq r}a_{i,j}^{3,s}\;e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\right\rangle_{1\leq s\leq n_{3}-r}

for generic
a_{i,j}^{t,s}\in K
.
It follows from the definition of
W_{r}
that the basis of the space
K^{r,r,r}/W_{r}
is given by
\{e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\otimes e_{k}\}_{i,j,k\in P_{r}}
. On the other hand, the basis of
\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]\oplus\mathcal{X}_{2}[2]\oplus\mathcal{X}_{3}[3]

is given by vectors
v_{t,m,s}
with
t\in[3],m\in[r],s\in[n_{t}-r]
and

v_{1,m,s}=\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq r}a_{j,k}^{1,s}\;e_{m}\otimes e_{j}\otimes e_{k}

and analogously for
t=2,3
. Note that the matrix
M
defined in the statement is the matrix of the natural projection

\pi:\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]\oplus\mathcal{X}_{2}[2]\oplus\mathcal{X}_{3}[3]\to K^{r,r,r}/W_{r}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\mathcal{X}_{2}[2]+\mathcal{X}_{3}[3]+W_{r}=K^{r,r,r}

holds if and only if
\pi
is surjective, or, equivalently,
M
has linearly independent rows.

∎

Now if we prove that the matrix
M
has generic rank
r(r-1)(r-2)
for any
r
(keep in mind that
M
depends on
r
), we get by the
2.4
and
Theorem 2.2
a lower bound on
Q(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
.
In fact, the upcoming combinatorial argument proves that the matrix has generic rank equal to
r(r-1)(r-2)
whenever it has at least as many columns as rows, that is whenever it is actually possible (
Theorem 2.15
). That will immediately give us the highest possible lower bound, the generic subrank itself (
2.16
).

Definition 2.7
.

Let

be a

\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
or zero, and no variable appears twice in one row or column. Define

if

Later, when we decribe the algorithm to find such a monomial, we will not use this formal submatrix notation. Rather, as in

and to all others as
crossed

Lemma 2.8
.

If there is a unique row monomial of a
k\times\ell
matrix
M_{0}
over an infinite field, then
M_{0}
has generically full row rank, that is rank
k
.

Proof.

For each
i
, we take every
a_{i}
in
M_{i-1}
. They are in different rows and columns and there is
k
of them, so they form a monomial in a certain

M_{0}
. By construction, there is no other way to get this monomial in any minor. Thus,
M_{0}
has a

minor which is non-zero as a polynomial, which in an infinite field means that it is non-zero on an open set of values of

.
∎

2.2
Algorithm

Now we describe an algorithm to find a unique row monomial, or, in the language of
2.6
, to cross out variables so that eventually all rows are crossed out.

Example 2.9
.

We may want to pick an element from the row
(1,2,4)
. Choose for example
a^{1,s}_{2,4}
in the column
(1,1,s)
for some
s
. We always have to cross out all remaining occurences of the chosen element. In
2.5
, it appears in the row
(3,2,4)
, but with higher

(5,2,4)
,
(6,2,4)
and so on (in columns
(1,5,s)

(1,6,s)

r-2

Suppose now

\begin{matrix}1,2,4&3,2,4&5,2,4.\end{matrix}

It will turn out to be useful to loop the row numbers from

2,3,5&4,3,5&1,3,5\\

4,5,2&1,5,2&3,5,2\\
5,1,3&2,1,3&4,1,3,\end{matrix}

and then prove that we can cross them out all.

In the following definition, we will call the three columns “orbits” and the table above a “
1
-block of size 3”. The

t=1

Now we formalize these ideas.

Definition 2.10

There is a natural
\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}

For

we say that a set
B
of
b

if for any two orbits
O_{1},O_{2}\in B
and some (or, equivalently, any)

there exists
p_{2}\in O_{2}
such that
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
differ only in the

-th place, e.g.

and
p_{2}=(i_{2},j,k)
for

. Note that
0\leq b\leq r-2

We also point out this simple property which will be useful later.

Lemma 2.11
.

If
t_{1}\not=t_{2}
, then a
t_{1}
-block and a

-block share at most one orbit.

Proof.

Note that there is a unique
t\not=t_{1},t_{2}
. From each orbit we can pick a unique representative which has a 1 on the
t
-th place. Assume the two blocks share at least one orbit, whose representative is
p
. Then all other representatives of (orbits of) the
t_{1}
-block can be obtained by changing the
t_{1}
-th number of

, and similarly, other representatives of the
t_{2}
-block can be obtained by changing the
t_{2}
-th number in

. Changing the
t_{1}
-th and
t_{2}
-th number clearly gives different results, so the blocks share no other orbit.
∎

In our algorithm, we will always cross out whole orbits. This leads us to a notion of a crossed orbit.

Definition 2.12
.

Assume that elements (rows) of every orbit are either all crossed or all uncrossed. Then we call an orbit
crossed
if every (or equivalently, any) row in the orbit is crossed. We say a
t
-block is

if it contains only uncrossed orbits and is maximal among those with respect to inclusion.

In
2.9
, maximal meant that we had to cross out all three rows that contained an uncrossed variable
a^{1,s}_{2,4}
and then similarly for the rest of the orbit. However, if a row (with its orbit) is already crossed,
a^{1,s}_{2,4}
in that row is no longer in the relevant submatrix, so we do not cross it and do not include that row (and orbit) in the block.

Now we have all notions to prove the main result. Fist, we introduce the method to cross out blocks.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let

. Consider a maximal uncrossed

-block of size

be the set of rows in the union of the orbits of

. Let

be a set of cardinality

C=\{(t,m,s)\mid m\in[r],s\in S\}
be a set of uncrossed columns.

The fact that no rows are crossed in

is included in the

Then it is possible to cross out precisely all rows in

Note that

, which is clearly necessary. Remember that whenever we cross out a variable, we must cross out all its occurrences. Let us first state a lemma.

Lemma 2.14

Let

be a variable in the matrix. Assume there is a row in

and there is a column in

Proof.

We can assume

The proof of

will allow us to cross out variables in an arbitrary order.

Proof of
Theorem 2.13

We pick any variable that lies in an uncrossed row of

To complete the proof we show that as long as there is an uncrossed column in

We prove it for

in the row

3
Generic subrank of higher-order tensors

In this section we will generalise the results of the previous section to higher-order tensors. Below we will state all results in these settings but omit parts of the proofs which are straightforward generalisations of the previous results and of the work in
[
DMZ24
]
.

First, note that for any
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
there is a non-empty Zariski-open subset
U\subseteq K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
and integer
r
such that for all
T\in U
we have
Q(T)=r
.

We define

X_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}:=\{T\in K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\>|\>T_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}=0\text{ for }(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in[r]^{k}\setminus\{(i,\ldots,i)\>|\>i\in[r]\},T_{i,\ldots,i}\neq 0\text{ for }i\in[r]\}.

Let

P_{r}^{(k)}:=\{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in[r]^{k}\>|\>\>\text{any subset of }(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\text{ of order }k-1\text{ has at least 2 disjoint elements}\}

and

W_{r}^{(k)}=\{T\in K^{r,\ldots,r}|T_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}=0\text{ for }(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in P_{r}^{(k)}\}.

Note that these definitions generalise the case
k=3
. Indeed, it follows from the proof of
[
DMZ24
, Theorem 3.9]
that tensors in
W_{r}^{(k)}
are sums of the flattenings of tensors of
X_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
with respect to one of the coordinates.
Now we can state the
k
-tensor version of
Theorem 2.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
r\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
such that
n_{i}-r\leq r^{2}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Let
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k-1}K^{r}
be a generic linear subspace of dimension
n_{i}-r
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Suppose

\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)}=K^{r,\ldots,r}.

Then
Q(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\geq r
. If characteristic of
K
is 0, then the converse holds.

As before, we can translate the condition of the theorem above to the following matrix being full rank:

Lemma 3.2
.

With the assumptions of
Theorem
above we have

\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)}=K^{r,\ldots,r},

if and only if the matrix
M\in K^{r(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}-kr)\times(r^{k}-kr^{2}+(k-1)r)}
defined by

M^{t,m,s}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}=\begin{cases}a^{1,s}_{j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}},&t=1,m=j_{1};\\
a^{2,s}_{j_{1},j_{3},\ldots,j_{k}},&t=2,m=j_{2};\\
\dots\\
a^{k,s}_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k-1}},&t=k,m=j_{k};\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

for generic
a^{t,s}_{\bullet,\ldots,\bullet}\in K
has rank
r^{k}-kr^{2}+(k-1)r
. Here the columns of
M
are labelled by triples
(t,m,s)
with
t\in[k],m\in[r],s\in[n_{t}-r]
, and the rows by tuples
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in P_{r}^{(k)}
.

We claim that our method to prove
Theorem 2.15
can be used to show the matrix is of full rank. To make this precise, we define the analogues of blocks and orbits in the generalised context.

Definition 3.3
.

There is a natural
\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
-action on the set
P_{r}^{(k)}
, which we identify with rows of
M
, given by
a\cdot(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})=(j_{1}+a,\ldots,j_{k}+a)\mod r.
For
t=1,\ldots,k
we say that a set
B
of
b
orbits under this action is an
t-block of size b
if for any two orbits
O_{1},O_{2}\in B
and some (or, equivalently, any)
p_{1}\in O_{1}
there exists
p_{2}\in O_{2}
such that
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
differ only in the
t
-th place.

The proof of the following lemma is completely analogous to
2.11
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
. If
t_{1}\neq t_{2}
, then a
t_{1}
-block and a
t_{2}
-block (with rows in the set
P_{r}^{(k)}
) share at most one orbit.

We can cross out maximal uncrossed blocks in a way analogous to
Theorem 2.13
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Consider a maximal uncrossed
t
-block of size
b
and let
B
be the set of rows in its orbits. Let
S\subseteq[n_{t}-r]
of cardinality
b
and let
C=\{(t,m,s)\mid m\in[r],s\in S\}
be a set of uncrossed columns. Then it is possible to cross out precisely all rows in
B
and all columns in
C
.

We are ready to state the main theorem of this section, generalising
Theorem 2.15
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
k\geq 3
. For any
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},r\in\mathbb{N}
such that

r\leq n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},\left\lfloor(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}-(k-1))^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\right\rfloor

the matrix
M
defined as in
3.2
has generic rank
r^{k}-kr^{2}+(k-1)r
over any infinite field.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Theorem 2.15

we repeatedly cross out maximal uncrossed blocks from the matrix to produce a monomial with coefficient
\pm 1
in some maximal minor. Since we can cross out blocks using
Theorem 3.5
, we only have to check that there is always a maximal uncrossed block with a suitable set of columns. With notation from the proof of
Theorem 2.15
it follows from
3.4
that

|B_{1}\cup\ldots\cup B_{k}|_{o}=|B_{1}|_{o}+\ldots+|B_{k}|_{o}-(k-1)\geq\mathcal{S}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{S}_{k}+1.

The rest of the proof is analogous to
Theorem 2.15
.
∎

\HighOrderMainCor

Proof.

We apply
Theorem 3.6
. The proof is analogous to
subsection 1.1
.
∎

4
Dimension of spaces of tensors of subrank at least
r

Recall that in order to bound the generic subrank of tensors in
K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
from above one bounds the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}=\{T\in K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\>|\>Q(T)\geq r\}
(see
[
DMZ24
]
for more details). As the spaces
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
are interesting on their own, we apply the methods developed in the previous sections to determine dimensions of these spaces precisely. In this section we often use the notation introduced in the previous section.

To begin with, we have in fact proved in
Theorem 3.6
that if

r=\min\left\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},\left\lfloor(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}-(k-1))^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\right\rfloor\right\},

then
\dim(\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})=\dim(K^{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}})=n_{1}n_{2}\dots n_{k}
. We can generalise this as follows.

\DimensionThm

Proof.

Recall that we have a map

\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}:\prod_{i=1}^{k}GL_{n_{i}}(K)\times X_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}\to K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}

(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n_{k}},T)\mapsto(A_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes A_{n_{k}})T

whose image is precisely
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
. The proof of this fact is analogous to the proof of
[
DMZ24
, Lemma 2.2]
. By the fiber dimension theorem

\dim(\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})=\dim(\prod_{i=1}^{k}GL_{n_{i}}(K)\times X_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})-\dim(\text{general fiber of }\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}),

hence, in order to bound the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
from above one has to bound the dimension of a general fiber of
\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
from below. Following the proofs of
[
DMZ24
, Proposition 2.4 and Theorem 1.5]
we get

\dim(\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-r\left(r^{k-1}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}+(k-1)\right)

Recall that the dimension of the image of
\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
is equal to the rank of of the differential of
\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
at a generic point.
A straightforward generalisation of the proof of
[
DMZ24
, Theorem 3.9]
shows that the image of a differential of
\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
at a generic point is

\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)}+Y_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}\subseteq K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}

where

Y_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}=\{T\in K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\>|\>T_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}=0\text{ for }(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in[r]^{k}\setminus\{(i,i,\ldots,i)\>|\>i\in[r]\}\}

and
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subseteq\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k-1}K^{r}
is a generic linear subspace of dimension
n_{i}-r
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.

It follows from the definition that

\dim(\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)}+Y_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})=\dim(Y_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})+\dim(\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)})-r.

It follows from the proof of
3.2
that

\dim(\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)})=r\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-kr+(kr-k+1)\right)

if and only if the columns of
M
defined as in
3.2
are linearly independent for a generic set of variables.
If this is the case then

\dim(\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})=\dim(\im\psi_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})=\dim(Y_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})})+\dim(\mathcal{X}_{1}[1]+\ldots+\mathcal{X}_{k}[k]+W_{r}^{(k)})-r

=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-r^{k}+r\right)+r\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-kr+(kr-k+1)\right)-r=\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-r\left(r^{k-1}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}+(k-1)\right).

Note that the assumptions imply that
r>(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{k}-(k-1))^{\frac{1}{k-1}}
, so the matrix
M
has more rows than columns. We will prove that the columns of
M
are linearly independent by adding columns to
M
to make it a square matrix and using
Theorem 3.6
to show it has full rank.

Suppose that we alter our matrix by increasing
n_{t}
for some
t
by 1. Then we added precisely
r
columns to the matrix
M
(indexed by
(t,m,n_{t}+1)
for all
m\in[r]
). Since both the number of rows and columns in our original matrix
M
is divisible by
r
we can apply such an operation finitely many times to make
M
a square matrix. Then by
Theorem 3.6
it has full row rank and thus full rank. Then since it is a square matrix, all the columns of the new matrix are linearly independent, hence all columns were linearly independent in the original matrix
M
.
∎

5
Further research directions

In this section we highlight several possible direction for further research.

5.1
Generic rank

While the asymptotic growth of a generic rank and its values for tensors of certain shapes (e.g., in
K^{n,n,n}
) are known, exact values are still unknown in general
[
Fri12
]
.
In the spirit of this paper, one can rephrase the question of finding exact values of a generic rank to a question about the generic rank of a matrix by taking the differential of the map
\psi_{r}:

\displaystyle\psi_{r}:\left(K^{n_{1}}\times K^{n_{2}}\times K^{n_{3}}\right)^{r}

\displaystyle\to K^{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}},

\displaystyle(a^{1,1},a^{2,1},a^{3,1},\;a^{1,2},a^{2,2},a^{3,2},\;\dots,\;a^{1,r},a^{2,r},a^{3,r})

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{r}\,a^{1,i}\otimes a^{2,i}\otimes a^{3,i}.

Note that the structure of the matrix of the differential is similar to that of
M
.

It is conjectured (see
[
Str83
]
and
[
Fri12
, Conjecture 5.1]
)
that when
3\leq n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq n_{3}\leq(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)
, then apart from the case
n_{1}=3,n_{2}=n_{3}=2k+1
, the generic rank is equal to

R(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=\left\lceil\frac{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}-2}\right\rceil,

which is the expected value.

One can ask whether a combinatorial method similar to the one developed in this paper, possibly with an adapted notion of a unique monomial, could be used to prove that the matrix has expected rank.

5.2
Equations of the varieties
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{r}}

Another interesting problem to consider concerns the description of the ideal
I(\mathcal{C}_{r})
. Note that it is a difficult problem as, for instance,
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{r}}\subset K^{r,r,r}=\{T\in K^{r,r,r}\>|R(T)\leq r\}
which is the
r
-th secant variety of the Segre embedding. Describing the equations of secants of Segre varieties is a major open problem as these varieties are loci of tensors of border rank at most
r
(see
[
LM06
]
and
[
Lan12
, Chapter 7]
for more information).

We note that in general there are still some similarities with the case of secants of Segre varieties since
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{r}}\subset K^{n,n,n}
is invariant under the action of
GL_{n}\times GL_{n}\times GL_{n}
so we can study
I(\overline{\mathcal{C}_{r}})
with the tools from representation theory.

5.3
(Generic) border subrank

For a tensor
T\in K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
we define its
border subrank

\underline{Q}(T)
as the maximal non-negative integer
s\leq n
such that

I_{s}\in\overline{\prod_{i=1}^{k}GL_{n_{i}}(K)(T)}

where
I_{s}:=I_{1}^{\oplus s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}e_{j}^{(i)}
and
\{e_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}
is a basis of the corresponding
K^{n_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Note that this definition is independent of the choice of the bases and we always have
Q(T)\leq\underline{Q}(T)
. Then we can define sets

\underline{\mathcal{C}}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}:=\{T\in K^{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\>|\>\underline{Q}(T)\geq r\}.

It follows from the definition that
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}\subset\underline{\mathcal{C}}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
. Moreover, one can prove a similar upper bound as in Theorem
1.1
for the dimensions of the sets
\underline{\mathcal{C}}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
(see
[
BCDR24
, Theorem 3]
) which, in fact, yields the same asymptotic growth for the generic border subrank
[
BCDR24
, Theorem 2]
:

\underline{Q}(n,n,\ldots,n)=\Theta(n^{1/(k-1)}).

In
[
BCDR24
, Theorem 4]
it is shown that the generic border subrank in
K^{n,n,n}
is at least
\lfloor\sqrt{4n}\rfloor-3
, which is bigger then the generic subrank for large enough
n
. An interesting research direction is to find the exact values (or at least give bounds similar to
[
DMZ24
, Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3]
) of the generic border subrank.

On the other hand, determining dimensions of
\underline{\mathcal{C}}_{r}^{(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}
seems to be a challenging problem as the only lower bound we are aware of is
[
Cha24
, Theorem 1.4]
:

\dim(\underline{\mathcal{C}}_{r}^{(r,r,r)})\geq\frac{2r^{3}+3r^{2}-2r-3}{3}.
