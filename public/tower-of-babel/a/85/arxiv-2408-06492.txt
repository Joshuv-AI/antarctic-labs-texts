A Random Walk on the Category of Finite Abelian p p -groups
Nikita Lvov

Abstract
We study an irreducible Markov chain on the category of finite abelian p p -groups, whose stationary measure is the Cohen-Lenstra distribution. This Markov chain arises when one studies the cokernel of a random matrix M M , after conditioning on a submatrix of M M . We show two surprising facts about this Markov chain. Firstly, it is reversible. Hence, one may regard it is a random walk on finite abelian p p -groups. The proof of reversibility also explains the appearance of the Cohen-Lenstra distribution in the context of random matrices. Secondly, we can explicitly determine the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the infinite transition matrix associated to this Markov chain.



Introduction

The Cohen-Lenstra distribution is a probability distribution
on finite abelian
p
-groups. It is the distribution that
assigns to each finite abelian
p
-group
G
a probability
inversely proportional to the number of automorphisms of that
group:

\text{I\kern-1.49994ptP}(G)\propto\frac{1}{\#Aut(G)}

(0.1)

It originated in number theory, in the study of the
statistical behaviour of class groups or quadratic fields. In
[
CL84
]

Cohen and Lenstra made the conjecture that, for odd
p
, the
p
-part of the
class group of a quadratic imaginary fields is distributed
exactly in such a way, as the discriminant ranges over all
possible negative values.

The prediction of Cohen and Lenstra agrees with numerical evidence and a small number of cases have been proven; see, for example, the survey
[
Woo23
]
. A natural question to ask is why this particular distribution (
0.1
) is to be expected. Is this a special property of class groups of quadratic fields? Or rather is it a property of finite abelian
p
-groups, that when such groups appear at random, without any additional structure, they tend to be distributed according to the Cohen-Lenstra measure? In 1989, Friedman and Washington gave credence to the latter point of view, by finding the occurrence of this measure in another context: cokernels of random matrices.

Theorem 0.1
.

[
FW89
]

If
\mathcal{M}_{n,n}
is a random matrix whose entries are
independent uniformly distributed elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{I\kern-1.53325ptP}\Big(coker(\mathcal{M}_{n,n})\cong G\Big)\propto\frac{1}{|Aut(G)|}

That is, the distribution of the cokernel of

\mathcal{M}_{n,n}
is the Cohen-Lenstra distribution in the
limit
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Moreover, as found in the more recent work of Maples, Wood,
Nguyen and others, the conclusion of
Theorem 0.1
continues to hold when we replace
"uniformly distributed" by "identically distributed",
indicating that the distribution (
0.1
) is indeed
somehow universal. These results are reminiscent of the central limit theorem.
In recent years, there has been a flurry of work devoted to
proving more precise and more general statements in this
direction, for example
[
Map13
]
,
[
Woo17
]
,
[
Woo19
]
,
[
NW21
]
,
[
NW22
]
,
[
CY23
]
,
[
Lee23
]
,
[
Yan23
]
. Again, we refer the reader to the survey
[
Woo23
]
.

In this paper, we study the Cohen-Lenstra distribution from
a somewhat different point of view. Taking the random matrix
model of
Theorem 0.1
as our starting point, we
study the distribution of the cokernel of a random
matrix, after conditioning on one of its submatrices.

The first interesting statement we obtain on this line of
inquiry is that this gives rise to an irreducible Markov chain
on the category of finite abelian p-groups, whose stationary
measure is the Cohen-Lenstra distribution. The description of
this Markov chain is the content of the first section. The generator of this
Markov chain is what we call the
Cohen-Lenstra operator
, and denote as
\Delta_{0}
.

This Markov chain has surprising properties. Firstly, it is
reversible. Hence, it can be described as a random walk on a
weighted graph whose vertices are finite abelian
p
-groups.
In the second section, we prove this statement and give a
simple explicit description of the graph, its edges and edge weights.

Finally, the property of a Markov chain being reversible is
equivalent to its generator being a self-adjoint operator,
with respect to a natural Hilbert space structure. Therefore, the spectral theorem
[
Bou23
, IV.194]
, applies to
\Delta_{0}
. In the final chapter, we determine the spectrum of
\Delta_{0}
explicitly. Our study of the spectrum culminates in the following result:

Theorem

(Main Theorem)
.

There is an
explicit
unitary operator
\mathcal{U}
, such that:

•

The image of
\mathcal{U}
contains the image of
\Delta_{0}
.

•

\mathcal{U}^{-1}\Delta_{0}\,\mathcal{U}
is the diagonal operator
|\#G|^{-1}
.

In the remaining part of the introduction, we go through the
contents of each section in more detail, and give exact statements of our results.

Relation with previous work

The idea of associating to a random matrix the random process consisting of the cokernels of its submatrices appears in
[

]

[

]

The relation of

We remark that there is another Markov chain which is often mentioned in the context of the Cohen-Lenstra measure, that is introduced in

Also, in recent work, studying

Survey of Section 2: Properties of
d^{*}
and
d
and the reversibility of the Markov chain induced by
\Delta_{0}

The main result of this chapter is that
\Delta_{0}
gives rise to a reversible Markov chain, with stationary measure
\mu_{0}
.

\mu_{0}(G_{1})\text{I\kern-1.49994ptP}(G_{1}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{0}}G_{2})=\mu_{0}(G_{2})\text{I\kern-1.49994ptP}(G_{2}\xrightarrow{\Delta_{0}}G_{1})\hskip 14.45377pt\forall G_{1},G_{2}\in X_{0}

This statement is equivalent to
\Delta_{0}
being self-adjoint with respect to a certain natural inner product. We prove this by showing that
\Delta_{0}
is the composition of
d
and its adjoint:

0.2.1
d
and
d^{*}
are adjoint operators

In a natural sense,
d^{*}
is the formal adjoint of
d
. To define what this means, we need to specify measures on the discrete sets
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
.

1.

The measure on
X_{0}
is
\mu_{0}
:

\mu_{0}(G)=\frac{c_{0}}{|Aut(G)|}

(0.5)

where
c_{0}
is normalized so that
\mu_{0}
is a probability measure.

2.

For
H\in X_{1}
, let
H_{tors}
denote the torsion part of
H
. The measure on
X_{1}
is
\mu_{1}
:

\mu_{1}(H)=\frac{c_{1}}{|H_{tors}||Aut(H_{tors})|}

where
c_{1}
is normalized so that
\mu_{1}
is a probability measure.

These measures induce inner products:

1.

For any two measures
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
on
X_{0}
,

\Big<\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\Big>_{X_{0}}=\sum_{G\in X_{0}}\frac{\nu_{1}(G)\nu_{2}(G)}{\mu_{0}(G)}

(0.6)

2.

For any two measures
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
on
X_{1}
,

\Big<\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\Big>_{X_{1}}=\sum_{H\in X_{1}}\frac{\nu_{1}(H)\nu_{2}(H)}{\mu_{1}(H)}

(0.7)

With these preliminaries we have the following theorem:

Theorem 0.2
.

With respect to the inner products (
0.6
) and (
0.7
)
d
is the adjoint of
d^{*}
:

\Big<\nu_{1},d\nu_{2}\Big>_{X_{1}}=\Big<d^{*}\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\Big>_{X_{0}}

Equivalently, for any
G\in X_{0}
and

\mu_{0}(G)\text{I\kern-1.53325ptP}(G\xrightarrow{d^{*}}H)=\mu_{1}(G)\text{I\kern-1.53325ptP}(H\xrightarrow{d}G)

(0.8)

Corollary
.

This yields two self-adjoint operators:

•

The operator
dd^{*}
is self-adjoint with respect to
\mu_{0}
.

•

The operator
d^{*}d
is self-adjoint with respect to
\mu_{1}
.

The first of these operators is the operator
\Delta_{0}
, whose study is the main subject of this paper.

0.2.2
Alternate Perspective

We give here an alternative perspective on the equality (
0.8
):

\mu_{0}(G)\text{I\kern-1.49994ptP}(G\xrightarrow{d^{*}}H)=\mu_{1}(H)\text{I\kern-1.49994ptP}(H\xrightarrow{d}G)

The pair of operators

(d,d^{*})

(0.9)

together form a single Markov chain, whose state space is the union of state spaces,
X_{0}\cup X_{1}
. The equality (
0.8
) is then equivalent to the statement that this Markov chain is reversible with stationary measure

(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

This will be a consequence of the following lemma:

Lemma 0.3
.

We can represent the Markov chain
(d,d^{*})
on
X_{0}\cup X_{1}
, as a random walk on a weighted graph whose edges are exact sequences:

0\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow H\rightarrow G\rightarrow 0

(0.10)

with weight:

\frac{1}{|Aut(\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow H)||G|}.

Aut(\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow H)
denotes the group of automorphisms of
H
that preserve the homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow H
.

0.2.3
Relation with previous work

The original motivation behind this chapter was to explain why
\mu_{0}
is a stationary measure for
\Delta_{0}
. This fact can be translated into a statement about random matrices which is not original. However, the other statements in this chapter have apparently not been previously considered.

It is worth noting that Markov chains that are self-adjoint with respect to their stationary measure, such as

Survey of Section 3: Eigenfunctions of

Before stating our theorems about

Definition
.

\Big<\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\Big>_{X_{0}}.

The exact expression we need to show is:

2.1.1

We give here an alternative perspective on the equality (

2.1.2

In this survey, we describe a direct proof of

\frac{|Aut(G)|}{|Aut(\mathbb{Z}_{p}\rightarrow H)|}

We outline the general proof. We use an orbit-stabilizer formula for actions of infinite groups.

•

•

To get the measure of the orbit, we calculate the change-of-measure factor (the "Jacobian") from

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}=ker(\Delta_{0})\,\cap\,L^{2}(X_{0},\mu_{0})^{*}

∎

3.10
Proofs of the Main Theorems

3.10.1
Curious Formula

Theorem 3.21
.

For any
F_{1},F_{2}\in X_{0}
, we have

c_{0}\sum_{G}\frac{\#Sur(G,F_{1})\#Sur(G,F_{2})}{\#Aut(G)}=\sum_{G^{\prime}}\frac{|\#Sur(F_{1},G^{\prime})||\#Sur(F_{2},G^{\prime})|}{|\#Aut(G^{\prime})|}

(3.13)

Remark
.

Taking
F_{1}
to be the trivial group recovers the well-known fact that all the moments of the Cohen-Lenstra measure are
1
.

We begin the proof by noting that the left hand side of (
3.13
) is a weighted sum over diagrams

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{2}}

Definition
.

Let
Hom^{\prime}(G,F_{1}\times F_{2})
denote the set of homomorphisms from
G
to
F_{1}\times F_{2}
that are
surjective
to both factors.

Then,

c_{0}\sum_{G}\frac{\#Sur(G,F_{1})\#Sur(G,F_{2})}{\#Aut(G)}=

c_{0}\sum_{G}\frac{\#Hom^{\prime}(G,F_{1}\times F_{2})}{\#Aut(G)}=

c_{0}\sum_{G,K}\frac{\#Sur(G,K)\#Inj^{\prime}(K,F_{1}\times F_{2})}{\#Aut(G)\#Aut(K)}

where
\#Inj^{\prime}(K,F_{1}\times F_{2})
denotes the number of injections from
K
to
F_{1}\times F_{2}
that are surjective to both factors. Now, by (
3.6
),

c_{0}\sum_{G}\frac{\#Sur(G,K)}{\#Aut(G)}=1

Hence, the above sum becomes

\sum_{K}\frac{\#Inj^{\prime}(K,F_{1}\times F_{2})}{\#Aut(K)}

(3.14)

This is the number of subgroups of
F_{1}\times F_{2}
that project surjectively to both factors.

Lemma 3.22
.

The number of subgroups of
F_{1}\times F_{2}
that project surjectively to both factors is:

\sum_{G^{\prime}}\frac{\#Sur(F_{1},G^{\prime})\#Sur(F_{2},G^{\prime})}{\#Aut(G^{\prime})}

Lemma 3.23
.

Every such subgroup
K
fits into a Cartesian square:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}

Proof.

Take

G^{\prime}\cong K\Big/\Big(ker(\pi_{1})+ker(\pi_{1})\Big)

∎

Hence, the problem reduces to counting Cartesian diagrams. A Cartesian diagram is completely determined by the pair of homorphisms:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G^{\prime}}

(3.15)

Two distinct diagrams (
3.15
) yield the same subgroup of
F_{1}\times F_{2}
if and only if they are related by an automorphism of
G^{\prime}
. This proves the lemma.

3.10.2
Construction of
\mathcal{U}

First, we define the map that takes finite linear combinations of functions
\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}
, to finite linear combinations of functions
\frac{\#Sur(\,\cdot\,,F^{\prime})}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}
by sending

\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}\rightarrow\sqrt{c_{0}}\frac{\#Sur(\,\cdot\,,F^{\prime})}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}

Lemma 3.24
.

This map is well-defined.

Proof.

This is true because the functions
\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}
are linearly independent.
∎

This map is norm-preserving by the identity in
Theorem 3.21
.

Lemma 3.25
.

\mathcal{U}
extends to a norm-preserving homomorphism from
L^{2}(X_{0},\mu_{0})^{*}
to the closure of the linear span of

\frac{\#Sur(\,\cdot\,,F^{\prime})}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}

Proof.

\mathcal{U}
extends to a norm-preserving homomorphism from the closure of the linear span of

\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}

to the closure of the linear span of

\frac{\#Sur(\,\cdot\,,F^{\prime})}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}

But every finitely supported function can be expressed as a linear combination of functions of the form
\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}
Finitely supported functions are dense in
L^{2}(X_{0},\mu)
. Hence, the closure of the linear span of

\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}
is
L^{2}(X_{0},\mu)

∎

3.10.3
A proof of the first main theorem

We begin with the following lemma:

Lemma 3.26
.

1_{G}
is in the orthogonal complement of

span\Big\{\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}\Big|F<G\Big\}

in

span\Big\{\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}\Big|F\leq G\Big\}

Proof.

1_{G}
is orthogonal to all
\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}
, for
F<G
. Hence, all that needs to be shown is:

1_{G}\in span\Big\{\frac{\#Sur(F,\,\cdot\,)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}\Big|F\leq G\Big\}

This can be shown by induction on
G
. The details are left to the reader.
∎

Now we apply
\mathcal{U}
to get

Lemma 3.27
.

\mathcal{U}(1_{G})
is in the orthogonal complement of

span\left\{\frac{\#Sur(\,\cdot\,,F)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}\Big|F<G\right\}

in

span\left\{\frac{\#Sur(\,\cdot\,,F)}{\#Aut(\,\cdot\,)}\Big|F\leq G\right\}

Hence
\mathcal{U}(1_{G})
is in the orthogonal complement of

span\Big\{\text{{Moment}}[F]\Big|F<G\Big\}=V^{<G}

in

span\Big\{\text{{Moment}}[F]\Big|F\leq G\Big\}=V^{\leq G}

Therefore,
\mathcal{U}(1_{G})
is a multiple of
E_{G}
. Hence:

\Delta_{0}\mathcal{U}(1_{G})=|\#G|^{-1}\mathcal{U}(1_{G})

Or alternatively,

\Delta_{0}\mathcal{U}(1_{G})=\mathcal{U}\left(|\#G|^{-1}1_{G}\right)

By linearity, we get:

Theorem

(The First Main Theorem)
.

\Delta_{0}\mathcal{U}\left(\nu\right)=\mathcal{U}\left(|\#G|^{-1}\nu\right)

Lemma 3.28
.

The Random Walk Operator on a Weighted Graph has norm
1
.

Suppose we have a graph with vertex set
V
a weighted edge set
V\times V
and probability measures
\rho:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\mu:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\rho(v,w)=\rho(w,v)

\sum_{w\in W}\rho(v,w)=\mu(v)

Definition
.

Let
L^{2}(V,\mu)^{*}
be the space of measures on
V
with norm

||\nu||\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\nu(w)^{2}}{\mu(w)}

Lemma 3.30
.

The weighted random walk operator:

\nu(\,\cdot\,)\mapsto\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\nu(w)\rho(\,\cdot\,,w)}{\mu(w)}

is a bounded on
L^{2}(V,\mu)^{*}
.

Proof.

\sum_{v\in V}\frac{1}{\mu(v)}\left(\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\nu(w)\rho(v,w)}{\mu(w)}\right)^{2}=

=\sum_{v\in V}\frac{\mu(v)}{\mu(w)^{2}}\left(\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\nu(w)\rho(v,w)}{\mu(v)}\right)^{2}\leq

\leq\sum_{v\in V}\frac{\mu(v)}{\mu(w)^{2}}\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\nu(w)^{2}\rho(v,w)}{\mu(v)}=

\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\rho(v,w)}{\mu(w)^{2}}\nu(w)^{2}=\sum_{w\in V}\frac{\nu(w)^{2}}{\mu(w)}

∎
