Improved Stein Variational Gradient Descent with Importance Weights Thanks: King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, Thuwal, Saudi Arabia
Lukang Sun, Peter Richtárik

Abstract
Stein Variational Gradient Descent ( SVGD ) is a popular sampling algorithm used in various machine learning tasks. It is well known that SVGD arises from a discretization of the kernelized gradient flow of the Kullback-Leibler divergence D KL ( ⋅ ∣ π ) D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\cdot\mid\pi\right) , where π \pi is the target distribution. In this work, we propose to enhance SVGD via the introduction of importance weights , which leads to a new method for which we coin the name β \beta -SVGD . In the continuous time and infinite particles regime, the time for this flow to converge to the equilibrium distribution π \pi , quantified by the Stein Fisher information, depends on ρ 0 \rho_{0} and π \pi very weakly. This is very different from the kernelized gradient flow of Kullback-Leibler divergence, whose time complexity depends on D KL ​ ( ρ 0 ∣ π ) D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right) . Under certain assumptions, we provide a descent lemma for the population limit β \beta -SVGD , which covers the descent lemma for the population limit SVGD when β → 0 \beta\to 0 . We also illustrate the advantages of β \beta -SVGD over SVGD by experiments.



1
Introduction

The main technical task of Bayesian inference is to estimate integration with respect to the posterior distribution

\pi(x)\propto e^{-V(x)},

where
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a potential. In practice, this is often reduced to sampling points from the distribution
\pi
. Typical methods that employ this strategy include algorithms based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (
MCMC
), such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(
Neal 2011
)
, also known as Hybrid Monte Carlo (
HMC
)
(
Duane et al. 1987
;
Betancourt 2017
)
, and algorithms based on Langevin dynamics
(
Dalalyan & Karagulyan 2019
;
Durmus & Moulines 2017
;
Cheng et al. 2018
)
.

One the other hand, Stein Variational Gradient Descent (
SVGD
)—a different strategy suggested by
Liu & Wang 2016
—is based on an interacting particle system. In the population limit, the interacting particle system can be seen as the kernelized negative gradient flow of the Kullback-Leibler divergence

\textstyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho\mid\pi\right):=\int\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)(x)\;d\rho(x);

(1)

see
(
Liu 2017
;
Duncan et al. 2019
)
.
SVGD
has already been widely used in a variety of machine learning settings, including
variational auto-encoders
(
Pu et al. 2017
)
, reinforcement learning
(
Liu et al. 2017
)
,
sequential decision making
(
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
, generative adversarial networks
(
Tao et al. 2019
)
and federated learning
(
Kassab & Simeone 2022
)
. However, current theoretical understanding of
SVGD
is limited to its infinite particle version
(
Liu 2017
;
Korba et al. 2020
;
Salim et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
, and the theory on finite particle
SVGD
is far from satisfactory.

Since
SVGD
is built on a discretization of the kernelized negative gradient flow of
(
1
)
, we can learn about its sampling potential by studying this flow. In fact, a simple calculation reveals that

\textstyle\min\limits_{0\leq s\leq t}I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{s}\mid\pi\right)\leq\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right)}{t},

(2)

where
I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{s}\mid\pi\right)
is the Stein Fisher information (see Definition
2
) of
\rho_{s}
relative to
\pi
, which is typically used to quantify how close to
\pi
are the probability distributions
\left(\rho_{s}\right)_{s=0}^{t}
generated along this flow. In particular, if our goal is to guarantee

\min\limits_{0\leq s\leq t}I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{s}\mid\pi\right)\leq\varepsilon,

result
(
2
)
says that we need to take

\textstyle t\geq\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right)}{\varepsilon}.

Unfortunately, and this is the key motivation for our work, the quantity the initial
\operatorname{KL}
divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right)
can be very large. Indeed, it can be proportional to the underlying dimension, which is highly problematic in high dimensional regimes.
Salim et al. 2021
and
Sun et al. 2022
have recently derived an iteration complexity bound for the infinite particle
SVGD
method. However, similarly to the time complexity of the continuous flow, their bound depends on
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right)
.

1.1
Summary of contributions

In this paper, we design a family of continuous time flows—which we call

\beta
-SVGD
flow—by combining
importance weights
with the kernelized gradient flow of the
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence. Surprisingly, we prove that the time for this flow to converge to the equilibrium distribution
\pi
, that is
\min_{0\leq s\leq t}I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{s}\mid\pi\right)\leq\varepsilon
with
(\rho_{s})_{s=0}^{t}
generated along

\beta
-SVGD
flow, can be bounded by
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon\beta(\beta+1)}
when
\beta\in(-1,0)
. This indicates that the importance weights can potentially accelerate
SVGD
. Actually, we design

\beta
-SVGD
method based on a discretization of the

\beta
-SVGD
flow and provide a descent lemma for its population limit version. Some simple experiments in
Appendix

D
verify our predictions.

We summarize our contributions in the following:

•

A new family of flows.
We construct a family of continuous time flows for which we coin the name

\beta
-SVGD
flows. These flows do
not
arise from a time re-parameterization of the
SVGD
flow since their trajectories are different, nor can they be seen as the kernelized gradient flows of the Rényi divergence.

•

Convergence rates.
When
\beta\to 0
, this returns back to the kernelized gradient flow of the
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence (
SVGD
flow); when
\beta\in(-1,0)
, the convergence rate of

\beta
-SVGD
flows is significantly improved than that of the
SVGD
flow in the case
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right)
is large. Under a Stein Poincaré inequality, we derive an exponential convergence rate of
2
-Rényi divergence along

1
-SVGD
flow. Stein Poincaré inequality is proved to be weaker than Stein log-Sobolev inequality, however like Stein log-Sobolev inequality, it is not clear to us when it does hold.

•

Algorithm.
We design

\beta
-SVGD
algorithm based on a discretization of the

\beta
-SVGD
flow and we derive a descent lemmas for the population limit

\beta
-SVGD
.

•

Experiments.
Finally, we do some experiments to illustrate the advantages of

\beta
-SVGD
with negative
\beta
. The simulation results on

\beta
-SVGD
corroborate our theory.

2
Preliminaries

We assume the target distribution
\pi\propto e^{-V}
, and we have oracle to calculate the value of
e^{-V(x)}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.1
Notation

Let
x=\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\right)^{\top},y=\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denote
\left<x,y\right>:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}y_{i}
and
\left\|x\right\|:=\sqrt{\left<x,x\right>}
. For a square matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the operator norm and Frobenius norm of
B
are defined respectively by

\|B\|_{op}:=\sqrt{\varrho(B^{\top}B)}
and
\|B\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{i,j}^{2}}
, respectively,
where
\varrho
denotes the spectral radius. It is easy to verify that
\left\|B\right\|_{op}\leq\left\|B\right\|_{F}
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the space of probability measures with finite second moment; that is, for any
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
\int\left\|x\right\|^{2}\;d\mu(x)<+\infty
. The Wasserstein
2
-distance between
\rho,\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

\textstyle W_{2}\left(\rho,\mu\right):=\inf\limits_{\eta\in\Gamma(\rho,\mu)}\sqrt{\int\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}\;d\eta(x,y)},

where
\Gamma\left(\rho,\mu\right)
is the set of all joint distributions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
having
\rho
and
\mu
as marginals. The push-forward distribution of
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
by a map
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
T_{\#}\rho
, is defined as follows: for any measurable set
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T_{\#}\rho\left(\Omega\right):=\rho\left(T^{-1}\left(\Omega\right)\right)
. By definition of the push-forward distribution, it is not hard to verify that the probability densities satisfy
T_{\#}\rho(T(x))|\operatorname{det}\operatorname{D}T(x)|=\rho(x)
, where
\operatorname{D}T
is the Jacobian matrix of
T
. The reader can refer to
Villani 2009
for more details.

2.2
Rényi divergence

Next, we define the Rényi divergence which plays an important role in information theory and many other areas such as hypothesis testing
(
Morales González et al. 2013
)
and multiple source adaptation
(
Mansour et al. 2012
)
.

Definition 1
(Rényi divergence)

For two probability distributions
\rho
and
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\rho\ll\mu
, the Rényi divergence of positive order
\alpha
is defined as

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits(\rho\mid\mu):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\mu}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)\;d\rho(x)\right)&0<\alpha<\infty,\;\alpha\neq 1\\
\int\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\mu}\right)(x)\;d\rho(x)&\alpha=1\end{cases}.

(3)

If
\rho
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
, we set
\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits(\rho\mid\mu)=\infty
. Further, we denote
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho\mid\mu\right):=\mathrm{D}_{1}\left(\rho\mid\mu\right)
.

Rényi divergence is non-negative, continuous and non-decreasing in terms of the parameter
\alpha
; specifically, we have
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho\mid\mu\right)=\lim_{\alpha\to 1}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits(\rho\mid\mu)
. More properties of Rényi divergence can be found in a comprehensive article by
Van Erven & Harremos 2014
. Besides Rényi divergence, there are other generalizations of the
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence, e.g., admissible relative entropies
(
Arnold et al. 2001
)
.

2.3
Background on
SVGD

Stein Variational Gradient Descent (
SVGD
) is defined on a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
with a non-negative definite reproducing kernel
k:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The key feature of this space is its reproducing property:

f(x)=\left<f(\cdot),k(x,\cdot)\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}},\qquad\forall f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0},

(4)

where
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}
is the inner product defined on
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the
d
-fold Cartesian product of
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
. That is,
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if there exist
f_{1},\cdots,f_{d}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
such that
f=\left(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\right)^{\top}
. Naturally, the inner product on
{\mathcal{H}}
is given by

\textstyle\left<f,g\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left<f_{i},g_{i}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{0}},\qquad f=\left(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\right)^{\top}\in{\mathcal{H}},\qquad g=\left(g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\right)^{\top}\in{\mathcal{H}}.

(5)

For more details of RKHS, the readers can refer to
Berlinet & Thomas-Agnan 2011
.

It is well known (see for example
Ambrosio et al. 2005
) that
\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)
is the Wasserstein gradient of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\cdot\mid\pi\right)
at
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Liu & Wang 2016
proposed a kernelized Wasserstein gradient of the
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence, defined by

\textstyle g_{\rho}(x):=\int k(x,y)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)(y)\;d\rho(y)\in{\mathcal{H}}.

(6)

Integration by parts yields

\textstyle g_{\rho}(x)=-\int\left[\nabla\log\pi(y)k(x,y)+\nabla_{y}k(x,y)\right]\;d\rho(y).

(7)

Comparing the Wasserstein gradient
\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)
with
(
7
)
, we find that the latter can be easily approximated by

\textstyle g_{\rho}(x)\approx\hat{g}_{\hat{\rho}}:=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left[\nabla\log\pi(x_{i})k(x,x_{i})+\nabla_{x_{i}}k(x,x_{i})\right],

(8)

with
\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\left(x_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{N}
sampled from
\rho
. With the above notations, the
SVGD
update rule

\textstyle x_{i}\leftarrow x_{i}+\frac{\gamma}{N}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\left[\nabla\log\pi(x_{j})k(x_{i},x_{j})+\nabla_{x_{j}}k(x_{i},x_{j})\right],\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(9)

where
\gamma
is the step-size, can be presented in the compact form

\hat{\rho}\leftarrow\left(I-\gamma\hat{g}_{\hat{\rho}}\right)_{\#}\hat{\rho}.
When we talk about the infinite particle
SVGD
, or population limit
SVGD
, we mean

\rho\leftarrow\left(I-\gamma{g}_{\rho}\right)_{\#}\rho.

The metric used in the study of
SVGD
is the Stein Fisher information or the Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (KSD).

Definition 2
(Stein Fisher Information)

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The Stein Fisher Information of
\rho
relative to
\pi
is defined by

\textstyle I_{Stein}(\rho\mid\pi):=\iint k(x,y)\left<\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)(x),\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)(y)\right>\;d\rho(x)\;d\rho(y).

(10)

A sufficient condition under which
\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{Stein}(\rho_{n}\mid\pi)
implies
\rho_{n}\to\pi
weakly can be found in
Gorham & Mackey 2017
, which requires: i) the kernel
k
to be in the form
k(x,y)=\left(c^{2}+\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}\right)^{\theta}
for some
c>0
and
\theta\in(-1,0)
; ii)
\pi\propto e^{-V}
to be distant dissipative; roughly speaking, this requires
V
to be convex outside a compact set, see
Gorham & Mackey 2017
for an accurate definition.

In the study of the kernelized Wasserstein gradient
(
7
)
and its corresponding continuity equation

\textstyle\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{t}g_{\rho_{t}}\right)=0,

Duncan et al. 2019
introduced the following kernelized log-Sobolev inequality to prove the exponential convergence of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{t}\mid\pi\right)
along the direction
(
7
)
:

Definition 3
(Stein log-Sobolev inequality)

We say
\pi
satisfies the Stein log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\lambda>0
if

\textstyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\mid\pi)\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda}I_{Stein}(\rho\mid\pi).

(11)

While this inequality can guarantee an exponential convergence rate of
\rho_{t}
to
\pi
, quantified by the
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence, the condition for
\pi
to satisfy the Stein log-Sobolev inequality is very restrictive. In fact, little is known about when
(
11
)
holds.

3.1
\beta
-SVGD
flow

In this paper, a
flow
refers to some time-dependent vector field
v_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This time-dependent vector field will influence the mass distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the continuity equation or the equation of conservation of mass

\textstyle\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{t}v_{t}\right)=0,

(12)

readers can refer to
Ambrosio et al. 2005
for more details.

Definition 4
(

\beta
-SVGD
flow)

Given a weight parameter
\beta\in(-1,+\infty)
, the

\beta
-SVGD
flow is given by

\textstyle v_{t}^{\beta}(x):=-\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}}\right)^{\beta}(x)\int k(x,y)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(y)\;d\rho_{t}(y).

(13)

Note that when
\beta=0
, this is the negative kernelized Wasserstein gradient
(
6
)
.

Note that we can not treat

\beta
-SVGD
flow as the kernelized Wasserstein gradient flow of the

(\beta+1)
-Rényi divergence. However, they are closely related, and we can derive the following theorem.

Theorem 1
(Main result)

Along the

\beta
-SVGD
flow
(
13
)
, we have

1

In fact, in the proof in
Appendix

B
we know a stronger result. When
\beta\in(-1,0)
, the right hand side of
(
14
)
is only weakly dependent on
\rho_{0}
and
\pi
and should be
\frac{\left|e^{\beta\mathrm{D}_{\beta+1}\left(\rho_{0}\mid\pi\right)}-e^{\beta\mathrm{D}_{\beta+1}\left(\rho_{T}\mid\pi\right)}\right|}{T|\beta(\beta+1)|}
, which is less than

.

Note the left hand side of

)
is the Stein Fisher information. When
\beta
decreases from positive to negative, the right hand side of

)
changes dramatically; it appears to be independent of
\rho_{0}
and
\pi
. If we do not know the Rényi divergence between
\rho_{0}
and
\pi
, it seems the best convergence rate is obtained by setting
\beta=-\frac{1}{2}
, that is

\textstyle\min_{t\in[0,T]}I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{t}\mid\pi\right)\leq\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}I_{Stein}(\rho_{t}\mid\pi)dt\leq\leq\frac{4}{T}.

It is somewhat unexpected to observe that the time complexity is independent of
\rho_{0}
and
\pi
, or to be more precise, that it relies only very weakly on
\rho_{0}
and
\pi
when
\beta\in(-1,0)
. We wish to stress that this is
not

Theorem

1
, we can see the term
\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}\right)^{\beta}
in

\beta
-SVGD
flow
(
13
)
is utilized to cancel term
\left(\nicefrac{{\rho_{t}}}{{\pi}}\right)^{\beta}
in the Wasserstein gradient of
(\beta+1)
-Rényi divergence. Actually, when
\beta\in(-1,0)
, this term has an added advantage and can be seen as the acceleration factor in front of the kernelized Wasserstein gradient of
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence. Specifically, the negative kernelized Wasserstein gradient of
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence

is the vector field that compels
\rho_{t}
to approach
\pi
, while
\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}\right)^{\beta}(x)
is big (roughly speaking this means

is close to the mass concentration region of
\rho_{t}
but away from the one of
\pi
), this factor will enhance the vector field at point
x
and force the mass around
x
move faster towards the mass concentration region of
\pi
; on the other hand, if
\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}\right)^{\beta}(x)
is small (this means
x
is already near to the mass concentration region of
\pi
), this factor will weaken the vector field and make the mass surrounding
x
remain within the mass concentration region of
\pi
. This is the intuitive justification for why, when
\beta\in(-1,0)
, the time complexity for

\beta
-SVGD
flow to diminish the Stein Fisher information only depends on
\rho_{0}
and
\pi
very weakly.

. The regime when

, which heavily depends on

and

. Mathematically speaking, the weak dependence on

on

3.2
1-SVGD
flow and the Stein Poincaré inequality

Functional
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\cdot\mid\cdot\right)
is non-symmetric; that is,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\cdot\mid\pi\right)\neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi\mid\cdot\right)
, and so is their Wasserstein gradient. The Wasserstein gradient of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi\mid\cdot\right)
at distribution
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
-\nabla\frac{\pi}{\rho}
(see
Appendix

A
), or, to put it another way,
\frac{\pi}{\rho}\nabla\log(\frac{\rho}{\pi})
, which may be regarded as the non-kernelized

1

Definition 5
(Stein Poincaré inequality)

We say that
\pi
satisfies the Stein Poincaré inequality with constant

\textstyle\int|g|^{2}\;d\pi\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\iint k(x,y)\left<\nabla g(x),\nabla g(y)\right>\;d\pi(x)\;d\pi(y),

for any smooth

\int g\;d\pi=0

While

also introduced the Stein Poincaré inequality, they presented it in a different form. Just as Poincaré inequality is a linearized log-Sobolev inequality (see for example

Lemma 1
(Stein log-Sobolev implies Stein Poincaré)

If

(

While the proof of the above lemma is a routine task, for completeness we provide it in
Appendix

B
. The following theorem is inspired by

-SVGD

Suppose

Since

4
The

\beta
-SVGD
algorithm

The

\beta
-SVGD
algorithm
2
2

2

For simplicity, we will often just call it

\beta
-SVGD
; not to be confused with the

\beta
-SVGD
flow.
proposed here is a sampling method suggested by the discretization of the

\beta
-SVGD
flow
(
13
)
. Our method reverts to the traditional
SVGD
algorithm when
\beta=0
.

As in
SVGD
, the integral term in the

\beta
-SVGD
flow
(
13
)
can be approximated by
(
8
)
. However, when
\beta\neq 0
, we have to estimate the extra importance weight term
\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}\right)^{\beta}
. Due to the lack of the normalization constant of
\pi
and the curse of dimension, we can hardly to use the kernel density estimation
(
Silverman 2018
)
to approximate
\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}
accurately in high dimension. Here, we use a different approach to approximate
\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}
, known as the Stein importance weight
(
Liu & Lee 2017
)
. This method does not rely on the normalization constant of
\pi
and can be scaled to high dimension. Given
N
points
\left(x_{i}\right)^{N}_{i=1}
sampled from
\rho_{t}
, a non-negative definite reproducing kernel
k
(can be different from the one in

\beta
-SVGD
) and the score function
\nabla\log(\pi)=-\nabla V
, the Stein importance weight
\hat{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
is the solution of the following constrained quadratic optimization problem:

\underset{\bm{w}}{\arg\min}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{K}_{\pi}\bm{w},\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1,\quad w_{i}\geq 0\right\},

(19)

where
\bm{K}_{\pi}:=\left\{k_{\pi}(x_{i},x_{j})\right\}_{i,j=1}^{N}
and

\displaystyle{k}_{\pi}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=k(x,y)\left<\nabla V(x),\nabla V(y)\right>-\left<\nabla V(x),\nabla_{y}k(x,y)\right>

(20)

\displaystyle-\left<\nabla V(y),\nabla_{x}k(x,y)\right>+\operatorname{tr}\left(\nabla_{x}\nabla_{y}k(x,y)\right).

It can be proved that as
N\to+\infty
,
N\hat{\bm{w}}
will approximate
\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{t}}}\right)
, see
Liu & Lee 2017
.
Problem equation
19
can be solved efficiently by mirror descent with step-size
r
, which can be simplified into the following:

\omega_{i}^{s+1}=\frac{\omega_{i}^{s}e^{-r\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{\pi}(x_{i},x_{j})\omega_{j}^{s}}}{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\omega_{l}^{s}e^{-r\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{\pi}(x_{l},x_{j})\omega_{j}^{s}}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N.

(21)

With matrix
\bm{K}_{\pi}
, the computation cost of mirror descent to find the optimum with
\varepsilon
-accuracy is
O(\nicefrac{{N^{2}}}{{\varepsilon}})
, which is independent of dimension
d
. In general,
N
cannot be too large because the cost of one iteration of
SVGD
is
O(N^{2}d)
, which quadratically depends on
N
.

Remark 2

Stein matrix
\bm{K}_{\pi}
can be efficiently constructed using simple matrix operation, since
\left\{\nabla V(x_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
have already been computed in the
SVGD
update.

4.1
Non-asymptotic analysis for

\beta
-SVGD

In this section, we study the convergence of the population limit

\beta
-SVGD
, that is

x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\gamma\left[\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{n}}\right)^{\beta}(x_{n})\wedge M\right]\int k(x_{n},y)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\pi}\right)(y)d\rho_{n}(y),

(22)

where

\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{n}}\right)^{\beta}(x_{n})\wedge M=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\max\left(N\omega_{i},\tau\right)\right)^{\beta}

and
M:=\frac{1}{\tau^{\beta}}
.

Specifically, we establish a descent lemma for it. The derivation of the descent lemma is based on several assumptions.

The first assumption postulates
L
-smoothness of
V
; this is typically assumed in the study of optimization algorithms, Langevin algorithms and
SVGD
.

Assumption 1
(
L
-smoothness)

The potential function
V
of the target distribution
\pi\propto e^{-V}
is
L
-smooth; that is,

\left\|\nabla^{2}V\right\|_{op}\leq L.

Our second assumption postulates two bounds involving the reproducing kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
, and is also common when studying
SVGD
; see
(
Liu 2017
;
Korba et al. 2020
;
Salim et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
.

Assumption 2

Kernel
k
is continuously differentiable and there exists
B>0
such that
\|k(x,.)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\leq B
and

\textstyle\left\|\nabla_{x}k(x,.)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\partial_{x_{i}}k\left(x,.\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}^{2}\leq B^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By the reproducing property
(
4
)
, this is equivalent to
k(x,x)\leq B^{2}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{y_{i}}k(x,y)\mid_{y=x}\leq B^{2}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and this is easily satisfied by kernel of the form
k(x,y)=f(x-y)
, where
f
is some smooth function at point
0
.

The third assumption was already used by
Liu 2017
, and was later replaced by
Salim et al. 2021
it with a Talagrand inequality (Wasserstein distance can be upper bounded by
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence) which depends on
\pi
only. However,

\beta
-SVGD
reduces the Rényi divergence instead of the
\operatorname{KL}
-divergence. Since we do not have a comparable inequality for the Rényi divergence, we are forced to adopt the one from
(
Liu 2017
)
here.

Assumption 3

There exists
C>0
such that
\sqrt{I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{n}\mid\pi\right)}\leq C
for all
n=0,1,\ldots,N
.

In the proof of the descent lemma, the next two assumptions help us deal with the extra term
\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{n}}}\right)^{\beta}
. Note that the fourth assumption is very weak. In fact, as long as
Z_{n}(x,y)\rho_{n}(x)\rho_{n}(y)

is integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then by the monotone convergence theorem, the truncating number
M_{\rho_{n}}(\delta)
is always attainable since
\left(\nicefrac{{\rho_{n}}}{{\pi}}\right)^{\beta}\left[\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{n}}}\right)^{\beta}\wedge M\right]
is non-decreasing and converges point-wise to
1
as
M\to+\infty
.

Assumption 4

For any small
\delta>0
, we can find
M_{\rho_{n}}(\delta)>0
such that

\textstyle\left|I_{Stein}\left(\rho_{n}\mid\pi\right)-\iint\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\pi}\right)^{\beta}(x)\left[\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{n}}\right)^{\beta}(x)\wedge M_{\rho_{n}}(\delta)\right]Z_{n}(x,y)\;d\rho_{n}(x)\;d\rho_{n}(y)\right|\leq\delta,

(23)

where
Z_{n}(x,y):=k(x,y)\left<\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\pi}\right)(x),\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\pi}\right)(y)\right>
.

Our fifth and last assumption is of a technical nature, and helps us bound
\left\|\nabla_{x}\left(\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{\rho_{n}}}\right)^{\beta}(x)\left(\int k(x,y)\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{n}}{\pi})(y)d\rho_{n}(y)\right)^{\top}\right\|_{F}
. It is also relatively weak, and achievable for example when the potential function of
\rho_{n}
does not fluctuate wildly.

Assumption 5

\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{n}}\right)^{\beta}\right\|\leq{C_{\rho_{n}}\left(\delta\right)}
in the region

\left\{x:\left(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{n}}\right)^{\beta}(x)\leq M_{\rho_{n}}(\delta)\right\}
.

Though Assumptions
3
,
4
and
5
are relatively reasonable, as we stated, we do not know how to estimate constants
C
,
M_{\rho_{n}}(\delta)
and
C_{\rho_{n}}(\delta)
beforehand.

With all this preparation, we can now formulate our descent lemma for the population limit

5
Conclusion

We construct a family of continuous time flows called

\beta
-SVGD
flows on the space of probability distributions, when
\beta\in(-1,0)
, its convergence rate is independent of the initial distribution and the target distribution. Based on

\beta
-SVGD
flow, we design a family of weighted
SVGD
called

\beta
-SVGD
.

\beta
-SVGD
has the similar computation complexity as
SVGD
, and due to the Stein importance weight, it converges more quickly and is more stable than
SVGD
in our experiments.

We use importance weight as a preconditioner in the update of
SVGD
, and this idea can be applied to other kinds of sampling algorithms, such as Langevin algorithm. There have been a number of generalised Langevin type dynamics proposed, see
Garbuno-Inigo et al. 2020
;
Li & Ying 2019
, however, the advantages of these dynamics over the original Langevin dynamics are unclear. Inspired by

\beta
-SVGD
flow (
4
) and
Theorem

1
, we can easily prove a similar theorem for the importance weighted Langevin dynamic with a stronger Fisher information criterion. We left this for future study.

Appendix A
Calculus

This section is devoted to provide rigorous verification for several claims in the main paper, these results are already known to readers who are familiar with Rényi divergence.
We first calculate the Wasserstein gradient flow of Rényi divergence. Let
\rho_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{t}v_{t}\right)=0,

for some vector field
v_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then when
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits\left(\rho_{t}\mid\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho_{t}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\frac{\int\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha}(x)d\pi(x)}{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\frac{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}(x)dx}{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\frac{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{t}v_{t}\right)(x)dx}{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\frac{\int\left<\nabla\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x),v_{t}(x)\right>d\rho_{t}(x)}{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{\int\left<\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x),v_{t}(x)\right>d\rho_{t}(x)}{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\left<\frac{\alpha\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}\nabla\log(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi})}{\int\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}d\rho_{t}},v_{t}\right>_{\rho_{t}}.

When
\alpha=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho_{t}\mid\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x)d\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d}{dt}\left\{\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}(x)\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x)\right\}d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\int\left(1+\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x)\right)\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int\left(1+\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x)\right)\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{t}v_{t}\right)(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int\left<\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x),v_{t}(x)\right>d\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\left<\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right),v_{t}\right>_{\rho_{t}}.

The Wasserstein gradient of the reverse KL-divergence:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi\mid\rho_{t}\right)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d}{dt}\int\log(\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}})(x)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}}{\rho_{t}}(x)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\int\operatorname{div}\left(\rho_{t}v_{t}\right)(x)\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int\left<-\nabla\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}}(x),v_{t}(x)\right>d\rho_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\left<-\nabla\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}},v_{t}\right>_{\rho_{t}},

so it is
-\nabla\frac{\pi}{\rho_{t}}
.

Next, we verify that
\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits\left(\rho\mid\pi\right)\geq 0
.
For
\alpha>1
, we have

\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho(x)=\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha}(x)d\pi(x)\geq\left(\int\frac{\rho}{\pi}(x)d\pi(x)\right)^{\alpha}=1

by the convexity of function
t^{\alpha}
for
t\geq 0
, so

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits\left(\rho\mid\pi\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho(x)\right)\geq 0.

When
\alpha=1
, by the convexity of function
t\log(t)
for
t\geq 0
, we also have

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\rho\mid\pi\right)=\int\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x)d\rho(x)=\int\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}(x)\log\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)(x)d\pi(x)\geq 0.

When
\alpha\in(0,1)
, function
t^{\alpha}
for
t\geq 0
is concave, so we first have

\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho(x)=\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha}(x)d\pi(x)\leq\left(\int\frac{\rho}{\pi}(x)d\pi(x)\right)^{\alpha}=1,

finally

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}}\nolimits\left(\rho\mid\pi\right)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\left(\int\left(\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right)^{\alpha-1}(x)d\rho(x)\right)\geq 0.

Lemma 2

Let
B
be a square matrix and
\|B\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{2}}
its Frobenius norm. Let
\epsilon
be a positive number that satisfies
0\leq\gamma<\frac{1}{3\left\|B\right\|_{F}}
, where
\varrho(\cdot)
denotes the spectrum radius. Then
I+\epsilon\left(B+B^{\top}\right)+\epsilon^{2}BB^{\top}
is positive definite, and

\displaystyle\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{9\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{1-3\epsilon\left\|B\right\|_{F}}+2{\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{F}}\right)

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\log|\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon B)|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{9\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{1+3\epsilon\left\|B\right\|_{F}}+2{\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{F}}\right).

Therefore, take an even smaller
\epsilon
such that
0\leq\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{6\left\|B\right\|_{F}}
, we get

\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-5\epsilon^{2}||B\|_{F}^{2}\leq\log|\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon B)|\leq\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-2\epsilon^{2}||B\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof 10
(proof of
Lemma

2
)

We follow the proof from
Liu 2017
. When
\epsilon<\frac{1}{\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}\right)}
, we have

\varrho\left(I+\epsilon\left(B+B^{\top}\right)+\epsilon^{2}BB^{\top}\right)\geq 1-\epsilon\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}\right)>0,

and so
I+\epsilon\left(B+B^{\top}\right)+\epsilon^{2}BB^{\top}
is positive definite.
By the property of matrix determinant, we have

\displaystyle\log|\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon B)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{det}\left((I+\epsilon B)(I+\epsilon B)^{\top}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{det}\left(I+\epsilon\left(B+B^{\top}\right)+\epsilon^{2}BB^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{det}\left(I+\epsilon\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)\right).

Let
A=B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}
, we can establish

\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(A)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{1-\epsilon\varrho(A)}\leq\log\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon A)\leq\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(A)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{\|A\|_{F}^{2}}{1+\epsilon\varrho(A)},

which holds for any symmetric matrix
A
and
0\leq\epsilon<1/\varrho(A)
. This is because, assuming
\left\{\lambda_{i}\right\}
are the eigenvalues of
A
,

\displaystyle\log\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon A)-\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left[\log\left(1+\epsilon\lambda_{i}\right)-\epsilon\lambda_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\epsilon\lambda_{i}}{1+s\epsilon\lambda_{i}}\mathrm{~d}s-\epsilon\lambda_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1+s\epsilon\lambda_{i}}\mathrm{~d}s,

while

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{\left\|A\right\|_{F}}{1-\epsilon\varrho(A)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1-\epsilon\max_{i}|\lambda_{i}|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{s\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1+s\epsilon\lambda_{i}}\mathrm{~d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\frac{\epsilon^{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1+\epsilon\max_{i}|\lambda_{i}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{\left\|A\right\|_{F}}{1+\epsilon\varrho(A)},

so we have

-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{\left\|A\right\|_{F}}{1-\epsilon\varrho(A)}\leq\log\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon A)-\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(A)\leq-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{\left\|A\right\|_{F}}{1+\epsilon\varrho(A)}.

(70)

Taking
A=B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}
into
Equation

70
and combine it with
Equation

69
, we get

\displaystyle\log|\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon B)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{det}\left(I+\epsilon\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\frac{\left\|B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}}{1-\epsilon\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{9\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{1-\epsilon\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)}+2{\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{F}}\right),

similarly

\log|\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon B)|\leq\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{9\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{1+\epsilon\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)}+2{\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{F}}\right)

where we used the fact that
\operatorname{tr}(B)=\operatorname{tr}\left(B^{\top}\right)
,
\left\|BB^{\top}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|B\right\|_{F}^{2}
and
\left\|B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right\|_{F}\leq\|B\|_{F}+\left\|B^{\top}\right\|_{F}+\epsilon\left\|BB^{\top}\right\|_{F}=3\|B\|_{F}
(since
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{\left\|B\right\|_{F}}
). Finally we use inequality
\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)\leq\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}\right)+\epsilon\varrho\left(BB^{\top}\right)\leq\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}\right)+\sqrt{\varrho\left(BB^{\top}\right)}
and

\displaystyle\varrho(B+B^{\top})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(BB+BB^{\top}+B^{\top}B+B^{\top}B^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(BB)+\operatorname{tr}(B^{\top}B^{\top})+2\operatorname{tr}(BB^{\top})

\displaystyle\leq 4\operatorname{tr}(BB^{\top})\qquad\qquad//\text{since}~\operatorname{tr}(BB)\leq\operatorname{tr}(BB^{\top})

\displaystyle=4\left\|B\right\|_{F}^{2}

and
\varrho(BB^{\top})\leq\left\|B\right\|_{F}^{2}

\varrho\left(B+B^{\top}+\epsilon BB^{\top}\right)\leq 3\left\|B\right\|_{F}.

(71)

Combining all of these, we finally get

\displaystyle\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{9\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{1-3\epsilon\left\|B\right\|_{F}}+2{\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{F}}\right)

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\log|\operatorname{det}(I+\epsilon B)|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(B)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{9\|B\|_{F}^{2}}{1+3\epsilon\left\|B\right\|_{F}}+2{\left\|B\right\|^{2}_{F}}\right).
