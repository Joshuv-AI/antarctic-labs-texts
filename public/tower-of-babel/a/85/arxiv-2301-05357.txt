An Inexact Feasible Quantum Interior Point Method for Linearly Constrained Quadratic Optimization
Zeguan Wu, Mohammadhossein Mohammadisiahroudi, Brandon Augustino, Xiu Yang, Tamás Terlaky *

Abstract
Quantum linear system algorithms (QLSAs) have the potential to speed up algorithms that rely on solving linear systems. Interior Point Methods (IPMs) yield a fundamental family of polynomial-time algorithms for solving optimization problems. IPMs solve a Newton linear system at each iteration to find the search direction, and thus QLSAs can potentially speed up IPMs. Due to the noise in contemporary quantum computers, such quantum-assisted IPM (QIPM) only allows an inexact solution for the Newton linear system. Typically, an inexact search direction leads to an infeasible solution. In our work, we propose an Inexact-Feasible QIPM (IF-QIPM) and show its advantage in solving linearly constrained quadratic optimization problems. We also apply the algorithm to ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm soft margin support vector machine (SVM) problems and obtain the best complexity regarding dependence on dimension. This complexity bound is better than any existing classical or quantum algorithm that produces a classical solution.

Introduction

Linearly constrained quadratic optimization (LCQO) is defined as optimizing a convex quadratic objective function over a set of linear constraints. This problem reduces to linear optimization when the the quadratic objective function is linear. LCQO has rich theory, algorithms, and applications. Many machine learning problems are LCQO problems, including variants of least square problems and variants of support vector machine problems
Nocedal and Wright 1999
;
Boser et al. 1992
. Some important optimization algorithms also have LCQO subproblems, e.g. sequential quadratic programming
Nocedal and Wright 1999
.

The modern age of IPMs launched by Karmarkar’s invention of the projective method for linear optimization (LO). Since then, a lot of variants of IPMs have been studied for not only LO problems but also for nonlinear optimization problems, including LCQO problems
Roos et al. 1997
;
Pólik and Terlaky 2010
.

Contemporary IPMs look for the optimal solution by moving in a neighbourhood of the central path. IPMs can be divided into two classes: feasible or infeasible. Feasible IPMs start with a feasible solution and keep feasibility; infeasible IPMs start with an infeasible interior solution and so do not require a feasible solution to start with. For LCQO problems with
n
variables, some feasible IPMs produce an
\epsilon
-approximate solution in at most
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon))
IPM iterations, while infeasible IPMs require
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
IPM iterations to generate an
\epsilon
-approximate solution
Gondzio 2013
;
Lu et al. 2006
.

At each IPM iteration a linear system needs to be solved to obtain the search direction, called the Newton direction. Such a Newton linear system is traditionally in the form of augmented system or the normal equation system. Classically these linear systems can be solved exactly using Bunch-Parlett factoriztion if the matrices in the systems are symmetric indefinite
Bunch and Parlett 1971
, or Cholesky factorization if the matrices are symmetric positive definite. The complexity of solving the linear systems is
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. The linear systems can also be solved inexactly using some inexact methods, e.g., Krylov subspace methods. Such inexact methods might take less iterations if the desired accuracy of the solutions to the linear systems is not high. But such inaccuracy of the solutions to the linear systems, i.e., inaccuracy of the search directions, might result in infeasibility of the solutions generated by IPMs. To maintain feasibility of solutions,
Mohammadisiahroudi et al. 2021
introduces the so-called orthogonal subspace system (OSS) for LO problems. A feasible solution can be recovered from an inexact solution to OSS. We extend their OSS for LO prolems to LCQO problems and provide an efficient method to construct the OSS. With the OSS, we can obtain an inexact feasible IPM – solving for search direction inexactly but maintaining the feasibility of solution throughout the process of our IPM. The feasibility of solution gives better IPM iteration complexity and the bottleneck becomes solving the linear system, OSS.

With the development of quantum technology, many quantum-assisted algorithms have been proposed for many optimization problems. Following the invention of quantum algorithms for solving linear systems of equations
Harrow et al. 2009
, many researchers are encouraged to study whether QLSAs would yield quantum speedups in classical algorithms. In particular, QIPMs have been proposed for for LO problems
Kerenidis and Prakash 2020
;
Mohammadisiahroudi et al. 2022
and semidefinite optimization problems
Augustino et al. 2021
that utilize QLSAs to solve the Newton linear system that arises in each iteration of IPMs. Similar ideas have also been applied to accelerate the solution of some machine learning applications, such as linear regression
Schuld et al. 2016
and the support vector machine training problem
Kerenidis et al. 2021
. However, linearly constrained quadratic optimization problems, which are fundamental to both optimization and machine learning, have not been formally studied in the quantum literature yet.

The remaining part of this paper is organized as follow: in Section 2, we introduce IPMs for LCQO and the OSS system; in Section 3, we discuss how to use quantum algorithms to find the Newton directions and analyze the complexity of our IF-QIPM; in Section 4, we apply our IF-QIPM to support vector machine problem. Discussions are provided in Section 5, and some technical proofs are moved to the Appendix.

2.2
IPMs for LCQO

In this work, LCQO is defined as follow.

{Definition}
[LCQO Problem]
For vectors
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
\rank(A)=m\leq n

(\text{P})\quad\begin{aligned} \min\ c^{T}&x+\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Qx,\\
{\rm s.t.}\;\;Ax&=b,\\
x&\geq 0,\end{aligned}\qquad\qquad(\text{D})\quad\begin{aligned} \max\ b^{T}y-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Q&x,\ \ \\
{\rm s.t.}\;\;A^{T}y+s-Qx&=c,\\
s&\geq 0,\end{aligned}

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of primal variables, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are vectors of the dual variables.
Problem
(P)
is called the primal problem and
(D)
is called the dual problem.

The full-row-rankness of matrix

implies that there is no all-zero row in matrix
A

{Assumption}

Matrix
A
has no all-zero columns.

{Remark}

When matrix

\displaystyle\min\ \begin{bmatrix}c\\

x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}Q&0_{n\times 1}\\
0_{1\times n}&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x\\
x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\;\;\begin{bmatrix}A_{\cdot 1}&\cdots&A_{\cdot(n-1)}&0_{m\times 1}&0_{m\times 1}\\

x_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}b\\
0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle x\geq 0,\ x_{n+1}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

The new problem is equivalent to the original one. The new problem is still a LCQO problem and has fewer all-zero columns than the original problem. So we can repeat the procedure to eliminate all the all-zero columns. In the worst case, we will get a new LCQO problem satisfying Assumption
2.2
with

{Assumption}

There exists a solution
(x,\ y,\ s)

Ax=b,\ x>0,\ A^{T}y+s-Qx=c,\text{ and }s>0.

The set of

\mathcal{PD}\coloneqq\left\{(x,\ y,\ s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax=b,\ A^{T}y+s-Qx=c,\ (x,s)\geq 0\right\}

can be defined as

\mathcal{PD}^{0}\coloneqq\left\{(x,\ y,\ s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:Ax=b,\ A^{T}y+s-Qx=c,\ (x,s)>0\right\}.

According to the strong duality, the set of optimal solutions can be defined as

\mathcal{PD}^{\ast}\coloneqq\left\{(x,\ y,\ s)\in\mathcal{PD}:xs=0\right\},

where
xs
denotes the Hadamard, i.e., componentwise product of
x
and
s
. Let
\epsilon>0
, then the set of
\epsilon
-approximate solutions to Problem (
2.2
) can be defined as

\mathcal{PD}_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\left\{(x,\ y,\ s)\in\mathcal{PD}:x^{T}s\leq n\epsilon\right\}.

(2)

Let
X
and
S
be diagonal matrices of
x
and
s
, respectively. Under Assumption
2.2
, for all
\mu>0
, the perturbed optimality conditions

\displaystyle Ax

\displaystyle=b,

(3)

\displaystyle A^{T}y+s-Qx

\displaystyle=c,

\displaystyle XSe

\displaystyle=\mu e,

\displaystyle(x,s)

\displaystyle\geq 0

have a unique solution
(x(\mu),\ y(\mu),\ s(\mu))
that defines the primal and dual central path

\mathcal{CP}\coloneqq\left\{(x,y,s)\in\mathcal{PD}^{0}|x_{i}s_{i}=\mu\text{ for }i\in\{1,\dots,n\};\text{ for }\mu>0\right\}.

IPMs apply Newton’s method to solve system (
3
). At each iteration of infeasible IPMs, a candidate solution to the primal-dual LCQO pair in (
1
) is updated by solving the following linear system to find the Newton direction:

\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
-Q&A^{T}&I\\
S&0&X\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta x\\
\Delta y\\
\Delta s\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{p}\\
r_{d}\\
r_{c}\end{bmatrix},

(4)

where
(r_{p},r_{d},r_{c})
are residuals defined as

\displaystyle r_{p}

\displaystyle=b-Ax

\displaystyle r_{d}

\displaystyle=c-A^{T}y-s

\displaystyle r_{c}

\displaystyle=\sigma\mu e-XSe,

where
\sigma\in(0,1)
is the barrier reduction parameter. If
r_{p}=0
and

, then the solutions
(x,\ y,\ s)
are primal and dual feasible. Alternatively, we can also define residuals in different ways as we will show later. Once the Newton direction is found, one can move along the direction but has to stay in a neighbourhood of the central path, which is defined at the end of this section.

When the linear system (
4
) is solved inexactly, that actually leads to inexact infeasible IPMs. Many researchers have analyzed the performance of inexact infeasible IPMs (II-IPMs). For LCQO problems,
Lu et al. 2006
propose an II-IPM using an iterative method to solve the Newton systems and obtain
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon}))
IPM iteration complexity. Here IPM iteration complexity does not include the complexity contributed by linear system solvers. However, it is known that feasible IPMs for LCQO problems can achieve
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon}))
IPM iteration complexity
Kojima et al. 1989
;
Monteiro and Adler 1989
;
Goldfarb and Liu 1990
. In
Gondzio 2013
, the author provides a general inexact feasible IPM for LCQO problems but has not discussed how to maintain feasibility when inexact linear system solvers are used. In this work, we will fill the gap by using a method inspired by some QIPM results
Mohammadisiahroudi et al. 2021
;
Augustino et al. 2021
as we shall discuss later.

In this paper, we consider the following neighborhood of the central path

\mathcal{N}_{2}(\theta)\coloneqq\left\{(x,y,s)\in\mathcal{PD}^{0}|\|XSe-\mu e\|_{2}\leq\theta\mu\right\},

(5)

where
\theta\in(0,1)
.

2.3
Orthogonal Subspaces System

Assuming that
(x,\ y,\ s)\in\mathcal{PD}^{0}
, to maintain the feasibility of the primal and dual variables, the first two linear equations in system (
4
) need to be solved with
r_{p}=0
and
r_{d}=0
exactly, which can be guaranteed if
\Delta x
lies in the null space of
A
, denoted as
\Null(A)
, and
\Delta s=Q\Delta x-A^{T}\Delta y
. Accordingly, we can rewrite system (
4
) if we represent
\Delta x
by a basis of
\Null(A)
. To do so, we can partition matrix
A
to
A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{B}&A_{N}\end{bmatrix}
, where
A_{B}
is a basis of
A
. Then we construct the following matrix

V=\begin{bmatrix}A_{B}^{-1}A_{N}\\
-I\end{bmatrix}.

Matrix
V
has full column rank and satisfies
AV=0
, i.e., the columns of
V
span the null space of
A
. Let
\Delta x=V\lambda
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n-m}
is the unknown coefficient vector for
\Delta x
. Subsequently, we can rewrite system (
4
) by substituting
\Delta x
and
\Delta s
in the third equation as

\displaystyle SV\lambda+X\left(QV\lambda-A^{T}\Delta y\right)=r_{c}\Leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}SV+XQV&-XA^{T}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\\
\Delta y\end{bmatrix}=r_{c}.

(6)

A similar system was proposed and called ”Orthogonal Subspaces System” (OSS) in
Augustino et al. 2021
;
Mohammadisiahroudi et al. 2021
and we use the same name in this work. The matrix in the OSS system (
6
) is of size
n\times n
, and it is nonsingular. Even if the OSS system is solved inexactly, primal and dual feasibility is preserved by computing
\Delta x=V\lambda
and
\Delta s=QV\lambda-A^{T}\Delta y
. Thus, we can conclude that residual will only show up in the third equation of (
4
), i.e.,
r_{p}=0
and
r_{d}=0
. This nice property of the OSS system brings much convenience in the analysis of the proposed inexact IPM, and allows to prove the to-date best iteration complexity.

3
Inexact Feasible IPM with QLSAs

In this section, we propose our IF-QIPM for LCQO problems. We start with the IF-IPM structure introduced by
Gondzio 2013
and describe how to convert it into an IF-QIPM. Then we analyze the construction of the OSS system, and finally, we analyze the complexity for our IF-QIPM.

3.1
IF-IPM for LCQO

In
Gondzio 2013
, the author studies a general conceptual form IF-IPM for QCLO problems by assuming the feasibility of primal and dual variables, which induces the following system

\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
-Q&A^{T}&I\\
S&0&X\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta x\\
\Delta y\\
\Delta s\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
r_{c}\end{bmatrix},

(7)

where
r_{c}=\sigma\mu e-XSe
with
\sigma\in(0,1)
being the reduction factor of the central path parameter
\mu
, i.e.,
\mu^{new}=\sigma\mu
. When system (
7
) is solved with
r_{c}=\sigma\mu e-XSe
inexactly yielding an error
r

\|r\|_{2}\leq\delta\|r_{c}\|_{2}
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
, then the inexact IPM produces an
\epsilon
-approximate solution to Problem (
2.2
) in
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations. The author of
Gondzio 2013
does not specify how to solve system (
7
) inexactly, how to preserve primal and dual feasibility, and how to satisfy the convergence conditions described in
Gondzio 2013
. Specifically, the convergence conditions are posed on the right-hand-side and the inexactness error of the system (
7
).

Now we present a general procedure how to solve system (
7
) inexactly, while the inexactness error occurs only in the third equation of system (
7
). Let
(\lambda,\Delta y)
be an inexact solution for system (
6
) and
r
be the corresponding inexactness error, so we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}SV+XQV&-XA^{T}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\\
\Delta y\end{bmatrix}=r_{c}+r.

The corresponding Newton step

\displaystyle\Delta x

\displaystyle=V\lambda

\displaystyle\Delta s

\displaystyle=Q\Delta x-A^{T}\Delta y

satisfies

\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
-Q&A^{T}&I\\
S&0&X\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\Delta x\\
\Delta y\\
\Delta s\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
r_{c}+r\end{bmatrix}.

Recall that once
(\lambda,\Delta y)
is determined, then
(\Delta x,\Delta s)
is also determined. An interesting property is that, if
(\lambda,\Delta y)
and
(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta s)
can be deduced from each other, then the OSS system and system (
7
) yield the same error term
r
. Hence the convergence conditions built upon system (
7
) can be directly examined using the residual
r_{c}
and error
r
of the OSS system. Let
\epsilon_{OSS}
be the target accuracy of the OSS system (
6
), i.e.,

\|(\lambda-\lambda^{\ast},\ \Delta y-\Delta y^{\ast}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{OSS},

where
(\lambda^{\ast},\ \Delta y^{\ast})
is the accurate solution. To make the IF-IPM converge, according to

, we need

\displaystyle\|r\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}SV+XQV&-XA^{T}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\\
\Delta y\end{bmatrix}-r_{c}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\begin{bmatrix}SV+XQV&-XA^{T}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}\epsilon_{OSS}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\|r_{c}\|_{2}.

So

\displaystyle\epsilon_{OSS}\leq\delta\frac{\|r_{c}\|_{2}}{\left\|\begin{bmatrix}SV+XQV&-XA^{T}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}}

is sufficient for the IF-IPM to converge. We present the IF-IPM in Algorithm

3.2.1
Bound for
\omega^{k}/\|M^{k}\|_{F}

In this section, all the quantities we consider are from the
k^{th}
iteration. For simplicity, we ignore superscript
k
in this section unless we need it. Using the property of trace, we have

\displaystyle\|M\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\trace(M^{T}M)

\displaystyle=\trace\left((SV+XQV)(SV+XQV)^{T}+XA^{T}AX\right)

\displaystyle=\trace\left((SV+XQV)(SV+XQV)^{T}\right)+\trace\left(XA^{T}AX\right)

\displaystyle=\trace\left(SVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(XQVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(SVV^{T}QX\right)+\trace\left(XQVV^{T}QX\right)+\trace\left(XA^{T}AX\right).

For the non-symmetric term, due to cyclic invariant property of trace, we have

\trace\left(XQVV^{T}S\right)=\trace\left(SXQVV^{T}\right).

Recall the central path neighbourhood we defined in (
5
), we define a matrix
E
such that

E=\frac{1}{\mu\theta}(XS-\mu I).

(14)

It is obvious that
E
is a diagonal matrix and satisfies

\|Ee\|_{2}<1,

which leads to

|\trace(E)|\leq\|Ee\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\|E\|_{F}=\sqrt{n}\|Ee\|_{2}<\sqrt{n}\text{ and }I-E\succ 0\text{ and }I+E\succ 0.

With this, we can have

\displaystyle\trace\left(XQVV^{T}S\right)

\displaystyle=\trace\left(SXQVV^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\trace\left((\theta\mu E+\mu I)QVV^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\trace\left(\theta\mu EQVV^{T}\right)+\trace\left(\mu QVV^{T}\right).

For the second term, we know
Q
and
V^{T}QV
are both positive semidefinite. So we can have

\trace\left(QVV^{T}\right)=\trace\left(V^{T}QV\right)\geq 0

because of the cyclic invariant property of trace. According to the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\trace\left(EQVV^{T}\right)^{2}\leq\|E\|_{F}^{2}\|QVV^{T}\|_{F}^{2}.

So we have

\trace\left(EQVV^{T}\right)\geq-\|QVV^{T}\|_{F}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\trace\left(XQVV^{T}S\right)

\displaystyle=\trace\left(\theta\mu EQVV^{T}\right)+\trace\left(\mu QVV^{T}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mu\left(\trace\left(QVV^{T}\right)-\theta\|QVV^{T}\|_{F}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-\theta\mu\|QVV^{T}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\mu}{4},

where the last inequality holds due to condition (
11
). So we can bound
\|M\|_{F}
by

\displaystyle\|M\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\trace\left(SVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(XQVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(SVV^{T}QX\right)+\trace\left(XQVV^{T}QX\right)+\trace\left(XA^{T}AX\right)

\displaystyle\geq\trace\left(SVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(XQVV^{T}QX\right)+\trace\left(XA^{T}AX\right)-\frac{\mu}{2}.

Since
XQVV^{T}QX\succeq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|M\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\trace\left(SVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(XA^{T}AX\right)-\frac{\mu}{2}.

Since
X
and
S
are both positive diagonal matrices, we have

\displaystyle\|M\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\trace\left(SVV^{T}S\right)+\trace\left(XA^{T}AX\right)-\frac{\mu}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}(VV^{T})_{ii}+\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}(A^{T}A)_{ii}-\frac{\mu}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\omega^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}.

As we said in the very beginning of this section, at each iteration
\omega
is indeed
\omega^{k}
but the superscript is ignored here. Now we are going to find a bound for
\mu
so we can further bound
\|M\|_{F}^{2}
. Since
\omega
is the upper bound for the magnitude of the primal and dual slack variables, we have

\displaystyle\omega^{2}

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}s_{i}.

Recall the definition of matrix
E
, see (
14
). So we have

\displaystyle\omega^{2}

\displaystyle\geq x_{i}s_{i}=\mu+\theta\mu E_{ii}\geq\mu-\theta\mu=(1-\theta)\mu.

So

\displaystyle\|M\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\omega^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}\geq\omega^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{1-\theta}\geq\omega^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{1-1/3}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{4},

where the last inequality follows from the bound for
\theta
, see (
11

\displaystyle\frac{\omega}{\|M\|_{F}}\leq 2=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

4
Application in Support Vector Machine Problems

In this section, we discuss how to use our IF-QIPM to solve SVM problems. We show that our algorithm can solve
l_{1}
-norm soft margin SVM problems with best complexity compared with any existing classical or quantum algorithms.

The ordinary SVM problem works on a linearly separable dataset, in which the data points have binary labels. The ordinary SVM aims to find a hyperplane correctly separating the data points with maximum margin. However, in practice the data points are not necessarily linearly separable. To allow mislabelling, the concept of soft margin SVM was introduced in
Cortes and Vapnik 1995
. Let
\{(\phi_{i},\zeta_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\{-1,+1\}|i=1,\dots,n\}
be the set of data points,
\Phi
be a matrix with
i
th column being
\phi_{i}
, and
Z
be a diagonal matrix with
i
th diagonal element being
\zeta_{i}
. The SVM problem with
l_{1}
-norm soft margin can be formulated as below.

\displaystyle\min_{(\xi,w,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2}+C\|\xi\|_{1}

(18)

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}\left(\langle w,\phi_{i}\rangle+t\right)\geq 1-\xi_{i},\ i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle\xi_{i}\geq 0,\ i=1,\dots,n.

Here
(w,t)
determines a hyperplane and
C
is a penalty parameter. In
Kerenidis et al. 2021
, the authors rewrote the SVM problem as a second order conic optimization (SOCO) problem and use the quantum algorithm they proposed to solve the resulting SOCO problem. They claim the complexity of their algorithm has
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
dependence on the dimension, which is better than any classical algorithm. However, the algorithm in
Kerenidis et al. 2021
is invalid. Their algorithm is an Inexact Infeasible-QIPM (II-QIPM) while they used the IPM complexity for Feasible-QIPM, which ignores at least
\mathcal{O}(n^{1.5})
dependence on
n
. They also missed the symmetrization of the Newton step, which is necessary for SOCO problems and makes their Newton step invalid.

Aside from
Kerenidis et al. 2021
, some pure quantum algorithms for SVM problems are also proposed. In
Rebentrost et al. 2014
, the authors propose a pure quantum algorithm for SVM problems. They claim the complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\kappa_{\text{eff}}^{3}\epsilon^{-3}\log(mn))
, where
\kappa_{\text{eff}}
is the condition number of a matrix involving the kernel matrix and
\epsilon
is the accuracy. In the worst case,
\kappa_{\text{eff}}=\mathcal{O}(m)
. Their complexity is worse than ours regarding the dependence of dimension and accuracy. In addition, their algorithm does not provide classical solutions. Namely, the solution is in the quantum machine and we can not read or use it in a classical computer. However, our algorithm produces a classical solution.

To convert the problem into standard form LCQO, we introduce
(w_{+},w_{-})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
,
(t_{+},t_{-})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a slack variable
\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Then we can get the following formulation

\displaystyle\min_{w_{+},w_{-},t_{+},t_{-},\xi,\rho}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|w_{+}-w_{-}\|_{2}^{2}+C\|\xi\|_{1}

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}\left(\left<w_{+}-w_{-},\phi_{i}\right>+t_{+}-t_{-}\right)+\xi_{i}-\rho_{i}=1,\ i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle(\xi,w_{+},w_{-},t_{+},t_{-},\rho)\geq 0.

It is a standard form LCQO problem with nonnegative variables
(w_{+},w_{-},t_{+},t_{-},\xi,\rho)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and parameters

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2m+2}\\
Ce_{n}\\
0_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m\times m}&-I_{m\times m}&0_{m\times(2+2n)}\\
-I_{m\times m}&I_{m\times m}&0_{m\times(2+2n)}\\
0_{(2+2n)\times m}&0_{(2+2n)\times m}&0_{(2+2n)\times(2+2n)}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Z\Phi^{T}&-Z\Phi^{T}&Z&-Z&I_{n\times n}&-I_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=e.

So we can use the proposed IF-QIPM for LCQO problems to solve the
\ell_{1}
-norm soft margin SVM problems and get an
\epsilon
-approximate solution with complexity

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{n,\bar{\omega},\frac{1}{\epsilon}}\left(n^{1.5}\left(\frac{\bar{\omega}^{2}}{\epsilon}+\sigma_{\max}(Q)\right)\kappa_{VAQ}+n^{2.5}\right).

This dependence on dimension is better than any existing quantum or classical algorithm.

5
Discussion

In this work, we present an IF-QIPM for LCQO problems by combining the IF-IPM framework proposed in
Gondzio 2013
and the OSS system introduced in
Mohammadisiahroudi et al. 2021
. Our algorithm has
n^{1.5}
dependence on
n
, which is better than any existing algorithms for LCQO problems. The dependence on the accuracy is polynomial, which is worse than classic IPMs. Iterative refinement method might help improve the dependence on the accuracy but that could be another work.

Block-encoding of the OSS system

In this section, we ignore the superscript
k
for simplicity. As described in Eq. (
9
), we first block encode each of the matrices involved in (
10
). With
V,\ A,\ S
and
X
given and are stored in a quantum accessible data structure (we ignore the complexity to store the classical information into the quantum machine). For the first matrix

M_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{(n-m)\times n}&V^{T}&0_{(n-m)\times n}\\
0_{m\times n}&0_{m\times n}&-A\end{bmatrix},

a

\left(\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}},\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),\epsilon_{1}\right)

-block-encoding of
M_{1}
can be implemented according to Lemma 50 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
efficiently.

The second matrix

M_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
S&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}

is both 1-row-sparse and 1-column-sparse. By the definition of
\omega
, each element of
M_{2}/\omega
has absolute value at most 1. According to Lemma 48 in
Gilyén et al. 2018
, a

\left(1,\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),\epsilon_{2}\right)

-block-encoding of
M_{2}/\omega
can be implemented efficiently.

The third matrix

M_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&Q&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&I_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}

can be decomposed into

M_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&Q&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}&I_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}.

Then we can block-encode the two matrices first, and then apply linear combination to obtain
M_{3}
. In fact, a

\left(\|Q\|_{F},\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),\epsilon_{3}\right)

-block-encoding of the left matrix can be implemented according to Lemma 50 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
efficiently
and a

\displaystyle\left(1,\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),\epsilon_{3}\right)

-block-encoding of the right matrix can be implemented efficiently according to Lemma 48 in
Gilyén et al. 2018
. With the state-preparation cost of the linear combination coefficient vector
(1,1)
neglected, a

\left(\|Q\|_{F}+1,\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),(\|Q\|_{F}+1)\epsilon_{3}\right)

-block-encoding of
M_{3}
can be implemented efficiently according to Lemma 52 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
.

The fourth matrix

M_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\\
X&0_{n\times n}\\
X&0_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix}

is 1-row-sparse and 2-column-sparse. After being scaled by
\frac{1}{\omega}
, each element of
M_{4}/\omega
has absolute value at most 1. According to Lemma 48 in
Gilyén et al. 2018
, a

\left(\sqrt{2},\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),\epsilon_{4}\right)

-block-encoding of
M_{4}/\omega
can be implemented efficiently.

For the matrix multiplication
M_{3}M_{4}/\omega
, a

\left(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2},\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),(\|Q\|_{F}+1)(\sqrt{2}\epsilon_{3}+\epsilon_{4})\right)

-block-encoding can be implemented efficiently according to Lemma 53 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
.

For the linear combination
M_{2}/\omega+M_{3}M_{4}/\omega
, the cost for the state-preparation of the coefficient vector
(1,1)
is negligible and thus a

\left(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1,\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1)(\epsilon_{3}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\epsilon_{4})\right)

-block-encoding can be implemented efficiently according to Lemma 52 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
.

For the matrix multiplication of
M_{1}(M_{2}/\omega+M_{3}M_{4}/\omega)
, a

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1)(\epsilon_{3}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\epsilon_{4})+(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1)\epsilon_{1}\Bigg)

-block-encoding can be implemented efficiently according to Lemma 53 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
.

Finally, considering that the complexity of state-preparation of the vector

(\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{2}\|M\|_{F}},\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{2}\|M\|_{F}})

can be neglected, a

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}\sqrt{2}\omega}{\|M\|_{F}}(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\poly\log(n)),

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}\sqrt{2}\omega}{\|M\|_{F}}(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1)^{2}\left(\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}(\epsilon_{3}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\epsilon_{4})+\epsilon_{1}\right)\Bigg)

-block-encoding of the coefficient matrix of system (
8
) can be implemented efficiently according to Lemma 52 from
Gilyén et al. 2018
.
We can choose

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{QLSA}}{\kappa_{M}^{3}}\frac{1}{2\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{2\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{4}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\epsilon_{2},

where
\mathcal{K}
depends on the initial data

\mathcal{K}=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\|V\|_{F}^{2}+\|A\|_{F}^{2}}(\sqrt{2}\|Q\|_{F}+\sqrt{2}+1)^{2}.

Now, considering that the complexity for all the block-encoding algorithms we have used so far have poly-logarithmic dependence on the dimension and accuracy, and that, for
i=1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\poly\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{i}})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\poly\log(\kappa_{M})\right),

the complexity for block-encoding will be dominated by the complexity for QLSA because QLSA has linear dependence on
\kappa_{M}
. So we can ignore the complexity of block-encoding.

Appendix B
Spectral Analysis for Matrix
\Psi

In this section, we provide the spectral analysis for the matrix

\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}S^{2}+2\mu Q&-\mu\theta E\\
-\mu\theta E&X^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(19)

Just like in the previous section, for simplicity, we ignore the superscript
k
. We can do the following decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}S^{2}+2\mu Q&-\mu\theta E\\
-\mu\theta E&X^{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}S^{2}&-\mu\theta E\\
-\mu\theta E&X^{2}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}2\mu Q&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Let us use the following notation

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}S^{2}&-\mu\theta E\\
-\mu\theta E&X^{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}2\mu Q&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

It can be proven that
\Psi_{1}
is positive definite. The majority of the proof of this conclusion comes from the paper
Mohammadisiahroudi et al. 2021
. For the reader’s convenience, we provide the complete proof here.

Matrix
\Psi_{1}
is a block diagonal matrix, with all the four blocks being diagonal matrices. So we can easily compute the eigenvalues using the characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle\det(\Psi_{1}-qI)

\displaystyle=\det\Big(\big(X^{2}-qI\big)\big(S^{2}-qI\big)-\theta^{2}\mu^{2}E^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big(x_{i}^{2}-q\big)\big(s_{i}^{2}-q\big)-\theta^{2}\mu^{2}E_{ii}^{2}\Big).

Clearly,
\det(\Psi_{1}-qI)=0
gives
n
quadratic equations and each quadratic equation gives two eigenvalues. The two eigenvalues from the
i
th quadratic equation are

q_{i+}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4x_{i}^{2}s_{i}^{2}+4\theta^{2}\mu^{2}E_{ii}^{2}}\Bigg)

and

q_{i-}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4x_{i}^{2}s_{i}^{2}+4\theta^{2}\mu^{2}E_{ii}^{2}}\Bigg).

Recalling the definition of
E
in (
14
), we can write

\displaystyle q_{i-}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4x_{i}^{2}s_{i}^{2}+4(x_{i}s_{i}-\mu)^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}+4(x_{i}s_{i}-\mu+x_{i}s_{i})(x_{i}s_{i}-\mu-x_{i}s_{i})}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4\mu(2x_{i}s_{i}-\mu)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4\mu(2\theta\mu E_{ii}+\mu)}\Bigg).

One can verify that the square root always exists because

\displaystyle(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4\mu(2x_{i}s_{i}-\mu)

\displaystyle\geq 4(x_{i}s_{i})^{2}-4\mu(2x_{i}s_{i})+4\mu^{2}

\displaystyle=4(x_{i}s_{i}-\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

With
\theta\in\left(0,\ \min\left\{\frac{1}{3\sqrt{n}},\ \frac{1}{4\|QVV^{T}\|_{F}+1}\right\}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle q_{i-}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4\mu(2\theta\mu E_{ii}+\mu)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4\mu(-2\mu\frac{1}{3\sqrt{n}}+\mu)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})-\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-\frac{4}{3}\mu^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\frac{4}{3}\mu^{2}}{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-\frac{4}{3}\mu^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\frac{4}{3}\mu^{2}}{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu^{2}}{3(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})}

\displaystyle>0.

This means that matrix
\Psi_{1}
is positive definite and its eigenvalues coincide with its singular values because
\Psi_{1}
is also real and symmetric. Analogously, we have

\displaystyle q_{i+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+\sqrt{(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})^{2}-4\mu(2\theta\mu E_{ii}+\mu)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+2\mu\sqrt{(2\theta E_{ii}+1)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg((x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+2\mu\sqrt{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})+\sqrt{2}\mu.

So the condition number of
\Psi
satisfies

\displaystyle\kappa(\Psi)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\Psi_{1})+\sigma_{\max}(\Psi_{2})}{\sigma_{\min}(\Psi_{1})+\sigma_{\min}(\Psi_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\max_{i}q_{i+}+\sigma_{\max}(\Psi_{2})}{\min_{j}q_{j-}+\sigma_{\min}(\Psi_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{i}\{x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2}\}+\sqrt{2}\mu+2\mu\sigma_{\max}(Q)}{\min_{j}\frac{\mu^{2}}{3(x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\max_{i}\{x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2}\}\left(\max_{i}\{x_{i}^{2}+s_{i}^{2}\}+\sqrt{2}\mu+2\mu\sigma_{\max}(Q)\right)}{\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\omega^{2}\left(\omega^{2}+\sqrt{2}\mu+2\mu\sigma_{\max}(Q)\right)}{\mu^{2}},

where the last inequality comes from the definition of
\omega
. Since
\omega^{2}\geq x_{i}s_{i}\geq(1-\theta)\mu
, we have

\kappa(\Psi)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}(\omega^{2}+\mu\sigma_{\max}(Q))}{\mu^{2}}\right).

Using Lemma
3.2.2
, we can also bound the condition number of matrix
M
by

\displaystyle\kappa_{M}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\kappa(M^{T}M)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\kappa(\Psi)}\kappa_{VAQ}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\omega^{2}+\mu\sigma_{\max}(Q))}{\mu}\kappa_{VAQ}\right).
