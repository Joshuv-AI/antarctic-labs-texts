Extraction of bare Form Factors for B s → K ​ ℓ ​ ν \mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathrm{K}\ell\nu Decays in non-perturbative HQET
Rainer Sommer

Abstract
We discuss the extraction of the ground state ⟨ K ⁡ ( 𝐩 ) | V μ ​ ( 0 ) | B s ​ ( 𝟎 ) ⟩ \langle{\rm K}({\bf p})|V_{\mu}(0)|{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}({\bf 0})\rangle matrix elements from Euclidean lattice correlation functions. The emphasis is on the elimination of excited state contributions. Two typical gauge-field ensembles with lattice spacings 0.075 , 0.05 0.075,\;0.05 fm and pion masses 330 , 270 330,\;270 MeV are used from the O( a a )-improved CLS 2-flavour simulations and the final state momentum is | 𝐩 | = 0.5 ​ GeV |{\bf p}|=0.5\,{\rm GeV} . The b-quark is treated in HQET including the 1 / m b 1/m_{\mathrm{b}} corrections. Fits to two-point and three-point correlation functions and suitable ratios including summed ratios are used, yielding consistent results with precision of around 2% which is not limited by the 1 / m b 1/m_{\mathrm{b}} corrections but by the dominating static form factors. Excited state contributions are under reasonable control but are the bottleneck towards precision. We do not yet include a specific investigation of multi-hadron contaminations, a gap in the literature which ought to be filled soon.

Introduction

Decays of b-quarks in the form
\mathrm{B}\to\pi\ell\nu
and
\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{s}}\to\mathrm{K}\ell\nu
are very relevant in
constraining the Standard Model of particle physics and hence also in the search
for deviations from it. Lattice QCD is the method of choice for predicting
the necessary form factors
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. While
computations are progresssing significantly, there are still few groups carrying them out. Crosschecks by independent (lattice) methods are largely absent.
In Ref.
[
10
]
we have started an investigation which takes significantly different avenues in almost every choice that can be made.
We use (improved) Wilson fermions,
treat the b-quark in non-perturbative HQET and
compute three-point functions for all time separations
rather than fixing its total time-span (called
\tau={t_{\rm K}}+{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}
later)
to one or few values.
We opt for non-perturbative HQET because a complete and practical method is known
to non-perturbatively renormalize the theory and match it to QCD
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. When completed, this will provide an independent
crosscheck.

Once these choices are made, one needs to carry out the basic steps
as written in Ref.
[
10
]
:

a)

obtain the
ground state
matrix elements

\langle{\rm K}|V^{\mu}(0)|{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\rangle
that mediate the transition,

b)

renormalize the currents (and thus matrix elements)
and, if an effective theory is used, relate them to QCD (“matching”),

c)

take the continuum limit of the matrix elements,

d)

extrapolate to the quark masses realized in Nature,

e)

map out the
q^{2}
dependence.

In the previous work we saw that the continuum limit
is smooth in the static approximation, which is expected to yield
the by far dominating part of the full result. In this paper we discuss the step a) in detail, including the
1/m
terms.
It is not at all an easy enterprise. As discussed at length below,
the issue is that ground state matrix elements are obtained at large
Euclidean time separations. At time separations of around
\frac{1}{2}
fm
— a typical QCD length scale — the low-lying states start dominating
the two- and three-point functions. Unfortunately the statistical quality
of the Monte Carlo estimates is typically deteriorating fast once 2 fm in total
(or 1 fm for
K
and
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
each) are reached. It is thus a matter of
the details of methods and QCD dynamics whether there is a window to
determine the desired form factors at the percent level.

In other words, control over the extraction of the bare form factors is crucial in a proper
determination of the decay rates. In this publication we thus explain our methods and
their limitations in detail.

A few comments are in order

-

We chose to define the correlation functions on
a lattice. Replacing
a^{3}\sum_{\bf x}\to\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bf x}

yields the continuum expressions.

-

The derivation of Eq. (
) (on a lattice) is based
just on the existence of a transfer matrix with standard
properties and on interpolating fields which are local in time;
non-locality (smearing) in space is allowed.

-

Of course, on a finite lattice the limit

t\to\infty
has to be replaced by a proper procedure.
Controlling it is the main topic of this paper. As a first,
straightforward, procedure we note that it would be sufficient to have

t
(as well as the spatial box length
L
) large compared
to the typical QCD scales
\frac{1}{2}{\rm fm},\;1/m_{\pi}
.

-

In order to obtain the physical form factors,
the current
V_{\mu}
has to be
properly renormalized and the continuum limit has to be taken.

So far we have spoken of standard relativistic (lattice) QCD.
The non-perturbative HQET expressions including
\mathcal{O}\left(1/m\right)
terms are obtained by the following
generic expansion of correlation
functions
[
11
,
17
,
18
]

\displaystyle\log({\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu})=\log Z^{\mathrm{HQET}}_{V_{\mu}}+\log([{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu}]_{\mathrm{stat}})+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\rho_{\mu}^{k}+\delta_{\cal O}+\mathcal{O}\left(1/m_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}\right)\,,

(2.10)

where
[{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}]_{\mathrm{stat}}
is the correlation function in the static approximation and

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}^{k}={[{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu}]_{k}\over[{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu}]_{\mathrm{stat}}}\,.

(2.11)

The "stat" terms refer to lowest order (static) HQET, while

k\in\{\rm kin,spin\}
are the terms due to the

1/m
corrections to the action, and the remaining terms in the sum over
k

are
1/m
corrections to the currents, see Sect.
6.2
.
In
\delta_{\cal O}
, we collect all terms which arise from the renormalization and
1/m

corrections to the interpolating fields.
Their form is irrelevant since they cancel
in the ratios of interest Eq. (
).
Apart from
\delta_{\cal O}
, all divergences strictly cancel within Eq. (
2.10
)
iff the parameters
\log Z^{\mathrm{HQET}}_{V_{\mu}}
and
\omega_{k}

are determined non-perturbatively. The expansion of

\log({\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}})
reads the same apart from the current-terms
which are absent. Finally, functions of correlation functions
such as the ratio Eq. (
) are trivially obtained from the
expansion of the correlation functions.

For completeness we remind the reader of the rule of the game:
the expansion is “derived” by considering the parameters

\omega_{k}
as
\mathcal{O}\left(1/m\right)
and expanding in
1/m

despite the fact that individual pieces may be highly divergent.
The logarithm is taken in Eq. (
2.10

all expressions are automatically linear in the parameters
and higher order terms in the parameters (and thus
1/m

Those would otherwise have to be dropped to preserve renormalizability.

3.1
Kaon correlation functions

While Eq. (
3.20

With our periodic boundary conditions in time, the ground-state contribution reads

\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}(t)\cong(\kappa^{(0)})^{2}(e^{-E_{\rm K}^{(0)}t}+e^{-E_{\rm K}^{(0)}(T-t)}).

(3.24)

Since the signal-to-noise problem in the Kaon sector is
very mild, we average
\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}(t)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}(T-t)
and always fit until the middle of the lattice,
t_{\rm K2}^{\rm max}=T/2

For the start of the fit range, we follow

[

32

E_{{\rm K},{\rm eff}}(t)=-\frac{1}{a}\log[\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}(t+a)/\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}(t)]

t_{\rm K2}^{\rm min}\approx 1.3-1.4\,\textrm{fm}
.
In this range, we fit the correlator to Eq. (
3.24

energy and amplitude. Note that the gap between the ground state and excited
states in the Kaon sector is large, approximately

1\,{\rm GeV}\approx 5\,{\rm fm}^{-1}

3.2
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
correlation functions

For the two-point heavy-light (
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
) correlator, we have three different
smearings (in addition to the unsmeared correlator, which is not used due to its
large contamination from highly excited states). With the diagonal and the
off-diagonal terms, we have six independent correlators in a symmetric
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rs}
matrix.
The determination of the parameters of the
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
correlation functions is divided
into several steps, which are described in the following subsections.

3.2.1
Energies from the GEVP

We first determine the energies by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem (GEVP) on
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rs}

[
33
]
. We use all
N_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}=3
interpolating fields. The GEVP is defined as

\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(t)v^{(n)}(t,t_{0})=\lambda^{(n)}(t,t_{0})\,\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(t_{0})\,v^{(n)}(t,t_{0})\,,\quad n=1,\ldots,N_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\,,

(3.25)

and yields approximations to the lowest
N_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}
energy
levels via

E_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm eff}}^{(n)}(t)=-\tilde{\partial}_{t}\log\big(\lambda^{(n)}(t,t_{0}))\big)=\frac{1}{2a}\log\big(\lambda^{(n)}(t-a,t_{0})/\lambda^{(n)}(t+a,t_{0})\big).

(3.26)

We use
t_{0}=\lceil t/2\rceil
since this asymptotically accelerates the
convergence (with
t
) to the energies
E_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(n)}

[
34
]
.
For each
n
separately we then find the GEVP estimate of the energy

E_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}^{(n)}
by a plateau fit (a weighted average) of
E_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm eff}}^{(n)}(t)
. The
upper end,
t_{\textrm{max}}
, of the plateau is chosen with a noise criterion: only

E_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm eff}}^{(n)}(t)
with a relative error less than 20% (50% for the excited
states) are included in the average. The start of the plateau,
t_{\rm min}
, is chosen by requiring

r(t_{\rm min})=\frac{|E^{(n)}_{\textnormal{plat}}(t_{\rm min})-E^{(n)}_{\textnormal{plat}}(t_{\rm min}-\delta t)|}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(t_{\rm min})+\sigma^{2}(t_{\rm min}-\delta t)}}\leq 3,

where
E^{(n)}_{\textnormal{plat}}(t_{\rm min})
is the result of the plateau fit
starting at
t_{\rm min}
and
\delta t\approx 2/(E_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(N)}-E_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(0)})
, which we
approximate by choosing
\delta t=0.3
fm.
For the ground state this criterion coincides with that of Ref.
[
35
]
,
ensuring that the statistical error dominates over the systematic one. Examples
of
E_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm eff}}^{(0)}(t)
are presented in Fig.
4

(together with
E_{{\rm K},{\rm eff}}(t)
for comparison). Note that a good
estimate of the ground-state energy will be important in
Sec.
5
.

For the excited states one should in principle use a higher value of
\delta t\approx 2/(E_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(N)}-E_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(n)})
but this is not possible with the current precision
of the data, as one ends up in regions dominated by noise. However, as opposed
to the ground state, for the excited-state energies we only need reasonable
rough estimates, as any residual systematic error will be eliminated in the
subsequent fitting steps as described in Sec.
4
.

3.2.2
Amplitudes

Having obtained the energies, we determine the amplitudes
\beta_{r}^{(n)}
. We
start with the diagonal elements of
{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}
,

{\cal C}_{rr}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(t)\cong\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}-1}(\beta_{r}^{(n)})^{2}e^{-E_{\rm{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}^{(n)}t}\,

(3.27)

and estimate the squared amplitudes through a linear fit. These
values are used as initial guess for a non-linear fit for the amplitudes
\beta^{(n)}_{r}
to all the
correlators, including the off-diagonal ones. In this way also
the relative signs of the amplitudes are obtained.

Then, we try the full fit for both amplitudes and energies whenever
possible. On certain ensembles the full fit becomes unstable with
N_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}=3
,
in this case we fall back to the fit with fixed energies.

The upper end of the fit range,
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm max}
, is common for all the six
correlators and is determined by the relative noise criterion, i.e. as the
last point at which the relative error is smaller than 2.5% for all of the

\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rs}
. For our ensembles this gives
2.1-2.4\;\textrm{fm}
. Also the
lower end of the fit range,
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm min}
, is the same for all
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rs}
.
It is a tunable parameter that is common for the determination of the amplitudes
and for the combined fit (cf. Sec.
4
).

3.3
Three-point static correlation functions

Due to the finite time extent of the lattice, the three-point function also receives
contributions from particles propagating (“wrapping”) around the torus in time
direction and is the sum of the contributions from the two
diagrams shown in Fig.

contributions of the corresponding lowest states can be written as

\displaystyle{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}(t_{\rm K},t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}})\cong

\displaystyle\kappa^{(0)}\varphi^{(0,0)}_{\mu}\beta_{r}^{(0)}e^{-E_{\rm{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}^{(0)}t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}e^{-E_{\rm K}^{(0)}t_{\rm K}}+

(3.28)

\displaystyle\kappa^{(0)}\xi_{\mu,r}e^{-E_{{\rm B}^{*}}t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}e^{-E_{\rm K}^{(0)}(T-t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}-t_{\rm K})},

where
\xi_{\mu,r}=\langle 0|V_{\mu}|{\rm B}^{*}\rangle\langle{\rm B}^{*}|P_{\rm hl}|{\rm K}\rangle
contains unknown matrix elements of the state

{\rm B}^{*}
, the lightest heavy-light state contributing to the “wrapper” diagram.
At static order the energy
E_{{\rm B}^{*}}
is equal to
E_{\rm{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}^{(0)}
.

Instead of introducing extra fit parameters
\xi_{\mu,r}
(and possibly others, if
excited states need to be included), we restrict ourselves to time separations
where the wrapper contributions are negligible.

For this purpose, at every fixed
t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
we fit the three-point function
to the form

{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}(t_{\rm K})\cong B_{\mu,r}e^{-Et_{\rm K}}+C_{\mu,r}e^{+Et_{\rm K}},

(3.29)

with
B_{\mu,r}
and
C_{\mu,r}
being linear fit parameters (which one can express in terms
of the amplitudes and matrix elements from Eq. (
3.28

E

being a non-linear fit parameter (although one could in principle set it to

E_{\rm K}
extracted from the two-point function). The fit is done in a region
where ground-state dominance in the Kaon sector is expected (starting at
approximately
t_{\rm K3}^{\rm min}=0.8
fm).
Fig.
6
shows an example of such a fit at fixed
t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}

For every
t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
,
\mu

r

t_{\rm K3}^{\rm max,wr}

the last

\frac{C_{\mu,r}e^{+Et_{\rm K}}}{B_{\mu,r}e^{-Et_{\rm K}}+C_{\mu,r}e^{+Et_{\rm K}}}<c_{\rm wr}\,\frac{\delta{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}(t_{\rm K},t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}})}{{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}(t_{\rm K},t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}})}\,,

From the two-point function fits, we have obtained (estimates of) the energies

\kappa^{(m)}

energies and amplitudes also from the three-point functions, which contain many
data points. To estimate the errors we use
[

The numerical results presented in this section are for the fit with
N_{\rm K}=1

and
N_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}=3

N_{\rm K}+N_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}

. We find that

To keep statistical and systematic errors under control,
only a small and carefully chosen subset of all the

-

Moreover, it is important to have a criterion for good determination of the

for

and we monitor several criteria.

4.2
Fit quality and results

The combined fit is uncorrelated, i.e. we use a diagonal covariance matrix.
For such a big matrix the full covariance matrix is very badly determined and conditioned (in
fact, if the number of data points is bigger than the number of measurements,
it is not possible to invert
[
38
,
39
]
).

As a way to monitor the quality of the uncorrelated fit, we use
\chi^{2}_{\rm exp}
, the expectation value of
\chi^{2}
given normally distributed
data with the measured covariance matrix
[
40
]
.
It can be estimated efficiently including
autocorrelations
[
41
]
, but
the uncertainty on its determination can be large and
it is not easy to exactly quantify what is a good fit.
In general we consider fits with
\chi^{2}\lesssim 2\chi^{2}_{\rm exp}
as
acceptable.

Another way of monitoring the fit quality is to look at the contributions to the

\chi^{2}
coming from different correlation functions. In particular, an
unusually large contribution from
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
should be monitored as it can indicate
that
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm min}
was chosen too small (even though the overall
\chi^{2}

looks acceptable because of the many points in the three-point functions).

Apart from monitoring the
\chi^{2}
, we analyze the stability of the extracted fit
values and errors with respect to changes of
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm min}
and
t_{\rm B3}^{\rm min}
.

The stability plots are always organized in the following way: the data is
divided in groups with different
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm min}
, which is also plotted on the

x
-axis in the middle of each group. Inside every group
t_{\rm B\Delta}^{\rm min}

varies from 0 to approx. 0.5 fm. Additionally, we highlight in different colors
the values for selected
t_{\rm B2/3}^{\rm min}
, which are described in the
following.

In choosing
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm min}
and
t_{\rm B3}^{\rm min}
one must find a good window
between too small values, where the fit is plagued by contamination from the
higher excited states (this can be seen by high
\chi^{2}
as well as by lack of
stability of the extracted fit parameters with respect to small changes in

t_{\rm B2/3}^{\rm min}
) and too high values, where it can no longer resolve the
three
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
states. The latter is demonstrated in Fig.
8
. At
large
t_{\rm B2/3}^{\rm min}
we see that for both ensembles at least one of the
energies is no longer resolved. Therefore, we refrain from showing the largest
values of
t_{\rm B2}^{\rm min}
in Figs.

Matrix elements at static order from ratios

5.1
Ordinary ratios

An alternative to extracting the ground-state matrix elements from fitting the
correlation functions directly is to construct an appropriate ratio of the
correlation functions such that the dependence on all or most of the other
parameters cancels in the limit of large time.

One example of such a ratio was actually used to
define
the form
factor, cf. Eq. (
). In the following it will be beneficial to generalize
this definition to the case where
{t_{\rm K}}\neq{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}
in the three-point function
(
\tau\equiv{t_{\rm K}}+{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}
):

\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{I}}_{\mu,r}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})=\frac{{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})}{\big[{\cal C}^{\rm K}(\tau){\cal C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rr}(\tau)\big]^{1/2}}\exp\big\{(\tilde{E}^{(0)}_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(\tau)-\tilde{E}^{(0)}_{\rm K}(\tau))\tfrac{{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}-{t_{\rm K}}}{2}\big\}\,.

(5.34)

In the limit
{t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}\to\infty
, this ratio converges to the desired bare form factor

\varphi^{(0,0)}_{\mu}
if additional parameters
\tilde{E}_{\rm K}(\tau),\tilde{E}^{(0)}_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(\tau)

satisfy
\tilde{E}(\tau)=E+\mathcal{O}\left(\exp(-\Delta E\tau)\right)
. Possible choices will be discussed below.

In Sec.
5.2
we will see the advantage of this definition, while
its obvious disadvantage is that in the denominator one needs the
two-point correlation functions at time
\tau={t_{\rm K}}+{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}
. This means that e.g.
for a simple estimate of a plateau in the three point function at

{t_{\rm K}}\approx{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}\approx 1\,{\rm fm}
one needs the two-point correlation functions at a
large time separation
\tau\approx 2\,{\rm fm}
, which is particularly problematic in
the case of
{\cal C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
where the signal-to-noise problem is much more severe than for

\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}
.

For lattices with short time extent, like A5,
\tau
may
come close to the middle of the lattice and we need to take into account the
influence of the second term in Eq. (
3.24
). We do that by multiplying the
ratio by an additional factor
\big(1+\e^{E_{\rm K}(T-4t)}\big)^{1/2}

which cancels the unwanted contribution up to excited states
which are negligible in this region.

In addition, one can consider another ratio

\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{II}}_{\mu,r}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})=\frac{{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})}{\big[{\cal C}^{\rm K}({t_{\rm K}}){\cal C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rr}({t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})\big]^{1/2}}\exp\big\{\tilde{E}^{(0)}_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(\tau)\tfrac{{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}}{2}+\tilde{E}^{(0)}_{\rm K}(\tau)\tfrac{{t_{\rm K}}}{2}\big\}

(5.35)

which has a more favourable signal-to-noise behaviour of the denominator
but requires energy estimates even for the choice
{t_{\rm K}}={t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}
.

A similar ratio is

\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{III}}_{\mu,i}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})=\frac{{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})}{\mathcal{N}^{\rm K}{\cal C}^{\rm K}({t_{\rm K}})\,\mathcal{N}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{r}{\cal C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rr}({t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}})},

(5.36)

where the additional normalization factors
\mathcal{N}^{\rm K}
,

\mathcal{N}_{r}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
can be expressed in terms of the correlation function
parameters
\kappa^{(0)}
and
\beta^{(0)}_{r}
respectively.
In practice, our data for
\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{III}}
shows very similar or slightly inferior
behaviour compared to
\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{II}}
, therefore we do not present the numerical results
for this ratio.

Let us now discuss the choice of the ground-state energy estimates. One can set them to the effective masses of the
corresponding two-point functions,
avoiding any fit procedure.
This, however, results in large statistical
fluctuations in the large-time region. For our data, it is beneficial to
instead use the time-independent estimates
E_{\rm K}^{(0)},E_{\rm{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}^{(0)}
extracted in
Sections
3.1
and
3.2

calculated the ratios for different choices of the plateau ranges of both
energies. From that exercise we find that the systematic error associated with using the fitted
ground-state energies is negligible with respect to our statistical
uncertainties.

6
Matrix elements at
1/m
order

The HQET expansion of B-meson observables becomes a precision tool
only when
1/m
terms are included. We now discuss the determination of
these crucial terms for the matrix elements. As in the static approximation
we have the option to perform fits to the (two-point and three-point) correlation functions, or to consider ratios or summed ratios.
Fits have been discussed and applied in Ref.
[
18
]

for the somewhat simpler case of the B-to-vacuum matrix element

f_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
. As there, in our present case, all parameters

\beta_{s}^{(n)},\,E^{(n)}
get triplicated with static and
1/m

pieces corresponding to kin and spin insertions. Furthermore pure exponentials turn into exponentials plus terms of the form

E^{\mathrm{kin}}_{n}t\exp(-E^{{\rm stat}}_{n}t)
. This proliferation of terms
in addition to the
1/m
corrections to the matrix elements themselves
makes an analysis in terms of fits cumbersome and difficult. We therefore
concentrate on the analysis of ratios, which we already have seen to be just as good as fits in the static approximation. We will also see that there
are some simplifications in the
1/m
expansion of ratios which make them
rather accessible.

As a preparation for expanding the ratios, we start with the underlying two- and three-point functions. First note that we choose
the arbitrary interpolating fields

{\cal O}_{\mathrm{bs},r}
, eq. (
2.14
), not to contain
a
1/m
piece. Therefore, the
1/m
expansion of the two-point function reads

\displaystyle\log(\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(t))

\displaystyle=-m_{\textnormal{bare}}t+\log({{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm stat}}(t))+\sum_{k\in\{\mathrm{kin},\mathrm{spin}\}}\omega_{k}\left(\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},k}(t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm stat}}(t)}\right).

(6.38)

Only the (dimensionful) HQET parameters of the action,

m_{\textnormal{bare}}
as well as
\omega_{\mathrm{k}}\sim 1/m
,
appear. Here and everywhere below all O
(1/m^{2})
terms are dropped without notice. Note also that
m_{\textnormal{bare}}
drops
out in the expressions for matrix elements.

The energy
E^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}-\partial_{t}\log(\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}(t))
is
1/m
-expanded as

E^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}=m_{\textnormal{bare}}+E^{{\rm stat}}+\omega_{\mathrm{kin}}E^{\mathrm{kin}}+\omega_{\mathrm{spin}}E^{\mathrm{spin}}
with
E^{x}
appearing in the
large time behaviour of

[
45
]

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\log{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm stat}}_{rr}(t)=E^{{\rm stat}}+\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\Delta E^{{\rm stat}}t}\right),

(6.39)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},\textnormal{k}}_{rr}(t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm stat}}_{rr}(t)}=E^{\textnormal{k}}+\mathcal{O}\left(te^{-\Delta E^{{\rm stat}}t}\right)\,,\quad k\in\{\mathrm{kin},\mathrm{spin}\}.

(6.40)

All
E^{x}
refer to the ground state; the first excited state contribution
leads to the term with
\Delta E^{{\rm stat}}=E^{{\rm stat}}_{2}-E^{{\rm stat}}_{1}
.
Because one always first expands in
1/m
and then takes a limit of large time, terms such as
\exp(-\Delta E^{\mathrm{kin}}t)
do not appear.

In the numerical applications we will take
\partial_{t}
to be the
forward derivative,
a\partial_{t}f(t)=f(t+a)-f(t)
.
On integrating Eq. (
6.40
)
, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},\textnormal{k}}_{rr}(t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm stat}}_{rr}(t)}={\rm A}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},\textnormal{k}}_{r}-E^{\textnormal{k}}t+\mathcal{O}\left(te^{-\Delta E^{{\rm stat}}t}\right)\,.

(6.41)

The integration constants
{\rm A}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},\textnormal{k}}_{r}
do depend on the
\mathrm{kin}
and
\mathrm{spin}
insertions as well as the smearing level used.

In complete analogy we have for the three-point functions (
t=t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}=t_{\rm K}
):

\displaystyle\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},k}_{\mu,r}(t,t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},{\rm stat}}_{\mu,r}(t,t)}={\rm A}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},k}_{\mu,r}-E^{k}t+\mathcal{O}\left(te^{-\Delta E^{{\rm stat}}t}\right).

(6.42)

Now we turn to the the ratios
and insert the
1/m
expansion of the quantities which enter their definition
in (
5.34
) and (
5.35
).
To understand the structure, consider the expansion

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{I}}_{\mu,r}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{I},{{\rm stat}}}_{\mu,r}(t)\left[1+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\,\rho^{\textnormal{I},{k}}_{\mu,r}(t)+\sum_{j}\omega_{\mu j}\,\rho^{\textnormal{I},{j}}_{\mu,r}(t)\right]

with

\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{I},{k}}_{\mu,r}(t,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},k}_{\mu,r}(t,t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},{\rm stat}}_{\mu,r}(t,t)}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},k}_{r}(2t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}},{\rm stat}}_{r}(2t)}\,,\quad k\in\{\mathrm{kin},\mathrm{spin}\}\,,

(6.43)

\displaystyle\rho^{\textnormal{I},{j}}_{\mu,r}(t,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},j}_{\mu,r}(t,t)}{{{\cal C}}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K},j}_{\mu,r}(t,t)}\,.

(6.44)

The sums over

(6.45)

\displaystyle\rho^{j}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho^{\textnormal{I},{j}}_{\mu,r}(t)\,,

(6.46)

7
Discussion

Flavor changing transitions are important channels for learning
about possible limitations of the standard model. Exclusive
semi-leptonic decays of B or
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
mesons are particularly
clean theoretically. However, lattice computations of the
relevant form factors are needed and they are non-trivial
in practice. A major reason is the infamous signal-to-noise
problem. At large Euclidean time separations the noise in
Monte Carlo evaluation of the correlation functions is too
large to determine the form factors (matrix elements) with
interesting precision.

While the issue is not new, we have exposed the problem in a
few graphs more clearly than often done, see Figs
4
,
14
.
For the three-point functions and derived (summed) ratios,
this was possible because we have evaluated correlation
functions at all time separations. We are only considering the
pseudoscalar sector, where the signal-to-noise
problem is very mild for a relativistic formulation
and at zero momentum. However, we need finite momentum
(see Fig.
2
for the momentum dependence)
and for reasons explained in the beginning of the paper
we use HQET
for the b-quark
(see Fig.
4
for the difference of Kaon and
static
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
meson). In HQET, the signal-to-noise
problem becomes worse as one decreases the lattice spacing.
Nevertheless, we are determining the matrix elements
at total time separations
\tau
of the three-point functions
of around 2 fm and for
a\approx 0.05\,{\rm fm}
. Only for the summed ratio we use about
half that time-separation (Fig.
14
), in agreement with the predicted better suppression of excited states after
summation.

Despite our use of HQET, these separations are larger or similar to the ones typically used. E.g. most recently Ref.
[
9
]
used a fixed
\tau\approx 2.2\,{\rm fm}
.

We have presented good evidence
that the chosen plateaux or fit-windows are reasonably safe, but nevertheless it would be better to have larger times accessible.
Maybe multilevel strategies

[
47
,
48
]
will help to reach those separations in the future. At present we derive our confidence
from the good agreement of

•

fits with
{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{>}}}{{\sim}}$}0.3-0.6
and 3 states in the
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
sector (and also with 2
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
states and
{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{>}}}{{\sim}}$}0.5-0.7
, cf. Appendix

B
)

•

ratios Eq. (
5.35
) with
\tau={t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}+{t_{\rm K}}\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{>}}}{{\sim}}$}\,2\,{\rm fm}
,

•

and summed ratios Eq. (
5.37
) at total separation

\tau\,\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{$\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textstyle{>}}}{{\sim}}$}\,1\,{\rm fm}
.

Note that also the precision of the different estimates
is quite comparable. It is therefore preferable to use
the technically easier ratio methods.

At the lowest (static) order in
1/m
the most relevant form factors
for
\mu=1
have an accuracy around 2%, which is good
for precision physics. The first order corrections
in
1/m
are actually more precise than that. The relative errors induced into the form factors are given by the absolute ones
of the quantities
\rho
in Tables
4
,
5
multiplied with the appropriate
\omega
-coefficients.
From the errors of
\rho^{\mathrm{kin}}
and
\rho^{\mathrm{spin}}
, using the non-perturbative
\omega_{\mathrm{kin}}\,,\omega_{\mathrm{spin}}

[
13
]
, we get error contributions of 1% and 0.5% to the form factors, respectively.
The uncertainties of
\rho^{j}
, translate into below 0.5% errors
assuming coefficients
\omega_{j}

which do not exceed the tree-level values by more than a factor of two.
Of course, the uncertainties in the coefficients
\omega
are to be added separately.

A positive result of our detailed analysis is thus that

1/m
corrections can be
determined precisely, when the coefficients (HQET parameters) are known
with reasonable accuracy. Once they are available it will be possible to provide further crosschecks on the existing analysis,
see e.g. Fig. 13 of
[
9
]
.

We would finally like to point out a possible danger in current and future
semi-leptonic form factor computations on the lattice.
It consists in the contribution of
multi-hadron states, such as
|{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(\ast)},\pi\rangle
or
|{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{(\ast)},{\rm K}\rangle
, to our Euclidean correlation functions.
Formally, these contributions are just particular ones in the
sums over excited states in Eqs. (
3.20
-
3.22
)
and are thus covered by our analysis. However, there are two properties
which make such states special.
First, when the spatial volume becomes large
and the light-quark masses small, there are several low-lying states
with small gaps
E^{(n)}-E^{(0)}
. All used methods may have difficulties in separating those.
This has also been pointed out recently in
[
49
]
.
Second, normalized overlaps

\beta_{r}^{(n)}/\beta_{r}^{(0)}

may be very similar for different smearing levels
r
when
n
corresponds
to a multi-hadron state.
11
11

11

See the discussion of the
field in the chiral effective theory representing the action density at finite flow time,
E(x,t)
in Ref.
[
50
]
.

As mentioned in Sect.
5.3
the GEVP-method is of little
help in such a situation.
For B-mesons these contaminations at finite
{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}
have not been
investigated at all, while there is considerable discussion in the analogous determination of nucleon matrix elements (see Refs.
[
51
,
52
]
and references therein).
It appears likely that form factors
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to K
,
\rm B\to\pi

are in somewhat better shape than nucleon matrix elements,
since larger Euclidean time separations are reached. Nevertheless
systematic studies, especially theoretical ones such as the ones
carried out in
chiral perturbation theory
[
53
,
54
]
for nucleon matrix elements are urgently needed to make quantitative statements.

Appendix A
Computation of the correlation functions

Integration over the fermion fields yields
the correlation functions as

{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}({t_{\rm K}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}};{\bf p})=\langle\widehat{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}\rangle_{U}\,,\quad\mathcal{C}^{\rm K}(t;{\bf p})=\langle\widehat{\cal C}^{\rm K}\rangle_{U}\,,\quad\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rr^{\prime}}(t)=\langle\widehat{\cal C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rr^{\prime}}\rangle_{U}\

(A.50)

averaged over the gauge fields,
U
, (with effective action including the
log of the quark determinant). The dependence of the functions

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{9}}{L^{3}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\gamma_{5}P_{\mathrm{f}}F_{-}W_{\rm K}S_{\mathrm{u}}\gamma_{\mu}P_{\mathrm{v}}F_{+}S_{\mathrm{h}}P_{\mathrm{i}}\gamma_{5}W_{r}S_{\mathrm{s}}W_{\rm K}\right]

(A.51)

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal C}^{\rm K}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{6}}{L^{3}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\gamma_{5}P_{\mathrm{f}}F_{-}W_{\rm K}S_{\mathrm{u}}W_{\rm K}\gamma_{5}P_{\mathrm{i}}F_{+}W_{\rm K}S_{\mathrm{s}}W_{\rm K}\right]

(A.52)

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal C}^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{rr^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{6}}{L^{3}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\gamma_{5}P_{\mathrm{f}}S_{\mathrm{h}}P_{\mathrm{i}}\gamma_{5}W_{r^{\prime}}S_{\mathrm{s}}W_{r}\right]

(A.53)

on the times
(x_{0})_{s}
(
s=\mathrm{f},\mathrm{v},\mathrm{i}
)
as well as the gauge fields
is suppressed. In the above expressions
S_{q}
are the quark propagators,
W_{\rm K}=G^{I^{\rm K}},\;G=1-\kappa_{\mathrm{G}}\,a^{2}\Delta
and
W_{r}=G^{I^{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}_{r}}
are the smearing
operators,
P_{s}
is the projection on time slice
(x_{0})_{s}
and
F_{\pm}=\exp(\pm i{\bf x}{\bf p})
is the multiplication with the Fourier phases on the corresponding time slice. Evaluation of the full
traces which include the space coordinates becomes possible
by representing the projector

P_{\mathrm{f}}=\langle\eta\eta^{\dagger}\rangle_{\eta}

(A.54)

in terms of a random U(1) field
\eta
with support on on time-slice

(x_{0})_{\mathrm{f}}
. This yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal C}^{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}\to{\rm K}}_{\mu,r}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{9}}{L^{3}}\left\langle\eta^{\dagger}F_{-}\gamma_{5}W_{\rm K}S_{\mathrm{u}}P_{\mathrm{v}}\gamma_{\mu}F_{+}S_{\mathrm{h}}P_{\mathrm{i}}\gamma_{5}W_{r}S_{\mathrm{s}}W_{\rm K}\,\eta\right\rangle_{\eta}

(A.55)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\phi_{\mathrm{u}}^{\dagger}\gamma_{5}P_{\mathrm{v}}\gamma_{\mu}F_{+}S_{\mathrm{h}}P_{\mathrm{i}}\gamma_{5}W_{r}\phi_{\mathrm{s}}\right\rangle_{\eta}

(A.56)

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{s}}=S_{\mathrm{s}}W_{\rm K}\,\eta\,,\quad\phi_{\mathrm{u}}=S_{\mathrm{u}}W_{\rm K}F_{-}^{\dagger}\,\eta\,

(A.57)

and similar for the two-point functions.
In this form we see that for each vector
\eta
two solutions of the Dirac equation are needed in order to compute the fields
\phi_{\mathrm{u}},\phi_{\mathrm{s}}
. The static propagator
S_{\mathrm{h}}
is inserted by explicit forward
propagation. Translation invariance in time is used by averaging over
all source time-slices
(x_{0})_{\mathrm{f}}
, each one with a random U(1) field
\eta
.
Since the averages Eq. (
A.50
) and Eq. (
A.54
) are independent of each other, any number of
\eta
fields per gauge field is correct; we
use a single one per gauge field and time
(x_{0})_{\mathrm{f}}
.

In practice, we compute propagators of periodic quark fields in
a gauge field
e^{i\theta_{\mu}a/L}U(x,\mu)
, which includes
the constant
U(1)
background field
\theta^{q}_{\mu}a
and only
use the integer part of the momentum in the Fourier factors
F_{\pm}
.
This is equivalent to Eq. (
2.18
), apart from a phase in the Gaussian
smearing, which we set to zero. Choosing a different phase in the smearing
along the lines of
[

might be a further optimization.

Appendix C
Note on the convergence of the static summed ratio

In Section
5.2
, devoted to static summed ratios, we observe that
the plateaux for
\mu=0
in Fig.
14
seem to start very early.
Taking the results for O7 at face value, one could start the plateau fit as
early as 0.4 fm. To see whether this is a genuine ground state dominance we investigate
truncated sums of the form:

\tilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\textrm{I}}_{\mu,r}(\tau)=\partial_{\tau}\,a\sum_{{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}=t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{\rm min}}^{\tau-t_{\rm K}^{\rm min}}\mathcal{R}^{\,\textrm{I}}_{\mu,r}(\tau-{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}},{t_{{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}}}).

(C.60)

By putting
t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{\rm min}
or
t_{\rm K}^{\rm min}
larger than 0 we can suppress
the excited states in the
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
and
{\rm K}
sector respectively.
12
12

12

Note
however that this increases the excited state corrections
from
\sim\tau\Delta\exp(-\tau\Delta)
to

\sim\tau^{\prime}\Delta\exp(-\tau^{\prime}\Delta)
with

\tau^{\prime}=\tau-t_{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}^{\rm min}-t_{\rm K}^{\rm min}
.

The
results of this procedure are shown in Fig.
23
. We see that the
excited states in the
{\rm K}
sector push the result upwards, while the excited
states in the
{\textnormal{B}_{\textnormal{s}}}
sector push the results downwards. Their cancellation
results in a flat “fake” plateau which can start at very early times. A
similar but much less pronounced effect is at work for

therefore not rely solely on the flatness of the plots but also devise an
