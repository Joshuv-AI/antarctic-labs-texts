Argument for the radiation-dominated behavior of matter fields in the preinflationary era
Taylor M. Ordines, Eric D. Carlson

Abstract
We investigate the leading-order behavior of matter fields in the preinflationary era using the semiclassical approximation. Many inflationary models assume without supporting arguments that the Universe was radiation dominated prior to inflation, leading to modifications of cosmological observables, such as the Cosmic Microwave Background power spectrum. In previous work, we demonstrated that conformally coupled scalar fields do have a radiation-like contribution to the stress-energy tensor at sufficiently early times. In this work, we extend these arguments to apply to massless spin-1 fields and massive or massless spin- 1 2 \frac{1}{2} fields. We find massless spin-1 fields always have a radiation-like contribution. For spin- 1 2 \frac{1}{2} fields, we find the contribution at early times is radiation-like assuming this is the dominant contribution to the stress-energy tensor.



I
Introduction

The inflationary paradigm explains many characteristics of our observed Universe.
Many models of inflation make use of a radiation-dominated behavior of matter fields in the preinflationary era in order to obtain modifications to the standard predictions of inflation and better explain observed phenomena (see
Das et al. 2015
and references therein for a review of models using a radiation-dominated preinflationary era).
For instance, various models involving the transition from a radiation-dominated era to inflation lead to modifications of the Cosmic Microwave Background anisotropy spectrum, such as a lowering of the quadrupole moment which appears to be anomalously suppressed
Bennett et al. 2003
.
The assumption that fields are radiation-dominated prior to inflation is central to such models, though it is non-trivial to demonstrate that such behavior was the case for our Universe, and it would be hopeless to try to detect particles due to these fields today given the effects of inflation.
It is therefore interesting to analyze the preinflationary era and the behavior of matter fields in it.

The primary objective of this work is to investigate the behavior of quantum fields in the preinflationary era.
A complete analysis of the the early universe would require a theory of quantum gravity, which for now is out of reach, but one could anticipate that after the Planck era some span of the preinflationary era would have curvature well below the Planck scale, in which case the semiclassical approximation should hold.
In this paper, we will assume the semiclassical approximation to hold in some portion of the preinflationary era, and we will use this framework to analyze quantum fields in a Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) spacetime and obtain their corresponding contributions to the energy density.

The case of massive scalar fields was investigated using the semiclassical approximation in
Anderson et al. 2020
.
There it was shown that under a set of conditions on the scale factor the fields did result in radiation-dominated contributions to the energy density.
The conditions on the scale factor were also argued to likely be valid for our Universe.
In the present work, we will investigate the corresponding behavior of other fields, namely massless spin-1 and massive or massless spin-
\frac{1}{2}
fields.

The analysis for spin-1 and spin-
\frac{1}{2}
fields is generally more complicated than for scalar fields.
For spin-
\frac{1}{2}
fields, the mass appears explicity in the expression for the counterterms at higher than zeroth adiabatic order, which leads to additional complications.
Performing adiabatic regularization with a zeroth-order parameterization of the states does not explicitly appear to produce finite energy densities until one considers the higher order contributions buried in the parameterization. For an overview of the adiabatic regularization procedure for spin-
\frac{1}{2}
, see
Landete et al. 2013
;
Landete et al. 2014
;
del Rio et al. 2014
.
For spin-1 fields one must consider the massive and massless cases separately.
Massive vector fields do appear in the Standard Model, but they are ultimately due to interactions with the Higgs field and are out of the scope of this paper.
For the massless case one can show that the analysis decomposes into four decoupled copies of a scalar field, as was done in Ref.
Chu and Koyama 2017
.
There the analysis was performed to obtain the trace anomaly, but obtaining the renormalized energy density from this groundwork is non-trivial.

In the following, we work in Planck units with
c=G=\hbar=1
and use the
(-,+,+,+)
signature for the metric.
We assume a spatially flat universe described by the FLRW metric

ds^{2}=a^{2}\left(-d\eta^{2}+d\vec{x}^{2}\right)\;,

(1)

where
a\equiv a(\eta)
is the scale factor and
\eta
is conformal time.
We will employ a prime, such as
a^{\prime}\equiv\partial_{\eta}a
, to denote differentiation with respect to conformal time.
We are interested in taking
a\rightarrow 0
at early times and determining whether one can assume radiation-dominated behavior.
However, we do not want to consider times in the Planck era, during which the semiclassical approximation is not assumed to be valied, so we denote by
\eta_{0}
the earliest conformal time of consideration and implicitly assume it to be past the Planck era.
In Planck units, this corresponds to a value of the Hubble parameter
H\equiv\frac{a^{\prime}}{a^{2}}\ll 1
.
We therefore aim to determine if the renormalized energy density
\rho_{r}
for each field is appropriately radiation dominated during a range of conformal time
\eta_{0}<\eta<\eta_{1}
.
We will allow for the possibility of a non-zero mass
m
for fermions, in which case we will insist that
m\ll 1
in Planck units.
If there are fermion fields with
m\gtrsim 1
, these will have mass comparable to or greater than the Planck mass, and then we will assume the fields have no contribution to the total energy density when
\eta\gtrsim\eta_{0}
.

The body of this article is split into three sections, one for each of the two spins of fields and one for a discussion of the results obtained.
Section
II
contains the analysis for spin-1 vector fields, beginning with a summary of the adiabatic regularization procedure and concluding with the renormalization of the energy density and our main result for the spin-1 case.
Section
III
contains the analysis for spin-
\frac{1}{2}
fields and begins with a summary of the modified adiabatic regularization procedure, as described in
del Rio et al. 2014
.
The subsequent renormalization and analysis is more complicated than that for the vector fields and is split into subsections: in Sec.
III.1
we give the renormalization counterterms and preview the assumptions built into our analysis; in Sec.
III.2
, we derive bounds on the renormalized energy density by splitting the contributions into high and low energy terms and analyzing each in turn; and in Sec.
III.3
we obtain the leading order behavior of the renormalized energy density and our main result for the spin-
\frac{1}{2}
case.
We close in Sec.
IV
with a discussion of our main results and final remarks.

II
Energy Density for Vector Fields

As discussed in the introduction, massive and massless vector fields must be treated separately.
In the Lagrangian description, the massive field is described by the Proca action
Schambach and Sanders 2018
, which in flat spacetime is a gauge-fixed theory involving the Higgs mechanism.
Working with interactions and the curved spacetime form of the action is beyond the scope of this paper.
We will focus instead on the massless case.

According to an argument in
Wald 1978
, any conformally invariant theory in a flat FLRW spacetime will have a stress-energy tensor that contains two terms, one of which is radiation-like and the other of which is the anomalous term.
Renormalizing the electromagnatic field, however, requires that masses are introduced for the photon and ghost fields, which break the conformal invariance.
These masses are then taken to zero to obtain physical results.
We therefore feel it is worth working through the details of this procedure to confirm that the argument in
Wald 1978
works.

The massless vector field in curved spacetime is given by the massless limit of the theory described by Lagrangian
Chu and Koyama 2017

\mathcal{L}=\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{1}{4}g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}-\frac{1}{2\xi}\nabla^{\mu}A_{\mu}\nabla^{\nu}A_{\nu}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}g^{\mu\nu}A_{\mu}A_{\nu}+ig^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\bar{\chi}\partial_{\nu}\chi+im_{\chi}\bar{\chi}\chi\right]\;,

(2)

where
A_{\mu}
is the four-vector,
\xi
is the gauge fixing parameter,
\chi
is the (complex) ghost field used to maintain gauge invariance,
m_{\chi}
is the mass of the ghost field, and
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
.
Masses are included in the Lagrangian (
2
) in order to properly renormalize the theory.
These masses can trivially be taken to zero before computing any unrenormalized observables, but they are crucial in obtaining the appropriate renormalization counterterms.
We will make clear when they are to be taken to zero in the following.
The energy-momentum tensor from Eq. (
2
) is

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\text{Maxwell}}+T_{\mu\nu}^{\text{G}}+T_{\mu\nu}^{\text{ghost}}\;,

(3a)

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\text{Maxwell}}\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}g_{\mu\nu}g^{\alpha\beta}g^{\rho\sigma}F_{\alpha\rho}F_{\beta\sigma}+g^{\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\mu}F_{\beta\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}m^{2}g^{\alpha\beta}A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}+m^{2}A_{\mu}A_{\nu}\;,

(3b)

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\text{G}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi}\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\left(g^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\right)^{2}+\left(g_{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}A_{\sigma}\nabla_{\rho}-A_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}-A_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\right)\left(g^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\right)\bigg]\;,

(3c)

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\text{ghost}}\equiv

\displaystyle ig_{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\rho}\bar{\chi}\partial_{\sigma}\chi-i\left(\partial_{\mu}\bar{\chi}\partial_{\nu}\chi+\partial_{\nu}\bar{\chi}\partial_{\mu}\chi\right)+ig_{\mu\nu}m_{\chi}^{2}\bar{\chi}\chi\;.

(3d)

Following the procedure in Ref.
Chu and Koyama 2017
, one can define the components of the four-vector
A_{\mu}
as a combination of temporal, transverse, and longitudinal parts,

A_{\mu}\equiv\left(A_{0},B_{i}+\partial_{i}A\right)\;.

(4)

These components and the ghost field can be expanded in terms of mode functions
Y_{a}
, for
a=0,L,T,\chi
, where
L
represents longitudinal and
T
transverse contributions:

A_{0}=\frac{1}{m^{2}a^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left[a_{\vec{k}}^{(0)}\left(\partial_{\eta}-\frac{2a^{\prime}}{a}\right)\left(maY_{0}\right)e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+a_{\vec{k}}^{(3)}kmaY_{L}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+\text{H.c.}\right]\;,

(5a)

B_{i}=\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\sum_{p=1,2}\left(\epsilon_{i}^{p}a_{\vec{k}}^{(p)}Y_{T}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+\text{H.c.}\right)\;,

(5b)

A=\frac{1}{m^{2}a^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left(a_{\vec{k}}^{(0)}maY_{0}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}-a_{\vec{k}}^{(3)}\partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{ma}{k}Y_{L}\right)e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+\text{H.c.}\right)\;,

(5c)

\chi=\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left(\frac{b_{\vec{k}}Y_{\chi}}{a}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+\frac{b^{\dagger}_{\vec{k}}Y_{\chi}^{*}}{a}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\right)\;,

(5d)

\bar{\chi}=\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\left(\frac{\bar{b}_{\vec{k}}Y_{\chi}}{a}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+\frac{\bar{b}^{\dagger}_{\vec{k}}Y_{\chi}^{*}}{a}e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\right)\;,

(5e)

where
a_{\vec{k}}^{(\mu)}
,
b_{\vec{k}}
, and
\bar{b}_{\vec{k}}
and their hermitian conjugates are annihilation and creation operators,
\epsilon_{i}^{p}
are the two polarization vectors of the transverse modes, and
H.c.
represents the hermitian conjugate of all preceding terms.
The creation and annihilation operators satisfy the commutation and anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\left[a_{\vec{k}}^{(a)},a_{\vec{k}^{\prime}}^{(b)\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\eta^{ab}\left(2\pi\right)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{k}-\vec{k}^{\prime})\;,

(6a)

\displaystyle\left\{b_{\vec{k}},\bar{b}_{\vec{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\left\{\bar{b}_{\vec{k}},b_{\vec{k}^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\right\}=i\left(2\pi\right)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{k}-\vec{k}^{\prime})\;,

(6b)

where
\eta^{ab}=\text{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
.
The polarization vectors satisfy

\sum_{i}k_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{p}=0\;,

(7a)

\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{p}\epsilon_{i}^{p^{\prime}}=\delta^{pp^{\prime}}\;,

(7b)

\sum_{p=1,2}\epsilon_{i}^{p}\epsilon_{j}^{p}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}}\;,

(7c)

with
p
the polarization index.
The modes
Y_{a}
satisfy the decoupled set of differential equation

\left(\partial_{\eta}^{2}+\Omega_{a}^{2}\right)Y_{a}=0\;,

(8)

where

\Omega_{a}^{2}\equiv\omega_{a}^{2}+\zeta_{a}

(9)

is defined for each contribution by

\displaystyle\omega_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv k^{2}+\xi m^{2}a^{2}\;,

(10a)

\displaystyle\omega_{L,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv k^{2}+m^{2}a^{2}\;,

(10b)

\displaystyle\omega_{\chi}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv k^{2}+m_{\chi}^{2}a^{2}\;,

(10c)

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{0,\chi}

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{a^{\prime\prime}}{a}\;,

(11a)

\displaystyle\zeta_{L}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{a^{\prime\prime}}{a}-\frac{2a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}}\;,

(11b)

\displaystyle\zeta_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\;.

(11c)

The modes satisfy the standard normalization conditions for a scalar field,

Y_{a}Y_{a}^{*\prime}-Y_{a}^{\prime}Y_{a}^{*}=i\;.

(12)

Note that in the massless limit
Y_{0}
and
Y_{\chi}
satisfy the same differential equations, so we can choose

Y_{0}=Y_{\chi}\quad\text{for}\quad m=m_{\chi}=0\;.

(13)

Using this mode decomposition, one can find contributions to the energy density
\rho\equiv\langle T_{\hat{0}\hat{0}}\rangle
from Eqs. (
3b
)–(
3d
) in terms of
Y_{a}
:

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{Maxwell}}=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m\to 0}\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}dk\,k^{2}\bigg[\omega_{T}^{2}\left|Y_{T}\right|^{2}+\left|Y_{T}^{\prime}\right|^{2}-\left(k^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}}\right)\left|Y_{0}\right|^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\partial_{\eta}\left(\left|Y_{0}\right|^{2}\right)-\left|Y_{0}^{\prime}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\omega_{L}^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}}\right)\left|Y_{L}\right|^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\partial_{\eta}\left(\left|Y_{L}\right|^{2}\right)+\left|Y_{L}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\bigg]

(14a)

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{G}}=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}dk\,k^{2}\left[\left(\omega_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\xi m^{2}a^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}}\right)\left|Y_{0}\right|^{2}-\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\partial_{\eta}\left(\left|Y_{0}\right|^{2}\right)+\left|Y_{0}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right]

(14b)

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{ghost}}=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m_{\chi}\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}dk\,k^{2}\left[-\left(\omega_{\chi}^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}}\right)\left|Y_{\chi}\right|^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\partial_{\eta}\left(\left|Y_{\chi}\right|^{2}\right)-\left|Y_{\chi}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right]\;,

(14c)

where
\Lambda
is a cutoff regulator which we will later demonstrate can be taken to infinity in the massless limit.

One may adiabatically renormalize the energy density by writing the vacuum states with the standard WKB ansatz

\displaystyle Y_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2W_{a}}}e^{-i\int^{\eta}d\bar{\eta}W_{a}(\bar{\eta})}\;,

(15)

where

\left(W_{a}\right)^{2}=\Omega_{a}^{2}-\left[\frac{W_{a}^{\prime\prime}}{2W_{a}}-\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{W_{a}^{\prime}}{W_{a}}\right)^{2}\right]\;.

(16)

Solutions to Eq. (
16
) can be approximated using
W_{a}^{(0)}=\Omega_{a}
as the lowest order and iterating to higher orders, keeping to the appropriate adiabatic order, given by the number of time derivatives on the scale factor, at each iteration.
Substitution of
W_{a}
to some adiabatic order
A
into Eq. (
15
) would then require expanding the square root only to terms of adiabatic order
A
.

In order to renormalize the energy density, one must take Eq. (
15
) to the appropriate adiabatic order and substitute into Eqs. (
14a
)–(
14c
) to produce the renormalization counterterms.
On dimensional grounds, one would need to keep to fourth adiabatic order to renormalize
\rho
.
The fourth order counterterms are

\displaystyle\rho_{c}^{(4)}=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m\to 0}\frac{1}{4a^{2}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg\{W_{0}+W_{L}+2W_{T}-2W_{\chi}+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{W_{0}}+\frac{\omega_{L}^{2}}{W_{L}}+\frac{2\omega_{T}^{2}}{W_{T}}-\frac{2\omega_{\chi}^{2}}{W_{\chi}}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}W_{0}}

\displaystyle+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}W_{L}}-\frac{2a^{\prime 2}}{a^{2}W_{\chi}}+\frac{a^{\prime}W_{0}^{\prime}}{aW_{0}^{2}}-\frac{a^{\prime}W_{L}^{\prime}}{aW_{L}^{2}}-\frac{2a^{\prime}W_{\chi}^{\prime}}{aW_{\chi}^{2}}+\frac{W_{0}^{\prime 2}}{4W_{0}^{3}}+\frac{W_{L}^{\prime 2}}{4W_{L}^{3}}+\frac{W_{T}^{\prime 2}}{2W_{T}^{3}}-\frac{W_{\chi}^{\prime 2}}{2W_{\chi}^{3}}\bigg\}^{(4)}\;,

(17)

where
\{\dots\}^{(4)}
implies that all
W_{a}
are taken to fourth order.

In order to analyze the early-time behavior of the energy density, we will parameterize the mode functions in terms of zeroth-order adiabatic states

Y_{a}=\alpha_{k,a}Y_{a}^{(0)}+\beta_{k,a}Y_{a}^{(0)*}\;,

(18)

Y_{a}^{\prime}=\alpha_{k,a}Y_{a}^{(0)\prime}+\beta_{k,a}Y_{a}^{(0)*\prime}\;.

(19)

From the normalization condition (
12
), one has

\left|\alpha_{k,a}\right|^{2}-\left|\beta_{k,a}\right|^{2}=1\;.

(20)

One could instead parameterize the mode functions in terms of higher-order adiabatic states, in which case the Bogoliubov coefficients
\alpha_{k,a}
and
\beta_{k,a}
would be constant to the given adiabatic order, but zeroth order will be sufficient to properly renormalize the theory.
Substituting Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
) into Eq. (
8
),
one obtains differential equations for the coefficients,

\displaystyle\alpha_{k,a}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{a}^{\prime}}{2\Omega_{a}}\beta_{k,a}e^{2i\theta_{a}}

(21a)

\displaystyle\beta_{k,a}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{a}^{\prime}}{2\Omega_{a}}\alpha_{k,a}e^{-2i\theta_{a}}\;,

(21b)

where
\theta_{a}\equiv\int^{\eta}d\bar{\eta}W_{a}(\bar{\eta})
.

One can then obtain the renormalized energy density in terms of
\alpha_{k,a}
and
\beta_{k,a}
by subtracting these renormalization counterterms from the unrenormalized energy density given in Eqs. (
14a
)–(
14c
).
Using a zeroth order parameterization, one finds that the renormalized energy density
\rho_{r}
separates into analytic and mode terms

\rho_{r}=\rho_{\text{an}}+\rho_{\alpha\beta}\;,

(22)

where the analytic terms
\rho_{\text{an}}
are finite higher-order terms, independent of the cutoff regulator
\Lambda
, coming from the counterterms (
17
),

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{an}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2880\pi^{2}}\left[62\,{}^{(3)}H_{00}+\left(3+\frac{5}{2}\ln\xi\right)\,{}^{(1)}H_{00}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2880\pi^{2}}\bigg[\frac{186a^{\prime 4}}{a^{6}}+\frac{216a^{\prime 2}a^{\prime\prime}}{a^{5}}+\frac{54a^{\prime\prime 2}}{a^{4}}-\frac{108a^{\prime}a^{\prime\prime\prime}}{a^{4}}

\displaystyle+\ln\xi\left(\frac{180a^{\prime 2}a^{\prime\prime}}{a^{5}}+\frac{45a^{\prime\prime 2}}{a^{4}}-\frac{90a^{\prime}a^{\prime\prime\prime}}{a^{4}}\right)\bigg]\;,

(23)

where
{}^{(1)}H_{00}
and
{}^{(3)}H_{00}
are higher order corrections to the Einstein field equations
Birrell and Davies 1982
, and the mode terms
\rho_{\alpha\beta}
are those coming from the zeroth order parameterization of Eq. (
18
),

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha\beta}\equiv

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{m,m_{\chi}\to 0}\frac{1}{a^{2}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg[\frac{k^{2}+\omega_{T}^{2}}{a^{2}\omega_{T}}\left|\beta_{k,T}\right|^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{2\omega_{L}}\left|\beta_{k,L}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\left(\frac{3\omega_{0}^{2}+k^{2}}{2a^{2}}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{4}}\right)\left|\beta_{k,0}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\omega_{\chi}}\left(\frac{\omega_{\chi}^{2}+k^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{4}}\right)\left|\beta_{k,\chi}\right|^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{\omega_{T}}\text{Re}\!\left(\alpha_{k,T}\beta_{k,T}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{T}}\right)+\frac{m^{2}}{2\omega_{L}}\text{Re}\!\left(\alpha_{k,L}\beta_{k,L}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{L}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\left(\frac{a^{\prime 4}}{a^{2}}-\frac{\xi m^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right)\text{Re}\!\left(\alpha_{k,0}\beta_{k,0}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{0}}\right)+\frac{1}{\omega_{\chi}}\left(m_{\chi}^{2}-\frac{a^{\prime 2}}{a^{4}}\right)\text{Re}\!\left(\alpha_{k,\chi}\beta_{k,\chi}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{\chi}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{2a^{\prime}}{a^{3}}\text{Im}\!\left(\alpha_{k,0}\beta_{k,0}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{0}}\right)+\frac{2a^{\prime}}{a^{3}}\text{Im}\!\left(\alpha_{k,\chi}\beta_{k,\chi}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{\chi}}\right)\bigg]\;.

(24)

Assuming
\beta_{k,a}
falls faster than
k^{-2}
, integrating the terms in Eq. (
24
) will yield finite results, even if
\Lambda\to\infty
, and hence the massless limit can be freely taken inside the integral.
One finds from substitution of Eqs. (
15
), (
18
), and (
19
) into Eq. (
8
) that
\alpha_{k,0}^{\prime}=\alpha_{k,\chi}^{\prime}
and
\beta_{k,0}^{\prime}=\beta_{k,\chi}^{\prime}
after taking the massless limit, which when combined with Eq. (
13
) allows one to choose the coefficients for the
0
and
\chi
contributions to be identical.
The mode term contribution to the energy density therefore drastically simplifies to

\rho_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}dk\,k^{3}\left|\beta_{k,T}\right|^{2}\;.

(25)

The ultraviolet cutoff can now be removed, and we take the limit
\Lambda\to\infty
.
The renormalized energy density ultimately only depends on the transverse mode functions, which are the only physical modes of the theory, and higher order dependencies on the background curvature:

\displaystyle\rho_{r}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\;k^{3}\left|\beta_{k,T}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2880\pi^{2}}\left[62\,{}^{(3)}H_{00}+\left(3+\frac{5}{2}\ln\xi\right)\,{}^{(1)}H_{00}\right]\;.

(26)

Assuming the higher order corrections are subdominant in the semiclassical approximation below the Planck scale,
\rho_{r}
for massless vector fields does have the expected radiation-dominated behavior.
This result agrees with the prediction in
Wald 1978
that other than the anomalous term all of the contributions in a flat FLRW metric of a conformally invariant field will act like radiation.

The higher-order terms in (
26
) are of the same form as those found for the trace anomaly in
Endo 1984
;
Chu and Koyama 2017
, in which the
{}^{(1)}H_{00}
term has a gauge-dependent coefficient.
This coefficient corresponds to a
\Box R
term appearing in the trace anomaly.
The exact value of this coefficient is dependent on the regularization scheme used, unlike for scalar and spin-
\frac{1}{2}
fields which respectively have the same coefficient regardless of regularization scheme.

III
Energy Density for Dirac Fields

We now turn our attention to Dirac spinor fields in the preinflationary era of an FLRW universe.
We follow the work and notation given in Ref.
del Rio et al. 2014
.
There, expressions are in terms of cosmic time
t
, related to conformal time by
a\,d\eta=dt
.
We summarize the procedure for obtaining the unrenormalized energy density here.

Consider Dirac spinor fields
\Psi(x)
that obey the Dirac equation in curved spacetime,

\left(i\gamma^{a}e^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\mu}}}_{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.95248pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.10892pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}\nabla_{\mu}-m\right)\Psi=0\;,

(27)

where
e^{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\mu}}}_{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.95248pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.10892pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}
is the vierbein,
\gamma^{a}
are the flat spacetime Dirac matrices satisfying
\{\gamma^{a},\gamma^{b}\}=2\eta^{ab}
, and
\nabla_{\mu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}+\Gamma_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative associated with the spin connection
\Gamma_{\mu}
.
For the metric (
1
), the Dirac equation (
27
) becomes

\left[\gamma^{0}\left(\partial_{\eta}+\frac{3a^{\prime}}{2a}\right)+\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}+ima\right]\Psi=0\;.

(28)

The field can be written in terms of creation and annihilation operators
D^{\dagger}_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\eta)
and
B_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\eta)
as

\Psi=\sum_{\lambda=\pm\frac{1}{2}}\int d^{3}k\left(B_{\vec{k}\lambda}\psi_{\vec{k}\lambda}+D^{\dagger}_{\vec{k}\lambda}C\bar{\psi}_{\vec{k}\lambda}^{\intercal}\right)\;,

(29)

where
C
is the charge conjugation matrix,
\lambda=\pm 1/2
represents the helicity eigenvalue, and

\left\{B_{\vec{k},\lambda},B_{\vec{k},\lambda}^{\dagger}\right\}=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{k}-\vec{k}^{\prime})

(30)

and similarly for
D_{\vec{k}\lambda}
and
D_{\vec{k}\lambda}^{\dagger}
, with all other anticommutators vanishing.
Working in the Dirac-Pauli representation for
\gamma^{a}
, the modes
\psi_{\vec{k}\lambda}
can be written as

\psi_{\vec{k}\lambda}(\eta,\vec{x})=\frac{e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}}{\sqrt{8\pi^{3}a^{3}}}\begin{pmatrix}h^{I}_{k}(\eta)\xi_{\lambda}(\vec{k})\;,\\
h^{II}_{k}(\eta)\hat{k}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\xi_{\lambda}(\vec{k})\end{pmatrix}\;,

(31)

where
\xi_{\lambda}(\vec{k})
are two-component spinors and are eigenvectors of the spin component along the
\vec{k}
direction, so that
\frac{1}{2}(\hat{k}\cdot\vec{\sigma})\xi_{\lambda}(\vec{k})=\lambda\xi_{\lambda}(\vec{k})
, with normalization
\xi^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\xi_{\lambda}=1
, and
h^{I}_{k}
and
h^{II}_{k}
are scalar functions that satisfy coupled first-order differential equations

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}h^{I}_{k}(\eta)

\displaystyle=-ikh^{II}_{k}(\eta)-ima(\eta)h^{I}_{k}(\eta)\;,

(32a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}h^{II}_{k}(\eta)

\displaystyle=-ikh^{I}_{k}(\eta)+ima(\eta)h^{II}_{k}(\eta)\;,

(32b)

and have the normalization

\left|h^{I}_{k}(\eta)\right|^{2}+\left|h^{II}_{k}(\eta)\right|^{2}=1\;.

(33)

The energy density for the Dirac field in terms of the mode functions
h_{k}^{I,II}
can be written as

\rho=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkk^{2}\left[ma\left(\left|h^{II}_{k}\right|^{2}-\left|h^{I}_{k}\right|^{2}\right)-k\left(h^{I}_{k}h^{II*}_{k}+h^{I*}_{k}h^{II}_{k}\right)\right]\;.

(34)

At this point, we will diverge from this procedure coming from
del Rio et al. 2014
, who themselves proceeded to obtain counterterms to a generic unrenormalized energy in an FLRW universe.
These counterterms were then used to prove conservation of the energy density and were applied to a de Sitter spacetime and a radiation-dominated universe.
As demonstrated there, one can make use of adiabatic regularization to renormalize the energy density, but the ansatz used for the WKB approximation of adiabatic states must be a modified form of the standard ansatz.
This renormalization procedure was first demonstrated in
Landete et al. 2013
and has been applied in other cases
Landete et al. 2014
;
Torrentí 2015
;
Barbero G. et al. 2018
;
Beltrán-Palau et al. 2020
.
Here, we will use the energy density (
34
) as well as the modified WKB ansatz to obtain renormalization counterterms, but we will use a different process to obtain an explicit form of the unrenormalized energy density.
Namely, we will expand the mode functions
h_{k}^{I,II}
in terms of adiabatic modes
g_{k}^{I,II}
via a Bogoliubov-like expansion in order to analyze early time behavior using the Bogoliubov coefficients.

First, one expands
h_{k}^{I,II}
in terms of adiabatic modes
g^{I,II}_{k}
,

\displaystyle h^{I}_{k}=\alpha_{k}g^{I}_{k}-\beta_{k}g_{k}^{II*}\;,

(35a)

\displaystyle h^{II}_{k}=\alpha_{k}g^{II}_{k}+\beta_{k}g_{k}^{I*}\;,

(35b)

where
g^{I,II}_{k}
are given to adiabatic order
A
,

g^{I,II}_{k}=g^{I,II(0)}_{k}+g^{I,II(1)}_{k}+\dots+g^{I,II(A)}_{k}\;,

(36)

with adiabatic order understood to be the number of conformal time derivatives,
and satisfy the differential equations (
32a
) and (
32b
), and
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
are
the time-dependent Bogoliubov coefficients and are constant to order
A
.
Coupled first order differential equations for
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
can be obtained from Eqs. (
32a
), (
32b
), and (
33
).
The unrenormalized energy density in terms of the adiabatic states is

\displaystyle\rho=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkk^{2}\bigg\{2\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}\left[ma\left(\left|g^{I}_{k}\right|^{2}-\left|g^{II}_{k}\right|^{2}\right)+2k\text{Re}\left(g^{I}_{k}g^{II*}_{k}\right)\right]+4ma\text{Re}\left(\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}^{*}g^{I}_{k}g^{II}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle+2k\text{Re}\left[\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}^{*}\left(\left(g^{II}_{k}\right)^{2}-\left(g^{I}_{k}\right)^{2}\right)\right]+ma\left(\left|g^{II}_{k}\right|^{2}-\left|g^{I}_{k}\right|^{2}\right)-2k\text{Re}\left(g^{I}_{k}g^{II*}_{k}\right)\bigg\}\;,

(37)

and the adiabatic renormalization counterterms are

\displaystyle\rho_{c}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkk^{2}\left[ma\left(\left|g^{II}_{k}\right|^{2}-\left|g^{I}_{k}\right|^{2}\right)-2k\text{Re}\left(g^{I}_{k}g^{II*}_{k}\right)\right]^{(4)}\;,

(38)

where
[\dots]^{(4)}
indicates that
g^{I}_{k}
,
g^{II}_{k}
are fourth order states.
In order for the energy density to be renormalized and all divergences eliminated, the adiabatic states in Eq. (
III
) must be at least of the adiabatic order at which the counterterms in Eq. (
38
) are divergent.

In order to obtain forms for the adiabatic states, one can obtain uncoupled second order equations from Eqs. (
32a
) and (
32b
),

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\partial_{\eta}-ima^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)g^{I}_{k}=0\;,

(39)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\eta}^{2}+\frac{a^{\prime}}{a}\partial_{\eta}+ima^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)g^{II}_{k}=0\;,

(40)

where

\omega^{2}\equiv k^{2}+m^{2}a^{2}\;,

(41)

and assume formal WKB series solutions, truncating at the desired adiabatic order.
However, Ref.
Landete et al. 2013
and later Refs.
Landete et al. 2014
;
del Rio et al. 2014
pointed out that the usual WKB ansatz does not satisfy the normalization condition (
33
), so one must use a modified WKB ansatz of the form

\displaystyle g^{I}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\omega+ma}{2\omega}}Fe^{-i\theta_{k}}\;,

(42a)

\displaystyle g^{II}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\omega-ma}{2\omega}}Ge^{-i\theta_{k}}\;,

(42b)

and the functions

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=1+F^{(1)}+\dots+F^{(A)}\;,

(43a)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=1+G^{(1)}+\dots+G^{(A)}\;,

(43b)

\displaystyle\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\int^{\eta}d\tilde{\eta}\left(\omega+\omega^{(1)}+\dots+\omega^{(A)}\right)

(43c)

are determined by repeated substitution of Eqs. (
42a
) and (
42b
) into Eqs. (
32a
), (
32b
), and (
33
).
There is an ambiguity in the exact forms following this method, but all local observables are independent of the ambiguity

, so one may fix the ambiguity by choosing
F^{(n)}(-m)=G^{(n)}(m)
for each order
n\geq 1
.
However, we are only interested in using zeroth order states, for which one obtains

\displaystyle g^{I}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\omega+ma}{2\omega}}e^{-i\theta_{k}}\;,

(44a)

\displaystyle g^{II}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\omega-ma}{2\omega}}e^{-i\theta_{k}}\;,

(44b)

which have a normalization from Eq. (
33
) of

\left|g^{I}_{k}\right|^{2}+\left|g^{II}_{k}\right|^{2}=1\;.

(45)

Note that we will continue writing
\theta_{k}
like we have in Eqs. (
44a
) and (
44b
) for simplicity and assume it to be understood that only the zeroth order term is kept.
Substituting Eqs. (
35a
) and (

) into the differential equations (

) and (

), one obtains differential equations for
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{-kma^{\prime}}{2\omega^{2}}\beta_{k}e^{2i\theta_{k}}\;,

(46)

\displaystyle\beta_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{kma^{\prime}}{2\omega^{2}}\alpha_{k}e^{-2i\theta_{k}}\;,

(47)

and substituting them into the normalization condition (
33
), one finds

\left|\alpha_{k}\right|^{2}+\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}=1\;.

(48)

III.2
Bounds on the Renormalized Energy Density

We will begin by splitting the integral into infrared and ultraviolet regions
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
by a cutoff
k_{c}
.
The infrared contribution is

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{k_{c}}dk\,k^{2}\left[2\omega\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}-\frac{k^{2}m^{2}a^{\prime 2}}{8\omega^{5}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{k_{c}}dk\,k^{2}\left[2k\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}+2\left(\omega-k\right)\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}-\frac{k^{2}m^{2}a^{\prime 2}}{8\omega^{5}}\right]\;,

(59)

and the ultraviolet contribution is

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{k_{c}}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}\left[2\omega\left|\beta_{k}\right|^{2}-\frac{k^{2}m^{2}a^{\prime 2}}{8\omega^{5}}\right]\;.

(60)

At time
\eta_{0}
, we define

B_{0}\equiv\int_{0}^{k_{c}}dk\,2k^{3}\left|\beta_{k}(\eta_{0})\right|^{2}\;.

(61)

If
B_{0}
is the dominant contribution to the renormalized energy density at time
\eta
, then
\rho_{r}\propto a^{-4}
as desired.
However, as one may anticipate given the apparent logarithmically divergent term in Eq. (
52
), this may not always be the case.
We will investigate this in the following sections.
Given that
\left|\beta_{k}(\eta_{0})\right|\leq 1
from Eq. (
48
), one finds from Eq. (

of

k_{c}\gtrsim B_{0}^{1/4}\;.

(62)

III.2.1
Ultraviolet Region

One is tempted to assume
\beta_{k}\rightarrow 0
sufficiently quickly at high
k
, as is often done with scalar fields
Anderson et al. 2020
.
However, because the final term in Eq. (
60
) produces a logarithm divergence, it is evident that doing so will introduce a divergent contribution to the energy density.
This situation is occuring because until now we have been working with a zeroth order parameterization of the states, but because the logarithmic divergence comes in at higher than zeroth adiabatic order, one would expect to need to work with at least a second order parameterization to eliminate this higher-order divergence.
These problems would indeed disappear working with a higher-order parameterization, but it becomes much more difficult to analytically obtain generic bounds on
I_{2}
doing so.

One can instead continue to work with a zeroth-order parameterization using
\alpha_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
.
We can understand what the appropriate higher order states look like at large

by integrating Eq. (
47

\beta_{k}
will take the form

\beta_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}-\frac{ikma^{\prime}}{4\omega^{3}}\alpha_{k}e^{-2i\theta_{k}}\;.

(63)

\bar{\beta}_{k}
, defined as

\bar{\beta_{k}}\equiv\beta_{k}+\frac{ikma^{\prime}}{4\omega^{3}}\alpha_{k}e^{-2i\theta_{k}}\;,

(64)

We then anticipate that
\bar{\beta}_{k}
will fall faster than the leading order term at large

\left|\bar{\beta}_{k}(\eta_{0})\right|<\frac{A_{0}}{k^{2}}\left(\frac{k_{c}}{k}\right)^{b_{0}}

, some

independent of

The ultraviolet integral (

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{k_{c}}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}\left[\left(1-\frac{k^{2}m^{2}a^{\prime 2}}{16\omega^{6}}\right)\left(2\omega\left|\bar{\beta}_{k}\right|^{2}+\frac{kma^{\prime}}{\omega^{2}}\text{Im}\!\left(\alpha_{k}\bar{\beta}_{k}^{*}e^{-2i\theta_{k}}\right)\right)-\frac{k^{4}m^{4}a^{\prime 4}}{128\omega^{11}}\left|\alpha_{k}\right|^{2}\right]\;.

\displaystyle a\ll B_{0}^{1/4}m^{-2/3}\;,

\displaystyle B_{0}\gg\left(\frac{m^{2}a^{\prime 2}}{k_{c}^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle B_{0}\gg\left(\frac{m^{3}a^{5}}{k_{c}^{3}}\right)

III.3
Friedmann Equation

The energy density is given by the sum of the contribution from the infrared and ultraviolet regions.
As shown in Secs.
III.2.1
–
III.2.2
, provided one is looking early enough such that Eq. (

and the logarithm in Eq. (

\displaystyle\rho_{r}\approxeq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\left[B_{0}+\frac{1}{4}m^{2}a^{\prime 2}\ln\left(\frac{ma}{2k_{c}}\right)\right]\;.

(90)

Using the Friedmann equation

\left(\frac{a^{\prime}}{a^{2}}\right)^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho_{r}

(91)

a^{\prime 2}\approxeq\frac{8B_{0}}{3\pi}\left[1-\frac{2}{3\pi}m^{2}\ln\left(\frac{ma}{2k_{c}}\right)\right]^{-1}\;.

(92)

The renormalized energy density can be written using (

\rho_{r}\approxeq\frac{B_{0}}{\pi^{2}a^{4}}\left\{1+\frac{2}{3\pi}m^{2}\left[\ln\left(\frac{2k_{c}}{ma_{0}}\right)-\ln\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)\right]\right\}^{-1}\;.

However, limits such as (

Of course, realistic models would not only have fermion fields but also an inflaton field, which would look like a cosmological constant, but, until the mass term becomes important at

IV
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper we have analyzed the early-Universe, preinflationary behavior of massless vector fields of spin-1 and massive or massless fermion fields of spin-
\frac{1}{2}
in the semiclassical approximation.
We showed for a range of conformal time after the Planck era that both types of fields have radiation-dominated behavior.
Along with the same result obtained for scalar fields in
Anderson et al. 2020
, we have demonstrated that all matter fields which one might anticipate to play a role in the preinflationary era do in fact produce a radiation-dominated energy density that is typically assumed in inflationary models.

In Sec.
II
, we summarized the adiabatic renormalization procedure for a massless vector field in a spatially flat FLRW universe, following the groundwork laid out in
Chu and Koyama 2017
.
We then used this procedure to renormalize the energy density contribution for such a field following a parameterization of the mode functions in terms of adiabatic states.
We found the renormalized energy density to have a radiation-dominated form similar to that for the scalar field in
Anderson et al. 2020
.

In Sec.
III
, we summarized a modified version of the adiabatic renormalization procedure for a massive or massless fermion field, using the modified WKB ansatz given in
Landete et al. 2013
.
We found that parameterizing the mode functions in terms of adiabatic states required higher than zeroth order contributions in order to properly renormalize the energy density at high energies.
We then made use of the leading second adiabatic-order contributions to the parameterization coefficients to obtain the leading order behavior of such high-energy contributions and used this to show the high-energy contributions are in fact subdominant to the radiation-like term in the remaining energy density contributions given a set of constraints on the scale factor.
These constraints are more stringent than those required for the scalar field
Anderson et al. 2020
, for which only a constraint on
(a^{2})^{\prime\prime}
was necessary.
We then found the next-to-leading order behavior of the energy density to be a logarithmic contribution and showed that it is subdominant compared to the radiation-dominated energy density for a the range of conformal time beyond the Planck era, where the constraint on the scale factor coming from the analysis of the high-energy contribution served as the upper bound on the range.

Our anaylsis of the fermion field assumed that fermionic matter was the dominant contribution to the energy density in order to make use of the Friedmann equation to obtain the approximate leading order behavior.
This result for fermions is different than that for scalars
Anderson et al. 2020
and vectors, which work in any case.
Our argument for fermions also only applies for the range
a_{0}<a<a_{1}
described in Sec.
III
, though we expect this range to be in the preinflationary era prior to the fields becoming matter dominated.
This result is weaker than those for the other two fields, but in application it is not.
One would not be able to observe these matter fields during this era directly but rather through their effects on other phenomena, such as in the Cosmic Microwave Background, so the results are not necessarily weaker in application.

Having demonstrated in the semiclassical approximation that the matter content in the post-Planck, preinflationary era will indeed be radiation-dominated, one can proceed with the procedure for the inflaton field in
Anderson et al. 2020
.
There, it was assumed that spin-
\frac{1}{2}
and spin-
1
massive fields could be modeled with conformally coupled massive scalar fields, which were argued to be radiation-dominated themselves in the preinflationary era.
Our results that spin-
\frac{1}{2}
fermion fields and massless spin-
1
vector fields are radiation-dominated, themselves, supports the procedure in
Anderson et al. 2020
, and the analysis for obtaining a renormalized energy density for a universe with a mixture of cosmological constant and classical radiation, and hence for obtaining the power spectrum of the Cosmic Microwave Background, is identical.
