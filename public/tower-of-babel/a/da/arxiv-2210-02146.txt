Centrality and the commutativity of finite products with coequalisers
Michael Hoefnagel

Abstract
We study centrality of morphisms in a setting derived from that of a pointed category in which finite products commute with coequalisers. The main results of this paper show that much of the behaviour of central morphisms for unital categories [ 5 ] is retained in our setting, including categories which are (weakly) unital, but also categories outside of the unital setting.

Proposition 1
.

The following are equivalent for a pointed category with binary products:

(i)

for any
f\colon X\times X\rightarrow Y
we have
f(x,0)=f(x^{\prime},0)
implies
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y)
,

(ii)

for any
f\colon X\times Y\rightarrow Z
we have
f(x,0)=f(x^{\prime},0)
implies
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y)
,

(iii)

for any
f\colon X\times Y\rightarrow Z
we have
f(x,0)=f(x^{\prime},0)
and
f(0,y)=f(0,y^{\prime})
implies
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

Proof.

For
(i)\implies(ii)
, consider the morphism
\alpha\colon(X\times Y)\times(X\times Y)\rightarrow Z\times Z
defined by
\alpha((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=(f(x,y^{\prime}),f(x^{\prime},y))
. Then
\alpha((x,0),(0,0))=\alpha((x^{\prime},0),(0,0))
so that by
(i)
we get
\alpha((x,0),(0,y))=\alpha((x^{\prime},0),(0,y))
and hence
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y)
. For
(ii)\implies(iii)
we have
f(x,0)=f(x^{\prime},0)\implies f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y)
and
f(0,y)=f(0,y^{\prime})\implies f(x^{\prime},y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
so that
f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Then
(iii)\implies(i)
is trivial.
∎

The notion of a
majority category
has been defined in the paper
[
17
]
(see also
[

between sets

given by

(x,y,z^{\prime})\in R\quad\text{and}\quad(x,y^{\prime},z)\in R\quad\text{and}\quad(x^{\prime},y,z)\in R\implies(x,y,z)\in R.

Then a category
\mathbb{C}
is a
majority category
if every internal ternary relation in
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the above condition internalised (via the Yoneda embedding) to internal ternary relations in
\mathbb{C}
(see
[
17
]
for the details). Majority categories capture, in a categorical way, what it means for a variety of universal algebras to admit a
majority
term, i.e., a ternary term

satisfying the equations

\displaystyle m(x,x,y)=x,

\displaystyle m(x,y,x)=x,

\displaystyle m(y,x,x)=x.

For instance, in the variety
\mathsf{Lat}
of lattices, the term
m(x,y,z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)\vee(y\wedge z)
or its lattice-theoretic dual both define majority terms. In a variety of rings satisfying the identity

for some

(such as a variety of rings generated by a finite field for example), then the term
m(x,y,z)=x-(x-y)(x-z)^{n-1}
defines a majority term.

Proposition 11
.

Every finitely complete pointed majority category is centralic.

Proof.

Given a morphism
f\colon X\times Y\rightarrow Z
with
f(x,0)=f(x^{\prime},0)
we define the ternary relation
R
between
X
and
X
and
Y\times Y

by

(x,x^{\prime},(y,y^{\prime}))\in R\Longleftrightarrow f(x,y)=f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Then we have

(x,x^{\prime},(0,0))\in R,\quad(x,x,(y,y))\in R,\quad(x^{\prime},x^{\prime},(y,y))\in R\implies(x,x^{\prime},(y,y))\in R,

and hence

∎

is) and the morphisms

and
\rho_{Y}(f\times f)
define cooperators for

by Proposition

∎

Proposition 23
.

Every commutative object in
\mathbb{C}
is the underlying object of an internal commutative monoid in
\mathbb{C}
(which is necessarily unique).

Proof.

By the classical Eckmann-Hilton argument
[
10
]
a unitary magma
(X,\rho_{X})
is a commutative monoid if and only if its multiplication
\rho_{X}
is a morphism of the unitary magmas
(X\times X,(\rho_{X}\times\rho_{X})m)
and
(X,\rho_{X})
, where
m\colon(X\times X)^{2}\rightarrow(X\times X)^{2}
is the middle interchange isomorphism
m\colon((x,y),(a,b))\mapsto((x,a),(y,b))
. Thus the result follows by Proposition
22
.
∎

Then Proposition
22
and Proposition
23
give us the following

Corollary 24
.

The full subcategory
{\mathsf{Com}}(\mathbb{C})
of commutative objects in
\mathbb{C}
is equivalent to the category
{\mathsf{CMon}}(\mathbb{C})
of internal commutative monoids in
\mathbb{C}
.

For any commutative object

\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Commutativity of binary products and coequalisers

Throughout this section, we suppose that
\mathbb{C}
is a category with binary products and coequalisers which commute. Further, we will suppose that
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the condition
{\mathsf{(T)}}
. By Proposition

\mathbb{C}
is strongly centralic. For each object

\mathbb{C}
consider a coequaliser

and

which gives us the diagram:

\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q_{X}}

\scriptstyle{\rho_{X}}

Proposition 29
.

The subcategory
{\mathsf{Com}}(\mathbb{C})
is a Birkhoff subcategory of

, i.e., it is a reflective subcategory of
\mathbb{C}
which is closed under products, subobjects and regular quotients. Moreover the reflection

.

The category

is a reflective subcategory of

Proof.

Suppose that

Every pointed factor permutable category is strongly centralic.

Concluding remarks

We leave the investigation of what may be called

[

for any elements

of

. This can be seen as a special case of the shifting lemma:

\scriptstyle{\Theta}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{Eq}(p_{2})}

\scriptstyle{\mathsf{Eq}(p_{1})}
\textstyle{(x,v)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Theta}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{Eq}(p_{2})}
\textstyle{(y,v)}

This observation was the main motivation behind this paper. We also remark here that some of the results in this paper took inspiration from the paper
[
8
]
— see Proposition 2.2 (and its proof) therein. Note that categorical conditions similar to some of the equivalent statements of Proposition
