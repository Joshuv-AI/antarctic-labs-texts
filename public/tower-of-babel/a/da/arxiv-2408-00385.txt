Quantitative Group Testing and Pooled Data in the Linear Regime with Sublinear Tests
Nelvin Tan, Pablo Pascual Cobo, Ramji Venkataramanan

Abstract
In the pooled data problem, the goal is to identify the categories associated with a large collection of items via a sequence of pooled tests. Each pooled test reveals the number of items in the pool belonging to each category. A prominent special case is quantitative group testing (QGT), which is the case of pooled data with two categories. We consider these problems in the non-adaptive and linear regime, where the fraction of items in each category is of constant order. We propose a scheme with a spatially coupled Bernoulli test matrix and an efficient approximate message passing (AMP) algorithm for recovery. We rigorously characterize its asymptotic performance in both the noiseless and noisy settings, and prove that in the noiseless case, the AMP algorithm achieves almost-exact recovery with a number of tests sublinear in the total number of items p p . Although there exist other efficient schemes for noiseless QGT and pooled data that achieve recovery with order-optimal sample complexity ( Θ ⁡ ( p log ⁡ p ) \Theta(\frac{p}{\log p}) tests), there are no guarantees on their performance in the presence of noise, even at low noise-levels. In comparison, our scheme achieves recovery in the noiseless case with a number of tests sublinear in p p , and its performance degrades gracefully in the presence of noise. Numerical simulations illustrate the benefits of the spatially coupled scheme at finite dimensions, showing that it outperforms i.i.d. test designs as well as other recovery algorithms based on convex programming.

Introduction

Consider a large collection of items, each of which is either defective or non-defective. In
group testing

[
1
]
, the goal is to identify the defective set via pooled tests, where groups of items are tested together, with as few tests as possible. In the original Boolean group testing model, which has been studied extensively
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
, each test returns a positive outcome if it includes at least one defective item and a negative outcome otherwise. Its variant, the
quantitative group testing
(QGT) model
[
6
]
, is useful when tests are more informative: each test reveals the number of defective items in that pool. QGT is of interest in a range of modern applications, including genomics
[
7
]
, multi-access communication
[
8
]
, and network traffic monitoring
[
9
]
.

A more general version of QGT, where each item belongs to one of
L>2
categories, is known as the
pooled data
problem
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. The goal is to identify the categories via a sequence of pooled tests, where each pooled test reveals the number of items of each category within the pool. The pooled data problem is essentially one of inferring categorical information from histogram queries, which has applications in medical testing and learning with privacy constraints
[
15
]
.

In this paper, we consider
non-adaptive
QGT and pooled data, where the tests are all designed in advance, making them amenable to being implemented in parallel. We also consider the
linear
regime, which for QGT, means that the number of defective items is proportional to the total number of items. For pooled data, the linear regime implies that the proportion of items in each category is non-vanishing as the number of items increases, a realistic assumption in practical applications.

1.1
Problem Setup

Quantitative group testing.

There are
p
items, whose labels are denoted using the binary vector
\beta\in\{0,1\}^{p}
, where
1
represents a defective item and
0
a non-defective item. Items are allocated to tests using a binary design (or test) matrix
X\in\{0,1\}^{n\times p}
, where
n
is the number of tests and
p
is the number of items. The
i
th row
X_{i,:}
determines the pooling design of the
i
th test, where
X_{ij}=1
indicates that the
j
th item will be included in the
i
th test, and
X_{ij}=0
indicates otherwise. Let
d
be the number of defective items with
d<p
. We consider the linear regime, where the fraction of defective items
d/p
converges to
\pi\in(0,1)
. Mathematically, we define the QGT model as

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(X_{i,:}\big)^{\top}\beta+\Psi_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in\{1,\dots,n\},

(1)

where
y_{i}
is the
i
th element of
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
X_{i,:}
is the
i
th row of
X\in\{0,1\}^{n\times p}
represented as a column vector, and
\Psi_{i}
is the
i
th element of the additive noise
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Under the noiseless setting (i.e., all entries of
\Psi
are zero), the output
y_{i}
is the number of defective items in the
i
th test. The goal of QGT is to recover
\beta
with as few tests as possible.

We will use the
almost-exact
recovery criterion, which is achieved by an estimator
\tilde{\beta}
if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}\rightarrow 0\text{ as $p\rightarrow\infty$.}

(2)

This is a weaker notion of recovery as compared to the exact recovery criterion
[
12
]
where we want the probability of error
\mathbb{P}\big[\tilde{\beta}\neq\beta\big]\rightarrow 0
as
p\rightarrow\infty
. We note that an almost-exact recovery criterion is meaningful in the linear regime, but not in the sublinear regime where the number of defectives
d=o(p)
, since setting
\tilde{\beta}
to be the zero vector would trivially satisfy (
2
).

Almost-exact recovery is related to the
approximate
recovery criterion

[
12
]
which requires that, with high probability, the number of errors is at most
q_{\max}\equiv q_{\max}(p)
. More precisely, approximate recovery requires that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}>q_{\max}\right]\to 0\ \text{ as }\ p\to\infty.

(3)

Almost-exact recovery is at least as strong a criterion as approximate recovery when
q_{\max}/p
is either of constant order or is allowed to decay to zero arbitrarily slowly with
p
. Scarlett and Cevher derived information-theoretic lower bounds on the number of tests required for both exact and approximate recovery
[
12
, Theorem 3]
. These bounds show that for noiseless QGT, approximate recovery with
any

q_{\max}
such that
q_{\max}/p=o(1)
requires essentially the same number of tests as exact recovery (
q_{\max}=0
). In their words, “recovering all the labels is essentially as easy as recovering all but a vanishing fraction of the labels”. This provides further justification for using the almost-exact recovery criterion, beyond the fact that it enables a precise asymptotic analysis of our scheme.

Pooled data.

The signal to be estimated is a matrix
B\in\{0,1\}^{p\times L}
, where each row is a one-hot vector. For example,
B_{j,:}=[0,1,0,\dots,0]
represents the
j
th item belonging to category 2 (the position of one in
B_{j,:}
). We consider the linear regime, where the fraction of items in each category
l
converges to
\pi_{l}\in(0,1)
, where
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\pi_{l}=1
. The model is

\displaystyle Y_{i,:}=B^{\top}X_{i,:}+\Psi_{i,:}\quad\text{for $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$},

(4)

where
Y_{i,:}
is the
i
th row of
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
represented as a column vector, and
\Psi_{i,:}
is the
i
th row of the additive noise
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
represented as a column vector. Under the noiseless setting (i.e., all entries of
\Psi
are zero), the output of each test
Y_{i,:}
tells us the number of items from each category present in the test, which can be viewed as a histogram. Similarly to QGT, an estimator
\widetilde{B}
achieves almost-exact recovery if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{pL}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\big\{\widetilde{B}_{jl}\neq B_{jl}\big\}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }p\rightarrow\infty.

The number of categories
L
does not grow with
p
.

Information-theoretic limits.

For noiseless pooled data in the linear regime, the information-theoretic limit on the number of tests required was established by Scarlett and Cevher
[
12
]
, closing the gap between previously derived upper and lower bounds
[
16
,
10
,
14
]
. It was shown in
[
12
]
that the minimum number of tests needed for exact recovery is
n^{*}=\gamma^{*}\frac{p}{\log p}(1+o(1))
, where

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\{1,\dots,L-1\}}\frac{2[H(\pi)-H(\pi^{(r)})]}{L-r},

(5)

while
H(\pi)=-\sum_{l=1}^{L}\pi_{l}\log\pi_{l}
is the Shannon entropy function, and
\pi^{(r)}=(\pi_{1}^{(r)},\dots,\pi_{r}^{(r)})
is a vector whose first entry sums the largest
(L-r+1)
entries of
\pi
, and whose remaining entries coincide with the remaining
(r-1)
entries of
\pi
. Setting
L=2
above gives the information-theoretic limit for noiseless QGT.

Lower bounds on the number of tests for pooled data with approximate recovery were also derived in
[
12
]
. As mentioned above, these bounds show that even when we allow for a vanishing fraction of errors, the number of tests required is still
\gamma^{*}\frac{p}{\log p}(1+o(1))
(noting that the lower order terms may differ from the exact recovery case). That is, the minimum number of tests required for almost-exact recovery is essentially the same as that for exact recovery.

For noisy pooled data where the entries of the noise matrix are independent with zero mean, it was shown in
[
12
]
that we require
n=\Omega(p\log p)
tests for exact recovery, in contrast with the sublinear
\Theta\big(\frac{p}{\log p}\big)
required in the noiseless case. This lower bound was extended to the approximate recovery in
[
12
, Theorem 4]
to show that the number of tests must be of order at least
p
even if we allow a vanishing fraction of mistakes. Pooled data with adversarial noise was studied in
[
11
]
, and in this setting the number of tests required can be substantially higher than with random noise.

Efficient Algorithms.

For noiseless pooled data (including the special case of QGT), Wang et al.
[
10
]
proposed a deterministic test design and a polynomial-time algorithm that achieves exact recovery with
n=\Omega\big(\frac{p}{\log p}\big)
tests, matching the optimal sample complexity above. However, both the test design and the recovery algorithm (based on Gaussian elimination) are tailored to the noiseless setting, and the guarantees do not extend to the noisy case. In this paper, we focus on
random
test designs, which are more robust with respect to the items included in each test, and on recovery algorithms whose performance degrades gracefully with the noise level.

For random i.i.d. designs (where
X_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\text{Bernoulli}(\alpha)
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
), efficient recovery using approximate message passing (AMP) algorithms was studied in
[
14
,
17
]
. Rigorous guarantees on the recovery performance of AMP, established in
[
17
]
, imply that with the i.i.d. Bernoulli design, the AMP algorithm needs

n=\Theta(p)
tests for almost-exact recovery, even in the noiseless QGT/pooled data setting. The spatially coupled Bernoulli design we propose here improves on the i.i.d. one, enabling an AMP algorithm that requires only
n=o(p)
tests in the noiseless case.

For the
adaptive
setting, where each test can depend on the outcome of previous test, Bshouty
[
6
]
proposed an efficient noiseless QGT scheme that identifies
d
defectives out of
p
items with
\frac{2d\log(p/d)}{\log d}(1+o(1))
tests. This is just over twice the information-theoretic lower bound of
\frac{d\log(p/d)}{\log d}
for any adaptive scheme
[
6
]
. We focus only on non-adaptive schemes in this paper.

1.2
Approximate Message Passing and Spatial Coupling

Approximate message passing (AMP) is a family of iterative algorithms that can be tailored to take advantage of structural information about the signals and the model, e.g., a known prior on the signal vector or on the proportion of observations that come from each signal. AMP algorithms were first proposed for the standard linear model
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, but have since been applied to a range of statistical problems, including estimation in generalized linear models and their variants
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
, as well as low-rank matrix and tensor estimation
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. In all these settings, under suitable model assumptions, the performance of AMP in the high-dimensional limit is characterized by a succinct deterministic recursion called
state evolution
. The literature on AMP is vast, and we refer the interested reader to
[
34
]
for a survey.

In this paper, we will use a
spatially coupled
design matrix
X
and a suitable AMP algorithm for recovery. The spatially coupled matrix has a block-wise structure, with blocks along a band-diagonal having i.i.d. Bernoulli entries and the remaining blocks being all zeros (see Figure
1
). Our scheme is inspired by a line of work on compressed sensing with spatially coupled designs
[
35
,
21
,
36
,
37
]
. For a noiseless linear model defined via a spatially coupled Gaussian sensing matrix, Donoho et al.
[
37
]
showed that AMP recovers the signal with high probability when the sampling ratio
\delta=n/p
exceeds the Rényi information dimension of the signal prior. The Rényi information dimension is zero for priors supported on a finite set, which implies that AMP can recover the signal with
n=o(p)
measurements. Recently, it was also shown that using a spatially coupled sensing matrix in a generalized linear model allows AMP to achieve the Bayes-optimal error (corresponding to an i.i.d. Gaussian matrix)
[
38
]
.

1.3
Main Contributions

In Section
3
, we describe the spatially coupled random test design and an AMP algorithm (SC-AMP) for signal recovery. In Theorem
3.2
we give a precise characterization of the performance of SC-AMP in the asymptotic regime where the number of tests
n
grows proportionally with the number of items
p
(with
n/p\to\delta
, a constant). Using this characterization, we bound the MSE of the algorithm in the low noise regime (Theorem
3.7
) and show that for noiseless QGT, the SC-AMP algorithm achieves almost-exact recovery with probability one, for
any
constant sampling ratio
\delta>0
(Corollary
3.8
). This implies that it achieves almost-exact recovery with a sublinear number of tests, i.e.,
n=o(p)
. In Section
4
, we generalize the SC-AMP algorithm to the pooled data setting and again establish almost-exact recovery for any constant
\delta>0
(Theorem
4.1
).

To our knowledge, ours is the first efficient scheme for QGT and pooled data in the linear regime that both requires a sublinear number of tests in the noiseless case
and
is provably robust to noise. Indeed, Theorem
3.7
and Corollary
3.8
give bounds which quantify the noise sensitivity of the asymptotic MSE (and the fraction of errors). More generally, for any fixed values of noise variance
\sigma^{2}
and sampling ratio
\delta
, our results (Theorem
3.2
for QGT and (
46
) for pooled data) give a precise asymptotic characterization of the fraction of errors made by the SC-AMP algorithm.

Numerical simulations show that the spatially coupled scheme outperforms the i.i.d. Bernoulli test design with AMP, as well as recovery algorithms based on convex programming.

At the heart of our theoretical guarantees is a rigorous analysis of an AMP algorithm for a generalized linear model (GLM) with a generic spatially coupled design matrix. (The matrix consists of blocks of independent entries drawn from an arbitrary zero-mean distribution satisfying certain moment conditions.) GLMs include many important nonlinear estimation problems such as phase retrieval and logistic regression. Theorem
5.2
shows that the AMP algorithm and its performance characterization originally developed for GLMs with spatially coupled Gaussian designs
[
38
]
remain valid for a much broader class of designs.

Key technical ideas.

Although the QGT model (
1
) is an instance of a linear model, an important constraint is that the test design matrix
X
can only contain binary entries. Therefore, we cannot apply the analysis from
[
37
]
, which assumes a spatially coupled Gaussian design matrix. To prove theoretical guarantees for our scheme, we reduce the SC-AMP algorithm to an abstract AMP iteration defined for any generalized white noise matrix. A state evolution result for this abstract AMP iteration was established by Wang et al. in
[
39
]
, using which we obtain a rigorous asymptotic characterization of the SC-AMP algorithm (Theorem
3.2
). To establish conditions for almost-exact recovery, we then need to analyze the fixed points of the SC-AMP state evolution. We do this in Theorem
3.5
via the potential function method
[
40
]
, a powerful tool for characterizing the fixed points of coupled recursions. This characterization then yields the noise robustness and exact-recovery results (Theorem
3.7
and Corollary
3.8
).

1.4
Other Related Work

Spatial coupling.

Spatial coupling was introduced in coding theory as a means to construct LDPC codes that achieve capacity with an efficient belief propagation decoder
[
41
,
42
]
. Spatial coupling has since been applied in many estimation problems to improve on the performance of ‘regular’ (or i.i.d.) designs. For Boolean group testing in the sublinear regime (the number of defectives is
p^{\theta}
for
\theta\in(0,1)
), spatially coupled test designs enable efficient recovery with the asymptotically optimal number of tests, in both the noiseless
[
43
]
and noisy settings
[
44
]
.
For QGT, Mashauri et al.
[
45
,
46
]
investigated efficient schemes based on spatially coupled LDPC codes, and showed that they outperform previous constructions based on generalized LDPC codes
[
47
,
48
]
.

Sublinear category regime.

A few recent works have studied pooled data in the sublinear category regime, where one category is dominant with
p-o(p)
items, and the remaining
(L-1)
categories have
d=o(p)
items. (In contrast, we consider the linear category regime, where the proportion of items in each category is
\Theta(1)
, i.e.,
\pi_{l}=\Theta(1)
for
l\in[L]
.)
For the sublinear category regime, the information-theoretic lower bound for exact recovery is
n=\Omega(d)
tests
[
49
,
50
]
. An efficient algorithm proposed in
[
49
]
achieves the lower bound when
d=\Theta(p^{\kappa})
, for a constant
\kappa\in(0,1)
. A lower complexity algorithm for the special case of QGT with
d=\Theta(p^{\kappa})
was recently proposed in
[
51
]
. For QGT in the sublinear regime, a number of recent works have proposed algorithms based on ideas from coding theory
[
45
]
and thresholding
[
52
]
, which require
\Omega(d\log p)
tests for exact recovery. Noisy versions of QGT were recently studied in
[

]
and
[

]
, and QGT in the adaptive setting has been studied in
[

,

]
.

Almost-sure and Wasserstein convergence.

Let

be a sequence of random elements taking values in a Euclidean space

converges almost surely to a deterministic limit

For a vector

and a random variable

, we write

as

. More generally, for vectors

\displaystyle a^{1},\dots,a^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W_{r}}}{{\rightarrow}}(A^{1},\dots,A^{k})\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

\displaystyle|\phi(a_{i}^{1},\dots,a_{i}^{k})|\leq C\big(1+\|(a_{i}^{1},\dots,a_{i}^{k})\|_{2}^{r}\big),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(a^{1}_{i},\dots,a^{k}_{i})\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(A^{1},\dots,A^{k})\big]\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

\mathsf{r}(\cdot):[n]\rightarrow[\mathsf{R}]
and
\mathsf{c}(\cdot):[p]\rightarrow[\mathsf{C}]
map a particular row or column index to its corresponding
row block
or
column block
index in
W
. The band-diagonal structure of the
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrix here is similar to the ones used for SC sparse regression codes
[
56
]
and for SC generalized linear models
[
38
]
, but the values of the non-zero entries are different. Here the base matrix specifies the Bernoulli parameters for each block of the design, whereas in
[
56
,
38
]
it specifies the variances for the Gaussian entries in each block.

The
i.i.d.
design matrix, denoted by
X^{\text{iid}}
, has each entry sampled i.i.d.
\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\alpha)
, for some fixed constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Note that the i.i.d. matrix is a special case of the SC matrix, with
\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{C}=1
and
W=1
. A key difference between the i.i.d. design and the SC design (with an
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrix) is that the latter includes many fewer items in each test. Indeed, with the i.i.d. design each item is included in a test with probability
\alpha
, whereas in the SC design, each test includes items from at most
\omega
adjacent column blocks, each with
p/\mathsf{C}
items (see Figure
1
). In the SC design, since the tests corresponding to the first and last row blocks involve the fewest items, the corresponding entries of
\beta
are easier to recover than the others. A good estimate for these entries helps the algorithm recover the entries in the adjacent blocks, creating a decoding wave that propagates from the ends towards the center.

With the i.i.d. design, the number of defectives per test has expected value
\alpha\pi p
and standard deviation
\sqrt{\alpha\pi(1-\alpha\pi)p}
. Similarly, for the spatially coupled design, it can be verified that the number of defective items per test has mean that is linear in
p
and standard deviation of order
\sqrt{p}
. Since the fluctuation around the mean contains the useful information in each test, and because AMP requires a design matrix with zero-mean entries, we recenter and rescale the data before applying the AMP algorithm. We now describe this preprocessing of the data, which was also done in
[
17
]
for i.i.d. designs.

The
rescaled i.i.d.
matrix, denoted by
\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}

\displaystyle=\frac{X^{\text{iid}}-\alpha 1_{n}1_{p}^{\top}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}}.

(11)

We note that
\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}
has independent entries with
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}_{ij}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}[\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}_{ij}]=1/n
.

The
rescaled spatially coupled (SC)
matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
is defined as follows. For
i\in[n],j\in[p]
, using the shorthand
\mathsf{r}\equiv\mathsf{r}(i),\,\mathsf{c}\equiv\mathsf{c}(j)
, its entries are given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{ij}^{\text{sc}}

\displaystyle=\frac{X_{ij}^{\text{sc}}-\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}&\text{ with probability $\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}$,}\\
\frac{-\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}&\text{ with probability $1-\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}$.}\end{cases}

(12)

It is straightforward to verify that
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}]=0
,
\mathrm{Var}[\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}]=\frac{\mathsf{R}W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}(1-\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}})}{n(1-\alpha)}
. In particular, for the
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrix in Definition
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}]=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathsf{R}}{n\omega}&\text{ if $\mathsf{c}\leq\mathsf{r}\leq\mathsf{c}+\omega-1$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(13)

Rewriting QGT.

The AMP algorithm and its analysis require the design matrix to have independent zero-mean entries, so we recenter and rescale the QGT model in (
1
) to express it in terms of the rescaled design (
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
or
\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}
). For the SC design, using (
12
), we have for
i\in[n]
:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}X_{ij}^{\text{sc}}\beta_{j}+\Psi_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{c}(j)}+\sqrt{\frac{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\mathsf{R}}}\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}\right)\beta_{j}+\Psi_{i}

\displaystyle\implies\,

\displaystyle y_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{c}(j)}\beta_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\mathsf{R}}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}\beta_{j}+\Psi_{i}

\displaystyle\implies\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}\left(y_{i}-\alpha\left(W_{\mathsf{r}(i)1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\beta_{j}+\dots+W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{C}}}\beta_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{i,:})^{\top}\beta+\frac{\Psi_{i}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}},

where
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}=\big[(\mathsf{c}-1)p/\mathsf{C}+1:\mathsf{c}p/\mathsf{C}\big]
for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
. Denoting the left-hand side above by

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}\left(y_{i}-\alpha\left(W_{\mathsf{r}(i)1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}\beta_{j}+\dots+W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{C}}}\beta_{j}\right)\right),

(14)

gives us the rescaled QGT model:

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{i}=(\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{i,:})^{\top}\beta+\widetilde{\Psi}_{i},\quad\text{ with }\quad\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}:=\frac{\Psi_{i}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}\,,\quad i\in[n].

(15)

The term
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}}\beta_{j}
in (
14
) is the number of defective items in the sub-vector of
\beta
indexed by
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
, for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
.
In the noiseless setting, the terms
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{c}}\beta_{j}
can be obtained with an extra
\mathsf{C}
tests, where we only include items from
\mathcal{J}_{c}
in the
\mathsf{c}
th test, for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
. The extra
\mathsf{C}=O(1)
tests do not affect our results since the limiting sampling ratio
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n/p=\delta
remains the same.
Since
\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{c}}\beta_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}\frac{\pi}{\mathsf{C}}
for
p\rightarrow\infty
via the strong law of large numbers, we can also estimate
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}}\beta_{j}
using
\frac{p}{\mathsf{C}}\pi
. (However the error in this estimate would be of order
\sqrt{p/\mathsf{C}}
.)

For an i.i.d. design
X^{\text{iid}}
, we similarly recenter and rescale the QGT model to express it in terms of the rescaled i.i.d. matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}
in (
11
). For
i\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle y_{i}=\big(X_{i,:}^{\text{iid}}\big)^{\top}\beta+\Psi_{i}\implies

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{i}=\big(\widetilde{X}_{i,:}^{\text{iid}}\big)^{\top}\beta+\widetilde{\Psi}_{i},

where
\tilde{y}_{i}:=\frac{y_{i}-\alpha d}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}},\quad\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}=\frac{\Psi_{i}}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}}
,

(16)

where
d
is the number of defective items.

Choice of
\alpha
.

For a spatially coupled design
X^{\text{sc}}
constructed from an
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrix, recall from (
12
) that the rescaled matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
has independent zero-mean Bernoulli entries with variances given by (
13
).
Notice that the distribution of
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
does not depend on
\alpha
. Hence, in the rescaled model,
\alpha
only affects the variance of the noise
\widetilde{\Psi}
, which is minimized when
\alpha=0.5
. We therefore use
\alpha=0.5
for all our experiments.

Taking
\alpha=0.5
, it is useful to compare the SC design with the i.i.d. Bernoulli(
0.5
) design. (For the i.i.d. design, taking the Bernoulli parameter to be
0.5
is optimal with respect to both the information-theoretic limits
[
12
]
and efficient recovery via AMP
[
17
]
.) Taking
\alpha=0.5
in Definition
3.1
, we have that the non-zero blocks of the SC design are drawn independently from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
\frac{1}{2}\big(1-\sqrt{1-1/\omega}\big)
. From the structure of
X^{\text{sc}}
(see Figure
1
), it follows that the expected number of items included in each test is at most
\frac{p}{\mathsf{C}}\frac{\omega}{2}\big(1-\sqrt{1-1/\omega}\big)
. For large
\omega
, this is approximately
\frac{p}{4\mathsf{C}}
items per test. In contrast, for the Bernoulli(
0.5
) design, the expected number of items per test is
\frac{p}{2}
. Thus, for large
\mathsf{C}
, the expected number of items per test is much smaller for the SC design than the i.i.d. design.

Noise scaling assumption.

In the rescaled QGT models (
15
) and (
16
), it can be verified that the terms
\big(\widetilde{X}_{i,:}^{\text{sc}}\big)^{\top}\beta
and
\big(\widetilde{X}_{i,:}^{\text{iid}}\big)^{\top}\beta
have zero mean and variance of constant order, for each
i\in[n]
. Therefore, for the rescaled model to be meaningful, the noise
\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}
should also have a mean and variance of constant order. This is guaranteed by the following assumption.
The empirical distribution of rescaled noise vector
\widetilde{\Psi}
in (
15
) converges to a well defined limit. More precisely, there exists,
\bar{\Psi}\sim P_{\bar{\Psi}}
with
\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{\Psi}^{2}\big]=:\sigma^{2}<\infty
, such that

\widetilde{\Psi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W}}{{\rightarrow}}\bar{\Psi}

as
n\to\infty
. We emphasize that the base matrix parameter
\mathsf{R}
is fixed as
n\to\infty
. A similar distributional assumption holds for the rescaled noise vector with the i.i.d. design in (
16
).

3.1
SC-AMP Algorithm

Consider the rescaled SC model in (
15
).
Given
(\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}},\tilde{y})
, the SC-AMP algorithm iteratively produces signal estimates, denoted by
\hat{\beta}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, for
k\geq 1
. For iteration
k\geq 0
, the algorithm computes:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\Theta}^{k}&=\tilde{y}\,-\,\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\hat{\beta}^{k}\,+\,b^{k}\odot Q^{k-1}\odot\widetilde{\Theta}^{k-1}\,\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
\beta^{k+1}&=(\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}})^{\top}(Q^{k}\odot\widetilde{\Theta}^{k})\,-\,c^{k}\odot\hat{\beta}^{k}\,\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\qquad\hat{\beta}^{k+1}=f_{k+1}(\beta^{k+1},\mathcal{C})\in\,\mathbb{R}^{p},\end{split}

(17)

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard (entry-wise) product, the function
f_{k}:\mathbb{R}\times[\mathsf{C}]\to\mathbb{R}
acts row-wise on the inputs
(\beta^{k},\mathcal{C})
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{\text{$p/\mathsf{C}$ entries}},\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{{\text{$p/\mathsf{C}$ entries}}},\dots,\underbrace{\mathsf{C},\dots,\mathsf{C}}_{{\text{$p/\mathsf{C}$ entries}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(18)

The algorithm is initialized with
\hat{\beta}^{0}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\beta}]1_{p}
and
\widetilde{\Theta}^{0}=\tilde{y}-\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\hat{\beta}^{0}
, where from (
8
) we recall that
\bar{\beta}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\pi)
.
To define
b^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, we use the partitions
[p]=\bigcup_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
and
[n]=\bigcup_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}&=\left\{(\mathsf{c}-1)\frac{p}{\mathsf{C}}+1,\dots,\mathsf{c}\frac{p}{\mathsf{C}}\right\}\text{ for $\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]$},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}=\left\{(\mathsf{r}-1)\frac{n}{\mathsf{R}}+1,\dots,\mathsf{r}\frac{n}{\mathsf{R}}\right\}\text{ for $\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}]$}.\end{split}

(19)

Then, letting

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}:=\frac{W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}(1-\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}})}{1-\alpha}=\begin{cases}1/\omega&\text{ if $\mathsf{c}\leq\mathsf{r}\leq\mathsf{c}+\omega-1$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(20)

the entries of
b^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are

\displaystyle b_{i}^{k}=\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}}{n/\mathsf{R}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{c}}f^{\prime}_{k}(\beta_{j}^{k},\mathsf{c}),\quad i\in[n],

where
f^{\prime}_{k}
is the derivative with respect to the first argument.
The second equality in (
20
) follows from Definition
3.1
.

Next, we define the function
f_{k}:\mathbb{R}\times[\mathsf{C}]
and the vectors
Q^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},c^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
in (
17
). These are defined in terms of block-wise scalar state evolution parameters, denoted by
\chi^{k}_{\mathsf{c}}
, for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
, which are recursively computed, as described in (
24
)-(
25
) below. We let

f_{k}(s,\mathsf{c})=\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{\beta}\,\big|\,(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G=s\big],\quad\text{ for }\,\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}].

(21)

The vectors
Q^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
c^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
have a block-wise structure and are defined as follows. For
i\in[n]
,
j\in[p]
, recalling that

\mathsf{r}(i)
and
\mathsf{c}(j)
denote the respective row-block and column-block indices, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{i}^{k}=\left(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{c}}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\bar{\beta}-f_{k}(\,(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G,\,\mathsf{c}\,)\big)^{2}\Big]\right)^{-1},\qquad c_{j}^{k}=-(\chi_{\mathsf{c}(j)}^{k+1})^{2}.\end{split}

(22)

We note that the time complexity of each iteration in (
17
) is
O(np)
.

We now give some high-level intuition about the SC-AMP algorithm and its state evolution characterization. For this, we need some additional notation to handle the block-wise structure of the iterates. For
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
and
\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}]
, we define
\beta_{\mathsf{c}}:=\beta_{\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p/\mathsf{C}}
and
\hat{\beta}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}:=\hat{\beta}_{\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p/\mathsf{C}}
to be the
\mathsf{c}
th blocks of
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\hat{\beta}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
respectively, and
\Theta_{\mathsf{r}}:=\Theta_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n/\mathsf{R}}
and
\widetilde{\Theta}_{\mathsf{r}}^{k}:=\widetilde{\Theta}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n/\mathsf{R}}
to be the
\mathsf{r}
th blocks of
\Theta:=\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\widetilde{\Theta}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
respectively. Similar notation simplifications will be used for other vectors where
\mathsf{c}
and
\mathsf{r}
will replace
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}
in the subscripts of the vectors.

State evolution.

The ‘memory’ terms
b^{k}\odot Q^{k-1}\odot\widetilde{\Theta}^{k-1}
and
-c^{k}\odot\hat{\beta}^{k}
in (
17
) debias the iterates
\widetilde{\Theta}^{k}
and
\beta^{k+1}
, ensuring that their empirical distributions are accurately captured by state evolution in the high-dimensional limit. These iterates have a block-wise distributional structure. Recall from the model assumptions that the empirical distribution of the signal
\beta
converges to the law of
\bar{\beta}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\pi)
. Theorem
3.2
below shows that, for each
k\geq 1
and
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
, the empirical distribution of
\beta_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}
converges to the distribution of
(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G
, where
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian independent of
\bar{\beta}
, and the deterministic parameter
\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is defined below via the state evolution recursion. Under this distributional assumption for
\beta^{k}
, the denoising function
f_{k}
in (
21
) produces a Bayes-optimal (MMSE) estimate of
\beta
from
\beta^{k}
.

State evolution iteratively computes the parameter
\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
as follows, for
k\geq 1
. Letting

\displaystyle\delta_{\textup{in}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{n/\mathsf{R}}{p/\mathsf{C}}=\frac{\mathsf{C}}{\mathsf{R}}\delta\,,

(23)

given
\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}
for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
, we compute
\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k+1}
as:

\displaystyle(\chi^{k+1}_{\mathsf{c}})^{2}=\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\left(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\big(\bar{\beta}-f_{k}(\,(\chi_{\mathsf{c}^{\prime}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}^{\prime}}^{k}G,\,\mathsf{c}^{\prime}\,)\big)^{2}\bigg]\right)^{-1},

(24)

where
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent of
\bar{\beta}
, and
\sigma^{2}
is the variance defined in the noise scaling assumption on p.
3
.
The recursion is initialized with

(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{1})^{2}=\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\Big(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}^{\prime}}\text{Var}(\bar{\beta})\Big)^{-1},\quad\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}].

(25)

Theorem 3.2

(State evolution result for SC-AMP)
.

Consider the QGT model (
1
) with a spatially coupled design defined via the
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrix in Definition
3.1
.
Let the model assumptions in Section
2
and the noise scaling assumption (p.
3
) be satisfied. Then, for the SC-AMP algorithm in (
17
), run on the recentered and rescaled QGT model (
15
) with the denoising functions
f_{k}
in (
21
), we have the following convergence guarantee. For each
k\geq 0
and
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big(\beta_{\mathsf{c}},\,\beta_{\mathsf{c}}^{k+1}\big)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W_{2}}}{{\rightarrow}}\big(\bar{\beta},\,(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k+1})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k+1}G\big)\end{split}

(26)

almost surely as
n,p\rightarrow\infty
with
n/p\rightarrow\delta
. Here
\bar{\beta}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\pi)
and
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are independent, and the parameter
\chi^{k+1}_{\mathsf{c}}
is defined in (
24
).

The theorem is proved in Section
5
, where the SC-AMP algorithm is shown to be a special case of an AMP algorithm for a
generalized
linear model with a generic spatially coupled design. We prove Theorem
3.2
by establishing a state evolution result for this general AMP algorithm (Theorem
5.2
).

Performance measures.

Theorem
3.2
allows us to compute the limiting values of performance measures such as the mean-squared error (MSE) and the normalized squared correlation, via the convergence property in (
7
). The MSE of the AMP estimate
\hat{\beta}^{k}
satisfies the following almost surely, for
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\|\beta-\hat{\beta}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\bar{\beta}-f_{k}((\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G,\mathsf{c})\big)^{2}\right],

(27)

while the normalized squared correlation of the AMP estimate
\hat{\beta}^{k}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle\hat{\beta}^{k},\beta\rangle}{\|\hat{\beta}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|\beta\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\mathbb{E}[f_{k}((\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G,\mathsf{c})\cdot\bar{\beta}])^{2}}{(\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\mathbb{E}[f_{k}((\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G,\mathsf{c})^{2}])\cdot(\mathbb{E}[\bar{\beta}^{2}])},\quad k\geq 1.

(28)

We can also obtain formulas for the limiting values of the false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR).
The choice of
f_{k}
in (
21
) outputs a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, but we can obtain an estimate in
\{0,1\}^{p}
by thresholding the AMP iterate
\hat{\beta}^{K}
in the final iteration
K
to output a hard decision. For some chosen constant
\zeta
, let us define the hard decision to be

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\left\{\hat{\beta}_{j}^{K}>\zeta\right\}=\mathds{1}\left\{f_{K}\big(\beta_{j}^{K},\mathsf{c}\big)>\zeta\right\},\quad\text{for }j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}},\,\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}],

(29)

where the indicator function is applied component-wise to
\hat{\beta}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}
. That is, we declare large entries of
\hat{\beta}^{K}
to be one (i.e., defective) and small entries of
\hat{\beta}^{K}
to be zero (i.e., non-defective). Based on the above function, let us denote the estimated defective set as
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{j:\hat{\beta}_{j}^{K}>\zeta\right\}
.

The false positive rate (FPR) and the false negative rate (FNR) are defined as:

FPR

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\{\beta_{j}=0\cap j\in\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\}}{p-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{FNR}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\{\beta_{j}=1\cap j\notin\widehat{\mathcal{S}}\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}}.

(30)

Corollary 3.3
.

Under the same assumptions as for Theorem
3.2
, with a threshold
\zeta\in[0,1]
for the final iteration
K
, as
p\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\textup{FPR}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\mathbb{P}\big[f_{K}\big(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{K}G,\mathsf{c}\big)>\zeta\big]\ \text{ and }\ \textup{FNR}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\mathbb{P}\big[f_{K}\big(\big(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{K}\big)^{2}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{K}G,\mathsf{c}\big)\leq\zeta\big].

(31)

The result follows from Theorem
3.2
by applying the convergence property to suitable indicator functions. The proof uses the same steps as the analogous result for i.i.d. Bernoulli designs
[
17
, Corollary 5.2]
, and is omitted.

3.2
Almost-Exact Recovery

Given
\hat{\beta}^{k}
, the SC-AMP estimate after
k
iterations, let us define the quantized estimate to be

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}^{k}_{j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\hat{\beta}^{k}_{j}>0.5,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(32)

Then, recalling the almost-exact recovery criterion in (
2
), the SC-AMP algorithm
achieves almost-exact recovery if

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{k}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}=0
.

In this section, we show that the SC-AMP algorithm can attain almost-exact recovery with
n=o(p)
tests, by proving that it
attains almost-exact recovery
for any
\delta>0
(recall that
\delta=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n}{p}
). To this end, we introduce the
potential function
to analyze the asymptotic MSE of SC-AMP as
k\to\infty
. Potential functions are widely used to characterize the limiting MMSE and mutual information in high-dimensional estimation problems (see, e.g.,
[
57
,
58
]
). Here we will use it only to characterize the asymptotic MSE of the AMP algorithm, both with and without spatial coupling (see Theorem
3.5
).

Definition 3.4
.

For
b\in[0,\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\beta})]
,
\delta>0
, the scalar potential function for the rescaled QGT model is defined as

\displaystyle U(b;\delta):=-\delta\Big(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(b/\delta)+\sigma^{2}}\Big)+\delta\log\Big(1+\frac{b}{\delta\sigma^{2}}\Big)+2I\bigg(\bar{\beta};\sqrt{\frac{1}{(b/\delta)+\sigma^{2}}}\bar{\beta}+G\bigg).

(33)

Here the mutual information
I(\cdot;\cdot)
is computed with
\bar{\beta}\sim P_{\bar{\beta}}
independent of
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{\Psi}^{2}\big]
is the second moment of the rescaled noise (see
p.
3
).

Figure
2
plots the potential function for various values of
\delta
. The next theorem characterizes the limiting MSE of the AMP algorithm via the minimizers and stationary points of the potential function.
For clarity, we refer to the AMP algorithm under the rescaled i.i.d. QGT model in (
16
) as the iid-AMP algorithm, and the AMP algorithm under the rescaled spatially coupled QGT model in (
15
) as the SC-AMP algorithm. The SC-AMP algorithm in (
17
) reduces to iid-AMP with the trivial base matrix (
\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{C}=1
).

Theorem 3.5

(MSE of SC-AMP and iid-AMP)
.

Consider the QGT model (
1
), and let the model assumptions in Section

and the noise scaling assumption (p.

1.

Consider a spatially coupled design defined via an

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\|\beta-\hat{\beta}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}<\left(\max\Bigg\{\argmin_{b\in[0,\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\beta})]}U(b;\delta_{\textup{in}})\Bigg\}+\gamma\right)\frac{\Lambda+\omega}{\Lambda}.

(34)

2.

With an i.i.d. design (i.e.,
1\times 1
base matrix with

Theorem 3.7

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\|\beta-\hat{\beta}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}<4\delta\sigma^{2-2\Delta(1+\epsilon)/\delta}+\epsilon.

\epsilon,\gamma>0

Corollary 3.8

(SC-AMP achieves almost exact recovery for any
\delta>0
)
.

Consider the setting and assumptions of Theorem
3.7
. Let
\tilde{\beta}^{k}
be the quantized estimate produced from the SC-AMP iterate
\hat{\beta}^{k}
, according to (
32
). Then, almost surely we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{k}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}\leq 4\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\|\beta-\hat{\beta}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}<4\left(4\delta\sigma^{2-2\Delta(1+\epsilon)/\delta}+\epsilon\right).

(38)

In particular, for noiseless QGT we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{k}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}=0
almost surely for any
\delta>0
.

Proof.

From the definition of the quantized estimate in (
32
), it follows that for
j\in[p]
, we have
|\beta_{j}-\hat{\beta}_{j}^{k}|\geq(0.5)\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{k}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}
, which implies
\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{k}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}\leq 4|\beta_{j}-\hat{\beta}_{j}^{k}|^{2}
. This gives the first inequality in (
38
). The second inequality follows from Theorem
3.7
. The result for the noiseless case follows by setting
\sigma=0
, and taking a sequence
(\epsilon_{k})
such that
\epsilon_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. We note that
\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\tilde{\beta}_{j}^{k}\neq\beta_{j}\big\}
exists for each
k
by the state evolution result in Theorem
3.2
.
∎

for a linear model with a spatially coupled

, for sufficiently small noise variance

\delta

[

]
. The key difference is that we use a binary-valued SC design for the QGT model rather than the Gaussian one in

4
SC-AMP for Pooled Data

In this section we extend the SC-AMP algorithm to the pooled data model in (
4
) with the spatially coupled design
X^{\text{sc}}
defined in (
10
). We apply SC-AMP to a centered and rescaled version of the pooled model, as we did for QGT in (
15
). Recalling the decomposition
X_{ij}^{\text{sc}}=\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}+\sqrt{\frac{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\mathsf{R}}}\widetilde{X}_{ij}^{\text{sc}}
, we have

\displaystyle Y_{i,:}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}X_{ij}^{\text{sc}}B_{j,:}+\Psi_{i,:}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{c}(j)}+\sqrt{\frac{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\mathsf{R}}}\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}\right)B_{j,:}+\Psi_{i,:}

\displaystyle\implies\,

\displaystyle Y_{i,:}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{c}(j)}B_{j,:}=\sqrt{\frac{n\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\mathsf{R}}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}B_{j,:}+\Psi_{i,:}\,.

Defining

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{i,:}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}\left(Y_{i,:}-\alpha\left(W_{\mathsf{r}(i)1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}B_{j,:}+\dots+W_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{C}}}B_{j,:}\right)\right),

and
\widetilde{\Psi}_{i,:}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\alpha(1-\alpha)/\mathsf{R}}}\Psi_{i,:}
gives us the rescaled pooled data model:

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{i,:}

\displaystyle=B^{\top}\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{i,:}+\widetilde{\Psi}_{i,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{L},\quad\text{ for }i\in[n].

(41)

The sets
\big(\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}\big)_{\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]}
are defined in (
19
). In the noiseless setting, the terms
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{c}}B_{j,:}
can be obtained with an extra
\mathsf{C}=O(1)
tests, where the
\mathsf{c}
th test
only includes items from
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
.

Model and noise scaling assumptions.

The signal matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times L}
and the rescaled noise matrix
\widetilde{\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L_{\Psi}}
are both independent of the design matrix. As
p\to\infty
, we assume that
n/p\to\delta>0
. As
p,n\to\infty
, the empirical distributions of the rows of
B
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
each converge to well-defined limits. More precisely,
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W}}{{\rightarrow}}\bar{B}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W}}{{\rightarrow}}\bar{\Psi}
, for
L
-dimensional random vectors
\bar{B}\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi)
and
\bar{\Psi}\sim P_{\bar{\Psi}}
.

4.1
Matrix SC-AMP Algorithm

The goal is to recover
B
from
\widetilde{Y}
generated according to the rescaled model (
41
).
The matrix SC-AMP algorithm is initialized with
\widehat{B}_{j,:}^{0}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}]
for
j\in[p]
, and

\widetilde{\Theta}^{0}=\widetilde{Y}-\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\widehat{B}^{0}
. For iteration
k\geq 1
, we compute:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}^{k}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{Y}-\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\widehat{B}^{k}+U^{k},

\displaystyle B^{k+1}

\displaystyle=V^{k}+\widehat{B}^{k},\qquad\widehat{B}^{k+1}=f_{k+1}(B^{k+1},\mathcal{C}),

(42)

where
f_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{L}\times[\mathsf{C}]\to\mathbb{R}
acts row-wise on its input, and the vector
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is defined in (
18
).
Similarly to QGT, the function
f_{k}
and the matrices
U^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
,
V^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times L}
are defined in terms of block-wise state evolution parameters. Here, the key state evolution parameters are two sets of
L\times L
covariance matrices, denoted by
\phi_{\mathsf{r}}^{k}
for
\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}]
and
\mathrm{T}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}
for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
. These matrices are computed recursively as given in (
45
) below.

Recalling the partition in (
19
), the function

f_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{L}\times[\mathsf{C}]\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows, for
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}},\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
:

\displaystyle f_{k}(B_{j,:}^{k},\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{B}\big|\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}=B_{j,:}^{k}\big],\quad G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathrm{T}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\right)\text{ independent of }\bar{B}.

(43)

The matrices
U^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
,
V^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times L}
are defined in terms of a matrix
Q^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\mathsf{R}\times L\mathsf{C}}
, whose sub-matrices
Q_{\mathsf{r},\mathsf{c}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
for
\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}],\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
, are given by

\displaystyle Q_{\mathsf{r},\mathsf{c}}^{k}

\displaystyle=(\phi_{\mathsf{r}}^{k})^{-1}\left(\sum_{\mathsf{r}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}^{\prime}\mathsf{c}}(\phi_{\mathsf{r}^{\prime}}^{k})^{-1}\right)^{-1}.

The rows of the matrix
U^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
in (
42
) are defined as

\displaystyle U_{i,:}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n/\mathsf{R})}\widetilde{\Theta}_{i,:}^{k-1}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}(i),\mathsf{c}}\,Q_{\mathsf{r}(i),\mathsf{c}}^{k-1}\,\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}}f_{k}^{\prime}(B_{j,:}^{k},\mathsf{c})^{\top},\quad\text{for $i\in[n]$}

(44)

where
f_{k}^{\prime}
denotes the
L\times L
Jacobian of
f_{k}
with respect to the first argument. The rows of the matrix
V^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times L}
are given by

\displaystyle V_{j,:}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}\,\widetilde{\Theta}_{i,:}^{k}\,Q_{\mathsf{r}(i),\mathsf{c}(j)}^{k},\quad\text{for $j\in[p]$}.

State evolution.

The memory term
U^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
in the matrix SC-AMP (
42
) debiases the iterates and ensures that:
i
) for
\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}]
, the row-wise empirical distribution of
\widetilde{\Theta}^{k}_{\mathsf{r}}
converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,\phi^{k}_{\mathsf{r}})
, and
ii
) for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
, the row-wise empirical distribution of
B^{k}_{\mathsf{c}}
converges to the law of
(\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})
, where
G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{T}^{k}_{\mathsf{c}})
. The
L\times L
covariance matrices

\phi^{k}_{\mathsf{r}}
and
\mathrm{T}^{k}_{\mathsf{c}}
are iteratively computed as follows for
k\geq 0
, starting from the initialization
\psi_{\mathsf{c}}^{0}=\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{B})
, for
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phi_{\mathsf{r}}^{k}=\mathrm{Cov}[\bar{\Psi}]+\frac{1}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\psi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\,,\qquad\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}],\\
&\mathrm{T}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}={\left[\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}{[\phi_{\mathsf{r}}^{k}]}^{-1}\right]^{-1}},\qquad\psi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k+1}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(f_{k}\big(\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k},\mathsf{c}\big)-\bar{B}\Big)\Big(f_{k}\big(\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k},\mathsf{c}\big)-\bar{B}\Big)^{\top}\Big],\quad\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}].\end{split}

(45)

where
G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{T}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})
is independent of
\bar{B}
, and we recall that
\widetilde{W}
is defined in (
20
).

The matrix SC-AMP algorithm was proposed and analyzed in
[
61
]
for a model with a spatially coupled
Gaussian
design matrix. Similarly to our analysis of SC-AMP for QGT, we could apply a reduction technique along with the universality result of
[
39
]
to establish a state evolution characterization for the matrix SC-AMP applied to pooled data. Such a result would be analogous to Theorem
3.2
, and show that for each iteration
k\geq 1
, the joint empirical distribution of the rows of
(B,B^{k})
converges as:

\displaystyle(B,B^{k})_{\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}},:}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle W_{2}}}{{\rightarrow}}(\bar{B},\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}),\quad\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}].

(46)

To analyze the limiting MSE and error rate of the matrix SC-AMP algorithm, we need to characterize the fixed point of the state evolution recursion in (
45
) (as
k\to\infty
). In QGT, the state evolution fixed point was characterized via the minimizer of a scalar potential function (Theorem
3.5
). Extending this approach to the pooled data setting is challenging as the state evolution parameters are now
L\times L
matrices rather than scalars. In the following section, we circumvent this issue by showing that a suboptimal AMP algorithm still achieves almost-exact recovery for any
\delta>0
. The suboptimal algorithm applies the SC-AMP algorithm column-wise to
\widetilde{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
, ignoring the correlation between the columns of the signal matrix
B
.

4.2
Almost-Exact Recovery via Column-wise SC-AMP

Given
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}},\widetilde{Y}
from the rescaled model (
41
), we run the SC-AMP algorithm
column-wise
on
\widetilde{Y}
. Specifically, for
l\in[L]
, we run the SC-AMP algorithm (
17
) with inputs
Y_{:,l}
and
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
to produce the estimate
\widehat{B}^{k}_{:,l}
after
k\geq 1
iterations. For the SC-AMP algorithm applied to column
l\in[L]
, the denoiser
f_{k}
in (
21
) is computed with
\bar{\beta}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\pi_{l})
. (We recall that the rows of the signal follow the prior
\bar{B}\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi)
where
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{L})
.)

The column-wise SC-AMP algorithm can be viewed as an instance of the matrix SC-AMP algorithm with a suboptimal denoiser, obtained by replacing the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}\mid\bar{B}+G^{k}_{\mathsf{c}}]
in (
43
) with the
marginal
conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}_{l}\mid\bar{B}_{l}+(G^{k}_{\mathsf{c}})_{l}]
, for
l\in[L]
.
Let us define the quantized estimate after
k
iterations of the column-wise SC-AMP algorithm to be

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{k}_{jl}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $\widehat{B}^{k}_{jl}>0.5$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(47)

where
\widehat{B}^{k}
is the estimate obtained from the SC-AMP algorithm. In practice, we can quantize the estimate in a better manner, by setting the largest entry in the row of
\widehat{B}
to one and the remaining entries in the row to zero. We do not use this form of quantization for our almost-exact recovery result since we want to directly apply the SC-AMP results for QGT to the pooled data setting.

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the noiseless pooled data problem with the assumptions stated on p.
4
, for any
\delta>0
. There exist finite
\omega_{0}
and
k_{0}
such that for all
\omega>\omega_{0}
,
k>k_{0}
, and sufficiently large
\Lambda
, the quantized estimate
\widetilde{B}^{k}\in\{0,1\}^{p\times L}
produced by the column-wise SC-AMP algorithm almost surely satisfies:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\widetilde{B}^{k}_{j,:}\neq B_{j,:}\big\}=0.

Proof.

The model assumptions imply that for each
l\in[L]
, the empirical distribution of column
B_{:,l}
converges in Wasserstein distance to
\text{Bernoulli}(\pi_{l})
. By Corollary
3.8
, we have that the SC-AMP algorithm applied to
\widetilde{Y}_{:,l}
satisfies
\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\widetilde{B}^{k}_{j,l}\neq B_{j,l}\big\}=0
almost surely, for each
l\in[L]
.
The result follows by noting that
\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\widetilde{B}^{k}_{j,:}\neq B_{j,:}\big\}\leq\frac{1}{p}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathds{1}\big\{\widetilde{B}^{k}_{jl}\neq B_{jl}\big\}
.
∎

We can also obtain error guarantees in the low-noise regime for the column-wise SC-AMP algorithm, similar to Theorem
3.7
and Corollary
3.8
. We remark that although column-wise SC-AMP is convenient for theoretical analysis, at finite dimensions it is inferior to the matrix SC-AMP algorithm that takes advantage of the correlation in the columns of
B
via the denoiser in (
43
). This is illustrated in the numerical experiments below.

5
Proof of Theorem
3.2

We start by defining
generalized white noise matrices
, which will be used in the proof of the theorem.

Definition 5.1
.

[
39
, Definition 2.15]

A generalized white noise matrix
\widetilde{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
with a (deterministic) variance profile
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is one satisfying the following conditions, for
i\in[n],j\in[p]
:

1.

All entries
\widetilde{X}_{ij}
are independent.

2.

Each entry
\widetilde{X}_{ij}
has mean 0, variance
n^{-1}S_{ij}
, and higher moments satisfying, for each integer
m\geq 3
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}p\cdot\max_{i\in[n]}\max_{j\in[p]}\mathbb{E}\Big[|\widetilde{X}_{ij}|^{m}\Big]=0.

(49)

3.

For a constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}\max_{j\in[p]}S_{ij}\leq C,\quad\lim_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\max_{i\in[n]}\Big|\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}S_{ij}-1\Big|=0,\quad\lim_{n,p\rightarrow\infty}\max_{j\in[p]}\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{ij}-1\Big|=0.

(50)

Definition
5.1
simplifies for the case of
S_{ij}=1
for all
(i,j)\in[n]\times[p]
. In this case, the entries are all i.i.d. with variance
1/n
, the third condition in the definition is trivially satisfied, and the second condition requires moments of order 3 and higher to decay faster than
1/p
. The rescaled i.i.d. design matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{iid}}
in (
11
) is a generalized white noise matrix, but the rescaled spatially coupled matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
in (
12
) is not. Indeed,
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
satisfies the first two requirements in Definition
5.1
and from (
13
), its variance profile satisfies the first and last conditions in (
50
), but not the second: for
i\in[n]
, we have
\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}S_{ij}=\frac{\mathsf{R}}{\mathsf{C}}
, which is close to, but not equal to
1
for large
\Lambda/\omega
.

We prove Theorem
3.2
via a more general result, for a
generalized linear model
with a spatially coupled design, where the observations
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are generated as:

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{i}=q\left(\big(\widetilde{X}_{i,:}^{\text{sc}}\big)^{\top}\beta,\,\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}\right)=q\left(\Theta_{i},\widetilde{\Psi}_{i}\right),\quad\text{ for $i\in[n]$}.

(51)

Here
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the signal to be estimated,
\widetilde{\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a noise vector, and
q:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known output function. We also allow
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
to be more general than the one in Definition
3.1
. The generalized spatially coupled matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
consists of independent zero-mean entries whose variances are specified by a generic base matrix
\widetilde{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{R}\times\mathsf{C}}
, which satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}=1\quad\text{for }\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}],\quad\text{ and }\quad\kappa_{1}\leq\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\leq\kappa_{2},\quad\text{for }\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}],

(52)

for some
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
. Given a base matrix
\widetilde{W}

satisfying (
52
), we construct the spatially coupled matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
with independent entries drawn from a distribution with zero-mean and variance

\mathbb{E}[|{\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}}_{ij}|^{2}]=\frac{\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}(i)\mathsf{c}(j)}}{n/R}
,
for
i\in[n],j\in[p]
.
We also assume that the higher moments
\mathbb{E}[|\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}|^{m}]
for
m\geq 3
, satisfy (
49
).

The first condition in (
52
) ensures that the expected squared norm of each column of
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
is
1
, and the second condition in (
52
) bounds the variance of each entry of
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\beta
from above and below.

High-level sketch of proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The proof consists of three reductions.

1.

In Section
5.1
, we introduce the spatially coupled generalized approximate message passing algorithm (SC-GAMP) for the generalized linear model in (
51
), and characterize its performance via state evolution (Theorem
5.2
). We then reduce the SC-AMP algorithm in (
17
) to SC-GAMP, and use the state evolution result of the latter to prove Theorem

3.2
.

2.

To prove the state evolution result for SC-GAMP (Theorem
5.2
), we show that the algorithm can be written as an instance of an abstract matrix-AMP iteration defined via a generalized white noise matrix. This reduction, shown in Appendix
A.1
, is similar to the one used in
[
38
, Appendix A]
for reducing the SC-GAMP algorithm for a Gaussian design to an abstract matrix-AMP iteration.

3.

To prove the state evolution result for the abstract matrix-AMP (Theorem
A.1
), we show that it is a special case of an AMP iteration for generalized white noise matrices,
for which a rigorous state evolution result was established in
[
39
]
.
We refer to the latter iteration as U-AMP, where the ‘U’ stands for universal. The technique for reducing the abstract matrix-AMP to U-AMP is similar to the one presented in
[
17
]
. This is shown in Appendix
A.2
.

As before, to simplify notation, for vectors
a\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will use
a_{\mathsf{c}}:=a_{\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}}
and
b_{\mathsf{r}}:=b_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}}
, where
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}
are defined in (
19
). There will be no notation simplifications for matrices.

5.1
The SC-GAMP Algorithm and its State Evolution

The SC-GAMP algorithm aims to estimate the signal
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
from observations
\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
generated according to the generalized linear model (
51
). For iteration
k\geq 0
, the algorithm computes:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Theta^{k}=\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\hat{\beta}^{k}-b^{k}\odot\widehat{R}^{k-1},\quad\widehat{R}^{k}=g_{k}(\Theta^{k},\tilde{y},\mathcal{R}),\\
&\beta^{k+1}=(\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}})^{\top}\widehat{R}^{k}-c^{k}\odot\hat{\beta}^{k},\quad\hat{\beta}^{k+1}=f_{k+1}(\beta^{k+1},\mathcal{C}),\end{split}

(53)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise product. The algorithm is initialized with some
\hat{\beta}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and

\Theta^{0}=\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\hat{\beta}^{0}
.
The functions
g_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[\mathsf{R}]
and
f_{k+1}:\mathbb{R}\times[\mathsf{C}]
act row-wise on their input, and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{C}&=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{\text{$p/\mathsf{C}$ entries}},\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{{\text{$p/\mathsf{C}$ entries}}},\dots,\underbrace{\mathsf{C},\dots,\mathsf{C}}_{{\text{$p/\mathsf{C}$ entries}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\\
\mathcal{R}&=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{\text{$n/\mathsf{R}$ entries}},\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{{\text{$n/\mathsf{R}$ entries}}},\dots,\underbrace{\mathsf{R},\dots,\mathsf{R}}_{{\text{$n/\mathsf{R}$ entries}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{split}

(54)

The entries of
c^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
b^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are defined as follows, for
j\in[p]
,
i\in[n]
:

\displaystyle c_{j}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}}{n/\mathsf{R}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{r}}\partial_{1}g_{k}(\Theta_{i}^{k},\tilde{y}_{i},\mathsf{r}),\quad b_{i}^{k}=\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\frac{\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}}{n/\mathsf{R}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{c}}\partial_{1}f_{k}(\beta_{j}^{k},\mathsf{c}),

where
\partial_{1}
denotes the derivative with respect to the first argument.

State evolution.

The ‘memory’ terms
-b^{k}\odot\widehat{R}^{k-1}
and
-c^{k}\odot\hat{\beta}^{k}
in (
53
) debias the iterates
\Theta^{k}
and
\beta^{k+1}
, ensuring that their empirical distributions are accurately captured by state evolution in the high-dimensional limit. Theorem
5.2
below shows that for each
k\geq 1
and
\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
, the empirical distribution of
\beta_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}
converges to the distribution of
\mu_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}\bar{\beta}+G_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}
where
\bar{\beta}
is the random variable representing the limiting distribution of the entries of the signal
\beta_{\mathsf{c}}
, and
G_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,(\sigma_{\beta,\mathsf{c}})^{2}\big)
is independent of
\bar{\beta}
. The deterministic parameters
\mu_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
are defined below. The result implies that the empirical distribution of the estimate
\hat{\beta}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}
converges to the distribution of
f_{k}\big(\mu_{\beta.\mathsf{c}}^{k}\bar{\beta}+G_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}\big)
. Thus,
f_{k}
can be viewed as a denoising function that can be tailored to take advantage of the prior in
\bar{\beta}
. Theorem
5.2
also shows that the joint empirical distribution of
the rows of
(\Theta_{\mathsf{r}},\Theta_{\mathsf{r}}^{k})
converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{k,\mathsf{r}})
, where
\Sigma^{k,\mathsf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is defined below.

We now describe the state evolution recursion defining
\mu_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k},\sigma_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Sigma^{k,\mathsf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. Define
\bar{g}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[\mathsf{R}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle g_{k}(\Theta_{i}^{k},\tilde{y}_{i},\mathsf{r})

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{k}(\Theta_{i},\Theta_{i}^{k},\widetilde{\Psi}_{i},\mathsf{r})\text{ for $i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}$},

(55)

since
\tilde{y}_{i}=q(\Theta_{i},\widetilde{\Psi}_{i})
. Starting with an initializer
\Sigma^{0,\mathsf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
for
\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}]
(defined later in (
59
)), the state evolution parameters are iteratively computed as follows for
k\geq 0
, and
\mathsf{r}\in[\mathsf{R}],\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}]
:

\displaystyle\mu^{k+1}_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}=\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{1}\bar{g}_{k}(Z_{\mathsf{r}},Z_{\mathsf{r}}^{k},\bar{\Psi},\mathsf{r})],\quad(\sigma^{k+1}_{\beta,\mathsf{c}})^{2}=\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\mathbb{E}[\bar{g}_{k}(Z_{\mathsf{r}},Z_{\mathsf{r}}^{k},\bar{\Psi},\mathsf{r})^{2}],

\displaystyle\Sigma^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}&\Sigma_{12}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}\\
\Sigma_{21}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}&\Sigma_{22}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}\end{bmatrix},

(56)

where
(Z_{\mathsf{r}},Z_{\mathsf{r}}^{k})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma^{k,\mathsf{r}})
are independent of
\bar{\Psi}
, and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{11}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}&=\mathbb{E}[(Z_{\mathsf{r}})^{2}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bar{\beta}^{2}]}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}},\\
\Sigma_{12}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}&=\Sigma_{21}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}=\frac{1}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\beta}f_{k+1}(\mu^{k+1}_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}\bar{\beta}+G^{k+1}_{\beta,\mathsf{c}},\mathsf{c})],\\
\Sigma_{22}^{k+1,\mathsf{r}}&=\frac{1}{\delta_{\textup{in}}}\sum_{\mathsf{c}=1}^{\mathsf{C}}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\mathbb{E}[f_{k+1}(\mu^{k+1}_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}\bar{\beta}+G^{k+1}_{\beta,\mathsf{c}},\mathsf{c})^{2}].\end{split}

(57)

Here
G_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(\sigma_{\beta,\mathsf{c}}^{k+1})^{2})
is independent of
\bar{\beta}
.

The SC-GAMP algorithm and its state evolution equations are similar to those introduced in
[
38
]
, the only difference being that the SC design matrix
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is now a generalized spatially coupled matrix instead of the spatially coupled
Gaussian
one used in
[
38
]
. We note that
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
is not a generalized white noise matrix since it has variance profile
S_{ij}^{\text{sc}}:=\mathsf{R}\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}(i),\mathsf{c}(j)}
, which is not guaranteed to satisfy the condition
\max_{i\in[n]}\big|\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}S_{ij}^{\text{sc}}-1\big|\rightarrow 0
in Definition
5.1
. Nevertheless,
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
can be related to a generalized white noise matrix
\widetilde{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
defined as follows. For
i\in[n],j\in[p]
, let:

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{ij}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}_{ij}}{\sqrt{\mathsf{R}\cdot\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}(i),\mathsf{c}(j)}}}&\text{ if }\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}(i),\mathsf{c}(j)}\neq 0,\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{{\rm i.i.d.}}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\frac{1}{n}\big)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(58)

(In the second line of the definition, we could use any sub-Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance
1/n
instead of
\mathcal{N}(0,1/n)
.)
From the construction of
\widetilde{X}^{\text{sc}}
(see below (
52
)), it follows that
\widetilde{X}
is a generalized white noise matrix with variance profile
S_{ij}=1
for all
(i,j)\in[n]\times[p]
.

The state evolution result for SC-GAMP requires the following assumptions on the model and the algorithm:

(A1)

As
n,p\to\infty
, we have
\frac{n}{p}\to\delta
. The signal

, so the state evolution reduces to

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\textup{mmse}\left(\Big(\sigma^{2}+\frac{x^{k}}{\delta}\Big)^{-1}\right),

with the initialization
x^{0}=\textup{mmse}(0)=\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\beta})
. Since
\textup{mmse}(s)
is strictly decreasing in
s\in[0,\infty)
, the sequence
(x^{k})
is monotonically decreasing in
k
, and since it is bounded below, it converges to a fixed point. Since the recursion is initialized at
x^{0}=\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\beta})
, the fixed point is given by the largest solution of
x=\textup{mmse}\left(\Big(\sigma^{2}+\frac{x}{\delta}\Big)^{-1}\right)
. Finally, we observe that the same equation is obtained by setting

7
Discussion and Future Directions

We have shown that for noiseless QGT and pooled data, a spatially coupled Bernoulli test design with an AMP recovery algorithm achieves almost-exact recovery with
n=o(p)
tests. A key open question is to determine how
n
scales with
p
for almost-exact recovery with SC-AMP. Deriving this scaling is beyond the reach of our asymptotic analysis, which requires that
n/p\to\delta>0
, but recent non-asymptotic analyses of AMP
[
32
,
63
]
might provide tools to address this question, and allow comparisons with the information-theoretic bound of
n^{*}=\gamma^{*}\frac{p}{\log p}
(see (
5
)).

Another open question is to determine the number of tests required for
exact
recovery in the linear regime for an efficient scheme with a random design. We recall that exact recovery requires
\mathbb{P}\big[\tilde{\beta}\neq\beta\big]\rightarrow 0
as
p\to\infty
, in contrast to the almost-exact recovery criterion in (
2
).

In this paper, the only assumption on the QGT signal vector
\beta
is that its empirical distribution converges to a Bernoulli distribution. The items are not required to be independent, and in some applications there may be known correlations between the items. Although the current SC-AMP algorithm does not exploit correlations between signal entries, it can be adapted to do so, using non-separable denoising functions
[
64
]
.

An interesting direction for future work is to study variants of QGT and pooled data with additional structure, e.g., constraints on the tests or side-information that captures correlations between the items. For example, in graph-constrained group testing
[
65
]
, the items are vertices on a graph, and items included in each test have to conform to constraints imposed by the graph. Recent work in Boolean group testing has also shown that exploiting correlations or community structure among the items can significantly improve testing efficiency
[
66
,
67
,
68
]
.
Designing efficient schemes for quantitative group testing in such structured settings is an open question.

\displaystyle\left(\Xi^{t}\right)_{r,s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\mathsf{R}}\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\mathbb{E}\big[f_{r}^{u}(\bar{e}^{1},\dots,\bar{e}^{t-1},\bar{c}^{1},\dots,\bar{c}^{L_{c}+1}=\mathsf{r})f_{s}^{u}(\bar{e}^{1},\dots,\bar{e}^{t-1},\bar{c}^{1},\dots,\bar{c}^{L_{c}+1}=\mathsf{r})\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\mathsf{R}}\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}\mathbb{E}[\bar{u}_{\mathsf{r}}^{r}\bar{u}_{\mathsf{r}}^{s}],

where for
s\geq 1

\bar{u}_{\mathsf{r}}^{s+1}=f_{s+1}^{u}(\bar{e}^{1},\dots,\bar{e}^{s},\bar{c}_{\mathsf{r}}^{1},\dots,\bar{c}_{\mathsf{r}}^{L_{c}},\mathsf{r})
.
We have shown that with the choice of side information in (

Applying Theorem

Lemma B.1
.

[

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}-\delta\left(1-\frac{\delta\sigma^{2}}{b+\delta\sigma^{2}}\right)+\delta\log\left(1+\frac{b}{\delta\sigma^{2}}\right)+2I\left(\bar{\beta};\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{b+\delta\sigma^{2}}}\bar{\beta}+G\right)

SC-AMP denoiser and state evolution parameters for QGT.

The Bayes-optimal denoiser
f_{k+1}
in (
21
) can be computed using the prior
\bar{\beta}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\pi)
. For
j\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
and
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(s,\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{\beta}\,\big|\,(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G=s\big]=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left[\bar{\beta}=1\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G=s|\bar{\beta}=1\right]}{\sum_{\bar{\beta}\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb{P}[\bar{\beta}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2}\bar{\beta}+\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}G=s|\bar{\beta}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\phi\big((s-(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2})/\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\big)}{\pi\phi\big((s-(\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k})^{2})/\chi_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\big)+(1-\pi)\phi(s/\chi^{k}_{\mathsf{c}})},

(119)

where
\phi(x)
is the standard normal density. Instead of precomputing the state evolution parameters
\big(\chi^{k}_{\mathsf{c}}\big)
, they can be estimated from the SC-AMP iterates as:

\left(\widehat{\chi}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\right)^{2}=\sum_{\mathsf{r}=1}^{\mathsf{R}}{\widetilde{W}_{\mathsf{r}\mathsf{c}}\left(\left\lVert{\widetilde{\Theta}_{\mathsf{r}}^{k}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{-1}}\qquad\text{ for }\mathsf{c}\in[\mathsf{C}],

(120)

where
\widetilde{\Theta}_{\mathsf{r}}^{k}=(\widetilde{\Theta}_{i}^{k})_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}}
is the restriction of
\widetilde{\Theta}^{k}
to indices
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{r}}
. The derivative
\partial_{1}f_{k}(\beta_{j}^{k},\mathsf{c})
, required for
b^{k}
in (
17
), can be obtained by applying the Quotient rule to the last expression in (
119
).

SC-AMP denoiser and state evolution parameters for pooled data.

The Bayes-optimal denoiser
f_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{L}\times[\mathsf{C}]\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
in the SC-AMP algorithm in (
42

\displaystyle f_{k}(s,\mathsf{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\bar{B}\mid\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}=s]=\sum_{l=1}^{L}e_{l}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\bar{B}=e_{l}]\mathbb{P}[\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}=s|\bar{B}=e_{l}]}{\mathbb{P}[\bar{B}+G_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}=s]}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\pi_{l}e_{l}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(e_{l}-s)^{\textsf{T}}\left(\mathrm{T}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\right)^{-1}(e_{l}-s)\right)}{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\pi_{l}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(e_{l}-s)^{\textsf{T}}\left(\mathrm{T}_{\mathsf{c}}^{k}\right)^{-1}(e_{l}-s)\right)},

(122)
