Simple closed curves, non-kernel homology and Magnus embedding
Adam Klukowski

Abstract
We consider the subspace of the homology of a covering space spanned by lifts of simple closed curves. Our main result is the existence of unbranched covers of surfaces where this is a proper subspace. More generally, for a fixed finite solvable quotient of the fundamental group we exhibit a cover whose homology is not generated by the lifts of curves in the complement of its kernel. We explain how the existing approach of Malestein and Putman (for branched covers) relates to the Magnus embedding, and by doing so we simplify their construction. We then generalise it to unbranched covers by producing embeddings of surface groups into units of certain graded associative algebras, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Consider a finite-degree cover
p\colon\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
of an orientable surface
\Sigma_{g,n}
of genus
g
with
n
punctures. The
simple closed curve homology

\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}|\Sigma_{g,n}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};M)
is defined as the subspace of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};M)
spanned by the set

\left\{[\widetilde{\gamma}]\in\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};M)\ \big|\ \widetilde{\gamma}\text{ is a component of }p^{-1}(\gamma)\text{ for a simple closed curve }\gamma\text{ on }\Sigma_{g,n}\right\}

This can be defined for any
\mathbb{Z}
-module
M
.

Our main theorem is:

Theorem 1
.

For any
g\geq 2
,
n\geq 0
, and odd prime
r
there is a cover
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
, whose degree is a power of
r
, and such that
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\textnormal{scc}|\Sigma_{g,n}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
.

The question whether simple closed curve homology is the whole of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Q})
was an open problem asked by Marché
[
13
]
and independently by Looijenga
[
10
]
. An affirmative answer for abelian and 2-step nilpotent covers, in the context of graphs instead of surfaces, was given by Farb and Hensel
[
4
]
. Koberda and Santharoubane
[
9
]
used TQFT methods to find covers of surfaces with
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})
, but their method could not rule out the possibility of
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})
being finite-index (equivalently, it did not apply to homology with rational coefficients). Another family of covers whose integral simple closed curve homology is a proper submodule was noted by Irmer
[
8
]
. A particular example of an unbranched cover
\Sigma_{649}\rightarrow\Sigma_{2}
with
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}|\Sigma_{2}}(\Sigma_{649};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\Sigma_{649};\mathbb{Q})
was noted by Marković and Tošić
[
15
]
based on the work of Marković
[
14
]
and Bogomolov and Tschinkel
[
2
]
.

This article builds on the work of Malestein and Putman
[
12
]
and earlier ideas of Farb and Hensel
[
4
]
. In
[
12
]
they constructed covers where
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
; however, their construction requires

, or equivalently, produces branched covers. Here we settle the general case and exhibit unbranched covers with
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
in all genera. We relate earlier approaches to the Magnus embedding
[
11
]
. We then produce similar embeddings of surface groups, which may be of independent interest.

1.1
Homologies of covers and subgroups

One can ask about the subspaces of homology of a covering space
\widetilde{\Sigma}
spanned by more general sets. Following
[
4
]
, we define an
elevation

\tilde{\gamma}
of a loop
\gamma
on
\Sigma
to be a minimal concatenation of lifts of
\gamma
which forms a closed curve in
\widetilde{\Sigma}
. Given a set
\mathcal{O}
of loops on
\Sigma
we define
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}|\Sigma}(\widetilde{\Sigma};M)
to be the subspace of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};M)
spanned by

\left\{[\tilde{\gamma}]\ \big|\ \tilde{\gamma}\text{ is an elevation of a curve }\gamma\in\mathcal{O}\right\}

We present a properness (in the subset/subspace sense) result for a large class of subsets in Theorem
2
. For a quotient
\theta\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma\rightarrow Q
write
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})=\textnormal{H}_{1}^{(\pi_{1}\Sigma\setminus\ker\theta)|\Sigma}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
to be the subspace spanned by elevations of curves which are nontrivial in
Q
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
g\geq 2
,
n\geq 0
, and
\theta\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}\rightarrow Q
be a nontrivial finite solvable quotient of odd order. There exists a normal cover
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
such that
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
. Furthermore, we may require that all prime factors of the degree of this cover divide
|Q|
.

Let us point out some consequences of Theorem
2
. They were previously known when the fundamental group is free, and can now be extended to (unpunctured) surfaces. Primitive homology (2) was introduced in
[
4
]
. Statement (3) generalises
[
12
, Theorem C]
.

Corollary 3
.

Let
g\geq 2
,
n\geq 0
. For each of the following sets
\mathcal{O}
of loops on
\Sigma_{g,n}
there exists a normal cover
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
such that
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}|\Sigma_{g,n}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
is not the whole of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
.

(1)

Theorem
1
:
\mathcal{O}
consisting of simple closed curves.

(2)

Primitive homology:
\mathcal{O}
consisting of the elements which belong to the generating set in some standard presentation of
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
.

(3)

More generally, any set
\mathcal{O}
contained in the union of finitely many
\text{Aut}(\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n})
-orbits.

Furthermore, for any odd prime
r
we may require the degree of the cover be a power of
r
.

Additionally, the following special case of Theorem
2
generalises Theorem D from
[
12
]
(where again their original proof required
n\geq 1
). Denote by
\mathcal{O}_{d}
the set of all loops which map to nonzero vectors in
\textnormal{H}_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n};\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{d})
, and define the
d
-primitive homology to be
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{d\textnormal{-prim}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})=\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}_{d}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
g\geq 2,n\geq 0
, and
d
be a square-free integer. There exists a normal cover
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
with
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{d\textnormal{-prim}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})

Remark 5
.

It was asked by Kent
[
13
]
whether we can take
\mathcal{O}
to be the set of all curves which do not fill

. This is not answered by Theorem

is a finite quotient of the fundamental group and

Remark 6
.

When
n\geq 1
we can remove the restriction of odd order of

from Theorem
2

).

Algebraic interpretation.

Let
R
be a finite-index subgroup of
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
and
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
. Algebraically, the elevation of
\gamma
is the element
\gamma^{m}
, where
m
is the minimal positive integer with
\gamma^{m}\in R
. Note that topological elevations of (unbased) loop
\gamma
are precisely the algebraic elevations of (based) loops in the conjugacy class in
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
corresponding to
\gamma
. Then
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}}(R;M)
is the subspace of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;M)
spanned by the set

\left\{[x^{m}]\in\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;M)\ \big|\ x\in\mathcal{O}\text{ and }m\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ such that }x^{m}\in R\right\}

When

is the subgroup corresponding to the cover and

is closed under conjugation, then naturally
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}}(R;M)\cong\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}}(\widetilde{\Sigma};M)
.

1.2
Other results

A couple of interesting statements about free groups was noted by Malestein and Putman
[
12
, Theorems F and G]
based on an argument of
[
9
]
. Our Theorem
2
allows to apply their argument to surface groups, giving the following corollary.

Theorem 7
.

Let
g\geq 2
, and
\theta\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}\rightarrow Q
be a finite solvable quotient of odd order. There exists an integral linear representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}\rightarrow\textnormal{GL}_{d}\mathbb{Z}
with infinite image such that every element of
\rho(\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}\setminus\ker\theta)
has finite order.

The statement of Theorem

remains true if we replace the complement of a kernel with any of the special subsets from Corollary

.

Our key technical contribution, which allows to adapt the proofs to surface groups, is the following variation on the Magnus embedding. Here
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{2}=\langle s_{1},t_{1},s_{2},t_{2}|[s_{1},t_{1}][s_{2},t_{2}]=1\rangle
is the surface group of genus 2, and
\mathbb{Z}[\![A,B]\!][i,j,k]
is the quaternion algebra of the ring of formal power series in two variables with integer coefficients.

Proposition

(Lemma
20
)
.

There exists a homomorphism
\overline{\tau}\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}[\![A,B]\!][i,j,k]^{\times}
satisfying

\overline{\tau}(s_{1})\equiv 1+Ai,\quad\overline{\tau}(t_{1})\equiv 1+Bj,\quad\overline{\tau}(s_{2})\equiv 1+Aj,\quad\overline{\tau}(t_{2})\equiv 1+Bi\qquad\text{mod }(A,B)^{2}

1.3
Outline and organisation

Our approach consists of two steps.

The first step is to construct covers whose homology can see the interesting curves. In Section
2.2
we reduce Theorem
1
on simple closed curve homology, Corollary
3
, and Theorem
2
to a special case of
2
– Proposition
4
about
d
-primitive homology.

Section
2.3
serves as a bridge between the first and second step. Here we view the homology of a cover as a representation of the deck group, and prove Proposition
4
. To control the representations we use a structure we name
polynomial substitute
(Definition
10
, and existence by Propositions
12
and
13
). Informally, they are a device for evaluating polynomials on the homology coefficients.

The second step is the construction of polynomial substitutes. We start with the case of punctured surfaces and free groups and prove Proposition
12
in Section
3.1
. We translate the proof from
[
12
]
into the language of Magnus embedding, which leads to significant simplifications. In Section
3.2
we prove Proposition
13
by carrying out an analogous construction for surface groups.

2
Relationships between homologies of covers

The aim of this section is to relate various flavours of cover homology, and eventually reduce the properness of
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\text{scc}}
,
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}}
and
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}
to the properness of
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{d\text{-prim}}
. Our approach is, given a set
\mathcal{O}
of curves on
\Sigma
, to find a cover
\widehat{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma
with a nicer set
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
(of curves on
\widehat{\Sigma}
) such that all further covers
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\widehat{\Sigma}
satisfy

\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\mathcal{O}|\Sigma}\left(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q}\right)\ \subseteq\ \textnormal{H}_{1}^{\widehat{\mathcal{O}}|\widehat{\Sigma}}\left(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q}\right)

Then properness of
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
-homology implies the properness of
\mathcal{O}
-homology.

2.1
Stability of curve characteristics under taking covers

Note that each of the properties of interest is stable with respect to taking covers, in the sense that if a curve has a property then so do all its elevations. These facts will be used in Section
2.2
to reduce the general Theorem
2
to the special case of
d
-primitive homology.

Lemma 8
.

Let

be a finite-degree cover,

a curve on
\Sigma
, and

its elevation to

. Then:

(1)

If
\gamma
is simple, then so is

.

(2)

If

\tilde{\gamma}
does not fill

.

(1)

2.3
Properness of

-primitive homology

Here we prove Proposition

. This will complete the proof of Theorem

given in Section

Proposition 9
.

Suppose
g\geq 2,n\geq 0
, and
d>1
is an odd square-free integer. Write
m=2g+\max\{0,n-1\}
. There exists a finite quotient
\rho\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}\rightarrow G
, whose size divides some power of
d
, a homomorphism
\alpha\colon G\rightarrow\left(\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{d}\right)^{m}
, and a nontrivial irreducible rational representation
V
of
G
with the following properties.

1.

The composition
\alpha\circ\rho
equals the abelianisation map
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}\rightarrow\textnormal{H}_{1}(\Sigma_{g,n};\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{d})=\left(\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{d}\right)^{m}
.

2.

Every element of
G\setminus\ker\alpha
has no nonzero fixed vectors in
V
.

Later, when deducing
4
, we will arrange

to be the deck group, and the representation
V
will give rise to a subspace complement to
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{d\text{-prim}}
. To control the action of elements of

and define below.

Definition 10

(polynomial substitute)
.

Let
r
be a prime,
F
a group, and
n
be the dimension of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(F;\mathbb{F}_{r})
. A characteristic
r
degree-
r^{k}

polynomial substitute
for

is a tuple
(G,C,\rho,\alpha)
consisting of a finite
r
-group
G
, a central subgroup
C\leq G
, and homomorphisms
\rho\colon F\rightarrow G
,
\alpha\colon G\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r}^{n}
satisfying the following properties.

(a)

The composition
\alpha\circ\rho
equals the mod-
r

F\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r}^{n}=\textnormal{H}_{1}(F;\mathbb{F}_{r})
.

(b)

For any
x\in G
we have
x^{r^{k}}\in C
.

(c)

For any homogeneous polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{r}[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]
of degree
r^{k}
there is a homomorphism
\Psi_{P}\colon C\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r}
of additive groups such that
\Psi_{P}(x^{r^{k}})=P(\alpha(x))
for all

.

Remark 11
.

Any homogeneous function
\mathbb{F}_{r}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r}
can be expressed as a polynomial of sufficiently large degree. One way to see this is via Lagrange interpolation. If

is nonzero, then the complement of the ray
\mathbb{F}_{r}^{\times}\cdot v
is a union of finitely many codimension-1 subspaces, and the product of their defining equations is a polynomial which vanishes everywhere except nonzero multiples of
v
. By taking linear combinations of such products, we can obtain a homogeneous polynomial with prescribed values at a given set of representatives for rays in
\mathbb{F}_{r}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
.

Before using polynomial substitutes as witnesses to Proposition

Let

Construction of
\rho,G,\alpha
.
Factorise
d=\prod_{i}r_{i}
. Let

be large enough integers, which we will specify in a moment. By Propositions
12
and
13
, for each factor

there exists a degree-

polynomial substitute
(G_{i},C_{i},\rho_{i},\alpha_{i})
for
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
. Define

\displaystyle G=

\displaystyle\prod_{i}G_{i}

\displaystyle C=

\displaystyle\prod_{i}C_{i}\leq Z(G)

\displaystyle\rho=

\displaystyle\prod_{i}\rho_{i}\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}\rightarrow G

\displaystyle\alpha=

\displaystyle\prod_{i}\alpha_{i}\colon G\rightarrow\prod_{i}\mathbb{F}_{r_{i}}^{m}\cong\left(\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{d}\right)^{m}

Now, let
k_{i}
be large enough so that there exists a homogeneous polynomial
P_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{r_{i}}[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}]
of degree

which vanishes nowhere on
\mathbb{F}_{r_{i}}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
(see Remark
11
). By the definition of polynomial substitute, there exists a group homomorphism
\Psi_{i}\colon C_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r_{i}}
satisfying
\Psi_{i}\big(x^{r_{i}^{k_{i}}}\big)=P_{i}\big(\alpha_{i}(x)\big)
for all

. Define

\Psi\colon C\rightarrow\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{d}\qquad\Psi=\sum_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}r_{j}\cdot\Psi_{i}

The homomorphism
\Psi
has the following property

\text{If }x\in G\setminus\ker\alpha\text{, then some power of }x\text{ lies in }C\setminus\ker\Psi.

(1)

Indeed, suppose
x\in G\setminus\ker\alpha
. Then
\alpha_{i}(x)
must be a nonzero vector in
\mathbb{F}_{r_{i}}^{m}
for some factor
r_{i}
. Write
s=\prod_{j\neq i}r_{j}^{k_{j}}
and
t=\prod_{j\neq i}r_{j}
. Using the definition of
\Psi_{i}
, and that

, we obtain congruences

\Psi\left(x^{r_{i}^{k_{i}}s}\right)\equiv t\Psi_{i}\left(x^{r_{i}^{k_{i}}s}\right)\equiv tP_{i}\left(\alpha\left(x^{s}\right)\right)\equiv tP_{i}\left(s\cdot\alpha(x)\right)\quad\mod r_{i}

where by
s\cdot\alpha(x)
we mean scalar-by-vector multiplication. We chose
P_{i}
to be nonzero on every nonzero vector in
\mathbb{F}_{r_{i}}^{m}
, so
\Psi\left(x^{r_{i}^{k_{i}}s}\right)
is nonzero modulo
r_{i}
, and thus also modulo
d
. This completes the proof of property (
1
).

Construction of the representation
V
.
Having
\Psi
with property (
1
) we are ready to construct the representation
V
. Let
\omega
be a primitive
d
-th root of unity and let
W=\mathbb{Q}[\omega]
be the cyclotomic field. We make
W
into a representation of

by letting
c\in C
act by
\omega^{\Psi(c)}
. Then we induce

.

Suppose that
x\in G\setminus\ker\alpha
fixes some

. By the property (
1
),
x
has a power

, which must also fix
v
. Choosing a set of coset representatives
\Lambda
for

in
G
allows us to describe the induced representation explicitly as
V^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda\cdot W
. Using this description we can write
v=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda\cdot v_{\lambda}
. Since
c
is central, we have

v=c\cdot v=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda\cdot(c\cdot v_{\lambda})=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lambda\cdot\omega^{\Psi(c)}v_{\lambda}=\omega^{\Psi(c)}v

But
\Psi(c)\not\equiv 0\ (\!\!\!\!\mod d)
, so

must be the zero vector. Thus we can take

to be any irreducible factor of

.
∎

Now we have to relate the homology of a cover to the representation theory of the deck group. We will use the following two lemmas. They are classical results of Gaschütz
[
7
]
and Chevalley-Weil
[
3
]
. Below
G
acts on

by conjugation and on

by multiplication.

Lemma 14

(Gashütz)
.

Let
R\triangleleft F_{n}
be finite-index,
G=\nicefrac{{F_{n}}}{{R}}
, and
k
be a field of characteristic 0. Then
\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;k)\cong k\oplus k[G]^{n-1}
as a
G
-module.

Lemma 15

(Chevalley-Weil)
.

Let
R\triangleleft\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}
be finite-index,
G=\nicefrac{{\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}}}{{R}}
, and
k
be a field of characteristic 0. Then
\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;k)\cong k^{2}\oplus\left(k[G]\right)^{2g-2}
as a
G
-module.

We are now ready to prove the properness of
d
-primitive homology. The key property of
(G,\rho,\alpha,V)
is that
V
is a representation of
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
which factors through a finite group and where
d
-primitive elements have no nontrivial fixed vectors.

Proof of Proposition
4
assuming Proposition
9
:

Consider the group
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
and a square-free integer
d
. Let
(G,\rho,\alpha,V)
be the structure provided by Proposition
9
. Set
R=\ker\rho\leq\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
. By Lemmas
14
and
15
,
\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;\mathbb{Q})
contains
\mathbb{Q}[G]
as a direct summand, and hence it also contains
V
.

Take any
d
-primitive
\gamma\in\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}
, and suppose that
\gamma^{m}\in R
. We want to prove that projection of the vector
[\gamma^{m}]\in\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;\mathbb{Q})
onto sub-
G
-representation

is zero. To this end, let us examine the action of
\rho(\gamma)\in G
on it. On the one hand,
\rho(\gamma)\curvearrowright\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;\mathbb{Q})
corresponds to conjugation by
\gamma
, so it fixes

. On the other hand,
\gamma
is

-primitive, so
\rho(\gamma)\in G\setminus\ker\alpha
, and from the defining property of

it follows that projections of invariants of
\rho(\gamma)
onto

are zero.

The above argument holds for any generator of

-primitive homology, so intersection of
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{d\text{-prim}}(R;\mathbb{Q})
with

is the zero subspace. In particular,
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{d\text{-prim}}(R;\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(R;\mathbb{Q})
. The cover
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma
corresponding to

is a witness to Proposition

.
∎

3
Existence of polynomial substitutes

This section is devoted to constructing polynomial substitutes (Definition
10
) for free and surface groups. This is the last piece we need to complete the proof of Proposition
9
and hence of all the theorems stated in Section
1
.

In Section
3.1
we prove Proposition
12
– the existence of polynomial substitutes for free groups. This completes the proof of Proposition
9
in the punctured case
n\geq 1
. The construction relies on the Magnus embedding of a free group into a group of units of a certain associative algebra.

In Section
3.2
we prove Proposition
13
– the existence of polynomial substitutes for surface groups. This will complete the proof of
9
in the unpunctured case
n=0
. The idea is to mimic the simpler proof of
12
, but work with algebras which allow us to preserve the surface relation.

3.1
Free groups and proof of Proposition
12

In this section we construct polynomial substitutes for free groups and prove Proposition
12
. This will complete the proof of Proposition
9
in the punctured case
n\geq 1
. The group
G
in Definition
10
will be a certain quotient of the group of units of a free associative algebra.

Magnus embedding.

Let
F_{n}=\langle s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\rangle
be the free group of rank
n
, and
\mathbb{Z}\langle\!\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle\!\rangle
be the
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
-adic completion of the free associative
\mathbb{Z}
-algebra on
n
generators. Explicitly, it consists of formal power series in non-commutative variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
with integer coefficients. We have the following

Lemma 17

(Magnus embedding)
.

There is a homomorphism
\overline{\rho}
from
F_{n}
to the multiplicative group of units
\mathbb{Z}\langle\!\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle\!\rangle^{\times}
which sends
s_{i}
to
1+X_{i}
.

The only nontrivial part of the proof is verifying that
1+X_{i}
is actually a unit. Its multiplicative inverse is given by
1+\sum_{k\geq 1}(-1)^{k}X_{i}^{k}
. More generally, any series with free term
\pm 1
is a unit, with inverse given by a formal geometric power series. Although we will not need this, we remark that the kernel of
\overline{\rho}
is in fact trivial
[
11
]
.

Construction of a polynomial substitute for a free group.

Define

\mathcal{F}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}\langle\!\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle\!\rangle}{(r)+(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{r^{k}+1}}

Explicitly,
\mathcal{F}
consists of polynomials in non-commuting variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
with
\mathbb{F}_{r}
-coefficients, where every product of more than
r^{k}
variables vanishes. This is a finite
\mathbb{F}_{r}
-algebra.

We define
G
to be the set of polynomials with constant term equal to one

G=1+(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\{P\in\mathcal{F}\ |\ P(0,\dots,0)=1\}

(2)

This is a subgroup of the multiplicative group of units
\mathcal{F}^{\times}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}^{\times}\cong\mathbb{F}_{r}^{\times}\times G
, and that the order of
G
is a power of
r
.

We define
\rho
as the composition of the quotient
\mathbb{Z}\langle\!\langle X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\rangle\!\rangle\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
with
\overline{\rho}
. Explicitly, it sends
s_{i}
to
1+X_{i}\in G
.

The group
G
surjects onto
\mathbb{F}_{r}^{n}
via forgetting degree two and higher and subtracting the constant term

\alpha\colon 1+a_{1}X_{1}+\dots+a_{n}X_{n}+P\mapsto\big(a_{i}\big)_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\mathbb{F}_{r}^{n}

(3)

where
P\in(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{2}
. The maps
\rho
and
\alpha
are compatible, in the sense that
\alpha\circ\rho
is the mod-
r
abelianisation
F_{n}\rightarrow\textnormal{H}_{1}(F_{n};\mathbb{F}_{r})
. This verifies condition
(a)
of definition
10
.

Inside
\mathcal{F}
we also have

C=1+(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{r^{k}}=\left\{1+P\ \big|\ P\in\mathbb{F}_{r}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]\text{ homogeneous of degree }r^{k}\right\}

(4)

All terms of degree at least
r^{k}+1
vanish in
\mathcal{F}
, so
C
is central in
\mathcal{F}
as a subalgebra, so it is central in
G
as a subgroup. Let us describe the group structure of
C
. If
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{r}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
are homogeneous of degree
r^{k}
, then
(1+P_{1})(1+P_{2})=1+P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{1}P_{2}=1+P_{1}+P_{2}
holds in
\mathcal{F}
. Therefore we have a group isomorphism

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi\colon C&\cong\left\{P\in\mathbb{F}_{r}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]\ |\ P\text{ homogeneous of degree }r^{k}\right\}\\
\varphi\colon 1+P&\mapsto P\end{split}

(5)

between
C
(with its multiplicative group structure inherited from
\mathcal{F}
and
G
) and the linear space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
r^{k}
. Note that this space has dimension
n^{r^{k}}
and a canonical basis consisting of monomials of degree
r^{k}
.

Verification of the polynomial substitute property.

It remains to check the conditions
(b)
and
(c)
of Definition
10
. Consider
x=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}+P
for some
P\in(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{2}
. Raising it to the
r^{k}
-th power in
\mathcal{F}
gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{r^{k}}&=\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}+P\right)^{r^{k}}=\\
&=1+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}+P\right)^{r^{k}}=\\
&=1+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}\right)^{r^{k}}=\\
&=1+\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r^{k}}}\prod_{l=1}^{r^{k}}a_{i_{l}}\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{r^{k}}X_{i_{l}}\end{split}

(6)

The result belongs to
C
, which confirms (b).

Let us view equation (
6
) through the isomorphism from equation (
5
). By examining the RHS of (
6
), and using monomials in
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
as the basis, we see that
\varphi(x^{r^{k}})=\alpha(x)^{\otimes r^{k}}
, with
\alpha
defined in (
3
) (resembling the Veronese mapping). In particular, every monomial in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of degree
r^{k}
appears as one of the coordinates of
\varphi(x^{r^{k}})
. Since every polynomial in
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is a linear combination of monomials, we can obtain any homogeneous degree-
r^{k}
polynomial in variables
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
by taking linear combinations of coordinate projections of
\varphi(x^{r^{k}})
. This confirms the condition (c).

3.2
Surface groups and proof of Proposition
13

In this section we construct polynomial substitutes for surface groups and prove Proposition
13
. This will complete the proof of Proposition
9
in the non-punctured case
n=0
.

Our proof strategy will be to mimic the construction for free groups from Section
3.1
. We will replace the vanilla Magnus embedding
17
with homomorphisms coming from two other algebras, each of which preserves the relation in surface groups. In Section
3.2.1
we showcase the algebra
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, which will generate
mixed
monomials – those which contain factors from more than one standard pair of generators (e.g.
a_{1}b_{1}a_{2}
or
b_{i}b_{j}
for
i\neq j
). In Section
3.2.2
we construct the algebra
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
, which will supply

monomials (e.g.
a_{1}^{u}b_{1}^{v}
). We combine them and finalise the proof of
13
in Section
3.2.3
.

We use the standard presentation
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}=\langle s_{1},t_{1},\dots,s_{g},t_{g}\ |\ \prod_{i}[s_{i},t_{i}]=1\rangle
, and write
a_{i}(s_{j})=b_{i}(t_{j})=\delta_{ij},a_{i}(t_{j})=b_{i}(s_{j})=0
for the standard basis of
\textnormal{H}^{1}(\Sigma_{g};\mathbb{F}_{r})
.

3.2.1
RAAG algebra and mixed terms

In this section we exhibit the algebra
\mathcal{M}
which admits an interesting mapping
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}^{\times}
.

Algebra
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.

Define

\overline{\mathcal{M}}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}\langle\!\langle X_{1},Y_{1},\dots,X_{g},Y_{g}\rangle\!\rangle}{(X_{i}Y_{i},Y_{i}X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq g}}

that is, the associative algebra of formal power series in non-commutative variables
X_{i},Y_{i}
with integer coefficients, where we ignore all terms with adjacent
X_{i},Y_{i}
. To be clear, we retain
X_{i}Y_{j}
and
Y_{j}X_{i}
when
i\neq j
.

Observation 18
.

There exists a homomorphism
\overline{\rho}\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\times}
which sends
s_{i}\mapsto 1+X_{i}
and
t_{i}\mapsto 1+Y_{i}
.

Proof.

The symbols
X_{i},Y_{i}
commute in the algebra
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, so
1+X_{i},1+Y_{i}
commute in the multiplicative group
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{\times}
. In particular the surface relation is mapped to 1.
∎

Analogously as before, define

\mathcal{M}=\frac{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}{(r)+(X_{i},Y_{i})^{r^{k}+1}}

This is a finite
\mathbb{F}_{r}
-algebra. We define
G_{\mathcal{M}},\rho_{\mathcal{M}},\alpha_{\mathcal{M}},C_{\mathcal{M}}
analogously as before:
G_{\mathcal{M}}
is the subgroup of series with trailing term 1 (see
2
),
\rho
is the composition of the quotient
\overline{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
with
\overline{\rho}
,
\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}
comes from forgetting quadratic and higher terms (see
3
) and
C_{\mathcal{M}}
contains series which are 1 plus terms of degree
r^{k}
(see
4
). Note this is compatible with abelianisation (condition
(a)
of definition
10
), in the sense that
\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}\circ\rho_{\mathcal{M}}
is the canonical map
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g}\rightarrow\textnormal{H}_{1}(\Sigma_{g};\mathbb{F}_{r})
. Every
r^{k}
-th power lands in
C_{\mathcal{M}}
, so
(G,C,\rho,\alpha)
satisfy condition
10
(b)
.

Analogue of the polynomial substitute property.

The group
G_{\mathcal{M}}
satisfies a weaker analogue of the property
10
(c)
. We say a monomial
\prod_{j}a_{j}^{u_{j}}b_{j}^{v_{j}}
is
mixed
if
u_{i}+v_{i}\geq 1
for at least two different
i
; in other words, it contains, with nonzero exponents, two factors representing different standard generator pairs.

Property 19
.

Let
P
be a degree
r^{k}
mixed monomial in
a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{g},b_{g}
. There exists a group homomorphism
\Psi_{P}\colon C_{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r}
, such that
\Psi_{P}\big(x^{r^{k}}\big)=P\big(\alpha_{\mathcal{M}}(x)\big)
for all
x\in G_{\mathcal{M}}
.

We will remediate this weakening later by using complementary Property
21
.

Proof of Property
19
:

Write

P=\prod_{i=1}^{g}a_{i}^{u_{i}}b_{i}^{v_{i}}

Since
P
is mixed, either
P=\prod_{i}a_{i}^{u_{i}}
or
P=\prod_{i}b_{i}^{v_{i}}
or there exist
i_{0}\neq j_{0}
with
u_{i_{0}},v_{j_{0}}>0
. In the first two cases set
Q=\prod_{i}X_{i}^{u_{i}}
or
Q=\prod_{i}Y_{i}^{v_{i}}
respectively. In the third case set

Q=\prod_{i\neq i_{0}}X_{i}^{u_{i}}\cdot X_{i_{0}}^{u_{i_{0}}}\cdot Y_{j_{0}}^{v_{j_{0}}}\cdot\prod_{j\neq j_{0}}Y_{j}^{v_{j}}

In either case, such
Q
has no adjacent
X_{i}Y_{i}
or
Y_{i}X_{i}
.

Recall that the group
C_{\mathcal{M}}
is isomorphic to the
\mathbb{F}_{r}
-vector space with the basis
\{1+R\}_{R}
, where
R
ranges over monomials of total degree
r^{k}
which do not have
X_{i},Y_{i}
as adjacent factors. We argued that
Q
does not have neighbouring
X_{i},Y_{i}
, which means that
1+Q
is an element of this basis. Let
\Psi_{P}
be the coordinate projection
C_{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{r}
onto the 1-dimensional subspace

spanned by
1+Q
. The exponents of

match the corresponding exponents of

, so
\Psi_{P}
satisfies
\Psi_{P}(x^{r^{k}})=P\big(\alpha(x)\big)
for all

(compare equation (
6
)).
∎

3.2.2
Quaternion algebra and products of intersecting generators

Here we construct the algebra
\mathcal{H}
and an interesting mapping
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\times}
.

Algebra
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
and a homomorphism from the genus two surface group.

Consider the commutative ring
\mathbb{Z}[\![A,B]\!]
and take its quaternion algebra

\overline{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{Z}[\![A,B]\!][i,j,k]

The variables
A,B
commute with each other and with
i,j,k
, while
i,j,k
square to
-1
and pairwise anti-commute. Note that
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
can also be considered as the ring of formal power series over the skew-comutative ring
\mathbb{Z}[i,j,k]
.

First we establish the existence of an interesting homomorphism from a surface group of genus two into its group of units.

Lemma 20
.

There exists a group homomorphism
\overline{\tau}\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}^{\times}
satisfying

\overline{\tau}(s_{1})\equiv 1+Ai,\quad\overline{\tau}(t_{1})\equiv 1+Bj,\quad\overline{\tau}(s_{2})\equiv 1+Aj,\quad\overline{\tau}(t_{2})\equiv 1+Bi\qquad\text{mod }(A,B)^{2}

(7)

Proof.

We make an ansatz

\displaystyle\begin{split}\overline{\tau}(s_{1})=1+Ai+Ek,&\quad\overline{\tau}(t_{1})=1+Bj,\quad\overline{\tau}(s_{2})=1+Aj-Ek,\quad\overline{\tau}(t_{2})=1+Bi\\
&\text{where}\qquad E=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\big(1+4A(B-A)\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}-1\right)\end{split}

(8)

It can be seen from the explicit Taylor expansion of
\sqrt{1+x}
that
E
is a series with integer coefficients and contains only terms of degree two or higher. This means that the ansatz (
8
) is of the desired form (
7
). To show that it extends to a group homomorphism, it suffices to verify that the images of generators in
\overline{\mathcal{H}}^{\times}
satisfy the defining relation of
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{2}
. This boils down to an involved but straightforward calculation. We will use the fact that
E
is a solution to the equation

AB-E=A^{2}+E^{2}

(9)

Denote group-theoretic and algebra-theoretic commutators as
[\bullet,\bullet]_{G}
and
[\bullet,\bullet]_{A}
respectively. Under
8
we have

\displaystyle[\overline{\tau}(s_{1}),\overline{\tau}(t_{1})]_{G}

\displaystyle=(1+Ai+Ek)(1+Bj)(1+Ai+Ek)^{-1}(1+Bj)^{-1}=

\displaystyle=1+[Ai+Ek,Bj]_{A}(1+A^{2}+E^{2})^{-1}(1+B^{2})^{-1}(1-Ai-Ek)(1-Bj)=

\displaystyle=1+2(1+A^{2}+E^{2})^{-1}(1+B^{2})^{-1}B\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\Big(-(A^{2}+E^{2})B+(AB-E)i-(A^{2}+E^{2})j+(A+BE)k\Big)=

\displaystyle=1+2(1+w)^{-1}(1+B^{2})^{-1}B\big(-wB+wi-wj+(A+BE)k\big)

(10)

where
w=AB-E
(compare (
9
)). Let
\theta
be the automorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
induced by the automorphism of
Q_{8}
sending
i\mapsto j,j\mapsto i,k\mapsto-k
. We chose the images of generators which are interchanged by
\theta
, so

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\overline{\tau}(s_{2}),\overline{\tau}(t_{2})]_{G}&=[\theta(\overline{\tau}(s_{1})),\theta(\overline{\tau}(t_{1}))]_{G}=\theta\left([\overline{\tau}(s_{1}),\overline{\tau}(t_{1})]_{G}\right)=\\
&=1+2(1+w)^{-1}(1+B^{2})^{-1}B\big(-wB+wj-wi-(A+BE)k\big)\end{split}

(11)

Expressions
10
and
11
are quaternionic conjugates, so their product is

\displaystyle[\overline{\tau}(s_{1}),

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}(t_{1})]_{G}[\overline{\tau}(s_{2}),\overline{\tau}(t_{2})]_{G}=

\displaystyle=\left(1-2(1+w)^{-1}(1+B^{2})^{-1}B^{2}w\right)^{2}+4(1+w)^{-2}(1+B^{2})^{-2}B^{2}(2w^{2}+(A+BE)^{2})=

\displaystyle=1+4(1+w)^{-2}(1+B^{2})^{-2}B^{2}\Big(-(1+w)(1+B^{2})w+B^{2}w^{2}+2w^{2}+(A+BE)^{2}\Big)

This equals 1, provided that the expression

-(1+w)(1+B^{2})w+B^{2}w^{2}+2w^{2}+(A+BE)^{2}

vanishes. Expanding and substituting back

4
Comments

It is natural to ask whether the assumption of solvability in Theorem
2
is necessary. We ask the following

Question 22
.

Let
g\geq 2
,
n\geq 0
and
\theta\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{g,n}\rightarrow Q
be an arbitrary finite quotient. Does there exist a cover
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g,n}
with
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})\neq\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Q})
?

Our proof fundamentally relies on detecting loops using abelian quotients, so solvable
Q
seems to be the most general we can hope for with the current approach. Let us note a simple obstacle to potential approches to question
22
:

Observation 23
.

Let
\widetilde{\Sigma}\rightarrow\Sigma
be a finite-degree normal cover with deck group
G
, and
\theta\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma\rightarrow Q
be a nontrivial finite quotient. If the sizes of
G
and
Q
are coprime, then
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})=\textnormal{H}_{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Consider
\gamma\in\pi_{1}\widetilde{\Sigma}
. We want to show that
[\gamma]\in\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})
. If
\gamma\notin\ker\theta
then this holds by definition. Otherwise, take any
\lambda\in\pi_{1}\Sigma\setminus\ker\theta
. Then
[\gamma]
can be expressed as the difference of
[\gamma\lambda^{|G|}]
and
[\lambda^{|G|}]
; both of these classes belong to
\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})
, so again
[\gamma]\in\textnormal{H}_{1}^{\theta\neq 1}(\widetilde{\Sigma};\mathbb{Z})
.
∎
