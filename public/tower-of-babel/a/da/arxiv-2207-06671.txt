Abstract
. Given g ∈ ℕ ∪ { 0 , ∞ } g\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\} , let Σ g \Sigma_{g} denote the closed surface of genus g g with a Cantor set removed, if g < ∞ g<\infty ; or the blooming Cantor tree, when g = ∞ g=\infty . We construct a family 𝔅 ⁡ ( H ) \mathfrak{B}(H) of subgroups of Map ⁡ ( Σ g ) \Map(\Sigma_{g}) whose elements preserve a block decomposition of Σ g \Sigma_{g} , and eventually like act like an element of H H , where H H is a prescribed subgroup of the mapping class group of the block. The group 𝔅 ⁡ ( H ) \mathfrak{B}(H) surjects onto an appropriate symmetric Thompson group of Farley–Hughes [ FH15 ] ; in particular, it answers [ AV20 , Question 5.37] positively. Our main result asserts that 𝔅 ⁡ ( H ) \mathfrak{B}(H) is of type F n F_{n} if and only if H H is. As a consequence, for every g ∈ ℕ ∪ { 0 , ∞ } g\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\} and every n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , we construct a subgroup G < Map ⁡ ( Σ g ) G<\Map(\Sigma_{g}) that is of type F n F_{n} but not of type F n + 1 F_{n+1} , and which contains the mapping class group of every compact surface of genus ≤ g \leq g and with non-empty boundary.



1.
Introduction

Asymptotic mapping class groups of surfaces offer a fascinating bridge between mapping class groups of compact surfaces and Higman-Thompson groups
[
Bri07
,
Deh06
,
FK04
,
FK08
,
FK09
,
AF21
,
GLU20
,
SW21a
,
Ara+21
]
. Originally introduced by Funar–Kapoudjian
[
FK04
,
FK09
]
as discrete versions of the diffeomorphism group of the circle, they are strongly related to other classical families of groups that serve as “artinizations” of Higman-Thompson groups, such as the
braided Thompson groups
of Brin
[
Bri07
]
and Dehornoy
[
Deh06
]
; see
[
FKS13
]
for an account on this topic.

A substantial piece of motivation for asymptotic mapping class groups is that they are subgroups of mapping class groups of infinite-type surfaces, now commonly known as big mapping class groups. In this direction, a recent result of Funar–Neretin
[
FN18
]
asserts that, the subgroup of the mapping class group of a closed surface minus a Cantor set whose elements can be smoothly extended to the closed surface coincides with a certain asymptotic mapping class group (the group of
half-twists
of
[
FNg
]
). Moreover, by a result of Funar and the first-named author, this group is in fact dense in the mapping class group of the underlying surface.

Work of many authors shows that asymptotic mapping class groups –as well as some of their Thompson-like relatives– tend to enjoy strong finiteness properties; more concretely, they are often of type
F_{\infty}

[
BG84
,
FH15
,
Flu+13
,
Bux+16
,
GLU20
,
SW21
,
Ara+21
]
.

In this paper we introduce a more general family of groups, which this time surject onto the
symmetric Higman-Thompson groups
of Farley–Hughes
[
FH15
]
(more generally the Neretin groups
[
Ner92
]
), thus providing an answer to
[
AV20
, Question 5.37]
. Moreover, we will prove that they realize all the possible types of finiteness properties. We now offer an informal definition of our groups sufficient to understand our main result, and postpone all details until Section
3
.

Let
O
and
Y
be compact, connected, orientable surfaces, where
Y
is further assumed to be closed. For each
d\geq 2
, write
Y^{d}
for the surface that results from removing
d+1
open disks from
Y
.
From this data, we construct a non-compact surface
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
by gluing copies of

to

in a tree-like manner; see Figure
1
. Following
[
Ara+21
]
, the surface
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is called the
Cantor surface
determined by
d
,
O
and
Y
. Each of standard copies of
Y^{d}
used to construct
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is called a

. We will be interested in the group
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
whose elements are isotopy classes of homeomorphisms of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
which
eventually
send blocks to blocks and, on each block in a connected component, they
act like
an element of a fixed subgroup
H
of the mapping class group of the block; see Section
3
for details. In the case when

is trivial, the group
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is the
asymptotic mapping class group
of
[

]
.

As we will see in Proposition

, there is a surjective homeomorphism from
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
onto an appropriate
symmetric Higman-Thompson group
, which simply records the action of elements of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
permute the ends of

. As a consequence, this answers Question 5.37 of
[
AV20
]
.

As we explain next, the group

has the same finiteness properties as
H
. Recall that a group
G
is of
type
F_{n}
if there exists an aspherical CW-complex whose fundamental group is

and whose
n
-skeleton is finite; a group is of
type
F_{\infty}
if it is of type
F_{n}
for all

. Our main result is:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
O
be a compact orientable surface, and
Y
a closed surface of genus
\leq 1
. Then
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is of type
F_{n}
if and only if
H
is.

Remark 1.2
.

A minor variation of our construction produces a slightly larger family
\mathfrak{B}_{d,r}
of groups, which surject onto the more general symmetric Thompson groups

; see Remark
3.1

Applications.
We now give an application of the above theorem. Write
\Sigma_{g}
, with
0\leq g\leq\infty
, for the closed surface of genus
g\geq 0
with a Cantor set removed (for
g<\infty
) or the

(for
g=\infty
); see Section

. By the classification of infinite-type surfaces
[

,
Ric63
]
, we know that

When

but not of type

For every

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we provide the background material needed in our arguments. We refer the reader to
[
FM12
]
and
[
AV20
]
, respectively, for details on mapping class groups of finite and infinite type surfaces.

2.1.
Surfaces

Throughout, all surfaces will be connected, orientable, and second countable. If a surface
S
has non empty boundary, we will further assume that
\partial S
has finitely many connected components. We say that
S
has
finite type
if
\pi_{1}(S)
is finitely generated; otherwise it has
infinite type
. An
end
of
S
is an element of

\varprojlim\pi_{0}(S\setminus K),

where the inverse limit is taken over all compact sets
K\subset S
, directed with respect to inclusion. We say that an end is
planar
if it has a neighborhood of genus zero; otherwise it is
non-planar
. Denote the set of ends by
\Ends(S)
and its subset of non-planar ends by
\Ends_{np}(S)
.

The set of ends
\Ends(S)
becomes a topological space by endowing it with the limit topology arising from the discrete topology on each of the terms defining the inverse limit above. Equipped with this topology,
\Ends(S)
is homeomorphic to a closed subspace of the Cantor set, and
\Ends_{np}(S)
is a closed subset of
\Ends(S)
. By work of Kerékjártó
[
Ker23
]
and Richards
[
Ric63
]
, two surfaces are homeomorphic if and only if they have the same genus and number of boundary components, and there is a homeomorphism between their space of ends that restricts to a homeomorphism between the subspaces of non-planar ends; see
[
Ric63
]
for a precise statement.

For
g\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, we denote by
\Sigma_{g}
the connected orientable surface without boundary, of genus
g
and with a Cantor space of ends, all of them planar; we remark that
\Sigma_{0}
is usually referred to as the
Cantor tree surface
. Similarly, let
\Sigma_{\infty}
be the
blooming Cantor tree surface
, namely the connected orientable surface with empty boundary, infinite genus, and whose space of ends is a Cantor set and every end is non-planar. Finally, for
0\leq g\leq\infty
and
b\geq 1
write
\Sigma_{g}^{b}
for the surface that results by removing
b
open discs from
\Sigma_{g}
.

2.2.
Curves

By a
curve
on
S
we mean the isotopy class of a simple closed curve on
S
. We say that a curve on
S
is
essential
if it does not bound a disc or an annulus whose other boundary component is a boundary component of
S
.

2.3.
Mapping class group

Given a surface
S
, write
\Homeo^{+}(S,\partial S)
for the group of orientation preserving self-homeomorphisms of
S
that restrict to the identity on
\partial S
. The
mapping class group
of
S
is

\Map(S):=\pi_{0}\left(\Homeo^{+}(S,\partial S)\right).

We will often blur the distinction between a homeomorphism and its isotopy class; however, if we want to make this distinction explicit, we will use
[f]
to denote the isotopy class of the homeomorphism
f
.

The mapping class group
\Map(S)
is countable if and only if
S
has finite type. When
S
has infinite type, we endow
\Map(S)
with the quotient topology of the compact-open topology on
\Homeo^{+}(S,\partial S)
; with respect to this topology,
\Map(S)
is a zero-dimensional, non-locally compact group; see
[
AV20
]
.

We will also need a version of the mapping class group that allows boundary components to be permuted. To this end, for each boundary circle
a\subset\partial S
we fix a homeomorphism
p_{a}:a\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
, which we call a
parametrization
of
a
. We say that an orientation preserving homeomorphism
f:S\to S

respects the boundary parametrization
if
p_{f(a)}\circ f_{|a}\equiv p_{a}
for all
a\in\pi_{0}(\partial S)
. We now set:

Definition 2.1

(Boundary-permuting mapping class group)
.

The
boundary permuting mapping class group

\Map_{\partial}(S)
is the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving self-homeomorphisms of
S
that respect the boundary parametrization, modulo isotopies that fix
\partial S
pointwise.

Remark 2.2
.

Since we are assuming that surfaces have a finite number of boundary components,
\Map(S)
is a (normal) finite-index subgroup of
\Map_{\partial}(S)
; moreover, the two groups coincide if and only if
S
has at most one boundary component.

Finally, given a surface
S
and
A\subseteq\pi_{0}(\partial S)
, we define
\Map_{\partial}(S,A)
to be the subgroup of
\Map_{\partial}(S)
whose elements restrict to the identity on every element of
A
. Of course, if
A=\partial S
, then
\Map_{\partial}(S,A)=\Map(S)
.

3.
Cantor surfaces and block mapping class groups

We now recall the definition of a
Cantor surface
from
[
Ara+21
]
. Let
O
be an arbitrary compact orientable surface, and
Y
homeomorphic to a sphere or a torus. Denote by
Y^{d}
the surface obtained from
Y
by removing
d+1
open disks. Write
b_{0},\ldots,b_{d}
for the boundary components of
Y^{d}
; we will refer to
b_{0}
as the
top
boundary component of
Y^{d}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,d
, we choose an orientation-reversing homeomorphism
\mu_{i}:b_{0}\to b_{i}
. We inductively define a family
\{O_{n}\}
of compact surfaces as follows:

(1)

O_{1}
is the surface that results by removing an open disk from
O
.

(2)

O_{2}
is the surface obtained by gluing (with respect to some pre-fixed orientation-reversing homeomorphism) a copy of
Y^{d}
, along its top boundary component, to the boundary of
O_{1}
created in the previous step;

(3)

For each
n\geq 2
,
O_{n}
is the surface obtained by gluing a copy of
Y^{d}
, along its top boundary component, to each of the boundary components of
O_{n-1}\setminus O_{n-2}
, using the homeomorphisms
\mu_{i}
above.

The
Cantor surface

{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is the union of the surfaces
O_{k}
above. The closure of each of the connected components of
O_{n}\setminus O_{n-1}
is called a
block
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
; by construction, every block is homeomorphic to
Y^{d}
. The
top boundary

\partial_{T}B
of a block
B
is the image of the top boundary of
Y^{d}
under the gluing map.

Example of a Cantor surface

Remark 3.1
.

Following
[
SW21a
]
and
[
Ara+21
]
, one may slightly generalize the above construction by first removing
r\geq 1
open discs from
O
and then gluing copies of
Y^{d}
to each of these boundary components. Our main result has a direct translation to the case of the surfaces
{\mathcal{C}}_{d,r}(O,Y)
obtained in this way; however, we have decided to restrict our attention to the case
r=1
in order to minimize the notation and level of technicality.

A
suited subsurface
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is a connected subsurface that is the union of
O_{1}
and finitely many blocks; in particular, a suited subsurface is always compact. A
suited curve
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is a boundary component of a suited subsurface that is not one of the original boundary components of
O
. Given a suited subsurface
Z
, we refer to the boundary curves of
Z
that are suited curves of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
as the
suited boundary components
of
Z
; the rest (if any) are called the
primary boundary components
of
Z
. Given a suited boundary component
a\subset\partial Z
, we say that a connected component
X\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)\setminus Z
is
adjacent to

a
if the intersection of the closures of
Z
and
X
is equal to
a
.

Remark 3.2
.

The space of ends of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is always homeomorphic to a Cantor set, and every two ends of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
are related by a homeomorphism of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
. In particular, by the classification of non-compact surfaces of Kerékjártó-Richards
[
Ric63
]
, one has:

•

If
Y\cong{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
, then
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)\cong\Sigma_{g}^{b}
, where
g
and
b
are, respectively, the genus and number of boundary components of
O
.

•

If
Y\cong{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
, then
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)\cong\Sigma_{\infty}^{b}
, where
b
is the number of boundary components of
O
.

For the sake of notational convenience, for the rest of the section we will denote
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
simply by
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
, unless we need to make explicit reference to the surfaces
O
and
Y
.

Definition 3.3

(Block homeomorphism)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
be a Cantor surface. We say that a homeomorphism
f:{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
is
block
if there exists a suited subsurface
Z\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
such that:

(1)

f(Z)
is also suited;

(2)

The restriction
f:{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\setminus Z\to{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\setminus f(Z)
sends blocks to blocks.

The suited subsurface
Z
in the definition above is called a
defining surface
for
f
. Note that any surface containing a defining surface for
f
is itself a defining surface for
f
. As an immediate consequence, the composition of two block homeomorphisms is also block; in particular, the set of block homeomorphisms of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
is in fact a group under composition.

Next, for each block
B\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
we fix, once and for all, a homeomorphism
\iota_{B}:B\to Y^{d}
that takes the top boundary component of
B
, denoted
\partial_{T}B
, to
b_{0}
, namely the top boundary component of
Y^{d}
. The homeomorphism
\iota_{B}
induces an isomorphism (abusing notation)

\iota_{B}:\Map_{\partial}(B,\partial_{T}B)\to\Map_{\partial}(Y^{d},b_{0}),

and we will use
\iota_{B}
to identify
\Map_{\partial}(B,\partial_{T}B)
with
\Map_{\partial}(Y^{d},b_{0})
without further mention. Note that
\iota_{B}
induces an order on the remaining boundaries of
B
from that of
Y^{d}
.

Definition 3.4

(
H
-block homeomorphism)
.

Let
H<\Map_{\partial}(Y^{d},b_{0})
, and
f:{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
be a block homeomorphism with defining surface
Z
. We say that
f
is
H
-
block
if for every suited boundary component
a\subset\partial Z
there is an element
h_{a}\in H
such that, for every block
B
in the connected component of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\setminus Z
adjacent to
a
, we have that

[\iota_{f(B)}\circ f\circ\iota_{B}^{-1}]\equiv h_{a}.

We will say that the boundary curve
a
is
labeled by the element

h_{a}
, that
f
is
labeled by the tuple

\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
outside of
Z
(where
n
is the number of suited boundary components of
Z
), and that
f

eventually acts like

\mathbf{h}
.

Definition 3.5

(
H
-block mapping class group)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
be a Cantor surface, and
H<\Map_{\partial}(Y^{d},b_{0})
. The
block mapping class group with local group

H
, denoted
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
, is the group of isotopy classes of
H
-block homeomorphisms of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
.

In the case when
H=\{1\}
, we will simply write
\mathfrak{B}_{d}=\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
. We remark that this case corresponds to the asymptotic mapping class groups of
[
Ara+21
]
which, as mentioned in the introduction, are in turn an abstraction of the ones considered by Funar, Kapoudjian and the first named author
[
FK04
,
FK09
,
AF21
]
.

Remark 3.6
.

The group
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is always countable, as
\Map(Y,b_{0})
is countable and so is the set of suited subsurfaces of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
. In stark contrast, the group of isotopy classes of block homeomorphisms of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is uncountable.

Remark 3.7
.

Considering the surfaces
{\mathcal{C}}_{d,r}(O,Y)
as in Remark
3.1
would give rise to more general block mapping class groups
\mathfrak{B}_{d,r}(O,Y)
. As will become apparent, the obvious adaptation of our arguments below would yield the analog of Theorem
1.1
for this (a priori, larger) set of groups.

3.1.
An alternate description

We now give a useful alternate representation of elements of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
, reminiscent of the
tree-pair
representation of elements of (symmetric) Higman-Thompson groups; see Section
4
.

Let
f\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
, and consider a defining subsurface
Z\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
for
f
, with suited boundary components
a_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Write
X_{i}
for the connected component of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\setminus Z
adjacent to
a_{i}
, and denote by
f_{Z}
(resp.
h_{i}
) the restriction of
f
to
Z
(resp. the element of
H
that
f
acts like on
X_{i}
). With this notation,
f
is determined by the tuple

\left(Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)\right),

where
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
. Finally, observe that, since defining surfaces are not unique, there are countably many different such tuples representing the same element of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
. We use
[Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)]
to denote the equivalence class (with respect to the obvious equivalence relation) containing
\left(Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)\right)
.

Remark 3.8
.

If one takes the labeling to be the same for all components, i.e.
\mathbf{h}=(h,\cdots,h)
for some
h\in H
, then the set of all such elements forms a subgroup
\mathfrak{DB}_{d}(H)
. Since the surjection
\mathfrak{DB}_{d}(H)\to H
mapping
[Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)]
to
h
splits, we have an isomorphism

\mathfrak{DB}_{d}(H)\cong\mathfrak{B}_{d}(\{1\})\rtimes H.

Now
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(\{1\})
is of type
F_{\infty}

[
Ara+21
]
, hence
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(\{1\})\rtimes H
if of type
F_{n}
if and only if
H
is
[
Geo08
, Section 7.2]
.

4.
The relation with symmetric Higman-Thompson groups

In this section we will relate our groups to the so-called
symmetric Higman-Thompson groups
, introduced by Farley and Hughes in
[
FH15
]
. We commence by reviewing the construction of symmetric Higman-Thompson groups; see
[
FH15
]
for a detailed treatment.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{d}
be the infinite rooted tree of degree
d\geq 2
, where every vertex other than the root has degree
d+1
. We endow
\mathcal{T}_{d}
with the path-metric that arises by deeming each edge to have length one; with respect to this path-metric, the end space of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is naturally homeomorphic with the set of geodesics emanating from the root, which in turn is homeomorphic to the standard Cantor set
\mathfrak{C}
.

Given a vertex
v\in\mathcal{T}_{d}
at distance
k\geq 0
from the root, we say that the vertex
w
is a
descendant
of
v
if
d(v,w)=1
and
w
is at distance
k+1
from the root. A subtree
F
of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is
complete
if, for every vertex
v\in F
, whenever
F
contains a descendant of
v
, it contains all of its descendants. Observe that if
F
is a complete finite rooted subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
then, for every
c\in\mathfrak{C}
there is a unique leaf
l
of
F
such that the geodesic in
\mathcal{T}_{d}
representing
c
contains
l
; we say that
l
is the leaf of
F

nearest to

c
.

4.1.
Higman-Thompson groups

Let
F_{-}
and
F_{+}
be complete finite rooted subtrees of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, with the same number of leaves, and
b
a bijection between their sets of leaves. Then the triple
(F_{-},b,F_{+})
extends to a homeomorphism
g:\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}
by identifying (in the obvious way) the infinite subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
below the leaf
l
with that below the leaf
b(l)
. We say that the triple
(F_{-},b,F+)
is a
tree-pair
representative of
g
; of course, there are infinitely many tree-pair representatives of any given homeomorphism.

A pleasant exercise shows that if two self-homeomorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
admit a tree-pair representative, then the same is true about their composition. Thus, we define the
Higman-Thompson group

V_{d}

[
Hi74
]
as the group of homeomorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
that have a tree-pair representative; we remark that
V_{2}
is the classical Thompson group
V
. A well-known fact
[
Bro87
]
is that
V_{d}
is of type
F_{\infty}
.

4.2.
Symmetric Thompson groups

Next, we recall the definition of the
symmetric
versions of the Higman-Thompson groups, introduced by Farley and Hughes
[
FH15
]
.

Enumerate the descendants of every vertex with
\{0,\dots,d-1\}
. Since the (Cantor) space of ends of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
can be identify with the set of geodesic of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, we can also identify it with the set of infinite sequences
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\ldots)
, where
0\leq t_{i}<d
for every
i\geq 1
.

Let
F_{-}
and
F_{+}
be complete finite subtrees of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, with the same number of leaves, and
b
a bijection between their sets of leaves. Write
l_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
for the leaves of
F_{-}
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of the symmetric group
\Sym(d)
on
d
elements. With respect to the above notation, for every leaf
l_{i}
we chose
g_{i}\in G
, and write
\mathbf{g}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
.
Then the tuple

(F_{-},(b,\mathbf{g}),F_{+})

induces a homeomorphism
\psi:\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{C}
as follows. Let
c\in\mathfrak{C}
be given in terms of an infinite sequence
(c_{1},c_{2},\dots)
, and consider the leaf
l_{i}
of
F_{-}
nearest to
c
, so that
(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m})
represents the leaf
l_{i}
. Then the leaf
b(l_{i})
is represented by a finite sequence
b(l_{i})
(using a slight abuse of notation), and define

\psi(c)=(b(l_{i}),(g_{i}(c_{m+1}),g_{i}(c_{m+2}),\dots).

We say that
\psi

eventually acts like

\mathbf{g}
, and the tuple
(F_{-},(b,\mathbf{g}),F_{+})
is called the
symmetric tree-pair representative
of
\psi
; as before, there are infinitely many symmetric tree-pair representatives of the same homeomorphism of
\mathfrak{C}
.

Again, if two self-homeomorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
admit a tree-pair representative, then the same is true about their composition.
Hence we define the
symmetric Higman-Thompson group

V_{d}(G)

with local group

G
as the group of homeomorphisms of
\mathfrak{C}
that admit a
G
tree-pair representative.
Observe that if
G=\{1\}
then
V_{d}(G)=V_{d}
.

Finally, we note that the groups
V_{d}(G)
are examples of
FSS groups
in the sense of Farley and Hughes, which are all of type
F_{\infty}

[
FH15
]
.

Remark 4.1
.

The groups
V_{d}(G)
are part of a larger family of symmetric Higman-Thompson groups
V_{d,r}(G)
groups, where one allows
r
-forests of
d
-valent rooted trees instead of just
d
-valent rooted trees. These groups are also FFS groups in the sense of Farley and Hughes
[
FH15
]
; as such, they are of type
F_{\infty}
.

4.3.
A surjective homomorphism

Recall from Section
3
that the Cantor surface
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
is the union of compact surfaces
O_{k}
, with
k\geq 1
.

We construct an abstract simplicial graph
\Gamma
as follows: the vertices of
\Gamma
are the blocks of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
plus
O_{1}
, and two vertices are adjacent in
\Gamma
if and only if the corresponding (suited) subsurfaces of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
share one suited boundary component. Observe that, by construction,
\Gamma
is an infinite rooted
d
-ary tree, with the root corresponding to
O_{1}
; in particular,
\Gamma
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}_{d}
. Fixing one such isomorphism, there is an obvious map

\tau:{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)\to\mathcal{T}_{d}

such that the preimage of the root is
O_{1}
, and the preimage of the midpoint of every edge of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is a suited curve. Next, there is a natural surjective homomorphism

q:\Map(Y^{d},b_{0})\to\Sym(d)

which records the manner in which the elements of
\Map(Y^{d},b_{0})
permute the boundary components of
Y^{d}
.

Let
H<\Map(Y^{d},b_{0})
and write
\bar{H}
for the
q
-image of
H
. Let
f\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
, and choose a tree-pair representative
(Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z))
for
f
. Then
f
induces a bijection
b_{f}
between the leaves of
\tau(Z)
and
\tau(f(Z))
; also, on the connected component
X_{i}
of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)\setminus Z
, the homeomorphism
f
induces, via
\tau
, an isomorphism
f^{*}_{X_{i}}:\tau(X_{i})\to\tau(f(X_{i}))
that acts like
q(h_{i})
. As such, the tuple

\left(\tau(X),(b_{f},q(\textbf{h})),\tau(f(X)\right)

is an element of
V_{d}(\bar{H})
. An exercise in the definitions of tree-pair representatives shows that the map

(Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z))\mapsto\left(\tau(X),(b_{f},q(\textbf{h})),\tau(f(X)\right),

descends to a map

\pi:\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)\to V_{d}(\bar{H}),

which is in addition a homomorphism. Moreover, one has:

Proposition 4.2
.

The homomorphism
\pi:\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)\to V_{d}(\bar{H})
is surjective.

Proof.

Consider a tree-pair representative
(F_{-},(b,\mathbf{g}),F_{+})
of an element of the group
V_{d}(\bar{H})
, and denote by
F_{-}^{\prime}
(resp
F^{\prime}_{+}
) the result of removing from
F_{-}
(resp.
F_{+}
) their leaves and the open half edges incident on these leaves. We know that
Z_{-}:=\tau^{-1}(F^{\prime}_{-})
and
Z:=\tau^{-1}(F^{\prime}_{+})
are suited subsurfaces of
\mathcal{C}_{d}(O,Y)
.
Let
\hat{f}:Z_{-}\to Z_{+}
be a homeomorphism such that the induced bijection between the sets of boundary components of
Z_{-}
and
Z_{+}
coincides with
b
. Writing
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
for the connected components of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
, for each
i
we choose an element
h_{i}\in H
with
q(h_{i})=g_{i}
. Then, the element
f\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
with tree-pair representative

(Z_{-},(\hat{f},\mathbf{h}),Z_{+})

maps to
(F_{-},(b,\mathbf{g}),F_{+})\in V_{d}(\bar{H})
under
\pi
, as desired.

\square

Remark 4.3
.

After Remarks
3.7
and
4.1
, a direct adaptation of the arguments above yield the existence of a surjective homomorphism

\mathfrak{B}_{d,r}(H)\to V_{d,r}(\bar{H}),

where
V_{d,r}(\bar{H})
is the symmetric Higman-Thompson group with
r
roots.

Remark 4.4
.

Although it is not very relevant to our discussion, it is worth mentioning that the group of block homeomorphisms of
\mathcal{C}_{d}(O,Y)
surjects onto the Neretin group of
[
Ner92
]
.

5.
Finiteness properties

5.1.
The complex

In this section, we will construct an infinite-dimensional contractible cube complex
{\mathfrak{X}}_{d}(O,Y,H)
on which
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
acts cellularly, closely related to the classical
Stein–Farley
complexes for Higman–Thompson groups
[
Ste92
,
Far03
]
, and very much inspired by the complex constructed in
[
GLU20
,
SW21
,
Ara+21
]
.

Here the surfaces
O
and
Y
and the integer
d
will play virtually no role in the discussion, and so we will simply write
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
and
{\mathfrak{B}}={\mathfrak{B}}_{d}(H)
. We remark that the definitions and results in this section are direct translations of those in
[
Ara+21
, Section 5]
; we include short proofs for completeness.

5.2.
The Stein complex

Consider all ordered pairs
(Z,f)
where
Z
is a suited subsurface of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
f\in{\mathfrak{B}}
. We will say a pair
(Z_{1},f_{1})
is equivalent to a pair
(Z_{2},f_{2})
, written
(Z_{1},f_{1})\sim(Z_{2},f_{2})
, if and only if there are representing homeomorphisms
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
(abusing notation) such that
f_{2}^{-1}\circ f_{1}
maps
Z_{1}
to
Z_{2}
where
Z_{1}
is a defining surface for
f_{2}^{-1}\circ f_{1}
as an
H
-block homeomorphism. A straightforward calculation confirms that this relation is symmetric, reflexive, and transitive, i.e. an equivalence relation.

We will denote by
[Z,f]
the equivalence class containing
(Z,f)
with respect to this relation, and write
{\mathcal{S}}
for the set of all equivalence classes. Importantly, the group
{\mathfrak{B}}
acts on
{\mathcal{S}}
via left multiplication as follows:

g\cdot[Z,f]=[Z,g\circ f].

Recall that by definition, a suited subsurface is the union of
O_{1}
and finitely many additional blocks. We define the
complexity

h(Z,f)
of
(Z,f)
to be the number of blocks in
Z
. We observe that if
(Z_{1},f_{1})\sim(Z_{2},f_{2})
then
h(Z_{1},f_{1})=h(Z_{2},f_{2})
and thus
h
gives a well-defined function
h:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition 5.1
.

The
complexity
of
[Z,f]\in{\mathcal{S}}
is
h(Z,f)
for some, and hence any, representative of
[Z,f]
.

Let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
be two classes in
{\mathcal{S}}
and define
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
if and only if
x_{1}=[Z_{1},f]
and
x_{2}=[Z_{2},f]
for suited subsurfaces with
Z_{1}\subseteq Z_{2}
. When the inclusion is proper, we write
x_{1}<x_{2}
. The following is a word-for-word adaptation of Lemmas 5.2 and 5.3 of
[
Ara+21
]
:

Lemma 5.2
.

({\mathcal{S}},\leq)
is a directed poset. Moreover, if
x<y\in{\mathcal{S}}
then
h(x)<h(y)
. In particular, its geometric realization
|{\mathcal{S}}|
is contractible

Following the same strategy of
[
Ara+21
]
, we now consider a second, finer relation
\preceq
on
{\mathcal{S}}
. Let
x_{1},x_{2}
be two vertices in
{\mathcal{S}}
. We will say
x_{1}\preceq x_{2}
if
x_{1}=[Z,f]
and
x_{2}=[Z\cup X,f]
where
X
is a disjoint union of blocks. As usual, if
x_{1}\preceq x_{2}
and
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, we will write
x_{1}\prec x_{2}
. This finer order is no longer a partial ordering as it is not transitive. Nevertheless, it is true that if
x_{1}\preceq x_{3}
and
x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}
then
x_{1}\preceq x_{2}\preceq x_{3}
. Thus we can define a simplex
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}
in
|{\mathcal{S}}|
to be
elementary
if
x_{0}\preceq x_{k}
.

Definition 5.3

(Stein complex)
.

The
Stein complex

{\mathfrak{X}}={\mathfrak{X}}(O,Y,H)
is the full subcomplex of
|{\mathcal{S}}|
consisting of elementary simplices.

We note that the Stein complex
{\mathfrak{X}}
is more tractable than
|{\mathcal{S}}|
but still has many of the properties we care about. For instance, the action of
{\mathfrak{B}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
preserves the relation
\preceq
, so thus
{\mathfrak{B}}
has a simplicial action on
{\mathfrak{X}}
. Moreover, a classical result of Quillen
[
Qui78
]
implies the following, whose proof is a verbatim copy of Lemmas 5.6 and 5.7 of
[
Ara+21
]
:

Proposition 5.4
.

The Stein complex
{\mathfrak{X}}
is contractible.

5.3.
The Stein-Farley cube complex

In this section, we group the simplices of
{\mathfrak{X}}
together to form a cube complex. Let
x
and
y
be vertices of
{\mathfrak{X}}
with
x\leq y
, and set
[x,y]=\{z\mid x\leq z\leq y\}
. The same argument of
[
Ara+21
, Lemma 5.8]
shows that
[x,y]
has the structure of a finite Boolean lattice; in particular, its geometric realization is (the barycentric subdivision of) a cube of dimension
h(y)-h(x)
. Moreover, every two cubes intersect over a face, by the same argument as in
[
Ara+21
, Lemma 5.10]
. In particular, we have (see
[
Ara+21
, Lemma 5.11]
):

Proposition 5.5
.

The complex
{\mathfrak{X}}
has the structure of a cube complex with each cube defined by an interval
[x,y]
with
x\preceq y
.

Definition 5.6

(Stein-Farley complex)
.

The complex
{\mathfrak{X}}
equipped with the above cubical structure is called the
Stein-Farley cube complex
associated to the Cantor surface
{\mathcal{C}}
and the subgroup
H<\Map_{\partial}(Y^{d},b_{0})
.

6.
Brown’s Criterion and Discrete Morse Theory on the Stein-Farley cube complex

As was the case in
[
Ara+21
]
, now that we have an action of
{\mathfrak{B}}
on the Stein-Farley complex, we will want to apply Brown’s criterion to deduce the finiteness properties
[
Bro87
]
. First, recall that a filtration
\{K_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
of a space
K
is called
essentially
n
-connected
if for every
i\geq 1
, there exists an
i^{\prime}\geq i
such that
\pi_{\ell}(K_{i}\rightarrow K_{i^{\prime}})
is trivial for all
\ell\leq n
.

We remark that the definitions and results in this Section are direct translations of those in
[
Ara+21
, Section 6]
; we include short proofs for completeness.

Theorem 6.1

(Brown’s Criterion)
.

Let
n
be in
{\mathbb{N}}
and assume that a group
G
acts on an
(n-1)
-connected CW-complex
{\mathcal{K}}
. Assume that the stabilizer of every
k
-cell of
{\mathcal{K}}
is of type
F_{n-k}
. Let
\{{\mathcal{K}}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be a filtration of
{\mathcal{K}}
such that each
{\mathcal{K}}_{j}
is finite modulo the action of
G
. Then
G
is of type
F_{n}
if and only if
\{{\mathcal{K}}_{j}\}_{j}
is essentially
(n-1)
-connected.

First, observe that the complexity function given in Definition
5.1
gives a function
h:{\mathfrak{X}}^{(0)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
which naturally extends affinely to each cube to obtain a
height function

h:{\mathfrak{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},

which is
{\mathfrak{B}}
-equivariant with respect to the trivial action of
{\mathfrak{B}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}.
Each cube has a unique vertex at which
h
is maximized (respectively minimized) which we call the the
top
(respectively
bottom
) of the cube. Note that this agrees with our definition of top and bottom above. Now, for any integer
k
, we define
{\mathfrak{X}}^{\leq k}
to be the subcomplex of
{\mathfrak{X}}
spanned by vertices with height at most
k
. An obvious adaptation of
[
Ara+21
, Lemma 6.2]
yields:

Lemma 6.2
.

The group
{\mathfrak{B}}
acts cocompactly on
{\mathfrak{X}}^{\leq k}
for all
1\leq k<\infty
.

Next, we prove:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
C
be a cube in
{\mathfrak{X}}
with bottom vertex given by
x=[Z,g]
. Then the stabilizer of
C
in
{\mathfrak{B}}_{d}(H)
is isomorphic to a finite index subgroup of
\Map_{\partial}(Z)\ltimes H^{m}
where
m
is the number of suited boundary components of
Z
.

Proof.

Fix a cube
C
in
{\mathfrak{X}}
and let
x=[Z,g]
be its bottom vertex. We may assume without loss of generality that
g={{\rm id}}
. The cube
C
is spanned by vertices obtained by attaching blocks to a collection
A
of suited boundary components. Now any element
f\in{\mathfrak{B}}
stabilizes the cube if and only if it both fixes
x
and leaves
A
invariant. The element
f
fixes
x=[Z,{{\rm id}}]
if and only if
f
sends
Z
to
Z
and is labeled by a tuple
\bf h
outside of
Z
and so it is therefore a subgroup of
\Map_{\partial}(Z)\ltimes H^{m}
. Moreover
\Map(Z)\ltimes H^{m}
preserves the cube and so the result follows from Remark
2.2
.

\square

Since mapping class groups of compact surfaces are of type
F_{\infty}

[
Har71
]
, and being of type
F_{n}
is invariant under short exact sequences and passing to finite-index subgroups
[
Geo08
, Section 7.2]
, the previous lemma implies:

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
C
be a cube in
{\mathfrak{X}}
. Then the stabilizer of
C
in
{\mathfrak{B}}_{d}(H)
has type
F_{n}
if
H
does.

6.1.
Descending links

We have now checked all of the conditions of Brown’s Criterion except one, namely that the filtration
\{{\mathfrak{X}}^{\leq m}\}_{m}
is essentially
(n-1)
-connected. In fact, we will show the connectivity of the pairs
({\mathfrak{X}}^{\leq m},{\mathfrak{X}}^{\leq{m+1}})
tends to
\infty
as
m
tends to
\infty
; here, recall that a pair of spaces
(L,K)
with
K\subseteq L
is
k
-connected if the inclusion map
K\hookrightarrow L
induces an isomorphism in their respective homotopy groups
\pi_{j}
for
j<k
and an epimorphism in
\pi_{k}
. To establish this, we will apply
discrete Morse theory
to reduce the problem to analyzing the connectivity of the
descending links
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a piecewise Euclidean cell complex, and let
h
be a map from the set of vertices of
{\mathcal{K}}
to the integers, such that each cell has a unique vertex maximizing
h
. Call
h
a
height function
, and
h(y)
the
height
of
y
for vertices
y
in
{\mathcal{K}}
. For
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, define
{\mathcal{K}}^{\leq t}
to be the full subcomplex of
{\mathcal{K}}
spanned by vertices
y
with
h(y)\leq t
. Similarly, define
{\mathcal{K}}^{<t}
and
{\mathcal{K}}^{=t}
. The
descending star

\dst(y)
of a vertex
y
is defined to be the open star of
y
in
{\mathcal{K}}^{\leq h(y)}
. The
descending link

\dlk(y)
of
y
is given by the set of “local directions” starting at
y
and pointing into
\dst(y)
, that is, the link of
y
in
\dst(y)
. For more details see
[
BB97
]
. Now the following Morse Lemma is a consequence of
[
BB97
, Corollary 2.6]
.

Lemma 6.5

(Morse Lemma)
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a piecewise Euclidean cell complex and let
h
be a height function on
{\mathcal{K}}
.

(1)

Suppose that for any vertex
y
with
h(y)=t
,
\dlk(y)
is
(k-1)
-connected. Then the pair
({\mathcal{K}}^{\leq t},{\mathcal{K}}^{<t})
is
k
-connected.

(2)

Suppose that for any vertex
y
with
h(y)\geq t
,
\dlk(y)
is
(k-1)
-connected. Then
({\mathcal{K}},{\mathcal{K}}^{<t})
is
k
-connected.

Recall that the height function for the Stein-Farley complex
{\mathfrak{X}}
is the affine extension of the complexity function
h
given in Definition
5.1
.

6.2.
From descending links to the complex of labeled blocks

In this subsection we will determine the connectivity properties of descending links. Behind all of our arguments is Hatcher-Wahl’s notion of a
complete join complex
, which we now recall:

Definition 6.6

(Complete join)
.

A simplicial map
\pi:K\to L
between simplicial complexes is said to be a
complete join
if

•

\pi
is surjective;

•

The restriction of
\pi
to every simplex of
K
is injective;

•

For each simplex
\sigma=\langle x_{0},\ldots,x_{p}\rangle
of
L
, the subcomplex of
K
consisting of those simplices that map to
\sigma
is equal to the join
\pi^{-1}(x_{0})\ast\cdots\ast\pi^{-1}(x_{p})
.

By a slight abuse of notation, we will say that
K
is a
complete join over

L
.

The interest of the above definition in our situation is the following result, which is Proposition 3.5 of
[
HW10
]
; here, recall that a complex is
weakly Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
m
if it is
(m-1)
-connected and the link of every
p
-simplex is
(m-p-2)
-connected.

Proposition 6.7
.

If
K
is a complete join complex
over a weakly Cohen-Macaulay complex
L
of dimension
m
, then
K
is also weakly Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
m
.

Let
x=[X,f]
be a vertex of
{\mathfrak{X}}
; we want to analyze the connectivity properties of its descending link. First, we may assume that
f={\rm id}
. Then a simplex in the descending link of
x
is determined by
z=[Z,g]
, where there exists a collection
\{Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{p}\}
of pairwise distinct and pairwise disjoint blocks such that
[Z^{\prime},g]\sim[X,\rm id]
with
Z^{\prime}=Z\cup\bigcup_{i=0}^{p}Y_{p}
.

Our goal now is to map
\lk^{\downarrow}(x)
onto a weakly Cohen-Macaulay complex in such a way that
\lk^{\downarrow}(x)
is a complete join complex over it. To this end, we have the following definition:

Definition 6.8

(Block Complex)
.

Given a suited subsurface
W
and a (not necessarily proper) subset
A
of boundary components of
W
, the (unlabeled) block complex
\mathcal{P}(W,A)
is the simplicial complex whose
p
-simplices are sets
\{Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
of pairwise disjoint isotopy classes of subsurfaces of
W
, each homeomorphic to
Y^{d}
, with the property that exactly one boundary component is essential in
W
, while the rest belong to
A
.

We remark that
\mathcal{P}(W,A)
is called the
piece complex
in
[
Ara+21
]
. The following is
[
Ara+21
, Theorem 10.1]
;

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
W_{g}^{b}
be the compact, connected surface of genus
g
with
b
boundary components, and let
A
be a (not necessarily proper) subset of these boundary components. Then
\mathcal{P}(W_{g}^{b},A)
is weakly Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
m
provided that
m\leq\frac{g-1}{2}
and
m\leq\frac{|A|+2d}{2d-1}
.

Let
x
be a vertex of
{\mathfrak{X}}
; as above, we can assume
x=[X,{\rm id}]
. We now define a map

\Pi:\lk^{\downarrow}(x)\to\mathcal{P}(X,A),

where
A
is the set of all suited boundary components of
X
. Let
z\in\lk^{\downarrow}(x)
. As such, we can write
z=[Z,g]
, where there exists a surface
Z^{\prime}
which is obtained from
Z
by adding pairwise disjoint pieces
Y_{0},\dots,Y_{p}
so that
g
maps
Z^{\prime}
to
X
where
Z^{\prime}
is a defining surface for
g
as an
H
-block homeomorphism. We then set
\Pi(z)=\{g(Y_{0}),\dots,g(Y_{p})\}
.

We first check that the map
\Pi
is well-defined; to this end, let
(W,h)
be another representative of
z
. As before, this implies there exists a surface
W^{\prime}
obtained from
W
by adding pairwise disjoint pieces
Y_{0}^{\prime},\dots,Y_{p}^{\prime}
so that
h
maps
W^{\prime}
to
X
. By the definition of the equivalence relation, this means
h^{-1}\circ g
maps
Z
to
W
and in particular,
g(Z)=h(W)\subseteq X
. Therefore,
\{g(Y_{0}),\dots,g(Y_{p})\}=\{h(Y_{0}^{\prime}),\dots,h(Y_{p}^{\prime})\}
, and we are done.

Next, we have:

Proposition 6.10
.

The map
\Pi
is a complete join.

Remark 6.11
.

In a previous version, we claimed that there was an isomorphism
\Pi
from the descending link to the
labeled block complex
. We are grateful to Chris Leininger for pointing out that this was not correct, and for suggesting the strategy for showing that it is a complete join.

Proof of Proposition
6.10
.

Write
x=[X,\rm id]
, and let
\{Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
be a
p
-simplex in
\mathcal{P}(X,A)
. Let
\{Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{p}\}
be a collection of disjoint blocks in
X
such that each
Y_{i}
has exactly two non-essential boundary components. Now,
X\setminus\cup_{i=0}^{p}Z_{i}
is homeomorphic to
X\setminus\cup_{i=0}^{p}Y_{i}
and thus there is a homeomorphism
g:X\rightarrow X
which takes
X\setminus\cup_{i=0}^{p}Y_{i}
to
X\setminus\cup_{i=0}^{p}Z_{i}
and such that
g(Y_{i})=Z_{i}
. We conclude that the map
\Pi
is surjective.

Note that the disjointness condition on the links and on the block complex and the fact that this property is naturally preserved by
\Pi
implies that the map is simplexwise injective.

It remains to show that the pre-image of a simplex is the join of the pre-images of its vertices. Clearly the pre-image of a simplex is contained in the join of the pre-images of its vertices so we only need to show the reverse inclusion. To this end, let
\sigma=\{Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
be an
p
-simplex in
\mathcal{P}(X,A)
. Take vertices

[W_{0},g_{0}],\ldots,[W_{p},g_{p}]

of
\mathfrak{X}
such that
\Pi([W_{i},g_{i}])=Z_{i}
; we want to see that these vertices in fact span an
p
-simplex in
\lk^{\downarrow}(x)
.

We now make several reductions. We first claim that we may assume that, for all
i=0,\ldots,p
, we have that
W_{i}\cup g_{i}^{-1}(Z_{i})=X
, so that
g_{i}^{-1}(Z_{i})
is a block and
g_{i}:X\rightarrow X
is an element of
{\mathfrak{B}}
with defining surface
X
. To see this, let
Y_{i}=g_{i}^{-1}(Z_{i})
, which by construction is a block, and consider any
H
-block homeomorphism
h
which takes
W_{i}\cup Y_{i}
to
X
and such that
W_{i}
is a defining surface for
h
, i.e. the image of
Y_{i}
is also a block. Now we see that
(W_{i},g_{i})\sim(h(W_{i}),g_{i}\circ h^{-1})
, and that this new representative has the desired properties.

We thus assume without loss of generality that
W_{i}\cup Y_{i}=X
and
g_{i}(X)=X
for all
i=\ldots,p
. Next, we claim that we can assume that the non-essential boundary components of
Y_{i}=g_{i}^{-1}(Z_{i})
are disjoint from those of
Y_{j}=g_{j}^{-1}(Z_{j})
for all
i\neq j
. Indeed, given any block
Y
with two non-essential boundary components, there is an
H
-block homeomorphism
f
with
f(X)=X
and
f(Y_{i})=Y
. Moreover
(W_{i},g_{i})\sim(f(W_{i}),g_{i}\circ f^{-1})
. Since
f(Y_{i})=Y
, we can freely choose the location of the block
Y_{i}
in
X
among blocks with two non-essential boundary components.

We thus assume without loss of generality that
Y_{i}
is disjoint from
Y_{j}
for each
i\neq j
. Finally, note that since
Y_{0},\dots,Y_{p}
are pairwise disjoint and
g_{i}(Y_{i})
are pairwise disjoint and the complements
X\backslash Y_{i}
are homeomorphic to the complements
X\backslash g_{i}(Y_{i})
, we can construct an element
g
in
{\mathfrak{B}}
with
g(X)=X
and which agrees with
g_{i}
on
W_{i}
. In particular, the collection
\{[W_{i},g]\}_{i=0}^{i=p}
spans a simplex in
\lk^{\downarrow}(x)
.

\square

7.
From global to local

Finally, in this section we prove that if
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is of type
F_{n}
, then so is
H
. In a nutshell, we will show that
H
is a
quasi-retract
of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
, following the strategy in
[
SW21
, Section 3.5]
.

Before we proceed, we briefly recall the definition of quasi-retraction between metric spaces.
Let
X
and
Y
are metric spaces. A function
f:X\to Y
is said to be
coarsely Lipschitz
if there exist constants
C,D>0
so that

d(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq Cd(x,x^{\prime})+D\text{ for all }x,x^{\prime}\in X.

A coarsely Lipschitz function
\rho:X\to Y
is said to be a quasi-retraction if there exists a coarse Lipschitz function
\iota:Y\to X
and a constant
E>0
so that
d(\rho\circ\iota(y),y)\leq E
for all
y\in Y
. In this situation, we say that
Y
is a
quasi-retract
of
X
. In the realm of groups, we say that a finitely generated group
Q
is a quasi-retract of another finitely generated group
G
if
Q
is a quasi-retract of
G
with respect to the word metric given by some (and hence, any) choice of finite generating sets. We have:

Theorem 7.1
.

[
Alo94
, Theorem 8]

Let
G
and
Q
be finitely generated groups such that
Q
is a quasi-retract of
G
. If
G
is of type
F_{n}
, so is
Q
.

After these definitions, observe that we have a canonical inclusion homomorphism

\iota:H\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)

given by the rule
h\mapsto[O_{2},({{\rm id}},(h,1,\ldots,1)),O_{2}]
, where recall that
O_{2}
is the surface that appears at the second step in the construction of
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}(O,Y)
; in other words,
\iota(H)
labels the leftmost suited curve of
O_{2}
by
h
and the other suited curves by the identity. Next, we define a map

r:\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)\to H

by setting
r(f)=h_{1}
, where
f=[Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)]
and
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
; observe that
r(f)
does not depend on the particular choice of such representative. We have:

Proposition 7.2
.

\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is generated by
\iota(H)
and
\mathfrak{B}_{d}=\mathfrak{B}_{d}(\{1\})
.

Proof.

Let
G
be the group generated by
\iota(H)
and
\mathfrak{B}_{d}
; we want to prove that
G=\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
. To this end, let
f\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
and choose a tree-pair representative
(Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z))
for it, observing that

[Z,(f_{Z},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)]=[Z,(f_{Z},{\rm id}),f(Z)]\cdot[f(Z),({\rm id},\mathbf{h}),f(Z)]

Since
[Z,(f_{Z},{\rm id}),f(Z)]\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}
, it suffices to show that an element of the form
[X,({\rm id},\mathbf{h}),X]
belongs to
G
. We can assume that
O_{2}\subseteq X
.

Suppose for the time being that
X=O_{2}
. In this case, let

\lambda_{j}(h):=(1,\cdots,1,h,1\cdots,1)
where the only nontrivial labeling
h
appears on the
j
-th position, then

[O_{2},({\rm id},\lambda_{j}(h)),O_{2}]=[O_{2},(g,{\rm id}),O_{2}][O_{2},({\rm id},\lambda_{1}(h)),O_{2}][O_{2},(g^{-1},{\rm id}),O_{2}],

where
g\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}
is any element supported on
O_{2}
and that interchanges the first and
j
-th suited boundary components of
O_{2}
, fixing the rest. Since

[O_{2},({\rm id},\lambda_{1}(h)),O_{2}]\in\iota(H)

and for
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1}\cdots,h_{d})

[O_{2},({\rm id},\mathbf{h}),O_{2}]=\prod_{j=1}^{d}[O_{2},({\rm id},\lambda_{j}(h_{j})),O_{2}],

we are done in this case.

Suppose then that
X
is a suited subsurface whose left most suited loop is also a suited loop of
O_{2}
. By the same conjugation argument as in the previous paragraph, we can assume that
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},1,\ldots,1)
. But then using the equivalence relation, we have

[X,({\rm id},(h_{1},1,\ldots,1)),X]=[O_{2},({\rm id},(h_{1},1,\ldots,1)),O_{2}],

which already lies in the subgroup generated by
\iota(H)
and
\mathfrak{B}_{d}
. Now for an arbitrary suited subsurface
X
, again using the conjugation argument, we can assume that
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},1,\ldots,1)
. But then
[X,({\rm id},\mathbf{h}_{1}),X]
is conjugate to
[X,(\mathrm{id},(1,\cdots,h_{1}),X]
via an element in
\mathfrak{B}_{d}
. Now we can find a suitable smaller suited subsurface
X^{\prime}
whose left most suited loop is also a suited loop of
O_{2}
, such that

[X,(\mathrm{id},(1,\cdots,h_{1})),X]=[X^{\prime},(\mathrm{id},(1,\cdots,h_{1})),X^{\prime}].

This is known to lie in the subgroup generated by
\iota(H)
and
\mathfrak{B}_{d}
by the previous step and hence we are done.

\square

Theorem 7.3
.

When
H
is finitely generated, it is a quasi-retract of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
.

Proof.

Fix finite generating sets
S_{H}
and
S_{B}
for
H
and
\mathfrak{B}_{d}
, respectively. By Proposition
7.2
,
\iota(S_{H})\cup S_{B}
is a finite generating set of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
. We will show that the map
r:\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)\rightarrow H
described above is coarsely Lipschitz with respect to the word metrics on
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
and
H
. To this end:

(1)

r(g\iota(s))\in\{r(g),r(g)s\}
for all
s\in S_{H}
and all
g\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
;

(2)

r(gg^{\prime})=r(g)
for any
g^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}
and
g\in\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
.

In particular,
r
is nonexpanding and hence coarsely Lipschitz. Since
\iota
is a group homomorphism, it must be coarsely Lipschitz as well. As
r\circ\iota={{\rm id}}_{H}
, we conclude that
r
is a quasi-retraction.

\square

Corollary 7.4

(Part “only if” of Theorem
1.1
)
.

If
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is of type
F_{n}
, so is
H
.

Proof.

Let us first prove the corollary for
n=1
. If
H
is not finitely generated, we can find an increasing family of subgroups
H_{1}\lneq H_{2}\lneq H_{n}\lneq\cdots
of
H
such that
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}H_{i}=H
. But then we would have a family of subgroups
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H_{1})\lneq\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H_{2})\lneq\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H_{n})\lneq\cdots
of
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
, such that
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H_{i})=\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
. This implies
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is not finitely generated ,which is a contradiction.

Suppose now
\mathfrak{B}_{d}(H)
is of type
F_{n}
for some
n\geq 2
. As above,
H
is finitely generated, and the corollary now follows from Theorem
7.1
and Theorem
7.3
.

\square
