An elementary approach to splittings of unbounded operators
Arieh Iserles & Karolina Kropielnicka

Abstract
Using elementary means, we derive the three most popular splittings of e t ⁡ ( A + B ) {\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)} and their error bounds in the case when A A and B B are (possibly unbounded) operators in a Hilbert space, generating strongly continuous semigroups, e t ​ A {\mathrm{e}}^{tA} , e t ​ B {\mathrm{e}}^{tB} and e t ⁡ ( A + B ) {\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)} . The error of these splittings is bounded in terms of the norm of the commutators [ A , B ] [A,B] , [ A , [ A , B ] ] [A,[A,B]] and [ B , [ A , B ] ] [B,[A,B]] .



1
Introduction

A popular approach in numerical analysis is to approximate a problem that we wish to compute by a combination of sub-problems whose solution is more accessible
[
blanes24smd
,
mclachlan02sm
]
. This leads to powerful computational algorithms but mathematical analysis of this procedure is often highly nontrivial.

Exponential splittings arise when we attempt to solve a complicated linear differential system whose ‘sub-problems’ are much easier to compute. They play a prominent role in a wide range of computational methods for evolutionary differential equations. Yet, the analysis of even the simplest splitting presents a substantial problem in the context of partial differential equations.

The starting point is the abstract linear equation,

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}u}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=(A+B)u,\quad t\geq 0,\qquad u(0)=u_{0},

(1.1)

where, for simplicity’s sake, we assume zero boundary conditions. Here
A
and
B
are linear differential operators, which might be unbounded. We stipulate that the solution of (
1.1
) is
well posed:
for every
t\geq 0
there exists
c_{t}
such that
\|u(t)\|\leq c_{t}\|u_{0}\|
– in other words, small perturbations in the initial condition lead to bounded changes in the solution for all
t\geq 0
.

We denote the exact solution of (
1.1
) by
u(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}u_{0}
: if
A
and
B
are matrices, the exponential can be (at least in principle) computed by a Taylor expansion but this is no longer the case once
A
and
B
are unbounded linear operators: we cannot regard them as ‘very large matrices’. However, in many instances computing both
{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}u_{0}
(the solution of
\,\mathrm{d}u/\,\mathrm{d}t=Au
,
u(0)=u_{0}
) and
{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}u_{0}
is easy (whether exactly or to a high degree of precision), hence
{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}
(for small
t>0
– the numerical procedure is typically accomplished by time stepping and
t
is the step size) might be approximated by the
Lie–Trotter splitting

Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t):={\mathrm{e}}^{tA}{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}.

(1.2)

If
A
and
B
commute, that is
[A,B]=AB-BA=O
, it is true that
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}
, otherwise
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)\neq{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}
but
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}
might still be a good approximation to the exact exponential, thus to the solution of (
1.1
).

As long as (
1.1
) is an ordinary differential equation, everything reduces to matrix analysis and it is trivial to expand the error into Taylor series, proving that
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}={\cal O}\!\left(t^{2}\right)
: Lie–Trotter is hence a first-order approximation. Not so, however, once either
A
or
B
are unbounded operators.

A helpful paradigm is the
linear Schrödinger equation

{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\Delta u+V(x)u,\qquad t\geq 0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.3)

with an initial condition given in a suitable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Thus,
A={\mathrm{i}}\Delta
and
B=-{\mathrm{i}}V
and we note that the differential operator
A
, which corresponds to the
kinetic component
of a quantum system, is unbounded. In some cases the
interaction potential

V
might be also unbounded.

The operators
A+B
,
A
and
B
are generators of strongly continuous semigroups
{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}
,
{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}
and
{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}
respectively

Strong continuity of
{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}
implies that
\|{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}\|\leq c{\mathrm{e}}^{\omega t}
for some
c,\omega>0
and all
t\geq 0
, meaning that the operator
A+B
is well posed for the problem (

)
[

]
.
hence
we are not allowed to use a Taylor expansion in the presence of unbounded operators,

[

]
. Therefore the standard, trivial proof that
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}
is a first-order splitting – that is, that
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)u_{0}={\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}u_{0}+{\cal O}\!\left(t^{2}\right)
for every

– is no longer valid!

The subject of this paper is detailed analysis of three most familiar splittings: the Lie–Trotter splitting (
1.2
), the
palindromic Lie–Trotter splitting

Y_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t):=\frac{1}{2}({\mathrm{e}}^{tA}{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}+{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{tA})=\frac{1}{2}[Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)+Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;t)]

(1.4)

(in this context ‘palindromic’ means that it is the same whether ‘read’ left-to-right or right-to-left) and the
Strang splitting

Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t):={\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}=Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;\tfrac{1}{2}t)Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;\tfrac{1}{2}t)

(1.5)

[
mclachlan02sm
]
. It is tempting to consider palindromic Lie–Trotter as an arithmetic mean of Lie–Trotter and its ‘reverse’
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;t)={\mathrm{e}}^{tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}
and Strang’s splitting as their geometric mean, but we make no further use of this observation.

An interesting aside: the Strang splitting is
symmetric,

Y_{\mathrm{S}}^{-1}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B,-t)
. This is an easy exercise, an outcome of palindromy and in finite-dimensional or bounded setting implies that there exists an operator
\Omega
such that
Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)={\mathrm{e}}^{\Omega(t)}
and
\Omega(-t)=-\Omega(t)

[

,

]
.

The standard means of constructing

is the symmetric Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula

.
An important consequence is that its order can be increased using the Yoshida device

. This, however is not the case with palindromic Lie–Trotter because it is easy to verify that it is not symmetric, even though it is palindromic.

An analysis of the Strang splitting for unbounded operators has been presented in the path-breaking paper of

. They assumed

for

\|[A,B]u\|\leq c_{1}\|(-A)^{\alpha}u\|,\qquad\|[A,[A,B]]u\|\leq c_{2}\|(-A)^{\beta}u\|,\qquad u\in\mathcal{X},

for some
c_{1},c_{2}>0
(all this is true for the linear Schrödinger equation (

)), proved that (

Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)u={\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}u+{\cal O}\!\left(t^{3}\right),\qquad u\in\mathcal{X},

and presented an error bound. Cf.
[

for the case when time-independent

are both unbounded. An important elaboration upon the model (

) is when

is allowed to depend on

, and this can be accommodated into the formalism of exponential splittings. We refer to
[

and

delvalle03fst
]
for details.

We assume for the time being just that the semigroups
{\mathrm{e}}^{tA},{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}
and
{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}
are well posed in
{\mathcal{X}}
: this is sufficient in our quest to express in Section 2 the errors of different splittings in an accessible form. The next step (and the theme of Section 3) is to use these forms to derive explicit upper bounds on the error and this requires stricter assumptions on functions
u\in\mathcal{X}
, e.g. that
\|[A,B]u\|
,
\|[[A,B],A]u\|
and
\|[[A,B],B]u\|
are all bounded.

In Section 2 we derive explicit expressions for the error of Lie–Trotter, palindromic Lie–Trotter and Strang splittings in terms of multiple integrals, thereby determining their order. Firstly we derive the error

E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}

from basic principles. Secondly, we represent

E_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}

in terms of
E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)
and
E_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;t)
and, finally, we represent

E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}

using nested integrals.

Once
A
and
B
are matrices, trivial Taylor expansion affirms that

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[A,B]t^{2}+{\cal O}\!\left(t^{3}\right),

(1.6)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}[A-B,[A,B]]t^{3}+{\cal O}\!\left(t^{4}\right),

(1.7)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{24}[A+2B,[A,B]]t^{3}+{\cal O}\!\left(t^{4}\right).

(1.8)

The rules of the game in this paper are simple:
we are not allowed to expand into Taylor series and, once we seek error bounds, must be careful with the presence of commutators containing unbounded operators.
Because of possible unboundedness, such commutators might make sense in upper bounds only for a subset of initial values in
\mathcal{X}
.

While the expressions from Section 2 are sufficient to obtain order, they are perhaps too opaque as realistic tools to estimate the error. In Section 3 we use logarithmic norms
[
soderlind2006tln
]
to produce tight upper bounds on the error committed by our three splittings.

Note that both Lie–Trotter and Strang splitting have their reversed counterparts (i.e., with
A
and
B
swapped),
{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}
and
{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tB}
respectively. (Palindromic Lie–Trotter is its own reverse.) All we need, though, to extend our results to the ‘reverse setting’ is to swap the role of
A
and
B
in our error terms and corresponding bounds.

The main tool in our analysis consists of demonstrating that each of our three splittings obeys a perturbed differential equation, whose solution affords us a handle on the size of the error. The use of a perturbed differential equation satisfied by a splitting has already featured in
[
sheng94gee
]
in a finite-dimensional setting. The framework of the present paper is substantially different.

2
Error expressions

2.1
The Lie–Trotter splitting

Throughout this paper derivatives of operators are always applied to the elements of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
but, for the sake of brevity, we consider them as ‘proper’ derivatives. Thus, for example,
\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}/\,\mathrm{d}t=A{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}
really means that
(\,\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}/\,\mathrm{d}t)u=A{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}u
for every
u\in\mathcal{X}
(or for a subset of
\mathcal{X}
).

It is easy to derive the following result from basic principles,

Theorem 2.1

The error of the Lie–Trotter splitting is

E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\,\mathrm{d}\xi{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(2.1)

Proof
By straightforward differentiation,

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)}{\,\mathrm{d}t}-(A+B)Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{tA},B]{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}

(2.2)

and, of course,
Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;0)=I
. Recall that the
Duhamel principle
is the familiar variation of constants formula in the context of partial differential equations: the solution of
X^{\prime}=CX+F(t)
, where
C
might be a matrix or an operator, is

X(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{tC}X(0)+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)C}F(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Letting
C=A+B
and
F(t)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{tA},B]{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}
in (
2.2
) implies that
X(t)=Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)
and we have

E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}[{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A},B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Letting
Z(\tau)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A},B]
, we obtain

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}Z(\tau)}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}-AZ(\tau)=[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A},\qquad Z(0)=O,

and using the Duhamel principle again

Z(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

The error expression (
2.1
) now follows by substitution into
E_{\mathrm{LT}}
.

\Box

The presence of two nested integrals in (
2.1
) indicates that
E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)={\cal O}\!\left(t^{2}\right)
and the order of the Lie–Trotter splitting is one.

It is sometimes, e.g. in the next section, useful to rewrite (
2.1
) in a form that separates between the leading
{\cal O}\!\left(t^{2}\right)
term and a higher-order one. Let

U(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

hence (
2.1
) equals

E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}U(\tau){\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Given any operator
Q
acting on
\mathcal{X}
, we let
P_{Q}(A,B;\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi A}Q{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}
. Therefore

P_{Q}(A,B;0)=Q,\qquad P_{Q}^{\prime}(A,B;\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi A}[Q,A]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}=-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi A}[A,Q]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}

and

P_{Q}(A,B;\xi)=Q-\int_{0}^{\xi}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta A}[A,Q]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta

(2.3)

– the first term on the right is
{\cal O}\!\left(1\right)
and the second
{\cal O}\!\left(\xi\right)
. Letting
Q=[A,B]
we have

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}P_{[A,B]}(A,B;\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(2.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}\!\left\{\tau[A,B]\right.

\displaystyle\left.\mbox{}-\int_{0}^{\tau}\!\int_{0}^{\xi}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta A}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right\}\!{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\tau{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\ \int_{0}^{\tau}(\tau-\eta){\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\eta)A}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Note that the first integral in (
2.4
) is
{\cal O}\!\left(t^{2}\right)
, while the second is
{\cal O}\!\left(t^{3}\right)
.

2.2
Palindromic Lie–Trotter

Theorem 2.2

The error of the palindromic Lie–Trotter splitting is

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\tau\hskip 30.0pt

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\tau}(\tau-\xi)\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)A}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\left.\mbox{}-{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)B}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Moreover, since both terms are
{\cal O}\!\left(t^{3}\right)
, the splitting is of order two.

Proof
It is obvious that

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)=\frac{1}{2}\left\{E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)+E_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;t)\right\}

and the theorem follows from (
2.4
) by standard algebra, taking the average of
E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)
and
E_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;t)
.

\Box

The expression (
2.5
) is inconvenient for the derivation of error bounds – the subject of the next section – because the norm of the first integral cannot be bounded by an expression of the form
ct^{3}
for some
c>0
. It is thus convenient to rewrite it. Let
Q
be an operator acting on
\mathcal{X}
or its subspace and define

R_{Q}(\tau)={\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}Q{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}Q{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}

– note that the term in curly brackets in the first integral in (
2.5
) is
R_{[A,B]}(\tau)
. Since
R_{Q}(0)=O
and

R_{Q}^{\prime}(\tau)-(A+B)R_{Q}(\tau)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}Q,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}+[A,{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}Q]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A},

by the Duhamel formula

R_{Q}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)(A+B)}\!\left\{[{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}Q,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}+[A,{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}Q]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi={\cal O}\!\left(\tau\right).

We are not done yet: Letting
F_{Q}(\xi)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}Q,B]
, we have
F_{Q}(0)=-[B,Q]
and
F_{Q}^{\prime}-AF_{Q}=[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}Q
, therefore

F_{Q}(\xi)=-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}[B,Q]+\int_{0}^{\xi}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,Q.

Likewise,

[A,{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}Q]={\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,Q]+\int_{0}^{\xi}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,Q

and, after straightforward algebra,

\displaystyle R_{[A,B]}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)(A+B)}\!\left\{-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}+{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\xi}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\left.\mbox{}+{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

We can now rewrite (
2.4
) in a form which is more amenable for an error bound. Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}R_{[A,B]}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)(A+B)}\!\left\{-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}+{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\xi}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\left.\mbox{}+{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}(t^{2}-\xi^{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\xi)(A+B)}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}(t^{2}-\xi^{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\xi)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\xi}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\eta)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\left.\mbox{}+{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta,

we deduce that

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)

(2.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}(t^{2}-\xi^{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\xi)(A+B)}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi\hskip 20.0pt

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\tau}(\tau-\xi)\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)A}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\left.\mbox{}-{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)B}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}(t^{2}-\xi^{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\xi)(A+B)}\int_{0}^{\xi}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta A}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\left.\mbox{}+{\mathrm{e}}^{(\xi-\eta)B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}\,\mathrm{d}\eta[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi A}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

The first two integrals are
{\cal O}\!\left(t^{3}\right)
and the last one
{\cal O}\!\left(t^{4}\right)
.

2.3
The Strang splitting

Recalling from (
1.5
) that

Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;\tfrac{1}{2}t)Y_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;\tfrac{1}{2}t),

we have

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)=Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;\tfrac{1}{2}t)+{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}t(A+B)}\}\{E_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;\tfrac{1}{2}t)+{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}t(A+B)}\}-{\mathrm{e}}^{t(A+B)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;\tfrac{1}{2}t){\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}t(A+B)}+{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}t(A+B)}E_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;\tfrac{1}{2}t)

\displaystyle\mbox{}+E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;\tfrac{1}{2}t)\cdot E_{\mathrm{LT}}(B,A;\tfrac{1}{2}t).

While it is tempting (and possible!) to construct
E_{\mathrm{S}}
from
E_{\mathrm{LT}}
in this manner, it is simpler to proceed from first principles.

Theorem 2.3

The error of Strang’s splitting is

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}\!(\tau-\eta)\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta A}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\eta A}\right.

(2.7)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\left.\mbox{}+2{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta B}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Proof
Similarly to the proof of Theorem
2.1
,

\displaystyle Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)={\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)}{\,\mathrm{d}t}-(A+B)Y_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA},B]{\mathrm{e}}^{tB}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}[{\mathrm{e}}^{tB},A]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}tA}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}[{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A},B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 80.0pt\mbox{}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}[A,{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Let

Z_{1}(\tau)=[{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A},B],\qquad Z_{2}(\tau)=[A,{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}].

Then
Z_{1}(0)=Z_{2}(0)=O
and by the magic of Duhamel

\displaystyle Z_{1}-\frac{1}{2}AZ_{1}=\frac{1}{2}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle Z_{1}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}(\tau-\xi)A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\xi A}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

\displaystyle Z_{2}^{\prime}-Z_{2}B={\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}[A,B]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle Z_{2}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{(\tau-\xi)B}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Therefore

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\xi A}\,\mathrm{d}\xi{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\mbox{}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi B}\,\mathrm{d}\xi{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi A}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\xi A}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}[A,B]{\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi B}\right\}\!{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Recalling the operator
R_{Q}
from the previous section, we let

\tilde{R}_{Q}(\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi A}Q{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\xi A}-{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi B}Q{\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi B}.

Since
\tilde{R}_{Q}(0)=O
, we have

\tilde{R}_{Q}(\xi)=\int_{0}^{\xi}\tilde{R}_{Q}^{\prime}(\eta)\,\mathrm{d}\eta=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\xi}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta A}[A,Q]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\eta A}+2{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}[B,Q]{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta B}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

The theorem follows because

E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}\tilde{R}_{[A,B]}(\xi){\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

We obtain

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathrm{e}}^{(t-\tau)(A+B)}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\xi}\!\left\{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{2}\eta A}[A,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\eta A}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\left.\mbox{}+2{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta B}[B,[A,B]]{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta B}\right\}\!\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,{\mathrm{e}}^{\tau B}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}\tau A}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

and, exchanging the two inner integrals, derive (
2.7
).

\Box

Note that (
2.7
) implies that
E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)={\cal O}\!\left(t^{3}\right)
.

3
Error bounds

A standard means of bounding exponentials of matrices is by using a
logarithmic norm

[
soderlind2006tln
]
. Thus, let
A
be a complex-valued square matrix,
A:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{Y}
is a normed space. The limit

\mu[A]=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\|I+\varepsilon A\|-1}{\varepsilon},

(3.1)

where
\|\,\cdot\,\|
is the norm of
\mathcal{Y}
, is the logarithmic norm of
A
. This concept is important because

\|{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}\|\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{t\mu[A]},\qquad t\geq 0,

(3.2)

and
\mu[A]
is the least number with this property. In particular, in the case of the standard Euclidean norm,
\mu[A]
is the largest eigenvalue of the symmetric matrix
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})
. Note that
\mu[A]
might be negative: a logarithmic norm is not a norm!

The definition (
3.1
) and the inequality (
3.2
) survive once
\mathcal{Y}
is replaced by a Hilbert space and
A
is a linear operator
[
soderlind2006tln
]
, while in the case of Euclidean norm
\mu[A]=\sup\sigma(\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*}))
, where
\sigma
is the spectrum – it is bounded once
A
generates a strongly continuous semigroup and
{\mathrm{e}}^{tA}
is well posed. For many cases of interest
\mu[A]=0
but we do not need to impose this condition.

We let

\omega=\mu[A]+\mu[B]-\mu[A+B].

The error bounds are expressed in terms of
\omega
, rather than individual logarithmic norms, and this renders them substantially simpler.

3.1
Lie–Trotter

It follows at once from (
2.1
) that

\|E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;t)\|\leq{\mathrm{e}}^{t\mu[A+B]}\frac{1-(1-\omega t){\mathrm{e}}^{\omega t}}{\omega^{2}}\|[A,B]\|.

(3.3)

The most ubiquitous case is
\mu[A]=\mu[B]=\mu[A+B]=0
– in that case letting
\omega\rightarrow 0
in (
3.3
) results in
\|E_{\mathrm{LT}}(A,B;T)\|\leq\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\|[A,B]\|
. This upper bound is stronger than the expression (
1.6
).

3.2
Palindromic Lie–Trotter

Palindromic Lie–Trotter splitting leads to a more complicated error bound,

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathrm{PLT}}(A,B;t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathrm{e}}^{t\mu[A+B]}(1+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\omega t)\frac{(1+\frac{1}{2}\omega t){\mathrm{e}}^{-\omega t}-(1-\frac{1}{2}\omega t)}{\omega^{3}}(\|[A,[A,B]]\|+\|[B,[A,B]]\|)

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\frac{3}{\omega^{4}}\left[\left(1+\omega t+\frac{1}{3}\omega^{2}t^{2}\right){\mathrm{e}}^{-\omega t}-\left(1-\frac{1}{6}\omega^{2}t^{2}\right)\right]\|[A,B]\|^{2}

and the error bound becomes

\frac{t^{3}}{12}(\|[A,[A,B]]\|+\|[B,[A,B]]\|)+\frac{t^{4}}{8}\|[A,B]\|^{2}

once
\omega\rightarrow 0
– compare with (
1.7
).

3.3
Strang

The Strang splitting lends itself to considerably neater error bound. Starting from (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}{\mathrm{e}}^{t\mu[A+B]}\frac{(1-\omega t+\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}t^{2}){\mathrm{e}}^{\omega t}-1}{\omega^{3}}(\|[A,[A,B]]\|+2\|[B,[A,B]]\|).

Once
\mu[A],\mu[B],\mu[A+B]=0
, thus
\omega\rightarrow 0
, we recover

\|E_{\mathrm{S}}(A,B;t)\|\leq\frac{t^{3}}{24}(\|[A,[A,B]]\|+2\|[B,[A,B]]\|)

– the leading error term in (
1.8
) becomes an upper bound!

4
Conclusion

Exponential splittings are an important strategy in computing ‘difficult’ time-dependent partial differential equations by converting them into problems which are considerably easier to evaluate. While the procedure is elementary and intuitive, its analysis is difficult, not least because of the presence of unbounded operators. Typically, this calls for advanced methods of functional analysis and semigroup theory
[
hansen09esu
]
. In this paper we demonstrate that this analysis can be accomplished by elementary means, accessible to advanced mathematics undergraduates.
