Joint Linear Trend Recovery Using ℓ 1 \ell_{1} Regularization
Xiaoli Gao Department of Mathematics and Statistics University of North Carolina at Greensboro Syed Ejaz Ahmed Department of Mathematics Brock University

Abstract




Abstract

This paper studies
the recovery of a joint piece-wise linear trend from a time series
using
\ell_{1}
regularization approach, called
\ell_{1}
trend filtering
(Kim, Koh and Boyd, 2009).
We provide some sufficient conditions under which a

\ell_{1}
trend filter can be well-behaved in terms of
mean estimation and change point detection.
The result is two-fold: for the mean estimation, an almost
optimal consistent rate is obtained;
for the change point detection,
the slope change in direction can
be recovered in a high probability.
In addition, we show that the
weak irrepresentable condition, a necessary
condition for LASSO model to be sign consistent (Zhao and Yu, 2006),
is not necessary for the consistent change point detection.
The performance of the

\ell_{1}
trend filter is evaluated
by some finite sample simulations studies.

Keywords: Change Point, Estimation consistency,
\ell_{1}
regularization, Linear trend filtering, Sign consistency.

1
Introduction

For a naturally-occurring time series with observation
y_{t}
and underlying
mean
\mu_{t}^{0}
at
1\leq t\leq n
, an important issue
is to recover the trend of the underlying mean vector,
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}=(\mu^{0}_{1},\cdots,\mu^{0}_{n})^{\prime}
.
Here
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
often
exhibits various kinds of trends in many real applications.
For example,
in the analysis of DNA sequences,

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
is assumed to be piece-wise constant
(Braun and Muller, 1998; Huang
et. al
, 2005). However,
in financial time series,

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
is often assumed to be piece-wise linear (Taylor, 2008).
Other applications can also be found in macroeconomics (Hodrick and E. Prescott, 1997),
climate research (Baillie and Chung, 2002) and social sciences (Levitt, 2004).
Various trend filtering methods have been
developed to recover the underlying mean vector from noisy data. We refer Kim
et al.
(2009) for a complete review of
many different trend filtering methods and applications.

1.1
Model assumptions and some background

Consider a model

\displaystyle{\rm(I)}\quad y_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu^{0}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t},~1\leq t\leq n,

(1)

where
\varepsilon_{t}
is the random noise
with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
,
1\leq t\leq n
.
The interest is to recover the mean vector
with joint piece-wise linear trend, meaning the underlying mean vector in model (
1
)
satisfying:

\displaystyle{\rm(I\!I)}\quad\mu_{t}^{0}

\displaystyle=a_{j}+b_{j}t,~t_{j-1}\leq t\leq t_{j}-1,~j=1,\cdots,J+1,~1\leq t\leq n,

(2)

and

\displaystyle{\rm(I\!I\!I)}\quad a_{j-1}+b_{j-1}t_{j}=a_{j}+b_{j}t_{j},~1\leq j\leq J.

(3)

Here
t_{0}=1
and
t_{J+1}=n+1
. For
1\leq j\leq J
,
t_{j}\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
denote
change points or kink points
where consistent linear trend changes.
The
(a_{j},b_{j}),j=1,\cdots,J+1
are
J+1
pairs of
local intercepts and slopes.
Model assumption (I​I​I) requires that true means at the kink point to be fitted consistently from
two corresponding adjacent linear trends. See a toy example in Figure
1
(a).
To recover the mean vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
under model I–I​I​I,
one can always get a maximum likelihood or least squares estimation
of those
(a_{j},b_{j})
’s first by controlling
the number of the kink points.
To list a few, one can see for example Feder (1975a,b), Bai and Perron
(1998) and Bhattacharya (1994).

However, such an dynamic optimization
approach is computational expensive (Hawkins, 2001).
Since the introduction of the well-known least absolute shrinkage estimator (LASSO) in
Tibshirani (1996), the
\ell_{1}
regularization technique has been widely used in many problems
when the underlying model or the true coefficients vector has some sparse properties.
Here sparsity mean the true model containing many zeros coefficients, but only a few non-zero ones.
The change point detection problem can be treated as a typical high-dimensional sparse model
in terms of two properties:
the dimension is high since the number of
unknown means equals
n
, the model is sparse since there are
and only a few true change points.
Thus an
\ell_{1}
regularization approach can be applied to detect non-zero changes,
and therefore identify the change points.
For example, when the mean vector in (
1
) is piece-wise constant,
the jump (adjacent difference mean) vector consists of
most zeros except only a few non-zeros where
abrupt changes occur. One can
penalize the
\ell_{1}
norm of the jump vector to obtain
a piece-wise constant mean estimation,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\rm TV}(\lambda_{n})=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\{(1/2)\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\mu_{t})^{2}+\lambda_{n}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|\mu_{t}-\mu_{t-1}|\},

(4)

where
\lambda_{n}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|\mu_{t}-\mu_{t-1}|
for some positive
\lambda_{n}
is
a total variation (TV) penalty.
Both theoretical and computational properties of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\rm TV}(\lambda_{n})

in (
4
)
have been well studied by Harchaoui and Lévy-Leduc (2010) and Rinaldo (2009).

Model in (
4
)
was used to detect abrupt change points where
all
b_{j}
’s are
0
in linear model (
1
).
A similar idea can be adopted for the recovery of joint piece-wise linear trend.
For
3\leq t\leq n
, we denote

\beta_{t}=\mu_{t}+\mu_{t-2}-2\mu_{t-1}
as the
potential slope changes at
t-1
and

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{\prime}
. Since
there are only a few local slopes for the underlying slope change vector,

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{0}
exhibits some sparse property.
Thus, instead of controlling the number of non-zero slope changes directly,
one can obtain
a piece-wise linear mean trend estimation for model I–I​I​I
by penalizing the
\ell_{1}
norm of the slope change vector,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\left\{(1/2)\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\mu_{t})^{2}+\lambda_{n}\sum_{t=3}^{n}|\mu_{t}+\mu_{t-2}-2\mu_{t-1}|\right\},

(5)

where
\lambda_{n}>0
is a tuning parameter controlling the number of estimated linear pieces.
Larger
\lambda_{n}
will generate smaller number of joint linear pieces.
Model (
5
) is the
\ell_{1}
linear filtering method
studied in Kim
et al.
(2009),
as a comparison
with the Hodrick-Prescott (H-P) filtering (Hodrick and Prescott, 1997),
a trend filtering approach to recover the piece-wise quadratic curve.
Kim
et al.
(2009) discussed some basic properties of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
:

(P1): As
\lambda_{n}\to 0
, then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
converges to
{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})^{\prime}
.

(P2): As
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
converges to the best affine fit to
{\mathbf{y}}
.

Tibshirani and Taylor (2011)
also provided some interesting dual algorithms to solve solution paths of
a linear trend filter (LTF) in (
5
).

However, many important questions on the properties of an LTF have not been answered.
Will an LTF (
5
) find
all kink points asymptotically? If all kink points are detected, will
the joint piece-wise linear mean trend be recovered consistently?
How to choose an optimal
\lambda
to obtain a well-behaved LTF?
In this paper, we will investigate some asymptotic properties of
an LTF under some sufficient conditions.
Specifically, under the joint piece-wise linearity assumption in model I–I​I​I,
we will first investigate some rate estimation consistency of
an LTF if a tuning parameter is well chosen.
Then, we will
provide some sufficient conditions under which
all underlying multiple kink points can be detected correctly in a large probability.
More importantly, those slope changes in direction can be recovered consistently for a well chosen
\lambda
.
As a by-product, we will justify that
a weak irrepresentable condition is not needed for the consistent change point detection.

Notations and Preliminaries

We list some preliminaries and notations to end this section.
Suppose there are
J+1
linear segments, separated by
kink points
t_{j}
’s for
1\leq j\leq J
. We make the following notations:

•

{\cal J}=\{t_{j},1\leq j\leq J\}
, the collection of the kink points;

•

{\cal B}_{j}=\{t_{j-1},\cdots,t_{j}-1\}
, the subset of
j
th segment;

•

|{\cal B}_{j}|
, the cardinal value of
{\cal B}_{j}
;

•

Suppose
b_{1},\cdots,b_{J},b_{J+1}
are all local slopes. Then the sub-differentials

\displaystyle c_{j}=\partial\sum_{k=2}^{J+1}|b_{k}-b_{k-1}|/\partial b_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{sgn}(b_{j}-b_{j-1})-\mbox{sgn}(b_{j+1}-b_{j})&\quad{\rm for}~3\leq j\leq J\\
\mbox{sgn}(b_{J-1}-b_{J-2})&\quad{\rm for}~j=J+1\\
-\mbox{sgn}(b_{2}-b_{1})&\quad{\rm for}~j=2\end{array}\right.,

where
\mbox{sgn}(x)=1,s,-1
if
x>0,=0,<0
with
-1<s<1
;

•

Correspondingly,
{{\cal J}^{0}}=\{t^{0}_{j},1\leq j\leq J^{0}\}
, the true kink point set;
b_{j}^{0},1\leq j\leq J^{0}+1
are the underlying
local slopes

{\cal B}_{j}^{0}=\{t_{j-1}^{0},\cdots,t_{j}^{0}-1\}
is the true
j
th segment and
|{\cal B}_{j}^{0}|
is its
cardinal value;

•

b_{\min}^{0}=\min_{1\leq j\leq J^{0}}|{\cal B}_{j}^{0}|
, the smallest segment size among all linear pieces;

•

a_{n}=\min_{1\leq j\leq J^{0}}|\mu_{t_{j}+1}^{0}+\mu_{t_{j}-1}^{0}-2\mu_{t_{j}}^{0}|=\min_{1\leq j\leq J^{0}}|b_{j+1}^{0}-b_{j}^{0}|
for
j\in{\cal J}^{0}
, the smallest slope change at true kink points.

If
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
is an LTF for
\lambda_{n}>0
, then
\widehat{t}_{j+1}(\lambda_{n})
,
\widehat{\cal J}(\lambda_{n})
,
\widehat{\cal B}_{j}
are defined correspondingly.
We sometimes omit
\lambda_{n}
from the estimation without causing any confusion.

Structure of the paper

2
Analytical properties of
\ell_{1}
trend filter

Let the jump value,
\nu_{t}\equiv\mu_{t}-\mu_{t-1}
be
the slope between
t-1
and
t
for
2\leq t\leq n
.
Then the slope change
\beta_{t}=\mu_{t}+\mu_{t-2}-2\mu_{t-1}=\nu_{t}-\nu_{t-1}
, for
3\leq t\leq n
.
To unify the notation, we also let
\nu_{1}=\mu_{1}
,

\beta_{1}=\mu_{1}
, and
\beta_{2}=\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}
.
Below we give two different expressions
of the linear trend filtering model in (
5
).

2.1
Total variation transformation

Under model assumptions I–I​I​I, the underlying slope vector

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{0}=(\nu_{1}^{0},\cdots,\nu_{n}^{0})^{\prime}
is piece-wise constant
with only a few abrupt changes.
Thus, we can rewrite model (
5
)
into a
penalized regression model of slope vector
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=(\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{n})^{\prime}

with the total variation penalty,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\lambda_{n})&=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}f({\boldsymbol{\nu}},\lambda_{n})\\
&=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\left\{(1/2)\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{tj}\nu_{j})^{2}+\lambda_{n}\sum_{t=3}^{n}|\nu_{t}-\nu_{t-1}|\right\},\end{array}

where
x_{tj}=1
for
1\leq j\leq t
and
0
otherwise for
1\leq t\leq n
.
Thus, by modifying the pathwise decent algorithm in Friedman
et al.
(2007),
we can find an estimation of the slope vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
first,
and then use the cumulative sum to obtain an estimation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Below we
give a detailed description of the modified pathwise decent algorithm.

Modified Pathwise Decent Algorithm

1. Start from
\lambda=0
.

2. Increase
\lambda
with a reasonable small value and run
the following
decent
step and
fusion
step, until no further changes occur.

3. Repeat 2 until a target
\lambda
is reached.

Suppose
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the slope vector obtained from the last step. Then
the current step includes both
decent
cycle and
fusion
cycle as follows.

Decent cycle:

In (
2.1
), check
\partial f/\partial\nu_{k}=0

for
\nu_{k}
belonging to the following three intervals:

(-\infty,\min\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\}],~(\min\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\},\max\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\}],~(\max\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\},\infty),

where
\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}
are solutions from the last step.
If no solution is found, update
\nu_{k}
into the one between
\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}

such that
f
decreases more.
Specifically, we
solve

\displaystyle\nu_{k}=(n-k+1)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=k}^{n}y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ik}\widetilde{\nu}_{i}+\lambda\cdot g\right),

(13)

where
a_{ik}=n-k+1,0
and
n-i+1
for
i<k
,
i=k
and
i>k
,
where
g=0,\pm 1
and
\pm 2
for
k=1
,
2
and
3
for
3\leq k\leq n-1
. Here
“
+
” or “
-
”
is decided in terms of which interval the
\nu_{k}
is checked. For example,
if

\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1}<\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}
for some
3\leq k\leq n-1
, then
g=2
and
-2
for

\nu_{k}<\widetilde{\nu}_{k-1}
and
\nu_{k}>\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}
, respectively.

Fusion cycle:

Enforcing
\nu_{k}=\nu_{k-1}=\cdots=\nu_{k-m}
for
1\leq m\leq k-1

and assuming
\nu_{k}=\cdots=\nu_{k-m}=\alpha

in the penalized objective function
f
,
check
\partial f/\partial\alpha=0
for
\alpha
belongs to any of the following three intervals:

\displaystyle(-\infty,\min\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-m},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\}],~(\min\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-m},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\},\max\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-m},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\}],~(\max\{\widetilde{\nu}_{k-m},\widetilde{\nu}_{k+1}\},\infty).

(14)

If a solution
\alpha
can be found, then we accept the fusion setting.
To be more specific, we
\alpha
is

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=k-m}^{n}y_{i}d_{m+i+1-k}-\sum_{i=1}^{m+n+1-k}d_{i}\sum_{i=1}^{k-m+1}\widetilde{\nu}_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}(\widetilde{\nu}_{i}\sum_{j=m+1}^{m+1+n-i}d_{j})+\lambda g\right)\left/\sum_{i=1}^{m+n-k+1}d_{i}^{2}\right.,

(15)

for three intervals in (
14
),
where
d_{i}=i
and
m+1
for
1\leq i\leq m
and
i\leq m+1
, and
g
is defined in the decent step.

Here (
13
) and (
15
) can be derived by direct computation.

2.2
LASSO transformation

Another possible approach to obtain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
is to
consider model (
5
) as
a LASSO model of the slope change vector
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\left\{(1/2)\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{tj}\beta_{j})^{2}+\lambda_{n}\sum_{j=3}^{n}|\beta_{j}|\right\},

(16)

where
z_{t1}=1
for
1\leq t\leq n
,
z_{tj}=t-j+1
for
j\leq t
and
z_{tj}=0
for
j>t
.
Thus the existing algorithm for LASSO can be adopted
to solve
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})^{\prime}
in (
16
) first.
A mean estimation can be obtained by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda)={\mathbf{Z}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\lambda)
,
where
{\mathbf{Z}}
is matrix consisting of all
z_{tj}
’s.
Theoretically, for a given tuning parameter
\lambda
, both
(
2.1
) and (
16
) should provide
the same solution.
However, since the tuning parameter selection technique is involved,
those two approaches can provide different final trend filters.
Combining with some existing tuning parameter selection techniques,
LASSO model (
16
) turns to
generate more non-zero
\widehat{\beta}_{j}
’s with small values around the true
kink points than the pathwise algorithm does.
In practice, the pathwise algorithm is preferred if one is more interested
recovering those change points. However, LASSO model
is preferred is one cares more about the mean trend estimation.
In section
4.2
, we use some simulation studies to demonstrate those differences in more details.
We also provide a theoretical justification in Section
3.2
.

In the next section, we investigate some asymptotic properties of an LTF,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
in (
5
). We provide some sufficient conditions under which a well-behaved

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
can be reached.

3
Asymptotic properties

In this section, we study the asymptotic properties of an LTF
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{n}
.
In some cases, we aim to find
an almost “unbiased” estimator of the mean trend vector, which motivate us
to obtain some rate estimation consistency properties under some conditions in Section
3.1
.
In other cases, we are more interested in the recovery of kink points,
which motivate us to investigate some sufficient conditions under which
those underlying kink points can be identified consistently.

We first make the following assumption on the random noise.

(A1). Random noise
\varepsilon_{i}
’s are i.i.d. with mean
0
and finite variance
\sigma^{2}
.
Furthermore, they are sub-Gaussian in the sense that
E[\exp(t\varepsilon_{i})]\leq\exp(\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2),1\leq i\leq n.

3.1
Estimation consistency properties

Consider a multiple change point model in I–I​I​I.
In order to obtain an almost “unbiased” estimator of the unknown mean vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}

from model (
5
), we make the following additional assumption on the underlying
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
.

(A2). The underlying mean
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
in model (
1
) has at most
J_{\max}
local linear pieces.

From (P2) in Section
2
, it is always reasonable
to generate an LTF
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
with finite number of linear pieces.

Lemma 1

There exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
always has at most
J_{\max}
local linear pieces for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is skipped since it is a direct result of (P2) in Section 1.
Then we have the following rate consistency of the mean estimation.

Theorem 1

Suppose (A1) and (A2) hold for model I–I​I​I.
Let

\lambda_{n}=16^{-1}\sigma A(BJ_{\max}\log n)^{1/2}n^{-1/2}-32^{-1/2}\sigma(J_{\max})^{1/2}n^{-1/2}

for some
0<A<1
and
B>2(1-A)^{-2}
. Suppose
\lambda_{n}>\lambda_{0}
for the
\lambda_{0}
in
Lemma
1
.
Then for an LTF
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
in (
5
),

P\left(n^{-1/2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}\|_{2}\geq\sigma(BJ_{\max}\log n/n)^{1/2}\right)\leq J_{\max}n^{[1-B(1-A)^{2}/2]J_{\max}}.

Theorem
1
reveals that we can obtain a consistent estimator of the underlying
mean vector
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
by
choosing
\lambda_{n}
appropriately. In addition, the consistency rate is

O(\log(n)/n)^{1/2})
, which is same as the one obtained by Harchaoui and Lévy-Leduc (2010)
for the piece-wise constant model in model (
4
).
Such a consistency rate,
O(\log n/n)^{1/2}
, is also comparable with the optimal rate obtained by Yao and Au (1988),
O(n^{-1/2})
.
In their work, the least squares estimation method is used to recover
the piece-wise constant when the number of change-points is bounded.
We postpone the detailed proof to the Appendix.

3.2
Sign consistency properties

In the last section, we provide some rate estimation consistency of the mean estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
if
the number of kink points for both
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{0}
are bounded. In many real applications, we
are more interested in the detection of underlying kink points where the underlying linear trends change.
In this section, we investigate the consistency of the kink points detection. More specifically,
we provide some sufficient conditions under which not only the locations but also the directions of
those slope changes are recovered with a large probability.
We make the following assumptions on the underlying
model and the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
:

(B1).
\lambda_{n}\leq 2a_{n}b_{\min}^{0}
;

(B2).
a_{n}^{2}(b_{\min}^{0})^{3}\to\infty
;

(B3). (a)
(\lambda_{n})^{1/2}\to\infty
; (b)
(2+\log((n-J^{0}))/\lambda_{n}<28\delta/\sigma
for some constant
0<\delta<1
.

Here (B2) requires either
a_{n}
, the smallest slope changes between any two adjacent linear segments,
or
b_{\min}^{0}
, the smallest linear segment size to be large enough.
Assumptions in (B3) require the tuning parameter
\lambda_{n}
to grow with
n
. In addition,
(B3-b) also provides a lower bound of the growth rate.
(B1) provides some further information on the growth speeds among
\lambda_{n}
,

a_{n}
and
b_{\min}^{0}
. Notice that there is some redundancy among those conditions. For example,
one can also deduce (B2) from (B1) and (B3-a). Here we list all those conditions for a better
understanding of the growth rates of
a_{n}
,
b_{\min}^{0}
and
\lambda_{n}
.

Denote an event for identifying all kink points
correctly as,

{\cal S}^{1n}(\lambda_{n})=\{\widehat{\cal J}(\lambda_{n})={\cal J}^{0}\}.

Furthermore, a stronger
event for detecting directions of slope changes
correctly is,

{\cal S}^{n}(\lambda_{n})=\left\{{\cal S}^{1n}(\lambda_{n})\bigcap\{\mbox{sgn}(\widehat{\mu}_{i+1}(\lambda_{n})+\widehat{\mu}_{i-1}(\lambda_{n})-2\widehat{\mu}_{i}(\lambda_{n}))=\mbox{sgn}(\mu^{0}_{i+1}+\mu^{0}_{i-1}-2\mu^{0}_{i}),\forall i\in{\cal J}^{0}\}\right\}.

Here
{\cal S}^{n}(\lambda_{n})
is stronger than
{\cal S}^{1n}(\lambda_{n})
since
not only locations of all kink points (where
\mu^{0}_{i+1}+\mu^{0}_{i-1}-2\mu^{0}_{i}\neq 0
) are detected, but also
directions of slope changes (sign of
\mu^{0}_{i+1}+\mu^{0}_{i-1}-2\mu^{0}_{i}
) at those locations are correctly recovered.
We call a kink point having a positive (negative) sign when slope increases (decreases) at this location.
Analogous to the variable selection, we give the following two definitions on the selection
consistency and sign consistency of kink points.

Definition 1

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is kink point detection consistent if
P({\cal S}^{1n}(\lambda_{n}))\to 1
when
n\to\infty
.

Definition 2

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is kink point sign consistent if
P({\cal S}^{n}(\lambda_{n}))\to 1
when
n\to\infty
.

In general, it is much more complicated
to check the event
{\cal S}^{1n}
directly. So
we investigate the kink point sign consistency in Definition 2
instead of the kink point detection consistency in Definition 1.
In the following theorem, we provide some sufficient conditions under which
an LTF
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
is kink point sign consistent.

Theorem 2

Suppose (A1) and (B1-B3) hold for a time series model I–I​I​I.
Then for an
\ell_{1}
trend filter
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})

The proof of Theorem

is given in the Appendix.
Theorem

indicates that model

of a joint piece-wise linear mean trend. Furthermore, the LTF based on a well chosen

3.3
Additional comments

We make two additional comments regarding the above asymptotic results in Section
3.1
and
3.2
.

Comment 1: Weak irrpresentable condition is not necessary
for kink point sign consistency.

In Section
2.2
, we discussed that the
\ell_{1}
trend filtering model can be also written into
a LASSO model in (
16
) with the low triangular design matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}
.
Theorem 2 of Zhao and Yu (2006) states that
that the weak irrepresentable condition
is a necessary condition for
a LASSO solution to be sign consistent under two regularity conditions.
Consider a general LASSO model,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(g)}(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\left\{(1/2)\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}^{(g)}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}x^{(g)}_{ij}\beta^{(g)}_{j})^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_{j}^{(g)}|\right\},

(17)

where

(y_{i}^{(g)},x^{(g)}_{i1},\cdots,x^{(g)}_{ip})
and

(\beta_{1}^{(g)},\cdots,\beta_{p}^{(g)})^{\prime}
represent the observed data and
coefficients vector in the general regression model.
Let
{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}=({\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{1}}},{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{2}}})
be the covariate matrix,
where
{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{1}}}
and
{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{2}}}
include only the important and unimportant covariates, respectively.
Let
{\mathbf{s}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{1}}}=\mbox{sgn}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{{\mathbf{1}}})
consist of sign mappings of non-zero coefficients in the true model.
Then model (
17
) satisfies the
weak irrepresentable condition
if

\displaystyle|{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)^{\prime}}_{{\mathbf{2}}}{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{1}}}({\mathbf{X}}^{(g)^{\prime}}_{{\mathbf{1}}}{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{1}}})^{-1}{\mathbf{s}}^{(g)}_{{\mathbf{1}}}|<{\mathbf{1}}.

(18)

Lemma 2

(Zhao and Yu, 2006) Supose two regularity conditions
are satisfied for the designed matrix
{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}
:
(1) there exists a positive definite matrix
C
such that
the covariance matrix
({\mathbf{X}}^{(g)})^{\prime}{\mathbf{X}}^{(g)}/n\to C
as
n\to\infty
, and
(2)
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}({\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{(g)})^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{(g)})/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty.

Then LASSO is general sign
consistent only if there exists
N
so that the weak irrepresentable condition in (
18
) holds for
n>N
.

Unfortunately, the LASSO model in (
16
)
does not satisfies the
weak irrepresentable condition (See an counter example in the Appendix).
However, there is no contradiction between the sign consistency result in Theorem
2

and Lemma
2

since those two regularity conditions in
Theorem 2 in Lemma
2
are not satisfied for the design matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}

in (
16
) because of the following lemma.

Lemma 3

The design matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}
in model (
16
) has two properties:
(a)
\rho_{1}<1/(4n)\to 0
when
n\to\infty
,
where
\rho_{1}
is the smallest
eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{Z}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{Z}}/n
and
(b)
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\prime}{\mathbf{z}}_{i}/n\geq n^{2}/4
.

Lemma
3
can be verified easily and
we skip the detailed proof in this manuscript.
Two properties (a) and (b) in Lemma
3
means
the both regularity conditions (1) and (2) in Lemma
2

are violated. Thus Theorem
2
is
not against Lemma
2
. In other words,
Theorem
2
indicates that
the weak irrepresentable condition is not necessary
for the change point detection.

Comment 2: An LTF may not reach the estimation consistency and sign consistency simultaneously.

The rate estimation consistency in Theorem
1
holds
for
\lambda_{n}=O(\log(n)/n)
. However,
from (B3-b), we know one of the sufficient conditions for the sign consistency in
Theorem
2
requiring
\lambda_{n}>O(\log(n))
.
So an
\ell_{1}
trend filter may not
be able to reach the estimation consistency and sign consistency simultaneously.
However, this claim is not theoretically justified since
all conditions assumed in both Theorem
1

and
2
are sufficient.

Numerical studies

In this section, we use some simulation studies to demonstrate the performance of the
\ell_{1}
trend filter.

4.1
Tuning parameter selection

As stated in Section
2
, for every
\lambda>0
,
we can first find an optimizer
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\lambda)
of (
2.1
) using the modified
pathwise decent algorithm. For such a
\lambda>0
, an
\ell_{1}
trend filter
is obtained from corresponding cummalative summation.
Since
\lambda
controls the number of abrupt changes in
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\lambda)
,
it is important to choose an optimal tuning parameter,
\lambda_{\rm opt}
,
from a sequence of
\lambda\in(\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max})
, where
\lambda_{\max}
is
a sufficient large
\lambda
such that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\lambda_{n})
reaches the best affine fit to
{\mathbf{y}}
. For a fixed

\lambda>0
, model (
2.1
) is a modeling procedure including both
model selection and model fitting. Zou
et al.
(2007) justified the number of
non-zero estimates of a LASSO estimates is an unbiased estimates of the degrees of
the freedom of the LASSO modeling procedure. In addition,
Tibshirani and Taylor (2011) also confirmed

\widehat{k}(\lambda)+2
to be an unbiased estimates of the
\ell_{1}
trend filtering procedure in
(

(19)

In addition, Ciuperca (2011)

parametric nonlinear multi-response model, where
the joint piece-wise linear model is a particular case.
Specifically for Gaussian error and least squares regression, we
can also choose optimal
\lambda

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm M}=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}\left\{\log\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\widehat{y}_{t})^{2}/n\right)+\widehat{k}(\lambda)(\widehat{k}(\lambda)+1)\log n/n\right\}.

(20)

Ciuperca (2011) demonstrated that MC has some advantages over SIC in terms
of change points detection for several linear trend cases. In the next section, we
use some simulation studies to demonstrate the performance of an LTF, where
both SIC and MC are adopted in the LTF modeling procedure.

5
Discussion

In this paper, we study the asymptotic properties of
the joint linear trend recovery using the
\ell_{1}
regularization approach.
By assuming the true model to be piece-wise linear,
we investigate both the estimation consistency and sign consistency of an
\ell_{1}
trend filter.
In terms of estimation consistency, the consistency rate
is optimal up to a logarithmic factor if the dimension of any linear space
where the true model and its estimates belong to is bounded from above.
In terms of sign consistency, we justify that
an
\ell_{1}
trend filter can not only recover the locations
where the underlying linear pieces connect
but also distinguish those slope changes in direction
with high probability under reasonable conditions.
Thus, by choosing the
tuning parameter
\lambda
properly, we can reach a well-behaved
linear trend filter to recover the underlying linear piece-wise
from some random noises.
The consistency results in this paper amplify
the study in Harchaoui and Lévy-Leduc (2010) and Rinaldo (2009), where
the
\ell_{1}
regularization approach is used to
recover the piece-wise constant for signal approximation.
As a by-product, we also justify that a weak irrepresentable condition is not necessary for the
change point detection. In addition, we evaluate the performances of two alternative expressions of the

\ell_{1}
trend filtering models in terms of both the total variation penalty and the
LASSO penalty. A modified pathwise algorithm is preferred than the
LASSO if the main focus is the kink points detection.

As in many recent studies for penalized regression, our results are proved for the penalty parameter that satisfy the conditions as stated in the theorems.
It is not clear whether the penalty parameter selected using data-driven procedures satisfies those conditions.
However, our numerical study shows a satisfactory finite-sample performance of the
\ell_{1}
trend filter.
Particularly, we note that the tuning parameter selected based on MC seems much better than the one from SIC for our simulated data.
Tuning parameter selection
is an important and challenging problem that requires further investigation, but is beyond the scope of the current paper.
