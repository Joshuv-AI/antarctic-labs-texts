Central limit theorem over non-linear functionals of empirical measures with applications to the mean-field fluctuation of interacting diffusions Thanks: This research benefited from the support of the “Chaire Risques Financiers”, Fondation du Risque.
Benjamin Jourdain and Alvin Tse

Abstract
. In this work, a generalised version of the central limit theorem is proposed for nonlinear functionals of the empirical measure of i.i.d. random variables, provided that the functional satisfies some regularity assumptions for the associated linear functional derivative. This generalisation can be applied to Monte-Carlo methods, even when there is a nonlinear dependence on the measure component. We use this result to deal with the contribution of the initialisation in the convergence of the fluctuations between the empirical measure of interacting diffusion and their mean-field limiting measure (as the number of particles goes to infinity), when the dependence on measure is nonlinear. A complementary contribution related to the time evolution is treated using the master equation , a parabolic PDE involving L L -derivatives with respect to the measure component, which is a stronger notion of derivative that is nonetheless related to the linear functional derivative.



Benjamin Jourdain and Alvin Tse

1.
Introduction and notations

Central limit theorems (CLTs) and their generalisations have long been studied in the last century. The first notable generalisation of the CLTs was proposed by Lyapunov in 1901, which only requires the random variables to be independent, but not necessarily identically distributed, under certain growth conditions of moments of some order
2+\delta
. The moment condition can be further weakened in the Lindeberg condition (proposed in 1922) and is used in most cases where weak convergence to a normal distribution is considered with non-identically distributed variables. See
[
23
]
for more details regarding the history of different versions of CLTs. Since then, the literature on different types of CLTs is enormous and there are corresponding versions for dependent processes, martingales and time series. In the mathematical statistics literature, particular attention has been paid to CLTs that are uniform over a class of test functions (see for instance Sections 2.5 and 2.8 in
[
33
]
), in order to extend the one-dimensional case of the indicator functions of the intervals
((-\infty,x])_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
which is covered by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov theorem. Von Mises
[
24
,
32
]
was the first to address the case of nonlinear functionals of the empirical measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}}
of independent and identically distributed
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random vectors
(\zeta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
through the use of Taylor expansions and we refer to Chapter 6 in
[
25
]
for a book presentation of the theory that he initiated. He explored the possibility that the first order term in the expansion provides a vanishing limit and then the lowest order term with nonzero limit converges to some non Gaussian distribution. While the limiting behaviour of the various terms in the expansion with derivatives computed at the common distribution
m_{0}
of the random vectors may follow from standard limit theorems from probability theory (in particular, the usual central limit theorem applies to the first order contribution), the main challenge is to prove that the remainder which mixes the empirical measure with
m_{0}
in the derivatives goes to zero. Let us now discuss this issue in the case treated in the present paper of first order expansions where the difficulty is not less.
In dimension
d=1
, Boos and Serfling
[
2
]
assume the existence of a Gateaux differential
\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}|_{\varepsilon=0^{+}}U\big(m_{0}+\varepsilon(\nu-m_{0})\big)=dU(m_{0},\nu-m_{0})
(for
\nu
any probability measure on the real line) linear in
\nu-m_{0}
of
U
at
m_{0}
such that

U\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}}\right)-U(m_{0})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}dU(m_{0},\delta_{\zeta_{i}}-m_{0})=o\left(\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}1_{\{\zeta_{i}\leq\cdot\}}-m_{0}((-\infty,\cdot])\right\|_{\infty}\right).

From the boundedness in probability of
\left(\sqrt{N}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}1_{\{\zeta_{i}\leq\cdot\}}-m_{0}((-\infty,\cdot])\right\|_{\infty}\right)_{N\geq 1}
, as a consequence of
[
13
]
, they deduce the weak convergence of
\sqrt{N}(U(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}})-U(m_{0}))
to a centered Gaussian random variable with asymptotic variance equal to the common variance of the independent and identically distributed random variables
dU(m_{0},\delta_{\zeta_{i}}-m_{0})
when they are square integrable and centered. For more flexibility, they remark that the conclusion remains valid when the third term in the left-hand side is multiplied by a random variable which converges in probability to
1
as
N\to\infty
. In addition to the limitation of their approach to dimension
d=1
, it relies on the uniformity of the approximation with respect to the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance, which is a strong assumption almost amounting to Fréchet differentiability of
U
at
m_{0}
for the Kolmogorov norm
\|\nu-m_{0}\|=\left\|\nu((-\infty,\cdot])-m_{0}((-\infty,\cdot])\right\|_{\infty}
:

U(\nu)-U(m_{0})-dU(m_{0},\nu-m_{0})=o\left(\|\nu-m_{0}\|\right),

with
dU(m_{0},\nu-m_{0})
linear but not necessarily continuous in
\nu-m_{0}
for this norm. When
m_{0}
is a probability on any measurable space, Dudley (
[
12
]
) obtains central limit theorems for
\sqrt{N}(U(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}})-U(m_{0}))
under the same notion of Fréchet differentiability with
\|\nu-m_{0}\|=\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\int f(x)(\nu-m_{0})(dx)\right|
where the class
{\mathcal{F}}
of measurable functions is such that a central limit theorem for empirical measures holds with respect to uniform convergence over
{\mathcal{F}}
. Clearly the requirements on
{\mathcal{F}}
impose some balance: the larger the value of
{\mathcal{F}}
, the easier Fréchet differentiability becomes, but the stronger the uniform convergence over
{\mathcal{F}}
becomes. The following is mentioned by Dudley
[
12
]
in p.76: “the Gateaux derivative has been considered too weak (see also p.110 in
[
14
]
, p.216 in Serfling
[
25
]
and p.40 in Huber
[
19
]
), unless there is some uniformity along different lines and such uniformity is all the more needed in this paper”.

The linear functional derivative of
U
(see
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
) that we recall and further investigate in the second section of the present paper and subsequently apply in the third section to study the asymptotic behaviour of
\sqrt{N}(U(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}})-U(m_{0}))
is also a Gateaux derivative, but with the additional weak requirement that
dU(m_{0},\nu-m_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},y)(\nu-m_{0})(dy)
, for some measurable real valued function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni y\mapsto\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},y)
with some polynomial growth assumption in
y
. Therefore, the linearity, square integrability and centered property mentioned above (when summarizing
[
2
]
and what we will also need) are automatically satisfied when the growth assumption is related to the index of the Wasserstein space that contains all the probability measures under consideration.
To avoid the uniformity leading to Fréchet differentiability required in the statistical literature, we suppose that the linear functional derivative exists not only at
m_{0}
but on a Wasserstein ball with positive radius containing
m_{0}
. This is a quite mild restriction, since when a central limit theorem holds for some statistical functional, it is in general not limited to a single value of the common distribution
m_{0}
of the samples. Then we linearise
\sqrt{N}(U(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}})-U(m_{0}))
into the sum of

(1.1)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}\bigg(\frac{N+1-i}{N}m_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{\zeta_{j}},\zeta_{i}\bigg)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}\bigg(\frac{N+1-i}{N}m_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{\zeta_{j}},x\bigg)m_{0}(dx)\bigg)

and a remainder. This decomposition is different from the one only involving
m_{0}
as the measure argument in the Gateaux derivative considered in the previously discussed literature on Von Mises differentiable statistical functions or in the recent papers
[
11
]
and
[
29
]
also using the linear functional derivative. It is aimed at enabling the analysis of the limiting behaviour of the sum by the central limit theorem for arrays of martingale increments while permitting to exploit that the very strong total variation distance between
m^{N,i}_{s}:=\frac{N+1-s-i}{N}m_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{\zeta_{j}}+\frac{s}{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}}
and
m^{N,i}_{0}
is smaller than
\frac{s}{N}
, in order to ensure that the remainder

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{s},y)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{0},y)\right)\,(\delta_{\zeta_{i}}-m_{0})(dy)\,ds

vanishes in probability as
N\to\infty
, as soon as
\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\nu,y)
satisfies some Hölder continuity with exponent
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
in total variation with respect to its first variable. In our CLT for nonlinear functionals
U
, we add some further regularity assumptions on
\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}
to check the Lindeberg condition and the convergence of the bracket of (
1.1
).

The second main result of this work is a CLT on mean-field fluctuations. Large systems of interacting individuals/agents occur in many different areas of science; the individuals/agents may be people, computers, flocks of animals, or particles in moving fluid. Mean-field theory was developed to study particle systems by considering the asymptotic behaviour of the agents or particles, as their number goes to infinity. Instead of considering a system with a huge dimension, one can effectively approximate macroscopic and statistical features of the
system as well as the average behaviour of particles. In a probabilistic setting, the limiting behaviour can be described by a type of SDEs, called McKean-Vlasov SDEs, whose coefficients depend on the probability distribution of the process itself. We consider the fluctuation between a standard particle system
(Y^{i,N})_{1\leq i\leq N}
(see (
4.7
) for its model) and its standard McKean-Vlasov limiting process
X
(see (
4.8
) for its equation). When the interaction only takes place in the drift coefficient and the diffusion coefficient is bounded from below (which, in particular, holds when the diffusion coefficient is constant), it is possible to express the density of the law of the interacting particle system with respect to that of independent copies of the McKean-Vlasov limiting process by Girsanov theorem. Then a central limit theorem may be derived by studying the limiting behaviour of this density using symmetric statistics and multiple Wiener integrals as in
[
27
]
and
[
26
]
.

When interaction also takes place in the diffusion coefficient, this is no longer possible and the standard approach in the literature involves an approximation of the
average position of a smooth test function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of the particles by (
4.8
) and its limiting fluctuation. More precisely, denoting
\mu^{N}
to be the empirical measure of all the particles and
{\mu}^{\infty}
to be the law of
X
, one considers the decomposition

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(Y^{i,N}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(X_{t})\big]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left\langle S^{N}_{t},\phi\right\rangle,

where the fluctuation measure
S^{N}
is defined by

S^{N}:=\sqrt{N}\big({\mu}^{N}-{\mu}^{\infty}\big)

and

\left\langle m,\phi\right\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\,dm
, for any signed measure
m
. The classical approach is to show that the sequence of random measures
(S^{N})_{N\geq 1}
converges in law as random processes taking values in some Sobolev space. This is done via a classical tightness argument, which implies the existence of a weak limit (through a subsequence) by the Prokhorov’s theorem. The limit is shown to satisfy an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in an appropriate space. In
[
17
]
, the Sobolev space being considered is
C([0,T],\Phi^{\prime}_{p})
, where
\Phi^{\prime}_{p}
is the dual of
\Phi_{p}
, with
\Phi_{p}
being the completion of the Schwarz space of rapidly decreasing infinitely differentiable functions under a suitable class of seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{p}
. This result was generalised in
[
22
]
to the Sobolev space
C([0,T],W^{-(2+2D),D}_{0})
, whereas the limiting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is in
C([0,T],W^{-(4+2D),D}_{0})
, where
D=1+\left\lfloor{\frac{d}{2}}\right\rfloor
. A similar result was proven in
[
11
]
to include mean-field equations with additive common noise. We remark that, in all these approaches, by considering measures to be in the dual of a Sobolev space, a linear dependence on the measure component is imposed implicitly.
Unlike the approach in
[
11
]
,
[
17
]
and
[
22
]
, we analyse the fluctuation under non-linear functionals
\Phi:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e. we consider the limiting distribution of the process

F^{N}:=\sqrt{N}\big[\Phi({\mu}^{N}_{\cdot})-\Phi({\mu}^{\infty}_{{\cdot}})\big]

in the space
C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
,
where
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the space of probability measures with finite second moments. This gives us a limiting CLT in mean-field fluctuations in the space
C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R})
.

The development of the theory in this paper relies on the calculus on the Wasserstein space. We use two notions of derivatives in measure in this paper. The first notion, the linear functional derivative, is an analogue of the variational derivative over a manifold (see
[
6
]
). Linear functional derivatives are used to prove the different versions of CLTs for i.i.d. random variables. The second notion, the L-derivative (see the notes by Cardaliaguet
[
5
]
), was introduced by Lions in his lectures at the Collège de France by defining a derivative in the
W_{2}
space based on the ‘lift’ to the
L^{2}
space of square-integrable random variables (see (
4.1
)). According to
[
15
]
, the L-derivative coincides with the geometric derivative introduced formerly in
[
1
]
. L-derivatives are used to prove the CLT for mean-field fluctuations.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 focuses on the notion of linear functional derivatives as well as their properties. Section 3 exhibits three versions of CLTs (with different sufficient conditions) through the properties of linear functional derivatives developed in Section 2. Finally, Section 4 develops the notion of L-derivatives followed by a version of CLT on mean-field fluctuations.

2.
Linear functional derivatives and their properties

The notion of linear functional derivatives appears in quite a few papers in the literature. It is defined as a functional derivative in
[
6
]
, through a limit of perturbation by linear interpolation of measures (see (
2.1
)). It can also be defined via an explicit formula concerning the difference between the values of the function evaluated at two probability measures (see (
2.4
)), as more often done in the literature of mean-field games and McKean-Vlasov equations, such as
[
7
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
31
]
. Corollary
2.4
shows that (
2.1
) implies (
2.4
) under some growth assumption. Conversely, if we assume that the linear functional derivative is continuous in the product topology of
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then one can easily check that (
2.4
) implies (
2.1
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\ell\geq 0
. A function
U:\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
admits a linear functional derivative at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if there exists a real valued measurable function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni y\mapsto\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu,y)
such that
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu,y)\right|/(1+|y|^{\ell})<\infty
and

(2.1)

\forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\;\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0^{+}}U\big(\mu+\varepsilon(\nu-\mu)\big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(\mu,y)\,(\nu-\mu)(dy).

Inductively, for
j\geq 2
, supposing that
U
admits a
(j-1)
-th order linear functional derivative

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{j-1}\ni\mathbf{y}\mapsto\frac{\delta^{j-1}U}{\delta m^{j-1}}(m,\mathbf{y})
at
m
for
m
in a
W_{\ell}
-neighbourhood of
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we say that
U
admits a
j
-th order linear functional derivative
derivative at
\mu
if for each
\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{j-1}
,
m\mapsto\frac{\delta^{j-1}U}{\delta m^{j-1}}(m,\mathbf{y})
admits a linear functional derivative at

i.e. there exists a real valued measurable function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni y\mapsto\frac{\delta^{j}U}{\delta m^{j}}(\mu,\mathbf{y},y)
such that
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{\delta^{j}U}{\delta m^{j}}(\mu,\mathbf{y},y)\right|/(1+|y|^{\ell})<\infty
and

(2.2)

\forall\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\;\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0^{+}}\frac{\delta^{j-1}U}{\delta m^{j-1}}\big(\mu+\varepsilon(\nu-\mu),\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta^{j}U}{\delta m^{j}}(\mu,\mathbf{y},y)\,(\nu-\mu)(dy).

Notice that

\frac{\delta^{j}U}{\delta m^{j}}(m,y_{1},\ldots,y_{j})=0,\quad\text{ if }y_{i}=0\,\,\text{ for some }i\in\{1,\ldots,j\}.

Definition 2.2

(Class

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j,k}(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})):=\bigg\{

The next theorem expresses a finite difference of the

-th order functional derivative as an integral of the

Theorem 2.3
.

Let

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

For simplicity of notations, the proof is presented for
j=1
. The argument for other values of
j
is identical. For
s\in(0,1)
and
0<h<s\wedge(1-s)
, by the definition of linear derivatives,

\displaystyle\frac{U(m_{s+h})-U(m_{s})}{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-s}\times\frac{U(m_{s}+(h/(1-s))(m^{\prime}-m_{s}))-U(m_{s})}{h/(1-s)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\to 0^{+}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{1-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{s},y)(1-s)(m^{\prime}-m)(dy)

\displaystyle\frac{U(m_{s-h})-U(m_{s})}{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\times\frac{U(m_{s}+(h/s)(m-m_{s}))-U(m_{s})}{h/s}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h\to 0^{+}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{s}\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{s},y)s(m-m^{\prime})(dy).

Hence
[0,1]\ni s\mapsto U(m_{s})
is differentiable on
(0,1)
with derivative
g(s):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{s},y)(m^{\prime}-m)(dy)
, admits the right-hand derivative
g(0)
at
0
and the left-hand derivative
g(1)
at
1
. This function is therefore continuous on
[0,1]
. Since
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\sup_{(s,y)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{s},y)\right|/(1+|y|^{\ell})<\infty
, the function
g
is bounded on
[0,1]
. Therefore
[0,1]\ni s\mapsto U(m_{s})
is Lipschitz continuous. We last apply the (only) theorem in
[
35
]
to deduce that

(2.5)

\int_{0}^{1}g(s)\,ds=U\big(m_{1}\big)-U\big(m_{0}\big)=U(m^{\prime})-U(m).

∎

We now state a chain rule concerning the computation of linear functional derivatives. It is an easy consequence of the classical chain rule and the fact the normalisation convention (
2.3
) clearly holds.

Theorem 2.5

(Chain rule)
.

Let
\ell\geq 0
,
\varphi:\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
be a function such that each of its coordinates admits a linear functional derivative at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We denote by
\frac{\delta{\varphi}}{\delta m}(\mu,y)
the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
with coordinates given by these linear functional derivatives. Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function differentiable at
\varphi(\mu)
. Then the function
U:\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

U(\mu):=F(\varphi(\mu))
admits a linear functional derivative at
\mu
given by

\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(\mu,y)=\nabla F\big(\varphi(\mu)\big).\frac{\delta{\varphi}}{\delta m}(\mu,y).

The following example is an easy but important consequence of the chain rule and will be used in subsequent parts of the paper.

Example 2.6

(A differentiable function of a linear functional of measures)
.

Let
\ell\geq 0
,
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|G(x)|}{1+|x|^{\ell}}<\infty

j

\frac{\delta^{i}U}{\delta m^{i}}(\mu,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=F^{(i)}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(x)\,\mu(dx)\bigg)\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}(G(y_{i^{\prime}})-G(0)).

3.

. Note that, by this growth assumption and Example
2.7

\mathcal{S}_{1,\ell/2}(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with
\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m,x)=\varphi(x)
. For general functionals
U\in\mathcal{S}_{1,\ell/2}(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, by the classical central limit theorem,

\sqrt{N}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\,(m^{N}-m_{0})(dx)\bigg]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\implies}}\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\bigg)\bigg).

One could consider the same remainder

(3.1)

R_{N}:=U(m^{N})-U(m_{0})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\,(m^{N}-m_{0})(dx)

as in the literature on Von Mises differentiable statistical functions and check using a linearisation in measure by Theorem
2.3
that, under extra regularity assumptions on
U
,
\sqrt{N}R_{N}
goes to
0
in probability as
N\to\infty
. For instance, when
U\in\mathcal{S}_{4,4}(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{8}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Theorem 2.5 in
[
29
]
which is inspired by Lemma 5.10 in
[
11
]
ensures that
\mathbb{E}[R_{N}^{2}]\lesssim\frac{1}{N^{2}}
. In Theorem
3.1
below, we will rather find weaker regularity assumptions under which

\sqrt{N}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}\bigg(\frac{N+1-i}{N}m_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{\zeta_{j}},\zeta_{i}\bigg)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}\bigg(\frac{N+1-i}{N}m_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{\zeta_{j}},x\bigg)m_{0}(dx)\bigg)

converges in distribution to
\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\bigg)\bigg)
by the central limit theorem for martingale increments
and the difference between this term and
\sqrt{N}(U(m^{N})-U(m_{0}))
goes to
0
in probability as
N\to\infty
.

Since the asymptotic variance is expressed in terms of
\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}
, one can easily compute its value via Theorems
2.5
,
2.8
and
2.9
. For functionals
U
which do not satisfy the regularity assumptions in Theorem
3.1
, the asymptotic variance in the central limit theorem can still be given by
\text{Var}\left(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\right)
. Indeed, for the example of the quantile function in dimension
d=1
, it is shown that under the assumptions of Theorem
2.10
,
\sqrt{N}(U(v,m^{N})-U(v,m_{0}))
converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
\frac{v(1-v)}{p_{0}^{2}(U(v,m_{0}))}
. Since
1_{\{\zeta_{1}\leq U(v,m_{0})\}}
is a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
v
and variance
v(1-v)
,
\text{Var}\left(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(v,m_{0},\zeta_{1})\right)=\frac{v(1-v)}{p_{0}^{2}(U(v,m_{0}))}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\ell\geq 0
,
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
m^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{{\zeta}_{i}}
, where
{\zeta}_{1},\ldots,{\zeta}_{N}
are i.i.d. random variables with law
m_{0}
.
Let
D(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})
denote the metric on
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equal to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}|(dy)
if
m_{0}
is discrete and otherwise to
1_{\{\ell>0\}}W_{\ell}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})+1_{\{\ell=0\}}\underline{W}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})
.

Suppose that there exists
r>0
such that

•

U
admits a linear functional derivative on the ball
B(m_{0},r)
centered at
m_{0}
with radius
r
for the metric
D
,

•

(3.2)

\exists C<\infty,\;\forall(\mu,x)\in B(m_{0},r)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\quad\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu,x)\right|\leq C\left(1+|x|^{\ell/2}\right),

•

\exists\alpha\in(1/2,1],\;\exists C<\infty,\;\forall\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in B(m_{0},r),\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(3.3)

\bigg|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu_{2},x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu_{1},x)\bigg|\leq C\bigg((1+|x|^{\ell})W_{0}^{\alpha}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})+(1+|x|^{\ell(1-\alpha)})\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}|(dy)\right)^{\alpha}\bigg),

•

(3.4)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\Big|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu,x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\Big|}{1+|x|^{\ell/2}}\text{ converges to }0\text{ when }D(\mu,m_{0})\text{ goes to }0.

Then the following convergence in distribution holds :

\sqrt{N}\bigg(U(m^{N})-U(m_{0})\bigg)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\implies}}\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\bigg)\bigg).

Remark 3.2
.

Using a
W_{0}
-optimal coupling between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, one easily checks (see for instance Theorem 6.15
[
34
]
when
\ell\geq 1
) that

(3.5)

W_{\ell}^{\ell\vee 1}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})\leq 2^{(\ell-1)\vee 0}W_{0}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}|(dy)\leq 2^{(\ell-1)\vee 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}|(dy).

Hence any ball with positive radius for the metric
1_{\{\ell>0\}}W_{\ell}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})+1_{\{\ell=0\}}\underline{W}(\mu_{2},\mu_{1})
contains a ball with positive radius for the metric
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}|(dy)
. Moreover (
3.4
) is weaker for the latter choice of
D(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
than for the former so that the assumptions of the theorem are satisfied for the latter when they are satisfied for the former. Unfortunately, when
m_{0}
is not discrete, then
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N}-m_{0}|(dy)
does not go to
0
as
N\to\infty
. This explains why we restrict the choice
D(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}|(dy)
to the case when
m_{0}
is discrete.

Proof.

For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and
s\in[0,1]
, let

(3.6)

m^{N,i}_{s}:=\bigg(1+\frac{1-i-s}{N}\bigg)m_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{\zeta_{j}}+\frac{s}{N}\delta_{\zeta_{i}}.

Notice that since
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the random measure
m^{N,i}_{s}
also belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We have
U(m^{N})-U(m_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(U(m^{N,i}_{1})-U(m^{N,i}_{0}))
. To be able to write the difference
U(m^{N,i}_{1})-U(m^{N,i}_{0})
in terms of the linear functional derivative
\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}
, we are first going to check that

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}D(m^{N,i}_{s},m_{0})

converges a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
.

First step : a.s. uniform convergence of
m^{N,i}_{s}
to
m_{0}

Since for
s\in[0,1]
,
m^{N,i}_{s}=sm^{N,i}_{1}+(1-s)m^{N,i-1}_{1}
under the convention
m^{N,0}_{1}=m_{0}
, we have

W_{\ell}^{\ell\vee 1}(m^{N,i}_{s},m_{0})\leq sW_{\ell}^{\ell\vee 1}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})+(1-s)W_{\ell}^{\ell\vee 1}(m^{N,i-1}_{1},m_{0})\leq W_{\ell}^{\ell\vee 1}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})\vee W_{\ell}^{\ell\vee 1}(m^{N,i-1}_{1},m_{0}).

Dealing in the same way with
\underline{W}
, we deduce that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left(1_{\{\ell>0\}}W_{\ell}(m^{N,i}_{s},m_{0})+1_{\{\ell=0\}}{\underline{W}}(m^{N,i}_{s},m_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left(1_{\{\ell>0\}}W_{\ell}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})+1_{\{\ell=0\}}{\underline{W}}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})\right).

Since a.s.
m^{N}
converges weakly to
m_{0}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{\ell}m^{N}(dx)
goes to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{\ell}m_{0}(dx)
as
N\to\infty
, for
\ell>0
, the sequence
W_{\ell}(m^{N},m_{0})
converges a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
and is therefore a.s. bounded.
Moreover,
W_{\ell}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})\leq(i/N)^{1\wedge 1/\ell}W_{\ell}(m^{i},m_{0})
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, by considering the two cases
i/N\leq\alpha
and
i/N>\alpha
, we deduce that

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}W_{\ell}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})\leq\alpha^{1\wedge 1/\ell}\max_{j\geq 1}W_{\ell}(m^{j},m_{0})+\max_{j\geq\lceil\alpha N\rceil}W_{\ell}(m^{j},m_{0}).

Choosing small values of
\alpha
followed by large values of
N
, we conclude that
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}W_{\ell}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})
goes to
0
a.s. as
N\to\infty
.

By Corollary 6.13
[
34
]
,
\underline{W}
metricises the topology of weak convergence on
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and therefore
\underline{W}(m^{N},m_{0})
converges a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
. Moreover, since
|x-y|\wedge 1\leq 1_{\{x\neq y\}}
,
\underline{W}\leq W_{0}
and

\underline{W}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})\leq W_{0}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})\leq\frac{i}{N}
. By adapting to
\underline{W}
, as well as the above reasoning for
W_{\ell}
, we deduce that
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\underline{W}(m^{N,i}_{1},m_{0})
goes to
0
a.s. as
N\to\infty
.

Let us now assume that
m_{0}
is discrete, i.e. there is a sequence
(y_{k})_{1\leq k\leq{\mathcal{K}}}
with
{\mathcal{K}}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\cup\{+\infty\}
of distinct elements of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}m_{0}(\{y_{j}\})=1
. Then, by the strong law of large numbers, a.s. for each
1\leq k\leq{\mathcal{K}}
,
m^{N}(\{y_{k}\})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}1_{\{\zeta_{i}=y_{k}\}}
converges to
m_{0}(\{y_{k}\})
. When
{\mathcal{K}}
is finite, we deduce that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N}-m_{0}|(dy)=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}(1+|y_{k}|^{\ell})|m^{N}(\{y_{k}\})-m_{0}(\{y_{k}\})|
converges to
0
a.s. as
N\to\infty
. When
{\mathcal{K}}
is infinite, we have, for each
\bar{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})

\displaystyle|m^{N}-m_{0}|(dy)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\bar{k}}(1+|y_{k}|^{\ell})|m^{N}(\{y_{k}\})-m_{0}(\{y_{k}\})|

\displaystyle+\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1+|\zeta_{i}|^{\ell})1_{\{y_{\bar{k}+1},y_{\bar{k}+2},...\}}(\zeta_{i})-\sum_{k>\bar{k}}(1+|y_{k}|^{\ell})m_{0}(\{y_{k}\})\bigg|+2\sum_{k>\bar{k}}(1+|y_{k}|^{\ell})m_{0}(\{y_{k}\}).

The third term of the right-hand side is arbitrarily small for
\bar{k}
large enough, whereas for fixed
\bar{k}
, by the strong law of large numbers, the sum of the two first terms converges a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
. Hence,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N}-m_{0}|(dy)
goes a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
. Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N,i}_{1}-m_{0}|(dy)=\frac{i}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{i}-m_{0}|(dy)
, by repeating the above reasoning performed for
W_{\ell}
, we obtain that
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N,i}_{1}-m_{0}|(dy)
converges a.s. to
0
. Since for
s\in[0,1]
,
|m^{N,i}_{s}-m_{0}|\leq s|m^{N,i}_{1}-m_{0}|+(1-s)|m^{N,i}_{1}-m_{0}|
, we conclude that
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N,i}_{s}-m_{0}|(dy)=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|y|^{\ell})|m^{N,i}_{1}-m_{0}|(dy)
converges a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
.

Second step : introduction of the linear functional derivative

Under the convention
\min\emptyset:=N+1
, we deduce that for the radius
r>0
of the ball introduced in the hypotheses of the theorem,

(3.8)

I_{N}:=\min\{1\leq i\leq N:\exists s\in[0,1]:D(m^{N,i}_{s},m_{0})\geq r\}

is almost surely
N+1
for each
N\geq N^{*}
, for some random variable
N^{*}
taking integer values. For
N\geq N^{*}
, we have, using (
3.2
) and Theorem
2.3
for the second equality,

\displaystyle U(m^{N})-U(m_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[U\left(m^{N,i}_{1}\right)-U\left(m^{N,i}_{0}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{s},y)\,(\delta_{\zeta_{i}}-m_{0})(dy)\,ds.

Setting

(3.9)

Q_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},\zeta_{i})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right),

we deduce that for
N\geq N^{*}
,
U(m^{N})-U(m_{0})-Q_{N}
coincides with

(3.10)

R_{N}:=\frac{1_{\{N\geq N^{*}\}}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{s},y)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{0},y)\right)\,(\delta_{\zeta_{i}}-m_{0})(dy)\,ds.

Therefore to check that
\sqrt{N}(U(m^{N})-U(m_{0})-Q_{N})
goes to
0
in probability as
N\to\infty
, it is enough to check that so does
\sqrt{N}R_{N}
, which is the purpose of the third step of the proof. By Slutsky’s theorem, to complete the proof, it is enough to check that
\sqrt{N}Q_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\implies}}\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\bigg)\bigg)
, which is done in the fourth step using the Central Limit Theorem for arrays of martingale increments.

Third step : convergence in probability of
\sqrt{N}R_{N}
to
0

Since
|m^{N,i}_{s}-m^{N,i}_{0}|(dy)\leq\frac{s}{N}(\delta_{\zeta_{i}}+m_{0})(dy)
, using (
3.3
) and Young’s inequality, we obtain that for
N\geq N^{*}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{s},x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i}_{0},x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg((1+|x|^{\ell})\left(\frac{2s}{N}\right)^{\alpha}+(1+|x|^{\ell(1-\alpha)})\left(\frac{s}{N}|\zeta_{i}|^{\ell}+\frac{s}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}m_{0}(dy)\right)^{\alpha}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{N^{\alpha}}\left((2^{\alpha}+1)(1+|x|^{\ell})+2|\zeta_{i}|^{\ell}+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}m_{0}(dy)\right).

Using that
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we easily deduce that
\mathbb{E}|R_{N}|\lesssim N^{-\alpha}
and since
\alpha>1/2
,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\sqrt{N}|R_{N}|=0
.

Fourth step : application of the Central Limit Theorem for martingales

Let us introduce the filtration
({\mathcal{F}}_{i}:=\sigma(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{i}))_{i\geq 1}
for which
I_{N}
defined in (
3.8
) is a stopping time.
By (
3.2
), for
1\leq i\leq N
, the random variable

X_{N,i}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},\zeta_{i})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)

is square integrable. Since
m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}1_{\{I_{n}=j\}}m^{N,j}_{0}+1_{\{I_{N}>i-1\}}m^{N,i}_{0}
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{i-1}:=\sigma(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{i-1})
-measurable and
\zeta_{i}
is independent of this sigma-field, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{N,i}|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\right]=0
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}_{N,i}|{\mathcal{F}}_{i-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)\right)^{2}m_{0}(dx)-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)^{2}\right).

The convergence of
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu,x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right|/(1+|x|^{\ell/2})
to 0 when
D(\mu,m_{0})
goes to
0
together with the a.s. convergence of
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}D(m^{N,i}_{0},m_{0})
to
0
imply the existence of a sequence of random variables
(\varepsilon_{N})_{N\geq 0}
converging a.s. to
0
as
N\to\infty
such that

(3.11)

\forall 1\leq i\leq N,\;\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right|\leq(1+|x|^{\ell/2})\varepsilon_{N}.

Since
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell/2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we deduce that

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right|

converges a.s. to
0
. By continuity of the square function, so do

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)^{2}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)^{2}\right|

and the smaller difference of mean values
\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)^{2}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)^{2}\right|
. On the other hand, using (
3.11
) and (
3.2
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right)^{2}+2\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right|\left|\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)-\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left((1+|x|^{\ell/2})\varepsilon_{N}+2C\left(1+|x|^{\ell/2}+1_{\{\ell>0\}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}m_{0}(dy)\right)^{1/2}\right)\right)(1+|x|^{\ell/2})\varepsilon_{N}.

Since
m_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we deduce that
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)\right)^{2}m_{0}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right)^{2}m_{0}(dx)\right|
converges a.s. to
0
and
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m^{N,i\wedge I_{N}}_{0},x)\right)^{2}m_{0}(dx)
to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right)^{2}m_{0}(dx)
. Therefore
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[X^{2}_{N,i}|{\mathcal{F}}_{i-1}\right]
converges a.s. to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)\right)^{2}m_{0}(dx)-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(m_{0},x)m_{0}(dx)\right)^{2}=\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\bigg)
as
N\to\infty
. By Corollary 3.1 p58
[
16
]
, to conclude that

\sqrt{N}Q_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{N,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\implies}}\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\text{Var}\bigg(\frac{\delta U}{\delta m}(m_{0},\zeta_{1})\bigg)\bigg),

it is enough to check the Lindeberg condition : for each
\varepsilon>0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{N,i}^{2}1_{\{X_{N,i}^{2}>\varepsilon\}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{i-1}\right]
goes to
0
in probability as
N\to\infty
.
When
\ell=0
,
\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}
is bounded on
B(m_{0},r)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and this condition is clearly satisfied. Let us suppose that
\ell>0
and check that it is also satisfied.

By (
3.2
),

\displaystyle\exists C<\infty,\;\forall(\mu,x)\in B(m_{0},r)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\left(\frac{\delta{U}}{\delta m}(\mu,x)\right)^{2}\leq C\left(1+|x|^{\ell}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{\ell}\mu(dy)\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle NX_{N,i}^{2}

4.2.
Mean-field fluctuation

We define Lipschitz-continuous (w.r.t. the product topology of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\begin{cases}Y^{i,N}_{t}=\xi_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}b(Y^{i,N}_{s},{\mu}^{N}_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(Y^{i,N}_{s},{\mu}^{N}_{s})\,dW^{i}_{s},\quad 1\leq i\leq N,\quad t\geq 0,\\

{d^{\prime}}

W
is a
{d^{\prime}}
-dimensional Brownian motion and
\xi\sim\nu
is independent of

. Analyses of the approximation of (

[
