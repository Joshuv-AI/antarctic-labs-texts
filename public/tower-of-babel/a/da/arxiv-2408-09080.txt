A Relational Category of Birkhoff Polarities
M. Andrew Moshier Chapman University

Abstract
Garret Birkhoff observed that any binary relation between two sets determines a Galois connection between the powersets, or equivalently, closure operators on the powersets, or equivalently, complete lattices of subsets that are dually isomorphic. Referring to the duality of, say, points and lines in projective geometry, he named the binary relations polarities . Researchers since then have used polarities (also known as formal contexts) as a convenient technical way to build complete lattices from “found” data. And so, various proposals for suitable morphisms between polarities have tended to have a particular application in mind. In this work, we develop the structure of a category of polarities and compatible relations, adopting Birkhoff’s original simple idea that the structure of a polarity is its the Galois connection. Hence, morphisms must be relations that, in a reasonable sense, preserve Galois connections. In particular, the dual equivalence of the category to the category of complete meet semilattices, completeness of the category, characterization of epimorphisms and monomorphisms, an epi/mono factorization system, as well as the star-autonomous structure of the category, all arise by extending Birkhoff’s original observation to morphisms.



1
Introduction

Following Garrett Birkhoff
[
1
]
,
a
polarity
is a binary relation between two sets,
determining a complete lattice as explained below.
A typical and motivating example is the polarity obtained
from a finite lattice by taking the join irreducibles
as one set, the meet irreducibles as the second set,
and the less than or equals relation between them.
These data are enough to reconstruct an isomorphic copy
of the lattice.
Or starting from any partially ordered set,
take two copies of set and the less than or equal relation.
These data determine the MacNeille completion
(the injective hull) of the poset.

Polarities are known more recently thanks to the concept
analysis community
[
5
]

as
formal contexts
,
where the standard notation is
(G,M;I)
or
(O,A;I)

standing for Gegenständen (Objects),
Merkmalen (Attributes) and Inzidenz (Incidence),
as formal contexts are meant by the concept analysis community
as a formalization of how objects can be related
to their attributes.
Though
(formal) context
is the common contemporary name,
we use the name
polarity
here to remind the reader
that the idea dates earlier to Birkhoff.
Birkhoff chose the term ‘polarity’ from an analogy with the duality
between points (poles) and polar lines in projective geometry.
Indeed, the self-dual nature of polarities will play a role
in this paper that is obscured by thinking of polarities
as comprised of objects and attributes.
To emphasize the dual nature of polarities,
we name a context by its incidence relation
and mark the underlying sets
as in
\mathcal{A}=(A^{-},A^{+};\mathcal{A})
.

Polarities determine complete lattices, and all complete lattices
arise up to isomorphism from polarities.
But complete lattices are, of course, the same structures
as complete meet-semilattices and as complete join-semilattices.
The differences only show up when morphisms are considered.
In this paper, we investigate a notion of morphism
for polarities that is, so to speak, “native”
to Birkhoff’s idea of taking polarities as objects of study.
We answer the question of what constitutes a general morphism
by taking the incidence relation of a polarity
as its identity morphism,
and extending that to general morphisms.
The category obtained this way is quite natural
and admits interesting purely combinatorical methods
of construction for its categorical structure.

In the formal context literature one finds various discussions
of possible ways to formulate a category of polarities.
The main author who has looked at possible morphisms
of polarities is Marcel Erné

[
3
,
2
]
.
This work deals with morphisms from polarity
\mathcal{A}

to polarity
\mathcal{B}
as pairs
of maps
{f^{-}}\colon{A^{-}}\to{B^{-}}
and
{f^{+}}\colon{A^{+}}\to{B^{+}}

satisfying obvious compatibility conditions.
Erné defines a
conceptual pair
to be such a pair of maps

(f^{-},f^{+})
that jointly preserves the incidence relation
and for which the pre-image under
f^{-}
of a Galois closed
subset of
B^{-}
is Galois closed in
A^{-}
and likewise,
the pre-image under
f^{+}
of a Galois closed subset
B^{+}

is closed in
A^{+}
.
The main point is that the obvious construction of a polarity
from a complete lattice, sending
L
to
(L,L,\leq)
,
is functorial and has a left adjoint that exhibits
complete lattices as a coreflective subcategory
of Erné’s category of polarities.
Polarities and conceptual pairs via the coreflection are
turned into complete lattices
and complete lattice homomorphisms.
The paper
[
3
]
provides a wealth
of other details and an elegant classification
of various special morphisms of complete lattices in terms
of their manifestation in maps between polarities.
Much of that paper is motivated by his earlier work

[
2
]
, in which Erné considered
characterizations of polarities, the corresponding
complete lattices of which satisfy various distributive laws.

An alternative to Erné is to regard a polarity
as a Chu space (over
2
).
Chu space morphisms are pairs of functions

{f^{-}}\colon{A^{-}}\to{B^{-}}
and
{f^{+}}\colon{B^{+}}\to{A^{+}}

also satisfying certain compatibility conditions.
Chu spaces, of course, have a significant literature of their own.
See Vineet Gupta’s PhD thesis
[
9
]

for an early application,
and Vaughan Pratt’s excellent survey
[
11
]

for an introduction and bibliography.

More recently, Robert Goldblatt
[
8
]

develops a category of polarities in the spirit of Erné
with pairs of functions that satisfy a natural “back and forth”
condition generalizing bounded morphisms that are familiar
in the literature on algebraic semantics of modal logic.

G.Q. Zhang, et al,
[
10
,
13
]
,
also considers categories in which the objects are
essentially polarities.
But in these papers, the morphisms are chosen to yield
a category equivalent to the category of information systems,
hence dually equivalent to Scott domains.
The morphisms do not have directly to do
with the usual interpretation of a polarity, nor a Chu space,
as specifying a complete lattice.
In effect, these papers use the terms “Chu space”
and “context” simply to mean “binary relation”
and then investigate a category that has little to do
with either Chu spaces or polarities.

In
[
6
]
, Gehrke investigates
a special class of polarities the author calls

RS-frames
.
These are meant as generalizations of Kripke frames
in the sense that one has “worlds” (as in a Kripke frame)
and “co-worlds”.
The S in RS stands for
separated
.
This is a fairly harmless condition that is roughly
analogous to being a
T_{0}
space
– worlds and co-worlds are “separated” by each other.
The R stands for
reduced
.
This is a substantive condition which translates, again roughly,
to say that the worlds and co-worlds are, respectively,
join and meet irreducible.
In classical modal logic, a world (that is an element of a Kripke
model) spells out the truth and falsity of each
proposition, and thus determines a prime filter
of propositions.
RS-frames capture a similar idea in more generality.
Polarities, regarded as generalized Kripke frames,
feature prominently algebraic proofs of cut elimination
for substructural logic as in
[
4
]
.

RS-frames constitute a very special case of polarities.
The complete lattices that RS-frames determine are
perfect
,
i.e., they are join generated by their completely join
irreducibles and meet generated by their
completely meet irreducibles.
Of course, such lattices are important for Gehrke’s application
to modal logic, but perfection is a rare property of complete lattices.
Indeed, the unit interval of reals regarded as a complete lattice fails
to have any completely join irreducibles or any completely
meet irreducibles.
So an RS-frame cannot describe this lattice.

We propose to consider general polarities with morphisms based
on the idea that the incidence relation is itself
the identity morphism on the object.
The Galois connection determined
by a polarity is the relevant structure.
Thus a morphism must also be a binary relation
between the lower set of the domain polarity
and the upper set of the codomain polarity.
Once we understand what composition must do,
the morphisms are defined as those relations for which
the incidence relations on the domain and codomain polarities
act as identities.

The initial work reported here was first developed during a visit
to St. Anne’s College, Oxford as a Plumer Fellow in 2011 and 2012.
The author thanks Hilary Priestley for being such
a gracious host and valued colleague.

2
The category of polarities

In this section, we present the category of polarities
and relations, and prove a few useful technical results.

For order theory, we follow the naming and general notation in

Continuous Lattics and Domains

[
7
]
.
In particular,
\mathord{\mathbf{SUP}}
is the category of complete lattices
and supremum preserving functions,
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
, the category of
complete lattices and infimum preserving functions,
and
\mathord{\mathbf{CLat}}
is the category of complete lattices
and functions that preserve both infima and suprema.

Functions into an ordered structure are always regarded
as being pointwise ordered, unless explicitly described otherwise.
In particular, antitone maps between ordered structures

P
and
Q
are ordered as if they are monotone maps
from the order opposite of
P
to
Q
.

The left adjoint of a monotone function
{f}\colon{P}\to{Q}
if it exists,
is denoted by
{f_{*}}\colon{P}\to{Q}
.
Likewise, the right adjoint of a monotone function
{g}\colon{Q}\to{P}
,
if it exists, is denoted by
{g^{*}}\colon{L}\to{M}
.
The reader will recall that morphisms in
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
always
have left adjoints in
\mathord{\mathbf{SUP}}
, and vice versa.

For poset
P
, the order opposite of poset
P
is denoted by
P^{\partial}
.
Thus the equivalence of the categories
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}

and
\mathord{\mathbf{SUP}}
sends
L
to
L^{\partial}

and leaves
{f}\colon{L}\to{M}
alone as a concrete function.
The dual equivalence from
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
to
\mathord{\mathbf{SUP}}
leaves objects
(complete lattices) alone,
and sends
{f}\colon{L}\to{M}
to
{f_{*}}\colon{M}\to{L}
.

The well-known correspondence between closure operators and
closure systems plays a part in the work,
so a quick reminder is useful.
A
closure operator
on a set
A
is a function

{c}\colon{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}\to{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}
that is monotonic,
inflationary (meaning
X\subseteq c(X)
holds for all
X
),
and idempotent.
A
closure system
is a family
M\subseteq\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)

that is closed under arbitrary intersections.
The set of fixpoints of a closure operator
(
\mathord{\textsf{M}}_{c}=\{X\in\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)\,\mid\,c(X)=X\}
)
is a closed system.
For a closure system
M\subseteq\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)
,
the function
\textsf{c}_{M}
defined by
X\mapsto\bigcap\{Y\in M\,\mid\,X\subseteq Y\}

is a closure operator.
The move from
c
to
\mathord{\textsf{M}}_{c}
and from
M
to
\mathord{\textsf{c}}_{M}

are inverses.

For a binary relation
R\subseteq A\times B
,
we write
R^{\intercal}
for its converse relation;

R[a]
for the usual “forward image” consisting
of all
\beta\in B
related to
a
; and

{R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}}\colon{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)}\to{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}
and

{R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}}\colon{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}\to{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)}

for the antitone maps defined by

\displaystyle R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(Y)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\beta\in Y}R^{\intercal}[\beta]

\displaystyle R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{a\in X}R[a].

So, it is easy to see that the following are all equivalent:

•

X\times Y\subseteq R
;

•

Y\subseteq R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X)
;

•

X\subseteq R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(Y)
;

•

Y\subseteq(R^{\intercal})^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(X)
; and

•

X\subseteq(R^{\intercal})^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)
.

Remark 2.1
.

As an aid to type checking, we generally use lower case
Latin letters for elements in the domain of a relation
and lower case Greek letters for elements of the codomain.

The functions
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
and
R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}

form a Galois connection on the subsets of
A
and
B
.
So both
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
and
R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}
send arbitrary unions to intersections,
and both composites
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}
and
R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

are closure operators on the domain and codomain of

, respectively.

Since
\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)^{\partial}
is the free
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
object
over the set

, any antitone map

{f}\colon{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)}\to{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}
(regarded as a function
from
\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)^{\partial}
) to
\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)
that happens
to send unions — meets in
\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)^{\partial})
—
to intersections arises uniquely as

for some binary relation between

and
B
.

Thus the set of relations
\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A\times B)

and the hom set
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}(\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)^{\partial},\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A))

are in a bijection.
One direction sends
R
to
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.
For the other direction,
suppose
{f}\colon{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)}\to{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}
is
an antitone map,
not necessarily sending unions to intersections.
Let
\lceil f\rceil
denote the binary relation

\lceil f\rceil\subseteq A\times B
defined by

if and only if

,
and
\lfloor f\rfloor
for the converse of

.

Lemma 2.2
.

The operation
R\mapsto R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
is right adjoint
to the operation
f\mapsto\lceil f\rceil
,
in the sense that
\lceil f\rceil\subseteq R
if and only if

f\leq R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.
Moreover,
\lceil R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\rceil=R
.
Likewise,
R\mapsto R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}
is right adjoint to

f\mapsto\lfloor f\rfloor
, and
\lfloor R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\rfloor=R
.

An antitone map
{f}\colon{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B)}\to{\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(A)}

sends arbitrary unions to intersections
if and only if
f=\lceil f\rceil^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.

Proof.

The adjunction facts are trivial to check.
As noted,
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
sends unions to intersections
for any binary relation
R
.
If
f
sends unions to intersections,
its behavior is determined by its behavior on singletons.
But
f
and
\lceil f\rceil^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
agree on singletons.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For binary relations

,
the following are equivalent:

•

R\subseteq S
;

•

R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

•

R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\leq S^{\mathord{\uparrow}}

R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\lceil R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\rceil\subseteq S
,

.
Likewise,

\lfloor R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\rfloor\subseteq S

∎

The complete lattices of fixpoints are

\displaystyle\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
{R}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}

Lemma 2.7

For polarities
\mathcal{A}
and

Proof.

Remark 2.8
.

Compatibilization of a given relation may involve transfinite recursion.
This is because the operator
c
sending
R
to

c(R)=\bigcup_{X\times Y\subseteq R}\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{A}}(X)\times\mathord{\textsf{cl}}^{\mathcal{B}}(Y)

is monotonic and inflationary, but may not be idempotent.
So the process by which

is obtained may need to pass through transfinitely
many iterates by letting
R^{0}=R

for each ordinal,
and

for limits.
Then
R^{*}
is
R^{\rho}
at the first stage for which this is a fixpoint of

Lemma 2.11

\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

Lemma 2.13
.

The category
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

\mathord{\textsf{pol}}(R)

\mathord{\textsf{rel}}(\mathcal{B})

\mathord{\textsf{pol}}(R)

\mathord{\textsf{pol}}(R)

\mathcal{B}

Lemma 2.14
.

For any polarity
\mathcal{A}
, there is a separating polarity isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Define

given by

\mathcal{A}[a]=\mathcal{A}[a^{\prime}]
.
Then define

[a]\hat{\mathcal{A}}\alpha

.
Now it is easy to check that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}=\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.
So
\hat{{\mathcal{A}}}
serves also as a morphism from
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
to
\mathcal{A}
, and
{\mathcal{A}}
serves as a morphism from
\mathcal{A}
to
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
.
The above identities translate to

-separating.

\check{\mathcal{A}}
that isomorphic to

Remark 2.15
.

The construction of a separating polarity from
\mathcal{A}
is actually a functor, and the full category of separating polarities is equivalent to
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

Contrast this with topological spaces,

.

\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

Proof.

\mathcal{A}

For a compatible relation
{R}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}

\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})

For compatible relations
{R}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}

and
{S}\colon{\mathcal{B}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{C}}
, composition is preserved:

\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R\fatsemi S)=R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}
.
So
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}
is indeed functorial
into the category of monotone maps between
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
objects. It remains
to check that
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R)
preserves infima.

Lemma 3.1
.

The functor
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}
sends a compatible relation
R
to an intersection
preserving map between Galois closed sets.
Hence, it co-restricts to a functor
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}^{\textsf{op}}\Rightarrow\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
.

Proof.

Consider polarities
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
and a family
\{Y_{i}\}_{i}
in
\mathord{\textsf{cl}}^{-}(\mathcal{B})
,

\displaystyle R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(\bigcap_{i}Y_{i})

\displaystyle=R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(\bigcap_{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y_{i}))

\displaystyle=R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(\bigcup_{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y_{i}))

\displaystyle=R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(\bigcup_{i}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y_{i}))

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y_{i})

Compatibility with
\mathcal{A}
means that the image of
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}

is in
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Note that
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}
is contravariant in spite of what the notation
suggests, because we have chosen to write composition in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

diagramatically (as is appropriate for relations) and in
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
applicatively (as is appropriate for functions).

For any object
L
in
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
, define its
obvious
polarity
to be

\mathord{\textsf{C}}(L)\mathrel{:=}(L,L,\leq_{L}).

Clearly,
\mathord{\leq_{L}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}}(X)

is the collection of all upper bounds of
X
, and similarly

\mathord{\leq_{L}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}}(X)
is the collection of all lower bounds.
So,
\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathord{\textsf{C}}(L)}(X)={\mathord{\downarrow}}\bigvee X

and
\mathord{\textsf{cl}}^{\mathord{\textsf{C}}(L)}(X)={\mathord{\uparrow}}\bigwedge X
.

For an
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
morphism
{h}\colon{M}\to{L}
, define the relation

\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)\subseteq L\times M
by

a\mathrel{\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)}b\mathrel{\,:\Longleftrightarrow\,}a\leq_{L}h(b).

Hence
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{id}}_{L})=\leq_{L}
.
Moreover,
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(Y)={\mathord{\downarrow}}h(\bigwedge Y)
, and

\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X)=\{b\in M\,\mid\,\forall a\in X.a\leq_{L}h(b)\}
.
So,
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X)={\mathord{\uparrow}}h_{*}(\bigvee X)
– recalling that
h_{*}
is the left adjoint of
h
.
In summary,
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)
is compatible with the polarities

\mathord{\textsf{C}}(L)
and
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(M)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

\mathord{\textsf{C}}
constitutes a contravariant functor from
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
to
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
.

Proof.

All that remains is to check that composition is
preserved.
Suppose
{g}\colon{N}\to{M}
and
{h}\colon{M}\to{L}
are morphisms in
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
.
Because
hg
preserves infima,
a\in\mathord{\textsf{C}}(hg)^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(Z)

if and only if
a\leq hg(\bigwedge Z)
for any
Z\subseteq N
.
And because
g
preserves infima,
a\in\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h)^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq_{M}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\mathord{\textsf{C}}(g)^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(Z)

if and only if
a\leq h(b)
for every
b\in M

satisfying
g(\bigwedge Z)\leq b
.
∎

Now we have the ingredients for our main theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

The category
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
is dually equivalent to the category
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
.

Proof.

For a given polarity
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}))
is the
polarity
(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}),\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}),\subseteq)
.
Define

\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq A^{-}\times\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
as
the membership relation restricted to
A^{-}
and
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
.

So for a family
\Xi\subseteq\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
,

\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(\Xi)=\bigcap_{X\in\Xi}X
.
For
X\subseteq A^{-}
,

\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X)=\{Y\in\mathord{\textsf{cl}}^{-}(\mathcal{X})\,\mid\,X\subseteq Y\}
.
Hence
\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)=\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{A}}(Y)
and

\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(\{X_{i}\}_{i})=\{Y\in\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})\,\mid\,Y\supseteq\bigcap_{i}X_{i}\}.

The latter is clearly the Galois closure
\mathord{\subseteq^{\mathord{\uparrow}}}\mathord{\subseteq^{\mathord{\downarrow}}}(\{X_{i}\}_{i})

in the polarity
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}))
. So

\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}
is an isomorphism.

For naturality, suppose
{R}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}

is a compatible relation.
Then
R\fatsemi\epsilon_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}\fatsemi\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R))

can be compared as follows. For
a\in A^{-}
and
Y\in\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{B})
, it is the case that

a\mathrel{R\fatsemi\epsilon_{\mathcal{B}}}Y

if and only if
a\in R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)
, which is equivalent to
{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)\subseteq R[a]
. And
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R))^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
sends
Y
to the principle downset in
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})

generated by
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)
.
The incidence relation on
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}))
is
\subseteq
restricted
to Galois closed subsets of
A^{-}
, so
\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}))^{\mathord{\uparrow}}\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R))^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(Y)
is just the upset in
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
generated by
R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)
.
Finally,
\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}
is membership, so

a\mathrel{\epsilon_{\mathcal{A}}\fatsemi\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R))}Y

if and only if
a\in R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(Y)
.

For a complete lattice
L
,
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathord{\textsf{C}}(L))
consists precisely of
the principle downsets of
L
. Since
L
is a complete lattice,
x\mapsto{\mathord{\downarrow}}x
is an isomorphism.
Naturality follows
from the simple calculation that for an
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
morphism

{h}\colon{M}\to{L}
,
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathord{\textsf{C}}(h))
sends a principle downset
{\mathord{\downarrow}}x

to the principle downset
{\mathord{\downarrow}}h(x)
.
∎

4
Complete Lattices

The duality between categories
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
and
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}

is actually a
2
-category fact because it preserves order on morphisms,
and of course
\fatsemi
-composition is monotonic on both sides.
This provides an easy way to locate the
morphisms in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
that correspond to complete lattice homomorphisms.
Namely, they will be precisely the “maps”, i.e., those morphisms
that possess a left adjoint. In addition, we find other, more direct characterizations.

Theorem 4.1
.

For a compatible relation
{R}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}
between polarities
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, define
R_{*}\subseteq B^{-}\times A^{+}

as
R_{*}=\lceil{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\rceil
. Then the following are equivalent.

1.

\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R)
preserves arbitrary joins;

2.

there exists compatible
{S}\colon{\mathcal{B}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{A}}
so that
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq R\fatsemi S

and
S\fatsemi R\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}
;

3.

there exists compatible
{S}\colon{\mathcal{B}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{A}}
so that
R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
;

4.

R_{*}
is compatible and
R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.

5.

R_{*}
is compatible and
{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}=R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}
.

6.

R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.

Proof.

Equivalence of (1) and (2) follows from the fact that the map

\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R)
preserves
joins if and only if it has a left adjoint,
and that this adjoint must also preserve arbitrary meets. Hence it must be
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(S)
for some
compatible
S
. Conversely, if an
S
satisfies the condition in (2), then
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(S)

is the desired adjoint to
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(R)
.

[(6) implies (4)] This is immediate because
R
is assumed to be compatible.

[(4) implies (3)] Trivial.

[(3) implies (2)] From (3),
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}=R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
. So
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq R\fatsemi S
.
And likewise,

\displaystyle(S\fatsemi R)^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle=S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}.

[(2) implies (6)] By virtue of the Galois connection,
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq R\fatsemi S
if and only if
{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
,
which implies
{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}=S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.
Hence
R_{*}\subseteq S
. Also
S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.

For the other inclusions,

\displaystyle S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle=S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle\leq S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

\displaystyle\leq\lceil{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\rceil^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

so
S\subseteq R_{*}
and
S^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\leq{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}
.

Suppose (4). Then
{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}=R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}{\mathord{\uparrow}}
.
Suppose (5). Then
R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}R_{*}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}.

∎

Thus we have a duality for complete lattices.

Theorem 4.2
.

The subcategory of
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
consisting of polarities and compatible relations

{R}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}
for which
\lceil{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}\rceil^{\mathord{\downarrow}}={\mathcal{B}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}R^{\mathord{\uparrow}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}

is dually equivalent to the category
\mathord{\mathbf{CLat}}
.

5
RS-frames

There is a simple procedure for constructing certain
monomorphisms and epimorphisms in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
.
Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a polarity and

X\subseteq A^{-}
.
Define the
-
-restriction

{\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X}

as the polarity
(X,A^{+};\mathcal{A}\cap(X\times A^{+}))
.
Similarly, for
\Xi\subseteq A^{+}
,
define the
+
-restriction

{\mathcal{A}}\downharpoonright{\Xi}
analogously, or if the reader
prefers, as
{\mathcal{A}}\downharpoonright{\Xi}=({\mathcal{A}}^{\partial}\upharpoonright{\Xi})^{\partial}
.
One can check that

\displaystyle({\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X})^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{\mathord{\uparrow}}(X^{\prime})

for
X^{\prime}\subseteq X

\displaystyle({\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X})^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(\Xi)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{\mathord{\downarrow}}(\Xi)\cap X

for
\Xi\subseteq A^{+}

So the relation
{\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X}
is a monomorphic compatible relation from
the polarity
{\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X}
to
{\mathcal{A}}
. Similarly,
{\mathcal{A}}\downharpoonright\Xi

is an epimorphic compatible relation from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{A}}\downharpoonright\Xi
.

By the foregoing paragraph, every subset of
A^{-}
determines a sub-object, but the converse
fails: there are monomorphisms (indeed, isomorphisms) in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

for which the domain is not isomorphic to any

{\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X}
. Polarities

P(A)=(A,A,\neq)
provide examples of this.

Recall that in Gerhke’s RS-frames, the S
stands for
separating
, precisely in the sense of Lemma
2.14
.
The
R
stands for
reduced
. Suppose we have a separating polarity
\mathcal{A}
.
We can understand reduced polarities (in Gehrke’s terms, reduced frames) as follows.

Restriction of
A^{-}
to a subset
X
produces a monomorphism into
\mathcal{A}
that may actually be an isomorphism.
In that case, the inverse is given by the relation
\mathord{\textsf{rel}}(\mathcal{A})

as a morphism from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}\upharpoonright X
.

For a polarity
\mathcal{A}
and
X\subseteq A^{-}
, say that
{\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X}
is a
-
reduction
of
\mathcal{A}
if
{\mathcal{A}}\upharpoonright{X}
is isomorphic. By dualizing,
we also have a notion of
+
reduction.
Say that
\mathcal{A}
is
reduced
if it has no proper
-
or
+
reductions.
In effect,
we can not remove anything from
A^{-}
or
A^{+}
while staying in the isomorphism class of
\mathcal{A}
.

Gehrke’s RS-frames
[
6
]
are precisely the separating, reduced polarities.
The reader
may find it useful to chase the definition of “reduced” as it is found in the formal context literature
(essentially the same as Gehrke’s) to see that it is precisely the one given here.
The point is
that our account of reduction gives a natural motivation for the definition.

Importantly,
there are polarities that are not isomorphic to any RS-frame. Consider the polarity
Q=(\mathord{\mathbb{Q}},\mathord{\mathbb{Q}},\leq)
.
For any two order dense subsets of
\mathord{\mathbb{Q}}
, say
A
and
B
, the usual back and forth proof that all countable
dense linear orders without endpoints are isomorphic adapts to show that
(A,B,\leq)
is a proper
reduction of
Q
.

Without separation, there is no upper bound on the cardinality of polarities that are isomorphic to a given
\mathcal{A}
.
That is, there is no upper bound on the cardinality of polarities
\mathcal{B}
for which
\mathcal{A}
is
a reduction.
But if we focus attention on separating polarities,
then it is quite easy to see that

\mathord{\textsf{C}}(\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A}))
is the largest separating polarity isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
.

6
Completeness of
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}

Next, we turn our attention to the existence of limits and co-limits.
The spirit of the last section is to
demonstrate that a desired polarity may be constructed directly from the data we are given.
One would hope that the construction of limits and co-limits would follow a similar pattern. Of course,
we know that
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
is (co)complete. So a co-limit in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
can be found simply by sneaking over to
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}

(contravariantly), finding the corresponding limit there, and then sneaking back.
The result can be quite unwieldy, as the functorial image
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
produces a complete lattice of subsets of
A^{-}
. We offer a purely
polarity-theoretic

construction that does not use this indirect method.
The self-duality of
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
means that the
construction of limits is the same as the construction of co-limits, so we concentrate
on limits here. Indeed, products and co-products coincide in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
for very obvious reasons. They coincide in
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
, of course.
But that fact is not so obvious directly in
\mathord{\mathbf{INF}}
.

To describe polarity constructions, we will need standard notation for products and coproducts (disjoint unions) in sets.
As usual
\prod_{i}X_{i}
denotes a cartesian product where the set of indices is omitted.
For
\mathbf{x}\in\prod_{i}X_{i}
, and
k
an index,
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is the projection.
And
\sum_{i}X_{i}
is the disjoint union.
For index
k
, and
x\in X_{k}
,
{}_{k}x\in\sum_{i}X_{i}
is the insertion of
x
in the disjoint union.

For a family
\{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\}_{i}
of polarities,
define the product polarity
\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
by the following data.

\displaystyle(\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i})^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}A_{i+}

\displaystyle(\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i})^{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}A_{i-}

\displaystyle{}_{j}a(\prod_{i}\mathrel{A_{i}}){}_{k}\alpha

\displaystyle\iff j=k\to a\mathrel{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\alpha

Lemma 6.1
.

For any family of polarities
\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i}
,
the polarity
\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the product in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
.

Proof.

For index
k
, define
P_{k}
to be the corestricted from
\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}

to
\mathcal{A}_{k}
.
That is,
{}_{j}a\mathrel{P_{k}}\alpha
if and only if
j=k
implies
a\mathrel{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\alpha
.

Evidently,
P_{k}
is compatible with
\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
on the right and with
\mathcal{A}_{k}
on the left.
For a polarity
\mathcal{B}
and a family of compatible relations
\{{R_{i}}\colon{\mathcal{B}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\}_{i}
, define the relation
T
by
b\mathrel{T}{}_{i}\alpha
if and only if
b\mathrel{R_{i}}\alpha
.
It is routine to show that this is the unique “tupling” morphism from
\mathcal{B}
to
\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
for which
T\fatsemi P_{k}=R_{k}

for each index
k
.
∎

The coproduct is exactly the same construction. Insertions into the coproduct are duals of the projections.

Lemma 6.2
.

For compatible relations
{R,S}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}
, an equalizer is

\mathcal{A}\upharpoonright E
where
E=\{a\in A^{-}\,\mid\,R[a]=S[a]\}
.

We leave the verification to the reader.

The two preceding lemmas plus Lemma
2.11
, sum up to the following.

Theorem 6.3
.

The category
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
is complete and cocomplete.

7
Tensors and internal homs

Though
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
is not cartesian closed (cannot be because it is self dual and is not trivial), it is
*
-autonomous, and therefore has an internal hom functor.
Recall that one characterization of a
*
-autonomous category
\mathord{\mathbf{A}}
is that it is symmetric monoidal possessing a contravariant functor
∗
satisfying

\mathord{\mathbf{A}}(A\otimes B,C^{*})\simeq\mathord{\mathbf{A}}(A,(B\otimes C)^{*})

naturally.
In that situation, the internal hom is
A\multimap B=(A\otimes B^{*})^{*}
.

We have our candidate contravariant functor
\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{\partial}
.
A suitable tensor product in
\mathord{\mathbf{Pol}}
, once again defined directly from the data in the constituent polarities, can be defined by generalizing the notion of a standard polarity
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
that we encountered in Lemma
2.16
.

For polarities
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, say that
T\subseteq A^{-}\times B^{-}
is
stable
if it is compatible with
\mathcal{A}

on the left, and with
\mathcal{B}^{\partial}
on the right.
That is,
X\times Y\subseteq R
implies
\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{A}}(X)\times\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{B}}(Y)\subseteq R
.
Let
s(T)
denote the smallest stable subset containing
T
.

Lemma 7.1
.

For any polarities
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,
and sets
X\subseteq A^{-}
, and
Y\subseteq B^{-}
, the set
\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{A}}(X)\times\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{B}}(Y)
is itself stable, so it is the least stable set containing
X\times Y
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the fact that
\mathord{\textsf{cl}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is monotonic and idempotent.
∎

Since
s
is a closure on
A^{-}\times B^{-}
,
we can define
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
to be
the standard polarity
\mathsf{A}_{s}
.
Specifically,
(a,b)\mathrel{(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})}T
if and only if
(a,b)\in s(T)
.
Keep in mind that
s(T)
concretely is a compatible relation

{s(T)}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}^{\partial}}
.

To extend
\otimes
to a functor,
consider compatible relations
{Q}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{B}}

and
{S}\colon{\mathcal{C}}\looparrowright{\mathcal{D}}
.
Then
S^{\partial}
is compatible from
\mathcal{D}^{\partial}
to
\mathcal{C}^{\partial}
.
And by definition, for any
R\subseteq B^{-}\times D^{-}
,

s(R)
is compatible from
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{D}^{\partial}
.
Thus,
Q\fatsemi s(R)\fatsemi S
is a well-defined (compatible) relation from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{C}^{\partial}
.

Define
Q\otimes S\subseteq(A^{-}\times C^{-})\times\mathord{\mathcal{P}}(B^{-}\times D^{-})
to be the relation

Future work

Erné, in
[
2
]
,
investigates characterizations of polarities
\mathcal{A}

for which
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})
satisfies various
distributivity laws.
In nice cases, the result is a condition expressible
in first-order using the two-sorted vocabulary
of a single binary relation.

From the point of view that polarities are generalized
Kripke frames (not to be confused with the frames
of point-free topology), Erné’s work points toward
a sort of correspondence theory for polarities,
in which a property of the lattices
\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(\mathcal{A})

that involves quantifying over closed sets,
and in cases such as infinite distributivity,
over sets of sets of closed sets,
is actually a elementary property of polarities.

The development of such a correspondence theory
would begin with two things.
First, one would like a first-order correspondent
for frame homomorphisms, i.e., a sentence in the first-order
language of four sorts
A^{-}
,
A^{+}
,
B^{-}
,
B^{+}
,
and three binary relations
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{R}
,
and
\mathbf{B}
that encodes the “frame’ condition”
that the interpretation of
\mathbf{R}
is compatible
with the interpretations of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
Second, one would like to have generalize this, for example,
to quantales in such a way that various
(unary) modal operators also fit into the generalization.

In a subsequent paper, currently in progress, we develop
this general elementary correspondence theory for polarities.
For example, an elementarily definable category
of frame polarities and morphisms is equivalent via

\mathord{\textsf{G}}^{-}(-)
to the category
\mathord{\mathbf{Loc}}
of locales
(point-free topologies)
and localic (point-free continuous) maps.
This opens the possibility for investigating traditional
locale-theoretic topics such as separation,
the structure of the frame of sublocales, uniformity,
and so on, in first-order terms.

In other work being pursued in parallel to this paper,
we consider topological extensions of polarities
that capture (non-distributive) lattices,
as opposed to general complete lattices.

Another area that warrants more investigation is a connection
to Giovanni Sambin’s development of formal topology via
what he calls basic pairs
[
12
]
.
Putting aside foundational issues (for Sambin the word “set”
does not encompass what the reader likely means),
a Sambinian
basic pair
is precisely a polarity.
A morphism between basic pairs is a pair of functions,
satisfying slightly weaker conditions that in Erné’s
or Goldblatt’s work, but for which equality is defined
more liberally.
In effect (classically), basic pair morphisms are
equivalence classes of pairs of functions.
We have reasons to believe that these equivalence classes
correspond to compatible relations.
If that bears out, we will have a very satisfying bridge
between Erné, Goldblatt, and our approach.
