An asymptotic-preserving method for the three-temperature radiative transfer model
Ruo Li, Weiming Li, Shengtong Liang, Yuehan Shao, Min Tang, Yanli Wang

Abstract
We present an asymptotic-preserving (AP) numerical method for solving the three-temperature radiative transfer model, which holds significant importance in inertial confinement fusion. A carefully designed splitting method is developed that can provide a general framework of extending AP schemes for the gray radiative transport equation to the more complex three-temperature radiative transfer model. The proposed scheme captures two important limiting models: the three-temperature radiation diffusion equation (3TRDE) when opacity approaches infinity and the two-temperature limit when the ion-electron coupling coefficient goes to infinity. We have rigorously demonstrated the AP property and energy conservation characteristics of the proposed scheme and its efficiency has been validated through a series of benchmark tests in the numerical part.



1
Introduction

The radiative transfer equation holds significant importance in inertial confinement fusion, and accurately modeling thermal radiative transfer presents a formidable challenge. In many radiative transfer scenarios, the timescale required for electron and ion temperatures to approach thermal equilibrium exceeds the timescale needed to achieve quasi-neutral states, as noted by Enaux et al. in
[
6
]
. Consequently, it becomes necessary to employ a three-temperature system, wherein radiation, ion, and electron energies are considered separately. For example, in the study conducted by Shiva et al.
[
25
]
, a three-temperature radiation hydrodynamic model is employed to show the influence of electron thermal radiation on laser ablative shock waves propagating through ambient atmospheric air.

Numerical simulations of the three-temperature radiation transport model (3TRTM) pose substantial challenges, as highlighted by Evans et al. in
[
8
]
. These challenges arise primarily due to the following reasons: 1) The intrinsic nonlinearity of the problem: The radiation intensity exhibits a nonlinear dependence on electron temperature, and the governing equations for electrons and ions manifest nonlinearity in diffusion and source terms. 2) High dimensionality of radiation intensity: The radiation intensity is a function of seven independent variables. 3) Restrictive time step constraints: The speed of light imposes stringent limitations on the time step length during simulations, as discussed by Enaux et al. in
[
6
]
. 4) Multiscale nature of physical parameters: The physical parameters, including opacity and the ion-electron coupling coefficient, exhibit significant variability across several orders of magnitude within the computational domain of certain benchmark tests.

The 3TRTM has two important limits: the three-temperature radiation diffusion equation (3TRDE) emerges as opacity approaches infinity, and the two-temperature limit arises when the ion-electron coupling coefficient tends towards infinity. The 3TRDE depends solely on space and time, resulting in a reduction in the dimension of independent variables. Nevertheless, it inherits the same challenges owing to nonlinearity and the multiscale ion-electron coupling coefficient. In the two-temperature limit, where ion and electron temperatures coincide, the model shares a similar form as the gray radiation transport model (GRTM), which only considers radiation-electron interactions.

Both of these limiting models, while simpler than the 3TRTM, present formidable challenges and have gathered significant attention from researchers. The 3TRDE comprises three reaction-diffusion-type equations with nonlinear diffusion and stiff nonlinear reactions. Efforts are made to develop robust and efficient schemes capable of handling nonlinearity in arbitrary geometries. For example, a positivity-preserving and conservative scheme is proposed in
[
5
,
22
]
and the discretization on unstructured grids can be found in
[
26
,
27
]
. The pursuit of maximum principle-preserving schemes is discussed in
[
32
]
, while semi-analytic solutions for the 3TRDE are explored in
[
20
]
. To enhance computational efficiency, adaptation, and multigrid methods
[
14
,
21
]
, and certain iterative nonlinear solvers
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
29
]
have been proposed.

GRTM, being a high-dimensional system, faces similar challenges as the 3TRTM. Numerical methods for solving GRTM have been extensively studied and usually fall into two categories: stochastic and deterministic methods. The widely adopted stochastic method is the implicit Monte Carlo method (IMC), originally introduced for the two-temperature model by Fleck and Cummings (1971) and subsequently improved in
[
23
]
. It has also been extended to the three-temperature model in
[
8
,
24
]
. However, the IMC method is limited by the statistical noise, and its ability to handle multiscale parameters for GRTE and 3TRTM has not been thoroughly explored. An AP scheme means that the discrete numerical scheme of the microscopic model will converge to a discretization of the macroscopic model as the scale parameter approaches the limit. To handle multiscale parameters and the high speed of light, numerous AP schemes for GRTE have been proposed in the literature. For instance, in
[
18
]
, McClarren et al. designed a semi-implicit time integration method that explicitly treats the streaming term while handling the material coupling term implicitly with a linearized emission source. The unified gas kinetic scheme, as developed in
[
28
]
, extends the unified gas kinetic scheme for the linear radiative transport equation to the nonlinear GRTE by employing a linearized iterative solver for the material thermal temperature. An AP scheme based on a prediction-correction approach that allows for larger time steps and can capture the front position of the degenerate case is proposed in
[
30
]
. A high-order AP scheme for GRTE is detailed in
[
4
]
. However, the literature on efficient AP solvers for 3TRTM is much less.

In this work, we introduce an AP splitting method to address the complexities of the 3TRTM. A carefully designed splitting method is developed that can provide a general framework for extending AP schemes for the GRTE to the more complex 3TRTM. The main idea involves dividing the nonlinear interactions between radiation, electrons, and ions temperatures in a specific way, such that the resulting split systems can effectively capture the diffusion limit when opacity tends towards infinity and the two-temperature limits when the collision time coefficient approaches infinity. More precisely, the original 3TRTM is divided into two parts. The first part consists of a seven-dimensional equation governing radiation density flux, featuring a modified source term, along with two ordinary differential equations (ODEs) for electron and ion temperatures. The second part is composed of three equations governing radiation density, electron, and ion temperatures, with all three functions being macroscopic, depending solely on space and time. To relax the time step requirement, a fully implicit solver is employed for both parts, solving nonlinear systems through carefully designed alternating iterations. While the first system is characterized by high dimensionality and nonlinearity, it is almost the same as the GRTE and can be effectively addressed by extending the efficient AP solvers proposed in the existing literature for GRTE, as discussed in the preceding paragraph. In this paper, we extend the AP scheme introduced in
[
10
]
for GRTE to 3TRTM and apply it to the alternating iteration method, eliminating the need to solve a large nonlinear system. On the other hand, the structure of the second system shows similarity to the 3TRDE, making it both easier to solve and the various robust and efficient existing schemes
[
30
]
can be utilized to solve it. For simplicity, we employ a fully implicit solver. The nonlinear system obtained by implicit discretization is also solved using the alternating iteration method. We subsequently establish the AP properties in two distinct limits: the 3TRDE and GRTE, and validate the energy conservation of the numerical scheme.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
, the details of 3TRTM and the two asymptotic limits are discussed. The AP temporal splitting method is proposed in Sec.
3
. The nonlinear iterative solvers for both systems are discussed in Sec.
4
with particular emphasis on the first high-dimensional system. The spatial discretization together with a discussion of the AP properties is shown in Sec.
5
. To validate the performance of the scheme, numerical simulations are conducted in Sec.
6
, including tests of the AP properties and tests on the homogeneous model problem, the Marshak wave problem with and without conduction terms, and a two-dimensional Riemann problem. Finally, some conclusion remarks are made in Sec.
7
.

2
Three-temperature radiative transfer model

In this section, we will present the detailed formulation of 3TRTM and focus on the derivation of the two limiting models: 3TRDE and the two-temperature limit.

2.1
The 3TRTM

In 3TRTM, the temperatures for ions (
T_{i}
), electrons (
T_{e}
), and the radiation field (
\psi
) are coupled together to represent the evolution of the radiation field, electron energy, and ion energy density in a hot plasma. The detailed model after nondimensionalization is as follows
[
8
]
.

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Omega\cdot\nabla\psi=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sigma acT_{e}^{4}-\sigma\psi+\frac{Q_{r}}{|\Omega|},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T_{e}+c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\sigma\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega-\sigma acT_{e}^{4}+Q_{e},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T_{i}+c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i})+Q_{i}.

(2.1c)

Here
\psi(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega)
represents the radiation intensity, where
\Omega
denotes the angular variable located on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and
|\Omega|
is the surface area of
\Omega
, equaling
4\pi
. The dimensionless parameter
\epsilon
is analogous to the Knudsen number in the Boltzmann equation, representing the ratio of the mean free path to the characteristic length.
\sigma({\bm{x}},T_{e})
denotes the opacity, a material-specific coefficient.
c
stands for the speed of light. The radiation constant
a
is defined as

a=\frac{8\pi k^{4}}{15h^{3}c^{3}},

(2.2)

where
h
represents Planck’s constant, and
k
denotes the Boltzmann constant.

E_{e}(t,{\bm{x}})
and
T_{e}(t,{\bm{x}})
refer to the energy and temperature of the electrons, while
E_{i}(t,{\bm{x}})
and
T_{i}(t,{\bm{x}})
represent the energy and temperature of the ions. The relationship between material temperature
T_{s}(t,{\bm{x}})
and material energy density
E_{s}(t,{\bm{x}})
is

E_{s}=C_{v_{s}}T_{s}>0,\qquad s=e,i,

(2.3)

where
C_{v_{s}}>0
represents the heat capacity.
Q_{r},Q_{e}
, and
Q_{i}
are the source terms for radiation, electrons, and ions, respectively. The thermal diffusion coefficients
D_{s}({\bm{x}},T_{s})
in the conduction terms
\nabla\cdot D_{s}\nabla T_{s}
(where
s=e,i
) exhibit nonlinear dependence on temperature as

D_{s}=K_{s}T_{s}^{5/2},\qquad s=e,i,

(2.4)

where
K_{s}
represents the plasma-dependent coefficient
[
6
]
. The timescale of ion-electron coupling is characterized by the collision time coefficient
\kappa
, which can take various forms, as documented in the literature
[
3
,
8
,
26
]
. In this paper,
\kappa
is treated as a constant but can vary significantly over several orders of magnitude. The radiation energy
E_{r}
is defined as

E_{r}=\frac{1}{c}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(2.5)

and the radiation temperature
T_{r}
follows the relation

E_{r}=aT_{r}^{4},\qquad T_{r}\geqslant 0.

(2.6)

In Eq. (
2.1
), (
2.1a
) describes the evolution of radiation intensity, while (
2.1b
) and (
2.1c
) govern the evolution of electron and ion temperatures. These equations couple conduction and energy exchange among radiation, electrons, and ions. Integrating (
2.1a
) over
\Omega
and combining it with (
2.1b
) and (
2.1c
) yields the conservation of total energy, expressed as

\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{r}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{F}_{r}=\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T_{e}+\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T_{i}+Q_{r}/\epsilon^{2}+Q_{e}/\epsilon^{2}+Q_{i}/\epsilon^{2}.

(2.7)

Here,
\bm{F}_{r}(t,{\bm{x}})
represents the radiation flux

\bm{F}_{r}(t,{\bm{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Omega\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(2.8)

2.2
Asymptotic behavior

In the 3TRTM (
2.1
), two limiting models emerge as the dimensionless parameter
\epsilon
approaches zero or when the collision time coefficient
\kappa
tends to infinity. These limits provide valuable approximations to the original model and have been thoroughly investigated in
[
6
]
. For the sake of generality, we assume the source term
Q_{s}
(where
s=r,e,i
) to be zero. In this section, we will present a detailed discussion of these two limits.

Diffusion limit

As
\epsilon
approaches zero, the radiation intensity
\psi
becomes independent of the angular variable
\Omega
and can be described by the radiation temperature
T_{r}
. Let
T_{r}^{(0)},T_{e}^{(0)}
, and
T_{i}^{(0)}
represent the leading-order terms of
T_{r},T_{e}
, and
T_{i}
, respectively. This leads to an equilibrium diffusion limit, where the corresponding local temperature
T^{(0)}
satisfies a nonlinear diffusion equation, expressed as follows
[
9
]
.

\displaystyle T_{r}^{(0)}=T_{e}^{(0)}=T_{i}^{(0)}=T^{(0)},\qquad E_{r}(T^{(0)})=a\left(T^{(0)}\right)^{4},

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial E_{e}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{r}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt+\nabla\cdot\bm{F}(t,{\bm{x}})=\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T^{(0)}+\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T^{(0)}.\end{aligned}

(2.9b)

In this approximation, the radiative flux
\bm{F}(t,{\bm{x}})
is related to the temperature through Fick’s law of diffusion, expressed as

\bm{F}(t,{\bm{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Omega\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega=-\frac{ac}{3\sigma}\nabla(T^{(0)})^{4}.

(2.10)

Eq. (
2.9
) is referred to as the equilibrium diffusion limit
[
6
]
. This limit provides a simplified description of the behavior of the system when the ratio of the mean free path to the characteristic length
\epsilon
becomes very small.

Two temperature limit

When the collision time coefficient
\kappa
approaches infinity, the system converges to a two-temperature limit, resulting in a simplified two-temperature model. In this limit, the electron temperature
T_{e}
and the ion temperature
T_{i}
become equal, denoted as the material temperature
T
, and the radiation transfer equations (
2.1
) can be reduced to a 2T (two-temperature) radiation transfer equation by summing up (
2.1b
) and (
2.1c
). The resulting 2T radiation transfer equation is

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Omega\cdot\nabla\psi=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sigma acT^{4}-\sigma\psi,

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{T}\nabla T+\sigma\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega-\sigma acT^{4},

(2.11b)

\displaystyle C_{v}=C_{v_{e}}+C_{v_{i}},\qquad D_{T}=D_{e}+D_{i},

(2.11c)

where
C_{v}
(
D_{T}
) represents the heat capacity (thermal diffusion coefficient), considering both the heat capacity (diffusion coefficient) of electrons
C_{v_{e}}
(
D_{e}
) and that of ions
C_{v_{i}}
(
D_{i}
).

3
Asymptotic preserving time splitting scheme

In many physically meaningful benchmark tests,
\epsilon
can vary from very small values to
\mathcal{O}(1)
, and
\kappa
can span several orders of magnitude, ranging from
\mathcal{O}(1)
to very large values. As a result, the numerical scheme should maintain uniform accuracy for all these extreme cases. Therefore, it is important to design an efficient scheme that is AP in both the diffusion limit as
\epsilon
approaches zero and the 2T limit as
\kappa
tends towards infinity. Moreover, the preservation of total energy (
2.7
) is of most importance for some benchmark tests, especially when the regularity of the solution is low
[
6
]
. In this section, we propose an AP conservative time-splitting method.

In this method, the 3TRTM is split into two systems. The first part is close to the GRTE and can be effectively addressed by extending the existing efficient AP solvers. The second part is a macroscopic model, where the radiation density, electron temperature, and ion temperature depend solely on space and time.

3.1
The AP splitting scheme

The splitting scheme is proposed in the framework of micro-macro decomposition
[
12
,
13
]
. The radiation intensity is decomposed into a macroscopic density part that is independent of the angular variable
\Omega
and the remaining part:

\psi(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega)=\rho(t,{\bm{x}})+g(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega),

(3.1)

with

\rho(t,{\bm{x}})=\langle\psi(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega)\rangle,\qquad g(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega)=\psi(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega)-\rho(t,{\bm{x}}),\qquad\langle\cdot\rangle=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\cdot\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(3.2)

By integrating (
2.1a
) over the angular space, one can derive the equation for
\rho
that depends on
g
. Subtracting the equation for
\rho
from (
2.1a
) leads to the following micro-macro decomposition system:

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\epsilon\nabla\cdot\langle\Omega g\rangle=-\sigma\left(\rho-\frac{acT_{e}^{4}}{|\Omega|}\right),

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}+\epsilon\nabla\cdot[\Omega(\rho+g)-\langle\Omega g\rangle]=-\sigma g,

(3.3b)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T_{e}+c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\sigma(|\Omega|\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),

(3.3c)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T_{i}+c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i}).

(3.3d)

The computational time
[0,t_{\rm end}]
is divided into time intervals
[t^{n},t^{n+1}]
for
n=0,1,2,\cdots
. Utilizing this micro-macro decomposition, the AP time-splitting scheme is formulated as follows.

1.

With the approximated solution
(\rho^{n},g^{n},E_{e}^{n},E_{i}^{n})
at time
t^{n}
, we solve the first system

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\epsilon\nabla\cdot\langle\Omega g\rangle=-\frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\rho-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}acT_{e}^{4}\right),

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}+\epsilon\nabla\cdot[\Omega(\rho+g)-\langle\Omega g\rangle]=-\sigma g,

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\frac{\sigma}{2}(|\Omega|\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),

(3.4c)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i}),

(3.4d)

for one time step
\Delta t=t^{n+1}-t^{n}
and get
(\rho^{n+1,\ast},g^{n+1,\ast},E_{e}^{n+1,\ast},E_{i}^{n+1,\ast})
.

2.

Using
(\rho^{n+1,\ast},g^{n+1,\ast},E_{e}^{n+1,\ast},E_{i}^{n+1,\ast})
as the initial condition, we update the second system

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\rho-\frac{acT_{e}^{4}}{|\Omega|}\right),

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}=0,

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T_{e}+\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\frac{\sigma}{2}(|\Omega|\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),

(3.5c)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T_{i}+\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i}).

(3.5d)

for one time step
\Delta t=t^{n+1}-t^{n}
and get
(\rho^{n+1},g^{n+1},E_{e}^{n+1},E_{i}^{n+1})
.

In the following, we will demonstrate that this splitting approach effectively captures both the diffusion limit and the two-temperature limit while conserving the total energy.

Diffusion limit

It is straightforward to demonstrate that as
\epsilon
tends to zero in (
3.4
), the leading-order terms
T_{r}^{(0)},T_{e}^{(0)}
, and
T_{i}^{(0)}
, satisfy

T_{e}^{(0)}=T_{i}^{(0)}=T^{(0)},\quad E_{r}(T_{r})=\frac{1}{c}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Omega=\frac{|\Omega|}{c}\rho=aT_{e}^{4}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)=E_{r}(T^{(0)})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\quad g=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(3.6)

Thus, from the relation between
E_{r}
and
T_{r}
in (
2.6
), we have
T^{(0)}_{r}=T^{(0)}
. By adding up (
3.4a
), (
3.4c
), and (
3.4d
) together, the source terms on the right-hand sides of these three equations cancel each other, resulting in the following conservative equation:

\frac{E_{r}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{e}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\nabla\cdot\langle\Omega g\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(3.7)

From (
3.4b
), one gets

g=-\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}\nabla\cdot[\Omega(\rho+g)-\langle\Omega g\rangle]-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c\sigma}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}.

(3.8)

Multiplying both sides of (
3.8
) by
\Omega
and integrating with respect to angular variable, one obtains

-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\nabla\cdot\langle\Omega g\rangle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\nabla\cdot[\langle\Omega\Omega\rangle\rho]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(3.9)

Here, we have used the fact that
g(t,{\bm{x}},\Omega)
is of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. Therefore, (
3.7
) can be expressed as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{E_{r}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{e}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{ac}{3\sigma}\nabla(T^{(0)})^{4}\right).

(3.10)

Similarly, as
\epsilon\to 0
, the leading-order terms of (
3.5
) yield

T_{r}^{(0)}=T_{e}^{(0)}=T_{i}^{(0)}=T^{(0)},\qquad\frac{|\Omega|}{c}\rho=E_{r}(T^{(0)})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(3.11)

By adding up (
3.5a
), (
3.5c
), and (
3.5d
), the interaction terms cancel out, and the limiting model for (
3.5
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{E_{r}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{e}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T^{(0)}+\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T^{(0)}.

(3.12)

It is evident that the combination of (
3.10
) and (
3.12
) constitutes a time-splitting scheme for the diffusion limit equation (
2.9
).

Two temperature limit

As
\kappa
tends toward infinity, let
T
be the material temperature in the limiting case. and it holds that

T_{e}=T+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}),\quad T_{i}=T+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}).

(3.13)

We can derive the limiting equations for (
3.4
) by summing up (
3.4c
) and (
3.4d
). The resulting limiting equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\epsilon\nabla\cdot\langle\Omega g\rangle=-\frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\rho-\frac{acT^{4}}{|\Omega|}\right),

(3.14)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}+\epsilon\nabla\cdot[\Omega(\rho+g)-\langle\Omega g\rangle]=-\sigma g,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{T}}{\partial t}=\frac{\sigma}{2}\big(|\Omega|\rho-acT^{4}\big),

where
E_{T}=(C_{v_{e}}+C_{v_{i}})T
. For the second system (
3.5
), as
\kappa\to\infty
, one obtains (
3.13
). By summing up (
3.5c
) and (
3.5d
), the limiting system of (
3.5
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\frac{\sigma}{2}\left(\rho-\frac{acT^{4}}{|\Omega|}\right),

(3.15)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{T}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{T}\nabla T+\frac{\sigma}{2}(|\Omega|\rho-acT^{4}),

where
E_{T}=\big(C_{v_{e}}+C_{v_{i}}\big)T
and
D_{T}=D_{e}(T)+D_{i}(T)
. It is evident that the combination of equations (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) constitutes a time-splitting method for the two-temperature limit model (
2.11
).

3.2
Comparison with another splitting method

Compared to the original system, in the first system (
3.4
), spatial derivatives only exist in (
3.4a
) and (
3.4b
), while the equations for the electron and ion temperatures reduce into ODEs as in (
3.4c
) and (
3.4d
). On the other hand, the second part (
3.5
) is a macroscopic system. In some benchmark problems, the conduction terms
\nabla\cdot D_{s}\nabla T_{s},s=e,i
are set to be zero
[
24
]
and the second system (
3.5
) reduces to an ODEs system that can be easily solved. The specific choices of 1) dividing the term
\sigma\big(\rho-\frac{acT_{e}^{4}}{|\Omega|}\big)
in (
3.3a
) and (
3.3c
) into two
\frac{\sigma}{2}\big(\rho-\frac{acT_{e}^{4}}{|\Omega|}\big)
, and placing them in (
3.4a
), (
3.4c
), (
3.5a
), and (
3.5c
); 2) dividing the term
c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})
in (
3.3c
) and (
3.3d
) into two
\frac{c\kappa}{2}(T_{i}-T_{e})
, and placing them in (
3.4c
), (
3.4d
), (
3.5c
), and (
3.5d
); are crucial for achieving the AP properties.

A splitting scheme for the macroscopic diffusion model is proposed in
[
12
,
13
]
. Extending this approach, one can design a splitting scheme for the 3T model by a straightforward extension. We split the 3T model into a classical two-temperature radiative transfer system and a macroscopic system for the electron and ion temperatures, as shown below:

\displaystyle\text{Microscopic part:}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Omega\cdot\nabla\psi=-\sigma(\psi-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}acT_{e}^{4}),\\
&\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\sigma(|\Omega|\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),\\
&\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.16)

\displaystyle\text{Macroscopic part:}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=0,\\
&\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T_{e}+c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e}),\\
&\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T_{i}+c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i}).\end{aligned}\right.

(3.17)

Here (
3.16
) has the same form as the GRTE, while (
3.17
) is a macroscopic system that depends solely on
T_{e}
and
T_{i}
. However, as
\epsilon
approaches zero, the diffusion limit of the splitting system (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) becomes

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial E_{r}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{e}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\frac{ac}{3\sigma}\nabla\left(T^{(0)}\right)^{4},\\
&T_{e}=T_{r}=T^{(0)},\quad E_{r}(T^{(0)})=a\left(T^{(0)}\right)^{4},\end{aligned}\right.

(3.18)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial E_{e}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}(T^{(0)})}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot D_{e}\nabla T_{e}+\nabla\cdot D_{i}\nabla T_{i},\\
&T_{e}=T_{i}=T^{(0)}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.19)

This method is no longer a time-splitting approach for the diffusion limit equation (
2.9

4
Temporal and angular discretization for the subsystems

In this section, we introduce the temporal and angular discretization based on the splitting method in Sec.
3
. We consider the nonlinearity in the coefficients
\sigma
and
D_{s}
and employ a fully implicit solver for both systems to ensure accuracy through the AP properties discussed in the previous section. However, the computational cost of nonlinear iterations for a high-dimensional system is extremely high, and efficient iterative solvers must be designed. We adopt two alternating iteration methods, so that, under a larger time step, these iterative methods can still converge quickly.

4.1
Splitting of
P_{N}

We discretize the angular variable by
P_{N}

18

19

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\epsilon\mu\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}=-\sigma\psi+\frac{1}{2}\sigma acT_{e}^{4},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D_{e}\frac{\partial T_{e}}{\partial x}\right)+c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\sigma\int_{-1}^{1}\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu-\sigma acT_{e}^{4},

(4.1b)

(4.1c)

where

[

In the framework of the
P_{N}
method
[
19
]
, the radiative intensity

\psi(t,x,\mu)\approx\sum_{l=0}^{M}\frac{2l+1}{2}\psi_{l}(t,x)P_{l}(\mu),

(4.2)

where

represents the Legendre polynomial of degree

and

\int_{-1}^{1}P_{m}(\mu)P_{n}(\mu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{m,n},\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N},

(4.3)

the expansion coefficients

\psi_{l}(t,x)=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{l}(\mu)\psi(t,x,\mu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad l=0,\cdots,M.

(4.4)

Therefore, it holds that

\psi_{0}=2\rho.

(4.5)

By substituting (

P_{k+1}(\mu)=\frac{2k+1}{k+1}\mu P_{k}(\mu)-\frac{k}{k+1}P_{k-1}(\mu),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

(4.6)

the

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\epsilon\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial x}+2\sigma\rho=\sigma acT_{e}^{4},

(4.7a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\left(\frac{2}{3}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial x}\right)+\sigma\psi_{1}=0,

(4.7b)

(4.7c)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi_{M}}{\partial t}+\frac{\epsilon M}{2M+1}\frac{\partial\psi_{M-1}}{\partial x}+\sigma\psi_{M}=0.

(4.7d)

Comparing (

becomes a system for
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\cdots,\psi_{M})^{T}=(\psi_{1},\bar{{\bm{u}}}^{T})^{T}

{\bf A}=(A_{i,j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(M-1)\times(M+1)}
and
{\bf B}=(B_{i,j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(M-1)\times(M+1)}

A_{l-1,l}=\frac{l}{2l+1},\quad l=2,\cdots,M,\qquad B_{l-1,l+2}=\frac{l+1}{2l+1},\quad l=2,\cdots,M-1,

(4.8)

and all other entries are zero. Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\epsilon\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial x}=-\sigma(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial x}=-\sigma\psi_{1},

(4.9b)

(4.9c)

where
{\bm{u}}=(2\rho,\psi_{1},\bar{\bm{u}}^{T})^{T}

\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D_{e}\frac{\partial T_{e}}{\partial x}\right)+c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\sigma(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),

(4.10)

and the equation for the conservation of the total energy (
2.7

\frac{\partial E_{e}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial E_{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D_{e}\frac{\partial T_{e}}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D_{i}\frac{\partial T_{i}}{\partial x}\right).

(4.11)

Eqs. (

Utilizing the splitting method introduced in the previous section, the two parts of the splitting approach for this

First part (microscopic part):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\epsilon\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial x}=-\frac{\sigma}{2}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),\\
&\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial t}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial x}=-\sigma\psi_{1},\\

&\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{\partial T_{i}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i}),\end{aligned}\right.

(4.12a)

Second part (macroscopic part):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-\frac{\sigma}{2}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),\\
&\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{\partial T_{e}}{\partial t}=\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D_{e}\frac{\partial T_{e}}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})+\frac{\sigma}{2}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4}),\\

&\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial t}=0,\quad\frac{\partial\bar{\bm{u}}}{\partial t}=\bm{0},\quad\bm{0}=(0,0,\dots,0)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.12b)

4.2
Fully temporal discretization and the iterative solver

In the split
P_{N}
system, the nonlinear terms include the fourth power of temperature and the nonlinear conduction term. Handling systems where both the conduction term and the fourth power term are implicit presents challenges. Explicitly treating the conduction term during the iteration process would impose a time step requirement of
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{2})
. On the other hand, explicitly treating the fourth power term during the iteration process would result in a time step requirement dependent on
\epsilon
. While the fully implicit scheme is utilized for both systems, designing an efficient iterative solver is crucial to avoid solving a large nonlinear system, especially for the first system (
4.12a
).

To iteratively solve these systems, we design an alternating iterative method. It consists of two parts: one implicitly solves a linearized system, and the nonlinear part is considered explicitly. The other part implicitly solves the quartic equation at each space point, while the linearized system part is considered explicitly. The iterations of these two parts are performed alternately until certain convergence criteria are satisfied, and the numerical solution of the implicit system can be obtained. An AP solver for GRTE is developed in
[
10
,
11
]
, whose computational cost is comparable to an explicit scheme, while its accuracy and stability are independent of the multiscale parameters
\epsilon
. In the subsequent part, we propose an efficient iteration method for (
4.12a
) based on the AP solver for GRTE in
[
10
]
.

According to the order analysis in
[
10
]
, the orders of the expansion coefficients are given by

\psi_{l}=\mathcal{O}{(\epsilon^{l})},\qquad\frac{\partial\psi_{l}}{\partial x}=\mathcal{O}{(\epsilon^{l})}.

(4.13)

For the convenience of readers, we provide the details of order analysis in
A
. In Eq. (
4.12a
),
{\bf A}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial x}
has a low order of
\epsilon
and is treated implicitly, while the terms
{\bf B}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial x}
are treated explicitly since their orders are higher. The terms on the right-hand side are treated implicitly, while the coefficient
\sigma
, which may nonlinearly depend on
T_{e}
, is treated explicitly to simplify the iteration process.

We now present the details of the iterative method for the microscopic part, as displayed in Eqs. (
4.14
) and (
4.15
). Eq. (
4.14
) is a direct generalization of the IMEX method presented in
[
10
]
. The approach outlined in
[
10
]
requires solving a fourth-order polynomial equation for each spatial grid, offering computational efficiency akin to an explicit scheme while benefiting from a time step at the same order of the mesh size. The advection terms of Eq. (
4.15

4.14

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}-\rho^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k}=-\frac{\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k}}{2}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4})^{n+1,\ast,2k+1},

(4.14a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\psi_{1}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}-\psi_{1}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k}=-\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}\psi_{1}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1},

(4.14b)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\bar{\bm{u}}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}-\bar{\bm{u}}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon{\bf A}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}+\epsilon{\bf B}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k}=-\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}\bar{\bm{u}}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}

(4.14c)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}-T_{e}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}+\frac{\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k}}{2}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4})^{n+1,\ast,2k+1},

(4.14d)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}-T_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i})^{n+1,\ast,2k+1},

(4.14e)

(4.15a)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\psi_{1}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-\psi_{1}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}+\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}=-\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}\psi_{1}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}

(4.15b)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\bar{\bm{u}}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-\bar{\bm{u}}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon{\bf A}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}+\epsilon{\bf B}\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial x}\right)^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}=-\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}\bar{\bm{u}}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-T_{e}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}+\frac{\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}}{2}\bigg(2\rho^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-ac(T_{e}^{n+1,\ast,2k+1})^{3}T_{e}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}\bigg),

(4.15d)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-T_{i}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i})^{n+1,\ast,2k+2},

(4.15e)

where

When solving (
4.14

Here, the coefficients are given by

\begin{gathered}\mathscr{C}_{4}=\frac{\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k}ac\Delta t}{2\epsilon^{2}+\sigma^{n+1,\ast,2k}c\Delta t},\qquad\mathscr{C}_{1}=C_{v_{e}}+\frac{C_{v_{i}}c\kappa\Delta t}{2\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}+c\kappa\Delta t},\\

Spatial discretization

The spatial discretization for the model (

\displaystyle\frac{l+1}{2l+1}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{l+1}}{\partial x}\right)_{j}^{\rm old}\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\mathcal{F}_{l+1}^{\rm old}\left({\bm{u}}_{j+1/2}^{L,\rm old},{\bm{u}}_{j+1/2}^{R,\rm old}\right)-\mathcal{F}_{l+1}^{\rm old}\left({\bm{u}}_{j-1/2}^{L,\rm old},{\bm{u}}_{j-1/2}^{R,\rm old}\right)\right),\ l=0,1,\cdots,M-1,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{l}{2l+1}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{l-1}}{\partial x}\right)_{j}^{\rm new}\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\mathcal{F}_{l-1}^{\rm new}\left({\bm{u}}_{j+1/2}^{L,\rm new},{\bm{u}}_{j+1/2}^{R,\rm new}\right)-\mathcal{F}_{l-1}^{\rm new}\left({\bm{u}}_{j-1/2}^{L,\rm new},{\bm{u}}_{j-1/2}^{R,\rm new}\right)\right),\quad l=1,2,\cdots,M.

(5.1b)

where
()^{\rm new}
is
()^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}
and
()^{\rm old}
is
()^{n+1,\ast,2k}
for the numerical flux in (
4.14
), while
()^{\rm new}
is
()^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}
and
()^{\rm old}
is
()^{n+1,\ast,2k+1}
for the numerical flux in (
4.15
).
The exact forms of
\mathcal{F}_{l}^{\rm old}
and
\mathcal{F}_{l}^{\rm new}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{l+1}^{\rm old}({\bm{u}}^{L}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},{\bm{u}}^{R}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{l+1}{2l+1}\frac{1}{2}(\psi_{l+1,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}+\psi_{l+1,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{R})-\frac{\alpha_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}(\psi_{l,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}-\psi_{l,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}),\hskip 9.24994ptl=0,1,\dots,M-1,

(5.2a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{l-1}^{\rm new}({\bm{u}}^{L}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},{\bm{u}}^{R}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{l}{2l+1}\frac{1}{2}(\psi_{l-1,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}+\psi_{l-1,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}),\hskip 9.24994ptl=1,2,\dots,M-1,

(5.2b)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{F}_{M-1}^{\rm new}({\bm{u}}^{L}_{j+\frac{1}{2}},{\bm{u}}^{R}_{j+\frac{1}{2}})=\frac{M}{2M+1}\frac{1}{2}(\psi_{M-1,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}+\psi_{M-1,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{R})-\frac{\alpha_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}(\psi_{M,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}-\psi_{M,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}),\end{aligned}

(5.2c)

with

\alpha_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\alpha(\sigma,\epsilon)=\frac{c}{\epsilon^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma_{j}+\sigma_{j+1}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\right),

(5.3)

and
{\bm{u}}^{L}
and
{\bm{u}}^{R}
are given by reconstruction. The simplest way is

{\bm{u}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{L}={\bm{u}}_{j},\quad{\bm{u}}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}^{R}={\bm{u}}_{j+1}.

(5.4)

To improve the resolution of the solution, linear reconstruction or WENO reconstruction can be used.

(5.5)

5.2
AP property

To demonstrate the AP property, it is necessary to show that for a fixed mesh size and time step, the numerical scheme should automatically converge to a scheme for the diffusion limit (
2.9
) as the parameter
\epsilon
approaches zero. Simultaneously, it should also converge to a scheme for the two-temperature limit (
2.11
) as the parameter
\kappa
tends to infinity. In the subsequent part, we illustrate the AP property of the numerical scheme presented in Sec.
4.2
and Sec.
5.1
.

AP property to the diffusion limit

As the parameter
\epsilon
approaches zero, the numerical scheme described in Sec.
5.1
converges to a consistent scheme for the nonlinear diffusion equation (
2.9
).

The fully discretized scheme for the
\rho,T_{e}
and
T_{i}
are

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho^{n+1,\ast}_{j}-\rho^{n}_{j}}{\Delta t}+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{1,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}=-\frac{\sigma^{n+1,\ast}_{j}}{2}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{e,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{i}-T_{e})^{n+1,\ast}_{j}+{\frac{\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{2}}(2\rho-acT_{e}^{4})^{n+1,\ast}_{j},

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{2}c\kappa(T_{e}-T_{i})^{n+1,\ast}_{j},

where

\mathcal{F}_{1,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast}+\psi_{1,j+1}^{n+1,\ast})-\alpha_{j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}\epsilon(\rho_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}).

(5.7)

Summing up the equations in (
5.6
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{e,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{1,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}.

(5.8)

The equation of
\psi_{1}
in (
4.12a
) is

\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast}-\psi_{1,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{0,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{2,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}=-\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast},

(5.9)

where

\mathcal{F}_{0,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}+\rho_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast}),\quad\mathcal{F}_{2,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\psi_{2,j}^{n+1,\ast}+\psi_{2,j+1}^{n+1,\ast})-\alpha_{j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}\frac{\epsilon}{2}(\psi_{1,j+1}^{n+1,\ast}-\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast}).

(5.10)

In the limit as
\epsilon
approaches zero, order analysis (as detailed in
A
) yields

\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\qquad\psi_{2,j}^{n+1,\ast}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(5.11)

With this, we can rewrite the source term in (
5.9
) as

-\sigma^{n+1,\ast}_{j}\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast}=\frac{2\epsilon}{3}\frac{\rho_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j-1}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(5.12)

Substituting (
5.12
) into (
5.7
), one obtains

\mathcal{F}^{n+1,\ast}_{1,j+1/2}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}\frac{\rho_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j-1}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}+\frac{2}{3\sigma_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast}}\frac{\rho_{j+2}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).

(5.13)

With equation (
5.13
), equation (
5.8
) is reduced to

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{e,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}

(5.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\Delta x}\left(\frac{2}{3\sigma_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast}}\frac{\rho_{j+2}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}-\frac{2}{3\sigma_{j-1}^{n+1,\ast}}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j-2}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).

As
\epsilon
tends to zero, the dominance of the sources in (
5.6
) leads to

T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast}=(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\qquad T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast}=(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\qquad 2\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}=ac((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(5.15)

By substituting (
5.15
) into (
5.14
), it holds that, as
\epsilon
approaches zero,

\displaystyle\frac{a((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-a((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{C_{v_{e}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n+1,\ast}-C_{v_{e}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{C_{v_{i}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n+1,\ast}-C_{v_{i}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n}}{\Delta t}

(5.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\left(\frac{ac}{3\sigma((T^{(0)})_{j+1}^{n+1,\ast})}\frac{((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j+2}^{n+1,\ast}-((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}-\frac{ac}{3\sigma((T^{(0)})_{j-1}^{n+1,\ast})}\frac{((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-((T^{(0)})^{4})_{j-2}^{n+1,\ast}}{2\Delta x}\right),

which is a numerical scheme for the nonlinear diffusion equation (
2.9
). We have completed the proof of the first part.

In the second part, as
\epsilon
tends to zero in (
4.12b
), it holds that

T_{e,j}^{n+1}=(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\qquad T_{i,j}^{n+1}=(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),\qquad 2\rho_{j}^{n+1}=(ac(T^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

(5.17)

Summing up the equations for
\rho
,
T_{e}
, and
T_{i}
in (
4.12b
) and employing (
5.5
), we can derive that

\displaystyle\frac{a((T_{j}^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1}-a((T_{j}^{(0)})^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta t}+\frac{C_{v_{e}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n+1}-C_{v_{e}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta t}+\frac{C_{v_{i}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n+1}-C_{v_{i}}(T^{(0)}_{j})^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta t}

(5.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{D_{e}((T^{(0)})_{j+1}^{n+1})+D_{e}((T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1})}{2}\frac{(T^{(0)})_{j+1}^{n+1}-(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}-\frac{D_{e}((T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1})+D_{e}((T^{(0)})_{j-1}^{n+1})}{2}\frac{(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1}-(T^{(0)})_{j-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{D_{i}((T^{(0)})_{j+1}^{n+1})+D_{i}((T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1})}{2}\frac{(T^{(0)})_{j+1}^{n+1}-(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}-\frac{D_{i}((T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1})+D_{i}((T^{(0)})_{j-1}^{n+1})}{2}\frac{(T^{(0)})_{j}^{n+1}-(T^{(0)})_{j-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}\right).

Considering (
5.16
), (
5.17
), and (
5.18
), we observe that the numerical scheme (
4.12
), with the spatial discretization (
5.1
) and (
5.5
), reduces to an implicit scheme of the diffusion limit (
2.9
). Furthermore, (
4.12a
) and (
4.12b
) converge to the numerical schemes corresponding to the 1D form of the split systems (
3.10
) and (
3.12
). This convergence completes the proof.

AP property to the two temperature limit

As the parameter
\kappa
tends to infinity, the numerical scheme proposed in Sec.
5.1
converges to a consistent numerical scheme for the two-temperature model given by (
2.11
).

As
\kappa
approaches infinity, it holds that

T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast}=T_{j}^{n+1,\ast}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}),\quad T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast}=T_{j}^{n+1,\ast}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}).

(5.19)

With
C_{v}=C_{v_{e}}+C_{v_{i}}
, by summing up the governing equation for
T_{e}
and
T_{i}
in (
4.12a
), we can derive

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-\rho_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{\mathcal{F}^{n+1,\ast}_{1,j+1/2}-\mathcal{F}^{n+1,\ast}_{1,j-1/2}}{\Delta x}=-\frac{\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{2}(2\rho-acT^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast},

(5.20)

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\psi_{1,j}^{n+1,\ast}-\psi_{1,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{0,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{2,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}=-\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}\psi_{1,j},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\bar{\bm{u}}_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-\bar{\bm{u}}_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\frac{{\bf F}^{n+1,\ast}_{1,j+1/2}-{\bf F}^{n+1,\ast}_{1,j-1/2}}{\Delta x}=-\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}\bar{\bm{u}}_{j}^{n+1,\ast},

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v}\frac{T_{j}^{n+1,\ast}-T_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{\sigma_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{2}(2\rho-acT^{4})_{j}^{n+1,\ast}.

Here,
\bf F
is a vector with
M-1
entries, and the
l

\psi_{l+1},l=1,\cdots,M-1
. When
\kappa
approach infinity in (
4.12b
), one obtains

T_{e,j}^{n+1}=T_{j}^{n+1}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}),\quad T_{i,j}^{n+1}=T_{j}^{n+1}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{-1}).

By summing up the governing equations for
T_{e}
and
T_{i}
in (
4.12b

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1}-\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta t}=-\frac{\sigma_{j}^{n+1}}{2}\left(2\rho-acT^{4}\right)_{j}^{n+1},

(5.22)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}C_{v}\frac{T_{j}^{n+1}-T_{j}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta t}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta x}\left(\frac{D_{T,j+1}^{n+1}+D_{T,j}^{n+1}}{2}\frac{T_{j+1}^{n+1}-T_{j}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{D_{T,j}^{n+1}+D_{T,j-1}^{n+1}}{2}\frac{T_{j}^{n+1}-T_{j-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{n+1}}{2}\left(2\rho-acT^{4}\right)_{j}^{n+1},

with
C_{v}=C_{v_{e}}+C_{v_{i}}
, and
D_{T}=D_{e}(T)+D_{i}(T)
. It is evident that (
5.20

5.3
Conservation of total energy

In this section, we will discuss the energy-conserving property of this numerical scheme. For the 3TRTE model (
4.1
), it conserves the total energy, as demonstrated by the theorem below.

Energy-conserving property

For the 3TRTE system (
4.1
), with the periodic boundary conditions in the spatial space and the absence of external source terms, the system conserves total energy. This conservation is precisely expressed by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}E_{\rm total}(t)=0,

(5.23)

where the total energy
E_{\rm total}(t)
is defined as

E_{\rm total}(t)=\int E_{e}(t,x)+E_{i}(t,x)+E_{r}(t,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(5.24)

The proof is straightforward by integrating (
4.11
) in the spatial domain, and we will omit it. For the numerical scheme (
4.12
), utilizing the spatial discretization methods presented in (
5.1
) and (
5.5
), it effectively conserves the discretized total energy. This conservation is expressed as follows.

Conservation of the discretized total energy

The numerical scheme (
4.12
), with the spatial discretization given by (
5.1
) and (
5.5
), conserves the total energy

\mathcal{E}_{\rm total}^{n+1}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm total}^{n}

(5.25)

for the 3TRTE system (
4.1
) with periodic boundary condition. The discretized total energy at time
t=t^{n}
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{\rm total}^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(E_{e,j}^{n}+E_{i,j}^{n}+E_{r,j}^{n}),

(5.26)

with
N
the total number of mesh points in the spatial domain.

To prove this, first, by summing up the governing equation for
\rho
,
T_{e}
, and
T_{i}
in (
4.12
), with the spatial discretization (
5.1
) and (
5.5
), it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1}-\rho_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e,j}^{n+1}-T_{e,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i,j}^{n+1}-T_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}

(5.27)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1,j+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{1,j-1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta x}\left(D^{n+1}_{e,j+1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{e,j+1}-T^{e,n+1}_{e,j}}{\Delta x}-D^{n+1}_{e,j-1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{e,j}-T^{e,n+1}_{e,j-1}}{\Delta x}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta x}\left(D^{n+1}_{i,j+1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{i,j+1}-T^{i,n+1}_{i,j}}{\Delta x}-D^{n+1}_{i,j+1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{i,j+1}-T^{i,n+1}_{i,j}}{\Delta x}\right),

with

D^{n+1}_{s,j+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(D^{n+1}_{s,j+1}+D^{n+1}_{s,j}\right),\qquad s=e,i.

(5.28)

Summing up over the spatial space, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1}-\rho_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e,j}^{n+1}-T_{e,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i,j}^{n+1}-T_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)

(5.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1,1/2}^{n+1,\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{1,N+1/2}^{n+1,\ast}}{\Delta x}

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta x}\left(D^{n+1}_{e,N+1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{e,N+1}-T^{n+1}_{e,N}}{\Delta x}-D^{n+1}_{e,1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{e,1}-T^{n+1}_{e,0}}{\Delta x}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta x}\left(D^{n+1}_{i,N+1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{i,N+1}-T^{n+1}_{i,N}}{\Delta x}-D^{n+1}_{i,1/2}\frac{T^{n+1}_{i,1}-T^{n+1}_{i,0}}{\Delta x}\right).

With the periodic boundary condition, we have

\mathcal{F}_{1,1/2}^{n+1,\ast}=\mathcal{F}_{1,N+1/2}^{n+1,\ast},\qquad D^{n+1}_{s,1/2}=D^{n+1}_{s,N+1/2},\qquad T^{n+1}_{s,0}=T^{n+1}_{s,N},\qquad T^{n+1}_{s,1}=T^{n+1}_{s,N+1},\qquad s=e,i.

(5.30)

Substituting (
5.30
) into (
5.29
) yields

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\rho_{j}^{n+1}-\rho_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{e}}\frac{T_{e,j}^{n+1}-T_{e,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+\epsilon^{2}C_{v_{i}}\frac{T_{i,j}^{n+1}-T_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}\right)=0.

(5.31)

Thus, we have completed the proof.

6
Numerical examples

In this section, the validation of the numerical method is conducted through a series of tests. First, the AP property of this splitting scheme is verified. Then, numerical examples including spatially homogeneous conditions, the 1D Marshak wave problem, and the 2D Riemann problem are investigated. In the first test, the periodic condition is applied, while the inflow boundary condition in
[
10
]
is applied for the last three tests.

6.1
Stopping criteria

We discuss the stopping criteria for each iteration to solve (
4.12
). The key stopping criterion is based on the error measure, denoted as
\mathcal{E}
, which ensures the accuracy of the solution. Two forms of this error,
\mathcal{E}^{n+1,\ast.2k+2}
and
\mathcal{E}^{n+1,2k+2}
, are adopted to monitor the convergence of the iterative process. The detailed form is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left\{\Delta x\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{M}\frac{2}{2l+1}\left(\psi_{l,j}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-\psi_{l,j}^{n+1,\ast,2k}\right)^{2}+2\left(2\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-2\rho_{j}^{n+1,\ast,2k}\right)^{2}\right.\right.

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\left(T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-T_{e,j}^{n+1,\ast,2k}\right)^{2}+\left(T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast,2k+2}-T_{i,j}^{n+1,\ast,2k}\right)^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2};

\mathcal{E}^{n+1,2k+2}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left\{\Delta x\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\left(T_{e,j}^{n+1,2k+2}-T_{e,j}^{n+1,2k}\right)^{2}+\left(T_{i,j}^{n+1,2k+2}-T_{i,j}^{n+1,2k}\right)^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}.

(6.1b)

The parameter
\bar{\epsilon}
is introduced as a stopping threshold for these errors. It is a problem-specific value and set to
10^{-10}
for both 1D and 2D problems in our simulation. The CFL (Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy) condition is utilized to determine the time step length for this splitting method, which is calculated as

\Delta t=C\Delta x/c,

(6.2)

where
\Delta x
is the spatial mesh size,
c
is the speed of light, and
C
is the CFL number.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced an AP numerical method for solving the three-temperature radiative transfer model. This method utilizes a splitting scheme to ensure the accuracy and stability of the solution in both the diffusion limit as
\epsilon
approaches zero and the two-temperature limit as
\kappa
tends to infinity. Additionally, the numerical scheme has been demonstrated to possess the AP property and conserve energy. We have presented various numerical examples, including homogeneous model problems, the 1D Marshak wave problem, and the 2D Riemann problems, to validate the AP property and the efficiency of the proposed numerical scheme. Future work may involve exploring more complex numerical tests and applications of this method in different physical contexts such as the frequency-dependent problems
[
33
]
. Furthermore, this splitting method can be generalized to provide an asymptotic-preserving coupling method of radiation and fluid, as well as radiation and plasma.

Appendix A
Order analysis

In this section, we present an order analysis of
\psi_{l}
, where
l=0,1,\cdots
. A similar analysis is available in
[
10
]
, and we included it here for the completeness of the algorithm. As stated in
[
10
]
, it is confirmed that for
\psi_{l}
, the following relationship holds:

\psi_{l}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{l}),\qquad l=0,1,\cdots.

(A.1)

To prove this, the Chapman-Enskog expansion is utilized, and the specific intensity
\psi
is expanded in power series of
\epsilon
as

\psi=\psi^{(0)}+\epsilon\psi^{(1)}+\epsilon^{2}\psi^{(2)}+\cdots.

(A.2)

Define

\psi_{l}^{(k)}=\int_{-1}^{1}P_{l}(\mu)\psi^{(k)}(t,x,\mu)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad l=0,1,\cdots.

(A.3)

Then, it holds that

\psi_{l}=\psi_{l}^{(0)}+\epsilon\psi_{l}^{(1)}+\epsilon^{2}\psi_{l}^{(2)}+\cdots.

(A.4)

We will show that for any fixed
k
,

\psi_{l}^{(k)}=0,\qquad\forall l>k.

(A.5)

First, if
k=0
, matching the order of
\mathcal{O}(1)
in (
4.7a
) we can obtain

\psi^{(0)}=acT_{e}^{4}.

(A.6)

Then, by the orthogonality of the spherical harmonic functions, it holds that

\psi_{l}^{(0)}=0,\qquad\forall l\geqslant 1.

(A.7)

Hence, for the case of
k=0
, (
A.5
) remains valid.
Subsequently, assuming that (
A.5
) holds for all
k
up to and including
n
, as stated

\psi_{l}^{(k)}=0,\qquad\forall k\leqslant n,\qquad l>k.

(A.8)

When considering the case where
k=n+1
, it is established that for
\psi_{l}
with
l>0
that

\sigma\psi_{l}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi_{l}}{\partial t}+\frac{\epsilon l}{2l+1}\frac{\partial\psi_{l-1}}{\partial x}+\frac{\epsilon(l+1)}{2l+1}\frac{\partial\psi_{l+1}}{\partial x}.

(A.9)

Matching the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+1})
yields

\sigma\psi_{l}^{(n+1)}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{c}\frac{\partial\psi_{l}^{(n-1)}}{\partial t}+\frac{\epsilon l}{2l+1}\frac{\partial\psi_{l-1}^{(n)}}{\partial x}+\frac{\epsilon(l+1)}{2l+1}\frac{\partial\psi_{l+1}^{(n)}}{\partial x}.

(A.10)

According to (
A.8
), it can be observed that
\psi_{l}^{(n+1)}=0
for values of
l
greater than
n+1
. With this established, we have successfully demonstrated (
A.5
), and consequently, (
A.1
) can be derived naturally.

Appendix B
WENO reconstruction

The 3-order WENO reconstruction
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle f^{L,1}=\frac{3}{2}f_{j}-\frac{1}{2}f_{j-1},\quad f^{L,2}=\frac{1}{2}f_{j}+\frac{1}{2}f_{j+1},

(B.1)

\displaystyle f^{R,1}=\frac{3}{2}f_{j}-\frac{1}{2}f_{j+1},\quad f^{R,2}=\frac{1}{2}f_{j}+\frac{1}{2}f_{j-1},

\displaystyle\omega_{L,1}=\frac{1/3}{\left[10^{-6}+(f_{j}-f_{j-1})^{2}\right]^{2}},\quad\omega_{L,2}=\frac{2/3}{\left[10^{-6}+(f_{j+1}-f_{j})^{2}\right]^{2}},

\displaystyle\omega_{R,1}=\frac{1/3}{\left[10^{-6}+(f_{j+1}-f_{j})^{2}\right]^{2}},\quad\omega_{R,2}=\frac{2/3}{\left[10^{-6}+(f_{j}-f_{j-1})^{2}\right]^{2}},

\displaystyle f_{j+1/2}^{L}=\frac{\omega_{L,1}f^{L,1}+\omega_{L,2}f^{L,2}}{\omega_{L,1}+\omega_{L,2}},\quad f_{j-1/2}^{R}=\frac{\omega_{R,1}f^{R,1}+\omega_{R,2}f^{R,2}}{\omega_{R,1}+\omega_{R,2}}.

Then, the numerical flux can be obtained from
f_{j+1/2}^{L}
and
f_{j-1/2}^{R}
.
