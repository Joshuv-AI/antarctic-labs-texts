Tree density estimation
László Györfi 1 , Aryeh Kontorovich 2 , Roi Weiss 3

Abstract

For a spanning tree T T defined on the vertex set { 1 , … , d } \{1,\dots,d\} , the tree density f T f_{T} is a product of bivariate conditional densities. An optimal spanning tree minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between f f and f T f_{T} . From i.i.d. data we identify an optimal tree T ∗ T^{*} and efficiently construct a tree density estimate f n f_{n} such that, without any regularity conditions on the density f f , one has lim n → ∞ ∫ | f n ​ ( 𝐱 ) − f T ∗ ​ ( 𝐱 ) | ​ 𝑑 𝐱 = 0 \lim_{n\to\infty}\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T^{*}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}=0 a.s. For Lipschitz f f with bounded support, 𝔼 { ∫ | f n ( 𝐱 ) − f T ∗ ( 𝐱 ) | d 𝐱 } = O ( n − 1 / 4 ) {\mathbb{E}}\left\{\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T^{*}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}\right\}=O\big(n^{-1/4}\big) , a dimension-free rate.



I
Introduction

A natural strategy for mitigating the curse of dimensionality in
estimating
probability distributions
is to employ
low-complexity family of approximation distributions.
For discrete distributions,

Chow and Liu 1968
suggested a family of
tree-based
approximations and gave an efficient
maximum-likelihood estimator
based on
Kruskal’s
optimal spanning tree
algorithm
[
14
]
.
We stress that this approach makes no

structural assumptions
about the sampling distribution, but rather constitutes a
modeling choice
.
Consequently, in this paradigm,
the goal is to approximate the optimal-tree
distribution
from the data, without any guarantees on how well the latter approximates the true sampling distribution.

Extensions
of the Chow-Liu approach
to continuous
distributions were studied by

Bach and Jordan 2003

and by

Liu et al. 2011

under various assumptions.
They contructed approximation tree densities, which are products of
bivariate conditional marginals.
A principal contribution of this paper
is to
introduce identification and estimation procedures
for which
strong
consistency
can be established
without
any
regularity conditions on the
underlying sampling density.
Our second contribution is to obtain risk rates
under mild assumptions.

By way of a motivating example,

Horváth et al. 2020

examine a model
of sensor networks, consisting of
d\gg 1
sensors.
The goal here is
to estimate an approximation to the
d
-dimensional density of the sensors’ measurements.
In the special case of
a linear sensor layout,
the joint distribution is naturally
modeled via
a Markov chain.
In this case,
the density is a product of bivariate conditional densities.
In general, we do not assume that the true density has this special form,
and instead seek
the best approximating product of bivariate conditional densities.
Thus,
out of the possible
\binom{d}{2}
bivariate conditional densities, an approximation of the true density depends only on
d-1
bivariate conditional densities.
Furthermore, any approximation has bounded complexity:
it is given by a spanning tree.

Formal setup.

Let
{{\mathbf{X}}}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional random vector with probability density
f({\mathbf{x}})
.
We denote the index set by

V=\{1,\dots,d\}
,
the densities of the one-
and two-dimensional marginals by

f_{i}(x_{i})
and

f_{i,j}(x_{i},x_{j})
,
respectively,
for

i,j\in V
,
i\neq j
.
A
spanning tree

T
on the vertex set
V

is an undirected graph

T=(V,E)
, where
|E|=d-1
;
we denote the set of all spanning trees
by
{\cal T}
.
Given the one- and two-dimensional marginals,
a

T\in{\cal T}

induces a joint density on

{\mathbf{X}}
as follows:

\displaystyle f_{T}({\mathbf{x}})=\prod\limits_{(i,j)\in T}\frac{f_{i,j}(x_{i},x_{j})}{f_{i}(x_{i})f_{j}(x_{j})}\prod_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}(x_{i}).

(1)

Any vertex
k\in V

may be arbitrarily designated as the
root
of
T
.
Once the root is fixed,
any

i\in V\setminus\left\{k\right\}

has a unique
parent

j(i)=j_{T}(i)\in V

(the reader is referred to

Cormen et al. 2009

for the relevant graph-theoretic background,
including spanning trees and Kruskal’s algorithm).
The parent notation enables expressing
(
1
)
more compactly:

\displaystyle f_{T}({\mathbf{x}})=\prod\limits_{i\neq k}f_{i|j(i)}(x_{i}\mid x_{j(i)})f_{k}(x_{k}),

(2)

where

k
is the designated root and
the conditional densities are given by

\displaystyle f_{i|j(i)}(x_{i}\mid x_{j(i)})=\frac{f_{i,j(i)}(x_{i},x_{j(i)})}{f_{j(i)}(x_{j(i)})}.

As in

Devroye and Györfi 1985
, we consider the
L_{1}
risk

\displaystyle\|f-f_{n}\|=\int|f({\mathbf{x}})-f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}};

(3)

this choice of metric is amply motivated therein.

This paper investigates
the problem
of finding
a spanning tree
T

on the
vertex set
V

and
establishing that
its induced probability density
f_{T}({\mathbf{x}})

approximates the
true
density
f({\mathbf{x}})

optimally or nearly so, under some criterion.
There are several
candidates for a goodness-of-fit measure,
perhaps the most natural of which
is the
L_{1}
metric:

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{L_{1}}=\left\{\bar{T}\in{\cal T}:\|f-f_{\bar{T}}\|=\min_{T\in{\cal T}}\|f-f_{T}\|\right\}

(
{\cal T}
being a finite set, the minimum is always achieved).
The shortcoming of the
L_{1}
criterion is
that minimizing it over
T\in{\cal T}
appears to
be a computationally hard problem,
with
no known efficient approximation algorithm.
Therefore, in line with the original Liu-Chow approach,
we adopt
the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
as our goodness-of-fit criterion.
The KL-divergence between
f({\mathbf{x}})
and
f_{T}({\mathbf{x}})
is defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T})=\int f({\mathbf{x}})\log\frac{f({\mathbf{x}})}{f_{T}({\mathbf{x}})}d{\mathbf{x}},

while the set of optimal spanning trees is

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{\operatorname{KL}}=\left\{T^{*}\in{\cal T}:\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T^{*}})=\min_{T}\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T})\right\}.

Our goal is to identify a spanning tree
T^{*}
belonging to the set
{\cal T}_{\operatorname{KL}}
and to estimate a best
tree density
f_{T^{*}}
from data.

To present the Chow-Liu approach, let
us assume, for the moment, that
the density
f
is known.
Consider the undirected complete graph defined on the set of vertices
V
.
The edges of the graph are weighted by the mutual information of the two-dimensional marginal probability distributions corresponding to the two vertices connected.
One can check that

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}H(X_{i})-\sum_{(i,j)\in T}I(X_{i},X_{j})-H({\mathbf{X}}),

(4)

where
I(X_{i},X_{j})
is the mutual information defined by

\displaystyle I(X_{i},X_{j})=\int\int f_{i,j}(x_{i},x_{j})\log\frac{f_{i,j}(x_{i},x_{j})}{f_{i}(x_{i})f_{j}(x_{j})}dx_{i}dx_{j},

(5)

and
H(\cdot)
denotes the differential entropy of a random variable or a
random vector

[
1
]
,
defined by

H({\mathbf{X}})=H(f)=\int f({\mathbf{x}})\log\frac{1}{f({\mathbf{x}})}d{\mathbf{x}}
.
Thus,
\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T})
is minimal when we take edges from the complete graph
along the spanning tree having the maximum weight, i.e.,

T^{*}\in{\cal T}_{\operatorname{KL}}
such that

\displaystyle T^{*}\in\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{T}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in T}I(X_{i},X_{j})\right).

An optimal spanning tree
T^{*}
can be obtained by applying Kruskal’s algorithm

[
14
]

to the
weighted
complete graph on
V

described above.
The latter is a simple
method for constructing an optimum spanning tree in an undirected weighted graph, with time complexity
O(d^{2}\log d)
.
To choose a tree of maximum total edge weight, we
first index the
d(d-1)/2
edges according to decreasing
weights
\{b_{i}\}
, so that
b_{i}\geq b_{j}
whenever
i<j
.
We then start by selecting
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
, and add
b_{3}
if
b_{3}
does not form
a cycle with
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
. We continue to consider
edges of successively higher indices, selecting an edge
whenever it does not form a cycle with the set previously
selected, and rejecting it otherwise.
In general

T^{*}
is not unique;
it is, however,
when all of the
I(X_{i},X_{j})
are distinct
for
i\neq j
.
Furthermore,
T^{*}
depends only on the ordering of the set of mutual informations,
\{I(X_{i},X_{j}),i\neq j\}
.

In the setting of this paper, the density

f
is not known. Instead,

n
independent copies of
{\mathbf{X}}

(the “data”)
are drawn:

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{n}=({\mathbf{X}}^{1},\dots,{\mathbf{X}}^{n}).

Tan et al. 2010
studied in detail the case when
{\mathbf{X}}
has a multivariate normal distribution.
In this case
I(X_{i},X_{j})=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho_{ij}^{2})
where
-1\leq\rho_{ij}\leq 1
is the correlation coefficient.
In their paper the density
f
is not arbitrary, it is a tree density
f_{T}
and an empirical identification algorithm of this tree
T
is introduced.
If the covariance matrix for the density
f=f_{T}
has full rank, then the identification error probability has exponential rate of convergence.
Section 8 in
Bach and Jordan 2003
is on stationary Gaussian time series, for which

\rho_{ij}=r_{|i-j|}
with some
r_{1},\dots,r_{d-1}
.
If
|r_{1}|>|r_{j}|
for all
j>1
, then the optimal spanning tree
T^{*}
is a chain consisting of the edges
(1,2),(2,3),\dots,(d-1,d)
.
Interestingly, the correlations
r_{j}
for
j>1
don’t matter.
For general density, this particular spanning tree
T^{*}
appears, when
X_{1},\dots,X_{d}
is a Markov chain.

Liu et al. 2011

considered a more general problem: namely,
the forest density estimate.
For identifying the best forest and for estimating the corresponding forest density, they
proposed a kernel-based approach.
If the bivariate and univariate densities are Hölder continuous and they are bounded away from zero (called
strong density assumption
), then
under the additional assumption that

I(X_{i},X_{j})
are finite and distinct for
i\neq j
,
the identification of the best
forest is consistent.
Note that the strong density assumption excludes
many densities of interest, including
Gaussian densities.
In addition,
Liu et al. 2011
give bounds on the rate of convergence of forest density estimate in terms of KL-divergence.
For example, in case of Lipschitz density
that satisfies the strong density condition, they show that
the excess KL-risk is of order

\displaystyle O\left(\ln n/n^{1/4}\right),

see Theorem 9 therein.

Paper overview and main results.

The main aim of this paper is to avoid the strong density assumption and the assumption that the
I(X_{i},X_{j})
,
i\neq j
, are distinct.
We introduce a tree estimate
T_{n}
and a corresponding tree-density estimate
f_{n}
such that without any regularity condition on the density
f
one has that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T_{n}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}=0

a.s. (Theorem
1
).
Furthermore, for Lipschitz continuous
f
with bounded support,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T_{n}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}\right\}=O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)

independently on the dimension
d
, (Theorem
2
).

For a best spanning tree
T^{*}
, we have an approximation error:

\displaystyle\|f-f_{T^{*}}\|.

Pinsker’s inequality implies an upper bound:

\displaystyle\|f-f_{T^{*}}\|^{2}/2\leq\operatorname{KL}(f_{T^{*}},f),

therefore

\displaystyle\|f-f_{T^{*}}\|\leq\sqrt{2\operatorname{KL}(f_{T^{*}},f)}.

Using the formula (
4
), we can estimate
\operatorname{KL}(f_{T^{*}},f)
. For the identification step, the term
\sum_{(i,j)\in T^{*}}I(X_{i},X_{j})
is already estimated, while we can estimate
\sum_{i=1}^{d}H(X_{i})
and
H({\mathbf{X}})
by Kozachenko-Leonenko algorithm.
If
\bar{T}
and
T^{*}
stand for
L_{1}
-optimal tree and for KL-optimal tree, respectively, then the previous argument implies a bound on the excess approximation error:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\|f-f_{T^{*}}\|-\|f-f_{\bar{T}}\|\leq\sqrt{2\operatorname{KL}(f_{T^{*}},f)}-\|f-f_{\bar{T}}\|\leq\sqrt{2\operatorname{KL}(f_{\bar{T}},f)}-\|f-f_{\bar{T}}\|.

(6)

For perfect approximation, we have
\|f-f_{\bar{T}}\|=0
, which yields
\operatorname{KL}(f_{\bar{T}},f)=0
, and
so (
6
) implies
\|f-f_{T^{*}}\|=0
, too.

It is important to characterize the distribution of
{\mathbf{X}}
, where

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T^{*}})=\min_{T}\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T})

is small. For example, if there is a permutation of the components of
{\mathbf{X}}
such that in this ordering the components form a first order Markov process, then
\operatorname{KL}(f_{T^{*}},f)=0
.
In general, if
\operatorname{KL}(f_{T^{*}},f)=0
, then without any regularity condition on the underlying density, our algorithm identifies such perfect spanning tree
T^{*}
.

An important application of this setup is the example of sensor network, where the sensors are geographically (arbitrary, squared lattice, hexagonal lattice, etc.) distributed.
We can assume that the mutual information of the neighboring sensors are dominating, and therefore one has to estimate only
c\cdot d
mutual information
I(X_{i},X_{j})
(instead of
\binom{d}{2}
), where
c\approx 4
.
In the Gaussian case mentioned above the correlation matrix has only
c\cdot d
non negligible elements.
In this sense the correlation matrix is sparse.

II
The identification of the best spanning tree
T^{*}

Using the data
{\cal D}_{n}
, we shall construct estimates
I_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})
. Based on these estimates, introduce the empirically
best spanning tree
T_{n}
:

\displaystyle T_{n}=\mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{T}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in T}I_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})\right).

(7)

In order to have universally consistent identification of a best tree, we need consistent mutual information estimates without any regularity
assumption on the underlying density.

Let
(X,Y)
be a random vector taking values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
probability density function
f_{XY}(x,y)
and with marginal densities

g_{X}(x)
,
g_{Y}(y)
.
The aim is to estimate the mutual information

\displaystyle I(X,Y)=\int\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}dxdy

such that the estimate is strongly consistent without any regularity
condition on the density
f_{X,Y}
.
Assume having the i.i.d. data
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
.
Several estimates of mutual information have been considered. Most of them are based on density estimates,
from which the consistency of differential entropies estimates is derived.

Györfi and van der Meulen 1987
considered histogram-based consistent estimators for differential entropy, which in turn can be used to estimate the mutual information.
The histogram based estimate of mutual information is defined as follows:
Let
{\cal P}_{n}
and
{\cal Q}_{n}
be finite or infinite partitions
of
{{\mathbb{R}}}
, and denote by
\mu_{n}
the empirical distribution of
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(A\times B)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}}_{\{X_{i}\in A,Y_{i}\in B\}},

Set

\displaystyle I_{n}(X,Y)=I_{n}(\mu_{n},\mu_{n,1}\times\mu_{n,2})=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{P}_{n},B\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\mu_{n}(A\times B)\log\frac{\mu_{n}(A\times B)}{\mu_{n,1}(A)\mu_{n,2}(B)},

(8)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{n,1}(A)=\mu_{n}(A\times{\mathbb{R}})

and

\displaystyle\mu_{n,2}(B)=\mu_{n}({\mathbb{R}}\times B).

Barron et al. 1992
showed the following: if
{\cal P}_{n}={\cal Q}_{n}
is the uniform partition with bin width
h^{\prime}_{n}\to 0
,
nh^{\prime 2}_{n}\to\infty
and
I(X,Y)<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{n}(X,Y)=I(X,Y)

(9)

a.s.

Wang et al. 2005
and

Silva and Narayanan 2010

introduced and studied data-driven, partitioning-based estimate of the mutual
information.

Let us now return to
the problem of identifying
T^{*}\in{\cal T}_{\operatorname{KL}}
.
Construct estimates
I_{n}(X_{i},X_{j})
as in (
8
) and select
T_{n}
according
to (
7
).
For identifying
T^{*}
, one has to generate
\binom{d}{2}
mutual information estimate, therefore we have to use a mutual information estimate of small computational complexity.
The estimate (
8
) has the smallest computational complexity among the algorithms mentioned before.
The error of the tree density estimate has two components: error of the identification and the error of the bivariate density estimates. It will turn out that the second error dominates the first one.

III
Estimating the best tree density

In this section we study the estimation problem of a best approximating density
f_{T^{*}}
.
The aim is to introduce a density estimate
f_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\int|f_{T^{*}}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}=0

a.s. without any regularity conditions on the density
f
of
{\mathbf{X}}
.

One may estimate the original density
f
, for example by the ordinary histogram rule with bin width
h_{n}>0
,
for which the consistency conditions are
nh_{n}^{d}\to\infty
and
h_{n}\to 0
.
In applications where
d
is large,
we typically do not have a sufficiently large sample, i.e.,
nh_{n}^{d}
is not sufficiently large.
This is the main motivation why the estimation of the best approximating density
f_{T^{*}}
is considered.

For a spanning tree
T_{n}
, we construct the density estimate
f_{n}
by estimating the conditional densities
f_{i\mid j(i)}(x_{i}\mid x_{j(i)})
.
As in

Györfi and Kohler 2007
, we estimate the conditional densities by the ratio of histograms.
For
1\leq k\leq n

denote the
k
th sample vector
by
{\mathbf{X}}_{k}=(X^{(k)}_{1},\dots,X^{(k)}_{d})

and
let
\mu_{n,i,j(i)}
and
\mu_{n,j(i)}
be the empirical distributions for the samples
((X^{(1)}_{i},X^{(1)}_{j(i)}),\dots,(X^{(n)}_{i},X^{(n)}_{j(i)}))

and
(X^{(1)}_{j(i)},\dots,X^{(n)}_{j(i)})
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mu_{n,i,j(i)}(A\times B)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}}_{\{X^{(k)}_{i}\in A,X^{(k)}_{j(i)}\in B\}}

and

\displaystyle\mu_{n,j(i)}(B)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{I}}_{\{X^{(k)}_{j(i)}\in B\}}.

To simplify the analysis,
we renumber the vertex set
V=\{1,\dots,d\}
such that for any
1\leq i<d
, the vertex subset
\{i,\dots,d\}
corresponds to a subtree of
T_{n}
with
i
being a leaf
and its parent satisfies
j(i)>i
.
In particular,
d
is the root of the tree and the vertices are ordered by their distance from the root.
Let
{\cal P}_{n}
denote uniform partitions of
{\mathbb{R}}
with bin width
h_{n}
.
For
x_{i}\in A\in{\cal P}_{n}
,
x_{j(i)}\in B\in{\cal P}_{n}
and
i=1,\dots,d-1
,
put

\displaystyle f_{n,i\mid j(i)}(x_{i}\mid x_{j(i)})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{n,i,j(i)}(A\times B)}{h_{n}\mu_{n,j(i)}(B)}

with
0/0=0
by definition and for
x_{d}\in A\in{\cal P}_{n}
,

\displaystyle f_{n,d}(x_{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{n,{d}}(A)}{h_{n}},

where
d
is the root of the spanning tree
T_{n}
.
Set

\displaystyle f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i<d}f_{n,i\mid j(i)}(x_{i}\mid x_{j(i)})f_{n,d}(x_{d}).

(10)

IV
Consistency and rate of convergence

Our first result is the density-free strong consistency:

Theorem 1
.

Assume that all
I(X_{i},X_{j})
are finite for
i\neq j
.
If
h^{\prime}_{n}\to 0
,
nh^{\prime 2}_{n}\to\infty
,
h_{n}\to 0
and
nh_{n}^{2}/\log n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{I}}_{T_{n}\in{\cal T}_{\operatorname{KL}}}=1

(11)

a.s., i.e.,
almost surely,

\displaystyle T_{n}\in{\cal T}_{\operatorname{KL}}

for all sufficiently large sample size
n
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T_{n}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}=0

a.s.

Notice that the dimension
d
does not appear in any of the conditions above, which allows for a dimension-free consistency.

The consistency result of Theorem
1
holds without any regularity conditions on the density
f
.
Without such conditions, the rate at which the
L_{1}
error converges to zero can be arbitrarily slow

[
7
]
.
So in order to obtain non-trivial rates of convergence, one needs
to impose some regularity condition on
f
.

We say that a function
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the
Lipschitz condition

with respect to the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|

if
for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,

|g({\mathbf{x}})-g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})|\leq L\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|.

Theorem 2
.

If
{\mathbf{X}}
is bounded,
the one-dimensional marginals
\{f_{i}\}
and the two-dimensional marginals
\{f_{i,j}\}

satisfy the Lipschitz condition, and
nh^{\prime 2}_{n}\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T_{n}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}\right\}=O\left(1/\sqrt{nh_{n}^{2}}\right)+O(h_{n})+O({h^{\prime}}^{\gamma}_{n})+O\left(\frac{1}{n{h^{\prime}}_{n}^{2}}\right)

(12)

with all
\gamma>0
.

Remark 1
.

The rate of convergence in the theorem is dimension-free, which means that it does not depend on
d
.
Note that the constants hidden in the
O
-notation of the last two terms in (
12
) depend on the density
f
beyond the Lipschitz constant (i.e., on
\delta>0
of (
13
) —
the minimum gap in the distinct mutual information values of different
X_{i},X_{j}
).
Thus, the bound is not in the minimax sense.

For the choice

\displaystyle h_{n}=c_{1}\cdot n^{-1/4}

and

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{n}=c_{2}\cdot n^{-1/4}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\gamma=2,

nh^{\prime 2}_{n}\to\infty
and so (
12
) has the form

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\{\int|f_{n}({\mathbf{x}})-f_{T_{n}}({\mathbf{x}})|d{\mathbf{x}}\right\}=O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)+O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)=O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)

such that the density estimation error
O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)
dominates the identification error
O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
.
The upper bound
O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)
on the rate of convergence cannot be improved.
For
d=2
, where there is no tree identification problem, this rate is a minimax lower bound for Lipschitz class and the ordinary histogram achieves this rate, see Lemma
3
below.
A simple embedding argument entails that this also holds for
d>2
when
\operatorname{KL}(f,f_{T^{*}})=0
.

Remark 2
.

Recall that
Liu et al. 2011
give bounds in a similar setup in terms of the KL-divergence.
In the case of Lipschitz density that satisfies the strong density condition, they show that
the excess KL-risk is of order
O\left(\ln n/n^{1/4}\right)
.
In Theorem
2
above we consider the
L^{1}
loss. The strong density condition is avoided due to two technical ingredients: (a) the analysis of the approximation error
J_{n,1}
in the proof of Proposition
3
and (b) the inclusion of the marginal density term in the loss of the conditional density estimation (i.e., (
22
)).

V
Proof of Theorem

Lemma 2
.

If
X,Y
are bounded
and the density
f_{X,Y}
satisfies the Lipschitz condition, then

\displaystyle I(X,Y)^{2}\leq\int\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\left(\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\right)^{2}dxdy<\infty.

(21)

Proof.

By Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\int\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\left(\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\right)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle\geq\left(\int\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}dxdy\right)^{2}=I(X,Y)^{2}.

To show the second inequality in (
21
),
let
L>0
be the Lipschitz constant of
f_{X,Y}
and note that
for any
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,

\displaystyle g_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{X,Y}(x,y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}\geq\int_{y-\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{L}}^{y+\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{L}}f_{X,Y}(x,y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}\geq\int_{-\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{L}}^{\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{L}}(f_{X,Y}(x,y)-L|y^{\prime}|)dy^{\prime}=\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)^{2}}{L}.

Similarly,
g_{Y}(y)\geq\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)^{2}}{L}

We write

\displaystyle\int\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\left(\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\right)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle=\int\int g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\left(\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\right)^{2}dxdy

and split the integral’s domain into those

\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}<1
and those for which
\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\geq 1
.
Since

is bounded for

\log^{2}t
is monotonic increasing for
t\geq 1
,
we use the bounds
g_{X}(x)\geq\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)^{2}}{L}
and

above to get

\displaystyle\int\int_{\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\geq 1}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\left(\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\right)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\int\int_{\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{g_{X}(x)g_{Y}(y)}\geq 1}f_{X,Y}(x,y)\left(\log\frac{L^{2}}{f_{X,Y}(x,y)^{3}}\right)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\int\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\left(\log\frac{L^{2}}{f_{X,Y}(x,y)^{3}}\right)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle=9L^{2/3}\int\int\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{L^{2/3}}\left(\log\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{L^{2/3}}\right)^{2}dxdy.

Since
f_{X,Y}
is Lipschitz in a bounded domain, it is bounded, and since

is bounded when
t\geq 0
is bounded, the last integral is bounded as well.

Lemma 4
.

Let
g,h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be nonnegative
with

\tilde{g}=\int g(x)dx<\infty

and
\tilde{h}=\int h(x)dx<\infty
.
Then,

\displaystyle\int\left|\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{g}}-\frac{h(x)}{\tilde{h}}\right|dx\leq 2\int\frac{\left|g(x)-h(x)\right|}{\max\{\tilde{g},\tilde{h}\}}dx.

Proof.

This was proven for the
\ell_{2}

norm in
Lee et al. 2014
,
but the argument works for any norm.
We have

\displaystyle\int\left|\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{g}}-\frac{h(x)}{\tilde{h}}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\int\left|\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{g}}-\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{h}}\right|dx+\int\left|\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{h}}-\frac{h(x)}{\tilde{h}}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\tilde{g}}-\frac{1}{\tilde{h}}\right|\tilde{g}+\frac{1}{\tilde{h}}\int\left|g(x)-h(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\tilde{h}-\tilde{g}\right|}{\tilde{h}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{h}}\int\left|g(x)-h(x)\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\tilde{h}}\int\left|g(x)-h(x)\right|dx.

By symmetry,

\displaystyle\int\left|\frac{g(x)}{\tilde{g}}-\frac{h(x)}{\tilde{h}}\right|dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\tilde{g}}\int\left|g(x)-h(x)\right|dx

as well. The claim now follows.

\Box
