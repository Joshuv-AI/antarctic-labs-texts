The boundary of bordified Outer space
Karen Vogtmann

Abstract
. Bux, Smillie and the author defined a deformation retract 𝒥 n \mathcal{J}_{n} of Outer space which is invariant under the action of Out ⁡ ( F n ) {\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n}) and has compact quotient; they then showed that 𝒥 n \mathcal{J}_{n} is homeomorphic to the Bestvina-Feighn bordification of Outer space. We prove that the boundary of 𝒥 n \mathcal{J}_{n} has a covering by contractible pieces with contractible intersections, and we identify the nerve of this cover. This strengthens the analogy between the Bestvina-Feighn bordification and both the classical Borel-Serre bordification of symmetric space and the Harvey bordification of Teichmüller space.



1.
Introduction

In
[
10
]
M. Culler and the author introduced a space on which the group
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
acts as symmetries, which quickly became known as Outer space. Outer space was modeled both on the Teichmüller space of a closed surface
S_{g}
, with its action of the mapping class group
{\mathrm{Mod}}(S_{g})
and on the symmetric space of positive definite quadratic forms, with its action of the special linear group
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
. In both of these more classical settings the space is contractible and the action has finite point stabilizers, allowing one to conclude that the rational cohomology of the quotient by the action is an invariant of the group. The paper
[
10
]
proves that Outer space also has these features, i.e. it is contractible and the action of
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
is proper.

The groups
{\mathrm{Mod}}(S_{g}),

{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
all contain torsion-free subgroups of finite index. Since stabilizers of the actions on Teichmüller space, symmetric space and Outer space are finite, these subgroups act freely and the integral cohomology of the quotient is an invariant of the subgroup. This obviously vanishes above the dimension of the space, but it turns out that it has to vanish in smaller dimensions as well. The
cohomological dimension
of a group captures this; it is defined as the largest dimension in which the group has non-vanishing cohomology with some coefficients. It is not hard to show that all finite-index torsion-free subgroups of a group have the same cohomological dimension, which is called the
virtual cohomological dimension
(
vcd
) of the group.

In order to determine the virtual cohomological dimension of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
(and many other arithmetic groups) Borel and Serre proved that
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
is a
virtual duality group
. This means that there is an
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
-module
D
and an integer
d
such that, for any torsion-free finite-index subgroup
\Gamma<{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
and any
\Gamma
-module
M
, there is a natural isomorphism

H^{k}(\Gamma;M)\cong H_{d-k}(\Gamma;M\otimes D).

One immediately concludes that
d
is equal to the cohomological dimension of
\Gamma
, so is the
vcd
of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
.
By work of Bieri and Eckmann
[
3
]
, the group
H^{*}({\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}});{\mathbb{Z}}[{\mathrm{GL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})])
serves as a dualizing module if it vanishes in all dimensions except
d
, where it is free abelian. This group is easily identified with the cohomology with compact supports of any contractible cell complex with a proper cocompact
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
action (see
[
6
]
, Proposition 11.3). Although the action of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
on the symmetric space
X_{n}
is proper, it is not cocompact. Borel and Serre defined a partial compactification
\widehat{X}_{n}
of
X_{n}
called the
Borel-Serre bordification
. They showed that the bordification is a contractible manifold with boundary and the action on
X_{n}
extends to a proper cocompact action on
\widehat{X}_{n}
. They then used Poincaré-Lefschetz duality to identify the cohomology with compact supports of
\widehat{X}_{n}
with the reduced homology of the boundary
\partial\widehat{X}_{n}=\widehat{X}_{n}\setminus X_{n}
, and showed that this boundary is homotopy equivalent to the associated spherical Tits building, which has the homotopy type of a bouquet of spheres.

The action of the mapping class group
Mod(S_{g})
on Teichmüller space is also proper but not cocompact, and Harer was able to use the same strategy for
Mod(S_{g})
as Borel and Serre had used for
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
, using a bordification of Teichmüller space defined by Harvey
[
13
]
and using Poincaré-Lefschetz duality to identify its cohomology with compact supports with the reduced homology of its boundary. In this case the boundary is homotopy equivalent to the curve complex of the surface, which Harer proved has the homotopy type of a bouquet of spheres
[
12
]
.

For
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
the tables were turned: the
vcd
of
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
was known from
[
10
]
to be equal to
2n-3
but it was not known whether
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
was a virtual duality group, which is a much stronger property. Like the actions of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
on its symmetric space and the action of
Mod(S_{g})
on Teichmüller space, the action of
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
is proper but not cocompact. In
[
2
]
Bestvina and Feighn defined a partial compactification
\widehat{CV}_{n}
of Outer space
CV_{n},
analogous to the Borel-Serre bordification, on which
Out(F_{n})
acts both properly and cocompactly. The problem thus became to determine the cohomology with compact supports of
\widehat{CV}_{n}
. Unlike symmetric spaces and Teichmüller space
CV_{n}
is not a manifold, so Poincaré-Leftschetz duality is not available, and instead of studying the boundary they found a different argument that took advantage of the fact that the
vcd
was already known. What they proved is that
\widehat{CV}_{n}
is
(2n-5)
-connected at infinity; they then applied results of Geogheghan and Mihalik to conclude that
H^{*}_{c}(\widehat{CV}_{n})
vanishes in all dimensions other than
2n-3
, where it is free abelian.

In
[
11
]
Grayson constructed an invariant deformation retract
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
of the symmetric space
X_{n}
; this is a manifold with boundary and the action of
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
on
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is cocompact. The boundary
\partial X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is covered by contractible sets with contractible intersections, so
\partial X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is homotopy equivalent to the nerve of this cover. This nerve is precisely the Tits building, so
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
serves the same purpose as the Borel-Serre bordification in the proof that
{\mathrm{SL}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
is a virtual duality group (it is widely assumed that
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
is in fact homeomorphic to the Borel-Serre bordification, but I could not locate a published proof). The retract
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
avoids many of the technical difficulties that Borel and Serre encountered in adding points to the space
X_{n}
and extending the action, and is generally easier to work with than the Borel-Serre construction.

Inspired by Grayson’s work, Bux, Smillie and the author constructed an invariant deformation retract
\mathcal{J}_{n}
of
CV_{n}
in
[
8
]
, showed that it is homeomorphic to the Bestvina-Feighn bordification
\widehat{CV}_{n}
of Outer space and used it to give a simpler proof that
\widehat{CV}_{n}
is
(2n-5)
-connected at infinity.

In the current paper we look more closely at the boundary
\partial\mathcal{J}_{n}
. We show that
\partial\mathcal{J}_{n}
is covered by contractible subcomplexes with contractible intersections (Proposition 2.10), so is homotopy equivalent to the nerve of the cover, extending the analogy with
X_{n}^{\varepsilon}
. We describe this nerve in terms of the combinatorial structure of
CV_{n}
(Theorem 2.11), and at the end of the paper we ask whether the analogy with symmetric spaces and Teichmüller spaces goes even further, i.e. whether
\partial\mathcal{J}_{n}
is homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of spheres.

The paper is structured as follows. The space
\mathcal{J}_{n}
decomposes into cells called
jewels
, and in Section
2
we review the definition of a jewel and determine the structure of its facets. In section 3 we glue facets that are on the boundary of
\mathcal{J}_{n}
together to form walls and show these walls are contractible with contractible intersections. We do this using a model of Outer space introduced by Hatcher in
[
14
]
. Namely, Outer space
CV_{n}
decomposes naturally as a disjoint union of open simplices, and Hatcher identified these with open simplices in the
sphere complex

\mathscr{S}(M_{n})
of a doubled handlebody
M_{n}
. We briefly review Hatcher’s construction, then reinterpret the jewels in Outer space in terms of the sphere complex and use this description to define the walls in
\partial\mathcal{J}_{n}
and to show they are contractible with contractible intersections. Finally, we identify the nerve of this covering with the subcomplex of
\mathscr{S}(M_{n})
spanned by simplices that are not in
CV_{n}
.

3.
Walls in the boundary of
\mathcal{J}_{n}

Jewel space
\mathcal{J}_{n}
is the union of all marked jewels
J(g,G),
and is homeomorophic to the Bestvina-Feighn bordification
\widehat{CV}_{n}
by
[
8
]
. The border facets form the boundary of
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. In this section we cover this boundary by contractible subcomplexes with contractible intersections. This is analogous to Borel and Serre’s covering of the bordification of symmetric space by Euclidean spaces
e(P)
associated to parabolic subgroups
P
. The nerve of Borel and Serre’s covering is homotopy equivalent to the associated spherical Tits building. In our case the nerve of the covering is homotopy equivalent to a subcomplex of the simplicial closure of Outer space, which we will identify with a certain sphere complex. We begin by briefly reviewing the relevant definitions.

3.1.
The sphere complex and Outer space

Outer space
CV_{n}
decomposes as a union of open simplices
\sigma(G,g)
, one for each equivalence class of marked graphs
(G,g)
. Here
g\colon R_{n}\to G
is a homotopy equivalence from the standard rose
R_{n}
, and
(g,G)
is equivalent to
(G^{\prime},g^{\prime})
if there is a graph isomorphism
h\colon G\to G^{\prime}
with
h\circ g
homotopic to
g^{\prime}
. The faces of some of these simplices are not in
CV_{n}
, namely the faces obtained by collapsing a subgraph that contains a loop, so this does not give
CV_{n}
the structure of a simplicial complex. However, adding all of the missing faces does give a simplicial complex, known as the
simplicial closure

CV_{n}^{*}
of Outer space; this is the smallest simplicial complex containing
CV_{n}
as a union of open simplices.
A nice way to understand
CV_{n}^{*}
is by identifying open simplices in
CV_{n}
with open simplices in the
sphere complex
of the 3-manifold
M_{n}=\#_{n}(S^{1}\times S^{2}).
One can then identify the full sphere complex
\mathscr{S}(M_{n})
with
CV_{n}^{*}
. We refer the reader to
[
14
]
for details, but sketch the ideas below.

An embedded
2
-sphere in a
3
-manifold
M
is
trivial
if it bounds a ball or is parallel to a boundary component. A
sphere system
is a set of isotopy classes of non-trivial
2
-spheres which has a set of pairwise-disjoint representatives. The sphere complex
\mathscr{S}(M)
is the simplicial complex with a
k
-simplex
\sigma(\mathcal{S})
for each sphere system
\mathcal{S}
in
M
with
k+1
elements.

Definition 3.1
.

A sphere system
\mathcal{S}
is complete if and only if all components of the manifold
M-\mathcal{S}
obtained by cutting
M
open along
\mathcal{S}
are simply-connected. It is core if it contains no separating spheres.

In the case
M=M_{n}
a sphere system
\mathcal{S}
is complete if each component of
M-\mathcal{S}
is homeomorphic to a punctured ball. Note that a complete system must have at least
n
spheres, and a complete system with exactly
n
spheres has exactly one complementary component. Every maximal sphere system cuts
M_{n}
into 3-punctured balls, so in particular is complete.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
14
]
, Appendix)
.

Outer space
CV_{n}
is the union of the interiors of simplices
\sigma(\mathcal{S})
in
\mathscr{S}(M_{n})
for
\mathcal{S}
complete and core.

The translation from the original description of Outer space as a space of marked metric graphs
(G,g)
goes as follows. Using barycentric coordinates on the simplices

can be thought of as a

is a graph, with one vertex for each component of

and one edge for each sphere in
\mathcal{S}
; for example, the dual graph to a minimal complete system is a rose and the dual graph to a maximal system is a trivalent graph. If we fix an identification of

up to conjugacy, i.e. it determines a marking of the dual graph. The weight on the sphere intersecting a given edge can be interpreted as the length of the edge; thus a sphere system determines a marked metric graph with volume one.

\varphi(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G
be the graph dual to
\mathcal{S}
. Then
J(G,g)
is isometric to
J(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

The subsystem
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{S}
is a core complement if and only if the edges dual to
\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}
form a core sub graph of the graph dual to
\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore, the truncation constants satisfy the conditions used in the proof of Proposition 2.2 of
[
8
]
, which characterizes the jewel
J(G,g)
.
∎

If we glue together all jewels associated to complete systems in
M_{n,s}
we obtain the jewel space
\mathcal{J}_{n,s}=\mathcal{J}(M_{n,s}),
as explained in
[
8
]
. We are interested in studying the
boundary

\partial\mathcal{J}_{n,s},
i.e. the union of the facets
\varphi(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})
for all complete core
\mathcal{S}
and core complements
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be an incomplete core system in
M
. Then
\mathcal{T}
is a core complement for a core system
\mathcal{S}
if and only if
\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}
is a core system in
M-\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

This follows from the definition of core complement, together with the observation that if a sphere in
\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}
does not separate
M-\mathcal{T}
, then it does not separate
M
.
∎

If
\mathcal{T}
is any subsystem of a core system
\mathcal{S}
, then
\mathcal{S}
is complete in
M
if and only if
\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}
is complete in
M-\mathcal{T}
. The lemma thus shows that there are lots of complete core systems
\mathcal{S}
containing a given incomplete system
\mathcal{T}
as a core complement.

The boundary
\partial\mathcal{J}_{n,s}
is the union of
walls

W(\mathcal{T})
for
\mathcal{T}
incomplete, where

W(\mathcal{T})=\bigcup_{\mathcal{S}\hbox{ \tiny complete core, }\\
\mathcal{S}\supset\mathcal{T}}\varphi(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})

To understand
W(\mathcal{T})
, we translate Lemma
2.2
into the language of sphere systems:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a core complement to a core system
\mathcal{S}
in
M
. Then

\varphi(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})\cong J_{M}(\mathcal{T})\times J_{M-\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T}).\qed

Proposition 3.10
.

For any incomplete core system
\mathcal{T}
in
M
,
W(\mathcal{T})
is contractible.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.9
we have

W(\mathcal{T})\cong J_{M}(\mathcal{T})\times\bigcup_{{}_{\mathcal{S}\hbox{ \tiny complete core, }\\
\mathcal{S}\supset\mathcal{T}}}J_{M-\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{T})\cong J_{M}(\mathcal{T})\times\mathcal{J}({M-\mathcal{T}}).

J_{M}(\mathcal{T})
is a truncated simplex, so is contractible. If
M-\mathcal{T}
has
k
components
M^{1}\cup\ldots\cup M^{k}
, then
\mathcal{J}(M-\mathcal{T})\cong\mathcal{J}(M^{1})\ast\ldots\ast\mathcal{J}(M^{k})
. Since each
\mathcal{J}(M^{i})
is a deformation retract of some
CV_{n_{i},s_{i}}
, each is contractible.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

\partial\mathcal{J}_{n,s}
is homotopy equivalent to the subcomplex
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n,s})
of the complex
\mathscr{S}(M_{n,s})
of core sphere systems spanned by incomplete systems.

Proof.

Note that any core complement
\mathcal{T}
is necessarily incomplete, since any sphere in
M-\mathcal{T}
for a complete system
\mathcal{T}
is separating. Therefore letting
N\to\infty
in the definition of the truncation constant
t(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}),
so that
t(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T})\to 0,
gives a map

\partial\mathcal{J}_{n,s}\to\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n,s}).

By Proposition
3.10
the pre-image of an open simplex
\sigma(\mathcal{T})
in
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n,s})
is homeomorphic to
(J_{M}(\mathcal{T}))^{0}\times\mathcal{J}({M-\mathcal{T}})
, where
(J_{M}(\mathcal{T}))^{0}
denotes the interior of the jewel
J_{M}(\mathcal{T})
. Since these pre-images are all contractible, the map is a homotopy equivalence.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

The subcomplex
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n})
contains the entire
(n-2)
-skeleton of
\mathscr{S}(M_{n}),
since it takes at least
n
spheres to cut
M_{n}
into simply connected pieces. Since
\mathscr{S}(M_{n})
is contractible, this shows that
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n})
is at least
(n-3)
-connected. The topology of
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n})
and the analogous complex for unreduced Outer space was studied by B. Brück and R. Gupta
[
7
]
, whose methods give another proof that
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n})
is
(n-3)
-connected, and also show that the unreduced version is
(n-2)
-connected.

It is easy to compute, using the Euler characteristic, that
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n})
is
(3n-6)
-dimensional. This situation reflects that of the arc complex
\mathcal{A}
of a surface with boundary. The complex
\mathcal{A}
embeds naturally into
\mathscr{S}(M_{n})
for appropriate
n
(take the product of the surface with an interval to get a handlebody, then double it; the arcs become discs, then spheres). For example, if the surface has genus
g>0
with one boundary component, the entire arc complex
\mathcal{A}
is contractible of dimension
6g-4
, the subcomplex
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
at infinity is
(6g-6)
-dimensional and
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
contains the
(2g-2)
-skeleton so is
(2g-3)
-connected. Harer proves that
\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
is in fact homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of
(2g-2)
-spheres
[
12
]
, and we ask whether the same is true here:

Question:
Is
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n})
homotopy equivalent to a bouquet of spheres of dimension
n-2
?

Recall that
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
is a virtual duality group with virtual cohomological dimension
2n-3
, so the dualizing module is
H^{2n-3}(Out(F_{n});{\mathbb{Z}}(Out(F_{n}))\cong H^{2n-3}_{c}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
. The space
\mathcal{J}_{n}
has dimension
3n-4
, so if
\mathcal{J}_{n}
were an orientable manifold Poincaré duality would identify
H^{2n-3}_{c}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
with
H_{n-1}(\mathcal{J}_{n},\partial\mathcal{J}_{n})
, which is isomorphic to
H_{n-2}(\partial\mathcal{J}_{n})\cong H_{n-2}(\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n}))
. But
\mathcal{J}_{n}
is not a manifold, and we ask

Question:
What (if any) is the relation between
H_{n-2}(\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n}))
and
H^{2n-3}_{c}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
?

Remark 3.13
.

For recent work on the structure of the dualizing module
H^{2n-3}_{c}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
for
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
, see the paper by R. Wade and T. Wasserman
[
18
]
.

Both of these questions can also be asked for
\mathscr{S}_{\infty}(M_{n,s})
(where the spheres would have dimension
n-3+s
) and
\mathcal{J}_{n,s}
.

3.3.
The Jacobian map

There is a natural map, called the
Jacobian
map, from
CV_{n}
to the symmetric space
Q_{n}={\mathrm{SL}}(n,\mathbb{R})/SO(n)
of positive definite quadratic forms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(see
[
1
]
). This map is simple to define using the description of a point of
CV_{n}
as a graph
X
together with a
marking

g
which identifies
\pi_{1}(X)
with
F_{n}
. Specifically, equip the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{E}
spanned by the edges
E
of
X
with the standard inner product, then restrict this to the subspace
H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{R}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
to get a positive definite quadratic form on
H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{R}})
. The marking
g
also identifies
H_{1}(X;{\mathbb{R}})
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so we can think of this form as a point of
Q_{n}
.

Question:
What is the image of
\mathcal{J}_{n}
under the Jacobian map? What is the image of
\partial\mathcal{J}_{n}
?
In particular, does the Jacobian map send the Jewel space to the Grayson retract?

The Jacobian map commutes with the actions of
{\mathrm{Out}}(F_{n})
on
CV_{n}
and and
GL(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
on
Q_{n}
, and the induced map on the quotients is called the
tropical Torelli map
by algebraic geometers (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
). The Jacobian map extends naturally to appropriate bordifications of
CV_{n}
and
Q_{n}
, and the corresponding Torelli maps were studied recently in papers by F. Brown
[
4
]
and F. Brown, M. Chan, S. Galatius and S. Payne

[
5
]
, who used them in particular to detect new unstable cohomology classes for
GL(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
and
SL(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
.
