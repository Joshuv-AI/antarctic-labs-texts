Two dimensional solitary water waves with constant vorticity, Part I: the deep gravity case
James Rowan, Lizhe Wan

Abstract
. We consider the two dimensional pure gravity water waves with nonzero constant vorticity in infinite depth, working in the holomorphic coordinates introduced in [ 32 ] . We show that close to the critical velocity corresponding to zero frequency, a solitary wave exists. We use a fixed point argument to construct the solitary wave whose profile resembles a rescaled Benjamin-Ono soliton. The solitary wave is smooth and has an asymptotic expansion in terms of powers of the Benjamin-Ono soliton.

1.
Introduction

We consider two dimension gravity water waves with nonzero constant vorticity and infinite depth, but without surface tension or viscosity.
The fluid occupies a time dependent domain
\Omega(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with infinite depth and a free upper boundary
\Gamma(t)
which is asymptotically approaching
y=0
.
Denoting the fluid velocity by
\mathbf{u}(t,x,y)=(u(t,x,y),v(t,x,y))
, the pressure by
p(t,x,y)
, and the constant vorticity by
\gamma\neq 0
, the equations inside
\Omega_{t}
are

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}u_{t}+uu_{x}+vu_{y}=-p_{x}&\\
v_{t}+uv_{x}+vv_{y}=-p_{y}-g&\\
u_{x}+v_{y}=0&\\
\omega=u_{y}-v_{x}=-\gamma.\end{array}\right.

On the boundary
\Gamma_{t}
we have the dynamic boundary condition

p=0,

and the kinematic boundary condition

\partial_{t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\text{ is tangent to }\Gamma_{t}.

Here
g>0
is the gravitational constant.

In
[

[

, the above system was expressed in holomorphic position/velocity potential coordinates
(W,Q)
, and the water wave equations with constant vorticity have the following form:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}W_{t}+(W_{\alpha}+1)\underline{F}+i\dfrac{\gamma}{2}W=0&\\
Q_{t}-igW+\underline{F}Q_{\alpha}+i\gamma Q+\mathbf{P}\left[\dfrac{|Q_{\alpha}|^{2}}{J}\right]-i\dfrac{\gamma}{2}T_{1}=0,&\end{array}\right.

where
J:=|1+W_{\alpha}|^{2}
and
\mathbf{P}
is the projection onto negative frequencies, namely

\mathbf{P}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}-iH),

with
H
denoting the Hilbert transform, and

\displaystyle F:=\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{Q_{\alpha}-\bar{Q}_{\alpha}}{J}\right],\quad

\displaystyle F_{1}=\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{W}{1+\bar{W}_{\alpha}}+\frac{\bar{W}}{1+W_{\alpha}}\right],

\displaystyle\underline{F}:=F-i\frac{\gamma}{2}F_{1},\quad

\displaystyle T_{1}:=\mathbf{P}\left[\frac{W\bar{Q}_{\alpha}}{1+\bar{W}_{\alpha}}-\frac{\bar{W}Q_{\alpha}}{1+W_{\alpha}}\right].

We seek to find solitary wave solutions of (
1.1
), in other words nonzero solutions having the form

\left(W(\alpha,t),Q(\alpha,t)\right)=\left(W(\alpha+ct),Q(\alpha+ct)\right)

for
t\geq 0
.
Here we assume that
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}W(\alpha)=0
, so that we only consider localized solitary waves and not periodic traveling waves, but both the solitary and periodic traveling waves problems have been widely studied.

The presence or absence of physical parameters such as gravity, surface tension, (infinite) depth, and vorticity can lead to or prevent the formation of solitary waves.

A variety of different coordinate systems have been used to study the existence of solitary waves; we mention two other works that also use holomorphic coordinates.
In the case of zero vorticity

Then using a similar method in
[

The authors, together with Ifrim and Tataru showed in
[
35

that for solutions concentrated at the amplitude and frequency scale
\epsilon
, a rescaling of the Benjamin-Ono equation

(1.2)

(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}|D|)u+uu_{x}=0

gives a good and stable approximation to the system (
1.1
) on the cubic time scale
\left[0,T\epsilon^{-2}\right]
where well-posedness was proved in
[
36
]
.
Here
|D|
is a Fourier multiplier defined in (
2.3
).

The approximation can be heuristically justified on the linear level by looking at the linearization of (
1.1
) around the zero solution,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}w_{t}+q_{\alpha}=0&\\
q_{t}-igw+i\gamma q=0,&\end{array}\right.

as well as its dispersion relation

\tau^{2}+\gamma\tau+g\xi=0.

Taking a quadratic approximation for the top branch of the dispersion relation (recall that
\xi\leq 0
in the holomorphic coordinate setting), we have

\tau=-\frac{g}{\gamma}\xi-\frac{g^{2}}{\gamma^{3}}\xi^{2},

so that
-\frac{g}{\gamma}
corresponds to
0
frequency.
The velocity
-\frac{g}{\gamma}
will be the critical velocity in the sequel.

It is well known that the Benjamin-Ono equation has the unique single
soliton solutions

(1.3)

u(t,c)=c\rho(c(x-ct)),\quad\mbox{ where }\rho(x)=\frac{4}{1+x^{2}},

and
c
is the velocity of the waves in the horizontal direction.
By the result in
[
35
]
, the system (
1.1
) has solitary-wave-like solutions on a cubic time scale
[0,T\epsilon^{-2}]
.
A natural question then arises, are there exact solitary waves solutions of (
1.1
) close to (after a suitable series of rescalings) the Benjamin-Ono soliton?
We give an affirmative answer to this question.

Instead of working with the solitary wave ansatz in (
1.1
) in the complex setting,
we derive and then work with a real-valued differential equation, the
Babenko equation
, for
U:=\Im W
:

(1.4)

\displaystyle(g+c\gamma-c^{2}|D|)U=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}-gU|D|U-\frac{g}{2}|D|U^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\left(U|D|U^{2}-U^{2}|D|U-\frac{1}{3}|D|U^{3}\right).

Historically, Babenko studied periodic traveling waves
[
7
,
8
]
, but the approach of finding an elliptic equation solved by the solitary wave profile works in the nonperiodic case as well.
The existence of a solitary wave for (
1.1
) is equivalent to the existence of a nontrivial solution of the equation (
1.4
), as we will show in Section
2
.

Our first result is that under some suitable conditions, the equation (
1.4
) is an elliptic equation, so that the solutions enjoy
good Sobolev regularity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that (
1.4
) has a solution
\Im W\in H^{2}
. If
g+c\gamma<0
and
2g\sup\Im W<c^{2}
, the solution of (
1.4
) is in
\cap_{k=2}^{\infty}H^{k}
.

Note in particular that the sign of the vorticity
\gamma
determines the sign of the possible velocities
c
.
If
\gamma>0
, then the velocity
c<-\frac{g}{\gamma}
, and if
\gamma<0
, then
c>-\frac{g}{\gamma}
.
In both cases, the solitary wave velociy
c
is just outside the range of velocities for dispersive waves.

Theorem
1.1
motivates us to find a nontrivial

H^{2}
solution of (
1.4
) in order to gain better Sobolev regularity.
Our main result shows that such a nontrivial solution does exist for certain velocities:

Theorem 1.2
.

Given any nonzero vorticity
\gamma
and positive constant
g
, there exists a small positive constant
\epsilon_{0}
such that for any velocity
c
satisfying
-\epsilon_{0}<g+c\gamma<0
, the system (
1.1
) has a unique nontrivial
H^{2}
solitary waves solution in the sense that
\Im W
is close to the Benjamin-Ono soliton after rescaling and space translation; its imaginary part is an even function, and its real part is an odd function.

Here the even and odd functions should be regarded as the class of functions modulo space translations.
For example, we can define the
H^{2}_{e}(\mathbb{R})
, the
H^{2}
space of even functions on
\mathbb{R}
as

H^{2}_{e}(\mathbb{R}):=\{f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}):f(\alpha)=f(2\alpha_{0}-\alpha)\text{ a.e. for some constant }\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Without loss of generality, we can simply let
\alpha_{0}=0
.

We will show in Section
4
that
\Im W
and
\Im Q
are even functions.
A key observation is that the Hilbert transform turns the even functions into odd functions.
Since
\Re W=H\Im W
, and
\Re Q=H\Im Q
, we know that
\Re W
and
\Re Q
are both odd functions.
By the discussion of Section
6
of
[
3
]
, we know the the profile of the water waves surface
\eta
in Zakharov-Craig-Sulem formulation is proportional to
\Im W
in the holomorphic coordinates.
This shows that the profile of the solitary wave surface is an even function modulo space translations.

Remark 1
.

In a recent paper
[
39
]
, Lokharu, Wahlén and Weber showed that for two-dimensional steady pure-gravity water waves with finite depth of positive constant vorticity
\gamma
,
\sup\Im W-\inf\Im W<\frac{2g}{\gamma^{2}}
.
Since
0
is in the range of
\Im W
,
we have
\sup\Im W<\frac{2g}{\gamma^{2}}
, and
\inf\Im W>-\frac{2g}{\gamma^{2}}
.
Their result does not contradict our Theorem
1.1
, since in our setting,
\left|c+\frac{g}{\gamma}\right|
is small and positive, so that
\frac{2g}{\gamma^{2}}<\frac{2c^{2}}{g}
, which gives an upper bound on
\Im W
.

Remark 2
.

We consider in this article the simple case where the solitary waves have a simple profile with velocity
c
.
However, the simplified model Benjamin-Ono equation (
1.2
) itself has
N
-soliton solutions, with
N
different velocities.
It is natural to ask whether the system (
1.1
) also has multi-solitary waves solutions, but this is beyond the scope of the present article here.

Furthermore, we show that the profile of the solution can be expressed as a power series in the Benjamin-Ono soliton
\rho
(
1.3
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\epsilon:=\left|\frac{g}{\gamma}+c\right|
, then the profile of water waves surface
\Im W
modulo translations has an asymptotic expansion in terms of
\rho
of the form,
\forall N\geq 1
,

(1.5)

\Im W(\alpha)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}\rho^{k}\left(\frac{\epsilon\alpha}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\right)+g_{N},

where the coefficients
b_{k}
depend on
\epsilon
and the remainder term
g_{N}
decays faster than
\langle\alpha\rangle^{-2N}
;
in particular,
\Im W(\alpha)=O_{L^{\infty}}\left((1+\alpha^{2})^{-1}\right)
asymptotically.

A more precise functional framework and statement for this theorem will be given in Section
5
.

Finally we prove the following theorem, giving the characterization of
\Im W
at or near the maximal speed:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\left(c_{M},-\frac{g}{\gamma}\right)
for
\gamma>0
(or
\left(-\frac{g}{\gamma},c_{M}\right)
for
\gamma<0
) be the maximal interval of velocities for which a continuous one-parameter family of even solitary wave solutions to (
1.1
) extending the solutions of Theorem
1.2
exists in a weighted Sobolev space
X
.
Then one of the following must happen:

(1)

the interval of possible velocities is infinite, so that
c_{M}=-\infty
for
\gamma>0
(or
c_{M}=\infty
for
\gamma<0
);

(2)

the norm of the solitary wave profiles in a weighted Sobolev space
X
is increasing to infinity along a subsequence as
c
approaches
c_{M}
;

(3)

the solitary wave is approaching the maximal height along a subsequence as
c
approaches
c_{M}
;

(4)

an eigenvalue (other than the one corresponding to translation symmetry) for the linearization of (
1.4
) around the solitary wave is approaching
0
along a subsequence as
c
approaches
c_{M}
.

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we use the variational approach to derive the Babenko equation (
1.4
), which is a nonlinear first order ODE that describes the profile of the solitary waves of (

For the following sections, we shall be studying the properties of the Babenko equation (
1.4

In Section

With this ellipticity condition, we are able to show the solution which we construct in the following section have better Sobolev regularity.
In Section

In Section

We then give a finer asymptotic decay of the solution profile and shows that it only involve the even powers of

.
Finally in Section

2.
Derivation of the Babenko equation

In this section, we derive the Babenko equation of the water waves system (
1.1
), which describes the profile of solitary waves.
The system (
1.1
) is expressed in holomorphic coordinates, and
W,Q
are both holomorphic functions.
A very nice property of holomorphic functions is that their real parts equal the Hilbert transform of their imaginary parts.
Therefore it suffices to consider just the imaginary parts.
According to the computation carried out in the appendix of
[
36
]
,
the total energy of (
1.1
) is

(2.1)

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{2}\int|D|\Im Q\cdot\Im Q+g(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)+\gamma|D|\Im Q\cdot(\Im W)^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{3}(\Im W)^{3}(1+|D|\Im W)\,d\alpha,

and the horizontal momentum is

(2.2)

\mathcal{P}=-\int|D|\Im Q\Im W+\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)\,d\alpha.

Here, let
PV_{.}
denotes principle value, the differential operator
|D|
is defined by

|D|f(\alpha)=\partial_{\alpha}Hf(\alpha)=PV_{.}\frac{1}{\pi}\int\frac{f(\alpha)-f(y)}{|\alpha-y|^{2}}\,dy.

An alternative definition of operator
|D|
which we will use frequently is given via the Fourier transform

(2.3)

\widehat{|D|f}(\xi)=|\xi|\hat{f}(\xi).

We derive the Babenko equation with the variational approach.
In
[
11
]
, it was observed by Benjamin and Olver that the solution of Babenko equation is characterised as a critical point of the total energy subject to the constraint of fixed momentum.
The velocity
c
can be viewed as the corresponding Lagrange multiplier.
In order to compute the functional derivative, we will need the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Given a function
f(x)
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
L\left(f(x),\nabla f(x),|D|f(x)\right)
to be a
C^{2}_{0}
functional of
f
,
\nabla f(x)
, and
|D|f(x)
, and consider the functional defined by

J(f)=\int L\left(f(x),\nabla f(x),|D|f(x)\right)dx.

Then the functional derivative of
J(f)
is given by

\frac{\delta J}{\delta f}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}-div\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\nabla f}\right)+|D|\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial|D|f}\right).

Proof.

For any smooth test function
\phi(x)
and small constant
\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\delta J(f,\epsilon\phi):=

\displaystyle J(f+\epsilon\phi)-J(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int L\left(f(x)+\epsilon\phi(x),\nabla f(x)+\epsilon\nabla\phi(x),|D|f(x)+\epsilon|D|\phi(x)\right)-L\left(f(x),\nabla f(x),|D|f(x)\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\epsilon\phi+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\nabla f}\epsilon\nabla\phi+\frac{\partial f}{\partial|D|f}\epsilon|D|\phi\,dx+O(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\int\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial f}-div\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\nabla f}\right)+|D|\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial|D|f}\right)\right)\phi\,dx+O(\epsilon^{2}),

using integration by parts and the self-adjoint property of operator
|D|
.
The result follows from the definition

\int\frac{\delta J}{\delta f}\phi(x)dx=\frac{\delta J(f,\epsilon\phi)}{\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}.

∎

Considering the functional derivative with respect to
\Im Q
, the first equation is

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im Q}=c\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im Q},

we have

(2.4)

\Im Q=-\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Im W)^{2}-c\Im W.

Similarly, for the functional derivative with respect to
\Im W
, the second equation is

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im W}=c\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im W}.

We have by computation

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im W}=

\displaystyle g\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)+\frac{1}{2}g|D|(\Im W)^{2}+\gamma|D|\Im Q\Im W+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|(\Im W)^{3},

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im W}=

\displaystyle-|D|\Im Q-\gamma\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)-\frac{\gamma}{2}|D|(\Im W)^{2},

which leads to

\displaystyle g\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)+\frac{1}{2}g|D|(\Im W)^{2}+\gamma|D|\Im Q\Im W+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}(\Im W)^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|(\Im W)^{3}+c|D|\Im Q+c\gamma\Im W(1+|D|\Im W)+c\frac{\gamma}{2}|D|(\Im W)^{2}=0.

Therefore by eliminating
\Im Q
using (
2.4
), we obtain that
U:=\Im W
solves the Babenko equation
(
1.4
).

As a consequence of the derivation, we immediately obtain

Corollary 2.2
.

If a nonzero
U\in H^{2}_{0}
solves (
1.4
), then (
1.1
) has a solitary wave solution modulo space translations

\left(HU(\alpha+ct)+iU(\alpha+ct),cHU(\alpha+ct)-\frac{\gamma}{2}H(U^{2})(\alpha+ct)+icU(\alpha+ct)-\frac{i\gamma}{2}U^{2}(\alpha+ct)\right).

3.
Elliptic regularity

In this section, we show that the Babenko equation (
1.4
) is essentially an elliptic differential equation under some suitable conditions, so that any
H^{2}
solution is a-priori smooth.
The construction of such a non-trivial
H^{2}
solution is performed in Section
4
.

Assume for the moment for
s\geq 2
, we have found a nontrivial
H^{s}
solution of the Babenko equation (
1.4
).
We rewrite (
1.4
) as

\displaystyle(g+c\gamma

\displaystyle-c^{2}|D|+2gT_{U}|D|)U=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U^{2}-\frac{1}{2}g|D|\Pi(U,U)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}|D|\Pi(U,U)U

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{6}|D|\Pi(U,U,U)-g[T_{U},|D|]U+\gamma^{2}U[T_{U},|D|]U-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}[T_{U^{2}},|D|]U

\displaystyle-gT_{|D|U}U-g\Pi(U,|D|U)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}T_{|D|U}U^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\Pi(|D|U,U^{2})

(3.1)

\displaystyle+\gamma^{2}T_{T_{U}|D|U}U+\gamma^{2}\Pi(|D|U,U^{2})+\gamma^{2}(T_{U^{2}}-T_{U}T_{U})|D|U.

Here,
T_{u}v
is the low-high frequency component of paraproduct, and
\Pi(u,v)
is the high-high frequency component of paraproduct, please see for instance Chapter
2
of
[
9
]
for definition and further introduction.

We define the elliptic operator
L
by

LU:=(g+c\gamma-c^{2}|D|+2gT_{U}|D|)U.

By writing the Babenko equation (
1.4
) as (
3.1
), each cubic term on the right hand side has either cancellation or can be estimated in
H^{s}
norm as indicated below.

We now estimate the right hand side of (
3.1
).
The first quadratic term can be bounded by
\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{2}
.
For the next three terms of the right hand side of (
3.1
), we use the following lemma on paraproduct.

Lemma 3.1

(high-high paraproduct)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\alpha+\beta>0
, then

\|\Pi(u,v)\|_{H^{\alpha+\beta-\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|v\|_{H^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Then for
s\geq\frac{3}{2}
,

\displaystyle\|g|D|\Pi(U,U)\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Pi(U,U)\|_{H^{s+1}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{2},

\displaystyle\||D|\Pi(U,U)U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}\|\Pi(U,U)\|_{H^{s+1}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3},

\displaystyle\||D|\Pi(U,U,U)\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Pi(U,U,U)\|_{H^{s+1}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3}.

Then we estimate three commutator terms.

Lemma 3.2
.

(1)

For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
,

\|[T_{u},|D|]f\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{1}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}.

(2)

For a function
u(x)\in L^{\infty}
, then
T_{u}
is an operator of order 0, and
\forall s\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|T_{u}f\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}.

For
s>\frac{3}{2}
,
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
can be embedded into
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then we have

\displaystyle\|g[T_{U},|D|]U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{2}U[T_{U},|D|]U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}\|[T_{U},|D|]U\|\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3},

\displaystyle\|\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}[T_{U^{2}},|D|]U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U^{2}\|_{H^{s}}\|U\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3}.

We now estimate the last seven terms of (
3.1
).
For
s>\frac{3}{2}
,

\displaystyle\|gT_{|D|U}U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\||D|U\|_{L^{\infty}}\|U\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|g\Pi(U,|D|U)\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\||D|U\|_{H^{s-1}}\|U\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}T_{|D|U}U^{2}\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\||D|U\|_{L^{\infty}}\|U^{2}\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3},

\displaystyle\|\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\Pi(|D|U,U^{2})\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\||D|U\|_{H^{s-1}}\|U^{2}\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3},

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{2}T_{T_{U}|D|U}U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|T_{U}|D|U\|_{L^{\infty}}\|U\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3},

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{2}\Pi(|D|U,U^{2})\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\||D|U\|_{H^{s-1}}\|U^{2}\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3},

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{2}(T_{U^{2}}-T_{U}T_{U})|D|U\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{3}.

Furthermore, we want to replace the
T_{U}
by
U
in
L
, to do this , we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
2
]
Proposition 2.10)
.

If
s_{0},s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
s_{0}<s_{1}+s_{2}-\frac{d}{2}
and
s_{0}\leq s_{1}
, then for all
a\in H^{s_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u\in H^{s_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\|au-T_{a}u\|_{H^{s_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|a\|_{H^{s_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|u\|_{H^{s_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Then we have

\|(T_{U}-U)|D|U\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}\||D|U\|_{H^{s-1}}\lesssim\|U\|_{H^{s}}^{2}.

Define

\tilde{L}u=(g+c\gamma-c^{2}|D|+2gU|D|)u,

and
f
equals the right hand side of (
3.1
) plus
(T_{U}-U)|D|U

We have thus shown that
\tilde{L}U=f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
.

To gain more regularity of the solution, we need to assume the following sign condition and the (possible) maximal height condition:

(3.2)

g+c\gamma<0,

(3.3)

2gU<c^{2}.

Proposition 3.4
.

If the sign condition (
3.2
)
and the maximal height condition (
3.3
)
both hold, then the operator
\tilde{L}
is elliptic of order
1
.

For elliptic equations, we recall the classical lemma of elliptic regularity.

Lemma 3.5

(Elliptic regularity)
.

For an elliptic operator
\Phi
of order
m
, if
u,\Phi u\in H^{s}
, then
u\in H^{s+m}
, and

\|u\|_{H^{s+m}}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{s}}+\|\Phi u\|_{H^{s}}.

Given that
U\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
, above classical theory of elliptic regularity shows that
U\in H^{s+1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Iterating this process gives
U\in H^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, for any
N>0
.

4.
Existence of nontrivial solution to the Babenko equation

In this section, we use the Benjamin-Ono soliton‘ to construct solitary waves for (
1.1
) with velocities slightly above the critical velocity
-\frac{g}{\gamma}
.

We note that the constant vorticity water waves problem admits a two-parameter family of scaling symmetries, and that we have a sign reversal symmetry where we can change the signs of both
\gamma
and
c
.
This means we can rescale the equations so that
g=1
and
\gamma=-1
.
After this rescaling, the assumption that the velocity is close to the critical velocity means we can write

c=1+\epsilon

for
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.

The rescaled version of the Babenko equation (
1.4
), we have

-\epsilon U-(1+\epsilon)^{2}|D|U+\frac{1}{2}U^{2}+U|D|U+\frac{1}{2}|D|U^{2}-\frac{1}{2}U|D|U^{2}+\frac{1}{2}U^{2}|D|U+\frac{1}{6}|D|U^{3}=0.

We now rescale again, using the scaling

(4.1)

U(\alpha)=\epsilon\phi\left(\frac{\epsilon\alpha}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\right)

and dividing by
\epsilon^{2}
, treating the terms with higher powers of
\epsilon
as perturbative:

(4.2)

\begin{split}-\phi-|D|\phi+\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}&=\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left(-\phi|D|\phi-\frac{1}{2}|D|\phi^{2}\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\phi|D|\phi^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}|D|\phi-\frac{1}{6}|D|\phi^{3}\right).\end{split}

Now consider

\rho(\alpha)=\frac{4}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

This is the single soliton solution of velocity
1
for the Benjamin-Ono equation; it satisfies the equation

-\rho-|D|\rho+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}=0.

When
\epsilon
is small, we will construct solutions to (
4.2
) close to
\rho
.
Subtracting the equations for
\phi
and
\rho
, we find that
v=\phi-\rho
solves the following equation:

(4.3)

-v-|D|v+\rho v=-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho).

where
\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)
is the right-hand side of (
4.2
)

Let

\mathcal{L}=-1-|D|+\rho

denote the differential operator on the left-hand side of (
4.3
).
This operator arises in the study of the linearization of the Benjamin-Ono equation around its soliton solution
\rho
.
Its principal part is elliptic, and it sends functions in
H^{1}
to
L^{2}
.

The spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{L}
is well-understood, see for example
[
12
]
or
[
38
]
.
There are four eigenvalues

\left\{-1,\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2},0,\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right\},

along with a continuous spectrum
(-\infty,-1]
, and the eigenvalue
0
is simple with
\rho^{\prime}
as the corresponding eigenfunction.
The eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
0
is also an odd function which can be computed explicitly.

Since the kernel of the operator
\mathcal{L}
is spanned by
\rho^{{}^{\prime}}
, where
\rho^{{}^{\prime}}
is an odd function, we work in spaces based on
H^{2}_{e}(\mathbb{R})
, the space of all even functions in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Note that
H^{2}_{e}(\mathbb{R})
is an algebra, and that
|D|
maps
H^{k}_{e}(\mathbb{R})
into the space
H^{k-1}_{e}(\mathbb{R})
of even functions in
H^{k-1}(\mathbb{R})
.
In this setting, the inverse of
\mathcal{L}
can be uniquely determined.

We write,

P_{\epsilon}(v)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)\right).

If we can find a fixed point of this operator, then we have a solution to (
4.3
).

Define the weighted Sobolev space
X
to be the space of all even functions such that

(4.4)

\|u\|^{2}_{X}=\|u\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\eta^{2}\|\alpha u\|^{2}_{H^{2}}

is finite, where
\eta
is a small constant to be chosen later.

We will show that
P_{\epsilon}
is a contraction on a small ball
B(0,\delta)
in
X
, so that it has a unique fixed point in that ball.
We work in the weighted space
X
rather than merely
H^{2}
to get better control on the asymptotic
\alpha
behavior of
v
and thus a clearer picture of the shape of the solitary wave.

We have

P_{\epsilon}(u)-P_{\epsilon}(v)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}(u-v)(u+v)+\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)-\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)\right).

We first show that

(4.5)

\left\|-\frac{1}{2}(u-v)(u+v)+\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)-\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)\right\|_{H^{1}}\leq C(\delta,\epsilon)\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

Recall the definition

\begin{split}\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(\phi)&=\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left(-\phi|D|\phi-\frac{1}{2}|D|\phi^{2}\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\phi|D|\phi^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}|D|\phi-\frac{1}{6}|D|\phi^{3}\right).\end{split}

In investigating the difference

\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)-\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho),

we work with each term individually, also tracking how many powers of
\rho
appear.

Terms not in
\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)-\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)
: We have

\left\|-\frac{1}{2}(u-v)(u+v)\right\|_{H^{1}}\leq\delta\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

Second-order terms
: The second-order terms without any factors of
\rho
contribute

\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-u|D|u+v|D|v\right\|_{H^{1}}=\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|u|D|(u-v)+(u-v)|D|v\|_{H^{1}}\leq 2\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}

and

\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{2}|D|u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|D|v^{2}\right\|_{H^{1}}=\frac{\epsilon}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\||D|(u^{2}-v^{2})\|_{H^{1}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

The second-order terms with one factor of
\rho
contribute

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-u|D|\rho+v|D|\rho\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\rho|D|u+\rho|D|v\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-|D|(\rho u)+|D|(\rho v)\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

Third-order terms
: The third-order terms with no factors of
\rho
contribute

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}u|D|u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v|D|v^{2}\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|(u-v)|D|u^{2}+v|D|(u^{2}-v^{2})\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\epsilon^{2}\delta^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}|D|u+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}|D|v\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|(u^{2}-v^{2})|D|u+v^{2}|D|(u-v)\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\epsilon^{2}\delta^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{6}|D|u^{3}+\frac{1}{6}|D|v^{3}\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|u^{3}-v^{3}\right\|_{H^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}\delta^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

The third-order terms with one factor of
\rho
contribute

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}\rho|D|u^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho|D|v^{2}\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|\rho\|_{H^{1}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|u|D|(u\rho)-v|D|(v\rho)\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-(u\rho)|D|u+(v\rho)|D|v\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}|D|\rho+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}|D|\rho\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{2}|D|(u^{2}\rho)+\frac{1}{2}|D|(v^{2}\rho)\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\delta\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

The third-order terms with two factors of
\rho
contribute

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|\rho|D|(u\rho)-\rho|D|(v\rho)\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|\frac{1}{2}u|D|\rho^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v|D|\rho^{2}\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}|D|u+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\rho^{2}|D|v\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-(u\rho)|D|\rho+(v\rho)|D|\rho\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{1}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\left\|-\frac{1}{2}|D|(u\rho^{2})+\frac{1}{2}|D|(v\rho^{2})\right\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\|\rho\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

Combining above estimates, we have the desired bound (
4.5
).

Because the operator
\mathcal{L}
, when restricted to even functions, has two nonzero eigenvalues in its discrete spectrum

\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\text{ and }\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}

a continuous spectrum contained in
(-\infty,-1]
, we know that

(4.6)

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\right)^{-1}\|g\|_{L^{2}}.

Since the operator
1+|D|
is elliptic, we know that

(4.7)

\|g\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{2}}+\|(1+|D|)g\|_{L^{2}},

where the implicit constant does not depend on
g
.
Moreover, since
\rho
is bounded, this elliptic regularity statement tells us that if

(1+|D|)f=\rho f-g,

then

\|f\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}}+\|\rho f-g\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}}+\|g\|_{L^{2}},

or, in other words, that

(4.8)

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{L^{2}}+\|g\|_{L^{2}}.

By applying the spectral bound (
4.6
), we have

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{2}},

so that (
4.8
) becomes

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{2}}.

Running the same elliptic regularity argument again, we have

(4.9)

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}g\|_{H^{1}}+\|g\|_{H^{1}}.

Applying this to

P_{\epsilon}(u)-P_{\epsilon}(v)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}(u-v)(u+v)+\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)-\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)\right)

and using (
4.5
), we have

(4.10)

\left\|P_{\epsilon}(u)-P_{\epsilon}(v)\right\|_{H^{2}}\leq C(\delta,\epsilon)\|u-v\|_{H^{2}},

so we have a contraction on
H^{2}_{e}
as long as
\delta
and
\epsilon
are small enough.

We now turn to the weighted space
(\eta\alpha)^{-1}H^{2}_{e}
.
Multiplying (
4.3
) by
\eta\alpha
, we have

\eta\alpha v-|D|(\eta\alpha v)+\rho(\eta\alpha v)=\eta[\alpha,|D|]v-\frac{1}{2}v(\eta\alpha v)+\eta\alpha\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho).

Writing
w=\eta\alpha v
, then

(4.11)

\mathcal{L}w=\eta[\alpha,|D|]v-\frac{1}{2}vw+\eta\alpha\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho).

Let

Q_{\epsilon}(w)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\eta\eta\alpha,|D|]\left(\frac{1}{\eta\alpha}w\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{w}{\eta\alpha}\right)w+\eta\alpha\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{w}{\eta\alpha}+\rho\right)\right).

If we can show that
Q_{\epsilon}
is a contraction from
X
onto
H_{e}^{2}
,
then together with the fact that
P_{\epsilon}
is a contraction on
H^{2}_{e}
,
we can show that
P_{\epsilon}
is a contraction on
X
.

We now show, for
w_{u}=\eta\alpha u
,
w_{v}=\eta\alpha v
solving (
4.11
),

(4.12)

\left\|\eta[\alpha,|D|](u-v)-\frac{1}{2}\eta\alpha\left(u^{2}-v^{2}\right)+\eta\alpha\left(\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)-\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(v+\rho)\right)\right\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\|(u-v)\|_{X}

for small enough values of
\eta
, where the constant
C
depends on
\eta
,
\delta
and
\epsilon
.

Commutator terms:

A computation shows that, for
f\in H^{2}
that vanishes at infinity,

\left[\alpha,|D|\right]f=\alpha H(f_{\alpha})-H(\partial_{\alpha}(\alpha f))=-Hf+[\alpha,H]f_{\alpha}=-Hf+2\int f_{\alpha}d\alpha=-Hf,

where we work on the Fourier side to conclude that (in the sense of distributions)

\mathcal{F}[\alpha,H]=[\frac{1}{i}\partial_{\xi},i\operatorname{sgn}\xi]=2\delta_{0},

so that

[\alpha,H]f_{\alpha}=\int 1\cdot f_{\alpha}d\alpha.

giving us the estimate

(4.13)

\left\|[\alpha,|D|]f\right\|_{H^{2}}\leq\|f\|_{H^{2}},

so that, as long as
\eta
is chosen small enough, we have

\left\|\eta[\alpha,|D|](u-v)\right\|_{H^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}\|u-v\|_{H^{2}}.

Remaining terms
: the remaining terms can be handled as in the previous unweighted argument, with the
\eta\alpha
in front placed with whichever difference term is measured
H^{1}
or
H^{2}
.
This proves the rest of (
4.12
).

Arguing with elliptic rgularity as before, we have

\left\|Q_{\epsilon}(w_{u})-Q_{\epsilon}(w_{v})\right\|_{H^{2}}\leq C(\delta,\epsilon)\|w_{u}-w_{v}\|_{H^{2}}.

As long as
\delta
and
\epsilon
are chosen small enough to ensure the contribution of the non-commutator terms is at least as small as that of the commutator terms, we have that
Q_{\epsilon}
is a contraction on
H^{2}_{e}
,
and thus
P_{\epsilon}
is a contraction on
X
.

We now need to choose a
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)
such that
P_{\epsilon}
sends the ball of radius
\delta
in
X
into itself.

Set
\delta=k\epsilon
for some positive constant
k
to be chosen later.

We have, arguing as above,

\|P_{\epsilon}(u)\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{2}\|u^{2}\|_{H^{2}}+\|\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)\|_{H^{2}}\right).

For the
u^{2}
term, we have

\|u^{2}\|_{H^{2}}\leq\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{2}<k^{2}\epsilon^{2}.

For the terms with no factors of
u
, we have

\|\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\|_{H^{2}}=C\epsilon+O_{H^{2}}(\epsilon^{2})

for some positive constant
C
.

For all other terms, there will be at least one factor of
u
.
This factor can be bounded in
H^{1}
,
L^{\infty}
, or
H^{2}
by a factor of
k\epsilon
, so, coupled with the factors of
\epsilon
in the definition of
\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}
, we can guarantee that

\|\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(\rho+u)\|_{H^{2}}=C\epsilon+O_{H^{2}}(\epsilon^{2}).

We now work in the weighted space
(\eta\alpha)^{-1}H^{2}
.
We have

\|Q_{\epsilon}(\eta\alpha u)\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{2\eta}\|u\alpha u\|_{H^{2}}+\|\eta[\alpha,|D|]u\|_{H^{2}}+\|\eta\alpha\tilde{g}_{\epsilon}(u+\rho)\|_{H^{2}}\right).

Since
\alpha\rho
and
\eta\alpha u
are bounded in
H^{2}
, we can argue as above.
The only change is the addition of the commutator term which can be handled using the estimate (
4.13
).
We thus have

\|Q_{\epsilon}(\eta\alpha u)\|\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon+O_{H^{2}}(\epsilon^{2}),

so by choosing
k
to be a sufficiently large numerical constant, we can ensure that for small enough
\epsilon
,

\|P_{\epsilon}(u)\|_{H^{2}}+\|Q_{\epsilon}(\eta\alpha u)\|_{H^{2}}<k\epsilon=\delta

for
u
in a ball of radius
\delta
around the origin in
X
.

Plugging this choice of
\delta
into the right-hand sides of above related inequalities, we see that by picking
\epsilon_{0}
small enough, we can guarantee that the constant on the right-hand side of (
4.10
) is smaller than
1
for all
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.
This means that we have found a
\delta
such that
P_{\epsilon}
is a contraction on the ball of radius
k\epsilon
around the origin in
X
for all
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
.

After untangling the definitions and rescalings, this guarantees the existence of a solitary wave of velocity

c=-(1+\epsilon)\frac{g}{\gamma}

for the constant vorticity water wave problem for a small open interval
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
.

5.
Asymptotic decay of the profile

In this section, we give a detailed analysis of the asymptotic decay of the function
\phi
constructed in Section
4
.

For
\epsilon
small enough, from (
4.1
) we see that the maximal height
\|U\|_{L^{\infty}}=O(\epsilon)
is also small.
The velocity is close to
-\frac{g}{\gamma}
, so that
\frac{c^{2}}{2g}\approx\frac{g}{2\gamma^{2}}\gg\epsilon
.
The maximal height condition (
3.3
) holds in this case.
By the result in Section
3
, we conclude that
U\in H^{N}(\mathbb{R})
, for any
N>0
.
Hence, we should also expect
\phi
to have nice regularity and decay properties when
\epsilon
is small.

Before going to a more refined asymptotic analysis, we first give a qualitative bound of asymptotic decay for
\phi
.
We recall the equation (
4.3
), which we write as

-(1+|D|)v=\mathcal{N}(v).

We have

(5.1)

\mathcal{N}(v)=-\rho v-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{1}(v+\rho)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{2}(v+\rho),

where

(5.2)

\mathcal{N}_{1}(\phi)=-\phi|D|\phi-\frac{1}{2}|D|\phi^{2},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{2}(\phi)=\left(\frac{1}{2}\phi|D|\phi^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}|D|\phi-\frac{1}{6}|D|\phi^{3}\right).

According to the result in
[
5
]
, the operator
1+|D|
is an elliptic operator of order
1
with fundamental solution

\Phi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau e^{-\tau}}{\alpha^{2}+\tau^{2}}\,d\tau.

\Phi(\alpha)
is a positive function that satisfies the properties

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(\alpha)d\alpha=1,\quad\Phi(\alpha)\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}.

Using Lemma
5.2
and Lemma
5.5
below, a direct computation show that
|\mathcal{N}(v)|\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\rho
,
so that

|v|=|\Phi\ast\mathcal{N}(v)|\leq\Phi\ast|\mathcal{N}(v)|\lesssim\frac{\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\Phi\ast\rho.

We bound the convolution
\Phi\ast\rho
by

|\Phi\ast\rho|\lesssim\int\Phi(t)\frac{1}{1+|\alpha-t|^{2}}dt=\int_{|t-\alpha|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Phi(t)\frac{1}{1+|\alpha-t|^{2}}dt+\int_{|t-\alpha|<\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Phi(t)\frac{1}{1+|\alpha-t|^{2}}dt.

For the first integral on the right-hand side,

\int_{|t-\alpha|\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Phi(t)\frac{1}{1+|\alpha-t|^{2}}dt\lesssim\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{2}}\int\Phi(t)dt=\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

For the second integral on the right-hand side, for large
\alpha

\int_{|t-\alpha|<\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Phi(t)\frac{1}{1+|\alpha-t|^{2}}dt\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\int_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}^{\frac{3\alpha}{2}}\frac{1}{1+|\alpha-t|^{2}}dt\lesssim\pi\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

Therefore, we show that
|v|\lesssim\frac{(1+\pi)\epsilon}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\langle\alpha\rangle^{-2}.

In order to give a more precise asymptotic characterization, we work in the weighted Sobolev spaces

H^{k,\sigma}=\{u:(1+|\alpha|^{2})^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}u\in H^{k}\},

and prove the main result of this section, which is a rescaled version of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be small enough and there exists a solitary wave solution
\phi
to (
4.2
).
For any positive integer
N\geq 1
, there exists real numbers
a_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq N
depending on
\epsilon
and a function
g_{N}\in H^{2,2N}
such that
\phi
can be expressed as

(5.3)

\phi=\rho+\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N}.

Remark 3
.

From (
5.3
), no odd powers of
\langle\alpha\rangle^{-1}
appear in the asymptotic expansion.
Additionally, the proof of the theorem goes through identically in the case that
\phi
and
\alpha\phi
lie in
H^{k}
for some
k>2
, with the remainder term
g_{N}
lying in
H^{k,2N}
.

To prove this theorem, we will need a few lemmas about the operator
|D|
and the function
\rho
.
The first result is that the set of polynomials in
\rho:=\frac{4}{1+\alpha^{2}}
is closed under
|D|
:

Lemma 5.2
.

For any integer
k\geq 1
, there exists a sequence of coefficients
r_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq k+1
such that

|D|\rho^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\rho^{j}.

For the second result, we give a decomposition of
\rho^{\ell}\ast f
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\in H^{k,2N}
for some positive integers
k,N\geq 1
be an even function, then there exists a sequence of coefficients
q_{j}
,
\ell\leq j\leq N
and a function
g_{N}\in H^{\infty,2N}
such that

(5.4)

\rho^{\ell}\ast f=\sum_{j=\ell}^{N}q_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N}.

A direct consequence of above lemma where we choose
f=\rho^{n}
is that the set of polynomials in
\rho
is closed under convolution, up to an error term controllable in an appropriate
H^{k,\sigma}
space.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
1\leq m\leq n
be two positive integers, and let
N\geq m
be a third positive integer.
Then there exists a sequence of coefficients
p_{j}
,
m\leq j\leq N
and a function
g_{N}\in H^{\infty,2N}
such that

(5.5)

\rho^{m}\ast\rho^{n}=\sum_{j=m}^{N}p_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N}.

The next result shows how the operator
|D|
acts on the even functions of
H^{k,2N}
:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f\in H^{k,2N}
for some positive integers
k,N\geq 1
be an even function.
Then there exists a sequence of coefficients
b_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq N
and a function
g_{N}\in H^{k-1,2N}
such that

|D|f=\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N}.

The final lemma, in a similar spirit to the preceding lemma, shows that
(1+|D|)^{-1}
preserves expressions of the form (
5.3
) up to raising the regularity of the remainder by one:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f\in H^{k,2N}
for some positive integers
k,N\geq 1
.
Then there exists a sequence of coefficients
c_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq N
and a function
g_{N}\in H^{k+1,2N}
such that

(5.6)

(1+|D|)^{-1}f=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N}.

We prove Theorem
5.1
assuming above auxiliary results, and the proofs of these lemmas are deferred to the end of this section.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

The asymptotic expansion is constructed inductively.
For our base case
N=1
, we have shown the pointwise bound
|v(\alpha)|\lesssim\langle\alpha\rangle^{-2}.

We first obtain a pointwise bound for
\mathcal{N}(v)
.
We know
\rho
is in
H^{k,1}
for any
k
.
If
v
is in
H^{k,1}
, then, since

\alpha|D|v=|D|(\alpha v)+[\alpha,|D|]v=|D|(\alpha v)-Hv

for all
v
decaying at infinity,
|D|v
is in
H^{k-1,1}
.
The product of two elements of
H^{k,\sigma}
is in
H^{k,2\sigma}
, as long as
k\geq 1
.
Similarly the product of three elements of
H^{k,\sigma}
is in
H^{k,3\sigma}
.

These facts and the explicit formulas (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) tell us that for
v\in H^{2,1}
, we can write
\mathcal{N}(v)
in the form

\mathcal{N}(v)=f_{1}+|D|f_{2},

where
f_{1}\in H^{1,2}
and
f_{2}\in H^{2,2}
by grouping all terms with a
|D|
in front into the
|D|f_{2}
and all products where
|D|
either doesn’t appear or appears in the middle into
f_{1}
.

Lemma
5.2
tells us that all the terms fully in terms of
\rho
can be expressed as a power series in
\rho
, doing the truncation at order
1
and placing the remaining terms in
H^{1,2}
.
Lemma
5.5
tells us that we can write

|D|f_{2}=b_{1}\rho+h_{1}

for some
h_{1}\in H^{1,2}
.

Given an expansion to order
N-1
of the form (
5.3
)

v=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}c_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N-1},

the nonlinear expressions in (
5.2
) are all one of

•

a product of powers of
\rho
,

•

a product of
\rho
with
g_{N-1}
, and thus in
H^{k,2N}
, or

•

the product
g_{N-1}^{2}
, which is in
H^{k,2N}
,

or the derivative of such a term.

Applying Lemmas
5.2
or
5.5
as appropriate, we get an expansion to order
N
of the form (
5.3
)

\mathcal{N}(v)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{c}_{j}\rho^{j}+\tilde{g}_{N}

for some
\tilde{g}_{N}\in H^{k-1,2N}
.

We now apply Lemma
5.6
to this expansion of
\mathcal{N}(v)
and get an expression of the form

v=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\rho^{j}+g_{N}

as desired.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

The function
\rho
can be rewritten as

\rho(\alpha)=\frac{1}{i}\left(\frac{2}{\alpha-i}-\frac{2}{\alpha+i}\right).

As a consequence of fractional decomposition, for any positive integer
\ell
,
\rho^{\ell}
can be decomposed as the sum

(5.7)

\rho^{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\beta^{(\ell)}_{j}(\alpha-i)^{-j}+\gamma^{(\ell)}_{j}(\alpha+i)^{-j},

where
\beta^{(\ell)}_{j}
and
\gamma^{(\ell)}_{j}
are coefficients such that
\beta^{(\ell)}_{j}=\gamma^{(\ell)}_{j}
when
j
is even and
\beta^{(\ell)}_{j}=-\gamma^{(\ell)}_{j}
when
j
is odd.
Furthermore, we can write

\partial_{\alpha}\rho^{k}=\sum_{j=2}^{k+1}-j\beta^{(\ell)}_{j}(\alpha-i)^{-j}-j\gamma^{(\ell)}_{j}(\alpha+i)^{-j}.

An important observation for the Hilbert transform is that

(5.8)

H\left((\alpha-i)^{-j}\right)=-i(\alpha-i)^{-j},\quad H\left((\alpha+i)^{-j}\right)=i(\alpha+i)^{-j}.

Applying (
5.8
) and extracting powers of
\rho
starting at
\rho^{k+1}
using (
5.7
), we can construct a sequence
r_{j}
such that

|D|\rho^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}r_{j}\rho^{j}

as desired.
∎

In the following proofs, the error term
g_{N}
can change from line to line as additional terms are placed into it, but it will always lie in the space claimed in the statement of the corresponding lemma.

We also remark that we will bound the
H^{k,\sigma}
norm of a function
f
by bounding either

\left\|\left(1-\partial_{\alpha}^{2}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(1+|\alpha|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}f\right\|_{L^{2}}

or

\left\|\left(1+|\alpha|^{2}\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}\left(1-\partial_{\alpha}^{2}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}f\right\|_{L^{2}}

depending on which is most convenient at a given point in the proof; both are equivalent to bounding the
H^{k,\sigma}
norm because of the commutation relation between differentiation and multiplication.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

Taking a spatial dyadic decomposition

f\approx\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f_{j},\quad f_{0}=\chi(\alpha)f,\quad f_{j}=\left(\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{2^{j}}\right)-\chi\left(\frac{\alpha}{2^{j-1}}\right)\right)f,

where
\chi
is a positive smooth even bump function that is adapted to
\{|\alpha|\leq 2\}
and equals
1
on the ball
\{|\alpha|\leq 1\}
.
Because
\alpha^{2N}f\in H^{k}
, we have

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2Nj}\|f_{j}\|_{H^{k}}\approx\|f\|_{H^{k,2N}}<\infty.

We work first at the
L^{2}
level and handle derivatives later.
We evaluate the expression
\left(\rho^{\ell}\ast f\right)(\alpha)
by looking at the contributions from
f_{j}
for
2^{j}<|\alpha|
,
2^{j}\approx|\alpha|
, and
2^{j}>|\alpha|
separately.
In the following computations, the implicit constant in the symbol
\lesssim
is always an absolute numerical constant not depending on
j
,
\alpha
, or
f_{2}
and suppressed to make the computation clearer.

Case 1:

2^{j}\ll|\alpha|
.
On the support of
f_{j}
, we can bound
\alpha-\beta
by a constant multiple of
\alpha
, so we have, also using Hölder’s inequality,

\left|\int\frac{f_{j}(\beta)}{\left((\alpha-\beta)^{2}+1\right)^{\ell}}\,d\beta\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{(\alpha^{2}+1)^{\ell}}\int f_{j}(\beta)\,d\beta\lesssim 2^{j/2}\rho^{\ell}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}.

We can replace
\rho^{\ell}(\alpha-\beta)
with powers of
(\alpha-\beta)^{-1}
greater than or equal to
2\ell
.
We write

\frac{1}{\left((\alpha-\beta)^{2}+1\right)^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{(\alpha-\beta)^{2\ell}}+\frac{P_{2\ell-2}(\alpha-\beta)}{\left((\alpha-\beta)^{2}+1\right)^{\ell}(\alpha-\beta)^{2\ell}},

where
P_{2\ell-2}
is a polynomial of degree
2\ell-2
, so that the difference is of order
-2\ell-2
.
Repeating this process with the error term every step until the error is of order
-2N-2
and thus can be safely added to the final remainder
g_{N}
, we can replaced convolution with
\rho^{\ell}
by convolution with a series of even powers of
\alpha^{-1}
.

We can write

(5.9)

\frac{1}{(\alpha-\beta)^{2m}}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2m}}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{2m}}=\sum_{n=2m}^{2N+2}\mu_{n}\frac{1}{\alpha^{n}}(-\beta)^{n-2m}+\frac{\mu_{2N+3}(-\beta)^{2N+3-2m}}{\alpha^{2N+3}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{2m}}

for some coefficients
\mu_{n}
.
Again the convolution with the final term can be placed in the overall error
g_{N}
.

Because
f
is assumed to be even, an odd power of
\beta
integrated against
f_{j}(\beta)
will give
0
, and only the even powers will remain.
For even
n
, we can perform the same decomposition as above and write

\int f_{j}(\beta)\mu_{n}\frac{1}{\alpha^{n}}(-\beta)^{n-2m}d\beta=\frac{\nu_{n,j}}{\alpha^{n}}=\sum_{p=\frac{n}{2}}^{N}\nu_{p,n}\rho^{p}+\eta_{2N+2},

where the remainder
\eta_{2N+2}
is of order
-2N-2
and can thus be added to the overall error
g_{N}
.

Performing this decomposition for each
n
in (
5.9
), and then doing that expansion for each
m
between
\ell
and
N
, inclusive, we get

\rho^{\ell}\ast f_{j}=\sum_{m=\ell}^{N}\lambda_{m}\rho^{m}+g_{N},\qquad g_{N}\in H^{k,2N}.

Case 2:

2^{j}\approx|\alpha|
. We have, by Young’s convolution inequality,

\|\rho^{\ell}\ast f_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\rho^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}.

Case 3:

2^{j}\gg|\alpha|
.
Here we have, bounding the
\rho^{\ell}
term directly by
2^{-2j\ell}
and using Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\left|\int\frac{f_{j}(\beta)}{\left(1+(\alpha-\beta)^{2}\right)^{\ell}}d\beta\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-2j\ell}2^{j/2}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}.

Summing these up over
j
, letting
m
be the value of
j
for which
2^{m-1}<|\alpha|<2^{m}
, we have a pointwise bound

(5.10)

|\left(\rho^{\ell}\ast f\right)(\alpha)|\lesssim\rho^{\ell}(\alpha)\sum_{2^{j}<|\alpha|}2^{j/2}\|\partial^{i}_{\alpha}f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}+\|f_{m}\|_{L^{2}}+\sum_{2^{j-1}\geq|\alpha|}2^{j(1-4\ell)/2}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}.

We now need to show that for
j>\left\lceil\log_{2}|\alpha|\right\rceil
,
\rho^{\ell}\ast f_{j}
can be grouped into the error term
g_{N}
lying in
H^{k,2N}
.

For the case
2^{j}\approx|\alpha|
, we have the estimate

(5.11)

\int\alpha^{4N}\left\|f_{m}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\alpha\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<\infty.

For the case
2^{j}\gg|\alpha|
, we have, bounding the integral in
\alpha
by a sum and rearranging,

\displaystyle\int\alpha^{4N}\left(\sum_{2^{j-1}\geq|\alpha|}2^{j(1-4\ell)/2}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\right)^{2}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{m(1+4N)}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}2^{j(1-4\ell)/2}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}2^{j(1-2\ell+2N)}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}2^{j(2-4\ell)}\right)\left(\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}2^{2Nj}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\right)^{2}.

The first inner sum is a convergent geometric series converging to a constant multiple of
2^{-m(4\ell-2)}
, and the second inner sum can be controlled by
\|f\|_{H^{0,2N}}
.
This means that

(5.12)

\int\alpha^{4N}\left(\sum_{2^{j-1}\geq|\alpha|}2^{j(1-4\ell)/2}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\right)^{2}\,d\alpha\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{0,2N}}^{2}<\infty.

Combining this with (
5.11
) and applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we see that the decomposition (
5.10
) gives the desired decomposition of
\rho^{\ell}\ast f
from (
5.4
) at the
L^{2}
level.

We now prove the remainder term lies in
H^{2K,2N}
for any positive integer
K
.
By interpolation, this ensures that
g_{N}\in H^{\infty,2N}
as desired.

A computation shows that

(5.13)

\partial^{2K}_{\alpha}\rho^{\ell}=\sum_{j=\ell+K}^{\ell+2K}p_{j}\rho^{j}

for some real coefficients
p_{j}
.
Writing

g_{N}=\left(\rho^{\ell}\ast f-\sum_{j=\ell}^{N}q_{j}\rho^{j}\right)

and differentiating
2K
times, placing all
2K
derivatives on the
\rho^{\ell}
term in the convolution, we have

\partial_{\alpha}^{2K}g_{N}=\left(\sum_{j=\ell+K}^{\ell+2K}p_{j}\rho^{j}\ast f-\sum_{j=\ell}^{N}\sum_{n=K}^{2K}q_{j,n}\rho^{j+n}\right),

where
q_{j,n}
are real coefficients arising from applying (
5.13
) to
q_{j}\rho^{j}
in the expansion up to order
N
of
g_{N}
.

Repeating the argument above shows that the remainder when subtracting this new power series in
\rho
from the sum of the convolutions with powers of
\rho
must lie in
H^{0,2N}
.
This means that
g_{N}\in H^{\infty,2N}
as desired.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

By applying Lemma
5.2
inductively, we know that for each integer
i\geq 0
,
|D|^{i}\rho
is a polynomial in
\rho
of degree
i+1
.
This means that for any integer
j\geq 1
, we can find a sequence of coefficients
p_{i}
such that

(5.14)

\rho^{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}p_{i}|D|^{i}\rho.

Using the fact that the Fourier transform of
\rho
equals
\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{-|\xi|}
, (
5.14
) tells us that the Fourier transform of a polynomial in
\rho
is given by a sum of terms of the form
|\xi|^{i}e^{-|\xi|}
:

\mathcal{F}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}\rho^{i}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}q_{i}|\xi|^{i}e^{-|\xi|}.

Homogeneity considerations tell us that, heuristically,
|D|
should act like convolution with
\alpha^{-2}
.
To make this more precise and give a full asymptotic expansion, we re-express its symbol
|\xi|
in a careful way to obtain a more detailed understanding of the (mildly singular) low-frequency behavior.

Using the Taylor expansions at the origin of the terms
|\xi|^{i}e^{-|\xi|}
, we can find coefficients
c_{j}
depending on
N
and a
C^{2N}
function
R_{2N}
such that

(5.15)

|\xi|=c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{2N}c_{j}|\xi|^{j-1}e^{-|\xi|}+R_{2N}(\xi).

Taking the inverse Fourier transform, we see that we can represent
|D|
as a sum of convolution with a multiple of the
\delta
distribution, convolution with a polynomial in
\rho
, and convolution with a remainder term
\check{R}_{2N}(\alpha)
.

Because
R_{2N}(\xi)
grows like
|\xi|
asymptotically, convolution with
\check{R}_{2N}
loses exactly one derivative.
Because
R_{2N}(\xi)
is
2N
-times continuously differentiable in
\xi
, its kernel decays strictly faster than
\alpha^{2N}
and thus convolution with
\check{R}_{2N}
preserves spatial decay of order
\langle\alpha\rangle^{-2N}
.
This means that
\check{R}_{2N}
sends
H^{k,2N}
to
H^{k-1,2N}
.

Since convolution with
\delta
is the identity, it remains to analyze the effect of convolution with a polynomial in
\rho
.
Convolution with a smooth function does not lose any derivatives, so we only need to consider how convolution with a power of
\rho
affects spatial decay.

Given any function
f\in H^{k,2N}
, we can construct the expansion (
5.15
) of
|\xi|
to order
2N
and extract the coefficient on
\rho^{j}
in the final decomposition that comes from (
5.4
) at each order
j
.

The final remainder
g_{N}
is constructed from the
\delta
and
\check{R}_{2N}
terms, the errors from each of the powers
\rho^{j}
, and the terms of the form
\rho^{j}
for
j>N
.
Considering the spaces these contributions lie in, we see that
g_{N}\in H^{k-1,2N}
as claimed.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

As in the proof of the preceding lemma, we approximate the symbol
(1+|\xi|)^{-1}
by functions of the form
|\xi|^{j-1}e^{-|\xi|}
to handle the (mildly singular) behavior at the origin, where the symbol is not smooth,

(5.16)

\frac{1}{1+|\xi|}=\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}(-1)^{j}|\xi|^{j}+\frac{|\xi|^{2N}}{1+|\xi|}.

Taking Taylor expansions around the origin, we can write

(5.17)

\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}(-1)^{j}|\xi|^{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}c_{j}|\xi|^{j-1}e^{-|\xi|}+\tilde{R}_{2N}(\xi),

where
\tilde{R}_{2N}(\xi)
is
2N
times continuously differentiable.
Note that unlike in the previous proof, the constant coefficient
c_{0}
is zero.

The initial error term
\frac{|\xi|^{2N}}{1+|\xi|}
is also
2N
times continuously differentiable since the factors of
\xi
in the numerator absorb the
\delta^{j}
-type singularities from derivatives falling on the denominator.
Moreover, all the
|\xi|^{j-1}e^{-|\xi|}
terms decay at infinity faster than any polynomial, so combining the expansions (
5.16
) and (
5.17
), we have a sequence of coefficients
c_{j}
such that

\frac{1}{1+|\xi|}=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}c_{j}|\xi|^{j-1}e^{-|\xi|}+R_{2N}(\xi),

where
R_{2N}
is a
C^{2N}
function decaying like
|\xi|^{-1}
for large
\xi
.

By repeatedly applying Lemma
5.2
and taking the inverse Fourier transform, we see that we can represent
(1+|D|)^{-1}
as a sum of convolution with a polynomial in
\rho
and convolution with a remainder term
\check{R}_{2N}(\alpha)
.

Convolution with a polynomial in
\rho
gives an expansion of the desired form (
5.6
) with an error term
g_{N}\in H^{k+1,2N}
by Lemma
5.4
.

The remainder term
R_{2N}(\xi)
is in
C^{2N}
and decays like
|\xi|^{-1}
, so convolution with
\check{R}_{2N}
preserves spatial decay and gains one derivative in
L^{2}
, so that
\check{R}_{2N}
is smoothing of order
1
and
\check{R}_{2N}(\alpha)\ast f\in H^{k+1,2N}
.

Summing the contributions to the error from the convolutions with powers of
\rho
(which is in
H^{\infty,2N}
) and the error from convolution with
\check{R}_{2N}
, we have
g_{N}\in H^{k+1,2N}
as desired.
∎

6.
Continuation of solitary waves for velocity

In this section, we discuss the continuation problem to solitary waves with higher speed.
By Theorem
1.1
we know that for velocities close to
-\frac{g}{\gamma}
, a solitary wave exists, and the construction guarantees that it depends continuously on the velocity.
We now ask to what extent this family of solitary waves can be extended continuously as a function of the velocity.

We first investigate the local problem, finding conditions under which the existence of a solitary wave at some velocity
c_{1}
in the above range implies the existence of a nearby solitary wave at a close velocity
c_{2}
.
Using a continuity argument, the local result then
implies a sufficient continuation criteria for the
solitary waves family, and the failure of the local result gives some possible features of the solitary wave of maximal speed (if it exists).
We conclude with the proof of Theorem
1.4
characterizing the behavior near or at the endpoint velocity.

To set the stage for our results, we start
with the Babenko equation at a velocity
c_{1}

as above, as in (
1.4
).

(g+c_{1}\gamma-c_{1}^{2}|D|)U_{1}=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}U_{1}^{2}-gU_{1}|D|U_{1}-\frac{g}{2}|D|U_{1}^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\left(U_{1}|D|U_{1}^{2}-U_{1}^{2}|D|U_{1}-\frac{1}{3}|D|U_{1}^{3}\right).

Given a solution
U_{1}
of velocity
c_{1}
, a key role is played by the associated linearized operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
, defined by

(6.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}w

\displaystyle=\left(g+c_{1}\gamma+\gamma^{2}U_{1}\right)w-c_{1}^{2}|D|w

\displaystyle+g\left(U_{1}|D|w+|D|U_{1}w+|D|(U_{1}w)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\left(2U_{1}|D|(U_{1}w)+w|D|U_{1}^{2}-U_{1}^{2}|D|w-2U_{1}w|D|U_{1}-|D|(U_{1}^{2}w)\right),

which will be used in a fixed point argument to find a solitary wave solution close to
U_{1}
with velocity
c_{2}
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{U_{1}}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}
because
|D|
is self-adjoint and the coefficients given in terms of
U_{1}
and its fractional derivatives are all real.

Using the paradifferential calculus as in Section
3
, we can distribute
|D|
to each factor like
\partial_{\alpha}
.
The third line of (
6.1
) has commutator structures
and will not contribute to the top-order behavior of
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
, so that
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
is elliptic as long as
\sup_{\alpha}U_{1}<\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2g}
.
For
c_{1}
close enough to
-\frac{g}{\gamma}
,

\sup_{\alpha}U_{1}\lesssim|g+c_{1}\gamma|\ll\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2g},

so that
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
is elliptic for velocities close to the critical velocity.

Moreover, because the solitary wave
U_{1}
decays at infinity and
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
is elliptic, its continuous spectrum is determined purely by the constant-coefficient portion
g+c_{1}\gamma-c_{1}^{2}|D|
,
so that the continuous spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
is

(-\infty,g+c_{1}\gamma]
.

To analyze the discrete spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}
, we perform the rescalings from Section
4
, writing

U_{1}=\frac{g+c_{1}\gamma}{\gamma^{2}}\phi\left(\frac{(g+c_{1}\gamma)\alpha}{c_{1}^{2}}\right),

We can now use the upper semicontinuity of the spectrum to guarantee that for

The discrete spectrum of

This eigenvalue can be associated to the translation symmetry of the equation, so by working in the class of even functions (which is preserved under

{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}

{\mathcal{L}}_{U_{1}}

Suppose that for some fixed nonzero vorticity

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The initial construction from Theorem
1.2
and the local continuation result of Proposition

guarantees the existence of an open interval of velocities

or
\left(-\frac{g}{\gamma},c_{M}\right)

Moreover, the fixed point construction shows that the map

from a possible velocity

to

.

If the interval for which solitary waves exist is finite, then this requires the length

\pm\infty
.
This means that if
c_{M}>-\infty

(or

at which

is approaching

By the previous proposition, we know that

in

(2)

of
