Exploring Human-AI Perception Alignment in Sensory Experiences: Do LLMs Understand Textile Hand?
Shu Zhong, Elia Gatti, Youngjun Cho, Marianna Obrist

Abstract
Aligning large language models (LLMs) behaviour with human intent is critical for future AI. An important yet often overlooked aspect of this alignment is the perceptual alignment. Perceptual modalities like touch are more multifaceted and nuanced compared to other sensory modalities such as vision. This work investigates how well LLMs align with human touch experiences using the “textile hand" task. We created a "Guess What Textile" interaction in which participants were given two textile samples –- a target and a reference –- to handle. Without seeing them, participants described the differences between them to the LLM. Using these descriptions, the LLM attempted to identify the target textile by assessing similarity within its high-dimensional embedding space. Our results suggest that a degree of perceptual alignment exists, however varies significantly among different textile samples. For example, LLM predictions are well aligned for silk satin, but not for cotton denim. Moreover, participants didn’t perceive their textile experiences closely matched by the LLM predictions. This is only the first exploration into perceptual alignment around touch, exemplified through textile hand. We discuss possible sources of this alignment variance, and how better human-AI perceptual alignment can benefit future everyday tasks.

A critical but often overlooked necessity to ensuring AI models general alignment with human lies in their

However, perceptual modalities vary in their explicitness and ease of evaluation

. In this work, we are exploring how well LLMs can achieve perceptual alignment with humans in a textile hand task. In other words, we explored how well LLMs can predict the textile the human is handling based on the descriptions of their touch experiences.

To investigate the perceptual alignment between humans and AI for touch, we designed a "Guess What Textile" interaction task and conducted a user study with 40 participants. We focused on the concept of “textiles hand" – the feel of textiles through touch
[
12
,
13
]
, because it reflects an integral everyday task, where a good perceptual alignment would be very desirable. Imagine for example that ChatGPT suggests a wool sweater on a 16-degree day which you find too warm and coarse; can it adjust its recommendation to something cooler, smoother and thinner yet adequately warm?

As part of the study, participants engaged in total of 80 interaction tasks, leading to an overall of 362 guessing attempts by the AI system. We analyzed the AI’s accuracy (i.e., success rate), as well as the participants’ validity and similarity ratings. While there was some degree of human-AI alignment, our results show a bias towards certain textile samples (e.g., silk satin being better aligned than cotton denim), and the need for improvements. We discuss the study results in light of current human-AI alignment approaches, and highlight future research avenues considering the emergence of multimodal LLMs.

In summary, this work makes the following three contributions. We first (1) address the gap in perception alignment between human touch and AI with an in-person user study involving LLMs. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first exploration of the level of alignment between human touch experiences and LLMs. Our approach emphasizes the importance of using comparative measures, such as validity and similarity, which encompass human subjective judgment in an interactive experience. We then (2) map sensory information onto the embedding space and measure representation similarities to encode semantics effectively in LLMs. Our observations indicate that LLMs exhibit perceptual biases across various textiles–showing significantly greater alignment with human perception for certain textiles compared to others. Finally (3) we present the design of an interactive task to study human-AI alignment, mirroring a real-life scenario. While we focused on the sensory experience of textiles, this interactive task can also be used in other everyday sensory interactions, such as selection of foods (e.g. choice between sweet fruits) or perfumes (e.g. recommendation of floral fragrances). Aligning human-AI sensory perception can have a profound impact on people’s daily lives in the future.

3.3
Textile Description Generation

Figure 4:
An overview of the AI Guessing System, i.e. "Guess What Textile?". The vector search process uses pre-built embeddings for 20 textile samples and compares them with a user query-generated vector to identify the best matching textile ID.

As part of our study, we examine the LLM’s behaviors by analysing their embeddings.
Hence, we use model embeddings as part of our AI system design.
Model embeddings, generally derived from the “internal states" of a pre-trained model, offer a way to gauge semantic similarity in the vector space.
Mathematically, an embedding is a learned representation of inputs, designed to capture the semantics of the input data and make it easier to perform other machine learning tasks
[
48
]
. This enables the proximity mapping of semantically ambiguous entities or textual elements to be located close to specific concepts. For instance, a cat and dog are placed closer in the embedding space rather than a cat and a banana.
In our particular case, similar textiles based on their descriptors should cluster in proximity. In other words, we examine whether LLMs grasp the concept of a “softer textile" by checking whether such textiles indeed cluster closely in the LLM’s embedding space.

. For the AI guessing system to make a prediction of the target textile sample, it would have to first encode the 20 possible textiles into LLM embeddings (top row in Figure
4
).
We employ OpenAI’s

to create our embeddings, as this model is among the top-performing on the market.

3.4
AI Guessing System Architecture

We used the 20 textiles descriptions to generate 20 unique vectors (embeddings
\mathcal{E}=\{v_{1},v_{2},...v_{20}\}
) encoded by an LLM. These embeddings, essentially textile descriptions translated into LLMs’ internal representational space, are generated once and repeatedly utilized in the vector search process described in Section
3.4
for each human input throughout the user study.
As illustrated in Figure
4
, each human description of the textile forms a vector that joins the vector search. The user interface is then able to provide a prediction of the target textile from the output of the vector search.

The vector search determines which vector in
\mathcal{E}=\{v_{1},v_{2},...v_{20}\}
has the closet match to the human descriptor
v_{query}
, and outputs its corresponding textile
ID\in\{1,2...20\}
. For instance, if
v_{1}
shows the best match to
v_{query}
in the search, ID
1
would be the output and we will guess
1
(cotton denim as shown in Figure
3
) is what the participant is describing in their textile experience. The process is repeated
t=5
times based on our pilot studies.

Mathematically, the vector search process has the following steps. First, we generate an embedding for the human query
using the predefined embedding model
f_{\text{embed}}

v_{query}=f_{embed}(query)

(1)

where query is the actual post-ASR human input in text form.

In our user study, the participant is randomly assigned with two different textiles (target and reference textiles), as illustrated in. On the other side, the LLM, only knows the start textile and has encoded the start textile in the embedding space
v_{start}\in\mathcal{E}
.
We generate a new predicted embedding vector
v_{predict}
by combining the an initial embedding vector
v_{initial}
and the query embedding vector
v_{query}
and normalize the output:

v_{predict}=\frac{v_{start}+v_{query}}{\|v_{start}+v_{query}\|}

(2)

where
\|v\|
is the Euclidean norm of
v
, calculated as
\sqrt{vv^{T}}
.

Given the predicted embedding
v_{predict}
and a set of 20 embedding vectors
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{20}\}
in an embedding space, the vector search is used to retrieve the nearest neighbors of
v_{predict}
based on the cosine similarity measure from the 20 vectors
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{20}\}
. The cosine similarity between the target vector and any embedding vector
v_{i}
is defined as:

\text{Cos}(v_{predict},v_{i})=\frac{v_{predict}\cdot v_{i}}{\|v_{new}\|\|v_{i}\|}

(3)

The nearest neighbors are retrieved by selecting the top
k
embeddings from
\mathcal{E}
based on their cosine similarity to
v_{predict}
. The
k
nearest neighbors of
v_{\text{new}}
, denoted as
\mathbb{K}
, are the set of
k
vectors from
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{20}\}
that have the highest cosine similarity to
v_{\text{new}}
:

\text{ID}=\text{top\_k}(\{\text{Cos}(v_{new},v_{i})|v_{i}\in\mathcal{E}\},k)

(4)

where
ID
represent the indices or identifiers of the
k
most similar embedding to the query. Since we are interested in the most matching vector, we use
k=1
in Equation
4
and use a single ID to generate a response for the user in Figure
1
.

It is important to note that the
query
from Equation
1
is not replaced but appended with each trial in the single round of the game. This happens when the AI system makes an incorrect prediction and the user proceeds to another trial. Here, previous queries are retained and the new query is appended, along with an added prompt stating,
“[previous query] You were asked to guess with the following additional information because your previous answer was wrong. [new query]"

This design enables the AI model to maintain awareness of past information within the interactive structure.

4
User Study Design

Using the "Guess What Textile?" task, we described in the previous section, we designed a user study to measure the Human-AI alignment for touch.
The aim of the study was to understand how well LLMs can predict the textile the human is handling based on the descriptions of their touch experiences.

4.1
Measuring Human-AI Alignment

To measure the degree of perceptual alignment between the human and LLM, we used the following three evaluation metrics:

\blacksquare

AI Success Rate in the "Guess What Textile?" task

\blacksquare

Validity Score by participants as a subjective assessment

\blacksquare

Similarity Score by participants as a subjective assessment

If the AI correctly identified the target textile within five attempts, we considered it as a success. The maximum attempt number was defined based on internal piloting of the study procedure with six participants. Ideally, the AI system should guess until the correct one. However, if it is in a misalignment situation, the guessing would take a lot of attempts, and participants may run out of descriptors and confidence in doing such a task, making the interaction boring. Hence, for the purpose of this study we set the success rate at five attempts for our analysis.

The two additional subjective measures (validity and similarity scores) captured the participants’ subjective judgement of the AI’s performance, as the accuracy (i.e., success rate) alone does not fully capture the human-AI alignment. For instance, AI might make an incorrect prediction, yet it is still closely aligned with human input. In such cases, we rely on human judgments to gauge the degree of error. A slightly incorrect answer could still indicate strong alignment if the human judges it to be valid and similar. This combination of objective and subjective metrics is unique to our approach, as prior works mainly rely on AI accuracy without human validation.

4.1.1
Validity Scores

For incorrect prediction, participants first rated how valid they found the AI’s interpretation in relation to their given descriptors. For instance, if a participant characterized a textile as "slightly lighter, but more rough and easier to fold [in comparison to the known reference textile]," they then rated the AI’s ability to interpret these specific attributes—lightness, roughness, and foldability. This process aimed to assess the perceptual alignment between human descriptors and AI interpretations. It is the main factor we investigated.

4.1.2
Similarity Scores

Following the assessment of validity, participants evaluated the perceptual similarity between the AI’s guessed textile and the actual target textile. Recognizing that no two textiles in our sample pool are identical, this similarity score focuses on the degree to which the textiles are alike in perceived characteristics.

Participants were asked to rate the similarity of the AI’s guess to the target textile, based on their touch experience. The rating was also done on a 10-point Similarity Score scale as the validity score, where 1 refers to "not similar at all" to 10 being "completely similar". This scale helps in understanding how closely the AI’s interpretations match the actual textile samples. Please see more details in user interface presented to participants (see Figure

4.2
Study Setup

We developed an user interface (UI), depicted in Figure
5
, to integrate our AI Guessing system. This interface facilitates participant engagement in the textile hand task.

In the user study and throughout this section, we define a complete interactive cycle — from initiation ("start the game") to conclusion ("game over") — as a single game session. Each prediction made by the AI guessing system represents an attempt to identify target sample. Each attempt will result in a guessed textile.

The "Start New Game" button incorporates a function to randomly assign two textile samples from a predefined pair of textiles. This function ensures comprehensive coverage of all textile samples under consideration. It is designed so that each textile sample is designated as the target textile four times. Additionally, the initial reference textiles for each target textile are distributed across the four meta-categories outlined in Section
3.3
, ensuring a broad and diverse examination of textiles.

4.3
User Study Procedure

Participants first went through a familiarization phase to get comfortable with describing their touch experiences. Participants were asked to handle a single textile sample and describe their tactile sensations to the AI system verbally. Participants were instructed to focus on sensory characteristics and were asked to avoid from directly mentioning the name of the textile itself. For example, instead of saying, "This feels like wool," they were encouraged to explain the sensations that led to this conclusion, such as "This feels like a bit furry, itchy warm, and slightly coarse".

When participants felt comfortable with the setup, we proceeded to the main study part, a comparative textile hand task using the "Guess What Textile?". As part of explaining the study to participants, we referred to this task as a "game" – a guessing game between you, the participant, and the AI system. The user interface presented to the participants that embeds the AI guessing system introduced in Section
3.3
.

Participants were presented with two textiles: a reference textile sample, which was known to the AI, and a target textile sample the AI had to identify based on participants’ descriptions. This comparative task was initiated by the researcher by pressing the "start the game" button as depicted in Figure
5(a)
. Participants then explored the textiles with their hands and informed the researcher when they were ready to describe their textile touch experience. The research activated the "click to speak" button on behalf of participants to enable microphone. This action triggered a refresh of the GUI, as shown in Figure
5(b)
, enabling participants to start describing the target textile to the interface. Then the AI guessing system takes the audio from microphone directly as inputs and make a prediction. The interface present its guessed textile number as "Are you having number
n
?" and read it aloud at the same time using the speaker, meanwhile participants’ description will be present on the GUI as illustrated in Figure
5(c)
. Later the research click "Rate the response" with a window showing "Is the AI guess correct?".

If the AI got correct, the task is accomplished. Otherwise, participants were presented with the guessed textile as a new reference textile, and asked to rate the validity and similarity scores based on the new reference textile as shown in Figure
5(d)
and Figure
5(e)
. Participants will start a new attempt on comparative task with the new reference textile and repeat above process. For AI’s incorrect prediction, a "Game Over" window will also pop up when the interactive describing and guessing reach 5 attempts to signal this is the end of one task.

4.4
Participants

A total of 40 participants (30 female, 10 male; aged 18-39, mean = 25.79, std = 4.12) were recruited for the in-person user study. None of the participants had any sensory or motor impairments that would affect their perception and handling of the textile samples. Participants had a diverse range of backgrounds, including psychology students, computer scientists, designers, artists, researchers, and university lecturers. All participants were either native English speakers or highly proficient in English. All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study and were compensated with a gift voucher for their participation in a 30-minutes study.
The study was approved by the local University Research Ethics Committee (UCLIC_2021_014_ObristPE).

5
Results and Discussion

We analyzed 80 "Guess What Textile?" tasks with 362 attempts (avg 4.53 attempts per task, std = 1.41) completed by 40 participants. Below we present the overall alignment based on our three measurements and then further detail the results for textile-specific performance comparisons.

5.1
Overall Alignment Performance

5.1.1
Success Rates

The primary performance indicators for AI alignment are based on the AI’s success rates across 80 completed tasks. The success rate is measured by counting the number of AI’s successful predictions of a textile and dividing this number by the total tasks completed. The AI correctly predicted the target textile in 18 out of the 80 completed tasks, resulting in an overall accuracy rate of 22.5%.
For the tasks where the AI was successful, it took an average of three attempts (std=1.20) to make a correct prediction. We further discuss the variance across textiles in Section
5.2
.

5.1.2
Validity and Similarity Scores

We analyzed both validity in each response and similarity in each comparison between the reference and target samples. If AI made a correct prediction, this means the prediction is completely valid (10) and completely similar (10). We thus only looked at attempts where the AI failed to make correct predictions. In essence, these two metrics function as a gauge through which humans assess the degree of inaccuracy in the AI’s predictions.

The AI’s predictions received an average validity score of 5.25 (std= 1.71), a moderate level of validity in the AI’s predictions as evaluated by the participants. With regards to the similarity ratings, the average similarity score across all comparisons was 4.77 (std=1.67). The distribution of validity and similarity scores are shown in Figure
6
. There appears to be a correlation between validity and similarity scores, with the highest frequency at a score of 1 and the second-highest at a score of 8, as illustrated in Figure

In our user study, we added two human subjective metrics for the participants that are the Validity and Similarity Scores as introduced in Section

For example, in our setup, the LLM might guess a relevant but incorrect textile based on the descriptions from human participants; however, if humans judge this relevance to be high enough, it suggests that a higher-level alignment is still present.

For instance, as observed in Figure

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we explored human-AI, specifically human-LLM, perceptual alignment using the textile hand concept. We developed a “Guess What Textile" interactive task and conducted an in-person user study with 40 participants.
Our results suggest some level of perceptual alignment, however we observed a bias of the LLM across various textiles. We observed that there is significantly greater alignment with human perception for certain textiles compared to others (e.g., silk satin versus cotton denim).
In our discussion of this exploratory work, we highlight that LLMs are still in their infancy concerning sensory judgment, particularly in the realm of tactile perception.

While, we contributed initial insights into the understanding of human-AI touch alignment, we have to also acknowledge some limitations.First, subjective sensory judgement inherently can vary widely among individuals
[
52
]
.
This diversity has long presented challenges in standardizing evaluations and design metrics that precisely encapsulate the depth of subjective experiences. Hence, this also affects our alignment measures. We have added new measures, especially the validity score, to our study, extending prior works; yet additional qualitative measures would shed light on the quality differences in subjective touch experiences.
Second, our study was confined to a limited selection of 20 textile samples, focusing specifically on tasks related to the feel of textiles. While this sample was selected out of a set of originally 100 samples, there is still scope to extend the choices to enrich the embedding space.

Furthermore, the advent of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), such as KOSMOS-1
[
53
]
, represents a major leap in emerging multimodal learning—including multimodal dialogue, image captioning, visual question answering, and vision tasks. While our study explores perceptual alignment in foundational language models, MLLMs’ ability to process multimodal inputs offers a richer information landscape. Future research can now investigate the potential of MLLMs to enhance human-AI perceptual alignment, exploring how these advanced models can enhance our understanding of multimodal human-AI interaction for everyday tasks, where AI products and services are increasingly embedded into many devices, beyond choosing clothing.
