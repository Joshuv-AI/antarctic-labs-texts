Determinantal point processes associated with Bergman kernels: Construction and limit theorems
Thibaut Lemoine

Abstract
We study determinantal point processes whose correlation kernel is the Bergman kernel of a high power of a positive Hermitian holomorphic line bundle over a compact complex manifold. We construct such processes in analogy to the orthogonal ensembles in random matrix theory, where the correlation kernel is the famous Christoffel–Darboux kernel. Using a near-diagonal expansion of the Bergman kernel, we prove that the scaling limit of these point processes is given by a multidimensional generalization of the infinite Ginibre ensemble. As an application, we obtain a convergence in probability of their empirical measures to an equilibrium measure related to the complex Monge–Ampère equations. We finally establish a large deviation principle for weighted versions of these processes, whose rate function is the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the Mabuchi functional.

Introduction

Determinantal point processes (DPPs) are a class of random configurations on a space
E
, whose points are guided by a repulsive interaction. They were first introduced by Macchi
[
49
]
as a general model, but many specific examples have been studied earlier, such as some classical models in random matrix theory
[
59
,
26
,
27
,
31
]
. One of the first motivations comes from quantum mechanics, where the wavefunction of a particle is described by
\psi:E\to{\mathbb{C}}
and its squared modulus is interpreted (up to renormalization) as a probability density with respect to a reference measure on
X
. Given an orthonormal family
(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N})
of such wavefunctions, assuming that each one represents a fermion, one can define a
N
-particle wavefunction by taking the Slater determinant as follows:

(1.1)

\Psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det(\psi_{i}(x_{j})).

The squared norm of this wavefunction is again interpreted (up to renormalization) as a probability density, the joint density of the
N
fermions. If we denote by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
their positions in
E
, the point process
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}}
is a determinantal point process (DPP),
i.e.
for any
1\leqslant n\leqslant N
its
n
-point correlation functions are given by

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\det(K(x_{i},x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n},

where the function
K:E\times E\to{\mathbb{C}}
is the reproducing kernel of
L^{2}(E)

K(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}(x)\overline{\psi_{i}}(y).

Determinantal processes arise in many mathematical contexts, from the eigenvalues of random matrices to zeros of Gaussian analytic functions
[
35
]
. Recently, DPPs have found applications in wireless networks
[
51
]
, machine learning
[
42
]
and numerical integration
[
2
]
. In this paper, we define and study determinantal point processes arising from the geometric quantization of compact complex manifolds, following the seminal papers
[
6
,
7
]
by Berman. Consider a compact complex manifold
M
of dimension

and a holomorphic line bundle
L\to M
, and assume that there is a Hermitian metric

on

with local weight
\phi
, whose curvature

-form

, or to a real (1,1)-form

which is the exact equivalent of (

-particle function can be interpreted as a section of the line bundle

. As in the general case, we can consider the reproducing kernel

As we will see, taking the norm of the
N_{k}
-particle wavefunction defined in (

1.2.1
Random matrices and orthogonal ensembles

In random matrix theory, many classical models satisfy an orthogonal or a unitary distributional invariance. The eigenvalues of such matrices can be interpreted as interacting particles, and their associated point process is simple as long as they are distinct almost-surely. or to the strong circular law for the Ginibre ensemble.

Theorem 1.7
.

The expectation of the empirical measure of zeros of Kac polynomials converge to the uniform measure on the unit circle as
k\to\infty
.

Theorem
1.6
has been recently generalized to many other cases by Butez and García-Zelada
[
18
]
using the model of jellium. If
\nu
is a rotationally-invariant measure on
{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\big|\log|z|\big|d\nu(z)<\infty,

a
jellium associated to

\nu
corresponds to a system of
N
interacting particles at equilibrium whose positions
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in{\mathbb{C}}
have distribution

\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\prod_{i<j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}e^{-2(N+1)\sum_{i}V^{\nu}(x_{i})}dv_{{\mathbb{C}}^{N}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

where the potential
V^{\nu}:{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

V(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log|z-w|d\nu(w).

Theorem 1.8

(
[
18
]
, Theorem 1.3)
.

Let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
be a jellium associated to
\nu
. Let
U
be a connected component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
and suppose that it is either an open disk or the complement of a closed disk. Then the sequence of point processes
\{x_{k}\text{ such that }x_{k}\in U\}
converges weakly, as
N\to\infty
, to a determinantal point process with kernel
B_{U}
.

They related this model to zeros of random polynomials defined by

(1.14)

P_{N}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}R_{k,N}(z),

where
(a_{k})
is a family of i.i.d. random variables and
(R_{k,N})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant N}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{C}}_{N}[X]
for the inner product

\langle P,Q\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{C}}P(z)\overline{Q}(z)e^{-2NV^{\nu}(z)}d\nu(z).

Theorem 1.9

(
[
18
]
, Theorem 1.7)
.

Assume that
(P_{N})_{N}
is a sequence of random polynomials of the form (
1.14
), such that almost-surely
a_{0}\neq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[\log(1+|a_{0}|)]<\infty
. Let
U
be a connected component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\mathrm{supp}(\nu)
and suppose that it is either a disk or the complement of a closed disk.

–

If
U=D_{R}
for
R>0
, then

\{z_{k}\text{ such that }z_{k}\in U\}\Rightarrow R\cdot(Z_{f}\cap D),

where
f
is the random analytic function defined on
U
by
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
.

–

If
U={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{D_{R}}
for
R>0
, then

\{z_{k}\text{ such that }z_{k}\in U\}\Rightarrow R\cdot(Z_{\tilde{f}}\cap D),

where
\tilde{f}
is the random analytic function defined on
U
by
\tilde{f}(z)=f(z^{-1})
.

This theorem implies that if
U=D_{R}
and
(a_{k})
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian random variables, then set
\{z_{k}\text{ such that }z_{k}\in U\}
converges weakly to a determinantal process with kernel
K_{D_{R}}
.

Let us mention two universality results that look like Theorem
1.1
, due to Bleher and Di: let
P_{k}
be a random polynomial of the form

P_{k}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sqrt{\binom{k}{j}}c_{j}x^{j},

where
c_{j}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables
[
10
]
or i.i.d. random variables with zero mean and unit variance such that the characteristic function of
c_{j}
satisfies a few conditions
[
11
]
. The correlations functions of the real zeros of
P_{k}
verify the following scaling limit:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k^{d/2}}\rho_{n}\left(x+\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{k}},\ldots,x+\frac{u_{n}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\rho_{n}^{\infty}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}),

where the limit
\rho_{n}^{\infty}
is “universal”, in the sense that it is common to a broad class of models. More results about the universality of zeros of random polynomials can be found in
[
57
]
.

1.2.3
Random holomorphic sections

Let
M
be a compact complex manifold of dimension
n
,
L\to M
a holomorphic line bundle with a smooth Hermitian metric
h
such that
\omega=\frac{i}{2\pi}\Theta_{h}
is a Kähler form. For a large integer
k
, one considers the Hilbert space
H^{0}(M,L^{k})
and endows it with the following probability measure:

d\mu(s)=\frac{1}{\pi^{N_{k}}}e^{-|c|^{2}}dc_{1}\cdots dc_{N_{k}},

for any section
s=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}c_{k}S_{j}^{(k)}\in H^{0}(M,L^{k})
decomposed on an orthonormal basis
(S_{\ell}^{(k)})
. As in the case of polynomials, we denote by
Z_{s}
the set of zeros of the section
s
; we also denote by
\rho_{n,m}^{(k)}
the
n
-point correlation function of the random set
Z_{s_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap Z_{s_{m}}
, where
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
are
m
independent random sections of
L^{k}
with measure
\mu
.

Theorem 1.10

(
[
12
,
13
]
)
.

Let
M
be a compact complex manifold with the Kähler form
\omega=\frac{i}{2\pi}\Theta_{h}
corresponding to a Hermitian holomorphic line bundle
(L,h)
. Let
\mathcal{S}=H^{0}(M,L^{k})^{n}
, for
n\geqslant 1
, endowed with the standard Gaussian measure
\mu
. Then the
n
-point correlation functions
\rho_{n,m}^{(k)}
satisfy the following universality in normal coordinates around
z\in M
:

\frac{1}{k^{nd}}\rho_{n,m}^{(k)}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{k}},\ldots,\frac{u_{n}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=K_{n,m,d}^{\infty}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})+O(k^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

where the function
K_{n,m,d}^{\infty}
is a rational function expressed using the infinite Ginibre kernel
B_{\infty}(u,v)
and its partial derivatives.

This result is the counterpart of Theorem
1.1
and is also based on the asymptotic expansion of the Bergman kernel. An analog of Corollary
1.3
is given by the following convergence obtained by Shiffman and Zelditch.

Theorem 1.11

(
[
53
]
, Theorem.1.1)
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.10
, for
\mu
-almost all
s=\{s_{k}\}\in\mathcal{S}
,

\frac{1}{k}Z_{s_{k}}\longrightarrow\omega,

weakly in the sense of measures.

Many generalizations of this theorem occured since then, some of which are given in
[
61
]
for so-called
quantum ergodic sections
, and some others in
[
3
]
. In a quite different direction, we can also mention the recent paper
[
1
]
by Ancona and Le Floch, where they consider the zeros of
T_{k}s_{k}
, where
s_{k}
is a Gaussian random holomorphic section of
L^{k}
and

is a Berezin–Toeplitz operator, which is the quantum observable operators corresponding to a classical observable

.

Theorem 1.12

(
[
1
]
, Theorem 1.9)
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
be a smooth function vanishing transversally and let
T_{k}
be a Berezin–Toeplitz operator with principal symbol

. Then, as

,

\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}[Z_{T_{k}s}]\longrightarrow\frac{\omega}{2\pi}

weakly in the sense of currents, and

\mathbb{E}[Z_{T_{k}s}]-\frac{k\omega}{2\pi}\longrightarrow\frac{i}{2\pi}\partial\overline{\partial}\log f^{2}

2
Background

In this section, we introduce all probabilistic and geometric tools needed for Theorem
1.1
, Corollary
1.3
, and Theorem
1.4
: determinantal point processes, Bergman kernels on compact complex manifolds, and finally large deviations. As this paper aims to be understood by both probabilists and by geometers, who often have quite different backgrounds, we will deliberately recall a few standard definitions and results that might seem trival or unnecessary for some readers, and they can skip parts of this section without harm.

2.1
Determinantal point processes

There have been many extensive presentations of determinantal point processes in the early 2000s
[
54
,
46
,
38
,
15
,
35
]
and most of what we will present here, as well as lots of further results, can be found in these references. We will first present point processes and their correlation functions, then we will define determinantal point processes.

Definition 2.1

(Simple point process as random measures)
.

Let
E
be a locally compact Polish space endowed with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(E)
. A
simple discrete measure
on
E
is a measure
\mu
on
E
defined by

\mu=\sum_{i\in I}\delta_{x_{i}},

where
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is a family of distinct elements of
E
with no accumulation point, indexed by a finite or countable set
I
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{d}(E)
the set of simple discrete measures on
E
. If
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space, a
simple point process
on
E
is a random variable
\mu:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(E)
.

Definition 2.2

(Simple point process as a random configuration)
.

Let
E
be a locally compact Polish space endowed with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(E)
,
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and
N
be a positive integer. A
configuration
on
E
is a locally finite subset of
E
, and the set of configurations on
E
is denoted by
\mathrm{Conf}(E)
. A
simple point process
on
E
is a random configuration
X:\Omega\to\mathrm{Conf}(E)
.

A configuration is often used in statistical mechanics to describe systems of particles. The equivalence between Definitions
2.1
and
2.2
follows from the fact that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathrm{Conf}(E)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{M}_{d}(E)\\
X&\longmapsto&\sum_{x\in X}\delta_{x}\end{array}\right.

is a bijection, and the condition of locally finiteness of the configuration
\{x_{i},i\in I\}
is equivalent to the absence of accumulation points. In the following, all point processes will be assumed to be simple, so that we can interpret them as random configurations. The first interesting observable of point process is the
number of particles
belonging in a Borel subset
B\subset E
: it is the random variable

\mu(B):\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\Omega&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{N}}\\
\omega&\longmapsto&\int_{B}\mu_{\omega}(dx)=\#(B\cap X(\omega)).\end{array}\right.

In this article, we will rather focus on a family of observables that describe the interactions of the system of particles.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu(E)}
be a simple point process on
E
. For any integer
n\geqslant 1
, the
n
-th
factorial moment measure
of
\mu
is the measure
\mu^{(n)}
on
E^{n}
defined as

\mu^{(n)}=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant\mu(E)\\
i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}\end{subarray}}\delta_{X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{n}}}\bigg].

If
\lambda
is a Radon measure on
E
and
\mu^{(n)}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda^{\otimes n}
, then the
n
point
correlation function
of
\mu
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{d\mu^{(n)}}{d\lambda^{\otimes n}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Let us be more explicit about the factorial moment measures and correlation functions, assuming that
\mu(E)=N<\infty
almost-surely. If
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
are Borel subsets of
E
, then

\mu^{(n)}(B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant N\\
i_{1}\neq\cdots\neq i_{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}[X_{i_{1}}\in B_{1},\ldots,X_{i_{n}}\in B_{n}].

In particular, for any
B=B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{N}
Borel subset of
E^{N}
,

\mu^{(N)}(B)=N!\mathbb{P}(X\subset B).

It is also possible to describe the correlation functions in terms of test functions. Indeed, if
f:E^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, then

\int_{E^{n}}f\rho_{n}d\lambda^{\otimes n}=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}\leqslant N}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i_{\sigma(1)}},\ldots,X_{i_{\sigma(n)}})].

In practice, if a point process is described as a random family of points, the following result by Johansson
[
38
]
is helpful to compute correlation functions from the joint density of the random points.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
be an integer, and
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}}
a point process on
E
such that
\mu(E)=N
almost-surely. Assume that the random family
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
has density
f
with respect to
\lambda^{\otimes N}
, where
f:E^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a symmetric function. Then for all
n\geqslant 1
, the correlation functions of
\mu
exist and they satisfy

(2.1)

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\frac{N!}{(N-n)!}\int\limits_{E^{N-n}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\lambda^{\otimes N-n}(dx_{n+1}\cdots dx_{N})&\text{if }n\leqslant N,\\
0&\text{if }n>N.\end{array}\right..

Definition 2.4
.

A point process
\mu
on
E
is
determinantal
of kernel
K:E^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
if for any
n\geqslant 1
, its
n
-point correlation function satisfies

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\det(K(x_{i},x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}.

Depending on the kernel
K
, there may or not exist such a determinantal point process (often shortened as DPP); the following theorem, found in
[
54
]
, provides a sufficient condition on
K
for its existence.

Theorem 2.2
.

If
K
is the kernel of a hermitian locally trace class operator on
L^{2}(E,\lambda)
, then there exists a determinal point process with kernel
K
if and only if
\mathrm{Spec}(K)\subset[0,1]
. If the DPP exists, then it is unique.

A particular class of determinantal point processes associated to a hermitian kernel is the class of
orthogonal ensembles
. They are related to reproducing kernels on finite-dimensional subspaces of
L^{2}(E,\lambda)
. One may find many results about them in
[
17
]
and
[
16
]
, in particular the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(\phi_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}
be an orthonormal family of
L^{2}(E,\lambda)
. The kernel

K_{N}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}E^{2}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{C}}\\
(x,y)&\longmapsto&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}(x)\overline{\phi_{i}(y)}\end{array}\right.

is a reproducing kernel on the subspace
\mathcal{H}
of
L^{2}(E,\lambda)
generated by
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}
. Moreover, if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are random variables on
E
of joint distribution

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{1}{N!}|\det(\phi_{i}(x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}|^{2}d\lambda^{\otimes N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

then the simple point process
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}}
is determinantal with kernel
K_{N}
. It is called the
orthogonal ensemble
of kernel
K_{N}
, and
K_{N}
is called the
Christoffel–Darboux kernel
on
E
with respect to
\lambda
.

In most known cases, the first way to understand the large
N
behaviour of orthogonal ensembles is to compute the limit of
K_{N}
(up to rescaling) when
N
tends to infinity. For instance, if
(P_{i})
is a family of orthogonal polynomials for the measure
\lambda
, then
(\phi_{i})
is an orthonormal family of
L^{2}(E,\lambda)
with
\phi_{i}=\frac{P_{i}}{\|P_{i}\|}
. If
E=\mathbb{R}
the associated kernel
K_{N}
satisfies the celebrated Christoffel–Darboux formula
[
55
, Thm 3.2.2]
:

(2.2)

K_{N}(x,y)=\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N}}\frac{\phi_{N}(x)\phi_{N-1}(y)-\phi_{N-1}(x)\phi_{N}(y)}{x-y},

where
a_{i}
is the leading coefficient of the polynomial
\phi_{i}
. An analog holds as well when
E={\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)=S^{1}
. In classical cases (Jacobi, Hermite, Laguerre), this formula is one of the main ingredients to study the asymptotics of
K_{N}
when
N
tends to infinity. Unfortunately, there is no such formula for Kähler manifold, and our approach will instead be based on direct asymptotic results on the Bergman kernel obtained in the last 20 years using various complex geometry techniques.

2.2
Complex manifolds and Hermitian line bundles

The aim of this section is to recall a few facts about complex manifolds, and it does not include any particular novelty. Good references for this content are
[
37
]
for an introduction,
[
22
]
for a more analytic point of view, or
[
32
]
for a more algebraic-geometric point of view.

Definition 2.5
.

A
n
-dimensional
complex manifold
is a topological space
M
endowed with a family
(U_{i},\phi_{i})_{i\in I}
of open subsets
U_{i}\subset M
and homeomorphisms
\phi_{i}:U_{i}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that, if
U_{i}\cap U_{j}\neq\varnothing
, then

\phi_{i}\circ\phi_{j}^{-1}:\phi_{j}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})\to\phi_{i}(U_{i}\cap U_{j})

is a biholomorphism between subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

Similarly, one defines a
n
-dimensional smooth real manifold by replacing
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and biholomorphism by smooth diffeomorphism. In particular, if
M
is a
n
-dimensional complex manifold, then it is also a
2n
-dimensional real manifold using the bijection
{\mathbb{C}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For any
x\in M
, the tangent space
T_{x}M
is the tangent space of the underlying real manifold, hence it is a
\mathbb{R}
-vector space; its complexified
{\mathbb{C}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}T_{x}M=T_{x}M\oplus iT_{x}M
splits into conjugate complex subspaces which are the eigenspace of the endomorphism
Id\otimes J
associated to the eigenvalues
i
and
-i
, where
J
is the operator of multiplication by
i
. These subspaces are

T_{x}^{(1,0)}M={\mathrm{Span}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}},1\leqslant k\leqslant n\right),\ T_{x}^{(0,1)}M={\mathrm{Span}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}_{k}},1\leqslant k\leqslant n\right).

By duality we have a similar splitting of the cotangent space, so we can define
(1,0)
-forms with the base
(dz_{k})
and
(0,1)
-forms with the base
(d\overline{z}_{k})
. Using the wedge product, we can then construct
(p,q)
-forms in a functorial way; we denote by
\Omega^{(p,q)}(M)
the space of
(p,q)
-differential forms on
M
, with the convention
\Omega^{(0,0)}(M)=\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M,{\mathbb{C}})
. In local coordinate, a
(p,q)
-form
\omega
of class
\mathscr{C}^{k}
can be written

\omega(z)=\sum_{|I|=p,|J|=q}\omega_{I,J}(z)dz_{I}\wedge d\overline{z}_{j},\ \text{with}\ \omega_{I,J}\in\mathscr{C}^{k}.

We define differential operators on such forms
\partial:\Omega^{(p,q)}(M)\to\Omega^{(p+1,q)}(M)
and
\overline{\partial}:\Omega^{(p,q)}(M)\to\Omega^{(p,q+1)}(M)
by

\partial\omega=\sum_{I,J}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\omega_{I,J}}{\partial z_{k}}dz_{k}\wedge dz_{I}\wedge d\overline{z}_{J},\ \overline{\partial}\omega=\sum_{I,J}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\omega_{I,J}}{\partial\overline{z}_{k}}dz_{k}\wedge dz_{I}\wedge d\overline{z}_{J}.

The real exterior derivative
n
satisfies the following splitting property:

d=\partial+\overline{\partial},

and we define another real operator on differential forms by
d^{c}=\frac{1}{2i\pi}(\partial-\overline{\partial})
, so that
{dd^{c}}=\frac{i}{\pi}\partial\overline{\partial}
. Note that any
(n,n)
-form
\omega
that does not vanish on any
x\in M
defines a measure on Borel sets
U\in\mathcal{B}(M)
by

(2.3)

\mu(U)=\int_{U}\omega,

and it coincides with the definition of the volume form of the underlying
2n
-dimensional smooth manifold. Note that any complex manifold is canonically oriented because biholomorphic maps preserve the orientation, therefore a volume form always exists, and all volume forms yield measures that are absolutely continuous with eachother, with nowhere-vanishing Radon–Nikodym derivatives. In this article, we will follow the usual convention that identifies any volume form with the corresponding Borel measure.

Definition 2.6
.

A
holomorphic line bundle
over a compact complex manifold
M
is a complex manifold
L
endowed with a holomorphic map
\pi:L\to M
such that any fiber
L_{x}=\pi^{-1}(\{x\})
is a one-dimensional complex vector space for
x\in M
, with the following extra condition: there exists an open covering
(U_{i})_{i\in I}
of
M
and biholomorphic maps
\psi_{i}:\pi^{-1}(U_{i})\cong U_{i}\times{\mathbb{C}}
commuting with the projection
\pi
and so that the induced map
L_{x}\cong{\mathbb{C}}
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear.

If
L\to M
is a holomorphic line bundle with the associated open covering
(U_{i},\psi_{i})
, then the functions
\psi_{i}
are called
trivialization functions
and the
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear functions

\gamma_{ji}(x):z\mapsto(\psi_{j}\circ\psi_{i}^{-1})(x,z)

are called the
transition functions
for any
i,j
and any
x\in U_{i}\cap U_{j}
. A
holomorphic section
of
L\to M
is a holomorphic function
s:M\to L
such that
\pi\circ s=id_{M}
. The space of holomorphic sections of
L
is denoted by
H^{0}(M,L)
and is a complex vector space of finite dimension
N
. A section
s\in H^{0}(M,L)
is entirely characterized by local holomorphic functions
f_{i}:U_{i}\to{\mathbb{C}}
that satisfy the compatibility conditions

(2.4)

f_{j}(x)=\gamma_{ji}(x)f_{i}(x),\ \forall x\in U_{i}\cap U_{j}.

For instance, for any
i\in I
set
f_{i}=\mathrm{pr}_{2}\circ\psi_{i}\circ s|_{U_{i}}
, where
\mathrm{pr}_{2}:U_{i}\times{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is the projection on the second coordinate: these functions satisfy (
2.4
). By construction, we have then

(2.5)

\psi_{i}\circ s(x)=(x,f_{i}(x)),\ \forall x\in U_{i}.

Another way of dealing with sections is to use
local frames
: if
e^{(i)}
is a nonvanishing section of
L
over
U_{i}
, then there are holomorphic functions
f_{i}:U_{i}\to{\mathbb{C}}
such that

s(x)=f_{i}(x)e^{(i)}(x),\ \forall x\in U_{i}.

In this case, the compatibility condition (
2.4
) implies

f_{j}(x)e^{(j)}(x)=\gamma_{ji}(x)f_{i}(x)e^{(j)}(x)=f_{i}(x)e^{(i)}(x),\ \forall x\in U_{i}\cap U_{j},

so that
f_{i},f_{j},e^{(i)},
and
e^{(j)}
are related through
\gamma_{ji}
. If two line bundles
\pi_{1}:L_{1}\to M
and
\pi_{2}:L_{2}\to M
are isomorphic, then so are
H^{0}(M,L_{1})
and
H^{0}(M,L_{2})
: assuming that for any
x\in M
there is an isomorphism
\Phi_{x}:(L_{1})_{x}\simeq(L_{2})_{x}
, then we have the isomorphism
\Phi:H^{0}(M,L^{1})\to H^{0}(M,L^{2})
defined by

\Phi\circ s(x)=\Phi_{x}(s(x)),\ \forall s\in H^{0}(M,L^{1}),\ \forall x\in M.

If
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are two holomorphic line bundles over
M
, we have then the canonical isomorphism

(2.6)

H^{0}(M,L_{1}\otimes L_{2})\cong H^{0}(M,L_{2}\otimes L_{1}).

It also holds for the external tensor product: if
L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}
are line bundles over
M
and
\mathrm{pr}_{i}:M^{k}\to M
is the projection onto the
i
-th coordinate, then the
external tensor bundle

L_{1}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes L_{k}

Definition 2.7

2.6
The Bergman kernel and its determinant

Let
M

Theorem 2.4

(

]
, Theorem B.2.7)

Let

be two vector bundles on

A:\mathscr{C}_{0}^{\infty}(M,E)\to\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(M,F)

Moreover, for any volume form

Definition 2.11
.

The
Bergman projection
is the orthogonal projection

P_{(\phi,\mu)}:L^{2}(M,L)\longrightarrow H^{0}(M,L),

and the Schwartz kernel
B_{(\phi,\mu)}
of

with respect to

.

Note that the kernel

is in particular the

of the Hilbert space

endowed with the inner product (

), as a consequence of (

.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(s_{\ell})
be an orthonormal basis of
H^{0}(M,L)
for
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(\phi,\mu)}
. The Bergman kernel can be written

(2.15)

B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}s_{\ell}(x)\otimes\overline{s_{\ell}}(y)\in L_{x}\otimes\overline{L_{y}},

In particular, on the diagonal, it defines a global function

(2.16)

B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\|s_{\ell}(x)\|_{\phi}^{2}.

Proof.

By unicity of the reproducing kernel of the Hilbert space
H^{0}(M,L^{k})
, we only need to prove that the kernel defined in (
2.15
) is reproducing on
H^{0}(M,L^{k})
. We have

\int_{M}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}s_{\ell}(x)\otimes\overline{s_{\ell}}(y)\right)s_{m}(y)d\mu(y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\int_{M}(s_{\ell}(x)\otimes\overline{s_{\ell}}(y))(1\otimes s_{m}(y))d\mu(y).

The RHS can be identified, according to Section
2.5
, to

\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\int_{M}h_{y}^{k}(s_{m}(y),s_{\ell}(y))d\mu(y)s_{\ell}(x),

and by orthonormality we obtain

\int_{M}B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,y)s_{m}(y)d\mu(y)=s_{\ell}(x).

We can extend this by linearity to get the reproducing property for all elements of
H^{0}(M,L)
. Equation (
2.16
) follows from similar arguments.
∎

The reproducing property of the Bergman kernel has a few direct consequences. For instance,

(2.17)

\int_{M}B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,x)d\mu(x)=\sum_{\ell}\|s_{\ell}\|_{\phi}^{2}=N,

and for any
x,z\in M

(2.18)

\int_{M}B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,y)B_{(\phi,\mu)}(y,z)d\mu(y)=B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,z).

In the last equation, the product
B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,y)B_{(\phi,\mu)}(y,z)
can be interpreted as follows: we have
B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,y)\in L_{x}\otimes\overline{L_{y}}\cong\mathrm{Hom}(L_{y},L_{x})
and
B_{(\phi,\mu)}(y,z)\in L_{y}\otimes\overline{L_{z}}\cong\mathrm{Hom}(L_{z},L_{y})
, so that the product is the composition of homomorphisms, and
B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x,z)\in\mathrm{Hom}(L_{x},L_{z})\cong L_{x}\otimes\overline{L_{z}}
as expected.

Remark 2.1
.

In Theorem
1.1
, in order to claim that such process is determinantal with kernel
B_{(\phi,\mu)}
, we need to make a clear statement about
how to define its determinant
7
7

7

In the reference paper
[
7
]
, Berman treats the Bergman kernel formally as if it was an integral kernel on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, which is only locally true. The main goal of this section is to ensure that the expected properties still hold on the global level of sections.
. Indeed, the determinant
\det(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}
, denoted by
{\det}_{n}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))

{\det}_{n}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\bigotimes\limits_{i=1}^{n}B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{\sigma(i)}),

which becomes, thanks to Proposition

(2.19)

{\det}_{n}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{N}\bigotimes\limits_{j=1}^{n}s_{i_{j}}(x_{j})\otimes\overline{s_{i_{j}}}(x_{\sigma(j)}).

However these equations do not make sense, because we are adding tensors of different vector spaces. At best, using canonical isomorphisms (
2.2
), one could rearrange the terms to define the determinant by

{\det}_{n}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{N}\bigotimes\limits_{j=1}^{n}s_{i_{j}}(x_{j})\otimes\overline{s_{i_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}}}(x_{j}),

so that

is interpreted as a section of

. We propose to go one step further, based on the discussion of Section

can then be defined as a function

(2.20)

{\det}_{n}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{N}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}h_{x_{j}}(s_{i_{j}}(x_{j}),s_{i_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}}(x_{j})).

Hereafter, we will take (
2.20
) as a rigorous definition of the Bergman kernel determinant.

If
L=M\times{\mathbb{C}}
is the trivial bundle with weight
\phi
, this determinant reduces to the (traditional) determinant of the weighted Christoffel–Darboux kernel:

\det(B_{\phi}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\det(K_{\phi}(x_{i},x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n},

so that our definition is consistent with the existing theory. By construction, the Bergman kernel depends heavily on the weighted measure

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
L
be a holomorphic line bundle over
M

\mu
be a finite measure on

Proof.

If
(s_{i})
is an orthonormal basis of
(H^{0}(M,L),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(\phi,\mu)})

By the definition of the determinant of the Bergman kernel, we have

\displaystyle{\det}_{n}

\displaystyle(B_{(\phi+\psi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{N_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}h_{x_{j}}^{\psi}(s_{i_{j}}(x_{j})e^{\frac{1}{2}\psi(x_{j})},s_{i_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}}(x_{j})e^{\frac{1}{2}\psi(x_{j})}),

so that the RHS is equal to

2.7
Equilibrium measure and energy

Until now, we only considered Hermitian metrics with positive curvature without specific assumptions on the regularity. In order to define the energy functional involved in the large deviations of Theorem
1.4
, we need to consider directional derivatives in the vector space
\mathscr{C}^{0}(M)
. Hovever, this functional is usually defined for much more regular functions, namely the Kähler class of a given Kähler form. We will explain how to circumvent this difficulty with the use of plurisubharmonic functions. The main references for these techniques are
[
4
,
45
]
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be an open set. A function
u:U\to[-\infty,\infty)
is
subharmonic
if it satisfies the following mean-value inequality for any
x\in U
and
r>0
:

u(x)\leqslant\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(x+re^{i\theta})d\theta.

If
M
is a compact complex manifold, a function
\psi:M\to[-\infty,\infty)
is called
plurisubharmonic
(psh) if it is upper semicontinuous (usc), and if
\psi\circ u
is subharmonic for any holomorphic
u:B(x,1)\subset{\mathbb{C}}\to M
.

We denote by
\mathrm{PSH}(M)
the space of plurisubharmonic functions on
M
. We see from the following result that the plurisubharmonicity generalizes the notion of having positive curvature for functions that are not regular enough.

Proposition 2.9

(
[
22
]
, Theorem 5.8)
.

For all
\phi\in\mathrm{PSH}(M)\cap L_{loc}^{1}(M)
,
{dd^{c}}\phi
is a positive current. Conversely, if
\phi\in L_{loc}^{1}(M)
has positive curvature form, then there exists
\psi\in\mathrm{PSH}(M)\cap L_{loc}^{1}(M)
such that
\phi=\psi
almost everywhere.

Let
L\to M
be a holomorphic line bundle and
\omega
be a
(1,1)
-form on
M
with continuous local potential. If
\phi
is the weight of a continuous metric
h
, we define the equilibrium weight

\phi_{e}(x)=\sup\{\psi(x):\psi\leqslant\phi\ \text{on}\ M,\ \psi\in\mathrm{PSH}(M)\}.

In particular, we see that

P:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{C}^{0}(M)&\longrightarrow&\mathrm{PSH}(M)\\
\phi&\longmapsto&\phi_{e}^{*}\end{array}\right.

is a projection, where
\phi_{e}^{*}
is the upper semicontinuous regularization of
\phi_{e}
.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
M
be a compact complex manifold of dimension
n
,
L
be a holomorphic line bundle over
M
endowed with a smooth metric
h
of positive curvature
\omega
. The
Monge–Ampère operator
with respect to
L
is the functional
\mathrm{MA}_{\omega}
defined for plurisubharmonic functions by

\mathrm{MA}_{\omega}(\psi)=(\omega+{dd^{c}}\psi)^{n}.

We also denote by
\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\phi}=\mathrm{vol}(L)^{-1}\mathrm{MA}_{\omega}(\phi)
the corresponding probability measure.

Now, if
\phi
is a continuous weight, we can extend the definition of the Monge–Ampère operator and the equilibrium measure by composing them with the projection
P
. In fact, the term “equilibrium measure” is an abuse, because it corresponds in general to a current instead of a differential form. Theorem 3.1 in
[
7
]
shows that if
\phi\in\mathscr{C}^{1,1}(M)
, then the equilibrium measure corresponds to a
L_{loc}^{\infty}
-form.

The Monge–Ampère measure defines a 1-form on
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M)
by setting, for
\phi,v\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M)
:

(\mathrm{MA}_{\omega}(\phi),v)=\int_{M}v\mathrm{MA}_{\omega}(\phi).

It is closed, therefore it admits a primitive
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
on
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M)
, which is unique up to an additive constant. An explicit expression of this primitive can be found in
[
8
,
45
]
for instance, and in these references they prove that it can be extended to arbitrary psh functions by setting

\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\phi)=\inf\{\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\psi):\psi\in\mathrm{PSH}(M,\omega)\cap\mathscr{C}^{\infty}(M),\psi\geqslant\phi\}.

It can then also be extended to continuous functions by

\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\phi)=\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(P(\phi)).

According to
[
4
, Theorem B]
and
[
45
, Lemma 6.13]
,
\mathcal{E}_{\omega}
is concave, continuous and Gateaux-differentiable on
\mathscr{C}^{0}(M)
, and for all
\phi,\psi\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(M)
,

\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\phi+\psi)-\mathcal{E}_{\omega}(\phi)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\psi(x)\mathrm{MA}(P(\phi+s\psi))(x)ds.

Note that this result is actually remarkable, because the projection
P
is not itself differentiable. This quantity is known in the context of Kähler–Einstein geometry as the
Mabuchi functional
or the
K-energy

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\phi,\psi)

[
48
]
(see also
[
44
]
for a more recent study). We introduce the renormalized versions
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eq}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eq}}
as the equivalent of these functionals for the equilibrium measure
\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\phi}
. In other terms, we have for all
\phi,\psi\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(M)
,

(2.25)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\phi,\psi)=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\phi+\psi)-\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\phi)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\psi(x)d\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\phi+s\psi}(x)ds.

2.8
Large deviations for empirical measures

The field of large deviations aims to quantify the probability of very rare events. Roughly, it consists in describing the asymptotic behavior, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, of a family of probability measures
(\mu_{\varepsilon})
, in terms of a rate function, and a speed that depends on
\varepsilon
.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a locally convex Hausdorff topological vector space, endowed with its
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
. A
rate function
(resp. a
good rate function
) is a function
I:\mathcal{E}\to[0,\infty]
such that for all
A\in[0,\infty)
, the level set
\{x:I(x)\leqslant A\}
is a closed (resp. compact) subset of
\mathcal{E}
. The family
(\mu_{\varepsilon})
of Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{E}
satisfies a
large deviation principle
with speed
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and rate function
I
if for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
,

-\inf_{x\in\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\Gamma}\limits^{o}}}I(x)\leqslant\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)\leqslant\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\log\mu_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)\leqslant-\inf_{x\in\overline{\Gamma}}I(x).

Let us illustrate the large deviations in a convenient setting. If
\Gamma
is a continuity set of
I
,
i.e.

I_{\Gamma}:=\inf\{I(x):x\in\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\Gamma}\limits^{o}}\}=\inf\{I(x):x\in\overline{\Gamma}\}
, then the large deviations mean that
\mu_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)\sim e^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}I_{\Gamma}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Definition 2.15
.

The
cumulant-generating function
associated with a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{E}
is the function

\Lambda_{\mu}:\lambda\in\mathcal{E}^{*}\mapsto\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\langle\lambda,X_{1}\rangle}]=\log\int_{\mathcal{E}}e^{\langle\lambda,x\rangle}d\mu(x).

In what follows, we consider the Banach space
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{M}_{1}(K)
, where
K
is a compact subset of a Polish space. Let us endow it with the distribution of a random measure. More precisely, let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let
\mu:\omega\to\mu_{\omega}
be a
\mathcal{E}
-valued random variable. Its distribution is given by

\mathbb{E}[\mu(f)]=\int_{\Omega}\int_{K}f(x)d\mu_{\omega}(x)d\mathbb{P}(\omega).

Given a probability measure
d\mathcal{P}
on
K^{N}
, the empirical measure
\widehat{\mu}_{N}
of a
N
-tuple
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
of random variables with values in
K
and joint distribution
d\mathcal{P}
defines a probability measure
dm(\nu)=\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\widehat{\mu}_{N}}(\nu)]
on
\mathcal{E}
, meaning that for any measurable function
F:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathcal{E}}F(\nu)dm(\nu)=\mathbb{E}[F(\widehat{\mu}_{N})].

For instance, for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{0}(K)
, set
F_{f}(\nu)=\langle f,\nu\rangle=\int_{K}f(x)\nu(x)
the integral of
f
with respect to
\nu
. We have in this case

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{N}(f)]=\int_{\Omega}\int f(x)d\widehat{\mu}_{N,\omega}(x)d\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(X_{i}(\omega))d\mathbb{P}(\omega),

and it follows that

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}_{N}(f)]=\int_{K^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})d\mathcal{P}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}).

The main tool that we will use for the proof of Theorem
1.4
is the celebrated Gärtner–Ellis theorem, that we recall below in an abstract form
[
23
]
.

Theorem 2.10

(Abstract Gärtner–Ellis theorem)
.

Let
(\mu_{\varepsilon})
be an exponentially tight family of probability measures on a Banach space
\mathcal{E}
. Suppose
\Lambda(\cdot)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\Lambda_{\mu_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot/\varepsilon)
is finite-valued, Gateaux differentiable and lower semicontinuous in
\mathcal{E}^{*}
with respect to the weak-
∗
topology. Then
(\mu_{\varepsilon})
satisfies a LDP in
\mathcal{E}
with speed
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and with good rate function
\Lambda^{*}
.

In the case of empirical measures of i.i.d. random variables, we get Sanov’s theorem: if we set
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{N}
and
d\mathcal{P}=\nu^{\otimes N}
,
we have

\frac{1}{N}\Lambda_{\widehat{\mu}_{N}}(Nu)=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{u(X_{1})}]=\log\int_{K}e^{u(x)}d\nu(x)=:\Lambda(u),

which is indeed a finite, Gateaux-differentiable function. Therefore, the empirical measures
(\widehat{\mu}_{N})
satisfy a LDP with speed
N
and good rate function
\Lambda^{*}
, which is known to be equal to the Kullback–Leibler divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot,\nu)
according to
[
23
, Lemma 6.2.13]
.

3
Weighted DPPs on complex manifolds

In this section, we construct a broad class of point processes, associated with line bundles on complex manifolds, which are weighted versions of DPPs whose kernel is the Bergman kernel of the line bundle. They are the main object of interest in this paper, and we define them in full generality (
i.e.
under assumptions that are much weaker than the main theorems). In fact, as we shall see, the main restrictions of the asymptotic results we obtain are due to Theorem
2.8
, and most of the properties of the point processes hold with minimal assumptions on the manifold and the line bundle.

3.1
Construction

Let
M
be a compact complex manifold of dimension
n
, endowed with a Borel probability measure
\mu
. Consider two holomorphic Hermitian line bundles
L,E
endowed with respective metrics
h,h^{\prime}
and weights
\phi,\psi
. Set
N=\dim H^{0}(M,L\otimes E)
and denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
the probability measure on
M^{N}
defined by

(3.1)

d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)}\|\det(s_{i}(x_{j}))\|_{\phi}^{2}e^{-\sum_{i}\psi(x_{i})}d\mu^{\otimes N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

where
(s_{i})
is an orthonormal basis of
(H^{0}(M,L),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(\phi,\mu)})
, and
Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)
is a normalization constant called
partition function
. By convention, if
\psi
is constant equal to zero, we will simply write
\mathcal{P}_{\phi}
and
Z_{N}(\phi)
. The main difference between the weights
\phi
and
\psi
is that the orthonormal basis
(s_{i})
depends on
\phi
but not on
\psi
; this is an important distinction that we will use carefully later when we will make
\phi
and
\psi
vary.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
M
be a compact complex manifold of dimension
n
,
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
M
,
L
and
E
be holomorphic line bundles endowed with Hermitian metric of respective weights
\phi,\psi
. A
weighted determinantal point process
with kernel
B_{(\phi,\mu)}
and weight
\psi
is the point process associated with a random family
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
with joint distribution
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
.

The terminology we have chosen for the point process is justified by the fact that the unweighted (
\psi=0
) point process is indeed determinantal: it is the point of Theorem
3.3
.
The model can be interpreted as follows: if we replace
L
by a high tensor power
L^{k}
of a line bundle with Hermitian metric
h^{k}
and
E
by the
s
-th tensor power
K^{s}
of the canonical line bundle, where
s\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
denotes the spin, then
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N_{k}})
are electrons in the lowest Landau level in the context of Quantum Hall effect
[
40
]
. In the asymptotic regime we consider, we will rather replace
L\otimes F
by
L^{k}\otimes F
, where
k
is an integer that we will let go to infinity and
F
is the trivial line bundle endowed with a weight
\psi+k\psi^{\prime}
.

The measure
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
can be interpreted as a Gibbs measure: let us define the Hamiltonian
H_{N}^{\phi,\psi}:M^{N}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

H_{N}^{\phi,\psi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=-\log(\|\det(s_{i}(x_{j}))\|_{\phi}^{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(x_{i}).

Then, we have

(3.2)

d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)}e^{-H_{N}^{\phi,\psi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})}d\mu^{\otimes N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}).

Note that this Hamiltonian is highly singular, as it is becomes infinite when two of the variables are equal or when one of the
x_{i}
is outside the domain
D_{\psi}=\{x\in M,\psi(x)<\infty\}
. As a consequence, the support of
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
is included in
(D_{\psi})^{N}\setminus\bigcup_{i\neq j}\Delta_{ij}
where
\Delta_{ij}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,_{N})\in M^{n}|x_{i}=x_{j}\}
. It would be appealing to see the family
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
as a Coulomb gas, in order to make a link with some results obtained on Riemannian manifold
[
30
]
. Indeed, in the case of the sphere it is known to be true However, showing that
H_{N}^{\phi,\psi}
is the Hamiltonian of a Coulomb interaction is a quite difficult task, considering that the Green function is in general not explicit (see
[
33
]
and references therein for recent results on Green functions on complex manifolds).

We will denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\phi}^{\psi}
the expectation with respect to
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
, i.e., for any measurable
F:M^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\phi}^{\psi}[F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})]=\int_{M^{N}}F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

or simply
\mathbb{E}_{\phi}
if
\psi=0
.

Observe that
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}
, and its Radon–Nikodym derivative is given by

(3.3)

\frac{d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}}{d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{e^{-\sum_{i}\psi(x_{i})}}{\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\left[e^{-\sum_{i}\psi(X_{i})}\right]}=e^{-\sum_{i}\psi(x_{i})}\frac{Z_{N}(\phi)}{Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)}.

As such, the measure
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
is therefore the same kind of perturbation of
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}
as the ones considered in Section 5.1 of
[
43
]
for the Ginibre ensemble.

3.2
Partition functions and change of weights

The partition function can be computed using a “weighted” version of the celebrated Andreief’s formula (which is a continuous generalization of the Cauchy–Binet formula) , cf.
[
62
, Proposition 7.1]
for instance.

Proposition 3.1
.

The partition function satisfies

(3.4)

Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)=N!\det\left(\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle_{(\phi+\psi,\mu)}\right)_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}.

In particular,

(3.5)

Z_{N}(\phi)=N!

Proof.

Let us first notice that, if we want the total mass of
\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\psi}
to be 1, then necessarily

Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)=\int_{M^{N}}\|\det(s_{i}(x_{j}))\|_{\phi+\psi}^{2}d\mu^{\otimes N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}).

The norm of the determinant can be rewritten as follows:

\|\det(s_{i}(x_{j}))\|_{\phi+\psi}^{2}=e^{-\sum_{i}\psi(x_{i})}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{N}h_{x_{i}}(s_{\sigma(i)}(x_{i}),s_{\tau(i)}(x_{i})).

It follows that

Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\varepsilon(\tau)A_{\sigma(i),\tau(i)},

where
A=(A_{i,j})
is the Gram matrix defined by

A_{i,j}=\int_{M}h_{x}(s_{i}(x),s_{j}(x))e^{-\psi(x)}d\mu(x)=\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle_{(\phi+\psi,\mu)}.

Let us fix
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
. We have

\sum_{\tau}\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_{i}A_{\sigma(i),\tau(i)}=\sum_{\sigma}\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_{i}A_{i,\tau(i)}=\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})\det(A),

where
A=(A_{ij})
is the matrix whose entries are the inner products of
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
. Thus, we get

Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\det(A)=N!\det(A),

and (
3.4
) follows. In the case
\psi=0
, the result is a consequence of the orthonormality of the sections
(s_{i})
for the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(\phi,\mu)}
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

The partition function
Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)
satisfies

(3.6)

Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)=N!\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\left[e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(X_{i})}\right].

Proof.

According to (
3.3
), we have

Z_{N}(\phi,\psi)=Z_{N}(\phi)\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\sum_{i}\psi(X_{i})}\right],

and we conclude by using (
3.5
).
∎

3.3
Determinantal property and scaling limit

In this section, we will show that the point process associated to
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}
is
determinantal. The main idea behind the proof is that the determinantal kernel, which appears to be the Bergman kernel, is the reproducing kernel of the Hilbert space
(H^{0}(M,L),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(\phi,\mu)})
. In contrast, when one adds the weight
\psi
, the measure
\mu
is replaced by
e^{-\psi}\mu
and we lose the reproducing property, hence the process is not determinantal anymore. We shall then prove Theorem
1.1
, with the use of Theorem
2.8
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
be a family of random variables with joint distribution
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}
. It is determinantal on
M
with kernel
B_{(\phi,\mu)}
.

Before giving the proof, let us remark that this is obvious in a local trivialization. Indeed, if
e_{U}
is a local nonvanishing section on
U
such that
\|e_{U}(x)\|_{\phi}^{2}=e^{-\phi(x)}
and

s^{(i)}(x)=f^{(i)}(x)e_{U}(x),\ \forall 1\leqslant i\leqslant N,\ \forall x\in U,

then for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in U^{N}
,

d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=|\det(f^{(i)}(x_{j}))|^{2}e^{-\sum_{i}\phi(x_{i})}

and in this case one can use Theorem
2.3
. The purpose of Theorem
3.3
is to ensure that that the determinantal property is preserved at a global scale and does not depend on the trivialization. Consider for instance
N=2
,
U_{1},U_{2}\subset M
such that
U_{1}\cap U_{2}=\varnothing
and for
i,j\in\{1,2\}
,

s^{(i)}(x)=f_{j}^{(i)}(x)e_{U_{j}}(x),\ \forall x\in U_{j},

then for
x_{1}\in U_{1}
and
x_{2}\in U_{2}
one has

\|\det(s^{(i)}(x_{j}))\|_{\phi}^{2}=(f_{1}^{(1)}(x_{1})f_{2}^{(2)}(x_{2})-f_{2}^{(1)}(x_{2})f_{2}^{(2)}(x_{1}))e^{-\phi_{1}(x_{1})-\phi_{2}(x_{2})}.

It cannot be interpreted anymore as a determinant of functions, and Theorem
2.3
fails to apply here. However, as we shall see, the algebraic tricks used to prove Theorem
2.3
still work.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let us compute the correlation functions of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
.According to Proposition
2.1
,
\rho_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
when
n>N
and

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{N!}{(N-n)!}\int_{M^{N-n}}p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})d\mu^{\otimes N-n}(x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{N})

when
n\leqslant N
,
where

p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\|\det(s_{i}(x_{j}))\|_{\phi}^{2}.

Let us start with the extremal case
n=N
. We have

\rho_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=N!p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

so that

\rho_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\|\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)s^{(\sigma(1))}(x_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes s^{(\sigma(N))}(x_{N})\|_{\phi}^{2},

and by sesquilinearity we obtain

(3.7)

\rho_{N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{N}h_{x_{i}}(s^{(\sigma(i))}(x_{i}),s^{(\tau(i))}(x_{i})).

We also have, according to (
2.20
):

{\det}_{N}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\leqslant N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}h_{x_{\ell}}(s^{(i_{\ell})}(x_{\ell}),s^{(\sigma^{-1}(i_{\ell}))}(x_{\ell})).

Now, if there are
\ell\neq m
such that
i_{\ell}=i_{m}
, then

\sum_{\sigma}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{\ell}h_{x_{\ell}}(s^{(i_{\ell})}(x_{\ell}),s^{(\sigma^{-1}(\ell))}(x_{\ell}))=0

because for each
\sigma
the product is the same as
\hat{\sigma}=\sigma\circ(i_{\ell}\ i_{m})
, and
\varepsilon(\sigma\circ(i_{\ell}\ i_{m}))=-\varepsilon(\sigma)
, so that both terms cancel each other. It follows that the sum over
i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}
is equivalent to a sum over
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, and we get

{\det}_{N}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}h_{x_{\ell}}(s^{(\tau(\ell))}(x_{\ell}),s^{(\sigma^{-1}\circ\tau(\ell))}(x_{\ell})).

If we do the following index change on the sum:
(\sigma,\tau)\mapsto(\tau,\tau\circ\sigma^{-1})
, we get:

{\det}_{N}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\varepsilon(\tau)\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}h_{x_{\ell}}(s^{(\sigma(\ell))}(x_{\ell}),s^{(\tau(\ell))}(x_{\ell})).

If we combine this equality with (
3.7
), we obtain the expected result

Now let us turn to the case
n<N
. We will prove it by induction on
n
, showing that for any
\ell<N
one goes from
\rho_{\ell+1}
to
\rho_{\ell}
the same way as one goes from
{\det}_{\ell+1}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))
to
{\det}_{\ell}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))
. By Proposition
2.1
,

\rho_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})=\frac{N!}{(N-\ell)!}\int_{M^{N-\ell}}p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})d\mu^{\otimes N-\ell}(x_{\ell+1},\ldots,x_{N_{k}})

and

\rho_{\ell+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell+1})=\frac{N!}{(N-\ell-1)!}\int_{M^{N-\ell-1}}p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})d\mu^{\otimes N-\ell-1}(x_{\ell+2},\ldots,x_{N}).

We deduce that

\rho_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})=\frac{1}{N-\ell}\int_{M}\rho_{\ell+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell+1})dv_{M}(x_{\ell+1}).

By assumption,

\rho_{\ell+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell+1})={\det}_{\ell+1}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant\ell+1},

and according to Lemma
2.7
we have

(N-\ell){\det}_{\ell}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))=\int_{M}{\det}_{\ell+1}(B_{(\phi,\mu)}(x_{i},x_{j}))d\mu(x_{\ell+1}),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Consider
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N_{k}})
with distribution
d\mathcal{P}_{k\phi+\psi}
. From Theorem
3.3
, we already know that the process is determinantal with kernel
B_{(k\phi+\psi,\mu)}
. Let us fix
x\in M
, and choose a set of normal coordinates
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
on
U\ni x
and a corresponding holomorphic trivialization
e_{U}
such that
\|e_{U}\|_{h^{k}}^{2}=e^{-k\phi-\psi}
. In these local coordinates, there are holomorphic functions
f_{\ell}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
for all
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant N_{k}
such that

s_{\ell}(u)=f_{\ell}(u)e_{U}(u),\ \forall 1\leqslant\ell\leqslant N_{k},\ \forall u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}.

According to (
2.15
), the Bergman kernel reads

B_{k\phi+\psi}(u,v)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{k}}f_{\ell}(u)\overline{f_{\ell}}(v)e_{U}(u)\otimes\overline{e_{U}}(v).

We can identify
e_{U}(u)\otimes\overline{e_{U}}(v)
to
e^{-\frac{1}{2}(k\phi(u)+\psi(u)+k\phi(v)+\psi(v))}
as in Section
2.5
, and the kernel becomes a Christoffel–Darboux kernel on
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
:

B_{k\phi+\psi}(u,v)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{k}}f_{\ell}(u)\overline{f_{\ell}}(v)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(k\phi(u)+\psi(u)+k\phi(v)+\psi(v))},\ \forall u,v\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d},

and its determinant is unambiguously defined, as opposed to the global case discussed in Remark
2.1
. For any
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in({\mathbb{C}}^{d})^{n}
,

\rho_{m}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{k}},\ldots,\frac{u_{m}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{m}B_{k\phi+\psi}\left(\frac{u_{i}}{\sqrt{k}},\frac{u_{\sigma(i)}}{\sqrt{k}}\right).

From the estimate (
2.24
) we deduce

\rho_{m}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{k}},\ldots,\frac{u_{m}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\varepsilon(\sigma)k^{nm}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(B_{\infty}^{\phi}(u_{i},u_{\sigma(i)})+O(k^{-\frac{1}{2}})\right).

When we expand the product on the RHS, every term other than
\prod_{i=1}^{m}B_{\infty}^{\phi}(u_{i},u_{\sigma(i)})
becomes
O(k^{-\frac{\ell}{2}})
with
\ell\geqslant 1
, and there are
2^{m}-1
such terms:

k^{-nm}\rho_{m}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{k}},\ldots,\frac{u_{m}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}B_{\infty}^{\phi}(u_{i},u_{\sigma(i)})+(2^{m}-1)O(k^{-\frac{1}{2}})\right).

We conclude the proof by expanding it further, yielding

k^{-nm}\rho_{m}\left(\frac{u_{1}}{\sqrt{k}},\ldots,\frac{u_{m}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\det(B_{\infty}^{\phi}(u_{i},u_{j}))+C_{m}O(k^{-\frac{1}{2}}),

where
C_{m}
is a constant that depends on
m
but not on
k
, so that it can be absorbed in the big
O
.
∎

3.4
Convergence to the equilibrium measure

In this section, we apply Theorem
1.1
as well as some moment estimates, in order to prove Corollary
1.3
.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

To get the convergence in probability of the empirical measures, we can prove that for any extraction function
\eta:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists an extraction function
\kappa
such that, with probability 1, the sequence of random measures
\hat{\mu}_{\eta\circ\kappa(k)}
converges in distribution to
\mu
. Furthermore, by the Portmanteau theorem
[
9
, Thm 2.1]
, it is equivalent to

(3.8)

\liminf_{k}\hat{\mu}_{\eta\circ\kappa(k)}(G)\geqslant\mu(G),\text{ for all open }G\subset M.

As
M
is a compact metric space, it admits a countable base
(U_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of open subsets. Let us show that for any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})
converges in probability to
\mu(U_{i})
. For any
i
, we have from (
1.7
)

(3.9)

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\int_{U_{i}}B_{(k\phi+\psi,\mu)}(x,x)dv_{M}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits_{k\to\infty}}\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\phi}(U_{i}).

If we prove that
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]\to 0
, we will get the convergence in probability of the random variables
\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})
. We can compute the variance as follows:

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]^{2}.

However, using the definition of the 1-point and 2-point correlation functions, we have

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})^{2}]=\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}}\left(\int_{U_{i}}\rho_{1}(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{U_{i}}\rho_{2}(x,y)d\mu^{\otimes 2}(x,y)\right),

hence

N_{k}^{2}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]=\int_{U_{i}}\rho_{1}(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{U_{i}}\rho_{2}(x,y)d\mu^{\otimes 2}(x,y)-\left(\int_{U_{i}}\rho_{1}(x)d\mu(x)\right)^{2}.

However, we have

\rho_{2}(x,y)=\rho_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(y)-B_{(k\phi+\psi,\mu)}(x,y)B_{(k\phi+\psi,\mu)}(y,x),

so that

N_{k}^{2}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]=\int_{U_{i}}\rho_{1}(x)d\mu(x)+\int_{U_{i}}|B_{(k\phi+\psi,\mu)}(x,y)|^{2}d\mu^{\otimes 2}(x,y).

Using the reproducing property of the Bergman kernel, it follows that

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]=\frac{2}{N_{k}}\int_{U_{i}}\frac{B_{(k\phi+\psi,\mu)}(x,x)}{N_{k}}d\mu(x),

and as the integral is uniformly bounded in
k
, we get that
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})]=O(N_{k}^{-1})
. Combining it with (
3.9
) yields the convergence in probability of
\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})
to
\mu_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\phi}(U_{i})
.

We are now able to prove (
3.8
). If
G
is an arbitrary open set of
M
, there exists
I\subset{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
. Take an extraction function
\eta:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
. From the probability convergence of
\hat{\mu}_{k}(U_{i})
for
i\in I
, one deduces by diagonal extraction that there exists a subsubsequence
\hat{\mu}_{\eta\circ\theta(k)}
such that for all
i\in I
,

\hat{\mu}_{\eta\circ\theta(k)}(U_{j})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{a.s.}}\mu(U_{j}).

Hence, almost-surely,

\liminf_{k}\hat{\mu}_{\eta\circ\theta(k)}(G)\geqslant\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\liminf_{k}\hat{\mu}_{\eta\circ\theta(k)}(U_{j_{n}})\right),

and the right-hand side is in fact
\mu(G)
, which yields (
3.8
).
∎

3.5
Cumulant-generating function and large deviations

This final section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.4
, which is based on an application of the Gärtner–Ellis theorem. Before diving into the proof, we will provide explicit formulas for the cumulant-generating function of the DPPs and their weighted versions.

Let
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
be distributed according to
d\mathcal{P}_{\phi}
. The log-Laplace transform of the linear statistics
\sum_{i}\psi(X_{i})
is

K_{\phi}^{\psi}(t)=\log\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\left[e^{-t\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(X_{i})}\right]=\log\det\left(\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle_{\phi+t\psi}\right),

where
(s_{i})
is an orthonormal basis of
(H^{0}(M,L),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{(\phi,\mu)})
. It can be proved (cf.
[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
or
[
7
, Lemma 6.2]
) that its first derivative with respect to
t
can be rewritten as an integral respect to the Bergman kernel, and the proof relies on an analysis of the Gram matrix
G_{t}=(\langle s_{i},s_{j}\rangle_{\phi+t\psi})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

The function
K_{\phi}^{\psi}
is derivable with respect to
t
, and satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}K_{\phi}^{\psi}(t)=-\mathbb{E}_{\phi+t\psi}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(X_{i})\right]=-\int_{M}\psi(x)B_{(\phi+t\psi,\mu)}(x,x)d\mu(x).

In order to apply the Gärtner–Ellis theorem, we have to study the limit of the rescaled cumulant-generating function

\Lambda_{k}^{\phi,\psi,\psi^{\prime}}(f)=\frac{1}{kN_{k}}\log\mathbb{E}_{k\phi+\psi}^{k\psi^{\prime}}\left[e^{\sum_{i}kf(X_{i})}\right]=\frac{1}{kN_{k}}\left(K_{k\phi+\psi}^{k(\psi^{\prime}-f)}(1)-K_{k\phi+\psi}^{k\psi^{\prime}}(1)\right).

Note that this functional is, up to a scaling factor, the generating functional of the partition function considered in
[
40
]
in the context of the integer quantum Hall effect for
d=1
. As we see below, it is (up to a minus sign) the Mabuchi functional.

Lemma 3.5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.4
,

(3.10)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Lambda_{k}^{\phi,\psi,\psi^{\prime}}(f)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\phi+\psi^{\prime},-f),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eq}}
is the Mabuchi functional.

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}^{\phi,\psi,\psi^{\prime}}(f)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}(f(x)-\psi^{\prime}(x))\frac{B_{(k(\phi+s\psi^{\prime}-sf)+\psi,\mu)}(x,x)}{N_{k}}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\psi^{\prime}(x)\frac{B_{(k(\phi+s\psi^{\prime})+\psi,\mu)}(x,x)}{N_{k}}d\mu(x).

We apply the weak convergence (
1.7
) for both integrals, and use (
2.25
) to obtain (
3.10
) as expected.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
\mu_{k}^{\phi,\psi,\psi^{\prime}}
be the empirical measure of the random family
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N_{k}})
with joint distribution
d\mathcal{P}_{k\phi+\psi}^{k\psi^{\prime}}
. According to Lemma
3.5
, the renormalized cumulant-generating functions
\frac{1}{kN_{k}}\Lambda
converges to the Mabuchi functional
\Lambda(f)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\phi+\psi^{\prime},-f)
. We have seen in Section
2.7
that it satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
2.10
, which yields the expected large deviation principle with speed
kN_{k}
and good rate function
\Lambda^{*}
.
∎
