Interpolation and linear prediction of data - three kernel selection criteria


Abstract
Interpolation and prediction have been useful approaches in modeling data in many areas of applications. The aim of this paper is the prediction of the next value of a time series (time series forecasting) using the techniques in interpolation of the spatial data, for the two approaches kernel interpolation and kriging. We are interested in finding some sufficient conditions for the kernels and provide a detailed analyse of the prediction using kernel interpolation. Finally, we provide a natural idea to select a good kernel among a given family of kernels using only the data. We illustrate our results by application to the data set on the mean annual temperature of France and Morocco recorded for a period of 115 years (1901 to 2015).

Linear prediction and kernel interpolation

Let
\mathbb{R}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}}
be the Hilbert space of real functions on
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}
with inner product
(.,.)
and norm
N(.)
. The dual of
\mathbb{R}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}}
is spanned by the point evaluation linear
forms
\delta_{x}:f\to f(x)
,
x\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1}\}
, that is

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}})^{*}=

\displaystyle(\delta_{x_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{x_{n+1}}).

Moreover, the dual norm
N^{*}
is defined by

\displaystyle(N^{*}(\mu))^{2}=\sup\{|\mu(f)|^{2}:\quad N(f)\leq 1\},

for all
\mu\in(\mathbb{R}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}})^{*}
.

Now, for any function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}}
and any sequence of real numbers
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
, we define the
linear prediction
of

f(x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\hat{f}(x_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i}),

with the error

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}rr_{n}(f):=|f(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})|,

and the worst error in the unit ball w.r.t. the norm
N(.)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}err(f):=\sup\{|f(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})|^{2}:\quad N(f)\leq 1\}=(N^{*}(\delta_{x_{n+1}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}))^{2}.

(1)

In the rest oh this paper, we endow the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\}}
with the scalar inner product

\displaystyle(f,f)=(f,f)_{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}f(x_{i})f(x_{j})k^{(-1)}(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f},

with
\boldsymbol{f}=(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n+1}))^{\top}
and
\mathbf{K}=[k(x_{i},x_{j}):\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n+1]
is a fixed
(n+1)\times(n+1)
symmetric positive definite matrix, with
k^{(-1)}(x_{i},x_{j})
denotes the
(i,j)
entry of
\mathbf{K}^{-1}
. The norm defined by
\mathbf{K}
is given by
N(f)=\|\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{f}\|
, with
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.

2.1
Min-max prediction and kernel interpolation

Definition 2.1

(Min-max prediction)
.

A linear prediction
f^{*}(x_{n+1})
of
f(x_{n+1})
is called min-max if

\displaystyle f^{*}(x_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{*}f(x_{i}),

(2)

where
\left(w_{1}^{*},...,w_{n}^{*}\right)
are given by the minimization of the
\mathcal{W}err(f)

1
.

The following result give us the optimal weights associate to the min-max prediction w.r.t. to the norm
\|\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\cdot\|
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The the worst error in the unit ball,
\mathcal{W}err(f)
, w.r.t. to the norm
\|\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\cdot\|
is equals

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}err(f)=\|\delta_{x_{n+1}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{x_{i}})\|_{\mathbf{K}^{1/2}}^{2}

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{K}^{1/2}}
denotes the dual norm defined by
the dual scalar inner product

\displaystyle(\delta_{x_{i}},\delta_{x_{j}})_{\mathbf{K}}=k(x_{i},x_{j}),\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n+1.

Proof.

From the general theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces,see
[
9
,
1
]
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{f}\|\leq 1}\{|f(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})|^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{f}\|\leq 1}\{[\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{f}]^{\top}[\mathbf{K}^{1/2}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)^{\top}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)\mathbf{K}^{1/2}][\mathbf{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{f}]\}

\displaystyle=\mbox{the largest eigenvalue of $[\mathbf{K}^{1/2}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)^{\top}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)\mathbf{K}^{1/2}]$}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)^{\top}\|^{2}=(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)\mathbf{K}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\|\delta_{x_{n+1}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{x_{i}})\|_{\mathbf{K}^{1/2}}^{2}.

∎

Corollary 2.3
.

The optimal weights of the min-max linear prediction of
f(x_{n+1})
are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{*}=(w_{1}^{*},\ldots,w_{n}^{*})=[k(x_{n+1},x_{1}),\ldots,k(x_{n+1},x_{n})][k(x_{i},x_{j}):\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n]^{-1}.

(4)

Proof.

The optimal weights are given by the
minimization

\displaystyle\arg\min\{\|\delta_{x_{n+1}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{K}^{1/2}}:\quad w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\},

(5)

which is the solution of the system

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})=k(x_{n+1},x_{i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(6)

it follows easily that
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
is given by
4
.
∎

The min-max prediction (

(7)

where

The following Proposition gives the error of interpolation.

Proposition 2.5

(Interpolation error )
.

The error of interpolation,
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{E}rr
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\mathcal{E}rr(f):=f(x_{n+1})-f^{*}(x_{n+1})=[\boldsymbol{k}_{n+1}^{(-1)}\boldsymbol{f}][k(x_{n+1},x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{*}k(x_{i},x_{n+1})].

(9)

Proof.

First, observe that we can write the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{f}
in the basis
\mathbf{K}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}[\boldsymbol{k}_{j}^{(-1)}\boldsymbol{f}]\boldsymbol{k}_{j}.

with

\mathbf{K}^{-1}
. Therefore

\displaystyle I(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}[\boldsymbol{k}_{j}^{(-1)}\boldsymbol{f}]I(\boldsymbol{k}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\boldsymbol{k}_{j}^{(-1)}\boldsymbol{f}]\boldsymbol{k}_{j}+[\boldsymbol{k}_{n+1}^{(-1)}\boldsymbol{f}]I(\boldsymbol{k}_{n+1}),

because the interpolation of

is exact for

.
Thus,

\displaystyle f-I(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\mathbf{K}_{n+1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}](\boldsymbol{k}_{n+1}-I(\boldsymbol{k}_{n+1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{k}_{n+1}^{(-1)}\boldsymbol{f}](0,\ldots,0,[k(x_{n+1},x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{*}k(x_{i},x_{n+1})])^{\top},

which completes the proof.

Min-max linear prediction with constraint

5

(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{q},u_{1},

be the coordinates
of
\boldsymbol{f}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\theta_{l}\boldsymbol{p}_{l}+\sum_{l=1}^{n+1-q}u_{l}\boldsymbol{b}_{q+l}.

It
follows that

\displaystyle Q(f,f)=\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{n+1-q}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{n+1-q}u_{l_{1}}u_{l_{2}}Q(b_{q+l_{1}},b_{q+l_{2}})=\|Q^{1/2}\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2},

and the kernel

Q=:[Q_{l_{1},l_{2}}:\quad l_{1},l_{2}=1,\ldots,n+1-q]
is invertible. If
the weights
\boldsymbol{w}
satisfy the constraint (
10
), then

\displaystyle|f(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})|^{2}=|\sum_{l=1}^{n+1-q}u_{l}\{b_{l}(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b_{l}(x_{i})\}|^{2},

therefore,

\displaystyle\sup\{|f(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})|^{2}:\quad Q(f,f)\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{|f(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f(x_{i})|^{2}:\quad\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}Q\boldsymbol{u}\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=(b_{q+1}(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b_{q+1}(x_{i}),\ldots,b_{n+1}(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b_{n+1-q}(x_{i}))Q^{-1}

\displaystyle(b_{1+q}(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b_{1+q}(x_{i}),\ldots,b_{n+1}(x_{n+1})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}b_{n+1}(x_{i}))^{\top}

\displaystyle=(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)\mathbf{R}Q^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\top}(-w_{1},\ldots,-w_{n},1)^{\top},

where the
(n+1)\times(n+1-q)
matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}=[\boldsymbol{b}_{q+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n+1}].

3
Stochastic approach

The statistical counterpart to the kernel interpolation is known as
kriging (see e.g.
[
1
]
). It is
based on the modeling assumption that

(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{n}),f(x_{n+1}))
is a realization of random
vector
Y_{x_{1}}
,
\ldots
,
Y_{x_{n+1}}
over the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
.
To predict
Y_{x_{n+1}}
known
Y_{x_{1}}
,
\ldots
,
Y_{x_{n}}
we need the
mean and the covariance matrix of the random vector
(Y_{x_{1}},\ldots,Y_{x_{n+1}})
.

We assume that the mean
(m(x_{1}),\ldots,m(x_{n+1}))
(also called the trend) and
the covariance function

\displaystyle k(x_{i},x_{j})=cov(Y_{x_{i}},Y_{x_{j}})

of the random vector
(Y_{x_{1}},\ldots,Y_{x_{n+1}})
exist.

If
Y_{x_{1}}
,
\ldots
,
Y_{x_{n}}
are assumed to be known, then the
best linear unbiased predictor (BLUP) of
Y_{x_{n+1}}

is given by

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{x_{n+1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{*}Y_{x_{i}},

where the weights

w_{i}^{*}
are the solution of the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min\{var(Y_{x_{n+1}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}Y_{x_{i}}):\quad w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}m(x_{i})=m(x_{n+1})\}.

(15)

If
the mean function
m
is modeled as

\displaystyle m(x_{i})=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\beta_{k}p_{k}(x_{i}):\quad i=1,\ldots,n+1,

and if
we consider the weights such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}p_{l}(x_{i})=p_{l}(x_{n+1}),\quad l=1,\ldots,q,

then
the optimal predictor

\displaystyle\hat{f}(x_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{*}f(x_{i})

of
f(x_{n+1})
in stochastic sense coincides with the
interpolation (
12
).

4
Three kernel selection criteria

Kernel interpolation and prediction approaches are based on the
knowledge of a symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbf{K}
and the trend

\boldsymbol{p}_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\boldsymbol{p}_{q}
. To apply kernel interpolation it amounts
to the assumption that one knows the degree of smoothness of the
function
f
. In the context of partial differential equations, the
function
f
belongs to some Sobolev space. In stochastic approach
the covariance matrix and the trend are chosen using the maximum
likelihood method or the Bayesian method.

Here we propose three natural criteria to compare two kernels
\mathbf{K}^{(1)}
and
\mathbf{K}^{(2)}
.
Known
f(x_{1})
,
\ldots
,
f(x_{r})
,
we predict
f(x_{r+1})
using the kernel
[k^{(l)}(i,j):\quad i,j=1,\ldots,r]
, and we obtain
the predictor
\hat{f}^{(l)}(x_{r+1})
, with
l=1,2
, and
r=2
,
\ldots
,
n-1
.
We propose the following three criteria to measure the performance
of the Kernel
\mathbf{K}^{(l)}
:

1)
MSPE(l)=:\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|f(x_{j+1})-\hat{f}^{(l)}(x_{j+1})|^{2}}{n-1}
.
We say that
\mathbf{K}^{(1)}
is better than
\mathbf{K}^{(2)}

w.r.t. the MSPE criterion
if

\displaystyle MSPE(1)<MSPE(2).

2)
MAXPE(l)=:\max\{|f(x_{j+1})-\hat{f}^{(l)}(x_{j+1})|:\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
We say that
\mathbf{K}^{(1)}
is better than
\mathbf{K}^{(2)}
w.r.t. the MAXPE criterion
if

\displaystyle MAXPE(1)<MAXPE(2).

3) We say that
\mathbf{K}^{(1)}
is statistically better than
\mathbf{K}^{(2)}
if

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}{\bf 1}_{[|f(x_{j+1})-\hat{f}^{(1)}(x_{j+1})|<|f(x_{j+1})-\hat{f}^{2)}(x_{j+1})|]}}{n-1}>1/2.

These criteria were also used in
[
DEM
]
.

5.1
Kernel and semikernels using cubic splines

Let
S=S_{3}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n+1})
be the set of
C^{2}
cubic
splines having the knots
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n+1}
. Note that every
element
s\in S
is a
C^{2}
map on
[t_{1},t_{n+1}]
and is a
polynomial of degree three on each interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
for

i=1
,…,
n

More precisely, let

\displaystyle p_{1}=s(t_{1}),\ldots,p_{n+1}=s(t_{n+1}),\quad q_{1}=s^{\prime}(t_{1}),\ldots,q_{n+1}=s^{\prime}(t_{n+1}),

\displaystyle u_{1}=s^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}),\ldots,u_{n+1}=s^{\prime\prime}(t_{n+1}),\quad v_{1}=s^{\prime\prime\prime}(t_{1}+),\ldots,v_{n}=s^{\prime\prime\prime}(t_{n}+)

be respectively the
values of
s
and its derivatives up to order three at the knots. We
have for every
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle s(t)=p_{i}+q_{i}(t-t_{i})+(t-t_{i})^{2}u_{i}/2+(t-t_{i})^{3}v_{i}/6,\quad t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}).

The following constraint for
h_{i}=t_{i+1}-t_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n

ensures the hypothesis that
s
is
C^{2}
:

\displaystyle p_{i}+q_{i}h_{i}+u_{i}h_{i}^{2}/2+v_{i}h_{i}^{3}/6=p_{i+1},\quad

(16)

\displaystyle q_{i}+u_{i}h_{i}+v_{i}h_{i}^{2}/2=q_{i+1},\quad

(17)

\displaystyle v_{i}=s^{(3)}(t_{i})=(u_{i+1}-u_{i})/h_{i}.\quad

(18)

It is well known (see

Now we need to parametrize the set

\displaystyle\Theta:s\in S\to\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n+3}.

Defining a parametrization

\displaystyle s=\sum_{i=1}^{n+3}\theta_{i}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}=\mathbf{B}\theta.
