On the Manin-Mumford Theorem for algebraic groups
Harry Schmidt and Immanuel van Santen

Abstract
. We describe the Zariski-closure of sets of torsion points in connected algebraic groups. This is a generalization of the Manin-Mumford conjecture for commutative algebraic groups proved by Hindry [ Hin88 ] . He proved that every subset with Zariski-dense torsion points is the finite union of torsion-translates of algebraic subgroups. We formulate and prove an analogous theorem for arbitrary connected algebraic groups. We also define a canonical height on connected algebraic groups that coincides with a Néron-Tate height if G G is a (semi-) abelian variety. This motivates a generalization of the Bogomolov conjecture to arbitrary connected algebraic groups defined over a number field. We prove such a generalization as well.

14L10 (primary), and 14L24, 14G40 (secondary)

1.
Introduction

We work over an algebraically closed field
\kk
of characteristic zero. All varieties, morphisms, algebraic groups
and group actions are defined over
\kk
and we work exclusively with the Zariski topology.

Let
G
be an algebraic group and denote by
G_{tor}
the set of torsion points of
G
, i.e. the subset of

g\in G
such that
g^{n}
is the identity for some integer
n>0
. It is a fundamental problem to describe subvarieties
in
G
such that the torsion points lie dense in them. This problem is commonly associated with the Manin-Mumford conjecture for
G
an abelian variety and was resolved by Raynaud
[
Ray83a
,
Ray83b
]
for
\kk=\mathbb{C}
. For a survey on the Manin-Mumford conjecture, see
[
Tze00
]
.
A generalization for any commutative connected algebraic group
G
is due to Hindry:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Hin88
, Théorème 2]
)
.

Let
G
be a commutative connected algebraic group and let
X\subseteq G
be a subset
such that the torsion points
X\cap G_{tor}
lie dense in
X
. Then there exist finitely many
connected algebraic subgroups
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
and
t_{1},\ldots t_{r}\in G_{tor}
such that the closure of
X
in
G
is given by

\overline{X}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}t_{i}S_{i}

and
(S_{i})_{tor}
is dense in
S_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
.

One of the aims of this article is to generalize the above statement to any connected algebraic group.
This follows the desire to translate results on commutative algebraic groups to not necessarily commutative algebraic groups. A recent example is a non-commutative version
of the famous sub-space theorem by Yafaev
[
Yaf22
, Theorem 1.2]
. Recently, Corvaja, Rapinchuk, Ren and Zannier have used a generalization of Manin-Mumford for the algebraic torus, namely Laurent’s theorem, in order to prove deep results on boundedly generated subgroups of
\GL_{n}

[
CRRZ22
, Theorem 1.1]
. Laurent’s theorem describes the Zariski-closure of subsets of finite rank subroups of an algebraic torus. Another example that is closer to our objective is the classification of finite subgroups of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
of maximal order
[
Fri97
,
Fei96
]
that is related to the uniform boundedness conjecture and Mazur’s classification of finite subgroups of the rational points of elliptic curves
[
Maz77
, Theorem 8]
.

However, the following
example shows that a straightforward generalization of Theorem
1.1
is already impossible for
\SL_{2}(\kk)
:

Example 1.2
.

Let
G=\SL_{2}(\kk)
. The trace morphism
\tr\colon G\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
is constant on conjugacy classes
and the set of torsion points is given by

G_{tor}=\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\}\cup\tr^{-1}(S)\,,\quad\textrm{where}\ S=\{\,t+t^{-1}\ |\ \textrm{$t\in\kk\setminus\{\pm 1\}$ is a root of unity}\,\}\,.

Let
X
be an irreducible closed hypersurface of
G
such that
\tr(X)
is dense in
\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
Then there is an open dense subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{A}^{1}
such that

\tr^{-1}(u)\cap X
is a (possibly reducible) curve for all
u\in U
. Since
U
intersects
S
in infinitely many points,
the set of torsion points
X\cap G_{tor}
contains infinitely many pairwise disjoint curves and thus lies dense in
X
.

Every connected
2
-dimensional subgroup
S_{1}
of
G
is conjugated to the subgroup
of upper triangular matrices in
G=\SL_{2}(\kk)
and in particular
t_{1}S_{1}
is isomorphic as a variety
to
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\})

for every (torsion) point
t_{1}\in G
. However, there are many (smooth)
irreducible hypersurfaces
X\subseteq\SL_{2}(\kk)
such that
\tr(X)

is dense in
\mathbb{A}^{1}
, but
X
is non-isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\})
: E.g., if
X

is the image under any automorphism of the underlying variety of
\SL_{2}(\kk)
applied to the image of the closed embedding

\mathbb{A}^{2}\to\SL_{2}(\kk)
,
(s,t)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
t&1+st\end{pmatrix}
.

The above example shows also that we cannot hope for such an easy description of the closed subvarieties of an algebraic group with a dense set of torsion points as in Theorem
1.1
.
However, we may describe such closed subvarieties “up to conjugation”.
If
X\subseteq G
is a subset of an algebraic group
G
, then
C_{G}(X)
denotes the union of all conjugacy classes of elements in
X
.
Our first main result is a generalization of Theorem
1.1
to arbitrary connected algebraic groups
G
:

Theorem A

(see Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group and let
X\subseteq G
be a subset such that

X\cap G_{tor}
is dense in
X
. Then there exist finitely many commutative connected
algebraic subgroups
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\in G_{tor}
such that the closure of
C_{G}(X)
in
G
is given by

\overline{C_{G}(X)}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\overline{C_{G}(t_{i}S_{i})}\,,

t_{i}
commutes with
S_{i}
and
(S_{i})_{tor}
is dense in
S_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
.

Morally, we replace the points in a commutative algebraic group by conjugacy classes in an arbitrary algebraic group.
For an affine reductive algebraic group
G
this also gives the strategy of the proof; more precisely: Denote
by
T\subseteq G
a maximal torus. We may assume that
X
is closed and stable under
G
-conjugation. Then one
can show that
the torsion points
X\cap T_{tor}
lie dense in
X\cap T
by
using the categorical quotient
G\to G/\!/G
with respect to conjugation.
Thus we may apply Theorem
1.1
to the closed
subvariety
X\cap T
of the torus
T
and apply the operator
\overline{C_{G}(\cdot)}
in order to get the result.

It is far from obvious to determine
C_{G}(X)
, when
X
is given and to decide whether it is a finite union of conjugacy classes of translates of commutative algebraic groups. An example, where this is done for certain matrix polynomial is
[
Ost
, Theorem 1]
.

A drawback of the subvarieties
\overline{C_{G}(tS)}
of
G
from Theorem
A
is that
the intersection of two such subvarieties is in general not a finite union of such subvarities, see §
4.2
,
in contrast to the torsion-translates of algebraic subgroups in a commutative algebraic subgroup in Theorem
1.1
, see Lemma
5.10
.
This failure can be fixed by replacing “torsion”
G_{tor}
with the coarser notion of
“unipotent-torsion”
G_{u-tor}
,
i.e.
G_{u-tor}
consists of those

g\in G
such that
g^{n}
is unipotent for some integer
n>0
, see Definition
5.2
.
Our second main result is a variation of Theorem
A
to this “unipotent-torsion”-setting.

For the formulation, we need the morphism
\pi\colon G\to\hat{G}/\!/\hat{G}
, which is the composition of the quotient

G\to\hat{G}
by the unipotent radical of
G
followed by the categorical quotient

\hat{G}\to\hat{G}/\!/\hat{G}
with respect to conjugation, see §
2.1
and §
2.2
.

Theorem B

(see Theorem
5.12
)
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group and let
X\subseteq G
be a subset such that

X\cap G_{u-tor}
is dense in
X
. Then there exist commutative connected algebraic subgroups

S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in G_{u-tor}
such that

\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\pi^{-1}(\pi(h_{i}S_{i}))\,,

where
h_{i}
commutes with
S_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
.

The subvarieties
\pi^{-1}(\pi(hS))
, where
S
is a connected commutative algebraic subgroup of
G
and
h\in G_{u-tor}

commutes with
S
(as in Theorem
B
) we call
unipotent-special
, see Definition
5.6
.
One can in fact show that the unipotent-special subvarieties are
irreducible, closed in
G
and the subset of unipotent-torsion points lie dense in them.
Moreover, the intersection of two unipotent-special subvarieties is the finite union of unipotent-special subvarieties
(see Proposition
5.9
). This makes them good candidates for being “special subvarieties” analagous to special subvarieties in the unlikely intersections and the conjecture of Zilber-Pink. For more details we refer the reader to the work of Barroero and Dill,
[
BD21
, Theorem 4.1]
. However we immediately point out that unipotent-special subvarieties do not fit into the framework of distinguished categories, because of the lack of final objects (Axiom (A3)).

We note that the unipotent-special subvarieties generally are not connected components of algebraic subgroups.
However, in certain situations one can give an explicit description of them. As an example we consider
G=\SL_{2}(\kk)
:
Here the unipotent-special subvarities different from
\SL_{2}(\kk)

are given as follows: For every root of unity
t
the set of
matrices in
\SL_{2}(\kk)
such that its trace is equal to
t+t^{-1}
form a unipotent special subvariety.
The unipotent-special subvarities that contain a dense set of torsion points are those whose trace is not equal to
\pm 2
.
One can show with elementary computations that none of these unipotent-special subvarieties is
a connected component of an algebraic subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\kk)
.

The proofs of Theorem
A
and
B
are both by reduction to the commutative case,
i.e. to Theorem
1.1
.
More precisely, for Theorem
A
we first consider the case
F\times L
, where
F
is commutative and
L
is affine and reductive, see Proposition
4.10
. This case can be done essentially via intersecting the subset under consideration with
F\times T
,
where
T
is a maximal torus in
L
, similar to the affine reductive case
described directly after Theorem
A
. The first serious difficulty arises when we replace
L
by an arbitrary affine algebraic group
H
(see Proposition
4.11
),
because no categorical quotient with respect to conjugation exists.
Moreover, the torison points do not behave well under the quotient by the unipotent radical
R_{u}(H)
of
H

(see e.g. Example
3.4
).
We overcome these obstacles by using a certain stratified version of a geometric quotient with respect to
R_{u}(H)
-conjugation on
H
, see Lemma
4.12
. In order to get the theorem in the general case (Theorem
4.3
)
we then use the so-called Rosenlicht decomposition of an algebraic group

G
: there is a canonical homomorphism
G_{\ant}\times G_{\aff}\rightarrow G
(which might have a
non-trivial kernel), where
F=G_{\ant}
is a connected, commutative and
H=G_{\aff}
is a connected, affine
algebraic subgroup of
G
.
Using the solution to the problem for
F\times H
provides us then with a solution for
FH=G
.
For Theorem
B
, the proof turns out to be very analogous but the framework is much
more natural. Since every closed irreducible subset
X\subseteq G
that dominates
\hat{G}/\!/\hat{G}
has dense
unipotent-torsion points (see Corollary
5.4
), there is no hope to describe all closed subsets of
G
with dense unipotent-torsion points.
In particular, we are forced to divide by the unipotent radical of
G
and thus this quotient
gives no obstacle by design of the setup.

For the rest of this introduction, we assume that our ground field
\kk
is equal to the field of algebraic numbers

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Another result that is related to a translation from the commutative to the non-commutative setting
is Breuillard’s height gap theorem
[
Bre11
]
that may be viewed as an analogue of Lehmer’s conjecture. He constructs a height on finite sets of matrices that, if restricted to singletons, almost coincides with a height coming from the operator norm
[
Tal00
]
(see
[
Bre11
, Remark 2.20]
). This in turn coincides with a Weil-height if restricted to a torus (see
[
Bre11
, §1]
). In this article, we construct a canonical height on
G
that is strongly related to Breuillard’s height (see (
17
)), if
G
is affine and with the quadratic Néron-Tate height if
G
is an abelian variety.
Moreover, it is invariant under
G
-conjugation.
It has the further upside that it vanishes exactly on the unipotent-torsion points of
G
(see Lemma
6.6
).
A knee-jerk reaction is to ask whether, going beyond Theorem
B
a Bogomolov-type result, such as
[
Ull98
,
Zha98
]
is true with respect to this height. This is our next result.

Theorem C
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group defined over
\overline{\Q}
. Let
X\subseteq G
, be an irreducible subset such that
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
is not unipotent-special.
Then, for a canonical height
\hat{h}_{G}
on
G
, there exists an
\epsilon=\epsilon(X)>0
, such that the set

\{\,g\in\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})\ |\ \hat{h}_{G}(g)<\epsilon\,\}

is not dense in
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
.

For the exact definition of the height
\hat{h}_{G}

we refer the reader to Definition
6.1
,
6.3
and
6.4
.
This canonical height also satisfies a certain Northcott property in the sense that a set of semi-simple elements of bounded canonical height and degree over
\Q
is contained in finitely many conjugation classes of semi-simple elements.

Our proof of Theorem
C
goes by reducing to the commutative case and applying the main theorem in
[
DP00
]
(cf. also
[
Küh22
, Proposition 6.1]
). The main new input for Theorem C is the canonical height constructed above and the observation that it vanishes exactly on the unipotent-torsion points.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Some structure results about algebraic groups

Notation 2.1
.

Throughout this subsection
G
denotes a connected algebraic group.
Moreover,
G_{\aff}
denotes the largest connected affine algebraic subgroup of
G
and

G_{\ant}
denotes the smallest normal algebraic subgroup of
G
such that
G/G_{\ant}
is affine.

By
[
Ros56
, Theorem 16, p. 439]
,
G_{\aff}
is the unique closed normal
connected affine subgroup of
G
such that
G/G_{\aff}
is abelian.
The subgroup
G_{\ant}
is connected, anti-affine (i.e.
\kk[G_{\ant}]=\kk
) and central in
G

(see
[
DG70
, Ch. III, §3, Théorème 8.2, Corollaire 8.3]
), and

G
can be written as
G=G_{\ant}G_{\aff}
(so called “Rosenlicht decomposition”,
see
[
Ros56
, Corollary 5, p. 440]
).

Moreover, we will constantly use the fact that for a homomorphism
f\colon G\to H
of algebraic groups the image

f(G)
is closed in
H
, see e.g.
[
GP11
, Exp. IV
B
, Proposition 1.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f\colon G\to H
be a surjective
homomorphism of connected algebraic groups. Then
f(G_{\ant})=H_{\ant}

and
f(G_{\aff})=H_{\aff}
.

Proof.

Since
\kk[G_{\ant}]=\kk
, we get
\kk[f(G_{\ant})]=\kk
. In particular,
f(G_{\ant})

maps to the neutral element via
H\to H/H_{\ant}
, as
H/H_{\ant}
is affine. Hence

f(G_{\ant})\subseteq H_{\ant}
. On the other hand,
f
descents to a surjection

G/G_{\ant}\to H/f(G_{\ant})
and since
G/G_{\ant}
is affine, it follows that
H/f(G_{\ant})
is affine
(see e.g.
[
Ros56
, Corollary 2, p. 440]
). This shows that
H_{\ant}\subseteq f(G_{\ant})
.

Note that
f(G_{\aff})
is a connected affine subgroup of
H
.
As
f
is surjective,
f(G_{\aff})
is normal in
H
and
f
descents to a surjection

G/G_{\aff}\to H/f(G_{\aff})
. Since
G/G_{\aff}
is abelian, it follows that
H/f(G_{\aff})
is abelian as well.
Hence,
f(G_{\aff})=H_{\aff}
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The proof shows: If
f\colon G\to H
is only a homomorphism of connected algebraic groups, then
we have still
f(G_{\ant})\subseteq H_{\ant}
and
f(G_{\aff})\subseteq H_{\aff}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The unipotent radical
R_{u}(G_{\aff})
of
G_{\aff}
is the largest
closed normal unipotent subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

Let
N\subseteq G
be a closed normal unipotent subgroup of
G
.
As
N
is solvable, it is affine (see
[
Ros56
, Corollary 4, p. 440]
).
Hence
N
is contained in
G_{\aff}
and thus
N\subseteq R_{u}(G_{\aff})
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\hat{G}\coloneqq G/R_{u}(G_{\aff})
. Then
\hat{G}_{\ant}
is a semi-abelian variety (i.e. an algebraic group
extension of an abelian variety by some torus) and
\hat{G}_{\aff}
is an affine reductive group (i.e.
R_{u}(\hat{G}_{\aff})
is trivial).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, the canonical projection
p\colon G\to\hat{G}
restricts
to a surjection
q\colon(G_{\ant})_{\aff}\to(\hat{G}_{\ant})_{\aff}
with
\ker(q)=R_{u}(G_{\aff})\cap(G_{\ant})_{\aff}
.
As
R_{u}((G_{\ant})_{\aff})
is the largest unipotent subgroup of
G_{\ant}

(by Lemma
2.4
applied to the commutative group
G_{\ant}
), we get

R_{u}((G_{\ant})_{\aff})=\ker(q)
. Thus
(\hat{G}_{\ant})_{\aff}\simeq(G_{\ant})_{\aff}/\ker(q)
is a torus.
Now,
\hat{G}_{\ant}
is semi-abelian, as an extension of the abelian variety
\hat{G}_{\ant}/(\hat{G}_{\ant})_{\aff}
by the torus
(\hat{G}_{\ant})_{\aff}
.

Again by Lemma
2.2
,
p\colon G\to\hat{G}
restricts to a surjection
G_{\aff}\to\hat{G}_{\aff}

with kernel
R_{u}(G_{\aff})
. Hence
\hat{G}_{\aff}
is affine and reductive.
∎

For the rest of this subsection, we focus on the case, where
G_{\ant}
is semi-abelian.

Definition 2.6
.

Assume that
G_{\ant}
is semi-abelian.
We call an element
g\in G

semisimple
if
g=g_{1}g_{2}
, where

g_{1}\in G_{\ant}
and
g_{2}\in G_{\aff}
is semisimple (in the usual sense).

If
f\colon G\to H
is a homomorphism of connected algebraic groups where
G_{\ant}
and
H_{\ant}
are semi-abelian,
then for every semisimple
g\in G
the image
f(g)
is semisimple in
H
, since

f(G_{\ant})\subseteq H_{\ant}
and
f(G_{\aff})\subseteq H_{\aff}

(see Remark
2.3
).

Moreover, we have the following description of the torsion points:

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that
G_{\ant}
is semi-abelian and fix a maximal torus
T\subseteq G_{\aff}
.
Then, the conjugates of the

a)

elements in
G_{\ant}T
are exactly the semisimple elements in
G
.

b)

torsion points in
G_{\ant}T
are exactly the torsion points in
G
.

Proof.

a)
: This follows from the fact that the semisimple elements of
G_{\aff}

are exactly the conjugates of the elements in
T
, see
[
Hum75
, Theorem 22.2]
.

b)
:
Let
g\in G
be a torsion point and write
g=g_{1}g_{2}
, where
g_{1}\in G_{\ant}
and
g_{2}\in G_{\aff}
.
Then there exists
n>0
such that
1=g^{n}=g_{1}^{n}g_{2}^{n}
. By possibly enlarging
n
,
we may assume that
g_{1}^{-n}=g_{2}^{n}\in(G_{\ant}\cap G_{\aff})^{\circ}\eqqcolon F
.
Since
F
is a torus, it follows that
g_{2}^{n}
is semisimple. Using the classical Jordan decomposition of

g_{2}
in
G_{\aff}
yields that
g_{2}
is semisimple. Hence, there exists
a\in G_{\aff}
with
ag_{2}a^{-1}\in T

and thus
g
is conjugated to
g_{1}ag_{2}a^{-1}\in G_{\ant}T
.
∎

We finish this subsection with a description of the Jordan decomposition in case
G_{\ant}
is semi-abelian.
Note that we don’t have such a Jordan decomposition in general, even not in case when
G
is commutative.

Lemma and Definition 2.8

(Jordan decomposition)
.

Assume that
G_{\ant}
is semi-abelian.
Let
g\in G
. Then there exist unique
g_{u},g_{s}\in G
such that
g=g_{u}g_{s}
,
g_{u}
is unipotent,

g_{s}
is semisimple and
g_{u},g_{s}
commute.
Moreover, if
g
commutes with some
b\in G
, then
g_{s}
and
g_{u}
commute with
b
as well.

The elements
g_{u}
and
g_{s}
are called
unipotent part
and
semisimple part
of
g
, respectively.

Proof.

We will constantly use that
G_{\ant}
is central in
G
.

Assume that
g=us
, where
u
and
s
are unipotent and semisimple, respectively, and
us=su
.
Let
b\in G
such that
b
commutes with
g
. Write
b=b_{1}b_{2}
and
s=s_{1}s_{2}
,
where
b_{1},s_{1}\in G_{\ant}
,
b_{2},s_{2}\in G_{\aff}
and
s_{2}
is semisimple. Then
us_{2}=s_{2}u

and
b
commutes with
us_{2}
and hence
b_{2}
commutes with
us_{2}
. Since
b_{2},u,s_{2}\in G_{\aff}
,
it follows that
b_{2}
commutes with
u
and with
s_{2}

(see e.g.
[
Hum75
, Lemma 15.1B]
). Hence,
b
commutes with
u
and
s
.

Assume that
g=us=u^{\prime}s^{\prime}
, where
u,s
commute,
u^{\prime},s^{\prime}
commute,
u,u^{\prime}
are unipotent and
s,s^{\prime}
are semisimple.
Since
u^{\prime}
commutes with
g
, it follows by the previous paragraph that
u^{\prime}
commutes with
u
. Analogously,

s^{\prime}
commutes with
s
. Hence
(u^{\prime})^{-1}u
is unipotent and
s^{\prime}s^{-1}
is semisimple, i.e.
s^{\prime}s^{-1}\in G_{\ant}T

for some maximal torus
T\subseteq G_{\aff}
. Since
(u^{\prime})^{-1}u=s^{\prime}s^{-1}\in G_{\ant}T
and since
G_{\ant}T
is semi-abelian,
we get that
u^{\prime}=u
and thus

s^{\prime}=s
. This shows the uniqueness of the decomposition.

Write
g=g_{1}g_{2}
, where
g_{1}\in G_{\ant}
and
g_{2}\in G_{\aff}
. Let
(g_{2})_{u}
and
(g_{2})_{s}

be the unipotent and semisimple part of
g_{2}
in
G_{\aff}
,
respectively, see
[
Hum75
, Lemma 15.1B]
. Then
g=(g_{2})_{u}g_{1}(g_{2})_{s}
,
(g_{2})_{u}
is unipotent,
g_{1}(g_{2})_{s}
is semisimple and

(g_{2})_{u},g_{1}(g_{2})_{s}
commute.
This proves the existence of the decomposition.
∎

2.2.
Conjugation in algebraic groups with trivial unipotent radical

Notation 2.9
.

Throughout this subsection
G
denotes a connected algebraic group such that the unipotent radical

R_{u}(G_{\aff})
is trivial. Let
Z\subseteq G
be the connected component of the center of
G

(

(

∎

Lemma 2.19
.

Consider

∎

Corollary 2.20
.

For every irreducible closed subvariety

, the

is irreducible.

Proof.

3.
Commutative algebraic groups with dense torsion subgroup

This section is devoted to a characterization of commutative algebraic groups
with dense torsion subgroup
G_{tor}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a commutative algebraic group. Then

G_{tor}
is dense in
G
if and only if
every homomorphism
\varphi\colon G\to\mathbb{G}_{a}
is constant.

For the proof we need some preparation.

Lemma 3.2
.

Every commutative connected algebraic group is divisible.

Proof.

Let
H
be a commutative connected algebraic group. Then

H_{\aff}
is a connected commutative affine algebraic group and hence it is divisible.
Moreover, as the quotient
H/H_{\aff}
is an abelian variety, it is divisible, and thus

H
is divisible as an extension of commutative divisible groups.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If
G
is a commutative algebraic group, then
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}G^{\circ}
where

t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in G
are torsion points and
G^{\circ}
denotes the connected component of
G
.

Proof.

Let
n
be the index of
G^{\circ}
in
G
. Then there exist
g_{1},\ldots g_{n}\in G

such that
G
is the union of the subsets
g_{1}G^{\circ},\ldots,g_{n}G^{\circ}
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
G^{\circ}
is divisible. As

g_{i}^{n}\in G^{\circ}
, there exists thus
a_{i}\in G^{\circ}
such that
g_{i}^{n}=a_{i}^{n}
.
Then the torsion elements
t_{i}=g_{i}a_{i}^{-1}\in G
,
i=1,\ldots n
will do the job.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

If
G_{tor}
is dense in
G
, then every homomorphism
G\to\mathbb{G}_{a}
is constant.
Now, assume that every homomorphism
G\to\mathbb{G}_{a}
is constant.

Let
G^{\circ}
be the connected component of
G
. If
(G^{\circ})_{tor}
is dense in
G^{\circ}
, then

G_{tor}
is dense in
G
by Lemma
3.3
. Thus we may and will assume that

G
is commutative and connected.

If
\dim G=0
, then the lemma is trivial. Hence we assume
\dim G>0
.
If
G
has only finitely many torsion points, then
G
is affine (as
G_{\aff}
is divisible and
(G/G_{\aff})_{tor}

lies dense in
G/G_{\aff}
, see
Lemma
3.2
) and thus
G\simeq\mathbb{G}_{a}^{r}
for some
r>0
.
As every homomorphism
G\to\mathbb{G}_{a}
is constant, we conclude that
G
contains infinitely many
torsion points.
Moreover, as
G
is commutative, the torsion points
G_{tor}
from a normal subgroup of
G
. As
G
contains infinitely many torsion points, the connected component of the closure

H\coloneqq\overline{G_{tor}}^{\circ}\subseteq G
is a normal connected subgroup of positive dimension, which
is divisible (see Lemma
3.2
).
This implies that the canonical projection

G\to G/H
restricts to a surjection
G_{tor}\to(G/H)_{tor}
.
As
\dim G/H<\dim G
, we conclude the proposition by induction.
∎

The following example shows that already in a solvable (non-commutative) algebraic group the set
of torsion points can be rather complicate to describe:

Example 3.4
.

Let
B\subseteq\GL_{3}(\kk)
be the subgroup of upper triangular matrices. Then we may and will identify

B
with
H\rtimes T
, where
T\subseteq B
denotes the subgroup of diagonal matrices,

H\subseteq B
denotes the subgroup of upper triangular matrices with only
1
on the diagonal
and the product is given by
(A,S)\cdot(A^{\prime},S^{\prime})=(ASA^{\prime}S^{-1},SS^{\prime})
.
We will compute the set of torsion points
B_{tor}
. For this we insert some notation:

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\in T\ \Big|\ \lambda=\mu=\varepsilon,\, \textrm{$\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon$ are roots of unity in $\kk$}\,\right\}

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\in T\ \Big|\ \lambda\neq\mu=\varepsilon,\, \textrm{$\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon$ are roots of unity in $\kk$}\,\right\}

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\in T\ \Big|\ \varepsilon\neq\lambda=\mu,\, \textrm{$\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon$ are roots of unity in $\kk$}\,\right\}

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\in T\ \Big|\ \mu\neq\varepsilon=\lambda,\, \textrm{$\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon$ are roots of unity in $\kk$}\,\right\}

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\in T\ \Big|\ \lambda\neq\mu\neq\varepsilon\neq\lambda,\, \textrm{$\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon$ are roots of unity in $\kk$}\,\right\}\,.

We claim that there is a decomposition into disjoint subsets

B_{tor}=\{I_{3}\}\times S_{0}\cup V_{H\times S_{1}}(c)\cup V_{H\times S_{2}}(a)\cup V_{H\times S_{3}}((1-\lambda\mu^{-1})b-ac)\cup H\times S_{4}\,,

where for
f\in\kk[a,b,c,\lambda^{\pm 1},\mu^{\pm 1},\varepsilon^{\pm 1}]
and
i\in\{1,2,3\}
we denote

V_{H\times S_{i}}(f)\coloneqq\left\{\,\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\
0&1&c\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\right)\in H\times S_{i}\ \Big|\ f(a,b,c,\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon)=0\,\right\}

and
I_{3}
denotes the identity matrix. Indeed, fix an integer
n>0
and let

P=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\
0&1&c\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\right)\in H\rtimes T\,,\quad\theta\coloneqq\lambda\mu^{-1}\,,\quad\eta\coloneqq\mu\varepsilon^{-1}\,.

Using that

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\
0&1&c\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{-1}&0&0\\
0&\mu^{-1}&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\theta a&(\theta\eta)b\\
0&1&\eta c\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

it follows that
P^{n}
is the neutral element in
H\rtimes T
if and only if

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}^{n}=I_{3}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\theta^{i}a&(\theta\eta)^{i}b\\
0&1&\eta^{i}c\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=I_{3}\,.

A calculation shows that this last assertion is equivalent to
\lambda^{n}=\mu^{n}=\varepsilon^{n}=1

and

(4)

a\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\theta^{i}=0\,,\ b\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(\theta\eta)^{i}+acS(\theta,\eta)=0\,,\ c\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\eta^{i}=0\,,

where

S(\theta,\eta)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\theta^{j}\right)\eta^{i}\,.

Note that in case
\theta\neq 1
we get

S(\theta,\eta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1-\theta^{i}}{1-\theta}\eta^{i}=\frac{1}{1-\theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\eta^{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\theta\eta)^{i}\right)\,.

Now, we fix
\lambda,\mu,\varepsilon
with
\lambda^{n}=\mu^{n}=\varepsilon^{n}=1
and make a case-by-case analysis:

0)

\theta=1
,
\eta=1
,
\theta\eta=1
: (
4
) is equivalent to
a=b=c=0
.

1)

\theta\neq 1
,
\eta=1
,
\theta\eta\neq 1
: (
4
) is equivalent to
c=0
.

2)

\theta=1
,
\eta\neq 1
,
\theta\eta\neq 1
: (
4
) is equivalent to
a=0
.

3)

\theta\neq 1
,
\eta\neq 1
,
\theta\eta=1
: (
4
) is equivalent to
(1-\theta)b-ac=0
.

4)

\theta\neq 1
,
\eta\neq 1
,
\theta\eta\neq 1
: (
4
) gives no condition.

This case-by-case analysis gives the claim, as for each
i=0,\ldots,4
,
case
i)
corresponds to

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\in S_{i}\,.

In the next example we show that Proposition
3.1
fails for non-commutative groups.

Example 3.5
.

Again, let
H\subseteq\GL_{3}(\kk)
be the subgroup of upper triangular matrices with only
1
on the diagonal
and let

T_{0}\coloneqq\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}\ |\ \lambda,\mu\in\kk^{\ast}\,\}\subseteq\GL_{3}(\kk)\,.

Then
T_{0}
acts on
H
by conjugation and by Example
3.4
it follows that
the closure of the torsion points in
H\rtimes T_{0}
is given by

A\coloneqq\overline{(H\rtimes T_{0})_{tor}}=\left\{\,\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\
0&1&c\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}\right)\ \Big|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\textrm{$a,b,c\in\kk$, $\lambda,\mu\in\kk^{\ast}$,}\\
\textrm{$(1-\lambda\mu^{-1})b=ac$}\end{array}\,\right\}\,.

Then
A
is an irreducible hypersurface in
H\rtimes T_{0}
. Though
A
is invariant under conjugation,
but
A
is not a subgroup of
H\rtimes T_{0}
, as the following example shows

\underbrace{\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,I_{3}\right)}_{\in A}\underbrace{\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,I_{3}\right)}_{\in A}=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,I_{3}\right)\not\in A\,,

where
I_{3}
denotes the identity matrix in
\GL_{3}(\kk)
. Every homomorphism
\varphi\colon H\rtimes T_{0}\to\mathbb{G}_{a}

has
A
in the kernel. Since
A
is a hypersurface in
H\rtimes T_{0}
, but not a subgroup,
the kernel of
\varphi
is the whole group
H\rtimes T_{0}
, i.e.
\varphi
is constant.

4.
Manin-Mumford theorem for connected algebraic groups

Notation 4.1
.

Throughout this section, let
G
be a connected algebraic group.
For a subgroup
H\subseteq G
and a subset
X\subseteq G
denote

C_{G}^{H}(X)=\{\,hxh^{-1}\in G\ |\ h\in H,x\in X\,\}\,.

If
H=G
, we simply write
C_{G}(X)

instead of
C_{G}^{G}(X)
. Moreover,
Z_{G}(X)
denotes the subgroup
of elements in
G
that commute with each
x\in X
.

Definition 4.2
.

If
X\subseteq G
is a subset, then we denote by
X_{tor}
the torsion elements from
G
that belong to
X
.
We call the elements in

\left\{\,\overline{C_{G}(tS)}\ \Big|\ \begin{array}[]{l}\textrm{$S$ is a connected commutative subgroup of $G$}\\
\textrm{such that $S_{tor}$ is dense in $S$ and $t\in Z_{G}(S)_{tor}$}\end{array}\,\right\}

special subvarieties
of
G
.

In case
G
is commutative, the special subvarieties are the closed subvarieties of the form

tS
, where
t\in G
is a torsion point and
S\subseteq G
is a commutative connected algebraic subgroup
such that
S_{tor}
is dense in
S
. Hence, these correspond exactly to the closed subvarieties in the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
.

The aim of this section is to prove the following generalization of
the Manin-Mumford Theorem (Theorem
1.1
)
for not necessarily commutative algebraic groups:

Theorem 4.3

(Manin-Mumford for algebraic groups)
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic group and let
X\subseteq G
be a subset.
If
X_{tor}
is dense in
X
,
then
\overline{C_{G}(X)}
is a finite union of special subvarieties of
G
.

In the first section, we gather some simple topological ingredients that are needed for the proof of Theorem
4.3

and in the second section we provide an example that shows that the intersection of two
special subvarieties need not be the finite union of special subvarieties. In the third section, we prove Theorem
4.3
for certain special cases
and reduced the full proof of Theorem
4.3
to this special cases at the end of this third section.

4.1.
Simple topological ingredients

Here we list several simple topological lemmas that we use in the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous surjective
map between topological spaces. Then:

(1)

If
X_{0}
is dense in
X
, then
f(X_{0})
is dense in
Y

(2)

If
Y_{0}
is dense in
Y
,
f
is closed and

\overline{f^{-1}(Y_{0})}=f^{-1}(Y_{1})
for some
Y_{1}\subseteq Y
,
then
f^{-1}(Y_{0})
is dense in
X
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

(
1
): Note that

Y=f(X)=f(\overline{X_{0}})\subseteq\overline{f(X_{0})}\subseteq Y
.

(
2
): Note that
Y_{0}\subseteq f(\overline{f^{-1}(Y_{0})})=Y_{1}
. Since
f
is closed and
Y_{0}

is dense in
Y
, we get
Y_{1}=Y
, i.e.
X=f^{-1}(Y)=\overline{f^{-1}(Y_{0})}
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
H
be a finite group that acts on a variety
X
such that the geometric
quotient
\eta\colon X\to X/H\eqqcolon Y
exists (e.g.
X
is quasi-projective).
If
Y_{0}
is dense in
Y
, then
\eta^{-1}(Y_{0})
is dense in
X
.

Proof.

Since
\eta^{-1}(Y_{0})
is
H
-stable, its closure
\overline{\eta^{-1}(Y_{0})}
in
X
is
H
-stable as well, i.e. there
exists a subset
Y_{1}\subseteq Y
such that
\overline{\eta^{-1}(Y_{0})}=\eta^{-1}(Y_{1})
. The claim follows now from
Lemma
4.4
2
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X^{\prime}\subseteq X\subseteq G
, where
X^{\prime}
is dense in
X
.
Then for any closed subgroup
H\subseteq G
,
C_{G}^{H}(X^{\prime})
is dense in
C_{G}^{H}(X)
.

Proof.

Consider the morphism
f\colon H\times G\to G
given by
(h,g)\mapsto hgh^{-1}
.
Then we get
C_{G}^{H}(X)=f(H\times X)=f(\overline{H\times X^{\prime}})\subseteq\overline{f(H\times X^{\prime})}=\overline{C_{G}^{H}(X^{\prime})}
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

If
X\subseteq G
is a subset and
X_{tor}
is dense in
X
,
then
(\overline{C_{G}(X)})_{tor}
is dense in
\overline{C_{G}(X)}
.

Proof of Corollary
4.7
.

We apply Lemma
4.6
to
X^{\prime}\coloneqq X_{tor}\subseteq X\subseteq G=H

in order to get that
C_{G}(X_{tor})=C_{G}(X)_{tor}
is dense in
C_{G}(X)
.
Hence the statement follows.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
X\subseteq G
be a special subvariety. Then:

(1)

X_{tor}
is dense in
X
;
in particular, special subvarieties of
G
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
.

(2)

If
p\colon G\to G^{\prime}
is an isogeny of algebraic groups,
then the image
p(X)
is a special subvariety of
G^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq G
be a commutative connected subgroup and let
t\in Z_{G}(S)_{tor}

such that
X=\overline{C_{G}(tS)}
and
S_{tor}
is dense in
S
.

(
1
): Corollary
4.7

implies that
(\overline{C_{G}(tS)})_{tor}
is dense in
\overline{C_{G}(tS)}
,
since
tS_{tor}
is dense in
tS
.

(
2
):
p(S)
is a closed commutative connected subgroup of
G^{\prime}
.
Moreover, since
p
is an isogeny, we get
p(S)_{tor}=p(S_{tor})
and this set
is dense in
p(S)
by Lemma
4.4
(
1
).
Using that
t\in Z_{G}(S)_{tor}
, we get
p(t)\in Z_{G^{\prime}}(p(S))_{tor}
. Note that
p(C_{G}(tS))=C_{G^{\prime}}(p(t)p(S))
.
Using that
p(\overline{Y})=\overline{p(Y)}
for any subset
Y\subseteq G
(here we use that
p
is closed),
the claim follows.
∎

4.2.
Intersection of special subvarieties

The following example shows that the intersection of two special subvarieties of a connected algebraic group
need not be the finite union of special subvarieties:

Example 4.9
.

Let
G=\GL_{2}(\kk)
and for
k\geq 2
consider

S_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\,A_{k,\lambda}\ \Big|\ \lambda\in\kk^{\ast}\,\right\}\subseteq T\coloneqq\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}\ \Big|\ \lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\kk^{\ast}\,\right\}\subseteq\GL_{2}(\kk)\,,

where

A_{k,\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{k}& 0\\
0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}\in\GL_{2}(\kk)\,.

The algebraic quotient by conjugation on
\GL_{2}(\kk)
is then given by

\pi\colon\GL_{2}(\kk)\to Z\coloneqq\mathbb{A}^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}\setminus\{0\})\,,\quad A\mapsto(\tr(A),\det(A))\,,

where
\tr(A)
denotes the trace of
A
, see

[
Kra84
, II.3.3, Beispiel 2]
. Moreover, denote by

\rho\coloneqq\pi|_{T}\colon T\to Z\,,\quad\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})

the restriction of
\pi
to
T
.
Since
\rho
is finite (it is the geometric quotient by the

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-action that exchanges the two diagonal elements,
see e.g. Corollary
2.17
), it follows that

\rho(S_{k})
is an irreducible closed curve in
Z
.
Hence,

(5)

\overline{C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k})}\subseteq\pi^{-1}(\rho(S_{k}))\,.

On the other hand, we have
\lambda^{k}=\lambda
if and only if
\lambda
is a
(k-1)
-th root of unity.
Thus if
\lambda^{k-1}\neq 1
, then the fibre
\pi^{-1}(\rho(A_{k,\lambda}))

consists of a single conjugacy class.
Hence, the non-empty open subvariety

U_{k}\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(\rho(\{\,A_{k,\lambda}\ |\ \lambda^{k-1}\neq 1\,\}))

of
\pi^{-1}(\rho(S_{k}))
is contained in
\overline{C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k})}
. Since the fibres of
\pi
are all
two-dimensional (Corollary
2.18
(
5
)),
and since
\pi^{-1}(\rho(S_{k}))
is irreducible (Corollary
2.20
),
it follows that the inclusion in (

) is in fact an equality.
Moreover
C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k})=U_{k}\cup S_{k}
is a constructible, but non-open subset in its closure.
Hence,
C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k})
is not locally closed in
\GL_{2}(\kk)
.

Now, let
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 2
be distinct. If
\lambda,\mu\in\kk^{\ast}
, then we have

\rho(A_{k_{1},\lambda})=\rho\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{k_{1}}&0\\
0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}=\rho\begin{pmatrix}\mu^{k_{2}}&0\\
0&\mu\end{pmatrix}=\rho(A_{k_{2},\mu})

exactly when
\{\lambda^{k_{1}},\lambda\}=\{\mu^{k_{2}},\mu\}
. This last condition is equivalent to the fact that
either
\mu=\lambda
,
\lambda^{k_{1}-k_{2}}=1
or
\mu=\lambda^{k_{1}}
,
\lambda^{k_{1}k_{2}-1}=1
.
This implies

\displaystyle\overline{C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k_{1}})}\cap\overline{C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k_{2}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\rho(S_{k_{1}})\cap\rho(S_{k_{2}}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\coprod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda^{k_{1}k_{2}-1}=1\ \textrm{or}\\
\lambda^{k_{1}-k_{2}}=1\end{subarray}}\pi^{-1}(\rho(A_{k_{1},\lambda}))\,.

Since
N\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(\rho(A_{k_{1},1}))
is two dimensional, but contains only the identity matrix

A_{k_{1},1}
as a semi-simple
\GL_{2}(\kk)
-conjugacy class, the torsion points

are not dense in

(see Corollary

2.18
(

),(

)).
Thus the intersection
\overline{C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k_{1}})}\cap\overline{C_{\GL_{2}(\kk)}(S_{k_{2}})}

cannot be the finite union of special subvarieties in

according to Corollary

(

).

4.3.
The proof of the Manin-Mumford theorem for algebraic groups

Before we give a proof of Theorem

we establish the case when the
connected algebraic group is
a certain product and reduce then Theorem

to this special case.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
G=F\times L
where
F
is a connected commutative group and
L
is a connected
affine reductive group.
If
X\subseteq G
is closed, stable under
G
-conjugation and
X_{tor}
is dense in

,
then

is a finite union of special subvarieties in
G
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{L}\colon L\to L/\!/L
be the algebraic quotient of the
L
-action by conjugation on
L

and let
\rho_{L}\coloneqq\pi_{L}|_{T}\colon T\to L/\!/L
be its restriction to a
maximal torus

in
L
. Then
\rho_{L}
is the geometric quotient of
T
by conjugation
with the Weyl group of
L
with respect to

, see Corollary
2.17
.
Moreover, consider the morphisms

\pi\coloneqq\mathrm{id}_{F}\times\pi_{L}\colon F\times L\to F\times(L/\!/L)

and

\rho\coloneqq\mathrm{id}_{F}\times\rho_{L}\colon F\times T\to F\times(L/\!/L)\,.

Let
Y\coloneqq\pi(X)
. We have

(6)

\rho^{-1}(Y)=X\cap(F\times T)\,,

since we get for all
f\in F
by Corollary
2.18
(
6
) that

\displaystyle\rho^{-1}(Y)\cap(\{f\}\times T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\pi(X\cap(\{f\}\times L)))\cap(\{f\}\times T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{f\}\times\left(\pi_{L}^{-1}(\pi_{L}(\{\,l\in L\ |\ (f,l)\in X\,\}))\cap T\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{f\}\times(\{\,l\in L\ |\ (f,l)\in X\,\}\cap T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\cap(\{f\}\times T)

(we used the fact that
\{\,l\in L\ |\ (f,l)\in X\,\}
is stable under conjugation by
L
and it is closed in
L
).
Since
\rho_{L}\colon T\to L/\!/L
is finite, the same folds for
\rho
. In particular

Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq.Y}}}{{=}}\rho(X\cap(F\times T))

is closed in
F\times T
.

By Lemma
4.4
(
1
)
\pi(X_{tor})
is dense in
Y
.
In particular,
Y\cap\pi(G_{tor})
is dense in
Y
.
By Corollary
4.5
applied to the geometric quotient

\rho|_{\rho^{-1}(Y)}\colon\rho^{-1}(Y)\to Y
and the dense subset

of

,
we get that
\rho^{-1}(Y)\cap\rho^{-1}(\pi(G_{tor}))
is dense in
\rho^{-1}(Y)
.

Note that
G_{tor}=F_{tor}\times C_{L}(T_{tor})
and therefore

\pi(G_{tor})=F_{tor}\times\rho_{L}(T_{tor})
. Hence we get

(7)

\rho^{-1}(\pi(G_{tor}))=\rho^{-1}(F_{tor}\times\rho_{L}(T_{tor}))=F_{tor}\times T_{tor}\,,

since
T_{tor}
is invariant under the Weyl group action
by conjugation on
T
.

Using (
6
), (
7
), it follows that

X\cap(F_{tor}\times T_{tor})
is dense in
X\cap(F\times T)
.
By applying the
Manin-Mumford Theorem for commutative algebraic groups
(see Theorem

) to

inside

,
we get connected closed subgroups

of

and

(8)

such that
(S_{i})_{tor}
is dense in
S_{i}
for all
i
.
Hence,
\overline{C_{G}(h_{i}S_{i})}
is a special subvariety of
G
for all

.

Since
X
is stable under
G
-conjugation and since
X
is closed in
G
, we get

X_{tor}\subseteq C_{G}(X\cap(F\times T))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq.MM_comm}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}C_{G}(h_{i}S_{i})\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\overline{C_{G}(h_{i}S_{i})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Eq.MM_comm}}}{{\subseteq}}X\,.

Since

Proposition 4.11
.

For the proof we need the following lemma:

Lemma 4.12

Let

be a connected affine algebraic group with unipotent radical

, where

By a repeated application of a certain version of Rosenlicht’s theorem (see

[

[

d)

e)
and

.

over

Since the morphism

Notation 5.1

(with respect to the Zariski topology) and we denote this bundle again by
\pi_{A}\colon\hat{S}_{\text{eq.}}\to A
.
We set
D_{\infty}
to be the divisor at infinity
D_{\infty}=\hat{S}_{eq.}\setminus\hat{S}
endowed
with the reduced scheme structure. For every integer
n\geq 2
, it holds that

[n]^{*}D_{\infty}\ \sim\ nD_{\infty}\quad\textrm{are linearly equivalent}\,,

where
[n]
is the multiplication by
n
morphism on
\hat{S}

[
Hin88
, Lemme 2(ii)]
. We set

\hat{h}_{\ell}\colon\hat{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,,\quad g\mapsto\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{h_{D_{\infty}}([2^{k}]g)}{2^{k}}\,,

where
h_{D_{\infty}}
is the Weil-height on
\hat{S}_{\text{eq.}}
attached to
D_{\infty}
(see
[
HS00
, Theorem B.3.2]
). This limit exists
and is non-negative, which can be shown using the Néron-Tate limiting argument
and the fact that
D_{\infty}
is an effective divisor such that the base-locus of
|D|
is outside of
\hat{S}
,

[
HS00
, Theorem B.4.1, Theorem B.3.2(e)]
. Note that if the forward orbit
\{\,[2^{k}]g\ |\ k\in\mathbb{N}\,\}
under
[2]

is finite for some
g\in\hat{S}
, then
\hat{h}_{\ell}(g)=0
.

We also choose an ample symmetric divisor
L
on
A

(e.g. one can take any very ample divisor
H
on
A
and choose
L=H+[-1]^{\ast}H
). Then for any
n\geq 2

[n]^{*}L\ \sim\ n^{2}L\quad\textrm{are linearly equivalent}\,,

see e.g.
[
HS00
, Corollary A.7.2.5]
. We set

\hat{h}_{q}\colon\hat{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,,\quad g\mapsto\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{h_{L}([2^{k}]\pi_{A}(g))}{4^{k}}\,,

where
h_{L}
is the Weil-height on
A
attached to
L
. This limit exists, is non-negative and
\hat{h}_{q}(g)
vanishes
if and only if
\pi_{A}(g)
is a torsion point of
A
,
[
HS00
, Theorem B.4.1, Proposition B.5.3(a)]
.

Definition 6.1
.

We define the
canonical height

\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}
on
\hat{S}
to be

\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}=\hat{h}_{q}+\hat{h}_{\ell}\,.

Using
[
HS00
, Theorem B.4.1(i), Theorem B.3.2(b),(c)]
it follows that

\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}
is the same as the Weil-height
h_{D_{\infty}+\pi_{A}^{\ast}(L)}
up to a bounded function.
By
[
Hin88
, Lemme 1]
the divisor
D_{\infty}+\pi_{A}^{\ast}(L)
is ample. Thus

h_{D_{\infty}+\pi_{A}^{\ast}(L)}
(and hence
\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}
)
satisfies the Northcott property
[
HS00
, Theorem B.3.2(g)]
.

Remark 6.2
.

The height
\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}
vanishes exactly on the torsion points of
\hat{S}
. Indeed, let
g\in\hat{S}
.
If
g
is a torsion point, then
\{\,[2^{k}]g\ |\ k\in\mathbb{N}\,\}
is finite and thus
\hat{h}_{q}
,

\hat{h}_{\ell}
both vanish on
g
. On the other hand, if
\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}(g)=0
, then
\pi_{A}(g)
is a torsion point, i.e. there exists
m\geq 2
such that
[m]g\in\hat{T}
. By
[
HS00
, Theorem B.3.2(b),(c),(d)]

there is a constant
C_{m}
such that
|h_{D^{\infty}}([m]q)-mh_{D^{\infty}}(q)|<C_{m}

for all
q\in\hat{S}
. By replacing
q
with

[2^{k}]g
, dividing by
2^{k}
and taking the limit we get thus

\hat{h}_{\ell}([m]g)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{h_{D_{\infty}}([m2^{k}]g)}{2^{k}}=m\hat{h}_{\ell}(g)=0\,.

Let
\iota\colon(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{t}\hookrightarrow\hat{S}_{\text{eq.}}
be a closed
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{t}
-equivariant
immersion with image the fibre of
\pi_{A}
over the neutral element of
A
. Note that

(15)

[2]^{\ast}\iota^{\ast}D_{\infty}=\iota^{\ast}[2]^{\ast}D_{\infty}\ \sim\ 2\iota^{\ast}D_{\infty}\,.

Let
P\in(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{t}
with
\iota(P)=[m]g
. There is a constant
C
such that

|h_{\iota^{\ast}D^{\infty}}([2^{k}]P)-h_{D^{\infty}}([2^{k}]\iota(P))|<C\quad\textrm{for all $k\geq 0$}

by
[
HS00
, Theorem B.3.2(b)]
. Dividing by
2^{k}
and taking the limit gives:

0=\hat{h}_{\ell}([m]g)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{h_{\iota^{\ast}D^{\infty}}([2^{k}]P)}{2^{k}}\,.

Since
\iota^{\ast}D_{\infty}
is (very) ample and using (
15
), it follows that

P
is a torsion point of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{t}
and hence, the same holds for

g^{m}
, see
[
HS00
, Propostion B.4.2(a)]
.

We now note that it is easy to construct a canonical height on
\hat{S}
that is invariant under the action of the Weyl group of
\hat{G}_{\text{aff}}
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
W
be the Weyl-group of
\hat{G}_{\text{aff}}
and let

\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}
be the canonical height constructed in Definition
6.1
. We define

\hat{h}^{W}_{\hat{S}}\colon\hat{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,,\quad g\mapsto\sum_{w\in W}\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}(wgw^{-1})\,.

Note that this is well-defined as
W
is finite and that
\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}^{W}
satisfies the Northcott property.
We can now define a canonical height on
G
.

Definition 6.4
.

For
\hat{g}\in\hat{G}
, we define

\hat{h}_{\hat{G}}(\hat{g})=\hat{h}^{W}_{S}(\hat{s})
, where
\hat{s}\in\hat{S}
is an element that is
conjugated to the semi-simple part
\hat{g}_{s}
of
\hat{g}
, see Lemma
2.7

for the existence of
\hat{s}
.
From Lemma
2.8
, Corollary
2.18
(
1
)
and Definition
6.3
it
follows that this is well-defined. For
g\in G
we then set

\hat{h}_{G}(g)=\hat{h}_{\hat{G}}(p(g))\,.

Remark 6.5
.

In other words, if
g\in G
and
\hat{s}\in\rho^{-1}(\pi(g))
, then
\hat{h}_{G}(g)=\hat{h}_{\hat{G}}(\hat{s})
,
since
\pi(g)=\pi_{\hat{G}}(\hat{g})=\pi_{\hat{G}}(\hat{g}_{s})
for
\hat{g}=p(g)
, see Corollary
2.18
(
3
).

The above constructed height function
\hat{h}_{G}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is invariant under
G
-conjugation and moreover:

Lemma 6.6
.

The height function
\hat{h}_{G}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

vanishes exactly on the unipotent-torsion elements of
G
.

Proof.

If
g\in G
is unipotent-torsion, then
\hat{g}\coloneqq p(g)
is unipotent-torsion in
\hat{G}
.
Lemma
2.8
implies that
\hat{g}=\hat{g}_{s}\hat{g}_{u}
with
\hat{g}_{s}
being torsion.
Then any element
\hat{s}\in\hat{S}
that is conjugated to
\hat{g}_{s}
is torsion as well
and in particular the forward-orbit
\{\,\hat{s}^{2^{k}}\ |\ k\in\mathbb{N}\,\}
is finite. Thus for every
w\in W
, we have that

\hat{h}_{q}(w\hat{s}w^{-1})
and
\hat{h}_{\ell}(w\hat{s}w^{-1})
vanish and hence,
\hat{h}_{G}(g)
vanishes as well.

On the other hand, if
\hat{h}_{G}(g)=0
for some
g\in G
, then the semi-simple part
\hat{g}_{s}
of

\hat{g}\coloneqq p(g)
is conjugated to a point
\hat{s}\in\hat{S}
with
\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}(\hat{s})=0
.
As the points on
\hat{S}
, whose canonical height vanish are exactly the torsion points of
\hat{S}

(see Remark
6.2
) we conclude the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
X\subset G
be an irreducible subset such that
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
is not a unipotent-special subvariety.
Then, there exists an
\epsilon=\epsilon(X)>0
, such that the set

\{\,g\in\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})\ |\ \hat{h}_{G}(g)<\epsilon\,\}

is not dense in
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
.

Note that
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
is irreducible by Corollary
2.20
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{\pi(X)}

is irreducible, the irreducible components of
\rho^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
have all the same dimension. Then none of these irreducible components is a torsion translate of a connected closed subgroup of
\hat{S}
, since otherwise
\overline{\pi(X)}=\rho(hS)
for a torsion point
h\in\hat{S}
and a connected
closed subgroup
S\subseteq\hat{S}
and thus
\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
would be a unipotent-special subvariety of
G
by Lemma
5.7
.

Let
Y=\rho^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)}))
.
From the Bogomolov conjecture for semi-abelian varieties proven in
[
DP00
]
(and by means of equidistribution in

[
Küh22
, Proposition 6.1]
(see also
[
Küh20
, §3]
)) follows that there exists
\varepsilon=\epsilon(X)>0
such that the set
Y_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{\,y\in Y\ |\ \hat{h}_{\hat{G}}(y)<\varepsilon\,\}
is nowhere dense in
Y
.
Since
\hat{h}_{\hat{G}}|_{\hat{S}}=\hat{h}_{\hat{S}}^{W}
is invariant under
W
-conjugation (see Definition
6.3
),
we get
Y_{\varepsilon}=\rho^{-1}(\rho(Y_{\varepsilon}))
.
From Corollary
4.5

it follows that
\rho(Y_{\varepsilon})
is not dense in
\rho(Y)
and with Lemma
4.4
(
1
) that

\pi^{-1}(\rho(Y_{\varepsilon}))
is not dense in
\pi^{-1}(\rho(Y))=\pi^{-1}(\overline{\pi(X)})
.
It remains to show that

\pi^{-1}(\rho(Y_{\epsilon}))=\{\,g\in\pi^{-1}(\rho(Y))\ |\ \hat{h}_{G}(g)<\epsilon\,\}\,.

However, this follows immediately from Remark
6.5
.
∎

We now make the connection to Breuillard’s height
\hat{h}_{B}

[
Bre11
, p.1058 and Sect. 2.2]
.
For this we assume that
G=\hat{G}=\GL_{t}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
and that

\hat{S}=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{t}
is the diagonal maximal torus. According to
[
Bre11
, p.1058]
we have that

\hat{h}_{B}(g)=h_{t}([1:\lambda_{1}:\cdots:\lambda_{t}])\,,

where
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{t})
is a tuple of eigenvalues of
g
and
h_{n}\colon\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is the Weil-height on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
given by

(16)

h_{n}([\mu_{0}:\mu_{1}:\cdots:\mu_{n}])=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in M_{K}}n_{v}\log\max\{|\mu_{0}|_{v},|\mu_{1}|_{v},\dots,|\mu_{n}|_{v}\},

where
K
is a number field containing
\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{n}
,
M_{K}
is the set of absolute values of
K
, normalized as in the
citation,
n_{v}=[K_{v}:\Q_{v}]
and
K_{v}
,
\Q_{v}
are the completions with respect to
v
.
Note that
h_{t}([1:\lambda_{1}:\cdots:\lambda_{t}])
does not depend on the ordering of the elements in the tuple
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{t})
.

The Weyl-group of
\GL_{t}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
with respect to
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{t}
is equal to the symmetric group

\mathcal{S}_{t}
. The divisor at infinity
D_{\infty}
of
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{t}
induced the composition of the
natural closed embedding
\iota\colon(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{t}\to\mathbb{P}^{2^{t}-1}
coming from the Segre embedding
with the Veronese embedding
\eta\colon\mathbb{P}^{2^{t}-1}\to\mathbb{P}^{2^{t+1}-1}
of degree
2
.
Note that
h_{2^{t+1}-1}\circ\eta\circ\iota=2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{t}h_{1}\circ\pr_{i}
, where
\pr_{i}\colon(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{t}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}

denotes the projection to the
i
-th factor, see
[
HS00
, Proposition B.2.4]
.
Using that
|h_{D_{\infty}}-h_{2^{t+1}-1}\circ\eta\circ\iota|
is bounded (
[
HS00
, Theorem B.3.2(a),(b)]
)
and that
h_{1}([1:\lambda])=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{h_{1}([2^{k}][1:\lambda])}{2^{k}}
for all

\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\ast}
, we get that our height
\hat{h}_{G}
on
G=\GL_{t}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is given by

\hat{h}_{G}(g)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{h_{D_{\infty}}([2^{k}](\lambda_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\lambda_{\sigma(t)}))}{2^{k}}=2\cdot(t!)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{t}h_{1}([1:\lambda_{i}])\,.

Now we do have for all
g\in G
the inequalities

(17)

\frac{1}{2\cdot(t!)}\hat{h}_{G}(g)\leq t\hat{h}_{B}(g)\leq\hat{h}_{G}(g)\,,

and so for
G=\GL_{t}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, Theorem
6.7
also holds with
\hat{h}_{G}
replaced by
Breuillard’s height
\hat{h}_{B}
.
