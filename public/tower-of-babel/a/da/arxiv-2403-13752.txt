Quantum-limited superresolution of two incoherent point sources with unknown photon numbers
Junyan Li 1, Shengshi Pang 1,2

Abstract
Superresolution has been demonstrated to overcome the limitation of the Rayleigh’s criterion and achieve significant improvement of the precision in resolving the centroid separation of two incoherent optical point sources. However, in recent years, it was found that if the photon numbers of the two incoherent optical sources are unknown, the precision of superresolution vanishes when the two photon numbers are actually different. In this work, we analyze the estimation precision of the centroid separation between two incoherent optical sources with the same point-spread functions and unknown photon numbers in detail, and show that when the photon numbers of the two optical sources are different but sufficiently close, the superresolution can still realized but with different precisions. We find the condition on how close the photons numbers of two optical sources need to be to realize the superresolution, and derive the precision of superresolution in different regimes of the photon number difference. We further consider the superresolution for two incoherent optical sources with different point-spread functions, and show that the competition between the difference of photon numbers, the difference of the two point-spread functions and the centroid separation of the two optical sources determines the precision of superresolution. The results exhibit various precision limits of the centroid separation distinct from that of two point sources with identical point-spread functions and equal photon numbers. The results are finally illustrated by Gaussian point-spread functions.

Introduction

Optical imaging is an important sensing technique that has contributed
to various research areas, such as medical technology, astronomy,
nanotechnology and biochemistry, etc. Typical imaging methods include
classical and quantum imaging
(
Rushforth and Harris 1968
;
Pérez-Delgado et al. 2012
)
,
fluorescence imaging
(
Denk et al. 1990
;

superlens
(

;

(

;
Abouraddy et al. 2001
)
,
quantum holography
(

;

)
and quantum lithography
(
Boto et al. 2000
;
Liao et al. 2010
)
,
etc. However, by the Rayleigh’s criterion
(
Rayleigh 1880
)
, when
the widths of two closely located point sources are larger than the
separation of the two sources, it becomes more difficult to distinguish
between the two sources and the measurement precision of the separation
vanishes using the classical direct imaging method. This imposes a
severe limitation on the resolution of imaging.

The Rayleigh’s criterion indicates the insufficiency of direct measurement
for high-resolution imaging in the positional space of optical sources.
To precisely measure the centroid separation of two point sources,
it is critical to find a suitable measurement basis beyond the positional
basis and a proper estimation method so that the imaging precision
can surpass the classical precision limit of direct imaging. The
quantum parameter estimation theory provides the mathematical tool
to solve this problem. The quantum parameter estimation theory is
rooted in its classical counterpart. The classical parameter estimation
theory optimizes the estimation precision of unknown parameters over
all possible estimation strategies and gives the optimal estimation
precision for parameters in a given parameter-dependent probability
distribution by the well-known Cramér-Rao’s bound. The quantum
parameter estimation theory considers the parameters in a quantum
system, and further optimizes the estimation precision over all possible
measurement basis in addition to the optimization of estimation strategies,
since different quantum measurement bases lead to different probability
distributions of the measurement results and thus to different estimation
precisions of the unknown parameters. The precision limit of quantum
estimation is characterized by the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, which
is determined by the inverse of the quantum Fisher information matrix

(
Helstrom 1969
;
Giovannetti et al. 2011
;
Liu et al. 2016
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
.

Inspired by the quantum estimation theory, Tsang et al. proposed a
technique of superresolution for two incoherent optical sources
(
Tsang et al. 2016
)

to break the Rayleigh’s curse. They found that by optimizing the measurement
basis the precision of estimating the centroid separation between
the two sources does not vanish when the separation approaches zero.
This is in sharp contrast to the Rayleigh’s curse, which shows the
power of quantum estimation theory and opens up a new avenue for precision
resolution of two incoherent optical point sources.

An implicit assumption in the original superresolution protocol is
that the photon intensities of the two incoherent optical sources
are known and equal. This assumption looks trivial at first glance,
but turns out to play a critical role in the superresolution: if the
photon numbers are unknown and need to be estimated simultaneously
with the centroid separation of the two sources, the estimation precision
of the centroid separation will vanish as the separation approaches
zero if the photon intensities are not actually equal and the Rayleigh’s
curse resurges
(
Řehaček et al. 2017
)
. As the photon intensities could
vary or fluctuate due to noises and technical imperfections in reality,
this imposes challenges to the application of superresolution in
practice. In recent years, the role of partial coherence in the superresolution
has also been investigated intensively
(
Larson and Saleh 2018
;

;
Larson and Saleh 2019
;
Tsang 2021
;
De et al. 2021
;
Wadood et al. 2021
;

)
,
and entanglement has been found useful to enhance the precision of
superresolution
(
Sajia and Qian 2022
;
Sajia and Qian 2023
)
.

In this work, we analyze the effect of unknown photon numbers on
the precision of superresolution from the viewpoint of quantum multiparameter
estimation in detail, and obtain the constraint condition on the difference
of the photon numbers between the two incoherent point sources to
overcome the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse. The results show the
precision may deviate from that with identical photon numbers, depending
on the magnitude of the difference of the photon numbers. We further
consider two incoherent point sources with different point-spread
functions, and find that the competition between the difference of
the point-spread functions and the difference of the photon numbers
determines the precision of superresolution for the centroid separation.
We analyze the competition between these parameters in detail, and
show that the superresolution can have various precision limits depending
on the magnitudes of these parameters, beyond the precision limit
of two optical sources with identical photon numbers and point-spread
functions.

II
Preliminaries

Suppose we have two point sources imaged on a one-dimensional plane
with unknown centroid positions
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. If the photon
numbers of the two sources are
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
respectively,
the state of the two sources can be represented by a quantum density
operator
(
Mandel 9 01
)

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}{\rm e}^{-iX_{1}\hat{P}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|{\rm e}^{iX_{1}\hat{P}}+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}{\rm e}^{-iX_{2}\hat{P}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|{\rm e}^{iX_{2}\hat{P}}.

(1)

Here,
\epsilon
is the ratio between the difference and the total of
the photon numbers of the two optical sources,

\epsilon=\frac{N_{2}-N_{1}}{N_{1}+N_{2}},

(2)

|\psi\rangle
is a state defined by a point-spread function on the
one-dimensional imaging plane assumed to be real and symmetric about
the origin point
(
Goodman 1996
)
throughout this paper,

|\psi\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\psi(x)|x\rangle,

(3)

and
{\rm e}^{-iX_{k}\hat{P}}
,
i=1,2
, are the displacement operators with
\hat{P}

defined as

\hat{P}=-i\partial_{x}.

(4)

Now, the question is: how precise can one measure and estimate the
centroid separation,

d=X_{2}-X_{1},

(5)

between the two incoherent point sources?

II.1
Direct imaging and Rayleigh’s curse

The most straightforward method to measure the centroid separation
is directly detecting and counting photons arrived on the image plane
from the two point sources
(
Van Trees 2001
)
. The position distribution
of photons on the image plane is

\Lambda(x)=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}|\psi_{1}(x)|^{2}+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}|\psi_{2}(x)|^{2}.

(6)

In order to quantify the precision limit of estimating the centroid
separation of two incoherent optical sources, the well-known Cramér-Rao
bound
(
Helstrom 1969
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Albarelli et al. 2020
)
from the
classical parameter estimation theory needs to be invoked, which is
reviewed in Appendix
A
. For the task of
resolving two point sources, the centroid positions of the two sources,

X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, or equivalently the mean and difference of
the two centroid positions,
\bar{X}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}
and
d=X_{2}-X_{1}

are unknown, so
\bar{X}
and
d
are the parameters to estimate.

The Cramér-Rao bound demonstrates that over all possible estimation
strategies, the covariance matrix
C
for the estimates of the parameters

\bar{X}
and
d
is lower bounded by

C[\bar{X},d]\geq N_{{\rm tot}}^{-1}\mathcal{J}^{-1}[\bar{X},d],

(7)

where
N_{{\rm tot}}
is total number of photons from the two optical sources,

N_{{\rm tot}}=N_{1}+N_{2},

(8)

the “
\geq
” sign represents semi-definite positivity of matrix,
and
\mathcal{J}[\bar{X},d]
denotes the Fisher information matrix,

\mathcal{J}_{ij}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Lambda(x)}\frac{\partial\Lambda(x)}{\partial g_{i}}\frac{\partial\Lambda(x)}{\partial g_{j}}dx,\;g_{i},g_{j}=\bar{X},d.

(9)

Note that the Cramér-Rao bound can always be asymptotically saturated
by the maximum likelihood estimation strategy when the total number
of photons
N_{{\rm tot}}
is sufficiently large
(
Helstrom 1969
)
.

For the direct imaging with an ideal continuum photodetector on the
image plane, if the intensities and the point-spread functions of
the two optical sources are the same, i.e.,
N_{1}=N_{2}=N
, the
position distribution of the arrival photons can be written as

\Lambda(x)=\frac{1}{2}I(x-\bar{X}+d/2)+\frac{1}{2}I(x-\bar{X}-d/2),

(10)

where
I(x)
is defined as

I(x)=|\psi(x)|^{2}.

(11)

When the centroid separation
d
is small, it can be shown by the
Cramér-Rao bound
7
that the estimation precision
of the centroid separation
d
by direct imaging is

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm direct})}=N_{{\rm tot}}/(\mathcal{J}^{-1})_{22}=\frac{1}{16}d^{2}\alpha_{2}N_{\text{tot}}.

(12)

The detail of derivation is provided in Appendix
A
.
Obviously, when
d\rightarrow 0
, the estimation separation precision
vanishes, so one can hardly obtain any information about the centroid
separation by direct imaging when the two point sources are sufficiently
close to each other. This is the Rayleigh’s curse for direct imaging
of two identical optical point sources.

The above result can be generalized to the case that the two point-spread
functions are the same but the photon intensities are unequal. In
this case, the position distribution of the photons can be written
as

\Lambda(x)=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}I(x-\bar{X}+d/2)+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}I(x-\bar{X}-d/2).

(13)

The estimation precision of centroid separation by direct imaging
can be found to be

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm direct})}=N_{{\rm tot}}/(\mathcal{J}^{-1})_{22}=\frac{d^{2}\alpha_{2}N_{\text{tot}}}{16}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)^{2}.

(14)

(See Appendix
A
for detail.) This indicates
that one cannot get any information about the centroid separation

d
by direct imaging in the limit
d\rightarrow 0
in the generalized
case either.

II.2
Quantum superresolution

To overcome the Rayleigh’s curse, one needs to further resort to the
quantum estimation theory, which optimizes the Cramér-Rao bound
over all possible quantum measurement schemes, including generalized
measurements that are described by Kraus operators
(
Nielsen and Chuang 2012
)
.

For the current quantum superresolution problem, the centroid positions
of the two point sources,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, or equivalently
the mean and difference of the two centroid positions,
\bar{X}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}
,
and
d=X_{2}-X_{1}
are unknown, so
\bar{X}
and
d
are the parameters
to estimate.

The quantum estimation theory for the quantum superresolution problem
is briefly reviewed in Appendix
B
, which tells
that the estimation precision of
\bar{X}
and
d
is lower bounded
by the following quantum Cramér-Rao’s inequality,

C[\bar{X},\,d]\geq N_{{\rm tot}}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}[\bar{X},\,d],

(15)

where
C[\bar{X},\,d]
is the covariance matrix for the estimates
of
\bar{X},\,d
,
\mathcal{Q}[\bar{X},\,d]
is the quantum Fisher information
matrix about
\bar{X},\,d
with entries

\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\hat{\rho}\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i},\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}\}],i,j=1,2,

(16)

and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
are the symmetric logarithmic derivatives (SLD)
of the density operator
\hat{\rho}
,

\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i})=\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho},\;g_{i}=\bar{X},d.

(17)

Note that the quantum Cramér-Rao bound for multi-parameter estimation
is not always achievable due to the potential noncommutativity between
the optimal measurements for different parameters. But if one is interested
in the overall estimation precision of different parameters, a weight
matrix
W
can be imposed on the unknown parameters and the matrix
quantum Cramér-Rao’s bound can be turned to a scalar form,

{\rm Tr}(WC)\geq N_{{\rm tot}}^{-1}{\rm Tr}(W\mathcal{Q}^{-1}).

(18)

The saturation condition for the above scalar quantum Cramér-Rao
bound is

{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}[\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i},\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}])={\rm ImTr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j})=0,

(19)

by recognizing that the quantum Cramér-Rao bound is equivalent
to the Holevo bound when this condition is satisfied and the latter
is always achievable asymptotically with a sufficiently large number
of states
(
Ragy et al. 2016
;
Řehaček et al. 2017
;
Suzuki et al. 2020
)
.

For the current problem of quantum superresolution, the interested
parameter is the centroid separation
d
, so one can choose

W=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

(20)

assuming
g_{1}=\bar{X}
and
g_{2}=d
, and
{\rm Tr}(WC)
gives
the estimation variance of the centroid separation. And the above
saturation condition can indeed be satisfied by the current problem,
due to the reality of
\psi(x)
which makes the symmetric logarithmic
derivatives
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
also real according to Eq. (
17
)
and thus the compatibility condition
{\rm ImTr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j})=0

can be satisfied. So the quantum Cramér-Rao bound provides an achievable
precision limit for the estimation of centroid separation allowed
by quantum mechanics.

A striking result found by Tsang et al.
(
Tsang et al. 2016
)
is that
the best estimation precision of the centroid separation
d
over
all quantum measurements for two incoherent point sources with the
same photon numbers, determined by the quantum Cramér-Rao’s bound,
keeps nonzero even when the separation goes to zero, implying the
Rayleigh’s curse is no longer effective when the quantum measurement
is optimized. The optimal precision of estimating the centroid separation

d
in the limit
d\rightarrow 0
is found to be

\left.\mathcal{H}_{d}\right|_{d\rightarrow 0}=2N\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle,

(21)

where the expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle
is taken over the state

|\psi\rangle
,
\langle\cdot\rangle=\langle\psi|\cdot|\psi\rangle
, and
N
is
the photon number of each point source. If the photon numbers of the
two point sources are allowed to be different, the above precision
limit can be generalized to

\left.\mathcal{H}_{d}\right|_{d\rightarrow 0}=N_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle.

(22)

For two Gaussian point-spread functions with the same width
\sigma
,
the above precision limit can be simplified to

\left.\mathcal{H}_{d}\right|_{d\rightarrow 0}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}{4\sigma^{2}}.

(23)

The derivation of the precision limits for a general
d
and for
the above
d\rightarrow 0
case is presented in Appendix
C.1
.

An interesting finding in recent years is the resurgence of Rayleigh’s
curse when the photon numbers
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
of two point sources
are unknown and need to be estimated as well
(
Řehaček et al. 2017
)
.
In this case, one needs to estimate three parameters,
\bar{X},\,d,\,\epsilon
,
simultaneously. If the photon numbers of the two point sources are
different, i.e.,
\epsilon\neq 0
, it was found that up to the second order
of
d
, the precision of estimating
d
was

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}{4\epsilon^{2}}{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})d^{2},

(24)

where
{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})=\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle^{2}
.

It can be immediately seen that in this case,
\mathcal{H}_{d}
is of order
O(d^{2})

and will vanish as
d
goes to zero, implying the superresolution
no longer works and the Rayleigh’s curse occurs again. This is the
resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse for two incoherent point sources with
unknown and unbalanced photon intensities
(
Řehaček et al. 2017
;
Řeháček et al. 2018
;
Sajia and Qian 2022
)
.

III
Superresolution for two identical point sources

In this work, we investigate the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse in
detail and study the condition for the superresolution to avoid the
the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse.

As a finite difference between the photon numbers of the two sources
can cause the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse while the equality between
the two photon numbers leads to superresolution, we are interested
in the condition on the photon numbers to realize the superresolution:
is it possible to realize the superresolution with a sufficiently
small but nonzero difference between the photon numbers of the two
sources and how close the two photon numbers need to be? In addition
to the photon numbers, we will also investigate the effect of slight
difference between the point-spread functions of the two optical sources
on suppressing the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse, and show its competition
with the difference of the photon numbers in determining the precision
of resolving the two point sources.

We consider two identical incoherent point sources located at
X_{1}
,

X_{2}
with unknown photon numbers
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
in this section.
The unknown parameters to estimate in this case is
\bar{X}
,
d
,
\epsilon
,
and
\epsilon
is the ratio between the difference and the total of the
photon numbers defined in Eq. (
2
). The density matrix

\hat{\rho}
of the two sources is given by Eq. (
1
). By working
out the eigenvalues and eigenstates of
\hat{\rho}
and applying the formula
of the quantum Fisher information matrix, the quantum Fisher information
matrix with respect to these three unknown parameters can be obtained
as

\mathcal{Q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}4\left[\kappa-\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\right]&2\kappa\epsilon&2\gamma\delta\\
2\kappa\epsilon&\kappa&0\\
2\gamma\delta&0&\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{1-\epsilon^{2}}\end{array}\right],

(25)

where

\kappa=\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle,\;\delta=\langle\cos(d\hat{P})\rangle,\;\gamma=\langle\sin(d\hat{P})\hat{P}\rangle.

(26)

According to the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, the estimation precision
of the centroid separation
d
can be obtained as

\mathcal{H}_{d}=N_{{\rm tot}}/(\mathcal{Q}^{-1})_{22}=\frac{\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\left[\gamma^{2}-\left(1-\delta^{2}\right)\kappa\right]}{\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)-(1-\delta^{2})\kappa}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})},

(27)

where
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}
is the optimal estimation precision when the photon
numbers of the two sources are equal, i.e.,
\epsilon=0
,
N_{1}=N_{2}=N
,

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}=2\kappa N,

(28)

The detail of derivation is provided in Appendix
C.2
.

To investigate the mechanism underlying the resurgence of Rayleigh’s
curse when the photon numbers
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
of the two sources
are different, we expand the numerator and denominator of the precision

\mathcal{H}_{d}
(
27
) to the fourth order of
d
respectively, and
the result turns out to be

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{Cd^{2}}{D_{1}+D_{2}d^{2}},

(29)

where

\displaystyle C=

\displaystyle 3N_{{\rm tot}}(1-\epsilon^{2})\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2}),

(30)

\displaystyle D_{1}=

\displaystyle 12\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle,

\displaystyle D_{2}=

\displaystyle 3{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})-4\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle,

and a common
d^{2}
has been reduced from both the numerator and
denominator.

Obviously, when the photon intensities of the two optical sources
are unequal, i.e.,
N_{1}\neq N_{2}
, the coefficient
D_{1}
is
finite, so
\mathcal{H}_{d}=O(d^{2})
, thus
\mathcal{H}_{d}
vanishes
when
d\rightarrow 0
, which is the resurgence of the Rayleigh’s curse.
In contrast, when the photon intensities of the two optical sources
are equal, i.e.,
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and
N_{{\rm tot}}=N_{1}+N_{2}=2N
,

D_{1}=0
, so the estimation precision of
d
(
29
)
becomes

\left.\mathcal{H}_{d}\right|_{d\rightarrow 0}=\frac{C}{D_{2}}=2N\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle,

(31)

and the superresolution scheme can work in this case, which is the
result by Ref.
(
Tsang et al. 2016
)
.

Nevertheless, a further observation of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
in Eq. (
29
)
suggests that even when
N_{1}\neq N_{2}
there is still opportunity
to have nonvanishing estimation precision for the centroid separation

d
if the coefficient
D_{1}
is sufficiently small. It can be
seen that if
D_{1}
has the order
O\left(d^{2}{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})\right)
,
i.e., the order of
\epsilon
is
O\left(d\sqrt{{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})/\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle}\right)
,
the precision
\mathcal{H}_{d}
keeps finite when
d
is small,
implying the quantum superresolution scheme can still work in this
case. Furthermore, if the order of the coefficient
D_{1}
is higher
than
O(d^{2}{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2}))
, or equivalently the order of
\epsilon

is higher than
O\left(d\sqrt{{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})/\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle}\right)
,
D_{1}

can be dropped when
d\rightarrow 0
, and
\mathcal{H}_{d}
is equal
to
C/D_{2}=N_{{\rm tot}}\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle
which restores the original result for the precision
of superresolution in
(
Tsang et al. 2016
)
.

The above observation immediately leads to the condition for the quantum
superresolution of two identical incoherent optical sources: the order
of the ratio
\epsilon
between the difference and the total of the
photon numbers of the two optical sources must be higher than or equal
to
O\left(d\sqrt{{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})/\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle}\right)
. The original result
in
(
Tsang et al. 2016
)
was obtained for the case
N_{1}=N_{2}
, but
is effective for the range of
\epsilon
that is higher than
O\left(d\sqrt{{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})/\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle}\right)
,
while the superresolution scheme can actually work in a broader range
including the case that the order of
D_{1}
is equal to
O\left(d^{2}{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})\right)
.
But the latter has a lower precision than the former, as the ratio
between the two precisions is

\frac{D_{2}d^{2}}{D_{1}+D_{2}d^{2}}<1.

(32)

It must be noted that this condition for the validity of superresolution
with unknown photon numbers does not mean that the photon number changes
with
d
, but rather impose a limitation on the magnitude of photon
number difference between the two optical sources to realize the superresolution.

IV
Superresolution for two arbitrary point sources

In practical applications, as the two photon sources are incoherent,
the point-spread functions of the two sources can differ from each
other in general. Such a difference in the point-spread functions
reduces the overlap between the two optical sources, so an intuitive
idea is that it may increase the distinguishability of the two sources
which could be helpful to the estimation precision of quantum superresolution
for the centroid separation of the two sources. In the following,
we generalize the result of the previous section and study the condition
of quantum superresolution for two different optical point sources.

Suppose the point-spread functions of the two point sources to distinguish
are
\psi_{1}(x)
and
\psi_{2}(x)
with centroid displacements

X_{1}
and
X_{2}
from the origin point respectively. Denote

|\psi_{k}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\psi_{k}(x)|x\rangle
,
k=1,2
. The
density matrix of the two sources can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}{\rm e}^{-iX_{1}\hat{P}}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|{\rm e}^{iX_{1}\hat{P}}

(33)

\displaystyle+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}{\rm e}^{-iX_{2}\hat{P}}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|{\rm e}^{iX_{2}\hat{P}},

where
N_{{\rm tot}}
is the total number of the photons,
N_{{\rm tot}}=N_{1}+N_{2}
,
and
\epsilon
is the ratio between the difference and the total
of the photon numbers of the two point sources defined in Eq. (
2
).

The estimation precision for the centroid separation can still be
obtained by the scalar quantum Cramér-Rao bound (
18
).
The quantum Fisher information matrix with respect to the unknown
parameters
\bar{X},d,\epsilon
turns out to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=

(34)

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}2\kappa_{\text{tot}}(1+\chi\epsilon)-4\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)&\kappa_{\text{tot}}(\chi+\epsilon)&2\gamma\delta\\
\kappa_{\text{tot}}(\chi+\epsilon)&\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{\text{tot}}(1+\chi\epsilon)&0\\
2\gamma\delta&0&\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{1-\epsilon^{2}}\end{array}\right],

leading to the exact result for the estimation precision of
d
as

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\left[\left(1-\delta^{2}\right)\left(1-\chi^{2}\right)\kappa_{{\rm tot}}-2\gamma^{2}(1+\chi\epsilon)\right]}{\left(1-\delta^{2}\right)(1+\chi\epsilon)\kappa_{{\rm tot}}-2\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})},

(35)

where

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{i},\,i=1,2,

(36)

\displaystyle\delta=

\displaystyle\langle\cos(d\hat{P})\rangle_{12},\;\gamma=\langle\sin(d\hat{P})\hat{P}\rangle_{12},

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\rm tot}}=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2},\;\chi=\frac{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}}{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}},

and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{i}=\langle\psi_{i}|\cdot|\psi_{i}\rangle
,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{12}=\langle\psi_{1}|\cdot|\psi_{2}\rangle
.

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}
is the optimal estimation precision when the photon numbers
of the two sources are equal, i.e.,
\epsilon=0
, in the limit
d\rightarrow 0
,

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}=\frac{1}{2}N_{{\rm tot}}\kappa_{{\rm tot}},

(37)

which can be simplified to Eq. (
28
) when the point-spread
functions of the two sources are the same. The derivation is provided
in Appendix
D
.

Considering the centroid separation
d
is small, we expand the numerator
and the denominator of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
to the fourth order of
d

respectively ,

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{A_{0}+A_{2}d^{2}+A_{4}d^{4}}{B_{0}+B_{2}d^{2}+B_{4}d^{4}}.

(38)

The coefficients
A_{i}
,
B_{i}
,
i=0,2,4
, are quite complex,
so they are provided in Eq. (
115
) of Appendix
D
.

It is obtained in Appendix
D

that

A_{0},B_{0}\propto 1-|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}.

(39)

So, if the difference between the two point-spread functions is not
small, i.e., the magnitudes of
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are of order

O(1)
, only the constant terms
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
remain in
\mathcal{H}_{d}

when
d\rightarrow 0
. The estimation precision of the separation

d
in the limit
d\rightarrow 0
can be simplified to

\left.\mathcal{H}_{d}\right|_{d\rightarrow 0}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\kappa_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\chi^{2}\right)\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}{2(1+\chi\epsilon)},

(40)

implying that the superresolution can work even if the photon numbers
of the two point sources are different in this case.

A more subtle case is when the point-spread functions of the two optical
sources are different but close to each other. In this case, the constant
terms
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
(
38
)
will become small though still nonzero. If the magnitudes of these
leading terms are comparable to
O(d^{2})
or even
O(d^{4})
(although
they are independent of
d
in physics), they will compete with the
second-order and fourth-order terms in Eq. (
38
) to
determine
\mathcal{H}_{d}
.

V
Example: Point sources of Gaussian distributions

To illustrate the above results, we consider the Gaussian point-spread
functions as a typical example. Assume
\psi_{1}(x)
and
\psi_{2}(x)

are Gaussian point-spread functions,

where
\sigma_{i}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\kappa_{i}}}=\lambda_{i}/2\pi{\rm NA},i=1,2

is the effective
numerical aperture
(
Pawley 2006
)
, and the spatial-frequency
variance
\kappa_{i}
for the Gaussian point-spread function is
\frac{1}{4\sigma_{i}^{2}},\,i=1,2
.
The two general Gaussian point-spread functions are illustrated in
Fig.

.

VI
Conclusions

The superresolution is an important quantum technique to overcome
the Rayleigh’s curse in resolving two closely located point sources,
manifesting the power of quantum estimation theory in the field of
quantum imaging. Nevertheless, the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse
when the photon numbers of the two point sources are unknown imposes
limitations on its applicability. This work studies the condition
for the quantum superresolution technique to work by analyzing the
competing roles of the difference in the photon numbers and the difference
in the point-spread functions between the two optical sources as well
as the centroid separation itself in determining the estimation precision
of the centroid separation. The results not only provide conditions
for the realizability of quantum superresolution, but also show various
precision limits of superresolution in different regimes of parameters,
which indicates that the competition between the difference of the
photon numbers, the difference of the point-spread functions and the
magnitude of the centroid separation plays a key role in the validity
of quantum superresolution. We hope this work can provide new insights
into the quantum superresolution theory and extend the applications
of quantum superresolution in practice.

Appendix A
Precision limit of direct imaging

Suppose the distribution of photons on the image plane is

\Lambda(x)=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}|\psi_{1}(x)|^{2}+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}|\psi_{2}(x)|^{2}.

(54)

Here,
\psi_{1}(x)
and
\psi_{2}(x)
are the point-spread functions
of the two sources, and
\epsilon
is the ratio between the difference
and the total of the photon numbers of the two sources,

\epsilon=\frac{N_{2}-N_{1}}{N_{1}+N_{2}}.

(55)

In order to quantify the precision limit of estimating the centroid
separation of two incoherent optical sources, the well-known Cramér-Rao
bound
(
Helstrom 1969
;
Yang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Albarelli et al. 2020
)
from the
classical parameter estimation theory needs to be invoked, which demonstrates
that over all possible estimation strategies, the covariance matrix

C
for the estimates of the parameters
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
is
lower bounded by

C[g_{1},...,g_{n}]\geq M^{-1}\mathcal{J}^{-1}[g_{1},...,g_{n}].

(56)

The “
\geq
” sign represents semi-definite positivity of matrix,

M
is the number of samples, and
\mathcal{J}[g_{1},...,g_{n}]

denotes the Fisher information matrix defined as

\mathcal{J}_{ij}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{P(x)}\frac{\partial P(x)}{\partial g_{i}}\frac{\partial P(x)}{\partial g_{j}}dx,

(57)

where
P(x)
is a probability distribution of random variable
x

dependent on the parameters
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
. The benefit of
the Cramér-Rao inequality is that its lower bound can always be
asymptotically saturated by the maximum likelihood estimation strategy
when
M
is sufficiently large
(
Helstrom 1969
)
.

For the task of resolving two point sources, the centroid positions
of the two sources,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, or equivalently the mean
and difference of the two centroid positions,
\bar{X}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}

and
d=X_{2}-X_{1}
are unknown, so
\bar{X}
and
d
are the parameters
to estimate, and
P(x)
is the position distribution
\Lambda(x)

(
54
) of the photons from the incoherent sources.

For the direct imaging with an ideal continuum photodetector in the
image plane, if the intensities and the point-spread functions of
the two optical sources are equal, the position distribution (
54
)
of the arrival photons can be rewritten as

\Lambda(x)=\frac{1}{2}I(x-\bar{X}+d/2)+\frac{1}{2}I(x-\bar{X}-d/2),

(58)

where
I(x)
is defined as

I(x)=|\psi(x)|^{2}.

(59)

When the centroid separation
d
is small, one can expand
\Lambda(x)

to the second order of
d
,

\Lambda(x)=I(x-\bar{X})+\frac{1}{8}d^{2}I^{\prime\prime}(x-\bar{X}).

(60)

Then according Eq.(
57
), the classical Fisher
information matrix for the parameters
\bar{X}
,
d
can be obtained
as

\mathcal{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}-\frac{\alpha_{2}d^{2}}{4}&0\\
0&\frac{\alpha_{2}d^{2}}{16}\end{bmatrix},

(61)

where

\alpha_{i}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\frac{1}{\Lambda(x)}\left[\partial_{x}^{i}I(x-\bar{X})\right]^{2}.

(62)

If one is interested in the parameter
d
only (though
\bar{X}

is still unknown), a weight matrix
W={\rm Diag}\{0,1\}
can be multiplied
to the both sides of the Cramér-Rao inequality (
56
),
and the variance of the estimate of
d
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\hat{d})

\displaystyle\geqslant{\rm Tr}(WJ^{-1}),

(63)

where
\hat{d}
denotes the estimate of the centroid separation
d
.
Considering the variance may diverge when
d\rightarrow 0
, we use
the reciprocal of the variance to quantify the estimation precision
of
d
. Thus the estimation precision of the centroid separation

d
by direct imaging is

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm direct})}=N_{{\rm tot}}/(\mathcal{J}^{-1})_{22}=\frac{1}{16}d^{2}\alpha_{2}N_{\text{tot}}.

(64)

Obviously, when
d\rightarrow 0
, the estimation separation precision
vanishes, so one can hardly obtain any information about the centroid
separation by direct imaging when the two point sources are sufficiently
close to each other. This is the Rayleigh’s curse for direct imaging
of two identical optical point sources.

The above result can be generalized to the case that the two point-spread
functions are the same but the photon intensities are unequal. In
this case, the position distribution of the photons can be written
as

\Lambda(x)=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}I(x-\bar{X}+d/2)+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}I(x-\bar{X}-d/2).

(65)

Expanding the
\Lambda(x)
to the second order of
d
, one can obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)

\displaystyle=I(x-\bar{X})-\frac{1}{2}d\epsilon I^{\prime}(x-\bar{X})+\frac{1}{8}d^{2}I^{\prime\prime}(x-\bar{X}).

(66)

The Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{J}
with respect to the parameters

\bar{X}
,
d
can be obtained, and the entries are

\mathcal{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}-\frac{\alpha_{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}{4}d^{2}&\frac{\alpha_{1}\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\alpha_{2}\epsilon}{16}d^{2}\\
\frac{\alpha_{1}\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\alpha_{2}\epsilon}{16}d^{2}&\frac{\alpha_{1}\epsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{16}d^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Then the estimation precision of centroid separation by direct imaging
is

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm direct})}=N_{{\rm tot}}/(\mathcal{J}^{-1})_{22}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\alpha_{2}}{16}d^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)^{2},

(67)

which means that no information about the centroid separation
d

can be acquired by direct imaging when
d\rightarrow 0
in this generalized
case.

Appendix B
Multiparameter quantum metrology theory
for superresolution

The quantum parameter estimation theory considers the estimation precision
for unknown parameters in a parameter-dependent quantum state by quantum
measurements. The core of the quantum parameter estimation theory
is the quantum Cramér-Rao inequality. For the single-parameter
case, the quantum Cramér-Rao inequality tells that the variance
of the estimate of the unknown parameter is lower bounded by the inverse
of the quantum Fisher information which maximizes the classical Fisher
information over all possible quantum measurements on the parameter-dependent
quantum state. For the multi-parameter case, the variance and the
quantum Fisher information are generalized to the covariance matrix
and the quantum Fisher information matrix respectively between the
different parameters.

In detail, if one measures a quantum system with its density matrix

\hat{\rho}
dependent on unknown parameters
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
to determine
these parameters, the covariance matrix for the estimates of these
parameters is bounded by the following quantum Cramér-Rao’s inequality,

C[g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}]\geq M^{-1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}[g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}],

(68)

where
M
is the number of the quantum states,
C[g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}]

is the covariance matrix for the estimates of
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
,

\mathcal{Q}[g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}]
is the quantum Fisher information
matrix about
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
with entries

\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\hat{\rho}\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i},\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}\}],i,j=1,2,

(69)

and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
are the symmetric logarithmic derivatives (SLD)
of the density operator
\hat{\rho}
,

\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i})=\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho},\;g_{i}=\bar{X},d.

(70)

To obtain the quantum Fisher information matrix for
\hat{\rho}
which
depends on the unknown parameters
g_{i}
, one can decompose the
density matrix
\hat{\rho}
as

\hat{\rho}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}|\lambda_{k}\rangle\langle\lambda_{k}|,

(71)

where
\lambda_{k}
and
|\lambda_{k}\rangle
are eigenvalue and
eigenstate of
\hat{\rho}
. The matrix elements of both sides of Eq. (
70
)
with respect to the eigenbasis
|\lambda_{k}\rangle
can be obtained
as

\langle\lambda_{k}|\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{h}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\lambda_{k}|\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}|\lambda_{h}\rangle+\langle\lambda_{k}|\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{h}\rangle\right),

(72)

which leads to

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}=\sum_{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{h}\neq 0}\frac{2\langle\lambda_{k}|\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{h}\rangle}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{h}}|\lambda_{k}\rangle\langle\lambda_{h}|.

(73)

Thus, the elements of the quantum Fisher information matrix can be
obtained as

\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(\hat{\rho})=2\sum_{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}\neq 0}\frac{\langle\lambda_{k}|\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{l}\rangle\langle\lambda_{l}|\partial_{g_{j}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{k}\rangle}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{l}}.

(74)

As the density matrix (
1
) of the two point sources is
low-rank, it is more convenient to compute the quantum Fisher information
matrix by the support of the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(\hat{\rho})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{k}\neq 0}\frac{4\langle\lambda_{k}|\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho}\partial_{g_{j}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{k}\rangle}{\lambda_{k}}

(75)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\lambda_{k},\lambda_{h}\neq 0}2\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}+\lambda_{h}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{h}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\langle\lambda_{h}|\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{k}\rangle\langle\lambda_{k}|\partial_{g_{j}}\hat{\rho}|\lambda_{h}\rangle,

which is a variant of the result in Ref.
(
Liu et al. 2014
)
.

For the quantum superresolution of two point sources, the centroid
positions of the two point sources,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, or equivalently
the mean and difference of the two centroid positions,
\bar{X}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}
,
and
d=X_{2}-X_{1}
, are the unknown parameters to estimate, so
the multi-parameter quantum Cramér-Rao bound (
68
) tells
that

C[\bar{X},\,d]\geq N_{{\rm tot}}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}[\bar{X},\,d],

(76)

where
N_{{\rm tot}}
is the total number of photons
N_{1}+N_{2}
,
C[\bar{X},\,d]

is the covariance matrix for the estimates of
\bar{X},\,d
,
\mathcal{Q}[\bar{X},\,d]

is the quantum Fisher information matrix about
\bar{X},\,d
with
entries

\mathcal{Q}_{ij}(\hat{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\hat{\rho}\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i},\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}\}],i,j=1,2,

(77)

and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
are the symmetric logarithmic derivatives
(SLD) of the density operator
\hat{\rho}
,

\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}+\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\rho})=\partial_{g_{i}}\hat{\rho},\;g_{i}=\bar{X},d.

(78)

If one is interested in only one or a few unknown parameters, e.g.,
the centroid separation as in the superresolution, a positive-semidefinite
weight matrix
W
can be imposed on both sides of the quantum Cramér-Rao
inequality (
76
), then the matrix quantum Cramér-Rao
bound turns to a scalar form,

{\rm Tr}(WC)\geq N_{{\rm tot}}^{-1}{\rm Tr}(W\mathcal{Q}^{-1}).

(79)

Note that for quantum multiparameter estimation, the optimal measurements
for different parameters are not always compatible, so the matrix
quantum Cramér-Rao bound is not always achievable. The saturation
condition
(
Ragy et al. 2016
)
for the above scalar quantum Cramér-Rao
bound is

{\rm Tr}(\hat{\rho}[\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i},\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j}])={\rm Im[Tr}(\hat{\rho}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{j})]=0,

(80)

as the scalar quantum Cramér-Rao bound
N_{tot}^{-1}{\rm Tr}(W\mathcal{Q}^{-1})

coincides with the Holevo bound in this case and the latter is always
asymptotically achievable when
N_{{\rm tot}}\rightarrow\infty
. As the point-spread
functions
\psi_{i}(x)
are real in the quantum superresolution problem,
the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
and the eigenvalues and eigenstates of

\hat{\rho}
are also real in this case as will be shown in the following
sections, so the compatibility condition (
80
) is saturated,
and the scalar quantum Cramér-Rao bound (
79
)
can be achieved by the maximum-likelihood estimation strategy for

N_{{\rm tot}}\rightarrow\infty
.

\bar{X},\,d,\,\epsilon

\mathcal{Q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}4\kappa-4\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)&2\kappa\epsilon&2\gamma\delta\\
2\kappa\epsilon&\kappa&0\\
2\gamma\delta&0&\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{1-\epsilon^{2}}\end{array}\right],

(94)

where
\kappa
,
\gamma
are defined in Eq. (
88
) and

\delta
is defined in Eq. (
85
).Taking the inverse of
the quantum Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{Q}
(
94
),
the estimation precision of the centroid separation
d
can be worked
out as

\mathcal{H}_{d}=N_{{\rm tot}}/(\mathcal{Q}^{-1})_{22}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\kappa\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\left[\left(1-\delta^{2}\right)\kappa-\gamma^{2}\right]}{(1-\delta^{2})\kappa-\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}.

(95)

If the two photon numbers
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
are different and
N_{2}-N_{1}

keeps finite, the precision of centroid separation (
95
)
can be simplified to

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right){\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})d^{2}}{4\epsilon^{2}},

(96)

which is of order
O(d^{2})
, where
{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})=\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle^{2}
.
This indicates that estimation precision for the centroid separation
vanishes when
d
is small, in drastic constraint to the above results
for resolving two identical incoherent optical sources with known
photon numbers. So the quantum superresolution scheme does not work
and the Rayleigh’s curse takes effect again in this case, which is
called the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse.

In order to study how to avoid the resurgence of Rayleigh’s curse,
we need to take a deeper insight into how the resurgence of Rayleigh’s
curse occurs by expanding the numerator and denominator of
\mathcal{H}_{d}

(
95
) to the second order of
d
,

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{A_{0}+A_{2}d^{2}+A_{4}d^{4}}{B_{0}+B_{2}d^{2}+B_{4}d^{4}},

(97)

with the expansion coefficients

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=0,\;B_{0}=0,

(98)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=0,\;B_{2}=12\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle,

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle=3N_{{\rm tot}}(1-\epsilon^{2})\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2}),

\displaystyle B_{4}

\displaystyle=3{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})-4\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle,

which turns out to be

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{3N_{{\rm tot}}d^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})}{12\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle+d^{2}\left[3{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})-4\epsilon^{2}\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle\right]},

(99)

Obviously, when the photon numbers of the two incoherent point sources
are the same, i.e.,
\epsilon=0
, the leading constant term of the
denominator of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
vanishes and both the numerator
and the denominator of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
are of order
O(d^{2})
,
so
\mathcal{H}_{d}
keeps nonzero and independent of
d
when
d\rightarrow 0
,
which is the quantum superresolution of two incoherent point sources.
In contrast, if the photon numbers of the two optical sources are
different, i.e.,
N_{1}\neq N_{2}
, the magnitude of the numerator
of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
is
O(d^{2})
but the magnitude of the denominator
of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
is
O(1)
if the difference between the two
photon numbers keeps finite. In this case when
d\rightarrow 0
the
separation precision vanishes, which is the resurgence of the Rayleigh’s
curse.

Take the Gaussian point-spread functions as an example. The estimation
precision
\mathcal{H}_{d}
(
95
) becomes

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\left[d^{2}+4\sigma^{2}\left(1-{\rm e}^{\frac{d^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right)\right]}{4\sigma^{2}\left[4\sigma^{2}\left(1-{\rm e}^{\frac{d^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right)+d^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\right]}.

(100)

If we further denote the ratio between the centroid separation and
the total width of the two point sources as

r=\frac{d}{2\sigma},

(101)

the estimation precision
\mathcal{H}_{d}
(
100
) can be simplified
to

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)\left({\rm e}^{r^{2}}-1-r^{2}\right)}{{\rm e}^{r^{2}}-1-r^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})},

(102)

where
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}
is the optimal estimation precision of
d
when the
photon numbers of the two sources are the same, i.e.,
N_{1}=N_{2}=N
,

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}=\frac{N}{2\sigma^{2}}.

(103)

This implies that the ratio between the estimation precision
\mathcal{H}_{d}

to the optimal precision
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{({\rm opt})}
is determined by the parameters
\epsilon

and
r
only.

By expanding both the numerator and denominator of Eq. (
100
)
to the second order of
d
, one can have

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\frac{N_{{\rm tot}}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)d^{2}}{32\sigma^{4}\epsilon^{2}+4d^{2}\sigma^{2}},

(104)

showing
\mathcal{H}_{d}
keeps nonzero when
d
is small if
\epsilon=0

which is the quantum superresolution result and vanishes if
\epsilon

is nonzero and keeps finite which is the resurgence of Rayleigh’s
curse.

The above analysis givens an important implication that the magnitude
of
\epsilon
plays a critical role in determining the effect of
the quantum superresolution. If
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are different
but the magnitude of
\epsilon
is of order
O(d\sqrt{{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})/\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle})

or higher, the order of the denominator of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
is still

O(d^{2})
then and
\mathcal{H}_{d}
will not vanish in this case.
This immediately leads to the condition for the quantum superresolution
with two identical incoherent optical sources in the main text.

Appendix D
Precision
for resolving two arbitrary point sources

For two arbitrary optical point sources, the density operator can
be written as

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}{\rm e}^{-iX_{1}\hat{P}}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|{\rm e}^{iX_{1}\hat{P}}+\frac{1+\epsilon}{2}{\rm e}^{-iX_{2}\hat{P}}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|{\rm e}^{iX_{2}\hat{P}}.

(105)

To obtain the quantum Fisher information matrix, we need to work out
the eigenvalues and eigenstates of
\hat{\rho}
. Note that
\hat{\rho}
can
be infinite-dimensional in principle, but has only two nonzero eigenvalues,
which turn out to be

\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\mp\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+\delta^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}\right),

(106)

and the associated eigenstates
|\lambda_{1,2}\rangle
are

|\lambda_{1,2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathscr{Q}_{1,2}}}\times\{{\rm e}^{-iX_{1}\hat{P}}|\psi_{1}\rangle\mp\frac{\left(\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+\delta^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)}\mp\epsilon\right)}{\delta(1-\epsilon)}{\rm e}^{-iX_{2}\hat{P}}|\psi_{2}\rangle\},

(107)

where
\delta
is the overlap between the point-spread functions
of the two sources,

\delta=\langle\psi_{1}|{\rm e}^{iX_{1}\hat{P}}{\rm e}^{-iX_{2}\hat{P}}|\psi_{2}\rangle=\langle{\rm e}^{-id\hat{P}}\rangle_{12}=\langle\cos(d\hat{P})\rangle_{12},

(108)

with
\langle\cdot\rangle_{i}=\langle\psi_{i}|\cdot|\psi_{i}\rangle
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{12}=\langle\psi_{1}|\cdot|\psi_{2}\rangle
,

\mathscr{Q}_{1,2}
are the normalization constants,

\mathscr{Q}_{1,2}=\left(1-\delta^{2}\right)\left(1+\frac{\delta^{2}\left(1-\delta^{2}\right)(1-\epsilon)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\epsilon^{2}-\delta^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\pm\delta^{2}(1-\epsilon)\pm\epsilon\right)^{2}}\right).

(109)

When the photon numbers of the two optical sources are unknown, one
needs to estimate the ratio
\epsilon
of the difference between the two
photon numbers to the total photon number in addition to the mean

\bar{X}
and the separation
d
of the two centroid positions. The
quantum Fisher information matrix
\mathcal{Q}
for parameters
\bar{X},\,d,\,\epsilon

can be derived by Eq. (
75

\mathcal{Q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}2\kappa_{{\rm tot}}(1+\chi\epsilon)-4\gamma^{2}\left(1-\epsilon^{2}\right)&\kappa_{{\rm tot}}(\chi+\epsilon)&2\gamma\delta\\
\kappa_{{\rm tot}}(\chi+\epsilon)&\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{{\rm tot}}(1+\chi\epsilon)&0\\
2\gamma\delta&0&\frac{1-\delta^{2}}{1-\epsilon^{2}}\end{array}\right],

(110)

where

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}=

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{X_{i}}\psi_{i}(x-X_{i})|\partial_{X_{i}}\psi_{i}(x-X_{i})\rangle=\langle\psi_{i}|P^{2}|\psi_{i}\rangle=\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{i},\;i=1,2,

(111)

\displaystyle\gamma=

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{X_{1}}\psi_{1}(x-X_{1})|\psi_{2}(x-X_{2})\rangle=i\langle\psi_{1}|P{\rm e}^{-idP}|\psi_{2}\rangle=\langle\sin(d\hat{P})\hat{P}\rangle_{12},

\displaystyle\kappa_{{\rm tot}}=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2},

\displaystyle\chi=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}}{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}}.

By taking the inverse of the matrix
\mathcal{Q}

(112)

Now, one can expand
\delta
and
\gamma

\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{12}d^{2}+\frac{1}{24}\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}d^{4},

(113)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=d\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{12}-\frac{1}{6}d^{3}\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12},

Plugging Eq. (

Appendix E
Competing quantities in the validity of superresolution

E.1
Critical quantities

It can be seen from the results for two identical point sources that
the validity of superresolution is highly sensitive to the magnitude
of the leading term in the denominator of
\mathcal{H}_{d}
that
may be comparable to

and it turns out that the ratio of the difference

is a critical quantity that determines the validity of superresolution

\mathcal{H}_{d}

\mathcal{H}_{d}
(

comparable to
\widetilde{d}

\epsilon

)

and
\chi\rightarrow 0
,
\xi\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}
,
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,
leading to

\displaystyle A_{4}\rightarrow

\displaystyle N_{{\rm tot}}\kappa_{{\rm tot}}\left(3\xi^{2}\kappa_{{\rm tot}}^{2}-8\xi\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}+\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}\right),

(131)

\displaystyle B_{4}\rightarrow

\displaystyle 2\left(3\xi^{2}\kappa_{{\rm tot}}^{2}-8\xi\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}+\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}\right).

In this case,

3\xi^{2}\kappa_{{\rm tot}}^{2}-8\xi\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}+\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle_{12}\rightarrow 3\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle^{2}-3\langle\hat{P}^{4}\rangle=3{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2}).

(132)

{\rm Var}(\hat{P}^{2})
is generally nonzero unless the point-spread function

\psi(x)
is approximately an eigenstate of
\hat{P}
or equivalently
an uniformly-distributed point-spread function over the position space.
But this is determined by the property of the point-spread function
of either optical source, not by the difference between the two point-spread
functions, so there is no new critical quantity determined by the
difference of the two point-spread functions from the fourth-order
terms in
\mathcal{H}_{d}
.

In summary, we can identify
1-\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle
,
\chi
and
\frac{1}{2}-\xi

as the critical quantities that are determined by the difference between
the point-spread functions and will compete with each other and with
the ratio
\epsilon
beween the difference and the total of the photon
number in determining the validity and the estimation precision of
quantum superresolution.

E.2
Relation between critical quantities

When considering the competition between the critical quantities obtained
above in determining the validity of superresolution, it should be
noted that those quantities are not independent of each other but
closely related, and we need to find out the relation between those
quantities which will impose constraints on the range of estimation
precision for the centroid separation.

Assume that the difference between
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle

is small. To facilitate the following computation, we first construct
an orthornormal basis for the subspace spanned by
|\psi_{1}\rangle

and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
,

|\psi_{1}\rangle,\;|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle=\frac{|\psi_{2}\rangle-\delta|\psi_{1}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}.

(133)

|\psi_{2}\rangle
can be decomposed onto the basis states
|\psi_{1}\rangle

and
|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle
as

|\psi_{2}\rangle=\alpha|\psi_{1}\rangle+\beta|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle.

(134)

It is straightforward to verify that

\alpha=1-u,\;\beta=\sqrt{2u-u^{2}},

(135)

where

u=1-\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle.

(136)

Now, substituting
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
(
134
)
into
\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}
, one can have

\displaystyle\chi=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}}{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}},

(137)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(u^{2}-2u)\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle+2(1-u)\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle+(2u-u^{2})\langle\psi_{1}^{\bot}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle}{(u^{2}-2u+2)\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle+2(1-u)\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle+(2u-u^{2})\langle\psi_{1}^{\bot}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle}.

In the above equation,
u
is a small parameter when the two point-spread
functions are close to each other. The numerator ranges between
O(u^{\frac{1}{2}})

and
O(u)
, and the denominator ranges between
O(1)
and
O(u)
,
so the order of
\chi
is between
O(u^{1/2})
and
O(u)
.

Similarly, for
\frac{1}{2}-\xi
, we note that

\displaystyle\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{12}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle,

\displaystyle=(1-u)\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle+\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle,

(138)

so,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-\xi=

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}\right)-\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{12}}{\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{2}+\langle\hat{P}^{2}\rangle_{1}},

(139)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle-u\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(2u-u^{2})\langle\psi_{1}^{\bot}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle}{(u^{2}-2u+2)\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}\rangle+2(1-u)\sqrt{2u-u^{2}}\langle\psi_{1}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle+(2u-u^{2})\langle\psi_{1}^{\bot}|\hat{P}^{2}|\psi_{1}^{\bot}\rangle}.

The numerator ranges between
O(u)
and
O(u^{2})
, and the denominator
ranges between
O(1)
and
O(u)
, implying that
\frac{1}{2}-\xi

scales between
O(u)
and
O(u^{2})
when the two point-spread functions
are close to each other.

In order to make the critical quantities comparable, we denote

u=1-\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle=b\widetilde{d}^{h},\;\frac{1}{2}-\xi=a\widetilde{d}^{f},\;\chi=c\widetilde{d}^{e}.

(140)

According to the above ranges of
\chi
and
\frac{1}{2}-\xi
in
terms of
u
, we can immediately obtain the constraint condition
on the exponents
h
,
f
and
e
,

h/2\leq e\leq h\leq f\leq 2h.

(141)
