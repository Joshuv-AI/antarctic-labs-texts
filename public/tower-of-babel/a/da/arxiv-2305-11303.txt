Dilatations of categories


Abstract
Dilatations modify categories by imposing that some morphisms factorize through some others. This is formalized by a universal property. This text is devoted to introduce and study this construction. Examples of dilatations of categories include localizations of categories and dilatations of rings. Acknowledgement . In this text, we provide a construction unifying all of these constructions in a single construction: dilatations of categories. Intuitively, localization is a process that adds and imposes inverses of elements or morphisms. In other words, localization adds some ”fractions” with prescribed denominators. Intuitively, dilatations do the same thing except that it only adds some fractions where both numerators and denominators are prescribed.
Let

. For any

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}

Dilatations of categories: definition via fractions

We now fix a category
{\mathcal{C}}
with objects
Ob{\mathcal{C}}
and morphisms
Mor{\mathcal{C}}
.

2.1
Sieves

Recall that if
X\xrightarrow{a}Y
belongs to
Mor{\mathcal{C}}
,
dom(a):=X
is called the domain and
cod(a):=Y
the codomain (note that
dom
and
cod
also make sense for directed graphs). Recall that a sieve in
{\mathcal{C}}
is a collection of morphisms stable by precompositions. Note that a union of sieves is a sieve. A sieve over an object
Y
is a sieve made of morphisms with codomain
Y
. Similarly, a cosieve is a collection of morphisms stable by postcompositions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
E
be a collection of morphisms of
{\mathcal{C}}
. The collection
e\circ n
with
n\in Mor{\mathcal{C}}
,
e\in E
and
cod(n)=dom(e)
is a sieve called the sieve generated by
E
.
This sieve is denoted
S^{{\mathcal{C}}}_{E}
. If
E=\{d\}
is a singleton, we put
S^{{\mathcal{C}}}_{E}=S^{{\mathcal{C}}}_{d}
. Note that if
d=Id_{X}
,
S^{{\mathcal{C}}}_{Id_{X}}
is the sieve made of all morphisms with codomain
X
. Similarly, we use the notation
CoS^{{\mathcal{C}}}_{E}
to denote the cosieve generated by
E
.

2.2
Directed graphs and localizations

In this section, we discuss localizations of categories. The content of this subsection is classical and does not claim originality (cf. e.g.

\Sigma

{\mathcal{C}}

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be the oriented graph defined as follows. The vertices of
{\mathcal{G}}
are equal to the objects of
{\mathcal{C}}
. The directed lines of
{\mathcal{G}}
are made of

1.

for each morphism

of
{\mathcal{C}}

dom(a)\xrightarrow{a}cod(a)
of
{\mathcal{G}}
,

2.

for each morphism
d
in
\Sigma
a directed line
cod(d)\xrightarrow{l_{d}}dom(d)
of
{\mathcal{G}}
(in particular
dom(l_{d})=cod(d)
and
cod(l_{d})=dom(d)
).

Definition 2.3
.

A
\Sigma
-sequence of directed lines in
{\mathcal{G}}
is a sequence, finite and possibly empty, of directed lines
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
such that
cod(x_{i})=dom(x_{i+1})
(
n\geqslant 0
is an integer). By convention, an empty sequence is just an element in the collection
Ob{\mathcal{C}}
. For a non-empty sequence
s=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we define the domain as
dom(s)=dom(x_{1})
and the codomain as
cod(s)=cod(x_{n})
. If a sequence
s
is empty and given by an object
X
, then we put
dom(s)=cod(s)=X
. Note that
\Sigma
-sequences of directed lines with compatible domains and codomains can be composed (by convention, composing a sequence
s
with an empty sequence
e
gives
s
).

Definition 2.4
.

We say that two
\Sigma
-sequences of directed lines
s
and
s^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{G}}
are equivalent if
dom(s)=dom(s^{\prime}),

cod(s)=cod(s^{\prime})
and if one can be obtained from the other by a chain of elementary equivalences of the following types:

1.

a sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
such that
x_{i},x_{i+1}
are equal to
a,a^{\prime}\in Mor{\mathcal{C}}
and
cod(a)=dom(a^{\prime})
, for some
1\leqslant i,i+1\leqslant n
, is equivalent to the sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x^{\prime},x_{i+2},\ldots,x_{n}
where
x^{\prime}
is the composition of
a
and
a^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
,

2.

a sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
such that
x_{i},x_{i+1}
are equal to
l_{d},d
with
d\in\Sigma
, for some
1\leqslant i,i+1\leqslant n
, is equivalent to the sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+2},\ldots,x_{n}
,

3.

a sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
such that
x_{i},x_{i+1}
are equal to
d,l_{d}
with
d\in\Sigma
, for some
1\leqslant i,i+1\leqslant n
, is equivalent to the sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+2},\ldots,x_{n}
,

4.

for any object
X
, the sequence
Id_{X}
is equivalent to the empty sequence at
X
.

In other words and informally, two
\Sigma
-sequences of directed lines are equivalent if one can be obtained from the other by the operations consisting in exchanging parts of sequences as follows:

\displaystyle X\xrightarrow{a}Y\xrightarrow{a^{\prime}}Z

\displaystyle\leftrightsquigarrow X\xrightarrow{a^{\prime}\circ a}Z

\displaystyle Y\xrightarrow{l_{d}}Z\xrightarrow{d}Y

\displaystyle\leftrightsquigarrow Y\text{ (the empty sequence at $Y$)}

\displaystyle Z\xrightarrow{d}Y\xrightarrow{l_{d}}Z

\displaystyle\leftrightsquigarrow Z\text{ (the empty sequence at $Z$)}

\displaystyle X\xrightarrow{Id_{X}}X

\displaystyle\leftrightsquigarrow X\text{ (the empty sequence at $X$)}.

The equivalence class of a sequence

is denoted by

.
Note that equivalence classes of
\Sigma
-sequences of directed lines with compatible domains and codomains can be composed associatively.

Definition 2.5
.

A
\Sigma
-fraction is an equivalence class of
\Sigma
-sequence.

Fact 2.6

Let
d,d^{\prime}
in
\Sigma
such that
cod(d)=dom(d^{\prime})
and let
d^{\prime\prime}
be their composite. Assume that
d^{\prime\prime}
belongs to
\Sigma
. Then

[cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime}}}dom(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d}}dom(d)]=[cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime\prime}}}dom(d)].

Proof.

This follows from the equalities

\displaystyle=[cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime}}}dom(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d}}dom(d)]

\displaystyle=[cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime}}}dom(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d}}dom(d)\xrightarrow{d^{\prime\prime}}cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime\prime}}}dom(d)]

\displaystyle=[cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime}}}dom(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d}}dom(d)\xrightarrow{d}cod(d)\xrightarrow{d^{\prime}}cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime\prime}}}dom(d)]

\displaystyle=[cod(d^{\prime})\xrightarrow{l_{d^{\prime\prime}}}dom(d)].

∎

Definition 2.7
.

The localization of
{\mathcal{C}}
relatively to
\Sigma
is the category
{\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]
whose objects are the objects of
{\mathcal{C}}
and whose morphisms are
\Sigma
-fractions. We have a canonical functor
L:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]
.

Proposition 2.8

((Universal property of localization))

Let
F:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
be a functor such that
F(d)
is invertible for all
d
in
\Sigma
. Then there exists a unique functor
F^{\prime}:{\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]\to{\mathcal{D}}
such that
F=F^{\prime}\circ L
.

Proof.

We first prove unicity. Assume that
F^{\prime}
exists. Let
X
be in
Ob{\mathcal{C}}=Ob{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
, then
F^{\prime}(X)=F^{\prime}(L(X))=F(X)
. Let
a
be in
Mor{\mathcal{C}}
, then
F^{\prime}([a])=F^{\prime}(L(a))=F(a)
. Let
d
be in
\Sigma
, then
F^{\prime}([l_{d}])\circ F(d)=F^{\prime}([l_{d}]\circ[d])=Id_{dom(F(d))}
and
F(d)\circ F^{\prime}([l_{d}])=F^{\prime}([d]\circ[l_{d}])=Id_{cod(F(d))}
. This implies that
F^{\prime}([l_{d}])=F(d)^{-1}
. This proves unicity. To prove existence, it is enough to prove that the assignements
X\mapsto X,[a]\mapsto F(a),l_{d}\mapsto F(d)^{-1}
(as before) provide a well-defined functor
F^{\prime}
. For this it is enough to prove that the assignements are compatible with the elementary equivalences of Definition
2.4
, which is immediate.
∎

2.3
Dilatations of categories: definition

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a category.

Definition 2.9
.

A center in
{\mathcal{C}}
is a collection
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
of pairs
[N_{i},d_{i}]
, indexed by a collection
I
and such that, for all
i
in
I
,
d_{i}
is a morphism of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
N_{i}
is a sieve over
cod(d_{i})
.

We now fix a center
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
(we sometimes use the notation
N_{d_{i}}
to denote
N_{i}
).

Put
\Sigma=\{d_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.

Definition 2.10
.

A
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
-fraction is a
\Sigma
-fraction such that a representative can be written as

X_{1}\xrightarrow{n_{1}}Y_{1}\xrightarrow{l_{d_{i_{1}}}}X_{2}\xrightarrow{n_{2}}Y_{2}\xrightarrow{l_{d_{i_{2}}}}X_{3}\ldots X_{k}\xrightarrow{n_{k}}Y_{k}\xrightarrow{l_{d_{i_{k}}}}X_{k+1}\xrightarrow{a}X_{k+2}

with
a\in Mor{\mathcal{C}}
,
k\geqslant 0
and
{i_{j}}\in I
,
n_{i}\in N_{i_{j}}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.

Fact 2.11

\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
-fractions with compatible domains and codomains can be composed associatively.

Proof.

This is immediate since the
N_{i}^{\prime}s
are sieves. Associativity is immediate.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

1.

If
X\xrightarrow{a}Y
is a morphism in
{\mathcal{C}}
, the

-fraction

is a

-fraction. In particular, the class

of the identity at an object

2.

The

is a

-fraction since it is equivalent to

Definition 2.13
.

The dilatation of
{\mathcal{C}}
with center
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
is the category
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
defined as follows. The objects of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
are equal to the objects of
{\mathcal{C}}
. If
X
and
Y
are objects in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
, morphisms between

and

are given by

-fractions (cf. Definition

shows that

is indeed a category, which is also denoted

Fact 2.14

We have a faithful functor
{\mathcal{C}}\left[\left\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ N_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}\right]\to{\mathcal{C}}\left[\left(\left\{d_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}\right)^{-1}\right]

The functor is obtained regarding

Assume that, for all

is the sieve

Universal property of dilatations and related results

We proceed with the notation from §

{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{C}}\left[\left\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ N_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}\right]

Proposition 3.1

The following assertions hold.

1.

We have a canonical functor
\Theta:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
defined as follows.
If
X
is an object of
{\mathcal{C}}
,
\Theta(X)=X
. If
X\xrightarrow{a}Y
is a morphism of
{\mathcal{C}}
,
\Theta(a)=[X\xrightarrow{a}Y]
.

2.

For any

I
and any

, there exists a unique morphism

in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
such that the following triangle commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces dom(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces cod(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces dom(d_{i})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[n]}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[d_{i}]}
.
Intuitively, the morphism

is thought of as a non-commutative fraction

of morphisms. Mathematically, we have

b=[X\xrightarrow{n}Y\xrightarrow{l_{d_{i}}}Z].

Proof.

1.

Obvious.

2.

Put

and

.
The class

equals the class

such that the triangle commutes. We now prove unicity. Assume that

satisfies that the composition

equals

. Let

is equal to the class
[X\xrightarrow{n}Y\xrightarrow{l_{d_{i}}}Z]
and also to the class
[X\xrightarrow{s}Z]
. This shows that

equals
[X\xrightarrow{n}Y\xrightarrow{l_{d_{i}}}Z]
.

∎

Recall that a bimorphism is defined as a morphism which is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism. Bimorphisms are also called regular morphisms.

Fact 3.2

A morphism whose image under a faithful functor is a bimorphism is itself a bimorphism.

Proof.

Let
F:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{B}}
be a faithful functor. Let
f
be a morphism of
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
F(f)
is a bimorphism. Let
a,b,c,d
be morphisms of
{\mathcal{A}}
such that
a\circ f=b\circ f
and
f\circ c=f\circ d
. Then
F(a)\circ F(f)=F(b)\circ F(f)
and
F(f)\circ F(c)=F(f)\circ F(d)
. So
F(a)=F(b)
and
F(c)=F(d)
. Consequently
a=b
and
c=d
. This proves that
f
is a bimorphism.
∎

Proposition 3.3

Let
i\in I
, then
\Theta(d_{i})
is a bimorphism in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

A morphism whose image under a faithful functor is a bimorphism is itself a bimorphism by Fact

. Now Proposition

follows from Fact

.
∎

Fact 3.11

Let

be a functor between two categories. Let

be a collection of morphisms of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then

.

Proof.

We have
E\subset S^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{E}
, so
S^{{\mathcal{B}}}_{H(E)}\subset S^{\mathcal{B}}_{H(S^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{E})}
. Reciprocally, let

, then there exists
b\in Mor{\mathcal{B}}
,
a\in Mor{\mathcal{A}}
and

. So
x
belongs to
S^{{\mathcal{B}}}_{H(E)}
.
∎

Definition 4.1
.

A cocenter
\{[V_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
is a collection of pairs
[V_{i},d_{i}]
where
d_{i}
is a morphism of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
V_{i}
is a cosieve from
\operatorname{dom}(d_{i})
.

We now fix a cocenter
\{[V_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
.

Fact 4.2

In
{\mathcal{C}}^{op}
, for all
i
in
I
,
V_{i}
can be regarded as sieve over
cod^{{\mathcal{C}}^{op}}(d_{i})=dom^{{\mathcal{C}}}(d_{i})
. In particular
\{[V_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
can be regarded as a center in
{\mathcal{C}}^{op}
.

Proof.

By definition, the collection of morphisms of a cosieve is a sieve in the opposite category.
∎

Definition 4.3
.

We put
{\mathcal{C}}\left[\{V_{i}\circ(d_{i})^{-1}\}_{i\in I}\right]=\left({\mathcal{C}}^{op}\left[\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ V_{i}\}_{i\in I}\right]\right)^{op}
. The category
{\mathcal{C}}\left[\{V_{i}\circ(d_{i})^{-1}\}_{i\in I}\right]
is called the codilatation of
{\mathcal{C}}
with cocenter
\{[V_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
.

Fact 4.4

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a category. Let
E
be a collection of morphisms of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then
CoS^{A}_{E}=S^{A^{op}}_{E}
.

Proof.

CoS^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{E}=\{x\circ_{{\mathcal{A}}}e|dom^{\mathcal{A}}(x)=cod^{\mathcal{A}}(e)\}=\{e\circ_{{\mathcal{A}}^{op}}x|dom^{{\mathcal{A}}^{op}}(e)=cod^{{\mathcal{A}}}(x)\}=S_{E}^{{\mathcal{A}}^{op}}
.
∎

1.

We have a canonical functor
\Upsilon:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}\left[\{V_{i}\circ(d_{i})^{-1}\}_{i\in I}\right]
.

2.

We have a canonical faithful functor
{\mathcal{C}}\left[\{V_{i}\circ(d_{i})^{-1}\}_{i\in I}\right]\to{\mathcal{C}}\left[(\{d_{i}\}_{i\in I})^{-1}\right].

3.

The functor

is an object in the category

shows that the localization
{\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]
is equal to the dilatation
{\mathcal{C}}[\{d^{-1}\circ N_{d}\}_{d\in\Sigma}]
where
N_{d}=S_{Id_{cod(d)}}^{{\mathcal{C}}}
. Obviously,
{\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]\big[[\Sigma]^{-1}\big]={\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]
and so
{\mathcal{C}}[\Sigma^{-1}]
belongs to
Cat_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\Sigma\text{-reg}}
. Now if
F:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
is another
{\mathcal{C}}
-category, such that
F(\Sigma)
is made of isomorphisms, again
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{D}}[F(\Sigma)^{-1}]
so that
{\mathcal{D}}
also belongs to
Cat_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\Sigma\text{-reg}}
. Moreover in this case it is obvious that for any
d\in\Sigma
, we have

S^{{\mathcal{D}}}_{F(N_{d})}\subset S^{{\mathcal{D}}}_{F(d)}.

So by Theorem
3.10
,
F
factors uniquely through
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
. So the universal property of dilatations generalizes the universal property of localizations.

5.0.2
Dilatations of commutative rings an semirings

Proposition 5.1

Let
A
be a commutative unital ring. Let
I
be a set and let
\{[M_{i},a_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
be a center in
A
in the sense of
[
M
]
. Let
A[\{\frac{M_{i}}{a_{i}}\}_{i\in I}]
be the dilatation of
A
with center
\{[M_{i},a_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
as in
[
M
]
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be the category attached to
A
. In particular
a_{i}
is a morphism of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
M_{i}
is a sieve for all
i
in
I
. We have
Ob{\mathcal{C}}=\{\bullet\}
, a singleton. Let
{\mathcal{C}}[\{\frac{M_{i}}{a_{i}}\}_{i\in I}]
be the category attached to the ring
A[\{\frac{M_{i}}{a_{i}}\}_{i\in I}]
.
We have an identification of
{\mathcal{C}}
-functors
{\mathcal{C}}[\{(a_{i})^{-1}\circ M_{i}\}_{i\in I}]={\mathcal{C}}[\{\frac{M_{i}}{a_{i}}\}_{i\in I}]
.

Proof.

Let
\Phi
be the functor
{\mathcal{C}}[\{(a_{i})^{-1}\circ M_{i}\}_{i\in I}]\to{\mathcal{C}}[\{\frac{M_{i}}{a_{i}}\}_{i\in I}]
given by
[\bullet\xrightarrow{m}\bullet\xrightarrow{l_{a_{i}}}\bullet]\mapsto\frac{m}{a_{i}}
. It is well-defined, surjective, injective and provides the desired identification.
∎

This shows that dilatations of categories generalize dilatations of rings.
As noticed in
[
M
]
, dilatations of commutative semirings make sense. The same argument as before shows that dilatations of semirings also provide examples of dilatations of categories.

5.0.3
Dilatations of monoids

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a small category with a single object, i.e. a (not necessarily commutative) monoid
M
. Let
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
be a center of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then
{\mathcal{C}}[\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ N_{i}\}_{i\in I}]
is a category with a single object endowed with a functor
{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}[\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ N_{i}\}_{i\in I}]
. In other words,
{\mathcal{C}}[\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ N_{i}\}_{i\in I}]
is an
M
-monoid
M^{\prime}
. We refer to this construction as dilatations of monoids. This generalizes localizations of monoids. Codilatations of monoids also make sense.

5.0.4
Dilatations of pre-additive categories and non-commutative rings

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a pre-additive category, in general a dilatation of
{\mathcal{C}}
is not pre-additive as we know that this fails already for localization, cf. e.g.
[
A
]
. Nevertheless, as in the case of localizations, it should be possible to study dilatations of pre-additive categories once we have an adapted calculus of fractions. This is related to investigate dilatations of non-commutative rings.

5.0.5
A characterization of dilatations of rings that does not hold for categories

In the context of dilatations of rings,
[
M
, Fact 2.14]
shows that for any sub-
A
-algebra
B
of a localization
A[(\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I})^{-1}]
, there is a multi-center in
A
such that
B
identifies with the associated dilatation of
A
. Here we explain that this characterization does not extend to dilatations of categories.

Fact 5.2

There exists a category
{\mathcal{C}}
, a collection of morphisms
\Gamma
, a functor
{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
that is the identity on objects, and a faithful functor
{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]
such that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{D}}}

commutes and such that
{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
is not isomorphic to
{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}[\{(d_{i})^{-1}\circ N_{i}\}_{i\in I}]
for all centers
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be the category with two objects
X
and
Y
and whose morphisms are as follows

Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}}(X,X)=Id_{X},Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}}(Y,Y)=Id_{Y},Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}}(Y,X)=\emptyset\text{ and }Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}}(X,Y)=\{a,b\}

Let
\Gamma
be the collection of morphisms of
{\mathcal{C}}
given by

\Gamma=\{b\}.

Then
{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]
is the category whose objects are
X
and
Y
and whose morphisms are as follows

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]}(X,X)

\displaystyle=\{Id_{X},b^{-1}a,b^{-1}ab^{-1}a,\ldots,(b^{-1}a)^{n},\ldots(n\in{\mathbb{N}})\},

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]}(Y,Y)

\displaystyle=\{Id_{Y},ab^{-1},ab^{-1}ab^{-1},\ldots(ab^{-1})^{,}\ldots(n\in{\mathbb{N}})\},

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\{b,a,ab^{-1}a,ab^{-1}ab^{-1}a,\ldots,a(b^{-1}a)^{n},\ldots(n\in{\mathbb{N}})\},

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=\{b^{-1},b^{-1}ab^{-1},b^{-1}ab^{-1}ab^{-1},\ldots,b^{-1}(ab^{-1})^{n},\ldots(n\in{\mathbb{N}})\}.

Now let
{\mathcal{D}}
be the subcategory of
{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]
whose objects are
X
and
Y
and whose morphisms are as follows

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{D}}}(X,X)

\displaystyle=Id_{X},

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Y,Y)

\displaystyle=Id_{Y},

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{D}}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\{b,a,ab^{-1}a\},

\displaystyle Hom_{{\mathcal{D}}}(Y,X)

\displaystyle=\varnothing.

Then we have canonical functors
{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}[\Gamma^{-1}]
. We claim that there is no center
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
such that
{\mathcal{D}}
identifies with the associated dilatation.
To prove this claim, we chose a center
\{[N_{i},d_{i}]\}_{i\in I}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
, put
\Sigma=\{d_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and exhaustively distinguish two cases:

1.

if (
a\in\Sigma
and (
Id_{Y}\in N_{a}
or
b\in N_{a}
)) or (
b\in\Sigma
and (
Id_{Y}\in N_{b}
or
a\in N_{b}
)), then
\#Hom_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}(X,Y)
is infinite,

2.

if (
a\in\Sigma\Rightarrow N_{a}\subset\{a\}
) and (
b\in\Sigma\Rightarrow N_{b}\subset\{b\}
), then
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{C}}
.

In all cases
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\neq{\mathcal{D}}
.
∎
