Studying time-like proton form factors using vortex state p ​ p ¯ p\bar{p} annihilation
Nikolai Korchagin

Abstract
Vortex states of particles — non-plane-wave solutions of the corresponding wave equation with a helicoidal wave front — open new opportunities for particle physics, unavailable in plane wave scattering. Here we demonstrate that p ​ p ¯ p\bar{p} annihilation with a vortex proton and antiproton provides access to the phase of proton electromagnetic form factors even in unpolarized scattering.



I
Introduction

A twisted (or vortex) state of a field is a non–plane-wave solution of a wave equation that, on average, propagates along a certain direction (e.g., axis z) and possesses a non-zero z-projection of the intrinsic orbital angular momentum (OAM).

Vortex states of optical photons were produced in 1990s and have since found numerous applications

Torres and Torner 2011
;
Andrews and Babiker 2013
;
Allen et al. 1999
;
Molina-Terriza et al. 2007
;
Padgett 2017
;
Knyazev and Serbo 2018

Bliokh et al. 2007
, and shortly afterwards were experimentally produced
Uchida and Tonomura 2010
;
Verbeeck et al. 2010
;
McMorran et al. 2011
.
The energy of these vortex electrons was low, up to 300 keV.
There are proposals to put high-energy electrons in vortex states or to accelerate low-energy vortex electrons to higher energies, but none of them has been realized yet.
Moreover, cold neutrons
Clark et al. 2015
;
Sarenac et al. 2018
;
Sarenac et al. 2019
;
Sarenac et al. 2022
and even atoms
Luski et al. 2021
in twisted states have been achieved experimentally.

In the last 10 years, the application of twisted states has become a flourishing research field in optics
Torres and Torner 2011
;
Andrews and Babiker 2013
;
Allen et al. 1999
;
Molina-Terriza et al. 2007
;
Padgett 2017

;
Juchtmans et al. 2015

Leach et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2012
, atomic and condensed matter physics.
However, in the particle and nuclear physics communities, vortex states are not well known, mainly because experiments reported so far are limited to very low energies.

Nevertheless, interest is growing, and there have been several proposals on how to bring vortex states of photons and massive particles to energies in the MeV or GeV range
Karlovets et al. 2022a
;
Karlovets et al. 2022b
. When demonstrated experimentally, they will offer new opportunities for nuclear, hadron physics, quantum electrodynamics.

Creating high-energetic twisted particles requires novel experimental setups and dedicated efforts. Nevertheless, certain collision experiments with twisted photons, electrons, and nucleons can be performed using existing technology.

The potential of vortex states for application in high-energy physics was demonstrated in number of cases. Some of them are:

More examples could be found in reviews

Although twisted protons or other hadrons have not been demonstrated yet, once they are, they will give a new playground for particle physics, enabling to study numerous phenomena even with non-relativistic protons.
For example, due to proton’s high mass, the energy released in
p\bar{p}
annihilation at rest is sufficient to produce strongly interacting particles, such as pions and kaons, as well as electromagnetically interacting ones (photons, electrons and muons).

The goals of this work is to demonstrate yet another possible application of vortex states in particle physics, specifically, how it can be used to study electromagnetic form factors (FFs) of the proton.
This paper shows that in twisted
p\bar{p}
annihilation to
e^{+}e^{-}

II
Twisted states and their scattering

This section gives a qualitative picture of vortex states and properties of double-vortex scattering.
More detailed description can be found in Appendices
A
,
B
and reviews
Ivanov 2022
;
Bliokh et al. 2017
.

What distinguishes a vortex from a plane wave (PW) is the structure of the wave front.
A plane wave has flat phase fronts that are globally normal to the propagation direction.
In contrast, the phase front of the vortex wave has a helical shape winding around the axis of propagation. As a result, the phase depends on the angular position about the axis.

The simplest type of a vortex state is the so-called Bessel state.
In cylindrical coordinates
(\rho,\phi_{r},z)
, a scalar particle with mass
m
, propagating on average along
z
direction, is characterized by a function

\begin{split}\Psi(r,t)&=\frac{N}{\sqrt{2E}}\psi_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{r})e^{-iEt+ik_{z}z},\\
\psi_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{r})&=\sqrt{\frac{\varkappa}{2\pi}}J_{l}(\rho\varkappa)e^{il\phi_{r}},\end{split}

(1)

where
E=\sqrt{\vec{k}^{2}+m^{2}}
and
N
is the normalization factor.
The integer topological charge
l
(also called the winding number) in the radial part quantizes the winding, such that the phase changes
2\pi l
during a full rotation about the axis. The phase factor of
e^{il\phi_{r}}
, that gives rise to this helical phase structure, is a characteristic feature of orbital angular momentum. For example, a similar phase factor appears in the azimuthal components of the electron wave functions in the hydrogen atom.

The helical phase structure of the vortex beam leads to an indeterminate phase at the axis of the beam, since it is connected to all possible phases of the wave. This central phase singularity is compensated by vanishing of
\psi_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{r})
on axis (at the location of the singularity). For
l\neq 0
, radial part
\psi(0)=0
in (
1
) so that the intensity is exactly zero on the axis. This gives the beam a cross-sectional distribution in the form of a ring, Fig.
1
.

In the momentum space, the Bessel state forms a circle of radius
\varkappa\equiv|\mathbf{k}|
with offset
k_{z}
from the origin along
z
axis, Fig.
1
.
The radial part of the wave function
\psi_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{r})
is related to its momentum representation
a_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{k})
by the usual Fourier transform
Jentschura and Serbo 2011a
;
Jentschura and Serbo 2011b
,

\begin{split}\psi_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{r})&=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}a_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}},\\
a_{l,\varkappa}(\mathbf{k})&={(-i)}^{l}e^{il\phi_{k}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\varkappa}}\delta(|\mathbf{k}|-\varkappa).\end{split}

(2)

\varkappa
quantifies the transverse momentum content, hidden inside the Bessel state, although on average
<\!\!\mathbf{k}\!\!>=0
.
The momentum vectors of the plane wave components form a cone with the opening angle
\theta
.
The paraxial regime, which will often be used below, corresponds to
\theta\ll 1
.
Other vortex states, like the Laguerre-Gaussian state, can be regarded as superpositions of many Bessel type vortex states.
In this paper we work only with pure Bessel states, because it simplifies the calculation for scattering amplitudes.

Until now, we have discussed scalar particles. It is possible to construct a vortex state for a particle with an internal spin degree of freedom, like electron or photon (see

We give a detailed description of spin-

Now let us consider the scattering of two Bessel states.
For simplicity, now we will focus on spinless particles in order to demonstrate novel kinematical effects, inherent for double-vortex scattering.
We define two counter-propagating Bessel states with a common axis
z
.
The final state is represented by two particles described as plane waves.

Bessel states in transverse momentum space form circles of radius
\varkappa_{1}
and
\varkappa_{2}
.

\mathbf{k}_{1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{2}
sum up to the total transverse momenta
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{k}_{2}
. Since angles
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are arbitrary,
\mathbf{K}
spans a ring (possible values of
\mathbf{K}
will lay inside the ring
|\varkappa_{1}-\varkappa_{2}|<\mathbf{K}<\varkappa_{1}+\varkappa_{2}
)

It is in contrast with PW scattering, where initial states in the momentum transverse plane are points. Thus,
{\bf K}
is also completely fixed (
{\bf K}=0
in c.m. frame).
Therefore, the cross section in double-Bessel scattering has an additional distribution over the total transverse momenta
{\bf K}
.

For final particles, the situation is similar to usual PW scattering. Since they are described by PW states, their transverse momenta sum up to some fixed
\mathbf{K}
.
It means that a given configuration of detected final particles corresponds to some point on
\mathbf{K}
-ring.
Although two Bessel state collision realizes many
\mathbf{K}
, for given momenta of detected particles, only one particular vector
{\bf K}
is selected.
There are two ways to put together the same vector
{\bf K}
.
Any given value of
{\bf K}
inside the ring could be achieved by two possible combinations of vectors
{\bf k}_{1}
and
{\bf k}_{2}
, Fig.
2
.
These two possibilities, denoted
a
and
b
, are related by reflection of
{\bf k}_{1,2}
with respect to
{\bf K}
.

Figure 2:
The two plane wave configurations in the transverse momentum plane that can lead to the final transverse momentum
{\bf K}
in double-Bessel scattering. The shaded ring represents possible values for
{\bf K}

It is a distinguishing feature of two-vortex scattering, which leads to the fact that the total scattering amplitude is a sum of two plane wave scattering amplitudes,
{\mathcal{M}}_{a}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{b}
, with different kinematics of initial particles,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i(l_{1}-l_{2})\phi_{K}}}{\sin(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{M}_{a}e^{i(l_{1}\delta_{1}+l_{2}\delta_{2})}+\mathcal{M}_{b}e^{-i(l_{1}\delta_{1}+l_{2}\delta_{2})}\Big).

(3)

Due to interference between
{\mathcal{M}}_{a}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{b}
, the differential cross section demonstrates oscillations in
\mathbf{K}
distribution, forming concentric interference fringes.
Contrast and form of fringes are defined by details of amplitudes
\mathcal{M}
and parameters of Bessel states (
E_{i}
,
\varkappa_{i}
,
l_{i}
).

What will one see in such a double-vortex into two PWs scattering experiment?
In every scattering event, provided that both final particles are detected,
one can sum final momenta and make a differential cross section distribution over
\mathbf{K}
-ring.
In current work we show that this distribution can be used to study electromagnetic FFs of nucleon.

III
Nucleon electromagnetic form factors in time-like region

Understanding the internal structure of the nucleon is a cornerstone of the theory of strong interaction.
As a composite fermion, the proton is described by two FFs: electric
G_{E}(q^{2})
and magnetic
G_{M}(q^{2})
.
Proton FFs in space-like regime
(q^{2}<0)
could be measured in the elastic electron-proton scattering. The annihilation channel, in
e^{+}e^{-}\to p{\bar{p}}
and
p{\bar{p}}\to e^{+}e^{-}
reactions, is used for studying the time-like region, since
q^{2}>0
.
In the space-like regime, FFs are real valued functions and related to distribution of electric charge and magnetization (note that such interpretation is valid only in the Breit frame
Pacetti et al. 2014
).
In contrast, in the time-like region, FFs are complex valued functions of total energy and related through a Fourier transform in time to the transition amplitude from a virtual-photon state to a hadronic state.

In the past, independent measurement of time-like FFs was unavailable due to limited luminosities of
e^{+}e^{-}
colliders. In the last decade, experimental efforts allowed measurement of the differential cross section and separation of
G_{E}
and
G_{M}
.

In the one photon exchange approximation, the differential cross section in c.m.s. for
p\bar{p}\to e^{+}e^{-}
is

\frac{d\sigma_{p\bar{p}\to e^{+}e^{-}}}{d\cos\theta}=\frac{\pi\alpha^{2}}{2q^{2}\beta}\Big[|G_{M}|^{2}(1+\cos^{2}\theta)+\frac{1}{\tau}|G_{E}|^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\Big],

(4)

where
\beta=\sqrt{1-1/\tau}
, with
\tau=q^{2}/(4M^{2})\geq 1
, is the velocity of the incoming proton.
\alpha
is the fine structure constant. In annihilation channel
q^{2}=s
, where
s
is the standard Mandelstam kinematical variable.
Note that Eq. (
4
) depends on the modulus of FFs.

On the other hand, as was mentioned before, time-like FFs are complex valued functions and, therefore, have phases.
The phase of an individual FF is not observable. However, the phase difference between two FFs could be measured.
Knowledge of this phase will bring new insights about the structure of the proton.
However, all unpolarized experiments are insensitive to the FF phase.

To measure the relative FFs phase, one can perform an experiment with polarized initial particles or measure polarizations in the final state.
If we consider the
p{\bar{p}}
annihilation in a reference system with the
z
axis along the antiproton beam momentum, and
xz
is the scattering plane, the dependence of the cross section on the polarizations
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
of the colliding antiproton and proton can be written as
Pacetti et al. 2014

\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{1},\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\right)=\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{0}\Big[1+A_{y}\left(P_{1y}+P_{2y}\right)\\
+A_{xx}P_{1x}P_{2x}+A_{yy}P_{1y}P_{2y}+A_{zz}P_{1z}P_{2z}\\
+A_{xz}\left(P_{1x}P_{2z}+P_{1z}P_{2x}\right)\Big]

(5)

where the coefficients
A_{i}
and
A_{ij}=A_{ji}
are analyzing powers and symmetric correlation coefficients.
Two of them are sensitive to the relative FF phase shift:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{0}A_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\alpha^{2}}{2\beta q^{2}\sqrt{\tau}}\sin(2\theta)\mathrm{Im}\{G_{M}G_{E}^{*}\},

(6)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{0}A_{xz}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\alpha^{2}}{2\beta q^{2}\sqrt{\tau}}\sin(2\theta)\mathrm{Re}\{G_{M}G_{E}^{*}\}.

(7)

A_{y}
could be measured as a transverse single spin asymmetry of the cross section.
One could also consider double-spin observables
Tomasi-Gustafsson et al. 2005
in order to extract
\mathrm{Re}\{G_{E}G_{M}^{*}\}
, which is useful if the relative FF phase is small.

Alternatively, one can measure polarization of final proton in unpolarized
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation
Dubničková et al. 1996
.
Experimentally, it could be achieved by including a polarimeter in the experimental setup of a collider experiment.

In this work we propose an alternative method to study the relative FF phase by using vortex states, which allows us to probe the phase even in an unpolarized process.

However, in twisted annihilation, it can be off the plane.

With the choice Eq. (
19
), the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair is represented by two long, almost opposite vectors in the transverse momentum plane. Their total sum is
{\bf k}_{3}+{\bf k}_{4}={\bf K}
.
The angle between
{\bf k}_{3}
and
{\bf k}_{4}
is close to
\pi
, however we do not want to put it exactly
\pi
, because, as we will see further, the asymmetry in respect to the scattering plane is associated with the relative FF phase.

Essentially, we want to pickup small variations in cross section caused by mirror reflection from the scattering plane, while disregard other minor effects.
One way to do it is to notice that the polar angle
\theta_{4}\approx\pi-\theta_{3}
varies only slightly if
{\bf K}\ll{\bf k}_{3,4}
, and it symmetric under reflection from the scattering plane.
In contrast, although
{\bar{\phi}}_{-}\approx-\pi
, it is odd with respect to the reflection. Therefore, we want to keep full azimuthal dependence in exponents
e^{i\xi{\bar{\phi}}_{-}}
.

Figure 5:
The kinematics of the final
e^{+}e^{-}
pair in the transverse momentum plane. For a given electron momentum
{\bf k}_{3}
, the positron momentum
{\bf k}_{4}={\bf K}-{\bf k}_{3}
has “freedom” since
|\varkappa_{1}-\varkappa_{2}|\leq{\bf K}\leq\varkappa_{1}+\varkappa_{2}
.

With help of these observations, under the condition Eq. (
19
), the lepton current Eq. (
18
) simplifies, because
\theta_{4}\approx\pi-\theta_{3}
leads to
c_{4}=s_{3}
and
s_{4}=c_{3}
:

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\sin\theta_{3}\cos(\xi\bar{\phi}_{-}),

\displaystyle L_{z}

\displaystyle=-2i\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\sin\theta_{3}\sin(\xi\bar{\phi}_{-}),

(20)

\displaystyle L_{x}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}(c_{3}^{2}e^{i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}}+s_{3}^{2}e^{-i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}}),

\displaystyle L_{y}

\displaystyle=2i2\xi\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}(c_{3}^{2}e^{i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}}-s_{3}^{2}e^{-i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}}).

Note that, similar to the hadron current, the longitudinal components depend on
{\bar{\phi}}_{-}
, while the transverse components depend on
{\bar{\phi}}_{+}
.

V
Twisted scattering amplitude

As mentioned in the previous section, due to the different azimuthal dependence of the current components, it is useful to separate the amplitudes into “longitudinal” and “transverse” parts. The first is formed by time and
z
components of the currents, while the second is formed by
x
and
y
components.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\parallel}+\mathcal{M}_{\perp},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=J_{0}L_{0}-J_{z}L_{z},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=-J_{x}L_{x}-J_{y}L_{y}.

Twisted amplitudes, in their turn, can also be represented as the sum of “longitudinal” and “transverse” parts, since the PW amplitudes
{\mathcal{M}}_{a,b}
enter the equation for the twisted amplitude
\mathcal{J}
linearly:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i(m_{1}-m_{2})\phi_{K}}}{\sin(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\mathcal{M}_{a}e^{i(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2})}+\mathcal{M}_{b}e^{-i(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2})}\Big).

(22)

This equation differs from Eq. (
II
), which was written for scalars, by replacing OAM
l_{i}
with half-integer total angular momentum
m_{i}
, since we are now dealing with fermions.
The double-vortex cross section is proportional to the following sum:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}|^{2}=|\mathcal{J}_{\parallel}+\mathcal{J}_{\perp}|^{2}=|\mathcal{J}_{\parallel}|^{2}+2\mathrm{Re}\{\mathcal{J}_{\parallel}\mathcal{J}_{\perp}^{*}\}+|\mathcal{J}_{\perp}|^{2}.

(23)

Every twisted amplitude will be calculated by substituting the corresponding PW amplitude into Eq. (
V
), as described in Section
II
and Appendix
B
.

V.1
RR/LL case

We begin by analyzing the case when the helicities of the proton and antiproton are equal
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}\equiv\lambda
.
Details of the calculation are given in Appendix
D.1
.
In this combination of helicities, the main contribution to the cross section comes from the “longitudinal” components of currents:

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}|{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}|^{2}

\displaystyle\propto 32E_{3}E_{4}\sin^{2}\theta_{3}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda})^{2}|G_{M}|^{2}

\displaystyle\times|W_{-}-\frac{1-G_{E}/G_{M}}{2M(1-\tau)}V_{+}P_{z}|^{2},

(24)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}}_{m_{2}}

\displaystyle=\cos(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2}).

(25)

A common factor
(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\sin(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})s})^{2}
is omitted here.
It is not essential for the discussion, since in twisted scattering we are interested in the oscillations of the cross section, encoded here by
\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}
, rather than the absolute value of the cross section.

The Eq. (
V.1
) has the same scattering angle dependence
\sin^{2}\theta_{3}
as the cross section for head-on annihilation of massive fermions with the same helicities into a pair of massless fermions.
We remind, that we use Eq. (
19
) to obtain Eq.
V.1
and it is not valid at near
0
(or
\pi
) scattering angles.

In the paraxial limit, the interference between “longitudinal and “transverse” twisted amplitudes is suppressed,
{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}^{*}\ll|{\mathcal{J}}_{||}|^{2}
. However, this interference contains a term
\propto\sin(\phi_{K}-\phi_{3})
, which generates asymmetry and depends on the FFs phase.
It is sum of two parts:

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}\mathrm{Re}\{{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{P,\perp}^{*}\}\propto

\displaystyle-16E_{3}E_{4}\sin 2\theta_{3}|G_{M}|^{2}\sin(\phi_{k}-\phi_{3})

\displaystyle\times\varkappa_{1}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda+1}_{m_{2}-\lambda}-\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda-1}_{m_{2}-\lambda})

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Im}\{G_{E}/G_{M}\}\frac{(|\vec{k}_{1}|+|\vec{k}_{2}|)}{1-\tau},

(26)

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}\mathrm{Re}\{{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma,\perp}^{*}\}\propto

\displaystyle-16E_{3}E_{4}\sin(2\theta_{3})|G_{M}|^{2}\sin(\phi_{k}-\phi_{3})

\displaystyle\times\lambda P_{z}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{|\vec{k}_{1}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}+\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{|\vec{k}_{2}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Im}\{G_{E}/G_{M}\}\frac{(|\vec{k}_{1}|+|\vec{k}_{2}|)}{1-\tau},

(27)

where
{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma,\perp}
and
{\mathcal{J}}_{P,\perp}
are “transverse” twisted amplitudes, raised respectively from the
\gamma^{\mu}
and
P^{\mu}
terms of the hadron current in Eq. (
11
).
The sum of Eqs. (
V.1
) and (
V.1
) simplifies when
K_{z}=0
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}\mathrm{Re}\{{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}^{*}\}\propto

\displaystyle 16E_{3}E_{4}\sin(2\theta_{3})|G_{M}|^{2}\sin(\phi_{K}-\phi_{3})

\displaystyle\times 2\lambda|\mathbf{K}|\Big(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\Big)^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Im}\{G_{E}/G_{M}\}\frac{(|\vec{k}_{1}|+|\vec{k}_{2}|)}{1-\tau}.

Even though this expression changes sign with helicity,
\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}
also differs, resulting in the asymmetry persisting after summation over helicity
\lambda
in the unpolarized cross section.

The remained term
|\mathcal{J}_{\perp}|^{2}
is even more suppressed in the paraxial limit than the interference
{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}^{*}
. Therefore, we omit it.

V.2
RL/LR case

When the proton and antiproton helicities are opposite,
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{2}\equiv\lambda
, the situation reverses. In the paraxial limit, the “longitudinal” components of the hadron current are suppressed by the small cone opening angle in comparison with “transverse” components, Eq. (
IV.1
). However, the entire
P^{\mu}
part of the current Eq. (
11
) has additional suppression by
V_{-}
(see
\bar{v}_{\lambda}u_{\lambda}
in Eq. (
IV.1
)).
In the considered kinematics, when
E_{1}\approx E_{2}
, it holds that
V_{-}\ll V_{+},W_{\pm}
.
Consequently, the contribution of the part containing the relative FF phase is negligible, and the twisted cross section is primarily defined by “transverse”
\gamma_{\mu}G_{M}
term of the current Eq. (
11
). For detailed calculations, refer to Appendix
D.2
.

The squared modulus of the twisted amplitude after summation over the polarizations of
e^{+}e^{-}
pair is

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}|{\mathcal{J}}_{(RL/LR)}|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\xi}|{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma,\perp}|^{2}\approx\left(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{\sin(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})s}\right)^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle\times 32|G_{M}|^{2}W_{+}^{2}E_{3}E_{4}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda})^{2}(1+\cos^{2}\theta_{3}).

Note that
1+\cos^{2}\theta_{3}
dependency of the
RL/LR
cross section in the paraxial approximation is similar to the behaviour of the annihilation cross section in head-on PW collision for fermions with opposite helicities.

When we are talking about twisted states of particles with spin, it is ambiguous when we say “unpolarized”.
For a plane-wave state, with its polarization vector independent of spatial coordinates, the unpolarized state is an equal mixture of particles in two orthogonal polarization states, for examples, with
\lambda=+1/2
and
\lambda=-1/2

When we consider a twisted particle with spin
\lambda=\pm 1/2

m

m-\lambda

The answer will depend on the state preparation in specific experimental setup.
If an experimental device can select states with a single
m

|m,\lambda=+1/2\rangle
and
|m,\lambda=-1/2\rangle

If one creates twisted states by imposing a given OAM

|m_{+}=l+1/2,\lambda=+1/2\rangle
and
|m_{-}=l-1/2,\lambda=-1/2\rangle
.
However, this state will evolve during beam propagation and may become very different in the focal spot due to the spin-orbital interaction.

Therefore, when calculating processes with unpolarized twisted fermion in realistic settings, one must specify according to which definition the twisted beam is unpolarized.
Fig.

It is natural to expect, that the first produced vortex states of the proton will be low-energetic.

\displaystyle E

VII
Conclusion

Vortex states open new opportunities for studying particles, unavailable in plane wave scattering.
One of the key features of double-vortex scattering is that the final particles have additional degrees of freedom in the phase space, leading to a non-trivial distribution in the total transverse momentum plane.
The pattern of this distribution is defined by the interference between plane wave amplitudes with different kinematics of incoming waves.
This interference leads, in particular, to the possibility to study Coulomb phase
Ivanov 2012
;
Ivanov et al. 2016a
.

This work proposes another application of this interference: if the scattering amplitude is proportional to some vector, different for two interfering amplitudes, one can detect the relative phase of the two electromagnetic FFs of the proton even in unpolarized scattering.
By studying the cross section distribution of the final
e^{+}e^{-}
pair in vortex
p\bar{p}
annihilation, it was found that its asymmetry is proportional to the sine of the relative phase.
Moreover, such twisted annihilation has a peculiar feature that,
in a single experiment with monochromatic proton and antiproton, one can probe the relative form factor phase at different Mandelstam
s
.

Appendix A
Description of twisted fermion

We construct a twisted fermion state in similar fashion as in
Serbo et al. 2015
;
Ivanov et al. 2016b
.
First, we consider a plane wave fermion with mass
M
and the four-momentum

k^{\mu}=(E,\,{\bf k},\,k_{z})
,
where
{\bf k}=|{\bf k}|(\cos\phi_{k},\,\sin\phi_{k})
,
|{\bf k}|=|\vec{k}|\sin\theta
,
k_{z}=|\vec{k}|\cos\theta
,
and helicity
\lambda=\pm 1/2
(the eigenvalue of the operator of the spin component along the electron momentum direction).
The plane-wave of such fermion is described by

\Psi_{k\lambda}(r)={N_{\rm PW}\over\sqrt{2E}}\,u_{k\lambda}\,e^{-i\vec{k}\vec{r}}\,.

(38)

The bispinors
u_{k\lambda}
in above equation are

\begin{split}u_{k\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{E^{+}}\,w^{(\lambda)}\\
2\lambda\sqrt{E^{-}}\,w^{(\lambda)}\end{pmatrix},&\,v_{k\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{E^{-}}\,w^{(-\lambda)}\\
2\lambda\sqrt{E^{+}}\,w^{(-\lambda)}\end{pmatrix},\\
w^{(+1/2)}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{i}\,e^{-i\phi_{i}/2}\\
s_{i}\,e^{i\phi_{i}/2}\end{pmatrix},&\,w^{(-1/2)}=\begin{pmatrix}-s_{i}\,e^{-i\phi_{i}/2}\\
c_{i}\,e^{i\phi_{i}/2}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(39)

where
E^{\pm}=E\pm M
,
c_{i}\equiv\cos(\theta_{i}/2)
, and
s_{i}\equiv\sin(\theta_{i}/2)
.
The bispinors are normalized as

\bar{u}_{k\lambda_{1}}u_{k\lambda_{2}}=2m\,\delta_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
.

We use this basis of plane-wave solutions to construct the Bessel vortex state:

\Psi_{\varkappa mk_{z}\lambda}(r)={N_{\rm Bes}\over\sqrt{2E}}\,\int\!\!\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,a_{\varkappa m}({\bf k})\,u_{k\lambda}\,e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}},

(40)

where the Fourier amplitude is

a_{\varkappa m}({\bf k})=(-i)^{m}\,e^{im\phi_{k}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\varkappa}}\,\delta(|{\bf k}|-\varkappa)\,.

(41)

This state possesses the definite longitudinal momentum
k_{z}
, the definite conical transverse momentum
|{\bf k}|\equiv\varkappa
, the well-defined helicity
\lambda
as well as the definite value of the total angular momentum
j_{z}=m
, which must be half-integer.

Since
m\pm\lambda
is an integer number, all components of the bispinor
a_{\varkappa,m}
depend on
\phi_{k}
as
\exp[i(m\pm\lambda)\phi_{k}]
and are well defined.

Note that, although the Bessel vortex fermion possesses a well-defined helicity, it cannot be interpreted as a state with a well-defined spin projection on any fixed axis.
The helicity operator involves the direction of
{\bf k}
and it does not correspond to any component of the spin operator.

The OAM and spin are not separately conserved due to the intrinsic spin-orbital interaction within the vortex electron.
However, in the paraxial approximation, when
\theta_{k}\ll 1
, this spin-orbital coupling is suppressed, and one deals with two approximately conserved quantum numbers,
s_{z}\approx\lambda
and
l=m-\lambda
.

Appendix B
Double-twisted scattering

Here we give details of the amplitude and cross section for scattering of two twisted states.
The final particles in our
2\to 2
annihilation process are plane waves with momenta
k_{3,4}
.
The initial proton and antiproton are described as pure Bessel states with the average longitudinal momenta
k_{1z}>0
for the proton and
k_{2z}<0
for antiproton.
Since the antiproton propagates in the
-z
direction, its
z
-projection of AM is
-m_{2}
.

The
S
-matrix element of the double-Bessel scattering can be written as

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{Bes}^{2}}{N_{PW}^{2}}\int\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}_{1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{d^{2}{\bf k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}a_{\varkappa_{1},m_{1}}({\bf k}_{1})a_{\varkappa_{2},-m_{2}}({\bf k}_{2})S_{PW}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i(2\pi)^{4}\delta(E)\delta(k_{z})}{\sqrt{16E_{1}E_{2}E_{3}E_{4}}}N^{2}_{Bes}N^{2}_{PW}\frac{(-i)^{m_{1}-m_{2}}}{(2\pi)^{3}\sqrt{\varkappa_{1}\varkappa_{2}}}{\mathcal{J}},

(42)

where
\delta(E)=\delta(E_{1}+E_{2}-E_{3}-E_{4})
,
\delta(k_{z})=\delta(k_{1z}+k_{2z}-k_{3z}-k_{4z})
and
\mathcal{J}
is the Bessel scattering amplitude, which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=

\displaystyle\int d^{2}{\bf k}_{1}d^{2}{\bf k}_{2}e^{i(m_{1}\phi_{1}-m_{2}\phi_{2})}\delta(|{\bf k}_{1}|-\varkappa_{1})\delta(|{\bf k}_{2}|-\varkappa_{2})

\displaystyle\times\delta^{(2)}({\bf k}_{1}+{\bf k}_{2}-{\bf K})\mathcal{M}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})

(43)

To obtain the differential event rate
d\nu
one has to take square of Eq. (
B
) and regularise the squares of the delta functions as
[\delta(E)\delta(k_{z})]^{2}=\delta(E)\delta(k_{z})Tdz/(2\pi)^{2}
. Dividing
d\nu
by the appropriately defined flux and using
dk_{3z}dk_{4z}
to eliminate
\delta(E)\delta(k_{z})
, one obtains the differential cross section (see
Ivanov 2022
and references therein for details).

We represent the cross section simply as

d\sigma\propto|\mathcal{J}|^{2}d^{2}{\bf k}_{3}d^{2}{\bf k}_{4}.

(44)

The dynamics of the scattering process is determined by the vortex amplitude
\mathcal{J}
defined in (
B
). It contains four
integrations and four delta functions, so that the integral can be done exactly for any non-singular
\mathcal{M}
. It is non-zero only when the total transverse momentum
{\bf K}={\bf k}_{1}+{\bf k}_{2}
lies within the annular region defined by the initial conical momenta:

|\varkappa_{1}-\varkappa_{2}|\leq|{\bf K}|<\varkappa_{1}+\varkappa_{2}

(45)

For any value of
{\bf K}
inside this region, the integral comes only from two points in the entire
({\bf k}_{1};{\bf k}_{2})
space, at which the momenta
{\bf k}_{1}=\varkappa_{1}(\cos\phi_{1};\sin\phi_{1})
and
{\bf k}_{2}=\varkappa_{2}(\cos\phi_{2};\sin\phi_{2})
sum up to
{\bf K}
, see Fig.
2
.
This happens at the following azimuthal angles:

configuration a:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\to\phi_{K}+\delta_{1}\quad

\displaystyle\phi_{2}\to\phi_{K}-\delta_{2}

(46)

configuration b:

\displaystyle\phi_{1}\to\phi_{K}-\delta_{1}\quad

\displaystyle\phi_{2}\to\phi_{K}+\delta_{2}

(47)

where
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are the internal angles of the triangle with the sides
\varkappa_{1}
,
\varkappa_{2}
, and
{\bf K}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{1}=

\displaystyle\arccos\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}^{2}-\varkappa_{2}^{2}+{\bf K}^{2}}{2\varkappa_{1}|{\bf K}|}\Big),

(48)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}=

\displaystyle\arccos\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{2}^{2}-\varkappa_{1}^{2}+{\bf K}^{2}}{2\varkappa_{2}|{\bf K}|}\Big).

(49)

With these definitions, the result for the vortex amplitude
\mathcal{J}
can be compactly presented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{i(m_{1}-m_{2})\phi_{K}}}{\sin(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{M}_{a}e^{i(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2})}+\mathcal{M}_{b}e^{-i(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2})}\Big).

(50)

Notice that the plane-wave amplitudes
\mathcal{M}_{a}
and
\mathcal{M}_{b}
entering this expression are calculated for the two distinct initial momentum configurations, but the same final momenta
k_{3}
and
k_{4}
. Interference of
{\mathcal{M}}_{a}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{b}
is a crucial change with respect to the PW scattering.

Appendix C
Current components

The hadron current components associated with
\gamma_{\mu}
term in Eq. (
11
) are:

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda_{2}}\gamma_{0}u_{\lambda_{1}}=

\displaystyle-\Big(\sqrt{E^{+}_{1}E_{2}^{-}}-4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\sqrt{E^{-}_{1}E^{+}_{2}}\Big)w_{2}^{(-\lambda_{2})\dagger}w_{1}^{(\lambda_{1})},

(51)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda_{2}}\gamma_{i}u_{\lambda_{1}}=

\displaystyle\Big(2\lambda_{2}\sqrt{E^{+}_{1}E^{+}_{2}}-2\lambda_{1}\sqrt{E^{-}_{1}E^{-}_{2}}\Big)w_{2}^{(-\lambda_{2})\dagger}\sigma_{i}w_{1}^{(\lambda_{1})}.

(52)

The second part, proportional to the momenta
P^{\mu}
, is

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda_{2}}u_{\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle=-\Big(\sqrt{E^{+}_{1}E^{-}_{2}}+4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\sqrt{E^{-}_{1}E^{+}_{2}}\Big)w_{2}^{(-\lambda_{2})\dagger}w_{1}^{(\lambda_{1})},

(53)

where
E_{i}^{\pm}=E_{i}\pm M
.

Further, the following notation will be used:

\displaystyle\sqrt{E^{+}_{1}E_{2}^{-}}\pm\sqrt{E^{-}_{1}E^{+}_{2}}=

\displaystyle V_{\pm},

(54)

\displaystyle\sqrt{E^{+}_{1}E^{+}_{2}}\pm\sqrt{E^{-}_{1}E^{-}_{2}}=

\displaystyle W_{\pm}.

(55)

The combinations of
w
spinors and Pauli matrices can be calculated using Eq. (
39
). We separate results into two cases: when helicities of
p
and
\bar{p}
are the same
(RR/LL)
, and when helicities are opposite (
RL/LR
).

\displaystyle(RR/LL)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda:

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(-\lambda)\dagger}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=(2\lambda)(s_{1}c_{2}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}-c_{1}s_{2}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}),

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(-\lambda)\dagger}\sigma_{x}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=-(s_{1}s_{2}e^{i\lambda\phi_{+}}-c_{1}c_{2}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{+}}),

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(-\lambda)\dagger}\sigma_{y}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=i(2\lambda)(s_{1}s_{2}e^{i\lambda\phi_{+}}+c_{1}c_{2}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{+}}),

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(-\lambda)\dagger}\sigma_{z}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=-(s_{1}c_{2}e^{\lambda i\phi_{-}}+c_{1}s_{2}e^{-\lambda i\phi_{-}}),

\displaystyle(RL/LR)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{2}=\lambda:

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(\lambda)\dagger}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=s_{1}s_{2}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}+c_{1}c_{2}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}},

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(\lambda)\dagger}\sigma_{x}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=(2\lambda)(s_{1}c_{2}e^{\lambda i\phi_{+}}+c_{1}s_{2}e^{-\lambda i\phi_{+}}),

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(\lambda)\dagger}\sigma_{y}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=-i(s_{1}c_{2}e^{\lambda i\phi_{+}}-c_{1}s_{2}e^{-\lambda i\phi_{+}}),

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(\lambda)\dagger}\sigma_{z}w_{1}^{(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=-2\lambda(s_{1}s_{2}e^{\lambda i\phi_{-}}-c_{1}c_{2}e^{-\lambda i\phi_{-}}),

where
\phi_{\pm}=\phi_{1}\pm\phi_{2}
.
Notice that the helicity of an anti-particle is opposite to the helicity of spinor
w
it is described by.
One can observe a pattern that time and
z
components are functions of
\phi_{-}
, while
x
and
y
components are functions of
\phi_{+}
. This observation motivates to separate amplitude onto “longitudinal” and “transverse” parts.

Analytical calculations simplifies in the paraxial approximation:
when cone angle of proton
\theta_{1}
is close to
0
and antiproton
\theta_{2}
is close to
\pi
.
Therefore

\displaystyle s_{1}s_{2}\to

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}=\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|},

\displaystyle c_{1}c_{2}\to

\displaystyle\frac{\pi-\theta_{2}}{2}=\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|},

\displaystyle s_{1}c_{2}\to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\theta_{1}(\pi-\theta_{2})=\frac{\varkappa_{1}\varkappa_{2}}{4|\vec{k}_{1}||\vec{k}_{2}|},

\displaystyle c_{1}s_{2}\to

\displaystyle 1.

Applying this approximation, for the
(RR/LL)
case one gets:

\displaystyle(RR/LL)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{0}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle 2\lambda V_{-}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}},

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{z}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle-2\lambda W_{-}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}},

(56)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{x}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle 2\lambda W_{-}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{+}}-\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{+}}\Big),

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{y}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle iW_{-}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{+}}+\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{+}}\Big),

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle 2\lambda V_{+}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}.

Note that the “longitudinal” and
\bar{v}u
components dominate in the paraxial limit, while the “transverse” components are suppressed by small cone angles, since
\varkappa_{i}/2|\vec{k}_{i}|\ll 1
.

In the case of opposite helicities the current components become

\displaystyle(RL/LR)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{0}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle-V_{+}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}\Big),

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{z}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle W_{+}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}-\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}\Big),

(57)

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{x}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle-W_{+}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{+}},

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}\gamma^{y}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle-2\lambda iW_{+}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{+}},

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\lambda}u_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle-V_{-}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}\Big).

Here the “longitudinal” and
\bar{v}u
components are suppressed relatively to the “transverse” components.
Moreover, the
\bar{v}_{\lambda}u_{\lambda}
has an additional suppression from the
V_{-}\rightarrow 0
if energies of proton and antiproton are close.
Therefore, contribution of the
P^{\mu}
term of the hadron current (
11
) is negligible for
RL/LR
helicities.

Appendix D
Plane wave and vortex scattering amplitudes

Since the components of currents are divided into two categories based on the azimuthal dependence, the scattering amplitudes follow the same and divided into the “longitudinal” and “transverse” parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=J_{0}L_{0}-J_{z}L_{z},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=-J_{x}L_{x}-J_{y}L_{y}.

Then, we need to calculate these plane wave scattering amplitudes for all possible combinations of particles helicities.

D.1
RR/LL case

Using Eqs. (
C
) and (
IV.2
), we obtain the “longitudinal” PW scattering amplitude

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s}G_{M}(2\lambda e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}})(-2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}})\sin\theta_{3}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\Big[(V_{-}+YP_{0})\cos(\xi\bar{\phi}_{-})+(W_{-}-YP_{z})i\sin(\xi\bar{\phi}_{-})\Big],

(59)

where

\displaystyle Y=

\displaystyle\frac{1-G_{E}/G_{M}}{2M(1-\tau)}V_{+}.

(60)

The
\cos(\xi\bar{\phi}_{-})
term in Eq. (
D.1
) is suppressed in studied kinematics since
(V_{-}+YP_{0})\ll(W_{-}-YP_{z})
. In addition, at large scattering angles, when
|{\bf K}|\ll\varkappa_{3,4}
, azimithal angle between electron and positron is
\bar{\phi}_{-}\approx\pi
.

The “transverse” amplitude is the sum of contributions from
\gamma^{\mu}
and
P^{\mu}
part of the hadron current Eq. (
11
). The amplitude rising from the
\gamma
term is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{a,b}^{(\gamma\perp)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s}\Big)G_{M}W_{-}(2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}})

\displaystyle\times\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{\pm i\lambda\delta_{-}}\bar{L}_{1}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{\mp i\lambda\delta_{-}}\bar{L}_{2}\Big),

(61)

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{1}

\displaystyle=e^{i\lambda(2\phi_{K}-\bar{\phi}_{+})}(-2\lambda+2\xi\cos\theta_{3}),

(62)

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\lambda(2\phi_{K}-\bar{\phi}_{+})}(2\lambda+2\xi\cos\theta_{3}),

(63)

where a shorthand
\delta_{-}\equiv\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
was used.

The contribution to the “transverse” amplitude from
P
part is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{(P,\perp)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s}\Big)YG_{M}(2\lambda e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}})(2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}})

\displaystyle\times\Big({\bf P}_{x}(c_{3}^{2}e^{i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}}+s_{3}^{2}e^{-i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}})

\displaystyle-2i\xi{\bf P}_{y}(c_{3}^{2}e^{i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}}-s_{3}^{2}e^{-i\xi\bar{\phi}_{+}})\Big).

(64)

This equation will be simplified later after substitution into Eq. (
B
), since the vector
P
is different for the
a
and
b
PW configurations contributing to the twisted amplitude. But first, we calculate the leading “longitudinal” twisted amplitude
{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}
.

Substitution of Eq. (
D.1
) into Eq. (
B
) turns the exponents of azimuthal angles
e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}
to cosines:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\parallel}\approx

\displaystyle D(16i\lambda\xi\varkappa_{3}G_{M})(W_{-}-YP_{z})\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda},

(65)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i(m_{1}-m_{2})\phi_{K}}}{\sin(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s},

(66)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}}_{m_{2}}

\displaystyle=\cos(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2}).

(67)

After summation over polarizations of the
e^{+}e^{-}
pair, the squared modulus of this twisted amplitude is

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}|{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}|^{2}=|D|^{2}32\varkappa_{3}^{2}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda})^{2}|G_{M}|^{2}|W_{-}-YP_{z}|^{2}.

(68)

In Eq. (
65
) we used
\varkappa_{3}\approx\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\sin\theta_{3}
, since
E_{3}\approx E_{4}
.

|{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}|^{2}
is the leading contribution to the cross section.

For calculation of the “transverse” twisted amplitude, it is useful to express
P^{\mu}
as

P^{\mu}=K^{\mu}-2k_{1}^{\mu}.

(69)

Then, substituting Eq. (
D.1
) into Eq. (
B
), one obtains the transverse twisted amplitude, associated with
P
part of current Eq. (
11
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{(P,\perp)}

\displaystyle=D4\lambda Y|G_{M}|\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\Big({\bf K}(L_{1}+L_{2})\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}

\displaystyle-2\varkappa_{1}\Big[L_{1}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda+1}_{m_{2}-\lambda}+L_{2}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda-1}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\Big]\Big),

(70)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}=

\displaystyle(1-2\xi\cos\theta_{3})e^{i(\phi_{K}-\bar{\phi}_{+}/2)},

(71)

\displaystyle L_{2}=

\displaystyle(1+2\xi\cos\theta_{3})e^{-i(\phi_{K}-\bar{\phi}_{+}/2)}.

(72)

Repeating the same procedure for Eq. (
D.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma\perp}=

\displaystyle DG_{M}(2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}})W_{-}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{|p_{1}|}\bar{L}_{1}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}+\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{|p_{2}|}\bar{L}_{2}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}\Big)

(73)

The next step is to calculate the interference between the “longitudinal” Eq. (
65
) and “transverse” Eqs. (
D.1
),(
D.1
) twisted amplitudes. It is the biggest contribution after the leading one Eq. (
68
).
Performing summation over electron-positron polarizations and keeping only terms
\propto\sin(\phi_{K}-\phi_{3})
, two interference contributions are

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}\mathrm{Re}({\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{P,\perp}^{*})\approx

\displaystyle|D|^{2}16E_{3}E_{4}\sin 2\theta_{3}|G_{M}|^{2}W_{-}\mathrm{Im}(Y)

\displaystyle\times\varkappa_{1}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda+1}_{m_{2}-\lambda}-\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda-1}_{m_{2}-\lambda})

\displaystyle\times\sin(\phi_{K}-\phi_{3}),

(74)

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi}\mathrm{Re}({\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma,\perp}^{*})\approx

\displaystyle|D|^{2}16E_{3}E_{4}\sin(2\theta_{3})|G_{M}|^{2}W_{-}\mathrm{Im}(Y)

\displaystyle\times\lambda P_{z}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{|\vec{k}_{1}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}+\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{|\vec{k}_{2}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\sin(\phi_{K}-\phi_{3}).

(75)

These two terms could be combined together and simplified if
K_{z}=0
, using

\displaystyle 2\lambda\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}=

\displaystyle 2\lambda(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{C}^{0}_{2\lambda}-\mathfrak{S}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{S}^{0}_{2\lambda})

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\lambda\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{C}_{1}^{0}-\mathfrak{S}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{S}_{1}^{0},

where
\mathfrak{S}_{m_{2}}^{m_{1}}\equiv\sin(m_{1}\delta_{1}+m_{2}\delta_{2})
and
\mathfrak{C}^{0}_{2\lambda}=\cos 2\lambda\delta_{2}
.
Collecting coefficients in front of
\varkappa_{1,2}

\displaystyle\varkappa_{1}:\quad

\displaystyle-2\lambda\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}+\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}+\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda+1}_{m_{2}-\lambda}-\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda-1}_{m_{2}-\lambda}

\displaystyle=-2\lambda\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{C}^{1}_{0}-\mathfrak{S}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{S}^{1}_{0},

(77)

\displaystyle\varkappa_{2}:\quad

\displaystyle-2\lambda\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}=-2\lambda\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{C}^{0}_{1}+\mathfrak{S}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\mathfrak{S}^{0}_{1},

(78)

and noting from geometrical meaning (see Fig.
2
) that

\displaystyle\varkappa_{1}\cos\delta_{1}+\varkappa_{2}\cos\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=|{\bf K}|,

(79)

\displaystyle\varkappa_{1}\sin\delta_{1}-\varkappa_{2}\sin\delta_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

(80)

the interference term is simplified to

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\lambda}\mathrm{Re}({\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}^{*})\approx

\displaystyle-|D|^{2}16E_{3}E_{4}\sin(2\theta_{3})W_{-}\mathrm{Im}(Y)

\displaystyle\times\sin(\phi_{K}-\phi_{3})\sum_{\lambda}2\lambda{\bf K}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda})^{2}.

The square of the “transverse” twisted amplitude
|\mathcal{J}_{\perp}|^{2}
is suppressed even more than the interference term
{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}^{*}
. Therefore, in paraxial limit, it could be neglected.

D.2
RL/LR case

In the case of opposite helicities of proton and antiproton, “longitudinal” PW amplitudes
{\mathcal{M}}_{\parallel}
are suppressed by small opening cone angles:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{\gamma,\parallel}=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s}\right)G_{M}W_{+}4i\xi\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\sin\theta_{3}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}-\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}\Big),

(81)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{P,\parallel}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s}\right)Y_{-}P_{z}4i\xi\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\sin\theta_{3}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{2|\vec{k}_{1}|}e^{i\lambda\phi_{-}}+\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{2|\vec{k}_{2}|}e^{-i\lambda\phi_{-}}\Big),

(82)

where
Y_{-}=\frac{1-G_{E}/G_{M}}{2M(1-\tau)}V_{-}
.
Substituting these amplitudes into Eq. (
B
), we obtain the “longitudinal” twisted amplitude

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}=

\displaystyle 4i\xi DG_{M}\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}\sin\theta_{3}\Big((-W_{+}\pm Y_{-}P_{z})\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{|\vec{k}_{1}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}+\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}

\displaystyle+(W_{+}\pm Y_{-}P_{z})\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{|\vec{k}_{2}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}\Big)

(83)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 4i\xi DG_{M}W_{+}\varkappa_{3}\Big(\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{|\vec{k}_{2}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}-\lambda}-\frac{\varkappa_{1}}{|\vec{k}_{1}|}\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}+\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}\Big),

where
Y_{-}P_{z}\ll W_{+}
was used to get the last line.

The dominant part of the “transverse” PW amplitude is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{\gamma,\perp}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\pi\alpha}{s}\right)(-W_{+}G_{M})2\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}e^{-i\lambda(\phi_{+}-\bar{\phi}_{+})}

\displaystyle\times(1+2\lambda 2\xi\cos\theta_{3}).

(84)

Similar to
{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}
, the part of the amplitude, rising from
P
term in the hadron current Eq. (
11
), is suppressed by to
Y_{-}
. Therefore,
{\mathcal{M}}_{P,\perp}\ll{\mathcal{M}}_{\gamma,\perp}
and whole twisted amplitude is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}\approx{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma,\perp}=

\displaystyle-DG_{M}W_{+}4\sqrt{E_{3}E_{4}}e^{-i\lambda(2\phi_{K}-\bar{\phi}_{+})}

\displaystyle\times\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda}(1+2\lambda 2\xi\cos\theta_{3}).

(85)

Taking modulus square and summing over polarization of
e^{+}e^{-}
pair one gets

\sum_{\xi}|{\mathcal{J}}_{\gamma,\perp}|^{2}=32|DG_{M}|^{2}W_{+}^{2}E_{3}E_{4}(\mathfrak{C}^{m_{1}-\lambda}_{m_{2}+\lambda})^{2}(1+\cos^{2}\theta_{3}).

(86)

The key observation here is that in the case of
RR/LL
helicities the interference term
{\mathcal{J}}_{\parallel}{\mathcal{J}}_{\perp}^{*}
contains only
|G_{M}|^{2}
, since
Y_{-}
part with the electric form factor
G_{E}
is suppressed. It is in contrast with
RR/LL
case, where the interference term contains both
G_{M}
and
G_{E}
, and is sensitive to their relative phase.
