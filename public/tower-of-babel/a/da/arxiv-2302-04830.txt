Abstract
. Classical hypergeometric functions are well-known to play an important role in arithmetic algebraic geometry. These functions offer solutions to ordinary differential equations, and special cases of such solutions are periods of Picard-Fuchs varieties of Calabi-Yau type. Gauss’ F 1 2 {}_{2}F_{1} includes the celebrated case of elliptic curves through the theory of elliptic functions. In the 80s, Greene defined finite field hypergeometric functions that can be used to enumerate the number of finite field points on such varieties. We extend some of these results to count finite field “matrix points.” For example, for every n ≥ 1 , n\geq 1, we consider the matrix elliptic curves B 2 = A ⁡ ( A − I n ) ​ ( A − a ​ I n ) , B^{2}=A(A-I_{n})(A-aI_{n}), where ( A , B ) (A,B) are commuting n × n n\times n matrices over a finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} and a ≠ 0 , 1 a\neq 0,1 is fixed. Our formulas are assembled from Greene’s hypergeometric functions and q q -multinomial coefficients. We use these formulas to prove Sato-Tate distributions for the error terms for matrix point counts for these curves and some families of K ​ 3 K3 surfaces.

Introduction and Statement of Results

Classical hypergeometric functions are well known to give periods of elliptic curves. To be precise, if
n
is a nonnegative integer, then define

(\gamma)_{n}
by

(\gamma)_{n}:=\begin{cases}1\ \ \ \ \ &{\text{\rm if}}\ n=0,\\
\gamma(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)\cdots(\gamma+n-1)\ \ \ \ \ &{\text{\rm if}}\ n\geq 1.\end{cases}

The
classical hypergeometric function
in parameters

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{h},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
is defined by

{}_{h}F^{\text{\rm cl}}_{j}\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\dots&\alpha_{h}\\
\ &\beta_{1}&\dots&\beta_{j}\end{matrix}\ \vrule\ x\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha_{1})_{n}(\alpha_{2})_{n}(\alpha_{3})_{n}\cdots(\alpha_{h})_{n}}{(\beta_{1})_{n}(\beta_{2})_{n}\cdots(\beta_{j})_{n}}\cdot\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Perhaps the most famous example illustrating the role of these functions in geometry involves the Legendre elliptic curves

(1.1)

E_{\text{\rm L}}(a):\ \ y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-a),\ \ \ \ a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}.

The theory of elliptic integrals shows, for
0<a<1,
that
the function

{}_{2}F_{1}^{\text{\rm cl}}(x):=\,_{2}F_{1}^{\text{\rm cl}}\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
\ &\ 1\ \end{matrix}\ \vrule\ x\right)
(for example, see
page 184 of
[
11
]
) gives the real period
\Omega_{\textrm{L}}(a)
of
E_{\text{\rm L}}(a)
by the formula

(1.2)

\Omega_{\textrm{L}}(a)=\pi\cdot\ _{2}F_{1}^{\text{\rm cl}}(a).

There is another kind of hypergeometric function,
the finite field hypergeometric function, that gives further information about these elliptic curves and higher dimensional varieties. These functions count points over finite fields.
To make this precise, we first recall their definition
which is due to Greene
[
8
]
.
If
q
is a prime power and
A
and
B
are two Dirichlet characters on
\mathbb{F}_{q}

(extended so that
A(0)=B(0)=0
),
then let
\left(\begin{matrix}A\\
B\end{matrix}\right)

be the normalized Jacobi sum

\left(\begin{matrix}A\\
B\end{matrix}\right):=\frac{B(-1)}{q}J(A,\overline{B})=\frac{B(-1)}{q}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}A(x)\overline{B}(1-x).

Here
\overline{B}

is the complex conjugate of
B
.
If
A_{0},\dots,A_{n},
and

B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
are characters on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
then the

hypergeometric function

in these parameters is defined by

{}_{n+1}F^{\text{\rm ff}}_{n}\left(\begin{matrix}A_{0}&A_{1}&\dots&A_{n}\\
\ &B_{1}&\dots&B_{n}\end{matrix}\ \vrule\ x\right)_{q}:=\frac{q}{q-1}\sum_{\chi}\left(\begin{matrix}A_{0}\chi\\
\chi\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}A_{1}\chi\\
B_{1}\chi\end{matrix}\right)\cdot\cdot\cdot\left(\begin{matrix}A_{n}\chi\\
B_{n}\chi\end{matrix}\right)\chi(x).

Here
\sum_{\chi}
denotes
the sum over all characters
\chi
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

It has been observed by many authors (see

]
,

More generally, we let

Motivated by (

\ &b+c+\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\ \vrule\ x\right)^{2}.}

and where the second equality holds whenever

Furthermore, this identity can be interpreted in terms of

In this note we show that the hypergeometric identities (
1.4
) and (

), combined with the combinatorial input from partitions and

-multinomial coefficients,
count suitable “matrix points” on these curves and surfaces. To make this precise, we first introduce some notation. If
n,m
are positive integers and

is a field, then let

denote the set of pairwise-commuting

-tuples of

-matrices over

Due to the noncommutativity of matrix multiplication, geometric problems related to studying matrix rational points on curves and higher varieties only make sense when the matrices are commuting, that is, when the matrix points are in
C_{n,m}(K).
We will be interested in counting tuples in
C_{n,m}(K)
which satisfy the equations defining some affine varieties. More precisely, we will consider the sets

\{(A,B)\in C_{n,2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}):B^{2}=A(A-I_{n})(A-aI_{n})\}

and

\{(A,B,C)\in C_{n,3}(\mathbb{F}_{q}):C^{2}=AB(A+I_{n})(B+I_{n})(A+aB),C\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\}

as matrix analogues of (the smooth affine parts of) the Legendre elliptic curves
E_{\text{\rm L}}
and the K3 surfaces
X_{a}
considered above.

To express our results, we introduce some notation.
If

to denote the number of times

and write

More precisely, if

are any complex numbers, and

The series expansion of

\sum\limits_{r=0}^{\infty}b_{r}q^{r}=(q;q)_{\infty}^{5}=\left\lparen 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}q^{m(3m-1)/2}(1+q^{m})\right\rparen^{5},

Finally, for an integer

We start by expressing the number of commuting matrices on a Legendre elliptic curve. More precisely, if

is a positive integer,

Theorem 1.1
.

If

\ &k+1\end{matrix}\ \vrule\ 4\right).

2.
Some zeta functions

Let
q
be a prime power. Recall that
\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
is the group of
n\times n
invertible matrices over the finite field
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
with
q

It will be repetitively used in this paper that

(2.1)

\lvert\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\rvert=(-1)^{n}q^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}(q;q)_{n}.

Now, let
X=\operatorname{Spec}R
be an affine variety over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Say

(2.2)

R:=\frac{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[T_{1},\dots,T_{m}]}{(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})}.

Following the work
[
10
]
of the first author, we define the set of
n\times n

matrix points
on

as the set of commuting tuples of matrices satisfying the defining equations for

(2.3)

C_{n}(X):=\bigg\{\underline{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})^{m}:[A_{i},A_{j}]=0,f_{i}(\underline{A})=0\bigg\}.

Note that
C_{1}(X)\cong X({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
. Though not needed in this paper, it is worth pointing out that the cardinality of

is independent of the choice of defining equations for

[
10
, Eq. 4.2]
and
[
10

:

(2.4)

\frac{\lvert C_{n}(X)\rvert}{\lvert\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\rvert}=\sum_{\dim_{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}}H^{0}(X;M)=n}\frac{1}{\lvert\operatorname{Aut}M\rvert},

where the sum ranges over all isomorphism classes of zero-dimensional coherent sheaves on
X
of degree
n
. This characterization also makes
\lvert C_{n}(X)\rvert
well-defined for any variety
X
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.

The number of matrix points on a smooth curve or a smooth surface is given by infinite product formulas for a zeta function associated to it. For any (affine) variety
X
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, consider its

(terminology of
[
10
]

(2.5)

\hat{Z}_{X}(t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lvert C_{n}(X)\rvert}{\lvert\operatorname{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\rvert}t^{n},

and recall the local zeta function

(2.6)

Z_{X}(t):=\exp\left\lparen\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lvert X(\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}})\rvert}{n}\,t^{n}\right\rparen.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
10
, Proposition 4.6(a)]
)
.

If
X
is a smooth curve over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, then
