Beyond the thermodynamic limit: finite-size corrections to state interconversion rates
Christopher T. Chubb, Marco Tomamichel, Kamil Korzekwa

Abstract
Thermodynamics is traditionally constrained to the study of macroscopic systems whose energy fluctuations are negligible compared to their average energy. Here, we push beyond this thermodynamic limit by developing a mathematical framework to rigorously address the problem of thermodynamic transformations of finite-size systems. More formally, we analyse state interconversion under thermal operations and between arbitrary energy-incoherent states. We find precise relations between the optimal rate at which interconversion can take place and the desired infidelity of the final state when the system size is sufficiently large. These so-called second-order asymptotics provide a bridge between the extreme cases of single-shot thermodynamics and the asymptotic limit of infinitely large systems. We illustrate the utility of our results with several examples. We first show how thermodynamic cycles are affected by irreversibility due to finite-size effects. We then provide a precise expression for the gap between the distillable work and work of formation that opens away from the thermodynamic limit. Finally, we explain how the performance of a heat engine gets affected when the working body is small. We find that while perfect work cannot generally be extracted at Carnot efficiency, there are conditions under which these finite-size effects vanish. In deriving our results we also clarify relations between different notions of approximate majorisation.

Introduction

Background.

Thermodynamics forms an integral part of contemporary physics, providing us with invaluable rules that govern which transformations between macroscopic states are possible and which are not
[
Giles(1964)
]
. In modern parlance thermodynamics is an example of a resource theory
[
Coecke et al.(2016)Coecke,
Fritz, and Spekkens
]
. These provide us with a general framework to study the question of state interconversion by leveraging the structure imposed by the free states and operations within a given theory. The resource-theoretic approach has recently garnered renewed attention in the field of quantum information (see
[
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013a)
]
for a recent review) and allows us to quantify notions like entanglement
[
Horodecki et al.(2009)Horodecki, Horodecki, Horodecki, and Horodecki
]
, coherence
[
Baumgratz et al.(2014)Baumgratz, Cramer, and Plenio
,
Marvian and Spekkens(2016)
,
Winter and Yang(2016)
]
and asymmetry
[
Marvian and Spekkens(2013)
,
Marvian(2012)
]
. In the study of entanglement, for example, local operations and classical communication constitute the free operations and separable states are treated as free states. In an analogous way, a rigorous resource-theoretic formulation of quantum thermodynamics was provided in Refs.
[
Janzing et al.(2000)Janzing, Wocjan, Zeier, Geiss, and Beth
,
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013b)
,
Brandão et al.(2013)Brandão, Horodecki, Oppenheim,
Renes, and Spekkens
]
, with thermal Gibbs states being free and the laws of thermodynamics being captured by the restricted set of free operations known as
thermal operations
.

One of the main problems studied within the resource theory of thermodynamics is single-shot state interconversion, i.e., identifying when it is possible to convert a given state to another using only free operations (or, alternatively, identifying what extra resources are necessary to enable such transformations). Although for general quantum states only partial results are known
[
Lostaglio et al.(2015a)Lostaglio, Korzekwa, Jennings, and Rudolph
,
Ćwikliński et al.(2015)Ćwikliński, Studziński, Horodecki, and Oppenheim
,
Narasimhachar and Gour(2015)
]
(see also Ref.
[
Gour et al.(2017)Gour,
Jennings, Buscemi, Duan, and Marvian
]
for the most recent progress), the full solution was found for the restricted case of transforming states with no coherence between distinct energy eigenspaces. For such
energy-incoherent states
the necessary and sufficient conditions for single-shot state interconversion are given by a thermomajorisation relation between the initial and final states
[
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013b)
]
. As a result the allowed transformations are in general irreversible, which is captured by the fact that the amount of work needed to create a given state, the
work of formation
, is larger than the amount of work one can extract from it, the
distillable work
. We note that formally the thermomajorisation condition is strongly related to the majorisation condition appearing within the resource theory of entanglement when studying transformations between pure bipartite states
[
Nielsen(1999)
]
.

An important variant of the interconversion problem, lying on the opposite extreme of the single-shot case, is asymptotic state interconversion. In this case one considers having access to arbitrarily many copies of the initial state, and asks for the maximal conversion rate at which it is possible to transform instances of one state to another with asymptotically vanishing error. It was found that this rate is given by the ratio of non-equilibrium free energies of the initial and target states
[
Brandão et al.(2013)Brandão, Horodecki, Oppenheim,
Renes, and Spekkens
]
. Thus, the asymptotic interconversion rate is directly linked with the amount of useful work that can be extracted on average from a given state. Moreover, in this regime all transformations become fully reversible, as work of formation and distillable work asymptotically coincide. Again, this result closely resembles that obtained in pure state entanglement theory, where the optimal interconversion rate is given by the ratio of the entanglement entropies of the initial and target states
[
Bennett et al.(1996)Bennett, Bernstein, Popescu, and Schumacher
]
.

In this work we study the interconversion problem in an intermediate regime, between the single-shot and asymptotic cases described above. We thus consider transformations of a finite number
n
of instances of the input state and tolerate a non-zero error, which affects the optimal conversion rate. By developing the notions of approximate majorisation
[
Horodecki et al.(2017)Horodecki, Oppenheim, and Sparaciari
]
and thermomajorisation, we extend a formal relationship between the resource theories of pure state entanglement and thermodynamics of incoherent states to the approximate case. This allows us to adapt recently developed tools for approximate entanglement transformations in Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
to study corrections to the asymptotic rates of thermodynamic transformations, the so-called second-order asymptotics (see, e.g., Refs
[
Tomamichel and Hayashi(2013)
,
Li(2014)
,
Tomamichel and Tan(2015)
,
Datta et al.(2016)Datta,
Tomamichel, and Wilde
,
Datta and Leditzky(2015)
,
Wilde et al.(2016)Wilde,
Renes, and Guha
,
Tomamichel et al.(2016)Tomamichel, Berta, and Renes
,
Wilde et al.(2017)Wilde,
Tomamichel, Lloyd, and Berta
]
for other recent studies of second-order asymptotics in quantum information). The crucial technical difference between Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
and our work is the reversed direction of the majorisation relation, resulting in free states being given by uniform states rather than pure states. The second-order corrections were known to scale as
1/\sqrt{n}

[
Brandão et al.(2013)Brandão, Horodecki, Oppenheim,
Renes, and Spekkens
]
, and our main technical contribution is identifying the exact constant (including its dependence on the error) and interpreting its thermodynamic meaning.

Motivation.

Probably the most famous thermodynamic result concerns the irreversible nature of thermodynamic transformations, and is often captured by the oversimplified statement “entropy has to grow”. The dynamics of a system interacting with a thermal bath is irreversible since transformations performed at finite speed lead to heat dissipation, resulting in a loss of information about the system. One thus often studies idealised scenarios, when the system undergoes changes so slowly that it stays approximately in thermal equilibrium at all times. In this quasi-static limit one recovers reversible dynamics. However, thermodynamic reversibility actually requires one more assumption that is usually made implicitly. Namely, the thermodynamic description is only valid when applied to systems whose energy fluctuations are much smaller than their average energy. This is true for macroscopic systems composed of
n\rightarrow\infty
particles, in the so-called thermodynamic limit, and is reflected by the reversibility of the interconversion problem in the asymptotic limit. However, for finite
n
the macroscopic results do not hold anymore, leading to another source of irreversibility.

In the emerging field of quantum thermodynamics (see Ref.
[
Goold et al.(2016)Goold,
Huber, Riera, del Rio, and Skrzypczyk
]
and references therein) a focus is placed on possible transformations of small quantum systems interacting with a thermal environment. The necessity to go beyond classical thermodynamics is motivated by the fact that at the nanoscale quantum effects, like coherence
[
Åberg(2014)
,
Lostaglio et al.(2015b)Lostaglio, Jennings, and Rudolph
,
Lostaglio et al.(2015a)Lostaglio, Korzekwa, Jennings, and Rudolph
,
Ćwikliński et al.(2015)Ćwikliński, Studziński, Horodecki, and Oppenheim
,
Uzdin et al.(2015)Uzdin,
Levy, and Kosloff
,
Korzekwa et al.(2016)Korzekwa, Lostaglio, Oppenheim, and Jennings
]
and entanglement
[
Alicki and Fannes(2013)
,
Perarnau-Llobet et al.(2015)Perarnau-Llobet, Hovhannisyan, Huber,
Skrzypczyk, Brunner, and Acín
,
Brask et al.(2015)Brask,
Haack, Brunner, and Huber
]
, start playing an important role. However, beyond these phenomena, in the quantum regime one also deals with systems composed of a finite number
n
of particles. Hence, thermodynamic transformations of such systems are affected by the effective irreversibility discussed in the previous paragraph. The results we present in this paper provide a mathematical framework to rigorously address this problem. We thus provide a bridge between the extreme case of single-shot thermodynamics with
n=1
and the asymptotic limit of
n\rightarrow\infty
, allowing us to study the irreversibility of thermodynamic processes in the intermediate regime of large but finite
n
.

Main Results.

In order to state our main results we first need to introduce some concepts that will be defined more formally in Sections
2
and
3
. Let us consider a finite-dimensional quantum system, characterised by its Hamiltonian
H
, in the presence of a thermal bath at fixed temperature
T
. The initial state of the system is in general out of thermal equilibrium, and the bath can be governed by an arbitrary Hamiltonian. Energy-conserving operations that interact the system with a bath in thermal equilibrium are then known as thermal operations. A simple example of a thermal operation just swaps the system with a bath (governed by the same Hamiltonian), replacing the initial state with a thermal Gibbs state. Gibbs states for
H
, denoted by
\gamma
, are thus free states in the resource-theoretic formulation of thermodynamics.

In the following we focus on a system comprised of a finite number
n
of non-interacting subsystems, each governed by the Hamiltonian
H
. Let us consider a pair of subsystem states
\rho
and
\sigma
that both commute with
H
.

Discussion.

One of the main applications of our result is to the study of thermodynamic irreversibility. In the asymptotic limit,
n\rightarrow\infty
, the optimal conversion rate
R^{*}
from
\rho
to
\sigma
is equal to the inverse of the conversion rate from
\sigma
to
\rho

[
Brandão et al.(2013)Brandão, Horodecki, Oppenheim,
Renes, and Spekkens
]
. We can thus transform
\rho^{\otimes n}
through
\sigma^{\otimes R^{*}n}
back to
\rho^{\otimes n}
, so that the rate of concatenated transformations
R_{r}^{*}
is equal to 1 and the process can be performed reversibly. However, using Eq. (
1a
) twice, one finds the correction term to reversibility rate
R_{r}^{*}
, which is proportional to
1/\sqrt{n}
. Moreover, if
\nu\neq 1
this correction term is negative for small errors. In fact, it diverges when the error approaches zero, preventing a perfect reversible cycle. However, pairs of states with equal ratios of relative entropy and relative entropy variance with respect to the thermal state (such that
\nu=1
) are reversibly interconvertible up to second-order asymptotic corrections, mirroring a recent result in entanglement theory
[
Ito et al.(2015)Ito,
Kumagai, and Hayashi
]
. Thus,
\nu
can be interpreted as the irreversibility parameter that quantifies the amount of infidelity of an approximate cyclic process.

One particular consequence of the discussed irreversibility is the difference between the distillable work,
W_{D}
, and the work of formation,
W_{F}
, for a given state
\rho

[
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013b)
]
. The former is defined as the maximal amount of free energy in the form of pure energy eigenstates
\psi
that can be obtained per copy of
\rho
; the latter as the minimal amount of free energy in the form of pure energy eigenstates
\psi
needed per copy to create the target state
\rho
. We note that in the special case when
\psi
is chosen to be the ground state, the distillation process can be considered as Landauer erasure (resetting to zero energy pure states), whereas the formation process can be seen as the action of a Szilard engine (creating states out of information). In single-shot thermodynamics
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
were shown to be proportional to max- and min-relative entropies with respect to the thermal state
[
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013b)
]
; while in the asymptotic scenario they are both equal to
W=k_{B}T\cdot D(\rho\|\gamma)
, the non-equilibrium free energy of a state
[
Brandão et al.(2013)Brandão, Horodecki, Oppenheim,
Renes, and Spekkens
]
. Here, using appropriately modified Eqs. (
1a
) and (
1b
), we show that for large
n
the values of distillable work and work of formation per particle lie symmetrically around the asymptotic value,
W_{D}\simeq W-\Delta W
and
W_{F}\simeq W+\Delta W
, and provide the exact expression for the gap
\Delta W
. Moreover, in the special case when the investigated state
\rho
is itself a thermal state at some temperature different from the background temperature,
\Delta W
can be directly related to the relative strength of energy fluctuations of the system.

Finally, we investigate how one can investigate the performance of heat engines with finite working bodies using an appropriately chosen interconversion scenario. This allows us to study finite-size corrections to the efficiency of a heat engine and the quality of work it performs. More precisely, we consider a heat engine operating between two thermal baths at temperatures
T_{\mathrm{h}}
and
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, and a finite working body composed of
n
particles initially at temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
. We show that, unless the irreversibility parameter satisfies
\nu=1
, near-perfect work can be performed only with efficiency lower than the Carnot efficiency
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}
. However, allowing for imperfect work allows one to achieve and even surpass
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}

[
Ng et al.(2017)Ng,
Woods, and Wehner
]
. Moreover, we find that it is possible for a finite working body to have two thermal states at different temperatures,
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and
T_{\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}
, such that the irreversibility parameter for them is equal to 1. Thus, in a particularly engineered setting, it is possible to achieve Carnot efficiency and perform perfect work, while the finite working body changes temperature from
T_{\mathrm{c}}
to
T_{\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}
.

Thermodynamic setting

2.1
Thermal operations

We begin by describing the resource-theoretic approach to the thermodynamics of finite-dimensional quantum systems in the presence of a single heat bath at temperature
T

[

, whereas the bath, with a Hamiltonian

\gamma_{B}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{B}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H_{B}}\right)},

(3)

where

denoting the Boltzmann constant.

[U,H+H_{B}]=0.

(4)

(Thermal operations)

Given a fixed inverse temperature

consists of completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps that act on a system

{\cal E}^{\beta}(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\left(U\left(\rho\otimes\gamma_{B}\right)U^{\dagger}\right),

Note that energy conservation condition, Eq. (

2.2
Thermodynamic state interconversion

The thermodynamic interconversion problem is stated as follows: given a system (i.e., fixing
H
) together with initial and target states,
\rho
and
\sigma

{\cal E}^{\beta}
(for a fixed

Ćwikliński et al.(2015)Ćwikliński, Studziński, Horodecki, and Oppenheim
]
(however, we note that the problem has very recently been solved for a larger class of free operations given by

[

]

energy-incoherent states
, i.e.,
\rho
and
\sigma

[

]
. First, note that within this incoherent subtheory a quantum state can be equivalently represented by a probability distribution. For a non-degenerate Hamiltonian

, the initial and target states,
\rho
and
\sigma

are diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, so we can identify them with probability distributions

and

and

and

\rho
and

[

]

]

Let

(6)

\bm{f}

flat
if all its non-zero entries are equal. Note that in the infinite and zero temperature limits,
\beta\to 0
and
\beta\to\infty
respectively, the thermal state
\bm{\gamma}
becomes flat. Specifically, in the former case
\bm{\gamma}\to\bm{\eta}
, and in the latter
\bm{\gamma}\to\bm{s}:=[1,0,\dots,0]
for Hamiltonians with non-degenerate ground spaces.

The most general transformation between two probability distributions is given by a stochastic matrix
\Lambda
satisfying
\Lambda_{ij}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}\Lambda_{ij}=1
. We denote by
\Lambda^{\beta}
a
Gibbs-preserving
stochastic matrix with a thermal fixed point, i.e.,
\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{\gamma}=\bm{\gamma}
. In particular, a matrix
\Lambda^{0}
that preserves the uniform distribution
\bm{\eta}
is also called
bistochastic
. A probability vector
\bm{p}
is said to majorise
\bm{q}
, denoted by
\bm{p}\succ\bm{q}
, if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\downarrow}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}^{\downarrow},

(9)

for all
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, with
\bm{p}^{\downarrow}
denoting a probability vector with entries of
\bm{p}
arranged in a non-increasing order. We then have the following central result that is used in the study of state interconversion:

Theorem 3

(Theorem II.1.10 of Ref.
[
Bhatia(2013)
]
)
.

There exists a bistochastic matrix mapping from
\bm{p}
to
\bm{q}
if and only if
\bm{p}\succ\bm{q}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\exists\Lambda^{0}:~\Lambda^{0}\bm{\eta}=\bm{\eta}~\mathrm{and}~\Lambda^{0}\bm{p}=\bm{q}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bm{p}\succ\bm{q}.

(10)

The above theorem can be generalised from bistochastic matrices
\Lambda^{0}
to Gibbs-preserving matrices
\Lambda^{\beta}
with arbitrary
\beta
. To achieve this we first need to introduce the following embedding map.

Definition 4

(Embedding map)
.

Given a thermal distribution
\bm{\gamma}
with rational entries,
\gamma_{i}=D_{i}/D
and
D_{i},D\in\mathbb{N}
, the embedding map
\Gamma^{\beta}
sends a
d
-dimensional probability distribution
\bm{p}
to a
D
-dimensional probability distribution
\hat{\bm{p}}:=\Gamma^{\beta}(\bm{p})
as follow:

\hat{\bm{p}}=\left[\phantom{\frac{i}{i}}\!\!\!\!\right.\underbrace{\frac{p_{1}}{D_{1}},\dots,\frac{p_{1}}{D_{1}}}_{D_{1}\mathrm{~times}},\,\dots\,,\underbrace{\frac{p_{d}}{D_{d}},\dots,\frac{p_{d}}{D_{d}}}_{D_{d}\mathrm{~times}}\left.\phantom{\frac{i}{i}}\!\!\!\!\right].

(11)

The potentially irrational values of
\gamma_{i}
can be approached with arbitrarily high accuracy by choosing
D
large enough.

Note that
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}=\bm{\eta}^{D}
, i.e., embedding maps a thermal distribution into a uniform distribution over
D
entries, and that
\Gamma^{\beta}
is injective, implying the existence of a left inverse
(\Gamma^{\beta})^{-1}
. The action of
(\Gamma^{\beta})^{-1}
on a
D
-dimensional vector
\bm{r}
is given by summing up all the entries belonging to the same block of
D_{i}
entries. Moreover,
\Gamma^{\beta}(\Gamma^{\beta})^{-1}
is a bistochastic map, that transforms each block of
D_{i}
entries into a uniform distribution, i.e., given an index
j
belonging to a block
D_{i}
, denoted by
j\in[D_{i}]
, we have

[\Gamma^{\beta}(\Gamma^{\beta})^{-1}\bm{r}]_{j}=\frac{\sum_{k\in[D_{i}]}r_{k}}{D_{i}}.

(12)

We can also introduce the embedded version of a matrix
\Lambda^{\beta}
,

\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}:=\Gamma^{\beta}\Lambda^{\beta}(\Gamma^{\beta})^{-1}.

(13)

Notice that
\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}
is a bistochastic matrix, as it clearly maps the set of
D
-dimensional probability distributions into itself, and it preserves the uniform state
\bm{\eta}^{D}
,

\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}\bm{\eta}^{D}=\Gamma^{\beta}\Lambda^{\beta}(\Gamma^{\beta})^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}=\Gamma^{\beta}\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{\gamma}=\Gamma^{\beta}\bm{\gamma}=\bm{\eta}^{D}.

(14)

Using the notion of embedding we can define
thermomajorisation
relation
[
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013b)
]
(originally introduced in Ref.
[
Ruch et al.(1978)Ruch,
Schranner, and Seligman
]
as
d
-majorisation). A probability vector
\bm{p}
is said to thermomajorise
\bm{q}
, denoted by
\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}\bm{q}
, if and only if the majorisation relation holds between the embedded versions of
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, i.e.,

\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}\bm{q}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\hat{\bm{p}}\succ\hat{\bm{q}}.

(15)

Note that for
\beta=0
thermomajorisation becomes standard majorisation, as the embedding map is the identity matrix. Now, we have the following equivalence

\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{p}=\bm{q}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}\hat{\bm{p}}=\hat{\bm{q}},

(16)

which, since
\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}
is bistochastic, allows us to use Theorem
3
to obtain

Corollary 5

(Thermodynamic interconversion)
.

There exists a Gibbs-preserving matrix mapping from
\bm{p}
to
\bm{q}
if and only if
\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}\bm{q}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\exists\Lambda^{\beta}:~\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{\gamma}=\bm{\gamma}~\mathrm{and}~\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{p}=\bm{q}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}\bm{q}.

(17)

Due to Theorem
2
, Corollary
5
specifies the necessary and sufficient conditions for energy-incoherent state interconversion under thermal operations. In Fig.
1
we present the chain of equivalence relations leading to this result.

\rho
and

\bm{p}

3.2
Information-theoretic notions and their thermodynamic interpretation

The
relative entropy
or Kullback-Leibler divergence of a probability distribution
\bm{p}
with
\bm{q}
is defined as

\displaystyle D(\bm{p}\|\bm{q}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}

(18)

whenever the support of
\bm{q}
contains the support of
\bm{p}
(otherwise the divergence is set to
+\infty
). Denoting the average of a random variable
X
in a state
\bm{p}
by

\langle X\rangle_{\bm{p}}=\sum_{i}p_{i}X_{i},

(19)

we can introduce a random variable
L
with

\displaystyle\Pr\left[L=\log\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\right]=p_{i},

(20)

so that the divergence can be interpreted as the expectation value of the log-likelihood ratio,
D(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})=\langle L\rangle_{\bm{p}}
. Similarly, we define the corresponding variance, the
relative entropy variance
, as

\displaystyle V(\bm{p}\|\bm{q}):=\Var_{\bm{p}}(L),

(21)

where

\displaystyle\Var_{\bm{p}}(X):=\langle(X-\langle X\rangle_{\bm{p}})^{2}\rangle_{\bm{p}}.

(22)

The following equalities are an immediate consequence of the embedding map introduced in Eq. (
11
):

\displaystyle D(\hat{\bm{p}}\|\hat{\bm{q}})=D(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})\quad\textrm{and}\quad V(\hat{\bm{p}}\|\hat{\bm{q}})=V(\bm{p}\|\bm{q})\,.

(23)

In this work we will mostly encounter these quantities in the special case when
\bm{q}=\bm{\gamma}
is the thermal distribution corresponding to inverse temperature
\beta
,

\gamma_{i}=\frac{e^{-\beta E_{i}}}{{\cal Z}},\quad{\cal Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}e^{-\beta E_{i}}.

(24)

Then, both
D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})
and
V(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})
can be interpreted thermodynamically. First note that

D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})=\beta\langle E\rangle_{\bm{p}}-H(\bm{p})+\log{\cal Z},

(25)

with
\langle E\rangle_{\bm{p}}
being the average energy and

H(\bm{p}):=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\log p_{i}

(26)

denoting the Shannon entropy of
\bm{p}
(as a function of a distribution
\bm{p}
it should not to be confused with the Hamiltonian
H
). Now, recall that the classical expression for free energy reads
U-TS
, with
U
being the average energy of the system,
T
the background temperature and
S
the thermodynamic entropy; and that the free energy of the thermal state is
-k_{B}T\log{\cal Z}
. We thus see that
D(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})/\beta
can be interpreted as a non-equilibrium generalisation of free energy difference between an incoherent state
\rho
(represented by a probability distribution
\bm{p}
) and a thermal state
\gamma
.

Now, to interpret
V(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})
let us first introduce a covariance matrix
M
for the log-likelihood
\log p
and energy in the units of temperature
\beta E
:

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{p}}(\beta E,\beta E)&\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{p}}(\beta E,\log p)\\
\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{p}}(\log p,\beta E)&\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{p}}(\log p,\log p)\end{array}\right],

(27)

where
\mathrm{Cov}_{\bm{p}}(X,Y)=\langle XY\rangle_{\bm{p}}-\langle X\rangle_{\bm{p}}\langle Y\rangle_{\bm{p}}
.
The relative entropy variance can then be expressed as

V(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}M_{ij}.

(28)

In a particular case when the distribution
\bm{p}
is a thermal distribution
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}
at some different temperature
T^{\prime}\neq T
, the expression becomes

V(\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}||\bm{\gamma})=\left(1-\frac{T^{\prime}}{T}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{c_{T^{\prime}}}{k_{B}},

(29)

where

c_{T^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}}{\partial T^{\prime}}

(30)

is the specific heat capacity of the system in a thermal state at temperature
T^{\prime}
.

Finally, let us note that one can define quantum generalisations of both the relative entropy,
D(\rho||\sigma)

[
Umegaki(1962)
]
, and the relative entropy variance,
V(\rho||\sigma)

[
Tomamichel and Hayashi(2013)
,
Li(2014)
]
. Moreover, for a quantum state
\rho
commuting with the Hamiltonian, these relative quantities with respect to the thermal state
\gamma
coincide with classical expressions,

\displaystyle D(\rho||\gamma)=D(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma}),\quad V(\rho||\gamma)=V(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma}),

(31)

where
\bm{p}
denotes the vector of eigenvalues of
\rho
.

3.3
Approximate interconversion

As already mentioned in Section
2
we will focus on approximate interconversions, allowing the final state
\tilde{\bm{q}}
to differ from the target state
\bm{q}
, as long as it is close enough. We measure distance between states using the
infidelity
,

\delta(\bm{p},\bm{q}):=1-F(\bm{p},\bm{q})\,,

(32)

where the
fidelity
(or Bhattacharyya coefficient) is

F(\bm{p},\bm{q}):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}\right)^{2}\,.

(33)

We will also use the fidelity
{\cal F}
between two (continuous) probability density functions
f(x)
and
g(x)
, defined as

{\cal F}(f,g):=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{f(x)g(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}.

(34)

The two important properties of the infidelity that we will use throughout the paper are as follows. First, since fidelity is non-decreasing under stochastic maps
we have

\delta(\Lambda^{0}\bm{p},\Lambda^{0}\bm{q})\leq\delta(\bm{p},\bm{q}).

(35)

Second, the distance
\delta
between two probability vectors is the same as between their embedded versions, i.e.,

\delta(\bm{p},\bm{q})=\delta(\hat{\bm{p}},\hat{\bm{q}}),

(36)

which can be verified by direct calculation.

Although we will be mainly concerned with “smoothing” the final distribution (allowing it to differ from the desired target one), it is useful to introduce two dual definitions of approximate majorisation and thermomajorisation.

Definition 6

(Pre- and post-thermomajorisation)
.

A distribution
\bm{p}

\epsilon
-pre-thermomajorises
a distribution
\bm{q}
, which we denote
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ^{\beta}}~\bm{q}
, if there exists a
\tilde{\bm{p}}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{p}}\succ^{\beta}\bm{q}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta(\bm{p},\tilde{\bm{p}})\leq\epsilon.

(37)

A distribution
\bm{p}

\epsilon
-post-thermomajorises
a distribution
\bm{q}
, which we denote
\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\bm{q}
, if there exists a
\tilde{\bm{q}}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}\tilde{\bm{q}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta(\bm{q},\tilde{\bm{q}})\leq\epsilon.

(38)

In a particular case of
\beta=0
, when thermomajorisation coincides with majorisation, we will speak of pre- and post-majorisation, denoted by
\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}
and
\succ_{\epsilon}
, respectively.

Let us make a few comments about the above definition. First, notice that due to Corollary
5
,
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ^{\beta}}~\bm{q}
means that in the vicinity of
\bm{p}
there exists a state
\tilde{\bm{p}}
and
\Lambda^{\beta}
that maps it to
\bm{q}
. Similarly,
\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\bm{q}
means that there exists
\Lambda^{\beta}
that maps
\bm{p}
to
\tilde{\bm{q}}
, which lies in the vicinity of
\bm{q}
. We illustrate this in Fig.
2
. Next note that both
\succ^{\beta}_{0}
and
\prescript{}{0}{\succ^{\beta}}
reduce to thermomajorisation
\succ^{\beta}
, specifically
\succ^{0}_{0}
and
\prescript{}{0}{\succ^{0}}
are equivalent to the standard majorisation relation
\succ
. Let us also mention that the concept of majorisation between smoothed distributions has been recently studied in Refs.
[
Renes(2016)
,
Horodecki et al.(2017)Horodecki, Oppenheim, and Sparaciari
,
Van Der Meer et al.(2017)Van
Der Meer, Ng, and Wehner
]
. Moreover, as with exact thermomajorisation, approximate thermomajorisation specifies the existence of a thermal operation between two energy-incoherent states. More precisely, due to Theorem
2
, Corollary
5
and Definition
6
, we have the following:

Corollary 7
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be quantum states commuting with the system Hamiltonian
H
. Denote their eigenvalues by
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, respectively. Then there exists a thermal operation
{\cal E}^{\beta}
such that
\delta({\cal E}^{\beta}(\rho),\sigma)\leq\epsilon
if and only if
\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
.

Finally, let us make an important comment concerning approximate majorisation. Consider two distributions,
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, such that
\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
. By definition there exists
\tilde{\bm{q}}
close to
\bm{q}
that is majorised by
\bm{p}
. As majorisation is invariant under permutations,
\bm{p}
also majorises any distribution
\Pi\tilde{\bm{q}}
, where
\Pi
is arbitrary permutation. However, the fidelity between
\bm{q}
and
\Pi\tilde{\bm{q}}
is, in general, permutation-dependent. It is the largest, when the
i
-th largest entries of
\bm{q}
and
\Pi\tilde{\bm{q}}
coincide for all
i
, and so it is equal to
F(\bm{q}^{\downarrow},\tilde{\bm{q}}^{\downarrow})
. Therefore, for a given
\tilde{\bm{q}}
satisfying some majorisation relation, we know that for every state
\bm{q}
there exists
\Pi\tilde{\bm{q}}
satisfying the same relation, and with

\displaystyle F(\Pi\tilde{\bm{q}},\bm{q})=F(\tilde{\bm{q}}^{\downarrow},\bm{q}^{\downarrow}).

(39)

Thus, in the context of approximate majorisation, while calculating fidelities between any two states we will assume, without loss of generality, that they are ordered.

\{a_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n}
in
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle\simeq b_{n}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle a_{n}-b_{n}

\displaystyle=o(1/\sqrt{n})\,,

(40a)

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle\lesssim b_{n}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle a_{n}-b_{n}

\displaystyle\leq f_{n}=o(1/\sqrt{n})\,,

where

3.5
Rayleigh-normal distributions

The dependence of the finite-size corrections to optimal interconversion rate on the infidelity is given by generalisations of the Gaussian distribution known as the Rayleigh-normal distributions. This family of distributions was first introduced in Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
in the context of LOCC entanglement conversion. In order to define it, let us first denote the Gaussian cumulative distribution function, with mean value
\mu
and variance
\nu
, by
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-\frac{(t-\mu)^{2}}{2\nu}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(41)

As a shorthand notation we will also use
\Phi
to denote
\Phi_{0,1}
. Following Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
we can now define

Definition 8

(Rayleigh-normal distributions)
.

For any
\nu>0
the Rayleigh-normal distribution is a distribution on
\mathbb{R}
, whose cumulative function is given by

\displaystyle Z_{\nu}(\mu):=1-\sup_{A\geq\Phi}\mathcal{F}\left(A^{\prime},\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\right)\!,

(42)

where the supremum is taken over all monotone increasing and continuously differentiable
A:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
such that
A\geq\Phi
pointwise; and
f^{\prime}(x)
denotes the derivative of
f(x)
.

We now present some relevant properties of the Rayleigh-normal distributions.

Lemma 9

(Section 2 of Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
)
.

The Rayleigh-normal distributions have the following properties:

•

The
\nu\to 0
case converges in distribution to the normal Gaussian,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\nu\to 0}Z_{\nu}(\mu)=\Phi(\mu).

(43)

•

The
\nu=1
case reduces to the Rayleigh distribution of scale parameter
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
,

\displaystyle Z_{1}(\mu)=R_{\sqrt{2}}(\mu):=\begin{cases}1-e^{-\mu^{2}/4}&\mu\geq 0,\\
0&\mu\leq 0.\end{cases}

(44)

•

The Rayleigh-normal distributions possess a duality under inversion of the parameter
\nu
of the form

\displaystyle Z_{1/\nu}(\mu)=Z_{\nu}(\sqrt{\nu}\mu).

(45)

As well as these properties, an explicit form for the Rayleigh-normal distribution can be given. If we define
\alpha_{\mu,\nu}
as the unique solution
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
, Lemma 3]
to

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}(x)}=\frac{\Phi(x)}{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x)},

(46)

and let

\displaystyle A_{\mu,\nu}:=\begin{cases}\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x)\frac{\Phi(\alpha_{\mu,\nu})}{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(\alpha_{\mu,\nu})}&x\leq\alpha_{\mu,\nu},\\
\Phi(x)&x\geq\alpha_{\mu,\nu},\end{cases}

(47)

then for
\nu>1
we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{arg~max}}_{A\geq\Phi}\mathcal{F}\left(A^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\right)=A^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu},

(48)

and so
Z_{\nu}(\mu)=1-\mathcal{F}\left(A^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu},\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\right)

[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
, Theorem 4]
. Using the duality property, Eq. (
45
), a similar expression can be given for
\nu\in(0,1)
. We present plots of Rayleigh-normal distributions for a few selected values of
\nu
in Fig.

.

4
Second-order asymptotics for thermodynamic interconversion

4.1
Statement of the main result

We are now ready to state our main result concerning the second-order analysis of the approximate interconversion rates between independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) states under thermal operations. We focus on initial and target states,
\rho
and
\sigma
, that commute with the Hamiltonian
H
, so that we can represent them as probability distributions,
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, over their eigenvalues. For two fixed distributions
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
we will be interested in the trade-off between three parameters in the asymptotic
n\to\infty
regime: the rate of conversion
R
, the infidelity
\epsilon
, and the inverse temperature of the bath
\beta
4
4

4

Note that for fixed
H
the inverse temperature
\beta
fully specifies the thermal Gibbs distribution
\bm{\gamma}
.
. Specifically, we will be interested in the triples
(\beta,\epsilon,R)
for which there exist Gibbs-preserving maps
\Lambda^{\beta}
such that

\displaystyle\delta\Bigl(\Lambda^{\beta}\left(\bm{p}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes Rn}\right),\left(\bm{q}^{\otimes Rn}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes n}\right)\Bigr)\leq\epsilon,

(49)

where
\bm{\gamma}
denotes the Gibbs state at inverse temperature
\beta
. By Corollary
5
and Definition
6
this condition is equivalent to approximate post-thermomajorisation,

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes Rn}\succ_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\bm{q}^{\otimes Rn}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes n},

(50)

and, by Corollary
7
, there exists a thermal operation transforming
\rho^{\otimes n}
into a state
\epsilon
away in the infidelity measure from
\sigma^{\otimes Rn}
.

We then define the optimal interconversion rate
R^{*}_{\beta}(n,\epsilon;\bm{p},\bm{q})
and the optimal infidelity of interconversion
\epsilon^{*}_{\beta}(n,R;\bm{p},\bm{q})
as

\displaystyle R^{*}_{\beta}:=

\displaystyle{\max}\left\{R\,\middle|\,\bm{p}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes Rn}\succ_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\bm{q}^{\otimes Rn}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes n}\right\},

(51a)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}_{\beta}:=

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{\,$\min$\hss}\phantom{\max}\left\{\epsilon\,\middle|\,\bm{p}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes Rn}\succ_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\bm{q}^{\otimes Rn}\otimes\bm{\gamma}^{\otimes n}\right\}.

(51b)

When it is clear from context we will drop the explicit dependence on
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
. Our main result is then given by the following theorem.

Theorem 10

(Second-order asymptotic interconversion rates)
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be energy-incoherent initial and target states with eigenvalues given by
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, respectively. Then, for inverse temperature
\beta
and infidelity
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the optimal interconversion rate has the following second-order expansions

\displaystyle R^{*}(n,\epsilon)\simeq\frac{D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})}{D(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})}\left(1\!+\!\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})}{n\,D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})^{2}}}\,Z_{1/\nu}^{-1}(\epsilon)\right)

(52a)

\displaystyle~~\simeq\frac{D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})}{D(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})}\left(1\!+\!\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})}{n\,D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})D(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})}}\,Z_{\nu}^{-1}(\epsilon)\right)\!,

(52b)

where

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{V(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})/D(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})}{V(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})/D(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})}

(53)

is the irreversibility parameter.

The full proof of
10
can be found in
Section

6
. Before presenting it, we will discuss some of its consequences and applications in
Section

4.2
, and prove auxiliary results concerning approximate majorisation in
Section

5
. But first, let us make a few technical remarks about the above theorem. Note that Eqs. (
52a
)-(
52b
) are simply related by the duality property of Rayleigh-normal distribution, Eq. (
45
). The reason to state both formulas is that this way one covers each of the special cases,
V(\bm{p}\|\bm{\gamma})=0
and
V(\bm{q}\|\bm{\gamma})=0
, avoiding the use of
Z^{-1}_{\infty}
, which is undefined. The special case when both relative entropy variances vanish is covered separately in
Section

6.2
, where an exact expression for
R^{*}(n,\epsilon)
is provided (the asymptotic expansion of which coincides with the appropriate limit of Eq. (
52a
)).

Furthermore, since all the involved states are energy-incoherent, one can replace probability distributions
\bm{p},\bm{q},\bm{\gamma}
in
10
with density matrices
\rho,\sigma,\gamma
. This way one can study interconversion between non-commuting states
\rho
and
\sigma
, as long as they both commute with
H
. For example, if the Hamiltonian is trivial,
H\propto\mathbbm{1}
,
\rho
and
\sigma
may be arbitrary states. Thus,
10
yields a complete second-order analysis of interconversion under noisy operations
[
Horodecki et al.(2003)Horodecki, Horodecki, Horodecki,
Horodecki, Oppenheim, Sen(De), and Sen
]
, as for trivial Hamiltonians thermal operations coincide with noisy operations.

Finally, using results originally derived in Ref.
[
Vidal et al.(2000)Vidal,
Jonathan, and Nielsen
]
(see
Appendix

B
), one can numerically evaluate the optimal interconversion rates. In
Appendix

C
we show that this algorithm can be executed with a runtime that is efficient in the system size. Using this, in
Figures

4
and
10
we can compare our second-order expansion to the exact interconversion rates. We find that even for relatively small system sizes, the second-order asymptotic expansion gives a remarkably good approximation to the optimal interconversion rates, especially when compared to the first-order asymptotics.

4.2
Discussion and applications

Although general state interconversion may seem to be a rather abstract problem, we will now show how the formalism can be applied to study more familiar thermodynamic scenarios. Since asymptotic conversion rates allow for reversible interconversion cycles and the results presented in the previous section describe finite-size corrections to these rates, our considerations will mainly revolve around irreversibility. We will first quantify it directly, by calculating the rate at which
n
copies of a system can be transformed from initial state
\rho
, through
\sigma
, and back to
\rho
. We will then discuss the gap between work of formation and distillable work that opens when one processes finite number
n
of systems. Finally, we will apply our results to study the performance of heat engines operating with finite-size working bodies.

4.2.1
Finite-size reversibility

We start by considering the following thermodynamic process

\displaystyle\rho^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\sigma^{\otimes Rn}\rightarrow\rho^{\otimes R^{\prime}Rn},

(54)

with optimal interconversion rates given by

\displaystyle R=R_{\beta}^{*}(n,\epsilon_{1};\bm{p},\bm{q}),\quad R^{\prime}=R_{\beta}^{*}(Rn,\epsilon_{2};\bm{q},\bm{p}),

(55)

where
\rho
and
\sigma
commute with the Hamiltonian and their eigenvalues are given by
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, respectively. Without the second-order asymptotic corrections derived in this work, the reversibility rate
R_{r}^{*}:=RR^{\prime}
is equal to 1, and Eq. (
54
) describes a perfect cyclic process illustrated in Fig.
5
a. However, including finite-size corrections, from Eq. (
52a
) we get

\displaystyle R_{r}^{*}\simeq

\displaystyle\left(1+\sqrt{\dfrac{V(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})}{nD(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})^{2}}}Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1+\sqrt{\dfrac{V(\bm{q}||\bm{\gamma})}{RnD(\bm{q}||\bm{\gamma})^{2}}}Z^{-1}_{\nu}(\epsilon_{2})\right),

(56)

with the irreversibility parameter
\nu
given by Eq. (
53
). Now, using the duality of Rayleigh-normal distribution, Eq. (
45
), and ignoring the terms of order
o(1/\sqrt{n})
we obtain

\displaystyle R_{r}^{*}\simeq 1+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})}{nD(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})^{2}}}\left(Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon_{1})+Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon_{2})\right).

(57)

The error is accumulated during both transformations appearing in Eq. (
54
). However, since the infidelity
\delta
is not a metric, we cannot simply add the errors. Instead, we note that
\sqrt{\delta}

is
a metric, and so it satisfies the triangle inequality. Thus, the total error
\epsilon
, i.e., the infidelity between the final state and the target state
\rho^{\otimes R_{r}^{*}n}
, satisfies
\sqrt{\epsilon}\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{2}}
. Actually, for
\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}<1
, one can obtain a tighter upper bound
[
Tomamichel(2015)
]
,

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq\left(\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}(1-\epsilon_{2})}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{2}(1-\epsilon_{1})}\right)^{2}.

(58)

Let us now introduce a threshold amount of infidelity,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}:=Z_{1/\nu}(0)=Z_{\nu}(0),

(59)

where the equality comes from duality of Rayleigh-normal distribution. Note that, if
\nu=1
, resulting in
\epsilon_{0}=0
, then for any finite error one can eventually achieve
R_{r}^{*}>1
, and a perfect transformation with
R_{r}^{*}=1
and arbitrarily small error can be achieved. Thus, pairs of states satisfying
\nu=1
are reversibly interconvertible up to second-order asymptotic corrections, analogously to a recent result in entanglement theory
[
Ito et al.(2015)Ito,
Kumagai, and Hayashi
]
. The use of such states in thermodynamic transformations is favourable, as it minimises the dissipation of free energy to the environment.

We will now show that the irreversibility parameter
\nu
quantifies the incompatibility of two states (in that transformation from one state to the other leads to irreversibility) also beyond the special
\nu=1
case. Consider a process in which one requires that the number of systems
n
stays constant at all times. In other words, we require
R=R^{\prime}=1
, which implies

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon_{0}.

(60)

Now, since
Z_{\nu}(0)\leq 1/2
, with equality achieved only for
\nu=0
and
\nu\rightarrow\infty
, the error rates satisfy Eq. (
58
) and the total error
\epsilon
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq 4\epsilon_{0}(1-\epsilon_{0})=4Z_{\nu}(0)\left(1-Z_{\nu}(0)\right).

(61)

We present the above bound as a function of
\nu
in Fig.
6
a. We see that the closer
\nu
is to 1, the less error will be induced while performing a thermodynamic transformation
\rho^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\sigma^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\rho^{\otimes n}
or, in other words, the more reversible the process will be.

(a) In the asymptotic limit,

4.2.2
Distillable work and work of formation gap

One particularly important consequence of irreversibility is the difference between distillable work and work of formation
[
Horodecki and Oppenheim(2013b)
]
. These quantify the amount of thermodynamically relevant resources that can be distilled from, or are needed to form, a given state. Similarly to the resource theory of entanglement, where Bell states act as standard units of entanglement resource
[
Horodecki et al.(2009)Horodecki, Horodecki, Horodecki, and Horodecki
]
, also within the resource theory of thermodynamics there are states acting as “gold standards” for measuring the amount of resources present in a state. These are given by pure energy eigenstates which, having zero entropy, have a clear energetic interpretation. The transformation requiring a change of an ancillary battery state
|{w}\rangle
, with energy
w
, into a state
|{0}\rangle
, with zero energy, is thus interpreted as performing work
w
; and a transformation allowing for an opposite change corresponds to extracting work
w
. Hence, in order to assess the thermodynamic resourcefulness of
n
copies of a given energy-incoherent state,
\rho^{\otimes n}
, we will now investigate how much the energy of a pure battery system has to decrease per copy of
\rho
to construct
\rho^{\otimes n}
, and how much can it increase per copy of
\rho
while transforming
\rho^{\otimes n}
to a thermal state?

​​
​​

T_{\mathrm{c}}
and

)). The optimal achievable infidelity during the process is plotted in solid line, while the bound on the infidelity generated during a continuous process (when the finite working body evolves through thermal states at all intermediate temperatures) is plotted in dashed line.

More formally, to calculate work of distillation
W_{D}
we want to find the maximal value
w
allowing for the thermodynamic transformation

\left(\rho\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 0\right|\right)^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\left(\gamma\otimes\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|\right)^{\otimes n},

(62)

where the second subsystem is a battery described by a Hamiltonian
H_{B}=0\left|0\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 0\right|+w\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|
. Similarly, to calculate work of formation
W_{F}
we want to find the minimal value
w
allowing for the thermodynamic transformation

\left(\gamma\otimes\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|\right)^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\left(\rho\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 0\right|\right)^{\otimes n}.

(63)

Using Theorem
10
we can obtain the optimal rate for transformation described by Eq. (
62
) as a function of
w
, set it to 1 and solve for
w
, thus arriving at the approximate expression for the work of distillation:

W_{D}\approx k_{B}T\left(D(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})+\sqrt{\dfrac{V(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\right),

(64)

with
\bm{p}
denoting the eigenvalues of
\rho
and
\Phi^{-1}
being the inverse of the normal Gaussian distribution. One can obtain the expression for the work of formation in an analogous way, this time looking for the optimal rate for transformation given in Eq. (
63
), resulting in

W_{F}\approx k_{B}T\left(D(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})-\sqrt{\dfrac{V(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\right).

(65)

W_{F}

[

]
. In the intermediate regime of large but finite
n
studied in this work, the values of
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
lie symmetrically around the value achieved in the asymptotic limit, where
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
coincide and are equal to the non-equilibrium generalisation of free energy. Note that the
y
axis above is in the units of
k_{B}T
.

First of all, let us briefly comment on the effect that imperfect transformations (characterised by infidelity
\epsilon
) have on the interpretation of distillable work and work of formation derived above. In the case of distillable work the non-zero infidelity means that the final battery state may differ from the pure state
\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|^{\otimes n}
, so one may actually distil less than
W_{D}
work per particle. Similarly, for the work of formation the final state of the battery may differ from the pure state
\left|0\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 0\right|^{\otimes n}
, so one may actually use more than
W_{F}
work per particle (by using the purity of the battery). To overcome such problems, one may employ the idea of
\epsilon
-deterministic work extraction
[
Åberg(2013)
]
in the following way. After the distillation process (the argument for the formation process is analogous) one can simply measure the battery in its energy eigenbasis. With probability larger or equal to
1-\epsilon
the battery state will collapse on
\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|^{\otimes n}
(and so
n\cdot W_{D}
work will be distilled), and with probability
\epsilon
the work gain will differ from the derived value. Additionally, one has to take into account the thermodynamic cost of measuring the battery (erasing memory), proportional to the binary entropy of
\epsilon
(note however that this cost is constant and so the cost per particle vanishes as
1/n
). Crucially, by choosing
\epsilon
to be arbitrarily small, one can approach deterministic work distillation arbitrarily well, i.e., distil
n\cdot W_{D}
work with probability arbitrarily close to 1.

Secondly, let us note that with the second-order asymptotic correction
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
lie symmetrically around the asymptotic value
W=k_{B}T\cdot D(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})
,

\displaystyle W_{D}\approx W-\Delta W,\quad W_{F}\approx W+\Delta W,

(66)

with

\displaystyle\Delta W:=-k_{B}T\cdot\sqrt{\dfrac{V(\bm{p}||\bm{\gamma})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon).

(67)

Notice that the above correction term is positive for small values of infidelity
\epsilon
, so that the resource cost of near-perfect formation of a state is always larger than the amount of resources than can be distilled from it. This symmetric gap that opens for finite
n
is illustrated in Fig.
7
, where we also compare it with the values of
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
for the single-shot scenario
n=1
(where
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
generally lie asymmetrically around the asymptotic value
W
).

Furthermore, our second-order correction for distillable work exactly coincides with the one derived in Ref.
[
Åberg(2013)
]
within an alternative thermodynamic framework, where state transformations are modelled by a sequence of energy level transformations (changes of Hamiltonian eigenvalues interpreted as performing/extracting work) and full thermalisations (replacing a state with the thermal state), rather than by thermal operations. This might have been expected, as the authors of Ref.
[
Egloff et al.(2015)Egloff,
Dahlsten, Renner, and Vedral
]
(and more recently of Ref.
[
Perry et al.(2015)Perry,
Ćwikliński, Anders, Horodecki, and Oppenheim
]
) showed that any transformation between energy-incoherent states which can be achieved via a thermal operation, can also be achieved by a sequence of level transformations and partial level thermalisations.

Finally, let us analyse the special case when the state under scrutiny is itself a thermal equilibrium state
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}
, at some temperature
T^{\prime}
different from the background temperature
T
. In the asymptotic limit
n\rightarrow\infty
, both distillable work
W_{D}
and work of formation
W_{F}
coincide with the standard thermodynamic result: the maximal (minimal) amount of work that can be extracted (needs to be invested) while changing the temperature of the system from
T^{\prime}
to
T
(from
T
to
T^{\prime}
) is given by its free energy change. However, we also obtain the second-order asymptotic correction to
W_{D}
and
W_{F}
, given by

\displaystyle\Delta W=-|T-T^{\prime}|\sqrt{\frac{k_{B}c_{T^{\prime}}}{n}}\,\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon),

(68)

where we have used Eq. (
29
) to relate the relative entropy variance with
c_{T^{\prime}}
, the heat capacity of the system at temperature
T^{\prime}
. In order to interpret this correction term, we first note that standard thermodynamic results apply when fluctuations of energy are much smaller than the average energy of the system. Now, to quantify the relative strength of fluctuations, we introduce a fluctuation parameter
f
as a ratio of the total energy variation and the total energy itself,

\displaystyle f:=\frac{\sqrt{n\mathrm{Var}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}(E)}}{n\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{B}c_{T^{\prime}}}{n}}\cdot\frac{T^{\prime}}{\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}}.

(69)

We then see that the correction term
\Delta W
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta W=-fw\,\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon),\quad w:=\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}\left|1-\frac{T}{T^{\prime}}\right|,

(70)

so that
\Delta W
is directly related to the relative strength of fluctuations
f
, and disappears when the standard thermodynamic assumption,
f=0
, holds. Note that
\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)
is negative for
\epsilon<1/2
. Moreover,
w
is the amount of work performed by an engine operating at Carnot efficiency between two heat baths at temperatures
T
and
T^{\prime}
, when the amount of heat equal to
\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}
flows in to, or out of, the bath at temperature
T^{\prime}
(the former for
T>T^{\prime}
, the latter for
T^{\prime}>T
).

4.2.3
Corrections to efficiency of heat engines

One of the consequences of studying thermodynamics in the quantum regime is that it may not always be plausible to assume that working bodies and thermal reservoirs are infinite. Thus, recent studies focused on the effects finite-size baths have on standard thermodynamic results like fluctuation theorems
[
Campisi et al.(2009)Campisi, Talkner, and Hänggi
]
, Landauer’s principle
[
Reeb and Wolf(2014)
]
, second
[
Richens et al.(2018)Richens, Alhambra, and Masanes
]
and third law of thermodynamics
[
Scharlau and Mueller(2018)
]
. Moreover, the performance of heat engines operating between finite-size baths was investigated in Refs.
[
Tajima and Hayashi(2017)
,
Ito and Hayashi(2016)
]
(by directly focusing on the behaviour of thermodynamic quantities and not on the interconversion problem) and Ref.
[
Woods et al.(2015)Woods,
Ng, and Wehner
]
(where the main focus was on the energetic structure of the finite bath and not on its size). Here, we will show how our results can be employed to investigate the performance of heat engines with finite working bodies, by studying the appropriately chosen interconversion problem. As we will discuss systems in equilibrium at different temperatures, we will indicate the (inverse) temperature in the subscript. More precisely, a system at temperature
T_{\mathrm{x}}
(at inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathrm{x}}
) will be denoted by
\gamma_{\mathrm{x}}
, and the corresponding partition function by
{\cal Z}_{\mathrm{x}}
. Also, note that equilibrium states are diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, so our results are applicable.

We consider two infinite baths at temperatures
T_{\mathrm{h}}>T_{\mathrm{c}}
, and a finite working body composed of
n
particles initially at a cold temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
(analogous considerations hold for the initial temperature being
T_{\mathrm{h}}
). As in the previous subsection, we also include a battery system comprised of
n
two-level systems, each described by Hamiltonian
H_{B}=w\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|
, initially in a zero energy eigenstate
\left|0\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 0\right|^{\otimes n}
. We now couple the working body at temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and the battery to the hot bath, allowing us to perform a thermal operation with respect to temperature
T_{\mathrm{h}}
. In particular, we consider the following transformation

\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 0\right|\right)^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}\otimes\left|w\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle w\right|\right)^{\otimes n}.

(71)

This transformation can be understood as a result of heat
Q_{\mathrm{in}}
flowing from the hotter background bath into an engine; part of it,
Q_{\mathrm{out}}
, then heats up the working body composed of
n
particles from
T_{\mathrm{c}}
to
T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}
, while the remaining energy is used to perform work
n\cdot w
on
n
particles comprising the battery. We schematically present this thermodynamic process in Fig.
8
.

(72)

while the optimal amount of performed work
W=n\cdot w
can be calculated similarly as in the previous subsection (by setting the rate from Eq. (

) for the transformation given by Eq. (

) to 1 and solving for

), yielding

w\approx k_{B}T_{\mathrm{h}}\left(\Delta D+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})}{n}}Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon)\right),

(73)

where we have introduced the following shorthand notation

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle:=\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}-\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}}},

(74a)

\displaystyle\Delta D

\displaystyle:=D(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})-D(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}}),

(74b)

and
\nu=V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})/V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})
. Now, using energy conservation,

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{in}}=Q_{\mathrm{out}}+W,

(75)

we can calculate the efficiency of the considered process to be

\eta=\frac{W}{Q_{\mathrm{in}}}=\left(1+\frac{Q_{\mathrm{out}}}{W}\right)^{-1},

(76)

with
Q_{\mathrm{out}}
and
W
given by Eqs. (
72
) and (
73
), respectively.

In order to interpret the obtained expression let us first analyse the limiting case. Ignoring the second-order asymptotic correction (sending
n\rightarrow\infty
), the extracted work is just equal to the change of the free energy of the working body. In Appendix
A
we show that this is exactly the amount of work that would be extracted by an engine operating at Carnot efficiency,

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{C}}(T_{\mathrm{x}}):=1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{x}}}{T_{\mathrm{h}}},

(77)

between an infinite bath at fixed temperature
T_{\mathrm{h}}
and a colder finite bath that heats up during the process from
T_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{c}}
to
T_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}
. In other words, without the
1/\sqrt{n}
correction we obtain an
integrated
Carnot efficiency
\eta^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathrm{C}}
that arises from an
instantaneous
Carnot efficiency
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}
at all times,

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathrm{C}}(T_{\mathrm{c}}\rightarrow T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}})=\left(1+\frac{\Delta E}{k_{B}T_{\mathrm{h}}\Delta D}\right)^{-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!.

(78)

The relation becomes even more evident when we consider the limit
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
. Then

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\Delta T\to 0}c_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\Delta T,

(79a)

\displaystyle\Delta D

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\Delta T\to 0}c_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\Delta T\left(\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{\mathrm{c}}}-\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{\mathrm{h}}}\right),

(79b)

with
c_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}
denoting the heat capacity of the system at temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, and so

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathrm{C}}(T_{\mathrm{c}}\rightarrow T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\Delta T\to 0}\eta_{\mathrm{C}}(T_{\mathrm{c}}).

(80)

Now, the finite-size correction leads to a modified expression for integrated efficiency, given by

\displaystyle\eta^{\mathrm{int}}\approx\eta^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathrm{C}}+\frac{k_{B}(T_{\mathrm{h}}-T_{\mathrm{c}})\Delta E}{(k_{B}T_{\mathrm{h}}\Delta D+\Delta E)^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{c_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}}{nk_{B}}}Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon),

(81)

where we have used Eq. (
29
) again to relate the relative entropy variance with the heat capacity of the system. We first note that there exists a threshold amount of infidelity
\epsilon_{0}
, given by Eq. (
59
), below which the correction term is negative. Since the infidelity between final and target states can be interpreted as performing imperfect work, near-perfect work can be performed only with efficiency strictly smaller than
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{int}}
. On the other hand, accepting infidelity
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{0}
allows one to achieve and even go beyond the integrated efficiency corresponding to instantaneous Carnot efficiency. This is in accordance with a recent result showing that the Carnot efficiency can be surpassed by extracting imperfect work
[
Ng et al.(2017)Ng,
Woods, and Wehner
]
.

As in the asymptotic limit, we also want to investigate the instantaneous efficiency, when
T_{\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}
is very close to
T_{\mathrm{c}}
. In particular, we will focus on the quality of performed work when the engine works at instantaneous Carnot efficiency. We thus require that the error
\Delta\epsilon
accumulated during an infinitesimal step that changes the temperature by
\Delta T\rightarrow 0
is equal to the threshold error
\epsilon_{0}
. Since then the correction term vanishes, we have
\eta^{\mathrm{int}}\approx\eta^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathrm{C}}
and we know that for small
\Delta T
this yields
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}
. Because the two considered thermal states are close,
\nu
is close to unity, and as such the infidelity of the process can be expanded as

\displaystyle\Delta\epsilon=Z_{\nu}(0)=Z_{1+\Delta\nu}(0)\approx\alpha\Delta\nu^{2},

(82)

where
\alpha\approx 0.0545
can be numerically evaluated. The expansion of
\Delta\nu
in terms of
\Delta T
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\nu\approx g(T_{\mathrm{c}})\Delta T,

(83)

with

\displaystyle g(T_{\mathrm{c}}):=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}}\left[\log V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})\right]\Biggr|_{T_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{c}}}.

(84)

As discussed in Section
4.2.1
, it is not infidelity, but its square root that satisfies the triangle inequality. We thus have that the instantaneous rate of accumulating square root infidelity is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sqrt{\epsilon}}{\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}}\approx\sqrt{\alpha}|g(T_{\mathrm{x}})|,

(85)

and so one can achieve the instantaneous Carnot efficiency by paying the price of an instantaneous rate of accumulating error. This can be then translated into the bound on the total accumulated error in the following way

\displaystyle\epsilon\leq\left(\int_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}^{T_{\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sqrt{\epsilon}}{\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}\right)^{2}=\alpha\left(\int_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}^{T_{\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}}|g(T_{\mathrm{x}})|\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}\right)^{2}\!.

(86)

It is straightforward to show that this upper bound is larger than
\alpha\log^{2}\nu
, which in turn is larger than
Z_{\nu}(0)
. This shows that the error accumulated in a continuous process (with the working body continuously passing through all intermediate temperatures) is in general larger than that of an optimal “one-step” process. We illustrate this in Fig.
6
b.

Finally, let us comment on a special case when
\nu=1
. For initial and target states being thermal equilibrium states at distinct temperatures (and different from background temperature
T_{\mathrm{h}}
), the value of
\nu
depends on the Hamiltonian of the investigated system. If it happens that for a given Hamiltonian there exist
T_{\mathrm{x}}
and
T_{\mathrm{x}^{\prime}}
such that
\nu=1
, then it is possible to achieve perfect work extraction at integrated Carnot efficiency
\eta_{\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{int}}(T_{\mathrm{x}}\rightarrow T_{\mathrm{x^{\prime}}})
. Interestingly, for any Hamiltonian there always exist such pairs of temperatures. To see this note that for both
T_{\mathrm{x}}=0
and
T_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{h}}
the relative entropy variance vanishes,
V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})=0
. Since it is a continuous function of temperature, we get that for any
T_{\mathrm{x}}
in the interval
(0,T_{\mathrm{h}})
there exists at least one other temperature
T_{\mathrm{x^{\prime}}}
such that
V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})=V(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x^{\prime}}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})
, resulting in
\nu=1
. This shows that by appropriately choosing the temperatures between which the heat engine operates, one may decrease or even avoid irreversible losses.

5
Results on approximate majorisation

We now proceed to the presentation of a few technical lemmas that may be of independent interest. These concern relations between different notions of approximate majorisation and thermomajorisation introduced in Section
3.3
. We first need the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 11
.

For fixed probability vectors
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
denote by
\tilde{\bm{p}}
any distribution that majorises
\bm{q}
, and by
\tilde{\bm{q}}
any distribution that is majorised by
\bm{p}
. Then the maximum fidelity between
\tilde{\bm{p}}
and
\bm{p}
over all such
\tilde{\bm{p}}
is equal to the maximum fidelity between
\bm{q}
and
\tilde{\bm{q}}
over all such
\tilde{\bm{q}}
, i.e.,

\max_{\tilde{\bm{p}}:~\tilde{\bm{p}}\succ\bm{q}}F(\bm{p},\tilde{\bm{p}})=\max_{\tilde{\bm{q}}:~\bm{p}\succ\tilde{\bm{q}}}F(\bm{q},\tilde{\bm{q}}).

(87)

The proof of the above lemma is based on the results first derived in Ref.
[
Vidal et al.(2000)Vidal,
Jonathan, and Nielsen
]
and can be found in Appendix
B
. Moreover, the proof includes an explicit construction of the state
\tilde{\bm{p}}^{\star}
maximising the left hand side of Eq. (
87
), so that one can calculate the value of optimal achievable fidelities appearing in Lemma
11
.

Now we can prove the following crucial result concerning pre- and post-majorisation, i.e., approximate thermomajorisation for
\beta=0
.

Lemma 12
.

Pre- and post-majorisation are equivalent, i.e.,
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\bm{q}
if and only if
\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}~\bm{q}
. This means that there exists
\bm{\tilde{p}}
such that
\bm{\tilde{p}}\succ\bm{q}
and
\delta(\bm{p},\bm{\tilde{p}})\leq\epsilon
. By
3
this implies that there exists a bistochastic
\Lambda^{0}
such that
\Lambda^{0}\bm{\tilde{p}}=\bm{q}
. Let
\bm{\tilde{q}}:=\Lambda^{0}\bm{p}
, so that
\bm{p}\succ\tilde{\bm{q}}
. Using the fact that fidelity is non-decreasing under stochastic maps, we then have

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{q},\bm{\tilde{q}})=\delta(\Lambda^{0}\bm{\tilde{p}},\Lambda^{0}\bm{p})\leq\delta(\bm{\tilde{p}},\bm{p})\leq\epsilon,

(88)

which means that
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}~\bm{q}\implies\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
.

Now, assume that
\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
. This means that there exists
\tilde{\bm{q}}
such that
\bm{p}\succ\tilde{\bm{q}}
and
\delta(\bm{q},\tilde{\bm{q}})\leq\epsilon
. Let

\tilde{\bm{p}}^{\star}:=\argmax_{\tilde{\bm{p}}:~\tilde{\bm{p}}\succ\bm{q}}F(\bm{p},\tilde{\bm{p}}).

(89)

By definition
\tilde{\bm{p}}^{\star}\succ\bm{q}
and, by Lemma
11
, we have

F(\bm{p},\tilde{\bm{p}}^{\star})=\max_{\bm{q}^{\prime}:~\bm{p}\succ\bm{q}^{\prime}}F(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime})\geq F(\bm{q},\tilde{\bm{q}}),

(90)

so that
\delta(\bm{p},\tilde{\bm{p}}^{\star})\leq\epsilon
. Thus
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}~\bm{q}\Longleftarrow\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
.
∎

The next lemma links post-majorisation of embedded vectors with post-thermomajorisation for
\beta\neq 0
.

Lemma 13
.

Post-majorisation between embedded vectors is equivalent to post-thermomajorisation between the original vectors, i.e.,

\hat{\bm{p}}\succ_{\epsilon}\hat{\bm{q}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bm{p}\succ_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\bm{q}

(91)

Proof.

First, assume
\hat{\bm{p}}\succ_{\epsilon}\hat{\bm{q}}
. This means that there exists a bistochastic matrix
\Lambda^{0}
such that
\Lambda^{0}\hat{\bm{p}}=\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}}
with
\delta(\hat{\bm{q}},\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})\leq\epsilon
. This, in turn, means that

\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{p}=\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}},

(92)

with
\Lambda^{\beta}=\Gamma^{-1}\Lambda^{0}\Gamma
being a Gibbs-preserving matrix and
\Gamma
a shorthand notation of the embedding map
\Gamma^{\beta}
. We thus conclude that
\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}}
. It remains to show that
\delta(\bm{q},\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})\leq\epsilon
. To achieve this we use the facts that embedding is fidelity-preserving,
\Gamma\Gamma^{-1}
is bistochastic and fidelity is non-decreasing under stochastic maps, so that

\displaystyle F(\bm{q},\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})

\displaystyle=F(\Gamma^{-1}\hat{\bm{q}},\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})

\displaystyle=F(\Gamma\Gamma^{-1}\hat{\bm{q}},\Gamma\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})\geq F(\hat{\bm{q}},\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}}).

(93)

Therefore
\delta(\bm{q},\Gamma^{-1}\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})\leq\epsilon
, which results in
\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
.

Now, assume that
\bm{p}\succ^{\beta}_{\epsilon}\bm{q}
. This means that there exists a Gibbs-preserving matrix
\Lambda^{\beta}
such that
\Lambda^{\beta}\bm{p}=\tilde{\bm{q}}
with
\delta(\bm{q},\tilde{\bm{q}})\leq\epsilon
. Through embedding this is equivalent to the existence of a bistochastic
\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}
such that
\hat{\Lambda}^{\beta}\hat{\bm{p}}=\hat{\tilde{\bm{q}}}
, resulting in
\hat{\bm{p}}\succ\hat{\tilde{\bm{q}}}
. It remains to show that
\delta(\hat{\bm{q}},\hat{\tilde{\bm{q}}})\leq\epsilon
. This, however, follows directly from the fact that the embedding map is fidelity-preserving, as
\delta(\hat{\bm{q}},\hat{\tilde{\bm{q}}})=\delta(\bm{q},\tilde{\bm{q}})\leq\epsilon
. We thus conclude that
\hat{\bm{p}}\succ_{\epsilon}\hat{\bm{q}}
.
∎

The statement of Lemma
13
can be rephrased as

\displaystyle\exists\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}}:~\hat{\bm{p}}\succ\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}},~\delta(\hat{\bm{q}},\tilde{\hat{\bm{q}}})\leq\epsilon

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!\!\Longleftrightarrow\quad\!\!\exists\tilde{\bm{q}}:~\hat{\bm{p}}\succ\hat{\tilde{\bm{q}}},~\delta(\hat{\bm{q}},\hat{\tilde{\bm{q}}})\leq\epsilon,

(94)

so that it can be interpreted as the fact that embedding (denoted by hat) and smoothing (denoted by tilde) commute when applied to the target state.

Finally, we present a result that links pre-majorisation of embedded vectors with pre-thermomajorisation for
\beta\neq 0
.

Lemma 14
.

Pre-majorisation between embedded vectors is implied by the pre-thermomajorisation between the original vectors, but it does not imply it, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{p}}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\hat{\bm{q}}\quad\Longleftarrow\quad\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ^{\beta}}\bm{q},

(95a)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{p}}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\hat{\bm{q}}\quad\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\Longrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\Longrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.625pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\Longrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.875pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\Longrightarrow}}}\quad\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ^{\beta}}\bm{q}.

(95b)

Proof.

We first prove Eq. (
95a
). Assuming
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ^{\beta}}\bm{q}
(and recalling that embedding is fidelity preserving) means that there exists
\hat{\tilde{\bm{p}}}
majorising
\hat{\bm{q}}
and satisfying
\delta(\hat{\bm{p}},\hat{\tilde{\bm{p}}})\leq\epsilon
. Then, by simply choosing
\tilde{\hat{\bm{p}}}=\hat{\tilde{\bm{p}}}
, we get
\tilde{\hat{\bm{p}}}\succ\hat{\bm{q}}
and
\delta(\hat{\bm{p}},\tilde{\hat{\bm{p}}})\leq\epsilon
, so that
\hat{\bm{p}}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\hat{\bm{q}}
.

Now, in order to prove Eq. (
95b
) we will construct a particular counterexample. Consider the distributions

\displaystyle\bm{p}=[1,0],\quad\bm{q}=[1/2,1/2],\quad\bm{\gamma}=[3/4,1/4].

(96)

Their embedded versions are then given by

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{p}}=[1/3,1/3,1/3,0],\quad\hat{\bm{q}}=[1/6,1/6,1/6,1/2].

(97)

We note that
\tilde{\hat{\bm{p}}}=[1/2,1/4,1/4,0]
majorises
\hat{\bm{q}}
and that
\delta(\hat{\bm{p}},\tilde{\hat{\bm{p}}})=(3-2\sqrt{2})/6=:\epsilon_{0}
. Therefore,
\hat{\bm{p}}\prescript{}{\epsilon_{0}}{\succ}\hat{\bm{q}}
. On the other hand, for a general two-dimensional distribution,
\tilde{\bm{p}}=[\tilde{p},1-\tilde{p}]
, we have
\delta(\bm{p},\tilde{\bm{p}})=1-\tilde{p}
, and the smallest
\tilde{p}
for which
\tilde{\bm{p}}\succ^{\beta}\bm{q}
is equal to
1/2
. This means that the optimal
\epsilon_{1}
for which
\tilde{\bm{p}}\prescript{}{\epsilon_{1}}{\succ^{\beta}}\bm{q}
holds is
\epsilon_{1}=1/2>\epsilon_{0}
. We thus conclude that
\hat{\bm{p}}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\hat{\bm{q}}
does not imply
\bm{p}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ^{\beta}}\bm{q}
.
∎

6
Proofs of the main result

We will now present a proof of our main result,
10
. We will do this by first showing a reduction to special case of bistochastic interconversion, which corresponds to infinite temperature. We recall that as we are considering energy-incoherent initial and target states,
\rho
and
\sigma
, we only need to consider their eigenvalues, denoted by
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, with the embedded versions of these given by
\bm{\hat{p}}
and
\bm{\hat{q}}
. Also note that the embedded thermal state
\bm{\hat{\gamma}}
simply corresponds to the uniform state
\bm{\eta}
. We can thus use the equivalence between approximate post-thermomajorisation and embedded majorisation, Lemma
13
, to obtain:

\displaystyle R^{*}_{\beta}(n,\epsilon;\bm{p},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon;\bm{\hat{p}},\bm{\hat{q}}),

(99a)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}_{\beta}(n,R;\bm{p},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R;\bm{\hat{p}},\bm{\hat{q}}).

(99b)

Conveniently, the second-order expansion only depends on the states through the relative entropy and relative entropy variance, both of which are invariant under embedding, as noted earlier in Eq. (
23
). We can thus solve the problem for embedded distributions and
\beta=0
, and in the final result exchange all
D(\bm{\hat{a}}||\bm{\eta})
and
V(\bm{\hat{a}}||\bm{\eta})
with
D(\bm{a}||\bm{\gamma})
and
V(\bm{a}||\bm{\gamma})
, respectively. For the remainder of this section we will henceforth drop the embedding hats and define
D(\bm{a}):=D(\bm{a}\|\bm{\eta})
. We also note that
V(\bm{a}\|\bm{\eta})=V(\bm{a})
, where

\displaystyle V(\bm{a})=\langle(-\log a_{i}-H(\bm{a}))^{2}\rangle_{\bm{a}}

(100)

is the entropy variance, and that
V(\bm{a})=0
if and only if
\bm{a}
is flat. Specifically, the irreversibility parameter given in Eq. (
53
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{V(\bm{p})/D(\bm{p})}{V(\bm{q})/D(\bm{q})}.

(101)

We will split the infinite-temperature proof into four parts, based on whether the relative entropy variances of the initial and target states are non-zero.

\begin{array}[]{|c|cc|}\hline\cr&V(\bm{q})=0&V(\bm{q})>0\\
\hline\cr V(\bm{p})=0&\text{Flat-to-flat}&\text{Formation}\\
V(\bm{p})>0&\text{Distillation}&\text{Interconversion}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

We start with the case where both initial and target states have zero relative entropy variance. We refer to this as flat-to-flat interconversion, since both the initial and target state are flat. Recalling that states with embedded distributions being flat are proportional to the thermal state on their support, we note that this case contains the conversion between sharp energy states as a special case. We will then consider the cases of distillation and formation, in which either the target or initial states are flat respectively. These are so-named because they contain both the distillation of, and formation from, sharp energy states. Finally we will consider the general interconversion problem, in which neither state is flat. We refer to the non-flat distribution case as general, because it in fact implies the three other results by using the limiting behaviours of the Rayleigh-normal distributions given in Eq. (
43
).

6.1
Central limit theorem

Before we present our proofs, we first formulate the main mathematical tool needed for such a small deviation analysis: a central limit theorem. Specifically, we want to give tail bounds on i.i.d. product distributions. Considering the standard central limit theorem, one can derive the following tail bound.

Lemma 15
.

For any distribution
\bm{a}
such that
V(\bm{a})>0
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i}\left\{\left(\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\right)_{i}\middle|\left(\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\right)_{i}\geq 1/k_{n}(x)\right\}=\Phi\left(x\right),

(102)

where
k_{n}(x):=\left\lfloor\exp\left(H(\bm{a}^{\otimes n})+x\sqrt{V(\bm{a}^{\otimes n})}\right)\right\rfloor
.

For completeness we provide the proof of the above known result in Appendix
E
. We will also rely on an alternate form of central limit theorem.

Lemma 16

(Lemma 12 of Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
)
.

For any distribution
\bm{a}
such that
V(\bm{a})>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}(x)}\left(\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\right)_{i}^{\downarrow}=\Phi(x),

(103)

where
k_{n}(x):=\left\lfloor\exp\left(H(\bm{a}^{\otimes n})+x\sqrt{V(\bm{a}^{\otimes n})}\right)\right\rfloor
.

We now want to convert the above result into the specific form of a bound we will use. For some rate
R(n)
, we define the
total
initial and target states as

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\bm{p}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{\eta}^{\otimes nR(n)},

(104a)

\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\bm{q}^{\otimes nR(n)}\otimes\bm{\eta}^{\otimes n}.

(104b)

We note that generally
R
depends on
n
, but will henceforth omit this explicit dependence.

We also introduce a quantity analogous to
k_{n}(x)
,

\displaystyle K_{n}(x):=\left\lfloor\exp\left(H(\bm{Q}^{n})+x\sqrt{V(\bm{Q}^{n})}\right)\right\rfloor,

(105)

that will be crucial in all our proofs. The central limit theorem for these distributions is given by the following result.

Lemma 17

(Central limit theorem for
\bm{P}^{n}
and
\bm{Q}^{n}
)
.

If
V(\bm{q})>0
and
R
is bounded away from zero, then
\bm{Q}^{n}
has the tail bound

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}(x)}Q^{n\downarrow}_{i}=\Phi(x).

(106)

Moreover, if we consider a rate of the form

\displaystyle{R_{\mu}(n)=\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\left[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{D(\bm{p})}{D(\bm{q})}\frac{V(\bm{q})}{n}}\,\mu\right],}

(107)

for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\bm{P}^{n}
also has a corresponding tail bound

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}(x)}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}=\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x),

(108)

with
\nu
given by Eq. (
101
).

Proof.

We start by noticing that
16
remains true if the product distribution is “smeared out”. Specifically, for any flat state
\bm{f}
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}(x)}\left(\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{f}\right)_{i}^{\downarrow}=\Phi(x),

(109)

where

\displaystyle\!k_{n}(x):=\left\lfloor\exp\left(H(\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{f})+x\sqrt{V(\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\otimes\bm{f})}\right)\right\rfloor\!.\!\!

(110)

Using this, if we make the substitutions
\bm{a}^{\otimes n}\leftarrow\bm{q}^{\otimes Rn}
and
\bm{f}\leftarrow\bm{\eta}^{\otimes n}
, recalling that
R
is bounded away from zero, we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}(x)}Q^{n\downarrow}_{i}=\Phi(x).

(111)

Applying the same argument to
\bm{P}^{n}
gives

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K^{P}_{n}(y)}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}=\Phi\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{V(\bm{p})}}\right),

(112)

where
K_{n}^{P}(y):=\left\lfloor\exp\left(H(\bm{P}^{n})+y\sqrt{n}\right)\right\rfloor
. Note that we did not include the variance in the definition of
K_{n}^{P}
(as we did in
K_{n}
), because we have not assumed
V(\bm{p})>0
. Indeed, if we interpret
\Phi(y/V(\bm{p}))
as cumulative of the zero-mean Dirac distribution for
V(\bm{p})=0
, then this also holds for
V(\bm{p})=0
.

We now want to express Eq. (
112
) as a summation up to
K_{n}(x)
for some
x
. Noticing that
R_{\mu}=D(\bm{p})/D(\bm{q})+o(1)
, our choice of rate
R_{\mu}
can be rearranged to give

\displaystyle H(\bm{Q}^{n})\simeq H(\bm{P}^{n})-\sqrt{n\frac{D(\bm{p})}{D(\bm{q})}V(\bm{q})}\mu.

(113)

Therefore,
K_{n}^{P}(y)=K_{n}(x)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{y}{\sqrt{V(\bm{p})}}

\displaystyle=x\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{Q}^{n})}{V(\bm{P}^{n})}}-\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\nu}}=\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\nu}}+o(1).

(114)

Finally, using the continuity of
\Phi
gives the desired tail bound

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}(x)}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}^{P}(y)}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}^{P}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\nu}}\right)}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\nu}}\right)=\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x).

(115)

Recalling that
\nu
is proportional to
V(\bm{p})
, we note that all of the above expressions are still well-defined if
V(\bm{p})=0
, where we understand
\Phi_{\mu,0}
to be the cumulative of the Dirac distribution with mean
\mu
.
∎

6.2
Bistochastic flat-to-flat

We start with the case of flat-to-flat conversion, where both the initial and target states are flat. In this boundary case, which was not considered in Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
, we can provide an exact single-shot expression for the optimal rate.

Proposition 18

(Bistochastic flat-to-flat)
.

For any initial state
\bm{p}
and target state
\bm{q}
such that
V(\bm{p})=V(\bm{q})=0
, and infidelity
\epsilon\in[0,1)
, the optimal interconversion rate is given by

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor\frac{nD(\bm{p})-\log(1-\epsilon)}{D(\bm{q})}\right\rfloor\simeq\frac{D(\bm{p})}{D(\bm{q})}.

(116)

Before proving this, we first consider the optimal majorising distribution in the case where all distributions involved are flat.

Lemma 19

(Single-shot flat-to-flat)
.

If we let
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
be distributions such that
V(\bm{a})=V(\bm{b})=0
, then

\displaystyle\min\left\{\epsilon\middle|\bm{a}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{b}\right\}=\max\left\{1-{\frac{\exp H(\bm{b})}{\exp H(\bm{a})}},0\right\}.

(117)

Proof.

First, from Lemma
12
we know that

\min\left\{\epsilon\middle|\bm{a}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{b}\right\}=\min\left\{\epsilon\middle|\bm{a}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\bm{b}\right\}.

(118)

Now, the right hand side of the above equation is minimised by a state
\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\star}
, whose explicit construction (found in Ref.
[
Vidal et al.(2000)Vidal,
Jonathan, and Nielsen
]
) we present in Appendix
B
while proving Lemma
11
. Using it one finds that

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\star\downarrow}=\begin{cases}\bm{a}^{\downarrow}&\text{if}~~\bm{a}\succ\bm{b},\\
\bm{b}^{\downarrow}&\text{if}~~\bm{a}\nsucc\bm{b}.\end{cases}

(119)

Since
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
are flat, in the first case we have

F(\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\star\downarrow},\bm{b}^{\downarrow})={\frac{\exp H(\bm{b})}{\exp H(\bm{a})}},

(120)

which leads to Eq. (
117
).
∎

We can now apply this to give an exact expression for the optimal rate.

Proof of
18
.

Applying
19
to
\bm{a}=\bm{P}^{n}
and
\bm{b}=\bm{Q}^{n}
we find that
\epsilon_{0}^{*}(n,R)
vanishes for any
R\leq D(\bm{p})/D(\bm{q})
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\bigl(1-\epsilon_{0}^{*}(n,R)\bigr)=D(\bm{p})-RD(\bm{q})

(121)

for any
R\geq D(\bm{p})/D(\bm{q})
. Converting the expression for the optimal infidelity into an expression for the optimal rate, and recalling that
nR^{*}(n,\epsilon)
must be an integer, gives
18
as required.
∎

6.3
Bistochastic distillation

We now consider distillation, in which the target state is flat.

Proposition 20

(Bistochastic distillation)
.

For any initial state
\bm{p}
and target state
\bm{q}
such that
V(\bm{q})=0
, and infidelity
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the optimal interconversion rate has the second-order expansion

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\simeq\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\Biggr].

(122)

Instead of utilising the techniques of Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
, we will, similar to the flat-to-flat case, prove
20
by first considering a single-shot expression for the optimal error.

Lemma 21

(Single-shot distillation)
.

Let
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
be distributions with
V(\bm{b})=0
. Then

\displaystyle\min\left\{\epsilon\middle|\bm{a}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{b}\right\}=\sum_{i>\exp H(\bm{b})}a_{i}^{\downarrow}.

(123)

Proof.

First, from Lemma
12
we know that

\min\left\{\epsilon\middle|\bm{a}\succ_{\epsilon}\bm{b}\right\}=\min\left\{\epsilon\middle|\bm{a}\prescript{}{\epsilon}{\succ}\bm{b}\right\}.

(124)

Now, to find a state minimising the right hand side of the above equation, consider a distribution
\bm{\tilde{a}}
such that
\bm{\tilde{a}}\succ\bm{b}
. Since
\bm{b}
is flat, this is equivalent to the statement that
\bm{\tilde{a}}
has a support which is no larger than that of
\bm{b}
. This condition is clearly necessary; it is sufficient as any distribution with
d
or fewer non-zero entries majorises the flat distribution over
d
entries. Using the Schwarz inequality one can then show that the distribution
\bm{\tilde{a}}
which contains at most
\exp H(\bm{b})
non-zero elements, and is closest to
\bm{a}
, is simply the truncated-and-rescaled distribution,

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{i}^{\downarrow}:=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{\exp(H(\bm{b}))}a^{\downarrow}_{j}}\begin{cases}a^{\downarrow}_{i}&\text{if}~~i\leq\exp H(\bm{b}),\\
0&\text{if}~~i>\exp H(\bm{b}).\end{cases}

(125)

It is a straightforward calculation to show that the infidelity of such a smoothed state is given by mass of the truncated tail

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{a},\bm{\tilde{a}})=\sum_{i>\exp H(\bm{b})}a_{i}^{\downarrow}.

(126)

∎

We can now use
17
to bound this tail, giving a second-order expansion for asymptotic case.

Proof of
20
.

Applying
21
to
\bm{a}=\bm{P}^{n}
and
\bm{b}=\bm{Q}^{n}
we find that

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}^{*}(n,R)=\sum\limits_{i>\exp H(\bm{Q}^{n})}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}.

(127)

Now consider a rate of the form

\displaystyle r_{\mu}(n):=\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\left[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p})}{n}}\mu\right],

(128)

for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. We then have

\displaystyle H(\bm{Q}^{n})=H(\bm{P}^{n})-\mu\sqrt{V(\bm{P}^{n})},

(129)

and so if we apply the first bound of
17
(with
\bm{P}^{n}
in place of
\bm{Q}^{n}
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{0}^{*}(n,r_{\mu})

\displaystyle=1-\Phi(-\mu)=\Phi(\mu).

(130)

Reversing the relationship between infidelity and rate, this implies

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\simeq\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\left[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\right],

(131)

as required.
∎

6.4
Bistochastic formation

For our final special case, we consider flat initial states.

Proposition 22

(Bistochastic formation)
.

For any initial state
\bm{p}
and target state
\bm{q}
such that
V(\bm{p})=0
, and infidelity
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the optimal interconversion rate has the second-order expansion

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\simeq\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{D(\bm{p})}{D(\bm{q})}\frac{V(\bm{q})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\Biggr].

(132)

This proof is more involved than distillation, and will involve some of the techniques used in the proof of the general interconversion problem, as first developed in Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
. As with distillation, we will attempt to bound the rate by bounding the optimal infidelity between the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
and a state
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
that majorises the total target state
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We will thus fix our rate
R_{\mu}(n)
to be given by Eq. (
107
) from
17
, and look for bounds on infidelity between
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
and
\bm{P}^{n}
. More precisely, our proof is split into two parts: achieveability (upper bound on optimal error/lower bound on optimal rate) and optimality (lower bound on optimal error/upper bound on optimal rate).

6.4.1
Achieveability

Sketch of construction.

The general idea here is to construct a distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
which is close to the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}

and
majorises total target state
\bm{Q}^{n}
. By the equivalence of pre- and post-majorisation, Lemma
12
, this will prove that there exists a distribution
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}
that is majorised by the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
and is close to the total target state
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We will start by defining two bins (sets) of indices,
B
and
B^{\prime}
. We will then construct a
scaled distribution

\bm{S}^{n}
such that for indices belonging to
B
it has the same shape as
\bm{P}^{n}
(i.e., it is flat), but has as much mass as
\bm{Q}^{n}
has over the indices belonging to
B^{\prime}
. The first property will guarantee that
\bm{S}^{n}
lies close to
\bm{P}^{n}
, and the second that it lies close to a
majorising distribution

\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
. We will then analyse
\delta(\bm{P}^{n},\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n})
, giving an upper bound on the optimal error.

Binning.

For some small
\zeta>0
, define two bins of indices

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\{1,\dots,K_{n}(\mu+\zeta/2)\},

(133a)

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\{K_{n}(\mu+\zeta),\dots,\infty\},

(133b)

where
\infty
is shorthand for the largest index, and
\mu
fixes the value of rate
R_{\mu}(n)
. We will consider
B
as a bin on the indices of
\bm{P}^{n}
and
B^{\prime}
on those of
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We denote the complements of these bins as
\bar{B}
and
\bar{B^{\prime}}
, respectively.

Scaled distribution
\bm{S}^{n}
.

For any
j\in B
define

\displaystyle S^{n}_{j}:=\frac{\sum_{k\in B^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{k}}{\sum_{k\in B}P^{n\downarrow}_{k}}\cdot P_{j}^{n\downarrow}=\frac{1}{\left|{B}\right|}\sum_{k\in B^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{k},

(134)

and
S^{n}_{l}:=1-\sum_{k\in B^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{k}
for some arbitrary
l\notin B
such that
\bm{S}^{n}
is normalised.

By construction the mass of
\bm{S}^{n}
on
B
is equal to that of
\bm{Q}^{n}
on
B^{\prime}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in B}S^{n}_{j}:=\sum_{j\in B^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{k}.

(135)

We now want to show that this implies the existence of a nearby majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
.

Majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
.

Instead of giving an explicit construction of
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
, we instead present an existence proof. Specifically we will leverage the following lemma:

Lemma 23
.

For non-negative vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
such that
\sum_{i}a_{i}=\sum_{i}b_{i}
, there exists a vector
\bm{\tilde{a}}
such that
\sum_{k}\tilde{a}_{k}=\sum_{k}a_{k}
,
\bm{\tilde{a}}\succ\bm{b}
and
\left\|\bm{\tilde{a}}-\bm{a}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\bm{b}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Consider a function over indices,
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, and the vector
\bm{\tilde{a}}
given by the follow action of
f
on
\bm{b}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{i}:=\sum_{j}\left\{b_{j}\middle|f(j)=i\right\}.

(136)

Clearly such a mapping can only concentrate a distribution, and so
\bm{\tilde{a}}\succ\bm{b}
. Now, among
\bm{\tilde{a}}
of the above form we choose that which is closest to
\bm{a}
in
l_{\infty}
-norm. Let
i
be an index at which the
l_{\infty}
-norm of
\bm{\tilde{a}}-\bm{a}
, denoted by
\Delta
, is achieved,

\displaystyle i\in\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{j}\left|{\tilde{a}_{j}-a_{j}}\right|.

(137)

We are going to assume that
\Delta>\left\|\bm{b}\right\|_{\infty}
and show that this would imply that
\bm{\tilde{a}}
cannot be optimal, proving
\left\|\bm{\tilde{a}}-\bm{a}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\bm{b}\right\|_{\infty}
by way of contradiction.

There are two cases to consider:
\tilde{a}_{i}>a_{i}
and
\tilde{a}_{i}<a_{i}
. We start with
\tilde{a}_{i}>a_{i}
. As
\tilde{a}_{i}>0
, there must exist some
\alpha
such that
f(\alpha)=i
and
b_{\alpha}>0
. As
\bm{a}\neq\bm{\tilde{a}}
and
\sum_{k}a_{k}=\sum_{k}\tilde{a}_{k}
, there must exist a
k
such that
\tilde{a}_{k}<a_{k}
. Consider changing the map to from
f(\alpha)=i
to
f(\alpha)=k\neq i
. This has the effect of lowering
\tilde{a}_{i}
by
b_{\alpha}
and raising
\tilde{a}_{k}
by the same amount. Given that
b_{\alpha}<\Delta
by assumption, this means that
\tilde{a}_{i}-a_{i}
changes from
\Delta
to
(0,\Delta)
, and
\tilde{a}_{k}-a_{k}
changes from
[-\Delta,0)
to
(-\Delta,\Delta)
. As such,
\left|{\tilde{a}_{i}-a_{i}}\right|
and
\left|{\tilde{a}_{j}-a_{j}}\right|
are now both strictly smaller than
\Delta
. Since all other entries of
\bm{\tilde{a}}
are unchanged, we have reduced the number of indices
j
such that
\left|{\tilde{a}_{j}-a_{j}}\right|=\Delta
by at least one. Similarly for the case
\tilde{a}_{i}<a_{i}
one can make an analogous argument by changing
f(\alpha)=k\neq i
to
f(\alpha)=k
for some
k
such that
\tilde{a}_{k}>a_{k}
. Iterating this we can keep decreasing the number of indices at which the norm is achieved, eventually giving us
\left|{\tilde{a}_{j}-a_{j}}\right|<\Delta
for all
j
, i.e.
\left|{\bm{\tilde{a}}-\bm{a}}\right|<\Delta
. This shows that the original choice of
\bm{\tilde{a}}
was not optimal as assumed, proving
\Delta\leq\left\|\bm{b}\right\|_{\infty}
by contradiction.
∎

With the use of the above lemma we can now get the desired majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
.

Lemma 24
.

There exists a distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
such that
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
and

\displaystyle\left|{\tilde{P}^{n}_{j}-S_{j}^{n}}\right|\leq 1/K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)

(138)

for all
j\in B
.

Proof.

The idea here is to apply
23
to the restriction of each distribution to its corresponding bin. Specifically if we take
\bm{a}:=\left.\bm{S}^{n}\right|_{B}
and
\bm{b}:=\left.\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}\right|_{B^{\prime}}
, then
23
gives us a vector
\bm{\tilde{a}}
such that
\bm{\tilde{a}}\succ\left.\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}\right|_{B^{\prime}}
and

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\tilde{a}}-\bm{a}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\bm{b}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left.\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}\right|_{B^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{j\in B^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle=Q^{n\downarrow}_{K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)}\leq 1/K_{n}(\mu+\zeta),

(139)

where the final inequality follows from normalisation of
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We now define our majorising distribution within bin
B
as
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\big|_{B}:=\bm{\tilde{a}}
, so that
|\tilde{P}^{n}_{j}-S^{n}_{j}|\leq 1/K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)
for any
j\in B
as desired; and, once again, in order to normalise the distribution we also define
\tilde{P}^{n}_{l}=1-\sum_{i}\tilde{a}_{i}
for some arbitrary
l\notin B
.

The fact that
\bm{\tilde{a}}
majorises the restriction of
\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}
to
B^{\prime}
, together with the sharpness of
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
outside of
B
, gives

\displaystyle\left.\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\right|_{B}\succ\left.\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}\right|_{B^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left.\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\right|_{\bar{B}}\succ\left.\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}\right|_{\bar{B}^{\prime}}.

(140)

Next, we note that majorisation spreads over direct sum, i.e.
\bm{\alpha}_{1}\succ\bm{\beta}_{1}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{2}\succ\bm{\beta}_{2}
implies
\bm{\alpha}_{1}\oplus\bm{\alpha}_{2}\succ\bm{\beta}_{1}\oplus\bm{\beta}_{2}
. This can be seen by using Theorem
3
(i.e., the equivalence of majorisation relation between two distribution with the existence of a bistochastic map between them), and noticing that bistochasticity is preserved under direct sum. Applying this to
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
gives the desired majorisation property:

\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}|_{B}\oplus\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}|_{\overline{B}}\succ\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}|_{B^{\prime}}\oplus\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}|_{\overline{B^{\prime}}}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
.
∎

Infidelity.

By now we have proven the existence of a majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
and bounded its distance from the scaled distribution
\bm{S}^{n}
on the restriction to
B
. The final step involves bounding the infidelity
\delta(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})
. To achieve this we will first show that the closeness of
\bm{S}^{n}
and
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
on
B
, as given in
24
, allows us to bound
F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})
in terms of
\bm{S}^{n}
.

Lemma 25
.

Asymptotically, the fidelity between the majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
and the total initial distribution
\bm{P}^{n}
can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n}\right)}\geq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{j\in B}\sqrt{S^{n}_{j}P^{n}_{j}}\right)^{2}\!\!.

(141)

Proof.

First, we apply
24
and the fact that
\sqrt{x-y}\geq\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}
for all
x\geq y\geq 0
to break the fidelity into the desired expression and an error term

\displaystyle\sqrt{F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow})}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j\in B}\sqrt{\tilde{P}^{n}_{j}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}

\displaystyle\hskip-35.56593pt\geq\sum_{j\in B}\sqrt{\max\{S^{n}_{j}-1/K_{n}(\mu+\zeta),0\}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}

\displaystyle\hskip-35.56593pt\geq\sum_{j\in B}\left[\sqrt{S^{n}_{j}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}-\sqrt{P^{n\downarrow}_{j}/K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)}\right].

(142)

We can express the second error term as

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in B}\sqrt{\frac{P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}{K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\left|{B}\right|}{K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{K_{n}(\mu+\zeta/2)}{K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)}}.

(143)

Given that
\zeta>0
is a constant and
V(\bm{q})>0
, we have that
K_{n}(\mu+\zeta/2)/K_{n}(\mu+\zeta)
is decaying exponentially as
n\to\infty
. Taking the limit inferior of Eq. (
142
) therefore gives the required bound.
∎

Using the above result on fidelity, we can now prove achieveability.

Proof of
22
(achieveability).

Substituting the definition of
\bm{S}^{n}
into
25
gives

\displaystyle{\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\!{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n}\right)}&\!\geq\!\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\!{\sum_{i\in B^{\prime}}\!Q^{n\downarrow}_{i}\sum_{j\in B}\!P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}.}

(144)

By applying
17
we then obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\Phi_{\mu,0}(\mu+{\zeta/2})=1,

(145a)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}1-\Phi(\mu+{\zeta}),

(145b)

and therefore

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n}\right)\leq\Phi(\mu+{\zeta}).

(146)

Due to the equivalence between pre- and post-majorisation, Lemma
12
, the above means that there exists a distribution
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}
that is majorised by the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
and such that

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta\left(\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n},\bm{Q}^{n}\right)\leq\Phi(\mu+{\zeta}).

(147)

As this is true for any
\zeta>0
we can take
\zeta\searrow 0
and conclude that the optimal infidelity is upper bounded

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{0}^{*}(n,R_{\mu})\leq\Phi(\mu),

(148)

which implies a corresponding lower bound on the optimal rate

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\gtrsim\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{D(\bm{p})}{D(\bm{q})}\frac{V(\bm{q})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\Biggr],

(149)

as required.
∎

6.4.2
Optimality

We now turn our attention to a corresponding second-order upper bound on the optimal rate. We will make use of the following lemma from Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
, which upper bounds the fidelity between a flat state and a majorising distribution.

Lemma 26

(Lemma 6 of Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
)
.

Let
\bm{a}
,
\bm{\tilde{a}}
and
\bm{b}
be any distributions such that
V(\bm{a})=0
and
\bm{\tilde{a}}\succ\bm{b}
. Also, let
M\leq\exp H(\bm{a})
. Then

\displaystyle\!\!\sqrt{F(\bm{\tilde{a}},\bm{a})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\leq N}a^{\downarrow}_{i}\sum_{j\leq N}b_{j}^{\downarrow}}+\sqrt{\sum_{i>N}a^{\downarrow}_{i}\sum_{j>N}b_{j}^{\downarrow}},

(150)

where
N:=\left|{\left\{i\middle|b_{i}\geq 1/M\right\}}\right|
.

We can now use the above lemma to obtain an optimality bound which matches that given for achieveability.

Proof of
22
(optimality).

Consider any distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
. Now choose
\bm{\tilde{a}}=\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
,
\bm{a}=\bm{P}^{n}
and
\bm{b}=\bm{Q}^{n}
. Also, notice that
M:=K_{n}(\mu-\zeta)
satisfies
M\leq\exp H(\bm{a})
. Hence, we can apply
26
to upper bound the fidelity,

\displaystyle\sqrt{F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i\leq N}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}\sum_{j\leq N}Q_{j}^{n\downarrow}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{\sum_{i>N}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}\sum_{j>N}Q_{j}^{n\downarrow}},

(151)

where
N:=\left|{\{i|Q^{n}_{i}\geq 1/K_{n}(\mu-\zeta)\}}\right|
. By the standard central limit theorem
15
, we have

\displaystyle\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle\!=\!\sum_{i}\left\{Q^{n\vphantom{\downarrow}}_{i}\middle|Q^{n}_{i}\!\geq\!\frac{1}{K_{n}(\mu-\zeta)}\right\}\!\to\!\Phi(\mu-\zeta).\!

(152)

The normalisation of
\bm{Q}^{n}
gives us that
N\leq K_{n}(\mu-\zeta)
, and so we can apply
17
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}(\mu-\zeta)}P^{n\downarrow}_{i}\to\Phi_{\mu,0}(\mu-\zeta)=0.

(153)

Applying these limits to Eq. (
151
) yields

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n}\right)\geq\Phi(\mu-\zeta)

(154)

for any
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
. Due to the equivalence between pre- and post-majorisation (c.f. Lemma
12
) the above means that for any distribution
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}
that is majorised by the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta\left(\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n},\bm{Q}^{n}\right)\geq\Phi(\mu-\zeta)

(155)

Taking
\zeta\searrow 0
this gives a lower bound on the optimal infidelity

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{0}^{*}(n,R_{\mu})\geq\Phi(\mu),

(156)

which implies a corresponding upper bound on the optimal rate

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\lesssim\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{D(\bm{p})}{D(\bm{q})}\frac{V(\bm{q})}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(\epsilon)\Biggr].

(157)

∎

6.5
Bistochastic interconversion

Finally we turn to the general case in which neither relative entropy variance is vanishing.

Proposition 27

(Bistochastic interconversion)
.

For any initial state
\bm{p}
and target state
\bm{q}
such that
V(\bm{p}),V(\bm{q})>0
, and infidelity
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the optimal interconversion rate has the second-order expansion

\displaystyle{R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\simeq\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p})}{n}}Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon)\Biggr],}

(158)

where
\nu
is given in
101
.

The proof is similar to that of formation, so it will also utilise many of the ideas inspired by Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
. The main complication is that for the general interconversion problem the binning of indices is more elaborate: we now have two
sets
of bins instead of two
individual
bins, and we need to introduce a function
A
which controls the relative placement of these bins. Once again we will break the proof into both achieveability and optimality bounds.

6.5.1
Achieveability

Sketch of proof.

As with formation, the idea here will be to give an explicit construction of
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
which is close to
\bm{P}^{n}
. Again, due to the equivalence of pre- and post-majorisation, this will prove that there exists a distribution
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}
that is majorised by the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
and is close to the total target state
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We will start by introducing two sets of bins for each distribution. Using these bins, we will once again construct a
scaled distribution

\bm{S}^{n}
which reflects the fine-grained features of
\bm{P}^{n}
(same shape within corresponding bins) and coarse-grained features of
\bm{Q}^{n}
(same mass within corresponding bins). We will then show that
\bm{S}^{n}
necessarily lies close to a
majorising distribution

\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
. Finally we will analyse the infidelity of this distribution with respect to the total initial distribution
\bm{P}^{n}
and, by taking the appropriate limits of the parameters in our construction, prove the desired achieveabilty bound of
27
.

In Section
6.4
our construction was parameterised by a single slack parameter
\zeta>0
. Here, we will have three parameters:
\lambda>0
,
I\in\mathbb{N}
, and a monotone continuously differentiable function
1\geq A\geq\Phi
pointwise. The parameter
\lambda
will control the width of our bins,
I
the number of bins, and
A
the relative placements of the two sets of bins.

Binning.

For
-I\leq i<I
we define our two sets of bins as

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle:=\{K_{n}(x_{{i}}),\dots,K_{n}(x_{i+1})-1\},

(159a)

\displaystyle B_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\{K_{n}(y_{{i}}),\dots,K_{n}(y_{i+1})-1\},

(159b)

where the two sequences are defined by

\displaystyle x_{i}:=\lambda\frac{i-1}{I}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{i}:=\Phi^{-1}\left(A\left(\lambda\frac{i+1}{I}\right)\right)

(160)

for
-I\leq i\leq I
. We will consider
B_{i}
as bins on the indices of
\bm{P}^{n}
and
B_{i}^{\prime}
on those of
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We note that
A\geq\Phi
implies
y_{i}\geq x_{i+1}+\lambda/I
, resulting in
B_{i}
being gapped away from
B_{i}^{\prime}
, i.e., all indices belonging to
B_{i}^{\prime}
are much larger than those belonging to
B_{i}
. This choice plays a role analogous to that of the slack parameter
\zeta
for the bins in
Section

6.4
. For convenience we also define

\displaystyle B:=\bigcup_{i=-I}^{I-1}B_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad B^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{i=-I}^{I-1}B_{i}^{\prime}

(161)

to be the union of bins, and
\overline{B}
and
\overline{B^{\prime}}
to be the corresponding complements.

Scaled distribution
\bm{S}^{n}
.

As in
Section

6.4
, we now define
\bm{S}^{n}
in each bin
B_{i}
to have the shape of
\bm{P}^{n}
within
B_{i}
, but the mass of
\bm{Q}^{n}
within
B_{i}^{\prime}
. As the bins
B_{i}
are all disjoint, for any
j\in B
there exists a unique
-I\leq i<I
such that
j\in B_{i}
. For such indices we define
\bm{S}^{n}
as

\displaystyle S^{n}_{j}:=\frac{\sum_{k\in B_{i}^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{k}}{\sum_{k\in B_{i}}P^{n\downarrow}_{k}}\cdot P^{n\downarrow}_{j}.

(162)

We normalise
\bm{S}^{n}
by taking
S^{n}_{l}:=1-\sum_{j\in B}S^{n}_{j}
for some arbitrary
l\notin B
.

Majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
.

We now want to prove a result analogous to
24
: the existence of a distribution that simultaneously majorises the total target distribution
\bm{Q}^{n}
and is close to
\bm{S}^{n}
(within each bin).

Lemma 28

(Existence of a majorising distribution)
.

There exists a distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
such that
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
and

\displaystyle\left|{\tilde{P}_{j}^{n}-S_{j}^{n}}\right|\leq 1/K_{n}(y_{i})

(163)

for all
j\in B_{i}
and
-I\leq i<I
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to that of
24
, with an application of
23
for each pair
\bm{S}^{n}|_{B_{i}}
and
\bm{Q}^{n}|_{B_{i}^{\prime}}
, with
-I\leq i<I
. This gives us
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
such that for all
-I\leq i<I
and
j\in B_{i}
it is close to
\bm{S}^{n}

\displaystyle\left|{\tilde{P}_{j}^{n}-S_{j}^{n}}\right|\leq\max_{j\in B_{i}^{\prime}}Q_{j}^{n\downarrow}\leq 1/K_{n}(y_{i}),

(164)

and possesses the majorisation properties

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}|_{B_{i}}

\displaystyle\succ\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}|_{B_{i}^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}|_{\bar{B}}\succ\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}|_{\bar{B^{\prime}}}.

(165)

Splitting the majorisation across the direct sum, as explained in the proof of
24
, gives us the desired overall majorisation

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}

\displaystyle\succ\bigoplus_{i=-I}^{I-1}\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}|_{B_{i}}\oplus\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}|_{\overline{B}}

\displaystyle\succ\bigoplus_{i=-I}^{I-1}\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}|_{B_{i}^{\prime}}\oplus\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}|_{\overline{B^{\prime}}}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}.

(166)

∎

Infidelity.

The next step involves bounding the fidelity between the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
and majorising distribution
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
given by the above construction. We will start by bounding the fidelity for a fixed set of parameters
A
,
\lambda
and
I
.

Lemma 29
.

For any monotone continuously differentiable function
1\geq A\geq\Phi
,
\lambda\geq 0
, and
I\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a sequence of distributions
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\left(\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x\!\right)^{\!2}\!\!,

(167)

with the prime superscript in
A^{\prime}
and
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}
denoting a derivative.

Proof.

The first part of the proof is analogous to the proof of
25
. More precisely using
28
(in place of
24
) and employing the fact that
B_{i}
is gapped away from
B_{i}^{\prime}
, we can apply the argument presented there to obtain

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n}\right)\geq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{j\in B}\sqrt{S^{n}_{j}P^{n}_{j}}\right)^{2}.

(168)

Inserting the definition of the scaled distribution
\bm{S}^{n}
yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow}\right)}

\displaystyle~\quad\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sqrt{S^{n}_{j}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}

\displaystyle~\quad=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{k\in B_{i}^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{k}}{\sum_{k\in B_{i}}P^{n\downarrow}_{k}}\cdot P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}\sqrt{P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}

\displaystyle~\quad=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}\sqrt{\sum_{j\in B_{i}^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{j}\sum_{k\in B_{i}}P^{n\downarrow}_{k}}.

(169)

Recalling that
\Phi(y_{i})=A(x_{i+2})
and applying
17
gives

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x_{i+1})-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x_{i}),

(170a)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\in B_{i}^{\prime}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{j}

\displaystyle=A(x_{i+3})-A(x_{i+2}).

(170b)

Substituting these into our lower bound on fidelity yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\geq\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}\sqrt{A(x_{i+3})-A(x_{i+2})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\times\sqrt{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x_{i+1})-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(x_{i})}

(171)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\geq\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}\sqrt{A\left(\frac{\lambda(i+2)}{I}\right)-A\left(\frac{\lambda(i+1)}{I}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\times\sqrt{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}\left(\frac{\lambda i}{I}\right)-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}\left(\frac{\lambda(i-1)}{I}\right)}.

Using the differentiability of
A
and
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}
we can express these finite differences as integrals

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}{\sqrt{\int_{\lambda\frac{i}{I}}^{\lambda\frac{i+1}{I}}A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\sqrt{\int_{\lambda\frac{i}{I}}^{\lambda\frac{i+1}{I}}\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x}}.

(172)

Finally, we apply the Schwarz inequality to arrive at the desired bound

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\!\!\sum_{i=-I}^{I-1}\int_{\lambda\frac{i}{I}}^{\lambda\frac{i+1}{I}}\!\!\!\!\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(173)

∎

Now, by taking the appropriate limits of our parameters
A
,
\lambda
and
I
, we will get the desired achieveability bound on the optimal infidelity, and therefore also on the optimal rate.

Proof of
27
(achieveability).

By
29
we know that there exists a family of distributions
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
majorising
\bm{Q}^{n}
and such that
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}F\left(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n\downarrow}\right)
is lower-bounded by

\displaystyle\left(\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}.

(174)

Due to the equivalence between pre- and post-majorisation, Lemma
12
, this means that there exists a family of distributions
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}
that is majorised by the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
and such that their fidelity with the total target state
\bm{Q}^{n}
is also lower bounded by the above expression, which implies a lower bound on the asymptotic ideal fidelity

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R_{\mu})}

\displaystyle~~\quad\geq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F\left({\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}},{\bm{Q}^{n\downarrow}}\right)}

\displaystyle~~\quad\geq\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(175)

As the left hand side is independent of
I
,
\lambda
and
A
, we can now take the desired limits. Note that the order of limits will be important: first we will take
I\to\infty
, then
\lambda\to\infty
, followed by a supremum over
A
.

Firstly, we take the limit inferior
I\to\infty
. As a consequence of the fact that
\lambda
is still finite, together with the continuous differentiability of
A
and
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}
, we have the point-wise limit

\displaystyle\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\sqrt{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\xrightarrow{I\to\infty}\sqrt{A^{\prime}(x)}\sqrt{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}(x)}.

(176)

Using the compactness of
[-2\lambda,2\lambda]
we can apply the dominated convergence theorem to move this limit inside the integral, which gives

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R_{\mu})}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\liminf\limits_{I\to\infty}\!\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x\!\!

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\lim\limits_{I\to\infty}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x+\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}\left(x-\frac{\lambda}{I}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(177)

Secondly, we want to take
\lambda\to\infty
. The existence of this limit follows from monotone convergence theorem, which we can apply due to the monotonicity of
A
and
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}
, together with the boundedness of the continuous fidelity. Taking the limit gives us a bound in terms of the continuous fidelity

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R_{\mu})}

\displaystyle\geq\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{A^{\prime}\left(x\right)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\mathcal{F}\left({A^{\prime}},{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}}\right)}.

(178)

Lastly, we want to take a supremum over all continuously differentiable monotone functions
1\geq A\geq\Phi
, which gives us the Rayleigh-normal distribution

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R_{\mu})

\displaystyle\!\leq\!1\!-\!\sup_{A\geq\Phi}\mathcal{F}\left({A^{\prime}},{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}}\right)\!=:\!Z_{\nu}(\mu).

(179)

Using the above and the duality property of Rayleigh-normal distributions, Eq. (
45
), we obtain the lower bound on the optimal rate

\displaystyle{R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\gtrsim\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p})}{n}}Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon)\Biggr].}

(180)

∎

6.5.2
Optimality

We now proceed to the proof of the optimality of
27
. To this end, we will employ two lemmas originally proved in Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
.
The idea is to start by showing that, after a particular coarse-graining, the fidelity between
\Phi
and
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}
is close to the optimal fidelity
\sup_{A\geq\Phi}\mathcal{F}(A^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu})={1-Z_{\nu}(\mu)}
.

Lemma 30

(Lemma 17 of Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
)
.

For any
\zeta>0
, there exist real numbers
s\leq t\leq t^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
such that
{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}/{\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}(x)}
is strictly monotone decreasing for
x\in(s,s^{\prime})
and

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(t)}{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(s)}{\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}(s)},

(181a)

\displaystyle\frac{1-\Phi(t^{\prime})}{1-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(s^{\prime})}{\Phi^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}(s^{\prime})}.

(181b)

Moreover, if we define
\mathcal{F}_{t,t^{\prime}}(\cdot,\cdot)
to be the fidelity of distributions which have been coarse-grained on
x\leq t
and
x\geq t^{\prime}
, specifically

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}_{t,t^{\prime}}(p,q)}:=

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sqrt{\int_{-\infty}^{t}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\int_{-\infty}^{t}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\sqrt{p(x)q(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\int_{t^{\prime}}^{\infty}p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\int_{t}^{\infty}q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}\,\,\Bigg)^{2}\!,

(182)

then this coarse-grained fidelity has an upper bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t,t^{\prime}}(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime})-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{A\geq\Phi}\mathcal{F}(A^{\prime},\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}).

(183)

Notice that
\Phi
and
\Phi_{\mu,\nu}
are exactly the distributions that appear in central limit theorem,
17
. Thus, we would like to relate the fidelity
F(\bm{P}^{n},\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n})
back to the Rayleigh-normal distribution via this coarse-grained fidelity between Gaussians. To be able to argue this for
any

\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
, we will first give a sufficient condition for a distribution
\bm{a}
to have the highest possible fidelity with respect to a second distribution
\bm{b}
among all distributions satisfying a majorisation-like condition.

Lemma 31

(Lemma 15 of Ref.
[
Kumagai and Hayashi(2017)
]
)
.

Let
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
be probability distributions such that
a_{i}/b_{i}
is strictly decreasing for all
i
. Then, for any distribution
\bm{c}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\quad\forall k,

(184)

we have

\displaystyle F({\bm{c}},{\bm{b}})\leq F({\bm{a}},{\bm{b}}),

(185)

with equality if and only if
\bm{c}=\bm{a}
.

We are now ready for the optimality proof.

Proof of
27
(optimality).

To prove optimality we need to show that for any
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}\succ\bm{Q}^{n}
, the infidelity between
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
and
\bm{P}^{n}
can be lower bounded by the Rayleigh-normal distribution. This, through Lemma
12
, will yield a lower bound on the infidelity between any final state
\bm{\tilde{Q}}^{n}
(i.e., any distribution majorised by the total initial state
\bm{P}^{n}
) and the total target state
\bm{Q}^{n}
. We will start by using the monotonicity of fidelity under coarse-graining to bound the fidelity between
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
and
\bm{P}^{n}
by the fidelity between their coarse-grained versions (with coarse-graining over particularly chosen bins of indices). We will then use
31
(along with the monotonicity properties of
30
) to bound the fidelity between coarse-grained versions of
\bm{P}^{n}
and
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
by the fidelity between coarse-grained versions of
\bm{P}^{n}
and
\bm{Q}^{n}
. Next, by applying
17
, we will show that this coarse-grained fidelity asymptotes to a fidelity between Gaussians. We will conclude by returning to the last part of
30
, which will allow us to give a final bound in terms of the Rayleigh-normal distribution.

Fix
\zeta>0
and
I\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
be those given by
30
and introduce

\displaystyle z_{i}:=t(1-i/I)+t^{\prime}(i/I)

(186)

for
0\leq i\leq I

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle:=\{K_{n}(z_{i}),\dots,K_{n}(z_{i+1})-1\}

(187)

for
0\leq i<I
, as well as two end bins

\displaystyle B_{-1}

\displaystyle:=\{1,\dots,K_{n}(t)-1\},

(188a)

\displaystyle B_{I}

\displaystyle:=\{K_{n}(t^{\prime}),\dots,\infty\},

(188b)

so that
\{B_{i}\}
now gives a partition of all indices. Using the monotonicity of fidelity under coarse-graining we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=-1}^{I}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\sqrt{\tilde{P}^{n\downarrow}_{j}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=-1}^{I}\sqrt{\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\tilde{P}^{n\downarrow}_{j}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}P^{n\downarrow}_{j}}=:r_{n}.

(189)

We now define the limiting coarse-grained versions of total initial and target distributions

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle:=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}Q^{n\downarrow}_{j},

(190a)

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle:=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}P^{n\downarrow}_{j},

(190b)

for
-1\leq i\leq I
. We note that the above limits all exist by

, specifically

\displaystyle a_{-1}

\displaystyle=\Phi(t),

(191a)

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle=\Phi(z_{i+1})-\Phi(z_{i}),

(191b)

\displaystyle a_{I}

\displaystyle=1-\Phi(t^{\prime}),

(191c)

\displaystyle b_{-1}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(t),

(191d)

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(z_{i+1})-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(z_{i}),

(191e)

\displaystyle b_{I}

\displaystyle=1-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(t^{\prime}).

(191f)

We would also like to analogously define a distribution
\bm{c}
that would be a coarse-grained version of
\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n}
, but we have no guarantees that the corresponding limits exist. In lieu of this, we will use the vectorial Bolzano-Weirestrass Theorem

5

5

We now want to apply

a_{i}/b_{i}
is strictly decreasing as required.

Now that we have shown that
a_{i}/b_{i}
is strictly decreasing, we can apply
31
. This gives us

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})\leq F(\bm{c},\bm{b})

\displaystyle\leq F(\bm{a},\bm{b}).

(196)

Expanding this out, we have

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{\Phi(z_{0})\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(z_{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=0}^{I-1}\sqrt{\Phi(z_{i+1})-\Phi(z_{i})}\sqrt{\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(z_{i+1})-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(z_{i})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{\left(\vphantom{\frac{num}{den}}1-\Phi(z_{I})\right)\left(1-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(z_{I})\vphantom{\frac{num}{den}}\right)}.

(197)

If we take
I\to\infty
, these finite differences above approach derivatives, and we get a bound in terms of a coarse-grained fidelity

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{F(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\sqrt{\Phi(t)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(t)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad~\quad+\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\sqrt{\Phi^{\prime}(x)\Phi_{\mu,\nu}^{\prime}(x)}~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad~\quad+\sqrt{\left(\vphantom{\frac{num}{den}}1-\Phi(t^{\prime})\right)\left(1-\Phi_{\mu,\nu}(t^{\prime})\vphantom{\frac{num}{den}}\right)}.

(198)

Finally, we apply the last part of
30
, which allows us to bound this in terms of the Rayleigh-normal distribution, giving

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta(\bm{\tilde{P}}^{n},\bm{P}^{n})\geq{Z_{\nu}(\mu)}-\zeta.

(199)

Taking
\zeta\searrow 0
, we can once more use the equivalence between pre- and post-majorisation, Lemma
12
, to conclude that

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R_{\mu})\geq Z_{\nu}(\mu).

(200)

Using the above together with the duality property of Rayleigh-normal distributions, Eq.17 (
45
), we obtain the upper bound on the optimal rate

\displaystyle R^{*}_{0}(n,\epsilon)\lesssim\frac{1}{D(\bm{q})}\Biggl[D(\bm{p})+\sqrt{\frac{V(\bm{p})}{n}}Z^{-1}_{1/\nu}(\epsilon)\Biggr].

(201)

Outlook

In this paper we have derived the exact second-order asymptotics of state interconversion under thermal operations between any two energy-incoherent states. It is then natural to ask whether such a characterisation is also possible for general, not necessarily energy-incoherent, states. Due to the fact that thermal operations are time-translation covariant, such that coherence and athermality form independent resources
[
Lostaglio et al.(2015b)Lostaglio, Jennings, and Rudolph
,
Lostaglio et al.(2015a)Lostaglio, Korzekwa, Jennings, and Rudolph
]
, it seems unlikely that the current approach can be easily generalised. Instead, one would need to rely on the full power of Gibbs-preserving maps
[
Faist et al.(2015)Faist, Oppenheim, and Renner
,
Korzekwa(2017)
]
that form a superset of the thermal operations. For such maps, we believe that a reasonable conjecture is in fact given by Eqs. (
1a
) and (
1b
), with the relative entropy and relative entropy variance replaced by their fully quantum analogues given in Refs.
[
Umegaki(1962)
]
and
[
Li(2014)
,
Tomamichel and Hayashi(2013)
]
, respectively.

We also provided a physical interpretation of our main result by considering several thermodynamic scenarios and explaining how our work can be employed to rigorously address the problem of thermodynamic irreversibility. We derived optimal values of distillable work and work of formation, and related them to the infidelity of these processes. This could potentially be used to clarify the notion of imperfect work
[
Åberg(2013)
,
Woods et al.(2015)Woods,
Ng, and Wehner
,
Ng et al.(2017)Ng,
Woods, and Wehner
]
, and to construct a comparison platform allowing one to continuously distinguish between work-like and heat-like forms of energy. We also discussed thermodynamic processes with finite-size working bodies, focusing particularly on the optimal performance of heat engines. We have shown that there are non-trivial conditions under which an engine can operate at Carnot efficiency and extract perfect work. This opens the possibility of engineering finite heat-baths and working bodies in order to minimise undesirable dissipation of free energy. Moreover, our formalism is general enough to address other interesting problems involving finite-size baths, like fluctuation theorems, Landauers’ principle or the third law of thermodynamics
[
Campisi et al.(2009)Campisi, Talkner, and Hänggi
,
Reeb and Wolf(2014)
,
Scharlau and Mueller(2018)
]
.

A number of natural technical extensions to our result suggest themselves. We have used the infidelity as our error measure, and conjecture that
10
will also hold when
\epsilon
is a bound on the total variational distance. Our second-order expansion falls into a larger class of results known as
small deviation
bounds, in which we consider a fixed error threshold
\epsilon
. Two natural extensions are to the regime of
large deviations

[
Hayashi(2006)
]
, in which a fixed rate is considered, and
moderate deviations

[
Chubb et al.(2017)Chubb,
Tan, and Tomamichel
,
Cheng and Hsieh(2017)
]
, in which the rate approaches its optimum
and
the error vanishes. Last, but not least, we expect that our treatment of approximate majorisation can be extended to cover other distance measures.

Appendix A
Work extraction with Carnot efficiency

Here we consider a thermodynamic process involving a heat engine with a finite working body. Suppose the working body starts at the cold temperature,
T_{c}
. Then, the heat flowing from the hot bath will steadily increase the temperature of the working body, from
T_{\mathrm{c}}
to
T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}
. We assume that thermalisation happens on a much shorter time-scale than the heat flow, so that the working body is at all times in thermal equilibrium. We are now interested in the amount of work that can be extracted if the engine at each time step operates with the maximal allowed Carnot efficiency (with respect to the background hot temperature and the instantaneous temperature of the working body).

By definition, the efficiency of an infinitesimal process involving the flow of heat
dQ_{\mathrm{in}}
from the hot bath to the engine,
dQ_{\mathrm{out}}
of which flows out to the cold bath, while the remaining energy is converted into work
dW
, is given by

\eta:=\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}W+\mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{out}}},

(202)

where the equality comes from the conservation of energy. Hence, the work extracted during the infinitesimal flow of heat
dQ_{\mathrm{out}}
into the cold bath at temperature
T_{\mathrm{x}}
that heats it up by
dT_{\mathrm{x}}
,

\mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}}}{\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}},

(203)

is given by

\mathrm{d}W=\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\,\mathrm{d}Q_{\mathrm{out}}.

(204)

The Carnot efficiency of an engine acting between the hot bath at fixed temperature
T_{\mathrm{h}}
and the working body at varying temperature
T_{\mathrm{x}}
is given by

\eta_{\mathrm{c}}(T_{\mathrm{x}})=1-\frac{T_{\mathrm{x}}}{T_{\mathrm{h}}}.

(205)

Thus, the work produced by an engine working at maximum allowed efficiency is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W=\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{h}}}{T_{\mathrm{x}}}-1\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}}}{\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}.

(206)

We now want to calculate the total work
W
extracted by such an optimal engine while the temperature of the working body changes from
T_{\mathrm{c}}
to
T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}
,

\displaystyle W=\int_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}^{T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}\mathrm{d}W.

(207)

By simply integrating by parts we get

\displaystyle\!\!W\!=\!\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{h}}}{T_{\mathrm{x}}}-1\right)\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}}\Biggr|_{T_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{c}}}^{T_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}\!+T_{\mathrm{h}}\int_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}^{T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}\frac{\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}}}{T_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}.\!

(208)

The second term can be calculated by switching from temperature
T_{\mathrm{x}}
to inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathrm{x}}
and recalling that the average energy is a negative derivative of
\log{\cal Z}_{\mathrm{x}}
over
\beta_{\mathrm{x}}
:

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{h}}\int_{T_{\mathrm{c}}}^{T_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}\frac{\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}}}{T_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}T_{\mathrm{x}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{h}}}\int_{\beta_{\mathrm{c}}}^{\beta_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\log{\cal Z}_{\mathrm{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\beta_{\mathrm{x}}}\,\mathrm{d}\beta_{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathrm{h}}}\left(\log{\cal Z}_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}-\log{\cal Z}_{\mathrm{c}}\right).

(209)

By noting that the entropy of a thermal equilibrium state is given by

\displaystyle H(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}})=\beta_{\mathrm{x}}\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{x}}}+\log{\cal Z}_{\mathrm{x}},

(210)

we thus have

\displaystyle W=\left(\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}}}-\frac{H(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}})}{\beta_{\mathrm{h}}}\right)-\left(\langle E\rangle_{\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}}-\frac{H(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}})}{\beta_{\mathrm{h}}}\right).

(211)

Finally, comparing the above with Eq. (
25
) we arrive at

\displaystyle W=k_{B}T_{\mathrm{h}}\bigl(D(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})-D(\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{c^{\prime}}}||\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{h}})\bigr),

(212)

which is equal to the change of free energy of the finite bath.

Appendix C
Efficient algorithm for calculating interconversion infidelities

The construction given in
Appendix

B
gives a natural algorithm for calculating
\max_{\bm{\tilde{p}}:\bm{\tilde{p}}\succ\bm{q}}F(\bm{p},\bm{\tilde{p}})
. The run-time of this algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
, where
d
is the size of the input distributions. We now want to argue that this algorithm can be adapted for states described by
\bm{p}^{\otimes n}
, such that the optimal interconversion rates can be numerically calculated in a time which is efficient in
n
, as is done in
Figures

4
and
10
.

The key property we will leverage is that whilst distributions such as
\bm{p}^{\otimes n}
have an exponential number of entries, they only possess a polynomial number of
distinct
entries (in this case
\mathcal{O}(n^{d-1})
). As majorisation is invariant under permutations, it is only the distinct entries (and their degeneracies) that are relevant to our calculation.

Consider taking as input two distributions
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
which possess
D=\exp(\mathcal{O}(n))
total entries, but each only
\mathrm{poly}(n)
distinct entries. This means there exists indices
1=i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{t}<i_{t+1}=D+1
, where
t=\mathrm{poly}(n)
, such that
P^{\downarrow}_{i}
and
Q^{\downarrow}_{i}
are constant on each interval
i\in\{i_{s},\dots,i_{s+1}-1\}
for
s\in\{1,\dots,t\}
.

The main step in the algorithm, and the bottleneck giving an exponential run-time, is calculating the pivot indices
\{l_{j}\}_{j}
used to construct
\bm{\tilde{P}}
. Specifically these take the form

\displaystyle l_{j}:=\argmax_{k<l_{j-1}}\frac{\sum_{i=k}^{l_{j-1}-1}Q^{\downarrow}_{i}}{\sum_{i=k}^{l_{j-1}-1}P^{\downarrow}_{i}}.

(230)

Using the constancy of
P^{\downarrow}_{i}
and
Q^{\downarrow}_{i}
on each of the intervals we can see that, for a fixed
s
, the function being optimised takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=k}^{l_{j-1}-1}Q^{\downarrow}_{i}}{\sum_{i=k}^{l_{j-1}-1}P^{\downarrow}_{i}}=\frac{\alpha k+\beta}{\gamma k+\delta},

(231)

for any
k\in\{i_{s},\dots,i_{s+1}-1\}
. As this function is monotonic as a function of
i
, we conclude that the indices
l_{j}
must lie on the edges of these intervals, i.e.
\{l_{j}\}_{j}\subseteq\{i_{s}\}_{s}
. This means that we can restrict our attention only to the these ‘edge indices’ without loss of generality, lowering the algorithms run-time down from
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
to
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
.

Using the above argument, we now have an efficient algorithm for computing
\epsilon^{*}_{0}(n,R;\bm{p},\bm{q})
. Utilising the idea of embedding, this also allows us to calculate the thermal variant of this,
\epsilon^{*}_{\beta}(n,R;\bm{p},\bm{q})
. Finally, by sweeping over
R
, we can use this to calculate
R^{*}_{\beta}(n,R;\bm{p},\bm{q})
. Examples of this are shown in
Figures

4
and
10
.
