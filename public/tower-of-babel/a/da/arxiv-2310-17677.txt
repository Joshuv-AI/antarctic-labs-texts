Validation of Spherical Fourier-Bessel power spectrum analysis with lognormal simulations and eBOSS DR16 LRG EZmocks
Henry S. Grasshorn Gebhardt, Olivier Doré

Abstract
Tuning into the bass notes of the large-scale structure requires careful attention to geometrical effects arising from wide angles. The spherical Fourier-Bessel (SFB) basis provides a harmonic-space coordinate system that fully accounts for all wide-angle effects. To demonstrate the feasibility of the SFB power spectrum, in this paper we validate our SFB pipeline by applying it to lognormal, and both complete and realistic EZmock simulations that were generated for eBOSS DR16 LRG sample. We include redshift space distortions and the local average effect (aka integral constraint). The covariance matrix is obtained from 1000 EZmock simulations, and inverted using eigenvalue decomposition.



I
Introduction

Upcoming galaxy surveys such as SPHEREx, Euclid, DESI, PFS, and others will
have significant constraining power on very large scales. This will allow
constraints on local-type primordial non-Gaussianity that is predicted by
inflation.

The typical power spectrum multipole estimator was introduced by

Yamamoto et al. 2006

(for a review and implementation
see
Hand et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. In the Yamamoto estimator, a single
line-of-sight (LOS) is chosen for each pair of galaxies, and for efficient
implementation that LOS is typically chosen to one of the galaxies in each
pair. However, while the Yamamoto estimator is a significant improvement over
the assumption of a flat sky with a fixed LOS, it still suffers from wide-angle
effects that can mimic a non-Gaussianity parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}\sim 5

(
Benabou et al. prep
)
. Further extensions to the Yamamoto estimator
such as using the midpoint or bisector between the galaxy pair as the LOS

(
Philcox and Slepian 2021
)
are also possible. However, while
certainly less, those estimators, too, will contain wide-angle effects.

The spherical Fourier-Bessel decomposition fully accounts for all wide-angle
effects, because it is the natural coordinate system for the radial/angular
separation.
(
Heavens and Taylor 1995
;
Percival et al. 2004
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
)

In this paper we validate the SFB power spectrum estimator developed in

Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
on the realistic eBOSS DR16
luminous red galaxy EZmocks
(
Zhao et al. 2021
)
.
For the covariance matrix we use all 1000 EZmocks.

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section

II
we review the SFB
basis, in
Section

III
we review our
SuperFaB
estimator, in

Section

IV
we detail the theoretical modeling including window,
weights, shot noise, local average effect, and pixel window. We validate our
analysis pipeline with lognormal mocks in
Section

V
, and with EZmocks in

Section

VI
. The likelihood is constructed and run with an adaptive
Metropolis-Hastings sampler in
Section

VII
. We conclude in

Section

VIII
. Many details such as a derivation of the local average
effect and velocity boundary conditions are delegated to the appendices.

II
Spherical Fourier-Bessel basis

Similar to the Cartesian basis power spectrum analysis, the spherical
Fourier-Bessel analysis also separates scales according to a wavenumber
k
.
This stems from the Laplacian eigenequation
\nabla^{2}f=-k^{2}f
that defines
both bases, with the difference coming mainly from the geometry of the boundary
conditions.

The spherical Fourier-Bessel basis offers several advantages and disadvantages
over the more familiar Cartesian-space analysis. The two chief advantages of SFB
is in the separation of angular and radial modes due to the use of spherical
polar coordinates, and in the complete modeling of wide-angle effects. Many
observational effects are local in origin, and, thus, manifest themselves
purely as angular systematics. Wide-angle effects come from the geometry of the
curved sky, and they have a similar signature as local non-Gaussianity
in the endpoint estimator

(
Yamamoto et al. 2006
;
Bianchi et al. 2015
;
Scoccimarro 2015
;
Hand et al. 2017
;
Castorina and White 2020
;
Beutler et al. 2019
)
. Due to the use
of a spherical coordinate system and spherical boundaries, SFB is well-suited
for both these types of systematics.

Besides the lesser familiarity of the SFB power spectrum compared to
Cartesian-space power spectrum multipoles, the SFB power spectrum suffers from
an increased computational cost due to the lack of the fast Fourier-transform,
and it suffers from an increase in the number of modes. The large number of
modes contains a wealth of information that may be exploited, such as
distinguishing between redshift space distortions and evolution of galaxy bias
and growth rate with redshift, to name a few. However, the presence of so many
modes also presents challenges in the analysis.

The spherical Fourier-Bessel basis is defined by the eigenfunctions of the
Laplacian in spherical polar coordinates with spherical boundaries. Thus, the
density contrast is expanded in SFB coefficients as

(
Nicola et al. 2014
, e.g.)

\displaystyle\delta({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\ell m}g_{n\ell}(r)\,Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta_{n\ell m}\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\delta_{n\ell m}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{r}}\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\delta({\bm{r}})\,,

(2)

where the second is the inverse of the first, and
Y_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})
are
spherical harmonics, and

\displaystyle g_{n\ell}(r)=c_{n\ell}\,j_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}r)+d_{n\ell}\,y_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}r)

(3)

are linear combinations of spherical Bessel functions of the first and second
kind, chosen to satisfy the orthonormality relation

\displaystyle\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,g_{n^{\prime}\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{nn^{\prime}}\,.

(4)

The index
n
denotes the wavenumber
k_{n\ell}
, and
c_{n\ell}
and

d_{n\ell}
are constants
(
Samushia 2019
;
Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
)
. For comparison, we include the SFB
transform over an infinite flat universe in

Eqs.

35
and
36
.

In this paper, we assume potential boundaries

(
Fisher et al. 1995
)
which ensure that the density contrast is
continuous and smooth on the boundaries. Further, we assume a spherical
boundary at some minimum distance
r_{\mathrm{min}}
and some maximum distance
r_{\mathrm{max}}

so that the SFB analysis is performed in a thick shell between
r_{\mathrm{min}}
and

r_{\mathrm{max}}

(
Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
;
Samushia 2019
)
. We have also verified that velocity boundary
conditions (that ensure vanishing derivative at the boundary) give essentially
the same result, see
Section

V.2.3
.

III
SuperFaB
estimator

For our SFB estimator we use
SuperFaB
. We refer the interested reader to

Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
for all the details, and give
only a very short overview here. In brief,
SuperFaB
is similar to the
3DEX approach developed in
Leistedt et al. 2012
.

SuperFaB
assumes the number density is given by a discrete set of
points,

\displaystyle n({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\delta^{D}({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}_{p})\,,

(5)

where the sum is over all points (galaxies) in the survey.
To calculate the fluctuation field, we include a weighting

(
Feldman et al. 1994
)

\displaystyle w({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+{\bar{n}}\,W({\bm{r}})\,C_{\ell nn^{\prime}}}\,,

(6)

and we approximate
C_{\ell nn^{\prime}}\sim${10}^{4}\text{\,}{\mathrm{\text{$h$}}}^{-3}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Mpc}}^{3}$
,
so that the observed density fluctuation field is

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=w({\bm{r}})\,\frac{n({\bm{r}})-\alpha\,n_{r}({\bm{r}})}{{\bar{n}}_{\mathrm{max}}}\,,

(7)

where
n({\bm{r}})
is the observed number density and
n_{r}({\bm{r}})
is a uniform
random catalog subject to the same window function and systematics.

{\bar{n}}_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum number density in the data catalog, in our
convention.

To perform the discrete SFB transform
Eq.

2
, we
first perform the radial integral for each
(n,\ell)
combination by directly
summing over the galaxies, and pixelizing on the spherical sky using the
HEALPix scheme
(
Górski et al. 2005
)
. The angular integration is
then performed using
HEALPix.jl

(
Tomasi and Li 2021
)
.

This SFB transform is performed for both the data catalog and the random
catalog, and the result is subtracted with appropriate normalization to obtain
the fluctuation field
\hat{\delta}_{n\ell m}
.

Finally, we construct the pseudo-SFB power spectrum

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{wWA}_{\ell nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m}\hat{\delta}_{n\ell m}\hat{\delta}^{*}_{n^{\prime}\ell m}\,,

(8)

where we attach the suffixes ‘
wW
’ for the weight and window, and the suffix
‘
A
’ for the local average effect, and the caret (
\hat{\phantom{x}}
)
indicates estimation from data. Window function and other effects will be
forward-modeled, and we turn to that next.

IV
SFB Power Spectrum Model

In this section we detail our approach for calculating a theoretical model for
the estimator of
Section

III
. We start with a full-sky calculation, and
then add the window function, weights, local average effect (LAE), and pixel
window. In the
window function
we generally include survey geometry and
radial selection function.

In the SFB basis, the full-sky linear power spectrum
C_{\ell nn^{\prime}}\simeq C_{\ell}(k_{n\ell},k_{n^{\prime}\ell})
on the lightcone in redshift space is expressed
as
(
Khek et al. 2022
)

\displaystyle C_{\ell nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,\mathcal{W}_{n\ell}(q)\,\mathcal{W}_{n^{\prime}\ell}(q)\,\gamma^{-3}\,P_{m}(\gamma^{-1}q)\,,

(9)

where
P_{m}(q)
is the matter power spectrum,
\gamma
is an
Alcock-Paczynski-like parameter, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n\ell}(q)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,q\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,w(r)\,\phi(r)\,D(r)

\displaystyle\quad\times e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{u}^{2}q^{2}\partial_{qr}^{2}}\left[b(r,q)\,j_{\ell}(qr)-f(r)\,j^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(qr)\right],

(10)

with the radial basis functions
g_{n\ell}(r)
defined in
Eq.

3
,
where
w(r)
is a weighting function,
\phi(r)
is a radial selection function,

D(r)
is the linear growth factor,
b(r,q)
is a scale-dependent linear galaxy
bias,
\sigma_{u}^{2}
is a velocity dispersion, and
f
is the linear growth rate.

The velocity dispersion term we approximate by expanding the exponential
operator (that acts on the spherical Bessels only) in a Taylor series to get

\displaystyle e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{u}^{2}q^{2}\partial_{qr}^{2}}j^{(d)}_{\ell}(qr)

\displaystyle\approx j^{(d)}_{\ell}(qr)-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{u}^{2}q^{2}\,j^{(d+2)}_{\ell}(qr)\,,

(11)

where
j_{\ell}^{(d)}(qr)
is the
d
th derivative w.r.t.
qr
.
1
1

1

Another
reasonably well-performing approximation is
(
Khek et al. 2022
)

\displaystyle e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{u}^{2}q^{2}\partial_{qr}^{2}}j^{(d)}_{\ell}(qr)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{u}^{2}q^{2}}j^{(d)}_{\ell}(qr)\,,

(12)

which is motivated by two extreme cases: when
q
is small, the exponential
operator does nothing; if it is large, the convolution implied by the
exponential operator averages over many oscillations, thus leading to a
vanishing value. However, for large
\ell
this approximation suppresses power
significantly.

The velocity dispersion is a combination of Fingers-of-God (FoG) effect and
redshift measurement uncertainty,
\sigma_{u}^{2}=\sigma_{u,\mathrm{FoG}}^{2}+\sigma_{z}^{2}
.
The FoG effect is modeled to be proportional to the linear growth rate
f(z)
.
The redshift measurement uncertainty is kept constant
1.05\text{\,}{\mathrm{\text{$h$}}}^{-1}\text{\,}\mathrm{Mpc}
in our model, taken
from
Ross et al. 2020
.

Local non-Gaussianity is modeled via a scale-dependent bias

(
Dalal et al. 2008
)

\displaystyle b(r,q)

\displaystyle=b(r)+f_{\mathrm{NL}}b_{\phi}(r)\,\frac{3\Omega_{m}H_{0}^{2}}{2q^{2}T(q)\bar{D}(r)}\,,

(13)

where for this paper we assume the universal bias relation

(
Dalal et al. 2008
;
Slosar et al. 2008
;
Afshordi and Tolley 2008
;
Matarrese and Verde 2008
)

\displaystyle b_{\phi}(r)

\displaystyle=2\delta_{c}\,[b(r)-1]\,,

(14)

T(q)
is the transfer function, and
\bar{D}(r)
is the growth factor
normalized to the scale factor
a
during matter domination,

\displaystyle\bar{D}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{D(r)}{(1+z_{\mathrm{md}})\,D(r_{\mathrm{md}})}\,,

(15)

where the “
\mathrm{md}
” suffix indicates a time deep within matter domination.

In its most complete form, the SFB power spectrum contains off-diagonal terms
where
k_{n\ell}\neq k_{n^{\prime}\ell}
. In a homogeneous universe we would have

C_{\ell}(k,k^{\prime})\,\propto\,\delta^{D}(k-k^{\prime})
. However, both redshift space
distortions and growth of structure on the light cone break homogeneity in the
observed galaxy sample. For details we refer the reader to

Pratten and Munshi 2013
;
Pratten and Munshi 2014
;
Khek et al. 2022
.

The bulk of the computation is spent on the spherical Bessel functions in

\mathcal{W}_{n\ell}(q)
in
Section

IV
. However, those only depend on the
combination
qr
, not on
q
and
r
separately. Thus, we choose
discretizations for
q
and
r
such that in
q
-
r
space the “iso-
qr
”
lines go precisely through grid points. This condition demands that the
discretizations for
q
and
r
are logarithmic, i.e.,

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle=\bar{q}_{\mathrm{min}}\,R^{i\Delta n}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle r_{j}

\displaystyle=\bar{r}_{\mathrm{min}}\,R^{j}\,,

(17)

where
\bar{q}_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\bar{r}_{\mathrm{min}}
are the central values of the lowest
bins,
\ln R
is the spacing in log-space, and
\Delta n\geq 1
is an integer
to allow sparse sampling of
q
.
Then,
qr
is sampled at only a few discrete
m=i\Delta n+j
,

\displaystyle(qr)_{m}

\displaystyle=\bar{q}_{\mathrm{min}}\bar{r}_{\mathrm{min}}\,R^{i\Delta n+j}\,.

(18)

This transforms the problem of calculating the spherical Bessels from a
2-dimensional to a 1-dimensional problem.

IV.1
Window function convolution

The window function limits the density contrast to that observed by a survey

\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}({\bm{r}})=W({\bm{r}})\,\delta({\bm{r}})
, where we define the window

W({\bm{r}})=\bar{n}({\bm{r}})/{\bar{n}}_{\mathrm{max}}
. In SFB space the window effect becomes a
convolution,

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}_{n\ell m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{NLM}W_{n\ell m}^{NLM}\,\delta_{NLM}\,,

(19)

where the coupling matrix is

\displaystyle W_{n\ell m}^{n^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,g_{n^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}(r)

\displaystyle\quad\times\int\mathrm{d}^{2}{\hat{\bm{r}}}\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,W(r,{\hat{\bm{r}}})\,.

(20)

As a result, the modes of the SFB power spectrum are coupled,

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\ell nn^{\prime}}=\sum_{LNN^{\prime}}\mathcal{M}_{\ell nn^{\prime}}^{LNN^{\prime}}\,C_{LNN^{\prime}}\,,

(21)

where we calculate the coupling matrix
\mathcal{M}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\ell nn^{\prime}}^{LNN^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{mM}W_{n\ell m}^{NLM}W_{n^{\prime}\ell m}^{N^{\prime}LM,*}\,.

(22)

However, in practice
Sections

IV.1
and
22
can be evaluated more efficienctly
as described in
Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
.

For the eBOSS data set, it is sufficient to assume a separable window,

\displaystyle W({\bm{r}})

\displaystyle=M({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\phi(r)\,,

(23)

where
M({\hat{\bm{r}}})
is an angular window, and
\phi(r)\,\propto\,n(z)
is the
radial selection function.

IV.2
FKP weights

The effect of adding arbitrary weights
w({\bm{r}})
, e.g., for optimizing the
statistical power similar to
Feldman et al. 1994
as in

Eq.

7
is the following.

Since in the limit of infinitely many galaxies both the observed number density

n({\bm{r}})
and the random catalog density
n_{r}({\bm{r}})
are proportional the window

W({\bm{r}})
,
Eq.

7
implies that the observed density
contrast is related to the true underlying theoretical density contrast by

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}=wW\delta\,.

(24)

However, the window
W({\bm{r}})
and the weights
w({\bm{r}})
enter the observed power
spectrum differently. In particular, for the clustering signal we simply change

W\to wW
, e.g., in
Eq.

19
. On the other hand, the shot noise
changes from
N\,\propto\,W
to
N\,\propto\,w^{2}W
. We leave the detailed
argument to
Appendix

B
.

IV.3
Local average effect

The local average effect or integral constraint comes from measuring the number
density of galaxies from the survey itself. This implies that the observed
density contrast in the survey is measured relative to the average true density
contrast. That is,
\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}({\bm{r}})\sim\delta({\bm{r}})-\bar{\delta}(z)
, where

\bar{\delta}(z)
is the average density contrast in a thin redshift bin. This
removes power from the largest angular scales. As a result the observed
clustering and shot noise power spectra are given by four terms each

\displaystyle C^{wWA}

\displaystyle=C^{1}-C^{23}-C^{4}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle N^{wWA}

\displaystyle=N^{1}-N^{23}-N^{4}\,,

(26)

where the terms on the right-hand side are derived in

Appendix

C
. In practice, all but the

C^{1}
and
N^{1}
terms essentially vanish for
\ell\gtrsim 2/f_{\mathrm{sky}}
.

IV.4
Pixel window

With our
SuperFaB
estimator only angular modes are affected by the
HEALPix pixelization scheme
Górski et al. 2005
. We include the
HEALPix pixel window in the theoretical modeling after the window and weights
convolution, and before adding the shot noise, since shot noise is not affected
by the pixel window.

To ensure the presence of the local average effect (
Section

IV.3

generate uniform random catalogs that match the radial number density
distribution of each “data” catalog in the following way. For each redshift
bin we draw exactly the same number of galaxies as in the data catalog. We
adhere to the angular varying-depth window at that redshift by first uniformly
drawing galaxies on the full sky, then rejecting points with probability
proportional to the depth of the window. In this way a random catalog is
created with the exact same redshift distribution as the data catalog (within
the resolution of the analysis), and an angular distribution matching the
angular window at each redshift.

Local average effect modeling

To better assess if there is a relevant difference between the boundary
conditions,

Since we use lognormal simulations that are inaccurate especially on small

VII
MCMC fitting and Covariance matrix

In this section we test our likelihood construction and MCMC fitting. The
covariance matrix is estimated from 1000 realistic EZmocks

(
Zhao et al. 2021
)
. We briefly describe how we invert such a
noisy covariance matrix using the eigenvector method outlined in

Wang et al. 2020
, and then we apply that to a few realizations
of the EZmocks.

Assuming the SFB power spectrum modes are Gaussian distributed, we construct
the likellihood as follows.

\displaystyle-2\ln\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{const}+(\hat{C}-C)^{T}M^{-1}(\hat{C}-C)\,,

(27)

where
\hat{C}
is the vector of measured
C_{\ell nn^{\prime}}
values,
C
is the SFB
power spectrum model and
M
is the covariance matrix.

VII.1
On the inversion of noisy convariance matrices

Figure 13:
Eigenvalues of the full covariance matrix estimated from 1000
EZmocks, sorted from smallest to largest, dashed means negative. The
largest 1000 eigenvalues are well-constrained. The smaller eigenvales are
consistent with numerical machine-level noise around a mean of zero. The
grey line indicates the cutoff for which we use eigenvalues in the matrix
inversion.

A covariance matrix estimated from simulations is afflicted by noise. In our
case, the number of modes exceeds the number of simulations. Thus, only 1000
eigenvalues of the covariance matrix are measured from the 1000 simulations,
and the rest are zero within machine precision. An example is shown in

Fig.

13
.

Therefore, the inverse of the measured covariance matrix will be dominated by
the noise of the unmeasured eigenvalues. To solve this we use the approach outlined in
Wang et al. 2020

that ensures that the inverted matrix is dominated by well-constrained
eigenmodes. The assumption is that the eigenvectors with large eigenvalues are
the best determined. Thus, only the eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues
are retained.

There are a few unfortunate consequences of this approach.
First, the modes
that have a small variance and, thus, would contribute most to the likelihood
are thrown away. However, here we are interested in the largest scales, and
these modes tend to have larger uncertainties due to the limited volume in the
survey. Thus, we expect these large modes to be retained in this approach.
Second, among the modes with large uncertainties, the noise in the covariance
estimate means that there is some stochasticity as to the exact modes that get
retained. Some modes will be over estimated, and those will definitely be
retained, which merely degrades the constraints. However, it is also possible
(e.g., if the numbers of modes is similar to the number of simulations) that some
covariance entries get underestimated, in which case we could get biased
constraints. We leave a more detailed investigation to a future paper.

The inversion works essentially as a lossy compression algorithm.
First, the variable condition number of an eigenmode
i
is defined as

\displaystyle\varrho_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}}{\lambda_{i}}\,,

(28)

where
\lambda_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{N}=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}
are the eigenvalues
sorted from smallest to largest. We then keep only the largest eigenvectors
starting at the smallest
i_{\mathrm{min}}
such that

\varrho_{i_{\mathrm{min}}}\geq\varrho_{\mathrm{threshold}}=1000
. The compression matrix

R
is then constructed from these
J=N-i_{\mathrm{min}}+1
eigenvectors
\bm{e}_{j}
,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=(\bm{e}_{i_{\mathrm{min}}},\ldots,\bm{e}_{N})^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{J\times N}\,,

(29)

which satisfies
RR^{\dagger}=1
. Then, the compressed data vector is

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=R\delta\,,

(30)

and the compressed covariance matrix is

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=RMR^{\dagger}\,,

(31)

which is positive-definite if
M
is positive-definite.
The likelihood analysis is then performed in compressed space with data vector

\Delta
and covariance matrix
C
.

Wang et al. 2020
have shown that the matrix
R
can be
recycled between iterations of the MCMC fitting procedure as long as the
parameter space that is being explored stays in a similar region.

For our MCMC we developed in Julia an adaptive Metropolis-Hastings sampler

(
Roberts and Rosenthal 2009
)
.
Adaptive
here means that
the covariance matrix of the proposal distribution is updated every few steps.
Therefore, the chain is Markovian only asymptotically in the limit of a large
number of steps. We use the following procedure to get a robust estimate of the
errors. The number of steps is a multiple of
M=n(n+1)
, where
n
is the
number of parameters. We first run the chain for
100M
steps to get an
estimate for the maximum likelihood point. Then, we run it again for
100M

points to get an estimate of the covariance matrix for the parameters. We then
run it twice more, each time updating both the starting point with the new
maximum lileklihood point and the initial covariance matrix. Finally, we run
the adaptive Metropolis-Hastings sampler for
700M
steps, and these samples
will be the ones used as output of the MCMC.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper we validate our pipeline for using the
SuperFaB
estimator
for cosmological inference on the spherical Fourier-Bessel pseudo power
spectrum. We develop a reasonably fast SFB power spectrum calculator along the
lines of
Khek et al. 2022
. We derive in detail the shot noise
and the local average effect with weights.

We start the validation with full-sky lognormal simulations in real and
redshift space
(
Agrawal et al. 2017
, similar to)
, and then
progressively add redshift-space distortions, realistic masks, radial
selection, and systematic weights using the EZmocks of

Zhao et al. 2021
. We also verify the use of potential and
velocity boundary conditions.

We model the EZmocks bias prescription by steps as shown in

Fig.

9
. For the NGC and SGC separately, we verify that the
model including all systematics is consistent with the measured modes of the
EZmocks,
Fig.

12
.

Finally, we construct a Gaussian likelihood for the power spectrum, we measure
the covariance matrix from all
1000
EZmocks with a
large-eigenvalue-inversion, and we verify that the measured
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
from 100
EZmocks is consistent with zero,
Fig.

14
, and that all the
parameters of the model are consistent with their fiducial values.

For the future we plan to apply the method on the data, improve the
covariance estimate, and develop the multi-tracer SFB analysis.

Appendix C
Local Average Effect with weighting

In this section we recognize that the average number density
{\bar{n}}
in

Eq.

7
must in practice be measured from
the survey itself. This is often called the
integral constraint

(
Beutler et al. 2014
;
de Mattia and Ruhlmann-Kleider 2019
)
or the

local average effect

(
de Putter et al. 2012
;
Wadekar et al. 2020
)
.

Compared to
Grasshorn Gebhardt and Doré 2021
we include a
weighting
w({\bm{r}})
. We also consider an extension where
{\bar{n}}(z)
is
independently determined at every redshift
(
de Mattia and Ruhlmann-Kleider 2019
)
.

Measuring the average number density is accomplished by dividing the total
number of galaxies in the survey (or the total number in each redshift bin) by
the effective volume. However, the total number of galaxies in the survey is a
stochastic quantity such that the average number density is given by

\displaystyle{\bar{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\bar{\delta}\,\right){\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}\,,

(44)

where
{\bar{n}}^{\mathrm{true}}
is the true density contrast if one were to measure on a
much larger survey volume. Defining the effective volume

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,W({\bm{r}})\,,

(45)

the average density contrast within the survey volume is

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{\mathrm{eff}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\bm{r}}\,W({\bm{r}})\,\delta({\bm{r}})\,,

(46)

or, if we measure the number density as a function of redshift,

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi f^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{sky}}(z)}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,W({\bm{r}})\,\delta({\bm{r}})\,,

(47)

where we define the effective sky fraction as

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{sky}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{r}\,W({\bm{r}})\,.

(48)

That is, we fold the radial selection
\phi(z)
into the effective sky fraction.
Next, we define the triplet
\mu=(n_{\mu},\ell_{\mu},m_{\mu})
. Then, the average
density field in SFB space becomes

\displaystyle\bar{\delta}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{\ell_{\mu}0}\delta^{K}_{m_{\mu}0}\begin{cases}V_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\,d_{\mu}d^{*}_{\nu}\,W_{\nu\rho}\delta_{\rho}&\text{ for }\bar{\delta}=const\,,\\
\widetilde{W}_{\mu\rho}\delta_{\rho}&\text{ for }\bar{\delta}=\bar{\delta}(z)\,,\end{cases}

(49)

where we sum over repeated indices and we defined the SFB transform of a unit
uniform field,

\displaystyle d_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4\pi}\,\delta^{K}_{\ell_{\mu}0}\delta^{K}_{m_{\mu}0}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{n_{\mu}0}(r)\,,

(50)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{\mu\rho}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}r\,g_{\mu}(r)\,g_{\rho}(r)\,Y^{*}_{\mu}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,Y_{\rho}({\hat{\bm{r}}})\,\frac{W({\bm{r}})}{f^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{sky}}(z)}\,,

(51)

which is the mixing matrix if the window is
W/f^{\mathrm{eff}}_{\mathrm{sky}}

(

. If the

With our model in

(
Taruya et al. 2021

\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{obs}}({\bm{r}})\equiv\delta^{wWA}({\bm{r}})

The SFB transform of

\displaystyle\simeq(wW)_{\mu\rho}\,[\delta_{\rho}-\bar{\delta}_{\rho}]\,,

to first order in

, and we defined the mixing matrix

The observed weighted correlation function is

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta^{wWA}_{\mu}\delta^{wWA,*}_{\nu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(wW)_{\mu\rho}\Big[\left\langle\delta_{\rho}\delta^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle-\left\langle\delta_{\rho}\bar{\delta}^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left\langle\bar{\delta}_{\rho}\delta^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle+\left\langle\bar{\delta}_{\rho}\bar{\delta}^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle\Big](wW)_{\lambda\nu}\,,

(54)

where we used that the mixing matrix is Hermitian,

(wW)^{*}_{\kappa\lambda}=(wW)_{\lambda\kappa}

Appendix

C
contains four terms. The first term is the clustering
term,

\displaystyle P^{1}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(wW)_{\mu\rho}\left\langle\delta_{\rho}\delta^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle(wW)_{\lambda\nu}\,.

(55)

The other terms in
Appendix

we call

,

, and

with similar definitions as

,
and they are due to the local average effect. The second and third terms are
related by taking the adjoint. That is,

\displaystyle P^{3}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(wW)_{\mu\rho}\left\langle\bar{\delta}_{\rho}\delta^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle(wW)_{\lambda\nu}=P^{2,*}_{\nu\mu}\,.

(56)

The second and third terms are the computationally most expensive to calculate.
The reason is that the correlation between the two isotropic fields

\delta({\bm{r}})
and
\bar{\delta}(r^{\prime})
comes from the anisotropic region on the
partial sky defined by the window function. Thus, the correlation

\left\langle\delta({\bm{r}})\bar{\delta}(r^{\prime})\right\rangle
is anisotropic, and this couples

\ell=0
modes with higher-
\ell
modes. Hence, treating

\left\langle\delta_{\rho}\bar{\delta}^{*}_{\lambda}\right\rangle
as a pseudo-power spectrum with

(\ell_{\rho},m_{\rho})=(\ell_{\lambda},m_{\lambda})
is inadequate, in general.
However, we use the heuristic that only modes up to

\ell_{\rho}\lesssim 1/(2f_{\mathrm{sky}})
couple to
\ell_{\lambda}=0
, since that is
approximately the width of the window function in spherical harmonic space.

We treat the clustering and shot noise terms separately so that
P^{i}=C^{i}+N^{i}
. We
define the observed weighted power spectrum and shot noise as

\displaystyle C^{wW}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(wW)_{\mu\sigma}C_{\sigma\rho}(wW)_{\rho\nu}\,,

(57)

\displaystyle N^{wW}_{\sigma\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\bar{n}}}\,(w^{2}W)_{\sigma\rho}=\frac{1}{{\bar{n}}}\,w_{\sigma\alpha}W_{\alpha\beta}w_{\beta\rho}\,,

(58)

where
C_{\sigma\rho}
enforces the isotropy condition
\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{\rho}

and
m_{\sigma}=m_{\rho}
, and the shot noise is from
Eq.

42

Then,

\displaystyle P^{1}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=C^{wW}_{\sigma\rho}+N^{wW}_{\mu\nu}\,.

(59)

To calculate the local average effect terms we treat them in two different ways,

\bar{\delta}
or
\bar{\delta}(z)

In either case, the result can be separated into clustering and shot noise
terms. Furthermore, we will only consider the pseudo-SFB power spectrum.

The radial part of the Laplacian eigenequation in spherical polar coordinates

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}g_{\ell}(kr)}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)+\big[(kr)^{2}-\ell(\ell+1)\big]\,g_{\ell}(kr)\,,

where function

\ell

modes for a given
\ell
. We then use that to derive the form
of the
g_{\ell}
. Following
Fisher et al. 1995
, we demand that
the orthonormality relation
Eq.

4

\displaystyle\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{n\ell}(r)\,g_{n^{\prime}\ell}(r)

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{nn^{\prime}}

(110)

is satisfied, where we defined
g_{n\ell}(r)=g_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}r)
. However, we
modify the approach in
Fisher et al. 1995
to integrate from

r_{\mathrm{min}}
to
r_{\mathrm{max}}
, which will in general add spherical Bessels of the second
kind to the solution.
Eq.

109
multiplied by
g_{\ell}(kr)
yields

\displaystyle\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}g_{\ell}(kr)}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,\big[\ell(\ell+1)-(kr)^{2}\big]\,g_{\ell}(kr)\,g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)\,.

(111)

Subtract from this equation the same equation with
k
and
k^{\prime}
interchanged,

\displaystyle\big[k^{\prime 2}-k^{2}\big]\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{\ell}(kr)\,g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,\bigg\{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}g_{\ell}(kr)}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)g_{\ell}(kr)\bigg\}\,.

(112)

Partial integration of the terms on the right hand side (r.h.s.) gives

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}r\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\!\left(kr^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)\right)g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)

\displaystyle=\left.kr^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)\right|_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}-kk^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)g^{\prime}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)\,,

(113)

where primes denote derivatives with respect to the argument.
Then,
Eq.

112
becomes

\displaystyle\big[k^{\prime 2}-k^{2}\big]\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{\ell}(kr)\,g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)

\displaystyle=\left.kr^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)g_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)\right|_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}-\left.k^{\prime}r^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)g_{\ell}(kr)\right|_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\,.

(114)

The r.h.s. will vanish for any
k
whenever

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=Akr_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})-Br_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}g_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})

\displaystyle\quad-akr_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})+br_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}g_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})\,,

(115)

for any constants
a
,
b
,
A
, and
B
(given by
k^{\prime}
).

To choose
a
,
b
,
A
, and
B
, we need to choose boundary conditions.
The Neumann boundary conditions are

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})

\displaystyle=0\,,

(116)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})

\displaystyle=0\,.

(117)

These will ensure that a spherical boundary in real space remains a spherical
boundary in redshift space as long as the observer is at
r=0
. Thus, the
theoretical redshift-space modeling will be greatly simplified.

Writing the basis as a combination of spherical Bessel functions of the first
and second kinds,

\displaystyle g_{\ell}(kr)=c_{n\ell}\,j_{\ell}(kr)+d_{n\ell}\,y_{\ell}(kr)\,,

(118)

where we anticipate that the function
g_{\ell}
will also depend on
n
.
Then,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{n\ell}\,j^{\prime}_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}\,r_{\mathrm{min}})+d_{n\ell}\,y^{\prime}_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}\,r_{\mathrm{min}})\,,

(119)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{n\ell}\,j^{\prime}_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}\,r_{\mathrm{max}})+d_{n\ell}\,y^{\prime}_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}\,r_{\mathrm{max}})\,.

(120)

This is equivalent to choosing
B=b=0
in

Eq.

115
. Thus, the conditions

Eqs.

119
and
120
lead to a set of
orthogonal basis functions
g_{n\ell}(r)=g_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}r)
.

Both
j_{\ell}
and
y_{\ell}
satisfy the two recurrence relations

\displaystyle j^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)

\displaystyle=-j_{\ell+1}(kr)+\frac{\ell}{kr}\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,,

(121)

\displaystyle j^{\prime}_{\ell}(kr)

\displaystyle=j_{\ell-1}(kr)-\frac{\ell+1}{kr}\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,.

(122)

Solving both
Eqs.

119
and
120
for
d_{n\ell}/c_{n\ell}
,

\displaystyle\frac{-kr_{\mathrm{min}}\,j_{\ell+1}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})+\ell\,j_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})}{-kr_{\mathrm{min}}\,y_{\ell+1}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})+\ell\,y_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{min}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{-kr_{\mathrm{max}}\,j_{\ell+1}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})+\ell\,j_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})}{-kr_{\mathrm{max}}\,y_{\ell+1}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})+\ell\,y_{\ell}(kr_{\mathrm{max}})}\,,

(123)

which needs to be satisfied for
k_{n\ell}
. When
r_{\mathrm{min}}=0
, then

d_{n\ell}=0
so that the
y_{\ell}
, which diverge as their argument vanishes,
do not contribute.

The solution
k_{n\ell}=0
is valid for
\ell=0
only, because otherwise the
normalization for
g_{n\ell}
does not exist, see
Eq.

118
and
the next section.

D.1
Normalization

The normalization of
g_{\ell}
is obtained by dividing

Eq.

114
by
k^{\prime 2}-k^{2}
, and taking the limit
k^{\prime}\to k=k_{n\ell}
,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g^{2}_{\ell}(kr)=\lim_{k^{\prime}\to k}\frac{-\left.k^{\prime}r^{2}g^{\prime}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}r)g_{\ell}(kr)\right|_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}}{(k^{\prime}-k)(k^{\prime}+k)}\,.

(124)

Thus, the key is to calculate

\displaystyle\lim_{k^{\prime}\to k_{n\ell}}\frac{g^{\prime}_{\ell}(k^{\prime}R)}{k^{\prime}-k_{n\ell}}

\displaystyle=R\,g^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}R)\,,

(125)

where
R
is either
r_{\mathrm{min}}
or
r_{\mathrm{max}}
, and we expanded
g^{\prime}_{\ell}
in a Taylor
series around
k^{\prime}\sim k_{n\ell}
. Hence,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left.r^{3}g_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}r)\,g^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}r)\right|_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\,.

(126)

We can evaluate by using

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}(kr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(kr)^{2}}\left[\left(\ell(\ell-1)-(kr)^{2}\right)g_{\ell}(kr)+2kr\,g_{\ell+1}(kr)\right],

(127)

which follows from
Eqs.

121
and
122
.

For the special case
k_{n\ell}=\ell=0
, the normalization is given by

\displaystyle\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left(r_{\mathrm{max}}^{3}-r_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}\right).

(128)

Choosing
k_{n\ell}
,
c_{n\ell}
, and
d_{n\ell}
that satisfy

Eqs.

123
,
120
and
126

guarantees the orthonormality of the
g_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\int_{r_{\mathrm{min}}}^{r_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}\,g_{\ell}(k_{n\ell}\,r)\,g_{\ell}(k_{n^{\prime}\ell}\,r)

\displaystyle=\delta^{K}_{nn^{\prime}}\,.

(129)

Note that the condition
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
is
not
enforced by the
g_{\ell}
.
Instead,
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
comes from the spherical harmonics, i.e.,
Eq.

33
.
